## Executive Summary

This 1989 study by Minckley, Buth, and Mayden documents the founding of a captive brood stock for the critically endangered [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), a large riverine cyprinid historically distributed throughout the [[Colorado River]] system from Wyoming to northwestern Mexico. Wild populations collapsed dramatically after the mid-20th century due to habitat alteration, dam construction/impoundment, and introduction of nonnative fishes, leaving only remnant, non-recruiting adult populations by the 1970s–1980s. Between 1976 and 1981, USFWS biologists captured 18 wild adult bonytails from [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona–Nevada) and used them to establish a captive brood stock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico, via both artificial propagation and natural pond reproduction. The authors performed allozyme electrophoresis on 24 naturally produced F₂ juveniles across 45 gene loci to assess genetic diversity of the hatchery stock relative to a congener, the [[Arroyo Chub]] (*Gila orcutti*), and to broader cyprinid benchmarks. Results indicated that despite the extremely narrow founding population (effectively 5 breeding pairs), the hatchery stock retained allozyme heterozygosity comparable to wild western North American cyprinids, supporting its use for reintroduction efforts, while underscoring the ongoing need to locate additional wild bonytail populations.

## Background & Description

- **Body form**: Streamlined, riverine cyprinid body plan adapted to large-river mainstream habitats.
- **Size range of wild brood fish (Lake Mohave captures)**:
  - Females: 46.9–56.4 cm total length (TL); some individuals exceeded 45 cm TL.
  - Males: 45.4–48.0 cm TL.
- **Age structure**: Otolith aging of dead specimens indicated exceptionally old individuals — apparent ages of 34, 40, 42, and 49 years among early captures; later recaptured fish included one >20 years old and estimates of ~6 annuli or ≥10 years for 1988 specimens.
- **Hybridization concerns**: Some upper Colorado basin specimens tentatively identified as bonytail showed morphological evidence of hybridization with [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) or [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). In contrast, Lake Mohave specimens were morphologically uniform with **no external evidence of interspecific hybridization**, and no other *Gila* species co-occur in that reservoir.
- **Growth**: Hatchery-stocked fish (1981 year class) released into Lake Mohave were projected to reach ~40 cm TL by 1986 and 40–60 cm TL (within adult wild size range) by 1988, based on hatchery and other artificial-habitat growth rates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic range**: Entire [[Colorado River]] drainage — larger mainstream rivers from Wyoming and Colorado (upper basin) to northwestern Mexico (lower basin).
- **Current wild status (as of study)**: Near-extinct in the wild.
  - Only 1 individual identified from the upper Colorado River since 1976 (captured 1984).
  - Fewer than 10 caught in the Green River subbasin.
  - No more than 5 individuals taken from Cataract Canyon.
  - Largest remaining wild aggregation: 34 specimens from [[Lake Mohave]] (1976–1988), a mainstream reservoir impounded in 1954.
- **Reproductive ecology**: Historical account (Jonez and Sumner 1954) describes ~500 bonytails broadcasting gametes over a gravelly shelf in Lake Mohave — evidence of natural mainstream/reservoir spawning behavior. Similar milling, chasing, and nudging behaviors suggestive of spawning were observed among >200 hatchery pond fish at Dexter.
- **Threats**: Habitat destruction/alteration from water development (damming, impoundment), introduction and establishment of nonnative fishes, and other anthropogenic factors. Comparable declines noted in the co-occurring [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in Lake Mohave — large adults persist but recruitment into adult populations has been negligible since roughly the 1950s.
- **Conservation intervention**: USFWS established a captive brood stock program in the 1970s at Dexter National Fish Hatchery to prevent extinction. Roughly 130,000 fish (original F₁: 54,941; hand-cultured F₂: 28,710; naturally produced F₂: 46,629) were stocked back into Lake Mohave between 1981–1987 to augment the wild population.

## Key Findings

- **Brood stock founding**: 18 wild bonytails captured from Lake Mohave (1976: 3; 1979: 2; 1980: 5; 1981: 8). Early captures (1976–1980) were disproportionately large, old females, raising concern over possible male scarcity/differential mortality; males were only secured in 1981 (5 males, plus 2 more females).
- **Artificial propagation success (1981)**: Five pairs (5 females × 5 males) were induced to spawn; ova totaled ~125,450 with a 95% average hatching rate (range 91–99%). ~80,000 larvae were transferred to Dexter grow-out ponds, yielding ~1,700 F₁ adults by 1988.
- **Genetic sampling**: 24 naturally produced F₂ juveniles (1985 year class; 65–95 mm SL) were examined electrophoretically across 45 soluble gene loci from muscle, liver, and brain tissue.
- **Polymorphic loci identified (6 of 45)**:
  - [[sAat-A]] (aspartate aminotransferase, muscle)
  - [[Gpi-B]] (glucose-6-phosphate isomerase, muscle)
  - [[Pgm-A]] (phosphoglucomutase, muscle)
  - [[Est-2]] (esterase, brain)
  - [[Cat-1]] (catalase, liver)
  - [[Iddh-A]] (L-iditol dehydrogenase, liver)
- **Hardy-Weinberg conformity**: Electromorph distributions at all six polymorphic loci matched Hardy-Weinberg expectations (exact test probability = 1.00 at five of six loci; Cat-1 = 0.43).
- **Heterozygosity comparisons** (based on 29 loci scored in common):
  | Species | N | % Polymorphic Loci | Observed Heterozygosity | Expected Heterozygosity |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Bonytail | 24 | 13.8% | 0.027 ± 0.017 | 0.026 ± 0.017 |
  | Arroyo Chub | 8 | 13.8% | 0.065 ± 0.038 | 0.061 ± 0.030 |
  
  - Arroyo chub heterozygosity was roughly **twice** that of hatchery bonytail.
  - However, bonytail heterozygosity (0.026–0.032 across full 45-locus and 29-locus datasets) fell only slightly below the mean reported for western *Gila* and related cyprinids (0.038 ± 0.008; Avise 1977), indicating the hatchery stock's genetic diversity is broadly consistent with natural cyprinid populations.
- **Interpretation**: Retained genetic variability in F₂ hatchery fish is attributed to Hamman's (1982) breeding protocol, in which each female's eggs were fertilized by a different male, maximizing genetic contribution from the five founding pairs. This method was successfully repeated in 1983–1984 spawning trials with younger hatchery-reared fish.
- **Management implications**: No morphological abnormalities were observed in Dexter stock or progeny; hatchery fish reproduced naturally in ponds and survived to adulthood after reintroduction into Lake Mohave. Authors concluded the Dexter stock is genetically suitable for continued reintroduction efforts in the lower Colorado River basin, while strongly recommending expanded searches for additional wild bonytail populations to safeguard against loss of genetic variability not captured in the current brood stock (per [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] directives).
- **Methodological note**: Meaningful interspecific heterozygosity comparisons require homologous loci in closely related taxa; among available congener data, only [[Arroyo Chub]] (Crabtree and Buth 1981) met this criterion for direct comparison with bonytail.
