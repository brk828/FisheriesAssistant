# Genetic Characterization of Sonoran Topminnow Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliopsis

## Executive Summary

This study uses five polymorphic [[Microsatellite Loci]] and a [[Major Histocompatibility Complex]] (MHC) gene to characterize 13 populations of [[Gila Topminnow]] and two populations of [[Yaqui Topminnow]] in Arizona. The aim is to identify [[Evolutionarily Significant Units]] (ESUs) and [[Management Units]] (MUs) to support the conservation of these endangered fishes. The authors report five natural groups of Gila topminnow, a proposed ESU and several MUs, the likely origins of restocked and recolonized populations, and a strong genetic divergence between the two taxa, which they recommend be considered for ranking as separate species. The authors describe their conclusions as preliminary.

## Background & Description

- **Gila topminnow** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*): once one of the most abundant fishes in the Gila River Drainage. It now occurs naturally in the United States in only four isolated Arizona watersheds, across eight populations. Other populations result from restocking, and at least one site on the [[Santa Cruz River]] appears to have been recolonized naturally.
- **Yaqui topminnow** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*): never widespread in the United States, because the Yaqui River drainage covers only a small part of southeastern Arizona. In the United States it occurs only on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] in the upper Yaqui River watershed.
- **Prior work:** Earlier studies by [[Parker Et Al 1999]] and [[Hedrick and Parker 1998]] used the same markers to differentiate Gila topminnow stocks from four major watersheds: [[Bylas Spring]] 2, [[Cienega Creek]], [[Monkey Spring]], and [[Sharp Spring]].
- **Samples in this study:**
  - Nine additional Gila topminnow populations, collected primarily by David Weedman (AGF): Bylas Spring 1, [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]], [[Watson Wash]] (Safford), [[Cottonwood Spring]], Red Rock at Cott Tank, Red Rock Falls, [[Coalmine Creek]], [[Sonoita Creek]] (below Patagonia Lake), and the Santa Cruz River (downstream from the Nogales Water Treatment Plant outflow).
  - Two Yaqui topminnow populations, collected by Kevin Cobble (USFS): Tule Spring and North Pond, both on the San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge.
- **Population history:** The Boyce Thompson population was used extensively for restocking and was thought to derive from Monkey Spring fish, and possibly Bylas Spring and/or the extinct [[Coscio Wash]] population. The Watson Wash population is a warm spring stocked from an unknown source. The Santa Cruz site appears to have been naturally recolonized in recent years from unknown sources.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Conservation framework:** The paper frames ESUs (populations evolving independently; [[Waples 1995]]) and MUs (populations with diverged allele frequencies and low gene flow; [[Moritz 1994]]) as the basis for conservation decisions.
- **Range contraction:** The Gila topminnow's natural U.S. range is now restricted to four isolated watersheds, and the Yaqui topminnow survives in a single U.S. refuge system, making genetic isolation and restocking history central management concerns.
- **Habitat examples:** Sampled sites include springs (Bylas Spring, Monkey Spring, Cottonwood Spring), a warm spring (Watson Wash), creeks (Cienega, Coalmine, Sonoita), and the Santa Cruz River, which receives treatment-plant outflow.
- **Genetic markers:** Dinucleotide [[Microsatellite Loci]] are used, and [[Mitochondrial DNA]] restriction-site data from [[Quattro et al 1996]] are cited as independent support.

## Key Findings

### Gila topminnow: five natural groups (preliminary)
1. **Bylas group:** Bylas Spring 1 and Bylas Spring 2 are very similar to each other and different from other populations, with low genetic variation (heterozygosity and number of alleles).
2. **Monkey–Cottonwood group:** Monkey and Cottonwood Springs are similar to each other and different from other natural populations, with intermediate variation.
3. **Red Rock–Coalmine–Sonoita group:** Sonoita Creek, Coalmine Canyon, Red Rock at Cott Tank, and Red Rock Falls cluster together, with Coalmine and Sonoita very similar. Red Rock at Cott Tank is variable at only one locus.
4. **Cienega Creek:** Shares nearly no alleles with Sharp Spring at MS loci C-15 and LL53, and has intermediate genetic variation.
5. **Sharp Spring:** Has higher than average genetic variation and shares no MHC alleles with Cienega Creek.

### Restocked and recolonized populations
- **Boyce Thompson:** Appears to descend primarily from Monkey Spring. It shows no overlap with Bylas Spring alleles at locus 6-10 or at the MHC gene, so substantial Bylas ancestry is unlikely. It carries a new MHC allele (Pooc-12) at low frequency and a relatively high frequency of Pooc-9, which has been found only in Sharp Spring so far. This may reflect ancestry from Coscio Wash, but this cannot be tested because no extinct-population samples exist.
- **Watson Wash (Safford):** Very similar to Boyce Thompson, consistent with Boyce Thompson as the source.
- **Santa Cruz River:** Clusters with the nearby Sonoita Creek samples and appears to descend primarily from lower Sonoita Creek populations, possibly with some upper Santa Cruz (Sharp Spring) ancestry. It is the most variable sample analyzed, with variation at all five MS loci and the highest heterozygosity (mean Ho = 0.520) and number of alleles (mean n = 4.4).

### Management recommendations (from Conclusions 1–4)
- Monkey Spring was previously suggested as an ESU, and Bylas Spring 2, Cienega Creek, and Sharp Spring as separate MUs. The new results are consistent with these recommendations.
- The Sonoita Creek group (Red Rock–Coalmine–Sonoita) should tentatively be considered another MU.
- Monkey and Cottonwood Springs should tentatively be considered a single ESU.

### Yaqui topminnow
- The two samples (Tule Spring, n = 20; North Pond, n = 19) are very similar in allele frequencies and in amount of genetic variation (mean Ho 0.393 and 0.425).
- Eight MHC alleles were found in the Yaqui samples, none of which occur in any Gila topminnow population.
- Locus 6-10 did not amplify reliably in the Yaqui topminnow, so it was excluded. Three loci (4-44, Acc, G53) are variable in Yaqui topminnows but invariant in Gila topminnows.

### Taxonomic divergence between the two taxa
- For six loci (including MHC), the two taxa share no alleles.
- At Pooc-OO56, Gila topminnow alleles range from 143 to 153 bp, while the Yaqui topminnow is fixed for allele 256, over 100 bp larger.
- Because most dinucleotide mutations differ by only about two base pairs, the authors infer very long and complete isolation between the taxa. This is consistent with mtDNA restriction-site differences reported by [[Quattro et al 1996]].
- **Conclusion 6:** Based on the genetic data, the mtDNA data, and morphological and behavioral differences (Minckley, personal communication), the two taxa should be considered for ranking as separate species.
