# The Endemic Herpetofauna of the Cuatro Ciénegas Basin, Coahuila, Mexico

#herpetology #biogeography #chelonia #anura #squamata

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Sandy Echternacht, examines the origins and relationships of the endemic reptiles and amphibians of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], an isolated desert basin in central [[Coahuila]], Mexico. Of the reptile and amphibian species known from the basin (the document gives only a conservative figure of "something over" a stated number, which is illegible in the scan), five are treated as endemic and three as relicts of populations now found further north. The author argues that all endemic forms are associated with mesic or aquatic habitats and that the basin functions as a moisture refugium, with origins tied to isolation during post-pluvial drying in the Pleistocene.

## Background & Description

- **Location:** About 9 miles (scan reads "Ps") west of Monclova in central Coahuila. The town of Cuatro Ciénegas lies at approximately 26°59' N, 102°0' W (minutes partly illegible in scan).
- **Dimensions:** About 30 miles long (west to east) and 6 to 15 miles wide.
- **Topography:** Bounded by [[Sierra del Carmen-Sierra Madre Oriental Filter Barrier]] features. Sierra de Anteojo (north) rises to about 2,750 m; Sierra de San Marcos (south) to about 250 m (as printed; the second value is likely a scan error). The basin floor lies at about 720 m above sea level. The northern tip of Sierra de San Marcos comes within about six miles of dividing the basin into two halves.
- **Geology:** The eastern half is the best biologically known and is overlain in many places by Cretaceous gypsum.
- **Hydrology:** Numerous thermal springs, mostly along the eastern edge of Sierra de San Marcos (one reaching 102°F), canals, and the [[Río Nadadores]] drain eastward toward the [[Río Grande]].
- **Formation:** The basin likely formed with the final uplift of the Sierra Madre Oriental, probably in the late Pliocene and continuing into the Pleistocene.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The basin's abundant water relative to surrounding arid terrain makes it a refugium for moisture-dependent species. The paper reviews the Pleistocene pluvial history of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]]: there is general agreement that pluvial periods occurred, but disagreement over their magnitude. [[Blair (1958)]] argued for forest corridors through Texas, whereas [[Martin and Harrell (1957)]] argued for less intense pluvial conditions. The author notes that an annual rainfall increase of only six to ten inches, as suggested by [[Milstead (1960)]], would suffice for these species to extend their ranges westward and southward, so extensive forest corridors were not required.

Multiple pluvial periods would have provided repeated opportunities for isolation, with differing isolation durations allowing varying degrees of divergence.

## Key Findings

**Endemic species and proposed origins**

- **[[Eleutherodactylus augusti fuscofemora]]** (Zweifel, 1956): Split from *E. augusti latrans* largely on femur color, with few specimens available. Collected at rodent burrow openings and caught in mouse traps in early morning. Origin proposed either from an extinct stock that vanished outside the basin during post-pluvial drying, or from an isolated relict population of *latrans*.
- **[[Pseudemys scripta taylori]]** (Legler, 1960): Closely allied with *P. s. elegans*, intergrading with it outside the basin. Both are thought to derive from an earlier lower Rio Grande form, with *taylori* possibly a relict isolated in the basin during post-pluvial times.
- **[[Terrapene coahuilae]]** (Schmidt and Owens, 1960s): Described as the most distinctive endemic, and the only known aquatic [[Terrapene]]. Legler regards it as the most primitive member of the genus; Milstead considers it a remnant of a southern extension of [[Terrapene carolina]], which occurred in the Balconian and Tamaulipan biotic provinces at the end of the Pleistocene.
- **[[Trionyx ater]]** (Webb and Legler, 1960): A soft-shelled turtle considered a derivative of a relictual pre-*Trionyx spinifer* stock. Sympatric with, but ecologically semi-distinct from, *T. spinifer emoryi*, which the authors believe reentered the basin relatively recently. Specific status rests on this sympatry without known intergradation; the author notes that the diagnostic nasal septum character is based on a single male specimen and that more study is needed.
- **[[Cnemidophorus scalaris pallidus]]** (Duellman and Zweifel, 1962): Most closely related to *C. s. septemvittatus*, which occurs in southwest Texas, northern Coahuila, and just south of the basin, and has been collected within 16 km of basin localities. Prefers habitats near water and is never found in adjacent arid areas. Probably a remnant of a population that became isolated in the basin during post-pluvial times.

**Relict species**

Three species (*Eumeces tetragrammus*, *Scincella laterale*, *Natrix erythrogaster transversa*) have populations further north. The author suggests similar isolation processes could explain their presence in the basin.

**Divergence pattern**

- *E. a. fuscofemora*, *T. ater*, and *C. s. pallidus* appear to have changed little from their nearest relatives.
- *T. coahuilae* and *P. s. taylori* appear to have diverged more substantially.

**Conclusions and future work**

- All endemic forms currently inhabit the more mesic or aquatic habitats within the basin.
- The author withholds detailed conclusions on relict origins in central Mexican deserts until more evidence on Pleistocene pluvial periods is available.
- Pollen analysis of core samples from basins such as Cuatro Ciénegas is identified as a promising, largely unexplored route to clarify past climate, with little such work done in Mexico so far.
