## Executive Summary

This review by [[Klaus D. Kallman]] synthesizes decades of experimental work on the genetics of **tissue and organ transplantation immunity** in teleost fish, with primary emphasis on the platyfish [[Xiphophorus maculatus]] and related poeciliids. The paper demonstrates that allograft rejection in fish is a true immunological phenomenon governed by histocompatibility (H) genes operating largely according to mammalian "genetic laws of transplantation," albeit with notable exceptions at weak histocompatibility loci. Key themes include **gene dosage effects**, **temperature dependence** of rejection kinetics, **tissue-specific differences in antigenicity** (heart vs. fin vs. scale vs. gonad), **estimation of the number of H-loci** in natural and inbred populations, and the use of transplantation as a tool to detect **clonal structure in unisexual (gynogenetic/parthenogenetic/self-fertilizing) fish** such as [[Poecilia formosa]] and [[Rivulus marmoratus]]. A novel case of spontaneous unisexual reproduction in [[Gambusia affinis]] is also reported.

## Background & Description

- **Graft types studied:** fin (caudal fin clips), scale, heart, gonad (testis/ovary), skin, kidney, and embryonic tissue transplants.
- **Heart allografts:** Transplanted hearts continued rhythmic contractions visible to the naked eye and grew in size proportional to host age; grafts often bulged through the body wall. Rejection was macroscopically marked by discoloration (loss of healthy blood-red color), focal points of rejection, lymphocytic/connective tissue infiltration, and a greyish-white appearance preceding cessation of contraction. Small twitches persisted as long as recognizable muscle remained.
- **Fin/scale grafts:** Used as the standard assay tissue for genetic dosage and interstrain comparisons; generally more antigenic (rejected faster) than heart tissue from the same donor.
- **Comparative tissue antigenicity:** When heart and fin from the same F₁ donor were grafted simultaneously into the same P₁ host, fins were **always rejected before hearts**, indicating heart tissue is relatively resistant to allograft rejection — potentially due to differences in the efferent (effector) arm of the immune response rather than differences in antigen recognition.
- **Embryonic protection:** In ovoviviparous [[Xiphophorus hellerii]], the fertilization membrane appears to shield developing embryos from maternal immune rejection; embryos transplanted without this membrane elicited allograft rejection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat contexts:** Freshwater poeciliids ([[Xiphophorus maculatus]], [[Xiphophorus couchianus]], [[Poecilia reticulata]], [[Gambusia affinis]]) from Mexican and southern US natural populations; the self-fertilizing hermaphroditic [[Rivulus marmoratus]] from mangrove/coastal habitats; the medaka [[Oryzias latipes]] (freshwater, Japan); the primitive bony tongue fish [[Osteoglossum bicirrhosum]] (South American freshwater); the loach [[Noemacheilus barbatula]] (European streams); and marine serranid/sparid/labrid fishes referenced for gonadal autograft studies.
- **Population genetic structure:** Tissue transplantation was used as a tool to infer breeding structure of wild populations. Small, isolated, or highly inbred populations (e.g., isolated [[Xiphophorus couchianus]] populations, orange-red medaka hatchery stock, aquarium-bred guppies) show increased graft acceptance due to shared H-antigens from inbreeding, while outbred natural populations show near-universal rejection between unrelated individuals.
- **Unisexual/clonal species as natural experiments:** [[Poecilia formosa]] (all-female gynogenetic species) and [[Rivulus marmoratus]] (self-fertilizing hermaphrodite) provided natural clonal populations in which transplantation was used to map clonal structure — descendants of a single female/hermaphrodite accept grafts from one another regardless of the "male parent" species used in gynogenetic mating.
- **Temperature as an ecological variable:** As poikilotherms, fish rejection kinetics are strongly temperature-dependent, contrasting with homeotherms (mammals) which can only be studied at relatively high, narrow temperature ranges — potentially masking dosage effects in mammalian systems.

## Key Findings

- **Genetic laws of transplantation apply to fish**, with a general one-to-one relationship between H-genes and transplantation antigens, though exceptions occur at weak histoincompatibility barriers.
- **Gene dosage effect confirmed:** F₁ hybrid grafts survive significantly longer than parental (interstrain) grafts in P₁ or backcross (Bc) hosts (e.g., 21.6% of F₁ grafts vs. 9.4% of P₁ grafts survived in Bc hosts). No host ever rejected an F₁ graft prior to rejecting the corresponding P₁ graft.
- **Dosage effect is temperature-dependent:** Minimal overlap between homozygous/heterozygous graft survival at low temperatures (20°C); dosage effect nearly disappears at high temperatures (Table 1).
- **Heart grafts show dosage effects more clearly than fin grafts** (Table 2); some F₁ hearts survived as long as autografts (460 days) before the experiment was terminated.
- **Tissue-specific antigenicity:** Heart tissue is more resistant to rejection than fin tissue from identical donor/host combinations, paralleling reported antigenicity differences between organs in mice and rats.
- **Interstrain grafts are rejected rapidly and uniformly**, consistent with an additive effect of several weak H-loci — later supported by mouse studies.
- **Estimated number of H-loci:**
  - Jamapa vs. New River strains of *X. maculatus*: 10–11 loci differ.
  - Jamapa *X. maculatus* vs. *X. couchianus*: 10–15 loci (best fit ~12).
  - Two Jamapa stocks of *X. maculatus*: only 3 loci differ.
  - Two unrelated goldfish F₂ generation: heterozygous for ≥3 loci.
  - These are minimum estimates, since H-loci linkage was assumed absent and single H-differences were assumed sufficient for rejection — both assumptions are now known to be imperfect.
- **Weak H-loci produce "paradoxical" results:** e.g., some female platyfish accept male grafts despite sex-linked locus differences; some [[Xiphophorus hellerii]] hosts reject a primary graft but accept a secondary graft from the same donor (or vice versa) — mirroring similar paradoxes documented in mice.
- **Eichwald–Silmser phenomenon** detected in platyfish scale grafts but not fin grafts, suggesting tissue-specific differences in graft antigenic behavior.
- **Natural population inbreeding drives graft acceptance:** Permanent graft survival in isolated *X. couchianus* populations and rejection between subspecies (*X. c. couchianus* vs. *X. c. gordoni*) supports the small-population/inbreeding hypothesis over species-level low H-locus diversity.
- **Gonad transplantation anomalies:** >66% of testis grafts into ovariectomized medaka survived >1 year and functioned physiologically, but ovary grafts into castrated males failed — possibly reflecting inbred hatchery stock plus lower gonadal antigenicity, though marine fish gonad transplants only succeeded as autografts.
- **Clonal detection via transplantation:**
  - [[Poecilia formosa]] populations from southern Texas comprise few stable clones over many years; only clones V and XI showed a chronic rejection pattern; inter-clone grafts otherwise rejected rapidly.
  - A Mexican *P. formosa* population showed longer-surviving interclonal grafts than the Texas population.
  - [[Rivulus marmoratus]] hybrid accepted a graft from the clone serving as its "male" parent, confirming clone homozygosity.
- **Novel finding — spontaneous unisexual reproduction in [[Gambusia affinis]]:** A virgin female produced two broods; grafts from unisexual offspring to the mother survived >9 months (expected under a shared-genotype model), while sibling-to-sibling and offspring-to-parent grafts among sexually produced fish were rejected within 5–12 days — first report of this phenomenon in this species.
- **Embryonic immune privilege:** Melanoma tissue transplanted into early-stage *Xiphophorus* embryos did not induce tolerance; fin grafts in 2-day-old *X. maculatus* were rejected within 11 days, indicating rapid immunocompetence onset. In [[Cymatogaster aggregata]], allograft sensitivity could be transferred from mother to embryo.
- **Third-party graft test** is valid for population-level averages but questionable for individual donor-pair predictions given high variability at weak histocompatibility loci.
- **Primitive teleost comparison:** [[Osteoglossum bicirrhosum]] shows somewhat longer allograft survival than typical teleosts, possibly reflecting a more "primitive" immune system, though this remains speculative absent data on natural significance of transplantation antigens.
