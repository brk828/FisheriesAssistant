# Effects of Jaw Tags and Fin Excision on Hatchery Rainbow Trout Fingerlings in Hunt Creek, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by David S. Shetter, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (vol. 96, no. 4, pp. 394–399), tested whether the marks commonly used in [[Fishery Biology]] research bias the results they are meant to measure. About 1,000 [[Rainbow Trout]] fingerlings in each of three groups (jaw-tagged, fin-clipped, and unmarked) were stocked into experimental sections of [[Hunt Creek]], Michigan, in October 1952 and followed through 1957.

Jaw tags slowed growth by roughly 15% during the first year and reduced survival and angler recovery. [[Fin Clipping]] had little effect on growth and produced results intermediate between tagged and unmarked fish. Stocking the rainbow trout did not measurably harm the native [[Brook Trout]] population or brook trout angling.

## Background & Description

Marked fish are widely used to estimate growth, survival, movement and exploitation, but the effects of marking are often not checked. Shetter notes that many investigators did not test whether marks altered the fish under study. This paper was written as a controlled test of two common marks.

**Study design**

- **Test fish:** 3,000 rainbow trout fingerlings from the [[Oden State Fish Hatchery]], Michigan, planted on 22 October 1952 at five sites between blocking weirs. The final planted counts were 995 tagged, 1,000 fin-clipped and 1,000 unmarked fish (2,995 total).
- **Jaw tags:** fingerling-size (#1) [[Monel Metal Jaw Tags]], 20 × 2 mm, 0.16 g each, flattened.
- **Fin clip:** removal of the adipose and right pectoral fins.
- **Site:** Hunt Creek, which held a good wild [[Brook Trout]] population. Blocking weirs limited unrecorded emigration, giving a long continuous record. The study area was 1.75 miles of stream.
- **Funding:** Dingell-Johnson Project F-27-R.

**Methods used to follow the fish**

1. [[Electrofishing]] samples in November–December 1952 and April 1953.
2. [[Modified Petersen Estimate]] population estimates by size group each fall, 1953–1957.
3. A complete [[Creel Census]] of the test stream.
4. Almost daily weir inspections, October 1952–October 1956.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream and fish community**

Hunt Creek is a brook trout stream in the north-central Lower Peninsula of Michigan. The stocked rainbow trout were superimposed on a fairly dense native brook trout population. Shetter suggests this overlap explains why some test fish tried to leave the study area in their first months after release.

**Movement**

Most rainbow trout stayed close to their release sites. In the first 6 months, 106 of 112 recaptured tagged fish (95%) were in the same 1-mile section they had been caught in during November. In April 1953, 46 of 56 recaptures (82%) were in the same section. Downstream migration was small, with 1 to 6 fish per month from October 1953 to March 1954, and a larger number in spring 1954. Of 287 rainbow trout caught by anglers in 1953–1955, all but three came from the experimental sections.

**Exploitation**

Trout had to reach the legal minimum of 7.0 inches to be harvested. Angling began in late April 1953, and anglers creeled the faster-growing fish first. This removal likely lowered the September electrofishing averages in later years.

## Key Findings

### 1. Jaw tags slowed growth; fin clips did not

- Mean total lengths at planting (October 1952) were 3.95 in (tagged), 3.75 in (fin-clipped) and 3.74 in (unmarked). Eleven months later they were 6.01, 6.33 and 6.27 in.
- Instantaneous growth rates of tagged fish were 10–21% slower than unmarked fish for comparable periods. Fin-clipped fish grew 1–5% faster.
- Tagged fish averaged about 0.5 inch less growth than unmarked and fin-clipped fish in their first 11 months.
- The author concluded that jaw tagging reduced first-year growth by about 15%, while fin clipping had no effect on growth.

### 2. Angler recovery differed among marks

Recoveries over 1953–1957 for the 2,995 fish planted:

| Group | Caught by anglers | Percent recovery |
|---|---|---|
| Tagged | 53 | 5.3% |
| Fin-clipped | 103 | 10.3% |
| Unmarked (normal) | 131 | 13.1% |

- No single-year exploitation difference was significant. The 5-year totals did differ (chi-square = 35.7, 2 df, P < 0.005).
- Tagged fish were recovered significantly less often than fin-clipped fish (chi-square = 16.42, P < 0.01). The gap between tagged and unmarked fish was larger.
- Exploitation rates for 1953–1955 ranged from 30.2% to 41.8% in 1953 and from 56.7% to 71.0% in 1954 across the three groups.

### 3. Tagged fish had higher non-fishing losses

- Losses from angling and migration totaled 7.8% (tagged), 12.6% (fin-clipped) and 17.2% (unmarked) of planted fish.
- Mortality from other causes removed 92.2%, 87.4% and 82.8% respectively.
- Chi-square tests on mortality were highly significant overall (chi-square = 39.3, 2 df, P < 0.01). Tagged fish died more than both fin-clipped (chi-square = 11.75) and unmarked fish (chi-square = 38.9). Fin-clipped fish also died more than unmarked fish (chi-square = 7.98).

### 4. Mechanisms proposed for the bias

- Slower growth kept tagged fish below the legal size longer. They were exposed to hooking loss and predation for more time before harvest.
- Some tags were probably lost to erosion or jaw growth after the first year (unpublished data of Stauffer and Hansen, cited by Shetter).

### 5. Downstream emigration showed no mark bias

- Downstream escapement from October 1953 to September 1954 was 25 tagged, 21 fin-clipped and 39 unmarked fish. This did not differ significantly from a 1:1:1 ratio (chi-square test).

### 6. Rainbow trout did not harm brook trout

- Average catch per hour of brook trout was 0.415 in 1949–1952 and 0.406 in 1953–1957 (P > 0.81), so stocking did not reduce angling success.
- Brook trout fall population estimates were, if anything, higher during 1953–1957 when rainbow trout were present. Catch-per-hour and population data are in Table 5 of the paper.
- A correlation between available rainbow trout and wild brook trout catch was not significant (r = −0.543, 3 df, P > 0.30).

### 7. Recommendations

- Marked fish remain useful, but investigators should identify possible bias and either correct for it experimentally or interpret results with it in mind.
- Shetter notes that the results agree with earlier reports on [[Brown Trout]] (Schuck, 1942) and on tagged and fin-clipped brook and rainbow trout (Cooper, 1953).
