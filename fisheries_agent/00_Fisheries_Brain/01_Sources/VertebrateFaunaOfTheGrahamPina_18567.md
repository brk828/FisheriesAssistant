# Vertebrate Fauna of the Graham (Pinaleno) Mountains, Southeastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Poeciliidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, edited by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University), summarizes summer field surveys (15 June–15 July 1966 and 8–13 July 1967) of the vertebrates of the [[Graham Mountains]]. More than 3,000 specimens were collected. About 340 vertebrate species are represented, an estimated 70–80% of the probable fauna, with the gaps mainly in birds. The fish fauna is depauperate: 17 species are recorded, only 3 of them native, and those are probably extirpated. The remaining fishes are introduced for sport fishing, bait or forage production, or mosquito control. Amphibians (11 species), reptiles (40), birds (about 170 recorded, 114 seen), and mammals are also treated. The report places the fauna in elevational zones and compares the northern affinities of the Grahams with the more Mexican fauna of the [[Chiricahua Mountains]].

## Background & Description

- The range rises to 10,713 ft from valleys generally below 4,000 ft. It is the northernmost of a chain of isolated "sky island" ranges along Arizona's eastern border.
- A base camp was set at Marijilda Canyon Campground (4,400 ft). Collecting concentrated on the east end and along the Swift Trail Road (Arizona Highway 366).
- Vegetation grades from desert grassland and mesquite/creosote, through oak-juniper-pinyon and ponderosa pine, to fir-spruce and aspen parks above 8,000 ft.
- Climate: about 13 in. of precipitation per year at the base, about 24 in. at 7,400 ft, and an estimated 35–45 in. at the peaks. Mean temperature at the base is 62° F.
- Aquatic habitats are few and highly modified:
  - Permanent streams: [[Ash Creek]], [[Grant Creek]], and [[Marijilda Creek]], all of which disappear into valley fill or are diverted for irrigation.
  - Impoundments: [[Riggs Flat Lake]] and a small pond at Snow Flat.
  - A thermal-spring "artesian belt" on the north and east sides, feeding many tanks and bait/food-fish ponds.
  - [[Roper's Lake]], maintained by groundwater pumping.
- The larger permanent streams, the [[Gila River]] and [[Aravaipa Creek]], lie far from the mountain base and are excluded from the fish accounts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish origins:** about a third of the fish species occur only under agricultural conditions or are stocked as "catchables."
- **Introduced fishes and native decline:**
  - [[Red Shiner]] introductions are invariably followed by extirpation of some native fish. Bait use and spread of such species need study and possible control.
  - [[Mosquitofish]] were rare in 1962 but by 1966 had reached all but the most isolated waters, probably through intentional introduction for mosquito control.
  - [[Green Sunfish]] overpopulate small waters and become stunted. They are often transferred by mistake.
- **Trout management:** the Arizona Game and Fish Department manages the trout waters. Unauthorized stocking continues to threaten the Apache trout program.
- **Faunal zonation:**
  - Three assemblages are recognized: desert (Lower Sonoran), rocky bajada and xeric woodland (Upper Sonoran/Transition), and montane (Transition through Boreal/Hudsonian).
  - Altitude records outside published ranges are usually explained by local conditions (cool mesic canyons, xeric south slopes), and some may reflect an "island effect."
- **Biogeography:** of 16 high-elevation species of obvious origin in the Chiricahuas (Table 3), 10 are derived from the Mexican Plateau, and only 3 of those 10 reach the Grahams. In the Grahams, 7 of 10 high-elevation forms of known affinity are northern, and 6 of those 7 also reach the Chiricahuas. Montane fauna probably arrived in Wisconsin glacial times or later.
- **Other introductions and losses:** introduced Abert's squirrels (1941–43) may have caused the decline, perhaps the extinction, of the endemic spruce squirrel (*Tamiasciurus hudsonicus grahamensis*).

## Key Findings

**Fishes (17 species; 3 native)**
- **Salmonidae**
  - [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) are regularly planted in Riggs Flat Lake and the Snow Flat pond. There is no natural reproduction.
  - [[Brown Trout]] (*S. trutta*) maintain a small naturally reproducing population in upper and middle Marijilda Creek.
  - [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) are stocked in Riggs Flat Lake and reproduce naturally in upper Marijilda Creek. Young-of-the-year were caught as low as 4,400 ft in 1966 but had disappeared by July.
  - [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*, Salt River form):
    - Stocking began in 1961, first in Grant Creek, where the fish hybridized with rainbow trout.
    - In 1965 Grant and Ash creeks were treated with an ichthyotoxin and restocked.
    - Some reproduction had occurred in both creeks, and Grant Creek was opened to angling.
    - The species survives naturally only in a few creeks above 9,000 ft in the White Mountains, in tiny high-gradient brooks where summer temperatures rarely exceed 60° F.
- **Native species**
  - [[Speckled Dace]]: a single specimen was taken in Grant Creek in 1965. The 1965 treatment probably extirpated the population.
  - Unidentified catostomids were caught in Grant Creek in 1961. They were most likely [[Gila Mountain-Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), possibly introduced inadvertently with trout.
  - [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) appears to have been extirpated from spring-fed ponds and canals east of the Grahams between 1962 and 1966, presumably through interaction with introduced *Gambusia*.
- **Other introduced species**
  - Cyprinids: [[Golden Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] are raised in artesian-belt ponds as bait and forage. One [[Red Shiner]] was seen at a commercial bait pond.
  - Ictalurids: [[Channel Catfish]] occur in commercial ponds, and [[Black Bullhead]] in a Sierra Bonita Ranch tank and stock ponds near lower Ash Creek.
  - Centrarchids: [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] are widespread below 4,500 ft. [[Black Crappie]] are known only from a pond near the lower end of Ash Creek.

**Amphibians and reptiles (11 amphibian and 40 reptile species)**
- Sixteen snake species were obtained. Stebbins (1966) places about 32 in the area's range.
- [[Tiger Salamander]]: 65 specimens from eight localities between 3,000 and 4,600 ft. Some neotenic larvae exceed 250 mm total length. Bait-trade introductions are common.
- [[Canyon Treefrog]]: more than 25 individuals could be seen in 100 yards of stream in lower Marijilda Canyon.
- Range extensions and records include:
  - Plains spadefoot, a range extension.
  - Texas horned lizard, a new Graham County record.
  - A Mexican hognose snake of 664 mm total length, a size record.
  - Spotted night snake at 8,750 ft, far above prior records of 6,400–7,000 ft.
  - Three zebra-tailed lizards exceeding the previous size record, the largest 95 mm.
- [[Twin-Spotted Rattlesnake]]: the Grahams are one of five Arizona ranges where it occurs.
- Whiptail abundance: 75, 96, and 117 southern whiptails were counted in a measured mile on three days in late June 1966.

**Birds**
- About 170 kinds were recorded, 114 of them seen in the study period (June–July). The accounts rely heavily on Phillips et al. (1964).
- Fish-related notes:
  - Great blue herons are reported as a major problem for commercial fisheries near Safford, and some operators kill them.
  - Belted kingfishers visit the fishponds.
  - Pied-billed grebes move into larger ponds in winter.
  - Roper's Lake hosted grebes, geese, and scaup.

**Mammals**
- Notable records:
  - A Mexican long-tongued bat extended the known range about 65 miles north.
  - Abert's squirrels are introduced, and the spruce squirrel was not seen in 1963–1967.
  - Cliff chipmunks reach the highest peak in the Grahams.
  - Brush mice and deer mice have mutually exclusive distributions, possibly through competition.
- Hispid cotton rats damaged fish-food stockpiles at a commercial fishery south of Safford.
- A muskrat was seen at a pond six miles south of Safford.

**Overall conclusions**
- Altitudinal anomalies should be interpreted against local habitat conditions.
- The mix of northern and Mexican biotic elements, together with the accessibility of the biota, makes the Grahams an area that should be intensively studied and preserved.
