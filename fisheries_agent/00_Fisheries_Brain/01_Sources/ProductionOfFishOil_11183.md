# Production of Fish Oil

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Clupeidae #fisheries-technology #fish-oil-processing

## Executive Summary

This 1967 U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries circular (Chapter 13 of *Fish Oils*, ed. M.E. Stansby) is a technical review of industrial methods for producing fish oil as a co-product of fish meal manufacture. Rather than presenting species ecology, the paper details the **conventional wet rendering process** (cooking, pressing, centrifugal separation, purification) and **alternative rendering methods** (dry rendering, enzymatic digestion, solvent extraction) used to process oily fish such as [[Menhaden]] and [[Sardine]] into fish meal, fish oil, and stick water. The paper is foundational to understanding mid-20th-century industrial-scale utilization of pelagic forage fish stocks for animal feed and, increasingly, human protein concentrate production.

## Background & Description

The paper distinguishes fish by oil content, a key variable in [[Fish Reduction Industry|fish reduction]] processing decisions:

- **Lean fish**: <2–2.5% fat — require minimal cooking/pressing equipment.
- **Oily fish**: >2.5% oil — require full conventional processing to reduce oil content in meal to marketable levels.

The three basic products of a conventional fish meal plant are:
1. **Fish meal** (typically retains 2–10% residual oil or more)
2. **Fish oil**
3. **Stick water** (protein/nutrient-rich process water)

Species referenced as industrially significant oil sources include the **menhaden** (heavily processed via direct steam cookers in the U.S. industry) and the **sardine** (used as the example species for a finished oil quality analysis).

Primary process stages of the conventional wet rendering method:
- **Cooking**: Direct steam injection cookers (simple, robust, used since the late 1800s, dominant in the U.S. menhaden industry, capacity up to 60 tons/hr) vs. indirect steam cookers (more fuel-efficient, capacity typically ≤20 tons/hr, more widely used internationally due to lower water dilution of stick water).
- **De-watering**: Continuous screw presses (single-screw historically dominant; multi-stage and twin-screw presses increasingly used for improved efficiency and handling of "soft" or "feeding" fish).
- **Separating**: Removal of residual solids from pressed liquor via screening and centrifuging, followed by three-phase ("sludger") nozzle centrifuges to separate oil, water, and solids.
- **Purification/Polishing**: Steam/hot-water stripping and recentrifuging to remove moisture and fine meal particles that would otherwise promote oxidative degradation and free fatty acid buildup during storage.

Alternative methods discussed:
- **Dry rendering**: Batch process combining cooking and drying in a rotary steam-jacketed cooker-drier (up to 5 tons capacity, 5–6 hr cycle), followed by hydraulic pressing; produces darker oil and meal with ~10% residual oil; more labor-intensive but flexible for irregular raw material supply.
- **Enzymatic digestion**: Mincing/homogenizing fish and treating with 0.25–0.50% enzymes (by whole-fish weight) to liberate oil for skimming/centrifuging; positioned as an emerging technique for fish protein concentrate (FPC) production.
- **Solvent extraction**: Two approaches — the azeotropic distillation-extraction **VioBin process** (using ethylene dichloride, then isopropanol for deodorization) and the **isopropanol extraction process** (developed by the Canadian Government, further studied by the U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries) — both aimed at producing low-oil, human-consumable fish protein concentrate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While the paper does not address wild population ecology directly, it reflects the industrial-scale exploitation context of pelagic oily fish stocks (notably [[Menhaden]]) that underpinned mid-20th-century U.S. and global fisheries:

- The scale of processing infrastructure described (single cookers processing up to 60 tons/hr) indicates the volume of biomass extraction associated with the [[Menhaden Fishery|menhaden reduction fishery]] at the time.
- The paper notes a growing global emphasis on utilizing "**trash fish**" (low-value/bycatch species) for high-protein fish meal and fish protein concentrate, foreshadowing expanded fisheries pressure on previously underutilized stocks.
- Water use and waste stream management (stick water, wastewater discharge) are discussed as a historical environmental consideration — noting that early reduction plants dumped process water overboard before centrifugal recovery and evaporator technology made water conservation economically necessary.
- The shift toward fish protein concentrate (FPC) production for direct human consumption represents an early example of efforts to redirect fisheries products from animal feed toward addressing global human protein needs — a theme connected to broader concerns about sustainable use of marine [[Forage Fish|forage fish]] populations.

## Key Findings

- **Oil yield principle**: Oil yield from conventional wet rendering is approximately equal to the oil content of raw material minus the oil retained in the dried meal (typically 2–10%+).
- **Cooking is critical and craft-based**: Under-cooking causes incomplete protein coagulation and poor oil/water recovery during pressing; over-cooking breaks down coagulated muscle tissue, preventing formation of a solid press core — both leading to poor oil recovery and excess oil retained in meal.
- **Steam economics**: Direct steam cooking requires ~300 lb. of steam per ton of fish to raise temperature from 15.5°C (60°F) to 90°C (195°F), introducing significant water load that became economically important once centrifugal separators and evaporators were adopted.
- **Centrifugation improves oil quality**: Compared to older settling-tank methods, modern centrifugal separation reduces oxidative degradation, yielding lighter color, lower free fatty acid content, and several additional gallons of oil recovered per ton of raw fish.
- **Sardine oil purification benchmark** (commercial finished analysis):
  - Moisture and volatiles: 0.11%
  - Insoluble impurities: 0.01%
  - Free fatty acid (as oleic): 0.45%
  — an oil of this quality resists significant deterioration during extended storage.
- **Press technology evolution**: Single-screw presses remain most common industry-wide, but multi-stage (e.g., 4-stage, 12-ft screw press) and twin-screw designs offer improved capacity and de-oiling/de-watering efficiency across a wider range of raw material conditions, including "soft" and "feeding" fish.
- **Centrifugal de-watering alternative**: Horizontal bowl centrifuges can de-water disintegrated cooked fish slurry, recovering slightly more oil than conventional pressing but leaving ~10% more water in solids than press-based methods.
- **Dry rendering trade-off**: Produces darker oil and higher-oil-content meal (~10% residual) compared to wet rendering; more labor-intensive and costly but flexible for small or irregular raw material supplies (e.g., alternated with meat scrap rendering).
- **Solvent extraction cost/quality trade-offs**: The VioBin (ethylene dichloride/isopropanol) process was, as of 1967, the only commercially operating solvent-extraction method in the U.S.; it produces both an undeodorized animal-feed-grade "fish flour" and a deodorized human-grade fish protein concentrate, though second-extraction oil is highly polymerized and cannot be combined with primary distillation oil. Isopropanol-only extraction (Canadian-developed) was estimated to be considerably more expensive.
- **Refining challenge for FPC by-product oil**: Oils recovered from enzymatic digestion and solvent extraction processes are generally inferior in initial quality (darker, more contaminated with solvents/pigments) and may require refining methods beyond those currently adequate for conventional wet-rendered fish oil.
