# Conservation Assessment for Inland Cutthroat Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This conservation assessment (USDA Forest Service General Technical Report RM-GTR-256, technical editor [[Michael K Young]]) reviews the state of scientific knowledge for five inland subspecies of [[Cutthroat Trout]] found largely on public lands in the Rocky Mountain and Intermountain West: [[Westslope Cutthroat Trout]], [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]], [[Bonneville Cutthroat Trout]], and [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]]. The subspecies were chosen because they are rare, occupy lands under many jurisdictions, and could be protected by coordinated management before further decline to extinction. Secure subspecies, those already listed under the Endangered Species Act, and those of uncertain taxonomic status were excluded.

The assessment concludes that all five subspecies have lost most of their historical range, mainly through [[Hybridization]] with introduced salmonids, habitat fragmentation and degradation, and angler harvest. Knowledge is highly uneven: the most abundant subspecies (Yellowstone and Westslope) are the best studied, while the Rio Grande and Bonneville subspecies have sparse life-history data. The authors state an implicit goal that these native fishes are worth saving and call for open, inclusive involvement of all stakeholders.

## Background & Description

The five subspecies share a common ancestor but have diverged to different degrees. One was proposed for elevation to full species status (Allendorf and Leary 1988), and another may comprise up to three genetically distinguishable groups (Shiozawa and Evans 1994). The authors avoided using data from one subspecies to interpret another where possible, though gaps in life-history knowledge made this unavoidable in some cases. Chapter length reflects the amount of available knowledge.

Each subspecies is summarized below:

- **Westslope Cutthroat Trout** occurs on both sides of the Continental Divide, mainly in Montana and Idaho, with disjunct populations in the mid-Columbia basin. It shows adfluvial, fluvial, and resident life histories.
- **Colorado River Cutthroat Trout** historically occupied the Colorado River drainage in Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, Arizona, and New Mexico. Remaining populations are mostly fluvial or resident headwater fish.
- **Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout** was once widespread in the upper Rio Grande, Canadian, and Pecos basins of New Mexico and Colorado. It is now restricted to headwater tributaries, with virtually no life-history data.
- **Bonneville Cutthroat Trout** occupied the Lake Bonneville basin (Idaho, Nevada, Utah, Wyoming). Its range is fragmented, with three possibly differentiated groups from the Bear River basin, the Bonneville basin proper, and the Snake Valley.
- **Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout** is the most abundant and widely ranging nonanadromous cutthroat subspecies. It was indigenous to the Snake River above Shoshone Falls and the Yellowstone River above the Tongue River, and it recolonized the Yellowstone drainage after glacial retreat about 12,000 years ago.

Supporting material includes a checklist of about 90 fish names (catostomids, cyprinids, cottids, salmonids, and others) and the literature cited across chapters. Those lists are summarized here rather than reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life history.** Across subspecies, life histories range from resident (completing life in tributaries) to fluvial (migrating between rivers and tributaries) to adfluvial (migrating between lakes and tributaries). Spawning generally occurs in spring or early summer, with temperature and discharge as cues. Westslope females matured at age 4 or 5 in most populations, while Bonneville males matured at age 2 and females at age 3 in Birch Creek. Yellowstone spawners in Clear Creek averaged 5.8 years of age.

**Habitat.** Spawning habitat generally consists of clean gravel in headwater reaches. Westslope spawning sites had gravel of 2 to 75 mm, mean depths of 17 to 20 cm, and mean velocities of 0.3 to 0.4 m/s. Fine sediment in substrates is linked to reduced emergence and embryo survival, though some populations persist in sediment-rich basins. Pools and cover are important for larger fish and for winter refuge.

**Thermal tolerance.** The critical thermal maximum for Westslope Cutthroat Trout was 27.1 °C, lower than that of brook trout (29.8 °C), brown trout (29.6 °C), and rainbow trout (31.6 °C). Some Yellowstone populations persist in geothermal streams at 27 °C through thermal refugia.

**Biotic interactions.** Diets are mainly invertebrates and terrestrial insects, with increasing piscivory in larger fish in some populations. Predators include bull trout, northern squawfish, birds (white pelicans and bald eagles at Yellowstone Lake), grizzly bears, and mink. Parasites include the [[Diphyllobothrium]] tapeworm in Yellowstone Lake, with infestation rates of 46 to 100%.

**Metapopulation structure.** Homing to natal streams produces partial isolation among subpopulations. The authors suggest that the spatial arrangement and connectivity of local populations influence long-term persistence, consistent with [[Metapopulation Theory]].

## Key Findings

**Distribution and range loss**
- Westslope Cutthroat Trout persists in only 27% of its historical range in Montana, and only 2.5% of its native range is genetically unaltered (Liknes 1984; Liknes and Graham 1988).
- In Idaho, populations rated "strong" (50% or more of historical potential) remain in only 11% of the historical range (Rieman and Apperson 1989).
- Bonneville Cutthroat Trout occupied 302 km of streams and 28,293 ha of lakes by 1988 (Duff 1988). Fewer than 5% of the thousands of kilometers of stream once occupied are known to be inhabited.
- Colorado River Cutthroat Trout occupied less than 1% of its historical range (Behnke 1979). Wyoming populations were limited to 40 streams and 2 lakes by 1977.
- Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout genetically unaltered populations occupy about 10% of historical stream habitat (about 2,400 km) and 85% of historical lacustrine habitat (Varley and Gresswell 1988). In Montana, only about 10% of the historical stream range still sustains genetically unaltered fish.
- Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout may have declined to 5 to 7% of its historical range (Stumpff 1992). In New Mexico, 62 pure or relatively pure populations are known, and 36 of 39 Colorado populations are rated genetic purity "A."

**Extinction risk**
- Using Dennis et al. (1991) methods, the authors estimated variance in population growth rates for Westslope populations at 0.07 to about 1.0. Stochastic risks rise quickly for populations falling below about 2,000 individuals.
- Hunt and Bjornn (1992) estimated only about 800 cutthroat trout remained in the North Fork Coeur d'Alene River.
- Extinction for many isolated populations may be "a matter of time," and the authors argue that maintaining well-connected habitat mosaics is essential.

**Causes of decline**
- Hybridization with introduced rainbow trout, nonnative cutthroat subspecies, or mixed stocks is the primary cause of decline for Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout (Allendorf and Leary 1988; Varley and Gresswell 1988).
- In Idaho, habitat loss was the primary cause of decline in 87% of Westslope reaches with depressed populations, fishing contributed in 47%, and genetic introgression and competition in 15% and 12% respectively (Rieman and Apperson 1989).
- Brook trout appear to replace cutthroat trout most often in lower-elevation, low-gradient streams. Fausch (1989) found cutthroat trout most abundant in reaches of 6 to 14% gradient and brook trout absent above about 15%, making steep reaches refuges for cutthroat.
- Water diversion, grazing, road building, culverts, and mining have fragmented or degraded habitat across the subspecies' ranges. For Colorado River Cutthroat Trout, diversion structures fragmented most streams in the North Fork Little Snake River watershed.
- Angler harvest is a major threat. Yellowstone Lake harvest rose through the 1950s until landing rates and average fish size reached unprecedented lows by 1968, even under a three-fish creel limit. Catch-and-release and size-limit regulations have since increased mean length in several populations.

**Management status and actions**
- Westslope Cutthroat Trout is a sensitive species in USDA Forest Service Regions 1 and 4 and a species of special concern in Idaho.
- Colorado River Cutthroat Trout is a Category 2 species under the USDI Fish and Wildlife Service and a sensitive species in Regions 2 and 4 of the Forest Service.
- Bonneville Cutthroat Trout is a Category 2 species, with state conservation plans in Idaho, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming.
- Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout is a Species of Special Concern-Class A (American Fisheries Society). Under the interagency guide for the Yellowstone River basin (YCTWG 1994), populations with at least 97% genetic purity are protected or restored.
- Common management tools include catch-and-release and slot limits, barrier installation, nonnative removal with piscicides (for example, brook trout removal from Arnica Creek), culvert modification, and broodstock programs.

**Research needs**
- Basic life-history information (age at maturity, fecundity, repeat spawning, and seasonal movements) is lacking for most populations of Colorado River and Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout.
- The authors call for studies of the competitive and predatory interactions between nonnative salmonids and native cutthroat trout, which have been hypothesized but rarely demonstrated.
- Historical and current range assessments, population trend monitoring, and reserve design for small, fragmented populations are needed.
- Research should examine life-history diversity and its genetic basis, since the authors consider loss of unique local populations the greatest threat to genetic variability.

**Overall conclusion**
The authors argue that these native fishes require coordinated, progressive management, and that open and honest involvement of agencies, interest groups, and the public is necessary. They emphasize that "a shared vision is the only insurance for a future for these subspecies."
