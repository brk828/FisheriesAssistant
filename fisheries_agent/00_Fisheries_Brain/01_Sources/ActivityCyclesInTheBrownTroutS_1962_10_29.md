## Executive Summary
This 1962 study by D. R. Swift, conducted for the Freshwater Biological Association, investigates the annual and diurnal (daily) swimming activity cycles of naturally-feeding wild [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*). Four wild trout (18–20 cm) were confined individually in netted cages on the bed of Windermere lake, relying solely on food organisms passing through the netting. Fish movement was recorded electronically via paired electrodes. The study found a consistent annual activity peak in May–June and a distinct diurnal pattern characterized by a sharp increase in activity at dawn, with higher activity during daylight hours compared to darkness. The paper discusses these patterns in the context of feeding behavior and possible environmental drivers (light and water temperature), though a definitive causal relationship was not established.

## Background & Description
- **Specimens:** Six wild *Salmo trutta* captured from [[Windermere]].
- **Size:** 18 to 20 cm in length.
- **Condition:** Fish confined for extended periods (Fish 3 and 5, released in November) showed continued growth in length but were noted to be in **poor condition**, suggesting inadequate food intake via passive netting capture during captivity.
- No specific morphometric or meristic data (fin rays, scale counts) were reported; the focus was purely behavioral/physiological (activity levels).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** Lentic freshwater lake environment ([[Windermere]], UK), specifically the benthic zone (lake bed) at 6–8 feet depth.
- **Feeding Strategy:** Fish were passive planktivores/opportunistic feeders in this experimental context, dependent entirely on invertebrate/organism drift passing through cage netting (no artificial feeding in this trial).
- **Trophic Interactions:** The study references comparative diurnal feeding rhythms in other species to contextualize trout behavior, including:
    - [[Goldfish]] (Hirata & Kobayashi, 1956; Hirata, 1957) — daylight feeding correlated with peak water temperature.
    - [[Baikal Grayling]] fry (Oliphan) — bimodal peaks (morning/evening).
    - [[Roach]], [[Bleak]], [[Prussian Carp]], [[Tench]], [[Perch]], [[Ruff]], and [[Burbot]] fry (Kuchin, 1900) — similar rhythmic feeding.
    - [[Volga Herring]] (Sushkina, 1939) and Danish herring (Musinic, 1931).
- **Predation/Light Response:** Discussion references [[Sockeye Salmon]] smolts exhibiting negative phototaxis (Hoar, 1955), raising the hypothesis that dawn activity increases might partly reflect a light-avoidance/escape mechanism rather than purely a feeding response.
- **Abiotic Drivers:** Water temperature appears correlated with nighttime activity levels, rising from January to August then declining, mirroring the temperature curve (see [[Water Temperature and Fish Activity]]).

## Key Findings
1.  **Annual Activity Cycle:** All four trout (Fish 1, 2, 3, 5) displayed minimal activity during winter months and a pronounced maximum activity peak during **May and June**.
    - Maximum weekly average of ~1000 passages (combined electrodes) for Fish 1, 3, and 5.
    - Fish 2 was markedly more active, reaching a weekly average of ~400 passages per day.
    - **Fish 3** uniquely displayed a **second activity peak in autumn**, driven largely by increased dawn/early-morning activity.
2.  **Diurnal Activity Pattern:** For most of the year, trout were significantly more active during **daylight hours** than during darkness.
    - Activity exhibited a **sharp, characteristic rise at dawn** each day.
    - This pattern held from January through June for both Fish 1 and Fish 3.
3.  **Summer Anomaly (Fish 1):** During July and August, Fish 1's activity dropped to a uniformly low level throughout the entire day, with only a slight increase noted during darkness hours.
4.  **Summer Anomaly (Fish 3):** During July, Fish 3 maintained a constantly high activity level throughout the entire day/night cycle (loss of the typical day/night distinction), before reverting to the standard daylight-active pattern from August–November.
5.  **Temperature Correlation:** Nighttime activity level correlated with seasonal water temperature trends — increasing steadily from January to August, then decreasing through the rest of the year (see [[Fig. 4 Temperature vs Activity]]). The author notes this is correlative, not necessarily causative.
6.  **Mortality/Attrition:** Fish 2 died at the end of September; Fish 1 escaped around the same time. Fish 3 and 5 were released at the end of November in poor bodily condition despite length growth, indicating chronic food limitation under the passive-feeding cage protocol.
7.  **Cross-Species Consistency:** The dawn activity spike aligns with feeding-activity patterns documented in numerous other freshwater and marine fish species, suggesting a broadly conserved [[Diurnal Feeding Rhythm]] across teleosts, though the exact stimulus (light vs. food availability vs. predator avoidance) remains undetermined.
8.  **Future Work Proposed:** The author indicates a follow-up experiment using artificially-fed trout to isolate the relationship between feeding regime and activity cycles.
