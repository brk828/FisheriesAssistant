# Mitochondrial DNA Variation in Razorback Sucker and the Phylogenetic Position of Uraeotyphlidae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from *Copeia* (1996, No. 3, pp. 542–550 and following) contains two papers. The main study, by [[Dowling]] and colleagues, examines [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) variation in the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Using [[Restriction Fragment Analysis]] of 143 specimens, the authors found high within-population diversity but little genetic differentiation among populations. They concluded that remnant populations have been connected by historical gene flow and recommended managing the species as a single breeding unit. A second paper by Wilkinson and Nussbaum examines the phylogenetic placement of the caecilian family [[Uraeotyphlidae]] using cardiovascular and other morphological characters. Its findings support a sister-group relationship with [[Ichthyophiidae]].

## Background & Description

**Razorback Sucker study.** The razorback sucker is a large catostomid (longer than 500 mm SL) that can live more than 50 years. It historically occupied large rivers and floodplain habitats across the Colorado River drainage, including the Gila River basin. Human impacts such as flow modification and introduced exotic species have extirpated it from much of its range. The species is listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. The authors describe the species as a useful model for gene-flow studies because western North American fishes were repeatedly isolated and reconnected by Tertiary tectonism and climate shifts.

**Uraeotyphlidae study.** The genus *Uraeotyphlus* comprises four species from Kerala, India. Earlier classifications placed it in Caeciliidae, but cranial and cladistic studies raised doubts about that placement. The authors reanalyzed its relationships using a combined data set of new and literature-derived characters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Population sizes:** The largest remaining razorback sucker populations are in the upper Green River basin (estimated at fewer than 1,000 adults) and [[Lake Mohave]] (fewer than 25,000). Natural populations elsewhere, including the Gila River basin, are extirpated or too small to estimate.
- **Sampling localities:** Tissues came from [[Lake Mohave]] (n = 49), [[Lake Mead]] (n = 15), [[Lake Powell]] (n = 23), the Green–Yampa rivers (count not legible in the scan), and the [[Upper Colorado River]] near Grand Junction, CO (n = 29). Twelve putative [[Hybridization|hybrids]] with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] came from near the confluence of the [[Little Colorado River]] and Colorado River, AZ.
- **Climate history:** The last glacial maximum peaked about 18 ka and ended about 10 ka. Warm-season air temperatures on the Colorado Plateau were about 6.3 °C cooler than today. Razorback suckers and related big-river fishes spawn and grow poorly when water temperatures fall well below 20 °C, so upper-basin reaches were likely thermally unsuitable during glaciation.
- **Reservoir effects:** Mainstem reservoirs were built between 1935 and 1962. Cold hypolimnetic releases from dams limit reproduction of big-river fishes downstream.

## Key Findings

### Razorback Sucker mtDNA study
- **Methods:** mtDNA was digested with eight restriction endonucleases. The analysis yielded 182 fragments and 38 composite haplotypes from 143 specimens. Hybrids and partial profiles were analyzed separately.
- **Within-population diversity:** [[Haplotype Diversity]] (h) was highest in Lake Mohave (0.98) and lowest in the upper Colorado River (0.00). Intermediate samples ranged from 0.73 to 0.82. Nucleotide diversity (π) was 0.67 in Lake Mohave, the highest value, and 0.04 in the upper Colorado River, and it declined progressively upstream.
- **Among-population structure:** Tests of geographic homogeneity were significant (χ² = 239.5 for 4+6 bp haplotypes, versus a simulated maximum of 194.5; χ² = 104.7 for 6 bp haplotypes, versus 63.5). The authors attribute this to a small amount of biologically meaningful divergence. About 23% of mtDNA variation lay among samples, falling to about 11% when the upper Colorado River sample was excluded. Jackknife tests of population subdivision (Φ-type statistic) did not differ significantly from zero.
- **Divergence:** Average sequence divergence was 0.00447 per nucleotide. Only about 22% (0.00097) was among populations, and the remainder (0.0035) was within populations. The most divergent haplotypes differed by only 0.7%.
- **Phylogeography:** More than 1,000 most-parsimonious trees were generated, and no phylogeographic structure appeared in the consensus. [[Haplotype Network]]s showed no geographic partitioning.
- **Hybridization:** Only 3 of 148 morphologically identified razorback suckers carried [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] mtDNA, indicating introgression. Five of 12 Little Colorado River fish carried flannelmouth mtDNA, suggesting that hybridization is more common there. Little Colorado River fish were excluded from the main analyses.
- **Sampling caveat:** The upper Colorado River sample came mostly from two floodplain ponds, possibly derived from as few as four females. Otolith data indicated most fish were from the 1983 year class, which matched a flood year.
- **Age and origin:** Lake Mohave fish were mostly older than 40 years and probably reflect the basin-wide population before dam construction. Fish from the Little Colorado River and ponds were younger than 15 years.
- **Interpretation:** The authors favored a "recolonization" scenario, in which lower-basin populations served as a source for upstream colonization after glaciation. Serial bottlenecks and reduced effective population size at the periphery could explain the clinal decline in diversity. The alternative "sink" hypothesis was rejected because it would require divergent upstream populations not seen in the data.
- **Conservation recommendation:** Because historical gene exchange was sufficient to prevent population divergence, the authors recommend treating the razorback sucker as a single breeding unit throughout its range. They note that most remnant populations lack enough brood fish to maintain discrete stocks.

### Uraeotyphlidae phylogeny study
- **Question:** The authors reexamined whether *Uraeotyphlus* belongs to Caeciliidae or represents a separate lineage.
- **Data:** Nussbaum's earlier analysis used 43 characters scored for 13 caecilian genera. The new study combined new characters with literature data.
- **Results:** Two derived cardiovascular features are shared by Uraeotyphlidae and Ichthyophiidae. Parsimony and compatibility analyses support Uraeotyphlidae as sister to Ichthyophiidae, rather than to the higher clade containing Caeciliidae, Scolecomorphidae, and Typhlonectidae.
