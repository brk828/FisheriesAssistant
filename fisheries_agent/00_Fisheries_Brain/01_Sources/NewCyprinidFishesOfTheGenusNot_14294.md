# New Cyprinid Fishes of the Genus Notropis from Texas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper formally describes three previously unnamed shiners of the genus [[Notropis]] from eastern Texas: *Notropis oxyrhynchus* (Sharpnose Shiner), *N. brazosensis* (Brazos Shiner) and *N. potteri* (Chub Shiner). The authors, [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Kelshaw Bonham]], state that the species had been withheld from publication for too long and that further delay would interfere with other ichthyologists and fishery biologists. The species are diagnosed using meristic counts (fin and scale counts, pharyngeal teeth) and morphometric measurements expressed in thousandths of standard length, plus detailed comparisons with closely related forms. The authors conclude that *oxyrhynchus* and *potteri* are native only to the [[Brazos River]] system, that *brazosensis* also occurs in adjacent coastal rivers, and that the *potteri* population in the [[Red River]] system most likely resulted from escaped bait minnows.

## Background & Description

The paper opens by noting that the freshwater fish fauna of eastern North America has grown through many new discoveries, many of which have not been formally named. Texas fishes had been little studied since the compilation by [[Evermann and Kendall]] (1894). The three species are not known from surveys of surrounding regions, including northeastern Mexico, New Mexico, Oklahoma and Louisiana.

**Methods.** Counts and measurements followed the standards of [[Hubbs and Lagler]] (1941, 1947), angles were measured as recommended by Hubbs (1946), and photographs and calculations were made by Bonham. Type specimens are deposited in the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]] and the Agricultural and Mechanical College of Texas.

**Notropis oxyrhynchus (Sharpnose Shiner).** The authors regard this species as the southern representative of [[Notropis Percobromus]]. It shares with that species an anteriorly deep body, sharp nose and large mouth, but is placed in the *atherinoides* series rather than the *rubella* series of the subgenus. Its defining feature is a very sharp muzzle: the front angle of the top of the head is 15° to 17° (versus 19° to 23° in *N. percobromus*), and the muzzle angle is 56° to 70° (versus 72° to 85°). Head length averages 276 per thousand standard length (range 246 to 306). The type is the holotype, an adult 50.8 mm standard length, from Brazos River at Wellborn Crossing, Brazos County, Texas, collected 21 October 1938.

**Notropis brazosensis (Brazos Shiner).** This species is considered the southwestern representative of [[Notropis Illecebrosa]]. It usually has 8 anal rays rather than the 9 typical of *illecebrosa*. Other differences include mouth structure, pharyngeal arch and teeth, and the shape of the lips. The holotype is an adult 49.2 mm standard length from the same Wellborn Crossing locality, collected 21 October 1938. The 24 paratypes measured ranged from 33.5 to 61.5 mm standard length.

**Notropis potteri (Chub Shiner).** This is a robust species with a broad head, thick muzzle and large mouth, which gives it a superficial resemblance to the [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*). The authors place it closest to [[Notropis Blennius]], but its distinctions justify full species status. The holotype is an adult 45.3 mm standard length, seined from Waco Creek, McLennan County, Texas, on 21 June 1938. The species is named in honor of [[George E. Potter]], who collected the first specimens. Potter had published a figure under the name *N. potteri* in 1938, but this did not meet the availability requirements of the [[International Rules of Zoological Nomenclature]] (Article 25, Item C), so the species is formally described here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat preference.** All three species are associated with silty water. *Oxyrhynchus* and *brazosensis* "abound" in the very silty water of the Brazos River and its main tributaries, and the authors state that these two commonly dominate the local fauna. The body form, mouth position, eye position and coloration of these shiners all indicate a mid-water or near-surface swimming habit.

**Distribution.**
- *N. oxyrhynchus* and *N. potteri* appear to be native only to the Brazos River system.
- *N. potteri* also occurs in some smaller and less turbid waters of the Brazos system.
- *N. brazosensis* extends into adjacent coastal rivers, including the San Bernard and Colorado rivers. A single doubtful specimen from Harris County, collected 18 May 1941, has only 7 anal rays. The species was not found in the Neches River system during recent collecting by Clark Hubbs.

**Introduction of *N. potteri* to the Red River.** The *potteri* population in [[Lake Texoma]] and the Red River was judged to be the result of escaped bait minnows. The authors re-examined more than 1,800 specimens from 1926 Red River collections, along with smaller series from 1925, 1929 and 1932, and found no *potteri*. The species was found in 1948 and 1949 at Lake Texoma (a heavily fished artificial lake) and in the Red River. The Red River and Brazos specimens showed no notable morphological difference, although the authors note that the measurements were made by different people and that specimen sizes differed.

**Survey gaps.** The authors call for more material from the region north of the mouth of the Brazos River, not only of these species but of the fish fauna generally.

## Key Findings

**Diagnostic counts and measurements**

| Species | Key diagnostic traits (from text and tables) |
|---|---|
| *N. oxyrhynchus* | Very sharp muzzle; snout about 29% longer than the eye in adults; head depth about 1.7 times in head length; mouth angle 32° to 38° |
| *N. brazosensis* | Typically 8 anal rays; fins fairly pointed and expansive; pale silvery body with a dusky lateral streak |
| *N. potteri* | Pharyngeal teeth 2,4–4,2 (second row of two teeth consistently present); dorsal rays consistently 8; pelvic rays typically 8; very thick, blunt muzzle |

**Anal ray counts (Table I, mean and sample size)**
- *N. percobromus*: 10.69 (n = 90)
- *N. oxyrhynchus*: The table lists 10.69 (n = 82), but the text states the average is 10.09. This discrepancy is in the source.
- *N. brazosensis*: 8.04 (n = 106)
- *N. illecebrosa*: 8.87 (n = 48)
- *N. potteri*: 7.02 (n = 85)

**Selected morphometric results**
- *N. potteri* body depth: 3.7 to 4.8 times in standard length (average 4.26, n = 40)
- *N. potteri* head length: 3.3 to 3.8 times in standard length (average 3.56, n = 61)
- *N. potteri* eye length: 4.2 to 5.5 times in head length (average 4.72)
- *N. potteri* opercle length: 2.2 to 2.7 times in the distance from snout to preopercular margin
- *N. oxyrhynchus* mouth angle: 32° to 38°, compared with about 48° or more in *N. percobromus*

**Taxonomic conclusions**
- *N. oxyrhynchus* is the southwestern counterpart of *N. percobromus*. Its closest affinities lie with the *atherinoides* group.
- *N. brazosensis* represents the southwestern form of *N. illecebrosa*, and the differences are considered more than subspecific.
- *N. potteri* resembles [[Notropis Sabinae]], [[Notropis Bairdi]] and [[Notropis Girardi]], but its nearest relative is *N. blennius*, which is split into two subspecies: *N. b. blennius* and *N. b. jejunus*. Even the subspecific split is described as tentative.

**Summary of distribution**
- The Brazos River system holds *oxyrhynchus* and *potteri* as native species, with *brazosensis* also occurring in lower adjacent river systems.
- The *potteri* population in the Red River is most likely the result of bait-minnow introduction.
