# Humpback Chub Population Estimate in Desolation and Gray Canyons, Green River, Utah, 2001–2003

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Utah Division of Wildlife Resources final report (September 2005), prepared for the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Endangered Fish Recovery Program]], estimates the adult population of [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in [[Desolation Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]] on the [[Green River]] for 2001–2003. Abundance was estimated with multiple-pass [[Capture-Recapture]] sampling at 12 sites, four of which are long-term trend sites monitored annually since 1989.

Adult point estimates (≥200 mm) were 1,254 (2001), 2,612 (2002) and 937 (2003). The authors caution that these estimates are preliminary because sampling moved from summer to fall in 2003, low flows reduced sampling area and mixing, and the 2002 [[Rattle Complex Fire]] fish kill and a rise in [[Smallmouth Bass]] may have affected the population. Juvenile numbers were too low for mark-recapture estimates. First-year adult estimates were derived from the 200–220 mm size class. Catch rates at long-term sites showed no significant trend since 1989. The study is the first sampling period after the 2002 amended recovery goals and is intended to refine the design before sampling resumes in fall 2006–2008.

## Background & Description

*Gila cypha* is a large-bodied [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]] endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It evolved in seasonally warm, turbid water under unregulated hydrology and is a canyon dweller of swift, deep water over rocky substrate. The document estimates the species now occupies about 68% of its original range. Declines are attributed to stream alteration (dams, irrigation, dewatering, channelization), habitat modification, predation and competition from nonnative fishes, parasitism, hybridization with other *Gila* spp., and pesticides and pollutants.

Humpback chub were first reported in Desolation and Gray Canyons in 1975, from surveys conducted 1967–1971. The Utah Division of Wildlife Resources has monitored the population since 1985. Six self-sustaining populations exist: one in the Grand Canyon (lower basin) and five in the upper basin. The Desolation/Gray population is considered the third largest in the upper basin, after Black Rocks and Westwater.

The species is protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The 1990 [[Humpback Chub Recovery Plan]] was amended in 2002. Downlisting requires, over five years: (1) no significant decline in adult point estimates for each of six populations; (2) recruitment of age-3 (150–199 mm) fish at least equal to mean annual adult mortality; (3) two core populations each above 2,100 adults; and (4) site-specific threat management. Delisting adds three viable core populations over three years. This study addresses only criterion 1 for downlisting. The Desolation/Gray population could be a third core population and so matters for delisting.

The objectives were to (1) estimate late juvenile and adult abundance in Desolation/Gray, and (2) test whether [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP) catch rates relate to population size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The reach runs from Sand Wash (RM 216) to about 12 river miles upstream of the town of Green River (RM 120). Desolation Canyon is carved from the Wasatch and Green River formations. Reach RM 216–186 (Sand Wash to Cedar Ridge Canyon) is mostly slow, flat water. Below that the canyon is more confined and steeper. Gray Canyon starts below Three Fords Rapid (RM 156) and ends at Swazey's Rapid (RM 132). Twelve sites were sampled, including long-term trend sites at RM 185, 174.4, 160.4 and 145.7. Four "wild card" sites were alternatives from earlier ISMP monitoring.

**Flows and temperature.** Flows were measured at USGS gage #09315000 (Green River, Utah).

| Year | Pass flows (cfs) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 2001 | 5,699–1,620 across three passes | Sampled on the descending hydrograph, June–July |
| 2002 | 5,600–1,810 (two passes) | Third pass cancelled; flows below 1,800 cfs damaged sport boats |
| 2003 | 1,050–1,470 | Fall sampling; no sport boats used because of low flow |

Mean main-channel water temperatures were 23.0, 19.0 and 11.5 °C across the three fall 2003 passes. Flows in 2001–2003 were the lowest in 100 years of record, roughly half the long-term monthly means.

**Habitat and sampling coverage.** Roughly 7% of the canyons was sampled, assuming about ⅓ mile per site. Large sections are low-gradient, slow runs that humpback chub rarely use. Some reaches are logistically inaccessible. Low flows reduced deep eddies, the habitat humpback chub generally prefer, and created shallow pools.

**Other stressors.** The [[Rattle Complex Fire]] burned more than 94,600 acres in the East Willow and Willow Creek drainages (Book Cliffs) in July 2002. Dead fish were reported along the Desolation/Gray shoreline after heavy early-September rain. Biologists found no humpback chub at Nefertiti Rapid (RM 140). Smallmouth bass catches rose from 9 (2001) and 13 (2002) to 46 (2003). Predation was not directly observed, but only young-of-year and juvenile chub were likely vulnerable.

**Bycatch.** [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] were still taken in trammel nets after the 2003 move to fall. Those caught in fall were smaller and recovered more readily. Some chub and pikeminnow suffered opercle damage from gilling. Most were revived in fresh water for up to 30 minutes, likely helped by cooler water.

## Key Findings

**1. Adult population estimates (≥200 mm, Darroch Mt model, Program CAPTURE)**

| Year | N | CV | Profile likelihood interval | p-hat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2001 | 1,254 | 0.31 | 733–2,697 | 0.053 |
| 2002 | 2,612 | 0.36 | 1,477–8,509 | 0.045 |
| 2003 | 937 | 0.21 | 636–1,520 | 0.083 |

Precision was poor in 2001 and 2002 and best in 2003. The 2003 estimate was the lowest, and the authors suggest this may reflect the seasonal change in capture efficiency rather than a real decline. The three-year average is close to the preliminary ~1,500 adults reported by USFWS (2002).

**2. Model comparison.** Program CAPTURE selection tools perform poorly with sparse data, so the Darroch Mt model was used throughout. Alternatives (Mo, Chao Mt) gave similar results. For 2003, Mo gave 945 versus 937 for Darroch Mt. A Lincoln-Petersen estimate using passes 1 and 3 gave 997 (SE 116; 95% CI 273–1,720). The authors read this as evidence that fish mixed adequately across the three passes, though proportions of second-pass marks recaptured on pass 3 suggest limited mixing.

**3. First-year adults (200–220 mm), 95% CI**
- 2001: 163 (149–192)
- 2002: 297 (275–343)
- 2003: 134 (123–159)

**4. Juveniles.** Juveniles were too scarce for mark-recapture estimates: 5 (2001), 24 (2002) and 4 (2003) collected by all methods. The authors recommend continued electrofishing, hoop nets, minnow traps and smaller-mesh (⅝″ or ¾″) inner-mesh trammel nets.

**5. Sampling effort and catch**

| Year | Trammel net hours | Humpback chub caught (trammel) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2001 | 2,803 | 214 (4 juveniles) | Electrofishing on one pass, upper six sites |
| 2002 | 2,009 | 239 (16 juveniles) | Electrofishing and hoop nets added |
| 2003 | 3,042 | 236 (3 juveniles) | 15% of chub recaptured within the same pass and site |

Trammel-net CPUE dropped significantly across passes in 2001 (p < 0.001), rose non-significantly in 2002 (p = 0.316), and rose significantly in 2003 (p = 0.004).

**6. Long-term catch rates (ISMP sites).** Catch rates at the four long-term sites varied significantly from 1985 to 2003. The regression was not significantly different from zero (r² = 0.020, p = 0.58). Removing the 1989 outlier left a slight, still non-significant increase (r² = 0.078, p = 0.29). The highest CPUE was in 1989 and the lowest in 1996. The 2001–2003 catch rates fell within the pre-study range.

**7. Catch rate versus population estimate.** A positive but non-significant relationship was found between 2001–2003 catch rates and population estimates (r = 0.868, p = 0.32). Single-pass ISMP data may not reflect annual abundance.

**8. Growth and movement.** Twenty-five recaptured chub grew an average of 10.8 mm per year (SD 9.3, range 0–31), similar to other upper-basin populations. Most recaptures stayed within a mile of their original capture site. Movements of 7–26 miles, both upstream and downstream, were recorded across years.

**9. Length structure.** Average total length of adult chub was 261 mm (2001), 239 mm (2002, all chub) and 256 mm (2003). Length-frequency analysis of ISMP data (1989–2000) showed shifts in size structure and some years with no apparent recruits. The 2001–2003 histograms were unimodal in 2001 and 2003, with juveniles present in 2002.

**10. Species identification.** Chub identification in Desolation/Gray has been difficult. Morphological characters (per Douglas et al. 1989 and 1998) do not clearly separate humpback chub from [[Roundtail Chub]]. Because [[Bonytail]] are being stocked upstream, the authors recommend either a new discriminant function or full morphometric measurements. They also recommend fin clips for genetic verification.

**11. Recommendations**
- Continue fall sampling in Desolation/Gray. Lower temperatures appear to reduce stress on fish.
- Use at least three passes because environmental conditions affect catch rates.
- Substitute the Surprise Canyon site (RM 176) for Big Bend (RM 153.5), where chub numbers were low.
- Add sites or expand concentration-site sampling to improve recapture rates.
- Use consistent gear and effort across the 2006–2008 sampling.
- Sample electrofishing in the evening (crepuscular period) when fish are most active.
