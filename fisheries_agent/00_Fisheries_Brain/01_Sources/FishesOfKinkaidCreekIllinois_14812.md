## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by Jerry L. Stegman surveyed the fish fauna of [[Kinkaid Creek]], a 22-mile tributary of the [[Big Muddy River]] in Jackson County, Illinois. The primary goals were to determine species composition and assess the creek's potential as a fisheries resource, supported by physico-chemical monitoring, food-habit (stomach/intestinal) analysis, and gonadal examination for spawning timing. A total of **38 species (representing 88 total species/hybrid entries in the broader survey table) and one hybrid cross** were documented across five sampling stations, with **1,258 fish examined** for diet. The study found low predation pressure, minimal interspecific food competition, and a fish community dominated by [[Cyprinidae|Cyprinids]] (minnows/shiners) rather than sport fishes. The author concluded that Kinkaid Creek's primary fisheries value lay in its **bait fish population**, since sport fish (bass, crappie, catfish) were poorly established, largely due to habitat instability, flooding, and lack of large impoundments.

## Background & Description

**Study Area:** Kinkaid Creek drains 52 square miles with an average gradient of 11 ft/mile, flowing through a narrow, steep-walled valley (rarely exceeding ¼ mile wide). Approximately 60% of the drainage basin is forested, with the remainder split between pasture and intertilled cropland.

The creek is divided into three geomorphically distinct reaches:
- **Lower reach** (near confluence with [[Big Muddy River]]): Deep (up to 25 ft), steep-banked, sand-bottomed when not silt-covered; subject to **backwater flooding** from the Big Muddy River, which can raise water levels up to 19 ft and even reverse stream flow, depositing and later "flushing" silt layers.
- **Middle reach**: Steep-walled valley, stream bed up to 20 ft deep, sand/gravel/bedrock substrate, well-developed pools (10–25 ft wide, up to 400 ft long) and riffles (10–75 ft long); water level fluctuation up to 15 ft.
- **Headwaters**: Moderately rugged, timbered hills; sharply meandering channel with vertical banks and fallen trees; smaller pool-riffle sequences (pools 8–15 ft wide, up to 40 ft; riffles up to 40 ft); water level fluctuation up to 16 ft.

**Sampling Design:** Five stations (I–V) were established — three on the main channel, two on tributaries — selected to represent varying habitat types. Each collection site comprised a pool and its associated riffle. Sampling ran **February 18 – July 4, 1958**, with three collection events per station (early March to late June).

**Collection Gear:**
- 12×4-ft knotted minnow seine (0.25-inch mesh) — used at all stations except I (too deep/snaggy)
- D.C. 180-volt, 250-watt portable electrical shocker — effective in clear water
- Fyke and hoop nets — used at Stations I and IV (deep water, snags, vertical banks)
- Sodium cyanide (1 p.p.m.) — used as a single-collection poison method at all stations except I in late June; a novel application of this technique (previously limited to farm ponds) to stream collecting
- At Station IV, an earthen dam and canvas divider isolated a still pool for poisoning

**Water Chemistry Methods:** Field pH via Taylor kit; dissolved oxygen via Winkler method (Welch 1948); turbidity via Hellige turbidimeter; stream flow via Gurley Model 622-E flow meter.

**Biological Sampling:** Plankton collected by filtering 20 L of pool surface water through a plankton net, examined via Sedgewick-Rafter counting cell. Benthic invertebrates sampled from shallow riffles using a Surber bottom sampler (deep pools proved unproductive due to hard sand substrate).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physico-chemical conditions (April–July 1958):**
- Water temperature: 58–85°F, tracking air temperature
- pH: fairly stable, 7.2–8.0 across all stations
- Dissolved oxygen: 5.1–9.3 p.p.m. — within the range considered adequate for fish (per [[Moore 1942]])
- Turbidity: moderate but highly variable with rainfall-driven water level changes; most extreme turbidity occurred in upper and lower reaches; the stream showed rapid post-storm clearing
- Flow: highly seasonal — e.g., middle-reach flow dropped from 10.8 cfs (mid-April) to 1.0 cfs (early July); headwater flow dropped from 3.5 cfs to 0.2 cfs over the same period

**Predator-Prey Dynamics:** Predation was generally low, attributed to the scarcity of large predatory fish. Principal predators identified were [[Crappie]] species, [[Green Sunfish]], [[Longear Sunfish]], and [[Creek Chub]]. This pattern paralleled findings by [[Starrett 1950]] in Iowa's Des Moines River, where predation was low in spring/summer but increased in fall as invertebrate prey declined — a trend the author expected to be even more pronounced in Kinkaid Creek due to seasonal flow contraction concentrating fish into smaller habitats.

**Interspecific Competition:** Following [[Lagler 1944]]'s reasoning that shared food resources do not necessarily indicate harmful competition if food is abundant, the author concluded that harmful interspecies competition for food was not significant among Kinkaid Creek fishes — supported by low rates of empty stomachs and low predation. No clear correlation was found between riffle invertebrate abundance and fish diet composition, suggesting substantial allochthonous food inputs (wind-blown terrestrial insects, streamside vegetation drop, watershed runoff) not directly sampled in this study.

**Plankton Dynamics:** Plankton density peaked in July: 73, 538, and 33 plankters/liter at Stations I, IV, and V respectively. Overall plankton counts were considered low, likely due to the flushing effect of spring rains — consistent with [[Shelford and Eddy 1929]], who found "new" water in Illinois' Sangamon River contained almost no plankton after nine days. Per [[Bajkov 1932]], plankton scarcity is a leading cause of fish fry mortality, implying that **larval fish competing for planktonic food** may represent the most ecologically significant competitive interaction in the system.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness:** 38 species plus one hybrid documented; dominant taxa were cyprinids (minnows/shiners).
- **Most abundant species:** [[Bluntnose Minnow]] (*Pimephales notatus*) — 27.8% of total catch; primarily a bottom feeder on dipterous larvae, ooze, and plant material; spawns after June.
- **Other abundant cyprinids:** [[Sand Shiner]] (12.0%), [[Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum*, 11.3%), [[Creek Chub]] (9.3%), [[Red Shiner]] (7.1%), [[Fantail Darter]] (6.6%).
- **Rare/incidental captures:** Single specimens of [[Longnose Gar]], [[Shortnose Gar]], and [[Bowfin]] taken only at Station I during flood stage — indicating these species enter the creek opportunistically via backwater connection with the Big Muddy River rather than maintaining resident populations.
- **Sport fish scarcity:** [[Channel Catfish]] (0.2%) and [[Spotted Bass]] (single sub-adults at two stations) were poorly established despite seemingly favorable habitat. [[White Crappie]] (0.8%) likely represents seasonal migrants during high water rather than a resident population.
- **Most abundant sport fish:** [[Green Sunfish]] (3.0%), whose diet was dominated by crustaceans, terrestrial insects, and small cyprinids; peak spawning in late June.
- **Novel methodology:** First documented stream (as opposed to pond) application of sodium cyanide poisoning as a collection technique (per [[Bridges 1958]]), proven effective when paired with downstream seining of disabled fish.
- **Species range note:** The author suggests the [[Orangethroat Darter]] (*Etheostoma spectabile*) may have been previously overlooked or misidentified as [[Rainbow Darter]] (*Etheostoma caeruleum*) in prior southern Illinois surveys — a potential distributional correction.
- **Spawning phenology:** Diverse timing was observed across species — e.g., [[White Sucker]] and [[Creek Chubsucker]] spawn April–May; [[Stoneroller]] and [[Silverjaw Minnow]] peak in May; [[Bluntnose Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], and [[Redfin Shiner]] spawn later (June–July); darters (Orangethroat, Fantail, Johnny) generally spawn late April through early June.
- **Management conclusion:** Due to lack of access roads, chronic flooding, and absence of desirable sport species, active fisheries management was deemed impractical without large-scale impoundment. The creek's principal fisheries value was identified as its **bait fish population**, concentrated at Stations II, III, and V.
- **Prior impoundment proposals:** The [[Department of Registration and Education 1957]] report had already identified Kinkaid Creek as a candidate site for a water-resource impoundment project, which the author endorses as the key step toward improving sport fish habitat.
