# Effects of Agricultural Drainage Development on Benthic Invertebrates in Undisturbed Downstream Reaches

#ichthyology #marine-biology #benthic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This study compares benthic macroinvertebrate assemblages in four southwestern Minnesota streams: two "natural" streams with little or no drainage modification and two streams whose watersheds were heavily modified by [[Agricultural Drainage]] (channelization, tiling, ditching). The authors, Paul C. Marsh and Thomas F. Waters of the Department of Entomology, Fisheries and Wildlife, University of Minnesota, tested whether agricultural development had degraded invertebrate communities through siltation and turbidity. Benthic abundance, biomass and [[Stream Drift]] rates were broadly similar across all four streams. The authors conclude that the expected impacts of drainage-driven siltation were not evident, and that the few differences observed were mainly attributable to substrate and site-specific factors rather than agricultural development.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Agricultural practices in the Midwest, including channelization, subsurface drainage and road construction, were known to increase sediment loads, flooding and siltation in receiving streams. Such changes were expected to reduce benthic habitat, food resources and intolerant taxa.
- **Intolerant taxa expected to decline:** [[Pseudocloeon Sp.]], Hydropsychidae, [[Simulium Sp.]], [[Physa Gyrina]], [[Fenissia Sp.]] and Pelecypoda.
- **Study area:** Four streams in southwestern Minnesota, two natural (Highwater Creek, Cottonwood River) and two modified (Ramsey Creek, Fort Ridgely Creek). Sites were located on the watersheds above the [[Fort Ridgely State Park]] and related research sites; the natural streams had no apparent channelization in their upstream reaches, while the modified streams had been channelized and drained.
- **Sampling:** Benthos, drift and physicochemical conditions were measured, with monthly sampling over the study period. Analyses used geometric means with 95% confidence limits and ANOVA, with F values reported for comparisons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Physicochemical conditions:** Water temperature, dissolved oxygen, alkalinity, hardness, pH, carbon dioxide, turbidity and suspended solids were measured. Turbidity and suspended solids were similar across streams, and the authors argued that these similar levels explain the lack of differences in invertebrate communities.
- **Hydrology and drought:** All streams except Cottonwood experienced dry periods of 3–10 months. Faunal abundances were severely depleted but recovered rapidly once flow returned. Recolonization is attributed to upstream pools, deeper substrate layers, hatching of desiccation-resistant eggs or encystment capsules, and egg-laying by females flying in from other areas.
- **Substrate and velocity:** Elmid beetles ([[Optioservus Sp.]] and [[Stenelmis Sp.]]) and Hydropsychidae were more abundant in the Highwater and Fort Ridgely creeks, where rough substrate and pockmarked stones provide attachment and net-building sites. Differences were linked to substrate type and channel orientation rather than drainage.
- **Watershed effects:** Turbidity and sediment yield are functions of increased flow, volume and rate, and are partly controlled by soil conservation practices and vegetation along the channel.

## Key Findings

1. **Overall similarity:** Benthic invertebrate faunas of the four streams were similar. Numerical abundance, biomass and drift rates were similar across the study period, with few exceptions on a monthly basis.
2. **Diversity:** Shannon–Weaver diversity indices were Highwater 3.83, Cottonwood 3.85, Fort Ridgely 4.09 and Ramsey 4.08.
3. **Total benthos:** Mean total benthos numbers were approximately 4,369 individuals/m² (Highwater) and 10,713 (Cottonwood, the highest natural-stream value), with modified-stream means of similar magnitude. Biomass and drift ranged similarly across streams.
4. **Dominant groups:** Ephemeroptera, Diptera (especially Chironomidae) and Trichoptera dominated the benthos. Chironomidae dominated drift at Ramsey.
5. **Localized differences:** Significant differences were restricted to a few taxa, such as the high abundance of elmid beetles and Hydropsychidae in Highwater and Fort Ridgely creeks. Some drift rates (for example Ephemeroptera and [[Physa Gyrina]]) were significantly greater in modified streams than in natural streams.
6. **Drift at Fort Ridgely:** The relatively high drift rate at Fort Ridgely accounted for all observed differences in overall drift rates; the dominant drift organisms there included [[Allocapnia Sp.]] and the noninsects Physa gyrina and [[Hyalella Azteca]].
7. **Drought recovery:** Benthic communities recovered rapidly after drought, and statistically significant differences between stream pairs were not altered by including or excluding post-drought months.
8. **Conclusion:** The authors conclude that agricultural drainage development had not produced the level of siltation and turbidity expected to depauperate the invertebrate fauna of the modified streams. Differences observed were attributed to substrate, flow regime and site-specific factors rather than drainage, though the authors note that the rarity of Hyalella azteca in the modified streams may reflect the absence of suitable pool habitat there.
