## Executive Summary
This 1962 paper by W. T. Edmondson investigates the relationship between phytoplankton (edible microalgae) population density and the reproductive rate of planktonic zooplankton — specifically the rotifer **[[Keratella Cochlearis]]** and calanoid copepods (including **[[Diaptomus Ashlandi]]** and **[[Diaptomus Vulgaris]]**) — in natural lake populations. Using the "egg ratio method" (proportion of egg-bearing females in a population), Edmondson quantifies reproductive rate as a function of food supply across four English lakes (Windermere North and South Basins, Esthwaite Water, Blelham Tarn) and three Washington lakes (Lake Washington, Lake Lenore, Chase Lake). The study establishes a loose but discernible positive relationship between algal standing crop and zooplankton egg-laying rate, modulated strongly by temperature and by age-structure effects in the population being sampled. Although the paper is centered on invertebrate zooplankton rather than fish, it references **[[Calanus Finmarchicus]]** reproductive physiology (Marshall & Orr, 1952) as a comparative baseline and cites a companion fish-cultivation/plankton study (Gauld, 1950) relevant to fisheries productivity contexts.

## Background & Description
Not a fish-morphology paper; morphological detail is restricted to zooplankton life-history staging used to validate the egg-ratio method:

- **Rotifers (Keratella cochlearis):** Lay eggs singly, carrying each egg externally until hatching before laying the next; individuals become "ovigerous" (egg-bearing) upon reaching reproductive maturity, allowing egg:female ratios to be scored directly from net-haul samples.
- **Copepods (calanoid, e.g. Diaptomus spp.):** Possess twelve distinguishable life-history stages (naupliar + copepodid instars), each recognizable by morphological criteria. Only the sixth (final) copepodid stage is sexually mature, with sexes easily distinguished at that stage — enabling separation of "mature female" vs. "total population" egg ratios.
- **Phytoplankton food-particle sizing:** Edible algal cells for Keratella were operationally defined as those <12 µm in longest dimension (most <10 µm), spanning genera *Chlorella*, *Stichococcus*, *Coccomyxa*, *Chlamydomonas*, *Chrysochromulina*, *Cryptomonas*, and *Chrysococcus*. Larger *Cryptomonas* cells were noted as likely inedible to Keratella due to size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Trophic role:** Rotifers and copepods occupy the primary grazer niche in lake pelagic food webs, consuming small phytoplankton and converting that energy into reproductive output — a key link between primary production and secondary (and ultimately fish) production in aquatic systems.
- **Habitat context:** Four stratified/mixed temperate lakes in northern England (via Dr. J. G. W. Lund's biweekly algal census data) and three limnologically distinct Washington lakes — Lake Lenore (unstratified, saline), Lake Washington (stratified, epilimnion-monitored), and Chase Lake (shallow, 4–5 m max depth) — provided contrasting thermal and productivity regimes for comparison.
- **Temperature-food interaction:** Reproductive rate response to phytoplankton density was strongly temperature-dependent: at high algal concentrations, egg-production rates were markedly higher at temperatures >15°C than in the 10–15°C range, indicating a synergistic rather than purely additive control of reproduction by food and temperature.
- **Food supply dynamics:** Because phytoplankton reproduces continuously, standing-crop concentration alone underrepresents actual food *supply rate*; a dividing food population can substantially increase effective food consumption by grazers even at constant standing density (paper estimates ~39% increase in consumption when food divides once daily under a 50%/day clearance scenario).
- **Comparative marine reference:** The controlled-feeding relationship between food concentration and reproductive rate demonstrated for the marine copepod **[[Calanus Finmarchicus]]** (Marshall & Orr, 1952) is used as the theoretical/experimental analogue supporting the pattern seen in natural freshwater populations, linking this limnological work to broader marine zooplankton-fisheries productivity research (also referenced via Gauld's 1950 sea-loch fish cultivation/plankton study).

## Key Findings
- **Egg-ratio method validated as reproductive-rate proxy:** Population reproductive rate (B, eggs·female⁻¹·day⁻¹) can be derived from the egg:female ratio (E) divided by egg development duration (D): B = E/D, applicable to any egg-carrying zooplankter.
- **Positive but loose food–reproduction relationship:** Across four English lakes, higher **Keratella cochlearis** reproductive rates were associated with denser edible-algae standing crops, but with wide scatter — some high reproductive rates occurred even at low algal concentrations, attributed to unmeasured recent food history, algal growth-vs-grazing state, and shifting population age structure.
- **Temperature amplifies food effect:** At high phytoplankton density, egg-production rates were substantially higher at 15–20°C than at 10–15°C, showing that temperature and food supply interact rather than act independently.
- **Age structure matters for interpretation:** Comparing copepod reproductive rate calculated from mature females only (M) versus total population (T) showed that in most lake-events (4 of 6 major reproductive peaks across Lake Lenore, Lake Washington, and Chase Lake), the timing of peak reproduction matched between methods; however, in one Lake Lenore event, age-structure shifts delayed the "mature female" peak by a full month relative to the "total population" peak, and Lake Washington showed a notably different seasonal trend between the two curves — demonstrating that natural shifts in age structure can meaningfully distort interpretation of population-level egg ratios.
- **Copepod reproduction tracks phytoplankton increases:** Increases in mature female copepod reproductive rate consistently occurred coincident with or shortly after upswings in phytoplankton population from a prior low point, though no simple linear relationship existed between momentary phytoplankton density and instantaneous egg-laying rate.
- **Food quality flagged as unresolved variable:** Certain anomalously high reproductive-rate data points may reflect qualitative food factors not captured by biomass/density metrics alone — e.g., reserve nutrient content of algal cells, or presence of biotin/vitamin B12 — flagged as needing further study (see also [[Trophic Relations Of Zooplankton]], Edmondson 1957/1961).
- **Methodological outlook:** The demonstrable, if imprecise, quantitative link between food abundance and egg production in field data supports further refinement of combined laboratory-assay, field-experiment, and field-descriptive approaches to understanding natural plankton population control — directly relevant to fisheries production models dependent on zooplankton secondary production.
