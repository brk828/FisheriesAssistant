# Feeding Ecology and Relative Abundance of Mullet (Mugilidae) in Natal and Pondoland Estuaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mugilidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 study by S. J. M. Blaber (University of Natal) examines the feeding ecology and relative abundance of eleven [[Grey Mullet]] species (family [[Mugilidae]]) in five estuaries of the Natal and Pondoland coast of South Africa, sampled over two years (1975–1976). Diet was broadly similar across species but varied between estuaries, and was largely governed by the occurrence of food items on substrates of each species' preferred particle size. Species avoid interspecific competition by selecting different inorganic particle sizes, and always rank in the same order by particle size. Relative abundance, by contrast, is interpreted as controlled mainly by [[Zoogeography]] rather than by food or substrate requirements.

## Background & Description

Mullet feed by sucking up the surface layer of substrate or grazing on submerged rock and plant surfaces. A previous study of ten grey mullet species in the [[St Lucia Lake System]] (Blaber, 1976) found that all species ingested large quantities of inorganic particles along with food, and that competition was reduced through particle-size preferences. The present study asked whether this pattern was unique to St Lucia or general across the family. Fish under 6 cm standard length were treated separately in a companion paper (Blaber & Whitfield, 1977).

Methods were as follows:
- **Capture:** seine, gill and cast nets, and rotenone (a piscicide). Standard length was recorded and stomachs preserved in 10% formalin.
- **Diet analysis:** stomach contents from the cardiac and pyloric regions were washed into Petri dishes and scored by frequency of occurrence. Substrate particles within stomachs were measured with an eyepiece micrometer.
- **Substrate analysis:** surface sediment (2 cm depth) was dried at 60°C and sieved; organic content was measured by [[Loss on Ignition]] at 500°C after carbonate removal with 0.1 N nitric acid. Ten samples were analysed for organic content at three Kosi sites and one site in the lower Sodwana estuary.
- **Fish with gill raker filaments** in the stomach were excluded, as this was attributed to regurgitation during gill-net capture (Blaber, 1975).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas.** Fish were collected from the Msikaba, Mtentu, Impenjati, Sodwana and Kosi estuaries. Kosi, Impenjati and Sodwana were sampled seasonally in 1975–1976; Msikaba and Mtentu were visited only once each in autumn 1975 and autumn 1976. The Kosi system includes [[Lake Nhlange]], Lake Sifungwe and Lake Mpungwini, and Mgobezeleni Lake drains into the Sodwana estuary.

**Substrate and food gradients.** Organic content was highest in the lower reaches of Kosi (6.7% dry weight, S.E. 0.38) and lowest in the shelf areas of Lake Nhlange (2 m deep; 0.23%, S.E. 0.03). Lower Sodwana estuary had 6.0% (S.E. 0.27). Particle size composition was similar across the three Kosi localities, and no correlation was found between organic content and particle size. Very fine sand (45–125 µm) was scarce at all Kosi sites.

**Biogeographic context.** Some species are cosmopolitan (Mugil cephalus, Liza dumerili). Others extend south from the tropics (Liza macrolepis, Valamugil spp.), while Liza tricuspidens and Myxus capensis reach the area from cooler waters. Liza tricuspidens is endemic to southern Africa.

**Salinity.** Foraminifera, the dominant food of Mugil cephalus in the lower Kosi estuary, are sensitive to salinities as low as 4‰, which occur in Lakes Nhlange and Sifungwe.

**Predation.** Smaller Mugil cephalus (50–200 mm) were abundant in the upper Kosi reaches, possibly to escape predatory fish such as Caranx spp., Sphyraena spp. and sharks that abound in the lower estuary (pers. obs.).

## Key Findings

**Relative abundance (catch per unit effort, Table 1)**
- Mugil cephalus, Liza dumerili and Valamugil buchanani are common across Natal and Pondoland estuaries; Liza alata and Valamugil cunnesius are uncommon.
- Liza macrolepis increases in abundance northwards. Valamugil robustus is rare south of Kosi, where it is the most common species; its catch was 79.0 in summer and 6.0 in winter.
- Liza tricuspidens is rare in Natal (few reach Kosi). Crenimugil crenilabis was represented by one adult, plus fry under 5 cm in Sodwana. Valamugil seheli is rare, probably vagrant from the tropics.
- Myxus capensis is locally common and prefers freshwater areas.

**Diet (Tables 2–4)**
- Pennate diatoms were the most frequent food of Mugil cephalus (Foraminifera at Kosi). Particulate organic matter was abundant in the Kosi lakes and Impenjati.
- Foraminifera dominated the diet of Liza macrolepis and L. dumerili in Zululand; pennate diatoms and particulate organic matter dominated in Pondoland.
- Liza alata fed mainly on filamentous algae (Chaetomorpha spp.).
- Liza tricuspidens was the most herbivorous species, eating red and green algae (Hypnaea spicifera, Enteromorpha spp., Gelidium spp.), supported by its large tricuspid teeth.
- Valamugil spp. fed on small items (pennate diatoms, flagellates, cyanophytes, particulate organic matter).
- Mixed-species schools at Kosi (n = 13–37 fish per sample) fed on similar items, with Valamugil consuming more particulate organic matter.
- Large terrestrial debris consumption (e.g. 88% of V. buchanani at Kosi, March 1976) followed flooding.
- Diet did not vary seasonally for most species, and little size-related change was noted.

**Particle size partitioning (Tables 5–6, Figs 2–4)**
- Each species ingests a wide range of particle sizes, but within one estuary overlap is minimal.
- At Kosi, mean particle size was about 400 µm for L. dumerili, 300 µm for L. macrolepis and 200 µm for Valamugil spp.
- At Msikaba, all species showed a double peak at about 50 µm and 250 µm, so peak values did not match the Kosi pattern.
- Preferred sand sizes (Wentworth scale): L. tricuspidens and L. alata coarse sand; L. dumerili medium to coarse; L. macrolepis, Mugil cephalus and Myxus capensis medium to fine; Valamugil spp. fine sand (0.125–0.25 mm).
- Competition among Valamugil spp. is possibly reduced because their main food (diatoms and organic matter) is usually abundant.

**Conclusions**
- Diet similarity, locality-dependent diet, and a constant particle-size ranking support the partitioning hypothesis from St Lucia.
- Mugil cephalus appears to prefer animal-rich substrates (Foraminifera), and its large individuals were most abundant where organic content was high, while small individuals were more abundant upstream, suggesting distribution is controlled by factors other than food.
- Bacteria may be an important food source, as suggested by Moriarty (1976), though they were not quantified here.
- Relative abundance is controlled primarily by zoogeography rather than food or substrate, and most species are flexible enough to use a range of substrates.
- The rarity of Liza alata may reflect narrower substrate preferences than the common species.
- Assimilation efficiency data (Hickling, 1970; Payne, 1976; Winberg, 1956) suggest omnivorous mullet benefit from consuming animal material.
