# Razorback Sucker Endangered Listing and Related Colorado River Fisheries Documents

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This packet centers on the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's (FWS) draft and final rule to list the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA), without designating [[Critical Habitat]]. The rule states that the species occupies about 25 percent of its historic range, is very rare, and has shown no significant recruitment to the adult population for about 30 years. The packet also includes a 1990 FWS field memorandum, the Federal Register proposal and comment history, a responses-to-comments section, Section 7 and conservation discussion, and reviewer edits from the Phoenix office. A separate meeting agenda for the Colorado River Sport Fish Committee (January 1991) is included.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The species was described by Abbott (1861) from a single mounted specimen. It was later placed in its own genus, *Xyrauchen*, by Kirsch (1889). It is also known as the humpback sucker.
- **Morphology:** Adults have an abrupt, sharp-edged dorsal keel behind the head and a large, fleshy subterminal mouth. Adults commonly exceed 3 kg (6 lb) and 600 mm (24 in). Juveniles lack an obvious keel, which makes them hard to separate from other suckers.
- **Historic distribution:** The species was once abundant across about 5,635 km (3,500 mi) of the basin, including Mexican states Baja California Norte and Sonora. The Colorado River was divided into upper and lower basins at Lee Ferry, Arizona, under the 1922 Colorado River Compact.
- **Current distribution:** The species now occupies about 25 percent of its historic range. In the lower basin, a substantial population exists only in [[Lake Mohave]]. Marsh and Minckley estimated about 60,000 adults there. In the upper basin, the population is centered on the [[Green River]] (Grand Valley area), with an estimated 758 to roughly 1,100 individuals (OCR-garbled upper bound).
- **Life history:** Adults migrate considerable distances to spawn. Lower basin spawning runs from January through April at 11.5–18 °C. Upper Green River spawning was observed from May 3 to June 15 at 10.5–18 °C. Spawning occurs over gravel or cobble bars swept clean of silt.
- **Age structure:** McCarthy and Minckley (1987) estimated Lake Mohave adults at 24–44 years old. Eighty-nine percent of 70 sampled fish hatched before or at impoundment. Scale-based aging had underestimated ages.
- **Growth and food:** Minckley (1983) estimated growth below 10 mm per year after age seven, based on scales. Adults feed on planktonic crustaceans, diatoms, algae, midge larvae, and detritus. Larvae select cladocerans (*Bosmina*) and avoid copepods in reservoirs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat alteration.** Fifteen dams have been built on the lower Colorado River and its major tributaries (Gila, Verde, Salt) since 1910. These dams have dewatered, cooled, or impounded most of the lower basin system. [[Glen Canyon Dam]] cooled water for 384 km (238 mi) through Grand Canyon. [[Lake Powell]] and [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] impounded about 500 km (310 mi) of razorback habitat and lowered temperatures in another 105 km (65 mi) of river.

**Flow regime.** Storage and diversion reduced mean annual discharge at the Green-Colorado confluence by 19 percent. Spring runoff fell by 28 percent in the Green River and 37 percent in the Colorado River during May and June. Peaking power operations at Flaming Gorge increased daily flow fluctuation by 400 percent at Jensen, Utah. These changes reduce the flooding of off-stream habitats thought to be important for adults and young.

**Temperature.** Bulkley and Pimentel (1983) found adult preferred temperatures of 22–25 °C and avoidance below 14.7 °C and above 27.4 °C. Winter temperatures fall well below this range. Reaches below Flaming Gorge and Lake Powell often stay under 15 °C in summer, and few razorbacks have been captured there since the dams were built. Incubation time varies inversely with temperature. Hatching success is poor below 15 °C, and eggs die almost completely below 10 °C.

**Predation and competition.** Predators on eggs and larvae include carp, channel catfish, smallmouth bass, largemouth bass, bluegill, green sunfish, and redear sunfish. [[Marsh and Brooks]] (1989) showed channel and flathead catfish could eliminate hatchery-stocked fish in the Gila River. Tyus et al. (1982) recorded 42 nonnative species established in the upper basin, and Minckley (1979) listed 37 in the lower basin. Northern pike and walleye have invaded the Green River, and tilapia and flathead catfish have become dominant in the lower river.

**Hybridization.** Hybrids with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] have been reported in the upper basin (for example, 16 hybrids to 73 razorbacks in one study). Recent electrophoretic work on Lake Mohave found less than 5 percent flannelmouth gene incidence, which Buth et al. (1987) considered insignificant.

**Companion listed fishes.** Three other fishes are already ESA-listed in the basin: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Federal agencies must consult under Section 7 for those species, but not for the razorback. Among the four rare native fishes, only the bonytail is considered less common.

## Key Findings

1. **Listing decision:** The FWS determined the razorback sucker is in danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of its range due to insufficient recruitment. It rejected the threatened classification favored by the Bureau of Reclamation and Nevada.
2. **Recruitment failure:** No significant natural recruitment to the adult population has been evident for 30 years. Only four juveniles (33–54 mm) have been collected from Lake Mohave since the 1950s.
3. **Population decline:** Upper basin fish are rare, and adult numbers have trended downward. Existing adults are mostly old, slowly dying individuals.
4. **Stocking results:** Arizona stocked about 9.5 million fish between 1981 and 1985, mostly as fry, with poor recapture rates. Reintroduction has not re-established the species.
5. **Causal factors (ESA five-factor analysis):**
   - A: Habitat loss through dams, diversions, and altered flows and temperatures
   - B: Overutilization not considered a current threat, since harvest is prohibited in all states
   - C: Disease not considered significant; predation is a major suspected cause of recruitment failure
   - D: Existing state and federal regulations are inadequate for habitat and nonnative species
   - E: Competition and predation from nonnative species; hybridization considered minor
6. **Critical habitat:** Designation was judged "not determinable or prudent." The FWS argued it would not stop nonnative predation or competition, would add little protection beyond Section 7, and would add little federal notification benefit.
7. **Public comment:** The proposed rule (46 FR 21154, May 22, 1990) drew 62 written and 18 oral comments from 11 federal agencies, 7 state agencies, 10 local governments, and 47 private organizations or individuals. About 60 people attended the August 14, 1990 Farmington, New Mexico hearing.
8. **Listing history:** The species was proposed as threatened in 1978 (43 FR 17375), withdrawn in 1980 after the ESA deadline lapsed, and petitioned by conservation groups in March 1989. The Recovery Team recommended threatened status in 1980 and endangered in 1987.
9. **Recovery and management:** The [[Colorado River Endangered Fishes Recovery Program]] aims to create self-sustaining populations, but it is cooperative, not regulatory, and covers only the upper basin excluding the San Juan River.
10. **Regulatory consequences:** Listing requires Section 7 consultation for federal actions affecting the species, and permits for reintroduction or augmentation. Nonnative stocking involving federal funds or hatcheries would also face Section 7 review. Some reservoir stocking with no federal nexus would not require consultation.
11. **Economic and political concerns:** Some elected officials in affected states may oppose listing because of economic impacts and resource-use conflicts. The FWS may consider only biological facts in its listing decision.
12. **Memo edits (December 5, 1990, Phoenix Ecological Services):** Suggested corrections to the rule text, including a change from "A large monotypic sucker" to "An endemic monotypic sucker," clarification of the keel description for juveniles, updated distribution (for example, adding Lake Mead and Grand Canyon downstream of Glen Canyon Dam), and missing references (Ecology Consultants 1978, Seethaler 1978, McAda and Kaeding 1989).
13. **Meeting agenda (January 9–10, 1991, Laughlin, Nevada):** The Colorado River Fishes Sport Fish Committee's 12th annual meeting covered a threatened and endangered fish roundtable, sport fish status reports for Lower Colorado and reservoir systems, Glen Canyon environmental studies, Lake Mead fertilization results, and a CAP Canal final report.
