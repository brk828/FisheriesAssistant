# Reproductive Behavior of Jordanella floridae: Quantitative Analysis of Parental Fanning

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 study by John C. Mertz and George W. Barlow, published in the *Zeitschrift für Tierpsychologie*, describes the reproductive behavior of the [[Florida Flagfish]] ([[Jordanella floridae]], family [[Cyprinodontidae]]) and quantifies its [[Parental Fanning]]. The authors describe territorial, courtship and spawning behavior in laboratory aquaria, and they test whether fanning is organized in time in a way that matches embryonic development. Jordanella is the only known tooth carp (Cyprinodontiformes) that fans its eggs, and it differs from related species because its sexual and parental behavior overlap. Fanning shows only weak temporal organization: nonparametric tests reveal a common trend across males, but individual males rarely follow it. The authors conclude that Jordanella is intermediate in the evolution of parental fanning.

## Background & Description

**Parental fanning.** Fanning is a parental behavior in which the male beats his caudal and pectoral fins to circulate water over the developing young. This may aerate the clutch and keep sediment from settling on it (Morris 1954). Few fanning studies have been quantitative, and they span widely different groups of fishes. The authors cite [[Bartow 1964 Evolutionary Scheme]], a hypothetical sequence for the evolution of fanning. In that scheme, the final step is the elimination of overlapping clutches, which allows fanning to synchronize with development and requires sexual behavior to stop during the parental phase.

**Study organism.** Jordanella floridae occurs in fresh waters of peninsular Florida and along the Gulf of Mexico coast to Yucatan. Its parental and sexual behavior overlap. The species spawns throughout the reproductive period. This is unusual among fanning species, and the authors describe it as "evidently unique" in this respect.

**Materials and methods.**
- Animals were laboratory-raised progeny of native Florida stock, aged 8–10 months.
- Males measured 40.5–49.0 mm standard length and weighed 2.4–4.0 g. Females measured 30.5–42.0 mm and weighed 1.1–3.1 g.
- Four aquaria (80 × 30 × 30 cm) were used, with a 14-hour photoperiod, water at 21.0–22.5 °C, pH 7.5–8.0, and 0.5 g marine salts per liter.
- Each tank held a [[Vallisneria]] planting, slate slabs, and a spawning mat of [[Filamentous Algae]] (*Gonatonema* spp.).
- A "dither" compartment held [[Zebra Fish]] and [[Guppy]] to supply continuous visual stimulation. The authors found that fish became sensitive to nonvisual stimuli when extraneous visual stimulation was removed.
- Observations were made in 15-minute sessions. Behavior was recorded on a multiple-event recorder, so the raw data were frequency counts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Territoriality and aggression.** Spawning takes place within a territory defended by the male. Territorial males dominate non-territorial males, and mutual fighting was not observed. Cross-specific intruders were tolerated near the spawning site and driven off without a display. The aquarium trade regards Jordanella as pugnacious, and the authors attribute this to territorial aggression.

**Substrate and nesting.** Spawning was restricted mostly to the algal mat when it was available, both in the laboratory and in an outdoor pond. The authors found no true nest construction. Males pulled algae strands and transported gravel, but these movements were not consistently oriented to one site. Depressions were not made in gravel. The authors suggest that gravel may be an unsuitable substrate compared with silt, mud or sand in natural habitats. Pit construction is known in related species such as [[Cyprinodon variegatus]] and [[Cyprinodon macularius]].

**Comparative context.** Related species show similar territorial and spawning patterns. However, [[T-Circling]] has not been described in either Cyprinodon species, and the spawning clasp in Jordanella is oriented more nearly prone than in Cyprinodon. The authors suggest that T-circling may have evolved to confine the spawn to a small area, which would make it a consequence of parental behavior.

## Key Findings

**1. Spawning behavior**
- A ready female approaches the territorial male with rapid, low-amplitude caudal beats and pale coloration. The male usually responds with fin display and does not attack her.
- The female controls the timing and location of spawning. She avoids the male's attempts to clasp her until she is physiologically ready and properly positioned on the spawning site.
- During the spawning clasp, the male's dorsal fin grips the female, and the pair vibrates at low amplitude and high frequency. The female then moves the pair by "jumps" toward the spawning site.
- Oviposition was observed in only three spawnings, with a single egg deposited before most jumps. The authors suggest that eggs are released one at a time, which contradicts Peters (1941), who reported clumped spawning.
- One pair spawned 13 times within 25 minutes.

**2. Nest building**
- No functional nests (pits excavated for eggs or young) were found. The authors conclude that Jordanella has the basic motor patterns for nest building but does not use them for spawning.

**3. Parental care**
- Defense of eggs was largely by males. Males removed and ate fungused eggs, though they did not usually eat viable eggs.
- Few young were recovered from the experimental aquaria, and the authors note that young may have been eaten. Males chased a recently hatched young only once. The authors conclude that the male is inhibited from eating his brood.

**4. Fanning measures**
- Six parameters were measured per 15-minute session: Bouts, Beats, Duration, Tempo, Beats per Bout, and Bout Duration.
- A bout is a group of beats separated from others by intervals of at least one second.
- No simple daily cycle was apparent, though values varied strongly from hour to hour.

**5. Quantitative results (Kendall's W, days 1–9)**

| Parameter | Kendall's W | Chi-square (df = 8) | Peak timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duration | 0.191 | 17.882 | Day 5 |
| Bouts | 0.256 | 22.528 | Day 4 |
| Tempo | 0.313 | 27.544 | Rises between days 1 and 2; plateau from day 6 |
| Beats per Bout | 0.473 | 41.659 | Day 7 |

All values were significant at p < 0.05. Bout Duration gave a significant result and a curve almost identical to Beats per Bout. Beats gave a significant result similar in shape to Duration.

**6. Relationship between fanning parameters**
- Beats and Duration were highly correlated in all males (r ≥ 0.981 across males).
- Analysis of covariance rejected the hypotheses of a common regression line, common slope, and zero regression (p ≤ 0.05) for all 15 bivariate combinations.
- Only three males (C, I and J) fitted the common trend well. The authors conclude that temporal organization is present but not well developed.

**7. Timing of fanning and hatching**
- The expected first hatching was day 7. Beats per Bout and Bout Duration tended to peak that day.
- Greatest fanning amount occurred on day 4 (by Bouts) or day 5 (by Beats and Duration), two to three days before the expected hatching.

**8. Additional behaviors**
- **[[Foraging]]:** Significant negative correlations with Beats were found in only four males, so no consistent inverse relationship was established.
- **[[Yawning]]:** Frequency was low, from 0 to 7 yawns per 15 minutes, with a mode of 2. Yawning showed no consistent relationship with fanning, unlike [[Badis badis]]. The authors caution that sampling error may be high.
- **Pulling:** Pull was positively correlated with Beats in all males except J and K. The authors cannot fully explain this and doubt that it reflects attempts to eat the young.
- **Spawning frequency:** Eggs were visible on all males by day 2, and spawning was observed in ten of the groups by day 2.

**9. Comparison with other species**
- In the [[Three-Spined Stickleback]], total fanning time, bout duration and number of bouts all peak on the day of hatching. In the [[Ten-Spined Stickleback]], bout number peaks on hatching day, but time and bout duration peak two days earlier.
- In the river bullhead (*Cottus gobio*), hatching correlates with the maximum number of beats per observation period.
- In Badis badis, as in Jordanella, hatching coincides with maxima in beats per bout and bout duration, but the underlying patterns differ.

**10. Conclusions on evolution**
- Jordanella has not reached the stage in which overlapping clutches are eliminated. The authors suggest that low egg numbers per spawning (perhaps eight to ten, though this is not measured) may force multiple spawnings and overlapping clutches.
- Parental behavior in Jordanella is interpreted as primitive. Peters (1941) had described its preparation for spawning as crude, and the present data support that view.
- The authors argue that a single fanning measure can mislead. Changes in the relationships among parameters drive the observed peaks, and the shapes of their distributions differ between species.
