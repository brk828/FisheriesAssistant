# Nesting Site of the Lollypop Darter *Etheostoma neopterum*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This short research note reports the first documented nests of the [[Lollypop Darter]] (*Etheostoma neopterum*), found on 15 April 1978 in tributaries of [[Birdsong Creek]] in Benton County, Tennessee, within the [[Tennessee River]] drainage. Two male-guarded nests were located on the undersides of stones, a nesting habit shared with the [[Subgenus Catonotus]] darters. The authors, Lawrence M. Pace and Richard L. Mayden of the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]], place the species' nesting behavior in a comparative context with its closest relative, the [[Spotted Darter]] group member [[Spottail Darter]] (*E. squamiceps*).

## Background & Description

The [[Subgenus Catonotus]] (genus *Etheostoma*) deposits adhesive eggs on the undersides of stones, usually in pools of small streams. Species with this reported nesting site before this study include *E. flabellare*, *E. squamiceps*, *E. kennicotti* (see [[Stripetail Darter]]), and *E. smithi* (see [[Slabrock Darter]]). Outside Catonotus, only a few darters use stone undersides for nesting: *E. (Boleosoma) olmstedi*, *E. (B.) nigrum* (johnny darters), and *E. (Nothonotus) maculatum* (spotted darter).

Howell and Dingerkus (1978) described *E. neopterum* from the [[Shoal Creek]] system in Tennessee and Alabama, where slab stones are scarce. There, breeding males and females were found only under shallow undercut banks in slow-water areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The nests were in the Tennessee River drainage at two depths and temperatures:

- Nest 1: 9 cm water depth, guarded by a 58 mm SL male, 577 eggs, water 14 °C.
- Nest 2: 20 cm water depth, guarded by a 65 mm SL male, 685 eggs, water 15 °C.

Both nests were under stones near the stream center in slow current. In one tributary, [[Stripetail Darter]] (*E. kennicotti*) was common, but no nests of that species were found and spawning had apparently not begun. In the other tributary, *E. neopterum* was the only Catonotus species present. Stone availability and slow-water microhabitat appear important to nest placement, given the scarcity of slab stones noted in the Shoal Creek system.

## Key Findings

- **First nest records:** Two nests were documented, containing 577 and 685 eggs, each guarded by a male.
- **Nesting substrate:** Eggs were attached to undersides of stones, consistent with other [[Subgenus Catonotus]] species.
- **Multilayer eggs:** On one nest, some eggs were laid on top of others. Other Catonotus species were reported to lay eggs in a single layer. The authors suggest limited space on the stone underside forced a second layer.
- **Sympatric species:** [[Stripetail Darter]] was common in one tributary but not yet spawning.
- **Predicted spawning behavior:** Based on [[Spottail Darter]] studies (Page 1974), the authors expect *E. neopterum* spawning to be head-to-head, with both sexes briefly inverted for only a few seconds. This remains untested for *E. neopterum*.
- **Reference context:** The work builds on Howell and Dingerkus (1978), the original description, and on comparative darter reproduction reviews by [[Winn 1958]].
