# Larval Razorback Sucker Predation by Odonate Naiads Under Laboratory Conditions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared by Michael J. Horn and submitted by Paul C. Marsh of [[Arizona State University]] to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (order number 1425-2-PG-81-11240, dated 25 May 1992), evaluates whether predation by larval dragonflies and damselflies (odonate naiads) constrains the on-site production of larval [[Razorback Sucker]] at [[Lake Mohave]]. The population at Lake Mohave consists mainly of old adults with recruitment failure, and the report describes it as likely to be extirpated within the decade. Two laboratory experiments showed that odonate naiads substantially reduced larval survival. Survival of small larvae (about 11.8 mm TL) fell to roughly 19–24% with predators present, compared with about 88% in controls.

## Background & Description

**Management context.** Razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is endangered. The augmentation project is run by the [[Native Fish Work Group]], a multi-agency body that includes the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the Bureau of Reclamation, the Fish and Wildlife Service, the National Park Service, the Nevada Department of Wildlife, and Arizona State University. Adults were moved from the main body of Lake Mohave at Arizona Bay into the isolated backwater of [[Yuma Cove]], where they spawn naturally. Larvae are reared there to a suitable size and then moved to Davis Cove to reach sub-adult size before reintroduction. The approach succeeded in 1985, but later attempts suffered high larval mortality. Routine monitoring showed that larval odonates were abundant in the cove, which prompted the experiments.

**Odonate biology.** The report describes [[Odonata]] as the most conspicuous insect group around water bodies. The order has two suborders: [[Anisoptera]] (dragonflies), which hold their wings horizontally at rest, and [[Zygoptera]] (damselflies), which hold their wings together above or tilted from the body. Both are voracious predators. Naiads are aquatic, cryptically colored, and breathe through gills (rectal gills in Anisoptera, three external caudal lamellae in Zygoptera). Every naiad has an extensible labium, a jointed mouthpart that shoots out hydraulically to capture prey. The larger naiads can take fish larvae, and in hatchery ponds with high densities, predation can be severe enough to affect larval fish production.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Early life stages:** Newly hatched larvae cannot swim sustainably and probably spend much of their time on or in the substrate, where they are exposed to predators. Larvae become active swimmers only after absorbing the yolk sac and developing a functional gas bladder. It is unknown whether swim-up larvae stay pelagic or remain benthic or near-benthic for part of the diel cycle.
- **Vegetation:** Many odonates climb through aquatic vegetation and ambush prey there. Extensive growth of [[Potamogeton]] (pondweed) in the Yuma Cove backwater may increase predation impacts on young razorback suckers.
- **Historical context:** The authors note that young razorback suckers historically occupied shallow warm backwaters, where odonate predation would have been part of the natural larval environment.
- **Laboratory conditions:** Experiments used a 12:12 photoperiod, continuous aeration from a single pump, and unfiltered Lake Mohave water. Odonates were captured at night by light trap in Yuma Cove.

## Key Findings

### Experiment 1 (12-day-old larvae, replicated)

- **Design:** Six aquaria, each with 100 larvae (mean TL 11.8 ± 0.7 mm). Tanks 1 and 3 had 20 damselfly naiads each, Tanks 4 and 5 had 20 dragonfly naiads each, and Tanks 2 and 6 were fish-only controls. Water temperature was 19 °C. Predators were run unmonitored for seven days.
- **Survivorship:** A significant treatment effect was found (F = 39.4, p = 0.0075). Final survival was about 88% in controls, 24% with dragonflies, and about 19% with damselflies (the damselfly value is derived from the Table 2 counts, since the report's own figure is garbled in the scan).
- **Tukey comparisons:**
  - Control vs. dragonfly: q = 10.44, p = 0.01
  - Control vs. damselfly: q = 11.24, p = 0.009
  - Dragonfly vs. damselfly: q = 0.82, p > 0.5 (no difference)
- **Consumption:** Both naiad types consumed significant numbers of larvae, with no significant difference in consumption rate between them.
- **Final sizes:** Surviving fish averaged about 13 mm TL.

### Experiment 2 (about 5-week-old larvae, unreplicated)

- **Design:** Tanks held 75 larvae (estimated 14–15 mm mean TL, range 11–17 mm). Tank 1 received 20 damselfly naiads, Tank 4 received 20 dragonfly naiads, and Tanks 2 and 3 were controls. Water temperature was 23 °C. The experiment ran for eight days.
- **Survivorship:** Tank counts gave 49% (Tank 1, damselfly), 44% (Tank 4, dragonfly), 79% (Tank 2) and 85% (Tank 3). Pooled, this gives about 47% survival with predators and about 82% in controls.
- **Limitation:** Replication was not possible because odonates were uncommon in Yuma Cove at that time, so no statistical tests were run.
- **Final sizes:** Surviving fish averaged about 17.5 mm TL.

### Naiad measurements

- **Damselflies:** Head width about 3.5–3.6 mm and total length about 14.2–14.4 mm (Experiment 1 tanks).
- **Dragonflies:** Head width about 5.8–6.8 mm and total length about 15.2–19.4 mm across both experiments.
- Many naiads were lost to mortality or emergence during the trials, and because the tanks were not checked continuously, the timing of these losses is unknown.

### Conclusions and recommendations

1. The authors conclude that odonate naiads can destroy substantial numbers of larval razorback suckers. Survival in the experiments might have reached zero if the trials had continued. They also caution that the lab results cannot be directly extrapolated to Yuma Cove, but substantial odonate populations there could plausibly limit juvenile production.
2. Predation impact is probably greatest on the earliest larval stages (before swim-up), which suggests that predator control could be considered. Chemical control (for example, an insecticide) or biological control (for example, an odonate predator) would ideally be applied before fertilized ova or larvae enter the system.
3. Stocking young fish above a size at which they are no longer vulnerable to odonates may be more attractive. The threshold was not determined in this study but is likely in the 20–30 mm TL range. This option may require off-site culture and early grow-out, which conflicts with the project's goals of simplicity and minimal intensive manipulation.
4. Removing aquatic vegetation is already recommended to improve recovery of juvenile razorback suckers and may also reduce odonate predation where it is occurring.
