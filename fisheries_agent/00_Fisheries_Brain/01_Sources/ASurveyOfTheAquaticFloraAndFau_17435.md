# Survey of the Fishes, Aquatic Invertebrates and Aquatic Plants of the Colorado River, Lee Ferry to Separation Rapids (1977–1979)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This contract report, prepared by Steven W. Carothers and C. O. Minckley of the Museum of Northern Arizona for the Water and Power Resources Service (dated February 4, 1981), provides baseline data on the aquatic resources of the [[Colorado River]] and selected tributaries between Lee Ferry and Separation Rapids within [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. Fieldwork ran from 1977 to 1979 and focused on fishes, with parallel surveys of aquatic invertebrates and aquatic plants, plus physico-chemical and bacteriological water quality.

The study documents an ecosystem reshaped by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (completed 1963). Of 27 fish species known from the reach, about 70% are exotic. Three of the eight original native fishes are extinct in Grand Canyon, the [[Humpback Chub]] is restricted largely to the [[Little Colorado River]] for spawning, and three natives (flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, speckled dace) remain widespread.

Note: the source text ends partway through Chapter 6 (Table 6-14), so the dietary and later sections are not covered here.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** About 389 km of river, with 27 tributaries and springs sampled. The mainstream was divided into 12 river sections (I–XII), each about 32 km long.
- **Sampling effort:** Six river expeditions (Nov.–Dec. 1977; Jan., May, June, Aug., and Oct.–Nov. 1978) and four helicopter fly-in surveys. River expeditions represent about 1,440 field person-days; about 40 person-days targeted the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] near the Little Colorado River Gorge.
- **Methods:**
  - Fish: [[Trammel Net]] and [[Gill Net]] sets, seining, and [[Electrofishing]] (converted to DC, 0–600 V). Movement was studied with Floy anchor tags on fish over 15 cm.
  - Age: scales for [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Speckled Dace]]; opercles for humpback chub, suckers and carp; pectoral spines for channel catfish.
  - Diet: mass method, with [[Electivity Indices]] and [[Kulczynski Similarity Index]] for dietary overlap.
  - Invertebrates: Surber and mini-Ponar samplers at tributary confluences and 200 m upstream.
  - Plants: transects with importance values, plus [[Diatoms]] counts from periphyton.
- **Creel census:** Bright Angel Creek, Nov. 1977–Mar. 1978, with 20 census days at six sites over about 3 km. A companion census covered the 24 km reach between Glen Canyon Dam and Lee Ferry.
- **Historical context:** Pre-dam fishes were described from archaeological material in Stanton's Cave (about 4,000 years old) and from early explorers' accounts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical regime**
- Pre-dam flows were seasonal and turbid, with spring peaks and flash floods. Post-dam flows are regulated by power demand: mean daily maximum discharge is about 566 cms and minimum about 130 cms.
- Water released from the dam is clear and cold, at 6–15 °C, and varies little through the year.
- Sediment at Lee Ferry fell from about 1,500 ppm to 7 ppm. The [[Little Colorado River]] now supplies most of the sediment between Lee Ferry and Lake Mead.
- Mainstream specific conductance was 830–1,600 µmhos/cm, with pH 7.1–7.9. (One passage gives a range up to 16,000 µmhos/cm, which appears to be a typographical error.)
- Tributaries vary: [[Bright Angel Creek]] and other dilute dolomitic streams have low conductivity, while the Little Colorado River is a saline sodium chloride stream, and Kanab Creek is a sulfate stream.

**Riparian and habitat change**
- Pre-dam flooding maintained an ephemeral zone. Post-dam, salt-cedar (*Tamarix*), arrowweed, coyote willow and seep willow dominate the former flood zone.
- Armoring of coarse gravels has reduced erosion in parts of the river, especially between the dam and Lee Ferry.

**Water quality**
- Bacterial studies found that mainstream bacterial quality was relatively stable, while tributaries varied widely with summer rains.
- The Arizona standard for primary contact recreation is a mean fecal coliform count below 200 per 100 ml.
- Grazing, septic systems and sewage effluent at Fredonia, Kanab, Supai and Phantom Ranch were identified as contamination sources.

## Key Findings

### Fishes

- **Species richness:** 27 species are known from the study area, of which 17 (63%) were collected in this study. Seventy percent are exotic.
- **Dominance:** Six species made up 99.4% of pooled collections: carp (41.6%), speckled dace (15.6%), flannelmouth sucker (13.8%), rainbow trout (13.3%), bluehead sucker (9.3%) and humpback chub (5.8%).
- **Native status:**
  - [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado River Chub]] (roundtail chub) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] are considered extinct in Grand Canyon. All three are federally listed as endangered.
  - [[Humpback Chub]] is endangered and still present, but reproduction appears to occur only near the Little Colorado River confluence (71–6,364 fish/ha in seining). Adults were about 15% of tributary catches at most.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] was considered extinct in the canyon until one adult was collected near the Paria confluence in 1978.
  - [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]] persist as healthy, reproducing populations. Juvenile suckers remain in perennial tributaries for two to three years before moving to the mainstream.
- **Introduced species:** Nineteen exotic species have been recorded. Ten are represented by one or two records, likely bait-bucket releases or failed transplants. Common exotics include [[Common Carp]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]] and fathead minnow.
- **Striped bass:** Localized, mostly in the lower 5 km of the study area (from R.M. 453 to Separation Canyon). Movement upstream may be limited by rapids or cold hypolimnetic water.
- **Hybrids:** Flannelmouth × razorback sucker hybrids were recorded by Suttkus et al. (1976).

### Life History

- **Humpback chub:** Ten specimens were aged, with four age classes (IV, V, VI, IX). The oldest was nine years and 380 mm. Growth was fastest in year one, and the mean annual increment fell to 18.6 mm by year nine. Reproduction runs March–May (possibly into July) at 16–20 °C.
- **Speckled dace:** Maximum age class II. Mean increments were 50.1 mm (year one) and 24.1 mm (year two).
- **Flannelmouth sucker:** Ages zero to ten. The adult population clusters near 450 mm. Females in age class IV grew significantly faster than males. Only 3 of 387 tagged individuals were recaptured.
- **Bonytail (from literature):** Seven years of age in the Green River, surface-feeding on terrestrial insects.
- **Colorado squawfish (from literature):** Maturity at about 400 mm and six years, with records of more than 30 kg.

### Food Habits

- Speckled dace in the mainstream fed mostly on immature midges and black flies, which were 33–100% of the diet by season.
- Northern and southern tributary dace fed mainly on net-spinning caddisfly larvae, black flies and midges.
- Juvenile humpback chub fed on benthic midge larvae and detritus. Adults were observed eating *Cladophora*.
- Flannelmouth suckers fed mainly on scuds and midges (17–73% of the seasonal diet), with *Cladophora* and detritus also important.

### Invertebrates

- Six classes and 51 insect families were collected; 27 tributaries were quantitatively sampled from November 1977 to October 1978.
- Tributary productivity ranged from 0 to 1,214 mg dry weight/m² and from 0 to 138,666 individuals/m².
- Mainstream productivity was low, except in eddies and in the section between Glen Canyon Dam and the Little Colorado River. Grab samples in side eddies sometimes yielded thousands of individuals per square meter.
- Productivity and diversity were lowest in spring and summer, when runoff and flash floods scour the benthos. At [[Diamond Creek]], invertebrate biomass rose from 4.9 mg/m² in summer to 21.1 mg/m² in winter.
- Confluence sites were less productive and diverse than upstream sites, likely due to daily mainstream fluctuations and substrate instability.
- Two freshwater shrimp (*Gammarus lacustris*) were introduced: about 50,000 at Bright Angel Creek in 1932, and others at Lee Ferry in 1965. Shrimp now abound in the mainstream.

### Aquatic Plants and Diatoms

- A total of 131 macrophyte species from 41 families were collected, with ten new to the Grand Canyon flora.
- Mainstream plants were mostly emergent or bank species. Submerged plants were limited to common poolmat (*Zanichellia palustris*) and water weed (*Elodea canadensis*), the latter known only from floating fragments.
- Tributary richness ranged from 43 species (Paria River) to 11 (Shinumo Creek).
- Sixty-one diatom taxa from eight families were found at primary tributaries. Diatom density peaked in fall; the highest counts were at the Paria River (154,693.5 diatoms/cm² in fall 1978) and Tapeats Creek.

### Water Quality and Human Use

- Mainstream temperatures ranged from 6 to 15 °C; pre-dam extremes differed by 29.5 °C.
- The [[Little Colorado River]] is the only major tributary of the saline sodium chloride type.
- Bright Angel Creek was the subject of a 20-day creel census, and [[Diamond Creek]] angler pressure was estimated from outfitter questionnaires (14 respondents).
- Heavy recreational use at Shinumo Creek, Deer Creek and other tributaries was noted as an impact on bacterial quality and invertebrate communities.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- The study provides baseline data for future directed investigations, especially on endangered fishes.
- Trends in fish distribution and abundance can now be assessed against these data.
- The authors identify the Little Colorado River as critical nursery habitat for humpback chub, and point to rapids and cold water as possible barriers to striped bass.
