# The Maine Smallmouth

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #fishery-management #warm-water-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1955 publication by the [[Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Game]] presents findings from a comprehensive 2-year study (1952-1953) of [[Smallmouth Bass]] populations in [[Big Lake]], [[Washington County]], Maine. The study examined spawning behavior, growth rates, fishing pressure, economic value, and management recommendations for this non-native species introduced to Maine in 1869. Key findings include documentation of normal growth rates, minimal impact of harvest on spawning success, substantial economic contribution ($240,000 annually for a 3-month season), and evidence that the fishery is sustainable under existing regulations. The study also addresses parasites, food habits, habitat requirements, and interactions with other warm-water species including [[White Perch]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Chain Pickerel]], and various forage fishes.

## Background & Description

The [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*) is a member of the [[Sunfish Family]] (Centrarchidae) and represents a more evolutionarily advanced fish than soft-finned species like trout and salmon, possessing true spines and complicated scale structure. Originally distributed in the St. Lawrence, Ohio, and upper Mississippi Rivers and the [[Great Lakes]], the species was first introduced to Maine in 1869, before the [[Civil War]]. By 1900, smallmouth had colonized waters as far east as [[Washington County]].

**Physical Characteristics**: The smallmouth is distinguished from the [[Largemouth Bass]] by its jaw-opening, which does not extend behind the eye. The species possesses 36 different common names across its range, including "green trout" in some southern areas.

**Sensory Systems**: The bass possesses all five human senses. Vision is keen; smell functions through four small openings in the snout with water drawn through forward openings and ejected from rear openings. Taste organs are located in the mouth. Hearing is achieved through an inner ear (sensitive to high-pitched sounds) and the [[Lateral Line]] (sensitive to low-pitched sounds), consisting of nerve-endings in jelly-like substance exposed through openings from gill-cover to tail. The body surface is touch-sensitive and detects temperature changes.

**Internal Anatomy**: The jaw contains small inward-pointing teeth plus plate-like grinding teeth in the throat at gill level. The body cavity divides into two parts: a forward section containing the heart (which pumps blood through gills for oxygenation), and a larger rear section containing liver, stomach, intestine, kidneys, and reproductive organs. At the stomach base are approximately 12 finger-like projections (single in smallmouth, forked in largemouth). The kidney lies against the backbone and constantly releases water from the salt-containing blood. The [[Air Bladder]], a gas-filled balloon below the backbone, permits effortless hovering at any depth.

**Reproductive Biology**: Males and females are externally identical; sex determination requires examination of internal reproductive organs. Female organs are pale orange containing eggs (approximately 8,000 eggs per pound of body weight when ripe); ripe eggs are about the size of No. 7 shotgun pellets (10 per linear inch) and contain a visible oil globule. Male organs are grey-white and much thinner than female organs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Requirements**: [[Smallmouth Bass]] thrive in Maine's shallow warm-water lakes, which differ fundamentally from the deep, cold, clear lakes preferred by [[Salmon]] and [[Trout]]. Optimal smallmouth habitat characteristics include:

1. Large surface area (hundreds to thousands of acres; [[Big Lake]] = 11,520 acres)
2. Shallow depth ([[Big Lake]] averages only 15 feet)
3. Water rich in microscopic life (plankton, both plant and animal)
4. Abundant large aquatic plants (milfoils, pond-weeds, starwort, pipewort, water lilies) in mud-bottomed coves
5. Good shoreline gravel for nest-building (hen's-egg-sized, constituting nearly two-thirds of Big Lake's 63 shoreline miles)
6. Limited oxygen below 35 feet in summer (Big Lake's 70-foot hole contains only 2 parts per million oxygen vs. 5-14 ppm in surface waters to 30 feet)
7. Stable water levels during spring and early summer spawning

A third "marginal" or "in-between" lake type exists with barely sufficient oxygen and cold water for trout and salmon. Bass introduced to these marginal waters drive out the less-hardy salmonids, creating management conflicts.

**Spawning Ecology**: Males dominate spawning activities. Normal-growing males reach spawning maturity at approximately 9 inches length and 3 years age. Spawning sequence:

- **Nest Construction**: Males select coarse gravel beds sheltered by tilted trees or boulders, in water depths from 10 inches to 12 feet. Using tail fins as sweeps, males assume uphill positions and clear circular areas with diameters 1.5-2 times their body length. Construction begins when water reaches mid-50s°F.
- **Timing**: In [[Big Lake]], nest-building commenced May 28 (1953), peaked June 14-15 at 64°F water temperature (114 nests per measured mile, averaging one nest per 46 feet of shoreline, concentrated to 12 feet apart in optimal areas), and concluded by mid-July. At least 4,218 nests were constructed across 37 miles of ideal spawning habitat.
- **Courtship and Spawning**: Females remain in deep water until suitable temperatures are reached. Nest-guarding males are very aggressive. Males conduct females to nests; courtship culminates with both fish lying side-by-side over gravel. Females extrude eggs (approximately 10,000 per female); males emit fertilizing milt. Adhesive eggs attach to cleaned gravel. Females depart permanently after egg-laying.
- **Parental Care**: Males provide complete parental care, guarding eggs and washing them with fin movements to remove wastes and maintain oxygenation. Eggs hatch in 3-4 days; transparent young develop among gravel crevices for another 3-4 days, darkening in color before arising as "black-fry" swarms. Total development from egg-laying to fry emergence requires 7-14 days depending on temperature. Males guard young for 4-5 days post-emergence as fry spread along shoreline, then abandon them.

**Food Web**: Fry feed on tiny planktonic organisms, especially water fleas (small shrimp-like animals). At 1-2 inches length, diet shifts to insects and small fishes. Adult diet is predominantly fish and insects, with [[Crayfish]] (when present) eaten about 7 times more often than any other item.

**Seasonal Diet Patterns** ([[Big Lake]] study):
- **June**: Insects (dragon fly, damsel fly, stone fly, May fly nymphs) throughout season; many young [[Chain Pickerel]] consumed
- **Late July-August**: Young [[Yellow Perch]], [[Sunfish]], chubs, shiners, and suckers form major portion; fish consumed 3 times more than insects in August
- **September**: Insects again form largest diet portion
- Land beetles and ants occasionally consumed; frogs found occasionally (probably angler-provided)

**Associated Species**: Maine bass lakes typically contain [[White Perch]], [[Chain Pickerel]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Sunfish]], suckers, [[Brown Bullhead]] (hornpout), chubs, and minnows. [[Largemouth Bass]] occurs occasionally but is not widely distributed in Maine. Minnows and young of yellow perch, pickerel, suckers, and sunfish provide best forage. Small bass also provide food for larger bass; bass-on-bass predation may exceed angler harvest.

**Competitive Interactions**: [[White Perch]] definitively competes with bass, consuming similar foods while producing vast numbers of offspring (approximately 40,000 eggs per half-pound female body weight vs. 8,000 per pound in bass). White perch grows very slowly (over 10 years to reach 12 inches vs. 5 years for bass). Large white perch numbers consume available food and become stunted. Young white perch were never found in bass stomachs. In bass-managed lakes, white perch is a competitor species; angler harvest should be encouraged to reduce food/space competition and improve white perch growth rates. White perch feeds primarily dusk to dawn, resulting in light angler harvest and disproportionate fishing pressure on bass.

**Parasites**: Three parasites documented:

1. **Yellow Grub** (*Clinostomum marginatum*): Unsightly but harmless to humans and lightly-infected fish. Varies from pinhead to match-head size, conspicuous in late August. Life cycle: adult lives in [[Great Blue Heron]] throat → eggs released from bird's mouth → active young worm enters snail → multiplies in snail → offspring leave snail and bore into bass flesh → become walled in tiny sacks → if fish eaten by heron, worm digests out and resides in bird throat. Most conspicuous around gill region but occurs on all body parts. Also found in [[Brook Trout]], [[Yellow Perch]], and [[Chain Pickerel]] gills. Adult also found in [[Double-Crested Cormorant]] (shag) and probably other fish-eating birds.

2. **Black Spot** (*Uvulifer ambloplitis*): Usually found in skin and fins, occasionally in outer flesh. Life cycle similar to yellow grub but involves [[Belted Kingfisher]]: kingfisher → snail → fish → kingfisher. Black coloration comes from bass's own coloring material, not the parasite.

3. **Bass Tapeworm** (*Proteocephalus ambloplitis*): Most serious parasite, harmful to bass but not humans. Adult lives in finger-like intestinal projections behind stomach. Life cycle: eggs shed in fish droppings → tiny water animals (similar to water fleas) eat eggs → eaten by young bass → young worm bores through intestine and locates among body organs → if young bass eaten by larger bass, worm digests out and enters finger-like projections to release eggs. If young bass NOT eaten and grows to maturity with worm in body cavity, worm feeds on developing reproductive organs (egg-sacs or milt-sacs), often destroying reproductive power. Can be carried by [[Yellow Perch]] and sunfishes. Fortunately absent from many Maine lakes. Introduction risk from emptied bait pails or unnecessary stocking programs.

**Growth and Mortality**: Average [[Big Lake]] bass growth (based on 736 fish, 700+ for detailed growth studies):
- End of Year 1: ~3 inches
- Year 2: 6 inches  
- Year 3: 8.5 inches
- Year 4: 11 inches
- Year 5: 13 inches

Males and females grow at identical rates. Only about 1 in 1,000 bass born each year lives to age 4. Conservatively, about one-half of all bass in a lake die annually from natural causes, whether fished or not. Oldest known bass from scale-mark records: 14+ years. In northern range, bass over 2 pounds is large; over 3 pounds is a real prize; over 4 pounds extremely rare.

**Scale Analysis**: Bass scales bear annual growth records. Scales are round-edged shingles with tiny comb-like projections, lapped by tight skin folds. Round-edged margins face backward (tail direction) offering minimal water resistance. Forward portion (hidden by overlap) is hand-shaped and embedded forward into body. Bass grows replacement scales when lost. Scales grow proportionally with fish. Winter cessation of feeding creates "year marks" (broken lines) on scales. Counting year marks determines age. Requires 40x magnification; accuracy good to 5 years with practice; older fish require much patience and experience as yearly growth decreases and winter marks compress.

## Key Findings

**Study Design and Rationale**: [[Big Lake]] was selected for the 2-year study (1952-1953) because: (1) it represents one of several important Maine bass fishing centers; (2) it has supported good smallmouth populations for over 50 years (since at least 1900); (3) it has been managed since at least 1934 under a law permitting capture of three legal bass per day on flies only from June 1-20, allowing assessment of spawning male harvest impacts; (4) it attracts many fishermen from the Eastern United States, permitting determination of dollar-value and fishing quality; (5) Commissioner [[Roland H. Cobb]] identified need for factual knowledge about Maine bass (most fishery study had focused on trout and salmon) and need to evaluate introduction requests and "strain weakening" claims.

**Spawning Study Methods**: Most accurate observations made at night using underwater light (12" x 12" stainless steel sheet with bayonet socket and automobile lamp powered by 6-volt battery, providing 20-foot radius definition in calm water). Nesting males timid during day often showed no fear of night light, permitting close examination (light moved to within one inch of snout; one male allowed nose-rubbing with light). Females showed great timidity. Rippled surfaces caused distortion; wave-affected observations were discarded.

**Spawning Findings**: 
- First nest-building: May 28 (water temperature mid-50s°F)
- June 7: One nesting male per 300 feet of well-graveled shoreline
- June 1-10: Storms and high winds; surface temperatures fluctuated 57-63°F
- **Peak spawning**: June 14-15 at 64°F (114 nests per measured mile; average one nest per 46 feet shoreline; concentrated areas had nests only 12 feet apart)
- Average nest depth: 3 feet (determined by best gravel location at average 8 feet below surface)
- Last nest observation: June 30
- Last dead-ripe female captured: July 7
- **Total nests constructed**: At least 4,218 across 37 miles of ideal habitat (63 total shoreline miles; figure excludes shallow gravel bars and sites with double potential used successively by two males)

Preferred nesting sites combined good gravel with cover (boulders, tilted trees, fallen trees, spreading hardwood branches). Best combinations reserved by largest males through dominance. Some sites used twice (example: large male's fry scattered by June 15; same nest occupied July 1 by smaller male whose fry just risen).

**Fishing Pressure and Harvest**:
- **Sample size**: 1,358 fishermen interviewed over 2 years
- **Residency**: Less than 1% (11 anglers) were Maine residents; 99% non-residents from as far as Florida and Nebraska; bulk from Massachusetts, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania
- **Catch rate**: Slightly better than one bass per hour for guided, experienced anglers
- **Release rate**: 9 out of 10 bass caught were released
- **Fishing density**: Even on maximum pressure days, anglers had at least 500 acres per personal fishing area
- **Catch variability**: Ranged from zero to 10 bass per hour; best recorded catch was 70 legal-sized bass in 8 hours by skilled lady-angler with guide (largest 3.5 pounds, 19 inches total length; all released)
- **Annual harvest**: Fewer than 4,000 bass actually removed from [[Big Lake]] annually; 75% are 5 years old or younger
- **Sustainability**: With over 4,000 nests constructed annually, each conservatively contributing at least two bass reaching age 5, the population more than holds its own against current fishing pressure

**Economic Valuation**:
- **Total annual value**: Minimum $240,000 for June 1 to Labor Day (3-month period) to two communities with combined population of only 1,044 persons (~$80,000 per month)
- **Cost per pound**: Average angler paid $11 per pound for harvested bass (range: $2 to $87 per pound)
- **Expenditure categories**: Licenses, food and lodging, guides' wages, boats and motors, bait/tackle/supplies, travel costs to camp
- **Typical resort economics**: One well-run resort averaged 340 guest weeks annually (one person for 7 days). Minimum daily cost per angler: $23 (meals, lodging, guide service) = $161 per guest week. Conservative estimate (assuming only half employed guides): 340 guest weeks worth ~$40,000, excluding licenses, bait, tackle, supplies, and travel costs. Minimum of 10 major resort units in [[Big Lake]] area plus several camp rental operations.
- **Drawing power**: In [[Princeton]] area, 98% of fishermen were bass fishermen (2% sought trout/salmon in nearby cold-water lakes). In [[Grand Lake Stream]] (3 miles from [[Big Lake]], at foot of [[West Grand Lake]]), one resort reported 80% bass fishermen; no resort reported less than 50% bass fishermen. Surprising given [[West Grand Lake]]'s reputation as salmon and lake trout center (famous [[Grand Lake Stream]] landlocked salmon "race" transported to Emperor of Japan's private pools). Bass predominance due to: (1) high esteem as game fish by visiting fishermen, (2) greater catchability than salmon during peak visitation period (school closing through Labor Day).

**Growth Rate Analysis**: Normal bass growth determined by averaging all bass growth rates recorded in fisheries literature. [[Big Lake]] bass showed normal growth rates (Figure 1, based on 736 fish). Growth rate importance: abnormally slow growth indicates overcrowding with underfed fish, requiring eased regulations to encourage harvest of small fish for normal growth restoration. Ironically, crowded lakes with small fish often called "fished-out" when problem is actually under-fishing.

**"Nature's Table" Concept**: In any lake, Nature sets a fixed number of feeding places. When bass first introduced to ideal lake, they grow abnormally fast (many empty places, extra meals easily obtained). Fish multiply rapidly; soon all places taken, each bass gets just enough to eat, growth becomes normal, and most fishermen satisfied. If all fish lived and kept multiplying, food shortage would develop; each fish gets only partial meal, causing abnormal slow growth (stunting). A few big fish might feed at expense of stunted brothers. This condition often caused by fishermen making laws to prevent reasonable harvest, forgetting about growth rates
