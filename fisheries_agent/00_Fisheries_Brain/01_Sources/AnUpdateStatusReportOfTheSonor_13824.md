# Status Report of Sonoran Topminnow and Desert Pupfish in Arizona (1990-1991)

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This status report documents field surveys of [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) populations in Arizona from May 1990 to August 1991. Both species are federally listed as endangered, having declined severely from historically widespread distributions throughout southern Arizona. The report evaluates natural populations, introduced populations, and potential reintroduction sites. [[Gila Topminnow]] (*P. o. occidentalis*) maintained 8 natural populations and 19 successful introduced wild populations. [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*P. o. sonoriensis*) had 1 natural and 14 introduced populations. [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*C. m. eremus*) persisted at one natural site, while [[Lower Colorado River Pupfish]] (*C. m. macularius*) existed at 3 introduced sites. Primary threats include habitat modification, predation by [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), flooding, and desiccation. The report emphasizes that both natural and introduced populations continue to decline, requiring aggressive site acquisition, renovation, and stocking programs.

## Background & Description

[[Sonoran Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]] were historically among the most abundant fishes in southern Arizona. Both species were federally listed as endangered (1967 and 1986 respectively) due to severe population declines. The [[Sonoran Topminnow]] comprises two subspecies: [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*) from the [[Gila River]] drainage and [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. sonoriensis*) from the [[Yaqui River]] drainage near Douglas, Arizona. [[Desert Pupfish]] also includes two forms: the [[Lower Colorado River]] form (*Cyprinodon m. macularius*) historically in the Gila River drainage, and [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon m. eremus*) endemic to [[Quitobaquito Springs]] in the [[Sonoyta River]] drainage.

Habitat modification, destruction, and predation by non-native fishes—particularly [[Mosquitofish]]—caused these declines. The recovery program represents a cooperative effort among Arizona Game and Fish Department, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Forest Service, and Bureau of Land Management. The monitoring program distinguished between natural populations (sites with fish prior to human transplants) and introduced populations (resulting from stockings). Sites were categorized by quality: Category 1 (expected to persist indefinitely without support), Category 2 (requiring anthropogenic support), Category 3 (low numbers or limited habitat), and Category 4 (threatened by exotic fish).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Sonoran Topminnow]] populations naturally undergo periodic extirpation followed by recolonization and explosive breeding, requiring diverse habitat types for refuge from flooding, drying, habitat modification, and exotic introductions. The species demonstrates metapopulation dynamics where individual sites function as metapopulations within a drainage-scale population. Recolonization is essential for long-term persistence; seven new topminnow populations colonized from nearby sites between 1985-1989.

Natural [[Gila Topminnow]] populations showed variable mosquitofish dominance. At [[Sharp Spring]], topminnow coexisted with mosquitofish in lower pools while upper pools remained mosquitofish-free. Recent mtDNA analysis revealed that Sharp Spring maintains the highest genetic heterogeneity among natural populations, correlating with fitness indices. [[Cienega Creek]] supported the largest topminnow population in Arizona, with over 8 miles of mature cienega and stream habitat shared with [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*) and [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), with no exotic fish present.

Environmental threats varied by site. Flooding eliminated populations at [[Horse Creek]], [[Bain Spring]], [[Upper Horrell Spring]], and [[Humbug Creek]]. Winter flooding at [[Cow Creek]] reduced the population to four individuals by spring 1991. Desiccation threatened smaller sites. [[Bylas Springs]] showed 96% mosquitofish dominance by 1991. Groundwater pumping in Mexico reduced artesian well flows at [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]], undermining stability of [[Yaqui Topminnow]] populations.

## Key Findings

**Gila Topminnow Status**: All 8 natural populations persisted through 1991, though 5 remained threatened by mosquitofish. Three populations ([[Cottonwood Spring]], [[Monkey Spring]], [[Cienega Creek]]) were considered secure. Of 192 introductions into wild habitats, only 19 (10%) were successful in 1991. Four successful sites ranked Category 1, nine Category 2, four Category 3, and two Category 4. Only Category 1 populations were expected to persist indefinitely without support, yet three Category 1 sites failed between 1989-1991, none lasting longer than 10 years.

**Population Failures**: Thirteen wild introduced populations failed between 1989-1991, primarily due to flooding. An additional 13 failed between 1987-1989, mostly from desiccation. Failed sites included [[Humbug Creek]], [[Horse Creek]], [[Upper Horrell Spring]], [[Cave Creek]], [[Bain Spring]], and [[Indian Spring]]. [[Middle Spring]] (natural population) was considered extirpated after fish were absent in two consecutive surveys.

**Genetic Considerations**: Most pre-1985 introductions used [[Monkey Springs]] stock. Post-1985 efforts attempted to use [[Sharp Springs]] stock (highest genetic heterogeneity), but only one successful wild population ([[Heron Spring]]) derived from this stock. [[Salt Creek]], from [[Middle Springs]] stock, faced imminent extirpation due to 94% mosquitofish dominance. Genetic diversity in introduced populations remained inadequate to safeguard natural population diversity.

**Yaqui Topminnow**: Fourteen wild populations were successful in 1991 (12 Category 2, 1 Category 3, 1 Category 4). Only [[Tule Pond]] harbored a natural population. [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] populations faced challenges from groundwater pumping in Mexico and lack of complete federal control. Mosquitofish persisted in [[Black Draw]]. Only [[Leslie Creek]] supported topminnows for more than 10 years. Recovery plan delisting criteria were not met.

**Desert Pupfish**: [[Quitobaquito Springs]] maintained a secure natural population of [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] with no exotic fish collected in 1991. Five introduced wild populations were successful: three containing [[Lower Colorado River Pupfish]] ([[Howard Well]], [[Yerba Mansa]], [[Cold Springs]]) and two containing fish of questionable genetic integrity ([[Bog Hole]], [[Finley Tank]]). The latter two populations prevented further reintroductions and required renovation. [[Lower Colorado River Pupfish]] were eliminated from Arizona prior to the 1950s.

**Management Recommendations**: The report emphasized need for: (1) aggressive site selection and stocking programs following recovery plan completion; (2) acquisition and renovation of large, complex habitats supporting metapopulation dynamics; (3) stocking numerous sites with custodial populations as short-term refugia; (4) priority focus on drainages including [[Babocomari Creek]], [[Arivaca Creek]], [[Sonoita Creek]], and [[San Pedro River]]; (5) renovation of [[Bog Hole]] and [[Finley Tank]] to eliminate genetically contaminated pupfish; (6) preservation of Sharp Springs genetic stock through careful management; (7) creation of fish barriers and renovation of systems like [[Santa Cruz River]] and [[Sonoita Creek]].

**Recolonization Dynamics**: Four years of monitoring documented seven new populations colonized from nearby sites, demonstrating natural recolonization capacity when sites are in proximity. Successful recolonization occurred at distances allowing fish movement during high flows. The report concluded that topminnow site quality may be determined primarily by immigration possibility rather than habitat characteristics alone.

**Site Evaluation**: Biological priority levels for future introductions were: (1) large sites with complex drainages allowing recolonization; (2) multiple small sites in close proximity; (3) isolated sites for custodial populations; (4) isolated sites whose previous failure resulted from preventable disturbance. The search for "perfect" sites was deemed counterproductive to preserving remaining genetic heritage.
