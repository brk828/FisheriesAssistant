# Phylogenetic Trends in the Genus Dryops (Dryopidae, Coleoptera)

#entomology #coleoptera #dryopidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented by [[August Wilhelm Steffan]] at the XI International Congress of Entomology (Vienna, 1960), analyses the phylogeny of the beetle genus [[Dryops]] (family [[Dryopidae]]). The genus contains 35 species according to Bollow (1938, 1939). Based on micromorphology of male genitalia, the species are divided into two subgenera, [[Subgenus Dryops]] and [[Subgenus Yrdops]]. Each subgenus forms a progressive evolutionary series in which genital complexity increases from one species to the next. The author argues that these series reflect orthogenesis, and that they run parallel to a directed range expansion from warmer to cooler habitats. He proposes that the trends result from orthoselection acting through pleiotropic genes, but he admits that this cannot be proven without genetic experiments.

## Background & Description

- **Genus and family:** [[Dryops]] Olivier 1791 is the nominate genus of Dryopidae. Its adults are tied to water. Larvae develop in damp soil or in the immediate vicinity of water, in mulm, detritus (Genist) or aquatic plants. Some adults spend most of their lives in water, while others visit it actively for feeding or egg-laying.
- **Subgenera:** Steffan (1961b) divided the genus into:
  - [[Subgenus Dryops]] (Olivier 1791), whose species have a ventral ridge (Ventralleiste) on the aedeagus.
  - [[Subgenus Yrdops]] (Steffan 1961), whose species lack ventral ridges and have a membrane sack no wider than the corpus.
- **Ecological separation:** *Dryops* s.str. species mostly live in and around standing waters and mires. *Yrdops* species live mainly in and around fast-flowing streams and rivers.
- **Sample of species:** The paper names eight species labelled A to H, in ascending order of genital complexity:
  - A: an unnamed species (described as probably a *species nova*)
  - B: *striatellus* (Fairmaire & Brisout 1859)
  - C: *luridus* (Erichson 1847)
  - D: *ernesti* (Des Gozis 1886)
  - E: *griseus* (Erichson 1847)
  - F: *similaris* (Bollow 1936)
  - G: *anglicanus* (Edwards 1909)
  - H: *auriculatus* (Geoffroy 1785), the most advanced species of the Dryops series
- **Other taxa in the paper:** *Dryops* (*Yrdops*) *striatopunctatus* (Heer 1841) is the most advanced species of the *Yrdops* series.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat of primitive species:** The "primitive" species of the *Dryops* series occur mainly in the Mediterranean region. They live in and on decaying plant material, in detritus, and in wrack lines near lowland waters. Rensch (1954) classed them as "Bewohner von Kleinstbiotopen" (inhabitants of micro-habitats), and they have relatively small body volumes.
- **Range expansion, *Dryops* s.str.:** Intermediate species (C, D, E, F) are centred in Central Europe. The most advanced species (G, H) extend furthest north, to Lapland. Species show an increasing preference for standing waters and mires, with *anglicanus* the clearest example. All species in this series remain animals of lowlands and low mountain ranges.
- **Range expansion, *Yrdops*:** Primitive species are restricted to lowlands. More advanced species enter low mountain regions. The most advanced species occur only in the upper zones of the Alps and adjacent high mountains. Ecologically, colonisation moved from larger rivers to smaller streams. The most advanced species live only in high-mountain streams, and *striatopunctatus* occurs even in alpine torrents.
- **Directional dispersal:** Steffan summarises the genus as showing a single dispersal tendency, from warmer to cooler habitats. In *Dryops* s.str. this occurs geographically (Mediterranean to Central to Northern Europe). In *Yrdops* it occurs orographically-ecologically (lowlands to low mountains to high mountains).

## Key Findings

1. **Two parallel phylogenetic series.** Within each subgenus, species can be arranged so that each successor shows further development and greater differentiation of the male genitalia (aedeagus and parameres). The author notes a few exceptions.
2. **Progressive aedeagus complication in *Dryops* s.str.:**
   - Primitive species (A–D) have a simple ventral ridge. In species B–D, the membrane sack bears small tooth-like scales.
   - Intermediate stages (E–G) show the ventral ridge split into three sclerites. Teeth enlarge, then fuse with the fold funnel. The distal membrane sack is reduced.
   - The endpoint (H, *auriculatus*) has fused teeth and ventral ridge, an oversized "Cardinalzahn" (cardinal tooth), and a compact sclerite complex at the distal end.
   - Parameres of advanced species (F, G, H) develop strong subapical inner swellings.
3. **Parallel trend in *Yrdops*:** Scales on the membrane sacks are replaced by teeth. These teeth are smaller in advanced species but more numerous. Basal and apophysis structures also become more complex, and paramere tips become thickened and differentiated.
4. **Opposite trend in female ovipositors:** In *Yrdops*, primitive species have well-developed ovipositors, some species lack them entirely, and the most advanced species have weak, slender ovipositors that are probably no longer functional. In *Dryops* s.str., the female structures are not described as reversed in the same way; the author notes instead that male genital complexity does not correlate with female structures.
5. **No correlation with external morphology:** No stepwise correlation was found between genital complexity and external body structures. The primitive species of both series look similar externally, while the endpoints differ strongly. *auriculatus* has a strongly arched body and longitudinally striated elytra. *striatopunctatus* is completely flattened and has coarse rows of large square punctures on the elytra.
6. **Body size correlates with genital complexity:** In both series, species with simpler genitalia are much smaller than species with more complex genitalia. The most advanced species are the largest, or nearly the largest, within their relatives. The author cites this as an example of [[Cope's Rule]].
7. **Interpretation as orthogenesis:** Following Stammer (1959), the author describes the series as orthogenetic, that is, directed by the organism's own internal laws. He acknowledges that the genital structures show no functional link to female organs, which rules out sexual-mechanical isolation as the selective mechanism.
8. **Stabilisation trend:** In *Dryops* s.str., the membrane sack becomes progressively stabilised. In *anglicanus* and *auriculatus*, the distal membrane sack has become a strongly sclerotised tubular body. The author speculates this may give a copulatory advantage, but he notes that selection pressure cannot be measured (Remane 1959).
9. **Causal hypothesis:** The author proposes that [[Orthoselection]] acting through [[Pleiotropy]] links the genetic basis of genital complexity to physiological and morphological traits involved in dispersal into new habitats. Genital structures are considered selectively neutral, so they change as a correlated by-product of selection on other traits. He concedes that identifying the primary target of selection would require genetic experiments.
10. **Conclusion:** The author rejects autonomous developmental forces without a physiological basis (following Rensch 1954). He argues that the observed trends arose from orthoselection during directional colonisation of new habitats, and that the selected trait is often a correlated form, function or behaviour rather than the visible organ itself.
