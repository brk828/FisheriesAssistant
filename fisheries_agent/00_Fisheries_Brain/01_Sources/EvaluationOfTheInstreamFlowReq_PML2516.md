# Evaluation of the Instream Flow Requirements of Native Fishes of Aravaipa Creek, Arizona, by the Incremental Methodology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 report by [[Paul R. Turner]] and [[Robert J. Tafanelli]] (New Mexico State University) evaluates the instream flow needs of native fishes in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, using the [[Incremental Methodology]] (IFG4 hydraulic simulation and probability-of-use curves). The study concentrated on [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), which were judged most sensitive to flow change. Field work ran in 1980–1981 at a 350-foot reach on the [[George Whittell Wildlife Preserve]] owned by [[Defenders of Wildlife]]. Weighted usable area (WUA) peaked near 20 ft³/sec for both species, and the authors recommended a minimum flow of 13 ft³/sec at the BLM East Aravaipa gaging station, with a desired optimum of about 23 ft³/sec during late spring through early fall.

## Background & Description

**Study origin.** Funding came from a Phase II instream flow study of Arizona streams by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), which was terminated under a joint agreement with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS). The New Mexico State University team, which had just finished a Phase II study of three New Mexico rivers, reused the remaining Arizona funds (about $1,125 of $15,000 initially allocated to AGFD). Original AGFD study sites on Black River and West Clear Creek were destroyed by 1979 floods, so the team restarted site selection.

**Study species.** Both focal species are small, sedentary-to-moderately mobile cyprinids endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]]. The status of both species was being reviewed by USFWS for "threatened" listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, and Aravaipa Creek was being considered as critical habitat.

**Native fish assemblage.** Aravaipa Creek retained most of its original physical character and nearly all native fauna, with seven native fishes recorded (per [[Minckley]] 1973). These include five cyprinids (spikedace, loach minnow, roundtail chub, longfin dace, speckled dace) and two catostomids (Sonora sucker, desert sucker). Study species were prioritized because they were judged more flow-sensitive than the others.

**Study area.** The reach was about 3/4 mile upstream of Turkey Creek and was characterized by sand and gravel substrate with some cobble. Channel width ranged from 10 to 26 ft, and mean column velocity ranged from about 1.0 to 3.0 ft/sec. The floodplain supported a mixed broadleaf community including cottonwood, willow, sycamore, and walnut.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology.** Minckley (1981) found that the downstream USGS gage peaked in winter (December–March) and summer (July–September), with lowest flows in April–June. The frequency distribution of mean daily discharge was bimodal at 5 and 10 ft³/sec. A flood of 16,200 ft³/sec occurred on December 18, 1978.
- **Water management.** Temporary sand-and-gravel diversion dams irrigate several hundred acres above the USGS gage near Mammoth, Arizona. The new BLM East Aravaipa gage (operational from August 1, 1980) became the main monitoring point for flows entering the canyon.
- **Flow conditions 1981–1982.** Mean daily discharge at East Aravaipa was 19.7 ft³/sec in water year 1981 and 14.7 ft³/sec in 1982. Monthly means fell to 10.2–11.0 ft³/sec during June, July, October, and November 1982. Flows dropped to 7.5–13.6 ft³/sec in June and July 1982.
- **Riparian and vegetation.** Water cress was present in summer 1981 but absent in May 1980, likely following January–March 1980 floods.
- **Substrate dynamics.** Sand and gravel substrates move readily during modest flow increases from thunderstorms, which complicated the hydraulic modelling.
- **Habitat associations.** Spikedace favor shallow riffles with sand and fine gravel and moderate laminar flow. Loach minnow favor cobble and gravel, a requirement linked to spawning and egg attachment on the undersides of flattened cobble.

## Key Findings

**Methods and data**

- Field measurements were taken at discharges of 11.1, 16.5, and 24.4 ft³/sec. The IFG4 simulation covered 5 to 40 ft³/sec.
- Habitat data came from electrofishing in Aravaipa Creek and the Gila River, New Mexico, in summer and fall 1981.
- Sample sizes for spikedace: 54 adults from the Gila River and 140 adults from Aravaipa Creek; 7 juveniles (August 27–28, 1981). Loach minnow: 141 adults from Aravaipa Creek, 112 adults from the Gila River, and 14 juveniles from Aravaipa Creek.
- Four of six transects were used in the final IFG4 simulation because streambed movement invalidated transects 1, 5, and 6.

**Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*)**

- Most commonly collected at mean column velocities of 1.3–2.7 ft/sec; rarely found below 0.6 or above 3.0 ft/sec.
- Preferred depths of 0.60–0.75 ft; not collected below 0.2 ft or above 1.2 ft.
- Preferred sand and gravel bottoms.
- Frequency-based depth preference peaked strongly at 0.5–0.6 ft.

**Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)**

- Collected across velocities of 0 to 3.6 ft/sec but preferred about 2.0 ft/sec.
- Collected at depths of 0.1–1.0 ft, most commonly 0.4–0.6 ft.
- Strong preference for cobble and gravel substrates; avoided sand- and fine-gravel-dominated areas.
- Juveniles were found at depths of 4.0–8.4 inches and velocities of 0.9–2.5 ft/sec.
- Eggs were attached to the underside of flattened cobble (average 5.3 × 7.1 inches in New Mexico). Cobble must not be embedded by fines.

**Weighted usable area and discharge**

- Peak WUA for adult spikedace and loach minnow occurred at about 20 ft³/sec. WUA decreased rapidly below 10 ft³/sec.
- Using the alternate frequency-based weighting factors reduced WUA by 30–70% but did not shift the peak.
- Loach minnow WUA was 30–45% lower under the alternate factors than under the computer-drawn curves.
- Suckers and roundtail chub showed WUA varying by less than 10% across 5–25 ft³/sec, so the authors considered them less useful for flow recommendations.

**Recommendations**

- An optimum flow of about 23 ft³/sec is desirable from late spring through early fall, with the gage reading adjusted by +3 ft³/sec.
- A non-consumptive water right guaranteeing a minimum of 13 ft³/sec at the BLM East Aravaipa gage should be obtained. Purchase of private water rights upstream may be considered.
- Any additional water withdrawal above the primitive area during low-flow periods or dry years should be opposed.
- Future studies should develop substrate preference curves, spawning and fry preference curves, and address the embeddedness of spawning cobble.

**Methodological concerns**

- Strong correlation between depth and velocity indicates an environmental bias; the curves describe tolerances within a narrow environmental range, not true preferences.
- Consultant Ken Bovee (CIFSG) advised that the curves should not be transferred to streams very different from those sampled. Consultant Jon Souder (USFWS) noted that marginal curves were comparable to trout curves in common use.
- Electrofishing sampling bias was rated 9.5/10 for loach minnow, 8.0 for spikedace, 3.5–4.0 for suckers, and 1.0 for roundtail chub.
- Roundtail chub, Sonora sucker, and desert sucker data came from Burro and Francis creeks (Turner and Tafanelli 1983) and were treated as less reliable.

**Conclusions**

Aravaipa Creek's spikedace and loach minnow populations depend on adequate summer and fall base flows. Maintaining at least 13 ft³/sec at the East Aravaipa gage, and ideally about 23 ft³/sec during low-flow seasons, is necessary to protect preferred habitat. Water development on the [[Gila River]] and [[San Francisco River]] was identified as a serious threat to native fishes.
