# Sodium Cyanide as a Fish Poison

#ichthyology #freshwater-fisheries #Centrarchidae #toxicology

## Executive Summary

This 1958 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report by W. R. Bridges evaluates **sodium cyanide (NaCN)**, sold commercially as "Cyanegg," as a candidate fish poison for freshwater fishery management. Unlike [[Rotenone]], the standard fish toxicant of the era, sodium cyanide at concentrations of **1 p.p.m.** produced complete kills of multiple warm-water fish species while permitting **live removal and revival** of affected fish if they were promptly transferred to fresh, untreated water. The compound was inexpensive (~68 cents/acre-foot), easy to apply, and dissipated from small ponds within about 4 days, allowing rapid restocking. The study's most novel contribution is the demonstration that sodium cyanide could be used as a **non-lethal fish salvage tool** — enabling collection, transfer, and marking of live fish populations during pond management operations, a use case not achievable with rotenone.

## Background & Description

- **Study purpose:** To evaluate sodium cyanide against three criteria for an ideal fish poison: (1) high toxicity to fish, (2) reasonable dissipation time, and (3) the ability for affected fish to be revived if removed promptly.
- **Institutional context:** Conducted at the Cooperative Fishery Research Laboratory, [[Southern Illinois University]], as part of a Master of Science thesis; published by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service due to broader fishery management relevance.
- **Chemical form:** Sodium cyanide was administered as "Cyanegg" pellets (≥96% NaCN), dissolved fresh daily for laboratory work.
- **Prior literature:** Existing cyanide toxicity research (Burdick & Lipschuetz 1948; Doudoroff 1956; Ellis 1937; others) focused on **industrial pollution effects**, not deliberate fishery poisoning applications — this study fills that gap.
- **Species tested (laboratory):** [[Longnose Gar]] (*Lepisosteus osseus*), [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus natalis*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*).
- **Field sites:** Eight farm ponds/pools near Carbondale, Illinois, containing diverse assemblages including [[Golden Shiner]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Swamp Darter]], [[Gambusia]], [[Flier Sunfish]], [[Steel-colored Minnow]], [[Black Crappie]], [[White Crappie]], [[Warmouth]], [[Bluegill]], [[Smallmouth Buffalo]], and [[Grass Pickerel]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** Small warm-water farm ponds (est. volumes ~6.0–9.7 × 10⁴ cu. ft.) typical of midwestern U.S. agricultural landscapes, and controlled laboratory aquaria (25–100 liter glass tanks) using Carbondale municipal water as diluent.
- **Water chemistry variables tested:** pH range (5.5–9.7) and temperature range (42–82°F), both manipulated to assess effects on toxicity, since **hydrocyanic acid (HCN)**, not the cyanide ion, is believed to be the primary toxic agent (per Doudoroff 1956; Wilne 1950). Lower pH increases the proportion of free HCN, theoretically increasing toxicity.
- **Non-target impacts:** Sodium cyanide at working concentrations showed **little observable toxicity to non-fish organisms** — frogs, snakes, turtles, and aquatic insects were unaffected; tadpoles surfaced but survived. Phytoplankton density appeared unaffected or even increased post-treatment, suggesting selective toxicity toward fish relative to other aquatic taxa.
- **Human/domestic animal safety:** At the 1 p.p.m. treatment concentration, no toxic effects were observed in a herd of ponies drinking from a treated pond; mammals would need to ingest 2–3 times their body weight in treated water to receive a lethal dose. However, the raw compound and hydrogen cyanide gas pose serious human health hazards, requiring trained technician handling.
- **Dissipation dynamics:** Toxicity in ponds lasted approximately **4 days**, supporting rapid pond restocking cycles — an ecologically favorable trait compared to longer-persisting piscicides.

## Key Findings

**Toxicity**
- 1 p.p.m. NaCN produced **100% mortality** across all six laboratory species tested.
- At 0.5 p.p.m., all green sunfish died, but **no goldfish** died — confirming goldfish as notably cyanide-resistant among tested species.
- Relative resistance ranking (most to least resistant): **goldfish > carp > yellow bullhead > green sunfish ≈ largemouth bass**.
- Low temperatures and high pH both **extended** time-to-death, though kills remained effective even at pH 9.7 and 42°F.
- Estimated cost: **~68 cents per acre-foot** of water treated.

**Duration of Toxicity**
- Laboratory: toxic effects of NaCN solutions lasted only **48–72 hours** before losing lethality to green sunfish.
- Field: caged green sunfish placed in treated ponds **96 hours** post-application showed complete survival, confirming a toxicity window of roughly 4 days.
- Restocked golden shiners survived well when reintroduced to a treated pond after 4 days.

**Removal & Revival of Fishes**
- Fish exposed to 1 p.p.m. NaCN began surfacing within **5–30 minutes**; removed fish placed in fresh water **fully revived within 5–30 minutes**.
- Maximum practical removal period for [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes/bass) was approximately **1 hour**; more resistant species (carp, goldfish, buffalo) had longer removal windows — goldfish removal in one pond lasted **over 7 hours**.
- Species-specific surfacing time groups:
  - **5–15 min:** swamp darter, crappies, flier sunfish, warmouth, green sunfish, bluegill, largemouth bass, steel-colored minnow, grass pickerel, longnose gar.
  - **15–30 min:** golden shiner.
  - **20–40 min:** yellow and black bullheads.
  - **30–60 min:** carp and smallmouth buffalo.
- Field removal survival was excellent: e.g., in Pond 5, ~15,000 golden shiners/minnows were seined out with only 4 losses over a week of observation; in Pond 7, only 3 of 250 removed crappie died over 10 days.
- Behavioral stages under 1 p.p.m. NaCN exposure (green sunfish pattern): normal (0–10 min) → surfacing/vigorous swimming (3–30 min) → alternating surfacing/sinking (20–50 min) → final loss of equilibrium and death (>50 min).

**Other Observations**
- No correlation found between test solution volume and toxic effect.
- Fish size had **little to no influence** on susceptibility or surfacing time.
- Sodium cyanide application via **outboard motor wash** (large ponds) or **dip net dissolution** (small ponds) required minimal time/equipment, reflecting high water solubility and rapid diffusion.

**Discussion & Management Implications**
- Sodium cyanide satisfied all three evaluative criteria: high toxicity, short persistence, and (uniquely) fish revivability.
- Suggested applications: (1) live fish collection for research, (2) harvest/transfer operations in hatcheries, (3) aid in population marking studies.
- Major caveat: extreme human toxicity risk from ingestion or HCN gas inhalation mandates restriction of use to trained technicians, with appropriate safety equipment (gas masks).
- Sodium cyanide is positioned as a valuable complementary tool to [[Rotenone]] in fishery management, particularly where **live fish salvage** is a management priority rather than simple eradication.
