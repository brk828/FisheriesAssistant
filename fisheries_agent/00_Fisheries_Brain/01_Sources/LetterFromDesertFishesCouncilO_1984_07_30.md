## Executive Summary
This 1984 letter from the [[Desert Fishes Council]], addressed to the California Fish and Game Commission, formally opposes a proposal by United Energy Corporation (UEC) to introduce five non-native fish species — [[Nile Tilapia]], [[Blue Tilapia]], [[Silver Carp]], [[Grass Carp]], and [[Bighead Carp]] — for aquaculture purposes near the [[Mohave River]] in California. The Council, representing roughly 400 scientists, resource managers, and conservationists, argues that introducing these species poses a substantial ecological risk to native and sport fishes, citing the potential for accidental escape via human error, transport mishaps, or catastrophic flooding events (e.g., a 100-year flood). The letter is not a primary research study but a scientific-advocacy document reflecting expert consensus on the dangers of exotic species introduction into fragile desert aquatic ecosystems.

## Background & Description
The letter does not provide morphological descriptions of the species in question, as its purpose is regulatory and policy-oriented rather than taxonomic. However, the species listed represent two distinct fish families with well-documented traits relevant to invasion risk:

- **[[Nile Tilapia]]** (*Oreochromis niloticus*) and **[[Blue Tilapia]]** (*Oreochromis aureus*) — Family [[Cichlidae]]; deep-bodied, laterally compressed mouth-brooding cichlids known for rapid maturation and high reproductive output.
- **[[Silver Carp]]** (*Hypophthalmichthys molitrix*), **[[Grass Carp]]** (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*), and **[[Bighead Carp]]** (*Aristichthys nobilis*) — Family [[Cyprinidae]]; large-bodied filter-feeding or herbivorous carps, notable for high fecundity and broad environmental tolerance, characteristics that underpin their status as prolific invasive species in North American waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- The proposed aquaculture facility was to be sited near the **[[Mohave River]]**, a desert waterway supporting sensitive native aquatic communities characteristic of [[Desert Fishes]] ecosystems.
- Concerns center on the risk of **unplanned introduction** of these exotic species into wild desert waters through:
  - Human error at the facility or during transport of live fish.
  - Catastrophic flood events (e.g., 100-year flood) breaching containment and connecting the facility to the river system.
- Tilapiine cichlids and cyprinid carps are recognized as competitors and potential predators/habitat modifiers that could displace native and sport fish populations if established in California's aquatic systems.
- The letter emphasizes that approval of these species could set a precedent, encouraging further aquaculture proposals using the same species in other California watersheds, compounding risk to statewide native ichthyofauna.

## Key Findings
- The [[Desert Fishes Council]] formally recommended **denial** of UEC's proposal to introduce *Oreochromis niloticus*, *Oreochromis aureus*, *Hypophthalmichthys molitrix*, *Ctenopharyngodon idella*, and *Aristichthys nobilis* for aquaculture in California.
- The Council commended the California Fish and Game Commission for its historical firm stance against undesirable exotic species introductions.
- Even facilities with "maximum security" measures carry inherent risk due to human error and catastrophic natural events (e.g., flooding of the Mohave River).
- The Council called for a **complete Environmental Impact Report (EIR)** assessing the potential ecological impacts of each species on California and neighboring states' aquatic ecosystems before any approval.
- It was recommended that any aquaculture research or trials be conducted instead using fish species **already approved** for aquaculture in California, avoiding introduction of new exotic taxa.
- The letter underscores that concerns raised are based on the professional consensus of scientists with direct, long-term experience managing desert aquatic ecosystems, not speculative or theoretical risk.
