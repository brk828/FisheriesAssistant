# Operation of Abernathy Channel for Incubation of Salmon Eggs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #salmon-propagation #hatchery-operations

## Executive Summary

This technical paper reports on seven seasons (1959-1966) of experimental operation of the [[Abernathy Channel]], a 2,000-foot artificial spawning and incubation channel near [[Longview, Washington]]. The facility was designed to incubate [[Fall Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) and [[Chum Salmon]] (*O. keta*) eggs under controlled conditions. Survival rates from eyed egg to migrant stage were substantially higher than reported for other channels and natural spawning: 68.0-78.5% for chinook salmon and 75.3-95.5% for chum salmon from eyed-egg plants. The study examined effects of sedimentation, egg planting density (up to 435 eggs per square foot), stage of egg development at planting, and water flow rates. A sedimentation system constructed in 1963 significantly improved survival by removing sand and heavy silt. Migration timing was successfully predicted using accumulated temperature units: approximately 1,800 units for chinook and 1,700 units for chum salmon.

## Background & Description

The [[Abernathy Channel]] was constructed in 1959 adjacent to the Salmon-Cultural Laboratory, approximately 16 miles west of Longview, Washington. It was the second channel built in the Washington-Oregon-Idaho area and the first operated specifically for egg planting and incubation rather than adult spawning. The channel measures 2,000 feet long by 10 feet wide and was designed as a combined experimental and production facility.

Water supply is drawn from [[Abernathy Creek]] through a quarter-mile pipeline. The head structure includes diffusion panels to equalize flow and serve as barriers to adult fish if needed. Two inclined-plane traps ([[Wolf Fry Traps]]) at the lower end collect migrating juveniles. In 1963, a sedimentation system consisting of a sand trap and settling basin (160 feet long, 40 feet wide, 5 feet deep) was added to remove sand and heavy silt from the water supply.

The channel differs markedly from other spawning channels in its operational parameters, particularly in egg density and water flow. While similar channels like [[Jones Creek Channel]] used 25-30 c.f.s. during spawning and 15 c.f.s. during incubation, and [[Robertson Creek Channel]] operated at 100 c.f.s., the Abernathy channel successfully operated at only 1.2 c.f.s. average discharge beginning in 1963-64.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sedimentation emerged as a major factor affecting egg survival in artificial channels. Before installation of the sand trap and settling basin, sediment deposition in upper channel sections substantially reduced survival. [[Shelton and Pollock]] (1966) reported 70% of sediment as sand before the trap installation. The sedimentation system effectively removed sand and heavy silt, though finer silt particles remained in suspension and passed through without settling.

Water temperature proved critical for egg development and migration timing. The 1965-66 chinook experiment experienced unusually warm conditions (average 60°F, maximum 67°F) during the first 2.5 weeks of incubation, resulting in only 14.6% survival compared to 68-78.5% in other years. [[Combs and Burrows]] (1957) and [[Combs]] (1965) demonstrated that mortalities increase in chinook and sockeye eggs at constant 60°F temperatures.

Natural food availability in the gravel environment provided an advantage over hatchery incubation. Stomach examinations showed 70-100% of channel migrants had fed on plankton in the gravel before migration, compared to less than 30% of hatchery-incubated fish. This early feeding may explain better survival rates of wild migrants versus unfed hatchery fry releases.

Migration patterns were strongly influenced by accumulated temperature units (defined by [[Wallich]] 1901) rather than photoperiod or absolute incubation time. Stage of yolk sac development was the principal factor influencing migration, with water temperature being the major factor controlling developmental stage.

## Key Findings

**Survival Rates by Species and Egg Stage:**
- [[Fall Chinook Salmon]] eyed-egg plants: 68.0-78.5% survival to migrant stage
- Fall chinook pre-waterhardened green eggs: 14.6% (influenced by unfavorable 60°F+ temperatures)
- [[Chum Salmon]] eyed-egg plants: 75.3-95.5% survival across five seasons
- Chum waterhardened green eggs: 87.8% survival
- Chum pre-waterhardened green eggs: 71.9% survival

**Egg Planting Density:**
- Densities tested ranged from 86 to 435 eggs per square foot for chinook, 140 to 400 eggs per square foot for chum
- Maximum density of 435 eggs per square foot showed no significant reduction in survival
- This rate is approximately three times the recommended density for spawning channels
- Physical capacity of channel estimated at 7 million eggs at 435 eggs/sq ft

**Sedimentation Control:**
- Pre-1963: 70% of sediment was sand; required expensive gravel removal and cleaning ($2,000+ per cleaning)
- Post-1963 sand trap and settling basin: eliminated sand deposition, allowed simple fire-hose cleaning (126 man-hours for entire channel)
- Upper section survival increased significantly after sedimentation system installation

**Water Flow Optimization:**
- 1959-60: 8 c.f.s. average discharge
- 1960-61 to 1962-63: reduced to 3 c.f.s. to reduce sedimentation
- 1963-64 onward: 1.2 c.f.s. average discharge (covers gravel ~2 inches deep)
- Lower flow rates (1.2 c.f.s.) successfully incubated eggs at maximum channel capacity

**Migration Timing Prediction:**
- Peak chinook migration occurred at approximately 1,800 accumulated temperature units (67-89% of total migration)
- Peak chum migration occurred at approximately 1,700 temperature units (63-70% of total migration)
- Temperature units proved more reliable than photoperiod or absolute incubation time for predicting migration
- Actual migration dates varied by 82 days for chinook and 29 days for chum across experiments

**Migrant Characteristics:**
- First chinook migrants: exposed yolk sacs, ≤34 mm length, ~0.39 grams
- Major chinook migration: yolk sac withdrawn, 38 mm length, ~0.40 grams
- Chum migrants generally slightly larger and heavier than chinook
- 29.8% of 1965-66 chinook migrants had exposed yolk sacs

**Feeding Behavior:**
- 70-100% of channel migrants had fed on plankton in gravel before migration
- Hatchery incubator fry on well water: 0% feeding opportunity
- Hatchery fry on creek water: ~30% had eaten before ponding
- Channel migrants readily accepted food and gained 150% weight in 3 weeks when transferred to hatchery

**Operational Efficiency (man-hours per million eggs):**
- Pre-waterhardened green eggs: 87 man-hours (includes gravel cleaning, trenching, spawning, planting)
- Waterhardened green eggs: 92 man-hours
- Eyed eggs: 107 man-hours (includes incubator handling, fungus treatment, dead egg picking)
- Annual costs (1967 wage scale): $271.44 to $333.84 depending on egg stage

**Residual Fish Management:**
- Less than 1% of fish remained past normal migration time
- Formalin treatment unsuccessful in displacing residents
- Water level manipulation minimally effective
- Seining removed only ~20% of residuals
- Doubling water flow (to ~2.4 c.f.s.) for 10 days displaced 83% of residuals within 48 hours

**Comparative Performance:**
- Abernathy survivals substantially higher than other channels: [[McNary Spawning Channel]] (6-40%), [[Rocky Reach Spawning Channel]] (11.8-42.3%), [[Priest Rapids Spawning Channel]] (31.5%), [[Carmen-Smith Spawning Channel]] (11-42%)
- Natural spawning in Abernathy Creek: 0.58% survival (estimated 16,700 migrants from 2,880,000 eggs)
- Channel production: 4,620,600 migrants from 5,888,100 eyed eggs (78.5% survival) in 1964-65

**1964-65 Large-Scale Experiment:**
- Total plant: 6,164,400 green eggs (4,144,500 Abernathy stock, 2,019,900 Little White Salmon stock)
- Green-egg mortality: 4.72% Abernathy, 4.0% Little White Salmon
- Final eyed-egg plant: 5,888,100 eggs in three groups
- Total migrants: 4,620,600 (78.5% from eyed eggs, 75% overall including green-egg loss)
- All hatchery fish marked with tetracycline for adult return evaluation (1966-1969)

**Environmental Measurements (1960-63 seasons, [[Bakkala]] 1966):**
- Dissolved oxygen: 11.8 ppm (eyed eggs), 9.7 ppm (hatching), 8.1 ppm (fry)
- Seepage velocities: 2.5 ft/hr (eyed eggs), 2.6 ft/hr (hatching), 2.0 ft/hr (fry)

**Egg Size and Migrant Size Correlation:**
- Abernathy chum eggs: 105.7 eggs/ounce → migrants 0.45 grams
- Quilcene chum eggs: 123.9 eggs/ounce → migrants 0.40 grams
- Difference statistically significant for both eggs and migrants
