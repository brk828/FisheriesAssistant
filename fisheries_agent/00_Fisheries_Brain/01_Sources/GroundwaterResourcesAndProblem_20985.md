# Ground-Water Resources and Problems of the Cactus Flat-Artesia Area, San Simon Basin, Arizona

#hydrogeology #groundwater-hydrology #artesian-aquifers

## Executive Summary

This 1947 U.S. Geological Survey water-supply report, prepared by [[Leonard C. Halpenny]] and [[Robert L. Cushman]] with a water-quality section by J. D. Hem, examines the ground-water resources of the [[Cactus Flat-Artesia Area]] in the lower (northern) end of the [[San Simon Basin]], Graham County, Arizona. It was prepared in cooperation with the Arizona State Land Department to provide basic data for a state ground-water code. The study covers about 65 square miles, though most wells lie within about 15 square miles.

The area's water supply is an artesian system of gravel and sand beds in the [[Older Alluvial Fill]], with a shallower unconfined system in the [[Younger Alluvial Fill]]. Flowing wells have expanded from 30 to 40 in 1915 to about 125 in 1946, and about 1,000 acres were irrigated that year. Artesian pressure has declined, interference among wells is increasing, and most ground water is high in sodium and fluoride. The report concludes that wells need repair, that spacing regulation is needed, and that regional relations with neighboring basins remain unknown.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and cooperation.** The Division of Ground Water (Water Resources Branch, USGS) studied the area in 1939-1940 as part of an inventory of the [[Safford Basin]], then in detail in 1946 as part of a statewide ground-water basin investigation requested by the Arizona Legislature. Supervision was by S. F. Turner, District Engineer (Ground Water).

**Location.** The area lies west of [[San Simon Creek]], bounded on the west by the [[Pinaleno Mountains]], on the north by the San Jose Extension canal in the Safford Basin of the [[Gila River]], and on the south and east by arbitrary survey lines. Drainage is ultimately to the Gila River through Stockton Wash, so the area could be assigned to the Safford Basin. The report keeps it in the San Simon Basin because artesian conditions resemble those near the town of San Simon, while noting that the artesian aquifers are probably separate from both basins.

**Climate.** No local records existed. The [[Thatcher Weather Station]] (elevation 2,900 feet, about 7 miles north and 2 miles west) reported:
- Average rainfall of 9.50 inches per year (34-year record)
- Average temperature of 62.5 °F (42-year record)
- Maximum 116 °F and minimum 7 °F
- Frost-free season averaging 203 days (about April 11 to October 31; 26-year record)

Cactus Flat is about 500 feet higher than Thatcher, so its rainfall is probably slightly above 9.50 inches per year.

**History of development.** Prehistoric Indians settled near water, and an Indian village site was found in the area. Stock grazing began in the San Simon Basin about 1867, and the first settlement at Artesia dates to about 1890. Dug and small-diameter wells in non-artesian aquifers existed by 1890, but prospecting there was curtailed in the early 1900s after deeper artesian aquifers were found. The first flowing wells were completed near Stockton Wash in 1902. Surface-water irrigation from a dam in Marijilda Canyon (about 1900) often failed in drought.

**Geology.** The trough containing the area is partly filled with unconsolidated gravel, sand, silt, and clay. The hard, largely impermeable Pinaleno Mountain rocks carry only minor water through fractures and springs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses no fish and no aquatic fauna. Its ecological content concerns groundwater-dependent vegetation and wetlands.

**Older Alluvial Fill (principal aquifer).** This unit was deposited in a closed basin with a shallow playa-type lake along its axis, and it is at least 1,700 feet thick. It has three zones:
- **Gravel zone** near the mountains, 1 to 2 miles wide, partly consolidated and moderately permeable, with caliche layers near the surface.
- **Transition zone** of interbedded sand and silt grading outward to silt with some sand.
- **Lake-bed zone** along San Simon Creek, of relatively impermeable silt and clay containing some salt and gypsum.

Gravel and sand beds are 2 to 10 feet thick and occur from 100 to at least 1,700 feet deep. Artesian water is confined between lake-bed silts and clays.

**Younger Alluvial Fill.** Gravel, sand, and silt in wash valleys cut into the older fill, ranging from 2 feet near the mountains to about 50 feet. The water table lies 20 to 45 feet below land surface, and the water is not artesian.

**Recharge.** The main recharge source is seepage from streams crossing the gravel-zone outcrops. Streams originating in the Pinaleno Mountains lost up to 20 cubic feet per second during spring 1940 and 1941. Clear snowmelt or slow winter rain infiltrates up to 1¾ times faster than silty flash-flood water in comparable studies on [[Queen Creek]], the Santa Cruz River, and Rillito Creek. Recharge is limited by near-surface caliche and steep slopes that shed rainfall into runoff.

**Cienegas and phreatophytes.** Two cienegas, where artesian leakage along fault zones reaches the land surface, have been affected by well development:
- One in section 5, T. 6 S., R. 26 E., along Jacobsville (Jacobsavn) Wash for about half a mile. It once irrigated eight farms in the late 1890s and early 1900s, and its flow fell to about 15 gallons per minute by October 1946.
- One in sections 10 and 15, T. 8 S., R. 26 E., along [[Stockton Wash]] for about a mile. Surface water disappeared by October 1946, though plant growth showed shallow water remained.

Mesquite (*Prosopis*) is the dominant phreatophyte. About 300 acres of mesquite grow in the two cienegas, using an estimated 1.5 acre-feet per acre per year, or about 450 acre-feet per year from the older fill. Evaporation from the less than 10 acres of continuously wet soil is about 5 acre-feet per acre per year, which is negligible overall.

## Key Findings

**Artesian conditions and wells**
- Flowing wells increased from 30 to 40 (1915) to about 100 (1934) and about 125 (1946), with 13 pumped wells in 1946.
- Wells range from 200 to 1,700 feet deep and 3 to 12 inches in diameter. The average is about 8 inches in diameter and 800 to 900 feet deep.
- Wells 903, 904, and 905 (spaced along a 15-foot line) draw from different aquifers. Well 903 is 1,216 feet deep, cased to 1,173 feet, and yields 90 gallons per minute. Well 904 is 1,022 feet deep, cased to 990 feet, and yields 70 gallons per minute. Well 905 is 700 feet deep, cased to 650 feet, and yields 20 gallons per minute.
- Insufficient casing has allowed caving and root obstructions, increasing friction head and reducing flow. Corroded casings leak water into the younger fill, and several old casings have rusted below land surface, marked only by vegetation.

**Artesian head decline and interference**
- Artesian pressure head has declined, though the original head is unknown. Well 722 rose to 3 feet above land surface in September 1940 but stood 4 feet below it in November 1946. Well 726 rose 9 feet above land surface in September 1940 but only 6 feet above in April 1942.
- Well interference is significant. Well 776 stopped flowing after three new wells were drilled 400 feet away into the same aquifer, and its water level fell almost to their orifice elevation.

**Water budget**
- Estimated spring recharge to the older fill is about 4,400 acre-feet (20 cubic feet per second over 4 months), plus 500 to 1,000 acre-feet from flood flows.
- About 5,600 acre-feet was discharged from flowing and pumped wells in 1946.
- About 2,000 acre-feet is diverted through canals, and roughly 700 acre-feet of that seeps back as recharge. About 4,900 acre-feet is applied to land, and roughly 1,200 acre-feet of that percolates down. Total irrigation recharge to the younger fill is about 1,900 acre-feet.
- Surface-flow recharge to the younger fill is about 500 acre-feet per year (1941 data).
- Underflow beneath Stockton and Graveyard Washes was about 3 cubic feet per second, or roughly 2,000 acre-feet per year (1941).
- Phreatophytes in the younger fill use an estimated 750 acre-feet per year.
- The report gives no legible value for the annual safe yield of the artesian aquifers in the source text (OCR garbled).

**Water quality**
- Fifteen ground-water samples were analyzed in 1946 (10 in table 2). Six more came from 1940-1942 Safford Valley work, and Knechtel's 1933-34 analyses covered 21 wells. Resampling in 1942 showed little change.
- Dissolved solids range from 163 ppm (dilute non-flowing well between Cactus Flat and Artesia) to 2,600 ppm (deep flowing well in Cactus Flat, 1946).
- Ground water is soft and dominated by sodium, chloride, and sulfate.
- Dilute water lies at shallow depths (under 500 feet) near the mountains. Deeper and more distant waters are more concentrated.
- Surface waters in Stockton and Marijilda Washes contain under 100 ppm, mostly sodium, calcium, and bicarbonate.

**Suitability for use**
- Nearly all waters fall in the "injurious to unsatisfactory" class for irrigation (Wilcox and Magistad). High sodium hardens soils, and some land has been damaged.
- Some deep artesian waters have too much dissolved solids and chloride for irrigation.
- Fluoride exceeds 1.5 ppm in nearly all analyzed waters, which may cause permanent tooth mottling in young children. The highest fluoride occurs in the deepest aquifers.
- Many waters are too mineralized to drink, though most are soft and acceptable for domestic use.

**Unanswered problems and recommendations**
- Water is lost from artesian aquifers to the younger fill through faulty or short casings.
- The relationship between the artesian aquifers here and those of the San Simon Basin and Safford Basin is unknown. Lateral movement direction and magnitude were not determined.
- Proposed ground-water regulation should require proper well spacing for wells tapping the same aquifer, to minimize interference.
- Wells should be cased to the producing aquifer, and shut-off valves should restrict flow to beneficial uses.
- Soluble salts appear to be accumulating in the irrigated area, though salt removal is a secondary concern to sodium hazard.
