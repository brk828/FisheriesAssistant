# Freshwater Fishes of the Wadi Batha Drainage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Journal of Oman Studies special report by [[Friedhelm Krupp]] documents the freshwater fishes of the [[Wadi Batha]] drainage in Oman. Three species occur there: [[Cyprinion Microphthalmum]], [[Garra Barreimiae]] and [[Aphanius Dispar]]. The study describes a new endemic subspecies, [[Garra Barreimiae Gallagheri]], and briefly discusses ecological adaptations to extreme desert conditions and the colonisation history of the drainage.

## Background & Description

Freshwater bodies in deserts are unstable habitats, and fishes there must tolerate fluctuating water level, temperature, oxygen content and salinity, often with limited food. Only six freshwater fish species have been reported from southeastern Arabia, and three of them occur in the Wadi Batha system. Within the Wahiba Sands region, freshwater fishes are known only from this drainage. Material was collected by [[W. Büttiker]], [[M.D. Gallagher]] and the author, mainly from [[Wadi Bani Khalid]], the largest and most diverse perennial tributary of Wadi Batha. Comparative specimens were examined at the [[British Museum (Natural History)]].

**Cyprinion microphthalmum muscatensis** from Wadi Batha does not differ morphologically from other populations of this subspecies. It has a modally lower lateral line scale count (mean X = 37.7, against 38.9 in Gulf of Oman drainage populations), a strongly convex dorsal fin margin and darker colouration. Its subterminal mouth and sharp-edged horny lower jaw sheath mark it as an aufwuchs feeder; gut contents from Sayq were over 90% filamentous algae.

**Garra barreimiae gallagheri n. ssp.** is described from a holotype male of 62 mm standard length (77 mm total length) collected 13–14 April 1987 in Wadi Bani Khalid north of Muqal. The type series is held in several collections, including the [[Senckenberg Museum Frankfurt]] (SMF), [[Naturhistorisches Museum Basel]] (NHMB) and the [[Oman Natural History Museum]] (ONHM). Key diagnostic traits:
- 8 branched dorsal fin rays (versus 7 in the nominal subspecies, described as a very stable character)
- 30–34 lateral line scales
- 10–14 gill rakers on the lower limb of the first gill arch
- Smaller mental disc with large papillae on its anterior and lateral parts, the central disc usually smooth
- Shorter fins, and a different body shape and colour pattern
- Males have much longer pectoral fins than females; all other fins are on average longer in males

Colouration is brownish-grey dorsally, light grey below the lateral line and whitish ventrally, with a dark lateral band ending in a dark dot at the caudal peduncle. The subspecies is named after M.D. Gallagher, who first collected it.

**Aphanius dispar dispar** material from six localities, including [[Mudhayrib]] (10 specimens, 26–44 mm sl), shows no morphological differences from other southeast Arabian populations. The species is described in detail by [[Krupp 1983]] and [[Villwock et al. 1983]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat partitioning in Garra barreimiae gallagheri:** The subspecies is almost ubiquitous in Wadi Bani Khalid. It occurs in small rock pools, shallow margins of larger nearly stagnant pools, and fast-flowing streamlets. It is most common over fine sand and mud with scattered boulders that offer shelter, but is also found over bare rock, gravel and aquatic plants.

**Feeding:** The inferior mouth and papillose mental disc indicate a substrate feeder. Gut contents were mainly unicellular algae, with filamentous algae, rotifer, nematode and crustacean fragments, and sand grains.

**Feeding and niche overlap:** The author argues that Cyprinion grazes on plant material and cuts filamentous algae, while Garra ingests loose organisms and detritus from sand and stone surfaces, so the two species occupy effectively the same niche. Both feeding modes appear to succeed in arid conditions.

**Aphanius dispar:** Described as a surface feeder that mainly eats dipteran larvae. Haas (1982) characterised it as an opportunistic omnivore, and it has been introduced for mosquito control in some artificial water bodies in Oman. Gut contents from January 1986 were mainly filamentous and unicellular algae, with small amounts of nematodes, crustaceans and aquatic insects.

**Distribution and adaptation:** Aphanius dispar is the most widespread of the three species and the only one able to inhabit the lower reaches of the wadi, owing to its tolerance of extreme temperatures and salinities. The other two species are restricted to higher altitudes.

**Biogeography:** The three Garra barreimiae subspecies are allopatric. G. b. shawkahensis lives in Arabian Gulf drainages of the United Arab Emirates, G. b. barreimiae in Gulf of Oman drainages of the Oman mountains, and G. b. gallagheri is endemic to the Wadi Batha system, which drains to the Arabian Sea.

**Absent groups:** Mid-level feeders (Barbus, which occur elsewhere in Arabia) and predators (Siluriformes, Cichlidae) are absent from Oman. The author sees no ecological reason for this and suggests they never reached southeastern Arabia.

**Colonisation history:** Aphanius, a secondary freshwater fish that tolerates marine conditions, may have reached the wadi through the sea. The cyprinids depend on direct freshwater connections. The high similarity with northern drainage fauna shows that Wadi Batha received its stock from the same source, while morphological differences in G. barreimiae indicate a period of isolation, probably linked to Neogene faulting of the Oman mountains.

## Key Findings

- Three freshwater fish species occur in the Wadi Batha drainage: Cyprinion microphthalmum, Garra barreimiae and Aphanius dispar.
- A new endemic subspecies, Garra barreimiae gallagheri, is described, distinguished mainly by an 8-branched dorsal fin (vs. 7 in the nominal subspecies), 30–34 lateral line scales and 10–14 gill rakers.
- The Wadi Batha fish fauna is unique at the subspecific level despite having only three species.
- Aphanius dispar is the most widespread species and the only one that can live in the wadi's lower reaches.
- Cyprinion and Garra appear to fill the same benthic feeding niche, with grazing and surface-detritus feeding respectively.
- The shared fauna with northern drainages indicates a common source, and the differences in G. barreimiae suggest isolation linked to Neogene faulting.
- Aquatic habitats in arid zones are unstable, and disturbance can eradicate part of the fauna, so these populations are of conservation concern.
