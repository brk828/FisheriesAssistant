# Effects of External and Internal Radio Transmitters on Upstream Migration of Adult Chinook Salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This field study compared the effects of external and internal radio transmitters on the upstream migration of adult [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) in the lower [[Snake River]] during fall 1976, spring 1976, and spring 1977. Fish were tagged at [[Little Goose Dam]] and tracked with automated receivers, fixed counting windows, and boat or truck surveys. Passage success and travel times of externally tagged fish were similar to those of control fish carrying only anchor tags, while internally tagged fish showed markedly lower passage success. The authors conclude that external radio tagging does not alter upstream migration when attachment is done properly, whereas internal placement in the digestive tract is critical and may impair migration.

## Background & Description

Telemetry is widely used to study fish movement and behavior, but the effects of transmitter attachment on fish were largely unknown at the time. Previous studies reported mixed results: external tags reduced swimming performance in Atlantic salmon smolts and cutthroat trout, while other work found little effect on feeding or migration. Internal tags were generally considered less stressful in the Pacific Northwest, but the authors noted a general lack of controlled field studies on this question, particularly for fish.

Transmitters were designed and built at the [[Bioelectronics Laboratory]], University of Minnesota. Spring 1976 transmitters weighed about 35 g and measured roughly 11.5 cm long by 2.7 cm in diameter, while tags used in fall 1976 and spring 1977 were reduced to about 25 g and 2.8 cm by 1.9 cm (the source text gives these dimensions with OCR errors, so values are approximate). Battery life ranged from 4 to 20 days. Receivers could distinguish up to 100 separate crystal-tuned frequencies. External tags were secured below the dorsal fin with a Teflon-coated wire and neoprene pads; internal tags were inserted through the esophagus into the stomach with a plastic inserter, with the antenna exiting through the tissue posterior to the stomach.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study took place in the lower Snake River, where adult salmon must pass a series of hydroelectric dams. Water temperatures during fall 1976 ranged from 16.7 to 20 °C, compared with 13.5 to 20 °C at [[Ice Harbor Dam]] in 1968 before [[Little Goose Dam]] was built. The authors suggested that additional reservoirs on the Snake River may have raised average summer and fall water temperatures. Coutant (1970) reported 21 to 22 °C as the upper lethal limit for jack chinook salmon, and the fall-run fish appeared thermally stressed. Low fall 1976 counts (1,068 salmon passing Little Goose Dam) and dark, listless fish near spawning indicated poor stock condition. Fall 1976 fish also moved to the mouth of the [[Tucannon River]], where temperatures were about 17 °C.

## Key Findings

- **Sample design:** 128 adult chinook salmon were tagged over three seasons: 60 controls with anchor tags, 25 with external radio and anchor tags, and 39 with internal radio and anchor tags, as summarized in Table 1 of the source (the table totals were partly garbled by OCR).
- **Tag weight:** Transmitter weight was kept below 2% of fish body weight (calculated maxima of 1.9% and 1.7%).
- **Upriver passage at Little Goose Dam:** All 39 externally tagged salmon were recorded in the spill basin upriver of the release site in spring. About half (8 of 22) of internally tagged fish were recorded there.
- **Passage over Little Goose Dam (spring):** 72% (39/54) of controls and 87% (34/39) of external-tag fish were recorded crossing or above the dam. No internally tagged fish were observed crossing or above the dam in spring or fall 1976.
- **Downstream movement:** 92% (22/24) of internally tagged fish moved downstream from the release point before their transmitters failed.
- **Passage at Lower Granite Dam (spring):** 39% (15/39) of controls and 56% (19/34) of external-tag fish were recorded.
- **Travel times:** Travel times of external and internal tag groups did not differ statistically (p > 0.05) in spring 1976. Across groups, no significant differences were found except for fish in spring 1975 (per the source) that lost external transmitters, which averaged 34 days.
- **Recoveries:** Three external transmitters and one anchor tag were returned by fishermen and a hatchery, suggesting that both control and external-tag fish reached spawning areas hundreds of kilometers upstream.
- **Detection efficiency:** Only one of three fish passing dams undetected was recovered; the authors estimated they failed to observe passage of no more than one of five external-tag fish whose batteries failed in the spill basin.
- **Mechanisms:** The authors attributed internal-tag failure to tissue damage at the stomach, tags lodged between the stomach and the pyloric sphincter, and stress from handling and fall water temperatures. A transporter oxygen failure may have affected one control and one internally tagged fish, both of which had no observable stomach damage.
- **Species comparison:** Four [[Steelhead Trout]] tagged in October 1976 (two controls, two internal-tag fish) showed better tolerance of internal tags than mature chinook salmon, as stomach atrophy is less extensive in steelhead.
- **Conclusion:** Fish species, life history, physiology, and study location must be considered when choosing a transmitter placement method. External tags can reliably track salmon migration with proper attachment, whereas internal placement in the digestive tract appears critical and may affect migration.
