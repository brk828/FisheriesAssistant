# Trace Element Contamination Near Copper Smelters and Mines in Hayden, Arizona

#ecotoxicology #plant-ecology #trace-metals #air-pollution

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1973 research proposal from [[Arizona State University]] to the [[National Science Foundation]] (RANN program). It requests about $354,983 over 2.5 years, starting April 1974, to quantify trace contaminants released by two copper smelters at Hayden, Arizona, and the drainage from a large open-pit mine. The team is multidisciplinary and draws on plant pathology, soil chemistry, analytical chemistry, water chemistry, botany and statistics. The study aims to identify trace elements present, quantify their distribution through soils, plants and [[Gila River]] sediments, characterize current particulate fallout, and document [[Sulfur Dioxide]] damage to native range plants through fumigation. The results are intended to feed a predictive model of trace contaminant impacts from copper production.

No fish or aquatic animal species are studied. Fish appear only as a possible future link in a food chain.

## Background & Description

**Problem statement.** The proposal argues that the national distribution and concentration of trace contaminants from primary copper production is poorly known. The [[Environmental Protection Agency]] bibliography (1973) reported no general studies of trace elements around copper smelters. Regulators need "hard" data from sound analytical methods to make management and regional planning decisions.

**Study area.** The [[Hayden Smelters]] area includes two smelters (American Smelting and Refining Company and Kennecott Copper Company). A third smelter at San Manuel lies about 30 miles south. The site lies about 70 miles ESE of Phoenix, on the Gila River, with the Pinal Mountains to the north.

**Pollution context.**
- Arizona's major pollution sources are motor vehicles, coal-burning power stations and copper smelters and mines.
- Smelting accounts for 99.7% of sulfur oxides in the Phoenix-Tucson metropolitan area (Morita, 1969).
- Nationally, copper operations emit 242,000 tons of particulates per year, 52% of them in Arizona (Vandergrift, 1971).
- The seven active Arizona smelters emit roughly 2,600 tons of sulfur per day (5,200 tons SO2 equivalent).
- Reported particulate concentrations for cadmium, copper, lead and zinc at Hayden are 6, 8, 1.2 and 4.6 times higher, respectively, than at any other U.S. location in the 1964 national network data.

**Competing land and water uses.** Ranching and agriculture conflict with copper operations. The proposed [[Buttes Dam]] (about 1976, [[Central Arizona Project]]) lies roughly 10–12 miles downstream. The copper companies plan to raise principal stacks from 250 ft to 1000 ft, which would change fallout patterns.

**Investigators.** The project is led by [[Thomas H. Nash III]] (Botany, principal investigator), with [[Thomas W. Barrett]] (soil chemistry and SO2 damage assessment), [[Chester R. Leathers]] (plant pathology), [[Michael L. Parsons]] (analytical chemistry) and [[Milton R. Sommerfeld]] (phytoplankton and water chemistry). Consultants in meteorology, sedimentology and statistics are planned.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pollutant types.** The proposal lists sulfur oxides, nitrogen oxides, ozone, hydrogen fluoride, PAN and carbon monoxide as major gaseous pollutants. Trace contaminants of concern include arsenic, cadmium, copper, fluoride, iron, lead, mercury, nickel and zinc.

**Effect categories.** Following Robinson (1970), effects are acute (hours to days, high dose), chronic (weeks, lower dose) and long-term (decades). Metal accumulation in soils and vegetation is classed as long-term.

**Vegetation.** Sonoran desert vegetation near the smelters is considered relatively tolerant of copper effluents, but plant communities are not fully healthy. Some species appear absent near the smelters. Tolerant plants may accumulate higher metal concentrations, so herbivores such as cattle may receive elevated contaminant doses.

**Cattle and crops.** Cattle generate about $300 million per year in Arizona. Agricultural income in the Florence-Casa Grande-Phoenix area exceeds $500 million per year, and the area is important for cotton. Gila River water is used there for irrigation.

**Hydrology and sediments.** Trace metals may be transported with sediment load, and deposition can create long-lasting reservoirs in bottom sediments. The Gila River system is proposed as the main route for transporting metals from the mining area toward downstream agriculture.

**Conflicts.** The proposal notes that the Bureau of Reclamation's 1972 environmental impact statement for the Buttes Reservoir does not consider smelter or mine trace metals. Litigation between ranchers and the copper industry is ongoing, and the proposal argues that data are needed to replace speculation with facts.

## Key Findings

The proposal's main points and planned work are summarized below.

**Research plan (five parts):**

1. **Semi-quantitative trace metal survey.** Samples are analysed by direct-current arc emission spectrography for about 50 metals. Roughly 15 samples per day are processed. A written report is due by December 31, 1974.
2. **Quantification across the [[Hayden Air Shed]].** Analysis uses [[Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometry]] for 15–20 selected elements. Planned sample sets include:
   - Soil and vegetation: 6 transects × 5 points, with 5 soil samples and 10 plant samples per point, totalling about 1,500 samples. Plants include the browse shrub bursage (*Ambrosia deltoidea*) and a range grass (*Bouteloua*).
   - Gila River sediments: at least 30 sites (about 480 samples), including 7 upstream control sites.
   - Water flux: a pilot study with about 288 samples over six sampling periods.
   - Capacity is projected at about 3,900 atomic absorption samples over 18 months.
3. **Particulate fallout.** [[Andersen Air Samplers]] collect size-fractioned particulates at four stations (three downwind, one "clean air" site) over 7–10-day periods in each season. The plan projects a minimum of 1,344 samples. Microscopy is used to separate smelter aerosols (smooth droplets) from natural dust (angular particles).
4. **[[Fumigation Studies]].** Plants are fumigated with SO2 in greenhouse chambers on the ASU campus and in open-topped field chambers at the Boyce Thompson Arboretum. The plan covers 25–30 species for symptom documentation and 4–5 species for seasonal response. One experiment tests SO2 effects on mesquite bean yield.
5. **Identification of future research.** Later work may track contaminant flux through three food chains: Gila River water to phytoplankton, zooplankton, fish and man; Gila River water to Florence crops to man; and soils to plants to cattle to man.

**Quality control.** All containers are polyethylene, acid-washed and rinsed in deionized glass-distilled water. Ultrapure chemicals (99.97–99.999%) are used. Standard reference soils and leaves are obtained from the U.S. Bureau of Standards.

**Statistics.** The design uses one-way ANOVA with nested sampling to separate spatial, sample and analytical variation. Broken-line regression is proposed to find the distance beyond which metal accumulation ceases. Log transformation may be used.

**Expected outputs.**
- Maps of isopleths of trace metal concentration in soils, plants and sediments.
- Identification of which elements are present at potentially harmful levels.
- Seasonal fallout patterns, before and after stack elevation.
- Documented SO2 injury symptoms and threshold concentrations for native range plants.
- A predictive model of current and future contaminant effects, intended for regional planning and regulatory use.

**Communication plan.** Monthly meetings with the State Divisions of Air and Water Pollution Control; semi-annual reports to agencies and to Kennecott and ASARCO; publication in journals such as *Ecology*, *Ecological Monographs* and *Environmental Science and Technology*; an illustrated SO2 damage bulletin; and slide presentations.

**Management and budget.**
- ASU is the fiscal agent. The project uses about 4 half-time graduate students, one post-doctoral fellow and a research associate.
- The total request is $354,983 (NSF plus ASU cost-share).
- The College of Liberal Arts and the Botany department committed release-time funds and about $3,000 per year for two years to hire a replacement faculty member.

**Support letters.** The Arizona Division of Air Pollution Control (Sept. 5, 1973) and the Division of Water Pollution Control (Sept. 21, 1973) both support the proposal. The letters state that the Division of Water Pollution Control expects the data to help with heavy metal monitoring in Gila River sediments.

**Key justification.** Concentrations of cadmium, copper, lead and zinc at Hayden exceed other U.S. sites, and SO2 is present in high concentrations. The proposal argues that a contamination problem is "almost unity" probable, and that the data are needed before the planned stack elevation and Buttes Reservoir.
