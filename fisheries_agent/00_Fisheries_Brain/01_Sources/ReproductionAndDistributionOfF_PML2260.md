# Reproduction and Distribution of Fishes in a Wisconsin Power Plant Cooling Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Centrarchidae #Percidae #Esocidae

## Executive Summary

This EPA-sponsored study by Dennis W. Rondorf and James F. Kitchell examines how thermal discharge from the [[Columbia Generating Station]] cooling lake (Lake Columbia, Wisconsin) alters fish populations, spawning timing, and larval distribution. The study is organized around three objectives: changes in adult fish community structure, reproductive timing and spatial limits of spawning, and distribution of larval and early juvenile fishes along the [[Thermal Gradient]]. The central conclusion is that heated water shifts the fish community toward thermally tolerant species, advances and shortens spawning seasons, and reduces larval fish diversity, with [[Entrainment]] of larvae a continuing concern.

## Background & Description

The cooling lake was created to dissipate waste heat from the [[Columbia Generating Station]], which has two 527-MW generating units. Impoundment and heated discharge produced a dramatic change in the fish community, with the species diversity of fish, measured using the [[Shannon-Weaver Diversity Index]], declining during the first year of thermal input. The research was conducted as a subproject of a broader ecosystem assessment of the cooling lake and used quarterly fyke net catches to track relative abundance over time.

Key species examined included [[White Bass]] and [[Black Crappie]] (selected because adults were growing well but not producing young), [[Gizzard Shad]] and [[Bluegill]] (larval studies, both of which increased in the cooling lake), and [[Northern Pike]] (which declined sharply).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime:** Heated effluent created water temperatures during winter and early spring at or above those that trigger spring spawning, altering the normal thermal and photoperiod cues for gametogenesis.
- **Habitat change:** Reduced aquatic vegetation resulting from thermal input is proposed as a likely cause of declines in some species, particularly those needing vegetation to spawn.
- **Temperature tolerance:** Species were compared using [[Preferred Temperature]] and [[Incipient Lethal Temperature]] values, classified by reproductive guild (nonguarding vs. guarding spawners).
- **Thermal behavior:** Juvenile fish show thermoregulatory behavior, and larval fish move horizontally in response to the thermal gradient, with a mode at roughly 28–31 °C.
- **Entrainment:** Pelagic larvae drift in the recirculating lake and can be pulled into the plant intake if lake turnover time is too short for metamorphosis into more mobile juveniles.

## Key Findings

**Adult community changes**
- [[Northern Pike]] mean quarterly catch-per-effort declined 99% between the first and third study years and disappeared from catches during the summer quarter of 1976.
- [[Bluegill]] abundance increased more than eight-fold between the first and third years, attributed to high thermal preference, open-water foraging, and suitable spawning habitat.

**Reproductive timing and spatial limits**
- A rapid temperature rise following a three-week power plant shutdown in 1977 stimulated spawning, reduced available spawning area, and caused black crappie to aggregate at the coolest available water.
- Elevated temperatures later induced resorption of black crappie ova, loss of secondary sexual characteristics, and were probably near the upper lethal temperatures of embryos and larvae.
- Combined operation of both generating units caused spawning to occur about 30 days (roughly one month) earlier in spring than when only Columbia I operated.
- Reproductive success was spatially limited to areas within thermal tolerance limits.

**Larval and juvenile distribution**
- Larval fish initially disperse into limnetic waters and later aggregate in warmer littoral areas as juveniles.
- Species diversity of larval fish catches was low in 1977, when water temperatures rose rapidly.
- The median capture temperature of larval *Lepomis* and gizzard shad was near 30 °C; abundance of larval gizzard shad completing diel vertical movements was reduced at temperatures above 31 °C.
- Pelagic larvae and early juveniles of bluegill and gizzard shad were most abundant at 28–31 °C.

**Conclusions**
- Reduced vegetation, fish mortality from temperatures above upper lethal limits, absence of colonization by warm-water species, and limited reproductive success together accentuated the decline in species diversity.
- Shutdown-and-restart cycles that cause rapid temperature changes can produce aberrant reproductive responses in spring-spawning fish.

**Recommendations**
1. Expect loss of aquatic vegetation in cooling lakes with heavy thermal loading; do not attempt to enhance vegetation-dependent species.
2. Account for earlier spawning (about one month) when setting closed seasons, since regulations may not apply to cooling lake fisheries; earlier spawning may also open a fishery while other lakes are closed.
3. Operate more than one generating unit during shutdown restarts, as multiple units buffer rapid temperature changes.
4. Estimate cooling lake temperatures and suitable areas during planning, providing adequate area within thermal tolerance limits for oogenesis, spawning, and incubation.
5. Size lake area and volume, or add cooling towers to increase turnover time, so that larvae can metamorphose before reaching intakes; accept entrainment as part of the cooling lake environment while minimizing it through design.
