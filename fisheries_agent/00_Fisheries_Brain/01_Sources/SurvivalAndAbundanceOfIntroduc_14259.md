# Survival and Abundance of Introduced Fishes near San Antonio, Texas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #introduced-species #Poeciliidae #Cichlidae #Loricariidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1978 study (Hubbs et al.) documents the substantial presence of introduced ("exotic") fish species in two Bexar County, Texas waters: the upper [[San Antonio River]] spring run and the thermally-influenced [[Braunig Reservoir]]. Exotic species comprised 35% of species richness in the San Antonio River (6 of 17 species) and 43% in Braunig Reservoir (6 of 14 species). The introduced fauna falls into three biogeographic categories: (1) south Texas natives displaced northward ([[Astyanax mexicanus]], [[Poecilia latipinna]], [[Poecilia formosa]], [[Cichlasoma cyanoguttatum]]); (2) estuarine species recently colonizing inland waters ([[Cyprinodon variegatus]], [[Menidia beryllina]]); and (3) true intercontinental exotics ([[Hypostomus plecostomus]], [[Poecilia reticulata]], [[Sarotherodon mossambicus]], [[Sarotherodon aureus]]) originating from South America, the Lesser Antilles, Africa, and Israel respectively. Notably, this paper documents the first record of a self-sustaining wild guppy (*Poecilia reticulata*) population in Texas, while the previously reported exotic *Belonesox belizanus* appears to have gone locally extinct, leaving the net count of established exotic poeciliids in Texas unchanged. The authors express significant concern about the ecological risk of continued exotic releases, particularly into spring-fed systems with endemic, adapted native biota.

## Background & Description

- **[[Hypostomus plecostomus]]** ("armadillo del rio," proposed common name): 50 specimens examined, ranging **13–238 mm SL**. Size distribution indicated even representation from juveniles to breeding adults, suggesting a long breeding season. Sample contained 13 mature/maturing males and 8 females; males attained larger maximum size (8 individuals ≥199 mm) than the largest female (182 mm). "Maturing" males ranged 130–180 mm; "maturing" females 116–181 mm. A gravid 182 mm female carried **716 yolked eggs, 3.25 mm in diameter**. Gut contents consisted of algae and vegetal material (herbivorous), contrasting with the arthropod diet of ecologically analogous native darters.

- **[[Poecilia reticulata]]** (guppy): Over 100 specimens preserved (young to breeding adults), plus additional live-maintained individuals. Males exhibited bright coloration typical of aquarium-selected ornamental strains; females occasionally showed modest coloration. Most large females were gravid.

- **[[Cyprinodon variegatus]]** (sheepshead minnow): Observed breeding in shallow water; no detailed morphometrics given, but habitat preference (soft sand/mud, minimal vegetation) noted.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**San Antonio River (spring run, sampled Nov. 18, 1977):**
- 17 total fish species; 11 likely native (*Notropis lutrensis, N. amabilis, N. volucellus, Ictalurus natalis, Gambusia affinis, Micropterus salmoides, Lepomis gulosus, L. cyanellus, L. megalotis, L. punctatus, L. macrochirus*).
- **[[Astyanax mexicanus]]**: abundant midwater schooling fish; introduced into the Guadalupe Basin 1908–1930, first San Antonio River record 1940 — a long-established population, likely sourced from Rio Grande/Nueces stocks.
- **[[Hypostomus plecostomus]]**: abundant benthic species occupying a niche functionally parallel to native darters (e.g., *Percina caprodes*, *Etheostoma fonticola*) — adults in swift current under boulders, juveniles in heavy vegetation/slow current. Diet (algae/detritus) suggests low direct food competition with darters, but possible competitive displacement of herbivorous cyprinids like [[Campostoma anomalum]] (stoneroller) or [[Dionda episcopa]] (roundnose minnow), both notably absent from samples.
- **[[Poecilia reticulata]]**: found abundantly only in a stenothermal well-fed ditch draining the San Antonio Zoo; first documented persistent wild Texas population (a prior Austin population in Waller Creek failed to overwinter — Edwards 1976). Abundance possibly enhanced by scarcity of predatory centrarchids in that ditch. Expected to eventually disperse to the main river.
- **[[Poecilia latipinna]]** (sailfin molly): abundant in shallow, low-current habitats; original Florida-stock escape documented in 1939, supplemented by Louisiana and possibly native Texas stock introductions.
- **[[Cichlasoma cyanoguttatum]]** (Rio Grande cichlid): abundant in quiet water; native range restricted to Nueces/Rio Grande systems; introduced via National Fish Hatchery (San Marcos, 1928–1941) stock originally from near Mission, TX.
- **[[Sarotherodon mossambicus]]** (Mozambique tilapia): found in low numbers in zoo drainage ditches, more abundant in adjacent zoo canals; likely aquarium-release origin; expected to competitively impact the non-native Rio Grande cichlid.
- **[[Belonesox belizanus]]** (pike killifish): previously reported (Barron 1964) but absent from current samples — apparent local extirpation.

**Braunig Reservoir (power-plant cooling reservoir, sampled Sept. 29, 1977):**
- 14 total species; 7 likely native (*Dorosoma cepedianum, D. petenense, Pimephales vigilax, Gambusia affinis, Lepomis cyanellus, L. megalotis, L. macrochirus*), 1 possibly native (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*).
- **[[Cyprinodon variegatus]]**: found over soft sand/mud with minimal vegetation, breeding observed in shallow southwestern cove waters; natural range is estuarine — inland occurrence may relate to broader west Texas dispersal patterns.
- **[[Poecilia formosa]]** (Amazon molly): sparse numbers in northeastern cove; first Bexar County record; gynogenetic reproduction favored by heated reservoir conditions; likely accompanied a game-fish stocking release (possibly from Lula Sams State Fish Hatchery); reproductively parasitizes its sexual host [[Poecilia latipinna]].
- **[[Menidia beryllina]]** (tidewater silverside): found on sandy beach habitat; naturally estuarine, but documented to disperse >100 km from reservoir release sites.
- **[[Sarotherodon aureus]]** (blue tilapia): present in large numbers, potentially the dominant fish species in the reservoir, possibly outcompeting native gizzard shad. Comparable population explosions documented in Trinidad Reservoir, TX, where a population crash occurred during a cold-weather power plant outage. Also documented in Canyon Reservoir (Guadalupe River) and near a power plant on the Canadian River, Oklahoma.
- Estuarine exotics (*Menidia beryllina, Poecilia formosa, P. latipinna, Cyprinodon variegatus*) are hypothesized to have accompanied stocked marine sport fish such as **red drum** (*Sciaenops ocellata*), which do not reproduce in the reservoir and require periodic restocking.

## Key Findings

- Exotic fish species constituted **35%** of species richness in the San Antonio River and **43%** in Braunig Reservoir — a substantial fraction of the local fish fauna in both systems.
- Four categories of exotic origin identified: (1) south Texas natives displaced northward, (2) recent estuarine colonizers, (3) intercontinental exotics from South America/Lesser Antilles/Africa/Israel.
- **First documented naturalized Texas population of [[Poecilia reticulata]]** (guppy), surviving in stenothermal spring/well waters analogous to other successful U.S. guppy introductions.
- **[[Belonesox belizanus]]**, previously reported by Barron (1964), appears to have gone extinct in the San Antonio system, keeping the net number of established exotic poeciliids in Texas unchanged despite the guppy's addition.
- **[[Hypostomus plecostomus]]** shows clear evidence of an established, actively breeding population with a long breeding season and a niche convergent with native benthic darters, though dietary differences (herbivory vs. arthropod feeding) suggest competitive displacement is more likely with native herbivorous cyprinids (stoneroller, roundnose minnow) than with darters.
- **[[Sarotherodon aureus]]** (blue tilapia) may dominate Braunig Reservoir's fish biomass, raising concern about repeat of the population explosion and subsequent cold-related mass mortality documented at Trinidad Reservoir.
- Estuarine species (sheepshead minnow, tidewater silverside) are increasingly found in inland impounded/thermal waters, likely facilitated by reservoir construction and possible co-introduction with stocked marine sport fish (red drum).
- Authors emphasize the greater ecological risk posed by exotic establishment in the spring-fed San Antonio River (which harbors an adapted, potentially fragile endemic native fauna) compared to the artificial, thermally/hydrologically confined Braunig Reservoir, and call for stricter precautions to prevent further unintentional exotic releases.
