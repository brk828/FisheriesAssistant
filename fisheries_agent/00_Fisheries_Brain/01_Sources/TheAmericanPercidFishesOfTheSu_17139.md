# The American Percid Fishes of the Subgenus Villora

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This systematic revision by [[Bruce B. Collette]] and [[Ralph W. Yerger]] (1962, *Tulane Studies in Zoology* 9(4)) argues that the darter subgenus [[Subgenus Villora]] should stand separate from [[Subgenus Hololepis]]. It also resurrects [[Etheostoma Okaloosae]] as a valid species, removing it from the synonymy of [[Etheostoma Swaini]], and documents geographic variation within [[Etheostoma Edwini]]. The authors base the work on more than 1100 specimens of *E. edwini* and nearly 200 of *E. okaloosae*. They conclude that *E. okaloosae* is a relict species, isolated in small streams tributary to Choctawhatchee Bay during Pleistocene sea-level changes.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic history.** [[Hubbs and Cannon]] (1935) described *Villora* as a genus based on *edwini*. Later workers reduced the darter genera to *Percina*, *Ammocrypta* and *Etheostoma*, and Bailey and Gosline (1955) placed *edwini* under *Hololepis*. Collette and Yerger reject that placement. Yerger's Florida panhandle fieldwork confirmed *okaloosae* as valid, and the authors note that Bailey, Winn and Smith (1954) had synonymized it with *E. swaini*.

**Diagnosis of Subgenus Villora.** The authors list the following diagnostic traits:
- Pored lateral-line scales 20–36; unpored 0–18; total 32–42
- Lateral line slightly arched, incomplete to complete
- Genital papilla of breeding female a low tube crowned with villi
- Prominent dark humeral spot
- No breeding tubercles
- First dorsal fin with 8–11 spines; second dorsal with 9–13 rays
- Vertebrae 33–38
- Males reach a larger maximum size than females

**Species accounts.**
- *Etheostoma edwini*: Holotype UMMZ 87892, a 38 mm male from the Santa Fe River at Poe Springs, Florida. Males in breeding color have orange-red spots on the body and fins. The authors propose the more descriptive common name "redspot darter."
- *Etheostoma okaloosae*: Holotype ANSP 69159, a 24.4 mm female from Little Rocky Creek, Okaloosa County, Florida. It has a shorter, more rugose genital papilla and lacks red body spots. It usually has 9 preoperculomandibular pores, while *edwini* and *swaini* usually have 10.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** *E. edwini* lives in clear to slightly turbid streams 6 inches to 2–3 feet deep, with moderate current, sand or gravel bottoms and variable vegetation. Recorded pH ranges from 6.4 to 7.8, with the most alkaline readings in limestone spring runs. *E. okaloosae* occupies small to medium clear streams (5–40 ft wide, 6 in–4 ft deep) with pH 6.6–6.9 and clean sand bottoms.

**Comparative stream chemistry (Crittenden, 1958).**
- Econfina Creek (with *E. edwini*): pH 7.5–7.8; methyl orange alkalinity 45.5–56.0 ppm; spring-fed and clear
- Cedar Creek (with *E. fusiforme barratti*): pH 7.3; alkalinity 35.0 ppm; sluggish and stained

**Niche partitioning.** In *okaloosae*'s streams, *E. okaloosae* is most common around bullrush (*Scirpus*) clumps over clean sand in slower water. The swifter channels are occupied by *Percina nigrofasciata*. Common associated fishes include *Notropis hypselopterus*, *Micropterus salmoides* and *Lepomis punctatus*.

**Biogeography.** *E. edwini* ranges from the St. Johns River west to the Perdido River and north to the Fall Line. It does not cross the Suwannee Straits, although the authors add the St. Johns drainage populations of Marion and Putnam counties to Neill's list of species that barely penetrate the peninsula. *E. okaloosae* is confined to streams flowing into Rocky and Boggy bayous near Niceville. The authors suggest that a Pleistocene peninsula or island above the 150-foot contour isolated these headwater populations.

## Key Findings

1. **Subgeneric status.** *Villora* is treated as a separate phyletic line, probably derived from *Oligocephalus*. It lacks breeding tubercles and has a nearly complete lateral line, a low villous genital papilla and a humeral spot.

2. **Species resurrection.** *E. okaloosae* is valid and closely related to *E. edwini*. It differs from *E. swaini* in preoperculomandibular pore number, genital papilla shape, lateral-line arch, squamation of the breast, nape and prepectoral area, and habitat.

3. **Relict status.** *E. okaloosae* is judged the more primitive species. It has a more nearly complete lateral line and a deeper body, and males lack body spots. The authors conclude it is a relict of a wider former range, now persisting in streams tributary to Choctawhatchee Bay.

4. **Geographic variation in *E. edwini*.** Three slightly differentiated races are recognized:
   - An eastern race (St. Johns and Suwannee) with more pored lateral-line scales, total lateral-line scales and dorsal spines (modal first dorsal spines 10; means 9.69 and 9.55)
   - An Ochlockonee race with modal nine preoperculomandibular pores
   - A western race (Pensacola, Perdido, Choctawhatchee) with more nape scales

   The Apalachicola populations lack distinguishing characters.

5. **Development.** Pored lateral-line scales begin to appear at about 12–13 mm SL and may not reach their definitive number until about 22 mm. The supratemporal canal is incomplete in juveniles. Adults usually have it complete, but some populations retain the juvenile condition.

6. **Reproduction.**
   - *E. edwini* breeds mainly from February through early April, with ripe ova seen as late as June. Fry at 11 mm SL in early December suggest some fall spawning.
   - *E. okaloosae* ovaries contained large ova from late January to early August. Spawning was observed on March 25, 1961, in Tom's Creek in swift water over Nitella clumps. Eggs were laid singly in the vegetation, and a 5.5 mm larva hatched on March 27.

7. **Sexual dimorphism.** Males are larger than females in both species, more so in *E. edwini* (largest male 48.8 mm; largest female 39.9 mm) than in *E. okaloosae* (largest male 43.5 mm; largest female 40.0 mm). The authors link the stronger dimorphism to territorial behavior.

8. **Pleistocene history.** Lowered sea levels could have joined the *okaloosae* creeks into one headwater system. A narrow Pleistocene peninsula or island may have isolated the population, allowing differentiation before sea-level rise reconnected it with the ancestral *Villora* stock.

9. **Study scope.** The paper provides frequency distributions for lateral-line, fin-ray, pore and squamation characters (Tables 2–8), proportional measurements (Table 9) and a museum-based specimen list. The authors also note that Collette's 1962 dissertation covers *Hololepis* in greater detail.
