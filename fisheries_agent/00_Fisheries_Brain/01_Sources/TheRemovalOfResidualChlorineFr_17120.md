# Removing Residual Chlorine from Tap Water for Use in Aquaria

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquarium-water-chemistry

## Executive Summary

This 1958 *[[Turtox News]]* article by [[Vernon W. Cole]] and [[William M. Lewis]] of the Cooperative Fisheries Management Research Laboratory at [[Southern Illinois University]], [[Carbondale]], reviews methods for removing residual chlorine from municipal tap water before it is used in aquaria. Chlorine, added as [[Gaseous Chlorine]] or as [[Chloramine]], remains at lethal concentrations in tap water and can persist after several days of aeration. The authors compared aging, [[Aeration]], [[Aquarium Filtration]] with mechanical media, [[Activated Charcoal]] filtration, and [[Sodium Thiosulfate]] treatment. Sodium thiosulfate was the fastest and most practical method, working on both chlorine and chloramine. The remainder of the issue consists of reader testimonials and Turtox product advertisements.

## Background & Description

Most municipal water supplies are chlorinated to control disease-causing organisms. Laboratories that keep aquatic organisms for teaching or experiments often have no alternative to municipal water, so dechlorination is a practical necessity.

The article cites earlier toxicity work on the two disinfectant forms:

- **Chloramine** (a chlorine-ammonia compound) was reported to be more germicidal and more toxic to aquatic animals than gaseous chlorine (Wilhelmi, 1922). Coventry, Shelford and Miller (1935) gave the following values:
  - 0.3 to 0.4 ppm killed trout fry instantly.
  - 0.06 ppm killed trout fry within 48 hours.
  - 0.4 ppm killed sunfish and bullheads.
  - 0.7 ppm killed hardy minnow species.
  - 1.2 ppm caused mortality in large carp and bullheads.
  - Chloramine does not decompose spontaneously and was not removed by three to four hours of boiling.
- **Gaseous chlorine** toxicity reports included 2.0 ppm killing cladocerans (Adams, 1927), 1.0 ppm being toxic to coarse fish (Davis, 1934), and 1.0 ppm killing goldfish within 96 hours (Ellis, 1937).

The authors state that the common residual-chlorine removal methods are aging, aeration, filtration through various media, and sodium thiosulfate. Each was tested against gaseous chlorine, and the chemical method was also tested against chloramine.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study is concerned with water quality in laboratory aquaria rather than natural ecosystems. Key environmental observations include:

- **Tap water variability.** Chlorine in Carbondale city water was sampled periodically from the aquarium room tap. Readings varied from day to day and rose as water ran. In the first few minutes the level ranged from 0.0 to 0.5 ppm. The source reports an average of "10 ppm" at 20 minutes, but this is inconsistent with the stated maximum of 3.1 ppm and is probably a transcription error. By 45 minutes the level stabilized at a maximum of 2.0 to 3.1 ppm. The average daily chlorine content was approximately 2.1 ppm, ranging from 1.2 to 3.1 ppm. The authors concluded that treating tap water directly would be harder than treating water already in the aquaria.
- **Test concentrations.** Experiments used 3.1 ppm chlorine in 20-gallon aquaria, which approximates the upper end of the observed tap range. Chloramine tests used approximately 2.0 ppm.
- **Water analysis constraint.** Where aquarium water is to be analyzed for dissolved oxygen, sodium thiosulfate should not be used.

## Key Findings

### 1. Aging is slow
- Aging a 20-gallon aquarium without aeration required a minimum of four days to lower chlorine from 3.1 ppm to a safe level.
- Larger volumes needed only slightly longer.
- Vigorous aeration shortened the time by only one day for the same chlorine level.

### 2. Mechanical filtration outperforms aeration
- A filter with short pieces of glass tubing as a purely mechanical medium (Lewis and Cole, 1957) reduced chlorine up to 2.5 ppm to safe levels in 20-gallon aquaria in slightly over 24 hours.
- The authors attribute this superior performance to the greater surface area of the medium.

### 3. Activated charcoal is effective but still slow
- One pint of [[Activated Charcoal]] in a 20-gallon aquarium at 3.1 ppm chlorine reached a safe level for fish in about 8 hours.
- Chlorine was completely eliminated in less than 12 hours.

### 4. Sodium thiosulfate is the fastest option
- Treated tap water can be used immediately after adding sodium thiosulfate.
- Stoichiometry suggested 2 ppm of thiosulfate per ppm of chlorine.
- Laboratory tests gave a working factor of 0.008 g of thiosulfate per ppm chlorine per gallon.
- Thiosulfate has low toxicity, so overtreatment is not dangerous.
- Fish added immediately after treatment survived the full two-week test with no visible damage.

### 5. Thiosulfate also neutralizes chloramine
- Chloramine was simulated by adding ammonium hydroxide to produce approximately 2.0 ppm chloramine.
- Thiosulfate detoxified this water at the same dosing rate used for gaseous chlorine.
- Fish added after treatment survived the two-week test in good condition.

### 6. Practical dosing rule
- When chlorine cannot be measured, the authors recommend assuming a maximum of 5.0 ppm.
- At this assumed level, a 10-gallon aquarium needs a thiosulfate crystal about the size of a corn kernel.
- Overtreatment at this level is considered harmless to aquatic organisms.
