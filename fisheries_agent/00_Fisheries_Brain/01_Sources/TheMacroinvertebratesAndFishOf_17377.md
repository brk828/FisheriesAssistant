## Executive Summary

This study examines the effects of drastically fluctuating stream discharge on both fish and macroinvertebrate communities in [[Trout Creek]], a small third-order montane stream in central Colorado. A severe drought during 1978 caused surface flow to cease entirely in portions of the study reach, followed by a return to normal flow in 1979. The paper documents contrasting responses among invertebrate taxa (some favored by drought, some by normal flow, some unaffected) and severe, near-total depletion of fish populations during the low-flow year, with rapid recolonization following flow resumption. The study links hydrological disturbance to shifts in functional feeding group composition and provides condition-factor (K) and growth data for three resident fish species: [[Brook Trout]], [[Longnose Sucker]], and [[White Sucker]].

## Background & Description

- **Species collected:** *Salvelinus fontinalis* (Brook Trout), *Catostomus catostomus* (Longnose Sucker), and *Catostomus commersoni* (White Sucker).
- **Condition Factor (K):** Calculated as K = W·10⁵ / L³ (W = weight in grams, L = length in mm).
  - Brook Trout: K = 1.10 (1978, drought year) vs. 1.16 (1979, normal flow) — not statistically different (t-test, P>0.05).
  - Longnose Sucker: K = 0.94 (1978) vs. 1.03 (1979) — not statistically different (t-test, P>0.05).
- **Physical condition:** Brook trout collected in 1978 were described as thin and moribund when handled, with average weight-at-age values below those reported in comparable North American studies ([[Carlander 1969]]). Longnose suckers in 1978 had lower average length values than in other North American populations but otherwise appeared physically normal.
- **Scale analysis:** Regenerated scales (an indicator of environmental/physiological stress) were common in fish sampled in 1979 (post-drought), particularly in white sucker and brook trout, with some individuals bearing exclusively regenerated scales. No regenerated scales were observed in 1978 samples, suggesting the stress signature appeared as a delayed/recorded response to the prior year's drought.
- Scale preparation followed [[Bagenal and Tesch 1978]] methodology (NaOH soak, mounting, 80x magnification).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Low-gradient, gravel/coarse-sand riffle-pool stream segment at 2235 m elevation, lined with willows (*Salix* spp.); green alga *Cladophora glomerata* dominant in summer, with sporadic macrophyte (*Veronica* sp.) beds.
- **Stream sections:** Study area divided into upper (650 m), middle (390 m), and lower (400 m) reaches with differing hydrological resilience to drought.
- **Discharge regime:** Mean daily discharge averaged 0.78 m³/min in 1978 (drought year) vs. 3.98 m³/min in 1979 (normal flow year). The lower section's streambed went completely dry in 1978; middle section retained only a narrow central channel; upper section retained the most flowing water and pools.
- **Thermal stability:** Water temperature did not differ significantly between years despite large discharge differences, indicating strong groundwater buffering in this alluvial stream segment — decoupling temperature stress from flow-driven habitat loss.
- **Spatial fish distribution during drought:** Fish were virtually absent from the lower two sections in 1978; the lower reach's eleven residual pools held no fish at all. Surviving fish were concentrated in the upper reach where flowing water and pools persisted.
- **Refuge hypothesis:** The authors propose that [[Manitou Lake]], upstream of the study reach, served as a drought refuge, with fish (especially trout) migrating downstream into the study area upon flow resumption — consistent with the higher trout densities observed in the upper section in 1979.
- **Invertebrate functional feeding groups:** Relative abundance shifted between years — collector-gatherers and collector-filterers dominated in the normal flow year (1979), while shredders and predators had greater relative importance during low flow (1978), reflecting habitat loss, reduced suspended food matter, and altered predation dynamics.
- **Predator-prey dynamics:** The predatory dragonfly *Ophiogomphus severus* appeared to benefit from low flow conditions, likely due to concentration of invertebrate prey in the reduced wetted channel and reduced fish predation pressure on nymphs during the fish population crash.

## Key Findings

| *Salvelinus fontinalis* | 3 | 17 | 0 | 4 | 0 | 3 |
| *Catostomus catostomus* | 1 | 3 | 0 | 6 | 0 | 3 |
| *Catostomus commersoni* | <1 | 16 | 0 | 14 | 0 | 2 |
| *Catostomus* spp. (uncaptured) | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 14 |
| Uncaptured Salmonidae/Catostomidae | 15 | 24 | 1 | 40 | 0 | 94 |
| **Total** | **19** | **60** | **1** | **64** | **0** | **116** |

- Fish populations were **severely reduced or eliminated** during the 1978 drought year, particularly in middle and lower sections.
- **Rapid recolonization** occurred following resumption of normal flow in 1979, with total fish density increasing roughly 3-fold in the upper section and dramatically in the middle/lower sections (from near-zero to 64 and 116 per 100 m², respectively).
- Suckers ([[Longnose Sucker]], [[White Sucker]]) became well-distributed throughout the study area by 1979, whereas [[Brook Trout]] densities remained highest in the upstream reach, supporting the hypothesis of upstream-lake-sourced recolonization for trout specifically.

### Macroinvertebrate Community (Table 1) — Selected Taxa Density (no./m², 1978 vs 1979)

- **Total macroinvertebrate density** roughly doubled from 10,824 (±1604) in 1978 to 22,876 (±3577) in 1979 — a statistically significant increase (ANOVA, P=0.03).
- **Mayflies (Ephemeroptera)** showed the most dramatic recovery: from 2219 to 8906 per m² (ANOVA, P=0.001), driven largely by *Baetis* spp. (1096 → 6653).
- **Taxa favored by drought (higher in 1978):** *Hydroptila* sp., *Ophiogomphus severus*, *Cricotopus* sp., *Eukiefferiella* sp., *Palpomyia* sp., *Tipula* sp. *Cricotopus* and *Palpomyia* were significantly higher in 1978 (ANOVA, P=0.008).
- **Taxa favored by normal flow (higher in 1979):** *Baetis* spp. and *Glossosoma* sp., significantly higher in 1979 (ANOVA, P=0.006).
- **Unaffected taxa:** *Tricorythodes minutus* and chloroperlid stoneflies showed no significant year-to-year change (ANOVA, P=0.17).
- **Notable extirpation:** *Rhithrogena hageni*, previously abundant in Trout Creek, was extremely rare in 1978 and not collected at all in 1979.
- **New/opportunistic colonizers during drought:** *Capnia* sp., *Ablabesmyia* sp., Hydracarina, and Oligochaeta — not previously abundant but established during the low-flow year.
- Only **Odonata** (*O. severus*) declined in density from 1978 to 1979 among major groups — consistent with predator response to prey concentration under low flow.

- Collector-filterers' increase in 1979 was linked to significantly higher numbers (ANOVA, P=0.02) of *Hydropsyche* sp. and *Simulium arcticum*.
- Scrapers were never abundant in either year.

### Drift Density (Table 3)

- Drift density increased as discharge declined through the summer in both years, peaking in August in both 1978 and 1979 (higher peak in 1979 despite lower benthic density that month in most other periods).
- Mayflies and Diptera dominated drift composition; terrestrial organisms and scrapers were rarely abundant in drift samples.
- Counterintuitively, drift numbers were **greater in 1978** (low flow) during 3 of 5 monitored months despite substantially lower benthic standing density that year — attributed to reduced-flow-induced drift behavior (per [[Minshall and Winger 1968]]).

### Broader Ecological Interpretation

- Macroinvertebrates showed greater resilience to drought than fish, attributable to life-history traits such as egg/nymphal/pupal diapause, aerial adult emergence, and substrate burrowing — allowing rapid recolonization via oviposition and downstream drift once flow resumed.
- Fish, lacking comparable drought-survival mechanisms, suffered near-total local population loss but recovered quickly post-drought via migration, likely from an upstream lake refuge ([[Manitou Lake]]).
- The decoupling of temperature stability from discharge variability (due to strong groundwater influence) suggests that in this alluvial system, the primary drought stressor for biota was **physical habitat loss** rather than thermal stress.
