# Zoological Evidence: Fish Distribution and Hydrographic History in the Desert Basins of Western United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This classic symposium paper by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Robert R. Miller]] correlates the present distribution of fishes in the interior (endorheic) desert basins of western North America with the Pleistocene and Postglacial hydrographic history of the region. The authors argue that freshwater fishes, being tightly confined to their waters, are among the most precise indicators of past drainage connections, and that the fish faunas of isolated basins record former [[Pluvial Lakes]] connections. They conclude that most local differentiation and endemism among desert fishes dates from the cold, moist glacial-age periods, while some connections may be much more recent. The paper also includes a systematic, basin-by-basin review of Pluvial lakes and disrupted drainages, summarised here.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Interior drainage of the [[Great Basin]], plus disrupted parts of the [[Columbia River]], [[Sacramento River]], [[Colorado River]], [[Yaqui River]] and [[Rio Grande]] systems, and isolated drainages on the Great Plains, in Mexico and in Guatemala.
- **Terminology:** "Pluvial" (capitalised) denotes the late Pleistocene (Wisconsin-age) high-water stages when basins held lakes; "earlier pluvial" refers to older, poorly dated wet periods; "Postpluvial" denotes subsequent desiccation.
- **Table I** (not reproduced) lists the Pluvial lakes and disconnected rivers of the Great Basin and disrupted Pacific and Colorado drainages, their past connections, and fish-faunal relationships.
- **Major Pluvial lakes:** [[Lake Bonneville]] (largest interior drainage; Bonneville fishes comprise 5 families, 12 genera (2 endemic), 22 species and at least 28 forms including subspecies) and [[Lake Lahontan]] (about 5 families, 10 genera, 24 species and perhaps about 40 forms; 8 of the known species are endemic to the basin).
- **Dating:** Authors state the biological evidence is consistent with a late Pleistocene age for the Pluvial periods but cannot firmly date them; they note small, closely inbreeding populations may speciate faster than previously thought.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Relict biota:** The Great Basin is described as "a land of relicts"; moisture-loving plants, animals and fishes persist in isolated springs and creeks.
- **Dispersal:** Fishes are argued to disperse only through surface-water connections (active or passive migration); underground dispersal and waterspout transport are rejected as unsupported.
- **Paleontology:** Miocene and Pliocene fish fossils (e.g., a catostomid, a cyprinid, a sucker and minnow from the Esmeralda Formation) indicate earlier, quite different faunas. Pleistocene [[Stickleback]] and [[Killifish]] fossils from Lahontan beds show a former coastal connection, implying that Nevada was once lowland linked to the Pacific.
- **Sterile basins:** A large "Area of Sterile Basins" in southern Nevada, Arizona and California has no native fish and is inferred to have been largely fishless even in Pluvial times, apart from a few lake basins with ephemeral connections.
- **Climate:** Pluvial periods are associated with greater moisture and cooler temperatures; the authors note the Great Basin was "well-watered" in the recent geological past.

## Key Findings

1. **Fish distribution tracks hydrography.** Native fishes of the Great Basin occur only where surface-water connections exist, making them reliable proxies for drainage history.
2. **Endemism reflects isolation.** Bonneville endemism (2 of 12 genera; 10 of 22 species) and Lahontan endemism (8 of its known species) indicate separation since Pliocene or early Pleistocene time for much of the fauna, with most local differentiation dating from the Glacial period.
3. **Multiple disrupted systems.** Numerous lake basins (e.g., Lake Franklin, Lake Steptoe/Lake Waring, Railroad Lake, Lake Carpenter, Lake Madeline, Lake Surprise, Lake Tecopa, Lake Manix) show evidence of former connections, usually through "earlier pluvial" overflow or stream capture rather than through recent Pluvial outflow.
4. **Key genera as markers.** The chub *Siphateles* (Lahontan, Bonneville, Snake and Klamath forms), the dace *Rhinichthys*, the cyprinodonts *Cyprinodon* (Death Valley, Salton and Pattie basins) and *Empetrichthys* all show patterns of past connection; the Railroad Valley *Crenichthys baileyi* group links to both Lahontan and Colorado drainages.
5. **Lahontan had no confirmed outlet.** Fish evidence calls for a discharge at some time into at least one surrounding system (northwest toward the Klamath, south-west to the Sacramento, or into Death Valley), though the outlet was not located and an "earlier pluvial" overflow is favoured.
6. **Lake LeConte.** The Salton Basin main stage ([[Lake LeConte]]) is argued to be an old (perhaps early Pleistocene) feature, whereas a last high stage around 800–1500 A.D. is supported by archaeology and fish remains (bonytail, *Gila robusta*, Colorado River fishes), and a marine (Gulf) stage is inferred to be recent and tidally introduced.
7. **Speciation and Colorado linkage.** Colorado River fish types reached Great Basin basins such as Pluvial White River, Carpenter River and Las Vegas River, and the authors argue that Colorado River and Pluvial lake fish faunas are intermingled only in limited areas.
8. **Research agenda.** The authors call for more intensive field studies in every interior basin, better dating of hydrographic events (e.g., varve chronology), accurate mapping, and joint geological–biological work to correlate mountain and continental glaciation.
