# Observations on the Longnose Sucker in Yellowstone Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary
This 1953 study by Brown and Graham investigates the [[Longnose Sucker]] (*Catostomus catostomus*), a species believed to have been accidentally introduced into [[Yellowstone Lake]] by live-bait fishermen in the early 1930s. Conducted during the summers of 1951–1952, the research characterizes the species' food habits, spawning migration behavior in [[Pelican Creek]], age/growth patterns, and lake-wide distribution. The sucker population grew from initial introduction to runs of up to 2,000 individuals in the early 1940s, with the Park Service maintaining a policy of destroying all captured specimens due to concerns over competition with native [[Cutthroat Trout]].

## Background & Description
- **Sexual Size Dimorphism:** Females are significantly larger than males.
  - **Males (n=365):** Total length range 10.6–17.9 in (avg. 14.0 in); weight range 0.31–2.25 lbs (avg. 1.18 lbs).
  - **Females (n=342):** Total length range 13.6–20.1 in (avg. 17.0 in); weight range 1.19–3.16 lbs (avg. 2.07 lbs).

- **Growth Dimorphism:** Females grow faster and reach a larger ultimate size than males; difference is minor (0.1–0.4 in) in the first four years but widens to ~1 inch/year by years 5–7.
- **Maximum Age:** 8 years (matching maximum age found in Missouri River populations studied by Kathrein, 1951).
- **Scale Development:** Smallest fish observed with scales was 38 mm total length; smaller juveniles may overwinter in the lake without scales (paralleling findings in [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Introduction History:** Believed introduced via live-bait fishermen from streams like the [[Shoshone River]] or [[Yellowstone River]] (below the falls) in the early 1930s.
- **Population Trends:** Grew from a few specimens (1931–1933) to 300–400 (late 1930s) to 1,500–2,000 (early 1940s). Trap counts: 1,863 (1947), 1,057 (1949), 1,302 (1950), 1,150 (1951).
- **Distribution:** Concentrated primarily near the lake outlet and mouth of [[Pelican Creek]], but found dispersed in smaller numbers in [[Clear Creek]], [[Chipmunk Creek]] (South Arm), and [[Arnica Creek]] (Thumb region).
- **Diet (Tributary specimens, n=74 stomachs with food):**
  - Algae: 69% occurrence, ~33% volume.
  - Higher aquatic plants: 40% occurrence, ~10% volume.
  - Aquatic Insects (Diptera most prevalent at 55% occurrence; also Ephemeroptera, Coleoptera, Trichoptera).
  - Debris/unidentified organic matter: 80% occurrence, ~15% volume.
- **Diet (Lake specimens, n=5 stomachs with food):** Dominated by Crustacea (*Gammarus* and *Daphnia*) and Diptera (Chironomidae), suggesting a shift toward benthic/planktonic invertebrates compared to tributary-feeding fish.
- **Trophic Interactions:** Considered a potential minor food competitor with [[Cutthroat Trout]], though evidence for significant competition was inconclusive. Frequently preyed upon by American White Pelicans (*Pelecanus erythrorhynchos*) nesting on [[Molly Island]].
- **Spawning Habitat:** Migrates from the lake into tributary streams (primarily [[Pelican Creek]]) starting in mid-to-late June, with the run typically concluding by mid-July.

## Key Findings
- **Water Temperature Correlation:** The number of suckers entering spawning traps correlated strongly with daily water temperature fluctuations; a cold spell caused an abrupt drop in migration numbers.
- **Spawning Duration:** Tagged fish remained in Pelican Creek for an average of 19 days (females) and 17 days (males), based on 17 recaptured fish out of 328 tagged.
- **Weight Loss During Spawning:** Spawning individuals lost significant body mass, averaging 4.7 ounces for females and 1.8 ounces for males (potentially confounded by tag-related stress).
- **Age at Maturity:** Males rarely spawn before age 4; females rarely spawn before age 5. Most spawning males were 5 years old (51.4%), while most spawning females were 6 years old (45.0%).
- **Growth Rate Comparison:** Compared to Missouri River longnose suckers (Kathrein, 1951), Yellowstone Lake suckers grew slower in year 1 but grew faster from year 3 onward, ultimately reaching a greater total length by year 8 (18.1 in vs 15.5 in).
- **Tagging/Distribution Results:** Of 403 tagged suckers, 74 were recovered: 41 found dead in traps, 23 recovered from pelican droppings on Molly Island, and 10 recovered by fishermen between Fishing Bridge and Pelican Creek mouth—suggesting a relatively restricted home range.
- **Management Context:** The [[Yellowstone National Park]] Service maintained a strict policy of destroying all captured suckers, which constrained the tagging study (specifically limiting the number of released females) and likely contributed to the high incidence of weakened fish subsequently preyed upon by pelicans.
