# Freshwater Fishes of the Levant: Ichthyogeography and Natural Aphanius Hybrids in Egypt

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of three items. It opens with a fragment of a reference list (Coad, Fishes of the North-eastern Atlantic and the Mediterranean and related works). It then presents a paper by F. Krupp on the [[Freshwater Ichthyogeography]] of the [[Levant]], and closes with a paper by W. Villwock on natural hybrids between two killifish species, [[Aphanius Dispar Dispar]] and [[Aphanius Fasciatus]], from Egypt.

Krupp's revision, based on about 12,000 specimens, recognises 58 valid species of freshwater fish in the Levant, plus seven undescribed species. He divides the region into ten ichthyogeographical subprovinces and argues that the [[Jordan River]] basin is a centre of speciation. He proposes that the [[Orontes River]] and [[Jordan River]] were colonised through separate routes linked to the [[Tigris-Euphrates System]] and to Pleistocene drainage changes.

Villwock demonstrates that the large Aphanius population of the [[Bardawil Lagoon]] in northern Sinai contains both parent species and F1 hybrids. The same mixed situation occurs in land-locked waters near al-Qanatir, west of the [[Suez Canal]].

## Background & Description

**Document composition.** The source mixes three distinct items:

- **Coad reference list.** This is a partial list of works on Northern Hemisphere fishes, including Trewavas (1983) on tilapiine cichlids, Vladykov & Kott (1979) on lampreys and Zhuravlev & Gomelyuk (1985) on ninespine sticklebacks. It ends with the author's address at the National Museum of Natural Sciences, Ottawa.
- **Krupp paper.** This covers the freshwater fishes of the Levant, with a species checklist (Table 1, 65 taxa), figures on drainage basins and subprovinces, and a long reference list.
- **Villwock paper.** This is a short study of natural hybridisation in Aphanius, based on field collections from Egypt and examination of morphology, gonad histology and enzyme patterns.

**Krupp's study region.** The Levant is a roughly 200 km wide strip of land along the eastern Mediterranean coast, extending from the Taurus Mountains to the Gulf of Aqaba. Its topography and drainage are shaped by a north–south faulting system that extends into the Red Sea rift. Three river systems drain the Levantine graben: the Orontes (Nahr al-'Asi), the Litani (Nahr al-Litani) and the Jordan (Nahr al-Urdunn).

**Taxonomic revision.** Krupp notes that about 130 nominal species had been recorded from the Levant, but only 58 proved valid. Roughly 40% of published records were based on misidentified material. Because zoogeographic analysis depends on accurate taxonomy, this error rate is a serious source of bias. Krupp's example is the range of [[Nemacheilus Insignis]], which differs markedly between the recent literature and his revision.

**Aphanius background.** Villwock notes that *A. dispar* occurs mainly along the shores of the Arabian Peninsula, Iran and Pakistan, reaching India, with some inland habitats of marine origin. *A. fasciatus* is mainly found in ancient and recent Mediterranean lagoons. A third species, *A. iberus*, occurs in Morocco and Spain. Earlier reports of *A. fasciatus* on the Palestinian and Israeli coast were not confirmed by Villwock, who found only disjunct *A. dispar dispar* populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dispersal and biogeography.** Freshwater fishes are well suited to zoogeographic analysis because they include many ecologically different species that depend on direct freshwater connections for dispersal. Krupp classifies the Levantine fauna by origin: Palaearctic, Afrotropical and "Indoasiatic" elements. He proposes "Indoasiatic" to replace "Oriental", which he considers misleading because it can refer to any region between the Middle East and Japan. The term refers to India, Indochina and Indonesia.

**Drainage history.** The distribution of fishes follows the geological history of the region.

- During the late Miocene and Pliocene, the upper Orontes flowed via the Proto-Orontes towards the Euphrates.
- Subsidence of the Gab valley and uplift of the Gabal Zawiya in the pre-pluvial Pleistocene split the Orontes into separate sections. Two internal basins formed, the Gab and the Hims basin.
- Up to the second faulting phase at the pre-pluvial/pluvial Pleistocene transition, the Litani and Jordan joined and drained through the Nahal Qison to the Mediterranean. Rifting of the Jordan valley then diverted this drainage south into the Dead Sea basin, and uplift separated the Litani from the Jordan.
- In the south, fish exchange between the Hijaz and the Dead Sea basin occurred via the Wadi al-Gizl system.

**Hybridisation context.** The Aphanius populations studied by Villwock are in lagoons and land-locked saline water bodies. Their mixed composition raises ecological and zoogeographical questions about how two valid species came into contact and how hybrids persist.

## Key Findings

**Levantine freshwater fish fauna (Krupp)**

- **Species count.** Sixty-five species of primary and secondary freshwater fishes occur in Levantine watercourses (Table 1). Of about 130 nominal species, 58 are valid and seven are undescribed. About 40% of literature records were misidentified.
- **Subprovinces.** Based on primary freshwater fish distribution, the Levant (part of the Mesopotamian province, sensu Berg 1934) is divided into ten ichthyogeographical subprovinces. The three subprovinces of the Syrian coastal rivers each have a distinct fauna and share only one species, *Capoeta damascina*.
- **Orontes.** This is the largest river in the Levant, with 34 species. Of these, 32 are primary freshwater fishes: 21 are Palaearctic, 10 are Indoasiatic and one, [[Clarias Gariepinus]], is Afrotropical. The Orontes shares 18 species with the Tigris-Euphrates system and only seven with the Jordan.
- **Jordan.** This is the second largest river, with 28 species. Of these, 18 are primary freshwater fishes: eight are Palaearctic, eight are Indoasiatic and two are Afrotropical. Six of its ten secondary freshwater fishes have Afrotropical affinities. The Jordan shares only three species with the Tigris-Euphrates, but six more of its species have their closest relatives in Mesopotamia.
- **Speciation centre.** The Jordan basin is an important centre of speciation for several lineages with Palaearctic affinities, including [[Acanthobrama]] and *Phoxinellus*. *Acanthobrama* is a Palaearctic genus endemic to the Middle East, of Mesopotamian origin. Its two main lineages arose in the Jordan system and later reached the Orontes, the Israeli coastal rivers and the Hijaz.
- **Critique of earlier colonisation models.** Krupp argues that the model of Steinitz (1954) and Por (1975), in which fishes moved from the Orontes via the Litani into the Jordan, cannot explain three observations:
  - the high endemism of the Jordan;
  - the fact that most Jordan primary freshwater fishes have sister species in the Euphrates rather than the Orontes;
  - the migration of Jordan-evolved species into the Orontes, with no evidence of movement in the opposite direction.
- **Pleistocene exchange.** Fish exchange between the Jordan–Dead Sea basin and the Hijaz occurred in both directions during the Pleistocene. Some species moved south (*Barbus apoensis*, *Acanthobrama hadiyahensis*), and others moved north (*Garra tibanica*, *Aphanius dispar*).
- **Afrotropical cichlids.** Tilapiine cichlids reached the Jordan valley from the south before the Pliocene and differentiated there. These include [[Astatotilapia Flaviijosephi]] and *Tristramella* spp. Later African cichlids (*Tilapia* s.l.) reached the southern Levant from the Nile via the Mediterranean.
- **Subprovince endemics.** The Nahr al-Kabir (N) shares an endemic population of [[Garra Rufa]] with the Nahr Quwaiq. The Nahr al-Marqiya subprovince has an undescribed endemic *Phoxinellus*.

**Aphanius natural hybrids (Villwock)**

- **Main result.** The large Aphanius population of the Bardawil Lagoon (northern Sinai) consists of *A. dispar dispar*, *A. fasciatus* and F1 hybrids of the two species.
- **Second locality.** The same mixed situation occurs in land-locked water bodies near al-Qanatir, west of the Suez Canal.
- **Methods.** Specimens were collected during three visits to Egypt (1983–1985). They were examined for all available morphological characters, the histology of their gonads and, to some extent, their enzyme patterns.
- **Significance.** The findings confirm a long-standing hypothesis by Tortonese (1947) that natural hybrids between these two species occur along the Suez Canal, and they raise ecological and zoogeographical questions about hybrid persistence in these waters.

**Key limitations**

- Krupp's revision is summarised in a table in a separate forthcoming publication (Krupp in prep.), so the full species data are not included here.
- Villwock's study is based on a limited set of localities and sampling visits.
