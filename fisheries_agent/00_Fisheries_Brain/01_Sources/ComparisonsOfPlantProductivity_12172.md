# Comparisons of Plant Productivity

#ichthyology #marine-biology #primary-productivity

## Executive Summary

This 1963 review by D. F. Westlake of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] sets out how to make valid comparisons of primary productivity among plant communities. It defines the key terms (standing crop, crop, yield, biomass, production, productivity), recommends a standard criterion (dry organic, ash-free weight), and specifies conversion factors for carbon and energy. It then compiles maximum-site annual net productivities for phytoplankton, marine and freshwater macrophytes, swamps, terrestrial herbs, forests and cultivated crops. The main conclusion is that the most productive natural and managed systems are temperate reedswamps, coniferous forests, intensively cultivated perennials and tropical rain-forest, swamps and cultivation, while phytoplankton and submerged macrophytes are comparatively poor producers. The document contains no fish data; its focus is plant and algal communities within aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems.

## Background & Description

The review opens by framing photosynthetic production as the primary energy source for nearly all life. It distinguishes gross production from net production (the fraction not respired by the plant), which the author adopts as the basis for comparison because it is most useful to ecologists and economists. The article's stated aim is to estimate the maximum average rate of net photosynthetic production sustainable over large areas (about 1 ha or more) through a year, under natural conditions or normal agricultural practice including fertilizing and irrigation, but excluding greenhouses, added CO₂ and artificial lighting.

Key methodological points include:

- **Units:** The author recommends kg/m² for biomass, metric tons per hectare (m.t./ha) for annual production and g/m² per day for daily productivity. The unit "m²" is warned against where it has been used to mean m² of area in older papers.
- **Dry weight:** Dry weight is determined by oven-drying at 104–105 °C; ash-free dry weight is obtained by ignition at 550 °C. The dry organic (ash-free) weight is adopted as the general criterion.
- **Carbon:** Organic carbon is typically 46–48% of organic matter in entire plants, with a range of about 44% to 54% under natural growth. Table 1 gives carbon and energy factors for biochemical fractions (for example, glucose 40% carbon and 3.73 kcal/g; protein 53% carbon and 5.7 kcal/g).
- **Photosynthetic quotient:** The author uses 1.2 (oxygen × 0.278 = carbon) for seasonally unfavourable communities and 1.25 (oxygen × 0.300 = carbon) for favourable tropical communities.
- **Energy:** Heat of combustion of entire plants is about 4–5.2 kcal/g dry organic weight in normal plants, against 3.73 kcal/g for glucose.

The review also notes that many published values are expressed in incompatible units, and that aquatic and terrestrial productivity have often been compared without standardised definitions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document discusses several ecological controls on productivity:

- **Underground biomass:** Underground organs are about 10% or less of submerged plant biomass but 50–85% for many aquatic and land plants with storage organs. Estimates diverge sharply between excavation and soil-core methods (for example, 7.3% versus 29–46% for *Zea mays*).
- **Phytoplankton sampling:** Productivity should be calculated using the photosynthetic (euphotic) layer, defined as water above the depth where light is about 1% of surface intensity, where gross photosynthesis is 10–20% of maximum, or the compensation depth.
- **Growing season:** Annual production is distinguished from growing-season productivity; for example, evergreen forests are assumed to grow all year, while many temperate grasses and aquatic plants are assumed to grow from spring to summer maximum.
- **Water and nutrients:** The most productive natural communities occur in high-rainfall areas or at water margins, and high agricultural yields largely depend on irrigation. Algae use much less water than terrestrial plants, which is relevant where water is costly.
- **Pollution and enrichment:** Phytoplankton in lakes and oceans is productive mainly where waters are enriched by human activity or in the tropics. Polluted, turbid rivers are considered unlikely to have high annual net productivity.
- **Light and gas exchange:** Submerged plants are disadvantaged by light absorption and scattering in water, deep mixing, and slow diffusion of dissolved CO₂ relative to gaseous CO₂ available to emergent vegetation.

Notable community examples include:

- ***Scirpus lacustris* (bulrush):** about 46 m.t./ha/year after correction, described as the most productive plant so far found in the cool temperate zone.
- ***Saccharum officinarum* (sugar cane):** about 58–65 m.t./ha/year in normal plantation cultivation, with the highest recorded value near 85 m.t./ha/year for the fodder grass *Digitaria decumbens* in a comparable study.
- ***Eichhornia crassipes* (water hyacinth):** a theoretical potential of about 150 m.t./ha/year under ideal, continuously growing conditions, but no reliable measured annual productivity was found.
- **Coral reefs:** annual net production of 32–39 m.t./ha, using Odum & Odum (1955) and Kohn & Helfrich (1957) data.

## Key Findings

- The author's Table 4 summarises probable annual average net primary productivities of fertile sites, with the highest figures in tropical agriculture using perennial plants, tropical rain-forest and reedswamp (up to about 75 m.t./ha/year). Reedswamp was revised to 38 ± 20% m.t./ha/year in the Addendum.
- **Phytoplankton:** Fertile freshwater and oceanic phytoplankton have low annual productivities (about 1–9 m.t./ha/year). Values above about 3 m.t./ha are generally found only in human-enriched waters or the tropics.
- **Freshwater macrophytes:** Submerged freshwater communities are much less productive than emergent communities. Fertile lake sites produce about 4–7 m.t./ha/year, while infertile lake sites produce about 1 m.t./ha/year. Tropical sites reach about 17 m.t./ha/year in Table 4.
- **Marine macrophytes:** The littoral seaweed *Ascophyllum nodosum* yields about 10–15 m.t./ha/year, and sublittoral *Laminaria* spp. about 18–31 m.t./ha/year. Coral reef net production is estimated at about 32–39 m.t./ha/year.
- **Swamps and marshes:** Fertile reedswamps and some salt marshes produce about 30–45 m.t./ha/year in the temperate zone. Some mangrove and emergent brackish communities reach up to 35 m.t./ha/year, and a marsh studied by Bray et al. reached 37 m.t./ha/year at its maximum site.
- **Forests:** Coniferous forests produce about 17–23 m.t./ha/year on average over the early growth period, and *Abies grandis* at least 45 m.t./ha/year at maximum. Deciduous forests average about 10–13 m.t./ha/year. Temperate forests generally produce 10–30 m.t./ha/year.
- **Cultivated crops:** Perennial crops and irrigated systems give high values (for example, *Medicago sativa* at 26–39 m.t./ha/year and *Zea mays* at 34 m.t./ha/year in Israel). Annual cultivated plants generally attain 25–35 m.t./ha/year, and temperate cereals are lower.
- **General pattern:** The most productive temperate communities are fertile reedswamps, coniferous forests and perennial plants under intensive cultivation. The most productive tropical communities are rain-forests, swamps and perennial crops. The author argues that increased productivity depends mainly on irrigation, year-round ground cover and using currently wasted plant parts.
- **Addendum (IBP):** The author notes the International Biological Programme's aims, including biomass methods that include underground production, combined with photosynthetic measurements, and studies of swamps, salt marshes and tropical forests.
