# Fishes in the Western Mississippi Basin (Missouri, Arkansas and Red Rivers)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #zoogeography

## Executive Summary

This book chapter (Cross, Mayden and Stewart, in Hocutt and Wiley, eds., *Zoogeography of North American Freshwater Fishes*, 1986) analyzes the freshwater fish fauna of the [[Western Mississippi Basin]], comprising the [[Missouri River]], [[Arkansas River]] and [[Red River]] basins and western Mississippi tributaries. The region is divided into 18 drainage units plus the Mississippi mainstream (19 units). The Recent fauna includes 266 species in 84 genera and 33 families, of which 235 are native residents and 34 are endemic. The chapter reconstructs preglacial and glacial drainage history from geologic, paleontological and faunal evidence, and explains present distributions as products of drainage reorganization, glacial displacement, stream captures and ecological filtering, with impoundment and introductions as major recent modifiers.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Western Mississippi drainages spanning five physiographic provinces: Coastal Plain, Interior Highlands, Central Lowlands, Great Plains and Rocky Mountains. Elevations range from above 4000 m to near sea level; mean annual precipitation is under 30 to about 150 cm.
- **Historical knowledge:** The fish fauna is well documented, beginning with 19th-century government surveys (e.g., Girard, Cope, Jordan, Meek, Evermann and Cox) and continuing with state distributional works for each of the region's states.
- **Paleontological record:** Fossils range from the Ordovician (about 500 mya) to the Pleistocene. This chapter treats only Cenozoic freshwater faunas. Sturgeons, paddlefish, [[Bowfin]] and [[Gars]] are the pre-Cenozoic lineages that persist in regional rivers.
- **Chronology:** The authors adopt revised Pliocene and Pleistocene boundaries (Pliocene from about 5.0 mya to 2.5 mya; Pleistocene younger than 2.5 mya) and argue that the traditional four-glaciation framework conflicts with the data. The type [[Nebraskan Glaciation]] till is bracketed at 1.2 to 0.7 mya, and at least seven tills occur in Nebraska.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Drainage history:** Cretaceous seaways were followed by a Paleogene plain. Metcalf (1966) recognized three preglacial components: the southward-flowing Teays/Mississippi, an Arctic ("Hudson Bay") system, and a [[Preglacial Plains Stream]]. The Arctic and Iowa (Des Moines) drainages are relatively well supported; the extent of the Plains Stream is ambiguous.
- **Glacial effects:** Early glaciation redirected Arctic outlets, diverted northern and Plains drainages southward, and mixed their faunas. The Kansan glacial maximum temporarily routed flow through the McPherson channel into the Arkansas system. The Mississippi later shifted to its present course at Thebes Gap, transferring [[Chologaster Agassizi]] into the western drainage.
- **Interior Highlands:** Drainage patterns here were largely stable since the Paleozoic, though the Ozark Dome was repeatedly uplifted and eroded, altering stream character.
- **Ecological controls:** Ecological factors, including flow variability, turbidity, temperature and habitat diversity, outweigh drainage divides in limiting most species' ranges. Four scenarios are proposed for highland disjunctions: preglacial wider ranges followed by glacial restriction; lowered sea-level dispersal across the Mississippi Embayment; ice-age transport along the Mississippi and lower Arkansas; and eastward shift of the Mississippi that added Coastal Plain lowland to the White and St. Francis basins.
- **Stream captures:** Proposed captures include the Belle Fourche–Little Missouri, White–Niobrara, Neosho–White, Neosho–Osage, Red–Brazos and Ouachita–Caddo connections. Several rest on genetic, faunal or topographic evidence; others remain speculative.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna size:** 266 species, 84 genera, 33 families. Of these, 235 (88%) are native residents, 31 are introduced or waifs, 2 are diadromous ([[American Eel]] and [[Alabama Shad]]), and 34 (14%) are endemic.
- **Dominant families:** Cyprinidae, Percidae, Catostomidae and Ictaluridae together make up about 70% of native fishes; Cyprinidae and Percidae contribute over 50%.
- **Diversity by drainage (native species):** White 150, St. Francis 139, lower Red 133, Ouachita (within Red) 133, lower Arkansas 117, lower Missouri 114, middle Arkansas 111, Meramec 96, Missouri River total 138, Red 152, Arkansas 141, upper Red 56, upper Arkansas 64, upper Missouri 36, Yellowstone 35.
- **Total diversity including introductions:** Missouri 173, Red 171, Arkansas 170, White 165, St. Francis 150, Meramec 108.
- **Endemism:** The Interior Highlands hold 27 of the regional endemics; the Rocky Mountains hold none. Notable endemics include [[White River Darters]] such as *Etheostoma euzonum*, *E. juliae* and *E. moorei*, and *Noturus flavater*.
- **Faunal similarity:** The White and St. Francis share 130 species (about 90% similarity); the Ouachita and lower Red share 118 (89%). Three phenetic groups emerge: a northern Plains–Central Lowlands Missouri cluster, a southern Plains (Red–Arkansas) cluster, and a southeastern Interior Highlands cluster.
- **Province patterns:** The Interior Highlands are the richest province (150 native species, 64% of the fauna); the Central Lowlands have 139; the Coastal Plain 122; the Great Plains 77; the Rocky Mountains 18. The Central Lowlands and Great Plains share the highest similarity (72%); the Coastal Plain and Interior Highlands share only 13% when marginal species are excluded.
- **Habitat associations (233 native nondiadromous species):** 166 (72%) small rivers, 122 (52%) creeks, 91 (39%) lakes, 84 (36%) large rivers; 3 cave-dwelling amblyopsids.
- **Westward and northward decline:** Missouri diversity drops from 114 species in the lower unit to 36 in the upper Missouri and 35 in the Yellowstone. Red River diversity falls from 133 to 56; Arkansas from 117 to 64.
- **Fossil evidence:** Several extant taxa, including [[Bowfin]], *Atractosteus spatula* and *Amia calva* (the latter in Miocene Kansas and Nebraska), occurred in the preglacial Plains but were later extirpated from the northern Plains. Percids first appear in the Irvingtonian (Wathena local fauna, about 1.2 mya).
- **Introductions (at least 27 species introduced; 40 more inhabit basins outside their native range):** Key examples include [[Common Carp]] (introduced widely in 1880; over half the Missouri River commercial food-fish catch), [[Grass Carp]] (introduced 1963 in Arkansas, now in Kansas ponds), [[Walleye]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Rainbow Smelt]] (released in Lake Sakakawea in 1976, then spread downstream).
- **Habitat alteration:** Impoundment has increased lentic habitat and changed downstream flow, temperature and turbidity. Plains specialists (*Hybopsis gracilis*, *H. gelida*, *H. meeki*, *Hybognathus placitus*, *H. argyritis*) have declined, and pelagic planktivores, centrarchids, Morone and salmonids have become dominant.
- **Gulf and Coastal Plain influence:** Several Gulf Coastal species enter the Red River fauna (e.g., *Hybognathus hayi*, *Notropis chalybaeus*, *Fundulus blairae*, *Menidia beryllina*), increasing its diversity above that of the lower Arkansas and Missouri.
