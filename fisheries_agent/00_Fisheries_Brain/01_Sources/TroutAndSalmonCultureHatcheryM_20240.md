# Trout and Salmon Culture (Hatchery Methods)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Fish Bulletin 164 is a practical handbook of hatchery methods for trout and salmon, published by the California Department of Fish and Game in 1976 by Earl Leitritz (retired, Inland Fisheries Branch) and [[Robert C. Lewis]], a fishery consultant. It was written to train new hatchery staff, serve as a reference for experienced personnel and private operators, and promote uniform, efficient operations. The bulletin covers hatchery water supply and chemistry, egg structure and spawning, egg measurement and handling, incubation, transport, rearing ponds, nutrition and feeding, grading, enumeration, transportation of live fish, diseases and treatments, anesthetics, and fish classification. Its central theme is that water quality, temperature, handling, and diet control egg survival and fish growth.

## Background & Description

The foreword traces trout culture to 1741, when Stephen Ludwig Jacobi began artificial propagation in Germany, and notes that major advances in methods occurred mainly after World War II. The bulletin was derived from an earlier edition (Fish Bulletin 107) and was updated in 1976 to reflect recent changes, including concrete raceway ponds, mechanical crowders and graders, automatic feeders, recirculating water systems, mechanical egg sorters, and air spawning. Sections were contributed by specialists, including [[Robert C. Lewis]] (selective breeding), Carl G. Hill and Lloyd C. Hume (incubator hatching), Howard L. Huddle (aquatic weed control), Robert Macklin (nutrition and feeding), Harold H. Hewitt (grading), Harold Wolf (diseases, treatment and anesthetics), and Leo Shapovalov (classification of fishes).

The volume is organized into major topics: [[Hatchery Water Supply]], [[Trout Egg Structure]], [[Spawning (Egg Taking)]], [[Egg Measurement]], [[Egg Picking]], [[Egg Incubators]], [[Hatchery Troughs]], [[Rearing Ponds]], [[Trout and Salmon Nutrition]], [[Feeding Practices]], [[Grading Fish]], [[Weighing and Enumerating Fish]], [[Fish Transportation]], [[Trout and Salmon Diseases]], and [[Anesthetics]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water supply.** Watershed characteristics (rainfall, mineral content, gradient, vegetation, land use) determine hatchery water quality. Limestone areas supply calcium and magnesium and raise bicarbonate alkalinity, which buffers pH. A moderate, even temperature of 45 to 60 °F is recommended. Springs are preferred over wells, streams, or lakes. Well water often carries excess gas, which must be removed by aeration.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Ellis et al. (1946) reported that 3 ppm is roughly lethal to freshwater fish at summer temperatures, and respiratory difficulty appears below 5 ppm. The lowest safe level for trout is about 5 ppm, with 7 ppm preferable. Oxygen solubility falls with rising temperature and altitude, and seawater saturates at about 75% of freshwater values.

**pH, carbon dioxide and alkalinity.** Natural waters typically range from pH 4 to 9. Ellis et al. found normal values of 6.7 to 8.6, with 90% of good fish faunas between 6.7 and 8.2. Normal dissolved carbon dioxide is about 2 ppm. California stream bicarbonates range from about 5 to 200 ppm. Waters with alkalinity below 35 ppm are considered soft and need buffering during disinfection.

**Total dissolved solids.** California surface waters range from 8.1 to 1,766 ppm. Moccasin Creek Hatchery (12 ppm) and Fillmore Hatchery (904 ppm) mark the lowest and highest mineral contents among state hatcheries. Excessive mineral content at Fillmore may impair egg development but can promote rapid growth after hatching.

**Toxic metals.** Very small amounts of zinc, copper, or lead are lethal to several fish species. Zinc from galvanized pipe can kill trout, and rainbow trout 2 to 4 weeks old died at pH 7.6 with 0.04 ppm zinc. Galvanized material must never be used in hatchery plumbing.

**Recirculation.** Mad River Hatchery (Humboldt County) runs 30 cfs of water, about 90% of which is reconditioned. Only 3 cfs of supplemental well water is added. Nitrifying bacteria on filter beds of rock topped with crushed oyster shell convert ammonia to nitrate, and the shell supplies calcium carbonate to buffer pH. Filters are cleaned at least twice weekly. The bulletin projects that by 1983 hatcheries may need closed reconditioning systems under tightening discharge rules.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Photosynthesis adds oxygen during daylight but consumes it at night, so overgrowth can cause oxygen depletion and fish kills. Herbicide concentrations that are safe for trout (for example, copper sulfate at 0.1 ppm) are far lower than those for centrarchids (1.0 ppm).

## Key Findings

- **Spawning efficiency:** Artificial fertilization of nearly 100% has been achieved in trout eggs. Eggs and sperm should be kept away from water for no more than three minutes before fertilization, and sperm should be added within about 30 seconds of mixing in a salt solution, which is one ounce of table salt per gallon of water.
- **Egg biology:** Egg death ("whitening") results from rupture of the yolk membrane, which releases salts and causes globulin to precipitate. Shocking (addling) deliberately ruptures infertile eggs so they can be removed.
- **Temperature limits:** Rainbow trout reared at a constant 60 °F grow about 1 inch per month, while at 45 °F they grow less than 1/4 inch per month. Brood rainbows must be held at or below 56 °F (preferably 54 °F) for at least 6 months before spawning. King salmon eggs held at 35 °F showed nearly 100% mortality.
- **Light sensitivity:** Direct sunlight kills trout and salmon eggs within minutes. Infrared lamps killed all exposed eggs in trials at Mt. Whitney and Mt. Shasta Hatcheries.
- **Selective breeding:** The Hot Creek fall-spawning rainbow strain was developed from spring-spawning McCloud River stock beginning around 1883. Selection raised the share of 2-year-old spawners from 53% to 98%, increased egg production of 2-year-old females fourfold over six generations, and raised one-year weight from 5 to 10.2 ounces. A 1953 Trout Broodstock Committee introduced a computer-scored selection index, with fingerling mortality (relative value 1.0) and fingerling size (0.9) weighted most heavily.
- **Egg size and fecundity:** At Mt. Whitney, spring rainbow averaged 427 eggs per ounce at age 2 (1,553 eggs per female) and 254 per ounce at age 3 (2,210 eggs), a 40% increase in egg size and a 42% increase in egg number.
- **Fertilization losses:** Broken eggs release albumen that clogs micropyles and is considered a major cause of poor fertilization. Overripe females produce hard, glassy "mooneyes" that are infertile.
- **Salmon handling:** All five Pacific salmon species die after spawning. Only king and silver salmon are common in California. Hatcheries use a mechanical "fish killer" and incision spawning.
- **Egg incubation:** Water-hardening takes up to 48 hours. Steelhead eggs at 51 °F hatch in 30 days (573.9 temperature units). The standard California vertical-flow incubator uses 3 to 10 gpm per 16-tray unit, with a malachite green flush of 3 ounces of stock solution daily.
- **Egg disinfection:** Incoming eggs are disinfected in a 1:300 [[Wescodyne]] solution for 10 minutes. Wescodyne at 1:20,000 is harmful to fish.
- **Trough oxygen:** Target dissolved oxygen is 7 ppm, with an absolute minimum of 5 ppm. A yearling rainbow's oxygen use rises from 3 cc/hour at 45 °F to 12 cc/hour at 68 °F, a 300% increase.
- **Rearing ponds:** The California standard is a concrete raceway 100 feet long, 42 inches deep, and 10 feet wide, with a 6-inch gradient per 100 feet. Concrete ponds replaced earthen ponds for hatcheries built after 1964.
- **Aeration:** Fresh-Flow aerators add about 2 ppm oxygen per series. Automatic feeders and mechanical pellet blowers reduce labor.
- **Nutrition:** Trout are carnivores with short intestines that cannot synthesize vitamins. The bulletin recommends 5 to 8% dietary fat, about 28% protein (with some evidence favoring 50%), and no more than 9 to 12% digestible carbohydrate. Of 16 recognized vitamins, 10 are considered essential to trout.
- **Feed quality:** Dry feed should not be stored longer than 60 days. Oregon Moist feed is used to start fry for 10 to 14 days.
- **Feeding:** Fish should be fed amounts below what they will consume. Table 15 estimates that about 7,654 pounds of food are needed to raise 25,000 rainbow from swimup to six per pound at 56 °F, a conversion ratio of 1.68 (about 1.88 at 51 °F, and 2.3 at 46 °F).
- **Grading:** Wilco and Murray-Hume bar graders leave 10 to 25% of small fish unsorted. The fish pump grader is the most efficient method for catchable and subcatchable fish.
- **Weighing:** Fingerlings are counted by weight-sample methods (16 or more per pound). Subcatchables and catchables may be weighed by scale or by displacement, where 1.02 pounds of water displaced equals 1 pound of trout (from a measured 1.018).
- **Transport:** California tank trucks range from 150 to 2,500 gallons, with capacities from 350-400 to 4,000-5,000 pounds of catchable trout. A Beechcraft carries 26 five-gallon cans, and fingerlings can be dropped from 300 to 1,000 feet into lakes as small as two acres.
- **Disinfection of planting tanks:** HTH (70% available chlorine) at 1/4 ounce per 25 gallons for 30 minutes, with glacial acetic acid added at 1 fluid ounce per 100 gallons where pH is above 6.
- **Disease control:** The bulletin lists bacterial diseases (columnaris, furunculosis, red-mouth disease), protozoans (Ichthyophthirius, Costia, Chilodonella), parasites (Gyrodactylus, Lernaea), fungus (Saprolegnia), and viruses (IPN, IHN, VHS). Treatments include salt, copper sulfate, formalin, malachite green, and antibiotics such as Terramycin.
- **Waste regulation:** The 1972 Federal Water Pollution Control Act amendments led to expected secondary treatment by 1977 and probable closed-loop systems by 1983.
