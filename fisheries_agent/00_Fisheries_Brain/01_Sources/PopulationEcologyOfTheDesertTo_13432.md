# Population Ecology of the Desert Tortoise at Goffs, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #testudinidae

*Note: this document concerns a terrestrial reptile (chelonian), not a fish. The tag line follows the required format, with the taxon tag adapted to the actual family.*

## Executive Summary

This 1984 report, prepared for [[Southern California Edison Company]] by Frederick B. Turner and Kristin H. Berry, documents a 1983 field study of the [[Desert Tortoise]] (*Gopherus agassizii*, also written *Scaptochelys agassizii* in the source) at Goffs, eastern San Bernardino County, California. Two 1-mi² plots were studied: Plot 1, a [[Bureau of Land Management]] Permanent Study Plot used for population structure and mortality, and Plot 2, used for egg production. The study aimed to measure naturally occurring rates of recruitment and mortality and to supply a basis for assessing the effects of [[Energy Development]] on tortoise habitat. The authors could not estimate egg infertility or early survival, the two critical life-table parameters they had identified in advance, and they recommend improved marking and nest-location methods for 1984.

## Background & Description

The desert tortoise is a highly visible species across its range in California, Arizona, Nevada and Utah. Studies have documented habitat disturbance and probable population declines, and the species was listed as Threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1979 after concern in southwestern Utah. Energy facilities (gas, water and transmission lines, roads, wells, power plants) are a major source of impacts. The California Energy Commission identified the tortoise as the key biological issue for a proposed coal-burning plant in Ivanpah Valley, and Southern California Edison was considering wind and solar projects that could require several hundred square miles of lowland desert over 20 years.

The study site lies in Fenner Valley, about 35 miles west of Needles and north of I-40, at an elevation of about 732 m. Plot 1 sits on a bajada with a 2% slope and southern aspect, dominated by [[Creosote Bush]], burro-bush, ratany and cholla cactus, with smoke tree, catclaw and desert senna in the washes. Cattle grazing and old vehicle tracks are present. Plot 2 lies about 7 miles southwest of Goffs. Plot 1 was laid out as one hundred 0.01 mi² quadrats in a 10 × 10 grid.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Weather.** Rain was heavy and unusual in 1983. Plot 1 received 110.4 mm in August 4–13 and 71.1 mm in September 26–October 14, far above the 1967 (95.5 mm) and 1968 (46.2 mm) annual totals. The authors judged reproduction in 1983 probably better than typical.

**Predators.** Potential predators included [[Kit Fox]] and [[American Badger]] (the main nest and carcass predators), coyotes, golden eagles, hawks, roadrunners, burrowing owls, ravens and shrikes. Avian predator observations rose from 0.05 per survey-hour in April to 0.28 per hour in September–October. Occupied kit fox dens peaked at 5 in June. Kit fox dens and excavated tortoise nests were concentrated in the northwestern half of Plot 1, probably linked to the highway along the southeastern edge.

**Vegetation and diet.** Annual plant production was about 40 g/m² (400 kg/ha). The grass [[Schismus Barbatus]] dominated, and [[Lotus Tomentellus]] was the most-eaten herb (37% of plant feeding observations). Tortoises favored herbs in spring, then switched to grasses and cacti (*Opuntia ramosissima*) as annuals dried. Plant similarity between the two transects was low (index S = 0.38).

**Soil ingestion.** Tortoises ate soil throughout the season, probably as a calcium (and possibly sodium) supplement. Soils from feeding sites had higher calcium (990 to 1073 ppm, versus 343 ppm in open desert) and sodium concentrations.

## Key Findings

**Population structure and density**
- 441 tortoises were registered in Plot 1 (April 18–October 14, 1983); 12 were hatchlings, leaving 429.
- Size classes among the 429: 119 (28%) <100 mm, 103 (24%) 100–179 mm, and 216 (50%) ≥180 mm (as reported in the executive summary).
- The sex ratio among tortoises >180 mm (115 males, 100 females) did not differ from 1:1.
- Capture-recapture (Schumacher-Eschmeyer chain method, four samples) estimated 219.3 tortoises >140 mm. Smaller tortoises were not estimated; the population of <140 mm animals exceeded 172.
- Earlier Goffs estimates ranged from 243 to 502 per mi² (see [[Burge]] 1980; Stratified Lincoln Index values).

**Reproduction**
- Nineteen radio-tracked females were X-rayed repeatedly from May to July 1983.
- Mean clutch size was 4.17 ± 0.22 (35 clutches; range 1–7). Mean clutch frequency was 1.89 ± 0.11.
- Clutch size was positively correlated with carapace length (r = 0.63 for all clutches). The regression is C = 0.054 × CL − 7.22.
- Egg size index showed no relationship with female body size.
- Fall copulation was observed (82% of 17 females mounted by mid-July to mid-September), but no eggs were found in fall radiographs.
- Estimated Plot 1 production: 796 eggs in 176 nests.

**Mortality and nest predation**
- At least 41 of 176 nests (about 23%) were destroyed by predators. This is a minimal estimate because rain obscured sign.
- Annual survival of tortoises >180 mm was estimated at about 97.3–98% (roughly 2% annual death rate, age-constant assumption).
- Remains of small tortoises suggested higher mortality, but the data were inconclusive.
- Of 72 carcasses collected, one was judged possibly killed by birds; most were attributed to canids or badgers.

**Provisional life table**
- Assumed female maturity at age 20; clutch frequency set at 1.5 for modeling (below the field estimate of 1.89); maximum life span 100 years.
- Of 12,691 eggs laid by a cohort of 100 females over 80 years, 50.8% survive predation and infertility, giving about 6,447 female hatchlings.
- Estimated annual death rate over the first 20 years is about 19%.
- The hypothetical stable age distribution predicted about 42% Juvenile 1 tortoises, versus about 3% observed at Goffs in 1983 and a 17-population California mean of 3.7%.

**Management recommendations**
- Develop new marking and labeling methods (e.g., injected dyes) to identify carcass origin.
- Locate nests, possibly by intensive monitoring of radio-tagged females.
- Fit more females with transmitters, and explore transmitters for tortoises <100 mm.
- Continue the Permanent Plot survey as a high priority; use a dog to find small tortoises in spring 1984.
- Prioritize the dynamics of the first three year-classes in future work.

**Limitations.** The authors stated that 1983 was their first season and that the study invested more than three times typical [[Bureau of Land Management]] contract effort, yet still failed to estimate egg infertility or early survival.
