# Planting "Keeper" Trout: Fall, Spring and Open-Season Stocking Results Compared

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary
This 1942 *Michigan Conservation* article by David S. Shetter and Albert S. Hazzard of the Michigan Institute for Fisheries Research summarizes five years of marking experiments. They compare when hatchery-reared "legal-size" trout (7 inches or longer) should be released: fall, spring (before opening day), or during the open season. The work covers 51 experiments and about 36,000 marked trout, in nine streams and seven trout lakes.

- **Streams:** Fall plantings gave uniformly poor returns to anglers. Spring and open-season plantings gave roughly two to six times more recoveries.
- **Lakes:** Results differed. Fall-planted [[Rainbow Trout]] in small lakes (under 100 acres) were recovered at high rates, 23 to 73 per cent.
- **Broader point:** Few anglers actually catch planted fish, so the economics of stocking adult trout depend on timing and water type.

## Background & Description
- **Angling pressure:** Pressure on Michigan waters has risen sharply since 1930, tracked by fishing license sales. Causes include shorter working hours, better roads and cars, and development of northern Michigan for anglers.
- **Intensity of use:**
  - Seven trout streams (creel census 1937–1939) averaged about 150 man-hours per acre per season.
  - Six lakes (1941) averaged 53.7 man-hours per acre per season.
  - [[Hunt Creek Experimental Area]] has never been below 180 man-hours per acre per season since the 1939 census began.
  - Pheasant hunting at Rose Lake Experimental Station never exceeded 2.45 man-hours per acre per season.
- **Stocking policy:** Over the previous ten years the Fish Division released legal-size trout "more as an experiment than a definite policy." These releases never exceeded 3 per cent of the 6–8 million [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] planted annually. Eastern states such as New Jersey, Connecticut and Massachusetts plant a larger share at legal size.
- **Cost:** Hatchery supervisors estimated each adult trout cost 15 to 25 cents by release.
- **Arguments for fall release:**
  - Roads are passable for tank trucks.
  - No winter feeding or labor is needed, and less pond space is required.
  - Some anglers believe fish should overwinter to become "wild" in appearance, action and eating quality. The authors state there is no scientific evidence for or against this.
- **Design:**
  - Known numbers of tagged or fin-clipped hatchery trout were planted at the three seasons.
  - Recoveries came from [[Creel Census]] clerks (furnished by the CCC and the U.S. Forest Service) or from voluntary angler and conservation officer reports.
  - Season definitions: fall = Labor Day to January 1; spring = about March 1 to the last Saturday in April; open season = last Saturday in April to Labor Day.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Streams:** Most stream data came from public waters in the northern Lower Peninsula. These include:
  - [[Pine River]]
  - [[Pigeon River]]
  - [[Au Sable River]] (North Branch and Main)
  - [[Little Manistee River]]
  - Baldwin Creek
  - Hunt Creek
  - The one exception was the East Branch of the Tahquamenon River in Chippewa County.
- **Possible regional differences:** Milder winters and fewer predators in southwestern Michigan might allow better fall survival. Experiments were under way in Dowagiac Creek (Cass County), Portage Creek (Kalamazoo County) and the Middle Branch of the Ontonagon River (Gogebic County).
- **Lakes studied:** Pickerel, Hemlock, North Twin and South Twin lakes (all under 100 acres), [[Burt Lake]] (27.3 sq mi), Crooked Lake (about 3,000 acres) and East Fish Lake (brook trout).
- **Movement and dams:** Dams near the Pigeon and Main Au Sable study sites may have limited upstream movement.
- **Growth in large lakes:** Planted rainbows in Burt and Crooked lakes grew 1 to 7 inches per year.
- **Brown trout in lakes:** They survive and grow where food and habitat suit them, but are difficult to catch and seldom stocked in lakes.

## Key Findings
**Streams: recovery by anglers (Table 1, 1937–1941)**

| Species | Fall (expts, fish, % recovered) | Spring | Open season |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brook Trout | 3, 2,798, 4.4% | 5, 5,300, 25.5% | 9, 9,751, 25.4% |
| Rainbow Trout | 4, 1,979, 4.9% | 4, 1,999, 19.6% | 10, 7,391, 25.7% |
| Brown Trout | 3, 1,500, 5.8% | 3, 1,448, 11.3% | 4, 1,000, 13.0% |

- Spring plantings produced 5.8 times (brook), 4.0 times (rainbow) and 1.9 times (brown) more recoveries than fall plantings.
- Open-season plantings were about equal to, or somewhat better than, spring plantings.
- No fall-planted brook trout were reported after the first open season, so there was no carryover. A very small number of brown and rainbow trout planted in spring or in the open season were taken in the second season.

**Few anglers benefit**
- Intensive creel censuses on six streams over four years found that 5.7 per cent ([[Au Sable River]] North Branch, 1939) to 20.6 per cent ([[Pine River]], 1938) of anglers caught marked hatchery trout.
- On average, about one angler in nine (reported as about 11.5 per cent) caught hatchery trout planted at legal size, regardless of season.

**Distribution (Table 2)**
- Fall planting gave no advantage over spring planting in spreading fish before opening day.
- Brook trout tended to move downstream 3–10 miles.
- Brown trout moved very little.
- Rainbow trout showed strong downstream movement, with the highest recovery in fish moving more than 10 miles. Spring-planted rainbows were more widely scattered than fall-planted ones.
- No upstream movement over 10 miles was noted. One unverified record had a tagged rainbow from the Pigeon River reaching Lake Huron off Sarnia, Ontario.

**Corroborating studies**
- Smith, [[Salmon Trout River]] (Marquette County, Huron Mountain Club, complete creel census):
  - 1,233 fall-planted brook trout (1938) gave only 3 recoveries in 1939.
  - 600 released in October 1939 gave 6 recoveries in 1940, about 1 per cent or less.
  - Spring-planted brook trout recoveries ranged from 16 to 67 per cent.
- Nesbit and Kitson, Massachusetts: about five times as many spring-planted brown and rainbow trout were recovered as fall-planted ones. The ratio was about 10 to 1 in some streams and about 2 to 1 in ponds.
- Cobb stated that fall-planted legal trout in Connecticut failed to survive in numbers to the next season, but offered no evidence.

**Lakes (Table 3)**
- Fall-planted [[Rainbow Trout]] in lakes under 100 acres were recovered at 23 to 73 per cent.
- Late-summer (August 4, 1940) planting in [[Burt Lake]] gave only 16.4 per cent, still at least three times better than any comparable fall stream planting. Likely causes were:
  - voluntary reporting only
  - the large drainage that must be fished
  - the late planting date
- Marked Burt Lake rainbows were recovered in every tributary and outlet stream.
- Adult rainbows may be stocked at any season in lakes with suitable temperature and habitat, with expected recovery of 20 to 70 per cent in following seasons. The result depends on lake size, timing relative to opening day, and angling pressure.
- At East Fish Lake (Montmorency County), the only brook trout lake with data, spring-planted brook trout returned about four times as many as fall-planted ones. Fall stocking of adult brook trout into lakes previously "poisoned out" has nonetheless been successful. Several such lakes treated in 1940–1941 were to be followed in 1942.

**Management implication:** For streams, release legal-size trout in spring or during the open season rather than in fall. Fall releases are efficient for hatcheries but return few fish to anglers. In suitable lakes, especially small ones, [[Rainbow Trout]] can be stocked in any season.
