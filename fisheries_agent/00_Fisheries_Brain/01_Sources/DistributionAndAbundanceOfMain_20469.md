# Distribution and Abundance of Mainstream Fishes of the Middle and Upper Colorado River Basins, 1967-1973

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

Holden and Stalnaker (1975), published in the [[Transactions of the American Fisheries Society]], report a field survey of fishes in the middle and upper [[Colorado River]] basin conducted from 1967 to 1973 across 12 sampling areas. Twenty-nine species were collected, of which 19 were introduced and 10 native. The native [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] dominated the study area. Four endemic large-river species, [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Humpback Sucker]], were rare and considered endangered. The authors attribute the decline of native fishes mainly to habitat alteration by high dams with cold tailwaters, and to introduced species.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Basin]] has a unique fish fauna, with Miller (1959) estimating that 74% of native basin fishes are endemic, a result of long periods of geographic isolation. The pristine river was warm, turbid and subject to sudden changes in flow. Endemic fishes fall into two groups: small-stream forms of the lower basin and large-river forms of the main stream and major tributaries. Because the lower basin has lost most large-river fishes (extremely rare or extinct below Lake Mohave), the upper and middle basins were identified as the last extensive natural strongholds for these species.

The large-river endemics treated in the paper are:
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- [[Humpback Chub]] complex (*Gila cypha*)
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
- [[Humpback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

The study area comprised the Green River system from Craig, Colorado, the Yampa River, and the Colorado River from Rifle, Colorado to [[Lake Mead]], excluding [[Lake Powell]], plus the lower Gunnison, Dolores, Price, White and Little Snake rivers. It was divided into 12 sampling areas, mostly canyon reaches including [[Dinosaur National Monument]], [[Desolation Canyon]], [[Canyonlands National Park]], [[Glen Canyon]], [[Marble Canyon]] and [[Grand Canyon]]. Areas 5, 6 and the upper part of 4 had low gradient and sandy bottoms; the rest had high gradients and gravel bottoms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Major habitat changes include two reservoirs built in the 1960s: [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] on the upper Green River and Lake Powell upriver of Marble and Grand Canyons. Flaming Gorge Dam was closed in November 1962 after a fish eradication program in September 1962. Below it, Vanicek, Kramer and Franklin (1970) found no native reproduction for 104 km downstream. Below Glen Canyon Dam, cold discharge, a daily 120-150 cm fluctuation in river level, and limited secondary production made the river unfavorable for native fishes.

Sampling methods included gill nets, seines, electrofishing and hook and line, with emphasis on seining shallow areas for juveniles as evidence of recent reproduction. Abundance was ranked as Abundant, Common, Rare or Occasional. Nomenclature followed Bailey et al. (1970), except for the humpback chub complex.

## Key Findings

**Species richness and origin**
- 29 species collected: 19 introduced, 10 native.
- Species richness was higher in high-gradient rocky reaches (areas 2 and 3 had 28 and 23 species) than in low-gradient sandy reaches (areas 5 and 6 had 13 and 12).
- Areas 7 and 8 had 15 species each, compared with 25 from similar Green River areas.

**Native species status**
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]: most abundant large native; over 6,000 adults collected, present in all sections.
- [[Bluehead Sucker]]: abundant in areas 1, 2, 3, 8, 9 and 10, associated with rocky substrate.
- [[Roundtail Chub]]: abundant in tributaries (areas 1, 2, 3, 9, 10), declining downstream; 375 adults taken.
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: 300 adults collected, 261 from area 2; juvenile recruitment was poor in 1969-71 despite stable adult numbers.
- [[Bonytail Chub]]: only 36 taken; three possible bonytails among several hundred young chubs; recruitment appears to have failed since Flaming Gorge closure.
- [[Humpback Chub]] complex: 61 taken, mostly from eddies; 26 near Echo Park in 1969 and 15 in area 11 in 1967, falling to one in 1970.
- [[Humpback Sucker]]: 53 taken, rare throughout; once abundant and used as food, now near extinction.
- [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] were the native species most likely to persist in tributary habitats.

**Introduced species**
- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*): common in middle sections, rare in cooler upper sections; the largest specimen was 5.9 kg.
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*): expanded its range up the Green River during the study; abundant in the Colorado River and Green River to Desolation Canyon by 1968. The authors suggest a late-1950s or early-1960s introduction near Grand Junction, Colorado.
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*): abundant only in area 8, near Grand Junction, where sewage enrichment created habitat.
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*): mostly planted or wandering fish, not self-sustaining except in Tapeats Creek.
- Other introduced fishes noted include the [[Redside Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Sand Shiner]], [[Creek Chub]], [[White Sucker]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Rio Grande Killifish]].

**Causes of decline**
- High dams and cold tailwaters are the principal cause; the authors add the Colorado River through Glen, Marble and Grand Canyons to the list of impacted reaches.
- Competition and predation from introduced species, especially channel catfish and small cyprinids such as the red shiner, may reduce juvenile survival of endemics.
- Reproduction of rare fishes was documented only in areas 4 and 5 (Green River) and the lower Yampa River.

**Hybrids**
- Flannelmouth x humpback sucker hybrids: 40 collected; no fertile hybrids reported in over 75 years of known hybridization.
- Bluehead x white sucker and flannelmouth x white sucker hybrids were also recorded.

**Conclusions**
- The future of the Colorado River's endemic fishes is considered poor. The authors state that little further alteration may push these species to extinction and suggest the river environment itself "should be considered 'Endangered.'"
