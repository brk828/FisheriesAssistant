# Fish Remains in Archaeology: Humpback Sucker Growth at Lake Cahuilla

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from Richard W. Casteel's *Fish Remains in Archaeology and Paleo-environmental Studies* shows how archaeological fish bones can reconstruct past fish biology and environments. The main case study uses a single [[Proatlas Vertebra]] of the [[Humpback Sucker]] ([[Xyrauchen Texanus]]) from an archaeological site on the former shoreline of [[Lake Cahuilla]], California, dated to about A.D. 1000. Growth rates back-calculated from annuli on the bone indicate that this prehistoric fish grew more slowly than modern fish from the [[Colorado River]]. The excerpt also briefly reviews broader patterns: climate-linked changes in fish faunas, range contraction of [[Sturgeon]] after deforestation and agriculture, and fishing-induced shifts in size and age structure.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is a catostomid sucker endemic to the lower [[Colorado River]] system in the American Southwest. The species is one of several native fishes there that have suffered serious declines following European contact. Modern information on its growth is limited, so the author turned to archaeological material.

Earlier Humpback Sucker remains had been identified from two archaeological sites in Arizona dated between A.D. 750 and 1400. The present specimen comes from an archaeological site west of the [[Salton Sea]], on a terrace 12 m above sea level. The site was occupied by the [[Cahuilla]] people around A.D. 1000, when the area held a large freshwater lake.

The specimen is a single proatlas vertebra, figured in dorsal and ventral views. The bone's structure allows annuli (growth rings) to be measured.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Paleolake setting.** During the occupation period, the site lay near the shore of Lake Cahuilla. The lake was fed by distributary channels of the Colorado River, which flowed westward into the [[Salton Sink]], a rift valley or structural trough. The lake's presence explains the freshwater fish remains at a site now in an arid desert.

**Broader paleo-environmental patterns (general discussion).** The excerpt also summarizes wider trends in the fish faunas of Eurasia:

- In the [[Amur Basin]], species such as grass carp are now found in the southern parts of the basin. Over the last 2000 years, climate changes are recorded in lake sediments in northern and central Europe, along with a reduction in the frequency of [[Bream]]. Reservoir construction is now making bream more abundant again.
- The author concludes that fish-fauna changes over the last 2000 years were largely the result of anthropogenic factors.
- [[Sturgeon]] distribution appears relatively stable until the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Deforestation and agriculture then reduced its range and abundance.
- Expanding fisheries, with their selective character, shifted the size composition of harvested stocks. Fishing also changes age structure and growth. Numerous studies report faster growth under heavy fishing pressure, likely because intraspecific competition for food decreases.

## Key Findings

**1. Methods: back-calculating growth from a proatlas vertebra.**
The growth rate was estimated with a generalized allometric relationship for the Catostomidae family:

log Y = 0.6143 + 2.6307 (log X), with regression statistics printed in the source as N = 503 and r = 0.98 (OCR text reads "V = 503r − 0.98").

Here Y is total live weight (grams) and X is the width of the proatlas (mm). The width of each annulus was measured, and the weight at each annulus was calculated with this equation. The results are presented as a reconstructed growth curve using [[Annulus Analysis]].

**2. Comparative modern samples.**
The same method was applied to modern specimens for comparison:

- Modern specimen from the lower Colorado River at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery, Mohave County, Arizona.
- Mean of three modern specimens from the Green River, Dinosaur National Monument, Utah.
- Prehistoric specimen from Lake Cahuilla, circa A.D. 1000.

**3. Statistical comparisons (paired t-tests, α = 0.05).**

| Comparison | Test statistic | df | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Modern Utah vs. modern Arizona | t = −1.83591 | 11 | Not significant |
| Archaeological vs. modern Utah | t = 2.66757 | 12 | Significant |
| Archaeological vs. modern Arizona | t = 4.95938 | 11 | Significant |

The two modern samples did not differ significantly in growth rate. The archaeological specimen differed significantly from both.

**4. Main conclusion.**
[[Humpback Sucker]] appears to have grown at a generally slower rate around A.D. 1000 in Lake Cahuilla than its contemporaries in the modern [[Colorado River]]. The author's evidence supports this conclusion, though it rests on a single archaeological specimen.

**5. Wider implications for archaeoichthyology.**
The case shows how archaeological fish remains can provide growth data unavailable from modern populations. The broader discussion adds that fishing pressure, habitat change, and reservoir construction have repeatedly reshaped fish populations over the last 2000 years. These changes make archaeological fish records valuable for paleo-environmental reconstruction.
