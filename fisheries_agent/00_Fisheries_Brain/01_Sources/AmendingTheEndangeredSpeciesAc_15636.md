# Amending the Endangered Species Act and Habitat Conservation Planning

#conservation-law #endangered-species-act #habitat-conservation-planning

## Executive Summary

This document contains two law review articles on the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of 1973. The first, by Donald J. Barry, explains why legislative attempts to weaken the ESA usually fail and why the Act has stayed largely intact. The second, by Robert D. Thornton, opens a proposal that section 10(a) of the ESA, which governs [[Habitat Conservation Plan]]s (HCPs), should protect whole biological communities rather than single species. The Thornton text is truncated after its introduction, so its argument is only partly present. Neither article presents original field or biological data; the species discussed serve as case studies of the law.

## Background & Description

**Barry article ("Amending the Endangered Species Act, the Ransom of Red Chief, and Other Related Topics").** Barry, a former attorney for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and later House staff member, frames the article around a freshman congressman's plan to require economic impact considerations in listing decisions. The article contrasts this with the 1990 experience of Senator Packwood, whose amendment to address the [[Northern Spotted Owl]] listing failed. The author argues that the ESA's resistance to amendment rests on four legislative maxims:

1. Committee jurisdiction is everything.
2. It is easier to do nothing than to do something.
3. It is better to move no bill than a bad bill.
4. You need a point of leverage to move an immovable object.

**Thornton article ("Searching for Consensus and Predictability").** Thornton, a private practice attorney who worked as Majority Counsel to a House subcommittee during the 1978 and 1979 ESA amendments, states that section 10(a) was meant to resolve conflicts between developers and conservationists by allowing "take" of listed species under an approved HCP. He notes that private parties often hesitate to invest in HCPs without assurance that future species listings will not impose new obligations. The introduction references the [[San Bruno Mountain Habitat Conservation Plan]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Northern spotted owl:** Range extends from northern California to southern British Columbia. Listed as threatened by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service on July 23, 1990. Listing impacts on the Northwest timber industry were described as potentially among the broadest of any ESA listing, though estimates varied widely.
- **Mount Graham red squirrel:** Endangered species in Arizona. Its population reportedly declined significantly after the [[Arizona-Idaho Conservation Act]] of 1988, which was intended to facilitate a telescope complex on Mount Graham.
- **Newlands Project and Pyramid Lake:** Contaminated drainage from the Newlands Project reached the Stillwater National Wildlife Refuge complex, which has been largely dried up, and reduced the Truckee River's natural flow into Pyramid Lake. The document states that this affected spawning of endangered and threatened fish, though the species are not named.
- **Sea turtles:** An amendment would have delayed implementation of rules requiring shrimp trawlers to use Turtle Excluder Devices to protect endangered sea turtles.

## Key Findings

**Barry article:**

- **Failed amendments:** The Senate rejected the Packwood spotted owl amendment by 62 to 34. Of three 1987 House amendments (including one to delist the Mount Graham species), the document reports vote tallies of 270 to 147 and 266 to 151 for two of them.
- **Committee structure:** The 101st Congress had 22 permanent, 5 select and 4 joint House committees. The Merchant Marine and Fisheries Committee holds exclusive ESA jurisdiction. Only 4 of its 45 members were from non-coastal or non-trade states, and none was from the Rocky Mountain region.
- **Enactment rate:** The 101st Congress introduced 7,390 bills in its first session, and 240 became public law. The author cites a three to six percent enactment rate for bills in general.
- **Holds and reauthorization:** Senators can place confidential holds on bills, and past ESA reauthorizations were used as hostage bargaining chips. Supporters' enthusiasm for another reauthorization fight was described as at an all-time low.
- **Lapsed authorization:** The ESA was not reauthorized for fiscal years 1986 through 1988, yet it remained fully in force because Congress continued appropriations. The author concludes that Congress is likely to keep funding the program beyond fiscal year 1992 regardless of reauthorization.
- **Mount Graham precedent:** Section 602 of the Arizona-Idaho Act deemed compliance with "Reasonable and Prudent Alternative Three" in the Fish and Wildlife Service's Biological Opinion to satisfy the ESA for the project's first phase. It also withdrew the 660,000-acre Kofa National Wildlife Refuge from hardrock mining. The author argues this package deal showed how leverage can move legislation, but later GAO criticism and litigation made similar approaches harder.
- **Fallon Paiute Shoshone settlement:** Section 209(j)(2) of the 1990 Act shielded Bureau of Reclamation Newlands operating criteria from judicial challenge for seven years "notwithstanding any other provision of law." Section 210(b)(9) states that the Act does not waive ESA requirements. The author argues that the leverage of the water settlement overrode the committee's objections, and the ESA-related concerns were resolved in favor of final passage.
- **Conclusion:** The ESA is likely to remain largely unchanged, with only modest amendments possible. Attacks on it will be costly, while careful drafting can protect a bill from committee jurisdiction and reduce the leverage of hostage tactics.

**Thornton article:**

- Section 10(a) was intended to reconcile development and conservation through HCPs.
- The author proposes that section 10(a) focus on the wider biological community rather than only single species, to give private parties the assurance they need to commit resources to HCPs.
