## Executive Summary

This December 1987 memorandum from the Arizona Game and Fish Department (Nongame Branch) summarizes two collaborative field experiments conducted in Fall 1987 with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Dexter National Fish Hatchery. The studies investigated **dispersal and predation dynamics** of hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] released into upper [[Eagle Creek]] (September 1987) and hatchery-reared [[Colorado Squawfish]] released into the [[Verde River]] above Clarkdale, Arizona (October 1987). A parallel reintroduction monitoring effort documented survival and growth of razorback suckers stocked earlier in June 1987. Methodologies centered on hoop-net trapping arrays and backpack electroshocking to track fish movement relative to release points and prior current-acclimation ("raceway conditioning") history.

## Background & Description

- **Razorback Sucker (Eagle Creek release, September 1987):**
  - Size range at release: **100–250 mm** (juvenile to subadult size class)
  - Recaptured fish from the earlier June 1987 stocking measured **95–110 mm**, showing apparent growth since stocking — evidence of post-stocking survival and somatic growth in situ.
  - Several individuals were preserved post-capture for **growth analysis and stomach content (diet) analysis**.

- **Colorado Squawfish (Verde River release, October 1987):**
  - Age class: approximately **four months old**
  - Size range: **60–110 mm TL** (total length)
  - Fish were marked with **two distinct colors of fluorescent dye** to distinguish two experimental cohorts:
    - Current-acclimated ("raceway-conditioned") fish
    - Unacclimated (control) fish
  - Acclimation protocol: **one week of raceway current conditioning** at Dexter National Fish Hatchery prior to transport.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **[[Eagle Creek]]:** Razorback suckers were released at the upper end of a channelized, high-velocity reach known as **"the narrows,"** a site chosen to test dispersal behavior under natural flow constraints. This reach represents a bottleneck habitat likely influencing directional movement and predator encounter rates.
- **[[Verde River]]:** Colorado squawfish were introduced above Clarkdale, AZ, replicating a prior year's study design testing how **prior current experience** affects post-release dispersal behavior — a key consideration for hatchery-to-wild transition success in lotic (flowing water) systems.
- Both species are native, imperiled large-river fishes of the **Colorado River Basin** ecosystem, and these experiments reflect early **conservation aquaculture and reintroduction** efforts undertaken amid broader endangered species recovery programs (both taxa are federally listed).
- Predation pressure from resident species (likely including nonnative predatory fishes) was an explicit variable under investigation, using hoop nets and electroshocking as complementary detection methods to capture both prey (target species) and potential predators.

## Key Findings

- **Razorback Sucker dispersal (Eagle Creek):** Preliminary hoop-net data showed an **upstream movement pattern**, but this was **limited to larger-sized individuals**; smaller fish did not exhibit the same upstream tendency.
- **Effect of raceway conditioning:** Current-acclimation via raceway conditioning **appeared to have little effect on dispersal** patterns of released razorback suckers — suggesting size/age may be a stronger predictor of post-release movement than prior current exposure, at least for this species/experiment.
- **Reintroduction monitoring success:** Razorback suckers stocked in June 1987 were **successfully recaptured** in September 1987 at increased size (95–110 mm vs. presumed smaller stocking size), indicating **survival and growth** under field conditions — a positive early indicator for reintroduction viability.
- **Colorado Squawfish experiment (Verde River):** Designed as a **replicate of a prior-year study**, using dual fluorescent dye-marking to directly compare dispersal outcomes between **current-acclimated vs. unacclimated** fish. Detailed results were pending further analysis by USFWS (Jim Brooks, Dexter Hatchery) at time of writing.
- **Methodological consistency:** Both experiments employed a standardized protocol — hoop nets set at preselected upstream/downstream distances, monitored over a **48-hour period**, supplemented by backpack electroshocking — enabling comparability between the [[Eagle Creek]] and [[Verde River]] studies.
- The work represents part of a broader, ongoing collaborative research and monitoring program between **[[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]** and **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]** (USFWS) supporting recovery of these Colorado River Basin native fishes.
