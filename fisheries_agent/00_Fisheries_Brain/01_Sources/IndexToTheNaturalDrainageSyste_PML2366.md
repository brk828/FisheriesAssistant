# Index to the Natural Drainage Systems of Arizona: A Digital Inventory of Perennial Lotic Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This technical report, a Southwestern Region Wildlife Unit Technical Report from the [[USDA Forest Service]] with contributions from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and [[Arizona State University]], provides a computer-compatible digital index to the naturally occurring perennial streams of Arizona. It catalogues **554 perennial waters**, giving location, drainage hierarchy, fish species records and bibliographic references. Data and supporting references are included for **320** of these waters; the remaining **234** lack even minimal data and are listed for completeness. The index was built to serve as a working tool for fisheries and wildlife planning and to integrate Arizona drainage data with the [[RW Wildlife Data System]].

## Background & Description

The authors (William Silvey, John N. Rinne and Roger Sorenson) note that state and federal agencies had long requested a consolidated source of fisheries data to meet planning needs, particularly on National Forest lands. The work draws on the [[Arizona Resources Inventory System]] (ARIS) numbering scheme and the RW storage and retrieval framework.

Key structural elements of the index:

- **Hydrographic hierarchy:** Four regions are coded in the 600 series: [[Colorado River Drainage]] (610, which covers more than 90 percent of the state's 113,909 square miles), [[Yaqui River Drainage]] (620), [[Sonoran Drainage]] (630) and [[Closed Basins]] (640).
- **Major tributaries of the Colorado River** are numbered from downstream to upstream, for example [[Gila River]] (611), [[Bill Williams River]] (612), [[Virgin River]] (613), [[Little Colorado River]] (614) and [[San Juan River]] (615).
- **Sub-drainages and streams** follow the same lower-to-upper numbering, so the digit string itself indicates position in the watershed. An asterisk marks an ephemeral channel noted only for location reference.
- **Location:** Coordinates are given to the nearest 15 seconds. Because all of Arizona lies within 31° to 38° N and 109° to 115° W, the N and W designations are omitted.
- **Species codes:** Fish records are linked to an RW numeric species list (Table 5). That list was adapted from [[Patton Vertebrate Checklist]] (1978) with additions for known Arizona fishes. Species flagged with an asterisk are threatened or endangered under state or federal lists, and some entries are introduced species.
- **Bibliography:** The index cites 119 references, from museum registers to agency survey reports dating up to 1980. [[Arizona State University Fish Collection]] (reference 5) and the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]] (reference 107) are cited most frequently for fish collections.

Each stream entry lists the RW species codes recorded there, with bibliographic reference numbers. The authors caution that a reference number indicates only that some investigation or collection has been documented; the extent of museum holdings requires review of the registers themselves.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Arizona's perennial waters are a small fraction of its landscape, and the report repeatedly emphasizes intermittency and rapid change. The authors note that many systems are ephemeral in parts and that fish persistence depends on the stability of flow. Several entries reflect ecological pressures on native fauna, including floods, mining effects and habitat alteration. Reference cited for these topics includes studies of flood impacts on a [[Sonoran Desert Stream]] and of copper mining effects on an intermittent stream in central Arizona.

Many native and listed taxa are concentrated in a few systems, including:

- [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]], which occur in desert springs and small streams and have been affected by introduced [[Mosquitofish]].
- [[Humpback Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]], which are large-river Colorado basin cyprinids.
- [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Woundfin]], which are native cyprinids and catostomids of the Colorado system.
- [[Apache Trout]] and [[Gila Trout]], which are native salmonids of Arizona's high-elevation headwaters.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] and other introduced sport fishes, which appear across many lower-elevation reservoirs and streams.

## Key Findings

- **Inventory scope:** 554 perennial waters are recognized across Arizona. Full data and references exist for 320 (about 58 percent), and 234 (about 42 percent) lack minimal documentation.
- **Dominant drainage:** The Colorado River system (610) includes the great majority of the state's land area and most of the indexed waters.
- **Coding system:** A hierarchical digit string (for example 611.0320 for the Verde River) identifies each water and its position within its drainage, enabling retrieval by location and by species.
- **Fish records:** Species records are provided for each stream where data exist, using RW codes that link to the species list (Table 5). The list includes native, introduced and threatened or endangered fishes.
- **Collections:** Arizona State University and University of Michigan museum registers are the most frequently cited fish collections, indicating their importance as primary sources.
- **Data limitations:** The authors state that the work will be out of date by publication. Periodic updates every 3 to 5 years are anticipated, and user contributions are expected to add waters and correct entries.
- **Purpose:** The index is intended as a planning and reference tool for fisheries management, endangered species recovery and integration with habitat databases on National Forest and Bureau of Land Management lands.
