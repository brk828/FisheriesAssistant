# Freshwater Fish Zoogeography in West Africa: Faunal Similarities Between River Basins

#ichthyology #marine-biology #african-freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This study by Hugueny & Lévéque (1994, *Environmental Biology of Fishes* 39: 365–380) uses quantified fish species composition to test and refine the ichthyological provinces of West Africa. Presence–absence data for 443 species (from 743 known) across 52 river units were analysed with [[Correspondence Analysis]], hierarchical clustering, and an optimization step. The analysis recovers three main biogeographic regions: [[Sudanian Region]], [[Upper Guinean Region]], and [[Lower Guinean Region]]. The Sudanian region splits into a sensu stricto unit and an [[Eburneo-Ghanean Region]]. Faunal similarity is spatially structured, but the partition retains significance after geographic distance is removed. The authors attribute patterns to Quaternary climate cycles, mountain barriers, river captures, lagoon connectivity, and possible forest and aquatic refugia.

## Background & Description

Fish zoogeography in West and Central Africa had received less attention than in East and Southern Africa. Earlier provincial schemes, including those of [[Roberts Zoogeographic Provinces]], Daget & Iltis (1965), and Blanc (1954), were largely empirical and not supported by quantitative data. Daget & Iltis used a Sudano-Guinean index (ratio of Sudanian to Guinean species).

The authors list five factors shaping present distributions:
1. Basin size and habitat diversity, which correlate positively with species number.
2. Historical climatic and geological events that connected or isolated catchments.
3. Speciation, limited by poor phylogenetic knowledge in African fishes.
4. Refuge zones during droughts, and relict zones behind barriers such as impassable falls.
5. Dispersal ability.

Data came from the CLOFFA checklists (Daget et al. 1984–1991) and regional checklists. Museum collections were not examined, so inventories for Cameroon and Gabon (except the [[Cross River]]) are likely less complete. The Niger was split into Upper and Middle Niger, Lower Niger, and [[Benue River]] units, giving 52 units. Species recorded in only one river, marine-peripheral species, and lacustrine endemics were excluded.

**Methods (summary):**
- Correspondence analysis on presence–absence data (chi-squared metric), retaining the first three axes.
- Euclidean faunistic distances, then UPGMA clustering.
- Moving-centre optimization.
- Partial [[Mantel Test]] (1000 permutations) to separate geographic from biogeographic effects, with geographic distance measured as catchment divides (1–8).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Region characteristics (Table 2):**

| Region | Mean faunistic distance (adjacent rivers) | % endemics (with single-river endemics) |
|---|---|---|
| Upper Guinean | 0.086 | 19 (35) |
| Lower Guinean | 0.153 | 49 (71) |
| Sudanian | 0.112 | 36 (53) |
| Eburneo-Ghanean | 0.117 | 13 (21) |

The authors describe these regions as centres of endemism. Fish endemism exceeds that of birds (5 and 6% in Lower and Upper Guinea) and mammals (25 and 34%), which is consistent with fishes having the weakest dispersal among these groups.

**Geography and barriers.** Most of West Africa is flat sedimentary basin at 150–600 m, with highlands above 1000 m. The [[Fouta Djalon]] and [[Guinean Range]] separate northward-flowing sources of the Niger, Senegal, and Gambia from short, torrential Atlantic coastal rivers. [[Mount Cameroon]] and the Adamoua range isolate Lower Guinea. Only two amphiguinean species, *Amphilius rheophilus* and *A. platychir*, cross the Fouta Djalon, suggesting it is nearly impassable. Contrary to vicariance expectations, adjacent-river similarity is higher in mountainous Upper Guinea than in flat Sudanian terrain, which the authors link to river capture being more likely in flat areas.

**Documented or assumed past connections (Table 3):**
- [[Chad Basin]] and Benue: overflow via the Logone and Mayo Kebi during pluvial periods.
- Chad and Niger: Saharan palaeo-rivers (Tilemsi, Dallol Bosso, Dallol Maouri, Tarka) active during humid phases; the [[Mega-Chad]] lake covered 300,000–400,000 km² at its largest.
- Niger and Senegal: capture of the Baoulé by a Bakoy tributary, around 18,000 BP.
- [[Nile Basin]] and Chad: Jebel Marra volcanism diverted a drainage of more than 60,000 km² westward, probably in the Miocene.

Faunal distances between these connected pairs were generally below the mean for adjacent rivers within regions. Of the 10 lagoon-linked pairs, 8 (80%) fell below the regional mean, supporting lagoon creek networks as dispersal routes for brackish-tolerant species such as *Brycinus longipinnis* and *Chrysichthys maurus*.

**Palaeoclimate timeline (summarized from the review):**
- ~30,000–20,000 BP: wet phase, with shallow Chad lakes and high Lake Bosumtwi levels.
- 20,000–15,000 BP: general aridity, Sahelian dune fields, Lake Chad desiccated, Senegal and Mauritania rivers not flowing.
- 12,000–8,000 BP: humid phase, permanent Saharan rivers and lakes.
- ~8,500 BP: sudden return of dense equatorial forest.

## Key Findings

- **Three main biogeographic regions** were recovered: Sudanian (sensu lato), Upper Guinean, and Lower Guinean. The Sudanian region splits into sensu stricto and Eburneo-Ghanean sub-regions. The Eburneo-Ghanean region runs from the [[Cess River]] (Nipoué, Ivory Coast) to the Pra (Ghana) and is neglected in earlier schemes.
- **Correspondence analysis axes** explained 13% (axis 1: Cameroon and Gabon versus the rest), 10% (axis 2: Guinean coastal rivers versus Sahelian rivers), and 7% (axis 3: Ivory Coast and Ghana) of total inertia.
- **Faunistic similarity is spatially structured.** Correlation between log-transformed geographic and faunistic distances was 0.414 (p < 0.001). After removing geographic distance, the partial correlation with biogeographic membership was 0.754 (p < 0.001), confirming real regional boundaries.
- **Endemicity is high:** 21–71% of species per region were endemic. Lower Guinea had the highest share (49, or 71% with single-river endemics).
- **Revision of Roberts (1975):** The Sudanian (Nilo-Sudan) province is larger than Roberts described, including the Gambia, small Ivorian rivers, the Wouri, and the Cross. The Upper Guinean region is restricted to Tomine through Saint John, and the Eburneo-Ghanean region is separated from it.
- **Eburneo-Ghanean endemics** include *Marcusenius furcidens*, *Marcusenius ussheri*, *Citharinus eburneensis*, *Barbus trispilos*, *Amphilius atesuensis*, *Schilbe mandibularis*, *Synodontis bastiani*, and *Aethiomastacembelus nigromarginatus*. Its overall fauna remains similar to that of the Sudanian sensu stricto region.
- **Refugia:** The [[Niger River]] and [[Nile Basin]] each have about 10% endemics (excluding lacustrine species), versus one dubious endemic in the Chad basin. The authors infer aquatic refugia in these large basins, with the Eburneo-Ghanean region and humid mountains as additional candidates.
- **Forest refuge hypothesis:** Disjunct forest-associated species (*Mormyrus tapirus*, *Isichthys henryi*) provide the most unambiguous support, but the authors list six difficulties. These include palynological evidence for only one forest refuge ([[Lake Barombi-Mbo]], Cameroon), the Upper Guinean western sector lying outside present or past forest, and the Miocene origin of wet–dry cycles.
- **Divergence explanation:** The Sudanian–Guinean split likely reflects three non-exclusive factors: geographic barriers, contrasting ecology (forest and mountains versus savanna and flat terrain), and distinct Pleistocene refugia. Phylogenetic data are needed to weigh them.
- **Comparison with other taxa:** Aquatic molluscs (Van Damme 1984) and Monogenea (Guégan 1990) partly agree with the fish regions. A genetic study of *Chrysichthys maurus* places the [[Cess River]] as the boundary between Guinean and Ivorian groups.
- **Area cladogram:** From phylogenetic work on barbiline cyprinids (Howes & Teugels 1989), the first split is between Sudanian and Guinean regions, then Zaire versus the remaining Guinean regions, then Lower versus Upper Guinea. This suggests a formerly undivided Guinean (forested?) region.
- **Open questions:** Timing of vicariant events, closest relatives of endemics, and whether area cladograms differ between savanna and forest taxa. The authors call for phylogenetic (morphological and genetic) studies, following Greenwood (1983).
