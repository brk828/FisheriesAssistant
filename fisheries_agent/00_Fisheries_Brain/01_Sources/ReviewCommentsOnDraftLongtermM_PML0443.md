# Long-Term Monitoring Plan for Fish Populations in Waters of the Gila River Basin, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #monitoring-plan

## Executive Summary

This draft plan, prepared by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Region, Phoenix Area Office) with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], sets out a long-term program to detect non-native fishes and track changes in fish assemblages in target reaches affected by the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water transfer into the Gila River Basin. The monitoring is required under the Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) of a 1994 Biological Opinion, which found that the transfer would jeopardize four listed fishes. The plan defines monitoring parameters, sampling reaches, stations, gears, periodicity, data management, and a management action framework for responding to new invasions. Reviewer comments from [[Paul Marsh]] (Arizona State University) are appended and largely concern statistical design, the scope of habitat and water-quality sampling, voucher and data-retention rules, and the need for an executive summary.

## Background & Description

- **Regulatory driver:** The CAP was authorized by Congress in 1968 and largely completed in 1993. The aqueduct can transfer non-indigenous fishes and other aquatic organisms from the Lower Colorado River to Gila Basin drainages.
- **Listed species:** The 1994 Biological Opinion named [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] as threatened or endangered, and found critical habitat of three of them would be modified.
- **Monitoring categories:** The plan follows a three-part framework: base-line monitoring, project monitoring, and trend monitoring.
- **Duration:** Monitoring is to persist through the 100-year life of the CAP or until ESA status changes make the Biological Opinion unnecessary.
- **Target reaches:** (1) CAP aqueduct; (2) Salt River Project canals; (3) Florence-Casa Grande Canal; (4) Salt River between Stewart Mountain Dam and Granite Reef Dam; (5) perennial Gila River below Coolidge Dam; (6) perennial San Pedro River below the U.S.-Mexico border.
- **Intended use:** A standardized guide for contracted and Reclamation investigators to ensure repeatable methods and comparable results.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Assemblage variability:** Natural and human-induced causes of variation in fish assemblage structure are hard to separate, and introduced species add to this variability in altered habitats. Baseline data are scarce.
- **Reference site:** [[Aravaipa Creek]] is described as relatively pristine and supporting an important native fish community, proposed as a regional reference for attainable conditions and natural variation.
- **Geomorphic stratification:** Native and non-native fishes respond differently to disturbance (e.g., flooding) across reach types, such as confined canyons versus poorly confined valleys. Reaches are therefore classified by gradient and channel confinement, validated by a helicopter overflight.
- **Hydrology:** Flows in all target reaches except the San Pedro River are artificially controlled, so sampling timing depends on release schedules or canal dewatering.
- **Canal habitats:** Canal habitats have not been typed in the literature and are considered too different from stream pools, riffles, and runs to use stream macrohabitat designations; canal stations are not stratified by macrohabitat.
- **Species of concern:** No federally listed fishes are expected in routine catches, but several candidate species occur. Non-native taxa in the species checklist include [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], and [[Tilapia]] (the checklist comprises roughly 30 taxa across the monitored reaches).

## Key Findings

**Statistical design (Objective 2 and Appendix I)**
- The statistical power analysis by [[Jeffrey R. Wilson]] (Arizona State University) used three existing data sets: Aravaipa Creek (1943–1990, 15 species), Gila and San Pedro rivers (1991–1993 Fall Fish Count surveys), and related collections.
- Assemblage structure is the only parameter requiring formal statistical testing. The recommended design is a repeated measures nested ANOVA, with power set at ≥ 0.8 and α = 0.05.
- Key sample-size results: a 25% change in a species' relative abundance is detectable at power > 80% if at least 30 individuals are collected per sample. With 60 individuals, a 10% change is detectable. A 30% or smaller change in species with fewer than 25 individuals per sample is not likely to be detected.
- Table of sample size versus power for a 30% change: 25 individuals gives power 0.80; 30 gives 0.87; 50 gives 0.98.

**Sampling design (Objective 3)**
- Three systematic fixed stations are to be established per geomorphic reach where possible, supplemented by randomly selected sites to detect new or rare species.
- Sampling effort follows the rule of thumb of about 35 times the mean stream width (Lyons 1992). Reclamation proposes intensive quantitative sampling over a 200 m reach plus extensive qualitative sampling of uncommon habitats.
- Blocking nets are not required for electrofishing, based on [[Simonson and Lyons]] (1995) and Vadas and Orth (1993).
- Electrofishing is the primary method. Pulse rates should be kept below 30 pulses per second if fish injury is a management concern.
- Canal sampling is restricted to dewatering periods. Autumn sampling (September 1 to November 30) is recommended for streams.
- Monitoring frequency is once annually at fixed stations, with random stations sampled at varying times.

**Data management and vouchers**
- A minimum of 50 length measurements per species is recommended.
- Voucher specimens are to be preserved in 10% formalin and deposited at the [[Arizona State University Collection of Fishes]].
- A dBASE V data-entry program and a relational database structure (Table 3, approximately 60 variables) are provided.

**Management action plan (Objective 5)**
- The plan calls for jointly developed management responses to detection of new non-native species or exceedance of assemblage criteria. Options include renovation of a reach with or without barriers, selective removal, controlled flooding, and formal reconsultation with the Fish and Wildlife Service.
- Initial response is intensive surveys extending upstream and downstream of the detection point.

**Reviewer concerns (Paul Marsh, Arizona State University)**
- Physical and chemical habitat sampling, except for parameters needed for electroshocker output, is considered costly and distracting from capturing fishes.
- Specimens should be enumerated by life-stage category instead of recording individual length and weight, which Marsh considers equally useful.
- A clearer link is needed between CAP operations and habitat changes, and between habitat data and management triggers.
- An abstract or executive summary is recommended.
