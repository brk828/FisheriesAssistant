# Studies of Biota in Dinosaur National Monument, Utah and Colorado (1963)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-invertebrates #limnology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This University of Utah Miscellaneous Paper (No. 1, May 1963, Institute of Environmental Biological Research) reports a July 20 to August 6, 1962 survey of the aquatic and terrestrial biota of [[Dinosaur National Monument]], conducted for the [[National Park Service]] under a cooperative agreement with the University of Utah. The volume covers a log of the expedition, the ecology and limnology of the [[Green River]], non-vascular plants (algae and bryophytes), and mammals. The river study concludes that low productivity in the Green and Yampa rivers is driven by sediment scouring, seasonal flooding, and dry climate. The fauna is dominated by mayflies, caddisflies and stoneflies, and the algal flora is poor. The mammal survey recorded 27 kinds of mammals in the monument, of which seven species were collected as specimens.

## Background & Description

The expedition was carried out by Stephen D. Durrant, Seville Flowers, Delbert W. Argyle and Douglas Anderson. It established five base camps at Gates of Lodore, Deer Lodge, Echo Park, Rainbow Park and Split Mountain. The monument lies at the eastern end of the [[Uinta Mountains]] where the Green River, arising in northern Wyoming, is joined by the [[Yampa River]] at Echo Park before flowing through Whirlpool Canyon and Split Mountain Canyon into the Uinta Basin. Rocks range from Precambrian to Cretaceous, and deep canyons with cliffs and talus shape the river environment.

Field methods included:
- Sampling of invertebrates at 14 described stations, using habitat-specific collecting and rearing cages.
- Algae collected in vials, preserved in lacto-phenol, and studied in the laboratory at the University of Utah.
- Mammals trapped with 825 traps, plus signs such as tracks and droppings.

The study explicitly notes that no fish survey was reported. Fishes are mentioned only in general interpretive recommendations, so this note does not report fish species data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and hydrology.** Regional climate is arid, with average annual precipitation of 6.84 to 13.41 inches across stations from 4,720 to 6,657 feet. Mean seasonal flow peaks in May and June from snowmelt, drops through summer, and stays steady in fall. Streamflow records from 1906 to 1959 show strong variability between seasons, years and locations. Little Snake River near Lily goes dry more than 3% of the time. The Green River at Jensen always carries water.

**Water quality.** Water is hard and mineral-rich, with mean total dissolved solids of 316 ppm at Jensen, 140 ppm at Maybell and 196 ppm at Lily. Mean specific conductance is 500 micromhos at Jensen. Field samples show pH 8.2 to 8.3, and water temperatures of 75 to 77 °F in late July.

**Habitat types.** Habitats were grouped into still-water types (pools, ponds) and running-water types (runs, riffles and rapids, rubble, snags, sandy bottoms). The river has a fall of about 540 feet over 45 miles, with grade varying from about 2.5 to 25 feet per mile by section (for example, 21.5 feet per mile through Split Mountain Canyon).

**Productivity.** Plankton is sparse, mostly diatoms with some blue-green algae, and periphyton is restricted to sheltered areas. Animal productivity follows from this algal base through three trophic levels (vegetation feeders, herbivores and predators).

## Key Findings

- **Invertebrate fauna.** The annotated list covers the Green River system and includes records from the 1962 expedition plus museum and literature records. Major groups include Ephemeroptera (mayflies, e.g. [[Isonychia sicca campestris]], [[Traverella albertana]], [[Ephoron album]]), Plecoptera (e.g. [[Claassenia sabulosa]], which was plentiful in monument rivers but not in Glen Canyon), Odonata, Hemiptera, Coleoptera, Trichoptera (e.g. [[Hydropsyche sp.]]), and Diptera.
- **Notable records.** Ephoron album was found at all stations on both rivers. Tricorythodes minutus yielded about 200 nymphs from a seep pool, with 129 at Deerlodge. The ground-dwelling mayfly Ephoron album produced 96 nymphs at Split Mountain Overflow Campground.
- **Habitat-specific fauna.** Hard-packed channel sand was largely sterile. Riffles, rubble and snags were most productive. Seep pools supported hemipterans (Corixidae, Notonecta, Gerris) and Chironomid larvae.
- **Algal flora.** The collections yielded 56 species and one variety: 22 blue-green, 3 yellow-green, 27 green, 3 euglenoid and 1 stonewort. Cottonwood Creek is dominated by blue-green algae (for example, Anabaena torulosa), while Pool Creek has the richest flora in the monument, mainly green algae.
- **Bryophytes.** The survey recorded 53 kinds: three liverworts and fifty mosses (including varieties). [[Tortula ruralis]] is the most frequent moss, and [[Hypnum vaucheri]] is the most frequent canyon moss. Platygyrium repens adscendens and Thuidium abietinum extend known western ranges by about 200 to 300 miles.
- **Mammals.** Trapping of 825 traps yielded 81 mammals, of which 52 were prepared as specimens, all rodents (4 families, 5 genera, 7 species). The most common was [[Peromyscus maniculatus]] (deer mouse). Including sign-based records, 27 mammal kinds were confirmed in the monument. Shooting was not allowed, so no bats, rabbits or squirrels were collected, although some were observed.
- **Conservation and interpretation.** The authors recommend limiting human use to aesthetic, transportation, and regulated recreation. They also flag threats from upstream water storage in [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], irrigation diversion, sewage pollution and rotenone treatment. Interpretive exhibits on fishes, food-energy cycles and aquatic insect life are suggested for visitor centers and campgrounds.
