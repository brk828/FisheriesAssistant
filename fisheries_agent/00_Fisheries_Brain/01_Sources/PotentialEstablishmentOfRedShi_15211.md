# Potential Establishment of Red Shiner Minnows and Consequences to Native Fish Faunas in Upland Streams of Eastern North America as Related to Climate Change

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1993 research proposal from the [[University of Oklahoma Biological Station]] (Kingston, Oklahoma), led by [[William J. Matthews]] (Principal Investigator) and [[Frances P. Gelwick]] (Co-Principal Investigator), and submitted to the [[Environmental Protection Agency]]. It asks whether the [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), a widespread Great Plains minnow that has never established in the cooler, clearer streams of the [[Interior Highlands]], could invade and damage native upland fish faunas if [[Global Climate Change]] warms those streams. The proposal tests two hypotheses in replicated outdoor [[Experimental Stream Units]] using five native upland minnows and a [[Ecomorphology]]-based framework. The planned work runs September 1993 to August 1996, with a total of eight experimental stream units and an University of Oklahoma matching commitment of $20,000 for stream construction.

## Background & Description

**Problem statement.** The red shiner is one of the most abundant stream fishes of central North America. It has caused severe declines of native fishes wherever it has been introduced west of the Continental Divide, including throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]]. In Illinois it hybridized with and "ultimately supplanted" the spotfin shiner and "displaced" the steelcolor shiner. The authors argue that warming could allow it to invade uplands that currently exclude it, and that bait-bucket and aquarium introductions could aid this spread.

**Research objectives (two hypotheses):**
1. During a period of global warming, red shiner introduction can have deleterious effects on native upland fishes that exceed or exacerbate the effects of environmental change alone.
2. Effects of red shiners will be greatest on native highland minnow species most similar to red shiners in overall morphology (ecomorphology).

**Study species.** The five native test species are all insectivore-omnivores selected because they are common, co-occurring Interior Highlands minnows that could compete with red shiners:
- [[Steelcolor Shiner]] (*Cyprinella whipplei*)
- [[Bigeye Shiner]] (*Notropis boops*)
- [[Striped Shiner]] (*Luxilus chrysocephalus*)
- [[Redfin Shiner]] (*Lythrurus umbratilis*)
- [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculaus*)

A common algae-grazing stoneroller (*Campostoma*) is added as a grazer, and [[Crayfish]] are added as an omnivore and predation component.

**Experimental design.**
- Eight independent stream units, each with three pools and two riffles, bolted together from fiberglass modules.
- Two-way ANOVA design: two temperature levels (cool, typical upland ambient, not exceeding 28 C; and elevated, ambient + 4 C, maximum 32 C) crossed with two red shiner levels (present or absent), blocked by year or season.
- Pools are 183 cm in diameter with water depth to 45 cm. Riffles are 183 cm long and 46 cm wide, with water depth of about 15 cm.
- Stocking densities: 30 individuals per species per unit without red shiners, and 20 per species with red shiners (150 fishes total per unit, or about 15 individuals per m²).

**Ecomorphological component.** Morphology will be quantified using 34 physical characters covering head, jaw, and body shape, with a minimum of 10 individuals per taxon. Sheared principal components analysis will be used. Ecological response similarity and morphological similarity matrices will be compared using a null-model Monte Carlo randomization test.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate predictions.** The proposal cites warming predictions of 3-5 C for the United States over the next century. [[Cushman and Spring 1989]] general circulation model estimates predict roughly 4-5 C year-round increases in the Midwest, with decreased summer precipitation. The authors assume stream temperatures will track air temperatures, and note that streams already reach 38 C or hotter on the plains.

**Biogeographic context.**
- The red shiner's native range surrounds the Ozark and Ouachita highlands of Arkansas, Missouri, and Oklahoma.
- Red shiners occur near the Appalachian uplands through human introduction in North Carolina and natural occurrence in western Kentucky and Tennessee.
- The Interior Highlands are described as among the richest centers of fish biodiversity in North America, with many endemic minnows, suckers, and darters.
- Upland streams commonly hold 20-25 fish species in 200 m of stream, compared with 5-15 species at a single Great Plains sampling locality.
- Of approximately 850 native North American fish species, more than 200 occur in natural streams in each of Arkansas, Missouri, Tennessee, Kentucky, and Virginia.

**Threatened and endangered fishes.** The proposal documents red shiner impacts on the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*, federally threatened), [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*, endangered), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), the last potentially threatened by predation on early life stages. Rotenone eradication programs have been used in some western rivers, sometimes with harmful effects on native species.

**Physiological tolerance.** Red shiners tolerate high temperatures, turbidity, and low oxygen, and survive better than upland fishes in thermal and oxygen stress tests. Upland darters are described as narrowly adapted to local thermal environments.

**Experimental realism.** Experimental units are outdoors and receive natural light, rainfall, insect input, and windborne nutrients. Blacklights attract terrestrial insects. Substrates come from upland commercial sources. Water chilling units and heating elements simulate the temperature treatments. Summer evaporative drawdown is planned twice each summer to simulate desiccation.

**Ambient benchmarks.** Least-disturbed Arkansas streams show summer maxima of about 28 C. Upland ambient lows are about 5 C.

## Key Findings

- **Central concern:** The red shiner has never established in Interior Highlands streams, where cooler, clearer conditions appear to favor native fish assemblages. Warming could remove that barrier, and bait or aquarium introductions could aid invasion.
- **Hypotheses tested:** (1) Red shiners plus warming will harm native upland minnows beyond warming alone; (2) effects will be strongest on the most morphologically similar native species.
- **Experimental plan:** Eight outdoor stream units; four treatment combinations; 150 fishes per unit at equal total density with or without red shiners; natural foods and crayfish included.
- **Response variables:** Microhabitat and mesohabitat use, feeding rates, activity cycles, growth, relative weight, relative liver weight, swimming fatigue, gonadosomatic index, disease markers, survivorship, egg production, spawning success, and young-of-year survival (counted by 15 July).
- **Timeline:** Block 1 (September 1993-February 1994) sets up and colonizes streams; Block 2 (March 1994-February 1995) runs four two-month trials; Block 3 (March-July 1995) covers reproduction; Block 4 (September 1995-July 1996) covers overwinter reproduction and survival; Block 5 (July-August 1996) covers shutdown and reporting.
- **Pilot results:** A two-month 1992 pilot showed normal algal growth, invertebrate colonization, and normal fish behavior and diets. Streams ran continuously from September 1992 to May 1993 without failures.
- **Budget and cost-sharing:** The University of Oklahoma provides $20,000 in new funds for stream units and three academic-year months of PI salary. The indirect cost rate is 26% MTDC. Field collection is planned at 3-4 trips per year in the first two years.
- **Supporting letters (Attachment A):** U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Dr. J. E. Johnson), Southern Arkansas University (Dr. H. W. Robison), Arkansas Game and Fish Commission (M. D. Gibson), and USDA Forest Service (B. G. Cochran) all express support. Johnson writes that red shiners "may be the principal reason" all seven fishes of the Tribe Plagopterini are listed or extinct.
- **Management implications:** If the outcome is positive, the authors propose banning red shiner use as bait or aquarium fish and restricting Great Plains fish introductions into upland regions. They also propose that ecomorphological analysis be considered in impact assessment for planned fish introductions.
- **Containment:** Red shiners will not be transported out of range. Experimental streams will be surrounded by an earthen berm, and concentrated bleach will be kept on site. Escaping fish would need to cross at least 200 m of dry ground before reaching standing water, and at least 300 m to reach the nearby reservoir.
- **Environmental impact statement:** The authors state that no negative environmental impact is expected, since collected species are common and no endangered or threatened species are involved.
- **Internal inconsistency to note:** The design section specifies three pools and two riffles per unit, while the budget and the Block 1 description mention a configuration of two pools and one riffle for the eight units. The three-pool, two-riffle configuration is used in the detailed design sections.
