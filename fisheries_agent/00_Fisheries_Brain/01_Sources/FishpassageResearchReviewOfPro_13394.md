# Fish-Passage Research: Review of Progress, 1961–66

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries circular (Circular 254, October 1968, by [[Parker S. Trefethen]]) summarizes the [[Fish-Passage Research Program]] from 1961 through 1966. The program studied how anadromous salmonids pass [[High Dams]] on the [[Columbia River]] and its major tributary, the [[Snake River]]. The work addressed four problem areas: migration through impoundments, collection and transport of juveniles, passage of migrants at dams, and adaptation to altered river environments. The program's central conclusion is that adult salmon can generally pass large reservoirs, but young migrants are unlikely to succeed without collection and transport. Losses at turbines and rising water temperatures were identified as growing threats, and the author called for research shifting toward salmon production.

## Background & Description

The document is a progress review, not a single experiment. It was prepared by the [[Bureau Of Commercial Fisheries]] (Seattle) and coordinated by the [[Columbia Basin Fishery Technical Committee]], which included fish and game agencies of Idaho, Oregon, and Washington. The investigation began in 1961 at the request of Secretary of the Interior Stewart L. Udall, concerning proposed high dams on the Middle Snake River.

Dam construction over more than three decades had converted free-flowing rivers into lake-like reservoirs. This altered temperature regimes, food supplies, flow patterns, predator-competitor relations, and disease relations, and it reduced spawning areas. The author notes that future hydroelectric peaking operations and thermal power plants would intensify these changes. Field stations in Washington, Idaho, Oregon, and California supported the work, and laboratories included the [[Fisheries Engineering Research Facility]] at [[Bonneville Dam]] and the [[Biological Laboratory]] in Seattle.

The program's staff in 1966 included about 40 named personnel across research programs such as [[Turbine Studies]], [[Fish Guiding]], [[Sonic Fish Tracking]], and [[Large Impoundment Studies]]. The document lists roughly 60 publications and about 20 manuscripts in progress; these are summarized here rather than reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Impoundment effects.** Passage of juvenile salmonids through a reservoir depends on reservoir length and volume, flow at the time of migration, and the physical and biological environment. The study reservoirs ranged from 4 to roughly 573 miles long (the printed figure appears to be an OCR error; figure 5 suggests about 57 miles for [[Brownlee Reservoir]]) with storage from 19,000 to 4,500,000 acre-feet.

**Thermal and oxygen stress.** Water temperatures in the Columbia Basin were becoming critical in late summer. In Brownlee Reservoir, surface temperatures reached 81°F, and dissolved oxygen where temperatures were below 70°F ranged from 0 to 4 ppm. Cold river water plunging beneath warmer reservoir water formed a sharply defined zone that delayed seaward migrants.

**Dissolved nitrogen.** Nitrogen supersaturation below spillways was linked to spilling characteristics. The potentially dangerous saturation zone was 105–110 percent. Concentrations were well above this zone at all points in the river during 1966 after extensive June spilling.

**Predation and competition.** Predators such as [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Northern Squawfish]] reduced survival of juvenile salmon. In Shasta Reservoir, [[Threadfin Shad]] supplied abundant forage that produced 3- to 5-pound salmon the year after release.

**Disease and parasites.** The internal parasite [[Ceratomyxa]] sp. was regarded as a serious survival problem in the Middle Snake River. Control of fungus and disease in adult spring chinook was tested with topical malachite green.

## Key Findings

### Effect of Impoundments on Migration

- **Adults:** Adult chinook released in Brownlee Reservoir reached spawning grounds as successfully as fish transported around the reservoir. Passage through the lake-like environment did not impair spawning.
- **Juveniles:** Few wild or hatchery chinook passed Brownlee during average drawdown and early filling. Passage succeeded for hatchery chinook, coho, and sockeye when low pool, high Snake River inflow, and high dam discharge coincided with migration.
- **Shasta Reservoir (Sacramento arm, 25 miles):** Chinook fry moved within 4 miles of the dam in 16 days before disappearing from samples, and no fish were seen near the dam even though water was spilled.
- **Lake Merwin (10 miles):** Surface-collector efficiency ranged from 10 to 65 percent. Survival of young salmon was low, mainly from predation by rainbow trout and northern squawfish.
- **Baker Lakes (upper 9.8 and lower 9.0 miles):** Passage of coho and sockeye was successful; 95 percent of sockeye leaving upper Baker Lake also passed through the lower lake.
- **Pelton and North Fork:** Passage and survival of adults were judged satisfactory at North Fork (4.0 miles) but unsatisfactory at Pelton (7.5 miles), where a dam at the upstream end compounded problems.

### Collection and Transportation of Juveniles

- Louvers, electrical devices, water jets, and air bubbles were partially successful but limited to low-debris sites with controllable flow. Louvers were most practical under those conditions. Black louvers outperformed white louvers day and night at velocities of 1.5 to 3.5 ft/s.
- Widening a bypass from 6 to 24 inches increased efficiency.
- A promising [[Traveling Screen]] tested in a 40-foot-wide flume diverted fish at velocities up to 5 ft/s.
- Pulsed direct current guided fish best where water velocity was below 0.5 ft/s. Adding an electrical field to louvers was no more effective than louvers alone.
- Water jets were impractical due to maintenance and water volume. Air-bubble screens worked in daylight but poorly in darkness, when most fish migrate.
- Low-frequency sound (35 cycles per second) produced the strongest avoidance response; broad-spectrum noisemakers did not divert fish.
- About 25 percent of seaward migrants entering Columbia River turbine intakes accumulated in gatewells, which allowed collection and bypass.
- A floating surface collector at Lake Merwin captured 10 percent of juveniles in the first year; after modification to depths of 28 feet, efficiency rose to 65 percent.
- At Mayfield Reservoir, efficiency for yearling coho was only 4 percent.
- **Transport and marking:** Wild Eagle Creek chinook released below Brownlee-Oxbow Dam showed an estimated survival of 19.3 percent (4,129 released, 32 recovered). Hatchery Rapid River fish showed 1.6 percent (50,000 released, 32 recovered). Fish handled at 59–64°F and marked in water 4–10°F cooler had average mortality of 1.5 percent, while fish marked at river temperature suffered immediate high mortality.

### Passage of Migrant Fish at Dams

- **Diffusion water:** Velocity standards for floor and wall diffusers could be increased by 100 percent without appreciable delay. Entrained air had no effect on passage.
- **Pipes:** Adults passed pipes of various configurations. A 2-foot pipe 90 feet long carried 12 fish per minute over 60 minutes and 15 fish per minute at a 20-minute peak. Lighted pipes sped passage for most species, except summer-run chinook.
- **Orifices:** Adult salmon preferred vertical over horizontal orifices and shallow over deep ones.
- **Fishway slope:** A 1-in-10 slope at [[Ice Harbor Dam]] performed similarly to the conventional 1-in-16 slope in proportion passing, rate of passage, and fallback. Steeper, cheaper fishways were then built.
- **Temperature response:** When river temperature reached 68°F, no fish entered a channel 15°F warmer than the river. Colder water slowed fish ascent, so cooling fishways is not a way to speed passage.
- **Fallback:** Of 30 sonic-tagged chinook tracked above Ice Harbor Dam, 3 fell back through spillgates. Estimated fallbacks were 2,350 of 12,279 fish counted during the spring run, and about 50 percent of recorded fallbacks were counted a second time at the dam, a possible source of count discrepancies.
- **Turbine losses:** Observations at [[The Dalles Dam]] and [[McNary Dam]] showed 70 percent of migrants entering intakes were within 15 feet of the ceiling. High turbine loads significantly increased mortality. Entrained air and rescheduled loads were proposed as ways to reduce losses.
- **Gatewell bypass:** Best efficiency at Ice Harbor Dam occurred with an 18-inch orifice in a gatewell corner at 5 feet depth.

### Adaptation to New Environments

- **Temperature:** Conditions were approaching critical levels in hot weather, and higher temperatures were predicted as more hydroelectric dams were built. Releases of cold water from deep upstream reservoirs were suggested as partial relief; the author considered statutory control of thermal pollution most appropriate.
- **Migration timing:** Arrival of adult salmon at [[Rock Island Dam]] did not change significantly over 24 years despite impoundments after 1951. Sockeye needed only 2 more days than before.
- **Movement rates:** Juvenile movement increased from 14 to 23 miles per day in the river and from 6 to 12–14 miles per day in McNary Reservoir after spilling began.
- **Activity cycles:** Juvenile sockeye and coho were most active between 6:00 p.m. and midnight; chinook migrants were most active from midmorning to late afternoon.
- **Homing:** Spring-run chinook spawned in historic tributaries to Brownlee Reservoir, whereas fall chinook continued about 75 miles up the Snake River to traditional areas. Attempts to segregate races by homestream water odor failed. Conditioning juveniles to eugenol required a concentration (0.18 ppm) considered too high for practical use.

### Outlook

The author concludes that losses of downstream migrants will certainly increase as river flows become more controlled, spilling declines, and most young fish pass through turbines. Adult salmon can reach spawning areas but with severe losses under adverse conditions. Recommended priorities include developing a prototype device to keep young salmon out of unsuitable areas, improving adult fishway attraction, and studying nursery-area productivity, native-stock reestablishment, transplanting adults, releasing hatchery juveniles, and planting eyed eggs.
