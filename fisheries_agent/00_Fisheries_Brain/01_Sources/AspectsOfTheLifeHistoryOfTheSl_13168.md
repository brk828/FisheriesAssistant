## Executive Summary
This study by Vives (1987) examines the life history of the slender madtom (*Noturus exilis*) in Flint and Cloud creeks, northeastern Oklahoma, sampled from February 1981 to May 1982. The research quantifies habitat selection (current speed, depth, substrate), diet, age/growth, and reproductive traits (fecundity, ova size, maturity timing), and directly compares these Oklahoma populations to a previously studied population in [[Green Creek]], Illinois (Mayden and Burr, 1981). Key divergences include smaller body size at age, earlier female sexual maturity (age 0+), fewer but larger ova, and different dietary proportions of dominant prey taxa in the Oklahoma populations, suggesting geographic variation in life history strategy potentially shaped by regional biotic/abiotic conditions.

## Background & Description
- **Size range:** Standard length (SL) up to 70.0 mm SL (oldest individual: 48-month-old male, 6.67 g total weight).
- **Length–Age relationship (both sexes):** SL = 31.1 + 1.45A − 0.011A² (r = 0.86, N = 202), where A = age in months.
  - No significant difference between male and female equations.
  - [[Green Creek]] madtoms attain greater body length at a given age than Flint Creek madtoms.
- **Weight–Age relationship (both sexes):** WT = 0.253 + 0.149A (r = 0.84, N = 202).
  - Adjusted body weight (ADJWT) equation: ADJWT = 0.042 + 0.13A (r = 0.90).
  - Green Creek madtoms are heavier at the same age than Flint Creek madtoms.
- **Weight–Length relationship:** log₁₀ WT = −1.52 + 2.70 log₁₀ SL (r = 0.93, N = 202).
  - log₁₀ ADJWT = −4.88 + 3.06 log₁₀ SL (r = 0.97).
  - At equal SL (65.0 mm), Flint Creek madtoms predicted heavier (4.65 g ADJWT) than Green Creek madtoms (3.63 g ADJWT) — i.e., Flint Creek fish are "plumper" at a given length, despite being shorter/lighter at a given age.
- **Total length conversion:** TL = 4.4 + 1.09(SL), N = 202, r = 0.98.
- **Age determination method:** Vertebral annuli (4th/5th vertebra), examined at 20–40× magnification; opaque rings = fast growth, translucent narrow rings = slow growth (laid down March–April, pre-spawning). Comparable to pectoral spine aging used by Mayden and Burr (1981).
- **Egg morphology:** Single dislodged egg cluster (Cloud Creek, 23 May 1982, water temp 22°C) contained 44 eggs, average diameter 4.47 mm (range 3.8–4.9 mm, SD = 0.259) — larger than Green Creek egg diameter average (4.11 mm).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
**Habitat Selectivity** (based on 77 madtoms, diurnal captures, compared to habitat-wide transect sampling via Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests):
- **Microhabitat use by capture location:** Riffles (55%), above riffles (21%), pools (11%), below riffles (9%), riffle edges (4%). Contrasts with [[Noturus exilis]] in Green Creek, where 45% were captured in pools (Mayden and Burr, 1981).
- **Current speed:** Captured across 8–92 cm/sec (mean = 37.8, SD = 20.97); significantly different from ambient distribution (K-S, P<0.005) — selective for intermediate speeds, avoiding <10 cm/sec.
- **Depth:** Captured at 5–42 cm (mean = 18.2, SD = 8.58); depth use matched ambient availability (K-S, P>>0.05) — **not selective** for depth. ~90% captured at depths <30 cm.
- **Substrate:** Strong preference for larger substrate particles; 79% of individuals captured near at least one shelter-providing rock. Substrate selection significantly differed from ambient availability (K-S, P<0.001).
- No significant habitat-use differences detected between smaller vs. larger individuals (current, depth, or substrate).
- **Nocturnal activity:** Confirmed more active at night, consistent with Taylor (1969) and Mayden and Burr (1981).
- **Predation risk:** Smallmouth bass (*Micropterus dolomieui*), a sympatric visual predator in Flint Creek, readily consumed *N. exilis* in laboratory trials, underscoring the importance of shelter-associated substrate for daytime refuge.

**Diet** (76 stomachs examined, 17 empty):
- Dominant prey by number: Ephemeroptera naiads (57.8%) and Chironomidae (Diptera) larvae (25.9%), together comprising 83.7% of identified food items.
- Occurrence frequency: Ephemeroptera in 66.1% of non-empty stomachs; Chironomidae in 57.6%.
- Baetidae (mayflies) were the single most consistently consumed taxon (48.8–54.1% of food items across size classes).
- Smaller fish (<61.2 mm SL) consumed significantly more chironomids (38.2%) than larger fish (20.7%) (z = 3.61, P<0.001); no significant size-based difference in ephemeropteran consumption.
- Diet composition contrasts with [[Green Creek]] population, where chironomids dominated (62.2% of diet, 72.3% occurrence) over ephemeropterans (10.2% of diet, 42.4% occurrence) — indicating regional dietary plasticity/opportunism.
- Comparable data from Curd (1959) on the Illinois River (Flint Creek's parent drainage) found similar high ephemeropteran (86.9%) and chironomid (80.3%) occurrence, supporting local dietary consistency.
- Some daytime feeding confirmed via undigested stomach contents, despite primarily nocturnal foraging peaks (dusk/dawn per Mayden and Burr, 1981).

## Key Findings
1. **Habitat selectivity:** *N. exilis* selects for specific current speeds and coarse substrate (gravel/cobble/rubble with shelter rocks) but uses depth in proportion to availability — indicating targeted microhabitat preference rather than generalized depth tolerance.
2. **Regional growth divergence:** Flint Creek [[Slender Madtom]] individuals are smaller at a given age than [[Green Creek]] (Illinois) conspecifics, though they are proportionally heavier ("plumper") at a given standard length.
3. **Earlier female maturity:** A significantly higher proportion of Flint/Cloud Creek females reach reproductive maturity in their first summer of life (age 0+) compared to Green Creek populations (χ² = 5.72, P = 0.025). Potential 0+ spawners were significantly longer (mean 47.4 mm) than non-spawners (mean 42.3 mm) (Wilcoxon, P = 0.013), suggesting maturity is size-triggered rather than strictly age-triggered.
4. **Reduced fecundity per body mass:** Flint Creek (16.21 ova/g ADJWT) and Cloud Creek (14.76 ova/g ADJWT) populations produce significantly fewer ova per gram of adjusted body weight than Green Creek (19.64 ova/g ADJWT) (ANOVA F = 11.53, P<0.005), though Flint and Cloud creeks do not differ from each other.
5. **Larger ova size:** Cloud Creek ova (mean 2.94 mm) are significantly larger than Green Creek ova (mean 2.48 mm) (ANOVA F = 3.73, P<0.05); Flint Creek ova (mean 2.83 mm) approach significance in the same direction.
6. **Equivalent gonadal investment:** No significant difference in gonosomatic index (GSI) among the three populations (Flint, Cloud, Green creeks) (ANOVA F = 1.16, P>0.25), indicating similar proportional energy allocation to reproduction despite differing ova packaging strategies (fewer/larger vs. more/smaller ova).
7. **Life history trade-off interpretation:** Oklahoma populations appear to invest more energy per offspring (larger ova/eggs) at the cost of total lifetime fecundity, potentially an adaptive response to regional survivorship pressures (predation, competition, high discharge/temperature variability), consistent with life-history theory (Williams, 1966).
8. **Spawning season:** Peak reproductive condition (elevated GSI, ovum diameter) occurred late April–early June in Flint Creek, matching timing reported for Green Creek populations. Evidence of possible extended/multiple spawning noted from a historical August specimen containing 52 mature ova.
9. **Spatial/evolutionary context:** Despite Flint and Cloud creeks being separated by only 8 km (but 280 river km and six dams from each other via drainage connectivity), their life history traits (fecundity, ova size) are more similar to each other than to the geographically distant Green Creek population — supporting a hypothesis of regional (biotic/abiotic) environmental shaping of [[Life History Strategy|life history traits]] in [[Noturus]].
