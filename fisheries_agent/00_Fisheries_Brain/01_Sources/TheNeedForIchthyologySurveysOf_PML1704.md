# The Need for Ichthyological Surveys of the Major Rivers of Western North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This short 1946 scientific article, reprinted from *Science* (December 6, 1946, vol. 104), argues that the freshwater fish faunas of the major rivers of western North America are poorly known and are being degraded faster than they can be documented. It makes the case for systematic ichthyological surveys of the [[Colorado River]], [[Sacramento River]], [[Klamath River]] and [[Columbia River]] basins before the native fauna is lost. The author's name is not clearly legible in the OCR text. The article acknowledges Dr. Leonard P. Schultz and Carl L. Hubbs for assistance.

## Background & Description

The article contrasts the size and ecological importance of these river systems with the limited ichthyological knowledge of them. Surveys are needed to support a critical appraisal of fishery management problems, including those created by the alteration of native faunas and the haphazard introduction of alien species.

The core of the article is a comparative checklist of freshwater fish genera in Table 1, which compares the Colorado, Sacramento, Klamath and Columbia basins. Genera restricted to a single basin are shown in bold, and anadromous salmon groups are omitted from the Sacramento and Klamath because they are not restricted to fresh water. The table is summarized here rather than reproduced in full.

The article also discusses the taxonomic and biogeographic significance of several groups:

- **[[Plagopterinae]]**: [[Plagopterus]] and [[Meda]] (both in the Colorado basin) form a unique subfamily of spined minnows found nowhere else in the New World.
- **[[Chasmistes]]**: the aberrant sucker is confined to Utah Lake in the Bonneville basin.
- **[[Gila (Siboma)]]**: a genus restricted to the Gila River basin and the Colorado system, with several relict species.
- **[[Archoplites]]** (Sacramento perch): regarded as a survivor of an ancient fauna that probably predated the barrier of the Rocky Mountains.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pressures on the fauna.** The author identifies several threats to the fish faunas of these rivers:

1. Effects of dams, diversions and other water projects, which have changed the rivers and created obstructions to fish movement.
2. Pollution, which is poorly documented.
3. Destruction of vegetation by livestock grazing, which is especially serious in the Southwest.
4. Haphazard introduction of alien species.

**Relict and endemic fauna.** The known fish fauna comprises a large number of relicts, many with restricted distributions or considered rare. Several Pleistocene relicts persist in isolated basins, such as the [[Pleistocene Lake Lahontan]] and [[Bonneville Basin]] system. Relict and endemic taxa are the main reason the article treats survey work as urgent.

**Biogeographic patterns.** The article quantifies how much of the western fauna is endemic to fresh water:

- Of the known western genera with strictly fluvial members, 31 of 45 (69%) are confined to the region.
- About 27% of the genera also occur east of the Rocky Mountains, and 6 occur on both sides of the North Pacific. (The OCR is unclear for the exact value of the latter.)
- Marine-derived genera include *Entosphenus*, *Lampetra*, *Salmo*, *Oncorhynchus*, *Acipenser*, *Lota* and others. Widespread genera include *Prosopium*, *Catostomus*, *Cottus* and *Pantosteus*.

**Basin-level endemism.** The article reports the following numbers of genera and endemic genera for each basin:

| Basin | Genera | Endemic genera | Percentage endemic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colorado River | 19 | 8 | 42% |
| Sacramento River | 18 | 7 | 39% |
| Columbia River | 25 | 8 | 32% |

The author notes that the Columbia figure is probably the least accurate, because the basin is the largest and its fauna is the least well known. The Sacramento figures are described as the most accurate, because that basin is smaller and better known.

## Key Findings

- **Survey need.** The fish faunas of the major western rivers are inadequately known, and the author argues that the faunas are being reduced by water projects, pollution, livestock grazing and alien introductions.
- **Relict character.** Most of the native fauna consists of relicts, many of them restricted or rare. Several groups, such as the Plagopterinae and *Archoplites*, have high evolutionary and biogeographic significance.
- **Endemism.** Endemic genera make up 32% to 42% of the genera recorded from the Colorado, Sacramento and Columbia basins, and 69% of the strictly fluvial western genera are confined to the region.
- **Urgency.** The original fauna is being altered, and the author warns that the changes will probably accelerate over the next decade. A clear picture of the original fauna may soon be unobtainable, so surveys should begin as soon as possible.
- **Practical uses.** Surveys would provide comparative material needed to solve fishery management problems, aid the interpretation of archaeological sites, and clarify the zoogeography of the region.
- **Data gaps.** The Columbia basin is the least accurately documented, and the article calls for systematic collecting throughout the region.
