# Propagation of Bait and Forage Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Fisheries Fishery Circular (No. 28, issued April 1, 1939) by [[Henry C. Markus]], an assistant aquatic biologist, is a practical guide to rearing bait minnows in small artificial or natural ponds. The author argues that scarcity of wild forage fish is hurting both bait dealers and the [[Game Fish]] populations that depend on minnows as their main food. Propagating bait in ponds is presented as a conservation measure that also yields better-conditioned bait. The circular covers which species to culture, pond design and water supply, vegetation management, spawning methods for pond-spawning, stream-spawning and vegetation-spawning groups, hatchery handling, feeding, bait-pail care, species identification, and crayfish culture.

## Background & Description

Minnows belong to the family [[Cyprinidae]], which holds most of the bait species, although some other small fish such as the mud minnow and brook silverside are also used. The author describes a minnow as having a short, soft dorsal fin with no spine and no more than 10 divided rays. Trout and whitefish are distinguished by an [[Adipose Fin]] that is absent in minnows. A good bait minnow is active and hardy on the hook, tolerates crowding, and is often brightly colored.

Species favored by bait anglers include:

- [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*)
- [[Horned Dace]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*)
- [[Common Shiner]] (*Notropis cornutus*)
- [[Redfin]] (*Notropis lutrensis*)
- [[Round Shiner]] (*Hybognathus nuchalis*)
- [[Red-Bellied Dace]] (*Chrosomus erythrogaster*)
- [[Blunt-Nosed Minnow]] (*Hybopsis notatus*)
- [[Black-Head Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*)
- [[Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum*)

The carp is explicitly excluded because it is not native and could spread further. In a 1934 Bureau questionnaire sent to chapters of the [[Izaak Walton League]], anglers ranked the preferred bait as golden shiner, horned dace, common shiner, mud minnow, and blunt-nosed minnow. The "mud minnows" sent in for identification turned out to be young [[Bowfin]] (*Amia calva*), which the author calls unwise bait because of their voracity toward game and food fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Declining wild supply.** Old-time bait dealers reported ever-smaller catches from natural waters. The author warns that removing minnows from streams and lakes harms game fish, since forage fish make up the greater part of their diet.

**Water supply.** Springs are the preferred source because they provide a constant flow. Excess flow should be bypassed to hold a constant level. Warmer water can increase minnow growth: many species grow faster as temperatures rise from 70° to 90°F. Tapped creeks and rivers are also suitable.

**Watershed and pond design.** Ponds should sit in a small creek valley with a modest watershed, since large catchments can wash ponds out. The watershed should be kept in grass, hay, or forest, because bare soil roils the water and harms both fish and their food. Decaying leaves supply organic matter that acts as fertilizer for the plankton and invertebrates minnows eat.

**Pond size and outlets.** Ponds may be any size, but the ideal is 1/4 to 1 acre; a 25 by 75 foot pond was cited as producing well. The depth runs from about 1 foot at the shallow end to 4 or 5 feet at the deep end. Every pond must be drainable. Upright pipe outlets conserve water, while catch-basin outlets suit floodwater.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Vegetation supplies oxygen during daylight, and eggs of several species are laid on it. Recommended types include filamentous algae, water milfoil (*Myriophyllum*), pondweed (*Potamogeton*), waterweed (*Elodea*), hornwort (*Ceratophyllum*), and [[Chara]]. Overgrowth is dangerous: at night plants consume oxygen, and fish in algae-choked ponds gasp at the surface before sunrise, especially after cloudy days. The author recommends planting no more than one-quarter of a pond's area.

**Food web.** Minnows feed directly on filamentous algae and transform algae into game fish food. Unicellular algae and other vegetation feed [[Plankton]] and small invertebrates, which in turn sustain both large and small minnows. Fertilizers (such as cow or sheep manure) and artificial foods (shrimp meal, fish meal, bran, soy-bean meal, bread scraps) feed the plants, waterfleas, and insect nymphs that minnows consume.

**Spawning guilds.** The circular groups species by spawning behavior:

- **Vegetation spawners** (golden shiner, redfin, red-bellied dace) scatter adhesive eggs over plants and do not guard them. They suit pond culture well.
- **Pond or rock/log spawners** (blunt-nosed, black-head) lay eggs in masses on the underside of rocks, tiles, or boards, which males guard.
- **Stream spawners** (horned dace, stoneroller) spawn in running water, and their eggs are usually stripped and hatched artificially.
- **Natural stream spawners** (common shiner) spawn over gravel nests in clear streams and resist artificial stripping.

## Key Findings

**Production figures reported in the circular**

- Golden shiners were grown to 4 inches in a single summer at the Bureau of Fisheries hatchery at Fairport, Iowa. Full-grown fish reach about 8 inches. About 50,000 salable bait per acre can be produced with little effort.
- A Oklahoma pond 40 by 80 feet was estimated to yield 100,000 redfins per season (unpublished data from A. D. Aldrich). Adult redfins average 2.75 inches.
- Round shiners produced 35,200 per acre in summer 1935 from a small number of New York breeders.
- Black-head minnows yielded 201,971 per acre, approximately 119 pounds, according to [[Lord]] (1927). A single pair of black-heads produced 4,144 young (Markus, 1934).
- Horned dace grown on a purely vegetable diet reached about 2½ inches (printed as "2% inches") in a single summer.

**Spawning and hatchery methods**

- Pond-spawning eggs from blunt-nosed and black-head minnows are laid under artificial nests of tile, rock, or slanted boards about 6 inches off the bottom, with nests about 12 inches below the surface or in about 18 inches of water. Eggs per nest range from 36 to 12,000.
- Males guard the nest, circulate water, and remove sediment. Eggs will not hatch without water motion and will smother under sediment.
- Breeders should contain more females than males, since males grow larger and may dominate when broodstock exceeds 3 inches.
- Artificial stripping is done by pressing the belly toward the vent. Ripe females release eggs readily; eggs that come out hard or bloody mean the fish is not ready. Milt is stripped from males directly onto the eggs in the pan, and four or five pairs may share one pan.
- Hatching troughs measure about 10 inches wide, 6 inches deep, and 12 feet long, with 6 to 10 gallons per minute flowing through. Water should be clear and between 65° and 85°F.
- Eggs hatch in 4 to 10 days depending on temperature; fry are released to the rearing pond about 24 hours after hatching is complete.

**Stocking and feeding guidance**

- A satisfactory crop may come from 50 pairs of breeders in a quarter-acre pond, but 100 to 200 pairs is preferred.
- Only one species per pond is ideal, though two often do well together. Species whose eggs are stripped can be combined more freely.
- Manure should be applied at about a bushel per quarter-acre pond every 10 days, with care to avoid over-fertilizing.

**Bait-pail care and crayfish**

- Dealers should avoid overcrowding and advise anglers to aerate pails by dipping and pouring water back about a dozen times.
- On long trips, adding ice to about 55°F slows breathing and reduces the need for fanning. Warmer water should be added gradually at the fishing site.
- For identification, specimens can be preserved in 10 percent formalin (one part formalin to nine parts water) and mailed to the Bureau for identification.
- Crayfish, especially soft-shelled individuals, are prized bait. Cambarus virilis is noted as a prolific pond species. A 44 mm female carried about 80 eggs and a 102 mm female about 374. Soft-shelled crayfish can be held on ice for a week or more.

**Overall conclusion:** The circular argues that a small, well-managed pond system, with proper water supply, vegetation control, and species-appropriate spawning methods, can supply high-quality bait sustainably. This reduces pressure on wild minnow populations and protects the game fish that depend on them.
