# Totoaba and Vaquita: The Illegal Totoaba Trade Threatens the Vaquita

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sciaenidae #phocoenidae

## Executive Summary

This investigative report, written by [[Vicki Monks]] for [[Defenders of Wildlife]] and published in the July/August 1992 issue of *Defenders*, documents an illegal fishery for the endangered [[Totoaba]] in the northern [[Gulf of California]]. Undercover field work in Baja California and Sonora found open sales of totoaba in markets, restaurants and roadside stands, and found that totoaba gillnets also kill the [[Vaquita]], the world's most endangered marine mammal. The authors argue that U.S. demand drives the fishery, and that enforcement in both Mexico and the United States has been weak.

## Background & Description

- **Totoaba (*Totoaba macdonaldi*):** A large sciaenid that resembles the white sea bass. The document describes it as reaching six feet in length and 300 pounds. It is prized for its flavor and texture, and its cheeks and throat are considered the best parts.
- **Vaquita (*Phocoena sinus*):** A small, elusive porpoise found only in the northernmost Gulf of California. Its numbers are estimated in the low hundreds. Local fishermen call it *duende*, or "phantom-like spirit."
- **Legal status:** Mexico outlawed totoaba fishing in 1975. The United States banned totoaba imports in 1977 under [[CITES]]. The vaquita was listed as endangered in the United States in 1985, after [[Defenders of Wildlife]] petitioned for listing in 1978.
- **Key people:** [[Christopher Croft]] (former NMFS observer, now with Defenders), [[Omar Vidal]] (Mexican marine biologist), [[John R. Hendrickson]] (totoaba researcher), [[Donald A. Thomson]] (University of Arizona ichthyologist), [[Irma Lagomarsino]] (NMFS biologist), [[William E. Perrin]] (NMFS scientist).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Bycatch:** Totoaba gillnets (mesh of about 8 to 12 inches, with squares of 12 inches described in one passage) trap vaquitas. Vidal estimated that about two thirds of vaquitas caught in gillnets die in totoaba nets. Other nets, such as shark gillnets, also catch them.
- **Vaquita mortality:** Scientists recover about two dozen or more carcasses or bones each year. Direct counts put gillnet deaths at 25 to 30 per year, and the true figure may be much higher because carcasses can wash up unseen along remote beaches.
- **Reproduction:** Because porpoises have low reproductive potential, Vidal said high vaquita mortality could bring extinction "very soon."
- **Colorado River delta:** Dams and irrigation since the 1920s have cut off freshwater flow to the gulf. Totoabas were reported to migrate to the river mouth to spawn. Thomson said that lack of freshwater may expose eggs, larvae and juveniles to predators and disrupt normal development. Local fishermen reported that the estuary had become less productive.
- **Overfishing:** The document cites roughly 266 shrimp trawlers and 816 pangas in the upper gulf. Trawlers take young totoabas as incidental catch, which Juan Carlos Barrera described as a major factor in keeping numbers low.
- **Population decline:** One cooperative president reported a drop from 125 tons of totoaba caught in one year to six tons in the following year. The authors note that the number of nets set for totoaba roughly doubled from 1990 to 1991, which may help explain the reported crash.

## Key Findings

- **Open illegal market:** The team found totoaba for sale in San Diego-area restaurants, San Felipe fish markets and El Golfo homes. Sellers in El Golfo offered fillets at $5 per kilo, about a third of the U.S. price. Most buyers were reported to be American.
- **Concealment:** Fishermen chop totoaba into headless, finless fillets to hide the species. Fillets require lab analysis to distinguish from sea bass.
- **Smuggling:** Vidal counted 40 fish, more than two tons, being loaded by an American at a fishing camp. Trucks reportedly drive onto beaches at night to collect fish directly from boats.
- **Weak enforcement:** Mexico began a crackdown in spring 1991, with patrol boats and marines. A December 1991 presidential decree set prison terms of six months to three years. In the U.S., four NMFS agents were assigned to border checkpoints for two weeks, and only eight agents covered all fisheries enforcement in the Southwest region. Mexico declined a joint enforcement effort, so NMFS made no major busts.
- **Corruption:** Fishermen described paying marines to fish for a few days, and noted that other officials might also demand payment.
- **Arrests:** In April 1992, two fishermen were arrested in El Golfo de Santa Clara. Federal prosecutor Armando Partido Zamudio said this was the first arrest for totoaba fishing in 17 years.
- **Vaquita video:** The team recorded the first known video footage of living vaquitas, a mother and calf, near [[Rocas Consag]]. Perrin confirmed the identity, saying "At least we know there are two vaquitas alive."
- **Vaquita sightings:** Since the species was described, there have been only 65 confirmed live sightings, as of 1992. Silber's 1986 to 1988 surveys made 51 sightings, representing an estimated 96 individuals.
- **Policy recommendations:** Defenders petitioned the Secretaries of Commerce and the Interior to require that totoaba-like corvinas be imported only with heads and tails intact, or to list them under the "similarity of appearance" provision of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. If refused, Defenders planned to petition the Treasury Secretary under the [[Marine Mammal Protection Act]] to ban all Mexican fish fillet imports. Hendrickson supported the whole-fish rule.
- **Central conclusion:** The authors argue that ending U.S. demand is essential. Both Mexico and the United States must enforce their laws, and the U.S. should help fund economic alternatives for fishermen.
