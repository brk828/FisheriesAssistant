# The Effects of Pollution on a Midwestern Stream

## Executive Summary
This 1952–1955 study by [[Arden R. Gaufin]], conducted for the U.S. Public Health Service, examined how municipal sewage and pulp/paper mill wastes altered the physical-chemical environment and benthic macroinvertebrate communities of the [[Mad River]] (Ohio), one of the state's few cold-water/trout streams. Using twelve sampling stations along the river's course, the study documented dissolved oxygen depletion, elevated free CO₂, and paper-fiber sedimentation below the cities of [[Urbana Ohio|Urbana]] and [[Springfield Ohio|Springfield]]. A total of 166 macroinvertebrate species (122 genera) were cataloged and classified into intolerant, facultative, and pollution-tolerant categories based on dissolved oxygen requirements and respiratory adaptations. The work is a foundational contribution to the use of [[Benthic Macroinvertebrates]] as [[Biological Indicators of Water Quality]] and directly informed subsequent saprobic/tolerance classification schemes. Although focused on invertebrates, the study explicitly notes that pollution-driven oxygen depletion caused repeated fish kills between Urbana and Springfield (1953–1955), underscoring consequences for the river's cold-water fish fauna.

## Background & Description
- **System studied:** The [[Mad River]] is a ~65-mile tributary of the [[Great Miami River]] (itself feeding the [[Ohio River]] near Cincinnati), draining a 632-square-mile watershed containing Ohio's greatest concentration of permeable glacial drift.
- **Hydrology:** Average gradient of 6 ft/mile, steeper in headwaters. Numerous springs and morainal bog lakes near Urbana sustain high, cool baseflow; major groundwater inflow occurs between Springfield and Dayton.
- **Channel modification:** A 20-mile reach between West Liberty and Tremont City had been dredged and channelized, reducing pool habitat and giving this section the character of a young, unstable stream.
- **Pollution sources (per Ohio Dept. of Health, 1951 — McDill):**
  - Four sewered municipalities discharging a combined B.O.D. population equivalent of ~150,000 (only ~40,000 equivalent removed by treatment; only the Fairborn plant provided secondary treatment).
  - **Springfield** (pop. ~98,000): dominant waste source, population equivalent of 75,000 (1949).
  - **Urbana** (pop. ~9,000): population equivalent of 36,000, with 30,000 discharged into **Dugan Run**, a Mad River tributary receiving both municipal sewage and two large paper mill effluents.
  - **West Liberty**: minor source, <2,000 population equivalent.
- **Paper mill wastes:** Two mills in Urbana (per United Cardboard Co. data, Oct. 1955) each discharged 900,000–1,000,000 gallons/day directly into Dugan Run, with a combined 5-day B.O.D. of 4,141 lbs, consisting largely of finely divided paper fiber suspensions.
- **Sampling design:** Twelve stations located by river-mile from the mouth; water chemistry sampled October 1952, January and May 1953, and a follow-up in October 1955 (prompted by fish kills). Diurnal (24-hr) chemistry sampling at six stations in November 1952. Benthic samples collected monthly in pools, runs, and riffles using a Needham handscreen sampler (1 yd × 30 in, ~1 yd² bottom area per sample).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Thermal regime:** Due to heavy spring inflow, the Mad River ran 7–15°F cooler than comparable regional streams in summer/spring/autumn and warmer in winter — e.g., September 1939 averages: Mad River 63°F vs. Miami 69°F and Stillwater 70°F; January–February 1953: Mad River 42°F vs. 35°F for the other two rivers. This cold-water thermal signature supports a [[Cold-Water Stream Fauna]] (including [[Trout]]) distinct from the warm-water fauna of adjacent rivers.
- **Hydrologic anomaly:** Study period (Sept 1952–June 1953) was unusually dry, 7–10 inches below the 38-inch annual precipitation average; only one scouring flood occurred (May 17–22, 1953). Discharge at Zanesfield ranged from 0.9 c.f.s. (min, January) to 170 c.f.s. (max, March); near the mouth, flows ranged 139–5,340 c.f.s.
- **Dissolved oxygen (DO) impacts:**
  - Below Urbana: DO dropped 2–4 p.p.m.
  - Below Springfield: DO dropped 3–9 p.p.m.; minimum recorded was 3.8 p.p.m. (34% saturation) at Tremont City, 20 miles downstream of Urbana.
  - Diurnal 24-hr run (Nov 5–6, 1952) showed DO between Urbana outfall and 3 miles below Springfield ranging 34–53% of saturation at night — classified per [[Suter and Moore 1922 Pollution Zones|Suter & Moore's (1922)]] scheme as the lower zone of degradation / upper zone of active decomposition.
  - River flow at Springfield consistently exceeded the Ohio Dept. of Health's critical flow threshold of 125 c.f.s., implying worse oxygen depletion likely occurred during September low-flow periods (~120 c.f.s.) than captured by sampling.
- **Free CO₂:** Increased corresponding to DO depletion — 2.6, 7.9, and 10.5 p.p.m. recorded below West Liberty, Urbana, and Springfield respectively (Oct. 1955); undetectable elsewhere.
- **pH and alkalinity:** pH varied minimally (7.5–8.4); water was carbonate/bicarbonate-rich (249–341 p.p.m. as CaCO₃), a condition generally favorable to high bottom-fauna productivity.
- **Physical habitat degradation:** Paper fiber wastes produced a milky-white water appearance for 15–20 miles downstream of Urbana and formed suffocating bottom blankets unsuitable for invertebrate attachment or respiration — a distinct stressor independent of DO.
- **Documented ecological crisis:** Extensive fish kills occurred between Urbana and Springfield from 1953–1955, prompting the follow-up 1955 sampling; below Springfield, grayish water, sewage odor, black sludge deposits, and rising gas bubbles from pools were observed for at least 5 miles downstream.

## Key Findings
- **Total biodiversity:** 166 macroinvertebrate species / 122 genera identified, spanning [[Ephemeroptera]] (19 spp.), [[Plecoptera]] (21 spp.), [[Trichoptera]] (15 spp.), [[Neuroptera]] (3), [[Coleoptera]] (24), [[Odonata]] (9), [[Hemiptera]] (6), [[Diptera]] (42), [[Crustacea]] (5), Poduridae (1), Hydrachnidae (1), [[Mollusca]] (8), [[Hirudinea]] (3), [[Oligochaeta]] (6), Planaridae (1), and [[Nematoda]] (2).
- **Headwater reference condition:** The cleanest section (above Zanesfield, May 1953) held the highest richness (58 species); 47% were mayflies, stoneflies, caddisflies, and beetles restricted entirely to clean water. No species exceeded 25 individuals/yd²; 27 species occurred at <3 individuals/yd².
- **Moderate enrichment (below West Liberty):** Only 14 fewer species than the reference site, but productivity increased sharply — four species exceeded 50/yd² and *Ephemerella simplex* averaged >100/yd². Intolerant taxa (*Isoperla transmarina*, *Isogenus duplicatus*, *Chimarra obscura*, *Helicopsyche borealis*) disappeared, replaced by facultative forms (*Chironomus decorus*, *Macrobdella* sp., *Limnodrilus* sp.) — a classic organic-enrichment "stimulation then simplification" pattern.
- **Heavy enrichment (below Springfield):** Richness fell drastically (6–14 species across sampling dates); community dominated by pollution-tolerant taxa: *Physa integra* (pulmonate snail), *Limnodrilus* sp. (sludge worm), Macrobdellidae (leeches), and *Chironomus riparius* (red blood-pigmented midge larvae) — all with specialized low-oxygen adaptations, occurring in large numbers.
- **Temporal degradation from paper-mill fiber migration (1952→1955):**
  - 3 mi below Dugan Run confluence: 27 species (1953) → 9 species (1955).
  - Tremont City (5 mi further downstream): 41 species (1953, including 13 mayfly/stonefly/caddisfly spp., *Caenis* sp. >100/yd²) → 11 species (1955), with all gill-breathers extinct except tolerant midges (*Chironomus decorus*) and isopods (*Mancasellus* sp.).
  - Just above Springfield: 46 species incl. 12 [[EPT Taxa]] (1952) → 15 species (1955), though remaining species (e.g., *Hydropsyche* sp. >200/yd², Planaridae >1,000/yd² in one sample) reached extreme densities in surviving refugia (fast riffles).
- **Downstream recovery:** ~11 miles below the Springfield outfall (north of Fairborn), the community showed full recovery to a varied, balanced assemblage, demonstrating natural [[Stream Self-Purification]] capacity — though further degradation recurred near Dayton after additional waste inputs below Fairborn.
- **Tolerance classification framework:** Building on [[Kolkwitz and Marsson Saprobic System|Kolkwitz & Marsson's]] saprobic concept and criteria from [[Gaufin and Tarzwell 1952]], species were classified using abundance thresholds:
  - **Numerous:** >50 individuals/yd²
  - **Common:** 5–50 individuals/yd²
  - **Scarce/few:** <5 individuals/yd²
  - **Intolerant** taxa (mostly [[Ephemeroptera]], [[Plecoptera]], [[Trichoptera]], gill-breathing Diptera) restricted to Clean Water Zone (DO 75–115% saturation).
  - **Facultative** taxa occurred across Clean Water Zone and Zone of Degradation (DO 40–115% saturation) — included surprising tolerant records such as *Stenonema ares*, *Taeniopteryx maura*, *Hydropsyche betteni*, and *Corydalis cornutus*.
  - **Tolerant/pollution-preferring** taxa (*Chironomus riparius*, *Physa integra*, *Macrobdella* sp., *Limnodrilus* sp., *Tubifex* sp., Planaridae) dominated the Zone of Active Decomposition (DO 1–40% saturation).
- **Respiratory adaptation as key determinant:** Air-breathing insects (beetles, water bugs) were largely independent of DO and distributed similarly in clean and polluted reaches; gill-breathers were tightly DO-dependent and served as the most reliable indicators of degradation.
- **Comparative agreement:** Gaufin's tolerance classifications aligned reasonably well with prior regional indicator lists from Illinois ([[Schiffman 1953]]), Wisconsin ([[Mackenthun et al. 1956]]), and Michigan ([[Surber 1953]]), though over half of collected taxa lacked prior literature classification.
- **Summary gradient:** Nearly 50% of species above Urbana were clean-water forms with <10% pollution-preferring taxa; below Urbana/Springfield in 1955, 50–100% of the community consisted of pollution-tolerant forms, with clean-water taxa entirely absent immediately downstream of both cities.
- **Fish relevance:** Although the study centers on macroinvertebrates, it explicitly links the documented DO sags and heavy organic/fiber loading to repeated fish kills (1953–1955) in the Urbana–Springfield reach, reinforcing macroinvertebrate community shifts as an early-warning proxy for conditions lethal to the river's cold-water fish community, including trout.
