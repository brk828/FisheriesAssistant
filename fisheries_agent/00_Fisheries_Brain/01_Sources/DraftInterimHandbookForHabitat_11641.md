# Interim Handbook for Habitat Conservation Planning and Incidental Take Permit Processing

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-species-policy

## Executive Summary

This is a November 9, 1993 draft of an internal [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) handbook. It governs how staff develop [[Habitat Conservation Plan]]s (HCPs) and process [[Incidental Take Permit]]s under section 10(a)(1)(B) of the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). The handbook has two stated goals: national consistency in the section 10 program, and streamlined, more responsive permit processing. Its main tool is a three-tier categorization of applications (large-scale or regional, medium complexity, and routine), each with a target processing time. The Director sent it to Regional Directors and Assistant Directors for review, with comments due 45 days after signature. The document is a policy and procedure text, not a research study, so it reports no original field data or fish population measurements.

## Background & Description

**Legal basis.** The 1982 ESA amendments created section 10(a)(1)(B). It allows non-Federal parties to take listed wildlife "incidental to, and not the purpose of" otherwise lawful activities. Before 1982, no such exemption existed, so lawful private activities that might harm listed species faced a direct statutory conflict with section 9. Section 10(a)(2)(A) requires an applicant to submit a conservation plan (the HCP) describing likely impacts, minimization and mitigation measures, funding, unforeseen-circumstance procedures, alternatives considered, and any additional measures FWS requires.

**Model and intent.** Congress modeled the process on the [[San Bruno Mountain HCP]] in California. The handbook frames the process as a partnership, not just a permit procedure. Congress also intended HCPs to cover candidate, proposed, and other species of concern, to prevent future listings.

**Authority and roles.** Under a February 12, 1992 delegation, Regional Directors took over incidental take permit authority from the Washington Office. Responsibilities are split among the applicant or consultant, the [[Field Office]] (led by the Field Supervisor), the [[Regional Office]] (Regional Director, ARD-ES, and ARD-LE), and the Washington Office (Director, AES, DTE, and OMA).

**Process phases.** The handbook describes three phases:
1. Pre-application coordination, where the HCP is developed and ends when a "complete application package" is accepted.
2. Formal application processing, which covers completeness review, [[Federal Register]] notice, internal section 7 consultation, and NEPA finalization.
3. Post-application, covering issuance notices, monitoring, and annual reporting.

**Historical context.** From 1982 to 1993 FWS issued 16 incidental take permits and 10 permit amendments. As of September 1993, about 65 conservation planning programs and applications or amendments were in development, more than double the number processed in the first ten years. The handbook says this workload trend will continue.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not analyze fish populations, water chemistry, or aquatic habitat. Its environmental content concerns how permits interact with species conservation and NEPA:

- **Scope of species.** Listed species are wildlife and plants. NMFS handles most listed marine mammals and anadromous fish, and the Marine Mammal Protection Act limits incidental take for many marine mammals. This means only a few NMFS-managed species may be eligible for ESA incidental take permits.
- **Recovery and jeopardy.** Permits cannot be issued if the take would "appreciably reduce the likelihood of survival and recovery" of a species in the wild. This standard mirrors the section 7 "jeopardy" test, and it applies to listed plants as well as wildlife.
- **Habitat as take.** Under 50 CFR 17.3, "harm" can include significant habitat modification that actually kills or injures wildlife. Habitat loss can therefore require a permit.
- **Environmental review.** [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) review is broader than ESA review. It covers water quality, air quality, land use, recreation, and social and economic effects. Issuing a permit is a Federal action that normally requires an [[Environmental Assessment]] or an [[Environmental Impact Statement]]. The handbook states that ESA permits are generally not eligible for a [[Categorical Exclusion]], except possibly for harassment-only permits.
- **Mitigation hierarchy.** The 1981 FWS Mitigation Policy (46 FR 7644) ranks responses in order: avoid, minimize, rectify, reduce over time, and compensate. The handbook also states preferences for replacement habitat location (same drainage basin first) and method (acquisition first, creation last).

## Key Findings

**Processing targets (from receipt of a complete package to permit issuance):**

| Category | Target |
|---|---|
| Large-scale or Regional HCP | 6 months |
| Medium Complexity HCP | 135 days |
| Routine Project | 90 days |

These targets are set to roughly match section 7 timelines. Section 7 consultations take about 3 to 4 months, while section 10 permits have sometimes taken 1 to 2 years. The handbook says this gap has led applicants to seek artificial section 7 "nexus" connections. Targets may be extended for controversy, litigation, document changes from public comment, or workload problems.

**Categorization criteria:**
- *Large-scale or regional*: planning areas typically in the thousands of acres, many landowners, controversial activities, or new mitigation strategies. Examples: [[San Bruno Mountain HCP]] and Balcones Canyonlands HCP.
- *Routine*: typically under 1,000 acres, one or two applicants, non-controversial, or with well-established mitigation. Example: [[Delano State Prison HCP]] (California).
- *Medium complexity*: projects between these two categories.

**Streamlining measures:**
- Routine permits are exempt from Solicitor review if the project is correctly categorized, the standard [[Implementing Agreement]] template (Appendix 5) is used, and the applicant does not request review.
- NEPA and section 10 notices are published jointly. Routine EAs need only one Federal Register notice, with an unsigned FONSI.
- Applications may be faxed to start processing, provided the original with signature follows.
- NEPA documents may be drafted by the applicant or a contractor (an applicant cannot draft an EIS). Incorporation by reference and combined HCP/EA formats are encouraged with caution.

**Mandatory HCP elements and standards:**
- Impacts, minimization and mitigation measures, funding, alternatives analyzed (at least one take-reducing alternative and a "no project" alternative), and additional measures.
- HCP boundaries must be exact. Large regional plans are preferred over small scattered ones, but overly complex plans should be avoided.
- Take can be expressed as individual animals or habitat acres.
- Implementing Agreements are required for all HCPs regardless of size. They should be signed only after all parties agree, and before permit issuance.
- Funding must be reliable. For long-term needs, an interest-bearing account can be used, with only interest spent. A Letter of Credit can secure mitigation performance.

**Unlisted species:**
- Congress intended HCPs to address unlisted species, so permits can be amended after listing without new mitigation requirements.
- Priority for inclusion: proposed species, then Federal candidate Category 1, then Category 2, plus any species likely to be listed during the project's life.
- The "advance HCP" approach prepares an agreement for species not yet listed, but not yet occupying the site.

**Issuance criteria (section 10(a)(2)(B) and 50 CFR 17.22(b)(2)):**
1. Taking is incidental.
2. Applicant minimizes and mitigates to the maximum extent practicable.
3. Funding and unforeseen-circumstance procedures are adequate.
4. Taking will not appreciably reduce survival and recovery.
5. Other FWS-required measures are provided.
6. Assurances exist that the HCP will be implemented.

**Permit terms and oversight:**
- Permits must have an expiration date. Terms of 30 years or more may be appropriate for long-term commitments.
- Annual reports are mandatory. Missing reports can lead to suspension or revocation. Permittees get at least 30 days to comply.
- Denials can be reconsidered (Deputy Regional Director) and then appealed (Regional Director, with 45-day decision timelines). The Regional Director's appeal decision is the final administrative decision of the Department of the Interior.
- Permit information is recorded in [[LEMIS]] (Law Enforcement Management Information System).

**Public notice:**
- The Federal Register notice of receipt of an application requires at least a 30-day comment period. The comment period for a draft EIS is 60 days, and for a final EIS it is 30 days.
- Objections to permit issuance for endangered species are allowed during the comment period. Objections for threatened species are not required by regulation but are recommended.
- Quarterly Federal Register notices of permit decisions are recommended. A 45-day waiting period is suggested before publishing denials or amendments.

**Examples cited in the handbook:**
- [[Coachella Valley HCP]]: EIS prepared; covered nearly the entire range of the Coachella Valley fringe-toed lizard.
- [[Clark County HCP]] (Nevada): EA only.
- [[Kern County HCP]] (California): under development; overlays the Metropolitan Bakersfield HCP.
- [[Simpson Timber HCP]] (California): a signed Implementing Agreement sample (Appendix 6).
- Delano State Prison project (1989): an example of a small project that discovered a listed species after design was complete. Its permit denial example in Appendix 4 concerned inadequate funding assurances.

**Open issues flagged for review:**
- Whether funds for non-habitat purposes, such as research, can count as compensation (generally not good practice, but not ruled out).
- How to handle overlapping HCPs with differing mitigation for the same species.
- Use of [[Compensation Credit System]] (mitigation banking) and [[Population Viability Analysis]] in mitigation design.
