# Study of the Fish Populations of Charette Lake (1955)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This New Mexico Department of Game and Fish job completion report (Federal Aid Project F-9-R-1, Job No. Bel) documents a 1955 season study of [[Charette Lake]], a 300-acre natural basin in [[Mora County]]. The lake was managed as a put-and-take [[Rainbow Trout]] fishery. Natural trout reproduction was judged insignificant, so the fishery depended entirely on hatchery stocking. The study combined [[Creel Census]] sampling, [[Gill Netting]], water quality monitoring, growth observations, and stomach analysis. Its aims were to assess relative species abundance, the success of hatchery plants, and the factors limiting angling success. Angling pressure proved far higher than expected, and the report concludes that heavier [[Fingerling Stocking]] is warranted, since fingerlings were the most cost-effective method.

## Background & Description

Charette Lake is a 300-acre natural basin about 20 miles west of Colmor, Mora County, New Mexico. It has a very small immediate drainage area and receives water by diversion from the [[Ocate River]] through its sister lake, [[Upper Charette Lake]]. The Department owns the lakes, a large diversion dam, several miles of canal, and 830 acres of deeded land in the unit.

A drought from 1952 to 1955 kept the lake below 150 surface acres and a maximum depth of 40 feet. Low water was partly due to a leak in an old outlet valve that could not be repaired until the Department gained full control of water rights in 1955.

The study had three objectives:
- Determine the relative abundance of fish species.
- Assess the success and influence of hatchery-stocked rainbow trout on the sport fishery.
- Identify the factors limiting game fish populations and angling success.

The fishing season ran from May 1 to November. It was divided into three segments, and census days were randomly selected, with holidays (weekends included) sampled at about 20 percent and weekdays at about 15 percent. One census day from the second-segment holidays was lost, so computations rely on the remaining days.

Sampling methods included:
- **Creel census:** A clerk was posted at the single access gate. Cars were checked as units, and lengths were recorded from every fourth car on heavy days and from every car or every second car on lighter days.
- **Gill netting:** Nets were 61 yards long and 8 feet deep, with mixed mesh sizes from 2 inches to 3 inches stretched, plus a 1-inch section. A planned mark-recapture estimate was abandoned because netted fish had poor condition and high release mortality.
- **Water quality:** Noon readings of surface temperature, air temperature, wind, weather, pH, and water conditions were recorded on census days, with periodic oxygen, pH, and temperature checks at depth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic vegetation.** Dense growths of *Ceratophyllum*, *Potamogeton*, and *Elodea*, along with filamentous algae and the plankton alga [[Aphanizomenon Flos-Aquae]], shaped the fishery. A ring of vegetation reached 20 to 30 feet from shore and formed dense mats from late June through October. It made light-tackle fishing very difficult and often impossible in midsummer. Anglers cleared landing lanes with grappling hooks. Aphanizomenon became very abundant in September and October of 1954 and 1955 and concentrated on the downwind shore.

**Water quality.**
- *Temperature:* Surface temperatures ranged from 58°F on opening day (May 1) to 72°F in early September. Surface and bottom temperatures seldom differed by more than 3 to 5 degrees.
- *[[Dissolved Oxygen]]:* Deep water became stagnant by mid-July. By August 1, oxygen fell from 9.5 ppm at the surface to 0.5 ppm at 18 feet. By September 1, it was only 1 ppm at 13 feet.
- *pH:* Water within the vegetation ring reached 11.0 or higher during daylight, while open central water rose only to about 9 from a normal 8.0 to 8.5. By early October, shoreline readings had dropped to 9.0. The report states that high pH made parts of the vegetated zone untenable for trout at certain times.
- *[[Turbidity]]:* Turbidity limited fishing at times, caused by wind (nearly constant at Charette), inflowing water, and decaying vegetation.

**Angling pressure.** Opening day had roughly 150 anglers. The count is partly illegible in the scan, but it was the season's peak, closely followed by 130 on May 29. The lowest daily count was three. Weekend and holiday pressure averaged 88 anglers per day in the first segment, 53 in the second, and 30 in the third. Weekday averages were 19, 19, and under seven. Total effort was about 5,926 angler-days, or roughly 43,000 angling hours in the tables. The text gives about 3,000 hours, which conflicts with those totals. Forty-eight percent of effort occurred in the first third of the season. Most anglers came from New Mexico (about 70 percent), with the rest from Texas, Oklahoma, Colorado, Kansas, and Tennessee. Many anglers were repeat visitors, so a small group probably accounted for much of the effort.

**Fishing methods.** Boat fishing, mainly trolling with tandem spinners ("Christmas Trees") and flatfish, was popular in earlier years. In 1955, boat fishing declined. Shore fishing was better early on, and road conditions discouraged boat trailering. By the last segment, there was seldom more than one boat on the lake.

**Species history.** Three non-minnow species now inhabit the lake: rainbow trout, [[Yellow Perch]], and [[Common White Sucker]]. Several minnow species are also present. [[Brown Trout]] were last recorded in 1954 and are considered functionally extinct. [[Cutthroat Trout]] disappeared earlier. The lake was historically managed for [[Centrarchid Fishes]] (crappie, bass, and bluegill), with a single chinook salmon planting in 1938. Crappie declined by about 1938. Yellow perch were introduced by local anglers in the 1930s against Department wishes, and their abundance has since been cyclic. From roughly 1943 to 1947, very few fish were planted, and the upper lake became the more popular fishery. Cutthroat trout that spread from there into Charette led to the lake's development as a trout fishery, and no other species has been planted since the late 1940s. The report notes some fish transfer from the upper lake.

**Diet.** Stomach analysis (see [[Food Habits]]) showed that snails and zooplankton were the main foods, supplemented by insects and larvae. Yellow perch posed a potential threat to fingerling trout, but small minnows were thought to be easier prey.

## Key Findings

**1. Trout dependence on stocking.** Natural trout reproduction was insignificant, so the fishery depended on hatchery plants. Plant success was judged generally good.

**2. Stocking totals (1955 season).** A total of 16,270 catchable and 97,795 fingerling [[Rainbow Trout]] were planted. Only the 1951–52 plantings had come close in intensity. Total trout planted weighed 2,837 pounds, at a cost of almost $3,000.00 (about 30 cents per trout creeled). Fingerlings cost under $500.00, compared with more than $2,500.00 for catchables.

**3. Catch and effort.** Approximately 10,000 trout were creeled by about 6,000 anglers, averaging 1.7 fish per angler. Catch per hour stayed at or below 0.5 trout for the first four and a half months. It reached a peak of 0.99 trout per hour in late season, when fingerling-origin fish entered the catch. The report notes that this late-season peak rate equaled 7.38 trout per angler. Creel totals per segment were about 2,851 (first), 3,120 (second), and 4,000 (third) trout (OCR-garbled).

**4. Catchable return.** A March 28, 1955 plant of 800 fin-clipped rainbow trout (averaging 9.2 inches) produced an estimated 357 creeled fish, about 45 percent. Of these, 73 percent were taken in the first segment, 16 percent in the second, and 11 percent in the last third. A summary statement gives an overall return of about 5 percent for catchable fish during the first season, and the report describes that figure as "somewhat less than 5 percent" of all catchable plants. The two figures are not reconciled in the text. Compared with [[Maloya Lake]], where the two-year average return was 52 percent, the return at Charette was judged "excellent" given the lower pressure there.

**5. Growth.** The 9.2-inch trout gained just over 6 inches in eight months, with more than 1 inch per month during the first four months. This matched a growth rate reported by Barker (1952). Fingerlings averaging 1¾ inches entered the catch as 6- to 7-inch fish during the planting season, which the report links to exceptional growth in the lake. Slow growth in August and September makes early fingerling planting important.

**6. Size structure.** Early-season trout were mostly 10 to 12 inches, and 32 percent of the test-net sample were larger fish up to 21 inches. Large females in early May had begun reabsorbing eggs, suggesting they could not spawn. By late season, few fish over 15 to 17 inches were caught, indicating heavy harvest of older trout. Fish of 6 to 10 inches dominated the October catch.

**7. Other species.** Yellow perch made up less than 10 percent of the total catch (abstract figure), or 10 to 15 percent in the first two segments (body text figure). None were reported in the last segment. Perch sizes were 9 to 11 inches. The common sucker was not abundant, and spawning adults of 15 to 18 inches occurred in the northeast shallows. Small suckers were rare, possibly due to poor spawning habitat or predation by perch.

**8. Management conclusions.**
- Heavier fingerling planting is recommended, since the fingerling method was successful and cheaper.
- Current catchable stocking alone was insufficient to maintain good fishing.
- Past stocking levels would not have sustained angling at the observed pressure.
- Lakes with good growth and high harvest-to-plant ratios are the best use of Department management funds.
