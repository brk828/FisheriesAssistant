# Letter Opposing Proposed Introduction of Rainbow Smelt into Lake Powell

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Osmeridae #invasive-species #freshwater-ecology #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary
This 1999 letter from the National Park Service (Glen Canyon National Recreation Area) to the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources formally opposes a state proposal to introduce non-native **[[Rainbow Smelt]]** (*Osmerus mordax*) into **[[Lake Powell]]**. The NPS argues the introduction would violate multiple federal mandates protecting park resources, including the **[[NPS Organic Act]]**, the **[[Redwood National Park Expansion Act]]**, the **[[Endangered Species Act]]**, and provisions of the **[[Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin]]**. The core scientific concern is the unpredictable ecological risk posed by smelt to native and **[[Threatened and Endangered Species|T&E fish species]]** in the Colorado River drainage, given the species' documented history of unpredictable ecological dominance and range expansion in other introduced systems.

## Background & Description

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Proposed introduction site**: [[Lake Powell]], a reservoir on the Colorado River within [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]].
- **Potential dispersal corridors** identified by NPS:
  - Downstream migration through **[[Grand Canyon National Park]]**, into **[[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]]** and **[[Lake Mohave]]** (acknowledged even by the State's own proposal).
  - Upstream migration into **[[Canyonlands National Park]]** and beyond (NPS's own added concern, not conceded by the State).
- **Ecological behavior of concern**:
  - Rainbow smelt have a **documented ability to become highly abundant** and act as a **major ecological force** in introduced systems, with impacts occurring at the population, community, and **ecosystem level**.
  - The species has shown **adaptability to a wide variety of habitats** in prior western non-native introductions, undermining assumptions that it would stay confined to limited lake/river reaches.
  - **Ecological response of rainbow smelt in western non-native waters is poorly understood**, and no quantitative data exist on smelt interactions with Colorado River T&E fish species.
  - Lake Powell's ecological processes are noted as **substantially different** from the northern and southern lakes cited as precedent in the State's proposal, reducing confidence in extrapolated outcomes.
- **Species interactions of concern**: Potential competition/predation effects on native fishes, other game fish, and specifically listed **Threatened and Endangered species** in the Colorado River drainage (implicitly referencing species such as **[[Humpback Chub]]**, **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]**, **[[Razorback Sucker]]**, and **[[Bonytail Chub]]**, though not explicitly named in this letter).

## Key Findings
1. **No demonstrated absence of conflict**: Under Section 4.4.3 of the Recovery Implementation Program, non-native species stocking must be confined to areas with a demonstrated absence of potential conflict with rare or endangered species — a standard the NPS asserts has **not been met** for rainbow smelt in Lake Powell.
2. **Regulatory prohibitions**: 36 CFR 2.1(a)(1)(iv) and 2.1(a)(2) prohibit injury to wildlife/fish and introduction of fish/wildlife into NPS ecosystem units, directly applicable to this proposal.
3. **Cross-jurisdictional risk**: Because smelt introduced in Lake Powell could disperse both downstream (Grand Canyon, Lake Mead, Lake Mohave) and potentially upstream (Canyonlands), impacts would not be contained to a single management unit, implicating the **"single national park system"** doctrine (1970 Organic Act amendment) and the 1978 Redwood Act non-derogation clause.
4. **Irreversibility**: The NPS characterizes the proposed introduction as an **irreversible action**, arguing that the risk/uncertainty profile does not justify proceeding without further research.
5. **Recommended alternative approach**: NPS recommends an **ecosystem management approach** to the Lake Powell sport fishery, exploring alternatives that enhance the *existing* multi-species fishery rather than introducing a new single exotic species.
6. **Formal outcome**: The National Park Service states it **will not support** the rainbow smelt introduction proposal, while expressing willingness to continue collaborative work with the State of Utah on alternative fishery management strategies.
