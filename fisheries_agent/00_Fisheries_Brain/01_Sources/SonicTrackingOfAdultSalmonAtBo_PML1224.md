# Sonic Tracking of Adult Salmon at Bonneville Dam, 1957

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service fishery bulletin (Fishery Bulletin 176, Volume 60, 1960, by James H. Jensen) reports the first detailed sonic tracking of individual adult salmon in the [[Bonneville Dam]] forebay on the [[Columbia River]]. Miniature [[Sonic Tag]]s were attached to 39 Columbia River salmon and 4 [[Steelhead Trout]], which were then tracked by boat-mounted receivers during August to October 1957. The study documents how fish behaved after release near the [[Washington-Shore Fish Ladder]], how they moved around the spillway and shoreline, and how they responded to dusk. The author also assesses whether tagging and handling biased the results. The work was intended to support fish-passage design at dams by giving a behavioral baseline for adult migrants.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Dams create high flows, spillway discharge, and turbulence that can delay or disorient upstream migrants. Managers needed behavioral data to design fish-collection systems and to reduce delay at dams, particularly for [[Chinook Salmon]] runs.
- **Method origin:** The bulletin credits [[Sonic Telemetry]] development to Trefethen and colleagues (1956, 1957). The author states it was not known exactly when or how the method was first used in the forebay area.
- **Team:** Study directed by Parker S. Trefethen and Gerald B. Collins (Seattle Biological Laboratory); tracking performed by John R. Pugh, John C. Mason, and the author. The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (Portland District) granted access, and the Oregon Fish Commission assisted with trapping.
- **Equipment:**
  - Tag: aluminum capsule about 2.5 inches long and 0.9 inches in diameter, with a nickel-chromium "hog-ring" fastener.
  - Transmitter: battery-powered transistorized oscillator with a resonating crystal; carrier frequency 132,000 cycles per second; pulse rate 1,000 to 2,000 pulses per second.
  - Combined weight of electronics and tag under 2 grams in water.
  - Usable range up to 800 feet; expected battery life 8 hours, though some lasted longer.
  - Receiver: a directional unit paired with a Sea-Scanar sonar-type display, mounted in an 18-foot boat and powered by a 110-volt generator. A tilt-angle meter estimated depth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and conditions.** Release occurred in the forebay area just above the spillway, bounded by the Washington shore, [[Bradford Island]], and the spillway face. During the observation period, 18 of the main spill gates were closed, so the release area was nearly lake-like. The only through-flow came from the Washington-shore fish ladder (about 200 cubic feet per second) and two screened auxiliary water-supply intakes. Current velocity at these points was under 2 feet per second and fell to near zero within a few yards.

**Tagging procedure.** Fish were taken from a floating trap at the ladder exit, moved by dipnet into a live box (roughly 4 by 2 feet), tagged with the hog-ring while submerged, held for about 20 minutes, and released by a sliding gate. Out of the water for a maximum of 5 seconds. Tagged fish were chosen, when possible, at about 10 pounds or heavier; the estimated average tagged weight was 14 pounds, and no fish under 5 pounds was tagged.

**Effects of handling.** About one in four fish thrashed briefly in the live box after tagging. The author judges the tag's weight negligible but its drag possibly significant for small fish. Experiments on fish response to the tag's sound (from earlier work by Burner and Moore 1953 and Moore and Newman 1956) suggested that fish quickly habituate to novel sounds, and the author concluded that tagging effects were likely but hard to quantify.

**Behavior after release.** The fish spent most of their time close to the Washington shore and the spillway face, reflecting the lack of current to orient them. They wandered in the release area, reversed course, and only later began sustained upstream movement. Movement was faster in deep water than near shore, and near-shore fish stopped more often.

## Key Findings

**Release-area behavior**
- 62% of fish stayed within 50 feet of the Washington shore while in the release area.
- Fish tracked in the release area spent more than 90% of a total of 47 hours swimming within 50 feet of one of three boundaries (Washington shore, spillway face, Bradford Island).
- Except for 5 exceptions, fish did not spend more than 10 minutes at a time away from shore, and seldom more than 2 minutes.
- Mean time spent in the release area was reported as about 1½ hours (OCR-garbled in the source; treat with caution). Times ranged from under 2 minutes to 4 hours 50 minutes, with one fish still present when contact was lost about 5 hours after release.
- Holding time in the live box was significantly related to time spent before upstream movement (10% level). Fish size was not significant.

**Movement and speed**
- After leaving the release area, most fish followed the shoreline and rarely swam in water deeper than 30 feet (the source text also cites 80 feet in the abstract).
- During daylight, average speed over the bottom was about 1.5 miles per hour; net upstream rate was about 1.2 miles per hour.
- Average deep-water swimming speed was 0.9 miles per hour, about twice the near-shore speed, with offshore fish moving continuously.
- On September 18, one fish made 30 crossings at the spillway and traveled 2.5 miles in the release area, the most of any fish. Its average distance was just over one-half mile.
- Seventy percent of fish moved downstream from the release point before returning upstream or before contact was lost; 9 came to within 50 feet of the spillway.

**Obstacles and navigation**
- Fish stopped or drifted back where they encountered log rafts tied along the shore; this was the only point above the release area where backtracking was common. Fish occasionally paused under the [[Bridge of the Gods]] piers.
- One fish (September 9) moved back 700 feet at the bridge.
- Water-intake points did not attract fish: fish rarely paused at the screened intakes.

**Dusk response**
- Three chinook salmon tracked from daylight into darkness each slowed or stopped as light faded. One stopped completely when dark; another paused for 1 hour 15 minutes after dusk.
- Fish that remained active after dark alternated between moving up and downstream over short distances at irregular rates.

**Summary statistics (from Summary)**
- 39 Columbia River salmon and 4 steelhead trout tagged and tracked individually in 1957.
- Tracked individuals were followed for up to 16½ hours and over distances of several miles.
- The study shows fish moving over bottom at 1.5 mph in daylight and slowing at dusk, with most movement near shore and over deeper channel water after release.

**Limitations noted by the author**
- Observations occurred during low-flow conditions with little current through the release area, which may not represent conditions at higher flows.
- Herding fish with boat motor or propeller noise failed to change their course or rate of travel, suggesting boat effects were minimal.
- Results may be biased by tagging and handling, and the author treated post-release, upstream movement as more representative of natural behavior than early release-area behavior.
