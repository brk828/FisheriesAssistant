# Federal Aid in Fish Restoration: The Dingell-Johnson Program, 15-Year Report (1952–1966)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sport-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife report, prepared with the [[Sport Fishing Institute]], reviews the first 15 years (1952–1966) of the [[Federal Aid in Fish Restoration Act]], known as the [[Dingell-Johnson Act]] or D-J program. The program is a cooperative state-federal arrangement that funds sport fish restoration, management, research, habitat work and angler access. It is financed mainly by an excise tax on fishing tackle and by state fishing license revenue. The report presents its accomplishments as evidence of progress and warns that growing angler numbers, habitat loss and competing water uses make continued support essential.

## Background & Description

**Origin and purpose.** The Act became law on August 9, 1950, after [[John Dingell]] of Michigan and [[Edwin Johnson]] of Colorado introduced it. Postwar interest in fishing had outpaced the capacity of state fish agencies, which lacked funds and personnel. The program preserves and improves sport fishing by managing fish populations and their habitat.

**Cost sharing.** States select and plan projects, perform the work and supply one-fourth of the cost. The federal Bureau supplies three-fourths as reimbursement for approved work. Eligible activities include research, population management, land acquisition, habitat development, fisherman access and administration.

**Funding source.** Federal funds come from a 10 percent manufacturer's excise tax on fishing rods, creels, reels, artificial lures, baits and flies. Each state's apportionment is 60 percent based on licensed sport fishermen and 40 percent based on land and water area. No state receives more than 5 percent or less than 1 percent of the total. Guam, Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands each receive $10,000 annually. The Bureau may use up to 8 percent for administration, and the average deduction has been 5¼ percent. States must dedicate sport fishing license revenue to fishery administration to participate.

**Scale of spending.** Net obligations over the period totaled $72,634,015: $34,330,345 for surveys, investigations and research; $26,255,144 for habitat improvement and development; $7,507,406 for land acquisition; and $4,541,120 for state administration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report frames fish management as manipulating fish populations and their environment for anglers. Key ecological concerns include:

- **Aquatic weeds** consume nutrients that would otherwise feed fish and can make waters unfishable. Chemical herbicides made weed control practical, but they must be screened for safety to fish, fish food organisms and people. Water hyacinth, water chestnut and lotus were the main problem species.
- **Sedimentation and water quality.** Erosion from livestock grazing, logging and land development fills spawning beds, washes away food and cover, and leaves low, warm late-season flows. Pollution from industry, mining, agriculture, forestry and highway construction also degrades streams.
- **Migration barriers.** Dams, natural jams and unscreened lake outlets block anadromous fish (salmon, steelhead) and allow unwanted species such as carp to invade.
- **Spawning habitat.** Egg and fry production is often the weakest link in fish life cycles. Filling of shoreline marshes eliminated northern pike spawning grounds.
- **Reservoirs and estuaries.** Impoundments fluctuate in level, alter downstream rivers and can destroy anadromous fisheries. Estuaries face filling, dredging and pollution, and their effects on fish are poorly understood.
- **Rough fish and disease.** Overpopulation by unwanted species (such as gizzard shad, carp and gar) and parasites or disease can ruin fisheries and raise hatchery costs.

Environmental change, rising human population and habitat loss are the report's central long-term threats.

## Key Findings

### Lake and Stream Improvement

- **Weed control:** More than $400,000 was used by 15 states and Puerto Rico for 27 intensive weed control projects. Texas, New York, Connecticut and Florida accounted for nearly two-thirds of that amount.
- **New fishing lakes:** 39 states built or reconstructed 246 lakes costing about $21 million, of which D-J paid $13 million. These lakes provide more than 31,000 acres of water and more than 2 million fishing days per year (over 80 days per acre in 1965). Lake fishing is estimated to bring at least $5 million a year to local economies.
- **Stock composition:** About a third of D-J lakes, mostly in mountainous states, are stocked with trout. Bass are stocked in nearly two-thirds, usually with bluegill. Channel catfish is a popular addition.
- **Stream protection:** Nearly $2 million was spent in 14 states on stream improvement. Michigan improved 11 large watersheds through livestock fencing, bank stabilization and in-stream structures. Wisconsin found long-term gains for trout fishing from watershed work.
- **Structures:** Low dams create pools for trout in Tennessee mountain streams.

### Fish Passage and Population Management

- **Barriers:** Eleven states spent $865,000 on barrier problems, $673,000 of it in the three Pacific coastal states. California removed natural barriers and jams, Oregon built fish ladders, and Washington installed lake outlet screens.
- **Stocking:** Stocking projects in 20 states cost about $267,000, half for restocking renovated lakes. Four states spent $40,000 importing forage fish.
- **Pike spawning:** $250,000 in D-J funds bought spawning sites. Minnesota acquired 2,627 acres of marsh for northern pike, and Iowa and Wisconsin obtained about 1,300 acres.
- **Lake renovation:** Chemical treatment restored good fishing on 135,000 acres in 33 states, using more than $1.6 million of D-J funds (1952–1966). Massachusetts, Minnesota, Montana, Oregon, Texas and Washington spent nearly $1.3 million of that total.

### Angler Access and Public Use

- **Access sites:** 38 states developed 1,137 fishing access sites, acquiring 27,849 acres of land at a cost of $6.8 million. These sites open an estimated 3.2 million acres of fishing water.
- **Fishing piers and maps:** Fishing piers were built with D-J data, and more than 2,000 lakes were mapped.
- **Participation:** 28,348,000 Americans fished in 1965, a 36 percent increase over ten years. Saltwater anglers numbered 8,305,000 in 1965, nearly double the 1955 figure.

### Research Priorities

- **Scale:** More than $34 million, or 47 percent of obligated funds, went to surveys, investigations and basic research.
- **Life history:** Studies covered bass, bluegill, northern pike, walleye, paddlefish, channel catfish, striped bass, weakfish, yellowtail and trout, among others.
- **Disease and pollution:** Disease research was active in 12 states, and a southeastern cooperative project was based at Auburn University. Pollution research was active in seven states, and pesticide detection was in progress.
- **Marine:** California's yellowtail studies showed that environmental change, not fishing, limited catches off California.
- **Artificial reefs:** Six coastal states, Guam and the Virgin Islands tested reefs. Junk car bodies deteriorated quickly in salt water.
- **Reservoirs:** In fiscal 1966, 19 states ran 31 reservoir projects costing $595,800, about 11 percent of the D-J research program.
- **Regulations:** Studies tested removal of size limits and winter fishing, creel limits and seasons.

### Program Support and Information Exchange

- **Reverted funds:** $891,000 in unexpended D-J funds reverted to the Bureau since 1951. Of this, $537,500 had been spent by the Bureau on sport fish research as of the report date.
- **Information sharing:** The Bureau began [[Sport Fishing Abstracts]] in 1955, built a Library Reference Service in 1965, and required state D-J research to register with the [[Science Information Exchange]] beginning in 1964.
- **Workforce:** The program created demand for professional fishery biologists and funded the training of many current agency staff.

### Future Outlook and Management Challenges

- **Demand growth:** The report projects 63 million fishermen by 2000, and a need for at least 1.3 billion fishing days, compared with about 523 million today.
- **Habitat:** Fishable stream mileage will remain roughly constant. Natural lake area will shrink, while impoundments, including farm ponds, are the only habitat type expected to grow. Reservoirs now provide almost 40 percent of freshwater fisherman-days.
- **Reservoir needs:** About 10 million additional acres of reservoirs over 10 acres are needed by 2000. Farm ponds are expected to number 2,885,000.
- **Marine:** Coastal fisheries are expected to support nearly 30 percent of sport fishing by 2000, with saltwater anglers rising from two per current angler to nine in 2000. Estuary quality will decline without protection.
- **Access gap:** The public had access to only 6¼ percent of Atlantic and Gulf coastlines and under 20 percent of the West Coast.
- **Funding:** Rising costs outpace license fee increases. The report concludes that the D-J program will matter even more in coming decades.
