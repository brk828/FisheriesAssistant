# Food Partitioning among Fishes of the Virgin River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 study in *Copeia* by Greger and Deacon examines whether food competition is an important biotic interaction between one introduced and six native fishes in the lower mainstream [[Virgin River]], a temporally variable arid-southwest stream. Benthic and drift invertebrates were sampled at two contrasting sites, one relatively undisturbed near Beaver Dam Wash (Arizona) and one below the [[Mesquite Diversions]] (Nevada), and stomach contents were analyzed by season in 1979. The authors found strong dietary partitioning among natives, with limited overlap, but biologically meaningful overlap between the exotic [[Red Shiner]] and [[Woundfin]] below Mesquite, and between red shiner and [[Speckled Dace]] at Beaver Dam Wash, suggesting that competition may now be restructuring the fish community.

## Background & Description

The arid southwest has lost many native fishes, mostly through human-caused habitat loss, and exotic introductions are frequently harmful to natives. The lower Virgin River has two segments: a relatively unmodified Arizona reach and a Nevada segment where irrigation diversions significantly reduce streamflow. Red shiner was dominant or codominant in Nevada in 1979 but made up less than 10% of the fish population in Arizona.

The fish community studied comprised:

- **Woundfin** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), a federally listed endangered species.
- **Virgin River Roundtail Chub** (*Gila robusta*), a proposed endangered subspecies, with the study's sample limited to 25 individuals per species per location.
- **Desert Sucker** (*Catostomus clarkii*) and **Flannelmouth Sucker** (*Catostomus latipinnis*).
- **Speckled Dace** (*Rhinichthys osculus*) and **Virgin River Spinedace** (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*).
- **Red Shiner** (*Notropis lutrensis*), an introduced species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sites and habitat.** The Arizona site near Beaver Dam Wash had a gradient of about 3.6 m/km, with alternating riffles and runs and a heterogeneous substrate of rock, gravel and sand. The Nevada site below the Mesquite Diversions had a lower gradient of 2.5 m/km and predominantly shifting sand. Low summer flows often let the Mesquite and Riverside diversions divert the entire river. Woundfin and red shiner were the only fishes consistently present below Mesquite.

**Flow.** Discharge at Littlefield, Arizona ranged from a minimum of 1.98 (units as reported in source) in June and September 1979 to nearly 44.74 in April-May, with April-May 1979 high relative to other years.

**Invertebrate food base.** Chironomid and simuliid larvae formed the major benthic food base. Near Mesquite, macroinvertebrates were dominated by simuliids in June and chironomids in September and December. Near Beaver Dam Wash, chironomids and simuliids dominated in December, while mayflies (*Baetis*, *Tricorythodes*) and other insects were important at other times. Benthic densities near Beaver Dam Wash (327/m²) and near Mesquite (162/m²) were one to two orders of magnitude lower than those in some other studied systems.

**Threats.** Red shiner had already reduced native fishes elsewhere in the Southwest (e.g., [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]] in the Gila system). By 1986, red shiner made up 97% of fishes collected near Mesquite and 92% near Riverside.

## Key Findings

- **Diet partitioning:** Desert and flannelmouth suckers fed on a mixture of sediment, detritus, filamentous algae and invertebrates. Roundtail chub consumed largely *Spirogyra*, *Cladophora* and diatoms. Woundfin and spinedace were omnivorous. Speckled dace and red shiner were insectivorous, consuming many small dipterans.
- **Native–native overlap was uncommon:** Biologically meaningful overlap (>0.60) occurred on only 4 of 55 possible occasions (7%).
- **Native–exotic overlap:** Overlap between red shiner and native fishes was meaningful on 4 of 16 possible occasions (25%). Below Mesquite, woundfin and red shiner overlapped in February and September, and the two shared food most strongly in June, when food abundance was reduced.
- **Electivity:** Chironomid larvae were nearly always selected over simuliid larvae. Fishes generally avoided the more rheophilic simuliids, except in June near Mesquite, when woundfin and red shiner selected them positively. Ceratopogonids were positively selected by woundfin and red shiner.
- **Ontogenetic shift in roundtail chub:** Young fish (>70 mm TL) fed almost entirely on macroinvertebrates, while adults (>110 mm TL) fed almost exclusively on algae and debris. The authors suggest this may partly explain the paucity of Virgin roundtail chub in former areas of abundance.
- **Woundfin diet varies by site:** Near Mesquite, organic debris and plants were never a major food source. Near Beaver Dam Wash, they were prominent throughout the year.
- **Speckled dace vs. red shiner:** Near Beaver Dam Wash, the two species shared about 50% chironomid larvae in their diets, yielding meaningful overlap in September and December. Speckled dace selected chironomids more strongly than red shiner did.
- **Conclusions:** Biotic interactions were relatively unimportant in structuring fish communities in general, but competition for food between red shiner and woundfin may occur under some circumstances below Mesquite. Coevolved partitioning between the two omnivorous suckers was the more likely coevolutionary pattern. The authors conclude that biotic factors may now be playing a significant role in restructuring the community.

*Sample size note:* Woundfin, roundtail chub and spinedace fed sample sizes were limited (e.g., spinedace insufficient in June; flannelmouth sample small). Results for these groups should be read with caution.
