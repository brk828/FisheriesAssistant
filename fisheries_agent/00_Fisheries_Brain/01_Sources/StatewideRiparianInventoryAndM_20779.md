# Statewide Riparian Inventory and Mapping Project: Methodology and Updated Accuracy Assessments for Perennial Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-vegetation

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department technical report (Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Technical Report 111, by [[Richard A. Winstead]], June 1997) documents the methods and accuracy of the [[Statewide Riparian Inventory and Mapping]] (SRIM) project for riparian vegetation along perennial waterways. The work responded to the 1992 directive under Arizona Revised Statute 45-101 ([[Waters Riparian Protection Program]]) and updates an earlier 1993 report by Valencia et al. that had been published with little field validation.

Field crews collected releve-based vegetation data from October 1992 to October 1994, recording more than 270 plant species along perennial waters. Of 1,866 randomly selected polygons, 1,671 were sampled, and 1,461 (87%) were correctly identified as riparian. Only 515 (35%) were correctly assigned to vegetation series, rising to 45% after similar classes were grouped. Final maps show about 165,260 acres of riparian area in 10 classes, roughly 101,500 acres (38%) less than the 1993 estimate.

## Background & Description

The project was run by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) Nongame Branch, with satellite imagery and aerial videography interpreted by the [[University of Arizona]] and field verification by AGFD biologists. Primary funding came from the AGFD Heritage Fund. The report defines riparian areas following [[Lowe 1985]]: vegetation in or adjacent to drainages that differs in species or life-forms from the surrounding non-riparian climax. This definition underpins [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission Policy A2.13]] on riparian habitat.

Field data served three purposes: identifying plant species (for classification and interpreter training), describing vegetation structure (as an indicator of likely wildlife use and community health), and recording land use and disturbance (grazing, mining, fuelwood cutting, adjacent development), which relate to riparian functions and values.

The data were stored in three linked dBASE files (VEGSITE, VEGDATA, PHOTOLOG/VEGPLOT) plus a tracking database (VERIFY) that recorded polygon status, classification and problem codes. Linkage was via unique polygon and plot identifiers and [[Universal Transverse Mercator Coordinates]] where GPS was used.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Sample plots were 0.5 acre (0.2 ha) and placed by stratified random selection of polygons, stratified by vegetation type and size. One plot was assigned per 2.5 acres, capped at ten per polygon. The accuracy target was 80% correct classification, compared with an assumed ceiling of about 85% for satellite-derived data ([[Jensen 1986]]). Crews used the [[Releve Technique]] with species prominence values on a 1 to 5 scale.

**Error sources.** Errors arose mainly from:
- Understory riparian plants (e.g., Arizona madrone, broadleaf deciduous trees, mountain willows) invisible beneath conifer and oak canopies.
- Species confusion on video, especially among broadleaf deciduous species and between [[Honey Mesquite]] and [[Tamarisk]].
- Narrow montane corridors in shaded canyons, reduced upland-riparian moisture contrast, and late-spring imagery affected by snowmelt.
- Coarse hand-digitized boundaries.

**Elevational gradient.** Grouped classes followed an elevational sequence similar to [[Pase and Layser 1977]] and [[Szaro 1989]]: tamarisk, cottonwood-willow and mesquite at low elevations, mixed broadleaf at middle elevations, and conifer-oak, mountain shrub and wet meadow at high elevations.

**Biotic communities.** Sampled plants were assigned to [[Brown and Lowe 1980]] biotic communities by intersecting sample points with upland community maps, and wetland indicator status followed [[Reed 1988]].

## Key Findings

- **Sampling outcome:** 1,671 of 1,866 polygons sampled (minimum required: 1,490). Unavailable, missed, inaccessible, submerged and under-width polygons accounted for the rest.
- **Riparian identification:** 1,461 polygons (87.4 ± 1.3%) correctly identified as riparian.
- **Series-level accuracy:** 515 polygons (35.2 ± 1.2%) correct; 45.4 ± 2.2% after grouping similar classes (655 of 1,444 grouped polygons with known elevation).
- **Boundary errors:** 271 riparian polygons (18.5 ± 1.7%) needed boundary adjustment to exclude non-riparian land, most commonly for mesquite at low elevations and meadow and conifer at high elevations.
- **Montane size:** Based on 319 field measurements, 86.2 ± 3.2% of montane riparian areas were too small to map; about 930 acres were removed from maps. Two-thirds of sampled polygons were one pixel (30 m) wide or less.
- **Acreage revision:** Final maps show about 165,260 acres in 10 classes, about 101,500 acres (38.0%) less than the 17-class 1993 estimate. A 42.1% acreage decrease among sampled polygons resulted from reclassifying 10,402 non-riparian acres out of 24,714 satellite-classed riparian acres.
- **Stable classes:** Tamarisk (35.1% vs 35.5%), mesquite (17.5% vs 18.1%), flood scoured (7.4% vs 6.3%), marsh (2.1% vs 3.0%) and Russian olive (<1.0% vs 0.7%) prevalence changed little between 1993 and the final maps.
- **Conclusion:** Riparian boundaries met the project accuracy standard, but satellite-based classification of plant communities did not. Map accuracy statewide remains quantitatively unknown, though a field check of the [[San Pedro River]] and [[Santa Cruz River]] showed close agreement.
- **Recommendations:** Refine the conifer-oak group (which mixes true riparian forest with upland conifer stands); subdivide cottonwood-willow and mesquite series by bioregion or life zone; and reanalyse field data for new plant community classes.
