# Fin Clips in the Evaluation of Stocking Programs for Muskellunge

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Fercus I. McNeil]] and [[E. J. Crossman]] tests whether fin clipping, a cheap and widely used marking method, can reliably evaluate [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy*) stocking programs. Using hatchery fingerlings in Ontario, laboratory tanks, an artificial stream and a field study in [[Nogies Creek]], the authors examined immediate and long-term mortality, growth, fin regeneration and the survival of stocked versus wild fish. Fin clipping added little immediate mortality, and anesthesia had no effect on survival or growth. However, removing a pectoral fin reduced long-term survival relative to removing a pelvic fin, and fins regenerated so well that clipped fish became unidentifiable within about two years. The authors conclude that fin clips do not meet the retention requirements of muskellunge stocking evaluations, which likely produces underestimates of the contribution of stocked fish.

## Background & Description

Fin clipping is a standard mutilation mark for young fish when individual recognition is not needed. Earlier esocid studies were few and short-term. [[Johnson]] (1965, 1975) found no significant increase in short-term mortality from removing a single paired fin in young-of-the-year muskellunge, with or without predators. Patrick and Haas (1971) removed entire pelvic girdles and found only a 5% mortality difference. Studies on other fishes, including [[Lake Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Walleye]] and [[Northern Pike]], were inconsistent, with some reporting negligible effects and others detrimental effects on survival.

Muskellunge stocked as young of the year typically reach legal size in Ontario only after three years, so the mark must persist for a long time. The authors also observed that wild pectoral fins are used almost constantly, whereas pelvic fins are used little, even during prey orientation.

The study had four objectives: (1) compare the effects of removing pectoral versus pelvic fins on survival and growth; (2) assess any benefit of surgical anesthesia; (3) document the incidence and extent of fin regeneration; and (4) compare survival and growth of marked hatchery and unmarked wild muskellunge in the same habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Field site.** Nogies Creek is a closed system with a small, shallow, heavily vegetated lake (32.4 ha; mean depth 1.8 m; maximum depth 5.0 m) and a 6.4 km meandering stream (4.7 ha). It supports a typical warmwater fish community.

**Field methods.** Young-of-the-year fish were marked on August 9 in 1976 and 1977 at the Deer Lake hatchery. Fish were anesthetized with [[MS-222]] (tricaine methanesulfonate, 1:10,000) for some groups and marked with a dorsal spaghetti tag (Floy Tag) for fish over 250 mm. In 1976, 1,882 fish (mean 160 mm) had their right pelvic fin removed. In 1977, 3,343 fish (mean 150 mm) were randomly divided into four treatments: left pelvic or left pectoral fin removed under anesthesia, and right pelvic or right pectoral fin removed without anesthesia.

**Laboratory and tank conditions.** The artificial stream held 14 fish in 16 × 30 × 12 cm sections at 15 ± 0.5 °C, with dissolved oxygen of 8.9–9.5 mg/L and a 12 h light/dark cycle. The tank experiment used 36 Ohio River strain young-of-the-year fish in three groups of 12 at 18 ± 0.5 °C for the first 40 days. Mortality in both experiments was attributed to apparatus malfunctions. In the tank experiment, a temperature failure dropped water to 10 °C on day 41, and a later malfunction on day 50 killed the fish.

## Key Findings

- **Immediate mortality:** Fin removal did not add to the immediate mortality caused by seining. In 1977, 33 fish (0.9%) died between clipping and stocking, comparable to 1976, and treatment groups did not differ significantly (G-test, P > 0.1).
- **Anesthesia:** MS-222 had no effect on subsequent survival of marked, stocked fish (P > 0.5), nor on growth.
- **Short-term survival:** Removing any single paired fin was equally detrimental to short-term (3-month) survival.
- **Long-term survival:** Over 10 months, removing a pectoral fin was significantly more detrimental than removing a pelvic fin. Survival was higher for pelvic clips than pectoral clips in 1978 (P < 0.001). Removing both fins of a pair may cause higher mortality than removing one, though this was suggested rather than tested directly.
- **Growth:** No significant differences in growth existed among treatment groups in the field (t-test, P > 0.1), in tanks (ANOVA, P > 0.75), or in the artificial stream (P > 0.1). Neither the fin removed nor the anesthetic affected growth.
- **Mechanism:** The authors proposed that impaired prey orientation from losing a pectoral fin increases the vulnerability period to predation, rather than causing size- or condition-dependent mortality.
- **Fin regeneration:** All fin-clipped fish in tanks began regenerating within 10 days, and regeneration was independent of the fin removed and of anesthesia. In the field, about 80% of handled clipped fish were regenerating by 12 weeks, and 92% on average over the 1977 season for the 1976 year class. Clipped fins reached 45% of unclipped fin length within 7 weeks and nearly 77% within 50 days after warm weather returned.
- **Identification failure:** In 1978, 5 of 19 fish judged unmarked had been tagged in 1977. Field examination of regeneration type and degree could not identify all clipped fish. By 2 years, roughly 20% or more of the 1976 year class was unidentifiable stocked fish, and the authors estimated that almost all recovered 1978 fish were of hatchery origin.
- **Stocked versus wild survival:** Stocked fish of the 1977 year class declined faster than wild fish in the first month, but then declined at the same rate. For the 1976 year class, survival in the second season matched that of wild fish. Catchability of tagged, stocked and wild fish was equal (P > 0.50).
- **Stocked versus wild growth:** No significant differences in length existed between stocked and wild fish of the same year class at any sampling period (t-test, P > 0.1), and small initial size differences did not affect growth rate.
- **Recovery rates:** Total recovery as a percentage of stocked numbers ranged from 3.16% to 6.40% across treatment groups.

**Conclusions and management implications:**
- Fin clips are unsuitable for long-term marking of young-of-the-year muskellunge, since the required retention period (at least 3 years) is not met. Their use would substantially underestimate stocked fish contributions.
- First-year survival estimates from fin clips remain valid for relative abundance of a single plant, but not for comparing hatchery contributions across plants or years, since stocking success varies.
- Previous long-term stocking evaluations based on fin clips should be interpreted cautiously and may be minimum estimates.
