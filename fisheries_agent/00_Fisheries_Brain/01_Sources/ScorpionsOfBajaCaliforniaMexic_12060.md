# Scorpions of Baja California, Mexico, and Adjacent Islands

#ichthyology #marine-biology #arachnology #scorpiones

## Executive Summary

This monograph by Stanley C. Williams (Occasional Papers of the [[California Academy of Sciences]], No. 135, 1980) revises the scorpion fauna of the [[Baja California Peninsula]] and its associated islands. The fauna comprises 61 species, 12 subspecies, 11 genera and 4 families, based on roughly 60,000 examined specimens from three peninsular and four island collecting expeditions. The work clarifies taxonomy, maps distributions, provides keys, and describes 13 new species and one new genus, [[Paravaejovis]]. It also synonymizes [[Serradigitus]] with [[Vaejovis]] and proposes the replacement name *Vaejovis montcazieri*.

Note: the document is a scientific monograph rather than a fish study. Because the source concerns arachnids, the standard ichthyology tags have been replaced by arachnological ones.

## Background & Description

The scorpion fauna was first described by H. C. Wood (1863), with six of eight species from Baja California. Early misidentifications by [[Banks]] (1910) and [[Ewing]] (1928) persisted until revision. [[Gertsch]] (1958) treated 16 species, three of them new, roughly doubling the known regional fauna. The present study, initiated in 1968, was supported by the [[National Science Foundation]] (grants GB 7679 and GB 23674).

Methods followed standard scorpion systematics. Measurements were taken to 0.1 mm with a calibrated eyepiece micrometer, and key characters included pedipalp chela proportions, trichobothria counts, pectine tooth counts, supernumerary granules, metasomal keel development and telson structure. Variability was assessed from a series of 20 mature individuals (half males).

Four families are recognized in the region:
- **Buthidae:** one species, [[Centruroides exilicauda]], the only buthid in Baja California.
- **Chactidae:** one species, [[Superstitionia donensis]].
- **Diplocentridae:** four species of [[Didymocentrus]].
- **Vaejovidae:** the dominant family, with 8 of 16 world genera (50%) and 55 of 155 species (35%) recognized in the region.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fauna is one of the richest in the world relative to area, which the author attributes to the peninsula's complex geological history. Rifting from mainland Mexico, northwesterly movement, sea-level changes, island formation and submergence, and volcanism together produced diverse habitats. These include marine sedimentary soils, volcanic and granitic terrain, wind-deposited sands, conifer and chaparral mountains, desert basins, salt flats, cactus and idria forests, and tropical thorn forest.

Eight biotic provinces are recognized: [[Californian Province]], [[Vancouveran Province]], [[Colorado Desert]], [[Vizcaino Desert]], [[Volcanic Province]], [[Magdalena Plain]], [[Cape Province]] and [[Island Province]]. The Island Province is richest (33 species), followed by the Volcanic Province (25 species). The Vancouveran Province is poorest (6 species).

Habitat and microhabitat notes include:
- *Vaejovis littoralis* is unique in living in the intertidal zone, where juveniles migrate along beaches above the tide line.
- *Vaejovis pattersoni* was found mostly under bark of dead trees at about 1,707 m in the Sierra de La Laguna.
- *Syntropis macrura* favors vertical volcanic cliffs and fractured rock.
- *Vaejovis janssi* on Socorro Island was recorded in jungle, scrub, sand, and trees up to about 7.6 m above ground.
- *Paruroctonus surensis* was found only on sand-dune surfaces at night using ultraviolet detection.

Gigantism is recorded in *Centruroides exilicauda* near La Paz and on Espiritu Santo, possibly linked to prey availability and prevailing sea winds.

## Key Findings

- **Diversity:** Baja California holds 4 of 6 world families, 11 of 128 genera (9%) and 61 of 1,437 species (4%), in a peninsula about 1,300 km long and 100 km wide on average. It is richer in genera and species than the [[Western United States]] and [[Mainland Mexico]] comparisons in Table 4, and is the most diverse North American scorpion fauna.
- **Endemism:** 46 of 61 species (75%) and 4 of 11 genera are endemic. Endemic genera are [[Nullibrotheas]], [[Paravaejovis]], [[Syntropis]] and [[Vejovoidus]]. Endemic species are most numerous in the Island (25), Volcanic (20), and Cape (13) provinces.
- **Taxonomic changes:**
  - 12 new *Vaejovis* species and one new *Paruroctonus* (*P. surensis*) described, plus *Paravaejovis* as a new genus.
  - *Serradigitus* Stahnke is treated as a junior synonym of *Vaejovis*.
  - *Centruroides sculpturatus* is treated as a junior synonym of *C. exilicauda*.
  - *Paruroctonus bajae* and *Vaejovis puritanus* synonymies are proposed (e.g., *V. schwenkmeyeri* and *V. terradomus* treated as races of *V. puritanus*).
  - *Vaejovis montcazieri* is proposed as a replacement name for the junior homonym *V. cazieri*.
- **Character evaluation:** Coloration, overall size and hirsuteness often reflect local race formation, not species limits. Ratio variability can be high across populations; for example, *C. exilicauda* varies enough that extreme subpopulations could be mistaken for different species.
- **Origins:** An archaic element predates separation from mainland Mexico roughly 4–6 million years ago. Recent northern invasions, probably during Pliocene and Pleistocene climate and sea-level shifts, account for the widespread northern species. The *wupatkiensis* subgroup appears to be undergoing active speciation on shallow-water islands.
- **Geographic affinity:** 15 of 61 species (25%) also occur outside Baja California. The closest outside affinity is the [[Colorado Desert]] (10 species in common).
- **Future work:** Remaining undiscovered taxa are expected mainly in the *wupatkiensis* and *minimus* subgroups of *Vaejovis*, in inaccessible regions and unsampled islands.
