# Radiotelemetry of Razorback Suckers in the Gila River, Eastern Arizona

## Executive Summary
This study by Marsh and Minckley represents a pilot radiotelemetry investigation of reintroduced [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Gila River, Arizona, following the species' extirpation from the basin in the late 1950s. As part of a 10-year reintroduction program initiated in 1981, researchers surgically implanted internal radio transmitters into 23 hatchery-reared and wild-caught adult suckers across two release groups (May and October 1988) to track post-stocking movement patterns and habitat use. Key findings revealed a strong and statistically significant bias toward downstream movement, high short-term mortality attributed to terrestrial/aerial predation (particularly during low-flow periods), and consistent selection of wide, sandy, mid-channel run habitats. The fate of 9 of 23 tagged fish remained undetermined, highlighting significant logistical challenges in tracking large-bodied riverine fish through canal systems and downstream reservoirs. The study established the technical feasibility of radiotelemetry for this species in the Gila River and generated management recommendations for future reintroduction monitoring.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Xyrauchen texanus* (Abbott) — [[Razorback Sucker]], family [[Catostomidae]]
- **Study specimens**:
  - Group I: Ten hatchery-reared fish (4, 6, or 7 years old) from Dexter National Fish Hatchery (DNFH), plus four wild adults trammel-netted from Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada (n=14 total)
  - Group II: Nine hatchery-reared fish (4 years old) from DNFH
- **Growth/size context**: Based on known growth rates, reintroduced fish could reach adult sizes >50 cm total length within 3–5 years (Hamman 1985; Marsh and Langhorst 1988)
- **Surgical implantation**: Internal radio transmitters (Smith-Root, Inc. or AVM Co.) coated with bee's wax to prevent extrusion; surgically placed in body cavity under MS-222 anesthesia
- **Transmitter specifications**: 11–18 g in air, 40-MHz frequency range; battery life 6–9 months (Smith-Root) or 12–14 months (AVM)
- Fish were also externally marked with dangler or spaghetti tags for secondary identification

## Ecological & Environmental Context
### Habitat Preferences
Radiotagged razorback suckers predominantly occupied **wide, sandy, mid-channel runs** of modest depth and current velocity — identified as the most common available habitat type in the study reach of the [[Gila River]]. This pattern was consistent with reduced but present use of eddies, quiet backwaters, and deep pools.

**Quantified habitat parameters:**

| Parameter | Group I (n=30 contacts) | Group II (n=11 contacts) |
|---|---|---|
| Stream width | 10 ± 5 m | 10 ± 3 m |
| Stream depth | 0.4 ± 0.3 m | 0.5 ± 0.4 m |
| Current velocity | 0.21 ± 0.17 m/sec | 0.30 ± 0.20 m/sec |
| Dominant substrate | Sand | Sand |
| Dominant habitat type | Run | Run |

No statistically significant difference in occupied habitat was found between Group I and Group II fish (t-test, n=39, p>0.05), nor between fish moving upstream versus downstream.

### Movement Patterns
- **Group I** (released 24 May 1988, discharge 1.64 m³/sec):
  - 3 of 14 fish moved upstream, averaging 7.2 km (range 0.2–23.7 km); tracked ~10 days (1–32 days)
  - 11 fish moved downstream, averaging 11.9 km (range 1.6–32.0 km); tracked ~19 days (4–32 days)
  - Most movement occurred during darkness within the first 12 hours (fish remained within 0.8 km of release site)

- **Group II** (released 12 October 1988, discharge 6.23 m³/sec):
  - 7 fish moved downstream, averaging 8.1 km (range 0.1–37.2 km); tracked ~39 days (1–144 days)
  - 2 fish lost contact shortly after release

- **Overall trend**: Significantly more downstream than upstream movement (chi-square = 13.50, df=1, p<0.01), consistent with earlier findings in juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] reintroductions in the same river (Brooks 1985; Jensen et al. 1987)

### Comparative Context
Habitat relations were similar to those reported for radiotagged adult razorback suckers in the [[Green River]], Utah (Tyus 1987), despite the Green River being substantially larger than the Gila River — suggesting conserved habitat selection behavior across differing riverine scales.

### Predation & Mortality Pressures
- High short-term mortality observed, with evidence of attacks by terrestrial or aerial predators/scavengers (some transmitters recovered bearing tooth marks)
- By 32 days post-release (Group I), 8 of 14 fish were confirmed dead
- Low September flows reduced riffle depths to only a few centimeters in places, increasing fish stranding risk and predator access
- One transmitter was located in an apparent raptor nest on a cliff above the river
- Predation dynamics align with prior documented concerns regarding [[Ictalurid Catfish]] predation on stocked razorback suckers (Marsh and Brooks 1989)

## Key Findings
1. **Directional movement bias**: Razorback suckers exhibited significantly greater downstream than upstream movement post-stocking (chi-square = 13.50, df=1, p<0.01), mirroring patterns seen in earlier studies of stocked juveniles.

2. **Habitat selectivity**: Fish consistently occupied wide, sandy, mid-channel runs with modest depth (~0.4–0.5 m) and low-to-moderate current velocity (~0.2–0.3 m/sec) — the dominant habitat type available in the study reach, rather than preferentially seeking refugia like pools or eddies.

3. **High early mortality**: Substantial predation-related mortality occurred shortly after release, particularly linked to low-flow conditions in September that stranded fish in shallow riffles, exposing them to terrestrial and aerial predators.

4. **Detection challenges**: Radio signal detection was severely limited in San Carlos Reservoir due to water depth attenuation (transmitters >3.0 m deep were undetectable) and high water conductance (>1000 μmhos/cm), consistent with known signal attenuation properties (Winter 1983).

5. **Unresolved fates**: The ultimate fate of 9 of 23 tagged fish (39%) could not be determined despite extensive canal and reservoir searches, suggesting possible predation, canal stranding/mortality in irrigated fields, or undetected movement into San Carlos Reservoir.

6. **Transmitter performance**: Battery life limitations likely explain the relatively short contact periods for many Group II fish, as most had been active near their rated lifetime by the study's end.

7. **Management recommendations**:
   - Implant transmitters at the hatchery with recovery time before release to reduce surgery-related mortality
   - Stock fish during high-flow periods (e.g., late autumn–winter) to reduce predation risk from stranding
   - Expand radiotelemetry to confined habitats (e.g., backwaters) to study diel movement patterns
   - Intensify sampling efforts in downstream receiving reservoirs to track reintroduced populations
   - Use radiotelemetry to guide conventional sampling efforts by pinpointing fish-occupied habitats, addressing historically inadequate recapture rates (Minckley et al. 1990)
