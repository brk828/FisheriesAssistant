# Long-Term Retention of Implanted Transmitters in Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #radiotelemetry #endangered-species

## Executive Summary
This 1988 study by Harold M. Tyus (USFWS) evaluated the long-term retention, growth effects, and survival effects of surgically implanted radiotransmitters in two imperiled Colorado River basin fishes: the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the (then-candidate) [[Razorback Sucker]], studied in the Green River, Utah, from 1978–1987. Across 97 implanted Colorado squawfish and 9 implanted razorback suckers, no transmitter expulsion was ever detected during radiotracking. Of 14 transmitters recovered from recaptured fish, retention ranged from 3 months to 8 years, strongly suggesting that surgically implanted transmitters using a lateral incision and beeswax coating can be retained for the life of the fish. No adverse effects on growth or survival were detected relative to externally dangler-tagged fish, supporting the safety and durability of this surgical implantation methodology for long-term telemetry studies of rare, hard-to-sample riverine fishes.

## Background & Description
- **Species studied**: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, family Cyprinidae) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, family Catostomidae).
- **Size range of implanted Colorado squawfish**: 482–770 mm (fish used in growth comparisons).
- **Transmitter casing materials**: polystyrene, polycarbamate, and acrylic resin; all except polystyrene were coated in **beeswax** prior to implantation.
- **Transmitter-to-body weight ratio**: averaged less than 1% of fish body weight in both species — well below the <2% threshold recommended by Marty and Summerfelt (1986) to minimize expulsion risk.
- **Surgical method**: Lateral incision through heavy ventrolateral musculature (per [[Tyus and McAda 1984]]), contrasting with the ventral midline incision technique used in comparable ictalurid/salmonid studies.
- **Tissue response**: Recovered transmitters were consistently encapsulated by connective tissue, sometimes fixed to old incision scar tissue; growth of tissue onto and apparently into the beeswax coating isolated the capsules from the viscera. No transmitter was found free in the body cavity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Both species inhabit large, turbid riverine systems of the Colorado River basin (Green River, Utah), environments where visual observation is precluded by turbidity, making [[Radiotelemetry]] a critical monitoring tool.
- **Conservation status**: *Ptychocheilus lucius* was federally listed as **endangered** (USFWS 1974); *Xyrauchen texanus* was a **candidate species** for listing (USFWS 1985) at time of publication.
- **Behavioral monitoring context**: Fish were tracked in natural riverine conditions for extended periods (routinely 5–6 months, with some individuals followed 12–18 months), supporting studies of [[Homing Behavior]], migration, and habitat use previously documented for these species (Tyus 1985, 1987).
- **Sampling methods for recapture**: Electrofishing, netting, and angling were used to recapture implanted individuals across the Green River basin.

## Key Findings
- **Zero detected expulsion**: No transmitter expulsion was observed during radiotracking of 97 Colorado squawfish and 9 razorback suckers (1978–1985).
- **Long retention times**: Of 14 recovered transmitters, 12 were retained in Colorado squawfish for 3 months to **8 years** (mean = 2.2 years); 2 were retained in razorback suckers for **1 year** (mean = 1 year).
- **Contrast with other species**: These results sharply contrast with rapid transmitter expulsion documented in [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) — 71% expulsion in 112 days (Summerfelt & Mosier 1984) and 53% expulsion in 23 days (Marty & Summerfelt 1986) — and in [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) — 59% expulsion in 175 days (Chisholm & Hubert 1985).
- **Surgical technique matters**: A lateral incision (avoiding the linea alba) appears to reduce herniation and expulsion risk compared to ventral midline incisions used in catfish/trout studies, which were prone to rupture and transmitter loss (especially in ripe females).
- **Beeswax coating efficacy**: Beeswax likely promoted retention by inducing mild tissue irritation and connective tissue ingrowth, encapsulating and immobilizing the transmitter — unlike less tissue-reactive coatings (e.g., silicone, silicone rubber) associated with implant migration in other studies.
- **No growth effects**: Growth rate of implanted Colorado squawfish (mean 11.2 mm/year, SD=10.2, n=14) did not differ significantly from nonimplanted dangler-tagged fish (mean 10.2 mm/year, SD=11.3, n=59; Student's t=0.3, P=0.76). Razorback sucker growth (2.5 mm/year, n=2) was similarly comparable to nonimplanted fish (2.2 mm/year, n=39).
- **No differential mortality**: Recapture rates for implanted fish (26% for Colorado squawfish, n=70; 22% for razorback sucker, n=9) compared favorably to nonimplanted dangler-tagged fish (16% and 19%, respectively), indicating no elevated long-term mortality from surgical implantation.
- **Weight ratio recommendation supported**: Findings corroborate prior recommendations (Marty & Summerfelt 1986; Mesing & Wicker 1986; Miller & Menzel 1986) that transmitter-to-fish weight ratios below ~1.5–2% minimize expulsion risk.
- **Practical implication**: Surgical implantation with lateral incision and beeswax-coated transmitters is a viable, low-impact method for long-term (potentially lifetime) monitoring of rare and endangered riverine fishes, critical given the difficulty of obtaining adequate samples for such species.
