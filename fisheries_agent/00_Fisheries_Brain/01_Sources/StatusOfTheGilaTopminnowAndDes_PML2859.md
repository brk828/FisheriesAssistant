# Status of the Gila Topminnow and Desert Pupfish in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Technical Report (NGTR 118, June 1997), authored by David A. Weedman and Kirk L. Young, reviews the status of two federally endangered desert fishes, the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) and the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), covering monitoring from 1992 to 1997. Of 15 known natural Gila topminnow localities, four no longer support the species, and no natural desert pupfish populations remain in Arizona apart from [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] at Quitobaquito Spring. Of 175 wild localities stocked or colonized, Gila topminnows are extant at only 18 (17 within historic range), and the number of successful reintroduced populations has stayed below the 20 required for downlisting since 1991. The report proposes extirpation criteria, a three-tier reintroduction framework under the [[Recovery Plan]], and an aggressive program of 15–20 site stockings per year starting in 1998.

## Background & Description

**Gila topminnow.** A small live-bearing poeciliid of the genus *Poeciliopsis* (19 known species, of which only *P. o. occidentalis* is native to the Gila Basin). Historically it occupied shallow margins of main river channels, backwaters, larger tributaries and springs, preferring quiet water with abundant aquatic vegetation. It was listed as endangered in 1967, and a recovery plan was approved in 1984.

**Desert pupfish.** A member of the family Cyprinodontidae. Arizona historically held two described subspecies, *C. m. macularius* and *C. m. eremus*, plus an undescribed Monkey Spring form. The desert pupfish was listed as endangered in 1986 (both subspecies). Its historic range covered the Lower Colorado River and tributaries, including the Gila, San Pedro, Salt and Santa Cruz rivers.

**Historical decline.** Declines followed settlement and were driven by dam construction that dewatered channels and blocked dispersal, introduction of nonnative predatory and competitive fishes (including [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Red Shiner]]), drainage of swamps and cienegas, groundwater pumping, arroyo cutting and stream desiccation (Miller 1961; Meffe et al. 1983). The Gila topminnow, once the most common fish in the Gila Basin, was reduced to 10 known populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Natural drought cycles** historically caused local extirpations, with survivors persisting in permanent springs, small streams and cienegas until recolonization.
- **Nonnative fishes** are the most frequently cited threat. Mosquitofish, green sunfish, largemouth bass, guppies, red shiners and bullheads are recorded at many sites.
- **Flooding and desiccation** account for most failures of reintroduced populations; cold winter temperatures, trampling by cattle and recreational impacts are also noted.
- **Genetic structure:** research indicates genetic and fitness differences among populations (Vrijenhoek et al. 1985; Quattro and Vrijenhoek 1989), so reintroductions after 1984 favored lineages such as [[Sharp Spring]] stock held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- **Desert pupfish contamination:** Quitobaquito pupfish may have hybridized with desert pupfish after mixed broodstock at the University of Arizona, creating genetic uncertainty at several sites.
- **Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument** harbors the only remaining natural *C. m. eremus* population, which supports roughly 5,000–10,000 individuals under normal conditions (USFWS 1993).

## Key Findings

**Management history**
- The 1981 Memorandum of Understanding (USFWS, USFS, Arizona Game and Fish Commission) designated "experimental reintroduction" as the enhancement tool. Because the experimental non-essential designation was never pursued, all reintroduced populations received full ESA protection, requiring Section 7 consultation and generating distrust among agencies.
- Downlisting criteria were met from 1985 to 1990 but downlisting was not pursued because long-term survival appeared tenuous. The number of successful reintroduced populations fell below 20 in 1991.
- The draft revised recovery plan (1994) proposes a three-level framework: Level 1 natural populations (protected), Level 2 primary replicates (two per natural population, persisting ≥10 years), and Level 3 secondary replicates (three per natural site).
- Assuming 10 natural populations remain, 50 replicate populations are needed for downlisting; four extant reintroduced populations are pure replicates, so 45 more are needed.
- The desert pupfish recovery plan (USFWS 1993) requires 10 Tier 2 populations in the lower and middle Gila River and at least 45 Tier 3 populations in Arizona, with downlisting criteria for *C. m. macularius* only; delisting was judged infeasible in the foreseeable future.

**Natural populations**
- 15 natural Gila topminnow localities were recorded; four (Cocio Wash, an unnamed San Pedro spring, Monkey Spring-area Ash Creek and Salt Creek-related sites) no longer support the species.
- Fresno Canyon population discovered January 1992; Coalmine Canyon extension found in 1996.
- Sheerly Spring contained only mosquitofish and Gila chub during five years of monitoring.
- Quitobaquito Spring: a May 1995 survey found large numbers of pupfish and no nonnative fishes; a black bullhead was collected in 1993 of unknown origin.

**Reintroduced populations**
- Since 1936, 175 wild localities have been stocked or colonized. Gila topminnows no longer exist at 157 of them; failure causes were speculated for 95 sites (drying/drought 56, flooding 29, trampling by cattle 7, cold temperature 6, dredging 1, poor water quality 1, mosquitofish 1) with 62 unknown.
- 17 extant reintroduced populations lie within historic range; 18 sites total include Yerba Mansa, outside historic range.
- The average age of extant reintroduced populations is 13+ years; the longest-running is Hidden Water Spring (stocked 1976).
- Of the 17 in-range populations: 7 are category 1, 6 category 2, 2 category 3 and 2 category 4; five meet the draft recovery plan downlisting requirements.

**Extirpation and provisional status**
- Recommendations include 40 wild Gila topminnow reintroduced populations declared extirpated and eight provisionally extirpated under the new criteria.
- Under the extirpation criteria, a population is declared extirpated after three consecutive thorough surveys (first and last at least two years apart) during the April–November breeding season, with voucher specimens and two-mile drainage surveys if the site is dry.

**Future actions and recommendations**
- Protect natural populations through state or federal ownership and conservation easements.
- Stock 15–20 sites each year beginning in 1998 for four to five years, using a prioritized reintroduction plan.
- Ten perennial stream reaches are recommended for future evaluation, including Aravaipa Creek, Arivaca Creek, Babocomari River, Fish Creek, Fossil Creek, Granite Creek and the Verde River near Perkinsville.
- Consult under Section 7 for each site before restocking, with NEPA and ESA compliance coordinated by AGFD.
- Full recovery, including downlisting, was projected at about 10 years after Tier 2 and 3 populations are established, roughly by 2012.

**Conclusions**
- Recovery progress has regressed since the late 1980s through continual loss of populations, and the number of extant reintroduced Gila topminnow populations is slightly fewer than in 1992.
- Significant progress was made in administrative terms by removing many historic localities from review through extirpation criteria. The authors expect this to build cooperation among agencies.
