# Restoring the Missouri River: A Historian's Campaign to Recover a Lost Ecosystem

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This feature profiles [[Stephen Ambrose]], a historian and author who retraced the route of the [[Lewis and Clark Expedition]] along the [[Missouri River]] for two decades. Comparing the explorers' journals with the present-day river, Ambrose found a heavily modified waterway: leveed, dammed, and channeled into a narrow barge canal, with about one fifth of its river and floodplain flora and fauna endangered or threatened. He is supporting a campaign, led in part by the advocacy group [[American Rivers]], to restore floodplain habitat and to change how the [[Army Corps of Engineers]] operates the lower Missouri's dams in favor of wildlife and recreation over commercial navigation. The article also places this effort within broader plans to modify hydropower projects on the Columbia and lower Snake rivers to aid salmon runs.

## Background & Description

Ambrose has traveled the Missouri each summer since 1976, following the expedition's route some 2,500 miles upstream from St. Louis to the river's source at Three Forks, Montana. His account of the expedition, [[Undaunted Courage]], became a bestseller. He is a retired history professor and the author of 20 books. At the time of the article he was 62 and recovering from a serious fall.

Ambrose compared the explorers' descriptions of abundant wildlife and braided channels with what he observed. He did not expect to see the enormous herds of bison, elk, and antelope or the flocks of waterfowl recorded by [[Meriwether Lewis]], including a flock of white pelicans reported as 3 miles long. He was most struck by the loss of the river's dynamic channel structure: islands, sandbars, and oxbows that had posed serious obstacles to the 1804 expedition.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Ambrose states that today's river is one third the size and three times as fast as the river the expedition explored.
- **Physical alteration:** Most of the Missouri's length has been leveed, dammed, and channeled, with floodplain acreage cut off from the channel.
- **Biodiversity loss:** About one fifth of the river's and floodplain's flora and fauna are endangered or threatened.
- **Flow regime concerns:** Advocates argue that the current operation of six mainstem dams on the lower Missouri suppresses natural seasonal flow patterns needed by fish and birds.
- **Economic comparison:** Barge traffic peaked at 3.3 million tons in 1977 and now carries less than half that amount, generating about $20 million annually. Boating, fishing, and other recreation are said to generate hundreds of millions of dollars.
- **Broader context:** Eight large hydropower dams on the Columbia and lower Snake rivers are also under consideration for flow changes to help restore [[Salmon]] runs along the route the expedition followed to the Pacific Ocean.

## Key Findings

- **Degraded river:** Ambrose judges the modern Missouri a "bloody disaster" compared with the river described in the expedition journals, though he says it can still be repaired.
- **Restoration goals:** The campaign seeks to return private floodplain acreage to the river, create side channels and wetlands, restore cottonwood stands and prairie, and revitalize urban riverfronts.
- **Proposed flow changes:** Advocates want the [[Army Corps of Engineers]] to increase spring flows to spur fish and bird reproduction and reduce summer flows to help nesting waterfowl, foraging wildlife, and anglers.
- **Recreation over navigation:** Lowering the upstream reservoirs could also improve recreational use. The authors argue that recreation's economic value outweighs commercial barge traffic.
- **Timing:** The campaign is tied to the [[Lewis and Clark Expedition]] bicentennial, a little more than five years away at the time of writing.
- **Guiding principle:** In Ambrose's words, "We can't restore the river Lewis and Clark knew, but we can repair a river that they would recognize."
