# Lower Colorado River Waterfowl Hunting and Desert Game: A Personal Reminiscence

#ornithology #waterfowl #anatidae

## Executive Summary

This is a first-person travel and hunting reminiscence, not a research paper. The author describes a trip of roughly three weeks down the lower [[Colorado River]] from [[Yuma]] to its mouth and about fifty miles beyond, written to guide future sportsmen. The text covers the best waterfowl habitat (hidden sloughs and ponds fed by summer floods), the abundance of [[Shore Birds]] and [[Sandhill Crane|sandhill cranes]] at the river mouth, a desert mirage effect, the local climate, and the relative difficulty of hunting [[Bighorn Sheep|mountain sheep]], deer and [[Wilson's Snipe]]. It closes with a short poem. The document contains no quantitative survey data, but it gives several useful counts and observations of bird abundance and distribution.

## Background & Description

The author made the trip "some years ago" and wrote in response to interest from the magazine *Western Field*, which had published a correspondent's account of the same region. The author notes that a trip of this kind can quickly remove a person from civilization and offers practical advice:

- **Timing:** Prepare for two to three weeks. The author suggests going in December, when the shooting was recorded.
- **Transport:** The recommended option is an excursion boat from Yuma, with a large steamboat large enough for forty to fifty people, operated by named captains (Mellen and Polhemus, if still in service). Smaller boats are available, and a gasoline launch is discussed as a backup. Wagon, horse or pack-train travel is possible, but a boat is needed to cross the river.
- **Route alternatives:** Following The Hardy (a side channel or river route near the mouth) is discouraged because navigation there is uncertain and the author found it a mud flat with a trickle of tidewater during his visit. Walking or wagon routes along the east side are possible, though the east-side wagon road no longer covers the full distance.

The author contrasts this experience with the complex, mechanized life of modern travel, favouring quiet and nature over "uproar."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Waterfowl habitat.** Ducks and geese concentrate in ponds and sloughs that are hidden from the river by dense willow, cottonwood, mesquite, [[Arrow Weed]], and wild flax. These depressions fill only during high summer floods, so their availability varies from year to year. One large pond, located on the east side of the river about a mile and a half from the river and perhaps three or four miles below the author's first pond, was judged the finest duck pond the author had seen. It had:

- Many arms bordered by very tall [[Tule Marsh|tule]] that provided excellent blinds
- A hard sandy bottom with water about one foot deep, avoiding deep mud
- Large concentrations of ducks and geese, with "about every kind of duck" present but no Canada geese

**Shore birds.** Near the river mouth, the author reports very large numbers of shore birds (willets, curlews, sandpipers, dowitchers and oystercatchers), with flocks along the tide line reaching three or four birds per square yard in strips roughly twenty to thirty feet wide, and thousands more circling overhead. The birds appeared to follow the shores of Lower California, since no migration across southern California could account for their numbers. Only a few cross the [[Mojave Desert]] in spring.

**Mirage and climate.** The extremely dry desert air produces intense mirages on the flat, bare terrain at the river mouth. Logs, earth clods and grass can appear as ducks, geese or cranes, and bare smooth ground can look like water. The author describes winter weather as mild, with cool nights, warm days and no insects, apart from storms off the Pacific coast that bring stronger winds and occasional rain.

**Sandhill cranes.** Large numbers winter in the region. The author notes they are graminivorous, feeding on grain and wild flax, and are unrelated to herons and bitterns. The flat ground makes stalking nearly impossible, so the hunter must find their flyways.

**Large mammals.** Deer are present but dense cover (willow, arrow weed, tule, flax) and the flat terrain limit visibility and shooting opportunities. Mountain sheep inhabit large desert ranges, but the author considers hunting them impractical because of the terrain, the need for carried water on the lower Colorado Desert, and the difficulty of recovering game. Wild pigs were also mentioned in the correspondent's account, which the author found accurate.

**Upland game.** Gambel's quail and Arizona quail offer good shooting. The author compares them to the valley quail of California, and notes they are naturally wary but tame when seen from a wagon.

## Key Findings

- **Best waterfowl ground:** Productive duck and goose shooting lies in hidden sloughs and ponds away from the main river, not on the open river. These depressions are easily overlooked and are often unknown to hunters.
- **Pond conditions:** Ponds fill mainly during summer floods, so their suitability varies by year. One mid-December visit found a pond too dry for good shooting that had been quite full a month earlier.
- **Largest pond observed:** The author describes a large east-side pond with dense tule, sandy bottom and very large numbers of ducks and geese, which he judged the finest duck pond he had seen.
- **Locating sloughs:** The river banks are monotonous and hard to navigate. The author could identify only two ponds with enough detail for a reader to find them, and urges the reader to seek local guidance.
- **Shore bird abundance:** Shore birds at the river mouth numbered in the thousands and were not hunted, with shooting "no difficulty" to arrange.
- **Mirage effect:** The flat, dry desert produces mirages that can make the landscape look like ponds full of game at close range.
- **Trip logistics:** Two to three weeks is the recommended duration. A large excursion boat from Yuma is the preferred option.
- **Missed species:** The author found no [[Wilson's Snipe]] in December at the river mouth, despite searching salt and fresh mud, but had seen the species in September on San Pedro Martir and in Mexico.
- **Absent species:** No Canada geese were seen or heard during the trip, though Hutchins's geese ("little honkers") were observed. The author notes that Canada geese occasionally pass his Mojave ranch.
- **Hunting conclusions:** Mountain sheep, deer and sandhill cranes are hard to hunt in this terrain, and the author does not regard them as good targets. Quail and waterfowl are the more practical quarry.
- **Document type:** The text is a historical personal account with no statistical sample sizes, no formal methods and no quantitative population estimates beyond the descriptive counts above.
