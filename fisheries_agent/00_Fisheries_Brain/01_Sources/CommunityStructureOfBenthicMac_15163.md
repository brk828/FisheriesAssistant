# Bait Fishes of the Lower Colorado River from Lake Mead to Yuma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 survey by [[Robert Rush Miller]] (University of Michigan) identifies the bait fishes sold along the [[Lower Colorado River]] between [[Lake Mead]], Nevada, and [[Yuma]], Arizona. It provides a dichotomous identification key covering 32 species, records the known or probable origin of each, and judges whether establishment in the river would harm the fishery. The survey found 31 species used as bait over the preceding 15 years. The author recommends four species without reservation, flags three for strict prohibition, and calls for coordinated state management of bait sources.

## Background & Description

The river's transformation into a series of reservoirs and placid stretches created new fishing demand. Introduced sport fishes such as [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Black Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] drew large numbers of anglers. Most anglers and many bait dealers did not know that escaped bait species could establish and damage the fishery. The paper was submitted for publication in May 1951 and was funded by a University of Michigan grant. Common names follow the California Department of Fish and Game. Field work in spring 1950 was done with the University of California, Los Angeles, the [[Scripps Institution of Oceanography]], and the California Department of Fish and Game. Sampling of bait tanks continued afterward.

The paper is organized around an illustrated key. Each step offers two opposed characters, such as adipose fin presence, fin position, scale counts, and fontanelle condition. Species accounts give native range, habitat, bait-tank records with dates and dealers, and an establishment judgment. Several records were checked by [[Carl L. Hubbs]], who identified many specimens.

The document also contains an unrelated second article, "Mechanical Aids for Bird Banding" by A. W. Miller and Russell Henry (California Department of Fish and Game). It describes band-spreading devices and is not part of the fish content.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat variety:** The author notes that reservoirs, cold-water tailraces below dams, oxbow lakes and irrigation canals let some species establish locally without affecting the whole fishery. This makes prediction difficult.
- **Source waters:** Bait came from many tributaries, including the [[Virgin River]] system, [[Santa Clara River]], the [[Green River]] drainage, the [[Sevier River]], [[Meadow Valley Wash]], the [[Bill Williams River]], and the Central Valley of California. Some bait was carried long distances, often by truck or air.
- **Dealer practices:** Some bait came from distant sources. Dealers at Pittman, Nevada, for example, picked up dace on the route from Green River. Mixed loads raised the risk of unintended introductions. Fathead minnows from New Mexico carried Mexican Banded Tetra into the river.
- **Disease risk:** Introductions from foreign waters could bring parasites and diseases harmful to native and acclimated fishes. The author judges any introduction potentially harmful.
- **Established species:** [[Carp]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Green Sunfish]] were already part of the river fauna. Among the bait species, only [[Utah Chub]] and the [[Bonneville Mottled Sculpin]] were noted as possibly established. The [[Golden Shiner]] is established in certain Arizona lakes, and [[Leatherside Chub]] had become established in [[Strawberry Reservoir]].
- **Vegetation:** The author judges the golden shiner unlikely to establish because the river lacks large weed beds. He warns that it could harm the fishery if extensive weed beds develop.

## Key Findings

**Identification and scope**
- The illustrated key separates 32 species, using adipose fin presence, fin structure, scale counts, gill raker counts, and mouth and fontanelle features.
- The author notes that mountain-suckers of the genus *Pantosteus* are hard to identify even for experts, so the key for them is only approximate.
- Of the 31 species used as bait in the past 15 years, more than half are minnows and suckers.

**Species accounts with the most important findings**
- **[[Mexican Banded Tetra]]** (*Astyanax fasciatus mexicanus*): Its aggressive, voracious nature was observed directly. It is considered potentially disastrous to the fishery if established, and it is recommended for strict prohibition.
- **[[Utah Chub]]** (*Gila atraria*): It is prolific and can crowd out other fishes. In [[Strawberry Reservoir]] it required a special trash fishery. It is recommended for strict prohibition.
- **[[Yellow Perch]]** (*Perca flavescens*): It is recommended for strict prohibition because establishment would likely cause irreparable harm.
- **[[Golden Shiner]]** (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*): Recommended without reservation. It is a good bass forage fish, and the author sees no establishment risk while submerged vegetation remains sparse.
- **[[Fathead Minnow]]** (*Pimephales promelas confertus*): Recommended without reservation. It is a popular panfish bait in the Midwest and is encouraged for use on the river.
- **[[Longjaw Mudsucker]]** (*Gillichthys mirabilis*): Recommended without reservation. It survives for some time in fresh water but cannot reproduce there, which suits it for bait. Propagation was being attempted, and the species was established in the [[Salton Sea]].
- **[[Bluegill]]** (*Lepomis macrochirus*): Already established and a favorite of anglers. Its continued use is harmless if wholesale seining does not deplete natural stocks.
- **[[Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio*): Already excessively abundant, so further introduction probably will not affect the fishery.
- **[[Goldfish]]** (*Carassius auratus*): Unlikely to adapt to the river, though it might gain a foothold in warmer reservoirs. Use is not recommended.
- **[[Bonneville Redside Shiner]]** (*Richardsonius balteatus hydrophlox*): Already established in Wyoming and spreading in the Green River drainage. It preys on grayling fry, so the author recommends against its use.
- **[[Sacramento Hitch]]** (*Lavinia exilicauda exilicauda*): Imported from California's Central Valley. Its further use is not recommended because its biological effect is problematical.
- **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): A vanishing species now known only from the Virgin River and tributaries. It should be protected from further reduction.
- **[[White River Spine-Dace]]** (*Lepidomeda* species): Its very restricted range argues for discouraging its use as bait.
- **[[Rio Grande Chub]]** (*Gila nigrescens*) and **[[Rio Grande Mountain-Sucker]]** (*Pantosteus plebeius*): Use should be discouraged because too little is known of their life histories.

**Subspecies and source findings**
- Fin-ray counts traced some [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] specimens to the upper Colorado River, and some to the Virgin River and Santa Clara River.
- Mountain-sucker records suggested several sources. The streamlined *Pantosteus delphinus delphinus* probably came from the Green River, and the chubbier *P. d. utahensis* came from Santa Clara River near Gunlock, Utah.
- [[Western White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni suckleyi*) was probably introduced into the upper Colorado near Hot Sulphur Springs about 1938 and has spread rapidly. The author does not recommend its use because native suckers may be as suitable.
- [[Utah Sucker]] (*Catostomus ardens*) is judged harmless if established, though it is known to eat trout eggs.

**Recommendations**
- Only four species are recommended without reservation: the Utah mountain-sucker, golden shiner, fathead minnow and longjaw mudsucker.
- Most of the remaining species are considered neutral or potentially undesirable.
- Mexican banded tetra, Utah chub and yellow perch should be strictly prohibited.
- Interested states should agree on a short list of safe species and encourage their propagation and distribution.
- Local rearing of a few bait species would meet angler demand and make it unprofitable to ship in mixed, potentially harmful fishes.
- Bait dealers should avoid practices that introduce harmful species or deplete supplies beyond recovery.
