# Brine Shrimp (*Artemia salina*): Biology, Culture, and Uses

#marine-biology #anostraca #artemiidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Commercial Fisheries leaflet (Fishery Leaflet 527, September 1961, by Lola T. Dees) describes the brine shrimp, *Artemia salina*, a crustacean that lives only in high-salinity water. It covers the species' taxonomy, natural history, reproduction, and ecology, along with its commercial value as live food for [[Aquarium Fishes]] and [[Fish Hatcheries]] and its use in classroom teaching and insecticide bioassays. The leaflet also gives practical methods for collecting, processing, hatching, and rearing the eggs, and lists commercial egg suppliers.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Brine shrimp belong to the genus *Artemia*, subclass Branchiopoda, order [[Anostraca]]. The document describes the present consensus as a single worldwide species, *Artemia salina*, with many varieties. Taxonomic status was described as long controversial, especially in Europe.
- **Morphology:** Adults are about one-third of an inch long. Males are smaller but stronger and more active than females. Adults have 11 pairs of swimming legs and 2 stalked compound eyes, with disc-like gills about midway on the legs. Body color ranges from pale yellow-green to blood red, depending on the concentration and chemical nature of dissolved salts.
- **Development:** Newly hatched young have a single eye and one main set of legs. They molt several times, and the adult form (2 eyes, 11 pairs of legs) is reached through these molts.
- **Relatives:** Freshwater fairy shrimp (*Branchipus*, *Eubranchipus*, and others) are closely related. Water fleas ([[Daphnia]]) are more distant relatives.
- **Egg viability:** Eggs stored in a dry, fairly cool place remain viable for at least 10 years, a discovery made in the United States about 1947 that enabled commercial marketing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Salinity tolerance:** Brine shrimp live and reproduce only in high-salinity water in natural salt lakes and evaporation ponds, and "cannot live long in fresh water." Sea water has about 3 percent salt.
- **[[Great Salt Lake]], Utah:** The salinity was about 25 percent in 1950. The lake is the remnant of [[Lake Bonneville]], a freshwater lake that drained to the Pacific Ocean via the Columbia and Snake Rivers. After decreased rainfall and falling lake level stopped its outflow, evaporation exceeded inflow from the Jordan and Bear Rivers, and salinity rose. Lake area ranged from 2,200 square miles in 1873 to 1,500 square miles in 1940.
- **[[San Francisco Bay]], California:** Brine shrimp are caught primarily in the salt-evaporating ponds.
- **Congregations:** Adults sometimes form ribbon-like windrows on the Great Salt Lake surface nearly half a mile wide and extending for miles, often making the water look red.
- **Temperature limits:** The document states that adults in the Great Salt Lake and San Francisco Bay die when water temperature falls below "69°C. (about 43° F.)". Note: these two values are internally inconsistent in the source (43°F corresponds to about 6°C) and should be verified against the original.
- **Food:** Brine shrimp eat free-floating algae and other microscopic organisms in brine ponds and salt lakes.
- **Predators and parasites:** Chief natural enemies are salt-water fly larvae, birds, and human collectors. No harmful parasites are known. High local abundance may result from few enemies.
- **Life-cycle response to habitat:** Shrimp stranded in evaporating tidepools bear living young as salinity rises. As food declines and salinity continues to climb, females switch to laying eggs. Eggs that lie on shore for weeks hatch when tides return them to the sea. Eggs are not harmed by low temperatures and hatch in large numbers in spring.

## Key Findings

**Reproduction**
- Sexual maturity is reached 18 to 21 days after hatching.
- Two reproductive modes occur: parthenogenesis (embryos from unfertilized eggs) when no males are present, and sexual reproduction when males fertilize eggs inside the female's reproductive tract.
- Eggs resembling fine brown sand are deposited once a tough shell forms. Eggs in water with specific gravity of 1.09 or higher float and do not hatch. Eggs hatch when specific gravity is below 1.09 and temperature is above 9°C.
- Some embryos hatch inside the ovisac and are ejected as nauplii (larvae).

**Uses**
- Classroom teaching: egg-to-larva development can be observed under a microscope.
- Insecticide testing: brine shrimp are described as superior organisms for testing insecticides, with high sensitivity to a broad range of compounds (Michael et al., 1956).
- Aquarium food: baby brine shrimp are a standard food for young tropical aquarium fishes after they pass the microscopic-food stage. Adults are excellent food for larger fish.
- Trout feeding trial (Utah Fish and Game Department, 1956): newly hatched [[Rainbow Trout]] fed brine shrimp showed growth and mortality approximately the same as trout fed turkey blood and liver or dry feed. [[Kokanee Salmon]] (landlocked sockeye) fed brine shrimp made excellent growth, while those fed blood and liver or dry feed lost weight. Native trout fed brine shrimp gained more weight than those on the other diets.
- Salt production: shrimp precipitate and filter out calcium carbonate and calcium sulphate impurities in evaporation ponds, which settle in fecal pellets before salt crystallizes. See [[Salt Production]].
- Human food: rarely used because shrimp are too small, although Indians of the Great Salt Lake region dried them for food when they were abundant.

**Egg Collection and Processing**
- Eggs float on water or are deposited on shore by wind. They are netted from shallow water or scooped from the shore with mud.
- Processing steps: high-pressure spray through a fine screen to remove mud, dead shrimp, and debris; sluice box to remove sand; overflow in fresh water to remove dead eggs, shells, and dust (fertile eggs sink); sun-drying in a thin layer; and a final blower cleaning.
- A sample is weighed and hatched, and the larvae are weighed to confirm yield quality. Eggs are vacuum-packed in cans for shipment.

**Hatching Protocol**
- Brine solution: 6 level tablespoons of iodine-free table salt (or rock salt) per 3 quarts of fresh water.
- Apparatus: an open glass or enamelware pan about 14 x 8 x 2 inches, with a seasoned wooden or glass partition blocking about one-fourth of the water. The partition clears the pan bottom by 1/4 inch.
- Dose: one-half level teaspoon of dry eggs in the smaller compartment, covered with cardboard or black cloth.
- Timing: hatching occurs within 24 to 48 hours at 75° to 80°F. Larvae, which are attracted to light, swim under the partition and are collected with a bulb syringe.
- Larvae cannot survive more than 12 hours in fresh water and must be rinsed free of salt before feeding.

**Rearing**
- Larvae are kept in a quart or larger container of sea water or brine solution with food.
- Suitable foods are yeast and one-celled floating algae.
- Maturity takes 4 to 5 weeks.
- Adults rarely produce eggs under laboratory conditions, and the reason is unknown.

**Commercial Supply**
- Eggs are sold through most tropical fish dealers and supply houses. Listed suppliers include Aquarium Stock Company (New York), [[General Biological Supply House]] (Chicago), Sanders Brine Shrimp Company (Ogden, Utah), [[San Francisco Aquarium Society]] (Hayward, California; also ships frozen brine shrimp), and Southern Biological Supply Company (New Orleans).
