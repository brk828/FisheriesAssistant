# Influence of Environmental Factors on the Growth of Norris Reservoir Sauger, *Stizostedion canadense canadense* (Smith)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1955 proceedings paper by William W. Hassler (North Carolina State College) examines how natural environmental variables affect first-year growth of [[Sauger]] (*[[Stizostedion Canadense Canadense]]*) in [[Norris Reservoir]], [[Tennessee]], using an age and growth sample of 3,393 fish across 1937 to 1949. Growth was compared with [[Water Temperature]] timing, the [[Agricultural Growing Season]], [[Precipitation]], [[Solar Radiation]], [[Air Temperature]], and [[Water Level Fluctuations]] using [[Correlation Analysis]]. Spring warming timing and early-year rainfall showed the strongest associations with growth. The paper's main aim is to raise awareness that environmental variation must be estimated before fishery management effects can be credited. A short appended section, from a different publication, describes [[Kentucky Department Of Fish And Wildlife Resources]] public relations and outreach efforts.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Sauger]], *Stizostedion canadense canadense* (Smith). The author treats the species as the focal taxon in all growth analyses.
- **Motivation:** Environmental effects on fish growth and abundance are rarely evaluated because projects are often too short, environmental records are sparse, or data are collected only in summer. Management techniques such as [[Population Manipulation]] and [[Environmental Alteration]] require these sources of variation to be estimated. Otherwise, management may be wrongly credited for environmental effects.
- **Growth metric:** [[First-Year Growth]] was chosen because (1) the largest length increments occur in year one, (2) first-year growth was independent of other years, since second-year growth was inversely correlated with first-year growth, and (3) there was no statistical difference in standard length between males and females in year one. First-year growth rates were also similar across reservoir regions, and genetic differences were not apparent.
- **Sample:** 3,393 Norris Reservoir sauger over 13 years. [[Table I]] reports a mean calculated standard length of 175 mm (standard error 0.6) across all year classes, with per-year-class values ranging from about 155 to 209 mm.
- **Data sources:** [[Hydraulic Data Division]] and Fish and Game Branch of the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] (water temperature, precipitation, water levels); [[Climatological Data Tennessee]] (U.S. Dept. of Agriculture; freeze dates and air temperature).
- **Thermal demarcation:** A water temperature of 60 °F at 0.5 ft depth defined the sauger growing season (spring first occurrence to fall last occurrence).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reservoir operation:** [[Norris Reservoir]] is a multiple-power storage impoundment, so water levels fluctuate within and between years.
- **Thermal regime:** Water at 0.5 ft reached 60 °F in spring and fall for an average of approximately 30 weeks per year (OCR text reads "3014") from 1937 to 1949. [[Stroud 1948]] reported temperatures at 10 ft depth for 1937 to 1946, averaging approximately 29 weeks (OCR "2934"). The author concludes that the 0.5 ft record reflects overall lake conditions.
- **Spawning and forage sequence:** Sauger spawn at about 50 °F in Norris Lake, from late February to early April. Fingerlings feed on plankton until [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) spawn at about 60 °F. Timing mismatch between sauger spawning and forage fish spawning may prolong or shorten the plankton-feeding period.
- **Population density:** Year-class abundance appeared to influence growth. Year-class abundance was ranked arbitrarily, so multiple correlation analysis was not applied. Low-abundance year classes (1942 and 1949) grew rapidly, possibly from reduced competition.
- **Vegetation inundation:** [[Stroud 1948]] linked high water that flooded vegetation on the exposed lake floor to increased growth of young-of-the-year [[Largemouth Bass]], and to growth of [[Black Crappie]] and basses in general.
- **Comparative studies cited:** [[Doan 1942]] (Lake Erie, air and water temperature), [[Van Oosten 1929]] (lake herring, no clear air temperature correlation), [[Hile 1941]] (rock bass, Nebish Lake; June and September temperature deviation significant), and [[Hile 1936]] (cisco, no growing-season correlation with air temperature).

## Key Findings

**Growth and sampling**
- Analysis covered 3,393 sauger over 1937 to 1949, with first-year growth as the response variable.
- The author judged that the study does not cover all factors and that its purpose is mainly to create awareness of environmental variability.

**Water temperature (strongest signal)**
- Elapsed days between spring and fall 60 °F (0.5 ft) events correlated positively with first-year growth: **r = 0.69**. Fisher's "t" test indicated the value would occur by chance less than **1 percent** of the time. The author calls this a significant positive correlation.
- Time in days for water to warm from 50 °F to 60 °F in spring correlated inversely with growth: **r = −0.51**, with a probability of **8 percent** under no correlation. The 1946 year was an exception to the trend.
- The 50 to 60 °F interval and the total growing season were not significantly related to each other (r = −0.35, exceeded **24 percent** of the time by chance). The author concludes each has its own effect.
- Proposed mechanisms: (1) lower metabolic rate during slow warming; (2) higher fry and fingerling survival in slow-warming years, increasing density and limiting growth; (3) prolonged plankton feeding when sauger spawn well before forage fish spawn.

**Agricultural growing season**
- Correlation between the elapsed agricultural growing season (freeze-free days from [[Climatological Data Tennessee]]) and the lake temperature growing season was **r = 0.10**, nonsignificant.
- Correlation between agricultural growing season and first-year sauger growth was also nonsignificant (**r = 0.10**).
- The author concludes that using "time between frosts" to evaluate lake conditions or fish growing seasons is of little value.

**Precipitation**
- Combined February and March rainfall at Norris Dam correlated negatively with first-year growth: **r = −0.57**, significant. The probability of this magnitude under no correlation is less than **4.00 percent** (as stated in the source).
- The author links this to the sauger spawning window (late February to early April).

**Solar radiation**
- No correlation between total annual solar radiation (percent possible sunshine) and growth.
- November was the only month with a significant correlation (**r = 0.60**). The author suggests it may be chance, but also that heavy November sunshine could prolong the growing season.

**Air temperature**
- Neither annual nor monthly mean air temperatures at Norris Dam correlated with first-year growth.

**Water level fluctuations**
- Annual maximum elevations (1937 to 1949, [[Table VI]]) produced a correlation of **r = 0.40**, with a relatively high chance probability; no definite graphical trend appeared.
- A possible cyclic pattern was noted: fast growth in 1942 and 1949, when maximum levels exceeded those of the three preceding years. However, fast growth also occurred in non-cyclic years (1938, 1941, 1945, 1946), so further evidence is required.
- The 1949 growing season was about six weeks longer than 1948 and five weeks longer than 1947, complicating the water-level interpretation.

**Summary conclusions (author's eight points)**
1. Multiple environmental factors were examined against first-year sauger growth in Norris Reservoir.
2. No discernible correlation between the agricultural growing season and the fish growing season; agricultural season not significant for growth.
3. Significant positive correlation between first-year growth and annual days between 60 °F water temperatures.
4. Inverse relationship between growth and the 50 to 60 °F warming interval in spring.
5. Significant negative correlation between growth and February–March rainfall.
6. No apparent correlation with annual solar radiation; November the only significant month.
7. No correlation with monthly or annual air temperatures.
8. Water-level fluctuations suggest a possible cyclic trend, requiring more data.

- Kentucky's Public Relations Division of the Department of Fish and Wildlife Resources had been operating for 10 years.
- Approximately 60,000 members of Junior and Senior sportsmen's clubs; 49,000 magazines mailed in September.
