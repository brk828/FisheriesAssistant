# Fish-Eating Birds and Fisheries: The Role of Fish-Eating Birds (1936 Progressive Fish Culturist Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-eating-birds

## Executive Summary

This 1936 issue of *The Progressive Fish Culturist*, issued by the [[Bureau of Fisheries]] of the U.S. Department of Commerce, is a collection of practical hatchery articles. Its central piece, by Clarence Cottam and P. M. Uhler of the Division of Wildlife Research, argues that fish-eating birds are generally wrongly blamed for the scarcity of food and sport fish. Diet studies show that these birds mostly eat abundant, low-value fishes and invertebrates, and that availability, not bird predation, is usually the limiting factor. The authors accept that local control is justified at hatcheries and stocked streams, and they recommend screening, frightening devices and limited shooting as preferred measures. The issue also contains a shorter piece by C. M. McCay of Cornell University on fish and fish culture research in modern Europe.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The first article (pp. 1–14) covers about twenty fish-eating bird species. The second (pp. 15–18) is a travel report on European fish culture and research.
- **Context:** The article notes that about 198 species and subspecies of birds, nearly 14 percent of American bird species, are classed as fish eaters in the [[American Ornithologists' Union]] check-list. These include gulls, terns, loons, grebes, petrels, pelicans, cormorants, herons, ibises, osprey, eagles, kingfisher and water ouzel.
- **Legal status:** Most of these birds are protected by federal and state law and can be killed only under special permit.
- **Stated purpose:** To set out a balanced policy that weighs the interests of anglers, commercial fishermen and bird lovers, rather than favoring one extreme.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Food availability:** Birds mainly take the most common and easily caught species. Fast, deep-water game fishes are seldom taken, while slow surface feeders such as menhaden, gizzard shad, killifish and minnows are most often eaten.
- **Other causes of trout loss:** Authorities cited in the article name warm water, stagnant and peaty water, low dissolved oxygen, pollution, lack of food and cannibalism as factors besides bird predation.
- **Carrying capacity:** Each stream has a limit on how many fish it can support. Once that limit is reached, more fish can survive only if the food supply increases.
- **Lake alkalinity case:** In a North Central lake where fish had disappeared, high alkalinity from long-term water-level decline, not birds, was the main cause. Night herons there fed mostly on salamander larvae and bullheads.
- **Overfishing and pollution:** The authors cite drought, a growing human population, drainage, pollution, overfishing and gunning as the main causes of fish and game scarcity.

## Key Findings

- **Pelicans:** On the Louisiana coast, 97 percent of fish taken were menhaden; no food fish were found in one area. In Florida, non-food fish made up 91.4 percent of the diet.
- **Western white pelican:** Field and laboratory work in Nevada cleared the bird of major depredation.
- **Double-crested cormorant:** A Maine study found 80 percent of the diet was sculpins or cunners, which are direct enemies of commercial fish.
- **Belted kingfisher:** Of 318 stomachs examined, less than half the fish were edible. Crayfish made up 16 percent of the food. The bird is a notable hatchery pest, and the article recommends protective measures there.
- **Great blue heron:** Of 189 stomachs, 45.16 percent was low-value fish, 24.8 percent valuable species and 8.15 percent insects. Its nesting colonies are a recognized public asset, but control at hatcheries is needed.
- **Green heron:** Of 277 birds, only a little over 1/20 of the diet was human food fish. At a Missouri hatchery, crayfish and aquatic insects that prey on fry were eaten in quantity.
- **Black-crowned night heron:** Of 117 stomachs, low-value fish made up 57.3 percent of the fish eaten, and valuable species only 12.7 percent. Its diet also included crustaceans, insects, frogs and rodents.
- **Yellow-crowned night heron:** Of 120 stomachs, only one fish was found, and it had no commercial value.
- **American bittern:** Of nearly 160 stomachs, less than a tenth of the food was valuable fish, mostly common panfish.
- **Water ouzel:** Of 60 stomachs, only six contained fish, and only one was a trout.
- **Osprey:** Of 43 birds, sluggish fish formed most of the diet. Only one bird had eaten a game fish, a trout.
- **Red-breasted merganser:** Of 150 stomachs, 34.26 percent was non-commercial fish and 14.38 percent valuable species, with no trout.
- **American merganser:** Of 107 stomachs, 33.27 percent was non-commercial fish and 32.79 percent valuable species. It is the merganser most often blamed for trout losses.
- **Hooded merganser:** Of 158 stomachs, 24.49 percent was non-commercial fish and 15.18 percent valuable species.
- **Gulls and terns:** Valuable fish make up a minor part of their diet. Complaints against them have been rare, though herring gulls may damage shellfish beds and blueberry plantations.
- **Estimated value of the Sea Gull Monument:** Built in Salt Lake City, Utah, in grateful memory of gulls' service against the cricket plague, it was described as costing more than $40,000.
- **Hatchery protection:** Screening or wire covering of tanks and small ponds is the most effective method. Trout raised in fully protected ponds until 9 to 10 inches long suffer fewer losses.
- **Control methods:** Recommended methods include tin "scare-birds," reflective tin on willow poles, carbide-gas exploders, lights on oscillating fans and wire grids over reservoirs. Trap use is discouraged.
- **Stream control:** Patrols of small groups of wardens can drive mergansers downstream to deeper water, as tested in Michigan.
- **European fish culture (McCay):** Fish form a larger share of foreign diets than in America. Research in Aberdeen (Torry Research Station), Friedrichshagen and Sweden is active. Russia is investing heavily in science, and the author argues that scientific research is the best long-term investment for America.
