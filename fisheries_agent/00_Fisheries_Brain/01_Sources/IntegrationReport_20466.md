# San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program: 1995 Integration Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Integration Report, prepared by [[BIO/WEST Inc]] in September 1995, summarizes research conducted since 1991 under the [[San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program]] (SJRRIP). The program was initiated in 1992 with two goals: conserving populations of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]], and proceeding with water development in compliance with federal and state law, interstate compacts, court decrees and trust responsibilities to tribal nations. The study area runs from [[Navajo Dam]] to [[Lake Powell]], roughly river miles (RM) 0 to 224.

The report organizes findings against the seven objectives (5.1 to 5.7) of the [[Long Range Plan]] (LRP) completed in January 1995. Most objectives are still in progress, since the 7-year research effort runs until 1997. The report is the first attempt to integrate the individual annual studies, which were carried out by a multiagency group under the Biology Committee and Coordination Committee.

## Background & Description

The SJRRIP grew out of a 1987 to 1990 study by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] and [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]], which confirmed that two endangered fishes inhabit the river. That finding led to Section 7 consultations on the [[Animas-La Plata Project]] (1991) and the [[Navajo Indian Irrigation Project]] (1991). The Bureau agreed to fund a 7-year research program, and the [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]] joined in funding. The research was folded into the SJRRIP.

Governance has two layers. A Coordination Committee ensures goals are met on time. A Biology Committee directs research through annual work plans and budgets and meets up to six times a year. The report differs from routine annual reports by covering activity since 1987, and it is written so that administrators and non-biologists can follow it.

Two fish species are the focus, with [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] both federally endangered. The rest of the community includes [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Roundtail Chub]], along with non-native species such as [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]] and [[Western Mosquitofish]]. Appendix B lists about 30 non-native species and hybrids documented in the basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geomorphic reaches.** [[Bliesner and Lamarra]] (1995) used linear discriminant analysis on 21 significant variables to delineate eight geomorphic reaches. Reaches 1 (RM 0 to 16) and 8 (RM 214 to 224) were the most distinct. Other researchers used different reach breaks, so cross-study comparison is difficult.

**Channel change.** Resurveys of eight of 23 transects below Hogback Diversion found only minor bed-elevation change since Navajo Dam was completed. Channel narrowing was attributed to reduced flooding and encroachment of [[Russian Olive]] and tamarisk, which may have reduced braiding and low-velocity habitat.

**Habitat availability.** The river is run-dominated. Runs made up 72.4 to 83.9 percent of total wetted area in 1993 and 1994 mapping. Slackwater and low-velocity habitats made up only 0.2 to 1.9 percent, and eddies and pools roughly 1 to 5 percent. Backwater area rose as flows fell, and [[Flow-Habitat Relationships]] models were under development.

**Hydrology.** Water years 1987 to 1990 were low-flow years with spring peaks of about 3,000 to 4,000 cfs, except 1987 (about 10,000 cfs). Test releases from Navajo Dam in 1992 to 1994 produced peaks near 9,000 to 10,000 cfs.

**Water quality.** Selenium, copper, zinc, mercury and [[Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons]] (PAHs) were identified as the most likely contaminant problems, mostly in localized reaches and tributaries. Sediment loading from runoff events, such as Canyon Largo, can be severe.

**Fish health.** Lesions, blindness, spinal deformities, fin erosion, tumors and the parasite Lernaea were recorded. Flannelmouth and bluehead suckers had an overall abnormality incidence of 2.7 percent across the four study years. No infectious agent had been identified as the cause.

## Key Findings

**Program status by LRP objective**

- **5.1 Interim management objectives:** Deferred until the 7-year research ends in 1997.
- **5.2 Habitat:** Geomorphic reaches, habitat mapping and flow-habitat models are well advanced. Flow recommendations will come at the end of the study period.
- **5.3 Endangered fish and native community:** Native suckers dominate large fishes. Reproductive success of Colorado squawfish has been poor, with a slight increase in 1993. Razorback sucker augmentation began in 1994.
- **5.4 Non-native fish:** Non-native abundance declined from 1991 to 1994 while native abundance rose, suggesting that Program flows may favor natives. A pilot study of mechanical removal of channel catfish began in 1995.
- **5.5 Water quality and contaminants:** Historical review (Abell 1994) and biota surveys (Wilson et al. 1995) are complete. Allowable contaminant levels and corrective actions await further work.
- **5.6 Information and education:** Not yet developed; budgeted after 1997.
- **5.7 Adaptive management:** Ongoing. The Integration Report itself is part of the adaptive loop.

**Colorado squawfish**

- Adults use run habitat most (46 percent of 80 ground contacts), then eddies (18 percent) and shorelines (15 percent).
- Spawning occurred at RM 131 to 132 in the Mixer area. Adults prefer RM 119 to 148.
- Only 18 young-of-year (YOY) were collected in 1987, 1 in 1988, 1 in 1990, 1 in 1992, 11 in 1993 and 7 in 1994, all in backwaters. No larvae were found below the spawning area, so the recruitment bottleneck is unexplained.
- About 20 adults have been collected above Lake Powell since 1991.

**Razorback sucker**

- Only one wild adult has been found since 1987 (April 1988, near Bluff, Utah).
- Experimental stocking in 1994 totaled 686 subadults (15 in March, 15 in October, 656 in November). Most of the 15 March-stocked fish were verified alive by late 1994.

**Roundtail chub**

- Rare in the mainstem. Adults were caught in small numbers (2 to 4 per year, 1991 to 1994). Most juveniles were found in secondary channels near the Mancos River mouth.

**Flows**

- Spring peaks appear necessary to flush fine sediment from spawning cobble.
- Late-summer low flows increase backwater habitat for YOY squawfish.
- Preliminary recommendations: a high spring peak and lower late-summer flows.

**Non-native fish**

- Red shiner and fathead minnow dominate low-velocity habitats and are most abundant in late summer and autumn.
- Channel catfish and carp dominate main-channel catches. Catfish over 450 mm consumed fish in the summer (32 percent of stomachs held flannelmouth sucker remains).
- Centrarchids had higher piscivory (fish remains in 27 to 35 percent of stomachs).
- Native and non-native diets overlap substantially in low-velocity habitats.

**Contaminants**

- Copper had high toxic potential for both endangered species at all five tested sites. The Gallegos Canyon mixture was most toxic, with synergistic copper effects.
- Larval life stages of Colorado squawfish were the most PAH-sensitive, with acute toxicity around 4 to 15 micrograms per liter.
- Selenium levels in whole fish occasionally reached concentrations linked to reproductive failure.

**Recommendations (LRP-linked)**

1. Test the Bliesner and Lamarra geomorphic reaches and report data by river mile.
2. Build a GIS-based common database covering all physical, chemical and biological data.
3. Expand research into why squawfish reproduction is limited, including habitat, contaminants, non-natives and other factors.
4. Begin feasibility studies for physical habitat modification, such as fish passage at diversions and backwater creation.
5. Enhance lower-river adult monitoring and radio telemetry below Mexican Hat.
6. Pilot the introduction of Colorado squawfish above some diversions.
7. Begin discussions on legally protecting recommended flows.
