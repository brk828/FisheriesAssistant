# Endangered Fish Die at Dexter Hatchery: A Comedy of Errors

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

A 1992 *Rocky Mountain News* article reports that a series of accidents at the federal [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico killed most of the endangered fish captured in Colorado for a recovery program. The fish were [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Of 34 fish that survived transfer, only three razorback suckers and three Colorado squawfish were still alive by the following Monday. Officials said the losses set back a recovery effort costing about $2 million a year, but did not threaten the species as a whole. Their main concern was that the remaining wild populations are very small and may not be reproducing.

## Background & Description

The article covers three listed fishes native to the [[Colorado River]] system. Figures are as reported in the article.

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]:** Listed as endangered since 1967. Can grow to six feet and 80 pounds. Known as "Colorado salmon" to early settlers. The article gives an estimated wild population of about "10,0S" (OCR-garbled; likely 10,000 or a similar figure), which should be verified against the original.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]:** Listed as endangered since 1964. Grows to about 20 inches. Distinguished by a hump behind the head. Small population in the Colorado River on the Colorado-Utah border.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]:** Listed as endangered in Colorado since 1979. Grows to 3 feet and 13 pounds. Small population in the northern Colorado River.

Captures were made since 1988 through [[Federal and State Recovery Programs]] costing about $2 million a year. Captured fish were: 16 razorback suckers, 10 Colorado squawfish and 20 humpback chub, a total of 46.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article attributes the decline of these species mainly to habitat change. Massive dams and reservoirs along the Colorado River have "nearly eliminated many species of fish." Wild populations are small, and officials said the captured fish were needed for genetic diversity "essential to the breeding program and survival in the wild."

Many of the captured Colorado squawfish were old and possibly past breeding age. A [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] biologist said this suggested the species may not stay viable in the wild. The [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] said it is unclear whether the remaining wild fish are stable or reproducing.

The hatchery itself had a 65-year-old reputation and held 200 to 400 individuals of each species. Officials said the hatchery was a standard site for keeping captive stocks as insurance against loss in the wild.

## Key Findings

**Cause of losses (per a Fish and Wildlife Service memo):**
- Ten humpback chubs were eaten last year, either by squawfish or by migratory birds, raccoons or other animals.
- Six razorback suckers, seven squawfish and the remaining humpback chubs died in the month before the article. Causes were disease, problems with the aeration system and chlorine in the water.
- By Monday, only three razorback suckers and three Colorado squawfish remained alive.

**Triggering event:**
- An electrician repairing the heating for the hatchery's incubation system on March 23 turned off the power that circulates water through the ponds. When he finished, he turned the power back on and opened a different water valve, which sent dechlorinated water into the ponds.
- A full investigation was underway. The hatchery had an outstanding reputation.

**Official assessments:**
- [[Tom Smylie]], assistant regional director of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in New Mexico: "It didn't move the species toward extinction, but it certainly didn't push us toward recovery." He said it was a "comedy of errors" and that the species would not be driven to extinction.
- Galen Binderbaugh, Denver director of the Fish and Wildlife Service, said it set the program back by an unknown amount.
- Clee Sealing, a senior fisheries biologist with the Colorado Division of Wildlife, said the loss was a tragedy and asked, "How many can you remove before you jeopardize the wild population?"

**Conservation outlook:**
- The Fish and Wildlife Service must prevent further extinctions and revive the remaining populations.
- Officials said the loss forced them to remove more fish from the wild, which raised concerns about the wild populations themselves.
