# Species and Speciation in Phylogenetic Systematics, with Examples from the North American Fish Fauna

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #cyprinidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This review, published in the *Annals of the Missouri Botanical Garden* (1985) by E. O. Wiley and Richard L. Mayden, applies [[Phylogenetic Systematics]] to the problem of inferring the mode of [[Speciation]] from patterns of descent. The authors first examine what a species is within the Hennigian framework, contrasting heuristic and process-based concepts, and then test predictions from [[Allopatric Speciation]], [[Peripheral Isolation]], [[Allo-Parapatric Speciation]], [[Stasipatric Speciation]] and [[Sympatric Speciation]] against North American freshwater fish clades. Their conclusion is that correlations between pattern and mode can be made, but that the available phylogenetic data are too sparse to resolve most cases.

## Background & Description

The paper opens by defining [[Phylogenetic Systematics]] (Hennig 1950, 1966) as a method set that integrates classification with the evolutionary "paradigm," in which organisms have parents and diversity results from descent with modification. Two attitudes toward species are distinguished:

- **Heuristic attitude:** species are defined operationally, for example as the smallest detected samples of self-perpetuating organisms with a unique set of characters (Nelson & Platnick 1981), or with a geographic criterion and at least one apomorphy (Rosen 1979).
- **Process attitude:** species are real, individual evolutionary entities. Hennig regarded species, and all supra-individual taxa, as segments of the temporal stream of interbreeding populations with a beginning and an end in time. [[Michael Ghiselin]] and David Hull reached the same conclusion, arguing that species are individuals rather than [[Natural Kinds]].

The authors stress that "species" can refer both to particular species (such as *Pinus ponderosa*) and to the class of biological or evolutionary species. A particular species can evolve only once and is extinct forever once it goes extinct.

Speciation is treated as several distinct processes that change species number within a clade. Genealogical patterns may differ between clades because of reconstruction error, undetected extinction or speciation, dispersal, or genuinely different modes of speciation. The authors aim to identify conditions under which pattern can reveal mode, while acknowledging that most groups will not satisfy them.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Three assumptions** frame the analysis:

1. **Character evolution** reliably records speciation history. This fails if vicariance outpaces character change, leaving a polytomy. Examples include *Fundulus catenatus* (at least five disjunct populations) and *F. diaphanus*-group taxa.
2. **No extinctions** within the clade. This is violated in *Fundulus parvipinnis* and *F. lima*, which have Pliocene and Miocene fossil relatives.
3. **Dispersal has not obscured the original pattern.** Sympatry, as in the *Fundulus olivaceus* group, can mask speciation.

**Geographic setting:** The Central Gulf Coast's Mobile Bay drainage separates many sister-species pairs. The Central Highlands (Ozark and Ouachita plateaus, plus the Tennessee Highlands of the Interior Low Plateau, Ridge and Valley, and Blue Ridge) hold largely high-gradient, clear-water, rocky-bottom fauna. Before the [[Pleistocene Glaciation]], Teays and Old Ohio drainages differed substantially from modern ones.

**Lake Waccamaw** (North Carolina) is an ecologically unique, spring-fed, acidic lake with three endemic species, each with a widespread sister species.

## Key Findings

**Modes of speciation evaluated:**

- **Vicariance (Model I):** Dichotomous phylogenies and biogeographic congruence are expected. Two large-scale vicariance events are proposed for the Northern Gulf Coast, one centred on the Mobile Bay drainage and one on the Mississippi River. The Mississippi event appears older. Several clades show both patterns.
- **Central Highlands test:** The authors report that speciation within the highland fauna cannot be correlated with the Illinoian Glacier. Most analysed species were likely present before the Pleistocene. Three clade groups (the *Notropis spectrunculus*, *Etheostoma niangue*/*E. saggitta* and *Percina cymatotaenia* groups) fit the pre-Pleistocene hypothesis, while others are compatible with either hypothesis. The paper's own discussion says that glaciation may have caused the disjunction but does not explain speciation.
- **Mini-vicariance in the Ouachita Highlands:** Eight fishes (two minnows, two madtoms, four darters, one undescribed) and six crayfishes show a common vicariance event linked to the Arkansas River dissection.
- **Peripheral isolation:** Lake Waccamaw's three endemics (*Fundulus waccamensis*, *Menidia extensa*, *Etheostoma* sp.) were examined. The authors consider the peripheral-isolate hypothesis of Hubbs & Raney (1946) plausible but unproven, and note that the lake's fauna has mostly not speciated, so ecological novelty alone does not drive differentiation. The *F. catenatus*/*F. albolineatus*/*F. julisia* hypothesis is not corroborated because *F. catenatus* is more closely related to *F. stellifer*.
- **Allo-parapatric and parapatric speciation:** No North American fish examples were found. The authors suggest the restriction of freshwater fishes to their streams limits contact zones.
- **Stasipatric speciation:** No examples; chromosome banding methods are needed.
- **Sympatric speciation:** No ecological examples. Hybrid-origin unisexual forms are documented: *Poecilia formosa* (gynogenetic) and *Menidia clarkhubbsi* (all-female), along with possible *Poeciliopsis* parthenogenetic forms.
- **Reductive speciation:** No documented cases (Wiley 1981); "compilospecies" is the nearest analogue.

**Methodological recommendations:** Mitochondrial DNA cladistics on geographic samples and analysis of ancestral clines are proposed to distinguish peripheral isolation from alternative scenarios (Fig. 42 of the original paper).

**Conclusions:** Large-scale vicariance explains much of the clade evolution, but glaciation does not offer a simple causal mechanism. Peripheral-isolation claims are rare and weakly supported, and the authors conclude that the phylogenetic and biogeographic information needed to test most models is lacking.
