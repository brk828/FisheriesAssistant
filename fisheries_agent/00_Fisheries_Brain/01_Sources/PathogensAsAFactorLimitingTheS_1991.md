*Note: This source concerns a non-fish taxon (larval salamanders) inhabiting fishless montane lakes. The framework below has been adapted to reflect the actual focus of the paper: pathogen-mediated limitation of cannibalistic polyphenism in [[Tiger Salamander]] larvae.*

## Executive Summary

This study by Pfennig, Loeb, and Collins (1991) investigates why cannibalism — despite its potential fitness benefits — remains rare across taxa, using the [[Tiger Salamander]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum nebulosum*) as a model system. This subspecies exhibits a discrete larval trophic polymorphism: a "typical" morph feeding on plankton/insect larvae, and a specialized "cannibal" morph that preys on conspecifics. The authors compared cannibal frequency across two Arizona plateaus (Kaibab vs. Mogollon) separated by the Grand Canyon, and found that the Kaibab Plateau population produced far fewer cannibals — even under common-garden laboratory conditions — despite similar larval densities (the known behavioral trigger for the cannibal morph). The key explanatory variable identified was **pathogen density**: lakes with high bacterial loads and recurrent mass-mortality events had significantly fewer cannibals, and cannibal frequency correlated negatively with bacterial density across ten natural lakes. Laboratory trials confirmed that cannibals which consumed diseased conspecifics reliably died from disease, whereas typicals avoided this fate. The paper proposes pathogen acquisition as a previously underappreciated cost of cannibalism, one strong enough to have shaped the evolution and distribution of a discrete cannibalistic morphology — not just cannibalistic behavior.

## Background & Description

*(Morphological description pertains to the discrete larval polymorphism, not to fish taxonomy.)*

- **Typical morph**: Feeds predominantly on zooplankton and aquatic insect larvae; smaller body size; delayed metamorphosis relative to cannibals.
- **Cannibal morph**: A behaviorally, developmentally, and morphologically distinct phenotype:
  - Larger body size than typicals
  - Metamorphoses at a younger age
  - Distinct head shape and tooth-bearing bone (dentary/vomerine) morphology
  - Elongated teeth relative to typicals (Powers 1907; Pedersen 1991)
  - Induced environmentally by high conspecific density (Collins and Cheek 1983), not fixed genetically — a case of **adaptive developmental plasticity** / **[[Trophic Polymorphism]]**
- **Parasite load as a morphotype-linked trait**: Cannibals carried significantly heavier burdens of intestinal nematodes than typicals (mean ± SEM: cannibals = 23.5 ± 7.1, n=6; typicals = 1.6 ± 0.5, n=5; P=0.006), independent of body size (no significant correlation between nematode count and snout-vent length in either morph).
- **Disease symptomatology**: Diseased individuals (both morphs) displayed lethargy and reddening ("erythema") around the venter and ventral limb surfaces prior to death — consistent with bacterial septicemia symptoms reported in other tiger salamander die-offs (Worthylake and Hovingh 1989).
- Elevated hepatic *Clostridium* concentrations were detected in diseased vs. healthy Kaibab Plateau animals (D. Nichols, pers. comm.), suggesting a bacterial pathogenic mechanism distinct from (but possibly synergistic with) the aerobic gram-negative bacteria assayed as a general pathogen proxy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Numerous natural, fishless sinkhole lakes on the [[Kaibab Plateau]] and [[Mogollon Plateau]] (Colorado Plateau region, Arizona), separated ~5 mya by Grand Canyon incision. Lakes are small (<50 m diameter), shallow (<1 m), seasonally ephemeral (filled by snowmelt and late-summer rain), and vegetated with emergent plants.
- **Community context**: Co-occurring biota include algae, protozoans, rotifers, cladocerans, fairy shrimp (Anostraca), molluscs, aquatic insects, and anuran larvae. Notably, **no fish or other salamander species are present** — tiger salamander larvae function as the apex predator in this system (cf. [[Holomuzki and Collins 1987]] "top predator" designation).
- **Trophic role**: The typical morph occupies a generalist planktivore/insectivore niche; the cannibal morph occupies a facultative intraspecific-predator niche within the same population, activated by density-dependent cues.
- **Anthropogenic influence**: Livestock grazing (introduced to the Kaibab Plateau ~1860) is implicated as a probable vector increasing bacterial loading via fecal contamination — lakes visited by livestock (Kaibab National Forest sites) had markedly higher bacterial counts than ungrazed lakes in Grand Canyon National Park within the same plateau.
- **Disease dynamics as a niche-structuring force**: Mass mortality events (bacterial blooms, likely *Acinetobacter*-type or *Clostridium*-associated pathogens) recur seasonally (late summer, often near metamorphosis) and disproportionately affect cannibal morphs, effectively suppressing the ecological viability of the cannibalistic niche in high-pathogen lakes.

## Key Findings

- **Regional divergence in cannibal frequency**: Kaibab Plateau lakes had significantly lower cannibal frequency (mean 0.7% ± 0.7 SEM) than Mogollon Plateau lakes (mean 11.7% ± 3.3 SEM), despite no significant difference in larval density between regions.
- **Common-garden confirmation of a heritable/developmental predisposition**: Even under identical laboratory density treatments, Mogollon-origin larvae produced significantly more cannibals than Kaibab-origin larvae (Two-way ANOVA: Region F=19.06, P=0.0001; Initial density F=8.72, P=0.0001; Region×Density interaction NS, P=0.405).
- **Mass mortality strongly associated with the low-cannibal region**: 5 of 10 Kaibab Plateau lakes exhibited mass mortality in 1990 vs. 0 of 7 Mogollon Plateau lakes (χ²=4.51, P<0.05).
- **Within-region effect of disease presence**: Kaibab lakes with diseased animals had 0% cannibals vs. 10.6% ± 3.1% in disease-free Kaibab lakes (P=0.014).
- **Bacterial density negatively correlated with cannibal frequency** across ten natural lakes (Spearman r = −0.69, P=0.039), and mass-mortality lakes had significantly higher bacterial counts than non-mass-mortality lakes.
- **Experimental confirmation of differential disease risk by morph**: All 7 cannibals exposed to a diseased conspecific consumed it and subsequently died within one week; by contrast, only 1 of 15 typicals similarly exposed died (nearly all typicals refused to consume the sick conspecific). Control cannibals fed healthy conspecifics had only 1/5 mortality.
- **Elevated parasite loads in cannibals**: Confirmed via direct nematode counts in gastrointestinal tracts (see Morphological Characteristics), supporting the hypothesis that cannibalism is a route of enhanced pathogen/parasite acquisition.
- **Faster metamorphosis in the lower-cannibal, higher-disease population**: Kaibab Plateau cannibals metamorphosed significantly faster than Mogollon Plateau cannibals (86 ± 4 days vs. 123 ± 27 days, P<0.05) — consistent with selection to escape a pathogen-risky larval environment.
- **Alternative resource-abundance hypothesis rejected**: High-bacteria lakes did not show elevated macroinvertebrate (prey) abundance for typicals, arguing against the idea that bacterial correlation with low cannibalism was merely a proxy for enhanced non-cannibalistic food resources.
- **Broader theoretical implication**: Because pathogens are frequently host-specific, cannibals face elevated disease risk relative to predators consuming heterospecific prey. This cost may be a general and previously underappreciated constraint explaining the rarity of both cannibalistic behavior and specialized cannibal morphologies across the animal kingdom — connecting to broader frameworks in [[Polis 1981 Intraspecific Predation]] and [[Read 1988 Parasite-Mediated Sexual Selection]].
