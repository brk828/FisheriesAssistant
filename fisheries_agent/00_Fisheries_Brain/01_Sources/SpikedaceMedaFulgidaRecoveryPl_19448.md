# Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The spikedace (*Meda fulgida*) is a threatened minnow that has been extirpated from most of its historic range in the [[Gila River Basin]]. It now survives only in the upper Gila River in New Mexico, and in [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Eagle Creek]], and the upper [[Verde River]] in Arizona. All known populations are considered under threat.

The plan, prepared by Paul C. Marsh of Arizona State University for U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) Region 2, sets out a recovery objective of protecting existing populations, restoring populations within historic habitat, and eventually delisting the species if possible. Delisting depends on quantitative criteria for a self-sustaining population, which cannot yet be defined. Major threats include dams, water diversion, watershed deterioration, groundwater pumping, channelization, and introduced predatory and competing fishes, especially the [[Red Shiner]].

The minimum estimated recovery cost is $115,000 per year over a minimum 20-year period (1989 dollars), excluding land or water acquisition.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and listing.** Spikedace is a small, sleek member of the minnow family Cyprinidae. It is the only species in its genus. FWS proposed it as threatened in 1985 and listed it in 1986 under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. Arizona lists it as threatened, and New Mexico lists it as a Group 2 endangered species, which gives it protection under the New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act. Neither state listing otherwise protects the species or its habitat.

**Morphology.** The body is slender and slightly laterally compressed, with small embedded scales. The dorsal fin has two spinose leading rays and seven rays in total. The anal fin usually has nine rays. Pharyngeal teeth are arranged in two rows with the formula 1,4-4,1. Barbels are absent. Sides are bright silvery with vertically elongated black specks, and the back is olive-gray to brownish. Breeding males become golden or brassy, especially on the head and fin bases.

**Historical distribution.** Spikedace was once widespread in moderate-sized, intermediate-elevation streams of the Gila system upstream of Phoenix, including the Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers, the Agua Fria, the San Pedro, and the San Francisco. It was locally extirpated from much of the Salt River by 1937. Transplants to Sonoita Creek (1968) and 7-Springs Wash (1970) were later extirpated.

**Present distribution.** In Arizona, the species occurs in three streams, each with at least moderate-sized populations in relatively undisturbed reaches. In New Mexico, it is restricted to the mainstem Gila River and its East, Middle, and West forks. The Eagle Creek population, first considered "quite small," has since been found to be more substantial. Undiscovered populations may exist on San Carlos and Fort Apache reservations, on Forest Service lands, and in Sonora.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Spikedace occupies flowing water usually less than one meter deep. Adults aggregate in shear zones where fast water meets slow water, including gravel-sand bars, eddies below riffles, and shallow areas above bars. Juveniles favor quiet pool margins over soft, fine-grained bottoms. Habitat use shifts seasonally and with life stage.

**Reproduction.** Breeding occurs in spring (April to June) and is triggered by a combination of discharge and water temperature. Males patrol shallow, sandy-gravelly riffles, and up to six males may approach a receptive female. The eggs are adhesive and demersal. Fecundity ranges from 90 to 250 ova and correlates with length and age. Ova diameter is near 1.5 mm. Incubation time and hatch size are unknown.

**Growth and lifespan.** Young fish reach 35 to 40 mm standard length (SL) by November and average about 40 mm SL at one year and 50 to 63 mm SL at two years. Maximum size is about 65 mm SL in Aravaipa Creek and 68 mm SL in the upper Gila. Most fish live one to two years, a few reach age three, and exceptional individuals reach four.

**Diet.** Spikedace are carnivores that feed mainly on insects drifting in the stream. Important prey include larval mayflies, hydropsychid caddisflies, and chironomid midges, typically 2 to 5 mm long. Post-larvae eat a variety of small soft-bodied animals, while adults specialize on drifting nymphal and adult mayflies.

**Co-occurring fishes.** Native associates include loach minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*), speckled dace, Sonora sucker, desert sucker, and longfin dace. Non-native fishes include [[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], flathead catfish, [[Largemouth Bass]], smallmouth bass, green sunfish, and introduced trout at higher elevations.

**Competition with red shiner.** The red shiner now occurs wherever spikedace was formerly found, except the San Francisco River above Frisco Hot Springs. Three hypotheses have been proposed: niche invasion, invasion of vacant niches left by native declines, and displacement of spikedace through competition or predation. Studies in the upper Gila supported the vacant-niche hypothesis, while the upper Verde shows apparent stable coexistence. A study by Marsh et al. (1989) documented displacement based on habitat shifts in the presence of red shiner.

**Causes of decline.** Habitat destruction and non-native fishes have acted separately and together. Dewatering, impoundment, altered flow regimes, channelization, and bank stabilization have eliminated populations. Grazing, mining, agriculture, and timber harvest add sedimentation and contaminants. A wastewater spill at the Cananea Mine, Sonora, killed all fishes along a 100-km reach downstream (Eberhardt 1981). The relative importance of habitat change and introduced fishes has not been established, so both must be managed.

## Key Findings

**Recovery objective and criteria.** The plan aims to protect existing populations, restore depleted and extirpated populations, and ensure self-sustaining populations in perpetuity. Quantitative delisting criteria cannot be set until more information is gathered. Recovery cannot occur in less than 20 years, since reintroduced populations are evaluated over a 10-year period.

**Critical habitat.** The 1985 proposal for [[Critical Habitat]] was deferred to 18 June 1987 and has not been finalized. The proposal provides only limited protection. The plan recommends:
- Aravaipa Creek, perennial reach of about 24 km (proposed).
- Verde River, about 57 km (proposed), plus a 1.5 km segment of Sycamore Creek, which is not expected to be included in the final rule.
- Gila River, three sections totaling about 73 km (proposed).
- A roughly 38 km reach of Eagle Creek, from the Phelps Dodge diversion dam upstream to Sheep Wash, for addition to critical habitat.

**Priority actions (9 categories, 1 to 9).**
1. Protect existing populations, including Section 7 and Section 9 compliance, instream flow protection, and barrier construction against non-native fishes.
2. Monitor populations and community composition through standard sites, methods, and a computerized database, with bi-annual spring and autumn estimates.
3. Identify the nature of interactions with non-native fishes, including field experiments.
4. Quantify habitat needs for substrate, velocity and depth, temperature, water chemistry, and watershed size and flood regime.
5. Enhance habitat for depleted populations.
6. Reintroduce populations into historic range. The San Pedro River system is identified as the most suitable candidate, with Aravaipa Creek as a donor source.
7. Define criteria for a self-sustaining population, including minimum stock size.
8. Develop captive propagation and hatchery programs as a contingency, since the species currently persists in substantial numbers.
9. Provide information and education to the public and professionals.

**Reintroduction protocol.** Success is evaluated five years after reintroduction, with an additional five years of monitoring for questionable populations. Failed populations may be restocked or abandoned after assessment.

**Implementation schedule.** Tasks are ranked by priority: Priority 1 tasks prevent extinction, Priority 2 tasks maintain current status, and Priority 3 tasks provide full recovery. Estimated costs include $100,000 for early protection and designation tasks, $7,000 per year for reintroduction monitoring, $10,000 per year for hatchery holding, and $1,500 per year for supplying hatchery fish. Land and water acquisition costs are excluded because they cannot be estimated until sites are identified.

**Plan status.** The plan is subject to revision as new findings emerge. Appendix B (public comments on this plan and the loach minnow plan) is distributed separately.
