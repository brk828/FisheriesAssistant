# Preliminary Fisheries Survey of Aquatic Habitats - Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Ictaluridae #Centrarchidae #Poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This memorandum documents a preliminary fisheries survey of stock-watering tanks at [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] in southern Arizona, conducted in December 1985 and April 1986. The survey assessed 45 tanks to evaluate their potential as supplemental growout habitats for endangered fish species recovery efforts. Five nonnative fish species were documented: [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*), [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), and [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). The refuge is located in the headwaters of the north-flowing [[Altar Valley]], part of the [[Santa Cruz River]] system ([[Gila River]] basin), and historically lacked native fishes. The survey also documented abundant amphibian populations including [[Tiger Salamander]]s (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) and ranid frogs.

## Background & Description

[[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] is located just east of the [[Baboquivari Mountains]] in southern Arizona. The refuge comprises largely the headwaters of the north-flowing [[Altar Valley]], part of the [[Santa Cruz River]] system within the [[Gila River]] basin. Southern portions of the refuge drain southward into Mexico, presumably into the [[Rio Concepcion]] watershed.

Native fishes were historically absent from the refuge, with even the perennial [[Arivaca Creek]] being fishless. However, nonnative fishes have been widely introduced in stock-watering tanks throughout Arizona and are known from the area.

Two survey efforts were conducted:
- December 1985: Lief Ahim and Ben Robertson ([[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]) surveyed eight tanks using seine and gillnet
- April 19-20, 1986: W. L. Minckley, J. E. Johnson, Paul C. Marsh, Bruce D. DeMarias, and [[Arizona State University]] ichthyology students visited 45 tanks (only three overlapping with the December survey)

Sampling methodology employed 6.4-mm-mesh seines of various lengths. No detailed habitat condition assessments were recorded during this preliminary survey.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The aquatic habitats surveyed consisted entirely of artificial stock-watering tanks of varying sizes and permanency. Most ponds exhibited phytoplankton blooms and were evidently rich in nutrients and highly productive.

**Aquatic Invertebrate Community**: Abundant and diversified invertebrate fauna was present, including principally insects and molluscs.

**Amphibian Populations**: [[Tiger Salamander]]s (*Ambystoma tigrinum*, cf. *mavortium*) were frequently encountered, representing an almost certainly introduced population. Specimens collected included metamorphosed adults, branchiate (aquatic) adults, and juveniles. The presence of branchiate adults indicates sufficient permanency for animals to overwinter for at least a year. Immature larvae and ova with developing embryos were noted, indicating reproduction occurred in January or February based on larval size.

Ranid frog tadpoles were observed, mostly appearing to be introduced [[Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*), though native spotted frogs of the *R. pipiens* group were also observed.

**Parasitological Note**: "Swimmer's itch" produced by penetration of skin by metacercaria of a bird-specific schistosome was contracted by some personnel. The fluke is not a parasite of humans but can remain viable under the skin for several days, causing substantial discomfort.

**Potential Amphibian Diversity**: The memorandum notes that numerous arid-land amphibians should be present in the area, some occurring in the United States or Arizona only in the vicinity of Buenos Aires NWR, including: narrow-mouthed toad (*Gastrophryne olivacea*), [[Sonoran Green Toad]] (*Bufo retiformis*), [[Colorado River Toad]] (*B. alvarius*), red-spotted toad (*B. punctatus*), Plains toad (*Bufo cognatus*), possibly Woodhouse's toad (*B. woodhousei*), western and Couch's spadefoot toads (*Scaphiophus hammondi*, *S. couchi*), Canyon tree frog (*Hyla arenicolor*), burrowing tree frog (*Pternohyla fodiens*), western barking frog (*Eleutherodactylus augusti*), and perhaps [[Tarahumara Frog]] (*Rana tarahumara*).

The [[Yellow Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon flavescens*) may also be represented by an isolated population in the area.

## Key Findings

**Fish Species Documented** (all nonnative):
1. [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*)
2. [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*)
3. [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*)
4. [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)
5. [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*)

All specimens are housed in the Collection of Fishes, [[Arizona State University]].

**Management Recommendations**:

*Nonnative Species*: None of the documented fish species is desirable on the refuge except [[Mosquitofish]], which serves a local function of mosquito control. This role can be duplicated by native and endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*) and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) in smaller tanks.

*Endangered Species Recovery*: Larger habitats appear suitable for rearing [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and/or [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). Stocking these species in the upper [[Santa Cruz]] drainage is justified despite being technically outside their native ranges because:
- They are within the [[Gila River]] drainage from which they were widely taken historically
- Production toward recovery is needed
- Additional refugium space and research animals are required
- Drainage relations preclude escape except into long reaches of dry stream bed

*Drainage Considerations*: No tank in the south-flowing drainage should be stocked with fishes indigenous to the [[Colorado River]] basin. However, depending on yet-to-be-confirmed drainage relations, southern habitats might be used for production and growout of other species (e.g., [[Sonoran Chub]], *Gila ditaenia*). The precise divide between north- and south-flowing terrain has not been accurately determined and should be established prior to fish introductions in southern parts of the refuge.

*Amphibian Management*: A viable management plan for enhancement and maintenance of semiaquatic amphibian species may be to maintain a percentage of smaller tanks free of potential competitors and predators such as fishes. Most arid-land amphibians breed in semipermanent pools filled by infrequent rains (exception: [[Tarahumara Frog]] requires permanent water).

**Survey Coverage**: A total of 45 tanks were visited in April 1986, with detailed locality data provided in Table 1 (Sections 15-34 across Townships 19S-21S, Ranges 8E-9E). Many tanks were dry at the time of survey. Tanks noted as "good tank" in field notes included Pavonal Tank, Mosca Tank, Cordova Tank, McKay Tank W and E, Rock Tank, and Aguirre Tank W.

**Additional Survey Needs**: Substantial ground survey will be required to detail available aquatic habitats and access, as numerous roads and smaller tanks were encountered that are not indicated on available maps. Additional work is needed to estimate sizes, permanency, and overall desirability of specific habitats for endangered species management projects.
