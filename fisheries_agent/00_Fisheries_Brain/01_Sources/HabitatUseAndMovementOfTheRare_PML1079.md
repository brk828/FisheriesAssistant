# Habitat Use and Movement of Rare Fishes in the Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1977 field study, conducted by [[Paul B. Holden]] of BIO/WEST, Inc. and funded by the Office of Biological Services (Western Energy and Land Use Team, Fort Collins, Colorado), examined habitat use and movements of four rare fishes in the Green River, Utah: the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]], and the rare [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Young rare fishes preferred backwaters with no current, silt substrate and depths over one foot, while larger individuals used deeper eddies and runs. Young-of-the-year razorback suckers were tentatively identified, representing what the authors believed to be the first wild young razorbacks recorded from the upper Colorado Basin. Exotic species were more common than native species in samples containing young rare fishes. The movement study of young squawfish was inconclusive.

## Background & Description

The upper Colorado River Basin contains two fishes listed as endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service: the Colorado Squawfish and the Humpback Chub. The Bonytail Chub and Razorback Sucker were rare and under consideration for endangered and threatened status, respectively. The principal research need was to determine the requirements for subsistence of these species, so that natural resource development in the upper basin would account for rare fish populations and their habitat.

Two factors were identified as most important for self-sustaining populations: (1) adequate spawning and rearing habitat, and (2) interaction with exotic fishes. The lower and middle Green River in Utah was described as the only reproducing area for Colorado Squawfish in the upper basin, while adults elsewhere lacked recruitment (Kidd, 1977).

Study objectives were to:
1. Determine the physical and chemical parameters of habitats used by all sizes of rare fishes, with emphasis on young fishes.
2. Determine the movements of rare fishes, especially young-of-the-year.
3. Determine the other fish species associated with rare species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and sampling.** The study covered 175 miles of the Green River from Jensen to Green River, Utah. The Jensen to Sand Wash reach was sampled August 5–20, 1977; the Sand Wash to Green River reach (Desolation and Gray Canyons) was sampled September 7–14 and 22–26, 1977. Habitats were classed as backwaters, eddies, runs and riffles. Velocity (to 0.1 fps), maximum depth, temperature and substrate (silt, sand, gravel, cobble, or combinations; silt subdivided into soft and firm) were recorded at each site. Methods included seining (used most often), electrofishing and a fry shovel. Rare fishes over about 100 mm TL were tagged with numbered fingerling tags, and juvenile squawfish under 100 mm were marked by clipping the anal fin. A reference collection was preserved in formaldehyde and transferred to isopropyl alcohol.

**Community context.** Holden and Stalnaker (1975) and other earlier surveys were cited to compare distribution trends. Recent evidence indicated little change in rare fish status, although squawfish apparently spawned only in the extreme lower portion of the study area in 1977.

## Key Findings

**Fish distribution and abundance**
- Over 330 individual samples were taken: 125 in runs, 121 in backwaters, 59 in eddies and 19 in riffles, roughly proportional to habitat availability.
- Nineteen species were collected, totaling over 19,500 specimens.
- The introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) was by far the most abundant fish; the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) was the most abundant native species.
- Only two Bonytail Chub were caught, making it the rarest native fish collected.
- Young-of-the-year squawfish (35–64 mm) and young-of-the-year razorback suckers (lateral line scales 80–88; six specimens, middle study area) were recorded. Only two adult razorbacks were seen, both in the middle area.
- Chub specimens from Desolation and Gray canyons showed morphology suggestive of Humpback Chub, with one adult and one juvenile possibly Bonytail Chub.

**Preferred habitat of rare fishes**
- Young-of-the-year squawfish were found only in backwaters with no current, firm silt bottoms and depths of 1–2 feet.
- Juvenile squawfish preferred similar backwaters but were less selective of depth.
- Subadult and adult squawfish (200+ mm) used a range of velocities and depths, preferring sandy-bottomed eddies and runs over backwaters.
- Young-of-the-year razorbacks preferred eddies with about 0.2 fps velocity, 0.1 ft depth and soft silt.
- Young-of-the-year humpback chubs preferred backwaters with no current, firm silt and about two feet depth, but also used eddies and runs at 0.4–0.8 fps. Adults were mainly in sand-bottomed eddies and runs.
- No size class of humpback chub selected a particular temperature.

**Movement of young-of-the-year squawfish**
- In a single backwater sampled five times over two and a half days, 4, 31, 29, 11 and 12 young squawfish were caught.
- Marked fish were not recovered elsewhere despite extensive searching, and only three recaptures occurred.
- The authors concluded the data were inconclusive but suggested little movement between the backwater and the main channel, with movement within the backwater the most likely explanation.

**Tagging**
- One adult and one juvenile bonytail chub were tagged, with no recaptures. Fourteen adult and nine juvenile chubs from Desolation and Gray canyons were also tagged, with none recaptured.

**Associated fishes and competition**
- Young exotic species were found more often than natives in samples with rare species, and red shiners co-occurred with rare fishes more than any other species.
- Young-of-the-year squawfish were most often associated with red shiners, fathead minnows, juvenile carp and young channel catfish.
- Young humpback chubs were most often associated with red shiners, fathead minnows and young channel catfish.
- The authors judged that competition and predation were possible but their impact on rare fish populations was unknown.

**Population status**
- Comparison with earlier surveys (Seethaler et al., 1976; Vanicek et al., 1970; Holden and Stalnaker, 1975; Kidd, 1977) suggested no recent decline; rare fishes appeared to be maintaining populations in the study area.
- The authors noted that the young razorback specimens represent a significant record, pending confirmatory taxonomic analysis.

**Threats and management implications**
- The [[Smallmouth Bass]] was newly established in the Green River and could prey on rare fishes, though turbidity in spring and early summer and the river's rigorous conditions were expected to limit its reproduction.
