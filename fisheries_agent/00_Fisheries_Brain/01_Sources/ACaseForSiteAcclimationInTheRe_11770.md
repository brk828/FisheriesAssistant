# Site Acclimation in Razorback Sucker Reintroduction

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Open-File Report (99-110), prepared with [[Northern Arizona University]], tests whether holding hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] at the release site for 2 to 3 days before release reduces short-term dispersal. Telemetry trials ran in [[Lake Powell]] and the [[Green River]] in Utah, with 30 fish per trial. Acclimated fish moved less and returned toward release sites more often than nonacclimated fish. The authors argue that current stocking protocols cause unnecessary stress and that repatriation should shift from production quotas toward survival, using site acclimation and environmental conditioning. The report is preliminary and has not undergone standard USGS editorial review.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It has declined in range and numbers and now survives in small, relic populations of old adults. It was federally listed as endangered in 1990 (USFWS 1990). Over 15 million hatchery-reared suckers were stocked in the lower Colorado River basin during the 1980s, with little reported success. Survival has been attributed to predation and [[Downstream Drift]]. Stocking fish larger than 30 cm improved survival, but downstream dispersal remained a problem.

Several researchers had recommended [[Site Acclimation]] and physical conditioning, but these had not been tested. The authors note that stocking protocols for recreational and endangered fishes have changed little for decades. Fish are usually released once they have adjusted to local water temperature, often without time to recover from handling, transport, and [[Stocking Stress]].

The species has a cartilaginous dorsal keel suited to external attachment of transmitters. This allowed transmitters to be attached without abdominal surgery, which reduces stress (Mellas and Haynes 1985).

**Study Design**

- **Source fish:** Sixty subadult razorback suckers from the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]], Vernal, Utah (30 per trial).
- **Lake Powell trial:** Fish transported to Castle Creek on 3 June 1997 and held in a cove (10 m by 20 m) near Castle Creek. Tracked with sonic transmitters (70 kHz).
- **Green River trial:** Fish hauled to Millard Bottom (RK-54) within [[Canyonlands National Park]] on 16 June 1998. Held in the flooded portion (10 m by 500 m) of Millard Canyon (RM-33.5). Tracked with radio transmitters (40 MHz).
- **Transmitters:** 8 mm in diameter, 3 to 5 cm long, 4 to 7 g, nominal life 60 to 90 days, attached with two shallow (6 to 10 mm) sutures.
- **Treatments:** Fish were split into a nonacclimated group with immediate access to the water and an acclimated group held behind a barrier net. Reservoir fish were held 72 hours. Riverine fish were held 48 to 72 hours.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dispersal behavior**

- Lake Powell suckers showed a "fright and flight" response after release and swam in open water at all hours. This behavior is abnormal and increases predation risk. After 4 to 5 days, fish became more secretive and nocturnal and used shoreline vegetative or rocky cover.
- In the riverine trial, most nonacclimated fish drifted downstream to the inflow area of Lake Powell. The authors attribute this sustained drift to chronic fatigue.
- Acclimated riverine fish slowed significantly after the second week.

**Physiological stress**

The report draws on the literature to argue that handling and transport stress alter cortisol and catecholamine levels, mobilize fat stores, reduce lymphocytes, impair osmoregulation, and cause egg resorption. Chronic stress can lead to exhaustion and death. Stress may take at least 2 weeks for blood chemistry to normalize. Crowding can produce submissive behavior and reduced access to preferred habitat.

**Conditioning to flow**

Earlier flow-conditioning tests often lasted only minutes or hours. Exposure of pond-reared fish to moderate velocities (0.1 m/s) for less than 2 days lowered muscle glycogen and caused fatigue. Experiments lasting longer than 2 weeks were more successful, with benefits similar to mammalian exercise training, including higher growth rates and improved circulation (Davison 1997).

**Comparisons with other taxa**

- **Fish:** Japanese work on ayu (*Plecoglossus altivelis*) and red sea bream (*Pagrus major*) linked behavioral indicators such as schooling, jumping, and defensive tilting to survival after release.
- **Wild versus captive:** Translocated wild animals show higher success (75% versus 38%) than captive-bred animals (Conant 1988, Griffith et al. 1989). Wild-captured suckers showed virtually no downstream movement, while hatchery fish drifted for nearly a month.
- **Mammals and birds:** Acclimated [[Black-Footed Ferret]]s stayed closer to release sites. Neutered captive-bred ferrets had 81% mortality versus 20% for wild-captured animals, even with site acclimation. Enlarged acclimation enclosures improved hunting and survival. Site acclimation for more than 60 days reduced dispersal and improved pack integrity in [[Gray Wolf]] reintroductions.

## Key Findings

**1. Site acclimation reduced dispersal distance and rate.**

- Average distance traveled was similar between acclimated and nonacclimated trials but far greater for riverine fish (68.3 km) than reservoir fish (11.6 km).
- Dispersal rate declined significantly with time for all fish (P=0.001).
- Four weeks after release, reservoir dispersal averaged 1.1 km (acclimated) versus 3.7 km (nonacclimated). Riverine dispersal averaged 55 km versus 81 km. The table reports these values at Day 58.
- Lake Powell acclimated fish declined from 3.1 to 2.6 to 1.1 km over Days 14, 30, and 58. Nonacclimated fish increased from 2.4 to 3.1 to 3.7 km.
- Four of the nine detected acclimated Lake Powell fish returned toward the release site.

**2. Riverine results showed a significant directional difference.**

- After the second week, acclimated riverine dispersal rate fell to 0.3 km/d versus 3.5 km/d for nonacclimated fish.
- Acclimated suckers reduced downstream movement significantly sooner (P=0.042, ANOVA).
- Of the 11 suckers detected downstream in [[Cataract Canyon]] or Lake Powell, only 2 (18%) were acclimated.
- Six fish (four acclimated, two nonacclimated) swam upstream after reaching the Green–Colorado confluence (53.8 km).
- No significant difference was found in overall distance (P=0.737) or speed (P=0.120).

**3. No suckers reached the San Juan River.** Maximum dispersal in Lake Powell was 12.4 km for nonacclimated fish, reached by Day 24. Acclimated fish reached 7.9 km. The difference was not statistically significant (t-test).

**4. Wild-captured fish dispersed less than hatchery fish.** A comparison of previous telemetry studies found a significant difference between wild-captured and hatchery-reared suckers (P=0.001). The authors caution that the studies differed in observations, locations, and monitoring frequency, and that age differences may have contributed.

**5. Current stocking methods are considered inadequate.** The authors state that two decades of poor stocking survival, the success of acclimation in terrestrial programs, and the "production quota" mentality justify a reevaluation of razorback sucker stocking.

**Recommendations**

- **Site acclimation:** Acclimate razorback suckers on site for a minimum of 1 week before release, and longer if space allows. The authors suggest testing a longer period, especially for larger numbers of fish.
- **Holding habitat:** Use calm water such as natural backwaters, seasonally isolated flood plain ponds, and reservoir inflow areas. Hold fish behind net barriers or in on-site facilities that can be opened discreetly. Avoid net cages.
- **Environmental conditioning:** Condition fish destined for stream habitat with limited backwaters to low and moderate velocities (<0.1 m/s) for at least 2 weeks. Methods include screened flow channels, hatchery raceways, large circular tanks, and irrigation canals.
- **Predation reduction:** The [[Upper Basin Recovery Program]] is treating a portion of an estimated 350 flood plain ponds to remove unwanted fishes. Reconnecting ponds to the river less often (every 2 to 5 years) may allow natural recruitment of razorback sucker and bonytail.
- **Recapture:** Some reservoir-stocked fish move upstream (in some cases more than 100 km) into tributaries. Recapturing and translocating these fish could help recovery in critical upstream habitats.
- **Quality over quantity:** The authors argue that releasing 1,000 well-conditioned, acclimated suckers may yield more survival than stocking 100,000 highly stressed, naive fish.

**Limitations noted by the authors**

- Sample sizes were small (30 fish per trial).
- Results are short-term (about 2 months).
- The Canyonlands study had fewer observations than Lake Powell because of remoteness.
- The Lake Powell detection counts in the table differ from those in the text, and the text reports "four weeks" where the table reports Day 58.
