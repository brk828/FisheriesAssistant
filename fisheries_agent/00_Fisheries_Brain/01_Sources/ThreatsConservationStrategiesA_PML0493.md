# Threats, Conservation Strategies, and Prognosis for Suckers (Catostomidae) in North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This review (Cooke et al., *Biological Conservation* 121, 317–331, 2005) uses regional [[Case Studies]] to examine threats, conservation strategies and prognosis for suckers, a diverse freshwater family of at least 76 species, most of them North American. Suckers are a model "non-game" family: they are rarely managed, rarely funded before listing, and often misidentified or lumped as "trash fish." The authors argue that suckers face multiple interacting stressors rather than single isolated factors, and that the dominant threats are agricultural and urban habitat loss and degradation, loss of connectivity (barriers), and introduced exotic species. Recommended responses include freshwater protected areas, barrier removal and fish-passage design, habitat restoration, hydrologic (flow regime) protection, sucker-specific taxonomic monitoring, and outreach to build a constituency for non-game fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Family scope:** Catostomidae comprise at least 76 species, of which more than 70 occur in North America; one species is endemic to China and one extends to Siberia (Smith, 1992). About 75 species are freshwater, with few tolerating low-salinity estuaries.
- **Ecological diversity:** Suckers occupy large warmwater rivers, coldwater streams, wetlands and lakes. Body forms range from deep-bodied lake and sluggish-river forms with terminal mouths, through small lower-lip-grazing stream forms, to sub-terminal-mouthed benthic feeders. Life histories span short-lived, functionally semelparous species to large, long-lived iteroparous ones.
- **Reproduction:** Spawning is typified by spring migration to spawning grounds, followed by quivering behavior in which fish are pressed together; sucker species are often benthic feeders on plant and invertebrate material.
- **Taxonomy and status:** Many species are regionally threatened, and an increasing number are federally listed under the Canadian Species at Risk Act and the US Endangered Species Act (ESA).
- **Regulatory context:** Non-game fishes lack the management attention given to salmonids, centrarchids, percids, esocids, ictalurids and acipenserids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Suckers are exposed to a set of recurring stressors across regions:

- **Barriers:** Natural (beaver dams, log jams) and human barriers (low-head dams, weirs) disrupt bedload, hydrology and migration.
- **Flow alteration:** Dams, irrigation withdrawals and dewatering change flow regimes and wipe out spawning and nursery habitat.
- **Water quality:** Selenium from irrigation drainage, silt loading, nutrient enrichment, eutrophication and algal blooms degrade habitat.
- **Exotic species and hybridization:** Predation and competition with introduced species, and inter-specific hybridization, add pressure.
- **Exploitation:** Generally a minor threat, though locally significant (commercial, sport, bow/spear and subsistence harvest).

Pressures are not uniform. A threat matrix in the paper scores relative threat levels for Midwestern river, Pacific Northwest stream, Southeastern river, Colorado Basin and Western lake systems, and notes that some threats are not applicable in certain regions.

## Key Findings

### Case Studies by Region

1. **River suckers of the Midwest (Grand River, southern Ontario):**
   - Six catostomid species were recorded in the middle Grand River, including the black redhorse, the only species federally protected in Canada.
   - [[Bunt and Cooke 2001]] found redhorses were the largest constituent of the fish community by biomass.
   - Fishway studies at the [[Mannheim Weir]] (Denil fishways) showed consistent annual migration patterns for catostomids, excluding black redhorse. Male white sucker and northern hog sucker began passage around 8 °C, using low-then-high-velocity fishways as temperatures approached 12 °C.
   - Greater redhorse congregated below the weir, but only five fish passed in a given year, revealing inter-species differences in attraction and passage efficiency.
   - White suckers using the high-velocity Denil fishway were significantly smaller than those using the low-velocity one.
   - Anchor and frazil ice can block winter movement.

2. **Stream suckers of the Pacific Northwest (Salish sucker):**
   - The Salish sucker is an evolutionarily significant unit isolated in glacial refugia. It occurs in few watersheds in the Puget Lowlands, Olympic Peninsula and Fraser Valley.
   - Primary pressures include channelization, land use change and water quality degradation. Recovery focuses on protecting existing populations, restoring adjacent reaches and landowner education, since the species mostly occupies private land.
   - Life-history traits (small size, short lifespan, early maturity, reduced fecundity) make it vulnerable to disturbance, yet also allow faster recovery than larger species.

3. **River suckers of the Southeast:**
   - The region is the most speciose for suckers (17 species in the Southeast, including many redhorses, per the paper).
   - Dammed rivers and reduced flows have reduced fluvial specialists. Operational rules for a hydropower dam on the Oconee River were renegotiated to protect robust redhorse spawning flows.
   - Recovery of the robust redhorse, rediscovered in the Oconee, depends on multi-agency partnerships, husbandry methods producing genetically diverse broodstock, and refugial populations.

4. **Suckers of the Colorado Basin:**
   - [[Razorback Sucker]] was historically common but rare after the 1940s. Only 10 fish were found in the upper Colorado mainstem between 1989 and 1996. The remaining middle Green River population was estimated at roughly 1,000 individuals in 1988.
   - Selenium contamination from irrigation drainage, documented from the 1980s onward, is implicated in reproductive failure. Adults accumulate selenium, and some fish chose selenium-rich habitats.
   - Flow renegotiation was undertaken for robust redhorse spawning in the Colorado system; a multi-agency recovery program is in place for four endangered fishes in the upper basin.

5. **Lake suckers of the West (Klamath and Truckee systems):**
   - Shortnose and Lost River suckers, Cui-ui and June sucker (all endangered) are affected by water diversion, dewatering, exotic species and eutrophication.
   - Access to as much as 90% of shortnose and Lost River sucker historic spawning habitat is blocked by the Sprague River dam.
   - Upper Klamath Lake pH can exceed 10.0 and dissolved oxygen can fall below 2.0 mg/L during summer blooms.
   - Recovery mixes technological fixes (fishways, Marble Bluff dam), habitat restoration and water management changes. Scientific consensus on how much water lakesuckers need is lacking.
   - Some once-numerous populations, in the millions, are now in the hundreds or thousands.

### Summary of Threats

- The most common threats across regions are agricultural and urban development with associated habitat loss, loss of connectivity, degraded water quality, and exotic species introductions.
- Locally important threats include saline water from diversion projects, hybridization and exploitation.
- Exploitation is not widespread, but the Lost River sucker was commercially harvested for oil rendering and canned food, and razorback suckers were a subsistence food source.

### Issues Retarding Sucker Conservation

- **Taxonomic lumping:** Monitoring and harvest data often treat catostomids as a single composite group, and identification keys are incomplete, especially for early life stages.
- **Natural history gaps:** Basic information on habitat needs, movement, tolerances and ecological roles is lacking for most species.
- **Misperception:** Suckers are often viewed as "trash fish," tolerant of poor habitat. They have been killed by chemical and mechanical removal to benefit game fish, though Holey et al. (1979) found no clear evidence of negative effects on prey populations or of benefits from removal.
- **Funding:** Dedicated funding typically appears only after listing. For Klamath basin endangered suckers, more than 500 studies were completed after listing, an order of magnitude more than before.
- **Bait use:** White sucker is the third most popular bait species in North America. Bait harvest may depress small isolated populations, and bait-related fish movements may homogenize fish faunas.

### Approaches to Conservation

- **Protected areas:** Freshwater protected areas (FPAs) that protect habitats and natural flow regimes are recommended, though the authors know of no published FPA study focused on suckers.
- **Barrier removal and fishways:** Barrier removal and fish passage devices benefit many suckers; fishways should be designed for species-specific passage.
- **Flow regimes:** Hydropower operations should incorporate flow variability to protect aquatic biota, though these may conflict with power demand.
- **Habitat restoration and remediation:** Effective when targeted at specified shortcomings and matched in spatial scale to the species' ecology (e.g., Salish sucker hotspots of tens to hundreds of meters versus greater redhorse over larger distances).
- **Genetics and captive breeding:** Genetic tools are used to clarify taxonomy, delineate stocks and improve captive breeding (e.g., robust redhorse).
- **Outreach and education:** Considered perhaps the best approach. Few constituent groups lobby for non-game fishes compared with groups focused on game fish. The authors also advocate conservation of system function.

### Conclusion and Prognosis

- Suckers face multiple stressors and interacting threats. Their wide distribution, life-history diversity and taxonomic complexity pose challenges to conservation.
- Without a constituent group lobbying for non-game fishes, catostomids and similar species face continued risk from "ignorance, misunderstanding, and neglect."
- The authors predict that freshwater fish biodiversity in North America, and more so in developing regions such as Asia and Africa, may be quietly lost over the next century if conservation emphasis stays on economically valuable fishes.
- Holistic, multi-species approaches (such as adaptive management) are favored over single-species recovery plans, particularly in the United States under the ESA.
