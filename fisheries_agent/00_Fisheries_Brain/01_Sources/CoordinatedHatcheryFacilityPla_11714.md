# Coordinated Hatchery Facility Plan for Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This plan, prepared by [[Richard S Wydoski]] for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP) in the Upper Colorado River Basin, is a reference guide for the captive propagation of the four [[Endangered Colorado River Fishes]]: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. It covers genetics management, stocking guidelines, broodstock sizing, space and water requirements, existing and proposed hatchery facilities, costs, and the disposition of surplus captive-reared fish.

The central conclusion is that recruitment of wild stocks is the main limiting factor, and that captive propagation should be an interim tool rather than a substitute for habitat recovery. Existing facilities provide about 6 acres of ponds in 1994 (about 7.2 acres in 1995), against an identified need of 27.3 acres, a deficit of 20.1 acres. Proposed augmentation and restoration stocking would add about 43.25 acres. The plan recommends developing genetic refuges first, expanding the Ouray Endangered Fish Facility, and evaluating state-proposed native fish facilities.

## Background & Description

The four large-river fishes are endemic to the Upper Colorado River Basin and were listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, as amended. The razorback sucker was listed in 1991. Recovery plans exist for the bonytail, humpback chub, and Colorado squawfish. The RIP began on October 1, 1988, with a 15-year recovery goal. Its five elements are in-stream flows, habitat development, native fish stocking, non-native species management, and research and monitoring.

The fishes are long-lived. Razorback suckers aged 24 to 44 years were found in Lake Mohave. Recruitment is episodic and tied to streamflow. Propagation objectives are refugia, research and development, information and education, and augmentation or restoration stocking. The plan was drafted as a revision of an April 15, 1993 document and was finalized May 25, 1994. Consensus on some points was not reached, and the author states the plan reflects majority Biology Committee opinion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Recruitment bottleneck:** Early life-stage survival is limited by predation and competition from non-native fishes, loss of nursery habitat, and altered flow regimes. Dams and diversions block migration.
- **Flooded bottomlands:** These off-channel habitats are considered likely primary nursery areas. They have higher zooplankton and benthic productivity and warmer water than the main channel or backwaters. Year-class strength is correlated with flooded area in wet years.
- **Drift:** Larvae of all four species are thought to drift passively downstream after swimup, moving toward low-velocity habitats such as eddies, backwaters, and off-channel areas.
- **Stock status:** Razorback suckers are declining, with the upper Colorado River catch falling from 206 fish in 1975 to none in 1989–1992. The Green River estimate fell from 948 fish (1980–1988) to 543 fish (1991). Bonytails are nearly extirpated from the upper basin. Humpback chub stocks are small but stable in isolated canyons, with catastrophic risk at Black Rocks and Westwater canyons near a railroad. Colorado squawfish are stable in most reaches but very low in the upper Colorado above Westwater Canyon and declining in the Gunnison River.
- **Water and temperature:** Growth stops below 10 °C (50 °F). The working growing-season temperature is about 21 °C (70 °F). Unionized ammonia becomes toxic above 0.0125 ppm.

## Key Findings

**Priorities and stocks**
- Species priorities: razorback sucker 1, bonytail 2, humpback chub 3, Colorado squawfish 4.
- Four presumptive razorback sucker stocks, five humpback chub stocks, and five Colorado squawfish stocks were identified. The conservative approach keeps all possible stocks pending genetics results.
- Immediate broodstocks target two razorback stocks (Escalante Ranch–Green River and upper Colorado above Westwater Canyon), the Lake Mohave bonytail stock, and one Colorado squawfish stock.

**Breeding strategy**
- Target: 25 pedigreed pairs (25 males × 25 females), giving an effective population size of 50 and an inbreeding rate of 1%.
- Minimum: a 5 × 5 di-allele cross (effective population size 10, inbreeding rate 5%).
- Where sexes are unequal, factorial matings capture all fish of the least numerous sex.

**Attrition and broodstock numbers**
- Targeted survivors per family lot at the end of each growing season: 500, 250, 200, 160, 152, 144 (for razorback suckers and Colorado squawfish, which mature after six to seven seasons). Bonytail and humpback chub mature in about four to five seasons.
- Attrition: fertilized eggs 90%; 1st to 2nd season 50%; 2nd to 3rd and 3rd to 4th 20% each; thereafter 5% per year.
- About 30 adults per family lot are targeted for each of the primary and backup refuges.

**Culture and facilities**
- Ponds of 0.1 to 0.2 acre are optimal (0.25–0.33 acre optimum and 0.5 acre absolute maximum per consultants). Small ponds allow family lots to be kept separate and reduce handling stress.
- Ponds produce about twice the growth of raceways.
- Carrying capacity: 500 lb per acre in the first season, 1,000 lb in the second, and 1,500 lb per acre after the third, with 1,500 lb per acre as a standard for fish over 8 inches.
- Evaporation is about 4 acre-feet per acre per year, with about 2.5 water exchanges per season.
- Physical conditioning to current velocity may be needed before stocking, based on bonytail studies at Utah State University.

**Space and water needs**
- Existing: about 6 acres in 1994 and 7.2 acres in 1995.
- Immediate and short-term need: 27.3 acres, with 382.2 acre-feet of water.
- Deficit: 20.1 acres.
- Proposed augmentation and restoration stocking: 43.25 acres, with 605.5 acre-feet of water.
- Total for all needs: 70.55 acres, with 987.7 acre-feet of water.
- Broodstocks for all 15 presumptive stocks would need 29.8 acres of ponds and 417.2 acre-feet.

**Facility recommendations**
- **[[Ouray Endangered Fish Facility]], Utah:** The priority site. Funded with $970,000 in FY 1994 for a water conditioning reservoir, drain lines, a security fence, and about six 0.2-acre ponds. The full build-out is estimated at $1,715,200 in construction, with O&M of about $351,440 (plus about $105,707 for expansion staffing).
- **[[Horsethief State Wildlife Area]], Colorado:** Six ponds totaling 3.5 acres, a refuge for endangered fish, with no room to expand. Built for about $230,000.
- **[[Wahweap State Fish Hatchery]], Utah:** A potential expansion site with 600 gpm of 19.4 °C well water and a 7–8 month growing season. Planning is funded at $70,000.
- **[[Craig Municipal Facilities]], Colorado:** A proposed backup refuge on 40 acres of idle lagoons, with an estimated $1.35 million for 36 ponds. O&M is estimated at $150,000–$200,000 per year.
- **State native fish facilities:** The Colorado Native Aquatic Wildlife Propagation Facility (estimated at $4.95 million, excluding land and water) and Utah native fish facilities (including [[Gandy Warm Springs]]) are recommended for possible cost-sharing.

**Recommendations for Program action (ten specific recommendations)**
1. Review and adopt a stocking policy for captive-reared fish.
2. Finalize a Genetic Management Plan and a propagation handbook.
3. Enforce quarterly reporting of captive fish to a central Program office.
4. Revisit facility needs annually.
5. Give the Propagation Subcommittee responsibility for culture recommendations.
6. Place endangered stocks in primary and backup refuges.
7. Shorten the 1994 and 1997 genetics study timeframes.
8. Focus first on genetic refuges and broodstocks of priority species.
9. Evaluate cost-sharing with state native fish facilities.
10. Adopt the disposition guidelines, under which surplus fish are used for research, education, or other purposes, and only unfit or unneeded fish are euthanized under accepted protocol.

**Stocking plans proposed (not yet approved)**
- Razorback sucker augmentation in Colorado waters: 300,000 4-inch, 75,000 8-inch, and 18,000 12-inch fish.
- Bonytail restoration in Colorado waters: 191,406 two-year-old fish.
- Bonytail restoration in Utah waters: 100,000 fish at 150 mm.
- Colorado squawfish restoration and augmentation: 1,953 fish for the Colorado River reach and 2,070 for the Gunnison River.

The only Program-approved stocking is 60 adult razorback suckers in the upper Colorado River near Grand Junction, for radiotelemetry habitat studies.

**Key caveats**
- Long-term needs (beyond 5 years) cannot be defined with certainty.
- Past stocking of these fishes has had poor success.
- Large-scale stocking should wait until genetics results, ecological requirements, and responses to flow and habitat work are known.
- Surplus-fish euthanasia is a sensitive public issue.
