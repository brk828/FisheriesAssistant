## Executive Summary

This 1995 notice, submitted by the Arizona Center for Law in the Public Interest on behalf of private citizens, documents chronic and repeated violations of NPDES Permit No. AZ 0020150 governing the [[Nogales International Wastewater Plant]], jointly operated by the City of Nogales and the U.S. Section of the [[International Boundary and Water Commission]]. Violations spanning 1990–1995 include exceedances of fecal coliform, biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), total residual chlorine, mercury, lead, pentachlorophenol, settleable/suspended solids, and human enteric virus limits in effluent discharged to the [[Santa Cruz River]]. The document also describes unpermitted, ongoing chlorination of natural washes (Nogales Wash, Trickey Wash, Cerro Pelon, West Wash) since 1991, and failure to implement pretreatment/waste-minimization programs targeting industrial pollution sources originating in Nogales, Sonora, Mexico. While the paper contains no direct fish population or morphological data, it establishes a detailed water-quality contamination record for a freshwater system in the arid Southwest that would bear directly on habitat suitability for native and introduced fish species in the [[Santa Cruz River]] drainage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although no organisms are directly studied, the report characterizes the **abiotic ecological conditions** of the receiving waters relevant to aquatic life in the [[Santa Cruz River]] and connected wash systems:

- **Receiving water body:** Effluent from the treatment plant discharges into the [[Santa Cruz River]], an intermittent desert stream supporting riparian and aquatic habitat in southern Arizona.
- **Cross-border pollutant loading:** Influent to the plant originates partly from untreated/inadequately treated industrial wastewater transported via the international trunkline from Nogales, Sonora, Mexico, introducing heavy metals (mercury, lead) not removed by the treatment process ("metals... either settle to the sludge or pass right on through the treatment plant and out into the Santa Cruz River in the effluent").
- **Toxicant classes documented:** Fecal coliform bacteria, BOD (organic loading/oxygen demand), total residual chlorine, mercury, lead, pentachlorophenol, settleable and suspended solids, and human enteric virus — each with direct or indirect relevance to aquatic organism health, including fish (e.g., chlorine toxicity to gill tissue, mercury bioaccumulation, oxygen depletion from BOD loading).
- **Habitat disturbance events:** A February 17, 1994 diesel fuel discharge from Nogales, Sonora into the international trunkline contaminated the treatment plant's sewage ponds, representing an acute pollution event with potential downstream ecological consequences.
- **Unpermitted chlorination discharge:** Since ~January 1991, continuous chlorine discharge into Nogales Wash (and periodic discharge into Trickey Wash, Cerro Pelon, and West Wash) has occurred without NPDES permitting or environmental review, despite monitoring data showing chlorination is "not always effective at reducing fecal coliform counts to safe levels" — indicating a persistent, unregulated toxicant input to a freshwater habitat corridor.

These conditions collectively describe a degraded/stressed freshwater ecological niche characterized by episodic toxic loading, chronic organic pollution, and unregulated chemical disinfection byproducts — factors well-documented in aquatic ecology as detrimental to native fish assemblages (e.g., [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]], and other Sonoran Desert-endemic fishes historically present in the [[Santa Cruz River]] drainage), though the document itself does not name specific fish taxa.

## Key Findings

- **Fecal coliform bacteria** violations of the daily maximum limit occurred repeatedly from 1990–1994, including a cluster of 7 violations in June 1994 alone.
- **BOD removal efficiency** (required minimum 85% removal by weight) was violated across 15 separate months between 1992–1995, indicating chronic under-treatment of organic waste loading to the [[Santa Cruz River]].
- **Total residual chlorine** daily maximum violations occurred in 8 distinct months (1992–1995), with 7 violations in June 1994 alone — a toxicant of particular concern for gill-breathing aquatic organisms.
- **Mercury** daily maximum concentration violations occurred in at least 10 months (1992–1994); the City of Nogales attributed 58 mercury standard violations in the international trunkline in 1993 alone to Mexican-side industrial sources.
- **Lead, pentachlorophenol, and human enteric virus** limits were each violated at least once (January 1994, October 1994, and October 1994, respectively) — persistent organic pollutants and heavy metals with known bioaccumulative and toxic effects in aquatic food webs.
- **Whole effluent toxicity testing** revealed a violation of the weekly average acute toxicity limit (minimum 40% survival threshold) in January 1992, a direct biological indicator of effluent lethality to test organisms.
- **Settleable and suspended solids** violations (1992, 1994) reflect inadequate solids removal, contributing to sedimentation and turbidity stress on the receiving stream.
- **Pretreatment and waste-minimization program failures**: The copermittees failed to identify controllable pollutant sources in Mexico, failed to submit required waste-minimization and pretreatment reports (due 1992), and failed to implement any corrective measures — a systemic regulatory failure enabling continued toxic loading into the watershed.
- **Sludge management deficiencies**: The February 1993 sludge disposal plan lacked required chemical analyses for priority pollutants and failed to specify contractor qualifications, disposal sites, or loading rates; subsequent annual reports (1993, 1994) omitted required sludge quality/quantity data entirely.
- **Unpermitted chlorination**: Continuous, NPDES-unpermitted chlorine discharge into Nogales Wash since January 1991 (with periodic discharge into three additional washes) constitutes an ongoing Clean Water Act and NEPA violation, with documented incomplete effectiveness at pathogen reduction.
- **Acute contamination event**: A February 1994 diesel fuel discharge from Mexico via the international trunkline created an explosion hazard requiring evacuation of 50 city blocks and contaminated the treatment plant's sewage ponds, illustrating the vulnerability of the binational wastewater infrastructure to uncontrolled hazardous discharges affecting the [[Santa Cruz River]] ecosystem.
