# Desert Fishes Council Letter on Conservation Philosophy and Fisheries Management

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #fisheries-management #native-species

## Executive Summary

This 1991 correspondence from E.P. Pister, Executive Secretary of the [[Desert Fishes Council]], to Bruce Schmidt of the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] clarifies philosophical positions on fisheries management presented at the Western Division AFS meeting in Bozeman. The letter addresses the perceived conflict between "management" and "conservation" biologists, arguing there should be no distinction between the two. Pister advocates for prioritizing biodiversity preservation and native species protection over short-term angling success, using the [[Colorado River]] system as a cautionary example of habitat alteration and problematic fish introductions. The document includes an abstract for Pister's conference presentation titled "We Have Met the Enemy, and They Are Us!!" which critiques fishery biologists who prioritize catch-per-hour metrics over biotic community integrity.

## Background & Description

The correspondence responds to misinterpretation of Pister's presentation at the Western Division American Fisheries Society meeting. Pister clarifies his intent was not to create division between management and conservation biologists but to unify the profession around biodiversity preservation as the twentieth century concluded. He references the [[Keystone Biodiversity Strategy]] meeting attended immediately before the Bozeman conference, where governmental agencies were viewed with suspicion regarding their commitment to long-term biological integrity versus short-term economic and political considerations.

Pister draws heavily on [[Aldo Leopold]]'s philosophy from *A Sand County Almanac*, particularly Leopold's A-B dichotomy among conservationists: Group A views land as soil for commodity production, while Group B regards land as biota with broader functions. He notes that environmental ethics courses remain rare in fisheries and wildlife curriculum, producing what he terms "missiles without guidance systems." The letter references past management decisions like [[Flaming Gorge]] and the [[Green River]] as examples of mistakes based on different value structures and naivete.

The author acknowledges his 40-year career managing trout fisheries and extensive use of rotenone (Pro-Noxfish) to secure populations of threatened and endangered salmonids and cyprinodonts by removing introduced competitors, typically [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Brown Trout]]. However, he expresses concern about potential invertebrate extinctions attributable to past negligence with fish toxicants.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] system serves as the primary example of a "disaster" model through its complex of habitat alteration and fish introductions, though Pister notes similar points could be made about many of the nation's river systems. The document references specific extinctions: [[Lampetra minima]] (a lamprey species) and the [[Bigmouth Lakesucker]] (*Chasmistes stomias*) through improper rotenone use, as mentioned by Carl Bond during the conference.

The letter emphasizes that ecosystems have no vacuums—sport fishery success based on introduced game and forage fish species cannot compensate for endangerment or extinction of native species. As human populations in the West continue to expand while gamefish populations diminish, Pister argues for utilizing the entirety of the native aquatic biota in innovative ways to satisfy demand.

The [[Great Basin]] is referenced as a region where future generations might fish for native [[Cutthroat Trout]] fed by native [[Gila]] species, representing both biologically and morally acceptable management. The document emphasizes preserving genetic diversity within species as analogous to maintaining broad, less specialized education in natural resource management—both provide adaptability to unexpected future conditions.

## Key Findings

**Primary Arguments:**

1. **Agency Credibility Crisis**: Governmental agencies are viewed with suspicion by biodiversity advocates who believe management direction is determined more by short-term economics and political expediency than long-term biological integrity. The fundamental question posed: "Will future generations remember us more favorably by angler success during 1991, or by the integrity of our aquatic biotas in 2091?"

2. **Professional Philosophy Gap**: The A-B dichotomy identified by [[Aldo Leopold]] applies strongly within fish and wildlife agencies, with B-group conservationists (those viewing ecosystems holistically) remaining in the minority. Too many biologists first ask what can be done to improve angling rather than what can be done to protect and enhance biodiversity.

3. **Rotenone as Conservation Tool**: When used prudently, rotenone can be valuable for rehabilitating native fishes and enhancing biodiversity. Pister reports using "hundreds of gallons of Pro-Noxfish" over 40 years to secure threatened and endangered populations. However, improper use has caused extinctions, and concern exists about unrecorded invertebrate taxa losses.

4. **Leopold's Conservation Maxim**: The guiding principle offered is Leopold's statement: "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. It is wrong when it tends otherwise." Conservation is best defined as "living in harmony with nature."

5. **Future Management Vision**: Human perceptions and demands evolve in unanticipated ways. Since current desires and trends are transient, the reasonable approach is preserving and appreciating the natural ecosystem's wonder, beauty, and complexity. Native species should form the foundation of future recreational fisheries.

6. **Educational Deficiency**: The tendency of fisheries and wildlife schools to produce graduates focused solely on utilitarian courses without environmental ethics training creates professionals unprepared for the realities and concerns inherent within the [[Endangered Species Act]].

7. **Professional Responsibility**: Fishery biologists must become a major force in maintaining biodiversity rather than remaining "a significant part of the problem." This requires looking ahead—"Way ahead!!"—and maintaining critical perspective on status quo management procedures and philosophies.
