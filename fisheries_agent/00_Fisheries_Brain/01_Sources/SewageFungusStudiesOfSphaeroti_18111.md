# Sewage Fungus: Laboratory Recirculating Channel Studies of Sphaerotilus Slimes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sphaerotilaceae

*Note: The source concerns microbial and protozoan slime communities, not fish. The fish-related content is limited to slime effects on spawning and hatching. The tags above follow the system template and do not reflect a fish focus.*

## Executive Summary

This 1971 study by Curtis, Delves-Broughton and Harrington describes six flow-through recirculating laboratory channels built to grow and measure [[Sewage Fungus]] slimes under controlled velocity, flow, temperature, light and nutrient conditions. The authors aimed to obtain quantitative data on slime growth rates, specific nutrient removal, and the conditions favouring different slime-forming organisms, particularly [[Sphaerotilus Natans]]. Sphaerotilus-dominated slimes were maintained by using low organic nitrogen inputs, unglazed tile substrata and reduced dissolved oxygen. Slime biomass scaled with influent organic carbon (glucose or acetate) above a limiting concentration of about 1 mg C/l, and phosphate concentration over 0.04–0.50 mg P/l had no effect on growth.

## Background & Description

Sewage fungus can form massive macroscopic slimes under organic pollution. These slimes deoxygenate water, impair fish spawning and hatching, and reduce recreational value. Earlier work was either pure-culture laboratory research, which the authors considered hard to extrapolate to rivers, or qualitative field studies. Outdoor artificial channels lose control of light and temperature, and small laboratory channels are harder to interpret.

**Channel design:**
- Six channels, each 1.2 m long and 0.2 m deep, with a central dividing wall around which nutrient medium recirculates.
- Stainless steel construction with an epoxy base and removable PVC or unglazed porcelain tiles for benthic sampling.
- Adjustable overflow weir giving depths of 0.025–0.2 m (6.6–52.6 l).
- Water velocity of 0.2–0.5 m/s, with a reproducible flow (±2%) from orifice plates.
- Temperature controlled between 7 and 25 °C in water baths; illumination from adjustable fluorescent lamps.

**Analytical methods:** Slime was dried at 105 °C and weighed. Whole-channel biomass was estimated by mapping growth zones (agreement within 10% of total removal). Taxonomy was assessed by [[Phase-Contrast Microscopy]], and organic carbon was measured after carbonate removal. Water samples were analysed for organic carbon, nitrogen species and soluble phosphate. The bore-hole water supply was chlorine-free, with organic carbon below 10 mg/l, oxidised nitrogen of 4.3–4.6 mg N/l and soluble phosphate of 0.02–0.03 mg P/l.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Sewage fungus is attributed largely to inadequately treated sewage effluents, the main cause of outbreaks in the UK. Glucose and acetate are the major soluble biodegradable organic compounds in sewage.
- Channel slime communities were dominated by Sphaerotilus with zoogloeal bacteria, or by fungi (chiefly *Fusarium aquaeductum*) with zoogloeal bacteria. Other contaminants included [[Flavobacterium X]], a filamentous bacterium.
- Ciliated protozoa were present in all slimes. The commonest taxa were *Glaucoma scintillans*, *Chilodonella uncinata*, *Uronema nigricans* and *Trachelophyllum pusillum*. Some species, such as *Colpidium campylum* and *Euplotes moebiusi*, were tied to specific slime types. Their presence was attributed to water or air contamination and resembled river populations.
- Slime formation was controlled by organic nitrogen supply, dissolved oxygen (Sphaerotilus formed reliably only below about 5–6 mg/l), and substratum roughness (Sphaerotilus preferred rough unglazed tile).
- The authors suggest that the 1 mg C/l threshold is a reasonable estimate of the concentration of simple biodegradable pollutants that can be tolerated without slime growth in a river.

## Key Findings

1. **Biomass–nutrient relationship (glucose):** Slime biomass was proportional to influent organic carbon. Carbon removal was proportional to biomass at about 0.3 g C/day per g slime dry weight. Less than 20% of removed carbon was incorporated into slime. Economic coefficients were below 20% in most periods.
2. **Acetate:** Growth followed a similar pattern. Carbon removal was proportional to biomass, and economic coefficients were generally below 20%, falling as experiments progressed. Phosphate removal was about 0.002 g P/day per g dry weight.
3. **Limiting concentration:** Below about 1 mg C/l there was no measurable increase in slime growth. Phosphate removal was not observed at these concentrations.
4. **Phosphate effect:** Over 0.04–0.50 mg P/l, phosphate had no effect on slime growth or economic coefficients. Slime phosphorus content was significantly lower at 0.04 mg P/l. Phosphorus removal was almost complete at 0.04 and 0.10 mg P/l, and 20–53% at 0.50 mg P/l. Removal exceeded incorporation into slime, so incorporation was not the only removal route. Planktonic bacteria may have contributed.
5. **Comparison with [[Wuhrmann et Al]] (1966):** Maximum biomass of about 30 g dry weight/m² matched the channel maxima. Channel nutrient extraction rates were roughly twice those reported for a 135 m outdoor channel (about 3.7 g organic C/m²/day). Accumulation rates were 1.2 g/m²/day (glucose) and 1.8 g/m²/day (acetate) in the channels, compared with 1.8 g/m²/day in the outdoor study.
6. **Economic coefficient context:** Hohnl (1955) reported 19% for glucose in batch culture. Unpublished Laboratory river estimates ranged from 60% for initial colonisation to 15% for established slime of 15 g dry wt/m².
7. **Sloughing and sampling:** Growth was uneven and sloughing sometimes occurred, which complicated quantitative replication. Whole-channel mapping gave reliable estimates.
8. **Research implications:** The channels reproduce river-like slime communities and provide controlled quantitative data for upstream and downstream pollution assessment. The authors note that slime growth in rivers is only rarely phosphorus-limited, although oligotrophic streams may be exceptions.
