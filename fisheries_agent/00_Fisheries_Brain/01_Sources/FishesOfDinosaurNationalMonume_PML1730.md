# Fishes of Dinosaur National Monument

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a natural-history survey of the fishes of [[Dinosaur National Monument]], centered on the [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]] in the [[Colorado River]] basin. It describes a harsh, silty, highly variable canyon environment in which few species persist, and notes that only eight native species occur, while about half of the monument's species have been introduced. It profiles the fish families and species present, flags conservation concerns for native fishes such as the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]], and records how the construction of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] has altered flow and temperature in the Green River.

## Background & Description

The monument's perennial water is mainly the Green and Yampa rivers. Most tributaries flow only after heavy rain, so the notable exception is [[Jones Creek]], which enters the Green from the north between Echo Park and Island Park. The Green and Yampa cut spectacular canyons with steep gradients (the Green drops 650 feet over about 60 miles, roughly 11 feet per mile, rising to 21 feet per mile through [[Split Mountain]]). Historically, flows reached nearly 20 miles per hour, and flash floods raised water levels 30 or more feet.

Species are grouped by family. Each account indicates whether the fish is native or introduced. Families covered include:

- [[Salmonidae]] (trouts and whitefishes): [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]], both introduced.
- [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows): [[Carp]], [[Colorado Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Redside Shiner]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Fathead Minnow]].
- [[Catostomidae]] (suckers): [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and [[Humpback Sucker]].
- [[Ictaluridae]] (catfishes): [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Black Bullhead]].
- [[Cottidae]] (sculpins): [[Colorado Mottled Sculpin]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Green and Yampa rivers are extreme in silt load, flow variability, and temperature change. Only fishes able to tolerate these extremes persist. The native [[Cutthroat Trout]] of the Green drainage and the [[Mountain Whitefish]] never inhabited the monument, likely because of the heavy silt load.

Native species show specialized adaptations to swift, turbulent water. The Humpback Chub has a hump, large sickle-shaped fins, and a largely scaleless body that help it resist flood flows of up to 18,000 cubic feet per second. The Colorado Squawfish is a large, streamlined predator. Bluehead and Humpback suckers have scraping jaws and streamlined bodies for feeding on algae.

Flow and temperature have changed since [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closed in November 1962. The dam is about 45 miles upstream of the monument. Flows below it are a fraction of former levels, the water is cold enough to support introduced trout, and the spectacular rises and falls have ceased. The document states that the effects on fish life have not yet been determined.

Introduced species, including carp, trout, and channel catfish, have displaced or competed with native fishes. The Colorado Chub and Squawfish are declining as rivers are modified through dams, diversions, and competition from game fishes.

## Key Findings

- **Water sources:** The Green and Yampa rivers are the only permanent waters; most tributaries are seasonal.
- **Fish composition:** About half of the monument's fish species are introduced. Only eight native species occur. Three native species were used as food by Native Americans, and the Colorado Squawfish is a sport fish today.
- **Conservation concern:** Native fishes are described as part of a unique heritage that is losing ground to development. The Humpback Chub is becoming scarcer as its habitat disappears. The Colorado Chub is decreasing in abundance.
- **Flow alteration:** Flaming Gorge Dam (closed November 1962) reduced flows, made the river cold enough for trout, and removed flow spikes. Biological effects remain unstudied.
- **Brown vs. rainbow trout:** Brown trout tolerate warmer, less clear water and have been more common in the big rivers. Rainbow trout may replace them if the river becomes consistently cold.
- **Species accounts:** The text profiles about 20 species in detail, plus six additional introduced species with sporadic or no recent records, including [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Creek Chub]], [[White Sucker]], [[Walleye]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Green Sunfish]].
