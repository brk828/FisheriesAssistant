# Minimally Invasive Tissue Sampling for Genetic Analysis in Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This book chapter, by Morizot, [[Schmidt]], [[Carmichael]], [[Stock]] and Williamson, evaluates whether genetic data (chiefly allozyme and other protein markers) can be obtained from fishes without killing them. It introduces the term **[[Minimally Invasive Tissue Sampling]]** and compares candidate tissues against six criteria: low lethality, minimal effects on fish health and fitness, sufficient material for analysis, minimal post-sampling treatment, applicability to most fish taxa, and minimal technician training. Tissues assessed include [[Blood Sampling]], [[Liver Biopsy]], [[Eye and Vitreous Humor Sampling]], [[Muscle Biopsy]], [[Gamete Sampling]], [[Scale Epithelium Sampling]], [[Mucus Sampling]], [[Barbel Sampling]] and [[Fin Biopsy]]. The authors conclude that fin tissue is the most informative and broadly applicable option currently available, and that non-lethal sampling can support nearly all population-genetic questions.

## Background & Description

Protein markers resolved by [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]] and [[Isoelectric Focusing]], along with mitochondrial and nuclear DNA analyses using restriction endonucleases, reveal variation among species, subspecies, local populations and individuals. Most such studies have relied on lethally collected tissue. The chapter identifies four situations where non-lethal sampling is especially valuable:

1. Allele frequency estimation and marker identification in endangered species, natural populations or captive colonies.
2. Selection of broodfish carrying specific markers.
3. Assessment of genetic purity in hatchery stocks.
4. Collection of genetic data in [[Mark-Recapture Studies]].

The authors use the term "minimally invasive" rather than "non-invasive" or "low-risk" because it describes the procedures more accurately. Their baseline for comparison is about 90 frequently resolved protein loci (gene products) in fishes, drawn from a table of protein systems studied in fishes.

Blood was historically the preferred tissue, but use declined after the 1970s, when researchers shifted to necropsied tissues such as liver and muscle. Those tissues expressed tissue-specific isozymes that were absent from or too dilute in blood.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The chapter notes several environmental and logistical factors that affect sampling choices:

- **Field logistics:** Blood processing needs generators, centrifuges, dry ice or liquid nitrogen freezers, and sterile supplies. These demands motivated the search for alternatives.
- **Captive versus wild populations:** Tissue regeneration allows repeat sampling in captive fish, but usually not in wild fish. Post-sampling treatment, such as antibiotics, is also harder to provide in the wild.
- **Habitat-dependent fitness effects:** Fin function may be critical in fast-flowing streams and rivers but less important in hatchery conditions. Fin removal may therefore carry greater risk in the wild.
- **Size constraints:** Many methods (liver, muscle, blood, eye removal) are impractical in small fishes such as guppies, and some sampling techniques require a dissecting microscope.
- **Infection:** Wounds from scale or fin removal pose infection risks that need careful control.

## Key Findings

**Blood**
- The most extensive recent study used blood plasma and hemolysate from largemouth bass to assess 70 protein-coding loci. About half of the locus products were resolvable from blood, excluding esterases and plasma proteins.
- Blood sampling can meet criteria 1, 2 and 5 with careful technique. Cardiac puncture carries risk, and caudal vein or gill bleeding requires fragile capillary tubes. For guppies, the authors sampled suborbital sinuses with fire-polished capillary pipettes.
- Processing is the main shortcoming. Standard anticoagulants perform poorly in fish blood. Sodium heparin (10,000 units/ml) and 3% sodium citrate prevent clotting briefly, but these were not tested in marine fishes. Plasma must be separated by centrifugation and frozen quickly.

**Liver**
- Partial hepatectomy produces regeneration, which supports a liver biopsy approach. [[Harvey]] and colleagues developed a largemouth bass method involving a 1 cm³ lobe plug.
- Approximately two-thirds of frequently studied locus products can be scored from liver, making it the most informative tissue in the authors' analyses.
- Mortality in largemouth bass is "relatively low." Fish health effects are largely undetermined, and recovery in natural environments may be precarious.
- Liver biopsy is impracticable in small fish. Only about 1–2 mg could be excised without significant damage.

**Eye and vitreous humor**
- Over 60% of standard locus products could be scored from eye tissue in the authors' studies. Eye tissue also carries isozymes that are expressed mainly or only in the eye.
- Whole-eye removal raises concerns about mortality, fish health and aesthetics. Vitreous humor sampling is a gentler option. Seeb et al. extracted 0.5 ml from chum salmon eyes without measurable harm, but this approach is impracticable for small fish and has not been well assessed for enzyme expression.

**Muscle**
- Crawford et al. used a dermal punch to extract 1 cm³ muscle plugs from steelhead trout. Mortality was 0.2%, and Seeb et al. reported no effect on survival in a large-scale salmon study.
- Muscle biopsy approaches or exceeds 60% expression of routinely studied locus products. Adding parvalbumins brings resolvable gene products to more than 50.
- Muscle is an excellent source of nuclear and mitochondrial DNA but is generally poor for karyology.
- Seeb et al.'s mark-recapture study reported extensive recruitment of genetically marked chum salmon into the spawning population. Fitness effects in small or juvenile fish remain untested.

**Gametes**
- Eggs and sperm are potentially non-invasive. Mork and Heggberget used Atlantic salmon and brown trout eggs to resolve glucosephosphate isomerase polymorphisms. Testis-specific isozymes have been identified in sperm for creatine kinase and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase.
- Unpublished work suggests more than 30 locus products may be resolvable in poeciliid testes, but sufficient sperm quantity remains untested.
- Gamete collection is routine in hatcheries, but eggs of livebearers are generally unavailable, small species may yield too little material, and spawning seasons are short.

**Scale epithelium**
- Carmichael et al. examined scales and epidermis from largemouth bass caudal peduncles. Almost 60% of the 64 loci in their survey could be resolved.
- Removing a single scale from a large species can supply substantial material, but sampling small fish is infeasible. Infection at scale-removal sites is a serious concern.

**Mucus**
- Herzberg (tilapia) and Wu and Wu (*Oreochromis*) used surface mucus, but only soluble proteins and esterases were examined. Their methods required concentration by dialysis or lyophilization.
- The authors expect that enzymes strongly expressed in epithelial tissue could be resolved from skin mucus alone, which would reduce infection risk. This remains to be tested.

**Barbels**
- Barbel removal is not broadly applicable (criterion 5 is not met), but catfish barbels are an excellent minimally invasive tissue. More than 60% of the survey loci could be resolved.
- Removing one barbel appears to have little effect on feeding or survival. Sampling needs only quick freezing and minimal equipment.

**Adipose fins**
- Vuorinen and Piironen found that the distal 3–5 mm of salmonid adipose fins could be biopsied even from juveniles without significant harm. About 1/6 of the 74 enzyme systems tested could be scored from adipose tissue (figure partly garbled in scan).
- In catfish, nearly 60% of more than 60 locus products could be detected consistently.
- The main drawback is high fat content, which produces smeary patterns. Toluene extraction or centrifugation through filters may be required.

**Caudal and pelvic fins (Fin Biopsy)**
- In largemouth bass, more than 60% of a sample of 64 loci could be scored from caudal or pelvic fin samples. Fin samples fall slightly below liver and slightly above muscle in information potential.
- The authors consider fin sampling the most informative and broadly applicable minimally invasive method currently available. It meets criteria 1 and 3–6 well, but its fitness effects (criterion 2) must be examined before wider use.
- Fin clips are a traditional marking method, yet some studies using fin clips for marking did not analyze the fin biopsies for isozymes.

**Study design and tissue selection**
- Species-specific variation matters. GALT (galactose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase) shows near-fixed allelic differences between Florida and northern largemouth bass subspecies. GALT, ACOZ, FDP2, MDHB and others could not be scored in bass caudal fin samples, even though they are expressed in catfish fins.
- Comparing expression in minimally invasive tissues against necropsied tissues (brain, eye, liver, muscle, heart, kidney) is an essential preliminary step.
- Scoring 30–50 locus products is usually feasible with minimally invasive methods when enough individuals are available. Repeated sampling of fin or brood tissue can extend coverage.
- For individual identification, such as hybrid detection or verifying inbred or gynogenetic status, multiple tissues may be combined. The authors warn against "overkill."
- Future prospects include expanding the expression database, characterizing the 40–50 locus products never tested in minimally invasive tissues, and developing methods for nuclear DNA, mitochondrial DNA, chromosome and chemical analyses.

**Conservation and practical implications**
- The authors argue that sampling from commercial fisheries has negligible impact. However, losing single individuals of genetically unique stocks or strains becomes increasingly costly, and minimally invasive sampling can conserve irreplaceable biological resources.
