# Population Study of Fish in Elephant Butte Lake (1955)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Federal Aid job completion report (Project F-6-R, Job B-1, Santa Fe, New Mexico, dated March–April 1956) documents a 1955 population study of fishes in [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]], an irrigation storage reservoir on the [[Rio Grande]] where fishing is a secondary use. The study aimed to determine the species present, their relative numbers, distribution and spawning success, and the influence of rough fish on sport fishing. Four capture methods were used: [[Gill Netting]], hoop netting, seining and [[Rotenone Sampling]]. The authors found 22 species, a fish community dominated by rough fish (carp, buffalo and carpsucker), and a lake whose frequent irrigation draw-downs were judged to hinder spawning of game fish.

## Background & Description

**Reservoir and project context.** Elephant Butte Dam (completed 1916) is a 306-foot rubble concrete gravity dam forming a reservoir with a capacity of 2,198,000 acre-feet. The surface area was originally over 38,000 acres at spillway level. [[Caballo Dam]], an earth embankment 22 miles downstream, was completed in 1938 with a capacity of 346,000 acre-feet. The reservoir is part of a Bureau of Reclamation system serving about 155,000 acres of cultivated land along roughly 200 miles of the Rio Grande, with 88,000 acres in New Mexico and 67,000 acres in Texas. The region lies in the lower Sonoran zone (semi-desert cactus and mesquite flora). When full, the north end has extensive shallows, while the south shore is precipitous.

**Study areas.** The lake was divided into three main areas, A, B and C, with subdivisions (A1, A2, B1, etc.). Area A is the deepest part and has steep, rocky shores. Area B is intermediate. Area C is the shallow, turbid upper end with gently sloping, silt-covered shores.

**Methods.**
- *Gill nets:* 300-foot experimental nets of 8 and 12 ft depth with 1, 1½, 2½, 3 and 4-inch bar-measure mesh panels, plus a 6-ft trammel net (2½-inch mesh) and an 8-ft, 5-inch mesh net for large fish. Nylon bobbinette sewn over the lead and float lines nearly eliminated tangles.
- *Hoop nets:* four nets from 30 to 52 inches in diameter (mesh 1½–2 inches), plus a wooden-frame box trap covered with 1-inch chicken wire.
- *Seines:* 100-ft seines (1-inch and ⅜-inch mesh), combinable to 200 ft, and a 25-ft minnow seine.
- *Rotenone:* 0.8 percent rotenone applied at 1 part per million to coves blocked by seines, with power-pump spraying in shallow brushy areas.
- *Age and growth:* 15–20 scales per fish, plus dorsal spines and opercular bones from catfish, aged by counting annuli. The authors note that lengths were taken at capture, so they may underestimate full-year growth.
- *Diet:* stomachs preserved in 10 percent formalin; empty stomachs excluded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water level fluctuation.** Irrigation draw-downs produce rapid, large changes in lake level (Figure 2 covers 1951–1955). The authors argued that rapid rises and falls hinder spawning, remove shelter and vegetation, leave shallows without cover or insect hatching sites, and reduce plankton production. Declining water through the spawning season was thought to strand young fish in open water, exposing them to predators. Lake elevations were consistently lower across the 1945–1955 record (Figure 3).

**Largemouth bass and crappie timing.** Largemouth bass spawn from about May, with crappie spawning somewhat earlier. Water fell until May in every year except 1952. The authors inferred that 1952 was the only favorable year among those examined for crappie, and that the largest crappie year class came from 1952. Other years were judged marginal.

**Transparency.** Visibility, measured with a seven-inch black-and-white Secchi disc at the Area B station, ranged from 16 inches in November to 58 inches in February, averaging about 32 inches. Wind-driven waves, silt-laden inflow and rough fish activity were identified as the main causes of turbidity.

**Water chemistry.** Surface oxygen was usually supersaturated, from 6.05 to 11.0 ppm with an average of 8.8 ppm. No carbon dioxide was detected, and pH was consistently 8.5. No thermocline was observed, and oxygen remained above critical levels at all depths. A State Public Health Laboratory analysis (collected 30 August 1954) reported total solids of 1,730 ppm, total alkalinity of 106 ppm as CaCO₃, and hardness of 589 ppm as CaCO₃.

**Temperature.** Surface temperatures ranged from a February low to a 76.0 °F high in September. Bottom water reached 80.0 °F in late July through early September, when silt-laden inflow was denser than the lake water. The bottom three to five feet during that period carried roughly three-quarters silt by volume.

**Seasonal migration.** Gill net and hoop net data showed that most catfish and rough fish moved from shallow water in summer to deeper water in winter. Carp and buffalo migrations were sudden and correlated with temperature drops. The authors attributed summer concentrations in Area C to better feeding in warm, shallow, vegetated margins. Area A served as a winter refuge with stable temperatures but poor summer feeding. Oxygen did not appear to control fish depth.

**Other biota.** Gizzard shad were very abundant. Other species present in small numbers included suckers, redfin shiner, gambusia and fathead minnows. A large population of soft-shell turtles was noted in the upper lake, and several were captured in traps and gill nets.

## Key Findings

**Community composition**
- 22 fish species were recorded (Table 1), including Largemouth Black Bass, both crappies, bluegill, Walleye, Channel, Blue and Flathead catfish, carp, buffalo, carpsucker and gizzard shad.
- Rough fish (carp, buffalo and carpsucker) made up 65 percent of the population, and game fish 35 percent by number. Shad were excluded from these percentages.
- Carp made up 33 percent of the population excluding shad. Buffalo, carp and carpsucker together made up 65 percent of the catch excluding shad.
- A total of 14,813 fish were caught by all methods.

**Rotenone and shad**
- Rotenone recovered 5,656 fish. Shad made up 90.5 percent by number and 55 percent by weight. The authors estimated that recovery was only about 60 percent of the shad killed.

**Growth and condition**
- Age and growth were generally slow for most species. [[Largemouth Bass]] grew below average for most age groups, with the largest aged fish at 19.5 inches (VII year group).
- [[Channel Catfish]] showed rapid growth, with the largest specimen at 17.5 inches (III year group).
- [[Blue Catfish]] grew rapidly, reaching 27.0 inches (IV year group).
- [[Flathead Catfish]] growth was described as excellent. The largest was 52 inches (XII year group), and the authors stated that flathead outgrew those from Oklahoma and the upper Mississippi.
- [[Carp]] growth was below average. [[Black Crappie]] and [[White Crappie]] showed lower weight per length than crappie from other waters.
- Length-weight condition was below average for most spiny-rayed fish, while catfish were above average.
- Bluegill appeared to do poorly, with the largest fish at 6.5 inches.

**Diet**
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: shad (7% occurrence, 16.2% volume), insects (29%, 19.1%), other fish (18%, 17.1%).
- Crappie: shad dominated (88% occurrence, 87.5% volume).
- [[Channel Catfish]]: shad (62%, 50.8%); insects were common by occurrence (34%) but lower by volume.
- [[Blue Catfish]]: animal debris dominated (73%, 68.2%); shad 36% occurrence.
- [[Flathead Catfish]]: carp were the main food (50% occurrence, 7.5% volume). Game fish made up 32 percent by occurrence.
- Shad were judged the best forage fish, since all game fish examined fed heavily on young shad.

**Stocking**
- Stocking began in 1919. Carp were planted in the Rio Grande in the 1880s (6,880 fry near Albuquerque, 2,140 at El Paso).
- Table 2 lists stocking from 1919 to 1955, including large plantings of Largemouth Bass, Walleye (5,000,000 in 1935 and 750,000 in 1939–40 and 1940–41) and bream.

**Marking**
- 1,793 fish were fin-clipped or tagged. [[Buffalo]] were most numerous (782 fin-clipped), followed by [[Channel Catfish]] (345 tagged) and [[Largemouth Bass]] (312 tagged). Returns were very few.

**Recommendations**
1. Study methods of [[Rough Fish Removal]], since rough fish dominated the lake.
2. Conduct further food habit studies of game fish.
3. Make periodic checks of population trends, spawning success, age and growth, and stocking results.
4. Make an experimental planting of [[Walleye]]. The authors noted good walleye growth and an abundant gizzard shad food base, and suggested annual fry plantings for five years to test feasibility.
