# Positive Phototaxis in Larval Bonytail

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This paper, published in the [[Journal of Fish and Wildlife Management]] in December 2015, tests whether larval [[Bonytail]] (*[[Gila Elegans]]*), an endangered fish, show positive phototaxis (movement toward light). The authors note that bonytail recruitment is poor in the wild, which limits recovery. Because larvae are rarely captured in the field, the study combines controlled aquarium experiments with field sampling to test whether light-baited traps can capture larvae efficiently. The text is an OCR scan, and parts of the methods and results are only partly legible, so the numerical results are not reported here.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Bonytail (*Gila elegans*), a freshwater fish listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service made the listing determination in 1980.
- **Life stage studied:** Swim-up larvae, which are the stage most difficult to sample.
- **Conservation context:** Wild populations are described as lacking natural recruitment. Large recruitment events are rare, even in small populations, and the authors attribute part of the problem to nonnative species. Recovery therefore depends partly on capturing and rearing larvae.
- **Related work:** Earlier studies used [[Light Traps]] to capture larval [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]], and the authors cite prior work on phototaxis in larval [[Atlantic Halibut]] and on visual control of phototaxis in [[Lamprey]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Larval bonytail are hard to sample in the environment. The authors state that light traps were not specifically designed to sample larvae, but they were used because larvae show phototactic behavior. The study site references include [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona–Nevada) and [[Cibola High Levee Pond]]. The authors identify nonnative species as a likely factor in poor recruitment, though the excerpt does not give details on specific species or mechanisms.

## Key Findings

**Study design (as described in the methods)**

- **Trap construction:** Traps were built from a commercially available acrylonitrile-based cone fitted with a fine mesh (about 500 µm), with a light source at the top opening. Trap designs were lighted or unlighted, using an [[Light-Emitting Diode]] (LED) lamp in the lighted version.
- **Aquarium trials:** Traps were tested in separate aquaria. The experiments compared paired traps in darkness, with combinations of lighted and unlighted traps, and used larvae from a single tank, with groups of about 50 larvae released per trial.
- **Replication:** Trials were replicated, and the data analysis used a null hypothesis of no difference in larval captures between trap types.
- **Field component:** Field sampling used the same trap designs in the environment. The excerpt does not clearly report the number of field trap nights.
- **Handling:** Recovered larvae were euthanized in [[Tricaine Methanesulfonate]] (MS-222) for preservation.
- **Statistics:** The analysis follows standard biometric methods (Zar 1999; Sokal and Rohlf 1981).

**Main conclusion**

- The authors' study focuses on positive phototaxis in larval bonytail. The results support that larvae are attracted to light, which makes light-baited traps a practical tool for capturing wild larvae for monitoring, research, or repatriation.
