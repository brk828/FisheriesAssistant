# Native Fishes as Natural Resources in Arid Lands of the Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Cyprinodontidae #Poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

W. L. Minckley's chapter, published in an edited volume on native plants and animals as resources in arid lands of the Southwest (Committee on Desert and Arid Zones Research, A.A.A.S., Contribution No. 8), argues that the native fishes of the arid Southwest are valuable natural resources that have been sharply reduced by human modification of aquatic habitats. The chapter reviews their historical food and sport uses, their value to [[Basic Research]] (especially genetics and evolution), their [[Ecological Role]] in insect control and as indicators of environmental change, their use in bioassays, and their esthetic and educational worth. Its central message is that these fishes should be managed for sustained yield rather than exploited and lost.

## Background & Description

Western North America has a depauperate freshwater fish fauna of roughly 200 species, from the Stikine River of British Columbia to the Rio Yaqui, Mexico (Miller, 1958). Political counts are Arizona about 22 native species, Nevada about 36, and Utah 26, compared with 152 in Mississippi, 160 in Ohio, and 133 in Iowa. Families common in the East, such as [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes and basses) and [[Ictaluridae]] (catfishes), were largely absent from the West until introduced by people. About 100 of the 200 western species belong to only two families, [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows) and [[Catostomidae]] (suckers).

Notable taxa discussed include:
- **[[Colorado River Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the largest native North American minnow, reaching about six feet and perhaps 100 pounds.
- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila robusta*), used as food by Indians and considered palatable though bony.
- **[[Humpback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*), frequent in lower Colorado River archaeological sites.
- **[[Cui-ui]]** (*Chasmistes cujus*) of Pyramid Lake, Nevada, and **Utah Lake suckers** (*Catostomus ardens*).
- **[[Pupfish]]** species (*Cyprinodon* spp.), including the Death Valley endemic *C. salinus* and the dwarfed *C. diabolis* of Devils Hole.
- **[[Gila Mountain-Sucker]]** (*Pantosteus clarki*), the dominant sucker in the Salt River.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*), a widespread western species with many local forms.
- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*), a hardy fish of sandy desert streams.
- **[[Moapa Dace]]** (*Moapa coriacea*), a thermal endemic of warm springs in southern Nevada.
- **[[Little Colorado River Spinedace]]** (*Lepidomeda vittata*), now restricted to East Clear Creek, Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Human-driven declines:** Water development has drastically reduced range and abundance of native aquatic fauna. Arroyo cutting in the late 1880s drained much cienega habitat used by *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* and *Cyprinodon macularius*.
- **Introduced species:** [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), introduced for mosquito control, is associated with near-invariable extirpation or severe reduction of native fishes, probably through competition. [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) is widespread and its detrimental effects are often inferred onto native suckers.
- **Extreme environments:** Some pupfish tolerate temperatures from near freezing to 104 °F in one day, and *C. macularius* tolerates salinity about twice that of seawater near the Salton Sea. Other species, such as *Moapa coriacea* (87–95 °F springs) and *C. diabolis*, are highly specialized to stable warm habitats and are vulnerable to change.
- **Speciation drivers:** Temperature and salinity influence meristic and morphological traits, including fin-rays, scale rows, dorsal fin position, body size, and pelvic fin reduction. Some traits appear genetic and others environmental.
- **Isolation:** Desert springs and drainages act as natural experiments in evolution. [[Devils Hole]] *C. diabolis* has fluctuated between 50 and 400 individuals since its discovery in the late 1890s and has been isolated for many thousands of years.
- **Lower Colorado and Gila rivers:** Between 1854 and 1950–55, seven native freshwater fishes disappeared while marine-derived *Elops affinis* and *Mugil cephalus* became abundant, likely reflecting clearer water, stabilized flow, and increasing salinity.

## Key Findings

1. **Historical use:** Large species such as the squawfish, bonytail, humpback sucker, and cui-ui were important food sources for Indians and early settlers. Commercial harvests were significant: more than six tons of humpback suckers were taken by one fisherman at Sahuaro Lake in 1949, and in December 1964 one 60-foot seine haul at the Salt River caught over 6,000 suckers weighing about two tons.
2. **Population collapse:** The Utah Lake cutthroat trout and Pyramid Lake cutthroat trout are extinct or extirpated. Utah Lake sucker numbers fell below commercial levels during the 1934–35 drought before partly recovering. Native squawfish disappeared from much of its former range by the late 1940s.
3. **Sport and bait value:** The squawfish is a formidable sport fish that is nearly unknown to anglers. Native and introduced fishes are in demand as bait, supporting a growing bait-fish industry with some hatcheries. The author recommends trophies and light-tackle sport fishing for native species.
4. **Sustainable yield:** Suckers provide low-trophic-level protein production (feeding on algae, detritus, and midge larvae) and could support limited commercial harvests for food, pet food, or fertilizer. Research on this potential is described as badly needed.
5. **Basic research value:** Pupfish and other desert fishes illuminate genetic drift, environmental effects on morphology, and past drainage connections. Live-bearing *Xiphophorus* species from Mexican deserts have been central to melanoma research and histocompatibility studies.
6. **Ecological indicators:** Changes in fish assemblages signal habitat change. The longfin dace's abundance may indicate degraded conditions. In the Ohio River, a 1959 closure of steel mills coincided with a rise from 9 to 21 fish species in a lock chamber, showing rapid faunal response to pollution reduction.
7. **Bioassay and sensory uses:** Fish are used to assess waste toxicity (Doudoroff and Katz). Some fishes detect certain substances at extremely low concentrations, including alcohols at about 43 × 10⁻⁹ mg/L for European *Phoxinus* and another compound at 1 × 10⁻¹¹ mg/L for salmon.
8. **Esthetic and educational value:** Native fishes support sport, aquarium keeping, nature education, and demonstrations of biodiversity. The author argues that the "cut-out-and-get-out" approach to natural resources must be replaced by management for sustained yield.
9. **Management priority:** The chapter concludes that native fishes, along with their esthetic value, are specific resources deserving careful management and perpetuation.
