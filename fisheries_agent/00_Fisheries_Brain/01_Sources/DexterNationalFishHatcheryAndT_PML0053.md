# Dexter National Fish Hatchery and the Recovery of Southwestern Imperiled Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This Endangered Species Bulletin article (1997) by Linda Andreasen, a Fishery Biologist with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) Division of Fish Hatcheries, and Buddy Jensen, Manager of the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]], describes how one facility shifted from stocking nonnative game fish to recovering listed native fishes. The article argues that habitat loss, dams, water diversion, groundwater pumping and nonnative species have driven many [[Southwest Native Fishes]] toward extinction. It reports on captive propagation, reintroduction and cooperative recovery work, and it stresses that long-term success depends on balancing human water demands with aquatic habitat needs.

## Background & Description

The article opens with national context. The number of federally listed threatened or endangered species has roughly doubled over the past decade. About 107 threatened or endangered fish species are listed in the United States. Under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA), nearly 30 percent of listed animal species in the continental States are aquatic, and the figure is reported as rising to 46 percent when shellfish (mussels and clams) are included.

Dexter National Fish Hatchery was established in 1932 to produce warmwater game fish for the Southwest. Recovery work on listed species began in 1978, after surveys in the 1970s showed that the region held a large share of imperiled fishes. Roughly one-third of the nation's listed fish species occur in the Southwest. The facility is located in the [[Pecos River]] Valley of southeastern New Mexico, on the northern fringe of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]].

Dexter has biological and coordination responsibilities beyond the Pecos River. These include four other FWS Southwest Region ecosystems:
- Upper/Middle [[Rio Grande]]
- Lower Rio Grande
- [[Gila River]], Salt and Verde Rivers
- Lower [[Colorado River]]

These ecosystems share watersheds and endangered-species concerns with Mexico.

Dexter holds 15 listed species, drawn from nine U.S. States (TX, NM, AZ, CA, NV, UT, CO, WY and OR) and two Mexican States (Chihuahua and Sonora). The species table lists scientific names, families and federal status codes: E (Endangered), T (Threatened), a species of concern, and one species extirpated from the U.S. The main species are:
- **Catostomidae:** [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Warner Sucker]], [[Yaqui Sucker]]
- **Cyprinidae:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Chihuahua Chub]], [[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]], [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]], [[Woundfin]], [[Beautiful Shiner]]
- **Ictaluridae:** [[Yaqui Catfish]]
- **Cyprinodontidae:** [[Leon Springs Pupfish]], [[Desert Pupfish]]
- **Poeciliidae:** [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Big Bend Gambusia]]

The Colorado squawfish is described as North America's largest minnow, weighing up to 80 pounds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article describes the Southwest's waters as changed dramatically over the past 100 years. Many rivers in this arid region have been dammed and diverted to serve human settlement, and scores of unique freshwater ecosystems were altered or destroyed. Some organisms have declined in both numbers and range, and others have disappeared entirely. Habitat destruction and nonnative species have taken a heavy toll on native fishes, from small live-bearing fishes of the Poeciliidae to the Colorado squawfish.

Several threats are specific to particular fish groups:
- **Colorado River fishes** evolved in once-muddy, turbulent water and are adapted to swim it with humped or ridged backs, winglike fins and streamlined bodies. They now compete with more than 100 nonnative species that suit the dammed reservoirs and clear connecting streams.
- **Desert spring fishes** are losing habitat as groundwater pumping lowers water tables and often dries springs.

Captive "genetic refuges" at Dexter are meant as short-term measures. They hold populations until habitat restoration and removal of nonnative species make reintroduction possible.

The article also notes that the [[National Fish Hatchery System]] comprises 65 hatcheries, 5 Fish Technology Centers and 9 Fish Health Centers. Thirty-three of these facilities work on coordinated restoration for 39 imperiled species.

## Key Findings

- **Recovery program:** Dexter began listed-species recovery in 1978 and has shifted from stocking nonnative game fish to restoring native species. Few facilities in the world house as many rare and endangered fish.
- **Current holdings:** 15 listed species are held and cultured, from nine U.S. States and two Mexican States.
- **Techniques:** Ongoing work includes establishing captive gene pools, [[Cryopreservation]] of fish sperm, refining spawning and culture methods, diet studies, captive broodstock, [[Genetic Fingerprinting]] of stocks, and producing fish for reintroduction.
- **Chihuahua chub rediscovery:** The species was thought to be extirpated in the U.S. until a small remnant population was found in the Archuleta/Moreno Springs of New Mexico. Dexter's captive propagation and stocking then reestablished another population nearby. The extant population in Chihuahua, Mexico, continues to decline due to habitat loss.
- **Desert pupfish and Gila topminnow:** Numerous populations were reestablished at sites in Arizona over the past 10 years using fish reared at Dexter.
- **Colorado squawfish in the San Juan River:** Genetically diverse captive broodstocks were established cooperatively by FWS and the States. Many recently stocked fish (NM, CO, UT) overwintered successfully. [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] monitoring found greater survival than expected, with fish eating and growing well.
- **Leon Springs pupfish:** The last wild population lives in a headspring in an active oil and gas field. A cooperative effort among private landowners, an oil company, a gas company, the Natural Resources Conservation Service, the Texas Parks and Wildlife Department and FWS built an earthen dam to protect it from oil spills. A refugia population was also established. In 1995, the wild population was hybridized when someone illegally introduced the nonnative sheepshead minnow (*Cyprinodon variegatus*). Dexter now maintains the last remaining population of pure Leon Springs pupfish.
- **Partnerships:** Dexter coordinates with FWS, other federal agencies, States, Tribal, university and private-sector partners, and Mexican States of Chihuahua and Sonora.
- **Conclusion:** The authors argue that future recovery depends on a balance between human water demands and the habitat needs of fish and wildlife. Public outreach and partnerships are increasing support for the work, and the program has implications for endangered-fish management throughout the United States.
