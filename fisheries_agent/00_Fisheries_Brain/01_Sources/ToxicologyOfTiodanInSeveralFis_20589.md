# Toxicology of Thiodan in Several Fish and Aquatic Invertebrates

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (Investigations in Fish Control No. 35, January 1970) by Richard A. Schoettger, a fishery biologist with the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], evaluates [[Thiodan]] (the trade name of [[Endosulfan]]), a chlorinated organochlorine insecticide, as a potential fish control agent. The study measured acute toxicity to [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Western White Sucker]], [[Daphnia Magna]] and [[Damselfly Naiad]] nymphs, tested whether Thiodan harms trout eggs, examined how water hardness and pH alter toxicity, and traced uptake, storage and metabolism in fish using carbon-14 labeling and chemical assays.

The central conclusion is that Thiodan is highly toxic to fish, with median tolerance limits (TLm) of 0.3 to 8.1 ppb, but has little value as a selective piscicide because it kills rough fish and game fish alike. It can serve as a general fish toxicant where selectivity is unimportant. Residues persist in edible muscle tissue, and alkaline water (pH 8.4 to 9.4) degrades its toxicity within days.

## Background & Description

Fishery managers use chemicals to remove unwanted fish when seining, drawdown, draining or biological control fail. At the time, the main piscicides in the United States and Canada were [[Rotenone]] and [[Toxaphene]]. Thiodan had been applied experimentally to four small Canadian lakes, where it was highly toxic to fish, broke down faster than toxaphene and cost less than rotenone, but was possibly harmful to plankton and bottom fauna.

Thiodan belongs to the [[Organochlorine Insecticides]] group, which also includes aldrin, dieldrin and endrin. These compounds are persistent; toxaphene-treated waters can stay toxic for 2 to 3 years. The Department of the Interior had banned persistent chemicals in its waters. The author notes alternative agents such as [[Guthion]], an organophosphate, and [[Antimycin A]] (Fintrol), which degrades rapidly.

The technical material was 96.4 percent pure, with A and B isomers melting at 108 to 109 °C and 206 to 208 °C. Thiodan is insoluble in water and stable in sunlight, but hydrolyzes to sulfite and Thiodan alcohol in the presence of moisture, bases or acids. The carbon-14 labeled tracer (5.91 µCi per mg) came from the manufacturer. The work was based on a 1966 Colorado State University doctoral dissertation and was supported by the National Institutes of Health.

Earlier studies by Adlung (1957), Ludemann and Neumann (1960 to 1962) and Cuerrier (1960) were judged insufficient under the criteria set by [[Lennon and Walker]] (1964). The stated objectives were to determine toxicity to additional species, the effect of pH and water quality on toxicity, and uptake and metabolism in fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Test organisms and conditions.** Fish were held in aerated tap water at 10 °C, fasted for 48 hours and acclimated for 24 hours before trials. Trout and suckers were tested in 20-liter glass aquaria, naiads in 6-liter vessels and Daphnia in 1-liter plastic vessels of reconstituted water. Test temperatures were 1.5 and 10 °C for trout, 10 and 19 °C for suckers and Daphnia, and 8 and 19 °C for naiads. Standard tap water had a pH of 7.4 and total hardness of 45.0 ppm as calcium carbonate.

**Environmental persistence.** Thiodan degraded more slowly in cool water. Degradation at alkaline pH was temperature dependent, and a 2-hour exposure to 50 ppm of Thiodan was not toxic to fertilized trout eggs.

**Non-target impacts.** Field trials killed frogs and aquatic insects. Plankton declined but later recovered. The author considered that a concentration of about 50 ppb, as used in one field trial, could harm Daphnia populations.

## Key Findings

**1. Acute toxicity to fish (TLm, ppb, 24 to 120 hours):**
- Rainbow trout at 1.5 °C: 5.9 to 0.7 ppb. At 10 °C: 2.1 to 0.3 ppb.
- Western white suckers at 10 °C: 8.1 to 2.5 ppb. At 19 °C: 6.6 to 2.8 ppb.
- Toxicity generally increased with warmer temperatures and longer exposure, rising significantly up to about 72 to 96 hours.
- Extrapolated 100-percent lethal concentrations were about 3 to 5 ppb for trout and 8 to 10 ppb for suckers.

**2. Invertebrates are more resistant:**
- Daphnia magna: 68.0 to 53.5 ppb at 19 °C and 178 to 47.5 ppb at 10 °C. Extrapolated 100-percent lethal concentration was about 90 to 100 ppb.
- Damselfly naiads (Ischnura sp.): 235 to 62 ppb at 8 °C and 275 to 75 ppb at 19 °C. Extrapolated 100-percent lethal concentration was about 425 ppb, and naiads were more sensitive at cold temperature, an unexpected result attributed possibly to older test animals.
- The abstract states that fish were "at least seven times more susceptible" than invertebrates, while the body text says Daphnia were "at least six times" more resistant than suckers or trout. Both figures are reported as published.

**3. Symptoms and recovery:** Poisoned fish first became hyperexcitable and swam rapidly, then surfaced, lost equilibrium, sank and developed erratic opercular movements. Trout darkened and suckers mottled. Most surviving trout died within 5 to 7 days after transfer to clean water. In the carp study by Ludemann and Neumann, effects were largely irreversible, although one carp recovered after exposure to 14 ppb.

**4. Mammalian comparison:** Thiodan is among the more toxic chlorinated insecticides to fish but one of the least toxic to mammals. Reported rat oral LD50 values ranged from 40 to 50 mg/kg (corn oil) to 110 mg/kg (cottonseed oil), and rabbit dermal LD50 was 359 mg/kg. In carp, technical Thiodan was not lethal at 119 to 234 mg/kg, while 24% miscible Thiodan killed at 49 and 195 mg/kg. The author suggests Thiodan is absorbed more readily through gills and oral membranes than through the intestine.

**5. Field and other reported toxicity:** A 46 ppb application to a 27-acre pond killed all minnows, perch, sunfish, bullheads and suckers within 7 days. Cuerrier (1960) removed several fish species from a lake at 15 ppb. Adlung (1957) killed goldfish at 10 ppb in 20 hours, and Ludemann and Neumann recorded a 48-hour LC50 of 11 ppb for carp.

**6. Selectivity:** Concentrations lethal to rough fish such as carp and suckers were also lethal to trout and northern pike. Thiodan is therefore unsuitable as a selective piscicide. It could still be used where game fish are a small share of the population, provided it does not harm other organisms and breaks down quickly.

**7. Trout eggs:** Fertilized rainbow trout eggs tolerated 30- or 120-minute exposures to 10 to 50,000 ppb without reduced hatching success (control hatch 70.8 to 76.6%, treated 68.2 to 94.4%). Higher concentrations could not be tested because Thiodan precipitated out of solution. Continuous exposure was more damaging: Berger (unpublished, personal communication) found 750, 500 and 250 ppb killed trout fry in 7, 12 and 20 days, and 100 ppb killed all treated fish by 30 days. Antimycin A killed eggs at 10 ppb, which indicates a different mode of action.

**8. Water quality:**
- Calcium and magnesium salts at 500 ppm hardness did not change toxicity to suckers (survival similar; statistical probability 0.81).
- Mildly acidic conditions (pH 6.4) had no significant effect in the early tests, though a later stored-solution test suggested slight degradation.
- Alkaline conditions detoxified the compound. Solutions at pH 8.4 aged 24 and 48 hours caused average mortalities of 60% and 15%, indicating a three- to five-fold loss of toxicity. Nearly all toxicity was lost within 24 hours at pH 9.4, with mortality in only 4 of 32 potentially toxic solutions and none above 20%.
- Berger (personal communication) found 9 days were needed to detoxify 10 ppb at pH 9 and 12 °C, so cooler water slows detoxification.
- Thiodan hydrolyzes to Thiodan alcohol, which is probably far less toxic to fish.

**9. Uptake and tissue deposition (radioactive tracer):**
- Western white suckers exposed to 20 ppb of 14C-labeled Thiodan at 19 °C accumulated mean residues of 1.1 to 8.4 µg/g dry tissue. Highest levels were in liver (8.4), gut and feces (5.8), and blood (4.4); muscle was lowest (1.1).
- Fish exposed to 80 ppb died within 2.25 to 9.25 hours. Their tissues held roughly 2 to 3 times the residues of the 20 ppb fish. Liver averaged 22.9 µg/g.
- Muscle held 33% of the added Thiodan at 20 ppb and 14% at 80 ppb, suggesting saturation of muscle lipids.
- Radioactivity in liver, gut, blood, heart, gill, kidney and brain was significantly correlated with exposure time (r = 0.67 to 0.97), but muscle and skin were not (muscle r = 0.29).
- The radiotracer study was halted after about six months when the labeled stock was found to have degraded to Thiodan alcohol.

**10. Metabolism and biliary excretion:** Water-soluble metabolites in gallbladder bile of northern creek chubs and western white suckers made up 94 to 100% of bile radioactivity. Bile concentrations ranged from 88 to 158 µg/g (chub) and 36.5 to 48.0 µg/g (sucker). Incubation with [[Beta-Glucuronidase]] converted the metabolite to a benzene-soluble form, identified as a glucosiduronic acid conjugate of Thiodan alcohol. The proposed pathway is oxidation to Thiodan sulfate, conversion to Thiodan alcohol, conjugation with glucuronic acid, biliary release and fecal excretion. This pathway parallels reported mammal and house fly metabolism.

**11. Goldfish subacute study:** Ten goldfish (96 to 337 g) were exposed daily to fresh 7 ppb solutions.
- The two smallest males died on days 4 and 5. Females died up to day 13. The two largest fish survived 20 treatments.
- Weight correlated with number of treatments before death (r = 0.89). Muscle lipid content correlated with survival (r = 0.77).
- Muscle residues ranged from 0.95 to 2.54 µg/g wet weight. Mean residues were 1.67 µg/g in muscle, 2.48 in skin, 1.67 in liver-associated samples, 12.76 in abdominal fat and 3.40 in bile.
- Muscle lipids appeared to store and dilute Thiodan in heavier fish, but mortality was more closely tied to the total amount in muscle.
- Thiodan was adsorbed onto scales and fins. Gonad residues were 0.67 to 0.73 µg/g wet weight, raising concern about effects on egg fertility.

**12. Human health implication:** Residues in edible muscle suggest that fish exposed to Thiodan are probably unfit for human consumption.

**13. Research gaps:** The author notes conflicting literature on whether fish can form glucosiduronic acid conjugates, and recommends further work on kidney involvement and on constant-flow or renewed-exposure tests, which may reveal greater toxicity than static tests.

**Summary conclusion:** Thiodan is nontoxic to trout eggs and highly toxic to fish of all ages after hatching. It is less toxic to most invertebrates. Its residual toxicity can be minimized by applying it just before increases in water basicity, but it is not suitable as a selective piscicide.
