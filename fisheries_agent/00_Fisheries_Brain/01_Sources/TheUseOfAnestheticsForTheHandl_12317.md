# The Use of Anesthetics for the Handling and the Transport of Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #fish-physiology #anesthesia

## Executive Summary

This 1960 research paper by William N. McFarland from the [[Institute of Marine Science]], [[University of Texas]], Port Aransas, provides comprehensive experimental evidence for the use of chemical anesthetics in fisheries operations. The study demonstrates that properly induced anesthesia (specifically deep sedation, Stage I-2) significantly prolongs survival time in sealed containers by reducing metabolic oxygen consumption and suppressing stress responses to external stimuli. Testing multiple anesthetic compounds on [[Fundulus parvipinnis]] (killifish) and [[Girella nigricans]] (opaleye), McFarland establishes that anesthetized fish groups survived 12-13% longer than controls under non-stimulated conditions, with dramatically greater benefits when fish were subjected to external disturbances. The research provides practical dosage recommendations for operational procedures (tagging, marking, weighing) and transport applications, identifying tertiary amyl alcohol, methylparafynol, and chloral hydrate as optimal agents. Recovery from anesthesia was complete without long-term effects, and the study establishes theoretical limits suggesting 2-3 fold increases in transportable fish loads are achievable through metabolic suppression.

## Background & Description

### Historical Context and Motivation

The investigation addresses increasing demands on fisheries resources requiring improved fish handling techniques. Prior work by Aitken (1936), Reese (1953), and Calhoun (1953) suggested anesthetics could benefit fish transport, but experimental demonstrations remained limited. McFarland's research provides the first comprehensive physiological framework for anesthetic use in fisheries.

### Anesthetic Process in Fishes

The study establishes that behavioral responses during anesthesia follow a predictable sequence across diverse species and chemical agents, paralleling patterns in higher vertebrates. This indicates sequential suppression of discrete central nervous centers. Key findings include:

- **Tolerance**: Fish tolerance to anesthetics is narrow; increasing minimal dosage by 10-15 fold causes death
- **Control**: Specific depths of anesthesia can be maintained for 12-48 hours through careful concentration manipulation
- **Dosage-depth relationship**: Increasing concentration by 1.5-2.0 fold advances anesthesia by one level
- **Molecular weight correlation**: Narcotic potency increases with molecular weight

### Classification of Anesthetic Stages

The paper presents a detailed classification system (Table 1):

**Stage 0**: Normal - reactive to stimuli, normal equilibrium and muscle tone

**Stage I-1**: Light sedation - slight loss of reactivity to visual and tactile stimuli

**Stage I-2**: **Deep sedation** - total loss of reactivity except to strong pressure, slight decrease in opercular rate

**Stage II-1**: Partial loss of equilibrium - partial loss of muscle tone, swimming disrupted, increased opercular rate

**Stage II-2**: Total loss of equilibrium - total loss of muscle tone, decreased opercular rate

**Stage III**: Loss of reflex reactivity - total loss of reactivity, very slow respiratory and heart rates

**Stage IV**: Medullary collapse - respiratory movements cease, followed by cardiac arrest

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Experimental Species and Collection Sites

**[[Fundulus parvipinnis]]** (California killifish): Collected from sandy point and slough in south [[San Diego Bay]], San Diego County, California. Size range 31.5-77.5 mm standard length (0.52-12.5 grams).

**[[Girella nigricans]]** (opaleye): Obtained from intertidal reef at [[Portuguese Bend]], Los Angeles County, California. Size range 34.5-73.0 mm standard length (0.75-11.6 grams).

### Acclimation and Holding Conditions

Fish were maintained in 20°C copper-treated seawater (0.10 ppm copper) for two weeks prior to temperature acclimation. This treatment healed capture damage and removed ectoparasites. [[Fundulus]] groups were acclimated to 18-21°C and 24-27°C ranges for minimum two weeks. [[Girella]] were acclimated to prevailing ocean temperatures (14.6-18.8°C). Fish were fed chopped mackerel and shrimp four times weekly, with feeding discontinued 24 hours before experiments.

### Physiological Factors in Transport Mortality

The study identifies six primary factors limiting fish transport capacity:

1. **Dissolved oxygen** - primary limiting factor in sealed-system experiments
2. **Carbon dioxide and acidity** - pH declined from 7.65-7.85 to mean 7.10-7.19; CO₂ increased from 3-6 ppm to 12.4-18.6 ppm
3. **Waste products** - organic nitrogen increased fourfold but remained non-toxic; inorganic nitrogen (ammonia) increased negligibly (maximum 0.09 ppm, far below lethal levels)
4. **Temperature** - affects metabolic rate and anesthetic efficacy
5. **Excess activity** - causes oxygen debt and lactic acid accumulation
6. **Physical damage** - from collision with tank walls during hyperactivity

Oxygen deficiency proved the primary cause of mortality in closed-system experiments. Mean oxygen at 50% mortality: 0.25 ml/L for [[Girella]] (range 0.15-0.40), 0.22 ml/L for [[Fundulus]] (range 0.11-0.62).

## Key Findings

### Operative Procedures: Anesthetics for Immobility

**Recommended Anesthetics for Tagging, Marking, Weighing**:

- **[[M.S. 222]]** (methane tricainesulfonate): 0.02-0.030 grams/gallon - fastest recovery, most desirable for operations requiring rapid handling
- **Tertiary amyl alcohol**: 6-7 ml/gallon - rapid induction, oily liquid
- **Methylparafynol** (Dormison): 3.5-4.5 ml/gallon - rapid induction, easily dispensed

**Concentrations for 12-hour Deep Anesthesia** (Table 2):
- Urethane: 13+ grams/gallon (not recommended due to carcinogenic properties)
- Chloral hydrate: 9.0-11.5 grams/gallon
- Sodium amytal (saltwater): 0.45-0.65 grams/gallon
- Chlorobutanol: 0.03-0.04 grams/gallon
- Tribromoethanol: 0.02-0.025 grams/gallon

**Recovery Characteristics**:

Recovery was complete when anesthesia did not exceed Stage III (loss of reflex reactivity). In six experiments where Stage IV (medullary collapse) was induced but not prolonged beyond 2-3 minutes, recovery was complete in all but one fish. Recovery time varied by anesthetic:

- **M.S. 222**: Fastest recovery (10-13 minutes from Stage IV)
- **Tertiary amyl alcohol**: Rapid recovery (20 minutes from Stage I-2)
- **Chlorobutanol**: Moderate recovery (51-100 minutes depending on concentration and exposure)
- **Urethane**: Slow recovery (80-180 minutes)

**Long-term Effects**: No adverse long-term effects observed. Nineteen [[Girella nigricans]] and 29 [[Clinocottus analis]] (wooly sculpin) collected with methylparafynol gained 10.1% body weight over 21 days (11.5% for Girella, 9.3% for Clinocottus). [[Clinocottus]] deposited eggs in aquarium, indicating normal reproductive function. Six large opaleyes (600-1,300 grams) were anesthetized 27 separate times over three months with no mortality or apparent effects.

### Transport Applications: Metabolic Suppression and Survival

**Closed-System Mortality Experiments**:

Groups of fish were sealed in one-gallon jars under controlled conditions and allowed to deplete oxygen to lethal levels. Time to 50% mortality served as the experimental endpoint.

**[[Girella nigricans]] Results** (Figure 1):
- Anesthetized groups (pooled data from tertiary amyl alcohol, methylparafynol, chlorobutanol, M.S. 222) lived 12-13% longer than controls
- Regression analysis: Y = (9.94)X⁻⁰·⁸⁴⁶ for anesthetized vs. Y = (8.82)X⁻⁰·⁸⁴¹ for controls
- Difference between means significant at p < 1%
- No stimulation applied; benefits attributed solely to reduced basal metabolism

**[[Fundulus parvipinnis]] Results - Low Temperature (18-21°C, Figure 2)**:

With external stimulation applied, anesthetized groups showed dramatic increases in survival time:

- **Chlorobutanol** (1 grain/gallon): Y = (19.13)X⁻⁰·⁸³⁸ - most effective
- **Tertiary amyl alcohol** (2 ml/gallon): Y = (16.74)X⁻⁰·⁸³⁸
- **M.S. 222** (1 grain/gallon): Y = (14.79)X⁻⁰·⁸³⁸
- **Methylparafynol** (1 ml/gallon): Y = (14.45)X⁻⁰·⁸³⁸
- **Stimulated controls**: Y = (11.64)X⁻⁰·⁸³⁸

Chlorobutanol-treated groups survived 64% longer than stimulated controls (19.13/11.64 = 1.64). All differences significant at p < 1%.

**[[Fundulus parvipinnis]] Results - High Temperature (24-27°C, Figure 3)**:

- **Tertiary amyl alcohol** (2 ml/gallon): Y = (8.610)X⁻⁰·⁶⁰⁶
- **Methylparafynol** (1 ml/gallon): Y = (7.904)X⁻⁰·⁶⁰⁶
- **Non-stimulated controls**: Y = (8.111)X⁻⁰·⁶⁰⁶
- **Stimulated controls**: Y = (6.112)X⁻⁰·⁶⁰⁶

Anesthetized groups performed equivalently to non-stimulated controls, demonstrating complete suppression of reactivity to external stimuli. This represents a 41% increase over stimulated controls (8.610/6.112 = 1.41).

**M.S. 222 Temperature Lability**: At high temperatures (24-27°C), M.S. 222-treated [[Fundulus]] lost sedation after 30-60 minutes and became as reactive as stimulated controls, contrasting with effectiveness at lower temperatures. This indicates thermal instability of this anesthetic.

**Sodium Amytal Ineffectiveness**: Three groups treated with 4 grains/gallon sodium amytal showed no difference from controls, attributed to calcium antagonism of this anesthetic in seawater.

### Recommended Anesthetics for Fish Transport

**Most Desirable Compounds** (intermediate potency, readily soluble, calcium-compatible):

1. **Tertiary amyl alcohol**: 2.0 ml/gallon - oily liquid, maintains sedation 24+ hours
2. **Methylparafynol** (Dormison): 1.5-2.0 ml/gallon - oily liquid, easily measured
3. **Chloral hydrate**: 3.0-3.5 grams/gallon - solid requiring weighing, slower induction

**Not Recommended**:
- Ethyl alcohol, tertiary butyl alcohol (low potency)
- M.S. 222 (thermally labile, especially at high temperatures)
- Sodium amytal (calcium antagonism in hard/marine water)
- Chlorobutanol, tribromoethanol (excessive potency requiring precise measurement)
- Urethane (carcinogenic, low potency)

### Pretreatment Requirements

**Critical Finding**: Anesthetic pretreatment is essential for maximum effectiveness. Six control groups of [[Fundulus]] introduced to tertiary amyl alcohol without the standard one-hour pretreatment showed no statistical difference from non-anesthetized controls. 

**Recommended Protocol**: Minimum 1-15 minutes pretreatment, preferably one hour, before transport. Visual indicators of sedation (reduced swimming, loss of reactivity) do not correspond to immediate metabolic suppression. Black et al. (1939) demonstrated that mere handling can cause maximum oxygen uptake for several hours.

### Theoretical Load Capacity Increases

**Maximum Theoretical Limit**: If fish metabolize at maximum rates during transport (4-5 times basal metabolism), and anesthetics reduce metabolism to basal levels, loads could theoretically increase 4-5 fold.

**Practical Limit**: 2-3 fold increases more realistic due to:
- Reduced water volume per fish at higher loads
- Increased CO₂ and ammonia concentrations
- Non-proportional increases in aeration efficiency
- Mechanical limitations of transport systems

**Empirical Validation**: Reese (1953) and Calhoun (1953) reported doubling and occasional tripling of normal trout transport loads using sodium amytal, consistent with practical estimates.

### Concept of "Level of No Excess Activity"

The study applies Fry's (1947) concept of "level of no excess activity" (where active respiration equals basal metabolism) to fish transport. Key insights:

- **Oxygen depletion** naturally restricts fish activity during transport, but creates hazardous conditions near lethal thresholds
- **Anesthesia advantage**: Achieves "no excess activity" while maintaining oxygen, CO₂, and ammonia at safe levels, removing the hazard of limiting factors
- **Deep sedation** (Stage I-2) produces metabolic rates approaching basal levels without equilibrium loss or involuntary movements

### Prevention of Hyperactivity Damage

Research by Black (1955, 1957a,b,c, 1958) demonstrates that fish forced to exercise to fatigue accumulate high lactic acid levels and incur oxygen debts requiring hours for recovery, often with incomplete recovery or mortality. 

**Transport Applications**:
- Fish are hyperactive during initial transport phases (Haskell, 1940b)
- Post-planting mortality common even at non-maximal loads
- Sedation prevents physiological damage from hyperactivity
- Reduces self-inflicted physical damage from collision with tank walls
- Combined with rounded tanks and mild orienting currents, enables transport of highly active species like [[Thunnus germo]] (albacore)

**Albacore Transport Trials** (September 1956, [[Marineland of the Pacific]]): Several albacore were successfully sedated with tertiary amyl alcohol (2.0 ml/gallon for light sedation; 10-15 ml/gallon for deep anesthesia). Deep anesthesia (Stages II-2 and III) induced in 1-3 minutes. Fish became limp and easily handled. Recovery of equilibrium occurred within 5 minutes after transfer to transport tank. Anesthetized albacore initially swam more slowly and avoided tank walls compared to non-anesthetized specimens.

**Pacific Mackerel Recovery** (A.W. Ebeling, personal communication): [[Pneumatophorus diego]] anesthetized in 0.3-0.4% tertiary amyl alcohol (Stage II-2 to III) regained equilibrium and swimming within 5-15 minutes. However, schooling behavior remained abnormal for 30 minutes, attributed to persistent mild sedation affecting position maintenance within the school.

### Dosage Precision and Safety

**Narrow Tolerance Window**: Increases of 1.5-3.0 fold above sedation-maintaining concentrations induced partial loss of equilibrium (Stage II-1), detrimental for transport. Webb (1958) reported that doubling M.S. 222 dosage from 0.1 to 0.2 grams/gallon when carrying excessive bluegill loads caused equilibrium upsets and higher mortality.

**Temperature and Size Effects**: Dosages require adjustment for temperature and fish size. Higher temperatures generally require lower concentrations due to increased metabolic rates and anesthetic uptake. Larger fish may require proportionally less anesthetic per unit weight.

### Methodological Innovations

**Experimental Design**: One-gallon sealed jars in temperature-controlled aquarium with individual compartments and viewing ports. Variables measured:
- Dissolved oxygen (Alsterberg-Winkler method, 1.66% titration error)
- pH (Beckman Model N portable pH meter)
- Alkalinity (acid titration to pH 4.5, 1.8% error)
- Carbon dioxide (calculated from pH and alkalinity using nomographs)
- Nitrogenous wastes (modified Kjeldahl method)

**Oxygen Correction Factor**: Since initial oxygen content varied (2.95-5.16 ml/L), a correction factor was applied based on the experimental demonstration that oxygen depletion was a linear function of time, independent of oxygen tension above 0.5 ml O₂/L.

**Statistical Analysis**: Covariance analysis used to compare regression lines of time to 50% mortality versus mean fish weight. Log-log plots produced linear relationships with correlation coefficients from -0.84 to -0.99.

**Behavioral Observations During Stimulation**:
- **Anesthetized fish**: Hovered over bottom or sat quietly, no reaction to light flashing
- **Control fish**: Descended rapidly to bottom, increased pectoral fin movements, higher metabolic rates
