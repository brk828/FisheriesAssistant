# Acquisition of Habitat Preference Data by Radiotelemetry

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ptychocheilus

## Executive Summary

This paper is one chapter from the proceedings of a 1985 workshop, published by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1988 as Biological Report 88(11), on developing and evaluating [[Habitat Suitability Criteria]]. Author [[Harold M. Tyus]] assesses [[Radiotelemetry]] as a way to collect microhabitat data on fish in large, turbid rivers, using a case study of [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the [[Green River]] of Utah. The study implanted 92 fish with radio transmitters between 1980 and 1985 and compared the resulting habitat data with [[Electrofishing]] collections. The paper concludes that radiotelemetry is a strong method for habitat use data in conditions where visual observation is impossible, although tracking success falls in high-conductivity water.

## Background & Description

The proceedings compile 22 papers presented at a workshop held at [[Colorado State University]], Fort Collins, in December 1985. The workshop addressed study design, professional judgment, field data collection, data-to-criteria conversion, and field verification. Its purpose was to improve the reliability of habitat suitability criteria, which are central to the [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) and [[PHABSIM]] modeling.

Underwater radiotelemetry had been used mainly for fish movement studies, not microhabitat work. Earlier telemetry relied on ultrasonics, which are affected by water temperature, turbulence, sediment, and air entrainment. Radio transmitters avoid these problems but lose signal strength in water, especially water of high [[Conductivity]]. Radio transmission in salt water is practically impossible.

The paper has two parts: a theoretical background on fish radiotelemetry, and a case study evaluating tracking success, habitat use, and data partitioning. The case study covers a radiotelemetry program on Colorado squawfish, a large migratory predator, in the Green River. The Green River flows through long, flat, shallow reaches (under about 10 ft in much of the study area) and has conductivities ranging from about 200 to 2,000 µmho.

### Equipment and Methods

- **Transmitters:** Four module types were tested. Two were "antennaless" surgical modules from AVM (mercury batteries, types C and D) and two from Smith-Root (lithium batteries, types A and B). Modules were coated in beeswax and implanted intraperitoneally by surgery.
- **Sample:** 92 Colorado squawfish were implanted across 1980–1985. Type A n = 25, B n = 9, C n = 43, D n = 15. Nine razorback suckers were also implanted but were excluded from the comparisons.
- **Rated life:** Types A, B, C, and D were rated at 150, 300, 180, and 550 days respectively.
- **Receivers:** Smith-Root RF-40 and SR-40 units tuned to 40.500–40.700 MHz. Whip antennas were used for searching, and loop or Yagi antennas for pinpointing.
- **Tracking:** Fish were tracked weekly, mainly from two boats drifting downstream, with some fixed-wing aircraft tracking.
- **Habitat measurement:** Habitat was recorded when a fish stayed in one spot for 30 minutes. Depth, velocity (measured at 0.6 of depth), and substrate were recorded at the signal source, and later at inshore and offshore points (added from 1984).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Radiotracking success depended on water conditions. Tracking was marginal at conductivities of 400 µmho or more. Performance declined sharply above 700 µmho, where high water conductivity and low water levels in summer reduced signal strength. Water conductivity during the May–September tracking seasons (1980–1985) averaged about 540 µmho. Temperatures ranged from 0 to 25 °C. Winter temperature drops near 0 °C caused transmitter frequency and pulse-rate deviations, and in some cases caused tracking failure.

Colorado squawfish were easier to track than [[Razorback Sucker]] because they used shallow habitats most of the time. Razorback suckers used deeper habitats in spring and fall, making them harder to follow, and larger batteries could not be used on the smaller suckers (under about 500 mm).

Spawning took place in July and August. Fish used a two-part pattern:

1. **Resting-staging habitat** in deep pools or large shoreline eddies, where fish rested, fed, and where males gathered around females.
2. **Deposition-fertilization habitat** in riffles, where eggs were laid and fertilized, with brief movements to cobble bars between the two.

Outside spawning, adults were found in eddies and runs along shorelines, often over sand and silt, and at drop-offs next to sand bars. Visual observations in shallow water showed fish sheltering behind boulders or other cover. Movements suggested heavy use of the eddy-run interface.

## Key Findings

- **Radio performance:** Radiotelemetry was used in conditions where visual observation was impossible, and was judged the least biased method for habitat use in these large rivers because gear selectivity is avoided and the same fish can be followed for long periods.
- **Battery life:** Observed field contact averaged 47.3% of theoretical battery life. The range differed between the four radio types, and there was no significant difference in average battery life between the lithium and mercury modules.
- **Module size:** Radios were under 1% of average fish body weight and about 10% of fish length. Module-to-body-weight ratios stayed below 1.5%, which is consistent with the low weight ratio used in other studies.
- **Tracking success:** The overall performance ratio of 47.3% was compared with other investigators' work. Wick et al. (1983) reported a ratio of 49.6% for 12 fish in the Yampa River, a smaller tributary with lower conductivity.
- **Growth and retention:** Recaptured implanted fish showed no significant growth difference from nonimplanted fish of the same size. Transmitter expulsion was not observed, which the author attributes to the module-to-weight ratio and beeswax coating.
- **Electrofishing bias:** Habitat data from 101 electrofished Colorado squawfish (1980–81) differed significantly from radiotelemetry observations. Habitat distributions differed (chi-square, P < 0.001), as did substrates (P < 0.005) and depths (91 fish, mean 1.23 m, SD 0.55, vs. 244 radiotelemetry observations, mean 1.40 m, SD 0.79; P < 0.04). Velocities did not differ (P > 0.5). The author attributes the bias to electrofishing gear, which moves or herds fish.
- **Scale of habitat heterogeneity:** At 84 fish locations, depths and velocities differed between inshore and offshore points (inshore mean depth 1.46 m vs. 1.85 m offshore, P < 0.02; offshore velocity 0.53 m/s vs. 0.25 m/s inshore, P < 0.002). This suggests that point measurements at fish locations may not represent the mean depths and velocities used as inputs to [[PHABSIM]] models, so more data points across the channel may be needed.
- **Habitat use by season:** Radiotelemetry allowed habitat use to be partitioned into migration, spawning, and resident periods. No data were collected during migration.
- **Overall conclusion:** The author concludes that radiotelemetry gives habitat data that are unbiased and representative. A large number of contacts allows habitat use to be divided by season and by behavior, which improves the interpretation of habitat use in heterogeneous rivers.
- **Methodological recommendations:**
  - Choose equipment suited to high-conductivity water and use the most suitable antennas.
  - Use receivers with both search and tracking capabilities, since programmable receivers require the exact transmitter frequency.
  - Evaluate growth rates to confirm that implants do not alter behavior.
  - Collect more hydrologic information than is typical in physical habitat studies, because heterogeneous habitats require it.
