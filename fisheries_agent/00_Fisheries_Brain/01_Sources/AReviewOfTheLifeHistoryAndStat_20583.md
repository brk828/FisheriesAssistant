# Review of the Life History and Status of the Desert Pupfish (Cyprinodon macularius)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This review by Allan A. Schoenherr (Fullerton College), presented at the *Desert Ecology 1986* symposium, compiles what is known about the [[Desert Pupfish]] and its allies and documents a serious population decline. The species was once common in the [[Gila River]], the [[Lower Colorado River]], and the [[Salton Sea]] region. Human activity, including dam building, water diversion, groundwater pumping, pesticides, and invasive vegetation, reduced its range. The most rapid losses came after the introduction of non-native fishes, which act through predation, aggression, and disruption of reproduction. A final rule listing the species as endangered was published on 31 March 1986 and took effect on 30 April 1986, after about nine years of effort. The paper is intended to support a recovery program and government management plans.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** *Cyprinodon macularius* is the only officially named desert pupfish species. The [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]] is a distinct but unnamed species, and it is now extinct. Several forms are recognized:

- *C. m. macularius*: populations in California and the lower Colorado River.
- *C. m. eremus*: the [[Quitobaquito Spring]] population, described by Miller and Fuiman (1987).
- The Rio Sonoyta population, which may warrant subspecies status.

Electrophoretic genetic distance between Salton Sea and Santa Clara Slough fish is 0.014, which the author considers normal within-drainage variation. Distance between Quitobaquito and Salton Sea fish is 0.042. Morphology indicates larger differences than electrophoresis does.

**Morphology.** Males grow to 73 mm total length (TL) and become dark blue with yellow tails during breeding. Females and juvenile males are tan to olive with 5 to 8 disrupted vertical bars. Scales number about 26 along the lateral line. Teeth are tricuspid and incisor-like, and fin ray counts are given for each fin type.

**Historical distribution.** Around 1900 the species occupied sloughs and marshes along the lower Colorado River as far north as Blythe, California, and the San Pedro and Santa Cruz rivers in Arizona. Remnant spring populations in the Salton Trough were left by Lake Cahuilla, which dried around A.D. 1500. The 1905 flood that formed the modern Salton Sea reintroduced the fish. By the 1960s they were very common in Salton Sea shoreline pools.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiological tolerances (Table 3 summary).**

- Minimum dissolved oxygen: 0.13 mg/L, the lowest recorded for any fish. Fish bury into oxygen-free, hydrogen sulfide-rich mud.
- Critical thermal maximum: 44.6 °C (Quitobaquito), the highest recorded for a fish. Fish were found in water at 41 °C in nature, and a 48.9 °C spring record is cited from Jordan and Richardson (1907).
- Thermal minimum: 7 °C.
- Salinity: eggs 0 to 70 g/L, juveniles 0 to 90 g/L, adults 0 to 70 g/L. *C. milleri* from Cottonball Marsh tolerated 14 to 160 g/L.
- Optimal growth: freshwater at 15 to 20 °C, seawater at 25 to 35 °C.

Only fish acclimated to high temperatures can handle the metabolic cost of osmoregulation in hypersaline water, which explains why pupfish are among the few fishes in many desert localities.

**Reproduction.** Breeding runs from April or May through August in variable-temperature habitats and year-round in constant warm water. Females lay 1 to 4 eggs per spawning event, 50 to 800 eggs per season, and up to 20 eggs per day. Eggs hatch in about 10 days at 20 °C. Hatchlings are 4 to 5 mm TL. Maturity comes at about 15 mm SL, after about 1 year in variable temperatures or 2 to 3 months in constant warm water. Maximum age is 3 years.

**Breeding systems.** Dominant males defend territories. In small, low-density habitats, a "consort pair" system is used instead. Males recognize their own spawn by odor and sometimes eat the spawn of rivals. [[Sneak Mating]] by subdominant males, which resemble females, adds variation to the system.

**Diet and feeding.** Pupfish are opportunistic bottom feeders. They eat chironomid midge larvae, detritus, algae, aquatic vegetation, snails, and occasionally their own eggs. In rice fields they consume cladocerans, coleopteran larvae, and mosquitoes. The author notes that pupfish may outperform [[Gambusia Affinis]] for [[Mosquito Control]] because they forage throughout the water column. Feeding and resting behavior are closely tied to temperature. Daily movements in Salton Sea pools track temperature and food processing.

**Flooding and drying.** Pupfish have lived with natural flood and drought cycles for millions of years. Floods tend to eliminate non-native fishes more than native pupfish. Pupfish can also recolonize after near-total drying, with eggs hatching after about a week of dry-bed rest.

## Key Findings

**Population status.** Only two natural populations are of reasonable size: [[Santa Clara Slough]] in Sonora, Mexico (the largest), and [[Quitobaquito Spring]] in Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona. Other populations are unstable or small. These include [[San Sebastian Marsh]] (designated critical habitat), upper [[Salt Creek]], and [[Rio Sonoyta]]. The Table 1 counts for known populations (October 1987) are:

| Locality | Native | Introduced |
|---|---|---|
| Salton Sea | 2 | 7 |
| Santa Clara Slough | 5 | 6 |
| Quitobaquito Spring | 1 | 4 |
| Rio Sonoyta | 3 | 1 |
| Monkey Spring | 0 | 0 |

These are minimal counts, because private-property populations are suspected but unreported.

**Salton Sea decline.** Pupfish made up less than 5% of fish caught in 1979 and 0.2% in 1983. In 1983, 6 pupfish were caught in 40 drains. In 1987, 3 pupfish were found among 1,128 fish caught in drains near Niland.

**San Sebastian Marsh.** Populations fluctuate sharply with flooding. Pupfish were absent from 1983 to 1986 and were introduced again in 1985 without success. Pupfish reappeared after floods and were present with [[Tilapia]] and mosquitofish by 1987.

**Causes of decline (Table 2 summary).**

- **Habitat loss:** Dams on the Gila (Roosevelt, 1910; Coolidge, 1929) and the lower Colorado (Hoover, Parker, Imperial) dried sloughs. Irrigation drains were bulldozed and concrete-lined.
- **Groundwater pumping:** A proposed jojoba farm upflow of San Sebastian Marsh was abandoned for reasons unrelated to pupfish, and Mexican agricultural pumping threatens Quitobaquito and Rio Sonoyta.
- **Salinity:** The Salton Sea refuge near Niland reached about 100 g/L and lost its fish in 1981. Projected brine from the Yuma desalination plant could raise Santa Clara Slough from 32 to about 90 g/L.
- **Pesticides:** Fish at Quitobaquito contained parathion and DDT, and 1976 fish kills were attributed to parathion. Thirteen pesticides are authorized near San Sebastian Marsh.
- **Invasive vegetation:** [[Tamarisk]] is among the most serious threats because of its high transpiration. Cattails have covered drains and marshes. Bulrushes nearly covered the Palm Spring refuge, which was cleared in 1985.
- **Introduced fishes:** Of 65 North American desert fishes, more than two-thirds have declined while interacting with non-natives. Nearly 50% of introduced southwestern fishes are piscivores. Largemouth bass ([[Largemouth Bass]]) caused the extinction of the Monkey Spring population. [[Sailfin Molly]] and [[Tilapia]] displaced pupfish in the Salton Sea, and Tilapia zilli reduced pupfish in shallow water. Golden shiners and mosquitofish were introduced into refugia.
- **Hybridization:** Introductions of genetically distinct stocks could swamp local genomes. The Rio Sonoyta subspecies is an example.
- **Disease:** A nematode infected 13 of 58 Quitobaquito fish (22%). The author calls this a serious level for a small refugium. A 1987 fish kill was caused by gas bubble disease from a leaking pump, and about 850,000 channel catfish were destroyed at the Imperial Valley hatchery in 1987.

**Recovery recommendations.** The author concludes that the species' survival depends on a series of small refugia managed by state and federal agencies. The recommendations are to protect habitat from groundwater pumping, pesticides, tamarisk, and salinity change; to prevent further introductions and use only native-range genetic stock; to monitor for pathogens; and to implement a recovery program soon.
