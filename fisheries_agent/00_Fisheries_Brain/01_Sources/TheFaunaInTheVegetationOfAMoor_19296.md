# The Fauna in the Vegetation of a Moorland Fishpond

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-invertebrates #community-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1965 paper by T. T. Macan, published in *Archiv für Hydrobiologie*, describes the animal fauna living in the vegetation of [[Hodson's Tarn]], a small, electrolyte-poor moorland fishpond in the English [[Lake District]]. The study covers the years up to 1960, a period when the tarn held essentially no fish, prior to the planned introduction of 500 [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) to examine predation effects. Sampling used a [[Pond Net]] and a quantitative [[SCS Sampler]], supplemented by emergence frames and a floating cage. The fauna is dominated by mayflies, caddisflies, corixids, snails and newts, and the author concludes that species partition the tarn by depth, with a sharp difference between shallow marginal and deep-water communities.

## Background & Description

Macan frames the study around three basic needs: identifying species present, documenting life histories of common species, and tracking how numbers vary across places, life stages and years. The tarn measures about 4427 m² in extent and reaches 3 m depth, and is described as a typical artificial moorland fishpond, poor in electrolytes though not extremely acid. Four vegetation types were recognised:

- **Emergent** beds, chiefly [[Carex Rostrata]], with patches of [[Eleocharis Palustris]] and [[Glyceria Fluitans]] at the edge.
- **Floating-leaved** bed of [[Potamogeton Natans]], expanding over the period.
- **Sward** of [[Littorella Uniflora]] in water down to about 1 m, sometimes exceeding 1000 plants/m².
- **Milfoil** stands of [[Myriophyllum Alterniflorum]] in deeper water, thinning late in the period.

Temperature records (thermograph installed 1957) show strong year-to-year variation in the timing of spring warming. The water level dropped about 25 cm during dry spells because of a leak in the dam.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sampling was carried out at 11 stations initially, later 13. Net sampling was non-quantitative, while the SCS sampler was quantitative but slow, so total SCS catches were small. Collections moved from fortnightly (1955) to monthly (1956–1957) and then to three seasonal collections per year (1958–1960). Emerging adults were monitored with wooden frames (1956–1957) and then a weekly floating cage (from 1959).

Key ecological points:

- **Competitive exclusion:** Macan tests the [[Competitive Exclusion Principle]] using congeneric pairs. For example, *Enallagma cyathigerum* and *Pyrrhosoma nymphula* have similar feeding and life histories but partition the tarn by oviposition depth.
- **Life-history classes:** Development takes more than one year (e.g., Pyrrhosoma, Enallagma, Aeshna, Erpobdella), one year (summer or winter species), or less than one year (e.g., Cloeon, which may run two generations in warm summers).
- **Predation context:** Eels (*Anguilla*) could not be excluded, though only a few were believed present.
- **Non-fish fauna:** Chironomidae were not identified; microscopic Protozoa were ignored; Entomostraca and water mites were treated in separate studies.

## Key Findings

1. **Habitat partitioning by depth:** The fauna splits into a shallow marginal community (e.g., *Corixa castanea*, *Leptophlebia*, *Pyrrhosoma*, *Deronectes*) and a deeper community (e.g., *Triaenodes bicolor*, *Holocentropus dubius*, *Cloeon simile*, *Enallagma*). *Nymphula nymphaeata* larvae were confined to *Potamogeton natans*.
2. **Dominant taxa:** *Leptophlebia* spp. were among the most numerous organisms in the tarn. Table 8 estimates about 8.7 million Leptophlebia in September 1958 across the tarn, the largest total of any species.
3. **Life histories:** *L. marginata* emerges about a month earlier than *L. vespertina* (peak emergence in late May for the latter's season). *Cloeon simile* emerges several weeks earlier than *C. dipterum* and has an autumn generation.
4. **Emergence and hatching:** Adult *Leptophlebia* emerge from about mid-April to late June. Hatching of *Cloeon dipterum* adults in 1960 started in early June and lasted two months.
5. **Sampling efficiency:** The net caught about 4 times more *Limnaea pereger* than the SCS (the best test organism, because it cannot cling or flee), but 27 times more *Corixa castanea* and 8 times more *Enallagma*, while *Erpobdella* was 4.5 times more abundant in the SCS. The author estimates the net sampled about 1500 cm² per 4375 cm² of the sampler's equivalent area.
6. **Year-to-year variation:** Population size depends more on conditions during development than on the number of eggs laid. Several species showed large fluctuations (e.g., the big 1957 *Pyrrhosoma* year-class, linked to a low water level exposing Littorella for oviposition).
7. **Community composition:** The list of casual species was short (table 5), suggesting that invaders quickly leave an established stable community.
8. **Macrophyte preference:** Littorella hosted the largest summer numbers, but no shallow-water plant was clearly better than others as a harbourage for fish food across all seasons.
9. **Comparison with Grib Sø:** Autumn totals in the tarn were somewhat higher than the Danish [[Grib Sø]] reedswamp and Fontinalis figures (about 4500/m² in Fontinalis and 1000/m² in reedswamp), though spring and summer were similar.
10. **Production pattern:** Most species were summer growers. Winter growers, such as *Leptophlebia*, contribute most in winter and provide fish food at times when other food is scarce. The author argues that a tarn dominated by summer growers overwintering as eggs (e.g., *Lestes sponsa*) would not support fish.
11. **Future work:** Weighing specimens by length class would allow biomass and production calculations, which had not yet been done.
