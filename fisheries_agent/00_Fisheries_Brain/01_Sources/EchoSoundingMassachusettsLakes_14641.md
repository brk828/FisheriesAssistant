# Echo Sounding Massachusetts' Lakes, Ponds, and Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1952 methods paper by [[Richard H. Stroud]] and [[Frank Grice]] of the [[Massachusetts Division of Fisheries and Game]] describes how a portable [[Echo Sounder]] replaced handline sounding in the state's [[Statewide Lake and Pond Survey]]. Handlining gave inaccurate average depths and unreliable maximum depths and was labor-intensive. Echo sounding let field crews map bottom contours quickly and accurately. In 1951 the survey covered 108 ponds, nearly four times the earlier pace, with only a 60 percent personnel increase, saving an estimated $40,000. The paper also gives a practical description of the instrument, its mounting, field procedures, and contour map construction.

## Background & Description

The [[Statewide Lake and Pond Survey]] of biological conditions in public-access waters began in summer 1942 and has run every year except 1943. It covers about 400 waters, usually larger than 20 acres, averaging about 85 acres. About 65 percent are under 100 acres, 95 percent are under 500 acres, and the largest surveyed is 1,721 acres. Only one exceeded 100 feet in maximum depth. Roughly 60 percent are under 25 feet deep, 90 percent under 50 feet, and 98 percent under 75 feet.

From 1942 through summer 1950 the survey averaged 31 water units per year, with a five-man field party each summer. Pressure from sportsmen's groups to finish sooner led to a review of the 1950 operations, which identified ways to speed up and improve the work.

The paper's main subject is the [[Echo Sounder]], a portable instrument similar to the one described by [[Burrows 1949]]. It is mounted on a 12-foot skiff and powered by a 6-volt automobile battery, with a transformer that supplies 110-volt, 60-cycle alternating current. A later editorial note reports a newer lightweight echo sounder on the market, which the editors believe needs a doubled recording scale for fresh-water work.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Accurate depth data matter for both management and angling. Average depth is important for [[Lake Reclamation]] and chemical treatment planning, and maximum depth and the locations of drop-offs, bars, and depressions guide management actions and angler advice. Handline averages were "highly erroneous," and maximum depths were found only by accident. Hand soundings could not reliably locate these features without costly effort.

Bottom conditions affected readings. On flocculent muddy bottoms the sounder's indicator band broadened and sometimes "searched." The leading edge of the band was taken as the top of the muddy layer. Dense rooted aquatic vegetation caused similar searching, though reflections from the true bottom also occurred. In shallow water (less than about 15 feet) operators needed some experience to read the dial. Checks with a sounding chain found no errors after familiarization.

## Key Findings

- **Handlining was inadequate.** Two boats with two-man crews needed half a day for an average-sized water, and larger lakes took a full day or more. Average and maximum depth results were unreliable.
- **Echo sounding improved speed and efficiency.** In 1951, two four-man field parties covered 108 ponds, a nearly fourfold speed increase with only a 60 percent personnel increase. The authors estimated savings of about $40,000 over three additional seasons.
- **Data quality improved.** The 1951 season yielded "far more voluminous and detailed information" on environment and fish population per unit surveyed than any previous year.
- **North Pond case study.** In North Pond, Berkshire County, handlining gave an average depth of about 19 feet, while echo sounding gave about 12 feet. The pond was reclaimed with 360 pounds of 5-percent [[Rotenone]] powder. Using the handline figure would have wasted about 210 pounds of material.
- **Instrument specifications.** The transducer operates at 400 soundings per minute with a three-stage tuned amplifier and three receiving units. It is housed in a "fish" about 2 feet long and 4 inches square, suspended about 6 inches below the surface on brass rods clamped to the gunwales.
- **Ruggedness.** The sounder survived rock strikes at full speed and rough roads, and the spare-parts kit was not used in more than 100 ponds over a couple of months. Early adjustment of the keying contact was needed but largely disappeared after a few weeks.
- **Field procedure.** One operator handled the equipment, boat, and data, averaging three ponds per day, including travel between ponds. A 6-volt battery lasts about 8 hours of continuous sounding, so operators alternated two batteries.
- **Transect design.** Surveys used about 12 transects per pond on average, ranging from 5 to 63 depending on shoreline and bottom irregularity. Runs began in water less than 5 feet deep. Start and end distances from shore were estimated visually up to three boat-lengths (about 12 feet each), beyond which positions were plotted on the map.
- **Contour map construction.** At constant boat speed, travel time is proportional to distance, so the points where each 5-foot contour is crossed can be plotted on transects by simple proportion. Smoothed curves through these points produce the final map. One master map can be traced into many copies.
