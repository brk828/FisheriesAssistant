# Biochemical Genetics, Cytogenetics and Morphometrics of the Colorado River Gila Complex

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document, authored by Mark J. Rosenfeld (Department of Biology, University of Utah), reports three linked studies on the endangered chubs of the genus *Gila* in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The first uses [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] to build a biochemical genetic database for the Colorado River *Gila* complex. The second is a preliminary karyotype investigation with banding trials. The third is a [[Principal Component Analysis]] of morphometric variation among [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]] collections. The document also includes an internal U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum forwarding the cytogenetic report. The main conclusion is that the taxa are morphologically distinct but show little fixed genetic divergence, so genetic and morphological evidence remain in tension. Chromosome and morphometric data suggest diagnostic characters that could aid identification, especially for juveniles.

## Background & Description

The systematics of the *Gila* complex have long been unresolved. Species were originally described from very few specimens, and [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*G. elegans*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*) are often confused where they co-occur. *G. robusta* has been split into the widespread Colorado River form (*G. r. robusta*) and the Lower Basin endemic [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] (*G. r. seminuda*). Intermediate "intergrade" morphs are common in the Upper Basin, and their genetic status is unknown. Hybridization has been proposed as the origin of *G. r. seminuda*.

Three sets of material are reported:

- **Allozyme study:** Tissues were drawn from 94 Upper Basin and 63 Lower Basin fish, plus 26 [[Gila Bicolor Newarkensis]] from a Great Basin spring in Newark Valley, Nevada, used as an outgroup. Samples came from the [[Colorado River]] mainstem, the [[Green River]], [[Little Colorado River]], [[Gunnison River]] hybrids and [[Lake Mojave]] hatchery stock. Specimens were obtained over 1983–1987 under the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Some fish were sacrificed; [[Westwater Canyon]] humpback chubs were sampled by non-lethal muscle biopsy and then released.
- **Cytogenetic study:** Karyotypes were prepared from juvenile *G. cypha* (Black Rocks and Little Colorado River), *G. elegans* (Lake Mojave) and sub-adult/adult *G. robusta* (Gunnison River). Giemsa staining and [[Silver-Formalin Staining]] for [[Nucleolar Organizing Region]]s (NORs) were used.
- **Morphometric study:** Photographs of 261 chubs were digitized using box-truss and traditional measurements. The sample included 157 fish from 1985 Green River collections, 90 from Black Rocks, one questionable "bonytail" morph, and 13 preserved fish from Horseshoe Canyon collected in 1953.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document places all three studies within a conservation setting. Habitat degradation and dams have reduced *Gila* numbers, and *G. cypha*, *G. elegans* and *G. robusta seminuda* are protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973). *G. elegans* is nearly extinct in the wild; the Lake Mojave stock is described as the only known non-breeding population. Damming has removed suitable habitat, and the author notes that the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure followed a rotenone treatment of the Green River that killed fish at least 160 km downstream and eliminated aquatic insects. Such events are offered as possible causes of the low genetic diversity observed in some populations. Dam construction (including Lake Powell) is also suggested as a possible driver of morphological shifts between 1953 and 1985 samples.

Biological context for the taxa includes small desert spring habitats (*G. bicolor newarkensis*), which are associated with bottlenecks and genetic drift, and larger, more open river habitats, where that species is more variable (unpublished data cited by the author).

The memorandum adds an agency perspective. Reintroductions were beginning, and the Fish and Wildlife Service sought to preserve the genetic integrity of hatchery and wild stocks. It suggests fin-clip tissue culture as a non-lethal method for a possible reference collection.

## Key Findings

**Allozyme genetics**

- Of 23 presumptive loci assayed, 7 were monomorphic across all OTUs and 16 varied.
- Polymorphism in Colorado River *Gila* ranged from 20% to 36% (excluding hybrids), at or above the 20% average reported for fishes in general (Nevo et al., 1984). *G. bicolor newarkensis* was 12% polymorphic.
- Average heterozygosity (H) ranged from 0.02 to 0.09 among Colorado River *Gila* (excluding hybrids) and was 0.02 for *G. bicolor newarkensis*. The *G. elegans* estimate was 0.04, based on 11 of 24 known animals.
- *G. bicolor newarkensis* was well separated from all Colorado River OTUs (more than 0.50 distance units in the liver-only analysis) through fixed differences at Adh-A and Icdh-1.
- No loci were fixed for different alleles between the morphologically recognized species. Taxa were separated mainly by allele frequency differences, so the author concludes that taxonomic differences are based on incomplete allelic differentiation.
- Diagnostic-leaning alleles were noted: Aat-1(b) and Cabp-1(c) in *G. cypha*; Pgm-A(b) fixed in Little Colorado River *G. cypha*; Mdh-2(b) in the hybrids; and Icdh-2(c) at higher frequency in *G. elegans*. These are unconfirmed because sample sizes were small.
- Some taxa were electrophoretically indistinguishable, including Desolation Canyon and Westwater Canyon *G. cypha* and Upper Basin *G. robusta robusta*. This may reflect recent genetic mixing.
- The author argues that divergence is probably underestimated, given the small number of loci, small sample sizes, and limits of starch gel electrophoresis. For example, Little Colorado River *G. cypha*, Lake Mojave *G. elegans* and Black Rocks *G. robusta robusta* shared the same Ldh-B electromorph on starch gels.

**Cytogenetics**

- The modal chromosome count was 50 for all populations, representing at least 63% of scored spreads and taken as the true diploid number. At least 200 cells were scored per population, and about 40 spreads were measured per population.
- Chromosome no. 19 is submedian in *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* but median in *G. robusta*. Differences were significant (*G. robusta* vs. *G. cypha*: t = 5.3, df = 78, p < 0.001; *G. robusta* vs. *G. elegans*: t = 8.7, df = 78, p < 0.001). The author proposes this as a possible means to separate juvenile *Gila* spp.
- A NOR doublet or triplet on the short arm of chromosome no. 9 is shared by all Colorado River *Gila*, and a NOR on chromosome no. 3 is also shared. The author describes the chromosome 9 character as the first known instance in fishes, though its phylogenetic meaning is unclear.
- Black Rocks and Little Colorado River *G. cypha* differ in NOR features on chromosome no. 7. The silver-formalin results are described as preliminary and not yet quantified. A Costia infection killed the laboratory *G. robusta* stock.
- Standard Giemsa staining cannot give unambiguous karyotypes; G- and CT-banding are needed, and CT-banding is related to G-banding. Fin-tissue cell culture is proposed for non-lethal sampling.

**Morphometrics (PCA)**

- 31 measurements were analyzed. The first three axes explained 87% of variance (PC1 76%, PC2 7%, PC3 4%). PC1 is interpreted as a size axis.
- On PC2 and PC3, head and mid-body measurements separated the chubs. Measurements of mid-body and head proportions had diagnostic value. Nuchal hump and caudal peduncle measurements did not appear necessary for identification.
- Black Rocks fish separated into a discrete "roundtail" cluster, with "humpback" and unidentified morphs together. Separation was not absolute.
- Westwater Canyon fish sorted less cleanly; most fell in the humpback-unidentified group, and at least five fell in the roundtail cluster.
- Whirlpool Canyon (Green River) specimens were exclusively roundtail-like. Coal Creek specimens clustered as humpback-intermediate.
- Differentiation appeared size-dependent; smaller fish of each form resembled one another.
- Morphological shifts between 1953 and 1985 samples are suggested but caveated, because preserved and live fish were compared.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- The author's conclusions are hypotheses for testing by biochemical methods: (1) roundtail chubs form a distinct lineage; (2) intermediate morphs and humpback chubs belong to one species; (3) hybridization between these lineages is rare or non-existent; (4) the taxonomic status of the bonytail morph is unclear; (5) most Green River sites are dominated by humpback chubs; (6) Whirlpool Canyon is inhabited exclusively by roundtail chubs.
- Further sampling is needed to confirm allele-frequency differences, and to add loci for samples where only subsets were assayed.
- Methods that may improve differentiation include [[Karyotype Analysis]] with banding, [[Morphometric Box-Truss]] shearing to remove size effects, and mitochondrial DNA analysis.
