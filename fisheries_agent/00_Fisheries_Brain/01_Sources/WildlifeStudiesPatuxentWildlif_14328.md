# Effects of Pesticides on Fish and Wildlife - 1964 Research Findings

#wildlife-toxicology #pesticides #environmental-contamination #DDT #reproductive-biology

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive report summarizes 1964 pesticide research findings by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], covering both wildlife studies (conducted at [[Patuxent Wildlife Research Center]] and [[Denver Research Center]]) and fishery investigations. The document represents the fifth annual report under Public Law 85-582 (August 1, 1958), which authorized the Secretary of the Interior to undertake comprehensive studies on pesticide effects on fish and wildlife resources. Research focused on chlorinated hydrocarbon insecticides, particularly [[DDT]] and [[Dieldrin]], examining their distribution in ecosystems, accumulation in tissues, effects on reproduction, and acute toxicity across multiple species. The work encompassed field monitoring, laboratory toxicity testing, analytical methodology development, and experimental studies of reproductive success in contaminated populations.

## Background & Description

The research program addressed critical conservation problems including population declines in [[Bald Eagle]], [[Osprey]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], and other top predators. The peregrine falcon no longer bred in the eastern United States by 1964, bald eagles showed exceedingly low reproductive success (producing young in only 3 of 16 nests studied in mid-Atlantic States), and ospreys were rare north of [[Chesapeake Bay]]. Similar declines were documented in Great Britain for kestrels, sparrow hawks, and peregrines.

Research emphasized multiple approaches: (1) monitoring pesticide residues in important species through field collection and analysis; (2) experimental studies determining effects of specific residue levels on reproduction and mortality; (3) ecological studies of pesticide distribution in whole ecosystems; (4) development and standardization of analytical methodology; and (5) toxicological testing protocols for rating chemical hazards.

Key species studied included mallard ducks, black ducks, Canada geese, bobwhite quail, ring-necked pheasants, Coturnix quail, cowbirds, sparrow hawks, bald eagles, golden eagles, ospreys, herring gulls, robins, and various other birds. Fish toxicity studies examined cutthroat trout, bluegills, and warm-water species. Marine investigations at [[Gulf Breeze, Florida]] tested effects on phytoplankton, mollusks (oysters), crustaceans (shrimp), and juvenile fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report documented universal distribution of pesticide residues across ecosystems, from Antarctic penguins and seals (containing 1.3 to 152 parts per billion DDT metabolites in fat and liver) to birds in industrially contaminated areas (crows with up to 119.3 ppm DDT in muscle, 1,603 ppm in fat). The [[Lake Michigan]] ecosystem study revealed biological magnification: sediments contained 0.014 ppm DDT compounds, the crustacean *Pontoporeia affinis* contained 0.41-0.44 ppm (concentration factor of ~50×), fish contained 4.52-5.60 ppm (~10× crustacean levels), and herring gulls reached 2,441 ppm in body fat.

Wetland habitats received pesticide contamination from multiple sources: direct application for mosquito control, agricultural runoff into ponds and potholes, and forest treatments washing into aquatic systems. The report noted that animals found dead following treatment with one pesticide often contained multiple chemicals, complicating interpretation of cause of death.

Field studies documented pesticide persistence in various environments. DDT persisted on forage species and in forest environments. Parathion persistence and effects were studied in marsh habitats. The kinetics of pesticide poisoning in [[Dutch Elm Disease]] control areas showed DDT concentrated in the top inch of soil, but earthworm residues were eight times greater than surface soil levels. Adult robins on sprayed campus areas contained far higher residues than control areas, with peak mortality in late April.

## Key Findings

**Birds of Prey Studies:**

- Osprey reproductive success differed dramatically between Connecticut (13% success, eggs averaged 6.5 ppm DDT+DDE+DDD) and Maryland Potomac River (58% success, eggs averaged 3.4 ppm). Connecticut eggs that failed to hatch showed higher ppm values, but this was partly artifact of moisture loss in decomposing eggs—adjusted wet-weight calculations were essential to avoid bias.

- Bald eagle monitoring revealed DDT residues (primarily DDE) in muscle tissue ranging from trace amounts to 118 ppm across specimens from Alaska to eastern states. Fat residues in eight bald eagles ranged 13-363 ppm. Dieldrin was found in five of six recently analyzed specimens; three also contained heptachlor epoxide traces.

- Experimental DDT poisoning in bald eagles showed brain residues at death were remarkably consistent (despite different dosage levels and time to death), indicating brain levels are diagnostic of DDT poisoning. Histological examination of testes from DDT-fed eagles showed normal spermatogenesis at sublethal levels, suggesting reproductive failure likely occurs through other mechanisms.

- A sparrow hawk colony of 42 pairs was established at Patuxent for experimental reproduction studies, as these hawks are more suitable than eagles or ospreys for extensive testing.

**Waterfowl Studies:**

- Exploratory wing sampling of 20 specimens from 14 waterfowl species collected fall 1963 showed all but one contained DDT residues (0.03-3.90 ppm in muscle fragments). Wood duck showed highest levels (2.74 ppm in one sample).

- Black duck egg survey from Maine to Maryland (spring 1963) found DDT and metabolites in eggs from all but one of 37 clutches. Experimental studies with black ducks on diets containing 23, 10, and 40 ppm DDT were initiated December 1964 to compare reproductive success with egg residue levels.

- Canada geese samples from three Mississippi Flyway populations (Illinois, Alabama, Missouri) were collected late winter 1964 for residue analysis of muscle, fat, liver, and ovaries to investigate potential pesticide contributions to reproductive differences.

- Radioisotope-labeled DDT distributed over 4-acre Ohio marsh (0.2 lb/acre, typical mosquito control rate) was detected in water, aquatic vegetation, invertebrates, and waterfowl, demonstrating uptake and accumulation patterns.

**Ecosystem Contamination:**

- [[Lake Michigan]] Green Bay study showed biological concentration from 9.9 ppm in soil to 140.6 ppm in earthworms to 443.9 ppm in adult robin brains (dry weight), while DDT proportion declined from 74.7% to 70.9% to 6.5% as DDT converted to metabolites.

- Herring gull colony on Sister's Island, Green Bay showed extremely low reproductive success (0.41 young fledged per breeding pair vs. 0.54-1.1 reported elsewhere). Adult nesting gulls averaged 2,441±334 ppm total DDT compounds in body fat. Eggs averaged 226.8±38.2 ppm (wet weight). Of eggs laid, 29% disappeared and 38% failed to hatch (vs. 6.2-9.2% hatching failure reported in other studies). Three dead week-old chicks contained 295-428 ppm total residues in whole carcasses.

- Loon die-off on [[Great Lakes]] (approximately 3,000 deaths in 1963, possibly more in 1964) was attributed primarily to Type E botulism, but 11 specimens were analyzed for pesticides. DDT residues (primarily DDE) ranged from trace to 7.6 ppm in liver, trace to 6.4 ppm in muscle—levels far below those considered fatal based on other species studies.

- Antarctic Adelie penguins and crabeater seal (species spending entire lives in Antarctic, feeding on non-migrating prey) contained 13-152 ppb DDT metabolites in fat and liver, demonstrating global pesticide distribution.

**Toxicity and Mortality Studies:**

- Brain residue levels diagnostic of DDT-induced death were consistent across species: cowbirds averaged 66±6 ppm DDT+DDD (wet weight, range 35-99 ppm), similar to levels reported for sparrows, rats, and bald eagles. DDE appeared not critical in cowbirds, as survivors contained more than those that died.

- Delayed mortality in DDT-dosed cowbirds occurred 1, 8, 11, 15, and 28 days after removal from toxic food, with six of seven deaths following unusual disturbances (handling, netting). Dead birds showed typical DDT death condition: fat essentially gone from storage sites, no muscular emaciation, full gall bladders.

- Acute toxicity testing protocol was developed using mallard duck, bobwhite quail, and ring-necked pheasant. LC₅₀ (concentration producing 50% mortality in 5 days toxic diet plus 3 days clean diet) was adopted as standard measure. DDT was used as concurrent standard for comparison across tests and laboratories.

- 1964 acute toxicity results for bobwhite quail (LC₅₀ in ppm): endrin 6.0, toxaphene 23.7, dieldrin 26.9, aldrin 59.0, heptachlor 107, DDT 841, lindane 1,581, methoxychlor >5,000. For mallards: endrin 5.1, dieldrin 381, heptachlor 331, DDT 1,869, lindane 1,485, methoxychlor >5,000.

- Malathion field applications (0.64 and 0.43 lb/acre, some areas receiving two applications) showed no mortality in searches along 47 miles of wildlife habitat. Bird counts remained as high in treated as untreated areas. Bluebird reproduction was equally successful on treated (11 nests succeeded, 2 failed) and untreated areas (26 succeeded, 8 failed).

- Penned pheasant studies with malathion at 1, 5, and 10 lb/acre showed no deaths, no internal effects on autopsy, and fully successful reproduction the following spring (no differences from controls in egg numbers, fertility, or young survival).

**Fish and Aquatic Organism Toxicity:**

- TFM (lampricide) testing on mallard ducklings showed no mortality at 500-5,000 ppm in diet. Sixty test birds continued on same levels through January 1965, then all placed on 2,000 ppm through reproductive season. Food consumption, growth, and survival remained completely normal; seven drakes sacrificed showed grossly and histopathologically normal tissues.

- Marine organism acute toxicity studies at Gulf Breeze tested multiple pesticides. Phytoplankton showed percentage decreases in productivity during 4-hour exposure. Oyster shell growth EC₅₀ values at 96 hours were determined. Adult shrimp 24- and 48-hour EC₅₀ values (causing 50% mortality or loss of equilibrium) were measured. Juvenile fish 24- and 48-hour EC₅₀ mortality values were established.

- Experimental field studies examined: malathion effects on cutthroat trout at Jackson, Wyoming; mirex effects on bluegills at Marion, Alabama; sodium arsenite and diquat effects on bluegills at La Crosse, Wisconsin; Casoron effects on bluegills at Tishomingo, Oklahoma and warm-water fish at Denver; paraquat effects on fish at Denver.

**Analytical Methodology:**

- Comparison of acid-wash cleanup vs. acetonitrile partitioning for DDT recovery from eggs showed no significant difference in percentage recovery between methods. However, percentage recovery was significantly poorer in eggs fortified with larger amounts of chemical (100 μg/egg: 73-99% recovery; 400 μg/egg: 36-43% recovery).

- Dieldrin recovery comparison showed acetonitrile partitioning far superior to acid-wash cleanup. Field-collected specimens analyzed by both methods showed acetonitrile recovered 2.0-115 ppm dieldrin (dry weight) while acid wash recovered only 0.1-1.4 ppm from same samples.

- Transition from colorimetric methods to gas chromatography and thin-layer chromatography was nearly complete by end of 1964. Electron capture gas chromatography and thin-layer chromatography with silver nitrate reagent were primary methods, with sensitivity to 0.1 microgram for thin-layer.

- Critical methodological issue identified: decomposing eggs lose weight (especially moisture), artificially inflating ppm values. Adjusted wet-weight calculations (using egg volume × 0.8 g/cm³ to estimate original fresh weight) were essential. Bias also appeared when expressing residues as ppm of lipids, as lipids were apparently lost during decomposition.

**Research Needs Identified:**

The report concluded with principal research needs: (1) effects of pesticides in combination with each other and other pollutants; (2) residue levels in brain/tissues diagnostic of death; (3) pesticide quantities in eggs affecting hatchability and chick survival; (4) rates of residue storage and loss in bodies; (5) valid testing methods for organophosphorus insecticides; (6) how environmental/physiological stresses affect pesticide reactions; (7) behavioral effects causing parental failure; (8) quick preliminary testing methods for wider pesticide range; (9) analytical studies of whole contaminated ecosystems and food chains; and (10) sampling of declining populations suspected of pesticide effects.
