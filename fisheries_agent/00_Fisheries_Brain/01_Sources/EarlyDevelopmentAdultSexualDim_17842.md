# Razorback Sucker Early Development, Sexual Dimorphism, and Fecundity

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1975 final report to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, prepared by [[Eric S. Gustafson]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] at the [[Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory]], Arizona State University, covers the first-year task of a planned five-year life history study of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen texanus]]*) in [[Lake Mohave]]. The work addressed three topics: early embryonic and larval development, adult [[Sexual Dimorphism]], and [[Fecundity]]. The report is an internal contract document and is not a formal publication; the authors note that data will be published later with agency approval.

The study describes a 45-stage developmental series from fertilization to the juvenile stage, identifies six sexually dimorphic characters, and provides relative fecundity estimates from five females. It also reports three threats to the Lake Mohave population: reduced recruitment and isolation behind dams, bacterial disease in captive broodstock, and hybridization with a [[Catostomus]] species.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] drainage. It was once abundant in the mainstream and its larger tributaries but declined steadily from the early 20th century. The authors attribute the decline to habitat modification, including impoundments, channelization, lowered water tables, pollution, and competition from introduced exotic species. At the time of writing, the species was legally protected only in Nevada and California.

Lake Mohave was thought to hold the largest reproducing population, but in recent years all individuals encountered there were large, which may indicate poor reproductive success. The species was nearly extinct in the lower basin below [[Davis Dam]]. The first-year work was designed to provide criteria for a management program aimed at preventing the extinction of this native genus.

**Methods overview**

- **Adult collection:** Trammel nets were set in March and April 1975 at two Arizona-shore localities: approximately 66 river miles north of Davis Dam (immediately below [[Hoover Dam]]) and a cove in Cottonwood Basin, about 20 river miles north of Davis Dam.
- **Spawning and embryo rearing:** Females were injected with [[Human Chorionic Gonadotropin]] to induce egg maturation. Only one female produced eggs. About 5,000 ova were fertilized with sperm from two males. Embryos were reared at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and then at Arizona State University at a constant 20°C.
- **Observation:** Specimens were examined with a stereomicroscope (2–2,000×) and preserved in acetoformalic acid. Sampling was hourly for the first 30 hours, then every two hours, and later three times daily.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** In November 1975, 20 males and 68 females were netted at Locality 2. Standard length, fin lengths, and urogenital papilla length were measured following [[Hubbs and Lagler]] (1970). A total of 63 fish from several sources were also examined.
- **Fecundity:** Ovaries from five mature females were weighed, and egg numbers were estimated volumetrically (after [[Kandler and Pirwitz]], 1957).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population status and habitat change**

- Declines are linked to a series of anthropogenic changes, including dam construction. [[Davis Dam]] was completed in 1953, isolating a substantial razorback population within Lake Mohave and blocking upstream spawning migrations described by earlier workers.
- Historical losses of native fishes are attributed to lower mean water temperatures and desiltation of the river below Hoover Dam, combined with predaceous exotic fishes.
- Water-level fluctuations downstream destroy developing embryos along shorelines. In 1975, stranded razorback eggs were found along the banks below Hoover Dam after water levels dropped more than one meter.

**Disease and hybridization**

- More than 30 brood-stock fish taken in 1975 died at Willow Beach Hatchery from bacterial infections. Examination by [[B. D. Rosenlund]] (USFWS) revealed systemic infestations of [[Pseudomonas]] sp. and [[Aeromonas]] spp. The infections were present before the fish were removed from the reservoir.
- Field data indicate at least 10% hybridization between *X. texanus* and an undetermined [[Catostomus]] species in Lake Mohave. The authors describe the consequences for ecology and genetic stability as unknown but potentially serious.

**Life history context**

- Embryos and larvae develop rapidly in the first days after fertilization, which requires careful management in hatchery settings.
- Post-capture disease susceptibility of adults was judged to limit the feasibility of tagging studies.

## Key Findings

### 1. Early development (45 stages)

- The developmental series is divided into four phases after [[Hubbs]] (1943): embryological (fertilization to hatching), prolarval (hatching to yolk absorption), postlarval (yolk absorption to ossification of pelvic fin rays), and juvenile (pelvic fin ray ossification to mature gonads).
- Staging follows [[Balinski]] (1947) for cyprinoid fishes.
- Fertilized eggs from the 1974 cohort measured 2.9 mm in diameter. Those from 1975 measured 1.8 mm. By hatching, both sets of embryos had reached equivalent lengths.
- The embryonic phase runs from fertilization through 131 hours (Stage 27, hatching at 7.3 mm). The report summary states that hatching occurs in about six days, and that larvae begin active feeding about three days later.
- Key developmental events include blastopore closure (Stage 15, 45 hours), heart beat (Stage 22, 83 hours), circulation (Stage 24, 117 hours), and pectoral fin buds (Stage 26, 125 hours).
- In the prolarva, the mouth remains terminal until day 64. The larval stage ends on day 75 with the mouth in a ventral position.
- In the postlarva, dorsal and anal fin rays develop by day 40, pelvic fin rudiments appear by day 48, and pelvic fin rays are fully ossified by day 75 (Stage 42, 35.0 mm).
- Juveniles reach Stage 43 (scale rudiments, day 105) and Stage 45 (nuchal keel, day 227, 90.9 mm).
- Figure-referenced stage descriptions and measurements are given in the source (Figs. 2–10).

### 2. Juvenile growth

- Fish hatched in March 1974 were about two years old at the time of writing. They averaged about 150.0 mm total length, with a range of 70.0 to near 200.0 mm.
- At this stage, the fish showed no external sexual differences.
- The authors describe the growth rate as seemingly slow.

### 3. Sexual dimorphism

Six characters are sexually dimorphic:

- **Seasonal (November through June):** coloration and tuberculation.
- **Season-independent:** differential size (standard length and weight), pelvic and anal fin length, and length and morphology of the urogenital papilla.

Key differences:

| Character | Males | Females |
|---|---|---|
| Coloration (breeding) | Dark olivaceous to black dorsally; bright-orange venter; dark-pink to reddish lateral stripe | Drab olivaceous dorsally, off-white belly |
| Pelvic fin (% SL, mean) | 14.4 | 11.7 |
| Anal fin (% SL, mean) | 20.0 | 16.0 |
| Urogenital papilla (% SL, mean) | 5.5 | Not reliably readable in source |

- Males have large conical tubercles on the anal fin and lower caudal lobe. Females have lower-profile, fewer tubercles on the anal and lower caudal lobe, and rarely on the caudal peduncle. Neither sex had tubercles on pelvic or pectoral fins in females examined.
- Females are larger than males in the sampled population. Mean weights were 2.07 kg for males and 3.04 kg for females (Fig. 11). Mean standard lengths were 44.8 cm for males and 51.0 cm for females (Fig. 12).
- The urogenital papilla of females is longer, fleshier, and broader and rounder. In males it is thinner and more acuminate.
- The authors note that an earlier report of tuberculation restricted to the anal fin in males (Branson, 1961, 1966) was erroneous.

### 4. Size structure and maturity

- Lake Mohave fish sampled over a 10-year period were relatively large.
- Males: 1.38–2.90 kg (X = 2.97 kg as stated in source) and 30.5–54.2 cm standard length (X = 44.8 cm).
- Females: 2.10–6.67 kg (X = 3.04 kg) and 31.2–58.5 cm (X = 51.0 cm).
- The smallest males (30.5 cm) and females (31.2 cm) caught were mature.
- Scale annuli suggest that all fish sampled were over seven years old, but these preliminary data were not presented in the report.
- The source reports the male mean weight of 2.97 kg as outside the given male range. This is reproduced as published.

### 5. Fecundity

- Relative fecundity was expressed as eggs per cm of standard length, because the authors considered weight-based and condition-based estimates prone to spurious correlation (after [[Bagenal and Braun]], 1968).
- Egg counts from five females ranged from 74,600 to 120,000 total ova in both ovaries. Eggs per cm SL ranged from 1,680 to 2,264.
- The regression line was Y = a + bX, with b = 3.43 and a = 142.64.
- Four females had similar estimates. One had a proportionally larger ovary and substantially more eggs per cm, and the reason was not determined.
- The authors judged fecundity to be high and to increase with length or weight.
- No evidence of senility (decreased fecundity in very large fish) was found. However, the maximum recorded size of more than 7.0 kg had not been approached by recent specimens.

### 6. Management recommendations

- Maintain selected stocks of young razorback suckers in captivity to determine age at maturity and growth variation.
- Stock only fish of at least 20.0 cm to maximize survivorship.
- Obtain fish from size classes not yet sampled to improve fecundity estimates.
- Monitor hybridization with *Catostomus* closely.
- The stated overall aim is to build criteria for a management program to save the genus from extinction.
