# Proposal to Determine Ages and Patterns of Growth of Selected Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #ColoradoRiverBasin

## Executive Summary
This 1986 research proposal, submitted by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, addresses the urgent conservation problem of declining recruitment in big-river fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The core objective is to establish reliable age and growth data—primarily through **otolith analysis**—for four imperiled species: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The proposal is motivated by evidence that traditional scale-based aging methods are unreliable for these species, and that populations of [[Razorback Sucker]] consist almost exclusively of large, presumably old individuals with little evidence of recruitment. The project proposed processing ~40 fish specimens (plus up to 10 SEM preparations) for a total budget of $7,500, with a project timeline running from July 1986 to a final report by December 1987.

## Background & Description
- **Otoliths** are identified as the most reliable structure for age determination in these species, superior to cleithra, opercles, vertebrae, branchiostegal rays, fin rays, and scales (per [[McCarthy 1986]] study on [[Razorback Sucker]]).
- Scales were found to be **unreadable or inaccurate** for aging [[Bonytail]] (Lake Mohave specimens) and questionable for [[Humpback Chub]] from the Little Colorado River (Keading and Zimmerman 1983).
- Otoliths from three large [[Bonytail]] specimens showed **34 to 49 annuli**, indicating substantial longevity in this species.
- Methodology for otolith preparation: mesial sectioning via thermoplastic cement mounting and hand-grinding with fine sandpaper (Taubert and Coble 1977 technique), examined under:
  - Dissecting light microscopy (in 70% ethanol) for external features.
  - Phase-contrast microscopy under immersion oil at sectioning points.
  - Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with gold coating (Echlin 1978), if necessary.
- **Otolith radius vs. standard length regression** showed a close positive correlation in [[Razorback Sucker]], enabling back-calculation of age-size relationships using the Carlander et al. (1953) formula:
  
  `L' = C + S'/S (L - 0)`
  
  where L' = fish length at annulus formation, C = fish length at structure origin, S' = annulus radius, S = otolith radius, L = fish length at capture.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Target species occupy **big-river habitats** within the Colorado River system of western North America, historically including mainstem rivers and, in the case of [[Razorback Sucker]], lower Colorado basin reservoirs.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] populations in lower Colorado basin reservoirs demonstrated a pattern of **disappearance 40–50 years post-impoundment**, hypothesized to result from **recruitment failure** rather than simple mortality (Minckley 1983).
- Extant wild populations of [[Razorback Sucker]] are composed exclusively of **large, old individuals**, suggesting a lack of successful reproduction/recruitment over recent decades — a hallmark pattern of population senescence in altered riverine systems.
- The study implies these species are adapted to historically dynamic, large-river conditions that have been disrupted by damming and impoundment, altering natural recruitment dynamics.
- Known-age hatchery specimens from **Dexter National Fish Hatchery** (bonytail, humpback chub, Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker) served as calibration/validation standards against wild-caught individuals, linking captive-reared growth data to wild population inference.

## Key Findings
- **Recruitment failure hypothesis**: Disappearance of [[Razorback Sucker]] from reservoirs 40–50 years after impoundment supports a population-level pattern of aging without replacement, consistent with observed all-large-individual population structures.
- **Scale aging is unreliable**: Multiple independent studies (McAda and Wydoski 1980; Minckley 1983; Keading and Zimmerman 1983) found scale-based age estimates for Colorado River big-river fishes to be inaccurate or internally inconsistent.
- **Otolith validation**: [[McCarthy 1986]] cross-validated otolith-based aging against known-age hatchery [[Razorback Sucker]], confirming otoliths as the superior structure for age and growth determination, with a strong otolith radius–standard length correlation supporting back-calculation methods.
- **Extreme longevity evidence**: Otolith annulus counts of 34–49 in [[Bonytail]] specimens indicate these big-river fishes can attain remarkable ages, reinforcing concerns about long-lived populations with no younger cohorts (i.e., aging populations without recruitment).
- **Project design**: Utilizes an existing backlog of specimens collected by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, avoiding additional sacrifice of imperiled fish; involves dual otolith processing (ASU + independent collaborator) for cross-validation of results.
- **Broader implication**: Establishing reliable baseline age/growth data was viewed as essential to assessing whether **population senescence and aging out** (rather than active recruitment failure alone) is a general threat across multiple Colorado River big-river fish species, informing future conservation and management strategies for the [[Colorado River Fishes]] recovery programs.
