# Effect of Suspended Sediment on Vulnerability of Young Razorback Suckers to Predation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether turbidity affects predation on young [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered [[Catostomidae]] fish of the [[Colorado River]] basin. Laboratory trials compared larval survival under three suspended-sediment levels (0, 250, and 2,000 mg/L) against a native predator, the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and a nonnative predator, the [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*). Larvae strongly preferred clear water. In clear water they were highly vulnerable, with green sunfish consuming 99.6% and squawfish 90% of prey. Increasing turbidity improved larval survival and erased the difference between predators. The authors conclude that predation is a plausible cause of recruitment failure in [[Lake Mohave]], but that predation is unlikely to be the main limiting factor in the upper basin, where other stressors are implicated.

## Background & Description

**Study system and species.** The razorback sucker is a monotypic, endemic catostomid that can exceed 0.75 m in length and is marked by a flattened head and a pronounced keel anterior to the dorsal fin. It was historically abundant from the Green River in Wyoming to the Gulf of California. Dam construction, nonnative fish introductions, and habitat alteration have sharply reduced it. The Colorado River once supported four "big river" species, all now listed as endangered: the Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail, and razorback sucker.

**Population status (as reported).**
- Lake Mohave: about 25,000 adults (Marsh 1994; Mueller 1995). Larvae are abundant, but few larger than 20 mm have been collected. Adults are thought to be persisting from reproductive events before or soon after dam closure in 1953.
- Lake Mead: about 500 fish (P. Marsh, personal communication).
- Green River: 984 ± 190 adults (Lanigan and Tyus 1989) and 524 ± 173 (Modde et al. 1996), with values reported as 95% confidence intervals.
- Upper Colorado and San Juan rivers: only a few adults; only a few thousand larvae captured in the upper basin.

**Study design.**
- Larvae came from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and were tested only once to avoid learning effects. Water temperature was 18–23 °C on a 12 h light:12 h dark photoperiod.
- Predators were 2- and 3-year-old Colorado squawfish (about 9.0 cm TL) and green sunfish.
- Suspended sediment was [[Commercial Bentonite]] mixed into tap water at 0, 250, and 2,000 mg/L. Turbidity was reported in nephelometric turbidity units (NTU), with 250 mg/L roughly equivalent to the figure given by Chilton (1991).
- Test chambers were divided into three subchambers. Preference was tested by ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests, and predation was analysed with ANOVA and ANCOVA.
- Each predation trial used 10 larvae and a single predator, with a 10-minute exposure. The 1994 and 1995 data were statistically similar (ANOVA, p = 0.94) and were combined.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Declines and drivers.** Native fishes across North America are declining, largely through habitat alteration. Dams block movement, inundate habitat, and stabilise river physics and chemistry. Nonnative fishes compete with and prey on natives, and larvae are especially vulnerable. Many introduced species in the Colorado basin are sight feeders that eat fish larvae. Examples include [[Red Shiner]], [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Largemouth Bass]].

**The turbidity question.** Impermeable reservoirs settle out suspended sediment, so tailwater streams and reservoirs are clearer than historical rivers. Clear water may benefit visual predators by increasing their reactive distance. The "turbidity as cover" hypothesis (Gregory 1993, after Stanley et al. 1978) proposes that high turbidity reduces predation on larval fish. Elevated sediment is also linked to reduced spawning, feeding, growth, and recruitment in other species.

**Nonnative predation in Lake Mohave.** Razorback suckers begin spawning in December, earlier than other fishes in the lake, so their larvae may be the main food for larvivores in early spring. In spring 1985, 40% of green sunfish captured in Lake Mohave had each eaten more than four larval razorback suckers (Marsh and Langhorst 1988). Previous work showed that larvae moved to predator-free environments grew to maturity, which supports the predation hypothesis.

**Other stressors in the upper basin.** Selenium in eggs and adult muscle may be high enough to cause reproductive failure (Waddell and May 1985; Hamilton and Waddell 1992). Copper and zinc may also be harming reproduction (Buhl and Hamilton 1996). Lack of suitable floodplain habitat is considered the primary limiting factor for juvenile survival in the upper basin (Modde et al. 1996; Modde 1997).

## Key Findings

**1. Larvae prefer clear water.** Young razorback suckers selected clear water over both turbid treatments:
- At 0 vs. 250 mg/L, no preference was detected (p = 0.82 and 0.24).
- At 2,000 mg/L, larvae avoided the turbid subchamber (Kruskal-Wallis, P = 0.005).
- Subchamber position had no effect when water was clear (ANOVA, P = 0.63).

The authors suggest that the preference may reflect natural nursery habitats such as backwaters, tributaries, and floodplain pools, where turbulence is low and the upper water column stays clear.

**2. Clear water is highly dangerous.** At 0 mg/L:
- Green sunfish consumed 99.6% of larvae (only 1 of 2,070 survived, with 207 trials).
- Colorado squawfish consumed all but 213 of 2,130 larvae (10.0 ± 4.1% survival).
- Difference in predator success was significant (P < 0.001).

**3. Turbidity improves survival and equalises predators.**

| Turbidity | Green sunfish survival | Colorado squawfish survival | Predator difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 mg/L | 0.05% (1 of 2,070) | 10.0% (213 of 2,130 consumed, see Finding 2) | Significant (P < 0.001) |
| 250 mg/L | 47.6 ± 6.8% (985 of 2,070) | 43.5 ± 9.6% (939 of 2,160) | None (t-test, P = 0.61) |
| 2,000 mg/L | 67.7 ± 7.2% (1,367 of 2,030) | 59.8% (1,286 of 2,150) | Not significant (P = 0.22) |

At 2,000 mg/L, green sunfish success was significantly lower than at 250 mg/L (P = 0.002). Age-related survival regressions were significant only at 2,000 mg/L.

**4. Implications for Lake Mohave.** The authors argue that nonnative predation, especially by green sunfish in clear, littoral water, could eliminate larval razorback suckers in Lake Mohave, and that combined predation by centrarchids, salmonids, cyprinids, and clupeids likely accounts for total recruitment failure there.

**5. Predation is less likely to explain upper-basin failure.** Mainstem Green, San Juan, and Colorado rivers keep high suspended-sediment loads during the early-summer spawning period. Survival improved under turbidity, so predation is probably less limiting there. Selenium, metals, and loss of floodplain habitat are considered more likely causes of recruitment failure.

**Conclusion.** Turbidity mediates predation on young razorback suckers. Restoring sediment regimes and protecting floodplain habitat may be as important as controlling nonnative predators for the conservation of the [[Colorado River]] native fish community.
