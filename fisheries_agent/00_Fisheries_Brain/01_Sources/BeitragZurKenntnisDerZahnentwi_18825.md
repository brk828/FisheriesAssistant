# Odontogenesis and Dentition in Oviparous Toothcarps of the Tribe Aphaniini

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1972 monograph by Ruth Franz and Wolfgang Villwock (University of Hamburg) reports the first ontogenetic and histological study of tooth development in [[Aphanius]]-like toothcarps (tribe Aphaniini, family [[Cyprinodontidae]]). Most Aphaniini bear tricuspid teeth in a single row on each jaw. The exception, [[Aphanius (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus]], has conical teeth in two to three irregular rows and is nearly scaleless. The authors show that all studied species develop three successive tooth generations, each formed labially (in front of) the previous one, a pattern unusual among vertebrates. The first generation is always conical; in adults of typical Aphanius, the second (tricuspid) generation forms the functional dentition. The authors propose that the reduction of tricuspid teeth and scales in Aphanius (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus correlates with extreme salinity of its habitats.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic scope:** The Aphaniini are oviparous Old World cyprinodonts. The authors list nine recent Aphanius species, six non-endemic (e.g. [[Aphanius mento]], [[Aphanius dispar]], [[Aphanius fasciatus]], [[Aphanius iberus]], [[Aphanius sophiae]], [[Aphanius apodus]]) and three Anatolian endemics ([[Aphanius anatoliae]], [[Aphanius chantrei]], and [[Aphanius (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus]]).
- **Historical context:** Earlier authors ([[Ozarslan]] 1958, [[Setuk]] 1960, [[Axelrad]] and others) treated conical teeth as a subfamily marker (Fundulinae) and disputed the placement of the Anatolian forms. Subsequent kinship analysis by [[Villwock]] (1964) placed all Anatolian endemics within Aphanius, with [[Anatolichthys]] and [[Kosswigichthys]] reduced to synonyms or subgenera.
- **Material:** Laboratory-bred [[Aphanius mento]] (Ain Fashkha) and [[Aphanius dispar]] (Faluja, Iraq), preserved material of *A. dispar richardsoni* (Dead Sea), *A. anatoliae* (Cumra, Egridir), *A. anatoliae splendens* (Gölcük-Gölü), and *A. (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus* (Hazar-Gölü), plus hybrids and juvenile [[Aphanius iberus]] for comparison.
- **Methods:** Complete serial sections (7 µm for jaws, 10 µm for adult pharynx), Heidenhain azan staining, whole mounts, and binocular counts of teeth on preserved specimens.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Aphaniini inhabit fresh, brackish, coastal marine and hypersaline waters. Several Anatolian populations of [[Aphanius anatoliae]] and [[Aphanius (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus]] live in waters of extreme chemistry or at the edges of extreme salt lakes, such as the Burdur, Acı (Tuz) and Salda lakes.
- Some of these waters are reported to be poor in gastropods, and molluscs are entirely absent from some, which the authors suggest would impede calcification in resident fishes.
- The authors argue that scale reduction and tricuspid-to-conical tooth reduction may be adaptive responses to extreme salinity. This is a working hypothesis, not a tested result.

## Key Findings

**Dentition and tooth morphology**
- Teeth of Aphanius-like cyprinodonts are mostly tricuspid in one row. [[Aphanius (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus]] is the exception, with conical teeth in 2–3 rows; its counts were 16–20 large conical teeth in the outer row (Sözer 1942).
- Counts vary between individuals. Example from Sözer (1942): *A. fasciatus* 12–16 teeth per jaw; *A. chantrei* 17–18 upper and 17–20 lower.
- In the adult *A. (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus* sample (23 specimens), outer large conical teeth averaged 11.5 (dentale) / 12.1 (premaxilla) in males and 15.7 / 14.5 in females (per the table's reported means). The male sample had only eight large outer upper teeth, which the authors suggest may indicate sexual dimorphism; this is tentative.

**Tooth generations (Odontogenesis)**
- All studied Aphaniini form **three successive dentitions**. The first generation is always conical (primary teeth). The second generation is tricuspid in typical Aphanius, and the third is tricuspid as well.
- Each new generation forms **labially**, in front of the previous one. The authors note this as an exception to the lingual-to-labial direction otherwise reported in vertebrates, with parallels only in the Plectognathi (Tretjakoff 1926).
- Odontogenesis begins by day 4–5 with epithelial thickenings in the buccal and pharyngeal epithelium of *A. mento* embryos. Embryos have 5–6 conical primary tooth germs per jaw half at hatching.
- In *A. mento*, the second generation begins on 2-day-old juveniles, and the first tricuspid crowns ossify around day 4 after hatching. The third generation begins at about day 9–10 (*A. mento*) and day 7 (*A. dispar* Faluja).
- The primary (first generation) teeth are lost in typical Aphanius adults and survive only as isolated lateral teeth in juveniles. In *A. (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus* they persist alongside the secondary teeth.
- Attachment is by ankylosis to bone sockets, with the pulp continuous with the sockets' marrow spaces.

**Pharyngeal dentition**
- Pharyngeal teeth are conical in all species, with the upper and lower sets in the range of about 25–35 per bone in *A. splendens* (25–30 upper; 30–35 lower), and 25 each in *A. (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus*. Earlier Ozarslan counts for *A. fasciatus* were 30 per bone.
- The fourth ceratobranchial bears dorsally directed teeth in all Aphaniini older than ten days.

**Hybrids**
- Hybrids of *A. (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus* with scaled, tricuspid species (e.g. *A. mento*, *A. iberus*, *A. dispar*, *A. sophiae*) show variable dentition, ranging from conical to intermediate to tricuspid. The authors state that tooth shape and scale cover segregate partly independently in hybrids, consistent with polygenic control.

**Conclusions and hypotheses**
- The conical first-generation teeth are shared by Aphaniini and probably by cyprinodonts in general. Only the secondary dentition therefore carries systematic value.
- The authors propose that tricuspid form arose first in the last-forming (third) generation and was then transferred to earlier generations, by analogy with mammalian molar evolution as described by [[Remane]] (1959).
- Tricuspid teeth, which were more effective for grasping food, may have made the first generation redundant. Loss of the first generation and partial suppression of the third would follow.
- The authors hypothesize a correlation between extreme salinity, scale reduction and conical teeth in [[Aphanius (Kosswigichthys) asquamatus]]. Scale reduction is considered secondary, so tooth reduction would be a degenerative process ("negative archallaxis", [[Sewertzoff]] 1931).
- Fossil cyprinodonts (e.g. *Prolebias*, *Aelia*, *Aphanius kirgisicus* and *A. longipinnis* from the Kirgiz Miocene) are briefly reviewed. They are cited to support the view that tricuspid teeth are the evolutionarily younger condition.
