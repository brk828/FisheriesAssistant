# New Locality for the Gila Topminnow, *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* (Poeciliidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This short research note reports a new locality for the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), a small live-bearing fish whose range has contracted sharply across the [[Gila River]] system in Arizona and New Mexico. The species declined because of habitat change and an apparent inability to compete with the introduced [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish). The authors describe two previously unknown populations in adjacent springs in Graham County, Arizona, on the [[San Carlos Apache Reservation]]. They also outline a cooperative conservation effort and note that the small size of the springs makes the populations' future uncertain.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* (Gila topminnow), family Poeciliidae.
- **Historical range:** Once widespread throughout the Gila River system in Arizona and New Mexico.
- **Decline:** Range reduction is attributed to changing habitat and competition from the introduced [[Gambusia Affinis]], which was itself introduced into the region.
- **Prior record:** [[Mirckley and Deacon]] reported the last known remnant population in Santa Cruz County, Arizona, in early 1968.
- **Discovery:** Additional populations were found in early 1968 in two adjacent springs in Graham County, directly north of Bylas, on the [[San Carlos Apache Reservation]].

- **Habitat alteration:** Recent [[Phreatophyte Clearing]] in the area diverted the water to spread across the Gila floodplain. The water now reaches the river over a 3-meter embankment.

## Key Findings

1. *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* persists in two previously unrecorded springs in Graham County, Arizona, directly north of Bylas.
2. The species' historic range covered the entire Gila River system in Arizona and New Mexico, but it has been severely reduced.
3. Competition with the introduced Gambusia affinis and habitat changes are identified as the main causes of decline.
4. Gambusia occurs in the nearby Gila River but had not colonized either spring at the time of writing.
5. Habitat modification by phreatophyte clearing has altered spring hydrology and drainage.
6. The [[San Carlos Tribal Council]] and the [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]] are cooperating on efforts to perpetuate these stocks.
7. The authors judge the future of the population to be tenuous, given the proximity of Gambusia and the very small spring size and discharge.
