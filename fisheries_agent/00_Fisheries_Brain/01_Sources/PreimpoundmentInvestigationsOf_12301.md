# Pre-impoundment Investigations of the Green River and Colorado River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This completion report from the [[Utah Department of Fish and Game]] (Federal Aid Project F-4-R-6, Job V, covering August 1, 1958 to July 31, 1959) documents pre-impoundment surveys of two river systems slated for reservoir creation: the Colorado River through Glen Canyon, which became [[Lake Powell]] behind [[Glen Canyon Dam]], and the Green River above and below the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] site. The authors, Donald B. McDonald and Phil A. Dotson, assessed fish species composition, food habits, growth, bottom fauna, algae, aquatic plants, water chemistry, and fishery use. They concluded that the rivers were chemically adequate for fish but had low productivity, with turbid water, shifting sand, and scarce food limiting fish populations. The report recommended stocking game species suited to each reservoir, controlling rough fish on the Green River, and conducting long-term post-impoundment monitoring.

## Background & Description

The investigations were designed to determine present ecological conditions, predict changes after impoundment, and guide game fish stocking. Field methods included seines, gill nets, primacord and dynamite, set lines, rotenone, and handscreens. Fish were preserved in formaldehyde and stomach contents were analyzed. Bottom fauna were preserved in 70 percent alcohol and identified by the University of Utah Department of Biology. Algae were preserved in lacto-phenol and identified by Dr. Seville Flowers. Water chemistry was measured in the field using a portable kit, with dissolved oxygen by the [[Winkler Method]] (sodium azide modification in the Colorado study) and turbidity by a Hellige Turbidimeter in the laboratory.

The Glen Canyon study area covers roughly 170 miles of the Colorado River, plus tributaries such as [[Aztec Creek]], Warm Spring Creek, Escalante River, and San Juan River. The Flaming Gorge study covers the Green River within the proposed impoundment and 29 miles below the dam site to the Utah-Colorado state line, with tributaries including [[Carter Creek]], Sheep Creek, Eagle Creek, and Cart Creek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Colorado River (Glen Canyon).** The river is highly turbid and seasonally variable. Flow at Hite peaked at 61,800 cfs (June 6) and fell to 2,400 cfs (September 17). Turbidity ranged from 30 ppm (Hite, January 1959) to 380 ppm (Kane Creek, July 31, 1958). The bottom is mostly shifting sand, and aquatic plants are nearly absent. Riparian vegetation is dominated by [[Tamarix]] and willow. Tributaries are mostly intermittent, with clear spring-fed sections and muddy backwaters at their mouths.

**Green River (Flaming Gorge).** The river is shallow (mean about 3.5 feet in Wyoming) and becomes deeper in the gorge (mean near 5 feet, maximum 18 feet). Silt-sand bottoms shift constantly, with rubble and boulders in the gorge and near Swallow Canyon. Turbidity reached 2,500 ppm after an August 1959 flash flood. Riparian vegetation is sandbar willow, narrowleaf cottonwood, and salt cedar. Submerged aquatic plants are scarce.

**Water chemistry.** No conditions were found that would be deleterious to fish in either river or tributaries. Dissolved oxygen was 7.2 to 13.4 ppm in the Colorado River and 4.7 to 7.5 ppm in the Green River. Carbon dioxide was absent or trace (never above 3 ppm in the Colorado). Carbonates and bicarbonates varied widely, and pH ranged from 7.2 to 9.0 in the Colorado system and 8.2 to 8.4 in the Green River. Summer water temperatures exceeded 80°F in the Colorado system and reached 92°F in Bullfrog Creek.

## Key Findings

**Fish fauna and species records**
- 17 fish species are known from the Glen Canyon area, and 16 from the Flaming Gorge area. The Colorado River hosts a restricted fauna, while tributaries are more diverse.
- Two species were recorded in Utah for the first time: the [[Fathead Minnow]] (Glen Canyon, 1958) and the [[Southwest Plains Killifish]] (five specimens near Warm Creek, the only Utah record).
- The fathead minnow was also new to the Green River drainage in 1959.
- Native suckers ([[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Humpback Sucker]], Bluehead Mountain Sucker) and the [[Colorado River Squawfish]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] persisted, but the report notes the squawfish appeared to be declining.
- The [[Channel Catfish]] was the most abundant fish in the Colorado and San Juan Rivers.
- In the Green River, the [[Redside Shiner]] was the most abundant fish, at 49.2 percent of the river catch (excluding tributaries). One seine haul below Green River, Wyoming yielded 971 shiners.
- Flannelmouth sucker made up 14.7 percent and bonytail chub 7.3 percent of the Green River catch.
- Only one [[Largemouth Bass]] site was found (three fish in Aztec Creek), and growth was slow compared with Lake Mead.

**Growth, food and condition**
- Growth was generally slow in the Glen Canyon area. Channel catfish from 13 specimens (3.5 to 9.7 inches) were mostly slow growing.
- Flannelmouth suckers and [[Speckled Dace]] fed mainly on algae and small organisms. [[Green Sunfish]] fed on aquatic insects, bivalves and small cyprinids.
- Stomach analysis of 17 bonytail chubs (Green River, August 1959) found 3 empty stomachs. Animal material was mostly terrestrial insects, with ants forming 58.7 percent of insect items. Plant matter, mainly woody fragments, was also consumed.
- [[Condition Factor]] ("K") trends were noted for bonytail chub (K decreased with size across 44 fish), flannelmouth sucker (little change across 45 fish), and rainbow trout (decreased with size, 10 fish). Other species samples were too small for conclusions.

**Aquatic insects and plants**
- 66 genera of aquatic insects were collected. They were abundant in tributaries but sparse in the Colorado and San Juan Rivers, where shifting sand and scouring limited them.
- Stoneflies were absent from the Glen Canyon collections and sparse in the Green River.
- Impoundment was predicted to shift insect communities: midges, flies, dragonflies, beetles and bugs were expected to increase, while mayflies, stoneflies and caddisflies were expected to decline.
- Filamentous green algae (Spirogyra, Zygnema, Cladophora) dominated the Glen Canyon algal flora.

**Fish health**
- Flannelmouth and bluehead mountain suckers showed symptoms of a bacterial ulcer disease, found on no other species. Leeches were common on several species, and one bluehead sucker carried a plerocercoid of *Ligula intestinalis*.

**Trout and spawning**
- Spawning areas were scarce in both reservoirs. Sheep, Carter, Eagle and Cart Creeks were the only Utah streams with known trout reproduction, and only 3 of 9 surveyed streams below the dam were judged potential spawning streams.
- Carter Creek held the largest rainbow population, with rainbows making up over 50 percent of its sampled fish.

**Fishery use**
- Little fishing occurred on Glen Canyon's rivers; channel catfish were the principal catch. Boat trips were mainly for scenery.
- Fishing pressure on the Green River was low, but Carter, Cart and Sheep Creeks received heavy pressure after construction of Flaming Gorge Dam began.

**Recommendations**
- **Glen Canyon (Lake Powell):** Continue limnological and fishery research through impoundment. Avoid forage fish stocking until native values are known. Consider [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Kokanee Salmon]] for cold water, and largemouth bass and/or black crappie for warm water. Evaluate spawning areas (including clearing littoral areas near springs for kokanee) and build access roads and launch facilities at Wahweap, Bullfrog and Hite-White Canyon.
- **Flaming Gorge (Green River):** Chemically treat the river and tributaries from Seedskadee Dam site to Flaming Gorge Dam, ideally in fall 1962, to eradicate rough fish. Consider a hatchery for trout and kokanee. Build more access roads below the dam. Control discharge rates, increasing them at about 400 cfs per hour to protect the stream fishery. Conduct post-impoundment studies for at least 5 years, both in the reservoir and below the dam. Consider other game species in case the proposed species cannot sustain themselves.

**Summary conclusion:** The rivers and tributaries met chemical requirements for fish, but high turbidity, shifting sand, scouring floods and low organic production kept productivity low. The tributaries, though chemically suitable and clear in their springs, were small and intermittent, limiting their value as game fish habitat.
