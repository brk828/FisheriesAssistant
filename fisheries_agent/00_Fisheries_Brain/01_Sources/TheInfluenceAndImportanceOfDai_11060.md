# Weather and Rain Chemistry: Atmospheric Ion Supply to Lake District Waters

#freshwater-ecology #atmospheric-chemistry #rain-chemistry

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Gorham]] (1958, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*) examines how daily weather controls the supply of dissolved ions in precipitation at a rural site on [[Hawkshead Moor]] in the English [[Lake District]]. Daily rain samples were collected from 21 January 1955 to 25 January 1956. The author finds that [[Chloride]] comes mainly from [[Sea Spray]], while [[Sulphate]] and [[Nitrate]] come mainly from [[Air Pollution]], largely as [[Sulphuric Acid]]. Daily precipitation is the strongest control on ion supply, but wind speed, wind direction and temperature also shape it. The paper then traces the consequences of these inputs for upland tarns, [[Blanket Bog]] pools, lakes and [[Woodland Soil Solution]], and considers their possible ecological effects.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses the cycle of dissolved materials among soils, fresh waters and the sea. Previous work had analysed nitrogen compounds, chlorides and sulphates in rain and snow in many parts of the world, but the day-to-day weather controls on their supply were poorly known. The Lake District was chosen because it is mountainous, receives over 1250 mm of rain per year in much of the area, and supplies chloride and sulphate in comparable, easily measured amounts from largely different sources. Chloride and sulphate together account for well over 90% of total dissolved anions.

**Sampling and methods**

- **Site:** A firebreak in young pinewoods at High Man, about 280 m above sea level. The collector was a 1-gallon polythene bottle under a 30 cm funnel, exposed continuously so that dry deposition was captured in the next wet sample.
- **Sample collection:** Precipitation was collected at about 9 a.m. on the day after each day with at least 0.1 mm of rain.
- **Analyses:** Chloride was measured, and sulphate plus nitrate were measured together by the [[Mackereth Method]]. Sodium and potassium were measured by flame photometry, pH by glass electrode, and calcium by versenate titration. Nitrate was measured separately in about 70% of samples.
- **Weather data:** Wind came from Sellafield (about 25 km away), temperature from Ambleside (9 km away), and precipitation from the daily sample volumes.
- **Statistics:** Ion and precipitation data were log-transformed, and correlation and partial (third-order) regression analyses were used.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sources of ions**

Sea spray enters the air as jets from bursting bubbles, and it is strongly linked to wind. Sulphur from fuel combustion enters the air as sulphur dioxide, which oxidises to hygroscopic sulphuric acid. Coal chloride appears to fall out near its source, while coal sulphate travels further.

**Regional deposition**

Annual deposition at Hawkshead Moor was about 92 kg/ha of chloride, 24 kg/ha of sulphate sulphur, and just over 2 kg/ha of nitrogen as nitrate. About 60% of chloride and nearly 40% of sulphate fell in December to February. Summer chloride was only about 4% of the annual total, while sulphate declined much less.

**Waters receiving rain inputs**

- **Upland tarns:** Their chloride and sulphate concentrations follow a steep decline with distance from the coast. Rain is the main source of their ions, especially in the central volcanic hills.
- **[[Blanket Bog]] pools:** Their surfaces are isolated from mineral soil, so they depend almost entirely on rain. [[Ringinglow Bog]], near an industrial city, has very high sulphuric acid and calcium levels.
- **Lakes ([[Windermere]] and [[Coniston Water]]):** Rain supplies most of their chloride and sulphate, but some input is unexplained.
- **Woodland soils:** Mor humus waters are strongly acid and leached, while mull waters are only faintly acid and rich in calcium and nitrate.

## Key Findings

1. **Daily precipitation is the dominant control on ion supply.** The correlation coefficient was 0.71 (zero order) and 0.73 (third order) for chloride, and 0.84 and 0.82 for sulphate plus nitrate. Ion concentrations fall steeply in light showers and much less in heavy rain. Chloride concentration fell by 0.15 mequiv./l. between 1 and 10 mm, but only 0.003 mequiv./l. between 50 and 60 mm.

2. **Chloride and sulphate have different sources, and weather affects them differently.**
   - Wind direction correlated negatively with both ions, and more strongly with sulphate plus nitrate (-0.45, against -0.24 for chloride). The sulphate source lies to the south and east, in the industrial Midlands.
   - Wind speed had a strong effect on chloride (r = 0.49 third order) and a weak one on sulphate plus nitrate (r = 0.20). Between minimum and maximum wind speeds, sulphate plus nitrate roughly doubled, while chloride rose more than tenfold.
   - Temperature had a significant negative effect on chloride (third-order r = -0.39), but not on sulphate (r = -0.13). This suggests greater sea-salt input in winter gales, and possibly greater evaporation of sea-spray droplets over the North Atlantic.

3. **Nitrate is mostly of pollution origin.** Nitrate averaged just under 10% of sulphate (r = 0.83 for 126 samples). The eighteen least-polluted samples had uniformly low nitrate, averaging 0.0035 mequiv./l.

4. **Rain is strongly acid, and the acidity is largely from pollution.** About half of samples had pH below 4.5, 8% were below pH 4, and only 4% were above pH 6. Hydrogen ions accounted for about 30% of sulphate (r = 0.85 for 157 samples). Calcium was about 17% of sulphate at very high pollution loads, compared with 67% in city rain.

5. **Sodium and chloride are mainly from sea spray, but with exceptions.**
   - Sodium and chloride correlated very strongly (r = 0.97 for 166 samples). The ten days with chloride above 500 mequiv./100 m² averaged a Na/Cl ratio of 0.86, close to the sea-water value of 0.85.
   - "Excess chloride" (Na/Cl below 0.8) was found in about forty-two samples, mostly from easterly winds. This is attributed to pollution. Smoke solids from London chimneys had an average Na/Cl ratio of 0.47.
   - "Excess sodium" (Na/Cl at least 0.95) was found in about thirty samples, mostly from small showers, with higher pH. This may reflect a dust source, though lake data do not support dust as the main cause.
   - A single hail sample had a Na/Cl ratio of 6.5. A laboratory test (150 g sulphuric acid and 50 g sodium chloride in 1 litre, bubbled with air) produced 0.16 mequiv./l. of hydrochloric acid with no detectable sulphate. The author proposes that freezing or evaporation of mixed droplets could release gaseous hydrogen chloride, but this is not confirmed in the atmosphere.

6. **Lake inputs are largely explained by rain.** Corrected rain concentrations were 0.177 mequiv./l. of chloride and 0.115 mequiv./l. of sulphate plus nitrate, compared with 0.204 and 0.158 in the lakes. The gaps, 13% and 27% of lake chloride and sulphate plus nitrate, may reflect dry deposition, uptake by vegetation, or sampling bias.

7. **Some sulphate has a non-marine, non-coal source.** After correcting for sea spray and coal, about 0.02 mequiv./l. of sulphate remained, roughly one-quarter of the total. The author suggests this comes from hydrogen sulphide diffusing from continental-shelf muds and oxidising in the atmosphere.

8. **Tarn chemistry changes with distance from the sea.** In sixteen tarns sampled in February 1957, chloride rose sharply in waters less than 10 km from the coast. The fitted relation was c = 0.71d^-0.5. Sodium/chloride ratios stayed close to sea water throughout, unlike the increase inland seen in Swedish precipitation. Alkaline, bicarbonate-rich tarns occurred at both ends of the transect, on softer sedimentary rocks.

9. **Bogs and upland tarns depend most on rain.** Ringinglow Bog had a Na/Cl ratio of 0.62 and very high acidity and calcium. Some rain samples had up to 130 µg/l of zinc (Mackereth, personal communication). The author notes that heavy-metal toxicity is possible near polluted sites.

10. **Soil solutions differ from lake waters.** Mor humus waters lost about one-third of their rain hydrogen ions to neutralisation, and their potassium came mainly from vegetation decay rather than rain. Mull waters had very high nitrate (0.3 to 1.1 mequiv./l.) and about half their sodium came from weathering.

11. **Ecological implications are significant but uncertain.** Rain supplies nutrients such as nitrogen, potassium, calcium and sulphur, but sulphuric acid and smoke-derived organic compounds may be toxic to plants and may speed soil leaching. The author suggests that effects of industrial air chemistry would be easiest to detect in high tarns, where rain is the main nutrient source, using mud-core microfossil records.
