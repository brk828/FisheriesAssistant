## Executive Summary

This 1931 study by Carl L. and Laura C. Hubbs provides experimental confirmation that natural hybridization occurs between distinct genera of sunfishes (family [[Centrarchidae]]), overturning the presumption that intergeneric hybridization would be blocked by genetic or behavioral (psychological) barriers. Building on earlier observational work (Hubbs, 1920; 1926) suggesting that the rare nominal "species" *[[Lepomis euryorus]]* was actually a hybrid between *[[Apomotis cyanellus]]* (green sunfish) and *[[Eupomotis gibbosus]]* (pumpkinseed), the authors conducted controlled aquarium matings across multiple genus combinations. They successfully produced viable, fertile-appearing offspring from *Apomotis × Eupomotis* (both directions of the reciprocal cross) and *Apomotis × [[Helioperca incisor]]* (bluegill), demonstrating that no complete genetic or psychological barrier prevents intergeneric mating in sunfishes. Other combinations (*Eupomotis × Helioperca*, *Apomotis × [[Xenotis megalotis megalotis]]*, *Eupomotis × Xenotis*) were attempted but failed due to fungal infection, female mortality, or lack of full maturity — though partial evidence (fertilization, egg development) still supported the absence of a genetic block. The experimentally produced hybrids were morphologically, chromatically, and behaviorally intermediate between parental species, matching specimens collected in the wild, thereby confirming field identifications of natural hybrid sunfish. Notably, hybrid offspring exhibited markedly increased growth rates relative to parent species and were reported to be predominantly male and sterile.

## Background & Description

### *Apomotis cyanellus* × *Eupomotis gibbosus* Hybrids
- **Skin texture**: Less rough than *Eupomotis*, less slimy than *Apomotis* (intermediate).
- **Body shape**: Slenderer yet thicker than *Eupomotis*; not as slender/thick as *Apomotis*.
- **Gill rakers**: Intermediate length.
- **Pharyngeal arches/teeth**: Intermediate, though teeth as sharp as in *Apomotis* (attributed to softness of captive diet).
- **Cheeks/mouth**: Deeper cheeks and larger mouths than *Eupomotis*, but less extreme than *Apomotis*.
- **Fins**: Lower/shorter and rounder than *Eupomotis*, but not as low/round as *Apomotis* — especially pectorals and anal spines. Pelvic and anal fins show trace pale borders characteristic of *Apomotis*.
- **Opercular spot**: Coppery-red border, more diffuse than the bright red spot of *Eupomotis*.
- **Body coloration**: Combination of olive-rust undertone and violet luster (from *Apomotis*) with dull coppery flecks (from *Eupomotis*).
- **Banding pattern**: More conspicuous than *Apomotis* but less than *Eupomotis*; bluish interspaces marked with rows of dark spots resembling *Apomotis*.
- **Overall effect**: More "checkered" appearance than either parent species — a synergistic blending of traits rather than simple averaging.

### Growth Metrics
- Parent species standard lengths (aquarium-reared, pre-spawning):
  - Female *Apomotis*: grew from 80 mm (Oct 1928) to 95 mm (by spawning, Feb 1929).
  - Male *Eupomotis*: grew from 75 mm to 89 mm over same period.
  - Second-generation male *Apomotis* (reciprocal cross, 1931): 146 mm at spawning.
  - Female *Eupomotis* (reciprocal cross): 138 mm.
- **Hybrid offspring growth (first mating)**: At one year, standard length ranged 35–95 mm, average 54 mm.
- **Hybrid offspring growth (second mating)**: At one year, 46–96 mm, average 64.6 mm.
- **Hybrid offspring growth (third mating)**: At one year, 48–119 mm, average 69.5 mm.
- Hybrids' first-year growth exceeded natural pond-reared hybrids by 18 mm and substantially exceeded first-season growth of either pure parent species — consistent with findings in [[Increased Growth In Hybrid Sunfishes]] (Hubbs & Hubbs, 1931).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Preferred habitat for natural hybridization**: Ponds and small lakes with mucky substrates and dense growth of *Chara* (stonewort algae), which provide abundant food and favorable growth conditions but limited suitable nesting substrate.
- **Competitive spawning dynamics**: Limited nesting habitat intensifies interspecific competition for spawning grounds. Where *[[Eupomotis gibbosus]]* (pumpkinseed) is numerically dominant and its males more pugnacious/territorial than the more "docile" *[[Apomotis cyanellus]]* (green sunfish) males, cross-species mating (male Eupomotis × female Apomotis) becomes more likely.
- **Reproductive behavior**: All examined sunfishes exhibit complex nuptial behavior including redd (nest) construction by the male, a "nuptial gyration" over the nest, mate-guarding, and territorial aggression against intruders (including larger bass). Breeding occurs in quiet water with minimal egg/sperm drift, and nests are spaced apart due to male territoriality — contrasting with more "fortuitous" hybridization mechanisms proposed for [[Cyprinidae]].
- **Geographic distribution of hybrid combinations**: *Apomotis × Helioperca* hybrids (historically associated with the taxon *[[Lepiopomus ischyrus]]*) are rare in Michigan but common in the Southwestern United States.
- **Diet during larval/juvenile rearing**: Protozoans → *Paramoecium* → chopped tubificid worms → whole tubificid worms and mosquito larvae → chopped angleworms, dried shrimp, fly larvae, shredded bird meat (found to be a particularly effective diet for sunfish).
- **Predation/competition threat in captivity**: A "huge crop" of *Cyclops* (copepods) coincided with the failure/loss of one experimental hybrid brood, illustrating potential invertebrate predation pressure on sunfish eggs/larvae.

## Key Findings

1. **No absolute genetic barrier**: Four separate matings across three intergeneric combinations (*Apomotis × Eupomotis* [two reciprocal crosses] and *Apomotis × Helioperca* [two crosses]) each produced large numbers of viable offspring, definitively proving no complete genetic incompatibility between these sunfish genera.
2. **No absolute psychological (behavioral) barrier**: Normal courtship and nuptial behaviors (redd construction, gyration, egg-laying, mate defense) were observed in successful intergeneric pairings, demonstrating that behavioral isolation mechanisms are incomplete between genera.
3. **Reciprocal cross success**: Both directions of the *Apomotis cyanellus* × *Eupomotis gibbosus* cross (female Apomotis × male Eupomotis, and male Apomotis × female Eupomotis) were achieved, producing viable offspring in each direction.
4. **Confirmation of field-based hybrid identifications**: Laboratory-produced hybrids were morphologically, chromatically, and structurally indistinguishable from specimens previously collected in the wild and tentatively identified as natural hybrids, validating earlier taxonomic hypotheses (e.g., *[[Lepomis euryorus]]* = *Apomotis × Eupomotis* hybrid; *[[Lepiopomus ischyrus]]* = *Apomotis × Helioperca* hybrid).
5. **Enhanced hybrid growth (heterosis)**: Hybrid offspring consistently exhibited faster growth rates than either parent species in the same environment, a phenomenon further detailed in the companion paper [[Increased Growth In Hybrid Sunfishes]] (1931).
6. **Failed crosses attributable to external factors, not genetic incompatibility**: Failures in *Eupomotis × Helioperca*, *Apomotis × Xenotis*, and *Eupomotis × Xenotis* crosses were linked to female mortality, male aggression toward females, fungal infection of eggs, or incomplete sexual maturity — not to a demonstrated reproductive barrier.
7. **Sex ratio skew and infertility in hybrids**: Both naturally occurring and experimentally produced sunfish hybrids were found, with rare exceptions, to be exclusively or predominantly male, and all examined hybrids proved infertile — a topic slated for further investigation in follow-up papers.
8. **Species-specific aggression differences**: Male [[Eupomotis gibbosus]] were noted as more pugnacious/territorial on nests than male [[Apomotis cyanellus]], a behavioral asymmetry proposed as a mechanistic driver of the directionality of natural hybridization events.
9. **Taxonomic implication**: The findings reinforce the authors' broader argument for recognizing *Apomotis* and *Eupomotis* (and by extension *Helioperca*, *Xenotis*) as structurally and taxonomically distinct genera within [[Centrarchidae]], despite their capacity for intergeneric hybridization — a notable case of hybridization crossing genus-level taxonomic boundaries.
