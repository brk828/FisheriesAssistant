# Exploraciones en el Distrito Sur de la Baja California (Explorations in the Southern District of Baja California)

#geology #marine-geology #economic-geology

## Executive Summary

This field report, written in Spanish by mining engineer [[Vicente Gálvez]] for the [[Instituto Geológico Nacional]] (Anales del Instituto Geológico de México), describes a 1920 geological reconnaissance of the southern tip of the [[Baja California]] Peninsula, from [[La Paz]] to [[Cabo San Lucas]]. The expedition ran six itineraries covering a route of about 310 km, mapped the igneous, metamorphic and sedimentary rocks, sampled mineral occurrences, described drainage and hydrology, and reconstructed the geological history of the region. The report is a geology and mining document. Its aquatic content is limited to surface and groundwater hydrology, coastal landforms and a few marine fossils, with no biological survey.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and authorship.** After the 1919 commission's report, Gálvez proposed on 11 February 1920 a nine-point work plan to complete geological exploration of the southern peninsula. He was appointed chief of the commission on 13 April 1920. The team departed on 16 April and reached La Paz on 1 May 1920 aboard the pailebot "Astoria". Field geology was completed in October 1920. Topographer Juan Camacho continued survey work until early 1921. Gálvez was instructed to study the earlier report by the American geologist [[Gabb]] and to advise the state government on well sites.

**Prior exploration.** The report summarizes earlier work:
- 1884 commission: reached about 26°53′ (Mulegé) to 28°30′.
- 1911 commission: northern region, from the international boundary to the 28°–31°30′ line.
- 1913 commission: area east of Bahía Magdalena to the Mesa de La Vieja.
- 1918 commission: riolitic zone around La Paz, [[El Triunfo]] and [[San Antonio]] mineral districts, and parts between Todos Santos and El Gaspareño.
- 1919 commission: reached Santa Rosalía, with parts of the region from 26° north.

Gálvez concludes that the peninsula is well explored from the border to about 26°, and the southern region is known from about 23° to just south of 24°.

**Study area.** The mapped rectangle lies southeast of La Paz, bounded by the meridians of La Paz (west) and Cabo Pulmo (east), and by the parallels of [[Sierra de Cacachilas]] (north) and [[Sierra de la Trinidad]] (south). Coastal reconnaissance covered the [[Gulf of California]] shore from Ensenada de Palmas to Cabo Pulmo, and the [[Pacific Ocean]] shore from Gaspareño Point to Todos Santos. The author notes that a full coastal survey would require a vessel, because of difficult terrain and a lack of pasture.

**Itineraries.** The six named routes were:
1. El Triunfo–Las Trochas (north)
2. El Triunfo–El Alamo (east)
3. El Triunfo–La Ballena (southeast)
4. El Triunfo–Cerro Prieto (southwest)
5. El Triunfo–El Pescadero (southwest, longest, about 310 km polygon)
6. El Triunfo–San José del Cabo (cross-section route)

Dozens of ranches, arroyos and prospects are listed along these routes; the report names the most significant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography.** The main cordillera runs along the peninsula's Gulf side to Cabo San Lucas, divided by the Río San José into the [[Sierra de San Lázaro]] (west) and the Sierra de la Trinidad (east, highest point 880 m). Notable summits include Picacho de Los Soldados (1,281 m) and La Cieneguita (1,164 m) in the Cacachilas range. A list of San Lázaro summits reaches 2,440 m. Coastal headlands near Cabo Pulmo rise to 404 m, and hills near Todos Santos and Punta del Gaspareño reach 126–294 m.

**Coastal landforms.** Low beaches occur at La Ventana, Bahía de Palmas, La Ribera, Punta Arena and along the Pacific. Steep cliffs and headlands face the surf in other sectors. Marine terraces step up from the coast, and [[Alluvium]] and sand dunes (médanos) form the litoral belt.

**Hydrology.** The report describes a simple, consequent drainage pattern of short arroyos. Most flow only during the rainy season and then sink into the sand and gravel. Exceptions include the [[Río San José]] and the Todos Santos arroyo, which Eisen considered permanent. The Santa Ana arroyo was reported to flow continuously, probably fed by springs. Rainfall maps by Eisen and Vaslit give under 75 cm per year on the sierra tops, under 625 mm on the slopes, and under 20 cm on the plain. Gálvez estimates infiltration at about one-fifth of rainfall, with more on the plain. Springs occur at San Bartolo and in the Pitahayal and El Encinal areas, where people dig shallow wells in alluvium. The [[Ojo de El Agua Caliente]] is a thermal spring rising through granite. The report recommends efforts to develop groundwater for agriculture.

**Marine and aquatic remnants.** Fossil shells (Ostrea, Pecten, gastropods) were collected by Angermann at 600 m elevation in the Cacachilas sierra, in sand beds cemented by silica and interpreted as ancient beaches. Aguilera tentatively identified *Fasciolaria (Princeps Lam.)*, and Böse later assigned these deposits probably to the Pleistocene. At El Agua Amarga, sea caves cut into sandstone contain vertebrae, tibiae and other bones, which the author suggests may belong to fish and marine birds. No live marine or freshwater fauna was surveyed.

**Groundwater-bearing strata.** Sedimentary beds around El Encinal, Santiago and Miraflores are gently folded and interbedded with marl. These impermeable layers hold up subsurface water that surfaces as springs locally called *bateques*. A well at Pitahayal was drilled to 70 m without passing through the sandstones.

## Key Findings

**Rock types.**
- *Granites* (biotite and hornblende granites) dominate the Cacachilas and San Lázaro sierras. They are cut by aplite and pegmatite veins, some containing black tourmaline and garnet.
- *Diorites* (including hypersthene diorite and quartz diorite/tonalite) form the largest unit, from El Triunfo to Todos Santos. Diorites host most of the mineral districts.
- *Dynamic metamorphism* produced [[Gneiss]] (granitic and dioritic), schistose diorites, mica schists, slates and phyllites. Marble occurs at Cerro del Mármol, near La Estrella Polar.
- *Volcanic rocks* include rhyolites (La Paz area; Cerro Las Tachuelas and Las Lagunas near Cabo Pulmo), andesite and dacite porphyries, and diabase and hornblendite dikes.
- *Sedimentary rocks* include Quaternary alluvium and terrace gravels, sandstones (El Agua Amarga), marly limestones, tobas calizas, gypsum (Los Mártires) and white marl at the Chivato spring.

**Mineralization.** The report describes gold-silver quartz veins in fracture zones within granite and diorite. Strikes vary from NE 20° to NW 25° (Cacachilas), and up to NW 55° at El Pescadero. Dips are 55–80° east, and widths 0.02–1.20 m. The author compares them to veins at Silver Peak, Nevada, and suggests a granitic-solution origin. Assay examples (hand-sorted samples, not averages):
- Santa Ana: silver 34 g/t, gold trace.
- La Testera: silver 61 g/t, gold 6.67 g/t.
- Los Uvares (El Oro): gold 100 g/t, silver 12 g/t in one sample.
- La Estrella Polar (El Pescadero): silver 193 g/t, gold 386.67 g/t.

Gálvez stresses that these values are not representative and cannot be used for economic evaluation. Most workings reached only the oxidation zone, and several prospects were abandoned. Historic milling at Las Canoas used stamps, amalgamation plates, a Bartlett concentrator and cyanide tanks. The Valle Perdido hacienda used a Huntington mill and a Dodge crusher.

**Placers.** Alluvial gold occurs in arroyo beds, for example at La Brecha and Las Gallinas, recovered by seasonal blower machines.

**Mineral identification.** Analysis No. 429 identified a somewhat altered andalusite. Chiastolite crystals in phyllites were later confirmed by Carlos Castro.

**Geological history (as interpreted by Gálvez).**
1. Pre-Cambrian metamorphic rocks (mica schists and phyllites), tentatively assigned after Aguilera and Ordóñez.
2. Pre-Cretaceous intrusion of granite, diorite, gabbro and diabase.
3. Post-Cretaceous granite, aplite and pegmatite intrusions.
4. Rhyolite and diabase eruptions, and Tertiary rhyolite near La Paz.
5. Plio-Pleistocene brecciation and uplift.
6. Marine inundation in the Eocene–Miocene, which formed the [[Gulf of California]] and separated the peninsula from the mainland. A later uplift reached 329 m at Atezcalama.

**Conclusions.** The author recommends systematic exploration of the mineral districts, deeper drilling of veins below the oxidation zone, use of the Santa Ana water for irrigation, and development of groundwater. He also calls for a future geological map of Baja California, built from the 1911, 1913, 1918, 1919 and 1920 surveys, and notes that the map remains subject to revision.
