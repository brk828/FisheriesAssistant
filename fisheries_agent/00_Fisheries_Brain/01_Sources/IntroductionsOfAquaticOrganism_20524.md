# Introductions of Aquatic Organisms in Africa, Fish Hybridization References and SIPAM Network News (FAO Aquaculture Newsletter No. 17, December 1997)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This FAO Aquaculture Newsletter issue contains three items. The first is a reference list of about 15 papers on fish hybridization and induced triploidy, largely from aquaculture journals. The second, and main, article reviews aquatic species introductions into and out of Africa, drawn from the FAO introductions database. It records 139 species introduced into 42 African countries, with finfish dominating, and documents both socio-economic gains (notably the [[Nile Perch]] fishery on [[Lake Victoria]]) and serious ecological damage. The article calls for precautionary management under the [[ICES-EIFAC Codes of Practice]]. The third item reports on the [[SIPAM Network]], a Mediterranean aquaculture information network, covering its technical meeting, software and database developments, and planned expansion.

## Background & Description

**Hybrid reference list (page 1).** The references cover hybrid and triploid fish culture, including loaches (*Misgurnus* spp.), African catfish crosses (*Clarias gariepinus* × *Heterobranchus longifilis*), hybrid [[Tilapia]], [[Striped Bass]] hybrids, [[Pacu]] and [[Tambaqui]] reciprocal hybrids, sunfish (*Lepomis*) hybrids, salmonid hybrids such as splake, and sturgeon culture. Several papers address [[Triploidy]] to improve survival, and one reports inadvertent hybridization in a carp hatchery detected by nuclear DNA RFLP. The list is OCR-damaged and partly truncated, so some author names and titles are incomplete.

**Introductions article (pages 2–5).** The article, based on the FAO database of international introductions (Welcomme, 1988; Bartley and Subasinghe, 1996), defines *introduced species* (intentionally or accidentally moved outside their present range) and *transferred species* (moved within their range). Its scope excludes ballast-water and fouling introductions.

**SIPAM news (page 6).** The SIPAM (Mediterranean aquaculture information system) report describes a regional network of national coordinators coordinated by the [[FAO Regional Centre for SIPAM]] in Tunisia.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Scale:** The FAO database lists over 2,800 introductions worldwide, of which 430 were into Africa, about 30 out of Africa and about 140 among African countries.
- **Taxonomic composition:** 139 species from 87 genera and 46 families were introduced into Africa. Finfish made up 79%, crustaceans 9% and molluscs 7%.
- **Dominant species:** The five most frequently introduced species were [[Common Carp]] (28 records), [[Rainbow Trout]] (19), [[Largemouth Bass]] (19), [[Nile Tilapia]] (17) and [[Grass Carp]] (15). About 4% of species accounted for 23% of introductions.
- **Dominant families:** Cichlidae (116 records), Cyprinidae (81), Centrarchidae (50) and Salmonidae (40). About 9% of families accounted for 67% of introductions.
- **Timeline:** Before 1949 there were 93 records, 1950–1989 had 226 and after 1990 there were 22, with 77 of unknown date. The peaks were 1950–1959 (78) and 1960–1969 (60), during the search for suitable aquaculture species, stocking for man-made lakes, and control of disease vectors and weeds. Introductions fell after 1980, attributed to growing awareness of negative effects and to legislation in developed countries.
- **Origins and exports:** Africa received introductions from every continent except Oceania and Antarctica, with sources linked to colonial ties. Africa also exported [[Nile Tilapia]] from Ghana, Egypt, Kenya and Senegal to the Philippines, where it was improved under the [[GIFT Project]] into the "super tilapia" (Eknath et al., 1993).
- **Purposes:** Introductions were grouped into aquaculture development, biological control, and capture and sport fisheries. Other recorded categories were 5 accidents, 10 natural migrations, 8 introductions to fill "vacant niches", and 71 of unknown motivation.
- **Aquaculture status (Table 2):** Of 153 aquaculture introductions, 74 became established in the wild. Of 32 biological-control introductions, 22 became established.

## Key Findings

**Socio-economic benefits**
- Several species (34%) became widespread in rivers and lakes, and in a few cases established fisheries.
- The [[Nile Perch]] introduction into Lake Victoria contributed to the loss of over 300 haplochromine cichlid species. It also turned a small-scale artisanal fishery into a multi-million dollar commercial fishery supporting processing and export.
- The pelagic [[Limnothrissa Miodon]] (kapenta/sardine) became the basis of important fisheries in [[Lake Kariba]], [[Lake Kivu]] and [[Lake Cahora Bassa]], though it altered zooplankton composition.
- The [[Heterotis Niloticus]] fishery on the [[Nyong River]] in Cameroon is the third successful fishery cited.
- In the three flourishing fisheries, fish supplies rose two- to three-fold and per capita fish consumption was maintained despite population growth. These benefits were accompanied by deforestation for fuelwood and rural economic shifts.
- The three fisheries took 15 to 20 years to show their effects.

**Ecological harm**
- Where analysed (Lake Victoria, Nyong River), the fish fauna was drastically altered, native species were eliminated, and the situation was judged virtually irreversible.
- Ogutu-Ohwayo and Hecky (1990) found the effect of *L. miodon* on the pre-existing community and trophic ecology to be very small in Kivu and Kariba.
- Negative effects include habitat degradation, competition and displacement, stunting, predation and nuisance to fisheries.
- The article notes that few broad analyses covering both ecological and socio-economic parameters have been carried out.

**Management and policy**
- The [[ICES-EIFAC Codes of Practice]] (Turner, 1988) have been adopted by the Committee for Inland Fisheries in Africa (CIFA).
- FAO guidelines (1995a, 1995b, 1997) and the [[Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries]] emphasize a precautionary approach to reduce adverse impacts and minimize unintended introductions.
- The conclusion frames the core challenge as balancing hunger and poverty alleviation against sustainable use of natural resources and ecosystems.

**SIPAM network (page 6)**
- The second SIPAM 1997 technical meeting was held in Salerno, Italy, from 29 October to 1 November 1997, combined with an ICRAM meeting on the "Aquaculture Observatory for the Mediterranean". The article reports ten national coordinators attending, from Croatia, Cyprus, France, Italy, Malta, Morocco, Portugal, Spain and Turkey (nine countries are listed).
- The meeting found the SIPAM for WINDOWS software satisfactory. It will not be converted to 32-bit immediately, and modifications were agreed. The next technical meeting is proposed within eight months, subject to a host.
- Regional database release REGO01 contains more than 3,000 data entries from 12 participating countries. REGO02 is due in early 1998, and a pathology database redesign is planned.
- SIPAM will go online in 1998, with a prototype home page prepared by FAO.
- The Regional Coordinator visited France (June 1997) and Egypt (September 1997), with a Turkey visit planned for December 1997. Contacts with Algeria and Bulgaria aim to help establish national centres in 1998.
- Cooperation continues with the GFCM networks SELAM and TECAM. A joint SIPAM–SELAM–FEAP meeting on marketing database design is planned for early 1998.
