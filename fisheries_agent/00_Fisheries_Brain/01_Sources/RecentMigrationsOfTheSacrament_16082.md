# Recent Migrations of the Sacramento-San Joaquin River Striped Bass Population

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Harold K. Chadwick (California Department of Fish and Game, Inland Fisheries Branch, Stockton) uses tag returns from 18,300 tagged [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*, reported in the text as *Roccus saxatilis* and *Roccus sexatilis*) and angler catch records from 1958 through 1964 to describe migration patterns in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin River System]] of California. It compares these with earlier findings from the 1950-1952 tagging program. Larger adults migrated farther downstream, and migrations into San Francisco Bay and the Pacific Ocean were much greater in the late 1950s and early 1960s than in the early 1950s. The available environmental and population data were insufficient to determine the cause of this shift.

## Background & Description

The [[Striped Bass]] is an anadromous-type estuarine fish. In California, nearly all individuals live in the Sacramento-San Joaquin system and adjacent Pacific waters. The [[Sacramento River]] and [[San Joaquin River]] join near Pittsburg and drain California's Central Valley, flowing through Suisun Bay, Carquinez Strait, San Pablo Bay and [[San Francisco Bay]] to the Pacific Ocean. Upstream lies the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]], a network of roughly 700 miles of channels.

The earlier annual cycle (described by Calhoun, 1952) involved a mass upstream fall migration into Delta freshwater, overwintering there, dispersal and spawning in spring, and downstream movement after spawning (ending May to early June) to brackish or salt water for the summer. Young fish use the area from San Pablo Bay upstream as a nursery and begin adult migratory patterns at 2 to 5 years old. Legal-sized fish (16 inches or more total length) were tagged, mostly with disk dangler tags.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Salinity and flow:** Runoff is much greater in winter and spring, producing wide variation in salinity intrusion. Even in late summer, water is essentially fresh about 5 miles east of Antioch.
- **Spawning:** Striped bass spawn upstream of Pittsburg, mainly in the Delta portion of the San Joaquin River and in the Sacramento River from the Delta to above the [[Feather River]].
- **Factors limiting angler-based inference:** Returns reflect harvest, which depends on feeding behaviour, weather (wind, rain timing), boat-launch availability (scarce in Suisun Bay), turbidity (higher in the Delta), and changing fishing techniques. Bass are also likely caught mainly in the surf when in the ocean.
- **Environmental changes noted:** Pumping from the southern Delta into the [[Delta-Mendota Canal]] (well documented), improving sewage treatment in San Francisco Bay (with earlier periods of no dissolved oxygen in marginal areas), and possible changes among forage organisms (poorly documented).
- **Gear selectivity:** Drift and graduated-mesh gill nets biased samples toward larger fish. Fyke nets at [[Fremont Weir]] were size-selective for [[Chinook Salmon]].

## Key Findings

**Study design and tagging**
- The main tagging effort was 14,900 striped bass tagged in the western Delta in springs 1958-1961 (Table 1 totals include 4,378 in 1958, 4,262 in 1959, 4,317 in 1960 and 1,943 in 1961, with mean lengths around 21.7-23.1 inches).
- Other groups were tagged at [[Fremont Weir]] (890 fish, mean 19.0 inches, 1958), Prisoner's Point in the eastern San Joaquin Delta (1,146 fish, 1959), San Pablo Bay in fall 1958 (750 fish, mean 18.7 inches), and the western Delta in fall 1958 (628 fish).
- About 4,600 (31%) of the spring western Delta tags were returned through spring 1964.

**Migration by population component**
- Tagging locality within the western Delta and tagging time in spring had no significant effect on migration, so these were pooled.
- Length had the strongest effect: fish 15-20 inches had their highest returns from Carquinez Strait-San Pablo Bay; 21-24 inch fish from San Francisco Bay; and 25 inches or larger from the Pacific Ocean (Table 3). Ocean returns for the 25+ inch group were 16.9% of the total for that group.
- Sex had a weaker effect on recovery patterns than length, though the sample sizes were too small to separate the two conclusively.
- Upper Sacramento River (Fremont Weir) bass showed strong homing: in spring 1959 a much higher share returned to the upper Sacramento River.
- Eastern San Joaquin Delta tags were recovered in the eastern Delta at nearly three times the rate of western Delta tags, consistent with fish returning to their previous spawning area.
- Fall-tagged western Delta fish included mature bass that moved back to San Francisco Bay during winter and then returned to the Delta in spring, suggesting distinct components within the mature population.

**Sex ratio and spawning**
- Males made up 73% (1960) and 64% (1961) of the tagged spring fish; 49% and 47% of tagged males were under 22 inches, versus 10% and 6% of females.
- In the Delta, males dominated spring catches. In San Francisco and San Pablo bays, 76% of 823 striped bass observed between 8 April and 24 June 1961 were female, and at least 40% of females and 79% of males there were immature.
- Immature fish did not migrate to the Delta, and females, which mature later, dominated bay catches. Males probably remained on spawning grounds longer.

**Seasonal and annual migration**
- Summer: most bass were downstream of Suisun Bay, centred mainly in San Francisco Bay, with substantial numbers in the Pacific Ocean and in the Carquinez Strait-San Pablo Bay area.
- Fall: San Francisco Bay and Pacific Ocean returns declined through the fall, while Delta and Suisun Bay returns rose to a peak in November.
- Winter: the largest number of returns came from San Francisco Bay, indicating a substantial overwintering population there.
- Spring: most fish were in the Delta or upper Sacramento River, where returns peaked annually.
- The population was farthest upstream in 1959 and concentrated most heavily in San Francisco Bay in 1960, 1961 and 1963.
- Chi-square tests of annual return distributions rejected constancy at the 99.9% level (χ² = 321.1), though the authors note that fishery changes also contribute to these differences.

**Ocean migration**
- Ocean returns ranged from south of Monterey Bay (about 100 miles south of the Golden Gate) to Tomales Bay (about 45 miles north). Offshore recoveries reached 10 miles off San Francisco.
- Ocean recoveries were mostly confined to June through September, with catches usually peaking in July (except 1964, when about 60% were caught in August).
- In 1960, several fish were caught near the San Francisco lightship, the only offshore recoveries in the study.

**Changes between periods**
- The main change from the 1950-1952 period to 1958-1961 was a more extended seaward migration: 48% of returns came from San Francisco Bay and the Pacific Ocean, compared with 2% in 1950-1952.
- Bass remained downstream longer, did not return to the Delta until later in the fall, and spent less time there.
- Upper Sacramento spring returns rose from 3% to 7% of annual returns, while eastern San Joaquin Delta spring returns fell from 10% to 1%.
- Possible contributing factors identified were an older average age of bass (size limit raised from 12 to 16 inches in 1956 and commercial salmon and shad fishing that killed large bass stopped in 1957) and improved water quality in San Francisco Bay shallows. The author concluded that the causes could not be definitively determined.

**Temperature and ocean migration**
- Radovich (1963) proposed that seaward migration is greatest when coastal sea temperatures are warm. Chadwick found no strong correlation from 1958-1964: correlation between the seaward-movement index and ocean-return percentages was strongly negative (r = -0.827), and correlations with sea temperatures were weak (for example, r = 0.213 with mean annual Farallon Islands temperatures), none significantly different from zero.
- Excellent ocean fishing in 1958 occurred during high temperatures, but poor fishing in 1957 and 1959 also occurred at high temperatures.

**Comparisons with other populations**
- Migrations described for Coos Bay, Oregon are similar to those in the Sacramento-San Joaquin system.
- Chesapeake Bay large fish undertake extensive Atlantic coastal migrations, unlike the California fish. Small Chesapeake bass migrate less far than large California bass, and Chesapeake fish show homing to spawning sites (6 of 8 recaptured fish returned near the tagging site).

**Overall conclusion**
- Migration patterns within 1958-1964 were consistent, but differed from the early 1950s. Fishing success shifted toward San Francisco Bay and the Pacific Ocean and away from the Delta. The author recommends caution in interpreting angler-based returns because fishing effort and technique change over time.
