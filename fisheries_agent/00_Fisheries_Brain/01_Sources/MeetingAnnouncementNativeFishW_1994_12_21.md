## Executive Summary

This document is a meeting announcement and progress memorandum from the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation's [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), convened January 18–19, 1995, in Boulder City, Nevada. It summarizes the 1994 field season of the [[Lake Mohave Native Fish Rearing Program]], a conservation initiative targeting the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) in the Lower Colorado River system. The report is not a morphological or ecological research paper but a programmatic status update covering larval collection, backwater rearing success, recapture data from population monitoring events ("roundups"), and infrastructure changes (e.g., removal of the Davis Cove net). It also outlines an agenda for reviewing regional populations and planning the 1995 spawning season work.

## Background & Description

No morphological data (e.g., meristics, body proportions, coloration) are presented in this source. The document focuses exclusively on population management, larval rearing logistics, and recapture statistics for the [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]]. Age-class tracking is referenced via "year class" designations (e.g., 1992 and 1993 year classes), indicating that cohort-based growth and survival — rather than morphology — is the primary biological metric of interest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary habitat**: [[Lake Mohave]] and its associated ephemeral backwaters, which serve as critical rearing habitat for wild-caught larval razorback suckers.
- **Life stage habitat partitioning**: Wild larvae are captured from riverine/lacustrine spawning areas and translocated into isolated ephemeral backwaters for predator-reduced rearing before being released back into the main lake as juveniles.
- **Regional population network**: The NFWG monitors and manages populations across multiple interconnected systems in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]], including [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Havasu]], [[Lake Powell]], [[Willow Beach]], and [[Central Arizona]] populations, reflecting a basin-wide metapopulation management approach.
- **Threats noted**: Predation and competition from exotic species during spring roundup monitoring, and infrastructure-based fish exclusion methods (e.g., the Davis Cove net, later replaced by an earthen berm) to manage fish movement and predator access.
- **Brood stock development**: A subset of wild-captured larvae (~1,000 individuals) were transferred to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service facility at Parker, Arizona, for captive brood stock development, indicating an ex-situ conservation genetics component to the program.

## Key Findings

- **Recapture success (March 1994 "Razorback Roundup")**: 7 individuals from the 1992 year class and 3 individuals from the 1993 year class were recaptured, confirming that the stocking and marking techniques used by the program are effective for long-term monitoring.
- **Larval collection**: Over 10,000 wild razorback sucker larvae were captured through an intra-agency collaborative effort during 1994.
- **Backwater stocking**: Approximately 9,000 of the captured larvae were released into ephemeral backwaters at Lake Mohave for rearing.
- **Brood stock transfer**: The remaining ~1,000 larvae were sent to the Fish and Wildlife Service's Parker, Arizona facility for captive brood stock development.
- **Juvenile survival**: Of the larvae stocked into Lake Mohave backwaters, roughly 2,300 juvenile razorback suckers were recaptured during subsequent summer and fall monitoring and released into the main lake, representing an estimated **25% survival rate** from stocked larvae to recaptured juveniles.
- **Infrastructure change**: The Davis Cove exclusion net was removed and replaced with an earthen berm, likely to improve habitat management or reduce maintenance/predation issues.
- **Program structure for 1995**: Planned work includes continued larval collection (with helicopter surveys), Willow Beach egg/larvae propagation, Davis Cove fish salvage operations, expanded roundup tagging efforts, berm repair, and exploration of program expansion (e.g., new rearing pens, water circulators, and public outreach via razorback displays at National Park Service sites).
- **Regulatory context**: The program's activities are explicitly tied to Section 7 consultations under the Endangered Species Act, including biological opinions for [[Willow Beach]] operations, Reclamation's water operations, and Lake Mead sportfish stocking programs.
