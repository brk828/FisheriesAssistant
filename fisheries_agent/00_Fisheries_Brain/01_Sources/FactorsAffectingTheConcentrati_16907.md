# Factors Affecting the Concentration of Dissolved Oxygen in the Thames Estuary

#marine-biology #water-chemistry #estuary-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1956 conference paper, by Water Pollution Research Laboratory staff, analyses what controls dissolved oxygen (DO) in the [[Thames Estuary]]. Long-term sag-curve data, pollutant loads, tidal mixing theory and oxygen-balance accounting are combined to build a predictive model. The model reproduces observed oxygen profiles well enough to test management scenarios. Fresh-water flow at [[Teddington Weir]] is the dominant control on oxygen. Sewage outfalls near [[London Bridge]] are the main oxygen demand. Temperature is a secondary factor. The paper also shows that outfall position alone can decide whether the estuary becomes anaerobic.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The estuary extends from [[Teddington Weir]] (about 19 miles upstream of [[London Bridge]]) to [[Southend]] (about 43 miles below it). Distances are measured in statute miles along the navigable channel. The paper describes the Thames as one of the best-documented estuaries for DO.
- **Problem:** Prolonged oxygen depletion lets bacteria reduce sulphate in seawater to [[Hydrogen Sulphide]]. This causes odour nuisance, corrodes metals and discolours lead paints.
- **Data sources:** Near-daily samples at two mid-estuary points have been taken since 1880 (first by the Metropolitan Board of Works, later by the [[London County Council]]). Since 1920, sampling has averaged about 10 samples per day across the 62 miles from Teddington to Southend.
- **Half-tide adjustment:** Samples taken at different tide states come from different water masses. Plotting them against the position their water would occupy at half-tide collapses them onto one smooth [[Oxygen Sag Curve]].
- **Quarterly sag curves:** Average profiles were produced for each quarter from 1920 to 1955, plus several earlier years. The adjustment is calibrated for average tides, so spring and neap tides can introduce errors of a mile or two.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and oxygen.** Oxygen levels are lowest when fresh-water flow is low and temperature is high. Figure 2 contrasts flows of about 4,500 million gallons per day (m.g.d.) with flows near 500 m.g.d. The paper stresses that a seasonal or year-to-year change in DO cannot be read at a single point without correcting for flow.

**Long-term deterioration.** Sag curves for third quarters show progressive deterioration across the decades. The authors attribute it to a rising pollutional load, a falling rate of oxygen solution from the atmosphere, or both. Statistically, the general oxygen level held steady until 1934 and then fell by a constant amount each year from unknown causes.

**Temperature.** Temperature matters mainly within a few miles of London Bridge. Over the period studied, DO fell by about 2% of saturation for each °C rise. At the sag-curve minimum the effect is only about one-third of 1% per °C. Because oxygen solubility also falls as water warms, the effect on concentration (ppm) differs from the effect on percentage saturation.

**Position of the minimum.** The lowest DO point moves seaward with flow:
- At 200 m.g.d. or less: 10 to 12 miles below London Bridge.
- At 1,000 m.g.d.: about 15 miles below.
- At around 4,000 m.g.d.: a further seaward shift of about 5 miles.

**Pollutant sources.** Loads are expressed as 5-day [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]] (B.O.D.) and as [[Ultimate Oxygen Demand]] (U.O.D.). The main sources are:
- **[[Northern Outfall Sewage Works]]** (Barking, London County Council): about 150 million gallons per day (mgd) of settled sewage plus 40 mgd of activated-sludge effluent. Mean B.O.D. of the final mixed effluent, 1949 to 1954, was 240 ppm. Major upgrades began in 1955.
- **[[Southern Outfall Sewage Works]]:** about 100 mgd of settled sewage, entering on the opposite bank about 2 miles downstream. Mean B.O.D., 1949 to 1954, was 184 ppm. Combined B.O.D. from both outfalls was 290 tons per day, three-quarters of it from Northern Outfall.
- **[[Mogden Sewage Purification Works]]** (Middlesex County Council): mean B.O.D. of 18 ppm in 1950 to 1953. Its load is only about 2% of the outfall total, but it is probably largely responsible for poor upper-reach conditions at low flow.
- **Other works:** about 20 smaller works discharge directly. About 5, including West Kent Main Sewerage Board at Dartford, have appreciable effects.
- **[[Storm Water Discharge]]:** 11 pumping stations and about 15 storm reliefs lie between Hammersmith and Woolwich. Gravitational discharge was about 13% of total storm discharge in 1952. The average B.O.D. load is about 10 tons per day, but a single heavy storm can reach roughly 200 tons.
- **Industrial discharges:** a total B.O.D. load of about 50 tons per day. Individually these are small, except at a few sites.
- **Power-station flue-gas washing** (Battersea and Bankside): returns water with reduced oxygen and some sulphite. This depletes the estuary by about 4 tons of oxygen per day.
- **Fresh-water inputs:** upper-Thames B.O.D. is 2 to 5 ppm. Average load is under 20 tons per day, and up to 100 tons per day at high flow. The [[River Wandle]] is the largest tributary load (about 6 tons per day, likely underestimated).
- **Solids and the sea:** large volumes of solids are dredged annually. A mass-balance discrepancy suggests sea-borne solids enter the estuary, but their quantity and oxygen demand are unknown. This factor was initially ignored.

**Oxygen sources.** Oxygen enters mainly from the atmosphere, but also from:
- Well-aerated Teddington inflow.
- Tributaries.
- Seawater exchange.
- [[Photosynthesis]] by [[Phytoplankton]] in spring and early summer.
- Nitrate reduction and sulphate reduction within the estuary, which consume rather than supply oxygen.

## Key Findings

**1. Oxygen-balance and mixing model**

Tidal mixing cannot be neglected in the Thames. The authors use a two-parameter dispersion model (P₁ seaward, P₂ upstream) over a 6-mile mixing distance, solved numerically. The estuary is treated as unstratified, so distance is the only variable. The model reproduces observed salinity and temperature distributions well. Discharges are summed, provided that oxygen is not depleted to zero, one discharge does not affect another's oxidation rate, and no discharge alters another's flow distribution.

**2. Oxidation kinetics**

Oxidation is treated as exponential, with rate constants (day⁻¹) of 0.10, 0.15, 0.20 and 0.25 at 5, 14, 20 and 24 °C respectively. Nitrification is assumed to proceed alongside carbonaceous oxidation. The U.O.D. expression is U.O.D. = 2.67C + 4.57N (C = organic carbon, N = ammoniacal plus organic nitrogen). The U.O.D./B.O.D. ratio rises as effluents become more purified: about 2.8 for settled sewage, 4.0 for activated-sludge effluent and over 11 for Mogden final effluent.

**4. Oxygen balance (1950 to 1953)**

- Oxygen utilisation: about 841 tons per day.
- Subtracted from U.O.D. input: about 310 tons per day lost through dredging, and about 120 tons per day lost through seaward displacement and mixing.
- Non-atmospheric oxygen entry: about 167 tons per day.
- Atmospheric entry therefore estimated at 674 tons per day, giving an exchange coefficient *f* of **5.7 cm/hr**.
- Independent checks: flexible-tent measurements gave 10.4 cm/hr (acknowledged as too high). Oxygen-balance and B.O.D. methods gave 6.9 cm/hr. Mud uptake is only about 10 tons per day.

**5. Oxygen-source details**

- Teddington inflow: about 69 tons per day, plus about 6 tons per day from tributaries.
- Loss by seaward displacement: about 72 tons per day.
- Gain by seaward tidal mixing: about 78 tons per day.
- Nitrate reduction: about 56 tons per day, mainly from Teddington water.
- Sulphate reduction: about 30 tons per day.

**6. Model validation**

Calculations were run for flows of 500, 1,500 and 3,000 m.g.d., temperatures of 5, 14, 20 and 24 °C, and *f* values of 5.5 and 7.0 cm/hr. That gives 24 curves per discharge. Model fit was tested against 1951 to 1955 observations. The 5.5 cm/hr value fits better in every quarter. Known misfits include upper-reach oxidation that is faster than modelled, and a sudden slowdown near London Bridge at low flow. The authors attribute the slowdown to nitrate reduction starting before oxygen is fully exhausted.

**7. Management scenarios**

- **Flow augmentation:** adding 250 m.g.d. at Teddington raises DO about 10 miles below London Bridge by an average of 24% of saturation. At very low flow, the fully anaerobic zone (about 22 miles, from about 40 miles upstream ending near 30 miles below) shrinks by only 2 to 3 miles. The authors conclude this would be beneficial in the Kew to Putney reach but would not solve the worst conditions.
- **Unit-load response:** Figure 12 gives oxygen depletion per 10 tons U.O.D. per day input at 500 m.g.d. and 20 °C. Worked example: a new works discharging 4 mgd of settled sewage at 150 ppm B.O.D. (U.O.D. about 420 ppm, 7.5 tons per day) 2 miles below London Bridge produces a maximum depletion of about 0.45 ppm, about 5 miles below London Bridge.
- **Outfall relocation:** with outfalls at their present position (about 12 miles below London Bridge), the estuary has an anaerobic zone over 20 miles long. Moving outfalls about 18 miles further seaward would make the estuary fully aerobic under the same conditions. Discharge 30 or 40 miles below London Bridge would give conditions not much worse than the present ones.

**8. Wider applicability**

The authors argue that the method can be applied to other unstratified estuaries, given approximate cross-sectional areas, tidal ranges and fresh-water flow. The exchange coefficient is the hardest parameter to estimate for unpolluted estuaries. The authors judge predictive methods likely to come into use within a few years. The paper cites [[Streeter-Phelps Model|Streeter and Phelps]] (Ohio River, 1925) as an earlier success on tidal-free rivers. Predicting British rivers is harder because they are shallower and have mud deposits and photosynthesis effects.

**9. Caveats**

Deposition of solids, sea-borne solids and photosynthesis are only partly accounted for. Curves may be too high where solids settle out. The seaward boundary beyond Southend is less well defined, and the authors estimate that the modelled curves may be off by up to a mile.
