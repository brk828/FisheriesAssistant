# Experiments on the Commercial Production of Golden Shiners

#ichthyology #aquaculture #Leuciscidae #bait-fish #pond-culture

## Executive Summary

This paper presents experimental results on commercial production methods for [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), a popular bait minnow species in the southeastern United States. Research conducted at the [[Alabama Polytechnic Institute]] Agricultural Experiment Station from 1951-1956 examined various stocking rates, fertilization regimes, supplemental feeding protocols, and management strategies to optimize production of marketable-sized shiners. The study addressed the primary challenge of overproduction of undersized fish and developed methods to consistently produce marketable specimens (3+ inches). Key innovations included winter feeding protocols, transfer of young fish to rearing ponds, and disease prevention treatments.

## Background & Description

The [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*) is one of the most popular bait minnows for bass and crappie fishing in the Southeast. The species feeds primarily on algae and microcrustacea, with secondary consumption of insects, aquatic plants, snails, and occasionally small fish. Previous research by Swingle and Smith (1938) demonstrated that commercial fertilizers increase plankton and fish food production in ponds, providing an economical method to enhance food supply for planktivorous species.

Experiments were conducted in ponds ranging from 0.25 to 3.5 acres with maximum depths of 6 to 12 feet. Ponds were kept free of aquatic weeds except at shorelines. Standard fertilization protocol involved 3-5 applications during February at 2-week intervals using 200 pounds of 8-8-4 fertilizer per acre per application, followed by monthly applications through October. Occasional organic material (peanut meal) supplemented inorganic fertilization when response was slow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Golden shiner production in fertilized ponds demonstrated high variability in both numbers and biomass. Production ranged from 381.7 to 575 pounds per acre, with numbers varying from 34,170 to 393,913 individuals per acre. This variability occurred despite similar management protocols, indicating complex ecological interactions affecting reproduction and survival.

Water quality, particularly dissolved oxygen, emerged as a critical limiting factor. Supplemental feeding at high rates (20-40 pounds per acre per day) during warm months caused oxygen depletion to as low as 0.3 ppm at 4-foot depth, resulting in mortality events during September, October, and January. Winter feeding (November-January) at rates up to 20 pounds per acre per day did not cause oxygen-related mortality.

The primary ecological challenge was overproduction: golden shiners frequently reproduced so heavily that most young remained undersized (below 3 inches) by harvest. Rapidly growing shiners reached sexual maturity at 7-8 months of age, leading to unwanted spawning in rearing ponds.

## Key Findings

**Production in Fertilized Ponds:**
- Stocking 300 medium-sized (3-4 inch) brood fish on March 3 produced 381.7 pounds and 34,170 fish per acre
- Stocking 2,400 large (4-6 inch) brood fish on April 30 produced 575 pounds and 234,780 fish per acre
- Stocking 500 large brood fish in late January produced 393,913 fish per acre (highest number recorded)
- Late stocking (April-May) generally produced lower numbers but more marketable-sized fish
- Early stocking (January-March) produced highest numbers but often resulted in overcrowding

**Economic Importance of Size Distribution:**
- A pond producing 152,444 fish (386 pounds) per acre with only 11% marketable size yielded $244.38 value
- A pond producing 36,000 fish (449 pounds) per acre with 99% marketable size yielded $891.13 value
- Size proved more economically important than total numbers or weight

**Supplemental Feeding Results:**
- Summer feeding (20-40 pounds poultry laying mash per acre per day) caused mortality from oxygen depletion (0.3 ppm recorded)
- Winter feeding (November-January) with soybean cake at 10-20 pounds per acre per day caused no mortality
- [[Auburn No. 1 Fish Feed]] (35% soybean oil meal, 35% peanut oil meal, 15% fish meal, 15% distillers dried solubles) at 10 pounds per acre per day during summer/fall caused no mortality
- Winter feeding trials showed returns above expenses of $437.44 to $714.76 per acre in successful ponds

**Winter Feeding of Small Shiners:**
- Stocking 40,000 small shiners (3 pounds per 1,000) per acre with soybean cake feeding (8.2% of body weight daily) produced 95% survival and doubled weight
- Fish meal feeding at same rate produced 58% survival
- Stocking 150,000 small shiners per acre with lower feeding rate (2.7% of body weight daily) produced 80% survival and doubled weight, converting unmarketable fish worth $0 to marketable fish worth $1,800 per acre with $43.20 feed cost

**Transfer Method (Most Successful):**
- Transferring 40,000 young shiners (0.5 pounds per 1,000) from brood ponds to rearing ponds in June-July
- Fertilization plus Auburn No. 1 feed at 10 pounds per acre per day
- Harvest in October-November or following March
- Produced 34,705-40,800 fish per acre, 550-590 pounds per acre
- Nearly 100% of fish reached marketable size (3-5 inches)
- Gross income less expenses: $770-$912 per acre
- No oxygen-related mortality during summer or fall

**Holding Pond Management:**
- Up to 80,000 large shiners (1,000 pounds) held per acre during winter/spring
- Fed Auburn No. 1 feed at 1% body weight daily
- Fish gained 1 pound per 1,000 in 2 months
- Mortality less than 2%

**Disease and Parasite Control:**
- Potassium permanganate (10 ppm for 1 hour in tanks) controlled fungus, gill flukes, and external protozoans
- Formalin (15 ppm for 6 hours) eliminated gill flukes and external protozoans
- Acriflavine (1 ppm for 12 hours) reduced bacterial disease losses
- Pond treatments with 2 ppm potassium permanganate daily for 2-3 days proved effective
- Pre-stocking treatment of brood fish essential for best results

**Key Management Conclusions:**
- Overproduction of small fish was the primary challenge regardless of stocking rate, timing, or fertilization regime
- Transfer method consistently produced marketable fish but at lower densities
- Winter feeding successfully converted unmarketable small shiners to salable size
- Oxygen management critical when combining fertilization and supplemental feeding
- Economic returns depended more on size distribution than total production
