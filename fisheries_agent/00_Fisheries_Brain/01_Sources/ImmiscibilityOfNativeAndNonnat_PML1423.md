# Immiscibility of Native and Non-Native Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Marsh and Pacey argue that native and non-native fishes of the lower [[Colorado River]] overlap broadly in physical habitat and resource use, and that the presence of non-native fishes, rather than habitat alteration, is what prevents native fish from completing their life cycles. Non-native species, many of them predators, displace or exclude natives. Where non-natives are absent, native fishes such as [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] survive, grow, mature and sometimes reproduce, even in small and marginal habitats. The authors recommend [[Segregated Native Fish Management]], meaning predator-free habitats for natives, and call for prompt action because native fish declines continue.

## Background & Description

The historical Colorado River was an unregulated, free-flowing system of extremes. Discharge at Yuma, Arizona, ranged from near zero in summer droughts to more than 7,000 m³ s⁻¹ in major floods. Annual sediment loading averaged a very large amount from 1925 to 1935 (the scanned value reads "10% metric tons," probably 10⁹ metric tons). Water temperatures ranged from freezing in headwaters to above 30 °C in low desert channels.

The native fauna comprised only 36 species, most living in streams, springs and cienegas. The "big-river" fishes were:
- [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]] and Roundtail Chub (*Gila* spp.)
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- Flannelmouth Sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

The river is now dammed, diverted, channelized, polluted in places, and desiccated in others. Watershed impacts include mining, timber harvest, grazing, agriculture and urbanization. Few free-flowing reaches remain, and none are in historical condition. Most remaining habitats hold more fish species than historically recorded. Of more than 100 species present, about 70 are introduced. Non-natives come from Africa, Asia, marine environments, and other parts of the United States. They include Cyprinidae, [[Centrarchidae]], Salmonidae, Catostomidae and [[Ictaluridae]] members. Notable non-native predators include [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Walleye]] and some trouts. Small species can also be important predators. [[Green Sunfish]] is a known voracious predator on young native fish.

The study was prompted by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion (BO) on lower Colorado River operations (FWS 1997). It was conducted largely for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under the BO's Reasonable and Prudent Alternative No. 4.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** A computer-based keyword search of common and scientific fish names, habitat and chemical descriptors, and life functions returned almost 100,000 citations. About 8,500 were relevant, and roughly 400 were selected for evaluation. Data were organized into 21 matrices by species (native vs non-native), life stage (larva, juvenile, adult), environmental variable, and functional/behavioral attribute. These covered feeding, piscivory and cannibalism, territoriality, spawning, and reproductive mode. **Predation.** Table 2 summarizes published evidence of predation by non-natives on razorback sucker, compiled from the FWS (1998) recovery plan. The listed predators include [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]], [[Green Sunfish]], other sunfishes, [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Flathead Catfish]]. Predation was documented in laboratory, artificial and natural habitats.

**Case studies.** Table 3 lists case studies in which native fish were raised alone or with non-natives. Sites include golf course ponds, wetland ponds, a research park, a national wildlife refuge, a fish hatchery, and [[Lake Mohave]] backwaters. Most are small, earthen, and of variable water quality, with occasional oxygen depletion or extreme temperature and pH. Bonytail and razorback sucker completed life cycles at several sites with no non-natives. Survival was impaired at sites with sunfish, largemouth bass, channel catfish or green sunfish.

## Key Findings

- **Habitat and resource overlap.** Native and non-native fishes overlap broadly in physical habitat, resource use and life functions. No attribute of either group clearly favors one over the other.
- **Trophic and reproductive differences.** Non-natives include more piscivores, while most natives are functional generalists. Most natives are broadcast spawners with no parental care. Most non-natives build nests and provide at least some parental care.
- **Non-native predation as the main driver.** The authors conclude that non-native predation on early life stages is the single most important factor in native fish decline. They report no evidence that habitat per se caused the degraded status of the native community. Competition is acknowledged as important but considered less significant than predation.
- **Natives thrive without non-natives.** In predator-free altered habitats, bonytail and razorback sucker survived, grew, matured and in some cases reproduced and recruited. Bonytail are especially prolific.
- **Repatriation setbacks.** Repatriation programs for Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker (central Arizona and lower Colorado River) had limited success. Predation by non-natives was a major mortality factor in each case. Even the [[Lake Mohave]] razorback sucker program, widely considered successful, had to address non-native predation from its start.
- **Call to action.** The authors recommend segregated management, emphasizing predator-free habitats stocked with genetically defined native populations. They criticize redundant parallel planning programs by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and urge unity of purpose. Segregated management will not recover native fishes by itself, but it should improve their survival until recovery strategies are defined.
- **Data limits.** The authors state that other big-river fishes might respond similarly to natives, but experiments on them have largely not been performed.
- **Context of decline.** Despite major investment in the 1990s, including the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]], populations of the imperiled native fishes are stable in few places and declining in most.
