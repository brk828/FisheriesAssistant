# Peer Review of Streamflow, Geomorphology, and Food Web Studies for Endangered Fish Recovery in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #river-ecology #adaptive-management

## Executive Summary

This report summarizes a peer review and roundtable discussion conducted February 6-7, 1995, in Grand Junction, Colorado, evaluating the relationship between streamflow, geomorphology, and food web studies in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] Recovery Program for four endangered fishes: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). Six peer reviewers examined FY 1994 scopes-of-work and 1993 annual reports for ongoing [[Aspinall Unit]] and [[Flaming Gorge]] studies. While a thorough integration review was not possible due to inadequate documentation and the broad scope, reviewers commended the Recovery Program for initiating ecosystem-level studies (geomorphology and food web) to better define streamflows for recovery. The review identified critical needs for improved strategic planning through a systems approach, enhanced research proposal documentation, better annual reporting, and synthesis of existing information. Reviewers emphasized that much valuable information has already been collected and that informed management decisions could be made with available data, potentially leading to a recovery success story.

## Background & Description

The [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] Recovery Program was established to protect and recover four endemic endangered fishes while allowing future water development. The program is a collaborative effort involving the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], Western Area Power Administration, states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming, water users, and environmental organizations. Five program elements guide recovery efforts: (1) provision of instream flows, (2) habitat development and maintenance, (3) native fish stocking, (4) management of nonnative species and sport fishing, and (5) research, monitoring, and data management.

The Recovery Program evolved from early studies focused on ecological requirements of endangered fishes, particularly instream flow and habitat requirements for [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Recent expansion included geomorphology and food web studies, representing a shift toward ecosystem-level understanding. The peer review was initiated to evaluate methodology in food web studies and expanded to include streamflow and geomorphology projects under the [[Flaming Gorge]] and [[Aspinall Unit]] umbrella studies.

Six peer reviewers were selected representing three disciplines: geomorphology (E.D. Andrews, Ellen Wohl), large river ecology (Ken Lubinski, Jack Stanford), and food web relationships (Mark B. Bain, Wendell L. Minckley). The primary goal was to develop recommendations for reoperation of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Aspinall Unit]] dams to provide adequate streamflows for endangered fish recovery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] ecosystem has been significantly altered by dam construction and water development. The [[Aspinall Unit]] and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] have caused physical changes in the [[Gunnison River]] and [[Colorado River]], affecting channel morphology, sediment transport, and flow regimes. These alterations impact habitat availability for endangered fishes, particularly backwater habitats critical for early life stages.

Recommended streamflows were historically established based on fish use rather than optimal requirements, creating uncertainty about defining sufficient flows for recovery. The ecosystem approach recognizes that recovery must consider basin-wide processes rather than site-specific or individual river segments. Tributaries, upstream and downstream reaches, and the interaction between hydrological and geomorphological processes all influence habitat production and maintenance.

Nonnative fish species present additional challenges through predation and competition with endangered fishes during vulnerable early life stages, contributing to absent or low recruitment. The endangered fishes historically exhibited strong year classes, suggesting that recovery efforts should provide suitable conditions for periodic recruitment (e.g., one year out of 5-10 years) to maintain self-sustaining populations.

Flooded bottomland habitats and off-channel areas are recognized as ecologically valuable for endangered fish recovery. The relationship between discharge, channel morphology, and backwater dynamics is crucial for understanding habitat availability across different life stages.

## Key Findings

**Major Limitations Identified:**

1. **Topic Too Broad**: The three study areas (streamflow, geomorphology, food web) required more time than available for adequate evaluation of relationships and integration. Studies appeared segregated by focusing on either [[Colorado River]] or [[Green River]] rather than the entire Upper Basin ecosystem.

2. **Inadequate Documentation**: Scopes-of-work and annual reports lacked sufficient detail for comprehensive peer review. Objectives were not measurable with target dates; methods were vague without references; principal investigators were sometimes unclear; and literature reviews were often absent.

3. **Lack of Strategic Planning**: No comprehensive systems approach focusing on the five recovery elements. Studies appeared to reflect individual researcher interests rather than integrated strategic planning. The same individuals appeared on numerous studies, suggesting direction by a few persons rather than comprehensive planning.

4. **Fragmented Information**: Despite substantial data collection, information appeared fragmented and disjointed. Past work seemed redundant, with similar information still being collected on endangered fish biology, particularly [[Colorado Squawfish]].

**Specific Study Concerns:**

- Recommended streamflows based on fish use, not optimal requirements; uncertainty remains about defining sufficient flows for recovery
- Proposed test streamflows lack controls for evaluating physical changes and biological responses
- Short-term physical change studies not indicative of long-term streamflow effects
- Food web study methods may not adequately describe energy flow through ecosystem; limited to invertebrate distribution and abundance
- Little effort devoted to nonnative fish status and trends despite their importance
- Management decisions for [[Colorado Squawfish]] inadequate for other three endangered species

**Positive Developments:**

- Recovery Program correctly expanding to ecosystem approach integrating biology, hydrology, and geomorphology
- Sediment and channel dynamics studies (Project 37) provide essential first step for understanding geomorphological effects
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] populations considered stable and appear to have responded to streamflow modifications
- Program participants beginning to integrate designs and results across disciplines

**Critical Recommendations:**

1. **Systems Approach**: Implement comprehensive planning involving (1) inventory ("Where are we?"), (2) strategic planning ("Where do we want to be?"), (3) operational planning ("How do we get there?"), and (4) evaluation ("Did we make it?"). This represents true [[Adaptive Management]].

2. **Improved Research Proposals**: Write complete proposals with measurable objectives and target dates; describe expected results and integration with other studies; provide concise methods with justification and references; include thorough literature reviews; clearly identify principal investigators and their qualifications.

3. **Enhanced Annual Reports**: Evaluate data collected; compare with previous data; summarize accomplishments and whether objectives were met; explain necessary refinements in experimental design.

4. **Information Synthesis**: Analyze and synthesize available information routinely rather than attempting integration of fragmented data later. Available information may be close to a success story for [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]].

5. **Research Gap Identification**: Shift emphasis from [[Colorado Squawfish]] to critical life stages of other three endangered species. Revise [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] to monitor all endangered and nonnative fishes at critical life stages in priority reaches.

6. **Peer Review Process**: Continue independent peer review by three experts for non-complex studies; use review panels for large, complex, or controversial proposals; provide clear guidelines and complete documentation; allow dialogue between reviewers and investigators; ensure Program Coordinators summarize comments and make funding recommendations.

**Integration Needs:**

Researchers should make first integration of information from various disciplines, as they are most familiar with objectives, methods, data, and results. Basin-wide processes should be examined rather than site-specific processes. Future research should emphasize integration across disciplines as recommended by Stanford (1994). Stable isotope analyses recommended for more comprehensive food web dynamics assessment.

**Management Implications:**

Recovery should be viewed from ecosystem perspective for entire Upper Basin including tributaries. Measurable recovery objectives needed to evaluate program success in developing self-sustaining populations. Once achieved, efforts can expand [[Colorado Squawfish]] range into unoccupied historic habitat and recover/reintroduce [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] populations. Responses of endangered and nonnative fishes to enhanced or restored habitats may be better evaluation criteria than test flows alone.
