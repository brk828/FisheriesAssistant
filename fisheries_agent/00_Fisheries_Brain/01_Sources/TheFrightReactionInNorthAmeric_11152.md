# The Fright Reaction in North American Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by [[Wolfgang Pfeiffer]] (Department of Zoology, University of British Columbia) documents the [[Fright Reaction]] in North American freshwater fish for the first time in detail. Skin extracts from five [[Cyprinidae]] species and two [[Catostomidae]] species were tested in more than 120 experiments. Cyprinids showed a threshold of 1/1000 N extract or lower, while suckers required at least 1/100 N. Cyprinids responded strongly to extracts from other cyprinids, suckers responded strongly to sucker extract, and [[Coho Salmon]] skin produced no reaction in any tested school. Histological work linked the alarm response to [[Alarm Substance Cells]] in the epidermis, and ontogeny experiments showed that young fish carry alarm substance before they can respond to it.

## Background & Description

The [[Fright Reaction]] is an innate, chemically mediated antipredator response. When a predator wounds a fish, alarm substance is released from the skin and causes the school to flee and abandon feeding areas. Earlier work by [[Karl von Frisch]] (1938, 1941), [[Franz Schutz]] (1956) and Pfeiffer (1960, 1962) had described the reaction in European and Asian fish, mainly in the order [[Ostariophysi]]. The alarm substance originates in club-shaped epidermal cells, termed "Schreckstoffzellen" (alarm substance cells).

The study focused on two features of interest in North American fauna:
- The [[Catostomidae]] (suckers), which are confined to North America and had not been studied previously.
- The [[Northern Squawfish]], a predatory cyprinid, unusual in a family that is mostly non-predatory. It fills the ecological niche of the pike in British Columbia.

Species were selected from the Vancouver area to represent five cyprinid genera, forming an ecological cross-section of British Columbia cyprinids. Juveniles of all species school, while adult squawfish and longnose dace are solitary. The longnose dace is a bottom-dwelling species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predator-prey interactions:** Northern squawfish consumed a wide range of prey. In a May 1962 survey at Baker Lake, near Quesnel, B.C., 140 fish were examined. Squawfish of 17 to 26 cm total length (130 fish) ate mainly insect larvae and molluscs. Larger squawfish of 29 to 46 cm ate mainly fish, including redside shiners, suckers and a toad (*Bufo boreas*). Squawfish were cannibalistic, and 9 cm individuals were observed eating 4 cm companions. Because trout and char were rare in Baker Lake, squawfish there preyed mainly on their own species, shiners and suckers.

**Alarm chemistry in nature:** Alarm substance was released when a squawfish swallowed a smaller conspecific, and water from its tank alarmed test schools only after such predation. Large squawfish are probably indifferent to alarm substance in nature, as are young captive squawfish.

**Taxonomic relationships:** Cross-species reactions reflect close affinities. Hybrids are recorded between redside shiner and longnose dace, redside shiner and peamouth chub, northern squawfish and redside shiner, and northern squawfish and peamouth chub.

**Threshold comparisons:** The threshold of field-captured North American cyprinids (about 1/1000 N) matches that of field-captured European fish. Aquarium-hatched European and Japanese fish respond at about 1:50,000, which is 50 to 100 times lower.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution:** The fright reaction occurs in five North American cyprinid species and two catostomid species, extending its known range to the Americas.
- **Thresholds:** Cyprinids react at 1/1000 N extract or lower. Suckers require at least 1/100 N, and a sucker would flee only at high concentrations.
- **Intraspecific specificity:** Cyprinids react very strongly to conspecific extract, with similar values across species and similar to European cyprinids. Suckers react most strongly to their own alarm substance.
- **Interspecific tests:** Of 36 possible species combinations, 29 were tested. Every cyprinid reacted strongly to alarm substance from all other tested species. Cyprinids react much less strongly to sucker extract, and suckers react less strongly to cyprinid extract.
- **Null control:** Coho salmon skin produced no reaction in any tested cyprinid or sucker school, while the same schools responded to shiner extract, confirming responsiveness.
- **Histology:** Alarm substance cells occur in all species that showed the reaction. Sucker epidermis closely resembles that of cyprinids. Thicker epidermis in larger fish contains larger alarm substance cells.
- **Cell density and size:** Northern squawfish averaged about 1200 alarm substance cells per sq. mm, and longnose dace about 1600 per sq. mm. The carp had only about 400 per sq. mm, and its cells were smaller.
- **Carp comparison:** Carp show a poor fright reaction, with a threshold of 1/50 N compared with 1/1000 N for minnows. Total alarm substance cell volume in longnose dace and crucian carp is about 30 to 100 times greater than in carp, consistent with the weaker response.
- **Ontogeny:** Zebrafish contain alarm substance at 20 days but respond only from 32 days at 26 °C. Redside shiners respond from 42 days at 26 °C. Fish raised at lower temperatures, which grew more slowly, acquired the response later.
- **Sample base:** More than 120 experiments were conducted in total. The ontogeny study comprised 90 tests (75 with zebrafish and 15 with redside shiners).
- **Conclusion:** Very young cyprinids carry alarm substance but do not respond until they reach a certain size or developmental state. The fright reaction probably has a universal distribution among cyprinids and is an innate, conserved antipredator trait.
