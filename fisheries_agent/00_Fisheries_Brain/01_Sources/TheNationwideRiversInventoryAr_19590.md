# Nationwide Rivers Inventory: Arizona Component River Data Summaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riverine-habitats

## Executive Summary

This document is the transmittal package and river data summaries for the Arizona component of the [[Nationwide Rivers Inventory]], issued by the [[National Park Service]] Western Region in April 1982 (list dated January 1982). It covers 14 Arizona rivers or river segments that were judged to be significant, relatively natural and free-flowing. Each summary is a resource "blueprint" covering physical description, flow, natural values, development, land ownership, conservation status and information sources. The rivers contain endangered fishes (including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Gila Trout]]), trout and warmwater sport fisheries, and important bald eagle and peregrine falcon habitat. Water development, tribal water rights and proposed dams are the main competing pressures.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The inventory is a baseline dataset intended for Congress, agencies and private groups. Its uses include monitoring river condition, informing water and energy decisions, supporting state and local conservation, and checking how well the [[Wild and Scenic Rivers Act]] system represents the nation's rivers. Summaries were compiled from USGS reports, planning studies, natural area surveys and agency plans. They are meant to be updated as budgets allow.

**Selection criteria.** Rivers of 25 miles or longer were inventoried, plus shorter segments with exceptional recreation potential. Three criteria applied:
- degree to which the river is free-flowing;
- degree to which the river and corridor are undeveloped;
- outstanding natural and cultural characteristics.

Dams and channelization disqualified segments. Development within a quarter mile of the banks was scored by points, and segments with 100 or more points per mile were removed. Cities over 10,000 people, power plants and active strip mines also disqualified segments. Intermittent streams were excluded except in arid regions. Video and field evaluation by helicopter in 1979 refined the list.

**Scope of the Arizona component.** The 14 rivers, as listed in the contents, are:
1. [[Black River]]
2. East Fork Black River (including North Fork)
3. [[Chevelon Creek]]
4. [[West Clear Creek]]
5. [[Colorado River]]
6. [[Little Colorado River]]
7. [[Eagle Creek]]
8. [[Gila River]]
9. [[Paria River]]
10. [[Salt River]]
11. [[San Francisco River]]
12. [[Tonto Creek]]
13. [[Verde River]]
14. [[East Verde River]]

**Summary structure.** Each river summary follows a common format: inventory status, segment description, video coverage, notable features (scenic, recreation, geologic, fishery, wildlife, botanic, cultural), outstandingly remarkable values (confirmed or potential), water resources, land resources, conservation status, additional information and references.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography.** Most Arizona segments fall in the Basin and Range Province (Mexican Highland section, 22d) or the Colorado Plateaus (Grand Canyon section, 21e). The Colorado River segments also cross the Sonoran Desert section (22b). Only 7 of the 25 provinces and 24 of the 78 sections were represented in the wider national system at the time, and the arid Basin and Range and Colorado Plateaus had the fewest significant free-flowing rivers. See [[Fenneman Physiographic Provinces]].

**Fishes and aquatic habitat.**
- **Endangered natives:** The Colorado River segments hold [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]], all federally listed as endangered. The [[Razorback Sucker]] may soon be proposed as threatened. [[Woundfin]] and [[Gila Trout]] are federally listed, and the Gila trout is confirmed in the Gila watershed.
- **Chub spawning:** The lower nine miles of the [[Little Colorado River]] hold humpback chub breeding. This was the last identified spawning ground in the Lower Colorado River Basin.
- **Sport fisheries:** The Lees Ferry to Glen Canyon Dam reach is described as a trophy trout fishery, "the best in Arizona and perhaps the Southwest," drawing about 40,000 anglers in 1979. Lower Black River has one of the best [[Smallmouth Bass]] fisheries in the state. Other warmwater species include catfish, sunfish and bass.
- **Introduced species:** Carp, bullheads and various non-native fish have an adverse effect on native fishes, as noted for the Colorado and Salt rivers.

**Birds and mammals.** The [[Bald Eagle]] and [[Peregrine Falcon]] are federally endangered and use several corridors for nesting, wintering or migration, including the Black, Eagle, Gila, Salt, Tonto, Verde and East Verde rivers. Eagle Creek's lower gorge hosts about 10% of the U.S. black hawk population. The Chevelon Canyon area has almost 200 known vertebrate species, over half of them riparian-dependent. The Grand Canyon reach records about 284 bird species and roughly 22 terrestrial mammal species.

**Flow regime.** Many Arizona rivers are flashy, peaking in spring snowmelt and during late summer or early fall thunderstorms. Several were heavily flooded in the winters of 1978–79 and 1979–80. The Black River recorded 40,200 cfs on 12/18/78. Chevelon Creek exceeded previous highs by over 8,000 cfs. Dam regulation on the Colorado and Little Colorado systems changed flow patterns substantially.

**Water quality.** Most segments are rated suitable for full-body contact under the 1979 Arizona Surface Water Quality Standards. Exceptions include the Paria (partial body contact, turbid), Eagle Creek (partial body contact) and Colorado River segments with high salinity and sediment below Imperial Dam.

**Competing uses.** Principal pressures include:
- the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] peaking power proposals, which would raise maximum releases from 31,500 cfs to as much as 40,000 cfs;
- the proposed [[Camelsback Dam]] on the Gila River, which would inundate at least the upper half of the Gila Box;
- the Hooker Dam and [[Central Arizona Project]] storage studies;
- enlargement of [[Roosevelt Dam]], which would inundate the lower reach of Tonto Creek;
- water withdrawals for mining and municipal supply by [[Phelps Dodge Corporation]] at Morenci;
- tribal water rights claims on the Black, Salt and East Fork rivers.

**Land management.** Ownership ranges from National Forest and National Park lands to Indian reservations. Tribal consent is often required before protection can be extended, for example on the Black and Salt rivers.

## Key Findings

- **Inventory scale.** The national inventory identified about 61,700 miles in 1,524 segments as probably qualifying for the National System. This is just under 2% of the estimated 3,250,000 river and stream miles in the U.S. (excluding Alaska and Hawaii). Average segment length was 27.5 miles, compared with 40.5 miles nationally.
- **Arid-region gap.** Arid provinces have few listed rivers because of limited water and intense water demand. The Great Plains, Colorado Plateaus and Basin and Range provinces have the fewest significant free-flowing rivers.
- **Key segment figures (inventory length vs. total length):**
  - Black River: 113 miles inventoried, avg. flow 390 cfs (25-year record)
  - East Fork Black River: 26 miles, avg. flow 142 cfs (17 years)
  - Chevelon Creek: 62 of 82 miles, avg. flow 50.3 cfs (44 years)
  - West Clear Creek: 32 of 36 miles, avg. flow 66.3 cfs (14 years)
  - Colorado River: 370 miles inventoried in three segments (31, 11 and 228 miles) of a 1,450-mile river
  - Little Colorado River: 115 of 310 miles, avg. flow 233 cfs (32 years)
  - Eagle Creek: 58 miles, avg. flow 49.6 cfs (35 years)
  - Gila River: 155 miles in three segments (32, 26 and 97) of a 630-mile river
  - Paria River: 27 of 92 miles, avg. flow 29.4 cfs (56 years)
  - Salt River: 61 of 190 miles, avg. flow 618 cfs (55 years)
  - San Francisco River: 104 of 163 miles, avg. flow 75.2 cfs (52 years)
  - Tonto Creek: 60 of 66 miles, avg. flow 134 cfs (38 years)
  - Verde River: 14 of 193 miles, avg. flow 516 cfs (34 years)
  - East Verde River: 56 miles, estimated avg. flow about 55 cfs (16 years)
- **Confirmed values with endangered species.** Confirmed outstandingly remarkable features include bald eagle and peregrine falcon nesting or wintering habitat (Black, Eagle, Gila, Tonto, Verde, East Verde, Chevelon), endangered Colorado River fishes (Colorado and Little Colorado rivers), and Gila trout in the Gila watershed.
- **Largest river, least protected.** The Colorado River has the most comprehensive inventory coverage, but no video tape was made for it because of budget limits. Its Grand Canyon segment is world-renowned for scenery, geology and whitewater rafting.
- **Protection status.** Most segments have no formal protection beyond National Forest or Park management. The Paria River (Paria Canyon Primitive Area, about 18,909 acres in Arizona and 8,606 acres in Utah) and West Clear Creek (proposed State Natural Area and Forest Service wilderness recommendation) are notable exceptions.
- **Wild and Scenic study rivers.** Segments of the Salt, San Francisco and Verde rivers were designated by Congress in 1978 for study, so they are excluded from the Inventory. Forest Service draft studies recommend wild or scenic classifications for portions of these rivers.
- **Flood impacts.** Record floods in 1978–80 caused heavy damage, altered channels and debris loads (Eagle Creek, Gila River, Tonto Creek), and their ecological effects are unknown in several cases (Chevelon Creek).
- **Information gaps.** The summaries acknowledge incomplete data for several segments, including the San Francisco River fishery and wildlife, Gila River water quality for Segment C, and plant surveys on the Verde River. The document is intended as a living resource, with comments requested for updates.
- **Contacts for follow-up.** Key contacts include the NPS Western Regional Office (San Francisco), the Arizona Department of Game and Fish, the Arizona State Parks Board Natural Areas Coordinator, Bureau of Reclamation (Boulder City, NV), Bureau of Land Management Safford and Yuma districts, U.S. Forest Service offices, and the White Mountain and San Carlos Apache and Hualapai and Navajo tribes.
