# The American Shad

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Fishery Leaflet (No. 614, August 1968) by Randall P. Cheek of the [[Bureau Of Commercial Fisheries]] Biological Laboratory, Beaufort, N.C., reviews the life history, fisheries, and management of the [[American Shad]] (*[[Alosa Sapidissima]]*). The leaflet focuses mainly on Atlantic Coast stocks because little information was available on Pacific Coast populations. It documents a long decline in commercial catch since the 1896 peak, a growing [[Sport Fishing]] sector, and the failure of [[Hatchery Stocking]] to restore abundance. It identifies [[Water Pollution]], [[Dam Construction]], and [[Overfishing]] as the primary threats and recommends pollution abatement, fish-passage facilities, and regulation of fishing.

## Background & Description

The American shad is the largest member of the herring family [[Clupeidae]] in North America. It is known by common names including "white shad," "roe shad," and "shad." It has a compressed, fusiform body, single soft-rayed dorsal and anal fins, a deeply forked tail, a strongly serrated belly midline, and large easily lost scales. Its back is silvery with a bluish-green metallic luster. A large dark shoulder spot is followed by several smaller spots. A longer-than-deep cheek bone and the outline of the lower jaw distinguish it from the [[Alewife]], [[Blueback Herring]], and [[Hickory Shad]].

Mature males average 2 to 3 pounds and mature females 3 to 4 pounds. Shad weighing up to 15 pounds were recorded in the early years of fishing, but few now exceed 9 pounds.

**Introduction history:** In 1871, about 12,000 young fish were transported by train across the continent and planted in the [[Sacramento River]], California. Later plantings in the Sacramento and Columbia Rivers succeeded. Unsuccessful introduction attempts were made in the Mississippi River drainage, peninsular Florida, Colorado, the Great Lakes, and Great Salt Lake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Anadromy and spawning migration:** The American shad is an [[Anadromous Fish]] that spends most of its life at sea and returns to freshwater streams to spawn. Young spend their first summer in their natal stream and migrate to the ocean in fall when they are about 3 to 6 inches long. Males mature at 3, 4, or 5 years and females at 4, 5, or 6 years, with most spawners aged 3 or 4. Entry timing into rivers varies with latitude: as early as November in the St. Johns River, Florida; as early as February in North Carolina; and as late as July in eastern Canada. In the 19th century shad ascended streams 200 to 300 miles to the headwaters, but dams now confine them to areas below barriers.

**Spawning conditions:** Spawning begins once water temperature reaches at least 53°F and usually ends by 75°F. It occurs from after sunset to about midnight, with one female accompanied by several males. Eggs are released in open water and fertilized externally. Eggs are about one-sixteenth inch in diameter, swell to about one-eighth inch, are transparent to pale pink or amber, and are slightly heavier than water, so they sink and drift near the bottom. Hatching takes 3 to 8 days depending on temperature (6 days at 63°F). Each female lays 100,000 to 600,000 eggs, depending on size and natal stream.

**Feeding ecology:** Young shad have small teeth and feed mainly on insects and small crustaceans in their first summer, including surface feeding in the evening. After leaving rivers in fall, they lose their teeth and feed on [[Plankton Feeding]] by straining water through [[Gill Rakers]], as adults do. Adults do not feed during the spawning run, although they will strike artificial lures on spawning grounds, which biologists believe is an instinctive defense of those grounds.

**Migration:** Adult shad that survive spawning migrate to sea and north to the [[Gulf of Maine]], where they feed on plankton through summer and fall. They are believed to winter in deep water off the Middle and South Atlantic States. Tagging recoveries from Gulf of Maine releases extended from Georgia to Quebec. The guidance mechanism for homing is described as still a mystery. Post-spawning mortality differs by region: fish spawning in South Atlantic coastal streams die after spawning, and the proportion of survivors returning to sea rises progressively north of North Carolina. Survivors that spawn again are called "repeaters."

**Age and growth:** Age is determined from scale annuli, which resemble tree rings. The first ring is a false annulus marking the transition from fresh to salt water. Feeding stops during the spawning run, so the scale edge is resorbed, creating a "spawning mark" that is counted as an annulus. Average lengths are:

| Age | Average length |
|---|---|
| 1 year | 5 to 6 inches |
| 2 years | 9 to 10 inches |
| 3 years | 10 to 14 inches |
| 4 years | 15 to 16 inches |
| 5 years | 18 to 19 inches |

Growth is rapid for the first 3 years and slows afterward.

## Key Findings

**Economic history of the commercial fishery (Atlantic Coast):**
- In colonial times shad were very abundant. The peak landing was in 1896, with more than 50 million pounds.
- Production fell to about 25 million pounds by 1908, when shad still ranked third in value among U.S. fishery products.
- Since 1908 the yield has declined further. Shad now rank 40th in weight and 28th in value in the total U.S. catch.
- The current Atlantic Coast catch is about 10 million pounds.

**Pacific Coast:** The commercial fishery began in 1890 and reached 7 million pounds in 1915. Today it is about 1.5 million pounds annually. The author attributes this decline mainly to lack of market, not necessarily to lower abundance.

**Sport fishery:** Over the last 20 years shad have become important sport fish during spawning runs. The main sport rivers are the St. Johns, Ogeechee, Edisto, Pamlico-Tar, Susquehanna, Delaware, and Connecticut Rivers on the Atlantic Coast, and the Sacramento and American Rivers on the Pacific Coast. The St. Johns and Connecticut are the most important. The estimated annual sport catch is about one-half million shad (1.5 million pounds), with additional unrecorded catches in several rivers.

**Commercial gear:** Gear has changed little over time, though techniques and materials have. Native Americans used bush nets, weirs, spears, and bows and arrows. Later, haul seines were the most efficient and most used gear. Current principal gears are pound nets and stake gill nets in broad estuaries and bays, drift gill nets in lower river reaches, and seines, traps, gill nets, and bow nets in narrow headwater streams. Fish wheels are still used to a limited extent in some North Carolina streams. Most catch is shipped to Baltimore, Philadelphia, and New York during spawning runs. Females are more valuable commercially because of demand for [[Shad Roe]].

**Market trends:** Frozen and convenience fish products reduced demand for shad, which are usually sold whole and fresh. Roe is sold separately, and fillets are a limited product. The authors conclude future food use depends on developing new products acceptable to modern consumers.

**Sport tackle:** Shad are caught by trolling or casting small spinners, spoons, and weighted jigs, often using a two-lure "shad-rig." Their mouths are tender, so light tackle is recommended.

**Research and management:**
- [[Hatchery Stocking]] from 1880 to 1950 did not significantly increase shad abundance, despite hatcheries built in the late 1800s.
- Over the previous two decades, the Bureau of Commercial Fisheries and several states jointly and independently studied the Atlantic Coast resource. Studies have allowed effective management of shad populations in several rivers.
- The primary problems are pollution, dams, and overfishing. The most effective measures are pollution abatement, construction of [[Fish Passage Facilities]] (e.g., the [[Connecticut River]] fish lift at Holyoke, Massachusetts), and fishing regulation. Regulations are set by each state and vary.
- The leaflet estimates that restoring annual production to 19th-century levels would make the commercial catch worth more than $6.5 million, and would greatly expand sport fishing opportunities.

**Notable pollution case:** Young shad killed by pollution in the [[Anacostia River]], Washington, D.C., are illustrated as an example of habitat degradation.

**Key references:** The leaflet cites foundational works including Leim (1924) on factors limiting abundance, Stevenson (1899) on Atlantic Coast fisheries, Talbot and Sykes (1958) on Atlantic Coast migrations, and Walburg and Nichols (1967) on biology, management, and fishery status. The bibliography comprises about 13 references in total.
