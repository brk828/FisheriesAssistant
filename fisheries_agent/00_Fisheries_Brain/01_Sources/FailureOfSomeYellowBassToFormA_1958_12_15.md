# Failure of Some Yellow Bass to Form Annuli in Later Years of Life

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #freshwater-fisheries #FishAgeAndGrowth

## Executive Summary
This study, presented at the 20th Midwest Wildlife Conference (1958) by Marvin M. Buchholz of the Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit, examines anomalies in age determination of [[Yellow Bass]] (*Morone interrupta*) populations in Clear Lake, Iowa. Since its introduction in the early 1930s, the [[Yellow Bass]] population underwent an establishment phase followed by a decades-long decline in growth rate (1937–1949 and beyond). Using scale annulus analysis on fish collected from 1952–1958, the author demonstrates that several year classes (notably 1946, 1948, and especially 1949) ceased forming distinguishable annuli in later years of life, leading to significant errors in age and year-class assignment via traditional [[Scale Aging]] methods. This finding challenges standard assumptions in [[Fish Age Determination]] techniques and reveals that some individuals lived up to 9 years — two years beyond the previously accepted maximum lifespan for the species.

## Background & Description
- **Species:** Yellow Bass, *Morone interrupta* Gill (family [[Moronidae]])
- **Aging structure:** Scales, examined for annuli (growth rings) corresponding to total length increments.
- **Scale Pattern Anomalies:**
  - The 1946 year class exhibited an unusual alternating growth pattern: strong growth in years 1, 2, 3, and 6, but poor/absent growth in years 4, 5, and 7.
  - The dominant 1949 year class showed characteristic, recognizable scale patterns allowing retrospective identification of "false" 5-year-old assignments in 1956 and 1957 samples.
  - Many members of the 1949 year class displayed **severe scale resorption** after 1955, likely contributing to the failure to lay down new annuli and complicating retrospective back-calculation of length.
- **Growth Data (Table 1 summary):** Average calculated total length (inches) at each annulus for the 1949 and 1952 year classes showed near-identical patterns at annuli 2 (~2.8-3.3 in), 3 (~5.5-6.0 in), and beyond (~7.7-8.9 in through annulus 6), supporting the reclassification of "5-year-olds" as actually belonging to the older 1949 cohort rather than a true 5-year age class.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** [[Clear Lake]], a freshwater lake in Iowa; species introduced/established during the early 1930s (per [[Bailey and Harrison 1945]]).
- **Population History:**
  - Establishment phase: first 6–7 years post-introduction (early-to-late 1930s).
  - Relatively abundant year classes did not appear until the late 1930s.
  - Growth rate decline documented from 1937–1949 ([[Carlander, Lewis, Ruhr, and Cleary 1953]]) and continuing decline thereafter.
- **Predation Pressure:** The 1950 year class's young-of-the-year were eliminated by the following summer, likely due to [[Predation]], despite good reproduction that year (per [[Ridenhour 1958]]).
- **Mortality Events:** A die-off in November 1955 eliminated the entire 1952 year class — notably, this selective elimination did not affect the 1948 and 1949 year classes, possibly linked to relative year-class abundance.
- **Sampling Method:** Routine experimental [[Gill Netting]] (1951–1958) used to track year-class presence and abundance in the lake.

## Key Findings
- **Annulus Formation Failure:** Multiple year classes (1946, 1948, and especially 1949) failed to form annuli in certain later years of life, an anomaly not previously well documented for this species.
  - The 1949 year class apparently stopped growing and/or forming annuli after spring 1954.
  - The 1946 year class failed to produce annuli in 1954 and 1955.
  - The 1948 year class failed to form annuli in both 1955 and 1956.
- **Vanishing and Reappearing Year Classes:** The 1950 and 1951 year classes were completely absent from gill net catches for 3–4 consecutive years, then reappeared suddenly and in large numbers bearing what appeared to be "5 annuli" — but growth pattern analysis strongly suggests these were misidentified older fish (from the dominant 1949 year class), not true 5-year-olds.
- **Reinterpretation of Age Data:** Comparative growth curve analysis (Table 1) confirms the "5-year-old" fish captured in 1955 and 1956 more closely matched the established growth trajectory of the 1949 year class than either the 1952 year class (which would imply two false annuli) or a genuine 1950/1951 origin (which would imply a missed annulus).
- **Extended Longevity:** Some [[Yellow Bass]] were found to live up to **9 years**, two years beyond the previously accepted maximum lifespan for the species.
- **Methodological Caution:** The study concludes that [[Scale Aging]] via annulus counts can produce inaccurate year-class distribution and maximum age estimates when fish undergo periods of arrested growth or scale resorption, underscoring the need for corroborating evidence (e.g., mark-recapture, length-frequency, or independent year-class tracking) in fisheries age/growth studies.
