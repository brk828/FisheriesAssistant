# Professional Responsibility in Fish Conservation

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #pupfish

## Executive Summary

This invited editorial, published in [[Environmental Biology of Fishes]] (1999), argues that biologists, especially university researchers, have a professional obligation to take part in the conservation of fishes and their habitats. The author, a retired state agency fishery biologist with nearly 40 years of experience in the American Southwest, contends that species and habitat losses have reached a point where complacency is unacceptable. He calls for closer communication between agency and academic scientists, and for changes to university reward systems and undergraduate curricula so that [[Scholarship of Application]] receives at least equal weight with [[Scholarship of Discovery]].

## Background & Description

The editorial is a commentary, not a field study, and it reports no original sampling data. Its keywords are advocacy, biodiversity loss, professional responsibility, scholarship of application, and scholarship of discovery. The author frames the problem with a metaphor: scientists who continue esoteric research while habitats collapse are like architects who decorate a house whose foundation is failing.

The author sees three main drivers of loss: growing human populations, high resource consumption in Western nations, and economic forces that favor resource-dependent industries over ecological sustainability. He notes that the United States is now the world's third most populous nation.

The piece draws on the history of fish conservation in the Southwest. It cites [[Robert Rush Miller]]'s 1961 work *Man and the Changing Fish Fauna of the American Southwest* as an early warning, and the book *Battle Against Extinction* (Minckley & Deacon 1991) as a chronicle of native fish management.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Green River rotenone project.** Agencies planned to eradicate carp before introducing [[Rainbow Trout]] into Flaming Gorge and Fontenelle reservoirs in Utah and Wyoming. The [[Rotenone Detoxification]] station below the reservoirs failed, and most aquatic life was eradicated along 500 miles of a major Colorado River tributary. Native fishes already stressed by industrial pollution from Union Pacific Railroad yards and by dam construction (Flaming Gorge in 1962, Fontenelle in 1964) suffered further mortality. The author reports that most populations have recovered to pre-treatment levels where suitable habitat remains. Efforts to introduce predaceous [[Non-Native Sport Fishes]] into the Colorado system are still ongoing.

**Near-extinctions in the 1960s.** Before the U.S. National Environmental Policy Act and Endangered Species Act took effect in the early 1970s, conditions were especially dire:

- On August 18, 1969, the only remaining individuals of the [[Owens Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon radiosus*) were held in two buckets.
- All that remained of the genus *Empetrichthys* was housed in a horse watering trough on the Desert National Wildlife Refuge north of Las Vegas, Nevada.
- The [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) was saved from extinction only after a series of legal actions that ended at the U.S. Supreme Court.

**Global context.** The author cites Williams & Miller (1990) on escalating extinction rates during the twentieth century. He notes that a 1990 symposium at the University of Lancaster, attended by representatives of more than 30 nations, defined the problem of rare fish but produced little practical solution. A similar message came from the 1992 World Fisheries Congress in Athens.

## Key Findings

**1. Conservation interest among professionals is growing.** The author reports these attendance figures for conservation sessions at the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists (ASIH):
- A 1996 noontime workshop was planned for 50 people, but 135 attended and others were turned away.
- The 1997 ASIH meeting in Seattle drew an audience estimated at over 300 to its conservation symposium.

**2. Collective action has been effective.** The [[Desert Fishes Council]] was founded in 1969 at Death Valley National Monument. It has grown to more than 300 members from universities, government agencies, and the private sector, and has influenced research and management of North American desert aquatic ecosystems.

**3. Reward systems discourage conservation work.** A 1990 task force, established by [[University of California]] Senior Vice President William Frazer at the direction of President David P. Gardner, found that current practices gave neither sufficient encouragement nor reward for the broad range of activities the university's mission requires. Its 1991 report recommended that:
- Applied research and scholarship of application receive commensurate rewards.
- Publications be evaluated, not merely enumerated.
- Professional and community service be recognized when of sufficiently high quality.
- Innovation and scholarship of application receive at least equal weight with scholarship of discovery.

**4. Agency and academic scientists are interdependent.** Agency biologists rely on university data to design species recovery and habitat programs, and university researchers depend on the same resource. The author urges researchers to ask whether their work will help ensure that the organisms or habitats they study will still exist in 2048 or 2098. He cites [[Thomas Lovejoy]], who argued that society responds better to research aimed at good public policy than to purely private intellectual pursuits.

**5. Undergraduate education needs ethics and integration.** The author argues that specialization in graduate training, and curricula that fail to show how disciplines connect, produce people who cannot see the causes of ecological degradation. He notes that many Doctors of Philosophy in fish and wildlife biology know little about philosophy. He recommends courses in [[Environmental Ethics]] and a curriculum rich in the humanities. He credits his own training under [[Starker Leopold]] with shaping his career.

**6. Values must guide research.** Using a flood-evacuation illustration adapted from Bryan Norton, the author shows that people value things differently and that changing values takes generations. He quotes [[Aldo Leopold]]: "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the beauty, integrity, and stability of the biotic system." He argues this criterion applies equally to research.

**7. Trivial research should be questioned.** Drawing on an Einstein quotation about drilling holes where drilling is easy, the author argues that highly esoteric research pursued for career advancement or because funding exists is a moral failure when conservation needs are urgent.

**8. Philosophers and scientists must work together.** The author concludes that the next century will determine the fate of aquatic biodiversity. Conservation-oriented research, combined with input from environmental philosophers, is needed to convince an increasingly materialistic public of its moral obligation to preserve life, and of its link to human survival. He notes that politicians can ignore scientists but cannot long ignore voters.

**Overall conclusion.** The editorial calls on every researcher and manager to do their full share, and it warns against leaving future generations an abundant literature on extinct species. The author acknowledges that change in university culture will be slow, perhaps taking generations rather than years.
