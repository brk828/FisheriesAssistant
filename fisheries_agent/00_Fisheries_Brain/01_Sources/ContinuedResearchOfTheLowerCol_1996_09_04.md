## Executive Summary

This document is a status report from the Arizona Game & Fish Department (AGFD) to the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service (USFWS) detailing an ongoing telemetry study of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Imperial Division of the Lower Colorado River. The project aims to characterize [[Microhabitat Use]] patterns of razorback suckers via ultrasonic telemetry to inform habitat restoration and identification of quality habitat for this species, which was listed as endangered in 1991 and granted critical habitat status in 1994. The report requests authorization to source additional study fish (~40 individuals) from the [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] drawdown population to supplement an initial cohort of 25 fish released in December 1995, many of which were lost to mortality, emigration, or equipment failure. The document also outlines methodological corrections planned for the next phase, including improved transmitter technology and surgical suture protocols.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*), family Catostomidae
- **Size range of study specimens:** 489–592 mm total length (TL)
- **Notable morphology:** Not extensively detailed in this report beyond size; the species is generally characterized by a pronounced dorsal keel (razorback ridge), a trait presumed adapted to riverine flow conditions (not directly discussed in text)
- **Surgical modifications:** All 25 study fish were surgically implanted with ultrasonic transmitters and PIT (Passive Integrated Transponder) tags for individual tracking and identification

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat types utilized:** Main channel, side channels, backwaters, and impoundments — fish were released proportionally across habitat types based on availability
- **Study area habitat characterization:** 25 backwaters, 8 side channels, [[Squaw Lake]], the impoundment, and 12 segments of main channel were characterized under both high and low flow conditions
- **Habitat variables measured:** Water temperature, flow, turbidity, shoreline vegetation type, and other descriptive microhabitat characteristics
- **Key ecological finding:** Razorback suckers were documented using *each* available habitat type to some degree, though the intensity/preference of use relative to availability requires further statistical analysis
- **Environmental disturbance factor:** Increased river flows in late March/early April 1996 — linked to upstream reservoir releases preparing for the [[Grand Canyon Experimental Floods]] — coincided with a sharp increase in fish emigration from the study area and with a wave of suspected mortalities as water temperatures warmed
- **Broader basin context:** Habitat use data for razorback sucker is well-documented in the Upper Colorado River Basin and in some Lower Basin reservoirs, but data specific to the Imperial Division riverine reach has historically been scarce, making this study ecologically significant for regional management

## Key Findings

- **Initial cohort:** 25 razorback suckers (489–592 mm TL) tagged and released in December 1995 into the Imperial Division, distributed proportionally among habitat types
- **Attrition timeline:**
  - 1 fish died within the first week post-release
  - 4 additional fish left the study area by the fifth week (second survey)
  - 4 more fish left over the following four months (16 of 25 remained by ~5 months)
  - During a 3-week high-flow period (late March–early April 1996), 6 fish left the study area — a dramatically accelerated emigration rate compared to the prior 16-week period (8 fish)
  - Of the 10 remaining fish, 4–5 were suspected mortalities based on lack of movement across ≥3 surveys, with suspected mortality onset coinciding with spring water warming
  - Only **5–6 fish** remained providing usable telemetry data at time of report
- **Telemetry equipment failure:** Original ultrasonic transmitters had temperature-dependent frequency emission (calibrated for 70°F water), causing signal ambiguity as river temperatures fluctuated — this made it impossible to reliably distinguish or count emigrating fish
- **Security failure:** Theft of a remote sensing station during the critical high-flow emigration period eliminated the ability to monitor upriver movement at that time
- **Suspected cause of mortality:** Use of non-absorbable silk sutures (chosen for durability during extensive handling) may have acted as "wicks" permitting external infection of the peritoneal cavity — a risk that increases with warming water temperatures over time
- **Data sufficiency assessment:** Eight months of reconnaissance survey data is likely sufficient to detect habitat-type-level use vs. availability differences, but insufficient for fine-grained microhabitat variable analysis
- **Proposed next steps:**
  1. Release of ~40 additional study fish sourced from the [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] drawdown, in numbers proportionate to the reservoir's population
  2. Adoption of new consistent-frequency (non-temperature-dependent) transmitters to resolve fish identification issues
  3. Improved concealment/security measures for remote sensing stations
  4. Switch to absorbable sutures for the new cohort (released directly post-surgery) to reduce infection-related mortality
- **Inter-agency collaboration:** Involves USFWS, Bureau of Reclamation, California Department of Fish & Game, and AGFD; findings are intended to guide identification and restoration of razorback sucker habitat throughout the Lower Colorado River
