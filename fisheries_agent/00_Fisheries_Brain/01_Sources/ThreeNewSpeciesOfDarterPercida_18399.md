# Three New Species of Darters (Percidae, Etheostoma) of the Subgenus Nanostoma from Kentucky and Tennessee

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper, published in the [[Occasional Papers of the Museum of Natural History]] (University of Kansas, No. 101, 1982) by [[Lawrence M. Page]] and [[Brooks M. Burr]], formally describes three previously unnamed darter species of the subgenus [[Nanostoma]]: [[Etheostoma Baileyi]], [[Etheostoma Rafinesquei]], and [[Etheostoma Barrenense]]. The species had been recognized as distinct for at least 30 years but remained undescribed. The authors describe them using meristic counts, morphometrics, color patterns and distribution data, and discuss their relationships within the subgenus. The work was motivated in part by a forthcoming book on Kentucky fishes (Burr, 1980).

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** Undescribed species receive little scientific attention because only a few specialists know them. The authors hope the paper will encourage description of other well-known but unnamed North American fishes.
- **Subgenus status:** [[Nanostoma]] was recognized as a subgenus by Page (1981), and [[Ulocentra]] was relegated to its synonymy. Page (1981) diagnosed Nanostoma as containing five described species ([[Etheostoma Zonale]], [[Etheostoma Simoterum]], [[Etheostoma Duryi]], [[Etheostoma Coosae]], [[Etheostoma Etnieri]]), the three new species, and six or seven undescribed species.
- **Distribution of the group:** Except for the widespread [[Etheostoma Zonale]], Nanostoma species are limited to southern tributaries of the [[Ohio River]], tributaries of the [[Mississippi River]] below the Ohio, and Gulf of Mexico drainages in Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia and western Florida. Most inhabit clear, rocky pools of small to medium streams.
- **Methods:** Counts and measurements follow Hubbs and Lagler (1964), with modifications. Sample means and modes were compared geographically; where variation was not significant, samples were amalgamated by river system.

### Selected Meristic Data (as reported)

- **Maximum SL:** *E. baileyi* 47 mm (males) and 43 mm (females); *E. rafinesquei* 54 mm (males) and 49 mm (females); *E. barrenense* 53 mm (males) and 47 mm (females).
- **Lateral scales:** *E. baileyi* 45–56; *E. rafinesquei* 37–42; *E. barrenense* 42–49.
- **Transverse scales:** *E. baileyi* 12–18; *E. rafinesquei* 10–12; *E. barrenense* 12–14.
- **Branchiostegal rays (mode):** five in all three new species; the authors note six in *E. coosae* and most other Etheostoma.
- **Preoperculomandibular pores:** *E. baileyi* 9–11 (usually 10); *E. rafinesquei* modally 9; *E. barrenense* 8–10 (usually 9).
- **Dorsal fin spines:** 10–13 (*E. baileyi*), 10–12 (*E. rafinesquei*), 10–12 (*E. barrenense*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and behavior**

- ***E. baileyi*** occupies rock-bottomed, current-swept pools of small to large streams. Where populations are large, some individuals occur in rock riffles, especially along riffle margins.
- ***E. rafinesquei*** generally occupies second-, third- and fourth-order upland streams, most common in rock- or gravel-bottomed pools, and sometimes common in riffles or their margins in fall and spring, especially around emergent vegetation.
- ***E. barrenense*** occurs commonly in pools and occasionally along riffle and raceway margins over limestone bedrock, clean gravel, or around large rocks, in second- to fourth-order streams.

**Reproduction**

- Breeding individuals were collected from March to early June across the three species. *E. baileyi* was collected in breeding condition from late March to early June. *E. rafinesquei* was collected from mid-March to 25 May, with most ripe individuals in April. *E. barrenense* was collected from 18 March to 17 June, most prevalent in April and May.
- Spawning occurs in a vertical position, with eggs laid singly on the sides of stones or rocks. Field and aquarium observations by [[Winn]] (1958a, b) and [[Stiles]] (1974) documented territoriality and spawning.

**Distribution**

- ***E. baileyi*** inhabits the upper Cumberland and upper Kentucky River systems. In the Cumberland, below Cumberland Falls it is restricted to the Rockcastle River and Big South Fork; above the falls it is known from Jellico Creek, Clear Fork and the Cumberland River. It is more widespread and common in the Kentucky system, including the Red River and the South, Middle and North forks.
- ***E. rafinesquei*** occurs in the upper Green and lower Barren River systems, and is restricted to Kentucky. It is largely absent from the main channel of the Green River, apart from one upstream locality. It and [[Etheostoma Barrenense]] maintain allopatric ranges within the Barren system.
- ***E. barrenense*** is restricted to the upper Barren River system in Kentucky and Tennessee.

**Regional fauna**

- The Green River system supports 151 fish species, five introduced and five endemic. Endemic fishes include *E. barrenense*, *Moxostoma atripinne* (restricted to the upper Barren), *E. bellum* and *E. barbouri* (upper Green and upper Barren), and *E. rafinesquei* (upper Green and lower Barren). Two endemic crayfishes, *Barbicambarus cornutus* and *Orconectes barrenense*, are also restricted to the upper Green and Barren systems.

**Biogeography and dispersal**

- [[Etheostoma Baileyi]] and [[Etheostoma Sagitta]] are the only fishes restricted to the Cumberland and Kentucky River systems. [[Kuehne]] and [[Bailey]] (1961) attributed their presence in the Kentucky system to stream capture from the Cumberland, whereas Jenkins et al. (1972) proposed the reverse direction.
- The authors could not determine the direction of transfer. They suggest the Kentucky River was formerly a tributary of the upper Mississippi via the preglacial Teays River, as was the Missouri, and that darters first occupied the Kentucky and later invaded other streams.
- Within *E. baileyi*, the only notable variation is between Kentucky and Cumberland populations. Kentucky individuals have higher modal counts of lateral blotches, infraorbital pores, lateral scales and transverse scales.

## Key Findings

1. **Three species are formally named:** [[Etheostoma Baileyi]] (Emerald Darter), [[Etheostoma Rafinesquei]] (Kentucky Snubnose Darter) and [[Etheostoma Barrenense]] (Splendid Darter), all described as new species by Page and Burr.
2. **Diagnostic traits are largely color-based.** The authors note that the most diagnostic features of Nanostoma are color characteristics, which are also highly variable, complicating phylogenetic interpretation.
3. **Restricted ranges:** *E. rafinesquei* is endemic to Kentucky, and *E. barrenense* is endemic to the upper Barren River system (Kentucky and Tennessee). *E. baileyi* occupies the upper Cumberland and upper Kentucky systems.
4. **Taxonomic revisions:** The paper confirms the subgenus status of Nanostoma (with Ulocentra in its synonymy). It also reports that *E. atripinne* is at most intraspecifically distinct from *E. simoterum* (Etnier and Bouchard, in manuscript).
5. **Relationships:** The one clearly synapomorphic character is a modal count of five branchiostegal rays. *E. coosae*, *E. zonale* and *E. baileyi* appear to be early offshoots within Nanostoma, and the remaining species are probably more closely related to each other than to these three.
6. **Biogeographic hypothesis:** The distribution of *E. baileyi* and related darters is best explained by stream capture and dispersal between the Kentucky and Cumberland systems, with the direction of transfer unresolved.
7. **Call for further work:** The authors highlight that six or seven undescribed Nanostoma species remain, and that phylogenetic patterns may become clearer once they are described.
