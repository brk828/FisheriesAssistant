# Influence of Habitat Structure on Fish Assemblage Composition in Southeastern Blackwater Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1988 study by [[Gary K. Meffe]] and [[Andrew L. Sheldon]] (University of Georgia's [[Savannah River Ecology Laboratory]]) quantitatively sampled fish assemblages and 15 habitat variables in upper [[Coastal Plain]] blackwater streams of the central [[Savannah River]] drainage, South Carolina. The authors show that fish assemblage variation at the [[Mesohabitat]] scale is structured primarily by two gradients: [[Current Velocity]] and [[Stream Size]] (depth and width), together with correlated substrate and cover features. Ordination by principal components analysis (PCA) of habitat data and detrended correspondence analysis (DCA) of fish data gave concordant results, and the authors argue that assemblage structure is continuous rather than composed of discrete units.

## Background & Description

Stream fish distribution research has mostly been conducted in midwestern US streams and upland systems, while coastal plain streams, which extend from New England to Texas, have been less studied. The study used the [[Assemblage Ecology]] framing, where an assemblage is a taxonomic subset of a stream community. Sampling was performed at the mesohabitat level (pool, riffle and run analysis over tens of meters or less), an intermediate scale intended to reveal patterns not evident at macro- or microhabitat scales.

Study streams were low gradient (0.2–3 m·km⁻¹, maximum 8 m·km⁻¹) with sandy soils, scarce gravel and no cobble or boulder substrate. Woody debris from bottomland hardwood or cypress forest shapes channel form. Water was soft (conductivity 1.4–75 µmhos·cm⁻¹), acidic to circumneutral (pH 4.6–8.1 regionally; the study sites were normally acidic), and temperatures seldom fell below 8 °C or exceeded 24 °C.

Forty-four collection sites in six tributaries were chosen for accessibility, a distinctive quantifiable habitat type, and internal homogeneity. Sites were 4–15 m long and were blocked with weighted seines. Fish were collected by depletion electroshocking (five or more upstream passes), preserved in 10% formalin, identified to species using [[Bennett and McFarlane]] (1983), and measured by standard length. Current velocity was measured at 0.6 times local depth with a [[Marsh-McBirney]] current meter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Transformations:** Habitat data were log- or arcsine-square-root transformed. Biological data (density and biomass) were transformed with a Taylor's power law variance-stabilizing transformation (Elliott, 1971), yielding a density exponent of N^0.?? and biomass exponent of B^0.?? as reported in the text.
- **Model rationale:** The authors favored a multiplicative habitat suitability model (N = aX₁^b₁X₂^b₂…) over an additive one, since a zero value for any essential variable should give zero population density. This model is equivalent to [[Liebig's Law of the Minimum]].
- **Analytical tools:** PCA (run in Systat) summarized habitat; DCA (DECORANA, rare species downweighted) summarized fish distributions. The authors explain that DCA handles the nonlinear, horseshoe-prone structure of species turnover data.
- **Cover and substrate:** Cover types included wood, brush, coarse and fine roots, vascular plants, undercut banks and floating leaf packs. The authors interpret roots and woody debris as replacing rocks as the dominant structural component in these streams.
- **Scope limits:** Sampling was short-term (15 October–19 November 1985) and did not address annual or seasonal change.

## Key Findings

- **Sample:** 2,500 individuals of 30 species in 11 families were collected. Six rare species (one or two individuals each) were removed, leaving 24 species, several of which were split into size classes.
- **Dominant species:** [[Notropis lutipinnis]] (yellowfin shiner) was most abundant (985 individuals, 39.4%). Other common species included [[Aphredoderus sayanus]] (235), [[Notropis cummingsae]] (231), [[Lepomis auritus]] (152) and [[Nocomis leptocephalus]] (148). [[Anguilla rostrata]] (American eel) contributed the most biomass (2,203.6 g, 18.3%).
- **Habitat PCA:** The first four axes explained 59.8% of habitat variance. Axis I (20.0%) was a current velocity component ranging from fast sandy sites to slow, silty, leafy sites with roots and wood. Axis II (17.4%) was a stream size component ranging from deep, wide, muddy, undercut sites to shallow, narrow sites. Axis III (11.4%) contrasted gravelly sites with little cover against sandy, silty sites with plants. Axis IV (11.0%) contrasted woody sites with floating leaf packs against sites with riparian roots.
- **Species distribution:** Most species centroids fell in the slow, deep, muddy quadrant. Sunfishes (Lepomis), chubsuckers ([[Erimyzon]] spp.), American eels and yellow bullheads (Ictalurus natalis) were characteristic of slow, deep habitats. Darters ([[Etheostoma fricksium]], E. olmstedi, Percina nigrofasciata), northern hogsuckers (Hypentelium nigricans) and margined madtoms (Noturus insignis) were associated with faster water. Creek chub (Semotilus atromaculatus) was restricted to small, slow sites.
- **Anomaly:** Small redbreast sunfish aligned with headwater species because a logging-created pond downstream produced a successful spawn that year.
- **DCA validation:** Axis 1 correlated with current velocity (r = 0.684) and Axis 2 with depth (r = 0.725) and width (r = 0.628). Ordination on the 12 most abundant species matched the full 24-species ordination (Mantel R = 0.427, P = 0.008), so rare species did not drive the patterns.
- **PCA–DCA concordance:** Mantel comparison of species positions gave R = 0.566 (P < 0.001), indicating that the two independent methods agree.
- **Size-specific habitat use:** Small and large tadpole madtoms (Noturus gyrinus) overlapped in habitat, whereas small American eels used faster currents than large ones.
- **Taxonomic structure:** Genera and families clustered in similar ordination regions, suggesting a phylogenetic component to assemblage composition.
- **Biomass vs. density:** Biomass-based ordinations gave essentially the same results as density-based ones, so density was retained.
- **Continuum conclusion:** Cluster analysis was uninformative, and assemblages changed gradually along velocity and depth/width gradients. The authors compare this to the plant community continuum concept (Austin, 1985) and expect more discrete assemblages in higher-order or higher-gradient streams.
- **Overall conclusion:** Current velocity and stream depth are pervasive determinants of fish assemblage composition in low-gradient coastal streams of the southeastern United States, consistent with other stream studies cited in the paper.
