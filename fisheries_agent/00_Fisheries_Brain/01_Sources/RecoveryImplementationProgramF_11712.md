# Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin - FY 1994 Summary Report

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #endangered-species #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #river-ecology #habitat-management

## Executive Summary

This FY 1994 summary report documents the annual accomplishments of the Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The program addresses recovery of four federally listed endangered fish species: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The Recovery Program operates through coordinated efforts among federal agencies (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Reclamation), state wildlife agencies (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming), water management entities, and other stakeholders. Program activities span seven major categories: instream flow identification and protection, habitat restoration, nonnative fish control, propagation and genetics management, research and monitoring, information and education, and program management. Total FY 94 expenditures were approximately $5.5 million ($3.2M annual funding, $2.3M capital funds). The program enables water development to proceed in compliance with the Endangered Species Act while implementing recovery actions for the target species.

## Background & Description

The [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] encompasses major river systems including the [[Colorado River]], [[Green River]], [[Yampa River]], [[White River]], and [[Gunnison River]], spanning portions of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming. Historically, these rivers supported robust populations of four large-bodied endemic fish species now listed as endangered. The Recovery Program was established to coordinate recovery efforts while allowing continued water development under Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act.

The four target species occupy different ecological niches within the basin. [[Colorado Squawfish]] is a large piscivorous cyprinid that requires specific spawning habitat (cobble bars with appropriate flows and temperatures) and nursery backwaters for young-of-year survival. [[Razorback Sucker]] is a long-lived catostomid that historically spawned in flooded bottomlands and river bars. [[Humpback Chub]] occupies canyon habitats, with significant populations in [[Westwater Canyon]] and [[Black Rocks Canyon]]. [[Bonytail Chub]] is nearly extirpated from the upper basin, with recovery efforts focusing on captive propagation and potential reintroduction.

The Recovery Program operates through a committee structure including an Implementation Committee (policy oversight), Management Committee (program coordination), Biology Committee (technical guidance), and Water Acquisition Committee (flow protection). Annual work planning follows a Recovery Action Plan (RIPRAP) that identifies specific tasks, schedules, and responsibilities across multiple project categories.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The endangered fishes evolved in a dynamic riverine environment characterized by highly variable seasonal flows, warm summer temperatures, turbid conditions, and extensive floodplain connectivity. Spring snowmelt historically produced peak flows of 30,000-40,000 cfs or higher, inundating bottomlands and creating nursery habitat. Summer base flows maintained connectivity and thermal regimes suitable for spawning (typically 18-20°C for squawfish). Natural hydrographs included gradual recession flows that allowed young fish to move from flooded areas back to main channel habitats.

Modern alterations have fundamentally changed these systems. [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] on the Green River and the [[Aspinall Unit]] (Blue Mesa, Morrow Point, and Crystal dams) on the Gunnison River regulate flows, reduce peak discharges, alter thermal regimes, and trap sediment. Water diversions for agriculture reduce base flows, particularly during late summer and fall. Channelization through levees, dikes, and riprap has eliminated floodplain connectivity. Tamarisk (*Tamarix* spp.) encroachment has further stabilized channels and reduced habitat complexity.

Nonnative fish species pose severe threats through predation and competition. Channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*), smallmouth bass (*Micropterus dolomieu*), and red shiner (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) are particularly problematic. These species thrive under altered flow regimes and prey heavily on larval and juvenile endangered fishes. Deep backwaters that provide optimal habitat for young squawfish also harbor high densities of nonnative predators, creating ecological traps.

Water quality issues include selenium contamination in certain reaches, which may adversely affect razorback sucker reproduction and survival. Selenium levels vary spatially, with some floodplain restoration sites showing concentrations that could be detrimental to recovery efforts.

## Key Findings

**Instream Flow Protection:**
- The [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]] (CWCB) finalized a policy allowing interim protection of instream flows while uncertainties about habitat needs and water availability are resolved
- CWCB filed for a 581 cfs instream flow right for the 15-Mile Reach of the Colorado River near Grand Junction and filed to acquire 300 cfs of unclaimed irrigation return flows
- Utah adopted a policy legally protecting summer/fall flows in the Green River from Flaming Gorge Dam to the Duchesne River confluence
- 20,000 acre-feet of water was released from [[Ruedi Reservoir]] to benefit endangered fish in the 15-Mile Reach
- Water releases continued from Flaming Gorge Dam and Aspinall Unit in support of research to assess optimal operations for endangered fish

**Habitat Restoration:**
- Environmental compliance and final design initiated for fish passageway at [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] on the Gunnison River; construction scheduled for completion April 1996, opening 50 miles of historical habitat
- Floodplain habitat inventory completed for 870 miles of mainstem rivers (Colorado, Green, Gunnison, Yampa, White)
- [[Old Charlie Wash]] wetland restoration conducted at [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]]; approximately 20,000 razorback and flannelmouth suckers transplanted in spring, but no razorbacks collected in fall (likely due to predation or escape through faulty outlet structure)
- Seven sites in Green and Colorado rivers scheduled for restoration beginning 1995-1996
- Agricultural diversion structures on Yampa River identified as potential migration impediments; remediation scheduled for FY 96

**Nonnative Fish Management:**
- Colorado, Utah, Wyoming, and USFWS developed interim procedures for stocking nonnative fish in lakes and reservoirs to ensure consistency with endangered fish recovery
- Research at Ouray demonstrated that deep backwaters, while providing optimal habitat for young squawfish, harbor high densities of nonnative predators (especially channel catfish and smallmouth bass) making these habitats essentially unusable
- Shallow backwater construction identified as potential viable option for providing refuge habitat

**Propagation and Genetics:**
- Genetics Management Guidelines and Coordinated Hatchery Facility Plan finalized
- Plan identified need for minimum 27.3 acres of ponds for short-term needs (genetic refuges and broodstock development); only 6-7.2 acres available, creating deficit of 20.1 acres
- Water supply/conditioning reservoir and six 0.2-acre refugia ponds constructed at [[Ouray Endangered Fish Facility]]
- Experimental razorback sucker stocking conducted in Upper Colorado and Gunnison rivers; 41 adults implanted with radio tags, approximately 10% survival observed
- Valley City National Fish Hatchery (North Dakota) committed to redevelop bonytail family lots in 1995 at no additional cost after weather prevented 1994 operations

**Research and Monitoring:**
- [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP) summary report completed covering first 7 years (1986-1992); program monitors young-of-year squawfish, subadult/adult squawfish, and adult humpback chub
- Colorado squawfish population structure analysis in upper Colorado River showed 50% recapture rates allowing population estimates; study completed after four years of data collection
- White River study demonstrated that Colorado squawfish make long-distance migrations to spawning sites in Green and Yampa rivers; White River population is integral part of larger sub-basin populations
- Larval drift sampling on Green and Yampa rivers continued; 1994 samples showed very low numbers, likely due to extremely low Yampa flows reducing downstream transport
- Razorback sucker spawning studies confirmed egg deposition at Jensen spawning site with larvae drifting downstream up to 350 miles to Lake Powell
- Radio-tagged razorback suckers showed movement to spawning sites triggered by discharge rather than temperature, with greatest movement during rising discharge combined with daily maximum temperatures exceeding 14°C

**Flow-Habitat Relationships:**
- Videography and habitat mapping studies refined flow recommendations for 15-Mile Reach; draft report released October 1994
- Nursery habitat studies on Green River examined relationships between Flaming Gorge test flows and habitat availability, formation, and maintenance
- Geomorphology studies documented historical channel changes using aerial photography from 1937, 1954, 1968, and 1986
- Food web studies initiated on Colorado River using systems approach to determine if food availability is limiting factor; includes primary/secondary productivity, fish diets, and sediment transport analyses

**Aspinall Unit Studies:**
- Ichthyofaunal surveys in Gunnison River (Delta to Redlands) showed natives comprised 78% of adult/juvenile fish collected by electrofishing
- Seven adult squawfish radio-tagged in 1993 showed aggregation in river miles 30-35 during July-August when temperatures reached 19-20°C, suggesting possible spawning behavior
- Larval squawfish sampling conducted at multiple sites on Gunnison and Colorado rivers to assess spawning success and effects of experimental flows

**Section 7 Consultation:**
- Recovery Program participants finalized agreement clarifying how Section 7 consultations will be conducted on water depletion projects
- USFWS completed intra-Service consultation waiving depletion charge for projects less than 100 acre-feet
- Since program inception in 1988, 156 projects with potential to deplete over 184,000 acre-feet received favorable biological opinions
- Critical habitat designation finalized March 20, 1994; Recovery Action Plan modified to address designation

**Program Management:**
- Organization, Mission and Staffing Plan revised to improve program effectiveness, increasing Program Director's responsibilities and staff
- Initiative begun to provide long-term funding for the program
- Three public meetings held in Gunnison River Basin to identify concerns related to Aspinall releases, Redlands fish ladder, and flow protection
- Public attitudes survey completed
- Media training workshop conducted for Recovery Program participants

**Capital Projects:**
- Floodplain habitat inventory identified priority sites: Ouray NWR wetland complex (Green River), Walter Walker Wildlife Area and Golf-Course West (Colorado River 18-Mile Reach), Oldham Bottoms (15-Mile Reach), Johnson Boy's Slough and Escalante State Wildlife Area (Gunnison River)
- Preliminary findings suggest many sites have selenium levels that may adversely affect endangered fishes
- Steamboat Lake water lease negotiated: 2,000 acre-feet annually at $16/acre-foot, with potential increase to 3,300 acre-feet
- Yampa River Basin study explored Elkhead Reservoir enlargement (estimated $32 million) potentially making 20,000 acre-feet available for late-season flow augmentation
- Grand Valley Water Management study initiated to determine how Reclamation's Grand Valley Project might supplement flows in 15-Mile Reach
- Collbran Project identified as potential source of approximately 3,200 acre-feet for 15-Mile Reach flow enhancement

**Key Challenges Identified:**
- Nonnative fish predation emerging as single most challenging obstacle to recovery
- Selenium contamination at certain sites may preclude successful habitat restoration
- Need for additional propagation facilities (20.1-acre deficit for short-term needs)
- Uncertainty about optimal stocking sizes and strategies for reintroduction efforts
- Need to balance water development with flow protection in context of interstate compact obligations
- Limited understanding of overwinter survival factors for young-of-year fish
- Difficulty evaluating "success" at naturally-functioning restored floodplain habitats without control structures
