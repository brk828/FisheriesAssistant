# Blue River Barrier Fish Monitoring Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This technical report, prepared by [[Arizona State University]] researcher Paul C. Marsh in April 1999 for the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest]], proposes a fish monitoring program for the [[Blue River Barrier]] (BRB), a proposed fish barrier on the [[Blue River]] near its confluence with the [[San Francisco River]] in Greenlee County, Arizona. The barrier is a central feature of the [[Native Fish Conservation Initiative]], whose goal is to remove introduced fishes and restore the native ichthyofauna. The monitoring plan has three purposes: detecting project-related changes in fish communities, providing early warning of non-native invasion, and measuring overall program success. The protocol is adapted from a [[Bureau of Reclamation]] plan for the Gila River basin ([[Clarkson 1996 Monitoring Protocol]]). It calls for twice-yearly quantitative electrofishing at five fixed 200-m stations, supplemented by opportunistic qualitative sampling, for a minimum of five years after construction and, in autumn, for the life of the project.

## Background & Description

The Blue River basin supports six native fish species and historically may have held three more. The [[Blue River]] system is an important management area for federally listed [[Apache Trout]], [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]]. [[Razorback Sucker]] and Apache trout have previously been reintroduced. Non-native fishes recently found in the system include [[Rainbow Trout]] (and hybrids with Apache trout), [[Common Carp]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Mosquitofish]]. [[Smallmouth Bass]] occurs in the San Francisco River but had not been captured in the Blue River. Brown and brook trout have historical records only. Non-native crayfish are also present.

The report states that many native fish declines are linked to introduced species (see [[Minckley and Deacon 1968]]). It argues that an exotic species need not be an obligate piscivore to harm natives. Competition, predation on early life stages, interference with feeding and spawning, and parasite or pathogen introduction can all be damaging. The author concludes that restoring a self-sustaining, native-only fishery requires eliminating introduced species.

Table 1 lists the fishes known from the system, with native species grouped under Salmonidae, Cyprinidae and Catostomidae. The introduced group includes salmonids, cyprinids, catfishes, a livebearer and a centrarchid.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Monitoring definition.** Monitoring is defined as repeated, consistent biological sampling at the same places, at the same times of year under similar hydrologic and climatic conditions, using the same techniques. Consistent data allow statistical tests for trends and differences.

**Flow.** Sampling should occur during low flow, for example below 0.2 m³/s (10 cfs). Field discharge estimates (cross-sectional area method) should be compared with historical records from the [[USGS Blue River Gage]] near Clifton. That gage has an intermittent record from 1967 to 1994 and apparently has not operated since. The report recommends that the USFS and USBR encourage the USGS to resume flow measurement. Flow is relevant because native and non-native fishes differ in their resistance to flooding (see [[Minckley and Meffe 1987]]).

**Habitat.** Pool location and persistence depend largely on bedrock morphology beneath mobile substrates. The two largest pools in the lower Blue River are at T2S R31E Sec 30 and T2S R30E Sec 26. They are to be included in routine monitoring because they are expected to hold fish more consistently than smaller, less stable pools.

**Pool-dwelling species.** Adult Gila chub, adult suckers, and adult carp, channel catfish and flathead catfish preferentially use pools. Casual sampling restricted to riffles and runs could miss them.

**Access and stream structure.** The [[Forest Service Road 475C]] wet crossing (the Stacy or Juan Miller crossing) may partly impede fish movement at some flows. It would create an intervening management reach between the BRB and the crossing, which could be used to control non-native species before they reach sensitive upstream habitat. ATVs may be used only downstream of FSR 475C, because they are prohibited in the Blue Range Primitive Area.

## Key Findings

**Monitoring purposes and timing**
- Sampling should occur twice yearly, in spring and late summer–autumn, for at least two years before construction. It should then continue for at least five years after construction.
- Autumn monitoring should continue for the life of the project, given as an example of 100 years.
- Intensified sampling at higher frequency, and targeting individual species, should follow if non-natives are found after completion. This would be directed by the USFS after consultation.

**Sampling design**
- Quantitative sampling uses five fixed stations, each 200 m long: (1) above FSR 475C, (2) below FSR 475C, (3) midway between FSR 475C and the BRB, (4) above the BRB, and (5) below the BRB.
- Each station should be marked at its downstream end and located by GPS, so that it can be found after flooding. Each station should represent riffle, run and pool habitat.
- Additional sites are suggested above and below the Forest border, above and below Clifton, and at historic sites such as Baseline and Little Blue River.
- Opportunistic collections should cover the Blue River from FSR 475C to the San Francisco confluence.

**Gear and effort**
- Electrofishing is the standard quantitative method. It proceeds upstream, and real-time shocking time and equipment settings are recorded.
- Pools should be sampled with trammel or gill nets set parallel to flow and fished overnight. Catch is standardized by net area and hours fished.
- Qualitative sampling uses electrofishing, seines, hoop or trap nets, and trammel or gill nets. Each station should sample habitats in proportion to their abundance.
- A minimum of ten pools should be sampled in each fixed-station reach. Additional sampling extends up or downstream if ten pools are not available.

**Data and records**
- All fish are identified to species and life stage and enumerated. Voucher specimens go to a repository such as the [[ASU Collection of Fishes]], and photographs may supplement them.
- Crayfish are recorded as non-native taxa and voucher specimens are taken as needed.
- Physical records include weather, air and water temperature, water clarity, and discharge. Each habitat unit is described for length, area, width, depth, substrate, algae or macrophytes, banks and riparian vegetation. Fixed photo points and labelled diagrams are included.
- Data are to be entered into KeyEntry software, which includes quality assurance.

**Analysis and reporting**
- Existing data and new comprehensive field data are to be analysed to establish natural variation before interpreting changes. Trend tests follow the Clarkson (1996a) framework.
- Results are to be interpreted within current knowledge of species biology and community ecology.
- An annual summary report goes to the USFS and USBR. A comprehensive report every five years should assess trends and overall programme success.
- Any post-project occurrence of non-native fishes should trigger a response plan to eliminate or otherwise control them.
