# Genetic Status and Captive Broodstock Establishment for the Endangered [[Bonytail Chub]]

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper examines the genetic status of the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*[[Gila elegans]]*) and the captive broodstock established at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH). Using [[Allozyme Analysis]] and [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) sequencing, the authors show that the hatchery population (about 350 adults) carries a small fraction of the genetic variation found in remaining wild fish. Its founders trace to at most 10 parents from five matings. The authors conclude that wild fish must be incorporated into the [[Captive Broodstock]] to broaden its genetic base, and that about 30 pairs of future parents would retain roughly 90% of the maximum possible variation.

## Background & Description

The bonytail chub is a long-lived, large-bodied cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]] and is listed as endangered. It is considered one of the most imperiled native freshwater fishes in North America. Wild populations persist as a declining number of large, old adults, with little or no natural recruitment reported in the text. Recovery plans therefore relied on hatchery propagation and stocking into [[Lake Mohave]].

The broodstock at DNFH descends from wild fish captured in the late 1970s. Adult females from Lake Mohave were mated in 1981 with five adult males captured the same year (Hamman 1982), with each female's eggs fertilized by milt from a single male. The broodstock's genetic origin was previously examined by [[Minckley]] and colleagues (1989), whose F2 samples are used here for comparison.

The authors note that genetic deterioration, including [[Inbreeding Depression]] and [[Genetic Drift]], can reduce long-term persistence of captive endangered species. They present this study as a model for similar programs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stocking history:** Between 1981 and 1996, 41,517 and 161,371 hatchery-derived fish (two categories, labelled in the source as "F1" and "F2" progeny) were stocked into Lake Mohave. The "surplus" group was marked with oxytetracycline. Annual "volunteer" reproduction by unknown numbers of hatchery fish in holding ponds added further uncertainty.
- **Identity of wild fish:** Three categories of fish are present in Lake Mohave: truly wild fish, hatchery fish released in 1981, and hatchery fish released after 1981. Telling them apart is difficult.
- **Sample of Lake Mohave fish:** Marsh (1997) examined 17 fish caught between 1989 and 1994 with varying documentation. The authors cite Marsh's conclusion that not less than 10% and perhaps as many as two-thirds were wild. (The source text is OCR-degraded at this figure; 10% is the most likely reading.)
- **Predator and habitat exposure:** Recaptured hatchery-origin fish from Lake Mohave have survived predators and conditions absent from hatchery ponds, which the authors argue makes them more valuable than hatchery-reared fish for broodstock use.

## Key Findings

**1. Reproduction of wild-caught parents (Hamman 1982)**
- Female 1 produced no eggs.
- Females 2 to 6 produced 5,850 to 37,700 eggs (mean 25,090).
- Fertilization proportions ranged from 0.049 to 0.290 (mean 0.200), with about 95% of eggs fertilized on average (mean fertilized eggs 23,634).

**2. Allozyme variation in the F2 progeny**
- A sample of 30 F2 fish from DNFH was screened at three loci (sAAT-4, Gpi-B, Pgm-A).
- Allele frequencies did not differ significantly from earlier F2 samples (Minckley et al. 1989).
- Pgm-A showed a large, statistically significant excess of heterozygotes relative to Hardy-Weinberg expectations.
- Six three-locus genotypes were found. Genotype I occurred in 22 of 30 individuals, indicating that most were likely produced by a single mating.
- Using binomial probabilities, the authors calculated 0.513 for no such matings, 0.366 for one, and 0.121 for two or more (out of five matings). One mating was therefore about 3.02 times more likely than more than one.

**3. mtDNA variation**
- All 20 F2 individuals examined had identical mtDNA haplotypes.
- Simulations showed that this low diversity is consistent with a small number of contributing mothers, with the authors judging the mtDNA data strongly support unequal, few-mating contributions.

**4. Effective founder number and genetic retention**
- The original broodstock derives from at most 10 parents across five matings, contributing unequally.
- The F2 population is estimated to hold the genetic variation of about 8.5 wild fish under the high estimate, and as few as about 3.5 wild fish under the low estimate.
- The approximately 350 adult F2 fish at DNFH therefore contain only a small share of the wild population's variation.

**5. Recommendations for future broodstock**
- Incorporate wild fish into the broodstock as a priority; the authors call this essential for increasing genetic diversity.
- If only a few wild fish are confirmed, recaptured hatchery fish may be incorporated, since they have survived in Lake Mohave.
- Using about 30 pairs of parents retains approximately 90% of the maximum possible variation, and using many more than 30 pairs appears unnecessary. The authors note that 90% retention is a commonly suggested target for captive endangered species, though it erodes over multiple generations (Soulé et al. 1986).
- Breeding should follow pedigree-based management so that [[Effective Population Size]] and gene diversity are preserved (Ballou and Lacy 1995).

**6. Conclusion**
Current hatchery stock is genetically depauperate. Broadening its base with wild and recaptured fish is necessary to reduce further deterioration and support eventual recovery of the species.
