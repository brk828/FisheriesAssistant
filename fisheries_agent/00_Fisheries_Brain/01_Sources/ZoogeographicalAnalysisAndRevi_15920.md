# Zoogeographical Analysis and Review of Distributional Problems of Kinosternon flavescens in the American Southwest

#herpetology #zoogeography #Kinosternidae #taxonomy

## Executive Summary

This 1980 student research report examines unresolved distributional and taxonomic problems concerning [[Kinosternon flavescens]] (Yellow Mud Turtle) in the American Southwest following recent taxonomic revisions by Iverson (1976–1979) and Conant and Berry (1978). The author identifies three major distributional anomalies: (1) a single specimen of K. f. flavescens in the upper [[Rio Yaqui]] where [[Kinosternon sonoriense]] predominates, (2) two records of K. f. arizonense in the lower Rio Yaqui, and (3) the disjunct fossil type locality of K. f. arizonense in the [[San Pedro River]] (Gila Basin) far from its modern range. The analysis integrates geological evidence from Basin and Range orogeny, the [[Cochise Filter Barrier]], and Pliocene drainage patterns to explain these peculiarities, concluding that most anomalous records likely represent introductions, sampling artifacts, or misidentified localities rather than natural historical distributions.

## Background & Description

[[Kinosternon flavescens]] is the most morphologically distinct member of its genus, uniquely possessing an elevated ninth marginal scute. Despite this diagnostic feature, the genus has suffered from persistent taxonomic confusion. [[Kinosternon sonoriense]] was repeatedly misidentified as K. f. flavescens in Texas and elsewhere (Brown 1950, Carr 1952, Stebbins 1966). Other congeners are morphologically similar; Conant and Berry (1978) noted that female K. integrum "can be separated from female hirtipes only with difficulty, even when a large series of specimens are available for comparison."

The subspecies K. f. arizonense was originally described by Gilmore (1922) from a Pliocene (Blancan) fossil from the San Pedro River south of Benson, Cochise County, Arizona. Iverson (1979a) synonymized it with K. f. stejnegeri Hartweg 1938, yielding the current name by priority. Iverson (1979b) provided revised subspecific classifications recognizing K. f. flavescens and K. f. arizonense, with the latter occurring in Rio Sonora and Magdalena drainages in southwestern Arizona and northern Mexico.

K. flavescens does not reliably occur west of the Continental Divide in the United States except in the Rio Sonora, Magdalena, and closed Rio Casas Grandes basins. Early erroneous reports placed the species in Utah, Nevada, and the [[Gila River Basin]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Cochise Filter Barrier]] is a critical zoogeographical feature: a dry, 100–200 km wide gap between the [[Sierra Madre Occidental]] and [[Colorado Plateau]] in southeastern Arizona, southwestern New Mexico, northeastern Sonora, and northwestern Chihuahua. This high plain (ca. 1500 m elevation) is interrupted by steep, discontinuous north-south trending mountain ranges reaching 3000 m elevation. The barrier has profoundly influenced distributions of semi-aquatic organisms (Morafka 1977, Conant 1977).

Few semi-aquatic species have penetrated the upper Rio Yaqui or Gila basins from the east through this barrier. Degenhardt and Christiansen (1974) documented complete allopatry between K. f. flavescens (five localities in Playas Basin, Hidalgo County, New Mexico) and K. sonoriense (six localities on eastern slopes of [[Peloncillo Mountains]]), providing strong evidence that the Cochise barrier sets eastern and western limits for these taxa respectively.

Stock-watering tank construction has influenced persistence and range extensions of aquatic turtles in the Southwest (Degenhardt and Christiansen 1974, Mahmoud 1969), potentially facilitating movement through otherwise dry areas.

Geological context: The [[Basin and Range Orogeny]] established north-northwest trending basins in the modern drainage system. Melton (1960) proposed that prior to this orogeny, several major southwest-oriented rivers drained the San Juan Mountain/Mogollon Highland area. As mountain ranges uplifted, northwest-flowing tributaries eroded headward, capturing ancestral rivers during late Pliocene–early Pleistocene (Cooley 1968).

## Key Findings

**Problem 1: K. f. flavescens in upper Rio Yaqui**
- A single specimen (UA27953) from Cochise County, Arizona headwaters prompted Iverson (1979b) to extend K. f. flavescens range into northern Yaqui Basin
- At least six K. sonoriense localities occur in upper Yaqui vicinity, plus five in lower basin
- Zoogeographical evidence strongly contradicts natural occurrence: the Cochise filter barrier prevents eastward dispersal of K. f. flavescens and westward dispersal of K. sonoriense
- Degenhardt and Christiansen (1974) documented complete allopatry in southwestern New Mexico (five K. f. flavescens localities vs. six K. sonoriense localities)
- **Conclusion**: The isolated record likely represents introduction or recent invasion via stock-watering tanks, not historical natural distribution

**Problem 2: K. f. arizonense in lower Rio Yaqui**
- Two localities reported (A27958, 40529) in extreme western portion of lower Yaqui
- Improbable that K. f. arizonense would not invade upstream/eastward given: (a) hydrological conditions similar to rest of its range, (b) complete sympatry with K. sonoriense elsewhere (11 K. sonoriense records exist within Yaqui drainage)
- Human populations too low for likely introduction
- **Three alternative explanations**: (1) inadequate sampling, (2) recent invasion via stock tanks, (3) locality data geographically misplaced
- **Conclusion**: Localities may actually fall within [[Rio Fuerte]] drainage due to inaccuracy of Mexican drainage maps

**Problem 3: Fossil vs. modern distribution of K. f. arizonense**
- Fossil type locality (Upper Pliocene/Blancan) on San Pedro River south of Benson is completely disjunct from modern K. f. arizonense range
- By late Pliocene, K. flavescens or its precursor was already differentiated and presumably allopatric from K. f. arizonense
- **Geological hypothesis** (based on Melton 1960):
  - Ancestral San Pedro River was isolated from adjacent drainages and ancestral Gila to north
  - San Pedro and Rio Sonora drainages were integrated during this period (as Iverson 1979b suggested), accounting for fossil K. f. arizonense presence
  - Progressive uplift from Benson southward to Arizona-Mexico boundary shifted ancestral Rio Sonora/San Pedro headwaters toward Mexico
  - This forced living K. f. arizonense southward while leaving fossil at Benson
  - Subspecies became isolated in modern Rio Sonora and adjacent Mexican drainages
  - San Pedro commenced northward flow toward Gila as Arizona and Mexico drainages separated
- **Alternative explanation**: K. f. arizonense once inhabited entire Gila Basin and went extinct during/after Pliocene (considered less plausible because K. sonoriense survived or invaded while K. f. arizonense was eliminated)

**Taxonomic clarifications**:
- Conant and Berry (1978) resolved most species-level identification issues within [[Kinosternon]]
- Iverson (1979b) re-evaluated subspecific relationships within K. flavescens
- Many historical errors stemmed from misidentification and incorrect locality data
- Iverson (1979b) incorrectly listed a K. flavescens lectoparatype from Gila River, Arizona, illustrating persistent confusion

**Zoogeographical synthesis**:
- K. flavescens has been allopatric from K. sonoriense in Rio Yaqui Basin from late Pliocene until recent times
- Melton's (1960) antecedent drainage theory correlates well with regional zoogeography
- Theory supports long-term isolation of Gila River and [[Rio Grande]] aquatic faunas
- Predicts certain Gila-Yaqui faunal similarities (Fig. 6)
- Yaqui–Rio Grande similarities explained by stream captures (Miller 1958) occurring after Gila-Yaqui basin isolation
