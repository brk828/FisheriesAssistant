# Selenium in Eggs and Milt of Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) in the Middle Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Hamilton]] and [[Waddell]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, 1994) measured [[Selenium]] concentrations in eggs and milt of wild, endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] collected at the [[Razorback Bar]] in the [[Green River]], Utah, and compared them with muscle plugs from the same fish, control and reference fish, and the effects reported in other species. Egg selenium was above control and reference ranges, while milt was within them. Fertilized eggs from three streamside spawns did not hatch. The authors concluded that egg selenium is sufficiently elevated to suspect reproductive problems contributing to the decline of the species in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]], although they could not state unequivocally that reproduction is being harmed.

## Background & Description

- **Species status:** The razorback sucker was listed as federally endangered in 1991. Studies from 1979 to 1986 reported low adult numbers, little growth, and no apparent recruitment ([[Tyus]] 1987). Decline across the Colorado River system is attributed to dam building, habitat destruction and alteration, water quality impairment, and interaction with non-native fishes.
- **Study site:** Razorback Bar lies near the confluence of [[Ashley Creek]] and the Green River, where the fish were documented before spawning. Larval razorback suckers were reported in the slack waters there. Razorback suckers are almost exclusively found in quiet-water areas.
- **Contaminant source:** Soils are derived from [[Mancos Shale]], which contains about 1,100 µg/kg selenium. Ashley Valley has about 28,000 acres of irrigated land, with drainwater discharged to Ashley Creek or [[Stewart Lake]]. Low-intensity irrigation has operated for 60 years, while intensified irrigation and drainwater disposal began around 1979.
- **Sampling:** Three female and five male adults were captured between April 29 and May 6, 1992, by electrofishing or trammel net. Approximately 2 g of unfertilized eggs and 2 mL of milt were collected from each fish, then shipped on dry ice to the [[Patuxent Analytical Control Facility]] for analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water and fish selenium in the Green River system**

- Ashley Creek water ranged from 59 µg/L (1987) to 78 µg/L (1988). Ashley Creek had the highest selenium of all Green River tributaries sampled.
- In 1989, whole-body fish from Ashley Creek ranged from 40.1 µg/g dry weight in [[Black Bullhead]] to 122 µg/g in [[Fathead Minnow]].
- Stewart Lake discharges about 25% of its selenium into the Green River. Drainwater in 1988–89 ranged from 13 to 85 µg/L across three north-shore drains, and outlet water on the south shore was 11–12 µg/L.
- Whole-body fish from the Stewart Lake outfall in 1989 ranged from 11.2 µg/g (green sunfish) to 24.8 µg/g (fathead minnow).
- Previously, razorback sucker eggs collected near Razorback Bar had 4.9 µg/g (1988) and 28 µg/g (1991) selenium dry weight.
- Two other endangered Green River fish had elevated gonadal selenium: [[Humpback Chub]] at 7.3 µg/g (1986) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] at 6.5 µg/g (1982).

**Selenium uptake and transfer**

- The authors argue that adult razorback suckers use the Stewart Lake outfall and Ashley Creek confluences both before and after spawning, so selenium uptake from diet and water, and deposition into eggs, is occurring.
- Fish gonads normally contain low selenium. Control and reference fish gonads ranged from 2.0 to 3.1 µg/g dry weight.
- Historic contaminated systems include [[Belews Lake]] and [[Hyco Reservoir]] (North Carolina) and [[Martin Lake]] (Texas), where elevated gonadal selenium was linked to reduced reproduction and species disappearance.

## Key Findings

**1. Egg and milt selenium**

| Tissue | Selenium (µg/g dry weight) |
|---|---|
| Eggs (wild, n = 3 females) | 3.7–10.6 |
| Milt (n = 5 males) | <1.1–6.7 |
| Muscle plugs, females | 4.4–32 |
| Muscle plugs, males | 3.6–26 |

- Egg concentrations were above control and reference gonad values and above reference eggs from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (2.1 µg/g dry weight). Milt was well within the control and reference range.
- Egg and milt concentrations were significantly correlated with muscle plug concentrations: Pearson r = 0.91 for females (n = 3) and r = 0.87 for males (n = 5). Muscle values were consistently higher but not significantly different from egg or milt values.
- The detection limit was 1.1 µg/g dry weight for tissue analysis. Duplicate relative percent difference was 2.6%. NRCC reference material DOLT-1 recovery was 95%.

**2. Reproductive outcomes**

- Three streamside spawning pairs produced fertilized eggs, but none hatched. About 30% of the incubated eggs were believed to be fertilized, but none reached the eyed stage.
- Earlier hatching attempts at the same laboratory had succeeded, so the authors suggest the failures may be linked to elevated egg selenium.
- Dexter reference-fry data showed 30-day-old fry at 2.2 µg/g dry weight.

**3. Thresholds and effects**

- Background gonadal selenium (2.0–3.1 µg/g dry weight) is associated with no adverse effects.
- Larval effects were documented in [[Bluegill]] at about 8 µg/g in ovaries and in fathead minnow at about 24 µg/g. The authors judged the threshold for razorback sucker eggs to lie between these bounds.
- Razorback sucker egg concentrations (3.7–10.6 µg/g) fall within this range, so larval effects are a concern.
- The 28 µg/g egg sample from 1991 would most likely have yielded no surviving larvae.

**4. Mechanism**

- Selenium in eggs and larvae is attributed mainly to parental transfer through ova, not to milt or waterborne uptake by eggs. Reference eggs exposed to 10 µg/L selenium for 24 h took up only minor amounts.
- Parental transfer effects documented elsewhere include edema, lordosis, hemorrhaging, reduced hatch, and early mortality.

**5. Spawning behavior**

- Altered spawning behavior is not expected for razorback suckers and has not been reported. Other species exposed to selenium showed normal spawning activity, though fry survival was reduced.

**6. Conclusions and limitations**

- Egg selenium is above background, but the authors could not state unequivocally that reproduction is adversely affected. The sample size (three females, five males) was small due to the species' rarity.
- The authors concluded that egg selenium "is sufficiently elevated to suspect reproductive problems" contributing to decline in the Green River and possibly other endangered fish of the upper Colorado River basin.
- The study was funded in part by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
