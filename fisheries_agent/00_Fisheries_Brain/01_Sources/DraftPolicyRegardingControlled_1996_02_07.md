# Draft Policy Regarding Controlled Propagation of Species Listed Under the Endangered Species Act (1996)

## Executive Summary

This is a joint **Federal Register notice** (61 FR 4715–4720, February 7, 1996) issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] proposing a unified draft policy governing **controlled propagation** (captive breeding, refugia maintenance, and reintroduction) of species listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of 1973. While the policy applies broadly to plants and animals, it explicitly identifies **[[Pacific Salmon]]** (genus *Oncorhynchus*) as the primary aquatic/fish case study, referencing NMFS's earlier 1993 interim controlled propagation policy for salmonids (58 FR 17573). The document establishes criteria, risk assessments, and administrative procedures for when and how captive propagation may be used as a recovery tool for imperiled fish and wildlife populations, emphasizing that propagation is a supplementary tool—not a substitute—for in situ habitat-based recovery.

## Background & Description

The ESA (16 U.S.C. 1531 et seq.) charges the Services with identifying, protecting, and recovering species in danger of extinction, and defines "species" to include subspecies and distinct vertebrate [[Population Segment|population segments]] (DPS) that interbreed when mature (Section 3(16)). Although the ESA's stated intent is to conserve the **ecosystems** upon which listed species depend (favoring in situ, wild recovery), Section 3(3) explicitly recognizes propagation as a legitimate recovery tool.

This draft policy responds to the growing use of hatcheries, refugia, and captive-breeding programs—particularly for [[Anadromous Fish|anadromous fish]] such as Pacific salmon—by establishing consistent guidance across FWS and NMFS. It builds on NMFS's 1993 interim policy specific to salmon controlled propagation, generalizing those lessons to all ESA-listed taxa.

Key definitions established by the policy relevant to fish conservation:
- **Controlled Environment**: A human-manipulated setting (hatchery, aquarium, refugium) designed to prevent unplanned escape/entry of organisms or reproductive material.
- **Controlled Propagation**: Intentional mating, gamete/embryo transfer, and grow-out of offspring under human intervention, for supplementation, augmentation, or reintroduction purposes (excludes "experimental populations").
- **Rescue/Salvage**: Emergency removal of a species or population segment to a controlled environment when facing imminent extinction/extirpation.
- **Intercross/Intercross Progeny**: Crosses between individuals of different species, subspecies, or populations, spanning a continuum from minor historical [[Introgression]] to fully intermediate hybrid morphology and functional sterility.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The policy is directly relevant to species inhabiting **freshwater, estuarine, and marine environments**, particularly diadromous fish stocks whose recovery depends on both hatchery intervention and habitat restoration (e.g., Pacific salmon runs affected by dams, habitat degradation, and overharvest). It stresses that controlled propagation must be coordinated with **habitat conservation actions** (restoration, management) to avoid mere symptom-treatment without addressing the root causes of decline (e.g., degraded spawning habitat, blocked migratory corridors).

### Genetic and Ecological Risks Identified

The policy comprehensively enumerates risks associated with propagation programs, of high relevance to salmonid hatchery science and [[Genetic Management|genetic management]] of fish stocks:

**Risks from broodstock collection/captive rearing:**
- Removal of wild broodstock reducing abundance and genetic variability of source populations
- Catastrophic loss risk (equipment failure, disease outbreak) in captivity
- Increased inbreeding or adverse genetic effects from partial gene pool use
- Erosion of population-specific genetic differences via mixed-stock transfers
- Exposure to novel selection pressures in captivity reducing wild fitness ("[[Domestication Selection|domestication selection]]")

**Risks from reintroduction/supplementation:**
- [[Genetic Introgression]] diminishing local adaptation of wild populations
- Increased predation, competition (for food, space, mates) displacing wild conspecifics
- Disease transfer from captive to wild populations
- Incidental take of listed species during commercial/recreational harvest targeting co-occurring unlisted species (a specific concern for mixed-stock salmon fisheries)

## Key Findings

1. **Hierarchy of Recovery Tools**: Controlled propagation is sanctioned only as a secondary strategy—used when in situ measures (habitat protection, translocation) have failed, are likely to fail, or are insufficient alone to achieve recovery.

2. **Recovery Plan Linkage**: Propagation programs must be explicitly recommended in an **approved recovery plan** and supported by an **approved genetics management plan**, except under specific emergency exceptions (e.g., imminent extinction risk, ephemeral reproductive stages, short 1–2 year lifespans).

3. **Genetics Management Plan Requirements**: Must ensure propagated individuals are genetically and behaviorally/physiologically comparable to wild counterparts, follow accepted standards (e.g., AZA Species Survival Plan studbooks), and address disposal protocols for individuals unfit for release, unfit as broodstock, or surplus to research/recovery needs.

4. **Intercrossing Restrictions**: Deliberate intercrossing (hybridization between species/subspecies/populations) in propagation programs is restricted to cases where it is: recovery-plan recommended, genetically justified, scientifically controlled, and intended to compensate for genetic isolation caused by human activity — requiring Director/Assistant Administrator approval.

5. **Facility Redundancy**: Where feasible, propagation should occur at multiple locations to mitigate risk of catastrophic loss at a single hatchery/refugium — directly applicable to salmon hatchery network design.

6. **Disease & Range Containment**: Programs must minimize disease/parasite spread and prevent escape of captive stock beyond historic range boundaries — a key concern for hatchery-origin fish straying into non-target watersheds.

7. **Pacific Salmon as Precedent**: NMFS's 1993 interim controlled propagation policy for [[Pacific Salmon]] is cited as the direct precursor and model informing this broader interagency policy, reflecting salmon's central role in shaping U.S. hatchery/ESA policy.

8. **Annual Oversight & Reporting**: Regional Directors/Assistant Administrators must report annually (by October 31) on propagation program status, expenditures, cooperators, and objectives for species held in refugia, under propagation research, or active propagation — applicable to ongoing salmonid hatchery and captive broodstock programs.

9. **Cooperator Framework**: The policy establishes a framework (under ESA Sections 6 and 2(a)(5)) for partnerships with states, tribes, zoos, aquaria, and academic institutions in fish and wildlife propagation, requiring cooperators to submit genetics management and reintroduction plans consistent with Service protocols.

10. **Legal Framework**: Implementation must align with the ESA, Marine Mammal Protection Act, Animal Welfare Act, Lacey Act, Fish and Wildlife Act of 1956, and NEPA — situating controlled propagation of fish and wildlife within a broader regulatory ecosystem.
