# Trout Stream Fisheries of the Baboosic Watershed, New Hampshire (1955 Study)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

A 1955 study by the [[New Hampshire Fish And Game Department]] evaluated the trout stream fisheries of the [[Baboosic Watershed]], a roughly 50-square-mile area in southeastern [[Hillsborough County]], New Hampshire. It was a contribution to a State-Federal "pilot" resource development project. The main goal was to test the effectiveness of the hatchery trout stocking policy by tagging every stocked trout and recovering tags through angler returns and a partial creel census. Of 5,800 tagged [[Brook Trout]] stocked, a minimum of 57% of the watershed's stocked fish were harvested by anglers. Harvest was concentrated in the first week of the season, and the fishery depended almost entirely on current-year stocked fish. The 1955 summer was one of the hottest and driest on record, which left stream conditions largely unsuitable for trout survival and carry-over. The authors concluded the fishery was marginal and "put and take" management was justified, and they made recommendations for a revised stocking schedule and further study.

## Background & Description

The study was initiated in 1955 under the [[Soil Conservation Service]] pilot program that preceded [[Public Law 566]]. Its principal objective was to evaluate the effectiveness of the hatchery stocking policy by determining the minimum known harvest of hatchery-raised trout. Secondary objectives were to estimate carry-over of earlier stockings, assess environmental and sociological factors, document fish movements, and collect basic harvest statistics (fishing pressure, catch per effort, and stocked-to-wild ratios).

The Baboosic Watershed lies just west of the [[Merrimack River]], about equidistant from Manchester and Nashua, within fifty miles of the Boston metropolitan area. It covers about 32,000 acres (nearly 50 square miles), roughly 80% wooded with second-growth mixed hardwoods and conifers. Dairy, poultry, and orchard products dominate agriculture. Baboosic Brook drains [[Baboosic Lake]] (222 acres) and flows nine miles to the [[Souhegan River]]. Its four main tributaries are [[Joe English Brook]], [[Pulpit Brook]], [[McQuade Brook]], and [[Riddle Brook]], whose combined length is 132,000 feet.

Baboosic Brook is sluggish and muddy-bottomed, averaging 8 to 12 feet wide and about two feet deep, with many large, deep pools. The tributaries are steep and rocky in their upper reaches and level off near their mouths. Only Joe English Brook is accessible by road along most of its length. The other tributaries can be reached mainly at road crossings.

Methods centred on tagging. Stocked brook trout were tagged at the [[Merrimack Rearing Station]] with numbered Monel jaw tags stamped "Ret: N.H. F. & G." Fish were anesthetized with urethane, and a truck-mounted circulating-water system kept them alive during transport. A sample of 150 hatchery fish averaged 8.8 inches and 4.4 ounces, and were in excellent condition. Tag-return containers were placed at access points, and an angler-interview [[Partial Creel Census]] ran from opening day through June. Population sampling used a back-pack electro-shocker, [[Rotenone Sampling]], and a borrowed Massachusetts electro-shocker.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water conditions were the dominant control on the fishery. The Baboosic is subject to marked natural fluctuations in flow and temperature. Its tributaries were cooler because of heavy shading, springs, and the absence of impoundments. Lower reaches passed through swamps and farmland and ran warmer in summer. Siltation, erosion, and pollution were described as negligible, while flooding occurred mainly in spring.

The 1954 summer in lower New England was abnormally cool and wet, which favored carry-over of trout. In contrast, 1955 was among the hottest and driest seasons on record. May was the hottest and driest in eleven years. Water temperatures reached about 70°F in May, and in July average water temperatures in the shaded brooks rose into the upper seventies. Some brooks dried completely by mid-July, and pools reached about 80°F. Early August rains from hurricanes produced 13.67 inches of precipitation in New England, nearly ten inches above the 67-year average, but flood runoff was rapid and groundwater stayed low.

Fish communities were dominated by non-game "trash fish," including suckers, which crowded into remaining water and likely increased competition with trout. Electro-fishing and spot poisoning in late summer recovered only two trout (one tagged, one brown trout) among large numbers of rough fish.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and tagging**
- A total of 5,800 brook trout were tagged and released in Baboosic Brook and its tributaries in 1955: 4,200 preseason (April 15 to 20) and 1,600 inseason (May 19).
- Preseason stocking was 2,700 in the four tributaries and 2,500 in Baboosic Brook. Inseason stocking was 600 in three tributaries and 1,000 in Baboosic Brook.
- No handling, transport, or stocking mortality was recorded.
- No brown trout were stocked in 1955 because they had not reached legal length in the hatchery in time.

**Tag returns and harvest**
- Known minimum returns were 60% for Baboosic Brook and 57% for the watershed as a whole.
- Returns across eleven Baboosic stocking sites ranged from 43% to 72%.
- Joe English Brook had the highest tributary return (66%). Riddle Brook had the lowest (46%), and McQuade and Pulpit brooks were each about 52%.
- Using an estimated 15% non-return of tags (from Albert Swartz, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service), roughly 75% of tagged fish were probably harvested.
- Harvest timing for preseason stock: at least 26% were caught on opening day, at least 41% during the first week, and only 16% during the rest of the season.
- Inseason stocked fish were caught mostly soon after release, based on a marked rise in pressure over the three days after stocking.

**Accessibility and angler behaviour**
- Harvest tracked site accessibility and fishing pressure. Site No. 6 (inaccessible, 400 stocked) returned 43%, while nearby accessible sites returned 64% and 65%.
- Posted land (Site No. 3) appeared to reduce fishing pressure.
- Anglers were reluctant to fish away from road crossings, and tended to move between accessible points rather than fish long stretches.

**Angler success**
- On opening day, anglers caught 1.6 tagged fish per hour on Baboosic Brook (309 trout in 192.5 hours).
- The first-week rate fell to 1.34 fish per hour, or 0.74 excluding opening day. Riddle Brook rates were 0.59 (opening day) and 0.35 (first week).
- Catch per hour was 0.73 for May excluding opening day, compared with 0.26 in June, after no further stocking.
- The seasonal catch rate was 0.94 trout per hour.
- Of 411 anglers checked, 192 (47%) were successful. Of 376 checked on Baboosic Brook, 177 (47%) were successful.
- About 20% of successful anglers caught 49% (319 of 656) of the trout recorded.

**Bait and lures**
- Of 332 anglers questioned, 82% used worms, 11% used other lures, and 7% used flies only.
- Of 470 trout checked, 85% were taken on worms, 10% on other lures, and 5% on flies.
- Dense bankside growth limited fly fishing in many sections.

**Catch composition**
- Of 690 trout in the recorded catch, 599 (87%) were brook trout and 91 (13%) were brown trout.
- Of the brook trout, 555 (93%) were tagged current-year fish and 44 (7%) were untagged.
- Of the 135 untagged trout harvested, 91 (67%) were brown trout.
- Carry-over was low because conditions were poor in 1955, but it is unclear whether 91 carried-over browns was a good or poor result.

**Length and weight**
- During the creel census, 75 trout were measured and weighed. Compared with the hatchery sample, length increased by 0.09 inches and weight decreased by 0.03 pounds, differences the authors considered insignificant sampling error.

**Movement**
- Of 377 tagged trout checked for movement, 254 (67%) could be plotted accurately.
- Eighty percent of the plotted fish did not move at least half a mile from the release site.
- Of the fish that moved, 11% went upstream and 9% went downstream.
- Preseason fish that moved tended to go upstream, and inseason fish tended to go downstream.
- Ten fish moved far, including seven that left the watershed. Seven fish were recovered in other streams, including [[Chase Brook]], [[Colby Brook]], [[Stearns Brook]], and [[Ryan Brook]].

**Comparison with other streams**
- Baboosic Brook ranked highest in catch per hour (0.94) among the compared streams, including [[Swift River]] (0.63), [[Millers River]] (0.19), [[Westfield River]] (0.41), and [[Quaboag River]] (0.35). The Baboosic figure covered brook trout only, while the others included two or three species.
- Its current-year stocked share of the catch (80%) and its tag return (60%, known minimum) were also high. The Quaboag River had the highest return (80%).

**Climate and population**
- The 1955 population sampling recovered only two trout.
- Stream conditions in 1955 were far more adverse than in 1954, leaving the natural trout population very low.

**Conclusions**
- The watershed's trout fisheries are marginal and depend on "put and take" management.
- Natural production is limited by high summer temperatures, low flows, periodic flooding, and competition from trash fish.
- Initial utilization of stocked fish was high, and returns were concentrated at accessible sites.

**Recommendations**
- Continue preseason stocking one to two weeks before the season opens, close to the road, so fish can acclimate.
- Maintain the brook trout stocking level at the 1955 level until further studies show maximum utilization.
- Continue stocking brown trout alongside brook trout, at heavily fished sites, to extend the season. Consider rainbow trout as a substitute if brown trout utilization is poor.
- Stock legal fish, preferably 8 inches or longer, and some trophy fish of 12 inches or more.
- Do not stock within one-quarter mile of posted sections.
- Educate anglers about "put and take" management.
- Review night fishing regulations to improve brown trout harvest.
- Investigate explosives to create pools in tributaries, and consider destroying spawning sucker concentrations in Baboosic Brook.
- Conduct follow-up studies of the stocking policy, and repeat electro-fishing in years with sustained summer flow.
