# Beaver Management and Ecology in Wyoming

#mammalogy #freshwater-ecology #rodentia

## Executive Summary

This second-edition bulletin, produced by the [[Wyoming Game And Fish Commission]] under Federal Aid in Wildlife Restoration Projects W-31-D and W-30-R, argues that the [[American Beaver]] is one of Wyoming's most valuable watershed conservators. Its dams store water, stabilize stream flow, and often improve [[Trout]] habitat, so its indirect values should outweigh its pelt value. The bulletin also documents a live-trapping and transplanting program (634 beaver planted in 130 plants over three seasons), reports casualty rates that fell from 9.8% in 1947 to 3.0% in 1949, and calls for active management, including regular trapping and pelting, to limit damage and overpopulation. The authors state that many questions remain open.

## Background & Description

**Physical characteristics.** Mature beaver weigh 40 to 50 pounds, with unusually large individuals reaching 100 pounds, making the beaver the largest rodent in North America. Key adaptations include:

- Large, fully webbed hind feet and a flat, trowel-shaped tail used for propulsion, steering, balance, and signaling.
- Front feet with long digging claws and dexterous, hand-like use for handling building material and food.
- Valvular ears and closed nostrils underwater, and lips that close behind the incisors so the animal can gnaw underwater.
- Continuously growing incisors kept sharp by wear.
- A dense under-fur protected by coarse guard hairs, kept water-repellent by oil from two oil sacs.
- Castors (scent glands) in both sexes. Castoreum has commercial value and has sold for as much as $20 per pound in the past, though $6 to $7 per pound was the stated value at the time.

**Life history.** Beaver are mostly nocturnal. The authors believe they are largely monogamous, with a near 1:1 sex ratio among 634 live-trapped animals. Mating is believed to occur in February, and young are usually born between June 1 and June 20. Litters range from one to six kits (very rarely eight), with an average of 3.8 fetuses in 60 gestation studies. Young typically remain in the colony for nearly two years. Two-year-olds are driven out, which naturally extends beaver range. Average weights by age were: mature 41 lb, two-year-old 29 lb, one-year-old 24 lb, and kit 9 lb. The authors concluded that age cannot reliably be determined from weight or external measurements.

**Historical context.** Heavy fur trapping by competing companies, including the Hudson's Bay, Northwest, and American Fur companies, depleted Wyoming's beaver. The species was considered practically extinct in the region from 1860 to about 1905. The legislature closed the season in 1899, and beaver remain one of Wyoming's two protected animals (buffalo is the other). Restoration has reached only about one-third of the former range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and water storage.** Wyoming is a headwaters state feeding four major river systems. The authors note that one beaver pond on Coal Creek (4,680 feet in circumference) impounds 10,108,435 cubic feet of water. They calculate this volume would sustain the creek's flow for 117 days if drainage inflow were cut off. Water percolates through the earth below dams, so downstream flow is stabilized. Beaver ponds also serve as settling basins, and some of Wyoming's most fertile meadows formed this way.

**Vegetation.** In summer, beaver eat aquatic plants, grasses, and tree bark. In winter they rely on food caches of aspen, cottonwood, or willow stored in deep water near the lodge. Aldous (1938) estimated daily intake at 22 to 33 ounces of bark and twigs, and found that beaver utilized about 36% of available aspen bark and twig weight at five active dams.

**Associated wildlife.** Beaver ponds attract waterfowl, including mallard and teal broods. [[Muskrat]] use beaver lodges and works, and the authors describe the relationship as one-sided. [[Mink]] are drawn to ponds for muskrats and trout, and the authors observed one catching a ten-inch [[Cutthroat Trout]]. [[Moose]] and other big game feed in and around ponds, although dams can flood browse.

**Winter constraint.** Deep ice limits beaver movement and can cause starvation or smothering. Trout face the same constraint through reduced feeding range and oxygen. Beaver therefore seek the warmest water in a drainage. The authors noted that most Wyoming trout streams are colder than optimal for growth.

**Damage.** Beaver flood cropland and roads, plug [[Culverts]] and bridges, cut shade and fruit trees, interfere with irrigation, and can drown livestock. Downstream, where the land is more intensively used, beaver are often detrimental.

## Key Findings

**Economic context and pelt prices**
- In a 1946 sale, 608 pelts earned $24,320 (about $40 per pelt). In 1947, 836 pelts averaged over $34.
- Prices fell after late 1948. In July 1954, 1,430 hides averaged $5.10. In January 1955 the average rose to $16.50.
- Authors argue that the beaver's indirect benefits are "infinitely more valuable" than its pelt, and that pelt revenue should remain secondary.

**Harvest and permits**
- Since 1920, 207,422 beaver have been legally pelted in Wyoming, with over 85% taken by landowners under damage or fur-farm permits.
- Annual totals in the 1941 to 1954 table ranged from 5,345 (1950) to 11,623 (1953). In 1954, 9,320 were taken, including 2,708 by state trappers.
- 402 fur-farming permits were in effect. The authors say fur farming is supplemental income, and no confined beaver farm in Wyoming had demonstrated success.

**Trapping and transplanting**
- Restocking used [[Bailey Live-Trap]] and [[Hancock Live-Trap]] designs. The Bailey trap was preferred for its reliability and suitability for runs and channels. The Hancock trap held beaver with less water exposure.
- Nuisance beaver and overstocked streams both supplied planting stock. About half of the 634 transplants were nuisance beaver.
- Beaver were held in holding pens and given enough water to drink. Individuals were held for over two weeks with no ill effects.
- Casualties: 1947 had 29 deaths of 296 caught (9.8%); 1948 had 17 of 193 (8.8%); 1949 had 6 of 197 (3.0%). Most trap losses were due to soaking and chilling.

**Beaver-trout relationship**
- Beaver ponds were judged by the authors to offer the best fishing in their localities. The authors cite four ponds, with ages ranging from seven to over fifteen years, as examples.
- On the Coal Creek pond, 12,320 two-to-three-inch [[Eastern Brook Trout]] fingerlings were planted in August 1948. In a September 1949 sample of 12 trout, the largest measured 13.25 inches and the smallest 1.25 inches. Average dressed weight was about ¾ pound, and the fish were full of spawn.
- Beaver can benefit trout, but dams may block fall-spawning brook trout. Brook trout nonetheless reproduce well in beaver streams, often to the point of overcrowding.

**Little Cottonwood Creek Study Area**
- Ten beaver were planted on the Middle Fork of Little Cottonwood Creek on July 15, 1948. By July 1949, 55 dams formed five colonies along about 2.25 miles.
- Ten beaver were planted on [[Mickelson Creek]] on July 30, 1948. These formed three colonies with 14 dams. These dams impounded slightly more water than the 55 on the Middle Fork.
- Retrapping one year after planting showed that only one of six retrapped beaver on the Middle Fork gained weight. Every mature female taken on either stream was pregnant, suggesting reproduction was normal.
- The authors concluded that age cannot be judged by weight, tail size, or hind-foot length, and that growth depends strongly on environment.

**Management recommendations**
- Maintain beaver in all suitable headwaters, and remove them from damaging downstream locations.
- Harvest surplus beaver through steel trapping, which "must always be a part of any wise management plan."
- Do not adopt an open trapping season. Authors argue administrative costs would exceed profits, and the watershed value of beaver outweighs the fur harvest.
- Do not plant beaver on private land unless the owner is sincere about propagation.
- Set traps at the dam crossing or scent sites, weighted and offset-jawed, to drown animals quickly and reduce wring-offs. Use [[Beaver Scent]] where beaver are not scent-shy.

**Open questions noted by the authors**
- Whether two-year-olds mate with siblings or seek other mates.
- The long-term effect of beaver on trout populations, which the authors say has never been studied by qualified Wyoming personnel.
- Whether a pond's trout productivity lasts once its aquatic insect populations decline.
