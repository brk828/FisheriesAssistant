# The Invasion, Present Status, and Life History of the Sea Lamprey in the Great Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Petromyzontidae #invasive-species #GreatLakes

## Executive Summary

This 1949 report by Milton B. Trautman of the [[Franz Theodore Stone Laboratory]] documents the historical invasion, spread, and life history of the landlocked [[Sea Lamprey]] (*Petromyzon marinus*) throughout the [[Great Lakes]] system, with particular focus on the Ohio waters of [[Lake Erie]]. The Sea Lamprey was native to Lake Ontario but was physically barred from the upper lakes by [[Niagara Falls]] until man-made canals provided a migration pathway. Predicted by naturalist [[Jared P. Kirtland]] in 1851, the invasion did not materialize until 1921 in Lake Erie, followed by slower colonization of Lakes Michigan (1936), Huron (1937), and Superior (1946). Notably, despite earlier arrival, the Sea Lamprey remains rare in Lake Erie due to unsuitable (silted, dammed, polluted) spawning streams, while it became devastatingly abundant in Lakes Huron and Michigan, correlating with sharp declines in commercial [[Lake Trout]] catches. The report also details lamprey reproductive biology, larval (ammocoete) development, parasitic feeding mechanics, and potential economic uses as food, bait, and industrial products.

## Background & Description

- **Adult size:** 14–30 inches (35.5–76 cm) in length at sexual maturity.
- **Mouth:** Powerful, sucking disc-like oral apparatus used both for nest excavation (moving stones) and for parasitic attachment to host fish.
- **Dentition:** Newly transformed adults possess well-developed teeth (including sharp tongue teeth used for drilling into host flesh) and functional eyes, contrasting with the blind, toothless larval stage.
- **Larvae (ammocoetes):** Blind and toothless; grow to 4½–7 inches before undergoing metamorphosis.
- **Secretions:** Glands on the underside of the head secrete an anticoagulant fluid that prevents host blood from clotting during feeding.
- **Scarring capability:** Attachment suction strength requires a pull of more than 10 lbs to dislodge the animal from a host fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native distribution:** Landlocked in [[Lake Ontario]] "for untold centuries," prevented from reaching upper lakes by [[Niagara Falls]] as a natural barrier.
- **Invasion pathway:** Man-made canals connecting Lake Ontario and Lake Erie enabled range expansion; first Lake Erie record November 8, 1921 (Ontario waters near Merlin); first Ohio record November 14, 1927 (West Sister Island).
- **Spread timeline:**
  - 1930: St. Clair River (connecting Lake Huron and Lake St. Clair)
  - 1936: Lake Michigan (near Milwaukee, WI, and Elk Rapids, MI)
  - 1937: Lake Huron (Ocqueoc River spawning run)
  - 1938: Clinton River, Michigan (observed spawning by Trautman and Dr. H.J. Deason); subsequently recorded in 83 additional Michigan streams
  - 1946: Established in [[Lake Superior]], increasing rapidly thereafter
- **Habitat requirements for spawning:** Clean, silt-free streams with sand/gravel/rock riffle bottoms; sandy banks rich in organic debris for larval rearing. These conditions are abundant in tributaries of Lakes Huron, Michigan, and Superior but largely absent or degraded in Lake Erie tributaries (e.g., silted [[Maumee River]], dammed [[Sandusky River]], polluted [[Cuyahoga River]]).
- **Comparative abundance:** Despite earlier establishment, Sea Lamprey remains rare in Lake Erie (~1 specimen/year via bounty program at Stone Laboratory) compared to explosive abundance in Michigan streams (9,000+ caught in 1945 in Ocqueoc River traps alone).
- **Co-occurring parasitic species:** [[Silver Lamprey]] (*Ichthyomyzon unicuspis*, all upper lakes) and [[Chestnut Lamprey]] (*Ichthyomyzon castaneus*, primarily Lake Michigan and northern Lake Huron) — both long-established, non-invasive parasitic lampreys.
- **Host preference:** Prefers scaleless fishes (catfishes, sturgeons) or thin-scaled fishes (suckers, trout); avoids heavily armored spiny-rayed fishes like bass.
- **Predator-prey/parasite dynamics:** Typically does not kill hosts outright but causes scarring that reduces commercial marketability; some catches reported 60–100% scarred.

## Key Findings

1. **Historical prediction validated late:** [[Jared P. Kirtland]] predicted Sea Lamprey invasion of Lake Erie via canals in 1851, but the event did not occur until 1921 — a 70-year lag.
2. **Differential establishment success:** Despite Lake Erie's greater fish productivity ("more pounds of fish annually than any water area of equal size" in the Great Lakes), the Sea Lamprey failed to flourish there due to degraded/absent spawning streams, while it thrived in the cleaner tributaries of Lakes Huron, Michigan, and Superior.
3. **Native Lake Lamprey decline:** Historical interviews (commercial fisherman Charles Nielsen, Venice, Ohio) indicate the native Lake Lamprey population in Lake Erie/Sandusky Bay was once abundant (~12 per net lift, 30+ years prior) but has drastically declined, suggesting shared environmental degradation (siltation, damming, pollution) affected both native and invasive lamprey species similarly.
4. **Commercial fishery impact:** Lake Trout catch in Lake Huron (including Georgian Bay/North Channel) fell from 4,314,762 lbs (1936, pre-lamprey abundance) to 1,140,158 lbs (1944, post-lamprey establishment) — a ~74% decline, though the report notes causal linkage to lampreys is not fully proven since lampreys rarely kill hosts outright.
5. **Life cycle parameters documented:**
   - Migration begins at water temp >45°F, peaks >50°F
   - Nest-building begins at water temp >55°F
   - Egg-laying occurs at water temp >60°F
   - Fecundity: 24,000 to over 100,000 eggs per female
   - Egg incubation: 15–21 days (temperature-dependent)
   - Larval (ammocoete) stage: 1–6 years (estimates vary among investigators) until reaching 4½–7 inches
   - Post-metamorphosis parasitic feeding phase: ~17 months before sexual maturity, upstream spawning migration, and death (semelparous life history)
6. **Economic potential identified:** Historical and contemporary uses include human food (European delicacy traditions; colonial New England "spring Thanksgiving dinner" with salmon and shad), fish bait (cut-bait and live-bait; "lampereels" sold 10–15 cents each in southern Ohio; 450,000+ used annually as cut-bait in England), and industrial/pharmaceutical use (vitamin extraction, meal production at $80/ton in Oregon, 1942).
7. **Public perception shift:** Modern framing of lampreys via pejorative terms ("dracula," "blood-sucker," "vermin," "predator") has undermined public willingness to consider them as a food resource, despite historical precedent as a delicacy.
8. **Research priorities (as of 1949):** (1) detailed life cycle study for control method development, (2) causal relationship between Sea Lamprey abundance and Lake Trout decline, (3) exploration of economic/commercial uses as a management incentive.
