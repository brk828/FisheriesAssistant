# Thiara (Melanoides) Tuberculata and Its Probable Ecological Impact in Mexico

#malacology #invasive-species #thiaridae

## Executive Summary

This study assesses the probable ecological impact of the exotic freshwater snail [[Thiara (Melanoides) Tuberculata]] (Gastropoda: [[Thiaridae]]) in Mexico. The species was introduced around the 1960s, probably through the [[Aquarium Trade]], and has since spread to springs, streams, rivers and adjacent waters across many states. Its ecological effect on native mollusc faunas had not previously been documented. The authors report that the snail usually lives alone; where it coexists with other species, those are generally rare or scarce. At four localities in Coahuila and Nuevo León, the snail is the apparent cause of native species losses. The authors present quantitative data on abundance, species richness and apparent impact across several sites, while noting that the causal link is largely circumstantial.

## Background & Description

*Thiara (Melanoides) tuberculata* (Müller, 1774) is a prosobranch freshwater snail of Asian origin. Its success as an invader is attributed to several traits:

- **Dispersal:** by river capture or adventitious transport (Morrison, 1954), and frequently through aquarium trade shipments of aquatic plants and animals.
- **Desiccation resistance:** the operculum allows survival for long periods during transport.
- **Reproduction:** high reproductive capacity, including parthenogenesis, which the authors say makes eradication difficult.
- **Invasiveness:** described as a "traveling snail" (Smith, 1989), whose ecological significance depends on how it establishes in a new habitat.

**Known distribution history:**
- First Mexican record: Abbott (1973), around Veracruz.
- Jiménez et al. (1980) recorded it as *Oncomelania* sp. from Parras de la Fuente, Coahuila.
- Contreras-Balderas and Maeda-Martínez (1985) recorded it as *Tarebia* sp. from the same locality.
- Pointier and McCullough (1989) reported it for Mexico from Parras, Coahuila, material supplied by the senior authors.
- Contreras-Arquieta (1991) documented it as a recently introduced, well-established species in the San Juan sub-basin, Nuevo León, where it does not coexist with other species recorded there.
- Contreras-Arquieta et al. (in press) reported the species from 65 localities in 12 Mexican states.

**Parasite link:** Rodríguez-Vargas and Salgado-Maldonado (1991) identified it as an intermediate host of the trematode *Centrocestus formosanus* at Manantial Las Estacas, Morelos.

*The percentage listed for the two-locality Physa category does not match its count and appears to be an error in the source.

**Environmental factors:** Pointier and McCullough (1989) listed conditions that favor or limit thiarid densities:
- Favorable: permanent water, shallow water, emergent plants, well-oxygenated niches.
- Unfavorable: temporary water, deep water, deep-rooted plants, anoxic niches.

The Mexican observations largely agree with this pattern, with several exceptions:
- Río Salado (Coahuila): individuals buried up to 10 cm in silt.
- Ojo de Agua La Concha (Durango): thermal water, low oxygen, abundant to 4 m depth.
- Veracruz streams: rich in decomposing organic matter.

The physicochemical conditions across the distribution (temperature, salinity, hardness, dissolved oxygen) are described as highly variable. Sites in the Cuatro Ciénegas region are high in salinity, hardness and gypsum (Minckley and Cole, 1968).

**Sites of concern:**
- **Río Salado / Carrizo de La Montaña, Coahuila:** endemic Cuatro Ciénegas snails (*Nymphophilus minckleyi*, *Durangonella coahuilae*, *Pyrgulopsis manantialis*) were found only as empty shells. The authors note a current hydrological connection that may allow the snail to move upstream toward the endemic fauna of the Cuatro Ciénegas valley.

## Key Findings

**1. Río Salado de Nadadores, Coahuila (Cuadro 2)**
- Taylor (1966) recorded five native species, including *Cochliopina riograndensis*, *Physa virgata*, *Helisoma anceps*, *Gundlachia radiata* and *Pisidium compressum*.
- 2 April 1986, Santa Gertrudis: only *T. tuberculata* was found; estimated 100% loss of native fauna.
- 22 July 1987, Carrizo de La Montaña: only empty shells of native species plus one live *H. anceps*; estimated 80% loss.

**2. Parras de la Fuente, Coahuila (Cuadro 3)**
- Before 1974, the native fauna included *Physa virgata*, *Fossaria* sp. and *Gundlachia radiata*.
- By 1986, only *T. tuberculata* was found in the ponds, with a reported 100% loss in those localities. *G. radiata* was extremely rare.
- Reported counts of Thiara rose from 1 (1964) to 3 (1974) to "hundreds" (1986). Water decline in springs and the water table is also noted.

**3. Río La Silla, Guadalupe, Nuevo León (Cuadro 4)**
- Four native species were recorded between 1986 and 1988.
- 1990: only *T. tuberculata* was found, a 100% loss.
- 1991: three native species were collected (25% loss), but they did not share microhabitat with Thiara and were isolated in pools. The footnote states that 75% of the fauna is strongly affected.

**4. Presa Rodrigo Gómez "La Boca", Santiago, Nuevo León (Cuadro 5)**
- 1985: one juvenile *T. tuberculata* found.
- 1987–1991: not found in repeated surveys.
- September 1992: densities up to 300 individuals/m², mainly at the mouths of the Dolores and Cristalinas streams.
- Now coexists only with *Anodonta imbecilis* and the exotic *Corbicula manilensis*.
- Previously 11 species were present (including *Pyrgophorus spinosus*, *Physa mexicana*, *Biomphalaria havanensis*, *Planorbella trivolvis*, *Gundlachia radiata* and *Sphaerium* sp.).
- Estimated losses: 75% in the southern part of the reservoir and 36% overall. The table lists the overall loss as "360", which appears to be an OCR error for 36.
- Native gastropods persist in the northern and northeastern areas.

**5. Veracruz**
- Los Tuxtlas: Rangel-Ruiz (1984) did not record the species. A sparse population was found at Laguna Escondida in March 1990.
- Lago de Catemaco: in March 1990, four gastropod species and three bivalves were recorded. In August 1992, *T. tuberculata* was found at low densities (30–50 individuals/m²) only at Puerto de los Pescadores. Other species remained abundant.

**6. Other states**
- Durango: densities up to 1,500 individuals/m², with almost no prior faunal data before or after introduction, so losses cannot be quantified.
- Quintana Roo, Tamaulipas and others: similar lack of baseline data.

**7. International comparison**
- Lago de Yojoa, Honduras (Clark, 1987): 20 mollusc species in 1980, with *Biomphalaria havanensis* and *Pachycheilus largillierti* dominant. One Thiara was seen in 1980; by 1985 several hundred per m² were present, while *Biomphalaria* and *Pachycheilus* had become very rare. The authors interpret this as apparent competitive exclusion.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The authors conclude that *T. tuberculata* has a probable negative effect on Mexican mollusc faunas, especially in four localities in Coahuila and Nuevo León.
- The evidence is primarily visual and circumstantial, because baseline data are scarce and the faunal records are fragmentary.
- Early monitoring of recently invaded sites such as Catemaco is recommended to track impact.
- Eradication is considered difficult given the species' reproductive strategy.
- The distribution and environmental factors of the species need further study, particularly in connection with the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] endemic fauna.
