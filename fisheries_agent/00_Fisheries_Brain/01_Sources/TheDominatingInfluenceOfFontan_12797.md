# The Dominating Influence of Fontana Reservoir on Temperature and Dissolved Oxygen in the Little Tennessee River and Its Impoundments

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1949 limnological study, reprinted from the *Journal of the Tennessee Academy of Science*, examines how the deep [[Fontana Reservoir]] controls temperature and dissolved oxygen (DO) in downstream waters: [[Cheoah Reservoir]], [[Calderwood Reservoir]], and the [[Little Tennessee River]]. The immediate goal was to determine whether these waters were suitable for trout, since trout are more sensitive than warm-water game fish to high temperature and low oxygen. Fontana's impoundment had eliminated previously productive warm-water fisheries (bass and walleye). The authors concluded that water passes through the downstream reservoirs as thick density currents that change little in temperature or oxygen en route, that Calderwood may support rainbow or brook trout if food is adequate, and that the river below Calderwood appears suitable for brook or rainbow trout.

## Background & Description

The study was part of the [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] program to improve recreational use of Tennessee Valley waters. Authors Jack S. Dendy (Alabama Polytechnic Institute) and Richard H. Stroup (TVA, Norris, Tennessee) made observations on Fontana Reservoir in North Carolina and downstream waters in North Carolina and Tennessee. Methods included:

- Water temperature measured with a Foxboro resistance thermometer, with occasional pocket thermometer readings calibrated against it.
- Dissolved oxygen by the azide modification of the [[Winkler Method]], per *Standard Methods* (ninth edition, 1946).
- pH measured with a Helige comparator.

The reservoirs differ in size and age (Table 1 in the source):

| Reservoir | Dam height (ft) | Useful storage (acre-ft) | Length (mi) | Area (acres) | Gate closure |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fontana | 480 | 1,157,300 | 29 | 10,670 | Nov. 1944 |
| Cheoah | 230 | 7,900 | 10 | 640 | Apr. 1919 |
| Calderwood | 230 | 4,090 | 8 | 530 | Apr. 1930 |
| Santeetlah | 200 | 130,500 | 8 | 2,850 | June 1928 |

Fontana is operated for flood control, navigation, and power. Cheoah and Calderwood are small, deep reservoirs in narrow gorges that behave more like deep, slow rivers than lakes. Cheoah receives most of its water from Fontana, with perhaps one-fifth or less from Santeetlah and smaller tributaries; inflow to Calderwood from tributaries is negligible.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pre-impoundment conditions.** In August 1938, Calderwood was a typical warm-water habitat, with surface temperature 85 °F and 80 °F at 20 ft (data from Shields, 1939). Local fishermen reported good fishing in Cheoah and Calderwood before Fontana closed, and many fine walleye catches in the river below.

**Post-impoundment conditions.** In August 1947 the corresponding temperatures were 70 °F and 56.25 °F, showing drastic cooling. Fontana's water rarely warms above about 70 °F at the penstocks because the reservoir is drawn down from high summer levels, so cold water from depth passes through the turbines. Surface layers may exceed 80 °F, but they are drawn down only after autumn cooling.

**Water quality.** Fontana water is blackish from sulfite wastes from an industrial tributary, but turbidity and most oxygen demand are removed before water leaves the dam. Summer pH was about 7.0 at the surface and about 6.3 at penstock depth.

**Density currents and stratification.** Water from Fontana moves through Cheoah and Calderwood as thick density currents of fairly uniform temperature and DO. Above these currents lie thin, warmer, well-aerated surface layers; below them are colder, stagnant pools with low oxygen.

**Food supply.** Food was not studied in depth. Vertical plankton hauls found small copepod and cladoceran populations. Bottom fauna in the reservoirs was poor, with few oligochaetes and chironomid larvae. Terrestrial insects were important food, based on surface observations and fish stomach examination. Downstream, the bottom fauna near Scona Ferry was poor, but a shoal area 7 miles farther downstream held abundant caddisfly larvae and moderate numbers of immature mayflies, stoneflies, blackflies, and hellgrammites.

**Related reservoir systems.** The authors note similar patterns elsewhere: [[Hiwassee Reservoir]] discharges through [[Apalachia Reservoir]] as a homothermous density current, and cold water from [[Cherokee Reservoir]] and [[Douglas Reservoir]] passes through [[Fort Loudoun Reservoir]] at certain times of year.

## Key Findings

**Temperature and oxygen seasonality (Table 2 summary).** Between September 8 and November 3, water entering Cheoah was unsuitable for trout because of low oxygen and high temperature. The most severe conditions occurred on October 20:

- Maximum temperature: 68.5 °F (Fontana and Cheoah discharges)
- Minimum DO: 1.6 ppm (Fontana discharge, October 20)

Selected Fontana discharge values:

| Date | Temp (°F) | DO (ppm) |
|---|---|---|
| August 12 | 55.0 | 6.2 |
| September 8 | 61.6 | 4.3 |
| October 6 | 68.25 | 2.4 |
| October 20 | 68.5 | 1.6 |
| November 17 | 58.25 | 5.3 |

Cheoah DO was consistently higher on most dates than Fontana's, with the same temperature pattern. Table 2 is a summary; the full dataset is in the source.

**Oxygen recovery.** Water entering Calderwood usually had higher DO than water entering Cheoah. The authors attribute this to oxygen uptake in or just below Cheoah, which gained about 1 ppm en route. On September 8, the exception, the warmer water entering Calderwood probably came from a poorer-oxygen stratum.

**Downstream temperature.** Tailwater temperatures on August 12, 1947 were Fontana 55 °F, Cheoah 56 °F, and Calderwood 57.25 °F, and Scona Ferry (one mile below Calderwood) was 58.25 °F. Temperatures rose only modestly downstream, reaching 66 °F at Davis Ferry on July 30 and 66.5 °F at Vonore Bridge on October 6.

**Timing of influence.** A water mass takes four or five days to pass through Calderwood. Changes at the dams reach the river below within about two weeks.

**Management conclusions.**

- Calderwood might support rainbow or brook trout if conditions remained no worse than in 1947 and food is available; both had been stocked in tributaries.
- Lake trout performance in Maine lakes (Cooper, 1941) suggests thriving populations are rare where water exceeds about 60 °F. Calderwood is "at best... a borderline environment" for lake trout.
- Temperature cannot be controlled. A [[Reaeration Plant]] similar to the one on the Flambeau River, Wisconsin, might make Cheoah and Calderwood lake trout waters if oxygen is the limiting factor. Operating costs would be low because aeration would be needed only briefly each year, though installation costs would be high. Reduced pollution and reservoir aging might also correct the oxygen deficit naturally.
- More data on temperature, oxygen, and lake trout food are needed before any large-scale reaeration or stocking.
- The river below Calderwood is suitable now for brook or rainbow trout.

**Summary of conclusions.** Cold water from low Fontana levels eliminated the original warm-water fisheries. Waters from Fontana change little in temperature or oxygen en route through Cheoah and Calderwood. Calderwood fared better than Cheoah. Food availability is the key unknown for trout introductions.
