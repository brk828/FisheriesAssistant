# Nitrogen and Phosphorus Dynamics in Selected Southwestern Streams

#stream-ecology #nutrient-dynamics #desert-streams #periphyton #primary-production

## Executive Summary

This Master's thesis by Nancy Beth Grimm (Arizona State University, May 1980) investigated nitrogen and phosphorus dynamics in streams of the [[Gila River Basin]], Arizona. The study assessed potential nutrient limitation of primary production and the role of [[Periphyton]] in nutrient uptake across 38 stream sites sampled on 59 dates, primarily in summer 1979. All continuously flowing desert and semi-desert streams exhibited low nitrogen to phosphorus ratios (N:P < 14), suggesting nitrogen limitation. However, sources of isolated stream segments and floodwaters were nitrogen-enriched (N:P > 14), reflecting recent contact with terrestrial soils. Nitrogen uptake in stream segments was positively correlated with periphyton abundance (chlorophyll a and biomass), and predicted uptake from net production matched observed uptake. Phosphorus uptake could not be predicted from net production alone, suggesting control by additional factors such as sediment sorption. The research demonstrates that [[Periphyton]] play a dominant role in nitrogen uptake in Southwestern streams, while phosphorus dynamics are controlled by multiple factors beyond autotrophic assimilation.

## Background & Description

### Study Region and Climate

The [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona encompasses elevations from 650 to 1,480 meters within Upper and Lower Sonoran life zones. Climate is characterized by winter and summer precipitation with spring and autumn drought. Convective summer storms produce more localized and violent flooding than frontal winter storms. Vegetational formations range from Southwestern Desertscrub to subalpine forests, with most sites in [[Sonoran Desert]] and transitional zones to [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. Deciduous Riparian Woodlands dominated by cottonwood ([[Populus fremonti]]), willows ([[Salix exigua]], [[Salix goodingi]]), and sycamore ([[Platanus wrighti]]) characterize intermittent and perennial streams at lower elevations.

### Stream Hydrology

Streamflow in arid and semi-arid regions is governed by seasonal fluctuations in precipitation-evapotranspiration balance, rainfall intensity and timing, and channel permeability. Perennial flowing waters typically originate outside the desert. In [[Sycamore Creek]], winter discharge is largely mountain runoff responsible for 90% of annual water yield. Summer flow occurs in isolated segments where impermeable bedrock forces groundwater to surface. Many streams reach their mouths as surface flow only during floods. Channel width greatly exceeds stream width, leaving creek bottoms largely unshaded except in narrow canyons or where riparian galleries are extensively developed.

### Flooding and Nutrient Mobilization

Flooding mobilizes nutrients accumulated in desert soils. Most runoff occurs as sheet flow, particularly during violent summer storms, mobilizing ions and soil-associated organic materials from the desert floor. Fisher and Minckley (1978) reported large quantities of materials in transport and elevated dissolved solids concentrations, especially nitrogen, during a summer flood in [[Sycamore Creek]].

### Study Design

Thirty-eight streams and stream segments were sampled on 59 dates. Water was collected from sources of surface flow in isolated segments, floods, and continuously flowing sections for nutrient surveys. Nutrient decline was determined at 25 stream segments arising at distinct sources, with samples collected at the source and at least two points downstream. Uptake was calculated for 10 sites on 21 dates from concentration changes. Areal coverage of morphologically distinct algal types was estimated by transect method, with triplicate samples collected for chlorophyll a and biomass determinations.

### Analytical Methods

Water was filtered through Whatman GF/F glass microfibre filters (pore size = 0.7 μm). Nitrate was determined by reduction to nitrite followed by diazotization. Ammonium was measured using the phenolhypochlorite method. Orthophosphate was measured colorimetrically (Murphy and Riley 1962). Total dissolved phosphorus was determined after persulfate digestion. Conductivity, pH, alkalinity, chloride, sulfate, and silica were measured by standard methods. Chlorophyll a was extracted in methanol and measured spectrophotometrically. Biomass was calculated as ash-free dry mass.

### Production Modeling

Hourly net production was estimated using the Busch and Fisher (1980) model. Gross production (Pg) relates to chlorophyll a (C), light (L), and temperature (T): Pg = 6.41C^0.60 L^0.13 T^1.02. Respiration (R) relates to biomass (B) and temperature: R = 5.74B^0.76 T^1.17. Net production equals gross production minus respiration. A photosynthetic quotient (PQ) of 1.3 was used, appropriate when nitrate is the primary nitrogen source.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Desert Stream Uniqueness

Streams in the American Southwest differ substantially from those in other regions. While most streams are considered heterotrophic, deriving energy from allochthonous inputs, evidence indicates desert streams are at least seasonally autotrophic, supported by an autochthonous energy base. Primary production in most streams is thought to be light-limited with adequate nutrient supplies due to current effects. However, recent studies suggest phosphorus may limit primary production in some systems, while available data indicate nitrogen is in short supply in Arizona streams with characteristically low N:P ratios.

### Isolated Stream Segments as Study Systems

Isolated segments of intermittent streams are ideal for studying nutrient dynamics. Light limitation is unlikely because channels are much wider than streams themselves. Desert soils provide ample nitrogen supply. Sources and sinks are distinct, making inputs and outputs from streamflow easily measured. Upstream, subsurface biological impact is minimal, so source water is not in equilibrium with epigean biota, allowing assessment of biotic effects on water chemistry below sources. Although these systems may exhibit features similar to permanent springs, summer floods and high winter discharge obliterate them as units at other times, giving them biota more characteristic of unstable streams than springs.

### Chemical Characteristics

Most streams had alkalinities of 2-4 milliequivalents·liter^-1 and moderate sulfate concentrations, indicating carbonate dominance. Conductivity averaged 420 μmhos·cm^-1. All sites had fairly high silica content. Nitrate was the major form of inorganic nitrogen (90%), ranging from 0.07 to 105.00 μg-atoms·liter^-1 (mean = 23.22, n = 49). Ammonium ranged from 0 to 3.64 μg-atoms·liter^-1 (mean = 0.80, n = 40). Nitrate was significantly higher in segment sources and floodwaters than in other sites (p < 0.001).

Orthophosphate ranged from 0.10 to 6.85 μg-atoms·liter^-1 (mean = 2.00, n = 49), constituting 71% of total phosphorus (mean = 2.86 μg-atoms·liter^-1, range = 0.26 to 7.46, n = 38). Orthophosphate did not differ significantly between continuous streams and floodwaters + sources. Although some sites with N:P < 14 were segment sources or floodwaters, none of the continuous survey sites had N:P > 14.

### Nitrogen to Phosphorus Ratios

The Redfield ratio concept suggests that the nutrient in shortest supply relative to optimum elemental composition limits growth. Nitrogen is limiting where N:P < ~15, and phosphorus is limiting where N:P > 15. A N:P of 14:1 is reasonable for periphyton assemblages dominated by [[Cladophora]], common in Southwestern streams. Desert stream water is characterized by highly variable nitrogen and more constant phosphorus concentrations. N:P < 14 in all continuously-flowing streams suggests primary production is nitrogen-limited. Nitrogen concentrations were comparable to those reported for other nitrogen-limited systems.

## Key Findings

### Nutrient Limitation Patterns

1. **Nitrogen Limitation in Continuous Streams**: All continuously flowing desert and semi-desert streams exhibited N:P < 14, indicating nitrogen limitation of primary production. This contrasts with most previously studied lotic ecosystems where phosphorus is typically limiting.

2. **Terrestrial Nitrogen Enrichment**: Sources of isolated stream segments and floodwaters had N:P > 14 and elevated nitrogen concentrations, reflecting recent contact with terrestrial soils. The probable origin of abundant nitrogen is desert soils, where nutrients accumulate due to lack of water for transport.

3. **Downstream Equilibration**: During passage through stream segments, N:P dropped below 14 due to more rapid removal of nitrate than orthophosphate. In 23 of 25 isolated segments, source nitrate was > 3.5 μg-atoms·liter^-1 and declined downstream. Total nitrogen exhibited similar declines, reflecting the high proportion of nitrate.

### Periphyton Role in Nitrogen Uptake

4. **Correlation with Periphyton Abundance**: Nitrate uptake (range: -0.1 to 2.7 μg-atoms·m^-2·h^-1) was significantly correlated with periphyton abundance measured as both chlorophyll a (r = 0.733, p < 0.01, n = 20) and biomass (r = 0.760, p < 0.01, n = 20).

5. **Concentration Dependence**: Nitrate uptake was significantly correlated with concentration (r = 0.821, p < 0.01, n = 18), following the relationship: uptake = 0.12 × concentration + 0.15.

6. **Post-Flood Recovery**: Following a scouring flood at [[Sycamore Creek]] (site 2), nitrate uptake increased with periphyton recolonization. On post-flood day 1, chlorophyll a was 2 mg·m^-2 and uptake was 0.03 μg-atom·m^-2·h^-1. By day 44, chlorophyll a reached 98 mg·m^-2 and uptake increased to 0.6 μg-atom·m^-2·h^-1. Nitrate declined to 20% of source concentration at 500 meters on day 44 versus no decline on day 1.

7. **Predictability from Production**: Observed nitrate uptake was significantly correlated with uptake predicted from net production (r = 0.827, p < 0.01, n = 12). Seven of twelve observed uptake rates were within or close to the range predicted using O:C:N:P ratios of 138:106:16:1 (Redfield ratio) or 260:200:14:1 (periphyton-specific ratio).

### Phosphorus Dynamics Complexity

8. **No Correlation with Periphyton**: Orthophosphate uptake (range: -0.14 to 0.58 μg-atom·m^-2·h^-1) was not correlated with concentration (r = 0.324, p > 0.1), chlorophyll a (r = 0.299, p > 0.1), or biomass (r = 0.097, p > 0.01).

9. **Unpredictable from Production**: Expected and observed orthophosphate uptake were not correlated (r = 0.517, p > 0.1, n = 12). Predicted uptake was accurate for only 4 of 12 sites, too high at 3 sites, and too low at 5 sites.

10. **Multiple Controlling Factors**: Phosphorus decline was less pronounced than nitrogen decline. Orthophosphate declined with distance from source at 20 sites but remained constant or increased slightly at 5 sites. Results suggest phosphorus dynamics are controlled by factors beyond periphyton uptake, likely including sediment sorption equilibrium.

### Diel Patterns

11. **Continuous Uptake**: At site 5, nutrient uptake occurred continuously through day and night, not following the diel pattern predicted from net production. This contrasts with expectations that intracellular nutrient reduction depends on photosynthetically derived substrates.

12. **24-Hour Uptake Rates**: Daily integrated uptake at site 5 ranged from 8.6 to 22.3 mg-atoms·m^-2·d^-1 for nitrate and 1.13 to 2.26 mg-atoms·m^-2·d^-1 for orthophosphate across three sampling dates (28 June, 15 July, 5 August 1979).

### Alternative Nutrient Pathways

13. **Sediment Interactions**: Sediment sorption equilibrium appears important for phosphorus. The equilibrium concentration of sediment from site 2 was determined to be as high as 6.5 μg-atoms·liter^-1. Uptake N:P ratios deviated substantially from expected values (14:1), with 5 of 12 sites showing uptake N:P < 14 where observed phosphorus uptake exceeded predictions, suggesting additional phosphorus sinks.

14. **Denitrification Unlikely**: Denitrification rates are strongly dependent on nitrate concentration. Since nitrate concentrations in [[Sycamore Creek]] and most Southwestern streams were nearly 100 times lower than in streams where denitrification was documented (e.g., Swift's Brook, Tamagawa River), denitrification is probably not a significant nitrogen sink except possibly at locally high nitrate concentrations.

15. **Riparian Uptake Assessment**: Riparian uptake may be locally significant but is not of widespread importance. At site 2, summer floods removed small riparian plants, which did not regrow appreciably by post-flood day 44, yet nitrogen decline was much more pronounced than on day 1, suggesting riparian uptake was not the primary mechanism.

### Management Implications

16. **Nitrogen as Limiting Factor**: Southwestern streams are unique among lotic ecosystems investigated to date in their probable nitrogen limitation. If nitrogen controls primary production, management must address responses of stream ecosystems to nitrogen additions.

17. **Ecosystem Recovery**: The post-flood recovery study demonstrates rapid reestablishment of nutrient uptake capacity as periphyton recolonizes, with substantial uptake capacity restored within 21-44 days following complete scouring.

18. **Comparative Uptake Rates**: Nitrogen loss rates in [[Sycamore Creek]] (sites 2 and 5) were comparable to rates in Swift's Brook, Ontario (30.4 mg-atoms·m^-2·d^-1) and summer rates in Tamagawa River, Japan (5.3-16.4 mg-atoms·m^-2·d^-1). Higher uptake rates at sites 4 and 26 (48.0-65.0 mg-atoms·m^-2·d^-1) were comparable to English rivers (Great Ouse: 53.5; River Trent: 100.0 mg-atoms·m^-2·d^-1).
