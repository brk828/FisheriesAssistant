# Effects of Hydrilla and Grass Carp on Water Quality in a Florida Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 *Water Resources Bulletin* paper by Canfield, Maceina, and Shireman reports a whole-lake study of [[Lake Baldwin]], a eutrophic sinkhole lake in Orlando, Florida. It tracks water chemistry, water clarity, and planktonic [[Chlorophyll A]] as the exotic submersed plant [[Hydrilla]] expanded and was then eliminated by stocked [[Grass Carp]]. Hydrilla abundance was inversely related to most water-quality variables: as coverage rose, alkalinity, calcium, magnesium, potassium, and [[Total Phosphorus]] fell and [[Secchi Disc Transparency]] improved. After grass carp removed the plant, these trends reversed. The authors conclude that macrophyte effects depend on coverage, standing crop, and the percentage of lake volume infested, and that removing low-density infestations is unlikely to change lake limnology much.

## Background & Description

Excessive growth of [[Aquatic Macrophytes]], including the exotic [[Hydrilla]], had interfered with domestic, agricultural, industrial, and recreational uses of many lakes, increasing demand for weed control. The authors note that macrophytes are also an important part of lake ecosystems, so management should account for their limnological role. Few whole-lake experiments existed at the time; most prior work came from laboratories, ponds, or enclosures.

**Site description (Lake Baldwin):**
- Eutrophic karst lake, 80 ha, on the [[Orlando Ridge]] (28°34′ N, 80°19′ W).
- Maximum depth 7.7 m, mean depth 4.4 m, volume 3.5 × 10⁶ m³, shoreline development index 1.1.
- Roughly one-third of the shoreline is wooded (oaks, myrtles, water tupelo); the rest is developed for beaches and recreation.
- No point-source nutrient discharges.
- Bottom sediments are mainly sand (72% coverage), with organic layers in deeper areas.
- Before hydrilla dominance (after 1971), filamentous algae, tapegrass, musk-grass, stonewort, and southern naiad occupied shallow areas.

**Control history:**
- 1971–1975: [[Herbicide Treatments]] with the pelletized endothall formulation HYDOUT. Applications were 40,000 kg (1972), 21,000 kg (1973), 26,000 kg (1974), and 14,000 kg (1975). Control was only temporary.
- April 1975: 4,999 fingerling grass carp (125–150 mm total length) were stocked, but 94% mortality made this stocking unsuccessful. Predation by piscivores, notably [[Largemouth Bass]], was suggested as the cause.
- Summer–fall 1978: The lake was restocked at 24 grass carp per hectare, with fish larger than 300 mm and about 0.8 kg each, to avoid predation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Monitoring and methods**
- March 1977–May 1981: Surface (0.5 m) samples from mid-lake stations were collected about every three months by the Orange County Pollution Control agency. Parameters were pH, specific conductance, calcium, magnesium, potassium, total nitrogen, total phosphorus, chlorophyll a, and Secchi depth.
- June 1981 onward: The authors collected monthly samples from three mid-lake sites.
- Data from the 1979–1980 statewide survey (Canfield, 1981) agreed with the agency data, so the datasets were treated as compatible.
- Macrophyte cover was first estimated with line transects (1977–1978). From May 1978, a recording fathometer was used to map coverage and volume infestation. Fourteen transects totaling 11 km were run monthly until June 1979, then quarterly.
- Biomass was measured from 202 vegetation cores (0.257 m² sampler) taken in August and December 1978 and March 1979. Fathometer readings were calibrated against these to estimate standing crop.

**Regional limnology:** Lake chemistry is set by nutrient loading, morphometry, flushing, and sedimentation. Regional geology and hydrology give a first approximation of trophic status, but biota such as macrophytes can modify it. Macrophytes can precipitate chemicals, suppress phytoplankton, and act as nutrient sources or sinks.

**Comparable Florida lakes:** Similar hydrilla-chemistry linkages were reported for Little Lake Barton (Scott and Osborne, 1981). Other Florida lakes showed higher chlorophyll after grass carp eliminated submersed plants (Osborne et al., 1983; Shireman et al., 1983).

## Key Findings

**1. Hydrilla expansion and decline**
- Macrophyte coverage rose from 15% (early 1977) to about 80% (November 1978), driven by hydrilla.
- Dense surface mats covered 23% of the lake by July 1978, and hydrilla grew below 6 m depth.
- Peak coverage was in November 1978, but peak standing crop, 2 × 10⁶ kg wet weight, was in August 1978.
- After the second grass carp stocking, coverage fell by only 10% from November 1978 to June 1979. Standing crop fell 47% and volume infestation fell 51% over the same period.
- Hydrilla was eliminated by June 1980. Since early 1980, only small patches (<3% cover) of cattail, panicum, and water hyacinth have persisted.

**2. Water chemistry**
- No major trends in pH, conductivity, or total nitrogen were associated with macrophyte changes.
- While hydrilla increased, total alkalinity, calcium, magnesium, potassium, and total phosphorus declined.
- After hydrilla was removed, these parameters rose sharply.
- Mean 1978 total phosphorus was 0.11 mg/L (lake-wide average during peak hydrilla, per Figure 2).

**3. Water clarity and chlorophyll**
- Secchi transparency reached about 6 m during peak hydrilla. Major transparency increases occurred only when coverage exceeded roughly 60%.
- After hydrilla loss, Secchi depth fell to below 2.5 m, with most readings below 1.5 m.
- Chlorophyll a was estimated below 3 mg/m³ during peak hydrilla (regression-predicted from Secchi depth).
- By late 1979, chlorophyll a averaged 21 mg/m³, peaked at 35 mg/m³, and had a minimum of 9.6 mg/m³.

**4. Thresholds**
- Visible limnological change did not occur until hydrilla coverage exceeded about 40% and volume infestation exceeded about 10%.
- Complaints about weeds began at only 15% coverage, a level with little measurable effect on water quality.
- The authors therefore suggest that managing hydrilla at low infestation, including complete removal, will have little effect on chlorophyll, clarity, or overall chemistry.

**5. Phosphorus budget and grass carp role**
- Peak hydrilla contained an estimated 140 kg of phosphorus. This used wet-to-dry and P-content values from nearby Lake Fairview.
- After hydrilla removal, the lake supported about 2,400 kg dry weight of grass carp, containing about 72 kg of phosphorus.
- Subtracting this gave 68 kg of phosphorus released.
- If all of that were returned to the water, the potential concentration would be 0.31 mg/L, close to the observed mean of 0.30 mg/L. Without retention by fish, it would be 0.52 mg/L.
- The authors conclude that grass carp may have held phosphorus below the geologic potential, but that long-term benefit requires harvesting the fish, since dead fish recycle nutrients.
- Causality between grass carp and chemistry cannot be fully separated from the effects of hydrilla loss.

**6. Broader implications**
- Macrophyte effects on lake chemistry scale with standing crop and volume infestation. Lakes with low infestation should see little change after reduction. Lakes with heavy infestation may show marked shifts in trophic classification.
- Removing macrophytes did not degrade water quality beyond the potential set by regional geology and hydrology.
- Changes in fish biomass and species composition following long-term macrophyte loss remain unknown and are a priority for future research.
