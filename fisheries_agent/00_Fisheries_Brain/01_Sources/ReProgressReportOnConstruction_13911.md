# Progress Report on Fish Monitoring in Gila River Basin Under Central Arizona Project Biological Opinion (1995-1996)

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #conservation-biology #non-native-species #monitoring

## Executive Summary

This document comprises three related items from the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation regarding fish monitoring and barrier construction in the [[Gila River Basin]] under the 1994 [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion on the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP). The primary component is a comprehensive fish monitoring report covering 1995-1996 sampling of target streams and canals to detect non-native fish introductions and distribution changes. Monitoring targeted the CAP canal system, [[Salt River Project]] canals, [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]], and reaches of the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[San Pedro River]]. The report documents species richness, distribution patterns, and assemblage structure across these systems, revealing ongoing invasions by non-native species and variable native fish populations. Administrative memoranda address progress on constructing fish barriers on [[Aravaipa Creek]] to prevent non-native fish intrusions, with completion targeted for December 31, 1999.

## Background & Description

The Central Arizona Project transports Colorado River water via aqueducts and canals to central and southern Arizona. A 1994 FWS Biological Opinion determined that CAP operations would jeopardize four threatened or endangered fishes: [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The primary concern was potential transfer of non-indigenous aquatic organisms from the Lower Colorado River to Gila River Basin drainages via the CAP canal system.

Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives (RPAs) in the Biological Opinion required Reclamation to: (1) construct fish barriers on [[Aravaipa Creek]] and [[San Pedro River]]; (2) develop and implement baseline studies and long-term monitoring of non-native fish presence and distribution; (3) provide funding for native fish recovery; (4) manage non-native fishes; and (5) support information and education programs.

The monitoring plan established target reaches including the CAP aqueduct, SRP canals, Florence-Casa Grande Canal, Salt River between Stewart Mountain Dam and Granite Reef Dam, Gila River between Coolidge and Ashurst-Hayden dams, and San Pedro River downstream from the U.S.-Mexico border. Sampling employed electrofishing, gill nets, trammel nets, seines, and other gears depending on habitat characteristics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Gila River Basin represents critical habitat for multiple threatened and endangered native fishes. The region's aquatic ecosystems face ongoing threats from non-native species introductions, habitat modification, and altered flow regimes. The CAP canal system creates a potential vector for species transfers from the Colorado River system into previously isolated Gila River Basin tributaries.

Native fish assemblages in monitored reaches included [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*). Non-native species documented included [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), various sunfish species (*Lepomis* spp.), [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*), [[White Bass]] (*Morone chrysops*), and [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idellus*).

Habitat characteristics varied considerably across target reaches. The CAP canal features deep, swift-flowing concrete channels with pumping plant forebays. Natural stream reaches ranged from shallow riffles and rocky pools in upper Gila River sections to sandy, braided channels in lower San Pedro River reaches. SRP and Florence-Casa Grande canals are largely concrete-lined or sand-bottomed artificial waterways subject to periodic dry-ups for maintenance.

## Key Findings

**Administrative Progress (March 1998 Memoranda):**
- Reclamation acquired signatures representing 59% of living interest in Indian Trust Allotment No. 013736 for right-of-entry to conduct barrier preconstruction investigations on [[Aravaipa Creek]]
- Right-of-entry requested from Bureau of Indian Affairs on March 3, 1998
- Construction completion targeted for December 31, 1999
- Fish monitoring activities for 1995-1996 completed as required under RPA No. 2
- $500,000 in funding developed for native fish recovery (RPA No. 3) and non-native fish management (RPA No. 4)
- Arizona Game and Fish Department committed to developing 5-year information and education program (RPA No. 5)

**Species Richness and New Records:**
- CAP Canal: 13 species total in lower reach (1995); new records included [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*), [[White Bass]], and [[Grass Carp]]
- Gila River: 10 species in 1995, 8 species at single station in 1996; [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) collected in 1996 but not 1995
- San Pedro River: 9 species in 1995, 11 species in 1996; [[Sonora Sucker]] found downstream from Aravaipa Creek confluence, thought previously absent from mainstem
- Florence-Casa Grande Canal: 5 species in 1995, 12 species in 1996; new records included [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Threadfin Shad]]
- Salt River: 13 species in 1995, 11 in 1996; new records included [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), [[Mosquitofish]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*)
- SRP Canals: 19 species total from 1990-1996 samples; considerably more species than upstream Salt River source

**Distribution Patterns:**
- [[Red Shiner]] invasion pattern in lower San Pedro River correlated with absence of major flood events; species absent during years with floods exceeding 3,000 cfs
- Red shiner appeared in Aravaipa Creek in 1990 and again in 1997, both times following multi-year periods without significant floods
- USGS discharge records showed 1986-1991 period without floods exceeding ~1,500 cfs, followed by 3,000+ cfs flood in 1991 that apparently eliminated red shiner
- 11,000+ cfs flood in 1993 coincided with red shiner absence in 1993 collections
- Red shiner reappeared in lower San Pedro River in 1996 after another extended period without major floods

**Assemblage Structure:**
- Gila River: [[Red Shiner]] numerically dominant across study reach in 1995-1996, but native suckers and [[Longfin Dace]] comprised majority of biomass
- San Pedro River: [[Longfin Dace]] overwhelmingly dominant numerically, with phenomenal densities in lower stations; [[Mosquitofish]] second most abundant in 1995, [[Desert Sucker]] in 1996
- Salt River: Native suckers (especially [[Desert Sucker]]) most abundant overall; [[Largemouth Bass]] second most numerous; age-0 desert sucker captured only in riffles below Stewart Mountain Dam at impressively high density
- CAP Canal: Upper reaches dominated by [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Striped Bass]]; lower reaches dominated by *Lepomis* species and [[Largemouth Bass]]; [[Threadfin Shad]] numerically dominant at Bouse Hills in 1995 but virtually absent from lower canal
- Florence-Casa Grande Canal: Native suckers numerically dominant in 1995; [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]], and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) dominated in 1996
- SRP Canals: Considerable inter-annual instability in assemblage structure; dominance shifted among native suckers, [[Common Carp]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), and [[Tilapia]] species across years

**Electrical Barrier Performance:**
- [[Grass Carp]] detected above South Canal electrical barrier in 1995 and above Arizona Canal barrier in 1996
- South Canal barrier experienced power failure on December 23, 1993 (backup generator failed) and lightning damage to pulsers on September 2-3, 1994
- Grass carp likely transgressed barriers during these failure events
- Fishes observed transgressing barriers during shallow water conditions (~8 cm or less depth) during dry-up operations
- Thousands of [[Tilapia]] observed descending over South Canal weir immediately following electrical cutoff in 1997 due to incomplete headgate closure
- Small [[Desert Sucker]] observed transgressing South Canal barrier in upstream direction

**Sampling Challenges and Protocol Deviations:**
- Incomplete standardization of methods across stations in early monitoring years introduced bias for assemblage structure comparisons
- Quality control/quality assurance procedures not fully implemented; data entered and verified but post-processing verification steps incomplete
- Management action plan to direct responses to field contingencies not yet developed
- Right-of-entry issues prevented sampling of some stations in 1995
- Equipment failures (shocker pulsator breakdown, high conductivity damage) limited sampling effectiveness
- Scheduling difficulties with canal dry-up periods resulted in missed sampling windows

**Critical Deficiency Identified:**
The report concludes that the most serious deficiency is failure to develop a management action plan defining threshold criteria for species richness, distribution, and assemblage structure indices, and establishing management action contingencies. Without such a plan, the monitoring program functions only as surveillance rather than as an early warning system enabling corrective action to protect native fish populations.
