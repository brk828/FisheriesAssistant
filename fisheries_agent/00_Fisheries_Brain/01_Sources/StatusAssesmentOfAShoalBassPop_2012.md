# Status Assessment of a Shoal Bass Population in the Lower Flint River, Georgia

#ichthyology #Centrarchidae #Micropterus #black-bass #endemic-species

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Shoal Bass]] (*Micropterus cataractae*) is a black bass endemic to the [[Apalachicola-Chattahoochee-Flint Basin]] of Alabama, Florida, and Georgia, formally described in 1999 by Williams and Burgess. The species belongs to family [[Centrarchidae]], order Perciformes. Phylogenetically, shoal bass are closely related to [[Suwannee Bass]] (*M. notius*) and [[Redeye Bass]] (*M. coosae*), with the lineage originating ~17 million years ago in the Early Miocene. Sexual maturity occurs at age three; smallest mature female was 189 mm standard length (SL), 178 g. Fecundity ranges 5,396–21,779 eggs for females 314–442 mm SL (844–2,314 g). Maximum age is 8–10 years; world record 3.99 kg. Growth is fastest during first three years, slowest during years 5–7. Spawning occurs April to early June at water temperatures ~20°C, triggered by proper temperature and relatively high, murky flows. Multiple spawning bouts within a season are hypothesized. Nests are rarely observed; documented at 20–46 cm depth in coarse sand or rocky substrate near shoals. Dense spawning aggregations occupy certain shoal complexes while similar shoals may be unoccupied. Life stages: larval (0–50 mm TL), juvenile (51–150 mm TL), adult (>150 mm TL). Scales and sagittal otoliths used for aging; scales may underestimate age in older fish due to regeneration. False annuli occur but mechanisms unclear.

**Habitat Associations**: Shoal bass are habitat specialists occupying riverine shoal areas characterized by swift currents, large rock outcrops, bedrock, and boulder substrates. In [[Little Uchee Creek]], Alabama, ontogenetic habitat shift documented: larval fish prefer boulder substrates in deep water with low current velocities, rapidly transitioning to shallow shoal habitats as juveniles. Adults prefer deeper habitats near fast current with boulder and bedrock substrates adjacent. Species almost always associated with bedrock and boulder substrates. Seasonal variation: run and eddy macrohabitats preferred during winter and spring; deep pools preferred during summer and early fall. During droughts and low-water periods, adults depend on deep pool habitats as critical refugia when shoals become dewatered. Habitat connectivity especially important during prolonged droughts. In [[Chipola River]], 46% of age-0 and 76% of adults collected from pool habitats in summer/fall. In lower [[Flint River]], adults regularly sampled in deep pools over coarse limestone substrates. Species more flexible than previously thought, especially in southerly range portions where drought conditions more common. Adults often seek cover near large woody debris with various substrate types. Shoal bass do not typically inhabit or persist in impounded waters; extirpated from extensive reservoir habitats. Maintaining interconnected shoal and pool habitats imperative for long-term conservation.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Movement and home range highly variable among individuals, unrelated to fish size. Diel movement rates similar between daylight and darkness, directly related to water temperature. Daily movement related to habitat connectivity: averaged 403 m/d in unimpounded upper Flint River but only 20 m/d in short Chattahoochee River reach between dam and reservoir. Movement rates typically highest during spring months associated with spawning season. Spawning-related migrations documented to large shoal areas and into tributaries, presumably forming dense spawning aggregations. Great numbers of adults found immediately downstream of dams in early spring, possibly due to increased foraging opportunities or attempts to migrate upstream to spawn. In translocation study, 10 of 12 fish returned from [[Lake Worth]] to river after average 21 d (SD = 8) in reservoir. Mean daily movement rates peaked at 955 m/d (range 16–1,784 m/d) during river re-entry phase. During spawning season (late March to early May), movement rates decreased to ~150 m/d; majority of telemetered fish occupied [[Philema Shoals]], a 2.5-rkm spawning aggregation area. Post-spawn, as water levels dropped >1.5 m, movement rates averaged ~25 m/d; fish moved downstream into deeper pools. Apparent survival within spawning aggregation estimated at 0.15 from April 6–14 (suggesting fish moved in/out during early spawn), increasing to 0.71 from April 14–May 5 (fish less likely to leave as spawn progressed). Behavioral data suggest adults move in and out of spawning areas in large groups during early spawning season.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Shoal bass prey primarily on insects, fishes, and crayfishes. Insects, especially mayflies (Ephemeroptera), important to smaller fish (40–120 mm SL). In third year, diet transitions from insects to crayfishes and fishes. Crayfish most important diet component of larger shoal bass; fishes and insects second and third most important, respectively. Prey fishes include members of Cypriniformes, Perciformes, Siluriformes. Shoal bass observed foraging alone and in loose assemblages of 3–5 like-sized individuals in limestone shoals. Forage primarily in shoals based on observations and anecdotal angler evidence. Sympatric with native [[Largemouth Bass]] (*M. salmoides*) and [[Redeye Bass]] in ACF Basin. Non-native [[Spotted Bass]] (*M. punctulatus*), [[Alabama Bass]] (*M. henshalli*), and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*M. dolomieu*) introduced and established in regions of ACF Basin. Relative increases in non-native black basses and subsequent declines in shoal bass populations suggest non-natives may outcompete shoal bass, perhaps better adapted to altered habitats. However, initial investigations have not provided conclusive evidence or identified mechanisms of potential interspecific competition. Lab studies suggest life history differences between shoal bass and spotted bass may result in resource partitioning allowing coexistence.

**Environmental Drivers**: Spawning triggered by water temperature (~20°C) and relatively high, murky flows. Flow alteration especially severe below hydroelectric dams operated as hydropeaking facilities. Operation of [[Crisp County Dam]] as hydropeaking facility influences flow throughout 50-rkm study area, raising concerns that alterations of natural flow regimes affect spawning behavior and reproductive success. During extended droughts and low-water periods, shoal habitats become dewatered, forcing fish into deep pool refugia. Habitat connectivity critical during prolonged droughts. Seasonal water temperature directly related to diel movement rates. Post-spawn movements coincided with water level recession >1 m and declining stream flow; fish moved into deeper pools. Limnetic coldwater releases extirpated shoal bass from ~77 rkm of Chattahoochee River below Buford Dam. Gradual warming of waters caused by aging of upstream reservoir contributed to restoration success below [[Morgan Falls Dam]].

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Shoal bass in continual decline throughout range, probably due to negative effects of dams, non-native black basses, and angling mortality. Extirpated from several areas of native range due to damming. Historically occurred throughout much of ACF Basin where shoal habitat existed. In Alabama, currently limited to four Chattahoochee River tributaries with low abundances except small population at Moffit's Mill. In Florida, inhabit mainstem Apalachicola River from Jim Woodruff Dam to ~10 rkm downstream; also ~50 rkm of Chipola River mainstem. In Georgia, small populations scattered throughout Chattahoochee River from northern headwaters to Walter F. George Reservoir; extirpated from much of Chattahoochee mainstem. Most robust populations currently in mainstem Flint River except impounded waters of Lakes Blackshear and Worth. Introduced into Ocmulgee River (Altamaha Basin) in mid-1970s; now established from Lake Jackson Dam downstream to confluence with Oconee River. Anecdotal reports of illegal introductions above Lake Jackson into Yellow, Alcovy, and South rivers. In lower Flint River study population (50-rkm reach between Lakes Blackshear and Worth), spawning aggregation at Philema Shoals estimated to contain 87 adults (95% CI 47–188) on April 5–6, 181 adults (95% CI 101–374) on April 13–14, and 136 adults (95% CI 75–285) on May 4–5, 2011. Capture probability during sampling periods varied 0.11 (95% CI 0.05–0.22) to 0.15 (95% CI 0.07–0.29). Supplemental stocking by GADNR from 2000–2007 contributed 14–51% of year classes in lower Flint River population.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Major threats include: (1) **Habitat modification**: ACF Basin is second-most impounded watershed east of Mississippi River with >1,400 individual impoundments. Dams fragment and destroy shoal bass habitats by inundating shallow high-gradient shoal habitats. Dams alter downstream habitats by modifying natural flow and temperature regimes. Populations isolated in small tributaries vulnerable to extirpation from inbreeding depression. Other habitat threats include sedimentation, channelization, nutrient pollution, diversion of groundwater for municipal and agricultural purposes. (2) **Introgressive hybridization**: In lower Flint River study area, 18 of 152 (11.8%) fish identified as shoal bass were actually hybrids with non-native black basses. Seventeen were spotted bass × shoal bass hybrids backcrossed toward shoal bass with spotted bass genomic proportions 1.8–18.5%. One hybrid had shoal bass, Alabama bass, and spotted bass alleles. Low percentage of non-native alleles indicates spotted bass and Alabama bass probably not yet established within study reach; hybridization probably occurring from hybrids passing downstream of Crisp County Dam. Area traditionally used by GADNR to obtain genetically-pure brood fish now compromised. Hybridization rates ~20% documented in lower Flint River below Flint River Dam. In [[Lake Keowee]] (Savannah River), endemic redeye bass population nearly extirpated following Alabama bass introduction. (3) **Angling mortality**: Angling pressure high even in remote portions of lower Flint River. Dense spawning aggregations make populations vulnerable to overharvest, particularly during spring. Tournament translocation from riverine to reservoir habitats common practice. In translocation study, 2 of 12 telemetered fish died after tournament translocation in June 2011. Summer tournament observations: 2 of 15 shoal bass (13%) dead before weigh-in from hooking injuries; 12-hr post-tournament mortality estimated at 38%; at least 33% of tournament-caught shoal bass died. Effects of translocation may vary seasonally, with mortality reaching ~33% during summer months. Population-level effects remain unclear. Extended handling time and translocation of tournament-caught fish suspected to explain relatively high mortality rates.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: (1) Conduct range-wide population assessments to identify potential threats and develop population-specific management strategies. Similar status assessments needed throughout ACF Basin. (2) Implement long-term monitoring of shoal bass abundance and genetic integrity within adaptive management framework. Monitor spawning aggregation abundance and hybridization rates to assess effects of potential threats while deriving optimal management strategies. (3) Preserve and enhance shoal habitats and in-stream connectivity as priorities. Maintain interconnected shoal and pool habitats within native range imperative for long-term conservation. (4) Identify and maintain pure shoal bass brood stock for supplemental stocking and restoration efforts. Genetic screening necessary given 12% hybridization rate in area previously considered "pure." (5) Investigate population-level effects of angling mortality and tournament translocation. Future studies needed on post-release mortality and sub-lethal effects of translocated shoal bass. (6) Educate public about negative effects of introducing non-native species. Encourage anglers to harvest non-native black basses in ACF Basin streams and rivers, though effectiveness limited where non-natives considered sport fish (e.g., Lake Lanier). (7) Account for tag loss in mark-recapture models. Internal anchor tags (Floy FM-95W laminated) showed loss within 21 d post-tagging; directed studies needed to estimate retention rates over time for both PIT and internal anchor tags. (8) Consider seasonal restrictions on tournament translocation or handling time limits to reduce summer mortality. (9) Protect critical spawning habitats like large shoal complexes (e.g., Philema Shoals, Abrams Shoals) from hydropeaking operations and other flow alterations.

**Biological Reference Points**: Sexual maturity: age 3, minimum 189 mm SL (178 g) for females. Harvestable adult size: ≥305 mm TL used in study. Fecundity: 5,396–21,779 eggs for females 314–442 mm SL (844–2,314 g). Maximum age: 8–10 years. Maximum size: 3.99 kg (world record). Spawning temperature: ~20°C. Spawning season: April to early June. Spawning aggregation abundance (Philema Shoals, 2011): 87–181 adults (peak). Apparent survival within spawning aggregation: 0.15 (early season), 0.71 (late season). Hybridization rate: 11.8% in study population (lower Flint River between lakes), ~20% below Flint River Dam. Tournament mortality: ≥33% during summer months. Translocation return time: average 21 d (SD = 8) from reservoir to river. Movement rates: 955 m/d (river re-entry), 150 m/d (spawning), 25 m/d (post-spawn low water). Current harvest regulations (Georgia): up to 10 shoal bass per day in most of state (intended for all black bass species). Alabama: harvest banned in Chattahoochee River tributaries since 2006. Conservation status: Species of Special Concern (Alabama, Florida); Vulnerable (AFS); declining throughout range; never petitioned for federal ESA listing.
