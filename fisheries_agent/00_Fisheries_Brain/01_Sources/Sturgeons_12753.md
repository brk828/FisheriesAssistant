# Sturgeons (Acipenseridae): Biology, Fishery, and Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #acipenseridae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Commercial Fisheries leaflet (Fishery Leaflet 526, September 1961, by Lola T. Dees) is a general overview of the [[Sturgeon]] family, Acipenseridae. It covers morphology, life history, feeding, reproduction, behavior, the causes of population decline, legal protection, fishing gear, commercial products ([[Caviar]] and [[Isinglass]]), attempts at culture, and brief species accounts for North American and Eurasian sturgeons. The leaflet's central message is that sturgeons are commercially valuable but slow-growing and late-maturing, so they are vulnerable to overfishing and habitat loss, and they need state-level regulation to recover.

## Background & Description

Sturgeons are armored fish with rows of bony shields, a long snout, four barbels in front of a protrusible, toothless mouth, and an asymmetrical tail whose upper lobe is much longer than the lower one. The document reports that one species may reach 16 feet and more than a ton.

- **Taxonomy:** The 20 or more modern species and subspecies belong to the order Acipenseriformes and the family Acipenseridae, mostly genus *Acipenser*. Seven species occur in North America. Four North American species are [[Anadromous Fish]] (spawn in fresh water, spend much of life at sea). The six commercially important Asiatic and European species, except the viza, are also anadromous.
- **Body plan:** Bony plates cover the head. Each side has one gill opening enclosed by a gill cover. A large, well-developed [[Swim Bladder]] regulates buoyancy and allows the fish to hold its depth. The skeleton is largely cartilaginous.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Sex cannot be determined externally except near spawning, when females' bellies swell with ripe eggs.
- **Coloration:** Olive green, grayish, or brownish purple above, white below the side shields. Young may have small dark spots.
- **Relatives:** Two freshwater relatives in the family Polyodontidae, the [[Paddlefish]] (*Polyodon spathula*, to about 4–5 feet) and the swordbill (*Psephurus gladius*, China), differ by having naked bodies, very small eyes, long snouts, and large mouths.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Feeding.** Adults eat worms, insect larvae, crustaceans, mollusks, and fishes. They root along the bottom with sensitive barbels, protrude the mouth, and suck up prey. The leaflet notes opportunistic feeding: a white sturgeon in the Snake River ate half a bushel of onions, and lake sturgeon near grain elevators eat corn and wheat. Young sturgeons feed on microscopic organisms because of their small mouths. Columbia River white sturgeons have consumed eulachon, salmon, suckers, squawfish, and [[Sea Lamprey]]-type prey (lampreys are described as a prime food).

**Lifespan and growth.** Growth is slow and lifespan is long, with some individuals over 100 years old. Age was estimated from recaptures of tagged fish and from annual growth rings on pectoral fin rays. Lake sturgeon up to 152 years old have been recorded.

**Spawning habitat.** Spawning occurs in fresh water in spring or early summer, when water temperature reaches 55° to 70°F. Fish migrate upstream beyond tidal influence over gravel, rubble, or stone bottoms in running water. Lake sturgeon often spawn at the foot of falls in 2 to 15 feet of water.

**Behavior.** Sturgeons cruise slowly on sandy or muddy bottoms. They leap from the water, sometimes spectacularly, during spawning, when hooked, and when crossing shallow bars or riffles. Atlantic and lake sturgeons leap 5 to 7 feet when attacked by parasitic sea lamprey. Sturgeons are curious and were historically attracted to decoys through spearing holes in ice.

**Decline.** The leaflet attributes the decline of once-plentiful U.S. populations to:
- Deliberate eradication by commercial fishermen, since sturgeon tore nets.
- [[Overfishing]], intensified when transport and a market for caviar and smoked sturgeon developed; specialized gear took immature fish needed for replenishment.
- Pollution.
- Dredging and damming of spawning rivers.

**Protection.** Some states have begun restricting gear, seasons, and size or number of fish taken. Examples cited:
- California: any time of year, one per day, fish under 50 inches released.
- Washington: fish under 30 inches and over 72 inches protected. The upper limit protects fish near maturity size, since a female of that length produces far more eggs.
- Wisconsin: spear limit of one fish per season, minimum size 40 inches.
- South Carolina: open season March 1 to October 1 for catching and shipping sturgeon and caviar.

**Capture.** In the United States, sturgeon are taken commercially with large-mesh drift gill nets, setlines or trotlines (notably in the [[Columbia River]] and Mississippi area), and occasionally haul seines and traps. Gill nets are most effective at night and in turbid water, often set "sunken" along the bottom. In the USSR, setlines of more than five-eighths of a mile were common in the shallow [[Caspian Sea]]; freshwater fisheries relied mainly on haul seines. Sportsmen spear and angle for sturgeon.

## Key Findings

**Commercial value.** Sturgeon meat is valued for baking, frying, smoking, and similar preparations. U.S. commercial landings from 1949 to 1958 ranged from a low of 453,000 pounds in 1951 (worth $110,000) to a high of 819,000 pounds in 1956 (worth $144,000). The average was 657,700 pounds worth $127,200.

**Caviar and isinglass.** Eggs are sieved, salted, mixed for 5 to 8 minutes, drained, and canned or jarred. Only preserved sturgeon eggs may be labeled caviar under U.S. Food and Drug Administration rules. The U.S. forbids borax-preserved food, so American caviar uses salt only, while European caviar is often salted with borax and is sweeter. Isinglass is prepared from the air bladder and is used mainly to clarify wines and beers, and in limited manufacture of court plaster, cement, and waterproofing.

**Iranian caviar.** The Iranian government took over caviar processing after 1953, when the Irano-Soviet Fisheries Company was dissolved. Iran produced 126.4 tons of caviar in 1956–57.

**Culture.** Sturgeon culture has been "almost a complete failure" in the U.S. and Europe. Problems include unsynchronized maturity of males and females, fungal attack on eggs, and lack of suitable food for fry.

**Reproduction.**
- Females first spawn at about 13 to 14 years, males at 9 to 10 years. Not all fish spawn every year.
- Eggs may make up one-third of body weight. Some females spawn 2 to 3 million eggs per season.
- Eggs hatch in 3 to 7 days. Larvae are under one-half inch long and reach 4 to 5.5 inches in a month.
- Young remain in natal rivers for 1 to 3 years before migrating to sea. By 3 feet, marine species are at sea or in estuaries.

**Key North American species.**
- [[Atlantic Sturgeon]] (*A. oxyrhynchus*): Atlantic coast from north of the St. Lawrence River to the Gulf of Mexico. Up to 12 feet and 500 pounds, though over 200 pounds is rare. Tagging showed long migrations: one fish tagged in 1945 at Kamouraska, Quebec, was recaptured eight years later in Hermitage Bay, Newfoundland, after traveling at least 700 miles. Gill nets are the main commercial gear.
- [[Shortnose Sturgeon]] (*A. brevirostrum*): Cape Cod to Florida, more abundant in the south. Usually about 1.5 feet, maximum 2.5 feet. Of slight commercial importance, mostly caught in shad gill nets.
- [[White Sturgeon]] (*A. transmontanus*): The largest freshwater fish in North America, from northern California to northwestern Alaska. Up to 20 feet and over 1,000 pounds; one specimen weighed 1,900 pounds. Females mature at 13 to 16 years and 70 to 80 pounds. In a 1954 California tagging study, 994 white and 25 green sturgeon were tagged in San Pablo Bay; by April 1959 only 62 white and 3 green tags had been returned. Pectoral ray analysis gave about 40 inches at 8 years, 50 inches at 13, and 94 inches at 30.
- [[Green Sturgeon]] (*A. medirostris*): Also northern California to northwestern Alaska, rarely in fresh water. Up to 7 feet and about 350 pounds. Considered an inferior food fish.
- [[Lake Sturgeon]] (*A. fulvescens*): Southern Canada and the north-central U.S. Up to 8 feet and 310 pounds, though most Great Lakes fish are under 6 feet and 100 pounds. Movements seldom exceed 250 miles. An important St. Lawrence River food fish, mostly caught in gill nets.
- [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus platorynchus*): Mississippi River system north to Hudson Bay drainage, excluding the Great Lakes. Seldom over 3 feet or 6 pounds, spawning in May or June.
- [[Pallid Sturgeon]] (*S. albus*): Limited to the Mississippi River and larger tributaries, locally in Illinois. Probably about 4 feet and 16 pounds.

**Key Eurasian species.**
- [[Beluga Sturgeon]] (*A. huso*): Black Sea, Sea of Azov, Caspian Sea. Maximum 16 feet and about 2,200 pounds, producing up to 350 pounds of caviar. Adults typically weigh 240–290 pounds and yield 30–45 pounds of caviar. Soviet scientists crossed beluga with sterlet to combine beluga egg size with sterlet flavor.
- Russian osetr (*A. gueldenstaedtii*): Up to 7.5 feet and 176 pounds, producing up to 26 pounds of caviar. Some fish were found bearing gold rings placed more than 100 years earlier.
- Sevruga (*A. stellatus*): Up to 7 feet, 26 to 110 pounds, yielding 2 to 11 pounds of caviar. Its caviar and isinglass command higher prices.
- Sterlet (*A. ruthenus*): Averages 2 to 3 feet and 2 to 3 pounds. Historically reserved for the Russian Imperial Court.
- Viza (*A. glaber*): Now a freshwater fish that no longer descends to the sea.
- [[European Sturgeon]] (*A. sturio*): Only species in western Europe. Up to 9 to 10 feet, occasionally 16 feet and 900 pounds. Its spawning in British rivers is doubtful, though it spawns in large continental rivers. An English decree of Edward II claimed great sturgeon as royal fish.

**Conservation takeaway.** The leaflet concludes that protection of sturgeons depends on slow-growth-aware regulation: limiting catch, size, season, and gear. Protecting larger, mature fish and sustaining immature cohorts is central to any recovery, since the species' biology makes population replacement slow.
