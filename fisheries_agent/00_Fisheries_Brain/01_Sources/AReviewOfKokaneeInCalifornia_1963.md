# A Review of Kokanee in California

## Executive Summary

This report synthesizes the introduction history, life-history variability, and stocking record of **kokanee salmon** (*Oncorhynchus nerka*), the landlocked, nonanadromous form of [[Sockeye Salmon]], in California waters through June 1963. Kokanee were first introduced to California in 1941 at [[Salt Springs Reservoir]] using Idaho-sourced eggs, and the species subsequently spread to numerous lakes and reservoirs statewide via both natural reproduction and deliberate stocking. A pivotal event was the 1951 introduction of British Columbia-strain eggs (from Meadow Creek, tributary of the Duncan River, feeding [[Kootenay Lake]]) into [[Shasta Lake]], intended as forage for [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]], which resulted in a large self-sustaining population and renewed interest in kokanee as a game/forage fish for California's growing number of large cool reservoirs. The document emphasizes that different genetic strains/races of kokanee exhibit distinct life-history traits—particularly age at maturity—and that matching appropriate strains to specific water bodies is critical for successful management.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Kokanee are obligate freshwater, lacustrine fish completing their entire life cycle in lakes and connected tributary streams — unlike the anadromous [[Sockeye Salmon]] from which they are derived.
- **Evolutionary origin**: Kokanee likely evolved from "residual" sockeye populations that matured entirely within lakes (Ricker, 1959), favored by conditions that create barriers to anadromous migration (e.g., natural obstructions, or a warm epilimnion acting as a physiological barrier to smolt outmigration).
- **Reversion to anadromy**: Under experimental conditions in British Columbia and Washington, some marked kokanee have reverted to sea-run behavior, suggesting the anadromous potential is not entirely lost in all strains. Mature "sockeye" occasionally captured in the [[Sacramento River]] may represent such reverted escapees from [[Shasta Lake]].
- **Trophic role**: In California, kokanee have been explicitly used as a **forage fish** species, notably to support introduced [[Kamloops Rainbow Trout]] populations in Shasta Lake, in addition to serving as a direct sport fishery target (e.g., [[Donner Lake]]).
- **Geographic distribution**: Native range spans lakes of Pacific Coast drainages of North America, Siberia, and Japan, notably absent from Alaska despite large sockeye runs there. The largest number of native populations occurs in [[British Columbia]]. By the time of this report, kokanee had been transplanted to Oregon, Nevada, California, Arizona, Colorado, Wyoming, and several eastern states.
- **Population sources for California stocking**: British Columbia, Idaho, Washington, Montana, and Colorado, plus numerous California donor lakes (e.g., Donner Lake, Shasta Lake, Bucks Lake, Twin Lakes, Millerton Lake, Lake Arrowhead) used to supply eggs for further transplant within the state.

## Key Findings

- **First California introduction**: 1941, 67,000 fingerlings (Idaho egg source) planted in [[Salt Springs Reservoir]], Amador County, North Fork Mokelumne River; successful, with egg-take achieved by 1943 from 3-year-old maturing fish.
- **Early expansion (1944–1945)**: Stocked into [[Donner Lake]], [[Strawberry Lake]], [[Lower Echo Lake]], [[Waterhouse Lake]], and accidentally into [[Lake Tahoe]] via hatchery escapees; [[Upper Echo Lake]] followed in 1945.
- **Sport fishery establishment**: A significant kokanee sport fishery was established in Donner Lake by 1948.
- **Shasta Lake introduction (1951)**: British Columbia strain (Meadow Creek/Duncan River/Kootenay Lake origin) established a large, self-sustaining population, intended originally as forage for Kamloops rainbow trout; this success renewed statewide interest in kokanee for large, cool reservoirs.
- **Stocking scale**: From 1951 through June 1963, 27 additional California waters were stocked with kokanee, 16 of them between 1959 and mid-1963, limited primarily by egg availability rather than demand.
- **Age at maturity variability**:
  - Life cycle ranges from **2 to 7 years** across the species' range.
  - Four-year maturity predominates in the original North American range.
  - Five-year maturity occurs in the Skeena Trench (northern British Columbia) and in Upper Priest Lake, Idaho (introduced population).
  - Three-year (and occasionally two-year, in precocious males) maturity occurs in Upper Columbia Trench populations (southeastern British Columbia).
  - [[Kootenay Lake]] contains **three distinct races**: two maturing at 3 years, one at 4 years (Vernon, 1957).
  - General trend: age at maturity decreases with decreasing latitude and elevation, a trend apparently extended in California, where maturity ranges from **2 to 5 years**.
  - The **British Columbia strain stocked in Shasta Lake (1951)** showed unprecedented early maturity — substantial numbers matured in **under two years (20 months)** — a notable departure from the prior California pattern of 3–4 year maturity.
  - Uncertain whether accelerated maturation is a permanent adaptation to higher temperature/light or a temporary response to abundant food during early population growth (density-dependent growth effect).
- **Strain-mixing complications**: Historical stocking practice often mixed different genetic strains within the same water body (due to inconsistent egg source availability), confounding interpretation of local age-at-maturity data; some northern strains that originally matured at 4 years are now maturing at 3 years in certain California waters.
- **Genetic vs. environmental basis of racial traits**: While genetic variation supports the hypothesis of independent evolution of each kokanee population from a distinct ancestral sockeye run, many observed "racial" characteristics may instead be environmentally induced rather than genetically fixed.
