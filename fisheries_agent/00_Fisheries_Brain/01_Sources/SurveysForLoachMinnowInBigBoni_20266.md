# Loach Minnow Surveys in Big Bonito and Tonto Creeks, Fort Apache Indian Reservation (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] Albuquerque office by the Arizona Fishery Resources Office, summarizes stream surveys for the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Rhinichthys cobitis*) in the lower reaches of [[Tonto Creek]] and [[Big Bonito Creek]] on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]], Arizona, conducted from July to November 1993. No loach minnow were collected. A total of 1,569 fishes were taken in Tonto Creek and 38 in Big Bonito Creek, representing five species. The absence of the species is attributed mainly to the small size and scarcity of suitable [[Riffle Habitat]]. The author states that the results remain preliminary and that continued surveys are needed to delineate the species' distribution on the Reservation.

## Background & Description

**Species of concern.** The loach minnow is a specialized, bottom-dwelling fish of cobble-bottomed riffles in the [[Gila River Drainage]] of Arizona and New Mexico. Its populations are separated by large distances and represent the upstream ends of its historic range. Remaining populations occur in Aravaipa Creek, the Blue and White rivers in Arizona, and parts of the Gila and San Francisco drainages in New Mexico. The species was listed as threatened on October 28, 1986.

**Regulatory context.** In August 1992, the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] issued a jeopardy opinion on a proposed [[Indian Health Service]] water diversion and treatment facility on the North Fork White River. The opinion was based mainly on potential dewatering of riffles downstream of the facility, which would affect resident loach minnow. The White River population was considered a possibly distinct genetic component of the species.

**Survey rationale.** Earlier surveys focused on a few easily accessible reaches that often lacked suitable habitat, and many streams with potentially suitable habitat had never been surveyed. Surveys in remote parts of the Big Bonito Creek drainage began in July 1993 to address this gap.

**Study area.** Big Bonito Creek lies in the White Mountain geographic subprovince, drains the southern slopes of the Mount Baldy Wilderness Area, and flows to the [[Black River]]. Its lower drainage consists of deeply incised canyons bordered by basalt plateaus. Significant tributaries include Little Bonito Creek and Tonto Creek. Five locations were sampled on lower Tonto Creek and two on Big Bonito Creek, all at or below 2000 m elevation. Access required considerable hiking and manual transport of equipment.

**Methods.** Surveys used a [[Coffelt Mark 10 Backpack Electrofisher]] with the frequency selector set to CPS to reduce mortality. Stunned fishes were collected with 3/8-inch mesh block seines or dipnets. Riffles were sampled intensively and pools less often. Fishes were identified, measured to the nearest millimeter (mm), weighed to the nearest gram (g), and released. Some catches were measured only in subsample.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat conditions.**
- *Tonto Creek, locations 1–2:* Narrow channel and floodplain confined by steep terrain. Pools and runs with rubble and boulder substrates dominated, and riffles were rare and diminutive.
- *Tonto Creek, location 3:* Channel widened and riffles were more common.
- *Tonto Creek, location 4:* Centered at the confluence of Cienega Creek and Tonto Creek, with pool, run, and riffle habitats throughout.
- *Tonto Creek, location 5:* A 1-km reach downstream of Fort Apache Road 47, with habitats similar to location 4.
- *Big Bonito Creek, location 1:* About 800 m of stream east of the KL Ranch on a broad floodplain, with substantial riffle area. Riffle cobbles and gravels were often firmly embedded.
- *Big Bonito Creek, location 2:* Near the ID Ranch, several km above the Black River confluence. Dominated by boulder-strewn pools and long, sand-bottomed runs; riffles were rare.

**Disturbance history.** The drainage was considered relatively undisturbed because of its remoteness. The natural flow regime is largely intact, since [[Hurricane Lake]] (Big Bonito Creek) and [[Tonto Lake]] (Tonto Creek) do not regulate runoff. Non-native fishes have had access to the drainage since at least 1941. Brown trout were introduced into the Big Bonito system after Hurricane Lake was built in 1959. Tonto Lake was constructed in 1941 and its dam modified in the early 1960s. It was historically stocked with rainbow and cutthroat trout and is currently managed with rainbow trout and [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*).

**Threats to native fishes.** Loach minnow decline has been linked to disturbance of riffle habitat and to introduction of non-native species (Minckley 1973; Propst et al. 1988).

## Key Findings

- **No loach minnow were collected** in any survey location on lower Tonto Creek or Big Bonito Creek.
- **Tonto Creek:** 1,569 fishes were taken in 30 hauls across five locations.
  - [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*): 79.3%
  - [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*): 12.7%
  - [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*): 6.6%
  - [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*): 1.3%
  - [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*): 0.2%
  - Sonora sucker and trout were likely under-represented because sampling was biased toward riffles.
- **Big Bonito Creek:** Only 38 specimens were taken, representing the same five species. Location 2 yielded no fishes despite repeated sampling. In location 1:
  - Speckled dace: 86.8%
  - Desert sucker: 5.3%
  - Sonora sucker, rainbow trout, and brown trout: one individual each (2.6%)
- **Riffle habitat quality** was identified as the likely limiting factor. Tonto Creek riffles were small, few, and widely separated, and Big Bonito Creek riffles were embedded. None were judged suitable for loach minnow occupation, although Tonto Creek riffles in locations 3–5 were considered only marginally suitable.
- **Length data (Table 2)** included, for example, Tonto Creek location 2 speckled dace (N = 207, mean total length 62.95 mm, range 42–92 mm) and location 4 rainbow trout (N = 21, mean 87.38 mm, range 48–323 mm).
- **Native fish community:** The Tonto Creek native fauna was judged to be in "relatively excellent condition," and non-native trout did not appear to cause significant negative impacts. Rainbow trout under 100 mm were most frequent in riffle hauls, and adults were rare. No Apache trout were encountered.
- **Big Bonito Creek paucity** could not be explained with current data. Brown trout presence was implicated, but the gut of one adult brown trout from location 1 contained only trichopteran larvae and detritus.
- **Status:** Results are preliminary. Continued surveys are required before the species' distribution on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation can be defined.
