# Dissolved-Oxygen Requirements of Three Species of Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

Moss and Scott (1961) measured the critical dissolved-oxygen (DO) levels and standard metabolic rates of [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Channel Catfish]] at 25 °C, 30 °C, and 35 °C in the laboratory. Two designs were used: [[Shock Tests]], in which DO was dropped abruptly, and [[Acclimation Tests]], in which DO was lowered in daily steps. Critical levels rose with temperature in nearly every series. Acclimation tests gave lower critical values than shock tests at a given temperature. Standard metabolic rates changed little across 25–35 °C. The study also showed that handling stress must be allowed to settle for at least 24 hours before experiments, or critical values are overestimated.

## Background & Description

The paper is a reprint from the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 90, No. 4, October 1961, pp. 377–393). The work was funded by the National Council for Stream Improvement, with later support from the National Science Foundation (grant G-13328). It was carried out in the Department of Zoology, University of Georgia, Athens.

The authors used minimal-activity conditions so that oxygen demand approximated resting, or standard, metabolic rate. They argued that this makes results comparable across studies. The design also helped isolate DO effects from other variables. The authors noted that laboratory data on resting or active fish are hard to transfer to field conditions.

Fish were held in the laboratory and acclimated for at least 3 weeks at the test temperature. Fish for 35 °C tests were held for at least 30 days. Fish that survived one experiment were never reused.

### Apparatus and Methods

- Four gravity-fed stripping columns (2-inch Pyrex tubing) delivered heated, aerated water. Nitrogen was used to strip oxygen to set levels, controlled by a third-stage low-pressure regulator.
- Athens city tap water was used at first. Residual chlorine (sensitivity 0.03 p.p.m.) became a problem in spring 1959, so activated carbon filters were installed.
- Test vessels ranged from 250-mL flasks to half-gallon jars. Flow rates were 90–500 mL/min, adjusted to fish oxygen consumption.
- DO was measured by a modified [[Winkler Titration]], with duplicate sampling and reproducibility of 0.02–0.04 p.p.m.
- Respiration was expressed as mg O₂ consumed per gram of fish per hour. Bacterial oxygen demand was corrected, with a maximum correction of 10% of fish respiration.

### Study Species and Source

- **Bluegill** (*Lepomis macrochirus*) were seined from a farm pond at the USDA Piedmont Experiment Station near Watkinsville, Georgia.
- **Largemouth Bass** (*Micropterus salmoides*) came from the same pond and from [[Rock Eagle Lake]], Putnam County, Georgia. Bass were narcotized in 3% NaCl solution for handling.
- **Channel Catfish** (*Ictalurus punctatus*) were supplied by H. S. Swingle (Auburn University) and Jack Snow (Marion Fisheries Station, Alabama) in three lots.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors framed the study around three ways fish might face low DO in nature: a rapid drop from a pollutant, a gradual decline, or a fluctuating regime. Gradual declines may allow fish to acclimate and tolerate lower concentrations. Earlier work by [[Shepard 1955]] on [[Brook Trout]] and by Prosser et al. (1957) on goldfish showed measurable acclimation benefits.

Temperature was also central. The authors tested 25–35 °C because 35 °C was near the lethal limit for these species. Metabolic response to temperature was discussed in terms of the [[Van't Hoff Law]], with the note that thoroughly acclimated fish show smaller increases in metabolism than the Q₁₀ approach predicts.

The authors warned that the laboratory results (which assumed minimal activity) may not match field conditions. Activity, size, nutritional state, and handling all shape oxygen demand.

## Key Findings

### 1. Critical DO levels in shock tests

Shock tests gave the 24-hour survival threshold for each species at each temperature:

| Species | 25 °C | 30 °C | 35 °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bluegill | 0.75 p.p.m. | 1.00 p.p.m. | 1.23 p.p.m. |
| Largemouth Bass | 0.92 p.p.m. | 1.19 p.p.m. | 1.40 p.p.m. |
| Channel Catfish | 0.95 p.p.m. | 1.03 p.p.m. | 1.08 p.p.m. |

Largemouth bass values were slightly higher than bluegill values at all three temperatures. At 35 °C, channel catfish were the most tolerant of low DO.

### 2. Critical DO levels in acclimation tests

| Species | 25 °C | 30 °C | 35 °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bluegill | 0.70 p.p.m. | 0.80 p.p.m. | 0.90 p.p.m. |
| Largemouth Bass | 0.83 p.p.m. | 0.83 p.p.m. | 1.23 p.p.m. |
| Channel Catfish | 0.76 p.p.m. | 0.89 p.p.m. | 0.92 p.p.m. |

Acclimation values were lower than shock values in most cases. The gap ranged from 0.05 p.p.m. (bluegill, 25 °C) to 0.36 p.p.m. (largemouth bass, 30 °C). The authors expected larger and more consistent acclimation benefits. They attributed the smaller gaps to their methods. Acclimation endpoints averaged the DO over the last 24 hours of life, which often included early-morning deaths when no observations were made. Flow rates also left a measurable oxygen drop across the vessels.

### 3. Handling stress requires a 24-hour rest

- Bluegill, 25 °C: 1.50 p.p.m. without a rest period, 0.75 p.p.m. after a 24-hour rest.
- Bluegill, 35 °C: 2.00 p.p.m. without a rest period, 1.22 p.p.m. after a rest (the Results section gives 1.23 p.p.m.).

Handling and confinement raised oxygen consumption for at least 24 hours. The authors therefore set a 24-hour settling period as standard.

### 4. Sample sizes behind key shock-test values

- Bluegill at 25 °C: 18 fish averaging 7 g (1.5 p.p.m.; initial, no rest). Later, 20 fish averaging 5 g and 14 averaging 20 g gave 0.75 p.p.m.
- Bluegill at 30 °C: 16 fish averaging 6 g (1.0 p.p.m.).
- Largemouth bass: 10 fish averaging 14 g (25 °C); 15 fish averaging 4 g (30 °C); 15 fish averaging 4 g (35 °C).
- Channel catfish at 25 °C: 18 fish averaging 13 g (0.95 p.p.m.).
- Channel catfish at 30 °C: 20 fish averaging 22 g (1.03 p.p.m.). Two earlier lots of "overfed," excessively fat fish gave anomalous, higher-sensitivity results.
- Channel catfish at 35 °C: 15 fish (45 days acclimated) and 12 fish (28 days) gave 1.05 and 1.09 p.p.m., combined as 1.08 p.p.m.

### 5. Size effects on metabolic rate

- Bluegill and largemouth bass weighing more than about 15 g showed no measurable change in relative metabolic rate with size. Below that, relative rate decreased with increasing size.
- Channel catfish between 20 and 105 g showed no detectable difference in relative metabolic rate.
- The authors concluded that intraspecific comparisons are valid within these size ranges.

### 6. Temperature and standard metabolic rate

Standard metabolic rates were similar between 25 °C and 35 °C for each species. For example, bluegills of 7–16 g showed nearly the same rates at 25 and 30 °C and only a slight rise between 30 and 35 °C. Largemouth bass showed no appreciable increase between 25 and 30 °C. The bluegill rate at 25 °C was lower than that of the other species at the same temperature. Critical DO levels rose with temperature, while respiratory rates did not track temperature in the same way.

### 7. Nutritional state

Starvation did not significantly lower the relative metabolic rate of control fish over 11–15 days of acclimation tests. Obese channel catfish from the 30 °C lots were apparently more sensitive to low DO. Their oxygen consumption was not measured, so the authors could only suspect elevated metabolic demand.

### 8. Respiratory dependence and daily rhythms

- Fish that died during observed periods showed respiratory dependence 2–6 hours before death. Dependence was seldom recorded in fish that died overnight (12 p.m. to 8 a.m.).
- No consistent diurnal rhythm in oxygen consumption was found in any of the three species, based on acclimation data and closer-interval measurements on bluegill and channel catfish.

### 9. Study limitations

- Large fish (50 g or more) reached oxygen drops near 0.5 p.p.m. across the test vessel, which may have affected survival. Low-DO fish oriented into the inflow and nearly took the tube into their mouths.
- Some experiments were invalidated by mishaps. The authors identified these as unavoidable.
- The authors concluded that shock tests are reasonably good measures of the ultimate low oxygen tolerance. Acclimation tests were mainly valuable for the respiratory-rate data they provided.
