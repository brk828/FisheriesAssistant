# The Role of Introgressive Hybridization in the Gila robusta Complex (Teleostei: Cyprinidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study uses mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) sequence variation to assess introgressive hybridization in the [[Gila Robusta Complex]], a group of endangered [[Colorado River Chubs]] (genus *Gila*). Samples of 514 individuals were analysed with [[Restriction Endonuclease Analysis]] and [[Single-Stranded Conformational Polymorphism]] (SSCP) of [[Cytochrome B]] and [[NADH Dehydrogenase Subunit 2]] (ND2). Lower-basin populations of *G. robusta*, *G. elegans* and *G. cypha* carried distinct mtDNA lineages, with limited introgression. In the upper basin, nearly all fish carried *G. cypha* mtDNA, and no *G. robusta* mtDNA was found, even in morphologically pure *G. robusta*. The authors conclude that morphological and mtDNA variation have evolved under different forces, and that hybridization and local adaptation both shape evolution in this group.

## Background & Description

The complex comprises seven taxa: *G. cypha*, *G. elegans*, *G. intermedia*, *G. nigra*, *G. robusta jordani*, *G. robusta robusta* and *G. seminuda*. Three allopatric lower-basin taxes (*G. intermedia*, *G. nigra*, *G. r. robusta*) could not be distinguished by allozymes or mtDNA and were treated together as *G. robusta*. *G. robusta jordani* (Pluvial White River) and *G. seminuda* (Virgin and Moapa rivers) are thought to have originated through hybridization.

*G. cypha* and *G. elegans* are large (up to 500 mm), long-lived (>50 years) fishes, morphologically distinct and co-occurring with *G. robusta* in the mainstem Colorado River and large tributaries. *G. cypha* was once locally abundant in canyon-bound reaches but several populations have been lost. *G. elegans* was formerly common in larger rivers but is now nearly extirpated from the upper basin, persisting mainly in [[Lake Mohave]]. Both species are listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Hybridization is especially common in cyprinid fishes and can reduce genetic diversity through swamping, particularly when environments are disturbed or one species is rare. Hybridization may also transfer adaptations or generate new forms. The desert Southwest's shifting environments are presented as a setting where such processes could enhance biodiversity.

Historical distributions were broad, and allopatry in the lower basin is therefore hard to confirm. Upper-basin populations, including those in [[Cataract Canyon]] and [[Desolation Canyon]], are sympatric. The authors argue that cooler upper-basin water temperatures during the Pleistocene probably prevented persistence, so upper-basin populations were founded recently by fish moving upstream, which likely produced hybridization in [[Grand Canyon]].

Human modification was initially suspected of increasing hybridization. The authors argue that the extent of introgression predates modern human influence. Replacement of *G. robusta* mtDNA would take much longer than humans have persisted in the region, and isolated populations (Pluvial White River, about 14,000 years; Chevelon Creek, about 250,000 years) are old enough to exclude major modern effects.

## Key Findings

- **Lower basin:** Individuals of *G. robusta*, *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* were readily assigned by distinctive haplotypes. Three of the Grand Canyon *G. cypha* carried *G. elegans* mtDNA, which the authors attribute to hybridization or shared ancestral polymorphism.
- **Upper basin:** Of 384 upper-basin fishes, 50 carried *G. elegans* mtDNA (41 of these were *G. seminuda* from the Moapa and Virgin rivers). All remaining fish carried *G. cypha* mtDNA, and none carried *G. robusta* mtDNA, despite large samples of morphologically "pure" *G. robusta*.
- **Decoupling:** *G. cypha* mtDNA was distributed randomly among morphotypes. Morphological and mtDNA variation are therefore decoupled, and the authors suggest mtDNA moves freely among populations while morphology is shaped by local environment.
- **Hybrid taxon:** *G. robusta jordani* possessed *G. cypha* mtDNA, and all eight additional specimens analysed carried it. The authors recommend recognizing it as a separate hybrid taxon, *G. jordani*.
- ***G. elegans* diversity:** 49 individuals from 9 localities yielded 11 composite haplotypes. Two divergent lineages were found; one is widespread and the other is restricted to the Virgin River and Lake Mohave. The DNFH hatchery stock is monomorphic (haplotype ZZ), so the authors recommend adding wild genetic diversity to the broodstock.
- ***G. cypha* structure:** 254 individuals from 15 localities yielded 22 composite haplotypes. Overall AMOVA variation was 71% within populations (F_ST = 0.29, p < 0.001). Divergence between the Grand Canyon/Chevelon Creek group and the remaining populations was low (about 0.06%). The Little Colorado River region is the main source of structure.
- **Morphology versus mtDNA:** McElroy and Douglas (1995) found limited morphological evidence of introgression, while mtDNA showed much more extensive introgression. The results do not allow rejection of either hypothesis proposed by those authors (increased hybridization or local adaptation).
- **Conclusions:** The authors conclude that hybridization and local adaptation both play important roles in evolution. Maintaining reproductive plasticity may help these fishes adapt to extreme environmental fluctuation. mtDNA, as a maternally inherited marker, offers only a limited view of hybridization, and additional nuclear markers would be needed for more detail.
