# Prehistoric Overkill: Late-Pleistocene Generic Extinction and Human Arrival

#paleontology #mammalogy #extinction-biology #megafauna #pleistocene

## Executive Summary

This review, presented as a chapter by [[Paul S. Martin]] of the University of Arizona Geochronology Department, argues that the sudden late-Pleistocene wave of large-animal extinction, involving at least 200 genera with little or no replacement, was caused by [[Prehistoric Overkill]] by humans rather than by climate change. The author builds the case on the chronology of extinction relative to the spread of [[Human Dispersal]] and big-game hunting, using [[Radiocarbon Dating]] records from the Americas, Australia, New Zealand, Madagascar, Africa and Eurasia. The paper concedes significant uncertainties, including contaminated and "overshot" dates, but concludes that the pattern "leaves little room for any other explanation."

## Background & Description

The abstract and introduction frame the problem through the work of [[Alfred Russel Wallace]], who initially attributed late-Pleistocene extinction to glaciation but later concluded that only human action could account for the global sequence. The paper notes that [[Charles Darwin]] had already shown extinct Pleistocene fauna occurred in beds younger than the last glaciation.

Martin focuses on genera rather than species, because identifying valid species from fossil material is unreliable. He notes that generic extinction affected mainly large terrestrial herbivores (over 50 kg adult body weight, Table 1), together with their dependent carnivores and scavengers. Small vertebrates, freshwater mollusks, amphibians, reptiles, diatoms, vascular plants, marine plankton and cetaceans show little or no late-Pleistocene generic loss.

The historical framework includes [[O. P. Hay]]'s 1919 and 1923 extinction chronologies, which the author argues were disproved by radiocarbon and stratigraphic evidence showing several "early" genera survived into the last glaciation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pattern of loss:** In North America about 33 large-mammal genera were going or gone by the end of the late-glacial, more than in the rest of the Pleistocene combined. Extinction there is dated to within the last c. 15,000 years, with the major episode near 11,000 B.P.
- **Earlier extinction:** Blancan-age losses (over one million years ago) were followed by replacement; the late-Pleistocene loss was not.
- **Climate argument:** Martin argues that vegetation and climate had largely returned to interglacial "normal" by about 11,000 years ago, so climate change does not explain the timing. He contends that mammoth, horse, sloth and mastodon could have migrated, as living large mammals do.
- **Africa and Southeast Asia:** Major extinction there predates the late Würm, roughly 40,000–50,000 B.P., coinciding with the end of the Acheulean hunting culture. Martin argues this supports rather than refutes overkill.
- **Islands:** Losses of [[Moa]] in New Zealand, giant lemurs and [[Aepyornis]] in Madagascar, and giant marsupials in Australia are presented as cases where extinction follows human arrival.
- **Europe:** Western Europe shows less generic mammal extinction than other continents, despite glaciation theoretically trapping its biota.

## Key Findings

- **Global extinction is human-linked by chronology.** No continent or island is known where accelerated extinction definitely predates human arrival (outside Africa and Southeast Asia, where it is much older).
- **North American main wave:** The only major Pleistocene generic extinction episode in continental North America occurred close to 11,000 B.P.; thirty-one large-herbivore genera were lost at the end of the last glaciation (Table 1).
- **Small-mammal and aquatic contrast:** Of seventy Wisconsin-age small mammals, all but six are assigned to living species; of sixty-nine amphibians and reptiles, only three are extinct; of seventy-three freshwater mollusks, only one is extinct.
- **Radiocarbon reassessment:** Martin accepts that several previously claimed post-glacial survival dates for Mammuthus, Mammut, Equus, Camelops and sloths are contaminated or poorly associated, and that the evidence for postglacial survival rests on only three sites (Gypsum Cave, Whitewater Draw, Evansville).
- **Mastodon debate:** Claims of mastodon survival to about 6,000 B.P. are questioned because most reliable bone-associated dates are younger than 9,500 B.P. but organic dates cluster at 10,500–12,700 B.P., and because the distribution of Michigan mastodons (163 records, Skeels 1962) coincides with the fluted-point "Mason–Quimby Line."
- **New Zealand:** Of twenty-seven moa species, twenty-two have been found in association with prehistoric humans, and no species is definitely known to have disappeared before human arrival. Extinction is estimated at about 900 B.P. in the Conclusion, with dated moa-hunter sites ranging roughly from AD 1100 to 1670.
- **South America:** Extinction is considered to have coincided with, or slightly postdated, North American extinction, with sloth dung surviving to just over 10,000 years ago.
- **Competing explanations:** Martin finds that catastrophic events and climate change fail to match the chronology. He concedes the hypothesis has objections, including the absence of modern Paleolithic hunters causing comparable losses, and the unexplained survival of moose, elk and caribou.
- **Conclusion:** "Prehistoric hunters ten to fifteen thousand years ago (and in Africa over forty thousand years ago) exterminated far more large animals than has modern man," and "the late-Pleistocene extinction pattern leaves little room for any other explanation."
