## Executive Summary
This document is a formal comment letter (dated 10 September 1991) from Dr. W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service regarding a proposed modification to Hoover Dam operations. The letter raises significant concerns regarding the adequacy of the Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) and Environmental Assessment (EA) used to evaluate impacts on [[Lake Mohave]]. Specifically, it highlights that the biological data underpinning the 1983 EIS is outdated, failing to account for the introduction of a new predatory competitor, the [[Striped Bass]], and new scientific findings regarding native species of special concern. The letter emphasizes the critical importance of Lake Mohave as the primary refuge for the last biologically viable, self-sustaining populations of the [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]].

## Background & Description
*Note: As a policy/comment letter rather than a primary research paper, specific morphological data (e.g., meristics, body measurements) for the species is not detailed in this text. The following taxonomic identifications are confirmed:*

*   **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*): Federally listed as an **Endangered Species** at the time of writing.
*   **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Identified as "soon-to-be-listed" as an Endangered Species (pending federal listing status in 1991).
*   **[[Striped Bass]]** (*Morone saxatilis*): Described as a "new fish" to the system, indicating a non-native/introduced status.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
*   **Habitat:** The main body of **[[Lake Mohave]]**, a reservoir on the lower Colorado River situated between Arizona and Nevada, serves as the essential habitat for these species.
*   **Population Status:** Lake Mohave is identified as hosting the **largest and most biologically viable stocks** of both the Bonytail and Razorback Sucker. The author notes that "no other such populations are definitely known to exist," positioning this lake as the sole reservoir of genetic diversity necessary for the species' recovery.
*   **Biotic Interactions:** The ecosystem is described as being in a "state of change" due to the introduction of the Striped Bass, which is actively "influencing the biological system." This suggests a potential predatory or competitive interaction impacting the native [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] populations.
*   **Abiotic Factors:** The letter stresses that the **physico-chemical properties** (water quality, temperature, flow) of the water downstream of Hoover Dam are directly tied to the reservoir's operational scenarios, which in turn affect the survival of the resident fish fauna.

## Key Findings
1.  **Genetic Viability:** Despite unresolved issues regarding recruitment (reproduction to adulthood), the Bonytail and Razorback Sucker populations in Lake Mohave possess the necessary "genetic capacity, variability, and potential" to sustain themselves and support species recovery, provided management is appropriate.
2.  **Data Obsolescence:** The scientific/biological information used for the 1983 EIS and the current EA is deemed "largely out of date," rendering the current regulatory decision-making process potentially inadequate.
3.  **Introduction of Competitor/Predator:** The establishment of the [[Striped Bass]] represents a significant, unassessed variable altering the trophic dynamics of the Lake Mohave ecosystem.
4.  **Documentation Gap:** The current EIS/EA documentation is criticized for failing to adequately address the "main body of Lake Mohave," despite this area being the most critical habitat zone for the species of concern.
5.  **Management Recommendation:** The author urges "in-depth and comprehensive planning" for the survival of these species before implementing any further structural or operational modifications to the lower Colorado River system via [[Hoover Dam]].
