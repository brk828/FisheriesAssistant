# Deep-Water Fauna of Lake Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coregonidae

## Executive Summary

This short 1870s natural-history paper by P. R. Hoy, M.D., of [[Racine]], reports the deep-water fauna of [[Lake Michigan]], found 16 to 20 miles offshore in 50 to 70 fathoms. Hoy identified the food of the white-fish by examining stomach contents, and this work revealed several animals new to science: three fish, four small crustaceans, and one mollusk. The specimens were described by [[Theodore Gill]] at the [[Smithsonian Institution]] and by [[William Stimpson]] of the Chicago Academy of Science. The paper also proposes that the presence of marine forms points to a saltwater past for the lake.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Off Racine, the lake reaches 50 to 70 fathoms. The bottom is an impalpable, dark muddy ooze, with depressions holding partly decayed leaves. Commercial gill-net fishermen worked these "mud flats" for the largest and finest [[Whitefish]] and [[Lake Trout]].
- **Methods:** Hoy collected large numbers of stomachs from fish caught at various depths and diluted the ingesta to identify prey. This approach is an early example of [[Gut Content Analysis]].
- **Specimen routing:** Fish were sent to [[Spencer Fullerton Baird]] and then to Gill. Crustaceans went to Stimpson, identified by Baird as the best American authority on the group.
- **Key taxa:**
  - *Argyrosomus hoyi* (the [[Mooneye]] of fishermen): the smallest whitefish known from the Great Lakes, about 8 inches long and weighing one-quarter pound. It is an excellent pan-fish but too small for market. Trout eat large numbers of it.
  - *Argyrosomus nigripinnis* (the [[Black-Fin Whitefish]]): a large, handsome fish with black fins.
  - *Triglopsis* (Cottid), close to *T. thompsonii*. Hoy's specimen came from a trout's stomach. Gill suspected it was an undescribed species and proposed the name *T. stimpsonii*. It is a marine-type fish found in fresh water.
  - *Gammarus* (three species: *G. hoyi*, *G. brevistilus*, *G. filicornis*), freshwater shrimps.
  - *Mysis diluvianus*, a marine genus with many species in the North Atlantic and Arctic.
  - *Pisidium abysomus* (as printed), an undescribed small bivalve found in whitefish stomachs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Depth partitioning:** The Mooneye occurred only in water deeper than 40 fathoms. The Black-Fin was never caught in less than 60 fathoms and was not abundant in less than 70.
- **Trophic links:** Trout consumed large numbers of Mooneyes and fragments of the *Triglopsis* and crustaceans, showing that deep-water prey supported the upper food web.
- **Fishing pressure and variability:** In summer 1871 no Black-Fin were taken off Racine because fishermen had not gone as far offshore as the previous season.
- **Pressure sensitivity:** Crustaceans could not survive when brought to the surface. Living specimens died within a few hours, because they are adapted to the pressure of 50 to 70 fathoms.
- **Other fauna:** A parasitic leech attached to whitefish and a small white planarian were also recorded.
- **Historical biogeography (relict marine fauna):** Hoy argued that *Mysis* and *Triglopsis* imply the lake was once connected to the ocean and salt. The lake's gradual rise above sea level would have taken many years to flush out salt water, which is denser and tends to remain in deep basins. Salt springs on the lake bed, and the salt-bearing strata of Michigan, might have slowed freshening. Hoy also raised the possibility of brackish water in the deepest parts, but his apparatus was too imperfect to confirm or rule this out.

## Key Findings

1. **Newly described fauna:** Three fish, four small crustaceans, and one mollusk were new to science. Hoy's collections added new taxa to the Great Lakes fauna.
2. **Whitefish diet:** Stomach analysis showed that the deep-water diet of whitefish includes small crustaceans and mollusks, which Hoy found in the stomachs.
3. **Eleven known deep-water taxa:** Hoy's catalogue lists 11 animals known from the deep water off Racine. These include 2 *Coregonus*, 2 *Argyrosomus*, 1 *Triglopsis*, 3 *Gammarus*, 1 *Mysis*, 1 *Pisidium*, and *Salmo amethystus*.
4. **Expedition (24 June 1870):** A dredging trip on the lake yielded living crustaceans, but none survived more than a few hours at the surface.
5. **Relict marine hypothesis:** The marine *Mysis* and *Triglopsis* suggest Lake Michigan was once saline and connected to the ocean. Hoy proposed that salt persisted in deep water as the lake rose, and that brackish water might remain in the deepest areas. He could not confirm this.
6. **Taxonomic note:** Gill regarded the *Triglopsis* specimen as possibly undescribed and might name it *T. stimpsonii*, honoring Stimpson. Hoy also notes that *T. thompsonii* had not been recorded since its 1850 capture in [[Lake Ontario]].
