# Origin and Dispersal of the Alewife and Gizzard Shad in the Great Lakes

## Executive Summary
This 1956/1957 paper by Robert Rush Miller reconstructs the historical invasion, dispersal routes, and taxonomic identity of two clupeid (herring family) fishes in the [[Great Lakes]]: the [[Alewife]] (*Alosa pseudoharengus*) and the [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*), alongside the related [[American Shad]] (*Alosa sapidissima*). Using museum specimens, historical fisheries records, and correspondence with regional biologists, Miller traces the chronological spread of each species through the lake system, proposes canal-mediated and natural dispersal routes, and speculates on the possible native status of each species in specific lakes (Alewife in Lake Ontario; Gizzard Shad in Lake Erie). The paper documents a dramatic mid-20th-century population explosion of the Alewife, potentially linked to ecological disruption caused by the invasive [[Sea Lamprey]] (*Petromyzon marinus*), and provides a taxonomic key for distinguishing the three clupeid species.

## Background & Description
- **Taxonomy**: Miller argues for placing the Alewife in the genus *Alosa* rather than the traditionally used *Pomolobus*, citing lack of reliable distinguishing morphological characters between the two groups and questionable nomenclatural grounding for *Pomolobus* (per unpublished work by S.F. Hildebrand).
- **Species compared** (see [[Table 1 - Clupeid Comparison]]):
  - **American Shad**: unmodified last dorsal ray; dorsal fin origin well before pelvic fin insertion; long slender upper jaw; strong gill rakers (25–75, lower limb); vertebrae typically 52–64.
  - **Alewife**: similar fin structure to American Shad but shorter jaw, fewer gill rakers (20–40, lower limb), fewer vertebrae (typically 45–54; Atlantic coastal alewives have more vertebrae than Lake Ontario populations, per Graham 1956).
  - **Gizzard Shad**: distinctive elongated filamentous last dorsal ray; dorsal fin origin over/behind pelvic fin insertion; small inferior/subterminal mouth; deep body; very fine, numerous gill rakers (90–275, lower limb); vertebrae 52–70.
- Vertebral counts do not overlap between Alewife and American Shad, or between Gizzard Shad and American Shad — a reliable diagnostic character.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Both species are primarily marine/estuarine clupeids that have become established in freshwater lake systems; the Gizzard Shad favors shallow, warmer waters and low-gradient streams for spawning, while cold, deep water (as in [[Lake Superior]]) appears to be a barrier to establishment for both species.
- **Dispersal barriers and routes**:
  - [[Niagara Falls]] blocked natural upstream movement of the Alewife into [[Lake Erie]] from [[Lake Ontario]]; the species instead is hypothesized to have passed through the [[Welland Canal]] (available since 1829).
  - The Gizzard Shad likely entered the Great Lakes drainage naturally from the [[Mississippi River]] basin via a low divide near Fort Wayne, Indiana (Maumee-Wabash drainage connection), or via canal (Ohio Canal, completed 1830).
  - Gizzard Shad reached [[Lake Michigan]] via the [[Chicago River]] canal — a clear case of human-mediated invasion — while it spread to Lake Huron via the [[Detroit River]] and [[Lake St. Clair]], and to Lake Ontario/Quebec via the Welland Canal and St. Lawrence River.
- **Climatic factors**: The gradual warming climatic trend over the preceding half-century is proposed as a facilitator of the Gizzard Shad's northward range expansion; conversely, colder periods are associated with historical "winterkill" die-offs and local extirpation, explaining the perception of the species as a "recent invader" in areas where it may actually be a relict population from a warmer pre-Columbian period.
- **Biotic interactions**: The Alewife's population explosion in the 1950s coincides temporally with the establishment of the invasive [[Sea Lamprey]] in the upper Great Lakes. Data from South Bay, Lake Huron (1950s) show an inverse relationship between Alewife and shallow-water [[Cisco]] (*Coregonus artedii*, "lake herring") abundance, suggesting direct or indirect competitive/predatory interactions, though causality (versus normal population fluctuation) is not conclusively established.
- **Human use**: Both species have been used as low-value commercial resources — for food, fertilizer, animal feed (swine, cattle, trout), and oil pressing. The Gizzard Shad reached limited commercial importance in Lakes Erie and Huron (31,600 lbs valued at $930 in 1949; ~116,000 lbs total 1942–1954).

## Key Findings

### Alewife (*Alosa pseudoharengus*) Chronology
- No confirmed records from [[Lake Ontario]] prior to 1873 (after American Shad stocking by the U.S. Fish Commission) — origin as native vs. introduced remains unresolved.
- First specimens by lake:
  - **Lake Erie**: 1931 (Nanticoke, Ontario)
  - **Lake Huron**: 1933 (off Outer Duck Island, Ontario)
  - **Lake Michigan**: 1949 (west of South Manitou Island)
  - **Lake Superior**: 1954 (Pendill's Creek, Chippewa Co., MI)
- Species spread from Lake Erie to other lakes via the [[Detroit River]] and [[Lake St. Clair]] rather than via the proposed Trent River–Severn River waterway from Lake Ontario (per correspondence with W.B. Scott, contra Van Oosten 1935).
- Did not become locally abundant until: ~1950 (Lake Erie, Lake Huron), ~1956 (Lake Michigan); still not abundant but widely dispersed in Lake Superior as of 1956.
- Documented periodic mass die-offs in Lake Ontario since ~1890.
- South Bay, Lake Huron catch data (1951–1956) show Alewife pounds increasing from 0.2 to 22,428 while lake herring pounds declined from 36,018 to 3,476 over the same period — see [[Table 3 - South Bay Catch Data]].
- Documented spread into inland waters via bait bucket introduction (e.g., Crystal Lake, Mullet Lake, Betsie River, Platte River, Michigan).

### Gizzard Shad (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) Chronology
- Northern Atlantic coastal range limit: New York Harbor (Lower Bay/Sandy Hook Bay); reports from Cape Cod and New Brunswick (St. John River) are deemed erroneous or misidentifications of the Alewife.
- First reported from Lake Erie: 1848 (Cuyahoga River, Cleveland, per Kirtland), 18 years after completion of the Ohio Canal connecting Lake Erie and the Ohio River.
- A herring-like fish recorded by Samuel Wilmot around 1837 in Lake Ontario waters is hypothesized to be an early Gizzard Shad record (or possibly Alewife).
- First published Lake Ontario record: 1913 (reported as "very rare" by Nash).
- Confirmed in Lake Huron (Sarnia, Ontario) by November 1877 (specimen identified by Baird, verified by Miller, USNM No. 20836).
- First valid Lake Michigan specimens: October 13, 1953 (west of Muskegon, MI) and November 2, 1953 (near Gary, Indiana) — clear evidence of southern-origin colonization via the Chicago River Canal, not yet reaching northern Lake Michigan or Lake Huron.
- Not reported from Lake Superior — cold, deep water is hypothesized to prevent establishment.
- Hypothesis: Gizzard Shad may have entered Lake Erie naturally during a pre-Columbian warm-dry period, leaving relict populations that fluctuate dramatically with climate, rather than being purely canal-introduced.
- Notable historical abundance events: highly abundant in Detroit River/Lake St. Clair (1932, dip-netted by unemployed persons for food); mass mortality event at Erie, PA (Jan–Feb 1953); failed introduction attempt into Whitmore Lake, Michigan (1935, 124 individuals stocked, reproduction failed).

### Broader Implications
- Both species illustrate how canal construction (Welland Canal, Ohio Canal, Chicago River Canal) and climatic trends can facilitate range expansion of clupeids into novel freshwater systems.
- The Alewife's explosive mid-century population growth in the [[Great Lakes]] is presented as an early example of how invasive species disruption (Sea Lamprey) can trigger cascading changes in native fish community structure (Cisco decline).
- Miller calls for development of commercial utilization methods for the rapidly increasing Alewife population — an early recognition of what would become the major Alewife die-off and management issue in later Great Lakes ecological history.
