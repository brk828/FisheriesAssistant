# Seasonal Streamflow Effects and Resident Salmonid Movement in Beaver Creek, Idaho-Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This thesis (Master of Science, Utah State University, 1995, by Shanda S. Fallatt; major professor Dr. Jeffrey L. Kersner, Department of Fisheries and Wildlife) examines how seasonal streamflow changes affect physical habitat and resident [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarki*) and [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) in [[Beaver Creek]], a small mountain stream on the Idaho-Utah border. Chapter 2 compares high spring runoff with base flows in fall; Chapter 3 documents resident fish movement at scales larger than assumed by current models. The study concludes that seasonal flow changes were not apparently limiting to either species, while movement patterns challenge the [[Restricted Movement Paradigm]] underlying most fish-habitat models and monitoring programs.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Beaver Creek flows about 13 km in Idaho and 14 km in Utah (about 27 km total) and is fed by springs and snowmelt. Its hydrology is typical of the [[Intermountain West]], with base flows in summer and winter and high flows from spring snowmelt.
- **Inventory:** In August 1990 the author inventoried 13.1 km (5.1 km in Utah, 7.9 km in Idaho) on National Forest land, elevation 2723 m to 2492 m. The 1991 movement study used a 4 km inventory (about 5 km total study area).
- **Reach classification:** Four reach types were defined using gradient, sinuosity, channel shape, substrate ([[Rosgen Classification]]) and riparian vegetation:
  - High-gradient (HG, >4%): armored, conifer riparian cover
  - Low-gradient (LG, <1.5%): meandering, sedge-grass riparian cover
  - Medium-gradient (MG, 2-3%): thick willow cover
  - Beaver dam (BD): dominated by beaver dams with willow
- **Habitat units:** Classified as riffle, run, glide, main channel pool, lateral scour pool, step pool or pocket water ([[Bisson Habitat Typing]] system).
- **Human disturbance:** Forest Service roads, cattle and sheep grazing, poor road drainage, and recreation (especially in the Utah section, accessed from Highway 89) have altered riparian areas and caused bank erosion.
- **Funding:** USDA-Forest Service.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime:** Spring runoff expands channel area and depth, and raises velocity. Habitat surveys are usually run at base flows, so limiting factors present at high flow may go undetected ([[Platts Stream Survey Method]]).
- **Cover:** Only instream cover was counted (undercut banks, rootwads, woody debris >0.1 m diameter, woody aggregates, willow rootwads). Cover complexity was scored by counting pieces within a 20 cm square frame.
- **Velocity and depth:** Velocity was measured with a Montedoro-Whitney flow meter at six-tenths depth at twelve points per unit.
- **Species context:** Brook trout are known to prefer slower velocities (rarely above 30 cm/s, per Grant and Noakes 1987). Coastal cutthroat trout preferred mean velocities below 20 cm/s (Heggenes et al. 1991a). Young-of-the-year cutthroat use shallow, slow margins.
- **Spawning timing:** Brook trout emerged before high spring flows (May-June), and cutthroat trout spawned after peak flows, which likely reduced scour risk to eggs and fry.

## Key Findings

**Chapter 2: Seasonal streamflow and salmonid habitat**

- **Hydrology and physical habitat:** Units were larger, deeper, and faster in spring. Unit area was roughly twice as large in spring as fall (P<0.001). Mean spring velocity was 0.34 m/s versus 0.15 m/s in fall (P<0.001). Discharge ranged from 0.46 m³/s (spring) to 0.05 m³/s (fall). Staff gauge relative depth changed by 0.26 m between seasons.
- **Slow-water scarcity:** Slow-water habitat was scarce in spring. In the HG reach it covered 5% of area in spring versus 38% in fall. In the LG reach it covered 72% in spring and 26% in fall (as reported). Slow-water share also rose in the BD (37% to 63%) and MG (25% to 58%) reaches.
- **Woody debris and rootwads:** Woody debris was higher in fall. The HG reach had the highest woody debris density in both seasons (27.8 pieces/100 m in spring, 37.9 in fall, P<0.01). Rootwad density was higher in fall in all reaches except MG. The MG reach had the highest rootwad density (771.5/100 m in spring, 9.3/100 m in fall, P<0.001 as reported; the spring value was inflated by 16 units assigned a count of 50).
- **Cover:** Cover area and volume were greater in spring in three of four reaches. Cover area differed significantly between seasons at the reach scale (P<0.05). Slow water had more cover area and volume than fast water (P<0.001 and P<0.01). Cover complexity was two to three times higher in slow water than fast (P<0.001).
- **Cutthroat trout densities:** Highest in the HG reach in spring. Cutthroat trout densities were generally low in spring and higher in fall. In the LG reach, none were seen in spring versus 9.7 fish/100 m² in fall. At the fast-slow scale, spring densities were higher in slow water (2.2 vs 1.2 fish/100 m²), while fall densities were higher in fast water (8.0 vs 4.9 fish/100 m²). Highest fall density by habitat type was in runs (17.1 fish/100 m²). No seasonal, reach, or habitat differences were significant at any scale.
- **Brook trout densities:** Densities were higher in the BD reach than in other reaches (P<0.001). Brook trout were about five times more abundant in slow than fast water (5.6 vs 1.0 fish/100 m², P<0.05), mainly in beaver dam, pool, and glide habitats.
- **Correlations:** Very few fish-habitat correlations were significant, and most were influenced by outliers or small samples. In the BD reach, percent cover area correlated positively with cutthroat density (explaining 59% of variation, P<0.01) and brook trout density (71%, P<0.001), though these may reflect outliers. Brook trout density also correlated with mean depth in fall slow water (P<0.001).
- **Conclusion:** No limiting factors were detected at high flow. Lack of slow-water habitat in spring was the most likely limiting factor, but neither species appeared limited by it. The author recommends surveys at non-base flows where populations may be limited by discharge-driven habitat change.

**Chapter 3: Resident fish movement**

- **Core observation:** Both species moved across scales well beyond the assumed [[Restricted Movement Paradigm]]. Movement occurred among reaches (about 10³ m) and habitat units (about 10 m) within the 5 km study area, and some fish appeared to move beyond it.
- **Sampling:** 38 habitat units were snorkeled on seven dates (June 10, June 20, June 27, July 15, July 22, August 15, September 15, 1991).
- **Study-area densities:** Cutthroat trout ranged from 1.9 to 3.9 fish/100 m². A group moved in after peak flows (June 10-20), then apparently moved out. Brook trout ranged from 2.0 to 3.7 fish/100 m² until a mid-September rise to 5.7 fish/100 m².
- **Reach-scale variability:** Cutthroat trout coefficient of variation was lowest in the BD reach (41%) and highest in the HG reach (65%). Excluding the June 20 spawning sample halved HG variability. Brook trout variability ranged from 44% (LG) to 80% (MG).
- **Spawning-linked movement:** Spawning migrations explained much of the variation in HG cutthroat and BD brook trout densities. Brook trout moved into the study area in September, possibly from elsewhere in Beaver Creek or from the [[Logan River]].
- **Management implications:**
  - Fish-habitat models assume resident fish are spatially limited, so wide movement violates the model assumption and may explain weak habitat-density correlations.
  - Population monitoring designed around fixed sites may give misleading results, especially over short periods.
  - The author suggests monitoring brook trout in all habitat types except riffles in the BD reach before spawning. For cutthroat trout, the author proposes midsummer pool sampling in the HG reach, as densities there were consistent between June 27 and July 22 and variability was low.
- **Recommended research:** Radio-tag and track fish to measure movement, use larger sample sizes to detect subgroups of mobile fish, and build spatial and temporal variation into fish-habitat models.
- **Overall conclusion:** The study "raised more questions than it answered," but it demonstrates that wide-range movement needs to be considered in management.
