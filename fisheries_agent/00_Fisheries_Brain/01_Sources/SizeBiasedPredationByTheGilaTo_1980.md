# Size-Biased Predation by the Gila Topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*)

## Executive Summary
This study by Gerking & Plane Jr. (1980) documents **size-biased predation** by the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) under both field and laboratory conditions. In Monkey Spring, Arizona, adult female topminnows preferentially consume larger individuals of the amphipod [[Hyalella azteca]], with prey size positively correlated to predator size (mouth width and standard length). This pattern was reproduced in controlled laboratory trials using both live *Daphnia similis* and artificial food pellets, confirming that size-selective feeding is a robust, generalizable behavior. The work also identifies a two-phase feeding pattern (initial non-selective "frenzied feeding" followed by selective feeding) and highlights the limitations of scaling laboratory models to more complex, naturalistic multi-prey/multi-density scenarios.

## Background & Description
- **Predator species**: *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* (Baird & Girard) — the [[Gila Topminnow]], a small live-bearing fish restricted to isolated springs of southeastern Arizona.
- **Study population**: Collected from the **Monkey Spring system**, Santa Cruz County, Arizona — a simple, low-diversity ecosystem dominated by a cement irrigation canal substrate covered in the aquatic moss *Agroamblystegium fluviatile*.
- **Natural prey**: [[Hyalella azteca]] (Saussure), an amphipod that burrows through submerged vegetation mats; body length ranged 1.25–6.88 mm (mean 4.37 mm).
- **Laboratory prey surrogates**:
  - *Daphnia similis* (live cladoceran), collected from ponds near Gilbert, Maricopa County, AZ, held at 11°C to inhibit reproduction/molting.
  - Commercial pelletized fish food (T-W Manufacturing Co.), sorted into four size classes to mimic prey size distributions.
- **Measurement proxy**: Amphipod **head capsule length** was used as a durable, easily measured, and highly correlated proxy (r = 0.86, P < 0.01) for *Hyalella* total body length.
- **Species ecology note**: Only **adult females** (21–41 mm SL) consumed *Hyalella* in appreciable amounts; smaller **adult males** (18–24 mm) and juveniles were primarily detritivores — indicating an ontogenetic/sexual dietary shift linked to mouth size and gape constraints.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- This research extends a body of literature on **fish-mediated trophic cascades**, building on findings that planktivorous/benthivorous fish predation can restructure prey communities (e.g., [[Brooks And Dodson 1965]] on zooplankton size structure; Borutsky on chironomid biomass decline).
- Predation pressure by fish is framed as influencing **multiple trophic levels**, indirectly affecting phytoplankton, dissolved oxygen, nutrient cycling, and organic precipitation (citing Hurlbert et al. 1972 on *Gambusia affinis* — a related poeciliid).
- Size-selective feeding is presented as a **widespread phenomenon** across freshwater and marine fishes, with comparative taxa cited including:
  - [[Cyprinus carpio]] (common carp)
  - [[Carassius auratus]] (goldfish)
  - [[Alosa aestivalis]] and [[Alosa pseudoharengus]] (alewife species)
  - [[Osmerus mordax]] (rainbow smelt)
  - [[Perca flavescens]] (yellow perch)
  - [[Salvelinus gairdneri]] (rainbow trout, historical name)
  - *Coregonus* spp., *Salmo trutta*, *Salvelinus alpinus*
  - [[Ictalurus melas]] (black bullhead)
  - [[Lepomis macrochirus]] (bluegill sunfish)
  - *Melaniris chagresi* (a tropical atherinid, via Zaret 1972)
- The Monkey Spring system serves as a **simplified natural laboratory** — a nearly monoculture substrate of aquatic moss with a single dominant invertebrate prey species — allowing clean correlation of predator and prey size without confounding habitat complexity.
- Broader ecological implication: species with **gape-limited size-selective feeding** (e.g., [[Esox lucius]] unable to consume large [[Micropterus salmoides]]) may be far more common than currently recognized, since selectivity is only observable *within* the size range the predator can physically capture.

## Key Findings

### Field Observations (Monkey Spring)
- *Hyalella* occurred in the diet of **31% of adult females** (n=75) and was the dominant food item for **15%**.
- **Positive correlations** between predator size and mean/maximum ingested prey (head capsule length) across all three sampling dates (Feb, Apr, Sep 1974):
  - Mouth width × mean head capsule length: r = 0.64–0.84
  - Standard length × mean head capsule length: r = 0.66–0.80
  - Mouth width × maximum head capsule length: r = 0.68–0.73
  - All coefficients significant at P < 0.05 (Spearman rank correlation).
- Comparison of **stomach contents vs. benthic samples**:
  - Benthic *Hyalella* head capsules ranged 0.15–0.75 mm (mean 0.49 mm, n=216).
  - Ingested *Hyalella* head capsules ranged only 0.25–0.55 mm (mean 0.37 mm, n=69).
  - Chi-square test confirmed ingested amphipods were **significantly smaller** than those available in the benthos (P < 0.01) — indicating an **upper size limit imposed by mouth gape**, excluding the largest amphipods (0.65–0.75 mm) entirely.
- **A/C Ratio Analysis** (mean ingested size ÷ mean available size within capturable range):
  - Pooled mean A/C ratio = **1.22**, meaning topminnows selectively consume amphipods ~22% larger than the average available prey within their capturable size range.
  - A/C ratio positively correlated with predator mouth width (r = 0.33, 28 d.f., P < 0.05) — **larger fish show stronger size preference** for large prey.

### Laboratory Feeding Experiments
- **Two-phase feeding behavior** identified via frame-by-frame video analysis and consumption-rate curves:
  1. **"Frenzied feeding" phase** (first 5 minutes): rapid, largely non-selective consumption reflecting post-starvation hunger (electivity index ≈ 0.005, near-neutral).
  2. **Selective feeding phase** (15–60 minutes): consumption rate drops sharply and selectivity for larger prey increases significantly.
- **Food pellet trials**:
  - Mean consumed pellet size in first 5 min = 1.92 mm vs. 1.75 mm offered; later periods averaged 2.17 mm (up to 24% larger than offered mean).
  - Feeding period effect: F = 6.0 (12, 4 d.f.), P < 0.05.
  - Fish size effect on pellet size selected: F = 7.0 (12, 3 d.f.), P < 0.01 — smallest fish class (15.0 mm) selected 2.10 mm pellets; largest (29.0 mm) selected 2.27 mm pellets.
- **Daphnia trials** (paralleled pellet results):
  - Frenzied phase: mean size selected 1.80 mm vs. 1.66 mm offered.
  - Selective phase (15–60 min): mean size rose to 2.31 mm.
  - Feeding-period effect: F = 19.5 (12, 4 d.f.), P < 0.01.
  - Fish-size effect: F = 4.0 (12, 3 d.f.), P < 0.05.
  - Prey size vs. fish size correlation: r = 0.67 (58 d.f.), P < 0.01.
- **Electivity Index (modified Ivlev's E)**:
  - At 30 minutes, smallest fish showed near-neutral/slight positive selection for smallest *Daphnia* (E = +0.013), while largest fish strongly avoided them (E = −0.188).
  - Largest fish showed strong positive selection for largest prey class (E = +0.733) vs. smallest fish (E = +0.040).
  - Pooled electivity for two smallest prey classes = −0.057 (not significant); for two largest classes = +0.299 (significant, F = 5.73, 10/3 d.f., P < 0.05).
- **Consumption rate dynamics**:
  - Sharp decline in consumption rate between 5–15 min across all fish sizes.
  - Time to reach steady-state consumption scaled with fish size: Size-class I stabilized by 15 min; Size-class II by 30 min; Size-class III by 45 min; Size-class IV (largest) never fully stabilized within the 60-min trial.

### Complex Multi-Prey Experiments (Equivocal Results)
- When two pellet sizes were offered simultaneously at varying densities and ratios, size-biased feeding was **only weakly evident** at intermediate densities (150–300 pellets/L) and only when pellet sizes were presented in **equal ratios**.
- Of 20 experiments varying small:large pellet ratios (2:1 to 1:2), only 7 showed bias toward the more abundant size class; 13 showed no clear preference.
- Authors conclude that **prey density, predator density, agonistic behavior among competing fish, and reaction distance** are critical unmodeled variables needed to fully mimic natural feeding dynamics — echoing recommendations from [[Ware 1972]].

### Energetics Implications
- Average *Hyalella* dry weight ≈ 0.49 mg; average stomach content ≈ 4.4 amphipods (~2.0 mg, ≈8.07 cal), based on caloric equivalents from Cummins & Wuycheck (1971).
- Since selective feeders take prey ~1.22× larger than random availability, a **randomly foraging topminnow** would net only ~6.61 cal/day.
- Over a month, size-selective feeding yields a theoretical **22% caloric surplus (~44 cal)** — a potential energetic advantage investable in growth or reproduction.

### Methodological Contribution
- Strong concordance between behavior on **live prey (*Daphnia*)** and **inanimate pellets** validates the use of artificial prey as a proxy in future feeding-selectivity experiments, streamlining experimental design (faster setup, no palatability/escape confounds) — provided artificial prey meets four criteria: size-range availability, uniform shape, suspension stability, and palatability.
