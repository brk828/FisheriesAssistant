# Secondary Production: A Review of Consumer Productivity in Freshwater Ecosystems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #zooplankton

## Executive Summary

This is a review article by W. T. Edmondson, published in the *Mitteilungen der Internationalen Vereinigung für Theoretische und Angewandte Limnologie* (Stuttgart, 1974, vol. 2, pp. 229–272). It surveys the state of knowledge on [[Secondary Production]] (production of consumer animals) in freshwater systems, focusing on invertebrates such as [[Zooplankton]], [[Rotifers]], [[Daphnia]], and benthic insects. Fish are explicitly excluded because of their separate, large literature, though some calculation methods were borrowed from fishery work. The review covers definitions, calculation methods, population dynamics, feeding and assimilation, biomass and chemistry, respiration and excretion, links to primary production, and the role of natural selection. The author argues for scrutinizing model assumptions, evaluating the budget framework, and integrating evolutionary perspectives.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Consumers at all trophic levels that gain nutrition by ingestion (herbivores, carnivores, omnivores). Microbial production (reviewed by Overbeck 1974), algivorous flagellates, and carnivorous plants such as *Utricularia* are excluded.
- **Context:** The review follows the tradition of [[Lindeman]] (1942), whose trophic-dynamic framework emphasized dissipative processes. It builds on work by Teal (1957), Richman (1958), and Slobodkin (1959). The author notes this is his second review for the International Limnological Association, the previous one being in 1961 for the Vienna Congress.
- **Definition used by the International Biological Programme (IBP):** Secondary production is the total of all growth increments of individuals alive at the start of an interval, plus those born during the interval, including any reproductive products released (Winberg 1971).
- **Budget equation:** P = C − F − U − R − G, where P = production, C = consumption, F = egestion, U = excretion, R = respiration, and G = gametes. Assimilation is A = C − F.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Production vs. primary production:** Secondary production has no single measurable chemical process analogous to photosynthesis. Assimilation corresponds to gross production, and production corresponds to net production.
- **Turnover:** Production is not a full measure of demand on the ecosystem. An individual *Daphnia* of 0.015 mg carbon respiring 0.125 µg CO₂/h would use an amount of carbon equal to its body content in under 6 days; over a one-month lifetime, more than five times its body content would be used.
- **Population growth example:** *Keratella cochlearis* grew exponentially for about 6 weeks in [[Lake Glan]] in 1933 (Carlin 1943 data).
- **Ecosystem structure:** Primary production together with allochthonous inputs sets an upper limit on secondary production. In a small spring studied by Teal (1957), secondary production was dominated by leaf fall from surrounding terrestrial vegetation.
- **Eutrophication:** Colonial blue-green algae such as [[Microcystis]] tend to dominate eutrophic lakes. The author suggests this may reflect grazing of smaller algae by zooplankton and colonial algae's resistance to grazing.
- **Lake comparisons:** In [[Severson Lake]] (Minnesota), *Diaptomus siciloides* accounted for about 45% of the production of ten primary consumer species. Rotifer contribution to zooplankton production ranges from 0.5% to 67% across lakes.

## Key Findings

**Methods of calculation**
- Many formulae reduce to a few general types, differing mainly by life-history type and resolution of stages.
- Four categories were identified by Waters & Crawford (1973): the removal-summation method (Boysen Jensen 1919; Teal 1957; Facer 1969), instantaneous growth rate (Ricker), the Allen curve (Mann 1969), and the Hynes method as modified by Hamilton (1969).
- Stepwise summation between widely spaced samples underestimates production. Zawisław (1972) estimated underestimation of 9–85%, but this was criticized by Ivanova (1973).
- Different methods can give production estimates differing by a factor of more than 1.5 from the same data.
- The P/B ratio (turnover) is widely used but poorly standardized; annual and daily bases must be clearly distinguished. Production efficiency (P/A) and the respiration-to-assimilation ratio are also used.

**Worked example (cohort production)**
- A population of 226 individuals/m² with mean weight rising from 0.15 g to 0.32 g in one year, with no change in number, gives production of 38.42 g/m².
- In the more realistic case (numbers falling to 41/m², mean final weight 0.32 g), eliminated biomass was 43.5 g/m² and survivor growth was 20.8 g/m², totalling 64.3 g/m² (from Boysen Jensen 1919).

**Population dynamics and birth/death rates**
- Egg-based methods (Edmondson's method, Edmondson 1968, and Lesile's formula) allow estimation of birth and death rates in planktonic animals that carry eggs.
- Edmondson's method: B = E/D (eggs per female divided by development duration), with instantaneous birth rate "b" = ln(1 + f). This is a lower-bound approximation, which Caswell (1972) showed is improved by Lesile's formula.
- Caswell reanalysed Wright's (1965) *Daphnia* data; the difference between the two birth-rate formulas was less than 10% under typical field conditions.
- Sampling intervals longer than the duration of life or development introduce large errors. In Wright's study, sampling was weekly (about twice the egg development time D).
- Egg development duration depends mainly on temperature (for *Keratella cochlearis*, about 3 days at 10 °C and 1.3 days at 20 °C), and is largely independent of food and water chemistry.

**Feeding and assimilation**
- Many zooplankton are omnivorous; clear herbivore/carnivore categories break down. Examples include *Asplanchna*, which eats small rotifers and larger algae, and *Diaptomus nevadensis*, which eats *D. sicilis*.
- Size spectra of food are central to food limitation. Planktonic animals do not partition food size strictly; bigger filterers also consume small particles (Gliwicz 1969).
- Blue-green algae are generally assimilated less well than other algae. Arnold (1971) found that all seven blue-green species tested were assimilated less by *Daphnia pulex* and supported less reproduction and longevity than *Ankistrodesmus*.
- Some algae pass intact through zooplankton guts; a gelatinous sheath may protect against digestion (Porter 1973).
- Feeding rates depend on temperature, body size, species, and possibly diel rhythm. Rotifers clear at about 0.1 ml/day per individual; *Daphnia* species range from about 1 to 250 ml/day.

**Biomass and chemistry**
- Weights of small organisms are often approximate, derived from volume or length relationships.
- Formalin-fixed material can have dry weight only about half that of fresh material in at least one case (Omori 1970).
- Potential biochemical indices (ATP, DNA, RNA, ETS, dehydrogenases) are discussed as proxies for biomass or activity. Each has limitations (e.g., DNA is not labile enough in detritus).
- Lipid and carbohydrate ratios may indicate nutritional state. Lipid dominated metabolism under low temperature and food, carbohydrate under high conditions (Stearns & Armitage 1968).

**Respiration and excretion**
- Excretion of soluble organic compounds has been recognized only recently. Many energy budgets omit it or combine it with egestion.
- Indirect assimilation calculated as production plus respiration can underestimate assimilation by a factor of 0.64 for *Hyalella* (Hargrave 1971).
- Moult production in arthropods is often omitted from budgets and can be a significant fraction of biomass.
- Zooplankton can release phosphate at rates large relative to phytoplankton requirements (Bartlow & Bishop 1965; Hargrave & Geen 1968).

**Natural selection and life history**
- Most production research has focused on proximate mechanisms; the author argues evolutionary and selective perspectives have been largely neglected.
- Phytoplankton face a trade-off: small size improves nutrient uptake but increases grazing risk. A decrease in spherical cell diameter from 10 µm to 2 µm could nearly double the grazing loss rate (Gliwicz 1970).
- Life-history variation among copepods (*Epischura*, *Diaptomus*, *Cyclops*) is related to environmental predictability. Shortening the duration of immaturity has a greater effect on population growth than lengthening the reproductive period (Cole 1954).

**Recommendations for future work**
- Scrutinize the assumptions of all current birth and death rate methods and evaluate their effects when violated.
- Involve mathematicians who understand biological processes.
- Conduct kinetic studies of feeding and assimilation as a function of temperature, since generalized equations are risky given physiological and genetic adaptation.
- Test whether colonial algae dominate eutrophic lakes because they resist grazing, using laboratory experiments on colonies of different sizes.
- Find a common unit (the calorie is not always appropriate) to express gains and losses in cost–benefit analyses of evolutionary strategies.

**Note on fish:** Fish are excluded from the review because of their large separate literature. This is a methodological boundary, not a finding about fish.
