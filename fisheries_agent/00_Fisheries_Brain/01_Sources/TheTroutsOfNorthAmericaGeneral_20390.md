# The Trouts of North America: General Remarks on Classification

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service leaflet (reissued March 1955) by [[Samuel F. Hildebrand]], senior ichthyologist of the Service, reviews the classification of North American trouts. The author states that the group is exceptionally difficult to classify, that ichthyologists rarely agree on which forms are species and which are subspecies, varieties or races, and that the problem is compounded by extinction, hybridization through artificial culture and inadequate early descriptions. Because of this, the leaflet does not provide keys or full descriptions. It instead lists the usually recognized names, with original citations and natural distributions, arranged in two genera: [[Salmo]] and [[Salvelinus]]. It also summarizes trout biology, including habitat, diet, spawning, growth, size, coloration and game value.

## Background & Description

The leaflet draws heavily on [[John O. Snyder]]'s work on the trouts of California (1940), which the author quotes at length. Snyder described the trout complex as "a huge mosaic" of geographic races that resist logical treatment.

**Taxonomic problems**
- The [[Steelhead]] was long considered distinct from the [[Rainbow Trout]]. Snyder concluded that a steelhead is a sea-migrant form of whichever species inhabits the stream (either a cutthroat or a rainbow), and that it does not warrant a separate binomial.
- The coast rainbow was long called *Salmo gairdneri*, but Snyder used *Salmo irideus* because the original description of *S. gairdneri* may have been based on a salmon.
- Identification by keys is considered impractical. Traits such as head shape, dentition, throat mark, squamation and habits are not reliable on their own.

**Distinguishing the family and genera**
- Salmonids are soft-rayed teleosts, with no spines in the fins. They have a small fleshy adipose fin on the back.
- Trout are separated from Pacific salmon by the anal fin: trout have 12 or fewer rays, and Pacific salmon have 13 to 16 or more.
- *Salmo* has black spots, moderately large scales (generally fewer than 200 in a lateral series) and a more fully toothed vomer.
- *Salvelinus* (the charrs) has red spots, white edgings on the lower fins, and very small scales (about 200 to 350 in a lateral series) that are often embedded in adults.

**Series within Salmo (western North America)**
- The **rainbow series** has larger scales (generally about 140 to 160), scattered black spots, and no red streak beneath the mandible.
- The **cutthroat series** has smaller scales, more numerous black spots, and a red streak beneath the mandible, which is the most reliable separating character.

**Atlantic salmon group**
- *Salmo salar* has little-developed vomerine teeth and large scales (generally fewer than 120 in a lateral series). Although it is called a salmon, it is classified as a trout.
- [[William C. Kendall]] (1935) recognized only one species of landlocked form, *S. sebago* (the [[Landlocked Salmon]]). He separated it from the Atlantic salmon by the proportional depth of the caudal peduncle, and he noted that the differences are averages only.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat**
- Clear, cool streams and lakes with water below 70 °F are the chief habitat of trout.
- Some species, including the Atlantic salmon, coast rainbow and coast cutthroat, go to sea for part of their lives. The [[Brook Trout]] and [[Dolly Varden Trout]] do so only in the northern parts of their ranges. Others remain entirely in fresh water, either because they are landlocked or by choice.

**Threats**
- Human-made barriers in streams block the migration of sea-run species.
- Pollution of streams and lakes has caused declines and, in some waters, local extermination.
- Introduced species have displaced native forms. For example, the [[San Gorgonio Trout]] may have become extinct after invasion by rainbow and brown trout.
- Hybridization through artificial culture has blurred the identity of some native stocks, such as the [[Shasta Trout]] and coast rainbow, and possibly the [[Lock Leven Trout]] and brown trout.

**Diet**
- Trout are carnivorous. They feed mainly on adult and larval insects, and also on fish eggs and young fish. They take food from the bottom to the surface.

**Spawning and early life**
- Trout spawn mainly in autumn or winter, on gravelly beds in swiftly flowing streams.
- Eggs are relatively large and few in number. Incubation takes about six weeks, and it is lengthened by low temperatures and shortened by high ones.
- Emerging young carry a large yolk-sac. Once the yolk is absorbed, they must find suitable food, and this is described as a critical stage.

**Growth and age**
- Growth is rapid when food is abundant and conditions are favorable, and it is stunted otherwise.
- Age is determined from scale annuli. Widely spaced rings indicate rapid growth, and the crowded winter ring forms the annulus.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomy is unresolved.** Trout are among the hardest North American fishes to classify. Many nominal species are probably only local races or color phases. The leaflet therefore gives names without keys or descriptions.
- **Two genera are recognized.** *Salmo* (rainbow, cutthroat, steelhead, Atlantic salmon, landlocked salmon, brown trout) and *Salvelinus* (brook trout, Dolly Varden, Greenland charr, lake trout) differ in spot color, scale size and vomerine teeth.
- **Name inventory.** The leaflet lists roughly 12 nominal forms in the rainbow series and about 25 in the cutthroat series, counting the related forms. Most are regional races or synonyms. Examples are the golden trout (*S. aquabonita*), Lake Tahoe cutthroat (*S. henshawi*), Yellowstone trout (*S. lewisi*), Rio Grande trout (*S. virginalis*) and Kern River trout (*S. gilberti*).
- **Atlantic salmon status.** The Atlantic salmon is depleted in the United States and now rarely reaches as far south as Cape Cod. Its original range extended to Delaware.
- **Brook trout forms.** Several named brook trout forms are considered probable subspecies or varieties. The species ranges from Saskatchewan and Labrador south to the headwaters of the Savannah River.
- **Lake trout (Mackinaw trout).** This species is readily recognized by its gray spots, lack of bright colors and deeply forked tail. It occurs in the Great Lakes, northern New York to Maine, and northward to the Arctic Circle.
- **Size records.** Brook and Dolly Varden trout usually run 12 to 15 inches. Reported maxima include Atlantic salmon of 70 to 100 pounds, Great Lakes trout of 50 to 80 pounds, rainbows over 20 pounds, and cutthroats of about 40 pounds. The author notes that the average angler catch is much smaller than these records.
- **Management relevance.** All trout are game fish and, except for the Great Lakes trout and Dolly Varden, are protected in United States waters. Their propagation and protection are mainly for sport fishing.
- **Sources.** The leaflet cites [[Kendall]] (1935), [[Needham]] (1938), [[Snyder]] (1940), [[Davis]] (1953) and [[Shapovalov and Taft]] (1954) as key references.
