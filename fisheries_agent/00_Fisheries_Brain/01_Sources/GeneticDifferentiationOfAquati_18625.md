# Genetic Differentiation of Artesian Spring Snails (Hydrobiidae) in Arid Australia

#marine-biology #hydrobiidae #gastropoda #freshwater-molluscs #population-genetics

## Executive Summary

This study applies allozyme electrophoresis to hydrobiid snails inhabiting [[Mound Springs]] of the [[Lake Eyre Supergroup]] in northern South Australia, part of the [[Great Artesian Basin]]. Samples comprised 96 populations from 32 springs in 18 spring groups, collected in April–May 1990 over a north–south extent of 255 km. The authors test the morphology-based species taxonomy of two indigenous genera, [[Fonscochlea]] and [[Trochidrobia]], assess genetic differentiation among isolated springs, and examine how distance and habitat type (mound type and spring size) shape it. The authors conclude that genetic structure largely follows an isolation-by-distance pattern, that small springs are more differentiated, and that protecting single springs is insufficient to conserve genetic diversity.

## Background & Description

The springs are small (often tens to a few square metres), shallow, flowing systems that support large animal numbers and are thought to be old, with a partly relictual fauna. Two main spring areas occur in South Australia: [[Dalhousie Springs]] in the far north, and the Lake Eyre Supergroup, a series of smaller springs extending between Oodnadatta and Marree. The hydrobiid fauna of the Lake Eyre springs comprises two indigenous genera, previously known from morphology alone to contain ten species.

The ten morphospecies were grouped into five morphologically discrete sets for analysis:
- Two large fully aquatic species: *F. aquatica* (up to 4.8 mm) and *F. accepta* (up to 3.8 mm).
- Three small aquatic species: *F. variabilis* (up to 2.8 mm), *F. billakalina* (up to 2.4 mm) and *F. expandolabra* sp. nov. (up to 3.5 mm).
- One amphibious species: *F. zeidleri* (up to 5.3 mm).
- Four *Trochidrobia* species (*T. punicea*, *T. smithi*, *T. minuta*, *T. inflata*), analysed together, with *T. punicea* examined separately.

Methods included horizontal starch-gel-free cellulose acetate electrophoresis of 21 enzymes (18–26 loci depending on group), analysed with BIOSYS-1 phenetic trees (Nei unbiased and Rogers' modified distances), PAUP cladistic analysis, and regression of genetic distance on geographic distance using a standardized statistic (LDG). Habitat was characterized by outflow length and width (habitat area), mound height and type (hard calcrete versus soft sand).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Setting:** Evaporation exceeds precipitation by a factor of 10 or more. Springs are fed by natural discharge from the Great Artesian Basin, in contrast to the rainfall-dependent streams of Wilsons Promontory, Victoria, studied previously for comparison.
- **Spatial structure:** Springs are arranged in an essentially linear pattern, with drainage at right angles to the spring line, so floods are unlikely to carry snails between spring groups.
- **Dispersal:** The authors judge accidental transport, mainly by birds, to be the most important dispersal route. Dispersal capacity varies: *Potamopyrgus* is highly dispersive, whereas ovoparous sexually reproducing hydrobiids in small forest streams disperse poorly.
- **Mound types:** Hard calcrete mounds (e.g. [[Blanche Cup Group]]) are long-lived and stable; soft sand mounds (e.g. [[Hermit Hill Group]]) are transitory.
- **Threats:** All springs lie on pastoral leases. Stock and feral animal damage, drawdown from increasing artesian water extraction, and fencing programs are cited as pressures, particularly on [[Strangways Springs]] and [[Billa Kalina Springs]].

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic validation**
- Ten morphospecies were examined. Most morphology-based taxa were broadly validated, with some revisions.
- *F. variabilis* and *F. conica* are genetically weakly differentiated (D = 0.043) and are treated as conspecific under *F. variabilis*.
- The "form B" populations from the northern springs and Freeling Springs are genetically distinct (D = 0.107 from typical *F. variabilis*). They are described as the new species *F. expandolabra* sp. nov., with the Freeling Springs populations regarded as conspecific with it.
- *F. billakalina* is confirmed as a separate species, with seven or more fixed allozyme differences from its congeners.
- Among *Trochidrobia*, species-level taxa are genetically discrete. *T. punicea* differs from *T. smithi* at D = 0.887, and the species-group distances in the study are large.
- *T. punicea* and *T. smithi* are sympatric for the first time, at [[Coward Springs]] (CS19) and [[Julie Springs]] (JS28). Sympatry was confirmed by allozyme differences, with genital dissection confirming *T. smithi* at BC14.

**Genetic structure and distance**
- Differentiation largely follows an isolation-by-distance model. Regression of genetic distance on log geographic distance was significant (P < 0.05 to P < 0.001) for six taxa, with r² ranging from 0.10 (*F. accepta*) to 0.73 (*F. zeidleri*).
- Genetic discontinuity is most marked between [[Beresford Springs]] and [[Strangways Springs]] (about 8.5 km apart). The authors attribute this to differing historical continuity of spring habitat, with Strangways having been active over a long period.
- Rogers' (modified) distances between Beresford and Warburton Springs are as low as 0.053, whereas within-group distances at Kewson Hill reach 0.373.
- *F. accepta* shows high gene flow, with Nm between spring groups greater than two, while *F. aquatica* has a value 25 times lower.

**Habitat effects**
- Small springs show greater genetic differentiation than large springs. Combined spring area in pairs within 10 km is strongly correlated with reduced differentiation (P < 0.001).
- The smallest size category (<200 m²) differed significantly from the two larger categories (Bonferroni P < 0.01).
- Mound type had no significant effect within spring groups when tested across taxa (P > 0.01).

**Management recommendations**
- Protecting individual springs, as in the earlier fencing program, conserves only part of the genetic variability within a spring group. That program also favoured large springs, whereas the greatest differentiation lies among small springs.
- Maintaining a subset of spring groups retains only part of the system's genetic variability.
- The authors argue that as many springs as possible should be maintained, that National Park or conservation-area protection is the most desirable goal, and that it is pointless if GAB extraction reduces or stops spring flow.
- Describing the springs as remaining "entirely within marginally economic pastoral leases," the authors consider this a national conservation concern.

**Study scope**
- Sampling covered 96 populations from 32 springs in 18 spring groups. Most loci were monomorphic in several taxa, and some genetic distances were based on small sample sizes (e.g. only 3–6 samples for some taxa).
- Appendices list sampled springs (Appendix 1) and allele frequency tables (Appendix 2.1–2.6), which are summarized here rather than reproduced.
