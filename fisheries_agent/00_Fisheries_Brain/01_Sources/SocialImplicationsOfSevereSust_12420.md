# Social Implications of Severe Sustained Drought: Case Studies in California and Colorado

#hydrology #social-science #drought-policy

## Executive Summary

This Water Resources Bulletin paper (Krannich, Keenan, Walker, and Hardesty, October 1995) reports household surveys in two water-dependent communities: the [[Grand Valley]] in western Colorado and [[Kern County]] in California's [[San Joaquin Valley]]. The study examines how residents perceive a hypothetical [[Severe Sustained Drought]] of about 20 years, which would be more severe than any drought previously recorded and is linked to tree-ring reconstructions of [[Colorado River Basin]] flows. Residents of both areas believe such a drought is likely within 20-25 years and that it would seriously affect their communities. However, they show little support for management strategies that would depart from "business as usual," such as mandatory conservation, water marketing, or reallocation from agriculture to cities. The authors conclude that this resistance severely limits the capacity of these communities to respond effectively.

## Background & Description

The paper argues that human social systems are closely tied to ecological systems and are highly vulnerable to environmental change that is poorly anticipated or rapid (Duncan, 1961). Existing water systems can usually cope with short-term scarcity, but conditions that are extreme and long-lasting could exceed the response capacity of organizations and social structures. Even ordinary droughts have produced mixed results for conservation programs (Hamilton, 1985; Howe et al., 1980).

The authors propose that water users are not a single community. They form distinct "ecological niches" with different dependence on and vulnerability to water (Hardesty, 1977). Effects of drought are therefore unevenly distributed, and any assessment must account for this variation.

**Study areas**

- **Grand Valley (Grand Junction, Colorado):** relies mainly on main-stem [[Colorado River Basin]] water for agricultural, industrial, and municipal use. Water diversions to Colorado's east-slope metropolitan areas have made water rights a frequent public issue.
- **Kern County (Bakersfield, California):** relies on a complex system of imported surface water from the Sierra Nevada and northern California, plus heavy [[Groundwater Pumping]]. It is only indirectly linked to Colorado River conditions.

**Survey methods**

- **Kern County:** a multi-wave mail survey of a probability sample of 1,053 households in early 1992. A total of 618 usable questionnaires were returned, a 59% response rate.
- **Grand Valley:** a personalized drop-off/pick-up survey of a probability sample of 200 households drawn from Mesa County assessor listings. A total of 147 surveys were returned, a 74% response rate.

Analysis compared perceptions across the two areas and used [[Ordinary Least-Squares Regression]] to identify which sociodemographic and attitudinal factors predicted responses.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses water supply and drought rather than aquatic organisms or habitats. Its environmental context covers the 1986-1992 western drought and the hydrology of the [[Colorado River Basin]]:

- **Grand Valley:** main-stem and most tributary supplies stayed adequate, so area-wide shortages did not occur. Some outlying areas that depended on small tributaries did face shortages.
- **Kern County:** surface water imports were severely curtailed, and groundwater reserves were drawn down. Municipal and industrial surface allocations were cut by as much as 70%. Agricultural users saw reductions of up to 100% of normal irrigation allocations. Purchasing replacement water from northern California was prohibitive for most farmers.
- **Hydrologic basis:** the hypothetical scenario comes from [[Tree-Ring Hydrology]] reconstructions of prehistoric Colorado River flows (Tarboton, 1993), which suggest that long droughts have occurred in the Southwest in the distant past.

## Key Findings

**1. Perceived seriousness of recent drought differed by area.**
- In Kern County, about 75% of responses fell in the "serious" range (6-10), with a mean of 7.2.
- In Grand Valley, the mean was 4.5, and only 30% of responses were above the midpoint.

**2. Household impacts from the recent drought were modest in both areas.**
- In Grand Valley, 9% of responses were in the "serious" range, with a mean of 1.8.
- In Kern County, the mean was 3.8, and 27% of responses were in the 6-10 range.
- The authors attribute this to [[Water Storage Buffering]]: Colorado River reservoirs in Grand Valley and groundwater reserves in Kern County absorbed the shock, so most users experienced limited inconvenience.

**3. Residents believe a severe sustained drought is likely in the long term.**
- The hypothetical scenario described an uninterrupted drought of about 20 years, with community water systems able to supply only half of normal use.
- Most respondents in both areas considered it only moderately likely within five years.
- A majority considered it highly likely within 20-25 years.
- The regression models explained very little of this perception (R² = .079 in Grand Valley and .064 in Kern County). The strongest predictor in both areas was the perceived seriousness of recent scarcity.

**4. Vulnerability concerns were higher in Kern County.**
- Mean concern about personal financial loss was 4.9 in Grand Valley and 6.1 in Kern County.
- Kern County respondents were also more likely to expect "very serious" effects on local economic opportunities. The authors link this to the importance of [[Agricultural Irrigation]] to the Kern County economy.
- Personal vulnerability was best predicted by occupation (agriculture), household size (Grand Valley), and perceived likelihood of future drought. Area economic vulnerability was predicted mainly by perceived seriousness of recent scarcity and perceived likelihood of future drought.

**5. Water priorities favor existing users.**
- Highest priority went to irrigation for permanent crops such as fruit trees and vineyards.
- Existing residential households and nonpermanent crop irrigators were also rated high priority.
- New residential developments and recreational uses (boating, fishing) were rated low priority in both areas.

**6. Support for "business as usual" strategies was highest.**
- The three most acceptable strategies in both areas were voluntary education and conservation programs, [[Water-Saving Irrigation Technologies]], and construction of new storage and delivery systems.
- Residents were ambivalent or opposed to strategies with personal or area-wide costs, including:
  - [[Growth Limitations]] and construction moratoria
  - Mandatory conservation enforced by fines
  - Within-state and across-state transfers from low- to high-population areas
  - [[Water Marketing]]
  - Legislated reallocation from agricultural to municipal and industrial uses
- Grand Valley respondents were strongly opposed to transfers to the east slope. Kern County responses on transfers were more evenly divided, reflecting its dependence on northern imports and exposure to Los Angeles demand.
- Surprisingly, growth limitations drew considerable support in both areas.

**7. Acceptance of radical strategies varied by respondent characteristics, but explanatory power was low.**
- Growth controls were more accepted by men, homeowners, those with post-high school education, lower-income households, and those who expected severe drought.
- Support for mandatory conservation was linked to shorter residence, homeownership, and larger households in Grand Valley.
- Support for water marketing had very weak explanatory power in both areas.
- Support for legislated reallocation was generally low and poorly explained by the variables measured.
- No strategy achieved consensus among residents.

**8. Authors' conclusions: response capacity is limited.**
- Perceived vulnerability does not translate into support for changes in water management. Recent success in avoiding major disruption may reduce readiness for a severe sustained drought.
- The authors expect that radical responses would arrive too late, after emergency conditions had already developed.
- They anticipate widespread economic dislocation, population shifts (slower growth and eventual outmigration), lifestyle changes, reduced well-being, and social and political conflict over scarce water.
- They suggest that educational efforts about vulnerability might build better response capacity, but they note that residents have no historical experience with drought of this scale.
