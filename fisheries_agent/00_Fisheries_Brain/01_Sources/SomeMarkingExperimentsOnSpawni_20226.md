# Marking Experiments on Spawning Populations of Char

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salvelinus-willughbii

## Executive Summary

This paper by E. D. Le Cren and C. Kipling, from the [[Freshwater Biological Association]], reports mark-recapture experiments on spawning populations of [[Windermere Char]] (*[[Salvelinus Willughbii]]*). The work supported the wider biological study of the species by [[Winifred Frost]]. The experiments addressed two questions: how fish move on and off spawning grounds within a season, and how population size, sex ratio and mortality change between years. Marking used [[Fin Clipping]] and [[Fin Punching]] for within-season work, and [[Subcutaneous Tagging]] with fin amputation for multi-year work. The authors conclude that the methods gave satisfactory population parameters that would have been hard to obtain otherwise, although small sample sizes limited precision.

## Background & Description

Windermere char form two distinct taxa, or "races":

- **Autumn spawners** grow more slowly. They spawn in November in about 1 m of water on the lake shore or in rivers.
- **Spring spawners** grow faster. They spawn in February on the lake bottom at about 20 m depth.

Both races spawn on distinct, limited, gravelly areas. Individuals return to the same spawning site with high consistency, so each site appears to hold a discrete spawning population. Fish are easy to catch on spawning grounds but are pelagic and hard to sample for the rest of the year.

Four spawning sites were studied (Table 1):

| Site | Water depth | Season | Gear |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Brathay River]] | Shallow (river) | Autumn | [[Seine Netting]] |
| [[Holbeck]] | Deep | Spring | Seine netting |
| [[Red Nab]] | Shallow | Autumn | Seine netting |
| [[Low Wray Bay]] | Shallow | Autumn | [[Gill Netting]] |

Low Wray Bay had no within-season study.

Gear details:
- The seine was 50 yd (45.7 m) long and 3.7 to 4.6 m deep, with mesh graded from 2.54 cm bar on the wings to 0.95 cm at the bag. It was always worked from the shore.
- The gill net was 27.4 m long, with 3.17 cm bar mesh. It was set on two consecutive nights each week.

Site-specific sampling problems:
- At Brathay, netting was impossible in flood.
- At Red Nab, seining succeeded only at dusk or after dark.
- At Holbeck, the net had to be hauled up a steep bank from deep water and could snag on obstructions.

Anglers were co-operative and returned marked fish for a reward based on market price.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Movement and sex-specific behaviour.** Males arrived early in the season and stayed for most of it. Females arrived in small groups throughout the season, stayed a short time, and left. At Red Nab, fish also moved diurnally: they entered the spawning area at dusk, males earlier than females, and none were present by day. The sexes differed less clearly at Holbeck than at Brathay.

**Sampling design implications.** The authors suggest weekly nettings would adequately sample males at Brathay and Holbeck, and females at Holbeck. Brathay females would need more frequent visits. At Red Nab, late-afternoon weekly nets would suffice for males, but females would need more frequent night-time netting.

**Recruitment.** Recruitment appeared erratic, with an abundance of small adults only every few years. Table 3 shows strong year-to-year variation; for example, the Brathay recruit percentage was 7% of males and 8% of females in 1950–51.

**Population trends.** The Brathay spawning population was much smaller than the others and declined between 1950 and 1951. A similar decline occurred at Red Nab. The authors linked this to the low recruitment in that period.

**Mortality and survival.** Male annual survival varied at least from about 25% to 60%, and female survival was higher than male survival. Mortality from angling appeared very small.

## Key Findings

**1. Within-season marking methods**
- Fin clips and leather-punched holes (about 4 mm diameter) formed a unique mark for each netting occasion.
- Punch holes gave clear short-term marks but tended to fill in after about six weeks, so they could not be used reliably for longer experiments.
- Marks were judged to have no adverse effects. Marked fish were no more or less catchable than unmarked fish, and seining was considered a random sample.
- Some recapture records contained errors, but the effect on results was negligible. Double-checking at recapture was recommended for future work.

**2. Within-season effort and results**
- Brathay (1952): nine seinings, 31 October to 25 November.
- Holbeck (1953): seventeen seinings, 7 January to 17 March. This was the maximum number of visits in any season.
- Red Nab (1952): ten seinings, 13 November to 8 December.

Table 4 summarises the within-season marking results:

| Site | Males marked | Females marked | Males recaptured ≥1 | Females recaptured ≥1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brathay | 36 | 87 | 69% | 37% |
| Holbeck | 207 | 378 | 39% | 29% |
| Red Nab | 140 | 160 | 11% | <1% |

- Mean duration of stay (fish recaptured at least twice): Brathay males 12.6 days, females 3.8 days. Holbeck males 11.4 days, females 9.1 days.
- Of the nine Brathay males caught on 21 and 25 November, six had been present since at least 3 November.
- Brathay females were never recaptured more than twice.

**3. Netting efficiency**
- Estimated efficiency of a single seine, based on males recaptured at the next netting:
  - Brathay: 50%
  - Holbeck: 15%
  - Red Nab: 2%
- Assuming fish recaptured two or more times were continuously present, Brathay male efficiency was estimated at 72%. The authors regarded this as an overestimate.
- These efficiency values are underestimates for the next-netting method, because some fish emigrated between nettings.

**4. Migration and immigration analysis**
- The [[Bailey Triple-Catch Method]] and more advanced trellis analyses were not applicable. Population size and migration rates changed too much, and the numbers were too small. Standard errors were very large.
- Simple methods (mean stay, number of recaptures, and recapture distributions) gave a usable picture of migration patterns.

**5. Year-to-year tagging**
- Fish were tagged subcutaneously and given a fin cut at Brathay and Red Nab (1950 and 1951), Holbeck (1951 and 1952), and Low Wray Bay (1950).
- Tag loss was assumed to be 20%, based on [[Le Cren 1954]].
- The longest interval between tagging and recapture was five years.
- Only Brathay and Red Nab had enough tagging in two successive years to apply the triple-catch method.

**6. Population estimates (winter 1951–1952)**
- Three autumn spawning sites (Brathay, Red Nab and Low Wray Bay, assumed equal): approximately 2,000 males and 9,000 females.
- Spring spawning site (Holbeck): approximately 650 males and 1,300 females.
- The population was larger in the previous year.
- These figures cover only mature fish from some north-basin sites. The total population of [[Windermere]] was not estimated.
- The Holbeck estimates from two-year recaptures and double recaptures were judged too high, probably because recruitment was not adequately separated, especially for males, which grow faster.
- Extrapolation estimates at Red Nab were too low and unreliable.

**7. Mortality and survival**
- Instantaneous mortality (Z) was calculated with the [[Holt-Spicer Method]].
- At Holbeck, male Z was 1.30 and female Z was 0.78. The difference was significant.
- Other values (Table 7): Brathay males 0.69 (1951–52); Red Nab males 0.55 (1951–52) and 0.99 (1952–53).
- Annual survival ranged from 0.27 to 0.58 across the table.
- The two lowest rates were for 1951–52, and the highest for the period after 1953. The authors suggested mortality may increase with age.

**8. Sex ratios**
- Brathay: 3.9 to 7.4 females per male.
- Red Nab: 4.9 females per male.
- Low Wray Bay: 4.3 females per male.
- Holbeck: 1.8 to 2.1 females per male.
- Autumn spawners therefore had about four females per male, against about two at the spring site.
- Catch-based sex ratios were judged misleading because of sex-specific movement.

**9. Angler returns**
- Twenty marked fish were returned by anglers, five of which had lost their tags but were identified by fin cuts. Two more were reported with incomplete information.
- The recovery rate was 1.1% of the 1,952 fish tagged. This is the figure given in Table 10, which also lists two unidentified returns.
- Returns were similar across the three sites, so fish from each site appeared equally vulnerable to angling.
- Angling mortality appeared very small.

**10. Conclusions**
- The fin-clip and punch method was adequate for describing migration behaviour.
- Subcutaneous tags with fin cuts were satisfactory for year-to-year work, and fin cuts were essential for identifying tagged fish and detecting double recaptures.
- Small sample sizes limited precision. At Holbeck in 1953, 22% of males and 25% of females of the estimated population were marked. At Brathay in 1951, 49% of males and 21% of females were marked.
- The authors recommended tagging Holbeck in 1953 and sufficient numbers in 1951 to obtain data from two successive years. They also noted that the low female recapture rates at Brathay and Red Nab could not have been foreseen.
- Overall, the experiments provided population parameters that would have been difficult to obtain by other methods.
