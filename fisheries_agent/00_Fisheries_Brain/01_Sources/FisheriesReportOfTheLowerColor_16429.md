# Fisheries Report of the Lower Colorado River (1951)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #river-ecology #multi-species-assemblage

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive 1951 report by the Fisheries Task Group of the Colorado River Great Basin Field Committee documents the fishery resources of the Lower Colorado River from [[Hoover Dam]] to the Mexican border, covering approximately 250 river miles across Arizona, Nevada, and California. The report divides the river into seven distinct sections, each assessed for physical characteristics, biological inventory, current use, and management recommendations. The fishery is characterized as diversified, supporting both cold-water trout populations in upper reservoir sections and warm-water species (largemouth bass, bluegill, channel catfish, crappie) throughout most of the system. Field surveys conducted during 1950-1951 reveal that the fishery is generally underutilized relative to its potential, with 68,235 fishermen visiting [[Lake Mohave]] in 1950 alone. The report emphasizes that severe drought conditions throughout the Southwest have made the Lower Colorado River "the last frontier" for resident freshwater anglers, with recreational use increasing year over year.

## Background & Description

The Lower Colorado River system in 1951 comprised newly formed reservoirs ([[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Havasu]]), free-flowing river sections, and extensive backwater sloughs and oxbow lakes. The report documents 18 game and non-game fish species, including introduced species such as [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigro-maculatus*), [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Redear Sunfish]] (*Lepomis microlophus*), [[Brown Bullhead]] (*Ameriurus nebulosus*), [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameriurus natalis*), and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus lacustris*). Native species include [[Bonytail]] (*Gila robusta elegans*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Non-native [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) are abundant throughout. Marine species [[Striped Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*) and [[Ten Pounder]] (*Elops affinis*) occur in the lower sections near the Gulf of California.

The physical environment varies dramatically by section. [[Lake Mohave]] (67.5 miles long, maximum elevation 647 feet) is a canyon-type reservoir with cold water (53-56°F) released from [[Hoover Dam]]'s lower outlets, supporting rainbow trout in upper sections while largemouth bass dominate lower areas. Temperature stratification creates natural separation: trout occupy waters above Cave Rapids/Emery's Landing where temperatures remain below 68°F, while bass thrive in warmer lower lake areas reaching 80-85°F in summer. [[Lake Havasu]] (25,000 surface acres, 46 miles long) is a warm-water reservoir behind [[Parker Dam]] with highly irregular shoreline, numerous coves, and depths averaging 22 feet (maximum 75 feet at dam). Water temperatures range from 45°F (January) to 90°F (July). Free-flowing river sections exhibit shifting sand bottoms, high turbidity, limited aquatic vegetation, and daily water fluctuations of 500-5,000 cubic feet per second depending on dam operations.

Aquatic vegetation is critical to fishery productivity. The report documents [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), [[Spiny Naiad]] (*Najas marina*), [[Widgeon Grass]] (*Ruppia maritima*), and [[Horned Pondweed]] (*Zannichellia* sp.) as key submerged species providing cover and food base. Emergent vegetation includes [[Cattail]] (*Typha domingensis*), [[Rush]] (*Juncus torreyi*), [[Tule]] (*Scirpus californicus*), and [[Spike Rush]] (*Eleocharis parishii*). Riparian zones support willow, cottonwood, salt-cedar, arrowweed, and mesquite. The report notes that [[Lake Havasu]]'s aquatic vegetation declined dramatically around 1946-1947, with spiny naiad nearly disappearing from coves, correlating with reduced bass fishing success.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Lower Colorado River system in 1951 was undergoing rapid ecological transformation due to dam construction and water management. [[Davis Dam]] (completed 1950) created [[Lake Mohave]], flooding former river habitat and fundamentally altering thermal regimes, flow patterns, and fish community structure. The report documents successful rainbow trout spawning in the mile below [[Hoover Dam]], with observations of redds (spawning nests) and large trout up to 18.5 pounds. Trout spawning season runs December-February, with the area closed to fishing during this period. Growth rates of rainbow trout decreased during lake filling but were expected to recover as aquatic vegetation and invertebrate populations (freshwater shrimp *Gammarus fasciatus*, snails *Limnaea* sp., aquatic insects) became established.

The warm-water fishery exhibits classic post-impoundment dynamics. [[Lake Havasu]] experienced excellent largemouth bass fishing in the years immediately following [[Parker Dam]] closure (1938), with catches declining by 1950-1951 to 0.37 bass per angler-day. The report attributes this decline to reduced fertility and loss of aquatic vegetation cover, resulting in fewer forage-size bluegill and green sunfish available for bass and crappie growth. Creel census data from Lake Havasu (1,035 anglers checked over nine months) showed: 386 largemouth bass, 246 channel catfish, 1,758 bluegill, and 88 black crappie caught, for a total harvest of 0.91 pounds per acre per year—considered low productivity. The lake received one angler-day per acre per year, considered light use.

Water quality varies substantially. Upper sections receive cold, clear water from [[Hoover Dam]]'s hypolimnetic releases. Turbidity increases downstream due to suspended sand and silt, reaching 150 parts per million (platinum wire standard) in [[Palo Verde Drain]]. Section VII (Imperial Dam to Mexican border) suffers severe water quality degradation: raw sewage from Yuma, Arizona increases coliform bacteria over 1,500 times; irrigation return flows add fertilizers but also dissolved salts; [[Mittry Lake]] experiences salt concentrations from 600 to 10,000 ppm due to evaporation (exceeding 10 feet per year), far beyond fish tolerance levels.

Dam operations significantly impact fish populations. [[Parker Dam]] releases vary from 11,000 to 20,000 cubic feet per second seasonally, with daily fluctuations of 500-3,500 cfs. The report recommends limiting water level fluctuations to less than two feet during bass spawning (March 15-May 15) and bluegill spawning (June 1-August 30). [[Laguna Dam]] flushing operations (Thursday mornings) cause four-foot water drops, stranding thousands of fish including large spawning channel catfish (some over 25 pounds). Observers reported returning over 3,000 catfish (6-8 inches) to water on single occasions. Dams also block fish migration: [[Parker Dam]] is a complete barrier; [[Headgate Rock Dam]] and [[Imperial Dam]] are partial barriers.

Food web structure differs markedly between cold and warm-water sections. Trout waters support plankton-crustaceans, small insects, *Gammarus* shrimp, and snails, though the sterile sand bottom limits larger food organisms. Warm-water sections with established vegetation support more diverse invertebrate communities. The report notes carp are abundant throughout and serve as forage for young bass. Native species (bonytail, razorback sucker, Colorado squawfish) are present but declining; bonytail and razorback sucker are "very common" in upper sections, while Colorado squawfish are "very rare" with last capture in 1949. The report expresses concern about potential introduction of [[Utah Chub]] (*Gila atraria*), described as "very predacious upon the young of game fish" and capable of destroying sport fisheries.

## Key Findings

**Section I - Lake Mohave (Hoover Dam to Davis Dam, 67.5 miles):**
- 68,235 fishermen in 1950; 47,554 in first five months of 1951 (61% increase)
- Creel census (825 anglers, six months): 3.25 fish per fisherman, 1.1 fish per hour, average weight 1 pound; over 95% rainbow trout
- 80% of vehicles from California, 15% Arizona/Nevada combined, 5% other states
- Record rainbow: 18 pounds (1950), 18.5 pounds (1951 to date)
- Successful trout spawning documented below Hoover Dam; spawning area closed December-February
- Temperature stratification creates natural species separation: trout above Cave Rapids/Emery's Landing (water <68°F), bass in lower lake (surface temperatures 80-85°F in August)
- Largemouth bass had "very successful spawn" April-May 1951; population expected to provide "some of the best bass fishing in the Southwest"
- Four access points: Emery's Landing, Cottonwood Landing, Willow Beach, Katherine Wash (under development)
- Arizona-Nevada reciprocal Special Use Permit required ($2.00, valid one year) for boat fishing on boundary waters

**Recommendations for Lake Mohave:**
- Manage upper section (above Eldorado Canyon) as trout fishery; continue stocking fry and fingerlings during cool months
- Continue spawning area closure below Hoover Dam (December-February)
- Plant rooted aquatic vegetation in shallow areas for cover and food production
- Restrict live bait to species already present; prohibit bass and trout as bait; require bluegill and crappie caught by hook-and-line for bait use (prevent Utah Chub introduction)
- Distribute additional *Gammarus* shrimp stocks if vegetation beds established
- Establish egg-taking and hatching unit on lake for restocking
- Continue biological studies of fish populations, thermal areas, food organism distribution under stabilized conditions
- Improve roads paralleling lake for public access and proper harvest

**Section II - Davis Dam to Needles (36 miles, Mohave Valley):**
- Limited use, primarily by local Bullhead City residents and Bureau of Reclamation personnel
- Upper portion (Davis Dam to Fort Mohave) supports trout; same species as Section I plus green sunfish
- Water released from Davis Dam lower outlets maintains cold temperatures suitable for trout
- Sterile sand bottom, swift shallow areas limit productivity
- Topock Swamp (lower portion) provides bass and catfish fishing; black crappie fishing good near Topock in winter
- Dredged channel (completed July 1951) will carry main flow from Needles to Topock, draining much of original swamp; new channel expected to be poor producer due to lack of vegetation, shifting sand bottom, increased turbidity
- Approximately 6,000 surface acres of original Topock Swamp will be flooded by Lake Havasu backing up through levee opening

**Recommendations for Section II:**
- Designate area between Davis Dam and southern Nevada tip as trout water
- Maintain sufficient trout fry plants to sustain present population
- Retain public land strips from county road to river above Needles for free access
- Improve access on Arizona side between Bullhead City and Fort Mohave

**Section III - Needles to Parker Dam (56 miles, including Lake Havasu):**
- Lake Havasu: 25,000 surface acres, 46 miles long, average depth 22 feet (maximum 75 feet at dam)
- 28,510 angler-days use in 1950 (one angler-day per acre per year—light use)
- Creel census (1,035 anglers, nine months 1950-1951): 0.37 bass, 0.23 catfish, 1.7 bluegill, 0.08 crappie per angler-day; 5.9 hours fished per angler; 2.39 total fish per angler-day
- Total estimated harvest: 9,264 largemouth bass (10,653 lbs), 5,904 channel catfish (2,952 lbs), 42,192 bluegill (7,386 lbs), 2,112 black crappie (1,207 lbs)
- Harvest rate: 0.91 pounds per acre per year (low productivity)
- 91% of anglers fished primarily for largemouth bass
- Bass fishing declined from post-impoundment peak (1938-1947); attributed to reduced fertility and near-complete failure of aquatic weeds from 1947 onward
- Submerged vegetation (spiny naiad) "greatly decreased in abundance" after 1946
- Water level fluctuations limited to 6 feet or less for past seven years (except 11 feet in August 1950)
- Eight boat rental concessions under U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service contract
- Topock Swamp: estimated 6,000 angler-days use; bass and catfish equally important; better quality fishing than Lake Havasu

**Recommendations for Section III:**
- Limit water level drawdown to one foot during March 15-August 30 (bass spawning and bluegill protection)
- Limit fluctuations to less than two feet for aquatic plant growth (November-February can drop additional two feet)
- Introduce golden shiner or other forage fish as buffer for young game fish and forage for bass/crappie
- Improve Arizona side access to Site 6 and Bill Williams Arm
- Continue study program: improve bass/crappie fishing or introduce new game species; analyze introducing/increasing submerged aquatics; analyze constructing brush shelters in coves

**Section IV - Parker Dam to Headgate Rock Dam (15 miles):**
- 15,000-20,000 angler-days estimated for 1950
- Heavy use by retired couples in trailers (October-May season); 150 trailers, 120 boats counted November 18, 1950
- Creel census (59 anglers, 220 hours): 1.3 channel catfish, 4.3 bluegill per angler-day; bass and crappie fishing poor
- Abundant sago pondweed along shore (differentiating characteristic vs. other sections)
- Water temperatures: 49°F (January) to 84°F (August); 65°F (April), 70°F (May), 80°F (June), 75°F (September), 60°F (November)
- Much bank fishing (unlike other sections); popular below Parker Dam powerhouse tailrace
- Daily water fluctuations: 500-5,000 cfs variation; April 1950 example showed 14,500-19,500 cfs range
- Smallmouth bass planted but establishment unknown

**Recommendations for Section IV:**
- No stocking of warm-water fish already present
- Minimize water level fluctuations to less than two feet during bass spawning (March 15-May 15) and bluegill spawning (June 1-August 30)
- Construct anchored brush shelters along river bank
- Retain public lands between road and river at strategic locations for camping, bank fishing, boat launching
- Complete road from Parker, Arizona to Parker Dam on Arizona side
- Continue study: channel catfish and largemouth bass ecology; desirability of introducing forage fish, new game fish, expanding brush shelter program

**Section V - Headgate Rock Dam to Palo Verde Drain (81 miles):**
- 11,890 angler-days river use (146 angler-days per river mile); Lost Lake/Alligator Slough 4,200 angler-days; Palo Verde Slough 7,300 angler-days
- Five major lentic waters: lake below Headgate Rock (20 acres), Lost Lake/Alligator Slough (58 acres), Bass Lake (20 acres), Miller's Pond (15 acres), Palo Verde Drain (navigable 12 miles)
- Channel catfish is "mainstay of sport fishery"; largemouth bass and bluegill most successful in lentic waters
- Palo Verde Weir area: most popular and productive; creel data (265 anglers, October-November 1950) showed 2.8 catfish, 0.23 bass per angler-day, 4.7-4.8 hours per angler
- Palo Verde Slough: 77 permanent homes, 89 boats, five concessions with 45 rental boats; formerly popular for bass but declined due to increased irrigation district activity and turbidity (150 ppm); now dependent on channel catfish
- West's Boat Rental (Palo Verde Slough, October-November 1950): 651 anglers caught 1,430 catfish, 38 bass (2.2 catfish per angler-day)
- Water temperatures at Palo Verde Weir: 52°F minimum, 90°F maximum; 52°F January average, 87°F August average
- Emory's soft-shelled turtle abundant in sandbar areas (specimens over 20 pounds common); little utilization despite excellent food value
- Bullfrogs abundant in Parker and Palo Verde irrigation districts, especially Palo Verde Slough
