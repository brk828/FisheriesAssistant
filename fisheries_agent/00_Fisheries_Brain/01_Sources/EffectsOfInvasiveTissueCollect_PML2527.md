# Effects of Invasive Tissue Collection on Rainbow Trout, Razorback Sucker, and Bonytail Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Management Brief, published in the [[North American Journal of Fisheries Management]] (1999), evaluates whether nonlethal invasive tissue sampling (fin, muscle, and liver) can be used on fish without significant harm. Techniques were first developed on hatchery [[Rainbow Trout]] and then applied to two federally endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]]: [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Acute effects (mortality) and chronic effects (growth suppression) were tested in controlled hatchery experiments. No mortality was attributable to tissue sampling in any species, and no significant growth differences were found in rainbow trout or razorback sucker. Bonytail chub mortality was driven by an [[Ichthyophthirius Multifiliis]] outbreak, which prevented evaluation of chronic effects. The authors concluded that the protocol satisfies the criteria of [[Morizot Criteria for Minimally Invasive Sampling]] and was used to issue [[Endangered Species Act]] research permits.

## Background & Description

Histological, genetic, contaminant, and stable isotope analyses of fish tissues require sampling that is often invasive and can kill the organism. Because of this, invasive methods are rarely applied to threatened or endangered species, even though tissue data could support recovery programs. Advances in small-sample techniques, especially [[Polymerase Chain Reaction]] for DNA analysis, have reduced the tissue quantities needed for analysis.

The authors reviewed alternatives. Blood, scales, epidermis, and mucus can be sampled noninvasively, but scales are mostly bone, not all species have them, and mucus and scales are prone to contamination and require specialized preparation. Gonadal products can be sampled with little injury but depend on fish being ripe. Muscle is a good source of metals, stable isotopes, and DNA, and muscle biopsies in salmonids and catostomids caused little mortality in earlier work. Liver is sensitive to toxicants and is widely used for histology and contaminant analysis, but liver biopsy of [[Largemouth Bass]] produced 20% mortality in an earlier study (Harvey et al. 1984). Fin tissue is useful for genetic work, and fin clips can reduce growth and survival.

The study was commissioned by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to develop a protocol that would let it issue ESA research permits with very low mortality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study species represent three families and are relevant to the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] recovery program. Razorback sucker (Catostomidae) and bonytail chub (Cyprinidae) are federally endangered, and few individuals are available for sampling. Fish listed under the ESA are protected by federal and state statutes, so sacrifice of individuals has been the traditional option, with concern that small samples may not represent the population.

Experimental animals were held at hatcheries under controlled conditions. Rainbow trout were kept in raceways with 14°C spring water and fed about 4% of body weight daily. Razorback suckers and bonytail chub were held in 3,790-L outdoor tanks with circulating well water (15°C), then moved indoors in September to 13°C water, and fed about 4–5% of body weight daily.

## Key Findings

- **Mortality:** No mortality was attributable to tissue removal. One of 72 rainbow trout died after jumping out of the raceway. Three of 96 razorback suckers died (two in control T1, one in T2). Bonytail chub had 77% mortality from ich, and no difference in survivorship was found between surgical and nonsurgical groups (Fisher exact test, P = 0.500).
- **Growth in rainbow trout:** Monte Carlo simulation (2,000 iterations) showed that all treatment means fell within the 95% confidence interval of the control group. Mean length rose by over 100 mm, and weight by at least fourfold, over the study period.
- **Growth in razorback sucker:** No difference in instantaneous growth rate among the six treatments (Kruskal-Wallis test, P = 0.3945). Twelve of 17 preserved fish had surgical incisions, and dissection showed no obvious adverse effects.
- **Growth in bonytail chub:** Only 22 of 78 fish survived to the end (N = 22). Growth was too limited for significance testing, though survival did not differ from controls. Four of seven preserved fish had surgical incisions with no adverse internal effects.
- **Sample yield:** Dermal punches yielded about 15 mg of liver or 50–60 mg of fin or muscle, sufficient for [[Polymerase Chain Reaction]] genetic analysis, selenium contaminant evaluation, and stable isotope analysis.
- **Procedure time:** Liver biopsy plus closure averaged 2.7 min (9 trials). Incision and suturing without tissue removal averaged 2.3 min (9 trials). Fish regained equilibrium and began swimming in under 5 minutes.
- **Methods:** Fin plugs came from the left pelvic fin using a 5-mm dermal punch. Muscle plugs used a 4-mm punch. Liver was accessed through a 15-mm incision, with a 3-mm punch. Individual fish in the endangered species were marked with [[PIT Tags]].
- **Recommendations:** The authors concluded the protocol satisfies Morizot et al. (1990) criteria for minimally invasive sampling. They advised caution with liver sampling, which should only be done by a highly practiced surgeon and only when no less invasive method exists. They also recommended against larger tissue samples or substantial method changes without further study. Dermal punches were preferred over fin clips.
- **Field application:** The protocol was applied to over 400 chubs (Gila spp.) in the Colorado River with no detected mortality. It was also used in a selenium study of 36 razorback suckers, sampled four times annually for 3 years (12 plugs per fish), with no mortality recorded.
