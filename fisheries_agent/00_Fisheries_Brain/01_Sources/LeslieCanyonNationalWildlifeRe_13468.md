# Instream Flow Request for Leslie Creek in Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge

#ichthyology #endangered-species #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #desert-ecology #water-management

## Executive Summary

This document is an instream flow request submitted by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to the [[Arizona Department of Water Resources]] for [[Leslie Creek]] within [[Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge]]. The refuge, located 17 miles north of Douglas, Arizona along the southwestern flank of the [[Chiricahua Mountains]], is one of only two remaining U.S. habitats for the nationally endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*) and [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurae*). These species once dominated stream and cienega habitats throughout the south-draining [[Yaqui Watershed]] but have declined drastically due to agricultural and groundwater development. The request seeks to secure minimum baseflow rights ranging from 0.34 cfs (July) to 0.81 cfs (November), totaling 419.9 acre-feet per year, derived from analysis of USGS gage records (1970-1977, 1982-present) using mean of median monthly daily flows. The perennial stretch of Leslie Creek extends approximately 0.75 miles within the refuge and provides critical habitat for endangered fish, native riparian vegetation, and diverse desert wildlife.

## Background & Description

[[Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge]] was purchased by USFWS from the [[Arizona Nature Conservancy]] in 1988, comprising 1,240 acres. The refuge protects a perennial desert stream system fed by groundwater from a Tertiary-aged basin between the [[Swisshelm Mountains]] and [[Chiricahua Mountains]]. The watershed drains 79.1 square miles of southwestern Chiricahua slopes through a broad mesa before entering Leslie Canyon. The creek is a flashy, third-order stream with baseflows averaging 0.57 cfs and flood flows ranging from 4 to 700 cfs.

The [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (family Poeciliidae) reaches maximum sizes of 50mm (females) and 30mm (males), feeds on detritus, algae, and aquatic invertebrates, and inhabits shallow stream waters. The [[Yaqui Chub]] (family Cyprinidae) rarely exceeds 100mm, consumes algae, terrestrial and aquatic insects, and occupies deeper pools near undercut banks. Both species were introduced to Leslie Creek in 1969 from [[Astin Spring]] by Minckley to ensure survival after widespread habitat loss. The native [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) and possibly the [[Mexican Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma ornatum*, a Category 2 candidate species) also inhabit the creek.

The perennial stretch divides into two morphologically distinct sections: an upper section (0-2,248 river feet) characterized by alternating pool-riffle sequences, braided stretches, deep pools (1.5-6 feet), fine substrates (sand/silt/pebble), dense watercress, and maintained flow; and a lower section (2,248-3,364 river feet) with shallow pools (0.5-2 feet), coarse substrates (cobble/pebble), reduced watercress, and losing flow. An intermittent arroyo entering from the south at 2,248 feet deposits coarse bedload that abruptly alters channel morphology and causes streamflow loss, eventually leading to intermittent conditions.

The riparian corridor averages 20-30 yards wide and extends approximately two miles, dominated by [[Velvet Ash]], [[Goodding Willow]], [[Arizona Walnut]], and [[Fremont Cottonwood]]. Dense understory includes mimosa, net-leaf hackberry, wild grape, acacia, saltbrush, and seepwillow. The surrounding desert ecosystem consists of mixed shrub-grass communities with Chihuahuan affinities, including mesquite, ocotillo, grama grasses, and oak savannah on the eastern mesa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Leslie Creek lies within the semi-arid [[Basin and Range Province]], receiving mean annual precipitation of 12.5 inches (Douglas, AZ: 14 inches, 1965-1989). Precipitation occurs in two distinct seasons: summer monsoons (July-September) and winter cyclonic storms (December-March). Temperatures range from lows of 20-30°F (November-March) to highs of 70-90°F (April-October), with consistent 30°F diurnal variation. Annual evaporation averages 10.6 feet.

Geologically, the watershed consists of deformed Paleozoic sedimentary rocks (Cambrian quartzites, Devonian limestones/sandstones/shales, Permian limestones) intruded by Tertiary volcanic rhyolite. The basin between the Swisshelm and Chiricahua ranges contains thick, highly permeable Tertiary alluvial sediments forming an excellent aquifer that supplies Leslie Creek's baseflow. Gold prospecting has occurred within the watershed; mining poses direct threats through mercury and cyanide contamination.

The [[Yaqui River Basin]] extends from southeastern Arizona into Sonora, Mexico. Within the U.S., only three locations maintain perennial flow: [[Rucker Canyon]], Leslie Canyon, and a 0.75-mile stretch in [[San Bernardino Valley]] (Black Draw). Historical Yaqui fish habitats have deteriorated throughout Arizona and Mexico due to groundwater depletion and agricultural development. [[Whitewater Draw]] and [[Black Draw]], major U.S. Yaqui drainages, have been drained for agriculture and now run dry 80% of the time. [[Astin Spring]], the broodstock source for southeastern Arizona Yaqui fishes, went dry in 1967.

The refuge supports diverse fauna dependent on the riparian corridor: mammals (white-tailed deer, coati mundi, cougar, javelina, possibly endangered [[Lesser Long-nosed Bat]]); raptors (golden eagle, great horned owl, red-tailed hawk); neotropical birds (yellow-billed cuckoo, blue grosbeak, summer tanager); amphibians (possibly new leopard frog species, desert toads); reptiles ([[Mexican Garter Snake]], a candidate species, Gila monster, various rattlesnakes); and diverse, largely unstudied insects.

Threats to Yaqui fish sustainability include: (1) water resource depletion from agricultural/municipal development, (2) non-native fish introductions (mosquitofish *Gambusia affinis* typically replace Yaqui Topminnow upon contact; sunfish recently reported in Leslie Creek), (3) mining waste (cyanide and mercury from gold extraction), and (4) excessive sediment loading from overgrazing or forestry. The [[Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan]] recognizes Leslie Creek as one of three possible U.S. reintroduction sites for threatened/endangered Yaqui species, including [[Beautiful Shiner]] (nationally threatened), [[Yaqui Catfish]] (nationally endangered), and [[Yaqui Sucker]] (candidate for listing).

## Key Findings

**Instream Flow Requirements:** The USFWS requests the following monthly baseflow rights for Leslie Creek within Leslie Canyon NWR, derived from mean of median monthly daily flows (USGS gage 09537200, 1970-1977, 1982-present):

- January: 0.62 cfs (38.3 acre-feet/month)
- February: 0.54 cfs (33.0 AF/month)
- March: 0.53 cfs (32.6 AF/month)
- April: 0.52 cfs (32.2 AF/month)
- May: 0.45 cfs (27.5 AF/month)
- June: 0.35 cfs (21.8 AF/month)
- July: 0.34 cfs (21.0 AF/month)
- August: 0.59 cfs (36.4 AF/month)
- September: 0.63 cfs (38.7 AF/month)
- October: 0.74 cfs (45.3 AF/month)
- November: 0.81 cfs (49.9 AF/month)
- December: 0.70 cfs (43.3 AF/month)
- **Total: 419.9 acre-feet/year**

Median values were selected over mean values because storm events skew means above actual baseflow conditions. The flow duration curve reveals that 5% of daily mean flows during the period of record were zero, primarily during the 1976-1977 drought. The highest monthly median baseflow was 2.5 cfs; the average monthly median flow was 0.57 cfs.

**Habitat Requirements:** The [[Yaqui Topminnow]] requires minimal streamflow and can survive in warm, shallow waters. The [[Yaqui Chub]] requires deeper pools with at least minimal velocity and permanence of surface discharge; studies in the [[Rio Sonora Basin]] showed the species only resides in perennial stretches. The [[Longfin Dace]] requires minimal stream flow velocity. Flows less than requested values would result in no-velocity zones and seasonal intermittency within the current perennial stretch, causing loss of critical fish habitat.

**Hydrologic Analysis:** The USGS gage (lat 31°35'25", long 109°30'30") is located 1,576 river feet downstream from the check dam within the upper perennial section. The gage consists of a data collection platform with digital continuous water-stage recorder and integral float-tape gage, installed February 23, 1988. The control structure is a concrete shallow sharp-crested v-notch weir allowing accurate low-flow measurements. The gage has operated for 15 years total (1970-1977, 1983-present), providing robust baseline data. A major flood on October 2, 1983 reached 3,090 cfs (peak gage height 7.33 ft) following Pacific tropical storm and Hurricane Octave.

**Watershed Threats:** The Leslie Creek hydrosystem encompasses the entire 79.1 square mile basin feeding the refuge. Any groundwater or surface water depletions within this area will directly impact baseflow. The eastern basin mesa contains valley-fill sediments with good groundwater supply that feeds Leslie Creek baseflow. Development and groundwater pumping in this area would severely impact the refuge. Two ranches (99 Bar, Bar Boot) currently operate check dams and windmill-pumped wells with apparently minimal impact. A 1976 Arizona Department of Game and Fish proposal for reservoir construction in the basin would be devastating to the hydro-ecosystem and would require amended instream flow requests for flushing flows.

**Conservation Significance:** Leslie Creek represents one of only two remaining U.S. habitats for endangered Yaqui fishes. The riparian corridor was assessed by botanist John Browning in the early 1980s as in nearly the best condition of approximately 200 southwestern riparian stands surveyed. The [[Arizona Nature Conservancy]] described the canyon as in excellent condition with dense herbaceous understory and vigorously reproducing deciduous trees. Few suitable sites presently exist in the U.S. where Yaqui fishes could be recovered in natural environments. Securing instream flow rights is essential for: (1) preserving critical habitat for endangered Yaqui fishes, (2) maintaining the hydrologic-riparian system supporting surrounding desert ecosystems and wildlife, and (3) preserving a unique, accessible wilderness providing recreational and scientific value.
