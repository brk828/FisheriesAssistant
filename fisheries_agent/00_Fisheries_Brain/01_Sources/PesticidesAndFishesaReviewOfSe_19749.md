# Pesticides and Fishes: A Review of Selected Literature

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pesticide-toxicology

## Executive Summary

This 1968 review by Donald W. Johnson of the [[Arizona State University]] Department of Zoology surveys the literature on pesticide interactions in aquatic habitats, with emphasis on effects on fishes. The author argues that research to date has focused on acute, directly lethal effects, measured mainly as the [[Lethal Concentration 50]] (LC50), and that this is insufficient for judging a "safe" environmental concentration. The review covers toxicity factors (temperature, water chemistry, life stage, species, mixtures), residue dynamics and metabolism in fish and food webs, analytical methods, and documented field effects on fish and invertebrates. It concludes that research should detect sublethal, population-level and ecosystem damage through long-term, field-based, year-round programs, and that direct mortality should no longer be the chief criterion of pesticide danger.

## Background & Description

The review frames pesticides as "economic poisons" whose broad, largely nonselective action can produce unanticipated side effects. Two main synthetic groups are distinguished:

- **[[Chlorinated Hydrocarbons]]** (e.g., [[DDT]], [[Dieldrin]], [[Endrin]], [[Toxaphene]], [[Lindane]], [[Aldrin]], [[Heptachlor]]): highly toxic to fishes, chemically stable, poorly metabolized and stored in fatty tissue. The author reports they are 500 (BHC) to 170,000 (methoxychlor) times more toxic to fathead minnows than to white rats.
- **[[Organophosphate Insecticides]]** (e.g., [[Malathion]], [[Parathion]], [[Diazinon]], [[Dipterex]]): generally less toxic to fishes (12 to 240 times more toxic to the rat than to the fathead minnow, per the author's caveated comparison), relatively unstable, rarely stored, but highly toxic to fish food organisms.

Herbicides (e.g., [[Dichlone]], [[Molinate]], [[Silvex]], [[Endothal]], [[2,4-D]]) are generally less toxic to fishes than insecticides, though field concentrations for weed control often exceed toxicity thresholds for [[Daphnia]] and some fishes. Table 1 lists the pesticides discussed; Table A lists about 60 fish species (including [[Atlantic Menhaden]], [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Striped Mullet]] and [[Sheepshead Minnow]]) used in pesticide literature.

Usage scale cited for 1959 in the United States: about 1 billion pounds of insecticides applied to 100 million acres (about 5% of land area), with insecticide sales of about 500 million dollars. Over 90,000 pesticides were registered in 1959, and 180 million pounds of DDT were produced in 1963.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish kills:** Extensive simultaneous fish kills occurred in 14 tributaries of the Tennessee River, Alabama, in 1950. From June to October 1960, 31 states recorded 185 fish kills from polluted water, 73 of them attributed to pesticides.
- **Toxicity modifiers:** Temperature, water chemistry (pH, [[Hardness]], calcium, dissolved oxygen, free carbon dioxide), salinity, turbidity and silt, and mixtures of toxicants all alter toxicity. Synergism was reported for malathion with EPN and for DDT with 2,4-D.
- **Life stage:** Eggs and larvae are generally more resistant than adults, but young trout fry (ten weeks old) were reported 25 times more sensitive to DDT than fish several months old.
- **Resistance:** Populations of [[Mosquitofish]] from cotton areas with DDT history showed resistance to normally lethal doses; some resistant fish carried residues about 100 times those of susceptible fish.
- **Food web:** Residues increase with trophic level. At [[Clear Lake]], California, the author cites concentrations rising from 5.3 ppm in plankton to 1,500–1,700 ppm in largemouth bass and 342–2,700 ppm in brown bullhead, after DDD treatment.
- **Sediment and runoff:** Silt and mud adsorb pesticides and act as transport vehicles into aquatic systems; a mud sample in one bayou contained 520 ppb DDT after rains.
- **Persistence:** Endrin remained toxic in water for at least a month; toxaphene was still toxic nine months after treatment in some lakes; DDT and dieldrin persist longest in soil (twelve years for 95% to disappear, per Edwards, 1965).

## Key Findings

1. **Acute LC50 data are inadequate for safety judgments.** Laboratory LC50 values depend on species, water and assay conditions, and do not extrapolate to field conditions. Holden (1964) suggested a field-permissible concentration roughly one-hundredth of the 24-hour LC50.
2. **Chronic and sublethal effects are poorly known.** Documented effects include residue accumulation in fat, liver and kidney damage, reduced reproduction, slowed reactions, loss of appetite, restricted growth, lowered disease resistance, hypersensitivity and convulsions. Cutthroat trout exposed to 3.0 mg DDT/kg body weight showed no reduction in egg number or volume but increased sac fry mortality.
3. **Field effects on invertebrates and fishes are substantial.** After DDT spraying at 0.25 lb/acre, downstream insect drift rose to 60,000 per hour at the surface versus 704 in controls. One study recorded a 99% decline in immature insects within six days at 0.4 ppm DDT. Johnson (1963) reported a 73% reduction in trout populations after DDT spraying and 94% mortality in longnose suckers at 1.0 ppm.
4. **Zooplankton and food organisms are highly sensitive.** LC50 values for the cladoceran *Simocephalus serrulatus* with DDT were 1.7 ppb at 10.0 C and 4.3 ppb at 27.0 C. Destruction of food organisms could cause starvation, particularly of young fishes.
5. **Residues vary among individuals and tissues.** Largemouth bass showed a 2:1 DDE:DDT ratio, while bluegill and brown bullhead showed 1:1. Fat, brain and ovary held the highest residues.
6. **Blood and sediment monitoring are promising.** A correlation of 0.95 was reported between DDT in blood and adipose tissue, and endrin acute toxicity could be diagnosed from blood concentrations. Acetylcholinesterase inhibition can indicate present and past organophosphate contamination.
7. **Water concentration alone is an inadequate danger criterion.** The author concludes that research should use indirect methods to detect sublethal population damage, combining monitoring with histological and ecological studies over year-round, multi-year periods.
8. **Analytical methods have improved but have limits.** Electron capture and microcoulometric gas chromatography are widely used, with detection limits of about 2 to 7 nanograms. Sample storage, container adsorption, and the cleanup step (about 80% residue recovery) can bias results.
9. **Calls for expanded research.** The review urges studies of bottom fauna above and below agricultural pesticide use, physiological and histological baselines (hematocrit, hemoglobin, liver and spleen weights), behavior and food-habit studies, and surveillance of reservoirs and marshes as well as major rivers.
10. **Unresolved conflicts.** The author notes contradictions over whether sublethal DDT levels damage fisheries (Allison et al., 1963 versus Hunt and Keith, 1962) and over the significance of "resistant" fish, and cites Carson (1962) and Ferguson's work as points of debate. The question "would flesh-eating animals, including humans, be harmed?" was described as officially unanswered.
