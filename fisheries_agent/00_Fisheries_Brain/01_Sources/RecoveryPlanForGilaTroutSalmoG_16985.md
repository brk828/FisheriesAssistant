# Gila Trout Recovery Plan (1978)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This recovery plan, prepared June 20, 1978 by the Gila Trout Recovery Team and approved by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], sets out how to improve the status of the [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*) until it can be delisted from endangered status. The species is endemic to the Mogollon Plateau of New Mexico and Arizona. Fewer than 10,000 individuals are thought to remain, mostly in small, stunted headwater populations. The plan's prime objective is to secure survival by protecting all five native populations, duplicating each in at least one additional stream, and maintaining the maximum genetic diversity. The plan names five recovery steps, a year-by-year schedule of agency responsibilities, and budgets. Agency reviews show disagreement over step order, cost allocation, and whether sport-fishery potential should drive the program.

## Background & Description

The Gila trout was described by Miller (1950) from Main Diamond Creek in the [[Gila National Forest]]. Five native populations are recognized: [[Main Diamond Creek]], [[South Diamond Creek]], [[McKenna Creek]], [[Iron Creek]], and [[Spruce Creek]]. Isolation by physical barriers protected these populations from hybridization with introduced rainbow trout, producing subtle morphological differences. South Diamond Creek fish have a significant increase in spot density. Spruce Creek fish are poorly understood and may represent a native population that deserves subspecific recognition, possibly sharing traits with [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*).

Diagnostic features include iridescent gold sides, copper-shaded opercles, and small, profuse spots (sometimes approaching 30 per cm²) concentrated above the lateral line. Adults show a faint salmon-pink lateral band and a yellow "cutthroat" mark. The karyotype is 2n = 56. Table 1 summarizes ranges of means for 10 taxonomic characters across the five populations. The authors state that sample sizes were purposely omitted and the table gives only a general overview.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical distribution.** Based on "old timer" interviews reported by Miller (1950), Gila trout occurred throughout the Gila River tributaries in New Mexico, downstream to a box canyon about 7 miles northeast of Cliff. Evidence also suggests former occurrence in the Verde and Agua Fria drainages in Arizona, and possibly in [[Eagle Creek]] and other San Francisco drainage streams. Some of these claims are disputed in the reviews.

**Causes of decline.** The plan cites hybridization with non-native salmonids, heavy fishing pressure, changes in stream conditions, and competition from [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*). Hybridization and competition with introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*S. gairdneri*) are considered the main factors restricting the species. Cutthroat stocking from 1893 onward also contributed hybrid influence.

**Present distribution.** Native populations occur in Iron, McKenna, and Spruce creeks (Gila Wilderness Area) and Main Diamond and South Diamond creeks (Black Range Primitive Area). Transplanted populations are in [[McKnight Creek]] (Mimbres drainage, outside the historic range), Sheep Corral (Canyon) Creek, and [[Gap Creek]] (Arizona).

**Population status.** Main Diamond Creek supports approximately half of all pure Gila trout. Estimates include about 4,300 age I and older fish in 1962 (Regan 1964) and 2,983–4,170 in 1969–1970 (Hanson 1971). Bickle (1972) estimated 3,000 fish there, and Mello and Turner (in preparation) reported 500–700 in South Diamond Creek (1976) and 400–500 in McKenna Creek (1975). Gap Creek's population was probably under 100 fish in 1977. Flood and drought are the main threats to numbers and age structure, and a severe forest fire could affect all populations.

**Habitat and life history.**
- Main Diamond Creek is a stunted population in a 4-mile headwater stream with no other fish species. The authors caution that this study system is highly modified and may not represent other habitats.
- Hanson (1971) recorded mean total lengths of 45 mm at the end of year one and 163 mm at year five. Largest hatchery fish on record reached 552 mm.
- Spawning occurs in April–June when mid-day water temperatures reach 8–12 °C. Fry emerge in early June, and 35–45 mm fry were collected in July.
- Fecundity is low: 150 eggs per female on average (Regan 1964) and 75.6 eggs per female (Hanson 1971).
- Diet is opportunistic, dominated by Trichoptera, Ephemeroptera, and Diptera.
- Pools with depth and cover are critical during drought, when flows can fall below one cubic foot per second.
- Recorded water temperatures ranged from 1 to 19 °C in Main Diamond Creek. Fish fed actively at 23 °C in an aquarium, and fish were found at 27 °C in McKnight Creek.

## Key Findings

**Conservation history**
- The Jenks Cabin Hatchery (Gila Wilderness) operated from 1923 until closure in 1939 because of poor access and low production.
- The New Mexico Department of Game and Fish closed Main Diamond Creek to fishing in 1958.
- Repairs to 108 log structures and 11 new structures in Main Diamond Creek (1965–66) were credited with helping the species survive the dry years of 1967 and 1971.
- The species was listed as rare and endangered in 1966, and it received protection under the [[Endangered Species Act Of 1973]] and the New Mexico Wildlife Conservation Act of 1974.

**Transplants**
- McKnight Creek: 307 fish from Main Diamond Creek (November 5, 1970) after a rotenone treatment and barrier construction. A 1971 drought reduced the population to 17 fish, so 110 more were added on April 27, 1972.
- Sheep Corral (Canyon) Creek: 89 fish introduced in 1972 into a barren section. The authors judge it marginal habitat.
- Gap Creek (Arizona): 65 fish transplanted in 1974 by the Arizona Game and Fish Department, with successful annual reproduction.

**Recovery plan steps**
1. Maintain and enhance existing populations and habitat, including monitoring, habitat evaluation, barrier maintenance, and regulated human activities. Non-native fish introduction is banned, and fishing closures remain until delisting.
2. Re-establish Gila trout within portions of the former range. This includes setting habitat minima, surveying candidate streams using the Forest Service "[[Stream Habitat Evaluation Technique]]," and transplanting from the nearest geographically appropriate population. Little Creek is the recommended first site, stocked with McKenna Creek fish.
3. Disseminate information to the public and professionals.
4. Assess Gila trout as a sport species, including response to angling pressure and identification of streams that could support a fishery.
5. Hold Gila trout in a hatchery as a refugium if a population is seriously threatened.

**Downlisting criterion.** Per the team's response to comments, the species can be downlisted when each of the five native populations is duplicated in another stream and the new populations are secure.

**Budget (Year 1–3 summaries)**
- Year 1: New Mexico Game and Fish $36,000; Arizona Game and Fish $1,500; U.S. Forest Service $52,000; U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service $8,000; New Mexico State University $1,000. The stated total is $98,590, but the listed agency amounts sum to $98,500.
- Year 2: total $37,000.
- Year 3: total $87,000.

**Agency and expert reviews**
- The New Mexico Game Commission (Huey, February 1978) wanted step 4 (sport-species assessment) addressed before range expansion. It proposed a priority order of 1-4-5-2-3 and framed two mutually exclusive aims: preserving the species as a "museum relic" or promoting it as a fishery species.
- The Arizona Game and Fish Department (Jantzen, March 1978) concurred with critical habitat but objected that costs were not divided among agencies and that cost bases were not explained.
- The U.S. Forest Service (March 17, 1978) gave the highest priority to re-establishment in larger streams, since small headwaters limit growth and sustain populations less well. It recommended adding Willow and Negrito creeks as candidate sites and noted that most candidate streams lie within wilderness, requiring NEPA review.
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service technical review asked that the prime objective be quantified and that the plan not justify restoration by sport fishing.
- One reviewer argued that sport-fishery evaluation should wait until after delisting, because harvestable numbers fluctuate yearly.
- The team's responses state that step order does not indicate priority, that restoration of other historic-range portions has first-year priority, and that Little Creek research (Mello 1978) supports its selection.
