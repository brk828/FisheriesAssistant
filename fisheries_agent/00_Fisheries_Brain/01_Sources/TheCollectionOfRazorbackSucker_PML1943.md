# Razorback Sucker Larval Light-Trap Collection and Predator Avoidance in Southwestern Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This note combines two bilingual (English/Spanish) conference abstracts. The first tests whether [[Light Traps]] can monitor larval density of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona. The second compares [[Predator Avoidance]] behavior in larvae of southwestern native fishes with that of Mississippi River system species. The light-trap study found that razorback sucker larvae responded only to white light, while many other aquatic organisms were attracted to all light sources. The predator study did not support the hypothesis that southwestern larvae are more "predator naive."

## Background & Description

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)** is described as a large, endangered [[Catostomidae]] native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its larvae are [[Phototaxis|phototactic]] until they reach about 14 mm in length. Researchers have traditionally collected larvae with hand-held spotlights and aquarium dip nets. Earlier attempts with light traps had only limited success, which motivated the trap development described below.

The predator study examined larvae of three southwestern natives: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and the razorback sucker. These were compared with [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*) and [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) from the [[Mississippi River System]]. The southwestern fauna evolved with few piscivorous predators, whereas Mississippi River species had long exposure to many piscivores.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The light-trap study was conducted in Lake Mohave, a reservoir on the Colorado River in Arizona. Its bycatch included Odonata (both Anisoptera and Zygoptera), Hemiptera, Bufonidae (toads), and zooplankton, showing that light traps attract a broad community of aquatic organisms, not only target fish larvae.

The southwestern fish fauna is described as depauperate, meaning it has relatively few species and few piscivorous predators. This evolutionary history framed the hypothesis that its larvae would show weaker anti-predator defenses. Keywords associated with the predator study include schooling, introduced and exotic species, and predation risk, which relate to the management of native fishes in altered southwestern waters.

## Key Findings

**Light-trap collection of razorback sucker larvae (Lake Mohave)**
- A total of 226 trap sets were completed, representing 1,327.6 trap hours of effort.
- PVC light traps were tested with four attractants: green [[Cyalume Light Sticks]], dry dog food, and white light at two intensities (12 W and 55 W) powered by 12-volt gel-cell batteries.
- Razorback sucker larvae were attracted only to white light. They did not respond to dog food or Cyalume light sticks.
- Odonata, Hemiptera, Bufonidae, and zooplankton were attracted to all light sources, so non-target bycatch is a practical consideration.
- The abstract does not report catch numbers per treatment, so the relative efficiency of the two white-light intensities cannot be assessed from the text.

**Predator avoidance in larval fishes**
- Southwestern larvae were hypothesized to be more "predator naive" than Mississippi River species, with less developed avoidance behavior.
- Results showed a trend toward equal or greater predator avoidance in southwestern larvae.
- This pattern was only mildly evident for survival and for the distance kept from the predator.
- The difference was strong in schooling behavior.
- Within the species and parameters tested, the naivete hypothesis was not supported.
- The abstract is truncated, so the sample sizes and statistical tests are not available in this text.
