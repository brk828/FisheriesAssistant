# Survey of Crayfishes of the Gila River Basin, Arizona and New Mexico

#decapoda #cambaridae #parastacidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1998 technical report, prepared by Terry C. Inman, [[Paul C. Marsh]], Brian E. Bagley and Carol A. Pacey at Arizona State University for the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Phoenix Area Office), is the first systematic survey of crayfishes in the [[Gila River Basin]] and, more broadly, in Arizona. No crayfish species are native to the [[Colorado River Basin]], including the Gila drainage. Field collections were made at 94 locales in Arizona and New Mexico, and retail aquarium and pet stores in the [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area]] were visited a dozen times.

Two non-native species were captured in the wild: [[Procambarus Clarkii]] (red swamp crayfish) and [[Orconectes Virilis]] (northern crayfish). Both were widespread, common to locally abundant, and occupied natural and artificial lotic and lentic habitats. The authors state that very large populations likely harm native fauna. Pet-trade species included [[Procambarus Alleni]], [[Procambarus Paeninsulanus]] and an unidentified Australian [[Cherax]]. The report concludes that release of these animals to the wild seems inevitable but should be discouraged.

## Background & Description

Crayfish (order Decapoda, infraorder Astacidea) are freshwater crustaceans whose closest relatives are marine lobsters. They belong to the superfamilies Astacoidea (families Astacidae and Cambaridae), which occur only in the Northern Hemisphere, and Parastacoidea (family Parastacidae), restricted to the Southern Hemisphere. North America north of Mexico has 338 recognized crayfish taxa, the greatest diversity of any region. Arizona has no native species, and the report notes that crayfish first appeared in state waters about 30 years before 1998, stocked for aquatic weed control and forage. Escape from bait buckets and discards by aquarium owners are considered the most likely explanations for their current spread.

**Identification.** Most keys rely on Form I adult males, a cyclic life stage in Cambarinae and Cambarellinae marked by corneous (horny) projections on the first pleopods. Northern populations usually have all males in one form, while in lower latitudes Form I and Form II males can coexist. Life stage should not be judged by body size, since molting can change size. Appendix A provides a key to Arizona genera and species.

**Methods.** Wild crayfish were collected with seines, dip nets and electrofishers, or by baited minnow traps. Specimens were fixed in 6% neutral formalin, rinsed, and preserved in isopropyl or ethyl alcohol. Retail specimens were recorded during store visits.

**Taxonomic note.** [[Orconectes Causeyi]] was considered in the region but was not among the samples. The authors treated it as synonymous with *O. virilis* because the morphological differences were not reliable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species profiles and habitat associations.**
- *P. clarkii* is a tertiary burrower that digs single-shafted burrows up to 2 m deep. It tolerates brackish water up to 10 ppt and occurs in streams, rice fields, permanent lakes, reservoirs and irrigation ditches. Its burrowing can damage earthen dikes and water-control structures. Sites where it occurred included the Colorado River mainstream, Salt River canals and a Hassayampa tributary.
- *O. virilis* was collected in every major Arizona drainage. Its burrows are generally under 1 m and pose little threat to earthen structures. It tolerates water temperatures of 1–32 °C and was recorded from about 300 m at Tempe to over 2,500 m in the White Mountains.
- *P. alleni* is native to central and southern Florida. It was sold in Tempe and at [[Lake Pleasant]], but no wild populations were found above or below Lake Pleasant.
- *P. paeninsulanus* is native to the Choctawhatchee River basin of southern Georgia, Alabama and Florida. No wild introductions have been reported.

**Diet.** All introduced species are opportunistic detritivores. They eat aquatic macrophytes, benthic and planktonic invertebrates, fish eggs, fish larvae and dead or dying fishes. Cannibalism has been recorded in *P. alleni*.

**Habitat associations in Arizona.** The authors found that both introduced species are generalists. Field samples included about three times as many lotic (stream) as lentic (lake or pond) sites. Adjusted for this bias, *P. clarkii* appeared more frequent in streams, and *O. virilis* more frequent in lakes and ponds. Sample sizes were small, and no statistical analysis was performed. Crayfish were absent from proportionally more lentic systems than lotic ones. Densities were highest in fine, soft substrates and near stream margins. Crayfish were generally scarce where plant beds were dense.

**Sampled localities.** Appendix E lists the sites, which include mountain lakes and streams in Apache and Greenlee counties, the [[Verde River]], the [[San Pedro River]], Phoenix-area canals, and [[Lake Mohave]]. Many sites also held native or listed fish such as [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Gila Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Little Colorado Spinedace]].

**Pet trade.** Pet stores carried few crayfish, possibly because of their predatory behavior toward aquarium fishes. Three taxa were noted, but only two were confirmed. A *P. paeninsulanus* sold under that name at a Tempe store was later identified as *P. clarkii*. The Australian specimen (18 cm long, a donated display animal) was likely *Cherax quadricarinatus*, but age and sex could not be verified. Its origin was unknown.

## Key Findings

- **No native crayfish species exist in Arizona or the Colorado River basin.** The authors describe both introduced species as now permanent residents in most areas of the state.
- **Two non-native species were established in the wild.** *P. clarkii* was found at fewer locations, mainly in the Colorado River mainstream and Phoenix-area canals. *O. virilis* was widespread across all major drainages. The two species were allopatric except in one Phoenix-area canal.
- **Pet-trade species carry a risk of release.** *P. alleni* was readily available at several retail outlets, and *P. paeninsulanus* was advertised, though its sale was not confirmed. The authors recommend discouraging release.
- **Known ecological impacts.**
  - *P. clarkii* damages earthen water-control structures and commercial crops, and it harms aquatic plants and fish in other countries.
  - *O. virilis* has been linked to lake trout declines in the Great Lakes and the decimation of a trout fishery in Utah. It preys on the eggs of [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] and may indirectly affect habitat.
- **Concern for endangered fishes.** The authors warn that *O. virilis* could threaten native Arizona fishes, especially [[Spikedace]], which is endemic to the Gila basin. Recent inventories suggest few remnant populations are doing well.
- **Regulation.** The report states that only Parastacidae (Australian crayfish) are prohibited in Arizona.
- **Research gaps.** Basic information on species identity and distribution was limited before this survey. The authors call for future research on the effects of *P. clarkii* and *O. virilis* on Arizona aquatic ecosystems.
