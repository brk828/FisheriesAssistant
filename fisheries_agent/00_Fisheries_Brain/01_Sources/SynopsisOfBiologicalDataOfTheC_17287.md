# Synopsis of Biological Data on the Chum Salmon (*Oncorhynchus keta*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This FAO Species Synopsis (Circular 315, Bureau of Commercial Fisheries, 1970) by Richard G. Bakkala compiles existing literature on the [[Chum Salmon]], *Oncorhynchus keta*, the most widely distributed Pacific salmon. The review covers nomenclature and morphology, distribution, [[Life History]] and bionomics, population structure and dynamics, the [[Fishery]], and protection and [[Artificial Stocking]] programs. The author notes that the review was restricted to English-language sources and to Japanese and Russian work translated into English, so some information is likely omitted. The synopsis is intended to make existing knowledge available in a standard format and to highlight gaps for future research.

## Background & Description

**Identity and taxonomy.** The valid name is *Oncorhynchus keta* (Walbaum) (type locality: Kamchatka River). The species is a [[Morphometrics|morphometrically]] defined "morpho-species" and is polytypic. Berg (1934) proposed seasonal races (summer and autumn), with autumn chums assigned to the infraspecies *autumnalis*, a name not widely used. The genus diagnosis follows Jordan and Evermann (1896), and Clemens and Wilby (1946) provided the species diagnosis.

**Diagnostic features.** Key characters include 10 to 16 branchiostegals, 19 to 26 smooth, widely spaced gill rakers on the first arch, 140 to 254 pyloric caeca (average about 205 in the key), no black spots on the caudal fin, and faint parr marks that barely extend below the lateral line. Breeding adults show reddish and dusky bars on the sides. Males develop a hooked snout and fang-like teeth.

**Cytology and proteins.** The diploid chromosome number is 74 (28 metacentric, 46 acrocentric). Muscle myogen electrophoresis gives stable, species-specific patterns useful for phylogenetic analysis.

**Morphology.** Katayama (1935) and Grigo (1953) provided morphometric data separating sexes and seasonal races. Meristic counts (e.g., vertebrae about 67.6, lateral-line scales about 135) are summarized from Hikita (1962) and Rounsefell (1962).

**Age notation.** The author adopts the Koo (1962) system: "0.3" denotes no winters in fresh water, three winters at sea, and a fourth year of life. Chum salmon never overwinter in fresh water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range.** Chum salmon have the widest distribution of any Pacific salmon. In North America, spawning extends from the [[Sacramento River]] (California) north to the Arctic coast of Alaska and as far east as the Mackenzie River. In Asia, the southern spawning limit is the Tone River (Honshu), with northward extension to the Lena River. Spawning occurs most often within 200 km of the sea, but fish spawn more than 2,500 km inland in the [[Amur River]] and [[Yukon River]].

**Freshwater habitat.** Eggs are deposited in streambed gravel, usually 15 to 30 cm deep, and incubate there until emergence in March to May. Survival is strongly affected by streamflow, sedimentation, dissolved oxygen, intragravel carbon dioxide, freezing, and redd superimposition. Lethal temperatures for fry are 23.8 °C and -0.1 °C, and fry prefer 12 to 14 °C.

**Marine habitat.** Ocean residence is limited to the [[Subarctic Region]] of the North Pacific and Bering Sea, bounded by a permanent halocline. Chum salmon are concentrated where surface temperature exceeds 2–3 °C and are rarely found below 1 °C. The preferred range is 2–3 to 11 °C. The southern limit shifts northward as surface waters warm, roughly following the 12–13 °C isotherms.

**Feeding.** Fry eat small benthic organisms, chiefly chironomid larvae. Marine diets are dominated by pteropods, euphausiids, amphipods, and copepods, with diet shifting with prey availability.

**Predators and competitors.** Predators include fish, birds, and marine mammals (e.g., sea lions, harbor seals, beluga whales). Competitors include other *Oncorhynchus* species, especially pink salmon, which compete for spawning space, and resident stream fishes that compete for fry food.

**Ecosystem interactions.** Hikita (1960) documented the animal community of a Japanese chum stream (Memu River), and Levanidov (1959) quantified competition between fry and co-occurring fishes in the Khor River. Chum salmon mix extensively with other salmon species at sea, and the authors' analysis suggests strong competition.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and migration**
- Adults enter coastal waters from June to November and reach spawning streams from July to January. Northern populations spawn earlier (August–early September peak) and southern populations later (October–November).
- Stocks from Asia and North America intermingle on the high seas. Asian fish dominate west of about 175° W, and North American fish dominate east of about 170° W.
- Maturing fish migrate about 40 km per day during their last 30 days at sea.

**Life history**
- Mature chum salmon range from age 0.1 to 0.6, with most maturing at 0.3. Southern populations have more young fish, and northern populations more older fish.
- Fecundity ranges from about 900 to 8,000 eggs, with most fish producing 2,000 to 3,000 eggs. Autumn runs average 2,500 to 4,300 eggs, and summer runs about 2,000 to 2,550.
- Egg-to-fry survival in natural streams is usually under 10 percent, but it reached 28 percent in the [[Memu River]] over three years. Hatchery egg-to-fry survival is about 70 to 90 percent.
- Fry migration to sea is obligatory within the first summer. Fry held in fresh water for 7–8 months die, due to loss of osmoregulatory capacity.

**Mortality**
- Hook Nose Creek (British Columbia) data: egg-to-fry survival 7.8 percent, and total marine mortality 97.4–99.2 percent (mean ocean survival 0.8 percent).
- Predation of fry ranged from 23 to 85 percent in Hook Nose Creek.
- Mortality is highest in the coastal juvenile period and during egg-to-fry stages.

**Population dynamics and abundance**
- Neave (1961) estimated the ocean population for 1936–39 at 1,355 thousand metric tons (chum salmon mature stock 510, immature stock 845).
- Asian catches were about 43 million fish in 1932–41 and about 41.5 million in 1955–60. North American catches averaged about 14.5 million in 1932–41 and 8.7 million in 1955–60.
- Recruitment is much lower than in 1936–39. World catches fell to about 134,000–149,000 metric tons in 1961–64.
- Reproduction rates declined in Alaska and British Columbia from about 3–4 returns per spawner (1920s) to about 1.5–2 (1950s). Hokkaido hatchery returns rose to about 6 per spawner in the 1950s.

**Fishery**
- The [[Japanese Mothership Fishery]], operating since 1952 in the North Pacific and Bering Sea, uses drift gill nets with mesh sizes of 12.1 and 13.0 cm stretched measure. Catches peaked at 10.5–18.5 million fish in 1955–60 before the Soviet quota reduced them.
- North American gear is mainly gill nets and purse seines. Traps were abolished in Alaska in 1959.
- Chum salmon ranked second among Pacific salmon in catch size, at 44.2 million fish per year (1953–62).

**Management and stocking**
- Japan operates extensive hatcheries (49 in Hokkaido, 82 in Honshu). About 57 percent of Hokkaido escapement is diverted to hatcheries. Estimated optimum escapement for maximum sustained yield is 350,000–500,000 spawners in Hokkaido and 100,000 in Honshu.
- North American hatchery output is small, with 10–12 hatcheries producing under 10 million fry annually.
- Regulations include quotas, time and area closures, gear restrictions, and the Northwest Pacific Fisheries Treaty.
- Stream-improvement measures (controlled-flow spawning channels, obstruction removal) have raised egg-to-fry survival, for example from 11 to 25 percent in the [[Big Qualicum River]].

**Knowledge gaps noted by the author**
- Causes of marine mortality are poorly understood.
- Migration routes from North American and Asian coasts to the high seas are unknown.
- The effects of parasites on adult health are not known.
- The influence of food supply on fry survival has not been studied.
