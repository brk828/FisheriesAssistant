# Living Fossils of Devils Hole: Pupfish in the Desert

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodon

## Executive Summary

This popular feature describes the [[Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon* spp.) of [[Death Valley]], California, and nearby Nevada. The fish survive in desert springs and pools that remain from [[Lake Manly]], a Pleistocene lake that once covered the valley. The article focuses on the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]], the only endangered pupfish within [[Death Valley National Monument]]. It covers the fish's biology, the unusual [[Devils Hole]] habitat, threats from development in [[Ash Meadows]], conservation measures such as refugium transplants, and the scientific value of the species for evolutionary biology and kidney research.

## Background & Description

- **Discovery and naming:** *Cyprinodon* was first discovered in 1898. The common name came in the 1930s, when [[Carl Hubbs]] studied the fish and noted that their playful behavior reminded him of puppies.
- **Size and diet:** Pupfish measure about one to two inches and weigh only a few grams. They feed on algae found near the water surface, including *Spirogyra* and *Plectonema*.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males are royal blue and become iridescent when excited, agitated, or breeding. They have dark striping and violet gill covers. Females are dull yellow-brown with a silvery underside.
- **Devils Hole morphology:** The Devils Hole fish lack the stripes and pelvic fins found in other species. They have large heads relative to body size. Scientists speculate that it is a dwarf species.
- **Known species:** The best-known pupfish are the Devils Hole, [[Salt Creek Pupfish]], and [[Cottonball Marsh Pupfish]]. All three and their habitats are protected within Death Valley National Monument.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and paleoenvironment**
- During the [[Pleistocene Epoch]], which ended 10,000 to 20,000 years ago, Lake Manly covered virtually all of Death Valley. It was approximately 175 miles long and 600 feet deep.
- As the climate warmed and glaciers receded, the lake evaporated. Average annual rainfall in Death Valley is now only 0.7 inches.

**Groundwater-fed habitats**
- [[Aquifer]] systems sustain seeps, springs, and shallows. Each habitat often has its own endemic species, some only a few hundred yards apart.
- Rainwater percolates down until it reaches a solid rock base about 300 to 500 feet below the desert floor. Water then rises through fractures or faults to form pools.
- The "fossil" water in Devils Hole fell as rain roughly 10,000 years ago on the [[Nevada Test Site]]. It took that long to travel about 50 miles underground.

**Devils Hole structure**
- The surface pool measures about seven by three meters. A rocky shelf lies just inches below the surface, where the fish feed and spawn.
- Below the shelf, the pool drops sharply into underground caverns. At about 80 feet, under [[Anvil Rock]], the main cavern opens into a large chamber with branches, one of which has an air pocket used by researchers.

**Trophic links**
- Owls nesting on rocky outcroppings above Devils Hole, including barn owls and great horned owls, drop castings into the water. These supply nutrients that promote algal growth, which is the base of the pupfish food chain.

**Population dynamics**
- [[Baugh]], a fisheries biologist with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], has conducted census counts. The Devils Hole population fluctuates between 150 and 600 individuals.
- Fish are generally found near the surface and shelf. A few have been found in side branches, which Baugh thinks may be due to divers' air bubbles blowing them there.
- Salt Creek pupfish numbers drop to about 1,000 in the dry season. In the cool season, when more fresh water flows down the channel, they can increase to as many as 1,000,000.

## Key Findings

**Threats and protection**
- The Devils Hole pupfish is the only endangered pupfish within the monument, though other endangered species occur outside it.
- Devils Hole was added as a noncontiguous unit of Death Valley National Monument in 1952. Its status was made more precarious by proposed development in Ash Meadows, which surrounds the site and is now a wildlife refuge.
- Ash Meadows' aquatic habitats have been drained, channeled, dammed, and in some cases pumped dry. Baugh stated this.
- A proposed vacation-home development threatened 20 to 30 other endangered plant and animal species. Development would have depleted the groundwater supplying Devils Hole.
- In 1976, the [[Supreme Court Ruling on Devils Hole]] held that the water in Devils Hole must be maintained at a level that allows fish access to the feeding shelf.
- [[Phil Pister]], a [[California Department of Fish and Game]] fishery biologist, stated, "The key to long-term preservation lies in habitat integrity."

**Conservation measures**
- Because Ash Meadows habitat had nearly been lost, scientists decided to spread the pupfish population to guard against extinction from natural or human-caused disasters.
- Fish were moved to two [[Refugium]] sites. These are large concrete tanks built for pupfish: one below [[Hoover Dam]] and one less than a mile from Devils Hole. Baugh described the strategy as "so all our eggs aren't in one basket." [[James Deacon]] of the University of Nevada studies the transplanted fish.
- Efforts to culture Devils Hole pupfish in aquariums have been largely unsuccessful. Only two aquarium-spawned fish have been raised to date.

**Physiology and research value**
- Salt Creek and Cottonball Marsh pupfish live in water up to five times as salty as seawater. Their [[Osmoregulation]] abilities are notable. The Salt Creek fish flow from [[McLean Spring]], which becomes highly saline after forced to the surface through a rock fault.
- The fish's tolerance of changing salinity provides information relevant to [[Kidney Dialysis Research]] and transplant research.
- Pupfish also offer insight into evolution and into life on Earth during the Ice Age, as creatures of another time.

**Ethical position**
- Pister argued that rationale for saving species should rest "less upon potential benefits to man than upon the inherent capabilities given to us in having a conscience." Benefits to humans are acknowledged as a factor in protection, but are not presented as the main justification.
