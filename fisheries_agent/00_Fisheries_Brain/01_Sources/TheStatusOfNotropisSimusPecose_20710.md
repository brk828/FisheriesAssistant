# Status and Biology of the Bluntnose Shiner (*Notropis simus pecosensis*) in the Pecos River, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], assesses the status and biology of the Pecos River subspecies of the [[Bluntnose Shiner]], *Notropis simus pecosensis*. Surveys ran from 23 September 1981 to 1 February 1982 at 19 locations. The subspecies was collected at 10 sites (324 specimens) between Fort Sumner and Artesia. Its distribution has contracted, its abundance appears much lower than in historic collections, and it persists mainly in two groundwater seepage areas. The authors recommend [[Federal Listing]] as a threatened species and restriction of any [[Critical Habitat]] designation to those two seepage areas.

## Background & Description

*Notropis simus* is an endemic cyprinid of the Rio Grande and Pecos River drainages of the southwestern United States and adjacent Mexico. [[Chernoff et al. 1982]] recognized two subspecies:

- ***N. s. simus*** occurred in the [[Rio Grande]] from El Paso, Texas, north to Abiquiu on the Chama River. It was last collected in 1964 near Peña Blanca and is generally considered extinct.
- ***N. s. pecosensis*** is the only extant form. It occurs in the Pecos River from Santa Rosa south to the Major Johnson Springs area north of Carlsbad.

The bluntnose shiner has been listed as endangered by the State of New Mexico for several years. Its federal listing and critical habitat designation were under consideration. Field identifications were confirmed by Dr. William J. Koster and Dr. James E. Sublette (Appendix D).

**Methods (summary):**
- Collection methods were seining, electrofishing and minnow trapping, chosen by site conditions.
- Age was estimated from length-frequency modes (N = 324) and scale annuli.
- Maturity and fecundity were assessed from ovaries. Male reproduction was not determined.
- Habitat and species associations were scored by how often each species occurred with the subspecies in each habitat (index scores 21 to 0).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study segments.** The Pecos River study area has three segments:

| Segment | Extent | Character | Native fish species |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper | Santa Rosa to Lake Sumner (~72 km) | Rough terrain, swift flow, riffles, spring-fed | ~19 |
| Middle | Below Lake Sumner to Lake McMillan (~346 km) | Low relief, meandering, sand/silt substrate | ~31 |
| Lower | Below Lake McMillan ~10 km downstream | Stairstep morphology, spring-derived flow, cobble and pools | Similar to lower middle segment |

**Flow regime.** Irrigation dictates river flow. During the April–October irrigation season, average monthly flows at one gauge ranged from 23.0 m³/s to 0.42 m³/s, and daily flows fell to 0.14 m³/s or less. Winter (November–March) monthly averages rarely exceeded 2.26 m³/s and were often below 0.28 m³/s. Two perennial reaches persist because of groundwater seepage:
- South of Fort Sumner, extending about 56 km from about 16 km south of the town, with seepage of 0.28–9.85 m³/s.
- Between Hagerman and Artesia, with cumulative seepage of about 1.4 m³/s.

**Associated fauna.** Thirty-one species in 11 families were collected in the historic range, and 23 occur in the present restricted range. Predation or competition by these species was judged unlikely to be a significant decimating factor. Twenty species were sympatric with the subspecies. Sympatric species are summarized in the Key Findings.

## Key Findings

### Distribution and abundance
- **324 specimens** were collected at 10 sample sites from Fort Sumner to Artesia (282 stream km). Six historic sites yielded 159 specimens and four new sites yielded 165.
- The subspecies was **absent from the northern extreme** (Santa Rosa to just below Lake Sumner) and the **southern extreme** (below Lake McMillan to ~10 km downstream).
- It was absent from **site 9**, 11 km east of Roswell, where flows had been diverted from about 1 km of channel, leaving stagnant pools.
- It was most abundant from about 35 km south of Fort Sumner to Roswell (site 10), with reasonably numerous populations from Lake Arthur to Artesia.
- Abundance has apparently declined. Historic collections included 1482, 818 and 249 specimens. The largest single recent collection was 76 specimens. Comparisons are not strictly valid because sampling methods differed.

### Age structure and growth
- Age-0: 251 fish (77.5%); age-1: 60 fish (18.5%); age-2: 13 fish (4.0%).
- Length ranges (standard length, SL): age-0 18.0–32.5 mm; age-1 32.6–45.0 mm; age-2 45.1–56.5 mm.
- Age-0 fish were spread through the full Fort Sumner–Artesia reach (~282 km). Age-1 fish occurred over ~209 km. Age-2 fish were restricted to about 89 km, from 35 km south of Fort Sumner to the U.S. 70 crossing north of Roswell.

### Reproduction
- Spawning appears prolonged, based on wide length ranges within age groups and recently hatched fish late in the season. Field timing seems to have coincided with the end of spawning.
- Mature oocytes were found on 24 September 1981 in age-2 females and age-1 females ≥41 mm SL (52 mm TL). Smaller age-1 females had only immature oocytes and likely mature in their third growing season.
- October and November specimens were post-reproductive. Spawning likely ends shortly after September, but this needs confirmation.
- Fecundity data are limited to two fish: a 57 mm SL (72 mm TL) female with 1049 eggs, and a 51.0 mm SL (65.5 mm TL) female with 85 eggs. Egg diameters were unimodal, 0.6–1.15 mm (typically 0.96–1.00 mm).

### Habitat associations
- The subspecies occurred in every major habitat from Fort Sumner to Artesia except stagnant pools.
- **Main channel** (sandy substrate, low velocity, depths 17–41 cm): 264 specimens. The report's table gives 81.5% of the total, although the text states 31.5%. The table value matches 264/324 and is likely correct. Use of the main channel increased with age, reaching 100% at age-2.
- **Backwaters:** 44 specimens (13.6%). Young-of-year were common here, but no age group used this habitat preferentially.
- **Riffles:** 13 specimens (4.0%), used mainly by age-0 and age-1 fish.
- **Pools:** 3 specimens (0.9%).
- Past surveys suggested use of spring outflows in otherwise unsuitable areas. This study did not confirm it, but the authors believe springs near Santa Rosa and below Lake McMillan explain earlier records.

### Species associations
- Of 20 sympatric species, five are **frequent associates**: *Hybognathus placitus*, *Hybopsis aestivalis*, *Notropis jemezanus*, *Notropis lutrensis* and *Notropis stramineus*.
- The first three are described as ecologically equivalent to the bluntnose shiner, with similar habitat needs and nearly parallel distributions.
- Other sympatric species include *Cyprinus carpio*, *Dorosoma cepedianum*, *Gambusia affinis* and *Carpiodes carpio*.

### Flow dependence and population structure
- Seepage areas support **self-perpetuating populations**. The seepage area south of Fort Sumner had all three age groups, indicating survival of mature fish and successful reproduction. The Hagerman–Artesia seepage area had age-0 and age-1 fish but no age-2 fish.
- Between Acme and Dexter, flows are often absent. At Acme, flows were ≤0.28 m³/s for about a third of each water year and absent for about 10% of it. Few mature fish occur there, indicating heavy annual mortality. Recruitment likely comes from downstream seepage populations during sustained runoff, and young-of-year probably make up most fish there.

### Status and threats
- Distribution and numbers have declined significantly over the last 40 years.
- **Upper segment:** apparently extirpated. Lack of suitable habitat is the main limiting factor. Fish toxicants and altered flow regimes may have eliminated the subspecies from some spring systems.
- Migration into the upper segment is blocked by [[Sumner Dam]] and two irrigation diversion dams. Historic records show steady decline since Sumner Dam was completed in 1937.
- **Middle segment:** the only portion where the species is extant, but its status is tenuous because of irrigation-driven dewatering.
- **Lower segment:** apparently absent. The subspecies has probably not been prominent there since [[McMillan Dam]] was completed in 1893. Recent sporadic captures near Major Johnson Springs may come from released bait buckets, or from a relictual population the surveys did not detect.

### Recommendations
1. **Federal listing:** recommend listing as a threatened species. Restrict any critical habitat designation to the two groundwater seepage areas (south of Fort Sumner and Hagerman–Artesia), which are considered essential to continued existence.
2. **Monitor and protect** essential populations, especially during low flow, with attention to numbers, fish condition and age structure. Address groundwater over-use that threatens these habitats.
3. **Reestablish** populations within the historic range. Stocking from natural populations should occur where conditions support long-term survival. The Santa Rosa spring outflows are currently the only prospective introduction sites.
4. **Develop habitat** from Acme to Dexter, where the main limit is perennial flow. Constant flows of at least 0.57 m³/s are needed. Plans for [[Brantley Dam]] near Major Johnson Springs specify releases of at least 0.57 m³/s into a special channel designed to simulate preferred habitat.
5. **Hold and propagate** in a hatchery. Ten fish held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in a lentic facility failed. Four fish were moved to a lotic facility, and the outcome was undetermined at the time of writing. Continued work is recommended.
