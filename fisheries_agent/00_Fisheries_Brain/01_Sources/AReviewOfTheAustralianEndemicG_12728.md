# A Review of the Australian Endemic Gobiid Genus *Chlamydogobius*, with Description of Five New Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gobiidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic revision of the Australian endemic goby genus [[Chlamydogobius]] (Gobiidae, subfamily [[Gobionellinae]]) recognises six species, of which five are described or redescribed here and four of those are new. Five are freshwater spring and bore species of inland Queensland, South Australia and the Northern Territory; one, *C. ranunculus*, is estuarine and coastal in northern Australia. Species are separated by fin-ray counts, vertebral counts, scalation and colour pattern, and an artificial key is provided. One new species is considered endangered and two others vulnerable. The author argues that the genus is most closely related to [[Mugilogobius]], [[Pseudogobius]] and [[Hemigobius]], and that its inland radiation reflects Miocene dispersal followed by Pleistocene aridification isolating spring populations.

## Background & Description

*Chlamydogobius eremius* (Zietz, 1896), the [[Desert Goby]], is among the best-known gobies in Australia and is widely kept in aquaria. Its populations are increasingly recognised as isolated and at risk from pastoral activity and falling water tables. The genus was erected by Whitley (1930) without diagnosis; Miller (1987) treated it as monotypic and maintained it as separate from *Mugilogobius*.

Diagnostic features of the genus include:
- A single epural and 27–29 vertebrae (10–11 precaudal, 16–18 caudal)
- Head pores absent and lateral line pores lost
- Gill opening restricted to the pectoral base, with a smooth shoulder girdle
- Snout papillae reduced to one or two per row; usually only one mental papilla per side
- A long intestine with at least two full loops and a dark-pigmented peritoneum
- A pterygiophore formula of 3-12210

Methods followed Hubbs and Lagler (1970) with electronic calipers, radiography, and clearing and double-staining. Type material is held in the South Australian Museum (SAM), Australian Museum (AMS), Museum and Art Gallery of the Northern Territory (NTM) and Queensland Museum (QM).

**Species accounts (summarised):**
- ***C. eremius*** (redescribed; 77 specimens examined): Lake Eyre drainage of South Australia. Lectotype designated (SAM F.525, 51 mm SL male, Coward Springs Railway Bore). Second dorsal I,6–8, anal I,5–8, pectoral 12–14, usually 28 vertebrae (11+17). Modal caudal vertebrae 17.
- ***C. gloveri*** n. sp. (23 specimens): known only from Dalhousie Springs, northern South Australia. Holotype SAM F.3463, 30 mm female. Usually 17 caudal vertebrae, second dorsal I,7, anal I,6, pectoral 12. Predorsal scales present (13–18).
- ***C. japalpa*** n. sp. (84 specimens): Finke River system, Northern Territory. Holotype NTM S.11436-007, 44 mm SL male. Second dorsal I,7–8, anal I,5–8, pectoral 12–14, 28–29 vertebrae. Modal 18 caudal vertebrae; the only species with 19 recorded.
- ***C. micropterus*** n. sp. (37 specimens): Elizabeth Springs, western Queensland. Holotype QM I.25096, 22.5 mm male. First dorsal reduced, with III–VI spines (mode V). Pectoral 11–13. Preserved colour pale. Listed as endangered.
- ***C. ranunculus*** n. sp. (167 specimens): coastal northern Queensland and Northern Territory. Holotype NTM S.11427-001, 27.5 mm male, Beatrice Lagoon, Adelaide River drainage. Equal soft dorsal and anal rays (I,6), pectoral 11–13, usually 27 vertebrae, low-salinity (0–9‰) muddy habitats. Only coastal species of the genus.
- ***C. squamigenus*** n. sp. (46 specimens): Thomson River drainage, central Queensland. Holotype SAM F.6595, 34 mm male. Diagnostic for cycloid scales on the cheek (other species have naked cheeks). Pectoral 13–14, 28 vertebrae. Listed as vulnerable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

*C. eremius* tolerates pH 6.8–11.0, temperatures of 5–41°C and salinities of 0–60‰ for short periods, and has been collected at dissolved oxygen of 0.8 ppm, usually below 5 mg O₂/L. Horsthemke (1989) found no planktonic larval stage: hatchlings of 5.3 mm settle to the benthos quickly. Large females lay up to 300 eggs (Horsthemke), while Glover (1971) counted 150–250 eggs in wild-caught ovaries. Egg size and direct development contrast with *Mugilogobius*, which has a pelagic larval phase of four to five weeks.

Glover (1971) introduced 50 male and 50 female *C. eremius* into Blanche Cup Mound Spring in 1970; the population flourished. *C. gloveri* occurs in 30 of the 35 fish-inhabited springs at Dalhousie (89 springs in total). Its waters reach 21.6–43°C, and Kodric-Brown and Brown (1993) found the species had apparently gone extinct in one small spring since 1989. *C. ranunculus* tolerates "bombholes", buffalo wallows and town drains, and shows aquatic surface respiration under hypoxia (Gee and Gee 1991).

**Threats:** Falling water tables caused by artificial bores, stock trampling, and the introduced pest fish *Gambusia holbrooki* (present in Edgbaston pools) are the principal threats identified.

**Palaeobiogeography:** Miller (1987) estimated that *Chlamydogobius* entered the continent about 20 million years ago and became isolated in central Australia about 1.6 million years ago. Early to mid-Miocene conditions probably allowed gobiid dispersal inland. Aridification from the late Miocene/early Pliocene, and dune development in the Pleistocene, isolated populations. Allozyme data (Adams, pers. comm.) show *C. eremius* and *C. japalpa* are very close, differing at one locus, although the author treats them as separate species on morphological grounds.

## Key Findings

- The genus comprises six species: five freshwater spring/bore taxa and one estuarine species (*C. ranunculus*). Five of these are treated here, including four new species.
- Diagnostic characters are vertebral counts, fin-ray counts, scalation and colour pattern. *C. eremius* typically has 28 vertebrae (11+17) and *C. japalpa* has 29 (11+19 recorded).
- *C. micropterus* (Elizabeth Springs, Qld) and *C. squamigenus* (Thomson River drainage, Qld) are restricted to single spring complexes; *C. micropterus* is listed as endangered in the 1993 ASFB Threatened Fishes supplement, and *C. squamigenus* as vulnerable.
- *C. gloveri* is known only from Dalhousie Springs and is named for [[John Glover]], whose work on desert gobies underpinned the study.
- *C. micropterus* has the reduced first dorsal fin that gives it its name, and pale preserved colour lacking breeding-male pigment.
- Morphological and genetic evidence (fixed differences of 16–21% between *C. gloveri*, *C. eremius* and *C. micropterus*) supports the separation of populations isolated across the [[Lake Eyre Basin]] and the [[Finke River]] system.
- Conservation status is poorly known for most species; the author recommends investigation, citing Jackson (1993) and Harris (1987).
- The author proposes that the desert gobies' closest relatives are estuarine Indo-West Pacific gobies (*Mugilogobius*, *Pseudogobius*, *Hemigobius*), which likely reached Australia via New Guinea.
