# Introduction of the Bonneville Cisco (Prosopium gemmiferum Snyder) into Lake Tahoe, California and Nevada

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Salmonidae #coregonid #fish-management

## Executive Summary

This 1965 report documents the introduction of [[Bonneville Cisco]] (*Prosopium gemmiferum*) from [[Bear Lake]] (Utah-Idaho) into [[Lake Tahoe]] (California-Nevada) in January 1964. The introduction aimed to provide forage for [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*) in deep waters during summer months when natural forage fish were scarce. Approximately 5,500 adult cisco were transported and released, along with 40,000 fertilized eggs. The [[Bonneville Cisco]] is endemic to [[Bear Lake]], where it supports an important winter dipnet sport fishery, and this represented the first attempted range extension for the species.

## Background & Description

The [[Bonneville Cisco]] is one of three endemic whitefishes in [[Bear Lake]] and is found nowhere else naturally. It is among the smallest North American coregonids, reaching maximum total length under 8.5 inches. The species exhibits distinctive morphology with a long, sharply pointed snout and bright silvery sides that develop longitudinal gold and bronze bars during spawning season.

Growth is relatively rapid for the first 3 years then levels off. According to Perry (1943), total length in inches approximates 2.1, 4.1, 5.7, 6.5, 7.0, and 7.2 at ages 1-6 years respectively. Bangerter (1964) found fish aged 3-7 years in the dipnet fishery averaged 6.5, 7.1, 7.5, 7.8, and 8.1 inches total length. Perry believed genetic rather than environmental factors controlled growth rate.

The species feeds almost entirely on zooplankton with heavy year-round reliance on the copepod *Epischura*, except during spring when cladocerans (*Bosmina* and *Chydorus*) and the copepod *Cyclops* increase in importance. During spawning runs, cisco feed heavily on their own eggs. Bottom-dwelling invertebrates are taken more commonly in winter months when cisco move inshore.

Unlike other cisco and whitefish species, [[Bonneville Cisco]] exhibits an extremely low incidence of parasitism. The species matures in either the 2nd or 3rd year of life, with 5- and 6-year-old fish dominating spawning runs followed by 4- and 7-year-olds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Bear Lake Habitat and Distribution:**
[[Bear Lake]] has a maximum depth of 208 feet and is moderately oligotrophic. The [[Bonneville Cisco]] is numerically the most abundant species in the lake. Distribution correlates with depth, temperature, and zooplankton abundance, with most dense concentrations along the steep east shore. Cisco are widely distributed except during summer when they remain in and below the thermocline, preferring temperatures less than 59°F. Using bottom gill nets, cisco were most concentrated at 50 and 100 feet depths with lesser numbers at 150 feet.

**Lake Tahoe Conditions:**
[[Lake Tahoe]] is extremely oligotrophic with maximum depth of 1,645 feet. Both waters are cold with dissolved oxygen suitable for trout at all depths and seasons. During summer, [[Lake Trout]] inhabit depths from 100 to 500 feet. The primary forage fish, [[Piute Sculpin]] (*Cottus beldingii*), is most abundant at depths less than 100 feet with smaller numbers down to 300 feet and very few deeper. Other forage fishes ([[Tui Chub]] *Siphateles bicolor*, [[Lahontan Redside]] *Richardsonius egregius*, [[Tahoe Sucker]] *Catostomus tahoensis*) favor shallow, warmer waters from mid-May through October.

Lake trout under 15 inches fork length eat plankton primarily, followed by sculpin. By frequency of occurrence, these remain the most important food items in fish up to 20 inches long. The vast limnetic zone of [[Lake Tahoe]] is devoid of fish life except for relatively few [[Rainbow Trout]] and a small population of [[Kokanee Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*). This area supports unusually low densities of zooplankton, but plankton occurs commonly to 300 feet and in lesser numbers to 500 feet.

**Spawning Ecology:**
Cisco spawn along a rocky 2-mile section on the east shore of [[Bear Lake]] between North and South Eden creeks during a 2-week period in January, sometimes extending into February. Eggs are dispersed over rock and rubble substrate in shallow water, sometimes less than a foot deep. Water temperatures during spawning and egg incubation range from 32 to 39°F, usually 34 or 35°F. The sex ratio on spawning grounds averaged 2.4 to 6.4 males per female with daily ratios often exceeding 10:1. Ten female cisco averaged 1,301 eggs each.

**Food Web Relationships:**
In [[Bear Lake]], examination of 28 [[Lake Trout]] stomachs showed sculpin in the majority except during December and January when cisco was more common due to inshore spawning schools. Cisco and sculpin were the most important foods in 24 [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarkii*) stomachs, while 67 [[Rainbow Trout]] stomachs yielded primarily surface insects.

Literature surveys showed that rapid lake trout growth is associated with abundant forage fish populations. In the Great Lakes and large, deep Canadian lakes, ciscos (genus *Coregonus*) are the most abundant forage fishes and often comprise the bulk of lake trout diets.

## Key Findings

**Rationale for Introduction:**
1. Studies showed [[Lake Trout]] in [[Lake Tahoe]] lacked forage during summer months when they inhabit depths from 100 to 500 feet
2. Existing forage fishes remain relatively dormant close to cover during colder months, while cisco moves widely and should be more available
3. The species seeks colder waters year-round with seasonal movements (except spawning migrations) corresponding to those of lake trout
4. [[Bonneville Cisco]] may prove superior to other cisco species as forage because they are smaller and have extremely low incidence of parasitism

**Transportation and Release (January 1964):**
1. Two 1,000-gallon fish-planting trucks hauled 3,500 and 5,000 cisco respectively over 24 and 27 hours
2. Temperatures maintained between 42 and 50°F using combination spray and oxygen aeration
3. Approximately 4,600 males and 900 females were released alive at Cave Rock (east side) and Pebble Beach (west side) over rubble substrate
4. Mortality was 41% for first load and 31% for second load; of dead fish, 55% were females and 45% males
5. Only 6% of dead females were spent, indicating most retained eggs

**Egg Introduction:**
1. Approximately 45,000 eggs were taken and fertilized from angler-caught fish, transported in two 1-gallon thermos jugs at 32.5 to 42°F for 22-27 hours
2. About 5,000 eggs placed in hatchery incubator trays; some hatched but no fry lived longer than 23 days
3. Estimated 40,000 eggs dispersed through syringe underwater at Rocky Point (northwest shore) and south of Sand Harbor (east shore) over depths from 6 inches to 3 feet
4. Green eggs placed in cheesecloth sacks under rocks in 12 inches of water; several reached eyed-stage after 31 days, some alive after 45 days but survival was low

**Bear Lake Sport Fishery:**
A very popular and productive dipnet sport fishery occurs during the spawning period despite severe winter conditions. Sportsmen harvested 159,292 fish in 1962, 118,728 in 1963, and 106,863 in 1964. Cisco are considered a delicacy with some fishermen traveling over 400 miles round trip.

**Potential Concerns:**
1. Cisco might compete with lake trout for food and space, especially with small trout; authors believed this risk minimal since studies showed lake trout do well in presence of abundant forage
2. Habitat differences between [[Lake Tahoe]] (extremely oligotrophic, maximum depth 1,645 feet) and [[Bear Lake]] (moderately oligotrophic, maximum depth 208 feet) might prevent establishment

**Expected Benefits:**
1. Fill forage gap in deep water areas during summer
2. Winter inshore spawning migration may supplement food supply of [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*)
3. With cisco as forage, a trophy rainbow fishery may develop in the vast limnetic zone
