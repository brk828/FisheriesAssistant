# Sixty-Two Years of Service to Ichthyology: Dr. Carl L. Hubbs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This special feature from *Tropical Fish Hobbyist* (April 1978) by Dr. Martin R. Brittan profiles [[Carl L Hubbs]], described as the dean of American ichthyologists at age 83. Over 62 years Hubbs published well over 400 papers, trained numerous doctoral students, built major fish collections at the University of Michigan and Scripps Institution of Oceanography, and worked on taxonomy, genetics, conservation and aquarium fishes. The remainder of the document consists of a subscription notice and a product advertisement for an aquarium heater.

## Background & Description

- **Honors:** Elected to the [[National Academy of Sciences]]. Colleagues named five genera and 22 species of fishes, plus additional taxa of lichens, algae, cave arthropods, molluscs, insects, a whale, a bird, a crab and a dry Nevada lake in his honor.
- **Birth and early life:** Born October 18, 1894, in Williams, Arizona, two months prematurely after his mother became lost in a horse-drawn wagon and was found by a train engineer on the [[Santa Fe Railroad]]. He grew up in southern California (San Diego, Turlock), where he fished and studied birds.
- **Education:** At Los Angeles Junior College he was influenced by George B. Culver, who sent him to [[David Starr Jordan]] at Stanford University. Hubbs published 13 scientific papers while still a student. He married fellow student Laura Clark in 1915; she later worked alongside him as a zoologist and geneticist.
- **Career path:**
  - Field Museum of Natural History, Chicago (first position, pupfish work).
  - University of Michigan, 1920–1944: built one of the largest fish collections in the world, focusing on American fishes; supervised 28 Ph.D. students; awarded his own Ph.D. there in 1927.
  - Scripps Institution of Oceanography, La Jolla, 1944–1969 (retired as Professor): expanded work on eastern Pacific fishes, deep-sea and fossil fishes, marine mammals and birds, California coastal Indian archaeology, and conservation; supervised over 20 Ph.D. students there.
- **Family:** Daughter Frances married ichthyologist [[Robert Rush Miller]]; sons Clark and Earl also contributed to the field (Clark as an ichthyologist, Earl as a biology teacher). Sister-in-law Frances Clark was a leading biostatistician for the California Department of Fish and Game.
- **Output:** Averaged 8 to 12 articles and monographs per year during the Michigan period.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Taxonomic focus:** Studies of [[Cyprinodontidae]] (egg-laying killifishes, including *Cyprinodon*), [[Poeciliidae]] and [[Goodeidae]] (live-bearers), [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes, where hybridization experiments clarified confused nomenclature), Great Lakes and Midwest fishes, Pacific Coast fishes, and western U.S. freshwater fishes.
- **Conservation:** Hubbs was an active fighter for conservation and a long-time participant in the [[Desert Fishes Council]], which meets in Death Valley and works to protect endangered endemic fishes of the southwestern United States and their fragile ecosystems.
- **Field work:** Collecting trips included Hot Creek Valley, Nevada (1934), and deep-sea night trawling and whale observation aboard the Scripps research vessel *Paolina T.*

## Key Findings

- Hubbs's career spanned more than six decades, with over 400 publications and a broad range from taxonomy to genetics and conservation.
- His hybridization experiments with sunfishes helped resolve a "taxonomic-nomenclatoral mess" in Centrarchidae.
- His studies of egg-laying killifishes and live-bearers are described as central to both his taxonomic and genetic work, including collaborations with Bob Miller and Laura Hubbs.
- He trained a large number of ichthyologists (28 Ph.D. students at Michigan and over 20 at Scripps); many of his students are now leaders in American ichthyology.
- The article credits Hubbs with inspiring conservationists and aquarists alike.
- Cited biographical sources include Horn (1976), Norris (1974), Hubbs, L. C. (1974) and Shor (1974).
