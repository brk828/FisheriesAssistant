# Spawning Ecology of Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in the Little Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the spawning ecology of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the lower [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), the largest remaining population of the species. A three-year field study (spring, March–June 1993–1995) combined fish captures, [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging]], and habitat measurements with weekly discharge and temperature records to determine spawning phenology and habitat use.

Spawning began in late March, peaked in mid-April, and waned in mid-May. Ripe males remained present throughout the season, while ripe females were relatively abundant only in April. Ripe fish aggregated in structurally complex habitat with clean gravel. The authors infer that part of the adult population migrates from the [[Colorado River]] into the LCR to spawn and then returns. The authors state that the findings should help identify spawning habitat for establishing additional spawning stocks.

## Background & Description

*Gila cypha* was not formally described until 1945 (Miller 1946), because the remote, turbulent [[Grand Canyon]] hindered earlier ichthyological work. The species has been reported from several canyon reaches of the upper Colorado River, including Black Rocks, Westwater, Cataract, Desolation/Gray and Yampa canyons. The authors report that its range has been reduced by more than 70% during the 20th century through human activity in the Colorado River. The species was listed as endangered in 1967, and recovery plans for the upper basin populations were approved in 1990.

Only six isolated reproducing populations remain. Habitat in these swift, deep, turbid canyon rivers makes direct observation difficult. Earlier habitat studies relied on nets, traps and electrofishing, which gave general habitat descriptions with little detail on spawning. Spawning in the genus *Gila* is generally considered broadcast spawning (Johnston & Page 1993). Reported spawning substrates for the species include gravel and cobble bars, boulder shorelines, and sand-boulder eddies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Two reaches were sampled in the lower 21 km of the perennially flowing LCR: a lower reach near Powell Canyon (2.3–3.4 km above the confluence) and an upper reach near Salt Trail Canyon (10.5–11.9 km above the confluence). Perennial base flow of about 6.31 m³/s is maintained by [[Blue Springs]], which feed warm (about 20°C), chloride-rich, carbon dioxide-charged water saturated with calcium carbonate. This setting produces ongoing [[Travertine]] deposition, forming reefs, terraces and dams in the lower 14.5 km.

**Hydrology.** Snowmelt floods occur in February–March, with discharge ranging from 15 to more than 50 m³/s. In contrast, mainstem Colorado River flows are 150–600 m³/s. Mean water temperature was below 12°C in February and above 20°C by mid-May. Mean temperature rose above 15°C in mid-April. The authors cite 14–15°C as the threshold for spawning and egg incubation (Hamman 1982, Marsh 1985).

**Community.** Humpback chub numerically dominated the LCR fish community in the study. Other native species included [[Speckled Dace]] (1,356), [[Bluehead Sucker]] (352) and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (25). Non-native species included Fathead Minnow (52) and Plains Killifish (2).

## Key Findings

**Sampling and population.**
- Across the two reaches, 1,597 net sets (March–June 1993–1995) captured 628 adults (>200 mm TL), 678 older juveniles (100–200 mm TL) and 782 yearlings (80–100 mm TL).
- Most adults (463/628) came from the upper reach, so habitat analyses used that reach only.
- Monthly CPUE of adults increased from March to May as discharge fell, then declined sharply in June. Large adults (>300 mm TL) dropped to near zero by August, while smaller adults declined by only half, suggesting that many smaller adults remain resident in the LCR.

**Spawning phenology.**
- Spawning began between late March and early April, peaked in April, and waned in May. Most adults were spent by early summer.
- The proportion of ripe-spawning fish peaked in April at 41%. Non- and near-spawning fish peaked in May at 84%.
- Mean relative condition (K) fell from 1.22 (females) and 1.08 (males) in March to 0.99 and 0.98 in June. December values (1.06 and 1.01) suggest condition peaks before spawning.
- Females ripened rapidly in March–April and were spent by May–June. Males stayed reproductively active longer. Of males, 46% were ripe, versus 7% of females.

**Movement and migration.**
- Of 603 adults captured in the study reaches, 112 (18.4%) were also captured at the [[Colorado River-Little Colorado River Confluence]]. The proportions were 23.2% in the lower reach and 16.9% in the upper reach.
- A greater share of large adults (26%) and near- and ripe-spawning fish (21%) were also captured at the confluence than smaller adults (11%) or non-spawning fish (16%).
- Some fish migrated about 18 km upstream to the upper reach.
- Within-reach recapture distances averaged 172 m within years and 325 m between years. Only 2 of 97 recaptured fish were caught in both reaches, indicating fidelity to specific sections.

**Spawning behavior and habitat.**
- Ripe males aggregated in areas of complex structure with large boulders, travertine masses, chutes, runs and eddies at depths of 0.5–2.0 m, and were associated with clean gravel.
- Ripe females moved to these male aggregations. Near-spawning females used habitats similar to non-spawning fish: mid-channel, less structurally complex, and with more travertine but less gravel. These groups were often within 50 m of ripe aggregations.
- Of 21 ripe aggregation sites, 17 were within or near (<40 m) travertine-boulder complexes. Three consistent clusters (A, B and C) occurred in all three years. Cluster A lies below a travertine dam; clusters B and C lie at the mouths of Big and Salt Trail canyons.
- The three clusters made up only 68 m (5%) of the upper reach transects but captured 68% of ripe fish.

**Statistical results.**
- Fish were significantly clumped in nets containing ripe fish. The overall catch rate was 1.98 fish per net, rising to 3.22 with ripe fish present and 3.92 when a ripe female was present.
- 70% (76/109) of ripe fish were aggregated, and 86% of these were males.
- Logistic regression and discriminant analyses identified SDPVA (standard deviation of positive vertical angle, a measure of bottom angular variation) and FGRAVL (frequency of gravel) as the strongest predictors of ripe aggregations. The discriminant model classified groups correctly 74% overall, including 85% of non-spawning fish, 22% of near-spawning fish and 75% of ripe aggregations.

**Abrasions and diel activity.**
- Abrasions were found on 72 adults. Of these, 25 (35%) were tuberculate and 34 (47%) were ripe or gravid. Abrasions appeared in April and May (about 15% incidence) and were mostly on the ventral fins, particularly the anal and caudal fins.
- Nearly 80% of ripe-spawning fish were captured at night, suggesting spawning occurs mainly at dusk or at night.

**Comparison with other species.**
- The authors contrast humpback chub with the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), where up to 500 fish were observed spawning in mass aggregations at Lake Mohave in 1954. In the LCR, aggregations were small (about 2–6 fish), numerically dominated by males, and consistent across years.

**Proposed spawning scenario.** Winter and spring floods deposit clean gravel behind boulders and travertine structures. As flows approach base level in March–April, ripe males gather at gravel sites. Ripe females join them, sometimes spawning with two or more males simultaneously. Eggs are semi-adhesive, and the authors suggest they are deposited on gravel and fertilized there. Males are thought not to guard sites. Individual fish repeat this cycle until their gametes are exhausted, usually by mid-May.

**Recommendations.** The authors recommend sampling gravel in aggregation areas for eggs and embryos, direct behavioral observation, further analysis of PIT tag records, and GIS mapping of available spawning habitat. They also suggest modifying flows in regulated reaches such as Grand Canyon to encourage spawning.
