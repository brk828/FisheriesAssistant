# Effects of Capture by Trammel Nets on Native Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #freshwater-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This conference abstract, presented at the 2008 Joint Annual Meeting of the [[Arizona Chapter of The Wildlife Society]] and the [[Arizona and New Mexico Chapters of the American Fisheries Society]], reports a controlled experiment on the survival of two native fish species after capture by [[Trammel Net]] sampling. It won a Fisheries Oral Student Award. The authors, Teresa Hunt and Alice Gibb, were affiliated with [[Northern Arizona University]] and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]. Treated fish showed substantial delayed mortality after trammel net capture, while controls showed very low mortality. The authors recommend that agencies reconsider trammel net sampling for imperiled fish populations.

## Background & Description

The study focused on adult (150–300 mm total length) individuals of two native species:

- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a federally imperiled catostomid.
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), a cyprinid native to the Southwest.

Trammel nets are a common sampling gear in fisheries work. The abstract's concern is that the stress of entanglement and capture might cause delayed deaths that standard survey protocols do not record. This is relevant to managing species already in decline.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Test temperatures:** 15, 20, and 25 °C. The authors state that observed capture effects were likely temperature-dependent and species-specific.
- **Holding conditions:** Fish were held for 2 weeks in outdoor 18,000-liter tanks, then reacclimated for a further 14+ days in a separate holding tank, where delayed mortality was monitored.
- **Comparison capture method:** A [[Seine Net]] was used for the control group, which was not entangled in a trammel net.
- **Potential sublethal effects:** The authors suggest that stress from trammel net capture, even among survivors, could impair foraging ability and affect later fitness.

## Key Findings

1. **High mortality after trammel net capture:** Fish captured by trammel net experienced very high mortality, reported as "up to" a percentage that is garbled in the scanned text and cannot be reliably recovered.
2. **Delayed mortality:** Deaths occurred over the following weeks, not only during or immediately after capture.
3. **Low control mortality:** Seine-captured control fish showed very low mortality. The exact control percentage is also illegible in the scan.
4. **Temperature and species dependence:** Observed capture effects were likely shaped by water temperature and differed between species.
5. **Sublethal concerns:** Surviving fish may suffer impaired foraging, which the authors suggest could reduce long-term survival.
6. **Undocumented mortality:** Delayed deaths have likely gone unrecorded in past studies because of the time gap between capture and death.
7. **Management recommendation:** Agencies should evaluate whether trammel net sampling is appropriate for imperiled fish populations.
