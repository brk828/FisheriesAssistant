# Seasonal Variation in Selected Nutrients of a River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1970 study by [[H. R. MacCrimmon]] and [[J. R. M. Kelso]] (Department of Zoology, University of Guelph) tracked biweekly water chemistry at five stations on the [[Grand River]] system in southern Ontario from August 16, 1967, to September 1, 1968. The city of [[Guelph]] was the dominant source of organic enrichment: station II, located 6.5 km below the city on the [[Speed River]], showed the most extreme nutrient levels, low dissolved oxygen, and high ammonia and orthophosphate. Downstream stations showed progressive recovery through natural processes, even within an intensively farmed watershed. The authors also argue that spot sampling is inadequate for understanding river nutrient dynamics and water quality.

No fish were sampled. The paper addresses water chemistry and nutrient dynamics only; the document does not report on fish or other aquatic organisms directly, beyond general references to effects on stream life.

## Background & Description

The study addresses a gap in knowledge about nutrient changes along a watershed "from source to mouth." The authors focus on parameters widely accepted as indicators of water productivity:

- Nitrogen forms (ammonia, nitrate-N, nitrite)
- Phosphorus (measured as [[Orthophosphate]])
- [[Silica]], [[Sulfate]], [[Iron]], [[Chloride]]
- Carbon forms in buffer systems ([[Carbon Dioxide]], [[Alkalinity]], hardness)

The [[Grand River Watershed]] is described as the largest catchment in the Great Lakes basin. The study area covered about 3300 km², roughly half the total drainage basin.

**Sampling stations:**

| Station | Location |
|---|---|
| I | Speed River headwaters above Guelph; surrounded by cedar swamp and few pastures |
| II | Speed River, 6.5 km below Guelph; affected by domestic and industrial effluents |
| III | Grand River immediately below the Speed–Grand confluence |
| IV | Grand River, 84 km from Lake Erie |
| V | Grand River, 45 km from Lake Erie |

Stations III–V lie in a mixed agricultural landscape.

**Methods (summary):** Samples were collected biweekly, 10:00 a.m.–12:00 noon EST, in HCl-washed polyethylene bottles without preservatives, and analyzed within about 3 hours. Analyses followed [[American Public Health Association Standard Methods]] (1962) and Hach procedures, using a portable laboratory. Dissolved oxygen was measured by the azide-modified [[Winkler Method]]. Statistical comparison used analysis of variance (randomized complete block design) and least significant difference tests at the 5% level. Discharge data came from the Ontario Inland Waters Branch.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dissolved oxygen** fell below 5 mg/L in summer at all stations, indicating organic loading. Station II had the lowest concentrations and the widest range (1.7–15 mg/L). DO was inversely related to water temperature.
- **Temperature** peaked at 26.3 °C (July 17, 1968) at stations IV and V. Station I had the narrowest range (0.2–16.2 °C).
- **Discharge** was highest in March and December and lowest from June through August.
- **Carbon dioxide and pH** showed an inverse relation. pH ranged from 7.25 to 8.81, and CO₂ from 0.0 to 14.5 mg/L. Lowest pH (7.2–7.5) coincided with high CO₂ (8.5–14.5 mg/L) during high discharge.
- **Alkalinity and hardness** were typical of regions with calcareous bedrock. Both were highest in late summer, fall, and early winter, and lower during high discharge (45–50 mg/L CaCO₃). The authors attribute the seasonal pattern to biological carbonate uptake in spring and summer, with carbonate returning to solution in cooler months.
- **Chloride** was low at station I, indicating modest pollution. Maximum levels (60–87 mg/L) at station II reflected city effluent. Elevated hardness and chloride at stations IV and V were attributed to local gypsum deposits.
- **Ammonia** was highest in January–February and at station II in summer (1.5–2.5 mg/L). Maximum recorded: 2.95 mg/L. Concentrations above 1 mg/L indicate organic pollution, and above 2.5 mg/L may be detrimental or lethal to aquatic organisms.
- **Nitrite** was lowest in winter and spring and highest in summer (0.025–0.180 mg/L). It followed nitrate only loosely.
- **Nitrate-N** showed no clear cyclic pattern; high values (1.24–1.90 mg/L) appeared irregularly in winter and generally declined as discharge rose.
- **Orthophosphate** ranged from 0.02–0.21 mg/L at station I to its highest and most variable levels at station II. Polyphosphates averaged about 40% above orthophosphate in eight samples.
- **Silica** peaked (9–11 mg/L) in late fall and winter, and was lowest in spring and summer (down to 1.1 mg/L at station III). The authors link this cycle to plankton production driven by temperature.
- **Iron** ranged from 0 to 0.73 mg/L and generally rose with discharge, reflecting suspended material and turbidity.
- **Sulfate** was highly variable and generally fell with high discharge. Concentrations over 250 mg/L at stations IV and V were attributed to gypsum. The authors state that sulfate levels (24–599 mg/L) were not limiting to algal growth.

## Key Findings

1. **Source of enrichment:** Guelph's effluent was the major source of organic enrichment. Station II had the highest mean levels of all inorganic nitrogen forms and orthophosphate, and the lowest dissolved oxygen.
2. **Downstream recovery:** Nutrient levels decreased progressively at stations III, IV, and V, indicating partial recovery through natural repurification despite intensive agriculture.
3. **Consistent vs. irregular patterns:** Dissolved oxygen, CO₂, pH, alkalinity, hardness, ammonia, nitrite, and silica followed consistent seasonal patterns at all sites. Nitrate-N, orthophosphate, iron, and sulfate varied irregularly over the 13-month period.
4. **Mean differences among stations:** Mean alkalinity did not differ significantly among sites. Ammonia at stations III–V was statistically related. Nitrate-N means did not differ significantly among stations II–V.
5. **Limits of spot sampling:** The authors conclude that biweekly spot sampling documents changes at specific times but provides limited insight into limnological processes. They call for long-term studies and publication of methods to improve understanding of eutrophication and water quality control.
