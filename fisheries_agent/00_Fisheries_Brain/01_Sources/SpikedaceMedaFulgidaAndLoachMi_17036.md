# Spikedace and Loach Minnow Relative to Introduced Red Shiner

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared by [[Arizona State University]] for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (with the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]), investigates whether the introduced [[Red Shiner]] contributes to the decline of two federally threatened native cyprinids, the [[Spikedace]] and the [[Loach Minnow]], in the [[Gila River Basin]]. The study combined comparative field sampling of habitat and diet across seven stream reaches in five streams (May–September 1987, with some 1989 collections) with an experimental [[Artificial Stream System]]. Field data showed spikedace occupied significantly slower currents when syntopic with shiners. Experiments showed spikedace shifting into slower water when shiners were present, driven largely by agonistic shiner behavior during spawning. Dietary overlap was minimal, so competition for food was judged a minor factor. The authors conclude that displacement occurred but that population-level effects remain unproven.

## Background & Description

**Study context.** The work was a 30-month contract. It tested the hypothesis that competition is a mechanism by which red shiner replaces spikedace. The report has eight sections covering species background, field methods, physical habitat, loach minnow studies, habitat and food overlap, experimental work, and conclusions.

**Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*).** A small, slender, monotypic cyprinid endemic to the upper Gila River system of Arizona and New Mexico. It is now restricted to scattered reaches, including [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Eagle Creek]], the upper [[Verde River]], and the mainstem Gila in New Mexico. It is listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and is classed as threatened in Arizona and as a Group 2 endangered species in New Mexico. Adults average about 40 mm SL at one year and 50–63 mm at two; longevity is typically one to two years. Breeding occurs in spring (April–June) over sandy-gravelly riffles. Fecundity is 90–250 ova. Adults are drift-feeding insectivores, mainly on ephemeropterans and trichopterans.

**Loach minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*).** A small, bottom-dwelling cyprinid with a reduced gas bladder. It is endemic to the Gila basin (Arizona, New Mexico, and formerly Sonora). It is listed as threatened and has been extirpated from Mexico. Remaining AZ populations occur in the White River, Aravaipa Creek, and the Blue River drainage. Fecundity is about 150–250 mature ova per female. Eggs are deposited adhesively on the underside of flattened rocks, and males guard the nest. Larvae are about 5 mm at hatching. Age-0 fish reach 30 to more than 40 mm by mid-summer, and age-I fish average near 55 mm SL. Loach minnow are benthic insectivores that rarely enter the drift.

**Red shiner (*Cyprinella lutrensis*).** A moderately small cyprinid native to the Mississippi and Gulf drainages. It was introduced to the lower Colorado River in the early 1950s and has since become one of the most abundant fishes in the Gila basin. It tolerates intermittency, high turbidity, high temperature, and low dissolved oxygen. In its native range it is most common in low-gradient, silt-sand habitats. Fecundity was reported at 485–684 eggs in Iowa. One captive female produced 113,510 eggs across 200 clutches over 21 months. Shiners are opportunistic omnivores.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline.** Native fish losses were attributed to habitat destruction and alteration. These included dewatering, impoundment, channelization, livestock grazing, mining, agriculture, and water diversion. Non-native predators and competitors, including channel catfish, flathead catfish, largemouth and smallmouth bass, and mosquitofish, also contributed. The report stresses that habitat alteration and non-native interaction likely acted together, and that it is difficult to separate their effects.

**Co-occurring fishes.** In the field, spikedace co-occur with loach minnow, speckled dace, Sonora and desert suckers, and longfin dace. Loach minnow co-occur with speckled dace, suckers, and longfin dace, and are most likely to interact with channel and flathead catfish. Red shiner now occupies every place where spikedace and loach minnow were formerly present, but the report notes that it is absent or rare where the natives persist.

**Study streams.** The seven reaches were East and West [[Aravaipa Creek]], [[Blue River]], [[Gila River]] at Gila and at Redrock (New Mexico), [[Sycamore Creek]], and [[Verde River]]. Reach-level habitat means were:
- Mean depth: 19.0 cm (Blue) to 28.1 cm (Verde).
- Mean current velocity: 15.3 cm/s (Sycamore) to 62.2 cm/s (East Aravaipa).
- Mean weighted substrate: 41.9 (Sycamore) to 142.3 (Verde).

**Diet and habitat overlap.** Spikedace diets were dominated by trichopterans, ephemeropterans, and chironomids. Red shiner diets were dominated by organic matter, ephemeropterans, and chironomids. Overlap indices (Horn, Levins, Schoener) were below the 0.60 threshold in the only syntopic comparison (Verde River).

## Key Findings

1. **Habitat shift in the wild.** At the two syntopic sites (Gila-Redrock and Verde), spikedace occupied significantly faster currents with shiners than alone (40.4 vs. 36.3 cm/s; F = 4.34, p < 0.03). Spikedace alone were in shallower water (19.8 vs. 28.9 cm) and smaller substrates (38.9 vs. 75.2). The authors interpret this as displacement.
2. **Slow-water preference.** Spikedace were found in significantly slower water than available habitat (39.3 vs. 45.6 cm/s; F = 22.93, p < 0.0001). At East Aravaipa, occupied velocity was 46.2 cm/s versus 73.6 cm/s where absent.
3. **Shiner habitat.** Shiners occupied slower, deeper water where present (19.4 vs. 37.6 cm/s, F = 86.2, p < 0.0001). At Sycamore Creek, they were in isolation from spikedace and selected deeper, slower water.
4. **Loach minnow habitat.** Occupied habitat was shallow, swift riffles. Mean occupied velocity across reaches was 46.4 cm/s, and mean depth was 16.5 cm (n = 196). Loach minnow occurred in only 1 of 102 samples at West Aravaipa. Co-occurrence with shiners was limited to Blue River, with two samples.
5. **Food habits.** Loach minnow feeding was crepuscular, with fullness at noon and midnight of 45% and 14% at East Aravaipa, and 88% and 73% at Blue River. Spikedace diets were dominated by trichopterans, with pupae at 28% of volume at East Aravaipa. Diversity was highest at East Aravaipa (23 items) and lowest at Verde (10 items, n = 11).
6. **Minimal dietary overlap.** The only syntopic comparison (Verde River) showed no overlap above 0.60 by any measure. Two hypothetical allopatric comparisons exceeded 0.60, but the authors judged these unjustified. The conclusion was that competition for food plays a minor role, if any.
7. **Artificial stream experiment.** Nine pool-run units were stocked with spikedace alone, shiners alone, or both (20 fish per unit; 10 of each when mixed). Spikedace shifted to slower currents in August, coincident with spawning. Shiners showed agonistic chasing, and spikedace occupied mid-run (subunit C2) more often when shiners were present. Shiner distribution was unchanged by spikedace. A Kolmogorov-Smirnov two-sample test gave χ² = 12.27 (p < 0.01) for spikedace alone vs. spikedace plus shiner.
8. **Limits.** The experiment lasted only six months and was terminated early by flooding and reduced stock. Field and experimental statistical tests were not identical. Population-level effects, predation on juveniles, and reproductive interference remain unknown.
9. **Overall conclusion.** Spikedace was displaced into atypical habitats in both nature and captivity. Red shiner aggression was observed, but the report states it remains unproven whether shiners cause spikedace declines and what mechanism would operate. Long-term life-cycle studies in controlled systems were recommended.
