# Problems of Trout Management in Michigan (1954)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Fish Division Pamphlet No. 13 (June 1954), by Albert S. Hazzard of the [[Michigan Department Of Conservation]] Institute for Fisheries Research, argues that rising angler demand is outpacing the trout supply in Michigan. Habitat work cannot greatly raise the natural harvest, and [[Trout Stocking]] supports only a limited share of the catch. The pamphlet reports experiments on [[Hooking Mortality]] showing that natural bait kills far more undersized trout than [[Artificial Flies]] do. It also reports on the [[North Branch Au Sable River]] regulation experiment, where a 10-inch limit and flies-only rule improved the wild trout population. It closes with recommendations on bait restrictions, creel limits, size limits and season timing.

## Background & Description

The pamphlet covers three trout species: [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]]. Brook trout are the native salmonid of most cold streams. Brown trout grow larger and tend to mature later. Rainbows rarely persist as permanent residents in Michigan streams.

Management is shaped by the growing number of anglers and the limits of the resource. Key figures include:

- **Stream yield:** Michigan streams produce 8.5 to 13.25 pounds of trout per acre per year. For a stream 16.5 feet wide, this is 136 to 212 one-eighth-pound trout per mile (average 174), or about 17 daily limits per season.
- **Stream extent and harvest:** Hazzard estimates 15,000 miles of trout stream, giving a total wild-fish harvest of about 2,610,000 trout, or about 13 per licensed fisherman.
- **Licenses:** 206,000 trout stamps were sold in 1953.
- **Stocking:** 1,264,550 legal-sized trout were planted in 1953. At an assumed 50 percent recovery, this adds about 700,000 fish, or about 3 per licensed fisherman.
- **Lake planting:** Roughly 300 lakes and ponds were planted from 1951 to 1953. Few have spawning-quality inlet streams.
- **Free-fishing group:** Intensive creel censuses indicate about 20 percent of trout anglers fish free (children under 17 and wives of resident license holders). Hazzard estimates 247,200 trout fishermen in total, with an average annual take of about 13 trout each.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishing pressure.** Non-resident license sales rose from 18,000 in 1914 to 297,861 in 1952, with dips during the Depression and wartime. Resident trout anglers rose from about 60,000 in 1928 to 180,000, at least threefold. Better roads, faster transport and shorter work weeks add to this pressure.

**Declining catch rates.** A general [[Creel Census]] based on conservation officer records shows a steady decline in catch per hour on trout waters. No comparable decline appears on non-trout waters or the Great Lakes. The "good old days" of at least one legal trout per hour ended in the early 1930s. Planting and [[Habitat Improvement]] have not offset rising pressure.

**Habitat limits.** Hazzard notes that even ideal habitat yields only modest annual poundage. Lakes add little wild production because few have natural spawning areas.

**Hooking and bait.** Hooks often lodge in the gullet, gills or other vital tissue, so the method of hooking is a major source of death for undersized trout.

## Key Findings

**1. Natural bait is far more lethal than artificial flies.**
- In the [[Hunt Creek]] screened-section experiments (1951–1952), 2.7 percent (23 of 847) of trout hooked on flies died, versus 36.8 percent (331 of 899) hooked on worms. Hazzard calculates that the worm was 13.6 times as deadly.
- The 1951 average was 37.5 percent mortality for worms and 1.7 percent for flies, about 22 times higher for worms.
- In one 1952 pair of tests, 96 of 165 trout (58 percent) caught on worm-baited hooks died, compared with 0 of 159 on flies. A second pair showed 34 of 140 (27 percent) on bait versus 2 of 112 on flies.
- For brook trout, 6 of 181 fly-caught fish died, compared with 42 percent of worm-caught fish.
- The largest bait hook tested gave the lowest bait mortality (20 percent), but even large hooks were often lethal when the bait could be swallowed.

**2. Hook location determines survival.**
- 76 percent of fly-caught brook trout were jaw-hooked. Only 2 of 84 fly-hooked brook trout died.
- 43 percent of worm-caught brook trout were gullet-hooked, and more than half of those died. Of 83 worm-hooked brook trout, 33 died.
- A hook lodged in the gullet can pierce the liver or heart. Gill, cheek, roof-of-mouth and tongue-base hooking were also lethal.
- Average size of fish caught was about the same for flies and worms, and the method did not greatly affect the size of fish that died.

**3. Recommendations on natural bait.** Hazzard recommends outlawing natural bait in good trout water (about 5 percent of total stream mileage) and in smaller trout lakes where artificial lures can provide an adequate harvest. He stresses that this is a conservation measure, not a sporting-ethics judgment.

**4. North Branch Au Sable River regulation experiment.**
- E. L. Cooper's growth study found that brook trout were over-harvested under the 7-inch limit, and most females were taken before their first spawn.
- A 10-inch size limit began on the restricted section in 1949. In 1950 the section was limited to [[Artificial Flies]] only, with a daily limit of 10 trout (no more than 5 brook trout). The restricted water extended about three miles downstream to Eamon's Dam.
- On the 10-inch water, the number of successful trips (one or more legal trout) has risen steadily. Almost all brook trout taken in 1950 were in their third year of life. In later years, more older fish were caught.
- Hazzard reports that the [[Twin Bridge Area]] brook trout population grew from 115 in 1948 to 420 in 1953 (about three times larger), with no decline in growth rate.
- In 1953 the brook trout population on the 10-inch water was twice that on the 7-inch water.
- Brown trout were more abundant on the 7-inch water, both under and over 5 inches.
- Stocking of 6,000 to 8,000 marked brook trout on the 7-inch water added to the catch there. Hazzard concludes that the 10-inch limit combined with "flies only" is the main cause of improved fishing on the restricted water.

**5. Size limits and maturity.**
- Most female brook trout in large productive streams first spawn at the end of their third growing season, averaging about 8.5 inches.
- Brown trout rarely spawn before the third or fourth season, at an average of 11 inches.
- Hazzard concludes that the 7-inch limit gives no protection to first spawning in large Michigan streams. In small, cold streams the limit may be adequate, provided hooking mortality is controlled.

**6. Creel limits.** A 1939 intensive creel census on seven streams found that only 6 percent of trips yielded more than 8 trout, and 50 percent of trips produced no trout. Hazzard suggests that reducing the daily limit to 8 would affect few anglers. It would spread the harvest and curb "meat fishing," especially in recently planted water.

**7. Season timing.**
- A [[Electric Shocker]] and creel study by E. L. Cooper (April 1951 to March 1952) showed that condition factor rises sharply from mid-April to a mid-June peak and falls in late summer. Winter is the low point.
- Postponing the opening of the general season two weeks (to the second Saturday in May) would give brook trout 0.7 inch more length and 69 percent more weight.
- Hazzard recommends that the season open on the second Saturday in May and that the general season close on Labor Day, except on designated fall rainbow waters. This would still allow more than 100 open days (110–122).

**8. Management priorities.** Hazzard's central conclusion is that as demand grows, the state must reduce the kill of undersized trout, especially on heavily fished streams. This means restricting bait, adjusting size limits and creel limits, and timing the season to match trout condition. He notes that stocking still matters for inexperienced anglers and for the tourist economy.
