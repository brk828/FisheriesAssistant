# Little Colorado Spinedace: Distribution, Variation, and Ecology of a Rare Endemic Cyprinid

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports on *Lepidomeda vittata* (the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]]), a rare freshwater minnow of the tribe [[Plagopterini]] that is endemic to the [[Little Colorado River]] basin of eastern Arizona. The species was nearly eliminated between 1939 and 1960. A single adult was collected from a dry-channel pool in [[Clear Creek]] in August 1960, and a sizable breeding population was discovered in East Clear Creek in July 1961. The author argues that the species now survives mainly in the Clear Creek drainage, where its persistence depends on the protection of that stream from chemical treatment. The paper also provides meristic and pigmentation data, describes sexual characters, and summarizes habitat and associated fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The species belongs to the distinctive tribe Plagopterini. [[Miller and Hubbs]] (1960) diagnosed it from the other three species of the genus by four teeth in the main row, 8 anal rays, usually more than 90 lateral-line scales, and typically 41 or 42 total vertebrae. New Clear Creek material confirmed this diagnosis and showed variability similar to earlier collections.
- **Meristic data:** New counts were reported for certain head pores and gill rakers. Dorsal ray counts were tabulated across a range of about 94 to 102, and vertebral counts averaged about 41.7. The OCR text is partly garbled for these tables, so the exact tallies should be checked against the original.
- **Pigmentation:** Adults show a concentration of large melanophores in a wedge-shaped patch just behind the eye. Two longitudinal rows of similar melanophores extend from above the pectoral fin insertion to the caudal fin base, separated by an unpigmented band along the back. The pigment pattern closely resembles that of [[Gila Robusta]] *intermedia*, and the author noted a similar resemblance to [[Gila Copei]] from the Lake Bonneville system.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Dimorphism is not marked. The most obvious difference is the larger pectoral fin in males. Males measured 56.1 to 69.0 mm standard length, and females 52.1 to 69.1 mm. Breeding males had reddish pectoral and pelvic fins, and females had pale or yellowish fins.
- **Morphology:** The head is broad, and the body is trout-like. A cream-colored spot is visible at the origin of the dorsal fin and near the base of the terminal caudal rays when fish are viewed from above.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical distribution and decline**
- Only four records existed between 1878 and 1939, but the species was probably abundant in the upper Little Colorado River and its cool tributaries: Silver Creek, Showlow Creek, Chevelon Creek, and Clear Creek.
- Permanent populations were unlikely below [[Grand Falls]], about 85 road miles northwest of Flagstaff. The falls (roughly 150 feet of vertical drop) marked the upstream limit of the large predator [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*).
- Between 1939 and 1960 the species disappeared from most of its range. A major collecting effort by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Robert R. Miller]] in late August to early September 1960 failed to find it in the Little Colorado River or its major tributaries.
- Likely causes include pollution, repeated poisoning with [[Toxaphene]] and [[Rotenone]] to remove carp and "trash fish," introductions of exotic species, and changes in stream flow.

**Recent occurrences and the Clear Creek refuge**
- On 25 August 1960, a single adult was seined from a stagnating pool in the dry channel of Clear Creek south of Winslow. This was the first record from that stream and county, and the first capture since 1939.
- On 15–16 July 1961, further surveys by the Miller and Hubbs families and Teruya Uyeno found a breeding population in East Clear Creek. It was studied again on 18 August 1962.
- Clear Creek was one of the few gamefish streams in the drainage that had not been chemically treated. The author concludes that continued survival depends largely on the management practices of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

**Habitat**
- The species occupies clear, flowing, relatively open pools 1 to 2 feet deep, with sand, gravel, rock, boulders, some silt, and bedrock substrates. The occupied reaches of East Clear Creek lie at roughly 6,000 to 6,600 feet elevation and flow through a canyon with walls 100 to 400 feet high. Streamside alders and willows shade the channel.
- Stations with permanent flow were mainly in trout water, where rainbow and brown trout were also caught.
- Water temperatures during July and August ranged from 58°F to 78°F. Marginal habitat at Clear Creek consisted of stagnating pools that were green to brown and had algal blooms.
- Adults congregate in pools without schooling and avoid the water surface. Young fish were observed in July 1961, which suggests spawning that month. Males in breeding condition were also noted.

**Associated fishes**
- Native associates in the Clear Creek drainage include [[Gila Robusta]] (scarce), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*, abundant), and two suckers, *Catostomus latipinnis* and *Pantosteus delphinus* (both common).
- The author suggests that the spinedace avoided predatory trout by staying in the open pools and retreating when disturbed.

## Key Findings

1. *Lepidomeda vittata* is endemic to the Little Colorado River basin of eastern Arizona. Historical records were scarce, with only four specimens recorded from 1878 to 1939.
2. The species was nearly extirpated across its range between 1939 and 1960, and a 1960 survey of the main river and major tributaries found no individuals.
3. A single adult was collected in Clear Creek on 25 August 1960, and a breeding population was found in East Clear Creek in July 1961.
4. The author concludes that the species is now most likely restricted to the Clear Creek drainage, and that its survival may depend on avoiding chemical treatment of the stream.
5. Diagnostic characters from Miller and Hubbs (1960) were confirmed: four teeth in the main row, 8 anal rays, more than 90 lateral-line scales, and typically 41 or 42 vertebrae.
6. Pigment patterns resemble those of *Gila robusta intermedia*. The author notes that these resemblances, together with the genetic relationship of the tribe, may indicate that the Plagopterini evolved from *Gila* or a similar genus, though larval material is needed to test this.
7. Males can be distinguished from females mainly by their larger pectoral fins.
8. The species is trout-like in habitat and behavior, favoring cool, clear, flowing pools in canyon streams.
9. The paper calls for the protection of Clear Creek from chemical treatment and recommends comparative larval studies of *Lepidomeda* and *Gila*.
