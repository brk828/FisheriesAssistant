# Contrasts among Calanoid Copepods from Permanent and Temporary Ponds in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #diaptomidae #calanoida #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

Gerald A. Cole's 1966 study analyzed 37 plankton samples from Arizona ponds (18 temporary, 19 permanent) collected in October–November 1962 to compare [[Calanoid Copepod]] populations, specifically the genus [[Diaptomus]], across contrasting hydrological regimes. Six species were identified — *D. albuquerquensis*, *D. siciloides*, *D. clavipes*, *D. novamexicanus*, *D. sanguineus*, and *D. nudus* — with the first three occurring in both habitat types. Key findings show that individuals from temporary ponds had significantly greater [[Cephalothorax Length]] and larger [[Clutch Size]] than conspecifics from permanent waters, and that congeneric coexistence of similarly sized species was far more common in temporary ponds. Cole proposes that this pattern arises either from genetic adaptation to ephemeral habitats or from superior nutrition tied to high particulate turbidity ([[Argillotrophic Nutrition]]) in temporary ponds, while predation in permanent waters (e.g., by fish) may select for smaller body size.

## Background & Description

This is **not primarily a fish study** — it is an invertebrate zooplankton ecology paper focused on freshwater [[Calanoid Copepod|calanoid copepods]] of the genus *Diaptomus* (family Diaptomidae). Fish are referenced only as an environmental/predation variable distinguishing permanent from temporary water bodies, and as indicator taxa for habitat permanence.

**Species accounts (order of relative abundance):**
- **[[Diaptomus Clavipes]]** — Largest diaptomid recorded; present in 14 ponds, evenly split between habitat types. Broad range from Alberta to Mexico and east to the Mississippi River.
- **[[Diaptomus Sanguineus]]** — Smaller than *D. clavipes*; rarest species (5 individuals), restricted to temporary waters; typically an ephemeral-pool specialist across North America.
- **[[Diaptomus Albuquerquensis]]** — Most common species overall (14 temporary, 8 permanent collections); medium-sized, intermediate between *D. clavipes* and the smaller *Leptodiaptomus* subgenus members; ranges from the Rocky Mountains into Central America.
- **[[Diaptomus Novamexicanus]]** — Slightly larger than *D. nudus* and *D. siciloides*; restricted to temporary ponds in this study (though known from permanent waters elsewhere, e.g., Little Mormon Lake).
- **[[Diaptomus Nudus]]** — Found only in permanent Kaibab Lake (P16) in this dataset; ranges from Alaska to Hudson Bay and the Rocky Mountain states.
- **[[Diaptomus Siciloides]]** — Found in 6 temporary and 14 permanent ponds; broadly distributed across North America; when co-occurring with *D. nudus*, the latter is usually slightly larger.

**Methods:** Samples were collected with No. 20 bolting silk plankton nets, preserved in 10% formalin, and analyzed for species composition, cephalothorax length (male/female), and clutch size (egg counts in ovigerous females). Ponds were classified as permanent or temporary based on water source stability, indicator species (e.g., [[Daphnia Ambigua]], [[Hyalella Azteca]], [[Helisoma Trivolvis]], *Rana pipiens*, and fish presence), and local knowledge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat classification challenges:** Presence of phyllopod crustaceans (anostracans, conchostracans, notostracans) does not reliably indicate temporary status, since many permanent desert ponds with fluctuating water levels lack fish and support phyllopods upon reflooding.
- **Fish as habitat indicators:** The presence of fish was used as one line of evidence suggesting pond permanence, alongside a "rich and varied plankton fauna." Notably, permanent lakes lacking fish (e.g., Mud Lake, Marshall Lake basin) hosted *D. clavipes* populations with cephalothorax lengths falling within the *temporary*-pond size range — supporting a predation-based explanation for size differences.
- **Predation hypothesis:** Citing Brooks & Dodson (1965), Cole suggests that fish and other predators (e.g., characins and atherinids in Lake Pátzcuaro, Mexico) may selectively remove large-bodied zooplankton in permanent waters, producing smaller "races" of *Diaptomus* — an early application of size-selective predation theory to copepod populations.
- **Turbidity and nutrition:** Temporary ponds exhibited much higher particulate phosphate (mean 61% of total phosphate on particles >0.8 µm) compared to permanent lakes (~25–30%), supporting an [[Argillotrophic Nutrition|argillotrophic]] (particle-based) trophic pathway that may fuel larger body sizes and clutch sizes in ephemeral habitats.
- **Physicochemical conditions:** Water temperatures ranged 1.1–24.4 °C; pH 7.6–9.0; Secchi transparency from 2.5 cm (turbid pools) to 1.2 m (Holiday Lake, P19). Elevations spanned 610–2133 m, including Mohave Desert-affinity sites near Congress Junction.
- **Co-occurring fauna:** Indicator taxa for permanent ponds included cladocerans (*Diaphanosoma*, *Bosmina*, *Daphnia ambigua*), the amphipod *Hyalella azteca*, the snail *Helisoma trivolvis*, and leopard frogs (*Rana pipiens*).

## Key Findings

1. **Body size:** Mean [[Cephalothorax Length]] was consistently greater in temporary-pond populations of *D. clavipes*, *D. albuquerquensis*, and *D. siciloides* than in permanent-pond populations; length-frequency plots were bimodal, reflecting the two habitat types.
2. **Clutch size:** Egg counts were significantly higher in temporary-pond females, especially in *D. clavipes* and *D. albuquerquensis*; less pronounced in *D. siciloides*.
3. **Size–fecundity correlation:** Strong positive correlation between female cephalothorax length and clutch size in *D. clavipes* (r=0.92), *D. albuquerquensis* (r=0.83), and *D. siciloides* (r=0.79); weaker in *D. novamexicanus* (r=0.26).
4. **Congeneric coexistence:** Co-occurrence of similarly or adjacent-sized *Diaptomus* species was common in temporary ponds (11 of 18) but rare in permanent ponds (4 of 19) (χ²=4.59, 0.05>P>0.02), consistent with reduced competitive exclusion time and/or superabundant food in ephemeral habitats.
5. **Species associations:** Using Cole's (1949) interspecific association coefficient, *D. clavipes* and *D. albuquerquensis* showed significant negative association in permanent ponds but random association in temporary ponds; *D. siciloides* and *D. novamexicanus* were significantly negatively associated overall.
6. **Temperature effects:** No significant correlation was found between water temperature and cephalothorax length across species/sexes, except a marginal negative correlation in male *D. albuquerquensis* (r=-0.444, p≈0.05) — suggesting temperature alone does not explain the size differences.
7. **Supporting long-term data:** Multi-year sampling (1963–1964) from stock tanks and volcanic lakes near the Mogollon Rim corroborated the general pattern: fishless permanent lakes (Mud Lake, Marshall Lake) yielded *D. clavipes* of "temporary-pond" size, while Kaibab Lake (P16) was an anomaly with large individuals despite permanence.
8. **Seasonal replacement:** Evidence suggests *D. siciloides* replaces *D. nudus* seasonally in some Colorado Plateau lakes (e.g., Soldier Lake, Annex Lake), potentially reflecting temporal niche partitioning akin to that seen in Lake Erie *Leptodiaptomus* (Davis, 1961).
9. **Proposed mechanisms:** Two non-exclusive explanations for larger body size/clutch size in temporary ponds: (a) enhanced nutrition from high particulate/detrital turbidity ([[Argillotrophic Nutrition]]), and (b) genetic selection for large body size and fecundity as an adaptation to ephemeral, high-risk habitats — potentially counterbalanced by [[Size-Selective Predation]] (e.g., by fish) in stable permanent waters favoring smaller morphotypes.
