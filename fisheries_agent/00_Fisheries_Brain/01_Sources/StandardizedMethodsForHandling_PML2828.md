# Standardized Methods for Handling Fish in Grand Canyon Research

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This field manual, prepared by [[David Ward]] of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] for the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Center]] (first issued November 2002, revised January 2006), sets out standardized procedures for handling, measuring, marking and recording fish during monitoring work in [[Grand Canyon]]. It covers the [[Colorado River]] mainstem and the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR). The core aims are to minimize fish stress and mortality, keep public trust and research permits intact, and produce consistent, verifiable data. The document includes processing flowcharts (diagrams 1 to 4), measurement and [[Pit Tagging]] protocols, [[Fin Clipping]] and [[Elastomer Tags]] codes, anesthesia and data-recording rules, and standardized field codes for rivers, species, habitat, sex and disposition.

## Background & Description

The manual distinguishes native from nonnative fish. The main native species discussed are [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. Nonnative species include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]] and other sport and forage fishes. The text stresses that handling should be adapted to each project, but a common set of general guidelines applies to all sampling.

Standard measurements are:

- **Total Length (TL)**: from the tip of the snout to the tip of the longest caudal fin ray, with lobes compressed.
- **Fork Length (FL)**: from the most anterior part of the head to the fork of the caudal fin, following the method of [[Length Measurement]] (adapted from Neilson and Johnson 1983).
- **Weight**: recorded to the nearest gram, and only under calm, dry conditions with working equipment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document links handling stress to ecological and population outcomes. Rough handling, extended time out of water, high water temperatures and crowded holding conditions can all cause delayed mortality, which may appear hours or days after capture (Stickney 1983). Stress can also produce abnormal physiological and behavioral responses that bias study results.

Specific guidance includes:

- Keep fish out of water for as little time as possible.
- Change water often when fish are held more than a few minutes, or when fish surface for air. Stress rises as water temperature rises.
- Hold no more than 8 to 10 fish in a workup bucket at a time. Keep the rest in a net in the river.
- Shake hoop nets before removal and check carefully for small fish trapped between mesh.
- Remove watch straps, jewelry and similar items, rinse sunscreen and lotion from hands, and keep dry hands and equipment from stripping protective skin coatings.
- Wet length boards and scales before use, because they heat quickly in sunlight.

Sampling should disturb the area as little as possible. The manual also refers to the [[Anesthesia Guidelines]], which apply to fish that require sedation for processing.

## Key Findings

**1. Standard processing sequence.** Each fish is scanned for a [[Pit Tagging|PIT tag]] before any other handling. Native fish are then measured (TL and FL), examined for external parasites, sexual characteristics and elastomer dye tags, and marked as required. Nonnative fish have TL measured and are checked for adipose or fin clips. Diagrams 1 to 4 give separate flowcharts for the Mainstem Colorado River, Little Colorado River and nonnative fish, including the handling of piscivores such as catfish.

**2. PIT tag frequencies and rules.** Two PIT tag frequencies are in use on the Colorado River:
- Old 400 kHz tags (10-digit).
- New 134.2 kHz tags (12-digit, beginning with 3D9).

All fish without a 134.2 kHz tag receive a new tag, even if they already carry an old one. Scanners must be checked for both tag types, and both numbers recorded on the data sheet. Tag location varies by species. For humpback chub, the tag goes into the abdomen to the right of the mid-ventral line, posterior to the left pelvic girdle. Needles should be sharp and clean, changed about every 20 fish, and sterilized in ethanol or isopropyl alcohol. The needle should penetrate only deep enough to place the tag in the body cavity. Preliminary laboratory data suggest that perforating the intestine is the main cause of PIT tag mortality.

**3. Size thresholds for tagging.** The manual sets tagging thresholds by species:
- Humpback chub over 150 mm TL should be scanned and tagged. Chub between 100 and 150 mm were added to tagging rules in later revisions.
- Nonnative trout over 150 mm TL without an existing PIT tag should be tagged (see Diagram 2).
- Carp over 150 mm TL should be tagged.
- Native fish under 150 mm TL in the mainstem receive a new tag only if they lack a 134.2 kHz tag.

**4. Fin clips and elastomer marks.** Adipose clips mark recaptures and hatchery or stocked fish, with the entire adipose fin removed at its base. Left and right pectoral, left and right pelvic, upper and lower caudal and anal fins are coded for secondary marking. Elastomer dye (pink, green or yellow) is injected into the head or dorsal musculature of suckers and chub as a secondary mark, and the color must be recorded.

**5. Key revision history.**
- Fall 2002: baiting of hoop nets was discontinued after laboratory studies showed that PIT tagging fed fish led to high mortality. Native fish over 150 mm TL without a PIT tag were tagged and dorsally fin-punched to evaluate tag loss.
- Spring 2003: new 134.2 kHz tags began to be applied to previously untagged native fish. Carp over 150 mm were tagged. Elastomer dye was tested on suckers.
- September 2003: tagging expanded to all chub over 100 mm and all other natives over 150 mm. Fish with old tags were double-tagged.
- Later revisions: rules for scanning and recording tag numbers were tightened, and a procedure was added for preserving catfish stomachs and their contents for later identification.

**6. Data recording standards.** Data should be written in the field, not reconstructed later. Blank fields mean the item was not checked, and "undetermined" means it was checked but could not be distinguished. Each record should include GPS coordinates or the waypoint number, and labels must be in pencil for preserved specimens. Preserved specimens (whole fish or stomach samples) must carry a label that links them to the data sheet.

**7. Field code standards.** The manual lists 31 river and tributary codes (for example LCR for Little Colorado River, COR for Colorado River, HAV for Havasu Creek, PAR for Paria River) and 35 species codes (for example HBC for Humpback Chub, RBS for Razorback Sucker, BNT for Brown Trout, CRP for Carp, UID for Undetermined Fish). It also defines habitat codes (shoreline, hydraulic units, cover types and dominant substrates on a 0 to 11 scale), sexual condition and characteristics, disposition codes (returned alive, dead released, dead preserved, dead stomach contents, skeletonized) and parasite types.

**8. Turbidity and reference materials.** Turbidity is recorded as high (Secchi depth below 0.5 m) or low (above 0.5 m). The reference list cites Anderson and Gutreuter on length-weight indices, Buckmeier et al. (2002) on ageing channel catfish, Valdez and Filbert (1993) on native fishes of Grand Canyon, and the CBFWA (1999) PIT tag marking manual.
