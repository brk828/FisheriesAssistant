# The Effect of *Chironomus riparius* Larvae on the Redox Potentials of Settled Activated Sludge

#limnology #benthic-ecology #Chironomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 study by R. W. Edwards examines how larvae of the midge *[[Chironomus riparius]]* (Diptera: Chironomidae) alter the physico-chemical structure of settled activated sludge from a sewage treatment works. Although the taxon studied is an aquatic insect rather than a fish, the paper is foundational to benthic pollution ecology, directly informing models of [[Benthal Oxygen Demand]] in polluted streams. Larvae were introduced at two densities (12,500/m² and 50,000/m²) into consolidated activated sludge tanks and monitored for changes in pH, redox potential (E₇), and overlying-water chemistry (ammonia and oxidized nitrogen) over several months. The larvae's tube-dwelling, irrigating behavior increased the depth of the oxidized surface layer, raised pH throughout the sludge column, and altered nitrogen cycling in the overlying water — demonstrating that benthic invertebrate activity can be a major, previously underappreciated, driver of redox and nitrogen dynamics in organic sediments.

## Background & Description

- **Organism:** *Chironomus riparius* Meigen — a tube-dwelling, benthic dipteran larva (chironomid "bloodworm").
- **Life stages used:** Newly hatched 1st instar larvae were introduced into experimental tanks; ammonia excretion measurements were conducted on 4th instar larvae.
- **Functional morphology:** Larvae construct burrows/tubes within the sediment and generate irrigation currents by body undulation to draw water through their tubes for respiration — the key mechanical mechanism driving the sediment redox changes reported.
- **Tube form plasticity:** Referencing Berg (1938), the tube architecture of related species (*C. bathophilus*, syn. *anthracinus*) is noted to vary with the oxygen concentration of the overlying water, implying larval morphology/behavior is environmentally plastic.
- **Development/emergence:** Larvae pupated and began emerging as adults ~25 days after introduction, coinciding with a reversal (upward drift) of redox isopleths as biological irrigation ceased.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Settled and consolidated [[Activated Sludge]] deposits (organic carbon 31%, organic nitrogen 5%) derived from sewage treatment, analogous to natural [[Benthal Deposits]] in polluted rivers and eutrophic lakes.
- **Population densities:** Experimental densities (12,500–50,000 larvae/m²) were chosen to bracket real-world values; densities >100,000/m² have been recorded in organically polluted streams (Edwards, 1957), while the lower density used is comparable to chironomid densities in eutrophic lakes/reservoirs (Mundie, 1956).
- **Niche role — bioirrigator/bioturbator:** *C. riparius* functions as an ecosystem engineer, actively pumping oxygenated water into anoxic sediment, thereby restructuring the vertical redox stratification of the deposit and coupling benthic and pelagic (overlying water) nutrient cycles.
- **Trophic/biogeochemical context:** The larvae mediate exchange between an upper oxidized "aerobic" sludge layer (historically ~3 cm deep, characterized by high E₇) and a lower black, ferrous-sulphide-rich anaerobic layer. Their activity increases the relative importance of aerobic decomposition versus purely diffusion-limited anaerobic breakdown, linking directly to concepts of [[Redox Potential]] stratification and [[Sulphide Deposition]] as also described by Baas Becking & Ferguson Wood (1955).
- **Comparative context:** Related literature cited situates this within a broader guild of benthic tube-dwellers/invertebrates affecting sediment chemistry, including oligochaetes (Ravera, 1955) and other chironomids (*Chironomus plumosus*, Rossolimo 1939; Ganapati 1949).

## Key Findings

- **pH effects:** Introduction of larvae raised pH first at the sludge surface, then progressively at all depths (substantial rise by 21–28 days). Undisturbed control tanks (Tanks 3 & 4) showed no consistent pH trend. Effects were more pronounced at the higher density (50,000/m², Tank 2) than the lower density (12,500/m², Tank 1).
- **Redox potential (E₇) effects:** Undisturbed sludge showed a sharp two-layer structure — an oxidized upper layer (~3 cm, E₇ = +170 to +300 mV at 1.5 cm) over a reduced, black FeS-rich lower layer (E₇ = −120 to −203 mV at 6 cm). Larval activity deepened the oxidized zone, blurred the sharp redox interface, and reduced visible blackening (sulphide) — with larval emergence (~day 25) associated with an upward retreat of the isopleths back toward baseline structure.
- **Sludge consolidation:** Larval activity caused physical consolidation of the sludge, reducing total deposit depth by ~1 cm (from an initial 10 cm).
- **Overlying water chemistry:** Larvae presence coincided with increased pH, increased ammonia concentration, and decreased oxidized nitrogen (NO₂⁻+NO₃⁻) in the water above the sludge. In Tank 2, ammonia rose from 12 to 28 p.p.m. over 7 days (~2.3 p.p.m./day) — roughly five times greater than what direct larval excretion alone could account for (measured excretion rate: 0.37 mg N/g wet larval weight/day at 17°C).
- **Mechanistic interpretation:** The ammonia increase is attributed mainly to enhanced mud–water exchange (irrigation) rather than direct excretion; nitrate/nitrite loss is linked to increased mixing with mud interstitial water where, below E₇ = 350 mV at pH 7 (per Mortimer, 1941), nitrate is reduced to ammonia. Net ammonia gains are diluted by volatilization and by denitrification, evidenced by high N₂ content in sludge gas.
- **Implications for Benthal Oxygen Demand:** By deepening the oxidized layer, chironomid activity increases the fraction of "aerobic" decomposition relative to "anaerobic" decomposition, which — counterintuitively — can *increase* total (ultimate) oxygen demand of a deposit, since gaseous anaerobic byproducts like methane escape without exerting oxygen demand once vented (cf. Grindley 1955; ~40% of Thames Estuary benthal oxygen demand estimated lost as methane).
- **Theoretical significance:** These findings challenge purely diffusion-based models (Mortimer, 1942) of oxidized-layer thickness in lake muds, showing that invertebrate bioirrigation can be a dominant, density-dependent control on redox stratification — a key consideration for accurately modeling [[Stream Pollution]] and river self-purification dynamics.
- **Comparative baseline:** Prior shorter-settled sludge (8 weeks) showed even larger pH increases with comparable larval densities (6.85 → 7.73 at 6 cm), suggesting sludge maturity/consolidation modulates the magnitude of larval effects.
