# Electric Fishing at Sea: German Proposals and Whale Electrocution (1949)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-technology

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service leaflet (Fishery Leaflet 348, reissued) compiles a 1949 American Consulate report on a proposed [[Electric Trawling]] system for German sea fishing, invented by [[Konrad Kreutzer]] and [[Herbert Peglow]]. The leaflet also reprints a German supplement from *Fischereiwelt* (1949) that includes an article by Peglow, a review of inland [[Electrofishing]] by [[Denzer]], and two papers on [[Whale Electrocution]]. The inventors claimed an electric net could match a conventional net roughly ten times its area, and argued that the method was more conservative because it could select fish by size. The fisheries experts who reviewed the device in July 1949 judged it favorable, and the inventors received DM 80,000 to complete tests. No peer-reviewed data were presented; the inventors had not published quantitative results.

## Background & Description

- **Source and context:** Reported by Robert B. Houston, Jr., American Vice Consul, in American Consulate Report No. 11 (Bremerhaven, June 24, 1949), after a visit on June 18, 1949, aboard the former German Navy minesweeper R 96 at the Hamburger Werft near [[Hamburg]].
- **The inventor:** Dr. Konrad Kreutzer, a physicist with several basic patents on the [[Selenium Rectifier]]. He was led to the idea through wartime work on electro-shock apparatus for Siemens-Reiniger. (The supplement names him "Conradin" Kreutzer and calls him a physiologist; the two names appear to refer to the same person.)
- **Collaborator:** Herbert Peglow, an engineer. Together they formed the Arbeitsgemeinschaft Peglow-Kreutzer, Hamburg.
- **Underlying principles (as cited by the inventor):** Earlier experimenters reported that commercial fish show [[Electrotaxis]] at roughly 0.5 to 1.5 volts head-to-tail, [[Electronarcosis]] at about 1.5 to 5.0 volts, and death at higher voltages sustained long enough for electrolysis. Fish orient with their heads toward increasing potential, so a positive anode draws them in.
- **Patent status:** The device was reported as patented in the United States, but the consulate could not locate a matching patent under the inventors' names. A pending application was considered possible.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Freshwater vs. seawater:** Freshwater is a poor conductor, so inland electric fishing is effective at modest power. Seawater's high conductivity requires much larger power, which the inventors addressed through pulse shape and generator design.
- **Size selectivity:** Peglow argued that all fish of one type have roughly the same body-potential sensitivity, so larger fish, which pick up a higher voltage across their bodies, respond first. Adjusting voltage could therefore select fish by size and protect small fish.
- **Recovery:** Paralysis or narcosis was said to last only seconds, with rapid recovery, so fish were not expected to be harmed.
- **Bottom contact:** Current was claimed to penetrate the seabed, so fish sheltering under rocks could be driven into the net, and the net could float above the bottom. This would let trawling avoid stony or uneven grounds.
- **Discard problem:** The inventors estimated that 90% of fish caught in German trawler nets at that time were too small to land, and many discards were dead. Electric fishing was presented as a way to reduce this waste.
- **Inland constraints (Denzer):** Electric fishing was then possible only in waters no larger than a small river with a maximum water level of two meters. Efficacy depended on water chemistry, suspended matter, temperature and substrate. Gear that worked on primary rock often failed on alluvial soil.

## Key Findings

### Proposed Sea System (Kreutzer and Peglow)

- **Waveform and pulse:** Impulsive, approximately triangular anode voltage, with a sharp rise and slow decay. Pulse length about 2 ms; pulse rate 2 to 20 per second, depending on the target fish's natural tail-beat frequency.
- **Current:** Low voltage (seawater resistance is low) but peak pulse current of about 10,000 amperes.
- **Electrodes:** Anode in or along the net, cathode a rounded or large iron sphere near the boat, to reduce electrolysis. The inventor was vague on arrangement; Peglow suggested a central conductor down a conventional net.
- **Performance claim:** Based on freshwater trials on Lake Constance and small saltwater tests, a 100 m² electric net could match a conventional 1,000 m² net. The calculated generator was a 6,000 rpm, 20 kVA alternator with two switching tubes in full-wave arrangement.
- **Trawl version (Peglow):** A collecting anode rope placed ahead of the trawl opening, with the trawl boards as cathode. The fishing field could be "ten times larger" than the trawl mouth, and electrotactic effects were said to stop fish fleeing the approaching net.
- **Economics (Peglow):** Equipment and installation estimated at 3% to 5% of a medium-sized ship's value. Fuel use was expected to be offset by shorter voyages. Extra crew were said to be unnecessary, and about 6 to 8 m² of engine-room floor space would be needed.
- **Safety:** Installations were claimed to be shielded on board, with emergency deck switches, and the field was said to be too weak to affect people near the ship.

### Funding and Regulatory Status

- **Initial request:** DM 30,000 (about $9,000) sought from trawler owners or public funds to equip the experimental boat.
- **Foreign investment:** A planned American food-packer investment was blocked by the Military Government's ban on foreign investment in Germany.
- **Award:** In October 1949 (Report No. 26) the inventors received DM 80,000 (about $19,048) from the Ausgleichsstock.
- **Power and permits:** A 200 kVA alternator was needed for the experimental model. Permission from the British Military Government was required; the Fisheries Control Officer had shown interest.
- **Schedule:** Sea tests were planned to begin in February 1950.

### Expert Review

- **Meeting:** The inventors presented to university professors, marine scientists and fishing-industry representatives in Hamburg on July 8, 1949.
- **Reviewers named:** Dr. Mond, Dr. Luckner (Physiological Institute, Hamburg), Dr. Kollath (State Physics Institute, Hamburg), and Dr. Denzer (Director, Fisheries Institute of Nordrhein-Westfalen).
- **Verdict:** Reported as favorable; all assistance was recommended to complete the tests (Report No. 28, July 18, 1949).

### Inland Electrofishing Experience (Denzer)

- **History:** Schönfelder (1925), Wilkening (1926) and Schiemenz (1927) carried out early large-scale trials. Scheminsky explained the physiology in 1924 and gave a general description in 1941 ("Tabulae Biologicae"). Smolian (1944) reported on 15 years of work.
- **Gear:** Gasoline dynamos of 300 to 5,000 W, or battery units using 12 V automobile batteries with converters. Typical current was 200 to 360 V and about 0.8 to 15 A; some foreign systems (Switzerland, Sweden, United States) used up to 2,000 V.
- **Uses:** Catching spawning fish, removing oversized fish, disease testing, pollution assessment, water-quality checks and research.
- **Recommendations:** Greater cooperation among practitioners, scientists and engineers, and combined use of electric and conventional gear.

### Whale Electrocution (Weber, Schubert, Reichert)

- **History:** First experiment 1881 (Alfred Nielsen, Norway). Birkeland resumed work in 1904. Albert Weber began in 1929 for A/S Elektrisk Hvalskytning and found a workable solution in 1935.
- **Method:** 200 V, 50-cycle AC through a one-pole lead to a harpoon electrode. Current returned via the mucous membranes of the mouth and barbels.
- **Current by hit location (Weber):**
  - Back muscle: 35 to 60 A; fatal in about 10 seconds to 2 minutes.
  - Visceral hits below the spine: 78 to 92 A.
  - Lungs and heart: instant death.
  - Harpoon hook shunting: above 100 A.
- **Comparative results (Reichert):**

| Measure | Electrocution | Gun harpoon |
|---|---|---|
| Time from hit to towing | ~10 min | ~45 min |
| Forerunners per season | 8 | 25 |
| Whaling lines used | none to 1 | at least 6 |
| Shells used | none | 400 |
| Whales lost | none | 2 to 6 |

- **Drawbacks:** Misses still occur. Electrocuted whale meal is darker and sells for less, since the blood is not drained. Experiments on large whales (about 100 tons, swimming about 10 knots) were considered technically hard.
- **Skin thickness (Schubert):** Epidermis horn layer about 2 to 3 mm (blue whale), 4 mm (finback), 6 to 9 mm (humpback), 1.5 mm average (sei). Blubber 10 to 15 cm in rorquals and humpbacks. Skin nerves were reported as incompletely developed, a point the author wanted rechecked.
- **Suggested improvement (Reichert):** Interrupted direct current might be more effective than continuous AC for whales.

### Caveats

- The inventors withheld details and had not published quantitative results.
- The electrode arrangement and the 100 m² versus 1,000 m² equivalence rest on the inventors' own calculations.
- Voltage and current figures for ocean use are design targets, not measured outcomes.
- The leaflet itself notes that the method's effects on whales and at-sea efficacy had not been established.
