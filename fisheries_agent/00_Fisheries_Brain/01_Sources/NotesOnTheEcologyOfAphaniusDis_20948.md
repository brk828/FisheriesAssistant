# Ecology of Aphanius dispar in the Sultanate of Oman

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This field and laboratory study documents the natural history of [[Aphanius Dispar]] in northern [[Oman]], a species previously unstudied in the country. The fish was found in most permanent freshwater bodies with a connection to the sea, occupying habitats that differ in flow, chemistry, temperature and substrate. It is highly euryhaline, tolerating full-strength seawater, and it breeds year-round with a probable peak in April to June. The author concludes that its tolerance of varied conditions, voracious appetite for mosquito larvae, and resistance to pollution make it a promising candidate for [[Biological Mosquito Control]] in regions where [[Malaria]] is endemic, preferable in some settings to [[Gambusia Affinis]].

## Background & Description

- **Regional fauna:** The Arabian peninsula has a depauperate freshwater fish fauna. Nine species occur south of the northern border of Saudi Arabia: eight cyprinids and one cyprinodontid (Banister & Clarke, 1977).
- **Oman's fishes:** [[Cyprinion]] and [[Garra]] (Garra barreimiae) are the two cyprinids found in most of Oman's few permanent watercourses. A third, *Garra longipinnis*, is known only from the village of Shurayah near Saiq.
- **Distribution:** *A. dispar* is widespread along coastal regions bordering the Red Sea, Persian Gulf, Northern Arabian Sea and eastern Mediterranean. It may have occupied the Mediterranean before the construction of the [[Suez Canal]] (Kornfield & Nevo, 1976).
- **Prior research:** Earlier work focused mainly on physiology, particularly [[Euryhalinity]] (Lotan, 1969–1973; Skadhauge & Lotan, 1974). Ecological studies were scarce, and none had addressed Omani populations.
- **Methods:** Field observations were made in November–December 1978 at 12 locations, including Wadi Sumail, Wadi Fanja, Boshar, Nakhl and Izki. Observation time was 24 h at Boshar and 40 h at Wadi Fanja, with 1–2 h at other sites. Specimens were also examined from preserved material collected in April and June 1976 by Mr Said Matta. A 30 × 30 × 45 cm aquarium held three females and one male for four weeks for salinity and temperature trials. Water temperature was measured with mercury thermometers, pH with indicator paper, and hardness by titration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat and distribution
- **Batinah coast:** No natural water bodies exist. Open water depends on pumped groundwater for irrigation, through numerous wells and cisterns. *A. dispar* was introduced into some of these sites for mosquito control and reproduced successfully, even in wells.
- **Inland habitats:** Fish occur in birkets (man-made cisterns), wadis and [[Falaj]] systems. Falajs are ancient, narrow irrigation channels fed from the Jabal al Akhdar that carry rapid flow.
- **Association:** Wherever fish occurred, *A. dispar* was present, often alone. Exceptions were a swampy, cattail-choked area in [[Wadi Fanja]] (dominated by Cyprinion at about 2 m depth) and a warm spring-fed falaj at Shurayah (Garra longipinnis alone). A hot spring falaj at [[Nakhl]] held no fish.
- **Pollution tolerance:** Fish persisted in a cistern at [[Boshar]] heavily polluted with soap.
- **Predation:** Herons were seen feeding in wadis and were probably responsible for the scarcity of large adults in small or shallow pools.

### Microhabitat and feeding-guild partitioning
- The species uses sandy, rocky and soft-detritus substrates. In flowing water it stays near the bottom. In cisterns it uses all depths, and in falajs it sticks to algae-covered margins.
- Where Aphanius, Garra and Cyprinion coexist, they partition the water column: *Aphanius* swims near the bottom, *Garra* feeds on the bottom with a subterminal sucker mouth, and *Cyprinion* occupies mid-depth.

### Water chemistry and temperature
- Water temperatures at occupied sites ranged from 28.4 °C (Sumail) to 38.4 °C (Nakhl), and pH from 7.4 to 8.0 (one record at pH 8.5).
- Total hardness ranged from 19 to 36 degrees, with carbonate hardness from 9 to 29 degrees.

### Comparison with Cyprinodon and desert fish evolution
- *A. dispar* closely resembles North American *Cyprinodon* in general biology and is nearly indistinguishable in the field. Both are euryhaline and eurythermal, which explains why cyprinodontids dominate warm desert fish faunas.
- Xeric conditions in Arabia arose around the end of the Pleistocene (about 1,000–30,000 years BP). Unlike the speciation seen in *Cyprinodon* in the American Southwest, Middle Eastern *Aphanius* has not diversified greatly, probably because most populations retain access to the sea, which permits gene flow. The genus includes three species groups with seven species, all considered Tethys Sea relicts (Steinitz, 1951).

## Key Findings

### 1. Salinity tolerance
- Four juveniles (c. 3 cm TL) tolerated stepwise transfers through 33%, 50%, 60% and 100% seawater at 24-h intervals with no stress and continued feeding.
- Garra and Cyprinion showed stress at 33% and all died at 50%.
- Of five fish moved directly to 60% seawater, all survived. Of five moved directly to 100% seawater, all died within 12 h.

### 2. Thermal tolerance
- Upper lethal limits were 39.8–42.4 °C for fish acclimated to 20.5 °C, and 45.1–46.0 °C for fish acclimated to 38.4 °C.
- Lower limits were about 5.0–5.8 °C for Fanja fish and 5.8–9.6 °C for Nakhl fish. Some apparently dead fish recovered when warmed to 14–18 °C, but none recovered from 4.8 or 5.2 °C.
- Upper lethal limits were 39.5–40.2 °C for Garra and 36.5–37.0 °C for Cyprinion, so the two cyprinids tolerated less heat than *Aphanius*.

### 3. Diet and gut morphology
- Six males, eight females and one juvenile were dissected. Relative gut length (% of standard length) averaged 272.1 (range 214.5–305.7) in males, 229.3 (158.5–302.2) in females, and 111.1 in the juvenile. These values indicate a largely herbivorous diet.
- Gut contents were about 90–95% unicellular algae (desmids, diatoms) and 5–10% filamentous algae. One fish contained about 80% dipteran larvae.
- Given the opportunity, fish readily eat animal prey. Three fish (two females, one male) consumed 1,142 mosquito larvae over four days, averaging 96 larvae per fish per day despite abundant algae.

### 4. Reproduction and seasonality
- Reproduction probably occurs year-round, with a probable peak in April–June and lowest frequency in winter.
- November–December: 5–10% of males were in reproductive coloration at any time. Of 26 females examined from Fanja, 20% (5 fish, 3.68–5.12 cm SL) had a few ripe eggs (3–5), and ovaries were 17–20% of SL.
- April–June: Of 17 females examined (16 collected June 1974, one April 1976), 69% had ripe eggs (mean 20, range 3–41), with ovaries at 20–25% of SL.
- The smallest female carrying eggs measured 2.6 cm SL.
- Tentatively, winter females produce about five eggs per day and spring females about twenty.
- Young of 34 mm were abundant in dense grass thickets with some flow.

### 5. Breeding behaviour
- Spawning occurs where there is some flow and vertical surfaces of plants or algae-covered rocks. It starts about an hour after sunrise and peaks at midday.
- Territorial males (territories about 30 cm across) patrol in circles and figure-of-eight patterns. Male–male encounters involve brief, intense aggression: parallel alignment, fin spreading, circling and flank bites.
- Females never defend territories. They form loose, changing all-female aggregations and approach males to spawn.
- The male places his chin on the female's nape, and the pair spawns single eggs in 1–2 seconds per egg. Terminal reproductive behaviour matches that of [[Cyprinodon]] and other cyprinodontids.

### 6. Biological control potential
- The fish's eagerness for mosquito larvae, broad tolerance of water conditions and resistance to pollution make it a strong candidate for control of mosquito-borne disease, notably [[Malaria]].
- The author suggests that it may be preferable to *Gambusia affinis*, which is less effective as a larvivore and has reduced native fish faunas through competition (Danielson, 1968).

### 7. Ecological parallels
- Coexistence of cyprinids with cyprinodontids in Oman (Cyprinion, Garra and Aphanius) resembles the Death Valley System of California and Nevada (Gila, Rhinichthys, Catostomus and Cyprinodon). In both regions, three feeding guilds are present: mid-level feeders, substrate feeders, and opportunistic omnivores.
