## Executive Summary

This 1944 report by Douglas D. Moss (Connecticut State Board of Fisheries and Game) presents preliminary studies aimed at predicting annual [[American Shad]] (*Alosa sapidissima*) run sizes in the Connecticut River for fisheries management purposes. The study draws on commercial catch records dating to 1890, angler creel census data from 1941–1944, and scale-based age/spawning-history analysis of 4,724 shad specimens collected during the 1944 angling season. A central contribution is the identification and interpretation of "spawning marks" on shad scales — scars hypothesized to form during each spawning migration due to cessation of growth, tissue resorption, and scale erosion — as a tool for estimating repeat-spawning frequency and yearly mortality rates. The author develops mathematical formulas relating relative abundance and mortality but concludes the method has limited standalone value without an independent index of run abundance, since scale-derived mortality rates always lag one year behind the sampled run.

## Background & Description

- **Scale annuli and spawning marks**: Discrete, conspicuous marks distinguishable from ordinary winter annuli, believed to form only during the freshwater spawning migration.
  - Mechanism proposed: cessation of feeding and somatic growth during spawning migration, with reproductive tissue development drawing on stored body materials (notably fat reserves) rather than food intake, leading to localized scale margin resorption/erosion.
  - Distinguished from "false annuli," which are known to complicate shad [[Scale Reading]] (per multiple cited authors).
  - Alternate mechanism proposed in discussion (based on a Russian study of related herring): post-spawning fat loss causes skin to slacken over the reduced body frame, physically crumbling scale margins at the socket edges — producing the spawning mark through a mechanical rather than purely physiological/resorptive process.
- **Length-frequency data (1944 season, n=4,724)**:
  - Overall distribution approximated a bell curve; peak of the curve at **18 inches** total length, containing 999 specimens.
  - Average length of females exceeded average length of males.
  - Average length of the catch shifted progressively as the season advanced, alongside a progressive shift in sex ratio.
  - Distinct year/age classes were **not** resolvable from length-frequency data alone.
- **Scale sample composition (848 impressions read at College Park, MD)**:
  - 555 scales: no spawning mark (virgins)
  - 210 scales: one spawning mark
  - 70 scales: two spawning marks
  - 11 scales: three spawning marks
  - 2 scales: four spawning marks

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Life history / migration**: [[American Shad]] are anadromous [[Clupeidae|clupeids]], migrating from marine/estuarine feeding habitats into freshwater rivers (the Connecticut River) to spawn. The study assumes river-specific homing — that Connecticut River shad return to their natal river to spawn.
- **Feeding behavior during spawning migration**: Adults are believed to eat very little or not at all while in fresh water, relying on stored body reserves (particularly fat) to fuel migration energy costs and gonadal maturation — a pattern corroborated by [[Leim 1924]], who documented notable fat depletion in spawning-run fish compared to individuals sampled in salt water during late summer.
- **Repeat spawning**: The study assumes (following [[Greeley 1936]]) that surviving adult shad spawn every year, making the spawning run a mixture of virgins and repeat spawners of various ages, distinguishable via scale mark counts.
- **Comparative scale physiology**: Cited parallels to other species support the plausibility of migration/spawning-induced scale resorption, including [[Leuresthes Tenuis]] (California grunion), which ceases growth during its protracted May–July spawning season, forming a "breeding annulus," and haddock (per H. Thompson 1926), in which poor-condition spawning fish showed slight scale absorption.
- **Fishing pressure interaction**: The angler fishery (Enfield Dam) sits upriver of the commercial fishery, meaning fish sampled there have already passed through commercial harvest pressure downstream, a relevant structural feature of the study system's [[Fishing Intensity]] dynamics.

## Key Findings

1. **Historical catch variability**: Commercial catch records (1890–1944) reveal the Connecticut River shad fishery is characterized by alternating periods of scarcity and abundance.
2. **Angler catch statistics (Enfield Dam, 1941–1944)**: Total anglers, hours fished, and shad caught all varied substantially year to year (e.g., 6,859 shad caught in 1941 vs. 9,870 in 1944); average hours fished per shad taken ranged from 1 hr 14 min (1943) to 2 hr 52 min (1942).
3. **No correlation with environmental variables**: Catch-per-hour showed little to no correlation with river volume, water temperature, or available fishing space across the four years studied.
4. **Length-frequency limitations**: Age/year classes could not be distinguished by average length alone, though sex-based and seasonal size differences were evident.
5. **Mortality rate estimation via spawning marks**: Plotting the observed spawning-class distribution (1,000 virgins : 378 : 126 : 20 : 4) against theoretical constant-mortality curves suggested an apparent mortality rate between **62% and 73%** for Connecticut River shad, based on comparisons between successive spawning-class totals.
6. **Confounding factor identified**: The apparent mortality rate calculated by comparing consecutive spawning classes is confounded by year-to-year variation in virgin recruitment — a decline in a spawning class could reflect true mortality, a genuinely smaller virgin cohort in a prior year, or both simultaneously (demonstrated via hypothetical Table 3 scenarios spanning 1938–1944).
7. **Formulas developed**: The author derives an "Index of Abundance of Virgins" (IV) formula and a corresponding "Mortality Rate" (MR) formula intended to correct for inter-annual differences in sample size and virgin recruitment, in principle allowing true mortality rate estimation if a reliable independent abundance index exists.
8. **Practical limitation**: Scale-derived mortality rate estimates are inherently a "year behind" — a given year's sample reveals the mortality rate of the *previous* year's cohort, not the current year, limiting direct predictive utility for real-time management unless paired with reliable abundance indices and fishing effort (net count) data.
9. **Proposed management pathway**: If mortality rate can be shown to correlate with fishing effort (number of nets fishing), and if virgin recruitment can be estimated from a 10-year running average, it may become possible to forecast the size of a future year's shad run to a degree useful for management, though this remains speculative pending further data.
10. **Acknowledgment of alternative international research**: Discussion notes a contemporary Russian study on related herring species describing a similar "spawning mark" phenomenon attributed to post-spawning fat loss and skin slackening causing mechanical scale crumbling — an alternative causal mechanism to the resorption hypothesis favored in this paper, left unresolved for Atlantic Coast shad.
