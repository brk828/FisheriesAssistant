# The Temperature of a Moorland Fishpond

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #limnology #hydrobiology

## Executive Summary

This 1966 paper by T. T. Macan and Rachel Maudsley in *Hydrobiologia* reports seven years (1958–1964) of continuous water-temperature records from [[Hodsons Tarn]], a small moorland pond in the English Lake District that contains fish. A [[Thermograph]] recorded temperature, which was then compared with [[Air Temperature]] from the nearby [[Ambleside]] meteorological station. The study aims were to archive the measurements, to relate water temperature to air temperature, sunshine and wind, and to test correlations between temperature and the emergence or development of aquatic invertebrates. The authors conclude that water temperature follows air temperature closely but diverges under specific weather conditions, and that emergence timing of several insects tracks the date water reaches 10–11 °C rather than accumulated degree-hours.

The document is an ecological research article, not a fish study. Its biological focus is on aquatic insects, with no fish-specific data reported.

## Background & Description

- **Study site:** Hodsons Tarn lies 190 m above sea level, has a surface area of 0.4427 ha, and reaches a maximum depth of 3 m. Its catchment is roughly 30 times the tarn's surface area, with one small inflow and seepage from surrounding bogs.
- **Instrumentation:** A recording [[Thermograph]] (Cambridge Instrument Company) was first used in a small stony stream (Macan 1958) and moved to the tarn in 1957. The bulb was generally 15–20 cm below the surface. Air data came from Ambleside, 6 km north and 46 m lower, and wind data from Ferry House, which the authors describe as a poor exposure.
- **Data limitations:** Ambleside recorded only daily maximum and minimum air temperature, so times were assumed to be noon and midnight. No satisfactory humidity data were available.
- **Objectives:** (1) archive temperature measurements for comparative use; (2) examine the relation between water temperature and air temperature, sunshine and wind; (3) seek correlations between temperature and biological events.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime.** Winter water temperature was generally 1–2 °C under ice. Spring warming varied between years: 11 °C was reached on 14 April 1959 and on 25 April 1963. The tarn exceeded 21 °C in most summers but never for long. The highest maxima tended to occur in June and the highest minima in July or August. Autumn cooling compressed the spread between earliest and latest dates.

**Water versus air.** Water was warmer than air for almost all of May to September. Water lagged air during abrupt changes. Thermal stratification was temporary, broken up by wind-driven seiches; no permanent stratification was found. Wind kept the basin well mixed given the valley's north–south wind alignment.

**Aquatic invertebrates.** The paper gives biological context drawn partly from earlier studies:
- *Leptophlebia* spp. (the only animals in the study that grew through winter) emerged on different dates each year, with a difference of about 14 days between earliest and latest emergence.
- *Pyrrhosoma nymphula*, a summer-growing dragonfly, showed a similar emergence pattern.
- *Lestes sponsa* overwinters as an egg and completes development in one summer, so cold years could prolong development into unfavourable air temperatures.
- *Cloeon dipterum* produced two generations per year only in warm summers. Its numbers fluctuated when fish were absent and nearly disappeared after fish were introduced, so predation probably acted with temperature stress.

**Fish relevance.** The text mentions that fish were introduced into the tarn, which is why it is described as a "fishpond". No fish species, counts or fish-specific temperature tolerances are reported.

## Key Findings

1. **Temperature record.** Monthly maximum and minimum water temperatures are given for 1958–1964 (Table 1). Hours spent at each temperature are tabulated in Table 4.
2. **Extremes (Table 5).** Greatest one-day rise was 5.0 °C; greatest one-day fall was 3.9 °C; greatest difference between average water temperatures on successive days was 2.67 °C; greatest air temperature range in one day was 22.8 °C; greatest air–water difference was 7.2 °C (water warmer) and 5.5 °C (air warmer).
3. **Air–water coupling.** Averages generally move together, with water lagging abrupt changes. Sunshine and wind drive divergence: cold northerly or easterly winds with sun warm the water while cooling the air; warm, cloudy Atlantic air raises air temperature (mainly the minimum) while water changes little.
4. **Winter comparison (Table 2 and Table 3).** Winters 1959/60 (mild) and 1962/63 (harsh) were compared using degree-hours above 0 °C and 4 °C. The 1962/63 totals were lower in every month.
5. **Emergence timing.** Emergence dates for *Leptophlebia* and *Pyrrhosoma* were not related to degree-hours. Instead, they tracked the date water reached 10–11 °C in each year, such as the 1959 early and 1963 late pattern.
6. **Growth comparison.** Moon (1938) measured nearly 4000 nymphs in winter 1934/35, finding steady growth with a slowdown at the end of January and acceleration in April. Pleskot (1953) suggested the first growth phase is temperature-independent, whereas the second phase is temperature-dependent.
7. **Rate effects.** Blunck (1924) showed that *Dytiscus marginalis* larvae starve at winter temperatures. Macan (1964) noted *Lestes sponsa* emergence shifting between July and August–September with summer temperature. Pacaud (1948) found that *Calopteryx splendens* ate about twice as much at 20 °C as at 10 °C and three times as much at 28 °C.
8. **Sampling recommendation.** Because lake temperature varies less in time and space than stream temperature, single readings (ideally a maximum–minimum thermometer, taken around 1600 h GMT) can be useful. The authors conclude that similar-sized waters should respond to air temperature in much the same way.
9. **Conclusions on distribution.** Low temperature does not kill any species in British standing water. It can restrict distribution by slowing development. Whether southern species would colonise the tarn under shorter winters remains speculative.
