# Natural Fishing Experiments in Marine Reserves 1983–1993: Community and Trophic Responses

#ichthyology #marine-biology #reef-fish-community

## Executive Summary

This study examined how fishing affected the density, biomass, species richness and family/trophic structure of the [[Coral Reef Fish]] community at two central Philippine islands, [[Sumilon Island]] and [[Apo Island]], from 1983 to 1993. Differing histories of [[Marine Reserves]] and fished areas at each island created a series of [[Natural Fishing Experiments]], monitored with repeated [[Visual Census]] surveys at four sites. Fishing removed roughly 15% (Sumilon) and 25% (Apo) of standing biomass per year. Total community biomass responded significantly, density did not, and family/trophic-group structure remained resilient except during an intense 1984 episode of [[Explosive Fishing]] and [[Muro-Ami Fishing]] at Sumilon. The authors attribute this resilience to the open nature of populations, likely upstream recruitment, the absence of obvious keystone taxa, and a relatively [[Non-Selective Fishing]] fishery.

## Background & Description

The paper is framed by concern over the sustainability of fisheries. [[Pauly and Christensen]] (1995) estimated that about 8% of world aquatic primary production supports world fisheries, rising to 35% on non-tropical continental shelves. [[Pauly et al.]] (1998) showed that fisheries were, on average, targeting lower trophic levels than two to three decades earlier. The authors note that multispecies responses to perturbation are poorly understood, with coral reef examples from Jamaica and Kenya linking fishing removal of herbivorous and urchin-feeding fishes to major shifts in benthic community structure, though effects on overall fishery productivity remain uncertain.

The study sites differed in reef geomorphology and management:

- **Sumilon Island:** The reserve was protected at various times between 1974 and 1993, and had a complex management history. It was open to municipal fishers using hook and line, spears, fish traps and gillnets, and was subjected to explosives and [[Muro-Ami Fishing]] in 1984–85. It was protected again from 1987 to 1992 and reopened in 1992–93. The non-reserve reef was heavily fished and produced high yields of reef fish.
- **Apo Island:** The reserve (sanctuary) was community-protected from 1982, but legal protection was not in place until August 1985. Local marine management was successful during most of the study. The non-reserve area was fished throughout by roughly 200 fishers using gillnets, bamboo traps and spears.

Because the protected and fished areas arose from management decisions rather than experimental design, the authors refer to them as natural experiments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reef structure.** At Sumilon, the reserve reef slope lay at 2–3 m depth at mean low tide, was mostly consolidated, and had relatively high coral cover in 1983 and 1993. Its slope was steep (roughly 45–55°) between 9 and 17 m. The non-reserve reef had shallower sand-covered slopes with less coral. At Apo, the reef crest lay at 6–7 m, with a high cover of living corals. Slopes were steep (35–40°) and the substratum overlay volcanic rock.

**Coral cover.** Sumilon reserve live coral cover (mean %) fell from 51.0 in 1983 to 30.2 in 1985, 30.0 in 1986, 32.3 in 1990, 31.3 in 1991, 43.8 in 1992 and 55.8 in 1993. Sumilon reserve was the only site with a visually obvious change in coral cover. Other sites showed no significant change in shallow-slope live coral cover between 1983 and 1993.

**Sampling design.** Surveys used replicate 50 m × 20 m areas. A single observer (G. R. Russ) swam three 50 m × 7 m strips using SCUBA, counting fishes at depths of 14–17 m and taking about 40–50 minutes per census. Six replicates were placed at each site (five at Apo non-reserve in 1983), giving 167 censuses across four sites and seven years. Replicates were re-located each year within ±5–10 m of prior positions.

**Fishery sampling.** Annual catch estimates came from Sumilon for five years (1978–80, 1983/84, 1985/86) and from Apo for three years (1980/81, 1985/86, 1986). Biomass was calculated from density and size-structure data using published [[Length-Weight Relationships]]. Fishing intensity was the percentage of community biomass removed annually. Selectivity was the mean percentage of a family in the catch divided by its mean percentage of biomass at the non-reserve sites.

## Key Findings

**1. Fishing intensity was high.** Fishing removed on average 15% (Sumilon) and 25% (Apo) of standing stock of coral reef fish per year. The authors note these are likely underestimates for periods when previously unfished areas were opened, such as Sumilon reserve in 1984 and 1992.

**2. The fishery was relatively non-selective.** Most families and trophic groups were caught roughly in proportion to their contribution to community biomass. [[Caesionidae]], [[Acanthuridae]] and [[Carangidae]] dominated both the yield and the biomass. [[Pomacentridae]] and Anthiinae made up a large share of biomass but almost none of the catch, because their small size makes them invulnerable to the gears used. Carangidae were a large share of the catch but a small share of visual census biomass, probably because they were caught in deeper, off-reef waters. Rank correlations between catch and biomass were significant at all four sites (for example, Sumilon reserve Spearman R = 0.919, p < 0.001). Apparent selectivity was noted toward Serranidae at Sumilon and large predators at Apo.

**3. Total community biomass responded, density did not.** Fishing significantly affected total biomass at three of four sites. Key changes:

- **Sumilon reserve:** biomass fell 28% between 1983 and 1985 during fishing, rose by a factor of 1.6 between 1985 and 1991 after fishing ceased, and fell 29% between 1991 and 1993 when fishing resumed.
- **Sumilon non-reserve:** biomass rose by a factor of 2.0 between 1988 and 1991 when fishing ceased, and fell 33% between 1991 and 1993.
- **Apo reserve:** biomass rose by a factor of 1.53 by 1988 (six years of protection) and 1.55 by 1991 (nine years).
- Total community density was not significantly affected by fishing.

**4. Species richness was largely unaffected.** Richness changed significantly only at Sumilon reserve, where it declined about 17% between 1991 and 1993 after fishing resumed. The authors attribute the biomass-but-not-richness pattern to heavily fished groups such as Caesionidae and Acanthuridae contributing far more to biomass than to numbers or species.

**5. Family/trophic structure was resilient.** Multivariate cluster analysis of biomass for 19 family/trophic groups showed strong spatial patterns, with all four sites differing significantly from one another and persisting for over a decade. The exception was Sumilon reserve, where the 1984 explosives and drive-net fishing changed structure markedly. Compared with 1983, the 1985 community showed significant decreases in Caesionidae, Pomacentridae, large predators and Chaetodontidae, and significant increases in Scaridae, small invertebrate-feeding Labridae and planktivorous Labridae (Cirrhilabrus) and Naso. The 1988 community had not recovered. By 1990–91 the community was more similar to 1983 but still significantly different. The 1992–93 fishing, using standard gears, had no significant effect on structure. The Apo reserve and non-reserve and Sumilon non-reserve showed no significant structural change.

**6. No evidence of trophic cascades.** The authors found little evidence of secondary effects, such as increases in prey after declines in large predators. They suggest that predators' opportunistic feeding and recruitment-driven prey variation may explain this. [[Hixon]] and [[Beets]] (1993) and [[Hixon]] (1991), however, argue that predation is an important structuring process on reefs.

**7. Implications for management.** The authors conclude that fishing strategies exploiting all groups roughly in proportion to their abundance may be less likely to cause dramatic shifts than targeting one or a few groups. They caution that such a strategy may still cause shifts if fishing becomes excessive. No evidence of [[Phase Shifts]] was observed, but the decade-long study may have been too short to detect them. The authors note that many multispecies systems may not show simple reversibility after overexploitation, and that the study period is short relative to the maximum longevities of many reef fishes (up to 25 years in some species).

**Conclusion.** Fishing intensity at Sumilon and Apo was high enough to affect total community biomass, but not density, significantly. The community displayed strong resilience of family/trophic structure, which the authors attribute to three community attributes (open populations, likely upstream recruitment supply, and no evident [[Keystone Species]]) and one fishery characteristic ([[Non-Selective Fishing]]).
