# Life History of the Tui Chub (Siphateles bicolor) from Eagle Lake, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 life history study by J. B. Kimsey of the California Department of Fish and Game examines the [[Tui Chub]] (*Siphateles bicolor*), the most abundant fish in [[Eagle Lake]], Lassen County. The work was launched because the [[Eagle Lake Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri aquilarum*) was near extinction, and the chub's relationship to the trout had to be understood before a management program could be designed. The study covers systematics, distribution, seasonal movements, diet, spawning, egg development, growth, condition, economic use, and the chub's role as forage and "buffer" species. The author concludes that the Eagle Lake population is a hybrid of *obesus* and *pectinifer* forms, and that the species may be useful as forage in fluctuating reservoirs.

## Background & Description

**Study context.** The work was the fifth in a series on native California Cyprinidae, following the [[Venus Roach]], [[Sacramento Hitch]], [[Greaser Blackfish]], and [[Sacramento Squawfish]]. [[Harry (1951)]] had described the embryology and early development of the Eagle Lake chub.

**Eagle Lake.** The lake lies at 5,100 ft elevation and covers approximately 15,000 acres (as cited). Its closed basin drains 498 square miles, with a mean seasonal runoff of 91,000 acre-feet. The main tributary is the 26-mile [[Pine Creek]], whose lower 20 miles and several smaller inlets are intermittent. An irrigation tunnel begun in 1924 lowered the lake about 30 feet; the project has since stopped and the tunnel is blocked. Wave-cut terraces show that the lake has fluctuated widely over time, with higher levels about 30 and 50 feet above the 1924 level. The author links Pleistocene high stands to a surface outflow into [[Lake Lahontan]] via Willow Creek Valley, which is consistent with four of the five native fish species being of Lahontan origin.

**Taxonomy.** The genus *Siphateles* has two California species, and *S. bicolor* has four subspecies. [[Snyder (1917)]] had reported both *S. pectinifer* (fine gill-rakers) and *S. obesus* (coarse gill-rakers) in Eagle Lake. Meristic data from 292 specimens showed intergradation between them. The gill-raker count is bimodal, while other diagnostic characters vary uniformly. The author therefore treats the population as *Siphateles bicolor*: *obesus* x *pectinifer*. Two body forms (an obese form with a nuchal lump, and a slender form) occur but do not correlate with gill-raker counts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Total alkalinity in 1948 ranged from 534 ppm at the mouth of Pine Creek (June) to 900 ppm in the shallow northern section (August), averaging about 709 ppm across stations. pH ranged from 8.4 to 9.6.

**Temperature and oxygen.** Surface temperatures fall well below freezing in winter, when up to 18 inches of ice can form. The summer maximum was 70°F (August 1948). Oxygen is plentiful near the surface but is frequently absent or too low to support fish below about 35 ft in summer. An ill-defined thermocline lies between 35 and 50 ft, though near-daily southerly winds often prevent a clear thermocline. Winter oxygen conditions are unknown. A station off Canyon Point (1948–1949) recorded dissolved oxygen falling from about 10 ppm near the surface to values as low as 1.2 to 1.3 ppm at the deepest sampled depths in summer.

**Distribution.** The chub is lacustrine and was never seen in tributary streams except in the lower Pine Creek estuary. Gill nets in that estuary caught no adults in 1948 and 1949. Post-larval fish, however, were seined there in November 1947, and the chub spawns heavily in Delta Bay, into which Pine Creek empties. Chub were absent from the lowermost semipermanent Pine Creek pools (1947–1950) but appeared upstream of the slough weir in 1953–1954, possibly from bait introductions or migration above the weir when it was open.

**Seasonal movements.** Same-year-class fish school together until first spawning. Adults concentrate on spawning grounds in spring. In winter (late December through March, not directly observed), adults are inferred to remain in deep water. On April 10, two gill nets at the edge of the ice took 114 sexually mature adults in 12 hours, showing a mass movement from the ice-covered deep south basin into the shallow, ice-free northern and middle sections. [[Snyder (1917)]] reported a similar movement in [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada. From May through September, shallow northern and middle sections filled with adults, and by late September the shores were full of young of the year. Large fish remain in the deep south basin, and fish occasionally enter the low-oxygen zone, where they were always dead and silvery in nets.

**Parasites.** The chub is lightly parasitized, with nematode and cestode intestinal parasites and occasional encysted stages, apparently without ill effects. Fish from the Pine Creek estuary were noticeably more heavily parasitized than those from open water.

**Predator relationships.** Schools are located by flocks of white pelicans and cormorants, whose movements track the seasonal shifts of the fish.

## Key Findings

**Diet.** Newly hatched fish feed almost at once on rotifers, diatoms, desmids, and other microscopic material. In a July 1948 sample of 30 adults (12.0–26.8 cm standard length, mean 17.4 cm), the stomach contents were:
- Animal plankton (copepods and cladocerans) in 83.3% of stomachs
- Higher plant remains (mainly *Potamogeton*) in 46.6%
- Surface insects in 16.6%, including one school feeding exclusively on a mayfly hatch
- Chironomid larvae in 13.3%
- Caddisfly cases in 6.6%

Scales in three stomachs were judged incidental and not evidence of piscivory. No correlation was found between gill-raker number and diet.

**Spawning.** Fish usually spawn for the first time in the spring of their third year. Ripe spawners were first taken on June 16, 1949, and a male was found sexually mature on May 10, 1950, at 57°F. Spawning occurs at about 60°F in water no deeper than three or four feet, and ends around July 1. Eggs are 1.5–1.9 mm in diameter, adhesive, and attach to rooted plants (*Myriophyllum spicatum*, *Ceratophyllum demersum*, and *Potamogeton* sp.). An 11-inch female produced 11,200 ripe eggs, a probable underestimate because eggs mature unevenly.

**Egg survival experiment.** Six screen-topped jars were placed in the lake, with eggs either attached to plants or free, and either in clear water or over bottom mud. Eggs in mud, or submerged in mud, died, while eggs attached to plants or kept clear of mud survived. The author concludes that only eggs kept off the bottom and out of mud develop normally. Eggs fertilized on July 25, 1948, hatched in nine days at laboratory air temperatures of 40–90°F. Plant-attached eggs torn loose by diving birds may explain newly hatched fish found in plankton tows far from spawning areas.

**Post-larval life.** Young remain in plant beds until about 1–2 cm long, then appear in large numbers along shorelines. Dense shore schools persisted until late September. By December 1950 the shallows were empty, and the author concludes that young of the year move into open water at their first winter and return to the shallows only to spawn.

**Growth.** Growth was measured from scales of 121 specimens. For 67 scales, the scale radius correlated with standard length at r = 0.97. Annuli were readable to the fifth year. Growth is regular until the fourth year, then tapers. The mean standard lengths by age are:

| Age | Mean standard length |
|---|---|
| 1 | 6.6 cm (2.7 in) |
| 2 | 11.2 cm (4.4 in) |
| 3 | 16.7 cm (6.6 in) |
| 4 | 21.6 cm (8.5 in) |
| 5 | 24.9 cm (9.8 in) |
| 6 | 28.1 cm (11.1 in) |
| 7 | 32.5 cm (12.8 in) (tentative) |

The largest unaged individual was a female of 40.9 cm (16 in). Scale aging became unreliable beyond five years because of spawning checks and erosion.

**Condition factor (K).** Across 139 specimens (12.4–34.9 cm), the mean K was 1.92 (range 1.04–2.94), with lower values in smaller fish. The condition dropped after spawning, from 1.98 (61 spawners, June 16–25, 1948) to 1.82 (42 fish, July 17–27), then recovered to 1.94 (36 fish, August 3–27). The author calls the differences slight but attributable to spawning.

**Economic value.** The chub has good flavor but many small bones. Local residents ate it, and a water-pack canning trial produced a palatable product similar to canned tuna. Sport fishing is mainly in the south basin, using worms, and moonlight surface-school fishing with small spinners. A commercial seining permit (1942–1944) yielded commercial quantities only in 1943, when about 8,000 pounds were taken in May from Delta Bay spawning concentrations. An export order was canceled, the market rejected the fish as too bony, and only a few hundred pounds were eaten. In 1949 the best remaining value offered was $20 per ton for reduction. The author notes that anglers view such fish as "rough fish," an attitude that developed recently as chemical eradication made way for game-fish stocking.

**Management role.** In warmwater management the chub falls in the same category as the [[Greaser Blackfish]]. The two share similar young-stage diets, but the chub is more animal-oriented as an adult. Its slower growth is attributed to the harsher climate of Eagle Lake. Its spawning temperature matches that of the [[Largemouth Black Bass]], and their spawning habitats do not conflict.

**Buffer species for the trout.** The chub is an important buffer species for the endemic Eagle Lake Rainbow Trout, which migrates up Pine Creek each April to spawn above Bogard. The author suggests that newly hatched trout are trapped upstream until the next spring freshets. Yearlings enter the lake at about 5–7 inches. Pelicans, mergansers, and other fish-eating birds gather at the creek mouth. Tahoe Suckers, Lahontan Redside Shiners, and Lahontan Speckled Dace also run into Pine Creek at this time, and the chub's abundance during trout migration spreads predation pressure away from the trout. Because chub swim near the surface, they remain available to birds throughout the year.

**Competition and introductions.** If trout enter the lake as fry or fingerlings, the chub would likely compete for food and could depress the trout population. The author describes the chub as a serious nuisance where it has been introduced into trout lakes stocked with fingerlings. He attributes this mainly to poor natural trout spawning conditions, and he judges competition between planted trout and the chub to be severe. Where the chub is native to large lakes, good trout fisheries often exist. Chub in low-productivity waters such as [[Donner Lake]], Nevada County, do not grow as large, and the species may serve as a forage fish for largemouth bass in fluctuating reservoirs.

**Conclusions.** The Eagle Lake chub is best treated as a hybrid population (*Siphateles bicolor*: *obesus* x *pectinifer*) with a bimodal gill-raker pattern not tied to other characters. It eats mainly zooplankton, higher plants, and insect larvae, and it spawns on plants at about 60°F in shallow water. It occupies shallows during its first year and returns there only to spawn. The species may be useful as a forage fish in fluctuating reservoirs.
