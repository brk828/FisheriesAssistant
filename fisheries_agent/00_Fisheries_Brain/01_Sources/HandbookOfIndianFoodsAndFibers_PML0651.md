# Indigenous Fish Use and Aquatic Subsistence in Western North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ethnoichthyology

## Executive Summary

This document is an ethnographic and archaeological compilation on how Indigenous peoples of arid western North America and central Mexico used lakes, rivers, marshes and coastal waters for food, tools and materials. Fish are one strand among many. Most of the text covers wild plants, mammals, birds, insects and salt, and only some sections address fish directly. The fish content covers the Great Basin lakes, the Owens Valley, the Central Valley rivers, the Chumash coast, the Lower Colorado River and the Sonoran and Central Mexican regions. The sources are archaeological sites, coprolite analyses, historical accounts and early ethnographers. No new survey data are presented.

## Background & Description

The compilation draws on several lines of evidence:

- **Coprolite analysis.** Dietary remains from dry caves reveal food use. Over 6,000 human coprolites were recovered at [[Lovelock Cave]] in western Nevada, dating from about 2500 B.C. to the historic period. Hundreds more came from Hidden Cave, Humboldt Cave and other sites. The document notes that coprolites from seasonal campsites record only the season of occupation.
- **Fish remains and midden analysis.** Fish bones, otoliths and fishing gear are recovered from middens. Key examples are the Talepop village and the Wadi Beach lakeshore sites.
- **Ethnographic accounts.** Early observers include Steward, Latta, Kroeber, Spier, Father Kino and Padre Garcés.

The text also includes a note on fish identification: the lacustrine sucker from Lovelock Cave was examined by ichthyologists who concluded that Humboldt Lake was a freshwater lake at the time of occupation (Hubbs and Miller, 1948).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Great Basin lakes.** The Great Basin was "arid," with less than 5 inches of precipitation per year. Even so, Indigenous groups intensively exploited lacustrine resources. Lacustrine reliance is documented from about 3000 B.C. and increased until about A.D. 1800. Waterfowl, cattail (*Typha*) and bulrush seeds made up a large share of the diet. Fish were abundant enough that large quantities of net sinkers, hooks and fishing equipment have been found on shores and lake beds.

**Owens Valley.** Fish included Owens sucker (*Catostomus* sp.), dace (*Rhinichthys*), two species of chub (*Gila*), a stickleback (*Gasterosteus*) and three pupfish species. Trout were absent from Owens River and were later transplanted from streams on the west slope of the Sierra.

**Central Valley and Sierra foothills.** The Yokuts and neighboring groups fished the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers, where salmon ran. Individual and cooperative methods were both used. Large king salmon, reaching over four feet and sometimes over 100 pounds, were a major food source.

**Chumash coast and kelp beds.** Kelp beds (*Macrocystis*, *Laminaria*, *Fucales*) formed dense habitat for marine fish, invertebrates and sea mammals. The document cites North (1971), who listed 299 species for the kelp-bed and rocky-habitat fauna of California and northern Baja California, of which 113 were "generally common." Upwelling in the Santa Barbara Channel supported high primary productivity and therefore high fish abundance.

**Lower Colorado River and Lake Cahuilla.** The prehistoric lake was a large body of water. Its fish fauna included five species common to the lower Colorado River. The Colorado pikeminnow was "the white salmon of the Colorado," the most important fish of the Mojave and Yuma. It was caught year-round, and specimens weighing up to 80 pounds have been recorded.

**Sonoran Desert and Central Mexico.** Fish were used in several cultures. The Seri and Tarahumara used plant-based fish poisons. The Mexica lagoons of the Valley of Mexico supported ducks, fish and frogs.

## Key Findings

**1. Lacustrine food systems in the Great Basin**
- Lovelock Cave yielded over 6,000 human coprolites from about 2500 B.C. to the historic period.
- Coprolite analyses across Great Basin caves show strong reliance on lake foods from about 3000 B.C. onward, persisting until about A.D. 1800.
- Lacustrine sucker remains (*Chasmistes*, including hybrids with *Catostomus*) were identified by Hubbs and Miller (1948), who concluded that Humboldt Lake was freshwater at the time of occupation.
- Fish hooks were made from bent sharpened bone or wood. Some had a line tied to the end, and others were attached to a lashing.

**2. Owens Valley fishing**
- The valley's native fish fauna listed above was used by local groups. Trout were introduced from outside the drainage.

**3. Central Valley fishing and fish technology**
- Cooperative methods included fish dams, weirs, V-shaped traps and sieve-like nets.
- Individual methods included A-frame dip nets, detachable-point harpoons and hooks on set lines.
- Fish poisons included buckeye (*Aesculus californica*) and soap plant (*Chlorogalum*).
- River mussels and eels were also used. Eels were dried for winter storage.

**4. Chumash marine economy**
- Plank canoes were 10–18 to 25–30 feet long, built from planks sewn together and sealed with yop (a mixture of wogo tar and pine pitch).
- Marine fish were one of the two most important food resources, along with acorns.
- Historical sardine schools were reported at more than ten million fish, which declined sharply over the 20th century.
- Johnson (1982) analyzed fish remains from the Talepop village and ranked the ten most important prehistoric species. These included Pacific sardine, soupfin shark, northern anchovy, mako shark, Pacific angel shark, bat ray, horn shark, shovelnose guitarfish, blue shark and albacore. Rockfish and white seabass were also in the list.
- Fish of kelp beds and rocky reefs included grass rockfish, olive rockfish, kelp bass, opaleye, several surfperches and California sheephead.
- Fish of sandy bottoms included Pacific angel shark, leopard shark, shovelnose guitarfish, bat stingray, white seabass, white croaker, starry flounder and California halibut.

**5. Early coastal fishing expansion**
- Otoliths found at a site near Point Conception show that inhabitants of a 9,000-year-old midden first fished intertidal species, then took deeper-water species using better gear and mobile craft. They did not extend far beyond the outer kelp beds. Fitch and Lavenberg (1971) noted that the seas were "just as inhospitable" during the 9,000 years of occupation as today.

**6. Lower Colorado River fish and fishing**
- Fish were caught with drag, gill and dip nets, traps and willow scoops.
- Fish poisons were not used by the Yuma, but Gifford (1933) recorded that the Cocopa used willow leaves to stupefy fish.
- Fish made up about 20 percent of the diet by one account. Plant foods contributed about 20 to 25 percent, and rabbits, quail and doves about 15 percent.

**7. Aquatic plant and invertebrate foods**
- Cattail (*Typha*) and arrowhead (*Sagittaria*) were important. Cattail rhizomes and pollen were eaten, and arrowhead tubers were a staple.
- Grasshoppers were harvested in large numbers in Owens Valley and boiled, roasted or pounded into a paste.
- The Mexica ate *ezcahuitli* (aquatic worms) from the lagoon muds.

**8. Fish poisons and plant use across regions**
- Plant-based fish poisons were used across the study area, including buckeye, soap plant, poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*), and various Asteraceae.
- The Tarahumara used many plants as fish poisons, and fish poisons remained the most important way of obtaining fish in the region into the modern era.

**9. Broader ecological context**
- Burning practices influenced habitat. Indigenous groups burned brush and grass to increase grass shoots and game, and they also set fires to maintain productive plant communities.
- Lake Cahuilla supported a large waterfowl population. Some species were later identified from bone deposits and coprolites by Wilke (1978).
