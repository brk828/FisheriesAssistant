# Razorback Sucker Morphology, Hybridization, and Tribal Restoration Partnership in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a two-part correspondence set. A 1985 letter from the [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]] pledges cooperation with the California Department of Fish and Game to help recover the [[Razorback Sucker]], a fish once a dietary staple of the [[Mohave People]] and therefore culturally significant. A 1982 letter from Dr. Gene R. Wilde (University of Nevada, Las Vegas) reports preliminary morphometric results on razorback suckers and razorback × [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] hybrids from [[Lake Mohave]]. Wilde concludes that the lake population shows strong evidence of introgressive hybridization, though he notes that genetic data would be needed to prove it.

## Background & Description

- **Tribal project (1985):** The Tribal Council approved a request from Ms. Linda Ulmer, Fisheries Biologist, on September 11, 1985. The project aims to bring the razorback sucker back from the brink of extinction. The Tribal Museum Director, Charles Lamb, and the Tribal Fish and Game Department were to work closely with state biologists. The council also suggested that unused ponds in the canal system built during the reservation's construction might be used for reintroducing native species, and it hoped the project could serve as a model for other tribes.
- **Study design (1982):** Wilde's team examined approximately 330 razorback suckers from three Lake Mohave sites: Arizona Bay (largest sample), Telulla Cove, and the Six-Mile Cove area. Eight measurements were taken using standard methods, modified in how total length was measured. The analysis focused on males because significant sexual dimorphism in some traits made mixed-sex comparisons unsuitable.
- **Comparative baseline:** Wilde had little data on the range of variation in the species. Analyses used only fish over 100 mm SL, since smaller fish showed no allometry. Hubbs and Miller's (1953) data showed relatively low variation, with coefficients of determination (r²) roughly in the 0.8–0.9 range (partly illegible in the scan). Lake Mohave fish showed greater variation in these characters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Wilde proposed two explanations for the high morphological variation in Lake Mohave razorback suckers:

1. **Selective pressures:** Differing selective pressures in lacustrine (lake) versus riverine conditions may allow a wider range of genotypic expression.
2. **Introgressive hybridization:** Gene flow from [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] populations.

Wilde also reasoned about timing. If the native population shows no recruitment, hybridization probably occurred in the impoundments of [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Havasu]] and continued until shortly after [[Lake Mohave]] formed. If the population does recruit, hybridization may have intensified after Lake Mohave was impounded and may still be ongoing.

Wilde noted that increased water depth in lakes may influence gape and lip morphology in ways not seen in riverine fish, a factor that would produce variability without hybridization.

## Key Findings

**Morphological measurements**

- **Body depth:** Intended to measure the prominence of the nuchal keel. Expected hybrids to have lower values, but a gradation in keel size was not reflected in measurements.
- **Peduncle depth:** Expected to be lower in hybrids. Some razorback suckers had relatively narrow peduncles, which supports a flannelmouth influence.
- **Head depth:** Not expected to distinguish hybrids from razorback suckers; collected mainly for further characterization.
- **Lip width:** Highly variable (r² = 0.19). Not interpreted meaningfully at that time.
- **Lip length and gape:** Lip length was correlated with gape in both Miller's fish and Wilde's fish. Gape was expected to be lower in hybrids and somewhat greater in razorbacks, which was generally true, though Wilde noted that some razorbacks had relatively small gapes.

**Lower lip lobe separation (the key diagnostic trait)**

- In razorback suckers, the lobes of the lower lip are widely separated (large separation, per Lockington 1881 and Hubbs and Miller).
- In flannelmouth suckers, the separation is slight.
- In razorback × flannelmouth hybrids, separation is intermediate.
- Six classes of separation were recognized. Wilde considered a small separation to be clearly a flannelmouth trait. The presence of this trait in Lake Mohave razorback suckers was described as strong evidence of introgressive hybridization.

**Conclusions and limitations**

- Wilde concluded that the data "strongly suggest" introgressive hybridization but cannot prove it. He recommended genetic analysis (for example, the Hubbs and Miller and Wilde data sets) to confirm the hypothesis.
- Hybrids are generally intermediate to parental species, but backcrosses cannot be predicted in the same way. Backcrossed fish may be morphologically indistinguishable from pure razorback suckers if razorback genes are dominant. In Lake Mohave, flannelmouth traits (lower lip lobe separation, peduncle depth, gape, nuchal keel development) appear dominant or codominant.
- Wilde offered to show Ulmer the lip variation at the Lake Mohave field site, using photographs of normal-appearing razorbacks, hybrids, and a "good" razorback sucker.

**Tribal partnership outcomes**

- The Colorado River Indian Tribes approved the joint project and welcomed further cooperation. Their letter also suggested that future projects could use canal ponds for native species reintroduction.
