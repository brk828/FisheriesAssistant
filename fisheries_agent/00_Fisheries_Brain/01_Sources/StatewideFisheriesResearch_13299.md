# Fishery Investigations of the Colorado River from Glen Canyon Dam to the Paria River Confluence

#ichthyology #salmonidae #catostomidae #fisheries-management #dam-impacts #hydroelectric-peaking

## Executive Summary

This performance report documents the second year (July 1980 - June 1981) of a three-year investigation into the biological impacts of fluctuating water levels on the [[Colorado River]] fishery below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The study area extends from the dam to the [[Paria River]] confluence, encompassing the renowned [[Lee's Ferry]] fishery. A total of 2,537 fish representing seven species and one hybrid were collected from four sampling stations. [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) dominated the fishery (59% of collections, 87% of creel), with [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) comprising 7% of collections. Native [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) represented 25% of all fish collected. The study employed trammel nets, tagging programs (>3,000 fish tagged), creel census (14,000+ angler days), invertebrate sampling, and spawning observations. Water level fluctuations from dam operations were identified as causing significant stranding mortality of spawning trout, dessication of invertebrate food base, and beach erosion affecting recreational access.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has been acclaimed as one of the Southwest's premier trout fisheries since 1970. The dam, closed in 1964, transformed the river from a warm, turbid system experiencing floods exceeding 100,000 cfs to a cold, clear tailwater with controlled releases. [[Rainbow Trout]] have been regularly stocked since 1964, with supplemental stockings of [[Brook Trout]] and [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]]. The isothermic characteristics of the river (cold water released from Lake Powell) permit continuous fish growth year-round.

Four fish sampling stations were established: approximately 9, 18, and 27 km below the dam, and at the mouth of the [[Paria River]]. Fish were sampled primarily with 100 m trammel nets drifted during dark hours (3:00-5:00 AM), which proved 97% capture efficient compared to daytime drifting (30-40% fewer fish due to visual avoidance). The [[Paria River]] lower 300 m was sampled with modified hoop nets and seines.

The study area encompasses approximately 48 ha of identified spawning habitat across 18 major spawning areas. The river supports a diverse macroinvertebrate community dominated by the amphipod *Gammarus lacustris* (scuds), chironomid midges, and extensive mats of the filamentous alga *Cladophora glomerata*. Riparian vegetation is dominated by three species of salt cedar (*Tamarix* spp.) that established after dam closure eliminated pre-dam flood scouring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] tailwater below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] represents a unique cold-water fishery in the desert Southwest. Water temperature and annual volume remain relatively constant, but daily discharge patterns fluctuate dramatically due to hydroelectric power generation. Normal flows rarely exceed 25,000 cfs, with typical maximum releases around 26,000 cfs, though the dam can release up to 33,000 cfs.

The aquatic food web is atypically dominated by aquatic plants. Food habit analysis revealed [[Rainbow Trout]] diet consisted of 58% *Cladophora glomerata* by volume, 22% *Gammarus lacustris* (scuds), and 17% chironomid midges (*Chironomus* sp.). This heavy reliance on periphyton is unusual for salmonid populations. The benthic macroinvertebrate community is concentrated in *Cladophora* mats growing on the rim of the river trough below the low water line. Invertebrate densities in exposed periphyton mats may reach 3,500 organisms/m².

Native fish species persist in the system: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] is abundant in both the [[Colorado River]] and lower [[Paria River]] (25% of all collections), while [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*) represented only 4% of collections, suggesting population monitoring is warranted. [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) are common in shallow reaches of the [[Paria River]]. No federally threatened or endangered fish were surveyed. [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) are relatively uncommon (3% of collections) but locally abundant in spillway pools below the dam where water temperatures may be 10°C higher than the main river.

Non-resident species occasionally enter through the dam, including verified reports of threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*), channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and when Lake Powell reached capacity in June 1980, striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*) and walleye (*Stizostedion vitreum*) entered through outlet pipes.

Water quality analysis showed no evidence of disease or parasitism in examined specimens. Heavy metal concentrations (zinc, copper, iron, mercury) in [[Rainbow Trout]] flesh and liver were within background levels compared to pollution-free waters and hatchery-reared fish. The USFWS Fish Disease Control Center reported negative results for all viral, bacterial, and parasitic analyses.

## Key Findings

**Fish Population Structure and Distribution:**
- 2,537 fish collected representing seven species and one hybrid
- [[Rainbow Trout]]: 59% of collections, 87% of creel; average size 47.3 cm TL, 1.38 kg
- [[Brook Trout]]: 7% of collections, 8% of creel; average size 35.1 cm TL, 0.61 kg
- State record [[Brook Trout]] (5 lb 2 oz) caught April 1981
- [[Cutthroat Trout]]: 2% of collections, 5% of creel (from single 1978 stocking of 60,000 fish)
- Well-defined habitat segregation between rainbow and brook trout contributing to excellent growth for both species
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]: 25% of all collections, abundant in both rivers
- [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]]: 4% of collections, warranting close population monitoring

**Tagging and Movement:**
- Over 3,000 fish tagged with color-coded disc tags (trout) and anchor tags (carp, suckers)
- 83 tags returned by anglers, 34 fish recaptured during surveys
- One [[Brook Trout]] recaptured near Badger Rapids below Lee's Ferry
- One [[Brook Trout]] and one [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] recaptured 60 miles below Lee's Ferry near Little Colorado River
- Five [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] originally tagged by Museum of Northern Arizona in 1978 recaptured, demonstrating movement up to 75 miles

**Hatchery Fish Releases and Survival:**
- 844 [[Rainbow Trout]] (24.5 cm TL), 966 [[Brook Trout]] (22.4 cm TL), and 857 [[Cutthroat Trout]] (14.5 cm TL) tagged and released at three locations (Glen Canyon Dam, "Nine-mile", Lee's Ferry) between July-November 1980
- Scale analysis of 270 rainbow scales examined; isothermic conditions permit continuous growth
- Spawning marks and release checks distinguishable on scales
- One in five rainbows shows slower growth during first 3-6 months, potentially indicating wild vs. hatchery origin

**Food Habits and Trophic Ecology:**
- Diet atypically dominated by aquatic plants: 58.3% *Cladophora glomerata*
- *Gammarus lacustris* (scuds): 22.1% by volume, 57.7% frequency of occurrence, average 18 per stomach
- Chironomidae (midges): 17.2% by volume, 62.9% frequency of occurrence, average 144.7 per stomach (larvae 34.6, pupae 80, adults 0.8)
- Other items: Physa sp. snails (16.4% frequency), oligochaetes, leeches, orthopterans (all <1%)
- Common poolmat (*Zannichellia palustris*) provides microhabitat for scuds in high-velocity areas

**Spawning Ecology and Recruitment:**
- 18 major spawning areas identified covering approximately 48 ha
- Most redds constructed along loose cobble bars in zone of water fluctuation
- Main channel not utilized due to heavy armoring or shifting sand
- Greatest water level fluctuation concurrent with winter spawning run (third week of November)
- Redds typically exposed within 12 hours of construction due to water level drops
- Adult fish often return to same spawning territory or redd as water rises, but eggs dessicated
- Small numbers of fry infrequently observed; no evidence that natural recruitment above Lee's Ferry contributes significantly to fishery
- Stranding mortality of spawning adults documented during water level fluctuations

**Creel Census and Angler Use (April 1980 - March 1981):**
- Conservatively 14,000+ angler days (likely underestimate due to summer 1980 high flows)
- 2,112 anglers checked over 24-hour sampling periods (2 weekdays, 2 weekend days per month)
- Resident anglers: 49-83% depending on month
- Boat fishing: 58% of anglers (38-87% by month)
- Shore fishing: 36% of anglers (2-40% by month)
- Angling methods: bait 46-73%, combination 24-51%, lure 1-16%, fly 0-6%
- Total fish creeled (expanded): 1,202-2,891 per month
- Average angling day: 6.6-8.5 hours
- Successful anglers: 30-61% depending on month
- Catch rate: 0.089-0.35 fish/hr (average ~0.2 fish/hr)
- Harvest rate decreased from 0.24 fish/hr in 1978 to 0.2 fish/hr in 1980-81
- Average [[Rainbow Trout]] creeled: 46.5 cm TL, 1.4 kg (3+ pounds) - significantly larger than other Arizona trout fisheries (average 28 cm, 0.23 kg)
- Average size of rainbows increasing from 1968-69 (1.2 kg) to 1980-81 (1.5 kg)

**Proposed Dam Modifications and Predicted Impacts:**

The Bureau of Reclamation proposed retrofitting Glen Canyon Dam to add 250,000 kw capacity, increasing maximum discharge from 33,000 cfs to 40,000 cfs with longer periods of low flow:

*Impacts on Fish Populations:*
- Increased stranding mortality of spawning trout during longer low-flow periods
- Adult spawning fish killed by stranding during water level fluctuations; reluctant to abandon territories until redds complete
- Deaths occur during minimum release at night; dead fish swept into main river as water rises
- Predicted flow regime expected to increase annual spawning mortality

*Impacts on Food Base:*
- Scuds and midge pupae sensitive to dessication; concentrated in *Cladophora* mats below low water line
- *Cladophora* destroyed by prolonged exposure (2-3 hours) at summer temperatures
- Estimated 3,500 macroinvertebrates/m² mortality in exposed periphyton mats
- Decline in fish condition noted following summer low flows in 1978-1979
- Predicted longer summer and winter low flows could detrimentally impact food organisms and threaten trophy fishery status
- Narrower food base would jeopardize stocking success and retard growth rates

*Impacts on Beaches and Riparian Communities:*
- 14 species of emergent aquatics and riparian plants in riparian zone spanning up to 50 m from high water line
- Salt cedar (*Tamarix* spp.) dominates riparian zone; expanded since dam closure eliminated flood scouring
- Predicted 40,000 cfs flows would uproot emergent aquatics and destroy riparian plants unable to withstand prolonged inundation
- Based on Grand Canyon studies: 45% of beaches flooded at 40,000 cfs, one-third fewer campsites
- 30% of riparian vegetation removed in "critical" areas
- Estimated 50% of established beaches (campsites) in Glen Canyon would be removed
- 70-80% of overnight camping occurs at 17 locations, most adjacent to high water line near salt cedar
- Upper talus generally unsuitable for camping (unshaded, boulder-strewn, steep, catclaw and camelthorn)

*Impacts on Angler Use:*
- 58% of anglers fish from boats; accessibility determined by water release patterns
- Anglers cannot reach upper river until ~5,000 cfs to pass "Three-mile bar"
- Longer low-flow periods would reduce boat size (draft) that can be safely operated
- Reduced accessibility to upper river and shortened angling day (currently 7.5 hours average)
- Greater inconvenience to boaters and increased safety hazards

**Management Recommendations:**

1. *Evaluate "quick-kill" regulation:* 1980 regulation requires anglers to kill or release fish immediately. Difficult to enforce and potentially counter-effective if anglers continue fishing after limit and return gutted/filleted fish when larger ones caught. Needs reevaluation.

2. *Increase enforcement effort:* Page Wildlife Management District filled less than half the time since 1978. Arizona's top trout fishery deserves greater enforcement.

3. *Designate as trophy trout water:* Lee's Ferry produces average 3-pound rainbows vs. 0.5-pound average for other Arizona trout fisheries. Fewer large fish (15+ lb class) caught each year, though fishery stable and average size increasing. Increasing angling pressure (10,000 in 1978 to 14,000+ in 1980-81). Proposed special regulations:
   - Legal minimum size limit (18-20 inches)
   - Fly or lure only, possibly barbless hooks
   - Size restriction on portion of bag limit (e.g., 3-fish limit with 2 fish minimum 19 inches/5 pounds)
   - Expected harvest rate drop to ~0.1 fish/hr
   - Control total annual rainbow harvest (e.g., 20,000 kg/yr, 60-70 kg/ha) by adjusting size restrictions and bag limits
   - Essential monthly creel survey (40 man-days/year initially)
   - Two-thirds of states with trout fisheries have minimum size restrictions; 48% require lure-only, 56% have fly-only restrictions in selected waters

4. *Enhance wild trout production:* Maintain static flows for three-month period during spawning to keep redds covered. Similar approach in Montana's Madison River increased wild trout from 32% to 40%. Other hydroelectric facilities manipulating releases for incubation increased sport fish populations up to ten-fold.

**Aquatic Vegetation:**
- 24 species of aquatic and riparian plants identified
- Most emergent aquatics grow at lower edge of high water line, partially inundated during peak discharge
- Common poolmat (*Zannichellia palustris*) dominates submersed macrophytes, restricted to lower trough below low waterline
- Poolmat grows on fluvial dunes in high-velocity areas and on trough rim in complex with *Cladophora*
- Most abundant along extreme lower edge of fluctuation zone; less resistant to dessication than periphyton
- Provides microhabitat for scuds in high-velocity areas over shifting sand where filamentous algae not established
- Salt cedar leaves occasionally eaten by trout (0.6% frequency, 0.3% volume) but insignificant in diet
- Riparian plants important for recreation: shelter, summer shade, firewood availability
- Essentially all major camping areas in or adjacent to salt cedar stands

**Macroinvertebrate Community:**
- Benthic macroinvertebrates sampled along main axis of river trough with surber net and SCUBA
- Zone of fluctuation randomly sampled with bottom dredge at range of flows
- Partial inventory includes: Physa sp. (gastropod), oligochaetes, *Gammarus lacustris* (amphipod), crayfish (*Procambarus clarki*), stoneflies (Nemouridae), dragonflies and damselflies, water boatmen, water striders, caddisflies, beetles, midges (Chironomidae), and others
- Samples being examined and quantified

**Water Quality:**
- Samples taken February and August 1980 from Colorado and Paria Rivers
- Colorado River: alkalinity 110-132 mg/L, hardness 269-300 mg/L CaCO₃, pH 6.3-7.1, conductance 700-810 µmhos/cm
- Paria River: alkalinity 150 mg/L, hardness 680 mg/L CaCO₃, pH 7.04, conductance 1200 µmhos/cm
- Heavy metals in [[Rainbow Trout]] tissue: iron 0.1 mg/kg, copper 0
