# Cuatro Ciénegas Fishes: Research Review, Habitat-Diversity Test, and Dexter Hatchery Recommendations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This note combines two documents. The first is a 1984 journal paper by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) that reviews research on the fishes of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] in Coahuila, México, and tests whether species diversity tracks habitat size in a local setting. The second is a 1984–1985 evaluation letter and report by Minckley on personnel, facilities and programs at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH), New Mexico, which holds endangered fishes for conservation.

The basin supports at least 16 native fish species, 8 of them endemic. Species richness per collection rises with habitat size in stable springs and spring-fed rivers, while unstable habitats (playas, ephemeral pools) and canals show little size relationship. Stable habitats are dominated by ostariophysine and percoid fishes, and unstable habitats by cyprinodontoids.

## Background & Description

The Cuatro Ciénegas intermontane basin has an unusually diverse fish fauna for a North American desert. Eight families are represented, including Nearctic groups (Cyprinidae, Ictaluridae, Centrarchidae, Percidae) and Neotropical groups (Characidae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, Cichlidae). Minckley notes that little beyond anecdote had been published on their ecology before this review.

Sampling was done with seines, gill nets and hoop nets from 1958 to 1981, supplemented by underwater observation. Records were compiled from 46 localities on the basin floor, totaling 462 samples:

| Habitat type | Localities/samples given | Collections |
|---|---|---|
| Headsprings (lagunas and pozos) | 17 headsprings | 191 |
| Rivers and creeks (ríos, riachuelos) | 10 | 127 |
| Playa lakes, marshes, ephemeral pools | 11 | 83 |
| Canals | 10 | 61 |

Key endemic and notable taxa:

- [[Notropis xanthicara]], a rheophilic minnow named by Minckley and Lytle (1969).
- [[Cyprinodon atrorus]] and [[Cyprinodon bifasciatus]], pupfishes described by Miller (1968). *C. bifasciatus* is restricted to headsprings.
- [[Lucania interioris]] and [[Gambusia longispinis]], both found in weedy ephemeral habitats.
- [[Xiphophorus gordoni]], a platyfish with a taxonomic history. Rosen and Bailey (1963) treated it as a subspecies of *X. couchianus*, while Rosen (1979) restored it as a full species. Minckley retains it as a full species.
- [[Cichlasoma minckleyi]], a cichlid that Minckley considers a [[Species Flock]] rather than a single polytypic species.
- An undescribed *Etheostoma* species.

Several forms are considered differentiated at the subspecific level but lack formal names, including *Dionda episcopa*, *Ictalurus lupus*, *Lepomis megalotis* and *Micropterus salmoides*. *Astyanax mexicanus*, *Pylodictis olivaris*, *Gambusia marshi* and *Cichlasoma* cf. *cyanoguttatum* are undifferentiated or little studied. *Gambusia marshi* shows two color types and chromosome counts of 2N = 42, 46 and 48.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat types and conditions.** Headsprings are thermal (25–38 °C) limnocrenes ranging from more than 20 ha and 5 m deep to under 10 m² and 10 cm deep. Basin-floor rivers and creeks range from about 2–3 m³/s to a few L/s and are largely spring-fed. Marshes are relatively stable and tied to lagunas, pozos and rivers. Playa lakes and ephemeral pools are terminal or near-terminal sinks with the most severe conditions, including wide swings in temperature and total dissolved solids (Minckley and Cole 1968).

**Human modification.** Canals drain springs or divert river water, lowering lagunas and shrinking marshes. Of 18 major headsprings listed in 1958–60, 9 were directly modified, and by 1981 all of these plus Poza la Becerra had been canalized. Agriculture has occupied the area since the 16th century, so some changes are old. Of 191 headspring samples, 36 came from modified habitats and were analyzed separately.

**Setting.** The basin covers less than 1,500 km², is endorheic, and lies at 700–760 m elevation with little relief. Minckley describes it as a "natural laboratory" with stable habitats at one extreme and highly unstable ones at the other. Dispersal barriers are minimal on the basin floor, so a species' absence from a locality is read as a response to environmental factors rather than physical exclusion.

**Connection to the Rio Salado.** The basin now drains, partly through human activity, into the upper Río Salado system, which also holds *Gambusia marshi*.

## Key Findings

**Habitat size and diversity.**
- In natural, stable lagunas, pozos, rivers and creeks, richness rose with habitat size. Correlation was highest for flowing systems (r = 0.79) and appeared linear.
- In unmodified headsprings, fish richness levelled off above an arbitrary rank of about 3.0 (more than about 200 m²), at a mean of 6.9 ± 0.33 species per collection (n = 98), despite a maximum available pool of at least 14 species.
- Linear correlation for unmodified lagunas (r = 0.63) was not significantly higher (p < 0.05) than for canalized lagunas (r = 0.60). Minckley concludes that extinction with decreasing habitat size is not a factor within observed limits, even when size reduction is artificial.
- Playas, marshes and ephemeral pools showed little correlation between size and richness. Water quality (high salinity, temperature extremes) strongly limited diversity there. Marsh data points sat at the high end of faunal values, indicating amelioration of severe conditions.
- Canals showed essentially no correlation between habitat size and fish diversity.

**Assemblage structure.**
- Large springs have a distinctive, predictable fauna of *Astyanax mexicanus*, *Notropis xanthicara*, *Ictalurus lupus*, *Cyprinodon bifasciatus*, *Gambusia marshi*, *Micropterus salmoides* and *Cichlasoma minckleyi*. Spring-fed rivers add *Etheostoma* sp. and *Lepomis megalotis*.
- Ostariophysine and percoid fishes segregate sharply from cyprinodontoid assemblages in ephemeral pools and playa lakes.
- *Cyprinodon atrorus* tolerates high temperatures and salinities in desiccating playas and ephemeral pools. It occurred alone in 14 of 64 collections where it was recorded.
- *Lucania interioris* and *Gambusia longispinis* co-occurred in 64% of their recorded collections.
- Canals were dominated by hybrid *C. atrorus* × *C. bifasciatus*, *Gambusia marshi* and *Cichlasoma minckleyi*.

**Sampling caveats.** Gill and hoop nets set overnight may have increased records of *Ictalurus lupus*, and possibly *Pylodictis olivaris* and *Lepomis megalotis*. *Pylodictis olivaris* had been caught only twice by collectors, with other records being sightings or remains. Incomplete field notes meant some records (e.g., *Cichlasoma* cf. *cyanoguttatum* in eastern canals) were excluded from analysis.

**Extinction prediction.** Minckley predicts that shrinking springheads, perhaps below about 200 m², would lose rheophiles first (*Astyanax*, *Notropis*, *Dionda*, *Etheostoma*), then larger predators, leaving cyprinodontoids. He compares this to documented faunal changes in other American desert basins.

**Hatchery evaluation (DNFH).** In the 1984–85 letter and report, Minckley recommends:
- **Personnel:** reorganize around a Hatchery Manager with designated assistant managers for production/broodstock and refuge/research. Add an office assistant, a clerk/typist and trainee positions. Create career tracks so skilled staff can advance without relocating.
- **Facilities:** build a large laboratory with variable-height work tables, a fume hood and sinks; provide individual offices and expanded library holdings in fish ecology, systematics and biology.
- **Aquarium holding facility:** build an enclosed, climate-controlled facility (15–18 °C in winter, below 25 °C in summer) with individual in- and outflows and natural lighting. Minckley argues that single-locality endemics and species critically endangered in nature need backup stocks, and that the current eight-aquaria setup is inadequate.
- **Broodstock management:** maintain large species (razorback sucker, bonytail) with substantial numbers. For razorback sucker, he suggests 500 to 1,500 wild-caught fish, with spawning limited to alternate years to preserve genetic integrity. Bonytail stock of about 1,500 fish derives from only 12 wild-caught adults, so genetic analysis and pedigree breeding are needed.
- **Cost comparison:** a one-species holding facility at a university was estimated at about $5,000 initial cost and about $2,800 per year, rising to about $4,000 for two species.
- **Security:** Pecos gambusia was apparently contaminated and replaced with Sonoran topminnow broodstock. Fish moved between ponds by gulls, ospreys and possibly people. Largemouth bass appeared in the sump, possibly from stocking. Minckley recommends pond-to-pond inspection, identification checks, netting against predators and at least two daily observer visits.
- **Research and monitoring:** prioritize propagation and rearing studies, and field studies of natural history. Form a Research Advisory Group of no more than eight technical members. Keep voucher specimens (at least 50 or more per species, at least once a year) in permanent museum collections.
- **Fisheries assistance:** split duties among field monitoring, on-site hatchery studies and liaison with state and academic programs. Field studies should not be overshadowed by administrative duties.
