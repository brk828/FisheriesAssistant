# Influence of the Vir River Valley Reservoir on the Hydrobiological and Ichthyological Conditions in the River Svratka

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 monograph from the Academy of Sciences of the Czechoslovak Republic (Acta sc. nat. Brno, 2(1)) assesses how the construction and operation of the deep [[Vir River Valley Reservoir]] on the [[Svratka River]] altered the physical, chemical and biological conditions of the river, mainly in the 25 km section below the dam. Comparisons use pre-dam data (1952–1954, 1953) and post-dam data (1960–1965). The authors conclude that the changes are overall favourable for the biocoenosis and for [[Brown Trout]] in particular, largely because the [[Auxiliary Dam]] balances discharge, moderates floods and ice formation, and stabilises temperature. Their central recommendation is that all energetic reservoirs operating at peak load should be equipped with such an auxiliary dam.

## Background & Description

The authors note that valley reservoirs have transformed most Czechoslovak rivers, that more than 80 had been built by the time of writing, and that further networks of small reservoirs were planned. Fishery interests were often neglected in water-construction planning, since energy, retention, irrigation and recreation usually took priority. The study was framed as a quasi-experiment on the section below the dam to guide practical mitigation.

The Vir Reservoir was built in 1947–1956 in the deeply set valley between Vir and Dalečín. Key characteristics are:

- Flooded area: 212 ha
- Average depth: 25 m; maximum depth at the wall: 65 m
- Power-plant outlet: 18 m above the reservoir bottom, in the hypolimnion
- Mean water retention: 120 days (theoretical 186 days)
- Minimum outflow set by project: 2.3 m³/s
- Main purpose: water supply and energy production, with a two-stage automatic balancing system (the [[Auxiliary Dam]]) below the reservoir

The study area covered the central Svratka from Krásné (km 146.5) to Stěpanovice (km 82.0). Main sampling localities included Krásné, Jimramov, Unčín, Dalečín, Vir, Korouzné, Štěpánov, Kasány, Nedvědice, Černvír and Stěpanovice. Water quality was assessed as roughly β-mesosaprobic, with pollution mainly from municipal sewage and from industry (ore mining, paper mills) upstream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Discharge and sedimentation.** Before the dam, discharge was highly unbalanced. The mean annual flow at Vir was about 3.91 m³/s, the minimum daily mean 0.51 m³/s, and the flood with a 100-year return period was 199 m³/s. After construction, high winter and spring flows were reduced and low late-summer flows were raised. Heavier, more persistent muddy sediments accumulated below the dam, while sandy and gravelly deposits prevailed above it.

**Water temperature.** Below the dam, the outflow was colder than the inflow from April to September and warmer from October to March. The annual amplitude fell from 16.4 °C in the inflow to 7.7 °C in the outflow. Maximum summer temperatures were reduced, and the thermal regime resembled that of spring-fed sections. The authors noted that the difference between the inflow and the outflow was smaller than predicted by Bratranek's (1953) relation, which expected a mean cooling of 2.5 °C. The measured difference was 4.8 °C at Dalečín–Vir, and only 0.96 °C (or 0.6 °C from Hydraulic Research Institute data) below the auxiliary dam.

**Ice.** Frost phenomena decreased, ice barriers became rare, ice cover was thinner (4–20 cm versus up to 74 cm above the dam), and bottom-ice no longer formed.

**Chemistry.** The outflow from the hypolimnion had slightly lower dissolved oxygen, BOD₅ and pH, and higher free CO₂, nitrates and some cations. Within about 2 km below the dam, most values approached their original levels through aeration and assimilation. Minimum dissolved oxygen in the outflow was 4.5 mg/l (38% saturation) in Zelinka's (1959) data, while the lowest value below the dam in later Institute data was 6.51 mg/l (57.7%).

**Bacteriology.** No striking changes were recorded in bacterial numbers. Only psychrophilous bacteria showed a decreasing trend below the dam.

**Phytobenthos.** Macrophyte and algal cover was far greater below the dam: 88% at Korouzné versus 32% at Dalečín. Bryophytes ([[Fontinalis Antipyretica]], *Amblystegium riparium*) and filamentous algae (*Oedogonium*, *Microspora*, *Spirogyra*, *Vaucheria*, *Tribonema*, later *Cladophora glomerata*) dominated below the dam.

**Zoobenthos.** Numbers and biomass were 1.8–3.5 times and 1.5–2.8 times higher, respectively, below the dam. At Korouzné the mean was 4,530 individuals/m² and 30.24 g/m², compared with 1,290 individuals/m² and 10.73 g/m² at Dalečín. Caddisfly larvae contributed most of the biomass. Thermophilous taxa declined or disappeared, while psychrophilous taxa such as [[Rhithrogena Semicolorata]] and *Gammarus pulex fossarum* became established.

**Organic drift.** A total of 1,620 specimens of about 61 taxa were collected from five stations (42 samples, 1964–1965). Crustaceans and rotifers made up 65.7% of the drift. Korouzné was the richest locality, carrying about 56% of all organisms. Downstream drift declined to 7% at Stěpanovice. Reservoir-derived plankton (for example *Daphnia hyalina*, *Eudiaptomus gracilis*) accounted for less than a third of taxa, and the authors judged most of the drift too small for early fish stages.

## Key Findings

**Ichthyofauna**

- The 1953 pre-dam survey recorded 14 species. Combined surveys (1953, 1960–1963) list 23 species belonging to 9 families.
- Several species were lost or declined, including [[Chondrostoma Nasus]] (Nase). Its share in electrofishing catches fell from 47% below the dam to 3%, and from 54% above it to about 20%. Its failure to recruit was attributed to temperature and discharge variation affecting early life stages, and to predation on eggs and fry by perch and roach above the reservoir.
- [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta m. fario*) increased substantially below the dam. It was absent from 1953 catches but made up 26% of the 1963 catch, and it became the main angling species (76.8% of catch weight).
- [[Minnow]] (*Phoxinus phoxinus*) and [[Bullhead]] (*Cottus gobio*) also increased below the dam.
- Above the reservoir, [[Roach]] (*Rutilus rutilus carpathorossicus*) reached 31% of the fish community, and perch rose from about 1% to 9%. The reservoir fish population was considered the main driver of this change.
- The percentage of phytophilous species rose above the dam, but declined below it.

**Ecological classification**

The authors used the Kryzhanovskij and Balon ecological groups. Lithophilous (rock-spawning) species dominated throughout. The percentage of lithophilous species that hide or protect their eggs increased below the dam. The authors classed brown trout and minnow as oligostenothermic and cyprinids such as chub and barbel as polystenothermic.

**Angling yield**

- Above the reservoir (fishing ground 12), the catch of Chondrostoma nasus fell by about 150 kg per year after 1960, while trout catches stayed stable and grayling catches rose.
- Below the reservoir (grounds 9–10), the total yield did not change significantly, but its composition shifted sharply toward trout. Chondrostoma nasus fell from 63.4% to 12.7% of the catch.
- Fish production below the dam remained well below the theoretical level implied by zoobenthos supply, suggesting untapped potential.

**Overall conclusions**

The authors judged that the reservoir produced favourable changes in biocoenosis, both qualitatively and quantitatively. Benefits for brown trout included greater food supply, more days in the optimal temperature range, reduced ice damage, and a longer growing season. The auxiliary dam was identified as the key mitigation structure, and the authors recommended that it be a standard component of all peak-operating energy reservoirs. Further research was recommended on the effects of sedimentation and on the growth of trout and other species.
