# American Rivers STAC Annual Meeting Memorandum and Materials (1998)

#river-conservation #watershed-management #hydropower #policy

## Executive Summary

This document is a memorandum package from [[American Rivers]] announcing the annual meeting of the Scientific Technical and Advisory Committee (STAC) scheduled for October 8, 1998, at [[Arizona State University]]. The package includes meeting agenda, discussion papers on Southwest rivers, the [[Fishable Waters Act]], criteria for [[Low Impact Hydropower]], and an Internet chat room proposal. The meeting focuses on Southwest river restoration issues, particularly addressing the [[Colorado River]], [[Gila River]], [[Rio Grande]], and smaller tributaries. Materials emphasize watershed-based approaches to river conservation, voluntary stakeholder engagement, and distinguishing environmentally preferable hydropower operations.

## Background & Description

The STAC meeting convenes conservation scientists, board members, and regional staff to discuss strategic directions for river protection and restoration in the Southwest region. The Southwest Regional Office, established in 1991, covers Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado, Utah, Nevada, and southern California—an arid region where water scarcity creates unique challenges for river conservation. The meeting agenda allocates substantial time (2.75 hours) to Southwest rivers presentation and discussion, featuring presentations from [[Jack C. Schmidt]] (Utah State University) on large river restoration and [[Julie Stromberg]] (Arizona State University) on small river restoration. Additional agenda items include the [[National River Center]], [[Fishable Waters Act]], green marketing, and website development.

Supporting materials detail ongoing projects including [[Glen Canyon Dam]] adaptive management, [[Lower Colorado River]] multi-species conservation, native fish distribution studies in the [[Gila River]] system, and [[Rio Grande]] water management addressing the endangered [[Silvery Minnow]]. Wild river protection efforts focus on [[Pinto Creek]], [[Virgin River]], [[Fossil Creek]], and Arizona Wild and Scenic Rivers designations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Southwest region presents distinctive ecological challenges due to aridity, with rivers and riparian areas constituting only a small fraction of the landscape despite their disproportionate ecological importance. Water scarcity drives intense competition among agricultural, municipal, and environmental uses. The region's rivers have experienced dramatic impacts from dams, diversions, and channelization, affecting native fish communities, riparian vegetation, and aquatic habitat.

Key ecological concerns identified include:
- **Flow regime alteration**: Dams like [[Glen Canyon Dam]] have eliminated natural flood pulses and sediment transport
- **Native fish imperilment**: Species like the [[Virgin River Spinedace]] and [[Silvery Minnow]] face extinction from habitat loss
- **Riparian degradation**: Diversion and groundwater pumping have dried stream reaches, eliminating riparian corridors
- **Water quality**: Non-point source pollution from agricultural and urban runoff affects 40% of the nation's waters
- **Habitat fragmentation**: Dams block fish passage and disconnect floodplains from river channels

The [[Fishable Waters Act]] proposal recognizes that while the 1972 [[Clean Water Act]] successfully addressed point-source pollution, it failed to address physical habitat destruction, watershed-scale processes, and biological integrity. The Southwest's unique travertine-depositing systems like [[Fossil Creek]] (43 cfs year-round flow from springs) represent rare aquatic ecosystems requiring specialized protection strategies.

## Key Findings

**Southwest River Conservation Strategy:**
- American Rivers has worked since 1991 on both "repairing big rivers" (Colorado, Gila, Rio Grande) and "protecting wild rivers" (Pinto Creek, Virgin River, Fossil Creek)
- [[Glen Canyon Dam]] adaptive management represents the first attempt to use dam operations to restore natural river processes downstream
- The [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] provides collaborative framework for riparian restoration and native species protection
- Arizona Wild and Scenic Rivers proposal encompasses 40+ rivers (233 miles formally recommended by Interior Department in 1996)

**Fishable Waters Act Proposal:**
- Addresses the failure to meet "fishable and swimmable" goals for 40% of U.S. waters after 26 years
- Proposes voluntary, incentive-based watershed approach rather than EPA regulatory enforcement
- Establishes locally-controlled Watershed Councils with state administration and federal funding through [[Department of Agriculture]]
- Targets non-point source pollution, habitat restoration, stream flows, and floodplain management
- Could restore approximately 1 million miles of rivers and streams (4 million surface acres), supporting 1.4 million anglers fishing 24 million days annually, generating $2 billion in economic activity
- Political strategy recognizes current Congress more likely to support voluntary programs than EPA regulations

**Low Impact Hydropower Certification:**
- Developed collaboratively by American Rivers and [[Green Mountain Energy Resources]] over 18 months
- Establishes six criteria: fish protection, satisfactory flows, water quality preservation, flooded habitat mitigation, cultural resource protection, and recreational opportunity
- Defers primarily to Resource Agency recommendations rather than establishing rigid standards
- Testing on 61 facilities (2,749 MW) showed 69% would pass without land mitigation requirements; 41% would pass all criteria immediately
- Certification valid one year with annual renewal process
- Provides market differentiation for environmentally preferable hydropower in restructured electricity markets
- Does not represent ceiling for mitigation efforts in relicensing proceedings

**Implementation Considerations:**
- Low Impact Hydropower administration under development, considering independent non-profit structure
- Certification process includes completeness review, public internet posting, comment period, and expert review
- Compliance monitoring includes violation reporting and potential suspension/revocation
- Criteria frozen until January 1, 2001 to provide market certainty
- Enhancement funds must include spending schedule with 75% spent within 15 years
- Buffer zone requirements (200 feet around impoundment) or equivalent habitat dedication/funding phased in by January 1, 2002

**Organizational Development:**
- Internet chat room proposal to feature STAC members discussing conservation topics monthly
- Maximum 30 participants with moderated format to maintain dialogue quality
- Companion bulletin boards for extended discussion
- Strategy positions American Rivers as leader in open communication and partnership approaches

The materials reflect a strategic shift toward collaborative, watershed-based approaches while maintaining scientific rigor through STAC expertise and agency deference in standard-setting.
