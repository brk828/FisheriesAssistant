# Winter Catch and Food of Lake Trout in Algonquin Park Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salvelinus

## Executive Summary

This Ontario Department of Lands and Forests study examines the [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush namaycush*) during the ice-covered period in several [[Algonquin Park]] lakes. It compares winter [[Ice Fishing]] catches and winter diet with summer and other seasonal conditions. Winter catches exceeded angling returns at other times of year in some lakes. The ice-fishery caught smaller fish than summer [[Trolling]] and removed a high share of immature trout. Winter diet was less varied, with many empty stomachs and low [[Plankton Feeding]], but fish prey became more important where [[Thermal Stratification]] breaks down.

## Background & Description

Knowledge of [[Lake Trout]] life history under ice cover was described as nearly absent. The [[Research Division]] of the [[Ontario Department of Lands and Forests]] studied the winter sports fishery and winter food in southern [[Algonquin Park]]. The winter fishery had grown in popularity. Most angling occurred on about a dozen lakes within a mile of the highway crossing the southern corner of the Park. Anglers also travelled from towns 70 to 80 miles away. Local residents fished a wider range of lakes, including more inaccessible waters.

The [[Ice Fishing]] method used minnow-baited lines on slender four-foot gads set at a 45-degree angle over a hole about a foot in diameter. A strike bent the tip of the pole violently.

Study lakes named in the text include [[Lake Louisa]], [[Canisbay Lake]], [[Brewer Lake]], [[Costello Lake]], and [[Lake Opeongo]] (13,400 acres). Lake sizes given for the smaller lakes are Brewer Lake (109 acres), Canisbay Lake (350 acres), and Costello Lake (96 acres).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Seasonal feeding:** Diet was split into spring (May and June), summer (July and August), fall (September and October), and winter. Summer diet in plankton-feeding lakes relied heavily on plankton. Minnows, the only forage fish in Lake Louisa, were in shallow water and largely unavailable to trout in summer.
- **Thermal barriers:** Breakdown of [[Thermal Stratification]] in fall and winter allowed trout to reach forage species that were unavailable in summer.
- **Forage species:** [[Perch]] and [[Whitefish]] were the main forage fish. [[Pumpkinseed]] were rarely taken in winter, though eaten in summer.
- **Bottom fauna:** [[Mayfly Larvae]] were the main bottom organisms in stomachs in all seasons. [[Crayfish]] were taken occasionally.
- **Zooplankton:** Data from Dr. Langford of the Ontario Fisheries Research Laboratory showed that [[Copepods]] and [[Cladocerans]] declined through winter. By March and April they were possibly only one-fifth as abundant as in October.
- **Maturity:** Canisbay trout first mature at about 12 inches, and Louisa trout at about 11 inches, both at age 5.

## Key Findings

### Winter Catch and Size Structure

- Winter catches exceeded angling returns for the rest of the year in these lakes. Estimates were: Brewer Lake about 50 trout (1950-51); Canisbay Lake about 800 (1950-51) and probably over 1,000 (1952-53); Costello Lake about 50 (1952). These are rough estimates, not accurate catch data.
- The ice-fishery took smaller fish than summer trolling:
  - **Canisbay Lake:** 60% of winter trout were 12 inches or under (fork length), versus 15% of the summer catch. Mean lengths were 11.9 inches (winter) and 14.7 inches (summer).
  - **Lake Louisa:** The 12-inch-and-under group made up 50% of the winter catch (mean 12.4 inches) and 10% of the summer catch (mean 14.5 inches).
- Brewer and Costello lakes showed a high share of small fish, though trolling data were insufficient for direct comparison. Between half and two-thirds of the winter catch in these two lakes were immature.
- **Lake Opeongo** was the exception: ice-caught fish were somewhat larger than trolled fish. The author linked this to anglers using large hooks and minnows.
- Winter fishing therefore removed a high proportion of immature stock. Summer trolling had left mature fish to spawn in lakes such as Canisbay and Louisa. The author expected winter methods to change this pattern.

### Winter Diet

- A total of **442 lake trout stomachs** came from 18 Algonquin Park lakes. Twenty were collected by [[Gill Netting]] and the rest by the winter sports fishery (winters 1948-52).
- Stomach contents across all lakes (Table I totals): empty 153; plankton 61; insect larvae 50; fish 205; bait minnows 85; crayfish 5. Bait minnows were excluded from fish percentages. Stomachs containing only bait minnows were treated as empty.
- Winter diet was less varied than at other seasons. Empty stomachs were common, which suggests lower feeding activity.
- **Plankton feeding** was low in winter. In Lake Louisa, which is predominantly plankton-feeding in summer, only one trout in nine took plankton under ice.
- **Bottom feeding** was almost negligible in three of five lakes. Insect larvae were eaten more often in winter than at other times in Lake Louisa and Canisbay Lake.
- **Fish feeding** rose sharply in lakes with fish-eating populations in summer:
  - Lake Louisa: fish made up 58% of winter stomachs versus 15% in summer, about four times as often.
  - Lake Opeongo: fish made up 61% in winter and 56% in summer. Fish-feeding continued through winter.
  - Costello Lake was the only lake where fish-feeding fell in winter.
- **Forage species shifted:** Perch were eaten about twice as often as whitefish in Opeongo trout in winter, while summer usually showed the reverse. Minnows rose sixfold in Brewer Lake trout.

### Changes Through the Winter

- Table III suggested that the frequency of food types changed through winter, not that different foods replaced each other. Empty stomachs tended to increase as winter progressed.
- **Plankton** occurrence declined. In Lake Louisa, plankton frequency peaked in early June and late October, with lows in late August and March, following the classical plankton cycle.
- **Fish** occurrence increased steadily through winter, at least until the end of March. The author linked this to decreasing plankton abundance.

### Conclusions

- Winter angling in these Algonquin Park lakes removes many immature trout, altering size structure and spawning stock.
- Winter feeding is less intensive and less varied than in other seasons. Fish prey gains importance where thermal barriers disappear.
