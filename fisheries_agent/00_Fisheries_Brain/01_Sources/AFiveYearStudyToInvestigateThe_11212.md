# Aspinall Unit Operation Effects on Endangered Fishes in the Gunnison and Colorado Rivers: FY 1993 Annual Report

#ichthyology #endangered-species #flow-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #habitat-management

## Executive Summary

This combined project report documents six interrelated studies examining the effects of [[Aspinall Unit]] reservoir operations on endangered fish populations in the [[Gunnison River]] and [[Colorado River]] systems during FY 1993. The studies focused on three federally endangered species: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Research components included ichthyofaunal surveys, larval drift sampling, nursery habitat availability assessments, radiotelemetry tracking, and geomorphological habitat quantification. The work was conducted collaboratively by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Colorado Division of Wildlife, and Utah Division of Wildlife Resources to provide biological justification for flow recommendations concerning the operation of three upstream reservoirs (Blue Mesa, Morrow Point, and Crystal) that have significantly altered streamflow and water quality in downstream reaches. Results will inform a biological opinion on Aspinall Unit operations and identify recovery actions appropriate for the Gunnison River system.

## Background & Description

The [[Aspinall Unit]] consists of three reservoirs constructed on the upper Gunnison River as part of the Colorado River Storage Project. While these reservoirs occupy portions upstream from historic habitat of endangered fishes, flow alterations and water quality changes have significantly affected downstream reaches. The 75-mile study reach of the Gunnison River extended from the confluence with the [[North Fork Gunnison River]] downstream to the [[Colorado River]] confluence, with particular focus on the section from Delta, Colorado to [[Redlands Diversion Dam]].

The fish community in the Gunnison River during 1993 comprised 21 species and three hybrid forms. Native fishes represented 77.2% of total catch (13,571 individuals), dominated by [[Bluehead Sucker]] (33.5%), [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (28.5%), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (14.7%). Non-native species included [[White Sucker]] (5.3%), [[Common Carp]] (8.0%), [[Northern Pike]] (0.08%), and various cyprinids. Hybrid crosses between white and bluehead sucker (1.8%), white and flannelmouth sucker (1.9%), and flannelmouth and bluehead sucker (0.2%) were documented. One [[Humpback Chub]] (TL=255 mm) was captured near river mile 22.0 with photographic documentation.

Larval drift sampling in the Colorado River utilized standardized dawn collections with flow-metered conical plankton nets (4m long, 0.5m mouth diameter, 10cm tail). Sampling occurred at multiple stations: upper Colorado River (RM 171.2), Loma (RM 153.7), lower Gunnison River (RM 1.8), Westwater Canyon (RM 127.5), and Moab (RM 65.4). Nursery habitat assessments focused on backwater areas, side channels, and low-velocity zones critical for young-of-year survival.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The 1992 water year represented low-flow conditions with annual mean discharge of 4,583 cfs compared to the 42-year average of 6,144 cfs at the state line gage. Spring runoff peaked at 15,800 cfs, with flows exceeding 10,000 cfs for only 18 days. In contrast, 1993 experienced prolonged high flows that delayed spawning and affected habitat availability. The most recent prior year with very high flushing flows was 1986, when peak discharge reached 32,800 cfs and flows exceeded 10,000 cfs for 119 days. These flushing flows are considered critical for channel and habitat maintenance; without periodic high flows, degradation of spawning and nursery areas is expected to negatively impact recruitment.

Water temperature plays a critical role in spawning timing. In 1992, the first [[Colorado Squawfish]] larvae were collected July 1 at Loma, with peak collections July 12-19. Extrapolation from larval sizes indicated spawning occurred primarily June 21-July 2 when flows dropped from 6,320 to 5,000 cfs and temperatures approached optimal ranges. [[Humpback Chub]] spawning in [[Westwater Canyon]] reportedly occurs at main channel temperatures of 13-23°C, with optimal hatching success at 20°C. The 1993 high flows and cooler temperatures delayed spawning substantially compared to 1992.

Habitat availability varied dramatically with flow regime. Higher 1993 flows reduced available backwater habitat to primarily large permanent areas, concentrating young fish in remaining suitable sites. Submerged vegetation, unavailable at typical sampling flows, provided important habitat when present during higher water. The geomorphological study documented that backwater and side channel morphology depends on flow-controlled scour and fill patterns, with regulated flow regimes potentially causing net filling and habitat loss over time.

## Key Findings

**Ichthyofaunal Survey (Study 19-A):**
- Ten adult [[Colorado Squawfish]] were captured and PIT-tagged in 1993: five upstream of [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] (TL: 497-847 mm) and five immediately downstream (TL: 597-901 mm)
- Two fish captured downstream in 1993 had been originally tagged in the lower Gunnison River in 1991 and 1992
- One [[Humpback Chub]] (TL=255 mm) captured near RM 22.0 with photographic documentation
- No [[Razorback Sucker]] were captured during 1992 or 1993 sampling
- Seven adult [[Colorado Squawfish]] were implanted with radio transmitters (280- or 360-day units)
- Electrofishing effort: 135.1 hours in 1992, 75.1 hours in 1993
- Young-of-year sampling in 1992 yielded 22,985 fish, 91% non-native (60% [[Fathead Minnow]], 19.5% [[Sand Shiner]], 8.5% [[Red Shiner]])

**Radiotelemetry Movement Patterns:**
- 84 individual radio contacts documented March 3 to October 21, 1993
- One fish relocated upstream to RM 14.7 remained above [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] for 78 days before moving downstream and eventually passing over the dam between July 2 and August 2; the fish survived and returned upstream from the Colorado River in late August
- Six of seven radio-tagged fish heavily utilized the reach between Little Dominguez Canyon (RM 30.0) and Peeples Orchard (RM 35.0) during late July-early August
- 39 of 84 radio contacts (46%) occurred in this 5-mile reach when water temperatures reached 19-20°C
- Five fish were within a 0.9-mile reach (RM 32.8-33.7) on July 30, suggesting potential spawning aggregation in riffle habitat with gravel/cobble substrate
- Maximum upstream movement documented to RM 48.4 in October

**Larval Production and Drift (Studies 19-B, 19-D):**
- In 1992, 52 [[Colorado Squawfish]] larvae collected at Loma (CPUE=4.3 per 12,076 m³), two from lower Gunnison (CPUE=0.00018 per 11,188 m³), none from upper Colorado River site
- At Westwater Canyon: 86 larvae (CPUE=0.91 per 87,934 m³); at Moab: 286 larvae (CPUE=6.98 per 41,416 m³)
- The 5-fold higher CPUE at Loma versus Westwater suggests limited spawning between these sites and that larvae produced above Loma do not drift as far as Westwater
- The substantial increase in CPUE at Moab strongly indicates increased spawning downstream of Westwater
- Average larval size and age similar across sites: Loma 9.4 mm (12.7 days), Westwater 8.8 mm (10.4 days), Moab 8.9 mm (10.8 days)
- Peak collections at Moab and Westwater occurred July 15-20, 1992
- Preliminary 1993 results: 39 larvae at Loma, none from other Colorado River or Gunnison River sites, reflecting delayed spawning due to high flows
- Native species dominated drift samples at Loma and Westwater; non-natives dominated at Moab

**Nursery Habitat (Studies 19-C, 19-D):**
- Upper Colorado River reach (RM 164-154) sampled intensively August and September/October 1992
- 71,440 preserved fish processed from 79 seine collections (average 940 per sample)
- Non-native fishes comprised 96% of catch: [[Fathead Minnow]] (64%), [[Red Shiner]] (22%), [[Sand Shiner]] (10%)
- Native fishes represented <2% of total: [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] accounted for 90% of natives
- Lower Colorado River (RM 20-30) sampling in 1993 yielded few young-of-year [[Colorado Squawfish]] relative to 1992, attributed to delayed spawning
- March 1993: 59 juvenile squawfish collected in lower reach (58.8% of habitats), mean length 56 mm (increased from 37 mm in September 1992)
- Consistent habitat selectivity for backwaters formed by secondary channels across all sampling periods
- October 1993: 87 squawfish collected in lower reach, including one juvenile at 165 mm
- Excellent reproductive year documented for native suckers in 1993

**Geomorphological Analysis:**
- Three backwater/side channel sites monitored through 1993 runoff: RM 162.5, RM 159.9, RM 175.5
- Six cross-sections surveyed at RM 162.5 (March 22, May 14, June 1, July 1, August 5)
- Ten cross-sections at RM 159.9 (March 23, May 25, July 2, August 6)
- Eight cross-sections at RM 175.5 (May 11, June 9, July 5, August 7)
- Main channel cross-sections surveyed every two miles throughout 15- and 18-mile reaches
- Analysis in progress to determine relationships between discharge, scour, and fill patterns
- Channel slope and substrate data will allow calculation of discharges responsible for habitat maintenance

**Humpback Chub in Westwater Canyon (Study 19-E):**
- 538 juvenile and adult *Gila* spp. collected: 321 [[Roundtail Chub]], 134 [[Humpback Chub]], 83 unidentified
- 435 chubs PIT-tagged; 80 juveniles (TL 54-155 mm) not tagged
- 126 young-of-year *Gila* spp. collected with seines in September
- 18 roundtail and three humpback chubs recaptured; most showed no net movement
- One fish moved 1.5 miles upstream; three moved approximately one mile downstream
- Growth rates varied from -9 mm to +45 mm between captures
- Scale samples collected from 80 roundtails (TL 150-384 mm) and 56 humpbacks (TL 200-334 mm) for age/growth analysis
- Age-1 *Gila* spp. (TL 62-79 mm) collected in low numbers on all four early life history trips
- Larval and young-of-year *Gila* spp. (TL 13-20 mm) identified from June 25 samples
- Electrofishing proved effective for collecting juvenile chubs in this reach
- Preliminary analysis suggests high 1993 flows were beneficial for *Gila* spp. reproduction

**Flow-Habitat Relationships:**
- 1992 represented a low-flow year following five consecutive below-average years (1988-1992)
- Lack of recent flushing flows (last major event 1986) may have caused general habitat degradation
- 1993 high flows provided flushing effect but reduced available backwater habitat area
- Spawning peaks appear associated with secondary flow spikes following main runoff recession
- Growing season length and young-of-year size at fall related to flow timing and temperature regime
- Habitat availability indices can be compared across river sections and years to assess channel condition

**Management Implications:**
- The reach between RM 30.0-35.0 on the Gunnison River identified as potentially important spawning area based on radiotelemetry aggregations during appropriate temperatures
- Additional radiotelemetry work recommended to confirm spawning locations and timing
- Larval and post-larval monitoring should continue annually to establish long-term trends
- Juvenile and adult monitoring stations should be established similar to ISMP protocols
- Electrofishing should be intensified in spring at Redlands Diversion Dam plunge pool to capture additional fish for tagging
- Flow recommendations must balance spawning/drifting flows with habitat availability determined by prior-year flushing flows
- Both factors likely important in determining year-class strength
