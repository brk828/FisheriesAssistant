# Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center: FY 1994 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This annual report from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (Dexter NFH & TC), covering Fiscal Year 1994, documents the culture, genetic management, cryopreservation, and recovery support for 14 federally listed threatened and endangered fishes of the American Southwest and northern Mexico. Dexter held a concentration of listed species that the report describes as unique in the region. Key work included record-level production of [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] spawning, unsuccessful spawning attempts with [[Warner Sucker]] and [[Yaqui Catfish]], and continued captive propagation of desert and Leon Springs pupfishes and Gila topminnow. The report also covers staffing changes, a water-rights trade, facility construction, and funding requests. It concludes that the station's listed-species work was funded entirely through the Fish Hatchery operations budget, not the Endangered Species Program, and requests additional funds.

## Background & Description

**Setting and history.** Dexter is in the Pecos River valley of southeastern New Mexico, about 20 miles southeast of Roswell and 200 miles southeast of Albuquerque. The station was authorized in 1931 to meet demand for warmwater game fish. It occupies 540 acres in the Chihuahuan Desert fringe at about 3,500 feet elevation, with roughly 12 inches of average annual rainfall and a 180 to 200 day growing season. The station was designated a Technology Center in FY 1991, one of only six in the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS).

**Culture facilities.** In FY 1994 the station had four 6 x 40 ft raceways, ten 3 x 10 ft fiberglass circular tanks, and 46 earthen ponds totaling about 22 surface acres. A [[Yaqui Catfish]] overwintering pond was lined with HYPALON. Water supply relied on shallow wells with water rights of 318 and 1,795 acre-feet per annum. The 1,000-foot artesian well failed in FY 1991 when its casing collapsed at 150 feet, and it was replaced through a trade with the Bureau of Reclamation (see Ecological & Environmental Context).

**Staffing.** The FY 1994 Technology Center had eight permanent full-time employees, led by Center Director Buddy L. Jensen. Dr. J. Holt Williamson was hired as Geneticist, reporting for duty on December 2, 1993, after a detail with FWS Region 6. Longtime employee Troy Winham retired on May 3, 1994.

**Construction.** Congress appropriated $1.4 million in FY 1993 for design, planning, and construction of a wet lab, genetics laboratory, and office space. The wet lab was completed late in FY 1993 and awarded in April 1994. Building demolition forced Technology Center staff to relocate to Quarters No. 3.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Watershed and ecosystem teams.** Dexter was assigned to the Upper/Middle [[Rio Grande Ecosystem Team]], which includes the [[Mimbres River]] in south-central New Mexico and the closed Laguna del Guzman basin in northwestern Chihuahua. The team's plan had eight strategies, with Dexter identifying action items under data acquisition (Strategy 7) and aquatic biodiversity promotion (Strategy 8). Dexter also supports recovery teams for the Apache Trout, Gila Trout, Virgin River fishes, Warner Sucker, and Upper Colorado River fishes, plus working groups for Desert and Yaqui fishes.

**Species held.** The station's Table 1 lists 14 listed fishes in five families: Catostomidae (Razorback Sucker, Warner Sucker), Cyprinidae (Colorado Squawfish, Bonytail Chub, Chihuahua Chub, Pahranagat and Virgin River roundtail chubs, Woundfin, Guzman Beautiful Shiner), Ictaluridae (Yaqui Catfish), Cyprinodontidae (Leon Springs and Desert pupfishes), and Poeciliidae (Gila Topminnow, Big Bend Gambusia). Twelve are endangered and two (the roundtail chubs' respective status as listed in the table) are threatened. Most species come from New Mexico or neighboring states and northern Mexico. The report notes that only Pahranagat roundtail chub and Warner sucker are maintained for Region 1.

**Water quality and habitat.** The report describes Dexter's water as having both shallow groundwater and artesian sources, with ponds designed to reduce water loss. Waste water is collected in sumps isolated from surface runoff. Lined ponds reduce seepage but can lower recruitment of some species.

## Key Findings

### Genetics and Cryopreservation
- **Captive breeding guidelines.** Region 2 genetic management guidelines were drafted to match those in Region 6 and will guide genetics plans for listed fishes at Dexter and other Region 2 facilities.
- **Bonytail Chub.** Staff created 28 unique family lots from paired matings of 1981 year-class Lake Mohave fish, using PIT-tagged broodstock. Most egg lots were lost shortly after arrival at Valley City NFH, North Dakota, and the work is to be repeated in FY 1995.
- **Razorback Sucker.** Dexter broodstock were used to produce young for reintroduction into Arizona waters. Sperm from about 50 Lake Mohave razorback suckers was collected and stored at [[Louisiana State University]].
- **Cryopreservation.** Dexter's program supported field collection and short-term storage of sperm and eggs for razorback sucker and bonytail. Stewardship funding of $100,000 supported big river fish cryopreservation, and another $25,000 was added at year end.
- **Allozyme and DNA surveys.** Basin-wide genetic surveys of razorback sucker (allozyme and mitochondrial DNA) and Colorado Squawfish (allozyme and nuclear DNA) were scheduled for completion or are ongoing. Results will guide lower basin management and Dexter production.
- **Genetic analyses for salmonids.** A $25,000 allozyme contract was let for Apache Trout and a $10,000 contract for Gila Trout, with additional support from the Stewardship program.

### Species Production and Status
- **Razorback Sucker.** Seventeen females were injected with HCG and 34 males were used. The report gives 424,658 eggs taken and 74,908 swimup fry produced, about 17.6 percent survival from green egg to swimup. Distribution included the CSU Larval Fish Lab, NFCRC Yankton and Columbia, Dexter's sump pond, Page Springs SFB, and Lake Havasu, among others. About 1,289 adults totaling 1,624 pounds were stocked in the Salt River.
- **Colorado Squawfish.** Four F1 domestic 1974 year-class females were spawned on June 1, 1994, using carp pituitary, and all spawned. They produced 327,233 eggs (mean about 81,808 per female) using eight males. Due to hatchery demolition, fish were spawned in outdoor raceways. Dexter currently maintains 29 F1 domestic 1974 and 1981 year-class broodfish and 894 1991 year-class future broodstock.
- **Bonytail Chub.** Spawning on April 22, 1994, used 28 females and 28 males, producing 1,245,496 eggs (average 44,482 per female). A second spawn on May 11 yielded 131,968 eggs. Swimup fry totaled 254,500. The station maintains 400 domestic F1 1981 year-class broodfish and held about 10,000 1994 young for Lake Mohave stocking.
- **Woundfin.** Two-year-old Dexter-produced F1s were stocked in a 0.20 acre pond in May 1993. Spawning baskets produced many fry. Draining in August and October 1993 recovered adults and young-of-year. Some fish were sent to Dr. Steven Vives at Georgia Southern University for experiments on temperature and photoperiod effects on spawning.
- **Chihuahua Chub.** 450 fish (5 to 6 inches) were stocked in McKnight Creek, a tributary of the Mimbres River, in October 1992. Monitoring in spring 1993 showed fish overwintered well and survived a high-magnitude flood. The captive population originated from only ten fish in 1979, and the report recommends adding wild fish to increase genetic diversity. The year ended with 101 fish.
- **Warner Sucker.** Spawning attempts in 1993 and May 1994 did not produce viable embryos. In May 1994, seven females produced about 621,282 eggs, but none were fertilized. In late September 1994, the pond was drained and no fish or remains were found, with 20 Warner suckers unaccounted for. The report suggests high summer water temperatures near 90 degrees F as a possible cause, but states that no cause was confirmed.
- **Pahranagat Roundtail Chub and Virgin River Roundtail Chub.** No intentional spawning occurred in FY 1994. The Virgin River population is recovering and may be removed from inventory in 1995.
- **Guzman Beautiful Shiner.** Maintained for refugia and eventual reintroduction into the Mimbres River. The Dexter stock was collected from the Rio Piedras Verde, a tributary of the Rio Casas Grandes, Chihuahua.

### Yaqui Catfish
- The captive stock of 201 survivors was analyzed morphometrically and electrophoretically: 152 were pure, 30 were hybrid intergrades with channel catfish, 5 were hybrid backcrosses, and 14 were undetermined.
- About 112 pure fish remained at Dexter (about 40 of them believed sexually mature) and 40 were held at Uvalde NFH, Texas. A 20 percent return to Mexico (31 fish) occurred in 1992.
- Spawning trials in 1993 and 1994, using open pond, paired pen, and raceway methods with HCG and LHRH, produced no spawns. Trials continue, and the report plans a native-habitat life history study in the upper Rio Yaqui.

### Other Species
- **Leon Springs and Desert pupfish.** Earthen pond populations were strong, with about 3,000 of each species moved to winter holding ponds. Desert pupfish are held for refugia and for reintroduction in Arizona. Leon Springs pupfish: 2,350 provided to the EPA in Gulf Breeze, Florida, and 25 to the University of Arkansas.
- **Gila Topminnow.** The Sharps Spring stock is held for reintroduction. Over 3,000 were moved to an overwintering pond, and 25 went to the University of Arkansas.
- **Big Bend Gambusia.** Did not survive well outdoors. About 3,000 spring-pond fish were stocked and about 250 were brought inside for overwintering.

### Fish Health, Cooperation, and Outreach
- Yaqui catfish carried a low incidence of yellow grub, which is removed manually. The main health concern is reintroduction of the anchor worm (*Lernaea*), mostly from wild-caught fish. The report recommends close review of all new introductions.
- Dexter worked with dozens of agencies, tribes, universities, and Mexican institutions, including Arizona Game and Fish, Colorado Division of Wildlife, and Centro Ecologico de Sonora.
- Over 2,500 people visited the hatchery, with 11 guided tours for schools and groups.

### Funding and Future Needs
- **Maintenance.** $208,013 in MMS (Maintenance Management System) funds was spent in FY 1994 on well maintenance, asbestos removal, re-roofing, UST remediation, and a raceway power upgrade.
- **Water rights.** The Bureau of Reclamation purchased 390 acre-feet of shallow water to trade for the 318 acre-feet of artesian rights, with the final usable quantity still unknown.
- **Genetics laboratory.** A proposal requested $125,000 to start a starch-gel electrophoresis lab, or $200,000 for a full laboratory. Near year's end, $30,000 was received for genetics equipment.
- **NAFTA proposal.** A plan to collect up to 200 Yaqui catfish and 100 to 200 Yaqui suckers from the Rio Bavispe, Sonora, received no FY 1995 funding but planning and permitting continue.
- **Endangered Species Program.** The report argues that since all 14 species are listed, the Endangered Species Program should fund this work, and requests a FY 1995 operations budget of roughly $47,000 plus additional sums for maintenance and construction.
