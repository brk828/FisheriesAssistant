# Genetic Variation and Evolution in New World Cichlids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Kornfield and Koehn (State University of New York, Stony Brook) uses starch-gel [[Isoenzyme Electrophoresis]] to compare [[Genetic Heterozygosity]] in three cichlids: the widespread [[Rio Grande Cichlid]] (*Cichlosoma cyanoguttatum*) from a variable river environment in Texas, and two endemic, undescribed *Cichlosoma* species from the thermally stable [[Cuatro Cienegas Basin]] in Coahuila, Mexico. The work tests whether environmental heterogeneity maintains genetic variation. The Rio Grande Cichlid was monomorphic at every locus studied, which contradicts the prediction. The endemics were variable at about 25% of loci but had very low individual heterozygosity (3.95%). The authors conclude that isoenzyme loci may not reflect whole-genome evolution, and that speciation in the endemics probably involved a restricted portion of the genome.

## Background & Description

The study is framed by the rapid "explosive speciation" of cichlids in the [[African Great Lakes]], such as [[Lake Victoria]] flocks. The Cuatro Cienegas endemics show a smaller-scale parallel: morphological divergence, endemism and sympatry within a limited area.

- **Rio Grande Cichlid (*C. cyanoguttatum*):** Occurs throughout the major rivers of Texas and northern Mexico. The authors consider it the presumed ancestor of all three Cuatro Cienegas endemics.
- **Algae-eating endemic:** The most abundant and widely distributed endemic. It has very fine [[Pharyngeal Dentition]] and light supporting musculature, and feeds mainly on vegetation and algae.
- **Snail-eating endemic:** Less abundant and more restricted. It has thick, heavy pharyngeal teeth for feeding on snails.
- **Third endemic:** Very restricted and presumed predatory. It has an elongated body and strong jaws. Only the two common forms were electrophoretically studied.

Externally, the two common endemics are indistinguishable, but their pharyngeal teeth and cranial morphology differ (Plate 1).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime.** Temperature in the Cuatro Cienegas lagunas is very stable because of constant circulation from underground thermal springs.
- At Laguna West Mojarral, 22 measurements over ten years ranged from 33.5°C to 34.0°C (Arnold, 1972). Maximum observed annual surface variation was 5.0°C.
- Taylor (1966) reported annual ranges of 0°C to less than 3.0°C for three additional lagunas.
- When surface or marshy-edge conditions become suboptimal, fish move deeper toward the water inflow.

**Rio Grande.** Water temperature near Mission, Texas ranges seasonally from 14.2°C (Hubbs, 1951) to over 25°C. Periodic flooding is expected to alter nutrient content and suspended matter.

**Sampling localities (Appendix).** Four Mexican localities were sampled. They are shallow to moderately deep ponds and lagunas, from a 5-meter-diameter pond to a pond with maximum depth of 5 meters. Sample sizes were 32, 90, 29 and 7 (N = 158, matching the 114 algae-eating and 44 snail-eating specimens collected). The authors estimated that a laguna holds roughly 1,000 individuals at most, based on subjective underwater observation.

**Theoretical basis.** The study draws on the hypothesis that environmental heterogeneity maintains genetic variability. Supporting evidence came from [[Drosophila willistoni]] (Powell, 1971), marine bivalves (Koehn and Mitton, 1972; Levinton, 1973), and [[Deep-Sea Organisms]] (Gooch and Schopf, 1972), which showed a negative correlation.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Forty *C. cyanoguttatum* were collected in October 1971 from the Rio Grande near Mission, Texas, and shipped live to New York.
- The endemics were collected by hook and line in July 1971 from four localities in the Cuatro Cienegas Basin.
- Thirty-eight *C. cyanoguttatum* were analyzed for each enzyme.
- Liver and muscle tissue were analyzed by horizontal starch-gel electrophoresis, following [[Koehn and Rasmussen Method]] and the buffer and stain references in Table 1.
- Ten enzyme systems were examined, including [[Lactate Dehydrogenase]], esterase, [[Malate Dehydrogenase]], [[Glutamate Oxaloacetate Transaminase]] and phosphoglucomutase.

**Locus and variability results**
- A minimum of 18 electrophoretic loci were studied across the three species.
- Only [[Lactate Dehydrogenase]] (LDH) clearly followed a simple two-allele genetic model. Other systems were treated as variable or invariant on the basis of band patterns, and the total locus count was uncertain (Table 2).
- *C. cyanoguttatum* was monomorphic at all loci in 38 individuals. Its heterozygosity was 0.00% per individual.
- The endemics (N = 130 sampled from several localities) were polymorphic at 0.250 to 0.307 of loci, depending on the liberal or conservative enumeration used.
- Heterozygosity per individual was 3.95% in the endemics.
- No electrophoretic differences were found among localities, so individuals were pooled.

**Interspecific comparison**
- Genetic identity between *C. cyanoguttatum* and the endemics was a Jaccard coefficient of 0.44. The authors describe this as within the range reported for sibling species pairs.
- The two endemics showed identical electrophoretic phenotypes at all studied loci. The authors note that this sharply contrasts with other species comparisons and raises questions about their specific status.

**Evidence for species status**
- No hybrids were observed in no-choice laboratory mating experiments.
- Over 3,000 field specimens showed no intermediate dentition.
- Cranial morphology differs in line with pharyngeal dentition.
- Two non-pharyngeal characters, pectoral ray length and caudal peduncle length, differed significantly between the endemics.
- Congeneric species in African cichlid flocks show a similar pattern of morphological differentiation.
- Comparison with [[Astatotilapia Alluaudi]] (Greenwood, 1965b) showed that dentition in that species is influenced by diet, whereas dentition in the Mexican endemics was not changed by diet swaps.

**Interpretation and conclusions**
- The authors consider the homozygosity of the endemics consistent with a fairly constant environment. They find it hard to explain the monomorphism of the Rio Grande Cichlid, which lives in a variable environment.
- Founder effects, inbreeding and [[Genetic Drift]] were considered. The authors judged them inadequate because identical alleles at all loci in both endemics would be improbable under drift, and because no inter-population differences were noted. Drift remains possible for *C. cyanoguttatum* if its effective population size is much smaller than assumed.
- The central conclusion is that isoenzyme loci may not reflect whole-genome evolution. Speciation in the endemics apparently involved reorganization of a restricted part of the genome, possibly loci affecting mating behavior, rather than random genetic reorganization.
- The authors state that either population geneticists have not yet identified the factors controlling overall heterozygosity, or generalizations about heterozygosity cannot be made.

**Note on data:** The text gives the endemic heterozygosity as "-3.95" in the Results and "3.95%" in the Summary. This note reports 3.95%.
