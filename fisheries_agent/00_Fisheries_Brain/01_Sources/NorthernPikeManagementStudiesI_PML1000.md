# Northern Pike Management Studies in the Yampa River, Colorado, 1999-2002

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This final report from the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at [[Colorado State University]] evaluates mechanical removal of nonnative [[Northern Pike]] from the [[Yampa River]], northwestern Colorado, for the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] (Project No. 98a). Northern pike, first detected in the river in the late 1970s, are considered a predatory threat to the federally endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], which has [[Critical Habitat]] in the lower half of the river.

Removal targeted about 75 miles of the river within critical habitat, with three objectives: (1) remove juvenile and adult northern pike from critical habitat; (2) relocate removed fish to isolated ponds or reservoirs that conform to [[Nonnative Fish Stocking Procedures]] and are accessible to anglers; and (3) determine removal effectiveness. The authors state that all three objectives were met.

Sampling from 1999 to 2002 used [[Electrofishing]], [[Fyke Net]] and seine gear, and [[Block-And-Shock Sampling]] in backwaters. Over the four years, 1,042 northern pike were removed and translocated to [[Yampa State Wildlife Area]] ponds or [[Rio Blanco Reservoir]]. Removal reduced pike numbers overall, but effects varied by reach. Predation attempts on Colorado pikeminnow were documented, and injury rates increased each year.

## Background & Description

Northern pike are a circumpolar coolwater species that the report describes as widely introduced as a gamefish outside their native North American range. Colorado introductions began in 1874 on the eastern plains, with later stockings in northeastern reservoirs and in western reservoirs in the 1960s.

Northern pike were ranked among the six nonnative piscivores of greatest concern in the Upper Colorado River Basin (Hawkins and Nesler 1991). The [[Yampa Aquatic Plan]] (CDOW 1998) recommended trapping and translocation of northern pike, [[Smallmouth Bass]], channel catfish, and white sucker from critical habitat, while still providing angling opportunities.

The origin of Yampa River pike is unresolved. The first documented capture was a 670 mm total length fish caught at Lily Park in October 1979, estimated at six or seven years old. [[Elkhead Reservoir]] was widely considered the original source, but the authors judged the stocking records (571 fish, 50-100 mm, in 1977) inconsistent with the 1979 capture. Pike were first observed in [[Catamount Reservoir]] in 1995. Their presence there is attributed to escapement from [[Stagecoach Reservoir]], where pike were established by 1994. A fall 1992 drawdown of Elkhead Reservoir, which released gamefish including pike and smallmouth bass into Elkhead Creek and the Yampa River, is proposed as the likely cause of a major increase in river numbers.

The study area included a presumed spawning reach near Hayden and four reaches (Hayden, Juniper, Maybell, Lily Park). The river drains 3,410 square miles, averages 1.2 million acre-feet of discharge annually, and is snowmelt-driven with spring peak flows.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Critical habitat reaches are separated by high-gradient canyons, and the reaches differ in character:

- [[Juniper Reach]]: about 30 miles, from Milk Creek to the Maybell Diversion Dam. Its upper 17 miles run through [[Little Yampa Canyon]].
- [[Maybell Reach]]: about 28 miles, low-gradient and irrigated, passing the town of Maybell.
- [[Lily Park Reach]]: about 10 miles, from Cross Mountain Canyon to Yampa Canyon. The [[Little Snake River]] adds fine sand to its lower 5 miles.
- [[Hayden Reach]]: 20 miles, sampled only in 1999.

Northern pike concentrated in two short areas immediately downstream of canyons (below Juniper Canyon and Cross Mountain Canyon), as well as in spring backwaters. These concentration areas acted as sinks that were recolonized between sampling trips. Backwaters are also valuable to Colorado pikeminnow, which use them in spring because of warm water and abundant small prey. Pike densities were high in these habitats, and Colorado pikeminnow were often absent or scarce where pike were numerous.

Pike predation on native fishes was documented. Prey removed from pike included [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], rainbow trout, white sucker, northern pike, and black bullhead. Most prey were partly digested, suggesting the predation was not recent.

## Key Findings

**Removal totals and effort**
- 1,042 northern pike were removed over four years and translocated to Yampa State Wildlife Area ponds or Rio Blanco Reservoir (White River drainage).
- In the Hayden Reach, 114 northern pike (biomass 184.4 kg) were captured; 72 were released at capture site, 19 were translocated, 4 died during holding, and 19 young-of-year were preserved as voucher specimens.
- Across critical habitat from 1999 to 2002, 1,000 northern pike were removed: 956 translocated and 44 (4%) died in transport or were euthanized for severe injury. Total removal electrofishing effort was 405 hours.
- Electrofishing in the main channel captured 67% of all pike removed, and backwater gears captured 32%. Block-and-seine and block-and-shock accounted for 21%, and fyke and trammel nets for 11%.
- Removed biomass by reach: Juniper 510.9 kg, Maybell 480.? kg (partly illegible in scan), and Lily Park 295.5 kg. Lily Park's per-mile biomass (29 kg/mile) was almost double Juniper's (17 kg/mile).
- Pike over 600 mm TL made up 38% of fish removed and 65% of removed biomass.

**Removal effectiveness (EL-CPUE)**
- Mean shoreline electrofishing CPUE declined 73% for pike of all sizes and 67% for pike over 600 mm between 2000 and 2001.
- From 2001 to 2002, CPUE continued to decline at Lily Park but increased in Juniper and Maybell. In Juniper, CPUE for pike of all sizes rose to 73% of its 2000 level, and CPUE for pike over 600 mm rose above its 2000 starting value.
- Regression slopes were least negative at Juniper, moderate at Maybell, and steepest at Lily Park. Only the Lily Park decline for all sizes was statistically significant (P=0.01); the authors attribute the weak power to only three years of data.
- Starting CPUE in 2000 was 10 fish/hr at Lily Park, 5 at Maybell, and 3 at Juniper.
- Backwater block-and-shock CPUE fell from 22.5 fish/hr in 2000 to 12.9 fish/hr in 2001 (over 50%). Fyke-net CPUE fell from 0.13 to 0.05 fish/hr.

**Abundance estimate**
- In 2000, the population was estimated at 1,277 northern pike, based on 11 recaptures of 83 marked fish (capture probability 13%). The confidence interval is partly illegible in the scan.
- From 2000 to 2002, 928 pike were removed, about 73% of the estimated population.

**Size structure and recruitment**
- Total lengths ranged from 28 to 953 mm in the Hayden Reach and 110 to 1,015 mm in critical habitat.
- Using a length-at-age relationship from another researcher's aged cleithra, pike averaged 419 mm at age 2, 520 mm at age 3, 591 mm at age 4, and 647 mm at age 5.
- Strong cohorts entered critical habitat. The 2001 cohort reached over 600 mm in 2002 and drove the increased catch rates over 600 mm in Juniper and Maybell.
- Of 51 pike under 350 mm captured in critical habitat, 80% came from Juniper, 20% from Maybell, and none from Lily Park. Of 195 pike of 350-450 mm, 75% came from Juniper, 21% from Maybell, and 4% from Lily Park.
- Spawning was confirmed in the Hayden Reach by young-of-year pike (28-70 mm TL) collected on June 4 and 11, 1999. Spawning within critical habitat was judged absent or negligible.

**Colorado Pikeminnow predation**
- Pike-attack injuries were documented on Colorado pikeminnow, with the annual injury rate rising from 9% in 1999 to 26% in 2002.
- By the end of 2002, 18% (n = 39) of the 221 unique Colorado pikeminnow handled had accumulated pike-attack injuries.
- Injured fish ranged from 468 to 647 mm TL. Cumulative injury rates were 20% at Juniper, 15% at Maybell, and 9% at Lily Park, and were positively correlated with the number of pike captured in each reach.
- The largest prey removed from a live pike was 457 mm TL, 72% of the pike's 635 mm length. Prey averaged 54% of predator length (range 42-72%).
- Based on the 72% ceiling, the authors estimate pike of 600 mm TL could prey on 425 mm Colorado pikeminnow, the size at which they typically recruit into the Yampa River above Yampa Canyon.

**Conclusions**
- All three objectives were accomplished. Removal was most successful at Lily Park, the reach farthest from upstream sources of reproduction and recruits. Removal was least effective at Maybell and Juniper, close to sources of immigrating and recruiting fish.
- Northern pike outnumbered Colorado pikeminnow in the river and in several backwaters.
- Changes in length-frequency over time were attributed to growth of strong cohorts, not to removal.

**Recommendations**
- Increase annual removal in critical habitat, including more riverwide occasions, more samples in high-density areas, and removal in other seasons.
- Reduce immigration by extending juvenile and adult removal upstream of critical habitat, and tag pike in [[Elkhead Reservoir]] and Catamount Reservoir to measure adult escapement.
- Reduce recruitment by mapping young-of-year pike to locate high-production spawning sites, then block their access or eliminate young there.
- Identify reservoir and riverine conditions linked to strong cohorts, conduct periodic abundance estimates using tagging and multiple recaptures ([[Capture-Recapture Abundance Estimation]]), and develop bioenergetic models to set the removal level needed to benefit native and endangered fishes.
