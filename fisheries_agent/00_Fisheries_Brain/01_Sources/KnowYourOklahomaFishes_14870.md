# Know Your Oklahoma: Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #oklahoma-fishes-guide

## Executive Summary

This Oklahoma Game and Fish Department booklet, written by H. C. Ward with illustrations by Wallace Hughes and edited by Juanita Mahaffey, is an educational field guide to the state's freshwater fishes. It covers 16 of the 23 known fish families and about one-third of the more than 150 species recorded in Oklahoma. Each species account describes size, range, habitat, food, spawning and angling or economic value, with identification diagrams. The booklet also explains scientific naming and warns against confusing common names, especially the use of "perch" for sunfishes.

## Background & Description

The booklet was prepared for free distribution to sportsmen, State Game and Fish Department field personnel, and elementary and high school groups. It is funded by hunting and fishing license fees. The introduction notes that seven families were omitted for brevity: [[Salmon Family]] (Salmonidae), Pike (Esocidae), Topminnow (Cyprinodontidae), Pirate-perch (Aphredoderidae), Perch (Percidae), Sculpin (Cottidae) and Sheepshead (Sparidae).

The guide uses standard binomial nomenclature, with family names ending in *-idae* and an italicized scientific name following each accepted common name. It cautions that many anglers call various sunfishes "perch," which is misleading because true perch belong to the Perch Family (Percidae). That family includes yellow perch, walleye, logperch and darters.

The booklet includes a glossary defining terms such as adipose fin, barbels, heterocercal tail, keeled belly, lateral line, operculum, plankton and serrated ray. It also lists reference works, including Bailey's *A List of Common and Scientific Names of the Better Known Fishes of the United States and Canada*, Carlander's *Handbook of Freshwater Fishery Biology*, Forbes and Richardson's *The Fishes of Illinois*, and Hubbs and Lagler's *Fishes of the Great Lakes Region*.

Species accounts for roughly 48 species are organized by family, with the following families covered:

- **Lampreys** (Petromyzontidae): [[Chestnut Lamprey]] and Southern brook lamprey
- **Paddlefish** (Polyodontidae): [[Paddlefish]]
- **Sturgeons** (Acipenseridae): [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]]
- **Gars** (Lepisosteidae): four species, including [[Longnose Gar]] and [[Alligator Gar]]
- **Bowfin** (Amiidae): [[Bowfin]]
- **Mooneyes** (Hiodontidae): [[Goldeye]]
- **Herrings** (Clupeidae): [[Gizzard Shad]]
- **Suckers** (Catostomidae): eleven species, including [[Bigmouth Buffalo]]
- **Minnows** (Cyprinidae): five featured species, including [[Common Carp]] and [[Golden Shiner]]
- **Catfishes** (Ictaluridae): five species, including [[Blue Catfish]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Flathead Catfish]]
- **Eels** (Anguillidae): [[American Eel]]
- **Livebearers** (Poeciliidae): [[Mosquitofish]]
- **Bass Family** (Serranidae): [[White Bass]]
- **Sunfishes** (Centrarchidae): eleven species, including [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]
- **Silversides** (Atherinidae): Brook silversides
- **Drums** (Sciaenidae): [[Freshwater Drum]]

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Oklahoma's fish fauna reflects a mix of native species and widespread introductions. Several species are documented as abundant in large reservoirs, including [[Lake Texoma]], Grand Lake, Wister Reservoir and Great Salt Plains Reservoir. The booklet links the success of several species, including [[White Bass]], to the construction of these impoundments.

Feeding ecology runs through many accounts. Lampreys filter organic material from mud as larvae and become parasitic (chestnut lamprey) or non-feeding (brook lamprey) as adults. Paddlefish strain [[Plankton]] and insect larvae through their gill filaments. Suckers feed on bottom organisms, and gizzard shad graze mud-bottom material that converts to flesh of game fishes. Crayfish, mollusks and insect larvae are important prey for many species.

Several fish are described as "living fossils" because their lineages are very old. Gars and the bowfin are the main examples, and the booklet uses this to argue that gars may help maintain ecological balance in larger lakes by preventing any single species from dominating.

Habitat preferences are summarized for many species: clear, rocky streams (bluntnose minnow, hog sucker, rock bass), warm weedy lakes (gars, bowfin, largemouth bass, warmouth), deep river channels (blue catfish, white bass) and murky farm ponds (bullheads, red shiner, fathead minnow).

## Key Findings

**Taxonomy and identification**
- The booklet recognizes 23 known fish families and more than 150 species in Oklahoma. It covers 16 families and about one-third of the species.
- Lampreys are placed in a separate class (Monorhina) from true fishes. They lack jaws and paired fins and have seven external gill openings on each side.
- The paddlefish is "now considered to be one of the most primitive" of Oklahoma's fishes, although it was once placed with sharks.
- Carp are described as having two pairs of jaw barbels, and the minnow family includes over 40 species in the state.

**Species-level facts**
- **Paddlefish:** Specimens have been reported to six feet, but are not ordinarily taken heavier than 50 pounds. It has no teeth.
- **Shovelnose sturgeon:** Commonly does not exceed five pounds, compared with Russian sturgeon up to 3,000 pounds.
- **Alligator gar:** The largest gar. A specimen from Deep Fork River weighed 185 pounds and measured seven feet two inches.
- **Bowfin:** A 28-inch female can produce approximately 55,000 eggs. The male builds the nest and guards eggs and fry until the young reach about four inches.
- **Goldeye:** Seldom exceeds 12 inches. Canadian goldeye are described as a near delicacy when smoked.
- **Gizzard shad:** A few Lake Overholser and Great Salt Plains specimens reached 18 inches and three pounds. The booklet describes the species as "probably the most valuable in our lakes" because it feeds sport fishes.
- **Bigmouth buffalo:** Can reach 2.5 feet and 20 pounds or more.
- **Hog sucker:** Maximum length approximately two feet. Its eye sits far behind the middle of the head.
- **Carp:** Introduced to the United States in 1877, when R. Hessel of the United States Fish Commission brought 345 carp. In 1879 more than 12,000 young were distributed. Distribution was discontinued in 1897. A single female of three to five pounds may deposit 300,000 to 700,000 eggs in a season.
- **Golden shiner:** Life span of several years, sometimes five or six. It is used as a forage species for bass fingerlings.
- **Fathead minnow:** Average adult length about three inches, lifespan under two years. It is used almost exclusively in several State hatcheries as forage for game fish culture.
- **Bluntnose minnow:** A female can produce approximately 2,000 eggs per season.
- **Blue catfish:** Known to reach about 150 pounds, with an average of about 20 pounds. It is abundant in Lake Texoma.
- **Channel catfish:** Can reach 20 to 25 pounds, but the average is usually five pounds or less. Spawning eggs average around 10,000.
- **Flathead catfish:** Frequently reaches 50 to 75 pounds, with reports up to 100 pounds.
- **Black vs. yellow bullhead:** Black bullhead has 17 to 19 anal fin rays and black chin barbels. Yellow bullhead has 26 anal fin rays and white or cream barbels.
- **American eel:** Seldom exceeds four feet or five to six pounds elsewhere. Spawning occurs in the Atlantic, and adults die after spawning. Eels in large Oklahoma reservoirs were trapped at impoundment and cannot migrate to spawn.
- **Mosquitofish:** Bears live young. It is known for feeding on mosquito larvae, but commercial minnow producers often oppose its presence in culture ponds.
- **White bass:** Not known to live longer than four years in Oklahoma. Record size is 4 3/8 pounds and about 18 inches total length (the source's fraction is OCR-ambiguous). A single female may produce 600,000 to one million eggs.
- **Largemouth bass:** Maximum size probably around 10 pounds, average about two pounds. A one-pound female is expected to spawn approximately 15,000 eggs.
- **Smallmouth bass:** Oklahoma specimens seldom weigh more than two pounds. A one-pound female is expected to spawn approximately 7,000 eggs.
- **Spotted bass:** Reaches a maximum of about five pounds.
- **Bluegill:** Occasionally reaches 12 inches. A 5.5-inch female can spawn approximately 10,000 eggs.
- **Orangespotted sunfish:** A 4.25-inch female can spawn approximately 5,000 eggs.
- **Rock bass:** A one-half pound female can spawn approximately 4,000 eggs.
- **White crappie:** Maximum about three pounds. A 7.5-inch female can spawn approximately 14,000 eggs.
- **Freshwater drum:** Usual weights range from one to ten pounds, with past records up to 100 pounds. Its grunting is produced by vibrations of the air bladder wall.

**Management and angling**
- The booklet discusses gar control. It states "we have no definite evidence that gars should be eradicated," and notes there is no adequate method for doing so even if such proof existed.
- The guide recommends following accepted common names, because local names cause confusion among laymen.
- It notes that bullheads are often considered a nuisance in small farm ponds, but may be valuable in small murky ponds that do not support other species.
- Brush shelters are introduced into lakes to increase crappie harvest.
- Several sunfish, including the redear sunfish, were introduced from Texas and are widely distributed through State and Federal hatchery plantings.
