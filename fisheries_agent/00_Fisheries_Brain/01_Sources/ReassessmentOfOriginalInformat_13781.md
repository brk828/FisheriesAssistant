# San Pedro River Native Fishes and Water-Rights Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of related items concerning native fish management and a federal water-rights claim on the [[San Pedro River]] in southeastern Arizona. It contains:

- A 4 May 1990 memorandum from [[Minckley]] (Arizona State University, Fisheries Management) to [[William Jackson]], San Pedro ISF Quantification Team Leader, reassessing the fish-related water needs of the river.
- A 24 November 1989 [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) purchase order (AZ950-PHO-144, $2,500.00) retaining Minckley as a fishery expert.
- A draft project outline for a BLM expert assessment supporting a federal reserved water right for the [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]] (SPRNCA).
- Standard federal contract clauses (drug-free workplace certification and Federal Acquisition Regulation terms).

The central argument is that the river's annual water yield is nearly unchanged from historical levels, so the main problem for native fishes is the seasonal distribution of that water. Spring and autumn drying kills fish, and the memo recommends a natural watershed and floodplain repair process as the cheapest and most effective way to restore flows.

## Background & Description

The memo reviews Minckley's original 1987 report on fishes and aquatic habitats of the upper San Pedro system, and the [[Jackson et al. 1987 Assessment]] of water conditions. Minckley reports no corrections to either document. He adds new information on several species and on recovery planning.

The San Pedro fauna comprises 13 native species known to have lived in the river's drainage. Six are federally listed or soon to be listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]], including [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Sonoran Topminnow]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Among the remaining five, [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Gila Chub]] are state-listed and federal candidates, and the others remain locally common within their natural ranges.

The project outline states that the BLM San Pedro Study Team's May 1987 Project Completion Report is the basis for a BLM claim to a portion of the river's natural flow reserved for the SPRNCA, created by the Idaho-Arizona Conservation Act of 1988 (name as given in the document). The outline's purpose is to test the accuracy of that report for the federal reserved right.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime.** The memo states that, unlike most aquatic systems in the American Southwest, the San Pedro has high potential for recovery because its annual water yield is essentially identical to historical levels. The problem is timing: most flow occurs in winter and summer floods, while much of the channel dries in spring and autumn. Minckley and Meffe (1987) showed native fishes are scarcely influenced by flooding, and non-native competitors and predators tend to benefit from it. Drought, by contrast, is the critical threat: fish cannot survive desiccation, so an unchanged average flow offers little benefit.

**Watershed storage.** The preferred management option is to encourage natural repair of the watershed and [[Floodplain]] so that it stores flood discharge and releases it during low-runoff periods. The memo notes that streams rarely cut below the [[Water Table]], and that channels aggrade quickly after floods where the water table is high. Restoring a sufficiently high water table would allow a reversed gradient during drought, keeping surface flow.

**Habitat categories.** [[Jackson et al. 1987 Assessment]] grouped San Pedro fishes into four categories: (1) large, eroding rivers and associated floodplain habitats; (2) perennial streams of variable erosiveness; (3) spring-fed or river-associated habitats; and (4) ubiquitous species, including those of intermittent streams. Only category 4 persists in the mainstream river today, although fishes from categories 2 and 3 remain in the drainage or are available for reintroduction. Category 1 fishes would be hardest to restore without direct habitat reconstruction.

**Non-native species.** After permanent, natural water is secured, the memo calls managing against [[Non-Native Species]] the next major challenge.

**Refugia.** [[Aravaipa Creek]], on [[Bureau of Land Management]] and [[The Nature Conservancy]] (TNC) lands, protects remnant populations of spikedace and loach minnow. TNC also owns water areas in the basin, including Muleshoe Ranch, Canelo Cienega, and Binghamton Swamp, which protect Gila chub, speckled and longfin dace, and desert and Sonoran suckers.

## Key Findings

1. **Water yield is intact; timing is the problem.** Essentially the same volume of water flows down the San Pedro each year as in the historical past, but most of it arrives in winter and summer floods, leaving the channel dry in spring and autumn.

2. **Drought kills fish.** Average flows that match historical levels do not help fish facing desiccation. Discharge should be managed to prevent drying.

3. **Natural watershed repair is the preferred path.** Restoring floodplain and watershed storage so that high flows are retained and released in dry periods is described as the most desirable and least expensive management option, avoiding dams, canals, and water purchases.

4. **Recovery depends on the San Pedro.** Draft recovery plans for spikedace, loach minnow, desert pupfish, and Sonoran topminnow all rely heavily on the SPA as a reintroduction site. Recovery plans for Colorado squawfish also include reintroduction. The razorback sucker has been proposed for listing, and reintroduction is under discussion.

5. **Genetic management is underway.** Genetic studies are in progress for desert pupfish and Sonoran topminnow to ensure that reintroduced stocks closely match the originals and retain sufficient genetic diversity to maximize their chances of re-establishment.

6. **Ongoing agency commitment.** The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] have ongoing reintroduction projects for razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish, and the AGFD has a consistent commitment to maintaining native fishes in the system.

7. **Water needed for fish is the historical amount, distributed better.** The memo concludes that the water required to enhance and re-establish the native fauna is the amount present historically, or somewhat more, distributed more equitably across seasons.

8. **Expert quantification for litigation.** The draft project outline sets a schedule for BLM's expert team to validate the claim: initial meeting (December 4), literature update (December 11), evaluation of BLM groundwater and streamflow data and the "Rovey model" (January 30), a resource addendum to the 1987 report (February 16), and a consolidated report to BLM (March 1, 1990). The report is to go to the Department of Justice for the General Adjudication of the Gila River System.

9. **Team composition.** The designated experts are Hydrologist William Jackson (project leader), Riparian Specialist Paul Cuplin, Fisheries Biologist W.L. Minckley, Recreation Sociologist Bo Shelby, and Hydrogeologist Paul Summers.

10. **Procurement.** BLM Purchase Order AZ950-PHO-144 (24 November 1989) procured $2,500.00 in fishery expert services from Minckley, with delivery and payment at destination, net 30 days. The order is supported by a telephone quote of 21 November 1989. The remaining pages are standard federal contract clauses, including drug-free workplace certification and FAR terms, and contain no further technical findings.
