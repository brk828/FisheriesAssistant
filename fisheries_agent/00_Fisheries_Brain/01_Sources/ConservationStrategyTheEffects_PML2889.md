# Conservation Strategy: The Effects of Fragmentation on Extinction

#conservation-biology #population-ecology #lepidoptera

## Executive Summary

This conservation-biology paper, authored by Bruce A. Wilcox and Dennis D. Murphy, argues that [[Habitat Fragmentation]] is a serious threat to biological diversity and should be a central consideration in reserve design. The authors challenge the view, associated with the [[SLOSS]] debate ("single large or several small" reserves), that fragmentation is largely innocuous to most species. They argue that SLOSS is only a special case of fragmentation, that the population model used in the critiqued analysis ([[Simberloff and Abele]], 1982) is inadequate, and that single-species models ignore community-level effects. The paper is a theoretical and conceptual review rather than a field study, and its main supporting evidence comes from the [[Edith's Checkerspot Butterfly]] and from published mammal and community studies.

## Background & Description

The paper responds to a recurring question in applied population biology: whether one large reserve or several small reserves protect more species. [[Simberloff and Abele]] (1982) approached this by examining how subdividing a population affects its probability of extinction, and concluded that several small reserves are not necessarily more prone to extinction than one large reserve, and that fragmenting a single large reserve should not affect extinction rates.

Wilcox and Murphy state that their purpose is not to re-examine SLOSS per se, but to challenge the implication that fragmentation is innocuous and need not be considered in reserve design. They identify three sources of the apparent contradiction with the prevailing view:

1. The SLOSS problem is not equivalent to, or is at best a special case of, habitat fragmentation.
2. The population model used by Simberloff and Abele ignores key factors affecting population survival.
3. Their treatment is limited to single-species phenomena and ignores interspecific interactions and community-level processes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fragmentation components.** Expanding land use associated with human population growth fragments natural habitat. As fragmentation proceeds, average fragment size and total fragment area decline and fragment insularity increases. Fragmentation therefore has two components, habitat loss and insularization, both of which reduce biological diversity.

**Temporal collapse.** Studies of land-bridge island faunas ([[Land-Bridge Islands]]) indicate that biotic collapse after isolation can range from virtually instantaneous to a long period, depending on island size and taxon. The exponent of the upper timescale is garbled in the scan. The authors argue that the conservation problem posed by fragmentation is understanding this collapse process, whereas the SLOSS problem concerns which of two equal-area reserve configurations supports more species after collapse. SLOSS is therefore a special case of fragmentation.

**Static versus dynamic perspective.** The SLOSS framework assumes biotas on habitat islands are at equilibrium. A valid comparison requires that the biotas are not supersaturated from prior fragmentation and will not experience further fragmentation through conversion of surrounding habitat.

**Reduction in total area.** The authors note that predictions about fragmenting intact habitat are misleading because they ignore the overall reduction in population size caused by habitat loss. They quote [[Simberloff and Abele]] (1984) to the effect that "subdividing an existing refuge by cutting swaths out of it simultaneously decreases its area."

**Population extinction mechanisms.** Extinction can occur through four basic processes: [[Demographic Stochasticity]], environmental variation, [[Genetic Stochasticity]], and natural catastrophes (Shaffer 1981). The [[Richter-Dyn and Goel Model]] (RDG) links the ratio of per capita birth and death rates (Vp) to time to extinction.

## Key Findings

**Critique of the RDG-based SLOSS analysis**
- The RDG model suggests that when Vp exceeds about 1.5, a critical population size (N_c) of more than 20 allows a population to persist for a very long time.
- Simberloff and Abele used this result to argue that subdividing a refuge would not greatly increase extinction risk if at least one subrefuge exceeded N_c.
- The authors contend that demographic stochasticity is mainly responsible for extinction only in very small populations. Environmental variation is the main cause in larger populations, so survival time above about N = 20 is, in effect, meaningless from a practical standpoint.
- RDG alone is insufficient for assessing the effects of reduced population size on extinction probability.
- [[Shaffer and Sampson]] (1985) presented empirical evidence that the Vp and N_c values may not apply even to some non-subdivided populations.
- The RDG application assumed that each subpopulation survives independently. Richter-Dyn and Goel (1972) later extended the model to include migration between subpopulations, and Simberloff and Abele noted that migration could raise extinction probability when a population is subdivided. However, Richter-Dyn and Goel's extended model does not constrain the total habitat area, so it does not realistically describe alternative configuration strategies under a fixed habitat area.

**Wright and Hubbell model**
- Only [[Wright and Hubbell]] (1983) explicitly address the fixed-area problem. Their model shows that a single large area supports more species than two smaller areas of the same total size, with or without migration.
- The model avoids uncertainty about the value of Vp and is supported by data on extinction frequency in some insular bird populations.
- Critical population sizes can be estimated from year-to-year abundance data without assuming values for parameters.

**Metapopulation dynamics**
- Most populations are naturally subdivided into local populations, or [[Metapopulations]] (Levins 1970).
- Environmental heterogeneity exists within and among patches. Both factors complicate the development and application of realistic theoretical models.
- Metapopulation survival requires a mean extinction rate lower than the immigration rate (Levins 1970). Conversion of natural habitat is more likely to impede dispersal than isolation caused by unsuitable natural habitat.

**Three-part risk from fragmentation**
1. Demographic units may be destroyed, reduced in size, or subdivided, increasing their extinction rate.
2. Potential sources of immigrants may be lost.
3. Immigration may be impeded by conversion of natural habitat between patches. This third risk raises extinction probability even without loss of critical habitat per se.

**Edith's checkerspot butterfly case study**
- Ehrlich and colleagues monitored [[Edith's Checkerspot Butterfly]] populations for 15 years (as read from the garbled scan), documenting population structure, dynamics, regulation, and observed extinctions.
- On Jasper Ridge Preserve, a population supported by three nearly contiguous habitat patches consisted of three demographic units whose sizes fluctuated independently in response to annual rainfall.
- One unit went extinct, was re-established by immigration, and went extinct again several years later. A second unit was on the verge of extinction at the time of writing.
- Populations within dispersal distance were increasingly isolated or extirpated by habitat fragmentation.
- Local extinctions are described as normal ecological events supported by multiple empirical studies. Many natural populations are frequently at a "critical size" even when they number in the hundreds or thousands before extinction.

**Microhabitat heterogeneity**
- Within-habitat (microhabitat) heterogeneity has substantial effects on the population dynamics of insects and mammals.
- In coastal California, *Euphydryas editha* local populations often go extinct after drought. Larval mortality is high in southern-exposed microhabitats where host plants senesce early, while north-facing microhabitats support survival under such conditions. The pattern reverses in particularly wet years.
- The relationship between patch size or number and extinction probability is therefore not simple. The effects of fragmentation may be nonlinear and depend on the geographic scale of conservation action.

**Genetic considerations**
- The authors agree that assessing genetic stochasticity is problematic. Genetic stochasticity can increase extinction probability for populations much larger than RDG predicts.
- By the most conservative genetic criteria (Franklin 1980), the effective population size (N_e) needed to maintain genetic variability and evolutionary potential probably translates to actual population sizes in the hundreds for most species.

**Community-level effects and secondary extinctions**
- Loss of a few species through fragmentation can trigger cascades of secondary extinctions through interacting species and food webs (Terborgh 1976; Gilbert 1980).
- Feinsinger et al. (1982) found that on Trinidad and Tobago island fragments, a decrease in hummingbird species led to more erratic and less faithful visitation of plants by remaining pollinators.
- Most higher plants depend on mycorrhizal fungi, which in temperate forests may in turn depend on small mammals for dispersal (Maser et al. 1978). Harris (1984) suggests that the minimum habitat sizes and degrees of insularity needed by small mammals may determine how far old-growth forests can be fragmented without losing diversity and function.

**Empirical data on reserves**
- Mammalian population data from national parks and similar protected areas (East 1981, 1983; Schonewald-Cox 1983) show that many populations number fewer than 100, and more than half fall in a size range of roughly 100–1,000 (as read from the scan).
- A substantial share of large-mammal faunas in comparatively large, intact reserves may therefore already be at risk without further fragmentation. This supports predictions from island biogeographic theory that faunal collapse will occur in intact reserves (Soulé et al. 1979; East 1983).

**Invertebrates and conservation priorities**
- Large mammals are a somewhat biased example, since they make up only a tiny fraction of biota and are especially vulnerable because of body size and trophic needs. The growing list of endangered invertebrates (Wells et al. 1983) indicates that fragmentation harms many taxa.
- The authors argue that conservation strategy should ask which threatened or endangered species can be preserved under realistic options, rather than which species are maintained under hypothetical reserve strategies.

**Conclusions**
- Risk assessment must consider all relevant population-level and community-level factors, as well as physical or edge effects (Brittingham and Temple 1983; Lovejoy et al. 1984; Janzen 1983).
- Current ecological theory is inadequate for resolving many SLOSS details. Nevertheless, habitat fragmentation is described as the most serious threat to biological diversity and the primary cause of the present extinction crisis.
