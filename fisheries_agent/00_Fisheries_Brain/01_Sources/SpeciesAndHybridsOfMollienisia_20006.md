# Species and Hybrids of Mollienisia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1933 article by [[Carl L. Hubbs]], Curator of Fishes at the University of Michigan, was written to correct long-standing confusion among aquarists and ichthyologists about the species of the live-bearing genus *Mollienisia* (family [[Poeciliidae]]). Hubbs sorts the genus into two groups, the sailfins and the short-finned mollys, describes each species and its range, and reports hybrids between species. The article's most notable claim is that a hybrid between [[Mollienisia Latipinna]] and [[Mollienisia Sphenops]] exists only as females in nature and reproduces through [[Parthenogenesis]], which would be the first case of natural parthenogenesis reported for a vertebrate. An editor's note warns that newspaper coverage overstated this as a discovery of parthenogenetic fishes.

## Background & Description

*Mollienisia* is a viviparous (live-bearing) genus in the family Poeciliidae, well known to aquarium hobbyists. Hubbs divides the species into two groups based on dorsal fin structure:

- **Sailfins (*latipinna* group):** The dorsal fin has 12 to 19 rays and begins well toward the head in adult males.
- **Short-finned mollys (*sphenops* group):** The dorsal fin has 8 to 11 rays and begins behind or near the middle of the body, excluding the caudal fin.

### Species Profiles

- **[[Mollienisia Velifera]]** ("king of the live bearers"): Found in Yucatan. It is about half again as large as [[Mollienisia Latipinna]]. Its dorsal fin has 16 to 19 rays and begins immediately behind the head. Broods can reach 120 young, and the young are robust from birth. Males show brassy breast and throat colors with metallic blue reflections on the body and fins.
- **[[Mollienisia Latipinna]]** ("prince of the live bearers"): The common sailfin of the southern United States. Its range runs from southern Georgia to Key West, around the Gulf coast to Texas, and down the east coast of Mexico. It has many local races and subspecies. Its dorsal fin has about 12 to 16 rays. The small [[Brownsville Texas]] race has 12 to 14 rays. Salt-water races from Key West and [[Pensacola Bay]] are described as particularly fine.
- **[[Mollienisia Sphenops]]:** A wide-ranging species from northern Mexico to northern South America, represented by an undetermined number of local races. Its dorsal fin has 8 to 10 rays depending on the race. Some races are about the size of a guppy, while others are as large as velifera. The black molly is a melanistic sport of latipinna, not of sphenops, a point Hubbs stresses as a common misidentification.
- **[[Mollienisia Latipunctata]]:** Endemic to the [[Rio Tamesi]] and its tributaries just north of [[Tampico]], Mexico, where it lives alongside sphenops without hybridizing with it. Males have 9 to 11 dorsal rays. Females show a row of black spots along the sides.
- **[[Mollienisia Petenensis]]:** A relative of velifera known only from [[Lake Petén]], Guatemala. It had not reached aquarists at the time of writing.
- **[[Mollienisia Dominicensis]]:** A short-finned species recognized from the island of Haiti. It had apparently not reached aquarists.

### Nomenclatural Problems

Hubbs documents several errors in the literature:

- The name *Mollienisia sphenops* is often applied by aquarists to the black sport of latipinna.
- The name *formosa* was used incorrectly. The German aquarist Rachow figured typical sphenops under this name, and the English ichthyologist Regan applied it to latipinna from Tampico and to sphenops from Panama. The name [[Mollienisia Formosa]] was based on a hybrid.
- Velifera is not simply a better-developed form of latipinna, as some believed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal limits:** Hubbs suggests that the small size of latipinna in northeastern Florida and southeastern Georgia is probably due to low temperatures there, which are near the limit for the genus.
- **Hybridization in the wild:** Poor development of latipinna near Brownsville and Tampico may reflect past hybridization with sphenops, since the ancestors of latipinna were "none too careful in choosing mates."
- **Aquarium conditions:** Hubbs's aquaria were kept in the upper 70s °F, rarely below 70 °F or above 80 °F. The fish feed on dried shrimp, prepared foods and algae from tank walls. Adults generally do not eat their young, although velifera is sometimes cannibalistic.
- **Sperm storage:** Females store sperm for months, so the paternity of young can be unreliable unless the mother was kept away from mature males of other species.

## Key Findings

1. **Two species groups:** *Mollienisia* divides into the sailfin (*latipinna*) group and the short-finned molly (*sphenops*) group. Dorsal fin ray counts and fin position distinguish them.
2. **Species accounts:** The article covers velifera, latipinna, sphenops, latipunctata, petenensis and dominicensis, with the ranges and fin features of each.
3. **Taxonomic errors:** Misapplied names (black molly as sphenops, "formosa" applied to several species) caused confusion. Hubbs states that ichthyologists "blundered even more than the fish fanciers."
4. **Inter-specific hybrids:**
   - *velifera* × *sphenops*: Two specimens had 13 and 14 dorsal rays, intermediate between the parents (sphenops 9 to 10; velifera 16 to 19). Hubbs describes this combination as rarely seen.
   - *latipinna* × *sphenops* (called *Mollienisia formosa*): Usually 11 dorsal rays, intermediate between the parents (sphenops 9; latipinna 13 in the local race). These hybrids vary among localities, reflecting the local sphenops race that produced them. Males from the Rio Papaloapan show strong color and are active in courtship.
5. **Unusual sex structure in hybrids:** Over much of their range, the latipinna × sphenops hybrids occur only as females. Those in Tamaulipas live with sphenops and never encounter latipinna males. Those near Brownsville live only with latipinna.
6. **Proposed parthenogenesis:** Female hybrids mate with either parent species. The sperm appears necessary to start development but does not contribute to inheritance. Offspring resemble the mother and are always female, which Hubbs describes as "a kind of parthenogenesis, the first case of natural parthenogenesis indicated for a vertebrate animal."
7. **Experimental confirmation:** Hubbs says these conclusions were checked in aquarium matings. He also reconstructed the hybrid type by crossing *Mollienisia sphenops* with *Mollienisia latipinna*.
8. **Failed cross-genus hybrids:** No cross with another poeciliid genus had produced young. Males of *Lebistes reticulatus*, *Gambusia affinis* and *Poecilistes pleurospilus* had mated with *Mollienisia* females without producing offspring. Hubbs asks readers to report any cross-genus young, other than the common *Platypoecilus* × *Xiphophorus* cross.
9. **Editor's caution:** The editor notes that newspapers reported a sensational discovery of all-female fish that reproduce without males. The editor clarifies that the claim rests on Dr. [[Myron Gordon]] finding no males of the hybrid in certain areas, and that Hubbs's final paragraphs propose a new scientific thesis rather than a settled finding.
