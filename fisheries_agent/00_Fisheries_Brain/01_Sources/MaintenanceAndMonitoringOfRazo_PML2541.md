# Maintenance and Monitoring of Razorback Suckers Held at Niland Native Fish Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #razorback-sucker

## Executive Summary

This March 21, 1986 memorandum from [[Linda Ulmer]], Fishery Biologist, Region 5, records the outcome of a March 11, 1986 meeting on the first-year rearing of native fish at the Niland facility. Participants included [[Buddy Jensen]], a fish culturist with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], and Region 5 Inland Fisheries staff. The memo sets out protocols for disease control, aeration, water-quality monitoring, sampling, fertilization, pond management, feeding, overwintering and eventual stocking of [[Razorback Sucker]] fry and juveniles.

## Background & Description

The document concerns the rearing of [[Razorback Sucker]], a native fish held in the [[Niland Native Fish Ponds]]. Fish were reared in ponds that had previously held catfish, so the memo draws on [[Channel Catfish]] pond experience for comparison. The species is not described in morphological or taxonomic detail. The memo is operational, covering husbandry and monitoring rather than biology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Disease risk:** Swimming-pool filters were judged ineffective against bacteria and protozoans. Staff expected [[Ich (Parasitic Disease)]], a cool-season parasite that affects catfish ponds, to occur in the native fish ponds as well. Draining the ponds after native fish are removed by November was expected to limit it.
- **Thermal stratification and oxygen:** A [[Thermocline]] was expected at about 4 feet. Aerators were not recommended unless installed soon, because mixing could release [[Hydrogen Sulfide]] from the anaerobic bottom layer (hypolimnion) into oxygenated surface water.
- **Plankton and primary productivity:** Daily plankton tows and weekly preserved plankton samples were planned to track responses to fertilization.
- **Dissolved oxygen thresholds:** Levels of 2–3 ppm were identified as evidence that pond conditions were deteriorating rapidly.

## Key Findings

**1. Disease monitoring**
- Remove 6–10 fish per pond each month, freeze them, and send them to Dexter for disease testing.
- Martin Chan is to check for diseases and parasites monthly and report to K. Nicols.

**2. Aeration and water quality**
- Do not install aerators unless they will be installed soon, to avoid mixing hydrogen sulfide into surface water.
- Record daily dissolved oxygen and water temperature. Measure pH weekly, since it is relatively stable in the catfish ponds.
- Pond conditions were expected to remain good for the first year, with problems likely to appear afterward.

**3. Size-based sampling regimes**

| Size class | Approximate age | Method and frequency |
|---|---|---|
| 12 mm to ½ inch (phototaxis stage) | ~6 weeks | Night lights, 3 times per week, to attract larvae for abundance estimates. A significant drop in larvae signals a serious problem and calls for contacting Buddy. |
| ½ inch to 2 inches | ~8 weeks | Seine sampling with a 6–10 ft bagless fry seine; fish counted and weighed to estimate fish per pound. Feeding rates adjusted accordingly. |
| 2 inches and up | Thereafter | Once per month with a 20–25 ft seine; growth projected between sampling dates. |

The sampling interval for the ½-inch to 2-inch class is garbled in the scan and cannot be reliably reported.

**4. Fertilization and stocking**
- Fertilization rates are given in Table I (not reproduced here).
- Stocking rates were kept relatively constant during the first year so fertilization responses could be tested. Stocking densities are to be adjusted later.
- Daily plankton tows were to determine whether additional nitrogen treatments were needed.

**5. Pond management and fry relocation**
- Fish in Pond #2 are to be moved to Pond #11 about one month after stocking.
- Of the 25,000 fry originally stocked, half are to go either to the Colorado River or to one of the 25-acre ponds holding grass carp, if those ponds become available. This is repeated every six weeks until November.
- Pond #2 was flagged as critical because of its high fry density. Daily dissolved oxygen and temperature profiles were to guide decisions.
- If a thermocline forms, anaerobic bottom water should be pumped out before draining. Fresh water is then pumped in at the surface, and the pond is drained once it is well mixed.

**6. Overwintering**
- In November, 50–100 fish from each pond move to the supply pond.
- They are held through winter to assess growth and within-cohort variability, until they reach 12 inches and are stocked in [[Senator Wash Reservoir]].

**7. Feeding protocol**
- Feed by hand, spread evenly across the pond surface. Feeders are not recommended because they degrade pond conditions faster.
- Feed size and frequency are matched to fish size, from [[Trout Starter Feed]] for the smallest fish up to 1/8-inch pellets (subject to cost-benefit analysis) for fish 4 inches and larger, fed 2–3 times daily.
- Fish 1 to 1½ inches are fed enough to cover the pond surface three times daily.
- For fish over 1½ inches, Wayne Parker is to develop a feeding chart giving pounds of feed per day.

**8. Stocking plan**
- About 50% of the fish are expected to reach 7½ inches by November (the scan reads "7J," which is interpreted here as 7½).
- Fish are to be relocated and stocked in the Topock, Parker II and Imperial Divisions. Specific locations will be set out in the FY 1986/87 monitoring program report.
