# An Experiment in Hatching Silver Salmon (Oncorhynchus kisutch) Eggs in Gravel

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Oncorhynchus-kisutch #fisheries-science

## Executive Summary

This 1938–1939 study by Shapovalov and Berrian, conducted at the Big Creek State Fish Hatchery in California, experimentally compared the development and survival of [[Silver Salmon]] (*[[Oncorhynchus Kisutch]]*) eggs incubated in a simulated natural gravel redd versus eggs incubated in a standard hatchery basket (control). Eggs from five adult sea-run females were fertilized and split into two lots: 8,329 eggs buried in gravel to mimic natural spawning conditions, and 7,500 eggs held in a conventional wire hatching basket. The experiment quantified [[Temperature Units]] (t.u.) required for hatching and fry emergence, survival rates, and growth outcomes. A major unusually severe flood event introduced heavy siltation into the gravel trough during incubation, substantially reducing survival of the buried eggs (10.2% emergence vs. 48.2% survival in control to the equivalent time point). Despite far lower survival, the surviving gravel-incubated fry showed markedly lower subsequent mortality and comparable growth relative to control fish. This work builds on the authors' earlier steelhead trout ([[Salmo Gairdnerii]]) gravel-hatching experiments and offers insight into the effectiveness of natural salmonid propagation versus hatchery methods, as well as the vulnerability of naturally spawned eggs to siltation.

## Background & Description

- **Species studied:** *Oncorhynchus kisutch* (Silver/Coho Salmon), sea-run adults.
- **Parental fish data (n=5):**
  - Average length: 68 (units not specified, likely cm)
  - Average total egg count per female: 3,179 eggs
  - Average eggs obtained via stripping: 3,166 (99.6% recovery)
  - Eggs remaining in fish post-stripping: average 13 (range 9–25)
- **Egg size reference:** 10 fluid ounces of control eggs = 809 eggs (80.9 eggs/ounce).
- **Fry morphology at emergence:**
  - Fish emerging on Day 1 retained approximately half of their yolk sac unabsorbed.
  - Fish emerging on Day 14 had nearly fully absorbed yolk sacs.
  - This yolk-sac absorption gradient reflects developmental variation tied to emergence timing.
- **Fry size at 56 days post-initial emergence:**
  - Experimental (gravel) fish: 23.8 fish/ounce, averaging **1.19 g** live weight each.
  - Control (basket) fish: 27.6 fish/ounce, averaging **1.13 g** live weight each.
  - Experimental fish, though slightly larger on average, showed **much greater size variation** due to the extended emergence period.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning substrate simulation:** Gravel used was collected from Scott Creek streambed, washed of sand/silt via minnow-seine agitation, and selected to match natural silver salmon spawning gravel size/type — buried at a 5-inch depth over a 2-inch base layer, with ~1 inch of flowing water above.
- **Natural analog for redd conditions:** Perforated plates were used to force water flow through the gravel, replicating intragravel flow dynamics of a natural redd.
- **Siltation as an ecological stressor:** Unusually severe floods during the incubation period (particularly after the eyed-egg stage) introduced heavy silt loads into the trough water, which settled around eggs in the gravel. Post-experiment excavation revealed considerable silt and many partially developed, apparently smothered eggs — implicating **siltation as a major natural mortality factor** for salmonid eggs in gravel redds.
- **Nocturnal emergence behavior:** Fry predominantly emerged from the gravel at night, evidenced by consistently higher fry counts collected in mornings vs. afternoons, and direct observation of newly formed emergence holes appearing overnight. This behavior is hypothesized to reduce predation risk on emerging fry — an [[Anti-Predator Adaptation]] relevant to [[Salmonid Fry Emergence Behavior]].
- **Comparative context:** Prior steelhead trout (*[[Salmo Gairdnerii]]*) gravel-hatching experiments (Shapovalov 1937) showed much higher gravel emergence success (29.8% and 79.9%) compared to this silver salmon experiment (10.2%), attributed to more favorable (less silted) conditions in those trials — supporting the idea that natural emergence success is highly variable and condition-dependent (e.g., flood/siltation vs. mining pollution vs. favorable stream conditions).
- **Interspecific/intraspecific redd interference:** Discussion notes raised the ecological concern that later-spawning runs (e.g., chinook salmon or other salmonids) may re-spawn over existing redds, burying earlier-deposited eggs too deeply or exposing them to predators — a natural source of egg mortality distinct from siltation.

## Key Findings

- **Temperature units (t.u.) to developmental milestones** (from fertilization Jan 3, 1938):
  | Developmental Stage | Date | Temperature Units | Days |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Maximum hatch (control) | Feb 20 | 772.3 | 48 |
  | First emergence from gravel | Mar 9 | 1,084.3 | 65 |
  | Maximum emergence from gravel | Mar 12 | 1,155.6 | 68 |

- **Emergence timing:** Initial fry emergence occurred March 9 (25 fish); maximum single-day emergence (64 fry) occurred on day 4; total emergence period spanned **at least 38 days** (March 9–April 15), with a possible extension to May 4. This is comparable to but longer than the 16–20 day emergence windows observed in prior steelhead experiments.

- **Survival outcomes:**
  | Metric | Gravel (Experimental) | Control |
  |---|---|---|
  | Original eggs | 8,329 | 7,500 |
  | Eggs hatched (%) | — | 65.9% |
  | Fish emerged/surviving to equivalent time (%) | **10.2%** | **48.2%** |
  | Mortality (56 days post-emergence) | 48 (+5 injured in handling) | 905 |

- **Cause of low gravel survival:** Post-experiment gravel excavation revealed heavy silt deposition and numerous partially developed, smothered eggs — strongly implicating flood-driven **siltation** as the primary cause of reduced emergence success, rather than inherent flaws in the gravel-incubation method itself.

- **Post-emergence mortality patterns:** Despite drastically lower emergence success, surviving gravel-reared fry exhibited **far lower mortality** (48 deaths) than control fish (905 deaths) over the following 56 days, though control mortality dropped sharply in the final two weeks (~1 fish/day), suggesting an initial hatchery-basket-associated mortality spike not present in the gravel-reared cohort.

- **Growth comparison:** Gravel-reared and control fry reached comparable individual weights (1.19 g vs. 1.13 g) despite different rearing environments and food regimes; however, gravel-reared fish showed greater size heterogeneity, attributable to the extended, staggered emergence period.

- **Hypotheses for prolonged/staggered emergence** (from expert discussion):
  - Localized silt pockets within gravel may have created variable escape difficulty for different eggs/fry, independent of burial depth (which was held constant).
  - Variable **intragravel aeration** may have caused differential metabolic/developmental rates among eggs.
  - **Hatching synchrony effect**: mechanical agitation from a hatching egg may stimulate nearby eggs to hatch sooner (a phenomenon noted in hatchery baskets), meaning spatial clustering of eggs within the gravel could influence relative hatch timing — this may explain why eggs near the trough's downstream end (less obviously favorable for oxygenation) hatched first.
  - Individual/family-based (genetic) variation in developmental rate was considered but dismissed as unlikely to explain the pattern, since experimental and control eggs derived from the same five parental fish.

- **Methodological note on egg recovery accuracy:** Attempting to count remaining/dead eggs in gravel post-experiment proved unreliable due to widely varying decomposition states, echoing similar criticisms of redd-excavation methodologies used in other studies (e.g., work referenced by Hobbs in New Zealand), highlighting a persistent challenge in accurately quantifying natural spawning success via redd excavation.

- **Broader implication:** The study supports the view that gravel/redd emergence success in nature is **highly variable** depending on environmental conditions (siltation, flooding, pollution), and that hatchery-based artificial propagation may buffer against these stochastic natural mortality sources, informing debates on the relative value of [[Artificial Propagation]] vs. [[Natural Spawning]] in salmonid conservation and fisheries management.
