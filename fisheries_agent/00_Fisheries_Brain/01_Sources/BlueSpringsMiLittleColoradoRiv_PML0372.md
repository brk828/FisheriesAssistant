# Blue Springs as a Barrier to Distribution of Speckled Dace (Rhinichthys osculus)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary progress report (1977), with a request for additional 1972 funding, describes a field study of whether [[Blue Springs]] in the [[Little Colorado River]] gorge restricts gene flow between two [[Speckled Dace]] ([[Rhinichthys Osculus]]) populations. The authors, [[Steven W. Carothers]] and [[Stewart W. Aitchison]] of the [[Museum of Northern Arizona]], report that fish from the upstream population (Population A) are incapacitated by Blue Springs water, while fish from the downstream population (Population B) are not. Laboratory-style tank experiments suggest that movement between the populations, if it occurs, is one-way (upstream to downstream) and rare. The report outlines a three-phase study and requests funds for helicopter transport and field assistants.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), Cyprinidae. The dace occurs throughout the Little Colorado River gorge wherever permanent running water is present.
- **Origin of the study:** In 1968, Dr. V. A. Taylor observed that Population A fish that swam into Blue Springs water became immediately incapacitated, sank to the stream bottom, and died within about one hour, or were swept downstream.
- **Study sites:** [[Salt Trail Canyon Spring]] (mile 7.0), [[Big Canyon Spring]] (mile 7.5), and [[Blue Springs]] (mile 13). Sampling covered areas above and below Blue Springs.
- **Population definitions:** Population A lives above Blue Springs; Population B lives below it.
- **Effort to date:** The report states that 14 man-hours (reported as "hl+" in the OCR, read here as 14) had been spent in the gorge. The authors note that the figure is uncertain due to scan quality.
- **Project structure:** Three phases were planned:
  - **Phase I:** Determine which physical and chemical parameters of Blue Springs water cause fish to become incapacitated.
  - **Phase II:** Determine the extent to which Blue Springs inhibits gene flow between Populations A and B.
  - **Phase III:** Determine what morphological, physiological, and/or genetic differences exist between Populations A and B.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry at the study springs (Phase I).** A portable Hach chemical kit (model AL-35) measured seven parameters at Salt Trail Canyon Spring and Big Canyon Spring: total acidity, alkalinity, carbon dioxide, [[Dissolved Oxygen]], pH, total hardness, and temperature. Near Blue Springs, only dissolved oxygen and temperature could be measured because of floods and equipment limits.

- Temperature at the springs was very constant, never dropping below about 20 °C.
- Carbon dioxide ranged from 15 to 55 ppm.
- Dissolved oxygen ranged from 5 to 10 ppm.
- pH varied slightly around neutral (pH 7).
- Dace were abundant at both springs in running and standing water, so the authors assume all measured parameters fall within the fish's physiological tolerances.

**Blue Springs gradient.** The following values were recorded along the river:

| Location | Dissolved Oxygen | Temperature |
|---|---|---|
| At Blue Springs | Less than 1.0 ppm | 20.7 °C |
| 100 m above Blue Springs | 10 ppm | 21.1 °C |
| 1000 m above Blue Springs | 15 ppm | 29.8 °C |

The authors attribute the lethal conditions at the spring to a combination of very low oxygen, possibly high [[Carbon Dioxide]], and a temperature drop of about 9.1 °C at the spring compared with 1000 m upstream. No carbon dioxide readings were taken above mile 7.5, which the authors consider a critical gap.

**Literature context.** The authors cite [[Ellis et al.]] for a general threshold of 3 to 5 ppm dissolved oxygen as the minimum to which fish normally respond. They cite [[Hubbs and Hettler]] for the desert fish [[Crenichthys Baileyi]], which tolerates oxygen levels as low as 0.7 to 2.7 ppm. They also cite Shelford and Allee (1912, *Science*) for evidence that carbon dioxide is highly toxic to fish and may be more critical than oxygen.

**Water-quality observations.** The report notes that Population B fish, although they avoid Blue Springs, appear more tolerant of its water than Population A fish.

## Key Findings

### Phase I: Behavioral Experiments

Experiments were run on 15 September 1968 and repeated several times with consistent results.

- **Experiment 1 (Population A fish moved from 100 m above Blue Springs into Blue Springs water at 20.7 °C):** Fish swam erratically, gill covers distended and contracted rapidly, and fish rose to the surface to gulp air. Within three minutes, each fish was completely incapacitated, lying on its side on the bottom.
- **Experiment 2 (Population B fish moved from below Blue Springs into the same water):** No reaction; fish behaved normally.
- **July 1971 replication:** Experiment 1 reproduced the 1968 result. Experiment 2 showed an initial negative reaction, but fish fully recovered after about six hours.
- **Interpretation:** Temperature and oxygen changed only slightly during trials, with gradual increases in both. The authors propose that high initial carbon dioxide in Blue Springs water dissipates when the water stands. The inability to reproduce the 1968 Experiment 2 result in 1971 remains unexplained. One possibility is that the 1971 fish were recent Population A immigrants not yet adapted to downstream water. They were collected from a small pool left by high water below the spring.

### Phase II: Gene Flow Assessment

- Fifteen-liter plastic containers served as simultaneous experimental tanks.
- Population A fish actively avoid contact with Blue Springs water whenever possible.
- Any gene flow between populations must be **unidirectional (upstream to downstream)** and is considered **rare**.
- During high water, some Population A fish may survive floods and pass the spring. During low water, some may become trapped in the anesthetizing eddies of Blue Springs. Trapped fish that are not swept away die within an hour. Fish that escape may survive to the next spawning season and could contribute genetic material to Population B.

### Phase III: Planned Analyses (Not Yet Undertaken)

Detailed procedures were outlined and scheduled to begin in the near future:

- **Morphology:** Standard body measurements, scale counts, and color variation. The authors note these are tedious and may be of doubtful value.
- **Physiology:** Respiratory metabolism and environmental tolerance ranges for both populations. This requires transporting many live fish to the laboratory.
- **Genetics:** Measurement of genetic variance at various [[Isoenzyme Analysis|isoenzyme loci]]. The authors expect this phase to give the most precise information on how Blue Springs affects gene flow.

### Limitations Noted by the Authors

- Floods and missing equipment prevented full measurement at Blue Springs in early stages.
- No carbon dioxide data were collected above mile 7.5.
- The 1971 experiment could not replicate the 1968 Experiment 2 result, leaving the mechanism unresolved.
