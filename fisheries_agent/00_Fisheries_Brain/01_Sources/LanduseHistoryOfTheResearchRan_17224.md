# Land-Use History of the Research Ranch, Elgin, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-history

*Note on scope:* This document is a historical land-use review, not a fish study. Fish appear only as historical evidence (Colorado squawfish, humpback sucker, native fish decline). The tags above follow the system format; the document's dominant subject is terrestrial and range ecology.

## Executive Summary

Conrad J. Bahre's 1977 review traces human influence on the wild lands of the upper San Pedro and Babocomari drainages and the 7,830-acre [[Research Ranch]] near Elgin, Arizona, from the arrival of [[Big Game Hunters]] around 12,000 years ago to the late twentieth century. The paper argues that prehistoric peoples had a real but poorly measured impact, that the Spanish and Mexican periods changed the landscape far less than often claimed, and that the most profound transformation came from the [[Cattle Ranching]] boom of the 1880s and 1890s, which combined overstocking with drought. The author rejects climatic change as the sole explanation for landscape change, and concludes that cattle ranching caused most of the recent catastrophic change in the region. The Research Ranch, de-stocked since late 1967, is presented as a valuable "control area" for studying man-made and natural ecosystems.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Research Ranch comprises 7,830 acres of short-grass prairie and oak woodland, at an average elevation of 4,850 feet, with average annual rainfall of 17 inches (Bonham, 1972). About 75% of rainfall falls in July–August and December–mid March.
- **Ownership:** Set aside in 1968 by the [[Appleton Family]] for ecological research; not grazed since late 1967. Current holdings: 2,275 acres national forest (Chuney Allotment), 2,350 acres state land, and 3,215 acres private land.
- **Purpose:** To provide a land-use history as a baseline for a "land laboratory" assessing human impact on regional ecology.
- **Approach:** Three parts: (1) sequence of occupancy and ecological impact of each culture; (2) land-use activities affecting landscape evolution; (3) major changes over the last century and methods to account for them.
- **Author:** Conrad J. Bahre, Department of Geography, University of California, Davis. The volume includes a preface by [[Paul S Martin]] (Dept. of Geosciences, University of Arizona), who frames the work within megafauna extinction and grassland ecology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Prehistoric and Pleistocene baseline**
- Late Pleistocene megafauna (mammoth, bison, tapir, horse, sloth, dire wolf) were present; the Lehner and Naco kill sites date to about 11,000–12,000 years ago.
- Pollen and mollusk evidence suggests postglacial vegetation near Lehner was similar to today's, dominated by grassland or Chihuahuan Desert Scrub.
- Paul Martin argues that megafauna loss removed grazing diversity, so modern prairies could not have evolved without the large animals that once existed in them.

**Prehistoric cultures and resource use**
- Chronologies follow [[Charles Di Peso]] rather than [[Gordon R Willey]], with the Cochise, Preceramic Ootam, Formative Ootam, and Hohokam sequence.
- Subsistence centered on wild plant gathering (mescal, bellotas from [[Emory Oak]], yucca, sacaton seeds, cactus fruits) with horticulture (maize, beans, squash, cotton, amaranth) at Babocomari Village, Quiburi, and Santa Cruz de Gaybanipitea.
- Fish were abundant in prehistoric trash pits at Quiburi; a [[Colorado Squawfish]] of just under five feet is documented, and humpback sucker was also eaten.
- Cooking-pit bones of bison are disputed; the author considers historic bison in southeastern Arizona speculative.

**Spanish period (1539–1767)**
- Coronado passed through in 1540 with livestock, but no livestock were given to the Sobaipuri.
- Sobaipuri were pushed out by Apache pressure; Quiburi was abandoned in 1698 and the last Sobaipuri left the valley by 1762.
- Bolton's 1700 map figure of 100,000+ cattle is judged exaggerated; the author suspects very few livestock were present.

**Mexican period (1821–1853)**
- Three large cattle grants (San Ignacio del Babocomari, San Rafael del Valle, San Juan de las Boquillas y Nogales) were established in the late 1820s–1830s.
- The Babocomari grant reportedly ran 40,000 cattle (Bartlett, 1854), a figure the author considers absurdly high. Bartlett's 1851 account describes about 40,000 head at the Babocomari hacienda before abandonment.
- Grasses were overgrazed in bottomlands, creating openings for weeds; beaver and fish were still plentiful.
- The Gadsden Treaty (1853–1854) transferred the drainages to the United States.

**Anglo-American period (1854–1900)**
- Fort Buchanan (1857), Camp Wallen (1866), and Camp Crittenden (1867) were established and abandoned; malaria and Apache raids limited settlement.
- Fort Huachuca was founded in 1877. Silver discovery at Tombstone (1877) drove a population boom to about 6,000 by 1885, before falling to 1,200 by 1900.
- The Southern Pacific line (1882) connected the valley to markets and encouraged cattle expansion.
- Cattle numbers in Cochise County rose to 18,000 (1885), with roughly 36,000 head in the upper San Pedro in 1890 (Rogers, 1965).
- The 1893 die-off killed an estimated 50–75% of cattle; overstocking continued until the Taylor Grazing Act of 1934.
- Woodcutting for mines, smelters, and fuel denuded the Mule Mountains and other ranges; the Coronado National Forest was created in 1908.

**Land tenure and modern management**
- Homestead entries on the Research Ranch began in 1916; the Stock Raising Homestead Act (1916) allowed claims up to 640 acres.
- The Appleton family acquired the private portions in 1959 and 1965.
- Forest Service practices (contour plowing, exotic grass seeding, brush conversion, predator poisoning, fire suppression) further altered the landscape.

**Fire**
- Lightning and man-made fire were long part of the grassland, but evidence of frequent intentional burning by aboriginal peoples is weak. Apaches were the only group known to use fire extensively for hunting.
- Mature mesquite survives grass fires; only seedlings are killed, so fire alone is unlikely to control mesquite.
- Four fires burned the Ranch between 1974 and 1975 (about 300, 300, 350, and 750 acres), with the Bocks' studies finding no clear evidence that fire improved grass production.

**Weeds, woody invasion, and climate**
- More than 45 weedy species were recorded on the Ranch (list by Jane Bock). Dominant weeds include threadleaf groundsel (*Senecio longilobus*), snakeweed, and burroweed.
- Woody invasion (mesquite, white thorn) accelerated after 1893; the author attributes it primarily to livestock grazing and management, not climate.

**Hydrology and erosion**
- Stream entrenchment began in the San Pedro around the 1890s and in the Babocomari around the 1900s, which the author attributes to overgrazing, trampling, and loss of cover, with beaver dam loss also contributing.
- The author notes that earlier arroyo cutting occurred before livestock, so entrenchment is not solely a modern phenomenon.

**Wildlife and fish**
- Antelope were reintroduced to Fort Huachuca in 1949 but numbered only 77 in Game Unit 35 in 1975, with no fawn survival in 1974.
- Native fish declined after perennial flow was lost; of eleven indigenous species recorded from the San Pedro in 1846–1854, only three survived until 1950.
- Verde trout survived in the headwaters until recently; introduced smallmouth bass and stamp mill pollution are cited as additional pressures.

## Key Findings

1. **Megafauna and early hunters:** Late Pleistocene megafauna were present until hunters likely hastened their extinction around 8000–5000 B.C.; the author considers the question of prehistoric overkill unresolved.
2. **Prehistoric impact is underestimated:** Aboriginal populations, though sparse, probably changed vegetation through agriculture, fuel cutting, irrigation, and selective gathering.
3. **Fish history:** Large native fish (Colorado squawfish, humpback sucker) were present in prehistoric and early historic times; the streams now flow only intermittently.
4. **Spanish impact was limited:** The San Pedro was largely outside Spanish settlement; livestock numbers were probably small.
5. **Cattle as the dominant driver:** Overstocking in the 1880s–1890s, especially during drought, produced the most catastrophic changes in vegetation, soils, and drainage.
6. **Climate is insufficient as an explanation:** Tree-ring data do not match changes in vegetation or drainage, and the author argues for livestock-driven microclimatic and hydrological effects.
7. **Fire:** Fire's role in maintaining grassland is uncertain; mesquite is not controlled by fire, and suppression alone does not explain woody invasion.
8. **Research value:** The Research Ranch, de-stocked since 1967, offers a rare "control area" for maintaining natural ecosystems and studying wildfire, grassland productivity, and wildlife recovery.
9. **Paul Martin's conclusion:** Future grasslands may need stocking with a variety of large mammals, and game ranching may be a better alternative to cattle monoculture.
