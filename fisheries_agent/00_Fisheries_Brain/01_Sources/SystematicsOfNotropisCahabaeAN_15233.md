# Systematics of Notropis cahabae, a New Cyprinid Fish Endemic to the Cahaba River of the Mobile Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 monograph (Bulletin Alabama Museum of Natural History No. 9) by [[Richard L. Mayden]] and [[Bernard R. Kuhajda]] formally describes *Notropis cahabae*, the [[Cahaba Shiner]], a small cyprinid endemic to the main channel of the [[Cahaba River]] in the [[Mobile Basin]]. The species is compared with the sympatric [[Mimic Shiner]] (*N. volucellus*) and the [[Channel Shiner]] (*N. wickliffi*) using morphology, meristics, pigmentation, the cephalic lateralis system and 38 allozyme loci. The authors conclude that *N. cahabae* is sister to *N. wickliffi*, that *N. volucellus* is a complex of several taxa, and that the species' tiny, shrinking range warrants its state listing as endangered.

## Background & Description

The Mobile Basin ranks as the third most diverse drainage in North America, with 157 species, about 40 of them endemic (Swift et al., 1986). The Cahaba River, flowing 354 river kilometers, is described as the most ichthyologically diverse free-flowing river of its size on the continent, with 132 known species (Pierson et al., 1989).

The holotype is a breeding male, 39.8 mm SL (UAIC 9119.01), collected 22 June 1986 near Centreville, Bibb County, Alabama. A total of 420 paratypes are catalogued across multiple institutions. Diagnostic features include a lateral stripe on the caudal peduncle with straight dorsal and ventral margins, no well-defined predorsal stripe or spot, uniformly pigmented dorsal caudal peduncle scales, usually 34 lateral-line scales, usually 13 or 14 predorsal scale rows, 7 to 9 gill rakers, a scaled breast, and unique alleles at the Acp-A, Gpi-A, M-Aat-A and Tpi-A loci.

Meristic counts for the holotype series include dorsal fin rays of 8 in 78 of 80 specimens (x = 8.00), anal fin rays with a mean of 7.96, and 19 principal caudal rays in all 80 specimens. Males and females are similar meristically; sexes differ significantly in nine morphometric variables, with males having longer pectoral fins, heads and upper jaws. Breeding males are tuberculate on the head and pectoral rays. The name *cahabae* is the genitive of the river name.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range.** Historically, *N. cahabae* occupied about 140 river kilometers (76 mi), from Helena (Shelby County) to Centreville (Bibb County). It has since contracted to roughly 28 river kilometers (15 mi) between the Fall Line and Piper Bridge. Before 1984 it was not known below the Fall Line; four Gulf Coastal Plain locations were then recorded, represented by few individuals and probably resulting from waif dispersal over Cahaba Falls.

**Habitat.** The principal habitat is main-channel shallow shoals. The species occurs mainly over clean sand or sand-gravel substrate in 0.25 to 1.5 m of water, often just below swift riffles. Unlike the mimic shiner, it is not associated with small tributaries except during floods, when it moves into lower tributary reaches and backwaters. Spawning habitat is unknown but is presumed to be clean, broad, rocky riffles.

**Reproduction.** Reproductive activity runs from May to July, with actively spawning fish found only in mid- to late June. Young-of-the-year at 17 to 19 mm SL were recorded in late July. The authors note the species may be annual. Both *N. cahabae* and *N. volucellus* mature in their first year (35 to 37 mm SL), but *N. cahabae* produces significantly fewer and smaller oocytes and a smaller ovarian-somatic weight ratio (Oliver, 1986).

**Threats.** Declines are attributed to effluent from mining and development, with the authors citing deforestation, development and mining in the upper Cahaba watershed as ongoing pressures. Upstream populations have disappeared.

## Key Findings

- **New species:** *N. cahabae* is described as distinct from both *N. volucellus* and *N. wickliffi*, differing in body shape, cephalic lateralis canals, meristics, four unique alleles, and coloration.
- **Phylogeny:** The *N. volucellus* species group forms a monophyletic clade. Within it, *N. cahabae* and *N. wickliffi* are sister taxa, supported by the shared allele S-Mdh-B (b) and two coloration features. Shortest-tree length was 54.2.
- **Cryptic diversity:** *N. volucellus* appears to be a polytypic complex, with at least three forms identifiable (Mobile, Tennessee/Ohio and Kiamichi). Its Acp-A (c) and Pep-A (a) alleles are shared across the complex.
- **Morphometrics:** *N. cahabae* is nearly 100% separable from *N. wickliffi* and the Mobile form of *N. volucellus*, but overlaps considerably with Tennessee/Cumberland *N. volucellus*.
- **Genetics:** Of 38 loci, *N. cahabae* was polymorphic at only four (M-Aat-A, Est-1, Est-2, Gpi-A). Mean alleles per locus were 1.2, mean heterozygosity 0.063, and 20% of loci polymorphic, versus 1.4 alleles, heterozygosity 0.049 and 35% polymorphic loci in sympatric *N. volucellus*.
- **Biogeography:** The sister relationship between a Mobile Basin and a Mississippi Basin large-river species supports a Miocene Appalachian River linking the Tennessee and Mobile drainages before the Pleistocene, a pattern echoed by other fishes and molluscs.
- **Conservation status:** Listed as rare (Miller, 1972), endangered by the IUCN (Miller, 1977), by the American Fisheries Society (Deacon et al., 1979), and by the Alabama Department of Conservation and Natural Resources. The authors call for monitoring, habitat maintenance, and prevention of upstream degradation.
