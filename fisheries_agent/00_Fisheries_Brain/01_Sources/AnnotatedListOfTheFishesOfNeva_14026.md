# Annotated List of the Fishes of Nevada

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #checklist #conservation #Nevada #Great-Basin

## Executive Summary

This comprehensive checklist documents 160 native and introduced fishes referable to 108 species, 56 genera, and 19 families recorded for Nevada as of 1984. The list updates La Rivers' 1962 "Fishes and Fisheries of Nevada" and reflects twenty years of changes including species additions through official and unofficial introductions, extinctions, population declines, and improved taxonomic understanding. The [[Cyprinidae]] is the most diverse family with 39 subspecies, 31 species, and 18 genera. Introduced fishes constitute 58% of species (63 of 108), while native fishes represent 42% (45 species), though when subspecies are included, 58% (91) are native and 43% (69) are introduced. At least seven native fishes are extinct throughout their range, and four have been extirpated from Nevada but persist elsewhere. The natural drainage systems include the [[Great Basin]] (46 native fishes), [[Colorado River]] system (29 native fishes), [[Snake River]] (13 native fishes), and [[Amargosa River]] (6 native fishes).

## Background & Description

The first comprehensive list of Nevada fishes was published by La Rivers and Trelease in 1952, culminating in La Rivers' 1962 monograph. Over the subsequent two decades, significant changes occurred in the state's fish fauna through both authorized and unauthorized introductions, extinctions, population declines, and improved taxonomic resolution. The authors recognize 67 subspecies referable to 15 species, reflecting better understanding of distribution and geographic variation.

The checklist includes all taxa known from Nevada within historical times, encompassing both native species and introduced species regardless of establishment success. Families are ordered phylogenetically, with genera, species, and subspecies listed alphabetically within families. For [[Cyprinodontiformes]], the authors follow Parenti's 1981 classification. Common and scientific names generally follow the American Fisheries Society (Robins et al. 1980), with specific exceptions noted (e.g., "Virgin River spinedace" for *Lepidomeda mollispinis*, "poolfish" for *Empetrichthys* species).

Nevada's natural drainage systems are divided into four major basins: the [[Great Basin]] (including [[Lahontan Basin]], [[Bonneville Basin]], and other endorheic drainages), [[Colorado River]] system (including [[White River]], [[Moapa River]], [[Virgin River]], Meadow Valley Wash, and Las Vegas Creek), [[Snake River]], and [[Amargosa River]] (including [[Ash Meadows]]). Within the Great Basin, the Lahontan drainage supports the most diverse native ichthyofauna with 12 native fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Nevada fish fauna reflects the state's diverse aquatic habitats, from large pluvial lake remnants ([[Pyramid Lake]], [[Walker Lake]], [[Lake Tahoe]]) to isolated thermal springs in desert basins. Many native species exhibit high endemism and occupy restricted ranges in spring systems, particularly in the [[Great Basin]] and [[Amargosa River]] drainages. Thermal springs support unique endemic taxa including [[Desert Dace]] (*Eremichthys acros*), [[Moapa Dace]] (*Moapa coriacea*), and various springfish (*Crenichthys*) and pupfish (*Cyprinodon*) subspecies.

The increasing proportion of introduced fishes continues to burden native ichthyofauna. Since La Rivers' 1962 list, 29 additional introduced fishes have been recorded, bringing the total to 69 introduced taxa (including subspecies). Introductions include gamefish (salmonids, centrarchids, percids), baitfish (cyprinids), aquarium species (cichlids, poeciliids), and species from unauthorized releases. Many introductions have failed to establish, but successful introductions have altered native fish communities, particularly in the [[Colorado River]] system and spring habitats.

Habitat modification, water development, and species interactions with introduced fishes have contributed to extinctions and extirpations. At least seven native fishes are extinct throughout their range, including [[Alvord Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki* ssp.), [[Independence Valley Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor isolata*), [[Pahranagat Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda altivelis*), [[Grass Valley Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus reliquus*), [[Las Vegas Dace]] (*Rhinichthys* sp.), three [[Pahrump Poolfish]] subspecies (*Empetrichthys latos* subspecies), and [[Ash Meadows Poolfish]] (*Empetrichthys merriami*). Four additional native species have been extirpated from Nevada but persist elsewhere: [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta robusta*).

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic Diversity:**
- 160 total fishes (including subspecies) referable to 108 species, 56 genera, and 19 families
- 67 subspecies recognized within 15 species
- [[Cyprinidae]] most diverse: 39 subspecies, 31 species, 18 genera
- Only 6 of 19 fish families are native to Nevada

**Native vs. Introduced Species:**
- Species level: 63 introduced (58%), 45 native (42%)
- Including subspecies: 69 introduced (43%), 91 native (58%)
- 29 introduced fishes added since La Rivers' 1962 list
- La Rivers and Trelease (1952) reported 33 introduced fishes; La Rivers (1962) reported 40

**Conservation Status:**
- At least 7 native fishes extinct throughout their range
- 4 native fishes extirpated from Nevada but extant elsewhere
- Many native species have become much less abundant

**Drainage System Distribution:**
- [[Great Basin]]: 46 native fishes (including 3 also in Snake River)
- [[Lahontan Basin]] (within Great Basin): 12 native fishes (most diverse)
- [[Colorado River]] system: 29 native fishes
- [[Snake River]]: 13 native fishes
- [[Amargosa River]]: 6 native fishes

**Notable Endemic Taxa:**
- [[Desert Dace]] (*Eremichthys acros*): Soldier Meadows thermal springs, Humboldt County
- [[Moapa Dace]] (*Moapa coriacea*): Moapa River thermal springs, Clark County
- [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*): Devil's Hole, Ash Meadows, Nye County
- [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*): Endemic to Pyramid Lake and Truckee River
- Multiple endemic subspecies of [[Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor*): 17 subspecies recognized across isolated basins
- Multiple endemic subspecies of [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*): 15 subspecies across drainage systems
- Multiple endemic subspecies of [[White River Springfish]] (*Crenichthys baileyi*): 5 subspecies in thermal springs

**Introduced Species Impacts:**
- Widely established gamefish: [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*)
- [[Colorado River]] system heavily impacted: [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*)
- Tropical aquarium species established in thermal springs: [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*), [[Shortfin Molly]] (*Poecilia mexicana*), [[Convict Cichlid]] (*Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*), [[Spotted Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mariae*)
- Unsuccessful introductions documented for numerous species

**Taxonomic Changes:**
- Increased recognition of subspecies reflects better understanding of geographic variation
- Adoption of Parenti (1981) classification for [[Cyprinodontiformes]]
- Placement of *Empetrichthys* in [[Goodeidae]] (previously considered cyprinodontid)
- Recognition of undescribed subspecies requiring formal description (multiple *Gila bicolor*, *Rhinichthys osculus*, *Catostomus clarki*, *Salmo clarki* subspecies)
