# Short-Term PIT Tag Retention and Recapture in Wild Bluehead and Flannelmouth Suckers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether abdominally implanted [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag|PIT tags]] (12.5 mm) are retained and do not reduce recapture of wild [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*) and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) during spawning migration. Fish were captured at a [[Weir Trapping|weir]] on [[Cottonwood Creek]], a tributary of the [[Gunnison River]] near Delta, Colorado, in spring 2016. PIT tag retention was 99.9%, with only one tag loss in 2,645 tagged fish. Tagged fish were recaptured at a higher proportion than control fish marked only with a [[Caudal Fin Punch]]. No tagged female shed a tag with eggs. The authors conclude that abdominal PIT tags are an effective marking method for these species in short-term field studies.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** Bluehead and Flannelmouth suckers are native catostomids of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Both species are of conservation concern. Bluehead Sucker is thought to be extirpated from about half of its historic range, which makes unbiased monitoring data especially important. Other catostomids that co-occur in the stream include non-native [[White Sucker]], hybrid suckers, Longnose Sucker, Speckled Dace, and Roundtail Chub.

**PIT tagging.** PIT tags are a low-cost marking tool that is widely used in fish research. Their use rests on three assumptions: tags are retained, tagging does not harm survival or behavior, and tagging does not change recapture probability. These assumptions have been verified for many species but not all. Poor retention, reduced survival, and altered behavior have been reported in some taxa, including juvenile White Sucker (Ficke et al. 2012).

**Knowledge gaps.** Most earlier retention and survival work was done in laboratory or caged settings, which cannot test recapture effects or natural spawning behavior. Retention during spawning has been studied in only a few species (e.g., [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]]). Before this study, PIT tag retention in Bluehead and Flannelmouth suckers had not been thoroughly evaluated under wild conditions.

**Objectives.**
- Primary: estimate short-term PIT tag retention in wild, spawning fish.
- Secondary: determine whether PIT tagging reduced recapture rates, using an externally marked control group.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Cottonwood Creek is an ephemeral tributary of Roubideau Creek, which flows into the Gunnison River. Its hydrograph is snowmelt-driven. In 2016, flowing water was present from March 11 to June 15. The highest measured flow was approximately 2.4 (units garbled in the source; likely m³/s). Flow was low and clear until April 8, when higher-elevation snowmelt began.

**Trapping setup.** A weir was placed about 130 m upstream of the confluence with Roubideau Creek. It used two stream-spanning aluminum fences that funneled fish into an upstream trap and a downstream trap. Traps were operated from April 5 to May 27, 2016. Flow and debris forced removal on May 6, and the downstream weir and trap were reinstalled May 23. On several occasions, bar gaps were widened to 5.72 cm to reduce pressure from high flows; most adult suckers passed through these openings.

**Associated movement study.** The work was part of a larger PIT-tag-based study of movement and tributary use in the Upper Colorado River Basin. A submersible PIT-tag antenna downstream of the weir showed that a significantly higher proportion of Flannelmouth Suckers than Bluehead Suckers left the stream during the May 6–23 trap gap.

## Key Findings

**Tagging and control groups**
- 2,645 fish were abdominally implanted with 12.5 × 2.1 mm PIT tags and given a caudal fin punch: 2,249 Bluehead Suckers and 396 Flannelmouth Suckers.
- 2,660 control fish (2,026 Bluehead and 634 Flannelmouth) received only a caudal fin punch.
- Tags were placed posterior to the left pelvic fin using a hand-held implant gun. Ten people with varying experience implanted tags under the supervision of experienced taggers.
- All tags were implanted first (April 5–21), followed by caudal punches (April 22–May 6).

**Tag retention**
- Overall retention was 99.9%. Only one tag loss was recorded, in a confirmed male Bluehead Sucker. Retention was above 99% for both species.
- Tag loss was calculated as recaptured fish with a caudal punch but no PIT tag. The discussion reports tag loss below 0.1%.
- Hole punches on all recaptured tagged fish remained clearly visible at the end of the study.

**Recapture rates** (Table 2)

| Group | Bluehead Sucker | Flannelmouth Sucker | All fish |
|---|---|---|---|
| PIT-tagged | 32.1% | 14.4% | 29.5% |
| Caudal punch only | 26.4% | 9.3% | 22.3% |

- Overall, 593 fish were recaptured (534 Bluehead, 59 Flannelmouth).
- Tagged fish were recaptured at a higher proportion than control fish for both species. This is most likely because all PIT tagging was done before the caudal punches were applied, so recapture differences were not caused by tagging.
- Mean time from tagging to final recapture was 36.8 ± 7.2 days for Bluehead Sucker and 35.5 ± 9.3 days for Flannelmouth Sucker.
- Several recaptures were excluded because fish were found on the upstream weir fence less than 24 hours after tagging.

**Species differences**
- Flannelmouth Suckers had lower recapture rates than Bluehead Suckers, for both tagged and control fish. The authors attribute this to behavior: Flannelmouth Suckers left the stream in greater numbers during the May 6–23 trap gap, not to tagging effects.
- Tagged Flannelmouth Suckers were significantly longer than tagged Bluehead Suckers (P < 0.0001).
- Among control fish, Flannelmouth Suckers were also significantly longer than Bluehead Suckers.

**Sex and tag expulsion**
- Sex was assigned from expressed milt or eggs. Fish with nuptial tubercles but no expressed gametes were suspected males, and those without tubercles or gametes were suspected females.
- Confirmed females made up 3.9% (Bluehead) and 8.6% (Flannelmouth) of the sample. Suspected females made up 40.7% and 34.6%.
- Among 242 confirmed and suspected females, none shed a PIT tag. Few tagged fish were observed expressing eggs, and many appeared to be spent, suggesting females spawned while retaining their tags.

**Long-term retention and survival**
- Some Bluehead and Flannelmouth suckers tagged in earlier years were recaptured with tags still in place, some after up to ten years.
- Razorback Sucker, a related catostomid, retained abdominal or dorsal PIT tags for 511 days in captivity (Burdick and Hamman 1993), supporting the expectation of high long-term retention in these species.
- Survival of tagged fish was not directly measured. Few fatalities were found shortly after handling, and the authors report no evidence of tag-related mortality.

**Conclusions**
- Abdominally implanted PIT tags are an effective, low-loss marking method for spawning Bluehead and Flannelmouth suckers in short-term field studies.
- The data support earlier assumptions of unaltered survival, tag retention, and behavior in PIT-tagging studies of these species.
- Long-term retention was not directly tested, but evidence from previously tagged fish suggests tags remain in place for years.
