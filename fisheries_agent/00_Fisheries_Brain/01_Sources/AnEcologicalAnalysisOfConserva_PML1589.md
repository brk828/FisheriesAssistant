# Ecological Analysis of Conservation Priorities in the Sonoran Desert Ecoregion

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ecoregional-conservation

## Executive Summary

This April 2000 report, prepared by The Nature Conservancy Arizona Chapter, the Sonoran Institute, and IMADES (Sonora), presents a two-year bi-national analysis of the [[Sonoran Desert Ecoregion]], which covers about 55 million acres (22 million ha) across Arizona, California, Sonora, and Baja California. The team selected 353 Conservation Targets from six taxonomic groups (amphibians/reptiles, birds, fish, invertebrates, mammals, plants) plus 78 natural vegetation communities, and used numeric Conservation Criteria to identify a network of 100 landscape-scale and 79 Special Element Sites. The network covers 23,108,106 acres (9,355,508 ha). The landscape-scale sites capture 89% of the ecoregion's biophysical variation. Fewer than 20% of the network is managed to promote long-term persistence of targets, and only 9% of the aquatic-related sites are so managed.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Identify a science-based network of Conservation Sites that, if properly managed, would keep most of the ecoregion's biodiversity viable over the long term. The results are meant to frame regional projects, proposals, and budgets.
- **Scale and richness:** The ecoregion holds a high proportion of endemic plants, reptiles, and fish. It has over 2,500 pollinator species (including the highest known bee diversity in the world) and more than 500 bird species that breed, migrate through, or reside there.
- **Human context:** Population was 6.9 million in 1990, nearly double 1970 levels, and was projected to reach about 12 million by 2020. Threats include habitat conversion, overuse of surface and groundwater, loss of hydrologic regimes, recreation, and exotic species.
- **Subdivisions:** Four biotic subdivisions structured the analysis: [[Lower Colorado River Valley]] (about 30 million acres), [[Arizona Uplands]] (14.4 million acres), [[Plains of Sonora]] (8.4 million acres), and [[Central Gulf Coast]] (about 2.3 million acres).
- **Land tenure:** In Mexico most land is ejido or communal, and a small share has official protection. In the U.S. portion most lands are public, with river corridors often in private ownership.
- **Methods:** The team combined [[Natural Heritage Program]] databases (3,547 records), expert-nominated polygons from a May 1998 workshop at the University of Arizona (106 experts from 51 agencies and organizations), spatial land and vegetation data, and a [[Biophysical Model]] built from climate, vegetation, elevation, slope, and aspect.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Coarse filter:** Natural vegetation communities (78 associations) were grouped into 23 ecological groups. Examples include Palo Verde-Mixed Cacti Scrub, Creosote-Bursage Scrub, Desert Riparian Woodland, Interior Riparian Marsh, and Coastal Marsh.
- **Fine filter:** Species Conservation Targets were ranked with NatureServe-style Global Ranks (G1 to G5). Appendix 1 lists the full set of targets; for example, the Gila topminnow, Yuma clapper rail, and southwestern willow flycatcher are among those listed as federally endangered.
- **Aquatic systems:** No comprehensive aquatic community classification exists. Aquatic elements were captured mainly through fine-filter fish targets, nearly all native fish species were included. Fish are described as among the most threatened entities in the ecoregion. Aquatic groups included seeps, springs, playas, intermittent streams, and riparian and wetland communities.
- **Ecological gradients:** Ecological gradient targets were added to represent variation in landforms, elevation, soils, and vegetation, based on the assumption that maintaining that variability supports genetic diversity and species range shifts.
- **Historic baseline:** Historic extent for matrix communities was estimated for circa 1600 to 1800, before widespread European settlement.
- **Climate and geography:** Rainfall is bimodal, with winter frontal and summer convectional storms. Most of the desert lies below 2,600 ft. The [[Gran Desierto]] is the largest desert dune system in North America.

## Key Findings

- **Network of sites:** 100 landscape-scale Conservation Sites (41 in Arizona Uplands, 35 in Lower Colorado River Valley, 16 in Plains of Sonora, 8 in Central Gulf Coast) cover about 26.6 million acres (10.8 million ha), or about 50% of the ecoregion's landmass. Sites range from 403 acres to 5.7 million acres.
- **Special Element Sites:** 79 sites (44 in Lower Colorado River Valley, 32 in Arizona Uplands, 3 in Plains of Sonora) contain 99 occurrences, including 58 plants, 16 reptiles and amphibians, 11 fish, 5 birds, 4 invertebrates, and 3 mammals. 81% of occurrences are G1 or G2.
- **Biophysical representation:** The landscape-scale sites captured 89% of biophysical variation. 28 biophysical units remained under-represented, mostly in north-central Sonora (Arizona Uplands and Plains of Sonora).
- **Criteria met:** Criteria were met or exceeded for 10 of 23 ecological groups. Examples include Palo Verde-Mixed Cacti Scrub, Creosote-Bursage Scrub, Coastal Marsh, Coastal Mangrove Forest, Sinaloan/Foothills Thornscrub, and California Fan Palm Oasis. Criteria were not met for groups such as Desert Riparian Woodland (viability needs site review) and Semi-Desert Grassland (only six extant examples).
- **Fish targets:** Criteria for fish were departed from. The adopted goal was to maintain native fish in stream reaches where they occur, because of the precarious status of these targets. Table 11 lists the number of sites where fish targets are found or could be restored. Of 25 fish targets, a few occur in many sites (for example, Gila chub and desert pupfish), while others occur in one or few sites.
- **Management status:** Overall, 11% of the ecoregion is GAP status 1 or 2 (highest protection), 22% is status 3, and 67% is status 4. Of the Conservation Sites, 19% are status 1 or 2, 25.6% status 3, and 55.4% status 4. For the 18 riverine, riparian, and aquatic sites, 9% are status 1 or 2, 16.4% status 3, and 74.5% status 4.
- **Land ownership:** 87% of U.S.-side sites are federal or state public lands. In Sonora and Baja, 12.6 million acres were identified, including 2.7 million acres in Biosphere Reserves.
- **Major stressors:** Experts identified exotic and invasive plants and animals, recreation, residential and commercial development, and improper livestock management as major stressors at more than half of the 100 sites. Groundwater extraction and surface-water diversion were also key stressors at riverine sites.
- **Recommendations:**
  - Abate stressors on lands already designated for conservation, supporting adaptive management.
  - Integrate ecoregional data into multiple-use land management plans.
  - Develop collaborative programs on private lands, including the Partners for Wildlife Program and Sonora's [[Unidades de Manejo Ambiental]] (UMA) system.
  - Evaluate management at Conservation Sites and establish an exotic-species council.
  - Implement ecosystem monitoring based on stressors, not only species population counts.
- **Pilot projects:** Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument eradicated nearly 100 tons of [[Buffel Grass]] from 25 square miles over two years. The BLM Algodones Dunes project monitors special-status plants in relation to off-highway vehicle use.
- **Data gaps:** The report stresses that inventory is incomplete, especially for invertebrates, rare plants, and aquatic systems, and that the boundaries are conceptual starting points rather than final reserve designs.
