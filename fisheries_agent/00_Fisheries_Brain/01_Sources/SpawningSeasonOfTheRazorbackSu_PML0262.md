# Spawning Season of the Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus) in Lake Mohave, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study documents the full spawning season of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the [[Colorado River Basin]] that holds the largest remaining population of the species in the basin. Using visual observation, [[Trammel Net Sampling]], and night larval collections over 1982 and 1983, the authors found that spawning ran from November through May, larval collections were limited to November through March, and peak spawning occurred January through March at water temperatures of roughly 10–15 °C. The authors conclude that spawning conditions in the reservoir are adequate for reproduction, but recruitment of larvae into the adult population appears to be low, and the causes remain unresolved.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was once widespread and abundant in the Colorado River system but has declined markedly over the past 50 years. Upper Colorado River populations are scattered, and the largest known population there was estimated at fewer than 1,000 individuals. In the lower basin, the species is abundant only in Lake Mohave and rare elsewhere. Earlier work had placed the spawning season at January through May, but no prior study had described the complete season within a single year.

Lake Mohave was formed after the construction of [[Davis Dam]] in 1951. The reservoir extends about 108 km from [[Hoover Dam]] to Davis Dam. Its upper riverine reach (about 40 km per the text) has current velocities up to 2.1 m/s during high discharge, and water temperatures there stay at 12–13 °C because of hypolimnetic releases from Hoover Dam. Downstream, the lentic habitat spans the Little and Cottonwood basins, where convective heat loss lowers temperatures to about 10 °C in December and January. The [[Six Mile Area]] in Cottonwood Basin is the widest point of the reservoir (6.4 km) and is exposed to strong seasonal wave action. [[Arizona Bay]] and [[Yuma Cove]] are more sheltered, with northerly exposure, and gravel substrates. [[Tequila Cove]] is another spawning site near the Six Mile Area.

Methods included monthly visual surveys from December 1981 through August 1983 (by shore, day observation, and SCUBA), trammel netting with 9.14 × 1.8 m nets (3.8 cm inner mesh, 25.4 cm outer mesh), and night larval collection with dive lights and dip nets. Catch was standardized as fish per 100 m of net per 24 hr. Condition factor followed [[Condition Factor]] calculations after Lagler (1956), and sex was assigned using [[Sexual Dimorphism]] characters described by Minckley (1983). Larval spawning dates were back-calculated from stage of development and reservoir temperature, using laboratory-derived [[Larval Development Rates]]. The study was supported by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. The source file also includes journal reviewer comments and proof-routing pages, which are administrative and not summarized here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Spawning fish used shallow gravel shorelines, usually less than 2 m deep. Spawning behavior involved one to eight males chasing a female, then one or two males pressing laterally against her while vibrating; this flushed fine substrate and formed depressions. No prior substrate preparation was observed.

The authors describe [[Wave Action]] and reservoir drawdown (about 3–5 m) as regenerating gravel substrates by winnowing silt and sand and depositing new material from flash floods, while spawning activity itself helped keep areas silt-free. This contrasts with [[Gravel Spawning Substrate]] loss through compaction, which Loudermilk (1985) had identified as a major cause of poor reproduction in most reservoirs.

Other fishes in spawning areas included [[Channel Catfish]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Rainbow Trout]], Lahontan cutthroat trout, and [[Striped Bass]]. Channel catfish and threadfin shad were seen mainly at night. Green sunfish appeared offshore in spawning areas after March. Potential [[Introduced Species]] effects, including predation, were proposed as contributors to [[Recruitment Failure]], but direct evidence was not yet available.

## Key Findings

**Spawning season and timing**
- Razorback suckers spawned from November through May. Larvae were collected only from spawning activity between November and March.
- Water temperature was 13 °C in November at the start of spawning, fell to 9.5 °C in December–January, and rose to about 20 °C by May when spawning ended.
- Peak spawning (ripeness, activity, and larval collections) occurred from January through March at 10–15 °C.
- Spawning in November and December had not been previously reported. The authors suggest a fall maturation and spawning pattern exists in the reservoir.

**Population and sampling**
- Visual surveys in February 1982 counted about 7,600 spawning razorback suckers, accounting for 99.7% of fish observed at spawning sites. The total is approximate because the scan is partly illegible. Razorback suckers were seen at 129 locations, with the largest concentration (about 3,300 fish) in the Six Mile Area in water less than 2 m deep.
- Trammel nets captured 1,049 razorback suckers, including unmeasured individuals, across the 1982–1983 seasons. Catch in Arizona Bay (January–April) was 84.3% razorback suckers, and in Six Mile coves (February–April) it was 71.3%.
- Twelve hybrid razorback suckers (*X. texanus* × *Catostomus latipinnis*) were also netted.
- All trammel-netted razorback suckers were adults.

**Size, sex, and condition**
- Females were significantly longer than males in both years (Mann-Whitney U-test, p = 0.001). Mean total length was 554.1 mm (males) and 626.3 mm (females) in 1982, and 554.7 mm (males) and 625.4 mm (females) in 1983.
- Males outnumbered females, usually making up over 50% of netted fish. The highest male ratio was 79.2% in November and May 1982.
- Condition factors were generally higher in females. Male condition declined in March 1982 and stayed low until the next November.

**Reproductive state**
- At the start of spawning in November 1982, 89.5% of males and 20% of females were ripe.
- From January through March, more than 50% of both sexes were ripe. Ripe males remained in May after females had stopped spawning.
- Eggs from ripe females collected in November were fertilized and developed normally under laboratory conditions.

**Larval collections**
- Total of 1,205 larvae collected: 48 in 1982 (February, Arizona Bay and Six Mile Area) and 1,157 in 1983 (November–May).
- In March 1983, the Six Mile Area was vacated after storms, and redds appeared destroyed by wave action. No larvae were collected there. Larvae were still found at protected [[Tequila Cove]] in March. No larvae were collected after March.
- Larvae collected in January 1983 were estimated to have been spawned between late November and mid-December, matching observed spawning and ripe fish.

**Conclusions and open questions**
- Spawning conditions (gravel substrate, temperature, wave hydrodynamics) appear adequate for successful reproduction, and hundreds of larvae were produced.
- Recruitment into the adult population appears low, and the authors cite earlier work showing food does not limit larval survival in Lake Mohave.
- Predation by introduced species is plausible but unconfirmed. The authors call for direct evidence on why larvae do not transition to the juvenile stage, so that management can improve long-term survival of this rare species.
