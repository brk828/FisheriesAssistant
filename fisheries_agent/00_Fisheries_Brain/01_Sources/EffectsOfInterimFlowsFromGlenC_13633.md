# Effects of Interim Flows from Glen Canyon Dam on the Aquatic Resources of the Lower Colorado River, Diamond Creek to Lake Mead (Quarterly Report No. 3)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This quarterly report covers Trip No. 3 (December 1–13, 1992) of a monitoring investigation of the aquatic resources of the [[Colorado River]] from [[Diamond Creek]] (RM 226) to Lake Mead below Pierce Ferry (RM 286). The project was led by the [[Hualapai Wildlife Management Department]] with technical support from [[BIO/WEST Inc]] and the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program. Its purpose is to assess how [[Interim Flows]] from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] affect fish populations, habitat, non-native fish interactions and food resources.

Sampling used six gear types and yielded 1,593 fish of 11 species in eight families. Only two species, flannelmouth sucker and speckled dace, are native. The nine others are non-native. The endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were not captured. Water temperatures, river stage fluctuations and 30 drift samples were also recorded. Water-quality data from the Hydrolab instruments were not yet available and were deferred to the Annual Report.

## Background & Description

Before [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed in 1963, the Grand Canyon mainstem supported eight native fish species. Of these, [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Bonytail]] have been extirpated from Grand Canyon. Humpback chub and razorback sucker are federally endangered. Flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker and speckled dace remain but are reduced in distribution and numbers.

The study area was divided into four longitudinal strata reflecting different habitat complexes:

| Stratum | River Miles | General Character |
|---|---|---|
| A | 226–239 | Steep, narrow canyon; swift runs; boulder and cobble substrate |
| B | 239–252 | Steep canyon with lake sediment deposits; major tributaries include [[Spencer Canyon]] and [[Lost Creek]] |
| C | 252–265 | Wide canyon with vegetated sand/silt deposits; backwaters |
| D | 265–286 | Open, braided delta and lake inflow; low gradient; side channels |

Strata A and D were judged to have habitat suitable for humpback chub and razorback sucker, respectively, though neither was found.

Trip No. 3 used six camp sites (Bridge Canyon, above Spencer, below Lost Creek, Burnt Spring, the braided section at RM 267.5, and Scorpion Island). Ten personnel took part: three from the Hualapai department, four BIO/WEST biologists and three OARS river guides.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Mead and Lake Powell have strongly reshaped the fish community. Since the completion of Hoover Dam (1935) and Glen Canyon Dam (1963), many species were introduced as sport or forage fish, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Black Crappie]] and [[Walleye]]. Bait-trade species such as [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] also spread widely.

Key environmental observations:

- **Water temperature:** Mainstem temperatures ranged from a minimum of 8.6 °C (Bridge Canyon) to a maximum of 14.0 °C over the trip (Executive Summary). Site-level minima and maxima were 8.6–10.2 °C. The Lake Mead inflow was slightly cooler, which was attributed to cooler air temperature over the open water near Pierce Ferry.
- **Stage fluctuation:** Net stage changes were 42 cm at Bridge Canyon (5.9 hours in the Executive Summary; Table 10 gives 23.7 hours) and 56 cm above Spencer Canyon over about 10 hours. Maximum changes were 42 cm (Bridge Canyon), 56 cm (above Spencer), 38 cm (below Lost Creek) and 25 cm (Burnt Spring).
- **Vegetation and algae:** Green algae (*Cladophora glomerata*) clogged nets and limited set times to 4–6 hours. Riparian vegetation included willow, tamarisk and *Baccharis*.
- **Drift:** 30 drift samples were taken at three sites during rising and falling flows to test drift volume against stage.

## Key Findings

**Fish community**
- 1,593 fish of 11 species were captured. Native species were only flannelmouth sucker (14 fish: 2 adults, 3 juveniles, 9 young-of-year of 52–83 mm TL, mostly in Separation Canyon) and speckled dace (9 fish, mainly Spencer Canyon).
- The most abundant species were [[Red Shiner]] (885), [[Fathead Minnow]] (486), [[Common Carp]] (120), [[Plains Killifish]] (28), [[Mosquitofish]] (24) and [[Channel Catfish]] (21).
- Minor captures were green sunfish (2), striped bass (3) and walleye (1).
- Red shiners and fathead minnows dominated tributaries, while carp and channel catfish were the main mainstem species.
- Humpback chub and razorback sucker were not captured on any trip. Bluehead sucker was also absent. Habitat in Strata A and D was judged suitable for these species, so the absence is notable.

**Gear effectiveness**
- [[Electrofishing]] and 1.5-inch [[Trammel Net]]s were most effective for large and medium fish on the mainstem.
- [[Hoop Net]]s were of limited success in side channels and tributary mouths.
- [[Seine]]s were most effective for small fish in shallow shoreline habitats and tributaries.
- Sampling totaled 217 gear deployments, with the most in electrofishing (32), trammel nets (TK 48, TL 40) and seines (32).

**Recommendations for future trips**
- Sample Lost Creek slough, Spencer Creek and Separation Creek for razorback sucker in early spring.
- Sample the braided region (RM 267–270) more intensively.
- Monitor young-of-year suckers in Spencer and Surprise canyons and collect specimens to check for young razorback sucker.
- Sample all tributaries to define the extent of red shiner distribution.
- Preserve channel catfish intestinal tapeworms to test for Asian tapeworm.
- Use angling and trotlines in deep holes, and place remote cameras at backwaters and tributaries.
- Document riparian vegetation, macroinvertebrates and fish parasites to characterize habitat.

**Planned outputs**
- Hydrolab water-quality results, sediment and food-base analyses, and drift volumes (dry weight per 100 m³) are to be reported in the 1992 Annual Report (due January 1993). The Final Project Report is due February 1994.
