# Urban Sprawl and the Degradation of US Rivers: The Fox River Case (1999)

#aquatic-ecology #freshwater-ecology #water-pollution #urban-sprawl

## Executive Summary

This 1999 USA TODAY news article by Traci Watson examines how suburban development ("sprawl") is threatening US rivers. Its main case study is the [[Fox River]] in northeastern Illinois, which flows through [[Kane County]] and [[McHenry County]], two of the fastest-growing counties in the state. Development is increasing the volume and pollutant load of stormwater runoff, and nutrient-laden effluent from sewage treatment plants is driving algae blooms. The article also reports that [[American Rivers]] listed six of its ten most endangered rivers for 1999 as threatened at least in part by sprawl, a first for the organization, where earlier threats had mostly been mining and dredging. The document does not present new scientific data. Its figures come from advocacy groups, state agencies, and the US Census Bureau.

## Background & Description

The article is a newspaper feature dated Monday, April 12, 1999. It opens at the Fox River, which runs through some of the USA's richest topsoil. The land along the river was once farmland, but the article reports that new subdivisions and strip malls have replaced cornfields in a short time. Nancy Williamson of [[Friends of the Fox River]] describes this change: "Four years ago, this was all cornfields."

The Fox sits roughly 25 miles west of [[Chicago]]. The article notes that Kane and McHenry counties are among the fastest-growing counties in Illinois, according to the [[US Census Bureau]].

The piece places the Fox within a national pattern. [[American Rivers]], an advocacy group, publishes an annual list of the nation's ten most endangered rivers. Its president, [[Rebecca Wodder]], says the organization did not previously recognize how widespread the impact of sprawl was.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stormwater runoff.** The article explains how paved surfaces change river hydrology. Pavement does not absorb rain, so it sends runoff into streams faster and in larger volumes than normal. This abnormal flow reshapes the riverbed and increases flooding. The runoff also carries toxins, including oil and gas from streets, pesticides from lawns, and pet waste. The article notes that a river can be damaged by sprawl even if no water is withdrawn from it.

**Nutrient loading and algae.** The stretch of the Fox between Elgin and Aurora has high levels of [[Nitrogen]] and [[Phosphorus]], according to tests by the local [[Sierra Club]] chapter. Both are contaminants found in treated sewage, and both encourage algae growth. The article reports that the Fox turns "pea-green" in August and September. Retired chemistry professor Gil McArdle, who conducts the Sierra Club's water sampling, is quoted describing this color.

**Sewage treatment.** The river's sewage plants treat wastewater before discharge, but the article reports that they are struggling to keep up with growth. The river's water is already overloaded with pollutants found in sewage.

**Water withdrawal (Bear River).** The article notes that the Bear River in the Salt Lake City region flows into Bear River Bay, which supports hundreds of thousands of birds. The river is being drained by the region's rapidly rising water demand, much of it for lawns.

**Biodiversity (Alabama-Coosa-Tallapoosa).** The Alabama-Coosa-Tallapoosa River Basin in Georgia and Alabama is home to numerous species of mussels, fish, and snails. Local officials are considering the basin as a source of water for northern Georgia. One proposal calls for a major dam, which American Rivers says would cause serious harm to the river's flow and biodiversity.

**Other listed rivers.** The article's listing of other endangered rivers is partly illegible in the scan, so only the Bear River and Alabama-Coosa-Tallapoosa are described in detail.

## Key Findings

- **Sprawl is now a leading threat to US rivers.** Six of the ten rivers on American Rivers' 1999 most-endangered list are threatened at least in part by sprawl. This is the first year so many were under development pressure, compared with earlier years when mining and dredging dominated.
- **Projected pollutant increase in the Fox.** The McHenry County Defenders estimate that by 2010, 55% more pollutants will be washed into the Fox each year than in 1990.
- **Nutrient-driven algae blooms.** Sierra Club testing finds high nitrogen and phosphorus between Elgin and Aurora, which fuel algae blooms each late summer.
- **State agency assessment.** A report from the Illinois Office of Surface Water Resources says the Fox suffers from algae blooms and excessive sewage pollution. It predicts the situation will worsen unless sewage treatment improves.
- **Regulatory response.** According to the article, the Illinois Environmental Protection Agency plans to upgrade current sewage plants or build new ones. The Sierra Club argues this is not good enough.
- **Predicted ecological outcome.** Jack Darin of the Sierra Club Illinois chapter warns that continued sprawl will bring "more algae blooms (and) a decrease in aquatic life."
- **Hydrological and chemical impacts.** Impervious surfaces increase runoff volume and speed, alter streambed form, increase flooding, and deliver oil, gas, pesticides, and pet waste into waterways.
- **Water-use pressure.** Water demand for lawns and growth threatens the Bear River and its downstream bird habitat in Utah.
- **Biodiversity risk.** A proposed dam on the Alabama-Coosa-Tallapoosa Basin threatens its mussel, fish, and snail communities.
