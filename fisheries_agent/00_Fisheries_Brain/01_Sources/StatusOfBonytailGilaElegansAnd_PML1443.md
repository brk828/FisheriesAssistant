# Status of Bonytail and Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1991 paper, presented at the Desert Fishes Council meeting at Death Valley National Monument, summarizes the status of two federally endangered endemic Colorado River fishes, [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], in [[Lake Mohave]]. The authors, Paul C. Marsh and W.L. Minckley of Arizona State University, base their update on collections made over the past decade. Razorback sucker populations appear stable in abundance, size and condition, but recruitment beyond the larval stage has not been documented, which threatens long-term persistence. Bonytail is rarer, with no quantitative abundance estimates, and its status is heavily influenced by hatchery augmentation stockings.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), both endemic "large-river" fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Protection:** Both species are federally listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS). Bonytail is considered among the most critically imperiled western fishes.
- **Study site:** [[Lake Mohave]] is an artificial impoundment on the Colorado River mainstream, created in the early 1950s by closure of [[Davis Dam]]. It holds the largest known populations of both species and is the only habitat where razorback sucker can be reliably captured at virtually any time of year.
- **Prior work cited:** Minckley (1983) on razorback sucker; the USFWS (1990) bonytail recovery plan; Minckley et al. (1991) on recovery management.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Razorback sucker** spawning in Lake Mohave has been documented repeatedly and observed annually since the 1970s. Peak timing varies with weather and lake conditions, but no overall change in spawning intensity was detected over 20 years. Larvae are abundant in spring.
- **Competing and threatening species:** Abundance trends in razorback sucker track other species; CPE increased for [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) and decreased for [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*). Proposed causes of poor recruitment include predation by non-native fishes, nutritional constraints, and drift from the system.
- **Hatchery augmentation:** More than 130,000 bonytail (including larvae and young adults up to 16.5 cm TL) were stocked from 1981 onward, complicating assessment of wild populations.
- **Basin context:** Bonytail is considered so rare basin-wide that the immediate recovery goal is to prevent extinction.

## Key Findings

**Razorback Sucker**

- Individual and population characteristics showed no detectable difference between 1974–1981 and 1981–present data.
- Trammel net catch per unit effort (CPE) has stayed near 2.5 fish per 100 m² of netting per 24 hours over 18 years, with seasonal lows in summer. CPE is recommended as the standard status indicator.
- Mark-recapture estimates: about 4,400 adults marked since 1981 (most since 1988); roughly 60,000 fish occupy the reservoir, with indistinguishable annual estimates over the past 5 years.
- Mean total length: males about 55 cm and females about 62 cm TL, based on nearly 1,000 fish. Weight, anomalies, injury, disease, parasites and eye condition showed no detectable change over the past decade.
- **Recruitment failure:** Apart from 4 juveniles captured in 1987 by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], there is no evidence of recruitment beyond the larval stage. The authors warn that, unless remedied, the lake population may be lost within roughly one decade.

**Bonytail**

- No quantitative abundance estimates exist; fish are too uncommon for meaningful CPE or mark-recapture data.
- Reproduction in Lake Mohave has not been observed, though recruitment was documented by capture of one relatively young wild fish.
- A total of 57 bonytail were captured in Lake Mohave between 1974 and 1991. The largest single samples were 8 fish (March 1981 and May 1988) and 9 fish (May 1990). None were captured in 1975, 1977–78, 1982, 1984 or 1987.
- Since a 1988 USFWS request for brood fish, 34 bonytail were captured: 23 wild adults were sent to USFWS to augment brood stock, 7 sub-adults were released, and 4 died in the field.
- Of 39 bonytail captured since 1981, 11 were identified as stocked fish, so hatchery-origin fish may be indistinguishable from wild fish when unmarked.
- The recovery plan's priority tasks include capturing wild bonytail in Lake Mohave, estimated at $50,000 per year.
- The authors note that the return rate of stocked bonytail from Lake Mohave is remarkable compared with the low recapture rate of razorback sucker and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] released into the Gila River system, and recommend that resource agencies take note.

**Overall Conclusion**

The razorback sucker population remains stable but is not replacing itself, while bonytail depends on continued hatchery augmentation and careful monitoring to distinguish wild from stocked fish.
