# Latest Miocene to Pliocene Transition from Basin-Range Tectonic to Post-Tectonic Landscape Evolution in Southeastern Arizona

#geology #geomorphology #basin-range-tectonics

## Executive Summary

This paper by Menges and McFadden argues that the main phase of [[Basin and Range Province|Basin-Range]] vertical tectonism in two southeastern Arizona basins ended within a latest Miocene to early Pliocene interval of roughly 6 to 3 m.y.B.P. Evidence comes from [[Basin-Fill Stratigraphy]], [[Bedrock Pediment|bedrock pediments]], high-level alluvial fans, [[Quaternary Terraces]], paleomagnetism and soils. The study basins are [[Sonoita Creek Basin]] and [[Canada del Oro Basin]]. Tectonic quiescence has continued since then, with only small-scale Pliocene-Quaternary deformation. The authors attribute later landscape change mainly to nontectonic climatic and drainage-integration processes.

The document contains no fish, invertebrate or other aquatic biological data. Its aquatic relevance is limited to fluvial and drainage processes.

## Background & Description

The mountain and valley relief of southern Arizona was long linked to [[Basin and Range Province|Basin-Range]] faulting. Earlier workers dated the inception of this faulting to about 17 to 10 m.y.B.P. Estimates of its termination differ widely. Some workers extended activity into recent time, while Scarborough and Peirce (1978) proposed cessation before the mid-Pleistocene. The authors note that southeastern Arizona's rugged ranges and strong structure-topography conformity can give a false impression of recent tectonism. Several Quaternary piedmont fault scarps add to that impression.

The study uses the time boundaries in Table 1. Miocene is divided into early (24–16 m.y.), mid (16–11 m.y.) and late (11–5.6 m.y.). Pliocene is divided into early (5.6–3.2 m.y.) and late (3.2–1.8 m.y.). Pleistocene and Holocene follow.

The two basins lie in the [[Mexican Highland]] subprovince:
- **Sonoita Creek basin:** about 60 km southeast of Tucson, at 1,200–1,600 m altitude.
- **Canada del Oro basin:** about 25 km northwest of Tucson, at 900–1,450 m altitude.

Both are north- to northeast-trending, have thermic semiarid climates, and drain to the [[Santa Cruz River]]. Gravity studies show subsurface bedrock grabens with master boundary faults that have throws of 1,200–1,500 m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's environmental focus is geomorphic and climatic rather than biological. Key elements include:

- **Drainage:** Both basins have downcut into their basin fill. Their present drainage was integrated with the regional network, and the authors propose that stream piracy across low divides was the main trigger for Quaternary basin dissection. A base-level fall of this kind is sufficient to explain the observed dissection without invoking regional uplift.
- **Climate:** Pediment fans are linked to Quaternary climatic shifts, especially freeze-thaw weathering during glacial periods and hillslope stripping at the start of interglacials. Climate also controls the sediment-transport capacity of streams.
- **Vegetation and fauna:** The only biological evidence is a fossil equid tooth (*Neohipparion* or *Cormohipparion*) reported from a well about 1.5 km south of the magnetostratigraphic section. Its Hemphillian–Clarendonian age supports the proposed correlation.
- **Regional context:** Basin-fill deposition in the San Pedro Valley was paludal to lacustrine (upper Quiburis and/or lower St. David formations). The authors do not treat aquatic fauna in that setting.

## Key Findings

**1. Timing of tectonic termination**
- The principal Basin-Range disturbance ended in a latest Miocene to early Pliocene interval of about 6 to 3 m.y.B.P. in two basins near Tucson.
- Tectonic quiescence has continued to the present. Regional Basin-Range tectonism probably waned earlier in southeastern Arizona than in the southwest of the state.

**2. Sonoita Creek basin stratigraphy**
- Six informal units range from late Oligocene to late Pliocene. The four older units (Ts) are pre-Basin-Range and discordant with present structure. The two younger units (BF) are syntectonic or post-tectonic.
- Lower basin fill (BF~L~) is in high-angle fault contact with bedrock and may be up to 900–1,200 m thick in the subsurface graben.
- Upper basin fill (BF~U~) is 80–100 m thick, undeformed, and overlaps former fault zones.

**3. Magnetostratigraphy**
- A 51.3-m section near the top of BF~U~ was sampled at 26 horizons (mean interval 1.9 m) and measured in triplicate.
- The section records 45 unconformities and 34 paleosols, both increasing upsection.
- Polarity is best matched to the Epoch 5 through Gilbert Epoch sequence, giving an age range of 5.8 to 3.3 m.y.B.P.
- The section ends an estimated 15–25 m below the Martinez erosion surface, implying a minimum age of about 2.5–2.0 m.y. for the preserved BF~U~.

**4. Depositional rates**
- The mean sedimentation rate for BF~U~ is 19 m/m.y.
- Rates fall from about 30 m/m.y. in the lower section to about 15 m/m.y. in the upper section.
- Tucson and Picacho basins had rates of 390–210 and 520–260 m/m.y. respectively, roughly an order of magnitude higher.
- The decline is attributed to reduced tectonic subsidence and reduced relative vertical tectonic activity.

**5. Diastems and hiatuses**
- Most beds are bounded by diastem-like unconformities, often over paleosols. These imply hiatuses of 1,000 to 100,000 years.
- The increase in diastems and paleosols upsection is consistent with intermittent landscape stability in fluvial systems operating near a critical stream-power threshold over 2.5–3.8 m.y.

**6. Bedrock pediments**
- Extensive bedrock pediments extend 2–7 km (averaging about 3–5 km) mountainward from basin-bounding faults.
- Sonoita Creek pediments form two levels. The lower corresponds to upper basin fill, and the higher projects into the Martinez surface.
- Exposed bedrock in the Sonoita ranges shrinks from 622 km² today to 278 km² before dissection, a 55% reduction. The restored suballuvial pediments total 260 km².
- The authors treat this figure as a minimum, since gravity surveys suggest further pediments remain buried.
- A Canada del Oro pediment 2–4 km wide is etched across plutonic rocks. Pedimentation is slower on more resistant quartz diorite and metamorphic rocks.
- Pediment formation at the rate of about 1 km/m.y. for coarse-grained plutonic rocks implies minimum intervals of about 2 to 5 m.y.. Resistant silicic volcanic rocks would require longer.

**7. High basin stand fans**
- The Martinez surface (Sonoita) and the Cordonnes surface (Canada del Oro) are the highest geomorphic units in their basins.
- The Martinez fan gravels are 20 to 5 m thick. The Cordonnes gravels reach up to 100 m thick. Both contain boulders up to 1–3 m across.
- These fans are latest Pliocene to early Pleistocene (about 2 to 1 m.y.B.P.). Their soils indicate a minimum age of more than 1 m.y.
- Their thinness, association with pediments, and lack of deformation rule out a tectonic origin.

**8. Quaternary terraces**
- Sonoita Creek has eight composite terraces, each often made of 3–5 closely spaced surfaces. Canada del Oro has four major terrace levels.
- Terrace profiles are subparallel and extend undeformed across basin-bounding faults.
- Soil chronosequences in Canada del Oro suggest a mid-late Holocene to mid-Pleistocene age for post-Cordonnes units.

**9. Interpretation**
- Basin-fill stratigraphy and pedimentation record a progressive waning and eventual termination of large-scale vertical tectonism.
- Quaternary deformation is restricted to scattered piedmont scarps displacing late Pleistocene or older alluvium. These occur mainly within suballuvial pediment benches.
- Quaternary deformation is less frequent and of lower rate and magnitude than the earlier Basin-Range disturbance.
- Nontectonic climatic and geomorphic variables dominated Pliocene-Quaternary landscape evolution. Quaternary dissection was triggered by external base-level fall during basin integration.

[[Paleomagnetism]] and [[Paleosols]] are the main dating tools used.
