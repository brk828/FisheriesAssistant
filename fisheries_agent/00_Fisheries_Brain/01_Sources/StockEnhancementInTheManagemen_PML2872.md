# Stock Enhancement in the Management of Freshwater Fisheries: A European Perspective

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review condenses the 1982 [[EIFAC]] symposium on stock enhancement, held in Budapest, Hungary, in conjunction with the twelfth session of the European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission. The symposium examined two linked practices: stocking native fishes and introducing exotic species. Its aim was to define guidelines for both in European waters. The authors, R. L. Welcomme (FAO), C. C. Kohler and W. R. Courtenay Jr., report that most introductions were less successful than predicted and often carried risks to native communities. Stocking benefits were also found to be inconsistent. The symposium recommended a [[Code Of Practice]] for introductions and a Working Party on Stock Enhancement.

## Background & Description

**EIFAC history.** The European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission is a regional commission of the [[Food And Agriculture Organization]] (FAO) of the United Nations. It was founded in the 1950s to promote collaboration on inland fisheries, paralleling the International Council for the Exploration of the Sea, which handles marine fisheries. The first session was held in Helsinki, Finland, in July 1956. FAO accepted the proposal in June 1957 in Madrid, Spain, and EIFAC was established. Three subcommissions were formed at the third session in 1964: (I) Fisheries Biology and Management, (II) Fish Culture and Disease, and (III) Fish and Polluted Water. EIFAC meets every two years and includes 25 member nations.

**Symposium scope.** The symposium considered two topics in seven sessions:
1. Stocking with non-salmonids
2. Stocking with [[Brown Trout]]
3. Stocking with [[Atlantic Salmon]]
4. Introductions and transplantations: case histories
5. Introductions: country overviews and the [[Lake Kinneret]] case
6. Ecological and practical aspects
7. Conclusions and recommendations

Participants came from EIFAC members plus Bolivia, Canada, Egypt, the German Democratic Republic, Madagascar, the United States and Russia. The symposium was the first of two planned on management of European inland stocks. The second, on habitat modification, was scheduled for May 1984 in Aarhus, Denmark.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking native species.** Stocking benefits were often weak or conditional:
- In the Netherlands, pike populations depended mainly on available habitat, so habitat engineering was judged a better tool than stocking. In Finland, about 18 million pike fry and fingerlings were stocked in 1980 with little visible effect on catches.
- Bream growth was density-limited. Regular seining to remove up to 200 kg/hectare per year substantially improved growth of the remaining fish.
- [[Common Carp]] stocked in Finland since 1861 cannot survive their first winters in natural waters and must be overwintered indoors. Carp did not appear to harm other fish or the ecosystem.
- Stocked [[Whitefish]] in Finland produced variable results. In the Oulujoki watercourse, stocking of 109 small forest lakes gave recaptures in 91 lakes and net production in 56. Fingerlings produced higher returns than fry.

**Eel enhancement.** Dams and weirs have blocked upstream migration of [[European Eel]] for centuries, and some rivers have declined in recruitment. Stocking was generally judged profitable for eel fishers. Impacts on other species were disputed: Swedish representatives reported severe declines in crayfish where eels were heavily stocked, while Irish observations suggested eels dig up and eat trout eggs.

**Salmonid stocking.** Hatchery-reared [[Smolt]]s survive less well than wild smolts. Transport from four Finnish fish farms to coastal sites lowered blood oxygen-carrying capacity and energy reserves, and physiological imbalance persisted for 5 days. Survival of Atlantic salmon eggs and fry was reduced by winter floods, and by the presence of brown trout and older salmon.

**Exotic introductions.** Several species illustrate contrasting outcomes:
- [[Brook Trout]] in Norway thrived in acid waters with pH as low as 4.5, where it sometimes was the only fish present.
- [[Lake Trout]] in Finnish lakes performed best at 20–25 cm stocked into deep, clear lakes, and fed on coregonids and Arctic char.
- [[Coho Salmon]] escaped from French cage culture, with about 60,000 yearlings released into the Varenne River in 1974–1975. Upstream migration ceased after 1977.
- The [[Danubian Huchen]] was transplanted in 38 attempts, of which only 8 succeeded. It is now threatened within its European range.
- [[Silver Carp]] in a hypertrophic German Democratic Republic lake reduced epilimnetic zooplankton, which harmed pikeperch and perch fry. Harvesting silver carp in 1981 improved pikeperch survival.
- [[Grass Carp]] in Denmark was adversely affected by long ice cover. Growth in the first season varied from 23 to 46%.
- [[Pikeperch]] in Denmark created good stocks in about 70 lakes. In the Anglian region of the UK, accidental introduction was linked to a drastic decline in cyprinids, leading to a control programme.

**Crayfish and plague.** Crayfish plague, caused by *Aphanomyces astaci*, has been devastating native European crayfish since about 1860. Plague-resistant North American species, especially the [[Signal Crayfish]] (*Pacifastacus leniusculus*), were introduced from the 1970s onward. Europe now hosts four native and four exotic self-reproducing crayfish species.

**Country reviews.**
- France: 11 exotic fish species, of which pumpkinseed and black bullhead are nuisance species.
- Finland: at least 15 exotic fishes and one crayfish over about 130 years. Siberian whitefish, lake trout, tench, brown bullhead and signal crayfish established reproducing populations.
- Ireland: about 8 native species at the end of the last glacial age, now at least 20. Roach spread has been a nuisance.
- Romania: 15 non-native fishes since the last century, with grass carp introductions bringing four exotic parasites.
- United States: at least 96 exotic species introduced in the 48 contiguous states, mostly since World War II. Forty are established, and 168 native fishes have been transplanted beyond historical ranges.
- Lake Kinneret, Israel: 11 introduced species were listed. Rainbow trout, tench and European seabass failed to survive. Carp and *Tilapia galilaea* are established but supplemented by stocking.

**Niche theory.** Participants felt that introductions often assume vacant or potential niches. However, communities with strong interspecific relationships, which are typical of simple communities, are easily disrupted by exotics. Introductions into Lake Balkhash (USSR) succeeded because native species were quickly supplanted.

## Key Findings

1. **Introductions are usually unsuccessful.** Most transplantations performed below predicted benefits, and there is almost always a risk of negative influence on indigenous species.
2. **Stocking is not a universal fix.** Habitat, density and environmental quality often matter more. Stocking persistently in the Netherlands produced too many fish, and cessation of stocking in some areas left stocks stable or improved.
3. **Outcomes depend on context.** Success or failure of an introduction "is essentially a matter of local attitudes", as seen with grass carp and pikeperch.
4. **Exotics are not always rejected.** Exotic species may perform better in artificially altered waters, as with brook trout in Norwegian acid lakes.
5. **Legislation alone is insufficient.** Loopholes exist for scientific, experimental aquaculture and aquarium imports. Control over implementation and education of water users were identified as the key gaps.
6. **Recommendations.** The symposium recommended that EIFAC:
   - Adopt a code of practice to reduce the risk of adverse effects from introducing or transferring inland fish species.
   - Establish a Working Party on Stock Enhancement to review and recommend that code, and to prepare stocking guidelines for various species groups.
   - Decision-makers should conduct benefit/risk analyses based on ecological studies before stocking any non-indigenous fish.
   - Research on the ecology, behaviour and physiology of fishes should be increased.
