# Evaluation of a Long-Term Predator Removal Program: Abundance and Population Dynamics of Invasive Northern Pike in the Yampa River, Colorado

#ichthyology #Esocidae #invasive-species #endangered-species-recovery

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary:** [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) is a large-bodied, piscivorous species invasive to the upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. First introduced to the [[Yampa River]] basin during the 1970s, Northern Pike are now established and common throughout the system. Mean total length (TL) of captured individuals was 465 mm (range: 59–1,120 mm TL). The species exhibits high fecundity and longevity. Age-0 and age-1 fish comprised 36% of captures in upper reaches during 2012 sampling, demonstrating ongoing reproduction. Annual survival rates for mean-length (465 mm TL) Northern Pike varied by reach: highest downstream (mean = 0.54; range = 0.36–0.71), lowest upstream (mean = 0.25; range = 0.12–0.38), and intermediate in middle reaches (mean = 0.32; range = 0.18–0.48). Survival rates increased with TL up to 625 mm, then decreased for larger individuals, producing bell-shaped length-dependent survival curves. Growth was relatively slow; 81% of first recaptures occurred within the same year as initial captures, with within-year growth averaging only 4 mm.

**Habitat Associations:** Northern Pike occupy riverine habitats throughout 175 km of the Yampa River, from upstream reaches (Hayden to Craig, river kilometer [rkm] 275.2–216.0) through middle reaches (South Beach–Little Yampa Canyon–Juniper, rkm 216.0–146.4) to downstream reaches (Maybell–Sunbeam, rkm 142.7–94.1). The species also inhabits [[Yampa Canyon]] (rkm 72.1–0.0) and has dispersed into the [[Green River]], including [[Lodore Canyon]], Echo–Split, and middle Green River reaches. Off-channel habitats include reservoirs ([[Lake Catamount]], [[Stagecoach Reservoir]], [[Elkhead Reservoir]]) that serve as propagule sources. Spawning occurs in tributary confluences (e.g., Walton Creek) and other upstream locations. The Yampa River has a relatively natural hydrograph driven by spring snowmelt runoff, though small upstream reservoirs exist.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration:** Northern Pike exhibited predominantly downstream movement (90% of reach-changing individuals), with some extensive dispersal events. Of 1,911 individually marked fish captured multiple times (2001–2012), 21% changed reaches, while 73% remained in their initial capture reach. Total distance traveled ranged from 0.0 to 388.5 km (mean = 19.3 km). The maximum recorded movement was 388.5 km downstream from Lake Catamount to the middle Green River over an unspecified period. Northern Pike from the Yampa River moved into the Green River and established a reproducing population upstream of Lodore Canyon in Browns Park National Wildlife Refuge, approximately 56 km upstream from the confluence. Upstream movements were relatively rare (9% of reach changes) and short, covering only one or two reaches. The proportion of fish leaving their initial capture reaches declined from upstream to downstream: 34% left the above-Hayden reach, 19% left Hayden to Craig, 17% left South Beach–Little Yampa Canyon–Juniper, and 13% left Lily Park. Temporary emigration parameters (γ″ and γ′) in the top-ranked model were fixed at zero, indicating observable fish remained observable while unobservable fish remained unobservable, with minimal movement into or out of the study area during annual sampling periods. Approximately 121 Northern Pike (6% of multiply-captured individuals) escaped from off-channel translocation sites and were later recaptured in river reaches; 80% of these recaptures occurred in the Hayden to Craig reach adjacent to translocation ponds.

**Trophic & Community Interactions:** Northern Pike function as apex predators and compete directly with the endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). A 758-mm TL Northern Pike consumed an adult Colorado Pikeminnow estimated at 500 mm TL, demonstrating predation on large native fishes. Bioenergetics modeling ranked [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*) as the foremost problematic invasive species in the upper Colorado River basin and Northern Pike as second-most problematic due to high abundances, habitat overlap with native fishes, and ability to consume multiple life stages of native species. Northern Pike exhibit trophic adaptability, consuming a variety of species when preferred prey become less abundant, thus affecting multiple native fish species simultaneously. The species has effectively replaced Colorado Pikeminnow as the top predator in the middle Yampa River. Northern Pike threaten four endangered fishes: Colorado Pikeminnow, [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*).

**Environmental Drivers:** Mean May discharge of the Yampa River (USGS gauging station 09251000) was included as a covariate in model structures for capture probability but was not retained in the top-ranked model. Capture probability varied substantially by year (mean across all reaches and passes ranged from 0.13 in 2009 to 0.22 in 2008 for 465-mm fish), suggesting environmental conditions influence detectability. Escapement from reservoirs and spawning success appear linked to hydrologic conditions, though specific relationships were not quantified. Unlimited reproductive habitat and escapement of adults from reservoirs provide virtually unlimited sources of annual production to sustain the Yampa River population. Climate change and environmental disturbances that alter habitat may facilitate further expansion of this invasive species.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory:** Annual abundance estimates were highest in the upstream Hayden to Craig reach (range = 1,144–4,078 fish; density = 19–69 fish/km), followed by the middle South Beach–Little Yampa Canyon–Juniper reach (range = 819–3,499 fish; density = 12–50 fish/km), and lowest in the downstream Maybell–Sunbeam reach (range = 232–746 fish; density = 5–15 fish/km). Years of highest abundance were 2004, 2005, and 2009 in the upstream reach; 2004 and 2009 in the middle reach; and 2004, 2007, and 2009 in the downstream reach. Abundance estimates were relatively low in all reaches from 2006 to 2008. After removal each year, Northern Pike abundance increased annually by nearly 1,000% (mean = 993%; range = 136–3,974%) in the upstream reach, over 875% (mean = 878%; range = 57–2,988%) in the middle reach, and over 375% (mean = 388%; range = 52–975%) in the downstream reach due to recruitment and immigration. Northern Pike densities were 4–58 times higher than current Colorado Pikeminnow density (1.2 fish/km in 2008): 16–58 times higher in the upstream reach, 10–43 times higher in the middle reach, and 4–13 times higher in the downstream reach. The proportion of larger Northern Pike (>450 mm and >600 mm TL) generally declined throughout the study period in all reaches. By 2010, proportions of fish in both large size categories in the middle reach and those over 600 mm TL in the downstream reach declined to less than half of 2004 levels. Over 5,500 Northern Pike were removed from Lake Catamount during 2008–2010, twice the number removed from the three main Yampa River study reaches during the same period.

**Threat & Risk Assessment:** Northern Pike pose a substantial predatory threat to endangered fish recovery in the upper Colorado River basin. Colorado Pikeminnow abundance in the Yampa River declined from 2000–2003 to 2006–2008 and continues to decline, despite abundance increases in four other major population areas of the Green River basin during the same period. The invasive predator's high abundance, overlap in habitat use with native fishes, and ability to consume multiple life stages constitute major impediments to endangered species recovery. Escapement from reservoirs (Lake Catamount, Stagecoach Reservoir, Elkhead Reservoir) and former translocation sites provides persistent propagule pressure. One day of sampling in October 2009 at a known spawning location (Walton Creek confluence) captured many times the number of age-0 Northern Pike captured during weeks of downstream sampling, illustrating high reproduction in upstream reaches. The species' propensity for downstream movement increases risk of establishment in additional portions of the upper Colorado River basin. Northern Pike have spread from reservoirs, established reproducing populations, and are a primary focus of costly control efforts. The species has invaded hundreds of lakes and rivers in south-central Alaska, regionally extirpated native species, and threatened economically important salmonid populations, demonstrating its invasive potential across western North America.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations:** Current removal efforts are inadequate to reduce Northern Pike abundance because recruitment and immigration exceed removal and mortality. Removal rates averaged 31% (range = 9–48%) in the upstream reach, 30% (range = 13–52%) in the middle reach, and 32% (range = 19–44%) in the downstream reach. Removal resulted in only a 7% mean decrease in survival rates in the upstream reach and 8% in the middle reach, though it produced larger decreases (mean = 16%; range = 13–21%) in the downstream reach where it was likely the primary mechanism reducing numbers. Investigation of reproduction and recruitment is needed, along with source management, spawning disruption, and increased removal to further reduce abundance and aid native fish recovery. Well-timed, targeted sampling of age-0 and age-1 Northern Pike under various environmental conditions is necessary to identify sources, estimate recruitment rates, and understand factors advantaging reproduction. Control of off-channel propagule sources via screening, enclosure nets, or elimination should be implemented. Identification of spawning locations and disruption of spawning are critical. Removal efforts in Lake Catamount began in 2007 and continue annually. Translocation of Northern Pike has ceased. Current density targets (1.9 fish/km or equivalent to Colorado Pikeminnow density, whichever lower) are likely too high given residual main-channel propagules and propagule pressure outside critical habitat. Even higher mortality rates from removal and other causes—combined with reduced recruitment and immigration—are needed to reduce Northern Pike abundance from sustainable or increasing to a less-viable state. In lower-density downstream reaches with less recruitment and immigration, increased removal efforts could force density down to target levels, though suitability of even that level for native fish recovery is unknown. Continued removal remains important to reduce numbers of largest individuals, alleviate competition and predation, and restore the native apex predator. Decisions to forego tagging and use only removal should be made carefully, as robust evaluation of removal effects requires tagged fish.

Current Northern Pike densities exceed targets by 4–58 times across reaches.
