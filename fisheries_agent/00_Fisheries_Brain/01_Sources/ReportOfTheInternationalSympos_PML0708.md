# Proceedings Report: International Symposium on the Early Life History of Fish (Oban, May 1973)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ichthyoplankton

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Report (No. 161, restricted distribution, October 1973) is the proceedings-style record of the International Symposium on the Early Life History of Fish, held at [[Dunstaffnage Marine Research Laboratory]], Oban, Scotland, 17–23 May 1973. It was organized by IABO (International Association of Biological Oceanography) in cooperation with FAO, ICES, SCOR and the Steering Committee chaired by [[J.H.S. Blaxter]]. The symposium gathered 62 papers from 238 scientists in 25 countries.

The report summarizes the discussions of eight topical sessions (population studies, feeding and metabolism, behaviour, physiological ecology, taxonomy, distribution, rearing, and developmental events and manipulation), gives the general conclusions, and lists participants, papers and abstracts. Its central theme is that fish eggs and larvae are valuable tools for stock assessment, ecosystem understanding, aquaculture and pollution research, and that the stock–recruitment link is mediated by early-life processes that remain poorly understood.

## Background & Description

The symposium arose from several concerns:

- FAO and national efforts to estimate marine fish resources independently of catch data, where plankton surveys of eggs and larvae were seen as useful tools (earlier recommended by the FAO Advisory Committee on Marine Resources Research, ACMRR).
- Growing interest in stock–recruitment relationships in heavily exploited populations.
- Demand for reared larvae in pollution toxicity testing, aquaculture and genetics.

The meeting was preceded by a steering committee (Blaxter as chair and editor of the Proceedings) and was held in Oban with support from the Royal Society, the Scottish Marine Biological Association and FAO/ICES/SCOR. Travel funding was limited, and several scientists withdrew at short notice. The report also includes a speech by Sir Frederick Russell, who recalled the historical development of early-life-history studies from [[G.O. Sars]] onward, and paid tribute to pioneering workers such as Ehrenbaum, Garstang and Mcintosh.

The report notes the work of the [[ICES]] Working Group on Fish Eggs and Larvae and the value of [[Ichthyoplankton Surveys]] for long-term monitoring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Key environmental themes raised across sessions:

- **Stock–recruitment:** The lack of a simple relationship between spawning stock and recruitment is widely recognized. Explanations discussed include temperature effects on egg development and hatching time, and density-dependent mortality in early larvae (predation and food shortage). Food limitation is considered critical; Cushing and Jones argued that a food-limiting model could explain the high larval mortality (about 10% per day) seen in gadoids. The "critical period" concept of [[May]] (1973) was reviewed, with the conclusion that it cannot be generalized across species.
- **Temperature:** Bottom-up effects on hatching, growth and survival were repeatedly described. Cod year-class strength was linked to winter–spring sea temperatures, with cooling associated with weaker year classes.
- **Distribution and patchiness:** Plankton patches, frontal aggregations, island effects and upwelling were all shown to concentrate larvae. Larval distribution was linked to water masses, seamounts and currents.
- **Pollution:** Oil dispersants, cadmium, sewage and thermal discharges (power plants) were studied for effects on eggs and larvae. Herring eggs exposed to cadmium showed reduced hatching and survival; larvae avoided dispersants only at high concentrations.
- **Feeding:** Larvae were shown to be size-selective feeders, with food requirements varying by species. Mortality from starvation at sea was considered rare, though this was not fully resolved.

## Key Findings

**Population studies (Session 3.1)**
- Stock–recruitment relationships were often weak or absent, but correlations improved when temperature effects on hatching were considered (North Sea herring).
- Egg and larval surveys were judged to be a reliable alternative to catch statistics for long-term monitoring (e.g., Tanaka's Japanese sardine work; Gjøsæter and Saetre's Norwegian capelin estimates matched other assessments).
- Herring year-class strength in the Gulf of Riga was predicted from larval abundance, growth and condition factors.

**Feeding and metabolism (Session 3.2)**
- Larvae depend on live food: copepod nauplii and Artemia are important prey, and most early feeding is on small zooplankton.
- Starvation experiments showed larval herring and plaice lose energy and weight before death, with lipid depletion occurring first.

**Behaviour (Session 3.3)**
- Larval fish showed species-specific capture and feeding behaviour, with prey patch detection important.
- Anchovy larvae were shown to avoid predators at low light, and light influences feeding.

**Physiological ecology (Session 3.4)**
- Laboratory tolerance limits do not directly predict field tolerance; multifactorial experiments were recommended.
- Egg mortality at sea was high, and wave action was considered unlikely to be the main cause.

**Taxonomy (Session 3.5)**
- Larval characters often clarify family relationships better than adult characters. Gonostomatid and sternoptychid families were proposed to be combined into one family, Sternoptychidae, with two subfamilies (Sternoptychinae and Gonostomatinae).
- Pigmentation-based identification of tuna larvae (Thunnus, Makaira) was found to be unreliable compared with skeletal characters.

**Distribution (Session 3.6)**
- Herring larvae concentrate in front of islands rather than in eddies (Miller).
- Larval distribution in the Indian Ocean was driven by monsoon-related upwelling.

**Rearing (Session 3.7)**
- Rearing of many marine species is now feasible if parent fish can be obtained; live food (rotifers, Artemia) and microencapsulated diets are central.
- Sole larval survival depended on a dissolved organic substance in seawater (Fulcher's work), and this remains unresolved.

**Developmental events and manipulation (Session 3.8)**
- Temperature and salinity during incubation affect development, and triploid and gynogenetic fish were proposed for exotic introduction risk assessment.

**General conclusions (Section 4)**
- Priority research areas were identified: high egg mortality at sea, patchiness of food, larval feeding and predation at sea, food identification in larval guts, tropical and Southern Ocean ichthyoplankton, and multifactorial physiological ecology.
- The report stresses that ichthyoplankton studies link fisheries management, ecosystem science, aquaculture and pollution assessment, and that these fields must collaborate closely.

**Historical note**
- Sir Frederick Russell's speech traces the study of fish eggs and larvae from 1854 to the present and closes the report.
