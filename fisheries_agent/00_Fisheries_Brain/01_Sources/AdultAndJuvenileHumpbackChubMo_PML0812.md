# Adult and Juvenile Humpback Chub Monitoring in the Yampa River Population, 2003–2004

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Colorado River Fish Project documents a two-year (2003–2004) monitoring study of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the Yampa River population within [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. The study aimed to define the population's distribution, length frequency, juvenile abundance and adult abundance. Adults were sampled with [[Electrofishing]], [[Trammel Netting]] and angling; juveniles were collected by [[Seining]] and backpack electrofishing.

Only 13 adult humpback chub were captured, which made a population estimate impossible. Catch per unit of effort (CPUE) fell sharply compared with earlier studies. Of 349 juvenile *Gila* spp. collected, 30 were questionably identified as *G. cypha*. The authors conclude that the population is in decline and "appears near extirpation," and they recommend suspending the population estimation objective until more fish are available.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is a federally listed endangered cyprinid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]], listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1967 (32 FR 4001). It evolved in swift, canyon-bound reaches and is seldom locally abundant. The Upper Basin contains five geographically separate populations, including the Yampa population in Yampa, Lodore, Whirlpool and Split Mountain canyons. The report describes this population as one of the smallest existing humpback chub populations.

Recovery goals require no net loss of existing populations, measurable recruitment, and a monitoring program to track status. Abundance estimates are therefore central to measuring recovery progress.

Prior work on the Yampa population includes:
- [[Karp and Tyus 1990]]: 109 unique humpback chub captured in Yampa Canyon and 3 in Whirlpool Canyon; 11 of 76 tagged fish recaptured.
- [[Haines and Modde 2002]]: 83 adults captured in Yampa Canyon; population estimate of 391, described as imprecise.

**Study area:** The Yampa River segment (river kilometers 0–87) and the Green River from Lodore Ranger Station to the bottom of Split Mountain Canyon (rkm 674–593) lie entirely within Dinosaur National Monument. The Yampa River has an average annual discharge of about 61 m³/s, with a spring peak near 390 m³/s and late-summer base flow near 15 m³/s. [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] regulates the upstream Green River, with discharge averaging 27.8 m³/s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Adult sampling methods:** Adults (>200 mm TL) were sampled in spring, summer and autumn 2003–2004, mostly on the descending limb of the hydrograph and during base flows. Electrofishing used raft-mounted pulsed DC equipment, and trammel nets (23 m × 2 m, 2.5 cm mesh) were set in Whirlpool and Split Mountain canyons. Supplemental angling captures came from a separate catfish-removal and volunteer angling project.

**Juvenile sampling methods:** Juveniles were collected in September and October using seines (7.6 m × 1.5 m, 0.5 cm mesh) and backpack electrofishing in side channels, isolated pools, main channels and backwaters. Samples were fixed in 10% buffered formalin and transferred to 70% ethanol.

**Identification challenges:** Adults were identified using anal fin ray counts, caudal peduncle dimensions, fin and head shape, the presence of a nuchal hump, and mouth position. Juveniles lack reliable distinguishing characters, so identification relied on dissecting-microscope examination and verification by [[Colorado State University Larval Fish Laboratory]] staff.

**Threats and non-native species:** The report links the decline of native fishes to non-native species proliferation and altered hydrology and thermal regimes. [[Smallmouth Bass]] were rare in Yampa Canyon in 1997 but rose to 19.4% of the adult fish composition by 2004. Over the same period, native species composition fell from 84.3% to 45.4%. Predation on humpback chub by non-native fishes is documented in the Little Colorado River and suspected in Dinosaur National Monument.

## Key Findings

**Adult population**
- 13 adult humpback chub were captured in 2003–2004: 10 in Yampa Canyon and 3 in Whirlpool Canyon. The report also describes 12 potentially unique fish and 1 definitive recapture.
- By gear, 2 were caught by trammel net, 5 by electrofishing and 6 by supplemental angling.
- Total effort was 258 h of electrofishing, 1,917 h of angling and 557 h of trammel netting.
- Mean adult length was 279 mm TL, similar to Karp and Tyus (1990; 278 mm) but larger than Haines and Modde (2002; 245 mm).
- CPUE for Yampa Canyon electrofishing dropped from 1.03 (1987–1989) and 0.80 (1998–2000) to 0.03 in 2003–2004 (5 captures). Angling CPUE fell from 0.6 to 0.004.
- No gear proved superior, and none alone caught 50% of fish.
- Abundance estimates were not possible due to the low number of captures. Only one recapture was recorded.
- One possible humpback chub was captured between rkm 0 and 8 in Yampa Canyon but could not be positively confirmed before release.

**Juvenile population**
- 47 juvenile samples were collected (12 in 2003, 35 in 2004), yielding 13,379 fish in total.
- 349 *Gila* spp. were collected, of which 30 were questionably identified as *G. cypha*; *Gila* spp. made up 2.6% of the juvenile catch.
- Mean length of questionably identified juveniles was 47.9 mm TL for *G. cypha* and 43.9 mm TL for *G. robusta*.
- Length data suggest two year classes: age-0 (<70 mm) and age-1 (70–130 mm). Only one fish was in the age-1 class.
- Juveniles were found in mid and lower Yampa Canyon (rkm 18.5–37) and at Island Park, further downstream than the adult range.
- The presence of juveniles indicates some reproduction is occurring.

**Conclusions**
- The population has declined dramatically since the 1940s, when Tyus (1998) reported much higher abundance. Sampling in 2003–2004 followed a drier period than the wet years during which earlier studies were conducted, which the authors note as a caveat.
- Catch rates in 2003–2004 were lower than in 1998–2000, and the population "appears near extirpation."
- Trammel netting was effective in deepwater Whirlpool Canyon, but earlier investigators abandoned it in Yampa Canyon because of high stress on chubs.
- Angling is labor-intensive, causes mortality, and is not feasible for generating a population estimate.

**Recommendations**
1. Eliminate Lodore Canyon and Split Mountain Canyon from further population-study efforts; expand effort if incidental captures occur there.
2. Annually monitor *Gila* spp. nursery areas in Yampa Canyon, Whirlpool Canyon and Island Park.
3. Sample [[Cross Mountain Canyon]] and the [[Little Snake River]] for humpback chub, since suitable habitat exists there and radio-tagged fish from the Little Snake moved into Yampa Canyon.
4. If Yampa Canyon fish prove genetically unique, remove them to refugia to preserve genetic material.
5. Suspend the population estimation element of recovery for this population until more individuals are available for sampling.
