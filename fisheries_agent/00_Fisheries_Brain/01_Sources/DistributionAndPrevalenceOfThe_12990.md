# Distribution and Prevalence of the Asian Fish Tapeworm in the Colorado River and Tributaries, Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bothriocephalidae

## Executive Summary

This 1997 study by [[Mark J. Brouder]] and [[Timothy L. Hoffnagle]] (Arizona Game and Fish Department) documents the distribution and prevalence of the [[Asian Fish Tapeworm]], *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*, in the mainstem [[Colorado River]] and its tributaries in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. The parasite is largely confined to the warm [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) because cold mainstem water does not reach the roughly 20°C needed for its life cycle. The study reports the first records of infection in the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and in [[Plains Killifish]], and raises concern that proposed warming of the river could allow the parasite to expand its range.

## Background & Description

*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi* is a pseudophyllidean cestode first described from Japan (Yamaguti, 1934). It spread to Europe, Russia, and North America, probably with introductions of [[Grass Carp]] in the early 1970s. Cyclopoid copepods serve as intermediate hosts, and a broad range of fish, particularly cyprinids, serve as definitive hosts.

Grand Canyon's native fish fauna has declined sharply since the closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963, which converted a warm, muddy river into a clear, cold one. Of the original 8 endemic fishes, reproducing populations of only 4 remain. Three species (Colorado squawfish, bonytail chub, roundtail chub) are lost from this reach, and razorback sucker is extremely rare. The remaining natives are humpback chub (federally endangered), flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and speckled dace. A proposed [[Multilevel Intake Structure]] (MLIS) would release warmer epilimnetic water in spring to aid native fish, but warming may also increase parasite and disease risk.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal limit:** Maturation requires temperatures above 20°C. LCR temperatures exceeded 20°C from May to September 1993 and reached 26.1°C. Mainstem temperatures reached only 18.4°C (1991 to 1994), while backwaters reached 28.0°C in shallow areas.
- **Hydrology:** The LCR base flow of about 5.6 m³/s comes from springs roughly 21 km from the mouth. Floods can exceed 850 m³/s, which flushes zooplankton and disrupts backwaters.
- **Host ecology:** Backwaters are important rearing areas for larval and juvenile native and exotic fish, which are planktivorous and regularly ingest copepods. Plains killifish are omnivorous surface feeders, and copepods are only occasionally part of their diet.
- **Kanab Creek:** Mean temperatures exceeded 20°C from May through August and reached 34°C, making it suitable for reproduction.
- **Humpback chub population:** Estimates for fish over 150 mm TL ranged from 4,508 to 10,444 (Douglas and Marsh, 1996).

## Key Findings

- **Prevalence, 1994 (1,669 fish sampled):** Humpback chub 22.5% (27/120); speckled dace 3.8% (7/185); fathead minnow 2.2% (5/234); plains killifish 10.3% (3/29). Flannelmouth suckers (n = 329), bluehead suckers (n = 562), and rainbow trout (n = 210) were uninfected.
- **Prevalence, 1995:** Fathead minnow 2.4% (2/85); speckled dace 1.4% (2/148).
- **Sampling scope:** 1,902 fish of 7 species in 4 families. Fish were collected by seine, hoop net, dip net, minnow trap, and electrofishing across 8 mainstem reaches and up to 7 tributaries.
- **New host records:** Humpback chub (first refereed report) and plains killifish.
- **Spatial pattern:** Most infected fish (66.7 to 100% by species) were captured in Reaches 2 to 4, near the LCR confluence, and probably emigrated from the LCR.
- **Long-distance records:** Five infected fish were caught at least 92.8 km and up to 202.1 km downstream of the LCR, including one speckled dace in lower Kanab Creek, which suggests possible range expansion.
- **Limiting factors:** The authors identify water temperature as the main constraint, with copepod availability, flow dynamics, and host abundance also contributing.
- **Warming risk:** The MLIS could raise mainstem temperatures by 3 to 10°C, which could allow establishment in other tributaries and possibly the mainstem. Warming may aid young native fish in the short term but could harm them later through higher parasite prevalence.
- **Conservation concern:** High infection intensity can cause fish mortality. Given the small humpback chub population, infection is a serious threat.
- **Recommendation:** Further study of possible colonization of Kanab Creek was described as warranted and planned.
