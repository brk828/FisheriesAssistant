# Summary of Sample Year 2009 Fish Surveys for the Gila River Basin Long-Term Monitoring Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final annual report summarizes fish sampling in selected waters of the [[Gila River Basin]], Arizona, during sample year (SY) 2009 (July 7, 2009 to January 11, 2010). The work was performed by Marsh & Associates, LLC, under [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Contract R09PD32013 as part of a long-term monitoring plan. The monitoring stems from biological opinions on delivering [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water from Lake Havasu to the Gila River basin. Its main goals are to establish baseline data on non-native fish presence and distribution and to detect changes in species composition across the CAP aqueduct and selected rivers, streams, and canals.

Across all waters, 25 fish taxa were captured, with no new taxa detected. Six were native and 19 were non-native. Native fishes made up 56% of 6,014 individuals captured at all basin stations. Natives dominated only in the least-altered streams, while canals and the Gila River were dominated by non-natives.

## Background & Description

**Program basis and protocols.** Monitoring follows protocols detailed by Clarkson (1996a–c). Streams were stratified by geomorphology or flow. Replicate 200-m "quantitative" stations serve as the source of distribution and assemblage data. [[Electrofishing]] is the primary gear, and qualitative sampling may be added up- or downstream to find rare species.

**Canal sampling.** Canal sampling is more opportunistic and is usually done during low-flow or "dry-up" conditions. Only the CAP canal has fixed stations, and its data come mainly from pumping plant forebays using a large array of gears. [[Salt River Project Canals]] and the [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]] require searches for water and fish concentrations during outages, relying on seines, dip nets, and entanglement gear.

**Waters sampled.** Ten waters were sampled: [[San Pedro River]] (below the U.S.–Mexico boundary), Gila River (Coolidge Dam to Ashurst-Hayden Diversion), [[Salt River]] (Stewart Mountain Dam to Granite Reef Diversion), CAP Canal, SRP South and North (Arizona) Canals, the Florence-Casa Grande Canal, [[Aravaipa Creek]] (below fish barriers), and lower [[Cienega Creek]]. Results are not compared with prior years here; the report refers readers to earlier reports by Clarkson, Marsh, and Kesner.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native versus non-native balance.** Native fishes were concentrated in Cienega Creek (100% of catch), Aravaipa Creek (95%), and the San Pedro River (52%). Natives were absent from the Gila River, CAP Canal, and Florence-Casa Grande Canal (0%). The Salt River was about 3% native. Above the [[Electrical Fish Barrier]] on the SRP South Canal, natives made up 47% of catch, and in the SRP North Canal natives were about 31% overall.

**Barriers and canal reaches.** Electrical fish barriers divide several canals into reaches above and below the structure. These reaches are artificial and were not part of the original protocol. Above-barrier sites usually had more natives, while below-barrier catches were dominated by non-native species such as [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]], and threadfin shad.

**Habitat access.** Stream sampling depends on landowner and agency permission. Several sites were limited by access restrictions, high flows, or a nesting [[Bald Eagle]] pair.

## Key Findings

**Species richness by water (SY 2009):**
- [[Cienega Creek]]: 3 species
- [[Salt River]] and [[Florence-Casa Grande Canal]]: 6 species each
- [[Aravaipa Creek]]: 7 species
- Gila River: 8 species
- CAP Canal: 9 species (all non-native)
- San Pedro River: 10 species
- SRP South Canal: 11 species
- SRP North Canal: 12 species

**San Pedro River:** [[Longfin Dace]] was the most abundant species overall and dominated all three reaches. [[Desert Sucker]] was found at two stations, with 52 specimens in the upstream reach. [[Mosquitofish]] made up 36% of the catch, followed by fathead minnow (about 5%) and black bullhead (about 2%). Red shiner and channel catfish were recorded at Charleston (station 1-1-3), which is a new record for that reach.

**Gila River:** Eight species were captured. Non-native species were found, and no natives were caught in 2009. The only native catch in the previous seven years was one desert sucker in 2007. Mosquitofish was the most abundant species (about 59% of 511 individuals), followed by [[Red Shiner]] (about 12%) and channel catfish (about 9%). No upper-reach stations were sampled because access was unavailable.

**Salt River:** Only six species were taken, from 81 individuals. [[Largemouth Bass]] made up 86% of the catch, followed by bluegill (5%), Sonora sucker (3%), and green sunfish (about 2%). Upstream stations could not be sampled because of high flows.

**CAP Canal:** Nine non-native taxa were captured from 271 individuals. [[Redear Sunfish]] was most abundant (32%), followed by largemouth bass (24%) and striped bass (12%). Common carp (10%) and grass carp (11%) were also common. Grass carp dominated the upper reach, striped bass the middle reach, and redear sunfish the lower reach.

**SRP South Canal:** Eleven species were recorded above the barrier, including three natives. Native fishes made up 47% of 494 fish. [[Sonora Sucker]] was the most abundant species (about 40%), followed by [[Red Shiner]] (about 32%) and channel catfish (about 7%). The lower reach was not sampled. An unverified report of pacu (Colossoma sp.) could be the first record of this South American characid in the SRP canal system.

**SRP North (Arizona) Canal:** Twelve species were captured, including two natives, from 592 individuals. Red shiner was most abundant (34%), followed by Sonora sucker (about 19%) and mosquitofish (about 15%). Above the barrier, Sonora sucker was dominant (37% of 151 fish). Below the barrier, red shiner was dominant (43% of 541 fish).

**Florence-Casa Grande Canal:** Six non-native species were captured from about 305 individuals, with no natives. Threadfin shad was most abundant (41%), followed by mosquitofish (28%) and channel catfish (24%). Above the barrier, mosquitofish dominated (61%). Below it, threadfin shad (about 45%) and channel catfish (about 48%) were the main species.

**Aravaipa Creek:** Seven species were captured, including four natives. Longfin dace made up 94% of about 1,340 individuals, followed by mosquitofish (3%) and red shiner (2%). Lowland leopard frogs were found below the barrier.

**Cienega Creek:** Three native species were found: Gila chub, longfin dace, and Gila topminnow. Longfin dace dominated with 972 individuals (94% of the catch), and Gila chub was collected only in a pool above the upper station.

**Recommendations:**
- Start the access permission process early in the sample year to ensure permits are in hand before fieldwork begins.
- Use the new "Box Canyon" station to replace Box-o Wash (Gila River station 2-4-3), which had access problems.
- Identify a contingency alternate for Goldfield Administrative Site (Salt River station 3-1-2), where a bald eagle nest could limit future access.
