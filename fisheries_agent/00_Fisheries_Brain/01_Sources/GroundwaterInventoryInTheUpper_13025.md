# Ground-Water Inventory in the Upper Gila River Valley, New Mexico and Arizona: Scope of Investigation and Methods Used

#hydrology #ground-water #water-balance #phreatophytes

## Executive Summary

This 1941 paper (S. F. Turner and L. C. Halpenny, reprinted from the *Transactions of the American Geophysical Union*) describes the scope and methods of a ground-water inventory of two inner valleys of the [[Gila River]] in New Mexico and Arizona. The study was run by the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Division of Ground Water from January 1, 1940, under a cooperative agreement with the Arizona State Water Commissioner, with a grant from the U.S. Engineer Office. The work aims to quantify ground-water available to supplement surface-water irrigation and to assess how planned flood-control structures would alter river flow and ground-water storage.

The study builds a [[Water-Balance Equation]] for each valley and measures each term. It covers the [[Duncan-Virden Valley]] and the [[Safford Valley]], which are separated by a canyon about 25 miles long. The paper is largely methodological. Its main quantitative product is a preliminary 1940 water-balance for Duncan-Virden Valley, which closes with a balance error of 637 acre-feet.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and sponsors.** The Arizona State Water Commissioner wanted to know how much ground water could support irrigation. The Army Engineers wanted to know how flood-control structures on the upper Gila would affect the relation between river flow and ground-water storage. The Safford Valley had been studied before, with reference to the [[San Carlos Irrigation Project]] and the eastern Arizona water supply.

**Study area.** The Duncan-Virden Valley begins below the mouth of Blue Creek in New Mexico and runs to within seven miles of the junction with the San Francisco River. The Safford Valley begins about two miles below the mouth of Bonita Creek and extends to [[Coolidge Dam]] at the lower end of [[San Carlos Reservoir]]. The study is limited to the inner valleys, excavated in relatively impermeable Pliocene and early Pleistocene lake-bed deposits. These inner valleys are filled with alluvial silt, sand, and gravel that yield large quantities of ground water. The river has cut a recent flood-plain through this fill.

**Land use.** Valley lands have been cultivated since about 1870. Main crops are cotton, alfalfa, small grains, and corn, plus some truck farming in Duncan-Virden Valley. Irrigation comes from both river diversion and ground-water pumping.

**Prior work.** The [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Surface-Water Division has published stream-flow records for the area for many years. The Ground-Water Division has issued two Water-Supply Papers covering part of the region (References 2 and 3).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper treats ground water as the product of several competing processes:

- **Withdrawals:** irrigation diversion from river flow and pumping from ground water.
- **Losses:** transpiration by river-bottom [[Phreatophytes]] (cottonwood, [[Tamarisk]], [[Baccharis]]) lowers the water table.
- **Exchange with the river:** the aquifer loses water to the river by effluent seepage and gains from it through influent seepage.
- **Recharge:** rain infiltration, wash losses, canal seepage, and artesian inflow from lake-beds.

Winter wash flow near the mountain fronts is largely absorbed by channel sand and gravel within a few miles. Part of this water probably recharges the [[Artesian Aquifers]], and part enters the valleys as wash underflow. Floods add to [[Bank-Storage]] along the river, and most of that water returns to the channel as stage falls, unless plant transpiration or pumping has drawn the water table below river level.

## Key Findings

**Field methods and data coverage**

- **Surface flow:** Temporary gaging stations with automatic-start [[Water-Stage Recorder]] units were placed on 11 large washes in Duncan-Virden Valley and nine in Safford Valley. Average cost of a temporary station with recorder and wooden gage-well was about $40. Staff gages were placed on about 15 medium washes per valley, and small washes were estimated in the field.
- **Water levels:** Monthly measurements came from 85 irrigation and domestic wells in Safford Valley and 39 wells in Duncan-Virden Valley. Semi-monthly or weekly readings came from 79 observation wells in the Safford flood-plain. Records were obtained from about 800 wells in Safford Valley and about 150 in Duncan-Virden Valley.
- **Monitoring lines:** Observation wells were installed along 11 lines across the river bottoms, about five miles apart, with 7 to 13 wells per line about 500 feet apart.
- **Seepage runs:** River flow was measured at 3- to 5-mile intervals during summer seepage runs, roughly monthly. Winter runs were not made because high flows prevented accurate measurement. Water was sampled for mineral content at each measurement.
- **Low-flow stations:** Four low-flow gaging stations in Safford Valley recorded small day-to-day changes, and their diurnal records reflect phreatophyte water use.
- **Experiment station:** Established near Safford in April 1940, with seven soil tanks installed May 10, 1940. Two tanks held tamarisk, two held baccharis, and three were bare soil (one was alkali-encrusted silt). The tanks show consumptive use per month, for example 53.1 inches for average pan evaporation across May 10 to December 31, 1940.
- **Meteorology:** The station has a Class-A evaporation pan, rain gage, and psychrometer. Rainfall is recorded at 12 other gages in the two valleys. Five evaporation pans operate in Safford Valley and four in Duncan-Virden Valley.

**Artesian and spring water**

- Artesian intake areas are probably at the base of the mountains, where hard rock contacts the overlying materials.
- The largest artesian well, the [[Mack Well]] near Pima, flows about 1,350 gallons per minute. Other artesian wells range from 5 to 200 gallons per minute.
- The largest artesian springs are at [[Indian Hot Springs]], reported at 2,500 gallons per minute.
- Main artesian areas are the Cottonwood Wash valley and parts of Safford Valley near Artesia, Geronimo, Pima, and San Jose.

**Diurnal phreatophyte signal**

- Low-flow gages show daily flow changes of up to five second-feet against a total flow of 35 second-feet. Daily peak and low flows in the river coincide with the peak and trough water-table stages in nearby wells, which confirms that plant transpiration drives the daily cycle. Water use can be estimated from these fluctuations using the method of [[White (1932)]].

**Preliminary water-balance, Duncan-Virden Valley, 1940**

- **Inflow items:** Gila River at Fuller's Ranch gage 146,300 acre-feet; precipitation items, including irrigated lands 9,350, river-bottom transpiration area 2,350, and artesian springs 1,000; underflow of large washes 2,000; surface flow of washes −1,500. The stated total is 169,760 acre-feet. The itemized entries sum to 160,760, so the printed total may contain an error.
- **Outflow items:** Gila River at Clifton gage 130,670 acre-feet; river-bottom vegetation transpiration 10,130; irrigated-area transpiration and evaporation 24,183; wetted sand evaporation 1,918; open-water evaporation about 2,296 combined (river 1,535, canals 86, irrigated land 675). Total 169,197 acre-feet.
- **Balance:** Inflow less outflow is 563 acre-feet. The reported gain in ground-water storage is 1,200 acre-feet, and the balance error is 637 acre-feet.

**Methodological assumptions**

- The valleys are treated as relatively watertight, so inflow equals outflow plus or minus storage change.
- Rainfall on non-irrigated desert areas is assumed to equal evaporation and transpiration from those areas.
- Ground-water storage change is computed from water-level change multiplied by the specific yield of the aquifer materials, referenced in the paper to [[Specific Yield]] measurements.
