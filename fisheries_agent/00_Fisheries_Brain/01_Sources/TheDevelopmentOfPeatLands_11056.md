# The Development of Peat Lands

#ichthyology #marine-biology #peatland-ecology

## Executive Summary

This review, by Eville Gorham of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] (Ambleside, England), reprinted from *The Quarterly Review of Biology* (Vol. 32, No. 2, June 1957), synthesizes how peat lands (bogs, fens, and related wetlands) form and develop. It outlines a common vertical and horizontal succession in northern Britain, from [[Reed Swamp]] through [[Sedge Fen]] and [[Carr Woodland]] to [[Raised Bog]], then examines the chemical changes that accompany bog development, and finally explains how climate, topography, geology, and biota modify this course worldwide. The author stresses that the interactions among these factors make bog ecology complex, and that the field remains fragmentary.

Note on scope: the document concerns peat-forming wetland vegetation and soils. It contains no fish or species-level aquatic animal data.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Bogs, fens, and related peat lands in temperate and subpolar regions, with reclamation and peat use relevant across northern Europe.
- **Historical roots:** Early observers laid the groundwork. [[Gerard Boate]] classified Irish bogs in 1652, William King described their development in 1685, and [[Linnaeus]] recorded bog flora in the mid-18th century. Nineteenth-century work by De Luc, Aiton, and Rennie linked lake infilling to peat formation.
- **Key early contributions:** Rennie attributed peat formation to waterlogging-induced retardation of decay (absence of oxygen, low temperature, stable moisture, and an antiseptic principle in plants). William Aiton recognized the cyclical alternation of hummocks and hollows in raised bogs.
- **Discarded hypothesis:** The Dutch view that a bog is a living organism was rejected, though it was still championed by Doctor Anderson in 1794.
- **Terminology:** Usage differs. The [[Uppsala School]] restricts "bog" to ombrogenous habitats, while Pearsall used "lacustrine bog" and British workers used "valley bog" for transitional and mineral-water-influenced types.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Waterlogging and decomposition.** Peat accumulates chiefly in waterlogged habitats, where restricted oxygen slows decay. Peat can reach about 15 feet commonly and more than 30 feet in exceptional cases. [[Sphagnum]] mosses decay especially slowly because they grow in acid, nutrient-poor sites with few cellulose-decomposing organisms.

**Vertical and horizontal succession.** A typical northern British profile, from base to top, includes:
- Basal clay, overlain by lake mud (where a lake basin was present)
- Reed (*Phragmites*) peat, then sedge peat and brushwood remains
- Thick Sphagnum peats with heather and cotton grass remains, sometimes with buried pine stumps

The horizontal shoreline zonation mirrors this: reed swamp, sedge meadow, grass meadow, carr woodland, then Sphagnum-*Molinia*-*Myrica* bog and raised bog. Silting shifts these zones outward over time.

**Raised bog formation.** Sphagnum holds 10 to 20 times its own weight in water, buffering the bog against drought and flood. Raised bogs grow convex because nutrient-rich mineral-soil water inhibits lateral spread. Their surfaces are "ombrogenous," fed only by precipitation.

**Hummock-hollow regeneration.** Gorham describes a lenticular cycle in which *Sphagnum cuspidatum* colonizes pools, is succeeded by *S. papillosum*, *S. magellanicum*, and *S. fuscum*, and builds hummocks that eventually overtop and drain into new pools. The author notes that this model does not fully explain elongated hummock patterns on sloping ground, and that these patterns need further study.

**Chemical environment.** Rain is dominated by sodium chloride from sea spray in northern Britain, plus sulfuric acid from industrial pollution. Percolating mineral soil water carries calcium and magnesium bicarbonate, which keeps acidity low (pH generally above 4.5). Ombrogenous bogs are much more acid, with pH generally below 4.2 and occasionally below 3.0, largely from free sulfuric acid. Nutrients such as phosphorus and nitrogen are scarce, and nitrate is vanishingly small in rain-fed bogs.

**Climate.** Raised bogs are absent from southern Sweden below about 45 cm of annual rain, become common above about 60 cm, and decline above 100 cm as blanket bogs spread. Granlund's data show that bog convexity increases with rainfall. Climatic fluctuations since the post-glacial era are recorded in the peat as "recurrence surfaces."

**Topography.** Relief controls local moisture and nutrient flow. Deep blanket peats can cover slopes of about 20 degrees, with a risk of bog flows and peat slides. In Polesie, Poland, "raised bog" vegetation can occur on watersheds where the water table is only slightly convex.

**Geology.** Hard, nutrient-poor rocks (granites, Pocono sandstones) favor Sphagnum bogs, while limestone and calcareous flushes restrict peat accumulation. Trace elements in peats relate to bedrock.

**Biota.** Beavers alter drainage, and earthworms in tropical East Africa eat Sphagnum. Bog floras differ strongly between regions; Australian and Tierra del Fuego bogs, for example, have far fewer Sphagnum species.

## Key Findings

1. **Peat formation is driven by reduced decomposition under waterlogging,** with Sphagnum's slow decay and nutrient scarcity reinforcing accumulation.
2. **Bog development follows a predictable succession** (reed swamp, sedge fen, carr, lacustrine bog, raised bog), shaped by silting and peat accumulation.
3. **Raised bogs are a product of precipitation-only nutrition and Sphagnum water retention,** with growth rate and height related to rainfall (Granlund: a 500 m bog reaches 0.5 m at 50 cm rain, 3.4 m at 100 cm rain).
4. **Ombrogenous peats are highly acidic and nutrient-poor,** with hydrogen ions dominating adsorbed cations. Sea spray supplies sodium and magnesium, and plant roots exchange hydrogen ions for nutrients via "contact exchange."
5. **Phosphorus and nitrogen limit growth in rain-fed bogs,** and fertilization markedly increases plant growth.
6. **Standing crops of wet-habitat vegetation in Britain** are about 4,000 to 14,000 kg/ha, similar to dry-land vegetation and above average British woodland leaf fall (about 3,500 kg/ha).
7. **Climate, topography, geology, and biota interact,** so the same peat outcome can arise from different factor combinations. Lower rainfall, warmer temperatures, and basic rocks tend to inhibit peat formation.
8. **Human activity** (drainage, burning, grazing, forest clearance, and atmospheric pollution) has accelerated erosion and altered bog floras, particularly in the Pennines.
9. **Research gaps** remain in hummock-hollow regeneration, the role of subspecific variation (e.g., *Eriophorum vaginatum*, *Schoenus nigricans*), and the biological significance of trace elements.
