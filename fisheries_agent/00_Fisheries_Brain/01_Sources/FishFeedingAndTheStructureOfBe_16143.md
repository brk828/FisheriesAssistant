# Fish Feeding and the Structure of Benthic Communities in Temperate Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #benthic-ecology

## Executive Summary

This review, by J. H. Choat of the [[University Of Auckland]], evaluates whether feeding by temperate-water fishes significantly shapes the structure of shallow benthic communities. Fish predation is compared with the well-studied effects of invertebrate predators. The author concludes that although some studies make strong claims for fish control of [[Benthic Community Structure]], the evidence is often weak, contradictory, or confounded by design problems. The potential for temperate fishes to produce clear, general effects on benthic prey has not yet been demonstrated.

## Background & Description

Shallow marine environments support whelks, starfishes, crustaceans, and numerous teleost and elasmobranch fishes. Invertebrate predators are known to modify prey distribution and abundance, and [[Ecological Theory]] predicts their influence should increase in subtidal settings lacking emersion and wave shock. Fishes possess greater mobility, orientation ability, and sensory capacity than invertebrates, which led the author to expect them to be major determinants of subtidal community structure.

Only 5 to 9 percent of references in major reviews of marine community structure addressed fish predation, and many of those concerned freshwater or tropical species. The review therefore examines the structure and ecology of temperate fish faunas themselves, which are less studied than tropical faunas. Key comparisons with [[Tropical Reef Fishes]] include:

- **Species richness:** tropical faunas are far more species-rich, but richness alone is a poor proxy for feeding intensity without abundance data.
- **Herbivory:** herbivorous fishes such as [[Scarid Parrotfishes]], acanthurids, and siganids are largely absent from temperate waters. Temperate herbivores are mostly browsers of macroscopic algae, including kelps.
- **Behavior:** tropical reef fishes show rapid, orderly shelter-seeking at twilight. The author notes that no data yet show piscivorous predators directly determine this behavior.
- **Mobility:** temperate fishes appear more sedentary, with diurnal foraging in circumscribed areas and fewer feeding migrations than tropical carnivores such as [[Snappers]] and grunts. Seasonal migrations, however, may be common in soft-substratum species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Feeding categories.** The review distinguishes grazers, which take benthic organisms nonselectively (often microalgae or newly settled invertebrates), from browsers, which take recognizable sessile organisms. Predators on mobile prey are classed as ambushers, searchers, or pursuers. Planktivores may remove large numbers of larvae from the water column, potentially altering later settlement.

**Evaluation of effects.** The author sets out three requirements: direct observation of feeding, gut content analysis (which alone cannot show community effects), and field experiments. Field experiments fall into four types: (a) exclusion of fishes using cages or shields; (b) inclusion within cages; (c) manipulation of shelter or feeding substrata; and (d) removal or addition of fishes. Laboratory experiments are described as corroborative only.

**Hard substrata.** Reef grazing and browsing have not been shown to modify temperate hard-substratum biota extensively. Exclusion studies that recorded community changes mostly lacked positive identification of fish predators, though four southern-hemisphere cases implicated [[Monacanthid Filefishes]] feeding on ascidians, and labrids, cheilodactylids, monacanthids and tetraodontids were linked to mussel removal. The best-known claim concerns [[Oxyjulis Californica]] in [[Macrocystis Kelp Forests]], which the review judges tentative and contradictory. Fish predation on intertidal fauna is poorly tested; labrids in southeastern Australia removed limpets, and a gobiesocid in Chile and South Africa showed foraging ranges of interest.

**Motile fauna.** Temperate reef fishes consume many motile invertebrates. Echinoids of the genera [[Strongylocentrotus]] and [[Centrostephanus]] are a focus, with predation by labrids such as [[Semicossyphus Pulcher]] and by fishes such as [[Atlantic Wolffish]] and [[American Plaice]] implicated in shaping echinoid distribution, behavior, and size structure. Results were often confounded by depth or locality.

**Soft substrata.** Fish predation on unvegetated sediments has been studied more often than on reefs, mainly in shallow northern-hemisphere settings. Caging experiments usually raised infaunal density, but attributing this specifically to fish was difficult because decapods and other predators co-occur. In seagrass beds and [[Spartina Marshes]], the evidence that fishes control invertebrate seasonality is not consistent. The most compelling modification evidence concerns foraging [[Cownose Rays]], which destroyed large seagrass tracts unpredictably.

**Experimental design problems.** Key issues include local patchiness in predator and prey, cage effects on light, sedimentation, and settlement, edge effects, differential settlement versus survival, and the risk that exclusion of fishes allows micrograzer build-up. Few studies used nested analyses or factorial designs to capture interactions among vegetation, predators, time, and disturbance.

## Key Findings

- Fish predation has been underrepresented in marine community ecology, with only 5 to 9 percent of references in major reviews addressing it.
- Temperate and tropical fish faunas differ in species richness, herbivory, behavior, body size, and mobility, but many proposed latitudinal trends rest on richness data alone and remain untested.
- Few studies demonstrate that temperate fishes modify hard-substratum biota. The kelp-forest claims about Oxyjulis are described as tentative and internally contradictory.
- Caging experiments in soft sediments commonly increase infaunal density, but cage artifacts and co-occurring predators prevent firm attribution to fishes.
- Foraging elasmobranchs, notably cownose rays, produced the clearest documented habitat modification in seagrass and soft-bottom systems.
- Predation is poorly linked to community stability. Recruitment variability, synchronous mass mortality, and commercial fishing complicate interpretation.
- Future work needs data on fish abundance and density, predator distribution, recruitment, life histories, factorial and nested designs, and experiments that account for predator interactions and local patchiness.
- The author concludes that the potential for temperate fishes to produce general, recognizable effects on benthic prey has not yet been realized.
