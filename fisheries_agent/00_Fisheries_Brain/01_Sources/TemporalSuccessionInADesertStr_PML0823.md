# Temporal Succession in a Desert Stream Ecosystem Following Flash Flooding

#freshwater-ecology #stream-succession #aquatic-insects

## Executive Summary

This study describes the postflood recovery of a Sonoran Desert stream, [[Sycamore Creek]] (Arizona, USA), as a model of temporal succession in running waters. Three flash floods in August 1979 (peak discharges of 7.0, 3.0 and 2.0 m³/s) nearly eliminated algae and reduced invertebrate standing crop by 98%. Physical conditions returned to near preflood levels within 2 days, and the biota recovered within 2 to 3 weeks for most components. The authors tracked algae, [[Macroinvertebrates]], [[Gross Primary Production]], community respiration and [[Nutrient Dynamics]] for 63 days, then compared the resulting trajectories with the successional generalizations of [[Odum (1969)]] and [[Margalef (1968)]]. Agreement was only partial. The stream became strongly autotrophic and exported organic matter downstream, a pattern the authors argue is characteristic of open, frequently disturbed systems rather than of closed, mature ecosystems.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** Most succession theory was developed in terrestrial, especially forest, systems. Extending it to streams is difficult for several reasons: aquatic organisms have short life spans relative to disturbance frequency, discrete communities are hard to define, and current imposes a spatial vector on temporal change. Stream succession has often been described as longitudinal zonation, which is specific to each drainage and hard to generalize. Flooding is the most frequent stream disturbance, and recovery after floods typically takes several months to a year or more in other systems.

**Why desert streams.** Lowland Sonoran Desert streams are permanent only where groundwater or precipitation from higher elevations feeds them. Otherwise they are intermittent, with surface segments separated by dry, sandy underflow stretches. Isolated cloudburst floods scour these streams, restoring the physical structure within hours or days but leaving a greatly reduced biota. The authors argue that such infrequent, catastrophic floods with stable interflood conditions are ideal for studying succession in running waters.

**Study site.** Sycamore Creek drains 505 km² of rugged, mountainous terrain and is a tributary of the Verde River. The primary study reach was a 500-m section at 610 m elevation. Mean discharge was about 0.6 m³/s, with stream width of 5 to 6 m and mean velocity near 15 cm/s. Substrate was mostly sand and gravel. Water was moderately hard calcium-magnesium bicarbonate water, with conductance near 500 µS/cm. Nitrate was present at 40 to 100 µg/L in typical conditions, and soluble reactive phosphorus was relatively stable at about 50 µg/L.

**Methods (summary).** Sampling occurred on days 2, 3, 5, 13, 22, 32, 48 and 63 after flooding. Morphometry was measured at 15 transects. Algal patches were mapped and sampled for biomass, chlorophyll *a* and community structure. Invertebrates were collected with core samplers (a minimum of 12 cores per date, in a stratified random design). Gut contents were analyzed for food habits. Gross primary production and community respiration were estimated with a light-and-dark chamber model (Busch and Fisher 1981). Nutrients were measured by standard colorimetric methods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical template.** Stream morphometry, conductivity, pH, alkalinity and silica stayed nearly constant across the study period and showed no significant diel or spatial variation. Nitrogen and phosphorus, by contrast, varied considerably. Nitrate-N was the predominant inorganic nitrogen form, and ammonium made up only about 4.5% of inorganic N on the six dates it was measured. Nitrate and SRP declined as water flowed through the reach. Upstream groundwater sources had much higher nitrate (500 to several thousand µg/L, averaging about 700 µg/L), so the study reach was partly subsidized by upwelling water.

**Algal communities.** Algae were the only significant primary producers at the site. Macroscopically distinct patch types were identified, including bare sediment (patch 1), diatom-dominated assemblages (patch 2, mainly *Achnanthes* and *Gomphonema* species), a *Cladophora* epiphyte type (patch 3), a blue-green-dominated type (patch 4) and a *Cladophora*-blue-green mat (patch 5). Patch 3 was restricted to the channel periphery. Patch distribution depended on current and substrate: *Cladophora* persisted where it was less exposed to removal by current.

**Invertebrate community and trophic structure.** A total of 48 invertebrate taxa was collected from August to October, with 38 to 43 present on any given date. Flooding caused a net loss of 5 taxa, all of which eventually reappeared. The dominant taxa were the mayflies [[Baetis quilleri]], [[Leptohyphes packeri]] and [[Ticorythodes dimorphus]], the crane fly [[Cryptolabis sp.]], and chironomids. Early in recovery, primary consumers ate mostly allochthonous detritus. Within 2 weeks, diatoms became the principal food. Fast-growing collector-gatherers made up over 85% of density and biomass throughout. Only two species, *Graptocorixa serrulata* and *Ochrotrichia sp.*, functioned as algal shredders, and no leaf shredders were present.

**Fish.** The only fish species highlighted is the [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), an omnivorous cyprinid that consumes large quantities of algae. Late in the sequence, its consumption exceeded primary production, which suggests the fish can regulate algal standing crop in these systems (Fisher et al. 1981 cited in the text).

**Recolonization pathways.** Recolonization was mainly aerial. Aquatic adults capable of flight and aerial adults that emerged before flooding oviposited on the receding flood. Drift was important only where upstream reaches were undisturbed. Vertical movement from sediments was not a significant route, and sandy sediments offered no reliable refuge because of scour.

**Ecosystem metabolism.** Gross primary production and ecosystem respiration rose rapidly after flood recession. By day 5 and for the remainder of the sequence, P<sub>g</sub> exceeded respiration, and P<sub>g</sub>/R averaged 1.46, so the system was autotrophic. Macroinvertebrate respiration accounted for 14 to 22% of total respiration after day 5. The ecosystem exported organic matter both downstream and laterally to drying stream banks.

## Key Findings

- **Rapid physical and biological recovery.** Physical conditions were restored within 2 days. Algal standing crop reached nearly 100 g/m² within 2 weeks. Invertebrate dry biomass reached 7.3 g/m² within 1 month.
- **Recovery is largely aerial.** Invertebrate recolonization occurred mainly through immigration of aerial adults and oviposition, supported by the rapid development of several mayfly and dipteran taxa (some completing a generation in under 2 weeks).
- **Flood severity.** The August 1979 floods reduced invertebrate standing crop by 98%. Biomass and chlorophyll reached over 75% of their ultimate levels within a month. Invertebrate biomass and numbers reached 5% of their 63-day values in 13 and 35 days, respectively.
- **Successional sequence of algae.** Diatoms dominated early, and filamentous greens and blue-greens dominated later. The transition matrix showed dynamic, not strictly directional, change: *Cladophora* that detached and drifted exposed sediment for diatom recolonization.
- **Biomass early in recovery.** Early biomass accrual (about 7 g/m²/d between days 2 and 5) exceeded what in situ algal production could plausibly supply. The authors attribute much of it to immigration of algae from scoured, redeposited sediments, not drift from upstream.
- **Stable ecosystem-level properties.** Pigment diversity (O.D.<sub>480</sub>/O.D.<sub>665</sub>) remained stable from day 2 to 63. Collective properties such as biomass and metabolism stabilized while community composition continued to change.
- **Organic matter budget.** Net import accounted for 11% of total input and net export for 14% of total output. Lateral export to drying banks accounted for 6% of output. From day 13 to 63, 56% of net production was exported and 44% contributed to standing crop.
- **Nutrient uptake.** Uptake of nitrate and SRP rose rapidly after flooding and tracked organic matter accretion, consistent with the [[Vitousek and Reiners (1975)]] model. The N:P ratio of uptake was lower than the Redfield ratio, implying N and P were also lost by denitrification and/or adsorption to sediments.
- **Limited agreement with Odum (1969).** The predicted pattern for P/B and P/R did not hold, and species diversity patterns depended on the taxonomic group. Biochemical diversity did not increase, and the authors suggest abandoning that index. Predictions on detritus, food chains and nutrient conservation were partly supported. Stability to perturbation was judged inadequate as a basis for assessing maturity, because life-history traits are adaptations to flooding and drying.
- **Desert streams as a distinct system.** Frequently disturbed, open stream ecosystems depart from the closed-system criteria used to define successional maturity. Autotrophic, net-exporting streams can persist in a steady, productive state with P/R well above 1, which the authors argue requires revising general theories of succession.
- **Nitrogen as the likely limiting nutrient.** [[Grimm (1980)]] found nitrogen most likely limited primary production in southwestern streams. Late-stage crashes of algal assemblages were associated with low or undetectable nitrate and blue-green nitrogen fixers.
- **Research priority.** The authors call for a hybrid approach to succession, combining population-level and community-level perspectives, and for studies that treat stream succession within its physical and chemical context.
