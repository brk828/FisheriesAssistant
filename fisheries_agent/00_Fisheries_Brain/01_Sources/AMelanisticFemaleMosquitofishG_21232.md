# Melanistic Female Mosquitofish (Gambusia affinis holbrooki)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This note reports the first melanistic female [[Gambusia Affinis Holbrooki]] (Eastern Mosquitofish) in the scientific literature. The fish was collected on 26 July 1983 from a roadside ditch near Oviedo, Seminole Co., [[Florida]]. Melanism had previously been documented only in males. A year-long monthly sampling program at six sites in east-central Florida (September 1983 to August 1984), covering more than 27,000 fish, found no other melanistic females. The laboratory-held female produced two normally pigmented broods, and one of her daughters later developed melanic markings at sexual maturity. The authors argue that the melanism is not the product of the androgenic effects seen in paper mill effluent.

## Background & Description

- **Study organism:** The [[Southeastern Semispecies]] of the mosquitofish, *Gambusia affinis holbrooki*, in the family [[Poeciliidae]].
- **Melanism:** Produced by patches of unusual macromelanophores in the dermis (Regan, 1961). It ranges from weak (scattered spots) to moderate (marbled or mottled) to strong (black body and fins).
- **Prior literature:**
  - [[Regan 1961]] described melanophore patterns in normal and melanistic fish and mated melanistic males to normal females to examine inheritance.
  - [[Martin 1977]] reported that melanistic males were very aggressive and dominated normal males at high laboratory densities.
  - Martin (in press) examined inheritance by analyzing progeny from melanistic males crossed with normal females.
- **Gap identified:** Both Regan (1961) and Martin (1977) stressed that melanistic individuals are rare and restricted to males. Aquarium literature (e.g., Innes, 1966; Sterba, 1966) mentions melanistic females as extremely rare and prized by aquarists. Innes (1966) suggested some dealers had developed black strains that "breed true," but the genetics remain unclarified.
- **Specimen:** Adult female, 38.0 mm standard length, deposited at the Florida State Museum (UF 42005). A steel pin was inserted beneath the skin to correct body contortion from preservation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Collection site:** Roadside ditch in an agricultural area, likely receiving pesticide and fertilizer runoff. The authors found no evidence that the site was more polluted than other mosquitofish localities.
- **Sampling design:** Monthly collections at six sites from September 1983 to August 1984. Localities spanned three counties: [[Seminole County]], [[Orange County]], and [[Brevard County]].
- **Laboratory conditions:** The female was kept in isolation at 26 ± 1 °C on a 14L:10D photoperiod and fed daily on Tetramin Staple flake food plus frozen brine shrimp.
- **Comparison case:** [[Howell et al. 1980]] documented a mosquitofish population in [[Paper Mill Effluent]] that showed abnormal masculinization, including precocious male development and masculinized females with gonopodia and male sexual behavior. The authors attributed this to an unknown androgenic compound in the effluent.

Male melanism by locality ranged from 0.16% (T22S, R31E, Sec. 11, Orange Co.; 4 of 2,483) to 2.91% (T21S, R35E, Sec. 34, Brevard Co.; 28 of 962). The Oviedo site (T21S, R31E, Sec. 3, Seminole Co.) had 26 melanistic males among 1,503 (1.73%) and no melanistic females.

## Key Findings

1. **First record of a melanistic female.** The Oviedo specimen is the first female of this morph reported in the scientific literature.

2. **Melanism is rare in both sexes.** Across all six sites, no melanistic females were found among 18,561 females. Melanistic males made up 1.40% (126 of 8,994). The authors conclude that melanism "must be exceedingly rare in females."

3. **Laboratory breeding history:**
   - **Brood 1 (23 August 1983):** Nine young rescued alive after the female killed or ate some. All normally pigmented.
   - **Brood 2 (13 September 1983):** 12 normally pigmented young. The authors do not believe any were eaten.
   - **Brood 3 (27 November 1983):** All young premature and stillborn. No count is given.
   - After this birth, the female remained healthy through winter and spring, but produced no further young.

4. **Aggression toward males:** The female was very aggressive toward melanistic males and killed most within several days of exposure. Mating was uncertain, so paternity of the broods is unknown.

5. **Necropsy findings (26 May 1984):** The female, found dead, carried 23 apparently normal, mid-development embryos. The ovary also contained 11 (+1) degenerate embryos apparently being reabsorbed. These may represent "missed" broods from December to April or part of the final brood.

6. **Progeny expression:**
   - Six progeny were alive at the time of writing: two mature males and four females.
   - Both males and three females were normally pigmented.
   - The fourth female developed melanism as she approached sexual maturity, first as scattered spots, then as seven large lateral melanic blotches on the trunk and caudal peduncle.
   - This indicates the melanism in this lineage can be expressed in female offspring at maturity, even though the parent's brood was normally pigmented at birth.

7. **Rejection of androgenic masculinization:** The melanistic female shows no signs of masculinization. Her anal fin is identical to a normal female's (Turner, 1941), and the Oviedo population displays normal secondary sexual characters. The authors therefore reject a paper-mill-type androgenic mechanism.

8. **Ongoing research:** The melanistic daughter is being used in genetic studies by [[Robert A. Angus]] at the University of Alabama, Birmingham, to clarify inheritance of the trait.
