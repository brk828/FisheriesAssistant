# Management Plan for the Big-River Fishes of the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) plan, prepared by a team appointed in 1999 by the Region 2 Regional Director, is the [[Lower Colorado River Management Plan]] (LCRMP) for the "big-river fishes." It supplements the existing recovery plans for four federally endangered species: [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The plan sets programmatic strategies to maintain genetic refugia and establish self-sustaining populations in the lower basin, with the longer-term goal of downlisting and delisting. It divides the lower basin into six subunits and relies on three strategies: production facilities, isolated habitats, and unique opportunities such as reservoir drawdowns. Implementation is voluntary and subject to budget and priority constraints, and no initial timeline is set.

## Background & Description

**Scope and status.** The plan is a programmatic amendment and supplement to the 2002 [[Recovery Goals]]. It is not a project-level implementation plan, and it becomes binding only when signed by the Regional Director and approved by the state directors of Arizona, California, and Nevada. The document is dated 2004 and cited as USFWS (2004).

**Basin.** The Colorado River basin drains roughly 650,000 km² of western North America. For management, the lower basin runs from [[Lee's Ferry]] to the international boundary with Mexico and includes the Grand Canyon, [[Lake Mead Subunit|Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave Subunit|Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Havasu Subunit|Lake Havasu]], the Parker-Imperial reach, and the Gila River basin. Historically, the river had canyon-bound and alluvial reaches with extensive floodplain marshes and backwaters. Dams, diversions, and channelization have replaced this with managed flows and reservoirs.

**Big-river fishes.** These species are characterized by endemism and unique morphological and behavioral adaptations. Today their remaining populations are small and isolated. The authors identify two main causes of decline:
- habitat alteration from dam construction, diversions, altered thermal regimes, sediment interruption, and floodplain isolation;
- introduction of [[Nonnative Fishes]], which are largely ecological generalists and predators or competitors of native eggs and young.

Decades of monitoring show that nonnative species prevent native populations from completing their life cycles in the few remaining habitat patches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species status at a glance:**
- **Bonytail:** small populations in Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu, both designated critical habitat. Stocking is ongoing under biological opinions. Recruitment has been documented at the High Levee Pond on [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]].
- **Humpback chub:** occurs in Grand Canyon, with the largest concentration in the lower 14.2 km of the [[Little Colorado River]].
- **Colorado pikeminnow:** wild populations are extirpated from the lower basin. Reintroduction is under way in the Verde and Salt rivers as an [[Experimental Nonessential Population]].
- **Razorback sucker:** small populations persist in Grand Canyon and Lake Mead. The largest population is in Lake Mohave. Numbers in Lake Havasu and Parker-Imperial are unknown.

**Management of habitats.** The subunits are:
- [[Grand Canyon Subunit]]: riverine reach controlled by [[Glen Canyon Dam]]; humpback chub focus.
- [[Lake Mead Subunit]]: large, deep reservoir with a riverine lower segment; razorback sucker focus.
- [[Lake Mohave Subunit]]: about 61 km reservoir with a 33 km riverine section below Hoover Dam.
- [[Lake Havasu Subunit]]: riverine reach below Davis Dam into Lake Havasu, with Topock Marsh.
- [[Parker-Imperial Subunit]]: riverine reach below Parker Dam with backwaters and marshes, including Imperial NWR.
- [[Gila River Subunit]]: Gila, Salt, and Verde rivers, including San Carlos and Horseshoe reservoirs.

**Land and water management.** Management involves many agencies, including USFWS, the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (USBR), the National Park Service, the Bureau of Land Management, state wildlife agencies, and tribes. Water releases are controlled by USBR and the Corps of Engineers. Flood control, water storage, delivery, and other human needs constrain all actions.

## Key Findings

**Strategies**
1. **Strategy 1: Augmentation (production facilities).** Hatcheries raise fish to stocking size, supplemented by wild-collected early life stages. The goal is to build genetic refugia and expand existing mainstem populations.
2. **Strategy 2: Isolated Habitats.** Semi-natural or newly created backwaters, ponds, and bays are used as long-term genetic refugia and production sites. Nonnative fish must be monitored and removed as needed.
3. **Strategy 3: Unique Opportunities.** Reservoir drawdowns may allow adult or larval native fish to establish. Adults must be stocked during drawdowns when predators are absent or reduced.

**Stocking and monitoring**
- Colorado pikeminnow: 11,216 fish >30 cm stocked into the Verde River since 1995; 84 recaptured.
- Razorback sucker: 21,414 released into the Verde River since 1994 (137 recaptured); one stocking of 2,046 into the Salt River in 1996.
- Lake Mohave: about 4,000 wild razorback sucker in 2003, expected to decline through natural mortality; about 20,000 subadults stocked since 1992.
- Lake Havasu: subadult razorback stocking began in 1993; a target of 30,000 subadult bonytail was set by a 1993 biological opinion.
- Stocking of small numbers is difficult to detect in large subunits, so the plan calls for large releases.

**Recovery criteria (downlisting and delisting)**
- Humpback chub (Grand Canyon): a core population above 2,100 adults (MVP), with trends in adults and recruitment of age-3 fish meeting specified thresholds.
- Bonytail: a genetic refuge and two self-sustaining populations, each above 4,400 adults (MVP).
- Razorback sucker (Lake Mohave): a genetic refuge and two self-sustaining populations, each above 5,800 adults (MVP).

**Genetic management.** Because populations have contracted sharply, the plan prioritizes:
1. maximizing the number of individuals contributing to the next generation, preferring wild-collected larvae;
2. stocking tagged wild adults into isolated bays for natural spawning;
3. hatchery broodstock with pedigree control to minimize inbreeding;
4. using Lake Mohave wild adults as broodstock, PIT-tagged and released after one use.

The first option is preferred because it retains the most genetic variability.

**Conditioning hatchery fish.** Three techniques are proposed to improve survival: environmental enrichment, life-skills training, and hard versus soft release. Razorback sucker that were "hard released" showed reduced risky behavior. Fish >30 cm are favored for stocking.

**Future needs.** The plan lists these priorities:
- secure long-term funding (congressional appropriations, federal and state agencies, and private sources);
- expand hatchery rearing capacity for fish >30 cm;
- conduct stakeholder outreach;
- create a Lower Colorado Basin Management Group with a full-time coordinator;
- hold annual working sessions and synthesize research on life history, habitat, and threats.
