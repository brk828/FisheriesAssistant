# Grand Canyon River Resource Monitoring Project: 1977 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila

## Executive Summary

This annual report, prepared by [[Steven W. Carothers]] of the [[Museum of Northern Arizona]] under a contract with the [[National Park Service]] (Project No. CX821070012), documents the first year (1977) of the River Resource Monitoring Project in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. The project covered four 14- to 18-day river expeditions, a 4-day endangered species survey of the [[Little Colorado River]], and about 700 field person-days, with a further 250 person-days of preliminary analysis to date.

The report centers on three themes:
- **Human impact on beaches:** Human debris and charcoal in beach sand, trampling, and sand grain size were measured on riparian beaches.
- **Food-chain effects:** Human food waste was found to raise densities of the [[Red Harvester Ant]] and the [[Common Ant Lion]] by about an order of magnitude.
- **Riparian vegetation and fish:** Vegetation zones were mapped on five beaches from aerial photographs. The endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) was sampled at several localities.

Most results are preliminary. The authors state that no statistical evaluations were performed until at least four sampling periods were completed.

## Background & Description

**Objectives.** The project set six objectives:
1. Establish the rate and magnitude of environmental degradation in the [[Riparian Zone]] of the [[Colorado River]], with reference to recreation.
2. Determine which environmental elements best correlate with that degradation.
3. Develop tests and procedures that non-technical personnel can use to monitor it.
4. Predict vegetation trends, assuming periodic large water releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]].
5. Inventory plants and animals of the riparian zone, with attention to listed endangered or threatened species.
6. Monitor population trends of the genus *Gila* and predict effects of dam construction and water releases.

**Methodological changes.** Early in 1977 the team agreed with the National Park Service's COAR office to change parts of the work plan that proved oversampling, technically inadequate, or logistically impossible. These changes did not alter the six objectives.
- **Debris sampling:** Beach sand samples were doubled from 10 to 20 per beach. Charcoal and human litter counts were enlarged from 1/4 m² to 1 m² plots. Future surveys drop the July sampling period and keep March (pre-season) and September (post-season) sampling.
- **Vegetation mapping:** Planned ground transects were abandoned because low-profile aerial photographs were needed. Aerial photography was flown by the Museum of Northern Arizona, and ground-truth transects were deferred to March 1979.
- **Organisms:** The study also tracked an unidentified herbivorous ant, and the ant lion, alongside the harvester ant.

**Data sources.** Animal observations (birds, mammals, reptiles, amphibians) were recorded during the field trips but had not been reduced to tables. Plant collections were awaiting identification. A November–December expedition was funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]; its fish and terrestrial vertebrate data were included because they are relevant to this project.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Beach debris and sediment.** Human debris and charcoal accumulate in beach sand. Preliminary results suggest some natural purging occurred during the low-use season, but the data were not yet sufficient to confirm this. The river running season was delayed to mid-June in 1977, so the July sample reflects a period of very low use from September 1976 to June 1977.

**Flow regulation.** Since [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was completed, the river's flow has been largely controlled. The historic spring floods that periodically scoured the lower riparian zone have stopped. Zones once renewed by scouring are becoming permanently established, while zones maintained by high water are no longer recharged. An artificial riparian zone has formed along the controlled shoreline. Beaches appear more affected than other zones.

**Beach types.** Two processes shape the beaches:
- River flow and eddies control silt deposition. Slow water in eddies, as at [[Saddle Canyon]] and [[Nankoweap Beach]], favors silt and nutrient deposition and vegetation establishment.
- Source material also matters. Side-canyon floodplain beaches, such as Saddle Canyon and [[National Canyon Beach]], receive material from periodic flash floods. Beaches such as [[Deer Creek Beach]] are built mainly by the river or wind and tend to be more unstable.

**Vegetation zones.** Four zones occur in various combinations:
- **[[Saltcedar Willow Zone]]:** Found on all five beaches, adjacent to the river. Dominated by saltcedar (*Tamarix chinensis*) and willow (*Salix* spp.).
- **Sandy beach:** Limited vegetation, mainly grasses (*Sporobolus*, *Stipa*) and small shrubs (*Pluchea*). Shifting wind-blown sand creates unstable habitat at Saddle Canyon.
- **Rocky beach:** Supports grasses (*Sporobolus*, *Stipa*, *Bromus rubens*), shrubs (*Chrysothamnus*, *Gutierrezia*, *Viguiera*) and forbs (*Salsola kali*, *Dyssodia*, *Erigeron*).
- **[[Mesquite-Catclaw Zone]]:** Dominated by mesquite (*Prosopis juliflora*) and catclaw (*Acacia greggii*). The understory is mainly red brome (*Bromus rubens*) and cheatgrass (*Bromus tectorum*), which stabilize sandy substrate.

**Beach mapping.** The five beaches studied were Saddle Canyon (River Mile 47R), Nankoweap (52R), Deer Creek (136L), National Canyon (166L) and [[Granite Park Beach]] (209L). Photographs were flown in October 1977 and enlarged to about 1:2500. The authors estimate measurement accuracy at ±15%, and results are not final until ground-truthing is complete.

**Red harvester ant and ant lion.** Human food is incorporated into beach deposits. Harvester ants scavenge at campsites, and the energy then moves through the food chain. The study compared herbivore ([[Red Harvester Ant]]) and carnivore ([[Common Ant Lion]], *Myrmeleon* sp.) populations.

**Humpback chub habitat.** The Little Colorado River mouth was the main concentration area for the chub. Netting took chubs with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Channel Catfish]] and speckled dace.

## Key Findings

**1. Human debris and beach sediment (March vs. July 1977)**
- Large charcoal particles (>1 cm) averaged 10.3 per m² in March and 7.9 per m² in July (grand means across beaches). The text also gives a July value of 2.9/m²; this discrepancy is unresolved in the source.
- Human debris averaged 8.8 per m² in March and 7.9 per m² in July.
- Small charcoal grand means were 12.1 (March) and 9.9 (July).
- Average trampling depth was about 5.5 cm and did not change between sampling periods.
- Average sand grain size was about 0.25 mm and did not change.
- Average charcoal particle size declined from 0.12 mm (March) to 0.08 mm (July), consistent with natural purging, though sample sizes were insufficient to test this.

**2. Food-chain effects of campsites**
- Population densities of both the red harvester ant and the common ant lion were about an order of magnitude higher in impacted camp habitats than in low-impact areas.
- The effect extends beyond cooking areas: ant lion densities were higher in sleeping areas where snacks were carried.
- Ant lions rarely captured large harvester ants and depended on smaller insects, so the two species occupy different food chains.
- Colony counts at each beach provide a usable density estimate, which could be monitored inexpensively from year to year.
- Harvester ant and ant lion densities covary closely along the river. Higher visitation is associated with higher densities.
- Campsite size varies, and normalizing density data to site size is recommended. This is unlikely to change the conclusions.

**3. Harvester ant behavior at campsites**
- Campsite ants are much less aggressive than those in non-camp conditions, except within about 50 cm of the hill.
- They collect liquid food (coffee, fruit drinks, gravies), removing wetted sand balls.
- A division of labor exists for large food droppings: excavators, miners, stevedores and teamsters.
- Ants are inactive at night. The authors judge them a manageable nuisance if campers avoid the 50 cm perimeter.

**4. Riparian vegetation**
- Vegetation zones were mapped on five beaches from October 1977 aerial photographs, with ground truthing still pending.
- Saddle Canyon has all four zones; Nankoweap is about 87% vegetated; Deer Creek is about 73% rock; National Canyon is about 63% rocky beach; Granite Park is about 61% sandy beach.
- The authors recommend that future transects be placed at Nankoweap because it resembles Saddle Canyon.

**5. Humpback chub (*Gila cypha*) population**
- A total of 39 humpback chubs was taken from the Colorado and Little Colorado Rivers in 1977, ranging from 61 to 423 mm total length.
- Catches by month: 1 in March (Little Colorado River mouth); 25 in July (Little Colorado River mouth, 99–423 mm); 3 in September (12.8 km upstream in the Little Colorado River, 270–295 mm, the first records from that area); 10 in November (3 at river mile 27, 1 at mile 70, 6 at the Little Colorado River mouth).
- Localities sampled: 13 in March, 11 in July and 27 in November.
- The Little Colorado River was the main population concentration. No chubs were taken from the other tributaries.
- Size-based age structure: about 2.6% young-of-the-year (<70 mm, by a Green River criterion), with juveniles and adults roughly equal. Several year classes (0–III and older) were represented, with estimated proportions of 2.6%, 33.3%, 15.3%, 10.2% and 35.8% for the successive classes.
- The authors suggest spawning may begin in March and extend into July–August. Several gravid individuals were noted, and reproduction was more successful in January 1978 collections.
- Food habits, reproductive strategies, movement and general ecology remain largely unknown, and the authors recommend continued monitoring with baseline data for later studies.

**6. Overall status**
- Human activity appears to be the main driver of altered food chains and beach conditions. The authors conclude that either a drastically altered ecological state must be tolerated or human detritus must be eliminated from campsites.
- Results to date are baseline and preliminary. Final conclusions await additional sampling periods, ground-truthing and taxonomic identification of plants and animals.
