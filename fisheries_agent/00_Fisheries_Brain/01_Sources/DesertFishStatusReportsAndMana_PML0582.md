# Desert Fish Status Reports and Management Proceedings, Colorado River Wildlife Council (c. 1974-1975)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of proceedings and meeting minutes from the [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]] (with its Great Basin and Lahontan counterparts) on the status and management of native desert fishes. It reports state and federal status updates by region: the [[Lower Colorado River]], the [[Upper Colorado River]], the [[Lahontan Basin]], the [[Interbasin]] area and the [[Oregon Lakes]] region. The main themes are habitat loss from dams, channelization, flow control, urban development, geothermal leasing and oil shale development, competition and predation by exotic species, and the captive propagation of endangered fishes. The document also records a 1975 symposium decision and several resolutions and management requests.

## Background & Description

The document is a compiled record of agency presentations rather than a single research study. Its speakers include staff from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], the California and Nevada fish and game departments, Oregon State University, and university teams in Utah. The Council's stated purpose is to coordinate management of desert fishes, and the document is recommended to "anyone actively interested in the management of desert fishes."

The fishes discussed include:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a large-river endemic.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The Upper Basin section calls this species the "humpback sucker," but the Lower Basin section uses "razorback sucker" for the same binomial, so the name is inconsistent within the source.
- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*) and **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*, including subspecies *G. robusta seminuda*). The text says both *G. robusta* and *G. elegans* "need listing as threatened forms."
- **[[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Salmo clarkii henshawi*) and the **[[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Salmo clarkii pleuriticus*).
- **[[Cui-ui]]** (*Chasmistes cujus*), a lakesucker endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]].
- **[[Soldier Meadows Dace]]** (*Eremichthys acros*), **[[Moapa Dace]]**, **[[White River Spring Fish]]** (*Crenichthys baileyi*), Railroad Valley springfish (*C. nevadae*), Pahranagat bonytail, and White River spinedace.
- Native *Rhinichthys osculus* (dace) and suckers in the Paria River, and *Plagopterus* in the Virgin River and tributaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The source describes several recurring threats:

- **Dams and flow regulation.** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] is said to have caused habitat changes that left nearly all large-river forms in the Grand Canyon area extinct or nearly so. Flow control and channelization on the Lower Colorado River have "altered habitat considerably." The Bill Williams River has been so modified that all native fish are assumed gone.
- **Sediment and passage barriers.** A sand delta at the Truckee River mouth blocked [[Cui-ui]] spawning runs, reducing abundance. The [[Marble Bluff Dam]] and fishway, then under construction, are intended to let the cui-ui and Lahontan cutthroat trout reach the Lower Truckee River.
- **Chemical contamination.** Chemical poisoning from an upstream source eliminated the *Gila robusta* population in Silver Creek, a Little Colorado River tributary.
- **Urban development and geothermal exploration.** Moapa dace habitat is affected by creek channelization and resort facilities on feeder hot springs. Geothermal leasing on private and BLM land is a potential threat to the Soldier Meadows dace.
- **Exotic species.** Black bass infested the Hot Creek Refuge, and introduced exotics compete with White River spring fish. African clawed frogs were also highlighted as a prohibited species problem.
- **Energy and water development.** BLM studies on Colorado squawfish, humpback chub and sucker in the White River are designed to assess two oil shale leases, including water quality. The Council also noted that many Green River native fishes have moved into the [[Yampa River]] to escape cold water released below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].

## Key Findings

**Lower Colorado River**
- Good populations of *Rhinichthys osculus* and native suckers exist in the Paria River, but *Plagopterus* appears to have been extirpated there.
- Small native sucker populations in the Zuni River are being protected through a proposed threatened listing and a habitat protection agreement with the tribe.
- The Virgin River has limited, apparently stable *Plagopterus* populations, and native sucker populations are not in immediate danger.
- Razorback sucker population sizes in [[Lake Mojave]] and [[Lake Havasu]] require further study.
- The [[Prohibited and Protected Committee]] listed 17 fish species and 20 reptile and amphibian species as prohibited, and was preparing a full checklist of Colorado River fishes, amphibians and reptiles.
- At [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], seven adult Colorado squawfish spawned in late May 1974 after hand spawning and chemical induction. About 5,000–7,000 fry were held in three systems. About 3,000 razorback sucker fry, raised from eggs hand-spawned from Lake Mojave adults, were also held, along with several adult razorbacks. Stocking locations were under consideration. (Fry sizes are garbled in the scan.)

**Upper Colorado River**
- The [[Endangered Species Committee]] of the Bonneville Chapter of the American Fisheries Society completed a report on threatened and endangered fishes in Utah. Paul Holden's dissertation covers the distribution, abundance and life history of Upper Basin fishes.
- Studies sponsored by the Cooperative Fisheries Unit at Utah State University include blood chemistry for Colorado River cutthroat trout, radio telemetry of Colorado squawfish spawning requirements in the Yampa River, and telemetry of humpback suckers.
- Utah and Colorado state agencies planned studies under the 1973 [[Endangered Species Act]].
- The Council recommended that the Yampa be designated a wild river.

**Lahontan Basin**
- Nevada produced nearly 563,000 Lahontan cutthroat trout in 1974. Angling on Pyramid and Walker lakes was significant, with 137,000 angler-days in 1973.
- The Lahontan cutthroat trout is federally listed as endangered. The Nevada Department of Fish and Game favored delisting because of extensive culture and use, but recommended protecting the pure-strain population in Summit Lake, Nevada.
- Cui-ui harvest on Pyramid Lake was restricted to Indian fishermen from 1969. Culture techniques are being tested, and the Marble Bluff fishway is expected to help the population recover.
- The Soldier Meadows dace is not federally listed but is protected under state regulation. It is found only in the Soldier Meadows area of Humboldt County. Relocation to other springs, particularly BLM lands, is being explored.

**Interbasin**
- Cal Allan considered the Moapa dace the most endangered fish in Nevada. An inventory is planned to determine whether transplants are feasible, and an artificial refuge is under consideration.
- White River spring fish are secure in many areas but are being displaced by exotics. Treatment of the Hot Creek Refuge and reintroduction are planned.
- The Railroad Valley springfish population remains secure. The Pahranagat bonytail and White River spinedace are also considered secure.

**Oregon Lakes**
- Carl Bond's report covers the Klamath Basin, Goose Lake, Summer Lake, Silver Lake, Warner Valley, Alvord and Harney basins, and other drainages. Ichthyologists have studied these areas for about nine decades, beginning with Cope's exploration.

**Session IV and Resolutions**
- A resolution was passed opposing interbasin transfer of indigenous fishes. The Secretary was directed to draft it for distribution to Great Basin and Colorado River Wildlife Council members.
- The chairman was authorized to write to the National Park Service asking that the artificial shelf in Devils Hole be removed and that artificial lighting be addressed.
- Coordination with state agencies was assigned to the area coordinator, who would also substantiate the endangered status of fishes in his area. The concept was judged favorable for its first year.
- Miriam Romero asked the Council to request that the Riverside District close the south end of Amargosa Canyon to off-road vehicles, and she recommended an interagency management meeting.
- The seventh annual symposium was set for Death Valley, November 12–14, 1975.
