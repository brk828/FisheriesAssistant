# Introduced Asiatic Clam (Corbicula) in Central Arizona Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyrenidae

## Executive Summary

This 1974 study by [[John N Rinne]] (Arizona State University) quantifies the distribution, density, and biomass of the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula*) in two central Arizona reservoirs, [[Roosevelt Lake]] and [[Apache Lake]], part of the [[Salt River Reservoir System]]. Densities were estimated with [[Quadrat Sampling]] (m² quadrats) and [[Ekman Dredge]] sampling. Clam abundance rose with water depth and down-lake distance from turbid inflows, and was highest on complex rock-rubble slopes. The author concludes that food supply, substrate complexity, turbidity and possibly [[Fish Predation]] jointly control clam populations.

## Background & Description

*Corbicula* was first recorded in Arizona in 1956 from the Phoenix canal system, most likely introduced by people (tourists, anglers, or aquarium hobbyists). It was first recorded in abundance in the Southwest in the Coachella Valley, California, in 1953. Following eradication from the Colorado River Indian Reservation irrigation systems in 1963, it re-invaded and has since spread through the entire lower [[Colorado River Basin]]. [[Sinclair]] (1971) described it as "currently the most costly liability of all exotic molluscs in North America."

In the Salt River system, clams are most abundant in Roosevelt and Apache lakes and are scarce in the lower reservoirs, Canyon and Saguaro lakes, for which the author had no explanation. Locally they form a major component of the benthic fauna of the upper two reservoirs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling conditions.** Specimens were collected in November 1970, 1971 and February 1972, mostly dead or moribund on exposed shorelines during low water. Exposure time before collection varied (from under one month to over a year), which could bias density estimates. Predation by mammals and birds and human disturbance were recognized shortcomings.

**Biomass estimation.** Shell-free dry and ash-free dry weights were measured on live clams. A power-function regression of size on weight gave r = +0.98 (equation: y = [0.0110608] × x^3.0129), used to convert mean quadrat size to biomass per unit area.

**Physical and trophic drivers.**
- Turbidity: Roosevelt inflow areas were more turbid and had more phytoplankton (chlorophyll-a). Clams were denser down-lake, suggesting inorganic suspended solids may suppress populations despite adequate food.
- Food supply: In Apache Lake, density and biomass declined down-lake with sparser food and lower turbidity, suggesting food may be more limiting than turbidity.
- Depth: Densities rose with depth, possibly reflecting photic-zone phytoplankton and/or reduced fish predation in deeper water.
- Substrate: Rocks and boulders provide interstices that shelter juveniles from predators.
- Predators: [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Smallmouth Buffalofish]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*) and [[Black Buffalofish]] (*Ictiobus niger*) consume large numbers of clams.
- Filtration: Clams clear suspended particles and deposit pseudofeces; filtration rate is related to algal concentration, and above critical algal densities they indiscriminately precipitate particles, likely to keep gills clear.
- Human harvest: Heavy harvesting at Frazier's Landing, a major boat launch, produced markedly lower densities.

## Key Findings

- **Roosevelt Lake:** Densities ranged from 12 to 243/m²; biomass from 1.4 to 137.8 kg/ha. Densities at sites about 1 km east of R-2 and north of Roosevelt were 2 to 6 times those near inflows.
- **Apache Lake:** Densities ranged from 5.0 to nearly 600/m²; standing crops from 0.65 to 1,060 kg/ha (shell excluded). Densities at the Burnt Corral transect (BC-1) peaked on rubble at 3.4–18 m depth (e.g., 383.0/m² at 9.0 m; 380.0/m² at 13.4 m), and were very low on sand-silt or sand-gravel substrates.
- **Substrate:** On paired samples in February 1972, rock-boulder substrate held 2.5 to 9.0 times the density of sand-gravel substrate.
- **Depth and size:** Average clam size did not change consistently or significantly with depth. ANOVA showed no significant depth differences among transects.
- **Lake comparison:** Roosevelt and Apache did not differ significantly (α = 0.05) in biomass or numbers, though trends ran inversely up- to down-lake between the two reservoirs.
- **Ekman dredge:** Yielded few clams, mainly because they occupy soft, fine sediments that dredges sample efficiently. Estimates (e.g., 43–86/m² at A-2) were comparable to quadrat results (26/m²).
- **Peak concentration:** The highest recorded density (about 1,500/m²) occurred in the canal below Granite Reef Dam, where water was clear and food adequate.
- **Conclusion:** Ideal habitat combines clear water, adequate phytoplankton food, and complex rocky substrate; fish predation and turbidity are important limiting factors.
