# The Life History of the Stripetail Darter (Etheostoma kennicotti) in Big Creek, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey Biological Notes report (No. 93, February 1975) by Lawrence M. Page documents the [[Life History]] of the [[Stripetail Darter]], *Etheostoma kennicotti*, in [[Big Creek]], Hardin County, Illinois. Based on 1,380 preserved specimens collected between 6 October 1970 and 21 April 1974, the study covers habitat use, reproduction, spawning behavior, growth, demography, diet, and interactions with other organisms. It is the second of three reports on subgenus *Catonotus* darters in southeastern Illinois and adjacent Kentucky, following [[Spottail Darter]] (*E. squamiceps*) and preceding *E. obeyense*.

## Background & Description

*E. kennicotti* was described by Putnam (1863) from specimens collected by Robert Kennicott in "a rocky brook in Southern Illinois." Its range includes the Ohio drainage of southern Illinois, small areas of Ohio River tributaries in northwestern Kentucky, the Green River and upper [[Cumberland River]] systems (Kentucky and Tennessee), and [[Tennessee River]] tributaries in Tennessee, Alabama, Mississippi, and Georgia.

Breeding males develop an orange-gold body, darkened head, nape, and breast, black vertical caudal and soft-dorsal banding, and swollen orange knobs on the first dorsal fin spines. Females and nonbreeding males share nearly identical, white-to-light-brown patterns. Hatchlings measure 5.7 mm total length. Maximum recorded size in Big Creek is 54 mm standard length; specimens to 69 mm occur in the upper Cumberland system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Big Creek is a clear, spring-fed direct tributary of the [[Ohio River]], with gravel riffles, shallow slab-rock pools, and deeper sand-and-gravel non-slab pools. Adults showed a strong preference for slab pools (for example, 82–100% of adults in most months of Table 1), which are shallow upper-elevation headwater pools used for cover. In contrast, [[Spottail Darter]] usually favors slab riffles. Slab pools were also the most productive habitat at other Illinois, Kentucky, and Tennessee sites sampled.

Spawning occurs under slab rocks in shallow (usually under 30 cm) upstream pools, where the current is slight. Deep water (over 40 cm) was avoided and nests were rarely found in swift water. Water temperatures during most spawning were 14–20 °C; aquarium spawning occurred at 19–22 °C.

The species co-occurs with ten commonly associated fishes in southern Illinois, including *Pimephales notatus*, *Semotilus atromaculatus*, and *Campostoma anomalum*. Its distribution and abundance appear limited by other [[Catonotus]] species, which compete for slab-rock spawning sites.

## Key Findings

**Habitat and density**
- Adults preferred slab pools; young preferred slab pools but were sometimes more common in non-slab pools.
- The highest density recorded was 6.5 darters/m² (young and adults combined) in slab pools in July.
- Young-of-year and older fish combined made up 55.7% of the sample; 41.0% were 1–2 years old and 3.3% were over 2 years.

**Reproduction**
- Males enlarge their genital papilla, develop large white spongy testes, and defend slab-rock nest cavities from March–April. No breeding tubercles develop.
- Females reached maturity at about 30 mm (about one year old); males at about 35 mm. Mature ova per ripe female ranged from 24 to 130 (17 females).
- Eggs averaged 2.1 mm in diameter, are translucent, and contain a single oil droplet. Nest counts ranged from 50 to 400 eggs (mean 212 from 12 nests), often including eggs from several females.
- Spawning occurred mainly from late April to early May. Eggs hatched in 145–150 hours (6.0–6.3 days) at 19–23 °C.
- Heavy feeding on chironomid larvae stimulated spawning in aquaria; only males guarded nests.

**Sexual dimorphism and territoriality**
- Of 20 characters tested in 41 specimens over 45 mm, four showed significant dimorphism (P < 0.005): females had more pored lateral-line scales; males had deeper caudal peduncles, longer caudal fins, and longer soft dorsal fins.
- Males are strongly territorial during spawning; females are occasionally territorial. The first male to spawn in an aquarium was the only male to spawn there.

**Growth, sex ratio and longevity**
- Growth slowed with age. Males were significantly larger than females by the third year (t = 3.46, df = 44).
- Females predominated overall (1.5 females : 1 male; χ² = 47.49, P < 0.005). Males showed greater longevity in the 2+ class.
- Maximum longevity in Big Creek is apparently 2+ years, versus 3+ years for *E. squamiceps*.

**Diet**
- 333 stomachs were examined; the main food items were chironomid larvae, copepods, mayfly naiads, cladocerans, and ostracods.
- Sixteen food taxa were recorded, compared with 22 for *E. squamiceps* in the same area.
- Small fish (<21 mm) fed mainly on mayflies, copepods, chironomids, and ostracods; large fish (>40 mm) on mayflies, chironomids, isopods, and amphipods.

**Interactions**
- No predation on this species was found; 5 *Esox americanus* and 12 *Lepomis cyanellus* stomachs contained no darters.
- Infestation by *Acanthocephalus jacksoni* (63 worms in 33 of 333 fish) occurred only from November through March, far lower than in *E. squamiceps*.
- A natural hybrid with *E. squamiceps* is known (one 40 mm male). Experimental crosses with *E. obeyense* produced eggs, but the young lived only a few days.
