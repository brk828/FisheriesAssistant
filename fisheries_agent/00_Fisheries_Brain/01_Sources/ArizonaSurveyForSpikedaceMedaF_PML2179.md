# Arizona Survey for Spikedace and Loach Minnow: Fort Apache and San Carlos Indian Reservations and Eastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 technical report, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Office of Endangered Species, documents a May 1985 field survey of the upper [[Salt River Basin]] and upper [[Gila River Basin]] in eastern Arizona. The survey tested suspected declines and widespread extirpation of two federally listed minnows, [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). Sampling covered 28 localities (16 in the upper Salt, 12 in the upper Gila). Native fishes remained widespread, but the target species were scarce: Loach Minnow was found at only two stream reaches in the upper Salt, and Spikedace was absent or extremely rare. The authors attribute these patterns to habitat deterioration, flow alteration, and interactions with [[Non-Native Fishes]].

## Background & Description

The report was authored by David L. Propst (New Mexico Department of Game and Fish), Paul C. Marsh ([[Arizona State University]] Center for Environmental Studies), and Wendell L. Minckley (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Dexter National Fish Hatchery). Earlier sampling had documented marked declines of native fishes in the region (Minckley 1973; Rinne and Minckley 1986), and several systems were known to support mostly non-native fish communities.

The survey targeted stream reaches with a prior record of either target species or a high probability of historic occurrence. Site selection prioritized habitat suitability over collection history.

**Habitat preferences of the target species:**
- Loach Minnow: turbulent riffles in intermediate-sized, moderate-gradient streams, with medium-to-high velocities, cobble-rubble substrate, and seasonal filamentous algae.
- Spikedace: lower-gradient, lower-velocity streams with eddying pools and gravel-sand bottoms.

**Methods:**
- Primary collection: [[Electrofishing]], supplemented by seines and gill nets where appropriate.
- Effort concentrated on micro-habitats of the target species (riffles, shallows, stream margins, pools, undercut banks).
- Fishes were preserved in 10% formalin and deposited in the [[Arizona State University Fish Collection]]. Larval fishes were abundant and remained unidentified; their data were to be issued as an addendum.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area:** Survey sites lay in Graham, Greenlee, Gila, and Navajo counties, ranging from small warmwater tributaries to several larger rivers. Table 1 lists the legal coordinates for all sites, most of which were on tributaries of the [[Salt River]] and [[Gila River]].

**Documented threats and drivers:**
- Flooding in the prior winter and spring had apparently favored native fishes. Non-natives were common before flooding and were reduced afterward, a pattern the authors link to [[Differential Selection by Flooding]].
- Low sampling efficiency and high discharges limited collections at several large-river sites, so some species were likely present but undetected.
- Habitat quality declines from overgrazing, impoundment, and other stream alterations were cited as reasons for the target species' decline.
- Residual populations of Loach Minnow are small and vulnerable to flow changes, degraded habitat, and interactions with non-native fishes.

**Native fishes formerly present but now extirpated or scarce:** [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo apache*, headwaters), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), the latter two in larger streams.

## Key Findings

**Overall results**
- Collections were made at 16 upper Salt River localities and 12 upper Gila River localities.
- Upper Salt River: five native and three non-native species were collected.
- Upper Gila River: collections were mostly from [[Eagle Creek]].

**Upper Salt River basin (Table 2, relative abundance)**
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) was the most common and widespread native, found at 14 of 16 localities (and probably present at two large-river sites where it was not collected).
- [[Desert Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarkii*) occurred at 12 of 16 sites.
- [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*) occurred at 8 of 16 sites.
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) was taken only from the Salt River mainstream and three stations on Carrizo Creek.
- Loach Minnow occurred in only two streams: East Fork White River and a short mainstem White River reach just downstream of the North and East Fork confluence.
- Non-native species were relatively restricted: [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*, *S. trutta*) and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*).

**Upper Gila River basin**
- [[Eagle Creek]] yielded, in decreasing order of abundance: [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), Sonoran Sucker, Desert Mountain Sucker, Speckled Dace, a problematic chub (*Gila* sp.), and Roundtail Chub. Rainbow Trout and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) were the non-natives collected. [[Red Shiner]] was observed but not collected.
- [[San Carlos River]] supported only non-native Fathead Minnow and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*).
- [[Blue River]] held only native [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*) and Desert Mountain Sucker.
- [[Aravaipa Creek]], a comparison site known to support the target species, yielded Longfin Dace, Loach Minnow, Spikedace, Desert Mountain Sucker, and Sonoran Sucker.

**Conclusions**
- The survey confirmed the suspected rarity of Loach Minnow and the absence or extreme rarity of Spikedace in the upper Salt and Gila basins.
- Four species (Speckled Dace, Longfin Dace, Sonoran Sucker, Desert Mountain Sucker) were widespread. Roundtail Chub, Gila chub, and the problematic chub were restricted and uncommon.
- The problematic chub is closer to Gila intermedia than to Gila robusta and may be of hybrid origin. Its taxonomic status is uncertain.
- Non-native species expected but not collected included [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), Red Shiner, bullhead catfishes, and [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*).
- Loach Minnow is concentrated in Aravaipa Creek, Arizona, and the Tularosa River mainstem, New Mexico. Elsewhere, residual populations are small and at risk.
