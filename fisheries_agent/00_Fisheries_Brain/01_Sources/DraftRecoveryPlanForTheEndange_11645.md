# Recovery Plan for the Endangered and Threatened Fishes of the Rio Yaqui

#ichthyology #conservation #Cyprinidae #Poeciliidae #Ictaluridae #endangered-species #recovery-plan

## Executive Summary

This recovery plan addresses four federally listed fish species from the [[Rio Yaqui Basin]]: two endangered species—[[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) and [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*)—and two threatened species—[[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*) and [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Cyprinella formosa*). All four species formerly occurred throughout the Rio Yaqui Basin in the USA and Mexico. Current USA populations are limited primarily to [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge]] in Cochise County, Arizona, and Hidalgo County, New Mexico. Current distribution in Mexico is unknown or declining. The plan was prepared by the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service, Douglas, Arizona, for approval by the Regional Director in Albuquerque, New Mexico.

## Background & Description

### Taxonomic and Physical Descriptions

**[[Yaqui Chub]]** (*Gila purpurea*): A robust cyprinid rarely exceeding 150 mm in length, first described by Girard in 1856. The head and anterior body are thickened, with the dorsal fin origin slightly behind the pelvic fins. A vertically elongated, diffuse, triangle-shaped caudal spot is usually present. Males become metallic blue during breeding season; females remain straw colored. In 1991, DeMarais concluded that all chubs except those from Rio San Bernardino (Black Draw) represented a new species, *Gila eremica*. Historic distribution was restricted to San Bernardino Ranch in Arizona and Sonora and the Sulphur Springs Valley of Arizona.

**[[Yaqui Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*): A small livebearer (Poeciliidae). Males usually do not exceed 25 mm in length; females average 30 to 40 mm. Both sexes are olive colored, with breeding males almost black. Scales of the dorsum are darkly outlined and fin rays outlined with melanophores, lacking dark spots. Historically abundant throughout the Rio Yaqui drainage in southeastern Arizona and in Sonora and Chihuahua, Mexico. Prefers shallow, warm, fairly quiet waters with dense mats of algae and debris, usually along stream margins or below riffles, with sandy substrates sometimes covered with organic mud and debris.

**[[Yaqui Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus pricei*): Usually dark gray to black dorsally, white to grayish beneath; darker overall than either channel or blue catfish. Barbels are jet-black except on the chin, where they are gray to whitened. The caudal fin is shallowly forked. Ecology and behavior are probably similar to [[Channel Catfish]]. Now extirpated in the USA; historic occurrence was likely in San Bernardino Creek. Collected from Rios Sirupa and Aros in Mexico in 1987 and 1990; currently held at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]].

**[[Beautiful Shiner]]** (*Cyprinella formosa*): Two subspecies exist—the Yaqui Beautiful Shiner (native to Rio Yaqui) and the Guzman Beautiful Shiner (native to Mimbres River and Guzman Basin in New Mexico and Chihuahua, Mexico). A small cyprinid rarely exceeding 50 mm, with silvery coloration and orange to yellow-orange fins. The body is compressed with a relatively pointed snout and oblique mouth. Breeding males have a bluish body often masked by an overall wash of orange, pink, or yellow. The Yaqui beautiful shiner was reintroduced into the USA in 1990; fish were collected in Arroyo Moctezuma, Chihuahua, Mexico and stocked into Twin Pond on [[San Bernardino NWR]] in May 1990.

### Historical Context

The Rio Yaqui basin of northwestern Mexico comprises about 73,000 square kilometers of mountainous terrain. The system lies between Longitude 27 and 32°N and Latitude 107 and 110°40', beginning as the Rio Papigochic in western Chihuahua, Mexico, entering eastern Sonora to receive the Rio de Bavispe, then flowing southward to enter the Gulf of California near Ciudad Obregon, Sonora. The USA portion encompasses 1,500 square kilometers (less than 2 percent of the Rio Yaqui watershed) in Cochise County, Arizona, and Hidalgo County, New Mexico.

Spanish presence in the San Bernardino valley dates back to 1694 when Father Eusebio Francisco Kino passed through the area. Cattle were introduced in 1822 when the San Bernardino Land Grant was acquired by Ignacio Perez. In 1884, John Slaughter purchased the Land Grant and operated a large cattle operation until 1937. [[The Nature Conservancy]] purchased the San Bernardino Ranch in 1980 and Leslie Canyon in 1988 to protect the Yaqui chub from extinction. These parcels were sold to the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service in 1982 and 1988 respectively to establish the San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Requirements

The diversity of aquatic habitats in the Rio Yaqui basin is high. Mountain creeks are cold and clear and support various trouts (*Oncorhynchus* sp.). Most fish species of the basin, including those treated here, live at intermediate to lower elevations in springs, cienegas, creeks, and moderate-sized rivers. [[Cienegas]]—stream-associated marshlands characterized by low, emergent aquatic plants and hydric-adapted trees such as willows—were of marked historical importance. Streams typically have alternating riffles and pools, heterogeneity in the form of undercut banks, cover associated with boulders and woody debris, and clean gravel bottoms in swifter areas, vegetated with abundant growths of algae.

In the United States, Yaqui fishes are heavily dependent on artesian wells and spring flows on [[San Bernardino NWR]]. Two stream sections, Leslie Creek and Black Draw, also contain Yaqui fishes. Natural waters that support fishes in Douglas Valley include Rucker Creek, Leslie and Whitewater Creeks, associated cienegas, and springs including a reach in Black Draw (= San Bernardino Creek or Rio de San Bernardino).

### Limiting Factors and Threats

Water development and pumping of underground aquifers constitute the greatest threat to survival of Yaqui fishes, followed closely by introduction of non-native fishes. Physical impacts associated with use and misuse of the watershed, diversion and modification of stream channels, excessive pumping of underground aquifers (reducing spring flows), lowering of water tables due to stream channel incision (leading to desiccation of cienega habitat), and introduction of exotics ([[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Channel Catfish]]) have contributed to the decline of native fishes. Yaqui topminnow populations have declined and disappeared coincident with the introduction of mosquitofish into the same habitat. Reasons for decline of Yaqui catfish include habitat loss and hybridization with introduced channel catfish.

### Conservation Status

A drought in 1969 nearly eliminated all Yaqui fishes in the USA. Astin Spring was the last habitat containing Yaqui chub and topminnow. Before the spring dried completely, Dr. W.L. Minckley captured and moved approximately 200 chub and topminnow into Leslie Creek. In 1976, Leslie Creek was hard hit by drought and fish were removed and taken to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]. Fish were held and propagated at Dexter until reintroduced onto the San Bernardino Ranch in 1980.

Current distribution: [[Yaqui Chub]] is found on San Bernardino and Leslie Canyon NWRs, in West Turkey Creek in the Chiricahua Mountains, in House Pond on the Slaughter Ranch Historic site, and a short distance into Mexico in San Bernardino Creek below the international border. [[Yaqui Topminnow]] is found only on the two wildlife refuges in the United States; present distribution in Mexico is unknown. [[Beautiful Shiner]] was reintroduced onto San Bernardino NWR in 1990 after fish were collected under permit in Mexico. [[Yaqui Catfish]] is currently being held at Dexter NFH & TC for future reintroduction onto San Bernardino NWR.

## Key Findings

### Recovery Objectives

The objective is to restore the Yaqui fishes as secure and stable members of the native fish fauna of the river systems where they once occurred. San Bernardino/Leslie Canyon NWR will serve as a refugium and source of stock for recovery. Due to limited habitat in the USA (at the northern limit of species range), recovery cannot be accomplished entirely in the USA. The Republic of Mexico must be made a full partner in recovery efforts for delisting to occur.

### Recovery Criteria

**For downlisting [[Yaqui Chub]] and [[Yaqui Topminnow]] to threatened status**, all the following conditions must be met within current occupied habitat for a period of 10 years:

1. Eradicate all non-native fish species from critical habitat
2. Secure and protect the San Bernardino aquifer so that all artesian well flows are maintained throughout the year; secure and protect the Leslie Creek watershed to ensure adequate flows
3. Protect critical habitat from human disturbances including mining, introduction of non-native fishes, water diversion and removal

**Yaqui Chub**: Downlist to threatened when (a) viable, self-sustaining populations have been established on San Bernardino/Leslie Canyon NWR, and (b) viable, self-sustaining population is established and secure in West Turkey Creek. Due to limited historic distribution of *Gila purpurea*, delisting is not currently considered an option.

**Yaqui Topminnow**: Downlist when stable populations have survived for at least ten years in all suitable existing and reclaimed SBNWR habitats.

**Beautiful Shiner**: Delist when (a) Arizona populations are established and secure, (b) the Guzman beautiful shiner is reestablished as a secure, viable, self-sustaining population in the Mimbres River and other available habitat within its historic range in New Mexico, and (c) viable, self-sustaining populations are secure within historic range in Mexico.

**Yaqui Catfish**: Delist when (a) recovery occurs in the Republic of Mexico, and (b) San Bernardino NWR is maintained as a refugium to maintain gene pool.

### Action Priorities

The plan identifies six priority needs:

1. **Develop cooperative effort with Mexico** for recovery of Yaqui fishes
2. **Secure habitat and water sources** for Yaqui fishes in USA and Mexico
3. **Conduct research** on biology and habitat requirements of Yaqui fishes
4. **Manage the fish and their essential habitats**
5. **Introduce and maintain self-sustaining populations** within historic range
6. **Monitor existing and established populations and habitats**

### Specific Management Actions

**Task 1**: Pursue an agreement and develop a management plan for long-term survival of Rio Yaqui fishes in Mexico. Because Yaqui fishes recovery cannot occur entirely in the United States, cooperation and support from Mexico is essential.

**Task 2**: Protect historic habitat by (2.1) maintaining subsurface waters to protect artesian well flows and surface waters, (2.2) obtaining instream flow rights sufficient to maintain surface flows for Leslie Creek and Black Draw, (2.3) acquiring and protecting essential habitats and waters, and (2.4) working with water users and appropriate public agencies to prevent overuse of water from the aquifer. Currently SBNWR and Mexican farmers use the same aquifer that supplies water to the SBNWR artesian system.

**Task 3**: Manage existing habitat and populations by (3.1) determining water required to maintain listed species at SBNWR, (3.2) determining aquifer configuration and capacity, (3.3) revising and continuing implementation of Refuge Master Plan (including water use plan, fish management plan for each species, and genetic monitoring plan), (3.4) enhancing and monitoring existing habitats, and (3.5) preventing introduction of non-native species.

**Task 4**: Determine habitat and biological requirements by (4.1) examining life histories, (4.2) determining impacts of intra- and inter-specific interactions, (4.3) determining habitat requirements and utilization, (4.4) determining genetic composition of existing populations, and (4.5) monitoring fish populations and occupied habitats (minimum twice per year).

**Task 5**: Assess habitat for reintroduction and reintroduce fishes within appropriate habitat in historic range by (5.1) identifying areas for possible reintroductions, (5.2) developing culture techniques for Yaqui catfish (breeding protocol, determining fish size/time of year/stocking density, stocking into suitable historic habitat), and (5.3) working with public agencies and private landowners to manage existing and reintroduced populations.

**Task 6**: Develop Information and Education programs for all four species at local, state, and national levels to give the public better understanding of Yaqui fishes, highlighting their plight and value as part of the natural fauna of the United States and Republic of Mexico.

### Current Population Status

Life history information is lacking on the Yaqui chub. They appear to prefer deep pool habitat and are able to withstand extreme changes in water flow. Feeding habits have not been defined, although they are opportunistic predators on invertebrates with some evidence indicating a preference for algae. Yaqui chub were almost extirpated in 1969 when a drought dried up Astin Spring; 200 chub were removed and placed into Leslie Creek. Stock from these fish were moved to Dexter NFH in 1976 and raised at the hatchery. Hatchery-produced fish from Dexter were put into the newly established SBNWR in 1980 to supplement stock thought to have survived in the well pipe and outflow of an artesian well on the ranch.

Yaqui topminnow food habits are rather generalized and include bottom debris, vegetative materials, amphipod crustaceans, and insect larvae, including mosquito larvae. The main threat has been the introduction and presence of mosquitofish for mosquito control.

Little is known about the life history of Yaqui catfish, although ecology and behavior are probably similar to channel catfish. Permission for collecting additional broodstock in Mexico is being sought to supplement existing fish. Yaqui catfish are declining in Mexico due to hybridization with non-native catfishes.

Little information is available about the biology or ecology of beautiful shiner. Hendrickson (1980) reported it to occur in riffles of small streams and in intermittent pools with a high percentage of riffle habitat in wetter periods. On SBNWR, shiners have adapted to pond habitats where they are thriving. Dexter currently maintains a stock of the Guzman beautiful shiner for possible reintroduction into the Mimbres River of New Mexico.
