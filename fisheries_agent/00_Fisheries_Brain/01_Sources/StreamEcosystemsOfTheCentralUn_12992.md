# Stream Ecosystems of the Central United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter, by Arthur V. Brown and William J. Matthews, reviews the ecology of streams in the central United States, a region of roughly 3 x 10^6 km² covering the Great Plains, Osage Plains, Coastal Plains of Texas and western Louisiana, and the Interior Highlands. The authors argue that streams here are shaped by a harsh, highly variable flow regime (drought-driven intermittency, spates, and extreme temperatures), by a riffle-pool geomorphology in alluvial gravel channels, and by widespread modification from reservoirs, channelization, agriculture and woody-debris removal. The chapter describes the physical setting, climate, geology, riparian vegetation, primary and secondary production, decomposers, particulate organic matter, fish assemblages and trophic structure, and ecosystem generalizations. The Illinois River (Arkansas and Oklahoma) is presented as a model fifth-order stream.

## Background & Description

- **Region:** The mid-continental United States, from the Mississippi River west to the Continental Divide, and from Canada south to the Gulf of Mexico, is mostly plains. Most of its rivers drain northwest to southeast and are tributaries of the Mississippi River.
- **Climate gradient:** Mean annual precipitation falls from more than 1000 mm along the lower Mississippi to about 200 mm at the western boundary, which lies in the rain shadow of the [[Rocky Mountains]]. Runoff ranges from about 100 mm/yr in central Minnesota to about 500 mm/yr in Louisiana, and less than 10 mm/yr just east of the Rockies.
- **Topography and geology:** Nine significant upland regions exist. Much of the area was inundated by shallow inland seas, leaving sandstone, limestone, shale and unconsolidated deposits. The northeast was modified by glaciation. The [[Interior Highlands]] (Ouachita Mountains and Ozark Plateaus) escaped recent glaciation and inundation and served as a biotic refuge.
- **Stream types:** Streams are classified as ephemeral (dry part of the year), intermittent (only pools persist in the dry season) or perennial. Large western-origin rivers follow the [[River Continuum Concept]] longitudinal profile but lose flow crossing the Great Plains.
- **Vegetation:** Stream-side vegetation is chiefly deciduous, including [[Gallery Forest]] communities of sycamore, oaks, elms, hackberry and willows. Prairie streams usually had gallery forests, while the eastern slopes of the Rockies are conifer-dominated but still have deciduous riparian corridors.
- **Model stream:** The [[Illinois River]] in northwestern Arkansas and northeastern Oklahoma (drainage area 4300 km², about 48% grassland used for grazing, the rest mostly forest) is proposed as a representative large-scale unit, a fifth-order stream with its terminal reservoir, [[Tenkiller Reservoir]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and temperature.** Flow is highly variable and unpredictable across much of the region. Headwaters tend to cease flowing from mid-summer to mid-fall. Spates are most likely from late fall through late spring. Recovery of benthic invertebrates after severe spates may take a year or more in the central region, versus one to two months in Sonoran Desert streams (Fisher et al., 1982). Pools may reach about 40°C, often worsened by high salinity. Ice cover can persist for months in North Dakota streams.

**Riffle-pool structure.** Alluvial gravel streams show distinct riffle-pool sequences. Pools (roughly 38–620 m long in orders 1–5) alternate with riffles (roughly 10–65 m). Pools serve as sediment traps and riffles act as debris dams, so these streams are retentive at low flow but flush organic matter during spates. In low-flow periods the system functions more like a chain of occasionally connected small lakes.

**Riparian and woody debris.** Canopies are often open, so primary production can be high. Woody debris is moderately abundant but seldom forms debris dams in alluvial channels. Beavers (Castor canadensis) are an important source of debris, damming northern streams but mainly denning in banks in the south. Snagging of large woody debris on the Mississippi and White rivers is practised for navigation.

**Land-use and alteration.** Agriculture, grazing, timber conversion to pine, oil production, wildfire control, pesticides and fertilizers, levees and channelization have all altered these systems. Damming is described as having altered regional streams "more significantly and irreversibly than any other of man's activities." Reservoirs reset the river continuum, and tailwaters resemble smaller, cooler perennial streams.

**Fish fauna and biogeography.** More than 270 freshwater fish species occupy central US streams. Historic dispersal occurred along a hypothesised north-south "Ancestral Plains Stream," while later vicariance events, including dissection of the upland and splitting of the Ozark and Ouachita uplands by the Arkansas River, produced regional isolation and endemism.

## Key Findings

**Physical and hydrological setting**
- Streams are mainly controlled by climate, geology and vegetation; flow is highly variable and unpredictable, with headwater cessation from mid-summer to mid-fall.
- Riffle-pool geomorphology dominates the Illinois River system and is the main determinant of patch dynamics, invertebrate distribution and fish trophic structure.
- Flow reversal occurs between riffles and pools when flow exceeds half bank-full.
- Reservoirs have seriously altered flow, temperature and biota in nearly all fifth-order and larger streams. No large river in the region remains a suitable natural reference.

**Primary production**
- Hill and Gardner (1987a) reported gross primary production (GPP) of 1.09 and 1.35 gC m⁻² d⁻¹ for perennial and intermittent Blackland Prairie streams in Texas.
- Gelroth and Marzolf (1978) reported 0.19–0.45 gC m⁻² d⁻¹ in an undisturbed Flint Hills stream, and 1.56 gC m⁻² d⁻¹ in a channelized, unshaded reach.
- Duffer and Dorris (1966) reported GPP varying from 3.79 gC m⁻² d⁻¹ in winter to 18.0 gC m⁻² d⁻¹ in summer in a Blue River granite outcrop community.
- Net primary production in the Illinois River was 0.14–0.21 gC m⁻² d⁻¹ in a fifth-order riffle, and 0.28–0.92 gC m⁻² d⁻¹ in the third-order Spring Creek (Woomer, 1986).
- Production seldom exceeded respiration anywhere in the region.
- Grazing by the algivorous [[Central Stoneroller]] (Campostoma anomalum) controls algal standing crop and species composition more strongly than nutrient limitation.

**Litter fall and organic matter**
- Gallery-forest litter fall in prairie gallery forests (about 400–700 g m⁻² yr⁻¹) equals or exceeds that of eastern US deciduous forests.
- Tall-grass prairie litter fall in Kansas (about 123 g m⁻² yr⁻¹) was about 34% of gallery-forest litter fall (about 357 g m⁻² yr⁻¹) (Gurtz et al., 1988).
- Benthic particulate organic matter storage in Texas prairie streams ranged from about 10 to 40 g DW m⁻² (Hill et al., 1988), much less than in the Kings Creek drainage.
- Central US streams are generally "leaky" for organic matter, with few debris dams and unpredictable high flows.

**Secondary production and invertebrates**
- Hellgrammite (Corydalus cornutus) production ranged from 1.62 to 13.19 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ in four south Texas streams (Short et al., 1987).
- Stonefly production in Battle Branch, Oklahoma, was 6.1 g DW m⁻² yr⁻¹ (Jop and Stewart, 1987).
- In Minnesota, North Branch Creek had the highest invertebrate wet biomass production (119.6 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ versus 27.0 and 36.9), linked to higher alkalinity (245 vs. 34 and 83 mg CaCO₃ L⁻¹) and fish standing stocks (466.4 vs. 46.6 and 134.9 kg ha⁻¹) (Krueger and Waters, 1983).
- Shredders are less abundant than elsewhere, and collector-gatherers and scrapers dominate. Species richness of intermittent reaches is about half that of perennial reaches.
- Zooplankton, mainly rotifers (Asplanchna priodonta, Branchionus caudatus) and copepod nauplii, are abundant and trophically important in Ozark riffle-pool streams.

**Decomposition**
- Leaf decomposition in the Illinois River, Arkansas, is much faster than in other north-temperate streams and nearly independent of shredders, suggesting rapid microbial processing.
- Decomposition in northeastern Kansas streams approximately equals rates reported elsewhere in north-temperate streams.

**Fish assemblages**
- A single river drainage may hold 33 to 173 fish species. A creek or small river may hold about 100 species (Strawberry River), under 50 in small Ozark streams, and about 30 in prairie-margin creeks. Arid western catchments may hold fewer than 20.
- In any 200–300 m reach, it is unusual to find more than about 25 species, and the typical number is 5 to 15.
- Riffles are dominated by benthic insectivores (darters, madtoms, sculpins). Pools support more water-column fishes, including minnows, sunfish and topminnows, along with piscivores such as smallmouth bass.
- Piscivores exert cascading effects through several trophic levels, and grazers such as Campostoma alter ecosystem properties including productivity, C:N ratios and invertebrate community structure.

**Ecosystem generalizations and conclusions**
- Headwaters (orders 1–3) are periodically stressed by extreme flow, temperature and water chemistry, but intervening stable periods allow strong biotic interactions.
- Biotic and abiotic controls both operate in most streams, but their relative importance differs across time and location. Disturbance by flood and drought, acting with seasonal change, forms the fundamental template for stream phenomena.
- Human modifications, including reservoirs, channelization, agriculture, and pine conversion, have substantially reduced the reference condition of these streams.
- The authors call for more research on decomposers, meiofauna, secondary production of whole fish assemblages, non-point-source pollutants, and the ecological role of woody debris.
