# Bonytail Chub (Gila elegans) — Captive Holding & Rehabilitation Case Report, Willow Beach NFH

## Executive Summary
This document is a hatchery chronology (not a morphological/ecological field study) describing the emergency holding, injury management, and disease treatment of 14 wild-caught [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) transferred from [[Lake Mohave]] to the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] between May 11–26, 1993. Fish were captured via trammel nets during spawning in "Carp Cove," resulting in severe physical trauma, skin abrasion, and high physiological stress. Over a two-week period, hatchery staff — led by biologist Glenn S. Green — implemented a sequence of temperature tempering, formalin flow-through treatments, potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) topical and flow-through applications, and standing salt (NaCl/MgSO₄) baths to manage fungal infection (*Saprolegnia* spp.) and secondary bacterial/endemic complications. Two of the 14 fish died during treatment; survivors were transported to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] on May 26, 1993. The report closes with management commentary questioning the ethics and chemical protocols used, given the fish's endangered status and its origin in a self-sustaining wild population.

## Background & Description
While this is a husbandry/incident report rather than a taxonomic description, several physical/pathological observations were recorded for the 14 [[Bonytail Chub]] specimens:

- **Diagnostic feature noted:** Characteristic dorsal hump — used as a landmark for describing net injury location (injuries occurred at the opercle region "just in front of" the hump).
- **Capture-related injuries (all 14 fish):**
  - Net marks completely encircling the head at the opercle region
  - Raw, scraped skin along lateral body surfaces, most severe in the caudal peduncle
  - Split and bloodied fins, most often the caudal fin
  - One individual with a discolored eye, likely a cataract
  - One individual with a bent, stiff caudal peduncle
  - Three individuals with severe, large open wounds on the body sides and caudal peduncle, causing stiff swimming
- **Disease progression:** 8 of 14 fish developed severe *Saprolegnia* (fungal) infections by May 18; three later developed secondary infections with visible tissue swelling (severe edema) around fungal lesions.
- **Reproductive state:** Fish were captured while actively spawning; one large gravid female was among the later deliveries — indicating specimens were reproductively mature adults collected during the breeding season.
- **Mortality:** Two fish died during the observation period — one lost equilibrium after KMnO₄ treatment and never recovered (died May 20); a second, weakened fish died May 26 just prior to transport.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range/habitat:** [[Bonytail Chub]] is native to large, warm, turbid rivers of the [[Colorado River Basin]]; the source population for this report was collected from [[Lake Mohave]] (a Colorado River reservoir), specifically from a spawning aggregation in "Carp Cove."
- **Conservation status:** Explicitly treated as an endangered species throughout the report; transport to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] was part of a captive propagation/conservation effort.
- **Capture context:** Fish were caught using trammel nets during active spawning behavior, a life-history stage associated with aggregation in shallow cove habitat — a factor management explicitly identifies as increasing vulnerability to capture stress and physical injury.
- **Interaction with captive/hatchery environment:** Held in a covered 24' x 1.5' x 2.5' hatchery tank; response to therapeutic water chemistry manipulation (salinity, magnesium sulfate, oxygenation) reflects physiological stress tolerance thresholds relevant to the species' resilience in altered/managed water conditions.

## Key Findings
- **Capture method was the primary driver of morbidity/mortality.** All 14 fish arrived with substantial physical trauma (net-line lacerations, abraded skin, damaged fins) attributed directly to trammel-net capture during spawning; the report's conclusion explicitly recommends alternative low-injury capture methods (electroshocking, hoop nets, fyke nets) for future collections.
- **Temperature tempering protocol:** Fish were gradually acclimated from field water temperatures (76–79°F) down to hatchery tank temperatures (57–58°F) over roughly 40–50 minutes per delivery batch.
- **Formalin flow-through treatment** (1/6,000 dilution, ~20 GPM, 1-hour duration) was used repeatedly (May 11–21) as a prophylactic/therapeutic fungicide against *Saprolegnia*, per guidance from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] (Roger Hamman).
- **Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄)** topical application (2 g/liter, painted on wounds) triggered an acute adverse reaction in the most severely infected fish (loss of equilibrium), leading to discontinuation of topical KMnO₄ and eventual fatality in that individual; a milder flow-through KMnO₄ protocol (4 mg/L) was later used per Pinetop Fish Health Center guidance.
- **Standing salt bath therapy** (NaCl 5.9 g/L + MgSO₄ 118 mg/L), based on protocols from Gary Carmichael, became the primary supportive treatment from May 18 onward, administered in a stagnant bath with supplemental industrial oxygen, and flushed/renewed every 8–12 hours to clear metabolic toxins. A 1% saline dip (30 seconds) was also used as a direct antiparasitic/antifungal treatment.
- **Multi-agency crisis response:** Treatment protocols were coordinated in real time among Willow Beach NFH, [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]], and the [[Pinetop Fish Health Center]], reflecting the intensive, improvisational nature of emergency care for a small endangered-species cohort.
- **Outcome:** 12 of 14 bonytail chubs survived to transport; 2 died during the 15-day holding period. Survivors were shipped to Dexter NFH&TC on May 26, 1993, with staff recommending a "couple weeks of R&R" prior to shipment (though final transport occurred sooner due to logistical scheduling).
- **Management critique:** The hatchery manager's closing statement raises concerns about (1) use of non-INAD-approved chemicals in treating an endangered species, (2) broader agency directives to reduce chemical use in fish culture due to environmental release concerns, and (3) a fundamental ethical question about whether capturing spawning [[Bonytail Chub]] from a stable wild population (Lake Mohave) for hatchery intervention was justified at all.
