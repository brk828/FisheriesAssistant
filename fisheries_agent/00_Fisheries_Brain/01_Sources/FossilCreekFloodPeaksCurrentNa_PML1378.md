# Fossil Creek Watershed Conditions and Flood Hydrology Analysis

#aquatic-ecology #watershed-hydrology #fluvial-geomorphology

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared for the [[Arizona Public Service Company]] and the [[Coconino National Forest]], evaluates how livestock grazing and related vegetation changes in the [[Fossil Creek]] watershed in [[Arizona]] could affect peak flood flows, erosion potential, and sediment transport. The analysis compares three [[Watershed Condition]] scenarios (Natural, Current, and Degraded) across several flood recurrence intervals. The scanned text is heavily degraded, so many numeric values below are approximate and are flagged as such. The report concludes that changes in watershed condition produced only small changes in peak flows and erosion potential.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Fossil Creek watershed, which lies partly within the Coconino and [[Tonto National Forest]] areas, with vegetation types including [[Ponderosa Pine]], pinyon-juniper, grasslands, chaparral, and [[Riparian Zone]] vegetation.
- **Management question:** Whether livestock grazing, which reduces ground cover, altered flood response enough to matter for downstream erosion.
- **Watershed condition scenarios:**
  - *Natural:* reference conditions with no grazing effects.
  - *Current:* present-day conditions.
  - *Degraded:* a worse-case state with reduced ground cover.
- **Scenario-based ground cover changes (partly legible):** Ponderosa pine ground cover was expected to show no change under the Current scenario and to decline under the Degraded scenario. Other cover types were projected to decline, with grassland cover falling to about 5 percent, pinyon-juniper/chaparral to about 20 percent, and riparian cover to about 25 percent.
- **Data sources:** Vegetation and soil parameters came from the Coconino National Forest Terrestrial Ecosystem Survey (TES) and Tonto National Forest data. Rainfall depths came from [[NOAA Atlas 2]] (Miller et al., 1973).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrologic method.** Peak flows were estimated with a rainfall-runoff model, the [[HEC-1 Model]] (U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, 1990), using a time-area relationship for each subbasin. Model inputs included initial and constant loss rates, surface retention, and soil parameters. Results were compared with peak flow estimates from [[USGS Regional Regression]] equations (Thomas et al., 1993, and Blakemore et al., 1994) and checked independently. The 2 to 100 year recurrence intervals were analyzed.

**Erosion potential.** Erosion was assessed with [[Tractive Force]] analysis, which estimates the shear stress that flood flows exert on the channel bed at surveyed cross sections. The analysis also estimated the largest [[Sediment Transport]] particle diameter that flows could move at each cross section. These calculations allowed the team to relate changes in peak flow to changes in the potential for channel erosion.

## Key Findings

1. **Flood flow comparison.** Model peak flows were compared against USGS-based estimates across the 2-, 5-, 10-, 25-, 50-, and 100-year events. Percent differences were reported for each event, with average standard errors reported in percent. Many values are illegible in the scan; the legible values suggest close agreement for the more frequent events and larger divergence for the 2-year event.

2. **Peak flows by watershed condition.** Fossil Creek peak flows were modeled for the Natural, Current, and Degraded conditions. The largest reported difference between scenarios is roughly 1,353 cfs (OCR uncertain). Differences were described as greatest for the higher-frequency events and were not large relative to total flow.

The table indicates little change in tractive force among scenarios. The 100-year event produced the highest force values.

4. **Sediment size mobilized.** The average diameter of sediment particles set in motion showed a similar pattern. Differences among scenarios were small, with the largest particles mobilized during the 100-year flood.

5. **Conclusion on erosion.** The report concludes that changes in watershed condition, driven by livestock grazing and vegetation loss, would produce little change in peak flows and little change in erosion potential. Any effect was described as minor relative to the natural variability of flood flows.
