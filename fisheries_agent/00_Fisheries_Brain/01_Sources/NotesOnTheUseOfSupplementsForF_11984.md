# Supplementing Fresh Meat in Trout Diets: Cost, Growth and Mortality in Michigan Hatcheries (1937)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1938 study by James T. Wilkinson, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, tested whether cheaper dry animal and plant meals could partly replace costly fresh liver in hatchery diets for [[Brook Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]. Ten diets were fed in semi-production troughs at the [[Wolf Lake State Fish Hatchery]] from April 13 to September 21, 1937. Fresh [[Sheep Liver]] served as the control. The main conclusions were that several meal-supplemented diets reared trout more cheaply, with mortality almost as low as the control, while diets containing [[Pork Melts]] were the least desirable because of heavy losses to ulcer disease. Pure liver gave the greatest growth in brook and rainbow trout, but some dry-meal diets grew brown trout better than liver.

## Background & Description

Michigan's trout propagation program had grown large, and fresh liver, the almost exclusive feed for young trout in state hatcheries, had risen in price as a meat-packing by-product. The study aimed to cut rearing costs by partly replacing fresh liver with less expensive supplements. The author noted that rearing stations differ, so results apply mainly to local conditions. Earlier work by Titcomb, Cobb, Crowell and McCay (1928), McCay (1933) and McCay and Tunison (1934, 1935) had already shown that dry meals could support good growth. Reviews by [[Mccay]] (1937a, 1937b) were cited as the main background on trout nutrition.

The experiment was designed to mirror hatchery practice, using larger fish numbers than typical laboratory nutrition trials, so it operated on a semi-production scale. Feeding used "as purchased" weights rather than dry weights, so results reflect practical costs.

Starting stock was 96,000 brook trout, 31,528 rainbow trout and 50,490 brown trout fingerlings. Average weights per thousand fish at the start were 377.5 g (brook), 360.4 g (rainbow) and 225.4 g (brown).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The hatchery drew water from an underground spring with a flow of approximately 1,200 gallons per minute into an open pond about 100 yards long, 30 yards wide and 18 feet deep. Brook trout already in this pond showed no external parasites or disease during summer 1937 checks. Few invertebrates or insects entered the troughs, and silt from heavy rain cleared quickly.

Water conditions recorded in summer 1937:

- Temperature: about 40–50 °F in colder months and 50–60 °F in warmer months.
- Dissolved oxygen: 6.7 to 9.9 ppm; about 1 ppm lower in rainy weather.
- Free carbon dioxide: 1.8 to 3.24 ppm, usually low when dissolved oxygen was high.
- pH: usually about 7.7 (range 7.5 to 8.1, excluding rainy weather).

Indoor troughs held about 87 gallons with roughly 5.8 gallons per minute of flow. Each trough in the lower row was fed by the outflow of the trough above, and food carry-over into lower troughs was judged insignificant.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design and methods**

- Ten diets were tested (Diet 2 was the sheep liver control). Brook trout were held in 40 troughs (about 4,805 fish per trough), rainbow in 20 and brown in 20 after the brook trout trial ended.
- Feeding followed hatchery routine: fish were fed once or twice daily, and given as much as they would eat, with an allowance for slight excess. Diet mixtures were laid out in chunks and stored for 12 hours or more so meat juices could be absorbed by the dry meal.
- Water added to diets (for example, 33.3 lb per 100 lb of meat-meal mix in Diet 4) was corrected out when calculating food eaten.
- Fish were weighed every two weeks in wet weight, which includes about 5.99 per cent water (after Embody, 1937). Food conversion factor (FCF) was the grams of food per thousand fish divided by the weight gain per thousand fish.
- Thinning was done at random by weight at the ends of Periods 3 and 6.

**Brook trout**

- Brook trout were removed from the trial after Period 6 because of an epidemic of ulcer disease. The Diet 18 group had no ulcer disease at any time.
- Mean brook trout mortality across periods was 7.50 per cent. Mean cost to rear one pound was $0.1850, compared with $0.2723 for the control.
- Diet 18 cost $0.1465 per pound (54 per cent of control) with 3.52 per cent mortality. Diet 6 cost $0.1396 per pound but had 13.72 per cent mortality.

**Rainbow trout**

- Mean mortality was 1.32 per cent and mean cost to rear one pound was $0.2574 (23-week period).
- Diets 4 and 17 gave about 20 per cent better growth than Diet 18. Diet 17 had the lowest mortality among rainbow trout.
- Diet 18 reared fish about 15 per cent more cheaply than Diets 4 and 17.

**Brown trout**

- Mean mortality was 5.07 per cent and mean cost to rear one pound was $0.2568.
- Diet 6 gave the best growth (2,452 per cent weight increase against 2,348 per cent for the control) and was second-lowest in cost, but had 6.15 per cent mortality.
- Diet 18 had the lowest mortality (3.43 per cent) with low cost. Diet 20 ran close behind.
- Diets 4 and 17 combined low mortality with good growth.

**Growth, conversion and cost**

- Pure sheep liver gave the greatest weight increase among brook and rainbow trout. Two dry-meal diets produced better growth than liver in brown trout.
- The best food conversion came from some dry-meal diets, though conversion differed by species and period.
- Sheep liver diets cost about twice as much per pound of trout as the other satisfactory diets.
- Pork melts raised costs: their price rose from $0.035 to $0.055 per pound after June 1, and the author found they could not compete with sheep liver unless their price stayed low.

**Mortality and disease**

- Disease caused most losses. Ulcer disease was most severe among fish fed pork melts (Diets 6, 21, 22 and 23). Potassium permanganate treatments (1:75,000, 30 minutes every 48 hours) failed to control it.
- The author cautioned that blaming pork melts was not conclusive, since melts had been used successfully elsewhere and fish on Diet 19 were also affected.
- [[Gyrodactylus]] sp. appeared occasionally but was controlled with a 20-minute permanganate bath.

**Recommendations and conclusions**

- Diets 4, 6, 17 and 18 could be reared more cheaply than the control, with mortality almost as low.
- Diet 18 was outstanding for low cost. Diets 4 and 17 were best where low mortality and good growth at moderate cost were wanted. Diet 6 gave good growth at low cost but high mortality.
- Diets containing pork melts were judged least desirable overall.
- An earlier correspondence (1910) reported that [[Grasshoppers]] above about 33 per cent of a beef-liver diet raised mortality and reduced growth. The author noted that grasshoppers could be cheaper if collected locally.

**Discussion highlights**

- Dr. A. S. Hazzard summarised the trade-off: straight meat for the fastest growth to large size, supplements for cheaper fish at moderate size. Wilkinson agreed.
- Acting Chairman Foster reported that dry foods fed at high percentages or for long periods could harm fish. He advised hatcheries to test dry-food use at their own stations first.
- Foster's work with [[Salmon Viscera]] and beef liver mixed 50:50 in a blower (3,500 rpm) produced a sawdust-like feed that scattered well. Chinook salmon losses were about 4 to 5 per cent over five months.
- Dr. H. S. Davis reported that unfrozen, good-condition fish produced fewer problems than frozen fish held in cold storage. He also found that straight meat diets gave mediocre growth compared with dry-product diets.
