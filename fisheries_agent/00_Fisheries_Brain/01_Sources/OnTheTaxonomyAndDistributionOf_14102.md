# Taxonomy and Distribution of Freshwater Ectoprocta in Michigan, Part I

#ectoprocta #freshwater-biology #gymnolaemata #phylactolaemata

## Executive Summary

This paper reports the taxonomy and distribution of freshwater Ectoprocta (bryozoans) collected across [[Michigan]] during a four-year study (summer 1957 to summer 1960). Collections came from 122 lakes, ponds, rivers, swamps, and ditches in 48 counties. The paper covers all families except [[Plumatellidae]], which the author reserved for two follow-up papers. Three species were new to the state, and the author reassessed several morphological characters long used to separate species and varieties. Much of the variation he documents is environmental or intraspecific, which weakens reliance on those characters.

## Background & Description

**Classification history.** The author reviews the main classification systems: Allman (1856), Jullien (1885), Kraepelin (1887), Vangel (1894), Rogick (1935), and Toriumi (1956a). Allman's and Kraepelin's schemes were the most widely used. Toriumi's studies were described as the most definitive of the twentieth century. They showed that features such as ectocyst incrustation, colony growth form, and lamellation of the zooecia vary strongly within species. The paper also notes the shifting use of varietal and subspecies designations, and suggests that the family Plumatellidae could profitably be examined from the standpoint of polytypic speciation.

**Collecting and preservation.** Colonies were collected by boat, wading, diving, and plant hook, from organic, marly, and sandy bottoms, lotic and lentic waters, and wave-washed shores. Living colonies were narcotized before fixation. The [[Narcotization]] method combined chloral hydrate with saturated menthol. [[Hyalinella Orbisperma]] responded best to chloretone. Colonies were preserved in alcohol or formalin, and later in a [[Sugar-Formalin Preservation]] reagent, which is recommended for gelatinous colonies.

**Taxa treated in this paper.** Five species are described in detail:
- [[Paludicella Articulata]] (Gymnolaemata; Paludicellidae)
- [[Pectinatella Magnifica]] (Phylactolaemata; Lophopodidae)
- [[Lophopodella Carteri]] (Phylactolaemata; Lophopodidae)
- [[Cristatella Mucedo]] (Phylactolaemata; Cristatellidae)
- [[Fredericella Sultana]] (Phylactolaemata; Fredericellidae)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Most species occurred throughout the state with no clear geographic pattern. Exceptions include:
- [[Pectinatella Magnifica]], which occurred more frequently in the southwestern portion of Michigan. The single Mackinac County record is tentative because only statoblasts were found, possibly carried by wind or current from the south.
- [[Plumatella Fruticosa]], with eight of ten collections from the northern half of the state, three of them in the Upper Peninsula. The author notes that this pattern may be even stronger because more collecting was done in the south.

**Kalamazoo County and Wintergreen Lake.** Eleven ectoproct species were recorded from Kalamazoo County, the most recorded for any county in the United States. All of the rare species found there, including [[Stolella Indica]], [[Stolella Evelinae]], and [[Lophopodella Carteri]], occurred in [[Wintergreen Lake]], a waterfowl sanctuary. The author proposes that waterbirds disperse [[Statoblast]]s. Supporting evidence includes de Guerne's observation of a statoblast in mud on a duck's foot, Harmer's report of one attached to a heron's beak, and Brown's finding that some statoblasts stayed viable after passing through a mallard's digestive tract. The author also reports that statoblasts of [[Plumatella Repens]] were retained for up to seven hours in the fur of a Microtus pennsylvanicus that had walked in water.

**Habitat notes.** [[Fredericella Sultana]] formed dense tufts on logs, rocks, rootlets, and sand. Notable colonies occurred in the outlet of [[Gull Lake]] and in Margreth Lake. [[Cristatella Mucedo]] colonies up to 35 cm and 50 cm long were found on stems of Nuphar advena in Wintergreen Lake.

## Key Findings

- **Study scope:** 122 habitats in 48 Michigan counties, sampled over four years. Figure 1 maps the distribution, and Table I lists collection sites and species.
- **New state records:** *Plumatella fruticosa*, *Stolella evelinae*, and *Lophopodella carteri* were reported for Michigan for the first time. *Stolella indica* had been reported earlier by Kenk (1949).
- **Taxonomic changes:** A species described by Kellicott (1882) as *Plumatella orbisperma* was transferred to the genus *Hyalinella*. The flabellum form of *P. emarginata* and the fungosa form of *P. repens* were recorded for the first time in Michigan.
- **Most widespread taxa:** The species with the most occurrences had no discernible distribution pattern. *Plumatella repens* was the most abundant and frequent, followed by *Fredericella sultana*, then *Paludicella articulata* in third place.
- **Paludicella articulata:** Tentacles numbered 12 to 19 (mean 15.7, 164 counts). Michigan specimens all had square orifices. Hibernacula were variable, and indented irregular forms predominated.
- **Pectinatella magnifica:** Tentacles numbered 62 to 85 (147 counts). Statoblasts measured 0.84 to 1.1 mm, with 12 to 22 hooks (average 16, 48 measurements). Colonies of 4 to 14 inches were typical, and the largest were found in the St. Joseph River and Hall Lake. The author considers the rose-red margin of the inner tentacles too variable for species discrimination.
- **Lophopodella carteri:** Tentacles numbered 67 to 89 (average 79, 145 counts). Michigan colonies typically had 35 to 55 polypides, and the largest had 70. Michigan tentacle counts were higher than those from Lake Erie (range 52 to 82, mean 64), and the author attributes this partly to environmental variation. Statoblasts were saddle-shaped, with 7 to 18 spines per end (average 10.5). Colonies moved up to 10 to 12 cm per day, though most observed colonies moved about half that distance.
- **Cristatella mucedo:** Tentacles numbered 78 to 96 (average 85, 104 counts). Statoblast size and spine counts overlapped both varieties (*idae* and *genuina*) that Kraepelin had distinguished, which undermines the criteria for those varieties.
- **Fredericella sultana:** Tentacles numbered 19 to 25 (average 22.2, 283 polypides). Sessoblasts were 0.31 to 0.52 mm long (141 measurements), with one to three per zooecium. Hyaline, unincrusted field specimens were reported for the first time, from several lakes in Mecosta, Chippewa, Grand Traverse, Oakland, and Schoolcraft counties. Together with Toriumi's rearing experiments, these suggest that incrustation is environmentally related and should be used cautiously in systematics. Septation ranged continuously from infrequent to nearly every zooecium, and was more common in older or more luxuriant colonies.
- **Reproduction:** A ciliated larva was observed in early September in a Mackinac County specimen. Other investigators usually found larvae in spring and early summer.
- **Conclusion:** Many morphological characters used to separate freshwater bryozoan species, including incrustation, septation, statoblast spines, and tentacle number, vary with age, environment, and season. The author therefore advises caution in using them to define species.
