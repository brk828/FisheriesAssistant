# Effects of Copepod Grazing on Two Natural Phytoplankton Populations

#marine-biology #copepoda #zooplankton

## Executive Summary

This laboratory and field study measured how four copepod species feed on natural phytoplankton from [[Bras D'or Lake]] and [[Morrison's Pond]], Cape Breton Island, Nova Scotia. Feeding and filtration rates were corrected for animal density and experiment duration and then extrapolated to field conditions. Comparing estimated zooplankton ingestion with [[Primary Production]] suggested that copepods consumed all average daily production in Bras d'Or Lake and about two-thirds in Morrison's Pond. The authors conclude that [[Copepod Grazing]] is an important factor limiting aquatic primary production, although the estimates are acknowledged to be approximate.

## Background & Description

Herbivorous zooplankton link primary producers to higher trophic levels, so their feeding is central to [[Energy Flow In Aquatic Ecosystems]]. Most earlier feeding measurements used pure or mixed algal cultures, which may not reflect the heterogeneous particle sizes and cell types that zooplankton encounter in nature (see [[Burns and Rigler]], 1967). This study instead used natural phytoplankton assemblages.

**Study sites**
- **Bras d'Or Lake:** an enclosed arm of the Atlantic Ocean with surface salinities of 20–25‰ and summer surface temperatures of 15–20 °C. Rotifers and ciliates were rare here.
- **Morrison's Pond:** a small, shallow water body with salinities similar to the lake except after surface runoff. Rotifers and ciliates were common here.

**Organisms studied**
- **Copepods:** [[Pseudocalanus Minutus]], [[Temora Longicornis]] and [[Oithona Similis]] from Bras d'Or Lake; [[Acartia Tonsa]] from Morrison's Pond. Copepodites (CIV–CV), adults and nauplii were used.
- **Phytoplankton:** almost entirely [[Flagellates]] under 15 µm in maximum dimension. Common forms included cryptomonads (*Cryptomonas* sp.) and chrysomonads (*Ochromonas* sp.).

**Methods (summary)**
- Copepods were collected with nylon nets (mesh 336, 239 or 158 µm) from short horizontal tows, then held at in situ temperature.
- Phytoplankton were strained through 35 µm netting and used at natural densities.
- Grazing was measured in 6–8 hr bottle experiments (275 ml bottles, dark, on a rotating disc at 0.25 rpm). Animals were added at about twice natural density (20–60 per bottle).
- Cell counts used an inverted microscope after Lugol's preservation.
- Filtration rate followed [[Fuller and Clarke]] (1936). Feeding rate was expressed as µg of food ingested per copepod per unit time, with biomass estimated from cell dimensions and a specific gravity of 1.0.
- Oxygen uptake was measured with Schölander microvolumetric respirometers in 1 hr experiments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food selectivity.** Adult copepods and late copepodites ingested all flagellate sizes, but removal was most efficient for 5–15 µm cells. Nauplii only ingested cells under 5 µm in diameter. Adult *P. minutus* and *T. longicornis* did not eat the chain-forming diatom *Chaetoceros* sp., a dominant spring form in Bras d'Or Lake. Of a mixed natural assemblage, only flagellates were eaten and *Ceratium* sp. was not. Filamentous green algae and other chain-forming diatoms were also not eaten. The authors attribute this selectivity partly to particle size and shape and to the spacing of bristles on the second maxillae of *P. minutus* copepodites (average distance 3–5 µm at the distal end), through which many small flagellates could pass.

**Seasonality.** *Ceratium* and *Chaetoceros* were abundant in winter and spring but uncommon in summer. The authors therefore assumed they contributed little to the summer primary production used in the comparison.

**Density and duration effects.** Feeding rates fell as copepod density and experiment duration increased. Laboratory densities (125–175 per liter) exceeded field densities (65–100 per liter in both water bodies), so corrections were needed before extrapolating to nature.

**Pond versus lake.** The authors suggest that the residual production in Morrison's Pond supported the large rotifer and ciliate populations found there but not in Bras d'Or Lake.

## Key Findings

**1. Selective feeding by cell size.** Flagellates of all sizes from 1 to 15 µm were ingested, with 5–15 µm cells removed most efficiently. Filtration and feeding rates were highest on larger cells, even though small cells (1–2 µm) were far more numerous. The authors state that filtration rates from a single cell size are unlikely to give a meaningful estimate of food removal from a mixed population, so all sizes, concentrations and removal rates should be considered together.

**2. Density-dependent feeding.** In *Acartia tonsa* the relation between feeding rate and copepod density was log y = 3.38 − 1.17 log x, where x is copepods per liter (Fig. 2). Feeding fell as density rose, possibly through refiltration of the same water or accumulation of metabolites such as ammonia. Laboratory rates were corrected upward by two-thirds to field densities.

**3. Duration-dependent feeding.** Feeding rates declined with experiment length (Table 1). For *A. tonsa* copepodites (CIV–CV), mean rates were 13.2 µg animal⁻¹ day⁻¹ at 4 hr, 11.3 at 6 hr, 6.5 at 8 hr, 4.5 at 10 hr, 3.0 at 12 hr, 1.5 at 15 hr and 1.0 at 24 hr. The decline may reach 90% over 24 hr. Filtration rate stayed roughly constant at 7.0 ± 1 ml copepod⁻¹ day⁻¹ up to 24 hr, so the authors attributed the decline to falling cell concentrations. Using [[Richman]]'s (1966) data (feeding fell about 35% over the 6–8 hr cell-concentration change), rates were corrected upward by one-third. The authors call this procedure "somewhat arbitrary". No consistent diel rhythm was detected in four consecutive 6 hr experiments performed on six occasions.

**4. Respiratory energy balance.** Using a mean calorific value of 5269 cal/g dry weight for phytoplankton and an oxycalorific coefficient of 3.38 cal/mg O₂, the copepods respired 25–41% of ingested energy (Table 2 values range from 26.0% to 41.2%). The authors conclude that natural flagellate concentrations were more than sufficient for maintenance. They note that comparisons with [[Richman]] (1964; 77% respired in *Diaptomus oregonensis*) and [[Comita]] (1964; 53%) are difficult because of differences in temperature and food.

**5. Grazing versus primary production (before correction).**
- Average summer primary production at the depth of maximum photosynthesis (1962–1964, ¹⁴C technique, from [[Geen and Hargrave]] 1966) was about 50 mg C m⁻² day⁻¹ at 5 m in Bras d'Or Lake and about 200 mg C m⁻² day⁻¹ at 1 m in Morrison's Pond.
- Total phytoplankton carbon ingested daily (Table 3, summer 1964) was 79.0 mg C m⁻² day⁻¹ in Bras d'Or Lake and 200.7 mg C m⁻² day⁻¹ in Morrison's Pond. The lake value exceeded production by 58%, and *Acartia* in the pond consumed 100% of the average midsummer production.
- The authors consider these figures to lack long-term validity. Ingestion by rotifers, ciliates and veliger larvae was not included, which would make the discrepancy larger.

**6. Correction for underestimated production.** Known sources of underestimation were considered: bottle incubation for 10–20 hr (McAllister, 1969, 20–30%), excretion of labelled compounds, and loss of label during membrane-filter storage (Wallen and Geen, 1968). The authors suggest a composite correction of 30–50%. With a 50% correction, copepods removed an average of 100% of primary production in Bras d'Or Lake and 66% in Morrison's Pond at the depth of maximum photosynthesis. The discrepancy in the lake may also reflect errors in zooplankton abundance estimates, although these agreed with other years' data (Hargrave and Geen, 1968).

**7. Comparison with other systems.** Other studies reported zooplankton consumption of 26% of primary production in Long Island Sound (Riley, 1956), 85% of daily net primary production in Canyon Ferry Reservoir (Wright, 1958), and nearly all primary production in the Sargasso Sea (Menzel and Ryther, 1961). The authors conclude, in agreement with [[Cushing]] (1958), that zooplankton grazing is an important factor limiting aquatic primary production.

**8. Overall conclusion.** Zooplankton in the two study waters consumed a large share of primary production, and the results support the view that grazing limits phytoplankton standing crops. The authors emphasise that these estimates are approximate and depend on several correction factors.
