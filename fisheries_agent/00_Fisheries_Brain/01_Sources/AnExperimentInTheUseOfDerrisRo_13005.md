# Derris Root (Rotenone) Piscicide Treatment of Third Sister Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1941–1942 study, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, tested powdered derris root (5% [[Rotenone]]) as a piscicide in Third Sister Lake, Michigan. The aim was complete fish removal so that the [[Fish-Food Organisms]] could be studied without predation. A first application on May 6, 1941 was made in cold water and failed to remove all fish. A second, stronger application on August 18–19, 1941 eradicated the remaining fish. The authors also measured how long the toxin persisted at depth, how toxicity depended on temperature, and how zooplankton and invertebrates responded.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** [[Third Sister Lake]] is roughly oval, about 10 acres in area, with a maximum depth of 55 feet. It has no permanent inlets or outlets and lies in the [[Huron River Drainage]], near the center of Washtenaw County.
- **Physical setting:** Roughly one-quarter of the shoreline is sand and gravel; the rest is soft and peaty. Emergent and submerged vegetation covers about 35% of the lake area, extending to the 10-foot contour. The basin has steep sides with a sharp drop-off.
- **Bottom and water quality:** Pulpy peat lies between 2 and 25 feet; clay with a thin muck layer lies deeper. The lake stratifies thermally and chemically in summer, with marked oxygen depletion in the [[Hypolimnion]]. Mean alkalinity is about 90 p.p.m. CaCO₃, with pH 6.8–8.4.
- **Fish community before treatment:** Fourteen species were listed, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]], green sunfish, mud pickerel, bullheads, several shiners, suckers and darters.
- **Rationale for method:** The authors judged rotenone the most practical tool for complete fish removal. Fish removal was also meant to be temporary, with the population to be restored after the study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Treatment methods.** The first application used 312 lb of derris (about 1 part powder to 2 million parts water by weight). The second used 400 lb, and the concentration was somewhat higher. Powder was slurried in a 50-gallon drum, poured from a boat with an outboard motor, and dispersed in overlapping passes over the lake, with shallow edges treated by fire pump or dipper. In the second treatment, 85 lb were mixed into a batter, placed in weighted burlap bags, and dragged through the 15–25 foot zone.

**Temperature constraints.** The first treatment coincided with a cold, rainy period. Surface temperature fell about 20°F, reaching a maximum of only 48°F in the early morning of poisoning. The authors concluded that rotenone is much less effective in cold water. The second treatment occurred near the summer maximum, which favored toxicity, though a cooler zone at 18–25 feet remained.

**Vertical and horizontal persistence.** Caged fish placed at several depths and sites showed that:
- After the first treatment, fish above about 10 feet died within 12–30 hours. Fish below 10 feet survived until the experiment ended after 5–13 days.
- Surface water remained toxic for roughly 7 days. Deep water was never toxic.
- After the second treatment, cages at and above 15 feet were all dead within 24 hours, while those below 15 feet survived. Water at 25 and 30 feet was non-toxic even at high temperature.

**Laboratory toxicity.** Water samples were heated to test toxicity. Toxicity increased with temperature. None of the samples was toxic below about 57°F. Aeration and attempts to revive fish in clean water were not successful. The authors cited other studies reporting persistence of 1–30 days, depending on lake and concentration.

**Non-target effects.** Results varied by group:
- **Zooplankton:** [[Daphnia]] disappeared within 2 days of the first treatment and did not return until 5 weeks after the second. [[Diaptomus]] also vanished and returned about a month after the second treatment. Cyclops declined sharply after each treatment and partly recovered. Rotifers were reduced.
- **Phytoplankton:** Little change overall. The dinoflagellate *Peridinium* disappeared for nearly a year.
- **Invertebrates:** [[Corethra]] larvae, leeches and aeschnine dragonflies were strongly reduced after the first treatment. In cages, these groups died within 12 hours. Most other invertebrates, including snails, amphipods and most midge and caddisfly larvae, were not seriously affected. Dragonflies were far less affected by the second treatment.
- **Tadpoles** (*Rana catesbeiana*) were greatly affected by the first treatment and much less so by the second. Tadpoles became extremely abundant afterward.

## Key Findings

- **Initial treatment failed to remove all fish.** The May 6, 1941 application in cold water left survivors. Gill nets caught 5 large bluegills and 1 pumpkinseed between May 14 and 17, and small centrarchids increased from mid-July.
- **Second treatment achieved eradication.** On August 18–19, 1941 the remaining fish were killed. The recovered adult fish included 2 largemouth bass, 12 large bluegills, 1 common shiner and 1 mudminnow. Many thousands of young bluegills and sunfish also died. No fish were detected through June 1942, when long-eared sunfish reappeared, probably via the outlet during high water.
- **Species tolerance was similar.** Little difference was seen among species. Minnows and young fish died in larger numbers early, probably because they inhabited shallow water. Within species, individuals varied considerably.
- **Death order was consistent.** In lab tests, largemouth bass died first, followed by bluegill, pumpkinseed, then blunt-nosed minnow.
- **Toxicity declined with depth and time.** Surface water was most toxic. Below about 20 feet (first treatment) or 25 feet (second), water was non-toxic.
- **Derris lost activity quickly.** Powder collected from the bottom mud the day after poisoning had no observable toxic qualities.
- **Fish showing distress could not be revived.** Transfer to clean, aerated water did not save them.
- **Non-target impacts were uneven.** Zooplankton (Daphnia, Diaptomus), leeches, Corethra and aeschnine dragonflies were most affected, while most other invertebrates were largely spared.

Note: The article's stated plan was to restore the fish population after the study, though the report itself does not describe that restocking.
