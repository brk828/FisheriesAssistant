# Recovery of Grass Carp, Channel Catfish, and Centrarchids in Artificial Central Arizona Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Paul C. Marsh and W. L. Minckley, published in the *Journal of the Arizona-Nevada Academy of Science* (vol. 18, 1983), documents the fish community of a set of small artificial ponds at [[Sun Lakes]], a retirement community in [[Maricopa County]], Arizona. The ponds were stocked with [[Grass Carp]] for vegetation control alongside [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] for sport fishing. In April 1978, personnel from [[Arizona State University]], working under the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], reclaimed the ponds with [[Rotenone]] and measured the standing crop that remained. Mean total fish biomass was 532 kg/ha, dominated by grass carp (67%). Standing crops of grass carp and largemouth bass were positively correlated. Pond area did not predict yield. Fishing pressure and water quality appear to have shaped differences among ponds.

## Background & Description

Grass carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) has been promoted as a biological control agent for nuisance aquatic plants. It is banned in Arizona because of unknown potential for environmental disturbance, yet it has still been imported and stocked or has escaped in several places. Channel catfish and largemouth bass are popular introduced sport fishes in the southwestern United States, and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] has stocked them widely in public waters. The U.S. Soil Conservation Service and private owners have also stocked them into private ponds.

The Sun Lakes ponds were a case where grass carp, channel catfish, and largemouth bass were introduced together to address both vegetation control and recreational fishing. The town of Sun Lakes lies about 32 km SSW of [[Phoenix Metropolitan Area|Phoenix]], in T2S, R5E. It has a population of about 6,000.

**Pond setting and construction**
- Eight artificial ponds sit within "green belt" and golf course areas. Seven were sampled; the eighth was originally fishless and used to hold fish for restocking.
- Ponds are generally kidney-shaped, with surface areas of 0.44 to 1.02 ha (for the sampled ponds) and low shoreline development.
- They are sealed with clay or plastic liners and overlain by silt-sand substrate. Gravel and larger particles are rare.
- Water comes from a well, reclaimed wastewater, and infrequent local runoff. Ponds are interconnected by underground pipes.

**Stocking history**
- In 1971–1972, unknown numbers of fingerling channel catfish (76–102 mm) and largemouth bass, and juvenile grass carp (152–203 mm), were stocked.
- At least two [[Silver Carp]] (*Hypophthalmichthys molitrix*) were probably introduced as contaminants with the grass carp.
- Green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*), fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*), common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), and goldfish (*Carassius auratus*) were also stocked, at unknown dates and sizes.

**Reclamation methods**
- Brood stock of all species except grass carp was netted first (trammel, hoop, and seine nets, over one to three weeks). Catch from ponds 1 and 5–6 was not recorded but was a minor fraction of the total.
- Ponds were treated with 7.5% active-ingredient rotenone to a concentration of 2 mg/l.
- Fish were collected for three consecutive days. Specimens from the day of poisoning were measured (total length, cm) and weighed.
- Total biomass was estimated from mean weights on day 1 multiplied by total numbers collected over the three-day period.
- Simple linear regressions (y = mx + b) tested relationships among species biomasses and between pond area and biomass.

Note: the same issue of the journal also contains a book review of *Tales from Tiburon* (a Seri Indian travel anthology). It is unrelated to the fish study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation and forage**
- Grass carp removed essentially all aquatic vegetation. Vegetation was virtually absent in the Sun Lakes ponds.
- Forage fishes were also absent. The authors suggest largemouth bass may have depleted their forage base. They also suggest that vegetation removal by grass carp enhanced prey vulnerability to largemouth bass.

**Angling pressure**
- Records of angler effort and harvest were not kept, but the ponds received at least moderate pressure from residents and guests.
- Ponds 1–4 were closed to daylight angling. Ponds 5–8 were open to fishing 24 hours a day.

**Water supply and nutrients**
- Pond 1 is at the highest elevation and is the only pond that receives well water. It had the highest biomass per unit area.
- Biomass declined downflow from pond 1 through ponds 5, 6, 7, and 8. Possible causes include deteriorating water quality, nutrient uptake in upper ponds, and other factors limiting fish production.
- Pond 4 received nutrient-enriched reclaimed wastewater that boosted algal and macrophyte production, which may explain why it did not show the expected decline.
- Other uncontrolled inputs included grass clippings fed by groundsmen and inorganic fertilizer carried in runoff from golf course greens.

**Recruitment**
- Grass carp did not recruit in any pond.
- Recruitment of channel catfish and centrarchids was minimal.
- The near-absence of small fish of any species indicated low recruitment for the population as a whole.

## Key Findings

**Biomass and composition**
- Mean total fish biomass across the seven ponds was **532 kg/ha**.
- Species composition by standing stock: grass carp **67%**, channel catfish **30%**, largemouth bass **3%**, green sunfish **<1%**.
- The greatest total biomass was **1,159 kg/ha** in a 0.44-ha pond. The authors note this is comparable to some commercial culture operations.
- Table 1 reports density (No./ha), standing stock (kg/ha), and percentage composition for each pond. Only the pond totals are summarized here.

**Relationships among species**
- Standing crops of grass carp and largemouth bass were positively correlated (r = 0.8724, P = 0.025, n = 7, per the regression table).
- Pond area and total fish yield were unrelated.
- Largemouth bass, channel catfish, and grass carp showed no significant relationship with pond area (all reported as NS).

**Effects of angling and closure**
- Closed ponds (1–4) averaged **745 kg/ha**, about twice the **373 kg/ha** in open ponds (5–8).
- Largemouth bass: 19.5 kg/ha (closed) vs. 11.5 kg/ha (open).
- Channel catfish: 223 kg/ha (closed) vs. 108 kg/ha (open).
- Grass carp, which anglers did not take, showed the same trend: 500 kg/ha (closed) vs. 252 kg/ha (open). This suggests the difference may not come entirely from angling pressure.

**Growth and conservation**
- Grass carp growth among ponds paralleled standing stock, probably driven by similar factors (Marsh and Dhaenens 1984).
- The authors could not evaluate interactive effects between grass carp and other species with their data.
- They could not determine whether grass carp affected the reproductive success of other fishes, either directly or indirectly.

**Management implications**
- Standing stocks were "certainly extraordinary," and grass carp effectively controlled aquatic vegetation.
- The combination of grass carp, channel catfish, and largemouth bass in small ponds produced very high fish biomass but almost no recruitment.
- Given the species' status in Arizona, the study supports caution about introducing grass carp, which is already banned in the state.
