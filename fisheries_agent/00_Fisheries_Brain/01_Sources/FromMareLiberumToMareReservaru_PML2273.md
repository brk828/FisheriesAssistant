# From Mare Liberum to Mare Reservarum

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This policy perspective argues that the [[Freedom Of The Seas]] principle, rooted in [[Mare Liberum]], still shapes how fisheries are governed. The authors contend that treating marine resources as an open commons leads inevitably to overexploitation, and that national fisheries management, subsidies and voluntary codes cannot reverse global decline. They propose a shift from "free and open" to "common heritage" and call for global [[Ocean Zoning]] with large, fully protected [[Marine Reserves]]. The paper ends by proposing a series of international conferences, modelled on [[UNCLOS]], to begin this process, using the 20by20 target as a foundation.

## Background & Description

The paper is a short historical and policy essay published in *Marine Policy* (2003) by Garry R. Russ and Dirk C. Zeller. It is a perspective piece rather than an empirical study, so it reports no new field data or sample sizes.

- **Origins of open access:** In 1608, [[Hugo Grotius]] published *Mare Liberum*, which was written mainly to justify Dutch trading in the Indian Ocean. Fish were treated as open-access or common-property resources for about 400 years. The authors cite the [[Tragedy Of The Commons]] as evidence that this approach inevitably leads to over-exploitation.
- **Territorial expansion:** The 1930 [[Hague Conference]] accepted national territorial-water claims. After World War II, UNCLOS conferences led many nations to declare 12 nm territorial seas. Chile and Peru claimed 200 nm jurisdictions in 1947, but most nations adopted 200 nm [[Exclusive Economic Zones]] only after the Icelandic [[Cod Wars]] in the 1970s. An estimated 90% of fisheries yield is taken within 200 nm of coastlines, so most fisheries fell under national jurisdiction.
- **Remaining open access:** Fisheries outside EEZs are still largely unregulated. The paper highlights the largely illegal and unregulated fishery for [[Patagonian Toothfish]], marketed as "Chilean Seabass".

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame the problem through several interrelated ecological and governance issues:

- **Declining landings:** Global fisheries landings have declined since the early 1990s.
- **Stock collapses:** Society has a poor record of keeping stocks at sustainable levels and avoiding collapse.
- **Shifting baseline syndrome:** Inaccurate perceptions of past ecosystem states lead to an underestimate of the damage already done.
- **Interconnected systems:** No single stock, metapopulation or ecosystem can be managed in isolation. Climate change and global overfishing operate at global scales.
- **Straddling and highly migratory stocks:** These require large-scale, internationally enforced protection.
- **Overcapacity and subsidies:** Fishing subsidies drive overcapacity, and conventional decommissioning schemes have failed to achieve long-term capacity reduction.

## Key Findings

**1. Current governance has failed to prevent overexploitation.**
- Open-access fisheries outside EEZs remain uncontrolled.
- Within EEZs, traditional management has failed to control effort, causing "massive overexploitation" of resources.
- [[Individual Transferable Quotas]] are slow and controversial. They can cause high-grading and discarding and concentrate quotas in a few enterprises. They also do not protect against the negative effects of subsidies.
- The [[FAO Code Of Conduct For Responsible Fisheries]] is voluntary and non-binding, and is hard to monitor and enforce globally.

**2. A mindset shift to "common heritage" is necessary but not sufficient.**
- The authors argue that the "heritage" concept, adopted at UNCLOS III, requires responsible management for all of humanity, including future generations.
- They conclude that a change of mindset alone is insufficient and call for action on a global scale.

**3. Proposed global ocean zoning.**
- Zoning should cover the entire ocean, not only land margins, with responsibility assigned to individual states, international groupings such as the [[Antarctic Treaty]], or global organisations.
- Zoning should combine limited fishing areas, global reductions in overcapacity, and fully protected "no-take" reserves of substantial size.
- No-take reserves are framed as a form of bet-hedging and a practical application of the [[Precautionary Principle]].
- The authors cite the [[Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority]] as a national example of multiple-use zoning.

**4. Current closure coverage is extremely small.**
- There are some 1,300 marine reserves globally.
- Only 0.01% of the world's ocean is effectively closed to fishing.
- Regional no-take coverage ranges from 0.14% in California to 20% of the continental shelf in Bermuda.
- The GBRMPA is considering raising its no-take share from 4.7% of total area to at least 20–25%.

**5. Target closure levels.**
- Many studies use 20% closures. Recent modelling suggests that 40–50% closures may give the greatest fisheries benefits.
- The **20by20 proposal** calls for 20% closure by 2020. It is supported by over 1,600 scientists.
- The authors note that enforcing 20by20 would represent a 2,000-fold increase over the existing 0.01% global coverage.
- Some fisheries scientists go further, proposing that the sea be treated as closed to fishing except for small exceptions. Others call for reversing the burden of proof, so that exploiters must show their actions cause no damage.

**6. Recommended action.**
- The authors propose a series of [[UNCLOS-Style Conferences]] to discuss steps toward global "Mare Reservarum", ideally based on the 20by20 scenario.
- They also note that opposition to global zoning is likely, but argue that similar objections were raised before 200 nm EEZs and that communication, monitoring and surveillance technologies, along with existing legal instruments, make the goal achievable.
