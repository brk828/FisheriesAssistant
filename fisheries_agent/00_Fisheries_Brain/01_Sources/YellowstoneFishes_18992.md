# Yellowstone Fishes: A 1939 Pamphlet on the Park's Fish Fauna

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1939 pamphlet by James R. Simon, published by the Yellowstone Library and Museum Association, is an angler's and naturalist's guide to the fishes of [[Yellowstone National Park]]. It was written to update [[Smith and Kendall 1921]], which predated taxonomic revisions and stocking changes, and to explain the park's fish fauna, fishing regulations, propagation policy and fish-predator relationships. It is the only group of vertebrates visitors may take and kill inside the park.

The park holds 13 native species, 4 exotic species (17 full species), plus two additional varieties and one hybrid, for 20 forms in total. The fauna is confined to cold-water and boreal forms, limited by high altitude, a short growing season, Pliocene lava flows and natural waterfall barriers.

## Background & Description

The pamphlet covers six families, each with at least one native representative. It presents species descriptions with diagnostic characters, distribution, size, feeding and angling notes, and etymologies. Keys separate the families, the genera and the species within the trout, sucker and sculpin groups.

- **Salmonidae (trouts, charrs and salmons):** The most important family for the park. Includes [[Loch Leven Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Blackspotted Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Hybrid Trout]], [[Mackinaw Trout]] and [[Eastern Brook Trout]]. Mackinaw and brook trout are charrs, not true trouts.
- **Coregonidae (whitefishes):** [[Rocky Mountain Whitefish]] and [[Yellowstone Whitefish]], two varieties of one species.
- **Thymallidae (graylings):** [[Montana Grayling]], the only native member, with a large, brilliantly colored dorsal fin.
- **Catostomidae (suckers):** Two genera, four species: Jordan's sucker, flatnose sucker, rosyside sucker and northern sucker.
- **Cyprinidae (minnows, chubs and dace):** Four genera, each with one species: Utah Lake chub, silverside minnow, longnose dace and dusky dace.
- **Cottidae (sculpins):** Two *Cottus* species, the sculpin (*C. punctulatus*) and the blob (*C. semiscaber*).

The author distinguishes game fishes (Salmonidae, Thymallidae, Coregonidae), which are prized by anglers, from forage fishes (Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Cottidae), which are valuable as food for game fishes. Coloration is an unreliable identifier except for spotting, and trout flesh color depends on diet.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and food.** Park waters are described as exceptionally productive. Forest protection, the absence of grazing by domestic stock, and natural cover (boulders, fallen trees, stumps, brush) support fish and their food. Aquatic insects (mayflies, caddisflies), snails, freshwater shrimp, terrestrial insects and forage fishes are abundant. Common submerged plants include stonewort, hornwort, pondweed and mare's-tail. Sedges, with a few cattails and reeds, are the common emergents.

**Barriers and distribution.** Waterfalls on the plateau block upstream migration and isolate fish communities. The main barriers listed are Yellowstone Falls, Gibbon Falls, Bechler Falls, Cave Falls, Osprey Falls and Lewis Falls. Only one game fish, the blackspotted trout, is native to the plateau. Forage fish range is also limited: the plateau holds only sculpins (Gibbon River) and northern suckers (Yellowstone Lake).

**Drainage history.** Blackspotted trout occur on both sides of the Continental Divide. The author attributes this to Yellowstone Lake formerly draining into the Snake River system before a stream capture redirected it. Yellowstone Lake now drains into the Yellowstone River, part of the Missouri drainage. The shared Snake River fauna (Rocky Mountain whitefish, flatnose sucker, rosyside sucker, Utah Lake chub, silverside minnow and dusky dace) is linked to the ancient [[Lake Bonneville]], which drained into the Snake River.

**Thermal influence.** Thermal outflows did not appear to harm trout growth or natural reproduction. The [[Madison River]], averaging 69°F in summer, supports abundant aquatic plants and a long growing season. Fish were found in water up to 83–88°F below Boiling River, and rosyside suckers in Witch Creek above 80°F.

**Predators.** Ospreys, herons, mergansers, kingfishers, gulls, terns, cormorants and pelicans rely on fish, as do mink, otter and fisher. The white pelican breeds on Molly Island in Yellowstone Lake in one of the largest U.S. colonies. Forage fish serve as "buffers" for trout, sustaining predators that would otherwise prey mainly on trout.

**Parasites.** The author argues that parasites pose no danger to human health and that the public prejudice against them is aesthetic. Parasites recorded include *Diphyllobothrium* sp. (larval form in trout; adults in fish-eating birds and bears), *Bulbodacnitis scotti* (a round worm), *Ligula intestinalis* (a tapeworm in suckers at Heart Lake and Witch Creek) and *Crepidostomum transmarinum* (a fluke in trout intestines). The broad fish tapeworm of humans, *Diphyllobothrium latum*, is absent from the park.

**Conservation and human impacts.** The author notes the absence of silting from deforestation, sewage, irrigation withdrawals and shoal filling, which are common outside the park.

## Key Findings

**Fishing regulations**
- No license is required. The season runs from sunrise May 30 to sunset October 15, with exceptions in special areas.
- Minnows and other bait fish, fish eggs, and digging worms are prohibited. Canning or curing fish is prohibited.
- Fish under six inches must be handled with moist hands, unhooked and returned. Injured undersized fish retained count toward the daily limit.
- Recommended flies: Nos. 10–14 for grayling and whitefish, Nos. 9–12 for trout, and size 12 for all game fish. Colorado spinner No. 3 is useful in Yellowstone Lake.
- July is the best month to fish, after high water recedes.

**Propagation and stocking**
- Roughly 150,000 fish are taken by anglers each year.
- Natural survival from egg to a one-inch fingerling is about five percent; hatchery survival is as high as 95 percent.
- Blackspotted trout, Montana grayling and rainbow trout are propagated. The blackspotted trout is propagated at Lake Station, the Montana grayling at Grebe Lake, and the rainbow at Trout Lake.
- More than 25,000,000 pure blackspotted eggs are taken from waters tributary to Yellowstone Lake each year. The author states these are more clean blackspotted eggs than all other U.S. waters combined.
- Stocking follows drainages. Four drainages are stocked only with blackspotted trout. Rainbow and blackspotted trout are segregated where possible, and mixing is considered undesirable.
- Exotic species are no longer introduced; introductions of added exotics are prohibited.

**Yellowstone Lake**
- Covers about 90,000 acres at 7,731 feet, described as the largest lake at such altitude or the highest lake of its size in North America.
- Its only game fish is the blackspotted trout, which the author describes as largely free of hybridization.

**Species records**
- Jordan's sucker was first recorded in the park in 1937, with fourteen adults taken from the Yellowstone River above the Gardiner confluence.
- Flatnose sucker was first taken in Yellowstone in 1938 and is much less common than Jordan's sucker.
- Northern suckers trapped and destroyed by the Bureau of Fisheries in Yellowstone Lake tributaries numbered 382 in 1938, up from earlier years. The author attributes their introduction to anglers using them as bait.
- The landlocked salmon planted in Yellowstone and Duck Lakes in 1908 has not been seen since.
- Yellow perch survived as a stunted race in Goose Lake until 1938, when they were removed by poison.
- Two attempts to establish black bass are recorded.

**Management philosophy**
- Native species are encouraged across the widest possible area. Exotics are discouraged except where justified, such as brook trout in small headwater streams, Mackinaw in deep cold lakes, and rainbow in isolated drainages.
- The Loch Leven trout is described as harmful to native species. The rainbow interferes seriously with blackspotted trout spawning.
- Forage fishes are not controlled, even where they may affect fishing quality, because they support trout food supply and predators. The alleged egg-eating by suckers, minnows and sculpins was judged not conclusively proven.
