# Trophic Interrelations Among Introduced Fishes in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #introduced-species #trophic-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1982 study by W.L. Minckley analyzes stomach contents of 1,050 individuals representing 18 fish species from the mainstream lower [[Colorado River]] (Davis Dam to the U.S.-Mexico boundary) to characterize trophic structure in a heavily modified desert river system now dominated by non-native fishes. Of 44 recorded non-native taxa in the reach, 20 are locally/regionally abundant. The study reveals a simplified food web built on **autochthonous detritus, algae, and macrophytes**, rather than the allochthonous (terrestrial) inputs common in other large rivers (e.g., Missouri River). Key detritivores/planktivores such as [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Red Shiner]] serve as the primary forage base supporting a suite of introduced piscivores, including [[Striped Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]. The native fish fauna of the mainstream has been effectively extirpated, replaced by an exotic assemblage whose trophic relationships mirror those observed in their native ranges.

## Background & Description
While this paper is primarily an ecological/trophic study rather than a morphological one, several functionally relevant morphological notes are provided:

- **[[Redear Sunfish]]** (*Lepomis microlophus*): Possesses specialized **molariform pharyngeal teeth** adapted for crushing mollusk shells (e.g., [[Asiatic Clam]], *Corbicula fluminea*). However, fewer than 20% of ingested clam shells showed physical crushing damage, indicating that shell-crushing is not required for digestion — clams passed largely intact through the gut in this and other clam-eating species (carp, channel catfish, yellow bullhead, largemouth bass, mouthbrooder).
- **[[Sailfin Molly]]** (*Poecilia latipinna*) and **[[Mouthbrooder]]** (*Sarotherodon mossambica*, a tilapiine cichlid): Morphologically/behaviorally adapted for grazing within submerged macrophyte beds, consistent with high frequencies of benthic/epiphytic algae in their diets.
- Digestive tract structure varies by feeding mode: species with **ill-defined stomachs** (e.g., [[Common Carp]]) required examination of the anterior digestive tract rather than a discrete stomach for dietary analysis.
- **Semi-transparent body** of the introduced palaemonid shrimp (*Palaemonetes paludosus*) may reduce its vulnerability to predation except by specialized predators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
**Habitat context:** The lower Colorado River is a highly regulated system (Davis Dam to the International Boundary, 453 km), with suppressed natural flow variability, cold hypolimnetic releases near dams, warm epilimnetic releases downstream of [[Parker Dam]], and reservoirs (Lake Mohave, [[Lake Havasu]]) that trap organic/inorganic transport except for plankton passing through penstocks.

**Trophic guild structure identified:**
- **Detritivores:** [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Mouthbrooder]], [[Sailfin Molly]], [[Striped Mullet]] — feed principally on fibrous higher-plant detritus, some gelatinous organic material, and clam pseudofeces.
- **Facultative planktivores/omnivores:** [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]] — detritus plus algae, zooplankton, and Asiatic clams.
- **Benthic insectivores:** Nearly universal reliance on **chironomid dipteran larvae**; [[Rainbow Trout]] near Davis Dam also consumed simuliid larvae and hydropsychid trichopterans in roughly equal measure to chironomids.
- **Molluscivores:** Carp, channel catfish, yellow bullhead, redear sunfish, largemouth bass, and mouthbrooder consumed [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*), with some carp containing >30 clams and shells up to 2.5 cm.
- **Crayfish specialists:** Introduced [[Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarki*) was a major food source for large carnivores — catfish, [[Smallmouth Bass]] (which fed on it selectively), trout, carp, largemouth bass, warmouth, green sunfish, black crappie, striped bass, and the introduced [[Softshell Turtle]] (*Trionyx spiniferus*).
- **Piscivores:** [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Warmouth]], [[Black Crappie]], and [[Striped Bass]] were the most fish-dependent predators. Threadfin shad was the exclusive prey of trout; largemouth bass consumed shad, red shiner, and conspecifics/other centrarchids.

**Spatial variation in trophic structure across the study reach:**
- **Near Davis Dam:** Reservoir-derived plankton and detritus support filter-feeding invertebrates (e.g., simuliids); threadfin shad drawn through the dam support striped bass and larger rainbow trout; cold hypolimnetic water excludes warm-water species.
- **Topock Gorge:** Continued cold hypolimnetic influence; scarce red shiner and threadfin shad limit food availability for piscivores.
- **Lake Havasu:** Stable reservoir conditions; threadfin shad distribution tracks nutrient/plankton input zones, with largemouth bass distribution tracking shad; benthic invertebrate assemblages resemble other desert impoundments (see [[Rinne, Minckley And Bersell 1981]]).
- **Below Parker Dam:** Warm epilimnetic releases enhance warmwater fish habitat and benthic filter-feeders; macrophytes, benthic algae, and phytoplankton (partly from clam pseudofeces) form a large diet component; chironomids dominate insect prey.
- **Lowermost reaches:** Organic accumulation (reduced flooding, water diversion) favors expansion of detritivore populations (sailfin molly, striped mullet, mouthbrooder), though their broader distribution is limited more by temperature (winter cold, proximity to Davis Dam) and distance/barriers from the Gulf of California (for mullet) than by food availability.

**Comparative context:** Unlike the [[Missouri River]], where ~54% of ingested fish food derives from terrestrial (allochthonous) sources (Berner 1951), the lower Colorado River's sparse riparian zone and large channel-to-riparian ratio favor an **autochthonous-based food web**, consistent with Minshall's (1978) observations on open western streams generally.

## Key Findings
- **1,050 stomachs from 18 fish species** were analyzed using frequency-of-occurrence methodology (chosen over point-assignment or volumetric reconstruction methods to enable comparison across diverse taxa).
- The food web of the lower Colorado River mainstream is **simplified and autochthonous-based**, contrasting with allochthonous-dependent food webs in many other large rivers.
- **[[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Red Shiner]]** are the two most critical forage species, occupying the lowest trophic position among consumers and supporting the majority of the piscivore community; they were also reported as the most numerically abundant fish species in the river (Minckley 1979).
- **Asiatic clam pseudofeces** (bypassed particulate material extruded by *Corbicula fluminea*) represent an underappreciated but significant food pathway, transferring detritus and phytoplankton to fish detritivores/omnivores such as carp and channel catfish.
- **Empty stomach incidence correlates with trophic position:** No empty stomachs occurred among detritivores/facultative planktivores; large piscivores had high empty-stomach rates (flathead catfish 39.1%; striped bass 45%, per Edwards 1974; smallmouth bass, warmouth, and green sunfish also >20%). Largemouth bass, with a broader diet base, had a comparatively low rate (11.6%).
- **Channel catfish** showed surprisingly low reliance on benthic insects (chironomids at only 5.1% frequency) compared to findings in other North American streams (cf. Bailey and Harrison 1948).
- Several species act as **specialized piscivores**: warmouth fed heavily on poeciliids and red shiner; smallmouth bass and channel/flathead catfish were among the most piscivorous.
- The introduced [[Tiger Salamander]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum*), used commonly as bait, was found in stomachs of striped bass and channel catfish, indicating incidental human-mediated food web inputs.
- Despite occupying a novel, highly altered environment, the **food habits of introduced fishes in the Colorado River closely parallel those documented in their native ranges** (per Calhoun 1966 reviews), suggesting strong conservation of trophic niche despite ecosystem translocation.
- Native fish fauna (historically ~9 endemic species) is **effectively extirpated from the mainstream**; the only native species recorded in this diet study was [[Striped Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*).
- Reservoir and dam operations (hydroelectric pulsing, irrigation deliveries) create "almost-tidal" fluctuations that flush highly productive backwater plankton, vegetation, and fish biomass into the main channel, subsidizing downstream productivity.
