# Population Size and Structure of Humpback Chub in Black Rocks, 1998–2000

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] estimates the size and structure of the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) population in [[Black Rocks]], a short, deep-water reach of the [[Colorado River]]. Sampling took place in late summer to early fall of 1998, 1999 and 2000, using [[Trammel Net]]s as the primary gear, supplemented by [[Electrofishing]], trap nets and angling. Catch rates fell sharply in 2000. Using the closed-model estimator [[Model Mo]] in [[CAPTURE Program|CAPTURE]], adult abundance was 764 in 1998, 921 in 1999 and 539 in 2000, with a three-year mean of 741. Confidence intervals were wide, so the authors recommend using the three-year average as the best reference value. Direct handling mortality was very low, but the report notes delayed mortality (fungal infection) as a possible concern and recommends later-season sampling and evaluation of less stressful methods.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is a mid-sized cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River Basin. It was described in 1946 and is listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Humpback chub occur in discrete populations within canyon-bound reaches. The largest is in the Little Colorado and Colorado rivers in [[Grand Canyon]]; other populations occur in the Yampa and Green rivers, the Green River in [[Desolation Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]], the Colorado River in Black Rocks and [[Westwater Canyon]], and [[Cataract Canyon]].

Conflicts between water development and endangered fish began after the Endangered Species Act was passed in 1973. The [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] was created to fund recovery actions while allowing continued water development in the upper basin. Its [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP) monitored trends in Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon with short trammel-netting efforts every 2–3 years. ISMP data showed that humpback chub persisted and recruited, but they could not produce reliable abundance estimates. Developing quantifiable Recovery Goals required regular estimates of size and structure for the major populations, so studies began in Yampa Canyon, Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon in 1998.

Black Rocks is a 1-mile reach of deep water formed by erosion-resistant igneous rock, about 4 miles upstream of the Colorado–Utah state line. Its average depth is about 17 ft and its maximum about 60 ft, substantially deeper than other parts of the river. A shallow riffle near river mile 136.3 limited sampling upstream of that point.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and habitat.** Deep areas along the rock faces provide key habitat for chubs. The channel is narrow, with turbulent eddies, pools and runs. Sampling in the upstream and downstream parts of Black Rocks yielded much lower catch rates than the deep-water habitats where humpback chub concentrate.

**Water temperature and timing.** Sampling was scheduled after water temperatures began cooling, to reduce net-related mortality. Mean daily temperatures were 18–20 °C from late August to mid-September, 15–19 °C in mid to late September, and declined to about 10 °C by mid-October. The authors note that warm water may increase the risk of fungal infection after skin injury, and that very cool water may reduce fish movement and catchability.

**Water conditions in 2000.** Water levels were much lower in 2000 than in 1998 and 1999, and water temperatures were warmer. The authors suggest these conditions may have changed fish distribution or movement within Black Rocks. Low spring flows were also linked to reduced catches of [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] in the Green River.

**Co-occurring species.** [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) was the other Gila captured. Its mean catch per effort also declined in 2000, but less sharply than that of humpback chub.

**Movement between populations.** Tagged fish moved between Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon, about 10.5 river miles apart. The authors suggest the two locations may form a single population, a view reflected in draft recovery goals.

## Key Findings

### Methods and Handling

- **Sampling design:** Three trips in 1998 and 2000 and four in 1999, scheduled as a [[Mark-Recapture Sampling]] design. Each rotation lasted four days, with one week between trips.
- **Gear:** Multi-filament trammel nets (1-inch inner mesh), four to six set each morning and evening, checked at 1–2 h intervals (median 1.5 h), and left in place for no more than 4 h.
- **Handling:** Fish were identified as humpback or roundtail chub using [[Douglas et al. 1989]] criteria, measured (total length, ±1 mm), weighed (±1 g) and scanned for a [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag]]. Untagged fish were tagged before release. Fish were placed in a 1.5% salt bath for 0.5–1 min before release.
- **Live cages:** About 9% of humpback chub handled were held overnight in a soft-mesh live cage to assess short-term mortality.

### Relative Abundance

- **Total humpback chub handled:** 184 (1998), 293 (1999; four trips) and 68 (2000), excluding within-year recaptures.
- **Catch rates:** Mean [[Catch Per Effort]] was 0.3–0.55 fish per net hour in 1998 and 1999, but did not exceed 0.2 in 2000. The 2000 values differed significantly from those of 1998 and 1999.
- **Recapture rates:** About 11% within-year, and 30–47% overall, including recaptures of fish tagged by other investigators or in earlier years.

### Population Estimates

Estimates varied widely with the model chosen. In 1999, point estimates ranged from 635 (Schnabel) to 1,118 (Chao Mh). CAPTURE's model selection criteria identified Model Mo as the best fit.

| Year | Adult estimate (Model Mo) | 95% CI | CV | Capture probability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1998 | 764 | 512–1,246 | 0.23 | 0.08 |
| 1999 | 921 | 723–1,248 | 0.13 | 0.09 |
| 2000 | 539 | 223–1,497 | 0.54 | 0.04 |
| **Mean** | **741** | | | |

- Including three subadults (<200 mm TL) in 1998 raised that year's estimate to 948, but the difference was not significant.
- The 1999 CV was lowest because four passes were completed. The 2000 CV was high because capture probability was about half of the previous years and the fourth rotation was cancelled.

### Size Structure, Growth and Sex Ratio

- **Size range:** 18–40 cm TL. Length-frequency distributions were bimodal in all years, with modes at about 23–25 cm and 31–34 cm.
- **Juveniles:** Only three fish under 20 cm (the estimated adult minimum) were captured, all in 1998.
- **Growth:** 55 fish were recaptured across years. Growth averaged 7.58 mm/year (SE 0.57), ranging from 0 to 19 mm. Size groups split at 30 cm grew at virtually identical rates (7.58 mm and 7.59 mm).
- **Sex ratio:** Of 97 fish sexed in the last 1999 rotation, 46 were male and 51 female. This did not differ significantly from 1:1 (P > 0.1).

### Fish Condition and Mortality

- Of more than 500 humpback chub handled (many more than once), only two died, and both had trouble orienting in the live well.
- None of the 50 fish held overnight died.
- Two recaptured chubs (one roundtail in 1999, one humpback in 2000) had severe fungal infections. Their survival is unknown.

### Movement

- 14 humpback chub tagged in Westwater Canyon before 1998 were recaptured in Black Rocks.
- Three humpback chub tagged in Black Rocks were recaptured in Westwater Canyon, one in 1998 and two in 1999.
- Two fish were recaptured in both locations within the same year, which the authors cite as evidence that movement is more frequent than previously believed.

### Comparison with ISMP Data (1988–1997)

- Mean CPE was significantly lower in 1998–2000 than in earlier ISMP sampling. The regression slope over the 12-year record was significantly negative (P < 0.05).
- The proportion of fish over 30 cm averaged 70% under ISMP and 55% in this study. In 1991, 86% of fish collected were over 30 cm.
- A bimodal size distribution first appeared in 1994.
- Small fish occurred in every year, indicating ongoing recruitment.
- Nesler (2000) estimated 1994 and 1997 populations at 537 and 1,197 (or 2,267 at 90% survival, depending on the survival assumption). These bracketed the 741 mean but were too variable to show a population trend.

### Related Populations

- **Westwater Canyon:** Chart and Lentsch (1999b) estimated 5,621 (1994), 10,148 (1995) and 5,186 (1996), a mean of 6,985. A three-pass study for 1998–2000 is still being analysed.
- **Yampa Canyon:** Haines and Modde (2002) estimated 100–2,000 fish, with a best estimate of about 400. Recaptures occurred in only one of three years.

### Discussion and Conclusions

- Estimates differed among years but were not significantly different because of wide confidence intervals. The three-year mean (741) is the most reliable reference value.
- The closed-model assumption was violated, since movement between Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon occurred within years.
- Four sampling passes improve precision (lower CV, tighter intervals), but may increase cumulative stress on the population.
- Direct mortality was very low, but delayed mortality from fungal infection is possible.
- The 2000 decline may reflect true abundance, reduced catchability, or environmental effects such as low flow and warm water.

### Recommendations

1. Continue Black Rocks sampling to meet the Recovery Program time frame, keeping trammel nets while evaluating less stressful alternatives.
2. Sample from mid-September to mid-October, when water temperatures range between 10 and 15 °C.
3. Explore open-population models to compare with closed-model results.
