# Virgin Spinedace: Proposed Listing as a Threatened Species (Federal Register, 1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] proposed rule would list the [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*), a small minnow endemic to the [[Virgin River]] drainage, as a **threatened** species under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The Service estimates that about 40 percent of the species' historic habitat has been lost. The main threats are water development (dams, diversions, dewatering), habitat fragmentation, livestock grazing, mining contamination, and predation and competition from nonnative fishes. The Service found the species is not yet in danger of extinction across its range, but is likely to become endangered if current threats continue. Critical habitat was judged "not prudent" at this time and will be proposed later, together with two other Virgin River fishes.

The same Federal Register issue also contains an unrelated FCC radio allotment notice (Bamberg, South Carolina), which is not aquatic and is not summarized here.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The Virgin spinedace was first grouped with the Little Colorado spinedace (*L. vittata*), then recognized as an unnamed species (*Lepidomeda* sp.) by Miller (1952). Eddy (1957) assigned it to *L. mollispinis*, and Miller and Hubbs (1960) described it as the subspecies *L. m. mollispinis*. The name derives from Latin *mollis* ("soft") and *spina* ("spine"), referring to the soft-tipped dorsal spine.

**Tribe and relatives.** The species belongs to the western tribe [[Plagopterini]] (family [[Cyprinidae]]), which has three genera: *Lepidomeda*, *Meda* (spikedace, *M. fulgida*), and *Plagopterus* (woundfin, *P. argentissimus*). All extant members are very rare. The woundfin and White River spinedace are listed as endangered, while the spikedace, Little Colorado spinedace, and [[Big Spring Spinedace]] are listed as threatened. The Pahranagat spinedace is considered extinct.

**Morphology.**
- Adults are 80–120 mm (3–5 in) total length.
- Broad, flat, silvery body with a brassy sheen; adults often have sooty green or dark brown splotches on the sides.
- Rounded head and belly, terminal mouth, small scales.
- Paired-fin bases turn reddish-orange in breeding season.
- Eight dorsal rays; typically nine anal rays (range eight to ten).
- 77–91 scales on the lateral line; pharyngeal teeth in two rows.

**Life history.**
- Matures in about one year and lives about three years.
- Spawns April–June at mean daily water temperatures of 13–17 °C (55–63 °F).
- Spawning occurs over gravel and sand at the shallow downstream end of deep pools; a solitary female was observed releasing eggs that several males fertilized.
- Timing is controlled mainly by photoperiod and temperature.
- Feeds in the midwater column and rises to the surface for floating plant material, aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, and debris. Fish in poor condition were found where diets were mostly plant material (Rinne 1971).

**Habitat preferences.** The species favors clear, cool, flowing streams with pools, runs, and riffles. It uses pools with undercut banks, debris, or boulders, and in Beaver Dam Wash it occupied narrow, shallow runs with emergent vegetation. Studies by Deacon and Rebane (1979) and Hardy et al. (1989) found it prefers the shear zone between high and low velocities that includes cover.

**Petition history.**
- June 15, 1992: petition from the Bonneville Chapter of the [[American Fisheries Society]] to list the species as endangered.
- August 17, 1992: petition from the Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance requesting endangered listing.
- Petitioners reported a range reduction of about 40 percent and at least three extirpated populations.
- March 16, 1993 (58 FR 14169): the Service found the petitions presented substantial information that listing may be warranted, and began a status review.
- Under Section 4(b)(3), a final decision on a petition is due within 12 months; this proposal constitutes that final finding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic and current distribution.**
- Historically common-to-abundant in tributaries of the Virgin River and in some mainstem reaches above Pah Tempe (La Verkin) Springs near Hurricane, Utah. Probably less abundant in the mainstem below the springs.
- Historical collection sites include the Santa Clara River (Veyo, Shivwits, Gunlock), Ash Creek near Toquerville, the North Fork of the Virgin River in Zion National Park, Beaver Dam Wash, and sites near Bunkerville, Nevada.
- Valdez et al. (1991) estimated that about 40 percent (140 km; 87 mi) of historic habitat has been lost. Catch data from Hardy and Addley (1993) indicate a similar loss.
- The species no longer occurs in Nevada and is rarely found in Arizona. Nearly all remaining occupied habitat is in Utah, where about 60 percent of historic habitat is still occupied.
- Current Utah populations include the mainstem Virgin River above Quail Creek Diversion, three reaches of Beaver Dam Wash, the Santa Clara River between Veyo Springs and Gunlock Reservoir, isolated reaches of Moody Wash and Mogatsu Creek, and a 6.3 km (3.9 mi) reach of the lower Santa Clara River.
- Cross (1975) reported no records below Littlefield, Arizona, since 1942.

**Threats (five ESA factors).**

*A. Habitat destruction, modification, or curtailment.* This is the dominant threat.
- Water development: dams and diversions built for agriculture and municipal use dewatered reaches such as the DI Ranch (East Fork of Beaver Dam Wash), the Santa Clara River below Gunlock Reservoir, Mogatsu Creek, Ash Creek, Leeds Creek, and the mainstem Virgin River between Quail Creek Diversion and Pah Tempe Springs.
- Dams and diversions also fragment habitat, block fish movement, and cause low dissolved oxygen, high temperatures, altered pH and turbidity, and water chemistry changes. In North Creek, the Virgin Canal Diversion isolates fish in pools connected only by trickles.
- Livestock grazing causes devegetation, bank erosion, siltation, and degraded water quality. Valdez et al. (1991) found that 10 of 13 populations were threatened by grazing in riparian areas and by runoff from cattle feedlots. No exclosures existed in riparian zones.
- Reservoirs inundated historic habitat: Schroeder Reservoir (0.5 km), Baker Dam Reservoir (1 km), Gunlock Reservoir (2.9 km), and [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] (3.4 km, about 2.1 mi of high-quality habitat when filled in 1985). Virgin spinedace have since disappeared from Quail Creek, where they were formerly common-to-abundant.
- The Service notes that habitat affected by reservoirs exceeds the inundated area, but pre- and post-impoundment studies are lacking.

*B. Overutilization.* Not recorded as a factor in the decline. The species was once used as bait in the lower Colorado River, but this no longer occurs. Recent research has not been shown to harm populations.

*C. Disease or predation.*
- [[Largemouth Bass]] selectively prey on the species (Espinosa and Deacon 1973). Largemouth bass, brown trout, and rainbow trout stocked in reservoirs can migrate into spinedace habitat.
- Rainbow trout below Schroeder Reservoir likely prevent upstream recolonization of Beaver Dam Wash.
- Crayfish and [[Red Shiner]] prey on larvae and young-of-year in lower tributary reaches.
- The red shiner is considered a primary cause of the decline of the endangered [[Woundfin]] and is assumed to have a similar effect on the Virgin spinedace.
- The redside shiner was once considered a threat but has declined significantly; no competition with other native species has been documented.
- The [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) arrived in 1986 with the red shiner. It alone probably does not cause major declines, but it may have a significant adverse effect combined with stressors such as low water.
- An unknown parasite was found on spinedace in the Ash Creek drainage; its effect is unknown.

*D. Inadequate regulatory mechanisms.*
- Land ownership is a complex mosaic: about 54 percent of land along the Virgin River is privately owned; the Federal government manages the remaining 38 percent; the Paiute Indian Tribe manages about seven miles of the Santa Clara River within historic habitat; Utah holds four small parcels.
- Utah lists the species as protected, which prohibits direct take but not habitat destruction; enforcement is difficult.
- Nevada prohibits illegal take. Arizona provides no protection.
- The Service judged current protection inadequate to prevent the species from becoming endangered and eventually extinct.

*E. Other natural or human factors.*
- Drought dewaters intermittent tributaries. Historically fish could retreat to refuges, but dams and diversions now block these movements, causing local extirpation.
- Drought combined with intensified agricultural or municipal diversions causes greater habitat loss than drought alone.
- Pollution from municipal and agricultural return flows, pesticides and herbicides, cattle waste, and erosion affects water quality. Low flows increase the influence of mineral springs on water chemistry.
- Mining contamination threatens Beaver Dam Wash and Moody Wash; cyanide contamination from a gold mine on the East Fork of Beaver Dam Wash is under study.

**Conservation status and related species.** The Service listed the [[Big Spring Spinedace]] as threatened in 1985 (50 FR 12302). This proposal would list the only remaining subspecies of the Virgin spinedace and would effectively list the entire species. The American Fisheries Society listed the Virgin spinedace as threatened in its 1979 and 1989 lists of endangered, threatened, or special concern fishes.

## Key Findings

1. **Proposed status:** Threatened, under the Endangered Species Act. The Service determined that the species is not in danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of its range, so endangered status is not warranted at this time, but it is likely to become endangered if present threats continue.

2. **Range loss:** About 40 percent (140 km; 87 mi) of historic habitat has been lost (Valdez et al. 1991; confirmed by Hardy and Addley 1993 catch data). Nevada populations are gone, and few fish remain in Arizona. About 60 percent of historic habitat in Utah is still occupied.

3. **Primary threat:** Water development (dams, diversions, dewatering, and proposed impoundments on North Creek, Ash Creek, the North and East Forks of the Virgin River, and Beaver Dam Wash). Habitat fragmentation has isolated populations, limited gene flow, and reduced effective population size.

4. **Other threats:** Livestock grazing (10 of 13 populations threatened, per Valdez et al. 1991), mining and agricultural contamination, nonnative predators and competitors (largemouth bass, trout, red shiner, crayfish), the Asian tapeworm, drought combined with diversions, and inadequate state protection (none in Arizona).

5. **Critical habitat:** Not prudent at this time. The Service believes a multi-species designation covering the Virgin spinedace, the endangered [[Woundfin]], and the endangered [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*) under an aquatic ecosystem approach would provide greater benefit than separate rules. A proposed designation is planned for later, with public comment and hearings.

6. **Federal nexus:** Section 7 consultation applies to federal agencies (Bureau of Land Management, National Park Service, U.S. Forest Service) and to projects needing Army Corps of Engineers permits under Section 404 of the Clean Water Act. Flood control, reservoir, channelization, and highway projects may also be affected.

7. **Prohibitions:** Under 50 CFR 17.21 and 17.31, it is illegal to take, import, export, sell, or ship listed threatened wildlife. Permits (50 CFR 17.32) are available for scientific, propagation, and incidental take purposes.

8. **Comment and hearing deadlines:** Public hearing requests were due July 5, 1994; comments were due July 18, 1994. The Service sought data on threats, new population locations, range and population size, and current or planned activities affecting the species.
