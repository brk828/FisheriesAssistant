# The Distribution of the Fishes of Arkansas: A Multivariate Analysis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Matthews and Robison]] provides the first comprehensive, quantitative account of the distribution of native stream fishes across [[Arkansas]]. It draws on 2,323 collections made over roughly 20 years by the authors and other ichthyologists, assigned to 101 defined drainage units (DRUs) that cover the whole state. Using [[Principal Components Analysis]] and [[Detrended Correspondence Analysis]] (DCA), the authors identified five fish faunal regions and showed that fish distributions track a northwest-to-southeast environmental gradient running from upland to lowland. Environmental variables explain much of the pattern, but membership of a river system also has a significant independent effect, indicating a strong [[Zoogeography]] component.

## Background & Description

Arkansas lies across two major physiographic provinces. Northern and western Arkansas comprise the [[Ozark Mountains]] and [[Ouachita Mountains]], separated by the [[Arkansas River]] Valley. A relatively sharp [[Fall Line]] marks the transition to the lowlands of the [[Mississippi Alluvial Plain]] and the [[West Gulf Coastal Plain]] in eastern and southern Arkansas. Elevation, geology, soils, forest type, temperature and rainfall all coincide with this gradient.

Knowledge of Arkansas fishes accumulated in stages:

- **1850s:** The first scientific collections came from U.S. Army railroad surveys (Whipple, 1856), with species described mostly by Girard (1857, 1858).
- **1894:** Meek's *A Catalogue of the Fishes of Arkansas* (Meek, 1894), based on collecting from 1889–1896, was the most significant report of its time.
- **1940:** Black's *Distribution of the Fishes of Arkansas* used 339 collections from 268 locations (1938–39) plus earlier material. It described faunal regions but emphasized differences among major river systems and included no quantitative analysis.
- **Later:** Buchanan's *Key to the Fishes of Arkansas* detailed all known collections to that date. Regional studies by Mayden (1985) and others addressed the Ouachita fauna and its origins, but no comprehensive quantitative distribution analysis existed before this work.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Most collections were made with small-meshed seines fished over 200–400 m stream reaches, with seining accounting for about 91% of collecting. Electrofishing, gill netting and hoop netting were used less often. Introduced species, such as [[Bluegill]] and [[Largemouth Bass]], were excluded because they are widespread throughout the state and do not reflect natural distribution patterns.

**Drainage units.** The state was divided into 101 DRUs using 1:500,000 USGS base maps. Most units consisted of one medium or large creek plus its tributaries. Larger rivers were split into two or more units, with boundaries placed at points where environmental conditions changed abruptly, such as where the [[White River]] shifts from upland to lowland character near Batesville. Some divisions in larger rivers were arbitrary.

**Environmental variables.** Eleven variables were chosen a priori for each DRU:

- Mean January and July temperature (°F)
- Mean annual precipitation (in)
- Mean frost-free days
- Longitude and latitude
- Elevation (from the highest benchmark or the 200 ft contour)
- Turbidity (a six-point visual scale, from very clear to very turbid)
- Substrate (a scale from all silt/mud to all bedrock)
- Stream width (five size classes, from <10 m to >100 m)
- Geology (a scale from loose alluvium to limestone and dolomite, with intermediate values for mixed units such as the Boone Formation)

**Upland versus lowland.** Many of the environmental variables and fish distributions contrast upland with lowland species. Within each of these broad zones, however, distinct fish groupings still occur.

## Key Findings

**1. Four principal-component groups.** The [[Principal Components Analysis]] of fish distributions revealed four distinct, mutually exclusive groups of fishes.
- PC II explained the greatest share of variance among the components reported. Twenty-four species had absolute loadings above 0.500 on this axis. Many of them are restricted to, or most typical of, northern or southwestern Arkansas. Examples include *Notropis rubellus*, *N. telescopus*, *Percina caprodes*, and *Cottus hypselurus*.
- Other components separated lowland species, such as *Lepisosteus osseus*, *Amia calva*, *Notropis boops* and *Esox americanus*, from upland taxa.

**2. Five fish faunal regions.** DCA Axis I scores were broken into five regions at the largest natural gaps between sorted DRU scores (Fig. 9). These gaps were judged more meaningful than the smaller breaks, and the authors chose not to recognize two additional regions defined by only two DRUs.

- **Region 1 (northwest Arkansas):** The [[White River]] drainage together with the [[Neosho-Illinois River System]] (DRU 1–3), plus the upper [[Black River]]. Species include *Etheostoma caeruleum*, *E. euzonum*, *E. juliae*, *Percina evides*, *Cottus hypselurus* and *C. carolinae*.
- **Region 2 (southern Ozark and Ouachita uplands):** Streams tributary to the [[Arkansas River]], as well as the Ouachita uplands. Many species here, including *Notropis pilsbryi*, *N. telescopus*, *Phoxinus erythrogaster*, *Percina caprodes* and *P. ouachitae*, are also restricted to these uplands.
- **Region 3 (upland-lowland ecotone):** A transitional region with strong lowland influence. Its streams often drain the Gulf Coastal Plain, and it includes species with high loadings on PC I. Pflieger (1971) had earlier identified a similar ecotonal Ozark border group in Missouri.
- **Regions 4 and 5 (lowlands):** Two distinct lowland regions, one of which contains the big-river fauna of the Mississippi and the lower Red and Arkansas rivers (DRU 98–101). Species such as *Polyodon spathula* ([[Paddlefish]]), *Ichthyomyzon castaneus*, *Alosa chrysochloris*, *Hybognathus nuchalis* and *Ictiobus niger* are essentially absent from the Ozark and Ouachita uplands.

**3. Environmental correlates.** Correlation and multiple regression showed that fish distributions are significantly related to the measured environmental variables. Elevation, longitude and latitude, temperature and precipitation all contributed, with the upland-lowland contrast the dominant axis of variation.

**4. River system matters.** A Kruskal–Wallis test of DCA Axis I scores for all upland DRUs, with the five major river systems as treatments, was very highly significant (H = 23.032, P < 0.0001). Fish distribution therefore cannot be explained by environment alone. River system membership, and the history it reflects, contributes independently. The authors conclude that distribution is "a product of zoogeographic chance (i.e., river system) as well as numerous intercorrelated environmental factors."

**5. Anomalous units.** Three Neosho-Illinois DRUs (1–3) group with the White River fauna rather than with the Arkansas River fauna to which they hydrologically belong. The authors suggest this may reflect shared upland, headwater habitat, or a prehistoric connection between systems, or possibly recent human-mediated transfer of some taxa. The lower [[Little Red River]] (DRU 45) is an outlier within the White River system. It enters the White River far downstream, after the White has received the Black River, and it has a different environmental setting.

**6. Conservation and research implications.** Because several regions have species restricted to them, and because river-system history strongly shapes the fauna, conservation planning and biodiversity assessment in Arkansas should treat the faunal regions and river systems as units. The DRU framework and dataset also provide a baseline for detecting future changes in distribution.
