# Flagships, Umbrellas, and Keystones: Is Single-Species Management Passé in the Landscape Era?

#conservation-biology #landscape-ecology #multi-taxa

## Executive Summary

This review essay by [[Daniel Simberloff]] (Florida State University, *Biological Conservation*, 1998) asks whether managing individual species is still a useful conservation tool as conservation shifts toward [[Ecosystem Management]] and landscape scales. The author examines three single-species shortcuts, namely [[Indicator Species]], [[Umbrella Species]] and [[Flagship Species]], and finds each problematic. He then argues that [[Keystone Species]] offer a middle path that combines the strengths of single-species and ecosystem approaches. The paper is largely North American in scope and draws heavily on the [[Endangered Species Act]]. It is an argument and synthesis, not an original field study, so most of its "data" are illustrative case examples.

## Background & Description

The paper's motivation is the perceived impossibility of monitoring and managing every aspect of biodiversity. Single-species shortcuts are a response to that constraint:

- **Indicator species** are expected to reflect the status of other species or of physical and chemical conditions. The author considers only the first type.
- **Umbrella species** have habitat needs so large that protecting them is assumed to protect many others.
- **Flagship species** are charismatic, usually large vertebrates that anchor public campaigns. They need not be good indicators or umbrellas.

The author frames the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973) as the main legal vehicle for US biodiversity protection. It is reactive and species-focused, yet courts and agencies have stretched it to protect habitat and, indirectly, whole ecosystems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Case examples from the paper:**

- **[[Northern Spotted Owl]]** (*Strix occidentalis caurina*): The US Forest Service named it a management indicator species for the Pacific Northwest. The author notes that few data support its indicator role. The owl needs about 800 ha per pair in the middle of its range, so saving its habitat might also protect other old-growth species. Yet three amphibians of special concern, two anadromous fish, and the [[Marbled Murrelet]] are not fully covered, and about 6000 old-growth arthropod species have poorly known requirements.
- **[[Red-Cockaded Woodpecker]]** (*Picoides borealis*): Longleaf pine forest has declined from about 28 million ha to 600 ha of old growth and 4 million ha of second growth. The woodpecker's excavated cavities are used by at least 22 other species. Gopher tortoise burrows host 332 other species. The Forest Service's management plan covers 11 national forests totalling 1,361,000 ha, which contain 31 other listed species and 71 proposed for listing. Only four pages of the plan address these other species.
- **[[Florida Panther]]** (*Felis concolor coryi*): Roughly 40 individuals remain in south Florida. Male home ranges average 530 km² and female ranges 300 km². Florida's conservation land budget could be exhausted by panther habitat purchases alone. Proposed panther areas overlap with at least 24 of 51 other threatened Florida taxa and 29 threatened plants. Yet panther conservation costs about $1–4 million per year (pers. comm.) on top of land purchases for the 12,000 ha Panther National Wildlife Refuge. Thousands of Floridians pay $66 annually for a panther license plate. Genetic introgression from captive Central or South American cats prompted plans to import cats from Texas.
- **[[Tongass National Forest]]** (Alaska, about 6,812,000 ha as given): No single forest-dwelling species qualifies for protection under the Endangered Species Act, illustrating a gap for communities and ecosystems that the author says the US lacks law to protect.

**Species conflicts:**

- At **[[Ash Meadows]]** (about 20,000 ha near Las Vegas), at least 26 endemic species occur. The [[Ash Meadows Naucorid]] is the only protected aquatic insect under the Act, yet its population keeps declining because habitat is managed for the [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]], which numbers 200–500 individuals in a single small pool and is unlikely to be delisted.
- In the Everglades, the [[Everglades Snail Kite]] (about 600 individuals) needs high water levels, whereas the [[Wood Stork]] (perhaps 10,000 pairs) benefits from lower water. The US Fish and Wildlife Service opposed a Park Service proposal to alter water flow for storks because it would harm the kite.
- In California, the [[Giant Kangaroo Rat]] favours non-native plants and damages the endangered *Caulanthus californicus*, with no obvious management solution.

## Key Findings

1. **Indicator species are problematic.** There is no consensus on what an indicator should indicate (species richness, structure, or function). Choosing a good indicator would require a pilot study of co-occurrence and population correlations, which the author says has never been attempted. Scale also matters: large vertebrates may indicate other species needing large continuous habitat but not those in fragmented landscapes.
2. **Intensive management undermines indicators and umbrellas.** Moving, feeding or sheltering a single indicator species, as logging interests proposed for owls, is "a self-contradiction" because the species no longer reflects the status of the community it is meant to represent.
3. **Umbrella protection is often assumed rather than tested.** Whether protecting a wide-ranging species saves other species "is a matter of faith rather than research."
4. **Flagship species are costly and may not be ecologically useful.** They anchor campaigns but may not indicate or shelter other species. Campaigns for two flagships can also conflict.
5. **Ecosystem management is poorly defined.** Its definitions lack consensus, often stress human commodity use over protecting component species, and rely on adaptive management, which can make it hard to test mechanisms or reject hypotheses. The author warns that it could be a "Trojan horse" for continued environmental destruction, while its proponents see it as a way to achieve economies of scale.
6. **Ecosystem health is an ill-defined goal.** It can be used to justify sacrificing particular species, such as a spotted owl or red-cockaded woodpecker, if they conflict with commodity production. The concept relies on an imperfect superorganism metaphor.
7. **Processes can persist after species are lost.** Energy flow and nutrient cycling may continue in species-poor systems, so maintaining processes does not guarantee maintaining species. Many flagship species are top carnivores with little effect on processes if they vanish.
8. **Multiple use may not be feasible.** Some ecosystems may need to serve either resource extraction or species protection almost exclusively.
9. **Keystone species offer a promising alternative.** Keystones have effects far beyond their biomass, as with the sea star studied by [[Robert Paine]] and beavers, which change physical structure. Their study follows conventional analytic science, could yield insights into whole ecosystems, and avoids the ambiguous terminology of ecosystem management. Power et al. (1996) are pessimistic about quickly identifying keystones using simple attributes, and the author agrees.
10. **Keystone management is a hypothesis, not a proven strategy.** Managing a keystone might sustain its dependent species, but this must be tested. Keystone status can be context-dependent, and some ecosystems may have no keystones at all. Even so, the research would add knowledge useful for conservation.
11. **Conservation failures are often not scientific.** Many listed species disappeared because of economic, political or social factors, such as the dusky seaside sparrow's decline due to lax enforcement, rather than because biologists lacked knowledge.
12. **The Endangered Species Act faces political pressure.** The 1995 Supreme Court decision in *Babbitt v. Sweet Home* upheld a broad definition of "harm" that includes habitat destruction, by a 6–3 vote. Public anger over this use of the Act, shown in the spotted owl debate, has put the law itself at risk.
