# The Bantam Sunfish, *Lepomis symmetricus*: Systematics, Distribution, and Life History in Wolf Lake, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Volume 31, Article 10, September 1977) by [[Brooks M. Burr]] is a systematic, distributional, and life-history study of the [[Bantam Sunfish]] (*Lepomis symmetricus*), one of the least known members of the genus [[Lepomis]]. The author combined a literature review, morphological analysis of museum specimens, a distributional assessment, and a life-history study of 233 specimens collected at [[Wolf Lake]], southern Illinois, between June 1973 and May 1975. The species is small, cryptic, and restricted to vegetated, lentic habitats of the [[Coastal Plain]]. Its historic range reached northward to the [[Illinois River]] near Pekin and the Wabash River system, but it has been extirpated from much of that northern area. The report also assesses its conservation status and recommends continued protection.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The species was described by [[Stephen A. Forbes]] in 1883 from 15 syntypes collected in April and June 1880 from the Illinois River at Pekin, Tazewell County, Illinois. Because the original material is no longer tied to a known habitat, a lectotype (INHS 75004, 39.5 mm SL, juvenile) was designated to stabilize nomenclature. The synonymy is extensive, with more than 100 cited references from 1882 to 1976, and the genus placement has changed several times (e.g., *Apomotis*, *Lethogrammus*, subgenus *Lethogrammus*). Earlier work suggested the species is most closely related to *Lepomis cyanellus*. Electrophoretic, hybridization, and chromosome studies of *Lepomis* did not include *L. symmetricus*, so its placement remains unresolved.

**Diagnosis.** It is the smallest *Lepomis* (largest specimen 75.5 mm SL). Diagnostic traits include an incomplete or interrupted lateral line, long slender gill rakers (12–15, modally 13), a stiff opercle, and a dark opercular spot. Juveniles have a black blotch in the posterior soft dorsal fin, which fades with age. Other distinguishing characters include a single posterior pore on the post-temporal, a lateralis ending under the soft dorsal fin, and no teeth on the tongue or pterygoids.

**Meristics.** Key counts (see Key Findings) include lateral-line scales 30–38 (mode 32), caudal peduncle scales 17–22 (mode 19), dorsal soft rays 9–12 (mode 10), anal soft rays 9–12 (mode 10), and pectoral rays 11–13.

**Coloration.** Adults are dusky olive-brown to dark green with coffee-colored spots forming vague vertical bars. Breeding males darken on the head and develop a silvery-cream opercular spot outline, a grayish-black venter, and a brilliant red iris. Breeding females show 9–10 dark bluish-purple vertical bars with golden-green cheek spots.

**Variation.** Sexual dimorphism is limited to a significantly longer pelvic fin in males (F = 8.29, p < 0.05) and enlarged urogenital papillae in breeding females. Allometric change is mainly in color: the dorsal spot and red-orange fin pigmentation fade as fish mature. Meristic counts show clinal geographic variation, while body proportions do not (Table 5), making the species "remarkably conservative" across its range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** [[Wolf Lake]] is a long (ca. 1.9 km), narrow (ca. 0.1 km) oxbow of the [[Big Muddy River]] in the [[Mississippi River Drainage]], connected by swamp to the [[LaRue-Pine Hills Ecological Area]]. It is estimated to be at least 2,000 years old, and it remains relatively undisturbed. The northern shore is U.S. Forest Service land, and the southern portion is owned by the [[Trojan Powder Plant]].

**Habitat.** The lake has two habitats: a heavily vegetated shoreline with submerged logs and stumps (where *L. symmetricus* occurs) and an open, unvegetated deepwater center. Dominant plants include spatterdock, American lotus, common arrowhead, coontail, and duckweed. The bottom is decomposed vegetation, silt, and mud. Water depth ranges from 300 mm to 18 m. Dissolved oxygen averages 9.0 ppm. Temperatures range from 4–8 °C (December–February) to as high as 29 °C (July–August). In fall and winter fish stayed at 150–300 mm depth near the shore; in summer they moved to 600–1200 mm within the vegetated margin.

**Associated species.** The bantam sunfish was almost always collected alone in its habitat, while other *Lepomis* were usually found in more open, deeper water. Common associates were [[Bluegill]], [[Warmouth]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Banded Pygmy Sunfish]], and [[Creek Chubsucker]] (see Key Findings).

**Distribution and decline.** Along the Gulf Coast, the species ranges from Eagle Lake, Texas, east to Mississippi, and it is statewide in Louisiana. Historically it reached the Illinois River at Pekin and the Wabash system in White County, Illinois. It has not been collected at the type locality since 1880 or in the Wabash valley since 1882. The author attributes the decline to stocking of nonnative sunfishes, loss of aquatic vegetation, draining of swamps and sloughs, and agricultural and industrial pollution. The author also notes that the Mississippi River may act as a dispersal barrier in Mississippi, and that the species may be dispersing through oxbows and sloughs from the lower Mississippi Valley.

**Conservation status.** Miller (1972) listed the species as rare in Illinois and Missouri. It is protected under the [[Illinois Fish Code]] and is listed as endangered in Illinois (Ackerman 1975). It was recommended for Kentucky's rare and endangered list (Webb & Sisk 1975). In Oklahoma it occurs only in McCurtain County swamps, and in Arkansas it is known from many localities and is not considered threatened. Protection in refuges such as [[Mingo National Wildlife Refuge]] is the main safeguard.

**Parasites.** The Wolf Lake population was heavily parasitized by plerocercoids of the cestode [[Haplobothrium Globuliforme]] (25% of stomachs). A single adult acanthocephalan, *Pomphorynchus bulbicolli*, was also recorded. Earlier published parasite records attributed to *L. symmetricus* in Michigan and in Hoffman (1967) were judged to be misidentifications.

## Key Findings

**Systematics and morphology**
- Lateral line scales: 30–38, mode 32 (Table 2).
- Caudal peduncle scales: 17–22, mode 19 (Table 1).
- Dorsal soft rays: 9–12, mode 10; anal soft rays: 9–12, mode 10 (Tables 3–4).
- Gill rakers: 12–15, mode 13. Pectoral rays: 11–13 (mode 12, mean 12.2).
- Clinal geographic trends were detected in caudal peduncle scales, lateral line scales, and anal soft rays; body proportions showed no significant geographic variation.
- The most aberrant samples come from the formerly known Wabash River oxbows in White County, Illinois.
- The largest specimen measured was 75.5 mm SL (Louisiana); the largest from Wolf Lake was 63.0 mm SL.

**Specimens and distribution**
- 15 syntypes from Pekin, Illinois (1880) are extant; a lectotype (INHS 75004) was designated.
- 233 specimens were preserved from Wolf Lake and examined; the author's Specimens Studied section lists more than 70 lots across Illinois, Kentucky, Missouri, Tennessee, Arkansas, Oklahoma, Louisiana, and Texas.
- The species has declined sharply in northern Illinois and is now largely confined to the Coastal Plain.

**Life history at Wolf Lake (June 1973–May 1975)**
- Sexual maturity: females about 34 mm and males about 40 mm, with ages of 1 year or more.
- Mature ova: 219–1,600 per female (14 ripe females). The ovary-to-adjusted-body-weight ratio ranged from 0.070 to 0.308 (mean 0.107).
- Egg diameter: 0.6–0.9 mm; translucent orange with a single oil droplet.
- Spawning: captures of breeding fish occurred from 24 April to 30 May 1974, with most spawning likely in May at 18–22 °C. Typical spawning across the range is mid-April to early June.
- Males likely build shallow nests of 90–120 mm diameter in mud or sand near vegetation. Courtship in aquaria included prolonged nipping and chasing; a female was killed after 7 days of male harassment.
- Growth slows over the first year (about half of the first year's mean growth in about 10 weeks). Fitted relation: Y = 5.91 + 32.97 log X (X = age in months, r = 0.943).
- Males grew slightly faster than females, but differences were not significant at 13–18 or 19–24 months.

**Population and demography**
- Maximum density: 0.69 sunfish per m² in Wolf Lake (28 March 1974); LaRue-Pine Hills swamp reached 0.72 per m².
- Age composition: 85.4% ≤ 1 year, 12.4% 1+ to 2, 0.8% 2+ to 3, 1.2% over 3 years.
- Sex ratio: 1.4 females : 1 male overall (χ² = 5.97, P < 0.025); females predominated significantly among young-of-the-year.
- Longevity: 3+ years. The oldest Wolf Lake fish was a 3-year, 2-month-old female. Other specimens confirm a 3+ year lifespan across the range.
- Relative survival dropped sharply after the first year. Only three individuals older than 3 years were found. The author notes that sampling likely underestimates older fish.

**Diet**
- 176 stomachs examined: 29 were empty and 8 contained only green algae.
- Predominant foods: gastropods, cladocerans, ostracods, amphipods, dragonfly naiads, chironomids, and ceratopogonids.
- Small fish (< 21 mm) fed mainly on microcrustaceans, odonate naiads, and chironomids; large fish (> 40 mm) on gastropods, odonates, and amphipods.
- Feeding intensity peaked before and during spawning, and aquatic hemipterans were eaten only in summer. Some terrestrial prey (Fulgoridae) were taken from the water surface.

**Interactions**
- Competition: the species is syntopic with all other *Lepomis* except *L. gibbosus*, and the author judges that congeners are unlikely to limit its range strongly.
- Predation: no literature reports exist. Among 20+ potential predators examined (largemouth bass, black crappie, bluegill, warmouth, and others), none had eaten the species, although large gar were observed and may take some toll.
- Hybridization: none found in the literature (Schwartz 1972) or in the study.

**Conservation conclusions**
- The species is rare and in decline in Illinois, Missouri, and Kentucky, but common in Louisiana, eastern Texas, southern Arkansas, and parts of western Tennessee.
- Its survival depends on protection of shallow, vegetated, undisturbed oxbows, swamps, and sloughs.
- Recommended management: continued protection under state fish codes, refuge habitat preservation, and avoidance of nonnative stocking and habitat drainage.
