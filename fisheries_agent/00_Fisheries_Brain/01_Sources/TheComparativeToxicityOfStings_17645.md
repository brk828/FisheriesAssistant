# Comparative Toxicity of Stings of the Ictalurid Catfish Genera Ictalurus and Schilbeodes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 study by William S. Birkhead (University of Texas, Austin) compares the toxicity of venom extracts from the stings and caudal fins of six [[Ictalurid Catfish]] species from two genera, [[Ictalurus]] and [[Schilbeodes]]. Extracts were injected into [[Gambusia Affinis]] in a laboratory bioassay, and a preliminary series jabbed [[Mollienesia Latipinna]] directly with spines. The author concludes that interspecific differences in sting toxicity probably evolved in response to environmental selection, especially [[Predation]], and that the anatomy of the [[Venom Apparatus]] does not adequately explain these differences.

## Background & Description

Ictalurid catfishes, especially the madtoms, are known for stinging, but earlier accounts relied mainly on subjective descriptions of effects on humans. The venom apparatus is made up of dorsal and pectoral stings plus, in some species, an [[Axillary Gland]]. Each sting comprises a bony spine and its integumentary sheath. The venom-producing glandular cells lie in the integumentary sheath and resemble the large mucus glands of the skin. Earlier anatomical work by [[Reed 1907]] and [[Reed 1924]] proposed that spine serrae and the axillary gland determined toxicity. Other work on [[Galeichthyes Felis]] (Halstead et al., 1953) showed that venom is released by holocrine lysis when the spine epidermis is torn.

Species-specific anatomical notes from the introduction:
- [[Schilbeodes Nocturnus]] and [[Schilbeodes Gyrinus]] have venom in ductless glands along the length of the pectoral and dorsal spines.
- A rudimentary gland occurs at the sting tip in *Ameiurus (= Ictalurus) nebulosus*.
- In *Amiurus (= Ictalurus) catus*, venom glands are limited to the distal part of the sting.

The study tested the hypothesis that more toxic venoms occur in species living where predation is most intense.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Bullheads** ([[Ictalurus Melas]], [[Ictalurus Natalis]]) occur mostly in ponds, lakes, sloughs and sluggish stream sections. These habitats are typically inhabited by large piscivorous fish such as [[Micropterus]] (black bass), which the author suggests makes bullhead venom particularly effective against fish predators.
- **Madtoms and [[Ictalurus Punctatus]]** live mainly under stones in stream riffles. The author notes that mammalian predators such as [[Procyon]] (raccoons) wade riffles to forage, so these venoms may matter more against mammals than fish.
- Bioassay fish were held in the laboratory for 1 to 3 days (Mollienesia) and up to 38 days (catfish and Gambusia). Catfish were fed earthworms or canned dog food; Gambusia were fed brine shrimp.
- Extraction and assays were run at about 21 °C; Gambusia were held at 25 ± 1 °C.

## Key Findings

### Methods overview
- **Preliminary in vivo series:** 54 Mollienesia (1–8 g; mean weights 3.1–3.4 g) were jabbed in the caudal peduncle with juvenile [[Ictalurus Melas]] stings (n = 18), [[Schilbeodes Exilis]] stings (n = 18), or a dissecting needle as a mechanical control (n = 18). Reactions were scored every 24 h for 6 days.
- **Refined in vitro series:** Stings and caudal fins from six species were ground in glass-distilled water (1 mL per gram of tissue), centrifuged, and 2 µL of supernatant was injected 4 mm into the caudal peduncle of female Gambusia (~1 g). Fish were checked at 1, 9 or 12, 24, 48, 72 and 96 h. An amphibian Ringer's solution served as a control (n = 50).
- **Scoring:** Mollienesia responses were scored on a 0–6 melanophore-expansion scale, with 6 = death. Gambusia responses were scored on a 0–5 scale, with 5 = death.
- **Sample sizes (Gambusia, sting and fin extracts):** [[Ictalurus Punctatus]] N = 60, [[Ictalurus Natalis]] N = 51, [[Ictalurus Melas]] N = 50, [[Schilbeodes Nocturnus]] N = 40, [[Schilbeodes Leptacanthus]] N = 50, [[Schilbeodes Exilis]] N = 45.

### Results from the in vivo (jabbing) experiment
- The average melanophore reaction to *I. melas* stings was more than twice that to *S. exilis* stings. This contradicts the belief that *Schilbeodes* is the more toxic genus.
- Control fish recovered partially by 96 h. Fish jabbed with stings showed continuing rises in response, attributed to deaths and secondary wound infections.

### Sting extracts (in vitro)
- **Most toxic:** Extracts from *S. exilis* and *I. melas* stings caused high mortality, all within 12 h.
- **Edema:** Seen in all *S. exilis* sting survivors and in about 50% of *I. melas* sting survivors.
- **Hemorrhaging:** About 40% of *S. exilis* survivors.
- ***I. natalis* (moderate mortality):** About 60% of survivors became edematous and about 15% showed hemorrhaging.
- ***S. nocturnus* (absent mortality):** About 60% became edematous.
- ***I. punctatus* (low mortality):** About 20% became edematous.
- ***S. leptacanthus*:** Low mortality and no edema. Hemorrhaging was in 15–20% of fish in the three least toxic bioassays.

### Caudal fin extracts (in vitro)
- The greatest response came from *I. natalis* fin extracts. Mortality was moderate, about 60% of survivors were edematous, and about 20% showed hemorrhaging.
- *I. melas* fin extracts caused moderate mortality. About 40% of survivors were edematous, with no hemorrhaging.
- Fin extracts from *I. natalis* and *I. melas* were as toxic as or more toxic than sting extracts from *I. punctatus*, *S. nocturnus* and *S. leptacanthus*.
- *S. exilis* fin extracts were not especially toxic but caused hemorrhage in about 25% of fish.
- Fin extracts from *I. punctatus*, *S. nocturnus* and *S. leptacanthus* produced no more effect than Ringer's solution. Hemorrhaging appeared in about 10% of Ringer-injected fish.

### Discussion and conclusions
- Toxic extracts caused most deaths within 12 h. Less toxic extracts produced delayed deaths, possibly from secondary complications.
- Edema usually appeared within 12–24 h and often persisted to 96 h. Severe cases led to tissue sloughing.
- Melanophore expansion, which the author links to a high [[Melanin-Dispersing Hormone]] content in catfish fins, usually faded within 48 h.
- Because caudal fin extracts showed symptoms similar to sting extracts, the author suggests that some catfish caudal fins may contain venom cells, similar to the clavate cells described by Reed (1924) in *Ameiurus nebulosus*.
- The sting extracts appear hemotoxic. The author notes that neurotoxic properties have been reported in *Plotosus anguillaris* and *Heteropneustes fossilis*.
- **Reed's conclusions are not supported:**
  - Spine serrae do not necessarily enhance venom delivery. *S. exilis* has strongly serrate pectoral spines but is among the most toxic extracts. *I. melas* has the least serrate spines among *Ictalurus* and is also highly toxic.
  - Pectoral stings are not more toxic than dorsal stings, so the axillary gland does not seem to supply the pectoral spine.
  - No venom duct was found in *Galeichthyes felis*, and water-soluble venom is unlikely to travel on the epidermal surface.
- Toxicity is probably determined by the number of functional venom cells, the venom components, or both. Spine serrae may augment toxicity, but this needs confirmation.
- The author concludes that the catfish sting is probably adaptive and linked to predation pressure. The effectiveness of the sting as a predator deterrent was still under investigation.
