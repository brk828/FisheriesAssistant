# Southwest Center for Biological Diversity Letter Regarding Wellton-Mohawk Gila River Flood Channel Restoration Project

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #native-fish #riparian-ecology #environmental-law

## Executive Summary

This October 1994 correspondence from the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]] (SWCBD) to the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] challenges the proposed "Finding of No Significant Impact" (FONSI) for the [[Wellton-Mohawk Gila River Flood Channel Restoration Project]]. The organization argues that channelizing 56.2 miles of the [[Gila River]] would violate the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] (NEPA) and foreclose recovery options for Arizona's imperiled native fish fauna. The letter documents the critical status of Arizona's native fish species and contends that the $13.7 million project represents poor use of taxpayer funds compared to alternative flood management approaches. A supplementary fax discusses related litigation concerning the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) and biological opinions.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila River]] in Arizona represents one of the few remaining Southwestern river systems with rehabilitation potential. The proposed project would channelize 56.2 miles of river between [[Wellton]] and [[Mohawk]], moving 5.7 million cubic yards of material and placing 1 million yards of rip-rap along dike interiors. According to the [[Arizona Department of Environmental Quality]], this would be the largest project in terms of area of impact for any 404 Permit in Arizona, with an estimated cost of $13.7 million.

Arizona's native fish fauna has experienced catastrophic decline. Of 34 original native fish species: one ([[Monkey Springs Pupfish]]) is extinct, one ([[Yaqui Sucker]]) has been extirpated, 12 are federally endangered, 6 are federally threatened, and 11 are federal candidates for protection. Only the [[Bluehead Sucker]] has not been recommended for federal protection. The State of Arizona recognizes 17 species as endangered, 7 as threatened, and 2 as state candidates.

Ninety-five percent of Arizona's rivers have been destroyed by dams, agricultural conversion, water diversion projects, urban encroachment, cattle grazing, and excessive groundwater pumping. The [[Wellton-Mohawk Irrigation District]] has operated since the 1940s with controversial taxpayer subsidies including expensive drainage systems, artificially low electricity rates, commodity price supports, and a non-functional $258 million desalinization plant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Southwestern rivers like the [[Gila River]] are characterized by late winter and early spring flooding. Spring flooding is especially important for survival of mature willow-cottonwood forests. The 1993 floods successfully reestablished cottonwoods and willows, demonstrating the area's rehabilitation potential.

Natural processes in Southwestern rivers include flooding, erosion, scouring, and siltation. These create meandering channels that change dynamically over time. Oxbow formation and decline are part of this natural dynamic process, often developing into successional marshes that fill and degrade as river channels move.

Episodic disturbance is the primary characteristic favoring native species. Channelization and continued operation of [[Painted Rock Dam]] for the singular benefit of the [[Wellton-Mohawk Irrigation District]] would foreclose recovery options for the [[Lower Gila River]] permanently.

The Draft Mitigation Plan estimates "Acquisition and Dedication of Lands" at $300/acre. By the Corps' and District's own estimates, 45,666.67 acres of land could be purchased for flood easement at the project's $13.7 million cost. Purchase of flood-prone land for adequate easement would yield better public benefits without foreclosing future recovery options.

## Key Findings

**Legal and Policy Violations**: The proposed FONSI would violate NEPA requirements (42 USC 4332) mandating detailed environmental impact statements for major federal actions significantly affecting environmental quality. The finding would also violate NEPA implementing regulations (40 C.F.R. 1508.27) requiring analysis of significance in multiple contexts, consideration of wetlands and ecologically critical areas, evaluation of controversial effects, precedent-setting implications, and cumulative impacts.

**Conservation Status**: Arizona native fish represent living barometers of river condition. The critical status across nearly all 34 original native species reflects the 95% destruction rate of Arizona's rivers. Rehabilitation and protection of river habitat is critical at this historical juncture for native fish species survival and ultimately for the rivers themselves.

**Project Scale and Impact**: The project would be the largest in terms of area of impact for any 404 Permit in Arizona, channelizing 56.2 miles of river and moving 5.7 million cubic yards of material.

**Economic Analysis**: The project represents poor value compared to alternatives. The same $13.7 million could purchase over 45,000 acres of flood easement at the project's own estimated land values, providing better public benefits without environmental damage.

**Related Litigation**: A supplementary October 25, 1994 fax documents a pre-litigation meeting regarding the [[Central Arizona Project]] biological opinion. Officials from [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and [[Central Arizona Water Conservation District]] attended. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] could not refute concerns raised in a July 30, 1994 CAP notice letter. Technical feasibility of alternatives like ozonation was acknowledged; cost rather than biology drove decisions. [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] admitted considering money instead of biology in their biological opinion, repeating patterns from the [[Mt. Graham]] project.

**Legal Action**: SWCBD indicated intent to seek injunctional judicial relief if the Corps concludes a FONSI, requesting immediate notification before any irretrievable commitment of resources occurs.
