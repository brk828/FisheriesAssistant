# Disease Survey of the Colorado River Fishes (1980)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 report summarizes a disease survey of endangered and endemic fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]], conducted by the [[Fish Disease Control Center]] at Fort Morgan, Colorado, in cooperation with the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] and the [[Fish Disease Control Center]] partners. The survey examined 28 endangered fish and 97 exotic and other endemic fish specimens, using field collections, bacteriology, virology, and parasitological examination. The authors concluded that exotic-species diseases were unlikely to drive the decline of endangered fishes, that no virus was found in the endangered species, and that disease management will matter most for hatchery culture.

## Background & Description

The project aimed to:

- Assess whether disease agents have adversely affected populations of endangered fishes.
- Determine whether disease agents associated with exotic fishes have harmed endangered species.
- Identify potential disease problems for [[Hatchery Propagation]] of endangered fishes.

Endangered species examined included the [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). Exotic and other endemic fishes included [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*) and additional native species. Fish came from wild populations and from hatcheries, including [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].

### Methods

- **Sample collection:** Sample kits went to all field crews. Dead fish during capture or holding were chilled and shipped for analysis; formalin-fixed whole fish were also submitted. Field personnel made on-site collections of exotic and endemic species.
- **Virology:** Samples were inoculated onto cell lines considered phylogenetically closest to the host, following [[American Fisheries Society]] Fish Health Section procedures.
- **Bacteriology:** Samples from kidney and lesions were streaked onto Tryptic Soy Agar. Isolates were identified by morphological and biochemical characteristics. Kidney and spleen smears were Gram stained.
- **Parasitology:** Skin scrapings, gill arches, kidney tissue, and intestines were examined grossly and microscopically.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors noted that many bacteria and protozoans found here are common in aquatic environments and are often commensal. Disease risk appears tied to host stress and water quality. Potential stressors include low water temperatures and water quality problems in hatcheries. The report also points to [[Saprolegnia]] infections linked to handling injuries and poor water conditions.

Parasite host specificity appears high. Exotic-associated agents were largely host-specific and did not show a clear path to endemic species. The authors stressed that the biology of each pathogen must be understood before predicting its effect on wild populations.

## Key Findings

### Pathogens of Endangered Fishes
- **Virus:** None detected in the endangered species.
- **Bacteria:** Several isolates were recovered, all considered widespread in aquatic environments. [[Saprolegnia]] (a fungus) occurred on several specimens.
- **Protozoans:** An undescribed [[Myxobolus]]-like myxosporean was found in the liver, spleen, kidney, and skin of all endangered species (identification by Hoffman, personal communication). An undescribed microsporidian and an undescribed *Chilodonella*-like ciliate also occurred. Other protozoans included *Trichodina* sp., *Trichophrya* sp., *Ambiphrya*, and *Chilodonella* sp.
- **Trematodes:** Few were found. *Neascus* was recovered from two *P. lucius* specimens, and *Ornithodiplostomum* was found in one species.
- **Cestodes:** The tapeworm *Ophiotaenia* sp. was found in six *P. lucius* specimens and was sometimes abundant in the intestine. No cestodes were found in other endangered species.
- **Crustaceans:** [[Lernaea cyprinacea]] (anchor worm) was frequently found on all endangered species.

### Pathogens of Exotic and Other Endemic Fishes
- Exotic and endemic fishes had a greater variety of disease agents than the endangered fishes, possibly because more specimens (97) were examined.
- One virus, a [[Lymphocystis]]-type virus, was found in one exotic species. It is neither highly pathogenic nor highly contagious.
- Bacteria were similar to those in endangered species. [[Aeromonas]] and [[Pseudomonas]] were frequently found in nearly all fish and are often part of the normal flora. They cause disease mainly when stress shifts the host-pathogen balance.
- Protozoan findings included the same undescribed myxosporean in *G. robusta*, *G. cypha*, and *G. elegans*.
- Larval trematodes (*Neascus*, black and yellow grubs) were found in five fish species. Larval [[Ligula]] was found in smallmouth bass and another species. Unsegmented cestodes (Caryophyllaeidae) were found in *G. cypha*.
- Nematodes were found in several species, and *Lernaea cyprinacea* was common across many fish species.

### Health Status and Reproduction
- Endemic fishes were relatively healthy. The authors concluded that detecting a virus does not necessarily mean disease, because most populations are not in an epizootic.
- Reproductive condition was assessed on a few occasions. One *P. lucius* (CHN: 80-193) had an immature ovary with degeneration, and another (Tag #1165) was at an early stage of development and would not spawn until 1981. A sex determination request on another *P. lucius* identified it as male.

### Disease Implications for Cultured Endangered Fishes
- Extrapolating feral disease data to hatcheries is difficult. Culture practices and management quality will affect survival more than infectious agents.
- Pathogens such as aeromonads and [[Flavobacterium]]-type bacteria are likely to limit rearing success in hatcheries.
- Many external protozoans, especially ciliates, are already associated with hatchery fishes.

### Recommendations
- Disease agents from exotic species are unlikely to be involved in the decline of endangered fishes.
- Sampling resources should focus on endemic fishes only, and a smaller number of specimens should be used for each species.
- Previously unknown pathogens appear to occur only in endangered fishes, so the report calls for careful handling of rare specimens.
- On-site sampling is expensive but useful. Field-collected samples plus laboratory examination of fixed specimens should continue.
- Greater attention to hatchery diagnosis, environmental management, and consultation with fish health specialists would improve hatchery practices and culture success.
