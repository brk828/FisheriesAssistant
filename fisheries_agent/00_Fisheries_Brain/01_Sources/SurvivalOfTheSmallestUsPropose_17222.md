# Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy Owl Critical Habitat Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This newspaper article reports a federal proposal to list the [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy Owl]] as endangered and to designate its [[Critical Habitat]] in Arizona. The owl depends on riparian vegetation that has been lost to groundwater pumping, dam operation, and livestock grazing. A petition by a conservation group led to lawsuits that forced the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] to act. The document is a news report rather than a research paper, so it provides qualitative observations from scientists and officials rather than quantitative population data.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Cactus Ferruginous Pygmy Owl]] is described as no more than 7 inches long.
- **Nesting:** It nests in abandoned woodpecker holes in riverside trees or in nearby [[Saguaro Cactus]].
- **Activity:** Unusually for an owl, it hunts in daylight, mainly in early mornings and late afternoons.
- **Status:** Described by Galvin as the rarest raptor in Arizona, even more endangered than the [[Mexican Spotted Owl]].
- **Habitat requirements:** Survival is tied to clumps of old-growth [[Mesquite]] trees or [[Cottonwood and Willow Riparian Forest]]. These trees once lined most Central and Southern Arizona waterways.
- **Historical range:** Tucson was once known for the species. Old mesquite woodlands near downtown had trees 50 to 60 feet high that were prime habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Vegetation loss:** Robert Ohmart (Arizona State University) attributed riparian plant loss to heavy groundwater pumping, which dried many streams and washes. Dams curtailed or altered water flows, and hungry, thirsty cattle ate and trampled riverbank vegetation.
- **Ecosystem role:** Marshall stated the owl is a voracious predator that helps keep numbers of other species in check.
- **Riparian indicator value:** Galvin noted that the owl is one of dozens of riparian-dependent species in the Southwest facing extinction. Other rare birds, fish and mammals also depend on the vegetation that critical habitat would protect.
- **Recovery potential:** Galvin said riparian ecosystems recover quickly if given the chance, and the group wants efforts to restore the damaged waterways of Central and Southern Arizona.
- **Geographic range:** Populations also occur along the southern tip of the Rio Grande in Texas and into northern Mexico.

## Key Findings

- **Listing proposal:** The Fish and Wildlife Service proposed endangered listing and critical habitat for the owl. The proposal opened a public-comment period lasting until March 12, 1995.
- **Decision deadline:** The agency has until December 12, 1995, to list the owl as endangered and protect its critical habitat, or to decide against those actions.
- **Petition and litigation:** Galvin's group, the [[Greater Gila Biodiversity Project]], petitioned for protection in May 1992. Two lawsuits filed in U.S. District Court in Phoenix forced the agency to act.
- **Legal effects of listing:** Endangered status would prohibit killing or harassing the owl, or harming its eggs or nests.
- **Permit effects of critical habitat:** Anyone seeking a federal permit, or using federal funds to disturb river-area vegetation in the designated area, would need to notify Fish and Wildlife. The agency would either deny the permit or activity or require mitigation.
- **Texas differentiation:** For the Rio Grande population in Texas, where ranchers have protected habitat, the agency proposed a less-restrictive threatened status.
- **Conservation concern:** Yetman, of the [[Tucson Audubon Society]], said the owl is so close to extinction that sightings are kept secret. He also said the odds for recovery are not as good as hoped.
- **Dissenting view on timing:** Some observers feared the proposal might be too late to save the owl.
