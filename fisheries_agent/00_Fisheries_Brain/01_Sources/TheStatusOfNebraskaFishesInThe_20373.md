# Status of Seven Native Missouri River Cyprinids in Nebraska

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Nebraska Game and Parks Commission report (Hesse, 1993), prepared under the Federal Aid in Sport Fish Restoration (Dingell-Johnson) program, documents the decline of seven native cyprinids in the [[Missouri River]] in Nebraska. Based on seine sampling from 1970 to 1993, the authors report reductions in abundance of 70 to 98% for these species. Several have been extirpated from the reach upstream of [[Gavins Point Dam]]. The report attributes the decline mainly to altered sediment dynamics from dam construction, elimination of bankfull (channel-forming) discharge, and loss of critical habitats such as sandbars and off-channel areas.

The species are [[Sicklefin Chub]], [[Sturgeon Chub]], [[Flathead Chub]], [[Silver Chub]], [[Speckled Chub]], [[Plains Minnow]] and [[Western Silvery Minnow]]. The report calls for all seven to be listed as endangered in Nebraska and recommends restoring a flood-pulse regime, a floodplain corridor below [[Fort Randall Dam]], and thermal mixing of dam discharge.

## Background & Description

The minnow family (Cyprinidae) is large in the Missouri River Basin, with more than 40 native species, more than 30 of them native to Nebraska. At least 21 are associated with the Missouri River main channel on Nebraska's border. Native Nebraska minnows were historically more numerous in streams than in lakes. Minnows occupy both carnivorous and herbivorous niches and move energy from primary producers up the food chain in the river ecosystem.

The seven focal species share traits adapted to large, turbid, silt-laden rivers:

- **Sicklefin chub** has eyes partly covered by skin to limit abrasion, taste organs on the head to sort food from bottom sediments, and a preference for sandy bottoms in moderate current. It likely feeds on aquatic insect larvae and detritus. Spawning is thought to occur in spring.
- **Sturgeon chub** has a depressed head, slender tapering body, and epidermal keels on scales that reduce flow resistance and help it hold to the bottom. It is mostly carnivorous, favors gravel in strong current, and begins spawning in early June (Powder River, Wyoming).
- **Flathead chub** is long-lived, matures by age 2 to 4, and survives to at least age 10. Spawning in Iowa and Montana took place from mid-July to mid-August at 18 to 25 °C. Reported mature egg counts were 491 per female (Montana) and 4,974 total eggs per female (Iowa). It feeds mostly on drifting terrestrial insects.
- **Silver chub** has better-developed sight than the sturgeon and sicklefin chubs, feeds mainly on zooplankton and insect larvae, and is most common in semi-turbid rivers over sand and gravel. Spawning is reported in April and May.
- **Speckled chub** relies on external olfactory senses for feeding. It eats insect larvae, crustaceans and plant material, spawns from early May to late August, and is very short-lived (under 2 years).
- **Plains minnow** has a long, coiled intestine that indicates an herbivorous diet. It has a thin-lipped, crescent-shaped mouth for scraping the plant layer at the water-substrate interface. It is short-lived, usually under 2 years, and spawns during high or receding flows.
- **Western silvery minnow** is more common in backwaters and protected low-current silty areas. It consumes decaying plant material, diatoms, algae and fungi.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Historically, these fishes were distributed across the Missouri River mainstem and its tributaries, with ranges extending into the Mississippi and beyond in some cases. The sicklefin and sturgeon chubs did not ascend the Ohio or Mississippi above the Missouri confluence. Their ranges have become disjointed because of dam construction.

Key habitat drivers identified in the text include:

- **Turbidity:** Several authors noted that these species depend on cloudy water. Reduced turbidity after impoundment is a concern, and the report argues that turbidity provides in-stream cover against predation.
- **Sediment and channel dynamics:** Channel control structures and the loss of sandbars, backwaters and snags reduced habitat availability. Flows are also a cue for reproduction; [[Plains Minnow]] spawning was correlated with abrupt spring discharge increases.
- **Dam effects:** Dams near the study reaches reduced flood frequency and altered water temperature and photoperiod, which the report links to disrupted native reproduction.
- **Prey base:** Silver chub decline has been linked to loss of [[Hexagenia]] mayflies, an important part of its diet, in the Lake Erie basin.

The text also reports that the demise of these species likely contributed to declines in sauger, catfish, burbot and sturgeon, all of which were adapted to the same large, warm, turbid river conditions.

## Key Findings

**Methods and effort**
- More than 45,500 small fish were seined from the Missouri River adjacent to Nebraska between 1970 and 1993, requiring 1,648 seine hauls.
- Sampling used tarred nylon seines (15.2 m × 1.8 m, 6.1 mm mesh) with [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (CPUE) as the main metric. Effort was hard to standardize because of variable discharge, soft sediments and snags.
- Table 1 totals by reach were: upper unchannelized 10,593 fish (657 hauls, CPUE 16.1); lower unchannelized 8,902 fish (359 hauls, CPUE 24.8); channelized 26,063 fish (632 hauls, CPUE 41.2). The channelized reach had the highest CPUE, which the report attributes to tributary sediment inputs below Sioux City, Iowa.

**Population declines**
- Relative abundance declines were: flathead chub down 98%, silver chub down 70%, speckled chub down 77%, and plains/silvery minnows down 96%.
- In the upper unchannelized reach, total CPUE in 1983-93 was down 75% from 56.0 fish per haul in 1976 (Kallemeyn and Novotny, 1977).
- In the lower unchannelized reach, 1976 CPUE was 78.9, which the report states was 69% higher than the 1983-93 value.
- Fisher (1962), sampling Missouri in 1945 with a seine one-quarter the length of the study seines, captured 4,483 fish in 46 hauls. Plains and silvery minnows made up 68.0%, flathead chub 20.4%, silver chub 1.0%, and speckled chub under 0.1%. The author concluded that losses by 1970 were "quite dramatic" compared to these data.

**Distribution and extirpation**
- Sicklefin, sturgeon, flathead and speckled chubs appear to have been extirpated from the Missouri upstream of [[Lewis and Clark Lake]]. The abstract places this loss upstream of Gavins Point Dam.
- Silver, plains and silvery minnows were present only at very low levels (each under 1% of catch) in the upstream reach.
- One sicklefin and one sturgeon chub were collected in southeast Nebraska in 1988, the only specimens of those species recorded in the study.

**Other Nebraska streams**
- The Nebraska Department of Environmental Quality collected over 70,000 small fish at 350 stream sites (excluding the Missouri) in 1984-88. No sturgeon or sicklefin chubs were found. Flathead chubs (396 specimens) made up 0.6% of the catch and occurred in 8.8% of streams.
- In the lower [[Platte River]], [[Peters]] et al. (1989) sampled 874 electrofishing grids. Flathead chubs appeared in only 4% of grids, and plains minnows made up 473 specimens (9% of grids).

**Lower Niobrara River**
- [[Primacord Sampling]] in 1976-78 collected 3,083 flathead chubs (15.3% composition). The 1991 repeat collected 104 (12.5%).
- Flathead chub catch fell from 31 fish per blast in 1976-78 to 5 per blast in 1991. Table 4 gives 30.73 versus 5.00 fish per 15.2 m of primacord, and no speckled chubs were collected in either year.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- All seven species should be listed as endangered in Nebraska, and their status should be assessed quickly across the rest of their range.
- The sicklefin and sturgeon chubs face a very real likelihood of extinction. Both are candidate endangered species in federal status reports (Werdon, 1993a, 1993b).
- Recommended actions:
  - Provide a small floodplain corridor from [[Fort Randall Dam]] downstream, flooded annually in historic frequency and timing to renew off-channel habitat and sandbars.
  - Design sediment transport systems to restore balance downstream of Fort Randall and Gavins Point dams.
  - Mix dam discharge from surface to bottom to reduce cold, altered photoperiod effects on reproduction.
  - Restore the spring and early summer [[Flood Pulse]], which triggered annual reproduction in native Missouri River fishes.
