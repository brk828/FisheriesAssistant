# Construction and Operating Efficiency of Intermountain Area Stream Habitat Improvement Structures

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #freshwater-ecology #stream-habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

This report, authored by a Regional Aquatic Ecologist with the U.S. Forest Service Intermountain Region, synthesizes over 40 years of experience (1930s–1980) constructing and evaluating instream habitat improvement structures on federal (Forest Service and Bureau of Land Management) lands across Utah, Nevada, Wyoming, and New Mexico. Four major structure types — (1) single and multiple log, (2) gabion, (3) trash catcher, and (4) rock-boulder — were built to create plunge pools, reduce streambank erosion, and enhance habitat for native coldwater fisheries, particularly rare cutthroat trout subspecies ([[Bonneville Cutthroat Trout]], [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]], [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]]). The **single and multiple log plunge pool structure** emerged as the most durable, cost-effective, and operationally efficient design across a wide range of stream gradients (up to 20%), significantly outperforming gabion, trash catcher, and rock-boulder alternatives, especially during drought and high-discharge events. Proper hydrologic planning, construction technique, and ongoing maintenance were identified as critical to long-term structure performance.

**Log structure construction details:**
- Log species used: aspen, pinyon-juniper, spruce-fir
- Log diameter: 9–15 inches
- Bank burial length: 4–6 feet on each side of streambank (minimum)
- Notch dimensions cut into logs for concentrated overflow: 12–18 inches wide, 2–4 inches deep
- Low-gradient placement (0.5–2%): log caulked into streambed with natural vegetative/rock/soil debris
- High-gradient placement (>2%): woven wire mesh/hardware cloth or plastic sheeting used beneath streambed material to prevent undercutting
- Construction timed to summer low-flow conditions to ensure continuous bank-to-bank wetting, preventing alternating wet/dry decay cycles

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report centers on three rare, native coldwater cutthroat trout subspecies inhabiting the Intermountain Region's desert and mountain stream systems:

- **[[Bonneville Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Salmo clarki utah*) — endemic to the Bonneville Basin, Utah
- **[[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Salmo clarki pleuriticus*) — native to Colorado River drainage headwaters
- **[[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Salmo clarki henshawi*) — native to the Lahontan Basin, Nevada/Utah border region

**Habitat context:**
- These populations persist at low densities in harsh, arid mountain desert stream environments (e.g., [[Deep Creek Mountains]], Juab County, Utah), where habitat quality is limited primarily by **summer low flows** and **poor pool habitat** (insufficient size/depth of existing pools).
- Streams supporting these fish are small (often <7 miles long), with extreme hydrologic variability: spring runoff discharges >120 cfs and summer low flows as low as 0.24 cfs.
- [[Riparian Habitat]] and streambank vegetation recovery are closely tied to structure success; continued livestock grazing in improvement areas undermines structure integrity and habitat recovery via bank trampling, channel cutting, and sedimentation.
- Structures are noted to have "established better quality and more frequent pools for the rare cutthroat trout species living under harsh desert stream environments," directly linking instream engineering to population persistence.

## Key Findings

- **Operational hierarchy of structure efficiency (best to worst):** single/multiple log > gabion > trash catcher > rock-boulder.
- **Single log structures functioned across the widest range of conditions** — gradients from 0.5% to 20%, discharges from 0.1 to 200 cfs — and were the *only* structure type that remained functionally operative (as plunge-pool overflows rather than fish-passage barriers) during the severe **1977 drought**.
- Historic CCC-era log crib structures built in **1934–35** (some weighing up to 50 tons, rock-filled crib design) remained operational nearly 50 years later, demonstrating exceptional longevity when properly constructed and maintained.
- Evaluation of 20+ log crib structures on the **Bridger-Teton National Forest, Wyoming** (built 1935) found several still operational in 1980; unmaintained structures had largely degraded but retained structural remnants.
- Evaluation of 110 single log structures in the **Deep Creek Mountains, Utah** (BLM, 1979–1980) showed minimal maintenance need after 1–3 years in place, across gradients up to 20% and elevations of 6,300–8,600 ft.
- **Trash catchers proved the least suitable for high-gradient mountain streams** (>5% gradient); over 200 were removed in cooperative BLM/FS projects in south-central Utah due to failure from bedload sediment, debris, and storm discharge — some rebuilt and removed twice.
- **Gabions were the most expensive** structure type ($15–$150 per structure range, gabions at high end) but produced high-quality deep pools (4–8 ft) when sited correctly.
- **Continuous wetting is critical to log longevity** — structures subject to alternating wet/dry cycles from erratic discharge deteriorate rapidly; construction during summer low flow (ensuring bank-to-bank overflow) mitigates this.
- **Continued livestock grazing in stream improvement zones actively undermines structure performance**, causing bank erosion, channel cutting, and eventual washout during spring floods — highlighting a land-use management prerequisite for successful [[Stream Habitat Improvement]].
- A standardized BLM monitoring form (U.S.O. 6671-6) was developed to track physical pool/streambottom parameters pre- and post-construction, paired with photo-point monitoring and fisheries data collection.
- Cost-efficiency analysis favored structures built by trained agency crews with direct supervision over contractor-built structures, which had the highest cost and lowest operational efficiency due to placement/construction errors.
