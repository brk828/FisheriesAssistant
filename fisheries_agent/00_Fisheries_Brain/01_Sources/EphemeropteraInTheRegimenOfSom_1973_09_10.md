## Executive Summary

This paper is an ethno-entomological and cultural study rather than a strict taxonomic or ecological survey. It documents two independent human relationships with mayflies (order [[Ephemeroptera]]): (1) the consumption of a large, densely swarming mayfly species (tentatively assigned to the genus *Plethogenesia*, family [[Palingeniidae]]) by river-dwelling peoples along the Sepik River in Papua New Guinea, based on a 1930s eyewitness account by patrol officer Jim Taylor; and (2) the cultural and folkloric significance of the large European mayfly *[[Palingenia longicauda]]* in Hungary, where its mass synchronous emergence over the River Tisza inspired two folksongs using the insect's ephemeral adult life as a metaphor for fleeting human love. The paper situates these mayflies within a broader context of entomophagy in Papua New Guinea and animal symbolism in Hungarian folk music, while providing scattered morphological and phenological data on *P. longicauda*.

## Background & Description

- **Palingenia longicauda** (family Palingeniidae, described by Olivier 1791, placed in genus *Palingenia* by Burmeister 1839):
  - Adult body length: 25–38 mm
  - Cerci (tail filaments) length: 7–8 cm
  - Total length (body + cerci) can approach **~12 cm** in some individuals — among the largest mayflies in Europe.
  - Adults are described as "pale gold" in colour during mass swarms, "reddening" where swarm concentration is greatest.
  - Larvae (naiads) are **saprophytic/detritivorous** and burrow into holes in muddy riverbanks — a fossorial, tunnel-dwelling life history strategy typical of Palingeniidae.

- **Sepik River mayfly** (New Guinea, tentatively *Plethogenesia* sp., Palingeniidae, per suggestion of W. L. Peters, following [[Tshernova's 1970 Classification]] within superfamily Ephemeroidea, suborder Plectoptera):
  - No specific name confirmed; described only from field observation.
  - Colour: pale gold, reddening at points of highest swarm density (visually similar to *P. longicauda*).
  - Swarms observed covering "several hectares" of river surface, resembling "a field of ripening corn" when viewed on land.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Both species are associated with large, slow-flowing lowland rivers — the River Tisza (Hungary's second-largest river) for *P. longicauda*, and the Sepik River (near Ambunti, northeastern New Guinea) for the unidentified Palingeniidae species.
- **Larval ecology**: *P. longicauda* naiads are burrowers in river mudbanks, feeding saprophytically — an ecological role tied closely to sediment quality and stable, undisturbed riverbank habitat, making the species sensitive to river regulation and pollution (implied vulnerability, though not explicitly discussed in the source).
- **Mass synchronous emergence ("explosive hatch")**: Both taxa exhibit the classic Palingeniidae life-history trait of highly synchronized, spectacular mass emergences:
  - *P. longicauda* emerges over the Tisza in **late spring to early summer, mainly between 9–20 June**, with adults appearing in such enormous numbers ("hundreds of thousands") that they form thick surface layers on the river — locally termed **"tiszavirág" (Flowers of the Tisza)**.
  - The Sepik species was observed swarming in **September–October 1931**, covering "several hectares" of river surface for an extended stretch (~2000 m) near Ambunti village.
- **Adult life span**: Extremely short (classic ephemeropteran "one-day" adult life), reinforcing the folkloric use of mayflies as a symbol of transience.
- **Biogeography**: *P. longicauda* is recorded from Hungary, Germany, France (Walker 1853), Czechoslovakia (Obenberger 1959), and the Danube in Bulgaria (Russev 1973); it appears most abundant in the River Tisza.
- **Trophic/cultural interaction with humans**: Both mayfly populations were historically harvested by local riverine peoples as a food source — an example of **entomophagy** tied directly to the predictable, high-biomass timing of mass emergence events. The Sepik mayfly was collected by dragging containers along the water surface through swarm concentrations and consumed (e.g., cooked in batter, "as if it were whitebait-fish fry"). This places mayfly emergence within a broader regional pattern of insect-based food supplementation in Papua New Guinea, alongside crickets, stick insects, shield bugs, palm weevil larvae, and various scarab beetles (e.g., *Lepidiota vogeli*, *Oryctes centaurus*, *Scapanes australis*).

## Key Findings

- **First documented eyewitness account (1931, Sepik River)** of mass mayfly swarming and human consumption in Papua New Guinea, reported second-hand via patrol officer Jim Taylor to entomologist J. J. H. Szent-Ivany, decades after the observation (letter dated 17 March 1973).
- The Sepik mayfly could not be identified to species; W. L. Peters proposed a probable placement in genus **Plethogenesia** (family Palingeniidae), based on swarm description and ecological parallels with *P. longicauda*.
- *Palingenia longicauda* mass emergences over the River Tisza are a well-documented, culturally embedded natural phenomenon in Hungary, referenced in at least two folksongs that use the mayfly's brief adult life as a poetic metaphor for the transience of romantic love.
- The vernacular Hungarian term **"tiszavirág"** (Tisza-flower) specifically denotes this mass emergence event, while **"kérész"** is the general Hungarian term for Ephemeroptera as a whole.
- The paper draws a direct ethnological parallel between two geographically and culturally disparate human populations (Papua New Guinean river communities and Hungarian rural/folk communities) independently recognizing and responding to the same core biological phenomenon: **synchronized mass mayfly emergence** — one primarily as a nutritional resource, the other as poetic/artistic inspiration.
- Broader context: entomophagy in Papua New Guinea extends well beyond mayflies to include large numbers of beetle, orthopteran, hemipteran, and lepidopteran species, several of which (e.g., *Lepidiota vogeli*) are considered local delicacies and are seasonally harvested during predictable mass-swarming events, mirroring the phenological predictability exploited in mayfly harvesting.
- No quantitative population, water chemistry, or conservation status data are provided for either mayfly population in this source; the paper is primarily ethnographic/folkloric in scope rather than a formal ecological study.
