# Patterns of Association with Host and Habitat: Antibody Reactive with Sin Nombre Virus in Small Mammals of the Major Biotic Communities of the Southwestern United States

#mammalogy #epidemiology #muridae

## Executive Summary

This study surveyed small mammals for antibody reactive with [[Sin Nombre Virus]] (SNV), the agent of [[Hantavirus Pulmonary Syndrome]] (HPS), across nine biotic communities in Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and Utah during May through September 1994. Of 3,069 small mammals representing 69 species, antibody was found in rodents from all communities (overall prevalence 6.3%). Infection was concentrated in the [[Deer Mouse]] (the primary SNV reservoir), the [[Brush Mouse]], and the [[Western Harvest Mouse]], and was higher in males and heavier (presumably older) animals. The authors conclude that horizontal transmission among adult males is likely important, that maternal antibody may explain low infection in young deer mice, and that infection is spatially focal rather than uniform.

## Background & Description

HPS was first recognized after an outbreak of acute respiratory distress in the Four Corners area in summer 1993. The disease is caused by SNV, a member of the genus Hantavirus (family Bunyaviridae). By the time of writing, more than 140 cases with roughly 50% mortality had been recorded in 25 US states, and HPS had also been confirmed in Canada, Argentina, Brazil, and Paraguay (the etiologic agent differs in some of these areas).

Hantaviruses generally establish chronic, persistent infection in a single primary host, shedding virus in saliva, feces, and urine. In deer mice, antibody in blood correlated closely with viral RNA in lung tissue detected by [[Polymerase Chain Reaction]]. Other rodents, including the cotton rat, white-footed mouse, rice rat, and western harvest mouse, host other North American hantaviruses, and SNV appears to be the principal cause of HPS.

The study had three aims: (1) identify small-mammal species infected with SNV or related viruses; (2) compare community structure among major biotic communities; and (3) quantify variation in infection among rodent populations across habitats in a defined region. The Four Corners region was chosen because it was the site of the initial outbreak, accounted for 53% of US cases, had high rodent infection prevalence, and contained a close juxtaposition of most major North American biotic communities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study design.** Nine biotic communities were identified from vegetation maps and replicated across the four states: [[Great Basin Desert]] scrub, [[Chaparral]], [[Pinyon-Juniper Woodland]], [[Montane Forest]], [[Mountain Meadow]], [[Grassland]], [[Sub-Alpine Zone]], [[Alpine Tundra]], and desert. Sites visited per state were Arizona (13), Colorado (12), New Mexico (21), and Utah (13), with two pinyon-juniper sites per state. Each site received a 24-day trapping session, with a goal of at least 50 rodents per site. Traps were set in trap lines (approximately 200 Sherman and 40 Tomahawk traps per night), and cotton batting was added at high altitude. Total effort was 42,831 trap nights, for an overall trap success of 7.2%.

**Community structure.** The [[Deer Mouse]] was the most common species (31% of captures), followed by the brush mouse (12%) and the white-throated woodrat (7%). The genus *Peromyscus* made up 48% of captures. Community composition varied by habitat: heteromyid rodents (pocket mice and kangaroo rats) dominated desert (62% of captures); deer mice were a dominant member of most other biomes; brush mice made up 61% of chaparral captures; and sciurids (chipmunks) co-dominated montane, subalpine, and alpine zones. Middle-altitude zones were dominated by murids (80–95% of captures in chaparral, Great Basin scrub, and pinyon-juniper).

**Laboratory methods.** Blood was tested by [[Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay]] against recombinant SNV nucleocapsid antigen and a control antigen. Sera were positive if the titer was 1:400 or the summed adjusted optical density was ≥0.95. The assay could detect but not distinguish infections by Prospect Hill-like viruses (arvicoline rodents) and [[El Moro Canyon Virus]] (western harvest mouse). Statistical analyses included exact binomial tests for focality and simple linear regression for density effects.

**Safety.** Field teams used respirators, gowns, gloves, and shoe covers, and decontaminated equipment and traps after necropsy.

## Key Findings

**Prevalence by species** (positive/tested):
- Deer mouse: 11% (99/928), the primary reservoir.
- Brush mouse: 20% (of 355); 25% in chaparral and montane forest.
- Western harvest mouse: 23% (8/35); all eight positives were male.
- Mexican vole: 12% (3/24); all three positives were male.
- Pinyon mouse (*P. truei*): 3% (3/98), versus 20% reported in a previous study.
- Heteromyids: 0 of 533 positive apart from two pocket mice (*Perognathus fasciatus*, a sample of 2) and one *P. parvus*. Sciurids: 0 of 449 positive.

**Biome patterns.** Overall prevalence was lowest at the extremes: desert (0.4%) and alpine tundra (2.0%). Deer mouse prevalence was 0% in desert, 4% in chaparral, and 17% in pinyon-juniper. Infected deer mice were found in every habitat except desert.

**Geographic focality.** Seropositive deer mice occurred at 21 of 41 sites, with prevalence from 0% to 50%. Two sites had significantly more infected deer mice than expected (17 of 41 and 8 of 17) and three had significantly fewer (0 of 34, 0 of 26, and 5 of 102). Brush mice were infected at nine of 17 sites, with prevalence up to 35%. Infection was absent from some local populations while reaching 35–47% in others, suggesting focality at fine spatial scales.

**Density.** Prevalence was not related to deer mouse density (adjusted trap success 0–23%; R² = 0.2%, P = 0.85). The same was true for brush mice (P = 0.51).

**Sex bias.** Infection was strongly male-biased:
- Deer mice: 6% of 394 females versus 14% of 524 males (P = 0.0003).
- Brush mice: 5% of 165 females versus 34% of 190 males (P < 0.0001).

**Age and mass.** Prevalence increased with body mass class:
- Deer mice: 4% in the smallest class versus 17.4% in the heaviest.
- Within the smallest deer mouse class, prevalence was 14% in the lightest subclass, 4% in the middle, and 1% in the largest.
- All infected harvest mice were in the two heaviest classes.

**Antibody titers.** Deer mice and brush mice had the highest endpoint titers. In the two heavier deer mouse mass classes, the geometric mean titer exceeded 3,500, and 71% of titers in the heaviest classes were ≥6,400. The smallest class showed a bimodal titer distribution.

**Comparison with earlier work.** Deer mouse prevalence (11%) was lower than the 30% reported in a previous Southwest study, which was conducted during an HPS outbreak. The authors suggest that the earlier study reflected unusually high rodent densities. Earlier high prevalence in chipmunks and pinyon mice was not reproduced, which may indicate spillover into non-reservoir species only during high-density periods. Brush mouse infection (20%) was higher than in the earlier study (6% in pinyon-juniper), and the authors found infection at six sites with no deer mice, suggesting a possible different virus or host-virus system that requires genetic confirmation.

**Transmission inferences.** The male bias and mass-class pattern suggest horizontal transmission, probably through aggressive encounters between adult males. Low infection in young deer mice, with no sex bias, is consistent with passive maternal antibody that wanes with age, a pattern also seen in laboratory rat studies with Seoul virus.

**Reservoir criteria.** The authors note that high antibody prevalence in non-deer-mouse species does not make them epidemiologically important for HPS. Establishing a reservoir requires range overlap with human cases, commonness, contact with humans, infection throughout the human case range, and persistent shedding. Most non-deer-mouse hosts are rare or geographically restricted, though the western harvest mouse and brush mouse may cause some cases.

**Main conclusions.**
- SNV infection in deer mice occurs across most of its habitat range in the region, and deer mice should be considered potentially infected throughout their range in the contiguous United States.
- Antibody prevalence reported here should be treated as minimum estimates of infection.
- Genetic studies matching viruses from host species to human-case viruses are needed to clarify the epidemiological role of brush mice and other species.
- Long-term mark-recapture studies were under way to assess age, season, and temporal patterns in prevalence.
