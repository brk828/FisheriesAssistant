# Reservoir Work Group Meeting on Selective Withdrawal Operations at Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #native-fish-management #Cyprinidae #water-quality #dam-operations

## Executive Summary

This document is correspondence from the Bureau of Reclamation dated February 4, 1993, announcing a meeting of the Reservoir Work Group scheduled for February 12, 1993, in Phoenix, Arizona. The meeting focuses on selective withdrawal operations at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to modify downstream water temperatures for the benefit of [[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha) and other native fishes in the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The enclosed Issue Paper outlines agency roles, resource management needs, research priorities, and potential impacts of temperature modification on [[Lake Powell]], the lower Colorado River, and [[Lake Mead]]. The primary objective is to establish and maintain a second spawning population of humpback chub in the mainstem without deleteriously affecting existing populations or the broader ecosystem.

## Background & Description

The Reservoir Work Group comprises technical personnel affiliated with [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] and the NEPA process. The group's charge includes compiling and evaluating information related to instream temperature augmentation, identifying and prioritizing resource issues, determining necessary research and monitoring, and developing components for managing resources in the Canyon and in Lakes Powell and Mead under selective withdrawal operations.

**Agency Roles**: Primary management entities for aquatic and terrestrial resources include USFWS, Arizona Game and Fish (AGF), Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (UDWR), National Park Service (USNPS), and Native American communities. The Bureau of Reclamation manages water resources and operates multi-purpose projects including flood control and recreation. Western Area Power Administration markets electrical power generated at project facilities.

**Selective Withdrawal Concept**: The Multi-Level Intake Structure (MLIS) would allow withdrawal of water from warmer strata in [[Lake Powell]] rather than exclusively from the hypolimnion, thereby increasing downstream temperatures during critical spawning and rearing periods (May through October). Target release temperatures are 11°C for May and 15°C for June through October.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Lower Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] experiences cold, stable temperatures (8-10°C year-round) due to hypolimnetic releases. These conditions limit native fish reproduction and survival, particularly for [[Humpback Chub]], while favoring cold-water species like trout and creating conditions unsuitable for native fish spawning in the mainstem.

**Current Ecosystem Structure**:
- The [[Cladophora]]/diatom community dominates the Glen Canyon reach and forms a major component of the aquatic food base
- [[Gammarus lacustris]] takes refuge in Cladophora beds and grazes on epiphytic diatoms
- Aquatic dipterans comprise approximately 85% of drifting macroinvertebrates
- Below the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence, [[Oscillatoria]] becomes the dominant alga, providing lower nutritive value than Cladophora communities
- Non-native fish species compete with and predate upon native fishes

**Parasite Concerns**:
- Asian tapeworm (Bothriocephalus acheilognathi): 80% of young humpback chub were infested in 1990 surveys; highest density of hatched coracidium occurs at 20°C
- Lernaea cyprinacea (anchor worm): egg production does not occur below 24°C; critical low temperature for development is 15-20°C; highest infection rates found in backwaters and warm flooded lowlands exceeding 25°C
- Nematodes in trout: infestation may be temperature-related with [[Gammarus]] as potential intermediate host

## Key Findings

**Primary Management Objectives**:
1. Establish and maintain a second spawning population of [[Humpback Chub]] in the mainstem without deleteriously affecting existing populations or the ecosystem
2. Develop integrated resource management plan for [[Lake Powell]], [[Lower Colorado River]], and [[Lake Mead]] under temperature modification operations
3. Balance needs of target resources with potential impacts to reservoir and downstream ecosystems

**Critical Research Gaps Identified**:

**Lake Powell Investigations**:
- Simulation of selective withdrawal operations (withdrawal zone, facility design, operations)
- Reservoir water quality simulation under varying hydrologic conditions
- Limnological impacts to Lake Powell and Lake Mead
- Effects on heat budget, nutrient budget, salinity, dissolved oxygen, and reservoir circulation
- Impacts on reservoir productivity and fisheries

**Lower Colorado River Unknowns**:
- Lethal and optimum growth temperature data for Colorado River native fishes not available
- [[Cladophora]] temperature studies conducted between 15-25°C but not below 15°C (current river temperatures 8-10°C)
- Thermal tolerance and preference of [[Gammarus]] not studied below 15°C
- Life histories, thermal requirements, and tolerances of aquatic insect species (particularly dipterans) unknown
- Response of diatom communities to temperature increases: exposure to 18-21°C causes shift to smaller species growing closer to substrate, replacing larger upright species preferred by grazing macroinvertebrates
- Effects of reduced nutrient loading (nitrogen, phosphorus) on downstream food base
- Zooplankton entrainment and community response to temperature augmentation unknown

**Flow and Backwater Considerations**:
- Flow regulation may be used to stress or repel competitive and predacious non-native species or prevent parasite spread
- December 1992 backwater meeting conclusions: backwaters may be more important under warmed water conditions; primarily utilized by young of the year; steady flows without warming will not help native fish; warming water and creating backwaters both necessary
- Higher incidences of parasitically infected fish found in backwaters or warm flooded lowlands
- Native species do not exhibit strong "homing" to same backwaters year to year, unlike some non-native species

**Operational Criteria Needs**:
- Flow magnitudes, duration, and fluctuation patterns to accompany temperature augmentation not yet determined
- Potential need for cooling releases during low reservoir/low inflow periods when temperatures may become too warm for some species
- Lower release temperatures may be management option for controlling non-native fish or parasite spread

**Adaptive Management Components**:
- Long-term monitoring of Lake Powell water quality and biological productivity
- Continuous assessment of reservoir impacts under selective withdrawal operations
- Frequency of temperature augmentation operations dependent on humpback chub year class status
- Experimental management approaches proposed: "nursery" backwater raising of larval humpback chub; removal of non-native fishes near [[Little Colorado River]] and other tributaries

**Power Generation Impacts**:
When multi-level intakes applied to all 8 intakes (equal treatment), selective withdrawal operations have little impact on power generation and provide operational flexibility

**Additional Considerations**:
- Feasibility of using Navajo powerplant cooling water as supplement to warming releases from Glen Canyon Dam requires investigation
- Need to prioritize target resources inclusive of life history components and system interactions (food base, predators, parasites)
- Question posed: "How do we want the Canyons to look like in 20 years or so (under selective withdrawal operations)?"
- Plans may require amendment over time as new information surfaces and understanding expands
