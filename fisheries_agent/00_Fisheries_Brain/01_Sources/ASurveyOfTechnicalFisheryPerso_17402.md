# A Survey of Technical Fishery Personnel

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fishery-personnel

## Executive Summary

Kenneth D. Carlander ([[Iowa State College]]) conducted a mail survey of fish conservation agencies in April 1958 to establish how many technical fishery personnel worked in the [[United States]] and [[Canada]], and how they were trained, employed and distributed. The survey counted 1,905 individuals: 1,673 in the United States and 232 in Canada. Most were in [[Fishery Research]] (900), with smaller groups in [[Fishery Management]] (354), administration (249), survey and trouble-shooting (232), [[Fish Culture]] (138) and education (32). Over 91 percent held college degrees and almost 44 percent held advanced degrees. Most of the workforce was young, and only about 41 percent belonged to the [[American Fisheries Society]].

## Background & Description

The study was prompted by discussions of the professional status of fishery biologists and the need for professional standards. Little was known about how many people were working as fishery biologists, where they were, or how they were trained. A questionnaire was sent to state conservation agencies, universities offering fishery courses, federal bodies ([[Fish and Wildlife Service]], Soil Conservation Service, Public Health Service, Corps of Engineers), Canadian provincial agencies, the Fisheries Research Board of Canada, the Canadian Wildlife Service, treaty and interstate commissions, and some individuals. The author reported nearly 100 percent cooperation.

Inclusion required that at least one-third of a person's time be spent on fishery problems, with technical training or responsibility. Respondents recorded age, education, and the percentage of duties in each category. The definitions were deliberately general, so respondents set their own boundaries. The main ambiguity concerned fish hatchery staff: some agencies listed no hatchery personnel, some listed only technically trained supervisors, and some listed all supervisors. Non-supervisory hatchery workers and some research staff were not listed by any respondent.

Because duties were split by percentage, about two-thirds of respondents had responsibilities in more than one category. Each person was assigned to the category holding the largest share of their work; ties went to the first category listed. About 10 percent had non-fishery duties, mostly in bird, mammal and other wildlife management.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The survey describes the workforce, not aquatic ecosystems, but it does explain how fishery work differs between habitats:

- **Marine fisheries:** About one-third of personnel (605 individuals) worked on marine fisheries, mostly in research and mainly on commercial fisheries. The author notes that ocean fishery management relies chiefly on controlling the catch, which requires many researchers and few managers.
- **Freshwater fisheries:** The remaining two-thirds (1,300 individuals) worked mainly on inland waters, largely sport fisheries. Here, management is achieved more through [[Habitat Management]], which creates greater demand for management and survey staff. Almost half of freshwater personnel listed management, survey or trouble-shooting as their main job.

Employment also varied by sector. State and provincial agencies employed the largest share, and federal agencies were the next largest. Colleges and private employers accounted for much less.

## Key Findings

**Size and composition of the workforce**
- 1,905 technical fishery personnel were identified (1,673 U.S.; 232 Canada).
- By main responsibility: research 900; management 354; administration 249; survey and trouble-shooting 232; fish culture 138; education 32.
- Respondents averaged 66.3 percent of their time in fisheries work overall; about 10 percent had non-fishery duties.

**Employers** (per the abstract; the text gives 38.5 percent for federal agencies, and the discrepancy is in the source)
- State and provincial agencies: 50.4 percent (961 individuals).
- Federal agencies, including treaty commissions: 39.1 percent (744 individuals).
- Colleges: 8.9 percent (169 individuals; graduate students excluded).
- Non-governmental/private: 1.6 percent (31 individuals). The author considers this an underestimate, since surveys went mainly to government and academic bodies.

**Education**
- Over 91 percent held a college degree; almost 44 percent held an advanced degree.
- Highest degrees: 168 high school, 893 bachelor's, 542 master's, 293 doctorate (9 persons had no training data).
- Most people without college degrees were over 40 and had substantial field experience.

**Age**
- Over half of those in management, survey and trouble-shooting, and research were under 35, which the author attributes to the field's rapid growth.
- Average ages: fish culture 45; management and survey 35; research 35; education 40; administration 43; all combined 37.
- Few personnel were near retirement, so job openings were expected to come mainly from expansion.

**Professional society membership**
- Only 41.2 percent of personnel belonged to the American Fisheries Society, based on a November 1956 membership list. (The table gives 39.1 percent combined, and the source gives both figures.)
- Freshwater personnel were better represented (46.5 percent) than marine personnel (27.2 percent). The author suggests this may show the Society is not meeting marine biologists' needs.

**Geography**
- The Pacific Coast states (Region 1) had the largest number of personnel (483) but the lowest share with advanced degrees.
- Canadian personnel had a higher share of doctorates and master's degrees than any U.S. region. The Southeast had a similar share with at least a master's degree to Canada, but fewer doctorates.

**Marine versus freshwater comparison**
- Marine personnel: 605 total, with 474 in research. Freshwater personnel: 1,300 total, with 426 in research.
- Academic backgrounds were broadly similar, but marine administrators were more often PhDs. Freshwater administrators more often came up through management.

**Job classifications (state departments)**
- Biologists generally had broad responsibilities, with research as the main duty but other duties too.
- Fish and game assistants, who mainly tagged fish in the field, were judged to be subprofessional.
- Management staff were older on average than biologists. The author notes that management offered more openings for field-experienced people without specialized academic training, though many still held master's degrees.
- Top positions had low average ages, which the author links to the field's rapid growth.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Definitions of a technical fishery worker showed substantial agreement among agencies, though the author notes that more specific standards and specifications would be needed before a formal roster or register of professional fishery personnel could be created.
- The survey probably understated the workforce. It excluded many people in fish propagation, rough-fish control crews, conservation officers, area managers, and office staff, many of whom contribute to resource conservation.
