# Stoneflies (Plecoptera) of Northwestern South Carolina

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Plecoptera #aquatic-entomology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This 1968 study by McCaskill and Prins documents the seasonal occurrence and geographic distribution of stonefly (Plecoptera) taxa across 116 streams in a five-county region (Oconee, Pickens, Greenville, Anderson, Abbeville) of northwestern [[South Carolina]]. Sampling from December 1965 to March 1967 yielded **50 stonefly taxa**, with robust distributional data available for **22 nymph species**. The study is notable for documenting a pronounced decline in stonefly diversity and density from the mountainous [[Blue Ridge]] region (above 1000 ft msl) to the agricultural/industrial [[Piedmont]] region (below 1000 ft msl), implicating substrate instability, pollution, and land-use intensity—rather than temperature—as primary drivers of this pattern. This work complements broader [[Aquatic Macroinvertebrate]] and water-quality bioindicator literature, as stoneflies are recognized as sensitive indicators of stream water quality.

## Background & Description
While the paper is primarily distributional/ecological rather than morphological, it does address taxonomic-level identification challenges relevant to nymph morphology:

- **Nymph vs. adult identifiability**: Nymphs of *[[Leuctra]]*, *[[Allocapnia]]*, and *[[Peltoperla]]* genera could not be distinguished to species level morphologically, whereas adults of these genera were identifiable to species. This indicates that larval (nymphal) morphological characters in these genera are less diagnostic than adult characters (likely genitalic or wing venation traits).
- **Sampling gear specificity**: Nymphs were collected with a four-foot seine (11 meshes/inch) and a triangular dip net (20 meshes/inch), plus hand collection — implying nymphs occupy benthic substrate niches (under rocks, in leaf detritus) accessible to fine-mesh netting.
- **Substrate association**: *[[Allocapnia]]* nymphs were "normally in association with leaf detritus," suggesting a detritivore/shredder morphology and behavior consistent with functional feeding group classifications for Nemouridae.
- **Genus-level complexes**: *Peltoperla* spp. nymphs were morphologically indistinguishable from one another despite representing at least three species (*P. laurie*, *P. sipha*, *P. maria*) — highlighting cryptic morphological similarity within the genus at the nymphal stage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The study area spans two distinct physiographic/ecological zones, each supporting different stonefly assemblages:

**Mountainous Region (>1000 ft msl)**
- Predominantly forested, rural, granite outcrops, chemically soft water.
- Steep gradients, high and rapid runoff fluctuation.
- Annual precipitation: 60–76 in; runoff: 22–40 in.
- Mean altitude ~2000 ft msl; water temps 26°C to 2°C.
- Supported **19 species (11 genera)** — higher diversity.
- Exclusive taxa included *[[Pteronarcys scotti]]*, *[[Isogenus hastatus]]*, *[[Isogenus bilobatus]]*, *[[Paragnetina immarginata]]*, and *Peltoperla* spp.

**Piedmont Region (<1000 ft msl)**
- Agricultural/industrial land use, moderate gradients, sandy/muddy unstable substrates.
- Annual precipitation: 44–60 in; runoff: 18–22 in.
- Mean altitude ~650 ft msl; water temps up to 28°C (higher max than mountains), down to 6°C.
- Supported only **10 species (7 genera)** — a subset of the mountain fauna (no unique piedmont-only taxa found).
- Dominant/ubiquitous taxa: *[[Acroneuria abnormis]]*, *[[Acroneuria xanthenes]]*, *[[Perlesta placida]]*, *[[Perlesta frisoni]]*.

**Microhabitat notes:**
- *Acroneuria abnormis* — most abundant/widespread; found in riffles primarily, also pools; permanent streams of all sizes.
- *Perlesta placida* — restricted to permanent streams; never found in intermittent streams or mountain seepages.
- *Paragnetina media* — restricted to five large, rapid streams above 1000 ft msl.
- *Pteronarcys dorsata* — only in large, swift mountain streams.
- *Pteronarcys biloba* — restricted to streams below 1000 ft msl (opposite pattern from most taxa).
- *Isoperla clio* — found in slow-current areas with accumulated debris.
- *Peltoperla* spp. — broadest habitat tolerance among genera studied, found in streams of all sizes including mountain seepages and intermittent streams.

**Key abiotic gradient**: The 1000 ft msl elevation functioned as an ecological boundary correlating with the Blue Ridge/Southern Piedmont land-resource area transition (per Craddock & Ellerbe 1966), coinciding with shifts in land use, substrate type, and industrialization intensity.

## Key Findings
1. **Taxonomic richness**: 50 stonefly taxa identified across 6 families (Perlidae, Perlodidae, Pteronarcidae, Peltoperlidae, Chloroperlidae, Nemouridae) — expanding known South Carolina Plecoptera records beyond the prior 13 species reported for the Savannah River (Patrick, Cairns, & Roback 1967).
2. **Altitudinal diversity gradient**: Nearly double the species richness occurred above 1000 ft msl (19 species/11 genera) versus below it (10 species/7 genera); all piedmont species were also found in the mountains, but 9 mountain species were absent from the piedmont — indicating a nested distributional pattern rather than regional endemism.
3. **Pollution/land-use hypothesis**: The authors attribute the piedmont's reduced stonefly diversity primarily to agricultural and industrial contamination (pesticides, effluents) rather than temperature, citing known stonefly sensitivity to pesticides ([[Jensen and Gaufin 1964]]) and their established use as [[Bioindicator]] organisms for organic pollution (Gaufin & Tarzwell 1952).
4. **Substrate instability hypothesis**: Piedmont streams' sandy/muddy, unstable substrates were considered less suitable for Plecoptera colonization compared to the rocky, stable substrates of mountain streams (citing Cordone & Kelly 1961; Gaufin 1962).
5. **Temperature ruled out as primary driver**: Unlike findings in Rocky Mountain stonefly studies (Dobbs & Hisaw 1925; Knight & Gaufin 1966), temperature differentials between regions were minimal at the critical ~1000 ft transition zone, suggesting temperature was not the principal limiting factor for stonefly distribution in this South Carolina system.
6. **Cryptic nymph diversity**: Multiple genera (*Leuctra*, *Allocapnia*, *Peltoperla*) contained nymphs that could not be resolved to species, underscoring a taxonomic/methodological limitation requiring rearing studies to link nymphs with identifiable adults.
7. **Rare/localized records**: Several species (*Acroneuria georgiana*, *Isoperla confusa*, *Isoperla bellona*, *Nemocapnia carolina*, *Allocapnia virginiana*) were documented from single or very few historical collection events, indicating either genuine rarity or sampling limitations.
8. **Historical baseline value**: Several records rely on specimens collected decades earlier (1927–1940) by collectors such as D. Dunavan and identified retrospectively by T.H. Frison in 1944, providing a valuable historical baseline for comparison with the 1965–1967 survey data.
