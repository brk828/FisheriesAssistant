# Ecological Studies of the Topeka Shiner (Notropis topeka) in Deep Creek, Riley County, Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #leuciscidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 student report by W. L. Minckley, submitted to Kansas State College, examines the ecology of the [[Topeka Shiner]] (*Notropis topeka* Gilbert) in [[Deep Creek]], a small tributary of the [[Kansas River]] in Riley County, Kansas. Fieldwork ran from September 1 to November 25, 1956 across ten stations on roughly 1.8 miles of stream. The study addressed three objectives: the species composition and relative abundance of the fish community, the age and growth of the Topeka shiner, and its habitat relationships. The author found no chemical conditions detrimental to fish life, a stream poor in aquatic insect food, sixteen fish species, three age classes of Topeka shiner, and a strong preference for silt-bottomed pools. The species was most strongly associated with the [[Red Shiner]].

## Background & Description

The Topeka shiner was first described by Gilbert (1884) from specimens collected by W. F. Cragin in 1884 in Shunganunga Creek near Topeka. The historical review traces collections across Kansas, including the Smoky Hill, Solomon, Saline, and Neosho drainages, and tributaries of the Marais des Cygnes River. Records from the Arkansas River drainage were not confirmed: extensive 1956 collections by Metcalf failed to find the species there, and the author suggested that the [[Plains Sand Shiner]] may have been misidentified as the Topeka shiner.

Deep Creek rises in the Flint Hills of Wabaunsee County at about 1,350 feet elevation and flows north roughly nineteen miles to the Kansas River, with an average gradient of about twenty feet per mile. It is fed largely by springs; its tributaries are intermittent "wet weather creeks," which were dry during the study. Riparian vegetation is mostly timber (American elm, sycamore, oaks, black walnut, cottonwood, willow), with big bluestem grass dominating the upper one-sixth of the banks. About thirty per cent of the immediate banks were estimated as cultivated.

The study area began at U.S. Highway 40 and extended north; ten stations were chosen by bottom type, shading, and size. Stations ranged from clay hardpan and silt pools to gravel-rubble riffles and bedrock.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Dissolved oxygen ranged from 4.4 to 11.6 ppm, with the low reading at the bottom of Station 7 and the high at the surface of Station 1. Carbon dioxide exceeded 20.0 ppm at Stations 1, 2, 7, and 9, but high CO2 at Stations 1 and 2 was accompanied by high oxygen, and at Stations 7 and 9 oxygen remained above the 4 ppm minimum cited by [[Lagler]]. Methyl orange alkalinity ranged from 75 to 154.5 ppm, below the 250 ppm threshold considered non-detrimental by [[Ellis et al. 1948]].

**Turbidity.** Readings ranged from "very clear" (under 10 ppm) to 120 ppm. Higher turbidity was associated with silt bottoms, large pools, bare banks, and high fish density; small pools, spring-fed pools, and gravel, rubble, or bedrock pools were usually clear. No algal bloom was noted.

**Temperature.** Water temperatures varied between stations, attributed to springs, shading, and cooling from subsurface percolation.

**Bottom fauna.** Organisms per sample ranged from zero to thirty-one. Gravel-rubble bottoms held the highest average (23 organisms), and clay hardpan-silt the lowest (3). The mean standing crop was 41.2 organisms per square foot, with an average volume of 0.64 cc. Under [[Hazzard Food Grade]] standards this placed the stream in Food Grade three (poor).

**Fish community.** Sixteen species were collected. The most abundant by number were the [[Southern Redbelly Dace]] (39.7%), the [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] (7.6%), and the [[Stoneroller]] (8.2%). The Topeka shiner made up 6.3% of the total catch (253 fish). Three stocked species, [[Largemouth Bass]], crappie, and [[Channel Catfish]], were reported but not captured.

## Key Findings

- **Chemistry:** No chemical conditions detrimental to fish were detected; oxygen and alkalinity stayed within accepted limits despite some high CO2 readings.
- **Food base:** The stream was classed as poor in benthic food, with few aquatic insect larvae.
- **Age and growth:** Scale microprojection and length-frequency analysis (772 fish measured) identified three age classes. Growth was greatest in the first year and declined each year. Age class I averaged 48 mm and age class II averaged 58 mm total length at capture, and the author inferred a strong recent age-0 cohort.
- **Habitat:** Topeka shiners occurred most frequently over silt bottoms (about 84.5% occurrence in silt-hardpan categories by the Table 7 figures), in open water rather than vegetated pools, and never in riffles. No clear relationship with shading was found; apparent preference for turbid pools was attributed to the silt bottoms rather than turbidity itself.
- **Behavior:** Young-of-year fish schooled in 4 to 12 inches of water; adults were more solitary. Red "breeding coloration" on the fins persisted from spring through November. In aquaria, the fish acclimatized well, fed from the bottom, and gradually took surface food.
- **Association:** The [[Red Shiner]] was the species most closely linked with the Topeka shiner (association index 0.92; reciprocal 0.85). Stoneroller and orangespotted sunfish showed high coefficients from abundance, while white sucker, green sunfish, and johnny darter associated through shared silt-bottom preference.
- **Method check:** Rotenone sampling at Station 6 (Table 11) revealed that stonerollers, red shiners, and fathead minnows are poorly captured by common-sense seines.
- **Conservation implication:** The species was widely scattered across Kansas drainages, with the author noting that its distribution "may be general" in these systems.
