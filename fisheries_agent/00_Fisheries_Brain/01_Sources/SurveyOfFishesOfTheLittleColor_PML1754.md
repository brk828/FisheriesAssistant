# Fish Survey of the Little Colorado River and Vicinity, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report describes a September 1977 fish survey of the [[Little Colorado River]] from [[Blue Spring]] downstream about five miles to the vicinity of [[Big Canyon]], in the [[Grand Canyon]] region of Arizona. The survey was prepared by W. L. Minckley of the Department of Biology, [[Museum of Northern Arizona]], and was funded by the Office of Endangered Species, Region 2, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

Only two fish species were collected: the [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), which was widespread, and the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), of which three specimens were taken. The humpback chub record is the first from so far upstream in the Little Colorado River, although the species had previously been taken near the river mouth. The author concludes that the Little Colorado River is an important habitat for the humpback chub and recommends a long-term study that includes removing adults for propagation.

## Background & Description

The Little Colorado River is a tributary of the [[Colorado River]]. Collections from this drainage had generally been made in the headwaters region or near the mouth. Few collections had been made between Blue Spring and the river mouth. The survey was originally planned to cover the reach from Blue Spring to the confluence, but the team found that fire-damaged terrain and difficult access made the lower section impossible to sample.

Fish were collected with a 20 x 6 ft x 1/4-inch mesh minnow seine and one 20 x 6 ft gill net panel with a mesh size of 1 1/2 inches (sized as reported). All fish were preserved in formalin, except endangered species, which were measured, photographed, and released. Preserved specimens were deposited in the [[Ichthyology Collection]] at the Museum of Northern Arizona.

Four sampling stations were established:

- **Station 1 (Blue Spring):** The main source of inflow to the Little Colorado River between Lees Ferry and Lake Mead, and the largest spring in the drainage. Depth ranged from about one foot to over six feet, with a bottom of sand and gravel. Water temperature was about 20°C. Sampled by seining.
- **Station 2:** Located in a side channel below Blue Spring, about 100 ft long and 50 ft wide, with depths of one to ten feet. Water temperature was about 20°C. Sampled by seining.
- **Station 3:** Located 2.5 miles downstream of Blue Spring, a large pool with depths of one to four feet over a travertine bottom. Sampled by seining and gill netting.
- **Station 4:** Located five miles downstream, a pool about 200 ft long and 100 ft wide, with depths of one to more than 20 feet over a mud bottom. Bounded at each end by [[Travertine Dams]] 10 to 15 ft high, with a gradual slope over 100 ft or more. Sampled by seining.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Little Colorado River was described as a warm, relatively large tributary with limited inputs from other tributaries in the immediate area. Water temperatures in the survey reach were generally above 20°C. Bottom substrates were travertine, sand, and mud, and the pools were formed by travertine deposits. The author notes that the Little Colorado River is the largest tributary entering the Colorado River between the mouth of the [[Paria River]] and [[Bright Angel Creek]], and that the lack of other large tributaries in the immediate vicinity may increase the use of the Little Colorado River by humpback chub.

Two exotic species were discussed as potential competitors or predators:

- **[[Rainbow Trout]]:** Rainbow trout are prevented from occupying the Little Colorado River mainstem by high water temperatures (generally over 20°C) and the lack of suitable spawning habitat. They may reach the river mouth, but feeding and general activity are reduced above 20°C, so they are unlikely to penetrate far upstream. Because rainbow trout do not spawn in the Little Colorado River, they have little or no effect on the humpback chub there.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]:** Channel catfish carcasses were found along the bank near Blue Spring in June 1960 (Johnson and Sanderson, 1968). The author argues that if channel catfish and speckled dace can reach Blue Spring, the humpback chub may also be able to.

Historical reports support fish use of the area near Blue Spring. Heckler and Sanderson (1953) reported numerous unidentified fish up to ten inches long in the spring water.

## Key Findings

**Species collected**

- Two species were taken: the speckled dace and the humpback chub.
- The speckled dace occurred at all collecting localities.
- The humpback chub was collected only at Station 4.

**Speckled Dace**

- Most abundant above the outflow of Blue Spring, where most fish were adults about 20 to 70 mm total length.
- None were taken immediately downstream of Blue Spring, although numerous seine hauls were made there.
- Specimens at the other stations were small (30 to 60 mm total length), although a few larger individuals were taken.
- The speckled dace is widespread throughout the Colorado River within Grand Canyon and its tributaries and is generally the most abundant native fish in any collection. The Little Colorado River population appears distinct from mainstem populations, but the author considers it still referable to *Rhinichthys osculus* until systematic studies are done.

**Humpback Chub**

- Three specimens were taken at Station 4 by seining, ranging from 229 to 295 mm total length.
- The fish were in good condition and vigorous. No ectoparasites were observed.
- They were associated with speckled dace in slow current along the bank, in water three to eight feet deep, at about 20°C.
- This is the first record of the species this far upstream in the Little Colorado River. It shows that the species uses the river more extensively than previously reported and indicates a population of unknown size in the mainstem Little Colorado River.
- Although no humpback chub were taken at the upstream stations, the author considers it likely that they occur there, since the habitat is available and the speckled dace are present above Blue Spring.
- If humpback chub occur from Blue Spring to the mouth, they would use a 13-mile corridor of permanent river where a viable population of this endangered species exists.

**Habitat Importance**

- The author concludes that the Little Colorado River is an important habitat for the humpback chub, probably because of its warmer water temperatures, its habitat type, and its size.
- The river is the largest tributary entering the Colorado River below [[Glen Canyon]] within the surveyed reach, which the author considers an important factor in attracting humpback chub.

**Recommendations**

The author recommends a long-term study of the Little Colorado River from Blue Spring to the confluence with the Colorado River. The study would:

1. Remove a series of twenty (20) adults for propagation purposes. Determine the population size and age structure of humpback chub in the Little Colorado River mainstem and near the mouth in the Colorado River.
2. Reintroduce the species into areas where it previously occurred, provided water-quality levels are tolerable to the species.
3. Establish a brood stock that can be used in hybridization experiments with other members of the [[Gila Genus]], to provide better information on this genus.
4. Provide a pool of juvenile fish for use in research and in biocontrol experiments.
5. Determine all aspects of the life history of the humpback chub that are important for propagation and research, and determine whether any natural populations can be sacrificed for this research without endangering the population.
