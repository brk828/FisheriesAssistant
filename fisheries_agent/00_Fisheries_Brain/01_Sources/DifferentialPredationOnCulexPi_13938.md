# Differential Predation on Culex pipiens and Anopheles albimanus Mosquito Larvae by Gambusia affinis and Cyprinodon nevadensis, and the Effects of Simulated Reeds on Predation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 doctoral dissertation by Thomas Lester Danielsen (University of California, Riverside, Department of Biology) reports 16 laboratory experiments comparing the predation of two fish species, [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish) and [[Cyprinodon Nevadensis]] (desert pupfish), on two mosquito larvae species, [[Anopheles Albimanus]] and [[Culex Pipiens]]. The study focuses on two variables that affect prey: predator food preference and the escape facilities available to prey, tested using simulated [[Reed Environments]] of minimum and maximum density. The author concludes that both fish prefer *A. albimanus* over *C. pipiens* in open water, that dense reeds reduce the availability of *A. albimanus* to both predators, and that the two fish differ in how they select and reject prey.

## Background & Description

- **Subject:** Predation by two fish species on mosquito larvae, with a focus on biological control of mosquitoes.
- **Predators:**
  - [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish), Baird and Girard; from Afton Canyon (Mojave River), Santa Ana River near Riverside, and Midgeville (UC Riverside).
  - [[Cyprinodon Nevadensis]] (desert pupfish), Eigenmann and Eigenmann; from Spring House and Big Lake, Saratoga Springs, Death Valley National Monument, and a Midgeville holding pond.
- **Prey:**
  - [[Anopheles Albimanus]] Weidemann (laboratory-reared, third and fourth instar larvae preferred).
  - [[Culex Pipiens]] Fatigans Weidemann (= *C. p. quinquefasciatus* Say), laboratory-reared, third and fourth instar larvae preferred.
- **Experimental apparatus:**
  - Yellow polyethylene dishpans (28 × 33 × 15 cm), filled to 12 cm with tap water and DeChlor.
  - [[Simulated Reed Environments]] built from 1/4 inch dowels inserted into 1/4 inch galvanized hardware cloth; minimum density used every fourth hole, maximum density every other hole (twice the minimum).
  - A [[Benthosobservatory]] (a pond-level observation structure in a shallow pond) designed by Dr. Ernest C. Bay and Dr. T. W. Fisher.
- **Measurement:** Larvae killed = introduced minus left alive; larvae eaten = killed minus carcasses. Fish were weighed on a Mettler Balance type P-120 (division = 10 mg).
- **Theoretical framework:** Predation variables after Leopold (1933): prey density, predator density, predator food preference, prey physical condition and escape facilities, and alternate food abundance. The study addresses variables 3 and 4.
- **Funding:** National Science Foundation grants GB 3500 and GB 6668 (Dr. Lars H. Carpelan), with GB 3135 (Dr. Frank Vasek) for two months in summer 1967.
- **Committee/acknowledgments:** Dr. Lars H. Carpelan (chair), Dr. Ernest C. Bay, Dr. Eric T. Pengelley; statistical help from Dr. Robert Gill.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Literature context:** Most prior mosquito-fish control work was field-based or casual; few laboratory studies existed (Gerberich, 1946; Gerberich and Laird, 1965). Conflicting views existed on whether vegetation protects anopheline larvae (Bogoyavlenskii, 1936; Root, 1924; Barnikol, 1941; Sella, 1926; Hackett, 1931).
- **Prior selectivity work:** [[Hess and Tarzwell 1942]] used the forage ratio method and concluded *G. affinis* preferred culicines over anophelines. The author argues the forage ratio method does not account for environmental effects such as reeds.
- **Mechanism proposed (Renn, 1943):** Anopheles larvae cling to emergent stems via surface menisci, which may explain why *A. albimanus* survives better in reeds; *C. pipiens* hang from the surface and lack this attachment.
- **Natural habitat observations:** At Big Lake, Saratoga Springs, peripheral vegetation is inundated from late fall through late spring; *C. nevadensis* invades this vegetation, and open water remains almost fishless. Deacon et al. (1965) found feeding primarily near the bottom and most intensely in vegetation.
- **Midgeville pond:** Few *C. nevadensis* were seen after the first weeks of breeding; when drained, 130 fish were counted.
- **Environmental conditions (laboratory):** Air temperature generally 16.7 to 22.8 °C; water temperature generally 18.9 to 22.8 °C (varies by experiment). Benthosobservatory water temperature 11.1 to 14.5 °C.
- **Alternate prey noted:** Midge larvae, algae, small crustaceans, snail eggs and cladocerans were present in the Benthosobservatory.

## Key Findings

### Experiments 1 and 2: Open water vs. reeds, high prey density (100 larvae)
- In open water, *C. nevadensis* and *G. affinis* killed and consumed approximately the same numbers of *C. pipiens*.
- *C. nevadensis* tended to kill and consume more *C. pipiens* in reeds; a t-test found no significant difference between non-reed and either reed environment in Experiment 1, though an ANOVA reported significant differences for minimum and very highly significant differences for maximum reeds.
- Experiment 1: *C. nevadensis* averaged 52.8 mg/day *C. pipiens* eaten (mean fish weight 0.57 g); *G. affinis* averaged 38.6 mg/day (mean fish weight 0.56 g). The *Cyprinodon* averaged 14.2 mg/day more.
- *Gambusia* weight: GA-10, GA-1 and GA-8 showed no consistent relation between size and consumption; GA-8 weighed almost twice GA-10 or GA-1 but ate less.
- Experiment 2 (11 *C. nevadensis*, four trials per environment): data from the first two runs were considerably lower than later runs and were excluded from Figure 6 (last three trials used). Only AC-6 and AC-8 ate fewer larvae in maximum reeds.
- Overall, *C. nevadensis* tended to kill and eat more *C. pipiens* in reeds than in non-reeds.

### Experiment 3: Low prey density (10 larvae)
- Both species killed nearly all *C. pipiens* in the non-reed environment.
- *C. nevadensis* killed as many *C. pipiens* in reeds as in non-reeds.
- *G. affinis* killed fewer *C. pipiens* in reeds.
- Fish A-6GA and A-8GA were injured during weighing and performed abnormally; their data were retained but discussed as possibly affected.

### Experiment 4: Learning
- Old *G. affinis* (with 3–4 days of practice in maximum reeds) performed no better than naive fish; mean killed was 9.8 of 10 for new fish and similar for old fish (Table 10 totals: 110 non-reed and 108 maximum reed for old fish; mean 10 and 9.8).
- Learning does not appear necessary for *G. affinis* to find *C. pipiens* in reeds; hunger is more likely the factor.

### Experiments 5, 7 and 8: Mixed prey, *C. nevadensis*
- *C. nevadensis* preferred *A. albimanus* over *C. pipiens* in both open water and maximum reeds.
- Experiment 5 (50 + 50 larvae, 6 h, 12 fish): *A. albimanus* killed in non-reed mean 49.9 (total 599); maximum reed mean 41.3 (total 495). *C. pipiens* killed: non-reed mean 27.3 (total 328); maximum reed mean 32.3 (total 388).
- Experiment 7 (50 + 50 larvae, 8 fish per species, Daphnia-fed): *C. nevadensis* killed *A. albimanus* at a mean of 21.6 (non-reed) and 19.1 (reed); *C. pipiens* at 4.1 (non-reed) and 11.1 (reed) (Table 13).
- Experiment 8 (30 + 30 larvae, 8 fish per species): *C. nevadensis* mean *A. albimanus* killed 16.4 (non-reed) and 21.6 (reed); *C. pipiens* 2.5 (non-reed) and 12.5 (reed) (Table 15).
- *G. affinis* in Experiment 7 (Table 14): *A. albimanus* killed mean 17.5 (non-reed) and 8.0 (reed); *C. pipiens* 6.4 (non-reed) and 11.5 (reed).
- *G. affinis* in Experiment 8 (Table 16): *A. albimanus* killed mean 16.0 (non-reed) and 8.6 (reed); *C. pipiens* 12.2 (non-reed) and 13.0 (reed).
- Interpretation: in reeds, *G. affinis* killed more *C. pipiens* than *A. albimanus*; *C. nevadensis* maintained selectivity for *A. albimanus*.

### Experiment 6: *G. affinis* preference in open water
- Twelve *G. affinis* (each estimated over 1 g) given 30 + 30 larvae over six trials in non-reed tanks.
- *G. affinis* preferred *A. albimanus* in open water. Initially about 30% of *C. pipiens* and 70% of *A. albimanus* were killed; by trials five and six this shifted to about 10% *C. pipiens* and 90% *A. albimanus*.

### Experiments 9 and 10: High predation and independent presentation (*C. nevadensis*)
- Experiment 9 (inclusion required at least 36 of 60 larvae eaten; 9 of 12 fish qualified): seven of nine killed all 30 *A. albimanus* in non-reed tanks; none killed all 30 in maximum reeds. Mean *A. albimanus* killed 29.7 (non-reed) and 24.6 (reed); *C. pipiens* 17.6 and 20.1.
- Experiment 10 (larvae presented alone; 10 individuals qualified at 60% or more): *A. albimanus* mean 9.7 (non-reed) and 8.3 (reed).
- When presented alone, *A. albimanus* were killed more often than *C. pipiens* by *C. nevadensis* in both environments, supporting an "independent palatability factor" affecting *C. pipiens* consumption.

### Experiment 11: *G. affinis* alone (Daphnia-fed)
- Twelve females; each prey species presented alone with four trials per environment.
- *G. affinis* killed about equal numbers of *A. albimanus* and *C. pipiens* in non-reeds (mean 9.6 vs. 10.0 among qualifying fish); in maximum reeds, *C. pipiens* killed was slightly higher (8.9 vs. 7.5 among qualifying fish, Table 19).
- No independent palatability factor was detected in *G. affinis*; the author suggests *G. affinis* lacks the depressant effect.
- Predation on *A. albimanus* by *G. affinis* fell in maximum reeds, and fewer *A. albimanus* were killed than by *C. nevadensis* (Figure 21).

### Experiments 12 and 13: Hungry fish, 10 + 10 larvae
- Experiment 12 (eight qualifying fish; 10 *C. pipiens* per trial): mean killed 8.8 (non-reed) and 8.4 (reed); no reduction in reeds when predation was at or above 60%.
- Experiment 13 (10 + 10 larvae, 12 fish): five fish killed all 10 *C. pipiens* in maximum reeds; only two killed all 10 *A. albimanus* in maximum reeds. Ten of 12 fish killed all *A. albimanus* in non-reeds. Mean *A. albimanus* killed 9.6 (non-reed) and 8.7 (reed); *C. pipiens* 6.3 (non-reed) and 8.0 (reed).

### Experiment 14: *C. nevadensis* selection behavior (observational)
- Eleven *C. nevadensis*, two trials each; 100 larvae of each species per trial (except Cn-C: 50 and 84).
- Total strikes on *A. albimanus* = 493; acceptances = 333.
- *C. pipiens* strikes and acceptances are reported in Table 22; more *C. pipiens* were struck but fewer were accepted.
- Reported ratios: about 16 of 24 strikes accepted for *A. albimanus*; about 3 of 29 for *C. pipiens*.
- Repeated attacks on the same *C. pipiens* larva were observed, up to about 20 times, while repeated attacks on *A. albimanus* numbered about five.

### Experiment 15: *G. affinis* selection behavior (observational)
- Seven females and one male, 50 larvae of each species; about 15-minute trials.
- *A. albimanus* were struck slightly more often and accepted more frequently than *C. pipiens* (totals: 134 strikes and 95 acceptances for *A. albimanus*; 86 strikes and 52 acceptances for *C. pipiens*).

### Experiment 16: *G. affinis* in the Benthosobservatory
- Four females; 100 larvae of each species per trial over six trials; water temperature 11.1–14.5 °C.
- *A. albimanus*: 111 strikes, 97 accepted. *C. pipiens*: 58 strikes, 25 accepted.
- Nearly all struck *A. albimanus* were accepted; about half of struck *C. pipiens* were accepted.

### Behavioral observations (Benthosobservatory)
- *C. nevadensis* were more secretive, hiding on the bottom in folds of the enclosure, and rested on the bottom supported by their pectoral fins.
- *G. affinis* stayed off the substratum and constantly waved their pectoral fins.
- Movement elicited attacks from both fish; immobile larvae were often ignored.
- *G. affinis* frequently ignored moving *C. pipiens* and attacked *A. albimanus*.

### Summary of major conclusions
- Both fish prefer *A. albimanus* over *C. pipiens* in open water.
- *G. affinis* uses taste/tactile and visual cues to select; *C. nevadensis* uses taste/tactile cues only.
- *C. nevadensis* may kill more *C. pipiens* than it eats, through mortal injury during attack and rejection; *G. affinis* ate what it killed.
- Dense reeds reduce *A. albimanus* availability to both fish, most strongly for *G. affinis*. *C. nevadensis* maintained greater killing of *A. albimanus* than *C. pipiens* in reeds, whereas *G. affinis* did not.
- The author notes the study is not a definitive assessment of *C. nevadensis* for mosquito control; reproduction rates and alternate prey remain unresolved.
