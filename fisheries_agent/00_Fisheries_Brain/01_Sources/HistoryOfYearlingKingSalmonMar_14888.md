# History of Yearling King Salmon Marked and Released at Nimbus Hatchery

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by Warner, Fry, and Culver ([[California Department Of Fish And Game]]) reports on an experimental release of 20,579 marked yearling [[King Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) from [[Nimbus Hatchery]] into the [[American River]] in 1957. Unlike the hatchery's standard practice of releasing fish at ~90 days of age, these fish were reared to yearling size (~1 year) using eggs from a late-running American River stock. The experiment tested whether extended freshwater rearing would improve survival and spawning return rates. Results showed a markedly higher total return rate (3.56% of released fish accounted for as spawners) compared to 65 other historical king salmon marking experiments, particularly outperforming fall-run fingerling releases (mean 0.070%) and fall-run yearling releases elsewhere (0.072–0.073%). However, the yearling-rearing method proved economically inefficient for direct fishery contribution, as reared fish were costly to produce and contributed modestly to commercial and sport catches, largely due to precocious maturation (many returned as 3-year-olds) and slower growth from extended freshwater residence.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: [[King Salmon]] (Chinook Salmon), family [[Salmonidae]].
- **Study rationale**: Nimbus Hatchery normally releases king salmon at ~90 days old, but water temperatures at the hatchery are too warm for salmon rearing until late in the year (into November). Researchers used **late-running American River stock** (eggs taken late December 1955) on the hypothesis that offspring would naturally return during the cooler-water season more favorable for reproduction at Nimbus.
- **Marking methodology**: Fish were marked by excising the adipose fin and left ventral fin — a mark scheme assigned by the [[Pacific Marine Fisheries Commission]] specifically for this experiment. This same mark was used concurrently for other releases in the [[Columbia River]] and [[Puget Sound]] systems, but scale analysis reliably distinguished Nimbus-origin fish (held >1 year) from northern fish (held a much shorter time).
- **Release details**:
  - 20,579 yearlings marked in January–February 1957, averaging 8.1 fish/pound (range: 6.8–10.6 fish/lb).
  - 17 separate releases of ~1,000 fish each, from March 7 to April 8, 1957.
  - All released into the American River at the hatchery.
  - Estimated ~50,000 eggs were used to produce this cohort — roughly the egg production of 9 females, or ~22 fish total assuming a 3:2 male:female ratio (13 males including jacks).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal constraints**: [[Nimbus Hatchery]] water temperatures are a limiting factor for salmon egg incubation and juvenile rearing until into November, driving the hatchery's reliance on late-running stock and standard early releases.
- **Migration timing as heritable trait**: A key ecological finding was that progeny of the late-running parental stock also returned late — 92% of marked fish arrived at Nimbus after the critical warm-water period in 1958, and 83% in 1959 (vs. 67% of jacks in 1957), supporting the inference that **run-timing is an inherited characteristic**.
- **Precocious maturation**: An unusually high proportion (>53%) of marked fish matured as 3-year-olds ("jacks" for 2-year-olds separately tallied), compared to the more typical Pacific coast pattern of 4-year-old dominant spawners. This affected fish size, ocean residency time, and thus vulnerability/availability to commercial (26-inch size limit) and sport (22-inch limit) fisheries.
- **Freshwater residency effects**: Extended hatchery rearing to yearling size was linked to slower growth and smaller size at maturity, reducing commercial catch contribution despite improved overall survival to spawning.
- **Spawning distribution**: Marked fish were recovered both within the hatchery (via holding ponds) and via natural spawning in the American River downstream of Nimbus racks, indicating incomplete barrier effectiveness of the hatchery fish rack (some fish died or spawned above/at the rack rather than entering the hatchery).
- **Geographic recovery pattern**: All Nimbus-origin marked fish recovered in the ocean troll fishery were found in the San Francisco port area (specifically Bodega Bay and Point Reyes), despite ocean sampling coverage from Crescent City to Avila (San Luis Obispo County).

## Key Findings

### Ocean Recoveries
- **Commercial troll fishery**: Estimated 73 marked fish caught in 1958, 15 in 1959, 0 in 1960 (total weighted estimate: 88 fish).
  - 1958 average weight: 7.2 lbs dressed; total value ≈ $184.10 (at $0.35/lb).
  - 1959 average weight: 15.5 lbs; total value ≈ $116 (at $0.50/lb).
  - Combined commercial value: ≈ $300.
- **Sport fishery**: Not systematically sampled; only 2 marked fish turned in by San Francisco party boats. Minimum estimated value: $34 (based on estimate of ~$17/fish).

### Hatchery & River Returns
- **Total hatchery returns**: 492 marked fish across three seasons:
  - 1957–58: 102 fish (all males, 2-yr-old "jacks/grilse"), average fork length 35 cm.
  - 1958–59: 336 fish (111 males, 225 females), 3-year-olds; plus 21 males (<66 cm) recovered from the fish rack.
  - 1959–60: 54 fish (15 males, 39 females), 4-year-olds; plus 1 female removed from rack.
- **River (downstream) carcass recoveries**: 44 in 1957, 8 in 1958, 2 in 1959.
- **Estimated total returns to American River system** (hatchery + rack + downstream, adjusted for recovery efficiency ~25% below racks, ~92% above racks):
  - 1957: 278 total (all male)
  - 1958: 391 total (138 male, 253 female)
  - 1959: 63 total (19 male, 44 female)
  - **3-year total: 732 fish (435 males, 297 females)**

### Egg Production
- 264 marked females returned to the hatchery (1958–59 and 1959–60); 179 were spawned artificially (32% mortality in holding ponds due to fish arriving "green").
- Average eggs/female: 5,700; total hatchery egg take: 1,020,300 eggs.
- Estimated 33 large females spawned naturally in the river, producing an additional 188,100 eggs.
- **Total estimated egg production from marked fish: 1,208,400 eggs** — a ~20:1 return ratio relative to the ~50,000 eggs used to produce the original marked cohort (net gain of ~900,000+ eggs).

### Economic Analysis
- Estimated rearing cost (using 1958 trout-rearing benchmark of $0.72/lb): ~$1,830 for the 2,540 lbs of yearling salmon produced.
- Cost per returning spawner: $2.50/fish overall; $4.03/fish excluding 2-year-old jacks; up to $5.97/fish if 32% pond mortality recurred.
- Conclusion: Rearing to yearling size is **economically inefficient for direct fishery yield** (cost far exceeds ~$300 commercial + $34 sport value) but could be **cost-effective for rebuilding depleted spawning runs**.

- The **Nimbus yearling release rate of return (3.56%) was comparable to the best spring-run yearling releases** (Rogue/Umpqua Rivers, Oregon) and far exceeded any other fall-run release group, including the only other two fall-run yearling experiments (Pacific Northwest, ~0.072–0.073%).
- Three anomalously high-return experiments on the [[Deschutes River]] (Washington), involving colonization of a previously salmon-free stream via fishway, were excluded as atypical (0.68–0.98% returns under unique conditions).

### Fishery Health Indicator
- A "healthy" king salmon fishery typically shows a catch-to-escapement ratio of 3–4:1 (more fish caught than spawn).
- In this experiment, the ratio was inverted: **more than 8 spawners for every 1 fish caught commercially**, and likely >4 spawners per 1 fish caught even including sport catch — indicating the marked cohort disproportionately contributed to escapement rather than fisheries, consistent with early/small maturation reducing ocean fishery vulnerability.

### Conclusions
- Rearing fall-run king salmon to yearling size **may substantially increase spawning escapement** relative to standard fingerling releases, a novel finding for fall-run stocks (previously only demonstrated for spring-run stocks with naturally yearling-migrating life histories).
- The technique is **not economically justified for augmenting sport/commercial fisheries** but **may be valuable for rebuilding depleted spawning runs** in streams impacted by overfishing, pollution, or natural disaster, without the genetic risks of transferring eggs between watersheds.
- Authors recommend further replication to confirm consistency of these results across years.
