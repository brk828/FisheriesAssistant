# The Feeding Ecology of Juvenile Mullet (Mugilidae) in South-East African Estuaries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Mugilidae

## Executive Summary
This study by Blaber & Whitfield (1977) investigated ontogenetic dietary shifts in juveniles (10–59 mm standard length) of eleven [[Mugilidae]] species across seven south-east African estuaries (Msikaba, Mtentu, Impenjati, Umgeni, St. Lucia, Sodwana, and Kosi). All species spawn in coastal marine waters, with juveniles entering estuaries at approximately 10 mm standard length. Once in estuaries, juveniles undergo a consistent, rapid sequence of dietary transitions: from planktonic/migratory zooplankton feeding, to benthic zooplankton feeding, to meiobenthos consumption, and finally to microbenthos and sand-associated feeding characteristic of adults. The authors argue that estuaries — being shallower and calmer than the turbulent inshore marine environment of south-east Africa — provide the only conditions suitable for this transition from [[Planktonic Macrophagous Carnivory]] to [[Benthic Microphagous Omnivory]].

## Background & Description
- **Size range studied:** 10–59 mm standard length (juveniles only; fish >50 mm addressed in companion papers: Blaber 1976, 1977).
- **Species examined:** *Crenimugil crenilabis*, *Liza alata*, *Liza dumerili*, *Liza macrolepis*, *Liza tricuspidens*, *Mugil cephalus*, *Myxus capensis*, *Valamugil buchanani*, *V. cunnesius*, *V. robustus*, *V. seheli*.
- Identification of juveniles relied on the key of [[Van Der Elst And Wallace Key]] (1976).
- No distinct morphological descriptions provided beyond size-class groupings (5 mm increments) used to track dietary change; the paper is diet/behavior focused rather than morphometric.
- Sand grain ingestion frequency used as an indirect morphological/behavioral proxy for feeding mode transition (see Key Findings).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Life history strategy:** All species are marine spawners; juveniles recruit into estuaries at ~10 mm SL, consistent with the recruitment pattern described by [[Wallace And Van Der Elst 1975]].
- **Seasonality:** Most species recruit during spring and summer; *V. buchanani*, *V. cunnesius*, and *V. robustus* also recruited during winter months (May–July).
- **Microhabitat selection:** Juveniles of all species were captured exclusively in quiet water areas <1 m deep, indicating active habitat selection for shallow, low-energy estuarine zones.
- **Trophic ontogeny sequence:**
  1. Zooplankton (planktonic, e.g. Cladocera) → zooplankton associated with the benthos (10–15 mm)
  2. Benthic zooplankton → meiobenthos (Nematoda, Polychaeta, Oligochaeta, Harpacticoida) (10–20 mm)
  3. Meiobenthos → microbenthos and sand-associated particles (diatoms, algae, Foraminifera, Protozoa) (15–25 mm)
- **Exception:** *Myxus capensis* deviates from this pattern, undergoing an intermediate stage (15–45 mm) of surface-feeding on terrestrial insects, linked to its preference for freshwater upper-estuary reaches, before transitioning to microbenthos/sand feeding after 30 mm.
- **Diel feeding consideration:** Sampling was restricted to daylight hours, when migratory zooplankton (Calanoida, Cyclopoida, Ostracoda, Amphipoda, Isopoda, zoea larvae) are predominantly benthic, aligning prey availability with juvenile mullet feeding mode.
- **Interspecific competition avoidance:** Despite dietary overlap, competition is likely minimized because (1) shoals are single-species/single-length-class, (2) species enter estuaries at different times per spawning schedules, and (3) the shift to adult-type feeding occurs rapidly (within weeks) due to fast juvenile growth rates ([[De Silva And Perera 1976]]).
- **Biogeographic context:** The authors hypothesize that estuarine dependency for this feeding transition may be a regional phenomenon specific to south-east Africa's rough, narrow-shelf coastline; in areas like Mozambique with wider shelves and calmer seas, mullet may be less estuary-dependent.

## Key Findings
- **Sand grain ingestion (Fig. 2):** Sand was present in >90% of individuals of most species from 20 mm SL and 100% by 25 mm SL; *Myxus capensis* and *Liza macrolepis* lagged, reaching 100% sand presence only at 45 mm. Fish 10–15 mm largely lacked sand in gut contents (*M. cephalus*, *L. alata*, *L. dumerili* had none; *M. capensis* and *V. buchanani* had up to 10%).
- **Food item classification (Table 2):** 1493 individuals' stomach contents were categorized into four functional groups: Planktonic (P), Vertically Migrating (M), Meiobenthic (B), and Microbenthic/Inorganic (Bm). Notable meiobenthic taxa: Nematoda, Polychaeta, Oligochaeta, Harpacticoid Copepoda. Notable microbenthic/inorganic components: pennate/centric diatoms, unicellular/filamentous green algae, Cyanophyta, Foraminifera, flagellate Protozoa, particulate organic matter.
- **General dietary shift pattern (Fig. 3):** Six well-sampled species (*L. alata*, *L. dumerili*, *L. macrolepis*, *M. cephalus*, *M. capensis*, *V. buchanani*) showed two major transitions: planktonic/migratory → meiobenthos (10–20 mm), and meiobenthos → microbenthos (15–25 mm).
- **Rare species observations:** *C. crenilabis* and *V. cunnesius* (>25 mm) fed exclusively on microbenthos/sand; *V. robustus* and *V. seheli* (<15 mm) still contained migratory zooplankton; two *L. tricuspidens* (40–50 mm) fed on nematodes and isopods (meiobenthos), matching adult diet patterns reported in [[Blaber 1977]].
- **Comparative context:** The dietary transition sequence parallels that documented for *Mugil cephalus* in Japan ([[Suzuki 1965]]), suggesting a conserved developmental feeding strategy across the family.
- **Ecological hypothesis:** The authors propose that south-east African estuaries are essential nursery habitats enabling the shift from macrophagous planktonic carnivory to microphagous benthic omnivory, because the region's rough, high-energy inshore marine waters would prevent substrate/sand-associated feeding by small juveniles. This positions estuaries as non-substitutable developmental habitats rather than merely optional nursery grounds.
- **Broader implication:** Adult mullet (e.g., *M. cephalus*, *V. buchanani*) also occur in coastal marine waters, but the extent of their reliance on estuarine vs. marine feeding grounds remains unresolved, with the authors suggesting heavy estuarine dependency in southern Africa compared to regions with wider continental shelves (e.g., Mozambique).
