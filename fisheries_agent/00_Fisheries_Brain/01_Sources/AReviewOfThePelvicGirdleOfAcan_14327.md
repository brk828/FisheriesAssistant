# Acanthomorph Pelvic Girdle Anatomy and Intrarelationships

#ichthyology #marine-biology #acanthomorpha

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from the *Zoological Journal of the Linnean Society* (1992, 104: 209–242) presents a review of the pelvic girdle of [[Acanthomorpha]] by Stiassny and Moore. The authors use pelvic anatomy as a character system to test hypotheses of relationship among acanthomorph lineages, working from dissected and cleared specimens. They argue for the monophyly of the Acanthomorpha, [[Acanthopterygii]] and [[Percomorpha]], and propose that [[Holocentridae]] group with the remaining percomorphs. They also propose a subgroup of "higher percomorphs." The analysis is limited to pelvic data, which show heavy homoplasy and yield poor resolution when topological constraints are removed. The authors state that a far larger, multi-system dataset is needed.

The excerpt also includes the start of a separate 1992 paper on beetles (Pterogeniidae), which is outside the fish topic and is summarised only briefly below.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The pelvic girdle of acanthomorphs is structurally complex and has been under-described in phylogenetic work. Earlier studies focused on girdle position, spine presence and fin-ray counts rather than girdle morphology.
- **Terminology:** The authors follow the Potthoff (1980) scheme. The girdle has a central part (ossified pelvic cartilage), anterior and posterior processes, and four membrane-bone wings. The paper cites Potthoff's terms and the Winterbottom (1974) muscle nomenclature.
- **Methods:** Synapomorphies were used to define monophyletic groups. Character polarity was assessed by the [[Outgroup Method]]. Ontogenetic data were included where possible. Trees were generated with PAUP 3.0 using heuristic searches with stepwise addition, both constrained and unconstrained. Neoscopelus was the root taxon. Characters were coded as binary or three-state unordered.
- **Material:** Specimens were dissected under a stereomicroscope. Osteological material was cleared and double-stained (Dingerkus & Uhler, 1977) or prepared as dried skeletons. Alcohol specimens were used for musculature. Appendix 1 lists the material examined by family, with institutional abbreviations for AMNH, FMNH, MCZ, USNM, SU and YPM.
- **Taxonomic problem areas:** The authors discuss secondary reduction in [[Trachichthyiformes]] (stephanoberycoids), problematic [[Zeiformes]] (caproids as plesiomorphic, zeoids and plectognaths with modified girdles), and [[Lampridiformes]], which show extreme modification. Only *Lampris* was examined in detail.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document is a morphological and phylogenetic study rather than an ecological one, so habitat is treated only in passing. Several points bear on function and ecology:

- Extensor proprius muscle presence or absence is linked to swimming function. The authors suggest that absence may correlate with a bottom-dwelling habit or with fishes that lack a mechanism for neutral buoyancy, but they say this is untested.
- Harris (1938) showed that the extensor proprius raises the posteromedial fin border and so moves the fish downward.
- Pelvic spine and fin modifications are considered in functional terms, and reductions are treated as independent events.
- Pelvic girdle position (thoracic versus abdominal) varies among lineages and is linked to migration of the girdle during evolution.

## Key Findings

**1. Pelvic girdle halves (Character 1).** The plesiomorphic euteleost condition is loosely apposed plates. Acanthomorphs show a median overlap formed by bony expansions of the ischial processes. Overlap is present in polymixiids, basal paracanthopterygians (percopsids, aphredoderoids, *Sphenocephalus*) and reduced in most trachichthyiforms and berycids. Lampridiforms show none. A reinforced posterior sutural union is found in the holocentrid-plus-"higher percomorph" assemblage, which the authors interpret as evidence for its monophyly.

**2. Attachment to the postcleithrum (Character 2).** A ligamentous link to the distal postcleithrum, linked to anterior migration of the girdle, is treated as a synapomorphy of Acanthomorpha. Atherinomorphs show an autapomorphic attachment via a lateral process to the posteriormost proximal pleural rib.

**3. True pelvic spine.** A true pelvic spine is found only in atherinomorphs and percomorphs among extant acanthomorphs. The authors conclude that Acanthomorpha can be defined by dorsal and anal spines but not by pelvic spines, given the placement of Polymixiidae as the sister group to the rest of the clade. Absences in lampridiforms, pleuronectiforms and other groups are treated as independent reductions.

**4. Anterior pelvic processes (Character 4).** Spine-like anterior processes are a synapomorphy of Acanthopterygii. In percomorphs they are prong-like and ventrally displaced, which the authors treat as support for percomorph monophyly.

**5. Dorsal inclination and cleithral attachment (Character 5).** Most percomorphs have a girdle inclined dorsally and attached to the cleithrum or coracoid. This is taken as additional support for percomorph monophyly.

**6. Inter-pelvic ligament (Character 6).** A single inter-pelvic ligament is found only in holocentrids and "higher percomorphs." The authors use it as evidence for their monophyly.

**7. Autogenous lateral radials (Character 7).** Adult loss of lateral pelvic radials is a novelty synapomorphic for "higher percomorphs." Holocentrids retain an L-shaped lateral radial that defines the family (Zehren, 1979). The absences in atherinomorphs, some stephanoberycoids and lampridiforms are treated as independent losses.

**8. Pelvic cross-section (Character 8).** In "higher percomorphs," including caproids, scorpaenoids, percoids and labroids, the girdle has a distinctive cross-section, with internal wings horizontal or slightly inclined and external dorsal and ventral wings of approximately equal size. The authors treat this as further evidence of monophyly. Synbranchiforms lack a girdle, and dactylopteriforms, pleuronectiforms and gasterosteiforms were excluded from the summary cladograms because their girdles are too modified to place.

**Phylogenetic analysis results**
- Constrained analysis (trachichthyiform and zeiform monophyly enforced): two minimum-length trees, length 18, CI 0.611, RI 0.741. Figure 14A shows the strict consensus.
- Lampridiforms constrained near trachichthyiforms and berycids: eight minimum-length trees, length 20, CI 0.500, RI 0.667.
- Unconstrained analysis: 154 minimum-length trees, length 17, CI 0.647, RI 0.778. Resolution is poor and "little structure can be extracted."
- Lampridiforms placed at the base of Acanthomorpha is the main contrast with most previous authors, who place them near beryciforms. The authors present two alternative hypotheses (Figure 15A and 15B).
- The pelvic data show extensive homoplasy, interpreted as repeated reductions and reversals. The authors call for a complex matrix combining all relevant characters and taxa.

**Miscellaneous characters (not used in the cladistic analysis)**
- Extensor proprius muscle: absent in several bottom-dwelling or non-buoyancy-controlling groups listed; its distribution may be phylogenetic.
- Posterior processes: possibly phylogenetically informative within stomiids, serranids, holocentrids and others.
- Ventral wing: found in some perciforms, but not in any beryciform examined; elaborated in pomacanthids.
- Pelvic spine articulation (Mok & Shen, 1983): the authors judge its phylogenetic value questionable, because states are distributed across distantly related families.
- Scombrid "antero-dorsal plate": appears unique to Scombridae; other scombroids lack it.

**Beetle paper (not fish; included for completeness).** The same excerpt contains Burckhardt & Löbl (1992, pp. 243–291) on the Pterogeniidae of Indoaustralia. The family comprises 5 genera and 24 species. Three genera and 17 species are new, and one species is synonymised. A cladistic analysis of 23 morphological characters with *Sicacrypticus indicus* (Archeocrypticidae) as outgroup gave a single cladogram. Most species are restricted to insular tropical Asia and Oceania. None of the *Histanocerus* species occurs on both sides of [[Wallace's Line]].
