# DNA-Based Detection of Atlantic Cod Remains in Predatory Fish Stomach Contents

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gadidae

## Executive Summary

Rosel and Kocher (2002) developed and validated a DNA-based assay to detect partially digested [[Atlantic Cod]] (*Gadus morhua*) remains in the stomach contents of predatory fishes on [[Georges Bank]]. The assay targets a mitochondrial 16S rRNA segment using gadid-specific PCR primers followed by a cod-specific probe in a dot-blot hybridization step. It distinguishes cod from closely related [[Haddock]] and other prey, detects cod DNA after up to 12 hours of digestion in stomachs, and performed well on a blind test of 48 field samples and on 153 field samples overall. The authors position it as a tool for quantifying predation on cod for [[Predator-Prey Modeling]] and fishery management.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** Georges Bank is a shallow, well-mixed plateau in the western North Atlantic supporting a high-biomass ecosystem and historically important groundfish fisheries.
- **Problem:** Cod and haddock stocks on Georges Bank declined while dogfish and other species increased. Predation on juvenile and larval gadids by species such as [[Atlantic Mackerel]], [[Atlantic Herring]], and [[Silver Hake]] is considered a possible factor limiting recovery.
- **Limitations of existing methods:** Morphological identification of prey is difficult because prey are small and rapidly digested. Traditional immunoassays and serological methods require substantial sample quantities, are often species-specific, and may fail for young (larval) stages with low protein expression. Conventional PCR-based assays for haddock and cod have been unreliable on partially digested remains.
- **Design goals:** A sensitive, specific, high-throughput assay that could distinguish cod from haddock and be adapted to field use.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predator stomach samples** were collected during [[NOAA Coastal Ocean Program]] cruises on Georges Bank and frozen in liquid nitrogen.
- **Prey survey:** DNA from 20 prominent Georges Bank fish species was used to identify a target region, and specificity was tested against 23 fish species and eight invertebrate taxa (including cnidarians, molluscs, copepods, euphausiids, decapods, amphipods, and chaetognaths).
- **Digestion experiments** used mackerel fed larval and juvenile cod, with stomach and intestinal samples taken over times up to 12 hours. Some samples were collected from sand lance fed to predators.
- **Ecological implication:** Predators such as mackerel and herring may consume gadid early life stages, so detecting digested prey helps evaluate predation pressure on recruitment.

## Key Findings

**1. Target region and probe design**
- A portion of the mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene was sequenced from 20 fish species (Table 1).
- The primers yielded a variable region of 22 base pairs in which cod differed by a minimum of [[Sequence Divergence]] from all other fish examined.
- Gadid-specific primers amplify a short fragment; the sequence reported for the amplicon is 132 base pairs in the sensitivity tests.
- Nine of the 20 species were used to design the gadid primers and cod probe (marked with asterisks in Table 1).

**2. Specificity**
- All 23 fish species tested produced the expected PCR product with universal primers, confirming DNA quality.
- With gadid-specific primers, only cod and haddock products were produced among the fish tested; all cross-reactions with non-gadids were negative.
- Eight invertebrate taxa were tested. Their DNA amplified with universal primers, but the authors note that only the gadid-specific primers were tested against them, and cross-reactivity testing with invertebrates was limited.

**3. Sensitivity**
- Cod DNA was detected at a 1:1000 dilution in sand lance DNA (0.1 ng cod in 100 ng sand lance DNA).
- Dilutions of cod PCR product down to 1:1000 gave a detectable hybridization signal.

**4. Digestion time course**
- Cod larvae and juveniles were detected after digestion times up to 12 hours.
- Of 19 time-course samples, 13 were detected. Several failures occurred in intestinal samples, while stomach samples from the same time points were positive.
- The authors recommend collecting stomach rather than intestinal samples for detection of digested cod.

**5. Field validation**
- **Blind test:** Of 48 blind field samples, 15 were believed to contain cod, and 14 of these were positive. The assay correctly identified all but one sample; a sample containing plankton plus cod larvae gave no PCR product, possibly due to inhibition.
- Two samples identified in the field only as "cod or haddock" were negative for cod and were therefore interpreted as haddock.
- **Full application:** Of 153 field samples, 47 tested positive for cod. Field data suggested roughly 20–50% would contain cod, which the authors considered consistent with the results.

**6. Methodological lessons**
- Stomach contents must be fully homogenized before subsampling. Separate subsamples from the same stomach gave conflicting results when visible remains were selected and frozen separately.
- Of 20 faint-positive samples sequenced, eight showed mixed templates (cod and haddock), seven gave clean cod sequences, and five gave haddock sequences. This suggests haddock DNA can cross-hybridize and cause occasional false positives, at an estimated rate of about 7% of samples.

**7. Conclusions**
- The two-step PCR-plus-hybridization design excludes non-gadid DNA and reduces false positives.
- The assay can be used qualitatively to examine predation on cod and can be adapted for other prey such as haddock.
- Future work should clone faint-positive PCR products to test for trace cod DNA and examine individual variation in digestion and clearance rates.
