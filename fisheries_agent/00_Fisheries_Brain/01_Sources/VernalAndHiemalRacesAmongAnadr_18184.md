# Vernal and Hiemal Races among Anadromous Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #acipenseridae #petromyzontidae #cyprinidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This translated paper (originally by [[L. S. Berg]], 1934; translated by W. E. Ricker, 1959) reviews **seasonal races** within anadromous fishes: intraspecific groups that differ in the time they enter fresh water and the time they spawn. Berg calls the early-entering, same-year-spawning group *vernal* and the group that overwinters in the river before spawning *hiemal*, borrowing terms from cereal crops. The translator's preface notes that Berg's two-category scheme is too narrow, since later work has documented more than two runs in some rivers and almost every combination of arrival season, spawning season and freshwater residence time.

The review covers salmonids, sturgeons, lampreys and cyprinids from the Pacific, Atlantic, Caspian, Black, Baltic and Aral basins. Berg concludes that vernal and hiemal races are consistent, though not firmly fixed, and that they matter for fisheries management and fish culture.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** [[Anadromy]] is defined as migration from salt water to fresh water for spawning. The [[European Eel]] is cited as the reverse case (catadromous). Anadromous fishes are described as characteristic of temperate and some polar latitudes, and absent from the tropics.
- **Terminology:** The Russian *yarovoi* (vernal) and *ozimyi* (hiemal) are agricultural terms. The translator retained them to avoid confusion, because some "hiemal" races enter rivers in late spring or summer of the year before spawning. The word *rasa* ("race") carries no necessary taxonomic or genetic meaning.
- **Historical framing:** Berg's 1934 paper was the first attempt to gather scattered information on these groups. Later work, including [[Chinook Salmon]] runs on the Sacramento River and Briagin's four "races" of [[Russian Sturgeon]] in the Kura, has broadened the picture.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pacific salmon.** [[Chum Salmon]] in the Amur divide into summer and autumn runs. Summer keta enter in early July and spawn in mid-August. Autumn keta enter in mid-August and spawn from mid-September, and they travel much farther upstream, into the Zabaikalia region. Among [[Chinook Salmon]] in the Columbia, Rich and Holmes' marking experiments showed that eggs from spring fish reared in summer-run streams still returned as spring fish, and the reverse was also true.

**Atlantic salmon (semga and grilse).** In northern Russian rivers, [[Semga]] show a summer form that spawns the same autumn and an autumn form that overwinters in the river and spawns the following autumn. The [[Grilse]] (tinda) is a one-sea-winter fish. Among British and Scottish fish, spring salmon come into rivers from late summer, spawn the following winter, and can be distinguished from summer fish only by scale growth history.

**Brown trout and Caspian salmon.** [[Brown Trout]] (kumzha) in the Tweed and Ponoe show both races. The [[Caspian Salmon]] (*Salmo trutta caspius*) shows the clearest split in the Kura: a vernal race (lokh) and a hiemal race that travels farther upriver.

**Sturgeons.** Vernal and hiemal races are "developed extremely clearly" in acipenserids, and Berg argues this has not been recognized as parallel to salmonid patterns. Each species has a different balance between the two races by river. The [[Russian Sturgeon]] (osetr) is hiemal-dominant in the Volga and Ural but vernal-dominant in the Kura. [[Sevriuga]] is mainly vernal in the Ural, Volga and Kura. [[Beluga]] is hiemal in the Ural and Volga delta but mostly vernal in the Kura today. The [[Sharpnose Sturgeon]] is vernal-dominant in the Kura and hiemal-only in the Aral basin. The [[Sterlet]] has semi-anadromous races in the Volga delta and possibly in the Viatka.

**Lampreys and cyprinids.** The [[River Lamprey]] in the Neva has autumn and spring entries. Cyprinids that enter the Volga delta twice include the [[Vobla]], [[Bream]] and [[Carp]]. Their autumn fish are called "deep-hole" fish, and they run upstream earlier than the spring fish. The [[Cut-tooth]] (*Rutilus frisii*) in the Dnepr-Bug littoral runs in spring and autumn. Its Caspian subspecies, the kutum, peaks in spring.

**Other taxa.** Berg also lists [[Arctic Char]], [[Chekhon]], [[Aral Barbel]], [[Shemaya]], [[Issyk-Kul Osman]] and [[Pikeperch]] as having possible or documented races. Non-migratory forms (for example, freshwater trout and landlocked salmon) show that migratory habit itself is plastic.

## Key Findings

**Core contrasts between vernal and hiemal races (Berg's Conclusions):**

1. Hiemal races spend the coldest season in the river or just off its mouth. Vernal races enter at higher water temperatures.
2. Hiemal races enter a state of vegetative quiet, eating little or nothing and often lying in deep holes. Vernal races have a shorter quiet stage, if any.
3. Vernal races spawn in the same growing season they enter. Hiemal races spawn the following season or after a long interval.
4. Hiemal races usually mature and spawn earlier than vernal races.
5. Hiemal races usually travel farther upriver.
6. Hiemal fish are usually larger.
7. Hiemal races are usually more fecund.

**Quantitative examples:**

- **Amur keta:** Among 20 late summer keta caught August 24 to September 1, 1907, egg weight averaged 13.7% of body weight. Among 63 autumn keta taken at the same time, it averaged 5.6%.
- **Keta size and fecundity:** Mean male summer keta were 61 cm and autumn keta 65 cm (1907–1908 data). Summer keta carried about 2500 eggs and autumn keta about 3900.
- **Aleev's "transformation" experiment (White Sea):** A semga 59 cm long and 1.48 kg was marked in 1924 and recaptured October 11, 1925, at 67 cm and 3.15 kg. Berg rejects this as evidence, because the original fish was a zakroika (an autumn fish that had overwintered at the river mouth).
- **Kura vernal vs hiemal salmon (1915, Bankov fishery):** 49 female and 13 male lokh (vernal) were taken, against more than 20,000 hiemal fish. Vernal fish weigh up to 12 kg, while hiemal fish reach 51 kg.
- **Volga delta salmon (Derzhavin, 500 specimens):** Run lasted from August (5.8%) to December (0.2%). Peak was October (56.5%), with September 17.2% and November 20.2%. Mean weight was 8.7 kg (maximum 22 kg). The fish were hiemal.
- **Sex ratios:** Among grilse, the Niva and Teriberka produced only males. The Soiana had 9 females among 153 grilse. Voronia had 312 male and 9 female grilse. Grilse females were 19% in Norway, 17% in the Rhine, and about 50% in Scotland.
- **Scottish spring salmon:** The main run reaches up to 15 kg and runs February to early April.
- **Perch (Tiurin, Lake Chana):** The large fast-growing form was 83% female, which Tiurin interpreted as small perch transforming into large carnivorous fish.
- **Sevriuga (Volga, Grimm 1893, mean monthly catches in number):** May 6908 (35.2%), April 6457 (32.9%), June 4308 (22.0%), and August 602 (3.1%). Hiemal runs continue to November but are much lighter.
- **Osetr (Volga delta, mean monthly numbers):** April 4123 (24.5%), May 4829 (28.7%), June 3518 (20.9%), and July 2136 (12.7%).
- **Kura beluga (Ber & Danilevsky, 1829–1855, percentages):** Peak in October (28.6%) and September (14.0%). In the 1820s–1850s the hiemal beluga dominated the Kura, but by Berg's time the vernal race was the more frequently caught.
- **Kura sharpnose sturgeon (1841–1855):** Spring peak in March (31.6%), with October (22.7%) and November (15.4%) the autumn peaks.
- **Kutum (Kumbashinka River, 1925–1929, thousands):** Annual mean total 283.2 thousand, peaking in March–April.
- **Osman (Issyk-Kul):** Eggs number 5000–25,000. The summer form spawns June–July, while the winter form spawns from February (lakeshore) or in early April.
- **Sterlet (Viatka):** A 62 cm female taken June 8, 1929, had eggs in stage II–III, judged to be a hiemal fish spawning the following year.

**Berg's cereal analogy and vernalization:** Hiemal grains need a cold period before they can form heads. Vernal grains flower the same summer. Berg proposes that hiemal fish likewise need months in cold water before their eggs or milt mature. Lysenko's work showed that vernal and hiemal wheats differ in degree, and that cold treatment can convert hiemal wheat into a vernal-like form. Berg argues that a similar experimental change could be possible in fish.

**Practical significance:** Hiemal semga are larger and more valuable, but they cannot be caught with mature gonads in the lower river. Hatcheries must either collect fish from upper tributaries or hold them in ponds. Berg calls for experiments to test whether hiemal races can be made vernal.

**Caveats and open questions:** Berg states that vernal and hiemal races have no necessary taxonomic or genetic meaning, and that no undoubted natural transformation between races has been demonstrated. He notes that hybridization between races is unlikely to be impossible, and that the heredity of the races is unproven. He suggests seasonal races arose after the fish became anadromous, but this requires verification.
