# Acute Toxicity of Inorganics to Endangered Fish of the Green River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1995 study by [[Steven J. Hamilton]] (Yankton Field Research Station, National Biological Survey) measured the 96-hr acute toxicity of seven inorganic contaminants to early life stages of three federally endangered fishes of the [[Green River]]: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail]]. Tests used reconstituted middle Green River water. Toxicity values were compared with environmental concentrations measured in [[Ashley Creek]], which receives [[Irrigation Drainage]] water. The study found moderate hazard for boron, selenate, and selenite; low hazard for uranium and vanadium; and a high hazard for zinc and a moderate hazard for selenium, with adverse effects on these fish possible.

## Background & Description

The Green River provides habitat for three endangered species listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. Spawning areas for Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker are limited, and the critical life stages extend from the egg through the first year. Young fish use shoreline embayments near the mouth of Ashley Creek and the [[Stewart Lake]] outlet, where irrigation return flows are present. Little information was available on the distribution, abundance, or habitat use of Bonytail.

Following the discovery of contaminated irrigation drainage in California's [[San Joaquin Valley]] in 1982, the [[Department of the Interior]] began screening irrigation projects in western states for water-quality problems. The Green River system was selected because irrigation returns reach a national wildlife refuge and may affect migratory birds and endangered species. An earlier drainwater investigation reported selenium concentrations up to six times the 20 µg/L acute criterion at several sites, and also found elevated boron, uranium, vanadium, and zinc in water, sediment, and tissue.

Eyed eggs were obtained from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], shipped to the Yankton station, and reared at 23 ± 2°C. Tests followed ASTM (1989) static acute methods: groups of 10 fish were exposed to a geometric series of 6–8 nominal concentrations plus a control for 96 hours at 25 ± 1°C. Seven compounds were tested: boric acid, lithium chloride, sodium selenate, sodium selenite, sodium vanadate, uranyl nitrate, and zinc chloride. LC50 values were calculated with the moving average or Trimmed Spearman-Karber methods, and ranks were compared with the Friedman test.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Receiving water:** Ashley Creek and the Stewart Lake outlet carry irrigation drainage into the Green River nursery habitat of young Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker.
- **Hazard framework:** Hazard was assessed by comparing the biological effects concentration (96-hr LC50) with the expected environmental concentration (EEC). Margins of uncertainty below 100 indicate high potential hazard; those above 1000 indicate low potential hazard.
- **Life-stage context:** Swimup fry are the most vulnerable stage for several contaminants, and the narrow habitat use of these species increases the concern.
- **Recovery constraints:** The authors note that the absence of recruitment to Bonytail populations in the Green River, along with habitat changes, may slow recovery. They suggest that inorganic contaminants from irrigation could be a further obstacle.
- **Mixtures:** Inorganics can act additively or synergistically at concentrations below individual toxic thresholds. Dietary exposure was also noted as a relevant route for selenium and vanadium.

## Key Findings

**Toxicity ranking:**
- Across all species and life stages, the rank order from most to least toxic was: vanadium ≈ zinc > selenite ≈ lithium ≈ uranium > selenate > boron.
- No significant difference was found among the three species' sensitivity to the seven inorganics when geometric means were compared at the species level.

**Life-stage differences:**
- Colorado Squawfish were 2.5 times more sensitive to selenate and selenite at the swimup stage than at older stages.
- Razorback Sucker showed equal sensitivity across life stages for most compounds, but older stages were more sensitive to selenate.
- Bonytail showed equal sensitivity to selenite but was five times more sensitive to selenate at the swimup stage.
- Swimup fry were generally more sensitive to boron, lithium, selenite, and zinc. Juveniles were more sensitive to vanadium.
- Selenite was always more toxic than selenate.

**Uranium anomaly:** The LC50 and 95% confidence interval were identical for all species and life stages because all test results were the same across tested concentrations (13–470 mg/L).

**Hazard assessment in Ashley Creek:**
- Moderate hazard ratios for boron, selenate, and selenite.
- Low hazard ratios for uranium and vanadium.
- High hazard ratio for lithium.
- The authors concluded that boron, selenium, and zinc in Ashley Creek may adversely affect young endangered fish. Zinc posed a high hazard and selenium a moderate hazard.

**Comparisons with other studies:**
- Vanadium toxicity to the three species fell within reported ranges. Several studies showed greater sensitivity in larger or older fish, which is unusual for inorganics.
- Zinc toxicity decreased with age, consistent with previous work, and was influenced by water hardness and pH.
- Selenate was less toxic than selenite, a pattern seen in earlier salmonid studies.
- Lithium appears to be about as toxic as selenite and uranium, particularly to fry, but little literature exists.
- Uranium toxicity is strongly affected by water hardness.
- Boron LC50 values in the study were within the wide range reported in the literature.

**Conclusions:** The margins of uncertainty for boron, zinc, and selenium, based on acute toxicity, suggest these inorganics may be adversely affecting young Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker, and Bonytail in Ashley Creek. Further research on chronic and dietary exposure, and on mixture effects, was recommended.
