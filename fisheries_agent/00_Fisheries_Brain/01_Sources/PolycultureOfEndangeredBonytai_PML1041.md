# Bonytail–Razorback Sucker Polyculture in Recirculated Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), two federally endangered Colorado River fishes, can be reared together in a [[Recirculating Aquaculture System]] without harming either species. Both fishes are propagated as part of [[Recovery Plan]] efforts coordinated by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. The 120-day experiment at the [[Mora National Fish Hatchery]] used a 3 × 2 factorial design: three razorback sucker densities (0, 2, or 5 per 432 L tank) crossed with two bonytail size classes. Polyculture significantly reduced tank cleaning labor, did not reduce bonytail growth, and produced no razorback sucker mortality. Small bonytails reared with two razorback suckers per tank had lower survival, so the authors judged polyculture feasible but in need of further refinement.

## Background & Description

Both species are endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and were listed as endangered (bonytail in 1980; razorback sucker in 1991). Their declines have been attributed to habitat modification and degradation. Recovery plans call for captive propagation and population augmentation, which has increased demand for improved husbandry, especially in water-reuse systems where water conservation matters.

Scant biological information exists for either species. Both show ontogenetic shifts in feeding:

- **Bonytail:** Feeding progresses from benthic invertebrate foraging in juveniles to midwater, planktonic, and surface feeding; adults eat aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, plant matter, and algae.
- **Razorback sucker:** Juveniles move from midwater planktonic and benthic feeding to primarily benthic feeding (invertebrates, detritus, algae).

These differences suggest resource partitioning, which the authors expected to reduce negative interspecific interactions in polyculture. Bonytails are typically co-cultured with razorback suckers at a density ratio of 2:1 to 5:1 (bonytail : razorback sucker). The authors reported that razorback suckers were used because of their perceived ability to resuspend and reprocess settled solids, reducing manual tank cleaning. No prior composite culture of these two species in recirculating water had been documented.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Polyculture can improve culture efficiency, but outcomes vary with species combinations and densities. Documented effects include:

- Reduced size variation and improved growth in some systems, through suppression of [[Size Hierarchy]] effects.
- Negative outcomes in other systems, including poorer fish condition, decreased growth, and high mortality.

Most polyculture evidence comes from pond and cage systems, where benthic fish stir sediments to increase nutrient availability and phytoplankton production. In this study the system was a clear-water recirculating setup where phytoplankton blooms are undesirable. Water currents kept solids suspended and moved them to filtration units. Solids that settled had to be removed manually; otherwise they would break down into harmful compounds such as ammonia and nitrite.

Study conditions:

- **Tanks:** 24 circular fiberglass tanks (432 L, 91.4 cm diameter, 81.3 cm deep), covered and fitted with an internal standpipe.
- **Water:** Filtered recirculating water at about 7.5 L/min (about 25 exchanges/day). Estimated daily makeup water from well water was 15%.
- **Filtration:** Rotating drum microscreen filter, propeller-wash bead filter for nitrification, and boiler-assisted heat exchanger.
- **Temperature:** 21.9–22.4 °C, kept low as an energy-conservation measure.
- **Photoperiod:** 14.5 h light in late June, shortening to 11 h light in late October.
- **Diet:** Commercial sinking shrimp production feed (2.2 mm; 40% protein, 15% lipid), fed to satiation four times daily.

## Key Findings

**Study design and fish**

- Study period: 26 June to 24 October 2002 (120 days).
- Juvenile bonytails (N = 240, 2000 year-class) in two size classes:
  - Small: 17.6 ± 0.5 g; 127.2 ± 1.1 mm TL.
  - Large: 43.0 ± 1.2 g; 166.9 ± 1.7 mm TL.
- Razorback suckers: 56 captive-reared fish (1999 year-class), 269.8 ± 10.5 g and 285.4 ± 3.4 mm TL.
- Design: 3 × 2 factorial with four replicates. Each size class was stocked at 10 bonytails per tank, with twelve tanks per size class.

**Tank cleaning labor**

- Tank cleaning effort, measured as the number of required siphoning events per replicate, was significantly lower in polyculture tanks than in monoculture tanks.
- The required frequency of standpipe screen cleaning did not differ between monoculture and polyculture, which addressed the concern that razorback suckers might add labor.
- The authors noted that direct waste weighing would have given more quantifiable results and recommended it for future work.

**Survival**

- Razorback sucker survival averaged 100%, with no mortality in any treatment.
- Bonytail survival averaged 96%.
- Small bonytails at the low razorback sucker density (2 per tank) had significantly lower survival (83%) than all other treatments (range 98–100%). The interaction effect was P = 0.0435.
- Bonytail survival in this study was comparable to the 120-day survival (mean 94%) of monoculture bonytails in a previous study (Henne et al. 2006).

**Growth**

- Razorback sucker density did not significantly affect bonytail relative growth rate, specific growth rate, or final size in either size class.
- Razorback suckers at 2 per tank had significantly greater growth rates and final weight than those at 5 per tank.
- Relative and specific growth rates were significantly higher for small bonytails (0.90% and 0.59%) than for large bonytails (0.31% and 0.26%).
- Bonytail size did not significantly affect razorback sucker growth or final size.
- Small bonytail SGR (0.59%) and body metrics were consistent with monoculture values in Henne et al. (2006). Growth was generally low, which the authors attributed to suboptimal temperatures.

**Size variability**

- Size variability was significantly lower for small bonytails in polyculture, but growth did not increase relative to monoculture.
- No differences between monoculture and polyculture were found for large bonytails.

**Conclusions**

The authors concluded that co-culturing bonytails and razorback suckers:

1. Reduces labor by decreasing how often manual solid-waste removal is needed.
2. Does not reduce bonytail growth, though size variation can be lowered.
3. Does not appear to affect large bonytail survival, but small bonytail survival may depend on razorback sucker density, which requires further study.
4. Shows no measurable adverse effect on razorback sucker growth or survival.

Because bonytails are reared to supplement wild populations through small-scale releases, the authors argued that the lack of increased growth should not be viewed as negative. They concluded that polyculture could raise total production of both species and called the study a first step toward defining polyculture requirements. Future work should identify minimum bonytail size for polyculture, optimize razorback sucker density to protect small bonytail survival, and quantify waste removal directly.
