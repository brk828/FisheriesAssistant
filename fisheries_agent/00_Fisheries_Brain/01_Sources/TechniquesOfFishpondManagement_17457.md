# Techniques of Fishpond Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1943 U.S. Department of Agriculture publication (Miscellaneous Publication No. 528), written by Lawrence V. Compton of the Soil Conservation Service, reviews the literature on farm fishpond management and presents management methods tested mainly in the Southeastern United States. Its core argument is that a farm pond can be managed like a pasture: fertilization raises the plankton base, which supports a managed food chain of forage fish and carnivorous game fish. The main recommendation is a balanced stocking of [[Bluegill]] and [[Largemouth Bass]], with rooted aquatic plants removed and fertilizer applied at regular intervals. The paper was issued in wartime, and it frames farm ponds as a source of protein to supplement meat and marine fish supplies.

## Background & Description

The Foreword, signed by H. H. Bennett, Chief of the [[Soil Conservation Service]], places pond management within whole-farm soil and water conservation. It states that managed ponds on American farms have a potential production of 100 million pounds of palatable fish annually.

The introduction notes that U.S. pond culture had been local and sporadic, and that early work focused almost entirely on [[Carp]]. Recent investigations at the [[Alabama Agricultural Experiment Station]] established successful methods for raising largemouth bass, bluegill and other food fish. Pond culture had a longer history abroad:

- **France:** farmers raised fish on flooded grain fields as part of crop rotation since the Middle Ages.
- **Poland (1934):** about 185,000 acres of ponds yielded an estimated 22 million pounds of fish.
- **Philippines (1940):** 141,564 acres of ponds raised [[Milkfish]], with an estimated annual yield of 98 million pounds, and an industry worth not less than 50 million pesos.

The author notes that the most productive ponds have no rooted aquatic plants and are stocked with few species, which runs against common fish-culture ideas.

Terminology is also defined. "Production" is the annual amount produced after stocking, "standing crop" is the amount present at any time, and "harvestable crop" is the amount that can be removed without harming breeding stock. Output is reported as weight per acre of surface area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond design and size.** Ponds from 1/4 to 70 acres have been managed successfully. A pond of about 1/2 acre is the practical minimum for one family. Fertilized, it carries about 250 pounds of fish and yields about 100 pounds by hook and line per year. Depth should be at least 6 feet at the deepest point, with steep shore slopes to about 3 feet to discourage shore vegetation. Ponds with small or no overflow are the most economical, and continuously muddy ponds have low natural productivity.

**Aquatic plants.** Rooted plants such as cattails, waterlilies, [[Pondweed]] species and [[Spatterdock]] must not be allowed to establish. They tie up nutrients in summer and release them in winter, when fish grow little. Submerged plants can be controlled by applying fertilizer at monthly intervals during winter. Excessive decay can deplete dissolved oxygen, so fertilization should stop once most plants are decaying. [[Filamentous Algae]] harbor mosquitoes and interfere with fishing, and their growth signals that fertilization should end.

**Food chain.** Phytoplankton (algae, which tint the water and form water bloom) is the basic organic resource. Zooplankton (protozoans, rotifers, crustaceans) feeds on it. Insect larvae link plankton to fish. The text describes a chain in which the insect eats plankton, the [[Bluegill]] eats the insect, and the bass eats the bluegill. Table 1 lists stomach contents from Alabama ponds. Insect larvae (midges, caddisflies, dragonfly nymphs) dominate the bluegill diet. Young bass switch to fish when they reach about 2 ounces.

**Feeding guilds.** [[Golden Shiner]] and [[Goldfish]] feed mainly on plankton. Bluegill and [[Bullhead Catfish]] are largely insectivorous. Largemouth bass and [[White Crappie]] are primarily carnivorous. Cannibalism is described as normal in balanced populations. Forage fish should be species that become acceptable pan fish when large. A large golden shiner is not edible, while a large bluegill is "a fine pan fish."

**Nutrients and oxygen.** Algae convert dissolved carbon dioxide, water and minerals into organic matter. Silt reduces productivity by limiting light and smothering bottom organisms. Bacteria decompose dead organic matter into compounds usable by plants. Manure and organic decay can deplete dissolved oxygen in warm, still water, which can lead to fish suffocation.

## Key Findings

**Production by fish guild (fertilized southeastern ponds, annual, per acre):**
- Plankton feeders (e.g., golden shiners): 750 to 1,100 pounds
- Insect feeders (e.g., bluegill, bullhead): 500 to 600 pounds
- Carnivores (e.g., largemouth bass, white crappie): 150 to 200 pounds

**Population size and growth.** Stocking density controls the size of fish, not the pond's carrying capacity. In one Alabama experiment, fingerling bluegill were stocked at 6,400, 3,200 and 1,600 per acre. After one year each pond held about 300 pounds per acre, with average weights of 0.8, 1.5 and 3.8 ounces. Another pair of ponds, stocked at 180,000 and 1,500 per acre, produced 280 and 320 pounds per acre, with average weights of 0.025 ounce and slightly over 3 ounces. Large populations produced small fish in every experiment.

**Illinois artificial lakes.** Nine lakes totaling 50 acres had an average standing crop of 600 pounds per acre and 46 species. Ten species made up 96.5 percent of the crop. Rough and forage fish (red-mouth buffalo, gizzard shad, carp) made up 80.9 percent, while desirable species totaled 15.6 percent, or only 93 pounds per acre.

**Natural fertility.** Nonfertilized Alabama ponds supported 40 to 200 pounds of pan and game fish per acre, closely tied to soil fertility. Michigan glacial lakes of low fertility had a standing crop of 92 pounds per acre, of which 84 pounds were game and pan fish.

**Optimal stocking (balanced populations).** Balanced ponds contain about one-third of their weight in carnivores and two-thirds in forage fish (carnivore-to-forage ratio 1:2 to 1:3.5). Stocking rates for a fertilized acre are about 1,500 [[Bluegill]] and 100 [[Largemouth Bass]] fingerlings. Experiments found that bluegill alone overpopulate, and that bluegill with bass gave the best results. Three per-acre combinations were recommended:
- Bluegill and largemouth bass (nonfertilized: 400 bluegill, 80 bass; fertilized: 1,500 bluegill, 100 bass)
- Bluegill, white crappie and largemouth bass (fertilized: 1,500 bluegill, 75 bass, 25 crappie)
- Bluegill, bullhead catfish and largemouth bass (fertilized: 1,200 bluegill, 75 catfish, 100 bass)

**Fertilization.** Europe reported production increases of 28 to 300 percent from fertilizers. Plankton output increases in direct proportion to fish output, so doubling plankton roughly doubles fish. The most efficient plankton production occurred in distilled-water cultures at a 4:1:1 ratio of N, P and K (ppm). Light, frequent applications worked better than heavy, infrequent ones. Ammonia nitrogen performed better than nitrate, but ammonium sulfate can cause acidity in some waters, which requires lime. In Alabama trials, superphosphate plus sodium nitrate, potassium chloride and calcium carbonate produced 330 pounds of fish per acre (plankton 24.4 per liter), compared with 90 pounds in unfertilized ponds (plankton 4.3).

**Practical mixtures.** Per acre, one application of 100 pounds of 6-8-4 mixture plus 10 pounds of sodium nitrate (neutral or acid waters) or sulfate of ammonia (alkaline waters) is recommended. Unmixed alternatives are 40 pounds of sulfate of ammonia, 60 pounds of 16 percent superphosphate and 5 pounds of muriate of potash, with 15 pounds of ground limestone in neutral or acid water. Applications are made weekly, and the bottom visible at 18 inches signals the need for more fertilizer. Total applications run 8 to 14 per season, at an annual cost of $11 to $20 per acre.

**Organic fertilizers.** Commercial fertilizers are preferred. Organic fertilizers are less effective: fish production may double, but plankton increases only slightly. The reasons given are slow bacterial release, consumption of organic matter by insect larvae and zooplankton, and direct eating of seed meal by fish. In an Iowa trial, soybean meal increased plankton by 681 percent and produced the best bass fingerling production at 700 pounds per acre. Manure is low in phosphorus, and legume hays decay quickly. Organic-material recommendations are provisional, and the author notes the effects on mosquitoes and filamentous algae are untested.

**Disease.** Farm manure carries risks of liver flukes and Bang's disease, so manured ponds should not supply stock water where these occur.

**Eradication.** Where ponds cannot be drained, [[Rotenone]] can kill unwanted fish. It is applied at 0.25 ppm using 5 percent derris root, and ponds should not be restocked for at least a month. Use requires State fish and game approval. Draining and restocking is described as the fastest method for old ponds with unwanted populations.

**Harvest.** Ponds stocked in autumn with bluegill and bass should yield pan-size fish in one year. Bass should not be fished until after spawning. At carrying capacity, about 80 percent of the standing crop is of usable size, and about 50 percent of pan-size fish can be removed by hook and line within a year. Good fishing is described as self-regulating, because harvesting reduces competition among remaining fish.

**Obtaining fish.** Fish come from Federal and State hatcheries. Applications to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service are generally due around June 1. Southern deliveries of bass and bluegill fingerlings typically occur in autumn, and northern deliveries of bluegill fingerlings and bass fry occur in June or July.
