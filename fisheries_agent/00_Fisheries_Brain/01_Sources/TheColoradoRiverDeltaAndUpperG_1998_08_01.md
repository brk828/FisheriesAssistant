## Executive Summary

This document is **not a primary fish-science research paper**, but a **policy/funding proposal** submitted by the [[Pacific Institute]] to the National Fish and Wildlife Foundation, seeking support for the "[[Colorado River Delta and Upper Gulf of California Restoration Project]]." The proposal addresses habitat degradation in the [[Colorado River Delta]]-[[Upper Gulf of California]] ecoregion, a critical desert wetland system whose ecological decline stems from decades of upstream water diversion and development on the Colorado River. While it does not provide species-level morphological or taxonomic data, it identifies the delta-upper gulf system as the **largest and most critical complex of desert wetlands in North America**, proposed for designation as a [[Ramsar Site]] due to its importance for migratory waterfowl, shorebirds, and (implicitly) aquatic/fish fauna. The project's stated goals are to identify restoration opportunities, develop viable water-reallocation strategies, and convene binational stakeholders (the [[Research Advisory Committee]]) to address ecosystem and socio-economic tradeoffs, including those affecting the [[Salton Sea]] and the [[Cienega de Santa Clara]] wetland.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Although species-specific ecological data is absent, the proposal outlines the **broader aquatic/wetland habitat context** relevant to fish and associated fauna in the region:

- **Habitat type**: Desert wetlands, delta estuary, riparian corridor, and agricultural drainage systems along the lower [[Colorado River]] and its terminus in the [[Gulf of California]].
- **Hydrological stressors**: Dramatically reduced natural flow reaching the delta due to upstream water development (damming, diversion, agricultural and urban consumption), degrading habitat for fish, birds, and plants.
- **Key habitat nodes**:
  - The **[[Cienega de Santa Clara]]** — a wetland sustained partly by agricultural drainage and desalination plant discharge (Yuma desalination plant), highlighted as ecologically significant and water-supply-sensitive.
  - The **[[Salton Sea]]** — described as serving competing ecological (habitat), agricultural (drainage sump), and recreational functions; connected hydrologically to the delta system via the **New River** and Mexicali wastewater flows.
  - The **[[Upper Gulf of California-Colorado River Delta Biosphere Reserve]]** — a protected area administered jointly by Mexico's [[SEMARNAP]] and [[INE]] (National Institute of Ecology), indicating formal recognition of the region's ecological value.
- **Conservation status**: The delta-upper gulf ecosystem "supports a variety of endangered species" (unspecified in text, though the region is historically known for taxa such as the [[Vaquita]] and [[Totoaba]], not explicitly named in this document).
- **Migratory significance**: Proposed Ramsar designation is based on importance to migratory waterfowl and shorebirds, implying a broader trophic network supported by estuarine and wetland productivity, which would include fish populations as a prey/food-web base.

## Key Findings

- The Colorado River delta-upper gulf ecoregion is identified as the **largest and most critical series of desert wetlands in North America**, despite severe hydrological alteration.
- Restoration is framed as requiring **binational cooperation** (US-Mexico), given the transboundary nature of water supply, the 1944 US-Mexico Water Treaty, and proposed "environmental Minute" amendments.
- A dedicated **Colorado River Delta Region Water Balance** study (joint Pacific Institute–SEMARNAP–INE project) was underway to quantify cross-boundary water supply and demand, essential for identifying water that could be reallocated for **ecological restoration** purposes.
- The **Salton Sea Assessment** component explicitly evaluates competing values for the Sea — ecosystem habitat, agricultural drainage sump, and recreational use — and its interconnection with delta restoration via wastewater flow management.
- Restoration strategies under consideration included: modified flood-water management, urban wastewater reuse, agricultural drainage management, and wetland reconstruction, alongside economic cost-benefit analysis of water reallocation.
- The project emphasizes that ecosystem restoration and **socio-economic benefits for water-dependent communities** are not mutually exclusive, framing habitat restoration as compatible with sustainable regional water management.
- No quantitative fish population, catch, or species-diversity data are presented; the document is a **planning/funding instrument**, with actual scientific/ecological findings deferred to future deliverables (Salton Sea Assessment, Water Balance study, and Integrated Report, targeted for 1998–1999 publication).
