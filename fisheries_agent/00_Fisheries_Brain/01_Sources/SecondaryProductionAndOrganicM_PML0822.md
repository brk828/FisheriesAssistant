# Secondary Production and Organic Matter Processing by Collector Macroinvertebrates in a Desert Stream

#benthic-macroinvertebrates #Ephemeroptera #Diptera #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study estimates the annual secondary production of [[Collector Macroinvertebrates]] in [[Sycamore Creek]], a hot-desert stream in the [[Sonoran Desert]] of Arizona, and evaluates what that production means for [[Desert Stream Ecosystem]] function. Using the [[Size-Frequency Method]] on 1979 sampling data, collector secondary production is 135 g dry mass m⁻² yr⁻¹, one of the highest values reported for streams. The authors attribute this to a long growing season, continuous reproduction, rapid life cycles, and small body size. Laboratory [[Bioenergetics]] experiments show that collectors ingest far more food than they assimilate, so ingestion exceeds [[Gross Primary Production]]. The authors conclude that collectors drive rapid turnover of [[Fine Particulate Organic Matter]] (FPOM) through [[Fecal Reingestion]].

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a gap: annual macroinvertebrate secondary production had not previously been measured in Sonoran Desert streams. Published values for other regions vary widely (see Ecological & Environmental Context). The authors argue that even moderate production rates can significantly affect organic matter processing and nutrient cycling. They therefore place their production estimates within the broader stream ecosystem.

**Study site.** Sycamore Creek (a tributary of the [[Verde River]]) drains a 505 km² watershed with elevations from 427 to 2,140 m. At the study reach (650 m), the channel is wide, sandy, and relatively unshaded. Mean depth at low summer flow is 7 cm and mean velocity is about 15 cm/s. Precipitation is 40 cm/yr and potential evaporation is 300 cm/yr. In summer, discharge is about 0.03 m³/s and the stream flows in and out of its coarse substrate, forming discontinuous reaches. Flash floods are common, especially in late summer.

**Biota.** Aquatic insects dominate the benthic community. The predominant fish are [[Agosia Chrysogaster]] (longfin dace) and [[Pantosteus Clarki]] (Gila Mountain sucker), the latter abundant only in unusually wet years. Fish were not part of the production or energetics measurements.

**Water chemistry.** The water is moderately hard calcium and magnesium bicarbonate water, with specific conductance near 500 µS/cm and total alkalinity averaging 250 mg/L as CaCO₃. Summer pH varies daily between 7 and 8. Dissolved phosphorus is generally above 50 µg/L, and NO₃-N usually exceeds 100 µg/L. Outside floods, suspended solids are low and the water is very clear. Mean summer daily temperature is 20–25 °C.

**Methods.** Benthic samples were taken on 15 dates (about twice monthly) from 1 April to 1 November 1979, using an 80 cm² core to 10 cm depth. Samples were elutriated through a 270 µm sieve, preserved, and sorted. Five collector taxa, representing 85% of collector biomass and numbers, were measured for body length. Production of the remaining collectors was estimated by applying the average P/B ratio of the five major taxa. Energy budgets used the relations A = I − E, A = P + R, and P = G + Ex. Ingestion was not measured directly for small collectors, whose feces were indistinguishable from food, so it was calculated from the first two equations. Respiration was measured by micro-Winkler oxygen determination, and growth was estimated from body-length changes over 3-day periods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Regional comparison.** Annual collector production in Sycamore Creek (135 g m⁻² yr⁻¹) is exceeded only by the Speed River, Ontario (240 g m⁻² yr⁻¹). The table below lists the comparison values reported in the paper.

| Stream | Trophic category | Production (g m⁻² yr⁻¹) |
|---|---|---|
| Speed River, Ontario | All benthos | 240 |
| Sycamore Creek, Arizona | Collectors | 135 |
| Middle Oconee River, Georgia | Primary consumers | 56 |
| River Thames, England | Primary consumers | 21.4 |
| Factory Brook, Massachusetts | Predators | 3.5 |
| Bear Brook, New Hampshire | All benthos | 12.2 |

Note: the Speed River value is for all benthos, and the Thames and Oconee values are for specific trophic categories (as reported). The comparison is therefore approximate.

**Species-level comparison.** Annual production of *Baetis quilleri* in Sycamore Creek (21.9 g m⁻²) is roughly ten times greater than the values reported for cooler temperate streams: *B. vagans* in Valley Creek, Minnesota (2.1 g m⁻²), *B. bicaudatus* in the Logan River, Utah (1.4 g m⁻²), and *B. rhodani* in Czechoslovakian trout streams (0.9 g m⁻²).

**Hydrology and disturbance.** Summer floods are the main source of year-to-year variation. The stream experiences an average of two flash floods per year, but over the previous 15 years the number ranged from none to nine. Macroinvertebrate standing stocks recover within about 24 weeks. A year with several evenly spaced floods could markedly lower annual productivity. In August 1979, a catastrophic flood removed more than 95% of the biomass.

**Food resources.** Gross primary production in summer averages 8.5 g m⁻² d⁻¹ (Busch and Fisher 1981). The dominant primary producer, *Cladophora glomerata*, is unavailable to collectors until it dies and enters the detritus pool. Ecosystem respiration in the stream is large, and macroinvertebrate respiration accounts for only 2% of it. Total organic matter input may approach 1 kg m⁻² yr⁻¹. FPOM makes up 60–90% of organic matter (Gray 1981).

**Community structure.** Collectors dominate the macroinvertebrate community. Noncollectors (beetles and hemipterans, many of which are predators) make up about 15% of invertebrate standing stock but were excluded from the production estimate. Shredders are virtually absent, and large particulate organic matter is scarce.

## Key Findings

**1. High collector secondary production.** Annual collector production is 135 g dry mass m⁻² yr⁻¹ at a mean standing stock of 2.98 g m⁻². The production-to-biomass ratio is about 45 for the total collector fauna and 63–75 for the mayflies and chironomids. Turnover time for mayflies and chironomids is about 3 days, and for the total collector fauna it is 4.7 days.

**2. Taxon contributions.** The three small mayfly taxa made up about 25% of collector biomass but contributed about 37% of collector production. *Cryptolabis* sp. (a tipulid) made up 55% of biomass but contributed only 3% of production, because its generation time (22 days) is about twice that of the dominant mayflies and chironomids (10–13 days).

**3. Drivers of high production.** The authors attribute high production to:
- a long growing season (water temperature from 5 °C in winter to 33 °C in summer, with afternoon warming to 10–15 °C even on cold winter days);
- continuous reproduction;
- multivoltinism, with predominant mayflies and chironomids completing egg-to-adult development in 1–2 weeks ([[Multivoltinism]]);
- small size at maturity, which the authors suggest is an adaptation to an uncertain environment of periodic drying and flooding.

**4. Individual energetics.** Metabolic parameters for *Baetis quilleri*, *Tribelos* sp. and *Physa virgata* all increase with temperature over the measured range. Respiration of *B. quilleri* and *Tribelos* sp. at 20–25 °C ranged from 4.1 to 14.9 µg AFDM mg⁻¹ h⁻¹. *B. quilleri* had the highest temperature dependence, with growth Q₁₀ values of 6.1–6.4 between 20 and 25 °C. At 25 °C both *B. quilleri* and *Tribelos* sp. could complete larval development in the laboratory within 10–13 days.

**5. Low assimilation, high ingestion.** At higher temperatures, *B. quilleri* and *Tribelos* sp. ingested an amount of food equal to their body mass every 4–6 hours. [[Assimilation Efficiency]] was consistently low on standard food (7–15%). The authors interpret this as a viable strategy: with abundant but low-quality detritus (10–100 times collector biomass), it is energetically cheaper to feed continuously and assimilate only the most labile fraction.

**6. Gross growth efficiency.** At 25 °C, [[Gross Growth Efficiency]] ranged from 6.2% (*B. quilleri*, size-class I) to 7.8% (*Physa virgata*, *Tribelos* sp.). At lower temperatures it ranged from 1.9% to 11.7%. The authors state that one unit of production requires about 10 units of ingested food, nearly 90% of which is released as feces.

**7. Ingestion exceeds primary production.** At mean standing stock (3 g m⁻²), collectors ingest about 12.6 g m⁻² d⁻¹, which is 4.2 times their body mass per day. Ingestion is about 1.6 times summer gross primary production (8.5 g m⁻² d⁻¹). Collectors therefore consume organic matter faster than the ecosystem produces it.

**8. Post-flood dynamics.** During the 2-month recovery after the August 1979 flood, gross primary production rose from 0 to 7.4 g m⁻² d⁻¹, and insect biomass rose from 0 to 10.4 g m⁻². Collectors ingested 2.5 to 6.2 times gross primary production during this recovery, far above the annual average. By day 20 after flooding, collectors were ingesting about 3% of the total organic matter present each day. Use of new FPOM is even lower than gross primary production implies, because about half of production during recovery went into algal standing crop.

**9. Fecal reingestion and turnover.** Because insufficient organic matter is available from other sources, the authors conclude that collectors must reingest fecal material. The mean time between egestion and reingestion is estimated at 2–3 days. The authors propose that bacterial colonization improves the quality of feces in both digestibility and nitrogen content, making reprocessing worthwhile ([[Coprophagy]]). Comparable FPOM turnover of 71–102 days was estimated for a chalk stream (Ladle and Griffiths 1980), in contrast to the rapid turnover found here.

**10. Ecosystem significance.** Ingestion, not secondary production, is the main link between collectors and organic matter processing. Only emergent adults that do not return to the stream represent a net export of production. The authors conclude that the [[Collector Macroinvertebrates]]–[[Fine Particulate Organic Matter]] subsystem is central to organic matter processing in desert streams and is a major determinant of desert stream structure and function.

**Caveats.** Production was assumed to be zero from November to April. Because the study year had high discharge during that period, the authors consider this an underestimate for normal years, though they note the effect is probably small. Laboratory activity levels may underestimate those in nature, so the bioenergetic values are considered conservative. The authors are confident that laboratory assimilation efficiencies reflect field values, because the taxa completed their life cycles in the lab at field-like rates.
