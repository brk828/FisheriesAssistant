# Year-Class Abundance, Population, and Production of Walleye in Clear Lake, Iowa (1948–74)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Kenneth D. Carlander]] and [[Philip Michael Payne]] (Iowa State University) analyses 27 years (1948–74) of [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*) monitoring in [[Clear Lake]], a eutrophic [[Iowa]] lake. The authors test whether artificial [[Fry Stocking]] at varied rates explains year-class strength, and they estimate standing crop, growth, mortality and [[Secondary Production]]. Year-class abundance was significantly correlated with fry stocking, which ranged from 0 to 6070 fry/ha. Growth responses were mixed across age groups, and growth tracked water level. Standing crop and production varied widely, and production per unit biomass was lowest during low-water years. The paper was presented at the [[Percid International Symposium]] (PERCIS, 1976).

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Walleye]], a percid predator and the most abundant predatory fish in Clear Lake. Bailey and Harrison (1945) recorded 43 fish species from the lake.
- **Study system:** [[Clear Lake]] (approx. 44°N, 94°W), a 1747-ha eutrophic lake with a 3400-ha watershed. Clear Lake represents the original southern limit of walleye in lakes. Mean depth is 3.6 m (maximum 6 m), and the lake is not thermally stratified in summer (Table 2).
- **Monitoring history:** Fish populations have been monitored since 1942. Graduate students from the [[Iowa Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] collected scale samples and catch data each summer from 1947 to 1974, using gillnets and a 9.1-m bag seine.
- **Stocking history:** An earlier report (Carlander et al. 1960) covered 1948–57 and found that stocked year-classes were more abundant than naturally spawned ones. The 1961–72 stocking rates were varied on an irregular pattern. Fry came from walleye gillnetted at spawning areas.
- **Water chemistry (Table 2):** Total alkalinity 143 ppm; total dissolved solids 230 ppm; pH 8.6; transparency 1.4 m; [[Morphoedaphic Index]] 64.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water levels:** Levels were below the outlet from 1955 to 1960, and lake area fell about 8% at the lowest level (1 m down). Low-water years 1956–62 coincided with poor year-class strength and low production per biomass.
- **Temperature:** Ice-out timing, warming rate, summed degree-days above 5.5°C, and winter ice duration showed no significant correlation with year-class abundance. Summer temperature did not correlate with growth for 1948–73, although [[Carlander and Whitney]] (1961) found a correlation (r = 0.565) for 1935–57.
- **Prey and competitors:** [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) young abundance correlated with young walleye abundance (r = 0.606, 1956–72). [[Yellow Bass]] (*Morone mississippiensis*) was the more abundant prey before 1968, when an [[Aeromonas salmonicida]] infection nearly eliminated it. Walleye fry begin feeding on larval fish at about 31 mm, when yellow perch fry are available but yellow bass fry are not.
- **Density effects:** The authors note that growth usually declines as density rises. The morphoedaphic index (64) exceeds the optimum (40), indicating more eutrophic conditions than optimal. The number of competing fish species may explain the low walleye standing crop.

## Key Findings

1. **Fry stocking and year-class abundance:** Year-class abundance indexes for 1948–72 correlated significantly with fry stocked (r = 0.613, df 22). Excluding the very abundant 1972 year-class gave r = 0.563 (df 21). Excluding the low-water years 1956–61 gave r = 0.641 (df 17).
2. **Age-0 seine catches:** Young walleye seined along the shore correlated more strongly with stocking (r = 0.819, df 13). They were not well correlated with year-class abundance indexes (r = 0.179), suggesting that year-class strength changes between mid-first summer and ages I–III.
3. **Other factors:** No significant relationship with temperature, water levels, or yellow perch abundance was found, though the authors suggest fry stocking masked these effects. Yellow perch catch rates approached significance (r = 0.30, P < 0.10) once stocking effects were removed.
4. **Growth:** Instantaneous growth was negatively correlated with fry stocking at ages I (r = −0.42, P < 0.05) and II (r = −0.41), but positively correlated at age V (r = 0.70, P < 0.008). Growth correlated with water level (r = 0.79, P < 0.0001). The authors suggest that predation by older walleye on young fish may explain the age-V result.
5. **Mortality:** Annual mortality averaged 42% (range 22–63% across regressions; combined regression 42%).
6. **Population size:** Mark-and-recapture estimates (1953, 1958, 1959, 1966, 1972) were regressed on adjusted catch-per-effort (r = 0.974, df 3). Adult population estimates ranged from about 6,600 (1972) to 30,822 (1953) fish over 305 mm (Table 6).
7. **Standing crop:** Ranged from 2.63 kg/ha (1963) to 16.52 kg/ha (1954) for age 0+ fish. The peak age III+ standing crop was 11.30 kg/ha, below the mean of 16 kg/ha for lakes summarised by [[Carlander]] (1977). The mean age II+ standing crop was below the lowest reported value (6.1 kg/ha, West Blue Lake, Manitoba).
8. **Annual production:** Ranged from 1.01 to 9.31 kg/ha (age 0+ to VI); the mean was 3.31 kg/ha. Production per biomass (P/B) was low during the low-water years 1956–62.
9. **Age-specific P/B:** Declined with age (age I 2.24, II 1.00, III 0.43, IV 0.24, V 0.16, VI 0.11). Ages I and II accounted for 59% of annual production, ages I–III for 78%, and ages I–IV for 87%.
10. **Weight–length relationship:** Derived from 489 walleye collected in 1961–62 (Sriprasert 1974). The authors note this underestimates biomass by about 5%.
