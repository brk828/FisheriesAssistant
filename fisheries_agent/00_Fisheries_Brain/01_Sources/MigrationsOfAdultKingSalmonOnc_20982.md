# Migrations of Adult King Salmon in the San Joaquin Delta as Demonstrated by the Use of Sonic Tags

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game report (Fish Bulletin 151, 1970, by Richard J. Hallock, Robert F. Elwell and Donald H. Fry, Jr.) examines why [[San Joaquin River]] salmon runs collapsed after 1961 while [[Sacramento River]] runs did not. The authors attribute the collapse to an annually recurring oxygen block from pollution near [[Stockton]] combined with flow reversal in southern [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] channels caused by the [[Tracy Pumping Plant]]. From 1964 to 1967, 316 adult king salmon were tagged with [[Sonic Tags]] and tracked to measure their response to low dissolved oxygen, high temperature and reversed flows.

## Background & Description

King salmon (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) were the dominant salmon of California before 1963, making up more than 90% of the catch; coho (*O. kisutch*) were mostly ocean-caught fish from Oregon and Washington. Most California kings originate in the Sacramento-San Joaquin system. San Joaquin salmon are essentially all fall-run, since spring-run fish were lost below storage dams after the late 1940s.

Commercial catches since 1916 have averaged over 7,600,000 pounds. The San Joaquin escapement fell from over 58,000 fish in 1960 to 2,550 in 1961, then to 560 and 320 in the following two years. Sacramento runs showed no comparable disaster. The Delta covers over 700,000 acres of land and about 39,000 acres of water, with roughly 700 miles of channels, and tidal action produces strong reversing currents.

The study used trammel netting to capture fish, [[Sonic Transmitter Fish Tags]] operating at 127–130 or 160 kilohertz, portable hydrophone receivers carried by boat crews, and [[Shore Monitors]] recording tag passage on strip-chart and later tape.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pollution:** Fruit and vegetable cannery wastes at Stockton, plus fertilizer-driven algal blooms in return irrigation water, create a summer-to-fall oxygen block that lasts until flows increase.
- **Flow reversal:** The Tracy plant (rated 4,600 cfs, operating since 1951) pulls Sacramento water south through Old and Middle rivers. The [[Italian Slough Pumping Plant]] (10,000 cfs capacity) is expected to intensify reversal until a [[Peripheral Canal]] is built.
- **Migration routes:** Salmon can reach the San Joaquin tributaries via the main channel or through Old and Middle rivers. Sacramento fish may also enter the lower San Joaquin, move up the [[Mokelumne River]] and return to the Sacramento via [[Georgiana Slough]] or the [[Delta Cross Channel]].
- **Water quality thresholds:** The 1964 barrier and pumping experiment at the head of [[Old River]] shows that supplemental flow can restore oxygen levels.
- **Tributary conditions:** Storage dams and return irrigation water make tributary summer flows warm, so spawning streams become suitable only after fall cooling.

## Key Findings

**Behavioral responses (1964–1967 tagging)**
- Salmon avoided water below 5 ppm dissolved oxygen and waited downstream until the block cleared. Fewer than 5 ppm was regarded as a partial block, and less than 4.5 ppm as a near-total block.
- Temperatures above 66° F. formed a partial block. Three tagged fish moved upstream at 72° F., but runs became steady only at 66° F. or below.
- Relatively few fish used the reversed Old and Middle River routes (under 15% in 1964–1966, 41% in 1967).
- No consistent response to tidal currents was detected.

**Tagging summary**
- 316 salmon were tagged and released from 1964 to 1967, mostly at [[Prisoners Point]]. Of 220 fish tagged in 1965–1967, 72% were female, and 80% measured 30–38 inches.
- In 1964 the release site at Schad Landing was abandoned because most fish were Sacramento-origin.

**Proportion of San Joaquin fish at Prisoners Point**
| Year | San Joaquin share |
|---|---|
| 1964 | 46% |
| 1965 | 85% |
| 1966 | 29% |
| 1967 | 86% |

The 1966 dry year showed the lowest San Joaquin share. Chi-square tests showed the wet-year differences were significant at the 1% level.

**Sacramento-origin fish and relative catchability**
- Fish returning via the lower San Joaquin to the Sacramento were common, supporting the regular Mokelumne route.
- San Joaquin fish were 45 times as likely to be caught at Prisoners Point as Sacramento fish in 1964, 216 times in 1965, 10 times in 1966 and 47 times in 1967.

**1964 pilot intervention**
- A partial barrier across Old River plus pumped releases from the Delta-Mendota Canal raised oxygen levels and created positive flow past Stockton, although temperatures stayed high and migration was delayed.
- Flows past Stockton ranged from about 780 to 1,620 cfs after supplementation.

**Flow thresholds and recommendations**
- Migration occurred at flows as low as 252–391 cfs past Stockton in 1966, with a steady run beginning at 391 cfs.
- The authors recommend a minimum positive flow of 400 cfs past Stockton (or enough to hold dissolved oxygen at 5 ppm after October 1, whichever is greater), a minimum of about 200 cfs past Turner Cut, and an Old River barrier that never fully blocks salmon.

**Causes of the 1961 collapse**
- The 1961 fall flows past Stockton (103 cfs on November 5) were far lower than in other dry years (285–436 cfs), and the reversal in Old and Middle rivers was the strongest on record.
- Poor spring flows in 1959–1961 may also have reduced downstream migrant survival, affecting the 1962–1963 runs.

**Conclusions**
- The Peripheral Canal or a similar closed-circuit system is considered the best long-term solution.
- Pumping at the Italian Slough plant will make reversal stronger and later unless steps are taken.
