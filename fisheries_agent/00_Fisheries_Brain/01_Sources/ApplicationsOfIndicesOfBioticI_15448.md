# Applications of Indices of Biotic Integrity to California Streams and Watersheds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter examines how [[Index of Biotic Integrity]] (IBI) methods, developed mainly for eastern North American streams, can be applied to the low-diversity, highly endemic, and heavily invaded fish faunas of California. The authors identify five problems: low [[Species Richness]], high [[Endemism]], abundant [[Introduced Species]], [[Dams and Diversions]] that alter flow regimes, and the need for watershed-scale assessment. They illustrate responses with three case studies: a 100-watershed [[Watershed Index of Biotic Integrity]] (W-IBI) for the Sierra Nevada, a 15-year record from regulated [[Putah Creek]], and a lightly disturbed but heavily invaded [[Dye Creek]]. The authors conclude that IBIs are worth further development as a "first cut" tool for conservation priorities and citizen monitoring, but that their transferability and their treatment of nonnative-dominated systems remain unresolved.

## Background & Description

**Concept.** IBIs measure stream health through biological communities rather than through physical and chemical water quality alone. Following [[Karr and Dudley]] (1981), biotic integrity is "the ability to support and maintain a balanced, integrated, adaptive community of organisms having a species composition, diversity, and functional organization comparable to that of the natural habitat of the region." In practice, IBIs have relied almost entirely on fish abundance and diversity.

**Geographic pattern.** IBIs are most widely used in eastern North America, where fish communities are complex and largely native. In western North America their development has been difficult for five reasons: low species richness, high endemism, abundant introduced fishes, dams and diversions, and the need for whole-watershed measures.

**Study systems.** The chapter applies IBIs to three contrasting California settings:
- The Sierra Nevada range, a major water source and one of the most altered regions in a congressionally mandated assessment.
- Putah Creek, a regulated stream in the Sacramento Valley with both a native assemblage and a nonnative fishery.
- Dye Creek, a small Sierra Nevada tributary to the Sacramento River, mostly within a [[Nature Conservancy]] preserve, with low summer flows of 1 to 5 cfs.

**Key species mentioned.** Native fishes include [[California Roach]], [[Sacramento Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Sacramento Squawfish]]. Trout include native [[Cutthroat Trout]] and introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]. Nonnative warmwater fishes include [[Green Sunfish]]. Amphibians discussed include [[Bullfrog]] (introduced) and [[Foothill Yellow-Legged Frog]] (native).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Low species richness.** West of the Rocky Mountains, undisturbed streams rarely hold more than five or six native fishes. High-elevation streams often hold only one native species, usually a trout, or none. Coastal streams host seasonal, anadromous species that are hard to sample. Suggested remedies are to use standard metrics anyway, reduce the number of metrics, or add metrics from other taxa such as amphibians, [[Benthic Invertebrates]], stoneflies, mollusks, and crayfish.

**Endemism and zoogeography.** Adjacent drainages may share only a few species because of zoogeographic history, which limits region-wide IBIs. Proposed solutions include regional pools of metrics with standardized scores, and endemism-based metrics. Brown et al. (1992) documented morphological divergence of California roach among San Joaquin drainages, and Li et al. (1995) stressed genetic information for aquatic reserves, especially for [[Anadromous Fishes]] that hybridize with hatchery strains.

**Introduced species.** Introduced fishes often dominate western faunas, especially in disturbed warmwater streams at low elevations and in lightly disturbed montane streams. In waters that were originally fishless, introduced trout may eliminate large invertebrates and amphibians. The authors note a conflict: when natives are displaced by nonnatives through competition and predation rather than habitat change, the abundance of nonnatives still carries information about water and habitat quality.

**Flow regulation.** In California, dams typically capture peak winter and spring flows and augment summer flows, often to support salmonid fisheries. This uniform regime disfavors native fishes, which need high spring flows to spawn and can persist through summer low flow. Floods can reduce nonnative warmwater species. Releases can occasionally favor natives, and a regulated site can score as well as an unregulated one. A dam-induced intermittent stream may also score like a naturally intermittent stream, masking the change. The authors recommend caveats and emphasize that the closer a flow regime is to its original pattern, the more likely the original fish assemblage persists.

**Watershed-scale assessment.** Because site-specific data are often scarce, watershed IBIs may be built from the mean of site scores or from watershed-wide metrics scored with expert opinion. The W-IBI follows a naming convention from [[Kerans and Karr]] (1994) to signal its differences from standard IBIs.

## Key Findings

**1. Standard IBI metrics perform poorly in California, with exceptions.**
- A Mexican IBI (Lyons et al., 1995) with 10 metrics worked at sites holding 2 to 10 species, drawing on a pool of 23 native species in 10 families across three basins.
- California attempts with standard metrics succeeded poorly. Causes include low taxonomic diversity (pools of 5 to 15 species in 4 to 8 families, dominated by cyprinids and salmonids), near-universal spring spawning in response to high flows, abundant nonnatives, and elevational and thermal segregation of natives.
- Reducing metrics has worked elsewhere: Lyons et al. (1996) used five metrics for Wisconsin coldwater streams, and Hughes and Gammon (1987) reduced Karr's (1981) 12 metrics to seven for the Willamette River, which had 19 native species.
- For many California streams, two metrics (native species number and native fish abundance) describe fish community integrity, but may miss habitat or water quality changes when natives are displaced by nonnatives.

**2. Amphibians and invertebrates are proposed as additional metrics.** Amphibians with aquatic larvae, typically two to four species present, are sensitive to change and are suggested as especially useful, though not yet used systematically. Stoneflies, mollusks, and crayfish are also proposed, and a separate benthic IBI (B-IBI) has been developed elsewhere.

**3. Sierra Nevada watershed assessment.** The W-IBI uses six metrics (native ranid frogs, native fishes, native fish assemblages, anadromous fishes, trout, and stream fish abundance) scored 1, 3, or 5. Of 100 large watersheds:
- 7 were in excellent condition
- 36 were in good condition
- 48 were in fair condition
- 9 were in poor condition

Ratings correlated with measured disturbance and with subjective evaluations by agencies and environmental groups. The results informed recommendations for protecting aquatic biodiversity in the Sierra Nevada.

**4. Putah Creek shows a strong upstream-downstream gradient.** Sampling occurred 35 km below Putah Diversion Dam over three years, using an IBI with metrics on native and introduced species, age classes of native cyprinids and suckers, total species and abundance, top carnivores, and tolerant and introduced pond-type species. Key results:
- Assemblages dominated by natives scored highest, and integrity fell rapidly below the point where dam releases provide significant flow.
- Lower reaches held a diverse nonnative fish assemblage.
- Biotic integrity dropped during drought years.

Table 14.3 gives scores for eight sites across drought (1994), average (1995), and wet (1996) years. Scores ranged from 35 to 100, with a site-specific trend tied to drought conditions.

**5. A 15-year record documents drought effects.** At one Putah Creek site about 23 km below the dam, annual IBI scores ranged from 50 to 85 across 1980 to 1996. Scores declined in drought years and rose during or after wet years. Drought favors nonnatives that tolerate stagnant, eutrophic pools, and low flows reduce native spawning and juvenile survival. Emergency dam releases were credited with saving the native and nonnative assemblages during an extended drought.

**6. Dye Creek is dominated by invasion more than degradation.** The 1996 survey found:
- California roach as the principal native fish, with Sacramento sucker, rainbow trout, speckled dace, and Sacramento squawfish present in smaller numbers
- A south fork dominated by nonnative green sunfish and bullfrogs, with few refuges for roach in rockbound summer pools
- A north fork containing almost entirely native fishes and amphibians, including foothill yellow-legged frogs
- Nonnative fishes in the lower creek that appeared to come from farm ponds, except for green sunfish

Site IBI scores ranged from 36 to 100 (north fork sites 83 to 100). The watershed mean was 71 (good condition), slightly below the earlier W-IBI score of 80 (very good), which was assigned before detailed surveys revealed the extent of invasion. Low scores mainly reflected invasions, only partly reflecting degradation from ponds, ranch roads, and grazing.

**7. Unresolved interpretive questions.** Whether reaches dominated by nonnative fishes should receive fair to good ratings remains debated. By the standard definition of integrity (resemblance to the original fauna), integrity in such reaches is essentially lost, yet abundant nonnatives across multiple trophic groups indicate some positive water and habitat quality. The authors judge IBIs less useful in low-diversity systems like Dye Creek, where they add little beyond examining a few key species, and suggest that invertebrate data are needed to judge whether vertebrate-based ratings are justified.

**8. Recommendations.**
- Greater use of amphibian metrics in IBIs
- Development of aquatic invertebrate metrics to increase the number of metrics per index
- Two tiers of IBIs: one based on native species and one based on community diversity regardless of origin
- Use by citizen groups and trained amateurs for local stream monitoring

The authors describe IBIs as a "first cut" tool for ranking watersheds for conservation, and caution that the California IBIs presented here are idiosyncratic and that their broader applicability is still to be tested.
