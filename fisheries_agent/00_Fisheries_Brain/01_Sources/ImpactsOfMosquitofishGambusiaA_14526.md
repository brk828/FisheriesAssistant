# Impacts of Mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*) Predation on Plankton Communities

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This experimental study, published in *Hydrobiologia* (1981), tested how predation by the small cyprinodontiform fish [[Gambusia Affinis]] (western mosquitofish) reshapes freshwater plankton. Twelve manmade ponds at Riverside, California, were monitored for about 10 months (August 1967 to May 1968, with field notes to October 1968). Five control ponds were compared with seven ponds receiving fish at two densities (50 and 450 fish per pond), which quickly converged because of rapid [[Gambusia Reproduction]].

Gambusia nearly eliminated the cladocerans *Daphnia pulex* and *Ceriodaphnia* sp., reduced the copepod *Diaptomus pallidus*, had little effect on *Cyclops vernalis*, and drove large increases in several rotifers and in total phytoplankton. The authors use these results to argue for a general size-shift mechanism in zooplankton communities, a model for the calanoid/cyclopoid ratio, and a parallel between fish-induced and insecticide-induced plankton changes with consequences for [[Mosquito Control]] programs.

## Background & Description

The study was designed to test ideas of [[Brooks & Dodson]] (1965) and [[Paine]] (1966) on predators and prey diversity, at a time when experimental fish-plankton work with adequate controls and replication was scarce. Gambusia was chosen because of its wide tolerances, high reproductive rate, and use in mosquito control. It is native to the coastal southeastern United States and northeastern Mexico but has been spread across warm temperate and tropical regions, with documented harm to native pupfish in western North America.

Ponds were approximately 4 m × 6 m, excavated in decomposed granite and clay, with a mean depth of about 0.5 m, filled with unfiltered reservoir water. Macrophytes (chiefly *Zannichellia palustris*, *Spirogyra* sp., and *Cladophora* sp.) were raked out at 1–4 week intervals on every pond alike. Treatments were assigned non-randomly to maximize within-treatment variability and minimize between-treatment differences in initial conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Plankton sampling:** Zooplankton were collected by towed net at midday on about 25 dates; phytoplankton from 140 ml samples at each pond (from October 19 onward). Dominant crustaceans were *Cyclops vernalis*, *Diaptomus pallidus*, *Daphnia pulex*, *Moina affinis*, and *Ceriodaphnia* sp.; dominant rotifers were [[Keratella Cochlearis]], *K. quadrata*, *Trichocerca* spp., *Polyarthra* sp., and *Synchaeta* sp.
- **Seasonality:** Water temperatures ranged from about 8 °C to 25 °C. Fish impacts were stronger in summer, when Gambusia reproduction and feeding were high. Reddy (1975) reported 50–100% increased predation intensity between 20 and 30 °C.
- **Shading and temperature:** A [[Eucalyptus]] grove created strong among-pond temperature differences in winter (about 5 °C range within the day), which likely altered fish feeding rates in ponds 1 and 12.
- **Fish kills:** Gambusia mortality reached 100% in pond 7 (orange-stained water and blown bark) and roughly 70–90% in pond 12 (a *Platymonas*/*Carteria* bloom). Pond 10 lost all fish by mid-May 1968 during a bloom of *Chrysochromulina parva*, after which mosquito larvae and hemipterans invaded.
- **Invertebrates and amphibians:** Hemipterans (*Microvelia* sp., *Buenoa* sp., *Notonecta* sp.) and [[Hyla Regilla]] tadpoles were abundant in control ponds and largely absent from fish ponds (Table 3).
- **Literature comparison:** Tables 4 and 5 summarize six controlled and eight descriptive or quasi-experimental fish-introduction studies, covering taxa such as *Cyprinus carpio*, *Lepomis macrochirus*, and *Alosa* species.

## Key Findings

**Zooplankton and phytoplankton responses (fish vs. control ponds)**

- Cladocerans were most strongly suppressed: *Ceriodaphnia* was effectively eliminated by September 1 in ponds with 450 fish and by October 5 in ponds with 50 fish. *Daphnia* never developed in fish ponds, though it reached high spring densities in controls.
- *Diaptomus* declined on fish introduction. In early winter, densities in fish ponds averaged 5–10% of those in controls, and among-pond variability was much greater in fish ponds (range under 10-fold in controls vs. over 100-fold in fish ponds).
- *Cyclops* was affected least (no clear effect in most dates).
- *K. cochlearis* was the rotifer most affected, averaging more than 100 times more abundant in fish ponds than in controls. *Synchaeta* appeared in 6 of 7 fish ponds and never in controls. *Polyarthra* exceeded 5 individuals per liter in 4 fish ponds but never exceeded 0.5 per liter in controls. *Trichocerca* showed a similar pattern.
- Total phytoplankton increased in fish ponds at all seasons. Fish ponds were visibly green from about September 19, while controls remained clear.
- Gambusia decreased the probability of interspecific encounter (PIE) for crustaceans and increased it for rotifers.

**Water chemistry**

- pH and dissolved oxygen were higher in fish ponds on April 15–16, 1968, which the authors attributed mainly to greater phytoplankton abundance. Oxygen exceeded 100% saturation in both sets of ponds.

**Seasonal and replication conclusions**

- The fish impact was greater in summer than in winter, as shown by *Cyclops*, *Diaptomus*, total rotifers, and phytoplankton. Low winter temperatures appeared to "freeze" summer effects into winter.
- The authors recommend at least 4-fold replication and longer pre-treatment monitoring, because pre-treatment *Moina* data misled the initial assignment.

**Size-selective predation and competition**

- Fish produced a shift toward smaller zooplankton (higher rotifer/crustacean and small/large cladoceran ratios). This was explained by selective predation on large prey and by release of small species from competition with large ones.
- The calanoid/cyclopoid ratio fell in fish ponds, summer, and high-nutrient conditions. The authors propose a causal model (Fig. 8) in which fish, invertebrate predators, and nutrients interact through predator survival and prey selectivity. They note that cyclopoids are generally preyed on more by fish, while calanoids are more vulnerable to invertebrate predators.

**Management implications**

- Gambusia and [[Chlorpyrifos]] both caused comparable plankton shifts and algal blooms (Fig. 9). In a rice-field trial (Hoy et al. 1972), low Gambusia stocking and chlorpyrifos both raised mosquito larvae, and the authors conclude that mosquito control programs using biological agents are not inherently more effective or neutral than insecticide programs.
- Manipulation of coarse, zooplanktivorous fish has been proposed as a way to reduce eutrophication, with a large-scale test underway at Lake Trummen, Sweden.
