# The Development of Population Estimate Procedures in Small Trout Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #salmonidae #fisheries-management #[[Mark-and-Recapture]]

## Executive Summary

This 1960 study by Thomas F. Waters, conducted at the [[Pigeon River Trout Research Station]] in Michigan, evaluated four mark-and-recapture procedures for estimating populations of [[Brook Trout]] (age-classes 1955 and 1956 year classes) in two small lakes (Ford Lake and Hemlock Lake). The central research question was whether marked and unmarked fish exhibit **equal vulnerability** to recapture — a critical but rarely tested assumption in [[Mark-and-Recapture]] population estimation. The study found that "pure" procedures (using the same capture method for marking and recapture) produced systematically biased estimates, while "mixed" procedures (using different capture methods for initial marking versus recapture) produced the most reliable, internally consistent estimates. Key mechanisms identified were **hook resistance** (in angled fish) and **trap resistance** (in trapped fish), both of which caused marked fish to become less vulnerable to recapture by the same method that originally caught them.

## Background & Description

- **Study system**: Two small, intensively-managed trout lakes — Ford Lake (~10 acres, planted annually with 5,850 age-0 [[Brook Trout]]) and Hemlock Lake (6 acres, planted with 3,000 age-0 Brook Trout), both stocked in autumn with fin-clip marked young-of-year fish.
- **Purpose**: The parent research program used a complete creel census to evaluate experimental trout management. Population estimates were needed to track year-class survival from planting through successive winters and fishing seasons, complementing creel census data (catch, effort, catch-per-unit-effort).
- **Capture methods**:
  - **Angling**: conducted by research staff using artificial flies.
  - **Trapping**: initially hoop nets with wings (autumn 1956); later replaced with custom hardware-cloth traps (1/4-inch or less mesh, triangular cross-section, funnel entrance) in spring 1957.
- **Marking scheme**: Differential fin-clipping distinguished trap-caught fish (top caudal corner) from angler-caught fish (lower caudal corner); later, pectoral fin removal (permanent mark) vs. caudal clip was used to test mortality effects.
- **Four estimation procedures compared**:
  1. Angling and recapture by angling (pure)
  2. Trap and recapture by trap (pure)
  3. Trap and recapture by angling (mixed)
  4. Angling and recapture by trap (mixed)
- **Statistical methods**: [[Schumacher-Eschmeyer Method]] (1943) for multiple-sample estimates; [[Petersen Method]] (direct proportion) for two-sample estimates; 95% confidence limits per [[Ricker 1958]], [[DeLury 1958]], and Mainland et al. (1956) binomial tables.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Small, intensively-sampled trout lakes allowing near-complete population sampling — an ideal experimental setting rarely available in larger systems.
- **Population dynamics tracked**: Year-class fate from autumn planting through first winter, first summer of angling/natural mortality, second winter, etc., until the cohort disappeared from the fishery.
- **Fish behavior and size-selectivity**:
  - Both traps and angling discriminated against fish ≤6 inches total length, biasing early (autumn 1956) estimates toward larger size classes only.
  - Size distributions of trap- and angling-caught fish were statistically similar (T-tests showed no significant difference at >80% confidence level) in both lakes, indicating comparable size-selectivity between methods.
  - Escapement of smaller fish through trap mesh was documented and considered a contributing factor to some low estimates.
- **Environmental/temporal factors**: Sampling was conducted immediately after ice-out (spring) and just before season close (autumn) — periods of high brook trout activity that favored intensive, rapid sampling and justified assumptions of no recruitment and random distribution of fish.

## Key Findings

### Assumption Testing
- Assumptions of no recruitment, mark retention, random distribution, and correct mark recognition were considered safely met.
- **Equal vulnerability between marked and unmarked fish could NOT be assumed** — this was the primary focus of investigation.
- Differential mortality due to marking (fin-clipping) was tested and largely ruled out: fatal hooking mortality was ~1%; pectoral-fin removal vs. caudal-clip comparisons in Hemlock Lake produced nearly identical estimates (3,750 vs. 3,711), indicating fin-removal mortality was not a major bias source.

### Angling and Recapture by Angling (Pure Procedure)
- Produced estimates judged **too high**: 1,044 (Hemlock) and 833 (Ford) for the 1955 year class — well above all other procedures.
- Cause: **"Hook resistance"** — trout caught once by hook became less likely to be caught again, reducing their proportion in recapture samples relative to true abundance.
- Supporting evidence: Catch-per-unit-effort dropped sharply once ~40–50% of the population had been caught/marked.
- A "rest" experiment in Ford Lake (5 days angling, 10-day rest, second marking/angling period) reduced the estimate from 833 to 633, showing hook resistance partially dissipates over time but does not fully disappear.
- **Conclusion**: Angling-and-recapture-by-angling was judged invalid unless sufficient time is allowed between marking and recapture periods.

### Trap and Recapture by Trap (Pure Procedure) — Autumn 1956, Hoop Nets
- Produced estimates judged **too low**: 471 (Hemlock, vs. known minimum of 515) and 411 (Ford, vs. known minimum of 422).
- Upper 95% CI in Hemlock (508) still fell below the known minimum number captured, confirming systematic underestimation.
- Ruled out causes: release-point location, unequal trap "field of influence" overlap (Cooper 1952), and combining size classes (size-stratified estimate = 482, similar to combined 471).
- **Most probable cause**: individual variation in trap susceptibility (some fish more "trap-prone"), inflating the marked proportion in trap recapture samples.
- **Conclusion**: Trap-and-recapture-by-trap using hoop nets was invalid, producing low-biased estimates.

### Trap and Recapture by Trap (Pure Procedure) — Spring 1957, Hardware-Cloth Traps
- Produced estimates judged **too high**: Ford Lake 5,742 (vs. 5,850 planted — plausible only if near-zero mortality); Hemlock Lake >3,700 (vs. only 3,000 planted — impossible, confirming positive bias).
- Estimates decreased progressively over the sampling period and stabilized at lower "last day's sample" Petersen estimates: Ford 5,041; Hemlock 3,406 (pectoral) and 3,206 (caudal).
- **Cause**: Analogous **"trap resistance"** — fish caught in hardware-cloth traps became less likely to re-enter traps, mirroring the hook-resistance phenomenon in angling.
- **Conclusion**: Trap-and-recapture-by-trap using hardware-cloth traps was invalid, producing high-biased estimates — opposite direction of bias from hoop-net traps, underscoring that bias direction depends on trap type and cannot be predicted a priori.

### Mixed Procedures (Best-Performing)
- **Autumn 1956 (Hemlock Lake)**: Trap-and-recapture-by-angling (539) and angling-and-recapture-by-trap (528) agreed well; a correction for marked-fish accumulation during angling ("M corrected") refined the latter to 524, closely matching 528.
- **Autumn 1956 (Ford Lake)**: Trap-and-recapture-by-angling (432) and angling-and-recapture-by-trap (500) showed a detectable discrepancy; correcting field protocol (stopping angling, continuing trapping, holding M constant) reduced the estimate to 465, better aligning with 432. Best estimate judged ~450.
- **Spring 1957 (both lakes)**: Trap-and-recapture-by-angling produced the most plausible estimates relative to known plantings — Ford Lake: 4,410 (vs. 5,850 planted); Hemlock Lake: 2,591 (vs. 3,000 planted). Both were lower than subsequent "Autumn 1957 check estimates" (4,624 and 2,997 respectively), which were judged inflated likely due to fin-removal-associated mortality from the permanent marking method used for the check.
- **Overall conclusion**: Mixed procedures (different capture method for marking vs. recapture) consistently produced estimates compatible with known plantings and known minimum numbers, and agreed well with each other — establishing them as the superior approach for avoiding vulnerability bias.

### General Conclusions
1. **Pure procedures were inferior to mixed procedures** across both lakes and both sampling periods.
2. Angling-recapture-by-angling → biased high (hook resistance).
3. Trap-recapture-by-trap → biased low (hoop nets, variable trap susceptibility) or biased high (hardware-cloth traps, trap resistance), depending on trap design.
4. Mixed procedures (angling-and-recapture-by-trap; trap-and-recapture-by-angling) yielded the most defensible, mutually consistent estimates.
5. **Practical recommendation**: When passive capture methods depending on fish behavior are used for mark-recapture studies, employing a *different* capture method for recapture is critical to avoid systematic bias from differential vulnerability (i.e., "hook resistance" or "trap resistance") between marked and unmarked fish.
