# Food and Habitat Use by Different Sizes of Largemouth Bass (Micropterus salmoides) in Alamo Lake, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined how three size groups of [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) in [[Alamo Lake]], an Arizona reservoir, differed in foraging behavior and habitat use. Small bass (<25 cm TL) stayed in littoral cover, probably to avoid predation. Intermediate bass (25–38 cm TL) formed aggregations in open water and fed on [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*). Large bass (>38 cm TL) were solitary ambush predators near submerged structure. The authors attribute these differences to the interaction of behavioral flexibility, site-specific resources and [[Ecomorphological Constraints]], with allometric growth making large bass too bulky to pursue shad efficiently in open water.

## Background & Description

Most freshwater fish of North America are closely tied to the littoral zone. Centrarchids have seemed an exception to [[Niche Differentiation]] theory, which has prompted research into differences in foraging capability and constraints between species and between size classes within species. In natural North American lakes, small largemouth bass (<8.0 cm as printed in the source) are restricted to densely vegetated shallows and eat mostly invertebrates. Larger bass use deeper and more open littoral areas and are almost entirely piscivorous ([[Keast and Webb 1966]]; [[Hall and Werner 1977]]). Few natural lakes support moderate or large prey in the limnetic zone, and small fish are too vulnerable to wander far into open water ([[Mittelbach 1981]]).

Anglers and state biologists at Alamo Lake, however, reported many bass taken far from littoral habitat and numerous bass feeding on schools of threadfin shad in the limnetic zone. This pattern, documented by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] surveys from 1976 to 1979, contradicted the usual pattern. The study set out to determine (1) whether bass at Alamo Lake used limnetic as well as littoral areas, and (2) if so, what factors allowed this.

**Study site.** Alamo Lake is a monomictic desert highland impoundment at 357 m (1170 ft) elevation. It was created in 1958 when the [[Big Sandy River]] was dammed for flood control. The Big Sandy and [[Santa Maria River]] supply the main inflows. Average surface area was 728 ha (1,800 acres) before the 1977/78 and 1978/79 floods, rising to 2832 ha (7,000 acres) with the floods. During 1981–1982 it covered 1539–1791 ha (3,800–4,400 acres).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Field work began in early 1980 and included locating and tracking largemouth bass, locating threadfin shad and collecting bass stomach contents. Ultrasonic transmitters were used to track individuals in both littoral and limnetic zones. Echo-sounding was limited to limnetic areas because shallowness and vegetation made echo-location ineffective in the littoral zone.

- **Echo-sounding transects:** Monthly transects at dawn, midday and dusk crossed open bays and the upper, middle and lower lake in 1980 and 1981. Diel patterns were examined with transects every 2 hours for 24 hours once each season in 1981.
- **Species identification:** Three large fish species could occur in the limnetic zone: largemouth bass, [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*). Trammel netting and intensive angling confirmed that most heavy echo traces, especially at midwater and above, were largemouth bass.

**Telemetry.** Twenty-eight bass (16 females, 12 males), 35.6–54.0 cm TL, were tagged with ultrasonic transmitters (Sonotronics, Tucson, Arizona). Tags measured 16 × 60 mm, weighed 20 g, had reception ranges of about 600 m and functional lives of about 14 months. Each emitted a distinct frequency between 72 and 94 kHz. Fish were surgically tagged and released at their capture sites. Tracking ran from March 1981 to April 1982, with locations triangulated from fixed shore features and depths measured by echo-sounder.

**Age structure and diet.** Age classes were inferred from length-frequency data (after [[Bryant and Houser 1971]]) from March–April samples in 1980 and 1981 (N = 575 in 1980; N = 578 in 1981). Stomach contents from 3900 bass were analysed, with prey expressed as percentages of total number and volume. Stomachs of large bass were flushed with water, while entire stomachs were taken from small bass.

## Key Findings

**1. Shad schools and bass aggregations in the limnetic zone.** Large schools of threadfin shad (up to 750 m wide × 1200 m long) and bass aggregations occurred throughout all limnetic areas of Alamo Lake in 1980–1981. Shad stayed in tight schools near the thermocline or surface from midday to afternoon, then rose toward the surface at dusk, when bass aggregated below them. Bass were widely dispersed during midday. Intermediate-sized bass fed on shad at dawn and dusk.

**2. Tagged bass were solitary and mobile.** When located, tagged bass were usually solitary and stationary within cover or near the bottom in deep water. They moved several hundred metres, with some travelling more than 400 m from their release sites. Bass often remained at a single location for a period in similar habitat before moving. Average daily movements ranged from about 11 to 288 m (the source text is partly garbled at this point). Bass frequently occupied several different sites in one day.

**3. Year-class dominance.** A large year class produced in the 1977 spawning season dominated the population throughout the study. Its cohort was larger than others, consistent with a flood-associated recruitment pulse. The source describes survival of only a few bass from subsequent year classes, which the authors link to predation pressure from large bass.

**4. Diet by size.** Threadfin shad were the dominant prey of larger bass, making up 58% of their diets. Bass larger than 30.5 cm TL also ate more [[Tilapia]] (*Tilapia* sp.) and [[Bluegill]], and these prey were most common in spring. Juvenile bass ate mostly aquatic insects, which the source describes as a benthic diet. Spring was when shad were at their lowest abundance and when bass made up the largest share of the population. Shad remained the principal limnetic prey until young-of-the-year were produced. Diet values in the source are partly garbled, so percentages beyond the 58% figure are not reported here.

**5. Three behavioural groups.** The study found that foraging and habitat use differed clearly among three size groups:

- **Juveniles (<25 cm TL):** Restricted to littoral cover, probably to avoid predation. They were vulnerable to predation and could not compete with larger bass in open water.
- **Intermediate bass (25–38 cm TL):** Aggregated in limnetic areas at dawn and dusk, feeding on shad near the surface. They were too large for most predators and were relatively free of predation.
- **Large bass (>38 cm TL):** Solitary ambush feeders near submerged structure, despite still eating mostly shad.

**6. Ecomorphological explanation.** The authors argue that [[Allometric Growth]] explains the shift in large bass. As bass grow beyond about 30–35 cm, weight increases faster than length, so width and depth grow faster than length. This loses the streamlined fusiform shape of open-water pursuit predators. The bulkier body raises swimming drag and reduces efficiency in chasing shad. Large bass therefore shifted to ambush feeding, as [[Keast 1965]] also found for largemouth bass in [[Lake Opinicon]], Ontario.

**7. Synthesis.** Habitat use and foraging behaviour of largemouth bass in Alamo Lake, as in other lakes, resulted from the interplay of behavioural flexibility, site-specific resources and [[Ecomorphological Constraints]]. No single factor dominated. The authors argue that studies of fish should consider morphology as well as food and habitat, because [[Indeterminate Growth]] and allometric growth can make different sizes of one species function like separate species. Vulnerability to predation and swimming efficiency can override food availability in habitat selection.
