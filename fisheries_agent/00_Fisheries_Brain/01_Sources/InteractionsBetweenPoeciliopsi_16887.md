# Interactions Between Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis and Gambusia affinis in the Rio Yaqui Drainage, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #endangered-species #predation-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study examined interactions between the endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*) and introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) at [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]], southeastern [[Arizona]], from May 1985 through October 1987. The research tested whether *P. o. sonoriensis* exhibited greater resistance to *G. affinis* predation than its congener *P. o. occidentalis*, which has been extensively replaced throughout the American Southwest. Investigators compared body size, fecundity, reproductive effort, and caudal fin damage between the two species in syntopic (co-occurring) and allotopic (non-co-occurring) habitats including thermal springs (limnocrenes) and stream reaches. The study also evaluated a reintroduction of *P. o. sonoriensis* into upper [[Black Draw]] and monitored subsequent colonization dynamics and *G. affinis* reinvasion.

## Background & Description

[[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]] comprises two subspecies in the United States: *P. o. occidentalis* ([[Gila Topminnow]]) in the [[Gila River]] drainage and *P. o. sonoriensis* ([[Yaqui Topminnow]]) in the [[Rio Yaqui]] drainage of extreme southeastern Arizona. Both are small livebearing poeciliids native to Sonoran desert aquatic habitats. *P. occidentalis* was listed as federally endangered in 1973 due to habitat loss and predation by the introduced *Gambusia affinis*, which first appeared in Arizona in 1926 and reached the U.S. portion of the Rio Yaqui drainage by 1979. Previous hypotheses suggested *P. o. sonoriensis* might resist *G. affinis* better than *P. o. occidentalis* due to reportedly higher fecundity (14-49 embryos vs. 0-16 for comparable lengths) and observations of syntopic occurrence in Mexico where *G. affinis* remained rare.

The study area encompassed diverse aquatic habitats on [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] including nine thermal artesian springs (ca. 28°C limnocrenes), modified seeps and ponds, and approximately 2.4 km of intermittent and perennial stream along [[Black Draw]]. Water chemistry varied among sites: thermal springs exhibited lower conductivity and nutrient concentrations than [[Black Draw]], which showed higher alkalinity, chloride, sulfate, silica, and phosphorus. [[Black Draw-Source]], where perennial flow originates from a partially thermal spring (22°C), provided an intermediate thermal regime. [[Leslie Creek]], a separate perennial stream in the [[Swisshelm Mountains]] (elevation 1410 m), harbored *P. o. sonoriensis* and *Gila purpurea* introduced in 1969, with *Agosia chrysogaster* also present but *G. affinis* absent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Rio Yaqui]] basin headwaters in southeastern Arizona represent the northern extent of this drainage, which supports six endemic fish species now protected within the National Wildlife Refuge system. Aquatic habitats include constant-temperature thermal springs, seasonally variable streams subject to flash flooding from summer monsoon precipitation, and modified impoundments. Water temperatures ranged from 11-29°C depending on season and thermal influence. Thermal limnocrenes maintained nearly constant temperatures (27-28°C) year-round, while non-thermal stream reaches at [[Black Draw-Border]] fluctuated from 11°C in winter to 22°C in summer. [[Black Draw-Source]] maintained intermediate temperatures (19-27°C) due to partial thermal input.

Flash flooding represents a critical environmental factor structuring fish communities in this arid region. Monsoonal storms produce high-intensity, short-duration precipitation events causing abrupt channel-scouring floods in laterally constrained streams. In August 1987, 12.4 cm of precipitation generated floods reaching approximately 1.5 m depth at [[Black Draw-Border]], as evidenced by debris stranded in mid-channel cottonwoods. These disturbances contrast with the stable conditions in spring-fed habitats.

The fish community includes native species (*P. o. sonoriensis*, *A. chrysogaster*, *G. purpurea*) and introduced *G. affinis*. *Agosia chrysogaster* is omnivorous, feeding primarily on benthic algae and invertebrates. *Gila purpurea* consumes aquatic invertebrates and algae, with only incidental predation on *P. o. sonoriensis*. *Gambusia affinis* exhibits aggressive predation on larval and juvenile topminnows, as documented in laboratory and field studies, and also damages caudal fins of adult *Poeciliopsis* through aggressive interactions.

## Key Findings

**Reproductive Biology and Fecundity:**

- Instantaneous fecundity of *P. o. sonoriensis* (mean ± SD: 28 ± 22) was not significantly higher than *P. o. occidentalis* from [[Monkey Spring]] (21 ± 14, P > 0.10), contradicting the hypothesis that higher subspecific fecundity enables better resistance to *G. affinis* predation.

- Maximum fecundity recorded was 119 ova/embryos from a 46.0 mm SL female *P. o. sonoriensis* at [[Black Draw-Source]] in March 1986, ten months after reintroduction when population density was low.

- Fecundity declined dramatically in the second year after reintroduction: mean body wet weight decreased 43% and mean fecundity per female declined 72% between March 1986 (N=24, mean fecundity 82) and March 1987 (N=61, mean fecundity 21) at [[Black Draw-Source]], indicating density-dependent regulation of reproduction.

- Reproductive effort (percentage of total body weight invested in reproduction) was largely independent of female body length but varied significantly among habitats and in response to *G. affinis* presence.

- Both species exhibited superfetation (carrying ova/embryos in multiple non-consecutive developmental stages). Embryonic stages 2 and 5-6 most frequently co-occurred in *P. o. sonoriensis* (accounting for 20% and 49% of total mature ova/embryos, respectively).

- Photoperiod appeared more important than temperature in controlling reproductive seasonality: reproduction was insignificant or absent during winter (October, November, January) even in constant-temperature (28°C) thermal springs, though temperature influenced onset timing in spring.

**Syntopic Interactions at West Border Spring (Limnocrene):**

- *P. o. sonoriensis* comprised only 1.4-12.4% of total poeciliids collected at [[West Border Spring]] where *G. affinis* had been present for several years, with ratios ranging from 1:7 to 1:65 (mean 1:32). No significant trend in proportion occurred over the study period (May 1985-September 1987).

- Caudal fin damage frequency was 76% for *P. o. sonoriensis* at [[West Border Spring]] (mean severity 0.76 ± 0.43) compared to 5% at [[Cottonwood Spring]] and 0% at [[Bunting Spring]] where *G. affinis* was absent (P < 0.001).

- Female *P. o. sonoriensis* at [[West Border Spring]] were significantly larger and heavier than at [[Bunting Spring]] or [[Cottonwood Spring]] (allotopic sites), attributed to size-selective predation by *G. affinis* removing smaller individuals and reduced intraspecific competition.

- Reproductive effort of *P. o. sonoriensis* was significantly higher at [[West Border Spring]] (where *G. affinis* was present) than at thermal springs where *G. affinis* was absent, suggesting compensatory reproduction in response to predation pressure.

- Female *P. o. sonoriensis* were heavier than *G. affinis* of the same length at [[West Border Spring]] (P < 0.001 after adjusting for body length), though mean standard lengths were similar (27.7 mm vs. 26.9 mm, P = 0.506).

**Stream Dynamics at Black Draw:**

- At [[Black Draw-Border]] (non-thermal stream), proportion of *P. o. sonoriensis* fluctuated widely (0-75%, mean 34.8 ± 29.6%) with a significant downward trend of approximately 2% per month until July 1987 when none were collected (R² = 0.68, P = 0.012).

- Following monsoonal flash flooding in August 1987 (12.4 cm precipitation, ~1.5 m flood depth), *P. o. sonoriensis* proportion increased dramatically to >50% in September and October 1987, with ratios shifting from 1:90 to 1:0.46 and 1:0.22, supporting the differential-selection-by-flooding hypothesis.

- *Gambusia affinis* reinvaded [[Black Draw-Source]] springhead from downstream in summer 1986 (likely during continuous July precipitation without flash flooding), but relative abundance remained low (3.9-21.2%, mean 13.3 ± 7.4%) through October 1987.

- At [[Black Draw-Source]], both species were reproductively active in March at this slightly thermal site (22°C), but reproduction ceased by October. Average fecundity and reproductive effort of *G. affinis* were higher than *P. o. sonoriensis* during the four months compared (March, June, July, September 1987).

- Highest *G. affinis* fecundity recorded was 207 ova/embryos from a 49.3 mm SL female at [[Black Draw-Source]] in June 1987, less than one year after reinvasion, indicating high reproductive output at low population density.

**Comparative Reproductive Output:**

- At [[Leslie Creek]] (non-thermal, *G. affinis* absent), reproductively-active *P. o. sonoriensis* were smaller than at [[Black Draw-Source]] but exhibited higher fecundity and reproductive effort in June due to smaller average ova/embryo size (1.7 mg vs. 3.3 mg dry weight).

- *Gambusia affinis* generally exhibited higher fecundity than *P. o. sonoriensis* when syntopic, but reproductive effort was often lower, particularly at [[West Border Spring]] where predation pressure was severe.

- Fecundity of *G. affinis* was significantly higher at [[Black Draw-Source]] (recently invaded, low density) than at [[West Border Spring]] (established for >4 years), demonstrating density-dependent regulation in both species.

- Smaller mean standard length and lower fecundity of brood-carrying *G. affinis* compared to *P. o. sonoriensis* in July suggested earlier sexual maturity in *G. affinis*.

**Environmental Refugia and Persistence Mechanisms:**

- Persistence of *P. o. sonoriensis* in [[Black Draw]] appears due to two environmental factors: (1) recurrent flash flooding that differentially displaces *G. affinis*, and (2) the uniform-temperature springhead at [[Black Draw-Source]] providing refuge from predation and a source for downstream recolonization.

- The 0.5-m high gabion constructed in summer 1984 at [[Black Draw]] initially prevented upstream *G. affinis* migration but was eventually surmounted during sustained high flows in July 1986, demonstrating limited effectiveness of low barriers.

- Springhead environments may provide refuge through physical/chemical factors (clearer water, higher alkalinity at [[Black Draw-Source]]: conductivity 655 μS/cm, alkalinity 7.18 meq/L vs. 579 μS/cm and 4.57 meq/L at [[Black Draw-Border]]), though experimental studies are needed to confirm species-specific environmental preferences.

**Conservation Implications:**

- No reproductive or biological superiority of *P. o. sonoriensis* over *P. o. occidentalis* against *G. affinis* predation was observed; both subspecies require the same environmental conditions (flooding disturbance and/or springhead refugia) to resist extirpation.

- The study documented susceptibility of *P. o. sonoriensis* to severe population reduction or replacement by *G. affinis*, with gradual decline at [[Black Draw-Border]] indicating biotic factors alone are inadequate to prevent extirpation when syntopic with *G. affinis*.

- Compensatory reproduction (increased reproductive effort in response to predation) may temporarily prevent population extirpation but is insufficient for long-term persistence without environmental refugia or disturbance regimes favoring native species.

- Management recommendations include: (1) habitat modification at [[San Bernardino NWR]] to prevent *G. affinis* reinvasion from Mexico, (2) eradication of existing *G. affinis* populations following barrier construction, and (3) continued public education on native fish values to prevent future non-native introductions.
