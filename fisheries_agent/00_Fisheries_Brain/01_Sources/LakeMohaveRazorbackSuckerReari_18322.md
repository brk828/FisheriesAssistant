# Lake Mohave razorback sucker rearing program update June 1995

## Executive Summary
This report, issued in June 1995 by the Native Fish Work Group (NFWG), documents a multi-agency conservation effort to save the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) population in [[Lake Mohave]], a mainstem Colorado River reservoir impounded in 1950. Over 95% of the wild adult population resides here, but recruitment to adulthood is near zero due to intense predation by non-native fishes on eggs, larvae, and juveniles. The NFWG's strategy involves capturing wild larvae or eggs, rearing them in isolated, ephemeral lakeside backwaters to a predator-resistant size (~300 mm), and releasing them back into the lake. The program goal is to stock 50,000 fish to preserve the population's high genetic diversity before the aging wild cohort dies out (projected by ~2000, given a lifespan under 50 years and an impoundment date of 1950).

## Background & Description
- **Species**: [[Razorback Sucker]] — a large-bodied catostomid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]], named for its pronounced dorsal keel.
- **Growth trajectory (Yuma Cove 1992 cohort, Table 3)**:
  - Larvae at hatching: ~10-13 mm total length (TL)
  - 6 May: 37.5 mm (post-larval)
  - 16 June: 136.9 mm
  - 22 July: 203.3 mm
  - 12 August: 226.2 mm
  - 23 November: 354.1 mm (average weight 586 g)
  - Overall trend: fingerlings gained ~1 inch (25 mm) per month, growing from ~60 mm to 191 mm over six months.
- **Stocked fingerlings (Dexter NFH, 1993)**: 100–200 mm TL at stocking, reaching >300 mm by mid-August.
- **Advanced larvae (Yuma Cove, 1994)**: stocked at 26–35 mm TL, recaptured in October at >200 mm.
- **Target rearing benchmark**: 300 mm TL is considered the size at which juveniles gain sufficient resistance to predation by exotic species.
- **Weight data**: Average weight at 321.3 mm TL (October) was 411 g; at 354.1 mm TL (November) was 586 g.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Adult [[Razorback Sucker]] spawn in shoreline gravels of [[Lake Mohave]] in spring (mid-January through early April). Juveniles are reared in isolated, ephemeral lakeside backwaters (e.g., [[Yuma Cove]], [[Davis Cove]], [[Willow Cove]], [[Sidewinder Cove]]) cut off from the main lake by natural sand/gravel berms.
- **Ephemeral pond dynamics**: These backwaters fill in late winter/early spring as the reservoir rises and dry out in fall as reservoir levels drop — a cycle that both supports juvenile growth and naturally purges non-native fish that gain access during high water.
- **Predation pressure**: 
  - [[Common Carp]] observed feeding directly on razorback sucker eggs.
  - Young [[Sunfish]] heavily prey on larvae within 5-10 days post-hatch.
  - [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] feed readily on juvenile suckers.
  - This predation gauntlet results in essentially zero natural recruitment to adulthood in the open lake.
- **Genetic diversity**: Studies by Arizona State University and UC Davis found 22 of every 25 sampled adults derived from different parent pairs, indicating unusually high genetic diversity that is a conservation priority to preserve.
- **Historical pattern**: Similar boom-and-bust adult population dynamics (rapid post-impoundment population growth followed by senescence without recruitment) occurred in four other reservoirs this century: [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Havasu]], [[Lake Roosevelt]], and [[Lake Saguaro]].

## Key Findings
- **Population urgency**: With adult lifespan <50 years and Lake Mohave's impoundment in 1950, the wild adult population was projected to disappear by ~2000 absent intervention.
- **1992 pilot (Yuma Cove)**: 150 juveniles reared from spawn of 61 males/28 females; grew from 1 inch (March) to as much as 14 inches (November), averaging 12.5 inches; ~129 fish released >300 mm.
- **1993 backwater comparison (Tables 4 & 5)**: Testing egg-stocking, fertilized-egg ponds, wild-larvae ponds, and lab-held larvae ponds across multiple sites yielded >500 released juveniles. Survival ranged widely (e.g., 91.5% for Yuma Cove larvae vs. as low as ~6-28% for some larvae-stocked sites), leading to the conclusion that wild larvae should be captured, held in lab ~3 weeks until doubled in size, then stocked into backwaters.
- **1993 fingerling trial**: 69 fingerlings (100-200 mm) from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] stocked into backwater "AJ"; 57 of 69 (82%) survived and exceeded 300 mm target by mid-August.
- **1994 advanced larvae trial (Yuma Cove)**: 420 larvae (26-35 mm) stocked in June; 386 recaptured in October (91.5% survival), all >200 mm.
- **1994 fin-clip recapture trial**: 272 juveniles captured in Willow/South Sidewinder Coves, fin-clipped, released into Yuma Cove; 268 (98%) recovered in October.
- **1994 full-scale effort**: Over 10,000 wild larvae captured lakewide; ~9,000 three-week-old larvae (post-lab-rearing on brine shrimp nauplii) stocked into nine ephemeral backwaters; total of 2,200 razorback suckers reared and released to the lake by mid-October.
- **1995 expanded effort (Table 9 & 10)**: 20,146 wild larvae collected; 10,801 stocked into seven lakeside ponds; 7,529 transferred to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Estimated 3-week survival in the Bureau of Reclamation lab was 91% (18,330 surviving of 20,146 captured).
- **Spawning-site recapture evidence (Table 11)**: During the 1994 "Razorback Sucker Roundup," 7 of the fish reared and released in 1992 and 3 of those from 1993 were recaptured at natural spawning areas — direct evidence that lab/backwater-reared fish reach sexual maturity and return to spawn, validating the program's long-term recovery strategy.
- **Revised program direction**: NFWG shifted to a two-year rearing model — wild larvae captured and transferred to Willow Beach NFH; by the following January, juveniles exceed 100 mm and are moved to lakeside backwaters; by October of the second year, fish are harvested at ~300 mm and released to the lake.
- **Program goal**: Produce and stock 50,000 razorback suckers to sustain the genetically diverse population for future range-wide recovery efforts over the next several decades.
