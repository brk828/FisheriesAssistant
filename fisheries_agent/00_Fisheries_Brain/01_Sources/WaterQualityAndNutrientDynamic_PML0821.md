# Water Quality and Nutrient Dynamics of Arizona Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This completion report (Project A-106-ARIZ, Oct 1980 to Oct 1981, Arizona State University, Department of Zoology, Principal Investigator S.G. Fisher, graduate student N.B. Grimm) characterizes nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) concentrations and dynamics in [[Arizona Streams]]. A survey of 196 sites on 157 streams across 13 drainage basins was combined with diel nutrient budgets on [[Sycamore Creek]], a low desert stream, and uptake measurements on isolated algal communities. The study finds that streams are generally potentially N-limited during summer low flow, that springs and floodwaters carry high N, and that N is depleted downstream, probably by [[Autotrophic Uptake]]. The report contains no fish data; its focus is the physicochemical and algal-nutrient processes of the aquatic ecosystem.

## Background & Description

The study pursued three goals: (1) characterize ambient N and P levels in natural waters; (2) assess potential nutrient limitation by N or P; and (3) describe nutrient behavior, especially mechanisms and rates of uptake, in streams. Nutrient limitation was assessed with the [[N:P Ratio]] diagnostic, an approach with precedent in marine research (Redfield 1958) and freshwater work (Healey and Henzel 1979). Actual limitation was distinguished from potential limitation, following [[Liebig's Law of the Minimum]].

Study sites were classified by drainage basin, vegetation type (five formation classes: desertscrub n=87, grassland n=40, chaparral n=23, woodland n=21, forest n=25), and stream type: summer low flow (n=92), winter high flow (n=14), floods (34 sites sampled during floods), and springs or spring-sources (55 sites). Water was collected in acid-rinsed 250 mL bottles, filtered through Whatman GF/F filters, and analysed for nutrients and ions. Methods are summarised as: [[Nitrate Reduction Method]], ammonium by the phenol-hypochlorite method, SRP by the molybdate-blue method, and total dissolved N and P after UV oxidation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sycamore Creek:** a 50.5 km² watershed (elevation 1400–7100 ft; study reach ~2000 ft), spatially and temporally intermittent, discharge 0.02 m³/s in August–September 1981, temperatures 23–36 °C, coarse sand substrate.
- **Water chemistry:** Arizona waters were generally slightly alkaline (pH 5.5–8.5), with bicarbonate the dominant anion and calcium/sodium the main cations in Salt River basin waters.
- **Conductivity:** Salt River basin streams mostly 300–500 µS/cm; Southern Arizona sites ranged widely, with some Santa Cruz sites >1000 µS/cm and Bylas springs much higher.
- **Vegetation:** desertscrub sites lay in the Great Basin and Sonoran deserts; woodland (3000–5800 ft) and forest (>5000 ft) sites had markedly lower nitrate-N than desert, grassland and chaparral sites, though the difference was not statistically significant.
- **Algae:** the dominant taxa in chambers and diel studies included [[Cladophora Glomerata]], diatoms, blue-greens (e.g., [[Nostoc]]) and [[Hydrodictyon]].

## Key Findings

**Survey results**

- Nitrate-N varied widely across the 196 sites. Summer low-flow nitrate was typically low, while floods and springs were high. Maximum nitrate across all sites was 5.64 mg/L; mean nitrate was 0.252 mg/L; mean SRP was 0.041 mg/L (range 0–0.407 mg/L).
- Analysis of variance among stream types was significant for nitrate-N (F = 8.96, p < 0.0001), dissolved inorganic N (F = 20.5, p < 0.0001), DN, N:SRP and N:TDP, but not for SRP (F = 0.59, p = 0.520) or TDP (F = 0.45, p = 0.717).
- Most summer low-flow sites had N:P < 15 (potential N limitation). Exceptions included the Little Colorado basin and Big Bug Creek (site 29), and several southern sites.
- Springs and spring sources had higher N concentrations; N:P at springs ranged from 0 to 225, and some springs may be P-limited (e.g., site 98, N:P up to 229).

**Downstream declines and diel budgets**

- Nitrate-N declined downstream of spring sources faster than SRP, so N:P fell with distance. Longitudinal reductions were recorded for sites such as 35 (nitrate 1.150 → 0.2 mg/L downstream range) and 79/80 (see Table 33).
- In the Sycamore Creek 100 m reach, nitrate uptake was highest between 1200 and 2000 h and continued in darkness.
- Dissolved N and P dominated transport. Particulate N and P export rose to 106% of inputs on Diel III, attributed to a large [[Cladophora Glomerata]] drift event.
- Observed inorganic N uptake was lower than predicted on Diels I and II, but within the predicted range on Diel III. Predicted and observed P uptake did not fit consistently.
- Areal uptake rates (3.1, 5.9 and 12.6 mmol N/m²·d) fell at the lower end of earlier Sycamore Creek estimates (8.6–49 mmol N/m²·d).

**Isolated algal communities**

- Ambient nitrate uptake per unit chlorophyll a ranged from 2.2 to 43.6 µg N/mg chl·h. Replicate variability was high (coefficient of variation up to 63%).
- Enrichment raised uptake up to ten-fold, suggesting high capacity for N uptake when ambient concentrations are low.
- A single enrichment bioassay (22 September) showed a respiration response but no conclusive identification of limiting nutrients.

**Conclusions**

1. The work extends Grimm et al. (1981) to 157 Arizona streams: most are potentially N-limited during summer low flow.
2. Springs and floodwaters have significantly higher N concentrations and N:P than summer low-flow streams.
3. SRP and TDP did not differ significantly among stream types.
4. No significant differences were found among vegetation types, probably because stream type variation dominates.
5. Nitrate declines downstream of spring sources, consistent with uptake by autotrophs.
6. Stream-ecosystem N uptake increased with autotrophic community development following floods.
7. Dissolved N and P dominated nutrient transport; retention was lowest just after flooding.
8. Chlorophyll-specific nitrate uptake of the stream ecosystem fell within the range measured for isolated algal communities, supporting autotrophic uptake as the mechanism.
9. N enrichment of isolated communities increased uptake; metabolic responses to both N and P enrichment were observed, but nutrient limitation was not definitively established.
