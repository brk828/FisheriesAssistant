# Additional distribution notes on the Mozambique Tilapia of Texas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #palaemonidae #caprellidae #naididae #callianassidae

## Executive Summary

This Notes Section of the *Texas Journal of Science* contains five short distribution reports, mostly documenting first or unusual occurrences of aquatic taxa in Texas and adjacent waters. The notes cover a crustacean ([[Callianassa Latispina]]), an introduced cichlid fish ([[Mozambique Tilapia]]), a river shrimp range extension ([[Macrobrachium Olfersi]]), a skeleton shrimp found on a loggerhead turtle ([[Caprella Andreae]]), and an oligochaete worm record ([[Haemonais Waldvogeli]]). The contributions are observational and anecdotal rather than experimental, and the papers rely on museum specimens, field collections, and personal communications.

## Background & Description

The section is a collection of separate contributions by different authors, linked only by their subject as short regional notes. Each note reports taxonomic identity, locality, collector, and the reasoning behind a distribution claim.

- **Callianassa latispina** (ghost shrimp): known from only three published reports, which suggests it is uncommon across its range.
- **Mozambique Tilapia** (*Tilapia mossambica*): an exotic cichlid recorded from Canyon Lake, Texas, in a region where it had not been reported before.
- **Macrobrachium olfersi**: a freshwater river shrimp of the olfersi group, with new records in Mexico and the first records in Texas.
- **Caprella andreae** (skeleton shrimp): an amphipod associated with floating objects and sea turtles, recorded from the Western Gulf of Mexico for the first time.
- **Haemonais waldvogeli**: an aquatic oligochaete whose North American record is historical and poorly documented.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The notes describe a range of habitats and environmental conditions:

- **Freshwater lakes and reservoirs:** Canyon Lake (Texas), an irrigation reservoir and canal in Cameron County, and Lake Corpus Christi on the Nueces River.
- **Coastal and nearshore marine habitats:** surf zones of North Padre Island and the Gulf coast, where [[Callianassa Latispina]] has been collected.
- **Pelagic floating habitats:** driftwood, buoys, plants, and turtle carapaces, which McCain (1968) identified as the environment where [[Caprella Andreae]] lives and may be ecologically isolated.
- **Temperature limits:** Whiteside links a December 1972 fish kill in Canyon Lake to a surface water temperature of 12 °C, close to the lethal minimum of 10 to 12.8 °C reported for [[Mozambique Tilapia]].
- **Introduction pathways:** Holthuis and Provenzano (1971) suggest that the Florida records of [[Macrobrachium Olfersi]] result from introductions with exotic aquatic plants or fishes from South America.

## Key Findings

### 1. Callianassa latispina: an uncommon, possibly under-recorded ghost shrimp
- Only three published papers report this species, which suggests it is uncommon throughout its range.
- Harper proposes that re-examining *Callianassa* specimens from Gulf coast collections could show that *C. latispina* is more common and more widely distributed than currently known.

### 2. Mozambique Tilapia in Canyon Lake: an unreported population
- Hubbs (1961) recorded established populations only in San Antonio and San Marcos springs. No other Texas records were found.
- Whiteside collected several hundred small specimens at Potters Creek Park, Canyon Lake, on 16 June and 16 August 1972. Butler seined small specimens on 11 July 1972 and took one large specimen in a gill net on 17 October 1972.
- The large specimen probably reflects an accidental or deliberate introduction.
- Despite biannual collections over six years by the author and quarterly Texas Parks and Wildlife Department collections since 1964, these were the only reports from Canyon Lake.
- A fish kill on 20 December 1972 involved 200 to 400 dead or dying individuals, and witnesses reported that many more fish had surfaced before Butler's arrival. The surface water temperature on 22 December 1972 was 12 °C.
- No specimens appeared in 1973, but many were collected in 1974 and 1975, so some fish apparently survived the severe 1972 winter and established a reproducing population.

### 3. Macrobrachium olfersi: range extension into Mexico and Texas
- *M. olfersi* is one of four freshwater river shrimp species known from Texas.
- Holthuis (1952) reported its range as Florida and central Mexico to southern Brazil. Its northernmost Mexican record was Tamazunchale, San Luis Potosi.
- New records extend the range north in Mexico (Rio Corona, 22 mi NE of Ciudad Victoria, Tamaulipas) and document its first Texas occurrences:
  - Irrigation reservoir and adjoining canal of Water District No. 6, 2.5 mi NW of Brownsville, Cameron County.
  - Nueces River below Wesley E. Seale Dam of [[Lake Corpus Christi]].

### 4. Caprella andreae: first Western Gulf of Mexico record
- McCain (1968) listed North American records from Massachusetts, New Jersey, North Carolina, and Florida, plus Havana, Cuba. Only Key West and Havana had been reported from the Gulf of Mexico.
- On 11 March 1972, a dead Atlantic Loggerhead Turtle (*Caretta caretta*) was found in the surf at North Padre Island, Texas (approx. 27° 10′ N, 97° 20′ W). It carried 35 live caprellids among the barnacles (*Chelonibia testudinaria*) on its carapace.
- McCain confirmed the identification in letters dated 28 August and 12 September 1972.
- Eight specimens were retained by McCain, and the rest were deposited in the Texas A&I University collection. All specimens matched McCain's description with no abnormalities.

### 5. Haemonais waldvogeli: a poorly documented North American record
- *Haemonais* is a monotypic genus, based on the synonymy of three described species.
- Brinkhurst and Jamison (1971) list the species from Europe, Asia, Africa, South America, and North America.
- The only North American record is Hayden (1922) from Douglas Lake, Michigan. The note concludes that there are no well-established North American records.

### Overall Significance
The notes document an exotic fish that may have established itself in Texas (Canyon Lake), new Texas and Mexican records for a freshwater shrimp, a new Gulf of Mexico record for a skeleton shrimp associated with sea turtles, and a historical record for an oligochaete worm. Several notes call for more collecting or re-examination of existing specimens to clarify distributions.
