# Oases for Aquatic Life within Agricultural Watersheds and the Economic Value of the Lake Mead Fishery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two items. The main article, by [[Paul C. Marsh]] and [[James E. Luey]], argues that natural stream reaches inside intensively farmed watersheds can act as "oases" for aquatic life and as mitigation devices that may help restore damaged streams. The authors call for research on small watersheds and a large-scale pilot project on a major river. A second item, an economic valuation of the [[Lake Mead Fishery]] with emphasis on [[Largemouth Bass]], estimates a minimum recreational value of $1.41 million for an improved bass fishery. The scientific name of the species is not given in the text.

## Background & Description

**Oasis article.** The authors describe the drainage and channelization of watersheds in the north central United States, where the trend is ongoing. They define the [[Oasis Concept]] as natural, unaltered stream reaches that persist within developed agricultural watersheds. Their stated objectives are to:

1. describe impacts of agricultural drainage and [[Stream Channelization]] on aquatic biota;
2. examine the oasis concept and the role of biological oases in stream restoration;
3. provide perspectives on future research and management of flowing waters in agricultural regions.

**Drainage practices.** Drainage activities include clear-cutting riparian and terrestrial vegetation, draining marshes and wetlands, installing subsurface tile drainage, and ditch construction or stream channelization. These can permanently destroy ecosystem structure and function and reduce hydrologic buffer capacity.

**Lake Mead study.** This item is a recreational economic analysis. About two-thirds of sampled anglers said they would fish more at Lake Mead if the largemouth bass fishery improved. The authors note that a separate [[Largemouth Bass Club]] membership group was included in the analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Channelization effects.** Physical alteration reduces habitat diversity and changes substrate size and stability. Removing riparian vegetation and canopy increases solar radiation, which raises water temperature and alters dissolved gas dynamics. Agricultural chemical runoff can change water chemistry. Flow regimes shift from lotic (flowing-water) to more lentic (still-water) conditions. High insolation and nutrients increase primary production. Invertebrate communities shift from shredders to scrapers as they move from allochthonous (terrestrial) inputs to in-channel production. Fish communities show increased relative abundance of herbivore-omnivore species, and overall communities shift from sensitive species to tolerant ones.

**Scale of drainage.** Nearly 1.7 million acres were drained in 1974 alone. Minnesota, Illinois, Indiana, and Ohio lead in drainage, and the total area drained exceeds 130 million acres, even before 1970. Stream channelization data are harder to obtain because no formal census exists. Soil Conservation Service records for 1976 report:

- 196,934 miles of drainage field ditch;
- 388,810 miles of drainage main or lateral;
- 16,971 miles of open channels.

The authors question the reliability of these figures. Minnesota reported 21,700 miles of channelized streams in 1971, yet the Minnesota Department of Natural Resources lists only 25,000 miles of rivers and streams statewide.

**Downstream evidence.** Downstream effects have received little study. In the [[Black Creek Watershed]] of Indiana, a natural forested reach below channelized sections showed a 28 percent reduction in sediment loads under normal flow. It also had a more stable and diverse resident fauna. Later studies in southwestern Minnesota found no severe adverse effects of upstream drainage and channelization on fishes, macroinvertebrates, or physico-chemical parameters in natural downstream sections. The authors caution that these results cannot yet be generalized to other topographic, geologic, or climatic regions.

**Four parameters of ecological integrity.** Water quality, flow regime, habitat structure, and energy source determine ecological integrity. The authors argue that habitat structure is the most important contribution natural reaches make in mitigating upstream impacts.

**Resilience and thresholds.** Natural streams appear able to absorb upstream impacts that channelized streams cannot. Still, a threshold exists beyond which this capacity is exceeded. Erosion control in the channel and on the land surface is critical to maintaining natural stream function.

## Key Findings

**Oasis article**

- Natural reaches within intensively developed agricultural watersheds can support healthy, diverse biotic communities. The [[Ramsey Creek]] oasis in southwestern Minnesota, about 3 km downstream of a channelized reach, is one example.
- Downstream natural areas showed reduced sediment and turbidity at all but exceptional flows, and in one study a significant water-quality improvement.
- Tile drainage effluent was found to be of remarkably high quality, and tile drainage may reduce chemical inputs by reducing surface runoff.
- The authors hypothesize that natural areas can act both as oases and as mitigation devices. This hypothesis remains untested.
- Future work should include:
  - studies on small watersheds across varied geographic, topographic, and climatic regions;
  - larger sample sizes to identify mitigation mechanisms;
  - evaluation of cumulative effects in higher-order rivers;
  - a large-scale pilot project on an unaltered major river draining an agricultural region, expanding the oasis concept to all tributary systems.
- The authors call for cooperation among aquatic ecology, wildlife biology, hydrology, forestry, soil science, agricultural engineering, the [[Soil Conservation Service]], academic researchers, and local interests. They argue that agricultural and conservation goals need not conflict.
- Predicted benefits include research resources, preservation of diminishing natural resources, public recreational and aesthetic value, and watershed stabilization that also benefits farmers.

**Lake Mead economic analysis**

- Of the sampled anglers, 64.5 percent answered yes when asked whether they would fish more at Lake Mead if bass fishing improved. Of these, 49.9 percent would take more trips of the same length, 3.9 percent would stay longer on the same number of trips, 6.4 percent would take both more and longer trips, and 4.3 percent would not fish more but would enjoy it more.
- Overall, 60.2 percent of anglers indicated increased fishing days at Lake Mead. Among them, 5.3 percent would fish less at other lakes. Across the total sample, 21 percent would fish less at other lakes regardless of whether total fishing increased.
- Of an estimated 40,000 Lake Mead anglers, about 60 percent (24,000) said they would spend more time at the lake if bass fishing improved. Only 25 percent of the sample were club members, so general anglers also wanted a better bass fishery.
- Using a 17.5 percent club and 82.5 percent general-angler split, the authors estimate 4,200 club members and 19,800 general anglers would each spend at least one more day. At about $100 per day for club members and $50 per day for others, the estimated increased value is $420,000 plus $990,000, or **$1,410,000**. This is a minimum value, since many anglers indicated increases larger than one day.
- The authors caution that the number of additional days is unreliable. They also note that the $1.4 million figure must be compared with alternative uses of the lake, such as electricity generation and irrigation, before its significance can be judged.
