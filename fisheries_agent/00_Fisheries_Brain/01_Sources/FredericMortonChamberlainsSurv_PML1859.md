# Frederic Morton Chamberlain's 1904 Survey of Arizona Fishes, with Annotations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #historical-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document is a republished 1904 manuscript by aquatic biologist [[Frederic Morton Chamberlain]], annotated by [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University). The manuscript records Chamberlain's survey of Arizona fishes and aquatic habitats from 15 January to 28 April 1904, mostly in the [[Gila River Basin]]. Its main value is as a baseline: the annotations compare conditions in 1904 with fish occurrences sampled in the 1990s, and show that most of the streams Chamberlain visited had lost much of their native fauna. The journal front matter and the publication's own statements are included in the source and are summarized only briefly here.

## Background & Description

- **Journal context:** The *Journal of the Southwest* is a quarterly published by the Southwest Center at the University of Arizona. The issue reprints the Chamberlain manuscript with editorial commentary.
- **Author:** Chamberlain (1867–1921) was an assistant with the [[U.S. Fish Commission]] (later the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries) from 1897 to 1914. His work included freshwater surveys, salmon studies, oceanographic collections on the U.S.S. *Albatross*, and fur seal assessments.
- **Commission:** Dr. [[Barton Warren Evermann]], Chamberlain's former mentor, commissioned the 1904 Arizona survey.
- **Manuscript:** Handwritten, 52 pages, held in the Smithsonian Institution Archives (RU 7258, Accession No. 16447). Reproduced essentially verbatim, with brackets for clarifications and footnotes added by the editor.
- **Field effort:** About 30 days in the field, with collections and observations at more than 20 localities. Sites are numbered Stations I–XXV in the annotated text.
- **Specimens:** Voucher specimens are housed at the [[U.S. National Museum of Natural History]] (USNM).
- **Method notes:** Chamberlain's water temperatures are in Fahrenheit. Stream discharge was reported in "miner's inches," converted here at 1 miner's inch ≈ 0.025 cfs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline identified in 1904 and still active:**
- Destruction of vegetation by [[Cattle Ranching]] and overgrazing, which increased flooding and erosion.
- [[Irrigation Diversion]] through brush-and-sand dams, which removed water and blocked fish migration.
- [[Mining Pollution]] from copper concentrating operations, sediment, and possibly chemicals.
- Groundwater pumping, which lowered water tables and dried springs and river reaches.
- Introduced non-native fishes, which the annotator identifies as the most important modern factor.
- Arroyo cutting, which drained floodplains and killed riparian vegetation.

**Environmental change by the 1990s:**
- Of 22 sites or stream reaches visited in 1904, 14 are now dry except in flood or are so modified that native fish are sharply reduced.
- Native fish richness at sampled sites fell from an average of 3.2 kinds in 1904 to 2.3 in the 1990s. Historically, an average of 5.7 natives were recorded or expected per site.

## Key Findings

**Survey scope and fauna**
- Chamberlain was apparently selecting sites near the type localities of earlier-described species. He sought 12 of the 15 native Gila Basin fishes then recognized. He secured 8 of these at or near type localities, caught 5 others described from Arizona at other localities, and obtained 3 species not yet recognized by science.
- Of 16 native species he caught, one is extinct, eight are listed as Threatened or Endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and eleven are listed in Mexico.
- Chamberlain recorded or collected only seven non-native species. Over 40 non-native fishes are now known from the Gila Basin, and 84 translocated species were tallied for Arizona by Fuller et al. (1999).

**Notable observations**
- Colorado River at Yuma: bonytails and razorback suckers were abundant in seine hauls, and a Colorado squawfish weighing 9 lb. was observed. Another was reportedly about 100 lb.
- Santa Cruz River: Chamberlain described a Gila chub population and the Sonora topminnow, and recorded the sharp decline of Silver Lake and nearby water tables.
- San Pedro River: Chamberlain recorded declining water levels and the presence of [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] in the basin. The annotation notes that spikedace was last taken near Charleston in 1965.
- San Francisco and Blue Rivers: mine wash and flood sediment were said to have killed fish, and there were no fish seen at several sites.
- Gila River at Safford: fish were abundant in earlier years, but none had been taken in the previous two years. Mineral wash from [[Morenci Mine]] and Clifton was blamed.
- Sienega spring: a population of dark-colored Gila chub was found; it differs from other populations in color.

**Chamberlain's summary of causes**
- Chamberlain's causal model was deforestation, overgrazing, increased flood and erosion, sedimentation, dam obstruction, and mining. The annotator argues that these stressors remain in effect today.

**Historic and modern significance**
- The manuscript has been used by [[Robert Rush Miller]], Minckley, and others as a historical baseline for habitat and fish community change in the American Southwest.
- Annotations on [[Monkey Spring]] and Gila chub, spikedace, and loach minnow populations report modern isolation and listing status.

**Editorial notes**
- Some identifications by Chamberlain were corrected: his "speckled dace" from Verde River was identified as loach minnow, and the record was revised.
- Chamberlain's Ichthyology is considered sound for the period, though he probably under-sampled small fishes such as poeciliids because of the mesh of his seine.
