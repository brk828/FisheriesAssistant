# Threatened Freshwater Fishes of the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #threatened-fishes

## Executive Summary

This paper, reprinted from the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (Vol. 101, No. 2, April 1972, pp. 239–252) and written by [[Robert Rush Miller]] of the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]], is the first compilation of threatened native freshwater fishes for the United States. It lists [[Threatened Species]] for 49 of the 50 states (Rhode Island has none) and includes 305 threatened kinds in a formal classification cross-indexed by state. Each fish is assigned a status of endangered, rare, depleted, or indeterminate using [[IUCN Status Categories]]. The author argues that publishing these lists will support protective legislation and public concern. The lists vary in quality: some rest on extensive recent surveys, while others have little current information. The status assessments reflect information available as of July 1971.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** To provide up-to-date lists of threatened native fishes so that states and the federal government can take protective action. The author notes that recognition of threat is the first step toward perpetuating species.
- **Origin of the lists:** The work was produced by the Endangered Species Committee of the [[American Fisheries Society]] (AFS), in coordination with the Conservation Committee of the [[American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists]] (ASIH). Other sources included the [[Red Book of Rare and Endangered Fish and Wildlife]] (1968) and the [[IUCN Red Data Book]] (Vol. 4, Pisces, 1969).
- **Context:** Serious concern for U.S. native fishes grew mainly after 1963. The [[Endangered Species Preservation Act]] of 1966 was described as the first concerted federal effort to protect native fauna. The author also mentions a June 1971 bill to establish a Desert Pupfish National Monument, which would protect pupfishes east of Death Valley.
- **Nomenclature:** Family arrangements and common and scientific names follow the AFS Committee on Names of Fishes (Bailey et al., 1970). All state lists were submitted to the states for approval before publication.
- **Peripheral populations:** Common species that are rare in a particular state were listed as rare in some states but not others. The author argues that these disjunct populations merit inclusion because isolated gene pools may have practical and scientific value.

### Status Definitions

| Status | Definition (IUCN-based) |
|---|---|
| Endangered | Actively threatened with extinction; survival unlikely without special protective measures |
| Rare | Not under immediate threat, but in small numbers or a restricted or specialized habitat; requires careful watching |
| Depleted | Still numerous enough to survive, but heavily reduced and declining faster than can be sustained |
| Indeterminate | Apparently threatened, but insufficient data for a reliable assessment |

The author emphasizes that the shared factor among threatened species is vulnerability to extinction, not necessarily a current decline. Some "rare" species are relatively stable, and a "depleted" species may remain listed after its numbers begin to recover.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper identifies the main causes of decline in U.S. freshwater fish faunas:

- **Pollution:** industrial, agricultural, and domestic sources, including toxic chemicals and pesticides
- **Dam construction:** converts free-flowing (lotic) habitat to still-water (lentic) habitat
- **Land use:** deforestation and overgrazing
- **Channelization** of streams
- **Groundwater removal:** excessive extraction
- **Introduced exotic species:** especially predators or species with broad ecological tolerances. Exotics can also transmit parasites and diseases.

The author cites a wide literature on these factors, including [[Deacon and Bunnell]] (1970), [[Hubbs]] (1963), [[Lachner et al.]] (1970), [[Larimore and Smith]] (1963), [[Minckley and Deacon]] (1968), [[Myers]] (1965), and [[Smith]] (1971), among others. The paper also notes that the responsibility for managing and regulating fish harvest lies with the individual states, which must provide legal protection and enforcement.

## Key Findings

- **Scale of threat:** 305 native freshwater fish kinds are listed as threatened across the United States. The list covers 49 of 50 states. Rhode Island has no threatened fishes.
- **Taxonomic spread:** Threatened taxa occur in many families, including lampreys, sturgeons, paddlefishes, gars, salmonids (trouts, chars, whitefishes, and ciscoes), minnows (Cyprinidae, the largest group in the list), suckers, catfishes, cavefishes, killifishes and pupfishes, livebearers, sunfishes, perches and darters, sculpins, and gobies (Hawaiian stream species).
- **Western concentration:** Many listings are for western species with restricted ranges, such as the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (Nevada), the [[Humpback Chub]] (Arizona, Colorado, Utah, Wyoming), the [[Woundfin]] (Arizona, Nevada, Utah), the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, Utah), and the [[Cui-ui]] (Nevada).
- **Nationally endangered forms:** Forms recognized as nationally endangered are marked with an asterisk in Table 1. Examples include the [[Lake Sturgeon]] (listed for many states), the [[Gila Trout]] (New Mexico), the [[Pecos Gambusia]] (Texas, New Mexico), and the [[Gila Topminnow]] (Arizona).
- **Eastern and Midwestern listings:** Many eastern and central species are listed, especially darters, shiners, madtoms, and sturgeons. Examples include the [[Pallid Sturgeon]] (listed in about nine states), the [[Shortnose Sturgeon]] (listed along the Atlantic coast), and the [[Atlantic Salmon]] (listed in several northeastern states).
- **State-level protection:** Several states already legally protect listed species. California fully protects its asterisked species under its State Fish and Game Code (enacted 1970). Indiana protects all of its listed fishes from any harvest. Hawaii and Idaho have specific regulations for some species, such as Idaho's requirement that sturgeon be released.
- **Data quality:** Data quality varies by state. Some lists rest on recent extensive surveys, while others have "almost nil" current information. The author notes examples where a species may already be extinct in a state (for example, the Longjaw Cisco in Michigan, which is described as almost extinct, and the Sunapee Trout in Vermont, which is presumed extinct after more than 30 years without a report).
- **Extinction cautions:** Several entries are marked "possibly extinct" or "extinct." Kansas evidence suggests that the Bigeye Chub and the Blacknose Shiner no longer exist in that state.
- **Purpose and recommendations:** The author expects publication to strengthen protective legislation and public concern, while noting that ongoing surveys, funding, and skilled personnel are needed. The author states that "it will take money and the efforts of dedicated and skilled people" working through legal channels to halt the depletion of fish resources.
