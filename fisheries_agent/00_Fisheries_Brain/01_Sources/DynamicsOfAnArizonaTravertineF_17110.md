# Time-of-Flow Chemistry of a Travertine-Forming Desert Stream Leaving Montezuma Well, Arizona

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #travertine

## Executive Summary

This study tracks single water masses flowing from [[Montezuma Well]], a [[Limnocrene]] in central Arizona, down a 1.57 km [[Irrigation Canal]] over roughly one hour. Sampling took place at noon, dusk and dawn in June 1968 and October 1968. The authors report that dissolved solutes and milliequivalents fell along the canal, that free carbon dioxide was lost rapidly, and that pH rose downstream. Calcium carbonate precipitated as [[Travertine Deposition]], an estimated 88.8 kg per day. Chloride, sulfate, sodium, potassium and magnesium showed no consistent decline. Dissolved oxygen gains could not be reconciled with the large CO2 losses, and the data suggest nocturnal uptake of silica and phosphate by [[Diatoms]].

## Background & Description

[[Montezuma Well]] is a limnocrene with an area of 0.76 ha and a mean depth of 6.7 m. It occupies a circular depression in the Plio-Pleistocene [[Verde Formation]] at about 1,112 m above sea level. Since 1947 it has been a detached part of [[Montezuma Castle National Monument]]. The site lies in Upper Sonoran desert-grassland, with a mean annual temperature of about 10.6°C and mean annual precipitation of 28 cm.

Water enters through deep fissures on the bottom and leaves at a southeast outlet called the "swallet." The swallet water runs underground and re-emerges 60–65 m away, about 5 m lower, at a cave mouth. This outer outlet feeds an irrigation ditch that is partly an ancient Indian canal. The ditch averages about 1 m wide and is never deeper than 1 m.

Earlier studies (1960–64) used grab samples and recorded minima in specific conductance, filtrable residue, total alkalinity, EDTA hardness, free CO2 and hydrogen-ion concentration downstream. The 1968 work was designed as a time-of-flow study, following one water mass from origin to a fixed endpoint. The authors note that most lotic chemistry literature addresses mesic or humid streams, and that an arid-region travertine stream (Birch Creek, California) showed nothing as dramatic as the Montezuma effluent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stations and flow.** Three stations were used:

- **Station I:** the outer outlet at the cave mouth, about 65 m from the Well. Water takes about 7 minutes to get here from the swallet, based on earlier fluorescein dye tests. Aquatic mosses, *Batrachospermum* and colonies of the peritrich ciliate *Carchesium* were present.
- **Station II:** 1,053 m downstream. Travel time is 40–45 minutes. Patches of *Hypocotyle* and *Zannichellia* were present.
- **Station III:** 518 m further (15–20 minutes), at a Parshall flume just before the ditch leaves Monument property. Most of the flow is shaded by riparian trees. Total altitudinal drop is no more than 10 m.

**Flow volumes.** Inflow at Station I was 6,165 m³/day in June and 6,497 m³/day in October. Hourly discharge was about 256.8 m³/hr in June and 270.7 m³/hr in October.

**Climate and temperature.** June 3 was clear, with incoming radiation near its annual peak of about 625 cal/cm²/day. June 4 was cloudy. October 11 was overcast and October 12 was clear. Water temperature varied by 5°C during the June study and by only 1°C in October.

**Macrophytes.** The dominant macrophyte in the Well is *Potamogeton illinoensis*, whose beds bound the lake to about the 7.5 m contour. The authors describe the Well population as atypical for the species. Standing-crop dry weight from shore to 7.5–8 m is about 800 g/m². A week before the June study, Monument workers removed most aquatic macrophytes from the ditch to improve flow.

## Key Findings

**1. Solute loss along the canal.** Total milliequivalents and computed dissolved solids declined from Station I to III in every series. The decrease was greatest at noon and smallest at dawn. October computed decreases were 21.1, 9.2 and 1.3 mg/L for the noon, dusk and dawn series. Losses always occurred in the final 20-minute flow.

**2. Conservative ions.** Chloride, sulfate, sodium, potassium and magnesium showed no temporal or spatial trend, so they cannot account for the solute loss. Mean values were:

| Ion | June | October |
|---|---|---|
| Chloride (meq/L) | 0.996 | 0.969 |
| Sulfate (meq/L) | 0.235 | 0.226 |
| Sodium (meq/L) | 2.593 | 2.404 |
| Potassium (meq/L) | 0.161 | 0.117 |

Concentrations were slightly higher in June, possibly because the Well delivered more than 5% more water in October.

**3. Free CO2 and pH.** Free CO2 was highest at the outlet at dawn and fell at every station. Losses were 1.0–1.6 mg/L/min during most June flows and 1.6–1.7 mg/L/min in October. The June dawn run was an exception, losing 12.9 mg/L/min from a very high starting concentration (the authors report a gain of 791 mg/L from dusk to dawn). Mean pH increments from Station I to II and II to III were 0.72 and 0.23 units in June, and 0.76 and 0.07 in October. The rate of pH rise was about 0.014–0.016 units per minute. The authors argue that photosynthesis alone cannot explain the CO2 losses. They suggest that bubbles from *Potamogeton* beds (about 1 L gas/m²/daylight hour in 1961 funnel-jar experiments) and possible variation in gas entering through the fissures contribute.

**4. Dissolved oxygen and primary productivity.** Nearly all samples were at or above 91% saturation (one October sample was 88.4%). Oxygen rose from dusk to dawn in both studies, so nocturnal respiration could not be estimated from oxygen decline. Net productivity was estimated only from oxygen above 100% saturation, using the method of Odum (1956):

- June A (noon) series: 0.124 g O2/m²/hr
- June D series: 0.302 g O2/m²/hr
- October noon series: 0.074 g O2/m²/hr
- October dawn series: about 0.05 g O2/m²/hr
- October D series: 0.169 g O2/m²/hr

The authors conclude that areal productivity is low compared with eutrophic lakes, but that volumetric productivity is comparable to most eutrophic waters because the maximum depth is under 0.75 m.

**5. Calcium carbonate precipitation.** Calcium and alkalinity both decreased in the lower reaches. The authors estimate that 77.4 kg (June) and 81.2 kg (October) of CaCO3 left the outlet each hour. About 0.7–0.8 kg was added by Station II, and 3.5–3.9 kg was precipitated in the final flow. The total CaCO3 precipitated was **88.8 kg/day** (about 195.4 lb/day). The ditch flume must be cleaned of travertine regularly, and one dry segment near Station II looks as if it was concrete-lined.

**6. Calcite saturation.** The saturation index was positive at Stations II and III in nearly all series, meaning the water was supersaturated and calcite could precipitate. It was usually near zero or negative at the outlet. Langelier-based values were 0.01–0.25 pH units higher than those from the [[Dussart Nomogram]]. The authors note that the June outlet appeared undersaturated in one method and supersaturated in the other. October data suggest supersaturation throughout the 24-hour period at Stations II and III.

**7. Alkalinity and hardness.** Total alkalinity fell by 0.28 and 0.08 meq/L between the outlet and Station III in the June noon and dawn series. The October noon, dusk and dawn losses were 0.24, 0.16 and 0.04 meq/L. The alkalinity/hardness ratio was about 1.15 in fresh samples. The authors attribute this to **microparticulate CaCO3** that is dissolved by the acid used in alkalinity titration. Bubbling CO2 into stored water raised the alkalinity/hardness ratio toward 1.0 and increased both measurements.

**8. Calcium and magnesium.** Calcium at Station III was lower than at the upstream stations in all but one series. Mean calcium was 6.015 meq/L at the outlet and 5.738 meq/L at Station III in June, and 5.988 and 5.781 meq/L in October. The Ca:Mg ratio fell downstream (June: 2.8, 2.3, 2.2; October: 2.3, 2.3, 2.1), which the authors attribute to the greater solubility of magnesium compounds.

**9. Silica, phosphate and iron.** Silica was measured only in October. The mean for the series excluding dawn was 24.1 mg/L. At dawn, silica was lower (17.4 mg/L at Stations I and II, 17.5 mg/L at III). The authors interpret this as nocturnal diatom uptake. Ortho-phosphate left the Well at about 2.1 kg/day in early June 1968. It was lowest at all stations during the dawn series. The N:P ratio was about 2.5. Mean total iron was 0.038 mg/L, similar to June values. Other reported constituents were boron (2 mg/L), fluoride (0.25–0.5 mg/L) and manganese (undetectable).

**10. Overall conclusion.** The authors describe the outflow as a system in which free CO2 is lost at a high rate, pH rises, and calcium carbonate precipitates within about one hour. Total solutes fall, and the most striking CO2 changes occur at dawn, when the water's CO2 content is highest. The authors state that Montezuma Well has an exaggerated reputation for physicochemical stability, since these inputs and losses vary from day to day.
