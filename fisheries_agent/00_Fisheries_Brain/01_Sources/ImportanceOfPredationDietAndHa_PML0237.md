# Importance of Predation, Diet, and Distribution of Lepidomeda vittata (Little Colorado Spinedace) on the Coconino National Forest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1990 report by Dean W. Blinn and Clay Runck (Northern Arizona University), submitted to the [[Coconino National Forest]], examines why the federally listed [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*) persists in some reaches of the [[Little Colorado River]] system but not others. The study combined physico-chemical limnology along [[Nutrioso Creek]], electrofishing surveys in Nutrioso, Chevelon and East Clear creeks, stomach content analyses, and laboratory predation experiments. The authors conclude that [[Rainbow Trout]] predation is likely a major factor limiting the species' distribution, while [[Green Sunfish]] predation is strongly reduced by natural refuges.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Lepidomeda vittata* is endemic to Arizona, occurring in disjunct populations in the Little Colorado River and several north-flowing tributaries in Navajo and Apache counties.
- **Status:** Earlier reports indicated abundance in East Clear Creek, but recent surveys found very low numbers or local extirpation. The species was listed as Federally Threatened.
- **Prior work:** [[Minckley]] (1984) documented distribution in the Little Colorado River, Nutrioso Creek and Chevelon Creek; [[Marsh and Young]] (1988) reported a "viable and reproductive population" in the lower Nutrioso Creek.
- **Study rationale:** Nutrioso Creek offers a habitat gradient with and without spinedace within less than 50 km, making it suitable for identifying restricting parameters.
- **Sampling period:** Field work occurred in May and July 1990.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site:** Four sites along Nutrioso Creek on an elevational gradient, from 2469 m (Site 1, upstream, spinedace absent) to 2222 m (Site 4, downstream).

**Physico-chemical conditions:**
- Diel water temperature in May ranged 4.5–19 °C at Site 1 and 2.0–26.5 °C at Sites 2–4; July ranges were 12–19 °C and 13–25 °C.
- Dissolved oxygen in May ranged 2.9–11.4 mg L⁻¹ at Site 1 versus 5.1–11.0 mg L⁻¹ at Sites 2–4; in July, Site 1 varied 8.3–9.5 mg L⁻¹ versus 1.5–9.2 mg L⁻¹ downstream.
- Total alkalinity rose from 102–120 mg L⁻¹ (Site 1) to 295–360 mg L⁻¹ (Site 4); pH stayed between 7.5 and 9.5.
- Turbidity increased downstream and was considerably higher in July (up to 250 FTU).
- Discharge declined downstream; Site 4 was ponded and unmeasurable in July.

**Periphyton and macrophytes:** Diatoms dominated poor habitats (at least 60%) and about 30% of riffles; cyanobacteria were 55% of riffle periphyton. Filamentous green algae (*Mougeotia*, *Oedogonium*, *Ulothrix*) dominated pool habitats. Macrophyte composition varied by site, with *Myriophyllum* at Site 2 and the greatest diversity at Site 4.

**Macroinvertebrates:** Dipteran larvae (chironomids, ceratopogonids) made up at least 60% of insects in pools and at least 30% in riffles. Mean combined densities were 3692 animals m⁻² in pools and 3357 animals m⁻² in riffles. The highest density (13,510 animals m⁻²) occurred at Site 2 in May; the lowest (32 animals m⁻²) at Site 4 in July.

**Fish community:**
- Site 1 (Nutrioso Creek): only [[Rainbow Trout]] (94%) and [[Speckled Dace]] (6%); trout density 0.4 fish m⁻² (May) and 0.3 fish m⁻² (July).
- Sites 2–4: spinedace comprised about 26.2% of the fish community; highest density 2.6 fish m⁻² in a cobble-riffle at Site 3 in May.
- Chevelon Creek "Steps" area: spinedace, [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] made up over 90% of fish; spinedace were about 20% of the community. Green sunfish density was 0.12 fish m⁻², spinedace about 0.11 fish m⁻².
- East Clear Creek: spinedace not collected; speckled dace, fathead minnows and rainbow trout were most common.

## Key Findings

**Diet**
- Spinedace are opportunists. Stomach contents in May were dominated by chironomid larvae (36.3%) and mayfly nymphs (23.3%); in July terrestrial invertebrates (21.5%), crustaceans (17.9%) and detritus (12.2%) dominated.
- Chevelon Creek fish (June) ate chironomids and mayflies (33%), filamentous algae (26.9%) and vascular plant fragments (18.1%); over 20% of stomachs were empty.
- Larger fish showed more empty stomachs and ate more terrestrial insects and filamentous algae.

**Reproduction**
- Length–weight regressions were significant (p < 0.01) for both sexes; the male equation was wet weight = −5.93 + 0.15 × TL and the female equation was wet weight = −7.49 + 0.19 × TL.
- Egg diameter ranged from 20–50 µm (immature) to 1000–1290 µm (mature).
- Mature eggs were found only in May in Nutrioso Creek, suggesting spawning in late spring to early summer. Fish of 50 mm lacked mature eggs; some appeared at 64 mm.

**Laboratory predation experiments**
- [[Green Sunfish]] consumed significantly fewer spinedace when rock refuges were present (0.2 vs 2.3 fish, t = 2.77, df = 16, p < 0.001) and vegetation refuges reduced predation (0.9 vs 1.6 fish, p = 0.05).
- [[Rainbow Trout]] from Nutrioso Creek consumed about 1.7 spinedace per trial with or without rock refuges; the difference was not significant (p = 0.15). Hatchery trout did not prey on spinedace.
- [[Belostomatid]] predators (*Aedes* sp.) captured prey in 73% of trials with rock refuges and 20% without; the difference was not significant (p = 0.13 reported in the text, with df = 19).

**Laboratory feeding and survival**
- Spinedace consumed a wide range of food items (amphipods, daphnids, mayfly and damselfly nymphs, snails, crayfish, and filamentous algae), but whether any single item sustains them long term was unresolved.

**Main conclusions**
1. A relatively large population of spinedace occurs in Nutrioso Creek between Nelson Reservoir and Nutrioso.
2. Spinedace are scarce or absent where rainbow trout are abundant (upstream of Nutrioso), and the sharp boundary between species occurred over less than 10 km.
3. Algal periphyton and invertebrates did not appear limiting in May, but both were lower in July.
4. Rainbow trout predation is likely the principal factor restricting spinedace distribution across Arizona drainages; the authors recommend further field and laboratory predation studies to confirm this.
5. Refuges protected spinedace from green sunfish but not from rainbow trout, and sunfish and spinedace coexist at Chevelon Creek, where sandstone and vegetation refuges are abundant.
