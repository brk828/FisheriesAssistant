# The Native Fish Fauna of Major Drainages East of the Continental Divide in New Mexico

#ichthyology #zoogeography #biogeography #freshwater-fish #historical-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Ictaluridae #Cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Master's thesis (December 1984) presents a comprehensive synthesis of the native fish fauna inhabiting drainages east of the [[Continental Divide]] in [[New Mexico]], reconstructing the ichthyofauna as it existed circa 1550 AD. The study documents 51 native species across five major drainage systems: [[Rio Grande Basin]], [[Pecos River Basin]], [[Canadian River Basin]], [[Mimbres River Basin]], and [[Tularosa Basin]]. Analysis of nearly 10,000 museum records dating from 1851 to present, combined with paleontological evidence, archaeological fish remains, geomorphological data, and historical accounts, reveals that 14 native species or subspecies have become extirpated and 22 have severely restricted ranges since European settlement. The research establishes that most native species gained access to New Mexico waters during late Tertiary to mid-Pleistocene time through complex drainage connections with the [[Mississippi River]] system, [[Rio Conchos]], and northern Mexican drainages. Geographic isolation produced highly endemic faunas, particularly in the [[Pecos River]], [[Mimbres River]], and [[Tularosa Basin]].

## Background & Description

The earliest documentation of New Mexico's native fish fauna comes from naturalists attached to government survey parties during the 1850s. Without these early collections, the historical record would be severely deficient. Since the late Miocene, physiographical changes linked New Mexico to varying degrees with ichthyofaunal elements from the [[Rocky Mountains]], [[Great Plains]], [[Chihuahuan Desert]], [[Mexican Plateau]], [[Sonoran Desert]], and [[Great Basin]]. Fourteen families comprising 67 species historically occupied New Mexico's waters, with strikingly different faunas evolving east and west of the [[Continental Divide]].

The study area encompasses five major drainage systems east of the Continental Divide:

**Rio Grande Basin**: The [[Rio Grande]] originates in the [[San Juan Mountains]] of southwestern Colorado and flows 1,887 miles to the [[Gulf of Mexico]]. In New Mexico, it flows through deeply incised canyons in the [[Taos Plateau]] (approximately 1,000 feet deep), then through broad valleys. Stream gradients of 25 feet per mile are common upstream of [[Cochiti Pueblo]]; 5 feet per mile prevail downstream to [[El Paso]]. Prior to Anglo-European settlement, the Rio Grande was perennial throughout New Mexico, characterized by numerous marshes, oxbow pools, and fringe-forests of cottonwoods and willows. Evidence includes [[Scaphirhynchus platorynchus]] (Shovelnose Sturgeon) taken near Albuquerque in 1873 and [[Cycleptus elongatus]] (Blue Sucker) remains from Indian sites west of Santa Fe, indicating the upper Rio Grande was once a larger river of more reliable flow.

**Mimbres Drainage**: An isolated fluvial system of the endorheic [[Guzman Basin]]. Perennial flow exists in two discrete segments separated by 11 km of normally dry riverbed. The headwaters originate on the western slope of the [[Black Range]]. Antisell (1857) described the terminus as a series of pools or lagoons surrounded by willow thickets, with the river up to 2.5 feet deep flowing at 2.5 miles per hour in summer. Overgrazing since the late 1800s has made the river wider and shallower.

**Pecos Drainage**: The [[Pecos River]] originates in the [[Sangre de Cristo Mountains]] and extends 920 km SSE to its confluence with the Rio Grande in west Texas. Four major reservoirs (Santa Rosa, Sumner, McMillan, and Avalon dams) now impound water. Captain John Pope (1854) described the Pecos near the [[Delaware River]] confluence as having "bold and rapid current, at a rate of nine knots per hour... about eighty feet wide, and two and a half feet deep." South of the 32nd parallel, he noted the river was "always navigable for small stern-wheeled boats" with perpendicular banks about ten feet high and average width not exceeding 100 feet.

**Tularosa Basin**: An endorheic basin approximately 150 miles long and 60 miles wide (6,000 square miles) bounded by the [[Jicarilla Mountains]], [[Sierra Blanca]], [[Sacramento Mountains]], and other ranges. Perennial water is limited to isolated springs and seeps with highly alkaline water. Gibbs (1870) described "a valley of some 200 miles long and 20 miles wide... in which there is no stream and only a few alkaline springs and salt lakes or ponds."

**Canadian Drainage**: The [[Canadian River]] originates on the east slope of the Sangre de Cristo Mountains and flows approximately 312 river miles in New Mexico before joining the [[Arkansas River]], a tributary of the [[Mississippi River]]. The basin comprises about 12,500 square miles with approximately equal areas of mountainous, plateau, and plains regions. Perennial tributaries include the [[Vermejo River]], [[Cimarron River]], [[Mora River]], and [[Ocate Creek]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study documents dramatic anthropogenic impacts on New Mexico's aquatic ecosystems beginning after the Civil War. Western migration of Anglo-European settlers accelerated, with large populations settling especially in the Rio Grande Valley. By 1880, nearly all irrigable land in the Rio Grande Valley was under development, and water shortages were already being reported. By the end of the 19th century, grasslands were severely overgrazed, resulting in more erratic stream flows, accelerated soil erosion, and stream channels becoming wider and shallower. Lee (1907) described the Rio Grande north of El Paso as "mainly a flood-water stream subject to great fluctuations in volume."

Beginning in 1916, numerous large dams were constructed on the Rio Grande and major tributaries, including Cabillo, Elephant Butte, Jemez, Cochiti, Gallisteo, McClure, Abiquiu, El Vado, and Heron. Water releases from these reservoirs fluctuate severely and downstream flows are often critically depleted. The first Pecos River impoundment (McMillan Reservoir) was constructed in 1893. During irrigation season (April-October), releases fluctuate severely and downstream flows are often critically depleted. No flows have been recorded in localized portions of the Pecos River for 10% of each water year.

The geomorphic evolution of New Mexico's drainages was critical to fish distribution patterns. During middle to late Pliocene time, the Rio Grande flowed south from the San Juan Mountains across the [[Taos Plateau]], then along the east side of the Socorro Valley through the [[Jornada del Muerto]] into south central New Mexico, eventually reaching the [[Rio Conchos]] at Presidio. During late Pliocene to early Pleistocene, the Rio Grande was diverted west of El Paso into the playa region of northern Mexico. During late Pliocene to middle Pleistocene, the ancestral Pecos River was confluent with the ancestral Rio Conchos, which drained volcanic highlands of northern Mexico and flowed east and southeastward into the Gulf of Mexico. The climate was much wetter and cooler than present.

Major mountain ranges between the Pecos River and Rio Grande (Sangre de Cristo and San Andres mountains) were uplifted during late Pliocene time, depriving the ancestral Pecos River of much of its headwaters, which were diverted into the newly established south-flowing Rio Grande. Uplift of the [[Guadalupe Mountains]] and Sacramento Mountains during late Tertiary to middle Pleistocene time "beheaded" the ancestral Pecos River in the area of [[Sierra Blanca]]. Following this uplift, the beheaded ancestral Pecos River became a headward eroding stream that slowly worked north and eastward, capturing several streams during late Pleistocene time, including the [[Colorado River of Texas]] and the upper ancestral [[Brazos River]].

During late Tertiary to middle Pleistocene time, the upper Canadian River drained into the Pecos River in the vicinity of Fort Sumner. During late Pleistocene time (or possibly more recently), a headward-eroding lower Canadian River captured the ancestral "Canadian" tributary of the Pecos River, establishing the Pecos and Canadian rivers in their present courses.

Climatic conditions during the Pleistocene were critical to fish dispersal. During late Pleistocene time, life zones were shifted 1,200 m lower than their present position and alternating glacial and pluvial conditions prevailed. The last major pluvial maximum occurred approximately 20,000 years ago, with pluvial conditions persisting until 12,000 B.P. These conditions enabled species to occupy lower elevations and more southern latitudes. The ensuing warmer and drier climates of Holocene time made waters of southern latitudes unsuitable for many species, resulting in extirpations and range restrictions.

## Key Findings

**Species Composition by Drainage**: The study documents 51 native species east of the Continental Divide, with highly variable composition among drainages:
- Rio Grande Basin: 26 species
- Pecos River Basin: 38 species (highest diversity)
- Canadian River Basin: 24 species
- Mimbres River Basin: 5 species (highly endemic)
- Tularosa Basin: 1 species ([[Cyprinodon tularosa]])

**Zoogeographic Origins and Dispersal Routes**:

*Colorado Plateau Origin*: [[Catostomus plebeius]] (Rio Grande Sucker) likely entered the ancestral upper Rio Grande from the southeastern edge of the Colorado Plateau during middle to late Pliocene time by way of a mid-elevation transfer. Its ecological affinity for mid-elevation streams prevented eastward expansion over mountains but facilitated southern range expansion. The species likely gained access to the [[Guzman Basin]] (including Mimbres River) during late Pliocene to early Pleistocene when the Rio Grande flowed west of El Paso into northern Mexico.

[[Salmo clarki virginalis]] (Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout) also likely gained access to the upper Rio Grande from the Colorado Plateau during middle to late Pliocene time. Unlike C. plebeius, its ecological affinity for headwater habitats facilitated eastward expansion by headwater transfers into the Pecos drainage and possibly the Canadian drainage. Subsequent geographic isolation allowed genetic differences to accumulate between populations, with broad patterns conforming geographically to the three major drainages. Emory (1848) reported trout from the Mimbres River, suggesting a native salmonid may have once been present in the Guzman Basin, though it now appears extirpated.

*Mexican/Rio Conchos Origin*: During late Pliocene to middle Pleistocene time, when the ancestral Pecos River was confluent with the ancestral Rio Conchos, several species ascended the Pecos River into New Mexico, including: [[Anguilla rostrata]] (American Eel), [[Dorosoma cepedianum]] (Gizzard Shad), [[Astyanax mexicanus]] (Mexican Tetra), [[Dionda episcopa]] (Roundnose Minnow), [[Ictiobus bubalus]] (Smallmouth Buffalo), [[Moxostoma congestum]] (Gray Redhorse), an ancestral [[Cyprinodon]], and [[Lucania parva]] (Rainwater Killifish). The native occurrence of L. parva is substantiated by morphometric and meristic evidence; others are substantiated by early collection records and regional distribution patterns. [[Dorosoma petenense]] also gained access during this time, evidenced by a fossil from late Pliocene deposits near Loving, New Mexico, but was likely eliminated by arid Holocene conditions and cold winters.

The progenitor of [[Gambusia nobilis]] (Pecos Gambusia) likely gained access to the ancestral Pecos River during late Pliocene to middle Pleistocene time. This species evolved from a highly variable and widespread progenitor that also gave rise to five other modern Gambusia species occurring allopatrically from south central Texas to northeastern Mexico. G. nobilis evolved in isolated spring habitats, with intervening populations victimized by interglacial drought conditions and subsequent competition/hybridization with [[Gambusia affinis]].

*Yaqui Basin Origin*: During middle Pleistocene time, after the Rio Grande was diverted east from northern Mexico through El Paso Canyon, [[Notropis formosus]] (Chihuahua Shiner) and the progenitor of [[Gila nigrescens]] (Chihuahua Chub) likely gained access to the Guzman Basin from the [[Rio Yaqui]] drainage via a low elevation headwater transfer, perhaps via headwaters of the [[Rio Papigochic]] near Mifaca, Chihuahua, which may have been confluent with the [[Rio Casas Grandes]] of the Guzman drainage. An ancestral Cyprinodon and [[Pimephales promelas]] (Fathead Minnow) likely gained access by the same route.
