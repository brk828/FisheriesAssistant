# Good Aquaculture Feed Manufacturing Practice: FAO Technical Guidelines

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture-feed

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Department publication (2001; FAO Technical Guidelines for Responsible Fisheries No. 5, Supplement 1) provides technical guidelines for [[Good Manufacturing Practice]] (GMP) in the production of compound [[Aquafeed]] for farmed aquatic animals. It was compiled to support Article 9 of the [[FAO Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries]] (CCRF), particularly Article 9.4.3 on the selection and use of feeds and additives. The guidelines cover feed mill siting and design, ingredient purchasing and quality control, receiving, storage, processing, formulation, packaging and labelling, warehousing and shipping, sampling, product recall, plant hygiene, maintenance, personnel, and documentation. On-farm feed management is explicitly excluded and reserved for separate guidance.

## Background & Description

The document opens with a background on the [[Code of Conduct for Responsible Fisheries]], adopted by FAO Conference Resolution 4/95 on 31 October 1995. Key steps in its development include:

- Recognition that living aquatic resources are renewable but finite, prompting the 1982 [[United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea]] framework, under which coastal States control fisheries within areas embracing about 90 percent of the world's marine fisheries.
- The 1992 [[Cancun Declaration]] and the [[Rio Declaration]] (UNCED), which supported developing a code of conduct.
- The [[Agreement to Promote Compliance with International Conservation and Management Measures]] by Fishing Vessels on the High Seas (1993), which forms an integral part of the Code.
- A code of five introductory articles followed by six thematic articles, including Aquaculture Development (Article 9).

The guidelines were drafted by Dick Stephen-Hassard and [[Albert Tacon]] following a 1998 FAO Expert Consultation on Animal Feeding and Food Safety. A working paper was presented at the World Aquaculture Society conference (Las Vegas, February 1998) and at the Second Conference of the Feed Manufacturers of the Mediterranean (Reus, Spain, March 1999). A revised second draft was sent in March 2001 to over 200 representatives of the aquafeed and feed manufacturing industry in more than 50 countries. The guidelines are described as preliminary and subject to revision.

**Scope and terminology.** The document defines more than 40 feed-industry terms drawn largely from [[AAFCO]] (2000) and FAO/WHO (1999) sources, including *complete feed*, *compound feed*, *fish meal*, *shrimp meal*, *micro-ingredients*, *premix*, *HACCP*, *hazard*, *risk*, and *farm-made aquafeeds*. The reference list contains roughly 150 entries, mainly peer-reviewed literature, FAO/WHO reports, and industry bulletins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The guidelines emphasise that aquafeeds differ from terrestrial feeds because of the aquatic medium and the animals' biology:

- Slow-feeding species such as [[Marine Shrimp]] require feeds that remain physically stable in water for several hours.
- Farmed aquatic animals are generally much smaller than terrestrial livestock; marine shrimp reach marketable size at only about 20 grams.
- Feed production therefore demands high precision: ingredient particle size may need to be reduced to as small as 50 microns, and up to four dozen ingredients may be mixed into a very small feed particle.
- Plant-protein-based diets are typical for temperate freshwater species, whereas marine diets rely largely on [[Fish Meal]] and other fishery by-products.

Food safety is a central concern. Mycotoxins from mouldy feedstuffs can have detrimental effects at concentrations of a few parts per billion, and more than 100 mycotoxins exist. Rodenticide-treated seed (indicated by bright colour) is highly toxic to aquatic animals and humans. Pesticide and veterinary residues can make aquatic products unmarketable if national action levels are exceeded. Pathogens of concern include *Salmonella*, and the guidelines note that on-farm husbandry has the greater influence on product wholesomeness. The guidelines also warn against recycling farmed fish or shellfish processing wastes, fish meal, or dead animals into feed, to prevent disease spread.

The document states that adverse health impacts from compound animal feeds have been negligible under the widespread use of GMP (FAO, 1998), but that research is still needed to demonstrate the management benefits of quality assurance programmes and pathogen-negative feed.

## Key Findings

**1. Feed mill siting and design**
- Avoid areas prone to flooding, tidal inundation, and fire where possible; a mill that cannot deliver on schedule puts farmers at risk.
- Design for expansion and flexibility, and build out vermin and bird exclusion.
- Drain spills and runoff, and provide sump pumps in wet areas.

**2. Ingredient selection and purchasing**
- Buyers should hold written ingredient standards and buy only from reputable suppliers.
- Suppliers that repeatedly deliver substandard ingredients should be removed from the supplier list and reinstated only after corrective action.
- Ingredient specifications should be reviewed annually.
- Mouldy, dyed, or discoloured grain must not be used.
- Suppliers' certificates of analysis, insurance, and quality assurance programmes should be requested, and new suppliers should be inspected on-site.
- Feeding of farmed fish and shellfish processing wastes to farmed animals should be avoided.

**3. Receiving**
- Verify labels, lot numbers, and regulatory compliance; inspect colour, odour, foreign matter, and insects.
- Count bags against delivery tickets; record variances and report them for claims.
- Maintain a documented chain of custody and apply first-in, first-out rotation.
- Check bins daily for bridging, with strict safety precautions against engulfment.

**4. Storage and handling**
- Keep dry ingredients below 13 percent moisture, particularly in humid and tropical areas.
- Clean storage tanks monthly to prevent dust build-up, which harbours mould, insects, and fire risk.
- Store sacked feed off the ground on pallets and out of direct sunlight.
- Propionic acid and other antifungal agents may affect palatability and efficacy.
- Misformulated or returned feed must be segregated and its use confirmed by analysis.

**5. Processing and formulation**
- Magnets should be placed above all processing equipment to capture tramp iron.
- Medicated feed runs should be grouped and equipment flushed with ground corn meal, the flush routed into the same medicated batch.
- Steam conditioning enhances starch gelatinisation, improving digestibility and water stability.
- Pelleting is generally less expensive than extrusion; new hybrid machines combining both are under development.
- Feeds must be formulated by a competent nutritionist. Medicated additives must be stored separately, with restricted access. Obsolete formulas must be kept for at least one year after last use.
- Custom feeds require General Manager approval and a customer release from liability for impaired performance.

**6. Packaging, labelling, and warehousing**
- Labels must conform to the law of the jurisdiction where the feed is sold, state the species intended, and carry warnings for medicated feeds.
- Bags should be periodically check-weighed; scales calibrated annually by an authorised service.
- Shipping records must allow traceability of batches; bulk trucks carrying medicated feed must be flushed or sequenced.

**7. Sampling and analysis**
- Samples of bulk ingredients should be taken from the beginning, middle, and end of discharge, with grain sampled at least at 5 locations.
- Railroad box cars or barges: each compartment sampled at 3 intervals.
- Bagged shipments are sampled by diagonal probing and resealed with tape.
- Roughly 1 kg composite subsamples are retained, labelled by date, carrier number, supplier, and sampler.
- Analytical methods for toxicants should follow [[AOAC]] (1990) or equivalent national methods.

**8. Product recall and investigations**
- Recall classes: Class I (serious risk to life or health), Class II (potential hazard), Class III (no health threat, but significant customer or public relations implications).
- A Recall Committee with regulatory, production, sales, quality control, public relations, legal, purchasing, and nutrition members is convened for Class I, II, or III recalls.
- Customer complaints should be investigated within 24 hours using a standard checklist, with samples, photographs, and water tests.
- Complaints should never be ignored, since "no complaint is too small."

**9. Plant hygiene, maintenance, and personnel**
- Housekeeping, daily cleaning, and pest control (approved baits and fumigation by trained personnel) are required, with records of pesticide use.
- A preventive maintenance programme is required, with extensive equipment checklists (bucket elevators, screw conveyors, hammer mills, pellet mills, baggers, boilers, scales, and others) in Appendix III.
- Management should have formal training in feed technology; cross-training and refresher training are recommended.

**10. Documentation**
- Records must allow the full history of each batch to be determined and be retained for appropriate periods.
- The [[UKASTA Code of Practice]] for the Manufacture of Safe Compound Animal Feedingstuffs (1998, 2000) and the FAO Draft Code of Practice for Good Animal Feeding (FAO, 1998) are cited as models.

**Appendix IV (Draft Code of Practice for Good Animal Feeding, FAO 1998)** applies to all animal feeds (except free-range grazing) and sets general GMP principles, including ingredient sourcing from reputable suppliers, flushing equipment between formulations, waste isolation, and the use of potable water.
