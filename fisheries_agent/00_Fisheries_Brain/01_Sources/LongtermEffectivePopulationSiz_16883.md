# Long-term Effective Population Size of Three Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Garrigan, Marsh and Dowling (Arizona State University) applies a maximum likelihood coalescent estimator of long-term female effective population size (N<sub>ef</sub>) to mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) from three endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]]: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Conventional estimators based on temporal allele-frequency change capture only recent drift, so the authors use [[Coalescent Theory]] to infer historical demography. The three species illustrate three outcomes: humpback chub retains historical equilibrium variation despite recent declines, razorback sucker shows a historically expanding population with surviving rare alleles, and bonytail chub shows signs of a recent bottleneck that may have erased much historical variation.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), all endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and all federally listed as endangered.
- **Effective population size:** Defined as the size of an idealized population experiencing the same genetic drift as the observed population. N<sub>e</sub> is typically smaller than census size N because of variance in family size and temporal fluctuations in abundance ([[Hedgecock]] 1994; [[Frankham]] 1995; [[Nunney]] 1996).
- **Method:** The [[Fluctuate Software]] program (Kuhner et al. 1998) jointly estimates long-term N<sub>ef</sub> (θ = 4N<sub>ef</sub>μ) and an exponential growth rate (g) from aligned mtDNA sequences, using Metropolis-Hastings sampling of genealogies.
- **Historical context:** Bonytail and razorback sucker were once widespread in large streams, while humpback chub was restricted to canyon-bound reaches. Dam construction, water diversion, desiccation and non-native predatory and competitive fishes caused recruitment failure and declines ([[Minckley]] 1990; [[Minckley et al. 1991]]).
- **Life history:** Average lifespan is 20 to 50 years. Predation on early life stages is a primary obstacle to recovery, and longevity may have delayed the loss of genetic variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Humpback chub:** The only one of the three with apparently self-sustaining populations. The largest known population, in and near the [[Little Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], may number as many as 10,000 adults. Smaller stable populations occur in Westwater Canyon (Utah) and Black Rocks (Colorado).
- **Bonytail chub:** Extirpated from most of its range and extremely rare. It is regularly encountered only in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona/Nevada), and captures are too few for census estimates.
- **Razorback sucker:** Historically the most abundant of the three, with a basin-wide range from Wyoming to the Colorado River delta and the Gila River system. The Lake Mohave population fell from perhaps hundreds of thousands to fewer than 10,000 fish in about 50 years, and a middle Green River population shows a similar trend.
- **Captive propagation:** Programs for bonytail and razorback sucker began in the early 1980s, with hundreds of thousands to millions of young distributed. Repatriates are now routinely captured in Lake Mohave, but stockings elsewhere were largely unsuccessful, and near-total recruitment failure persists in all known populations of the two species.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and genetic markers**
- Samples: humpback chub (n = 18, Little Colorado/Colorado confluence, Grand Canyon); bonytail chub (n = 16) and razorback sucker (n = 49), both from Lake Mohave.
- Humpback and bonytail chub were assayed for two SSCP fragments of the ND2 gene; razorback sucker for a cytochrome b fragment. Variation was detected by SSCP and direct sequencing.
- Coalescent assumptions: neutrality, no recombination, and constant size at equilibrium. A transition/transversion ratio of 2.0 was assumed because no transversions were observed.

**Humpback chub (ND2, n = 18)**
- 5 haplotypes, 6 mutations (1 singleton), all transitions, none amino-acid changing.
- Mean pairwise differences 1.54; Tajima's D = -0.3935 (P = 0.358), not significant.
- Long-term N<sub>ef</sub> ≈ 150,000 under growth model (mutation rate 2 × 10⁻⁸ per site per generation, mammalian-equivalent); growth rate not significantly different from zero.
- Constant-size estimate ≈ 97,500; the two models do not differ significantly.
- Interpretation: historical equilibrium variation is retained, possibly because the Grand Canyon subpopulation has not yet lost variation through drift.

**Bonytail chub (ND2, n = 16)**
- 3 haplotypes (frequencies 31.3%, 25.0%, 43.7%), 6 transitional mutations (none singleton), 1 amino-acid change.
- Mean pairwise differences 2.82, almost double the humpback value.
- Tajima's D = 1.909 (P = 0.028), significantly positive, consistent with a bottleneck or balancing selection.
- N<sub>ef</sub> ≈ 62,000 with growth allowed (growth rate significantly negative) versus ≈ 89,500 under constant size; the two values differ significantly.
- Interpretation: recent declines may have removed much historical variation. Hatchery founding (effective founder size 3.5 to 8.5 individuals, Hedrick et al. 2000) cannot be excluded as the cause.

**Razorback sucker (cytochrome b, n = 49)**
- 10 haplotypes, 13 mutations (4 singletons), 2 amino-acid changes. Haplotype E is most frequent (59.2%).
- Mean pairwise differences 1.50; Tajima's D = -1.46 (P = 0.058), marginally non-significant but indicative of an excess of rare mutations.
- ML N<sub>ef</sub> ≈ 940,000 under growth versus ≈ 669,000 under constant size; the growth rate is significantly positive.
- Interpretation: a historically large, expanding population, with rare alleles surviving in the remnant Lake Mohave population. This is consistent with a northward post-Pleistocene expansion (Dowling et al. 1996a).

**Conclusions and management implications**
- The three species illustrate three outcomes: retained equilibrium variation (humpback chub), expansion with surviving rare alleles (razorback sucker), and probable bottleneck loss (bonytail chub).
- Long-term estimates are consistent with known natural history and support their use in setting conservation targets for remnant populations.
- Coalescent and temporal (allele-frequency) estimators measure drift over different time windows and complement each other ([[Waples]] 1989).
- Coalescent estimators have lower variance and use sequence data more efficiently than alternatives. This matters for small endangered-species samples. Probabilities that one added sample would change the final coalescence time were 0.006 (humpback), 0.007 (bonytail) and 0.001 (razorback).
- Adding polymorphic sites improves reliability more than adding samples alone.
