# Lower Colorado River Basin Native Fish Management Plan - Draft Focus on Big-River Fishes

#ichthyology #endangered-species #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #conservation-management #recovery-planning

## Executive Summary

This draft management plan addresses the conservation and recovery of four endangered "big-river" fish species in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]: [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Submitted by the Parker Fishery Resource Office to USFWS Region II in 1994, the plan emphasizes circumventing recruitment failure as the primary mechanism for initiating recovery. The document proposes a hierarchical approach through six management levels, from preventing immediate extinction (Level I) through eventual delisting (Level VI). Primary focus is on bonytail and razorback sucker, with humpback chub excluded due to ongoing [[Glen Canyon Dam]] Environmental Impact Statement processes and Colorado squawfish limited to experimental populations. The plan divides the lower basin into five ecoregions and proposes establishing natural/seminatural isolated habitats as refuges, coupled with hatchery production, genetic management, and eventual reintroduction to mainstem habitats.

## Background & Description

The four target species represent a coevolved assemblage of large-bodied, long-lived fishes historically dominant in the [[Colorado River]] mainstem and major tributaries. All share similar life-history patterns indicating adaptation to large, turbulent river systems and predator-prey relationships. [[Colorado Squawfish]] functioned as the apex native predator, growing to large size with specialized morphology for ambush predation. The three prey species (two chubs and razorback sucker) exhibit high reproductive potential, rapid growth, hydrodynamic body shapes, and falcate fins—adaptations interpreted as responses to predation pressure.

The plan recognizes fundamental differences between upper and lower basin conditions. The lower basin has experienced substantially longer timeframes of modification, with most development completed and few new projects anticipated, providing a relatively stable planning base. Population sizes of target species remain larger in the lower basin than elsewhere, and an extensive site-specific research base exists (reviewed by Minckley 1973, Deacon and Minckley 1974, Minckley and Deacon 1992).

Five ecoregions structure the planning framework: (I) mainstem Colorado from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to [[Hoover Dam]] including Paria, Virgin, and [[Little Colorado River]] tributaries; (II) mainstem from Hoover Dam to U.S.-Mexican boundary including [[Bill Williams River]]; (III) middle Colorado system (Little Colorado above Grand Falls, Virgin River); (IV) [[Gila River]] basin; and (V) Mexican drainages (Rios Yaqui, Sonoyta, Magdalena, and Willcox Playa).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Species declines are attributed primarily to recruitment failure caused by predation and competition from non-indigenous fishes. River modification through dam construction, flow regulation, and habitat alteration created conditions favoring introduced predators (particularly centrarchids and ictalurids) and competitors. Reservoirs, canals, and agency stocking programs provide constant sources of these non-native species. Natural processes that formerly created oxbows, backwaters, and isolated aquatic habitats—critical for early life-history survival—no longer function due to river controls.

Experimental data demonstrate all four taxa can survive to maturity if isolated from other fishes during early life stages. The [[Lake Mohave]] razorback sucker population, though reproducing, shows apparent recruitment failure with an aging population structure indicating imminent collapse (Marsh 1993). Wild-caught razorback sucker larvae exhibit phototactic responses enabling collection at different times and locations during the extended breeding season.

Habitat availability is identified as immediately amenable for use, with personnel and expertise concentrated for dedicated effort. However, the plan acknowledges that restoration of free-flowing river reaches (Level V) will ultimately be necessary for truly self-sustaining populations, requiring habitat improvement, barriers to non-native invasion, removal and control of non-indigenous species, and reestablishment of native fish communities.

## Key Findings

**Management Framework**: The plan proposes six hierarchical levels: (I) prevent extinction; (II) understand and acquire maximum genetic variability; (III) stabilize populations with sufficient numbers, structure, and genetic diversity; (IV) reintroduce and reestablish additional populations; (V) promote self-sustaining status; (VI) downlist or delist. Different species enter at different levels based on endangerment status and knowledge state.

**Primary Strategy**: Circumvention of recruitment failure through provision of predator-free habitats constitutes the core approach. This involves: (1) identification of natural/seminatural habitats (>5.0 ha surface area) that are isolated or can be isolated from other waters; (2) removal of non-indigenous fishes with piscicides; (3) stocking of endangered taxa; (4) rearing to sizes minimizing predation pressure (ca. 300 mm TL); (5) use for augmentation, reintroduction, or as buffers against catastrophic events.

**Hatchery Infrastructure**: [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] maintains broodstocks of all big-river fishes except humpback chub, providing propagation, rearing, distribution, research support, and genetic advisory capabilities. [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], Arizona Game and Fish Department's Bubbling Ponds facility, and Colorado River Indian Tribe ponds provide additional capacity for broodstock maintenance and production of genetically appropriate progeny.

**Genetic Management**: Level II objectives require maximization of genetic variability through: (1) maximizing numbers of individuals contributing equally to recruitment; (2) collection of wild-caught larvae at different times/places (for razorback sucker); (3) strict pedigree-governed artificial propagation with measured gamete contributions from individuals with known genetic attributes; (4) statistical breeding regimens ensuring allele representation. Genetic data are available for razorback sucker (Dowling and Minckley 1993) and bonytail at population level.

**Coordination Structure**: A Lower Basin Coordinator position is deemed necessary to form a Recovery Team, maintain order in site-specific management plan development, and coordinate among numerous agencies (Appendix B lists federal agencies including Bureau of Reclamation, Bureau of Land Management, National Park Service, Forest Service; state agencies; Indian tribes; academia; environmental groups). The Recovery Team would consist of biologists responsible for developing/revising recovery plans and providing biological information to the Regional Director through the coordinator. No more than half should be agency employees to avoid conflict.

**Species-Specific Approaches**: Bonytail enters at Level I (prevent extinction) but can simultaneously address Level II (genetics known) and Level III (stabilization). Razorback sucker raises Level I concerns due to Lake Mohave stock collapse, but genetic data exist (Level II) and stabilization/expansion efforts are ongoing (Levels III-IV). Humpback chub is excluded pending [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] provisions and Glen Canyon Dam jeopardy opinion resolution. Colorado squawfish recommendations are limited to lower mainstem, with recommendation to modify "experimental, nonessential" designation in Salt and Verde rivers to "experimental, essential" to allow recovery planning inclusion.

**Monitoring and Evaluation**: Fish health monitoring is required annually at minimum and prior to transfers. Periodic renovations will control non-indigenous fishes appearing in dedicated habitats. Evaluation focuses on determining interactions between non-native and native fishes, particularly large predators. Natural succession changes reducing habitat value will be incorporated into refuge management as open-water components of broader wetland/riparian communities.

**Opportunistic Actions**: The plan proposes taking advantage of reservoir drainage events (maintenance, drought) by stocking both adults and larvae coincident with refilling to exploit: (a) absence of predators allowing natural recruitment; (b) rapid growth potential and longevity establishing long-lived populations; (c) dramatically expanded population size allowing selection on diverse gene pools.
