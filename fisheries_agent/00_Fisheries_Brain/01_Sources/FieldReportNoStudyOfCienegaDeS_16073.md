# Field Survey of Cienega de Santa Clara and Environs, October 1992

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #wetland-ecology #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This field report documents the first comprehensive biological and hydrological survey of [[Cienega de Santa Clara]], a wetland in the [[Colorado River Delta]] in northwestern [[Sonora]], [[Mexico]], conducted October 13-18, 1992. The multi-institutional team assessed fish populations, avifauna, vegetation, invertebrates, and water quality in this wetland augmented by the [[Main Outlet Drain Extension]] (M.O.D.E., also known as [[Wellton-Mohawk Canal]]) constructed in 1978. The survey documented the presence of the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) in limited areas, extensive cattail marsh habitat supporting diverse bird communities including [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] (*Rallus longirostris yumanensis*), and a complex hydrological system influenced by both canal discharge (115,600 Ac-Ft/yr) and tidal flooding from the [[Gulf of California]]. Water salinity ranged from 2,000 ppm in the upper vegetated marsh to 42,000 ppm in the [[Colorado River Estuary]]. The study established baseline data and protocols for long-term monitoring of this ecologically significant wetland.

## Background & Description

[[Cienega de Santa Clara]] is located in the delta of the [[Colorado River]] in northwestern [[Mexico]] in the State of [[Sonora]]. The natural wetland was significantly augmented by construction of the [[Main Outlet Drain Extension]] (M.O.D.E.) in 1978, which discharges agricultural drainage water from Arizona. The area also receives mildly brackish water via the [[Riito Drain]]. The study was sponsored primarily by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] with participation from [[Centro Ecologico de Sonora]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Environmental Research Laboratory]] of the [[University of Arizona]], [[Comision Internacional de Limites y Aguas]], and other institutions.

The wetland ecosystem was divided into four sections for survey purposes: (1) the M.O.D.E. canal itself, characterized by desert shrub vegetation (creosote bush, white bursage, saltbush) with cattails growing in the canal; (2) the cattail marsh, the dominant vegetative community covering approximately 20-25 km², dominated by *Typha* spp. with pockets of *Phragmites communis*, with water depths averaging 65 cm; (3) the strip area, an ecotone between salt flats and cattail marsh (0-500 m wide) with shallow pools (10-40 cm deep) dominated by salt grass (*Distichlis* spp.), saltbush, and glasswort (*Salicornia* spp.); and (4) the open cienega, the largest section dominated by salt flats and open water with limited biodiversity and strong tidal influence from the [[Gulf of California]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Cienega de Santa Clara]] represents a critical wetland habitat in the otherwise arid [[Colorado River Delta]] region. Local residents from [[Ejido Luis Encinas Johnson]] reported that the extensive cattail-dominated wetland in the northern part did not exist prior to construction of the [[Wellton-Mohawk Canal]], and that they formerly caught penaeid shrimp in areas now covered by cattails. Water levels in the upper cienega fluctuate with canal flow rather than tides, dropping when canal discharge decreases.

The hydrological regime is complex, with two primary water sources creating distinct zones: (1) relatively fresh water (2,000-3,200 ppm salinity) from the M.O.D.E. (115,600 Ac-Ft/yr) and [[Riito Drain]] (6,300 Ac-Ft/yr) feeding the upper vegetated marsh, and (2) periodic seawater inflow from the [[Colorado River Estuary]] (salinity 42,000 ppm) during high tides affecting the lower cienega. Tides of 14 ft occur 6-16 days per month, while 16 ft tides (occurring less frequently and absent in March, April, and December 1992) appear necessary to flood the eastern shore areas. The mixing zone between these regimes shifts with tidal cycles and canal discharge rates.

Water quality measurements showed salinity ranging from 2,000-2,500 ppm in the upper vegetated areas to 5,000 ppm near shores, with pH values of 8.0-8.3. Water depth in vegetated areas ranged from 1.0-4.0 ft (average 2.8 ft or 85 cm). Estimated water turnover time through the vegetated section ranged from 41-124 days depending on assumed vegetative area (2,000-6,000 hectares). Evapotranspiration calculations suggest that if the cienega loses water at rates comparable to Arizona alfalfa fields (20 cm/month), salinity could increase by a factor of three from inlet to outlet, potentially explaining vegetation boundaries.

The algal mat covering much of the cienega bottom frequently breaks off and floats to the surface. Anaerobic conditions were commonly observed in bottom samples, especially at the end of the M.O.D.E. Cattails near shore at [[El Campo]] (salinity 5,000 ppm) were shorter and browner than those several kilometers from shore (salinity 2,000-2,500 ppm), where cattails reached 13 ft in height above 1.8 ft of water.

## Key Findings

**Fish Community Structure and Distribution:**

Fish collections using seines (10 ft × 4 ft × 1/8 inch mesh), dipnets, and minnow traps documented seven species. [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) were found in shallow (10-20 cm), vegetation-free areas but occurred in low numbers when large populations of exotic species were present, particularly [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*). The only area where pupfish were relatively abundant (approximately 40% of fish community) was a large isolated pond approximately 200 m north of the M.O.D.E. end bridge on the east side, which lacked surface water connections with the main cienega.

Other species documented included: *Poecilia latipinna* (sailfin molly), *Gambusia affinis* (mosquitofish), *Tilapia* sp., *Micropterus salmoides* (largemouth bass), *Lepomis punctatus* (spotted sunfish, identification needs confirmation), and *Cyprinella lutrensis* (red shiner). Fishermen were observed catching 1.5 ft tilapia and large carp. The presence of competitive species (sailfin molly, mosquitofish, red shiner, tilapia) and predatory species (largemouth bass, spotted sunfish) suggests pupfish are unlikely to inhabit the main water body in significant numbers.

**Avifauna Diversity and Abundance:**

The survey documented extensive bird diversity across the four habitat sections. In the M.O.D.E. section, abundant species included [[American Coot]] (*Fulica americana*), [[Great Blue Heron]] (*Ardea herodias*), [[Great Egret]] (*Casmerodius albus*), and [[Snowy Egret]] (*Egretta thula*). The cattail marsh supported the greatest diversity, including [[Western Grebe]] (*Aechmophorus occidentalis*), [[White Pelican]] (*Pelecanus erythrorhynchos*), [[Brown Pelican]] (*Pelecanus occidentalis*), multiple gull and tern species, herons, ibis, rails including the [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] (*Rallus longirostris yumanensis*), shorebirds, [[Osprey]] (*Pandion haliaetus*), [[Belted Kingfisher]] (*Megaceryle alcyon*), [[Marsh Wren]] (*Cistothorus palustris*), and [[Northern Harrier]] (*Circus cyaneus*).

The strip area showed high waterfowl abundance including [[Mexican Duck]] (*Anas platyrhynchos diazi*), [[Common Pintail]] (*Anas acuta*), [[American Wigeon]] (*Anas americana*), and various teal species. Shorebird diversity was high with [[American Oystercatcher]] (*Haematopus palliatus*), [[Black-necked Stilt]] (*Himantopus mexicanus*), [[American Avocet]] (*Recurvirostra americana*), [[Short-billed Dowitcher]] (*Limnodromus griseus*), [[Marbled Godwit]] (*Limosa fedoa*), [[Long-billed Curlew]] (*Numenius americanus*), and [[Greater Yellowlegs]] (*Tringa melanoleuca*) all recorded as abundant or common.

**Vegetation Patterns:**

The transition from desert to wetland was abrupt near [[Hunter's Camp]] and [[El Campo]]. The cienega begins with sparse *Sesuvium*, followed by *Distichlis* (likely *D. spicata*) covering the shore. Cattails (*Typha* spp.) dominate the marsh, with some *Phragmites* patches. Cattail height and vigor correlated with salinity, with taller, greener cattails (up to 13 ft) in lower salinity areas (2,000-2,500 ppm) and shorter, browner cattails near higher salinity shores (5,000 ppm). *Distichlis* (likely *D. palmeri*) was found along the [[Colorado River]] where salinity reached 37,000-42,000 ppm.

**Invertebrate Communities:**

Bottom samples from [[El Campo]], [[Hunter's Camp]], and the end of M.O.D.E. indicated fly larvae (Diptera, possibly Simuliidae) were the most common macroinvertebrates. Anaerobic conditions were frequently observed during bottom sampling, especially at the M.O.D.E. terminus. Shallow shoreline waters in the northern cienega contained dragonfly and damselfly nymphs (Odonata), adult aquatic beetles (Coleoptera), and unidentified shrimp species.

**Other Fauna:**

Mammal tracks, primarily raccoon, were abundant on the northwest shoreline. One coyote was observed October 16. A [[Lowland Leopard Frog]] (*Rana yavapaiensis*, identification needs confirmation) was found dead at the end of the M.O.D.E. and preserved as a voucher specimen.

**Hydrological Measurements:**

The M.O.D.E. inflow rate was estimated at 115,600 Ac-Ft/yr (1.43 × 10⁸ m³/year), compatible with [[Bureau of Reclamation]] estimates of 106,000-140,000 Ac-Ft/yr. The [[Riito Drain]] contributes an additional 6,300 Ac-Ft/yr (7.8 × 10⁶ m³/yr), for a combined input of 121,900 Ac-Ft/yr (1.5 × 10⁸ m³/yr) to the northern boundary. M.O.D.E. salinity measured 2,000 ppm by refractometer (Bureau of Reclamation average: 3,200 ppm).

No permanent surface water outflow was observed from ground or air, though water exchange with the [[Colorado River Estuary]] during tidal cycles may transport salts. The Colorado River at this location functions as an estuary with no freshwater flow and salinity (42,000 ppm) higher than the open Gulf (37,000 ppm). Water levels are entirely governed by tidal fluctuations, which are among the highest in the world.

**Access and Site Characteristics:**

Multiple access attempts from roads near [[Ejido Flor del Desierto]], [[Rancho Atlantida]], the shrimp farm, and the Rio Colorado were largely unsuccessful due to shallow water along the eastern shore. The area at the end of the [[Ejido Flor del Desierto]] road showed significant human impact: burned cattails, constructed walkways, raw sewage discharge, and a drainage canal that allows higher salinity water from adjacent salt flats to flow into Cienega II when flooded.

The western approach via the mouth of the [[Colorado River]], which showed flooding during aerial observation on September 26, 1992 (14 ft tide), was dry on October 17, 1992, with only moist soil and one rivulet (salinity 90,000 ppm) found within 2-3 miles east of the river. This drying pattern between October 6 and October 15 suggests that tides exceeding 16 ft (which occurred September 29 but not again until after October 15) may be required to flood the eastern shore areas and the South Tongue of the cienega.

**Recommendations for Future Monitoring:**

Long-term hydrological monitoring should include: calibrated stage recorders for M.O.D.E. and [[Riito Drain]] flow rates, weekly salinity measurements, water level and flow monitoring in upper and lower cienega sections, daily tide monitoring with tide gages and surveying in the western exchange area, and water level recorders at multiple locations to quantify water and salt budgets.

Avifauna monitoring protocols should establish: permanent survey points 200 m apart in T-formation transects across the cattail marsh for [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] breeding season surveys using conspecific playback, focal point surveys at shorebird concentration areas in the strip habitat during spring and fall migration (minimum 5 visits per season), and songbird counts at the same survey points.

Practical recommendations include: four-wheel drive vehicles for access to southern areas, canoe with 2-3 hp engine for cattail marsh access, abundant 2 m PVC pipe sections with permanent tags for marking sites, adequate camping gear to minimize travel time, compass and marking tape for navigation in cattails, and individual copies of aerial photos until quality maps are produced.

A meteorological observer (Juan Butron Mendez) in [[Ejido Luis Encinas Johnson]] was trained and equipped with maximum-minimum thermometer and rain gauge to collect daily data. Water and sediment samples were collected at multiple sites for analysis by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]].

Human activities documented include fishing by ejido residents (who prefer to enter at [[El Campo]] as water near M.O.D.E. end is too shallow), hunting activity evidenced by duck blinds, and sewage discharge affecting water quality in portions of the wetland. Cooperation was offered by both [[Ejido Luis Encinas Johnson]] and [[Ejido Flor del Desierto]] officials for ongoing studies.
