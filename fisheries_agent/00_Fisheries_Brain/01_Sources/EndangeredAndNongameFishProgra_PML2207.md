# Nongame Fish Conservation Programs in the Western United States (1984)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented at the 64th Annual Conference of the [[Western Association Of Fish And Wildlife Agencies]] (Victoria, British Columbia, July 16–19, 1984), reviews the state of [[Nongame Fish Programs]] across the 13 western states. It examines how each state lists, protects and funds rare and nongame fishes, and how these state efforts relate to the [[Federal Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 (amended 1978 and 1982). The paper was prepared on behalf of the [[Western Division American Fisheries Society]] Endangered Species Committee (1982–1984).

Nine of the 13 western states have nongame or rare-fish programs. State lists cover 172 fish taxa, but only 31 (about 20%) are also federally listed. The author concludes that state programs act as an early warning system for possible federal listings and reflect growing public recognition of the value of nongame species.

## Background & Description

The [[Federal Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 provides a framework for federal protection of threatened and endangered species, but many imperilled fishes are not federally listed. The paper therefore focuses on state-level action. Most state programs classify fishes into categories such as endangered, threatened, rare, sensitive, declining, or species of special concern. Federally listed species are a subset of these lists.

Funding is a recurring theme. Programs are supported by a mix of sources, including:
- [[Section 6 Funding]] under the Endangered Species Act, available through cooperative agreements with the [[US Fish And Wildlife Service]]
- [[Income Tax Checkoff]] programs, used by seven of the 13 states
- Hunting and fishing license fees
- Personalized license plates (Washington)
- Trust funds and general funds

Several states lack a state income tax, so checkoff funding is not possible (Wyoming and Nevada).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper highlights the particular vulnerability of western aquatic systems. Nevada, for example, comprises relict river systems and closed basins, which have produced distinctive fish species. Its arid climate and limited surface water, together with public and private development, have placed many native fishes in jeopardy.

Historically, western fish management emphasized sport fish. Many states consider nongame fishes, including suckers, chubs and cutthroat trout, to be important as forage and as components of native ecosystems. The paper also notes that nongame species have resource values that are not economically definable, citing [[Ehrenfeld]] (1976) and [[Pister]] (1976).

Several species discussed are federally listed or closely monitored, including:
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a federally listed large river fish
- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*) and [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*Chasmistes brevirostris*), which are protected in Oregon and California
- [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*)
- [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*), a federally listed salmonid
- [[Kendall Warm Springs Dace]], which is jointly managed by Wyoming and the USDA Forest Service

## Key Findings

**Overall status across the West**
- Nine of 13 western states have nongame or rare-fish programs (abstract, as reported).
- State designations total 172 fish taxa. Arizona, Idaho, New Mexico and Nevada list the majority of these.
- Only 31 (about 20%) of state-designated species and subspecies are also federally listed.
- Many state-listed taxa are listed by more than one state, which underscores the role of state programs as an early warning for federal listing.
- The author concludes that over half of the western states are actively engaged in nongame fishery management, reflecting growing public awareness of these resources.

**State-by-state highlights**
- **Arizona:** Actively involved in nongame fish management since the early 1970s. The state's "Threatened Native Wildlife" list (updated 1982) places species in categories including extirpated, in jeopardy, and possibly in jeopardy. Twenty-five fishes are included in Groups I–III. Arizona has a cooperative agreement with the US Fish and Wildlife Service and an income tax checkoff.
- **Alaska:** No nongame fish program or species list. No income tax checkoff.
- **California:** The first state to prohibit the import, take, possession and sale of rare species. Its 1971 Endangered Species Act protected the first group of rare and endangered animals. Reports on status are mandated every two years. The state appropriated $1 million for nongame species in 1974–1975, and this rose to over $9 million in 1981–1982. The list includes 13 endangered and 2 rare fishes, 9 of which are federally listed.
- **Colorado:** Official program covering 11 native taxa: three cutthroat trout subspecies, five Colorado River fishes, three darters and the Arkansas River speckled chub. Two categories, threatened and endangered, set management priorities. An income tax checkoff funds these efforts.
- **Hawaii:** No nongame fish program or endangered fish list. The state's cooperative agreement with the federal government is used for Section 6 funding, and its program focuses on endangered plants and birds. The author notes an active debate about allowing commercial bait harvest to reduce the nuisance to anglers.
- **Idaho:** Nongame planning began in July 1982, with a completed Fisheries Management Plan (1981–1985). Three categories (endangered, threatened, and species of special concern) include 2, 1 and 17 taxa, respectively. Five nongame fishes are on the list. Historically, species such as suckers, Utah chub and carp were managed as forage fish. The Shoshone sculpin was the only species to have received dedicated funds for status studies.
- **Montana:** The 1973 State Nongame and Endangered Species Act set a policy to perpetuate both nongame and game species. Twelve species and one subspecies are classed as "Special Concern," divided almost equally into classes A, B and C based on concern level. An income tax checkoff was planned for FY 1986.
- **Nevada:** Protected fishes are classed as "protected," "endangered," or "sensitive." The state lists 10 species and 2 subspecies as protected, 7 as endangered, and 2 species (1 undescribed) and 19 subspecies (6 undescribed) as sensitive. Sensitive species are unprotected candidates for reclassification.
- **New Mexico:** The endangered species program began in 1974 under the Wildlife Conservation Act. A 1979 handbook covers 119 animal species, of which 25 are fishes, split into two groups (11 and 14 fishes) by likelihood of survival. Funding comes from general funds and Section 6, and checkoff revenue is available for all department programs.
- **Oregon:** Has neither a nongame program nor a list of rare and nongame species. Four species (three minnows and one sucker) are protected by administrative rules, and a bag limit protects the Lost River sucker from excessive angling mortality.
- **Utah:** Officially established a nongame program in 1975. The 1971 Wildlife Resources Code allows immediate protection for any federally listed species. Eight species are listed, plus three each classed as "declining" and "status questioned." Programs are partly funded by a 1980 income tax checkoff.
- **Washington:** Developing a list of fish species in four categories: monitor, sensitive, threatened and endangered. Eight fishes are proposed for "monitor" status. Funding comes through a personalized license plate program, and the state has no cooperative agreement.
- **Wyoming:** No operational nongame fish program or income tax checkoff. Two cutthroat trout subspecies have been managed since 1964, and continued angling regulations protect these species. Kendall Warm Springs Dace, a federally listed species, is jointly managed with the USDA Forest Service.

**Funding and institutional patterns**
- Eight of the 13 states have cooperative agreements with the US Fish and Wildlife Service. Wyoming and Hawaii have agreements without operational nongame fish programs.
- Seven states use an income tax checkoff. Washington uses a personalized license plate. Nevada and Wyoming lack a state income tax, which precludes checkoff funding.
- Funding has been a major constraint. Montana's legislation for funding failed repeatedly over six years.

**Conclusions**
- State lists are valuable early warnings of possible federal listings.
- Rising public awareness is increasing support for nongame fish conservation across the West.
