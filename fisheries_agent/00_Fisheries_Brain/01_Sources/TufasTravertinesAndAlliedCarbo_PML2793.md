# Tufas, Travertines and Allied Carbonate Deposits

#geomorphology #sedimentology #limnology #cyanobacteria

## Executive Summary

This review by H.A. Viles and A.S. Goudie (1990, *Progress in Physical Geography*) synthesizes knowledge of [[Tufa]], [[Travertine]] and related freshwater calcium carbonate deposits. It covers terminology, classification, global distribution, morphology, biology, petrology, geochemistry, [[Calcium Carbonate Precipitation]], deposition rates, dating and geomorphic significance. The authors identify a multilingual terminological muddle and stress that tufa classifications are largely site-specific. Water flow position is the dominant control on tufa form, flora and fabric, and biological activity (especially [[Cyanobacteria]] and mosses) strongly influences deposition. Reported deposition rates range from below 1 mm per year to about 0.5 m per year. The paper also notes that dating methods carry significant limitations.

## Background & Description

**Terminology.** Many overlapping names describe freshwater carbonate deposits:
- **Travertine** derives from the Italian *travertino*, linked to Tivoli (ancient Tibur), a site of thermal-spring carbonate deposits.
- **Tufa** (calc-tufa) is a soft, porous calcareous rock formed in springs, waterfalls and lakes in limestone regions (Pentecost, 1981).
- **Sinter** (calc-sinter) is usually restricted to inorganically precipitated deposits. Cave deposits such as [[Flowstone]], dripstone and [[Rimstone Pools]] are often classed as calc-sinters.
- Other terms include oncolites, rivulites, krustensteine, stromatolite, crotite, carapace and bioherm.

Authors use "travertine" and "tufa" interchangeably, though some treat one as a special case of the other. Julia (1983) uses travertine as a general term, while Pentecost (1981) describes travertine as a hard, non-porous variety used for building.

**Classification.** Three main approaches are used:
- **Botanical:** Irion and Muller (1968) separated algal tufas, moss tufas and inorganic calc-sinters. Other types include [[Chironomid Tufa]] (algal tufa with abundant chironomid burrows). Pentecost and Lord (1988) classified Craven (Yorkshire) tufas by dominant cyanobacterial and bryophyte genera, plus unconsolidated "spring chalk."
- **Geomorphological/hydrological:** Chafetz and Folk (1984) recognized six travertine types, including waterfall or cascade deposits, lake-fill deposits, steeply sloping mounds, fans or cones, terraced mounds and fissure ridges.
- **Petrological and in-place versus clastic:** Julia (1983) considered the in-place versus clastic distinction the most useful criterion.

Position relative to water flow is the overall dominant control on shape, structure and flora.

**Distribution.** Tufa and travertine have been described from every continent except Antarctica. Notable UK active sites include the [[Pentland Hills]], the [[Craven District]] and north Wales. Pentecost (1978) mapped active and relict UK tufas, showing a cluster around the Pennines. Large active deposits occur at [[Plitvice Lakes]] (Yugoslavia), where spectacular lake barrages form. Records are sparse in some regions, often because surveys are lacking.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biology.** Tufas contain much living and dead biological material. Organisms either help deposit and consolidate the tufa or use it as a substrate.
- Cyanobacteria: *Phormidium*, *Schizothrix*, *Homeothrix*, *Rivularia*, *Lyngbya*, *Scytonema*, *Plectonema*
- Green algae: *Gongrosira*, *Oocardium*
- Mosses: *Cratoneuron*, *Eucladium*, *Gymnostomum*, *Bryum*, *Rynchostegium*
- Diatoms, lichens (*Thelidium microcarpum*), bacteria and [[Chironomid]] larvae
- Gastropods and bivalve fragments
- Dead plant remains, including *Phragmites* casts at [[Bet Shean]]

[[Rivularia Haematites]] forms hemispherical calcified colonies in lakes and streams. Pentecost (1987) found two calcification modes: dense winter calcite when growth is minimal, and less dense summer bands linked to photosynthesis.

**Hydrology and chemistry controls.** Carbonate precipitation is driven by CO2 degassing. Degassing can result from evaporation, turbulence, mixing of waters of different temperatures or CaCO3 content, and biogenic CO2 removal. Several studies show that inorganic processes often dominate. Jacobson and Usdowski (1975) found turbulence caused the greatest CO2 degassing at a Gottingen spring. At [[Falling Spring Creek]], Herman and Lorah (1987) identified thermal-spring outgassing, cave-water mixing and turbulent outgassing, and found metabolic CO2 uptake by photosynthesis insignificant. Golubic (1973) proposed that carbonate precipitation is mainly inorganic in upper river reaches and becomes more biological downstream.

**Erosion.** Tufa is easily erodible, and endolithic cyanobacteria may aid weathering. The authors found no published measurements of tufa erosion.

## Key Findings

- **Terminology is inconsistent.** Terms are used generally and in restricted senses, distinguished by porosity, genesis and location. The authors call this "multilingual terminological confusion."
- **Flow position controls form.** Cascade tufas form in turbulent water, hummock tufas in slow water, and stromatoid forms at seepages (Malham, north Yorkshire). Vertical water fall produces cascade tufas. Impounded or gently sloping water produces near-horizontal beds.
- **Rimstone pools are poorly understood.** Pools form in "flights" on slopes. Degassing explains growth but not initiation from slope irregularities. Varnedoe (1965) proposed that velocity increase over an obstruction lowers internal pressure, causing degassing and precipitation.
- **Pool scales vary widely.** Cave rimstone pools range from 10 cm to many metres in diameter, with bars from a few cm to about 1 m high. Plitvice tufa dams form landscape-scale pools with dimensions in metres.
- **Laminations may record seasons.** Irion and Muller (1968) reported algal tufa laminae of 0.6–7 mm, with crystals averaging 15 µm. Love and Chafetz (1988) reported laminated algal crusts from Oklahoma, 0.5–4 mm thick and averaging 1–2 mm. Dense spring algal mats, porous summer tufa and dense winter sinter crusts have been described.
- **Fabrics differ by type.** Algal tufas have crystals precipitated around filaments. Moss tufas are generally unlaminated, with stems encrusted in palisade-like calcite (50–100 µm in north Yorkshire). Travertine fabrics include laminated muds, ray crystals and bacterial shrub laminations, which may be annual. Pisoids and peloids are common in Hula Valley (Israel) travertines.
- **Geochemistry is source-dependent.** Freshwater carbonates are low in Mg and Sr compared with marine deposits, but relatively high in Fe and Mn. Low-magnesium calcite is typically dominant. Silica can act as a cement.
- **Supersaturation thresholds are high.** Dandurand et al. (1982) recorded precipitation starting at about 10× saturation. Herman and Lorah (1987) found precipitation in Falling Spring Creek once waters exceeded 15× saturation. Inhibitors include phosphate, Mg, humic and fulvic acids, and lack of nuclei.
- **Water chemistry data.** Reported Ca2+ values range from 11.0 mg l⁻¹ (Walker Lake, pH 9.45) to 149 mg l⁻¹ (Falling Spring Creek, pH 7.91). Plitvice Lakes averaged 94.6 mg l⁻¹ Ca2+. Roquefort-les-Cascades springs averaged 85.30 mg l⁻¹ Ca2+ at pH 7.32.
- **Isotopic disequilibrium complicates palaeoclimate work.** Dandurand et al. (1982) found disequilibrium in δ13C and δ18O in a Pyrenean stream. This produced δ18O-derived temperatures differing on average by about 5 °C from observed temperatures. A kinetic model may explain this better than an equilibrium model (Michaelis et al., 1985).
- **Deposition rates are highly variable.** Rates range from under 1 mm per year to about 0.5 m per year. Examples include Gwynedd (0.21–0.80 mm/a), Malham Tarn (0.01–1.30 mm/a), [[Tivoli]] (about 8–10 mm/a), [[Plitvice Lakes]] (about 10 mm/a), [[Hula Valley]] (0.32 ± 0.10 mm/a), Yellowstone's Mammoth Hot Springs (20–560 mm/a) and a Texas experimental dam (0.60 mm/a). Method choice strongly affects results.
- **Radiocarbon dating has specific problems.** Tufa carbonate has three isotopically distinct sources: soil CO2, bedrock carbonate (essentially free of 14C) and atmospheric CO2. Bedrock dilution can be corrected. Pazdur et al. (1988a) found inorganic sinter crusts and cyanophyte stromatolites gave the most reliable dates. Moss travertine showed high diagenetic error, while calcareous muds and algal oncoids showed redeposition errors.
- **Thorium-uranium dating has conditions.** It suits materials from about 1,000 to 400,000 years old if deposits crystallized at deposition, initially contained more than 0.1 ppm U without Th or Pa, and stayed chemically closed. Tufas are often open systems, giving only minimum ages (Szabo and Rosholt, 1982).
- **Regional Th/U patterns.** Hennig et al. (1983) analysed about 140 spring travertines and identified major growth periods over the last 130,000 years. Tufa deposits were interpreted as indicators of wet and warm phases.
- **Electron spin resonance (ESR) dating is limited.** Most samples lack suitable signals, but well-crystallized crusts may be suitable (Grün et al., 1988).
- **Geomorphic significance.** Tufas can form dams and waterfalls, armour valley slopes, provide palaeoclimate and lake-level records, and are quarried for building stone (for example, central Italian travertines).
