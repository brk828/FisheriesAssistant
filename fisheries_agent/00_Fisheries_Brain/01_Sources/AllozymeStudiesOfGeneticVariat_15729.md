# Allozyme Studies of Genetic Variation in Poeciliid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This document is an excerpt from a poeciliid monograph covering the genetic variation of livebearing fishes studied by protein electrophoresis, or [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]]. It reviews work on [[Gene Linkage]] in poeciliids, [[Unisexual Poeciliids]], and population variability in bisexual species, with the most detail on *Gambusia* (mosquitofishes). The authors' own unpublished survey of 20 *Gambusia* species shows that desert-restricted populations have markedly lower [[Heterozygosity]] than other populations, and that peripheral and isolated populations are often depauperate. The excerpt also covers [[Gene Diversity]] hierarchy, [[Nei'S Genetic Identity]] across taxonomic levels, cautions on [[Phylogenetic Reconstruction]] from electrophoretic data, and [[Hybridization]] in natural populations. It closes with a brief introduction to Chapter 13 (Smith et al.) on demographic and temporal genetic variation in *Gambusia*.

## Background & Description

Protein electrophoresis was first applied to poeciliids by Hewitt et al. (1963). It assumes that different forms of an enzyme (allozymes) or other protein are products of alternative alleles at one gene locus. The method is inexpensive and uses standard laboratory equipment, compared with [[Mitochondrial DNA]] analyses. The authors note that restriction endonuclease analyses offer higher resolution for some questions but are unlikely to replace protein electrophoresis as the main data source for natural populations in the near future.

The broadest electrophoretic survey of poeciliids, by Morizot and Siciliano (1984), assayed about 100 loci in *Xiphophorus* spp.; 63 showed intraspecific and/or interspecific variation. Experimental crosses have shown linkage groups of two to six protein-coding genes in *Xiphophorus* and *Poeciliopsis*, with homology between their linkage groups.

Unisexual poeciliids of the genera *Poeciliopsis* and *Poecilia* are all-female clones. Electrophoretic and karyological evidence shows they arise as interspecific hybrids between congeneric parents. Because of this origin they are typically several times more heterozygous than their bisexual relatives. Clonal variation in unisexuals corresponds with allozyme polymorphisms in the parent species, so each unisexual form comprises several lineages from separate hybridization events. This underlies Vrijenhoek's (1979a) "frozen-niche variation" hypothesis.

Smith and co-workers' studies of natural and semi-natural populations of *Gambusia affinis* are described as perhaps the most extensive effort to document demographic, geographic, and temporal patterns of allozymic variation in a vertebrate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Desert and spring habitats.** *Gambusia* occupy temperate rivers, isolated desert springs, tropical rivers, islands, and marine environments. Several highly restricted spring-dwellers, including *G. georgei* [sic; *G. geiseri* in tables], *G. heterochir*, *G. nobilis*, and the [[Gambusia Gaigei]] complex, are among the most geographically restricted vertebrates. Examples cited include *G. alvarezi*, endemic to a spring run less than 300 m long in Chihuahua, Mexico, and *G. longispinis*, endemic to springs in the Cuatro Ciénegas basin, Coahuila. The *G. gaigei* population at Big Bend National Park, Texas, is artificially maintained.

**Population history.** The text stresses that population history, population size, and [[Genetic Drift]] can strongly affect heterozygosity. Historical effects are invoked for the lower variability of marine *G. yucatana* and *G. puncticulata*, which may share a genetically depauperate ancestor, and for the consistently higher heterozygosity of *G. holbrooki* than *G. affinis* in the southeastern United States.

**Sympatry and hybridization.** Several spring-dwellers from Texas and New Mexico occur with the wide-ranging *G. affinis*, and hybridization has usually been described in these cases. Hybridization varies by spring: in one system, *G. nobilis* introgression occurred downstream of spring outflows, while in another there was virtually no hybridization despite overlapping microdistributions.

**Environmental correlates.** Simanek (1978a) found no association between heterozygosity and 13 habitat/climatic variables in *Poecilia latipinna*. The highest variability in that study (H = 0.10) occurred in the only spring population surveyed.

## Key Findings

**Methods and data scope**
- Authors' unpublished survey: 23 to 26 loci in 20 *Gambusia* species, assayed in two laboratories using horizontal starch-gel electrophoresis with two buffer systems (TC and TVB). Eighteen loci were common to both surveys.
- Tissues: skeletal muscle alone (Wildrick) or a mixture of head, epaxial muscle, and liver (Echelle).
- Statistical analysis covered 22 *Gambusia* species, including two additional species from Milstead (1980).
- Two heterozygosity measures are used: average heterozygotes per locus per individual (H) and proportion of loci polymorphic (P). H is preferred as less arbitrary and less sensitive to sample size (Nei 1975).

**Patterns in heterozygosity**
- No evidence for a trend of subtropical-tropical species being more variable than temperate species (F = 0.4, p = 0.52).
- No significant difference between habitat specialists (spring-restricted) and generalists (F = 0.2, p = 0.68).
- Desert-restricted *Gambusia* have reduced variability: H = 0.016 vs 0.047 (F = 6.3, p = 0.02); P = 0.050 vs 0.167 (F = 8.2, p = 0.01).
- Zero variability (H = 0.00, P = 0.00) occurred only in highly restricted desert populations: *G. alvarezi*, *G. longispinis*, a disjunct *G. marshi* population at Cuatro Ciénegas, and the *G. gaigei* population at Big Bend.
- *G. gaigei* was assayed for 60 protein-coding loci in two samples and showed no variability. The population passed through severe bottlenecks, including one with three individuals in 1956. The authors conclude that low heterozygosity was present before the recorded bottlenecks. Nei et al. (1975) found that a bottleneck to an effective size of two retains more than 65% of original variation.
- Spring size is positively associated with variability in two groups: *G. hurtadoi*, in a large irrigation spring, has relatively high H, while *G. gaigei* and *G. alvarezi* (H = 0.00) occupy much smaller springs. Among four *G. nobilis* populations, H = 0.051 to 0.060 in large artesian springs at Balmorhea, Texas, versus 0.004 to 0.031 in three smaller systems.
- Peripheral populations: in 21 populations of *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* (25 loci), seven had H = 0.00, all at northern range edges or upstream river reaches. In contrast, 26 samples of *G. affinis* and 50 of *G. holbrooki* (13 loci) had no H = 0.00, though none were peripheral.
- In *Poecilia* (29 loci, 13 populations, seven species), two populations had H = 0.00: the island population of *P. vanderpolli* on Curacao and a *P. mexicana* population at the southern range edge in Costa Rica.

**Population structure**
- Total gene diversity follows a hierarchical structure (Nei 1977). Wright's standardized variance (F-statistics) were computed with BIOSYS-1 (Swofford and Selander 1981).
- Locality:River F-value for *P. occidentalis* = 0.544; Locality:Total for *G. nobilis* = 0.516 to 0.603. Both are among the largest reported for fishes, so local populations of *P. occidentalis* are strongly isolated.
- *G. nobilis* and *G. affinis* from the same Pecos River springs (Milstead 1980): *G. nobilis* populations show much higher F-values, consistent with disrupted gene flow driving differentiation.
- Between the two mosquitofish species, gene diversity is 0.21, less than half the 0.53 between biotypes (subspecies) of *P. occidentalis*.
- The Pecos River *G. geiseri* populations show low differentiation, consistent with Hubbs and Springer's (1957) hypothesis that they were introduced from the San Marcos River.

**Genetic identity across taxonomic levels**
- Nei's (1978) identity (I) was used to compare four groups (Xiphophorus, *Poecilia sphenops* complex, gambusiines, and *Poeciliopsis occidentalis*). All show roughly similar differentiation levels across taxonomic levels.
- Between-species I is generally below 0.90, with two exceptions: the *G. gaigei* complex of three allopatric species (I = 0.95 to 1.00), and three of five comparisons of *P. mexicana* and *P. gracilis* (I = 0.90). Such high similarity suggests possible taxonomic problems, though identity should not by itself delimit species.

**Phylogenetic relationships**
- UPGMA dendrograms are the common summary tool, but they represent true phylogenies only if rates of change are similar across branches. Most systematists are not comfortable with this assumption.
- Greenfield and Wildrick (1984) used an "allele as character" approach on the *G. puncticulata* group but lacked an outgroup, so shared alleles were not well-supported synapomorphies. Wildrick is reanalysing with *Belonesox* and *Brachyrhaphis* as outgroups.
- An unpublished analysis by the Echelles supports a close relationship between *G. senilis* and the *G. gaigei* complex (*G. hurtadoi*, *G. alvarezi*, *G. gaigei*), a monophyletic group from the Rio Grande drainage.

**Conclusions on variability**
- Under near-neutrality, variation is set by effective population size and migration rate. Both tend to be small in peripheral populations, wide-ranging species, and highly restricted species.
- The authors favour repeated bottlenecking and long isolation over founder effects. Founder populations of livebearers with multiple insemination and sperm storage are unlikely to lose much variation. Rowe (1987) found no reduced heterozygosity in recently established *G. affinis* in Kansas.
- Associations between heterozygosity and fitness-related traits in poeciliids include: positive correlation with body size in *G. affinis*; positive association with growth rate in *P. occidentalis*; and negative associations with fluctuating asymmetry and morphological variation. These are interpreted as indicating reduced fitness in homozygous individuals.

**Summary and future research**
- Reduced variability in desert and peripheral poeciliid populations may be common in isolated desert fishes, which raises concern for short-term fitness and long-term evolutionary viability.
- Priority research areas include the link between heterozygosity and survival in desert fishes, [[Hybridization]] studies in viviparous systems (using embryos as an identifiable cohort), and molecular phylogenetics combining independent data sets.
- The sympatric *G. affinis* and spring-dwelling species provide small model systems for studying spatial and temporal interspecific interaction.

**Chapter 13 introduction (Smith et al.)**
- Genetic patterns result from complex interactions among causal variables. Surveys without life-history data can yield incomplete or erroneous conclusions.
- Evolution is a change in allele frequency driven by selection, dispersal, drift, and mutation. Changes are larger in populations that are reduced and then expand rapidly.
- Most freshwater fishes occur as a series of smaller semi-isolated groups rather than large, randomly breeding populations (Altukhov 1982).
