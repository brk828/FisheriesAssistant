# Subadult and Adult Colorado Pikeminnow Monitoring: Summary of Results, 1986-2000

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] summarizes shoreline electrofishing data from the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP), which tracked relative abundance of subadult and adult [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a large piscivorous cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. ISMP sampled 186 miles of four rivers in the upper basin from 1986 to 2000. Catch per unit effort (CPE) increased significantly over the period, indicating population growth, with the largest gains in the Green and White rivers. ISMP was terminated because its design could not produce robust population estimates, and the [[Recovery Program For The Endangered Fishes Of The Upper Colorado River]] shifted to mark-recapture estimation.

## Background & Description

The Colorado pikeminnow was once widespread in large rivers of the Colorado Basin but had been eliminated from reaches below [[Lake Powell]] by the late 1960s. Its range in the upper basin has since been reduced by reservoir construction, altered water quality, and barriers such as [[Taylor Draw Dam]]. More than 40 exotic species have been introduced to upper-basin rivers, many of which compete with or prey on native fishes. The species was listed as endangered in 1967, and conflicts between water development and endangered fishes after the 1973 Endangered Species Act led to the creation of the Recovery Program.

ISMP was designed to monitor trends in subadult and adult Colorado pikeminnow using shoreline electrofishing. Sampling reaches were chosen from the [[Sensitive Areas Documents]] as reaches holding relatively high densities of the species. Sampling occurred annually in April or May, before peak runoff, from rigid-bottom outboard electrofishing boats using two anodes (spherical anodes in front, stainless steel cables from the sides) with amperage kept at roughly 6-8 amps. Captured fish were checked for PIT tags, measured (total length, mm), weighed (g), and tagged if unmarked.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The monitored rivers differ in size and hydrology. The White River is smaller than the Green River (mean annual flow of about 1,099 cfs at Watson, UT, versus 8,402 cfs for the Green River at Green River, UT, in water year 1998), and electrofishing was probably more efficient in the smaller river. Taylor Draw Dam, closed on the White River in 1984, blocked fish movement into upstream historical habitat. [[Irving And Modde]] (2000) hypothesized that the barrier increased abundance below the dam because fish could not move further upstream, and that a rich downstream prey base may have raised carrying capacity for large predators.

Size structure reflects the upstream dispersal of maturing fish. Small fish were concentrated in the lower Green and lower Colorado rivers, while the Yampa River population consisted almost entirely of adults. Electrofishing is size-selective, so smaller pikeminnow may be under-represented in samples. Electrofishing efficiency in large, variable rivers is also influenced by environmental conditions. Scare-and-snare trammel netting, which targets backwaters, is more efficient than shoreline electrofishing for capturing fish in the Colorado River.

## Key Findings

- **Sampling scope:** ISMP covered 186 river miles across 13 reaches sampled every year (Green 77 mi in five reaches, Colorado 54 mi in three, White 30 mi in two, Yampa 25 mi in three). Alternating reaches added from 1994 expanded coverage by 189 miles, of which about 160 miles were sampled in a given year.
- **Captures:** Original-reach catches ranged from 49 fish in 1988 to 314 in 2000. Including alternating reaches, the 2000 total was 429. The Green River yielded the most fish over the study, followed by the White, Colorado, and Yampa rivers.
- **CPE metrics:** Four CPE measures (CPM/hr, CPM+obs/hr, CPM/mi, CPM+obs/mi) were significantly correlated with each other in all rivers. CPM/hr was selected for the remaining analyses.
- **Trend:** Mean CPE was significantly related to time in all rivers, with slopes significantly different from zero, indicating increasing abundance.
- **Green River:** River-wide CPE rose from less than 1 CPM/hr early in the study to more than 3 CPM/hr in 2000, with consistent increases after the first three years. Lower-river CPE increased steadily, while upper-river CPE fluctuated and dropped in 1999 and 2000.
- **Colorado River:** CPE was about 0.25 CPM/hr in 1986-1990 and rose to about 1 CPM/hr in 1992-1998. It dropped in 1999 and 2000, with 2000 significantly lower than 1995-1998.
- **Yampa River:** CPE was about 1 CPM/hr in 1986, fell below 0.5 CPM/hr from 1987 through the early 1990s, and remained below 1 CPM/hr through 1999. The river showed the smallest overall increase.
- **White River:** CPE rose from 0.27 CPM/hr in 1986 to 4.7 CPM/hr in 2000, the largest increase of any river, with brief plateaus in 1991-1992 and 1998-1999. By the end of ISMP, White River CPE exceeded that of the Green River, though this likely reflects higher electrofishing efficiency in the smaller river.
- **Population estimates:** Mean CPE correlated with Nesler (2000) population estimates for the Green and White rivers (1992-1997) but not for the Colorado or Yampa rivers. Osmundson and Burnham (1998) and Osmundson (2002) estimated Colorado River populations increasing through 2000, but CPE was not correlated with those estimates.
- **Size structure:** White and Yampa rivers were dominated by adult-sized fish. Green and Colorado rivers showed large annual variation, with years dominated by young fish that suggested recruitment into the adult population.
- **Conclusions:** The consistent CPE increase in all four rivers indicates that the upper basin population grew from 1986 to 2000. CPE cannot estimate absolute abundance, and its variability requires several years of data to detect change. Sampling reaches should cover as much occupied habitat as possible and be chosen randomly.
- **Recommendations:** (1) Evaluate the relationship between shoreline-electrofishing CPE and population estimates in all four rivers; (2) assess electrofishing variability and bias in large rivers; (3) develop size-dependent survival and population growth rates (lambda) to complement CPE and basin-wide population estimates.
