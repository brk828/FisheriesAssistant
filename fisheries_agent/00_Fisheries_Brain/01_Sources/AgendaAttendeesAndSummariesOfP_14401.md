# Cooperation and Conservation in the Lower Colorado River Basin Ecosystem

#ecosystem-management #conservation #binational-cooperation #colorado-river-delta #stakeholder-engagement

## Executive Summary

This document records proceedings from a May 1999 conference focused on cooperation and conservation in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] ecosystem, particularly the [[Colorado River Delta]] region. The meeting brought together diverse stakeholders including tribal representatives (particularly [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]] and [[Cocopah Indians]]), Mexican researchers and community organizations, U.S. environmental NGOs, and scientists. Participants identified major obstacles to delta conservation including lack of coordination among stakeholders, insufficient scientific data on delta resources, failure to recognize the delta as part of the broader ecosystem, and institutional barriers in water allocation policies. The conference resulted in formation of the Colorado River Conservation Caucus and establishment of communication mechanisms including a list-serve to facilitate binational dialogue.

## Background & Description

The conference occurred against the backdrop of two related legal/policy events: a Continuing Legal Education seminar on "The Law of the Colorado River" (May 20-21, 1999, Tucson) and a stakeholder workshop on delta conservation (May 22, 1999). The CLE conference covered legal frameworks including the 1922 Colorado River Compact, 1928 Boulder Canyon Project Act, [[Arizona v. California]] decision, [[Endangered Species Act]] implementation, Indian water rights, and U.S.-Mexico water treaty obligations. Topics included the [[Central Arizona Project]], [[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]] (MSCP) for the Lower Colorado, Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program, and the importance of the Mexican/Colorado River Delta.

The stakeholder workshop brought together approximately 30 participants representing tribal communities (CRIT Elders, [[Cocopah Indians]]), Mexican research institutions ([[ITESM-Guaymas]], [[CICESE]], Instituto Investigaciones Oceanologicas, [[CREAS]]), U.S. environmental organizations ([[Defenders of Wildlife]], [[Environmental Defense Fund]], [[Pacific Institute]], Southwest Center for Biological Diversity, Sonoran Institute), and academic researchers from University of Arizona and Texas A&M. The [[Pacific Institute]] presented findings from their [[Salton Sea]] restoration report, highlighting ecological and policy linkages between the Salton Sea and Colorado River Delta.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River Delta]] historically supported extensive wetlands, riparian habitat, and productive fisheries before upstream diversions eliminated most freshwater flows. Participants documented severe ecological degradation including loss of native vegetation, disappearance of wildlife habitat and migratory bird stopover sites, contamination from pesticides and heavy metals, and impacts on traditional subsistence practices of indigenous communities.

Scientific presentations highlighted critical ecological linkages: Manuel Salvador Galindo-Bect reported that the upper delta serves as breeding ground for fish larvae, with presence/absence of flows directly impacting larval production and affecting the livelihoods of [[Cocopah]] fishermen. Miguel Lavine discussed efforts to reconstruct historical physical environment conditions when river flows reached the delta, noting that current shrimp trawling sweeps the area 10 times annually. Ed Glenn described work on vegetation mapping and endangered species surveys, noting that approximately 2 million acre-feet (MAF) are released below the last diversion point but their fate and ecological function remain poorly understood.

The [[Salton Sea]] presentation by Michael Cohen and Jason Morrison revealed hydrological and biological connections between the Sea and delta region. The Salton Sea has become eutrophic from nutrient loading, experiencing mass die-offs of birds. With loss of wetlands throughout the Central Valley and Pacific coast, the Salton Sea has become crucial habitat along the [[Pacific Flyway]] for migratory birds. Proposed restoration alternatives including diverting flows from [[Wellton-Mohawk]] drainage (currently supporting [[Cienega de Santa Clara]] wetland) or pumping water from the Gulf of California could significantly impact delta ecosystems.

Water quality emerged as critical concern: Jacqueline Garcia reported studying pesticide contamination in the delta region and bioaccumulation of selenium and mercury through wetland food chains. Ernesto Reynoso noted that endangered species and Cocopah communities struggle due to lack of river flows and chemical contamination. CRIT Elders testified that pesticides have ruined water quality, preventing traditional agricultural use, and the tribe now requires piped water. They reported increased illness rates among tribal members and complete loss of habitat for wildlife and birds.

## Key Findings

**Major Obstacles Identified:**

1. **Information Gaps**: Lack of precise, quantitative scientific data on current delta conditions, inventory of delta resources, and requirements for restoration. Insufficient monitoring of fishery resources and their relation to water flows. Inadequate integration of science into management processes.

2. **Stakeholder Coordination Failures**: No common voice or coordination among diverse stakeholders. Failure to integrate numerous stakeholders into management and decision processes. Mexican communities lack opportunity to express perspectives and participate in policy development. Racism toward Mexico and Native American communities. Lack of coordination among NGOs with no cohesive educational or political strategy.

3. **Policy and Institutional Barriers**: The "Law of the River" framework fails to recognize environmental needs. U.S. approval of off-stream water storage, surplus criteria, and water transfers (e.g., [[Imperial Irrigation District]]-San Diego transfer) will eliminate limited water supplies potentially available for delta. The [[Multi-Species Conservation Plan]] addresses U.S. river management for 50 years without Mexican consideration. Unclear position of Mexican government on delta conservation.

4. **Communication Deficiencies**: Lack of information dissemination in useful, translated formats to local users. Insufficient communication between U.S. and Mexico at both government and grassroots levels. No unbiased route for broadcasting information on delta conservation, limiting residents' access to policy debate.

5. **Values and Economics**: Deeply rooted priority on financial gain over ecological values. Underlying value systems based solely on financial criteria. Shortsightedness regarding issues without immediate impacts. Increasing demands for limited water resources with tendency to increase supply rather than manage demand. Population growth pressures. Political and economic power of U.S. water users.

**Solutions and Action Items:**

The conference established the **Colorado River Conservation Caucus** with draft mission statement encompassing:
- Facilitate recognition of unheard conservation voices
- Halt degradation and restore ecological and sustainable social conditions
- Seek alternative water sources and specific water allocation for delta conservation
- Require U.S. water managers to consider effects on Mexican delta resources
- Monitor species dependent on flows
- Increase scientific understanding through resource inventory and adaptive management
- Stop toxic threats (Ward Valley nuclear waste site, pesticides)
- Strive for unified voice on policies affecting delta
- Relate economic health to ecosystem health
- Cultural enhancement and recognition of recreation/fisheries importance

**Communication Infrastructure**: Established delta-colorado list-serve hosted by [[ITESM-Guaymas]] (contact: selenedg@campus.gym.itesm.mx) for posting research, meeting notices, and facilitating binational dialogue. All postings in original language with translation assistance available.

**Short-term Actions**: 
1. Refine Caucus goal statement (lead: David Hogan, Southwest Center for Biological Diversity)
2. Develop list-serve and website (Carlos Valdes, Yamilett Carrillo, Jennifer Pitt)
3. Joint comments on MSCP
4. International instream flow letter
5. Potential litigation against [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]]
6. Develop media strategy

**Salton Sea-Delta Linkages**: The [[Pacific Institute]] report identified three critical problems with Salton Sea restoration: (1) focus stops at border without considering binational impacts, (2) agencies not basing restoration on science, (3) narrow focus only on salinity reduction and elevation stabilization. Proposed alternatives including diverting Colorado River flows through [[All-American Canal]], pumping from Gulf of California, or using Wellton-Mohawk drainage all have significant implications for delta ecosystems. Target elevation of -232 feet would expose lakebed, allowing windborne dispersion of salts and contaminants primarily southeast over Imperial and Mexicali valleys. Draft Environmental Impact Statement scheduled for October 1999 release provides opportunity for public comment.
