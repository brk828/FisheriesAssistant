# Hatchery Trout Nutrition, Biotin Deficiency and Water Quality Research

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This hatchery research report, produced by a New York State and Federal fish-culture laboratory, covers trout nutrition, disease, water quality and stocking biology. Its central finding is that [[Blue-Slime Disease]] in trout is linked to low [[Biotin]] storage in the liver, and that standard hatchery diets may sit close to a biotin deficiency. The report also tests [[Cod Liver Oil]] growth responses, the effects of dietary fat on body composition, the physiological effects of [[Ammonia]] accumulation in pond water, liver-extract treatment of [[Anemia]], and several commercial diet products. A final section tracks changes in brown trout after stream stocking.

## Background & Description

Over roughly the previous ten years, brown trout in New York State and Region 5 hatcheries had developed a bluish slime over the body surface that later sloughed away, leaving the fish patchy in appearance. Mortality was severe, and treatments had failed. The condition appeared around the time dry concentrates replaced earlier diets, and spleens (beef and pork) replaced livers. This led to suspicion that dry concentrates were harmful. The disease was inconsistent, however: it appeared at about the same time each year, and survivors often recovered without any diet change. The report therefore tested whether the disease was dietary or microbial.

Background work by [[Phillips Et Al]] (1947) showed that brown trout needed more dietary biotin than [[Brook Trout]] or [[Rainbow Trout]] to reach maximum liver storage. [[Rodgers]] (1949) found that beef liver cured affected brown trout. Phillips et al. (1949) also reported that cod liver oil increased blue-slime incidence and mortality while increasing growth, which led them to suggest biotin as the preventive agent.

**Methods (general):** Trout were fed diets built on a base of beef melts or beef spleen and the standard No. 6a dry mixture, fortified with [[Beef Liver]] or [[Brewer's Yeast]]. Fish were held for 20 weeks under established hatchery procedure (Tunison et al., 1939). Livers were analysed for biotin at intervals of about five weeks, with three analysis series in total. Some diets were run at 47°F and 52°F. Differences were tested by analysis of variance. The fat, anemia and practical-diet trials followed similar hatchery protocols.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report links fish health to pond conditions as well as diet. Ammonia and other excretory products accumulate in ponds with high fish density, high temperature and low water exchange. These products reduce the blood's capacity to bind oxygen. Water temperature influenced growth and food intake. Fish in colder water (47°F) grew more slowly than those in the hatchery supply, which varied from about 39°F in winter to about 60°F in summer. After stocking, fish fed on natural stream food, and their body fat changed in character. Trout in Fall Creek were the stocked population studied, and survival data were reported separately by Cornell University and the State of New York.

## Key Findings

### 1. Blue-slime disease and biotin
- Diseased fish consistently had low liver biotin. Feeding beef liver or [[Dried Brewer's Yeast]] prevented the symptoms, and both cured affected fish.
- Dried egg white added to a yeast-fortified diet produced typical blue-slime symptoms within about eight weeks. Liver storage was 534 milli-micrograms per gram in egg-white fish versus 1,217 in controls. Fish averaged about 14 g, so size may have limited mortality.
- Blue-slime appeared in brook and rainbow trout after a new fish meal, which contained less than half the biotin of the previous supply, was added in July. Fish meal made up 24% of the diet. Adding 5% dried brewer's yeast corrected the condition.
- Brown trout fed 50% beef liver (Diet 1154) showed good biotin storage and no blue-slime. Diets with 20% beef liver or 5% yeast did not prevent it in fish under 2 inches. The authors advised 33⅓% beef liver or 8% yeast for small fish, with lower levels for larger fish.
- Temperature had little effect on biotin storage. Fish that survived the disease outgrew it as they got larger.
- **Recommendation:** Add enough dried brewer's yeast to the dry-food portion to give an equivalent of 5% yeast in the diet of all hatchery brook, brown and rainbow trout.

### 2. Cod liver oil and growth
- Rainbow trout grew faster with cod liver oil at both 47°F and 52°F, with a similar response at each temperature.
- In brown trout fed biotin-fortified diets, cod liver oil increased growth. Fish that were fed unfortified diets could not be tested because of complete losses.
- Brook trout showed no growth change in cold water and reduced growth in warm water. Blue-slime was the likely cause. The authors concluded that the normal hatchery diet is closer to deficiency than previously thought.
- Summary: cod liver oil can increase growth, but biotin deficiency must be prevented.

### 3. Dietary fat and body composition
- Cottonseed oil, [[Crisco]] (a soft fat) and [[Beef Tallow]] (a hard fat) were each added at 5% to a base diet of 50% beef spleen and 45% No. 6a mixture, brook trout, 20 weeks.
- Water content did not differ between diets.
- Crisco and cottonseed oil significantly increased body fat. Beef tallow did not, indicating poor absorption and utilisation of the hard fat.
- Cottonseed oil fish grew slowest, and the report mentions the possibility of edema developing. Crisco fish gained weight mainly as fat rather than flesh.
- Body fat iodine number tracked the iodine number of the dietary fat. No fat supplement improved growth rate.

### 4. Metabolic products and ammonia
- Trout excretion of ammonia rises with activity and temperature. A rise from 47°F to 60°F may increase the rate about ten-fold.
- Ammonia was measured using Nesslerisation and a photoelectric colorimeter. Blood gases were measured on a [[Van Slyke Blood Gas Apparatus]] in fish of about 5–6 inches.
- At about 1.0 ppm ammonia, blood carbon dioxide rose 0.3 volume percent, nitrogen rose 0.15 volume percent, and oxygen fell 0.7 volume percent. The effect was noticeable from 0.3 ppm.
- The rate of ammonia accumulation is directly proportional to fish weight per cubic foot and to the rate of water change per hour.
- Literature suggests a maximum of 0.1 ppm in fish-culture water. The report suggests keeping ammonia below 0.2 ppm.
- Control options: reduce fish density, increase water flow, or lower pond water levels. Lower volume increases water change, and this dilution outweighed the effect of higher fish density.

### 5. Anemia and liver fractions
- Anemia was induced with a synthetic diet (casein-based, with corn starch, dextrin and cod liver oil). Normal blood counts run 1,000,000–1,500,000 cells per cubic millimetre. Treatment began below 800,000.
- Biopar A (whole aqueous liver extract) and Biopar C (70% alcohol-insoluble fraction) gave positive responses, with 720 and 770 thousand cells against a synthetic-diet control of about 500 thousand.
- Fed liver produced 820, 1,160 and 1,130 thousand cells across three 7-day periods.
- Biopar F injection (containing 0.57% phenol) caused excessive mortality and wiped out its lot.
- Vitamin B12 and a vitamin mixture (B12, folic acid, biotin) gave erratic results, but warrant further study.

### 6. Practical and commercial diets
- **Dried distillers solubles** (about 4.5 cents per pound) in place of part of the fish meal or dried milk gave excellent growth, low mortality and no blue-slime.
- **Commercial cat and dog food** replacing half the beef spleen gave growth equal to controls, with slightly higher losses. As a sole diet it failed: mortality was high, conversion poor, and a thiamin deficiency was suspected.
- **Canned carp** as a partial meat replacement gave average results for eight weeks, when the trial ended for lack of material. As a total diet it caused severe anemia after six weeks.
- **Canned trout food** as a supplement gave fair results: growth was inferior to controls, but no anemia or blue-slime occurred and losses were low. As a replacement for the meat portion it was a complete failure, probably because the mixture could not bind and food was unavailable to fish.

### 7. Stream stocking and chemical change
- The project was a cooperative programme between the Fish and Wildlife Service, [[Cornell University]] and New York State. The laboratory handled chemical analysis of fish before and after stocking.
- Fish had been reared on the same diet at 47°F (retarded) or in normal hatchery water. At stocking, retarded fish averaged 19.6 g and normal fish 80.3 g.
- Spring analysis: normal trout had about twice the fat content of retarded trout. By fall, hatchery fish showed no difference, suggesting that hatchery fat content tracks size rather than growth rate.
- After stocking, the fat content of both groups dropped markedly. Stream fish also had higher water content after one month. Iodine number rose in planted fish, indicating a softer body fat from natural stream food.
- Protein showed the least variation. Dry-basis comparisons were affected mainly by water content.
