# A Study of the Fishes of Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1936 Utah Academy of Sciences paper by [[Vasco M. Tanner]] is a regional fish fauna review. It aims to gather what was known about the native fishes of Utah, give keys for non-specialists, and compile a distributional list and bibliography. It recognizes 25 native species in 5 families, plus 23 introduced species. The paper also documents how habitat change, overfishing and drought had already reduced several native populations, most notably the [[Utah Lake Trout]] and the sucker populations of [[Utah Lake]].

## Background & Description

The paper's stated purpose is to compile all known information on the native fish fauna of Utah, with keys written for readers without training in ichthyology. The author argues that conservation work is urgent. Irrigation, grazing, lumbering, fires, new reservoirs and drained lakes had already changed the "balance of nature" over the preceding decades. Many once-abundant native species were believed to be "gone forever."

The introduction reviews the history of collection:
- [[Father Escalante]] (1776) and [[John C. Frémont]] (1844) were among the first non-Native visitors to record fishing in Utah waters.
- Scientific collections followed from the Beckwith and Gunnison (1859) and Simpson (1876) parties.
- [[Edward D. Cope]] described many new species from [[Hayden Survey]] and [[Wheeler Survey]] material. The most complete earlier study was Cope and Yarrow (1875), which listed 22 species.
- [[David Starr Jordan]] visited in 1880 and 1889. [[John O. Snyder]] visited [[Bear Lake]] in 1915 and described three endemic whitefish.

Most early collections are held by the National Museum (Washington, D.C.). This paper draws mainly on the [[Brigham Young University]] zoological collection, built up over many years by the author and by university expeditions over the previous decade.

Keys are arranged to follow Jordan's check list. The families are organized as follows:
- Native: Salmonidae, Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Medidae, Cottidae
- Introduced: Siluridae, Centrarchidae, Percidae

The paper also contains a Bibliography. The OCR text includes a page-1 fragment describing the beetle *Malachius utahensis*. It is unrelated to fishes and is not summarized here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat and faunal change are the paper's central theme. Several documented pressures are:
- **Diversion of spawning streams.** At [[Bear Lake]], diversion of streams used for spawning was reducing trout reproduction.
- **Overfishing and mesh regulation.** Fishing with seines and nets exceeded regulated limits in some cases, and illegal mesh use was reported.
- **Introduced species.** Introductions have "changed the food conditions" for native fishes. The Utah Lake Trout was rarely taken by the 1930s, partly because of introduced species.
- **Drought and lake shrinkage.** Utah Lake was severely affected by drought over four years, as detailed below.
- **Lake and stream chemistry.** The paper notes that the Utah Lake fish fauna had been studied in relation to lake chemistry, though specific data are not reproduced.

Food web information is limited but notable. [[Bonneville Cisco]] (*Leucichthys gemmifer*) fed mainly on plankton (*Diaptomus*). The Bear Lake whitefish were shown to depend on the food supply, which had been reduced by lowered lake levels.

Regional basins mentioned include the [[Bonneville Basin]], [[Great Salt Lake Basin]], [[Colorado River Basin]], [[Green River]] and [[Virgin River]] drainages.

## Key Findings

**Native fauna**
- 25 native species belong to 5 families. The keys cover only native species.
- Native Salmonidae include [[Utah Lake Trout]] (*Salmo utah*) and [[Colorado River Trout]] (*Salmo pleuriticus*). Tanner and Hayes (1933) argued there is "very little reason" to separate *S. utah* from other native trout.
- Three endemic whitefish occur at Bear Lake: [[Bonneville Cisco]] (*L. gemmifer*), [[Bonneville Whitefish]] (*P. spilonotus*) and [[Bear Lake Whitefish]] (*P. abyssicola*).
- Catostomidae has 4 genera with multiple species, including [[Utah Lake Sucker]] (*Catostomus fecundus*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Utah Sucker]] (*Notolepidomyzon utahensis*).
- Cyprinidae contains 8 genera, including [[Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Utah Lake Chub]] (*Siboma atraria*) and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys dulcis*).
- Cottidae is represented by 2 species of sculpin (*Cottus*).

**Taxonomic notes**
- *Catostomus ardens* is treated as a synonym of *C. fecundus*. Lateral line scale counts range widely, from 60 to 73.
- Snyder (1922) regarded *Siboma atraria* as a valid species rather than a synonym of *Leuciscus lineatus*.
- *Gila seminuda* is considered a synonym of *G. robusta*.

**Utah Lake Trout (*S. utah*)**
- In 1864 a single seine haul yielded "thirty-five and thirty-seven hundred weight" of trout. By the 1870s, "five hundred pounds is considered an enormous haul."
- Yarrow and Henshaw (1872) estimated the decline at about one-third, and reported a maximum size of 3 feet and 15.5 pounds. The average was about 14 inches and 1.5 pounds.
- By the 1930s the species was rarely taken in Utah Lake, Panguitch Lake or Provo River. It was most abundant at Bear Lake, where spawning-stream diversion was limiting recovery.

**Utah Lake Sucker (*C. fecundus*)**
- In 1881 Jordan and Gilbert described the species as occurring "in numbers which are simply enormous," and as "very destructive to the trout."
- Jordan (1891) reported a haul with about 200 mullet-suckers and one webug, along with 50 trout.
- Scale counts for 493 specimens ranged from 60 to 73 in the lateral line, with 11 specimens at 60 scales and 7 at 73.

**Bear Lake fishes**
- The [[Bonneville Cisco]] was taken with gill nets at 100 to 110 feet in September 1930. Digestive tracts of 30 specimens were examined, and over 95% of food consisted of *Diaptomus*.
- 113 specimens of *P. spilonotus* were taken in September 1930.
- The Bear Lake Whitefish reached 12 to 14 inches and was once shipped in quantity. It had declined, with the lowering of the lake and destruction of spawning areas cited as causes.

**Utah Lake drought (1932–1936)**
- The lake surface shrank from a normal 93,000 acres to about 50,000 acres.
- In winter 1934–35, hundreds of tons of suckers and carp died from freezing and crowding in deep holes. Commercial fishing was abandoned.
- In spring 1935 no suckers ran up the Provo River, which had never happened before.
- Proposals to restock the lake with suckers from Idaho were opposed.

**Introduced species**
- The Summary lists 23 introduced species, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Eastern Brook Trout]], [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Tench]].
- The author notes that the State Game Commission had not kept an accurate record of introductions, so the list relies on government reports and may be incomplete.

**Conclusion**
- The paper concludes that continued scientific study is required. It calls for investigations begun by the [[Federal Bureau of Fisheries]] and the [[Utah Department of Fish and Game]] to continue, so that future fishery problems can be addressed.
