# Fishes of the Smoky Hill River, Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #freshwater

## Executive Summary

This study documents the fish community of the Smoky Hill River in western and central Kansas, based on 62 electrofishing collections at eight stations from 29 May 1965 to 6 August 1966. The author recorded 13,959 specimens of 30 species (the text also cites 31 species for the same collections, a discrepancy in the source). The work establishes a baseline population record during the early stages of agricultural irrigation in the [[Cedar Bluff Irrigation District]], which was being assessed for possible [[Pesticide Contamination]] of soil, water, wildlife and fish. Standing crop, species diversity and seasonal abundance are analyzed alongside water-quality measures and a tagging study of fish movement.

## Background & Description

The study was published in the *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science* (Vol. 70, No. 1, 1967) by [[Robert C. Summerfelt]]. It extends earlier Kansas surveys of the [[Kansas River System]], [[Neosho River]], [[Marais des Cygnes River]], [[Big Blue River]] and [[Wakarusa River]].

Fishes were collected by electrofishing, with seining and trotlining used only as supplements to gauge sport-fishing potential. The standard unit of effort was one thorough electrofishing pass over a 100-yard (300-ft) permanently marked section. Specimens were preserved in formalin and isopropanol, or frozen for pesticide analysis. Identification relied on keys by [[Bailey]], [[Moore]], [[Eddy]] and [[Trautman]], and meristic methods followed [[Hubbs and Lagler]]. Physical and chemical measures (flow by the [[Robins and Crawford Method]], turbidity, dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide, alkalinity and pH) were taken at each visit, following [[American Public Health Association]] standards.

Eight stations were sampled along a west-to-east gradient, from near Russell Springs (station 1) to near Junction City (station 8). Stations 2 to 4, between Trego and Ellis counties, were the most intensively sampled, with 16 to 17 collections each. Station 2 was a wide, shallow sandy reach; station 3 lay 64 river miles below [[Cedar Bluff Dam]] and was shaded and more stable; station 4 had a bedrock bluff and pools; and stations 6 to 8 were turbid, sewage-influenced or reservoir-affected lower reaches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow and temperature:** Stream width, depth and flow increased from west to east. Midsummer water temperatures reached 36 °C because of scarce cover and few springs. Flow at station 2 ranged from 0.5 to 103 cfs, and turbidity from 25 to 5,500 units.
- **Hydrology:** Station 1 was intermittent and dried during the study period. Station 2 nearly disappeared in summer 1966 (depth 0.16 ft, width 8 ft). Station 7 was controlled by outflow from [[Kanopolis Dam]], which produced flood-type conditions.
- **Reservoir influence:** Gizzard shad, white bass, carp, river carpsucker and largemouth bass near stations 2, 3 and 6 were linked to [[Cedar Bluff Reservoir]] and [[Kanopolis Reservoir]], through stocking and upstream movement.
- **Groundwater:** Several spring-fed tributaries, including Salt Creek, carry relict fauna from the [[Ogallala Formation]]. Salt Creek yielded 104 specimens, including 10 white suckers that were absent from the mainstream.
- **Habitat use:** Species partitioned habitat by flow and cover. Red shiners favored undercut banks and slow pools. Sand shiners preferred open riffles over sand. Killifish favored warm, shallow riffles with slow current.

## Key Findings

**Species diversity and richness**
- 30 species from 13,959 specimens across 62 collections (31 species are cited elsewhere in the text).
- Station 2 and station 4 each yielded 20 species, an unexpectedly high number for the Dissected High Plains, where collections rarely exceed 10 species. Station 2's richness was partly attributed to its proximity to Cedar Bluff Reservoir.
- Including species of possible occurrence and five species reported from western Kansas by [[Metcalf]] (1966), the potential fauna is 42 species. Excluding crappies, golden shiner, goldfish and blacknosed shiner (not reproducing in the river), 37 species remain.
- Comparable regional counts: 45 species in the Big Blue and Wakarusa basins, 55 in the Neosho and Marais des Cygnes, 50 in south-central Kansas and only 22 in southwestern Kansas.
- Diversity index (species per specimen) was highest at station 1 and lowest at stations 2 to 4, where four or five species dominated.

**Dominant species** (four species constituted 76.9% of the catch; 14 species made up 98.55%)
- [[Sand Shiner]] (*Notropis stramineus*): 3,463 specimens, 24.81%, the most abundant species.
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*): 2,978 specimens, 21.34%. Station 4 held 42.31% of the total.
- [[Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma anomalum*): 2,834 specimens, 99.7% from stations 2 to 5. Spawning occurred in late April to early May, and young-of-the-year appeared in early June.
- [[Plains Killifish]] (*Fundulus kansae*): 86.19% from station 2.
- [[Bluntnose Minnow]] (*Pimephales notatus*): 931 specimens, 6.67%, nearly twice as abundant as the [[Fathead Minnow]].
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*): 201 specimens, 1.44%. Carp abundance tracked high water and upstream movement.
- [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*): 3.16%, most abundant centrarchid.
- Other notable taxa: [[Channel Catfish]] (greatest abundance at stations 4 and 7), [[River Carpsucker]] (50.62% from station 4), [[Creek Chub]] (58.78% from station 4), [[Suckermouth Minnow]] (35.23% from station 5), and [[Gizzard Shad]] (93 specimens, 85% from station 6).

**Rare and noteworthy records**
- Shovelnose sturgeon and longnose gar: single specimens, both from station 8 on 18 May 1966.
- Speckled chub: two specimens, both from station 8, marking its western limit in this river.
- Emerald shiner: single sexually mature female from station 8.
- Sturgeon chub: reported near Junction City in 1964 but not found in four trips to station 8.
- Walleye, smallmouth bass, bluegill and largemouth bass were each represented by one to three specimens, mostly from reservoir stocking or pond escapees.

**Taxonomic clarification: plains minnow**
- The 0.95% of specimens identified as *Hybognathus placitus* could be separated from *H. nuchalis* only by the shape of the basioccipital bone. Eye-to-head ratios (4.25 to 4.53) and most meristic characters pointed to *H. nuchalis*. The author noted possible hybridization.
- Station 8 yielded 71.97% of plains minnows, and station 2 yielded 25.00%.

**Standing crop (biomass)**
- Weighted mean standing crop was 19.57 lb/acre (surface) and 32.5 lb/acre-foot, a 39.8% difference attributed to the shallow river. Both values are treated as minimal estimates.
- Station means ranged from 0.1 lb/acre (station 8) to 28.1 lb/acre (station 4).
- Maximum collection value was 117.4 lb/acre at station 2 on 29 July 1966. The highest lb/acre-foot value was 234.7 at station 2.
- These values are much lower than the mean of 169.6 lb/acre for 33 eastern U.S. warmwater stream entries cited from [[Larimore]] (1961).
- Differences among stations 2, 3 and 4 were not significant at the 5% level. Biomass was negatively correlated with mean width and surface area (significant for lb/surface acre and lb/acre-foot), reflecting electrofishing efficiency and habitat diversity.

**Seasonal and temporal patterns**
- Standing crops at stations 2 to 4 showed spring and summer maxima and midwinter lows, but the study was too short to confirm a consistent cycle.
- Station 2 had the widest fluctuations, and the author noted that abundance of carp was inversely related to killifish abundance in high water.
- Between 1965 and 1966, standing crop in lb/acre-foot increased at each of stations 2, 3 and 4. The author concluded that electrofishing did not deplete populations, and local bait harvest appeared negligible.

**Migration and tagging**
- In 1965, 58 fish (21 carp, 35 river carpsuckers, one gizzard shad, one channel catfish) were tagged and released. Only one carp was recaptured, 13.6 miles downstream after about 192 days (roughly 0.08 miles per day).
- In 1966, recaptures of channel catfish, carp and river carpsucker were reported, including one carpsucker recaptured after 22 days and one carp after 35 days. Two fish recaptured at their marking station suggested no movement under stable water levels.
- The author judged the data insufficient for firm conclusions, but the results indicate high mobility in carp, channel catfish and river carpsucker.

**Management implications**
- The river offers potential for channel catfish and flathead catfish fishing in the lower mainstream, and trotline and pole fishing yielded satisfactory catches.
- The study recommends multi-year sampling to clarify seasonal and long-term variation in standing crop. Variation of up to 100-fold within a station was noted, and the author attributed the wide range of published standing-crop values partly to differences in sampling methods.
