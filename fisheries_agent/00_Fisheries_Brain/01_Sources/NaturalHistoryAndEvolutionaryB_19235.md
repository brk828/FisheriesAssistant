# Natural History and Evolutionary Biology

#herpetology #natural-history #viperidae

## Executive Summary

This essay by [[Harry W. Greene]] argues that [[Natural History]], the detailed study of where organisms live and what they do, is a necessary foundation for evolutionary biology, ecology, and conservation. Greene contends that natural history has lost standing since the late twentieth century, partly because of Popperian views of science, reductionism, and pressure for rapid results. He illustrates its value with snake and lizard feeding studies from California and Costa Rica, and closes with five recommendations for theoreticians, systematists, journals, granting agencies, and natural historians.

## Background & Description

Greene defines natural history as approximately synonymous with [[Autecology]] plus descriptive ethology. It covers behavioral repertoires (ethograms), reproductive chronology, clutch size, and ecdysis, but generally excludes fine-scale hormonal and physiological detail. He presents it as the "idea and induction" phase of the scientific method, analogous to descriptive anatomy and alpha systematics.

The essay identifies several reasons for the decline in regard:
- Strict Popperian views of science that ignore how theories originate.
- Reductionism and "technophilia" linked to molecular biology (citing [[Platt]]'s strong inference).
- Institutional pressure for fast results.
- Poorly documented accounts, such as snake diet lists that give prey taxa without prey size, sex, or other variables.

Greene contrasts this with comparative anatomy, where carefully illustrated descriptive work is still valued. He suggests that functional and evolutionary morphologists increasingly use holistic, comparative approaches, while ecologists and ethologists have narrowed their focus to topics such as clutch size and mating systems.

He also reports practical consequences. No [[National Science Foundation]] panel explicitly covers autecology and descriptive ethology. In 1983, reviewer instructions for *Herpetologica* implied that papers simply describing the life history of a previously unstudied organism were unacceptable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Prey-predator contrasts among desert snakes

Greene argues that knowing a prey species is common in predators' diets can hide major behavioral differences. The [[Western Whiptail Lizard]] (*Cnemidophorus tigris*) is a key prey item for several desert snakes, but each predator captures it differently:
- The [[Sidewinder]] (*Crotalus cerastes*) strikes relatively large whiptails from under bushes during the day.
- The [[Longnosed Snake]] (*Rhinocheilus lecontei*) is nocturnal and probably subdues whiptails in their burrows.
- The [[Coachwhip]] (*Masticophis flagellum*) is a fast, diurnal snake that chases relatively small, active lizards.

### Tropical viper observations at La Selva

Greene's observations at [[La Selva Biological Station]] in northeastern Costa Rica include a gravid female [[Fer-de-lance]] (*Bothrops asper*), weighing 2.2 kg, watched daily for several weeks in summer 1984. She stayed within a few square meters of a treefall gap, basked for several hours daily, and rested in a coil at night. She did not feed during the observation period. On several occasions she drank rainwater droplets from her back, once for more than ten minutes. Greene suggests that water-holding skin may matter for some tropical snakes, as it does for desert lizards and snakes, and notes that a laboratory morphologist might not have considered this without field observation.

### Bushmaster ecology

Radiotelemetry of the [[Bushmaster]] (*Lachesis muta*), a 2-4 kg viper 2-3 m long, gave the following results:
- A female slept during the day and hunted in an alert posture each night.
- She used three sites for 3-15 days each and moved about 50 m in a 35-day period.
- On the 15th night at the third site, she caught a rodent weighing at least 50% of her body weight.
- She then rested nine days before changing sites.
- Hunting sites were often within one meter of a palm (*Welfia georgii*), whose fruits attract the rodents that bushmasters eat.

Using these data and metabolic equations, C. R. Peterson (personal communication) estimated that an adult needs only about six typical meals per year.

## Key Findings

- **Natural history is a foundation, not a lesser activity.** Greene argues that detailed autecological data are "data-in-waiting" that can both provoke theory and test unforeseen predictions.
- **Current attitudes are partly justified by poor practice.** Incomplete accounts, such as taxonomic prey lists without size or context, lack value for broader questions.
- **Behavior links structure and environment.** Feeding studies show that similar prey can pose very different tasks for different predators, so behavioral detail is needed to test feeding adaptations.
- **Natural history constrains theory.** Greene argues that most foraging theory assumes search and handling costs that are similar, which fits small, abundant prey. Bushmaster data suggest a general foraging theory must allow for very infrequent feeding and multi-day decision scales.
- **Empirical gaps are large.** Greene notes that few careful, direct observations of hunting in wild animals exist outside large mammals, birds, and some lizards.
- **Recommendations:**
  1. Theoreticians and laboratory workers should engage with natural historians and avoid in-group jargon.
  2. Systematists should treat comparative natural history as essential to morphological evolution studies.
  3. Specialty journals should regularly publish high-quality autecological papers, following the model of the *Journal of Ecology*.
  4. Granting agencies should fund high-quality natural history for its lasting value and low relative cost, even without immediate applied answers.
  5. Natural historians should be rigorous, precise, and current on new techniques and theory. Greene explicitly rejects "sloppy" work and ill-conceived studies.
- **Urgency:** Greene argues that shrinking habitats and spreading extinctions make precise field documentation a matter of scientific responsibility.
