# Flood Plain Information: Colorado River, Davis Dam to Topock

#hydrology #flood-plain-management #colorado-river

## Executive Summary

This 1969 technical report from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Region 3) maps flood-prone land along about 42 miles of the lower [[Colorado River]] in the Mohave Valley, from [[Davis Dam]] to Topock, Arizona. It is a flood-hazard planning document, not a biological study. It is intended to support Federal, state and local agencies, and private developers, in [[Flood Plain Management]], zoning and land-use regulation.

The mapping uses a [[Levee-Design Flood]] of 50,000 cfs (Davis Dam to River Mile 443) and 70,000 cfs (River Mile 443 to Topock). The report separates flood-prone land into a [[Floodway]] and a [[Floodway Fringe]], and gives guidance on which development is compatible with each.

## Background & Description

The report was prepared under several sources of authority:

- **[[Boulder Canyon Project Act]]** (effective December 21, 1928), which authorized [[Hoover Dam]] for flood control, navigation and river regulation, and gave the Bureau of Reclamation flood-control responsibility on the lower Colorado.
- **[[Colorado River Front Work and Levee System]]** Act (authorized beginning with the act of June 28, 1946), covering operation and maintenance of the levee system in Arizona, Nevada and California, channel control and related studies. The work extends from Lee Ferry, Arizona, to the U.S.–Mexico border.
- **[[House Document No. 465]]** (89th Congress, 2nd Session, transmitted August 10, 1966), titled *A Unified National Program for Managing Flood Losses*. A task force found that average annual flood losses were still rising despite large federal flood-control investment, partly because of continued development of flood-prone land. It recommended making flood-hazard information an integral part of the federal flood-control program.
- **[[Executive Order No. 11296]]** (August 10, 1966), which directed agencies to evaluate flood hazards for federally owned or financed buildings, roads and facilities, and to share available flood-hazard information with state and local agencies.

The report states that flood-hazard information for tributaries is furnished by the [[Corps of Engineers]]. Requests for more detailed data go to the Regional Director, Region 3, Boulder City, Nevada.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes the river's physical setting rather than its biology. Three reaches are summarized:

- **Davis Dam to Big Bend (about 10 miles):** A reasonably stable channel confined by gravel deposits and mountain chains. Clear water released from Davis Dam has washed finer material out of the channel, leaving an armored, stable riverbed. Channel work here is limited to local bank stabilization and dikes at Big Bend, 9 miles below the dam.
- **Big Bend to Needles (18 miles, California):** Before 1957 the river meandered through an alluvial valley, with sharp bends and cavelike banks, which made the borderlands hard to use. Between 1957 and 1960 a dredged channel, levees and related works stabilized it.
- **Needles to Topock (14 miles):** Aggradation (sediment buildup on the valley floor) since 1941 threatened major flooding at Needles. Emergency protection began, and a dredged channel with rock-stabilized banks was completed between Needles and Topock in 1951.

Flood history provides context for the hazard. Before Hoover Dam, snowmelt floods damaged the lower basin nearly every spring. Peak flows of 300,000 cfs occurred in 1884 and 220,000 cfs in 1921. Hoover Dam, completed in 1935, greatly reduced flood peaks downstream, but significant floods remain possible below Davis Dam.

## Key Findings

**1. Flood-hazard extent and design flows**
- The mapped area is the Mohave Valley reach, about 42 river miles between Davis Dam and Topock.
- The levee-design flood is 50,000 cfs from Davis Dam to River Mile 443 (about 3 miles upriver of the mouth of Piute Wash) and 70,000 cfs from River Mile 443 to Topock.
- The design flood is treated as equivalent to a 100-year frequency flood. The report notes that computing its actual frequency was impractical.
- It combines releases from Davis Dam with tributary inflows below the dam.
- Floods larger than the design flood are possible, but the combination of factors needed would occur only rarely. The report says the maximum probable flood is seldom economically practical to design for, so some risk must be accepted.

**2. Flood control and regulation**
- Maximum mean daily inflow to Lake Mead (Hoover Dam) was reduced from 119,200 cfs to a maximum mean daily outflow of 35,500 cfs in 1941, and from 122,400 cfs to 30,000 cfs in 1952.
- Lake Mohave (Davis Dam) has storage capacity of about 1.8 million acre-feet, but it is generally operated at a high level to re-regulate Hoover releases and generate power. It has no flood-control operating provisions.
- Hoover flood-control rules limit releases so flows at the gauge below Davis Dam do not exceed 40,000 cfs.
- Operating under those rules with inflow comparable to the estimated 1884 flood would require releases of 77,000 cfs for about 2 months.

**3. Levee system**
- Levees in Mohave Valley were built for flows of this magnitude, with a 3-foot freeboard above Needles and a 2-foot freeboard below Needles.

**4. Floodway and floodway fringe**
- The **floodway** is the river channel and the part of the flood plain needed to convey the levee-design flood safely. Where levees are absent, it is the area needed for safe conveyance. In most reaches its width is about the distance between levees.
- The **floodway fringe** is flood-prone land not needed for conveyance. It may exist where levees are absent, or behind levees where openings let tributary floodflows through. It is usually subject to low-velocity flows or backwater.
- The maps (Plates 2–4) were drawn from Bureau of Reclamation topography and aerial photographs. Contour quality varies. Where field surveys disagree with the mapped flood extent, the computed water-surface elevations govern.

**5. Development guidance**
- **Floodway:** must stay unobstructed. Encroachment would reduce conveyance capacity, raise upstream flood heights and velocities, expand the flooded area, and could cause levee failure. Boat docks, camping and picnicking facilities, golf courses, parking and agriculture usually do not significantly reduce conveyance and are generally acceptable.
- **Floodway fringe:** the main task is recognizing flood potential and then designing for it. Tools include building codes, zoning ordinances, subdivision regulations and flood-proofing requirements, ranging from a full ban on building in the flood plain to flood-proofing standards.
- Keeping the floodway clear is a shared responsibility. The federal government controls through permit review and ownership of riverside land; state and local governments through zoning and building ordinances; and private developers by avoiding obstructive structures.

**6. Reference materials and contacts**
- The Bureau of Reclamation's *Guidelines for Construction in the Flood Plains of the Lower Colorado River* should be consulted before development in the floodway or floodway fringe.
- Related references are John R. Sheaffer's *Introduction to Flood Proofing* (University of Chicago) and the Corps of Engineers' *Guidelines for Reducing Flood Damages*, available from the Los Angeles District, Corps of Engineers.
