# Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Alternatives

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation #endangered-species #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document presents a comprehensive set of recovery alternatives for four endangered big river fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]], prepared for the Six Agency Committee in August 1993. The proposal outlines three primary recovery concepts: habitat creation and management, propagation, and control of non-native fishes. The document emphasizes that successful recovery must balance endangered fish restoration with maintaining water delivery and hydroelectric generation needs. A system-wide analysis framework is proposed using Geographic Information System (GIS) technology to identify conflicts and opportunities. The regulatory framework under the [[Endangered Species Act]] is detailed, including Section 7 consultations, Section 10 research permits, and Habitat Conservation Plan (HCP) development pathways.

## Background & Description

The four endangered fishes addressed are [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). These species historically migrated extensively throughout the basin, with individual squawfish reportedly migrating over 200 miles. The document notes that coordination with ongoing programs is essential, including the Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Implementation Program, the Colorado River Fisheries Project, and Glen Canyon Environmental Studies.

The proposal identifies key non-native species of concern including [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Sand Shiner]] (*Notropis stramineus*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), and [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*). At least 60 other non-native species inhabit the basin, many preying upon or competing with endangered fishes. Larvae and juvenile endangered fish are particularly susceptible to predation by non-native species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] ecosystem has been fundamentally altered by dam construction, flow regulation, introduction of non-native species, and habitat modification. Historical bottomland nursery habitats have been lost due to [[Tamarisk]] (*Tamarix* spp.) encroachment, which forms dense streamside bands preventing natural flooding patterns. Native fishes are adapted to temporary flooding with complete annual drainage of bottomlands, conditions that favor natives over non-native predators, competitors, parasites, and pathogens.

High spring flow releases have been demonstrated to significantly reduce non-native fish numbers and create improved endangered fish habitat through sediment dispersal, increased nutrient base, and decreased water clarity—conditions favoring species adapted to the historically murky Colorado River. Water temperature, turbidity, and flow timing affect reproduction rates of both native and non-native species.

The document notes that 69 threatened, endangered, and candidate (TEC) species occur in Colorado River corridors of the Lower Basin, 21 TEC species in Upper Basin corridors, and 73 TEC species throughout the entire basin. This includes mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, invertebrates, and plants, indicating ecosystem-wide conservation challenges.

## Key Findings

**Habitat Creation and Management Alternatives:**

1. **Construct Endangered Fish Rearing Areas**: Create tributary pools, floating rearing areas in calm mainstream sections, or fish fences around calm pools/coves. Non-native species would be physically removed and endangered fish larvae/juveniles introduced. Fish would be released when large enough to resist predation. Upper Basin sites preferred for warmer water temperatures.

2. **Adapt Gravel Pits for Rearing**: Acquire existing gravel pits and lowland areas adjacent to mainstream. Cut channels to allow high spring flows to flood areas and drain with summer flows, creating nurseries favoring endangered fish while inhibiting non-natives. More common in Upper Basin; Lower Basin sites require high spring releases.

3. **Adapt Streamside Areas for Rearing**: Restore bottomlands by cutting channels to allow flooding. Addresses tamarisk-caused embankments. May require annual tamarisk cutting and mosquito control. Applicable in both basins; some reaches retain established endangered fish populations while others require larval/juvenile introduction.

4. **Habitat Management**: Use existing dam/reservoir capabilities for occasional high spring flows to enhance endangered fish survival and reduce non-native fish numbers. Can occur in high water years, minimizing water delivery impacts. Side benefits include improved camping beaches and riparian habitat renewal. Applicable to most reaches below dams.

5. **Build Fish Passage Facilities**: Encourage endangered fish movements throughout basin, allowing re-establishment in historical ranges. Would avoid genetic isolation and distribute fish to suitable unoccupied habitats. Effectiveness and cost unknown; may also distribute non-native fishes. Could be built at any existing basin dams.

**Propagation:**

Culturing endangered fishes has proven successful; large numbers can be reared with relative ease and low expense. However, artificial culture alone is inadequate due to genetic problems requiring periodic introduction of wild genetic material. Propagation is a key element for producing stock to repopulate extirpated areas. Best used in reaches within historic range where populations are extirpated or no longer reproduce; limited use where fish reproduce successfully.

**Non-Native Fish Control:**

1. **Intensive Netting and Trapping**: Temporarily reduce predatory non-native fish numbers in selected areas. Results are short-lived as fish from outside areas invade quickly and surviving populations regenerate. Must be repeated regularly or combined with exclusion alternatives. Applicable for any reach.

2. **Poisoning Non-Native Fishes**: Several chemical pesticides show potential. Permanent extirpation throughout significant river reaches not yet accomplished. Relatively inexpensive with established methodology but not selective and may affect non-target species. Some target fish may avoid poisoning.

3. **Habitat Management**: Discourage habitats advantageous for non-natives through spring flow timing, water temperature, turbidity manipulation. High spring releases show impact on non-natives. However, ecology of both endangered and non-native fishes poorly understood. Applicable to all reaches below power-generating dams.

4. **Biological Control**: Introduce specific predators, parasites, or diseases to reduce non-native populations. No successful efforts yet established for Colorado River non-native fish species. Research-intensive and expensive in early stages with no assured success. Introduction of pathogens may be tightly regulated with possibility of unpredicted consequences.

5. **Angler Education/Regulation**: Programs instituted in Colorado and Utah for endangered fish identification. Angler impacts probably minimal compared to non-native fish impacts. Fishing regulations can be adapted to reduce non-native stocks or restrict fishing in propagation areas.

**System-Wide Analysis Framework:**

Proposes three-step process: (1) Identify and overlay minimum requirements using GIS to map water delivery/hydropower systems and TEC species habitat requirements, defining conflict points; (2) Prioritize species concerns by evaluating current knowledge, identifying gaps, defining critical habitat requirements, and assigning priorities; (3) Identify system-wide alternatives based on previous steps, developing detailed management plans meeting all constituent needs.

**Regulatory Framework:**

- **Section 10 Research Permits**: For scientific studies enhancing propagation/survival of threatened/endangered species. USFWS considers purpose adequacy, effects on wild populations, conflict with enhancement programs, extinction threat reduction, expert opinions, and applicant resources.

- **Section 7 Permits**: For federal agency projects with potential impacts on listed species/critical habitat. Involves biological assessment, formal consultation, and biological opinion from USFWS determining jeopardy/no jeopardy status with reasonable and prudent alternatives.

- **Section 10 Incidental Take Permits**: For groups without federal nexus through Habitat Conservation Plan (HCP) development. Requires: (1) minimizing and mitigating impacts to maximum extent practicable, (2) not reducing appreciably survival/recovery prospects, and (3) adequate funding assurance. HCP process involves strategy development, alternative identification, species identification, biological data review, take evaluation, monitoring/mitigation program development, funding identification, and NEPA documentation. Currently seven HCPs approved with over 20 awaiting approval.

The document emphasizes that the most effective recovery program would utilize most or all presented alternatives in combination, coordinated with ongoing recovery and research projects throughout the basin.
