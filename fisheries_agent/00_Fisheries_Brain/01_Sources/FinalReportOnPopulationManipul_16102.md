# Population Manipulation Studies in Three Kentucky Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Kentucky Division of Fisheries report, authored by James R. Charles, summarizes a [[Dingell-Johnson Project]] running from 1952 onward. The project tested whether manipulating fish populations in three warm-water streams could improve sport fishing, where [[Rough Fish]] heavily outnumbered [[Game Fish]]. Two streams, [[North Fork of Licking River]] (46 miles) and [[Whippoorwill Creek]] (12 miles), underwent [[Total Population Manipulation]] with [[Rotenone]] in 1952 and were restocked. A third stream, [[Floyd's Fork Creek]] (11.5 miles), underwent [[Partial Population Manipulation]] by [[Electric Seine]] rough fish removal in 1955.

Annual sampling showed that all three populations gradually reverted toward their original composition. The author concluded that population manipulation is affordable but offers little, short-lived benefit to game fish. The report recommends against total manipulation in unrestricted streams and against rough fish removal as a standalone management tool.

## Background & Description

The project was motivated by declining game fish populations in many Kentucky streams during the preceding two decades, attributed to erosion, siltation, pollution, and possibly fishing pressure, while rough fish increased. Available information on warm-water stream dynamics was considered insufficient for management.

The original objectives were to:
1. Determine whether total population eradication was feasible in streams with heavy rough fish populations.
2. Determine whether game fish could be established at levels above former abundance.
3. Determine whether population manipulation before watershed management or stream improvement was economical and biologically sound.

A fourth objective, added later, asked whether partial manipulation (rough fish removal only) could achieve the same ends.

**Study streams:**
- **North Fork of Licking River (Mason County):** The upper 46 miles were the experimental section. Average width ranged from 27.5 to 45.3 feet across thirds, with depths of 2.1 to 3.7 feet. Dense [[Water Willow]] covered riffles.
- **Whippoorwill Creek (Logan County area):** A 12-mile section in a rural, clear-water, rocky-bottom stream typical of south-central Kentucky. It supported a characteristic [[Smallmouth Bass]] population and had almost no pollution.
- **Floyd's Fork Creek (Oldham, Jefferson, and Bullitt Counties):** A 60.9-mile main stem, divided into six reporting sections. Average width was 55.4 feet and average depth 2.11 feet. Pollution was minor, mainly from a distillery's occasional fish kills.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Populations were sampled with rotenone at 2.5 pounds per acre-foot, usually on 1/4-inch-mesh block nets left overnight and checked over about two days. Floyd's Fork was sampled with an electric seine backed up by rotenone. Landowner leases were obtained in 1952. Scale samples were analyzed by the [[Direct Proportion Method]].

**Eradication and restocking (1952).**
- North Fork: 1250 pounds of rotenone treated 46 miles (about 230 acres). The crew estimated about 37,000 pounds of fish were present. Four days later, caged bluegill survived, indicating the toxin had dissipated. Restocking used [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[White Crappie]], and [[Longear Sunfish]] from hatcheries and seined ponds. Stocking was limited by availability.
- Whippoorwill Creek: 300 pounds of rotenone treated 12 miles (36 surface acres) in one day, removing about 537 pounds of fish. Largemouth, smallmouth, and rock bass, white crappie, warmouth, bluegill, and golden shiner were restocked. More than 4000 game and about 3000 forage fish were stocked in fall 1952.

**Rough fish removal (1955).** On Floyd's Fork, 11.5 miles were electroseined from the mouth upstream. Totals were 2065 fish of 39 species (1535 pounds), with 231 desirable fish (79.7 pounds) and 1834 undesirable fish (1455.3 pounds). Combined with sampling removals, 5445 fish weighing 2181.4 pounds were removed in 1955.

**Pre-treatment populations:**
- North Fork: 2662 fish weighing 466 pounds from 2.88 acres, about 162 pounds per acre. Game fish were 4.5% by number and 6.5% by weight, rough fish 29.9% and 70.0%.
- Whippoorwill: 302 fish weighing 22.4 pounds from 1.50 acres, about 15 pounds per acre. Rough fish (chiefly [[Redhorse]] and [[White Sucker]]) were 16.6% by number and 69.8% by weight.
- Floyd's Fork: 3611 fish weighing 726 pounds from 10.7 acres, about 68 pounds per acre. Game fish were 4.2% by number and 7.5% by weight. [[Gizzard Shad]] dominated.

**Post-treatment reversion.**
- North Fork standing crop: 82 pounds per acre (1953), 48 (1954), 49 (1955), and 55 (1956), versus 162 before eradication. [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] fell from about 28% of pre-treatment weight to only 2 specimens. Channel catfish, flathead catfish, and stonecat had not returned by 1956.
- Whippoorwill standing crop: 8 pounds per acre (1953), 26 (1954), 13 (1955), and 47 (1956). The 1956 figure was unexplained.
- Whippoorwill game fish weight share fell from 26.4% (1953) to 8.3% (1956). Panfish weight share rose overall.
- Floyd's Fork: after removal, game fish made up 3.7% of numbers and 2.9% of weight, against 4.1% and 4.3% before. Forage fish rose to 73.8% of numbers, mostly gizzard shad. Gizzard shad was the most abundant species across the stream in 1956.

**Validation.** Supplemental 1956 samples (5.02 acres on North Fork, 2.00 acres on Whippoorwill) closely matched the annual sampling areas, supporting the validity of the long-term data.

**Growth.** Growth data were limited by small sample sizes. In the undisturbed North Fork population, largemouth bass averaged 4.2 inches at the end of year one, and white crappie averaged 2.9 inches. Whippoorwill rock bass averaged 1.0 inches. On Floyd's Fork in 1955, largemouth bass took about 3 years to reach 10 inches, Kentucky bass slightly more than 4 years, and white crappie reached 9.0 inches by year four. Post-removal (1956) growth rates were higher in all age groups for the game species, but the author did not attribute this to rough fish removal.

## Key Findings

- **Total manipulation is feasible.** Eradication and restocking were carried out at costs described as no more prohibitive than comparable management techniques.
- **Game fish benefits were questionable and short-lived.** Both streams reverted toward original composition within about a year to several years. Standing crops stayed below pre-eradication levels.
- **Positive barriers were too costly.** Isolating stream sections with barriers such as [[Electric Fish Screens]] might have produced different results, but their cost was found to be prohibitive.
- **Partial removal was ineffective.** Removing 5445 fish from Floyd's Fork in 1955 gave no measurable improvement for game fish, and gizzard shad increased.
- **Conclusion for total manipulation:** It is not recommended for stream sections with unrestricted fish movement, or as a substitute for watershed management and stream improvement.
- **Conclusion for rough fish removal:** The author recommended that it no longer be considered as a single management tool for improving stream populations before watershed rehabilitation.
- **Environmental conditions matter.** The author suggested population manipulation might be unnecessary where environmental conditions are near optimum, though this remained untested.
- **Overall verdict:** Population manipulation alone, without habitat improvement, "holds little promise" as a management tool for the average Kentucky stream.
