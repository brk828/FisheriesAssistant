# The fishery of the lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #Ictaluridae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1940s survey by the California Division of Fish and Game, written by the author with [[Chester Woodhull]] and compiled in the wartime period (fieldwork largely in early 1942), describes the fishery of the lower [[Colorado River]] from the Mexican boundary to [[Boulder Dam]]. The river was transformed by a sequence of dams that trapped silt and stabilized flow, producing clear-water reservoirs such as [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Havasu]] and a large network of irrigation canals and drains. The result was a new sport fishery dominated by introduced species, especially [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]], while native fishes such as the [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Bony-tail]] and [[Colorado River Squawfish]] had declined sharply, probably by the late 1930s. The report is explicitly preliminary, intended as a compendium and guide for future management studies.

## Background & Description

The Colorado Basin is divided into an upper basin of high plateaus and mountains and a lower, arid desert basin. The lower river runs through about 250 miles of desert, much of it inaccessible except by boat, with lateral "lakes," oxbows and hundreds of miles of canals and drains. The survey was built on [[Field Survey Methods]] including physical and biological sampling, fish collections with hoop-nets, gill-nets and seines, and interviews with residents, wardens and refuge managers. Fish were described under standard headings (recognition characters, distribution, size, reproduction, food, place in the fishery).

The reach is described by segments, including:
- Mexican boundary to Laguna Dam (21 miles), with the old Alamo Canal and [[Pilot Knob Wasteway]].
- Laguna Dam to Imperial Dam (6 miles), a low diversion weir with a silting basin.
- Imperial Dam to Headgate Rock Dam (135 miles), including the [[All-American Canal]] diversion.
- Headgate Rock Dam to Parker Dam (15 miles), a small dam supplying the Colorado River Indian Reservation.
- Parker Dam to Topock (42 miles), including [[Lake Havasu]] and Mohave Canyon.
- Topock to Boulder Dam (112 miles), including Needles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions.** Mean annual precipitation is about three inches, with almost no natural tributaries; flow is derived mainly from snowmelt in the upper basin. Flows varied from about 3,000 to 150,000 c.f.s. historically. The river carried very heavy silt loads; [[Fortier and Blaney]] estimated about 138,000 acre-feet annually at Yuma before the dams, with about 80 per cent suspended silt. Boulder Dam was expected to retain about 187,000 acre-feet annually, and silt at Yuma fell to 2,071 acre-feet in 1940. Dissolved oxygen in 1942 ranged from 7.6 to 11.4 p.p.m. Total dissolved solids were high (around 700 p.p.m. in the river, and 860 to 1,578 p.p.m. in drains).

**Temperature.** Air temperatures reach 120°F; the river ranges from about 40°F to 90°F, with a low near 50°F in January and summer highs of 80° to 90°F. Water released from Lake Mead at depths of 120 to 320 feet was 54 to 61°F, making the river below Boulder Dam briefly suitable for [[Trout]], but permanent trout populations were judged impossible below about 25 to 30 miles. Bass were expected to spawn near 65°F, usually reached in March or April.

**Vegetation.** Riparian species include [[Cottonwood]] and willow, with [[Cattail]] and [[Bulrush]] stands at places such as Topock. Submergent plants (Najas, Ruppia, Potamogeton) were sparse, and blue-green algae were common in bays.

**Food web.** Benthic invertebrates were scarce in the main river. Lake Havasu samples averaged 11.0 chironomid larvae per sample, and Haughtelin Lake samples included oligochaetes, snails, mayfly and caddisfly larvae. Small crustaceans (cladocerans, ostracods, copepods) were abundant in cut-off waters. [[Forage Fishes]] included bluegill, green sunfish and mosquitofish.

**Predators and parasites.** Piscivorous animals included herons, pelicans, cormorants, mergansers, kingfishers, [[Bullfrog]], soft-shelled turtles, the [[Desert Garter Snake]] (considered only a suspect), and the Pallid Coon. Parasitism was not considered unusual.

**Anthropogenic effects.** The dams created fishing water and impoundments, but they also blocked migration (though no anadromous runs were affected) and entrained fish into the [[Colorado River Aqueduct]], where fish losses occurred at Gene Wash Reservoir in February 1940 (about 1,000,000 green sunfish, 30,000 largemouth bass and 30,000 channel catfish rescued).

## Key Findings

- **Fish fauna.** The survey and literature recognize about 19 fishes or families of interest. Native species included the humpback sucker, bony-tail, Colorado River squawfish, and the rarely recorded native fishes; the rest were introduced or exotic (carp, bullheads, bass, sunfishes, crappies, catfish, mosquitofish) or marine visitors (ten-pounder, mullet).
- **Native decline.** Residents and wardens reported the native fishes as "once abundant" but scarce by the 1930s. In 1942, only one native species (humpback sucker, 13 specimens) was collected in the river, and the author saw no bony-tail, "salmon," or humpback sucker over several years of patrols.
- **Largemouth bass.** The major game fish. Scales from 83 bass (January-May 1942) showed a year-class of 6.6 inches at age one, 9.6 inches at age two, 13 to 18 inches at ages three to four (about 1.1 pounds at age three). Most bass spawned first at age three. Spawning peaked in April 1942. Lake Havasu's largest known bass weighed 9 lb 4 oz.
- **Channel catfish.** Abundant and widespread; the most successful game fish in all water types. Contest weights of channel catfish from the Yuma Rod and Gun Club ranged from 14 lb 8 oz to 20 lb 8 oz. The largest known catch was a 22 lb 9 oz fish from Lake Havasu in 1941.
- **Bluegill and green sunfish.** Bluegills outnumbered green sunfish about 10 to 1 in 1942. Bluegills matured as small as 2.1 inches (males) and 3.0 inches (females) in Haughtelin Lake. Green sunfish declined sharply in Lake Havasu after 1940.
- **Mullet.** Large numbers in the river and the [[Salton Sea]]; Salton Sea mullet averaged 5.2 pounds in 1943 commercial catches, and sexual maturity was 16.5 inches (males) and 20.5 inches (females). Commercial fishing had been prohibited in 1931 and was reestablished experimentally in May 1942.
- **Ten-pounder.** First recorded in the drainage in 1941; a fish of 17.1 to 19.7 inches (1.75 to 2.75 pounds) was taken in the Salton Sea in May 1942, and it became a California game fish in August 1943.
- **Trout.** Occasional rainbow trout were reported downstream of Boulder Dam, with a 16-inch fish carrying two large layers of eggs in January 1943; the authors judged them stray migrants, not established populations.
- **Introduced species.** Crayfish (Cambarus), bullfrogs and soft-shelled turtles (Trionyx emoryi, probably introduced) have spread in the river and are a minor food resource. Goldfish, buffalofish and striped bass were possible additional introductions.
- **Fishing intensity.** Population along the river was small: Yuma (11,304 combined for the three largest towns, with Parker at 456). Lake Havasu's Needles Boat Landing was the largest resort. The practicable season was limited by summer heat and winter cold, with peaks in March to June and mid-September to November.
- **Regulations.** The report recommended a single Colorado River District with uniform rules and licences honored on both sides. Recommended bag limits: 10 bass or channel catfish per day (15 lb total), 40 aggregate for sunfishes, crappies, bullheads and other game or pan fish (20 lb). No bag limits for carp or mullet. Size limit of 10 inches for channel catfish and a raised limit for largemouth bass. Angling only, with restrictions on hooks and bait fishing.
- **Stocking.** The author saw no need for further warm-water stocking, given established populations; he recommended against introducing more exotics, and suggested that crappies (suspected bass competitors) and green sunfish were undesirable.
- **Future outlook.** The river was still in flux; silt reduction and stabilization were expected to improve fish habitat, although the author estimated 30 to 50 years for the new regime to establish.
