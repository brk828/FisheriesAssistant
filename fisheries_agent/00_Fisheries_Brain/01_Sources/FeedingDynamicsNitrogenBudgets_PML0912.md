# Feeding Dynamics, Nitrogen Budgets, and Ecosystem Role of Longfin Dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*) in a Desert Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Tharp and Grimm (Environmental Biology of Fishes, 1987) examines the feeding habits, diel feeding periodicity, daily ingestion, and nitrogen budgets of [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) in [[Sycamore Creek]], an intermittent [[Sonoran Desert]] stream. Sampling occurred in May and November 1983, when food quality differed but total algal and macroinvertebrate standing crops were similar. The fish acted as an opportunistic omnivore, eating mainly insects (mayflies) when they were abundant and mainly algae when they were scarce. Because algal food was low in nitrogen, the fish raised its daily dry-mass ingestion to compensate. The authors conclude that these fish, through rapid excretion of ammonia, play a significant role in recycling nitrogen to primary producers in this nitrogen-limited stream.

## Background & Description

*Agosia chrysogaster* is an abundant, successful cyprinid native to the Sonoran Desert in Arizona and northern Mexico. It is the most common fish in Sycamore Creek and is found in uniform coarse sand to fine gravel runs and shallow pools, as well as cobble-boulder riffles where the catostomid [[Pantosteus Clarki]] is most abundant. Introduced minnows, *Notropis lutrelensis* and *Pimephales promelas*, are minor but persistent components of the fauna.

Prior work on fish energy and nutrient budgets has focused mainly on energy, with fewer studies of nutrients. This paper addresses that gap by tracing ingested energy and nitrogen through to biomass and excretion, as part of a broader investigation of consumer roles in [[Nitrogen Cycling]] in desert streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stream conditions:** Summer-autumn discharge averages 0.01–0.05 m³ s⁻¹; winter-spring discharge is typically higher (0.1–1.5 m³ s⁻¹). Water temperatures ranged 15–25 °C in May and 19–23 °C in November.
- **Benthic food:** In May, the green alga *Cladophora glomerata* formed mats, alongside diatoms. Dominant invertebrates were the mayflies *Baetis quilleri* and *Tricorythodes dimorphus* and several [[Chironomidae]] species, which together made up more than 90% of macroinvertebrate biomass. In November, *Cladophora* was absent; benthos consisted of diatoms, flocculent detritus, fish and snail feces, and short filaments of *Oedogonium* and *Anabaena*.
- **Standing crops:** Chlorophyll *a* and total invertebrate densities and biomass were similar between dates, but mayflies made up a much smaller share of invertebrate biomass in November (Table 1).
- **Nitrogen-limited system:** [[Sycamore Creek]] is nitrogen limited, so the form and timing of nitrogen supply to primary producers matter.

## Key Findings

- **Opportunistic omnivory:** Of 39 fish in May, 11 (29%) had empty anterior intestines. Among the 28 with food, mean composition was 30% diatoms, 14% filamentous Chlorophyta, and 56% insects. In November, 17 of 50 fish (54%) had food; insects were significantly more prevalent (53%) and algae significantly less than in May (t = 2.64 and 2.70, d.f. = 53, p < 0.01).
- **Individual specialization:** Many fish ate only one food type. Insects alone made up 100% of gut contents in 11 of 28 fish in May (39%) but only 4 of 27 in November (15%). Algae alone made up 100% in 17 of 27 fish (63%) in November versus 9 of 28 (32%) in May.
- **Diel feeding periodicity:** Based on gut fullness, feeding was markedly diurnal in November (F = 8.66 and 15.78, p < 0.001) but relatively constant over the day in May (F = 1.19 and 2.34, not significant). Ingestion rate was a better measure of periodicity than gut fullness. Ingestion was significantly higher during the day than at night, especially in November (F = 15.95, p < 0.001; May F = 7.84, p = 0.009).
- **Gut evacuation:** Evacuation followed the [[Elliott and Persson Model]] (exponential). Three methods gave instantaneous evacuation rates of R = 0.496, 0.630, and 0.758 h⁻¹ (methods 1, 2, and 3), which were used to estimate ingestion.
- **Ingestion rates:** Daily dry-mass ingestion was far higher in November (215.4 mg DM fish⁻¹ d⁻¹) than in May (111.4 mg DM fish⁻¹ d⁻¹). Because food nitrogen content was lower in November (weighted mean 3.77% N versus 5.82% in May), daily nitrogen ingestion was similar: 8.12 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ in November versus 6.47 in May. The authors interpret this as compensatory feeding on low-quality food, a response not previously reported in fishes.
- **Nitrogen assimilation:** Food N fell from an average of 4.4% in the mixed diet to under 1% in feces. Egestion averaged 1.79 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹, 22–28% of ingestion, giving an apparent nitrogen assimilation efficiency of 72–78%. This is lower than the >90% reported for carnivorous [[Bluegill Sunfish]] but reasonable for an omnivore with refractory algal nitrogen.
- **Excretion and utilization:** Field excretion was 3.11 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ in May and 4.05 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ in November (including an assumed 10% urea addition). Calculated utilization was 1.41 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (May) and 4.67 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (November), with utilization efficiencies of 19% and 56%.
- **Population scale:** Budgets were scaled using a mean density of 8 fish m⁻² (n = 4, SE = 1.9). Population excretion was 13.2 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹ in May and 14.9 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹ in November.
- **Ecosystem significance:** Nitrogen stored in fish biomass made up 7% of total stream nitrogen standing crop (as stated in the text; the abstract reports 34%, so the two figures are inconsistent in the source). Fish excretion equaled 5–10% of average total stream nitrogen retention over a 20-day successional period. Because ammonia is immediately available to autotrophs, this rapid recycling may be important to the nitrogen economy of the stream, beyond what energy flow alone would suggest.
- **Feces as a resource:** Fish feces may enter the benthic organic matter pool, be reingested, or be mineralized to ammonia, all of which affect nitrogen availability.
