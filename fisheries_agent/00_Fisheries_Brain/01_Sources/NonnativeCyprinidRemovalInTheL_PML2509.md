# Nonnative Cyprinid Removal in the Lower Green and Colorado Rivers, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (November 2004, Publication #05-10, Utah Division of Wildlife Resources) evaluates whether mechanically removing [[Nonnative Cyprinids]] by seining backwaters can benefit endangered native fishes, primarily [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], in the [[Green River]] and [[Colorado River]] of Utah. The study was funded by the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] and ran from 1998 to 2000.

Removal covered a 50-mile reach of the Green River and three disjunct 10-mile reaches of the Colorado River. Four or five removal trips were made each year, in April to June on the Green River and July to August on the Colorado River. Every backwater in the study area was seined repeatedly on each trip.

Backwater-level depletion occurred within single sampling days, but it was not sustained across trips. Reach-level effects were temporary, and catch rose again because of reproduction, growth, and immigration. No significant shifts in species composition were detected, and the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP) could not detect treatment effects. The authors concluded that control is limited to short-lived, site-specific reductions, and they recommended continued and expanded efforts.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The target natives were [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The main nonnative competitors and predators were small cyprinids: [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), [[Sand Shiner]] (*Notropis stramineus*), and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*). The document notes that at least 40 nonnative fish species occur in the Upper Colorado River Basin, and that nonnative cyprinids are small, highly fecund, and extremely tolerant of temperature, pollution, discharge, and siltation.

**Rationale.** Nonnative cyprinids are thought to affect early life stages of natives through predation and competition, since they are abundant in habitats used by larval and young-of-year (YOY) native fish. Lentsch et al. (1996) proposed mechanical removal by seining in low-velocity habitats as a control method, and this study tested that approach.

**Hypotheses.** The study tested five working hypotheses:
1. Seining all backwaters in a reach depletes cyprinids at the backwater level.
2. Seining all backwaters in a reach depletes cyprinids at the reach level.
3. Seining shifts species composition.
4. Fall YOY ISMP sampling detects changes in cyprinid abundance and composition from the previous spring's removal.
5. Blocking adult cyprinids out of flooded tributaries increases habitat for larval natives.

**Methods.** A 4.8 mm mesh seine targeted fish over 25 mm TL, with a block seine isolating each backwater and a depletion series of hauls. Catch per unit effort (CPE, fish/10 m²) was the main metric. Analysis used ANCOVA on log-transformed CPE, with trip as a covariate. Backwaters above and below the treatment reaches served as controls, and these received only one seine pass.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Green River.** The treated reach was 50 miles, from Ruby Ranch to Mineral Bottom. The San Rafael River is the only major tributary entering the reach. Many dry washes flood at high water and create low-velocity habitat, and the largest flooded washes were often more than 500 m long and 2 m deep. Spring sampling was timed to precede razorback sucker spawning and to coincide with the ascending hydrograph. Peak flows were 24,100 cfs (1998), 30,000 cfs (1999), and 18,400 cfs (2000).

**Colorado River.** Treated sites were three 10-mile reaches: Dewey (RM 97–87), Moab (RM 65–55), and Lathrop (RM 30–20). Backwaters were small and ephemeral, and many dried as flows fell. Flows stayed below 12,000 cfs during sampling, and flows in 2000 were consistently under 4,000 cfs. Moab Wash carries ammonia from the Atlas Uranium Mine tailings pile. Peak flows were 28,500 cfs (1998), 19,000 cfs (1999), and 17,500 cfs (2000).

**Sampling effort.** On the Green River, 1998 effort was 88,149 m² over a seven-mile treatment reach. In 1999 and 2000, treatment effort was 80,160 m² and 119,115 m² across the full 50 miles. On the Colorado River, treatment effort was 22,194 m² (1998), 34,400 m² (1999), and 131,580 m² (2000).

## Key Findings

**Removal totals**
- Green River (1998–2000): 139,713 fish removed. Nonnative cyprinids were about 91% of the catch, and no other nonnative species exceeded 1% in any year.
- Colorado River (1998–2000): 219,709 fish captured across treatment and control reaches. Nonnative cyprinid removals were 13,740 (1998), 84,598 (1999), and 47,653 (2000). Native captures were dominated by larval suckers, including 8,829 in 1998.

**Hypothesis 1: backwater-level depletion**
- Depletion was observed within some individual backwaters on one-day sampling occasions.
- On the Green River, the five unique backwaters sampled each trip showed no significant effect of removal (df = 98, F = 1.95, P = 0.17).
- On the Colorado River, backwaters were seldom available for more than one trip, so trip-to-trip removal effects could not be evaluated.

**Hypothesis 2: reach-level depletion**
- Green River: the three-year mean CPE was 8.87 in treatment and 13.71 in control reaches (t = −2.82, df = 243, P = 0.005). The difference was mostly in 1998 and 1999, and treatment CPE did not decline relative to control.
- Green River: adult nonnatives declined on each trip in 1998 and 1999, but the net removal effect was not significant because catch increased through immigration, reproduction, and growth.
- Colorado River: no significant treatment-control difference. Adult declines within 1998 and 1999 were temporary, and catch in 2000 rose across trips.

**Hypothesis 3: species composition**
- No significant shifts in species composition were observed.

**Hypothesis 4: ISMP detection**
- Fall YOY ISMP sampling did not detect changes in nonnative cyprinid, Colorado pikeminnow, or razorback sucker abundance after removal. The authors attributed this partly to the limited statistical power of ISMP.
- Colorado pikeminnow: 60 individuals in 1998, 85 in 1999, and 1,489 YOY in 2000. Green River catch rates rose from 1997 to 2000 but stayed below the long-term mean.

**Hypothesis 5: block nets**
- [[Block Nets]] were tested in three Green River washes (School Bus, White, and Red). Adult nonnatives made up 31% inside versus 49% outside the nets at School Bus Wash, and 18% inside versus 10% outside at White Wash.
- Repeated net failures from overtopping, beaver damage, and flash floods meant the technique's usefulness could not be determined.

**Native response**
- No razorback sucker larvae or juveniles were identified during the project. One wild juvenile was absent from later electrofishing, and no YOY or juvenile razorback sucker were captured in fall or later surveys.
- Temporary reductions did not lead to a significant increase in razorback sucker or Colorado pikeminnow catch or recruitment.

**Authors' conclusions**
- Removal is limited to short-lived, site-specific reductions. Nonnative cyprinids are very difficult to eradicate, and recolonization is rapid.
- Several confounding factors weakened interpretation: high natural variability in cyprinid abundance, changing discharge and habitat, and changes in sampling protocols between years.
- Several hundred thousand nonnatives were removed, but the effort (five weeks per 30–50 river miles, with four people and two seines) was modest relative to the hundreds of river miles infested.

**Recommendations**
- Continue removal and evaluate additional methods, including:
  - More removal passes and higher frequency.
  - Earlier spring starts and fall removal to reduce over-winter competition.
  - Alternate gear, such as an electric seine, or chemical treatment.
- Assess both depletion across trips and treatment-control differences, since they can give conflicting results.
- Design studies to detect subtle native fish responses.
- Further test block nets for excluding nonnatives from productive off-channel habitats, especially on the Green River.
