# Carbon Black Increases Snowmelt and Forage Availability on Deer Winter Range in Colorado

#wildlife-management #mammalogy #Cervidae #habitat-manipulation #winter-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1975 USDA Forest Service research note documents an experimental application of carbon black to accelerate midwinter snowmelt on [[Mule Deer]] winter range in [[Colorado]]. The study tested whether artificially darkening snow surfaces could increase forage availability during the critical late-winter period when deer populations face starvation. On south-facing slopes, carbon black treatment melted over 40 cm of snow to bare ground in February despite average daylight air temperatures of -8.8°C. The treatment did not significantly alter snow density. The research suggests potential for targeted application on high-value winter range to reduce deer mortality during critical late-winter periods.

## Background & Description

Winter forage availability is a primary limiting factor for [[Mule Deer]] (*Odocoileus hemionus*) populations across much of North America. Snow depth, more than any other factor, determines forage accessibility on winter ranges. While snowfences had proven effective at relocating drifts away from browse stands, snow falling directly on shrubs still accumulated to depths prohibiting deer use.

The study applied principles long used in Russia and Japan for accelerating spring snowmelt on airport runways and increasing glacier runoff. Artificial darkening of snow surfaces increases absorption of short-wave radiation, thereby accelerating melt rates. Carbon black (Philblack N 550) was selected based on previous research by Megahan (1968) showing it more effective than soil, coal dust, or cinders for spring snowmelt acceleration.

Seven experimental sites were established on Junction Butte, Grand County, Colorado at 2,310-2,350 m elevation. Each site consisted of paired 10 m² treatment and control plots with similar aspect, slope, and vegetative cover. Natural windbreaks provided by dense [[Serviceberry]] (*Amelanchier alnifolia*) and other shrubs minimized windblown snow effects. Four sites faced generally north (aspects 35-105°), three faced south (aspects 140-200°).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area in [[Middle Park]], Colorado supports approximately 10,000 mule deer on less than 2,600 ha of winter range. In some years, thousands of deer die from starvation during February and March when snow depth limits forage access and deer reach their poorest physical condition. This critical late-winter period coincides with late pregnancy in does, making nutritional stress particularly consequential for population dynamics.

The experimental period (January 30 - February 16, 1974) represented severe winter conditions. Maximum daily air temperature exceeded 0°C only twice during the 16-day study. Mean air temperature during daylight hours averaged -8.8°C, with lows reaching -25.0°C. Despite these conditions, liquid water was observed near treatment plot edges when air temperature was -16°C.

Previous research in the area (Gilbert et al. 1970, Wallmo and Gill 1971) had documented the critical relationship between snow depth and mule deer distribution and survival. Complementary studies by Carpenter (1972) and Williams (1972) showed that nitrogen fertilizer increased herbage yields up to 87% and stimulated earlier growth initiation, suggesting potential synergistic benefits with carbon black treatment.

## Key Findings

**Application Protocol**: Carbon black was applied at 336 kg/ha (34.4 g/m²) using a backpack fertilizer spreader during calm early morning conditions. Three applications were made (January 30, February 4, and February 9, 1974), producing dark gray patches on snow surfaces with good coverage.

**Snowmelt Effectiveness - South-Facing Slopes**: Treatment dramatically accelerated snowmelt on the three sites with southerly aspects (140-200°). Average snow depth decreased 91.8% from 42.9 cm to 3.6 cm over 16 days, compared to only 7.4% natural decrease (40.6 to 37.6 cm) on control plots. All snow melted to bare ground except small patches shaded by tall shrubs.

**Snowmelt Effectiveness - East-Facing Slopes**: The four sites with easterly aspects (35-105°) showed more modest but still substantial results. Average snow depth decreased 50.5% from 53.3 to 27.4 cm, while control plots showed no decrease. Small areas melted to bare ground on treated plots.

**Weather Interference**: New snowfall temporarily negated treatment effects. Light snow 2 days after first application covered carbon black with 2-3 cm, halting melt on east-facing plots for 2 days. Heavier snowfall (7-8 cm) on February 4 buried carbon black and prevented melt for 4 days. However, subsequent melting re-exposed previous applications, tripling carbon black concentration on the surface after approximately 10 cm of snow melted.

**Snow Density**: Analysis of covariance showed treated snow was slightly denser than control snow before the second and third applications, but differences were not statistically significant (P<0.20). Carbon black treatment did not substantially alter snow density.

**Snow Crust Hardness**: Crust hardness values were too variable for statistical analysis. Treatment did not greatly alter crust hardness until snow depth reached 4-8 cm, at which point rapidly melting snow became wet and slushy, forming ice crust at night.

**Vegetation Response**: Green vegetation appeared early on melted plots. Control plots remained snow-covered for 4 additional weeks (until approximately March 15) after treated plots melted to bare ground. No obvious effects on vegetation were apparent from subjective examination the following summer, though long-term effects on soil structure, moisture, plant phenology, growth rates, and palatability were not investigated.

**Management Applications**: Aerial application cost was estimated at approximately $12 per hectare. Treatment of selected, highly productive, snow-covered sites totaling no more than 100 ha could appreciably increase forage availability. If preventing death of several hundred deer and improving condition of surviving does in late pregnancy, population effects could be considerable.

**Timing Considerations**: Late February represents optimal treatment timing when solar radiation is intensifying rapidly, forage needs are most critical, and heavy snowstorm incidence is decreasing on low-elevation winter ranges, increasing probability of successful treatment.

**Potential Synergies**: Combination of carbon black with nitrogen fertilizer could compound benefits (earlier growth, increased yields, higher protein content) at only slightly higher application cost than either treatment applied separately.

**International Context**: Finland (Rakkolainen 1971) reported extensive application of soot and "forest fertilizer" to 180 ha, improving nesting conditions for [[Black Grouse]] (*Lyrurus tetrix*), representing the only other documented large-scale wildlife management application of snow-darkening agents.
