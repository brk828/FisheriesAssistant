# Construction and Management of Kansas Ponds

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 bulletin from the [[Forestry, Fish and Game Commission]] (Pratt, Kansas), Bulletin No. 9, by Otto W. Tiemeier with Seth Way and Roy Schoonover, gives practical guidance on building and managing [[Farm Pond]]s for water supply, fish production, wildlife, and recreation. It replaces the 1911 bulletin by L. L. Dyche. The document covers site selection, dam and spillway design, water-level control, legal and cost considerations, fish species selection and stocking, harvest, fish kills, aquatic weed control, turbidity, and fertilization. Its central message is that a pond is only as good as its drainage area, and that fish stocking must be balanced with food supply and fishing pressure.

## Background & Description

The Commission noted that no one knew the exact number of Kansas ponds. The [[Soil Conservation Service]] (then the Agricultural Stabilization and Conservation Service) had aided more than 40,000 ponds from 1936 to 1953. The Commission estimated at least 60,000 ponds of all sizes in the state. The agency hoped to aid 50,000 more, and the Commission urged a goal of 100,000 or more because ponds meet growing demand for fishing. Many existing ponds were poorly built and few were fenced against livestock.

The bulletin states its purpose as assembling information on proper construction and management, drawn from the Commission, the Soil Conservation Service, the Kansas State College Agricultural Engineering Department, and neighboring states' agencies. It lists the main uses of a pond:

- Water supply for livestock, fire control, and limited irrigation
- Recreation such as swimming, boating, ice skating, and picnicking
- Fish production
- Habitat for farm game such as pheasants, quail, rabbits, and waterfowl
- Erosion and flood control

The bulletin also includes a verse by H. E. Coble (1950) describing a failed pond that was later rebuilt to the Commission's recommendations. The poem's story illustrates the bulletin's main lessons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rainfall and evaporation.** Average annual rainfall ranges from 40 inches in the southeast, to 30–35 inches in the northeast, 25 inches in the center, and 16–20 inches in the west. Rain is seasonal and sometimes torrential. Evaporation from open water exceeds 50 inches per year in the west and is about 30 inches in the east.

**Watershed and runoff.** Runoff is greatest from barren soil and tilled crops and least from woodland and pasture. A pond is no better than its watershed, so permanent grass or legume cover is recommended. Silt and nutrients from the watershed determine clarity and fish food.

**Water quality.** Clear water is cooler, holds more oxygen, and suits sight-feeding species such as bass and bluegill. Muddy water reduces fish production and recreational value. Clay turbidity from red soil areas can persist for a long time.

**Oxygen and fish kills.** Fish deaths are commonly caused by low oxygen rather than poisoning. Causes include winterkill in shallow ponds that freeze to the bottom, the decomposition of mature vegetation in late summer, and hot, cloudy days or silt-laden rain that shades vegetation. Decomposition of dead organic matter consumes oxygen.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Cattails grow in water 3–6 feet deep, so most of the pond should be deeper than this. Plants arrive through birds, muskrats, and inflowing water, and may be emergent (cattails), floating (water lilies, lotus), or submerged ("moss").

## Key Findings

### Site, Structure, and Design

- **Site selection:** Avoid permanent streams. The best site is a broad, flat-bottomed valley where the valley sides converge narrowly and steeply. Soil should be sandy clay or clay mixtures. Sand, gravel, shale, or limestone ledges make seepage hard to prevent. [[Bentonite]] has helped where seepage was limited.
- **Size:** Ponds under one-half surface acre are unsuitable for stocking. The bulletin recommends one to three surface acres. Drainage area guidance is five to ten acres per acre-foot of storage, with drainage generally not exceeding 50–60 acres per acre-foot.
- **Depth:** Keep a considerable portion 10–12 feet deep in the southeast and 12–14 feet deep in the west. Winter depth should be at least five feet to prevent winterkill.
- **Earth fill:** Remove the top six inches of soil and save it for the spillway and dam top. Dig a core trench about four feet wide, one foot into underlying clay, and fill it with sandy clay. Place layers no thicker than six inches at moist moisture content. Slopes are 2:1 downstream and 3:1 upstream. The crest is at least eight feet wide, and 10–15% of fill height is added for settling.
- **Trickle tube:** Install a tube of at least six inches diameter to release continuous trickles that erode the sodded spillway. The tube should have about one foot of fall per ten feet of length. Concrete collars go every 20 feet. Screens are not recommended because they clog.
- **[[Water Level Regulator]]:** A concrete inlet box with removable three-inch redwood planks allows the water level to be set at nearly any height or drained entirely. This structure can replace separate trickle and drain tubes.
- **Water supply and drainage pipe:** Use a pipe at least two inches in diameter (four inches if spring-fed) placed below the frost line with antiseep collars. A one-inch branch with a float valve feeds a livestock tank. A shutoff valve and capped drain allow complete drainage.
- **Spillway:** Make the [[Sodded Spillway]] at least ten feet wide with gradual side slopes, at least three feet below the fill top. Use riprap or concrete where the spillway meets the dam. Use a shallow drop structure if undesirable fish could migrate in through the spillway.
- **Vegetation:** Woody plants such as multiflora rose and cedars are recommended inside the fence for cover, but not on the fill, spillway, or water's edge.

### Costs and Legal Matters

- Earth moving is the largest cost, ranging from $0.12 to $0.25 per cubic yard.
- Government conservation payments were about 50% of construction cost, including fencing, tubing, and seeding.
- Under the [[Kansas Water Storage Law]] (Chapter 400, Laws of Kansas, 1941), landowners with an approved reservoir receive a ten-year assessed-valuation reduction. The schedule is $200 per acre-foot for the first ten acre-feet, then $150, $100, and $50 per acre-foot for successive five-acre-foot blocks. The total reduction is capped at $3,500 and at 40% of the contiguous acreage's assessed valuation.
- A permit from the Chief Engineer of the Division of Water Resources is required before building a dam over ten feet high or impounding more than ten acre-feet.

### Fish Selection and Stocking

Stocking rates were given per surface acre:

- 100 bass + 300 bluegills (clear water)
- 100 bass + 100 crappies + 300 bluegills (slightly cloudy water)
- 100 bass + 100 channel catfish + 100 crappies (murky water)
- 200 channel catfish alone
- 100 bass + 300 bluegills + 100–200 bullheads, optional

Key points:

- **[[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill Sunfish]]:** This is the most common combination. Bass do not spawn until age two, while bluegills spawn about twice as many young and may raid bass nests. The combination stays balanced mainly in clear water. Anglers tend to overfish bass, which allows bluegills to overpopulate. Small bluegills should be removed as caught.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]:** Preferred in Kansas for adaptability, rapid growth, palatability, and fighting ability. Channel catfish are a river spawner. Pond young suffer heavy predation because there are no ripples or shallows for escape. Survival is higher in slightly cloudy water. Channel catfish spawn only at about three to four years of age, so 200 per acre are recommended if stocked alone.
- **[[Crappie]]:** Does better than bass in cloudy water, but can become stunted.
- **[[Bullhead Catfish]]:** Usually avoided due to fast reproduction, but partly vegetarian and easy to catch. If stocked, add at least a year after bass and bluegills, at 100–200 per acre.
- **[[Carp]]:** Not stocked by the Commission, but common. Carp tolerate muddy water and can produce several thousand pounds per acre in Asia and Europe with supplemental feeding. A female may lay up to three million eggs. Carp are controlled mainly through harvest.
- **Sunfish:** Only bluegill should be stocked. Green, orange-spotted, and long-eared sunfish grow slowly and stunt.

### Harvest and Productivity

- Few drained Kansas ponds yielded under 200 pounds of fish per acre, and many yielded over 500 pounds.
- A bass needs four to six pounds of bluegills to make one pound of flesh.
- The bulletin recommends harvesting at least 50 pounds, and often more than 200 pounds, of fish per acre annually. This compares with 50–60 pounds of beef from an acre of good grassland.
- Bass and channel catfish can be harvested at about 12 inches. Stocked fingerlings often grow well for one to two years, but ponds are often overpopulated by three to five years, requiring draining and restocking.

### Obtaining and Restocking Fish

- Ponds must be at least one-half acre to qualify. Apply to the [[Forestry, Fish and Game Commission]] at Pratt as soon as the pond fills, by September 1. Deliveries run September through December.
- The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] at Farlington also supplies fish, usually in June, July, September, October, and November, with largemouth bass, bluegill, and channel catfish.
- Recipients should pledge that fishers hold current licenses, comply with regulations, and allow game protectors access.
- Restocking requires Commission permission. Draining is preferred over chemical removal. A pond should lie fallow for a year or at least one growing season to build fertility.
- Seine nets over fifteen feet long with mesh larger than one-fourth inch square are unlawful.

### Weed, Turbidity, and Fertilization Control

- **Ammate:** Controls emergent plants such as cattails. A spray of one pound Ammate crystals and two tablespoons detergent per gallon covers 100 square feet on cattails to 300 on water primrose.
- **2,4-D:** Controls broad-leafed emergents such as lotus, arrowhead, and spatterdock. A water spray of two tablespoons of 14% butylester per gallon, or an oil spray of 5% 2,4-D with kerosene, can be used.
- **Copper sulphate:** One part per million controls filamentous algae, but two parts per million kills many fish.
- **Sodium arsenite:** Three parts per million controls pondweeds. It is a violent poison.
- **Turbidity:** Carp and crayfish keep water roiled, so draining and restocking may be needed. Clay turbidity can be settled by organic matter. Oklahoma experiments found green vegetation equal to three times the weight of suspended clay clears water. Preventive erosion control is preferred.
- **Fertilization:** Alabama unfertilized ponds supported 40–200 pounds of fish per acre, while Kansas ponds without fertilization support 200–500 pounds. Fertilization is expensive ($20–$30 per acre per year), may cause algal mats and fish kills, must be continued once started, and is useless in muddy water. The recommended fertilizer is 8-8-4 at 80–100 pounds per surface acre plus 15–20 pounds of limestone, applied from May or June through September or October in amounts totaling about 800–1,000 pounds per season.

### Other Conclusions

- Fencing a pond keeps livestock from trampling the fill and muddying the water, and a livestock tank supplies water instead.
- Ponds should be planned as multiple-purpose systems that combine adequate drainage, sodded spillway, fence, drain, trickle tube, livestock pipe, suitable fish, and intensive fishing.
