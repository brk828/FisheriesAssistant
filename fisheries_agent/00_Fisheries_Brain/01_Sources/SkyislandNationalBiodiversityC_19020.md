# Sky-Island National Biodiversity Conservation Area Proposal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #biodiversity-policy

## Executive Summary

This 1992 proposal by the [[Sky-Island Alliance]] asks the U.S. Forest Service and Congress to designate the most ecologically rich mountain ranges of the [[Coronado National Forest]] as a biodiversity conservation area (a National Conservation Area, or possibly a [[UNESCO Biosphere Reserve]]) rather than a National Recreation Area. The document argues that the sky-island region is one of the most biologically diverse landscapes in North America and that recreation-focused designation would increase demand and damage sensitive habitats. It proposes eight mountain ranges for designation, stronger riparian and watershed protection, expanded inventory and monitoring, corridor protection, mineral withdrawals, and an advisory committee.

Note: the source concerns terrestrial and riparian land management and contains no fish-specific data. Fish are mentioned only in species-list counts and in cautions about non-native introductions.

## Background & Description

The Sky-Island Alliance was organized in 1991 as an association of conservationists and scientists working on the desert basin and range region of the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico (the Apachean/Madrean "sky-island" bioregion). Endorsing organizations as of May 1992 included the [[Arizona Native Plant Society]], the Grand Canyon Chapter of the Sierra Club, [[Sonoran Arthropod Studies]], [[Tucson Audubon Society]], and the [[Wilderness Society]] Southwest Office. A board of scientific advisors (including Dr. Reed Noss and Dr. Paul Martin) supports the effort.

The proposal responds to a U.S. Forest Service plan to designate parts of the Coronado as a National Recreation Area (NRA). The Alliance's larger goal is an international sky-island conservation effort; this first proposal concentrates on the Coronado in southeast Arizona and southwest New Mexico. The region joins four biotic provinces: the [[Sonoran Desert]], the [[Chihuahuan Desert]], the Rocky Mountain forests, and the Sierra Madrean forests. Five of Merriam's seven Life Zones occur there, and it is the only contiguous rise from both deserts to Canadian-type habitats in the U.S.

The eight proposed ranges are the Santa Catalinas, Rincons, Santa Ritas, Atascosa-Pajaritos, Huachucas, Chiricahuas, Peloncillos, and Pinalenos. The first six were chosen because they include all southeast Arizona ranges above 8,500 feet; the Atascosa-Pajaritos and Peloncillos were added for rare-species habitat and international migration corridors. Boundaries are to be drafted later.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Biodiversity:** The Coronado is described as having the greatest diversity of breeding land birds north of Mexico and the greatest diversity of mammal species of any area north of Mexico (USFWS 1978). Each mountain range is estimated to hold 800-1200 plant species, with the whole forest probably exceeding 2000. The [[International Union for the Conservation of Nature]] was recommending the larger U.S.-Mexico sky-island region as a Center of Plant Diversity.
- **Endemism and isolation:** Mountain summits act as isolated "islands" surrounded by arid "seas," promoting endemic populations such as the [[Mount Graham Red Squirrel]] and the Heliograph Peak fleabane.
- **Migration corridors:** Corridors are defined by north-south mountain chains and river systems. Bears, eagles and wolves depend on movement between ranges. Sky-island extinction rates are described as relatively high.
- **Riparian systems:** Riparian ecosystems in the West supported about 42% of North American mammal species, 38% of birds, 30% of reptiles and 14% of amphibians. In Arizona, about 250 of 543 vertebrate species depend on riparian habitat, yet riparian zones cover less than 1% of Arizona's vegetation and 2% of the Coronado.
- **Cienegas:** High-elevation wetlands (e.g., [[Bearwallow Springs]], [[Hospital Flat]], [[Treasure Park]]) cover much less than 1% of the ranges but hold about 5% of the Pinalenos' plant species.
- **Pressures:** Overgrazing, unstable roads, mining, recreation and off-road vehicle travel are named as main causes of watershed damage. Rangelands were overstocked by 73,000 animal unit months in 1986.
- **Funding:** Figure 5 (Sample 1990) is cited to show federal forest budgets favoring development over protection and rehabilitation.

## Key Findings

- **Designation choice:** The Alliance favors a biodiversity-focused conservation area over an NRA, arguing that recreation designation would increase demand and selectively harm the most sensitive communities (artificial lakes in riparian zones, ski areas on north-facing slopes, RV campgrounds on rare open terrain).
- **Economic claim:** Visitors to the [[Ramsey Canyon Preserve]] and [[San Pedro Riparian Conservation Area]] reportedly bring about ten million dollars a year to the Sierra Vista economy (Arizona Daily Star, 1991).
- **Sensitive species:** The Coronado's Forest Service lists include 56 sensitive plant species and 128 sensitive mammal, bird, fish, reptile and amphibian species, far more than any other Southwest forest.
- **Special areas:** Of eight proposed Research Natural Areas, the Coronado recommended two. One RNA was reduced to one-fourth its size. Of eight proposed Zoological-Botanical Areas, two were designated; the South Fork of Cave Creek was cut from 12,420 acres to 762 acres. The Alliance asks for reevaluation of the ZBA proposals in Alternative D of the EIS.
- **Riparian targets:** The Alliance asks that all degraded riparian areas reach better than satisfactory condition within two decades. The Coronado's own plan projected up to 50 years. Campgrounds at cienegas should be relocated and grazing curtailed.
- **Watersheds and range:** 31% of the forest's watersheds and 16% of grazing lands are in unsatisfactory condition. The Alliance urges fencing cattle out of riparian areas.
- **Research gaps:** Only mammals, birds and herpetofauna are well documented. Plants and insects remain inadequately inventoried. The Alliance calls for range-wide inventories, a monitoring program using 200-year vegetation records, and baseline areas with minimal human impact.
- **Climate monitoring:** Sky islands are presented as ideal sites for detecting climate change effects because restricted vegetation at range limits (e.g., Mt. Graham spruce-fir) responds quickly.
- **Mineral withdrawal:** The Coronado's proposed withdrawal from mineral entry for the Santa Catalina, Pinaleño and Chiricahua Mountains should be expanded to all listed-species areas, corridors, riparian zones and special designations, with inactive claims retired.
- **Recreation:** Recreation demand is rising; wildlife-related use reached 449,500 visitor-days in 1987. Non-native fish and game introductions are cautioned against where they could displace native species.
- **Staffing and advisory:** More field-based Forest Service staff are recommended, and a permanent multidisciplinary advisory committee should be set up in 1992, before the next forest planning round.
- **Conclusion:** Many 1986 Forest Plan targets were unmet due to budget shortfalls. The Alliance urges Congress and the Forest Service to increase funding and emphasize biodiversity in plan revisions.
