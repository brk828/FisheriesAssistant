# Trout and Salmon Culture (Hatchery Methods)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Fish Bulletin No. 107, prepared by [[Earl Leitritz]] for the California Department of Fish and Game (first printed 1959, reprinted 1963), is a practical handbook on [[Hatchery Methods]] for [[Trout and Salmon Culture]]. It is intended to orient new hatchery staff, serve as a reference for experienced managers, and promote uniform hatchery operations. Its scope spans hatchery water supply chemistry, egg structure and spawning, egg measurement and incubation, troughs and ponds, grading, weighing, transport, nutrition and feeding, disease diagnosis and treatment, anesthetics, and a classification of fishes. The text is largely procedural and emphasizes that water quality, temperature, handling, and timing strongly determine egg survival and fish growth.

## Background & Description

The bulletin was prepared at the request of California Department of Fish and Game hatchery personnel and was written to address everyday hatchery problems. The foreword notes that trout culture dates to 1741, when [[Stephen Ludwig Jacobi]] began artificial propagation in Germany, but that methods advanced slowly until shortly before [[World War II]]. Advances in chemicals for disease treatment, labor-saving devices, and [[Fish Egg Incubators]] were described as having revolutionized hatchery operations in the preceding decade.

Acknowledged contributors wrote individual sections on selective breeding, incubator hatching, aquatic weed control, nutrition, grading, diseases, anesthetics, and fish classification. The text is organized into a table of contents covering water supply, egg biology, spawning, measurement, picking, incubation, shipment, development, troughs, ponds, vegetation, nutrition, grading, weighing, transport, diseases, treatments, chemicals, anesthetics, nets, classification, references, and appendices on conversions and a glossary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water supply.** Watershed characteristics control hatchery water quality. Limestone areas supply calcium and magnesium and raise bicarbonate alkalinity, which buffers acids and alkalis. Sluggish streams, swamps, bogs, springs, and wells may be low in dissolved oxygen and pH and high in free carbon dioxide. Human activities upstream (dairying, grazing, agriculture, logging, mining) increase silting, pollution, and temperature change. The bulletin recommends a moderate water temperature between 45 and 60 °F, and a supply with moderate rainfall, moderate gradient, good cover, a submerged and covered intake, and enclosure against surface contamination. Springs are preferred over wells, streams, or lakes.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Citing [[M. M. Ellis]] et al. (1946), the bulletin reports that 84 percent of good fish faunas occurred where oxygen ranged from 5 to 9 p.p.m., that roughly 3 p.p.m. is near the lethal point at summer temperatures, and that 5 p.p.m. is the lowest safe level for trout, with 7 p.p.m. preferred. Hatchery troughs are to be kept at not less than 7 p.p.m.

**pH and carbon dioxide.** Natural waters typically range from pH 4 to 9. Ellis et al. found normal values of 6.7 to 8.6, and 90 percent of good faunas fell between 6.7 and 8.2. Carbon dioxide at equilibrium is about 2 p.p.m.; values of 1 p.p.m. or less indicate algal photosynthesis.

**Alkalinity and dissolved solids.** California stream bicarbonate alkalinity ranges from about 5 to 200 p.p.m. Total dissolved solids in California surface waters range from 8.1 to 1,766 p.p.m. Among hatcheries, [[Mt. Whitney Hatchery]] measured 57 p.p.m. and [[Fillmore Hatchery]] 1,080 p.p.m.; excessive mineral content at Fillmore was noted to hinder normal egg development while promoting rapid fry growth.

**Toxic metals.** Zinc from galvanized pipe can cause lethal effects. Rainbow trout two to four weeks old were killed at pH 7.6 with 0.04 p.p.m. zinc, and losses among fry followed flow through only 15 feet of 1¼-inch galvanized pipe at 30 gallons per minute. Galvanized material must always be avoided.

**Temperature limits.** Rainbow brood fish must be held at or below 56 °F (preferably 54 °F) for at least six months before spawning. Eggs of rainbow trout and king salmon do not develop normally above 56 °F constant, and incubation below 42 °F causes excessive loss. Mature king salmon held at 34 to 38 °F failed to ripen and died.

**Light.** Trout and salmon eggs are killed by direct sunlight after a few minutes of exposure. Infrared lamps killed all exposed rainbow eggs in trials at [[Mt. Whitney Hatchery]] and [[Mt. Shasta Hatchery]]. Sockeye experiments showed light-exposed eggs hatched earlier, fry were weaker in swimming speed and endurance, and they were smaller with one more vertebra on average.

## Key Findings

**Egg biology and fertilization**
- The trout egg shell is porous and elastic; the micropyle is the single entry point for sperm. Water-hardening absorbs about 20 percent of the egg's initial volume and closes the micropyle, so fertilization is most likely immediately after stripping.
- Whitening of dead eggs is caused by globulin precipitating after the yolk membrane ruptures and salts leak out. Shocking (addling) deliberately ruptures sterile eggs' membranes.
- One drop of sperm may contain enough spermatozoa to fertilize 10,000 eggs. Exposure of eggs or sperm to water for three minutes or more before fertilization results in virtually complete loss of viability.
- Broken eggs release albumen that clogs micropyles; a salt solution of about one ounce of table salt per gallon of water is recommended for soft-shelled or broken-egg lots.

**Spawning**
- Two-man spawning (holder and spawntaker) is the standard in California. Salmon are killed and spawned by incision; bleeding is not practiced at California state hatcheries but is routine at federal hatcheries.
- Overripe ("moon-eyed") eggs are hard and glassy and infertile; underripe eggs also give low fertility. Frequent sorting is considered "one of the greatest secrets" of egg taking.

**Selective breeding**
- The Hot Creek fall-spawning rainbow strain descends from spring-spawning McCloud River rainbow, transferred in 1883 and selected at Neosho, Missouri and Springville, Utah before reaching Hot Creek in 1933.
- Selection over several generations raised two-year-old spawning from 53 to 98 percent, quadrupled egg production of two-year-old females over six generations, and increased one-year-old weight from 5 to 10.2 ounces over five generations.

**Egg measurement and data**
- Table 3 records egg counts: Mt. Whitney domestic rainbow (spring) averaged 427 eggs per ounce at two years and 254 at three years, with egg production rising from 1,553 to 2,210 per female. Eggs size increased about 40 percent and egg number about 42 percent between those ages.
- The California volumetric method, Burrows displacement method, and Von Bayer trough are described; the volumetric method was adopted for its accuracy and simplicity.

**Incubation and development**
- Standard California egg baskets are 24 × 14⅜ × 6 inches; one trough may hold up to five baskets, carrying up to 150 ounces of trout eggs or 300 ounces of salmon eggs.
- Development was tabulated for sea-run steelhead at 51 °F, with eggs becoming critically tender around the seventh day and remaining tender until eyes are pigmented around day 16. Hatching was complete by roughly day 30 in Table 5.
- Temperature units (T.U.) are described as unreliable; the bulletin notes incubation time can vary by six days between lots from different parents.
- A 20-tray vertical-flow incubator needs only 3½ gallons per minute and was tested with about 140,000 rainbow eggs at Mt. Whitney.

**Hatchery troughs and ponds**
- The standard trough is 16 × 1.33 × 0.6 feet with about 64 gallons at a 5-inch outlet, receiving 12 to 15 gallons per minute.
- Trough oxygen demand rises with temperature: a yearling rainbow consumed 3 cc of oxygen per hour at 45 °F and 12 cc at 68 °F.
- Standard earthfill raceways are 100 feet long, 4 feet deep, and hold about 39,270 gallons at 36 inches depth. Circular ponds 14 feet in diameter with 30-inch depth and 50 gallons per minute inflow safely carried 400 pounds of trout at up to 60 °F.

**Aquatic vegetation**
- Vegetation is grouped as algae, emergent (tules, cattails), and submergent plants. Photosynthesis adds oxygen by day but consumes it at night, so overabundant growth can cause fish kills.
- Recommended herbicides include copper sulfate, Karmex W, Delrad 50 and 70, sodium arsenite, Dalapon, Aminotriazole, Estercide 4-D, Chem-Pels 2,4-D and Silvex. Normal tolerances are given for salmonids and centrarchids (for example, copper sulfate 0.1 versus 1.0 p.p.m.).

**Nutrition and feeding**
- Beef liver was historically the main feed but is incomplete; supplements such as ocean fish, fish meal, cereals and dry formulated feeds are used. Carbohydrates should not exceed 9 to 12 percent digestible content; fat should be 5 to 8 percent; protein around 28 percent is considered desirable.
- Of 16 known vitamins, 10 are considered essential to trout; only four have clear external deficiency symptoms.
- Wet and dry feeding schedules are tabulated by body weight and temperature (Tables 12–15). Table 15 estimates that about 7,655 to 10,506 pounds of dry food are needed to raise 25,000 rainbow from swim-up to six per pound, depending on temperature (conversion ratios about 1.7 to 2.3).

**Grading, weighing and transport**
- The Murray-Hume automatic bar grader and Morton adjustable grader are described; a 174,670-fish sample averaging 63.1 per pound was distributed across five size classes.
- Fingerlings are counted by scale; catchables use the scale or the displacement method (1 pound of trout displaces about 1.018 pounds of water, rounded to 1.02).
- Metabolic carbon dioxide below 15 p.p.m. keeps fish in good condition; around 25 p.p.m. causes distress. Sodium amytal at about one-half grain per gallon was reported to roughly double truck carrying capacity.

**Diseases and treatments**
- Major bacterial diseases: columnaris (temperatures above 56 °F), bacterial gill disease (the most common and most costly in California), peduncle disease, fin rot, furunculosis (*Aeromonas salmonicida*), ulcer disease, red-mouth disease, fish tuberculosis, and kidney disease.
- Major protozoans include *Trichodina*, *Epistylis*, *Chilodon*, *Costia* and *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* ("Ich"); treatments are mainly 1:500 acetic acid dips or 1:4,000 formaldehyde baths for one hour.
- Parasites include the copepods *Salmincola edwardsii* and *Lernaea carassii*, the monogenean *Gyrodactylus elegans*, *Hexamitus salmonis*, *Cryptobia borreli*, *Ceratomyxa shasta* (host-specific to rainbow and steelhead), *Sanguinicola davisi* and *Dermocystidium salmonis*.
- Virus diseases described include the kokanee and sockeye virus disease and infectious pancreatic necrosis. Feeding infected salmon viscera and carcasses is repeatedly identified as a source of outbreaks of kidney disease, fish tuberculosis, and sockeye virus disease, so such products should be used with caution.
- Chemicals discussed include copper sulfate, acetic acid, formalin, PMA, calomel, carbarsone, sulfamerazine, malachite green, acriflavine, MS-222 and quinaldine. Chlorine at 200 p.p.m. sterilizes equipment in 60 minutes and must be neutralized with sodium thiosulfate before release.

**Management recommendations**
- Use galvanized-free plumbing, aerate low-oxygen supplies, keep troughs at ≥7 p.p.m. oxygen, and avoid direct sunlight on eggs.
- Feed fish slightly less than they will consume; overfeeding raises conversion costs and can cause losses.
- Remove dead eggs by flush treatment with malachite green or by salt flotation, depending on hatchery layout.
