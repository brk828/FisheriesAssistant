# Divergence Time Estimates for the Early History of Animal Phyla and the Origin of Plants, Animals and Fungi

#ichthyology #marine-biology #molecular-clock

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Daniel Y.-C. Wang]], [[Sudhir Kumar]] and [[S. Blair Hedges]] (Pennsylvania State University) uses molecular clocks based on a larger set of genes than earlier work to estimate when major animal lineages diverged. Earlier molecular estimates for splits among animal phyla ranged from roughly 1200 to 670 million years ago (Ma), which left the timing of the [[Cambrian Explosion]] unresolved. The authors analysed 75 nuclear genes and estimated the chordate–arthropod divergence at 993 ± 46 Ma. They also report a basal position for nematodes with strong support and a Precambrian origin for at least six animal phyla, more than 400 million years before their first appearance in the fossil record. The document is a research article, so the findings below are the study's results and conclusions.

## Background & Description

Most animal phyla appear in the fossil record within a short interval, the Cambrian Explosion, about 530 Ma. If phylogenetic divergences among phyla occurred at the same time, this would represent an unparalleled burst of organismal change. If the phyla diverged much earlier, rapid rates of morphological change would not be needed. Molecular clocks can test these alternatives, but earlier estimates varied widely because of differences in:

- **Calibration approach:** some studies averaged many calibration points, while others used a few well-supported ones (e.g., [[Gu 1998]]).
- **Molecular markers:** [[18S Ribosomal RNA]] sequences, protein sequences, mitochondrial genes, or nuclear genes.
- **Number of genes:** the coefficient of variation of divergence times among genes was large (25–35%), so many independent genes were considered necessary (Kumar & Hedges 1998).

Most earlier molecular phylogenies relied on a single gene, usually 18S rRNA. This study instead sampled many genes to examine relationships among three animal phyla (arthropods, nematodes and chordates) and three kingdoms (plants, animals and fungi).

## Key Findings

**Materials and methods**
- The authors searched GenBank for protein sequences that allowed divergence times to be estimated among two or more animal phyla, or among plants, animals and fungi. Sequences shorter than 100 amino acids were removed. Sequences from at least one mammal and one bird were required.
- 75 nuclear genes met the criteria. Sequences were aligned with ClustalV, and among-site rate variation was modelled with a gamma distribution using gene-specific shape parameters.
- Calibration used the bird–mammal divergence (310 Ma) and the earliest fossil amniote and synapsid records, with the primate–rodent divergence (90 Ma) used for genes lacking a bird sequence.

**Results**
- Across all 75 genes, the average number of aligned residues per gene was 658.
- Divergence time estimates (in Ma, mean ± s.e.m.) from the multigene analysis, with results by gene subset and correction method:
  - Chordate–arthropod: 993 ± 46 (50 genes, the value reported in the abstract)
  - Chordate–nematode: 1113 ± 68 (approximately)
  - Arthropod–nematode: 1177 ± 79
  - Animal–fungi: 1537 ± 72 (approximately)
  - Animal–plant: 1547 ± 89 (approximately)
  - Fungi–plant: 1567 ± 97
- The three-way split of plants, animals and fungi was estimated at 1576 ± 88 Ma.
- Nematodes are basal, and the phylogenetic support for this placement is strong (p > 99%). The authors state it is not the result of rate biases.
- Basal animal phyla (Porifera, Cnidaria, Ctenophora) are inferred to have diverged about 1200–1500 Ma.
- The results suggest at least six animal phyla originated deep in the Precambrian, more than 400 million years before their first fossil appearance.

**Methodological caveats**
- Rate differences among lineages were tested, and some gene comparisons with significant rate heterogeneity were excluded. Outlying divergence values (the highest and lowest) were removed to reduce outlier influence.
- Clustering of chordates and arthropods was not fully resolved in all analyses. Some gene-level trees supported a different topology, and the authors note that a stable topology could not be established for all relationships.

**Conclusions**
- Molecular evidence places the origin of most animal phyla in the Precambrian, which challenges the idea that the Cambrian Explosion marks their origin.
- The study presents a more robust set of divergence estimates by combining many genes, several calibration points and multiple tests of rate heterogeneity.
