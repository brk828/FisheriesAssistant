# West Turkey Creek Native Fish Habitat Renovation Project: Biological Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Biological Assessment, prepared by Gary L. Helbing of the Douglas Ranger District Wildlife Staff (dated December 9, 1998), evaluates a proposal by the [[Coronado National Forest]] to manage the West Turkey Creek watershed as a native fishery. The project would remove all nonnative fishes using the piscicide [[Antimycin]], detoxify it with [[Potassium Permanganate]], and restock with [[Yaqui Chub]] and [[Longfin Dace]]. The assessment concludes that the project MAY ADVERSELY AFFECT the endangered Yaqui chub, because the piscicide and electrofishing would also kill native fish.

## Background & Description

**Proposed action.** Following a November 6, 1998 meeting involving the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]], [[Arizona State University]], Forest Service fishery staff, and the owner of [[El Coronado Ranch]], the Douglas Ranger District proposed to manage West Turkey Creek as a native fishery. The desired condition is a drainage containing only native fishes, including West Turkey Creek, perennial tributaries, and stock ponds on National Forest System lands. The project extends from the Chiricahua Wilderness Boundary to the National Forest boundary, and the planned actions are:

- Survey all waters for native and nonnative fishes.
- Hold native fishes in an off-site facility.
- Apply antimycin at least twice, with additional treatments as needed, then detoxify with potassium permanganate.
- Survey treated waters and retreat as necessary.
- Restock with Yaqui chub and longfin dace once nonnative fishes are gone, and maintain surveys to catch reinvasion.

**Scope and rationale.** The project supports Endangered Species Act responsibilities, the Coronado National Forest Land and Resource Management Plan, and the [[Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan]]. The planned life of the plan is five years from approval.

**Species evaluated.** The only federally listed species identified was the endangered Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Yaqui chub status.** Listed as endangered in 1984 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service because of habitat desiccation, cattle damage, and the threat of nonnative species. Critical habitat included aquatic habitats on [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]. A 1998 [[Habitat Conservation Plan]] for El Coronado Ranch allows incidental take linked to water facility operations.

**Ecology.** The chub is a small minnow that lives in deep pools, seeks cover under undercut banks and debris, and feeds on algae, insects, and detritus. It matures rapidly, spawns through the warmer months, and rebuilds populations quickly from few adults. Decline began with late-1800s arroyo cutting and cienega drainage. The species nearly went extinct by the late 1960s and survived through human intervention.

**West Turkey Creek habitat.** About 4.0 miles of potential chub habitat exist, of which about 2.5 miles lie on National Forest land. The chub was collected there in 1896 but disappeared early in the 20th century. Chubs from Dexter National Fish Hatchery were transplanted to ponds at El Coronado Ranch in 1986.

**Nonnative species and threats.** Stock ponds hold western mosquitofish, green sunfish, bluegill, largemouth bass, yellow bullhead, and common carp. Fathead minnow appeared in 1997, likely from a bait-bucket transfer, and it can prey on or compete with native fishes. Rainbow trout and green sunfish were earlier documented as competitors.

**Disturbance and land use.** The 1994 Rattlesnake Fire sent significant ash and debris into the creek, mainly affecting lower reaches via Saulsbury and Ward canyons. Forest Road 41 parallels the creek to a wilderness trailhead; there are 14 recreational summer homes and two semi-developed campgrounds. The Turkey Creek Grazing Allotment, permitted to El Coronado Ranch, allows 66 cow/calf pairs yearlong plus 25 cows from September to December, with no grazing in chub habitat during summer. Use was reduced in 1997 (non-use due to drought) and 1998 (25% of permitted use).

## Key Findings

- **Survey results (1996–1998):** Electrofishing in the upper West Turkey Creek found Yaqui chub in low numbers (2 to 19 individuals), with reproduction confirmed by fry and young-of-year. Rainbow trout and longfin dace were found in 1996. Fathead minnow dominated the nonnative catch in 1997, with no trout or dace. No exotics or longfin dace were found in 1998. Surveys were limited to pool habitat in the upper 1.0 mile.
- **Rucker Canyon:** Brook and rainbow trout, green sunfish, fathead minnow, Mexican stoneroller, and longfin dace were found before the 1994 fire. Brook trout were not found afterward, and the other nonnatives persist in stock ponds on Forest land.
- **Treatment rationale:** Small fish can hide in substrate crevices, so electrofishing, netting, angling, traps, and explosives cannot achieve complete removal. Multiple antimycin treatments are considered necessary.
- **Antimycin:** Described as approved by the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]], biodegradable in flowing water, and not affecting plants or terrestrial animals. The document reports no long-term effect on aquatic insects in treated streams. A Grant County review in New Mexico found no public health threat when label instructions are followed. The document also notes that antimycin is non-discriminatory and lethal to all gill-breathing animals, so native fish would be killed too.
- **Alternatives:** Rotenone, chlorine, and sodium cyanide were considered less suitable for small waters.
- **Effects determination:** Electrofishing may injure or kill Yaqui chub, and capture or transport may harm them. The piscicide would eliminate native chub and longfin dace not captured earlier. The assessment therefore concludes the project **MAY ADVERSELY AFFECT** the Yaqui chub or its habitat, though renovation is considered necessary for the species' long-term survival.
- **Cumulative effects:** Ongoing consultations include grazing on the Coronado and the proposed Johnson Peak Fire Management Plan. Illegal "bait bucket" transfers of exotic fishes and amphibians are expected to continue, requiring periodic monitoring and retreatment.
- **Recovery criteria:** Self-sustaining populations in West Turkey Creek, under a formal agreement, are part of the criteria for downlisting the Yaqui chub.
