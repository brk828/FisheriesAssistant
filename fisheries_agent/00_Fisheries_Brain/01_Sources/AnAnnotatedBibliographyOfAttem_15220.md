# An Annotated Bibliography of Attempts to Rear the Larvae of Marine Fishes in the Laboratory

#ichthyology #marine-biology #larval-rearing #aquaculture #bibliography

## Executive Summary

This 1971 NOAA technical report by [[Robert C. May]] compiles an annotated bibliography of scientific papers describing attempts to rear marine fish larvae in laboratory settings from 1878 through 1969. The work was prepared to support researchers at the [[Bureau of Commercial Fisheries]] (later [[National Marine Fisheries Service]]) [[Fishery-Oceanography Center]] in [[La Jolla]], where extensive larval fish research programs were developing. The bibliography includes 91 years of rearing attempts across multiple fish families, with detailed annotations summarizing methods, foods used, survival rates, and maximum times larvae were kept alive. Two appendices catalog the species studied (organized by family) and the types of food employed in rearing attempts.

## Background & Description

The document addresses a critical challenge in marine fisheries science: successfully rearing marine fish larvae under controlled laboratory conditions. The literature on this subject was widely scattered and often inaccessible, creating barriers for researchers. [[May]] conducted an extended literature search to compile all available papers describing larval rearing attempts through 1969.

The bibliography covers work on species from 29 fish families, including [[Clupeidae]] (herrings), [[Pleuronectidae]] (righteye flounders), [[Gadidae]] (codfishes), [[Scorpaenidae]] (rockfishes), [[Atherinidae]] (silversides), and many others. The geographic scope is global, with studies from European, North American, and Japanese laboratories.

Each annotation follows a standardized format documenting: fish studied (with current scientific nomenclature), foods offered (with notation of whether larvae accepted them), container types and volumes, water temperature, maximum time larvae survived, maximum length reached, survival percentages, and relevant remarks. This systematic approach enables direct comparison across studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The "critical period" in larval fish development—the transition from yolk-sac nutrition to exogenous feeding—emerges as a major survival bottleneck across studies. Many papers report complete mortality during this phase, highlighting the difficulty of providing appropriate first foods.

Temperature control proved essential for successful rearing. Studies document temperature ranges from 4.9°C to 31°C depending on species, with several authors attributing larval mortality to inadequate temperature regulation.

Container design evolved over the study period. Early work used simple glass jars with water changes; later studies employed larger volumes (up to 19,000 liters in [[Schumann]]'s work), circulating water systems, and specialized designs like plunger jars and rotating disc systems to maintain water quality and food distribution.

The food types tested reflect progressive understanding of larval nutritional requirements. Wild plankton was most commonly used, followed by [[Artemia]] nauplii. Phytoplankton cultures ([[Chlamydomonas]], [[Dunaliella]], [[Skeletonema]], [[Gymnodinium]]) were tested extensively but rarely supported survival beyond starved controls. Successful rearing often required progression through food sizes: small copepod nauplii or mollusk veligers for first-feeding larvae, then larger [[Artemia]] nauplii, then adult copepods or other prey for advanced larvae.

## Key Findings

**Historical progression**: The earliest successful rearing ([[Meyer]], 1878) kept [[Clupea harengus]] (herring) larvae alive for 5 months using wild plankton, reaching 65-70 mm length. This established that laboratory rearing was possible but extremely challenging.

**Most successful approaches**: Several studies achieved rearing through metamorphosis with high survival:
- [[Fluchter]] (1965): 80% of [[Solea solea]] (sole) larvae metamorphosed using [[Artemia]] nauplii
- [[Molander and Molander-Swedmark]] (1957): 18.6-64.2% of [[Pleuronectes platessa]] (plaice) metamorphosed on [[Artemia]]
- [[Shelbourne]] (1964): Maximum 66% of plaice eggs reached metamorphosis
- [[Fabre-Domergue and Bietrix]] (1905): Reared sole to 55-76 mm over 8 months using [[Dunaliella]] and larval fishes as prey

**Food selection patterns**: 
- [[Artemia]] nauplii were the most reliable food, used successfully in 47 studies
- Wild plankton supported survival in 31 studies but with variable results
- Phytoplankton alone rarely extended survival beyond starved controls
- Mollusk larvae (particularly [[Mytilus]] and [[Ostrea]]) proved effective first foods in several studies
- Copepod nauplii, especially [[Balanus]] and [[Tigriopus]], supported good survival when available

**Species-specific results**:
- Herring ([[Clupea harengus]]): [[Blaxter]] (1968) achieved 182 days survival using wild plankton plus [[Balanus]] nauplii, with 1-4% surviving through metamorphosis
- Anchovy ([[Engraulis mordax]]): [[Lasker et al.]] (1970) achieved 47% survival at 19 days using [[Gymnodinium splendens]] plus mollusk veligers
- Rockfishes (Sebastes spp.): Multiple Japanese studies successfully reared larvae on [[Artemia]] nauplii
- Sparids: [[Kasahara et al.]] (1960) achieved 13% survival at 52 days for [[Mylio macrocephalus]] using progression from protozoans to copepods to [[Artemia]]

**Critical factors identified**:
- Food particle size must match larval mouth gape and feeding capability
- Food density must be maintained at appropriate concentrations (often 50× natural levels per [[Nikitinskaya]], 1958)
- Water quality maintenance through circulation or frequent renewal is essential
- Container size matters: larger volumes (>100 liters) generally improved survival
- Temperature stability within species-appropriate ranges is critical

**Appendix I** documents 68 species from 29 families attempted in laboratory rearing, with [[Clupeidae]] (herrings) being the most studied family (9 species, 23 studies).

**Appendix II** catalogs foods across taxonomic groups: 38 phytoplankton species/types, 24 zooplankton groups, and 11 miscellaneous prepared foods were tested. The systematic organization reveals that successful foods were predominantly live zooplankton of appropriate size.

**Methodological insights**: The standardized annotation format reveals common problems: many studies report 0% survival, maximum times of only days to weeks, and failure to pass the critical period. Successful studies typically combined multiple food types in sequence, used larger containers with water circulation, and maintained stable temperatures.
