# Distribution, Biology, and Conservation of the Rare Black Bass Species

#ichthyology #Centrarchidae #black_bass #conservation_biology

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**

Four black bass species ([[Micropterus]]) have relatively restricted ranges and specialized habitat requirements:

- **[[Guadalupe Bass]] (M. treculi)**: Endemic to Central Texas, occurring in portions of the Brazos, Colorado, Guadalupe, and San Antonio river systems; introduced to Nueces River system. Designated State Fish of Texas in 1989. Average size 0.5-1 kg; world record 1.67 kg. Spawns in quiet, shallow areas near moving water; young occupy progressively swifter, deeper waters as they grow. Annual thermal range 4-35°C. Not reported: specific age at maturity, fecundity values, or growth parameters.

- **[[Redeye Bass]] (M. coosae)**: Endemic to Mobile basin (Alabama, Georgia, Tennessee, North Carolina, South Carolina), headwaters of Savannah River, and Chattahoochee River of Apalachicola system. Temperature preference intermediate between trout and other black bass. Not reported: specific life-history parameters.

- **[[Shoal Bass]] (M. cataractae)**: Endemic to Apalachicola River basin; naturally sympatric with redeye bass in upper Chattahoochee River without hybridization. Considerably larger than redeye bass; world record 3.97 kg. Described scientifically only in 1999 by Williams and Burgess. Morphologically distinguished by greater lateral line scale counts, absence of tongue tooth patch (vs. spotted bass), and dark vertical bars. Not reported: specific age, maturity, or fecundity data.

- **[[Suwannee Bass]] (M. notius)**: Smallest distribution of all black basses; likely a relict species from late Oligocene. Considered most primitive Micropterus by Bailey and Hubbs (1949). Relatively small (maximum ~400 mm); world record 1.76 kg. Low growth rate; exhibits general robustness. Not reported: quantitative life-history parameters.

**Habitat Associations**

[[Guadalupe Bass]]: Adapted for small stream environments; prefers flowing waters 2-10 m wide with large rocks, cypress roots, stumps. Overwinters in deep pools with currents. Does not flourish in typical Hill Country reservoirs or downstream of hypolimnion releases. Exhibits allotopic distribution with [[Largemouth Bass]] within Central Texas streams—absent from thermally stable, headspring-influenced locations where largemouth bass predominate.

[[Redeye Bass]]: Inhabits small, cool headwater streams; characteristically prefers consistently cooler temperatures. In Coosa drainage, streams typically <12 m wide with gradient of 4-7 m/km. Moving water preferred to pooled water. Rarely found in large rivers and reservoirs; possible competitive exclusion by spotted bass and largemouth bass in impoundments.

[[Shoal Bass]]: Prefers moving waters associated with rocks and shoals. In [[Chipola River]], inhabits fast-flowing shoal areas and deep pools with rock and sand bottoms; water temperature rarely exceeds 24°C due to spring flow. Larger, fast-flowing waters; eliminated from most of Chattahoochee River in Alabama due to impoundments.

[[Suwannee Bass]]: Most ecologically restricted Micropterus. Adapted for flowing water of spring outflows over rocky shoals and scoured limestone. Occurs only incidentally throughout most of Suwannee system; not common except in lower Santa Fe and Ichetucknee rivers (high water quality, stable conditions). Acidity of swamp areas limits upstream migration; more frequent as pH increases downstream. Occupies swifter stream reaches while largemouth bass prefers lentic waters in sympatric areas.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**

[[Guadalupe Bass]]: Young occupy gradually swifter and deeper waters as they grow. Spawning occurs in quiet, shallow areas near source of moving water; adults overwinter in deep pools with currents. Not reported: specific migration distances or seasonal movement patterns.

[[Suwannee Bass]]: Upstream migration limited by acidity of swamp areas in Suwannee River system. Not reported: detailed movement or home range data for any species.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**

[[Guadalupe Bass]]: Not reported in detail.

[[Redeye Bass]]: Preferred food items are crayfish and fish. Competition with [[Spotted Bass]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] may cause exclusion from reservoirs.

[[Shoal Bass]]: Preferred food items are crayfish and fish. Naturally sympatric with redeye bass in upper Chattahoochee River system without evidence of hybridization.

[[Suwannee Bass]]: Preferred food is crayfish for all stream locations studied, followed by other crustaceans or fish. Relative paucity possibly associated with lack of crayfish abundance where water quality unsuitable for crayfish. Sympatric with largemouth bass through most of range but occupies different microhabitats (swift vs. lentic).

**Environmental Drivers**

[[Guadalupe Bass]]: Stream flow declines due to human cultural activities and population growth represent ongoing threat. Does not flourish in reservoirs or below dams with hypolimnion releases. Annual thermal fluctuations 4-35°C tolerated.

[[Redeye Bass]]: Temperature preference intermediate between trout and other black bass. Stringent habitat requirements limit distribution; prefers consistently cooler temperatures. Increased growth and catch rates observed in two South Carolina reservoirs (1970s-early 1980s), but species rarely inhabits impoundments in Alabama systems.

[[Shoal Bass]]: Water temperature rarely ranges above 24°C in preferred spring-influenced habitats. Habitat reduced by impoundment, canalization, and pollution.

[[Suwannee Bass]]: Commonly associated with waters influenced by springs. pH gradient affects distribution—limited by acidity upstream, more frequent as pH increases downstream.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**

[[Guadalupe Bass]]: Numbers have decreased in recent history; classified as "depleted" in Texas (Hubbs 1976). On Watch List of Texas Organization for Endangered Species (TOES 1988). Listed as "special concern" by Deacon et al. (1979), Williams et al. (1989), and Hubbs et al. (1991). Only natural populations remaining pure are in Gorman Creek (Colorado River drainage) and San Saba, Llano, Pedernales, and Medina rivers (Garrett 1991). Nueces River population and Lost Maples State Park sanctuary population (Sabinal River) provide additional protection outside native range.

[[Redeye Bass]]: Concern over Martins Fork population (Kentucky) due to impoundment limiting fish to upper reaches affected by acidification. Diminished occurrence in streams near Birmingham, Alabama, likely due to habitat disturbances. Interest in angling has increased in northern Alabama streams; new Alabama record established (Choccolocco Creek, 1.42 kg).

[[Shoal Bass]]: Conservation status of special concern in Alabama, Florida, and Georgia. Considered threatened in Florida; likely to become endangered if present population trends continue. No longer inhabits Chattahoochee River along most of its reach due to impoundments. Comprises approximately 10% of catch in Flint River. Species of Special Concern by American Fisheries Society (Williams et al. 1989).

[[Suwannee Bass]]: Has smallest distribution of black basses. Not common except in lower Santa Fe and Ichetucknee rivers. Contribution to lower Santa Fe fishery is small even in areas with larger populations. General rarity makes catch rates low. Recently documented in St. Marks and Wacissa rivers (Florida panhandle); native origin questioned but protected nature of habitats lends credibility.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**

**Habitat Degradation**: Remains greatest threat to all four species. Stream flow declines, reservoir construction, habitat degradation affect all species. Channel dredging, flow interruption due to power generation, flood control, and lock operation threaten shoal bass. Logging activities increase sedimentation and remove canopy shading (redeye bass in Alabama). Urban development and increased water consumption threaten Suwannee bass. Changes in upstream swamp areas affect Suwannee bass even though swamps not directly inhabited.

**Genetic Introgression**: [[Guadalupe Bass]] affected by hybridization with [[Smallmouth Bass]] following intensive stocking program beginning 1974. Electrophoretic examination (1989) showed extensive introgressive hybridization in almost every Guadalupe bass stream system. Only confirmed genetic threat among the four species within native ranges. One specimen "interpreted" as hybrid between redeye bass and Alabama spotted bass (Hubbs and Bailey 1940), but no confirmed hybrids or introgression within redeye bass native range otherwise. Hybridization between redeye bass and smallmouth bass occurred in lower Tennessee and Cumberland rivers where redeye bass invaded after introduction to upper reaches.

**Impoundments**: Reduce available habitat and confer advantage on lacustrine-adapted species. Redeye bass rarely inhabits impoundments in native Alabama systems. Shoal bass eliminated from most of Chattahoochee River in Alabama due to impoundments. Guadalupe bass does not flourish in typical Hill Country reservoirs.

**Introductions**: Interspecific introductions contributing to decline. Recent evidence of multiple genetic stocks of redeye bass increases awareness that intra- and interspecific, nonendemic black bass introductions could harm native recipient stocks. Against Texas Parks and Wildlife policy to stock smallmouth bass within native range of Guadalupe bass, but hybrids remain problematic.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**

[[Guadalupe Bass]] Management:
- Since 1992, Texas Parks and Wildlife evaluating stocking program introducing pure Guadalupe bass into genetically contaminated stream systems to numerically and reproductively overwhelm hybrid swarm
- [[Johnson Creek]] (25 km tributary of Guadalupe River) stocked annually with average of almost 50,000 pure Guadalupe bass fingerlings for nine years (as of 2002)
- Electrophoretic results show reduction in hybrids and possible positive effect in main stem as pure Guadalupe bass "leak" into Guadalupe River
- Total eradication of smallmouth bass genome improbable in contaminated systems, but genetic restoration considered successful if smallmouth bass genetic influence reduced to low levels (1% of population; Allendorf and Leary 1988)
- Pure populations must be protected from hybridization with introduced smallmouth bass to preserve intact gene pools

General Conservation Priorities:
- Protect habitat on which species depend—each is habitat specialist allowing sympatric existence through unique adaptations
- Shield species from further losses by protecting habitat integrity
- Recognize intrinsic value as integral part of habitat diversity
- Maintain extrinsic value as sport fish providing recreation

Research Needs:
- Much to learn about evolutionary relationships with other black basses
- Unique competitive adaptations require study
- Value as indicators of environmental integrity should be investigated
- Paucity of peer-reviewed literature since 1986; most information in state agency reports

**Biological Reference Points**

Not reported: No specific harvest regulations, population targets, or quantitative management benchmarks provided in document.

Size Records:
- Guadalupe bass: Texas and world record 1.67 kg; average size 0.5-1 kg
- Redeye bass: Alabama record 1.42 kg (Choccolocco Creek)
- Shoal bass: World angling record 3.97 kg
- Suwannee bass: World angling record 1.76 kg; large specimens ~400 mm

Stocking Rates (Guadalupe bass restoration):
- Johnson Creek: ~50,000 pure Guadalupe bass fingerlings annually
- 1973 Nueces River introduction: ~2,000 individuals from Guadalupe River population
