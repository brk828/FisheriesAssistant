# The Parasitic Copepod Ergasilus sieboldi Nordmann, New to Britain

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ergasilidae #parasitology #freshwater

## Executive Summary

This report documents the first British record of the parasitic copepod *[[Ergasilus sieboldi]]* Nordmann, discovered in July 1967 on [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) in [[Howbrook Reservoir]], Yorkshire. Previously common on freshwater fishes throughout continental Europe but absent from Britain, this gill parasite was found infesting recently introduced trout that died approximately six weeks post-stocking. The discovery confirms predictions made by Gurney (1933) that this species would eventually be detected in or introduced to British waters. Heavy infestations can cause fish mortality through gill tissue destruction, blood withdrawal, and secondary infections.

## Background & Description

*[[Ergasilus sieboldi]]* is a parasitic copepod belonging to the family [[Ergasilidae]]. Adult females measure 1-2 mm in length and morphologically resemble cyclopoid copepods with specialized adaptations for parasitism. The antennae are modified for grasping gill filaments, featuring a complex chitinous skeleton basally that supports an elaborate muscular system and permits segmental articulation. Minute protuberances on the penultimate and ante-penultimate antennal segments enhance gripping efficiency.

The mouthparts are specialized for lacerating gill tissues. Females attach to gill filaments with heads directed toward the filament base, ventral surface opposed to the host epithelium, bringing the mouth against host tissue. Mandibles are sometimes visible in lateral view. Only females attach parasitically; they produce Cyclops-like egg sacs. Males are free-swimming, lack parasitic adaptations, and survive for much shorter periods than females, making them rarely observed.

The fifth pair of legs arises from a minute pedicel (possibly representing a reduced basal segment) rather than directly from the fifth somite. Careful examination with oil immersion reveals a delicate basal seta in addition to two terminal setae, resolving a taxonomic discrepancy between Markewitsch (1931) and Gurney (1933).

Larval development follows the basic pattern of cyclopoid copepods. Recent research by Einszporn (1965) has clarified feeding habits, though the complete mechanism of food collection remains incompletely understood due to observational difficulties with living specimens in situ.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

*[[Ergasilus sieboldi]]* is widely distributed on freshwater fishes throughout continental Europe, parasitizing multiple host species. The parasite is confined exclusively to gill filaments. Maximum reproduction occurs during the warmest part of the year, facilitating rapid population buildup under favorable conditions.

The [[Howbrook Reservoir]] (National Grid Reference MR 330976) lies in the [[Don drainage area]] between Sheffield and Barnsley, Yorkshire. The infected [[Brown Trout]] were part of a batch introduced approximately six weeks before the July 1967 discovery, presumably arriving free from infection. The rapid infestation buildup suggests a substantial parasite population already established in the reservoir, likely on other fish species.

Heavy infestations pose serious threats in fish ponds and heavily stocked waters where reinfection probability is high. Schaperclaus (1954) demonstrated an inverse relationship between [[Tench]] (*Tinca tinca*) condition factor and *E. sieboldi* burden. The parasite causes mortality through:
- Gill tissue destruction
- Blood withdrawal, particularly leucocytes migrating to damage sites
- Secondary bacterial and fungal infections of inflicted wounds

## Key Findings

**First British Record**: R.G. Templeton of the Yorkshire Ouse and Hull River Authority discovered 12 dead [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) in [[Howbrook Reservoir]] in July 1967, with gills heavily infested by *[[Ergasilus sieboldi]]*, representing the first confirmed British occurrence of this species.

**Infestation Intensity**: One examined trout (6.5 inches/~16.5 cm length) carried over 130 parasites on six incomplete gill sets, with total infestation estimated at 200 parasites—a considerable burden for such a small fish. Multiple parasites (sometimes two) were observed on single gill filaments.

**Rapid Population Buildup**: The infected trout were introduced approximately six weeks before discovery, presumably parasite-free, indicating rapid infestation during the warmest part of the year when reproduction peaks. This suggests a large established parasite population in the reservoir.

**Morphological Clarification**: Detailed examination confirmed the presence of a delicate basal seta on the fifth leg in addition to two terminal setae, resolving previous taxonomic uncertainty. The seta arises from the minute basal pedicel but is delicate and may be lost during handling.

**Pathological Significance**: Literature review indicates severe infestations can be lethal. Schaperclaus (1954) reported a 36 cm [[Tench]] carrying approximately 3,100 parasites, and Neuhaus found 3,000 specimens on a 25 cm Tench. Even sub-lethal infestations significantly affect host condition.

**Management Implications**: The species represents a highly undesirable inhabitant of fish ponds and stocked waters. The discovery fulfills Gurney's 1933 prediction of eventual British occurrence through either undetected presence or future introduction. Other fish species in [[Howbrook Reservoir]] are presumably also infected, though this remains unconfirmed.
