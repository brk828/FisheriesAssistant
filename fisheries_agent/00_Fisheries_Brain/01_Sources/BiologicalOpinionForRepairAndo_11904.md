# Biological Opinion: Aravaipa Creek Road Repair and Replacement

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #threatened-species #ESA-consultation

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal biological opinion issued by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] on February 15, 1995, regarding the repair and replacement of segments of the [[Aravaipa Creek]] road in [[Pinal County]], Arizona. The consultation addresses potential impacts to two threatened fish species: [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). The [[Federal Emergency Management Agency]] (FEMA) proposed to reroute approximately 1.1 miles of road that was damaged during January-February 1993 flooding, moving it uphill from the creek to prevent future washouts. The Service concluded that the proposed action is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of either species or adversely modify their designated critical habitat. The new alignment is expected to have long-term net beneficial effects by removing recurring impacts from road washouts, repairs, and temporary low-water crossings.

## Background & Description

[[Spikedace]] was listed as threatened on July 1, 1986, with critical habitat designated March 8, 1994, including approximately 15 miles of [[Aravaipa Creek]]. It is a small silvery fish with a well-developed dorsal fin spine, historically occurring throughout mid-elevations of the [[Gila River]] drainage but now restricted to five locations including [[Aravaipa Creek]], upper [[Gila River]], middle [[Gila River]], [[Eagle Creek]], and [[Verde River]]. The species inhabits flowing water with slow to moderate velocities over sand, gravel, and cobble substrate, particularly in shear zones, sheet flow areas, and eddies. Spawning occurs March through May over gravel and cobble substrates. Life span is approximately two years with reproduction primarily in one-year-old fish. The species feeds on aquatic and terrestrial insects.

[[Loach Minnow]] was listed as threatened on October 28, 1986, with critical habitat designated March 8, 1994, covering the same 15-mile segment of [[Aravaipa Creek]]. It is a small, slender, elongate fish with markedly upwardly-directed eyes. Historic range included the [[Verde River]], [[Salt River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[San Francisco River]], and [[Gila River]] basins, but the range has been reduced by approximately 85 percent. The species is a bottom-dwelling inhabitant of shallow, swift water over gravel, cobble, and rubble substrates, using interstitial spaces for resting and spawning. Life span is approximately 2 years. Spawning occurs primarily March through May, with some autumn spawning documented. Males may guard nests during incubation. The species feeds exclusively on aquatic insects.

[[Aravaipa Creek]] is a perennial tributary of the [[San Pedro River]] with approximately 10 cubic feet per second (cfs) base flow and median flow of 16 cfs. The creek supports a relatively intact native fish community including [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Spikedace]], and [[Loach Minnow]]. Nonnative species present include [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and occasionally [[Largemouth Bass]]. The two-year flood event is estimated at 3,790 cfs and the 50-year flood at 22,100 cfs; the January-February 1993 flooding peaked at approximately 13,000 cfs.

Recent taxonomic and genetic work indicates substantial morphological and genetic differences between remnant populations of both species. [[Spikedace]] from [[Aravaipa Creek]] is morphologically distinguishable from [[Verde River]] populations, with [[Gila River]] and [[Eagle Creek]] populations showing intermediate characteristics. Mitochondrial DNA and allozyme analyses show similar geographic variation patterns. For [[Loach Minnow]], biochemical genetic work recommends managing genetically distinctive units as separate populations to preserve existing genetic variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Aravaipa Creek]] watershed encompasses approximately 537 square miles, with perennial flow confined to a 15-20 mile segment within [[Aravaipa Canyon]]. The stream has a relatively low gradient (less than 1 percent) with substrate primarily gravel-cobble, some bedrock in the canyon center, and increasing sand and fine sediment below the canyon. Habitat consists predominantly of riffles and runs with pools formed by bedrock, canyon walls, and large woody material. The canyon bottom is narrow with steep side slopes (30 to 60 degrees).

The watershed has been subjected to substantial human uses including livestock grazing, mining, timber harvest, water development, irrigated agriculture, roads, and recreation. These activities have altered runoff, sediment transport, and groundwater recharge patterns. Historical accounts indicate the creek was more extensive, with a marshy perennial area near Klondyke that is now a dry incised channel. Riparian forests were historically more massive with larger younger tree components. The average perennial length has decreased, though with substantial yearly variation.

Human activities along the creek have resulted in channel alterations including diversion dams, channelization, rip rap, earthen dikes, and other channel controls. Mining tailings from a lead mill built at Klondyke in 1925 are located on the creek bank and are being eroded into the creek, with documented surface and groundwater quality violations.

Nonnative fish species are increasing in number and distribution. In 1981, four nonnative species were known from the watershed with only two recorded from [[Aravaipa Creek]] itself. By 1992, eight nonnative species were known from the creek with at least four reproducing. [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], and [[Black Bullhead]] are predatory species that consume [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) is considered highly detrimental through competition and/or predation, though it invaded the entire perennial length in 1990-91 but did not establish a population.

Four previous formal section 7 consultations have occurred concerning [[Aravaipa Creek]], all addressing effects to [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. These included the 1990 [[BLM]] Safford District Resource Management Plan (nonjeopardy finding), two 1993-1994 emergency consultations for rip rap placement (nonjeopardy findings), and a 1994 biological opinion on the [[Central Arizona Project]] finding jeopardy and adverse modification of critical habitat from potential introduction and spread of nonnative aquatic species.

## Key Findings

**Project Description and Rationale:** The proposed action involves rerouting approximately 1.1 miles (9,000 feet) of [[Aravaipa Creek]] road damaged during January-February 1993 flooding. Two sites were damaged: Site 1 (250 feet completely removed by erosion in section 27) and Site 2 (900 feet damaged by inundation in section 26). The road at Site 1 has washed out repeatedly in 1967, 1978, 1983, and 1993. The new alignment would be placed approximately 600 feet further upland (range 300-700 feet) from the creek on primarily [[BLM]] land with a 28-foot wide roadway and 100-foot wide right-of-way. Current traffic averages 12-14 vehicles per day (corrected from EA estimate).

**Construction Details:** The project requires extensive cut and fill with approximately 19,000 cubic yards of fill material to be obtained from the Clark Pit Landfill in Mammoth, requiring approximately 1,900 round trips. Approximately 12 intermittent washes would be crossed with concrete pipe culverts and gabions installed to prevent erosion. Staging would occur in a 1.5-acre existing cleared area at Brandenburg campsite and potentially in a previously cultivated field. Water for dust control would be trucked from Central Arizona College. Construction equipment would not use the existing damaged road sections or the low-water crossing at Site 1.

**Mitigation Measures:** Approximately 85 saguaro cacti would be removed and potentially transplanted. Catch basins, fine screens, and silt fencing would be installed downhill from construction to capture sediment-transporting runoff. A biologist would be on-site during construction to protect [[Desert Tortoises]] (*Gopherus agassizii*). Construction timing would be coordinated with [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] to reduce impacts to [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]] (*Ovis canadensis*) and nesting raptors. Chain link fencing would cover slopes in areas of unstable rock and soil to minimize material movement.

**Effects Analysis - Adverse Impacts:** The existing road alignment has recurring adverse impacts including machinery use in the stream channel during repairs, sediment input, likely stream diversions, direct mortality of fish, and adverse habitat modification. The periodic washout necessitates temporary low-water crossings that cause direct mortality, sediment generation, loss of riparian vegetation, channel changes, and increased erosion probability. The roadbed at Site 2 is located in what was originally riparian zone, causing erosion of sediment into the creek during rain events and flooding, with dust negatively impacting riparian vegetation and the stream.

Road construction would require extensive excavation and fill, with erosion and sediment from destabilizing soils and rock potentially equaling or exceeding current inputs. Potentially toxic substances (petroleum products) during construction could enter the creek, though staging areas are placed away from the floodplain and sediment catchment devices would be used. [[Loach Minnow]] eggs are particularly susceptible to crushing by equipment during spring or fall spawning seasons due to their attachment to rocks in shallow riffle areas.

**Effects Analysis - Beneficial Impacts:** Relocation of the road uphill would remove adverse impacts from the existing road location, frequent washout and repair cycles, and the temporary low-water crossing. The new alignment would prevent future recurring use of machinery in the stream channel and associated sediment inputs. Abandonment of the temporary low-water crossing at Site 1 would eliminate ongoing impacts, though the emergency section 7 consultation and nationwide section 404 permit would no longer apply to any future private maintenance.

**Old Alignment Management:** Portions of the old alignment would continue to be used by private landowners. The section from Brandenburg campsite to Site 1 would be signed for residents only with a barricade at Site 1 to prevent public use of the low-water crossing, though some private use may occur on private land. Two small portions of old alignment on [[BLM]] lands would revert to [[BLM]] and be allowed to revert to natural state. The consultation assumes [[BLM]] segments upstream from Site 1 would be closed to vehicular traffic; if later opened, additional section 7 consultation may be required.

**Utility Relocation:** The [[San Carlos Irrigation Project]] (Bureau of Indian Affairs) would relocate 30 poles and accompanying lines from the existing 16-foot wide easement to a new 50-foot right-of-way adjacent to the new road alignment after construction completion. The old utility right-of-way would be abandoned and revert to private landowners and [[BLM]].

**Cumulative Effects:** Ongoing watershed activities include livestock grazing and watering, irrigated agriculture (declining), groundwater pumping, stream diversion, bank stabilization, channelization, and recreation (increasing). Members of the Aravaipa Property Owners' Association report increasing non-wilderness recreation use. [[BLM]] is proposing expanded recreation facilities. Potential exists for increased residential or commercial use of private lands along the creek, which would likely result in increased water use, pollution, and streambank alteration through riparian vegetation suppression, bank trampling, and erosion.

**Biological Opinion Conclusion:** Implementation of the proposed repair and/or replacement of the [[Aravaipa Creek]] road and issuance of rights-of-way for the new road and utility alignments is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of [[Spikedace]] or [[Loach Minnow]] and is **not likely to destroy or adversely modify** the critical habitat of either species. The Service anticipates **no incidental take** of either species from the proposed action. Should any take occur, FEMA and/or [[BLM]] must reinitiate formal consultation and operations must be stopped if additional taking would cause irreversible adverse impact.

**Conservation Recommendations:** The Service recommends placing interpretive signs in the road reconstruction area to inform the public of resource protection efforts, addressing the need for road rerouting and benefits to [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], and the broader riparian and native fish communities of [[Aravaipa Creek]]. The Service requested opportunity to review draft sign content prior to completion and posting.

**Status and Threats:** Both [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] have declining status. Although listed as threatened, the Service has found they warrant uplisting to endangered status, with reclassification proposals pending but precluded by higher priority listing actions. The need for reclassification is based on increases in serious threats to a large portion of their habitats rather than documented declines in the species themselves. Habitat destruction and competition/predation from introduced nonnative fish species are the primary causes of species decline.
