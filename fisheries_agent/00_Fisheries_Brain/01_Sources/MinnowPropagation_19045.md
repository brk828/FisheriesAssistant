# Minnow Propagation: Pond Culture and Handling of Bait Fishes in Minnesota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bait-minnows

## Executive Summary

This 1948 Minnesota Department of Conservation bulletin, written by aquatic biologist [[John Dobie]], addresses a shortage of bait minnows caused by postwar fishing pressure. It argues that bait dealers should rear their own fish in [[Natural Ponds]] or [[Artificial Ponds]] rather than rely on heavily fished native streams and lakes. It covers pond site selection, species choice, stocking and fertilization rates, [[Weed Control]], harvest timing, transport and holding practices, identification of legal and illegal bait species, and [[Over-Wintering Fish]]. Production data from 1946 private and state ponds are also presented.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** Minnesota's roughly 10,000 lakes draw many tourists, and tourism is the state's third-largest business. Minnow and sucker sales for bait are an important part of it.
- **Market scale:** About 1,700 retail and 150 wholesale bait dealers operate each year. Average retail sales of about one gallon per week imply roughly 2,890,000 minnows sold to fishermen each week over a 17–18 week season. This figure excludes fish lost during seining and holding.
- **Seasonal problem:** Minnows congregate along shores and in streams during spawning, making them easy to seine in spring, when demand is low. By midsummer, when demand peaks, they have dispersed and are hard to catch in quantity.
- **Geography:** Demand is highest in northern resort areas with deep lakes and cold streams that produce few minnows. Dealers often haul fish hundreds of miles from warm southern lakes, with high losses in hot weather.
- **Regulation:** Where minnow seining threatened game fish, it was prohibited. Net mesh, transport equipment, aeration, and tank load limits were specified. Some shallow lakes were designated [[Minnow Lakes]] managed for bait fish.
- **Author:** Dobie joined the Bureau of Fisheries Research in 1940 after work with the U.S. Forest Service, the Wisconsin Conservation Department, and the Minnesota CCC program.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond requirements (natural ponds):**
- Size of about ½ to 1 acre, since larger ponds are hard to seine completely.
- Depth of at least 5 feet through summer to survive hot weather; about 15 feet to over-winter minnows.
- Little or no aquatic vegetation, which wastes fertility and hampers seining.
- At least one sloping, solid-bottomed shore for seine landings.
- High natural fertility, which lowers fertilization costs.
- Game fish can reduce the minnow population to nothing.

**Artificial ponds** are formed by a dam or dyke along a watercourse. They are more productive and easier to manage, but construction costs can be high. Good sites are natural depressions needing little dyking, with heavy soil and a tight bottom to prevent seepage. Water sources should be gravity-fed, with bypass for floods. Seining basins are either a walled depression about 1 foot deep at the outlet or an 8-foot basin outside the pond.

**Oxygen and temperature:**
- Shallow ponds are prone to oxygen depletion in hot weather, and heavy blooms can cause depletion too.
- Fish cannot adapt to rapid temperature change. A change of more than 10°F can be fatal, sometimes hours later.
- Holding and transport tanks should keep at least 3 ppm dissolved oxygen when full, with 5–6 ppm preferred. Water should be kept at 65°F or lower.
- In winter, [[Over-Wintering Fish]] techniques include keeping ice free of snow to admit light for photosynthesis, and [[Soot Application]] to melt snow and ice. One trial raised dissolved oxygen from 2.4 to over 7 ppm.

**Disease and predators:** Fungus disease spreads in crowded, unclean tanks. Dead fish accelerate it. Ponds heavily infested with aquatic insects were treated with a thin kerosene layer two days before stocking. Mudpuppies, salamanders, and game fish have destroyed minnow populations in some ponds.

## Key Findings

### Species suited to ponds
Over most of Minnesota, [[Golden Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Common Sucker]] are the most practical species.
- **Golden shiners** are good for spring and fall bait. They need aquatic vegetation for egg-laying (filamentous algae recommended) and must be over-wintered in a deep pond.
- **Fatheads** need spawning boards or rubble for spawning. They take about three months to reach 2 inches and may not reach bait size until the second year.
- **Suckers** do not spawn in ponds. They are stocked as eyed eggs or advanced fry from captured spawning runs. They grow fast and are hardy enough for summer bait.

### Stocking rates
- Fatheads: 1,000–5,000 adults per acre.
- Golden shiners: 500–2,000 per acre.
- Suckers: eyed-egg rates are higher than advanced-fry rates, since survival to salable size averages 10% for eyed eggs versus 50% for advanced fry.
- Understocked ponds produce large fish in low numbers. Overstocked ponds produce many small fish. Fertilized ponds can be stocked more heavily.

### Fertilization and feeding
- Most Minnesota dealers prefer fertilization over feeding because it is cheaper and needs no daily work.
- Procedure: apply 500 pounds of barnyard manure per acre at least two weeks before stocking, then maintain the bloom with about 300 pounds of commercial fertilizer per acre per season. The bloom should be thick enough that a hand is not visible at 1 foot depth.
- Natural ponds need lighter fertilization because of oxygen risk. In artificial ponds, adding fresh water relieves distress.
- A cited artificial feed formula: 275 lb cooked cornmeal and oatmeal, 200 lb bone meal, and 400 lb clam meal. Feed should be consumed within two hours, or it pollutes the pond.

### Weed control
Select weed-free sites and fertilize heavily early in the season so the bloom shades rooted plants. Where weeds are established, the herbicide 2-4-D gives the best control.

### Yield and production
- **1946 private ponds:** 53 licensed private hatcheries raised minnows on about 735 acres. Complete reports from 30 hatcheries covering 587 acres showed 8,690,890 minnows sold, averaging 14,805 per acre. Another 4,089,300 remained in ponds at season end, and the true total was higher.
- **Species share of production:** Golden shiners 27.0%, common suckers 20.6%, creek chubs 23.9%, fatheads 18.4%, western mudminnows 6.9%, brassy minnows 2.4%, redbelly dace 0.7%.
- **Sucker survival:** Thirteen hatcheries planted 11,229,820 eggs and harvested 598,240 sucker minnows (5.3%). Excluding six complete failures, survival was 9.6%.
- **State experimental ponds (1946):** 12 former game-fish ponds covering 26.5 acres produced 554,533 minnows, averaging 20,918 per acre. The top pond at Detroit Lakes yielded 180,018 bluntnose minnows per acre.
- **Fertilization effect (St. Paul):** A fertilized 5-acre pond stocked with 70 golden shiners per acre (350 total), treated with 4,650 lb commercial fertilizer and 450 lb dried sheep manure, yielded 421 lb (85.6 lb/acre). An unfertilized 1-acre pond with 200 shiners yielded 245 lb. Females averaged 650 young in both ponds. The fertilized pond produced fish that reached harvest size in 11 weeks versus 16 weeks in the poorer pond.
- **Yield range:** Pond yields ranged from zero to over 180,000 fish per acre, depending on conditions and operator skill.

### Harvest and timing
Crappie bait could be harvested by midsummer. Pike-sized minnows required late-summer harvest. Golden shiners are too fragile to seine in hot weather, so they were taken with a baited cheesecloth drop net in Michigan. Suckers reached bait size quickly but grew too large unless their food was reduced.

### Transport, tempering, and holding
- Slow tempering is the key loss-prevention method. Safe tempering needs at least 20 minutes per 10°F of difference, with a thermometer.
- Minnows hardened for 24 hours in cold water tolerate long hauls better. Missouri dealers sometimes haul about 1,000 miles in tanks insulated with 4 inches of cork and iced along the route.
- Holding tanks should be smooth, filtered, and small enough to sell out in four to six days. Replenish only after a full sale.
- Tanks are cleaned and sterilized with bleach solution (one-half pint of Hilex or Clorox in 15 gallons of water). All tools should soak in hypochlorite for several hours.
- Infected minnows may be dipped in one-eighth ounce of [[Malachite Green]] in 15 gallons of water for ten seconds. The solution loses strength within 24 hours and should not be reused.
- Feed held fish only what they clean up in 15 minutes, using foods such as soybean meal or finely ground oatmeal.
- Selling by gallon injures fish, so [[Minnow Weighing]] is recommended. Weigh fish in a basket of water, then convert using about eight pounds per gallon.

### Economics
In June 1947, pike-sized minnows wholesaled at $5.00 per gallon. At that price, small leased natural ponds were the most practical option. Dobie cautioned that minnow hatcheries could pay fair wages but would not make quick fortunes.

### Undesirable species
- **Illegal to use for bait:** [[Carp]] and [[Goldfish]]. Both can damage native game fish and are hard to distinguish from minnows when small. Goldfish lose color and grow large in new waters.
- **Should not be used:** Quillback and buffalofish (sucker family, can become nuisances), and bowfin (resembles the mudminnow).
- **Usable:** At least 40 minnow species in Minnesota can serve as bait. Common suckers and western mudminnows are acceptable.
- **Rules of thumb:** When in doubt, do not use a fish with more than ten dorsal rays. Never release bait minnows into a lake other than where they were caught. See [[Minnow Identification]].

### Research needs
- Sucker pond failures were common. Causes identified were lack of egg trays, lack of food for fry at hatching, and too many aquatic insects. The Fish Propagation Unit requested reports of failures.
- Growing minnows to bait size within one season remains unsolved. Selective breeding, as used in early European carp farming, was suggested as a path.
- The bulletin was to be revised as answers became available.

### Historical and reference notes
Pond culture is one of the oldest agricultural practices, with origins traced to a fish-pond treatise by the Chinese minister Fanli about five centuries BCE. The bulletin's suggested reading includes 13 works, among them Michigan forage-fish studies, the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service circular *Propagation of Minnows and Other Bait Species*, and Wisconsin and Ohio bait culture bulletins.
