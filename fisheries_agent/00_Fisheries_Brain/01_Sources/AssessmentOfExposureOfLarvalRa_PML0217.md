# Selenium Exposure of Larval Razorback Sucker in Natural Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[D. W. Beyers]] and [[C. Sodergren]] (Larval Fish Laboratory, Colorado State University) assessed whether waterborne and dietary [[Selenium]] from natural nursery-habitat waters harms larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered fish of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. Larvae were exposed for 28 days to five test waters (control plus three river localities and one dilution) using a [[Laboratory Food Chain]] of algae, rotifers and fish. Survival showed no detectable selenium-related effect. Growth was altered in a complex way: site waters increased growth through waterborne exposure, while site-water diets reduced it by about 14%, a loss the authors attribute to cocontaminants rather than selenium. The authors conclude that larvae were not strongly affected at dissolved selenium up to 20.3 µg/L and dietary selenium up to 21.8 µg/g.

## Background & Description

Razorback sucker populations in the Upper Colorado River basin are presumed to have declined due to loss of physical habitat, altered historical temperature regimes, interactions with [[Nonnative Fishes]], and degraded water quality. Selenium concentrations in some nursery habitats were found to exceed the US EPA criterion of 5 µg/L for freshwater aquatic life, and elevated selenium was measured in larval and adult fish in earlier work.

Selenium is a metalloid and essential micronutrient. Organic forms are more bioavailable and generally more toxic than inorganic forms. Maternal transfer of selenium to eggs may reduce survival and recruitment, and larval fish are often sensitive to contaminants, which motivated focusing on this life stage. The study was funded by the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] and the [[National Irrigation Water Quality Program]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Site waters were collected on three occasions (28 April, 10 May and 17 May 1999) from three localities on the Colorado River near Grand Junction:

- **De Beque (DB):** presumed uncontaminated
- **Orchard Mesa (OM):** moderately contaminated
- **North Pond (NP):** highly contaminated
- **North Pond 50% dilution (NPD):** NP water diluted with control water, simulating a 50% reduction in contaminants

The control was tap water treated to approximate "very hard" water and the Colorado River near the Colorado-Utah state line. Measured conditions included a test temperature of 20 ± 1 °C, dissolved oxygen of 6.3–7.5 mg/L, a 12:12 h light:dark photoperiod, and daily renewal of test solutions. Alkalinity, hardness, pH and conductivity rose from control to NP; for example, NP conductivity averaged 3,790 µS/cm against 1,010 µS/cm for DB (values as tabulated in the source).

## Key Findings

**Selenium concentrations**

- Water selenium ranged from below the quantitation limit (control and DB) to 20.3 µg/L (NP).
- Algae ranged from 0.183 to 3.74 µg/g; rotifers from <0.702 to 21.8 µg/g dry weight.
- Fish selenium ranged from 2.34 to 14.4 µg/g on the control diet and from 5.45 to 42.0 µg/g on the site-water diet.
- Whole-body concentrations in all four site waters exceeded the predicted toxic threshold of 4 µg/g.

**Survival**

- Survival ranged from 92.5% to 100%; five of nine treatments had 100% survival, including all NP treatments.
- No consistent relationship with selenium exposure was found, so lower survival elsewhere was judged to be random mortality. No statistical test was applied.

**Growth**

- Waterborne site-water constituents increased final mass relative to controls (p < 0.0001) and total length (p = 0.0081).
- Dietary site-water constituents reduced mass by about 14% (p < 0.0001) and length (p = 0.0012).
- The two effects roughly cancelled, so net growth approximated control levels. Without the control-diet treatments, the study would likely have concluded no growth effect.

**Interpretation**

- The growth reduction at DB, where selenium was below thresholds, implicates a cocontaminant.
- The highest dietary selenium (21.8 µg/g) was below levels reported to cause adverse effects in earlier [[Food Chain]] studies with [[Fathead Minnow]].
- The authors argue that current guidelines may not transfer to this setting, because thresholds are meant for all life stages, and most toxicity data use refined selenium forms rather than naturally cycling selenium.

**Conclusions and management implications**

- Biologically significant effects on larval survival and growth are not expected where selenium is at or below 20.3 µg/L in water and 21.8 µg/g in diet.
- The authors caution that absence of detected effects does not show populations are unaffected; other life stages, such as eggs and adults, and unmeasured factors were not tested.
- Ranking stressors by their effect on razorback sucker is recommended to target management effectively.
