# A Survey of the Mussels (Unionacea) of the Illinois River: a Polluted Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #unionidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Volume 30, Article 5, February 1971) by [[William C. Starrett]] surveys the freshwater mussel (Unionacea) fauna of the entire [[Illinois River]], a heavily polluted, sluggish stream. The 1966 field survey collected 4,247 live mussels from 429 collections and compared them with historical records from 1870–1912. The author concludes that pollution, siltation, reduced current from [[Navigation Dams]], and commercial harvesting have sharply reduced or eliminated many species, while a few pollution-tolerant species, chiefly [[Three-Ridge]], dominate. The study was designed to provide a basis for managing the river's mussel resource, which had become commercially valuable as a source of nuclei for [[Japanese Pearl Culture]].

## Background & Description

**Historical fishery.** The river was once among the most productive mussel streams per mile in the country. Around 1910 more than 2,600 boats fished between Peru and Grafton, and the maximum commercial shell yield (1909) was thousands of tons of button shells. In 1911 one buyer alone bought 600 tons from two landings near Peoria. Button manufacturing declined after World War II as plastics replaced shell.

**Renewed demand.** From about 1962 Japanese pearl culturists sought large, thick shells from Illinois waters as nuclei, since Tennessee River supplies had declined. Illinois mussel-fishing licenses rose from 69 (1961) to 1,279 (1966). The 1966 commercial catch from the Illinois River was 1,118.40 tons (valued at $109,460.83); statewide it was 3,557.65 tons ($604,307.83).

**Physical setting.** The river forms at the confluence of the Kankakee and Des Plaines rivers, is 272.9 miles long (327 miles from Lake Michigan), and drains 32,081 square miles. The 1965–66 discharge at Kingston Mines ranged from 3,990 to 51,700 cfs (mean 16,188 cfs). About 400,000 acres of bottomland were formerly flooded, and roughly 200,000 acres were drained for agriculture.

**Sampling methods.** Three gear types were used: an exploratory [[Crowfoot Bar]] (a 61.25-inch bar with 44 hooks), a [[Dredge (Dip Net)]] (14.75 inches wide), and hand wading. Shells were measured, weighed and aged by counting annuli. Standing crop was estimated from dredge collections (e.g., 1,480 pounds live weight per acre at river mile 1.0).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Navigation pools.** Locks and dams built in the 1930s (Dresden Island, Marseilles, Starved Rock, Peoria, La Grange) created navigation pools (Alton, La Grange, Peoria, Starved Rock, Marseilles, Dresden). Current fell from historic rates of 1.4–1.7 mph to about 0.6 mph at ordinary stages.

**Substrate and turbidity.** Most bottoms in the lower three pools were hard or soft mud with fine silt ("false bottom"). Towboat traffic resuspends silt; turbidity in the Alton pool ranged 36–320 Jackson units during minimum flow, and reached 2,000 units after a flash flood (June 24, 1964). Row-crop expansion (about 47% of the Illinois watershed in row crops in 1964) increased sedimentation. In Lake Chautauqua, sediment reduced storage capacity by 18.3% in 23.8 years.

**Pollution.** The river was "the Ruhr Valley of the Midwest." Population equivalents of wastes dropped from 6,211,471 (1922) to 2,417,000 (1960). Ammonia nitrogen reached 8.2 ppm in the upper river and 26.0 ppm in the Des Plaines River (1967). Total phosphates reached 8.3 ppm in the Marseilles pool; Peoria Lake total phosphorus averaged 1.13 ppm. Dissolved oxygen remained critical in part of the Peoria pool. Organochlorine pesticides were tested in mussel tissue by [[W. N. Bruce]].

**Host fishes.** Mussel larvae (glochidia) depend on fish hosts. Examples include crappies for Three-Ridge and Pig-Toe, channel catfish for Pimple-Back, and freshwater drum for Fawn's Foot. The author notes 101 fish species were collected from the river and bottomland lakes since 1950.

## Key Findings

- **Species list:** The historical fauna (1870–1912 and 1966–1969 records) comprises about 50 unionid taxa in Table 5, with several doubtful records excluded (e.g., *Margaritifera margaritifera*, *Fusconaia subrotunda*, *Lastena lata*, *Obovaria retusa*).
- **Dominance in 1966:** [[Three-Ridge]] (*Amblema plicata*) was the most abundant mussel (62.4% of live mussels; 2,650 specimens), followed by [[Pimple-Back]] (*Quadrula pustulosa*) and [[Maple-Leaf]] (*Quadrula quadrula*). [[Washboard]] (*Megalonaias gigantea*) ranked fourth.
- **Commercial value:** Washboard was the second most important shell for pearl culture; Three-Ridge was the most important commercially. Most Pimple-Backs and Warty-Backs were below commercial size.
- **Declines:** The [[Spectacle-Case]] (*Cumberlandia monodonta*), [[Ebony Shell]] (*Fusconaia ebena*, one live specimen), [[Pig-Toe]] (*Fusconaia flava* f. *undata*), [[Lady-Finger]], [[Butterfly]] and [[Elk-Toe]] were absent or nearly so; most species were lost from above Peoria by the 1920s.
- **Recovery:** Some species extended upstream since the mid-1920s, including [[Fragile Paper Shell]] (to river mile 229.0) and [[Slough Sand-Shell]], suggesting pollution tolerance.
- **Taxonomy:** Stansbery's revisions treated *Anodonta grandis corpulenta* as a subspecies of *A. grandis*, placed *Amblema plicata* forms under one species, and retained *Fusconaia flava* as form *undata* in the river.
- **Key conclusion:** The author attributes the loss of sensitive species mainly to pollution and siltation, with reduced current from dams contributing, and recommends management based on current knowledge of mussel beds.
