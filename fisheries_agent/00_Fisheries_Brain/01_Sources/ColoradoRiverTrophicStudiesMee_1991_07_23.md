# Colorado River Trophic Studies Meeting and Agenda

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #river-ecosystems #trophic-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document is an internal U.S. National Park Service memorandum from Lawrence E. Stevens (Grand Canyon Ecologist), dated 23 July 1991, announcing the quarterly meeting of the **Colorado River (Aquatic) Trophic Studies (CRiTS)** group. CRiTS is described as an informal multi-agency/multi-institution coordination body focused on aquatic food-base studies within the [[Colorado River Ecosystem]], specifically the regulated reach below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]]. While the source contains no raw empirical fish or invertebrate data, it provides a valuable structural snapshot of the coordinated research program organized around primary production, secondary production, fisheries, and energy-flow modeling in a dam-regulated river system — an important precursor framework to later [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES)]] trophic and food-base research.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The agenda frames the Colorado River ecosystem below Glen Canyon Dam as a tiered trophic system, targeted for an integrated **Colorado River energy model**. The planned trophic/energy hierarchy to be quantified includes:

- **Producers** — algal/periphyton primary production, tied to light availability and solar input modeling
- **Primary consumers** — grazers/aquatic invertebrates, including [[Aquatic Diptera]] studied by NPS-NAU
- **Fish** — the vertebrate secondary/tertiary consumer tier, addressed via AGF tailwater and downstream fishery studies and BioWest fisheries studies
- **Terrestrial insectivores** — organisms feeding on emergent aquatic insects
- **Terrestrial piscivores** — apex vertebrate predators explicitly named as [[Bald Eagle]] and [[Osprey]], linking the aquatic food base to riparian/terrestrial predator populations

Key abiotic drivers identified as structuring this trophic system include:
- Light/solar energy input (reach-by-reach solar input modeling)
- Light extinction in the water column
- River cross-sectional geomorphology affecting light and habitat availability
- Interim flow regimes and long-term monitoring needs from Glen Canyon Dam operations

This establishes the Colorado River tailwater as a **light- and flow-regulated aquatic ecosystem**, where dam operations control the physical template (light, temperature, flow) constraining primary and secondary production, ultimately propagating up to fish populations and terrestrial piscivores.

## Key Findings

- CRiTS functioned as a quarterly interagency coordination forum (NPS, USGS, AGF, BR, NAU, BioWest) for Colorado River aquatic food-base research circa 1991.
- Six major research components were being coordinated: (1) USGS synoptic studies, (2) AGF tailwater/downstream fishery studies, (3) NPS-NAU aquatic Diptera studies, (4) BR light studies, (5) the "eddies as nutrient pumps" project, and (6) BioWest fisheries studies.
- A central program goal was development of a **Colorado River energy model**, requiring reach-by-reach solar input modeling, river cross-section surveys, light extinction measurements, and standing crop estimates across five trophic levels (producers → primary consumers → fish → terrestrial insectivores → terrestrial piscivores).
- Management relevance was tied to evaluating **interim flow regimes** and designing **long-term monitoring** protocols for aquatic resources in the Glen Canyon Dam tailwater reach.
- The agenda reflects an early, explicit ecosystem-modeling approach linking dam-regulated hydrology and light regimes to fish production and apex avian piscivores ([[Bald Eagle]], [[Osprey]]), foreshadowing later GCES-EIS integrated studies.
