# Taxonomic Treatment of the Colorado River Chubs (*Gila robusta* Complex)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic study by [[Gerald R. Smith]], [[Robert Rush Miller]], and W. Daniel Sable (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, Arizona State University, and Wisconsin State University) tests whether the chubs of the [[Colorado River]] system represent one variable species or several. Using multivariate and univariate morphological analyses, the authors conclude that three forms in the big-river main channels, [[Gila Robusta]], [[Gila Cypha]], and [[Gila Elegans]], are morphologically distinct and behave as reproductively isolated species. They group these with peripheral populations as the [[Gila Robusta Superspecies]] and identify a new, practical key characteristic: a quantified [[Nuchal Hump]] ratio. The paper also corrects misinformation about the species' distribution and reports the threats posed by dams and exotic fishes.

## Background & Description

The study addresses long-standing uncertainty about the status of *Gila cypha*, which had been the most controversial of the big-river forms. The authors analyzed 34 morphological characters using [[Principal Components Analysis]] and examined 261 individuals in univariate analyses. The nuchal-hump development was measured in 72 individuals.

The three main taxa are described as follows:

- **Gila robusta (s.s.):** The most primitive of the three forms. Modally 9 dorsal and 9 anal rays. Fewest gill rakers of the three species.
- **Gila cypha:** Characterized by an abrupt nuchal hump, a concave frontal region above the eyes, and a snout that overhangs the upper lip. Modally 9 dorsal and 10 anal rays.
- **Gila elegans:** Has a less abrupt hump, a slender caudal peduncle, and a snout that does not overhang the upper lip. Modally 10 dorsal and 10 or 11 anal rays. Has the most gill rakers.

Two additional forms are discussed. *Gila robusta seminuda* of the Virgin River is closest to *G. elegans* in precaudal vertebral count. *Gila intermedia* of the Gila River basin is considered part of the robusta superspecies, though it may be a separate phyletic line.

The [[Type Locality]] of *G. elegans* and *G. robusta* is assigned to Grand Falls on the [[Little Colorado River]]. The authors reject the earlier claim that the type series came from the Zuni River, New Mexico, which was described as only a rivulet during collection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The three big-river species coexist in the main channels of the Colorado and Green rivers. They are absent from smaller tributaries, where the authors interpret a single intermediate-morphology population as selected for by reduced habitat heterogeneity. The [[Virgin River]] population contains 11 specimens of *G. robusta* that show none of the nuchal hump and span much of the variation seen in the three main forms. It is interpreted as a variable, locally adapted population rather than a hybrid swarm.

Diet appears omnivorous to carnivorous, with habitat-related specializations in feeding structures.

Dams are the primary threat. They destroy swift-water habitat upstream and reduce temperature and turbidity downstream. Introduced exotic competitors and predators, particularly on juveniles, are a further threat in modified reaches. The [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and the resulting [[Lake Powell]] reservoir effectively created an uncontrolled experiment in which the authors expect hybridization and loss of the specialized forms. The [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] also altered the Green River fauna, and the 1962 poisoning of the upper Green River was followed by erroneous published statements about the chubs.

## Key Findings

**Morphological discrimination**

- The three big-river forms (*G. robusta*, *G. cypha*, *G. elegans*) are discriminated with almost no overlap in the combined analysis of 34 characters. Components I (general size), II (vertebrae, dorsal and anal rays, gill rakers), and III (fin rays, gill rakers, pectoral length, eye diameter) summarize the main trends.
- *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* show complete separation. *G. robusta* and *G. cypha* overlap by one specimen (UMMZ 18?-281, specimen 2). That fish has 9 anal rays, which favors *G. robusta*, but its nuchal-hump ratio of 9.2 is typical of *G. cypha*.

**Nuchal hump ratio (Table 1)**

- A new repeatable ratio is derived from the depth of the frontal depression relative to the distance between the pectoral and pelvic fin insertions, measured with an instrument adapted from Eschmeyer and Poss (1977) and accurate to 0.1 mm. This replaces earlier subjective scoring.
- Reported ratio ranges (sample sizes in parentheses): *G. cypha* 5–13 (31); *G. elegans* 15–29 (20); *G. robusta* 28–207 (17); *G. seminuda* 31–121 (4).

**Meristic characters**

- Precaudal vertebrae (Table 3, excluding Weberian apparatus) mean: *G. robusta* 19.36 (n = 83); *G. seminuda* 21.00 (n = 33); *G. cypha* 19.06 (n = 95); *G. elegans* 21.94 (n = 49). Precaudal counts separate *G. robusta* from *G. seminuda* better than total counts, which range 39–44 (mode 42) in *G. robusta* and 40–45 (mode 42) in *G. seminuda*.
- Gill rakers on the second arch (Table 2) separate *G. robusta* (fewest) from *G. elegans* (most). Gill-raker number and precaudal vertebrae together discriminate *G. robusta* from *G. seminuda*.

**Taxonomic conclusions**

- The three big-river forms are treated as separate, reproductively isolated species. The authors acknowledge that isolating mechanisms may break down in disturbed reservoirs and in tributaries.
- All three entities are assigned to the *Gila robusta* superspecies, including *G. intermedia* and several peripheral populations in Mexico.
- *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* are inferred to have arisen from *G. robusta* by separate speciation events, since neither is intermediate in a way that suggests ancestry.

**Identification of juveniles**

- Criteria are provided for young fish: *G. robusta* identified to 20 mm, *G. cypha* to 54 mm, and *G. elegans* to 22 mm total length. Earlier identifications had combined young individuals as "Colorado chub" under 200 mm total length.

**Distribution corrections**

- No *G. cypha* were collected in the Green River in Colorado and Utah during 1964–1966 after three were taken in 1963. The claim of large numbers of humpback chubs released from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] is not supported.
- The only valid Grand Canyon record of [[Colorado Squawfish]] is an adult (about 320 mm S.L.) taken in 1975 at the mouth of Havasu Creek. The reports from between Glen Canyon Dam and Lees Ferry are above Grand Canyon.

**Management recommendations**

- Maintain large sections of unspoiled main-channel habitat.
- Prioritize research on habitat, food, reproduction, recruitment, hydrodynamics, and energetics.
- Specimens may be sent to the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology for identification.
