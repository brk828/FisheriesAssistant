# Aquatic Habitats and Fishes of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 final report, prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under Contract No. 14-06-3002-529 by W. L. Minckley of Arizona State University, describes an ecological survey of the Lower [[Colorado River]] mainstream from [[Davis Dam]] (river mile 422.0) to the U.S.–Mexico International Boundary (RM 704.2), sampled 1974–1976. The study measured fishes, benthic invertebrates, higher plants, algae (via pigment analysis), and physical-chemical water conditions across ten operational divisions.

Diversity, numbers, and biomass of most organisms increased from upstream to downstream, paralleling rising dissolved solids, nutrients, pH, turbidity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen variability as the river moved through hot desert and under repeated human use and reuse. Dams, diversions, channelized reaches, and agricultural drain inputs modified this pattern. The main constraints on high diversity, biomass, and population size were unstable bottoms and the intensity of channel modification.

## Background & Description

The Lower Colorado is among the most highly modified channels in North America, now under near-complete control by mainstream and tributary impoundments. The study area lies in the Southwestern Climatic Zone: local rainfall rarely exceeds 12 cm per year, summer temperatures often exceed 40 °C, and winters seldom drop below freezing. Natural vegetation is lower Sonoran desert-scrub.

Historically the river aggraded, but after [[Hoover Dam]] closed in 1935 and Parker and Imperial dams in 1938, the channel degraded. Channelization between Needles and Topock was completed in 1951, and Palo Verde and Cibola divisions were finished in 1966 and the late 1960s respectively. Channel widths range from 50 to 350 m (most channelized sections about 150 m); depths range from over 18 m in [[Lake Havasu]] to about 8 m in unmodified reaches and about 2.5 m averaged in channelized segments.

Ten operational divisions were delineated: Mohave Valley, Topock Gorge, Havasu, Parker, Palo Verde, Cibola, Imperial, Laguna, Yuma, and Limitrophe. Bottoms are boulder-gravel below dams and in structural uplifts, and sand–silt downstream.

Field and laboratory methods included seines, gill and hoop nets, trammel nets, Ekman dredge and Surber-type benthic samples, quadrat hand-picking, macrophyte biomass sampling, pigment extraction (chlorophyll and carotenoids), and in-situ physical-chemical measurements using a Hydrolab unit. Data were processed on a Univac 1110 system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish fauna.** About 44 species are recorded between Davis Dam and the Mexican Boundary; only 10 are native, all extirpated or exceedingly rare. Fewer than 18 species maintain substantial populations, and at any site major constituents rarely exceed 5 or 6 kinds. Key introduced species include [[Red Shiner]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Sailfin Molly]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Mozambique Mouthbrooder]] (African), and [[Striped Mullet]], which form a mix of tropical and introduced warm-water fauna.

**Benthos.** Invertebrate diversity, numbers, and biomass were low in large erosive rivers, dominated by chironomid larvae, oligochaetes, and the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula manilensis*). The lowest values occurred in lower Mohave Valley, Palo Verde, and Cibola divisions, which had rapidly shifting sand bottoms. Filter-feeders (simuliid blackflies, hydropsychid caddisflies, and Corbicula) were abundant below Davis and Parker dams, reflecting organic particulates from reservoir hypolimnia.

**Plants and algae.** Aquatic vegetation was locally abundant on coarse, stable substrates and in backwaters. Dominant submergent plants included sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*) and spiny naiad (*Najas marina*). Emergent stands of cattail (*Typha domingensis*) and sedges (*Scirpus* spp.) lined backwaters. Bluegreen algae dominated lentic mats, and diatoms formed films in current. Most planktonic algae were scoured from benthic forms rather than drifting in place.

**Physical-chemical conditions.** Conductance rose from about 900 µmhos/cm near Davis Dam to about 1,100 µmhos/cm near RM 670, then exceeded 4,000 µmhos/cm in Limitrophe Division below the Hellton-Mohawk Drain. pH ranged typically from 7.8 to 8.2 (total range 7.2–9.2). Turbidity typically ran 1–10 JTU in the channel. Dissolved oxygen approached saturation in upstream reaches and showed large diel swings in Limitrophe Division.

## Key Findings

- **Downstream gradient:** Fish numbers, weights, and diversity increased downstream (correlation with distance below Davis Dam: r = .41 for numbers in mainstream, r = .39 in backwaters; r = .60 for weight in backwaters, r = .43 in mainstream; r = .32 for number of kinds in mainstream, r = .87 in lentic habitat).
- **Red shiner dominance:** In mainstream seine catches, red shiner comprised 47.5% of all fishes, followed by threadfin shad (27.2%), sailfin molly (28.2%), mosquitofish (4.9%), Mozambique mouthbrooder (3.1%), bluegill (2.1%), carp (0.6%), and largemouth bass (0.5%). Red shiner was the most abundant species in 5 of 8 seined segments.
- **Backwater composition:** Backwaters supported different communities, with red shiner at 14.4%, mosquitofish at 21.8%, threadfin shad at 20.4%, and bluegill at 6.8% of numbers; young-of-the-year predatory centrarchids and ictalurids made up roughly 4% to 60% of backwater specimens.
- **Sampling efficiency:** Seines matched poison (pesticide kill) for only 2 of 9 common species. Overall, seining captured about 85% of species, under 25% of numbers, and about 10% of weight of the canal fish kill.
- **Temperature limits:** Water temperatures fell below 8.5 °C in some backwaters in winter 1974–75; about 10 °C is lethal to many poeciliids, most cichlids, and often threadfin shad. This restricts mollies and tilapias to the lowermost reaches.
- **Gill and hoop nets:** Both gear types fished most effectively at night. Parker Division produced the greatest recorded fish diversity, and Limitrophe Division’s Hunter’s Hole averaged 47 fish per night (28 striped mullet, 31.0 kg).
- **Benthic patterns:** Benthos was smallest in lower Mohave Valley, Palo Verde, and Cibola divisions. Mayflies, caddisflies, and beetles characteristic of boulder-gravel streams occurred below Davis and Parker dams.
- **Algal productivity:** Chlorophyll A was mostly magnitude II (1.00–9.99 mg/m³) in Mohave Valley. Imperial Division averaged 2 to 3 times higher in June–August 1975, and Limitrophe Division backwaters were spectacularly high, with 50 of 71 samples at magnitude III.
- **Pesticide spill:** Seining before the canal pesticide kill yielded 12 fish species, about 2,114.97 individuals per ha weighing 39.2 kg per ha; the kill afterward yielded 14 species totaling 1,084.04 fish weighing 22.7 kg per ha.
- **Nutrients:** Phosphate-phosphorus was highest in drain-influenced reaches (Cibola division drains up to 0.26 mg/L), and Limitrophe Division averaged near 0.2 mg/L with a maximum of 1.42 mg/L. Nitrate-nitrogen was generally about half as high in backwaters as in the mainstream.
- **Conclusion:** Variability in physical-chemical features increased downstream, and no single backwater or reach was found to present critical physical-chemical conditions for fish, apart from local exceptions in drains and near dams.
