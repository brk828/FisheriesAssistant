# Range Extension of Corbicula fluminea in the Ohio Drainage

#bivalvia #malacology #corbicula-fluminea

## Executive Summary

This short note in [[The Nautilus]] (July 1962, Vol. 76(1), pp. 35–36) reports the presence of the [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula fluminea*) in the [[Green River]] of Kentucky, within the [[Ohio River]] drainage. Collections in September 1961 recorded large numbers of individuals along about 20 miles of river, roughly 100 river miles upstream of the Green's confluence with the Ohio. The authors describe the species as spreading rapidly through navigable waterways of the Ohio drainage and suggest that commercial shipping may be a dispersal vector. They also note little ecological resistance to its dominance of available mussel habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Corbicula Fluminea]], commonly called the "Asiatic clam," a freshwater bivalve.
- **Prior records:** [[Sinclair and Isom 1961]] reported the species in the Tennessee and Cumberland drainages. No prior reports existed for the Ohio drainage above the [[Cumberland River]].
- **Collecting body:** The collections were made under the auspices of the Limnology Department of the [[Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia]].
- **Authors:** The note is attributed to Paul C. Mansh and John M. Berns, the latter writing the closing commentary on dispersal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and collections (September 1961):**
- Survey reach: about 20 miles of the [[Green River]], from below [[Central City Kentucky]] to above [[Paradise Kentucky]], corresponding to roughly river miles 82 to 102 on [[Corps of Engineers Navigational Charts]].
- Yield: "thousands" of individuals, with a maximum shell length of 21 mm. No single size class predominated.

**Substrate use:**
- Highest per-unit-area densities occurred on sand-mud substrates.
- Firmer substrates supported proportionally fewer individuals.
- Clams were also found in dredge samples from a dredged river channel bedded with firm clay-sand, under rocks, between boulders, and lodged in wood crevices.
- The authors concluded that substrate does not appear to be a limiting factor in the species' distribution.

**Upstream extent:**
- Several collections in the vicinity of [[Mammoth Cave National Park]] yielded no specimens, so the upstream limit on the Green was not extended to that point.

**Dispersal considerations:**
- No commercial traffic operates on the Green River above Paradise, Kentucky.
- The invaded reaches of the [[Tennessee River]] and [[Cumberland River]] are open to commercial navigation.
- The authors suspect [[Commercial Shipping]] as a dispersal agent, since the species appears to be spreading through the navigable waterways of the Ohio drainage.
- Unverified reports indicated the clam occurred as far upstream on the Tennessee River as [[Knoxville Tennessee]].
- Whether the species had reached other Ohio tributaries, such as the [[Wabash River]], was unknown.

## Key Findings

1. **Range extension:** *Corbicula fluminea* was documented along 20 miles of the Green River, Kentucky, about 100 miles upstream of its confluence with the Ohio. The authors described this as a considerable extension of its range within the Ohio drainage.
2. **Abundance:** The Green River collections yielded thousands of individuals, indicating established, abundant populations.
3. **Size structure:** Individuals reached a maximum length of 21 mm, with no dominant size class.
4. **Substrate tolerance:** Densities were highest on sand-mud substrates but the clam occurred on firm clay-sand, rock, boulders, and wood. Substrate was judged not to limit its distribution.
5. **Upstream limit:** No specimens were found in collections near Mammoth Cave National Park.
6. **Dispersal hypothesis:** Commercial shipping is the suspected vector, since invaded reaches of the Tennessee and Cumberland rivers are navigable and the Green above Paradise is not.
7. **Ecological impact:** The authors judged that little ecological resistance exists to prevent the species from completely dominating available mussel habitat.
8. **Research gap:** Invasion of other Ohio tributaries, such as the Wabash, remained unassessed.
