# Flow Recommendations for the 15-Mile Reach of the Upper Colorado River (October–June)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (Colorado River Fishery Project, April 1991; authors Douglas B. Osmundson and Lynn R. Kaeding) gives year-round flow recommendations for the [[15-Mile Reach]] of the [[Upper Colorado River]] for October through June. It addresses the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] and the rare [[Razorback Sucker]]. The authors conclude that reduced spring peak flows, caused by upstream [[Water Development]], have harmed both species and favored [[Introduced Minnows]]. They recommend restoring a range of high spring flows at a higher frequency, plus minimum winter and transition-month flows. The recommendations are described as provisional and are to be refined by a 1992 report.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The 15-mile reach runs from Palisade, Colorado to the confluence with the [[Gunnison River]] at Grand Junction. It is habitat for the Colorado Squawfish (adults year-round) and the Razorback Sucker (mainly during its April–June spawning season).
- **Status:** No young Razorback Suckers had been found in the upper Colorado River in the past 27 years. Adult captures in the [[Grand Valley]] had fallen sharply in the previous 17 years. Two running-ripe adults were captured in a flooded gravel pit in 1986.
- **Seasonal framework:** The water year was split by Colorado Squawfish habitat use: winter (November–February), spring (April–June), summer (July–September, handled in an earlier provisional report), and transition months (October and March).
- **Methodological shift:** Summer recommendations relied on the [[PHABSIM]] model. Spring and winter recommendations rely on empirical relationships, because PHABSIM cannot model backwaters, gravel pits and eddies and was judged unreliable for those seasons.
- **Scope:** The Prologue notes that [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] are not in the Grand Valley and are outside these reports. A riverwide approach is recommended later.
- **Funding:** The study was funded by the [[Recovery Program]] for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Channel structure and habitat use.** High spring flows build and maintain the [[Braided Channel]] pattern, including side channels, pools and backwaters. Backwaters serve as nursery habitat for young squawfish. Radiotelemetry of adult squawfish (1986–1989) showed selection for complex channels over simple single channels:

| Season | Fish locations | Locations in complex segments | Squawfish tracked |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spring | 174 | 84.5% | 21 |
| Summer | 169 | 71.0% | 17 |
| Winter | 85 | 62.4% | 8 |

Selection for complex channels was significant (P < .001). The complex-segment share of available habitat was 53.9% in spring, and simple segments were 55.1% of available habitat in summer and winter.

**Sediment and vegetation.** Reduced peak flows let vegetation stabilize bars and islands, which reduces braiding. Dams above sediment sources cannot prevent this. [[Tamarisk]] seedlings established on new sand and silt bars in 1988–1990. Peak flows of 10,796 cfs (1988), 6,641 cfs (1989) and 9,469 cfs (1990) did not flush them. The authors estimate that flushing flows above about 11,000 cfs, or longer duration, are needed. The exact requirement is unknown.

**Water temperature and spawning.** Main-channel temperatures of 20–22 °C occur in the 15-mile reach and are thought necessary for squawfish spawning and egg hatching. Razorback suckers appear to seek warmer off-channel water (flooded bottomlands and gravel pits) during spring high flows. The main channel averages about 13 °C at peak flow, while a flooded gravel pit held 22.5 °C in 1986.

**Introduced species.** Introduced minnows (red shiner, fathead minnow and sand shiner) dominate backwater nursery habitat. Predatory centrarchids (green sunfish, largemouth bass) are also present. Laboratory work found young squawfish competitively inferior to several introduced species. Predation on larval razorback suckers by red shiner, green sunfish and channel catfish was observed. Elimination of these predators is not currently possible.

**Flow regime alteration.** Major changes began in 1943–1953 with the Colorado-Big Thompson Project. Gunnison River changes began with Blue Mesa and Morrow Point reservoirs (1966). The authors compared the pre-development and post-development periods, and their conclusions were applied to the San Juan and Green rivers.

## Key Findings

**Spring flows and young fishes**

- Catch rates of larval [[Colorado Squawfish]] were highest in 1979 and 1980, when [[State Line]] peak flows were about 35,000 and 30,000 cfs. They were lowest in 1981 (11,200 cfs).
- In the 18-mile reach, larval squawfish abundance correlated with annual peak flow (r = 0.931, r² = 0.867, P = .069). Fall young-of-the-year (YOY) density correlated more strongly (r = 0.974, r² = 0.925, P = .026). The authors caution that correlation does not establish cause and effect.
- Native bluehead sucker and speckled dace larvae increased with peak flow. Introduced red shiner, fathead minnow and sand shiner larvae decreased.
- Highest YOY squawfish abundance occurred in 1985 and 1986 (State-line peaks of 38,200 and 32,800 cfs).

**Historic versus recent hydrology**

- Mean peak flow in the 15-mile reach is 56% of the pre-development mean (36 recent years versus 41 historic years).
- At the State-line gage, mean peak flow is 52% of historic. The Gunnison River's mean yearly peak is 52% of its pre-1937 value.
- Peak flows above 40,000 cfs at the State line occurred in 56% of historic years but only 8% of recent years.
- Peaks in the 30,000–40,000 cfs window still occur in about one year in four.
- Winter flows have increased (about 120–134% of historic), and March flows average 2,006 cfs.

**Razorback Sucker spawning**

- Of 157 razorbacks examined, 42 were in spawning condition. Of these, 40 (95%) were caught between 24 May and 17 June.
- Razorbacks spawn in spring, unlike squawfish, which spawn when main-channel temperatures reach 19–22 °C in late June to early September.
- The authors conclude that the razorback population collapsed because very high spring flows that flood bottomlands have become rare.

**Recommended spring flows (top of the 15-mile reach)**

| Frequency | Peak day (cfs) | April mean (cfs) | May mean (cfs) | June mean (cfs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| >25% of years | >23,500 | >3,900 | >12,900 | >16,200 |
| ~25% of years | 20,500–23,500 | 3,200–3,900 | 10,800–12,900 | 12,800–16,200 |
| ~50% of years | 14,800–20,500 | 2,400–3,200 | 8,300–10,800 | 10,000–12,800 |

The authors say the mid-point of each range should be the long-term target. They recommend against treating the window as equally acceptable, because a State-line peak of 38,200 cfs is preferred over 30,000 cfs.

**Recommendations for peak flows at the State line**

1. Maintain or increase the current roughly 25% frequency of peaks of 30,000–40,000 cfs, for squawfish young production and control of introduced fishes.
2. Raise the frequency of peaks above 40,000 cfs from about one year in 12 (8%) to one in four (25%), to aid razorback reproduction, maintain complex habitat, and flush introduced fishes from protected habitats.
3. In the remaining years, peaks should exceed 22,000 cfs. This is the minimum acceptable level for squawfish young production and for preventing tamarisk rooting on bars.

**Winter flows (November–February)**

- Adult squawfish and razorbacks are in a low-activity maintenance mode.
- Adult squawfish were mostly in pools and slow runs (mean depth about 3.8–5.1 ft). Razorbacks used pools and eddies (mean depth about 6.4–7.2 ft).
- Tentative recommendation: winter flows should not fall below historic levels, roughly 1,000–2,000 cfs with an average near 1,470 cfs. Seasonal storage of winter surplus for spring release is suggested.

**Transition months**

- **October:** Mean flow is about 61% of the 1902–1942 level. Adult squawfish used runs and pools (85% of habitat). Current or summer-recommended flows are judged adequate.
- **March:** Flow has risen by about 220 cfs (mean 2,006 cfs). The authors recommend maintaining the current March regime for razorback deep-water habitat, and they consider the 700–1,200 cfs summer range insufficient.

**Overall conclusion:** The authors believe returning spring flows to historic levels would be the safest option, but they consider it unnecessary. They judge that meeting the above ranges is the minimum needed to reverse the decline of both species. Maintaining the status quo is considered insufficient. The [[Yampa River]], which retains a largely unaltered flow regime and the largest remaining squawfish population, is managed under a separate maintenance strategy.
