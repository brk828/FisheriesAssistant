# Capture Rates of Razorback Sucker Larvae with Light Traps in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This appendix to the [[Native Fish Work Group]] 1992 Annual Report tests whether modified [[Light Trap]] designs can capture larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], a stronghold for an otherwise rare, federally Endangered species. Razorback sucker larvae are [[Phototaxis|phototactic]], but earlier light traps had caught few catostomid larvae, which the authors attributed to low light intensity and battery problems. The team built clear PVC traps and compared four attractants in field trials from February to April 20, 1992, at [[Yuma Cove]] and [[Arizona Bay]]. Light sources of 12-W and 55-W halogen bulbs captured far more larvae than chemical light sticks or dog food. The authors state that light-trap catches are an index of relative larval abundance, not a quantitative density estimate, but they still indicate the extent of the spawning effort.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was once common throughout the [[Colorado River]] basin. Habitat degradation and predation by non-native fishes made it uncommon to rare everywhere except a large relic population in Lake Mohave (Minckley et al. 1991). The species is federally listed as Endangered. A multi-agency program spawns and rears razorback suckers in isolated, on-site backwaters near Lake Mohave, producing fish large enough to survive when reintroduced to the reservoir. This appendix describes modifications to, and techniques for, monitoring spawning success. It also documents the first concerted attempt to collect larval catostomids with light traps.

Prior larval sampling relied on [[Spotlight Sampling]] and dip nets (Marsh and Langhorst 1988; Minckley et al. 1991; Bozek et al. 1991). Light traps had been used for many phototactic fish species, but they had not been effective for catostomids.

The trap design was adapted from [[Killgore 1991]] and constructed from 6-mm-thick clear PVC pipe and sheet material:
- Four 10-cm-diameter pipes were arranged in a shamrock pattern and fused to two flat 25-cm PVC disks.
- Trap tops were painted black to direct illumination toward the entrances (5-mm entrance slits, per the schematic).
- No flotation was added, so traps could be placed directly on the bottom.
- Power came from 12-VDC deep-cycle batteries located on shore, to address battery-life limits.

Light intensity was measured in tap water 20 cm from the light source with a Sperry SLM meter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Field work took place in Lake Mohave at two sites: Yuma Cove and Arizona Bay. The clearer water of Arizona Bay was a factor in light-attractant performance. Trap sets were run for about a week and collected with dip nets. Captured larvae were examined on site and released unharmed.

A subsample of larvae (N=48) averaged 11.3 mm total length (range 10.5 to 12.7 mm; SD 0.5). Light-trap captures overlapped with other aquatic organisms, including zooplankton and larval odonates, which the authors note were also attracted to light in earlier studies.

The authors cite earlier work on fish sensitivity to light intensity (Jones 1956; Woodhead 1956; Wolf and Zerrahn-Wolf 1956). Some species show photopositive responses only above a threshold intensity, and very high intensities can be harmful to larvae. Bulkowski and Meade (1983) reported phototaxis changes during early development of walleye.

## Key Findings

**Attractant comparison**
- Four attractants were tested: dog food, cyalume chemical light sticks, 12-W lights, and 55-W halogen bulbs.
- A total of **226 trap sets** were completed, representing **1,327 trap-hours** of effort: about 758 hours in Yuma Cove and 569 hours (N=91) in Arizona Bay.
- Initial sampling used cyalume light sticks and dog food, with substantially higher numbers of sets and trap-hours for those attractants.
- Dog-food traps (N=82) never exceeded 0.1 larvae per hour.
- Cyalume light-stick traps (N=92) produced a low maximum catch rate, indicating poor performance overall.

**Light intensity and source**
- Razorback sucker larvae responded to light only above roughly **190 lux**.
- 12-W lights averaged **2.8 larvae/h** at Yuma Cove (N=15) and **29.8 larvae/h** at Arizona Bay (N=11).
- 55-W halogen bulbs produced **5.0 larvae/h** at Yuma Cove (N=13), with a higher Arizona Bay average (the OCR text is partly garbled at this value, approximately 14 larvae/h).
- White light was more effective than colored light, consistent with Kurien et al. (1952).
- Higher-intensity sources outperformed lower ones, and Arizona Bay's clearer water appeared to favor brighter lights.

**Practical improvements**
- The 12-W and 55-W sources provided more power and longer battery life than chemical light sticks, which solved a key limitation of earlier light-trap work.
- The clear-PVC, no-flotation, bottom-set design with shore-based batteries was workable in the field.

**Conclusions and applications**
- Light traps can capture razorback sucker larvae when powered by bright, white light sources and adequate battery capacity.
- Light-trap catches are not quantitative density estimates, but they provide an index of relative larval abundance and show the extent of the spawning effort.
- The approach can help monitor the success of the captive spawning and rearing program for this Endangered species.
