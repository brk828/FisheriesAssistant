# Nonnative Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin: A Strategic Plan for Their Control

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Ictaluridae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by [[Harold Tyus]] and James F. Saunders III of the [[Center for Limnology]] (University of Colorado at Boulder) for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP) for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin, assesses how nonnative fishes threaten the endangered big-river fishes of the upper basin and provides a strategic plan for controlling them. Four of the seven large species in the "big river" assemblage are federally listed as endangered, and the other three are species at risk. Although dams, diversions and flow regulation caused major habitat change, the authors judge introduced fishes the most significant current threat.

A workshop held in Boulder on 30 November and 1 December 1995 identified three top-priority recovery reaches, the most serious nonnative threats, and candidate control measures. The plan sets a single goal, facilitating recovery of the endangered fishes by controlling the introduction and proliferation of nonnative, nonsalmonid fishes, and it operates through a basin-wide tier and a river-reach tier. The authors state that bold action is needed now and that best professional judgment should be used rather than further small-scale pilot studies.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin's native fauna evolved under extreme seasonal flow variation, turbid water and high aridity. Its 46 native fishes (species and subspecies) include 38 endemics. Native fishes fall into three habitat groups: cold-water salmonids and sculpins in headwaters, daces and minnows in small warmwater streams, and the "big river" fishes of the mainstream channel. The big-river group includes seven large species, four of which are endangered: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail]].

Introductions began with the creation of the US Fish Commission in 1872. At least 67 nonnative species have entered the Colorado River system in the last 100 years, and at least 36 species were introduced between 1930 and 1950, mostly game fishes from the eastern US. Key nonnatives include [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] and crappies. Escapement from reservoirs and floodplain ponds, bait-bucket transfers and illegal transfers all supply these species.

The report places the Colorado case within a national problem. The US Office of Technology Assessment (USOTA 1993) counted 4,500 foreign species established in the United States, and the Aquatic Nuisance Species Task Force (ANSTF 1994) found that centrarchids and ictalurids were the groups most often cited for endangering native fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and biological change.** Dams and diversions, more than 50 mainstream dams and major diversions by the 1960s, altered flow timing, sediment load, temperature and nutrients. Silt load in some reaches fell by about 90%. Nonnative fishes now occupy most suitable habitat. The authors argue that physical habitat is necessary but not sufficient for recovery, because introduced visual predators have benefited from reduced turbidity.

**Extinction risk.** Native big-river fishes have disappeared from about three-quarters of their original habitat. The report notes that small, isolated populations are vulnerable to stochastic events and that displacement by introduced species is a major cause of native fish decline (cited in 68% of reported cases in Wilson 1992).

**Biodiversity framing.** The report defines biodiversity to include species numbers, ecological roles and genetic material, and it links interacting threats (predation, competition, habitat alteration and isolation) that together drive extinction.

**Sociopolitical context.** Control faces conflict with sport fishing. In a survey of nearly 900 residents of eastern Utah and western Colorado (Vaske et al. 1995), 66% were unaware that stocking nonnative sportfish harmed endangered fishes. About 75% of respondents who learned of the harm opposed continued stocking. Recreational anglers (about 69% in the survey) felt that stocking improved fishing.

## Key Findings

**Nonnative impacts**
- Predation is the primary mechanism. Green sunfish consumed 90% of razorback sucker larvae offered in 4-minute trials (Karp and Tyus 1990). Red shiner and red-side shiner consumed 50% and 10% respectively in the same experiments.
- Valdez and Ryel (1995) estimated brown trout consumed 230,000 humpback chub annually in Grand Canyon, with rainbow trout consuming 27,975.
- Recruitment failure is the main cause of decline in [[Razorback Sucker]] populations, with almost no recruitment to adult size.
- Hybridization of [[White Sucker]] with native suckers is a further concern (Burdick 1995).
- Workshop participants considered [[Channel Catfish]] the greatest threat to listed fishes. Green sunfish, fathead minnow and red shiner were the next most serious threats.

**Escapement and expansion**
- Northern pike and smallmouth bass in the Yampa River rose sharply after the 1970s and 1980s; smallmouth bass numbers jumped after 1992 due to escapement from Elkhead Reservoir.
- Crappies in the Green River increased from about four fish per year to over one thousand per year in the early 1990s (Crowl, workshop presentation 1996).

**Priority reaches (workshop and plan)**
- Highest-priority recovery reaches: Yampa River (Deerlodge to Echo Park), Green River (Echo Park to Desolation Canyon), and Colorado River (Government Highline diversion to Cataract Canyon).
- Three basin-wide themes: prevent nonnative entry, remove nonnatives from the main channel, and exclude nonnatives from larval and juvenile native habitats.

**Control measures**
- Mechanical removal (trapping, netting, seining, electrofishing) is the main near-term tool. Chemical removal is limited to isolated waters because of risk to native fishes in the main channel.
- Flow management may suppress small cyprinids, but the effect is temporary and causality is unproven (Muth and Nesler 1993).
- Meronek et al. (1996) found only 43% of 250 US fish control projects met their objectives.
- Commercial harvest of channel catfish, as in the Missouri River (Hesse 1994, six-year response including a 36% increase in fish over 330 mm TL), was identified as a potentially large-scale option, but it requires subsidy and regulatory work.

**Institutional and legal framework**
- The ESA designation of critical habitat (USFWS 1994) names an environment free of nonnative predators as a primary constituent element.
- The Nonindigenous Aquatic Nuisance Prevention and Control Act (1990) was the first major federal initiative against nonnative aquatic introductions.
- Interim stocking procedures expired in December 1999, and revised protocols remained unagreed.
- Executive Order 12962 (June 1995) pledged federal support for recreational fishing, and a proposed policy statement acknowledged the conflict between recreation and endangered species protection.

**Recommendations and conclusions**
- The authors conclude that the time to conduct slow, small-scale pilot studies has passed. Best professional judgment should guide action, and control efforts should be tested and adjusted as they proceed.
- Without control, the authors expect at least two listed fishes to continue declining because recruitment is virtually absent.
- Reductions of 90% or more in target nonnative abundance will probably be needed in large areas.
- Mitigating lost sport fishing opportunities is a required component of implementation.
