## Executive Summary

On January 6, 1997, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) published a final rule listing the **Sonora tiger salamander** (*[[Ambystoma tigrinum stebbinsi]]*), the **Huachuca water umbel** (*[[Lilaeopsis schaffneriana ssp. recurva]]*), and the **Canelo Hills ladies'-tresses** (*[[Spiranthes delitescens]]*) as **endangered** under the Endangered Species Act of 1973 (16 U.S.C. 1531–1544). No critical habitat was designated for any of the three taxa, as the Service determined that public disclosure of critical habitat locations would increase risks of collection and intentional habitat destruction. All three species inhabit rare wetland ecosystems — **cienegas** (desert marshes), springs, streams, rivers, and man-made stock tanks — in Cochise, Santa Cruz, and Pima Counties, Arizona, and adjacent Sonora, Mexico. Threats include collecting, disease, predation, competition with nonnative species, habitat degradation/destruction, water diversion, dredging, groundwater pumping, and stochastic climatic events (floods, drought) compounded by small population sizes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** [[Cienega]] ecosystems — rare, spring-fed desert wetlands/marshes characteristic of the arid Southwest — along with associated springs, streams, rivers, and artificial **stock tanks** (small farm ponds).
- **Sonora Tiger Salamander:**
  - Remnant aquatic populations documented at **23 sites**, almost exclusively **stock tanks** in the San Rafael Valley and adjacent slopes of the **Huachuca** and **Patagonia Mountains**, Arizona.
  - Stock tanks lack the habitat stability of natural cienegas and require periodic sediment maintenance.
  - Livestock use of stock tanks does **not** adversely affect salamander populations; routine tank maintenance is compatible with conservation.
  - Threats specific to this species include relocation/introduction of nonnative aquatic species, tank drying, and destruction of occupied cienega habitat.
- **Huachuca Water Umbel:**
  - Known from **22 sites** in southeastern Arizona and northern Sonora, Mexico.
  - Occupies cienegas, springs, streams, and rivers.
  - **6 of 22** known populations have already been extirpated; habitat loss is largely irreversible ("not recoverable" in most cases).
- **Canelo Hills Ladies'-tresses:**
  - Restricted to only **five very small cienegas** in southeastern Arizona — among the most geographically restricted of the three taxa.
- **Co-occurring threats across all three species:** groundwater pumping, water diversion, dredging, habitat degradation, competition from nonnative species, and catastrophic natural events (flood/drought) exacerbated by small, fragmented population sizes.

## Key Findings

- **Regulatory outcome:** Final listing as **Endangered** for all three species; **no critical habitat designated** due to elevated collection/destruction risk associated with habitat disclosure.
- **Population status:** Sonora tiger salamander — 23 remnant stock-tank populations; Huachuca water umbel — 22 known sites (6 lost); Canelo Hills ladies'-tresses — 5 known cienega sites. All represent small, fragmented, and vulnerable population networks typical of relictual wetland-dependent taxa in arid landscapes.
- **Land use implications:** Listings are not expected to affect routine private landowner activities, including stock tank maintenance, continued livestock use, or private well operation, **unless** such activities would dry out a stock tank or destroy occupied cienega habitat.
- **Federal consultation requirement:** Federal agencies funding, authorizing, or carrying out actions (e.g., groundwater pumping requiring federal permitting) that may affect these species must consult with the USFWS under Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act.
- **Plant protection asymmetry:** Under the Act, listed plants receive full "take" protections only on **Federal lands**; private landowners face no take prohibitions for plants, though interstate/international trade of listed plants is prohibited.
- **Management pathway for salamander conservation:** Conservation measures at stock tanks will be developed through consultation between USFWS and the **Coronado National Forest**, given that most affected stock tanks fall within Forest Service grazing allotments.
- **Conservation assistance:** USFWS **Partners for Wildlife Program** offers technical and financial assistance to landowners seeking to restore or enhance cienega habitat.
- **Broader ecological significance:** This listing underscores the conservation importance of **cienega wetlands** — a rare and highly threatened freshwater habitat type in the arid Southwest — as critical refugia for endemic, range-restricted aquatic and wetland-dependent taxa, with parallels to conservation concerns for restricted-range desert fish species (e.g., [[Gila chub]], [[desert pupfish]]) that depend on similar isolated aquatic habitats.
