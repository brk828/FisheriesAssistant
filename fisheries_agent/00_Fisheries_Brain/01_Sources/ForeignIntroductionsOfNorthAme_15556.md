# Foreign introductions of North American fishes: Inadvisability of recommending North American fishes without careful appraisal of foreign fishes and ecology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #invasive-species #Centrarchidae #Cichlidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1947 essay by ichthyologist [[George S. Myers]], published in the *Progressive Fish-Culturist*, is a foundational early critique of uninformed international fish introductions. Myers argues that North American fish-culture agencies routinely recommend [[North American Fishes]] (e.g., largemouth black bass, sunfishes, carp) for introduction into foreign waters without adequate knowledge of local ecological conditions or existing native fish faunas. He presents several case studies—from [[Chile]], [[Trinidad]], [[Venezuela]], [[Brazil]], and [[South Africa]]/[[East Africa]]—to demonstrate how ignorance of local ichthyofauna and ecology led to poor or potentially disastrous introduction recommendations. The piece is an early call for rigorous ecological assessment prior to species introduction and endorses [[Thomas K. Chamberlain]]'s prior proposal (also in *Prog. Fish-Cult.* 9(2): 78-83) for an international "efficiency rating" system for pondfishes, plus a recommendation for a formal booklet on fish introduction ecology to be produced by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]].

## Background & Description

While this is primarily a policy/ecology essay rather than a morphological study, several species are described briefly:

- **Tucunaré (Cichla spp., Peacock Bass)**: Described as "good-sized" basslike fishes, **15 to 20 inches** in length; found in the [[Orinoco River]] delta region (Venezuela) near [[Trinidad]]. Noted to rise well to plugs and dry flies, and fight hard when hooked—angling qualities comparable to North American black bass.
- **Largemouth Black Bass (Micropterus salmoides)**: A large, predaceous, basslike fish; successfully established in cooler South African waters where no comparable native predator existed.
- **African Cichlids (Great Lakes cichlids)**: Described collectively as "basslike Cichlids," taxonomically related to the tucunaré (both in family Cichlidae). Noted as having the "finest set of species" of basslike cichlids on the African continent, inhabiting lakes up to 400 miles long (implicitly referencing the [[East African Great Lakes]], e.g., [[Lake Tanganyika]]).
- **Common Carp (Cyprinus carpio)**: No detailed morphology given, but ecological behavior (schooling, flood dispersal) is discussed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Myers frames introduction failures/successes almost entirely in terms of ecological mismatch or redundancy:

- **Trinidad case**: Lowland West Indian streams had abundant small forage fish but lacked a larger game/food fish. A U.S. agency recommended North American largemouth black bass despite the immediate availability of ecologically near-identical native [[Tucunaré]] from the nearby Orinoco delta (within ~50 miles), which are climatically and ecologically matched to Trinidad's lowland streams and are already regarded as among South America's finest food fishes (among ~3,000+ Neotropical freshwater fish species).

- **Chile case**: A California expatriate sought to introduce sunfishes and largemouth bass into a trout pond in southern Chile, disregarding the existence of native, cooler-adapted Chilean freshwater perches potentially already suited to those waters. Rainbow trout, by contrast, is cited as a genuinely valuable prior introduction to Chile.

- **Venezuela / Lake Valencia case**: A Venezuelan fisheries officer sought "bluegills" for introduction into [[Lake Valencia]] (Venezuelan highlands); the supplying U.S. agency instead seined an unidentified mixed assemblage of sunfish species from the Potomac River, raising concern about unintended multi-species introduction and loss of taxonomic control over what was actually released.

- **Brazil / carp case**: Carp ponds were widely established in southeastern Brazil circa 1932 based on promotion by a zoologist lacking practical pond-culture or carp expertise. Carp escaped from headwater ponds into the [[Rio Iguaçu]] via flooding ("cloudburst"), where they proliferated in "gigantic schools," reportedly displacing/reducing native fish stocks. This represents an early documented case of unintentional carp invasion via pond overflow. Myers also expresses concern about carp reaching the Amazon system, which holds ~300 native food-fish species.

- **Africa case**: Largemouth bass introduction succeeded in **South Africa**, where no native large predaceous basslike fish existed in cooler waters. However, proposals to extend this same introduction to **East Africa**'s tropical Great Lakes were criticized as ecologically unsound, since these lakes already possess a highly diverse native assemblage of basslike Cichlidae (the same family as the Venezuelan tucunaré), making the introduction ecologically redundant and risky.

## Key Findings

- **No universal pondfish**: Endorses and extends [[Thomas K. Chamberlain]]'s central thesis that no single fish species is universally suitable for introduction across all pond/habitat conditions.
- **Systemic ignorance drives bad recommendations**: North American advisers frequently recommend familiar domestic species (bass, sunfish, carp, crappie) to foreign requesters without investigating (1) local ecological conditions, or (2) native fish faunas already present, which may be equally or better suited.
- **Foreign requesters often arrive with fixed intentions**: Political and reputational pressures often mean foreign officials specifically seek the "famous" North American species rather than being open to ecologically sound alternatives.
- **Tropical faunas are poorly catalogued**: The immense freshwater fish diversity of tropical regions is often unknown even to local fisheries officers, since there are few regional handbooks or informational resources, exacerbating reliance on well-publicized (but not necessarily appropriate) North American species.
- **Documented invasive spread**: The Brazilian carp case is an early real-world documented instance of unintentional invasive establishment via flood escape from aquaculture ponds into a major river system (Rio Iguaçu), with anecdotal reports of native fish decline.
- **Context-dependent success**: The same species (largemouth bass) can be ecologically appropriate in one region (South Africa) and inappropriate in an ecologically different but geographically proximate region (East Africa) due to differing native faunal composition and thermal regimes.
- **Policy recommendation**: Myers supports development of (1) an international pondfish "efficiency rating" table (per Chamberlain) and (2) a Fish and Wildlife Service–produced booklet outlining basic fish ecology principles and a structured local-condition questionnaire for prospective foreign fish importers, to reduce ill-informed introductions.
