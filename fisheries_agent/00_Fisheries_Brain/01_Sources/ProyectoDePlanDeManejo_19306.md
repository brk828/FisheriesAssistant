# Sierra San Luis Wildlife and Aquatic Protection Zone: Draft Management Plan (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #protected-area

## Executive Summary

This is a draft management plan (*Proyecto de Plan de Manejo*, October 1994) for the proposed Zona de Protección de Flora y Fauna Silvestres y Acuáticas [[Sierra San Luis]], a 59,000 ha protected area on the northern edge of the [[Sierra Madre Occidental]] straddling the Sonora–Chihuahua border near Agua Prieta and Janos, Mexico. It was prepared by the [[Centro Ecológico de Sonora]] under the [[Sistema de Áreas Naturales Protegidas del Estado de Sonora]] (SANPES) for review by the area's landowners. The area is zoned into a 14,400 ha Protection and Recovery zone and a 44,600 ha Extensive Use zone. Its stated purpose is to protect water catchment in the northern Río Yaqui basin, maintain Sierra San Luis as a biogeographic corridor, and safeguard rare, threatened, endangered, endemic and game species. The plan is mostly preventive, since the document judges current threats to be relatively small. It sets out four management programs, a landowner-led governance structure, and a financing and planning cycle.

## Background & Description

**Location and extent.** The area lies between 31°05′ and 31°19′ N and 108°40′30″ and 108°58′ W. Elevation ranges from 1,300 to 2,520 m. The main massif, Sierra San Luis, runs northeast for about 25 km and is up to 8 km wide. The area drains to two basins: the [[Río Yaqui]] (western slope, via the Bavispe sub-basin and [[Cajón Bonito]]) and the [[Río Casas Grandes]] (eastern side, with no significant runoff into the reserve).

**Climate and geology.** Lower elevations are semi-arid with mean annual precipitation of about 334.6 mm and mean temperature of 17 °C. Montane zones are temperate to semi-cold, with mean annual temperatures of 10–12 °C and precipitation of about 500 mm, and snowfall above 10 cm is most common in the mountains. Rhyolite and dacitic tuffs of Tertiary age cover more than 70% of the area.

**Soils.** Lithosols dominate (over 70% of the area) and are shallow and easily eroded. Regosols cover about 25%, and xerosols less than 5%.

**Biotic communities.** Following [[Brown and Lowe (1980)]], four communities are recognized: Madrean montane coniferous forest, Madrean evergreen woodland, and two grassland types (plains and Great Basin grassland, and semi-desert grassland). The document also describes vegetation using Cook's (1986) classification: grassland, riparian gallery forest, oak association, pine–juniper woodland, chaparral, and coniferous forest.

**Land use and people.** The area has 22 private properties totalling 59,000 ha. Nine ranches lie wholly within the zone. Cattle ranching on natural grassland is the main economic activity. Palmilla (*Nolina microcarpa*) fiber harvesting is a seasonal, non-timber activity; the document notes that the Agua Prieta region supplies the entire state's production and that about 90% of output goes to the United States. The population density is about one inhabitant per 15.5 km², with six inhabited houses and 16 adults. Services such as schools, health care, electricity and piped water are absent within the zone.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats identified** (the document describes these as relatively minor and mainly preventive):
- Overgrazing, especially in the lower-carrying-capacity oak and coniferous communities, plus trampling of the low vegetation layer where most rare plants occur.
- Loss of vegetation cover from forest fires, with some conifers permanently damaged.
- Introduction of exotic flora and fauna. Eucalyptus, Bermuda grass (*Cynodon dactylon*), watercress (*Roripa nasturtium-aquaticum*), *Oxalis corniculata* and *Galium aparine* were recorded.
- Declining populations of critical species, possibly unrecognized for many taxa.
- Insufficient data on the current status of species and on regional biodiversity, particularly for flora.
- Illegal and irresponsible hunting of deer, black bear and wild turkey.

**Fish and aquatic context.** Nine fish species are recorded: eight native and one introduced. Seven of the eight natives are federally listed, which the document puts at 87.5% of natives. The Río Yaqui basin has seen expansion of exotic fishes, which the document cites as having devastating effects on native populations. Cajón Bonito is described as "one of the most intact desert watercourses in the American Southwest" (Rinne and Minckley, 1991). Fish populations in Sonora are considered in better condition than in parts of the United States. The plan prohibits the introduction of fish permanently.

**Regional context.** The document places Mexico among the megadiverse countries, with about 10% of the world's species, and describes national habitat losses. Sonora has 37 amphibian, 135 reptile, 484 bird and 149 mammal species, and about 153 Mesoamerican-endemic vertebrates.

## Key Findings

**Biodiversity inventory (as reported in the draft):**
- **Flora:** 157 plant species in 49 families. Only [[Juglans major]] (Arizona walnut) holds federal protection. [[Coryphantha robbinsorum]] and [[Talinum marginatum]] are considered rare. Full list in the annex.
- **Fauna:** 395 vertebrate species, of which 70 hold some protection status. Mammals number 76 species in 19 families, and birds number up to 248 species, of which 30 are federally listed. Reptiles number 51 species in 11 families, and 22 are protected. Amphibians number 11 species, and 5 are listed. Fishes number 9 species.
- **Notable taxa:** [[Ursus americanus]] (black bear, endangered, recently verified by CES researchers in 1991 and 1993); [[Canis lupus baileyi]] (Mexican gray wolf, within historic range, with credible border records); [[Panthera onca]] (jaguar, reported); [[Crotalus willardi obscurus]] (a newly found population of this montane rattlesnake, restricted to the massif); [[Rana chiricahuensis]] (Chiricahua leopard frog, threatened, with severe declines in the US); [[Rana yavapaiensis]] (Yavapai frog, rare); and [[Strix occidentalis]] (Mexican spotted owl, threatened by loss of coniferous forest).
- **Fishes:** [[Gila robusta]], [[Agosia chrysogaster]], [[Catostomus bernardini]], [[Cyprinella formosa]] and [[Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis]] are among the native taxa noted. The document notes that [[Ictalurus pricei]] identity is uncertain because of hybridization with channel catfish.

**Riparian habitat:** Gallery forests of Fremont cottonwood (*Populus fremontii*), Exigua willow (*Salix exigua*), Arizona walnut and Arizona cypress are described as the richest habitats and as energy subsidies for the surrounding landscape.

**Management zoning:**
- *Extensive Use zone* (44,600 ha): palmilla harvesting under SARH carrying-capacity volumes, ranching under the state livestock law, minimal new infrastructure, predator control approved by a technical committee, and exotic species introductions only for management purposes. Fish introductions are permanently banned.
- *Protection and Recovery zone* (14,400 ha): no non-domestic forestry or mining, managed annual hunting programs, a ban on exotic species, and limited infrastructure with tourism and recreation as the only new activities allowed.

**Management programs:**
1. **Conservation:** flora and fauna plans (including specific plans for the two rare plants), soil and vegetation restoration (fire contingency plans, erosion control, native grass propagation), and surveillance with landowner participation.
2. **Sustainable use:** ranching (carrying-capacity adjustments, financing for range improvements) and forestry/non-timber use (palmilla quotas, managed white-tailed deer and wild turkey hunting).
3. **Research and monitoring:** biological and socioeconomic monitoring, continuous monitoring of Cajón Bonito for exotic fish, assessment of predator impacts, and future reintroduction studies.
4. **Administration and operation:** a landowners' council, an administrator, annual operating plans (POA), and an accounting system.

**Governance and finance:** A four-tier structure comprises a Governing Body chaired by [[SEDESOL]] through the [[Instituto Nacional de Ecología]], a Landowners' Council, a Technical-Scientific Advisory Council, and the reserve administration. Management is based on [[Community-Based Conservation]]. Start-up funds come from public federal and state environmental programs and from private sources. Long-term financing is expected from landowner-driven range and wildlife activities. The plan identifies the willingness of landowners to participate as the single decisive factor for success.

**Planning cycle:** Formulation (this plan), Implementation (annual POAs), Control and Monitoring (quarterly reports), and Evaluation (financial audits and annual reports).

**Legal basis:** The plan relies on Articles 25–27 of the Mexican Constitution, the [[General Law of Ecological Balance and Environmental Protection]] (LGEEPA, Articles 44–47, 54, 66, 78), and the category "Zona de Protección de Flora y Fauna Silvestres y Acuáticas" (one of nine LGEEPA categories). Protected species status follows [[NOM-059-ECOL-1994]].

**Document note:** The source is a draft under landowner review, not a finalized decree. Several annex lists and taxonomic entries contain OCR-era errors, and some references are incomplete.
