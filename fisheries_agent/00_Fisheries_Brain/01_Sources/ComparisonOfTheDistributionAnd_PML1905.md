# Acclimated vs. Non-Acclimated Razorback Sucker Stocking Success in the Green River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the distribution, recapture rates, and growth of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) stocked into the middle [[Green River]] by two routes: fish acclimated for a full growing season in [[Floodplain Wetlands]] that entered the river voluntarily as subadults, and subadults stocked directly from the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]]. The work was part of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] recovery effort, which relies heavily on hatchery augmentation because wild numbers continue to decline. Authors are from the Colorado River Fish Project (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Vernal, Utah) and the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources.

The main result is that acclimation produced no clear benefit. Distribution and recapture rates of the two groups were nearly identical. The authors suggest that acclimated survival was underestimated, because some fish likely left the floodplains unrecorded. Long-term survival of both groups remains unknown.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a catostomid fish endemic to the Colorado River Basin. It historically occupied alluvial reaches of the mainstem and large tributaries. Its numbers fell sharply after water development and the introduction of nonnative fishes, and it was listed as endangered in 1991 (USFWS 1991). Most fish now in the basin are hatchery products (Mueller 1995; Birchell and Christopherson 2003). Wild numbers in the Green River kept declining after the last propagated-fishes symposium (Schramm and Piper 1994), so augmentation is considered essential for recovery (Nesler et al. 2002).

Earlier stocking had mixed results:
- Large releases of larval and fingerling fish in the lower basin in the early 1980s produced little survival (Minckley et al. 1991).
- Stocking of larger fish in the Verde and Salt rivers also performed poorly. Predation by nonnative fishes and parasites were major mortality sources (Marsh and Brooks 1989; Clarkson et al. 1993).
- More recent stocking of larger fish produced recaptures in the Colorado (Burdick 2003) and San Juan rivers (Ryden and Pfeifer 1996).

The study addresses two questions: how pre-stocking acclimation and size at stocking affect the distribution and survival of hatchery-reared razorback sucker in the Green River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The study covers the Green River from below Split Mountain Canyon to the Colorado River confluence. The abstract gives the upstream limit as river kilometer (rk) 352, while the Study Area section gives rk 516. The reach alternates between low-gradient alluvial sections and confined, high-gradient canyon sections. The alluvial reaches include the section from rk 516 to Sand Wash (rk 350), the entry to [[Desolation Canyon]], and the reach from Green River, Utah (rk 196) to the confluence. Flows are regulated by [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (rk 663) upstream. Spring flows, which run from April through July, are set mainly by the unregulated [[Yampa River]]. Base flows are controlled largely by dam releases (Muth et al. 2000).

**Floodplain sites.** The two acclimation sites were off-channel depressions, Above Brennan (16.5 ha) and Baser Bend (15.5 ha). Both held large numbers of nonnative fishes, notably [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*) and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*).

**Spawning habitat.** The [[Escalante Spawning Bar]] is the primary razorback sucker spawning site in the middle Green River. Most recaptures clustered near rk 504, which the authors describe as a well-documented spawning site.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and acclimation design**
- Roughly 2,000 subadult/adult razorback sucker (>250 mm TL) were stocked directly into the river between 1997 and 2001. Nearly all were stocked at or above rk 516.
- On April 27, 1999, 1,985 yearling fish averaging 106 mm were [[PIT Tagging|PIT-tagged]] and stocked into the two floodplains.
- The floodplain fish averaged 338 mm in April 2000. They gained river access in May 2000 when high spring flows connected the wetlands to the Green River.
- The maximum number of acclimated fish remaining when the floodplains connected to the river was estimated at 2,192.
- Fish stocked from 1986 to 1989 were fin-clipped, and fish stocked afterward were PIT-tagged.

**Sampling**
- Sampling in 2001 and 2002 used three [[Electrofishing]] passes covering both shorelines from Split Mountain Canyon to the confluence. The White River, a major tributary, was also sampled for 170 km.
- Collections targeted the spring ascending hydrograph (March–June).
- Fish without PIT tags were assumed to be floodplain-reared hatchery fish.

**Distribution**
- Most acclimated and non-acclimated fish were captured in the alluvial reach.
- The share collected downstream of the alluvial reach ranged from 25.3% (acclimated fish, 2001) to 8.9% (acclimated fish, 2002, as reported in the OCR text).
- Only four fish were found above rk 516.
- In 2000, [[Chi-Square Test|chi-square]] results showed a difference between groups (χ² = 20.5, 1 df, critical value 3.8), with more acclimated fish in the lower canyon reach.
- In 2002, no difference was detected (χ² = 0.2, 1 df).
- The alluvial/canyon distribution differed among years (χ² = 266.0, 1 df), with fewer stocked fish found in the canyon during the second year.

**Growth**
- The 2000 age-II fish showed the largest growth increments (Table 2).
- No detectable difference in first-year growth increment was found between acclimated and non-acclimated fish.
- Acclimated fish reached a greater total length, but sample sizes were small.

- Recapture rates of acclimated and non-acclimated fish were nearly identical.
- Fish stocked in 1996 had no recaptures in 2001 or 2002.

**Movement**
- Most stocked fish moved downstream. About a quarter of both groups moved nearly 170 km into Desolation Canyon during the first year.
- In a related study, stocked fish moved as far as 70 km in 4 weeks (Mueller et al. 2003). Flow-trained fish moved shorter distances than pond-reared fish.
- Fewer fish from both groups were recaptured below the alluvial reach in the second year, which the authors interpret as conditioning to lotic conditions.

**Size at stocking**
- The authors identify size at stocking as the most influential factor. No recaptures came from the large numbers of juveniles (under 250 mm) stocked in 1987–1990.
- Larger stocked fish survived better in the Colorado and San Juan rivers (Burdick and Bonar 2002; Brandenburg et al. 2003).

**Reproduction**
- Both acclimated and non-acclimated fish were captured at the Escalante spawning bar during the spawning seasons of 2000 and 2001.
- Stocked fish occupied the same spawning locations as wild adults (Bowen et al. 2001).
- Larval razorback sucker in the San Juan River suggest that larger stocked fish can contribute offspring (Brandenburg et al. 2003).

**Conclusions**
- Acclimation in floodplain depressions showed no clear advantage over direct stocking in the middle Green River.
- Acclimated survival may be underestimated because unrecorded losses probably occurred. If so, acclimated fish may have survived better and grown larger than non-acclimated fish.
- Long-term survival is uncertain. No stocked fish were recaptured more than once, and recapture rates for stocked fish, especially non-acclimated fish, fell quickly after stocking.
- The authors conclude that the magnitude of stocked fish's contribution to recovery remains to be determined.
