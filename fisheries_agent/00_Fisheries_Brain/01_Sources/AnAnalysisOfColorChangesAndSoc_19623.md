# Tilapia mossambica: Color Changes and Social Behavior

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 monograph in the *University of California Publications in Zoology* (Vol. 75, No. 1, pp. 1-58) by E. H. Neil presents a quantitative laboratory analysis of how [[Tilapia Mossambica]] (Cichlidae) changes body color in relation to its social and reproductive behavior. Over 1,000 fish were used, mostly reared in groups, with a subset of 74 males raised in isolation. The study links discrete color patterns (for example [[Neutral Coloration]], [[Dark 3 Coloration]] and [[Black Coloration]]) to courtship, territorial and fighting behavior, and argues that color can be used to predict what a fish will do next. The paper also addresses schooling, brood care, the mechanisms of color change, and the function of courtship coloration.

## Background & Description

The species is a member of the family Cichlidae, long known for rapid color change. Earlier work by [[Baerends And Baerends Van Roon]] (1950) and [[Seitz]] (1948) had described its color patterns and behavior in detail, but quantitative analysis of rapid color change in fish had been largely neglected. Neil adopted much of Baerends' behavioral terminology, adding asterisked terms for finer action sequences.

Key descriptive points:

- **Taxonomy and naming:** The species was previously called *T. natalensis* and *T. heudeloti* in some literature; Seitz's "natal perch" also refers to *T. mossambica*. The name "Tilapia" in the paper refers only to this species.
- **Natural range:** East Africa, from North Kenya to South Tanganyika, in quiet waters of small lakes, overflows and swamps. It has been introduced and cultured in Southeast Asia and Polynesia.
- **Sample and husbandry:** More than 1,000 fish from the [[Steinhart Aquarium]] and from Hawaii. Tanks ranged from 5 to 100 gallons, plus a 1,057-gallon tank and an outdoor pond (30 × 15 × 2 ft). Temperature was held at 26-29 °C. Juveniles were fed brine shrimp and Daphnia; adults received Artemia, Gambusia, horsemeat and alfalfa pellets.
- **Pigment system:** Three main chromatophore types: melanophores (rapidly dispersing dark pigment), liphophores (erythrophores and xanthophores) and guanophores (light-reflecting crystals). Melanophore granules can disperse in under one second, though some require up to two days for full dispersion.
- **Method:** Observations were recorded by voice on tape and transcribed in one-second intervals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Schooling.** Young fish formed tight schools from the time they left the mother's mouth. A minimum of ten young was needed for a school in which all individuals were Neutral and unafraid of the observer. Adult schools were looser, partly because courting and fighting males and brooding females disrupted them. Young under 1 cm were eaten by fish over 5 cm; young under 3 cm were eaten by aquarium-raised fish five times their size. Keenleyside (1955) was cited for schools preferring larger groups, a pattern also seen in *Tilapia*.

**Territoriality and cycling.** Nest-holding males dig pits, defend territories and court females. Ownership of territories changed about once a week in the laboratory. In four experiments with 10 males and 6 females per tank over 21 days, the number of territorial males rose slowly, fluctuated in the second week, and stabilized by the third week (often at three males). Neil considered territorial behavior probably cyclic but did not determine its cause.

**Sex and social cues.** Introducing ten females into a tank of males produced 9, 8, 11 and 17 Black males on days 1-4 (versus 0-3 in controls), the only experiment with a significant increase. Visual and chemical cues from females separated by a partition were not sufficient to trigger color change.

**Brood care.** Females held eggs and young in the mouth for about 11-14 days (eggs for 14 days), did not feed during that period and were highly aggressive. In laboratory conditions the mother-young bond weakened after about three weeks.

**Parental and functional comparisons.** Neil compared courtship coloration with other cichlids and fishes. In the field, females outnumbered males about two to one, and resident males were conspicuous, which the author considered a cost outweighed by the benefits of Black courtship coloration.

## Key Findings

1. **Color patterns and behavior.** Color states predict behavior. Dark 1 signals imminent courtship or fighting; Dark 3 is linked to high-intensity fighting; Black and Dark 2 to courtship; and Striped, Barred and Hatched patterns to fleeing or fright. Fin position, body orientation and mouth state added predictive power.

2. **Speed of color change.** Most color changes take seconds (e.g., Striped to Neutral in 3.3-4 s; Neutral to Dark 1 about 14 s in isolates; Dark 2 to Dark 3 about 41 s). Black takes more than 24 hours. Neil concluded these rapid changes were under nervous rather than hormonal control.

3. **Territory establishment.** One of six introduced males established a territory in a 70-gallon tank. Territory and Black coloration were complete by day seven; Banded Black appeared by day four. Males with established territories were Black, spent about 90% of their day in the territory, and pendeled (ritualized threat) with other residents.

4. **Courtship sequence.** Courtship is a chain of partly completed acts that depends on the female's response, including tilting, leading, twitch-leading, tailwagging and rolling. Rolling occurred when the female was near the nest. Tailwagging rates peaked when the male first met the female and when she first entered the nest. Females became motionless before most tailwagging bouts (965 of 1,126 bouts).

5. **Female spawning choice.** Females prefer the territory where they dig longest and where they help defend. Spawning required a male near the pit, and a female did not spawn for two hours behind an opaque partition but spawned within 25 seconds after the partition was lifted. Larger territories favored successful spawning.

6. **Isolate versus community males.** Isolated males (raised from 1-2 cm) showed the same courtship and fighting components as group-raised males, but darkened more slowly and performed more courtship activities per minute (3.7 ± 0.93 vs 2.6 ± 0.41 changes/min). Two isolates reached Banded Black versus six community males.

7. **Fighting and size.** In 18 successful encounters (standard lengths 3.7-5.6 cm), the larger male won in 17. Fish differing by 0.8 cm or more did not fight; fights in the 0-0.2 cm class were continuous and strong. Mouthfighting was the only fighting form that could cause serious injury, with three laboratory fish having jaws dislocated.

8. **Brooding and color.** Brooding females became Hatched when ejecting young, and Striped when isolated. Their color correlated with alarm level; females became Neutral again around the time they were ready to school separately.

9. **Function of courtship coloration.** Courtship color may help recognition by conspecifics, provide contrast for localization at a distance, allow rapid concealment from predators, and divert predators from females (who carry eggs) to expendable males.

10. **Limitations.** Neil reported that the observed courtship color patterns were not fully checked against the later egg-dummy work of [[Wickler]] (1962), and that some correlations (such as Dark 3 courting) were inconsistent with predictions. Further work was planned on field observations.
