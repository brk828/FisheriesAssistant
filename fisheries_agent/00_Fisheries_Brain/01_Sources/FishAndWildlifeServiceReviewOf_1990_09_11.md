# Fish and Wildlife Service Review of Public Notice 90-312-CL for Yuma Cove Native Fish Rearing Facility

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #catostomidae #[[Razorback Sucker]] #conservation-management

## Executive Summary
This document is a 1990 interagency regulatory review letter from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) evaluating a Department of the Army Section 10/404 permit application submitted by the National Park Service (NPS). The proposed action involves raising an existing sand and gravel berm at [[Yuma Cove]] in [[Lake Mohave]] to establish a pilot native fish rearing facility. The primary conservation objective is to protect a spawning and nursery habitat for the aging, wild [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) population by physically excluding invasive/exotic predatory fishes. The FWS, coordinating views across the Department of the Interior, expressed no objection to permit issuance, reflecting broad consensus among the [[Native Fish Work Group]] (AGFD, BR, FWS, NPS).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Target Species:** [[Razorback Sucker]], a large-bodied catostomid endemic to the Colorado River Basin, characterized elsewhere in the literature by a pronounced dorsal keel behind the head.
- **Habitat Context:** [[Lake Mohave]] is a reservoir on the mainstem [[Colorado River]] impounded behind Davis Dam; it supports one of the last significant remnant wild populations of razorback sucker, but the population is aging with little to no natural recruitment.
- **Threat — Exotic Predators:** Reservoir conditions have facilitated establishment of non-native predatory fishes, which are implicated in the failure of larval/juvenile razorback suckers to recruit into the adult population. Wave-driven breaching of protective berms allows these exotic predators to access enclosed cove nursery habitats.
- **Habitat Engineering:** [[Yuma Cove]] functions as a semi-isolated backwater/embayment. An existing sand-and-gravel berm across its mouth partially isolates the cove from the main reservoir, reducing predator ingress and creating favorable conditions for spawning and early-life-stage rearing.
- **Trophic/Community Role:** The project implicitly targets the ecological bottleneck of larval and juvenile survival, addressing predator-driven recruitment failure rather than adult mortality or spawning habitat scarcity per se.

## Key Findings
- **Permit Request:** NPS sought authorization to raise the Yuma Cove berm by 5 feet using approximately 1,575 cubic yards of sand and gravel sourced from an adjacent unvegetated borrow area landward of the berm.
- **Purpose of Berm Modification:** Prevent wave action from breaching the berm during high reservoir elevations, thereby blocking introduction of exotic fish predators into the enclosed native fish rearing area.
- **Pilot Project Framing:** The action is explicitly identified as a pilot study to test the effectiveness of using isolated cove habitats to spawn and grow resident razorback suckers, with the goal of recruiting young fish into the aging Lake Mohave population.
- **Interagency Collaboration:** The project is a joint initiative of the [[Native Fish Work Group]], comprising the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), Bureau of Reclamation (BR), U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS), and National Park Service (NPS).
- **Regulatory Outcome:** Following pre-application meetings and onsite inspections, the Department of the Interior (via FWS) raised no objections and supported permit issuance under the Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act (16 U.S.C. 661 et seq.).
- **Scientific Consultation:** Dr. W.L. Minckley and Dr. P. Marsh of Arizona State University — recognized authorities on Colorado River native fishes — were copied on the correspondence, indicating their advisory involvement in the [[Razorback Sucker]] recovery effort.
- **Broader Conservation Context:** This action is part of a larger multi-agency strategy to recover the razorback sucker, a species of significant conservation concern in the [[Colorado River Basin]], later listed as endangered under the U.S. Endangered Species Act (1991).
