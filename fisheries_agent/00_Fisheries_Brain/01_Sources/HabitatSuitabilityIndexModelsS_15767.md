# Habitat Suitability Index Models: Smallmouth Buffalo

#ichthyology #freshwater-fish #catostomidae #habitat-modeling #reservoir-management

## Executive Summary

This technical report presents Habitat Suitability Index (HSI) models for the [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*), a large freshwater fish native to the [[Mississippi Valley]] and [[Hudson Bay]] drainage systems. The models synthesize literature on habitat requirements and preferences to produce quantitative indices scaled from 0 (unsuitable) to 1 (optimal habitat). Two distinct models are provided: one for riverine environments and one for lacustrine (reservoir/lake) habitats. The models incorporate variables related to food availability, cover, water quality (temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, turbidity), reproduction requirements, and habitat structure. These tools are designed for impact assessment and habitat management activities, enabling resource managers to evaluate habitat quality and predict potential carrying capacity for [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] populations across different life stages.

## Background & Description

The [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*) is a member of the sucker family ([[Catostomidae]]) with a native range extending from the western [[Hudson Bay]] drainage in Canada southward through the [[Mississippi Valley]] to the Gulf States and northeastern Mexico. The species has been introduced west of the Continental Divide in Arizona and has been reported in the [[Missouri River]] drainage in Montana and South Dakota. Hybridization with [[Black Buffalo]] (*I. niger*) and possible hybridization with [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*I. cyprinellus*) has been documented.

Adults typically reach 1-8 kg in weight and 38-78 cm in length, though individuals may attain approximately 18 kg. Maturity is size-dependent rather than age-dependent, occurring at approximately 450 g body weight, which corresponds to age II in Arkansas ponds and age VII-X in [[Lewis and Clark Reservoir]], South Dakota. The species is an opportunistic bottom feeder; young consume primarily [[Cladocerans]] and [[Copepods]], while adults feed on abundant organisms including zooplankton, algae, and insect larvae. In reservoirs, feeding occurs primarily in shallow shoreline areas, with better growth observed over mud flats and shallow waters compared to rocky, deep areas due to increased abundance of benthic organisms and attached algae.

[[Smallmouth Buffalo]] typically inhabit large rivers, preferring deep, clear, warm waters with current. They characteristically occupy firm-bottomed channels, chutes, and cut-off areas of medium to large-sized rivers, frequenting low-velocity areas such as pools, creekmouths, and backwaters. The species also thrives in large reservoirs and lakes, particularly in fairly shallow water (1.2-1.4 m) with abundant aquatic or inundated terrestrial vegetation and silt bottoms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riverine Habitats**: In river systems, [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] demonstrate strong associations with specific habitat features. Adults utilize both main channel areas with moderate to strong currents (up to 100 cm/sec) and low-velocity refugia (< 20 cm/sec) in pools and backwaters. The species can inhabit high-velocity areas up to 160 cm/sec for short periods but requires pools and backwaters for resting and nursery functions. Growth is enhanced in moderately turbid to clear waters (25-100 JTU) compared to highly turbid conditions (> 100 JTU).

**Lacustrine Habitats**: In reservoirs and lakes, [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] distributions are strongly bottom-associated, with relatively uniform densities at all depths along the substrate. Standing crop increases as storage ratio (SR) decreases—the ratio of mean reservoir water volume to annual discharge volume. There is seasonal movement toward shallower water in spring and summer and deeper water in fall and winter, though no heavy seasonal concentrations occur except at spawning areas. The species performs well in upstream headwaters of reservoirs, adjoining inflowing streams, or areas with moderate current.

**Water Quality Requirements**: Optimal pH ranges from 6.5-8.5, with populations apparently unaffected up to pH 8.5 in southwestern reservoirs and rivers. Moderately warm temperatures are necessary for growth and reproduction. In 90% of streams sampled along the Texas coast and Mississippi Valley containing [[Smallmouth Buffalo]], weekly mean summer temperatures ranged from 17-32°C with an overall mean of 24°C. Seasonal growth begins at 18°C. While specific dissolved oxygen requirements are unknown, levels ≥ 5.0 mg/l are considered minimum for maintaining populations, with optimal levels > 6 mg/l. The species can tolerate turbid waters but exhibits better growth in clearer conditions.

**Salinity Tolerance**: [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] can tolerate relatively saline conditions. Laboratory studies on [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] juveniles showed tolerance up to 12 ppt for short periods, and [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] are assumed to have similar tolerances. Normal reservoir salinity levels range from 2,200 ppm to 20 ppm (2.2 to 0.02 ppt) total dissolved solids (TDS), with increased TDS correlating with increased standing crops.

**Reproduction**: Spawning is initiated by rising water levels and increasing temperatures, occurring during spring and summer (March-September) when temperatures range from 13-28°C, with optimum temperatures of 17-24°C. The maximum weekly average temperature for spawning is 21°C. Incubation requires 4-12 days at 14-21°C. Adhesive eggs are scattered over the bottom and left unattended. In rivers, spawning occurs in areas with very little current (< 20 cm/sec) such as backwaters, marshes, and pools. In reservoirs, spawning occurs in embayments and along recently flooded shorelines at depths of approximately 1.2-6.1 m. While spawning occurs over virtually any substrate, the species shows preference for vegetation (aquatic and inundated terrestrial) and submerged objects when available.

**Early Life Stages**: Warm, shallow, vegetated tributary embayments of reservoirs and quiet pools, marshes, and backwaters (< 20 cm/sec velocity) of rivers provide optimum conditions for fry survival and growth. Juveniles were collected almost exclusively at velocities < 20 cm/sec in pools or marsh and backwater areas. Temperature requirements for fry are assumed slightly more restricted than for adults, with maximum growth occurring at the warmer end of the 16-32°C range observed in occupied habitats.

## Key Findings

**HSI Model Structure**: Two comprehensive HSI models are presented—one for riverine habitats and one for lacustrine habitats. Both utilize a life requisite approach with four components: food-cover, water quality, reproduction, and "other" factors. The models incorporate 17 variables total, with some variables shared between models and others specific to habitat type.

**Riverine Model Variables** (13 variables):
- Food-cover component: percent vegetative cover (V₁₅), percent pools and off-channel areas (V₂)
- Water quality component: temperature for adults/juveniles/fry (V₅, V₇), dissolved oxygen for adults/juveniles/fry (V₈), pH (V₄), turbidity (V₃), optional salinity (V₁₂)
- Reproduction component: percent vegetative cover (V₁₅), percent pools and off-channel areas (V₂), pool velocity (V₁₁), dissolved oxygen for embryos (V₉), temperature for embryos (V₆)
- Other component: average stream width (V₁), average current velocity (V₁₀)

**Lacustrine Model Variables** (13 variables):
- Food-cover component: percent vegetative cover (V₁₅), percent littoral area (V₁₆)
- Water quality component: same as riverine model
- Reproduction component: dissolved oxygen for embryos (V₉), temperature for embryos (V₆), substrate type (V₁₄), percent vegetative cover (V₁₅), percent littoral area (V₁₆), percent area 1.2-6.1 m deep during spawning (V₁₇)
- Other component: storage ratio (V₁₃)

**Model Equations**: The riverine HSI is calculated as: HSI = (C_FC × C_WQ × C_R × C_OT)^(1/5), where components are calculated from geometric and arithmetic means of constituent variables. If any component < 0.4, the HSI equals the lowest component or the equation result. The lacustrine HSI uses: HSI = (C_FC × C_WQ × C_R² × C_OT)^(1/6), with similar conditional logic for low component values.

**Suitability Index Curves**: Detailed suitability index graphs are provided for all 17 variables, showing relationships between measured values and habitat suitability (0-1 scale). For example:
- Stream width: optimal at ≥ 32 m, declining to SI = 0.5 at 7 m
- Percent pools/off-channel areas: optimal at 15-20%, declining below 10% and above 30%
- Adult temperature: optimal at 22-26°C, declining outside 17-32°C range
- Dissolved oxygen (adults): optimal at ≥ 7 mg/l, declining sharply below 5 mg/l
- Turbidity: optimal at < 50 JTU, declining above 100 JTU
- Storage ratio: optimal at 0.1-0.25, declining as SR increases

**Model Validation**: Sample data sets demonstrate model performance across habitat quality gradients. For riverine habitats, HSI values of 0.89, 0.67, and 0.29 were calculated for high, moderate, and poor quality habitats respectively. For lacustrine habitats, HSI values of 0.79, 0.57, and 0.00 reflected similar quality gradients. The zero HSI in the third lacustrine example resulted from unsuitable embryo temperature (28°C), demonstrating the model's ability to identify reproductive bottlenecks.

**Simplified Models**: Two additional simplified models are provided for rapid assessment:
- Model 1 (Riverine): HSI = (number of criteria met) / 5, where criteria are: large/medium river (> 5 m width), current < 100 cm/sec, warm summer temperatures (22-26°C), clear waters (< 25 JTU), vegetated shallow areas for spawning
- Model 2 (Lacustrine): HSI = (number of criteria met) / 4, where criteria are: large reservoir, warm summer temperatures (22-26°C), clear waters (< 25 JTU), vegetated littoral zones

**Model Interpretation and Limitations**: The HSI does not directly represent population abundance; habitats with HSI = 0 may contain some [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] and high-HSI habitats may have few individuals due to factors beyond habitat quality (e.g., fishing pressure, stocking history, barriers to colonization). The proper interpretation is comparative: higher HSI indicates greater potential to support [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] populations over the long term, assuming habitat-related factors are primary determinants of population levels. The model has not been field-validated but was designed to meet the acceptance goal of producing indices positively related to spawning success and carrying capacity.

**Geographic Applicability**: The model applies throughout the native and introduced range in North America. However, the standard of comparison for each variable is the optimum value occurring anywhere within this region. Therefore, northern waters may never achieve HSI = 1.0 if temperature-related variables cannot reach optimal values found in southern portions of the range.

**Management Applications**: These models are designed for impact assessment and habitat management, enabling quantitative comparison of alternative management scenarios, evaluation of habitat restoration potential, and prediction of population responses to environmental changes. Model users are encouraged to modify variables and equations to meet specific study objectives, with guidance provided in Appendix A for reducing model complexity and measuring variables. The models represent complex hypotheses of species-habitat relationships rather than proven cause-and-effect relationships, and field validation is recommended before application to management decisions.
