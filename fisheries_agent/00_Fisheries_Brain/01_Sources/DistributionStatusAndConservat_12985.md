# Pecos Pupfish (Cyprinodon pecosensis) Conservation Status and Distribution Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This New Mexico Department of Game and Fish technical report (Hoagstrom and Brooks, 1998; Tech. Rpt. No. 2) reviews the distribution, status, and conservation of the [[Pecos Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon pecosensis*) across the Pecos River Basin of New Mexico and Texas. Sampling of all known populations was conducted between 1987 and 1998 using seining, [[Minnow Trapping]], and dip netting. The authors conclude that habitat loss from groundwater depletion, flow regulation, saltcedar invasion, and channel degradation caused the decline of the species, and that [[Hybridization With Sheepshead Minnow]] has greatly reduced the range of genetically pure populations. Only two populations remain: a relatively large population in Chaves County, New Mexico, and a single genetically pure population in [[Salt Creek Texas]]. The document is a technical report with appendices of locality data, not a proposal or budget.

## Background & Description

The Pecos pupfish likely originated during the middle-late Pleistocene and may have evolved from an ancestor similar to the [[Sheepshead Minnow]] (*C. variegatus*) after isolation of the Pecos River from the Gulf of Mexico. Related species include the [[Red River Pupfish]] (*C. rubrofluviatilis*), the [[Leon Springs Pupfish]] (*C. bovinus*), and the [[White Sands Pupfish]] (*C. tularosa*).

Morphology (after Echelle and Echelle, 1978):
- Generally small (<75 mm SL), deep bodied, with flattened heads.
- Silver-white body with brown-gray vertical bars; bars broken into blotches ventrolaterally in females.
- Upturned mouth, single dorsal fin, no lateral line, abdominal pelvic fins, cycloid scales.
- Males have a highly arched back and often black on the margin of the dorsal and anal fins.
- Typically 3 to 4 pre-orbital pores per side and 20 to 21 gill rakers.

Ecology and behavior:
- Tolerant of high temperature, high salinity, and low dissolved oxygen.
- Omnivorous bottom feeders; diet mainly diatoms, animal material, filamentous algae, and macrophytes (Davis, 1981).
- Reproductive traits vary with density; females in dense populations have smaller ovaries and fewer eggs per clutch (Garret, 1982).
- Males defend breeding territories, and smaller males breed as satellites or sneakers (Kodric-Brown, 1977; 1986).
- Male mortality is eight times greater than female mortality due to reproductive competition (Kodric-Brown, 1987).
- Breeding was more often disrupted by [[Plains Killifish]] at 19.5 ppt than at 29.6 ppt (Kodric-Brown and Mazzolini, 1992).

Regulatory context: The species was proposed for listing as endangered (Federal Register, 50 CFR Part 17).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and geology.** The Pecos River changes character from a high-elevation upland stream in the Sangre de Cristo headwaters to a high plains stream, then passes through the [[Roswell Basin]] (subsidence by dissolution, artesian [[San Andres Limestone]] aquifer, numerous sinks, lakes, and marshes), the [[Carlsbad Valley]] (Capitan limestone and Carlsbad Aquifer), the [[Delaware Basin]] (Rustler Formation, saline discharge, Nash Draw brine aquifer), the [[Toyah Basin]] (largest alluvial groundwater source in the Delaware Basin), and the [[Central Basin Platform]] before reaching the Edwards-Stockton Plateau near Iraan, Texas.

**Historic drivers of habitat loss:**
- Groundwater depletion, especially between 1910 and 1960 in the Roswell Basin and Carlsbad Valley; the Roswell artesian aquifer lost artesian pressure by 1953 (Thomas, 1959).
- Flow regulation by Sumner Dam (1937), Santa Rosa Dam (1980), and Brantley Reservoir (1989), with Red Bluff Reservoir capturing flows leaving New Mexico.
- Saltcedar (*Tamarix* spp.; introduced ca. 1912), which may lower the water table through evapotranspiration and increase channel incision.
- Oil field pollution, potash waste disposal, and periodic toxic algal blooms (*Prymnesium parvum*) causing fish kills.
- Loss of Pecos River flood flows that connected off-channel habitats.

**Water quality context.** Pupfish were often the sole species in isolated habitats above 20 ppt salinity, but were stunted and at risk of desiccation there. In springfed habitats below 20 ppt, they coexisted with other fishes and were in robust condition. Salt Creek, New Mexico, declined to 200 ppt salinity on 2 August 1994 after saltcedar ash entered the pool following a range fire, and pupfish returned by 29 September 1994 at 32 ppt.

**Wetland and sinkhole ecology.** Habitats on [[Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge]] (BLNWR), [[Bottomless Lakes State Park]] (BLSP), and the BLM Overflow Wetland (BLMOW) support Chara, Ruppia, Potamogeton, Typha, Scirpus, and Spartina. Pecos pupfish commonly coexisted with [[Plains Killifish]], [[Pecos Gambusia]] (*Gambusia nobilis*), and other fishes in sinkholes below 20 ppt.

## Key Findings

**Historic and current distribution**
- Historically, Pecos pupfish were widespread in the Roswell Basin and the Delaware Basin of New Mexico and Texas.
- Pecos pupfish are now present along roughly 70 km of the Pecos River valley, compared with roughly 750 km formerly.
- The oldest collections are from the upper Rio Hondo drainage near Hondo, NM (April 1916), suggesting a historic range beyond the floodplain if these were natural populations.
- Pecos pupfish were formerly in Laguna Grande de la Sal (Pupfish Spring), which ceased flowing between 1985 and 1990. The last recorded fish were on 18 July 1995 at 89.1 ppt; none were found on 5 June 1997 (92.8 ppt) or 22 October 1998 (95.7 ppt), and the population was presumed extirpated.
- Pecos pupfish in the late 1980s inhabited the Phipps gravel pits near Grandfalls, TX; by spring 1998 they were genetically contaminated by sheepshead minnow.

**Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge sinkholes**
- Pecos pupfish occupied 23 middle tract and three north tract sinkholes.
- The highest salinity recorded in an occupied sinkhole was 86.0 ppt (sinkhole 21, 19 June 1995).
- Sinkhole 2 received about 350 and sinkhole 3 about 490 reintroduced fish on 20 June 1995.
- Lake Saint Francis (sinkhole 37) provided fish for reintroduction at Inkpot and Little Inkpot in June 1996.

**Hybridization**
- Sheepshead minnow became established in the Pecos River, TX, between 1980 and 1984.
- By 1985, pupfish and sheepshead minnow formed a panmictic hybrid swarm between Red Bluff Reservoir and Independence Creek.
- Genomes of pupfish from 15 localities sampled in 1984 were 18 to 84% sheepshead minnow, depending on locality.
- Hybrid pupfish were first collected upstream of Red Bluff Reservoir in July 1994, and all pupfish collected downstream of Brantley Dam after 1994 were hybrids.
- Hybrids expanded into riffles and fast runs not typically occupied by pure Pecos pupfish.

**Salt Creek, Texas**
- Salt Creek, TX, contains the only known population of genetically pure Pecos pupfish outside Chaves County, NM; a June 1991 collection was free of contamination, and 1998 collections were also pure.
- The population is described as heavily imperiled and a high conservation priority.

**Population dynamics**
- Male Pecos pupfish mortality is eight times greater than female mortality (Kodric-Brown, 1987).
- Salinity above 35 ppt retards egg and larval development (Kinne and Kinne, 1962).

**Conservation recommendations**
- Short term: protect Pecos River floodplain and tributary habitat, monitor Salt Creek discharge, water quality, and pupfish status, and control saltcedar and erosion.
- Short term: monitor for hybrids throughout the Pecos Basin; if hybrids are restricted to the Pierce Canyon confluence, chemical elimination is a possibility; maintain barriers to hybrid dispersal into Salt Creek.
- Long term: regulate groundwater pumping in Chaves County (begun 1966, which reversed habitat loss), regulate drilling and pipelines near BLNWR/BLSP, restore channel slope and riparian areas, and control non-native fishes, especially bait-bucket introductions of sheepshead minnow, inland silverside, and gulf killifish.
- Establish hatchery stocks for Salt Creek if that population declines.

**Conclusions**
1. The Chaves County population remains on public lands in the Roswell vicinity, while downstream wetlands are unprotected. The Salt Creek, TX population is the last outside Chaves County and is not formally protected.
2. Habitat loss and degradation caused the decline and restrict recovery.
3. Genetic contamination has greatly reduced the range of pure Pecos pupfish and threatens remaining populations.
4. Habitat protection requires groundwater regulation, contamination control, river channel restoration, saltcedar control, proper range management, and protection of occupied habitats.
