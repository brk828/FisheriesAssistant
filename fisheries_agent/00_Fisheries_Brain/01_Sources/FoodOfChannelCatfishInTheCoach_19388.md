# Food of Channel Catfish in the Coachella Canal, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the diet of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*, 75–415 mm total length) collected in the earth-lined [[Coachella Canal]] in November 1980 during a [[Rotenone Sampling]] survey. Stomach contents from 153 specimens showed that the fish fed mainly on aquatic insects, especially the hydropsychid caddisfly [[Smicridea utico]], with odonates, [[Chironomidae]] midges, filamentous algae, and aquatic moth larvae (*Parargyractis confusalis*) also important. The author describes the feeding as omnivorous and opportunistic. The reported occurrence of Lepidoptera larvae in catfish diets is described as previously unreported for this species.

## Background & Description

Channel catfish were introduced into the lower [[Colorado River]] in 1892–1893 and were common by the early 1940s. They now occur throughout the river system, including hundreds of kilometers of artificial waterways, and support an important sport fishery. Food habit studies across North America (reviewed by [[Miller 1966]] and [[Carlander 1969]]) describe the species as generally omnivorous: young fish eat mainly benthic insects and other arthropods, while larger individuals become increasingly piscivorous.

The study was supported by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Boulder City, Nevada) and assisted by the California Department of Fish and Game. The author is affiliated with Arizona State University's Center for Environmental Studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and sampling.** Fish were collected at three stations in the earth-lined Coachella Canal, Imperial County:
- **Station A:** pool-like area and straight reach immediately below a concrete check-drop structure.
- **Station B:** linear reach with no structures.
- **Station C:** double-barreled concrete box siphon.

**Methods.** Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin, measured for total length, and weighed. Stomach contents were identified at 10X magnification, enumerated, and volumetrically estimated. Diets were analyzed for size groups (<100, 100–149, 150–199, 200–299, >299 mm TL). Intestinal contents from 20 fish were examined qualitatively for comparison.

**Prey context.** The author attributes the dominance of caddisflies and Lepidoptera larvae to their abundance on the concrete surfaces of the lined canal, citing [[Marsh and Steinmetz 1981]] on canal benthic invertebrates. [[Threadfin Shad]] were abundant, particularly at Station A, but were not a major prey item. The [[Asiatic Clam]] was the most abundant benthic invertebrate on natural substrates but was rarely eaten by catfish in this canal.

**Lining of canals.** The author notes that conversion of earthen canals to concrete may increase populations of net-spinning caddisflies and aquatic Lepidoptera, which could in turn increase channel catfish populations and enhance recreational fisheries in artificial waterways.

## Key Findings

- **Sample:** 153 specimens were examined (151 with stomach data in Table 1). Fish ranged from 75–415 mm TL (mean 161 mm) and 4.12–533.24 g (mean 46.66 g). Only two stomachs were empty.
- **Station differences:** Largest fish were at Station A (mean 168 mm, 52.79 g), smallest at Station B (mean 105 mm, 13.20 g), intermediate at Station C (mean 129 mm, 18.16 g).
- **Dominant prey:** *Smicridea utico* occurred in over 99% of stomachs, averaged 131 individuals per stomach, and made up 62.2% of overall biomass. One 323 mm fish contained 646 individuals. Roughly 20,000 caddisflies were counted in total, almost all larvae.
- **Lepidoptera:** *Parargyractis confusalis* larvae occurred in almost 62% of stomachs and made up 1.2% of biomass. Frequency was about 50% in fish under 150 mm and 80–89% in larger fish.
- **Chironomidae:** Found in more than 70% of stomachs; occurrence near 80% at Station A but 33% and 20% at Stations B and C. Contributed under 1% of biomass.
- **Odonata:** Mostly damselflies (*Hetaerina*); occurred in about 64% of stomachs and contributed nearly 16% of biomass, mostly at Station A. Larger fish consumed larger odonates, a relationship the author says is unique among the insects examined.
- **Other insects:** *Nectopsyche* sp. occurred in more than 26% of stomachs; *Baetis* sp. in about 18%, with low mean numbers (0.15–0.43 per stomach).
- **Non-insect prey:** Fish made up only 3% of the diet. Two Station A catfish contained threadfin shad, one with two whole shad comprising 77% of its gut contents. Crayfish (*Procambarus clarki*) occurred in about 8% of stomachs. Filamentous alga *Cladophora glomerata* occurred in about 10% of Station A fish and made up nearly 17% of biomass; in fish over 300 mm, algae occurred in 88% of guts and contributed 54% of biomass.
- **Clams:** Asiatic clam shell fragments were found in one intestine only.
- **Comparison:** Frequency values agree with Dill (1944) for another California canal. The author reports that Lepidoptera occurrence has not been documented in other North American fish diets reviewed by Carlander (1969, 1977).
- **Conclusion:** The author concludes that channel catfish are omnivorous and opportunistic, and that canal lining may increase prey and fisheries value.
