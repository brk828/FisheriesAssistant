# Radio Transmitters May Adversely Affect Adult Bluegills

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] information bulletin reports a controlled experiment testing whether implanted [[Radio Telemetry]] transmitters harm adult [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*). The authors question the common assumption that radio-tagged fish are not adversely affected, noting that transmitters implanted in the small peritoneal cavities of laterally compressed panfishes may pose a particular risk. Tagged and control fish were held for 8 weeks at a cold (6 °C) or warm (20 °C) temperature. Tagged fish at 20 °C suffered deaths, transmitter loss, weight loss and tissue damage. The bulletin concludes that telemetry data on panfishes, including [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), should be interpreted with caution.

## Background & Description

- **Study taxa:** [[Bluegill]] and [[Black Crappie]], both laterally compressed panfishes.
- **Motivation:** [[Radio Telemetry]] is widely used to study fish movement and habitat use. Researchers generally assume tagged fish are unaffected, but this may not hold when transmitters are placed in the small peritoneal cavity.
- **Study animals:** 20 adult bluegills per group (mean weight 126 g), in four groups:
  - radio-tagged at 6 °C
  - radio-tagged at 20 °C
  - controls (no transmitter) at 6 °C
  - controls at 20 °C
- **Surgical method:** Fish were anesthetized and weighed. A transmitter was implanted into the peritoneal cavity through an anterior-posterior incision on the lateral surface. The incision was closed with 4-0 nonabsorbable nylon suture. Control fish were anesthetized, handled the same way, and returned to their tanks.
- **Document type:** An internal [[Research Information Bulletin]]. These are not peer reviewed and may not be cited. Use of trade names does not imply government endorsement.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature as a stressor:** Two holding temperatures were compared, 6 °C and 20 °C. Warmer water was associated with more severe surgical complications and mortality.
- **Infection risk:** Saprolegnia fungus was found in both tagged and control fish at 6 °C but not at 20 °C. Healing in cold water was limited mainly to an epithelial covering, which the authors suggest leaves tagged fish in cold water vulnerable to microbial infection.
- **Sampling implications:** Fish deaths and transmitter loss at higher temperatures could substantially reduce sample sizes in telemetry studies.

## Key Findings

**Mortality, tag loss and growth (Table, 8 weeks, n = 20 per group):**
- Death and transmitter loss occurred only in radio-tagged fish held at 20 °C. None occurred in tagged fish at 6 °C.
- Percent weight loss was significantly higher in tagged than in control fish at both temperatures.
- Weight loss in tagged fish at 20 °C (−10.3%, SD 9.6) was significantly greater than in tagged fish at 6 °C (−4.1%, SD 1.2; OCR-uncertain value).
- Control fish gained or lost little weight: 20 °C controls +0.7% (SD 15.5) and 6 °C controls −2.3% (SD 2.3).
- Mortality, tag-loss and per-cell values in the table are partly illegible in the scanned source, so only the figures stated in the text are reported here.

**Gross pathology (tagged fish at 20 °C only):**
- Pelvic fin erosion
- Hemorrhaging and necrosis at the antenna exit
- Hemorrhaging and necrosis at the suture insertions
- Encapsulation of the transmitter
- Lost and loose sutures

**Histology and healing (four weeks post-surgery):**
- At 6 °C, tagged fish had an intact epithelial covering over the incision, with minimal further healing.
- At 20 °C, healing was more advanced and included dermal ingrowth and collagen deposition.
- Susceptibility to bacterial infection did not differ among the four treatment groups.

**Recommendations and conclusions:**
- Nylon sutures may be inadequate in warm water. The authors suggest an alternative suture material to prevent hemorrhaging, suture loss and transmitter loss.
- Telemetry data on bluegills and other panfishes, including [[Black Crappie]], should be interpreted with caution, and holding temperature should be considered in study design.
