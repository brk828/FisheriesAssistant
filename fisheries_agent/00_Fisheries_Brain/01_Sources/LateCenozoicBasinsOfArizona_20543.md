# Late Cenozoic Basins of Arizona

#geology #basin-and-range #cenozoic-basins

## Executive Summary

This field-guide paper by [[Robert B. Scarborough]] and [[H. Wesley Peirce]] (Arizona Bureau of Geology and Mineral Technology, Tucson) describes the late Cenozoic sedimentary basins of Arizona and the tectonic event that created them. The authors argue that the modern valleys of southern Arizona owe their positions to late Miocene basins formed by the [[Basin and Range Disturbance]], and that these basins were once partly closed drainage systems that later integrated into today's exterior drainage network.

The document is a geological and tectonic study. It does not address fish, other aquatic fauna, or aquatic ecosystems as such. Its aquatic relevance is limited to paleo-lake and playa deposits, evaporite sequences, and the reconstructed drainage history of ancient basins.

## Background & Description

The paper distinguishes a **basin**, a past center of sediment deposition or trapping, from a **valley** (bolson), an extant topographic low. Most modern southern Arizona valleys are spatially tied to late Cenozoic basins, but valleys are more extensive because of pedimentation (erosion) of adjacent mountain blocks.

The authors propose a restricted definition of the term "Basin and Range disturbance." They argue that it should describe the late Miocene normal-faulting event that produced block-faulted physiography, rather than being applied to earlier Cenozoic tectonic episodes. They prefer "disturbance" over "orogeny" because the event lacked significant igneous activity or regional compression. "Basin fill" refers to sediments deposited in basins created by this event, excluding deposits from modern integrated stream systems, which are generally coarser and no older than Pleistocene.

The paper describes four Cenozoic events for Arizona:

- **(a)** Eocene quiescence, with a low-relief erosion surface produced by subaerial erosion.
- **(b)** Massive late Oligocene–early Miocene calc-alkaline volcanism, plutonism, and associated sedimentation.
- **(c)** The Basin and Range disturbance and associated late Miocene–Pliocene basin filling.
- **(d)** Maximum basin filling in the middle Pleistocene, followed by stream downcutting, terrace development, and valley unloading along major rivers.

### Key Volcanic and Tectonic Timing

- Mid-Tertiary volcanic centers in southern Arizona include the Chiricahua Mountains volcanics (28–24 m.y.), the Galiuro-Winchester pile (29–22 m.y.), the Superstition Mountains silicic center (29–15 m.y.), and the White Mountains complex (36–20 m.y.), part of the larger [[Mogollon-Datil Group]].
- Sediments of the 32–20 m.y. interval are often 5,000–10,000 ft thick and consist of fanglomerates, fluvial overbank deposits, and lacustrine beds.
- Tectonically disturbed fanglomerates dated to 20–13 m.y. include the Rillito beds, the Big Dome Formation (17–14 m.y., K/Ar), and the Nogales Formation.
- The tectonic regime changed abruptly between 15 and 10 m.y. ago. An east-west directed regional extensional field produced north-south faulting, accompanied by minor alkali flood basalts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The basin-fill environments described here are of interest to aquatic and paleoenvironmental researchers:

- **Fanglomerate and piedmont facies:** Bedded cobble conglomerates with fluvial sand lenses and debris-flow deposits, with depositional dips of 3–7 degrees. Facies boundaries with valley-center deposits are generally confined within about 600 ft.
- **Valley-center facies:** Fluvial overbank deposits of sandy silts and silty sands, with clay beds, gypsum beds (the locale of Arizona's principal gypsum sources), volcanic ash, and local [[Diatomite]] deposits with marginally recoverable quantities.
- **Lacustrine and marly deposits:** Marly limestones near San Carlos and green and red silty clays near Benson and St. David, interpreted as lacustrine to swampy settings.
- **Fauna in deposits:** Scattered large mammal footprints and bone concentrations occur throughout the basin fill, suggesting that lakes were not very deep. Diatoms from saline inland playas are also reported. No fish remains are mentioned.
- **Evaporite environments:** The depositional setting of the thick evaporites is unknown, although the authors consider it likely that large volumes of water were involved.
- **Summary environment:** The fine-grained facies are described as "a series of local shallow playas or salt lake basins separated by large fan deposits," and as shallow, slightly saline lacustrine conditions with occasional sluggish flow through valleys.

### Basin Drainage History

The authors infer that basins were connected by spillways over bedrock shoulders during basin-fill time. Stream systems delivered dissolved minerals to a central Arizona depocenter, the [[Gila Low]], where they precipitated as evaporites. Sluggish streams deposited mudstone, siltstone, and minor evaporites in valley segments. The drainage system evolved from a regionally closed network to today's moderately well integrated, exterior system.

## Key Findings

### Deep Drillhole Data

Fewer than ten Arizona basins had been penetrated by deep drillholes at the time of writing. Summary data for these sites are given in the paper's Table 1, with basin-fill thicknesses, elevations, and estimated faulting separations.

- **[[Elfrida Basin]]** (Sulphur Springs Valley): Drilled to 5,450 ft without full penetration. Basin fill is 3,340 ft thick, ranging from +660 ft to +4,000 ft elevation. This is the highest known basin in Arizona.
- **[[Tucson Basin]]:** Drillhole reached 12,000 ft of inferred Cenozoic deposits, including about 6,050 ft of basin fill. A volcanic section has a K/Ar drillchip date of 11.6 m.y., suggesting the Basin and Range faulting postdates 11.6 m.y. here. The authors conclude that the onset in southern Arizona postdates 12 m.y.
- **[[Picacho Basin]]:** Drillhole penetrated 9,880 ft of Cenozoic sediments above metamorphic rock dated at 26 m.y. (K/Ar). It contains about 5,985 ft of bedded anhydrite, the thickest known evaporite sequence in Arizona, possibly the thickest anhydrite sequence in the world. Its floor lies at 6,740 ft below sea level, the lowest basin floor known in the state.
- **[[Luke Basin]]** (near Phoenix): Contains at least 3,600 ft of halite capped by 80 ft of anhydrite. The salt apparently upwelled into a basalt flow dated at 10 m.y.
- **[[Red Lake Basin]]** (northwest Arizona): Contains 4,000 ft of halite in one drillhole, with possible halite volume exceeding 100 mi³.
- **[[Safford Basin]]:** A drillhole found at least 600 ft of bedded gypsum below an elevation of 330 ft above sea level. The full evaporite section remains untested.
- **[[Willcox Playa]]:** Still a closed basin, but not drilled to great depth near its center.

### Halite Volume Estimates

Eaton and others (1972) suggested that 15 to 30 mi³ of nonmarine halite could have been deposited in the Luke basin. The authors note that the modern Gila, Salt, and Verde River inputs of NaCl could have deposited this in roughly 20,000 years.

### Tectonic Relief and Stratigraphic Throw

- Vertical movement from faulting generally exceeds 6,000 ft and ranges up to more than 11,000 ft across the sampled basins.
- Stratigraphic separation of about 20,000 ft is measured between the southern edge of the Colorado Plateau and the Higley basin, about 60 mi away. A modest regional gradient could double this value.
- Cumulative throws could offset pre-faulting units by 30,000 ft or more, measured from the Colorado Plateau edge to the lowest basin floors in the Gila Low.
- Basin floors may have subsided relative to sea level. A Humble Oil (now Exxon) test well 20 mi southwest of Yuma reached a "marine late Miocene" base at 6,800 ft below sea level.

### Drainage Evolution

Spatial patterns of basin-fill deposits closely parallel elements of today's drainage. Evaporite-bearing basins in the Lake Mead–Red Lake–Detrital valley area in the northwest share nearly equal top elevations, suggesting interbasin connection. The authors describe an orderly evolution from closed, interconnected basins to the modern exterior-draining system.

### Timing of Basin Formation and Termination

- The basin-making event began during late Miocene time (15–10 m.y. ago).
- Its termination is uncertain. Upper basin fill, which encroaches on erosion surfaces cut on mountain blocks, may be Pliocene or mid-Pleistocene in age. This suggests diminished faulting later in basin-fill time.
- In the authors' opinion, vertical tectonics have not been active in Arizona's Basin and Range province since mid-Pleistocene time, except for vertically offset Pleistocene surfaces in the San Pedro and Santa Cruz valleys.
- Modern seismicity in Arizona (Sumner, 1976) is not simply related to either these offsets or the position of the province.

### Terminology and Conclusions

- The authors argue that the Basin and Range disturbance should be used in a restricted sense, to avoid masking distinct earlier Cenozoic episodes.
- Relative to the Colorado Plateau, Basin and Range basins are downdropped. The maximum downdrop occurs between the Gila Low and the Mogollon Rim.
- The authors state there is more evidence for a collapsing Basin and Range province than for a rising Colorado Plateau ("plateau uplift") relative to sea level during the late Miocene–Pliocene.
- Whether each basin is a graben, half graben, or rhombochasm must be determined case by case.
