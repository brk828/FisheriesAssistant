# Extinction of Most Sandia and Potosí Valley Endemic Pupfishes, Crayfishes and Snails

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

Surveys of the arid southwestern [[Nuevo Leon]] highlands documented the extinction of several recently described and undescribed endemic pupfishes, crayfishes and snails from isolated springs. Four spring populations of [[Cyprinodon Longidorsalis]], [[Cyprinodon Inmemoriam]], [[Cyprinodon Ceciliae]] and associated crayfish are extinct, and the [[El Potosi Spring]] fauna is almost extinct. The authors attribute the losses to rapid aquifer depletion from unsustainable, illegal agricultural pumping, compounded by industrial and urban overexploitation. Federal legislation existed but was poorly enforced, and the authors call for regional management of geohydrological basins.

## Background & Description

The arid lands of southwestern Nuevo León were considered devoid of aquatic fauna until 1948, when a crayfish ([[Cambarellus Alvarezi]]) and a pupfish ([[Cyprinodon Alvarezi]]) were collected at the large Potosí spring. A dwarf pupfish, [[Megupsilon Aporus]], was collected in 1961. In 1984 a pluvial lake with large springs in the uplands was explored, yielding three endemic pupfishes and two closely related endemic crayfishes, with one crayfish and pupfish per spring. Later collections produced additional snails, including [[Valvata Beltrani]] and an undescribed *Valvata* species, several of which were known only from shells.

The pupfishes were formally described by Lozano-Vilano & Contreras-Balderas (1993). The crayfishes remained undescribed at the time of writing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Each spring was small and isolated, with fauna restricted to one or two endemic species.

- **[[Charco Palma]]** (Aramberri): spring head of 4 to 4.5 m² and 1.4 m deep, with a downstream pool of about 6 m². Water was used for drinking, orchard irrigation and cattle. The population was about 50 to 70 mostly half-grown fish, described by the authors as possibly the smallest population and habitat known for any vertebrate species.
- **[[Ojo La Trinidad]]**: type locality of *C. inmemoriam*. The spring fell from an original 10,000 m² to under 1,000 m² by 1984, and the species was not seen again.
- **[[Charco Azul]]** (Barreno): type locality of the brilliantly coloured, pugnacious *C. veronicae* and of two valvatid snails. About 15,000 m² with pools 2 to 4 m deep, and estimated populations of 10,000 to 12,000 fish and crayfish at first visit.
- **[[La Presa Spring]]**: type locality of *C. ceciliae*, with about 8,000 m² of spring area and pools 1 to 2 m deep.
- **[[El Potosi Spring]]** (Galeana): type locality of three aquatic species. About 10,000 m² with depths over 2.5 m, abundant submerged and emergent vegetation (*Ceratophyllum*, *Najas*, *Lemna*, *Utricularia*, *Typha*, *Scirpus*), and downstream seeps with abundant fauna.

Threats at Potosí included an earthen and rock dam, the introduced [[Goldfish]] (Carassius auratus, present before 1961), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (Micropterus salmoides), collected in 1974 with adults up to 300 mm TL in the deep central pool.

The authors note that northern Mexican surface and underground waters are overexploited. Of 36 aquifer cases reported, 35 were overexploited and 33 were being depleted (about 1 m per year in 1981). Of 37 surface-water regions, 24 had poor-quality water. Several hundred large pumps were installed in the [[Sandia Valley]] and surrounding valleys, and their number was still increasing.

## Key Findings

**Species status by locality**

| Locality | Species | Discovered | Status reported |
|---|---|---|---|
| Charco Palma | *C. longidorsalis* | 1984 | Extinct by Nov 1994 (survived ~10 years) |
| La Trinidad | *C. inmemoriam* (+ crayfish) | 1984 | Extinct by 1986 (single male type specimen) |
| Charco Azul | *C. veronicae* (+ crayfish) | 1984 | Barely surviving in Nov 1994; crayfish not seen in 1993–94 |
| La Presa | *C. ceciliae* (+ crayfish) | 1988 | Extinct 1990 |
| Potosí | *C. alvarezi*, *M. aporus*, *Cambarellus alvarezi* | 1948–1961 | Almost extinct by 1994 |

- **Charco Palma:** The spring was found very low by November 1994, with only mosquito larvae and almost no vegetation.
- **La Trinidad:** The population was in such poor condition by April 1984 that collecting was avoided to reduce the threat. The spring was dry and has flowed only once since, without fauna.
- **Charco Azul:** This is the only local pupfish population that survived for some years after discovery. Water depth fell to about 0.3 m in October 1993 and to 0.2 m on 5 November 1994, when water remained in only 3 and then 1 pool. Pupfish were seen but not crayfish.
- **La Presa:** Water dropped more than 1.5 m by June 1990. The spring dried completely in 1991, with only partial, transient recovery.
- **Potosí:** Estimates in 1968 were 10,000 to 12,000 *C. alvarezi* and crayfish and 5,000 to 6,000 *M. aporus*. Water level dropped about 1.5 m by 1984, reducing pool surface by 90%. By summer 1986 no water remained in the pool or reservoir. On 8 November 1994, only very few *C. alvarezi* and *M. aporus* and negligible crayfish were seen downstream.

**Causes of extinction**

- Aquifer depletion from irrigation pumping, which dried springs and stopped flow from the rest.
- Overexploitation for industrial and drinking needs in northern Mexico, which the authors link to the loss of 11 other fish species.
- Biological invasions and predation (goldfish, largemouth bass).

**Conservation actions and policy**

- In 1974, [[Contreras-Balderas]] moved numerous *M. aporus* to side springs free of bass; by 1980 the species was common in the creek.
- In 1976, a team tried to eradicate bass by bimonthly gillnetting. Some 286 individuals were removed, but in February 1977 many fry and a subadult were still found. The operation was halted for lack of success.
- The [[Mexican General Environmental Law]] (1988) did not protect the undescribed Sandia pupfishes or regulate water use.
- The 1993 official listing covered only the Potosí species. The June 1994 [[Normas Oficiales Mexicanas]] recognised the endangered status of Potosí endemics but not the Sandia species. The authors state the norms were poorly publicised and enforced.
- [[Cuatro Ciénegas]] was classified as a protected area in 1993, after 28 years of requests, but remains at risk from the same drought and pumping pressures.

**Totals and conclusions**

- Three endemic fish species are extinct and three more are under serious threat.
- Total documented north Mexican fish extinctions reached 15 by 1994. Six of 11 species extinct before 1989 came from arid and semiarid zones, as did 10 of 15 species extinct by 1994.
- 115 of 135 endangered or threatened Mexican fishes occur in arid and semiarid zones.
- In situ conservation efforts failed, and laws, listings and habitat protection alone are insufficient.
- The authors recommend regional management using [[Geohydrological Basin Management]] as the unit, keeping springs and their biota stable, and using them as monitoring systems for water quality and the sustainability of agricultural water use. They also call for greater involvement of scientists, technicians and stakeholders.
