# Pluvial Lakes, Fish Distribution and Hydrographic History of the Great Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1947 monograph by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Robert R. Miller]] (Bulletin of the University of Utah, Vol. X, No. 1) reconstructs the Pleistocene ("Pluvial") lake history of the Great Basin and adjacent desert drainages by combining physiographic evidence with the zoogeography of freshwater fishes. The authors argue that the present distribution of fishes in isolated desert waters records past hydrographic connections that are no longer visible in the landscape. They catalogue more than 60 basins, classify them by fish-fauna evidence, and identify the [[Lahontan System]], [[Lake Bonneville]], and [[Death Valley System]] as the major pluvial drainage systems, while calling for far more detailed field and geological work.

## Background & Description

The Great Basin is an interior drainage region between the [[Wasatch Mountains]] and [[Sierra Nevada]], containing many enclosed basins that lack outlets to the sea. The authors note that physiographers and glacial geologists have documented extensive shore features, travertine deposits, and stranded terraces indicating that these basins were once much wetter. Freshwater fishes were chosen as the central evidence because they are confined to the waters they inhabit and therefore reflect changing hydrography closely.

The paper presents its evidence in a series of numbered basin accounts (for example, Lake Lahontan, Lake Bonneville, Lake Franklin, Lake Steptoe, Lake Mono, Owens Lake, Lake Manly, Lake LeConte), each describing physiography, present fish fauna, and inferred drainage connections. A closing table summarizes pluvial lakes and disrupted stream systems.

Chronology is treated cautiously. The authors note that no pluvial lake has been firmly synchronized across the basin, that correlations with montane and continental glaciation (Tahoe, Tioga, Wisconsin stages) are contested, and that some historic lakes may be Postpluvial rather than Pluvial in age.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Major systems.** The [[Lake Bonneville]] basin is described as the largest interior drainage, with a fauna of 5 families, 12 genera, 22 species and at least 28 kinds counting subspecies, according to the authors' preliminary estimate. [[Lake Lahontan]] shows high endemism: of its fishes, 8 of the known species are confined to the basin, suggesting separation since the Pliocene or early Pleistocene. The [[Death Valley System]] is ranked third in area and faunal diversity, with at least 24 kinds, only one endemic species, and [[Cyprinodon]] pupfishes dominant.

**Connections.** Shared species indicate links among basins. Lahontan fishes resemble those of the [[Klamath River]] and Columbia systems, while the Bonneville and Snake faunas share forms such as the Bonneville chub. Rhinichthys and Siphateles populations in isolated springs are often closely related to those in neighboring basins, which the authors interpret as evidence of former connections or stream captures.

**Relict and introduced fishes.** Several isolated populations (e.g., Siphateles in Mohave and Steptoe valleys, Rhinichthys in Ruby and Spring valleys) are considered relicts, while others (Utah chub, trout, carp, mosquitofish) are recognized as human introductions that must be excluded from the zoogeographic analysis.

**Other taxa.** The authors note that pocket gophers, oaks, and molluscs (for example, the Lake LeConte snails) provide complementary biological evidence for past moist conditions and drainage relations, but this evidence is much less developed than the fish data.

## Key Findings

- **Fish as hydrographic indicators.** Freshwater fishes are used as proxies for Pleistocene drainage connections; isolated populations sharing forms indicate former links.
- **Pluvial lakes identified.** The paper treats dozens of basins; major named lakes include Lahontan, Bonneville, Franklin, Steptoe (Waring), Surprise, Madeline, Mono, Owens, Manly, Mohave, Tulare, Chewaucan, Fort Rock, Malheur, Railroad, LeConte, and Cahuilla.
- **Lake Lahontan.** Contains high endemism (8 endemic species) and was connected to the Klamath, Columbia, and Bonneville systems at various times, according to the authors' tentative reconstruction.
- **Lake Bonneville.** Has the most extensive fauna of interior basins, with 5 families, 12 genera and about 22 species; its outlet to the Snake River is reconstructed as possible during overflow.
- **Death Valley System.** Contains at least 24 kinds of fishes, with 2 species of Cyprinodon in Death Valley and Lake Manly, and a Pleistocene connection to Owens and Mohave basins.
- **Lake LeConte.** Interpreted as a relatively recent fresh-water lake (approximately AD 1000 to 1450 based on archaeological dating), with an earlier marine stage in the Salton Sink.
- **Chronology uncertainty.** The authors emphasize that the timing of major lake stages is not established; they propose that a single "earlier pluvial" versus late Pleistocene distinction cannot yet be resolved.
- **Call for future work.** Recommended research includes dating deposits, correlating hydrographic history with montane and continental glaciation, and integrating biological and geological data.
