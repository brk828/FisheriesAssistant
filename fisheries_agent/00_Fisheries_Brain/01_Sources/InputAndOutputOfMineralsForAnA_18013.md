# Water and Mineral Balances of Pennine Moorland

#limnology #peatland-ecology #invertebrates #nutrient-cycling

## Executive Summary

This study by D. T. Crisp, written from [[Moor House Field Station]] of The Nature Conservancy, builds an outline water and mineral balance for an 83 ha stream catchment, [[Rough Sike]], within the [[Moor House National Nature Reserve]]. It tests a suggestion by Pearsall (1950) that [[Peat Erosion]], grazing, [[Heather Burning]] and leaching reduce the nutrient fund of [[Blanket Bog]] systems. Inputs and outputs of water and the elements sodium, potassium, calcium, phosphorus and nitrogen were measured over the study year from 25 October 1962 to 31 October 1963.

Only [[Peat Erosion]] and elements dissolved in stream water were significant outputs. For phosphorus and nitrogen, the total output exceeded the input, indicating a net loss of these nutrients from the catchment. Sheep sales were negligible. The author concludes that the net loss is large relative to heather burning but represents only a small share of the catchment's total nutrient stock.

## Background & Description

The study was built on [[Mass Balance]] principles. Annual water and element budgets were compared against separately measured inputs and outputs. The catchment and its stream were characterised in detail:

- **Location and relief:** Rough Sike rises on the northern slopes of the Pennines at about 686 m O.D. and flows north to join two similar streams near Moor House (563 m O.D.). Its waters derive from limestone springs and from drainage through numerous small peat channels.
- **Stream bed:** The bed is stony, subject to flash floods, and almost unvegetated. Rooted vegetation was negligible and about 2% of the bed was covered by moss. The bed comprised 7–8% limestone bedrock, 62.6% stones (>10 cm) and 29.7% gravel and silt (<10 cm).
- **Catchment cover:** Eroding peat covers 11–20% of the catchment, compared with 11–16% on the eastern plateau of the reserve below 2200 ft (670 m). Blanket bog vegetation covers 80–85%, with some limestone and alluvial grassland.
- **Representativeness:** Rough Sike forms about 7% of the 1140 ha [[Trout Beck]] catchment. Its discharge was about 8.0% of Trout Beck discharge during a winter period when the weir was out of action, which supported its use as a reasonable sample.

### Methods

- **Precipitation:** Measured with the daily gauge at Moor House Climatological Station, about 270 m from the lower reaches of Rough Sike. Comparisons with weekly gauges (Conway & Millar, 1960) showed that a single gauge within a few kilometres gives a reasonable annual estimate.
- **Stream discharge:** Measured with a sharp-edged rectangular [[Weir Calibration|weir]] (notch width 2.04 m) fitted with a water level recorder. A revised formula, derived by [[Salt Dilution Method|salt dilution]] gauging, was used because the theoretical formula was unreliable at low heads. Heads were at or below 0.25 ft for about 90% of the study year.
- **Dissolved elements:** Weekly water samples were filtered and analysed. Concentrations were plotted against discharge and combined with discharge volumes to give annual element outputs.
- **Peat and particulate load:** Water was drawn through two [[Siphon Sampling|siphons]] and passed through self-cleaning brass gauze filters suspended in air. Filter efficiency under floods was estimated at about 20%, so results were multiplied by five to include finer particles.
- **Invertebrate drift:** Animals retained by the filters were hand-sorted and counted. Surface-film invertebrates were sampled with twenty sticky-traps on wooden rafts, exposed in fortnightly five-day periods.
- **Sheep and wool:** Losses were calculated from stocking density, age structure, body weights and published element compositions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The catchment is a nutrient-poor, acidic [[Blanket Bog]] system with limestone inputs in its stream bed and springs. Its vegetation is dominated by bog plants and [[Heather Moorland]], and its peat is actively eroding in parts. Living plants, litter and upper peat layers hold much of the nutrient pool. [[Allen 1964]] showed that such peat can adsorb added phosphorus and nitrogen, which may limit how much leaves the catchment in solution.

Grazing by upland sheep is an important land use. [[Swaledale Sheep]] graze the reserve in summer and move to lowland pasture in winter. Pearsall (1950) had suggested that sheep impoverish British uplands, but this study found their direct export of minerals to be very small. The author notes that sheep still affect nutrient distribution, vegetation, erosion and soil through treading.

The stream community is fed by terrestrial and aquatic inputs. Aquatic invertebrates such as [[Stonefly Nymphs|stonefly nymphs]] (*Leuctra* spp., *Taeniopteryx nebulosa*), [[Mayfly Nymphs|mayfly nymphs]] (*Siphlonurus lacustris*) and [[Caddisfly Larvae|caddisfly larvae]] drift downstream during floods. Terrestrial taxa such as earthworms, tipulid larvae and slugs were also carried in spates. Water beetles (*Agabus* and *Deronectes* spp.) were among the analysed taxa. Nitrogen content in these animals was high, about 8.4% of dry weight on average.

Hydrological processes shape the system. Flash floods carry large peat loads, and the peat carried during spates is the dominant route for the loss of phosphorus and nitrogen. Evaporation and discharge were compared with [[Penman Evaporation Estimates|Penman]] calculations and a lysimeter.

## Key Findings

### 1. Water balance

- Annual precipitation input was 2070 mm (2130 mm corrected for blowing snow and gauge error during the study year).
- Stream output was 1368 thousand m³ over the year, equivalent to about 80% of input water leaving the catchment.
- Evaporation losses were about 339 mm, rising to 406 mm when corrected values for the snow-affected months were used.
- The lysimeter gave higher evaporation estimates than the catchment method for the frost-free months.

### 2. Element inputs and outputs (kg/year)

| Element | Input in precipitation | Output in stream solution | Output minus input | Difference as % of input |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 2120 | 3755 | +1635 | +77 |
| Potassium | 255 | 744 | +489 | +192 |
| Calcium | 745 | 4461 | +3716 | +499 |
| Phosphorus | 38–57 | 33 | −5 to −23 | −13 to −42 |
| Nitrogen | 681 | 244 | −437 | −64 |

- The output of sodium, potassium and calcium greatly exceeded input. The author attributes this to solution of [[Limestone Weathering|limestone]] and other catchment rocks. An estimated 10,000 kg of limestone dissolving could account for the excess calcium.
- About 65% of input nitrogen and a large share of input phosphorus were retained in the catchment.
- Nitrogen input was revised downward by one-third after preliminary work by Gore, reducing the earlier estimate of 1021 kg/year to 681 kg/year. Phosphorus input was also revised down by up to one-third (to 38–57 kg/year).

### 3. Peat erosion

- About 93,000 kg dry weight of peat passed down Rough Sike in the study year. This corresponds to a mean loss of 0.8 mm/year from the whole catchment (1120 kg dry weight/ha/year).
- Because most of this came from the 10–20% of the catchment actively eroding, the loss equals less than 1 cm/year of surface peat from those areas.
- Peat lost in this way contained 171 kg potassium, 401 kg calcium, 37 kg phosphorus, 1214 kg nitrogen and 23 kg sodium per year.
- Peat erosion accounted for about 50% of total phosphorus output and about 80% of total nitrogen output. The author notes this peat accumulated over centuries and may be of limited present value to plants and animals.

### 4. Animal losses

- **Invertebrates in suspension:** About 4 × 10⁵ ± 1 × 10⁵ animals, with a total dry weight of 1.4 kg, drifted out of the catchment during the study year. The author assumed this loss is not offset by upstream migration of adult insects, which makes the estimate an overestimate of net loss.
- **Invertebrates on the surface film:** Sticky traps caught 213,400 insects/m² of trap surface (80 g/m² dry weight) across a full-year equivalent. Extrapolation to the stream surface gave an estimate of about 525 × 10³ insects (195 kg dry weight). After correction for aquatic insects that land and re-emerge, the estimated loss was about 101 × 10³ insects/year (49.8 kg dry weight).
- **Sheep and wool:** About 18 sheep graze the catchment in summer. Each year about 2.57 ewes and 5.14 lambs are sold, which removes about 155 kg of fresh flesh. About 5 kg of wool is also sold. Total element losses were small, for example 4.43 kg/year of nitrogen.

### 5. Net balance

- The net annual loss from the catchment was 1658 kg sodium, 661 kg potassium, 4119 kg calcium, 14–33 kg phosphorus and 786 kg nitrogen.
- Per hectare, the net loss of phosphorus was 0.17–0.40 kg/ha/year and that of nitrogen was 9.48 kg/ha/year.
- The author concludes that the net loss of phosphorus and nitrogen is consistent with depletion of the catchment's nutrient stock, though the nitrogen balance may change once atmospheric fixation and gaseous losses are quantified.

### 6. Comparison with heather burning

- The annual net loss of phosphorus (0.17–0.40 kg/ha) was 2–4 times the loss from a single [[Heather Burning|heather burn]], as estimated by [[Allen 1964]].
- The annual nitrogen loss (9.4 kg/ha) was about a quarter of the loss from a single burn.
- Because burns occur on 5–20 year rotations, the annual net losses here are large compared with the average annual losses from burning.
- Nutrient stocks in heather systems were estimated by [[Robertson & Davies 1965]] at 200–600 kg/ha of phosphorus and 2000–5000 kg/ha of nitrogen. The present annual losses are therefore a very small percentage of the total nutrient fund.

### 7. Conclusions and limitations

- Sheep have a negligible direct effect on the mineral resources of the catchment.
- Only peat erosion and dissolved outputs are significant, so these are the main pathways to monitor in similar catchments.
- Estimates of insect drift and sticky-trap catches are approximate. The sticky-trap method has known biases, such as aquatic insects that alight on the water and take off again.
- Siphon operation covered only about 31.7% of the study year fully, with a further 3.2% partial. The author estimated missed periods from similar flows.
- Nitrogen and phosphorus balances remain the least certain because of uncertain atmospheric inputs and gaseous losses.
