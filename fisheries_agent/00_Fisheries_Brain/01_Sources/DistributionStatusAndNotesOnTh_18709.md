# Distribution, Status, and Notes on the Biology of Zuni Bluehead Sucker (*Catostomus discobolus yarrowi*) in New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[David L. Propst]], [[Amber L. Hobbes]] and [[Terry L. Stroh]] reports the current distribution, status, habitat and reproductive biology of the [[Zuni Bluehead Sucker]], an endemic subspecies of the [[Little Colorado River]]. The subspecies now persists mainly as five semi-isolated populations in the upper [[Rio Nutria]] drainage, occupying only about 15 km of headwater streams. Its decline is attributed to habitat degradation, nonnative predators and past eradication efforts. Surveys were conducted from 1990 to 1993, with a detailed reproductive study in 1995. The authors conclude that conservation will require more active management than the species has received to date.

## Background & Description

The Zuni bluehead sucker is a subspecies of the bluehead sucker (*C. discobolus*). Its origin is disputed. [[Smith 1966]] and [[Smith et al. 1983]] proposed a hybrid origin involving the [[Rio Grande Sucker]] (*C. plebeius*), with introgression enabled by a late Pleistocene stream capture. [[Crabtree and Buth 1987]] used allozyme data to argue that the subspecies is a geographic isolate that introgressed with *C. plebeius* only in the Rio Nutria, and that shared traits may be retained primitive characters.

The name has also been debated. [[Sublette et al. 1990]] justified the epithet *jarrovi*, while [[Eschmeyer et al. 1998]] called the change to *yarrowi* "perhaps unjustified" but accepted *yarrowi* as the valid name.

The subspecies is listed as endangered by New Mexico ([[Propst 1999]]), as a species of special concern by Arizona ([[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], 1996) and by the [[American Fisheries Society]] ([[Williams et al. 1989]]). The authors note that little research or management attention had been given to it.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The Rio Nutria and Rio Pescado rise in the [[Zuni Mountains]] and join to form the [[Zuni River]], which flows west-southwest to join the Little Colorado River. Upper reaches lie at 2280 to 2515 m in ponderosa pine, juniper and piñon woodland. Lower reaches (about 2000 m) flow through alluvial valleys bordered by willow and cottonwood. Several small reservoirs interrupt flow, and livestock grazing is widespread.

**Habitat.** Sucker habitat in the upper streams is canyon-bound, with bedrock substrata, moderate gradient (about 2.8%) and low waterfalls (<0.5 m) that separate pools during base flow. Downstream reaches have a slight gradient (0.4%) and sand and silt substrata. Key habitat features:
- Abstract: most common in low-velocity (<10 cm/sec) pools and pool-runs, 0.5 to 1.5 m deep, with seasonally dense perilithic and periphytic algae.
- Results: most specimens were in moderately deep (0.3 to 0.5 m) areas over cobble, boulders or bedrock, edged by emergent *Typha*.
- Water quality: dissolved oxygen 4.5 to 8.5 mg/l in summer and about 10.0 mg/l in early spring; specific conductance 250 to 400 µmho/cm, rising to 570 µmho/cm at some sites in summer.

**Threats.** Channel incision and sedimentation have made many reaches unsuitable, and the subspecies is absent from silt-bottomed, incised channels. Nonnative predators, particularly [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), may limit distribution. Eradication efforts in the 1960s and early 1970s, using rotenone and toxaphene in 27 treatments in the Rio Nutria, likely killed many suckers. Impoundments and dry reaches further fragment populations.

**Associated fauna.** The historic fish fauna probably included [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) and the Zuni bluehead sucker. Only one roundtail chub record exists (1851), and none was found in this study. A single speckled dace was collected in the Rio Pescado. Nonnative species by the late 1970s included [[Fathead Minnow]] (most common and widespread), [[Plains Killifish]] and green sunfish. Northern pike was found in the lower Rio Nutria. The historic record of [[Bonytail]] in the Zuni River is considered doubtful.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status**
- The subspecies occurs mainly as five semi-isolated populations in the upper Rio Nutria, Agua Remora, Tampico Spring and the Rio Nutria and Pescado confluence. Historic range is uncertain.
- Current occupied habitat totals about 15 km. Several hundred individuals were typically seen in the upper 3 km of the Rio Nutria.
- Suckers were not found in any of the seven sampled isolated springs or in any artificial impoundment on the Zuni Indian Reservation.
- Records from the Zuni River and lower Rio Nutria are now incidental or absent. Suckers were captured in low numbers (<10) near the Rio Pescado and Rio Nutria confluence.
- In 1978, [[B. Hanson]] collected 85 specimens (50 to 215 mm TL) from the Rio Pescado and more than 2800 from the upper Rio Nutria (October 1978). Both samples showed successful reproduction and recruitment.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning occurs from April through early June, at water temperatures of 6 to 13°C. Peak GSI (4.56; range 3.11 to 6.31) occurred on 5 May at 10.1°C.
- Males mature at Age 2 and females at Age 3.
- Most females produce 400 to 600 ova per year, and no more than about 800. In one sample, 9 females with mature ova had 400 to 800 ova (mean = 485).
- Spawning may be bimodal, with most output early in the season. This is supported by GSI patterns, ova counts and two size groups of small fish (<50 mm SL) at Nutria-Tampico in July 1995.
- Spawning was not directly observed. The authors compare it with [[Kanab Creek]] bluehead suckers ([[Maddux and Kepner 1988]]), which spawned at 18.2 to 24.6°C, far warmer than the early-May sites.

**Age, growth and population structure**
- Most individuals were Ages 1, 2 and 3. Few survived to Age 4. Most individuals probably live no more than about 26 to 28 months. The largest specimen was a 212-mm male (120.5 g).
- Recruitment occurred annually at each site.
- Length-weight relationships were near-isometric ([[Ricker 1975]]). Males were significantly smaller than females at both the Nutria-Tampico (mean 106.1 vs 113.6 mm SL) and Silva (92.6 vs 98.8 mm SL) sites.
- Sex ratios favored males: 1.29:1 at Nutria-Tampico and 1.77:1 at Silva.
- Headwater populations had fewer age-classes and higher relative condition. Nutria-Tampico had the lowest condition, and the Nutria Box 95 population was significantly lower than most others.

**Movement**
- Movement among populations, where it occurred, was mainly downstream during high discharge. Dry reaches and low waterfalls limited upstream movement.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The authors describe the subspecies' decline over the past 50 years as marked.
- They call for a multi-year study of population dynamics, reproductive biology, recruitment, the role of algal production, and the effects of nonnative fishes.
- They state that more active management than the species has received will be needed to conserve this "distinctive product of hybrid origin."
