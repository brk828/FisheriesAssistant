# 2 ASU profs study 3 endangered fishes

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #ConservationBiology

## Executive Summary

This article reports on research conducted by ASU associate professor Paul Marsh and zoology professor W.L. Minckley investigating the rapid population decline of three fish species native to the Southwest United States: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail Chub]] (described as possibly the most endangered fish in North America). All three species have vanished from their natural range in central Arizona despite once being widespread and abundant. The research utilized an unconventional field laboratory — two ponds at ASU's Karsten Golf Course — where 5,000 juvenile individuals of the three species were stocked in Fall 1990 to study reproductive activity and interactions with invasive non-native fish species under semi-controlled conditions.

## Background & Description

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: Largest of the three species; can grow up to **6 feet in length** and **100 pounds** in weight.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Can grow to **3 feet** in length and **15 pounds**.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Smallest of the three; grows to **2 feet** in length and **3–5 pounds**.
- Juveniles stocked in the study ponds were approximately **3 inches long** at delivery (September–October) and were expected to reach nearly **10 inches** by the following May.
- Juveniles feed on **algae and plankton** during early growth stages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical range**: Once widespread and abundant throughout the Colorado River basin, including central Arizona (e.g., near the Salt River).
- **Historical human use**: All three species were historically staple food sources for people living near the Salt River — used as human food, animal feed, and even fertilizer due to their former abundance.
- **Current status**: Natural populations no longer occur in central Arizona; species persist only in isolated or managed refugia.
- **Threats identified**:
  - **Habitat alteration** via reservoir construction, which began as the "first of a one-two punch" harming native fish populations.
  - **Introduction of non-native sport fish** by settlers moving West, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Catfish]], and [[Sunfish]].
  - Non-native species are hypothesized to **prey on eggs and larvae** of the three native species.
  - Native fish are believed to be **evolutionarily naive** to these introduced predators, having evolved in a system historically lacking such predation pressure.
  - Possible **competition for food** and **behavioral displacement** by non-native species.
- **Study design**: Non-native fish were allowed to enter the golf course ponds naturally via water supplied from Salt River canals, creating a controlled experimental system to observe interactions between native and non-native species step-by-step.

## Key Findings

- The **non-native fish introductions** are considered the primary suspected driver of the decline of the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail Chub]], though researchers note **other contributing factors** (competition, behavioral displacement) may also be involved.
- Decline timeline: Populations began declining in the **early 20th century**, accelerated by the **1950s**, and have continued accelerating since.
- The construction of **Arizona reservoirs** is identified as an early, compounding factor in native fish habitat alteration, preceding the sport-fish introductions.
- All three species are long-lived (**40–50+ years**), enabling long-term monitoring studies.
- Researchers **do not attribute blame** to any single group; the decline is framed as an unintended consequence of Western development and settlement patterns (analogous to cattle introduction affecting elk populations).
- **Study logistics**: The ASU golf course ponds provide a cost-effective, accessible, and controlled research environment compared to traditional fieldwork, which the researchers note is typically time- and cost-intensive.
- **Public engagement**: A poster campaign was implemented instructing anglers to return any hooked specimens of the three protected species alive to the water.
- **Planned research milestones**: Reproductive activity monitoring and assessment of non-native species invasion impacts were anticipated to begin in **Spring 1991**.
