# Global Change and Freshwater Ecosystems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #percidae #centrarchidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review, by [[Carpenter Et Al]] (1992) in the *Annual Review of Ecology and Systematics*, assesses how global climate change may alter lake, stream, wetland and floodplain ecosystems. Freshwater holds only about 0.01% of Earth's water, yet it supports ecosystems and human needs, so its sensitivity to changes in precipitation, evaporation and runoff matters greatly. The authors limit their scope to [[Freshwater Fishes]], which interact strongly with lower trophic levels and serve as a useful indicator for other aquatic organisms, and to wetland interactions with atmospheric chemistry. They organize the review around transitional scales (decades to centuries, shifts in distribution) and perturbational scales (years to decades, floods, droughts and recruitment events), then cover ecosystem metabolism, climate feedbacks and research priorities.

## Background & Description

The document frames freshwater as a small, climate-sensitive fraction of the planet's water:

- **Biosphere water:** about 0.01% of Earth's water, with lakes (0.0087%) and soils (0.0057%) as the main reservoirs cited.
- **Fresh water overall:** about 2.58% of Earth's water, of which ice is 1.97% and groundwater 0.61%. The remainder is saline.
- **Climate control:** evaporation, water vapor transport and precipitation determine the amount and distribution of freshwater.

Fish ecology is framed through temperature. Freshwater fishes are grouped as coldwater (e.g., [[Salmonidae]]), coolwater (e.g., [[Percidae]]) or warmwater (e.g., [[Centrarchidae]] and [[Cyprinidae]]) forms, and their temperature tolerances set local and biogeographic range limits.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and geomorphology.** Greenhouse warming is expected to increase both precipitation and evaporation, but predictions of variability are more uncertain than predictions of means. Some regions will become wetter and others drier. Arid basins are more sensitive to precipitation change than humid basins, and seasonal shifts in streamflow may matter more than changes in total annual runoff, especially where winter precipitation now falls as snow. Arid and semiarid rivers show amplified runoff response and more severe flash floods, leading some authors to propose these systems as early warnings of global change.

**Riparian and terrestrial linkages.** The land-water interface is expected to expand. Changes in riparian vegetation, such as the spread of [[Saltcedar]] in the Colorado basin and the loss of American chestnut, have already altered aquatic communities. High CO2 may lower leaf nitrogen, reducing insect grazing and increasing leaf input to streams.

**Thermal habitat and range shifts.** Warming may extend suitable habitat for some fishes at high latitudes while squeezing species at low-latitude limits. Groundwater warming threatens eggs and larvae at low latitudes and altitudes, while elevated groundwater temperatures may extend optimal conditions at higher latitudes. East-west drainage patterns limit northward escape for many fishes.

**Water renewal and lake chemistry.** Water renewal rate scales how quickly lakes respond to nutrient and contaminant inputs. Declining renewal may raise nutrient and contaminant concentrations and reduce hypolimnetic oxygen, while drought can lower pH and acid neutralizing capacity in softwater lakes.

**Feedbacks.** Wetlands contribute about 22% of the annual atmospheric methane flux, and most wetland methane production occurs above 40°N. Large tropical rivers and wetlands influence regional climate through evaporation and rainfall patterns, and freshwater discharge can affect ocean circulation.

## Key Findings

- **Fish distributions will shift.** Warming may open high-latitude habitats to invasion and push low-latitude species toward local extinction. [[Shuter And Post]] (1990) found that all aspects of the life history of temperate North American fishes would be accelerated by warming, and the constraint of size-dependent winter starvation would be relaxed.
- **Great Lakes invasion potential.** [[Mandrak Invasion Analysis]] identified 58 candidate species from Mississippi and Atlantic drainages. Of these, 27 (mostly Cyprinidae and Centrarchidae) were judged likely to invade the Great Lakes if temperature limits were released. The Great Lakes had been colonized by at least 122 fish species after glaciation.
- **Thermal modeling of Great Lakes fishes.** Coupling thermal models with a [[Bioenergetics Model]] projected greater total annual growth through a longer growing season and more preferred-temperature habitat. Growth declines were expected where prey production was insufficient.
- **Diversity and productivity may rise at north temperate latitudes.** Fish diversity, production and yields are inversely related to latitude. The authors expect that compensatory replacement of glacial relicts by coolwater and warmwater species would raise overall diversity and productivity of north temperate lakes.
- **Recruitment variability will increase.** Lethal events such as the [[Lake Mendota]] summerkill of lake herring (*Coregonus artedii*) reduced zooplankton predation by nearly 50%, increasing [[Daphnia]] grazing and reducing phytoplankton. Fish recruitment depends on early life survival, so more variable habitat will make year-class success more erratic, with social and economic consequences for fisheries.
- **Drought and flow changes concentrate pollutants.** Reduced flows can concentrate pollutants and lower oxygen. Chessman and Robinson's study of the 1982–1983 drought documented poor water quality at record low flows.
- **Stream metabolism may shift.** Rising temperature raises respiration faster than primary production, lowering the P/R ratio. Hydrologic change can convert a stream from a processor (P/R < 1) into an exporter (P/R > 1).
- **Lake productivity may increase.** An empirical analysis suggested that warming would increase productivity at all trophic levels. Increased production may worsen hypolimnetic oxygen depletion.
- **Recovery timescales differ.** Biota of recipient streams may recover from debris flows within 1–2 years, while the geomorphic effects of slumps and earthflows can persist for thousands of years. Post-flood succession in desert streams is disturbance-controlled for about 30 days.
- **Wetland methane feedback is uncertain.** Permafrost thaw may expand northern wetlands while drying existing ones, and the net effect on methane emissions is unresolved.
- **Research priorities.** The authors call for landscape- and watershed-scale research that bridges hydrology, wetland ecology, stream ecology and limnology, mesoscale climate models, and explicit treatment of human land use and water use, which they argue cannot be separated from climate effects.
