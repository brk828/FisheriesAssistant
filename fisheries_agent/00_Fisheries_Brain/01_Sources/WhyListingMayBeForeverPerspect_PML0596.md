# Delisting Under the U.S. Endangered Species Act

#conservation-biology #marine-biology #endangered-species-act #environmental-law #vertebrate-conservation

## Executive Summary

This is a 2001 policy and legal analysis by [[Holly Doremus]] and [[Joel Pagel]] published in *Conservation Biology* as part of a special section on the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). The authors argue that [[Delisting]] (removing species from the protected list) requires evaluating not only biological recovery but also the strength of legal protections outside the ESA, termed [[Background Law]]. They conclude that the small number of delistings to date does not show the ESA has failed, that most currently listed species will need protection indefinitely, and that delisting should proceed cautiously. The paper is a critical commentary on U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) delisting practice rather than a field study, and it contains no new population data.

The section is framed by an editorial preface noting the growing role of conservation science in management decisions, and includes a bilingual abstract (English and Spanish).

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The ESA was passed in 1973 to prevent human-caused extinction. Its emphasis has since moved toward [[Recovery Plans|recovery]] and delisting. The USFWS began a delisting initiative by 2000, which produced the delisting of the [[American Peregrine Falcon]] and formal proposals to delist four other species: the [[Tinian Monarch]], the Douglas County population of [[Columbian White-Tailed Deer]], the [[Bald Eagle]], and the [[Aleutian Canada Goose]].

**Legal definitions.** The ESA defines an endangered species as one "in danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of its range," and a threatened species as one "likely to become an endangered species within the foreseeable future." Species that no longer meet these criteria must be delisted. The authors note that the decision rests on extinction risk, but that protection depends on controlling threats, which are generally linked to human activity.

**Scale of listing.** Nearly 1,200 species in the United States and about 600 worldwide had been listed under the ESA at the time of writing. As of 1988, fewer than 30 had been delisted. Many delistings resulted from extinction, taxonomic revision, or new information showing less threat than believed, and only a handful were attributed to recovery.

**Key statutory tools.**
- [[Section 7 Consultation]]: requires federal agencies to consult on actions that may affect listed species and bars actions that may jeopardize continued existence.
- [[Section 9 Prohibitions]]: prohibits "taking" of listed animals without a permit. Through USFWS regulation, "harm" includes significant habitat modification.
- [[Section 10 Permits]]: allows incidental take if it does not jeopardize the species and effects are mitigated to the maximum extent practicable.
- [[Citizen Suits]]: allow concerned individuals to enforce the ESA when government does not act.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats.** Citing [[Wilcove et al. 1998]], the authors report that habitat degradation affects 85% of imperiled species and that competition and predation by [[Exotic Species]] threaten 49%. Background law generally does not protect against either threat, and the ESA offers little protection against exotic species, although it provides the strongest available protection against habitat degradation.

**Habitat on private land.** Nearly 80% of listed species occur on private land, and many depend on non-federal land for more than half their habitat. Restrictive covenants and conservation easements can protect such habitat but are costly to assemble across many parcels and are enforceable only by the parties who negotiated them.

**Recovered species and their threats.** Of the species removed for recovery, the Brown Pelican, American Peregrine Falcon, and Arctic Peregrine Falcon declined mainly from persistent organochlorine pesticide residues, addressed by the 1972 DDT ban under the [[Federal Insecticide Fungicide and Rodenticide Act]]. The [[Gray Whale]] and the [[American Alligator]] declined mainly from commercial exploitation. Three Australian kangaroo (Macropus spp.) populations also declined mainly from commercial exploitation.

**Pending proposals.** All pending delisting proposals involve species that declined for reasons other than habitat loss, though habitat protection remains important to their recovery. The Bald Eagle declined mainly from pesticide pollution, the Aleutian Canada Goose from hunting and introduced foxes, and the Columbian White-Tailed Deer mainly from overhunting, with habitat loss also playing a role.

## Key Findings

**1. Delisting requires evaluating background law.** Delisting decisions must assess whether non-ESA mechanisms (laws, economic incentives, social pressures) will control the original threats after the ESA protections end. Biological population trends alone are not sufficient. Improvements in population may reflect ESA protections that will not survive delisting.

**2. Few delistings and limited evidence of ESA failure.** Only a small number of species have been delisted. The USFWS claimed 12 species were delisted for recovery by the end of 1999. The authors believe no more than eight have truly recovered. Four of the 12 (Palau Ground-Dove, Palau Fantail, Palau Owl, and Rydberg milk-vetch) were delisted because new data suggested they probably never met the listing standard.

**3. Most species will need continuing protection.** The authors expect that the majority of currently listed species, both plants and animals, will need ESA protection indefinitely. Virtually all species are declining when listed, and after a year on the list more than half of those with known status are stable or improving. Most improve with time on the list.

**4. The ESA offers unique protection, especially for habitat.** Background laws generally do not prohibit habitat modification. The [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]] and [[Marine Mammal Protection Act]] protect specific taxa against taking but not habitat loss. The [[Clean Water Act]] addresses industrial discharges but not agricultural runoff. The [[Federal Land Policy and Management Act]] covers only Bureau of Land Management lands and requires "multiple use" without guaranteeing wildlife habitat. The ESA potentially addresses the full range of habitat-destroying activities.

**5. Caution is warranted.** The authors argue that erroneous delisting may cause extinction, and that mistakes may be hard to reverse given the political barriers to relisting. They also note that the ESA prohibits only activities that might cause extinction, and that section 7 consultations from 1987 to 1994 (nearly 100,000 consultations) blocked only 54 projects. The authors cite a 1990 average listing cost of about $60,000 and permit processing costs of $50,000 to $100,000, though they argue that these costs are generally modest.

**6. Monitoring and review gaps.** The ESA requires at least a one-year monitoring period after recovery. The authors argue this is too short to detect biological or regulatory problems, since several generations may be needed to reveal trends. They recommend monitoring that includes the legal climate and periodic verification that background protections remain in effect, with status review triggered by substantial legal changes.

**7. Critique of USFWS delisting proposals.** The authors find that:
- The Bald Eagle proposal cites many laws but does not show they are adequate substitutes for the ESA, does not explain differences between ESA and BEPA take provisions, and provides little assurance of habitat protection after delisting.
- The Columbian White-Tailed Deer and Aleutian Canada Goose proposals do not identify the legally binding measures protecting habitat labeled "secure," and the goose proposal concedes its wintering habitat recovery goal has not been met.
- Federal courts have held that the USFWS cannot rely on promised future regulations or voluntary measures to justify decisions not to list (for example, [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity v. Babbitt]] and [[Save Our Springs v. Babbitt]]). The authors argue the same logic applies to delisting.

**8. Recommendations.** The authors recommend that:
- Recovery criteria include control of threats and specify the needed regulatory measures, even where the USFWS lacks authority to adopt them alone.
- Legal experts participate in delisting evaluations.
- The USFWS and [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] periodically verify background protections after delisting.
- Delisting proposals explain how background law will protect species, with legally binding and enforceable measures for most species.

**9. Conclusion.** The authors argue that the need for many species to remain listed indefinitely demonstrates the ESA's value as a barrier against extinction, not its failure. They contend that emphasizing the ESA's primary goal of preventing extinction will be more politically effective than promoting delisting as proof of success.
