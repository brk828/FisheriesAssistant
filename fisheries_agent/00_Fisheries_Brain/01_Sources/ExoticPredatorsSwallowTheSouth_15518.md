# Exotic Predators and the Decline of Southwest Native Frogs

#herpetology #amphibians #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This 1998 *High Country News* feature by Mark Muro reports on the decline of native amphibians in Arizona and New Mexico. [[Phil Rosen]], a herpetology doctoral candidate at the University of Arizona, and his colleague [[Cecil Schwalbe]], an ecologist, identify the spread of non-native [[American Bullfrog|bullfrogs]], crayfish and predatory fishes as the main driver. Habitat modification, including dams, stock tanks and diverted marshes, created conditions that favored these invaders. The article describes removal experiments, a public-private recovery project with ranchers, and a case for restoring natural wetland drying cycles. The [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] is the focal species, and federal listing was expected later in 1998.

## Background & Description

The article is a journalistic account rather than a peer-reviewed study. It is based on fieldwork by Rosen and Schwalbe, interviews with researchers, ranchers and local officials, and the authors' own counts and observations. Rosen's field site is a side drainage of the [[San Pedro River]], about 70 miles southeast of Tucson, which the article describes as one of the last free-flowing rivers in the Southwest.

The principal taxa discussed are:

- **Native amphibians:** [[Leopard Frog|Native Leopard Frogs]] and the Chiricahua leopard frog (*Rana chiricahuensis*), listed by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service as threatened or endangered in the planned 1998 action.
- **Invasive amphibians and reptiles:** [[American Bullfrog|Bullfrogs]], a native of the eastern United States that was stocked by state agencies until the early 1970s.
- **Invasive invertebrates:** [[Crayfish]], which eat native frog eggs, tadpoles and aquatic vegetation.
- **Native and non-native fishes:** Non-native sport fishes such as bass and trout, which prey on larval leopard frogs.
- **Other native fauna:** [[Mexican Garter Snake|Mexican Garter Snakes]] and [[Sonoran Turtle|Sonoran Turtles]], which the article says are also threatened where invaders are present.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors argue that the region's native communities evolved under abrupt flooding and frequent drying. Schwalbe summarizes the situation this way: "Abrupt flooding and frequent drying define native communities in the Southwest. Take that away and you take away the natives' advantages."

Several human-caused pressures are named:

- **Dams and flow changes:** Water developers built hundreds of dams that reduced seasonal flow extremes.
- **Stock tanks:** Ranchers dug thousands of stock-watering tanks near perennial springs, creating ponds where few had existed.
- **Diverted and channelized wetlands:** Marsh flows were diverted, impounded or confined to channels.
- **Sport-fish stocking:** The Arizona Game and Fish Department stocked non-native fish and bullfrogs in artificial lakes.
- **Population growth:** A human population boom increased pressure on these systems.

The article also lists pesticide runoff, smelter-related acid rain and increased ultraviolet radiation from ozone depletion as contributing factors. It treats habitat change as the most important cause for Arizona's amphibians.

The article notes that native fish have also declined sharply. Forty non-native fish species have overwhelmed 33 native species, extirpating five and leaving 19 others threatened or endangered. Invaders have also moved from lowland reservoirs and rivers into adjacent wetlands and into the higher waters of the southeastern "[[Sky Island Mountain Ranges]]."

## Key Findings

**1. Decline of native amphibians**
- Over the past 20 years, many populations of native amphibians that Rosen studies in Arizona and New Mexico have disappeared.
- Four of Arizona's five remaining native aquatic frog species have reached critical status in recent years.
- After 15 years of study, Schwalbe and Rosen report that native leopard frogs are now mostly restricted to scattered, peripheral sites, such as "two or three canyons" and "a number of remote cattle tanks."

**2. Bullfrogs as the main threat**
- Schwalbe identifies bullfrogs as "the primary threat to the remaining native frogs in Arizona."
- Stomach examinations reportedly found bats, snakes, turtles and a red-winged blackbird among bullfrog prey, along with leopard frogs.
- Bullfrogs were described as occurring "by the hundreds" along pond edges.

**3. Multiple invaders**
- Non-native fish eat larval leopard frogs in larger streams and impoundments.
- Crayfish eat native frogs, tadpoles and aquatic vegetation.
- The article reports that invaders have reached sky island mountain ranges.

**4. Failed and partial removal attempts**
- At the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]], researchers trapped, speared and shot hundreds of bullfrogs in a test wetland. The bullfrogs' evasiveness and fecundity let them rebound quickly.
- Leopard frogs were moved into fenced, newly created, bullfrog-free ponds, a technique suited to a federal refuge that avoids conflict with ranchers.

**5. Triage on private land**
- During the 1994–95 drought, the Magoffin family hauled roughly a thousand gallons of water a week to keep a Chiricahua leopard frog population alive in an earthen stock tank.
- The [[Malpai Borderlands Group]], a private ranchers' confederation, partnered with the researchers and Arizona Game and Fish specialist Mike Sredl.
- A grant later funded permanent water sources and new wells. The Magoffins now have two new wells, and the frogs have "a new lease on life" in at least one wet spot.
- Schwalbe reports that "80 to 90 percent of the surviving leopard frogs are in stock tanks," which makes these tanks important for translocation and conservation.

**6. Restoring natural wetland cycles**
- Rosen and Schwalbe advocate removing artificial water developments where feasible and restoring the cycles of flooding and drying.
- Rosen argues that leopard frogs can persist with natural drying, while bullfrog adults struggle with it. Flash floods can also wash bullfrog tadpoles and slow-water fish out of the system.
- At the refuge, managers are encouraging [[Black Draw]] to fill with sediment to restore cienega and stream conditions.

**7. Non-action as restoration**
- Rosen urged the U.S. Forest Service not to dredge silt from [[Rucker Lake]], an artificial pond in the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] that filled after the 1994 [[Rattlesnake Fire]]. The article states that this would save tens of thousands of dollars and allow Rucker Creek to return to stream conditions.
- Rosen reports that threatened fishes are already returning to the canyon. Local residents in Douglas, however, lobby to restore the lake's former sport fishery, which they describe as a tradition and an economic asset.

**8. Public engagement**
- Rosen and Schwalbe hope to form a multi-agency wetlands restoration task force.
- They emphasize that success depends on private landowners, whose stock ponds are "both the boon and the bane of the situation now."

**Overall conclusion:** The authors judge invasions on dry land to be largely irreversible, but they consider aquatic recovery possible through removal, restoration of natural hydrology and collaboration with landowners.
