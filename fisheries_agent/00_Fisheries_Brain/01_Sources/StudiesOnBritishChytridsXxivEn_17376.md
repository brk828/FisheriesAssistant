# Entophlyctis molesta sp. nov.: A Chytrid Parasite of Planktonic Green Algae in the English Lake District

#mycology #chytrid #entophlyctis

## Executive Summary

This 1965 paper by Hilda M. Canter, published in the *Journal of the Royal Microscopical Society*, describes and names a new chytrid fungus, [[Entophlyctis Molesta]] sp. nov. The fungus parasitizes [[Stylospheridium Stipitatum]], a green alga that itself grows as an epiphyte on the blue-green alga [[Gomphospheria Negeliana]]. Records from weekly plankton net collections (1948–1958) in several [[English Lake District]] lakes show the parasite is recurrent, especially in [[Esthwaite Water]] and [[Windermere]]. The author places the species tentatively in the genus *Entophlyctis* and separates it from the similar, poorly defined [[Entophlyctis Apiculata]] complex.

## Background & Description

The study belongs to a series titled "Studies on British Chytrids." The material was collected by the laboratory staff of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] at Ambleside, Westmorland, who sampled plankton for the author.

The fungus is monocentric and eucarpic. Its life cycle has three main parts:

- **Infection:** A [[Zoospore]] passes through the mucilage envelope of the host colony and usually settles on the dome-shaped apex of a *Stylospheridium* cell. Lateral attachment is very rare. No more than two infections were seen on one host cell.
- **Sporangium:** After encystment, the zoospore dissolves part of the host wall. A swelling develops inside the alga and becomes the bulk of the sporangium, while the zoospore forms the apical portion. The mature sporangium is broadly pear-shaped to oval.
- **Resting spore:** Formed sexually. A small male cell attaches apically or subapically to the female, and the fusing bodies are anisogamous. The spore wall thickens, and the mature spore is spherical to pear-shaped.

**Key morphological measurements (as reported):**

| Structure | Measurement |
|---|---|
| Sporangium | 4.5–12 µm high × 3–10 µm wide; 2–30 zoospores |
| Zoospore | 2.5–3 µm diameter; posterior flagellum about 12 µm long |
| Male cell | 2.5 µm diameter |
| Resting spore | Spherical, 5–8 µm diameter, or pyriform 5–7 µm high × 4–5 µm wide |
| Refractive globule (spore) | 2.5–5 µm diameter |

The mature zoospores are released in a non-motile state through an apical, inoperculate dehiscence. They then stream through the host mucilage and swim off. The empty sporangium wall collapses and is barely visible after dehiscence.

The rhizoidal system is a single short axis with a few distal branches. It was rarely seen, mainly in specimens where host contents were reduced. Its ultimate branches contact the reddish remnants of the host [[Chloroplast]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Host and spatial relationship:** The parasite grows between the host's outer cell wall and inner protoplast membrane. As the swelling enlarges, the host chloroplast is depressed and pushed toward the cell base. Only the rhizoids penetrate the protoplast membrane. The author suggests that when the sporangium empties, the protoplast and rhizoids move back toward the apex, possibly carrying chloroplast remnants with them.

**Seasonality and distribution:** Weekly phytoplankton net collections from 1948 to 1958 (eleven years) were used to track occurrence:

- *E. molesta* was recorded in Esthwaite Water every year of the period. Windermere north basin, Windermere south basin and Blelham Tarn had it in nine, eight and seven years respectively.
- It was most consistent in Esthwaite Water. In Windermere it occurred over a longer span, usually from October to March.
- In Esthwaite Water and Blelham Tarn, the main season is July to November.

**Comparison with *Rhizophidium ephippium*:** A second chytrid, [[Rhizophidium Ephippium]] (Canter 1950), also parasitizes *Stylospheridium*. It was recorded in Esthwaite Water in eleven years, Windermere south basin in seven years and Windermere north basin in three years. It peaked from September to January in Esthwaite and October to November in Windermere south basin. It had never been recorded from Blelham Tarn.

**Other notes:** The short rods in the *Gomphospheria* mucilage are bacteria. They live naturally with the healthy living alga and are not a sign of decay.

## Key Findings

1. **A new species is described.** *Entophlyctis molesta* sp. nov. is named and diagnosed in Latin and English. The type substratum is *Stylospheridium stipitatum*, and the type locality is the plankton of Windermere. The iconotype figures are 2J, L, O and P.

2. **The taxonomy of *E. apiculata* is unresolved.** The author concludes that the taxonomic status of all forms assigned to *E. apiculata* "is insoluble." The new species is closest in form to the description by [[Zopf 1884]], but with differences: the empty sporangium remains visible in Zopf's material, and Zopf apparently did not see the male cell. The form described by [[Geitler 1962]] on *Hypnomonas lobata* differs because the original zoospore cyst thickens and persists as an appendage on the sporangium.

3. **Tentative placement.** The fungus is placed tentatively in *Entophlyctis* and described as new because it does not clearly match Geitler's or Zopf's forms.

4. **Sexual reproduction in the resting spore.** The resting spore is formed sexually, with a male cell attached to the female. This contrasts with Zopf's view that the resting spore is asexually formed.

5. **Host-parasite interaction.** The parasite invades the space between the host's cell walls, displaces the chloroplast, and uses rhizoids that reach the host protoplast.

6. **Long-term plankton records.** The 1948–1958 weekly plankton records show consistent presence in the English Lake District, especially in Esthwaite Water and Windermere. Its seasonal pattern differs from that of [[Rhizophidium Ephippium]].

7. **Possible related form.** A chytrid on a *Chlamydomonas* collected near Wray Castle (North Lancashire) in March 1947 closely resembles Geitler's form. The author notes that rhizoids were not seen, so the identity remains uncertain.

8. **Open questions.** The author states that the origin of the rhizoids and their developmental stage remain unknown. Whether *E. molesta* is synonymous with Zopf's form, or with Geitler's, "must await further studies."
