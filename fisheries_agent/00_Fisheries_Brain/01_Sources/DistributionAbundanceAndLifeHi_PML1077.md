# Distribution, Abundance and Life History of the Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1973 doctoral dissertation by Paul Bernard Holden (Utah State University, Wildlife Science, Ecology) assesses the status of the fish fauna of the upper [[Colorado River]] basin, with emphasis on endemic large-river species. Sampling ran from 1967 to 1972 across the Green, Yampa, Colorado, Gunnison and related rivers. Introduced species outnumbered native species 19 to 10 among the 29 species collected. Four endemics, [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Humpback Sucker]], were rare and endangered. The study identifies the lower Yampa River, Desolation Canyon and the lower Green River in Canyonlands National Park as areas supporting reproduction of these rare forms. Main causes of decline were identified as habitat alteration by high dams and introduction of exotic species.

## Background & Description

The native fishes of the Colorado basin are highly distinctive. [[Miller]] (1961) calculated 71 percent endemism for native species, one of the highest figures in North America. The basin's long isolation and its shift from a cold, clear mountain stream to a warm, turbid desert river shaped this fauna. Endemic forms fall into two groups: small-stream forms, mostly in the lower basin, and large-river forms of the main stem and larger tributaries.

Large reservoirs such as [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], [[Lake Powell]] and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] now release cold, clear tailwaters. Few reports on upper-basin fishes existed before this study. Work by [[Vanicek and Kramer]] (1969) and the 1962 [[Flaming Gorge Eradication Program]] was among the key prior efforts. Several endemic species, including the native chubs and suckers, have been listed as rare and endangered by the Department of the Interior.

Native species include the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (the most common endemic), the [[Bluehead Sucker]], the [[Roundtail Chub]] and the polymorphic bluehead sucker forms. Introduced species include [[Channel Catfish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Sand Shiner]], [[Common Carp]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and habitat.** The study covered roughly ten river sections, from the upper Yampa near Craig, Colorado, through Dinosaur National Monument, Desolation and Gray canyons, and the Green River in Canyonlands National Park, to the Colorado River near Moab and Rifle. Canyon reaches of rocky and gravelly bottoms supported more species and more fish than the sandy-bottomed lower reaches. The Little Snake River was the least diverse, with 5 species. Marble and Grand Canyons were studied less intensively.

**Physical conditions.** Turbidity was highest in spring runoff and after storms. Summer temperatures rose to 21–23°C in most areas, and winter lows reached about 1–3°C. Lower reaches were warmer and more turbid in summer.

**Hydrologic and thermal effects.** Cold tailwaters from Flaming Gorge Dam left the 65 miles immediately downstream unsuitable for native fish, with no reproduction observed. The Yampa River, which joins the Green, moderates the cold water. After 1966 the late-summer Green River became colder than natural, which may affect species spawning in July and August. Marble and Grand Canyons, fed by cold water from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and lacking major tributaries, were judged largely unfavorable habitat.

## Key Findings

**Species status and collections**
- Twenty-nine species were collected, 19 of them introduced. All native fishes reported from the area were collected.
- Over 2,500 adult fish were collected (Table 2). Flannelmouth and bluehead suckers were the most abundant.
- Four rare endemics: Colorado squawfish (300 adults, 261 from the lower Yampa), humpback chub (39 taken), bonytail (35 taken) and humpback sucker (53 taken).
- The humpback chub complex was reported as a rare taxonomic unit; juveniles of humpback and non-humped forms were separated by a body depth/head depth ratio.

**Reproduction and habitat**
- Reproduction of the rare endemics was documented in the lower Yampa River, Desolation Canyon and the Green River to Canyonlands National Park.
- Juvenile squawfish were abundant near Echo Park in 1968 but declined sharply after that.
- Colorado squawfish moved into the lower Yampa in July–August as water warmed to 20–21°C and turbidity dropped. More than 90 percent of the squawfish caught in that surge were ripe males.

**Spawning periods (ripe-fish observations)**
- Flannelmouth sucker: about 12–15°C, late May–June.
- Bluehead sucker: about 15–18°C, June–early July.
- Roundtail chub: about 18–21°C, July.
- Colorado squawfish: about 21°C, late July–August.
- Eggs of bluehead sucker hatched in seven days at 18–21°C; fungal infection limited other attempts.

**Movement**
- A total of 3,322 fish were tagged, 2,589 of them flannelmouth suckers. There were 217 recaptures, mostly within one day in the tagging area.
- Most moved little, and downstream movement was more common. One group of flannelmouth suckers moved about 65 miles upstream from the tagging point below Flaming Gorge Dam.

**Hybridization**
- Three sucker hybrid combinations were recorded: flannelmouth × humpback sucker, flannelmouth × white sucker, and bluehead × white sucker. The bluehead × white sucker hybrid was abundant in the upper Yampa.
- Introduced white sucker spawning overlapped with native suckers, which was considered the probable origin of the hybrids.

**Major threats and conclusions**
- Dams and cold tailwaters are the main drivers of decline. Reservoirs do not support endemic reproduction, and the displacement of fish after Flaming Gorge closure illustrates the effect of dam operations.
- Competition from introduced species, including channel catfish and red shiners, is a second cause of decline.
- The author recommends protecting the lower Yampa River, Desolation Canyon and the Green River through Canyonlands, and warns that changes to the Green River below Flaming Gorge Dam would harm the rare fishes.
- The author concludes that the upper Colorado River system itself could be considered "rare and endangered".
