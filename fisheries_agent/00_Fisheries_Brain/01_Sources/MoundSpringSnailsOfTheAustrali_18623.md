# Mound Spring Snails of the Australian Great Artesian Basin

#malacology #conservation #hydrobiidae #endemic-species #artesian-springs #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document reports on the endemic aquatic fauna of artesian springs associated with the [[Great Artesian Basin]] (GAB) of Australia, with particular emphasis on hydrobiid snails. The GAB occupies approximately 22% of the Australian continent (1.76 million km²) and supports over 600 springs containing at least 24 endemic species of hydrobiid snails in five genera (three endemic), along with endemic fishes, crustaceans, and other invertebrates. Many springs have become extinct in the last century due to water extraction, and most remaining springs face threats from stock damage, water diversion, and mining operations. The springs represent unique "aquatic islands" in an arid landscape, harboring relict faunas potentially isolated since the late Tertiary or early Pleistocene.

## Background & Description

The [[Great Artesian Basin]] is one of the world's largest artesian systems, extending through western Queensland, southeastern Northern Territory, northeastern South Australia, and northwestern New South Wales. Artesian springs occur on the basin's fringes, forming distinctive "mound springs" - calcareous travertine deposits, wind-blown sand accumulations, and vegetated hills that provide permanent water sources in an otherwise arid landscape.

Springs range from damp seepages to actively flowing systems with large pools and outflows extending several kilometers. Total discharge from all GAB artesian springs is estimated at approximately 1500 liters per second. Water temperatures at vents range from ambient air temperature to 45°C (with [[Paralana Spring]] reaching 62°C). Total dissolved solids typically range from 2000-4000 mg/L, with pH varying from 7 to nearly 10. The [[Lake Eyre Supergroup]] shows TDS of 2000-10,000 mg/L, while [[Dalhousie Springs]] ranges from 650-2000 mg/L.

The endemic hydrobiid fauna shows two major radiations: one in the [[Lake Eyre Supergroup]] (two endemic genera: [[Trochidrobia]] and [[Fonscochlea]], with 10 species) and another in the [[Dalhousie Supergroup]]. In the Lake Eyre radiation, four or five species coexist in most springs, with one species ([[Fonscochlea zeidleri]]) being amphibious. The aquatic species have speciated geographically, while the amphibious species, more resistant to desiccation, has not. Species of [[Trochidrobia]] are aquatic and photopositive, contrasting with [[Fonscochlea]] species that retreat from light.

At [[Dalhousie Springs]], the hydrobiid radiation appears less stabilized, with considerable variation within populations. Different morphotypes occur in different spring microhabitats, apparently responding to physical differences including spring size, water body type, current speed, and particularly water temperature. Snails appear larger and thicker-shelled where they coexist with catfish predators.

Additional hydrobiid species occur in the [[Springvale Supergroup]], [[Eulo Supergroup]] (Queensland), and [[Lake Callabonna]] springs ([[Lake Frome Supergroup]], South Australia). [[Edgbaston Springs]] in the [[Barcaldine Supergroup]] contains six sympatric species of [[Jardinella]] plus a bithyniid.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The springs exist in an extreme climatic regime with temperatures fluctuating from below 0°C to over 40°C. Rainfall is erratic, averaging 125-250mm annually in South Australia and western Queensland, 250-500mm in the rest of Queensland. All watercourses are ephemeral, with severe flooding occurring occasionally (every 8-10 years around [[Lake Eyre]], annually in some Queensland areas).

Spring vegetation is dominated by sedges (typically [[Cyperus]] species) that stabilize sediments and provide shelter for aquatic fauna. Green and blue-green algae are conspicuous. [[Phragmites]] and occasionally [[Typha]] form dense stands. Only one plant, the button grass [[Eriocaulon carsonii]], is considered endemic to artesian springs, found in springs near Hermit Hill, northern Flinders Ranges, [[Elizabeth Springs]] (western Queensland), and recently in northern New South Wales.

The endemic invertebrate fauna includes:
- **Crustaceans**: [[Phreatomerus latipes]] (phreatoicid isopod, monotypic genus and subfamily, widespread in [[Lake Eyre Supergroup]]); [[Austrochiltonia]] species (gammarid amphipods in Lake Eyre, Dalhousie, and Barcaldine Supergroups); [[Ngarawa dirga]] (ostracod, monotypic subfamily, abundant in Lake Eyre springs); unpigmented blind amphipod in Dalhousie seeping mounds; atyid prawns ([[Caridina]] sp. in Coward and Elizabeth Springs; [[Caridina thermophila]] in Barcaldine Supergroup); freshwater crayfish ([[Charex]] sp., possibly endemic to Dalhousie Springs)
- **Other invertebrates**: Flatworm (Macrostomida, one of only two Australian records); undescribed endemic oligochaete genus

Nine fish species inhabit South Australian mound springs, with two apparently restricted to springs. The introduced [[Mosquito Fish]] ([[Gambusia]]) is common in some Queensland springs and poses a predation threat to endemic animals.

The springs function as "aquatic islands in an arid sea," with each Spring Group representing a small island group and Spring Complexes forming archipelagos. Eleven Spring Supergroups are recognized around the GAB. Surprisingly, marked morphological differentiation does not occur within Spring Groups and Complexes in the [[Lake Eyre Supergroup]], though preliminary electrophoretic studies indicate greater diversity than morphological studies suggest.

Hydrobiid snails and crustaceans are absent or reduced in diversity in isolated springs, indicating the importance of maintaining multiple springs within groups rather than attempting to salvage single springs.

Springs in northern and eastern basin areas (except one notable exception) do not support endemic faunas, containing essentially the same fauna found in other regional water bodies. Regular catastrophic flooding in western Queensland and occasional flooding near Lake Eyre south may cause extinction or severe depletion of spring fauna, though this is a natural phenomenon unlike human-caused spring termination.

## Key Findings

**Conservation Status and Threats:**
- Numerous springs have become extinct in the last hundred years due to water extraction from the GAB
- Most remaining springs are threatened by stock trampling and fouling, water extraction, and mining operations
- All but one very small group of springs in northwestern New South Wales are now dry
- Many Queensland springs (western, northern, southern areas) are extinct
- Some remaining springs are so reduced in flow they are highly vulnerable to stock damage with inevitable extinctions
- Approximately 211 megalitres per day removed from the basin in South Australia alone, compared to 83 megalitres for all springs in that state
- The Olympic Dam uranium mine bore field is located near an important South Australian spring group

**Species Diversity and Endemism:**
- At least 24 species of hydrobiid snails in five genera (three endemic) inhabit GAB springs
- Lake Eyre Supergroup: 10 endemic hydrobiid species in two endemic genera ([[Trochidrobia]], [[Fonscochlea]])
- Dalhousie Supergroup: single radiation showing unstabilized speciation
- Edgbaston Springs: six sympatric [[Jardinella]] species plus one allopatric species and a bithyniid
- Endemic crustaceans include monotypic genera/subfamilies ([[Phreatomerus latipes]], [[Ngarawa dirga]])
- Two fish species apparently restricted to springs (of nine total in South Australian springs)
- One endemic plant: [[Eriocaulon carsonii]] (button grass)

**Evolutionary and Biogeographic Patterns:**
- Endemic genera and subfamilies suggest considerable isolation period
- Fauna likely represents relicts from wetter late Tertiary or early Pleistocene climate, or older fauna associated with artesian springs through much of Tertiary
- Pleistocene fossil mounds contain the two endemic hydrobiid genera, indicating fauna was diverse then
- Different selection pressures drive speciation in Lake Eyre versus Dalhousie Supergroups
- Lake Eyre: sympatric taxa readily differentiated, considerable ranges, essentially indistinguishable populations in widely separated spring groups
- Dalhousie: physically different springs contain morphologically distinct snails, differentiation responds to physical differences and predation pressure
- Electrophoretic studies indicate greater diversity than morphological studies reveal

**Management Implications:**
- Important to maintain multiple springs in a group rather than single springs
- Flooding may be important dispersal agent but can cause extinction/severe depletion of spring fauna
- Pastoralist modifications (damming, digging) usually result in extinction of endemic invertebrate fauna
- GAB may have achieved new steady state with recharge and discharge approaching equilibrium, provided no new major developments occur
- Bore capping and control programs in Queensland and South Australia reducing waste from free-flowing stock bores
- Recent incorporation of [[Dalhousie Springs]] in a National Park represents growing conservation concern
- Similar protection of other spring groups essential, coupled with proper management and conservation of artesian water
