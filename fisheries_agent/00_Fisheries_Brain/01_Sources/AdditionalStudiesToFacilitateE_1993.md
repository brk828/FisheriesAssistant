# Additional Studies to Facilitate Examination of the Peak-Flow Period in a High-Flow Year, 1993, Green and Yampa Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 Colorado River Recovery Program report documents supplemental larval fish sampling conducted by the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] (Colorado State University, PI Robert T. Muth) in the [[Green River]] immediately downstream of its confluence with the [[Yampa River]] at Echo Park. The study was initiated in response to an unusually high projected peak-flow event in spring 1993, considered a rare opportunity to evaluate physical and biological conditions during peak discharge. The primary goal was to augment existing USFWS/National Park Service sampling for larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) originating from a known spawning site in the lower Yampa River just upstream of the Green River confluence. Sampling occurred in two periods — 26–30 May and 7–9 June 1993 — using light-traps, fine-mesh seines, and drift-nets. Presumptive razorback sucker larvae were captured in drift-net and light-trap samples during both periods, though positive identification required confirmation via PCR. The study was deemed successful in meeting its stated objective, with a final report submitted to USFWS and USBR on 30 June 1993 within budget ($5,237.00).

## Background & Description

- **Presumptive Razorback Sucker larvae**: Identified provisionally based on **external morphology** and **timing of capture** (consistent with known spawning phenology at the Yampa River site); genetic confirmation via **PCR** was recommended but not completed within this report.
  - Late May sample: 2 individuals, approx. **8 mm TL**
  - Early June sample: 3 individuals in the 1–10 mm interval, 1 individual approx. **10–11 mm TL**
- **Nonnative cyprinids** (red shiner, sand shiner, fathead minnow) dominated seine catches as **age-1+** individuals rather than larvae, indicating established resident populations in backwater/shoreline embayment habitat.
- **Native catostomids** (flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker) were represented only as **age-1** individuals in seine samples — no larvae of these species reported.
- Drift-net catches were generally sparse across taxa (common carp, sand shiner, speckled dace, white sucker), typically single or few individuals per size class, reflecting low overall larval fish density in mainchannel drift during the sampling window.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Zero-velocity nursery habitats** (backwaters, shoreline embayments) sampled via light-traps at night and seines by day were dominated by **nonnative cyprinids**, particularly [[Red Shiner]] and [[Sand Shiner]], suggesting these habitats function as significant nursery/refuge zones for invasive species in the regulated Green River.
- **Mainchannel drift** (sampled via drift-nets at dawn/dusk) yielded the presumptive razorback sucker larvae, consistent with the species' known life-history strategy of **larval drift dispersal** downstream from riverine spawning sites shortly after hatching.
- The **Echo Park confluence zone** serves as a critical downstream receiving habitat for larvae originating from the documented [[Yampa River Spawning Site]] for razorback sucker located just upstream in the lower Yampa River.
- High **suspended organic-debris loads** during the peak-flow period required continuous net monitoring to prevent clogging/back-flushing, reflecting the dynamic, sediment-rich conditions characteristic of spring peak discharge in this system.
- Sampling was explicitly timed to coincide with an anomalously high 1993 peak-flow event, framed by the Recovery Program as a rare opportunity to study [[Peak-Flow Period]] effects on spawning success and larval transport for the endangered razorback sucker.

## Key Findings

- **No razorback sucker larvae** were collected via seining in low-velocity daylight habitats in either sampling period; all presumptive captures came from **drift-nets** and **light-traps**, not seines.
- **26–30 May 1993**: 14 light-trap samples yielded only red shiner and sand shiner (no larvae); 15 seine samples yielded no larvae (dominated by age-1+ nonnative cyprinids and a few age-1 natives); 21 drift-net samples yielded 2 presumptive razorback sucker larvae (~8 mm TL) plus low numbers of common carp, sand shiner, and speckled dace.
- **7–9 June 1993**: 8 light-trap samples yielded 3 presumptive razorback sucker larvae plus 1 at ~10–11 mm TL, alongside red shiner, sand shiner, and flannelmouth sucker; 8 seine samples again yielded no larvae; 12 drift-net samples yielded common carp, speckled dace, and white sucker but **no fish larvae**.
- Presumptive identification of razorback sucker larvae relied on **external morphology and capture timing**; the report explicitly flags the need for **PCR-based genetic confirmation**, highlighting a methodological limitation common to early-life-stage catostomid identification given morphological similarity among sucker species.
- The study reinforces the importance of the **lower Yampa River spawning site** as a source of razorback sucker recruitment into the Green River, supporting integration with concurrent Recovery Program studies on razorback sucker reproduction in the [[Green River Subbasin]].
- Overall fish abundance in drift-net and light-trap samples was low, and native large-river fishes (aside from presumptive razorback sucker larvae and age-1 flannelmouth/bluehead sucker) were poorly represented compared to nonnative cyprinids, consistent with broader concerns about [[Nonnative Fish Invasion]] impacts on endangered Colorado River Basin fishes.
- Study was completed on schedule and on budget, with the Recovery Program's Biology Committee-directed goal of augmenting razorback sucker larval monitoring at Echo Park considered **met**.
