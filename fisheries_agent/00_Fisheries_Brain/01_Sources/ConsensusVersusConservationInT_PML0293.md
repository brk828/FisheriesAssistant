# Consensus versus Conservation in the Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Implementation Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This policy-focused study evaluates [[Consensus-Based Management]] through the [[Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]], a multi-stakeholder plan created in 1987 to allow continued water development in the Upper Colorado River Basin while recovering four endangered native fishes. The authors combined a review of secondary sources (agency documents, newspapers, law review articles, case law) with interviews of 22 informants representing environmental groups, state and federal agencies, water users, fish biologists, hydrologists, and academics.

The authors conclude that the program enabled water development and broadened stakeholder participation, but failed at its central biological goal: fish populations have not recovered. They attribute this failure to two weaknesses: participants prioritize political and bureaucratic goals over species recovery ([[Displacement Behavior]]), and unequal power among stakeholders. They recommend judging success by population growth, maintaining the threat of a [[Jeopardy Opinion]], and reducing funding imbalances.

## Background & Description

**Focal species.** The program addresses four rare fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]:
- [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

The source's OCR text garbles some scientific names; the standard binomials above are used here.

**Historical pressures.** Since the [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922, irrigation projects, dams, and other water depletions have reduced native fish populations. Fish poisonings and the introduction of exotic sportfish further eroded native fauna. The source states that water development has "all but eradicated" native fishes (Holden 1973, 1991).

**Regulatory context.** The [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) required water users to pass federal permitting before development could proceed. A project could be denied if it jeopardized a listed species, unless a "reasonable and prudent alternative" was offered. Facing litigation, ESA amendment, a development halt, or negotiation, the states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming chose negotiation. A memorandum of understanding in the early 1980s laid the foundation, and three years of negotiation and public comment produced the 1987 program.

**Participants.** Participants included the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS), the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the three basin states, water users, and environmental groups. The program was designed as a model of [[Consensus-Based Management]] that would increase nonfederal participation.

**Methods.** The authors reviewed progress reports and annual status reports (RIPRAP assessments and sufficient-progress reports) and conducted interviews. Table 1 in the source lists interviewee categories and counts, which are summarized here: 22 informants in total.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water and habitat.** The program's stated purpose was to recover native fishes while maintaining or increasing water diverted from the river. Its five recovery elements are:
1. Habitat management
2. Habitat development and maintenance
3. Native fish stocking
4. Non-native species management
5. Research, monitoring, and data management

**Population status.** Fish populations were not recovering. By the most optimistic estimates, numbers had not changed during the program's lifetime. At worst, two of the species were thought to be locally extirpated. Several biologists believed the fishes would remain on the endangered list indefinitely and need ongoing human intervention. The source notes that measuring small fish populations is extraordinarily difficult and that the evidence is not uniform, though scientific evidence "seems to show" continued decline.

**Threats identified.** The authors identify flow management, sport fishing in native fish habitat, and non-native species as key issues. They note that agencies often pursued easier actions, such as hatchery construction and non-native eradication, rather than restricting water use.

**Development outcomes.** Since 1988, over 200 water development projects involving nearly 700,000 acre-feet (863 million cubic meters) of water had been approved. None had been litigated under the ESA.

## Key Findings

**1. Development succeeded; recovery did not.**
The program enabled water development and research on rare fishes, and all relevant stakeholders were included, at least superficially. However, the fishes were not recovering and are expected to stay listed for the foreseeable future.

**2. Success was measured by bureaucratic checklists.**
Progress was judged by checklist items in annual status reports, not population trends. Meeting a stocking target counted as success regardless of effects on existing populations. The authors argue this "advances bureaucracy but does little to advance actual recovery."

**3. Displacement behavior.**
Drawing on Merton (1957), the authors describe a pattern in which organizational means (process, consensus, reporting) displaced the program's goal of species recovery. Environmentalists' focus on [[Endangered Species Act]] reauthorization and agencies' preference for hatcheries illustrate this. Because the process supersedes recovery, the authors observe that no participant is clearly responsible for population declines.

**4. Unequal power among stakeholders.**
Water users dominated because they controlled much of the funding. Water developers employed a lobbying group working with USFWS to present funding proposals to Congress, and hydropower revenues (managed by the Bureau of Reclamation and Western Area Power Administration) provided the remaining funds. Water users had a clear, unified purpose; environmentalists did not, and some were reluctant to challenge the program for fear of harming ESA reauthorization.

**5. The consensus veto cuts both ways.**
Each interest group holds veto power. Participants reported that the veto sometimes blocked harmful actions but more often produced inactivity and marginal policies.

**6. Recommendations.**
The authors propose three reforms:
- **Link program success to population growth.** Actual population trends, not bureaucratic accomplishments, should be the gauge. This requires stronger, more centralized USFWS control to ensure reliable counts and consistent monitoring.
- **Maintain the threat of a jeopardy opinion.** Consensus should not immunize projects from jeopardy findings. Development should be prohibited if populations continue to decline, and short-term alternative goals should be set alongside delisting.
- **Mitigate power differentials.** Increased federal funding, ideally with USFWS providing the largest share (possible within the same Department of the Interior), would reduce the influence of water-development interests.

**7. Conclusion.**
The authors argue that the program is not an unequivocal success, and that conflicting interests made it unlikely any program could fully reconcile them. They recommend fixed, measurable goals tied to the real resource. As the authors put it, "we cannot blindly rely on a failing recovery program based on a successful consensus-based bureaucracy."
