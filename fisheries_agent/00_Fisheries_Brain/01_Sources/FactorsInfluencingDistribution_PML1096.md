# Factors Influencing Freshwater Mussel Distribution in the Blanco River, Central Texas

#malacology #unionidae #freshwater-mussels

## Executive Summary

This document is a bundle from *The Nautilus* (Vol. 94, No. 4, October 30, 1979). Its core is a study by Horne and McIntosh (Southwest Texas State University) on the environmental factors shaping [[Freshwater Mussels]] distribution in the [[Blanco River]], a tributary of the [[Guadalupe River]] in central Texas. The study examined the effects of flow, substrate and organic enrichment from the San Marcos secondary [[Sewage Treatment]] plant, combining field counts with laboratory tolerance tests to [[Ammonia Toxicity]] and low [[Dissolved Oxygen]]. The bundle also includes a reference list from the same issue and a short taxonomic note on the volutid gastropod *Marginella condemoi*, reallocated to a new genus.

## Background & Description

Freshwater mussels (Bivalvia, Unionacea) are long-lived benthic filter feeders. The authors note that they can serve as indicators of stream conditions because they are sessile, live up to about 17 years, and accumulate pollutants such as pesticides and metals at higher concentrations than sediment or water. Their use as indicators has been constrained by limited knowledge of species-specific pollution responses.

Eight mussel species were recorded in the Blanco River study area, together with shells of two additional taxa. Five native species, plus the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula manilensis*), were subjected to laboratory tolerance tests. The Asiatic clam is a recent invader in the region, and its rapid spread is discussed as a reproductive advantage over native, dioecious, slow-growing species.

The companion *Nautilus* material includes a reference list (mostly malacology and pollution-biology citations) and a taxonomic note by Miguel A. Klappenbach reallocating *Marginella condemoi* Carcelles, 1953, to the Volutidae subfamily Odontocymbiolinae. Neither section contains field or quantitative data relevant to the mussel study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** The Blanco River rises on the Edwards Plateau and flows into the [[San Marcos River]] about 1 km above its confluence with the Guadalupe. The bedrock is uplifted limestone, giving calcareous, hard water. The mean annual flow at the Kyle gauging station was 4.3 m³/s over a 19-year record. The river ceased to flow in several summers (1956, 1963, 1964 and 1967 per U.S.G.S. records). During the 1976–1977 sampling year the minimum flow was 1.5 m³/s, and no zero-flow periods occurred.

**Sewage influence.** The secondary treatment plant serves the northeast portion of San Marcos and treats less than 10% of the city's total sewage load. Its mean flow over the sampling year was 0.84 million gallons per day (range about 0.05 to 1.1 mgd). Effluent enters the river about 2 km upstream of the main study sites and is naturally diluted by river flow under normal conditions.

**Physicochemical conditions.**
- Water temperature ranged from 11.5 °C (January 1977) to 31.0 °C (August 1976).
- Secchi transparency ranged from 0.8 to 2.1 m, increasing at low flow and decreasing at high flow.
- Where effluent entered, all measured parameters increased except dissolved oxygen and pH. Ammonia rose about 300%, the largest increase recorded.
- Two km downstream, most parameters returned to near-upstream values. Dissolved oxygen returned to the upstream concentration only at the furthest downstream station.
- Mercury and chlorine were below detection limits in the effluent and enriched channel.

**Substrate and flow.** Gravel–cobble substrates dominated fast-flowing reaches, while mud-silt occurred on the margins of large, slow pools. The thin-shelled *Anodonta imbecillis* and small-bodied *Toxolasma texasiensis* were restricted to the finest substrates and were rarely found. Species with heavy shells, such as *Quadrula petrina* and *Amblema plicata*, were the only ones found where mean velocity was 1 m/s or more.

## Key Findings

**Field distribution and abundance**
- Quantitative counts used ten 1 m transects in 10 m² plots: three stations above and two below the effluent.
- *Amblema plicata* and *Quadrula petrina* were the most abundant native species.
- *Corbicula manilensis* was absent from quantitative samples in the spring and summer of 1976, but immature individuals appeared upstream in 1976 and became evident in the study area in 1977.
- Living *Corbicula* densities reached up to 50 m⁻² in uncontaminated headwaters. No living individuals were found below the effluent entrance.
- Mussel numbers and species richness were markedly lower downstream of the effluent. Large numbers initially found just below the outfall had largely disappeared by July 1977, possibly because of flood transport from upstream populations.
- The authors attribute the reduced downstream population to organic enrichment, since substrate and physical factors were similar above and below the outfall.
- *Lampsilis bracteata* is listed by Athearn (1970) as rare and endangered in central Texas. The continued presence of several species in the Guadalupe and San Antonio drainages suggests that their survival is not directly threatened.

**Laboratory tolerance tests**
- Tests lasted 168 hours, with a minimum acclimation of five days and at least eight specimens per species. Specimens were not fed during testing.
- Low dissolved oxygen (0–0.5 mg O₂ L⁻¹) was lethal to 17% of mussels in seven days, according to the abstract. The conclusions give a figure that OCR renders unclearly, so this value should be checked against the original.
- Ammonia at about 5 mg NH₄⁺-NH₃ L⁻¹ (approximately 0.26 mg NH₃-N L⁻¹ at pH 7.5–8.0) was lethal to 40% of mussels in seven days.
- Ammonia was the most lethal stressor. Its toxicity increases with pH, and the authors argue that the proportion of toxic un-ionized NH₃ rises sharply above pH 7.4.
- *Corbicula manilensis* had the highest survival under low oxygen, combined low oxygen and high ammonia, and diluted sewage. *Amblema plicata* had the lowest survival under these conditions. *Cyrtonaias tampicoensis* also showed low survival under low oxygen.
- Survival under high ammonia alone did not differ significantly among species at 95% confidence.
- In diluted sewage (side channel, one-month exposure), *Corbicula* showed significantly higher survival, *Amblema plicata* significantly lower, and the remaining species intermediate.
- Mussels that stayed gaping or released glochidia in ammonia or low-oxygen conditions died more quickly than those that closed their valves, which the authors interpret as a sign of stress.

**Conclusions reported by the authors**
1. Low dissolved oxygen was lethal to mussels within seven days.
2. Ammonia at about 5 mg NH₄⁺-NH₃ L⁻¹ was lethal to 40% of mussels within seven days.
3. Even in waters without signs of pollution, ammonia can be lethal to mussels during low flow.
4. *Corbicula manilensis* is more tolerant of sewage stressors than other species, and *Amblema plicata* is less tolerant.
5. Although physicochemical measurements did not indicate stressful conditions on the sampling days, mussels in the Blanco River appear adversely affected by enrichment from the San Marcos secondary treatment plant. Fewer mussels occurred downstream than upstream.

**Management implications**
- Flow matters. During zero-flow periods, the authors estimate that the effluent would be the primary water source downstream, and ammonia would likely become the critical stressor.
- The authors suggest that mussels can serve as indicators of non-polluted conditions, and that reduced numbers of "clean-water" species may be more informative than the abundance of pollution-resistant forms.
- Assessment of tolerances requires caution, because age-class differences and limited reproduction data were not captured.
