## Executive Summary

This 1995 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) memorandum documents an Intra-Service Section 7 Endangered Species Act consultation regarding the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (AGFD) proposal to stock nonnative sportfish at 90 locations across Arizona between 1995 and 1999. The review evaluates potential impacts of stocking nonnative species (primarily trout and catfish) on federally listed native fish species, including the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Gila Topminnow]], and [[Little Colorado Spinedace]]. The 90 sites were grouped into nine geographic categories spanning the Lower Colorado River, Little Colorado River, Agua Fria River, Gila River, Salt River, Santa Cruz River, and headwaters of the Rio Yaqui. For each category, a "no effect" determination was supported based on low probability of nonnative fish escapement into listed species' habitat, existing AGFD mitigation measures, and habitat/flow conditions limiting spatial overlap between stocked and native fish. The document underscores that habitat degradation (particularly coldwater dam releases and flow modification) rather than direct stocking is the primary limiting factor for many listed species' recruitment, while acknowledging that continuous monitoring and adaptive management are required given the dynamic nature of Arizona's fishery resources.

## Background & Description

This document is primarily a regulatory and management report rather than a morphological study; direct morphological data is minimal. However, it references key life-history and functional traits relevant to species vulnerability:

- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): A pelagic cyprinid whose spawning is habitat-limited to areas of warmwater spring inflow in the mainstem Colorado River or within the [[Little Colorado River]], reflecting thermal sensitivity tied to its physiology.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): A large-bodied catostomid; its characteristic recruitment failure is linked to the vulnerability of larval/fry stages to predation, particularly by introduced piscivores such as [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]].
- **[[Little Colorado Spinedace]]** (*Lepidomeda vittata*): A small-bodied cyprinid subject to naturally cyclic population fluctuations, adding uncertainty to impact assessments from nonnative trout predation/competition.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*): A small live-bearing poeciliid historically associated with spring-fed systems; populations have been locally extirpated following the interruption of spring flows (e.g., upstream of Roper Lake).

No detailed morphometric, meristic, or anatomical data (e.g., fin ray counts, body proportions) are provided in the source text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lower Colorado River (Lees Ferry–Grand Canyon):** [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] critical habitat overlaps with introduced trout populations established between Glen Canyon and Hoover Dams. AGFD telemetry data show stocked trout at Lees Ferry disperse minimally downstream (mean ~1 mile, max <5 miles), while the nearest humpback chub spawning site lies >40 miles downriver — spatially decoupling stocking impacts from listed species' critical spawning habitat. Coldwater releases from Glen Canyon Dam (Lake Powell) are identified as the dominant limiting factor for native fish recruitment, not stocking activity.

- **Lakes Mohave and Havasu (mainstem reservoirs):** [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] populations suffer from lack of recruitment due to mainstem habitat modification (reservoir conditions), not direct stocking effects. AGFD cooperates with USFWS to maintain populations here; safeguards are considered sufficient to protect [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Gila Topminnow]] in tributaries.

- **Lower Colorado River to International Boundary:** Remnant [[Razorback Sucker]] populations are believed to derive largely from Dexter National Fish Hatchery fingerling stockings rather than natural recruitment. Heavy predator loads (notably [[Flathead Catfish]]) limit recovery potential; growing fry to sub-adult size before release is proposed as a mitigation strategy to reduce predation by [[Rainbow Trout]] and other predators.

- **Little Colorado River:** Critical habitat for [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] overlaps with trout stocking sites (Nelson Reservoir, Blue Ridge Reservoir, Knoll Lake). Evidence exists of wild trout predation on spinedace; a prior Biological Opinion prompted AGFD to modify stocking procedures at these sites specifically to reduce jeopardy.

- **Agua Fria River & Gila River ponds/lakes:** [[Gila Topminnow]], along with [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] in the Gila system, face low-probability but nonzero risk from escapement of stocked nonnative fish from managed ponds/lakes into occupied habitat. Historic topminnow habitat upstream of Roper Lake has been lost due to interrupted spring flows.

- **Salt River:** [[Apache Trout]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Gila Topminnow]] face very low probability of adverse impact from stocked fish incursion.

- **Santa Cruz River:** [[Gila Topminnow]] habitat lies near Patagonia and Parker Canyon Lakes; despite proximity, no trout or channel catfish have been documented in topminnow-occupied habitat.

- **Rio Yaqui Headwaters (Rucker Canyon Lake):** [[Beautiful Shiner]], [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Yaqui Catfish]], and [[Yaqui Topminnow]] face negligible conflict risk from potential escapement at this single stocking site.

- **Secondary ecological interactions:** Nonnative fish stocking was also evaluated for indirect effects on the [[Bald Eagle]], [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], and [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]. Increased fish abundance was assessed as generally beneficial to bald eagle prey availability, though risk of tackle entanglement in eaglets from discarded/hooked fish was noted as an unquantified but non-jeopardizing concern.

## Key Findings

- Of 90 proposed sportfish stocking locations across Arizona (1995–1999), all nine geographic groupings received a "no effect" determination under Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act, based on low probability and/or insignificance of impacts to listed native fish.
- **Habitat degradation, not stocking per se, is identified as the primary driver** of native/listed fish decline in Arizona — particularly altered flow regimes and coldwater dam releases (e.g., Glen Canyon Dam) limiting recruitment of [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- Stocked trout at Lees Ferry show minimal downstream dispersal (mean ~1 mile), spatially decoupling stocking from humpback chub spawning habitat located >40 miles downstream.
- Predation by nonnative [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] is identified as a major bottleneck for [[Razorback Sucker]] recruitment in the Lower Colorado River below the international boundary reach; rearing fry to sub-adult size before stocking is proposed as mitigation.
- AGFD had already implemented proactive mitigation measures (modified stocking schedules, reduced nonnative introductions, water rights securement) prior to this consultation, which were viewed favorably by USFWS.
- Cyclic natural population fluctuations in [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] complicate attribution of population change to stocking-related predation/competition.
- [[Gila Topminnow]] has been locally extirpated from spring systems upstream of Roper Lake due to anthropogenic interruption of spring flows, independent of stocking impacts.
- No documented co-occurrence of nonnative trout/catfish with [[Gila Topminnow]] in the Santa Cruz River drainage despite geographic proximity to stocking sites.
- Secondary/indirect effects on avian species ([[Bald Eagle]], [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]) from stocking-related human disturbance or fishing tackle were assessed as discountable or insignificant at the population level.
- The memorandum emphasizes that the 5-year consultation window is a relatively long period for static impact assessment and calls for continuous monitoring, adaptive management, and re-initiation of consultation if new information indicates adverse impacts to listed species.
