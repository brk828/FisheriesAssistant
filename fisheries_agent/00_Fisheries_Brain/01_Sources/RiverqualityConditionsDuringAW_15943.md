# River-Quality Conditions During a 16-Week Steel Mill Shutdown in the Upper Ohio Valley, 1959

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This ORSANCO reference report, dated September 1, 1961, uses the 1959 steel strike as a natural experiment. Upper Ohio Valley steel mills stopped for 16 weeks (July 15 to November 7, 1959), and river sampling at 12 stations ran from July 21 to December 10, 1959. The study compares stream chemistry, temperature and biology during the shutdown with mill-operating years (1957, 1953, 1952). Steel-mill effluents clearly raised [[Phenolic Compounds]], threshold odor and dissolved iron. Coal-mine drainage, however, dominated hardness, sulfate and dissolved solids, and alkalinity responses were opposite on different rivers. A [[Fish Fauna Survey]] at Montgomery Lock and Dam showed a marked biological improvement in July 1959.

## Background & Description

The Ohio River forms at Pittsburgh, PA, from the [[Allegheny River]] and [[Monongahela River]]. Its upper basin drains 19,110 square miles at the Point, and 24,600 square miles at Wheeling, WV. The [[Beaver River]] (3,145 square miles) enters 25 miles below Pittsburgh and is relatively free of mine drainage. The [[Mahoning River]] also carries relatively little mine drainage.

The study was run by the [[Ohio River Valley Water Sanitation Commission]] (ORSANCO) with the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], the pollution-control agencies of Ohio, Pennsylvania and West Virginia, and volunteer water-treatment plant managers. The Commission had a 45-station permanent monitoring network since 1951. Twelve supplementary stations were added for the strike study (Table I). Total cost was $15,000, with a net ORSANCO cost of $7,500.

Upper Ohio Valley steel capacity was 45,000,000 ingot tons per year, 36 percent of the national total. About one-third of that capacity sits in the first forty miles of the Monongahela above Pittsburgh. Water use was estimated at 25,000 to 35,000 gallons per ton of steel, well below the commonly quoted 65,000 gallons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dilution and flow:** Flows were low and stable for 12 to 14 weeks. Hurricane "Gracie" rains then brought high, unstable flows. Reservoir releases supplied up to 67 percent of Monongahela flow during drought.
- **Mine drainage:** Coal-mine drainage is the dominant source of free acid, iron salts, sulfate and hardness in the Monongahela, lower Allegheny and Ohio. About 70 percent of upper-basin mine drainage enters the Monongahela.
- **Steel-mill chemistry:** Mills neutralize cooling water with lime, which adds alkalinity to acid rivers. This partly reverses acid mine impacts.
- **Thermal load:** Mill discharges warmed the river. Estimated thermal loading above Wheeling was about 790 billion Btu per day. Heat dissipation was rapid, with about 2.5 days to dissipate half of the excess temperature under certain conditions.
- **Phenol attenuation:** Phenols self-purified in summer but persisted in winter.

## Key Findings

**Staff findings (1959 shutdown vs. operating periods):**

1. **Phenols, odor and iron:** Phenol averaged 3.0 ppb during shutdown versus 16 ppb after restart. Threshold-odor numbers averaged 8 versus 29 at eight stations. Dissolved iron averaged 0.1 ppm during shutdown and rose two to seven-fold after restart.
2. **Manganese:** Levels in the Allegheny, Mahoning and Ohio were one-tenth to one-half of previous low-flow values.
3. **Dissolved solids:** Changes in hardness, sulfate and dissolved solids on the Monongahela and Allegheny were minor, because mine drainage overshadowed steel effluents. One steel mill alone discharged over half a million pounds of dissolved solids daily.
4. **Mahoning River (Lowellville):** Shutdown reduced hardness by 20 percent, sulfate by 50 percent and dissolved solids by 30 percent.
5. **Alkalinity:** On the Monongahela, alkalinity fell to zero during shutdown (versus 4 to 6 ppm before the strike). Steel mills restore alkalinity to the acid river. On the Mahoning, alkalinity rose from 8 to 35 ppm range to up to 80 ppm.
6. **Acid load:** The upper Ohio's total acid load is about 1,200 tons per day. Steel contributes about 27 percent (333 tons per day of 1,216).
7. **Thermal:** Most stations were 3 to 10 °F cooler during shutdown. Lowellville was up to 20 °F cooler. Wheeling's thermal load was about 75 billion Btu per day lower.
8. **Fluoride:** Strike averages were 0.4 ppm, about half of operating-period values. Non-steel sources contribute 8 to 10 tons per day, versus about 6 tons per day from upstream steel plants.
9. **Phenol loads:** At Midland, loads were under 100 pounds per day during the strike, versus 250 to 350 pounds per day in 1957 and 1953.

**Biological observations:**

- The [[Fish Fauna Survey]] at Montgomery Lock and Dam (mile 31.7) was sampled on four dates: August 29, 1957; July 30, 1958; June 27, 1959; and July 26, 1959. The July 26, 1959 collection, eleven days after shutdown, had greater abundance and more species, especially pollution-intolerant minnows such as the common shiner, rosyface shiner and bigeye chub. Six minnow species were new to the site. W. L. Minckley concluded that the change was "a function of the alleviation of industrial discharge."
- Water-treatment managers reported fewer taste-and-odor problems and lower iron. The Monongahela intake at Hays reported a pH drop to 3.9 and the first algae bloom in 35 years.

**Conclusions:** The steel industry is a significant but secondary contributor to dissolved solids, which are dominated by mine drainage. Steel mills strongly affect phenols, odor, iron and temperature. Flow variability is a dominant control on most parameters. The report's own estimates carry uncertainty, notably the acid-balance and manganese comparisons.
