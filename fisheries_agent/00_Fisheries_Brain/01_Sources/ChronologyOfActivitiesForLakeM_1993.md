# Chronology of Activities for Lake Mohave Backwaters — Spring 1993

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary
This report documents a 1993 spring propagation and grow-out program for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in artificial and natural backwater coves of [[Lake Mohave]], on the Arizona-Nevada border of the Colorado River. Wild-caught adult females were spawned, and the resulting eggs and larvae — along with laboratory-reared larvae and Dexter-hatchery juveniles — were stocked into several designated backwaters (Yuma Cove, Nevada Egg, Arizona Juvenile, Nevada Larvae). The report tracks spawning success, larval survival, growth rates via seining and PIT-tag recapture, and anticipates challenges from seasonal water-level drawdown and temperature/dissolved oxygen stress later in the season.

## Background & Description
While this report is primarily operational/ecological rather than morphometric, it provides several growth and size benchmarks for early life stages of the [[Razorback Sucker]]:

- **Eggs**: Green, water-hardened eggs; ~10,000 eggs per 50 mL subsample taken per female.
- **Larvae (wild-captured, Yuma Cove/NE, mid-March)**: Size not specified numerically but noted as substantially fewer in number than the prior year.
- **Laboratory-reared larvae stocked 6/1/93**: ~20 mm TL (total length) at stocking into Yuma Cove.
- **Juveniles recovered 6/15/93 (Yuma Cove)**: Average length 41 mm TL (from 26 juveniles across seven seine hauls), grown from the 20 mm stock in ~2 weeks.
- **Dexter hatchery juveniles stocked to Arizona Juvenile (AJ) cove, 4/6/93**: ~80 individuals; larger individuals were PIT tagged.
  - One PIT-tagged individual: stocked at **206 mm TL**, recaptured 6/15/93 at **325 mm TL** — a gain of 119 mm in ~10 weeks.
- **Lab-reared larvae stocked into NL/AJ (week of May 5)**: ~2,000 larvae per cove; an additional 40 individuals at 20 mm TL added to NL.
- **AJ cohort sampled week of June 14**: average length **61 mm**.

These data illustrate rapid early growth rates achievable under backwater rearing conditions, relevant for comparison with other [[Colorado River Fish|Colorado River native fish]] restoration efforts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large-bodied, long-lived catostomid endemic to the Colorado River Basin, now listed as endangered, with wild recruitment largely absent due to habitat alteration and predation by nonnative species. This report reflects a conservation strategy relying on:

- **Backwater coves** as low-velocity, warm, food-rich nursery habitats mimicking historic Colorado River floodplain/backwater conditions used for larval and juvenile rearing.
- **Artificial propagation**: wild adult females captured and spawned by hand (14 females spawned 3/3–3/4/93, 2-4 males providing milt per female), a common hatchery-supplementation technique for endangered catostomids.
- **Genetic monitoring**: sub-samples of eggs sent for genetic analysis (Tom Dowling, Arizona State University), reflecting concern for maintaining genetic diversity in supplementation programs — relevant to [[Genetic Management of Endangered Fish]].
- **Predation/competition pressure inferred**: substantial larval mortality attributed possibly to algal overgrowth smothering eggs, and to fish avoiding daytime-accessible seining zones (see Key Findings), suggesting nocturnal habitat use as a predator-avoidance behavior.
- **Abiotic constraints**: Water level drawdown (Lake Mohave predicted to reach 630 ft by mid-October) threatens to desiccate all lake backwaters, including Yuma Cove, necessitating fish relocation. Rising water temperatures and potential low dissolved oxygen (as observed the previous year) pose lethal risks to razorback suckers as summer progresses.
- **Interconnected cove system**: Larvae captured via spotlighting/dipnetting in Arizona Bay were transferred to Nevada Larvae cove for growout, illustrating a multi-site, staged rearing approach across the [[Lake Mohave Backwater Network]].

## Key Findings
- **14 female razorback suckers spawned** (3/3–3/4/93); ~150,000 water-hardened eggs stocked to Yuma Cove during the week of March 3, plus 70,000 more the week of March 14 (from 7 females); excess fertilized eggs redirected to Nevada Egg cove.
- **Low larval survival in 1993 compared to 1992**: Larvae captured in YC and NE during week of 3/15/93 confirmed some hatching, but numbers were substantially lower than the previous year.
- **Total apparent loss of the March-stocked cohort**: No fish from the original 70,000-egg stocking (mid-March) were observed or captured in YC or NE after mid-April; likely causes include excessive algal growth smothering eggs.
- **Supplemental stocking and improved recovery**: 400 lab-reared larvae (~20 mm TL) added to Yuma Cove on 6/1/93; by 6/15/93, seven seine hauls yielded 26 juveniles averaging 41 mm TL, confirming survival and growth.
- **Arizona Bay larvae transfer largely failed**: 350–400 larvae moved to Nevada Larvae cove in early March appeared healthy on 3/19/93, but only a single fish was recovered in early April — attributed to mortality or to fish occupying seine-inaccessible microhabitats.
- **Dexter hatchery stock outperformed wild-spawned cohorts**: 80 juveniles stocked to AJ on 4/6/93 with PIT tags showed strong growth — one recaptured individual grew from 206 mm to 325 mm TL by 6/15/93.
- **May stocking success**: ~2,000 lab-reared larvae stocked into NL and AJ each (week of May 5) showed confirmed survivorship upon sampling two weeks later; AJ cohort averaged 61 mm TL by mid-June (though high water later prevented further sampling).
- **Sampling bias identified**: Daytime seining substantially underrepresents fish presence. A documented 1992 comparison showed 4 daytime seine hauls in AJ yielding zero fish, versus 4 nighttime hauls yielding 44 fish — underscoring nocturnal habitat use and the need for night sampling or trammel netting for accurate population assessment.
- **Planned adaptive management**: A 1/2-inch trammel net was scheduled for deployment the week of 6/28/93 to better assess survivors too large for effective seine capture.
- **Anticipated late-summer risks**: Predicted lake drawdown to 630 ft by mid-October will eliminate all lake backwaters (including Yuma Cove), requiring fish relocation before habitat loss; elevated temperature and potential low dissolved oxygen (as seen in 1992) remain ongoing threats to backwater-reared razorback suckers.
