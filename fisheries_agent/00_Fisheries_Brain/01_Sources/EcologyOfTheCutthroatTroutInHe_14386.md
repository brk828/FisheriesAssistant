# Ecology of the Cutthroat Trout in Henrys Lake, Idaho

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines why [[Henrys Lake]], a shallow, fertile reservoir in eastern Idaho, supported an unusually large population of [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*). Field work covered 1950–1952, with the main sampling in 1951. The author integrates limnology, benthic and plant-associated invertebrate sampling, fish growth and maturity analysis, feeding ecology, reproduction, and disease observations. The population is characterized as short-lived. Its low average age results from disease, asphyxiation, predation, angler harvest, and post-spawning mortality. These factors together limit population size, even though the lake is productive.

## Background & Description

**Study system.** Henrys Lake covers about 6,500 acres at 6,472 ft elevation, with a maximum depth of 21 ft and an average depth of 12 ft. Its 104-square-mile watershed lies on the [[Continental Divide]]. The lake was enlarged by a 15-ft dam built across its outlet in 1922. Before that, a 1912 survey recorded a maximum depth of 6.5 ft and an average depth of 4.8 ft, yet the lake still held a large cutthroat population.

**Fish community.** Native species are cutthroat trout, [[Rocky Mountain Sculpin]], and [[Mountain Whitefish]]. Introduced species are [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Eastern Brook Trout]], and [[Redside Shiner]]. The redside shiner was probably introduced through anglers' minnow buckets. Rainbow trout and rainbow × cutthroat hybrids were rare in the sport catch. In the 1951 creel census, cutthroat and brook trout were taken in a 4:1 ratio.

**Origin of stock.** Little if any of the existing cutthroat population descends from the original native stock. Fish from [[Yellowstone Lake]], Wyoming, and Gold Creek, Idaho, were introduced.

**Methods.** The study used:
- Horizontal plankton hauls with a 200-mesh net
- [[Ekman Dredge]] samples from three stations, monthly, June–September 1951
- A townet for plant-inhabiting fauna
- Scale-based [[Age and Growth Analysis]]
- Stomach content analysis of 116 trout
- Tag-and-recovery of spawners

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical-chemical conditions.** Ice and snow cover the lake from about mid-November to mid-May. Under the ice, decomposition and respiration lowered [[Dissolved Oxygen]] from 8.1 ppm (September 1951) to 5.9 ppm (April 1952). Ammonia rose from 0.3 to 0.5 ppm, and carbon dioxide rose from under 1 ppm to 16.5 ppm, which dropped pH from 8.4 to 7.2. Bicarbonate averaged 130 ppm during the ice-free period. Surface temperatures in 1951 ranged from 48°F (early June) to 68°F (mid-August) and dropped to 51°F by late September. Wind mixing prevented [[Thermal Stratification]] in 1951. The lake was classed as a temperate lake of the third order.

**Plankton.** [[Blue-Green Algae]] dominated phytoplankton: [[Aphanizomenon Flos-Aquae]], [[Gloeotrichia]] sp., and [[Anabaena]] sp. The authors noted that blue-green algae can indicate high nitrogen and organic waste loading, possibly from heavily grazed meadowland. The zooplankton [[Daphnia]] spp., *Diaptomus leptopus*, and *Cyclops* were common but hard to count because phytoplankton was so abundant.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Higher vegetation covered the lake bottom except for the wave-swept inshore zone. Important species were pondweeds (*Potamogeton richardsonii*, *P. zosteriformis*, *P. pectinatus*), waterweed (*Anacharis canadensis*), and smartweed (*Polygonum amphibium*). Dead willows along drowned stream channels provided invertebrate habitat.

**Benthic and plant-associated fauna.** Bottom fauna averaged 175 organisms per sample and 6.1 cm³ per square foot. Dominant taxa included [[Midge Larvae]], amphipods, and leeches. The standing crop was 586 lb/acre for bottom fauna and 20 lb/acre for plant-inhabiting fauna, a combined total of 606 lb/acre for 1951. The authors compared these values with [[Heenan Lake]], California, and [[Fish Lake]], Utah.

**Growing season.** Scale analysis indicated trout growth occurs only during the approximately 180-day open-water period, from mid-May to mid-November.

## Key Findings

**1. Growth and body-scale relationship**
- Body–scale relationship follows a second-degree parabola: L = 21.59 + 6.879R + 0.03874R², where R is the anterior scale radius (× 37).
- Total length = 1.153 × standard length. Total length = 1.031 × fork length.
- Growth in length is most rapid in years 1–2 and slows after maturity. Growth in weight keeps increasing through years 2 and 3.
- Length-weight relationship (n = 351): log W = −4.6353 + 2.9529 log L, with W in grams and L in millimeters.
- Condition factor (K) was about 1.72–1.86 across groups. Older fish in age-groups IV–VI kept high condition despite slow length growth.
- Growth variation within age groups was attributed to time spent in tributary streams versus the lake, seasonal conditions, and sex-specific age at maturity.

**2. Age, maturity, and longevity**
- Males matured at ages II–III and females at ages III–IV.
- The maximum age was 6 years, but the angler-effective longevity was only about 4 years, because age-groups V and VI made up just 8% of the June–July 1951 sport catch.
- Age-groups II–IV made up 80% of the June–July 1951 sport catch.

**3. Mortality and tagging**
- Of 500 mature trout tagged in May 1950 and released, 12 were taken in sport catches and 20 (4%) were recovered in total. About 2.4% returned to the creel, indicating severe post-spawning mortality.
- Asphyxiation killed an estimated 3,600 cutthroat of all ages at the mouths of three tributaries in January 1952, attributed to local oxygen depletion.

**4. Sex ratio**
- In 1951, the overall sport-catch ratio was 100 males to 110 females.
- The female-to-male ratio declined with age and through the fishing season. Immature females dominated the June catch, possibly because recently spawned mature trout were feeding less.

**5. Feeding ecology**
- The [[Midge-Shrimp-Damselfly Food Group]] made up 90% by number and 74% by volume of the diet.
- [[Midge Larvae]] and pupae (*Tanytarsus nigricans*, *Tendipes plumosus*): 35% by number, 10% by volume.
- [[Gammarus Lacustris]] and *Hyalella azteca*: 27% by number and volume. Shrimp were possibly the basic food item throughout the season.
- [[Damselfly Nymph]] (*Enallagma cyathigerum*): 28% by number, 37% by volume. Nymphs were most available during the June–July shoreward migration for emergence.
- Daphnia ranked fourth by volume (7%).
- Feeding intensity dropped in August (average 16 food items per stomach, only 62% of stomachs containing food). Warm surface water and low oxygen probably reduced activity. Anglers succeeded in August only near cool sub-surface springs and tributary mouths.

**6. Reproduction and fecundity**
- Spawning runs at [[Hatchery Creek]] began in late March 1950, peaked after ice break-up in mid-May, and declined over the following two weeks.
- Fecundity increased with female size. Fecundity data from 1953 appear in Table 14.
- Over 11 million eggs were taken in 1950, with 93% average fertility.

**7. Disease and pathogens**
- Pathogenic bacteria, resembling [[Bacterium Salmonicida]] (the cause of [[Furunculosis]] in trout), infected mature spawners. Estimated infection rates were 3%, 5%, and 15% in 1950, 1951, and 1952. About 50% of trout in the trap were infected late in the 1952 season.
- Fungal infection affected 8 of 256 trout (about 3%).
- The authors suggested few infected trout survive, since no blistered or scarred trout were found in creels.

**8. Predation and parasites**
- Major predators were the blue heron, kingfisher, and osprey, mainly where trout congregated near the surface.
- A larval nematode (*Eustrongloides* sp.) was found in 1 of 116 trout examined, encysted in the stomach wall. Adults are parasites of fish-eating birds.

**9. Population conclusion**
The authors concluded that disease, asphyxiation, predation, angler harvest, and post-spawning mortality together limit how old individuals grow and how many remain in the population. The curves of growth in length and weight describe a short-lived population. Management information was the study's stated aim.
