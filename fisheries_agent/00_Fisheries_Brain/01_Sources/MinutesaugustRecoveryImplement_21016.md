# Colorado River Recovery Implementation Committee Minutes, August 30, 1989

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

These are the final minutes of the August 30, 1989 meeting of the [[Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]] Committee, convened by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) to coordinate recovery of four endangered fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin: the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The meeting covered the FY-90 work plan and budget, a petition to list the razorback sucker, the policy for using [[Section 7 Consultation]] funds and [[Depletion Charge]] revenue, and contested Section 7 consultations, most notably the [[Sandstone Project]] in Wyoming. Key decisions included approval of the FY-90 work plan, a policy directing future depletion charges toward [[Water Acquisition]], support for purchasing the [[Cross Mountain Ranch]] water rights, and a directive to develop five-year "road maps" for each program element. The Wyoming dispute over how "sufficient progress" should be judged was left unresolved.

## Background & Description

The Recovery Implementation Program is a cooperative effort among federal agencies (FWS, [[Bureau of Reclamation]]), state agencies (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming), water users, and conservation groups. Its purpose is to recover endangered fishes while allowing water development to continue. The program uses Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] to collect depletion-related contributions from water project proponents.

The four target species are large-river fishes that have declined across their former range:

- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*, Catostomidae): The subject of a 1989 petition to list. The petition attributed its decline to predation by exotic fish and to dams and diversions that fragmented the river into disjunct impoundments and tailwaters.
- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*) and **Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*) (Cyprinidae): Previously listed as endangered.

The meeting record includes an attendance list of about 45 participants, drawn from federal, state, utility, water-user and environmental organizations. It also includes an agenda and supporting attachments on sediment, propagation, Section 7 funding, consultations, the petition, water rights, and a letter from the [[Wyoming Wildlife Federation]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and flow.** Much of the program concerns instream flows in the [[Yampa River]], the [[15-Mile Reach]] of the Colorado River, and the [[Little Snake River]]. The Little Snake River contributes one-third of the flow but two-thirds of the sediment to the lower Yampa River, linking water quantity to sediment dynamics.

**Sediment.** The sediment ad hoc work group noted that the role of sediment in the fishes' life cycle is poorly understood. Spawning occurs in both low- and high-sediment seasons, and it is unclear whether eggs settle into cobble voids, lie on top of cobbles, or attach to suspended sediment. The group proposed a discussion paper covering problem definition, measures and studies. Proposed studies included a flushing-flow flume study with fish eggs, backwater habitat development, reoperation of [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], literature review, identification of sediment source areas, and investigation of two-dimensional modelling for geomorphological processes. The paper was due to the Technical Committee in early October 1989.

**Propagation.** The [[Propagation Genetics Management Plan]] (second draft, August 17, 1989) states that artificial propagation is a tool for initiating and sustaining natural reproduction and for maintaining rare gene pools in captivity, not an end in itself. Its stated goal is self-sustaining natural populations in current and historical habitats. The plan lists seven objectives, including a Genetics Management Plan, rare fish refugia as gene banks, and facility siting. Its FY-90 activities include genetics studies of *Gila* spp. and razorback sucker, larval feeding trials, and marking and tagging studies.

**Nonnative fishes.** The committee noted that the Service and states had not done enough to address stocking of nonnative fishes. Colorado and the Service already had an informal consultation agreement for West Slope introductions. The Service agreed to ask Utah and Wyoming for meetings on stocking procedures.

## Key Findings

**1. FY-90 work plan and budget**
- Research guidance went to roughly 50 researchers, who submitted 70 proposals. The Technical Group ranked these and used them to draft the FY-90 work plan, which the Management Group then modified.
- The Service's FY-90 obligation is $624K.
- The Implementation Committee approved the FY-90 work plan. Pustmueller abstained on the [[San Juan Management Plan]] project.
- An additional $20K was approved for the Vernal Fisheries Office to complete final summary reports for the [[Flaming Gorge Biological Opinion]].
- Utah anticipated difficulty meeting its FY-90 commitment and would explore in-kind services. All other participants expected to meet their shares.
- The $312.4K contributed by the Colorado Water Conservation Board to the hatchery feasibility study will be credited only if the Implementation Committee finds the end product useful.

**2. Accountability and research integration**
- Valdez recommended four measures: research technical committees, coordination at the annual researchers' meeting, required reports on all funded projects, and a numbering series for reports.
- McDonald recommended five-year "road maps" for each of the five program elements, to be prepared by the Management Group for the next meeting and updated yearly.
- Pitts asked for a summary report on each FY-89 project, with expenditures by project. Hamill committed to deliver it by the February 1990 meeting.

**3. Section 7 funds and depletion charges**
- The Service has collected $1,290,631 under the "Windy Gap" mitigation approach. Of this, $762,000 has been spent. After FY-89 commitments of $62,000 (USBR water-supply study), $37,500 (Little Snake yield and appraisal) and $25,000 (literature compendium), $404,131 remains.
- The Management Group recommended that unallocated Section 7 funds and future depletion charges be reserved for water rights acquisition and directly related work, such as yield analyses and appraisals. Flow recommendations were specifically excluded. The committee approved this with exceptions allowed through the Management Group.
- Under the program's earlier policy, no more than 50% of depletion charges would go to flow acquisition unless the committee decided otherwise.
- The Yampa Basin Feasibility Study (project 9) received a $60K credit to the Colorado Water Conservation Board, paid as $20K per year over three years, against its $100K investment. No credit applies if the project is built.

**4. Petition to list the razorback sucker**
- The petition, dated March 14, 1989, was received March 15, 1989, from Sierra Club, National Audubon Society, The Wilderness Society, Colorado Environmental Coalition, Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance and Northwest Rivers Alliance.
- FWS found the petition presented substantial information that listing may be warranted. The finding was published in the Federal Register in August 1989. A status review is underway, and public comment closes in December 1989 (the minutes give December 12, the notice December 15).
- A final decision is due within one year of receipt. The committee noted that the Recovery Program is not a regulatory mechanism that could preclude listing.

**5. Section 7 consultations: the Sandstone dispute**
- FWS reviewed 28 Section 7 consultations involving water depletions in the upper Colorado drainage: 21 finalized and 7 pending.
- For the Sandstone Project (average annual depletion of 30,600 af), FWS proposed a $306,000 contribution ($10/af) and release of 20,000 af of firm annual yield for Yampa flows until "sufficient progress" is made.
- Wyoming, through State Engineer Jeff Fassett, objected that progress was wrongly judged on water acquisition alone, that Wyoming lacks legal means to deliver water to Colorado fish, and that the depletion charge and water release should not both be required. Wyoming also cited the inconsistency with the [[Muddy Creek Project]] opinion, which did not require relinquishing firm yield.
- Reclamation, Utah, Colorado and Pitts sided with Wyoming. Pustmueller supported the Service.
- Hamill cited a Solicitor's opinion requiring "reasonable certainty of future results." McDonald asked the Service to develop a framework for future Section 7 decisions. The Service said it would keep working on the Sandstone opinion. The Muddy Creek opinion was reported acceptable to Colorado.

**6. Water rights and instream flows**
- **Yampa River:** The Phase 1 final report addresses flow needs qualitatively. The draft Phase 2 report with interim flow recommendations was due October 1, 1989. Validation studies will be considered for the FY-91 work plan.
- **Cross Mountain Ranch:** The 27,000-acre ranch, with 3,700 af of senior Yampa water rights, is offered at $2.5 million. The committee unanimously supported appraisal by [[Nature Conservancy]] and possible purchase by the [[Bureau of Land Management]]. Its adopted motion covers the Lily Park Pumps (36.67 cfs, 1886 appropriation) and the Cross Mountain Pumps (17.78 cfs, 1900 appropriation). Conditions include conversion to instream use under Colorado law and no payment until the water courts approve that change.
- **15-Mile Reach:** The final report on July–September flows was transmitted. The Implementation Committee asked Colorado to investigate alternatives, with Reclamation participating. Colorado suggested that Green Mountain Reservoir operations and the Orchard Mesa check be considered.
- **Ruedi/Green Mountain releases:** The Colorado Water Conservation Board proposed leasing Ruedi water from the United States for instream flows. A draft contract was sent to Reclamation, with a technical meeting set for September 11, 1989. If unresolved, an instream water right filing would be needed.

**7. Other outcomes**
- The Wyoming Wildlife Federation sent a letter of conditional support, asking that propagation be used only when a hatchery is required for research, that hatchery-reared fish be shown to survive and reproduce in the wild, and that natural habitat be protected. The federation also said it would fund specific water rights acquisitions only after Implementation Committee approval.
- Three final poster designs were selected for education and outreach.
- The next meeting is set for February 21, 1990, in Lakewood, Colorado.
