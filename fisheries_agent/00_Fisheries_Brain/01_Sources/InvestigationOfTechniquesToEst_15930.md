# Arctic Grayling and Apache Trout Lake Fisheries: Techniques to Establish and Maintain Featured-Species Lake Fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department Technical Report (No. 12, Federal Aid Project F-14-R; final report by Clarkson and Dreyer, September 1992, revised February 1996) evaluates a "featured species" management concept for two high-elevation Arizona impoundments over 1986-1990. [[Arctic Grayling]] and [[Apache Trout]] were stocked into [[Lee Valley Reservoir]], while [[Bear Canyon Lake]] received only Arctic grayling. Brook trout stocking ceased in 1986 and gear and bag restrictions were applied in 1987.

Lee Valley Reservoir produced strong angler catch rates (up to 2.20 fish/hr in 1989) but harvest rates stayed near or below the 0.1 fish/hr management standard. Bear Canyon Lake performed poorly, and the authors recommend reconsidering its put-grow-and-take grayling fishery. The authors judge physical-chemical conditions (high pH, winter oxygen depletion, eutrophication) the greatest barrier to long-term success at Lee Valley Reservoir.

## Background & Description

**Management context.** The 1985 [[Arizona Coldwater Fisheries Strategic Plan]] introduced the "featured species" concept, intended to provide opportunities to catch uncommon and unique species, for which demand was expected to exceed supply, especially in lakes. It proposed Arctic grayling and Apache trout as candidates. Earlier grayling and Apache trout lake fisheries in Arizona (1960s to early 1980s) were gradually fished out after culturing was abandoned.

**Management standards** for featured-species lake fisheries:
- 1 to 7 angler days per surface acre per week
- Sustained harvest rate of 0.1 to 0.3 fish per hour
- Harvestable fish of 9 to 12 inches (grayling) and 8 to 14 inches (Apache trout)

**Study objectives** included age, growth and survival of stocked fish; angler use, catch rates and harvest; angler attitudes; egg-take potential; bag and size limits; stocking times and rates; and trophic relationships. The planned stocking-density experiment was not implemented because of egg shipment failures and hatchery space limits. Apache trout were added in 1988, once they became available from [[Alchesay National Fish Hatchery]].

**Species background.** Apache trout are Arizona's endemic salmonid. Lee Valley Reservoir historically held Apache trout and [[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]]. Brook trout were introduced in 1953 and dominated the fishery under a put-grow-and-take regime before 1986.

### Study Waters

**Lee Valley Reservoir** (Apache County)
- Highest-elevation reservoir in Arizona, at 2,871 m, on the east flank of Mt. Baldy within the [[Petran Subalpine Conifer Forest]]
- Dam built 1899; spillway-level volume about 518,000 m³; mean surface area about 14 ha (maximum 18.1 ha)
- Maximum depth near 6 m; mean depth 2.9 m
- Mean annual inflow about 579,000 m³; mean annual seepage 244,000 m³
- Mean annual precipitation 76 cm; mean annual evaporation 112 cm

**Bear Canyon Lake** (Coconino County)
- Atop the Mogollon Rim at 2,304 m, in the [[Petran Montane Conifer Forest]]
- Dam built 1964; about 26 surface hectares; maximum depth 17 m
- Drainage basin about 1.6 km²; mean annual precipitation 46 cm; evaporation about 155 cm
- Long, narrow, steep-sided, and low in productivity; fish production was predicted to be poor (Kemmerer 1965)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lee Valley Reservoir**
- Water temperatures ranged from 1 to 19°C, with weak summer thermal stratification.
- Surface pH ranged from 6.0 to 9.6, highest during algal blooms. The 3-month period in 1988 with pH above 9.0 likely killed much of the Apache trout population.
- Under-ice dissolved oxygen fell to marginal levels (<4 mg/l) in some March measurements, causing partial winterkill of Arctic grayling.
- Conductivity ranged from 40 to 61 µS/cm; Secchi depth from 0.4 to 3.7 m.
- Historic creel data showed harvest rates falling from 0.38 fish/hr (1969) to 0.25 (1986).

**Bear Canyon Lake**
- Summer stratification was pronounced in 1987, with an oxygen-rich lens near the thermocline at 8 to 11 m. Epilimnion oxygen always exceeded 6 mg/l. No low-oxygen fish kills were recorded.
- Surface pH ranged from 5.7 to 6.1, rising to 7.0 in July. Conductivity was 28 to 33 µS/cm.
- Zooplankton densities were roughly an order of magnitude lower than at Lee Valley Reservoir, and benthos was not sampled.

**Trophic setting.** Lee Valley zooplankton were dominated by [[Daphnia]] before 1987. In 1987, rotifers rose and Daphnia fell, while [[Bosmina]] and cyclopoid copepods increased. Benthos was dominated by oligochaetes (up to 11,644/m² at mid-reservoir), with [[Pisidium]] clams, [[Hyalella azteca]] and leeches also abundant. Arctic grayling fed heavily on Diptera, Cladocera, Hyalella and plant material. Apache trout were omnivorous and, in this study, were the first documented piscivores of their species. Diet overlap between Apache trout and grayling was moderate (0.41 to 0.68 in 1988).

**Interpretation.** The authors propose that introduced grayling suppressed large-bodied Daphnia, which altered plankton structure and, through the [[Trophic Cascade Hypothesis]], possibly nutrient cycling and primary production. Abiotic factors (pH swings, winter oxygen) remain an alternative explanation.

## Key Findings

**Angler use and catch**
- Lee Valley angler use in 1987-90 was about half the 1986 estimate of 20,963 hours, yet still within management limits (2.4 and 2.7 angler days/acre/week in 1987 and 1989).
- Mean annual catch rates rose from 0.81 fish/hr (1987) to 2.20 fish/hr (1989), then fell to 1.66 (1990). Monthly peaks reached 4.39 fish/hr for grayling (May 1989).
- Mean angler success at Lee Valley was 50% (1987), 70% (1988-89), and 62% (1990).
- Bear Canyon Lake use was 3,700 hours in 1987 and 7,000 in 1988. Catch rates stayed below 0.2 fish/hr. Mean angler success was 6.1% (1987) and 4.5% (1988).

**Harvest**
- Lee Valley harvest rates were 0.22 fish/hr (brook trout, 1987), 0.06 (1988), 0.11 (grayling, 1989), and 0.12 (grayling, 1990). Apache trout never reached legal size.
- Estimated total yield to anglers at the end of 1990 was 546 kg and 2,618 fish.
- Bear Canyon harvest rates were 0.04 (1987) and 0.02 (1988) fish/hr.

**Angler attitudes**
- Most Lee Valley anglers favored bait prohibition (82.5 to 89.7%) and grayling introduction (76.1% in 1987, 87.9% in 1988). Approval of the 12-inch size limit was 93.6% (1989) and 94.5% (1990).
- Bear Canyon anglers were largely dissatisfied, with 62.5% rating 1987 trips "poor" and 54.7% "very unsatisfied" with fish numbers.

**Growth and survival**
- The 1986 grayling cohort (stocked as 43,672 fingerlings in November 1986) grew from 76 mm in May 1987 to 236 mm by November 1987 and 301 mm by November 1988. Maximum length recorded was 365 mm.
- Growth of the 1986 cohort slowed after age 3, coinciding with the 1988 stocking. Age at maturity was 3 years.
- Lee Valley grayling growth to age 4 exceeded that of Wyoming lakes in Curtis (1977).
- Peterson estimates: grayling 11,969 (1988), 8,189 (1989), 5,355 (1990). Apache trout 1,865 of 5,071 survived to October 1988.
- Stocked Apache trout (age-1) reached 234 mm and 127 g by November 1988, but nearly all died over winter.

**Egg take and hatcheries**
- Eggs were obtained from Wyoming (Meadow Creek) and Alaska. Alaska eggs failed repeatedly (about 100,000 in 1986; 525,000 in 1987; 199,000 in 1988).
- A spring 1989 egg take produced about 880,000 eggs from roughly 500 females. A 1990 trap-net collection yielded about 900,000 eggs.

**Management implications**
- Maintain the Lee Valley bag and gear restrictions.
- Stock about 5,000 age-1 grayling in spring to sustain current harvest. The equilibrium-yield model predicts spring stocking of age-1 fish could more than double yield compared with autumn age-0 stocking.
- Expect poor over-winter survival of Apache trout. Stock catchable-size fish if harvest is expected.
- Control eutrophication (for example, buffering with lime, phosphorus inactivation, fencing cattle from the watershed). Evaluate the effect of planktivorous grayling on algal blooms.
- Reconsider the Bear Canyon Lake grayling program. The authors suggest a relaxed-regulation put-and-take fishery, followed by a later creel survey.
