# Generalized Master Plan of Environmental Studies for the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #environmental-planning

## Executive Summary

This 1972 proposal, prepared by the [[Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory]] at [[Arizona State University]], sets out a generalized master plan for environmental studies of the lower [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] ecosystem. The authors argue that the basin is among the most heavily controlled and used river systems in the world, and that its managers lack the system-wide ecological data needed to predict the effects of future operational changes. The plan proposes three linked research components: historical and basic biological surveys, impact studies tied to planned operational changes, and long-term surveillance to establish a "quasi-baseline." It also proposes a coalition of about 50 researchers across eight universities and agencies, centralized data storage, and computer-linked data retrieval. Implementation depends on free access to operational data from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and on rapid inter-agency cooperation.

## Background & Description

The document opens by describing the lower basin as a low-desert system shaped by high human demand. Its stated purpose is to justify a research program before it presents the plan itself. Key background points include:

- **Water quality constraints:** Roughly three-fourths of the [[Total Dissolved Solids]] reaching the Yuma, Arizona, area enter the lower basin through [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Hot-desert conditions, evaporation, saline springs, and municipal, irrigation, and industrial backflows further concentrate dissolved solids, leaving water marginal for users by [[Imperial Dam]].
- **Growing demand:** Power use was estimated to double roughly every 10 years. Industrial and irrigation demand was beginning to rival domestic use. Water-based recreation and land-based recreation (camping, off-road vehicles) were growing faster than local populations.
- **Stepwise control:** Control was implemented in stages, including [[Roosevelt Dam]], [[Hoover Dam]], [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], and Glen Canyon Dam, plus Salt River Project pumped-storage upgrades. The authors view this stepwise development as a research opportunity because it creates natural breaks for pre- and post-modification comparisons.
- **Aesthetic values:** The authors state that aesthetic judgments vary among people and agencies. They suggest a separate "psycho-sociological" study, but say that ecological information must come first.

The appendices contain preliminary field checklists for the Gila Forks and Lake Roberts recreation areas of the [[Gila National Forest]] (compiled June 1970). These cover reptiles and amphibians, mammals, and birds, plus a fish list.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors describe several ecological pressures and data gaps:

- **System-level data gaps:** Earlier studies were scattered. The authors reject extrapolation from non-desert reservoirs and computer modeling without field data, because desert climate and high variability weaken statistical correlations.
- **Operational effects:** Flow volume through generator penstocks alters density currents in reservoirs and downstream reaches. Stabilized reservoirs (e.g., for pumped-storage operation on the Salt River) could support new riparian and aquatic vegetation and raise adjacent water tables, changing desert vegetation and attracting new animals.
- **Nutrient trapping:** Upstream dams act as "nutrient traps," which reduce nutrient input to [[Lake Mead]] and downstream reservoirs such as [[Lake Havasu]] and Lake Mohave. Impacts may take years to stabilize.
- **Baseline limits:** Because ongoing modifications introduce new variables, the authors state that no true baseline can be established. They use the term "quasi-baseline" instead.
- **Historical data:** Early [[United States Geological Survey]] water-quality records predate major control structures. The authors note strong correlations between parameters such as silt load and nitrate-nitrogen, which could be used to reconstruct pre-development conditions.

## Key Findings

**1. Three-part research design**

- **Basic surveys:** Centralize all published and unpublished literature, field notes, and reports, and conduct biological surveys in under-studied areas. The authors consider this first component "in part completed."
- **[[Impact Studies]]:** Prioritize areas facing imminent change, such as [[Lake Havasu]], where stabilization and additional flow-through are planned for intake toward Phoenix. Impact studies require pre-modification lead time, monitoring during implementation, and long-term follow-up.
- **[[Surveillance Studies]]:** Monitor control and to-be-modified sites monthly, aquatic and terrestrial, to build the quasi-baseline. Techniques include sampling transects across lakes and stream reaches, photographic records at USGS benchmarks, and time-lapse photography. The authors cite EROS-1 satellite coverage of the lower basin about every 18 days.

**2. Coalition and staffing**

- The coalition includes about 50 professionals at eight universities or agencies, plus at least twice that many graduate students or technicians.
- Institutions named include the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, Desert Research Institute, Prescott College, Museum of Northern Arizona, Northern Arizona University, New Mexico State University, University of Arizona, and Arizona State University.
- Undergraduates, working under supervision, could place 10 to 20 people in the field per project at low cost, and the authors frame this as training for future resource managers.

**3. Data management**

- Data would be stored in the newly installed Univac-III at Arizona State University and shared on request.
- The authors propose linking it by teletype to the University of Arizona's CDC-6400 and to Bureau of Reclamation operational data.
- Advanced statistics (non-parametric tests, cluster analysis) would identify key variables and redundant data, to reduce field effort.
- Collaboration with the [[Office of Arid Lands]] information retrieval system is planned.

**4. Prerequisites and priorities**

- The Bureau of Reclamation must share projected and emergency operational schedules so that field teams can be deployed in advance.
- Water quantity and quality priorities are already set by law and by treaty with Mexico. Recreation, refuge protection, protection of rare and endangered species, and research control areas are proposed as additional priorities.

**5. Fish-related content**

The appended checklist lists roughly 16 fish taxa from the Gila area, including:

- Trout: [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*), rainbow trout, brown trout, and eastern brook trout.
- Suckers: Gila sucker, Rio Grande mountain sucker, and Gila mountain sucker.
- Minnows and dace: Meda spinedace, dusky dace, brown dace, Gila spinydace, and the [[Gila Topminnow]].
- Catfish and sunfish: yellow bullhead, flathead catfish, smallmouth bass, and largemouth bass.

The checklist is a preliminary field list for a national forest area. The document does not report abundance, sampling results, or population data for these fish, and it does not link them to specific impacts within the master plan.

**6. Summary of the proposal**

The authors conclude that the plan can proceed only if operational data are made freely available and inter-agency cooperation is achieved quickly. They also state that the coalition has enough expertise to begin, though some specialists may need to come from outside the group.
