# FY 1999 Annual Monitoring and Research Plan: Colorado River Ecosystem

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fish

## Executive Summary

This draft Fiscal Year 1999 Annual Monitoring and Research Plan, prepared by the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Center]] (GCMRC) of the U.S. Department of the Interior, sets out the monitoring, research, and information-technology projects for the second year of a five-year Long-Term Monitoring and Research Strategic Plan (1998–2002). The program supports the [[Adaptive Management Program]] created under the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] and the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] Environmental Impact Statement. Its focus is the effects of dam operations on the [[Colorado River Ecosystem]], which runs from the dam forebay to the western boundary of Grand Canyon National Park, plus limited work on tributaries such as the [[Little Colorado River]] and Paria River, and on [[Lake Powell]].

The total FY 99 Adaptive Management Program budget is **$7,193,000**. Fish-related work is concentrated in the biological program, which covers native fish, the [[Lees Ferry Trout]] fishery, the [[Aquatic Food Base]], and riparian birds and vegetation. The plan is a continuation of FY 98 activities with limited new work, and it is intended to feed the FY 2000 plan, which is expected to change more substantially.

## Background & Description

The plan is a draft for review by the Adaptive Management Work Group (AMWG) and Technical Work Group (TWG). Its stated scope is:

- **Study area:** the Colorado River mainstem corridor and linked riparian and terrace zones, about 293 river miles, plus Lake Powell.
- **Flow thresholds:** physical, biological, and recreational impacts are addressed for flows up to 100,000 cubic feet per second (cfs). Cultural impacts are addressed up to 256,000 cfs under the Programmatic Agreement.
- **Governance:** the Bureau of Reclamation and AMWG set objectives; GCMRC runs the science. The plan notes that the Programmatic Agreement cultural program remains Reclamation's legal responsibility.

The Strategic Plan has five components: adaptive management, a conceptual model of the ecosystem, synthesis of existing knowledge, desired future conditions set by the AMWG, and an annual report on Valued Ecosystem Components (VECs). Stakeholder objectives span nine resource areas: hydropower, water, sediment, fish and aquatic biology, vegetation, threatened and endangered species, terrestrial wildlife, cultural resources, and recreation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dam effects on fish habitat and food base.** The plan states that abrupt changes after dam construction altered sediment transport, flow patterns, and thermal regimes. These changes affected habitat, predation, parasitism, disease, food base, and trophic links, and they are reflected in current abundance, reproduction, survival, and distribution of native fish. Interim flows since August 1991 have reduced stage fluctuations, stabilized shorelines and backwaters, and increased macroinvertebrate food production. Effects on humpback chub have not been documented.

**Temperature.** Mainstem water temperatures of 8–14 °C are below the 16–22 °C optimum range for reproduction and survival of eggs and larvae of these warmwater species. Low-temperature hypolimnetic releases from the dam are thought to impair native fish recruitment.

**Recruitment factors.** The plan identifies four main influences on native fish recruitment: hydrology and transport, food production and availability, water temperature, and predation.

**Parasites and invasive species.** The Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) has been reported in humpback chub since 1990, with unknown effects. Non-native fish compete with and prey on native species.

**Riparian and terrestrial context.** Reduced flooding and sediment transport have increased aquatic food productivity, established trout, and expanded wetland and riparian vegetation. Nearly 320 bird species have been recorded downstream of the dam since the 1920s.

## Key Findings

### Native fish (FY 99 biological program)

- **Native species present:** four species persist in the ecosystem: humpback chub, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker, and speckled dace. Reproductive success and recruitment appear impaired in all four. Razorback sucker have not recently been observed, apart from individuals thought to be hybrids with flannelmouth suckers, and the plan strongly suspects extirpation.
- **Humpback chub (endangered):** nine mainstem aggregations occur, with minimal exchange between them. Mainstem estimates are 3,500–4,000 adults, and one aggregation holds almost 90% of that total. The [[Little Colorado River]] aggregation is about 4,500 adults. It is the only documented area of spawning (March–May) and successful recruitment. The plan notes a suspected recent decline in this aggregation, with causes unknown.
- **Flannelmouth sucker:** spawning aggregations gather at tributary mouths, with the highest numbers in spring. Juvenile suckers are uncommon in the mainstem, and the plan hypothesizes that larvae die from thermal shock as they drift from warm tributaries into the cold mainstem. About 500 adults are observed annually at river mile 4 above Lees Ferry, despite water near 10 °C.
- **Lees Ferry rainbow trout:** about 70% of tailwater rainbow trout are now naturally produced, compared with mostly hatchery origin about ten years ago. Large trout over 5 pounds were numerous before 1983 but are now rare. Stakeholder goals are for natural reproduction to make up at least 50% of reproductive effort and for the population to be maintained at about 100,000 fish. A symposium on cold-tailwater trout fisheries is planned, with proceedings in FY 99.

### Biological Opinion and planning actions

- The 1994 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Opinion calls for a **second self-sustaining humpback chub population** in the Grand Canyon region by 2002. The FY 99 plan proposes a Request for Proposals (RFP) to produce a report and schedule for establishing it.
- The November 1997 Fall Test Flow Biological Opinion requires a program to evaluate factors limiting overwintering survival of young-of-year humpback chub, including habitat restriction, predation, reduced sediment, and cold water.
- A proposed RFP would design **endangered fish research flows**, combining low, steady summer and fall flows with higher spring flows, which FWS considers critical to native fish.

- Aquatic food base objectives include determining whether the food base limits fish productivity in the Glen Canyon reach, and whether large dam-release fluctuations affect it. Key questions include the role of *Cladophora* recruitment and the effect of temperature on lower trophic interactions.

### Native fish and birds of note

- **Southwestern willow flycatcher:** nests in tamarisk stands, and in the 1990s all recorded nests were over dry ground on elevated terraces. It failed to breed successfully in upper Grand Canyon in 1997 because of brown-headed cowbird parasitism.
- **Kanab ambersnail:** the Vaseys Paradise population, 31.5 river miles downstream from Lees Ferry, is affected by high dam flows. The plan notes the need to distinguish this species from two morphologically similar snails.

### Physical resources

- The physical program focuses on sediment budgeting, mainstem flows, tributary inputs (especially the Little Colorado and Paria rivers), and sandbar and camping-beach dynamics.
- A central question is whether the ecosystem is in sediment surplus or deficit. Beach/habitat-building flows only preserve sandbars if tributary sand supply exceeds export to Upper Lake Mead.
- FY 99 physical science costs total **$840,000**, with logistics of $335,000, for a grand total of **$1,175,000**.

### Cultural, socio-economic, and Lake Powell programs

- **Cultural:** the GCMRC program budget totals **$390,000**. The Programmatic Agreement program is budgeted separately at about **$800,000**. Tribal ethnobotanical proposals are expected to cost about $100,000.
- **Socio-economic:** the program totals **$63,000**, covering beach changes, wilderness experience, trout angler satisfaction, and boater satisfaction.
- **Lake Powell:** a 33-year water-quality record was analyzed in FY 97. The analysis showed dam operations significantly affect reservoir stratification and mixing, and those effects pass downstream. The FY 99 program continues current monitoring at a budget of **$250,000**, including quarterly lake-wide sampling, monthly forebay sampling, and tailwater monitoring.

### Information technology and governance

- Information technology has a budget of **$408,000**. Tasks include building a centralized database, GIS, data standards, web interfaces, and a library, with a stated commitment to free public access except for sensitive data such as tribal archaeological sites.
- A **Science Advisory Board** of up to 10 interdisciplinary scientists is proposed as an independent review panel. It would serve for three-year terms and review annual plans, budgets, and results.
- Independent Review Groups are budgeted at $243,000.

### Overall budget

- The FY 99 Adaptive Management Program budget is **$7,193,000**, which the plan states is sufficient for the programs it outlines.
- For comparison, the Strategic Plan projected about $7.0 million per year, with $8.0 million for FY 2000 to accommodate new monitoring technology.
