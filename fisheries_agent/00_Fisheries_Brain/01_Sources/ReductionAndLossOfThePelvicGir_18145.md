# Reduction and Loss of the Pelvic Girdle in Gasterosteus (Pisces): A Case of Parallel Evolution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gasterosteidae

## Executive Summary

This Contributions in Science monograph (No. 257, Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County, May 22, 1974) by [[Michael A. Bell]] compares pelvic girdle variation in a Recent population of [[Threespine Stickleback]] from [[Paxton Lake]], [[Texada Island]], British Columbia, with a fossil assemblage of *Gasterosteus doryssus* from the Pliocene [[Truckee Formation]] near Hazen, Nevada. Both samples show a similar range of pelvic structure, from fully developed to absent, with intermediate vestiges. The author concludes that the morphological similarity reflects parallelism rather than convergence, and that the two samples represent independent phyletic lines. The nominal species *G. apodus* is treated as a junior synonym of *G. doryssus*. The paper also places pelvic reduction within a broader pattern of parallelism across the [[Gasterosteidae]] (including [[Ninespine Stickleback]] and [[Brook Stickleback]]) and in other organisms.

## Background & Description

Variable loss or reduction of the pelvic girdle had been reported in the ninespine stickleback (*Pungitius pungitius*), the brook stickleback (*Culaea inconstans*) and several populations of *Gasterosteus aculeatus*, but pelvic variation in *Gasterosteus* had not been described in detail before this study. The fossil species was first described as *Merriamella doryssa* by [[Jordan 1907]], who considered it an atherinid because its specimens lacked a pelvic girdle and spine. [[Hay 1907]] described related material as *G. williamsoni leptosomus*, and [[Mural 1973]] split the Truckee material into two species, *G. doryssus* (normal pelvis) and *G. apodus* (vestigial or absent pelvis). The present study re-examined this material and tested Mural's species separation.

Materials included:
- **Fossil sample:** about 115 specimens measured (LACM 17439–17475, 17924–17945, 28090–28126; CAS 50033–50054), plus UCMP and Repenning specimens, all preserved as skeletons on laminated diatomite slabs.
- **Recent sample:** LACM 32008-1 (202 specimens) from the benthic form of Paxton Lake; 67 were cleared and stained for pelvic structure.
- **Comparative samples:** ten Recent *G. aculeatus* lots from southern and central California (roughly 60 to 350 specimens each) used for dorsal spine frequencies.

Measurements followed [[Hubbs and Lagler 1964]] and included standard length, vertebrae, predorsal radials, dorsal spines and rays, anal rays, lateral plates, spine length and pelvic class. Group differences were tested with the [[Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test]] at the 0.05 level.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stratigraphy and age.** The fossils come almost entirely from the Middle Member of the Truckee Formation, a diatomite unit about 460 m thick. The Lower Member yielded at least one specimen with normal pelvic spines (UCMP 99674). The Truckee Formation has been dated variously, from Miocene to Early or Middle Pliocene. The author suggests the fossil sticklebacks are at least 10 million years old.

**Paleoclimate and paleoecology.** Plant evidence from the [[Hazen Flora]] indicates an equable climate, warm but rarely hot summers, mild winters, and annual rainfall of at least 38 to 44 cm. Mollusks in the Lower Member suggest a large body of water with abundant aquatic vegetation. Diatomites indicate quiet, slightly saline near-shore water in a larger basin-and-range system. The only fish genera known from the Truckee Formation are *Gasterosteus* and *Fundulus*, which are euryhaline. Predation evidence is limited: one colubrid snake had a stickleback in its stomach, and coprolites lack stickleback bones.

**Dispersal routes.** The author evaluates two possible invasion routes from the ocean into Nevada: a northern route across the Sierra Nevada through river valleys north of Yosemite, and a southern route through the [[Death Valley]] region. Faunal evidence (e.g., [[Miller 1958]]; [[Hubbs and Miller 1948]]) favors the southern route, which the author considers most likely.

**Texada Island.** The island was submerged by postglacial marine transgression, so the Paxton Lake habitat is probably less than 5,000 years old.

## Key Findings

- **Similar pelvic variation in both samples.** The pelvic girdle ranged from developed to vestigial to absent in both the Recent and fossil material. Three pelvic classes (developed, vestigial, absent) were recognized. Developed fossils ranged from 34.0 to 73.3 mm SL (24 specimens); vestigial fossils, 67 specimens; absent fossils, 24 specimens.
- **Correlated characters.** In both samples, specimens with developed pelvic girdles had more dorsal spines. Recent fish with developed pelvic girdles had more lateral plates. Developed fossil specimens had more predorsal radials. Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests found significant differences in dorsal spine number (fossil DEV vs VEST D = 61.16%, p ≤ 0.01; Recent DEV vs VEST D = 36.37%, p ≤ 0.05), dorsal fin rays and lateral plates.
- **Stratigraphic segregation.** In the fossils, developed-pelvis specimens were generally found separately from reduced-pelvis specimens. The only exception was slab LACM 17452. Vestigial and absent forms occurred together.
- **No species split.** Variation did not indicate more than one species in either sample. *G. apodus* is a junior synonym of *G. doryssus*, which should be retained until the biological species concept can be applied to extant populations.
- **Differences between samples.** Fossils lack lateral plates entirely, whereas 59% of Recent sticklebacks have 1 to 7 plates on at least one side. Dorsal spine patterns on predorsal radials differ. Recent fish have 30 to 33 vertebrae (mean 31.54) and fossils 31 to 35 (mean 33.32). Recent dorsal fin rays average 11.06 (range 9 to 12) versus 9.03 (range 7 to 10) in fossils.
- **Parallelism, not convergence.** Because the two samples are separated by great gaps in time and space, and zoogeographic evidence supports independent origins, the author concludes that the similarity reflects parallelism driven by shared genetic systems.
- **Predator-driven reduction.** The author proposes that predator selection, documented by [[Hoogland et al. 1957]] and [[Hagen and Gilbertson 1972]], drives reduction of defensive structures. Spines act as deterrents, so alternative adaptations such as streamlining may be favored.
- **Broader pattern.** Parallel pelvic reduction is also reported in *Pungitius* and *Culaea*. The author cites this as support for [[Vavilov 1922]]'s law of homologous series in variation, and notes that parallelism complicates species-level systematics and zoogeography. Other parallel examples include [[Johnny Darter]] subspecies, [[Myoxocephalus quadricornis]] freshwater isolates, and autopolyploidy in *Epilobium*.
