# Loss of Stream Passage in the Decline of the Colorado Squawfish, with a Black-Footed Ferret Recovery Case Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document combines two conservation papers. The first is a symposium paper on the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*[[Ptychocheilus Lucius]]*), an endangered freshwater cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Radiotelemetry work showed that the species undertakes long-distance spawning migrations, and the paper argues that dam-induced blockage of stream passage is a likely prime cause of its decline. The second is a case-study outline of the [[Black-Footed Ferret]] (*[[Mustela Nigripes]]*) recovery effort in Wyoming, carried out by an [[Isu/Biota Research Team]]. The ferret paper is a framework for future case studies rather than a report of results.

## Background & Description

**Colorado Squawfish.** The Colorado squawfish is the top carnivore of the Colorado River system. It once supported a commercial fishery. Its range and abundance have declined steadily since the early 1900s, and the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] listed it as endangered in 1974 under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The species now occupies only about 25% of its original range.

**Black-Footed Ferret.** The black-footed ferret is the only ferret native to North America. Its original range closely overlapped that of the [[Prairie Dog]]. Ferrets were rarely observed for much of the twentieth century. A small population discovered in Wyoming in 1981 offered a conservation opportunity the authors describe as unparalleled in the United States. The authors also cite broader concern that the extinction of many species over the next 2–3 decades could be catastrophic.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dam effects on riverine habitat.** The decline of the Colorado squawfish has been linked by most researchers to water development. Dams convert riverine habitat to lacustrine habitat and create cold, fluctuating tailwaters. They also change temperature, turbidity and salinity, and alter seasonal and daily flow patterns. The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] (1978) identified these changes as causes of the decline in numbers and range.

**Lower basin.** The Colorado squawfish has disappeared from the lower Colorado River basin. The [[Grand Canyon]] section of the mainstem is the only essentially unregulated reach, and its temperature regime is not favorable for the species. Closure of a mainstem dam was followed by attempts by large numbers of fish to pass the dam. The fish no longer occur above it.

**Upper basin.** The [[Green River]] basin holds the greatest abundance of the species. The [[Yampa River]] mainstem is not controlled and moderates conditions in the Green River. Planned projects would dam Green River tributaries, and a diversion on the Green River could obstruct fish passage near Green River, Utah. No passage evaluations had been documented for these projects.

**Fish passage and reservoir operations.** Adults and juveniles would both need passage routes. Juvenile passage and the manipulation of dams to provide downstream nursery habitat were described as potentially more difficult than adult passage. Reservoir operation is also closely tied to survival.

**Ferret habitat.** The paper gives little ecological detail. It notes that the ferret's range is associated with prairie dog colonies and that its current distribution is largely unknown.

## Key Findings

### Colorado Squawfish

- **Long-distance spawning migration.** Radiotelemetry by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in 1981 documented migrations that appear critical to the species' life history.
- **Spawning site discovery.** Eight fish were radiotracked in the [[Lower Green River]] in 1981. Collections at this site produced 35 ripe fish, including three recaptured fish previously tagged in the upper Yampa and Green Rivers.
- **Successful reproduction.** Larval fish were collected at the site and confirmed by field studies in 1982 by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the [[Colorado Division Of Wildlife]] and the [[National Park Service]].
- **Historic migration.** Earlier researchers also noted increased numbers of ripe fish at this location from 1968 to 1975, suggesting the migration is longstanding.
- **Movement distances.** Fish moved both upstream and downstream to reach the spawning site. Average one-way distances were reported as roughly 24–153 km. Further studies suggested that only two spawning sites may exist in the upper Green River basin, with travel distances of up to 250 km. The sites are widely separated and located in restricted canyon habitats.
- **Potamodromy.** Long-distance spawning migration of freshwater fish, or [[Potamodromy]], is uncommon in North America. Some Asian cyprinids migrate as far as 1000 km. Colorado squawfish migrations are hard to observe because the fish live and move within the same large river system year-round, and only very large specimens (over 500 mm) migrate.
- **Larval and juvenile movement.** Larvae drift downstream from spawning grounds into shallow, productive nursery habitat. Juveniles then move upstream and ascend tributary streams, repopulating upstream habitats. The authors argue this strategy is adaptive under historic flow regimes but may be undermined by artificial habitat changes.
- **Passage as a primary cause.** The authors conclude that blockage of stream passage is possibly the prime factor in the species' loss. No research had yet been directed toward passage through dams. Passage of downstream migrants had been observed only occasionally in recent times.
- **Recovery needs.** Recovery requires maintaining existing populations and reintroducing fish to parts of their former range. Open questions include whether adults are obligate spawners that must return to natal spawning grounds, and the age at which imprinting occurs. Stocking hatchery-reared fish may be ineffective if they do not migrate to suitable spawning grounds. Reintroduction of eggs or young below dams was proposed. No research had yet addressed these needs.
- **Conclusion.** The authors state that proposed upper-basin projects may result in the species' extinction or extirpation from the wild. Recovery to non-endangered status will require much more information about its migratory requirements and its ability to adapt to fish passageways.

### Black-Footed Ferret

- **Research team.** The [[Isu/Biota Research Team]] at Idaho State University and Biota Research and Consulting, Inc. led the study. It included Louise Richardson, Denise Casey, Steven Forrest, Thomas W. Campbell III, and the lead author.
- **Stated objectives.** The paper aimed to describe the team's role and thinking in ferret recovery, outline aspects of the program useful to similar efforts in other states, encourage agencies and ranching communities to document their own efforts, and suggest that many points apply to threatened and endangered species programs generally.
- **Historic record.** Ferrets were so rarely seen that little was known about them until 1964, when a ferret was found in western South Dakota. From then until the study period, one or two families were found in the area nearly every year. In total, eleven litters and about 90 different ferrets were found.
- **Funding and support.** The study received support from several conservation organizations, including the [[New York Zoological Society]], [[Wildlife Preservation Trust International]], the [[National Wildlife Federation]], [[The Nature Conservancy]], and [[World Wildlife Fund]]. Local residents of the Big Horn Basin were also crucial to the work.
