# Performance of Stationary and Portable Passive Transponder Detection Systems for Monitoring Fish Movements

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This methods study, by Barbin Zydlewski, Haro and Whalen (affiliated with the [[Conte Anadromous Fish Research Center]], USGS Biological Resources Division), tests two new [[Passive Integrated Transponder]] (PIT) detection systems for long-range monitoring of fish movement. Earlier PIT systems used 12 mm tags and small coil antennae, which limited them to laboratory work and shallow water (about 18 cm). The new systems use 23 mm [[Texas Instruments TIRIS]] tags and read tags at distances of up to 0.45–0.5 m. A [[Stationary Detection System]] spanned the full 8 m width of [[Smith Brook]], and a [[Portable Detection System]] (backpack-mounted) was used to scan habitat for tagged fish. Both were tested on [[Atlantic Salmon]] ([[Salmo Salar]]) parr and smolts. The stationary system was about 93% efficient and operated under all flow conditions. The portable system was effective for locating fish in habitat without recapture.

## Background & Description

**Study context.** PIT technology was increasingly used to track individual fish, but most applications were confined to laboratories or small-scale field trials because of short read ranges (Prentice et al., 1990; Armstrong et al., 1996; Greenberg & Giller, 2000). The authors aimed to overcome these range limits.

**Tagging.** Fish were implanted internally with 23 mm long, 3.4 mm diameter, 0.6 g (in air) tags (Texas Instruments model RI-TRP-RRHP). Key tagging constraints:
- Tags should not be used in fish under 8.5 cm without a suture.
- Sutures are suggested for fish between 6.4 and 8.4 cm (Roussel et al., 2000).

**Study site.** Smith Brook is a small tributary of the [[West River]], about 8 m wide at the stationary site.

**Stationary system.**
- Two open-coil inductor loop antennae, each 4 m wide by 1.2 m high, spanned the channel.
- Tags were interrogated every 100 ms, and the full flow volume was scanned at all discharges without obstructing fish passage.
- Each antenna connected to a [[TIRIS 5-2000 Reader]] powered by two 12 V deep-cycle marine batteries (60 Ah in total, depending on configuration). Battery run time was 2–4 days, depending on air temperature.
- Data were logged to two palmtop computers (Hewlett Packard HP 200LX). Equipment was housed in a weatherproof box outside the flood zone. For sub-zero operation, equipment was insulated and heated with a thermostat-controlled element.

**Portable system.**
- A backpack unit carried a TIRIS 5-2000 reader powered by a 12 V, 25 Ah sealed battery, providing about 20 h of continuous operation.
- It used a 2 m antenna wand with a 0.5 m diameter loop.
- A palmtop computer (HP 100LX) stored data and gave an audio beep on each detection.
- Total system weight was about 10 kg.

**Tagged population.** Between 200 and 500 age 1+ and 2+ parr (>9.0 cm) were tagged upstream of the stationary system in each of three consecutive autumns beginning October 1997. The stationary system ran from April–June 1998 and again from October 1998 onward.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and weather:** Both systems were built to withstand high flows, freezing temperatures and flooding. The [[Fixed Smolt Trap]] downstream was disrupted by several washouts at high flows, while the stationary antennae kept working under all flow conditions.
- **Substrate and cover:** Portable detection was tested through logs, rocks, cobble, ice and substrata at depths of up to 1 m, with little or no loss of range.
- **Fish welfare:** In laboratory trials, parr survival after tagging and tag retention for both hatchery-reared and stream-reared parr exceeded 99%. Tagging had no significant effect on growth. Wild tagged parr showed size-at-age comparable with untagged parr.
- **Migration timing:** Spring smolt migration in Smith Brook coincided with rising water temperature, and the last 60% of smolt movement occurred during that rise.

## Key Findings

**Stationary system performance**
- Efficiency was about 93% (the error term is illegible in the scan), based on tagged fish, smolts collected in a downstream fixed trap, and tagged drifts.
- Maximum read range was 0.45 m from the antenna plane. Tags passed through the centre with their long axis parallel to the antenna plane could not be read.

**Smolt migration, spring 1998**
- Of 230 parr tagged in October 1997, 24 individuals (probably smolts) were detected at the stationary system from April to June 1998, about 10% of the tagged population. Many fish were too small to smolt. The authors note this recruitment rate falls within the 9–37% range reported from adjacent West River tributaries (Whalen et al., 2000).
- Only fish over 11.0 cm at tagging in October 1997 were detected migrating as smolts.
- Twenty of the 24 fish passed the stationary system between 25 April and early May. No fish were detected after 6 May.
- Detection at the stationary system preceded capture at the fixed trap 0.5 km downstream by about one week. The authors concluded the stationary system gave a more accurate picture of smolt migration timing.

**Portable system performance**
- Read range was up to 0.5 m when the tag's long axis was perpendicular to the loop plane and up to about 0.1 m when parallel to it.
- Tags were detectable through logs, rocks, cobble and ice with little or no loss of range.
- Tagged fish could be detected within a 10 cm² area of the wand loop.
- In two originally tagged sites scanned after the smolt migration, 27 tagged fish changed position after one sweep, indicating live fish. Five did not move when rocks were removed, possibly representing stationary fish, lost tags or dead fish.
- Fish detected by the portable system were smaller (n = 32) than those tagged the previous autumn (11.4 cm, n = 683; p < 0.001, Mann-Whitney test).
- About 50% of fish from each site scanned with the portable system were accounted for by the stationary system.

**Advantages and limitations**
- The stationary system was rarely limited by climate, unlike [[Flat-Bed Antenna Arrays]], traps, weirs, snorkel surveys and electrofishing.
- Large tags allow reading distances over 1 m water depth without disturbing fish.
- Both systems have simple antenna designs and installation, and minimal power needs (1 A continuous draw per reader at 12 V).
- Limitations: no data on growth or physiology; cannot separate two fish in the antenna field at the same time; rare tag orientations go undetected; the larger tags require larger fish.

**Potential applications**
- Estimating winter survival
- Measuring migration timing between two or more stationary systems
- Tracking overall run timing past passage structures
- Monitoring fine-scale juvenile movements and microhabitat preference
- Studying habitat use, movement and migration in most animal groups
