It is a policy/legal comment letter from the City of Phoenix addressing the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Final Biological Opinion (BO) on Lower Colorado River operations, focused on riparian habitat and the endangered **Southwestern Willow Flycatcher**, a bird species, not a fish. The note below is synthesized faithfully to the actual content: riparian habitat classification, water-management operations, and species-habitat controversy along the Lower Colorado River system — an aquatic-adjacent ecosystem management issue rather than fish biology.

## Executive Summary
This letter, submitted by the City of Phoenix to the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, disputes key habitat assessments in the Fish and Wildlife Service's Final [[Biological Opinion]] governing [[Lower Colorado River]] maintenance and operations. The core scientific dispute centers on whether **salt cedar (tamarisk)** vegetation should be classified as "suitable" nesting habitat for the endangered [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], alongside native cottonwood-willow stands. Phoenix argues that excluding salt cedar from suitable habitat calculations artificially inflates the perceived ecological impact of river operations (particularly [[Lake Mead]] delta inundation) and creates inconsistencies with the parallel Biological Opinion for [[Modified Roosevelt Dam]], where salt cedar-equivalent habitat was treated differently. The letter is primarily a regulatory and habitat-classification critique, not a fish-focused study, but it documents riparian vegetation composition and hydrological-habitat interactions relevant to Colorado River basin management.

## Background & Description
*Not applicable — no fish or aquatic organism morphology is described in this source.* The document instead addresses riparian vegetation structure relevant to avian nesting habitat:
- **Cottonwood-willow habitat**: native riparian vegetation type, cited as covering only **4,547 acres** along the Lower Colorado River.
- **Salt cedar (tamarisk) habitat**: non-native riparian vegetation type, covering **~44,000–44,843 acres** between Davis Dam and the Mexican border — roughly ten times the cottonwood-willow acreage.
- Vegetation survey data (1996) of 27 occupied flycatcher sites on the Lower Colorado River: 21 sites surveyed, with salt cedar as the **dominant habitat in 52%** of sites and **salt cedar-willow mixtures in an additional 15%**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Species in focus**: [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], an endangered riparian-nesting songbird dependent on dense streamside vegetation along the [[Lower Colorado River]], [[Lake Mead]] delta, [[San Pedro River]], and [[Roosevelt Lake]].
- **Habitat dependency**: The species nests in both native cottonwood-willow stands and non-native salt cedar thickets; the letter cites evidence from [[Modified Roosevelt Dam]] operations demonstrating salt cedar functions as legitimate nesting habitat.
- **Hydrological interactions**: Reservoir operations (impoundment levels at Lake Mead, Roosevelt Lake) directly affect riparian habitat availability through inundation and delta habitat loss — a dynamic analogous to riparian-aquatic interface effects seen in fish habitat studies (e.g., backwater effects, sediment/vegetation zonation).
- **Disturbance ecology**: Reference to the **San Pedro PZ ranch fire**, noted as only partially destructive — three breeding pairs were reportedly unaffected, contradicting the BO's characterization of "lost" habitat.
- **Habitat loss framing**: The BO characterizes the loss of **1,219 acres** of willow habitat in the Lake Mead delta as "catastrophic," a characterization Phoenix disputes given the much larger extant salt cedar habitat base.

## Key Findings
1. **Habitat classification bias**: The BO's definition of "suitable" habitat excludes salt cedar despite field data showing salt cedar as the dominant vegetation type at a majority of occupied nesting sites (52% dominant, +15% mixed) — a methodological inconsistency Phoenix argues inflates perceived impacts.
2. **Cross-consultation inconsistency**: The Lower Colorado River BO and the [[Modified Roosevelt Dam]] BO treat comparable habitat and risk scenarios differently (e.g., extirpation risk language, reproduction-loss assumptions, habitat-loss claims for Roosevelt Lake and San Pedro River).
3. **Disputed impact severity**: The Roosevelt BO assumes total loss of birds/reproduction from inundation, while the Lower Colorado River BO acknowledges only partial (unquantified) reproductive loss from Lake Mead delta habitat inundation — an inconsistent severity standard.
4. **Fire impact overstatement**: The claimed destruction of San Pedro River habitat by the PZ ranch fire is contradicted by Bureau of Reclamation biologist Henry Messing, who reported at least three breeding pairs unaffected.
5. **Regulatory implication**: The Fish and Wildlife Service's own RPA #11 recognizes the need to protect salt cedar habitat, which Phoenix argues legally undercuts the BO's earlier analytical exclusion of salt cedar from "suitable habitat" — supporting a call for **reinitiation of consultation**.
6. **Broader basin relevance**: The letter frames these habitat-classification issues as materially affecting Central Arizona Project water deliveries and the City of Phoenix's Colorado River water allocations, given ongoing litigation by the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]] seeking to link the two Biological Opinions.
