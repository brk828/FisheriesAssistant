# A New Gila Topminnow Locality in Southern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service Research Note (RM-382, February 1980) by [[John N. Rinne]], [[Bryce Rickel]], and [[Dean Hendrickson]] reports a newly discovered population of the [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), an [[Endangered Species]], in an intermittent stream in Redrock Canyon on the [[Coronado National Forest]]. The authors argue that the population should be protected from mining, water extraction, off-road vehicles, grazing, and nonnative fishes if it is to persist in this desert environment. They also recommend using the Redrock system for transplanting gravid females to expand the species' range.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow is a small livebearer native to low desert streams of Arizona. It was once among the most common low-desert native fishes in the state but has declined sharply in range and abundance (Minckley et al. 1977), leading to its designation as endangered (U.S. Department of the Interior 1976).

Before this discovery, the species occurred naturally at only seven localities: six in the [[Santa Cruz River]] drainage and one along the [[Gila River]] near Bylas. Four sites were on private land, and one each on Indian reservation, state, and [[Bureau of Land Management]] lands. Reintroduction attempts in its native range have had limited success (Minckley 1959), owing to site location, variable surface water and flooding, and the presence of nonnative fishes, principally [[Western Mosquitofish]]. An eighth natural population in the [[San Pedro River]] Valley was eliminated by water mining within months of discovery (McNatt 1979).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Redrock Canyon system is grazed by livestock and has a history of mining, including exploratory bore holes. Key habitat characteristics recorded at Gate Spring:

- **Channel:** bedrock with numerous inflowing seeps; riffles rarely exceeding 15 cm and pools about 50 cm deep; extensive algal mats.
- **Air vs. water temperature (May 1979):** air 36 °C, water 22 °C; water temperatures ranged from 14 °C (7:00 p.m.) to 24 °C (1:00 p.m.).
- **Mainstream water:** less than one-third as hard and half as saline as the elevated spring.

Topminnows occupied algal mats almost exclusively, retreating into them when approached. Associated fauna included adult [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) below the falls near Redrock Ranch, young-of-year dace about 6 km downstream, and abundant [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) in a headwater tank. Only one mosquitofish was captured in the reach.

## Key Findings

- **Discovery:** Topminnows were first observed in Redrock Canyon in winter 1978, though reported there as early as the late 1960s. On May 8, 1979, they were seen in a side-channel pool about 0.2 km upstream of a 2 m bedrock waterfall.
- **Population estimate:** Electrofishing a 150 m reach below Gate Spring in late May 1979 yielded fewer than 100 fish (estimated, after winter reduction). Five voucher specimens were preserved under federal permit PRT-2-23-C.
- **Flood effects:** On August 29, 1979, after recent flooding, topminnows remained at Redrock Ranch and Gate Spring and in small pools, but abundance was lower than in May, apparently because of the flood.
- **Management recommendations:**
  - Fence roughly 150 to 200 m of stream channel to exclude livestock.
  - Protect the stream reach above and within occupied habitat from mining and water extraction.
  - Resurvey the headwater tank and other impoundments for [[Western Mosquitofish]] and remove them from the drainage.
  - Prohibit vehicle travel up the stream channel to Gate Spring.
  - Re-examine adjacent springs (e.g., Cottonwood Springs) during summer.
  - Transplant gravid females on an experimental basis, monitoring for population perpetuation and range expansion, since only one other population occurs on federal lands.
