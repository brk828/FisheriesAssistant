## Executive Summary
This study by Karr (1964) examines the age, growth, condition, fecundity, and food habits of the [[Fantail Darter]] (*Etheostoma flabellare*) collected primarily from Bluff Creek, a tributary of the Des Moines River in Boone County, Iowa. Of 118 specimens collected, only one was found in the main river channel, indicating strong habitat partitioning between the small-stream-dwelling Fantail Darter and the river-dwelling [[Slenderhead Darter]] (*Percina phoxocephala*), despite both species occupying similar swift riffle habitats. The study establishes sex-specific growth curves via scale annuli analysis, calculates length-weight relationships and condition factors, and quantifies diet composition (93% insects) and fecundity across seasons, suggesting a peak spawning period in May.

## Background & Description
- **Length conversion**: Total Length (TL) = 1.19 × Standard Length (SL) for fish ranging 17–70 mm TL.
- **Body-Scale Relationship**: L = 0.03 + 1.227R (where L = total length in mm, R = anterior scale radius in mm × 80). The near-zero intercept indicates scale growth is directly proportional to body length growth.
- **Condition Factor (K)**: Average K = **1.749** for 104 specimens (17–70 mm TL), notably higher (stockier body form) than co-occurring [[Slenderhead Darter]], [[Blacksided Darter]], and [[Johnny Darter]], which ranged from 1.362–1.392 (Karr, 1964).
- **Length-Weight Relationship** (combined sexes, n=104):
  `Log W = -2.780 + 2.835 Log L`
  - Regression coefficient <3.00 indicates fish become relatively less plump (slimmer) as they grow longer — the *opposite* trend from [[Slenderhead Darter]], [[Blacksided Darter]], and [[Johnny Darter]].
  - **Female-specific**: Log W = -1.907 + 2.372 Log L (n=19)
  - **Male-specific**: Log W = -2.812 + 2.894 Log L (n=54)
  - Analysis of covariance found **no significant difference** (5% level) between sexes in slope, elevation, or variance of length-weight regressions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Fantail Darters were restricted almost exclusively to swifter riffle areas in **Bluff Creek** (small tributary stream), typically over rocks >3 inches in diameter.
- **Habitat Partitioning**: Although the [[Slenderhead Darter]] occupies structurally similar riffle habitat in the **Des Moines River**, the two species show minimal spatial overlap — Fantail Darter is characterized as a small-stream specialist, while Slenderhead Darter is a river-dwelling species.
- **Sampling methods**: Long-handled dip-net kick-seining (101 fish) was far more effective than seining (17 fish) or electric shocking, which proved ineffective in rocky riffle habitat due to logistical constraints (dragging boat-mounted equipment over rocks).
- **Diet (Food Habits)**: Stomach analysis of 50 individuals (693 identifiable animal items) revealed:
  - **93.0%** Insecta (by frequency of items)
  - Dominant insect orders: **Trichoptera** (Hydropsychidae, 18.3%), **Diptera** (mainly Chironomidae, 61.9%), **Ephemeroptera** (Heptageniidae, Baetidae, Caenidae, combined 12.8%)
  - Minor items: Ostracoda, Hydrachnida (water mites), one recently hatched crayfish (Malacostraca)
  - Incidental inorganic material (small stones) and plant matter likely ingested accidentally during benthic feeding.
  - Consistent with earlier findings by Turner (1921) that Fantail Darters feed predominantly on midge and mayfly larvae.
- **Reproductive Ecology**: Fecundity estimates and mature egg counts (large, yellowish eggs vs. small, milky-white immature eggs) suggest a spawning peak in **May**, based on:
  - Two females (Nov 1962) with >800 undeveloped eggs each.
  - Seasonal mature egg counts (Table 3): April 1963 mean = 496 total eggs (119 mature); June 1962 mean = 213 total (60 mature); July 1962 mean = 171 total (23 mature) — indicating a decline in mature egg counts post-spawning.
  - Comparable to Lake (1936), who found a New York spawning peak of May 15 with females egg-free by June/July.

## Key Findings
- **Growth Rates by Sex** (Total Length in mm at each annulus, calculated via nomograph):
  | Sex | Annulus I | Annulus II | Annulus III | Annulus IV |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Female (n=57) | 18.7 | 34.8 | 42.4 | — (none observed) |
  | Male (n=23) | 18.5 | 36.7 | 49.0 | 62.0 |
  | Combined (n=101) | 20.3 | 35.3 | 46.5 | 62.0 |

- **No females** in the sample had formed a fourth annulus, suggesting either differential mortality or a shorter reproductive lifespan compared to males, or a sampling artifact.
- **Growth increments decline with age** for both sexes (e.g., males: 18.5 → 18.2 → 14.8 → 12.0 mm per annual increment), consistent with typical fish growth deceleration.
- **Lee's Phenomenon** was observed (per Ricker, 1958 methodology) — younger fish appeared to grow faster than older fish did at the same age, likely due to either (1) sampling bias toward larger young fish, or (2) faster-growing individuals having shorter lifespans than slower-growing ones.
- **Comparative Growth**: Fantail Darters grew slower on average than [[Slenderhead Darter]], [[Blacksided Darter]], and [[Johnny Darter]] through the first 3 annuli, but by the fourth annulus were 15 mm shorter than Blacksided Darter and 6 mm longer than both Slenderhead and Johnny Darter.
- **Sex ratio** in aged sample (age groups II–IV): 57 females to 23 males (~2.5:1), possibly reflecting sex-specific mortality, growth, or capture bias.
- Overall, the study reinforces that morphometric (condition, length-weight) and life-history (growth, fecundity, diet) traits of the Fantail Darter diverge notably from sympatric darter species in the same watershed, correlating with its specialized small-stream riffle niche.
