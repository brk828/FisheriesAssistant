# Sport Fishery Survey of the Shenandoah River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sport-fish

## Executive Summary

This 1954 report from the [[Conservation Commission of West Virginia]] (Division of Fish Management) documents a five-day summer survey of the [[Shenandoah River]] in West Virginia's eastern panhandle. The survey assessed whether [[Year-Round Fishing]] in a six-mile experimental section affected the river's sport fishery, compared against an upstream regulated area and other state waters. Its authors concluded that the river had recovered from industrial pollution of the 1940s, that its fish populations were well developed, and that year-round fishing showed no evidence of harm. They recommended keeping the six-mile section open year-round and extending the same status to the 35-mile upstream reach to the Virginia-West Virginia border.

## Background & Description

**Historical decline and recovery**
- Until 1940, the Shenandoah was highly productive. Largemouth bass, smallmouth bass, yellow belly sunfish, channel catfish, and crappies were abundant.
- Toxic industrial wastes from several Virginia manufacturers caused fish kills and a decline in angling. The lethal effects extended through the 20-mile West Virginia section to the confluence with the [[Potomac River]] at [[Harpers Ferry]].
- Organized support formed by 1946. [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] studies followed, with help from sportsmen's organizations. The pollution source was identified and corrective measures were taken (see [[Pollution Abatement]]).
- By 1950 the river was recovering, with steady improvement in aquatic life and renewed angler use.

**Regulatory history**
- In 1953 the [[Izaak Walton League]] (Jefferson County Chapter) requested experimental year-round fishing. The Commission approved a six-mile area from the Old Shannondale Ferry to the [[Millville Dam]]. The biological staff could not survey the area that summer.
- In 1954 the request was renewed. The Berkeley County Chapter of the Izaak Walton League, which had contributed to pollution abatement, urged caution. The Commission kept the section open for 1954, pending a summer survey.

**Survey design (week of July 18, 1954)**
- Duration: five-day intensive survey, more than 300 man-hours.
- Activities: bottom sampling, fish population studies, habitat assessment, reproduction checks, age and growth studies, chemical analysis, fish tagging, and general stream observations.
- Personnel: Paul Hooper (Assistant Chief, supervising), biologists Jack Larmoyeux, Cari Sullivan, and Harvey Beall, three fishery aids, Robert Lennon and Phillip Parker (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Leetown Microbiological Laboratory), and Conservation Officers Woodrow Parsons (Berkeley County) and George Dunaway (Jefferson County).
- Objective: determine the effects, if any, of year-round fishing by comparing the experimental area with the regulated upstream area and other state waters.

**Study areas**
- **Study Area A:** large shoal about one mile downstream from the [[Shannondale Ferry]] (experimental area).
- **Study Area B:** shoal about one mile upstream from the Millville Dam, below the pool known as the Big Eddy (experimental area).
- **Study Area C:** the Rocks shoal, nearly three miles upstream from the Shannondale Ferry and about half a mile from the Virginia-West Virginia border (upstream regulated area).

**Methods**
- Electrofishing was used in all areas with roughly equal effort ([[Electrofishing]]). Efficiency was slightly reduced by stream size and muddy water, but a representative sample was collected.
- Fish were measured, weighed, and scale-sampled, then released.
- Some game fish were tagged to track movement and recaptures ([[Fish Tagging]]).
- Seines and nets were also used, though they were less effective.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish community**
- 24 species were recorded. Relative abundance was given as a = abundant, c = common, s = scarce.
- **Game fish:** [[Smallmouth Bass]] (a), [[Largemouth Bass]] (c), Rock bass (s), [[Yellow Belly Sunfish]] (a), Green sunfish (c), Pumpkinseed sunfish (s), Central long ear sunfish (s), Bluegill sunfish (a).
- **Forage fish:** Satinfin shiners (a), Rosyface shiners (a), Bluntnose minnow (a), five other *Notropis* minnow species (c), Barred fantail darter (c).
- **Non-game fish:** [[Channel Catfish]] (a), Yellow bullhead (c), White sucker (c), Redhorse sucker (a), Hog sucker (c), American eel (c), Carp (c).

**Water chemistry**
- The watershed is fertile, with moderate total alkalinity and sufficient [[Dissolved Oxygen]].
- Chemical conditions had stayed favorable for fish since pollution abatement (see [[Water Chemistry]]).

**Food base (benthic and forage)**
- Mayfly and stonefly nymphs and caddisfly larvae were abundant on the rubble bottoms of the shoals.
- Hellgrammites were plentiful in all life stages.
- Forage minnows formed large schools in all three areas. A 20-foot minnow seine confirmed the observations.
- Fish were to be identified to subspecies and added to West Virginia distribution records.

**Angling activity**
- No standard [[Creel Census]] was conducted. Information came from conversations with anglers and observed catches.
- Methods ranged from trotlines and throwlines used at night to shore and boat fishing by day with artificial or natural baits.
- The proportion of out-of-state anglers is unknown, but the influx from Maryland and Virginia is believed to be substantial.

**Regional comparison**
- Growth rates of the principal game fish were above average compared with the Elk River, Wheeling Creek, Lost River, South Branch of the Potomac, and Cacapon Rivers.
- Population composition, reproductive success, food availability, habitat, and water chemistry were all judged to favor the Shenandoah over these streams.

## Key Findings

**Age and growth**
- Age and growth were estimated for 37 yellow belly sunfish (*Lepomis auritus*) and 49 smallmouth bass. This was a general examination, not a precise study.
- Length-frequency data: 145 smallmouth bass (Graph 1) and 131 yellow belly sunfish (Graph 2).
- The majority of smallmouth bass (76%) reached legal size during their third summer (age II+). Yellow belly sunfish averaged about 6.4 inches in that age class.
- The source table of average total length by age class is partly illegible from OCR, so individual values are not reported here.

**Causes of excellent growth (as stated by the authors)**
1. An abundance of preferred forage fish and bottom organisms.
2. Fertile water with good chemical quality.
3. Rejuvenated populations expanding after pollution abatement.
4. A large river with high carrying capacity.

**Statistical comparison of study areas**
- Analysis found no significant difference between the three study areas.
- Physical, chemical, and biological findings all indicated excellent fishing potential relative to other West Virginia streams.

**Population status and reproduction**
- Desirable species (yellow belly sunfish, smallmouth bass, channel catfish) had recovered from the 1940s pollution, and their populations were well developed.
- Scale readings showed successful reproduction of game fish in recent years.

**Carrying capacity and year-round fishing**
- The river has high carrying capacity and can support heavy fishing pressure.
- No evidence showed that year-round fishing had adversely affected the sport fishery.
- Year-round fishing allowed anglers to fully enjoy the sport and better harvest the fishery surplus.

**Recommendations (opinion of the biological staff)**
- Keep the six-mile section from the Millville Dam to the Shannondale Ferry open to year-round fishing.
- Extend year-round status to the upstream area of 35 miles to the Virginia-West Virginia border.
