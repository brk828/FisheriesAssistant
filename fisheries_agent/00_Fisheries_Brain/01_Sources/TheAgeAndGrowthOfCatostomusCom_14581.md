# Age and Growth of Catostomus commersoni in Doe Run Creek and Goose Creek, Kentucky

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the age and growth of [[White Sucker]] (*[[Catostomus commersoni]]*) from two small Kentucky streams that drain to the [[Ohio River]]: [[Doe Run Creek]] in Meade County and [[Goose Creek]] in Jefferson County. Ages were estimated by counting annuli on scales, and growth was back-calculated with a nomograph. The Doe Run population grew noticeably more slowly, with smaller annual increments, than the Goose Creek population. Both Kentucky series grew more slowly than suckers previously reported from Minnesota and Ohio. Sex-related growth differences were evident only in Goose Creek, and the length-weight relationships of the two populations were similar. The author notes that the samples are small and that the Doe Run fish may represent a distinct race.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The white sucker is a catostomid fish generally regarded as a small-stream species. Its scales were the basis for age determination.

**Doe Run Creek.** This is a spring-fed stream in Meade County that empties into the Ohio River near the [[Fort Knox Military Reservation]]. In its upper reaches, water temperature is stable year-round, never falling below 54°F or rising above 58°F. Three dams occur on the creek, which may influence growth patterns, though the author states that too few specimens were available to test this. Specimens came partly from W. L. Minekley's collections made in the upper three miles of the creek, all taken October–December 1959.

**Goose Creek.** This small stream in eastern Jefferson County also drains to the Ohio River. Some springs occur in its watershed, but most flow is presumed to come from surface drainage. Specimens were taken near the mouth, so the author cautions that results may reflect the Ohio River as well as the creek itself. Goose Creek specimens were collected in June 1958 and May 1959 and are held in the [[University of Louisville]] collections.

**Sample sources.** Doe Run specimens came from Minekley's collections. Goose Creek specimens came from University of Louisville holdings. Table 1 lists the number of fish per collection date and locality. Sample sizes are small, a limitation the author emphasizes repeatedly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Scales were removed from the area just below the dorsal fin, on the right side and above the lateral line. Where scales were regenerated, sampling moved progressively farther back until a usable scale was found. Annuli were counted for age. Using a microscope at ×45 magnification, the distance from the focus to each annulus was measured along the middle of the most anterior interradial space. Growth was then back-calculated on a [[Carlander Nomograph]] (Carlander, 1953), assuming an intercept of zero. See [[Scale Aging]] and [[Annulus]].

**Comparative data.** Age and growth data for white suckers from Minnesota (Kuehn, 1949; Moe, 1944) and Ohio (Roach, 1948) were used as benchmarks.

**Environmental notes.** The thermal stability of Doe Run's upper reaches, the presence of dams, and the spring influence on the watersheds are the only habitat conditions described. No water chemistry, invertebrate, or plankton data are reported.

## Key Findings

**1. Growth rate differs between the two Kentucky populations.**
- Goose Creek fish showed more rapid growth over five years.
- Doe Run fish grew over seven years, with a smoother and slower curve.
- The author suspects the Doe Run population is a distinct race, but states that the small samples may introduce considerable error into the averages.

**2. Kentucky fish grow more slowly than northern populations.**
- Compared with Minnesota and Ohio data, the Kentucky fish were smaller at each annulus.
- Doe Run fish were "very far behind," as shown in Figure 1.
- The Doe Run series showed smooth growth with small annual increments.

**3. Sex-related growth differs only in Goose Creek.**
- In Goose Creek, females grew appreciably faster than males, and grew larger as well.
- The author cautions that these results rest on very few scale determinations.
- In Doe Run, the sample comprised 12 males and 18 females (30 fish total). The mean growth curves for the sexes "crisscrossed" repeatedly and showed little variation, so the sexes appeared roughly equal in growth. See [[Sexual Dimorphism]].

**4. False annuli appear in Doe Run scales.**
- Doe Run scales frequently showed what appeared to be false annuli. Annuli I and III were singled out in the Figure 4 caption as suspected false.
- When these marks were included in averaged calculations, they produced a smooth growth curve, so the author concluded they were probably true growth rings.
- The annuli were clear over most of each scale, except the anterior portion, where circuli obscured the rings.
- Goose Creek scales had normally spaced annuli, and the cited literature did not mention false annuli.

**5. Length-weight relationships are similar.**
- Sample sizes were insufficient for firm conclusions, but the two populations showed little difference (see [[Length-Weight Relationship]]).
- Doe Run fish were slightly heavier for a given length, possibly because many gonads were ripe or near maturity.

**Overall conclusions.** The author concludes that Doe Run white suckers have a smooth growth rate with small annual increments, while Goose Creek fish grew faster. Both Kentucky series grew more slowly than the Minnesota and Ohio populations. Females in Goose Creek outgrew males, while Doe Run sexes were roughly equal. Length-weight relationships were similar between the two streams. The author treats all these results as provisional because of small sample sizes.
