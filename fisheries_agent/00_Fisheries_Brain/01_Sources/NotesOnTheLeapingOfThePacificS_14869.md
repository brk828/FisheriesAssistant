# Notes on the leaping of the pacific salmon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Oncorhynchus #animal-behavior

## Executive Summary
This early 20th-century field study by Henry B. Ward, conducted under the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries, presents preliminary observational data on the leaping behavior of the [[Pacific Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*, the red/sockeye salmon) at Dorr Falls on Naha Stream near Loring, Alaska. The paper challenges the popular romanticized image of salmon leaping — largely built around the [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*) — by presenting photographic and direct observational evidence that the red salmon's jumping at obstacles is frequently inaccurate, poorly directed, and often results in injury or failure. The study distinguishes clearly between two distinct leaping behaviors: obstacle-surmounting leaps at waterfalls versus aimless "sporting" jumps performed in open water.

## Background & Description
- **Body posture during obstacle leaps**: Rigid body throughout aerial flight; no observable mid-air movement.
- **Dorsal surface curvature**: Typically slightly concave during successful leaps and at the start of most jumps; convex posture observed near the end of a jump, and considered comparatively rare.
- **Pectoral/lateral fins**: Expanded and angled outward during flight, functioning as stabilizing "wings" or soaring organs; this configuration is maintained throughout the leap, including in unsuccessful attempts, until water contact.
- **Caudal fin**: Used for powerful propulsive strokes upon reaching the crest of the falls, enabling the fish to complete the ascent into calmer water above — consistent with prior observer reports.
- **Open-water jump posture**: Body bent slightly concave toward the direction of origin; fish falls back onto the water flat on its side (a "belly-whopper" splash) rather than diving head-first, in clear contrast to the falls-jumping posture.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context**: Naha Stream connects salt water to Heckman Lake, a spawning-maturation lake used by red salmon and site of an [[Alaska Packers Association]] hatchery.
- **Migratory behavior**: Salmon ascend from salt water in schools ("runs"), typically triggered by heavy rainfall that rapidly raises stream levels due to the drainage basin's characteristics.
- **Staging behavior**: Upon reaching the pool at the base of Dorr Falls, schools rest before actively attempting the falls the following day.
- **Obstacle**: Dorr Falls — a ~3-4 foot vertical drop with one lower breakpoint (~1 foot lower) along a rock spur near the left bank — represents the only true fall (versus rapids) along the stream's course to the lake.
- **Site selection for jump attempts**: Fish did not preferentially select the topographically easier (lower) breakpoint. Jump-site selection appeared correlated with **pool depth** below the falls rather than fall height — the deepest, most perpendicular, and highest-drop section (extreme left) was among the most frequented jump sites, while shallow points were avoided entirely.
- **Behavioral contrast**: Open-water "sport" jumps (observed in Behm Canal, the Loring estuary, and two lakes along Naha Stream) are morphologically and behaviorally distinct from falls-jumping, suggesting different motivational/stimulus origins (mild excitement vs. goal-directed obstacle navigation).

## Key Findings
- **Optical illusion of continuous jumping**: Although leaping appeared continuous to the naked eye, photographic sampling (30 random exposures) captured fish airborne in only 6 frames, with just 1 frame showing two fish simultaneously airborne — demonstrating that apparent "constant" leaping is a perceptual illusion.
- **Low jump-height success rate**: In roughly 9 out of 10 attempts, fish failed to jump high enough to reach the crest of the falls; jumps rarely exceeded the height of the fall's crest at all.
- **High collision/injury rate**: Fish frequently struck the rock face or falling water sheet head-first with force sufficient to stun them, causing them to be swept downstream before recovering.
- **Variable jump length and origin point**: Takeoff distance from the falls varied considerably; jumps originating farther back in smoother water, even when long, often ended with the fish striking rocks head-first at the base rather than clearing the falls.
- **Lack of directional accuracy**: Many leaps were oblique — directed toward the bank, parallel to the falls, or even away from the falls entirely into open water — indicating that leaping salmon may be poorly able to judge correct direction, height, and length simultaneously, or are diverted by unpredictable water currents/swirls.
- **Two distinct leap types identified**:
  1. **Falls-leap**: Head-first entry/dive posture, rigid body, expanded lateral fins, aimed at surmounting a vertical obstacle.
  2. **Open-water "sport" leap**: Sideways splash-landing, concave-body takeoff, low vertical height, interpreted as possibly linked to mild excitement rather than goal-directed navigation; observed exclusively in [[Pink Salmon]] behavior context here, and not seen replicated in other species by the author.
- **Overall behavioral conclusion**: The author concludes that red salmon leaping at obstacles demonstrates a "clear lack of definiteness as well as accuracy," contrasting sharply with the idealized, precise leaping powers popularly attributed to the [[Atlantic Salmon]] in cultural and naturalist literature.
- **Call for comparative data**: The author explicitly requests published observations from other researchers on leaping behavior in this or related species, indicating this is an acknowledged preliminary/incomplete dataset intended to stimulate further comparative study.
