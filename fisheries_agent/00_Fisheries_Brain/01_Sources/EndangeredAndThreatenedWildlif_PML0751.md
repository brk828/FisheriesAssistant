# Critical Habitat Proposal for Four Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

The document is a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) proposed rule, published in the Federal Register on January 29, 1993, to designate critical habitat under the [[Endangered Species Act]] for four endangered fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The Service proposed about 3,820 kilometers (2,094 miles) of river for designation, with considerable overlap among species. The package also includes a comment-period extension and hearing notice (March 1993), and several letters and a recovery-team memorandum on the proposal. Economic and biological support documents were still in preparation at the time of the proposal.

## Background & Description

The four fishes are native to the mainstream Colorado River, historically dominated by cyprinid minnows and catostomid suckers. Each is listed as endangered, and all four have declined because of habitat loss from flow regulation, altered temperature and sediment regimes, and proliferation of [[Nonnative Fishes]].

- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): once one of the most abundant and widely distributed fishes; lower-basin populations were extirpated from natural riverine environments. The largest riverine population (about 1,000 fish) occurs in the upper Green River Basin.
- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): the only living representative of its genus in the Basin, endemic to the region; native stocks survive only in the Upper Basin, mainly the Green River basin.
- **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*): remains dated to about 4,000 years ago but described as a species only in 1946; largest populations occur in the Little Colorado River and Black Rocks areas.
- **Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*): the rarest native fish in the Colorado River, now represented mainly by old adults (ages of 40 years or more) in [[Lake Mohave]] and possibly other lower-basin reservoirs.

Life-history data are sparse for all four species, and low numbers or lack of young suggest recruitment failure as a cause of decline. Loss of genetic diversity in small remnant stocks is a further risk.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The fishes evolved under natural fluctuating seasonal and annual flows. Dams and diversions have fragmented their historic range, and altered water quality, quantity, temperature and sediment transport. Altered habitats now favor introduced nonnative fishes, and little or no unaltered habitat remains for these species.

Key habitat requirements noted in the document include:

- **Razorback Sucker:** spawns in late winter to spring over mixed substrates (silt to cobble) at 10.5 to 21 °C; reservoir populations, such as in Lake Mohave, reproduce; adults use eddies, backwaters, and flooded areas; larvae prefer shallow littoral zones before drifting downstream.
- **Colorado Squawfish:** adults can migrate up to 320 km (200 miles) during spawning; larvae are transported downstream for about 160 km (100 miles) before settling in lower-gradient reaches; spring floods that inundate lowlands are important for reproductive conditioning.
- **Humpback Chub:** spawning follows the highest spring flows at water temperatures near 20 °C; flow reduction and low temperatures in the Grand Canyon were cited as factors curtailing spawning.
- **Bonytail Chub:** spawning reported in June and July at about 18 °C; wild adults are still capable of reproduction, and stocked fish in ponds produced large numbers of young.

Critical habitat is defined under the Act to include physical and biological features essential to conservation, organized around "primary constituent elements" such as water, physical habitat and biological environment.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed designation:** approximately 3,820 km (2,094 miles) of Colorado River system habitat; species-specific proposals were 2,935 km (1,824 miles) for Razorback Sucker (52% of historical range), 1,848 km (1,148 miles) for Colorado Squawfish (29%), 610 km for Humpback Chub (about 18% of historical range) and 348 km for Bonytail Chub (about 15%).
- **Geographic scope:** designated reaches span Colorado, Utah, New Mexico, Arizona, Nevada and California, including portions of the Green, Yampa, Gunnison, San Juan, Colorado, Little Colorado and Gila rivers.
- **Legal basis:** the Service was under a court order (following a suit by the [[Sierra Club Legal Defense Fund]]) to propose razorback sucker habitat by January 1993, and it chose to propose habitat for all four species together.
- **Economic framework:** the economic analysis considers only impacts from designation beyond those already caused by listing. Flow scenarios and hydropower, water supply and recreation costs were to be evaluated. The Service concluded that significant economic impacts were unclear.
- **Consultation effects:** Section 7 requires federal agencies to consult on actions that may adversely modify critical habitat, and to confer on discretionary actions. Agencies are not precluded from irreversible commitments while habitat is merely proposed.
- **Public comment:** the comment period was extended from January 29 to April 15, 1993, with three public hearings (Phoenix, San Bernardino and Lakewood) held March 29–31, 1993.
- **Critique (Marsh and Minckley letter):** the reviewers questioned designating habitat for all four species together, argued that habitat and status data for humpback chub and bonytail were outdated, and noted that the proposed rule focused too narrowly on physico-chemical habitat while neglecting nonnative predators and competitors.
- **Razorback Sucker rearing success:** the letter reported successful rearing and recruitment in habitats free of nonnative fishes, and that stocked fish in Lake Mohave survived and reproduced under certain conditions.
- **Recovery Team recommendations:** the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] endorsed designation and recommended additional extensions of about 200 river miles or less, mostly within existing proposed reaches or adjacent to national parks. It also recommended including main-stem reservoirs such as Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu, and noted that some proposed reaches exceeded recovery-plan priorities.
- **Reclamation objections:** the Bureau of Reclamation (Lower Colorado Region) disputed the magnitude of potential economic impact and argued that Lower Basin habitat is unlikely to sustain the species given predation by nonnative fishes such as channel catfish and largemouth bass.
- **Regulatory status:** the proposal was not expected to require an Environmental Assessment under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]], and it imposed no direct costs or recordkeeping requirements on small entities.
