# Aravaipa Creek Fish Monitoring and Survey Results from 1999 and 2000

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department technical report (Technical Report 198, November 2002) presents fish monitoring data from [[Aravaipa Creek]], a spring-fed tributary of the [[San Pedro River]] in the Galiuro Mountains. Sampling was conducted in October 1999 and November 2000 at nine fixed stations spread across three geomorphic reaches, using seining and backpack electrofishing. The creek supports the most diverse native fish assemblage in a single desert Southwest stream, but nonnative species have recently increased. The data serve as baseline for evaluating a [[Fish Barrier]] completed on the West End in April 2001.

## Background & Description

Aravaipa Creek runs nearly 75 km from headwaters near Klondyke to its confluence with the San Pedro River. It is one of the few unaltered perennial streams in Arizona. Seven native species occur there, including two federally listed as threatened: [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). Other natives are [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*).

Nonnative fishes reported include fathead minnow, [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*), and [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), with common carp, black bullhead, [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), and [[Largemouth Bass]] also reported at lower levels. The red shiner, native to the Mississippi-Rio Grande basins, is implicated in declines of spikedace and loach minnow through predation and competition.

Monitoring protocol was developed in 1990 by a cooperative group including Arizona State University, the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the Bureau of Land Management, The Nature Conservancy, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation. A 1994 Biological Opinion found that delivery of [[Central Arizona Project]] water to the Gila River basin would jeopardize the razorback sucker, Gila topminnow, spikedace, and loach minnow, prompting the barrier.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reaches:** Upper (East End) reach, a broad valley of about 17 km; middle (Wilderness Area) reach, the sheer-walled Aravaipa Canyon of about 10 km; lower (West End) reach, a steep-sided valley.
- **Stations:** Three per reach, each 200 m long, subdivided into run, riffle, and pool subhabitats. Cover and substrate were recorded.
- **Hydrology:** A natural hydrograph with flash floods and bimodal spates limited nonnative establishment historically. A flash flood cancelled the planned October 2000 trip.
- **Seasonality:** Earlier [[Bettaso]] quarterly sampling (1992–1994) showed fish collections peaking in August and declining through autumn and winter, which the authors cite as a likely reason for lower 2000 numbers (November sampling versus October in 1999).
- **Recreation and land use:** The Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area, managed by the [[Bureau of Land Management]], permits 50 visitors per day and is within easy reach of Arizona's largest metropolitan areas.

## Key Findings

**Overall catch**
- Fish collections were lower in 2000 than in 1999 (Table 1), with the report attributing this mainly to the November timing.
- [[Longfin Dace]] was the most collected species, comprising 37.9% of catch in 1999 and 51.6% in 2000.
- Spikedace totals fell about fifteenfold, from 1,025 in 1999 to roughly 70 in 2000; relative abundance dropped from 26.2% to 5.2% of catch. The authors consider this likely due to natural temporal variability, not habitat change.

**Native species**
- Loach minnow was among the most common fish at the East End and was most common in the Wilderness Area in 1999. It made up 6.8–8% of catch in 1999 and a higher share in 2000. Electrofishing captured far more loach minnow than seining (about 20 times higher CPUE at Chimney Rock in 1999).
- Spikedace were concentrated in the East End and Wilderness Area and nearly absent from the West End. Seining captured more than twice as many spikedace as electrofishing in both years.
- Roundtail chub comprised less than 3% of catch, were absent from the West End, and were more often caught as adults. The authors suggest chubs may outswim seines or hide in deep cover, and that pools were a small fraction of available habitat.
- Speckled dace was the least encountered native fish (<1% of catch). All were caught at the East End, where electrofishing captured over five times as many as seining.
- Sonora sucker comprised 5.7% (1999) and 4.9% (2000) of catch; electrofishing captured 5 times more in 1999 and 13 times more in 2000.
- Desert sucker comprised 8.9% (1999) and 10.9% (2000) of catch; it was the second most common fish in the Wilderness Area in 2000.

**Nonnative species**
- Yellow bullhead, red shiner, and green sunfish were collected in both years; mosquitofish were collected only in 1999.
- Nonnatives were most abundant at the West End and absent from the East End, except for one green sunfish at Turkey Creek in 1999.
- Red shiners, absent from 1992–1994 collections, appeared at the Painted Cave, Wagner Ranch, White's Ranch, and (in 2000) Sycamore Tree stations.
- USBR sampling in October 2001 found red shiner relative abundance more than 10% below the barrier.

**Habitat**
- 1999: runs 8,666 m², riffles 5,793 m², pools 529 m², backwaters 78 m².
- 2000: runs 8,104 m², riffles 2,966 m², pools 4 m², backwaters 130 m².
- The East End had the most run habitat (70.5%) in 1999; the West End had the most riffle habitat (51.9%) in 1999; the Wilderness Area had the most run habitat (78.2%) in 2000.

**Management recommendations**
- Monitor areas above, below, and between the new West End barriers to assess their effectiveness against nonnative invasion from the San Pedro River.
- Conduct a comprehensive watershed fish survey, including cattle tanks, and eradicate nonnatives above the barriers if warranted.
- Combine 1999–2000 data with historic AGFD, BLM, TNC, and ASU collections to establish long-term trends.
- Expand public education and interpretation in the Wilderness Area to limit recreation impacts.

**Sampling limitations:** Captures of speckled dace and roundtail chub were too rare for firm inference about distribution or habitat use. The Sycamore Tree site lacked 2000 habitat measurements.
