# Ecological Life History of the Warmouth (Centrarchidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Volume 27, Article 1, August 1957) by R. Weldon Larimore presents a multi-year ecological life history of the [[Warmouth]] (*Chaenobryttus gulosus*), studied intensively in two central Illinois habitats: [[Venard Lake]], a 3.2-acre artificial impoundment stocked only with warmouths and [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Park Pond]], an 18-acre flooded strip-mine area holding 36 fish species. The work covers habitat preferences, oxygen tolerance, food habits, seasonal and daily feeding, reproduction and spawning behavior, early life history, age and growth, parasitism, behavior, economic value, and stocking experiments with other sunfishes.

## Background & Description

The warmouth is a centrarchid sunfish, sometimes called the "stump-knocker" because of its association with stumps and submerged cover. The bulletin notes it is a quiet-dispositioned fish that moves little except during nesting, avoids intense light, and seeks weed masses, stumps or rocky banks.

Study areas were:

- **Park Pond:** Vermilion County, east-central Illinois. An abandoned stripmine flooded with Salt Fork River water, owned by the Pollywog Association since 1932. Its 1946 area was 18 acres. Thirty-six fish species were recorded, most introduced by flood waters or by the Illinois Department of Conservation.
- **Venard Lake:** An "old" artificial impoundment one mile south of Bloomington, McLean County, with an area of 3.2 acres in 1947. It was drained in 1946 and restocked in April 1947 with 225 yearling largemouth bass (4–8 in), 15 largemouth bass (9.6–13.7 in, average 11.9 in) and 101 warmouths (5.1–8.1 in, average 6.8 in).

Water chemistry at Park Pond was high in mineral content, with sulfates especially high, but buffering prevented extreme acidity typical of mine water (Table 1).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat characteristics**
- Dense weed beds, soft bottoms, brush, roots and stumps are typical. The species occurs in Everglades muck waters, Florida ponds, Alabama sloughs, and Illinois lakes.
- Warmouths are most frequent and abundant in muddy or turbid water, which the author interprets as greater tolerance of turbidity rather than a direct preference. Best angling in central Illinois was in moderately clear water; Oklahoma populations grew slowest in continuously turbid waters.
- Depth: small warmouths (under about 5 in) remain in shallow protective cover year-round, including under ice; large individuals occupy deeper water. Warmouths did not group together in winter.
- Dissolved oxygen: the critical oxygen tension was 2.5 cc/L (3.6 ppm) at 20 °C. Oxygen consumption fell abruptly below this level. In a crowded aquarium, all warmouths survived while all dead fish were bluegills. Warmouths are among the last species to die when fish are held in tanks without aeration.
- Stream gradient: warmouths are rare in fast creeks but common in sluggish, low-gradient streams and rivers.

**Food habits** (Park Pond: 391 stomachs with food out of 515 collected; Venard Lake: 356 of 413)
- Park Pond diet was dominated by crayfish (50% of total volume), followed by fish (36%).
- Venard Lake diet was dominated by dragonfly nymphs (38% of volume) and included isopods (*Asellus*, 11%) and mayfly nymphs (15%).
- Feeding peaked in the early morning in summer; afternoon stomachs were largely empty (50% empty in afternoon versus 4% in morning, Park Pond).
- Warmouths and largemouth bass at Venard Lake shared many foods but partitioned habitat: warmouths fed on bottom and bank organisms, bass on surface and open-water prey.
- The author concludes there is no specific diet; warmouths take whatever food is readily available.

**Reproduction and early life**
- Gonad development begins in March–April and spawning runs from mid-May to mid-August in central Illinois, with a peak in early June.
- Large warmouths (over 5.4 in) spawned earlier and for longer than small fish (3.5–5.4 in).
- Estimated egg counts ranged from 4,500 to 63,200 per ovary. Venard Lake females produced more eggs than Park Pond females of similar length (for example, 40,400 versus 12,500 for 5.3–5.4 in females).
- Males build nests, usually near a projecting object on loose rubble or silt; no colonies were formed. Courtship and spawning involve a threatening male and a thumping female that releases about 20 eggs per extrusion.
- Eggs hatched in about 34.5 hours at 25–26.4 °C. Larvae starved in 10–11 days without food and began feeding by day seven.
- Juveniles received no parental care after leaving the nest.
- Predation on postlarvae by bass and other fish was heavy in Venard Lake in 1947.

**Growth and age**
- Sexual maturity: 3.1–3.5 in total length; Venard Lake fish matured at age 1, Park Pond fish at age 2.
- Scale annuli were a reliable year-mark. Park Pond annuli were completed between April 7 and May 20, 1949; Venard Lake fish completed them over a shorter span.
- Calculated length relationship: L = 0.5278 + 1.048 S (L total length in inches; S anterior scale radius at 41× in inches).
- Length–weight relationship for 866 Park Pond warmouths: log W = −4.49867 + 3.04902 log L (W in grams, L standard length in mm).
- The largest Park Pond warmouth was a 9.6-inch male weighing 1.0 pound, aged 6.
- Compensatory growth was slight and did not overcome the first-year size advantage.
- Growth fluctuated with calendar year, linked to floods (1942, 1943) and rotenone thinning (1945, 1946).

**Parasites**
- Park Pond warmouths were heavily infested with the strigeid fluke *Posthodiplostomum minimum*, the bass tapeworm *Proteocephalus ambloplites*, and the nematode *Camallanus oxycephalus*.
- Venard Lake fish were largely parasite-free, except for leech infestations (*Illinobdella moorei*) in autumn and winter 1948 that damaged caudal fins.
- No direct harmful effect of parasites on condition could be demonstrated.

**Economic relations**
- The warmouth is a valued sport fish in the lower Mississippi valley and Gulf states, often taken on live bait. It is of little commercial value; sale is illegal in most states.
- Anglers consider it a good table fish and a game fish, though catch rates are usually low in Illinois.
- It is a useful laboratory species, tolerant of confinement and feeding on a wide range of foods.

**Species combinations (stocking experiments)**
- Seventeen central Illinois ponds were stocked with 11 fish combinations including warmouths, largemouth and smallmouth bass, bluegills, redear sunfish and green sunfish.
- Warmouths typically produced small broods each year without seriously restricting companion species. Broods were small when adults were introduced into populations already dense with other sunfish.
- Warmouth × bluegill hybrids were never produced in the experiments.

## Key Findings

1. **Habitat:** Warmouths are associated with dense weeds, stumps and soft, silty bottoms; they tolerate turbidity and low oxygen better than most sunfishes. Critical oxygen tension is 2.5 cc/L at 20 °C.
2. **Diet:** Diet is broad and variable. Park Pond fish relied on crayfish and fish; Venard Lake fish relied on dragonfly nymphs and isopods. Feeding is concentrated in the morning.
3. **Competition:** Overlap with largemouth bass at Venard Lake was reduced by differences in feeding area and habit.
4. **Reproduction:** Spawning runs mid-May to mid-August with peak in early June. Large fish spawn earlier and longer. Fecundity ranged from 4,500 to 63,200 eggs per ovary, and Venard Lake fish were more fecund.
5. **Maturity:** Sexual maturity at 3.1–3.5 in total length, reached at age 1 in Venard Lake and age 2 in Park Pond.
6. **Growth:** Length increments peak in the second year of life; first-year size advantage persists. Growth varied among year classes and calendar years, and was poor in isolated, dense-weed slough populations.
7. **Parasitism:** Heavy infestations in Park Pond did not measurably harm condition, though Venard Lake fish had higher condition coefficients.
8. **Behavior:** The species is non-gregarious, with no school formation, and the male provides nest guarding but no care after fry leave the nest.
9. **Management:** Warmouths do not become dominant at the expense of other fishes, and they seldom establish dense populations that support angling unless introduced early or in the absence of dense sunfish stocks.
