# Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Management Plan (1988)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Land Management (BLM) document, the Wilderness Management Plan for the [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]] in southeastern Arizona, sets management objectives, policies and actions for a ten-year period through fiscal year 1997. The plan covers environmental elements (soil, water, vegetation, insects and disease, fish and wildlife, cultural resources), social elements (recreation) and managerial elements (administration, information and education, scientific study, search and rescue, range, lands, minerals, aircraft overflights and fire). It includes an implementation schedule, an environmental assessment comparing four alternatives, a cost estimate, and appendices with the enabling legislation, boundary description, visitor rules, participants and public comment responses. The proposed action emphasizes preserving wilderness character through [[Limits of Acceptable Change]] (LAC) standards, a permit system and native-species protection.

## Background & Description

Aravaipa Canyon was designated wilderness by the [[Arizona Wilderness Act]] (Public Law 98-406) on August 28, 1984. Congress cited the rare presence of a perennial stream that supports "an extraordinary abundance and diversity of native plant, fish, and wildlife." The area was first proposed for retention in public ownership in 1968 and established as a primitive area on January 10, 1969. It was later expanded and then incorporated into the wilderness system.

Key descriptive facts:

- **Area:** 6,699 acres administered by BLM, located north of the Galiuro Mountains, about 90 miles southeast of Phoenix and 55 miles northeast of Tucson.
- **Canyon length:** an 11-mile stretch of canyon with nine major side canyons.
- **Geology:** largely volcanic (Tertiary Galiuro Volcanics), with Precambrian bedrock exposed at the western end.
- **Watershed:** about 541 square miles, with roughly 69% upstream of the eastern wilderness boundary.
- **Wildlife inventory:** 46 mammal, 46 reptile, 7 native fish and 8 amphibian species, plus more than 200 bird species (BLM 1983).
- **Access:** two county roads, the Aravaipa Road (west) and the Klondyke Road (east).
- **Land context:** BLM acquired 51,028 acres north and south of the wilderness in 1986, managed under the separate [[Winkelman Management Framework Plan]].

The plan is guided by four wilderness management goals: long-term preservation of wilderness character under a principle of non-degradation; visitor use that leaves the area unimpaired; use of the minimum tool necessary; and management of non-conforming but accepted uses to prevent undue degradation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water and flow.** Aravaipa Creek is intermittent through Aravaipa Valley and perennial through the wilderness, fed by at least ten springs and numerous seeps. Average flows range from about 18 cubic feet per second (cfs) at the east end to about 25 cfs at the west end. BLM requested instream flow rights of 15 cfs, pending state approval, to protect the riparian ecosystem and native fishes. Average annual rainfall was 19.45 inches at Klondyke and 19.95 inches at the West Aravaipa site (1978–1985).

**Flooding.** The October 1983 flood, with an estimated peak of 23,000 cfs, removed vegetation and trees. Radiocarbon dating indicated it was the largest flood in 1,100 years. The plan notes that flooding is a natural process that rejuvenates the riparian zone while sometimes causing severe damage. A later USGS-based estimate of 70,800 cfs is discussed in the public comments.

**Native fishes.** Seven native species occur in Aravaipa Creek. The [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]] are federally listed as threatened, and critical habitat designation was under consideration. Other natives include [[Roundtail Chub]], longfin dace, speckled dace, Sonoran sucker and desert mountain sucker. The plan notes that no other Arizona stream is known to support so many native fishes without substantial introduced species. Exotic green sunfish persist in some side-canyon pools.

**Vegetation.** Riparian vegetation was heavily modified by livestock grazing before grazing was excluded from the canyon bottom in 1974. Young trees are establishing, but middle-aged trees are absent. Exotics include Bermuda grass, watercress and [[Salt Cedar]], which is targeted for hand removal.

**Wildlife.** The [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]] herd is estimated at 160 animals and is expanding. The [[Black Hawk]], a State of Arizona threatened species, nests in cottonwoods and sycamores and is sensitive to disturbance. Peregrine falcons, the desert tortoise and the tiger rattlesnake also occur.

**Water quality.** Earlier studies found mercury above recommended standards (Minckley 1972). Later analyses found all elements except iron within standards. Mining runoff and upstream agriculture are potential concerns, but their impacts are not yet known.

**Cultural resources.** Only about 5.5% of the wilderness had been surveyed, and 22 sites had been identified. Occupation spans from Cochise culture (10,000–2,000 years before present) through Hohokam, Mogollon, Salado and Apache periods, and includes historic ranch sites and the Camp Grant context.

## Key Findings

**Management framework**
- Management will use the [[Limits of Acceptable Change]] system, with nine interrelated steps, to set standards and monitor conditions. LAC planning starts in 1987.
- A social carrying capacity study by the University of Arizona began in 1985 and is due for completion in 1988.

**Visitor use**
- Access is controlled by a permit system limited to 50 people per day (20 at the east entrance and 30 at the west). Maximum stay is three days and two nights, and group size is limited to 10.
- A use fee of $1.50 per person per day was introduced in April 1984.
- Visitor days ranged from 2,090 to 14,072 across 1974–1986 (Table 1); use peaked in 1982 and fell after the 1983 flood.
- Pack and saddle stock groups are limited to five animals, and overnight stock use in the canyon is prohibited.
- The permit requirement was modified so that tablelands-only use does not require a permit.

**Water and watershed**
- BLM will pursue instream flow rights of 15 cfs, implement a water quality monitoring plan, and consider Unique Waters designation.
- Fecal coliform levels were well under full-body contact standards, and bacteria were mostly of animal origin.

**Grazing and vegetation**
- Grazing non-use in the canyon bottom will continue. Grazing on the tablelands is permitted at existing levels, with allotments totaling 408 AUMs in South Rim, 36 in Hell Hole, 36 in Dry Camp and 10–8 in Painted Cave.
- Natural revegetation and succession are the preferred restoration methods. Salt cedar will be removed by hand.

**Wildlife management**
- The bighorn sheep management focuses on spatial segregation from visitors and protection of high-use nursery band areas.
- Visitor use will be discouraged near peregrine and black hawk nests during critical periods, from March to June.
- Exotic fish in side canyons may be eliminated, followed by native reintroduction.

**Administration and fire**
- Two full-time rangers will be stationed at the east and west entrances. The plan calls for replacing the West Aravaipa residence and acquiring public land for administrative sites.
- Fire will be managed under limited suppression, with a fire management plan to be written.
- BLM and FAA will maintain a 2,000-foot minimum altitude advisory for aircraft over the wilderness.

**Cost and planning**
- Annual operating costs total $61,500 for current expenditures, plus $5,000 for water quality analysis, giving $66,500.
- Non-recurring costs total $115,500, including the carrying capacity study ($15,000), LAC plan ($7,500), exotic fish elimination ($10,000) and the ethnographic study ($18,000).

**Environmental assessment**
- Four alternatives were analyzed: Proposed Action, No Action, Resource Protection, and Recreation Enhancement. The proposed action concluded there would be no significant adverse consequences.

**Public comments**
- Of 27 letters from 12 individuals, 8 organizations and 7 agencies, BLM revised policies on bighorn sheep, campfires, the fee system and the exotic-fish discussion. Campfires will continue to be allowed, subject to LAC monitoring, and the plan retains the permit system.
