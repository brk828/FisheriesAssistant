# Survival of Brook Trout from Egg to Fingerling Stage in Two Michigan Trout Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by David S. Shetter, from the Michigan Department of Conservation's [[Institute For Fisheries Research]], estimates how many wild [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) survive from egg deposition in winter to the first fall fingerling stage. Two methods were combined: controlled spawning in screened diversions at [[Hunt Creek]] (1943–48), and mark-and-recapture population estimates in Hunt Creek and the [[Pigeon River]] (1949–58). Egg-to-fingerling survival in the diversions ranged from 4.5 to 31.7 percent. Population-based estimates were lower, at 2.7 to 6.7 percent. Across 24 observations, survival averaged 8.6 percent, or 4.7 percent after excluding four unusually high values. The author concludes that most mortality occurs after fry emerge from redds, between March and September.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Brook trout, a char native to eastern North America, studied as wild, self-sustaining populations in small trout streams.
- **Objective:** Quantify survival from mid-winter eggs to the fingerling stage of the following early fall, using two independent approaches.
- **Method 1 (Diversion experiments):** Adult males and females of known size were confined in screened diversions of Hunt Creek ([[Diversion II]], 193 ft × 9–14 ft, 0.05 acre; [[Diversion III]], 284 ft × 8–14 ft, 0.07 acre). Eggs deposited were estimated from spawner length using regression equations. Surviving young-of-the-year were then enumerated. A similar design was used by Smith (1947) at Convict Creek, California.
- **Method 2 (Population studies):** Successive fall electrofishing surveys (mark-and-recapture) estimated the number of spawners and the number of fingerlings surviving to the next fall. Fecundity was computed from adult female counts and egg counts per female.
- **Egg estimation:** Two regression lines were used, one for stream fish (20 fish, 6.3–9.2 in) and one for lake/pond fish (36 fish, 7.6–16.4 in). August egg counts were reduced by a factor of 0.80 to account for atresia, following [[Vladykov]] (1956).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Hunt Creek was regarded as good brook trout habitat. Diversions had gravel spawning substrate, and surface ice formed only at the edges. Diversion III had spring seepage along about one-third of its west bank, and Diversion II received somewhat less groundwater. Riffles predominated in Diversion III and pools in Diversion II.
- **Spawning density:** Female density never exceeded one per 435 square feet of stream. About 20 percent of the bottom area of Hunt Creek experimental sections was estimated to be suitable for spawning.
- **Associated fishes:** The [[Slimy Sculpin]] (*Cottus cognatus*) was abundant in the diversions. Bluntnose minnow, fathead minnow, and northern redbelly dace were relatively rare. The Pigeon River supported brook trout, [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], white sucker, common shiner, creek chub, blacknose dace, longnose dace, central mudminnow, and mottled sculpin.
- **Predators and competitors:** Two to five extraneous brook trout (4.4–9.6 in) were present in five of six diversion experiments. No correlation was found between their numbers and fry survival. Smith's (1947) Convict Creek data showed a similar lack of effect despite 18 to 264 brown or rainbow trout present.
- **Fishing regulations:** Pigeon River and Hunt Creek were under a creel limit of 10 fish and a minimum length of 7 inches.

## Key Findings

**Diversion experiments (Hunt Creek, 1943–47 spawning seasons)**
- Six diversion experiments yielded survival of 4.5% (1943), 5.x% (1944), 31.7% (1945), 6.8% (1946), 8.8% (1946 second run), and 7.6% (1947). Apart from 1945, all values were 8.8% or less.
- The 1945 value of 31.7% (95% CI 23.8–47.7) was the highest observed. The 1943 value was lower, possibly because confinement began late and some eggs were already laid.
- Females eggs laid ranged from roughly 1,373 to 3,832 per experiment (estimated).
- Only 6 of 27 females retained eggs, and retention was not an important factor.
- Survival did not differ significantly between Hunt Creek and Convict Creek by the White rank test, with or without the three highest values.

**Population studies (Hunt Creek and Pigeon River)**
- Egg-to-fall survival from 12 population estimates ranged from 2.7% to 6.7%. This was significantly lower than the diversion results at Hunt Creek.
- Pigeon River estimates (Cooper 1953) were 3.6% (1950), 4.5% (1951), and 4.3% (1952).
- Spawning females ranged from 17 to 22 per acre in the Pigeon River and 189 to 275 per acre in Hunt Creek.
- Surviving young per acre ranged from 214 to 248 (Pigeon River) and 985 to 1,702 (Hunt Creek).

**Overall synthesis**
- Across 24 observations, survival ranged from 2.7% to 43% and averaged 8.6%.
- Excluding four unusually high values, survival ranged from 2.7% to 8.8% with an average of 4.7%.
- The author regarded survival above 10% as unusually high. The three highest diversion values were judged unrepresentative, since 32% survival would have produced 30,000–60,000 fall fingerlings, several times above any population estimate.
- Survival in the diversions was noticeably higher than in the natural channel year to year. Possible causes are low fish density and fewer bird and mammal predators at the diversions.

**Mortality pattern**
- Egg-to-fingerling mortality was about 92–95%.
- Literature shows low egg mortality in redds (Hobbs 1940; Hazzard 1932; Brasch 1949, average 6.5%, with 21.3% from deposition to emergence).
- Most brook trout fry emerge in March in Michigan. The author concluded that most egg-to-fingerling loss occurs between March and September.
- Using 1,000 eggs as the base, the author's summary shows a loss of 213 between deposition and emergence, and a further loss of 701 to 740 between emergence and end of summer. Survival to end of summer was 47 (on the 4.7% basis) or 86 (on the 8.6% basis) per 1,000 eggs.
