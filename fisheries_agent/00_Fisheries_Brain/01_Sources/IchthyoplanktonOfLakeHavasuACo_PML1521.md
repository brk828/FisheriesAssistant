# Ichthyoplankton of Lake Havasu

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ichthyoplankton #clupeidae #cyprinidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study describes the larval fish (ichthyoplankton) community of the river and reservoir sections of the [[Lake Havasu]] impoundment on the lower [[Colorado River]] (Arizona-California). Sampling in 1985 and 1986 captured 6,517 larvae representing eight species in six families. [[Common Carp]] and [[Threadfin Shad]] dominated, and larval timing tracked water temperature. Only one native larva, [[Razorback Sucker]], was collected, and the authors conclude it most likely drifted in from upstream rather than reproducing locally.

## Background & Description

Mainstream impoundments on the lower Colorado River support important recreational fisheries. Non-native species such as [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] are stocked or established, and native fishes are rare. Adult population data exist for the basin, but larval fish records for the lower river were scarce before this work. The survey was designed to document which species reproduce in the area and when.

The study area had two sections:

- **Upstream river section:** a relatively cold, narrow reach (under 0.15 km wide, about 5 m deep in places) extending roughly 27 km downstream from [[Davis Dam]].
- **Downstream reservoir section:** [[Lake Havasu]] itself, with mean depths of 9–10 m and a maximum depth exceeding 19 m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Larvae were collected with plankton nets of 505 µm mesh, towed from bottom to surface. River tows lasted 5 min and reservoir tows 10 min. River stations were sampled about weekly and reservoir stations about three times per week. Sampling in 1985 ran from March to June in the river and from late February to June in the reservoir. Upstream reservoir stations were suspended in 1986 because of high water. Specimens were identified using keys and descriptions in the regional literature (e.g., [[Larval Fish Identification]] keys by Snyder, Minckley, and Moran and Mansueti). Abundance was expressed as catch per effort (CPE), the mean number of larvae per tow.

**Water temperature and spawning.** Reproductive timing followed temperature. Threadfin shad usually spawn when water reaches about 21 °C. Carp move into shallows at about 16 °C and peak at 18–20 °C. Largemouth bass and other centrarchids spawn in the spring and summer, following warming temperatures. In 1985, midwater temperature reached 18 °C by mid-April. In 1986, the water was already about 15 °C when sampling began, reached 15 °C by 1 April, and stayed above 21 °C after mid-May. Larval composition changed over the sampling season, and each species' occurrence tracked these temperature patterns.

**Historical context.** Earlier surveys recorded 27 fish species from the study area. Larvae of only eight forms were collected here. Several native fishes, including the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Bonytail]], were extirpated or are considered rare. Seven other species, including [[Channel Catfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Flathead Catfish]], were absent from collections. The authors suggest these species mostly occupy benthic or marginal habitats, or are rare in the area, and that some larvae are notoriously hard to identify.

**Razorback sucker drift.** Razorback sucker larvae were collected early in the season, and they are thought to have drifted from spawning areas upstream. Passive downstream travel from the upstream lake to Parker Dam would take at least 13.2 days. Larvae arriving in lower Lake Havasu would be expected to be about 12–15 mm long, but the collected specimens averaged only 9.5 ± 0.7 mm (n = 23), with the largest at 10.3 mm. The authors conclude that these larvae probably did not originate in Lake Mohave. They also note that no documented reproduction by the species has occurred in the area since the 1940s and 1950s.

## Key Findings

- **Community composition:** 6,517 larvae from eight species in six families were identified.
- **Dominant species:** Common Carp made up 49% (1985) and 56% (1986) of total larvae. Threadfin Shad was the second most abundant species in 1984 and the most abundant in 1985 (per the data reported).
- **Minor taxa:** Bluegill, Largemouth Bass, Black Crappie, Razorback Sucker, Channel Catfish, and Striped Bass made up a small share of the catch. Centrarchids comprised only about 3% of the total in one year.
- **Interannual difference:** Total CPE was lower in 1986 than in 1985 by roughly a factor of two. Threadfin shad CPE and carp CPE also declined in 1986, with carp CPE about 34% of the 1985 value and centrarchid CPE about 3%.
- **Peak CPE:** Cyprinid CPE peaked at 28.6 larvae per tow in May, and clupeid CPE peaked at 19.2 in June.
- **Temporal pattern:** Razorback sucker larvae appeared earliest, in early March, and were rarely caught after April. Other taxa began appearing in mid-March and persisted into June.
- **Temperature link:** Spawning timing and larval occurrence matched published temperature thresholds for each taxon.
- **Native fish:** Only Razorback Sucker larvae were collected from the native group, and they were probably drifted from upstream.
