# Integrating Economics into Priority Setting and Evaluation in Conservation Management

#conservation-biology #conservation-economics #threatened-species

## Executive Summary

This review, published in [[Conservation Biology]] (Volume 17, No. 1, February 2003) by Kenneth F. D. Hughey, Ross Cullen and Emma Moran, examines how economic tools can improve [[Priority Setting]] and [[Conservation Evaluation]] for threatened and endangered species and natural areas. The document is a terrestrial conservation paper. It does not address fish, marine species or aquatic ecosystems, so the aquatic-specific fields above are marked N/A or describe only the terrestrial context.

The authors reviewed the conservation, economic and environmental policy literature and found two major areas where evaluation and prioritization occur: initial setting of conservation priorities and later assessment of project and program effectiveness. They argue that economics is rarely integrated into either. They propose two guiding questions for decision-makers: where scarce resources should be invested, and which investments have been most successful. Answering them requires [[Cost-Effectiveness Analysis]] and [[Cost-Utility Analysis]].

## Background & Description

Global expenditure on nature reserves, the cornerstone of conservation, was estimated at US$5 billion per year (James et al. 1999). Even so, the number of natural areas and threatened species needing management far exceeds available resources (Weitzman 1998). The authors note that the full range of costs is rarely considered when making informed choices, and that previous reviews (Kleiman et al. 2000; Clark 1996) did not incorporate economic analysis.

The paper outlines three economic approaches that can contribute to prioritization and evaluation:

- **[[Cost-Benefit Analysis]] (CBA):** asks whether gains to society exceed the sacrifices required. It treats conservation as one goal among many and measures allocative efficiency. Its known problems include valuing nonmarket goods, anthropocentrism, discounting, uncertainty and ecosystem complexity.
- **[[Cost-Effectiveness Analysis]] (CEA):** finds the least-cost means to reach a conservation objective based on a single nonmonetary output measure, such as bird pairs or survival probability. It cannot show distributional effects unless opportunity costs are included.
- **[[Cost-Utility Analysis]] (CUA):** adapted from health-care evaluation and measures outputs in units of utility. In health care these units are [[Quality-Adjusted Life Years]] (QALYs). For conservation, [[Conservation-Output Protection-Years]] (COPY) serve the same function, allowing different activities to be compared.

Portfolio management is also discussed as a complementary approach, drawing on [[Markowitz Portfolio Theory]] to maximize conservation gains under risk.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Priority-setting approaches.** Ecological approaches reviewed in Table 1 include:
- [[Centers of Endemism]] (International Council for Bird Preservation 1992)
- [[Biodiversity Hotspots]] (Myers et al. 2000)
- [[Flagship Species]] (World Wide Fund for Nature), useful for gaining support
- [[Threatened Species]] listings (World Conservation Union)
- [[Phylogenetic Diversity]] (Vane-Wright/Faith approaches, Walker & Faith 1995)
- [[Representativeness]] and multicriteria, ecologically based methods

Most of these are useful for identifying priority areas but do not consider costs. The "squeaky wheel" approach, driven by public petitions and lobbying, is noted as reflecting public interest rather than scientific concern.

**Optimization and the Northern Spotted Owl.** Montgomery and colleagues used opportunity costs of alternative land uses to model the marginal cost of owl protection. Montgomery et al. (1994) showed that marginal costs rise rapidly as survival probability approaches 100%. The authors argue that conservation plans should be viewed as buying incremental changes in survival likelihood.

**Evaluation approaches.** Table 3 reviews [[Ecological Productivity]] measures, applied to [[North Island Kokako]] recovery (Innes et al. 1999), and [[Threat-Reduction Assessment]] (Salafsky & Margoluis 1999), which is practical but ignores costs and assumes all threats are equally important.

**Case studies of endangered species.**
- Bryant and Harris (1996) reviewed 16 endangered-species projects in Tasmania. All 16 achieved positive results, but only one reversed a declining population trend.
- Cullen et al. (1999, 2001) applied CUA to threatened-species projects in New Zealand and reported a large range in cost per conservation-output protection year.
- Hughey et al. (unpublished) found large differences in output and cost-effectiveness among North Island Kokako management sites.
- Macmillan et al. (1998) found that government expenditure on Scottish woodland restoration was negatively correlated with ecosystem benefits.

## Key Findings

1. **Economics is largely absent from priority setting and evaluation.** Economic analysis has mostly been used to explain why species are endangered, to measure opportunity costs, and to design incentives, not to set priorities or evaluate outcomes.

2. **Current evaluation questions are incomplete.** The two typical questions ("Where are the priority areas?" and "Are our management interventions ecologically effective?") do not address comparative utility across projects or consider the costs of management. This leaves opportunity costs unexamined.

3. **Two core decision questions.** Decision-makers should ask (1) where scarce resources should be invested and (2) which investments have been best. Answering the first requires an ecological measure of expected output and expected cost. Answering the second requires combining output with costs.

4. **Few studies use real output and cost data.** Only Cullen et al. (1999, 2001) and Hughey et al. (unpublished) used actual output and cost data to apply CUA or CEA to conservation project evaluation.

5. **Economic tools are underused.** Cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analysis have begun to be applied to endangered-species evaluation and could support both interspecies comparisons and intraspecies intervention choices.

6. **Project Merit in New Zealand.** The New Zealand Department of Conservation is developing a multicriteria, CEA-based decision support system to forecast conservation gains and compare them with project costs. The authors note it is complex and data-hungry but has strong potential.

7. **Recommended economic ideas for conservation.** The paper highlights diminishing marginal productivity, economies of scale and scope, specialization gains, returns to research and development, and risk-return tradeoffs.

8. **Conclusion.** Conservation managers need a range of tools, and in some cases these tools must include economics. Implementing cost-effectiveness and cost-utility approaches is expected to improve endangered-species management and conservation management more broadly, and the authors emphasize that such analysis is relatively light on data and time requirements.
