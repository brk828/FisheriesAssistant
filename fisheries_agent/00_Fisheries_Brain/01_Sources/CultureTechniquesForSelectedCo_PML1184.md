# Culture Techniques for Selected Colorado River Imperiled Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report by Buddy L. Jensen of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] describes captive culture of imperiled desert and Colorado River fishes at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (Dexter NFH), New Mexico. Habitat alteration by dams, flow regulation and groundwater decline has driven steady losses of native southwestern fishes. Since 1974 Dexter has held, spawned, reared and distributed native species, holding up to 20 species and 13 at the time of writing. The paper focuses on three large Colorado River fishes: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. It details broodstock maturation, hormone-induced spawning, egg handling, incubation temperature and fry stocking results.

## Background & Description

The hatchery was created to pursue seven objectives: maintaining a protected gene pool of imperiled desert fishes, developing culture techniques, studying biological requirements, supplying live and preserved fish to agencies, exchanging expertise, publishing findings, and running a public information program. Work followed the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 and recovery plans. Dexter began holding native fishes in 1974, and by 1978 the facility was fully devoted to this work.

Table 1 lists 13 species held as of December 1, 1983, across the families Cyprinidae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae and Catostomidae. Federal status ranged from Endangered (E) to Proposed (P) and Candidate (C). Representative taxa include Colorado Squawfish, Bonytail Chub, Razorback Sucker, Yaqui Chub, Woundfin, Beautiful Shiner, Comanche Springs Pupfish, Leon Springs Pupfish, Desert Pupfish, Gila Topminnow, Big Bend Gambusia and Yaqui Sucker. Two Chihuahua Chub populations are held, one from the Mimbres River, New Mexico, and one from the Rio Florida, Chihuahua, Mexico.

**Facility.** Dexter NFH lies near the Pecos River in the Chihuahua Desert, about 200 miles southeast of Albuquerque, at roughly 3,500 feet elevation. Average annual rainfall is about 12 inches and the growing season is 180 to 200 days. Three wells (45 to 150 feet deep) pump 1,750 gpm of water at a constant 64°F, with pH 7.2 to 7.8, total hardness averaging 2,140 mg/l and total dissolved solids about 3,500 mg/l. Culture infrastructure includes a holding house with sixteen 300-gallon tanks, a hatching battery and a laboratory, 35 earthen ponds totaling about 17 acres (surface areas 0.1 to 1.8 acres), a 150-foot flow-through artificial stream, and 13 ponds under construction. Effluent is collected in sumps isolated from other surface waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document attributes the decline of these fishes primarily to human activity. Large dams in deep canyons blocked natural flow fluctuations, creating reservoirs and dewatering or altering downstream reaches through hypolimnetic releases. Groundwater depletion by irrigation pumping reduced or dried many desert springs. These changes, from lotic to lentic conditions and altered flow, temperature and drying regimes, produced a steady decline in the native southwestern fish fauna.

The Colorado River system's large fishes are long-lived, which the author links to their survival despite minimal recruitment. Razorback suckers in Lake Mohave were aged at 30 to 35 years, and bonytail chub at 40 and 42 years. Colorado squawfish, by extrapolation, are likely 50 or more years old.

## Key Findings

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**
- Adults reach about three feet and 12 pounds. Recent collections in the upper and lower basins have produced no young fish, and there is no evidence of recruitment into the aging adult population.
- Under a cooperative agreement between Arizona and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the Service stocks 100,000 fingerlings annually into Arizona waters for ten years, in place of listing the species.
- Males and females reared at Dexter matured at about two years old. Two-year-old males averaged 15.6 inches and 1.6 pounds. Maturity appears most closely tied to size.
- Females are injected with human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG) at 100 IU per pound intramuscularly, every 24 hours until ovulation is complete. Eggs are very adhesive, so they are "clayed" with bentonite after fertilization. Optimal incubation is about 70°F, with hatching at 96 to 144 hours.
- In 1983, 46 of 55 wild females (83.6%) spawned successfully. Mean fecundity was 124,522 eggs, and egg viability at 72 hours averaged 75.1%, up from 90.5% in 1982 (hmm: the 1982 figure is given as 90.5% in the source).
- Of 281,658 fry stocked, 159,967 advanced fry were harvested (56.8% return). Restocked fry returned 136,419 three-inch fingerlings (96.1%), and 53,849 were restocked to produce five-inch fingerlings, yielding 49,433 fish (91.8%). Feed conversion was 2.03.

**Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)**
- North America's largest cyprinid; historically reached six feet and nearly 100 pounds. It is extinct in the lower basin, with a remnant population in the upper basin.
- Hatchery fish reach sexual maturity in their sixth year. Spawning occurs in late May or early June at about 70°F. All females must be spawned within a one- to two-week window after ripening.
- Females receive carp pituitary (CP) at 2 mg per pound of body weight, intraperitoneally. Males receive HCG at 150 IU per pound if milt fluidity declines.
- In 1983, 26 females (25 domestic, one wild) were spawned. Domestic fecundity ranged from 57,766 to 113,341 (mean 77,436). The wild female produced 242,xxx eggs (value garbled in source). A total of 2,178,883 eggs was taken.
- Egg viability averaged 59% in Heath incubators and 66% in jars, mean 60.7%. Fry loss from "bunching" in tank corners was severe and occurred at night.
- Of 234,000 fry stocked in rearing ponds, 160,000 advanced fry were returned (68.3%). From 120,000 advanced fry, 87,153 three-inch fingerlings were harvested (72% return). Feed conversion was 3.90. Fingerlings were tagged and stocked in ground-water ponds and Colorado River backwaters near Grand Junction, Colorado.

**Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*)**
- Among the large native fishes, this species is closest to extinction. Extensive collecting over five years yielded only 18 fish, of which five remained alive (two females and three males).
- Dexter's broodstock descends from fry from a 1981 spawning at Willow Beach NFH.
- Twenty-four females were spawned over four weeks in May using CP. Fecundity ranged from 1,015 to 10,384 (mean 4,677), and 119,764 eggs were taken with mean viability of 60.5%.
- Fry were distributed to the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the California Department of Fish and Game. No fingerlings were reared at Dexter.

**Conclusions**
- Recovery requires protection, habitat preservation and management, research, captive propagation, reintroduction and public education.
- Without evident recruitment in Razorback Sucker and Bonytail Chub, long lifespans are the main reason these species have not yet gone extinct.
