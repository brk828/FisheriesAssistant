# Observations on the Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in the Colorado and Little Colorado Rivers, Grand Canyon National Park, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This field study by Minckley, Carothers, Jordan and Usher (Museum of Northern Arizona) documents the life history, distribution, reproduction, food habits, behavior and decline of the [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), a [[Colorado River Endemic Fish]] listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1973. Data were collected from 1975 to 1979 across a 285-mile (456 km) study reach from [[Lee Ferry]] to [[Pearce Ferry]] on [[Lake Mead]]. The authors identify the [[Little Colorado River]] as the species' primary spawning and nursery habitat and attribute the decline chiefly to altered flow regimes from [[Glen Canyon Dam]], particularly cold water releases and daily water fluctuations. The paper recommends critical habitat designation for the Little Colorado River and the mainstream Colorado River below Glen Canyon Dam.

## Background & Description

The humpback chub is one of the few remaining endemic fishes of the Colorado River system. It was known to early river explorers, including the Kolb brothers, who reported catching enough for supper near the mouth of the Little Colorado River in the early 1900s. It was formally described by [[Miller 1946]] from Bright Angel Creek (River Mile 601) in Grand Canyon, and was thought to be restricted to that area for over twenty years.

Later records extended its range to the Green River (1968–1971), the Yampa River, and the mainstream Upper Colorado River near Grand Junction. Fossil and archaeological remains from Stanton's Cave and Catclaw Cave (near Hoover Dam) show that the species occupied portions of the Colorado River well below Grand Canyon as early as 4000 B.P.

Life history information is sparse. Growth data came from length-frequency analysis in earlier work, and this study aged ten fish using the opercle method. Study methods included electrofishing, trammel and hoop/gill nets, seining and fyke nets, with tributaries surveyed mainly by seining. Research was supported by contracts from the Department of Energy (Boulder City), the National Park Service (Grand Canyon) and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region II).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and movement.** By 1976 the known range within Grand Canyon ran from Glen Canyon Dam (River Mile 613.6) to Shinumo Creek (River Mile 580.6). This study extended it downriver: one fish was collected at River Mile 555 below Dubendorff Rapids (August 1978), and anglers on commercial river trips reported fish at River Mile 494 below Whitmore Wash (summer 1978) and at the mouth of Havasu Creek (August 1979). The authors suggest the species probably occurs in [[Lake Mead]], about forty miles further downstream. Adults were primarily taken in eddies adjacent to fast currents in the mainstream. In the [[Little Colorado River]], adults used most available habitats, including eddy-side pools, quiet pools and areas below travertine dams. Numbers decline downriver from Lee Ferry. Forty-one humpback chubs were taken from the Colorado River during 1977–1979, compared with 611 from the Little Colorado River over the same period.

**Movement.** Tagging with fingerling tags began with 223 fish tagged and none recaptured, so movement conclusions are inconclusive. Observations from eight trips indicate larger fish use the confluent zone heavily in spring and summer, less so in fall and winter, and the main adult population may move into the mainstream in spring and summer.

**Reproduction, age and growth.** The reproductive season runs from March through June and possibly July, based on fish in reproductive condition in March and April and smaller fish collected in June and July. The Little Colorado River is the key spawning and nursery site. Young-of-the-year and yearling densities ranged from 167 to 6,364 fish per hectare depending on locality and season, and the upper Little Colorado River appears to be used more heavily for spawning than the confluent zone. Mainstream reproduction is suspected but not confirmed: ripe fish were collected in April 1979 at 11 °C, and reproduction is considered likely only at temperatures above 16–18 °C. Aged fish reached 86 mm total length in year one and 125 mm in year two; the oldest aged fish was 358 mm and nine years old. Growth of young-of-the-year fish was 19 to 72 mm between June and October 1978.

**Food habits.** The four adults examined were empty. Young-of-the-year fish (N = 3) contained Dipterans, including Chironomidae and Dolichopodidae. Adults were observed foraging on bottom, surface and drifting [[Cladophora Glomerata]] in the Little Colorado River, and readily took food dropped by commercial river companies.

**Behavior.** Humpback chubs school. Adult schools in the Little Colorado River were composed only of humpback chubs, while smaller-fish schools were mixed with young [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*). Mainstream fish were generally collected in groups of two or three. In July 1978, 87 fish collected at the confluence showed three apparent size classes (under 200 mm, 200–300 mm, over 300 mm), suggesting size segregation among schools.

**Reasons for decline.** Impacts on native fishes of the American Southwest include exotic species, pollution, fishing pressure and introduced parasites. The authors judge altered flow from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to be the most significant cause within Grand Canyon. Since Lake Powell filled in 1963, water temperatures have fallen from near ambient pre-dam levels to 7–12 °C, which has essentially halted mainstream reproduction. Daily fluctuations tied to hydroelectric demand are also damaging: vertical fluctuations exceed 1.5 m at high flows and are generally under one meter at low flows. Backwaters and ponded confluent zones formed at high flow are ephemeral, lasting 10–15 hours. On the Paria River, flow can drop from 35 m wide and 2 m deep to a channel one meter wide and 0.2 m deep within hours.

## Key Findings

- The humpback chub has a documented range from Glen Canyon Dam to River Mile 494, with a probable extension into Lake Mead.
- The Little Colorado River is the principal spawning and nursery area, with 611 fish collected there versus 41 in the mainstream (1977–1979).
- The reproductive season is March through June (possibly July); mainstream spawning is suspected but unconfirmed.
- Aged growth: 86 mm at year one, 125 mm at year two, maximum recorded age nine years (358 mm).
- Diet is limited; young fish eat Dipterans and adults take Cladophora glomerata and dropped food.
- Schooling is documented in both the Little Colorado and mainstream Colorado Rivers, with size segregation in some schools.
- Cold, fluctuating flows from Glen Canyon Dam are the leading cause of decline.
- Recommendations: designate the Little Colorado River from Blue Springs (13 miles / 20.8 km upstream) to the confluence and the Colorado River from Glen Canyon Dam to Separation Rapids as critical habitat; conduct further ecological studies and long-term population monitoring.
