# Research in Fisheries, Contribution No. 280 (March 1968): College of Fisheries, University of Washington

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report from the [[Fisheries Research Institute]] at the [[University of Washington College of Fisheries]] (March 1968) summarizes the year's work across salmon biology, estuarine ecology, invertebrate fisheries, radioecology, water utilization and food science. The core research focus is [[Pacific Salmon]] (genus *Oncorhynchus*) and [[Steelhead Trout]] at sea, in Alaskan nursery lakes, and in Washington estuaries and streams. Major themes include ocean migration and tagging, racial and feeding studies of juvenile salmon, forecasting and escapement estimation for Alaskan runs, estuarine water quality and fish use in the Duwamish, shellfish pathology and toxicity, and bacterial spoilage and pathogens in seafood. The document is a table of contents plus results-oriented summaries, not a single study.

## Background & Description

The report is Contribution No. 280 of the College of Fisheries. Its contents list about 70 pages of project summaries, faculty and staff rosters, graduate student lists, fellowships and scholarships, publications for 1967, and abstracts of Ph.D. theses. High-seas salmon work began in 1955 as a joint program of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the [[Fisheries Research Board of Canada]] and the Fisheries Agency of Japan, coordinated under the [[International North Pacific Fisheries Commission]]. Related program areas include the [[Chignik Sockeye Salmon]] and [[Kvichak Sockeye Salmon]] studies, the [[Nushagak District]] research, the [[Duwamish Estuary]] studies, and the [[Shellfish Pathology]] program. Sponsors include the U.S. Bureau of Commercial Fisheries, the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, the Washington Department of Fisheries and private industry.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Key habitats described are:

- **Open ocean:** Juvenile sockeye, pink and coho salmon migrate northwestward along the coast of the Gulf of Alaska. Immature sockeye south of Adak and juvenile salmon in the eastern Bering Sea were sampled.
- **Alaskan nursery lakes:** Iliamna Lake, Lake Aleknagik, Tikchik Lake and the Wood River system support zooplankton-based food webs that limit salmon production.
- **Estuaries:** Chignik Lagoon serves as a secondary nursery for sockeye smolts. The Duwamish Estuary, Elliott Bay and Bellingham Bay receive municipal and industrial wastes, with low dissolved oxygen in summer.
- **Shellfish beds:** Humboldt Bay, Yaquina Bay, Willapa Bay, Hood Canal and Puget Sound host oysters and mussels affected by pathogens and parasites.
- **Laboratory and experimental systems:** A gnotobiotic freshwater microcosm and tanks used for radiation and endrin studies.

Environmental variables monitored include temperature, dissolved oxygen, salinity, nutrients, zooplankton, phytoplankton, and water flow. Concerns include urban development around Big Beef Creek, pollution from sewage and the Renton treatment plant, and potential radionuclide introductions near Johnston Atoll.

## Key Findings

**High-seas salmon sampling and tagging (1967)**
- Three major operations caught 45,897 salmon and tagged 7,170 (sockeye, pink, chinook, chum, coho, steelhead).
- South of Adak, 3,801 immature sockeye were caught (about 78% .2 age), suggesting .2-age fish will dominate the 1968 [[Bristol Bay]] run.
- Juvenile sockeye migrated slowly in the eastern Bering Sea (maximum 300 miles by 15 September) but rapidly along the Gulf of Alaska coast (about 1,000 miles by mid-August for fish from the Fraser River region).
- A new molded plastic internal anchor tag ([[Dennison Swiftacher Tag]]) was used; returns are needed to evaluate it.
- Coho return rates from Gulf of Alaska juvenile tagging were 2.9% (1964), 4.4% (1965) and 2.0% (1966). No returns came from Puget Sound.

**Age and feeding studies**
- Coho scale studies suggest hatchery marks may be misread as annuli, inflating apparent freshwater age.
- Young sockeye (10–28 cm) fed on euphausiids, amphipods, copepods, larvae and pteropods; feeding rates varied within 24 hours.
- In Lake Aleknagik, pink fry grew 0.6 mm/day, about 21 times faster than sockeye fry in the same lake. Pink fry fed mainly on zooplankton, while sockeye fed mainly on midge (Chironomidae) items.

**Kodiak Island pink salmon**
- The 1967 run was among the smallest in 50 years, with a pack of only 18,000 cases; the island was closed to commercial fishing for the season.
- Fingerling indices varied among bays; Alitak Bay Portage Bay catches reached 9,240 pink salmon per 10-minute haul.
- A 1967 trawl gear study (125 paired hauls) found good correlation between paired catches only during the darkest hours (r = 0.98 between 2300 and 0100 hours).

**Chignik sockeye**
- Chignik Lagoon held 34 fish species; sockeye smolts were most strongly associated with adult threespine stickleback.
- Tagging data (1956–1967) showed 100% of tag recoveries lay within 58°N, 168°W to 187°45′W. The center of distribution of maturing fish lies in the central Gulf of Alaska near the Alaska gyre.

**Nushagak District**
- Even-year pink catches averaged 1.2 million since 1958, compared with under 250,000 annually from 1922 to 1956.
- Benthic surveys in 1965 (over 300 samples in eight lakes) found amphipods rare, unlike five other large North American lakes. Salmon spawner density tracked zooplankton, while whitefish tracked benthos.
- Spawner estimates: adipose-fin removal was the reliable mark; dorsal-fin perforation was not. Tag loss ranged from 22.3% to 73.7%.

**Kvichak sockeye**
- The run fell from 46,566,000 (1965) to 4,914,000 (1967), reflecting strong cyclic variability in survival.
- In Iliamna Lake, the 1965 year class produced over 21 times as many yearling smolts as the 1960 class, even though its parent escapement was only about 1.7 times larger.

**Lake Clark and Newhalen River**
- Dennison-tagged salmon ascended the 25-mile Newhalen River in an average of 23 days (20–30 days depending on tagging date).

**Big Beef Creek (coho and chum)**
- Estimated adult coho spawning population was 5,536 fish. Smolt estimates totaled 64,803 (lake, stream above and below the lake).
- Lake temperatures averaged 12°F warmer than the upper stream in summer.
- A new chum spawning channel (600 ft long, 24 spawning pens) is testing four gravel grades.

**Bellingham Bay and Duwamish Estuary**
- In Bellingham Bay, 271,362 chinook and 176,640 coho were marked and released; 128 marked chinook and 41 marked coho were recaptured.
- Dissolved oxygen in the Duwamish was predicted from streamflow, tidal prism and adjacent stations. In summer 1967 bottom water reached 1.0 mg/l.
- Survival of fingerling chinook during downstream migration was estimated at 0.513 (flow about 1,470 cfs) and 0.678 (flow about 1,550 cfs), preliminary values.
- Lamprey predation (river lamprey, *Lampetra ayresi*) wounded up to 7% of captured chinook.
- Adult chinook movements were tracked with sonic tags (47 released in group 1; about 140 of 305 tagged fish recovered at Green River Hatchery).

**Demersal fishes**
- Beam trawl work at Duwamish Head used a 42-haul grid over 24-hour periods. Feeding maxima varied with fish size.
- Hemangioma incidence was below 1% in *Parophrys vetulus* and 8.6% in *Platichthys* (9 of 105 fish).

**Invertebrate fisheries**
- Humboldt Bay Pacific oyster mortality fell to under 7.1% (1967). The 1965 year class showed only 0–4.2% mortality in its second summer.
- The copepod *Mytilicola orientalis* infected oysters and mussels; infested oysters had lower condition indices.
- Spot shrimp (*Pandalus platyceros*) reared from eggs passed five larval stages before postlarval stage; survival was 83 days.
- *Gonyaulax washingtonensis* toxin reached mussels at 75–92% recovery; oysters showed no measurable uptake over three months.

**Radioecology**
- Johnston Atoll (Pacific) fallout radionuclides were detected in January 1967; only cesium-137 was found in tuna muscle.
- Zinc-65 in oysters had a biological half-life of about 125 days.
- At Fern Lake, a 1965 fertilization (814 tons) raised winter zooplankton dry weight from about 30 mg/m² to 128 mg/m²; steelhead production over 9 months doubled.

**Water utilization and toxicity**
- The Water Resources report suggested 45 subtopics in eleven general topics for fish and wildlife.
- The ammonia-urea plant effluent test found 48-hour TLm of about 47 ppm NH3-NH4 (0.60 ppm NH3) at 29 ppth salinity; toxicity declined with salinity.
- Endrin was passed from the yolk sac to the embryo; fry showed toxicity symptoms at hatching.

**Food science**
- Psychrotrophic *Pseudomonas* became dominant during spoilage of iced English sole (over 90% of flora in late storage).
- *Salmonella* grew at 8°C in English sole tissue but not below 6°C.
- *Vibrio parahaemolyticus* was found in Puget Sound at 30–350/ml water, 100–2,000/g sediment and 65–4,000/g oysters during summer.

**Fern Lake and Fish Diet**
- Fern Lake's benthic dry weight averaged 3.26 g/m²; Diptera emergence produced about 0.5 g/m² annually.

**Conclusions and plans**
- The 1968 Bristol Bay forecast and Kodiak forecast are being prepared; recovery of 1967 tags will be needed to evaluate the new tag.
- The Washington Cooperative Fishery Unit (four research assistantships in fiscal year 1968 at $4,000 each) began operations; projects include the [[Geoduck]] life history and coho-steelhead behavior.
