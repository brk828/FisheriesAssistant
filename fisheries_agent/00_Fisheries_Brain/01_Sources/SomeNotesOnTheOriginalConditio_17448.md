# Hopi Agriculture, Little Colorado River Change, and Northern Arizona Crafts

#ethnobotany #freshwater-ecology #riparian-vegetation

## Executive Summary

This is a compilation of three articles and notices from the *Museum Notes* of the [[Museum Of Northern Arizona]] (1937–1938). The main items are: (1) an ethnobotanical account of Hopi "embryonic agriculture," including semi-domestication of wild plants and seed transfer through ceremonial [[Katcina]] distribution; (2) a historical and geomorphic account of how the [[Little Colorado River]] shifted from a perennial, cottonwood-lined stream to a wide, sandy, intermittent channel after overgrazing, drought and floods; and (3) a short review of Northern Arizona Indian crafts, mainly pottery, plus announcements of new museum bulletins. No fish or aquatic fauna are described. The aquatic relevance lies in riparian vegetation and river hydrology.

## Background & Description

**Hopi agriculture (A. F. Whiting).** Corn from ceremonial sources, known as "Kiitcina corn," is distributed by katcina impersonators and treated as sacred. It is usually planted early and eaten green. Cross-fertilization makes most of this corn unsuitable for seed. Of about fifty families interviewed, only two reported raising crops received from katcinas before the previous spring, so the system rarely functions as a practical seed-distribution mechanism. The article notes that the [[Black-Seeded Hopi Sunflower]] may descend from pre-maize agriculture, citing V. N. Jones (1936).

**Little Colorado River history (H. S. Colton).** The Little Colorado is a principal tributary of the [[Colorado River]]. Its red silt gave the Colorado its name, attributed to Oñate. Between Holbrook and Cameron it now flows, when it flows at all, through a wide treeless valley with a broad sandy bed and steep cut banks. Historical sources describe a different river:

- 1583: Luxán (Espejo expedition) described "groves of poplars and willows."
- 1598: Jaramillo and Quesada named it the Río Alameda ("river of groves"), noting great cottonwood groves.
- 1851: Lt. Sitgreaves was blocked by extensive swamps above Winslow.
- 1853: Lt. Whipple measured the river at about 30 feet wide, with alluvial banks 8–10 feet high and cottonwoods along the banks.
- 1858: Lt. Ives described swift, chocolate-colored water, shoals, snags and sandbars in a channel about 50 yards wide and 5–6 feet deep.
- 1858: Beale noted good grass and predicted a large population.

**Excavation and dated house (1935–1937).** The Museum's 1935 expedition excavated a one-roomed red sandstone structure near Grand Falls, about 40 miles east of Flagstaff. It proved to be the [[Hart House]], built in 1878 or 1879 by Frank Hart, not a prehistoric pit house. Artifacts on the floor included white glazed crockery, horseshoe fragments, sheep bones and a tin can. Oral history from William Roden, who ran sheep there from 1884, supplied the context.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pre-1880 riparian system.** The river was narrow and perennial, with cottonwood and willow groves and beaver living along its banks. The house was then more than 100 feet from the water.

**Degradation sequence.**
1. Heavy cattle and sheep grazing from the 1870s–1880s reduced grass cover.
2. A severe drought caused stock to overgraze. Navajo herds then cut down young cottonwoods for fodder.
3. Floods in the early 1890s, with no grass to hold water, undermined and uprooted cottonwoods. Their whitened trunks mark the high-water limit of the 1840s–1850s.
4. The narrow channel overflowed its banks and deposited about 30 inches of sediment on the flats, burying the 1880 soil surface. Floods also poured over the house walls and filled its rooms with debris.
5. By 1935 the east wall had fallen. In the two years to summer 1937 the river cut back about 14 feet, leaving only the back wall of the Hart House.

**Present conditions.** Former hills covered in grama and mesquite-type grasses are now desert pavement of polished pebbles. Navajo sheep prevent young trees from establishing. [[Tamarisk]] (*Tamarix*), introduced from Asia onto upper tributaries, is spreading on sandy flats. Sheep avoid it, so it is not grazed. Colton suggests this may mark the start of a new era of stability.

**Wild plant management (Hopi).** Several wild species are tolerated or cultivated in or near fields:
- Rocky Mountain bee weed is allowed to mature in corn fields to seed the next crop.
- Little wild potato (*Solanum jamesii*) is left in corn fields and often gives a good yield.
- Devil's claw (*Martynia*) is left in fields for its spiny fruits, which are thought to attract lightning and rain, and for ceremonial use.
- Cottonwood and willow are transplanted into neighboring washes.
- A cattail (*Typha angustifolia*) patch in Oraibi Wash, said to come from near Tuba City, supplies gifts for children at the Niman Katcina ceremony.
- Wild tobacco is occasionally seeded by individuals for ceremonial use. Wild dock root, used for dyeing, is now hard to find near the towns.

Whiting concludes that this embryonic agriculture has probably operated for a long time but contributes little to modern Hopi crops or the native economy.

**Crafts and cultural change (Northern Arizona).** Pottery in the Southwest dates to at least the 8th century A.D. Pottery making for domestic use ceased among most groups around 1900, persisting only among the Hopi, Navajo and Mojave. Hopi pottery is made by hand by women, is golden yellow or red, and is painted with geometric designs in red, black and white. Dealers have long demanded it, and thousands of small vessels are produced each year.

## Key Findings

- **Seed transfer is weak:** Only 2 of about 50 interviewed Hopi families had grown crops received from katcinas before the previous spring.
- **Possible pre-maize sunflower domestication:** The black-seeded Hopi sunflower may descend from early pre-maize agriculture, per Jones (1936).
- **Managed wild plants:** Several weeds, wild potatoes, cattails and ceremonial plants are deliberately tolerated or propagated, but this practice is economically minor.
- **Historical perennial stream:** Early explorers recorded a narrow, perennial river lined with cottonwood groves, swamps and willow thickets.
- **Overgrazing and flood cycle:** Grazing, drought and early-1890s floods removed riparian cover, leading to channel widening and sediment deposition of about 30 inches.
- **Rapid bank erosion:** The river cut back about 14 feet in two years (1935–1937) at the Hart House site.
- **Invasive tamarisk:** Introduced tamarisk is establishing on sandy flats, where sheep avoid it.
- **Pottery traditions:** Pottery production has largely disappeared outside the Hopi, Navajo and Mojave.
- **Museum publications:** *Handbook of Northern Arizona Pottery Wares* (Colton and Hargrave, Bulletin 11, Aug. 1937, 267 pp., 62 illus., $4.00) and *Winona Village* (McGregor, Bulletin 12, Oct. 1937, 84 pp., 20 plates, 75¢) were announced. Bulletin 13, *How Some Important Northern Arizona Pottery Types Were Dated* (McGregor, Jan. 1938, 60¢), was in press.
