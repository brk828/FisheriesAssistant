# Native Fishes of the Grand Canyon Region: An Obituary?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by W. L. Minckley, from the 1990 [[Colorado River Ecology and Dam Management]] symposium proceedings, argues that the highly endemic fish fauna of the Colorado River downstream from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has been largely extirpated. Dams, diversion and regulation stabilized the river, which favored introduced nonnative fishes and harmed native species. Minckley uses historical accounts to reconstruct a pristine ecosystem, document native declines, and identify alien fish establishment as the ultimate cause of extirpation. Despite legislation and scientific effort in the 1970s and early 1980s, extinction trends were not reversed, and he judges the fauna's survival doubtful given late-1980s political and economic pressures.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] drains more than 600,000 km² of mountains, plateaus and basins. Its natural regime included high spring snowmelt runoff, low flows in summer and winter, and late-summer monsoon spates in the south. Pre-dam discharge at Yuma, Arizona, varied over five orders of magnitude (0.5 to 7,100 m³ s⁻¹, per Dill, 1944). Canyon reaches provided refugia: cool, nutrient-rich groundwater from alluvial reservoirs fed scoured pools and shaded undercuts.

Colorado River fishes show distinctive morphology associated with swift, turbulent habitat: fusiform, streamlined bodies, predorsal humps or keels, pencil-thin caudal peduncles, expansive falcate fins, thick leathery skin, and tiny or embedded scales. Adults are large and long-lived.

The fauna is unusually endemic. Evermann and Rutter (1895) recorded 32 indigenous species, and Minckley et al. (1986) documented 32 as well. Carlson and Muth (1989) compiled 44 taxa, 93% endemic when undescribed forms and subspecies were included. Of the eight fishes native to [[Grand Canyon National Park]], six are endemic. The fish fauna was documented through archaeological remains, early explorer accounts (including [[John Wesley Powell]] and the [[Stanton Railway Survey]]), and scientific collections by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] and [[Robert R. Miller]] beginning in the late 1930s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pristine conditions.** Fishes likely occupied valley reaches and moved into canyons during low flow to avoid declining water and high summer temperatures. Flooding rarely harmed fish populations. Food was limited during high turbidity but abundant during long low-flow periods, with algae, invertebrates and detritus available.

**Post-dam changes.** Use is intense: the basin has the highest consumptive water use of any U.S. river, and less than 1% of virgin flow reaches the mouth. At least 117 impoundments with capacities above 1.0 × 10⁶ m³ exist in the upper basin, and more than 40 diversions export water. Minckley does not attribute the decline primarily to physical habitat loss, which he considers the basin still large and diverse. He concludes that dams cause extirpation mainly through two direct effects, blockage of spawning migrations and cold hypolimnetic releases, and that the critical driver is the introduction and enhancement of nonnative fishes.

**Thermal regime.** Releases from [[Lake Powell]] enter the reach at 7.0–10°C and rarely warm to 16°C by [[Lake Mead]], more than 400 km downstream. Humpback chub eggs require 15°C or higher for successful development.

**Nonnative fishes.** The original fauna of about 30 native species has grown to 80 or more. Red shiners, fathead minnows, channel catfish, green sunfish, mosquitofish and [[Striped Bass]] all play roles in displacement or predation. Mosquitofish eat native topminnow young, and flathead catfish are invading the Salt River Canyon.

## Key Findings

- **Extirpation trend.** Of the four federally listed or proposed endangered native fishes of Grand Canyon (humpback chub, bonytail, Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker), only humpback chub persists as a reproducing population; the others are extirpated or exceedingly rare. Speckled dace, flannelmouth sucker and bluehead sucker remain relatively common.
- **Historical decline.** The fish fauna collapsed from downstream to upstream, following the sequence of river regulation beginning just after 1900.
- **Native fish inventory.** Table 7-1 lists native and introduced fishes recorded from Grand Canyon National Park, including five native cyprinids and three native catostomids, with the rest introduced (salmonids, shads, catfishes, centrarchids, and others).
- **Early sampling.** A 1958 Glen Canyon survey at 27 sites took 17 species, of which six were native and ten nonnative.
- **Humpback chub.** The lowermost [[Little Colorado River]] is the only reproducing lower-basin population. Adults there were estimated at 7,000–8,000, 5,783, 7,060 and 10,120 fish across the sampled periods.
- **Colorado squawfish.** Roughly 8,000 adults were estimated in the upper Green and Yampa rivers for 1980–1989. The largest historical specimens approached 1.8 m and 36 kg.
- **Razorback sucker.** About 50,000 adults persist in [[Lake Mohave]], with limited recruitment, and no recruitment has been indicated for any wild population for more than 20 years. Larvae are heavily predated by nonnatives.
- **Bonytail.** Now the most endangered freshwater fish in North America, persisting almost exclusively under artificial conditions, with the Lake Mohave population showing only limited recruitment.
- **Trout fisheries.** Trout were introduced to cool Marble/Grand canyon tributaries in the 1920s, and cold Lake Powell releases now sustain mainstream trout, a fishery Minckley views as separated from native warmwater fishes by temperature.
- **Striped bass and Lake Powell.** The striped bass collapse in Lake Mead, Lake Powell and Lake Mohave is cited as a management catastrophe. A proposed rainbow smelt introduction into Lake Powell is opposed, with Minckley predicting harm to native and nonnative fishes alike.
- **Management recommendations.** Control of nonnative warmwater species is identified as the single most important requirement. Warming Glen Canyon releases would enable reintroduction of squawfish and bonytail but would displace trout, creating conflict.
- **Conservation politics.** Minckley argues that agency research is often short-term and mandate-driven, much agency work remains in unpublished "gray" literature, and conservation lost priority in the 1980s.
- **Conclusion.** The prognosis is poor. The Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program allows water development to proceed, lacks a definition of recovery, and offers no fallback if fishes do not recover within its 15-year period. Minckley concludes that biologists have done their part and that a successful management program could be devised, but current politics stand in the way.
