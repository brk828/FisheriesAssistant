# Survey of Crayfishes of the Gila River Basin, Arizona and New Mexico

#crustacea #decapoda #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This report surveys crayfishes in the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico, with notes on occurrences in other Arizona drainages and adjoining states. Arizona and the [[Colorado River Basin]] have no native crayfish species, but crayfish are widely introduced and in places naturalized. The study had no prior systematic inventory to build on. Field collections were made at 94 locales in Arizona and New Mexico, and a dozen visits were made to retail aquarium and pet stores in the Phoenix metropolitan area.

Two non-native species were captured in the wild: [[Procambarus Clarkii]] and [[Orconectes Virilis]]. Both were widely distributed, common to locally abundant, and found in natural and artificial lotic and lentic habitats. The authors state that very large populations likely harm indigenous fauna. Pet-store stock included [[Procambarus Alleni]], [[Procambarus Paeninsulanus]], and an unidentified Australian [[Cherax]]. The authors conclude that release of these species into the wild seems inevitable but should be discouraged, because some have the potential to cause significant habitat damage and to affect other aquatic biota.

## Background & Description

Crayfish belong to the order Decapoda and the infraorder Astacidea, which includes marine lobsters. Two superfamilies are recognized: Astacoidea (families Astacidae and Cambaridae, Northern Hemisphere) and Parastacoidea (family [[Parastacidae]], Southern Hemisphere). Crayfish occur on every continent except Antarctica and Africa. North America north of Mexico has 338 recognized taxa (308 species and 30 subspecies, per Taylor et al. 1996). Diversity is highest in the eastern, southern, and central US, while only one genus (Pacifastacus) is native to Pacific slope drainages. Arizona is the only state the authors identify as having no native crayfish species.

Crayfish first appeared in Arizona about 30 years before this report. They were stocked by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and the US Fish and Wildlife Service for aquatic weed control and as forage for sport fish. Other introduction pathways include discard by aquarium owners and escape or release from [[Bait Bucket Release]]s, which the authors consider the most likely explanation for present widespread distribution.

Identification relies on the first-form (Form I) adult male, which occurs in the Cambarinae and Cambarellinae and is recognized by corneous terminal elements on the first [[Pleopods]]. This stage is absent in Pacifastacus. Methods included seines, dip nets, electrofishing, and baited [[Minnow Traps]]. Fish baits from oily species (smelt or anchovy) were noted as more attractive to crayfish than non-oily species (cod or pollock), following Kutka et al. (1992). Specimens were fixed in 6% neutral formalin and preserved in alcohol.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat associations were a central theme. Both introduced species are generalists found in most aquatic habitat types. Table 1 shows that both species were captured more often from streams than from lakes and ponds. The authors note this partly reflects sampling bias, since about three times as many lotic as lentic systems were sampled. After adjustment, [[Procambarus Clarkii]] appeared more frequent in streams, while [[Orconectes Virilis]] appeared more frequent in lakes and ponds. Sample sizes were small, no statistical analysis was performed, and the authors describe the results as inconclusive.

Within habitats, crayfish abundance was generally greater in moderately turbid water. Shallow gradients, slow current, and fine or soft substrates were preferred in streams. In lentic systems, crayfish favored fine substrates with modest macrophyte growth, but were generally absent from dense plant beds.

The authors also document ecological impacts. [[Procambarus Clarkii]] is a [[Tertiary Burrower]] whose burrows can reach 1 to 2 m and destabilize earthen dikes and water control structures. Large [[Orconectes Virilis]] populations were linked to declines of [[Lake Trout]] in the Great Lakes and to the decimation of the trout fishery at [[Newcastle Reservoir]], Utah. In Arizona, O. virilis was implicated in affecting the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] by preying on eggs and altering habitat. The authors flag concern for threatened native fishes, especially the [[Spikedace]], which is endemic to the Gila River basin and survives in only a few remnant populations.

Survey localities spanned Apache, Cochise, Coconino, Gila, Graham, Greenlee, Maricopa, Yavapai, Yuma, and other counties. Many were occupied by native or listed fishes such as [[Gila Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Apache Trout]], and [[Roundtail Chub]].

## Key Findings

- **Non-native species dominate:** Only two species were found in the wild, [[Procambarus Clarkii]] and [[Orconectes Virilis]]. Both were widespread, common, and occupied diverse habitats. P. clarkii occurred at fewer locations and was generally restricted to the Colorado River mainstream, with exceptions in the Phoenix area and a Hassayampa tributary. O. virilis was found across all major Arizona drainages.
- **Sampling totals (Table 1):** Of 101 sites attempted, 23 were lentic and 78 lotic. P. clarkii was captured at 10 locations (3 lentic, 7 lotic). O. virilis was captured at 61 locations (9 lentic, 52 lotic). No crayfish were found at 30 locations (11 lentic, 19 lotic).
- **Pet trade:** Three species were found in stores. [[Procambarus Alleni]] was widely available at several outlets and was sold as the "Australian blue lobster" at Petsmart, although the authors note it is neither from Australia nor a lobster. P. paeninsulanus was advertised, but store specimens were later identified as P. clarkii, and the species' presence in trade could not be confirmed. The Australian [[Cherax]] was a display-only specimen of uncertain identity, possibly C. quadricarinatus.
- **Taxonomic decision:** [[Orconectes Causeyi]] was synonymized with O. virilis. Morphological measurements from Eagle Creek (Graham County) matched neither species cleanly, so the authors did not recognize O. causeyi as distinct in Arizona.
- **Native status and regional risk:** No crayfish are native to Arizona or the Colorado River basin. Neighboring states have their own faunas, which could move into Arizona waters through interbasin transfer. Mexico has 46 named crayfish species or subspecies, all native.
- **Conservation concern:** The authors recommend research on the effects of P. clarkii and O. virilis on Arizona aquatic ecosystems. They also warn that releases of other species, particularly those with habitat-altering burrowing behavior, should be discouraged.
