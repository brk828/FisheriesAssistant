# Eight Men Indicted for Blasting Quartzite Falls on the Salt River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fish-assemblage

## Executive Summary

Eight men from the Phoenix area were indicted by a federal grand jury for destroying [[Quartzite Falls]] on the [[Salt River]] in Arizona using explosives in 1993. The falls, located in the [[Salt River Canyon Wilderness]] within the [[Tonto National Forest]], had acted as a natural barrier that kept [[Non-Native Sport Fish]] from moving upstream into the headwaters, where [[Native Fishes]] live. A local ichthyologist warned that the loss of the barrier could lead to declines in native fish populations upstream. The men face charges of destruction of federal property and conspiracy, each carrying up to 20 years in prison and fines of up to $250,000. The indictment and the article's account come from The [[Arizona Republic]] reporting.

## Background & Description

Quartzite Falls was a rapid on the upper Salt River inside the Salt River Canyon Wilderness, east of [[Roosevelt Lake]]. It was known to [[Whitewater Rafting]] guides and clients as a major challenge and a long-standing hazard. According to a rafting company co-owner, it had been a bottleneck and a point of congestion, and two people had been killed at the falls the year before the article was published.

The falls also served an ecological function. Reporting describes it as a natural barrier that blocked non-native sport fish, which prey on native fish, from migrating upstream.

The indicted individuals were:

- [[William Kenneth Stoner]], 34, of Phoenix, a former river guide
- [[Stephen Martin Cortright]], 39, of Phoenix
- [[James Edward Lewus]], 40, of Phoenix
- [[Christopher Martin Meehl]], 34, of Phoenix
- [[Mark Edward Meehl]], 42, of Phoenix
- [[Michael William Meehl]], 38, of Phoenix
- [[Richard Merrick Scott]], 39, of Phoenix, who had training and experience with explosives
- [[William Coburn Kelley]], 28, of Glendale

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article presents the falls' destruction primarily as a threat to native fish, not as a purely recreational loss. [[W.L. Minckley]], a zoology professor at [[Arizona State University]] and a leading authority on native fishes of the Southwest, said the falls' loss could be detrimental to native fish populations upstream. He described the decline of native fish as a clear regional trend and stated that little could be done to reverse the damage at that point.

Non-native sport fish were the main concern. The falls kept these predators out of the upper river, so their removal of the barrier put native populations at risk of predation and competition. The article does not name specific species, populations, or survey data for the upper Salt River.

Gail Peters, Arizona director of the environmental group [[American Rivers]], described the falls as the most memorable feature of a full Salt River Canyon run. She expressed concern that the river would now be perceived as easier to navigate, noting that it remained a challenging route.

## Key Findings

**Chronology of the alleged offense (as stated by the grand jury):**

- **May 1993:** [[William Kenneth Stoner]] guided an overnight rafting trip for four of the men, including [[Richard Merrick Scott]]. Scott and Stoner discussed destroying the falls.
- **About August 27, 1993:** Scott bought 144 pounds of [[Explosives]], along with safety fuses, detonating cord, and blasting caps.
- **The following day:** Scott and five other men carried the explosives to the river's edge.
- **The next day:** Scott and four men rafted to Quartzite Falls and assembled the bombs. Two men used safety lines to lower Scott and Stoner into the river, where they placed the explosives. Scott ignited one or more bombs.
- **September and October 1993:** Additional blasts were set off at the falls. In October, Scott bought 10 more pounds of explosives.

**Charges and penalties:**

- Destruction of federal property
- Conspiracy
- Each defendant faces up to 20 years in prison and fines of up to $250,000.

**Official statements:**

- U.S. Attorney [[Janet Napolitano]] hoped the prosecution would deter others and called the explosions "mindless destruction."
- Assistant U.S. Attorney [[Paul Charlton]] stated that no indication existed that any commercial rafting firm was involved.

**Commercial connection:**

- [[Pat Blumm]], co-owner of [[Desert Voyagers Guided Rafting Tours]] in Mesa, said Stoner had worked for his firm for two or three seasons as a competent guide, but that he had never heard Stoner or anyone else discuss blowing up the falls.

**Conservation concerns:**

- Minckley expressed concern about the survival of native fishes in the upper Salt River now that the barrier is gone.
- Peters worried that the river's reputation could draw less experienced rafters who expect it to be easy.
