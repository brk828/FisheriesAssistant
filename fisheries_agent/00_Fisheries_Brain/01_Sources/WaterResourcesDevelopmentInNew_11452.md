# U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Water Resources Program in New Mexico (1979 Edition)

#hydrology #water-resources #flood-control

## Executive Summary

This biennial State pamphlet, issued by the [[Albuquerque District]] of the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] and dated 14 January 1980, describes the Corps' water resources development program in New Mexico. It supersedes the January 1977 issue. The program covers flood control, sedimentation control, municipal and irrigation water supply, recreation, environmental enhancement, flood plain management, and emergency assistance. It is organized by river basin: the [[Canadian River]], [[Pecos River]], [[Rio Grande]], and closed basins (Tularosa and Mimbres). The text does not address fish species, aquatic invertebrates, or water chemistry; its aquatic content concerns reservoirs, streams, and flood regimes.

## Background & Description

- **Mission:** Work began in 1824, when Congress directed navigation work on U.S. waterways. Responsibilities later expanded to flood control, water supply, hydroelectric power, recreation, and conservation.
- **Authorization process:** Local communities request help from Congress. The Corps acts only when Congress authorizes an investigation or project and appropriates funds for each phase. A flow chart in the pamphlet traces this sequence from local request through survey report, Board of Engineers review, authorization, design, and construction.
- **Navigation:** Navigation is not practicable on any New Mexico stream. Recreational boating on lakes and reservoirs has prompted consideration of anchorage areas.
- **Regulatory role:** The Corps issues permits under the [[Clean Water Act]] for discharge of dredged or fill material, and under the 1899 River and Harbor Act for navigable waters. The Albuquerque District administers all permits for New Mexico.
- **Climate and hydrology:** Average annual rainfall ranges from less than 8 inches in deserts to more than 20 inches in mountains. Spring snowmelt swells major rivers. Intense summer thunderstorms fill normally dry arroyos with fast flows that carry sediment and debris.
- **Project inventory:** The pamphlet describes completed, under-construction, and authorized projects, including dams, diversion channels, levees, and local protection works.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Watershed framework:** Basins are studied as units. The main development areas are the Canadian, Pecos, and Rio Grande watersheds, plus the Tularosa and Mimbres closed basins. Environmental quality is considered throughout project planning.
- **Flash flooding:** Arroyos and perennial streams are both prone to flash floods. Sediment loading is a primary concern, and several projects include dedicated sediment storage.
- **Closed basins:** The [[Tularosa Basin]] has no defined watercourses and no Corps projects. The [[Mimbres Basin]] drains 1,770 square miles, and its lower 40 miles are ephemeral. No Corps projects or studies are authorized there.
- **Wetlands and environment:** The text references wetland protection, non-structural solutions, and environmental enhancement as current Corps priorities. It gives no specific wetland or habitat data.
- **Recreation and visitation:** Reservoirs are used for fishing, boating, camping, and picnicking. Visitor counts are given for January 1977 through June 1978.

## Key Findings

### Major Projects by Basin

**Canadian River Watershed**
- [[Conchas Lake]]: Completed 1939 at a cost of $15.8 million. It was the Corps' 17th dam. The drainage area above the lake is 7,409 square miles. The permanent pool is 70,500 acre-feet at 4,155 feet elevation. The lake provides 259,600 acre-feet of conservation storage, 198,800 acre-feet of flood storage, and 70,500 acre-feet of sediment storage. About 420,000 people visited from January 1977 through June 1978. Four major floods occurred since completion (1941, 1942, 1965).

**Pecos River Watershed**
- [[Los Esteros Lake]]: Authorized by the 1954 Flood Control Act. The estimated cost is $27,000,000 (federal funds). The dam is 1,920 feet long and 212 feet high, and it drains 2,434 square miles. Storage includes 200,000 acre-feet for irrigation, 82,000 acre-feet for sediment, and 167,000 acre-feet for flood control. Completion is scheduled for 1980.
- [[Two Rivers Dam]]: Completed 1963 at $4.9 million. The project drains 1,027 square miles. It has no permanent lake, and it empties after each flood. More than 5,700 people visited from January 1977 through June 1978.

**Rio Grande Watershed**
- [[Abiquiu Dam]]: Operational in 1963 at $21.2 million. It is 325 feet high and 1,540 feet long, and drains 2,146 square miles. Total storage is 1,215,000 acre-feet, with 568,400 acre-feet for flood control and sediment. About 239,000 people visited from January 1977 through June 1978.
- [[Cochiti Lake]]: An all-federal project costing $94.4 million. The dam is 251 feet high and contains more than 65,000,000 cubic yards of material. It drains 11,695 square miles. Flood storage is 83,000 acre-feet and sediment storage is 105,000 acre-feet. The permanent recreation pool is 1,200 acres. About 563,000 people visited from January 1977 through June 1978.
- [[Galisteo Dam]]: Completed 1970 at $14.1 million. The dam is 158 feet high and drains 596 square miles. It stores 79,600 acre-feet for flood control and 9,400 acre-feet for sediment. About 4,300 people visited from January 1977 through June 1978.
- [[Jemez Canyon Dam]]: Completed 1954 at $4.1 million. The dam is 136 feet high and drains 1,004 square miles. It stores 73,000 acre-feet for flood control and 33,100 acre-feet for sediment. About 34,500 people visited from January 1977 through June 1978.
- [[Albuquerque Diversion Channels]]: Authorized 1954 and built from February 1965 to spring 1972 at $28.3 million, of which $11.2 million was non-federal. The north channel is about 10 miles long and all concrete. The south channel is about 6 miles long and combines concrete and riprap.
- [[Rio Grande Floodway]]: A joint Corps and Bureau of Reclamation program. It includes about 125 miles of levee rehabilitation (completed 1962 at $2.7 million) and a $3.1 million Albuquerque levee system. The Espanola reach was deauthorized in August 1977.
- [[Las Cruces Local Protection Project]]: Dam completed May 1975 at $5.5 million, of which $0.8 million was non-federal. The dam is 67 feet high and 15,570 feet long. The outfall channel was scheduled for 1979.
- [[Socorro Diversion Channel]]: Completed 1964 at $2.7 million, of which $0.3 million was non-federal.

**Authorized, Not Yet Built**
- Rio Puerco/Rio Salado: [[Hidden Mountain Dam]] (about 12,000 feet long, 150 feet high, 480,000 acre-feet, about $22.0 million) and [[Loma Blanca Dam]] (about 6,000 feet long, 145 feet high, 120,000 acre-feet, about $18.0 million). Both are in preconstruction planning. The final Environmental Impact Statement was filed in August 1974.
- Santa Fe River and Arroyo Mascaras: Authorized by the Water Resources Development Act of 1976. A final environmental statement was filed in August 1978.

**Other Water Resource Projects**
- [[San Juan-Chama Diversion Project]]: A Bureau of Reclamation project completed in 1970. It diverts an average of 110,000 acre-feet per year from the Upper Colorado River Basin into the Rio Chama, feeding Cochiti Lake's recreation pool and supplementing Rio Grande Basin supplies.

### Flood Plain Management and Surveys

- The Albuquerque District evaluated 444 sites for flood hazards in FY 1978.
- The Los Angeles District completed 52 flood hazard reports and a flood plain information report for the Rio Puerco at Gallup.
- The pamphlet lists about a dozen authorized surveys covering the Canadian, Pecos, Rio Grande, Gila, Colorado, and other basins. Several have indefinite completion dates. The Canadian River survey is scheduled for 1985.
- Rampaging rivers in southwestern New Mexico in winter 1978 were declared disaster areas, requiring Corps assistance.

### Summary of Program Themes

1. Flood and sediment control are the primary purposes of most dams.
2. Recreation is secondary, often operated by the State or local partners.
3. Cost-sharing with local sponsors is common.
4. Visitor numbers are reported for only a few sites over a 17-month period.
