# An Explosion of Green: Forest Recovery and Wildlife Return in the Eastern United States

#forest-ecology #reforestation #wildlife-conservation

## Executive Summary

This April 1995 essay by Bill McKibben argues that the eastern United States is undergoing an unplanned, largely unnoticed reforestation after two centuries of clearing. Forest cover has rebounded across New England, New York and the southern Appalachians, and wildlife such as [[Whitetail Deer]], [[Black Bear]], [[Wild Turkey]], [[Coyote]] and possibly [[Eastern Cougar]] has returned. The recovery is attributed mainly to farm abandonment and economics rather than deliberate conservation, and it is fragile. The author identifies threats from [[Industrial Forestry]], [[Overdevelopment]] and rural economic decline. He proposes large protected wilderness blocks, such as a proposed [[Maine Woods National Park]], and the return of [[Gray Wolf]] populations as models for the region and the wider world.

## Background & Description

The essay opens with two anchor points: the 1587 birth of Virginia Dare on [[Roanoke Island]], and the 1987 release of [[Red Wolf]] pairs into the [[Alligator River National Wildlife Refuge]]. The red wolf had been extinct in the wild and was reintroduced from captive populations. Its wild-born descendants reached 61 puppies by the winter counted, and a third generation was present.

Historical sections trace the forest's collapse and recovery:

- **Pre-European landscape:** Native peoples cleared land and burned some forests, keeping them open and parklike. The disruption was usually temporary.
- **European settlement:** Clearing for pasture, grazing, exhausted corn-planted soils and intensive timber and fuel use reduced forest cover in many eastern areas from 70 percent to 25 percent or less by the late nineteenth century.
- **Agricultural abandonment:** Competition from [[Midwest Agriculture]] and the [[Erie Canal]] (1835) drew farmers and sheep operations west. In Vermont, sheep numbered 1,681,000 in 1840 and were cut roughly in half thirty years later.
- **Recovery:** New Hampshire is now 90 percent forest. Vermont rose from 35 percent woodland in 1850 to 80 percent today. Forest cover in New York grew by more than a million acres per decade through 1980.

The essay also profiles key voices, including [[Peter Dunwiddie]] (pollen and photographic evidence of reforestation on Cape Cod and Martha's Vineyard), [[Steve Trombulak]] (conservation biology and island biogeography), [[Michael Kellett]] (RESTORE, Maine), [[John Harrigan]] (New Hampshire editor and study commissioner) and [[Jamie Sayen]] (Appalachian wilderness advocate).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biodiversity hotspot:** The southern Appalachians, with the Smokies' more than 1,300 flowering plants and about 100 tree types, are described as possibly the most diverse temperate forest in the world. The Blue Ridge area has been free of glacial or marine cover for hundreds of millions of years.

**Megafauna loss:** Scientists debate whether early human arrival caused the extinction of eastern megafauna such as mammoths, mastodons, ground sloths and saber-toothed cats.

**Wildlife recovery:**
- Whitetail deer now exceed 18 million, about forty times the late-1800s count.
- Perhaps 40,000 black bears roam the East.
- American alligators, listed as endangered in 1967, rebounded to about two million within ten years.
- Wild turkeys introduced into western Massachusetts in 1972 now exceed 10,000.
- Coyotes, absent until the 1930s, now occur in nearly every Massachusetts town.
- Reports of eastern cougar (puma) sightings total 1,800 in the past decade, though officials say none are officially confirmed. Vermont officials confirmed scat from at least one mountain lion.

**Threats to recovery:**
- **Acid rain and ozone:** Mount Mitchell's upper forest is dying and skeletal.
- **Invasive pests and pathogens:** Beech-bark blight and woolly adelgid attack beech and hemlock.
- **Clear-cutting:** Salamander catches were five times higher in mature stands than in forests clear-cut less than ten years earlier. Petranka estimates clear-cutting kills about 14 million salamanders annually.
- **Slow recovery:** Wildflowers in the southern Appalachians had not returned even ninety years after cutting.

**Edge and habitat fragmentation:** Small, fragmented habitats are more prone to extinction, and roads kill amphibians during vernal pool migration.

## Key Findings

1. **Unplanned reforestation:** The eastern United States is reforesting after two centuries of clearing. Farm abandonment and economic shifts drove the change, not deliberate policy. Recovery "is an accident of climate, soil, and economics," and is not guaranteed.

2. **Wildlife rebound:** Deer, bears, turkeys, alligators, coyotes and possibly cougars have returned or increased. The author says this reflects habitat recovery and sportsmen's conservation efforts led by [[Theodore Roosevelt]].

3. **Industrial forestry as the major threat:** Clear-cutting in Maine covered nearly 2,000 square miles from 1980 to 1992, roughly the area of Delaware. The author says the cutting in the 1980s proceeded faster, relative to forest size, than rainforest clearing in South America (Kellett's claim). Whole-tree harvesting removes nutrients, and herbicides favor spruce and fir.

4. **Development pressure:** The Sterling Forest proposal (17,500 acres, five planned towns, 13,000 housing units, 8 million square feet of commercial space) exemplifies the threat of [[Suburbanization]]. Second-home land on Moosehead Lake rose from $50,000 (1986) to $250,000 (1988) for a forty-acre lot.

5. **Economic decline in rural areas:** Logging jobs in the Maine woods fell 40 percent from 1984 to 1992, largely due to mechanization. Lumber workers in the southern Appalachians averaged $15,850 a year, about $1,000 below the service-sector average. Scott Paper laid off nearly a third of its workforce.

6. **Proposed remedies:**
   - Protected wilderness blocks, including a three-million-acre [[Maine Woods National Park]] (roughly $200 per acre, funded partly by the [[Land and Water Conservation Fund]]).
   - Corridors between protected areas, and road underpasses or seasonal closures.
   - "Value-added" wood processing and a "sustaining forest" model instead of a "working forest."
   - Ecotourism: tourism and recreation in southern Appalachian national forests contribute $379 million annually, versus $32 million from logging on public land.

7. **Wolf return as indicator:** The wolf is extinct across most of the East. Massachusetts enacted the first New World bounty in 1630. Wolves are now about 5 percent of their original lower-48 range. Minnesota data show that fewer than 1 percent of 7,000 farms within wolf range reported a raid, and wolves cull weak deer.

8. **Global relevance:** The author uses Haiti (forest cover fell from more than 80 percent to under 1 percent this century) and the Philippines to argue that eastern recovery offers hope for other regions. He calls for a "new frontier of recovery."
