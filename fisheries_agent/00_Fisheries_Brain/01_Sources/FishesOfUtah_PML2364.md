# Fishes of Utah: Colorado Chub and Leatherside Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 publication from the Utah State Department of Fish and Game, authored by William F. Sigler and Robert Rush Miller, is a regional fish guide that covers native and introduced fishes of Utah. The fragment provided focuses on two cyprinid minnows of the genus *Gila*: the [[Colorado Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and the [[Leatherside Chub]] (*Gila copei*). It describes their importance, range, morphology, life history, habitat, and a management note on chemical control of unwanted fish. The document closes with an extensive bibliography and a brief note on hybrid suckers.

## Background & Description

The document is a state-agency species account, not a research paper. The text is partly degraded by OCR, and some numerical values are uncertain; they are reported here only where the text is legible.

**Colorado Chub (*Gila robusta*)**
- Common names: Colorado chub, roundtail, bonytail, Verde trout, Gila trout.
- Considered too bony to be of much food value, although large individuals were eaten by Indians, especially along the lower Colorado River.
- Young are important forage fish and serve as sport fish for youngsters in parts of the range; they take a spinner, doughball, or worm readily.
- Two subspecies occur in Utah: the roundtail (*G. r. robusta*) and the bonytail (*G. r. elegans*).
- Morphology: elongate, well-streamlined body with strong fins, small scales, and a narrow caudal peduncle suited to swift currents. Dorsal and anal fin rays number 8 to 11, usually 9 or 10. Lateral line scales number about 75 to 95. Pharyngeal teeth are in two rows with the formula 2,5-4,2.
- Coloration: dusky to greenish on the back, silvery or white below, with fins reddish to red at the base. Breeding males show reddish coloring on the sides of the head and body.
- Size: attains nearly 17 inches total length, though usually much smaller. One specimen weighed 1.2 pounds.

**Leatherside Chub (*Gila copei*)**
- Common names: leatherside chub, leatherside minnow.
- An excellent bait minnow, used for this purpose in Wyoming and on the lower Colorado River from Lake Mead to Yuma.
- Probably utilized as forage by game fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range and distribution**
- The Colorado Chub is native to the Colorado River Basin, from near the river's mouth well up into headwaters in Wyoming and Colorado. Its abundance is decreasing as the basin continues to be modified by human activity.
- The Leatherside Chub is native in Utah only to the eastern and southern parts of the Bonneville Basin, in rivers draining into Great Salt Lake (Bear, Logan, and Weber rivers) and into Utah Lake (Provo River drainage).

**Habitat**
- Adults of both Colorado chub subspecies occupy big rivers and major tributaries, while young occur in adjacent shallower, quieter waters. The species also occurs in lakes in the upper Colorado River system in Wyoming.
- Substrate is typically clay, soft mud, or mud and sand, occasionally rock, gravel, or rubble, with little or no vegetation. Water may be clear or muddy, and greenish-brown in the Green River.
- The species is well adapted to the swift currents of the [[Green River]] and Colorado River.

**Management**
- Partial control of unwanted fish by spot poisoning with rotenone has been practiced for many years. The document states that the only practical method for eliminating large populations is to attempt a total kill, rather than partial treatment.

**Hybridization**
- A short section discusses hybrid suckers (*Catostomus* spp.) in Utah. The hybrid appeared in a drainage where it had become established after the release of bait fishes. Identification depended on meristic and morphological comparisons with presumed parental species (*plty rhynchus*, mountain sucker, and bluehead sucker). The text notes that the two parental species may be established in Strawberry (Utah) waters.

## Key Findings

- **Colorado chub life history:** Spawning occurs in late spring to early summer. Observations include a spawning Colorado chub on May 8 in the Colorado River below Davis Dam, Nevada, and newborn young near Cottonwood Landing, Nevada, on June 15, 1950.
- **Spawning aggregation:** In Lake Mohave, Nevada, in 1954, divers estimated about 500 individuals spawning on a gravelly shelf. Each female was attended by one to five males, and no nest was constructed. Eggs were broadcast and adhered to rocks in water up to 30 feet deep.
- **Specimens from the spawning area:** Forty-two males and 21 females were recovered, ranging from 11 to 14 inches and averaging about 12 inches.
- **Fecundity:** A 12-inch female contained about 10,000 eggs, estimated by the volumetric method. Eggs were about 1/32 inch in diameter and pale white.
- **Diet:** The Colorado chub is omnivorous, feeding largely on insects, crustaceans, and snails, with filamentous algae often present in stomachs.
- **Range contraction:** The Colorado chub is decreasing in abundance due to habitat modification within the Colorado River Basin.
- **Bait value:** The Leatherside Chub is valued as a bait minnow across Wyoming and the lower Colorado River.
- **Control method:** Rotenone spot poisoning has been used, but the document concludes that total kills are the only practical option for eliminating large populations of unwanted fish.
- **Bibliography:** The document includes a general reference list and an extensive list of works cited in the text, spanning from Cope and Yarrow (1875) to Rivers (1962). These cover Utah, Nevada, Colorado, Wyoming, and Pacific Northwest fish faunas, as well as hybridization, life histories, and fish management.
