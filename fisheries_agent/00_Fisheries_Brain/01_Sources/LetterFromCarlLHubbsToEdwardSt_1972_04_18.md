# Letter from Carl L. Hubbs to Edward Steele on Aravaipa Creek Fish Conservation

## Executive Summary
This 1972 letter from ichthyologist [[Carl L. Hubbs]] to conservationist [[Edward Steele]] addresses an emerging threat to the native fish fauna of [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona: the introduction of non-native black bass (largemouth and possibly smallmouth, *[[Micropterus salmoides]]* and *[[Micropterus dolomieu]]*). Hubbs commends Steele's advocacy through [[The Defenders of Wildlife]] and endorses a survey to be conducted by [[W. L. Minckley]]. The letter is a rare primary-source document illustrating mid-20th-century applied conservation reasoning, weighing eradication methods (chemical piscicides, seining, electrofishing, sportfishing, sedatives) against risks to non-target native biota, most notably endemic mollusks. It references the precedent of the [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]] restoration, where piscicide use inadvertently threatened an endemic snail species monitored by [[Dwight Taylor]]. Hubbs advises a cautious, staged approach to protect Aravaipa's endemic fishes, including [[Meda fulgida]] (spikedace) and other native taxa referenced in the text.

## Background & Description
The letter contains no direct morphological descriptions of the fish species discussed, consistent with its nature as correspondence rather than a taxonomic or anatomical study. However, it identifies taxa relevant to morphological and systematic concern:

- **Native taxa at risk** — Referenced as "[[Meda]]" (likely *[[Meda fulgida]]*, the spikedace, a small elongate [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid minnow]] endemic to the Gila River basin) and a second native form, tentatively *[[Poeciliopsis occidentalis]]*, the Gila topminnow, a small live-bearing poeciliid.
- **Introduced predators** — *[[Micropterus salmoides]]* (largemouth bass) and possibly *[[Micropterus dolomieu]]* (smallmouth bass), both centrarchid piscivores markedly larger and more predatory than the small-bodied native cyprinids and poeciliids of Aravaipa Creek.
- **Taxonomic uncertainty** — Hubbs explicitly notes there is no confirmed evidence of distinct races or subspecies among Aravaipa Creek's endemic fishes, though he raises the possibility of "slight physiological" differentiation that could render translocated stock poorly adapted to the creek if restocking became necessary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type**: [[Aravaipa Creek]] is described as a natural desert stream area targeted for formal protection, supporting a distinct endemic fish assemblage within the arid Southwestern U.S. ([[Gila River Basin]] drainage).
- **Native community**: Small-bodied endemic cyprinids and poeciliids adapted to permanently flowing desert stream conditions, implicitly vulnerable to introduced piscivorous competitors/predators.
- **Invasive pressure**: Introduction of *[[Micropterus salmoides]]* into an adjoining pond, with concern that individuals have "worked into the stream" — raising the specter of centrarchid establishment altering trophic structure in a historically bass-free system.
- **Non-target biota**: The letter draws an explicit ecological parallel to the [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]] and the [[Fish Slough]] area, where broad-spectrum piscicide treatment intended to remove introduced [[largemouth bass]] and [[Cyprinus carpio|carp]] possibly extirpated a locally endemic snail species — underscoring that stream rehabilitation actions affect the entire biotic community, not fish alone.
- **Management framing**: Hubbs frames the situation as a systems-level conservation problem, advocating protection of "the entire habitat and biota," not fish in isolation — an early articulation of ecosystem-based conservation thinking applied to desert stream restoration.

## Key Findings
- **Threat identification**: Introduction of non-native black bass into Aravaipa Creek is flagged as "definitely disquieting," with smallmouth bass establishment considered especially concerning due to greater invasiveness/predation risk relative to largemouth bass.
- **Tiered management strategy proposed**:
  1. Eliminate largemouth bass established in an isolated pond — low-risk, straightforward action.
  2. For bass that may have entered the main stream, delay action and monitor to see if they establish, since stream conditions may prevent naturalization.
  3. Reserve broad chemical piscicide treatment as a *last resort*, given demonstrated collateral risk to non-target endemic invertebrates.
- **Alternative control methods enumerated**: seining, intensified sportfishing (angling pressure), electrofishing ("electric shocker"), and non-lethal chemical sedation using [[quinaldine]] (a temporary fish anesthetic) — alone or in combination — proposed as lower-impact alternatives to broad-spectrum poisoning.
- **Precautionary case study**: The Owens Valley/[[Fish Slough]] incident is cited as a cautionary precedent — heavy piscicide use to remove introduced largemouth bass and carp possibly caused unintended extirpation of an endemic snail species (per concerns raised by malacologist [[Dwight Taylor]]), illustrating collateral ecological risk of chemical eradication.
- **Ark/refugia recommendation**: If drastic in-stream treatment becomes necessary, Hubbs recommends first trapping/seining substantial populations of the vulnerable endemics into a side channel or holding container as a living refuge population during treatment — an early application of the conservation "ark" concept.
- **Reintroduction/genetic caution**: Post-treatment restocking should prioritize native Aravaipa Creek stock; Hubbs cautions that even absent confirmed subspecies-level differentiation, subtle local physiological adaptation could make non-native-source fish poorly suited to reintroduction into Aravaipa Creek.
- **Institutional collaboration**: The letter documents an active, multi-expert conservation network — [[Carl L. Hubbs]], [[W. L. Minckley]] (field survey lead), and [[Robert Rush Miller]] (consulted authority) — coordinating around establishment of the Aravaipa Creek natural area under advocacy from [[Edward Steele]] and [[The Defenders of Wildlife]].
- **Cost-benefit note**: Precautionary measures (holding refugia, staged monitoring) are characterized as a minor cost relative to the overall project, with Hubbs urging against risk-taking in irreversible conservation decisions.
