# Upper Campbell Blue Allotment Grazing Permit Draft Environmental Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Draft Environmental Assessment (EA), issued by the [[Alpine Ranger District]] of the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]] in September 1995, evaluates whether to issue a 10-year cattle grazing permit to Dan Heap on the Upper Campbell Blue Allotment. The document is a public-comment package, not a field study. It compares three alternatives under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] and examines effects on soils, vegetation, riparian areas, water and air quality, threatened and sensitive wildlife, economics, social values, and cultural resources.

The agency's preferred option is [[Alternative C]], which balances permitted livestock with allotment capacity. Current permitted use (245 cow/calf pairs) exceeds estimated capacity. Riparian areas are about 90% in unsatisfactory condition, and the aquatic habitat of [[Apache Trout]] and [[Loach Minnow]] is directly affected by grazing. Fish-related conclusions drive much of the analysis.

## Background & Description

- **Project:** Issuance of a 10-year term grazing permit for the Upper Campbell Blue Allotment, covering roughly 20,000 acres in T.4–5N., R.29–31E. (Gila and Salt River Meridian). Typical use runs June through October under a deferred grazing system.
- **Current permit:** Held by Dan Heap (#01359), who has been a permittee on this allotment since the early 1970s. The current permit expires December 31, 1995.
- **Proposed action:** The 1995 scoping proposal (245 cattle, June 1 to October 31) was modified to add mitigation for Apache trout and Loach minnow. The modified version is Alternative B.
- **Decision maker:** The Forest Supervisor decides on permit issuance. A later separate allotment management plan analysis would address management systems, range facilities, and stocking levels.
- **Public involvement:** A notice (published September 12, 1995, in the *White Mountain Independent*) announced a 30-day comment period ending October 12, 1995. Eight allotments were noticed as part of the wider permit set, and 20 responses were received from about 71 letters sent during scoping. Twenty-three individuals or groups ultimately responded to contacts with 24 permittees and 12 individuals.
- **Significant issues:**
  1. Permitted livestock use should be in balance with grazing capacity.
  2. Grazing season should match site-specific conditions, since early summer grazing overuses small areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Soils and watershed.** Using the Universal Soil Loss Equation from the Terrestrial Ecosystems Survey, about 94% of the allotment's soils are in satisfactory condition and capable of withstanding grazing. About 6% are unsatisfactory, and none are inherently unable to withstand grazing. Projected satisfactory condition after 10 years is 100% under Alternative A, 95% under Alternative B, and 100% under Alternative C. Coleman Creek's watershed is in satisfactory condition. Of its roughly 3,210 acres in the allotment, about 2,950 acres are satisfactory.

**Vegetation.** Late spring to early summer grazing concentrates use in riparian zones and meadows. Over time this has shifted composition from sedges and native cool-season grasses toward [[Kentucky Bluegrass]], rushes, and forbs. Stocking has been based on total forage across the allotment rather than usable acreage. Capacity classes show about 15,491 acres of full capacity, 796 acres of potential capacity, and 3,709 acres of no capacity. Forage production estimates on ponderosa pine sites are 80 to 148 lb/acre currently, against a potential of 75 to 275 lb/acre.

**Riparian areas.** About 13 miles of drainages (Campbell Blue Creek, Coleman Creek, East Fork of the Black River) were assessed. Roughly 90% of the riparian habitat is unsatisfactory. Under Alternative A, riparian areas would be expected to recover within 10 years, though not fully. Alternatives B and C would improve conditions gradually, with Alternative C improving more rapidly.

**Water quality.** The Arizona Department of Environmental Quality rates Campbell Blue Creek as "partially limited," with pH and dissolved oxygen as limiting factors. Turbidity affects the downstream Blue River. Natural conditions contribute to limited water quality, and grazing-related hoof action increases sediment. Air quality is in attainment in the Alpine District, and none of the alternatives would worsen it.

**Threatened, endangered, and sensitive species.** Seven federally listed species may occur in the action area:
- Endangered: Mexican gray wolf (extirpated, not present), American Peregrine Falcon
- Threatened: Bald Eagle, [[Mexican Spotted Owl]], Apache trout, Loach minnow
- Proposed endangered: Jaguar (transient only)

Coleman Creek is essential for Apache trout recovery and is in an exclosure. Loach minnow critical habitat lies several miles downstream of the allotment, and grazing-driven sediment could affect it. The ASNF Biological Assessment considered 35 sensitive species. Of these, 29 were not expected to trend toward listing. Six species were flagged as possibly affected if grazing exceeds certain levels: [[Northern Water Shrew]], [[Northern Goshawk]], [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]], [[Northern Leopard Frog]], [[Narrowheaded Garter Snake]], and [[Roundtail Chub]]. The text says "5 species" but lists six.

**Economics.** Alternative B supports an estimated 8.4 direct and indirect jobs across the three permits in Apache County and about $1,673 in payments to counties. Cumulative jobs under Alternative C would be about 5.4. Those 8.4 jobs equal 3.8% of the 220 jobs the county study estimates for cattle ranching. Apache County received $285,497 in Forest Service receipts in 1994, and these three permits contributed 0.5% of that. Because stocking exceeds capacity, Alternative B would likely yield a shortened season and reduced payments. Actual use has averaged 70% of permitted use over the past 10 years.

**Social and recreation.** Ranching is a long-standing family-based way of life in the region. Dispersed recreation (camping, hiking, hunting, fishing, mountain biking, and skiing) is common, and the Aspen and Diamond Rock campgrounds lie within the allotment. In 1994, 2,895 people visited Aspen and 3,481 visited the Diamond Rock campgrounds. Some recreationists object to livestock in riparian areas. Alternative A would remove the conflict but disrupt ranching families. Alternative C would reduce the number of livestock recreationists encounter.

**Cultural resources.** No known standing ruins or historic structures are affected. Issuing the permit would have no effect on properties listed on or eligible for the [[National Register of Historic Places]]. Future range structures would still require Section 106 consultation.

## Key Findings

1. **Stocking exceeds capacity under current use.** Alternative B keeps 245 cattle, which the analysis says exceeds allotment capacity, so livestock would reach utilization limits and be moved early in many years.
2. **Alternative C is preferred.** Balancing stocking with capacity (189–190 cattle, July 15 to October 31) and applying utilization limits is expected to best protect soil, riparian areas, and downstream water quality, though it reduces the permit's economic value.
3. **Utilization standards drive protection.** Limits are 45% in riparian areas, 40% in uplands, 20% average (40% maximum) on grasses and forbs in goshawk territories, and 20–40% in Mexican spotted owl critical habitat. Monitoring uses paired exclosure plots and cages in key areas within 1/2 mile of water.
4. **Apache trout are protected.** Coleman Creek (about 1 mile through the allotment) is fenced. Cattle are kept about 1/2 mile from the creek, and a fence would be built if the natural barrier fails.
5. **Loach minnow habitat is indirectly at risk.** The species does not occur in the allotment, but sediment from trampling under Alternative B could reach its critical habitat downstream. Alternative C would reduce that risk.
6. **No alternative is likely to jeopardize listed species.** The EA concludes that Alternatives B and C are not likely to adversely affect bald eagles, peregrine falcons, or Mexican spotted owls, and that Alternative B is not likely to adversely affect Apache trout. Alternative A would provide the greatest long-term habitat benefit.
7. **Economic trade-off.** The no-action alternative would eliminate roughly 8.4 jobs and related county payments, and reduced stocking under Alternative C would cut payments and may affect operation viability.
8. **Comment-period outcome.** The EA states that comments not raising significant NEPA issues were not carried into the decision. A final decision was to follow the comment period ending October 12, 1995.
