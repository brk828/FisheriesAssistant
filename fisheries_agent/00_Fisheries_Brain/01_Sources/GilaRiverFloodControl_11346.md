# Gila River Flood Control in Graham County, Arizona (Senate Document No. 436, 1919)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-ecosystem-management

## Executive Summary

This 1919 U.S. Senate document transmits a report by engineer [[Frank H. Olmsted]] to Secretary of the Interior [[Franklin K. Lane]], produced under the Indian Appropriation Act of May 18, 1916. The report sought a practical design to stop the [[Gila River]] from eroding banks and overflowing in Graham County, Arizona, and to confine the stream to a fixed channel. Its recommendations combine a continuous pile-and-cable bank-protection structure in the valley reach with a large-scale upper-watershed program of check dams, reforestation, grazing control, drift retention and reservoir evaluation. The document is primarily an engineering, hydrological and land-use study; it contains no fish or species-level biological data. Aquatic content is limited to general observations on stream sediment, flow and channel conditions.

## Background & Description

- **Authority and scope:** Work was done under a contract dated August 26, 1916, with a $10,000 appropriation. The report is in two parts: Part I covers Graham County river conditions and protection plans; Part II covers the whole [[Gila River Watershed]] above San Carlos.
- **Study area:** Graham County lies on the middle Gila, reaching from San Carlos to above Solomonville, roughly 66.6 miles of channel. The Gila drains 12,020 square miles above San Carlos and is described as the muddiest river in the world (attributed to [[R. H. Forbes]], University of Arizona).
- **Agricultural land:** About 35,000 irrigable valley acres plus 5,000 acres of bench land; 27,600 acres were irrigated at the time, mostly in the [[Safford Valley]] (26,600 acres) with 1,000 acres on the San Carlos Indian Reservation.
- **Population:** The Safford Valley population was 6,950, including 480 Indians below the reservation line.
- **History:** The report summarizes early Spanish exploration, [[Hohokam Culture|prehistoric irrigation]] remnants, Apache occupation, and the Gadsden Purchase (1854). Early floods were recorded in 1833, 1869 and 1884 through Pima calendars and oral accounts.
- **Recent floods:** Documented floods occurred in January 1916 and October 1916. Between October 1915 and September 1916, 2,145 acres were washed away (1,155 acres in Safford Valley; 990 acres on San Carlos Reservation).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Channel and erosion**
- Channel width: 150–200 feet in earlier times, now averaging 1,935 feet. In 1875, a township survey measured an average width of 138.6 feet and an area of 103.57 acres; that length now covers 1,503 acres.
- Channel evaporation on the reach from San Carlos to the East and West Forks (214 miles) was estimated at 90 cubic feet per second, or 0.42 cubic foot per second per linear mile.
- The [[San Simon Valley]] tributary has gullies 500–800 feet wide and 10–25 feet deep along 60 miles.

**Sediment and silt**
- October 1916 flood silt samples ranged from 0.08% (day 6) to 3.65% by weight. Peak silt was about 48 hours after crest (roughly 3.3–3.5% by volume). A single sample at Cliff showed 4% silt volume after 24-hour settlement.
- Investigators concluded that flood flows, not normal flow, carry most of the silt, largely from the high mountain area.

**Hydrology and rainfall**
- Rainfall rises with elevation: about 5 inches on the lower Gila, roughly 12 inches at 3,000 ft, over 20 inches above 7,000 ft.
- Average annual run-off above San Carlos (1895–1915) was 397,562 acre-feet, about 4.3% of the 9,327,000 acre-feet precipitated.
- Fort Apache data show that the 1904–1916 period had a mean annual rainfall of 18.23 inches, versus 15.74 inches (1892–1903) and 21.58 inches (1880–1891). The author argues that watershed condition, not rainfall alone, now controls flood peaks.

**Drainage tributaries**
- The [[San Francisco River]] drains 2,895 square miles; the October 1916 crest at Clifton was 107,870 cubic feet per second. The January 1916 storm was judged the most disastrous flood in the lower Gila.
- The [[Blue River]] drains 605 square miles, is normally clear and free of silt, and had a peak of 31,900 cubic feet per second in October 1916.
- The [[East Fork of the Gila River]] drains 1,137.6 square miles with a peak of 16,800 cubic feet per second (14.8 cubic feet per second per square mile); it is considered a comparatively minor flood feeder.

**Vegetation and land cover**
- About 58% of the 2,535-square-mile woodland area carries commercial timber. Dominant species include western yellow pine, Douglas fir, white fir and pinyon-juniper.
- Overgrazing by cattle, sheep and goats is identified as a major cause of sod loss and gully erosion.

**Aquatic biota**
- The document contains no fish, invertebrate, plankton or aquatic-species data. Its aquatic relevance lies in sediment load, flow regime and channel form.

## Key Findings

1. **Bank protection design (Type A):** Reinforced concrete pilings at 60-foot spacing, linked by cable or boom lines, with wooden log panels (8×8 inch Oregon pine uprights) on the riverbed. The design aims to slow the flow so that silt precipitates. Estimated cost: $2.92 per linear foot of bank protection, plus $0.10 per cubic yard of channel excavation.
2. **Protection extent:** 371,850 linear feet of Type A bank protection across Sections A–P (mile 0 to 66.6), with a uniform channel width of 800 feet. The works were estimated at $1,109,242.
3. **Cross-stream control:** Tributary works totaling $293,250 (north- and south-bank feeders such as Salt Creek at $30,000 and Bylas Creek at $20,000).
4. **Total protection program:** $1,402,492 including 15% contingency and overhead. This equals about 28% of the estimated $5,000,000 in direct benefits.
5. **Benefit estimate:** About 32,855 acres of threatened land at $125 per acre gave $4,106,900, plus $200,000 for bench lands, for a total of about $4,306,900 (up to $5,000,000 with valley improvements).
6. **Rejected alternatives:** [[Rock Levee]]s (about $20 per running foot, roughly $7,397,000 total), [[Brush Mattress]] work (requires distant brush), and [[Dean Deflector]]s (26 installed at $331 each, but over half of those on the Graham County front had been lost downstream).
7. **Upper-watershed program:** A series of recommended measures: forest-ground improvement in the Gila, Datil, Apache, Gila and Crook National Forests; a systematic check-dam network from the headwaters down; and cross structures (types B and D) in the larger tributaries.
8. **Check dams:** Check dam costs were estimated at about $1 per vertical foot in Los Angeles. A total of about $2,127,515 was proposed for the San Francisco system, with a 15% contingency bringing the total to about $2.4 million.
9. **Peak-flow reduction:** A model of Chase Creek (28 square miles) predicted a 28% reduction in peak flow at Clifton from widespread check-dam use.
10. **Reservoirs:** The [[San Carlos Reservoir]] (about 700,000 acre-feet at over $5,000,000) was judged the most attractive storage option. Six other sites were evaluated (for example, Two Forks, Alma and Black Canyon). The author advised against most of them unless the watershed was first treated.
11. **Reforestation:** Fort Bayard trials supported planting western yellow pine, particularly on drainage lines where moisture is higher.
12. **Flood canals:** A proposed Lordsburg flood-relief canal was dismissed as too costly and legally unsupported at the time.
13. **Historical comparison:** The report cites European torrent-control programs (France, Switzerland, Austria, Germany) and an analysis of China's Yellow River as models for upstream retardation over downstream levees.
14. **Policy stance:** The author argues for a coordinated, system-wide approach to Gila management, not piecemeal local works, and emphasizes obligations to the Pima Indians, whose irrigation water had been appropriated upstream.
15. **Data limitations:** Flood discharge estimates may be 20% off, and crest measurements have a 10–15% accuracy limit. The author describes the work as tentative and recommends monitoring.
