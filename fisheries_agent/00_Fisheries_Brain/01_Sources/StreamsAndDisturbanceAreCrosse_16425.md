# Streams and Disturbance: Are Cross-Ecosystem Comparisons Useful?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #stream-ecology #disturbance-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[Stuart G. Fisher]] and [[Nancy B. Grimm]] (Arizona State University), prepared for the third Cary Conference in Ecosystem Science, asks whether comparing ecosystems is a useful way to understand [[Disturbance]] as a driver of ecosystem structure and function. It uses running-water systems as the test case, weighing comparisons of single streams, similar streams, very different streams, and streams versus other ecosystems such as [[Forest Ecosystems]]. The authors conclude that comparison is valuable at every level but involves a trade-off: broadly comparable hypotheses sacrifice mechanistic detail, while comparisons among similar streams yield more specific results. They recommend that regional studies are the most profitable approach at the current stage of the science.

No fish data are reported; the document addresses the broader stream ecosystem, including algae, invertebrates, detritus and nutrients.

## Background & Description

The authors frame [[Disturbance]] as rapid change causing substantial mortality, shifts in abiotic resource distribution, and disruption of ecosystems. They define it as a punctuated killing that alters the ratio of organisms to resources, and stability as the combination of [[Resistance]] (capacity to avoid change) and [[Resilience]] (rapid recovery after disturbance).

Stream ecology has emphasized openness to material exchange and up- and downstream linkage. Key concepts include:

- [[River Continuum Concept]] (Vannote et al. 1980), a comparative framework built on stream order within a single drainage, which was tested mainly in temperate North American streams.
- [[Nutrient Spiralling]] (Newbold et al. 1981), combining in situ cycling and downstream transport.
- Leaf litter processing as the energy base of wooded streams.

Table 1 lists factors to which stream differences have been attributed, including flow regime, permanence, temperature, stream order, water chemistry, light, terrestrial vegetation, organic matter supply, catchment history and land use, and biotic interactions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Disturbance types.** Disturbances may be autogenic (internal, mostly biological, smaller in space and time) or allogenic (external, usually abiotic). Table 2 lists about 20 disturbance types with spatial and temporal scales, from invertebrate grazing and crayfish burrows to flash floods, fire, drought-related stream drying and regional floods. Spatial and temporal scales of disturbance are positively correlated, and disturbances interact (for example, flash floods behave differently depending on time since a fire).

**Forests versus streams.** Table 3 contrasts the two systems. Forests are dominated by large, long-lived plants, have closed nutrient cycling, low edge:area ratios and vertical transport linkages, and are structured biotically. Streams have high surface-to-volume ratios, open nutrient cycling, unidirectional and longitudinal transport, and are structured abiotically by substrate, slope and riparian forest.

**Stream type diversity.** The authors note that streams drain nearly every terrestrial ecosystem type and have been studied in hot and cold deserts, chaparral, tropical forest, grassland and arctic tundra. Comparisons among these have largely been unplanned and anecdotal.

**Large rivers.** Large rivers (for example, the Mississippi, Nile, Ob and Amazon) are poorly replicated and geologically unique, which limits their usefulness for building general constructs. Millions of river miles of small-order streams in North America are considered a better basis for comparative analysis.

## Key Findings

1. **Comparison is useful at every level, with trade-offs.** Comparing disparate systems (for example, forests and streams) generates broad concepts, such as [[Steele 1985]]'s comparison of periodic phenomena in ocean and land. Comparing similar systems yields more specific, testable results. Generality increases as reliability of tests decreases (Figure 5).

2. **Hypotheses should be organized around the disturbance, not the ecosystem.** Analogous disturbances across systems are proposed, for example fire versus flood and cyclone versus drought (Table 4). Examples: a disturbance that mobilizes nutrients and removes organic matter is followed by autotrophic succession; resilience is independent of mortality but sensitive to available nutrients.

3. **Resistance is highly variable.** Table 5 shows reductions in standing crop ranging from 0% to 100% across studies, depending on disturbance magnitude, taxon, life history stage and habitat harshness. Size does not guarantee protection: small immature insects were less resistant to flash floods than larger ones (Gray 1981), while microorganisms were sometimes unaffected (Goulder 1986). Structurally complex streams allow incorporation of disturbance through refuges such as the hyporheic zone and floodplain pools.

4. **Sycamore Creek illustrates threshold responses and life history trade-offs.** Algal resistance to flash floods shows a threshold response, varying nearly 4-fold across floods (27 disturbances). Mayflies and chironomids showed high flood mortality, while adult beetles and hemipterans were scarcely affected. Several beetle species time larval development to avoid flood-prone periods. Table 6 lists five life history tactics, with three highly successful in this stream.

5. **Resilience scales with organism size but lacks a common currency.** Recovery time is roughly related to organism size (Table 7). Options for measuring resilience include absolute time, number of generations, percent of dominant species' life span, percent of disturbance return interval, and initial rate of increase (Table 8). No agreement exists, which hinders comparison.

6. **Flashy versus stable streams show contrasting controls.** Table 9 compares Sycamore Creek (Arizona, 424 km², 5.5 floods per year, mean interflood period 67 days) with Fort River (Massachusetts, 105 km², 30 floods per year, mean interflood period 12 days). Multiple regression (Table 10) explained about 68% of variation in chlorophyll a in Sycamore Creek, with days since flood and magnitude of the last flood as the best predictors. In Fort River, light was the only significant variable, explaining little variation. The Sycamore Creek invertebrate model explained only 26% of variance.

7. **Nitrogen links disturbance and biotic control in Sycamore Creek.** Invertebrate resilience was indirectly related to inorganic nitrogen availability (Figure 2; r = 0.74, n = 51). When nitrogen is low, biotic competition dominates; when nitrogen is ample, time since disturbance predicts population density. Algal chlorophyll was not predicted by nitrogen, because late-succession Cyanobacteria increase when nitrogen is low.

8. **A conceptual model links disturbance regime to control mechanism (Figure 4).** Frequent floods make time since disturbance dominant. Infrequent floods shift control toward biotic interactions. Rare floods lead to morphometric constraints and eventual phase shifts during drying. The authors argue that the disturbance-versus-biotic dichotomy is probably false, as both can operate simultaneously or sequentially.

9. **Recommendation.** The authors propose that hypotheses be tested first in similar streams before broader application, and that regional studies are currently the most profitable approach. Comparison is framed as an optimization problem, balancing generality against the adequacy of controls, which is summarized in Figure 5.
