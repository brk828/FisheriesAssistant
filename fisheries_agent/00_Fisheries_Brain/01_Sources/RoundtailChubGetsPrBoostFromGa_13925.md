## Executive Summary

This 1992-era newspaper article, written by an outdoor editor, reports on an Arizona Game and Fish Commission public meeting in which the [[Roundtail Chub]] was declared an official game fish — only the second native Arizona species to receive this designation, following the [[Apache Trout]]. The piece frames this administrative action as a public-relations and conservation strategy intended to elevate the profile of native fish species, contrasted against popular non-native gamefish like [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Channel Catfish]]. The article also references a native-fish reintroduction plan for [[Lake Havasu]] and summarizes results from an angler survey on license purchases and species preferences.

## Background & Description

- The **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*) is a fish native to Arizona, colloquially nicknamed the "Verde trout" due to a superficial resemblance to trout.
  - Morphological notes: trout-like body shape; small head with trout-like markings; white flesh; more bones than typical sport trout.
  - Family: [[Cyprinidae]] (minnow family), distinct from true trout (Salmonidae).
- The **[[Razorback Sucker]]** is also mentioned as a protected native Arizona fish, notable for a distinctive sharp dorsal keel ("razorback") behind the head. The article notes this species is rare, with populations described as declining.
- The **[[Apache Trout]]** is cited as the only other native Arizona species previously classified as a game fish, establishing a precedent for the Roundtail Chub's new designation.
- The **[[Colorado River Squawfish]]** (now more commonly referenced taxonomically as the Colorado Pikeminnow) is mentioned as a large-bodied native species that could be considered for future game fish status if conservation numbers improve.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat/Range**: The species discussed are native to Arizona waterways within the [[Colorado River Basin]], including river systems such as the Verde River (implied by the "Verde trout" nickname) and man-made reservoirs like [[Lake Havasu]].
- **Conservation Management Action**: The Arizona Game and Fish Commission approved a plan to manage coves on [[Lake Havasu]] for native species restoration:
  - Cove mouths would be sealed off using nets.
  - Non-native/existing fish within sealed coves would be eradicated using chemical treatment (piscicide).
  - Native fish species would then be stocked into the treated coves.
  - Native fish would be allowed to grow to a target size before nets are removed and the cove reopened to the connected water body.
- **Competitive Pressure from Non-Natives**: The article implicitly frames native species (Roundtail Chub, Razorback Sucker, Colorado River Squawfish) as being ecologically and culturally overshadowed by popular introduced sport fish ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Crappie]]).
- **Regulatory Rationale**: Elevating native species to "game fish" status is portrayed as a mechanism to increase public engagement, angler attention, and protective regulation for imperiled native taxa.

## Key Findings

- **Regulatory Milestone**: The [[Roundtail Chub]] was declared a game fish by the Arizona Game and Fish Commission, becoming the second native Arizona fish (after the [[Apache Trout]]) to hold this status.
- **Conservation Tool**: A cove-isolation and chemical treatment protocol was approved for [[Lake Havasu]] to facilitate reintroduction of native fish species by removing existing (likely non-native) fish populations before restocking.
- **Angler Survey Data** (survey of 8,000 licensed anglers from 1992; ~2,200 respondents):
  - 80% of respondents indicated they had purchased or intended to purchase a 1993 fishing license.
  - 5% would not renew, citing license price and poor fishing quality as primary reasons.
  - Arizona's large inland reservoirs accounted for the most angler use, at nearly 2 million user-days annually.
  - Gear preference: 67% used both bait and lures; 15% used bait only; 18% used flies/lures only.
  - Catch-and-keep behavior: 51% said keeping at least some fish was necessary for a "successful" trip; 44% did not feel the need to keep fish.
  - **Species popularity ranking** among surveyed anglers:
    1. [[Trout]] — ~38%
    2. [[Largemouth Bass]] — ~32%
    3. [[Channel Catfish]] — ~8%
    4. [[Striped Bass]] — 4th
    5. [[Crappie]] — 5th
