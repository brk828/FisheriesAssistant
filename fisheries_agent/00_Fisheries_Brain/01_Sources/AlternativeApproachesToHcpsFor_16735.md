# Alternative Approaches to HCP's for Aquatic Species - The Upper Colorado River and Central Platte Recovery Programs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #acipenseridae #endangered-species #water-law #habitat-conservation

## Executive Summary

This conference presentation examines alternatives to [[Habitat Conservation Plan]]s (HCPs) for protecting aquatic endangered species, focusing on two major recovery programs: the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]] and the [[Central Platte River Recovery Program]]. The document argues that traditional HCPs under Section 10(a) of the [[Endangered Species Act]] may be inadequate for aquatic species affected by water development across broad geographic ranges. Instead, programmatic "Recovery Programs" that address Section 7 consultation requirements while pursuing species recovery goals may provide superior regulatory certainty for water users. The [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]] targets four listed fish species ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]]), while the [[Central Platte River Recovery Program]] primarily addresses three listed bird species ([[Whooping Crane]], [[Interior Least Tern]], [[Piping Plover]]) and one fish species ([[Pallid Sturgeon]]).

## Background & Description

### Listed Species and Critical Habitat

**Upper Colorado River Basin:**
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (listed 1967)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (listed 1967)
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (listed 1980)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (listed 1991)
- Critical habitat designated 1994 throughout much of the [[Colorado River Basin]]

**Platte River Basin:**
- [[Whooping Crane]] (federally listed)
- [[Interior Least Tern]] (federally listed)
- [[Piping Plover]] (federally listed)
- [[Pallid Sturgeon]] (federally listed, occupies Lower Platte River between [[Elkhorn River]] and [[Missouri River]] confluence)
- 59 miles of [[Central Platte River]] designated as critical habitat for [[Whooping Crane]]

### Legal and Regulatory Framework

The document discusses the relationship between Section 7(a) and Section 10 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Unlike land development projects with limited federal nexus, water development typically involves ongoing federal connections through project operations. Key concerns include:

- Whether HCPs can effectively limit federal agency obligations under Section 7(a)(1)
- The challenge of addressing impacts from activities lacking federal nexus
- The difficulty of proving "take" under Section 9 for water depletions occurring hundreds of miles upstream
- Uncertainty regarding "no surprises" assurances under HCPs

### Upper Colorado River Recovery Program Structure

Created in 1988 through cooperative agreement between:
- Secretary of the Interior
- Administrator of [[Western Area Power Administration]]
- Governors of Colorado, Wyoming, and Utah

Participants include: [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Colorado River Energy Distributors Association]], [[Colorado Water Congress]], [[Utah Water Users Association]], [[Wyoming Water Development Association]], [[National Audubon Society]], [[Environmental Defense Fund]], [[The Nature Conservancy]], [[Colorado Wildlife Federation]], [[Wyoming Wildlife Federation]], and individual water users and districts.

The HCP concept was considered but rejected due to:
- Concern that HCPs did not address Section 7(a)(2) consultation issues
- Reluctance to consider water depletions as "take" under Section 9
- Need to recover species rather than merely avoid jeopardy

### Central Platte River Recovery Program Development

Historical context:
- 1970s: Section 7 began impacting water development throughout three-state basin
- [[Grayrocks Dam Project]] (Wyoming): Construction enjoined in late 1970s, ultimately allowed via ESA exemption process with $7 million trust fund for habitat acquisition
- [[Narrows Project]] (Colorado): Received jeopardy biological opinion in 1983 requiring release of nearly one-third of project yield

1990s developments:
- Federal government extended ESA application to existing projects requiring federal approvals
- Colorado: Cities of Boulder, Fort Collins, Greeley, Loveland, and utilities sought special use permit renewals from [[U.S. Forest Service]] for projects in [[Arapaho National Forest]] and [[Roosevelt National Forest]]
- Wyoming: [[Bureau of Reclamation]] operations at Seminoe, Pathfinder, Alcova, Glendo, and Guernsey reservoirs required Section 7 consultation
- Nebraska: [[Lake McConaughy]] hydropower and irrigation operations underwent [[Federal Energy Regulatory Commission]] relicensing

June 10, 1994: Memorandum of Agreement executed by Governors of Wyoming, Colorado, Nebraska and Secretary of Department of Interior to develop recovery implementation program.

July 1, 1997: "Cooperative Agreement for Platte River Research and Other Efforts Related to Endangered Species Habitats along the Central Platte River, Nebraska" executed to implement interim activities pending NEPA and ESA review.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Upper Colorado River Basin

The [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]] goal: "recover the endangered fish while providing for water development to continue and proceed in the Upper Colorado River Basin in accordance with interstate compacts and federal and state law."

Major program elements:
- **Habitat management**: Instream flow protection under state law
- **Habitat development and maintenance**: Restoration of floodplain habitat and fish refugia
- **Population augmentation**: Stocking programs
- **Non-native species management**
- **Research, monitoring, and data management**

Funding: Approximately $50 million spent to date; current projections call for additional $120 million for Upper Colorado and [[San Juan Recovery Program]]s.

The program structure includes:
- Implementation Committee
- Management Committee
- Technical committees (Biology, Water Acquisition, Information and Education)

Key documents:
- 1988 MOA
- Recovery Implementation Program (RIP)
- Section 7 Agreement
- Recovery Implementation Program Recovery Action Plan (RIPRAP)
- Enforcement Agreement between [[Fish & Wildlife Service]] and [[Colorado Water Conservation Board]]

### Platte River Basin

Target species primarily birds, not fish, using the river and adjacent wet meadow and sand pit habitat seasonally for roosting, nesting, and feeding.

Hydrological connectivity: The 59-mile critical habitat reach in Central Nebraska is hydrologically connected to [[South Platte River]] and [[North Platte River]] many miles upstream, making individual water use activities throughout the Colorado-Wyoming-Nebraska basin subject to ESA Section 7 scrutiny.

### Water Development Challenges

The document identifies fundamental challenges with applying HCP concepts to aquatic species:

1. **Geographic scale**: Water depletions hundreds of miles upstream affect downstream critical habitat
2. **Federal nexus gaps**: Substantial depletions result from activities without federal nexus, making comprehensive HCPs impossible
3. **Causation difficulties**: Tremendous distances and innumerable factors make proving "take" under Section 9 very difficult
4. **Interstate water allocation**: [[1922 Colorado River Compact]] and [[1948 Upper Colorado River Compact]] allocate water among states, complicating mitigation strategies

## Key Findings

### Upper Colorado River Recovery Program Implementation

**Original consultation framework:**
- Program intended as reasonable and prudent alternative for Section 7 consultations
- Existing depletions covered without additional contributions
- New depletions covered by one-time charge of $10 per acre foot

**1990s modifications:**
- 1990: [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] determined "sufficient progress" not made toward legal protection of instream flows
- Service began imposing project-specific requirements beyond $10/acre foot depletion fee
- Mid-1990s: Draft Biological Opinion for [[Ute Pipeline Project]] suggested additional project-specific measures for existing depletions
- Water users viewed this as significant departure from programmatic approach principles

**Current status:**
- USFWS proposed biological opinion on Recovery Program itself (ongoing, outcome uncertain)
- Past 8 months: Meetings to discuss scope of biological opinion, including:
  - Geographic reach and depletions to be addressed
  - Assignment of reasonable and prudent alternatives to existing vs. future depletions
  - "Reopener" provisions applicable to biological opinion

### Central Platte River Recovery Program Structure

**Proposed Program Elements (First Increment):**

1. **Term**: 10-13 years following 3-year Cooperative Agreement term; specific duration depends on water conservation study results

2. **Resource Goals**:
   - **Water**: Improve occurrence of USFWS target flows by average 130,000-150,000 acre feet per year at Grand Island, Nebraska
     - 70,000 acre feet from proposed water projects within respective states
     - 60,000+ acre feet from yet-to-be-defined water supply/conservation activities
   - **Land**: Protection and restoration of at least 10,000 acres of habitat in Central Platte area between Lexington and Chapman, Nebraska

3. **Contributions** (50/50 Federal/State split):
   - Federal Government: $37.5 million cash
   - Colorado: $15 million (cash and cash equivalent)
   - Nebraska: $15 million (cash and cash equivalent)
   - Wyoming: $7.5 million (cash and cash equivalent)

4. **Future Depletions**: Each state monitors and reports effects of new water activities; impacts mitigated pursuant to respective state proposals if Program implemented

5. **Regulatory Certainty**: Program serves as reasonable and prudent alternative for Section 7 compliance for all water-related activities in respective states while milestones met; if state withdraws or Program terminates, USFWS may reinitiate Section 7 consultation over all activities relying on Program

6. **Termination**: Voluntary participation; any Governor or Secretary of Interior can withdraw; withdrawing state receives credit for contributions made; individual water activity proponents may opt out and pursue "stand alone" ESA compliance

7. **Subsequent Increments**: Must be agreed to by parties

### Comparative Analysis: Recovery Programs vs. HCPs

**Advantages of Recovery Programs:**
- Address Section 7(a)(2) consultation requirements directly
- Avoid conceding water depletions constitute "take" under Section 9
- Focus on species recovery rather than merely avoiding jeopardy
- Provide programmatic approach across broad geographic ranges
- Accommodate activities without federal nexus through state participation

**Uncertainties:**
- Long-term viability of programmatic approach when project-specific requirements imposed
- Relationship between Recovery Programs and Section 7(a)(1) agency obligations
- Validity of regulatory certainty assurances
- Effectiveness of "reopener" provisions

**Critical Questions:**
- Can agreements under Section 10 effectively limit agency obligations under Section 7(a)(1)?
- Can HCPs achieve recovery goals for species requiring broad geographic range management?
- What value does programmatic approach provide if project-specific mitigation still required?

### USFWS Target Flow Criteria

Under disputed USFWS target flow criteria: 417,000 acre feet of additional water per year needed to recover [[Whooping Crane]], [[Interior Least Tern]], and [[Piping Plover]] and meet needs of federally designated critical habitat along [[Central Platte River]] in Nebraska.
