## Executive Summary

This document is a **draft strategic plan** produced by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Southwest Region, March 1999) addressing the imminent extinction risk of the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), described as **the most endangered fish in Arizona and the Colorado River Basin**. Rather than a morphological or ecological field study, the source is a management and funding document outlining hatchery production targets, distribution logistics, and a two-phase action plan to prevent extinction and rebuild populations of large-bodied bonytail (>25 cm) in [[Lake Mojave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. The plan identifies a production shortfall at existing hatchery facilities and requests approximately **$526,820** to meet annual stocking obligations of ~55,000 fish, requiring production of ~200,000 juveniles annually over a ten-year horizon.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of *Gila elegans*. The only quantitative morphological/size benchmark given is a **management-defined size threshold of >25 cm total length**, which distinguishes "large individuals" targeted for population establishment and biological opinion compliance in Lake Mojave and Lake Havasu. This size class is presumed to correlate with reduced predation vulnerability (particularly from nonnative predatory fishes) and improved likelihood of contributing to self-sustaining wild populations, though the mechanism is not explicitly discussed in the text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic range**: [[Colorado River Basin]], with populations historically distributed through both upper and lower basins; this plan focuses specifically on the **lower Colorado River Basin**.
- **Current stronghold**: The largest extant population persists in **[[Lake Mojave]]** (spelled "Mohave" in Figure 1), with a secondary managed population target in **[[Lake Havasu]]**.
- **Habitat type**: Large-river/reservoir impoundments (RPA-3 impoundments) within the lower Colorado River system.
- **Threats to persistence**:
  - [[Dam Construction]] — altering flow regimes and fragmenting habitat
  - [[Water Depletion]] — reducing available aquatic habitat
  - [[Nonnative Fish Introduction]] — implicated as a major driver of decline, likely through predation and/or competition
- **Community context**: The bonytail is one of 29 native fishes in Arizona, of which 93% (27 species) are listed as endangered, threatened, or of special concern, and 19% have already been extirpated — indicating a broader pattern of imperilment among [[Endemic Southwestern Fishes]] adapted to harsh, arid-region aquatic environments.

## Key Findings

- **Conservation status**: *Gila elegans* was listed as endangered by the USFWS in **1980**; it is considered the most critically endangered fish species in both Arizona and the entire Colorado River Basin.
- **Regulatory drivers**: Two USFWS **biological opinions (1993–1994)** mandate the development and maintenance of large bonytail (>25 cm) populations specifically in Lake Mojave and Lake Havasu.
- **Production shortfall**: Existing hatchery infrastructure cannot meet required output; the Service estimates a need for **~200,000 bonytail produced annually for 10 years** to sustain an obligation of **~55,000 fish/year**, accounting for rearing mortality.
- **Two-Phase Strategic Structure**:
  - **Phase One**: (A) Prevent extinction; (B) Expand lower basin populations — includes immediate funding requests for hatchery infrastructure, staffing, and evaluation.
  - **Phase Two**: Eleven longer-term research/management goals, including establishing **two genetically distinct broodstocks**, long-term monitoring, disease surveys, genetics/broodstock management programs, propagation technique refinement, pilot rearing facilities, a master bonytail database, fitness evaluation protocols, disease resistance improvement, post-establishment habitat restoration planning, and a native fish outreach program.
- **Production and distribution network** (Figure 1):
  - Production hub: **[[Southwestern Native Aquatic Resources and Recovery Center]]** (Southwest Technology Center, Dexter)
  - Rearing facilities: **[[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]**, **AZFRO-Parker (Parker FRO)**, and the **[[Achii Hanyo Project]]** (Parker, AZ)
  - Distribution endpoints: Lake Havasu, Lake Mojave, lower Colorado River impoundments (RPA-3), with unresolved potential distribution to the upper basin and other undefined areas.
- **Budget breakdown** (Grand Total: **$526,820.00**):
  - Achii Hanyo Project: $256,200.00 (fishery biologist position, well/electrical infrastructure, hatchery evaluation)
  - Parker Fisheries Resource Office: $75,000.00 (biological technician, product monitoring)
  - Southwest Technology Center: $109,000.00 (tagging/delivery expenses, broodstock genetic production plan)
  - Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery: $86,620.00 (water reuse system expansion, feed/chemical/temporary staffing)
- **Core management goal**: Ensure the long-term persistence of *Gila elegans* within its historic range through coordinated hatchery production, genetic management, and habitat-based population expansion.
