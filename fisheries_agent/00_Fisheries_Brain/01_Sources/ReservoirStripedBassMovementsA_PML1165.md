# Reservoir Striped Bass Movements and Site Fidelity in Relation to Seasonal Patterns in Habitat Quality

#ichthyology #marine-biology #morone

## Executive Summary

This two-year telemetry study followed adult [[Striped Bass]] (*[[Morone saxatilis]]*) in [[Lake Gaston]], a hydropower impoundment on the [[Roanoke River]] along the Virginia-North Carolina border. The authors tested whether seasonal changes in the availability of cool, oxygenated summer habitat affected fish movement, distribution, and site fidelity. Movement rates did not differ significantly among seasons or sexes. Fish occupied consistent home ranges year-round, even when suitable summer habitat was restricted to the upper lake. The authors conclude that spatially explicit management strategies are appropriate for reservoir striped bass populations. Tagging methods also strongly affected fish behavior, because gill-netted fish tended to move downstream through turbines.

## Background & Description

Striped bass have been stocked into reservoirs across the United States, mainly to establish recreational fisheries and to control clupeid (herring-like) populations. Reservoir populations are often characterized by slow growth and high natural mortality, particularly in southern systems. Stocking success depends on forage availability, but environmental conditions, especially temperature and dissolved oxygen, also shape adult population dynamics.

The background draws on several key thermal and oxygen thresholds:

- Subadult striped bass survive and grow at temperatures up to about 30°C. Older fish show stress above 26–27°C.
- Striped bass require at least 2–3 mg/L dissolved oxygen.
- Adults stop feeding as water approaches 27°C. Mortality becomes likely if temperatures remain above 28°C for extended periods.
- Summer fish kills have been widespread in southern reservoirs.

Lake Gaston is an 8,215-ha impoundment, with a mean depth of 6 m and a maximum depth of 29 m. It is classified as eutrophic (chlorophyll *a* = 6.5 µg/L), and water levels are generally stable (±0.3 m). The reservoir receives discharges from [[John H. Kerr Dam]] upstream, and its downstream end is regulated by [[Lake Gaston Dam]], which operates for hydroelectric generation. Fish were tracked over two years using [[Telemetry]] and [[Site Fidelity]] analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat thresholds.** Suitable summer habitat was defined as water at or below 26°C with dissolved oxygen of at least 2 mg/L. Summer stratification restricted this habitat, particularly in downlake areas. In 1998, suitable conditions in the upper lake were influenced by releases from John H. Kerr Dam, and tailrace water quality varied with generating schedules.

**Summer behavior.** Earlier studies reported that striped bass avoid water warmer than about 25°C and aggregate in dam tailwaters during summer ([[Cheek Et Al 1985]]; [[Moss 1985]]). In contrast, Lake Gaston fish did not conspicuously aggregate in summer. Some fish remained in downlake areas, dropping downstream only when discharges reduced dissolved oxygen to nearly 0 mg/L. The authors suggest that downlake fish were likely exposed to 27–28°C or less than 2 mg/L dissolved oxygen for no more than one month each summer.

**Mortality and condition.** Estimated seasonal natural mortality was concentrated in summer months and yielded an annual natural mortality rate of only 0.11 ([[Hightower And Jackson 2000]]). This matches the rate assumed in coastal population models. Relative weights were below the 50th percentile reported for inland populations, so the Lake Gaston population may be in poorer condition than many other reservoir populations.

**Methodological effects.** Turbine passage and capture method affected survival and distribution. Gill-netted fish frequently moved downstream through turbines after release. Electrofished fish had higher postsurgery survival.

## Key Findings

**Study design and sample**
- Adult striped bass (minimum weight 1.8 kg) were captured by gill net or electrofishing from December 1996 to May 1997.
- Transmitters used a 2-week-on, 2-week-off duty cycle, giving a 2-year battery life. Searches occurred every 4 weeks from January 1997 to December 1998.
- Gill-netted fish: 52 implanted, with 37 collected in December 1996–January 1997 near Lake Gaston Dam and 15 from [[Stonehouse Creek]] (16 km upstream). Electrofished fish: 47.
- The total sample was 51 fish as of May 1997. By December 1998, 13 fish remained alive in Lake Gaston.

**Movement and site fidelity**
- Movement data were collected on 42 fish, yielding 294 monthly movement measurements. The number of relocations per fish ranged from 1 to 20.
- Observed monthly movement ranged from less than 1 km to 44 km.
- Mean monthly movement was reported as 7,340 m in the Results. The abstract gives SE = 544 m; the mean is partly illegible in the scan, so verify against the original.
- No significant seasonal difference in movement was detected (reported P = 0.14). Sex had no significant effect (P = 0.87).
- Distribution among upper, middle, and lower lake areas differed significantly among seasons ([[Chi Square Test]], χ² = 55.34, P < 0.001). Fish were most often found in the upper lake in spring and the lower lake in winter.
- Individual fish showed site fidelity to specific areas across seasons and years, except during spring spawning runs. Only one of the fish tracked throughout the study showed no fidelity.

**Relocation and survival**
- Of the 52 gill-netted fish, only 12 were relocated in Lake Gaston. Searches downstream found 28 fish, extending to Albemarle Sound.
- Only 5 of the 12 gill-netted fish remaining in the lake were confirmed alive one month after surgery.
- Electrofished fish showed no turbine passage. Of 47 electrofished fish, 46 survived at least 2 months.
- Monthly relocation rates ranged from roughly 46% to a high value that is unclear in the OCR (likely 100%), with the lowest rates early in the first year.

**Condition**
- Mean relative weight of winter gill-netted fish (1996–1997) was 87.9 (SE = 10.9), below the 50th percentile reported by [[Brown And Murphy 1991]].

**Management implications**
- The authors recommend that telemetry studies in hydropower reservoirs release fish as far from dams as practicable and avoid gill nets.
- Stocking programs should distribute fish throughout suitable habitats to reduce high-density and low-density areas. Stocked fingerlings in [[Watts Bar Reservoir]] also remained near stocking sites.
- Angling pressure may affect fish at smaller spatial scales than the whole reservoir. Local stocking rates could be adjusted to match fishing pressure.

**Comparison with other studies**
- Earlier work in [[Robert S. Kerr Reservoir]] ([[Wilkerson And Fisher 1997]]) and in [[Watts Bar Reservoir]] ([[Cheek Et Al 1985]]) reported higher movement rates or greater fidelity to tributaries. The authors attribute the differences mainly to their monthly, rather than daily, relocation schedule and to capture timing. Their fish were sampled during spawning in the dam tailrace, which may give a more random sample of the lake population.
- Earlier studies also reported that gill-netted fish were rarely relocated, consistent with the downstream flight response observed here.
