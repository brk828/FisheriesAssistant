# Petitions to Reclassify the Loach Minnow and Spikedace from Threatened to Endangered

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

On September 17, 1993, the [[Greater Gila Biodiversity Project]], the [[Southwest Center for Biological Diversity]], and the [[Biodiversity Legal Foundation]], together with individuals [[David Hogan]] and [[Peter Galvin]], petitioned the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) to uplist the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) from threatened to endangered status. The petition is filed under the [[Administrative Procedure Act]] (5 U.S.C. 553) and the [[Endangered Species Act]] (16 U.S.C. 1533(b)), and is addressed to Secretary of the Interior Bruce Babbitt.

The petitioners argue that the original 1986 threatened listings were biologically inadequate, that both species continue to decline from habitat degradation, and that one spikedace population is likely extirpated. The main threats are dewatering, habitat alteration, water chemistry change, and non-native species, driven chiefly by [[Livestock Grazing]], with contributions from [[Water Impoundment and Diversion]], [[Mining]], and [[Logging]]. The document also reports that several agencies and recovery bodies had already recommended uplisting.

## Background & Description

**Petitioners.** The petition is signed by two individuals and three conservation organizations. Hogan is a conservationist and ecologist monitoring riparian and aquatic destruction in the Southwest. Galvin is a conservation biologist who owns land along the San Francisco River in Catron County, New Mexico.

**Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*).** A small, stream-dwelling cyprinid of turbulent, cobble-bottomed riffles at up to about 7,200 feet elevation. It swims above the substrate in short darts and seeks slower water during high flows. Breeding males (late March to early June) develop bright red-orange coloration around the mouth, gill openings, and fins. The air bladder is reduced, which the text links to energy-efficient life in high-velocity habitat.

**Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*).** A sleek minnow of the lower ends of riffles, gravel-sand bars, shallow areas, and quiet eddies. It breeds April through June, when males turn brassy-golden. Adults are typically found in shallow water under 30 cm with swift flow.

**Classification.** The document reproduces extensive synonymy for both species from Minckley (1973). The synonymy is not reproduced here. The text also notes a pending taxonomic change: spikedace in the [[Verde River]] may be a new sub-species or species (see Key Findings).

**Regulatory context.** Both species were listed as threatened in 1986. The petition argues that little habitat protection followed the listing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic and current range.**
- Loach minnow: once found throughout the Gila River basin above present-day Phoenix. It now occupies about 15% of its former range. In New Mexico it occupies just over half its former range; in Arizona it survives only in [[Aravaipa Creek]], the [[Blue River]], and a short stretch of the [[White River]].
- Spikedace: once widespread in moderate-sized, intermediate-elevation streams. It now occupies less than 6% of its former range, in the Gila River and short stretches of its forks in New Mexico, and in Aravaipa Creek in Arizona.

**Reproduction and life history.**
- Spikedace spawn mid-March to May over shallow (<15 cm) sand-gravel riffles. Sexual maturity occurs at age 1, eggs number about 100 to more than 800, and maximum life span is about three years. Larval drift depends on water levels.
- Loach minnow spawn from late March to early June, depositing eggs under flattened cobbles. Eggs per nest range from 9 to 260, fecundity is 144 to 1,200 oocytes, and females mature in their second summer. Maximum life span is reported as about two years, with a 36-month maximum in the [[Cliff-Gila Valley]] and typical survival under 18 months.

**Diet.** Both species are primarily insectivorous. Spikedace feed on Ephemeroptera, Chironomidae, and Trichoptera. Loach minnow feed on chironomids, simuliids, mayfly nymphs, and other benthic insects, and are preyed on by non-native ictalurids and centrarchids.

**Habitat requirements.** Spikedace favor 40-82 cm/s velocities and 15-18 cm depths in one study. Loach minnow favor cobble and gravel with high-velocity flow, and avoid sand and fine gravel.

**Threats and drivers of decline.**
- Dams and diversions: contribute to dewatering and altered temperature and chemistry, and enable non-native invasion. The [[Central Arizona Project]] is said to provide access for Colorado River fishes to the Gila.
- Mining: lowers water tables and alters stream chemistry through tailings percolation.
- Logging: reduces shade, increases snowmelt and runoff, and produces flood surges.
- Non-native species: the [[Red Shiner]] displaces spikedace into habitats they otherwise avoid and physically strikes them in trials.
- Grazing: described as the root cause of most degradation. The document cites [[Lynn Jacobs]] and USFWS recovery language on riparian damage, dewatering, and sedimentation.

## Key Findings

- **Uplisting case.** The petitioners state that the 1986 listings were inadequate and that both species have declined further since. They ask for expedited action.
- **Range loss.** Loach minnow occupies about 15% of its former range. Spikedace occupies under 6%. A 1968 to 1983-84 study documented spikedace retreat of about 1 km per year and a 40% range loss in the Gila River since 1978.
- **Extirpation.** Spikedace in [[Eagle Creek]] were found in the mid-to-late 1980s, but recent surveys found none. The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] believes the population is extirpated.
- **Taxonomic change.** Spikedace in the Verde River may warrant recognition as a new sub-species or species. If redescribed as a new species, it would qualify for endangered listing, and the document states that this would remove a significant portion of the species' range.
- **Aravaipa Creek.** Both species are confined to a 24 km (15-mile) stretch. About 75% of the stream within it is owned or managed by [[The Nature Conservancy]]. Threats remain and are worsening, and red shiners may return from the [[San Pedro River]] in high water.
- **Gila River.** Remnant populations occupy a 108 km (67-mile) stretch of the upper Gila. Land management varies across [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[Forest Service]] (Gila National Forest), New Mexico State Trust, and private holdings. Grazing reductions are described as minimal and largely reallocations, not true reductions.
- **Grazing.** The petition criticizes agencies for failing to reduce grazing. It calls for suspending livestock grazing in occupied watersheds, or at minimum a buffer of at least half a mile wide.
- **Agency inaction.** Federal and state agencies report few management actions specifically responding to the 1986 listings. Several recovery bodies recommended uplisting: the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] (which voted in favor of uplisting spikedace), the Arizona Game and Fish Department, and the USFWS itself (1992 status reviews).
- **Conservation role.** The document singles out The Nature Conservancy as the only non-government entity to have taken steps toward habitat protection.
- **Conclusion.** The petitioners conclude that both species will go extinct if current trends continue. They argue that recovery requires addressing all watershed factors together, not only the most obvious direct impacts.
