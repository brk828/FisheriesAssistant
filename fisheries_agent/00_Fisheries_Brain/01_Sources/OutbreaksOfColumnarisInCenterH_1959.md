## Executive Summary

This 1960 field and laboratory report by Billy G. Isom documents two sequential fish-kill epidemics caused by [[Columnaris Disease]] (agent: *Cytophaga columnaris*, also referenced as *Chondrococcus columnaris*) in two impounded reaches of the Cumberland River system in Tennessee: **Center Hill Reservoir** (May 1959) and, roughly a week later, the downstream **Old Hickory Reservoir**. The outbreaks caused substantial mortality among game fishes, most notably [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] in Center Hill Reservoir and [[White Crappie]] in Old Hickory Reservoir. The die-offs coincided with a sharp rise in surface water temperature (64°F to 73°F in 24 hours), supporting the well-established link between rapid thermal stress and columnaris virulence. Notably, the species-susceptibility pattern observed contradicted prior literature (Davis 1953), which had classified black basses as *less* susceptible relative to buffalofishes and crappies. Laboratory work confirmed pathogen isolation from bluegill kidney tissue and white crappie, and transmission experiments demonstrated oral, dermal, and gill-based routes of infection with 75% experimental mortality.

A brief companion note by Norman G. Benson on unrelated pond fish mortalities associated with cloudy weather and dissolved oxygen depletion in Davidson County, Tennessee, is appended in the source but pertains to a distinct phenomenon (algal photosynthesis/respiration imbalance rather than columnaris) and is summarized separately below.

## Background & Description

Gross pathological signs observed in affected fish (rather than baseline fish morphology) included:
- Rare-to-absent hyperplasia
- White spots on skin/fins
- Necrotic tissue lesions
- Frayed fins
- Secondary fungal invasion of damaged tissue

**Pathogen characteristics** ([[Cytophaga Columnaris]]):
- Gram-negative myxobacterium
- Isolated on Bacto-tryptone/agar medium (0.25–0.50% tryptone, 0.5–0.9% agar, pH 7.3), per Ordal and Rucker (1944)
- Production of microcysts in culture was not established in this study, leaving classification ambiguous between *Cytophaga columnaris* and *Chondrococcus columnaris* — the distinction hinges on an unstable taxonomic character (microcyst formation; Rucker, Earp, and Ordal 1954)
- Lethal to [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) at 26°C in laboratory transmission trials
- Optimum temperature range not determined in this study, but cited as 25–30°C by Garnjobst (1945)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Location & System:**
- [[Center Hill Reservoir]]: impoundment on the Caney Fork River (mile 26.6), spanning DeKalb, White, Warren, and Putnam Counties, Tennessee; 18,220-acre surface area at elevation 648 ft; managed by U.S. Corps of Engineers primarily for flood control and power production; surrounding land use is agricultural with wooded lake shores; heavily used for recreational sport fishing
- [[Old Hickory Reservoir]]: located downstream on the Cumberland River system; received the epidemic approximately one week after Center Hill

**Water quality context:**
- No significant domestic or industrial pollution reported in Center Hill Reservoir
- Surface water temperature rose from 64°F (April 30, 1959) to 73°F (May 1, 1959) — a 9°F increase within 24 hours
- Peak fish mortality reported May 2–3, 1959, closely following the thermal spike
- Epidemic had largely abated by May 7, 1959

**Host community affected:**
- Center Hill Reservoir: primarily [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] (80–90% of dead fish were black bass), also [[Bluegill]] and [[White Crappie]]
- Old Hickory Reservoir: primarily [[White Crappie]] (85–90% of affected fish), also largemouth bass, [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Blue Catfish]], and [[Common Carp]]

**Transmission pathways (experimental):**
- Oral cavity
- Dorsal slit (skin injury)
- Gill filaments
- All three routes produced 75% mortality in test bluegills
- Pathogen also isolated directly from tank water via loop transfer and membrane filtration (as little as 0.1 mL), though water alone did not reliably transmit infection to healthy fish in further trials

## Key Findings

1. **Species susceptibility contradicted prior literature.** Davis (1953) classified black basses as *less* susceptible to columnaris than buffalofishes and crappies, and scaleless warm-water fishes as particularly vulnerable (Snieszko 1958). This study found black basses constituted 80–90% of mortality in Center Hill Reservoir, directly contradicting the expected pattern; only white crappie among the "more susceptible" species aligned with prior reports.

2. **Temperature spike as a likely trigger.** A rapid 9°F rise in surface water temperature over 24 hours preceded peak mortality by roughly 24–48 hours, consistent with known columnaris ecology in which sudden thermal stress precipitates outbreaks in latently infected or newly exposed populations.

3. **First reported isolation of the pathogen from these host species in natural waters.** No prior references were found documenting isolation of this pathogen from *Lepomis macrochirus*, *Micropterus salmoides*, or *M. dolomieu* in natural (non-hatchery) waters; isolation was successfully made from the kidney of a moribund bluegill.

4. **Taxonomic ambiguity remains.** Because microcyst production—a criterion distinguishing *Cytophaga columnaris* from *Chondrococcus columnaris*—was not demonstrated in culture, and because this trait is itself unstable, the precise species-level identity of the isolate could not be firmly resolved.

5. **Multiple transmission routes confirmed experimentally.** Oral, dermal (slit), and gill-filament exposure to cultured pathogen each produced 75% mortality in bluegill, confirming that columnaris can establish infection via multiple portals of entry.

6. **Reservoir hydrology may have facilitated pathogen spread downstream.** The approximately one-week lag between the Center Hill outbreak and the Old Hickory outbreak downstream suggests possible waterborne transport of the pathogen or independently triggered outbreaks under similar regional thermal conditions.

7. **Companion note (Benson, Davidson County ponds):** A separate, mechanistically distinct fish-kill phenomenon was documented in small ponds, linked not to columnaris but to a cloudy period following several days of high air temperature, implicating a collapse in the photosynthesis–respiration balance and consequent [[Dissolved Oxygen]] depletion. Elevated afternoon oxygen supersaturation (16–20 ppm, >200% saturation) during preceding sunny/hot days was proposed as a precursor warning sign, with recommendation that hatchery managers monitor midafternoon oxygen levels during cloudy spells following heat waves to preempt such mortalities.
