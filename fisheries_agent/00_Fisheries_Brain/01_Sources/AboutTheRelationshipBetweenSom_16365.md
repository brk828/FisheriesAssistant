# Bacteriological Parameters and Physico-Chemical Factors in Crestuma/Lever Reservoir

#microbial-ecology #bacteria #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1990 study by [[Maria Leonor Fidalgo]], published by the [[Instituto de Zoologia Dr. Augusto Nobre]] at the [[Faculdade de Ciências do Porto]], examines the abundance of heterotrophic bacteria, total coliforms, fecal coliforms and fecal streptococci in the [[Crestuma/Lever Reservoir]] on the [[River Douro]], Portugal. Sampling ran monthly from September 1988 to July 1989 at three stations, at surface and near-bottom depths. The bacterial counts were compared with water temperature, BOD₅, nutrients, chlorophyll a and phaeopigments. Bacterial abundance showed no clear seasonal pattern and only one significant inverse correlation (heterotrophic bacteria with chlorophyll a near the bottom at CIII). The author attributes this weak relationship largely to biotic interactions (e.g., [[Phagotrophy]] by phytoplankton and zooplankton grazing) and to sampling frequency that is too coarse for bacterial generation times.

## Background & Description

**Study rationale.** The author notes that aquatic micro-organisms have been far less studied than [[Phytoplankton]] and [[Zooplankton]], mainly because of methodological difficulties in estimating bacterial abundance and taxonomy, not because of low ecological importance. Bacteria mineralise most detrital organic matter, drive nutrient cycling and contribute to [[Self-Purification]] of water. The study aimed to characterise the relative abundance of heterotrophic bacteria and their relationships with biotic and abiotic factors.

**Study area and stations.** Three stations were sampled in the middle of the river channel:

- **CI**: about 300 m upstream of the [[Tapada do Outeiro Thermal Power Plant]].
- **CII**: in front of the thermal power plant.
- **CIII**: close to the water pumping station serving [[Vila Nova de Gaia]].

**Sampling design.** Water was collected at about 0.5 m below the surface and about 1.0 m above the bottom using a [[Ruttner Sampler]]. Bacteriological samples were kept in sterile glass bottles, refrigerated and analysed as soon as possible. Physico-chemical samples were stored refrigerated in plastic bottles and processed the same day. Temperature was measured in situ.

**Methods.**

- *Heterotrophic bacteria*: spread plate technique on [[Plate Count Agar]], incubated at 22 °C for 48 h, colonies counted under a stereomicroscope (reported as colonies/ml).
- *Total and fecal coliforms*: [[Multiple Tube Fermentation]] with five tubes per dilution in MacConkey broth (37 °C, 48 h). Confirmation in brilliant green lactose bile broth at 37 °C (total coliforms) and 44.5 °C (fecal coliforms). Results expressed as MPN/100 ml.
- *Fecal streptococci*: membrane filtration (0.45 µm) onto m-Enterococcus agar, confirmed in Kanamycin Aesculin Azide broth. Black colouration was taken as a positive confirmation for *S. faecalis*. Counts were made from October 1988 onwards only.
- *Physico-chemical variables* (Table 1): temperature (mercury thermometer), BOD₅ (Winkler), phosphate, total phosphorus, nitrite, nitrate and silicate (spectrophotometric, after [[Strickland and Parsons]] 1972), ammonium (Koroleff method), and chlorophyll a and phaeopigments (spectrophotometric, after [[Lorenzen]] 1967).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical setting.** Surface water temperature followed the seasons, from about 7.5–8.0 °C in February to 22–23.7 °C in July and September. Near-bottom temperatures followed the same pattern. BOD₅ values were "rather low all the year round", ranging from 1.21 to 5.46 mg/l, with the highest values usually in January.

**Nutrients.**

- *Ammonium* was undetectable on most dates. Mean values were 0.001–0.010 mg N-NH₄/l.
- *Nitrite* averaged about 0.02 mg N-NO₂/l at all stations.
- *Nitrate* was consistently higher than ammonium and nitrite. It rose from January to March–April, which the author links to run-off and low algal uptake.
- *Phosphate* was variable, with a surface mean of 0.06 ± 0.02 mg P-PO₄/l across stations.
- *Total phosphorus* averaged 0.09 ± 0.02 mg P/l at the surface and about 0.10–0.11 mg P/l near the bottom. It tracked phosphate but was slightly higher.
- *Silicate* averaged about 1.0–1.1 mg Si/l and declined from March onwards, possibly reflecting diatom development.

**Biotic variables.**

- *Chlorophyll a* was generally below 10 mg/m³ from September to April. The surface maximum occurred in July at all stations, and the near-bottom maximum in May.
- *Phaeopigments* were usually lower than chlorophyll a on the same date, except near the bottom in July. Surface means were 2.96 ± 3.18 mg/m³ (CI), 2.14 ± 1.68 mg/m³ (CII) and 4.71 ± 7.89 mg/m³ (CIII).

The source notes that coliform bacteria are mainly allochthonous and tend not to persist in aquatic systems because of thermal stress, sedimentation, predation and physiological factors. Heterotrophic bacteria are more often indigenous to the habitat.

## Key Findings

1. **Heterotrophic bacteria were highly variable and showed no clear overall pattern.** At CI and CII (surface) the author recorded four peaks (October, December–January, March–April, July). CIII showed three (October, March–April, July). The CI surface count ranged from 127 (May) to 1217 colonies/ml (July).

2. **Correlations with the environment were mostly non-significant.** Table 3 gives the correlation coefficients. The only significant inverse relationship between heterotrophic bacteria and an environmental variable was with chlorophyll a near the bottom at CIII. The author describes this as "quite surprising" given bacteria's dependence on phytoplankton.

3. **Coliforms and fecal streptococci were significantly correlated with each other at some stations.** At CI, fecal streptococci correlated significantly with total and fecal coliforms. At CII (bottom), fecal streptococci correlated with total coliforms. A significant correlation between total coliforms and heterotrophic bacteria was also found near the bottom.

4. **Coliform counts were highly variable.** Total coliforms ranged from 14 to 1600 MPN/100 ml across stations and depths. The highest value (1600 MPN/100 ml) occurred at CIII surface in November–December. Fecal coliform MPN never exceeded 130 at CIII surface.

5. **Biotic interactions may be more important than nutrients.** Citing [[Bird and Kalff]] (1986), the author notes that natural phytoplankton can be phagotrophic and gain a substantial share of energy and nutrients by ingesting bacteria. Zooplankton grazing, bacterial inhibitors (following [[Klein and Alexander]] 1986), toxic substances and detritus size are also proposed as drivers of bacterial variability.

6. **Sampling frequency is a key limitation.** Following [[Jones]] (1977), the author argues that monthly sampling is too infrequent relative to bacterial generation times. Population size may stay constant between dates while species composition changes completely.

7. **Public health relevance.** The author notes that high bacterial numbers are unsatisfactory for drinking water and recreation. The real hazard, however, arises when water is contaminated by human and animal feces, so indicator organisms remain important for monitoring.

8. **Unresolved question.** The study ends by asking whether heterotrophic bacteria are influenced by the same ecological factors as phytoplankton and zooplankton. The data did not answer this clearly.
