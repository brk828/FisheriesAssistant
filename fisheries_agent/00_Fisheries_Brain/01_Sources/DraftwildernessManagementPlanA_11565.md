# Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Management Plan (Draft, 1987)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft wilderness management plan, issued by the [[Bureau Of Land Management]] Safford District, sets out objectives, policies and actions for the 6,699-acre [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]] in southeastern Arizona for the 10-year period through 1997. The plan is organized around four wilderness management goals, the [[Limits Of Acceptable Change]] (LAC) framework, and environmental, social and managerial program elements. Its ichthyological interest centers on [[Aravaipa Creek]], which supports seven native fish species, two of them federally listed as threatened. The plan also includes an [[Environmental Assessment]] of four alternatives, a cost estimate, implementation schedule, the enabling [[Arizona Wilderness Act]] text (Public Law 98-406), and appendices on boundaries and visitor rules.

## Background & Description

- **Location:** North of the Galiuro Mountains, about 90 miles southeast of Phoenix and 55 miles northeast of Tucson, reached by county roads from the west (Aravaipa Road) and east (Klondyke Road).
- **Area:** 6,699 acres administered by BLM; no state or private inholdings, though two private parcels of about 80 acres each are surrounded on three sides.
- **Geology:** Largely volcanic (Tertiary Galiuro Volcanics), with Hell Hole Conglomerate on the east end and Precambrian bedrock at the west end; the canyon formed as downcutting kept pace with uplift of the Galiuro Mountains fault block.
- **Biodiversity cited:** 46 mammal, 46 reptile, 7 native fish, 8 amphibian species and over 200 bird species.
- **Wilderness history:** Primitive area established January 10, 1969; wilderness recommended in 1979 and designated by Public Law 98-406 on August 28, 1984 (the Arizona Wilderness Act).
- **Designated boundary:** Legal description in Appendix 2 covers 6,699 acres (versus about 6,670 acres in the Act).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aravaipa Creek and watershed**
- Watershed of about 541 square miles; roughly 69% lies upstream of the eastern wilderness boundary.
- The creek is intermittent through Aravaipa Valley but perennial from about four miles northwest of Klondyke through the wilderness.
- Annual rainfall averages about 15 inches regionally; BLM stations recorded 19.45 inches (Klondyke) and 19.95 inches (West Aravaipa) averaged over 1978–1985, with 33% falling in the summer rain period.
- Flash floods are a defining natural disturbance. The October 1983 flood had an estimated peak discharge of 17,600 cubic feet per second and removed vegetation and trees.
- At least ten springs and numerous seeps supplement flow.
- BLM requested an instream flow right of 15 cubic feet per second (cfs), expected to be granted in the early 1990s after adjudication of the San Pedro watershed.

**Water quality threats**
- Upstream agricultural diversions (up to six at times between Klondyke and the wilderness), fertilizer and pesticide use, and livestock grazing.
- Heavy-metal risk (mercury, cadmium) from historic mining runoff; the effects on water quality are not yet known. Bacteriological conditions were not assessed in prior studies.

**Riparian vegetation**
- Riparian zone was formerly densely wooded (per an 1867 account); now largely scattered trees.
- Grazing has been excluded from the canyon bottom since 1974, with regeneration noted but old trees scarce.
- Exotic species include Bermuda grass, salt cedar and watercress; salt cedar is the principal competitive concern.

**Fish and wildlife**
- Seven native fish inhabit Aravaipa Creek; the plan notes no other Arizona stream is known to support as many native fishes without substantial introduced species.
- Native assemblage: [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Spikedace]] (both federally listed as threatened), [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Sonoran Sucker]] and [[Desert Mountain Sucker]].
- Exotic fish such as green sunfish persist in side drainages; the plan calls for their eradication and native reintroduction.
- Frequent flooding maintains the native assemblage by flushing exotics.
- Aravaipa Creek was under consideration by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] as critical habitat for spikedace and loach minnow.
- Desert bighorn sheep (about 160 individuals), black hawks (state-threatened), peregrine falcons and desert tortoise are also present.

## Key Findings

**Fish-focused findings**
- Aravaipa Creek is a stronghold for seven native fishes, including two federally threatened species.
- Water quantity (15 cfs instream flow) and quality are identified as central to fish and riparian survival.
- Heavy metals from upstream mining runoff are a potential threat, because they can concentrate in tissues and enter the food chain.
- Exotic fish elimination in side canyons, planned with AGFD and USFWS, is an explicit management action; the plan states native fish reintroduction will follow eradication.
- Flooding is treated as a natural process to be allowed to operate, not controlled.

**Management plan findings**
- **Goals:** Long-term protection of wilderness character under non-degradation; wilderness dominance in conflicts with visitor use; minimum tool necessary; and limiting non-conforming uses.
- **Strategy:** Implement the LAC process (nine steps) to set measurable indicators of acceptable change; LAC planning begins in 1987.
- **Carrying capacity study:** Begun in 1985 by the University of Arizona, with recommendations due in 1988.
- **Visitor use:** Permit system limited to 50 people per day (up to 20 from east, 30 from west); maximum stay three days and two nights; $1.50 per person per day fee introduced April 1984. Table 1 shows visits peaking in 1982 (14,072 visitor days; 4,940 visitors) and falling after the 1983 flood to 2,854 visitors in 1985.
- **Grazing:** About 588 Animal Unit Months within the wilderness across four allotments; canyon bottom excluded from grazing.
- **Cultural resources:** Only about 5.5% surveyed (University of Arizona, 1975) and 22 sites recorded by BLM; sites include Cochise, Hohokam, Mogollon, Salado and Apache remains.
- **Minerals:** No mining claims or leases existed at designation; a 1978 USGS/Bureau of Mines survey found no minable ore deposits.
- **Cost estimate:** Current annual expenditures of $61,500; planned annual operating cost of $66,500 including $5,000 for water quality analysis; one-time non-recurring costs of $90,500 (including $15,000 carrying capacity study, $10,000 exotic fish elimination).
- **Environmental assessment:** The proposed action is preferred over No Action, Resource Protection and Recreation Enhancement alternatives; no irreversible or irretrievable commitments of resources are expected.
