## Executive Summary
This 1969 experimental study investigated why fish kills were being reported in Kill Creek (a tributary of the Kansas River) downstream of the Sunflower Army Ammunition Plant (S.A.A.P.), which discharges sulfuric acid neutralized with slaked lime (CaO). Researchers created a "synthetic neutral mixture" of calcium oxide and sulfuric acid (chemically approximating the plant effluent) and exposed [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and several other native fish species to it under controlled laboratory conditions. The key finding was that mortality was driven primarily by the **concentration of suspended solids** (calcium sulfate precipitate) kept in suspension via aeration, not by the dissolved ionic components (calcium sulfate solutes) alone. High suspended solids concentrations (24,200–30,400 mg/l) caused 100% mortality within 48 hours, whereas low concentrations (367–509 mg/l) caused negligible mortality. Clear supernatant (ions only, no solids) was essentially non-toxic. The study concludes that acid-waste treatment plants should remove settleable solids prior to discharge to reduce fish stress in receiving streams.

## Background & Description
- **Test organism:** *Ictalurus punctatus* (Channel Catfish), sourced from the Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Commission hatchery at Pratt, Kansas.
- **Two size classes tested:**
  - **Small catfish:** n = 36, average total length 3.65 in, coefficient of variation (C.V.) 6.79%.
  - **Large catfish:** n = 29, average total length 6.56 in, C.V. 8.23%.
- No significant difference in mortality response was found between size classes, suggesting surface-area-to-volume ratio did not meaningfully affect susceptibility to the neutral mixture under these conditions.
- **Observed sublethal morphological/physiological effects in neutral mixture exposure:**
  - Skin color change from grey to pale yellow within 24 hours (vs. light grey in controls/supernatant).
  - White acicular (sawtoothed) calcium sulfate particles (avg. longest dimension 50 microns; range 10–138 microns; SD 26 microns) adhered to body mucus.
  - Sloughing/stripping of mucus coat, with strands of mucus trailing from gills.
  - Blood clots observed in gills of 6 of 27 fish exposed to unchanged neutral mixture (none in controls or clear supernatant fluid groups).
  - Behavioral change: continuous swimming and surface-breaking in turbid neutral mixture, vs. resting on the bottom in tapwater/clear supernatant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range/context:** Channel Catfish and associated species are indigenous to Kill Creek and the Kansas River near their confluence, downstream of the S.A.A.P. effluent outfall.
- **Co-occurring species tested in preliminary trials (found NOT grossly sensitive to the neutral mixture):**
  - [[Notropis Lutrensis]] (Red Shiner)
  - [[Notropis Umbratilis]] (Red-finned Shiner)
  - [[Lepomis Cyanellus]] (Green Sunfish)
  - [[Gambusia Affinis]] (Mosquitofish)
  - [[Ictalurus Melas]] (Black Bullhead)
  - These species survived 7–23 days in neutral mixture, while test Channel Catfish died within 24 hours in the same conditions — indicating species-specific sensitivity.
- **Habitat/water chemistry context:**
  - S.A.A.P. effluent: waste acid at pH ~1, neutralized with CaO slurry to near pH 7, then discharged via a settling pond into Kill Creek.
  - Neutral mixture: extreme hardness (1490–1730 ppm) vs. tapwater controls (106–130 ppm).
  - pH of all test jars (experimental and control) declined ~1.5 units over the test period, attributed to accumulation of fish metabolic waste rather than the neutral mixture itself.
  - Relates to broader literature on [[Suspended Solids Toxicity]] and [[Turbidity]] as ecological stressors distinct from direct toxicity — turbidity (optical property) does not necessarily correlate with weight-based suspended solids concentration or acute toxicity.
  - Turbidity can have complex, sometimes beneficial, long-term ecological roles (e.g., providing nest-site cover to enhance Channel Catfish reproduction and juvenile survival per [[Cross 1967]]), contrasted with acute mechanical/physiological harm from high particulate loads.

## Key Findings
- **Suspended solids concentration, not dissolved ions, drove acute mortality:**
  - High aeration (2 airstones) suspended 24,200–30,400 mg/l solids → 12/12 catfish died within 2 days.
  - Moderate aeration (1 airstone) suspended 367–509 mg/l solids → only 1/19 fish died within 2 days; longer-term survival was 10/15 over 22–28 days.
  - Clear supernatant fluid (dissolved ions only, solids removed) → 11/12 catfish survived 22–28 days, comparable to tapwater controls (17/22 survived).
- **Species-specific sensitivity:** Only Channel Catfish showed gross acute sensitivity among six tested species; this justified selecting it as the primary test organism.
- **Mechanism of toxicity:** Suspended acicular calcium sulfate crystals mechanically irritated gills (causing blood clots) and stripped protective mucus from skin/gills, likely impairing respiration, osmoregulation, and excretion — consistent with [[Doudoroff 1957]]'s hypothesis that suspended solids are rarely directly lethal but exacerbate other physiological stresses.
- **Chemical identity of the mixture:** The neutral mixture's main component was calcium sulfate (CaSO₄·2H₂O), formed via the reaction Ca(OH)₂ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄·2H₂O, contributing to extreme water hardness (1490–1730 ppm) and existing as fine acicular precipitate particles (avg. 50 microns).
- **Comparative toxicity thresholds:**
  - [[Trama 1954]]: saturated CaSO₄ solution (2,980 ppm) caused no significant mortality in bluegill over 96 hrs — consistent with this study's finding that dissolved CaSO₄ alone (supernatant) was not acutely toxic.
  - [[Wallen 1951]]: fatal turbidity level of montmorillonite clay for Channel Catfish was 85,000 ppm (Jackson turbidimeter units).
  - [[Wallen, Greer & Lasater 1957]]: 96-hr median tolerance limit of CaSO₄ in turbid water for *Gambusia affinis* exceeded 56,000 ppm — Channel Catfish in this study died within 48 hrs at only 24,200–30,400 ppm, indicating much higher sensitivity than *Gambusia*.
  - [[Cairns 1968]]: healthy fish (28 species, including Channel Catfish) were documented living in the Kansas River at turbidity >72,000 ppm, reinforcing that turbidity (optical measure) is a poor proxy for acute suspended-solids toxicity (weight-based measure).
- **Practical/management implication:** Because acute mortality was strongly tied to suspended solids concentration rather than dissolved solute toxicity or low pH (fish can tolerate pH 5–9 per [[Doudoroff 1957]]), the authors recommend that acid-waste treatment facilities like S.A.A.P. **remove settleable solids from neutralized effluent before discharge** to reduce mechanical/physiological stress on fish populations in receiving streams such as [[Kill Creek]] and the [[Kansas River]].
- **Water quality monitoring notes:** Dissolved oxygen was maintained between 5–9 ppm throughout experiments via aeration; no oxygen-stress behaviors (e.g., surface gaping) were observed even when D.O. endpoints could not be measured directly due to interference from accumulated fish waste.
