# Fish Protective Devices: A Compilation of Water Intake Screens and Operating Experience in the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-protection

## Executive Summary

This report compiles concept designs, operating experience, and screening technologies used in the United States to protect fish at water intakes. It was prepared under the 1974 US/USSR Agreement on Thermal Power Plant Heat Rejection and Water Supply Systems, which required the exchange of fish protection designs and operational experience. The main emphasis is on [[Thermal Power Plant]] intakes, where the problems are [[Impingement]] (fish pinned against screens) and [[Entrainment]] (fish drawn through the cooling system). Briefer sections cover [[Hydroelectric Dam]] screening, the screening of major water diversions, and designs drawn from other water supply systems.

The report's central message is that intake design has shifted from meeting only the physical needs of a facility toward protecting the biology of the source water body. Physical screens remain the dominant approach, with behavioral stimuli and fish-handling devices used as supplements.

## Background & Description

The report draws on a telephone survey of more than forty active practitioners and on a review of earlier state-of-the-art studies. Fish screens have been used in the United States for over 50 years, initially to keep fish and debris out of small irrigation diversions. Research in the 1950s and early 1960s focused on fish passage at hydroelectric units on Pacific Northwest rivers. Attention moved to thermal plant impingement and entrainment in recent years.

Several factors have increased the scale of the problem:

- Rapid growth in recreational use of streams, lakes, and oceans.
- Rapid growth in the number of hydro and thermal plants.
- Plant sizes increasing from a few hundred megawatts to several thousand.
- Inadequate documentation of operating experience and environmental impact.
- Sharp increases in per-unit plant costs, accelerated by environmental features.

The report excludes sub-surface wells, which are assumed to avoid impingement and entrapment in principle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water withdrawal and source bodies.** Industrial cooling withdrawals in the United States amount to about 70 trillion gallons per year. Steam-electric plants withdraw about 80% of this, roughly 15% of total flow in US rivers and streams. Cooling water is drawn from rivers, lakes, reservoirs, estuaries, and oceans, and each type requires a different design:

- **Rivers:** unidirectional flow limits recirculation, but intakes must sit above silt accumulations and account for ice and debris.
- **Lakes and reservoirs:** stratification and weak currents raise recirculation risk, and multiple ports may be needed.
- **Estuaries:** reversing flow, stratification, and the most diverse biological community of any water body make siting the most difficult.
- **Oceans:** surf zones, storm waves, and high biological activity near shore often push intakes offshore.

**Impingement and environmental context.** Impingement is not a serious threat to the overall ecological balance of US water bodies. It has been a problem at fewer than 10% of existing thermal installations. However, the report warns that growing water use requires more careful siting to preserve ecological balance.

**Legal drivers.** The [[National Environmental Policy Act]] of 1969 strongly shaped intake philosophy by requiring environmental statements on long-term productivity. Section 316(b) of the [[Federal Water Pollution Control Act]] Amendments of 1972 requires intakes to reflect the best technology available for minimizing adverse impact. The report notes that the provision allows wide interpretation and does not say whether new intakes differ from existing ones, which drives interest in backfitting.

**Siting principles.** The report advises that siting be based on protecting the most susceptible important species and life stages. Sites near spawning, nursery, or feeding areas should be avoided. Hydrologic and biologic surveys are recommended. Outfalls should not communicate with intakes, and recirculation should be avoided.

**Intake types.** The two basic designs are:

- [[Shoreline Intake]]: intake, trash rack, and screenwell combined in one structure, most common along river and lake banks.
- [[Offshore Intake]]: inlet separated from the pump well by a closed conduit, used where shorelines shift or where surf and siltation dominate.

Approach channels are a special case, and the report warns they can trap small fish if not properly designed.

## Key Findings

**Design governed by approach velocity.** The approach velocity is the most important design parameter for protecting aquatic life. It should not exceed what important, vulnerable species and life stages can resist. Reported ranges:

- Shoreline intakes: 0.2–0.8 ft/sec.
- [[Velocity Capped Intake]] (offshore, Southern California): 1.5–2.0 ft/sec. Higher velocities produced proportionally higher fish intake.
- Net screen velocities must be low enough to avoid abrasion and gill-cover suffocation.

**Screen mesh.** Conventional traveling screens use monel wire with 3/8- or 1/2-inch mesh. Trash racks use 3/8-inch flat steel bars. Maximum debris size is set at about half the inside diameter of condenser tubes.

**Conventional screens cause mortality.** Fish impinged on standard [[Vertical Traveling Screen]]s are often killed by intermittent operation (suffocation) and by high-pressure spray. A backfitted design added a trough and low-pressure wash. Early results showed low mortality, and a major manufacturer expanded its product line to include it.

**Screen types compared.** The report covers several variants, most with limited US use:

- Double entry, single exit and single entry, double exit designs; recessed areas in some designs create entrapment zones.
- [[Fixed Screen]]s: discouraged because they require immediate operator attention and produce crude removal, and many have been replaced with traveling screens.
- [[Louver]]s: effective for guiding fish but prone to clogging under high debris loads. The [[Delta Fish Protective Facility]] reported 65–90% diversion efficiency, depending on fish size and species.

**Behavioral barriers.**

- [[Sound]]: fish respond to selected frequencies, but sound deterrents at Indian Point and Surry were ineffective.
- [[Electric Screen]]s: used at Michigan City since the mid-1940s. Operating parameters are 300–600 V and 5 to 1 pulses per second. A test at a northeastern plant found about a 70% reduction in fish in the equipped bay, but the power state had little effect, suggesting the result may have come from sound pressure. A later study found only a 20% improvement when the pulser was energized. Limits include inability to screen downstream migrants, mixed sizes and species, and estuarine or ocean use.
- [[Air Bubble Screen]]s: effective at repelling schooling alewives at a Lake Michigan plant, with shutdowns falling from several to one or two. Recent work showed they work in darkness. They were ineffective against salmonids.
- Light: fish acclimate quickly; no successful light barrier was identified.

**Fish handling.** The [[Fish Pump]] and collector removed fish safely at Contra Costa, handling hundreds of fish per hour (sizes up to 14 inches), with 98% or greater survival. Pumps suit uniform small fish, while the [[Fish Elevator]] at San Onofre suits variable sizes.

**Major intake installations.**

- [[Indian Point Power Station]] (Hudson River): fish kills since 1962; about two million US dollars spent from 1962 to 1970 without solving the problem.
- [[San Onofre Power Plant]] and [[Huntington Beach Power Station]]: offshore velocity-capped intakes with fish elevators and quiet zones.
- [[Zion Power Station]], [[Point Beach Power Station]], and [[Kewaunee Power Plant]] (Lake Michigan): capped, gallery, and inverted-cone offshore intakes, the last with an air screen. Operating experience is limited.
- [[Nine Mile Point Station]] (Lake Erie): proposed hexagonal louver intake with approach velocity of 0.1 fps, tested with alewives 5–13 cm long.

**Infiltration and perforated pipe intakes.** The [[Montour Power Plant]] infiltration bed on the Susquehanna River was designed for about 16,000 gpm but provides about 11,000 gpm. It clogs with algae and fines. A proposed sand-filter design at Kiket Island (Puget Sound) would use seven filter units totaling 120,000 ft² for sac fry protection. Perforated [[Perforated Pipe Intake]]s have a design velocity of about 0.50 fps, based on fry salmon swimming ability.

**Hydroelectric screening.** Protection targets downstream migrants on the Columbia and Snake Rivers.

- [[Horizontal Traveling Screen]] (Model VII): minimum diversion efficiency of 91% for sac fry, near 100% for larger fish. Survival was 99% for sac fry impinged 2–15 minutes but 39% at 60 minutes. Fingerling survival exceeded 97%. The Eel River installation worked when mechanical problems were absent.
- [[Conveyor Screen]]: intercepts fish in the top third of the flow field at turbine intakes. It was installed on hydro dams in 1969 and reached satisfactory performance in 1972. Future work includes mesh sizing and travel speed.

**Other screen types.** [[Inclined Screen]]s, [[Drum Screen]]s, and rotating disc screens are in use in irrigation and water supply, with limited thermal-plant documentation. Disc screens are judged to offer no advantage and to carry a high impingement probability.

**Overall conclusion.** Intake design now emphasizes biological protection alongside physical needs. The report calls for continued research, because effective, coordinated screening systems are not yet fully available.
