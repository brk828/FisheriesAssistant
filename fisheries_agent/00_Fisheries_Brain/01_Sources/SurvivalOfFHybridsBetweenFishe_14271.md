# Survival of F1 Hybrids Between Fishes of the Subfamily Etheostomatinae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 study by [[Clark Hubbs]] and [[Kirk Strawn]] (Department of Zoology, University of Texas, Austin) reports laboratory hybridization experiments among North American darters (subfamily Etheostomatinae). The authors artificially crossed darter species by stripping and mixing eggs and sperm, then tracked embryonic development, hatching, and larval survival. Hybrids were produced across intergeneric and intersubgeneric combinations, with the taxonomic distance of laboratory-producible hybrids approximating that of natural hybrids in fishes, a level higher than in other animal groups studied. Survival was limited mainly by husbandry (especially parental diet) and by larval feeding requirements rather than by genetic incompatibility. The study concludes that the one fertile F2 combination tested shows no genetic infertility between two allopatric nominal species.

## Background & Description

Natural hybridization among North American freshwater fishes had been reviewed by [[Carl L. Hubbs]], who documented numerous natural hybrid combinations. Darters are external fertilizers, and parthenogenesis had not been demonstrated in the group, so stripped eggs could be observed from fertilization until death. The authors restricted crosses to darters inhabiting limestone waters ("hard-water darters") because many species showed reduced viability when moved to soft water, and vice versa. Darter taxonomy at the superspecific level had changed considerably, so the authors followed the nomenclature of [[Carl L. Hubbs]] (1955) for consistency.

Parental species included:
- *Etheostoma* (Oligocephalus): *caeruleum*, *grahami*, *lepidum*, *spectabile*
- *Etheostoma* (Microperca): *fonticola*
- *Etheostoma* (Poecilichthys): *euzonum*
- *Etheostoma* (Claricola): *juliae*
- *Etheostoma* (Nanostoma): *zonale*
- *Hadropterus scierus*, *Percina caprodes*

Five intergeneric or intersubgeneric combinations were reported as hybridized for the first time: *E. (Microperca)* × *Hadropterus*, *E. (Microperca)* × *Percina*, *E. (Oligocephalus)* × *E. (Poecilichthys)*, *E. (Oligocephalus)* × *E. (Nanostoma)*, and *E. (Poecilichthys)* × *E. (Nanostoma)*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** Hard-water streams of the Edwards Plateau and adjacent limestone areas (for example, Colorado River at Austin, 160 ppm; San Felipe Spring at Del Rio, 218 ppm) contrasted with soft-water streams of eastern Texas (for example, Lake Cherokee near Longview, 18–30 ppm; Neches River at Evadale, 31–49 ppm), per Lohr et al. (1952).
- **Collection localities:** Stocks came from 23 localities (Table 1), mostly Texas (Guadalupe, San Marcos, Colorado, San Saba, Concho, Frio, and Devil's river systems) plus Arkansas (Brush Creek, Buffalo River, Little Buffalo River).
- **Rearing temperature:** 21–24 °C at room temperature; embryonic development timing depended on temperature and parents.
- **Larval feeding ecology:** Species differ in free-swimming larval duration: *E. spectabile* under one day, *E. lepidum* about 10 days, *P. caprodes* over 30 days at room temperature. Species with long larval stages (e.g., [[Hadropterus Scierus]], [[Percina Caprodes]]) were prone to starvation under laboratory conditions.
- **Diet:** Lab stocks fed mainly Houston dwarf white worms showed defective eggs and inviable larvae. Diets including mosquito larvae or brine shrimp improved viability. Newly laid eggs of wild *E. lepidum* were bright yellow, while those from white-worm-fed females were colorless; egg color correlated with larval viability.
- **Mating behavior:** Female *E. grahami* laid under rocks and on tank sides, while *E. lepidum* laid on glass wool or filamentous algae. Largest males monopolized breeding sites in single-species tanks.

## Key Findings

**Scope and effort**
- Development was observed in 113 hybridization experiments and 57 control experiments.
- 28 hybrid combinations (counting reciprocals) were attempted; individuals of 20 (71%) survived long enough to be placed in aquaria.
- Eight parental species were raised under the same conditions as controls; at least some individuals of four (50%) survived to aquaria placement.

**Taxonomic level of hybridization**
- Laboratory hybrids could be produced at roughly the same taxonomic level as natural hybrids. No intertribal hybrids were raised, and six interfamilial crosses between darters (Percidae) and sunfishes (Centrarchidae) failed, with developmental death before pigmentation or at hatching.
- The authors argue that the taxonomic rank at which hybrids form in fishes is higher than in other animal groups, and compare the pattern to higher plants (Stebbins, 1950).

**Natural hybrids**
- Only one natural darter hybrid was documented in the University of Texas field investigations: *Hadropterus scierus* × *Percina caprodes*. Two specimens were reported from the San Marcos River (1949) and Colorado River at Austin (1953), the latter now mislaid.

**Survival and feeding**
- Hybrids often showed survival rates near the most successful parent at a given stage, with no hybrid exceeding either parent at any single stage. Overall hybrid survival was higher than either species in some combinations because each parent was successful at a different stage.
- Survival was lower than controls in two combinations (*E. caeruleum* × *E. lepidum*; *E. spectabile* × *E. lepidum*). Excluding one sample, the latter's survival rose from 48.3% to 61.6%.
- Hybrids of *E. spectabile* × *P. caprodes* were the most frequently attempted combination (in both reciprocals), with raised hybrids showing the feeding habits and survival of *P. caprodes* after the larval stage.

**Morphology, behavior, and development**
- All reared hybrids were morphologically intermediate between their controls and could not be assigned to either parent.
- Behavior was intermediate: *P. caprodes* adults swim freely, *E. spectabile* adults rarely leave the bottom, and hybrids stay on the bottom for an intermediate time.
- Pre-hatching development followed the maternal parent's pattern (e.g., hybrids from *E. spectabile* eggs developed pigmented eyes at the same time as *E. spectabile* controls). After hatching, developmental patterns were intermediate.

**Fertility and F2 generation**
- *Etheostoma lepidum* × *E. grahami* hybrids were the only F1 and F2 generation produced from naturally laid eggs. Early attempts produced only defective larvae, but later crosses fed on mosquito larvae succeeded.
- F1 hybrid females that were early in the series produced no viable larvae; one F1 pair receiving brine shrimp produced 16 hatched larvae from 23 eggs.
- Both reciprocals produced F2 offspring, and backcrosses to parental stocks were performed. The authors find no genetic infertility between these species and conclude further work is needed to decide whether they are distinct species or geographic variants.

**Allopatry and viability**
- The degree of allopatry (same locality, same stream system, different systems) did not affect hybrid viability. Four of five combinations with better survival at decreasing sympatry were intergeneric, and the authors suggest Arkansas *E. spectabile* eggs were simply more fragile.

**Mechanisms of isolation and hybrid frequency**
- Species with less elaborate courtship and less control over individual eggs (primitive, less reduced genera) produce more natural hybrids. The authors suggest mate selection is the main isolating factor, not hybrid inviability.
- Since egg viability showed no significant difference among hybrids, the authors question [[Bailey]], Winn, and Smith's (1954) use of natural hybrid frequency as a relationship indicator, and argue that the lumping of two darter genera on this basis may be invalid.

**Parental husbandry and egg quality**
- Defective eggs and inviable larvae occurred in both hybrid and control spawns, almost always from laboratory stocks. Such larvae could not feed (they struck at protozoa or brine shrimp but could not ingest them) and died within three days to two weeks.
- Viability correlated with the color intensity of newly laid eggs, which the authors attribute to an essential metabolite lacking in white-worm diets.
- Nipping of females by males in 5-gallon aquaria damaged fins, and eggs from ragged females rarely hatched viable larvae.

**Methodological notes**
- Survival was calculated both as a total across all samples and as an average of per-sample rates. The authors consider the true rate to lie between these figures, and note that small samples are highly sensitive to single deaths.
- Survival figures were judged to be biased in favor of *E. lepidum*, since rearing techniques were developed from that species. Survival figures for all combinations are therefore considered minimal.
- Table 2 summarizes survival across more than 40 combination rows and Table 3 summarizes allopatric crosses; both are cited here by reference rather than reproduced.

**Conclusions (Summary points)**
- Laboratory hybrid survival approximates that of the most successful parent at each stage, and hybrid gene pools were mixed.
- Darter females fed only white worms produced eggs as often as those fed mixed diets, but these eggs were less viable.
