# Northern California Salmon and Steelhead Runs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 preliminary analysis by [[Garth I. Murphy]] and [[Leo Shapovalov]] of the California Division of Fish and Game compares fish-counting records from four northern California stations with commercial catch statistics for [[King Salmon]], [[Silver Salmon]] and [[Steelhead Trout]]. Correlation analysis finds little relationship between run sizes across streams or between parental and offspring runs, leading the authors to conclude that most abundance fluctuations stem from freshwater factors or localized density-dependent ocean mortality. They propose stream regulation, habitat improvement, maintenance of natural conditions and screening diversions as management measures, and note an experimental [[Hatchery Program]] under way on the Mad River.

## Background & Description

- **Species treated:** [[King Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*), [[Silver Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*), and [[Steelhead Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*), all anadromous salmonids.
- **Data sources:** Exact fish counts over fishways and measurements of the commercial catch, which the authors describe as reasonable abundance measures because salmon demand usually leads fishermen to take all they can.
- **Counting stations (four):**
  - [[Benbow Dam]] on the South Fork of the Eel River
  - [[Sweasey Dam]] on the Mad River (built 1938)
  - [[Klamathon Racks]] on the Klamath River
  - [[Shasta Racks]] on the Shasta River
- **Record span:** Counts began on the South Fork Eel and Mad rivers in 1938; Table 1 lists annual counts by counting year (e.g., 1925-26 designated as "1925"), and Table 2 gives weekly 1948-1949 totals as an example of run timing.
- **Run timing:** The first run at each station invariably follows the first heavy rains, because summer flows are too low for fish to ascend riffles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stream vs. ocean mortality:** The authors frame the central management question as whether stream and ocean mortality are density dependent or density independent, i.e., whether each habitat caps population size.
- **Ocean behavior:** Young king salmon in the lower Eel River (observed 1950) moved downstream in compact schools that broke up toward the river mouth; scattered young king salmon were also caught by bait seiners in Humboldt Bay. Larger king salmon (over 20 inches) range widely along the coast.
- **Predation:** Predation is probably the most important density-dependent mortality source for small ocean-phase salmon, though it becomes effectively density independent when salmon form a minor part of the forage-fish community.
- **Largemouth bass analogy:** Work by Murphy on [[Largemouth Black Bass]] in [[Clear Lake]], California showed a life-history stage when bass were both chief forage fish and chief predator, making competition and predation density dependent.
- **Climatic zones:** The Sacramento and Columbia rivers lie in very different climatic zones; the Mad and South Fork Eel share one, which the authors cite to explain their correlated steelhead counts.
- **Historical decline:** Salmon are less abundant than in early exploitation, when streams were relatively unspoiled, and many once-existent runs are gone or severely reduced.

## Key Findings

**Run trends and variability**
- No long-range trends are apparent at any station, with two exceptions: the Mad River king salmon counts (low for 1947-49, with 1949 the lowest on record) and the Shasta River king salmon counts, described as "disastrously small."
- Fluctuation magnitude differed by species on the South Fork Eel: king salmon about 7-fold, silver salmon about 3-fold, steelhead about 2-fold. The authors suggest this relates to ocean-entry size (king salmon go to sea in their first year; silver salmon and steelhead as yearlings or older).
- The Mad River fishway was tested as a cause of low counts; counters reported only short, incomplete blockage at certain water stages, and the authors note a five-cycle lag before the decline, which argues against the fishway as the main cause.

**Parent-offspring relationships**
- Using a four-year cycle for king salmon and steelhead and a three-year cycle for silver salmon, parent-offspring correlations were positive for king and silver salmon but none were statistically significant (Table 3).
- Explanations offered: survival in streams and ocean varies independently of adult numbers; age structure and age-specific return rates vary from year to year.

**Inter-stream and commercial catch correlations (Table 3)**
- Only four correlations were significant:
  1. King salmon: Sacramento River commercial landings vs. San Francisco landings
  2. King salmon: Sacramento River commercial landings vs. Eureka landings
  3. King salmon: Columbia River commercial landings vs. South Fork Eel River counts (judged a likely "nonsense correlation")
  4. Steelhead: South Fork Eel River counts vs. Mad River counts (attributed to shared climate and similar sport-fishing intensity)
- Interpretation: San Francisco troll catch is probably drawn mainly from Sacramento-San Joaquin fish; the Eureka troll fishery draws more from several lesser streams, making it more stable.
- Silver salmon runs in several Oregon streams correlate highly by commercial catch (McKernan, Johnson and Hodges, 1950), attributed to common freshwater factors.

**Density dependence**
- A hypothetical random-number model (Table 4) showed that if ocean mortality were strongly density dependent and coastwise, run sizes would correlate strongly (r = 0.784); the observed lack of correlation therefore argues against widespread coastwise density-dependent ocean mortality.
- Localized density-dependent ocean factors remain possible, but the authors consider them unlikely for large king salmon.
- For small ocean-phase salmon, mortality is judged nearly density independent, except possibly in estuaries of small coastal streams.
- Conclusion: most fluctuations likely arise from freshwater-phase factors or localized density-dependent ocean effects. Because present runs are below past peaks, increasing seaward migrants could raise populations.

**Management recommendations**
1. Regulation and vigilant law enforcement to ensure enough adults reach spawning beds.
2. Stream improvement to enlarge spawning and nursery areas.
3. Maintaining natural conditions in existing streams.
4. Screening diversions and rescuing stranded young fish.

**Hatchery critique and plans**
- The authors argue that hatcheries have flaws: natural hatch rates under normal conditions are about as high as in hatcheries, so eggs taken from wild runs would have hatched naturally anyway.
- Rearing hatchery fish to normal migration age could bypass stream mortality, but a program must return at least one dollar of fish for each dollar spent.
- Exceptions may apply to rehabilitating depleted runs, runs blocked by dams, or newly opened habitat.
- An experimental program to test economic feasibility was being initiated on the Mad River.

**Research priority**
- The authors call for sustained effort to determine why streams produce small runs in some years and large runs in others, noting the uncertain results of Silliman (1950) and McKernan, Johnson and Hodges (1950).
