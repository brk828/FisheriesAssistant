# Spawning ecology of flannelmouth sucker in two small tributaries of LCR

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-biology #brachionichthyidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This note combines two unrelated research items. The first is a short conservation profile of the [[Spotted Handfish]] ([[Brachionichthys Hirsutus]]), a small, critically endangered marine fish endemic to southeastern [[Tasmania]]. The second is a study of spawning behavior, spawning habitat, and timing of the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] ([[Catostomus Latipinnis]]) in two small tributaries of the lower [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. Both papers address how poorly understood populations are responding to habitat change, and both argue for baseline data before management decisions.

## Background & Description

**Spotted handfish (*Brachionichthys hirsutus*)**
- Common names: Spotted handfish, common handfish. Family [[Brachionichthyidae]], which is the most speciose of the few marine fish families endemic to Australia (five of the eight recognised species are noted as threatened).
- Small benthic fish, maximum size 120 mm. Body covered with small spinules; dermal tentacles usually absent. Body white or cream with numerous small, closely set orange, brown, or blackish spots or stripes.
- Uses modified pectoral and pelvic fins to rest on or crawl slowly across the bottom. The modified first dorsal fin spine (illicium) is used to probe egg masses.
- Conservation status: Critically endangered (1996 [[Iucn Red List]]); endangered under Schedule 1 of the Australian Commonwealth [[Endangered Species Act]].

**Flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*)**
- Endemic catostomid of the Colorado River basin. Four of the original eight native fish species have been extirpated from Grand Canyon. The flannelmouth sucker remains locally abundant there, but its historic range is reported to be reduced.
- Lives in a river heavily modified by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (completed 1963), which lies about 25 km upstream of Grand Canyon and stabilised the natural seasonal swings in temperature, discharge, and sediment load.
- Study streams: the [[Paria River]] (drainage about 3,650 km², 88 km long, entering the Colorado 25 km below the dam) and [[Bright Angel Creek]] (drainage 262 km², about 28 km long, entering 165 km below the dam).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Handfish habitat and threats**
- Occurs at depths of 5 to 40 m, most commonly 5 to 10 m, on coarse to fine sand. Often found in shallow, shell-filled depressions or near low-relief rocks.
- Diet includes small crustaceans and polychaete worms.
- Spawning occurs in September to October. Egg masses of 80 to 250 eggs are attached mainly to stalked ascidians, with sponges or seagrasses also used. Eggs are about 3 to 4 mm in diameter, each in a flask-shaped envelope, and linked by fine tubules of unknown function. The female guards and tends the mass.
- There is no pelagic larval stage. Eggs hatch after 7 to 8 weeks as fully formed juveniles (6 to 7 mm SL).
- Causes of decline are not yet determined. Suggested factors include predation by the recently introduced northern Pacific seastar, [[Asterias Amurensis]] (possibly directly on egg masses or indirectly via predation on ascidians used as spawning substrate), siltation of the [[Derwent River Estuary]], heavy metal contamination of sediments, and urban effluent.

**Flannelmouth sucker habitat and threats**
- Sites in the Paria River are dominated by sand, with coexisting native [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]], and introduced [[Rainbow Trout]].
- Bright Angel Creek has low summer temperatures (below 23 °C) and sustains resident introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]. Brown trout were found to feed on recently deposited sucker eggs.
- The Paria River has high summer temperatures (up to 34 °C), frequent summer flash floods, and limited refugia, which probably make it an unlikely rearing area.

## Key Findings

**Handfish: conservation status and recommendations**
- The species was protected under State Fisheries legislation in 1994 and under Australian Federal legislation in 1996.
- A survey over its previously documented range confirmed its endangered status (Barrett et al. 1996).
- Current research is documenting reproductive styles, habitat requirements, and captive husbandry techniques.
- Recovery is limited by a lack of basic biological and ecological data. The authors call for urgent further study and identification of threatening processes.
- Conservation is seen as closely linked to management of the whole Derwent River estuary system. Actions suggested include reducing habitat change from rural, urban, and industrial sources, and controlling introduced marine species.
- If the wild population keeps declining, the authors recommend refining husbandry methods and establishing a captive breeding programme for possible reintroduction.

**Sucker: spawning behaviour**
- Spawning was first documented in these two tributaries. Observations were made on 20 March 1992 and from 28 March to 10 April 1993 in the Paria River, and on 16 to 19 March 1993 in Bright Angel Creek (detailed behavioural observations were made on 18 March 1993).
- Mating was promiscuous. Females were typically paired with two or more males, and partners changed between events.
- Of 34 spawning acts observed in Bright Angel Creek, eggs were deposited at 12 sites averaging 0.51 m² each (total 6.16 m² within a 101 m² stream section). One site hosted 60% of the observed spawning events.
- In 59% of observed mating acts, two males and one female were involved. Mating lasted 2 to 8 seconds. Nest excavation did not occur.
- Male flannelmouth suckers did not guard sites, and no territorial or defensive behaviour was observed. The authors describe the species as a "non-guarding, open substrate lithophil."
- The sex ratio in Bright Angel Creek was 2.3 : 1 males to females (range 1 to 4; mode 2). The pooled Paria River catch was 2 : 1.

**Sucker: spawning habitat**
- Paria River spawning: depths 5 to 25 cm (mean 15.4 ± 4.8 cm, n = 50); mean column velocity 0.16 to 1.0 m/s (mean 0.49 ± 0.22, n = 50); substrate 2 to 97 mm (mean 18 ± 21 mm, n = 50).
- Bright Angel Creek spawning: depths 19 to 41 cm (mean 28.2 ± 5.3 cm, n = 65); mean column velocity 0.23 to 0.89 m/s (mean 0.57 ± 0.24, n = 65); substrate 6 to 289 mm (mean 49 ± 39.1 mm, n = 257).
- Water temperatures during spawning: 9 to 18 °C in the Paria River and 13 to 15 °C in Bright Angel Creek.
- Both streams used substrate category 4 (16 to 32 mm) out of proportion to its availability, despite very different stream-wide substrate matrices. Eggs were 3.4 to 3.8 mm (mean 3.5 ± 0.11 mm, n = 50) in the Paria River, and 2.5 to 3.8 mm (mean 3.3 ± 0.28 mm, n = 25) in Bright Angel Creek.
- Statistical comparisons used [[Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test]]s, with significance set at α < 0.05.

**Sucker: spawning distribution and timing**
- In the Paria River, spawning was observed at river kilometres (RK) 2.8, 4.0, 6.0, 9.2, and 9.8. Fish ascended at least to RK 9.8 (the synopsis gives 9.8 km). No spawning occurred between the confluence and RK 2.7.
- In Bright Angel Creek, spawning did not extend beyond RK 1.2, although there was no barrier below RK 9.5 and no significant habitat differences were found between upper and lower sections.
- In 1993 Paria River males were first caught on 13 February, females on 1 March, and spawning was first observed on 28 March at 9 °C. The percentage of ripe females rose from 12% (1 March, n = 32) to 72% (8 to 10 April, n = 25), coinciding with peak catch rates.
- Bright Angel Creek spawning took place on a stable-to-rising hydrograph, whereas the Paria River spawned on a receding or stable one. The authors suggest that tributary flow conditions alone do not cue spawning, since spawning was relatively synchronous across streams with dissimilar temperature and flow patterns.

**Sucker: size structure and population concerns**
- Upstream Paria River spawners ranged from 385 to 580 mm (mean 476 ± 37.5 mm, n = 275), significantly smaller than fish captured at the mouth (mean 502 ± 41.1 mm, n = 785).
- Females (mean 511 ± 34.8 mm, n = 95) were significantly larger than males (mean 460 ± 24.4 mm, n = 172).
- Spawners in 1993 were about 50 mm longer on average than spawners in the same reach in 1981 (1981 mean 426 ± 33.4 mm, n = 286), suggesting a shift in stock size structure.
- Several of the largest confluence fish showed traits of putative flannelmouth × razorback sucker hybrids. Removing them did not change the size difference.
- The authors caution that the Paria River's large spawning stock may not be recruiting successfully: only 8 larvae were caught in 1992 and none in 1993, and very few juveniles have been caught in the Colorado River since 1981.
- Successful recruitment appears to occur in the mainstream Colorado and the [[Little Colorado River]], but the authors could not confirm this for the Paria River or Bright Angel Creek.
