# Distribution, Habitat and Abundance of the Topeka Shiner (*Notropis topeka*) in Kansas

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This 1959 study by Minckley and Cross documents the historical and contemporary distribution, habitat preferences, and population dynamics of the [[Topeka Shiner]] (*Notropis topeka*) in Kansas. The species has an unusually restricted range compared to other Great Plains fishes, occurring from Minnesota/South Dakota south through Iowa, Nebraska, Kansas, and Missouri. Within Kansas, the authors document a significant historical range contraction: populations that were once widespread west of 97° longitude and outside the [[Flint Hills]] region have largely disappeared, while populations within the Flint Hills have increased—likely due to interacting effects of long-term climatic warming, reduced groundwater tables, and agricultural land-use changes affecting stream turbidity, siltation, and intermittency.

## Background & Description
- No detailed external morphological description is provided in this ecological paper (original description by Gilbert, 1889, as *Cliola (Hybopsis) topeka*, from Shunganunga Creek, Shawnee Co., Kansas — the type locality).
- **Growth and age data** (from Deep Creek, Riley County, scale analysis of 66 specimens, Oct. 13, 1956):
  - 1956 year class (age-0): 20–43 mm total length (TL), average 28 mm (n=50)
  - 1955 year class (age-1): 43–56 mm TL, average 48 mm (n=13)
  - 1954 year class (age-2): 51–64 mm TL, average 58 mm (n=3)
  - March 1958 sample: 48–61 mm TL, average 55 mm, predominantly 1956 year class
- Aging was performed via scale examination and cross-checked with length-frequency analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
### Habitat Preferences
- Found almost exclusively in **quiet, open pools** of small, clear streams draining upland prairies.
- Characteristic stream morphology: series of large pools (up to 100 ft long, >2 ft deep) connected by short riffles/smaller pools; discharge typically <5 cubic feet per second.
- Substrate: predominantly **gravel**, with some rubble and sand; largest pools sometimes bedrock or clay hardpan overlain by thin silt layer.
- Plankton blooms common in summer pools; **rooted aquatic vegetation uncommon** in Kansas populations (contrasts with Iowa/South Dakota habitat descriptions emphasizing vegetated pools).
- Described as **pelagic in habit**, occupying riffles only when locally abundant.
- Avoids continuously strong-flow streams and streams that are muddy/highly intermittent.
- Tolerates and often thrives in streams approaching intermittency, provided pool levels remain stable via gravel percolation or spring inputs.

### Reproduction/Spawning Notes
- Successful spawning correlated with stream intermittency/stable low-flow conditions (e.g., Deep Creek, 1956).
- Poor spawning success observed in years of restored normal flow (1957), while co-occurring species spawned successfully — suggesting an inverse relationship between *N. topeka* reproductive success and high-flow conditions.

## Key Findings

1. **Restricted global range**: *N. topeka* has a notably small distribution relative to other Great Plains cyprinids, spanning only five states.

2. **Kansas distributional contraction**: Of ~37 historical Kansas localities, populations west of 97° longitude (documented 1882–1887) have essentially vanished from modern collections (confirmed absent at 30 western sites surveyed in 1958 by Cross & Nelson).

3. **Range shift toward Flint Hills**: Early records (1884–1889) were exclusively **outside** the [[Flint Hills]]; by the mid-20th century, nearly all Kansas records came **from within** the Flint Hills, indicating a geographic inversion in the core range.

4. **Local extirpations documented**: The species has disappeared from its **type locality** (Shunganunga Creek), as well as Wildcat Creek and Rock Creek — all streams now characterized by broader valleys, more cultivation, siltation, muddy substrates, and (for Wildcat/Shunganunga Creeks) domestic pollution.

5. **Population increases in narrow-valley Flint Hills streams**: Deep Creek, Mill Creek (Wabaunsee Co.), Mill Creek (Riley Co.), and Carnahan Creek — all with narrow valleys and greater proportional grassland cover — showed stable or increasing *N. topeka* populations, especially during the 1952–1956 drought.

6. **Drought-abundance correlation**: Topeka shiner comprised 7% of the Deep Creek fish assemblage in spring 1956, rising to ~20% by fall 1956, coincident with the severe 1952–1956 Kansas drought (the driest 3-year period, 1952–1954, in state record).

7. **Climate and land-use hypothesis**: Authors propose that regional warming (supported by glacial retreat data) has lowered groundwater tables and increased stream intermittency, compounded by agricultural cultivation increasing turbidity/siltation — rendering non-Flint Hills streams unsuitable while paradoxically improving Flint Hills habitat suitability by reducing excessive natural flow (~15 ft/mile gradient).

8. **Conservation warning**: Authors predict that if agricultural intensification increases in the Flint Hills (currently limited by shallow soils and steep relief), the same siltation/turbidity/thermal stress that eliminated populations elsewhere could cause decline or extinction of the species within its last stronghold.

9. **Type locality note**: The species was originally described by Gilbert (1889) from Shunganunga Creek, Shawnee County — a locality from which it has now been fully extirpated, underscoring the significance of the observed range collapse.
