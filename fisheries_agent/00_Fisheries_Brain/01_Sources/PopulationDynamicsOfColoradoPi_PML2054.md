# Population Dynamics of Colorado Pikeminnow in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final report (July 2002) analyzes capture-recapture data for the endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), an endemic cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]], collected in 1991–1994 and 1998–2000. Using closed-model abundance estimation ([[Program CAPTURE]]), the study found that the whole-river population of fish ≥250 mm TL averaged 582 in the early period and 742 in the recent period (a 27% increase, not statistically significant). Adults (≥500 mm TL) rose from an average of 362 to 490 (about 35%). Sex ratio was 1:1, females grow faster and reach larger sizes, and body condition declined. Non-native [[White Sucker]] and [[Black Bullhead]] increased in backwaters. The report recommends continued three-year sampling blocks followed by three years of rest, with increased sampling effort, and emphasizes the link between [[Spring Runoff]] and recruitment.

## Background & Description

[[Colorado Pikeminnow]] were historically distributed across warm-water reaches of the Colorado River basin, from Wyoming, Utah and Colorado south to the Gulf of California. By the 1970s they had been extirpated from the basin below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and from parts of the upper basin, and they lost roughly 80% of their former range. The species was listed as endangered in 1967 and later protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

In the upper basin, populations persist in the Colorado, Green and San Juan rivers and tributaries. The Green River and its tributaries (White and Yampa) hold the largest population, the San Juan the smallest. The Colorado River population has low adult numbers and infrequent strong year classes, which limit long-term viability.

The study area covered 286 km of mainstem Colorado River, from the Green River confluence (river km 0) to Price-Stubb Dam at rk 303, plus 3.5 km of the Gunnison River. [[Westwater Canyon]] (20 km) was excluded because pikeminnow are very rare there. The lower reach (rk 0–180.7) was sampled twice a year and the upper reach (105.7 km) three times a year, using trammel-netting of backwaters ("scare-and-snare") and shoreline electrofishing. Fish were tagged with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]].

Sex was determined by external genital morphology. Training at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] gave 100% concurrence in a blind test of 107 fish, then 97% accuracy against sacrificed fish (60 individuals).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Adult definition:** A 500 mm TL minimum length was used. Smallest ripe males (435–489 mm) and females (485–564 mm) were recorded at spawning sites, and mature fish were found as small as 428 mm. The criterion was judged conservative but practical.
- **Community context:** Seven large-bodied species (three native, four non-native) were tracked. Native [[Roundtail Chub]] declined significantly, [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] fluctuated, and [[Common Carp]] and [[Channel Catfish]] showed no consistent trend.
- **Hydrology:** [[McAda and Ryel 1999]] linked year-class strength to flows. High spring runoff in 1983–1984 and moderately high runoff in 1985–1987 produced strong year classes, which were the main source of adult increase in the mid-1990s.
- **Fish passage:** The [[Redlands Fish Ladder]] (completed 1996) on the Gunnison River reconnected the mainstem population with a small remnant population (estimated 11–13 individuals). [[Price-Stubb Dam]] still blocks upstream movement.
- **Sampling-related mortality:** Known researcher-induced mortality averaged about 3 fish per year (0.45% of the population, 0.4% of adults). Propagation caused 15 of the 25 known adult deaths. Electrofishing of ripe fish at a spawning bar was also flagged as a concern.

## Key Findings

**Population abundance**
- Whole-river population (≥250 mm), 3-year averages: 582 (1991–1994) vs 742 (1998–2000); the difference was not significant (Z = 0.247, P = 0.805).
- Upper-reach point estimates: 394 (1998), 307 (1999), 415 (2000) for adults; 2000 whole-river total 822 (CI 309–1,335).
- Adults (≥500 mm), whole river: 362 vs 490 (about 35% increase).
- Precision was poor: CV ≤20% was reached only in the upper reach for 1993, 1998 and 1999. Whole-river CV ≤20% was met only in 1998 (total) and 1999 (adults).

**Catch rates**
- Upper-reach backwater netting increased significantly after 1991 (ANOVA P = 0.000016). Lower-reach catch rates fell in 1999–2000 to the lowest values recorded.
- ISMP electrofishing rates rose after 1991 in both reaches. Lower-reach rates declined in 1999–2000.
- Non-natives: white sucker rose from 0.36 to 0.95 fish/net, and black bullhead from 0.4 to 1.8 fish/net (1992–2000, P < 0.05). Roundtail chub fell from 3.2 to 1.7 fish/net.

**Dispersal (1998–2000)**
- 156 recaptured fish; long-distance movements (>10 km) were 19 of 37 recaptures from lower-reach origin (63% upstream). Five (26%) moved from the lower to the upper reach.
- In the upper reach, 21 of 33 long movements (64%) were upstream. Two fish moved downstream from the upper to the lower reach, which had not been observed previously.
- 43 fish used the Redlands ladder (1996–2000), mostly in July–August.

**Sex ratio and growth**
- Sex ratio: 1999, 51% male and 49% female (n = 171 sexed fish); 2000, 51% male and 49% female (n = 135). This matches hatchery fish (51% male, 49% female).
- Females grow faster and reach larger sizes; the largest female measured 965 mm, and no male exceeded 800 mm.
- Females reach 600 mm in about 14 years and 700 mm in 19 years. Males take about 17 and 33 years to reach these sizes.
- A 900 mm fish is estimated at 39 years (female-specific curve), revised from 56 years.
- Effective population size (N_e = 500) would require about 500 breeding adults (250 of each sex), although the proportion of adults that breed each year is unknown.

**Body condition**
- Mean relative condition (Kn) declined for almost all length classes between periods in both reaches.
- For 500–599 mm fish, condition in the upper reach was lower in 1998–2000 than in 1991–1994 (P < 0.05), then rose in 2000.
- Lower-reach condition for 500–599 mm fish rose significantly from 1998 to 2000.

**Recruitment and length frequency**
- Recruitment came from the 1985–1987 year classes and from 1990–1994 classes, the latter weaker.
- The modal length class shifted from 550–599 mm (1991–1994) to 600–649 mm (1998–2000). Fish >600 mm made up 56% of the population in the recent period versus 40% earlier.

**Predictive exercise**
- Predictions of adult change conflicted with point estimates in most years (for example, a 4.3% predicted decrease vs a 42% estimated increase for 1999–2000). The method was judged unreliable for forecasting.

**Sampling effects**
- In 1994, a spawning site was electrofished and 10 fish captured, which raised concerns about disruption of spawning. Electrofishing was found to reduce hatching rates in other species.
- Management concern about broodstock removal and captive-spawning attempts; propagation caused most adult deaths.

**Recommendations**
1. Continue capture-recapture monitoring.
2. Sample in three consecutive years, then rest three years (six years between field-effort endpoints).
3. Increase effort to four passes in both reaches (four two-person crews, mid-April to mid-June) to reduce CVs below 20%.
4. Prioritize flow regimes that support recruitment and expand adult habitat.

**Conclusions**
- Abundance increased in the 1980s and 1990s, with the adult population relatively stable in 1998–2000 by most lines of evidence.
- The lower reach likely declined in total numbers after 1998 despite point-estimate increases, owing to low recapture counts.
- Female-male size differences arise from growth rate, not mortality.
