# Waterlogging, Iron Availability and Heather Mortality

#botany #plant-ecology #ericaceae

## Executive Summary

This 1970-era freshwater ecology study tests whether waterlogging kills two closely related heaths, [[Bell Heather]] (*Erica cinerea*) and [[Cross-Leaved Heath]] (*Erica tetralix*), through iron toxicity. Earlier field work showed that the two species occupy different parts of the wet-heath gradient, and the authors suspected that differing tolerance to toxic substances in waterlogged soil explained this. Three pot experiments were run. Waterlogging raised iron availability and iron uptake, and high iron levels in the plants preceded death. The authors conclude that iron toxicity, rather than ferrous iron per se, drives waterlogging mortality, and that root iron immobilisation may offer partial protection.

## Background & Description

- **Study taxa:** Two close heath species, *Erica cinerea* and *Erica tetralix*, whose distributions differ in relation to soil wetness.
- **Working hypothesis:** A difference in tolerance to toxic substances in waterlogged soil might explain the difference in distribution between the two species.
- **Scope:** Experimental tests of lethality under waterlogging, the course of iron accumulation in tissues, and the toxicity of different iron sources. Manganese uptake was also examined.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experiment 1 (waterlogging, two species)**
- Plants were grown in an acidic soil (pH given as roughly 3.4–4.5 in the scan) with a fairly high total iron content and a relatively low total manganese content.
- Containers were kept flooded with water about 1 cm above the soil surface. Controls were watered at 2–3 day intervals.
- The experiment ended when most *E. cinerea* plants had died, about one month later.

**Experiment 2 (time course, *E. tetralix* only)**
- The design matched Experiment 1, but plants were harvested at intervals to track iron build-up.
- Leaf and stem water content were also measured.

**Experiment 3 (iron source and concentration)**
- Shoots of both species were grown in solutions of three iron sources: [[Ferrous Sulphate]], [[Ferric EDTA]] and [[Ferric Citrate]].
- Iron was supplied at 1, 10 and 100 ppm Fe.

## Key Findings

1. **Waterlogging mortality:** Most *E. cinerea* plants died under waterlogging, and the experiment ended about one month after flooding began. *E. tetralix* showed similar symptoms in Experiment 2.
2. **Iron availability:** Waterlogging markedly increased exchangeable and soluble iron in the soil, along with a reduction in [[Redox Potential]]. Soluble iron levels were not very high, possibly because manganese concentrations were low.
3. **Iron accumulation in tissues:** Leaf and root iron in both species rose considerably above controls. The largest increases were in the roots of *E. cinerea*, suggesting that iron is immobilised in roots to some degree and so limits further upward transport.
4. **Root iron sheaths:** Iron-compound sheaths on the larger roots of *E. cinerea*, and on the few remaining *E. tetralix* roots under waterlogging, may result from [[Root Oxygen Diffusion]]. The authors propose that these sheaths protect against ferrous iron toxicity by blocking uptake, a mechanism reported in other plants growing in waterlogged soils.
5. **Manganese:** Soil manganese levels varied and increased only in the *E. cinerea* waterlogged treatment.
6. **Water content and symptoms:** Reduced water content in both species coincided with visible waterlogging symptoms (red pigmentation and leaf loss) and preceded death.
7. **Iron source and toxicity (Experiment 3):** Ferric EDTA produced the highest toxicity to both species, probably because of its greater mobility. Toxicity increased gradually over the full experimental period. Death was faster in *E. cinerea*.
8. **Iron in shoots:** Stem and leaf iron rose with increasing iron concentration, and absolute levels were higher in stems than in leaves. Ferrous and ferric sources gave similar results, which suggests that high iron levels, rather than the ferrous form itself, cause the damage.
9. **Central conclusion:** Waterlogging increases iron availability in the soil, so more iron is taken up by both species. High internal iron levels are responsible for the death of *Erica* plants under waterlogged conditions. Protection against iron toxicity may come from immobilisation of iron in or at the root surface, particularly through oxygen diffusion from the roots.
