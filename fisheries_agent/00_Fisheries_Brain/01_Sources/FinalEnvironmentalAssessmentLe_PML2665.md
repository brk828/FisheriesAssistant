# Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge: Habitat Acquisition for Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) Negative Declaration and Final Environmental Assessment (May 1988) for acquiring approximately 1,360 acres of upland, riparian, and aquatic habitat in [[Leslie Canyon]], Swisshelm Mountains, Cochise County, Arizona, for inclusion in the National Wildlife Refuge System as the Leslie Canyon National Wildlife Refuge. The Service concluded that the action is not a major Federal action significantly affecting the human environment under Section 102(2)(c) of the [[National Environmental Policy Act]], so no environmental impact statement is required. The refuge purpose is to protect populations of two federally listed endangered fishes, the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] and [[Yaqui Chub]], and riparian habitat in [[Leslie Creek]]. Three alternatives were evaluated; the preferred option combines fee acquisition of 1,240 acres with a habitat protective easement on about 120 additional acres to buffer flood flows.

## Background & Description

**Proposal and authority.** The Service proposed acquisition under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended, with potential funding from the [[Land and Water Conservation Fund]] (Act of 1955, as amended). The Nature Conservancy, owner of the fee area, led development of the proposal.

**Study area.** The property spans roughly 900 feet of relief, from about 5,500 ft at the northern end to about 4,600 ft on the canyon floor. Leslie Creek drains about 79 square miles, forming from ephemeral drainages originating in the southern Chiricahua Mountains and flowing west to its confluence with Whitewater Draw about 15 miles northwest of Douglas, Arizona.

**Soils.** Four soil associations are present: White House-Tubac-Forest (deep, well drained, fine-textured); Kimbrough-Cave (shallow, over lime-cemented hardpan); Luzena-Faraway (shallow, cobbly and gravelly over andesite and rhyolite); and Tortugas (shallow, cobbly and stony loam over limestone).

**Climate.** The area is typical of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. Summer temperatures often exceed 100°F and winter lows are frequently below 20°F but usually above 0°F. Annual precipitation at Douglas averaged nearly 13 inches from 1974 to 1981.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Leslie Creek flow is highly erratic. In 1977, 310 days had flows below 0.05 cubic feet per second (cfs), while a peak of 563 cfs was recorded on August 8, 1977. Flows exceeded 1.0 cfs on only 11 days from October 1983 to September 1984, and a maximum discharge of 4,600 cfs was recorded on July 21, 1984. Extended no-flow periods are common, and the stream is a braided channel in the upstream reach.

**Vegetation.** Vegetation is Chihuahuan Desert scrub, shrub-dominated with a sparse grass and forb understory (grama, three-awn, dropseed) and locally abundant cacti and succulents. The upstream floodplain is nearly pure [[Alkali Sacaton]] grassland. A dense riparian woodland of alder, willow, ash, cottonwood, and scattered walnut extends about half a mile through the canyon.

**Wildlife.** No quantitative wildlife inventories exist. The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (1972) listed about 70 bird species likely in riparian habitat and at least 20 more in uplands, including rare species such as black-bellied whistling duck, black hawk, thick-billed and tropical kingbirds, and swamp sparrow. About 15 mammal species may occur, including mule deer, javelina, badger, bobcat, and coyote.

**Fish translocation.** The two endangered fishes were introduced into Leslie Creek in 1967 from Astin Spring. Historically they occurred in tributaries of the [[Rio Yaqui]] in Arizona and Mexico. In the United States they are now found only in Leslie Creek and [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]].

**Socioeconomics and culture.** Public access is restricted by the owners, and livestock grazing is not permitted. A county road crossing the area has led to some trespass picnicking. Archaeological, historic, and Native American heritage values are unknown; a survey was scheduled for fiscal year 1989 and prehistoric resources are expected to resemble those at San Bernardino.

## Key Findings

- **Alternatives compared:** (1) No action, rejected because disposal and uncontrolled development or grazing would destroy riparian and aquatic habitat and endangered fish populations; (2) fee acquisition of 1,240 acres, which gives legal protection but not adequate long-term flood protection; (3) fee acquisition plus a habitat protective easement on about 120 acres upstream, **the preferred alternative**.
- **Flood buffering:** The easement protects the sacaton grassland, which disperses high flows, reduces velocities, and absorbs flood energy such as the July 1984 event. Without it, scouring and sediment deposition would damage fish habitat and adjacent riparian zones.
- **Exotic fish protection:** Refuge status would guard against illegal introduction of exotic fishes that could eliminate resident endangered populations.
- **Cultural resource protection:** Acquisition extends protection under the National Historic Preservation Act, Archeological Resources Protection Act, American Indian Religious Freedom Act, and related CFR provisions.
- **Consultation:** The Arizona Game and Fish Department, regional experts, academia, and citizen groups were consulted. The Arizona Congressional Delegation provided appropriations, and the Arizona office of The Nature Conservancy led the proposal.
- **Data gaps:** No quantitative wildlife surveys existed, and cultural resource values remained unknown pending the planned survey.
