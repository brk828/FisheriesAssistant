# Colorado River Ecology and Dam Management Symposium: Reviewer Comments and Proceedings Critique

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of reviewer comments on the proceedings of the May 1990 Colorado River Ecology and Dam Management Symposium, compiled by the [[National Research Council]] (NRC) Water Science and Technology Board in February 1991. It includes a cover memo from Sheila D. David, letters from Reviewer #3 (21 December 1990) and Reviewer #6 (13 December 1990), and an undated summary review from Reviewer #2. The reviewers agree that the proceedings give a useful overview of the research and management history of the river near [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. They disagree sharply about how the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] should respond to ecological change. The reviews also criticize the lack of an ecosystem perspective, weak statistics, inconsistent editing, and the absence of concrete recommendations in the Findings and Recommendations section. Authors could revise their papers by 25 February 1991.

## Background & Description

The symposium proceedings cover the ecology and management of the Colorado River below Glen Canyon Dam, including the [[Lake Powell]] reservoir and [[Grand Canyon]]. The chapters address:

- Role of science in management (Chapter 1)
- Hydrology (Chapter 2)
- Sediment transport (Chapter 3)
- Lake Powell limnology (Chapter 4)
- Algae and invertebrates (Chapter 5)
- Native fishes (Chapter 6)
- Riparian vegetation (Chapter 7)
- Dam operations (Chapter 8)
- Law and politics, including the [[Law of the River]] (Chapter 9)
- The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) research program (Chapters 10 and 11)
- Closing remarks (Chapter 12)

The NRC memo asked authors to respond to reviewer comments by 25 February 1991, and noted that reviewers were asked mainly to check factual correctness and to review the committee synopsis and recommendations. Reviewers were free to comment beyond that scope.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reviews place the river within several linked management and ecological issues:

- **Dam impacts:** Reviewer #3 argued that the ecological changes caused by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] are irreversible and that the dam "is here to stay." The goal should be to minimize further irreversible effects while meeting Congressional mandates for water storage, power generation, and recreation.
- **Legal constraints:** The reviewers note that the Law of the River and the 1922 compact require upstream delivery of water to the Lower Basin, regardless of reservoir storage losses. Reviewer #3 noted that the calculation of natural flow was "the best that could be done" with the hydrologic science available at the time.
- **Exotic species:** Reviewers debated the role of [[Rainbow Smelt]] introductions and other non-native fishes. The [[Exotic Species Hypothesis]] for native fish decline was judged persuasive by one reviewer but insufficiently tested by another. [[Tamarisk]] was described as beneficial to avifauna but as an invasive exotic.
- **Riparian change:** [[Riparian Vegetation]] has increased, with benefits to wildlife and a loss of sandbars. Reviewers asked whether the riparian zone can be called a "naturalized ecosystem."
- **Reservoir and river biota:** [[Reservoir Benthos]] (including mayflies, crayfish, and [[Corbicula]]) received little treatment. Reviewer #6 argued that benthos matter for nutrient cycling as well as fish food.
- **Sediment:** [[Sediment Transport]] in the canyon was described as possibly in balance, but reviewers said storage and residence-time data are needed to judge system stability.

## Key Findings

### Overall assessment
- Reviewer #2 called the volume "a very useful overview" and said the NRC synthesis "was long overdue."
- Reviewer #2 identified two gaps. First, past GCES work was fragmented and lacked sound design and an integrating ecosystem view. Second, NRC recommendations were strong but unlikely to be implemented by agencies, especially the Bureau of Reclamation. Reviewer #2 proposed a research steering committee of 6 to 8 top ecosystem ecologists at the Interior level.

### Management and policy
- Reviewer #3 supported integrating science into management and suggested a standing advisory group (at the secretarial level or Commissioner level) for outside peer review.
- Reviewer #3 argued that decision makers must act even when information is incomplete, and that later findings do not make earlier decisions wrong.
- Reviewer #3 said the Bureau should solicit academic research, while noting that academic results have often been disappointing.
- Reviewer #3 criticized the emphasis on whether hydrologic calculations were perfect, arguing that the Law of the River governs the delivery requirement.
- Reviewer #6 agreed with Chapter 9 (Ingram et al.) and recommended moving it earlier in the volume.
- Reviewer #6 recommended banning all new introductions until GCES II research is complete, rather than using smelt as only an example.
- Reviewer #6 said Luna Leopold's call for an NPS research organization overlooks existing Cooperative Research Units at several universities.

### Section-specific critiques
- **Synopsis:** The Committee Synopsis was judged "overly vague." Reviewers asked for a definition of "proper management," a concrete example of management with an ecosystem perspective, and a clearer statement of what flows should achieve in the national park. One reviewer said the phrase "scientific talent is less likely to be found in the small business sector" should be removed.
- **Hydrology (Chapter 2):** Reviewer #2 said the chapter gave an inadequate view of flow regime, did not define the compact, and did not treat high and low flows or timing of releases. The main message on [[Lake Powell]] water budgets was not adequately supported.
- **Sediment (Chapter 3):** Reviewer #2 found almost no statistical tests. Shifts in sediment transport in the early 1940s were not shown to be significant. The only explanation considered was rainfall change. Reviewer #2 also noted that a change in sampling devices was never cross-calibrated and called this the first research priority. A compartment model with storage and residence-time estimates was recommended. Reviewer #2 said the chapter cites only 17 references.
- **Hydrology and ENSO:** Reviewer #3 noted the absence of any link between ENSO and river discharge, though the literature shows such relationships in the Southwest.
- **Lake Powell limnology (Chapter 4):** Reviewer #2 called this "a very good review." The reviewer requested vertical profile figures, light and Secchi data, and clearer evidence that phosphorus limits production. The reviewer also questioned the depth of the withdrawal current (30 to 50 m) relative to penstock depth.
- **Algae and invertebrates (Chapter 5):** Reviewer #6 found the chapter uneven and recommended it focus on algae or become a separate benthic invertebrate chapter. Reviewer #6 noted that no invertebrate species were listed for the 1967 introductions, and that the Gammarus description was contradictory ("introduced" versus "unique"). Reviewer #2 said no standing crop data were reported for invertebrates, and no food web was constructed. The reviewer also questioned an reported 1600-fold reduction in cell densities after impoundment.
- **Native fishes (Chapter 6):** Reviewers praised the chapter as a valuable compilation and as "the best single summary" of Colorado River fishes (Reviewer #6). Reviewer #2 criticized the chapter's advocacy: it presents the exotic hypothesis and gives little weight to habitat alteration. The reviewer also noted that the sport fishery was not treated anywhere in the volume.
- **Riparian vegetation (Chapter 7):** Reviewer #3 asked for evidence supporting the "naturalized ecosystem" label and for clearer definitions of "proper management." The aquatic-system section was judged redundant and should be removed.
- **Dam operations (Chapter 8):** Reviewers asked for clarification on whether the 3600 or 7200 cfs/hr ramping rates were appropriate. The chapter's acronyms (such as WAPA) were not defined, and figures were hard to read.
- **Law and politics (Chapter 9):** Reviewer #2 found this "extremely useful." Reviewers asked whether the 1968 Wild and Scenic Rivers Act affected the proposed Bridge and Marble Canyon dams.
- **GCES research program (Chapters 10 and 11):** Reviewer #2 found these chapters "especially discouraging." The critique listed 12 points. The main criticisms were:
  - No ecosystem approach or conceptual models (such as nutrient spiralling or trophic dynamics).
  - No mathematical modeling.
  - No treatment of spatial heterogeneity or patch dynamics.
  - Use of "resources" for ecosystem attributes.
  - Overemphasis on fish without evidence of their ecosystem role.
  - Null-hypothesis framing that is correlative rather than mechanistic (citing [[Platt Multiple Hypotheses]], 1963).
  - Little attention to long-term research.
  - No plan to involve scientists outside agencies.
- **Closing remarks (Chapter 12):** Reviewer #2 said Leopold's main point is that there may be no long-term research after the EIS is completed. The reviewer concluded that only an independent authority could achieve NRC goals.

### Editing and consistency
- Reviewer #6 said the papers need an editor for consistent style (some include abstracts, others do not), reduced redundancy, and checked scientific names.
- Reviewer #2 and Reviewer #6 noted that species names were not underlined and many were misspelled. Reviewer #6 said errors appeared in the Stanford and Ward and Minckley chapters.
- Reviewer #2 asked for metric units throughout, since most chapters used them and the volume was inconsistent.
- Reviewer #2 noted several small errors, including "Lee Ferry" versus "Lee's Ferry," "ethic" versus "ethnic," "Marsh et al" versus "March," and "dam" versus "day."
- Reviewer #3 asked for a map of the Colorado system in Chapter 1.
