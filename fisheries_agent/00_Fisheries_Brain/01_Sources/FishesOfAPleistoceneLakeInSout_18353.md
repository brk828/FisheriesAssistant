# Fishes of a Pleistocene Lake in South Dakota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

Ossian (1973) revises the fossil fish fauna of the [[Ree Heights]] site in [[Hand County]], [[South Dakota]]. The lake beds, long thought to be [[Oligocene]], are shown to be [[Pleistocene]] in age, most likely [[Wisconsinan Glaciation]] deposits in the [[Cary Substage]]. The fauna comprises 521 identifiable fishes from five families, plus frogs, a turtle and plant remains. The study combines fish osteology, [[Palynology]], [[Diatomite]] sedimentology and modern habitat data to reconstruct a shallow, marshy, high-prairie lake with a somewhat cooler and wetter climate than today. It also argues that most of the modern upper Great Plains fish fauna reached the region through the [[Mississippi River]] rather than the [[Missouri River]].

## Background & Description

**Site history.** The site was first reported in an 1882 mining claim. Its fossils were described by [[Edward D. Cope]] in 1891 as Oligocene, a determination made without a site visit. Later collectors included A. G. Tagg, C. Bertrand Schultz (1931), David H. Dunkle (1948) and Morris F. Skinner (1954). Skinner concluded the deposit was Pleistocene, and J. C. Harksen concurred. The present revision rests on field work in 1966 and 1969.

**Materials and methods.** The matrix is a fine, evenly bedded diatomite. Fishes are often preserved in lifelike positions with fine detail. Specimens were hardened with shellac and alcohol, and some were prepared by casein glue and plaster embedding. More than 1,200 modern fish skeletons were prepared to build a comparative osteological collection. Identifications follow standard references such as [[Taylor 1969]] (Noturus revision) and [[Bailey and Allum 1962]] (Fishes of South Dakota).

**Stratigraphy.** A 1,293+ cm vertical section (Table 1) alternates fossiliferous laminated diatomite with unfossiliferous sand, gravel and limonite layers. Fishes occur only in the diatomite. Mankato till caps the sequence.

**Fauna inventory.**
- 521 identifiable fishes: 268 to species and 253 to genus.
- Eight frogs (Rana cf. pipiens), plus a turtle destroyed in a house fire.
- Rare gastropods and plant megafossils, with most botanical evidence from diatoms, pollen and spores.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Setting.** The site lies on the Leonard L. Fawcett ranch in the Ree Hills, which drain to the [[Missouri River]] and form a divide with the James River. The surrounding Pierre Shale bedrock and buried gravel-filled valleys show that Cary-age rivers were nearby. Table 2 lists the habitat preferences of the modern counterparts, and most species are lentic (standing-water) forms.

**Depth zonation.** Catfishes were found only at least 8 feet below the top of the lake beds, on muddy diatomite bottoms, and not in sandy intervals. Cattail fossils suggest water depth was no more than about 3 feet there. Lepomis humilis was the most common fish, found throughout the shallow zone. Larger Perca flavescens came from deeper water.

**Taphonomy.** Specimens are nearly undisturbed. This suggests the fishes died in a winterkill or drought and were buried before scavengers could scatter their bones.

**Paleoclimate and vegetation.** Pollen of spruce (Picea), pine (Pinus), hickory (Carya), oak (Quercus; probably bur oak, Quercus macrocarpa), birch (Betula), grasses and composites was recovered. Birch and some conifer pollen indicate cool, moist conditions. Myriophyllum pollen indicates slow or still water. Diatom genera (Cocconeis, Cymbella, Fragilaria and others) are mostly lentic. Together these data point to a high-prairie landscape with trees confined to lowlands and stream courses, a lightly wooded floodplain, and a series of shallow marshy ponds connected by slow channels. Flood events deposited sand layers. Rangifer (caribou) fossils from earlier work were reinterpreted, because recent caribou records as far south as Minden City, Michigan, weaken the subarctic inference.

**Zoogeographic source.** Bailey and Allum (1962) found that 55 of 93 extant South Dakota species appear native to the Mississippi River system, against 28 derived from the Missouri River route. Ossian argues that the Ancestral White River, linked to the Mississippi through the Minnesota or Des Moines basins, was the main colonization path, and that the Missouri remained open during glaciation. Table 4 shows that most Ree Heights species had Mississippi connections. Only [[Lepomis Humilis]] and [[Black Bullhead]] are considered more likely to have used the Missouri route. Several others could have used either.

## Key Findings

**Age.** The deposit is Pleistocene, not Oligocene. The fish fauna matches late Wisconsinan and Illinoian faunas.

**Species list and first records.** The fauna includes:
- [[Black Bullhead]] (Ictalurus melas): five complete or nearly complete skulls and other elements; first Ree Heights record.
- [[Least Madtom]] (Noturus cf. hildebrandi): one nearly complete fish plus a second specimen; tentative, pending Lundberg's osteological study. First fossil record.
- [[Banded Killifish]] (Fundulus diaphanus): holotype AMNH 8089; a second, now-lost specimen. Represents a minor range extension.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (Micropterus salmoides): one sub-adult partial skeleton; second fossil occurrence, probably closer to M. salmoides than to M. dolomieui.
- [[Orange-spotted Sunfish]] (Lepomis humilis): 33 nearly complete fishes and 57 skulls; the commonest fish at the site.
- [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]] (Lepomis gibbosus): six nearly complete fishes and 14 skulls or partial skeletons; first fossil record, range extension.
- [[Bluegill Sunfish]] (Lepomis macrochirus): three skulls and partial skeletons from deeper water; first fossil record, range extension.
- [[Yellow Perch]] (Perca flavescens): the largest fishes recovered, over 300 mm standard length; larger fish in deep water, smaller fish in shallows.
- [[Channel Darter]] (Percina cf. copelandi): one headless specimen; tentative, first fossil record, range extension.
- [[Iowa Darter]] (Etheostoma exile): seven complete fishes and two partial skeletons; first fossil record.
- Two cyprinids, Proballostomus longulus and Sardinius blackburni, were reassigned to the Cyprinidae. Both are known only from Cope's holotypes, and neither can be placed more precisely.

**Zoogeography.** Most Ree Heights fishes point to a Mississippi River connection. Noturus cf. hildebrandi and Percina cf. copelandi lie far from their present ranges, which the author attributes largely to gaps in knowledge of Pleistocene distributions.

**Paleoecology.** The site was a series of shallow, lentic marshy ponds on a river floodplain, with a cool, moist, lightly wooded high-prairie setting similar to, but cooler and wetter than, modern Hand County.

**Methodological conclusion.** Identifications depend heavily on comparative skeletal collections, because pectoral spines and other single characters vary too much within genera to be decisive.
