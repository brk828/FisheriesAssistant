# Flow and Temperature Recommendations for Endangered Fishes of the Green River Below Flaming Gorge Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (September 2000) from the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] synthesizes the Flaming Gorge Flow Recommendations Investigation (1990–1996). Its purpose is to recommend dam operations that restore flow and temperature conditions for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in the Green River downstream of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. The report covers hydrology and geomorphology, species life histories, and integrated, season-specific flow and temperature recommendations for three river reaches. It was prepared by a multi-agency team including the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Utah and Colorado wildlife agencies, Colorado State University's Larval Fish Laboratory, and Argonne National Laboratory.

## Background & Description

The report is organized as a technical assessment rather than a single-species study. It has five main parts:

- **Introduction** (Chapter 1): Describes ecosystem changes in the [[Colorado River]] basin and the 1992 [[Biological Opinion]] on operation of Flaming Gorge Dam.
- **Study approach** (Chapter 2): Covers study area, objectives, research flows, and workshops and technical reviews.
- **Hydrology and geomorphology** (Chapter 3): Analyzes seasonal, peak and base flows, tributary inputs, daily hydropower fluctuations, water temperatures, ice conditions and channel processes.
- **Fishes of the Green River** (Chapter 4): Gives the fish community overview and species accounts for the three endangered fishes.
- **Flow and temperature recommendations** (Chapter 5): Provides reach-by-reach recommendations, example flow patterns, and comparison with the 1992 Biological Opinion. Chapter 6 lists references, and appendices give supporting hydrology, study abstracts and workshop participant lists.

The fish community was assessed as 12 native species in four families and 25 nonnative species in nine families. Native fishes were grouped into three categories: big-river warm-water species, cool- or cold-water species, and generalists. Dam operations are the main disruption. Cold, clear releases created a [[Tailwater Trout Fishery]] and eliminated most successful reproduction by native fishes in Reach 1. Inflow from the [[Yampa River]] makes downstream reaches closer to pre-dam conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dam effects on the Green River**
- Before the dam, the river had strong seasonal flow variation, and water temperatures ranged from near freezing to above 20°C. Warm-water reaches supported native fishes that were reproducing successfully.
- Hypolimnetic releases after closure eliminated most warm-water reproduction upstream of the Yampa River confluence.
- Penstocks were modified in 1978 to draw warmer water, and tailwater releases were managed not to exceed 13°C. The report notes this is still below the optimum growth temperature for the main trout species.
- A 1962 [[Rotenone Treatment]] to remove "coarse" fishes killed rare endemic fishes in Dinosaur National Monument.

**Reach structure**
- **Reach 1:** Flaming Gorge Dam to the Yampa River confluence. Fully regulated, with cold tailwater conditions.
- **Reach 2:** Yampa River confluence to the White River confluence. Includes [[Desolation Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]] downstream areas and is more natural.
- **Reach 3:** White River confluence to the Colorado River confluence, including [[Desolation Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]] with regulated-natural flow regimes.

**Nonnative species**
- Nonnative fishes dominate the basin's fish fauna and are implicated in declines of native fishes. Dam-created conditions favored nonnative proliferation.
- Examples include common carp, sand shiner, fathead minnow, channel catfish and smallmouth bass.
- [[Nonnative Fishes]] are a key management concern, with control options including reduced stocking and harvest regulation.

**Humpback chub habitat and life history**
- Humpback chub is endemic to the Colorado River basin and is specialized for warm, turbid, large-river conditions.
- It is an obligate warm-water species that requires relatively warm water for spawning, egg incubation, and larval survival. Optimum growth temperatures are reported as near 20°C.
- Adults use large recirculating eddies, and juveniles use shoreline eddies and other low-velocity habitats during spring runoff.

## Key Findings

**Species status**
- Humpback chub, Colorado pikeminnow and razorback sucker are federally listed as endangered.
- [[Bonytail]] is considered functionally extinct in the wild, with only a few scattered individuals.
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] is the only big-river fish not listed as endangered and remains common in many locations.
- The lower Colorado River population near Marble and Grand canyons is the largest and most stable humpback chub population. [[Valdez and Ryel 1995]] estimated 3,750 adults larger than 200 mm TL in the main-stem Colorado River.

**Humpback chub reproduction**
- Eight fish averaged 2,523 ova per fish (estimated 5,262 ova per kilogram of body weight). Eleven Little Colorado River fish yielded 4,831 ova per fish.
- Incubation takes about 115–160 h at 19–20°C.
- Hatching success and larval survival are temperature dependent (Hamman 1982, as summarized by Valdez and Ryel 1997):

| Temperature (°C) | % Hatching success | % Larval survival |
|---|---|---|
| 12–13 | 12 | 15 |
| 16–17 | 62 | 91 |
| 19–20 | 84 | 95 |
| 21–22 | 79 | 99 |

- Spawning occurs over a 4–5 week period, usually on the descending limb of the spring hydrograph at water temperatures above 17°C.
- Spawning peaks in [[Westwater Canyon]] occurred between 19 June and 5 August across 1992–1996, with peak spawning often at 18–21°C.
- In [[Desolation Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]], hatching ranged from 7 June 1994 at 283 m³/s to 1 July 1995 at 731 m³/s (Chart and Lentsch 2000).
- Humpback chub may live 20–30 years.

**Flow recommendations**
- [[Chart 2000]] recommended that during moderate-to-wet years, peak flows in Desolation and Gray canyons should reach near 708 m³/s, with the spring-runoff period lasting 60 days or more to benefit Gila reproduction.
- Eddy habitats increased in frequency and decreased in size from base flow up to about 198 m³/s. Above 198 m³/s, total eddy area stayed essentially unchanged while individual eddy size increased.
- Base flows of about 57 to 113 m³/s in Desolation and Gray canyons are recommended to maintain stable shoreline habitat in winter and to increase juvenile overwinter survival.
- Higher summer and autumn base flows in Reaches 1 and 2 are recommended to keep warm nursery conditions, with flow management targeting a summer base-flow temperature in Lodore and Whirlpool canyons.

**Temperature recommendations**
- Increased release temperatures from Flaming Gorge Dam, paired with low summer and autumn base flows, are recommended to benefit humpback chub in Lodore Canyon and upper Reach 2 (Whirlpool and Split Mountain canyons).
- Managers were advised to avoid flow and temperature changes that reduce spring peak flows, since spring runoff provides spawning and nursery habitat.

**Key uncertainties and needs**
- Flow requirements for winter are poorly known for humpback chub.
- Reliable population estimates in the upper basin are difficult because recapture rates are low.
- Suspected links between flow and reproduction are not established for all species, and the report calls for long-term monitoring to test the recommendations.
