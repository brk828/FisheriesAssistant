# Otolith Utility for Humpback Chub Management in the Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-cypha

## Executive Summary

This interim progress report, submitted in February 1993 to the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] and the [[Texas Memorial Museum]], evaluates whether [[Otolith]] (ear stone) structure and chemistry can reconstruct age, growth, and habitat histories of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*[[Gila Cypha]]*) in the [[Grand Canyon]]. Ages were estimated from [[Lapillus]], [[Asteriscus]] and [[Opercle]] samples from 90 fish (47 juveniles, 43 adults) collected between 1988 and 1992. The author reports that presumptive [[Daily Growth Increments]] are clearly visible in young-of-the-year lapilli, particularly from the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), and that adult ages from opercles agree reasonably well with independent asteriscus estimates. Validation experiments largely failed or remain incomplete, and the chemical analyses were still in progress. The results are explicitly described as highly preliminary.

## Background & Description

[[Humpback Chub]] is a large-bodied, endangered freshwater cyprinid-type fish endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system, notable for being difficult to sample and poorly understood. Otoliths are calcareous inner-ear structures that grow by daily accretion, much like tree rings, and are chemically stable; unlike scales, they are not readily reabsorbed. The author proposed that these properties could record daily growth, birth dates, movement between habitats, and possibly birthplace, providing life-history data (spawning timing, cohort recruitment, mortality) critical for managing this species.

**Sample base**

- Juveniles: 47 specimens aged 0 to 1+ years.
- Adults: 43 specimens with estimated ages of 2 to 23 years.
- Origin: 71 from the LCR and 19 from the mainstream Colorado River, collected at various Grand Canyon sites between 1988 and 1992.
- Structures prepared: opercles from 35 specimens, one [[Asteriscus]] from each of 47, and one [[Lapillus]] from each of 56. Seventeen specimens had all three calcareous structures examined.

**Methods**

- Adults were euthanized, measured and weighed in the field; skeletons were dried, and otoliths were cleaned using [[Dermestid Beetles]] or forceps.
- Young-of-the-year (1990 to 1992) were preserved in ethanol.
- Ages were estimated by F. Gary Scoppettone (opercles), Michael McCarthy (asteriscus) and Dr. R. Brothers (lapilli), with the author performing an independent review. Minimum and maximum ages were reported where uncertain, and counts were averaged over two readings.
- The [[Sagitta]] was judged unsuitable because of its delicate form and irregular increments after the larval/juvenile stage.
- Standard length was used throughout because total length was less precise. A TL–SL regression (n = 126, 13 to 100 mm SL) gave a reported correlation of approximately 0.992; the scan makes the exact coefficients uncertain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study rests on the sharp environmental contrast between two habitats:

- **[[Little Colorado River]] (LCR):** warmer, with water quality that clearly distinguishes it from the mainstream.
- **[[Colorado River]] mainstream:** colder and near-constant in temperature, with growth rates reported as much lower than in the LCR.

The author suggests that fish moving between these waters experience abrupt temperature and water-quality shifts, which may leave structural or chemical marks on otoliths. Some mainstream specimens also appear to have spent brief periods in waters much warmer than the mainstream, possibly tributary mouths. Unusual floods or cold spells may also create event-specific marks usable as cohort markers.

The author also notes that humpback chub in the lower canyon may or may not belong to the LCR gene pool, and that a 1990s accidental spill in the Zuni River basin could provide a distinctive isotopic or elemental marker.

## Key Findings

**Age and increment validity**

- Presumptive daily increments are clearly visible in lapilli of young fish, especially those from the LCR before their first winter. Increments can be read to the edge, giving age estimates described as having near-daily precision.
- Back-calculated hatch dates for LCR young-of-the-year agree with anecdotal observations of spawning activity. Daily periodicity has not been formally validated.
- Increment resolution declines with age, and counting becomes subjective in later years without extensive preparation or [[Scanning Electron Microscopy]].
- Adult lapilli ages are complicated by sequences of narrow increments that are hard to separate from annuli. Annual periodicity also requires validation, ideally with [[Otolith Marking]] and PIT tags.

**Adult ages**

- Opercles appear to provide reasonable yearly ages for adults up to about three years, and are less laborious to prepare than otoliths. However, bone is susceptible to calcium mobilization, whereas otoliths are considered more stable.
- Opercle and asteriscus age estimates agreed in fairly good agreement for the compared specimens; asteriscus increments were comparatively difficult to interpret.
- Table 1 lists age estimates for 50 selected skeletonized adults from 1989 to 1990. The report states 18 specimens were more than one year old and 22 were one year old, but this line is partly illegible in the scan.

**Growth patterns**

- Growth is highly heterogeneous, and size is not a good predictor of age (Figures 13 and 14).
- Variation appears to be established early in life. Nearly all young-of-the-year from the LCR grew faster than comparable mainstream fish.
- Sex is unknown for many specimens; sex-related size differences are thought to contribute to the scatter in length at age.

**Movement and birthplace**

- Abrupt growth transitions in some adults are thought to reflect movement between the LCR and mainstream.
- Specimen 328 (about 98 days old) was captured at River Mile 192.3, roughly 130 miles below the LCR. No 1991 year-class young-of-the-year from the LCR had been examined, so its origin remains unresolved.
- Hatch dates from five 1992 mainstream specimens fell in May and June; LCR young-of-the-year hatched in March and April 1990. A single mainstream specimen collected in October 1990 was estimated to have hatched around 30 May 1990, later than the latest LCR hatch date that year. The author notes this may reflect small sample size or sampling bias.

**Experiments (mostly unsuccessful)**

- *Temperature transfer experiment:* Fish held in the LCR and mainstream, or moved alternately between them, suffered high mortality. Survival was linked more to caging than to temperature, and the specimens were not examined.
- *Hatchery experiment:* Because humpback chub were not being cultured, [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) were used as a surrogate at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] at 15, 21 and 27 °C. Growth was very low, and results for the temperature treatments had not been examined. The author recommends repeating the experiment in hatcheries with larger samples.

**Chemistry**

- Analysis of micro-spatial variation in otolith elemental composition was in progress. X-ray methods were found to have low precision and high detection limits, so the author turned to a [[Proton Probe]] at the Institute of Geological and Nuclear Sciences in Lower Hutt, New Zealand.
- Planned comparisons: 5 LCR young-of-the-year, 5 hatchery-reared young-of-the-year, and 5 fish known to have moved between the LCR and mainstream, plus [[Stable Isotopes]] analysis and temperature-controlled experiments. Preliminary proton probe results were expected within a month or two.

**Data needs and recommendations**

- Otolith size–fish size relationships for small fish (14 mm SL, reportedly about 30 days old, is the smallest specimen available) remain undefined.
- Recommended additional specimens: wild-caught larvae with known water-quality and discharge histories; adults with known tag-recapture or radiotelemetry histories; and fish from the lower canyon.
- Temperature, discharge and wider water-chemistry records (ideally covering roughly three decades) are required for each river.
- Intentional field marking with tetracycline or alizarin, combined with PIT tags, was suggested to validate daily and annual marks.

**Conclusions**

- Otoliths show considerable potential for humpback chub management, providing near-daily age estimates for young LCR fish and reasonable yearly ages for adults up to about three years.
- Growth rates in the mainstream appear much lower than in the LCR, and brief warm-water episodes and abrupt growth transitions suggest movements between habitats.
- Daily increment formation, the periodicity of annual marks and the chemical markers still require rigorous validation.
- The author expected that [[Otolith Microchemistry]] could yield unique markers for LCR versus mainstream residence, but these claims remained unconfirmed at the time of the report.
