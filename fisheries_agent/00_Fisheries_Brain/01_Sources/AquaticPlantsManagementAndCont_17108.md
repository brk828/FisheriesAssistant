# Aquatic Plants: Management and Control in Pennsylvania

#aquatic-botany #limnology #aquatic-weed-control

## Executive Summary

This Pennsylvania extension-style guide explains how aquatic plants (flowering plants and algae) shape freshwater ecosystems, why they are sometimes desirable and sometimes a nuisance, and how managers can control them. It covers the three growth forms of flowering plants ([[Submergent Plants]], [[Floating Plants]], [[Emergent Plants]]), the ecological role of [[Algae]] and [[Plankton]], and the main hazard of dense growth: [[Dissolved Oxygen]] depletion and [[Fish Kill]]s. Control options are grouped as [[Mechanical Control]], [[Environmental Control]], and [[Chemical Control]], with detailed herbicide dosages, water-use restrictions, permit requirements, and a summary table (Table 1). The document is dated to 1966 herbicide recommendations and says the current year's recommendations should be checked.

## Background & Description

Aquatic plants occur in streams, rivers, lakes, ponds, marshes, and wetlands. They may dominate a water body, occupy only the shoreline margin, grow submerged out of sight, or exist as suspended microscopic particles. They belong to two groups: flowering plants and algae.

The need for management depends on how a water body is used. Fishing, boating, swimming, water skiing, duck hunting, domestic, industrial, and agricultural water use may all occur on one water body, and each user values plants differently:

- **Swimmers and reservoir managers** want clear water free of weeds and algae.
- **Waterfowl hunters** want plants that attract ducks and geese.
- **Anglers** value plants that shelter and feed fish, until they become widespread.
- **Conservationists** favor watershed and bank vegetation to control erosion and protect water quality.

**Flowering plants** are grouped by water depth:

- **Submergents** (e.g., [[Pondweed]], [[Water Milfoil]], [[Coontail]], waterweed, [[Bushy Pondweed]]) grow mostly underwater and obtain nutrients from the surrounding water. Flowers are wind- or insect-pollinated, and stalks withdraw below the surface to mature fruit.
- **Floaters** are attached (e.g., [[Water Lily]], [[Spatterdock]]), rooted in mucky bottoms and drawing nutrients from mud, or unattached (e.g., [[Water Hyacinth]] of the south and [[Duckweed]] of the north). Wolffia, the smallest flowering plant, has a single leaf usually under one-sixteenth inch in diameter.
- **Emergents** (e.g., [[Cattail]], arrowheads, rushes, reeds, sedges) grow along shorelines, rooted in the bottom, with stems and leaves above water. Leaf shape varies with depth, so identification can be difficult.

**Reproduction** occurs by seed (some seeds germinate at once, others wait years in bottom mud), by vegetative runners or fragmentation, and by winter buds (turions) in Coontail and Potamogeton. Fragmentation matters for control: mechanical cutting can spread fragments that root elsewhere.

**Algae** include filamentous mats ("pond scum"), suspended plankton that create "blooms," and [[Stonewort]] (Chara and Nitella), which smell musky, may be encrusted with calcium, grow up to 2 or 3 feet tall, and can cover a pond bottom.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Growth controls.** Factors that inhibit plant growth include deep water, steep shorelines, unstable bottoms, cold, colored or turbid water, and low fertility. Dense growth usually occurs where water is shallow, gently sloped, stable, warm, clear, and fertile. Soft water is generally low in fertility and supports sparse vegetation; hard water is highly fertile and supports dense, diverse growth. Most aquatic plants grow best on a sand and organic soil mix, while soft, mucky soils may be unfavorable.

**Nutrients and pollution.** Nitrogen and phosphorus from sewage effluent, home waste systems, and fertilized agricultural watersheds stimulate both algae and water weeds. Housing subdivisions, cabins, and cottages are often the source. The document notes that even well-treated sewage still carries effective nitrates and phosphates. Industrial and mining pollution, by contrast, can inhibit growth; the document focuses mainly on nutrient enrichment.

**Food chain.** Algae and flowering plants are primary producers. Plants absorb carbon dioxide and release oxygen during photosynthesis, and diatoms are described as the most important nutrient producers in natural waters, including oceans. Because of energy loss at each trophic level, longer food chains support fewer individuals. The stems and leaves of submerged plants host a community of bacteria, fungi, algae, protozoans, insect larvae, rotifers, and small crustaceans that feed fish.

**Animal use.** Fish and amphibians lay eggs in plant beds. Fry use them for shelter and food. Forage species important to [[Largemouth Bass]] need shallow, vegetated water for spawning. Pondweeds, wild celery, arrowhead, wild rice, and reeds feed waterfowl. Snapping turtle diets are about nine-tenths vegetable matter, and plants make up two-thirds of the painted turtle's diet. [[Muskrat]]s eat rootstocks, tubers, and stems, and use plants for houses and dens.

**Filling.** Over thousands of years, ponds fill with organic matter and become dry land. Plants speed this by building up dead material and slowing water flow so sediment settles. Most bogs and swamps were once lakes.

**Oxygen and fish kills.** Plants produce oxygen only in light. Under warm, calm, cloudy summer conditions, or under ice and snow cover in winter, plants respire and decompose, consuming oxygen. This can reduce levels below what fish and their food organisms need.

**Conflicting values.** An aquatic weed is defined as "an aquatic plant that grows when and where it is not wanted." Spatterdock can be valuable fish habitat yet a nuisance near a boat dock, so compromise is often needed.

## Key Findings

**Control framework and planning**
- Methods are mechanical, biological, and chemical, often combined. Changing the environment can use any of these.
- Control requires identifying the plant and understanding its life history before acting.
- Managers should weigh all consequences before starting a program.

**Mechanical control**
- Removal is most effective for small quantities near shorelines (mowing, raking, digging, pulling). Repeated treatments are needed because of extensive tubers and roots.
- Cutting or dragging chains across the bottom is ineffective for submerged plants and can spread them through fragmentation.
- Collecting cut vegetation to prevent windrows on shorelines is a major problem.

**Environmental control**
- Deepening shallow ponds to **10 feet or more** is the most permanent option; sunlight rarely supports vigorous growth below that depth.
- **Partial drawdown** in late fall or early winter to freeze exposed bottom: effective control is "usually assured if the bottom mud freezes to a 4-inch depth for a period of 3 weeks or longer."
- **Steep sides** (5 feet or deeper) reduce growth, but may be hazardous to children.
- **Fertilization** for plankton blooms, for small waters (a few acres or less) with slow water exchange and warm-water fish objectives. Rule of thumb: the hand should not be visible when the arm is immersed to the elbow. Start with 100 lb of 10-10-10 per acre about May 1, with weekly follow-ups until cloudy. Stop when a light-colored stick disappears at **15 inches** and resume when visibility reaches **20 inches**.
- Fertilization is hazardous in northern, ice-covered ponds; ponds should not be fertilized from September to the following spring.
- **Black plastic** 2 to 3 mil thick, placed on the bottom in early spring, prevents rooted growth in small ponds.

**Chemical control: general safeguards**
- Any chemical introduced into Pennsylvania waters is regulated by the [[Pennsylvania Fish Commission]] and the Water Quality Division of the [[Pennsylvania Department of Health]]. Start by contacting the local Fish Commission warden or biologist for a permit.
- Most failures are attributed to calculation errors or improper use, not the herbicide itself.
- Treat no more than **one-third** of a pond at a time, spacing applications at least one week apart, to avoid oxygen depletion from decaying plants.
- Treat in late spring or early summer, when plants are young and growing actively.
- Large-scale programs should be left to commercial applicators.

**Dosage calculation**
- Concentrations are expressed in parts per million (ppm) of active ingredient. Percentage of active ingredient is on the manufacturer's label.
- Volume is surface area (acres) × average depth (feet) = acre-feet. Example: a 2-acre pond averaging 4 feet holds 8 acre-feet.
- One acre-foot weighs about 2.7 million pounds; 1 ppm requires about 2.7 lb of chemical per acre-foot. For ½ ppm in the 8 acre-foot example, the document gives 10.8 lb.
- A small pond of 5,000 sq ft × 4 ft (20,000 cu ft, 1,250,000 lb of water) needs 1.25 lb for 1 ppm.
- Tables 2 and 3 give gallons and pounds needed per acre-foot at 1 to 10 ppm for various formulations.

**Algae control**
- For domestic water supply reservoirs and swimming areas, [[Copper Sulfate]] (bluestone) is described as the only safe and effective treatment at that time.
- Typical concentration is about 1 ppm; 1.35 lb per acre-foot is cited for a ½ ppm concentration. High-alkalinity water (150–250 ppm) may need up to about 1 ppm. Hard water may need three or four treatments per summer.
- If no result appears within 7 days, reapply with a 50% increase.
- **Trout are very sensitive** to copper sulfate; do not treat waters containing trout. Repeated applications reduce fish productivity.
- Apply on warm, sunny days, when algae are most vulnerable.
- Do not use copper sulfate on an impoundment also treated with other herbicides.
- [[Diquat]] also controls algae at ¼ to ½ ppm (OCR-garbled in source), but ponds treated with it may not be used for domestic supply.

**Submerged plant control**
- [[2,4-D]] is particularly effective on water milfoil; coontail, fanwort, and bladderwort can also be controlled with it. Most pondweeds, waterweed, and wild celery (tapegrass) do not respond. Granules: 20 lb active ingredient per acre.
- [[Silvex]] at 2 ppm controls fanwort, bladderwort, bushy pondweed, waterweed, and milfoil.
- [[Endothall]] ("Aquathol") at 1 to 3 ppm: pondweeds and coontails 1 ppm; milfoil 3 ppm.
- Diquat is effective on milfoil, bladderwort, and waterweed but not in muddy water.
- [[Simazine]] showed good test results but is not registered or approved for aquatic weed control by USDA, so it cannot be recommended.
- As of April 15, 1966, 2,4-D and 2,4,5-T were not approved for drinking water treatment.

**Emergent plant control**
- Emergents are valuable duck foods and should not be eliminated where waterfowl are wanted.
- 2,4-D low-volatile ester: 2 lb active ingredient per acre as a foliage spray, up to 15 lb per acre as granules.
- Foliar sprays should be applied in early summer, before flowering. Mixtures with 2,4,5-T or silvex are needed for cattails, sedges, common reed grass, and rushes.
- Fuel oil (No. 2 or No. 3) improves penetration but can injure fish through oily films.

**Floating plant control**
- Waterlilies and spatterdock resist 2,4-D alone. The recommended mix is 5 lb of 2,4,5-T (or 8 lb silvex) with 10 lb or more of 2,4-D per acre.
- Do not spray if water is colder than 50°F or plants are not actively growing.
- Do not apply copper sulfate or other algaecides for 3 days before or after treatment.

**Water-use restrictions after treatment**
- 2,4-D granules: recreation immediately; not for irrigation, stock, or drinking during the season.
- Endothall: swimming after 1 day, eating fish after 3 days, irrigation or stock after 7 days; not for drinking water.
- Diquat: irrigation, recreation, or stock after 10 days; not for drinking water.
- Mixtures with 2,4,5-T or silvex: recreation immediately; not for irrigation during the season.

**Hazards**
- Many herbicides are toxic to fish and humans, but tolerance usually exceeds the level needed to kill weeds. Newly hatched fish are less tolerant than older fish.
- Tomatoes, grapes, and other broadleaved plants are very sensitive to 2,4-D and related chemicals, and spray drift can injure them at surprising distances.

**Environmental changes after control**
- Sudden removal of dense vegetation often triggers algal blooms (blue-green algae cause pea-soup green water), which can be more objectionable than the original weeds.
- Some fish (pickerel, golden shiners) depend on vegetation, so parts of the water should be left untreated.

**Summary conclusions**
- Aquatic plants provide habitat for fish and waterfowl, but unchecked growth conflicts with water uses.
- There is no easy cure-all. Dredging may help but can create hazards and fish-management problems.
- Chemical control can succeed, but regrowth requires diligent maintenance; periodic spot treatment is easier and cheaper than whole-area treatment later.
- The author concludes that current control techniques are "far from adequate" and that research is needed on methods that limit weeds without harming people or desirable organisms.
