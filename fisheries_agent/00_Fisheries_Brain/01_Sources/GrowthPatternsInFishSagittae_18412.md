# Growth Patterns in Fish Sagittae

#ichthyology #marine-biology #otoliths

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[Giorgio Pannella]] reviews the structure, calcification, and growth-increment patterns of fish sagittae (the largest of the three paired [[Otoliths]] in teleost ears). It argues that sagittae record a detailed life-history archive: daily, tidal, lunar, and seasonal growth patterns, plus growth discontinuities caused by stress, spawning, and habitat change. The chapter stresses that these records are only reliable once their microstructure is interpreted, and it outlines a working hypothesis linking pattern types to ecology. Fossil preservation is poor, limiting paleoecological use.

## Background & Description

- **Otolith terminology:** Three pairs of otoliths sit at the base of the semicircular canals: the lapillus, sagitta, and asteriscus. The sagitta is the largest and has been studied most intensively. Sagittae are generally concavo-convex, subelliptical, scutiform bodies ranging from millimeters to centimeters.
- **Composition:** Teleost otoliths are mainly [[Aragonite]] crystals within an organic matrix of otolin, a protein rich in aspartic and glutamic acid. Otolin is closer in amino acid composition to bivalve shell matrix than to vertebrate collagen (Table I).
- **Historical use:** [[Reibisch Age Estimation]] (1899) first proposed using sagittae to estimate fish age. Sagittae have also served taxonomic and phylogenetic studies.
- **Structure:** Each sagitta has a kernel (the prerhythmic nucleus, around which an opaque ring forms) and an incremental region. Growth is anisotropic, fastest along the anterior-posterior axis, so sections should be cut in the central plane along the maximum growth axis. Orientation should always be reported, using [[Werner Section Symbols]].
- **Growth phases:** Post-kernel growth proceeds through three stages: fast, nearly isotropic growth with few interruptions; reduced increment thickness with frequent interruptions; and slow growth with marked discontinuities.
- **Methods:** The [[Acetate-Peel Replica Technique]] (Pannella, 1971) made fine structure visible without ultrathin sectioning and opened the field of growth-pattern analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Calcification and physiology.** Otolith calcium deposition is part of whole-body [[Calcium Metabolism in Fish]]. Plasma calcium is about 300-350 mmol/L in marine teleosts and 250-275 mmol/L in freshwater species. Otoliths respond to periodic changes in diffusible calcium much as bone and scales do. Calcification proceeds in two stages: secretion of an organic matrix from sites linked to the endolymphatic sac, then CaCO3 deposition around organic fibers. Macular cells in the saccule appear to secrete calcium carbonate into the otolith fluid. Otoliths are not inert: resorption episodes have been recorded.

**Environmental drivers.** Temperature is probably the most important control on seasonal slowdown, with food supply of second-order importance. Salinity, pH, and ionic ratios also regulate internal water and salt balance and so calcification. Feeding is strongly linked to otolith growth; in [[Eel]] (Anguilla rostrata), for example, sagittae stop growing during starvation.

**Tides.** Tidal fluctuations in the study area are under about one foot in amplitude, yet they produce recognisable growth bands. Northern Puerto Rico has irregular semidaily tides; southern Puerto Rico has irregular daily tides, making the island useful for testing tidal effects.

**Habitat.** Reef and shallow-water fish show more discontinuities than pelagic forms. Deep-water fish generally have poorly defined increments. Tropical fish increments are thicker than those of temperate and cold-water species.

## Key Findings

**1. Pattern classification.** Following [[Pannella and MacClintock]] (1968), growth patterns fall into three types: private (one individual), universal (all members of a population), and semiuniversal (a fraction of a population). Table III separates periodic (subdaily, daily, multidaily), interruption, and aperiodic/random patterns.

**2. Subdaily patterns.** Subdaily lines occur in rapidly growing early increments, with 3–20 per 24 hr in *Lutjanus vivanus* and 3–8 in *Tilapia* sp. Planktic larval fish can show about 12 subdaily lines per daily increment, an aid to recognising larval stages.

**3. Daily increments.** In a published study of over 150 species, increments were classified by boundary sharpness into four types (indistinct; gradual or diffuse; distinct thick; distinct thin; see Table IV). Experiments on [[Mozambique Tilapia]] showed that a 24-hr light-dark cycle entrains the internal clock that produces daily rings. Experiments on marine species were less clear, and transplantation caused stress patterns. Some temperate annuli contained about 360 increments each, consistent with daily deposition.

**4. Lunar and tidal patterns.** In *Merluccius bilinearis*, fortnightly patterns consist of 6–7 thin daily increments followed by 6–8 thick ones, making 13–15-increment bands. In *Lachnolaimus maximus* and *Acanthurus chirurgus* from Puerto Rican reefs, discontinuities recur with periodicities of about 7, 14, and 27–28 increments. Pannella proposes that two periodicities (lunar and solar) may operate, producing 14- and 28-increment patterns. Spawning in tropical species may be cued to tides, as in [[California Grunion]] and *Lutjanus synagris* at full moon.

**5. Seasonal patterns and annuli.** Hyaline (winter/slow-growth) and opaque (summer/fast-growth) zones are visible in temperate sagittae under reflected light. Hyaline bands result from a higher otolin-to-aragonite ratio; in *Merluccius bilinearis*, winter increments drop to one tenth of summer thickness. Tropical fish have no winter zones, and the zones that do form have several causes (temperature of 4–6.5 °C annually, spawning, salinity, photoperiod). Counts of tropical zones often exceed plausible age, so they are not reliable annual markers without microstructural checks. Aging by annuli becomes difficult south of about 28–30° latitude.

**6. Discontinuities.** Growth discontinuities are typed in Table V by growth-rate change before and after the break (FS, SS, SF, FF, DD) and by erosional or non-erosional character. Stress events include starvation, storms, spawning, migration, and transplantation. In one *Tilapia* experiment, one day of intense thermal and anaerobic stress caused a severe FS-type discontinuity with resorption. A 4-hour transport stress produced a lesser FS disturbance. Marine fish appeared never to recover fully after transplantation.

**7. Ontogenetic and habitat trends.** Increment thickness decreases with age, and the thickest increments lie in the nucleus. Larval increments appear only in fish with pelagic larval stages. Discontinuities increase with age and are most frequent in reef and shallow-water fish. *Merluccius bilinearis* and *Urophycis chussor* show continuous early growth, whereas *Pseudopleuronectes americanus* shows discontinuities even early on.

**8. Ecological synthesis.** Table VI proposes a working hypothesis linking pattern type to information: subdaily patterns to activity and feeding cycles; daily increments to food, temperature, and rest-activity rhythms; tidal bands to tidally controlled food and migration; seasonal zones to season, latitude, and reproduction; discontinuities to stress type, periodicity, and duration. The author notes that this is preliminary and that Fourier and time-series analyses are needed.

**9. Fossil otoliths.** Fossil otoliths are common in Cenozoic and late Mesozoic sediments and are useful for taxonomy, bathymetry, and climate reconstruction. However, daily patterns were not visible in Cretaceous and Miocene fossils, and diagenesis rarely preserves them. Season-of-death and age determinations may still be possible.

**10. Conclusions on calcification.** Sagittae calcification is understood only within whole-body calcium metabolism. Otoliths respond to periodic changes in diffusible calcium, an organic precursor is required, endolymph physicochemistry controls deposition, and feeding influences endolymph composition and so sagitta growth.
