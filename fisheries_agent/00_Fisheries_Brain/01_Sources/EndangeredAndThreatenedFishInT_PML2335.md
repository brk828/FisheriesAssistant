# Endangered and Threatened Fish in the Yampa and Green Rivers of Dinosaur National Monument

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, from the *Proceedings of the First Conference on Scientific Research in the National Parks* (New Orleans, November 9–12, 1976; published as NPS Transactions and Proceedings Series No. 5, 1979), summarizes the status of endangered and threatened fish in the Yampa and Green Rivers within [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. It draws on a literature review and recent research by the [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]]. The authors report that the [[Colorado River]] system's four endemic fish species have declined mainly because of dam construction, altered flow and temperature regimes, competition with introduced fish, and related habitat changes. Native fish are declining across most of the upper basin, and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] has substantially altered the Green River downstream of it.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River system has been heavily modified during the 20th century, chiefly by dams. The four endemic species considered are the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The squawfish and humpback chub are federally listed as "endangered" (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, 1973); the bonytail and razorback were recommended for listing as "endangered" and "threatened," respectively, by the Desert Fishes Council.

The study area covers the Green River through the monument and the Yampa River, both of which originate as cold, clear mountain streams and flow through desert canyons. [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], completed in 1963 near the Utah–Wyoming border, converted the upper Green River from seasonally high, turbid flow to a colder, more stable regime with rapid daily fluctuations. The Yampa joins the Green at Echo Park, about 105 km below the dam. The study recognizes three habitat types plus a transitional section:

- **Green River above Echo Park:** cold-water environment controlled by the dam.
- **Yampa River:** relatively free-flowing, warm-water river.
- **Green River below the monument:** warm-water river largely recovered from dam effects, extending about 550 km to the confluence with the Colorado River in [[Canyonlands National Park]].
- **Green River between Echo Park and the lower monument boundary:** a gradient zone of about 42 km between cold and warm conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The native fish fauna of the Colorado River system is highly endemic. [[Miller]] (1959) recorded 20 native species, and [[Holden]] and [[Stalnaker]] (1975a) recorded 10 native and 19 introduced species in the main stems of the upper basin. Within the monument, [[Holden]] (1973) found all 19 native species and most introduced species, but did not find largemouth bass, bluegill, green sunfish, or walleye in high numbers. Introduced species are a continuing competitive and predatory threat.

Dams alter flow patterns, reduce scouring peak flows, lower summer and raise winter water temperatures, and reduce turbidity. Irrigation and dewatering increase salinity and dissolved solids. Reservoirs favor other species and are unsuitable for successful reproduction of large-river fish. Channelization and unstable banks from livestock grazing reduce backwaters and alter the invertebrate food supply. Pollution and sedimentation further degrade habitat.

The Colorado River is described as among the most utilized and contested rivers in the world. It supplies water to about 15 million people and carries major coal, oil shale, and uranium resources, so further development is a pressing concern.

## Key Findings

- **Introduced and new records:** Surveys recorded new or previously undocumented introduced species, including plains killifish (one specimen near the monument), Utah chub (one specimen at Box Elder), and sand shiners (Lily Park and the Little Snake River). Smallmouth bass were captured in the Green River at Horseshoe Bend and below the monument.
- **Declining populations:** Studies by the Unit between 1968 and 1972 found that most native fish are declining in abundance. In most areas, only adults of several native species remain, which suggests a lack of reproduction.
- **Colorado squawfish:** Once abundant across the system, now rare and restricted to parts of the upper basin. Adults are found in the Yampa and lower Green, but only a few individuals remain. Reproduction has declined steadily since the 1960s. Sexual maturity is reached at about 420 mm total length. Spawning has not been directly observed. Young were collected in the Green River in 1975, but few juveniles were found in 1970.
- **Humpback sucker:** Considered rare by most investigators. Spawning occurs on gravel bars in the Yampa, at Box Elder Park, and near Grand Junction, Colorado. Successful reproduction has not been documented in recent years; neither young nor juveniles were found despite repeated efforts. Hybridization with the razorback sucker appears to be increasing.
- **Humpback chub:** Previously known only from the Flaming Gorge basin and the Yampa near Dinosaur National Monument. Newly recorded localities include the Yampa and Green Rivers, where the species was found in eddies. The authors suggest it may be present in denser populations elsewhere in the basin.
- **Bonytail chub:** Formerly abundant, but numbers have sharply declined since the construction of Flaming Gorge Dam. Only a few individuals have been recorded in the lower Yampa and in the monument, and no bonytail were collected in the monument in the recent survey. The species appears close to extinction.
- **Flaming Gorge effects:** Large native fish disappeared from the 105 km reach below the dam because of cold discharge and were replaced by introduced [[Rainbow Trout]]. The Yampa, which retained its natural character, moderated the altered Green River enough to allow all pre-impoundment species to persist.
- **Conservation planning:** A Colorado Squawfish Recovery Team was formed in December 1975. The authors call for defining "critical habitat" under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 for aquatic species, and they argue that the Yampa is a potential refuge for endemic fish.
- **Management recommendations:** The authors urge that decisions on environmental alterations rest on sound ecological principles, and that interpretive programs in the monument highlight the ongoing evolution of its endemic fish, not just its geology.
