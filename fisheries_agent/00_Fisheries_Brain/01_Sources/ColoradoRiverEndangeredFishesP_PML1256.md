# Colorado River Endangered Fishes Program Report, 1982–1983

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This multi-author progress compilation reports on endangered big-river fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]] during 1982, with plans for 1983. It covers radiotelemetry of adult [[Colorado Squawfish]], larval and young-of-the-year (YOY) sampling, stocking of hatchery-reared squawfish, studies on the [[White River]] and [[Yampa River]], sediment transport and habitat modeling, propagation at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], and recovery coordination for the lower basin species ([[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]]). It also includes a genetics commentary on a possible razorback sucker hybrid and a short objection letter about a proposed water project. Many results are explicitly preliminary, and several reports were pending.

## Background & Description

The compilation brings together reports from the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[California Department of Fish and Game]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS), the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], and university researchers. Work was partly funded through the [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] framework, a Memorandum of Understanding with the Bureau of Land Management, and the Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program.

The focal species are four endangered big-river fishes:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): the largest native cyprinid of the basin and the principal radio-tracking target.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): a catostomid whose hatchery and reintroduction programs were expanding.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): aged from otoliths.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): monitored at Black Rocks and in the Grand Canyon area.

Hybrids of *Gila* species were also produced at Dexter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several findings describe habitat use and water conditions:

- **Backwaters and warm refugia.** Adult squawfish were drawn to the [[Walter Walker Wildlife Area]] during runoff. The area was often 5–8 degrees warmer than the Colorado River, and a thermal plume was evident at the outlet. Earlier FWS work had identified high-water side-channel backwaters as important young-squawfish habitat.
- **Lake Powell and the Gypsum Canyon area.** Water levels were appreciably lower in 1982 and 1981 than in 1980, so [[Gypsum Canyon]] became more lotic. The authors suggested these physical differences may have affected squawfish use of the area.
- **Downstream spawning reaches.** YOY squawfish were found only below [[Westwater Canyon]] on the Colorado River, while larvae occurred further upstream.
- **Sediment and flow.** On the Yampa River, most sediment moved early in the runoff season. Flows above about 12,000 cfs carried only about 7 percent of the total sediment load, but may flush sediment stored in eddies and bank irregularities.
- **Water chemistry, White River.** Normal copper concentration of about 10 ppb rose to roughly 600 ppb after storm runoff, and copper and zinc bioaccumulation was documented in fish tissue. The principal investigator doubted that squawfish were successfully spawning in the White River, citing armored, cemented substrates with poor interstitial space.

## Key Findings

### Radiotelemetry of Adult Colorado Squawfish

- **Colorado and Gunnison rivers (near Grand Junction).** Sixteen fish were implanted with transmitters in May and early June 1982, three of them from the Gunnison River. Transmitters were expected to last up to 18 months.
- **Long-distance movement.** One squawfish originally captured in [[Gypsum Canyon]] was located in July in the upper reach of Lake Powell, near [[Cataract Canyon]]. It was later found at Black Rocks in early August and near Clifton in late September. It covered nearly the entire barrier-free upper Colorado River, about 200 river miles, in less than one month.
- **Gypsum Canyon group.** Six fish were implanted in 1982. Five moved downstream after release and were later found in Lake Powell.
- **Yampa River (Dinosaur National Monument).** Of 12 radio-tagged fish, 12 were detected in Yampa Canyon by late June or early July. Trammel-net sampling (July 2 – August 7) collected adults between river miles 21 and 5. Peak spawning appeared to occur in the last week of July and first week of August 1982.
- **Post-spawning movements.** All four radio-tagged fish on the spawning bars were relocated upstream of their original capture sites.

### Larval and YOY Sampling

- **Larvae.** Qualitative larval sampling took place at 2–5 river-mile intervals during July and August. Larvae under 12 mm TL were found in several locations, including the most downstream area near Cataract Canyon.
- **Yampa River larvae.** In 1982, 12 larval squawfish were collected in the lower 11 miles of Yampa Canyon (August 21–23). Preliminary results suggest squawfish reproduction occurs in the lower Yampa.
- **Hypotheses for the YOY pattern.** Two explanations were proposed for the absence of YOY upstream: (1) larvae drifted downstream, or (2) larval mortality was high in upstream reaches. Both will be tested.

### Hatchery-Reared Squawfish Studies

- **Stocking.** About 30,000 fingerlings (35–85 mm) from Dexter were divided into six lots of 2,160 to 10,000 fish each and marked with unique coded microtags. They were stocked into four Colorado River backwaters and two flooded gravel pits.
- **Predator removal.** Predators were mechanically removed from one pit before stocking.
- **Predation.** Between 35 and 100 percent of the largemouth bass handled in each collection contained tagged squawfish. No bluegill did, while green sunfish and black crappie occasionally did. Tags were detected with an electronic device, avoiding sacrifice of predators.
- **Persistence.** Contrary to expectations, stocked fish remained in all four backwaters as water cooled.

### White River Studies (1982)

- **Capture and tagging.** 21 squawfish were captured in a 71.7-mile reach. Eight were implanted with transmitters (453–711 mm TL). All but one moved downstream after implantation.
- **Movements.** Contact with four fish was lost by early July. One moved to the Yampa River, one to Gray Canyon on the Green River, and one moved from the White River into the Green River.
- **Larval drift nets** (June–August) and seining caught no squawfish. Electrofishing catch rates ranged from 0.21 to 0.37 squawfish per hour.
- **Report.** A final report was due by February 1983.

### Dexter National Fish Hatchery Propagation (1982)

- **Razorback sucker.** About 7.3 million eggs yielded 1,660,000 fry. 570,000 fry were stocked in the Salt River, Arizona, and 310,000 were placed in rearing ponds, producing 48,000 fingerlings (15.7 percent return).
- **Colorado squawfish.** 33 females were spawned. Fry totaled 605,000; 400,000 were stocked into rearing ponds, with 33,000 returned (8.32 percent return). OCR-degraded egg counts were not reliably recoverable.
- **Hybrids.** Six crosses involving *Gila* species were produced.
- **1983 plans.** Target production includes 100,000 razorback fingerlings, 100,000 squawfish fingerlings, and 150,000–200,000 squawfish for studies and stockings in Colorado and Utah.

### Bonytail Chub, Razorback Sucker and Age Determination

- Dr. Bruce Taubert aged two bonytail chubs from otoliths as 32 and 39 annuli. Additional specimens were awaiting reading.
- Taubert confidently aged 17 razorback suckers at 30–32 annuli. Ages for all fish, including those without confidence readings, ranged from 20 to 41 annuli.
- Arizona Game and Fish planned razorback fry introductions to Salt River tributaries in March 1983 and established permanent low-flow monitoring stations, sampled twice yearly.

### Upper Basin Studies

- **Larval and juvenile sampling.** A Colorado Division of Wildlife crew sampled a 95-km reach of the Yampa River and a 32-km reach of the Colorado River. In 1981, 23 larval squawfish came from the Yampa and one from the Colorado, indicating a spawning period of June 25 – August 3 on the Yampa. No juvenile (age class I) squawfish were found.
- **Red shiners** dominated Yampa collections; only six of 17 species taken on the Colorado in 1981 were native.
- **Hydraulic geometry and sediment transport (USGS).** Peak total sediment transport on the upstream Box Elder site was estimated at roughly 62,000 tons per day when streamflow was about 7,400 cfs. Total load for April–September 1982 was about 90 percent of the annual load.
- **Yampa Canyon (Colorado State University).** The river is controlled primarily by geology. Sediment supply and the sand bedload are key concerns, and the bed pavement would not break under reduced flow scenarios.

### Water for Energy Program

- The program developed a flow model (IHYDROS, Bureau of Reclamation) and a habitat model (PHABSIM). The habitat model was operational, but the linkage and temperature modeling still needed development. EPA funding was withdrawn for the final year, and model completion was stalled pending new funds.

### Policy and Management

- **Dinosaur National Monument water rights.** A court ruled that the National Park Service must define flow requirements needed to preserve native fishes within six months, and that recreational considerations were not a legitimate factor.
- **Program cutbacks.** All agencies were reducing Colorado River endangered-fish programs for 1983. FWS planned reduced investigative teams at Grand Junction and Vernal.
- **Lower Basin consortium.** The inaugural meeting (August 1982, Arizona) formed an ad hoc committee to coordinate recovery actions for the four big-river fishes. A stocking of 25,000 fingerlings into Lake Mohave backwaters was agreed for June 1983.
- **Arizona and California.** Each agreed to accept 100,000 squawfish fingerlings in 1983. California obtained $30,000 in funding to build four quarter-acre grow-out ponds.

### Genetics and Hybridization

- A UCLA electrophoretic study of razorback and flannelmouth suckers was underway, expected to take six weeks.
- A UNLV letter (June 2, 1982) reidentified a Lake Mohave specimen as a backcross between a razorback sucker and a razorback × flannelmouth sucker hybrid. Lateral-line scale counts (86–87) were at the upper range for razorback suckers. The author concluded that a nuchal keel does not reliably indicate genetic purity and suggested introgression of flannelmouth sucker genes into the Lake Mohave population.

### Correspondence

- A water district general manager objected to a proposed water project affecting the Colorado River, and requested that the FWS prepare an environmental impact statement under NEPA.
