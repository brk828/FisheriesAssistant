# Yaqui Chub Genetics and Morphology After Recovery Efforts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study assesses the genetic and morphological status of the endangered [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*), a small minnow that was nearly extirpated from the United States in the late 1960s. Most of its habitat dried up, but fish were moved to [[Leslie Creek]] shortly before [[Astin Spring]] failed in 1969. Captive propagation at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH) then supplied stock for reintroductions into reclaimed habitats on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]. The authors used [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] and [[Principal Components Analysis]] to compare populations with their management histories.

Genetic variation was considerable, and it was largely attributable to documented or inferred founder effects and bottlenecks rather than to hatchery selection. Morphology was generally uniform across time and space. The authors found no evidence of changes from captive manipulation or ecophenotypic responses to novel environments. The species reproduces quickly and establishes large populations from small stocks.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Gila purpurea* (Girard), a small minnow restricted to a single tributary system of the upper [[Rio Yaqui Basin]] in Sonora, Mexico, and Arizona, USA. It is listed as endangered by the [[US Fish And Wildlife Service]] (USFWS).
- **Context:** Captive breeding of endangered fishes raises concern about genetic and morphological change, which could compromise successful reintroduction. The authors cite a USFWS estimate that 35 southwestern species require artificial propagation, and they consider that figure too low. At least 25 fishes in the region are already extinct.
- **Taxonomy:** [[Hendrickson]] and colleagues (1981) noted that chubs from western parts of the range were distinct. DeMarais (1991) proposed that all but the Rio San Bernardino populations represent a new species, *Gila eremica*. The holotype and [[Syntype]] specimens were collected from the San Bernardino area, including [[Black Draw]].
- **Historical distribution:** Chubs were once known across the upper Rio Yaqui and tributaries of the Rios Sonora and Mitape. A possible record from the endorheic Sulphur Springs Valley (Arizona) is unsubstantiated, since the specimens were destroyed in the 1906 San Francisco earthquake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat decline.** Chub decline began with regional arroyo cutting in the late 1800s. Floodplain wetlands and streamside cienegas were drained except where springs or artesian wells sustained flow. By 1965, chubs survived in Astin Spring. The largest remaining stock, estimated at 20 or fewer individuals, persisted in marshes fed by an artesian well until about 1976.

**Refuge and management.** The Nature Conservancy acquired the San Bernardino Ranch in 1979. The USFWS took ownership in 1991, establishing a refuge of approximately 1000 ha. The refuge aims to secure viable populations of seven native Rio Yaqui fishes, four of which are federally listed. Management includes clearing emergent vegetation from springs and ponds, removing non-native fishes, and excluding livestock.

**Co-managed species.** Re-established or planned populations include the [[Sonoran Topminnow]], [[Yaqui Sucker]], [[Beautiful Shiner]], and [[Yaqui Catfish]].

**Threats.** Non-native [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) invaded [[House Pond]], where they are incompatible with Yaqui topminnows. Managers drained and poisoned the pond in 1984–85 before restocking it with chubs from [[North Pond]] in 1986. Leslie Creek was proposed for a reservoir project in 1970–72, which was later shelved. The watershed was judged too small to support an impoundment. A catastrophic drought in 1971–76 then nearly eliminated the population.

## Key Findings

### Population history and recovery
- **1969:** About 200 adults each of Yaqui chub and Sonoran topminnow were transferred from Astin Spring to Leslie Creek, where both species established populations. The Yaqui sucker was extirpated from the US when Astin Spring dried.
- **1976:** About 225 chubs were moved to the DNFH, New Mexico, as insurance against extinction. These fish reproduced successfully in captivity.
- **1980:** Dexter stock was released into aquatic habitats on the San Bernardino Ranch. More than 30,000 fish went into House Pond and about 1100 into North Pond. Fifty fish went to Black Draw. The House Pond and North Pond stockings succeeded, and the Black Draw stocking was not recorded until 1987.
- **1986:** About 8600 individuals from Dexter were stocked into West Turkey Creek, after the captive stock was no longer needed for secure populations on the refuge.

### Allozyme results
- **Sampling:** Muscle and liver tissues were collected in April 1990 from five localities: Black Draw, Leslie Creek, North Pond, House Pond, and Turkey Creek. Thirty-two presumptive loci were screened, and 6 were polymorphic.
- **Heterozygosity:** Observed values ranged from 0.037 ± 0.018 (Leslie Creek) to 0.060 ± 0.029 (House Pond and Turkey Creek). These values were comparable to the marine and freshwater fish mean of 0.051 ± 0.034 reported by Nevo (1978) and the cyprinid mean of 0.038 ± 0.008 reported by Avise (1977).
- **Allele patterns:** Leslie Creek lacked one of three sACON-A alleles present in all other populations. House Pond, North Pond, and Turkey Creek shared the same alleles at each locus. Black Draw had the highest number of alleles per locus and the most polymorphic loci because it carried uncommon alleles at PGM-A and PEP-D.
- **Structure:** Significant spatial heterogeneity was found at 4 of 6 polymorphic loci and across all loci. Population subdivision was relatively minor, however, and about 96% of gene diversity occurred within samples rather than among them.
- **Founder effects:** Allele frequencies in North Pond did not differ significantly from Leslie Creek fish in the source stock, indicating little differentiation through captive propagation. The authors attribute the missing sACON-A allele to a probable genetic bottleneck in Leslie Creek during the drought.
- **Black Draw:** Black Draw fish probably did not descend exclusively from the Dexter stock. Their distinct alleles suggest a separate origin, possibly immigration from Mexico.

### Morphology results
- **Measurements:** Twenty-six morphometric characters were recorded on 144 preserved specimens. Variables were sheared by body size before principal components analysis.
- **Size factor:** The first principal component (PC1) explained 95.66% of total variance as a size factor. It did not separate samples.
- **Shape:** Sheared components H2 and H3 showed no divergence among populations. Temporal samples from Black Draw and Astin Spring were similarly uniform. Only the 1989 Black Draw collection diverged on H2.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** The authors interpret the main H2 variation as sexual dimorphism, mainly from pectoral and pelvic fin length. Variation among samples likely reflects differences in sexual maturity and reproductive condition.
- **Syntypes:** The syntypes differ morphologically from most preserved material. Only two of 133 preserved specimens (from 1943 and 1976) fell within the polygon encompassing 11 syntypes. Preservation effects on linear measurements complicate this comparison.

### Life history and reproduction
- **Growth:** In 1980, more than 30,000 adults and young were recovered from North Pond, which had been stocked in 1976 with 200 adults (rate of increase = 3.56). In 1986, more than 8600 fish were present in a comparable pond stocked with 100 adults (rate = 9.27). Both populations received only infrequent supplemental feeding.
- **Spawning:** Spawning appears prolonged, from spring to October or early November, and may occur year-round in artesian-fed habitats. The species is likely a multiple spawner.
- **Maturity:** Individuals reach reproductive size at about 40 mm total length within three months of hatching. Spring- and summer-spawned fish are reproductive by late autumn. Under optimal conditions, the species may produce up to three generations per year.
- **Longevity:** Maximum body size is about 150 mm, with a life expectancy of three to four years.

### Management implications
- Genetic diversity within stocks remains high, and differentiation among populations is low. The authors attribute this to the species' high reproductive rate and to founder effects that reduced diversity in some stocks.
- Stocks from a single source population were sufficient to establish self-sustaining populations, and the species was considered well suited to captive propagation because of its rapid growth and adaptability.
- The authors recommend genetic monitoring of populations intended for reintroduction to help track diversity during recovery.
