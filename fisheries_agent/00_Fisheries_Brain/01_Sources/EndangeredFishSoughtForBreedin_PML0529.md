# Bonytail Chub Rescue Search in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

A newspaper report describes a weeklong search by about 50 state and federal researchers on [[Lake Mohave]] for the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*[[Gila Elegans]]*), one of the rarest vertebrates in North America. Teams worked from five radio-connected camps along the Arizona and Nevada shores, aiming to find even one surviving fish to improve the genetic diversity of captive breeding stock at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico. The article explains that the species has not reproduced successfully in the wild for decades, that its captive population is descended from a small number of adults, and that the wider native "big river" fish community of the [[Colorado River]] has collapsed since the river was dammed.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The bonytail chub (*Gila elegans*) is described as one of the rarest vertebrates in North America and is listed as endangered (listed in 1980).
- **Native fish community:** The article names four native "big river" fish now threatened with extinction: the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], the prehistoric-looking [[Razorback Sucker]], the [[Humpback Chub]], and the bonytail chub.
- **Historic abundance:** Fifty years ago all four were common throughout the lower Colorado River basin, including the Salt and Gila rivers. As late as 1949, a commercial fishery at [[Saguaro Lake]] harvested razorbacks by the wagonload using pitchforks.
- **Cause of decline:** Damming of the Colorado River changed it from fast, muddy river to regulated waterways connecting idle reservoirs. The introduction of sport fish, mainly trout and several bass species, further eliminated the natives.
- **Captive population:** Surviving bonytails are held at the Dexter hatchery, which specializes in endangered species, and at other research facilities. The Dexter fish are offspring of 10 adults netted in Lake Mohave for breeding in 1981.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reproduction:** None of the native species has reproduced successfully in the wild for at least 35 years. No evidence of wild bonytail reproduction in Lake Mohave has been found.
- **Population trend:** Since 1974, 36 adult bonytails have been seen in the wild. Biologists estimate the average age of surviving wild bonytails at more than 40 years.
- **Habitat threats:** Researchers say the reservoir has become habitable mainly for non-native predators such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and catfish, which prey on young bonytails. Bonytail young, if released into the reservoir, would likely be eaten before reaching maturity.
- **Genetic concern:** The captive stock descends from very few wild adults. The original adults have now died, and researchers worry the surviving offspring may not carry enough genetic variation to sustain the species.
- **Non-native fish control:** Each year since 1983, researchers have removed or transplanted large numbers of non-native fish. The article reports that about 19,000 fish were handled last year, though the species is partly illegible in the scan.

## Key Findings

- **Genetic rescue:** The search aims to locate at least one surviving bonytail to add to the Dexter breeding program. Researchers say finding one fish would help but would not guarantee recovery.
- **Scale of effort:** The team of about 50 people, operating from five camps, is the largest bonytail search assembled in Arizona to date, according to the article.
- **Recovery plan:** A soon-to-be-released recovery plan drafted by federal researchers is described as "sort of a condor plan," after the [[California Condor]] recovery program, which maintains the species entirely in captivity.
- **Expert views:** [[Bill Kepner]], a fisheries biologist for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], warned that the captive offspring may not be enough to perpetuate the species. [[Lee Minckley]], an Arizona State University researcher, described the bonytail as likely to be lost, saying the fish's recovery is otherwise doubtful.
- **Immediate challenges:** Searchers face rough conditions on the lake, and they use electrofishing and trammel nets. Sampling so far has produced no bonytail captures in the first days, and the search is expensive.
- **Outlook:** The article concludes that the bonytail population may already be near extinction, and that even a successful find would leave scientists uncertain about long-term survival.
