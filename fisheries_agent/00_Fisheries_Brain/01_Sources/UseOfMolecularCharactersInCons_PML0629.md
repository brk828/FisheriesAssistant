# Use of Molecular Characters in Conservation Biology: Implications for Management of the Red Wolf

#ichthyology #marine-biology #canidae

## Executive Summary

This letter, by [[Dowling]], [[Minckley]], [[Douglas]], [[Marsh]], and [[DeMarais]] (Arizona State University), responds to critiques of their earlier letter on the taxonomic status of the [[Red Wolf]] (*Canis rufus*). The authors argue that molecular data, especially [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA), cannot by themselves settle questions of species status or hybrid origin. They contend that the red wolf is morphologically distinct from the [[Coyote]] and [[Gray Wolf]] and deserves protection under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) whatever its origin. The letter rejects [[Wayne and Jenks 1991]] claims that red wolves are a hybrid swarm, and it challenges the view that genetic divergence measures species status. It also gives a broader argument for conserving distinctive evolutionary entities regardless of species-concept debates.

## Background & Description

- **Context:** [[Wayne and Jenks 1991]] used mtDNA variation to argue for extensive hybridization in the red wolf. Their work was discussed by [[Gittleman and Pimm 1991]], [[O'Brien and Mayr 1991]], and [[Rennie 1991]], and it became part of a wider controversy over the red wolf's status and conservation.
- **Scope:** The letter addresses two linked issues: (1) the use of genetic characters to identify species and hybrids, and (2) the role of [[Hybridization]] in evolution. Both bear on how the ESA treats hybrids and species.
- **Responses in the same issue:** [[Nowak 1992]] concluded that the red wolf is neither a product of recent coyote-gray wolf hybridization nor a taxon of hybrid origin, but a descendant of an intermediate stage in wolf evolution. [[Phillips and Henry 1992]] reached views largely in line with the authors'. [[Wayne 1992]] criticized the earlier letter.
- **Scale of the dispute:** The letter describes the debate as concerning the red wolf and, more broadly, the conservation of endangered taxa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Molecular limits:** Molecular methods add character sets for testing divergence and phylogeny, but they do not replace other data. Typically only a small fraction of the genome is sampled, so a failure to find diagnostic markers does not show conspecificity.
- **mtDNA rate issue:** Wayne and Jenks expected mtDNA differences based on a "standard" rate calibrated from primates (Hillis and Moritz 1990). The authors note that a lower mtDNA evolutionary rate in wolves relative to primates would explain the lack of divergence. Rate variation has been documented in turtles ([[Avise et al. 1992]]) and sharks ([[Martin et al. 1992]]).
- **Gene-tree problems:** Single-gene or linked-gene phylogenies can differ from species phylogenies because of random lineage sorting (Pamilo and Nei 1988). Mitochondrial DNA lineages can also be discordant with organismal phylogenies (Neigel and Avise 1986).
- **Fish examples:** Pupfishes (genus *Cyprinodon*) are morphologically distinct yet show little allozyme or mtDNA divergence (Turner 1974; Echelle and Dowling 1992). Plethodontid salamanders are morphologically similar yet highly divergent molecularly (Larson 1989). The authors cite a cyprinid fish case, [[DeMarais et al. 1992]], in which [[Gila Seminuda]] arose through introgressive hybridization.

## Key Findings

1. **Molecular data are incomplete for species status.** Lack of diagnostic mtDNA characters does not show conspecificity. Genetic divergence levels are not valid measures of specific status, since speciation may involve very few genetic changes.

2. **Red wolves are morphologically distinct.** Multivariate analysis of cranial measurements identified the red wolf as distinct from both coyote and gray wolf ([[Nowak 1992]]). The authors argue that Wayne ignored this morphological evidence.

3. **Hybrid origin remains unresolved.** The authors "neither rejected nor accepted" a hybrid origin. They argue the available data are insufficient to distinguish competing hypotheses. Morphological intermediacy is neither necessary nor sufficient to demonstrate hybrid origin. Most convincing tests would use codominant, single-locus nuclear markers ([[Phillips and Henry 1992]]).

4. **Hybrid taxa versus hybrid individuals.** The letter defines a hybrid as an individual of mixed ancestry from a single mating of two taxa. A hybrid taxon is a self-perpetuating group following an independent evolutionary trajectory. The ESA protects recognized entities regardless of origin. Genetically distinct hybrid taxa should not be denied protection because of mixed ancestry.

5. **Taxonomic decisions are left to systematists.** The authors say that mammalian systematists should decide the red wolf's taxonomic status. Conservation biologists should not let species-concept disagreements block efforts to protect evolutionary entities.

6. **Management guidance:** Where sufficient numbers persist to preserve genetic integrity, hybrids and conditions promoting hybridization may be removed. For rare taxa, all individuals may need protection even if introgressed (Echelle 1991). The authors would argue for preserving hybrids of any kind if no other means exist to prevent extinction of a lineage.

7. **Florida panther precedent:** Introduction of South American cougars has introgressed their mtDNA into the endangered [[Florida Panther]] (*Felis concolor coryi*). The authors support continued protection because of the panther's rarity and distinctiveness, despite introgression.

8. **Conservation principle:** A central goal of conservation biology is to preserve the diversity of life, which requires considering evolutionary potential as well as taxonomy. The authors conclude that, whatever the red wolf's origin, it must continue to be protected.
