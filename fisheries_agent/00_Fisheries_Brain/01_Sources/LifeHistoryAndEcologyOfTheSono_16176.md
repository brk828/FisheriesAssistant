## Executive Summary

This preliminary report presents five years (1990–1994) of mark-recapture data on an isolated population of the [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] (*Kinosternon sonoriense*) inhabiting [[West Turkey Creek]] and associated stock tanks in the Chiricahua Mountains of southeastern Arizona. Although the study organism is a freshwater turtle rather than a fish, it occupies the same aquatic habitats (stream pools and farm ponds) often studied in fish ecology and offers a rich dataset on growth, reproduction, and demography of a long-lived aquatic vertebrate. Key results include a female-biased adult sex ratio (1:1.29), rapid juvenile growth (~17.5 mm carapace length/yr), minimum female maturity at age 5, average clutch size of 6.7 eggs strongly correlated with body size, and evidence that at least 23% of females produce two clutches per season. The authors also document egg morphometrics, yolk lipid composition, and raise concern that introduced [[Bullfrog|Bullfrogs]] may be suppressing hatchling survival and long-term population stability.

## Background & Description

*Kinosternon sonoriense* is the only native freshwater turtle occurring in the Chiricahua Mountains and ranges more broadly from western New Mexico through central/southern Arizona into northwestern Mexico. Prior ecological work on the species (Hulse, 1982; Rosen, 1987) left major gaps in knowledge of nesting ecology and age-specific biology. This study, initiated in 1990 and still ongoing at time of publication, sought to:

1. Document long-term life-history trait values (growth, maturity, clutch size/frequency, egg composition).
2. Identify sources of intraspecific variation in these traits.
3. Provide baseline demographic data to support conservation and management of long-lived aquatic organisms.

**Study Area:** ~3 km reach of West Turkey Creek (WTC) at ~1675 m elevation on the El Coronado and Rock Creek Ranches, plus associated stock tanks (farm ponds) fed by less-permanent WTC tributaries. A second site, Rock Creek, isolated from WTC by ~5 km, was added in 1994.

**Methods:** Turtles were captured by hand, dip net, and baited hoop traps; individually marked via marginal scute notching; measured (carapace length [CL], plastron length, shell height/width) and weighed. Radiographs were used to determine clutch size (CS), egg widths (XREW), and pelvic aperture width, and to establish minimum size/age at sexual maturity — a technique borrowed from freshwater turtle reproductive ecology (Gibbons & Greene, 1979). Growth rings on plastral scutes were used to age juveniles (<9 rings). Oxytocin injection was used experimentally to induce oviposition in 15 females at Rock Creek in 1994.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** ~90% of captures occurred in stock tanks (lentic, pond-like habitats), versus only 5% in stream pools and 5% on land — suggesting a strong preference for lentic habitats, possibly reflecting the species' evolutionary history in the now-desiccated pluvial [[Lake Cochise]] (which began drying ~10,000 years ago), isolating populations in permanent streams like WTC.
- **Historical biogeography:** Lake Cochise, once covering much of Sulphur Springs Valley 24,000 years ago, likely served as ancestral habitat; its desiccation is proposed to explain the turtles' continued preference for still-water habitats over flowing stream beds.
- **Invasive species threat:** [[Bullfrog|Bullfrogs]] (*Lithobates catesbeianus*) were introduced into the WTC drainage circa 1983 and have since reached high population densities. Bullfrogs are known predators of turtle hatchlings (Schwalbe & Rosen, 1988), raising concern that they may reduce recruitment of age-class-1 juveniles enough to destabilize the *K. sonoriense* population, since long-lived organisms require high juvenile survivorship to maintain stable populations.
- **Reproductive phenology tied to monsoon climate:** Nesting is hypothesized to be concentrated during the July–August monsoon rains, with females retaining shelled eggs in the oviduct until conditions are suitable for terrestrial nesting. Embryonic diapause likely postpones development through fall/winter, with hatching the following spring/summer — implying an incubation-to-emergence period that may exceed 11 months, paralleling patterns seen in [[Kinosternon Baurii]] and [[Deirochelys Reticularia]].
- **Comparative population context:** WTC turtles attain larger maximum body sizes and grow faster than other Arizona *K. sonoriense* populations (e.g., Sharp Spring: ~10 mm/yr vs. WTC's 17.5 mm/yr), suggesting site-specific environmental quality (possibly linked to stock-tank/lentic habitat productivity) strongly influences growth and life-history outcomes.

## Key Findings

### Population Structure & Demographics
- Total of 580 individuals marked; 913 recaptures over 1990–1994.
- Capture habitat breakdown: 90% stock tanks, 5% stream pools, 5% land.
- Of 573 live captures: 18.5% juveniles, 35.8% adult males, 45.7% adult females.
- Adult sex ratio: 1 male : 1.29 females (significantly female-biased; χ² = 6.93, p < 0.01).
- Juvenile proportion (~18.5%) is roughly half that reported for a 1970s Tule Creek population, a possible signal of reduced recruitment.

### Growth & Body Size
- Hatchlings (n=4, captured early August): mean mass 3.0 g; mean CL 22.2 mm.
- Growth from hatching to age 6 was approximately linear, averaging **17.5 mm CL/yr** — faster than other Arizona *K. sonoriense* populations (e.g., Sharp Spring ~10 mm/yr) and faster than *[[Kinosternon Hirtipes]]* from Mexico (~14.0 mm/yr).
- Adult females: mean CL 130 mm (max 160 mm); mean mass 340 g (max 610 g).
- Adult males: mean CL 128 mm (max 168 mm); mean mass 298 g (max 660 g).
- No significant sexual size dimorphism between age classes 5–10 yr (F=0.88, p=0.35).
- WTC population ranks among the largest-bodied *K. sonoriense* populations studied in Arizona.

### Sexual Maturity
- Minimum age at maturity for females: **5 years**; smallest gravid female: 106 mm CL, 150 g.
- This matches most other Arizona *K. sonoriense* populations and *K. hirtipes* (6 yr) from Mexico, though literature shows substantial within/between-population variation (5–9 yr at some sites), highlighting the difficulty of estimating maturity without long-term mark-recapture data.

### Reproduction & Clutch Dynamics
- Overall mean clutch size: **6.7 eggs** (range 2–11; n=231 clutches), significantly and positively related to female body size (Spearman's RHO = 0.62, p<0.05).
- Body size (CL) explained ~42% of variation in clutch size; a 75 mm range in adult female CL corresponded to an average increase of 5 eggs.
- Minimum of 23% of adult females (9 of 39 tracked) produced ≥2 clutches within a season; minimum inter-clutch interval was 20 days.
- Clutch frequency estimates vary widely across populations and studies (1–4 clutches/year reported elsewhere), underscoring the need for long-term data.
- No significant difference between actual measured egg widths (mean 16.85 mm) and radiograph-derived widths (XREW, mean 16.94–16.95 mm; paired t-test, p>0.469), validating radiography as a non-invasive proxy for egg size.

### Egg & Pelvic Morphometrics
- Egg dimensions: mean length 28.93 mm, mean width 16.85 mm (length:width ratio ≈1.72).
- XREW significantly and positively related to CL of females; larger females produce wider (larger) eggs.
- **No pelvic constraint on egg width detected**: slope of pelvic aperture width vs. CL (0.144) was significantly steeper (~3.5×) than the slope of egg width vs. CL (0.041) (Wilks' Lambda test, p=0.0001) — contrasting with species like *[[Chrysemys Picta Marginata]]* and *[[Deirochelys Reticularia]]* where pelvic constraint on egg size is evident.
- Negative relationship found between XREW and residuals of the clutch size–CL regression (RHO=-0.2104, p=0.0008), suggesting a trade-off between egg size and clutch size independent of body size.
