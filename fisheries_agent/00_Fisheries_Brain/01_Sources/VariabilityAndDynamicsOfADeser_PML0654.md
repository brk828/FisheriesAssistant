# Variability and Dynamics of a Desert Stream Fish Community

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study uses a 30-year record from [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, to test how well different metrics detect long-term change in a native desert stream fish assemblage, and what environmental drivers explain that change. The creek is intrinsically variable, with [[Flash Floods]] and temperature extremes, yet it still supports one of the few intact native fish assemblages in the upper [[Sonoran Desert]]. The authors compared four community-change measures ([[Persistence Index]], [[Kendall's W]], [[Time Lag Regression]], and [[Detrended Correspondence Analysis]]) and related community shifts to base flow, flood events, and [[Exotic Species]]. They conclude that community change was driven mainly by chronic shifts in base flow in the early 1980s and by exotic species later, not by natural environmental extremes. Conservation of remnant native fishes therefore requires both isolation from invaders and maintenance of natural [[Flow Variability]].

## Background & Description

**Study system.** Aravaipa Creek drains roughly 1,500 km² (1,409 km² above the USGS gauge near Mammoth). It is divided into upper, canyon, and lower reaches. The lower 8 km is ephemeral at modal discharge, and surface flow usually sinks before joining the [[San Pedro River]] at about 650 m elevation. Modal discharge is about 3.4 m³/s, and major floods are common. Much of the canyon is a federally designated Wilderness Area, and The Nature Conservancy owns much of the adjacent land.

**Native fauna.** Seven native fishes occur in the creek. Two are federally listed as threatened: [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*). Five are candidate species: [[Headwater Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), Desert Sucker (*Catostomus clarkii*), Sonora Sucker (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), and Speckled Dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*). Historically, the southwestern native assemblage was species-poor, with rarely more than five species at any site.

**Exotic fauna.** Eight exotic species have been recorded since the early 1980s, including Fathead Minnow, Green Sunfish, two bullheads, Common Carp, Western Mosquitofish, and Largemouth Bass. The [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) was first found in 1990 in the lower reach. It is of special concern because it has been implicated in native fish declines elsewhere in the Southwest.

**Sampling.** Sampling began in 1963 under W. L. Minckley and colleagues, using double-weighted seines. Sites were not fixed, because scouring rearranges habitat, so effort was distributed across habitat diversity in each reach. Analyses used spring (March–April) surveys to limit recruitment noise. Data were pooled within reaches, and the analysis period was 1975–2000 for the upper and lower reaches and 1970–2000 for the canyon. Flow records came from USGS gauge 09473000 (1965–1999).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Intrinsic variability.** Sonoran Desert flash floods can change flow by several orders of magnitude within hours, scour channels, raise turbidity, and shift water temperature by more than 15 °C. Drought can push water temperatures above 40 °C and leave pools stagnant and algae-choked.
- **Anthropogenic pressure.** Native southwestern fish populations are declining largely from habitat alteration and introduced species. [[Base Flow]] modification is among the most widespread human disturbances in streams, because it alters depth, velocity, and substrate, which in turn reshape fish communities.
- **Flow–exotic link.** Flash floods differentially remove lentic-adapted nonnative species, so flood regulation may increase the vulnerability of native communities to invasion. Stabilized flows behind dams have been linked to easier colonization by invasive species.
- **Geomorphic change.** The large 1983 flood rearranged channel morphology near the confluence with the San Pedro River. Afterward, the downstream connection, previously limited to high-runoff years, became common in winter, increasing opportunities for exotic fishes to enter.

## Key Findings

**1. Flow regime changed substantially.** Mean daily minimum flow rose from 2.3 cfs (0.0g m³/s as printed in the scan) between 1965 and 1977 to 10.7 cfs (0.30 m³/s) between 1978 and 1999, roughly fourfold. The seasonal cycle was dampened, so recent years had higher and more stable base flows. In 1983, daily mean flow jumped from 67 cfs (1.9 m³/s) to 16,000 cfs (453.4 m³/s) within two days, then returned to normal by the fifth day. Increased base flow was attributed to more winter precipitation from 1978 onward and reduced farming and groundwater irrigation.

**2. Community composition shifted, but native persistence stayed high.**
- Persistence values for all reaches were ≥0.90, indicating high species persistence from 1965 to 1999.
- The lower reach had the lowest persistence, linked to the rarity of *M. fulgida* in recent years and to its high temperature fluctuations, extreme discharge, and high exotic proportion.
- Concordance of species rank abundances was significant in all reaches (Kendall's W). W was high (>0.70) for the canyon and lower reaches but lower (0.48) for the upper reach, driven by a large rise in *M. fulgida* and a matching decline in *C. clarkii*.

**3. Directional change was detected in two reaches.**
- Time lag regression showed significant directional change in the upper and canyon reaches, where community vectors diverged as time lags increased. The upper reach showed the steepest and most directed change.
- The lower reach showed no linear trend. It shifted in the 1990s back toward a 1970s-like assemblage dominated by *A. chrysogaster*, a pattern visible only in the DCA time vectors.
- DCA plots agreed: upper and canyon reaches moved from *A. chrysogaster* dominance (1970s) to more even composition (1980s) to high *A. chrysogaster* and *M. fulgida* (1990s).

**4. Metrics differ in sensitivity.** Persistence and concordance proved too coarse for conservation monitoring. Persistence is influenced by detection of rare species and does little to predict loss, while the other metrics captured directional shifts. The authors support the view that no single statistic detects change; an array of methods is needed.

**5. Drivers differed by period and reach.**
- Early-1980s changes were synchronous across the whole creek, suggesting a system-wide driver such as hydrology. Regression trees identified [[Base Flow]] (minimum daily flow near 0.25 m³/s, or 8.7 cfs) as the best predictor in the upper and canyon reaches, and declines in *A. chrysogaster* began in the late 1970s.
- In the lower reach, the best predictor was year (break between 1990 and 1991). Exotic proportion and first occurrence of *C. lutrensis* explained the remaining structure, with the lower reach diverging from the rest of the system in 1990, the year of highest exotic proportion and first *C. lutrensis* record.

**6. Exotic proportions remained low, but flow variability appeared to suppress them.**
- Upper reach: exotics contributed under 0.5% of the community.
- Canyon reach: exotics contributed under 1% in any year.
- Lower reach: exotics have been present since the early 1960s, with contributions varying between 0% and roughly 3% (the exact upper bound is garbled in the scan).
- Exotic richness and proportion decreased with higher flow variability, following a few years of very high peak flow. Results were not statistically significant, and the proportional data could not separate native increase from exotic decline.

**7. Floods had no direct effect on natives.** The 1978, 1983, and later large floods did not produce detectable changes in the native assemblage, consistent with earlier work showing that these fishes are resilient to floods unless populations are already stressed or rare.

**8. Recent *A. chrysogaster* recovery may reflect habitat recovery.** No hydrograph change explained the 1990s resurgence of *A. chrysogaster*. The authors hypothesize that the 1983 flood altered habitat and that a recovery period preceded a large year class. Bar formation and revegetation were documented by 1993, but no quantitative habitat data exist to test this.

**9. Conservation implications.**
- Community change was driven by chronic base-flow change in the early 1980s and by exotics more recently, not by natural extremes.
- Connectivity with the San Pedro River increases exotic entry, so isolation of remnant native assemblages may be critical.
- Protecting these fishes requires preventing invasions and maintaining or restoring the natural flow regime, which limits exotic persistence.

**10. Methodological limits.** Proportional data cannot distinguish native increase from exotic decline. Rare species such as *T. cobitis* may be underrepresented because low abundance has little effect on proportions. Sampling was non-standardized across crews and motivations, which the authors addressed by analyzing presence/absence and proportional composition.
