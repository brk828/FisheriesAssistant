# Lower Colorado River Basin Fisheries Management Plan for Big-River Fishes (Preliminary Draft, December 1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary draft, prepared by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) Arizona Fishery Resource Office and submitted to the Regional Director, Southwest Region, proposes a management plan for four federally endangered "big-river" fishes in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]: [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The plan argues that the fishes' decline stems mainly from altered ecosystems and from negative interactions with [[Non-Indigenous Fishes]], which prevent recruitment. It therefore emphasizes ecosystem-level habitat development, protected refugia, captive propagation, genetic management and recruitment-failure circumvention, with a lower-basin coordination team to implement it.

## Background & Description

**The river.** The Colorado River basin covers about 650,000 km² of western North America, roughly one-twelfth of the contiguous United States, and drains parts of seven states plus a small area of northwestern Mexico. Elevations range from below sea level at the [[Salton Sea]] and Laguna Salada basins to more than 4,000 m in the Rocky Mountain headwaters. Historically the river ran slow and turbid through valleys and violent rapids through canyons, with snowmelt-driven cold spring flows and warm summer flows. Native fishes evolved and adapted to these conditions.

**Management division.** The basin is split at [[Glen Canyon Dam]], Arizona, into upper and lower basins. The lower basin comprises the Colorado mainstem and the Paria, Little Colorado, Virgin, Bill Williams and Gila sub-basins. A recovery implementation plan already exists for the upper basin; this document addresses the lower basin.

**The big-river fishes.** The four species are among the largest and most unusual freshwater fishes in the world. All are federally listed as endangered. Three have designated critical habitat downstream of Glen Canyon Dam. Colorado Squawfish has none in the lower basin, having been extirpated there in the 1960s.

**Plan rationale.** The plan draws on literature reviews by Minckley and colleagues and on unpublished data from cooperating scientists. It differs from upper-basin recovery plans for three reasons: lower-basin dam development is largely complete and stable; population sizes of bonytail, humpback chub and razorback sucker are larger in the lower basin; and an extensive site-specific research base supports fact-based recovery actions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Human impacts:** Hydroelectric power generation, irrigation, livestock grazing and dam construction altered the watershed. Dams block historic movement corridors, release water too cold for spawning, and remove natural flow variance.
- **Non-indigenous predators and competitors:** Introduced fishes, including predators that prey on eggs, larvae and juveniles, are identified as a major cause of indigenous fish depletion. Sources include bait tanks, canals and agency stocking.
- **Free-flowing habitat:** Free-flowing reaches are fewer and less extensive in the lower basin than in the upper basin. The plan notes that upper-basin habitat is more amenable to management and renovation.
- **Mexican drainages:** Independent basins draining into Mexico must ultimately be included in regional ecosystem planning but are excluded from this version.
- **Shared ecology:** The four species share a coevolved predator-prey assemblage and responded similarly to human impacts, becoming rare at about the same time through recruitment failure. Experimental data show all four can survive to maturity when isolated from other fishes during early life stages.
- **Status of populations:**
  - Bonytail: caught infrequently in Lakes Mohave and Havasu.
  - Humpback chub: a viable population persists in the Grand Canyon area, mostly in the lowermost Little Colorado River.
  - Razorback sucker: the largest remaining population is in Lake Mohave, with wild individuals occasionally captured in the Grand Canyon region and below Davis Dam.
  - Colorado squawfish: no remaining wild lower-basin populations; experimental, nonessential populations were reintroduced in the Salt and Verde rivers.

## Key Findings

**Goals and objectives.** The ultimate goal is to stabilize self-perpetuating populations of all four species with genetic variability reflecting wild populations, occupying portions of their historic habitat. Six levels of progress are defined:
1. Prevent extinction.
2. Perpetuate existing genetic variability.
3. Achieve stabilization with sufficient numbers, population structure and genetic variability.
4. Re-introduce and re-establish stocks.
5. Achieve self-sustaining populations.
6. Down-list to threatened or de-list.

Bonytail enters at Level I, while humpback chub is closer to Level III.

**Management actions:**
- **Refugia in river coves:** Dedicated, enhanced or developed habitats, usually under 5.0 ha, are isolated with fish-exclusion barriers on designated critical habitat and on [[Lower Colorado River National Wildlife Refuges]] (Bill Williams River, Cibola, Havasu, Imperial). Resident fishes are removed and the four species stocked within three years, starting on refuges.
- **Large isolated habitats:** Creation or renovation of habitats of about 80 ha or larger, on refuges, within five years, managed under an ecosystem concept.
- **Expansion:** Expansion to other federal lands and habitats within ten years, especially for humpback chub in the Gila basin.
- **Reservoir refilling:** Stocking adults and larvae during reservoir refilling after drawdowns, exploiting the absence of predators and the longevity of the species.
- **Free-flowing restoration:** Restoration of free-flowing reaches in the Salt, Gila, Verde, Bill Williams and mainstem Colorado rivers.

**Razorback sucker larval collection.** Wild larvae can be collected because the species shows a positive phototactic response. Collections are made at different times during the extended breeding season across Lake Mohave to maximize genetic diversity. Weaknesses include unknown numbers and genetics of contributing parents, possible effects on physiological development and behavior, and limited fish-health information.

**Captive propagation and hatcheries:**
- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] holds brood stocks of all four species and provides genetic advisory support. Its capabilities should be expanded.
- [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] currently rears razorback sucker and holds a refugia population of bonytail.
- The Arizona Game and Fish Department's [[Bubbling Ponds Hatchery]] is soon to come online.
- Humpback chub is the exception among the four species, with no dedicated hatchery program.

**Genetic management.** Genetic concerns are immediate because the species are near extinction. Recommendations, in decreasing order of resemblance to natural conditions, include:
- maximizing the number of wild contributors;
- using embayment isolation with stocking of wild-caught breeding fish;
- artificial propagation on a strict pedigree, if wild larvae or embayment space are unavailable;
- mixing wild fish with "domestic" F1 brood fish to limit inbreeding.

Cryopreservation (gene banking) of gametes is recommended. Samples should be collected from at least 100 individuals of the razorback sucker and from the other species. Genetic information should draw on the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for the upper basin.

**Fish health.** Natural and semi-natural habitats must be screened for health problems before renovation and stocking. Hatchery and stocked fish should be monitored at least annually and before any site-to-site transfer.

**Monitoring and research.** Surveys should locate habitat for additional populations and assess survival, reproduction and competition in refugia. Studies should examine relationships among the four fishes and their predators and competitors, and the potential benefits of controlled water releases. Evaluation of all management actions is required.

**Coordination.** A lower-basin coordinator should:
- form a Recovery Team drawn from federal, state and private organizations, with no more than half from agencies affecting the taxa;
- meet with the Regional Director at least twice a year;
- convene annual working sessions to consolidate information and set broodstock protocols.

Cooperating agencies are listed in [[Appendix B: Agencies and Coordination Partners]], covering federal agencies (Bureau of Reclamation, Bureau of Land Management, Fish and Wildlife Service, National Park Service, Forest Service), state agencies, tribal nations, universities and non-governmental organizations.

**Regulatory issues:**
- The Colorado Squawfish experimental population designation in the Salt and Verde rivers should be changed from "experimental, nonessential" to "experimental, essential" so these populations can contribute to recovery.
- The USFWS Region II has already requested withdrawal of an earlier Arizona Game and Fish Department proposal for experimental, nonessential designation in the lower Colorado mainstem.
- Humpback chub recovery is also being addressed under a Grand Canyon Protection Act environmental study and a jeopardy biological opinion on operations of Glen Canyon Dam.

**Listing status.** [[Appendix A: Indigenous Fishes of the Lower Colorado River Basin]] lists indigenous fishes across Bio-regions I to V, with extirpated taxa marked. Bio-region I lists humpback chub, bonytail, Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker, all as endangered.
