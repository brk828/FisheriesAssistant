# Physiological Responses of a Native and an Introduced Desert Fish to Environmental Stressors

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the physiology of two similar cyprinids, the native [[Mojave Tui Chub]] (*Gila bicolor mohavensis*) and the introduced [[Arroyo Chub]] (*Gila orcuttii*), to test whether differential responses to abiotic stressors help explain why the arroyo chub displaced the tui chub from the fluctuating [[Mojave River]]. Laboratory trials measured [[Metabolic Rate]] under temperature and [[Hypoxia]] stress and [[Swimming Performance]] under high water velocity. The arroyo chub showed more appropriate responses on every measured axis, except low-temperature hypoxia tolerance, where the tui chub did better. The authors conclude that differences in adaptation, combined with [[Hybridization]] and selective floods, contributed to the replacement of the tui chub without requiring competition as the causal mechanism.

## Background & Description

Persistence of an organism depends on its response to abiotic factors and its interactions with other organisms. When an introduced species replaces a native one, the biotic interaction is usually assumed to be responsible. The authors argue that differential abiotic tolerance may shape whether displacement or coexistence results (see [[Abiotic Disturbance]]).

Previous explanations for the displacement invoked [[Competitive Exclusion]] and hybridization (Hubbs and Miller 1943; Hubbs 1955; Miller 1961; Moyle 1976; U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1984). The authors predicted that the arroyo chub, with a longer evolutionary history in fluctuating streams, would tolerate Mojave River stressors better. Because the interaction occurred about 40 years before the study, the prediction was tested by comparing physiological responses rather than observing the interaction directly.

**Habitat description.** The Mojave River runs from the San Bernardino Mountains to the usually dry Soda Lake bed. It is subterranean for most of its length, leaving disjunct surface reaches. The region has seasonal temperature extremes, and water temperature varies from 0.0° to 36.0°C yearly, with 10–15°C monthly variation near Victorville. Low flows favor community respiration and low oxygen. Episodic, intense rainfall produces flash floods, and subsequent dry periods strand fish. High temperatures, low oxygen, and high flow rates were therefore chosen as the test stressors.

**Faunal history.** Tui chubs are native only to the Mojave River system, where they were confined to stream habitats for a relatively short period. Before that, they were part of a subspecies complex in lakes and sluggish rivers across western drainages. Arroyo chubs are native to streams of the Los Angeles Plain and had a longer history in stream environments. Their introduction into Deep Creek, a Mojave headwater, occurred before the 1930s. Arroyo chubs replaced tui chubs in headwaters but were isolated from lower reaches by subterranean flow. Heavy rains in 1937 and 1938 spread arroyo chubs downstream, and tui chubs declined. By the early 1960s, tui chubs could no longer be found in the system (Miller 1969).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Mojave River is a [[Temporal Desert Riparian System]] whose fish face recurrent stress: thermal extremes, hypoxia from respiration in low flow, and flood scour and stranding. The authors contrast this with lake environments, which seldom undergo rapid temperature rises, do not demand high activity, and may be cold and oxygen-poor under ice or stratification. Low-temperature hypoxia is therefore a selective force in lakes but not in flowing water, where cold water usually holds more dissolved oxygen (Hynes 1970).

The authors cite Meffe (1984) as a parallel case, where native fishes were better adapted to floods than introduced predators, and flooding minimized predation effects and allowed coexistence. The contrast suggests that the outcome of a biotic interaction (displacement versus coexistence) depends on the abiotic regime.

Tui chub origins in lake systems and the apparent mismatch between the species' adaptations and river conditions are linked to the late Cenozoic history of [[Soda Lake]] and the Mojave basin.

## Key Findings

**Study design and methods**

- Fish were collected from Lark Seep on China Lake Naval Weapons Center (tui chubs) and Big Tujunga Creek in the San Gabriel Mountains (arroyo chubs), chosen to represent the genetic stocks of the original introduction.
- Fish were held at the University of California, Davis, at 18–24°C with unchlorinated well water. All were reproductively mature.
- Fish were acclimated to 10°, 20°, and 30°C for 5–8 weeks in groups of 20. Twelve fish per species per temperature group were used for metabolic trials.
- Resting-routine [[Metabolic Rate]] was measured by static [[Respirometry]] in 210-mL jars, with one control jar per run to correct for microbial respiration. Measurements were taken under normoxia (21–17 kPa) and hypoxia (about 5 kPa), then again after a 5°C overnight temperature increase.
- Swimming performance was tested in a modified Blazka-type apparatus with 15 paired trials. Fish were compared on which was pinned against the back baffle first.

**Metabolic responses**

- Metabolic rates were significantly higher at 20°C than at 10°C for both species under the same oxygen conditions. At 30°C, both species exceeded the 20°C values under normoxia, but only 30°C arroyo chubs showed the difference after the 5°C increase.
- Arroyo chubs at 10°C and 20°C increased metabolic rate significantly with a 5°C increase. Tui chubs responded similarly but to a lesser degree under normoxia, and 10°C and 20°C tui chubs did not respond under hypoxia.
- This difference produced significant interspecific differences in metabolic rate after the temperature increase.
- Both species showed slight reductions in metabolic rate under hypoxia, significant in four of five cases for both. The 10°C arroyo chub depression produced a significant interspecific difference.
- Arroyo chubs lost equilibrium at 10°C under hypoxia but recovered on return to normoxia.

**Survival and temperature tolerance**

- Tui chubs acclimated to 30°C did not survive the 5°C overnight increase. Arroyo chubs survived it, including those at 30°C.
- Two of 20 arroyo chubs acclimated to 10°C died during temperature decrease, and arroyo chubs had difficulty acclimating to 10°C. Tui chubs tolerated 10°C and hypoxia better.
- The authors suggest tui chub tolerance of low-temperature hypoxia would be advantageous in cold, long-ice-covered lakes, not in the flowing Mojave River.

**Swimming performance**

- Tui chubs were pinned first significantly more often than arroyo chubs (sign test, P < .05). Both species struggled under high water velocity, often touching the back baffle, and pinned fish appeared exhausted.
- Fish masses and standard lengths did not differ significantly between species and were not correlated with trial outcomes.
- Behavioral observations of interactions during trials were recorded but not quantified in the reported text.

**Statistical methods**

- Data were analyzed as mass-independent oxygen consumption rates, log-transformed.
- Analyses used ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, Duncan's new multiple-range test, *t* tests for interspecific comparisons, and a sign test for swimming trials.
- Mass differences among acclimation groups were not significant except between 10°C and 20°C arroyo chubs.

**Conclusions and interpretation**

- Arroyo chubs responded more appropriately to desert-river stressors than tui chubs, supporting the prediction that differences in evolutionary history drove differential physiological fitness.
- Arroyo chubs' larger metabolic increase with warming may help them stay active and escape stress in fluctuating streams. Tui chubs' lower activity and poorer swimming performance would reduce prey capture, predator escape, and flood resistance.
- Tui chubs washed out in floods, as in Thompson's (1921) observation of thousands dying on Soda Lake, and Hubbs and Miller (1943) observed a similar event. Arroyo chubs' share of samples increased after the flood.
- Hybrids, which showed low fertility and intermediate morphology (Hubbs and Miller 1943), were probably also selected against. Environmental stressors that removed tui chubs and hybrids selectively could have displaced tui chubs without competition.
- Tui chub population numbers likely fluctuated widely, rising during benign periods and falling sharply under stress. Arroyo chubs were not expected to be as severely affected.
- The authors conclude that the interplay of abiotic stressors (high temperature and floods) with biotic interaction (hybridization) produced displacement, and that the study underscores the role of abiotic factors in shaping biotic outcomes.
