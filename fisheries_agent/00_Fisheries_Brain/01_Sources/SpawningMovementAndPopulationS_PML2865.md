# Spawning, Movement and Population Structure of the Flannelmouth Sucker in the Paria River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 M.S. thesis (University of Arizona, Wildlife and Fisheries Science, by Steven Joseph Weiss) examines the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) in the lower [[Paria River]], a tributary that enters the [[Colorado River]] about 25 km below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. The study covers spawning timing, habitat selection, use of the [[Confluence Area]], growth, longevity, and recruitment, and was supported by a Bureau of Reclamation contract through the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. The Paria is presented as a rare, relatively undisturbed spawning ground for a species whose range has shrunk and which the USFWS listed as a Candidate Category 2 species.

## Background & Description

The flannelmouth sucker is the only endemic "big river" fish native to the Colorado River still commonly found throughout the [[Grand Canyon]]. Dams and exotic fish have severely altered the native fauna. Four native species are extirpated or nearly so (Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker, bonytail chub, roundtail chub), while [[Humpback Chub]] persists mainly near the Little Colorado River. The flannelmouth is a non-guarding, open-substrate lithophilic breeder with adhesive demersal eggs, the largest eggs (3.8–3.9 mm) of any catostomid in the Colorado Basin. Its diet is largely algae and debris, with amphipods and midge larvae important in the Grand Canyon. Hybridization with introduced white sucker (upper basin) and [[Razorback Sucker]] is a documented threat.

The study objectives were to: (1) document spawning timing and location relative to season, flow, temperature, depth and substrate; (2) document confluence use across Colorado River flows; (3) analyze population demographics for recruitment; (4) compare current flow and temperature regimes with historical conditions; and (5) survey the upper Paria basin for native fishes and invertebrates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** The Paria rises from springs at the base of cliffs in [[Bryce Canyon National Park]], Utah, flows about 88 km, and drains 3650 km². It is augmented by a 19 km canal from the East Fork of the Sevier River. The lower 16 km are low gradient desert, and the last 2 km lie in the [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]]. Base flow is 3.5–35.3 cfs, and flash floods (>50 cfs) are common in summer. Instantaneous flows during the study ranged from 2.5 to 1790 cfs. Suspended sediment averaged 114,000 ppm at the mouth, with floods reaching about 780,000 mg/l. The Colorado River ranged from 5,000 to 20,000 cfs and about 5–20 °C during the study.

**Ecological fit.** The Paria most closely resembles the pre-dam Colorado in temperature, sediment load and most water chemistry parameters (Cole and Kubly 1976), which makes it a useful reference system.

**Fish community.** Besides flannelmouth sucker, the study recorded [[Speckled Dace]] year-round, plus incidental bluehead sucker (n = 3), rainbow trout (n = 24), common carp (n = 1), and one moribund striped bass.

**Invertebrates (Appendix B).** Taxa were collected from 12 orders and numerous families, with a total of 58 organisms identified to genus or species. Dipterans (Chironomidae, Simuliidae) and Coleoptera (Dytiscidae, Hydrophilidae) were the most widespread. Speckled dace were absent north of Highway 12 in Utah and upstream of a rock slide near Bushhead Falls.

## Key Findings

- **Size and spawner structure:** Spawning fish averaged 478 mm TL (n = 246), 53 mm longer than spawners sampled in 1981 (425 mm TL, n = 286). Females (mean 524 mm, n = 455) were larger than males (mean 470 mm, n = 522).
- **Recruitment:** No post-larval fish under 379 mm TL were caught in 1992 or 1993. Juveniles (22–32 mm) were caught in spring 1992, and no young-of-year were found in 1993. The authors find no evidence of successful local rearing in the last 12 years, yet adults of 380–400 mm enter the population each year, presumably from downstream.
- **Spawning run:** Spawning occurred throughout the lower 10 km of the Paria. Ripe fish were first caught on March 20, 1992, and February 13, 1993 for males. Peak spawning (CPUE up to about 30 fish per 100 m²) occurred March 28–April 9 and April 8–10 when temperatures were about 7–20 °C. The sex ratio above the confluence favored males roughly 2:1.
- **Spawning habitat:** Fish spawned in shallow water (mostly <25 cm), over gravel and cobble (mean substrate 16.6 mm and 23 mm at the 2.8 km and 6.0 km sites). Bottom velocity at spawning sites (0.38 m/s) was significantly lower than availability (0.57 m/s, p < 0.05). Mean column velocity at spawners was 0.48 m/s, and fish were not observed spawning in reach 1.
- **Confluence use:** Daytime CPUE averaged 0.65 fish/100 m² (73 of 93 hauls empty); night CPUE averaged 1.3 fish/100 m² (p = 0.001), with fish in shallow, slack water over fine substrate. No spawning was observed in the confluence area, which is dominated by fine sand or silt.
- **Flow effects:** Spawning in 1992 ended abruptly before a flash flood, whereas 1993 spawning was prolonged under relatively stable flows. Mean daily flows stayed below 50 cfs for the whole spawning period in half of the 20 years examined (1956–1976 record).
- **Growth and longevity:** PIT-tagged fish recaptured in the Paria showed no measurable growth (77 fish). Floy-tagged fish grew about 7.3 mm/year as adults. Longevity is estimated at about 30 years, based on age at maturity (4–5 years) and maximum size (600 mm).
- **Movement:** Recaptures from up to 229 km downstream (Kanab Creek) show a spawning run that combines Glen Canyon residents with migrants from the [[Little Colorado River]] (97 km downstream).
- **Confluence-area fish:** 21 fish of 500–620 mm were caught at the confluence; several large specimens showed traits suggesting hybridization with razorback sucker.
- **Management implication:** Flash floods and sediment loads may reduce spawning success, but long lifespan means that failed year classes need not threaten persistence. Dam-induced hypolimnetic releases and predation by ictalurids were discussed as possible limits on recruitment.
