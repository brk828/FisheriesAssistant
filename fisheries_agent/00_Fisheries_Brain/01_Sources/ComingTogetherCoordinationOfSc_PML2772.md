# Colorado River Basin Science and Research Management Symposium 2008 Abstracts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is the abstract volume for the Colorado River Basin Science and Research Management Symposium, held November 18–20, 2008, at the Doubletree Resort Hotel in Scottsdale, Arizona, under the theme "Coming Together: Coordination of Science and Restoration Activities for the Colorado River Ecosystem." It collects about 100 oral and poster abstracts across plenary and concurrent sessions covering regulated-river geomorphology, climate, reservoir limnology, native and nonnative fishes, aquatic food webs, invasive mussels, riparian restoration, archaeology, economics, and adaptive management. The dominant theme is that a heavily regulated river system requires coordinated, adaptive, multi-agency management to recover endangered fishes while meeting water and power demands.

## Background & Description

The volume is organized into a program overview, a detailed agenda, oral presentation abstracts, poster abstracts, and an author contact directory (about 150 contacts). Plenary sessions addressed adaptive programs, invasive species, and measuring societal values. Concurrent sessions covered sediment, geomorphology, water quality, native fish propagation, riparian vegetation, and monitoring.

Central management frameworks described include the [[Glen Canyon Dam Adaptive Management Program]], established in 1997 after the 1996 Record of Decision, and the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]], begun in 1988 and authorized by Public Law 106-392. Other frameworks are the [[San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]] and the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]], a 50-year Habitat Conservation Plan covering at least 26 species, with implementation starting October 2005.

Key keynote speakers and affiliations included U.S. Geological Survey, Bureau of Reclamation, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, National Park Service, NOAA, and several universities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishes of concern.** The four Federally endangered big-river fishes, [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], have smaller populations and reduced ranges than historically. Historical accounts describe their recruitment as dictated by flood and drought cycles, with predation by introduced fishes now suppressing natural recruitment.

**Regulation effects.** Glen Canyon Dam traps the entire upstream sediment load. Downstream segments show bed degradation and armoring, and the river has lost much of its predam turbidity, seasonal flows, and temperature variability. Reservoir operations are being modified to mimic natural hydrographs.

**Invasive species.** Nonnative [[Rainbow Trout]] and other salmonids, red shiner, smallmouth bass, and channel catfish compete with and prey on natives. The [[Quagga Mussel]] was discovered in Boulder Basin of Lake Mead in January 2007, the first known dreissenid occurrence in the Western United States.

**Climate.** Streamflow variability is among the highest of any large US river, and reconstructions show multi-decade droughts over the past millennium. Projections indicate decreasing or unchanged precipitation with continued warming (about 2°F over 35 years).

**Riparian and wetland systems.** [[Tamarisk]] and Russian olive invasion, floodplain wetlands, springs, and delta restoration are major topics, with projects in the US and Mexico.

## Key Findings

- **Adaptive management effectiveness.** A decadal assessment of the GCDAMP found success in ecosystem-scale experiments and in bridging science and management, but limited progress on long-term experimental programs and persistent polarization.
- **Recovery progress.** The Upper Basin Recovery Program has expended $187 million since inception and provides ESA compliance for more than 1,500 water projects depleting over 2 million acre-feet annually.
- **Native fish status.** On the Green River (2002–2007), all native species except Colorado Pikeminnow declined relative to 1994–1996, though strong populations remained. Reproduction by Colorado Pikeminnow and Razorback Sucker was detected in Lodore Canyon in 2006.
- **Little Colorado River population.** The 2008 spring Humpback Chub estimate was 5,850 fish (>150 mm), significantly higher than 2001–2007 estimates except 2007.
- **Nonnative predation.** Rainbow and brown trout consumed nearly 30,000 fish during a 2003–2004 study; trout consumed 85 percent more natives than nonnatives, despite natives being 30 percent less abundant.
- **Razorback sucker survival.** First-year survival of hatchery-reared fish rose with stocking length, from near 0 below 200 mm to above 0.75 for fish over 500 mm. Summer-stocked fish had the lowest survival.
- **Sand and controlled floods.** Sandbar size did not increase overall during the 1996 or 2004 controlled floods, indicating more sand is needed. The 2008 high-flow experiment is being analyzed for effectiveness.
- **Food webs.** Invertebrate production below Glen Canyon Dam is sufficient for rainbow trout energetic demands, though much energy passes through nonnative taxa such as New Zealand mudsnails.
- **Quagga mussel risk.** Standardized monitoring designs for Lake Mead required 56 adult and 42 veliger sampling stations for 95 percent confidence within ±35 percent.
- **Bill Williams River.** Managed flows widened channels and promoted native cottonwood and willow recruitment, while beaver dams were largely destroyed by releases of 150 to 280 m³/s.
- **Economic and social findings.** Existing valuation studies are dated and incomplete, and the 1995 EIS understated the power-system cost of the 1996 Record of Decision. Native American participation in the Glen Canyon program was judged to be insufficiently integrated.
