# Phosphorus-Sediment Dynamics in the Colorado River Reservoir System

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #phytoplankton

## Executive Summary

This preliminary symposium paper (Las Vegas, Nevada, 1991) examines how the formation of [[Lake Powell]] in 1963 altered the [[Suspended Sediment]]-[[Phosphorus]] dynamics of the [[Colorado River]] inflow to [[Lake Mead]]. The authors argue that trapping of sediment behind [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has reduced the particulate phosphorus delivered downstream, leaving the Upper Basin of Lake Mead phosphorus-deficient. The work is based on monthly sampling from August to October 1981 and on historical sediment records. The document contains no fish data; its subject is nutrient chemistry and phytoplankton productivity in a regulated freshwater reservoir chain.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River has been progressively regulated by a chain of reservoirs, beginning with [[Hoover Dam]] and the formation of Lake Mead in 1935. Lake Mead historically received unregulated inflow until 1963, when Lake Powell was impounded by Glen Canyon Dam, approximately 480 km upstream. The reservoir is a large, deep-storage system roughly 190 km long, extending from the mouth of the Grand Canyon to Glen Canyon Dam. The Colorado River supplies about 98% of Lake Mead's annual inflow. The Virgin and Muddy Rivers contribute about 2% to the Overton Arm, and treated effluent from the [[Las Vegas Wash]] adds further nutrient loading.

Regulation has produced several physical changes:

- Spring discharge peaks from upper-basin snowmelt flooding have been eliminated.
- Cold hypolimnetic releases have lowered river temperatures by 5–10 °C in spring and summer.
- Suspended sediment loads have fallen sharply.
- Turbid overflows that once extended across the Upper Basin of Lake Mead were not observed in 1977–78.

Phosphorus is widely reported as the most common nutrient limiting phytoplankton productivity. The authors note that little attention has been paid to the interaction between suspended sediments and dissolved inorganic phosphorus. Removal of inorganic phosphorus by suspended sediment appears to occur through [[Sorption]] rather than precipitation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment-phosphorus coupling.** Loosely bound phosphorus on suspended sediments is more readily available than precipitated forms. Earlier work on the Illinois River and in other turbid rivers and lakes had documented active adsorption of dissolved phosphorus onto sediments. [[Mayer and Gloss]] proposed that phosphorus desorbed from suspended sediments in the turbid Colorado River sustained dissolved phosphorus in Lake Powell, and that a similar mechanism operated in Lake Mead when it received turbid inflows before 1963.

**Reservoir trapping.** Lake Powell acts as both a sediment trap and a phosphorus sink. Phosphorus retention in Lake Powell was among the highest reported for reservoirs. Retention in Lake Mead was lower, which the authors attribute to:

- a [[Density Current]] that routes much of the phosphorus load into the hypolimnion, and
- hypolimnetic discharge from Hoover Dam, which rapidly removes phosphorus from the reservoir.

**Upper Basin phosphorus deficiency.** The Upper Basin of Lake Mead is now described as severely phosphorus-deficient. The authors link this to the reduction in suspended sediment loading, which has removed the main source of available phosphorus.

**Biological consequences.** The paper notes that historical sediment loads (up to about 140 million tons per year) were apparently sufficient to sustain the dissolved inorganic phosphorus pool and higher productivity. The authors suggest that reduced sediment inputs have lowered phytoplankton productivity in the reservoir. They do not present direct biological measurements.

## Key Findings

**1. Sediment load collapse after impoundment.**
- Before Lake Powell, the Colorado River at the Grand Canyon gaging station carried suspended sediment loads that were extremely high, reaching up to 140 million tons per year in wet years, mostly during spring runoff.
- Impoundment in 1963 reduced suspended sediment loads at the Grand Canyon station by a range of roughly 26–90%. The scanned value is partly illegible, so the exact range should be checked against the original.
- The Paria River, which enters the mainstem above the Grand Canyon station, is now the only appreciable sediment source, though its contribution spikes during floods.

**2. Strong total-P to suspended sediment relationship.** Preliminary measurements on the Colorado River above Lake Powell and on the San Juan River inflow showed a close linear correlation between total phosphorus and suspended sediment concentration. The regression reported is:

- y = −164.4 + 0.709x, r = 0.992, n = 9

The data were collected from August to October 1981. The authors state that they are testing whether the relationship holds in other seasons.

**3. Phosphorus budgets and retention.**
- More than 95% of phosphorus loads entering Lake Powell were in particulate form ([[Gloss et al.]]).
- Lake Powell retained about 96.3% of total phosphorus.
- Lake Mead's retention coefficients were lower than Lake Powell's, for the reasons outlined in the Ecological Context section.

**4. Phosphorus in Lake Mead sediments.**
- Total phosphorus in Lake Mead sediments was high, at 300–1000 ppm ([[Prentki et al.]]).
- Phosphorus sedimentation rates in Lake Mead fell by about 93% after Lake Powell was formed.
- Inorganic phosphorus bound to suspended sediment dominates total phosphorus in river inflows, a pattern consistent across pre- and post-Lake Powell sediment layers.

**5. Desorption capacity.** Suspended sediments in the Colorado River above Lake Powell showed the capacity to desorb phosphorus, releasing approximately 20–30 µg/L of dissolved inorganic phosphorus.

**6. Bioavailable phosphorus fraction.**
- Available phosphorus was estimated with the equation of [[Lee et al.]]: Available P = SRP + 0.2·PP, where SRP is soluble reactive phosphorus and PP is total particulate phosphorus.
- Estimates for August and September 2000–1981 sampling ranged from 7.1% to 19.2%, with a mean that the scan does not make clear.
- The authors suggest that a small fraction of total phosphorus, roughly 16–30% on the scan, is biologically available. Prentki et al. had reported an average of about 9%.
- On a volumetric basis, available phosphorus was only a low single-digit percentage of total phosphorus.

**7. Methods.**
- Sampling: composite surface samples (several 3-L Van Dorn casts) collected monthly from August through October 1981 at the primary inflow stations to Lake Powell and Lake Mead.
- Dissolved phosphorus: filtered through 0.45 µm membranes and analyzed by [[Kellar et al.]] methods.
- Total and particulate phosphorus: persulfate digestion of unfiltered samples and of sediments collected on 0.4 µm filters.
- Available sediment phosphorus: NaOH extraction following [[Sagher]], [[Williams et al.]] and Shukla et al.
- Sediment:water ratio: natural river water was used to approximate in-situ conditions, in contrast to the high sediment:water ratios common in earlier work.
- Suspended sediment and discharge data: USGS Water Resources Data for Arizona and Nevada.

**8. Management implication.** The authors conclude that the historical sediment load was sufficient to sustain the available phosphorus pool and productivity in Lake Mead. The loss of that load has therefore been a major driver of the present phosphorus deficiency. Nutrient management in the Colorado River system will need to account for sediment regulation at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and for the effects of [[Las Vegas Wash]] effluent.

**Note on the data.** The results are explicitly preliminary, and the authors state that the 1981 sampling is limited to a single season. Several table values and percentages in the scanned text are partly illegible, so the values above should be verified against the original publication before citation.
