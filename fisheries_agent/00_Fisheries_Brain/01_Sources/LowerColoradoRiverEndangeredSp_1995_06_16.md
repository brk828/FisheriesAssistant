## Executive Summary

This briefing paper (June 16, 1995) documents the formation and recommendations of the **Lower Colorado River Endangered Species Conservation Program**, a multi-agency partnership between the U.S. Department of the Interior, Arizona, California, and Nevada water/wildlife agencies, and Tribal governments. The document is primarily a policy and cost-allocation framework rather than a primary biological research paper, but it embeds significant conservation-status data on three endangered "big river" fish of the [[Lower Colorado River]]: the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The Working Group recommends pursuit of a multi-species **Habitat Conservation Plan (HCP)** under ESA Section 10(a)(1)(B), an ecosystem-based approach covering ~34 listed/candidate species, and a slate of Interim Conservation Measures (ICMs) — including hatchery grow-out programs, stocking targets, and research initiatives — aimed at preventing extinction and preserving genetic diversity of the bonytail chub and razorback sucker during a three-year Program development period.

## Background & Description

- **Program origin**: Initiated via a March 7, 1994 partnership agreement between the Lower Division States/Tribes Endangered Species Steering Committee and the USFWS Regional Director, in response to conflicts between listed/candidate species conservation and water/power management on the Lower Colorado River.
- **Governing instrument**: A Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) for a Lower Colorado River Species Conservation Program, built upon an earlier "Partnership" MOU (developed 1994) signed by the U.S. Department of the Interior, Arizona Department of Water Resources, Arizona Game and Fish Commission, Colorado River Board of California, California Department of Fish and Game, Colorado River Commission of Nevada, and Nevada Division of Wildlife.
- **Geographic scope**: Mainstem Lower Colorado River and its 100-year floodplain, from below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Lake Mead]] down to [[Hoover Dam]] and the Southerly International Boundary (U.S.–Mexico border).
- **Species targeted**:
  - [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) — Cyprinidae; federally endangered "big river" fish.
  - [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) — Cyprinidae; federally endangered "big river" fish.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — Catostomidae; federally endangered "big river" fish, notable for an aging/senescent wild population in the study area.
- A broader **species list of 34 listed and non-listed taxa** (adapted from the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation's biological assessment of ongoing river operations) was proposed as the starting point for a comprehensive multi-species program, with additional species between Glen Canyon Dam and Lake Mead to be added by USFWS review.
- The recommended regulatory vehicle is a **multi-species Habitat Conservation Plan (HCP)**, providing incidental take permits, prelisting agreements for candidate species, mitigation banking, and a proposed 50-year program horizon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reservoir/riverine habitat mosaic**: Populations are managed across a series of impounded and free-flowing reaches, including [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Havasu]], and [[Lake Mead]] (notably the Overton Arm coves), plus off-channel backwaters, tribal lands, and National Wildlife Refuges (e.g., [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] — Three-Fingers Lake and High Levee Pond).
- **Population status — Razorback Sucker**: The wild [[Lake Mohave]] population is characterized as "aging," prompting the Lake Mohave Native Fish Work Group Program's goal to replace it with **50,000 young adult fish over five years**. The Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Program targets introduction of **30,000 razorback suckers by 2003**.
- **Population status — Bonytail Chub**: USFWS committed to stocking Lake Mohave with **25,000 bonytail chub** as mitigation for prior stocking of non-indigenous fish species (Willow Beach Stocking Mitigation). The Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Program also targets **30,000 bonytail chub by 2003**.
- **Threats framing**: Endangered species status is discussed in the context of water and power resource development, operations, and maintenance activities (i.e., anthropogenic hydrologic alteration of the mainstem river), rather than described via direct field/ecological data in this document.
- **Genetic diversity concerns**: The MOA explicitly identifies genetic diversity preservation, alongside extinction prevention, as the two core near-term biological objectives for bonytail chub and razorback sucker — implying documented concerns about population bottlenecks/aging cohorts in existing wild stocks.
- **Non-indigenous species interaction**: Historical stocking of non-native fish species in Lake Mohave is identified as a driver requiring mitigation stocking of native bonytail chub, indicating a recognized ecological conflict between sport-fish/non-native stocking programs and native endangered fish conservation.

## Key Findings

- **Recommended conservation strategy**: A multi-species **Habitat Conservation Plan (HCP)**, chosen over eight other strategies evaluated in a Feasibility Assessment Report (Dec. 1994), for maximal regulatory certainty (Section 10(a)(1)(B) incidental take permit) and flexibility (candidate species and habitat inclusion, mitigation banking).
- **Species list scope**: ~34 listed/non-listed species currently used in Reclamation's biological assessment serve as the baseline Program species list; may expand pending USFWS identification of additional taxa between Glen Canyon Dam and Lake Mead.
- **Interim Conservation Measures (ICMs)** prioritized for razorback sucker and bonytail chub include:
  - Native fish grow-out facility development on Tribal lands (Hualapai, Colorado River Indian Tribes, Fort Mojave, and other Lower Colorado River Tribes) and National Wildlife Refuges.
  - Retrofitting/developing hatcheries: [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (USFWS), Niland Hatchery (closed CDFG facility), Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery (AGFD, near Sedona, AZ).
  - Research programs: sonic/telemetry tracking studies, productivity studies, and fish culture technique refinement (stocking rates, fertilization, vegetation suppression).
- **Model program**: The **Lake Mohave Native Fish Work Group Program** (established 1991) is highlighted as the best-suited existing ICM vehicle due to measurable goals, demonstrated augmentation success for razorback sucker, and available federal cost-sharing.
- **Funding**: Federal cost-sharing for ICM projects estimated between **$300,000–$660,000** (FY1996, contingent on Congressional appropriations), within a broader annual Program budget of **$1.5 million**.
- **Program timeline**: Three-year Program development period (from MOA signing) with an intended 50-year long-term regulatory assurance horizon.
- **Cost allocation modeling**: Six alternative interstate cost-sharing formulas were developed using combinations of equitable apportionment (33% each state), Colorado River water allocation, hydroelectric power allocation (Parker-Davis and Hoover resources, via Western Area Power Administration data), and composite "state interest" weightings — reflecting the tri-state (AZ, CA, NV) governance structure underlying fish conservation funding decisions.
