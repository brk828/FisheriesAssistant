# Status of Bonytail and Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This status update, by Paul C. Marsh and W.L. Minckley of Arizona State University, assesses two federally endangered [[Colorado River]] fishes, [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. The lake holds the largest known populations of both species. Razorback sucker abundance appears stable, but the population shows no recruitment beyond the larval stage, so the authors expect it to be lost by the end of the decade unless the problem is remedied. Bonytail is much rarer and cannot be quantified, yet it is not extinct, and wild and stocked fish are being recaptured in Lake Mohave.

## Background & Description

Lake Mohave is an artificial impoundment on the Colorado River mainstream, created in the early 1950s when [[Davis Dam]] closed (Allan and Roden 1978). Both species are among the imperiled "large-river" fishes of the Colorado River basin and are listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS 1983, 1991).

- **Razorback sucker** can be reliably captured in Lake Mohave at virtually any time of year. During the winter-early spring spawning season, hundreds of adults can be handled in only a few days.
- **Bonytail** is far less abundant. Its encounter probability is highest in spring (March to May). The authors describe it as arguably the most critically imperiled western fish species, but note that it is not extinct.

Background sources include the bonytail recovery plan (USFWS 1990) and the razorback sucker reviews by Minckley (1983) and Minckley et al. (1991). This paper updates the status of both species based on collections over the past decade.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Razorback sucker**
- Data from 1974 to 1981 (Minckley 1983) were compared with collections from 1981 to the present. Individual and population characteristics were not detectably different between the two periods.
- Relative abundance is measured by [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] using [[Trammel Net]]s. CPE is lower in summer but has stayed near 2.5 fish per 100 m² of netting per 24 hours over the past 18 years. CPE has tracked changes in other species, including an increase in [[Striped Bass]] and a decrease in [[Rainbow Trout]].
- Absolute abundance comes from [[Mark-Recapture]] estimates. About 4,400 adults have been marked since 1981, mostly since 1988. The population estimate is approximately 60,000 fish, and annual estimates over the past five years are indistinguishable.

**Bonytail**
- No quantitative abundance estimates exist. Estimates were never attempted before the species became rare, and current catches are too low for meaningful CPE or mark-recapture data.
- Lake Mohave has been an important site for captures and augmentation, though the species' broader status is far more precarious.

**Threats to recruitment**
- Several explanations have been proposed for the lack of razorback sucker recruitment, including predation by [[Non-Native Fishes]], nutritional constraints, and drift out of the system (Minckley et al. 1991).

## Key Findings

### Razorback Sucker

- **Abundance:** CPE has remained relatively constant near 2.5 fish per 100 m² per 24 hours, with no evidence of change over 18 years. Mark-recapture estimates are about 60,000 fish, with no detectable change over five years.
- **Size:** Since the 1980s, average total length has been stable at roughly 55 cm for males and 62 cm for females, based on measurements of a large sample. The earlier increase in length (1960s to early 1980s) has stopped.
- **Health:** Weight, anomalies, injury, disease, parasites, and eye condition (blindness, cataracts, bulging) show no detectable change over the past decade.
- **Spawning:** Spawning has been documented repeatedly (Bozek et al. 1991) and observed annually since the 1970s. Timing varies with weather and lake conditions, but the intensity of spawning has not changed in 20 years. Larvae are abundant in spring (Marsh and Langhorst 1988).
- **Recruitment failure:** Apart from four juveniles captured in 1987 by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Marsh and Minckley 1989b), there is no evidence of recruitment beyond the larval stage. All adults are large and old (McCarthy and Minckley 1987), and no fish appear between the post-larval and adult stages. The authors project loss of the Lake Mohave population by the end of the decade unless the problem is addressed.
- **Monitoring recommendation:** The authors consider mark-recapture unsuitable for short-term assessment because significant delays can occur before abundance changes are detected. They recommend CPE as the standard status indicator.

### Bonytail

- **Captures:** A total of 57 bonytail were captured in Lake Mohave from 1974 to 1991, with additional fish taken by the Arizona Game and Fish Department and anglers. None were captured in 1975, 1977-1978, 1982, 1984, or 1987. The largest single samples were 9 fish (May 1990) and fish from March 1991 and May 1988.
- **Broodfish collection:** After USFWS requested broodfish in 1988, 34 bonytail were captured: 6 in March, 27 in May, and 1 in November. Of these, 23 were wild adults transferred to USFWS to augment broodstock, 11 were sub-adults, and 4 others died in the field (7 sub-adults were released).
- **Reproduction:** Reproduction in Lake Mohave has not been observed. Recruitment has been documented only through the capture of at least one relatively young wild fish (Minckley et al. 1989).
- **Recovery plan priorities:** The immediate goal of the recovery plan (USFWS 1990) is to prevent extinction. Seven of nine "Priority 1" tasks involve acquiring wild bonytail, holding them in refugia, and propagating, rearing, and stocking them. One task calls for collection attempts in Lake Mohave, names USFWS and the states as responsible parties, and estimates an annual cost of $50,000.

### Stocking and Augmentation

- More than 130,000 hatchery fish, including larvae and young adults up to 16.5 cm TL, have been stocked. The source text dates this program to 1991, although the first stocking is described as occurring in 1981.
- Early stocked fish were marked with [[Tetracycline Marking]], and 1990 juveniles were fin-clipped. Other stocked fish were unmarked.
- Of 39 bonytail captured since 1981, 11 were identified as stocked fish: 4 by fin clip, 3 by small size, and 4 mortalities aged as the 1981 year class by [[Otolith Aging]].
- The authors describe the return rate of stocked bonytail as remarkable compared with the few recaptures of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and razorback sucker among millions reintroduced to the [[Gila River]] system. They urge agencies managing bonytail recovery to take note.

### Conclusions

- Razorback sucker is stable in numbers and condition but faces a critical lack of recruitment.
- Bonytail is critically rare but persists, and augmentation has likely contributed to its status.
- Monitoring should rely on CPE for short-term assessment, and recovery efforts must address recruitment and the captive-rearing and stocking program.
