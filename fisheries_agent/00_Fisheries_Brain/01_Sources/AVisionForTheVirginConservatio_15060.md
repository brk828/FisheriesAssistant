# A Vision for the Virgin: Conservationist Approaches to River Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 report by the [[Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance]] (SUWA) is a conservationist "blueprint" for restoring balance to management of the Virgin River, a roughly 200-mile desert river flowing through Utah, Arizona and Nevada into [[Lake Mead]]. The report argues that water management has been dominated by agricultural and municipal-industrial (M&I) demand while instream resources, especially native fishes, riparian areas and scenic values, receive minimal protection. It documents rapid population growth, a history of dam proposals, the 1989 failure of the [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] dam, and threats from transbasin diversions to [[Las Vegas]]. It then reviews legal tools (Clean Water Act Section 404, federal reserved water rights, Wild and Scenic River designation, Areas of Critical Environmental Concern, Endangered Species Act) and non-structural water management options, and closes with specific prescriptions.

## Background & Description

**Scope and structure.** The document is a seven-chapter policy report with bibliography and appendices (maps and figures, not reproduced here):

- **Chapter I – The Virgin River Basin:** geography, geology, surface and groundwater, prehistory, colonial history and early dam projects.
- **Chapter II – Demands on the Virgin River:** instream resources, riparian areas, sensitive fishes, recreation, M&I and agricultural sectors.
- **Chapter III – The Virgin River Traditions:** attitudes such as "It's All Ours," "Bigger Is Better," "Have Cake, Will Eat," "The Insatiable Thirst," "It's Cheap, So Use It" and "Effects Have No Cause."
- **Chapter IV – Threats:** dam impacts and specific proposals (North Creek, Ash Creek/Sand Hollow/Pah Tempe, Cedar City transbasin diversion, North and East Forks, Beaver Dam Wash, Fort Pearce Wash, Cooperative Water Project, Quail Creek operations, groundwater).
- **Chapter V – Legal Approaches**, **Chapter VI – Non-Structural Approaches** (water planning, conjunctive use, reclamation, conservation, pricing reform, water markets) and **Chapter VII – Conservationist Blueprint** (local and state reform, a proposed Virgin Interior Basin Authority).

**Physical setting.** The river drains a basin of about 5,900 square miles over elevations from 2,600 to 11,300 feet. Its headwaters (East and North Forks) arise near [[Zion National Park]]. The river passes through the [[Virgin River Gorge]] and the "Strip" of northwest Arizona, and enters Lake Mead National Recreation Area. The basin lies at a transition between the Grand Staircase and Basin/Range provinces, and the report stresses the biological importance of these [[Ecotone]] zones.

**Geology.** Repeated marine transgressions during the Paleozoic and Mesozoic left gypsum-rich, highly erodible sediments; the document notes sedimentary records up to 19,000 feet thick in places.

**Prehistory and history.** Anasazi (including Virgin Branch) sites lie along the river, with Red Cliffs occupation from about A.D. 470 to 1140. Escalante first entered the valley in 1776. Mormon settlement began around 1852, with St. George settled by 300 families in 1861.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and water quality.** The average annual flow at the Virgin gaging station, upstream of most diversions, is 130,610 acre-feet per year. Flows are highly variable and can disappear in summer under agricultural diversion. Water quality is impaired by:

- Heavy metals from abandoned mines at Silver Reef and industry.
- Biological oxygen demand from animal wastes at a limited number of sites.
- Fertilizers and pesticides in agricultural runoff.
- High salinity: soils are saline and erodible, and irrigation evaporation loss of 50–70% raises salt concentrations by 200–300%.

The report states that almost 90% of Utah's segment fails state standards for TDS, temperature and metals. The Nevada BLM reports the river consistently exceeds federal drinking water standards.

**Groundwater.** The [[Navajo Sandstone Aquifer]] is the principal groundwater resource, supplying about 80% of well water in the basin. Up to 800 springs are believed active. [[Toquerville Spring]] (30 cfs, 460 mg/l TDS) is of good quality, whereas [[LaVerkin Hot Springs]] (11 cfs, over 9,000 mg/l TDS) is essentially unusable.

**Riparian areas.** Although riparian zones account for less than 1% of public land acreage in the Arizona Strip, they provide some of the richest wildlife habitat in the area. They also moderate stream temperature, which is critical for native fish.

**Sensitive fishes.** Three native species are of primary concern:

- [[Woundfin Minnow]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): listed as endangered in 1970. Its range has been reduced to the Virgin mainstem from LaVerkin to Lake Mead.
- [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*): listed as endangered in 1989. It is now found only between Pah Tempe and the Mesquite Diversion.
- [[Virgin River Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*): a Category 2 species; in March 1993 FWS found a listing petition warranted further consideration.

Nine other "scientifically critical" fishes also depend on Virgin habitat, including the flannelmouth sucker, desert sucker and Moapa dace. Native fish habitat has shrunk from about 75 miles to about 20 miles of river since the 1960s.

**Other sensitive species.** Bald eagles roost in riparian cottonwoods; leopard frog populations may depend on instream flow. Research on many species is lacking.

## Key Findings

**1. Imbalanced management.** Instream resources receive minimal emphasis, protection or funding. The authors argue that the prior appropriation doctrine historically ignored instream uses.

**2. Population and demand growth.**
- Washington County, Utah, roughly doubled in population during the 1980s (26,065 to 48,560).
- St. George grew from 13,146 (1980) to 28,502 (1990). Projections range from about 60,000 (FCAoG, 2020) to 250,000 (City buildout study).
- Las Vegas reached about 925,000 people, the fastest-growing metropolitan area in the country.
- Mesquite grew from 922 (1980) to over 2,110 (1993).

**3. Agriculture.** Agriculture uses most of the water but generates little income. In the basin, agricultural operations divert 123,291 acre-feet and deplete 57,264 acre-feet. Estimated on-farm efficiency is about 40%. Projections suggest farmland will be absorbed by urban growth. The report states that M&I water earns about sixty times the economic return of agriculture while using five times less.

**4. Dam history.** The Dixie Project (abandoned 1976 for cost and site reasons, estimates up to $60 million), the Warner Valley energy-water project (found infeasible by BLM) and the Quail Creek Reservoir (40,000 acre-feet, completed 1985) were all pursued. Kolob Reservoir, built in the early 1950s, is the only one of these projects actually completed.

**5. Quail Creek failure.** On December 31, 1988, a 200-foot section of the Quail Creek dike collapsed. Peak flows reached about 60,000 cfs and about 30,000 acre-feet passed through St. George, causing roughly $12 million in damage. The post-failure survey found that about four and one-half miles of the best native fish habitat was destroyed. The failure was attributed to dissolution of gypsum beneath the dam. The report criticizes the project's cursory environmental assessment and the addition of hydropower without further review.

**6. Proposed projects threatening river values.**
- *North Creek reservoir* (about 20,000 acre-feet): BLM denied the right-of-way, citing oil wells in the Virgin oilfield as a "fatal flaw."
- *Ash Creek/Sand Hollow/Pah Tempe*: new reservoirs and diversion of saline Pah Tempe Springs.
- *Cedar City transbasin diversion*: about 6,100 acre-feet per year from Upper Mill and Three Creeks.
- *North Fork (Bulloch site)* and *East Fork (Parunuweap)*: threats to Zion's Narrows, Parunuweap Canyon and potential wilderness.
- *Beaver Dam Wash*: applied for about 40,000 acre-feet; threatens the Lytle Ranch Research Preserve.
- *Fort Pearce Wash*: 135,000 acre-feet offstream reservoir, with geological and salinity concerns.
- *Cooperative Water Project* (Las Vegas Valley Water District): would import about 70,000 acre-feet per year from the Virgin, requiring a diversion dam near Halfway Wash. The report notes that the project would reduce flows into Lake Mead by at least 70,000 acre-feet per year, and argues that wheeling water from Lake Mead would avoid many impacts.

**7. Groundwater rush.** Applications have been filed in the Beaver Dam area and by Washington County interests, with the cities of St. George, Ivins and Santa Clara filing jointly for 49 cfs.

**8. Legal protection is underused.**
- *Clean Water Act Section 404*: unauthorized fill projects and after-the-fact permits were issued, including work in endangered species habitat.
- *Federal reserved water rights*: Zion National Park's rights are being quantified; BLM has ended its practice of filing extensions for Beaver Dam Mountains wilderness.
- *Wild and Scenic River designation*: the Dixie Resource Area's May 1993 Planning Update found 17 of 63 waterways eligible, deleting others on questionable grounds. Arizona's Strip RMP found the entire Arizona segment eligible.
- *ACEC designations*: administrative only, with no statutory protection.
- *Endangered Species Act*: the authors state that critical habitat for the native fishes is undesignated, and they recommend designating all historical, current and potential habitat.

**9. Non-structural alternatives.** The report calls for water planning, conjunctive use, water reclamation, conservation (including agricultural efficiency), water pricing reform and market approaches. It criticizes existing pricing structures (e.g., Mesquite's uniform rates) for failing to discourage waste.

**10. Recommendations (SUWA).**
- Designate Virgin River segments as eligible for wild and scenic status and pre-classify them, with interim protection.
- Designate ACECs (e.g., Santa Clara River/Gunlock, Lower Virgin River, Warner Ridge/Fort Pearce).
- Designate critical habitat for native fishes under the ESA.
- Adopt interstate, cross-jurisdictional management, including a proposed Virgin Interior Basin Authority.
- Shift from structural to demand-side water management, with reform of pricing and planning.

**Note on scope.** The document is a policy and advocacy report; its quantitative figures are drawn largely from agency plans and interviews and are reported here as cited by the authors.
