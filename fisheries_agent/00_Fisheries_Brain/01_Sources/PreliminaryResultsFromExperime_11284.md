# Preliminary Results from Experimental Introduction of Rainbow Trout as an Additional Game Fish in Large Minnesota Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Minnesota Division of Game and Fish investigational report (Report No. 279, dated October 9, 1964, by R. E. Schumacher) presents preliminary 1963 test-netting results from six large lakes stocked with [[Rainbow Trout]] to create a [[Two-Story Fishery]]: a cold-water lower stratum for trout beneath a warm-water upper stratum for the indigenous fish. The author states that the 1963 data are not expected to give a final answer on whether these lakes can be managed for rainbows. Results were mixed. Only [[Long Lake]] and [[Hungry Jack Lake]] supported notable rainbow catches; [[Lily Lake]] and [[Big Trout Lake]] yielded no suitable trout habitat or trout catches, and [[Kabekona Lake]] showed promise through natural spawning in its tributary.

## Background & Description

The rainbow trout was stocked in larger lakes to occupy a niche not filled by desirable species. Target lakes fell into two groups:

- **Warm-water lakes with deep cold water:** lakes with abundant dissolved oxygen in deep water during critical summer months, but steep bottom slopes and limited shallows that could not support a large warm-water fishery.
- **Former [[Lake Trout]] waters:** lakes where lake trout had declined to low abundance, with rainbows intended to use the upper zone the lake trout no longer occupied.

The goal was a two-story fish population fully using the lake environment. Six lakes were test-netted in summer 1963 using trapnets and standard 250-foot experimental gillnets (multiple mesh panels from 3/4 to 2 inch bar mesh). Netting was stratified by depth (0–25 ft, 26–35 ft, and 36 ft and deeper, as chemistry permitted) to relate species distribution to the [[Thermocline]] and oxygen availability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Physical and chemical suitability was evaluated using temperature (trout water defined as summer maxima below 70 °F) and dissolved oxygen (minimum above 5.0 ppm), summarized in Table 1 of the report. Key contrasts:

- **[[Lily Lake]] (Washington County):** Oxygen was very limited below the thermocline; the trout-suitable stratum was only about 4 percent of lake volume in June. Large [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]] and [[Yellow Perch]] populations were present. Limited spawning area was thought to restrict [[Northern Pike]] year-class success.
- **[[Long Lake]] (Clearwater County):** Deep light penetration allowed photosynthesis below the thermocline, producing near-saturated oxygen at depths used by trout. The lake was described as moderately infertile with high marl content (47 percent CaCO3 by volume). The outlet flowed continuously (estimated minimum 0.6 cfs).
- **[[Big Trout Lake]] (Crow Wing County):** Connected by a wide, unrestricted channel to the [[Whitefish Chain]] (Whitefish Lake); the outlet is governed by Cross Lake Dam and could exceed several hundred cfs during rapid drawdown.
- **[[Greenwood Lake]] (Cook County):** A typical oligotrophic lake of northeastern Minnesota, originally holding only suckers, whitefish and [[Lake Trout]]. Walleye moved in from Sunfish Lake, and lake trout were nearly eliminated before recovering.
- **[[Hungry Jack Lake]] (Cook County):** Originally held lake trout and northern pike; lake trout had disappeared by 1955. Walleye were introduced in the 1930s and declined after a 1948 peak. [[Smallmouth Bass]] dominated the catch.
- **[[Kabekona Lake]] (Hubbard County):** A remote 2,252-acre lake with a brook trout tributary, [[Kabekona Creek]]. Only 231 acres (10.3 percent of surface area) lay between the 15 ft and 30 ft contours, which concentrated walleye into a narrow band.

## Key Findings

**Lake-by-lake results**

- **Lily Lake:** Recommended for removal from the experiment. Occasional stocking of small numbers of rescue northern pike was the only stocking suggested. No further two-story consideration.
- **Long Lake:** Well suited physically and chemically to two-story management. Panfish were roughly 8x (bluegill), 4x (pumpkinseed) and 12x+ (rock bass) statewide medians in trapnet catches. Rainbow trout catch averaged 1.1 per gillnet set (Table 6). Most trout appeared to derive from the 1963 yearling stocking. A strong 1961 walleye year class (about 57 percent of walleyes seen) and a strong northern pike 2-annulus year class were documented. Stocking records are summarized in Table 9.
- **Big Trout Lake:** No rainbow trout were caught despite extensive netting in suitable strata. Largemouth bass exceeded four times the statewide median in trapnets. The author recommended dropping the lake from experimental plans until a trout barrier could be built between Big Trout and Whitefish lakes, and suggested [[Kokanee Salmon]] as an alternative.
- **Greenwood Lake:** Rainbow catch was 0.1 per gillnet set; only three rainbows were taken, all two-annulus fish. Lake herring declined steadily from 1955 to 1963 after commercial removals of 19,417 lb (1951–1954), 60,293 lb (1955–1959) and 33,27x lb (1959–1962). A gravel-filled filter crib was installed in September 1963 to limit downstream trout loss.
- **Hungry Jack Lake:** Rainbow trout catch was 2.0 per gillnet set, but length-frequency and age data suggested they likely came from the single 1962 yearling stocking. Walleye recruitment was absent and smallmouth bass were far less abundant in 1963 than in 1955.
- **Kabekona Lake:** Rainbow catch was 0.1 per set; three rainbows (11–15 in) were taken. Walleye were concentrated at 15–20 ft (64 percent of catch in stratified sets). Fry stocked in Kabekona Creek in May 1961, 1962 and 1963 were monitored by electrofishing and a two-way trap; 17 upstream-migrating rainbows were counted in April–May 1964, including spawning adults. The author considered this evidence of "imprinting" and homing behavior, and the lake's self-sustaining population potential encouraging.

**Overall conclusions**

- Migration through unrestricted outlets was the most significant physical problem; Big Trout and Greenwood both lost trout downstream.
- Competition with warm-water fish, predation, and fry/fingerling size at stocking were identified as additional causes of poor survival.
- Stocking fry at 2,400 per pound (near sac-fry size) was thought to reduce survival; yearling stocking appeared more successful.
- Rainbow trout were the only species considered for two-story management in Long and Hungry Jack lakes. Alternatives such as [[Dolly Varden]] or kokanee salmon were proposed if rainbows failed in Long Lake.
- The report's conclusions are explicitly tentative and need longer-term confirmation.
