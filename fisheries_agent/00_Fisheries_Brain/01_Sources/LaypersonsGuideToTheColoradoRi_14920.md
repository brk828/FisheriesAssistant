# The Colorado River: Water Allocation, Development and Native Fish Recovery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Layperson's Guide from the [[Water Education Foundation]] traces the history of the Colorado River's development, the compromises that divided its water among seven U.S. basin states, Mexico and American Indian tribes, and current issues including water marketing, water quality, endangered species and environmental restoration. Its fish content concerns four endangered native species: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]]. The document frames the river as one of the most sought-after, complex and controversial water resources in the United States. Dams, diversions and non-native species have reduced native fish populations, and recovery programs now run alongside continued water and power development.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River flows about 1,450 miles from Colorado to the Gulf of California and drains a basin of roughly 250,000 square miles. The document reports an average of about 5 trillion gallons flowing annually, though average annual flow at [[Lee's Ferry]] is put at closer to 15 million acre-feet (MAF). Yearly flows have ranged from a low of 5 MAF to more than 24 MAF in the recorded record. The basin exports more water than any other U.S. river basin, supplying Denver, Salt Lake Valley, Albuquerque, Cheyenne and southern California.

Human water development dates to about 600 A.D., when the Anasazi built a distribution system in Chaco Canyon, and the Hohokam built canals from the Salt and Gila rivers. Spanish explorers introduced livestock and ditch systems (acequias), and Mormon settlers expanded irrigation in the Salt Lake Valley. [[John Wesley Powell]] mapped the Green and Colorado rivers in 1869 and argued that large-scale settlement was impractical given limited water. His 1878 proposals were partly incorporated into the Federal Reclamation Act of 1902.

Water is governed by the [[Law of the River]], a body of compacts, treaties, legislation, court decisions and federal actions. Its key pillars include the [[Colorado River Compact]] of 1922, which split the basin into Upper and Lower basins, the [[Boulder Canyon Project Act]] of 1928, and the [[Arizona v. California]] decree of 1964. The Lower Basin apportionment is 7.5 MAF, and the Upper Basin must deliver 75 MAF to the Lower Basin every 10 years. Presently, about 80 percent of Colorado River water is used for agriculture.

The four endangered fish are described as follows:
- **Colorado Squawfish**: found only in the Upper Basin, and the only native species that is not more abundant in the Lower Colorado.
- **Razorback Sucker**: the Lower Basin holds 97 percent of the adult population in the basin.
- **Bonytail Chub**: the Lower Basin holds the only wild stock.
- **Humpback Chub**: the Lower Basin holds the largest stock.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document states that dams have changed the river's character. Originally a muddy, seasonally warm river, parts of the Colorado are now cold and clear because of dams and reservoirs. Manmade dams have altered habitats, blocked spawning migrations, favored many new (non-native) fish species, and depleted native populations toward extinction. Non-native plants have also displaced native vegetation.

Dam operations affect fish directly. Summer releases to meet downstream demand alter the natural hydrograph, removing the spring floods that create warm, nutrient-rich wetland habitat for spawning and juvenile growth. Peak hydropower releases at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] fluctuate with hourly power demand and degrade fish habitat. Proponents of restoring the river argue that [[Lake Powell]] wastes about 8 percent of the Colorado's flow through evaporation and seepage, and that the dam's trapped sediment threatens its long-term use.

Salinity is a major ecosystem and economic concern. The river carries more silt than most rivers because of erosion from the arid Colorado Plateau and salt-bearing shale. Water at Imperial Dam holds about 2,000 pounds of salt per acre-foot, compared with 300 pounds per acre-foot in California's Sacramento River. Annual salinity costs to Lower Basin states are approaching $1 billion.

The [[Salton Sea]], formed in 1905 when the Colorado broke through irrigation canals, is saltier than the Pacific Ocean (25 to 30 percent saltier, per the document). It hosts close to 400 bird species on the Pacific Flyway, and periodic fish die-offs of introduced tilapia result from low dissolved oxygen during algae blooms and from cold winter water. The document also reports bird die-offs, including 150,000 eared grebes in 1992 and 1,400 endangered brown pelicans from botulism.

Lower Basin conservation efforts cover more than fish. The [[Lower Colorado Multi-Species Conservation Program]] is a joint federal and state 50-year program intended to maintain and restore habitat for some 85 plant and animal species within the 100-year floodplain. One of these is the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]].

## Key Findings

**Water allocation and use**
- Water is fully accounted for; the Lower Basin has been using close to or beyond its 7.5 MAF basic apportionment in recent years.
- Under the 1964 decree, California's basic apportionment is 4.4 MAF, Arizona's is 2.8 MAF and Nevada's is 300,000 acre-feet, with shares of surplus water.
- Upper Basin use was about 4.2 MAF in 1997, with an expected yield of about 6 MAF after Lower Basin deliveries. The document also estimates Upper Basin use will reach 79 percent (6.4 MAF) of the 6 MAF by 2030. This figure is internally inconsistent, since 6.4 MAF exceeds 6 MAF, and it is reported here as written.
- Probability of surplus water is projected to fall from about 50 percent in the decade before 2010 to 40 percent by 2020 and 30 percent before 2030.
- [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) delivered about 1.4 MAF in 1997, against a design capacity of 2.2 MAF.

**Native fish recovery**
- The [[Upper Colorado Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] began in 1984 as a 15-year effort with about $59 million spent so far. The San Juan River program is estimated for completion in 2007.
- In 1994, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service designated 1,980 miles of critical habitat for the four endangered fish.
- Lake Mohave holds about 35,000 adult razorback suckers. The goal is 50,000 by the turn of the century. More than 15,000 hatchery fish have been released, and about 70 percent are believed to be spawning successfully.
- A fish ladder at the Redlands Diversion Dam opened 50 miles of historic habitat. Since 1996, 19 endangered Colorado squawfish and about 15,000 other native fish have used it.
- In spring 1996, an experimental flood through the Grand Canyon redistributed sand, scoured non-native vegetation and restored fish habitat. The Bureau plans to repeat such floods as conditions allow.

**Southwestern Willow Flycatcher**
- Nesting had not been confirmed on the lower Colorado since 1902. Surveys beginning in 1996 found six nesting attempts that year and 36 in 1997, with an estimated 230 to 500 nesting pairs remaining rangewide.

**Water quality**
- The 1974 [[Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Act]] addresses salinity commitments to Mexico under Minute 242 (1973).
- The $250 million [[Yuma Desalting Plant]] was completed in 1992 to reclaim 80,000 acre-feet per year. It has been on standby since a Gila River flood damaged its intake, at a cost of $6 million per year. Lining the Coachella Canal has reduced saline return flows to Mexico, so the plant has not been needed.

**Water marketing and transfers**
- The Draft Offstream Storage Rule, released in December 1997, would create a framework for interstate water banking. Arizona's [[Water Banking]] Authority, created in 1996, is the prototype.
- The [[Imperial Irrigation District]] and San Diego County Water Authority signed an agreement in April 1998 for IID to lease up to 300,000 acre-feet per year to San Diego for an initial 45 years.
- The 1989 [[Metropolitan Water District of Southern California]]–IID conservation agreement is expected to save about 108,000 acre-feet annually.

**Tribal and Indian water rights**
- Indian water rights were recognized in the 1908 Winters v. United States decision. The 1964 decree measured reservation water by practicable irrigable acreage.
- Tribes are farming about 60 percent of their irrigable acreage on five lower-basin reservations.

**Animas-La Plata Project**
- Authorized in 1968 to deliver about 190,000 acre-feet to tribes, municipalities and farms, the project remains unbuilt. Its cost estimate is $744 million for the original plan, against $290 million for a scaled-back "ALP Lite" plan.

**Future outlook**
- The document says that controversy over the river will persist as long as people choose to live in the seven basin states and Mexico. Its main themes are conservation, improved management of existing water sources and continued tension between water for people and water for fish.
