# Status of the Gila Topminnow and its Co-occurrence with Mosquitofish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service research paper (RM-198, December 1977) by [[W. L. Minckley]], [[John N. Rinne]] and [[James E. Johnson]] reports that the [[Gila Topminnow]] and the introduced [[Mosquitofish]] coexist for variable periods, especially in complex habitats and near springs, before mosquitofish ultimately eliminate the native fish, apparently by direct predation. Surviving topminnow populations persist in large springs that mosquitofish do not occupy, so these springs serve as a source for repopulation if conditions become suitable. The authors base the report on field discoveries from 1976–1977, re-examined museum records and literature, and sampling in the Santa Cruz River and Sonoita Creek drainages of Arizona.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) is a small poeciliid fish. It was once widespread in low-elevation (<1,800 m) aquatic habitats across the arid American Southwest and was considered the most abundant "low desert" fish in the area before 1940. Its range and abundance have been greatly reduced. The U.S. Department of the Interior listed it as endangered in 1976.

The species prefers shallow water (<20 cm deep) that is warm (generally >20°C). Under former conditions, such places were used almost exclusively by the topminnow, [[Pupfish]] and, seasonally, young native minnows and suckers, none of which are highly predaceous. Introduced fishes, especially mosquitofish and juvenile sunfish and [[Largemouth Bass]], prey heavily on adult and young topminnows.

Two causes of decline are identified: drastic changes in aquatic habitats over the past 70–80 years, and predation by introduced fishes, principally mosquitofish, since about 1930. Published studies show mosquitofish can eliminate the native livebearer within a year of introduction, in both natural and experimental settings.

The authors state that recovery requires (1) discovery of natural habitats where populations can be established, (2) renovation of formerly occupied waters and reintroduction, and (3) creation of quasi-natural habitats for maintaining topminnows. Because replacement by non-native fishes can occur in less than a season, the authors call for continuous surveillance, public education to limit unauthorized fish movements, and enforcement of existing laws, possibly with new legislation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Collections were made in streams draining semi-desert oak-grassland in Pima and Santa Cruz Counties, Arizona. The area holds essentially the entire present range of the Gila topminnow, with two additional natural populations known from [[Bylas Springs]] and [[Cocio Wash]].

**Upper Santa Cruz River.** The stream originates in the Canelo Hills at about 1,800 m and flows south into Mexico. Its drainage area is 213 km². Since 1949, discharge at a gage 2.8 km north of the border has ranged from none to almost 140 m³/s, with a mean near 0.1 m³/s. The channel is incised 2–6 m into silt-sand and organic deposits. Remnant [[Cienega]] habitats persist along spring seepages. Aquatic vegetation includes sago pondweed, water milfoil, naiad, stonewart, watercress and sedges. Roughly 900 km² of the drainage lies in Mexico, where native fish status is essentially unknown.

**Sonoita Creek.** This incised, erosive stream has a drainage area of about 541 km² and mean discharge near 0.2 m³/s, ranging from none to about 400 m³/s. [[Lake Patagonia]], filled in the early 1970s, stabilized flow in the lower creek and created habitat for introduced still-water fishes. The fauna originally included the same five native fishes as the upper Santa Cruz River, plus speckled dace and a pupfish. Three native taxa have since been eradicated, and at least 12 introduced species have been recorded.

**Co-occurrence history.** Records of the two species together in Arizona date from 1926 (Gila River at Dome and Salt River near Tempe) and 1941 (Tonto Creek above Roosevelt Dam). The topminnow was not recorded again at the first two sites, which are now dry or supported by wastewater. Tonto Creek held topminnows until at least 1951, but only mosquitofish had been taken there since 1964.

## Key Findings

**Coexistence in the upper Santa Cruz River**
- **April 1976:** At site A, mosquitofish outnumbered topminnows by about 100:1 (visual estimates only). Two nearby sites held green sunfish and mosquitofish but no topminnows.
- **September 1976:** Topminnows were present at all but one drying pool, at higher abundance than before (about 6 individuals per 150 mosquitofish). All life-history stages were present. Adults were never seen schooling together, and topminnows tended to occupy open water and areas of current.
- **March 1977:** Ratios were 23:1 mosquitofish to topminnow at site A and 3:1 at site D. Green sunfish made up 97% of the fishes taken in an isolated pool at site B.
- Before this discovery, one locality had been sampled five times between 1964 and 1974 without recording topminnows.

**Coexistence in Sonoita Creek**
- Topminnows occurred sporadically from 1964, usually in quiet, protected places. Mosquitofish were rare until about 1969 and then spread rapidly.
- In December 1976, both species were taken together downstream of Lake Patagonia.
- In 1977, topminnows were present in borrow pits near the dam. Downstream in the creek, topminnows made up more than half of the poeciliid fauna, and longfin dace dominated the overall fauna.
- In both streams, the two species segregated by habitat: mosquitofish occupied quieter water, while topminnows used moderate current near shore or associated with logs and debris.

**Springs as refugia**
- Mosquitofish did not penetrate the source of Cottonwood Spring (Sonoita drainage) for at least 2 years, although they had direct water connection to it.
- At [[Sheehy Spring]], the source pool was occupied only by topminnows and [[Gila Chub]]. Mosquitofish and chubs lived downstream.
- Current topminnow populations, except at Cocio Wash, occur in or near constant-temperature, permanent springs. These springs are the likely reservoirs for recolonization.

**Range-wide resurvey (1950 to May 1977)**
- Resurveys of all known localities showed replacement of topminnows by mosquitofish at 15 sites, elimination of all aquatic life by desiccation at 7 sites, and persistence of topminnows at 7 isolated springs or stream segments.
- In more than 150 samples from the Gila River system below 1,800 m (1964–77), three of the seven current topminnow localities were newly discovered. About 107 mosquitofish collection sites were recorded.

**Interaction duration**
- In Sonoita Creek, potential interaction had lasted about 7 years, based on the known introduction and spread of mosquitofish.
- In the other stream, mosquitofish had been present for at least 13 years, and the topminnow was thought to be eliminated until its rediscovery in 1976.

**Conclusions**
- Published accounts and experiments show replacement within about a year. Field records show that coexistence can last much longer in complex habitats and near springs.
- The non-native species ultimately replaces the native fish, apparently by direct predation.
- Large springs not inhabited by mosquitofish are the key refuges. The authors argue that coexisting populations are a useful opportunity to study how the native fish might be maintained.
