# Biological Opinion on Transportation and Delivery of Central Arizona Project Water to the Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) biological opinion, dated June 1993, evaluates the formal section 7 consultation requested by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BR) on delivering [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water to users in the Gila River basin in Arizona and New Mexico. The opinion addresses only the risk that the project introduces and spreads non-native aquatic species; the Santa Cruz River subbasin is excluded and will be consulted on separately.

FWS concludes that the proposed delivery is likely to jeopardize the continued existence of the threatened [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] and the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and is likely to adversely modify proposed critical habitat for spikedace, loach minnow, and razorback sucker. It finds no jeopardy for [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], or [[Bald Eagle]]. The reasonable and prudent alternative (RPA) requires physical barriers, electrical barrier maintenance, non-native fish monitoring, $500,000 annually in funding for 25 years, and an education program.

## Background & Description

The opinion covers the CAP, a roughly 100-year-life aqueduct system that diverts Arizona's [[Colorado River]] allocation at [[Lake Havasu]]. The system was due for completion in 1992, and in 1990 a total of 744,506 acre-feet was delivered under interim contracts to 38 agricultural and municipal users (Appendix 1 lists them, including the [[San Carlos Irrigation Project]], Maricopa-Stanfield, Hohokam, and Central Arizona irrigation districts, and the Gila River Indian Reservation).

The listed and proposed species profiled are:

- **Spikedace** (threatened, listed 1986): small silvery fish; four known populations remain (Aravaipa Creek, upper Gila, Verde River, Eagle Creek).
- **Loach minnow** (threatened, listed 1986): olive-colored; found in six locations, including Aravaipa Creek, upper Gila, San Francisco and Tularosa rivers, White River, and Blue River.
- **Gila topminnow** (endangered, listed 1967): livebearer; nine natural populations remain, all but one in the Santa Cruz basin.
- **Desert pupfish** (endangered, listed 1986): critical habitat at Quitobaquito Spring and in Imperial County, California.
- **Razorback sucker** (endangered, listed 1991): grows past two feet; now sporadic in about 750 miles of the upper and 200 miles of the lower Colorado River.
- **Colorado squawfish** (endangered, listed 1967): large minnow up to six feet; experimental non-essential populations established in the Salt and Verde rivers.
- **Bald eagle** (endangered): desert nesting population breeds earlier than northern counterparts; nests along the Bill Williams, Agua Fria, Verde, Salt, and Gila rivers.

The main causes of decline are habitat destruction and alteration, plus competition and predation from non-native fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Connectivity.** Historically, Gila basin streams were linked by surface flow. Dams and dewatering fragmented them, which limited non-native spread. The CAP reconnects these systems with a perennial waterway from the Colorado River to the Gila mainstem, Hassayampa, Agua Fria, Salt, and Verde rivers, and the San Pedro River.

**Entry routes.** Fish enter the CAP mainly from Lake Havasu, with nine non-native species documented in the aqueduct (Mueller 1989). Lake Pleasant, once enlarged for CAP storage, is a second source. Irrigation return flows, turnouts, and the Florence-Casa Grande Canal (which heads behind [[Ashurst-Hayden Dam]]) provide routes to the Gila River. Ashurst-Hayden Dam is not an effective fish barrier. [[Bait Bucket Introductions]] by anglers are a major additional pathway, especially near Picacho Reservoir and San Carlos Reservoir.

**Environmental baseline.** Of the 14 native fishes that remain in or have been restocked into the Gila basin, 71 percent are federally listed as endangered or threatened (8 species) or are candidates (2 species). Only 4 have no special status. Non-native fishes such as [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Mosquitofish]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] have had substantial adverse impacts, while [[Fathead Minnow]] appears to have had little effect.

**Non-native threats.** The opinion considers five species from Matter (1991): [[Striped Bass]], [[White Bass]], [[Blue Tilapia]], [[Rainbow Smelt]], and [[Grass Carp]]. Blue tilapia is the one considered likely to reproduce in the San Pedro system, and its diet overlaps with spikedace and loach minnow. Other concerns include [[Asian Clam]], [[Crayfish]], and the [[Asian Tapeworm]], which is associated with red shiner introductions. The opinion also notes that aquarium-fish dumping poses threats from several families.

**Key sub-basins.** The Salt and Verde rivers hold one-third of remaining occupied spikedace habitat. Aravaipa Creek is rated at high risk of extirpation. The upper San Pedro basin is described as among the most promising recovery habitats, and a [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]] was established on 36 miles of river corridor.

## Key Findings

- **Jeopardy determinations:** Likely jeopardy for spikedace, loach minnow, Gila topminnow, and razorback sucker. No jeopardy for desert pupfish, Colorado squawfish, or bald eagle.
- **Critical habitat:** Adverse modification is likely for proposed critical habitat for spikedace, loach minnow, and razorback sucker.
- **Probability of introduction:** The opinion states that over the 100-year life of the CAP, the probability is high that one or more non-native fish species will use the CAP to colonize listed-species habitat. Timing and identity cannot be predicted.
- **Cumulative effects:** Repeated introductions raise the probability of establishment. Non-native fishes may act additively, multiplicatively, or synergistically. Non-Federal introductions (sport stocking, bait, aquarium dumping, aquaculture escapes) are expected to have strong adverse effects.
- **Population-specific risks:** Aravaipa Creek populations of spikedace and loach minnow would probably be extirpated. The Cow Creek and Humbug Creek Gila topminnow populations will probably be extirpated. Razorback sucker reestablishment in the Verde, Salt, and Gila rivers will most likely be prevented.
- **Bald eagle:** Effects are uncertain. Eagles are not prey-specific, so effects depend on whether forage remains abundant and catchable. Blue tilapia may concentrate in shallow warm water, making it more available to eagles.
- **Reasonable and prudent alternative:**
  - Construct four drop-structure barriers: two on Aravaipa Creek (completed within 3.5 years) and two on the San Pedro River between Redington and Fairbank (completed within 6 years).
  - Maintain and operate the existing electrical barriers on the SRP and Florence-Casa Grande canals.
  - Monitor non-native fish in the CAP, SRP canals, Florence-Casa Grande Canal, Salt River, Gila River below Coolidge Dam, and San Pedro River below Redington.
  - Pay $250,000 annually for 25 years to FWS for conservation of listed fishes (Term 6).
  - Deposit $250,000 annually for 25 years into the Gila Basin Non-native Aquatic Species Control Fund for research and control (Term 7). Together these total $500,000 annually.
  - Run an information and education program for at least 5 years on bait-bucket transfers and aquarium dumping.
- **Incidental take:** Take of spikedace, loach minnow, Gila topminnow, and razorback sucker is anticipated but cannot be quantified. It is considered exceeded if the RPA is altered or not carried out, which requires reinitiation. No take of bald eagle is authorized.
- **Conservation recommendations (discretionary):** Construct additional barriers (Redfield Canyon, Babocomari River, Hassayampa River, Agua Fria River above Lake Pleasant); encourage annual dry-up of CAP-connected canals and ditches; organize a multi-agency effort to resolve sport fishing and native fish conflicts; oppose introductions of new non-native species to the lower Colorado basin.
- **Rejected alternatives (Appendix 2):** These included screening or treating CAP water with ozone or chemicals (infeasible or harmful), closing the Gila River at Coolidge Dam to fishing (outside BR authority), rotenone treatment of Aravaipa Creek, and requesting that agencies halt stocking or live bait use (judged unenforceable or ineffective).
- **Reinitiation triggers:** Exceeded take, new information, modified project actions, or a new listing or critical habitat designation.
