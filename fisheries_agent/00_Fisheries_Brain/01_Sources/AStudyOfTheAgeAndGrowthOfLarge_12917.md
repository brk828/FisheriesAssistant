# A Study of the Age and Growth of Largemouth Bass in False River and Old River, Pointe Coupee Parish, Louisiana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #freshwater-ecology #largemouth-bass

## Executive Summary

This 1965 Master's thesis by Jerry Brashier examined age and growth of [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) in two Louisiana oxbow lakes: [[False River]] and [[Old River]], located 15 miles apart in [[Pointe Coupee Parish]]. Collections occurred March–November 1964 using electric shockers and angler catches (241 bass from False River, 208 from Old River). Old River experiences annual flooding from the [[Mississippi River]] at stages above 13.8 feet msl (averaging 300 days/year), while False River water levels remain stable. Despite similar water chemistry, Old River bass grew faster, heavier, and exhibited higher condition factors than False River bass. Old River six-year-old bass (19.0 inches) matched the length of eight-year-old False River bass (19.3 inches). The study attributes superior growth in Old River to nutrient replenishment, food concentration during water fluctuations, and higher overall fish productivity (533.8 vs. 197.7 pounds/acre standing crop).

## Background & Description

**Study Sites:**
- **[[False River]]**: 3,212-acre oxbow lake cut off from Mississippi River in 1722; 22 miles shoreline; maximum depth 55–96 feet; densely populated shoreline with camps and homes; abundant aquatic vegetation including [[Coontail]] (*Ceratophyllum demersum*), duckweed (*Lemna* sp.), and [[Water Hyacinth]] (*Eichhornia crassipes*); standing crop 197.6 pounds/acre (53.3 pounds game fish); F/C ratio 4.1
- **[[Old River]]**: 4,100 acres at low water; 31 miles shoreline; average depth 15 feet; forested with [[Bald Cypress]] (*Taxodium distichum*), [[Black Gum]] (*Nyssa sylvatica*), [[Black Willow]] (*Salix nigra*); flooded annually by Mississippi River through "the Narrows" channel; standing crop 533.8 pounds/acre (105.8 pounds game fish); F/C ratio 3.7

**Water Chemistry:**
Both lakes classified as "hard water" with bicarbonate alkalinity 115–156 ppm. False River exhibited summer thermal stratification (0 ppm dissolved oxygen at 26–58 feet depth on August 19, 1964). Calcium ranged 20–36 ppm (medium to rich calcium lakes). pH values 7.0–9.8, higher in surface waters during summer photosynthesis. Nitrate nitrogen highest in early spring, decreased in summer except in hypolimnion.

**Collection Methods:**
- 241 bass from False River (24 hook-and-line by author, 51 from fishing rodeos, remainder electric shocker)
- 208 bass from Old River (59 from fishing rodeos, remainder electric shocker)
- Two shocker types used: 110V 1,600W AC (less effective) and 220V 3,000W AC (maximum 76 bass in 2 hours)
- Standard and total lengths measured to 0.1 inch; weights to 0.1 pound (>1 lb) or nearest gram (<1 lb)

**Age Determination:**
Scales taken 1 inch posterior to depressed pectoral fin, mounted between glass slides, projected at 23× magnification. Annuli counted on projected scales. Calculated lengths determined using nomograph with correction factors: False River = 1.15, Old River = 0.53.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Differences:**
The fundamental ecological distinction between these systems lies in hydrological regime. Old River's annual flooding creates a dynamic "flood-drawdown" cycle that:
1. Adds nutrients from Mississippi River water and decaying terrestrial vegetation
2. Produces invertebrate food (insects, [[Crayfish]]) in flooded areas
3. Concentrates fish and prey during drawdown, increasing predation efficiency
4. Exposes bottom sediments to air, mineralizing organic matter and replenishing nutrients

False River maintains stable water levels year-round, lacking these productivity pulses. The lake's greater depth (maximum 55–96 feet vs. 15 feet average) correlates negatively with productivity, as demonstrated by Rawson (1952) and Hayes (1957).

**Food Web Structure:**
Old River's higher percentage of harvestable sunfish (82.9% vs. 49.1%) indicates increased predation pressure from bass concentrated during drawdowns. False River's abundance of small sunfish suggests competition with young-of-year bass for food resources. [[Crayfish]] abundance in Old River provides important forage, particularly in backwater habitats (Lambou 1959).

**Thermal Regime:**
False River's summer stratification (thermocline at 20–26 feet, anoxic hypolimnion) limits available habitat during warm months. Surface temperatures ranged 39–92°F across seasons. Old River's shallower profile likely prevents persistent stratification, maintaining more uniform oxygen distribution.

**Soil Characteristics:**
Both lakes situated in Mississippi River floodplain with fertile alluvial soils. False River: Commerce-Mhoon silt/clay loams (pH 6.8, 81% base saturation). Old River: Mixed alluvial lands, Sharkey clay (pH 6.2, 95% base saturation), and Commerce-Mhoon types. High calcium and magnesium content in soils reflected in water chemistry.

## Key Findings

**Growth Rates:**
- **First-year growth**: Old River bass averaged 8.0 inches total length at first annulus; False River bass 6.8 inches
- **Second-year growth**: Old River 12.0 inches; False River 10.7 inches
- **Third-year growth**: Old River 14.3 inches; False River 12.9 inches
- **Comparative maturity**: Old River six-year-old bass (19.0 inches) equivalent to False River eight-year-old bass (19.3 inches)
- **Young-of-year (August 1964)**: Old River 5.3 inches, 43 grams (n=30); False River 4.2 inches, 17.4 grams (n=9)
- **Young-of-year (November 1964)**: Old River 7.7 inches, 103 grams (n=11); False River 4.7 inches, 22.7 grams (n=12)

**Weight Relationships:**
- **One-year-old**: Old River 12.3 oz; False River 7.2 oz
- **Two-year-old**: Old River 21.1 oz; False River 14.6 oz
- **Length-weight equations**: 
  - False River: log W = -0.8500 + (3.2200)(log L)
  - Old River: log W = -0.7688 + (3.1495)(log L)
- At equivalent lengths, bass weighed similarly, though False River bass >14 inches were slightly heavier

**Condition Factors:**
- **Average C values**: Old River 58; False River 55
- **Average K values**: Old River 2.86; False River 2.67
- Both lakes exceeded K values from 10 other U.S. regions listed by Carlander (1953), which ranged 1.93–2.63
- Old River bass showed higher condition factors in all age groups
- **Seasonal patterns**: False River age groups I–II showed better condition in spring; Old River age group I better in late summer

**Growth Seasonality:**
Fastest growth occurred early spring following annulus formation. Age group I bass from False River grew 1.6 inches by May, 3.0 inches by mid-June. Old River age group I grew 1.9 inches by mid-May, 4.0 inches by August. Growth slowed markedly in late summer and fall for both populations.

**Length Distribution:**
Collections did not form typical declining length-frequency distributions due to gear selectivity. Electric shockers selected smaller bass (only one >18 inches collected); fishing rodeos yielded larger bass. Average lengths: False River 10.8 inches (mode 10–10.9 inches, 13.7%); Old River 11.8 inches (mode 11–11.9 inches, 16.7%).

**Productivity Indicators:**
- Old River standing crop (533.8 lb/acre) was 2.7× higher than False River (197.6 lb/acre)
- Game fish comprised 19.8% of Old River biomass vs. 27.0% at False River
- Higher abundance of harvestable-size sunfish in Old River (82.9% vs. 49.1%) indicates effective predation during drawdown concentration events

**Environmental Mechanisms:**
Superior growth in Old River attributed to:
1. Annual nutrient pulses from Mississippi River flooding
2. Production of invertebrate foods in flooded terrestrial vegetation
3. Concentration of prey during drawdown phases
4. Mineralization of organic matter during sediment exposure
5. Shallower mean depth increasing overall productivity
6. Higher crayfish availability in fluctuating water levels

**Regional Comparisons:**
Old River and False River young-of-year bass (7.7 and 4.7 inches by November) exceeded growth in other Louisiana lakes studied by Lambou (1968): Lake Bistineau 4.5 inches, Clear Lake 4.2 inches, Lake Providence 3.5 inches. First annulus lengths (8.0 and 6.8 inches) comparable to other southern waters but exceeded northern populations, consistent with temperature and growing season effects noted by Bennett (1937) and potential genetic factors (Strawn 1961).
