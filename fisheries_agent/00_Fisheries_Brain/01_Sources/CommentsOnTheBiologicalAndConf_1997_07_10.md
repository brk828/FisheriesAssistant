# Comments on the Biological and Conference Opinion on Lower Colorado River Operations and Maintenance

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Catostomidae #Cyprinidae #ConservationPolicy #EndangeredSpecies

## Executive Summary

This document is a critical scientific commentary by ichthyologist **W.L. Minckley** (Arizona State University) addressing the 1997 U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service's "Biological and Conference Opinion" (BO) governing Bureau of Reclamation operations on the Lower Colorado River (LCR). Minckley critiques the BO's fragmented, single-species management approach — particularly regarding the endangered **[[Bonytail Chub]]** and **[[Razorback Sucker]]** — arguing it lacks the ecosystem-level synthesis needed for genuine recovery. Central scientific themes include: the genetic management of augmentation stocks, the ecological superiority of numerous small, manageable habitat impoundments over large ones (primarily for non-native species control), and a detailed refutation of the BO's assumptions about historical "recruitment" events in Lake Mead and Lake Mohave. A companion memo provides an in-depth analysis of razorback sucker recruitment biology, population age structure, and the demographic mathematics of long-lived, low-turnover fish populations facing extinction.

## Background & Description

- **Species of primary concern:**
  - **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) — federally endangered cyprinid of the LCR.
  - **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — federally endangered catostomid; population age estimates from otolith analysis (McCarthy & Minckley 1987) suggest individuals living **~26–44 years**, with total lifespan estimated at **40–50+ years**.
- **Context:** Comments respond to the 1997 [[Biological and Conference Opinion]] (BO) governing Lake Mead-to-Southerly International Boundary operations, and its relationship to the forming **[[Multi-species Conservation Plan]] (MSCP)**, a 50-year holistic biota management plan.
- **Key water bodies discussed:**
  - **[[Lake Mead]]** — impounded starting in the 1930s; harbors small, poorly understood razorback sucker population.
  - **[[Lake Mohave]]** — formed by Davis Dam (partial filling/drainage 1946–1951, final filling 1954); source of the primary razorback sucker augmentation stock.
  - **[[Grand Canyon]]** reach — potential historic source population for Lake Mead fish prior to cold hypolimnetic releases from Lake Powell (post-1962-63).
- Secondary species mentioned in the BO but treated more briefly: **[[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]]**, **[[Yuma Clapper Rail]]**, **[[Flat-tailed Horned Lizard]]**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat succession & floodplain ecology:** Minckley argues the historic LCR floodplain was characterized by numerous small aquatic habitats in varying successional stages, to which native biota were adapted — favoring a strategy of many small (3–15 acre) constructed water bodies over a few large ones (>100 acres).
- **Non-native fish invasion as primary threat:** Repeatedly emphasized as the dominant barrier to native fish recruitment — citing the BO's own language: *"the habitat values of backwaters to native fish are negated to a large degree by the subsequent invasion of non-native fish."* Adult natives may persist, but juvenile recruitment fails once non-natives establish.
- **Management tractability:** Small habitats are more manageable via draining, refilling, or piscicide application; large lake-like habitats resist non-native eradication and are costlier and less permitted-friendly to treat.
- **Reproductive/recruitment ecology of Razorback Sucker:**
  - Fecundity estimated at **~1800 eggs/cm standard length** per female.
  - Even minimal survival-to-adulthood increases (e.g., 0.1% → 1.0%) could double a theoretical population, "resetting" persistence trajectories despite ongoing decline.
  - Long lifespan buffers demographic collapse: an annual recruitment:mortality imbalance of 1:2 could sustain a shrinking population for **>90 years**.
  - Documented low-level recruitment (age 2–12 yrs via otolith aging) from canals below Parker Dam, seepage ponds in southern California, Lake Mohave, and the lower Little Colorado River.
  - Larvae recorded in Lake Mead in spring 1997 (P. Holden, pers. comm.), indicating some ongoing reproduction despite presumed high predation.
- **Predator dynamics:** The BO's core recruitment provision assumes a historically "predator-free" or "rapid-growth-out-of-predation-range" window during reservoir filling (1930s Lake Mead; 1950s Lake Mohave) allowed the surviving adult cohorts to establish — a scenario Minckley finds plausible but empirically unverifiable given current data constraints.

## Key Findings

1. **Fragmented, single-species BO structure is scientifically unsound.** The BO lacks ecosystem-level synthesis despite rhetorical commitment to "ecosystem management," risking duplicative, uncoordinated Reclamation working groups instead of integrated action ahead of the MSCP.

2. **Genetic management of augmentation stocks is critical.** Use of non-Lake Mohave razorback sucker stock below Parker Dam requires close attention to genetic variability (citing [[Dowling et al. 1996]] mitochondrial DNA studies).

3. **Ambiguity in stocking targets undermines accountability.** The BO conflates "stocking 50,000 fish" with "establishing a population of 50,000 fish" — vastly different management outcomes. Minckley recommends stocked fish exceed **~35 cm total length** to reduce predation vulnerability.

4. **Small, numerous constructed habitats (3–15 acres) are preferable to large impoundments (600+ acres)** for both cost-effectiveness and non-native species control, supporting a rotational/successional management model.

5. **The BO's Recruitment Provision (RPA 10) is scientifically unsupportable as written.** It is based on unverified verbal/hearsay reports rather than citable data, and demands historic ecological conditions ("1950s recruitment conditions" in Lake Mohave; unspecified Lake Mead/Grand Canyon conditions) that cannot be reconstructed or empirically validated.

6. **Two prerequisites make BO-mandated recruitment research infeasible:**
   - Age determination of individual razorback suckers requires otolith extraction (lethal), unacceptable for a listed endangered species.
   - Fine-grained historical river/reservoir operational data at the necessary spatiotemporal resolution does not exist and cannot be reconstructed.

7. **Existing scientific and technical knowledge is already sufficient for recovery action** (per [[Minckley et al. 1991]]); further vague calls for "research on habitat preferences" risk becoming unproductive "money sinks" and should be replaced with synthesis of existing data followed by targeted, hypothesis-driven gap-filling studies.

8. **Documented low-level wild recruitment already occurs** at multiple LCR sites (Lake Mohave, canals, Little Colorado River), contradicting an implicit BO assumption of complete reproductive failure — although populations remain in a **detectable decline post-~1990** ([[Marsh 1994]]).

9. **Inequity in cross-species compensation requirements:** The BO mandates extensive habitat compensation for Southwestern Willow Flycatcher losses but fails to require equivalent compensation mechanisms for bonytail chub/razorback sucker habitat losses from Reclamation operations.

10. **Recommendation:** RPA 10 (Recruitment Provision) should be withdrawn as an actionable management mandate and reframed strictly as a continued basic-research question into razorback sucker biology, given the infeasibility of its underlying premises.
