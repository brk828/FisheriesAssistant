# Selenium Toxicity, Speciation and Bioaccumulation in Aquatic Ecosystems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a long, multi-part encyclopedia-style review of selenium as an environmental contaminant affecting fish, wildlife and invertebrates, with a strong focus on the western United States and the San Joaquin Valley of California. It contrasts the largely hazard-focused fish and wildlife literature with the largely beneficial-focused human health literature, and attributes much of the divergence to dose and food-chain biomagnification. The review covers chemical forms, sources, bioconcentration, toxicity thresholds for fish and birds, regulatory criteria, and tissue concentration benchmarks. Its principal conclusion is that selenium's safe margin is narrow, that organic forms (notably [[Selenomethionine]]) are the most bioavailable and toxic in the diet, and that speciation matters more than total concentration for setting safe levels.

## Background & Description

Selenium (Se, atomic number 34, atomic weight 78.96) is a semimetallic element in group VI with chemistry resembling sulfur and tellurium. It occurs in four natural valence states (+6, +4, 0, -2), and the document notes it forms about 0.09 ppm of the Earth's crust. Selenium is essential at low doses but toxic at higher levels. Human uses include photocopying, glass, stainless steel, fungicides, solar cells and rectifiers. The document was prepared as an encyclopedia section, with much of its aquatic material drawn from an earlier [[Selenium Hazards Synoptic Review]] by Eisler (1985) and a later summary by Sorenson (1991). The text carries OCR noise and several revision notes, and the later sections reproduce a 1990 report by [[Moore et Al Report|Moore et al.]] almost verbatim.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sources:** Combustion of coal is the largest single human source, accounting for more than 60% of industrial selenium emissions. Agricultural subsurface drainage, mining, sewage effluent, irrigation return flows and phosphate fertilizers also contribute.
- **Geology:** Seleniferous Cretaceous and Tertiary marine shales of the Coast Ranges are the main source of selenium in western San Joaquin Valley soils. Pedocal (alkaline, arid) soils favor mobile selenates, whereas acid pedalfer soils in the East favor insoluble forms.
- **Aquatic concentrations:** Ocean average is 0.09 µg/L. The USGS 1974-1981 survey found median river concentrations below 1 µg/L. San Joaquin Valley drainage has reached up to 4,000 ppb, and Kesterson drainage 24-430 µg/L.
- **Cycling:** Selenium is methylated by microbes, cycled through the food web and built up in slow, productive waters such as sloughs, wetlands and evaporation ponds. Finer, organic pond sediments accumulate more selenium than sandy riverine sediments.
- **Bioconcentration:** Reported BCFs at 1 µg/L as Se-methionine were about 16,000 for algae, 200,000 for daphnids and 5,000 for bluegill. Lemly (1989) gave a range of 500 to 35,000 for 5-30 µg/L exposures. Biomagnification factors between trophic levels were reported as 3 to 7.
- **Plants and predators:** Selenium accumulator plants (e.g., [[Astragalus]] species) are described as "converter plants" that transform inert selenium into soluble forms and may spread it when they die or are transported by livestock.
- **Kesterson National Wildlife Refuge:** This site was a major focus of the document as a case of selenium-driven avian reproductive failure and deformities. Benthic invertebrates there reached 26-119 µg/g (dry weight) in food-chain organisms, and mosquitofish from a contaminated California pond reached 26-98 mg/kg.

## Key Findings

**Toxicity thresholds and criteria**

- Excess selenium as low as 3-8 ppb in water can cause life-threatening effects in feral freshwater fish (Sorenson 1991).
- EPA freshwater criteria: chronic 5 µg/L (four-day average) and acute 20 µg/L (one-hour average). Marine criteria are 71 µg/L chronic and 300 µg/L acute.
- The USFWS (1986) recommended target levels for cleanup of Kesterson and the San Luis Drain: water 2 ppb, sediment 4 ppm dry weight, warmwater fish food 5 ppm and waterfowl food 3 ppm.
- The California Central Valley standard for evaporation ponds requires special management (e.g., hazing) when aquatic invertebrates reach 4 ppm dry weight.
- Fish tissue benchmarks: skeletal muscle no more than 5 ppm and liver or gonads no more than 10 ppm (dry weight) to protect fish reproduction. Whole-body levels above 0.5 mg/kg are considered harmful to fish and predators. The "3/30 guideline" suggests predator effects are rare below 3 mg/kg and common above 30 mg/kg.
- Dietary thresholds: Hamilton et al. (1990) suggest dietary selenium below 3 µg/g for fish safety. Skorupa et al. (1989) estimated 5.6 ppm for aquatic birds.

**Species-level results**

- **[[Bluegill Sunfish Selenium Studies]]:** In a 323-day trial, 30 ppm selenite and 30 ppm selenomethionine diets caused significant adult mortality. Thirty-seven percent of fish on 30 ppm selenomethionine developed true lens cataracts. Larvae from parents on selenium diets showed significant mortality and teratogenesis. Acute 96-hour LC50 for selenomethionine was about 0.009-0.010 mg/L.
- **[[Chinook and Coho Salmon Selenium Toxicity]]:** Coho were more sensitive than chinook. Waterborne selenite was more toxic than selenomethionine, which was more toxic than selenate. In 96-hour tests, chinook selenite LC50 was 13.8 mg/L in freshwater, and selenomethionine was above 21.6 mg/L. Advanced fry at 280 ppb selenium had significant growth reductions. Dietary selenomethionine at 30 ppm or 60 ppm impaired seawater osmoregulation.
- **[[Fathead Minnow Selenium Exposure]]:** At 20 and 36 mg/L selenate, almost all larvae developed edema and died within seven days, although reproduction and mating were normal. Dietary exposure at up to 29.5 ppm did not affect reproduction but reduced adult growth.
- **[[Rainbow Trout Selenium Exposure]]:** Waterborne selenite at 47.2 µg/L caused significant mortality, and bone calcium was reduced above 12.4 µg/L. The 96-hour LC50 was 1.80 mg/L.
- **[[Mosquitofish and Kesterson Contamination]]:** Whole-body mean of 197 ppm dry weight was reported in polluted California mosquitofish. Trinity River (Texas) mosquitofish reached a maximum of 0.52 mg/kg.
- **[[Striped Bass Selenium Tests]]:** The 96-hour LC50 for selenomethionine was 0.003-0.004 mg/L, and 3.0 mg/L of a selenate:selenite mixture did not affect 90-day growth or survival.

**Relative toxicity and form**

- Organic selenium (especially selenomethionine) is more bioavailable, more bioconcentrated and more toxic than inorganic forms. Organic compounds can comprise 30-60% of selenium in some fresh and marine waters.
- The ranking of toxicity given (most to least) is hydrogen selenide, selenomethionine (diet), selenite, selenomethionine (water), selenate, elemental selenium, metal selenides, and methylated compounds.
- Selenite was about 10 times more toxic to fish than selenate.
- Mixtures of selenate and selenite were additive in toxicity to chinook and coho salmon.

**Interactions with other contaminants**

- Selenium interacts antagonistically with arsenic, cadmium, mercury, silver and thallium in some circumstances, but synergistic effects with mercury at low aqueous mercury concentrations have also been observed.
- Sorenson (1991) cautions against using selenium supplementation to treat mercury-contaminated waters, because synergistic effects can eliminate an entire fish population.
- Sweden's lake supplementation with selenium was followed by negative effects on fish reproduction.

**Human health contrast**

- Selenium is described as a potential carcinogen at high doses, and as protective against cancers at low doses. The IARC (1975) found no suggestion of carcinogenicity in humans.
- The document argues that dose and food-chain factors largely explain the contrast between the hazard-focused wildlife literature and the benefit-focused human health literature. The narrow window between deficiency and toxicity is central to this argument.

**Regulatory and management points**

- Selenium is listed as one of 129 EPA priority pollutants and is a Clean Water Act toxic pollutant.
- The drinking water MCL is 0.01 mg/L (10 µg/L).
- The California Central Valley regional board adopted chronic objectives of 2-10 µg/L as monthly means for several wetlands and rivers, but the USEPA disapproved some of these objectives.
- Texas soils had a statewide 90th percentile of 1.9 mg/kg dry weight.
- Chile's fish limit of 0.05 mg/kg is the lowest legal limit cited; the U.S. apparently has no federal fish tissue limit.
