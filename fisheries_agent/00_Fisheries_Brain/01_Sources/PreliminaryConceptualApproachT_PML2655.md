# Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery: Preliminary Conceptual Approach and Hatchery Program Review

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1985 package from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) on recovering endangered fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. It contains a cover correspondence letter (May 1985), an internal memorandum on a task force review, a draft "Preliminary Conceptual Approach to Recovery of Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin" (dated May 10, 1985), and a Colorado Water Congress proposal and report on hatchery rearing of [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] (March 1985, by Richard A. Valdez, Ecosystem Research Institute).

The conceptual approach argues that a recovery program is needed, with eventual delisting as the goal. It proposes near-term, intermediate-term and equilibrium-period actions, a Section 7 consultation approach limiting mitigation to projects with direct physical impacts, and a set of funding sources. The hatchery review concludes that existing facilities can produce only about 350,000 fingerlings per year against an estimated need of 500,000, and that a new principal hatchery (estimated at $8.3 to $8.6 million) is likely required.

## Background & Description

**Species covered (four rare Colorado River fishes):**
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): federally endangered.
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): federally endangered.
- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*): federally endangered.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): candidate species, not fully protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

The documents are administrative and policy texts rather than field research. The conceptual approach was prepared for the [[Colorado River Coordinating Committee]], with input from the [[Biology Subcommittee]] and [[Hydrology Subcommittee]]. Its legal framework rests on Sections 4, 7 and 3 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] and on the [[Interstate Water Compacts]] and [[State Water Rights Administration]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The document states that Basin development (agriculture, mineral extraction, hydroelectric power, water supply) has largely altered the air, land and water conditions for these species. Such changes are described as "largely irreversible."
- Named threats include predation by non-native fish, historical irrigation diversions that restricted range, operation of federal reservoirs and hydroelectric projects (including [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]]), and reduced spring flows and cold-water releases.
- A 1983 event combining high spring runoff with Flaming Gorge operations produced a longer-than-normal period of cold water in the Green River, which "may have eliminated significant numbers of newly hatched endangered fish" that year.
- The Biology Subcommittee found, after more than a year of review, no evidence that water depletion had adversely affected endangered species. The document also notes that the most optimistic development projections show no significant flow or habitat change for about 60 years.
- The 1922 [[Colorado River Compact]] guarantees average delivery of 75 million acre-feet per decade from the Upper to the Lower Basin, which the text notes sustains flows through fish habitat.
- The field study on which the document relies began in 1979, with data collection described as incomplete and not allowing firm population-status determinations.

## Key Findings

**1. Preliminary Conceptual Approach (May 1985 draft)**
- Recovery should be based on a realistic assessment of the species' status and their environmental conditions, with identification of cause-and-effect relationships.
- Recovery activities fall into three periods:
  - **Near-term:** establish appropriate hatchery stock levels (high priority), design and implement monitoring, limit or eliminate stocking of non-native game fish, selectively remove predatory game fish where needed, and begin research.
  - **Intermediate-term:** maintain hatcheries and stocking, control predators, evaluate habitat modification, and pursue operational changes at major federal projects.
  - **Equilibrium period:** maintain hatcheries and monitoring, implement habitat improvements if needed, and make releases from federal projects during critical flow periods through federally purchased water rights or new project components.
- The document recommends a 1-to-2-year review of the species' status and suggests scientifically based delisting criteria.

**2. Institutional Responsibilities**
- Under Section 4 of the [[Endangered Species Act]], the Secretary of the Interior (through the FWS) is responsible for developing and implementing recovery plans.
- The Regional Director of FWS Region 6 (Denver) holds primary responsibility. A recovery team could include representatives of FWS, [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Western Area Power Administration]], state wildlife and natural resources agencies, and environmental, energy and non-federal hydropower interests.
- A technical committee could create hydrology and biology subcommittees.

**3. Section 7 Consultation Approach**
- The authors propose that mitigation requirements apply only where a project has direct, identifiable physical impacts that jeopardize the species. Examples include physical alteration of key channel segments, significant temperature changes that shift habitat type, and channel blockage that reduces range.
- Under an effective recovery plan, depletion under state water rights systems "would not adversely affect the species."
- Reasonable and prudent alternatives may include fish passage, habitat replacement, fish ladders at other sites, fish stocking, participation in recovery activities, and fish screens.

**4. Funding Options**
- Sources listed include voluntary contributions, a depletion charge under Section 7, a percentage of [[Colorado River Storage Project]] revenues, sporting goods surcharges, [[Dingle-Johnson Funds]], state legislative appropriations, a "non-game check-off" on Colorado tax returns, a surtax on game fish licenses, and Congressional appropriations.
- The document judges depletion-charge and water-sales surcharge funding unreliable and recommends the more dependable sources listed above.

**5. Hatchery Program Review (Valdez, 1985)**
- Hatchery technology for large-scale production of all four species is available, though maintaining wild gene pools and physical facilities remain problems.
- The FWS estimates a need of 500,000 fingerlings per year. Existing facilities can produce about 350,000. Of 11 sites evaluated, only four ([[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], [[Hotchkiss National Fish Hatchery]] and the [[Logan Fisheries Experiment Station]]) have current production capability.
- Estimated cost for a new principal hatchery is $8.3 to $8.6 million, with operating costs of about $333,000 per year. Valentine (1983) estimated $8,371,024 in construction costs.
- Recommended options range from no action to building one new facility plus improving an existing one. The review considers the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] the best candidate for a new facility, with improvements at existing sites.

**6. Species Status and Releases (as reported in the hatchery review)**
- **Colorado Squawfish:** first hatchery fry in 1974. About 1,500 released at Potash, Utah (1980); about 30,000 near Grand Junction, Colorado (1982), with additional releases in 1983 and 1984; about 10,700 in the lower Gunnison River (1984). A total of 118,250 marked squawfish were released (figure as stated in the review).
- **Humpback Chub:** first spawning trial in 1980. About 7,300 released in Cataract Canyon in December 1981. No pure humpback chub were produced in 1982 to 1984.
- **Bonytail:** wild adult broodstock was very limited. A total of 83,547 bonytail were released into Lake Mohave from 1981 to 1983.
- **Razorback Sucker:** a cooperative agreement with Arizona calls for 100,000 fingerlings annually for 10 years. A total of 6,415,438 razorback suckers were released in the wild through 1984, mostly in Arizona tributaries.
- The review states that the main hatchery goal has been production techniques and small numbers for research, with relatively few releases of the endangered fishes to date.

**Note on data:** The hatchery review gives release totals that conflict in places with the species-specific sections (for example, squawfish totals and bonytail totals). Both are reported here as given in the source.
