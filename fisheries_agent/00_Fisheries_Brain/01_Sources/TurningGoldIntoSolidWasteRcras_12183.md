# RCRA's Intrusion into the Industrial Process: Solid Waste Jurisdiction and Non-Ferrous Mining

#environmental-law #hazardous-waste #rcra

## Executive Summary

This article, a law review piece in the Arizona State Law Journal's symposium on toxic substances and hazardous waste, argues that the [[Resource Conservation and Recovery Act]] (RCRA) should be confined to discarded materials and should not reach ongoing industrial processes, including beneficial recycling. The authors, practitioners at Phelps Dodge Corporation and Kimball & Curry, P.C., focus on the [[Non-Ferrous Mining]] and mineral processing sector. They examine the statutory history of RCRA's "solid waste" definition, the [[American Mining Congress v. EPA]] decision, and EPA initiatives they see as expanding RCRA jurisdiction, notably the [[Strawman II]] mining proposal and thermal treatment rules for [[Industrial Furnaces and Boilers]]. The document contains no biological, aquatic-ecological, or species data.

## Background & Description

The article is a legal and regulatory analysis, not an ecological study. Its background section describes the mineral production chain:

- **Extraction and beneficiation:** Ore is removed and concentrated by crushing, milling, and [[Froth Flotation]], which produces tailings.
- **Processing:** Concentrate is smelted, producing [[Slag]] and off-gases treated under the [[Clean Air Act]]. Sulfur dioxide is converted to sulfuric acid.
- **Leaching:** Copper and gold producers recover metals from low-grade ore piles by solution leaching ([[Dump Leaching]]).
- **Recycling:** Dusts, sludges, process water, and by-products are often recirculated or reprocessed on site or from other industries.

The authors frame RCRA as having two programs: hazardous waste management under subtitle C, which is detailed and federally enforceable, and solid waste management under subtitle D, which only provides federal guidelines for state programs. The statutory definition of "solid waste" is the key jurisdictional threshold, and the authors stress the phrase "discarded materials."

The legislative history is presented as showing that Congress aimed to end "the last remaining loophole" of unregulated land disposal, to encourage resource recovery, and not to regulate in-process manufacturing. The authors cite the 1984 Hazardous and Solid Waste Amendments as stating three principles: land disposal is least favored, recycling is favored, and there is no general directive to regulate resource recovery that does not involve land disposal.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not present ecosystem, species, or water-quality data. Its environmental references are regulatory and risk-related:

- EPA is described as concluding that mining wastes "typically pose lower human health and environmental risks than other industrial wastes," partly because mine sites are often in dry climates with deeper groundwater, lower population density, and greater distance to drinking-water receptors.
- The authors argue that EPA had identified no threat from ore stockpiles, product storage, or other non-waste areas, which were outside its studies.
- Active leaching operations are noted as already regulated under the [[Clean Water Act]] NPDES permit program and state groundwater programs.
- EPA's stated concern about recycled materials was the risk of spills and leaks from recycling facilities, and the risk of [[Overaccumulation]].
- The article cites the [[Environmental Defense Fund v. EPA]] litigation over mining waste regulatory determinations.

The authors' central environmental argument is that other statutes (CWA, CAA, CERCLA, Safe Drinking Water Act, OSHA, and state media programs) already control releases, so expanding RCRA duplicates them.

## Key Findings

1. **Jurisdictional thesis:** RCRA reaches only discarded materials. The authors say EPA's expansion of "solid waste" to in-process and recycled materials conflicts with congressional intent and with [[American Mining Congress v. EPA]], 824 F.2d 1177 (D.C. Cir. 1987). That decision was decided 2-1 and held that EPA had acted "in contravention of Congress' intent" by regulating in-process secondary materials.

2. **Post-decision litigation:** EPA's 1988 proposed amendments (53 Fed. Reg. 519) were challenged, and the D.C. Circuit declined on August 31, 1988 to hold EPA in contempt. In [[American Petroleum Institute v. EPA]], 906 F.2d 729 (D.C. Cir. 1990), the court found that K061 slag reclaimed in HTMR furnaces was "discarded" and therefore subject to land disposal restrictions, distinguishing it from the AMC materials.

3. **Compliance cost estimates cited:**
   - EPA estimated subtitle C regulation of mineral processing facilities at over $70 million per year, excluding land disposal restriction and corrective action costs.
   - Average additional annual costs per copper-sector facility were as high as $2,379,091; several sectors faced costs of 4 to 7 percent of shipment value.
   - For 1985 metal mining scenarios, EPA estimated $7 million to $854 million per year industry-wide.
   - For the B-Wastes scenario with Option 1, annual costs were: copper $35.1 million (120% of direct production costs), gold $0.8 million (23%), silver $0.6 million (40%), lead $6.5 million (21%), zinc $1.4 million (39%).
   - Industrial furnace and boiler standards were estimated at $15.3 million per year before taxes. EPA projected only 15% of about 1,000 burners would obtain full permits, with 20% discontinuing burning and 60% using the small quantity burner exemption.
   - Proposed corrective action costs ranged from $7 billion to $42 billion nationally, and $6.3 to $26.9 million per facility on a mean present value basis.

4. **Mining waste volumes (1982, per EPA's 1985 Report to Congress):** 212 million tons of leached materials, 418 million tons of tailing, and 706 million tons of overburden. The authors note that contaminated water, stockpiled ore, and subgrade ore volumes were not published but are "undoubtedly very substantial."

5. **Strawman II critique:** The May 21, 1990 EPA draft (Strawman II) would regulate "regulated materials," including non-waste leach materials, pregnant and barren solutions, accumulated water, mill tailings, overburden, stockpiled ore, and subgrade ore, under subtitle D. The authors argue that EPA itself said its existing authority does not extend to many of these materials, that the definition has no effective limit, and that it could reach smelters receiving ore concentrates and products such as asbestos. They also note that EPA's own cost analysis did not evaluate Strawman II's economic impact.

6. **Thermal treatment rules:** The authors criticize the February 21, 1991 final rule (56 Fed. Reg. 7134) and the April 27, 1990 proposal. Their concern is that EPA's position that any material burned in an incinerator is a solid waste, even as an ingredient, could extend RCRA to all heated materials. They also criticize the [[Indigenous Materials Principle]] as applied to primary furnaces, which would force other-industry wastes to land disposal. They note the [[Derived-From Rule]] and [[Mixture Rule]] discourage legitimate recycling.

7. **Recycling exclusions and sham recycling:** Recycling exclusions under 40 C.F.R. § 261.2 (direct ingredient use, substitutes for commercial products, and closed-loop return) are described, along with the [[Sham Recycling]] test and the 75% speculative accumulation test.

8. **Cited industry effect:** A Bureau of Mines paper (1990) reported that spent potliner recycling by primary aluminum producers "virtually ceased" after listing, with 22% previously burned for energy and fluoride before listing.

9. **Policy recommendations:** The authors urge that Congress and EPA (a) focus RCRA on true wastes and disposal facilities, (b) require EPA to show that any expansion is necessary, consistent with resource recovery, and economically practicable, (c) conduct an economic impact study before finalizing mining waste rules, and (d) weigh the costs and benefits of RCRA against other environmental laws. They conclude that EPA's trajectory risks "turn[ing] the gold of the nation's industrial base into nothing but solid waste."

10. **Pending legislation:** The article notes that H.R. 3735 (May 9, 1990 draft) would redefine "secondary material" and "discarded material" to reverse the AMC decision, and that RCRA reauthorization was expected in 1991.
