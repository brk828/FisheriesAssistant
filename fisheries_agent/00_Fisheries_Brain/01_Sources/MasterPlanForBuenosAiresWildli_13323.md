# Master Plan for Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge Environmental Assessment

#wildlife-management #endangered-species #Odontophoridae #grassland-ecology #habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

This 1986 draft environmental assessment presents the master plan for the newly established [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] in [[Pima County]], [[Arizona]]. The 112,500-acre refuge was created primarily to protect and restore populations of the endangered [[Masked Bobwhite]] (Colinus virginianus ridgwayi). The document evaluates six management alternatives, ranging from no action to maximizing endangered species or economic uses. The preferred alternative emphasizes balanced programs that prioritize [[Masked Bobwhite]] recovery while maintaining diverse wildlife populations and compatible public uses. The refuge encompasses semi-arid [[Sonoran Savanna Grassland]] that has been degraded by historical grazing, with mesquite encroachment threatening optimal bobwhite habitat. The plan addresses vegetation restoration, water management, public recreation, and coordination with state agencies while navigating complex land ownership issues involving 90,178 acres of [[Arizona State Trust]] lands.

## Background & Description

The [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] encompasses 112,500 acres in the southern [[Altar Valley]] of southeastern Arizona, with elevations ranging from 3,000 to 4,800 feet. The refuge was established with the primary purpose of reestablishing and maintaining a viable population of the endangered [[Masked Bobwhite]], a subspecies historically native to the [[Sonoran Savanna Grassland]] ecosystem.

**Vegetation Communities**: Two primary vegetation types characterize the refuge. The upland type was formerly dominated by [[Sonoran Savanna Grassland]] but has been largely replaced by semi-desert grassland due to historical grazing impacts. Perennial grasses are much reduced, with several species potentially locally extirpated (e.g., common tanglehead). [[Sand Dropseed]] (Sporobolus cryptandrus) is now one of the most common perennial grasses. Invasive species including snakeweed (Gutierrezia sp.), burroweed (Aplopappus tenuisecta), and introduced lovegrass (Eragrostis sp.) have increased substantially. [[Mesquite]], once confined to drainageways, has become ubiquitous across uplands. The bottomland type is dominated by mesquite riparian association with Jerusalem-thorn parkinsonia. Sacaton (Sporobolus wrightii) and vine-mesquite panicum (Panicum obtusum) were historically abundant but have been displaced by introduced Johnsongrass (Sorghum halepense).

**Climate & Hydrology**: The area experiences typical southwestern climate patterns with winter precipitation, spring drought, summer precipitation (60% of 16 inches annual average occurring July-August), and fall drought. Mean annual temperature is mid-60°F, with summer temperatures averaging high 80°F. The refuge lies within the [[Tucson Active Management Area]] for groundwater. Water resources include registered wells and filed surface water rights. Aquatic habitat is limited to approximately 270 acres, including larger water bodies like [[Aguirre Lake]], [[Mormon Lake]], and [[Grebe Pond]].

**Wildlife Communities**: Over 150 bird species have been documented on the refuge. Mammals include [[White-tailed Deer]] (Odocoileus virginianus), [[Mule Deer]] (O. hemionus), javelina (Dicotyles tajacu), coyote (Canis latrans), cougar (Felis concolor), and numerous rodent species. Upland game birds include [[Gambel's Quail]] (Callipela gambelii), [[Montezuma Quail]] (Cyrtonyx montezumae), [[Scaled Quail]] (Callipepla squamata), mourning dove (Zenaida macroura), and white-winged dove (Z. asiatica). Raptors include golden eagle (Aquila chrysaetos), American kestrel (Falco sparverius), prairie falcon (F. mexicanus), red-tailed hawk (Buteo jamicensis), and peregrine falcon (Falco peregrinus). The [[Yellow Mud Turtle]] (Kinosternon flavescens flavescens) is listed as threatened by Arizona Game and Fish Department. Waterfowl utilize refuge ponds during winter and migration periods.

**Land Ownership Complexity**: Of the total refuge acreage, 90,178 acres are [[Arizona State Trust]] lands leased by FWS for grazing rights only, with all other development rights retained by the state. FWS purchased 21,258 acres of private land without mineral rights, which were retained by previous owners. [[Phillips Petroleum Company]] held leases on approximately 18,000 acres for oil exploration (expiring 1988). This complex ownership pattern significantly affects management options under all alternatives.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] represents a critical remnant of the [[Sonoran Savanna Grassland]] ecosystem, which has undergone substantial degradation throughout the southwestern United States. Historical overgrazing has driven successional changes from diverse native grasslands toward mesquite-dominated shrublands, threatening habitat for grassland-dependent species.

**Ecosystem Degradation**: The refuge exemplifies widespread ecological changes in southwestern grasslands. Mesquite encroachment from bottomlands into uplands represents a fundamental shift in community structure. Native perennial grasses have declined dramatically, with several species potentially extirpated locally. Weedy half-shrubs (snakeweed, burroweed) have increased substantially, likely due to reduced fire frequency. Introduced lovegrasses occupy prominent expanses. This vegetation shift reduces habitat quality for the [[Masked Bobwhite]], which requires open grasslands with scattered brush for cover and nesting.

**Erosion & Watershed Dynamics**: Years of overgrazing have created significant erosion problems. Large cuts like [[Brawley Wash]] represent severe degradation unlikely to recover without intensive intervention. Reduced vegetative cover has decreased infiltration rates and increased runoff, leading to soil loss and arroyo formation. Many stock tanks depend on runoff for water; changing vegetation patterns affect water availability. Increased mesquite cover may limit aquifer recharge despite improved infiltration from grazing rest.

**Endangered Species Context**: Beyond the [[Masked Bobwhite]], the refuge potentially provides habitat for multiple threatened or endangered species. The document identifies potential management targets including [[Aplomado Falcon]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], [[Mexican Wolf]], ocelot, [[Sonoran Pronghorn Antelope]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]], and endangered southwest cacti. However, available evidence indicates no endangered fish species historically occurred within refuge boundaries. The historic presence of [[Sonoran Pronghorn]] is under review. Managing for multiple endangered species creates conflicts (e.g., brush for ocelot versus grassland for bobwhite and pronghorn).

**Rare Plant Species**: The [[Santa Cruz Striped Agave]] (Agave parviflora) occurs on the refuge. Other plant species under consideration for federal protection may be present, requiring ongoing research. All cactus family members and several other native plants are protected by the [[Arizona Native Plant Law]] (revised 1981).

**Aquatic Habitat Limitations**: Only 270 acres (0.24% of refuge) are classified as wetland-riparian habitat. Without active management, aquatic habitats would decline as dams deteriorate and siltation increases. Fish communities are currently limited to non-native species. Water availability for wildlife is constrained by dependence on runoff-fed stock tanks and limited permanent water sources.

**Socioeconomic Context**: The refuge exists within a matrix of competing land uses. Mineral rights on both private and state lands remain outside FWS control, with potential for mining activities despite the [[Altar Valley]] being classified as "non-mineral." Historical mining districts along the eastern boundary (Arivaca-Oceanic, Arivaca-Amado, Arivaca-Las Guijas) produced copper, lead, zinc, gold, and silver. Economic activities including beekeeping, woodcutting, grazing, and recreation generate local revenue but may conflict with wildlife management objectives.

## Key Findings

**Six Management Alternatives Evaluated**: The interagency planning team considered six alternatives for refuge management, each with distinct implications for wildlife, habitat, public use, and socioeconomic factors:

1. **No Action** (caretaker management): Continued [[Masked Bobwhite]] releases with minimal habitat management. Ecosystem would continue evolving toward desert shrub community, eliminating bobwhite habitat long-term. No public interpretation or recreation facilities. Wildlife diversity would initially increase but ultimately decline substantially. Aquatic habitat would degrade to seasonal ponds. State lands remain under state control with only grazing rights leased by FWS.

2. **Maintain Current Ecosystem - Status Quo**: Limited habitat management to maintain existing conditions. Mesquite and brush encroachment controlled but no habitat expansion or restoration. Bobwhite habitat limited to existing areas. No enhancement of other wildlife populations. Minimal public use facilities and interpretation. Aquatic habitat would eventually decline without dam maintenance.

3. **Maximize Masked Bobwhite Production**: All resources dedicated to bobwhite recovery. Conversion of varied habitats to [[Sonoran Savanna Grassland]] wherever possible. Aggressive erosion control and rangeland improvement techniques (grazing, burning, reseeding, brush manipulation). Competing wildlife species or predators controlled or removed. Severely restricted public use. Wildlife diversity would decrease. Aquatic habitat maintenance not prioritized. Woodcutting permitted as management tool; controlled grazing if beneficial.

4. **Maximize Endangered Species**: Management for multiple threatened/endangered species beyond bobwhite, including [[Aplomado Falcon]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], [[Mexican Wolf]], ocelot, [[Sonoran Pronghorn Antelope]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]], and endangered cacti. Requires creation of diverse habitats (dense mesquite bosques for ocelot, marshes for waterfowl, grasslands for bobwhite/pronghorn, oak woodlands for wolf). Refuge likely partitioned to protect diverse habitats. Competing species minimized. Ponds treated to remove non-native fish; water hauled/pumped for endangered fish. Limited public use. Effects on overall wildlife diversity unpredictable. Requires FWS control of state lands.

5. **Maximize Economic Uses While Maintaining Bobwhites**: Economic activities (beekeeping, woodcutting, grazing, mining, commercial horse tours, camping, concessions, cactus harvesting) maximized with single constraint: cannot extirpate bobwhite population. Large-scale mining permitted. Expanded beekeeping and mesquite cutting. Commercial horseback riding. User fees for all activities. State lands remain under leasehold. Major negative impacts on habitat quality, water resources, and wildlife diversity likely. Some consequences contrary to FWS policy. Generates significant local/state revenue.

6. **Balanced Programs Emphasizing Masked Bobwhite - PREFERRED ALTERNATIVE**: Primacy given to [[Masked Bobwhite]] establishment and stable, diverse grassland community development. Management for other wildlife populations also important. Public use program subordinate to wildlife goals but significant feature. Moderate erosion control to protect habitat. Some aquatic habitats maintained and improved. Vegetation diversified for bobwhite and other species. Considerable recreation and public information programs with full-time outdoor recreation planner. Brush controlled to provide suitable habitat mixture. Grazing and woodcutting permitted if beneficial to habitat management. Assumes FWS management of state lands for control and flexibility. Mining permitted with surface disturbance minimized; oil/gas exploration in compliance with federal regulations.

**Preferred Alternative Rationale**: The balanced approach (Alternative F) was selected because it:
- Fulfills primary refuge purpose (bobwhite recovery) while avoiding single-species tunnel vision
- Provides habitat diversity supporting wide variety of wildlife
- Allows substantial public recreation, interpretation, and education
- Maintains management flexibility through diverse habitat types
- Generates moderate local economic benefits through compatible uses
- Avoids irreversible habitat degradation from uncontrolled economic exploitation
- Represents combination of positive facets from all alternatives while avoiding most serious environmental impacts

**Habitat Management Strategies**: Under the preferred alternative, rangeland improvement techniques include controlled grazing, prescribed burning, reseeding, and brush manipulation to restore [[Sonoran Savanna Grassland]] in appropriate areas. Gully plugs, checks, and other rehabilitation methods would accelerate arroyo healing. Vegetation manipulation would create brush-grassland interface required by [[Masked Bobwhite]]. Flooding selected areas would develop marshes and aquatic environments. Cottonwood and willow planting would enhance riparian systems. These practices would temporarily increase erosion potential but proper application would minimize impacts. Improved range condition would increase infiltration rates and decrease runoff, allowing more aquifer recharge.

**Wildlife Management Outcomes**: The preferred alternative would significantly benefit the wildlife community through vegetation diversification. Mesquite-grass bottomland management for bobwhite would also provide habitat for deer, javelina, and doves. Best remaining [[Masked Bobwhite]] habitat in the United States would be preserved. Habitat improvements would increase upland wildlife species. No predator control unless bobwhite establishment seriously impeded by predation. Permitted hunting would not significantly affect wildlife community. Entire refuge closed to quail hunting until bobwhite downlisted. Aquatic habitat improvements would benefit waterfowl and other water-dependent species.

**Public Use Program**: Under preferred alternative, significant compatible public use allowed. Self-guided and conducted auto and walking tours available from visitor center (main ranch house). Public allowed on all roads/trails not specifically closed. Environmental interpretation emphasized with full-time Outdoor Recreational Planner. Refuge open to migratory game bird and resident game hunting (September 1 - March 31 seasons set with [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]]). Hunting and trapping outside concentrated public use areas. Dispersed primitive camping or designated RV areas allowed until campground developed within 30-minute drive.

**Socioeconomic Considerations**: Economic uses (grazing, woodcutting) allowed only if compatible with primary refuge purposes. Economic activities used as management practices for habitat enhancement where possible. Beekeeping permitted outside main public use area and away from water bodies. Local enterprises would benefit from increasing public visitation. Mineral exploration and extraction permitted (FWS has no control over mineral rights; valid leases/claims honored under any alternative). Removal of nonrenewable mineral resources represents irreversible commitment.

**Water Resource Management**: Under preferred alternative, restoration of [[Sonoran Savanna Grassland]] in selected areas would reduce erosion and increase infiltration rates. Decreased mesquite should allow more aquifer recharge and raise water table. Larger water bodies ([[Aguirre Lake]], [[Mormon Lake]], [[Grebe Pond]]) not greatly affected but inflow from refuge lands would carry reduced silt loads. Dams and dikes on existing water bodies would be maintained. Pumping used to maintain year-round water in selected tanks. This contrasts with No Action alternative where many runoff-dependent tanks would lose water as infiltration increases and mesquite expands.

**Irreversible/Irretrievable Commitments**: The preferred alternative requires no irrevocable resource commitments beyond personnel expenditures. Land alterations (vegetation type changes) could be reversed, albeit expensively. Mineral extraction represents irreversible commitment of nonrenewable resources, but FWS lacks control over mineral rights. Prevention of extensive commercial development may represent major denial of long-term use, but not total preclusion—lands could be developed if refuge status removed in future.

**Consultation Process**: Extensive interagency coordination involved specialists from [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Arizona Land Department]], [[U.S. Forest Service]] ([[Coronado National Forest]]), [[Soil Conservation Service]], [[University of Arizona]], [[Arizona State Parks]], [[Audubon Society]] ([[The Research Ranch]]), and private consultants. This collaborative approach ensured diverse expertise in range management, wildlife biology, endangered species, habitat management, fire ecology, and public recreation.

**Archaeological/Historic Resources**: No archaeological sites recorded on refuge to date (no field investigations conducted). Southern [[Altar Valley]] archaeological record incomplete due to limited research. Several historic structures potentially eligible for [[National Register of Historic Places]], including [[Pozo Nuevo Schoolhouse]], historic corrals, and [[Sasabe]] townsite. [[Buenos Aires Ranch]] has well-known history requiring further documentation.

**Implementation Challenges**: Complex land ownership creates significant management constraints. State control of 90,178 acres (80% of refuge) limits FWS authority over non-refuge uses (mining, camping). Preferred alternative assumes FWS management of state lands through agreement, easement, or purchase to ensure control and flexibility. Without state land control, regulation of mining and other activities remains with [[Arizona]] and could undermine management efforts. Mineral rights on 21,258 acres of purchased private land retained by previous owners, limiting FWS control over potential extraction activities.

**Long-term Productivity**: Short-term and long-term goals align: protect, maintain, and enhance [[Masked Bobwhite]] habitat while providing public use and enjoyment. Dedication to these goals will ultimately increase long-term stability and productivity of grassland ecosystem. Major trade-off is prevention of extensive commercial development, though not total preclusion of use. Refuge lands could be developed if refuge status removed in future, maintaining long-term flexibility.
