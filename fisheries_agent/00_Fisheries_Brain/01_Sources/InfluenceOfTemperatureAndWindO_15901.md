# Influence of Temperature and Wind on the Survival of Early Stages of Yellow Perch

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Michael D. Clady]] (Cornell University, 1976) estimates egg-to-larval survival of [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) year-classes from 1969 to 1975 in [[Oneida Lake]], New York. Egg production was derived from [[Mark-and-Recapture]] adult stock estimates and fecundity, and larval cohort abundance from annual [[Larval Survey]] sampling. Early survival ranged from 1.6% to 18.4% and was positively correlated with water and air temperature and negatively correlated with wind. Mean air temperature and mean daily wind velocity together explained 87% of the variability in survival. The author concludes that year-class strength is shaped by many interacting, relatively minor mortality factors rather than a single catastrophic event.

## Background & Description

Yellow perch populations show large fluctuations in year-class strength, and earlier attempts to link environmental conditions in early life to recruitment had largely failed. Because yellow perch are an important forage and game species across northern lakes, and because relative year-class rankings in Oneida Lake were consistent through the first summer, the study focused on mortality in the earliest life stages. The objective was to relate survival from egg to larva to [[Water Temperature]] and [[Wind Velocity]] to identify when the bulk of mortality occurs.

Methods combined:
- **Egg production:** Adult population estimated in alternate years (1969, 1971, 1973, 1975) using [[Bailey Modification of the Petersen Method]] with finclipping; intervening years projected using survival rates derived from the square root of biennial survival. Fecundity was modeled from 117 females (log fecundity = 2.9396 + 0.0066 × total length in mm). Sex ratio assumed 1:1 based on 48.7% females in 9,927 fish.
- **Larval abundance:** Density estimated from [[High-Speed Sampler]] catches at 42–54 randomly chosen stations within 4–6 strata, with lake volume to 5.5 m (9.7 × 10^7 m³). Larvae were defined as fish ≥ 8 mm mean total length; smaller fish were prolarvae.
- **Environment:** Water temperature at 2 m from a thermograph over the 3 weeks before cohorts reached 8 mm; air temperature and wind from Hancock Airport, 19 km from the lake.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Oneida Lake does not stratify thermally before June, so the 2 m temperature was considered representative of the 0–5.5 m zone. Perch spawn at 7–11 °C. Water temperatures during the 3 weeks before cohorts reached 8 mm rose from 8–10 °C (embryo stage) to 12–19 °C (prolarval stage). Annual mean temperatures fell in a narrow 11.0–12.5 °C range.

Wind effects were physical as well as thermal. Strong winds in 1974 displaced eggs from spawning sites, and some eggs were stranded on beaches; stranded eggs were estimated at almost 1% of total egg potential. Moderate currents are considered necessary for egg circulation and hatching. Laboratory work by [[Hokanson and Kleiner]] (1974) indicated optimum embryo survival at 13–16 °C, with mortality exceeding 20% only at extremes below 8 °C or above 20 °C. The lake's fluctuating temperature regime may explain the field results being lower and more variable than laboratory predictions.

## Key Findings

- **Egg production was stable** (2.3-fold variation in adult stock; egg totals of about 39–55 billion viable eggs). Multi-age adult structure and low adult mortality buffered year-class differences.
- **Survival varied roughly ninefold** between cohorts, from 1.6% (1973) to 18.4% (1975). Mean survival was 7.7%. Mortality between spawning and the larval stage, rather than egg production, drove cohort size.

- **Temperature:** Survival correlated positively with mean water temperature (r = 0.75, P < 0.05) and with mean air temperature (r = 0.89, P < 0.01). Warming rate was less strongly correlated (r = 0.50). Low survival occurred when temperatures stayed near 10–14 °C; high survival occurred when temperatures rose rapidly to 16–19 °C.
- **Wind:** Survival correlated negatively with mean daily wind velocity (r = −0.61) and with mean daily maximum wind (r = −0.54). Above-average May air temperatures coincided with below-average wind.
- **Combined model:** Mean wind velocity and mean daily air temperature explained 87% of survival variability (R² = 0.87).
- **Mortality sources:** Wind-driven egg loss could explain the 82–98% total mortality variation in an unfavorable year, but a consistent base mortality above 80% in all years is not explained by wind alone. No evidence showed that temperature or wave action was lethal to pelagic prolarvae; the density increase during hatching in 1974 (mean length rising from 6.3 to 8.1 mm in 1967) supports low pelagic mortality.
- **Interpretation:** Low year-to-year variation in egg-prolarval mortality, especially 1969–1972, suggests a complex of interacting minor factors rather than one catastrophic event.
- **Historical support:** Air temperatures from May 5–25 correlated with fingerling catches from 1959–67 (r = 0.74, P < 0.05), suggesting temperature influenced year-class size before 1969 as well.
