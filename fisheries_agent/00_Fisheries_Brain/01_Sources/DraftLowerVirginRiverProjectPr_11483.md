# Lower Virgin River Project, Nevada: Preliminary Findings Report (Plan Formulation, 1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This is a draft Preliminary Findings Report (PFR) from the Bureau of Reclamation (Lower Colorado Region) and the Las Vegas Valley Water District (LVVWD), circulated for a 30-day comment period ending April 30, 1993. It evaluates a dual-purpose project on the lower Virgin River in Nevada. The project would divert saline river water, desalt it for municipal and industrial (M&I) use in the Las Vegas Valley, and remove salt that would otherwise reach the Colorado River. The preliminary plan is judged feasible from an engineering standpoint. Its two major environmental issues are effects on two endangered fishes, the [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin River Chub]], and effects on the threatened [[Desert Tortoise]]. The preliminary cost of water ($709 per acre-foot) looks competitive, but the cost of salt removal ($299 per ton) does not look competitive with other Colorado River salinity projects. Uncertainty remains over future river flow, water rights, and environmental impacts.

## Background & Description
- **Objective:** Determine the technical, environmental and economic feasibility of a salinity control and water supply project. The study will yield a report combined with an environmental impact statement under the [[National Environmental Policy Act]].
- **Setting:** The project would begin about 2 miles east of Mesquite, Nevada, and extend about 75 miles southwest to Las Vegas. About 2,400 acres are irrigated by diversions at Mesquite, Bunkerville and Riverside Bridge.
- **Need for salinity control:** The Virgin River carries an average of about 426,000 tons of salt per year into the [[Colorado River]]. The Colorado carries about 9 million tons past Hoover Dam each year. The Salinity Control Forum estimated that 1.3 million tons must be removed by 2010 to meet numeric criteria (723 mg/L below Hoover Dam, 747 below Parker Dam, 879 at Imperial Dam).
- **Need for water:** Southern Nevada demand is projected to exceed supply in 2007, possibly postponed to 2013 under a pending Southern California Edison arrangement. LVVWD's [[Cooperative Water Project]] is the main alternative source.
- **Authority:** Public Law 96-375 (1980).
- **Preliminary plan (Alternative 2, Riverside Diversion):**
  - A 400-ft ogee-crest [[Riverside Diversion Dam]] with a sluiceway, and a pumping plant of 500 ft³/s.
  - A 3.1-mile, 135-inch pipeline to [[Halfway Wash Reservoir]], an offstream reservoir with about 113,000 acre-feet of operating capacity behind a zoned earthfill dam (maximum height 220 ft, about 1 mile long).
  - About 34 miles of 69-inch pipe to a desalting plant at Dry Lake Valley.
  - Desalting by cold lime-soda pretreatment, then reverse osmosis (50% recovery) and electrodialysis (an additional 40%), for 90% overall recovery.
  - Brine evaporation ponds (63 ponds of 20 acres each, about 1,260 acres), and a 4-million-gallon storage tank about 10 miles north of Las Vegas.
  - Two 230-kV switchyards with maximum loads of 43 MW and 38 MW.
- **Other alternatives:** Alternative 1 (diversion near Mesquite with a 24-mile canal, about 5% more costly), Alternative 3 (ten underground infiltration galleries, environmentally preferred but uncertain in capacity), and Alternative 4 (infiltration galleries plus ground-water recharge at Dry Lake Valley before desalting).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Climate and hydrology:** The basin is semi-desert in the eastern Mojave, with 3–6 inches of annual precipitation and about 80 inches of evaporation. Basin area upstream of Halfway Wash is about 5,980 square miles. Average discharge at the Littlefield, Arizona gauge (1930–1991) was about 170,000 acre-feet per year. The summer minimum is about 70 ft³/s (65 from Littlefield Springs and 5 from Beaver Dam Wash). Reaches downstream of Riverside Bridge are often dry in summer.
- **Water quality:** Littlefield Springs (over 2,500 mg/L) and LaVerkin Springs (over 10,000 mg/L) are major salt sources. Diverted water is projected to average about 2,200 mg/L TDS. One ton of salt per acre-foot equals 735 mg/L.
- **Sediment:** The river carries heavy suspended and bedload sediment, and flash floods carry 15–20% silt by volume. Total annual sediment inflow to Halfway Wash Reservoir is estimated at 830 acre-feet (740 from diversions plus 90 from the wash).
- **Aquatic habitat and fishes:**
  - The river is a wide, shallow, meandering, sandy, turbid, unstable channel. Five native and nine introduced fish species have been reported.
  - Four native species are of special concern: [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin River Chub]] (federally endangered), and [[Virgin River Spinedace]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (Category 2 candidates).
  - Only the woundfin and chub occur regularly upstream of Riverside Bridge.
  - Of the exotics, [[Red Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Yellow Bullhead]] have reproducing populations. The red shiner is dominant downstream of Mesquite, often 90% or more of the fauna in slow water, and is a major limiting factor for natives.
  - Recovery team sampling over 10 years identified habitat loss and degradation, and exotic species, as the main causes of decline.
- **Riparian and terrestrial habitat:** The riparian zone supports 169 bird, 22 mammal, 18 reptile and 4 amphibian species. [[Salt Cedar]] is present in over 75% of the flood plain. Uplands are [[Mojave Desert]] scrub dominated by creosote bush. The [[Overton Wildlife Management Area]] lies just downstream of the proposed dam.
- **Desert tortoise:** The Mojave population is listed as threatened, with low to moderate densities along the pipeline route and possible isolated high-density areas.
- **Other species:** Special status plants (e.g., Geyer milkvetch, golden bear poppy) and 19 other special status species were identified, but the bald eagle and peregrine falcon are not expected to be adversely affected.
- **Cultural resources:** 156 sites were found in the project area.

## Key Findings
- **Water supply:** Target average diversion is 70,000 acre-feet per year. Annual diversion would range from 35,000 to 140,000 acre-feet, giving an average of 63,000 acre-feet per year of 500 mg/L product water (57 MGD blended product) and removing about 210,000 tons of salt per year. The desalting plant is designed for a maximum feed of 80,000 acre-feet per year.
- **Upstream depletions:** Utah and Arizona depletions are projected to rise from 84,081 acre-feet (1990) to 150,526 acre-feet (2040). Future flow and salinity at the diversion are uncertain, so the project is designed to adapt, for example by partial equipment replacement.
- **Cost:** Construction is $816.0 million (October 1992 dollars), with about $145 million interest during construction (4 years at 8.25%) and $35 million annual operation, maintenance and replacement. The capitalized total is about $1.4 billion ($1,376.9 million).
- **Cost allocation:** The Separable Cost-Remaining Benefits method allocates $745.5 million (54.1%) to salinity control and $631.4 million (45.9%) to M&I water. The result is $299 per ton of salt and $709 per acre-foot of water (6.5% interest). Setting salt removal at $100 per ton would raise water to $1,274 per acre-foot; $75 per ton gives $1,345 and $50 per ton gives $1,416.
- **Fish impacts:** Concerns include loss of woundfin and chub habitat and of eggs, larvae, young-of-year and adults at the intake, and effects on riparian habitat and on nutrient and sediment delivery to the Overton Arm of Lake Mead. Informal consultation with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service indicates the designs may affect sensitive fish. Intake fish screening could be costly. A conservation plan is planned to complement Recovery Plan "priority recovery actions", and downstream flow bypass is assumed.
- **Water rights and law:** LVVWD's applications are pending before the Nevada State Engineer. Nevada, Utah and Arizona have a Memorandum of Understanding but no timetable. Some basin states interpret the [[Law of the River]] as barring additional Virgin River development.
- **Public involvement and schedule:** Public meetings were held October 28–29, 1992 (about 45 attendees in Las Vegas, about 35 in Mesquite). The Plan of Study schedules the draft environmental statement for September 1994, the final for June 1995, and the Record of Decision for October 1995.
- **Critical issues:** Endangered fish, desert tortoise, future riverflow, Nevada water rights, the Colorado River entitlement question, validation of pretreatment and desalting costs (including a mobile pilot plant), sediment and nutrient transport, cost allocation, and the high cost of water and of salt removal.
