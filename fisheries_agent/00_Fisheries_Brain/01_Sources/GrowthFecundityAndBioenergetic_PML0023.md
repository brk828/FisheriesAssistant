# Life Cycle, Fecundity and Bioenergetics of *Corbicula manilensis* in Lake Arlington, Texas

#malacology #freshwater-biology #corbicula

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Aldridge]] and [[Mcmahon]] (1978) reports the first detailed description of the reproduction, growth, fecundity, carbon biomass production, and bioenergetics of the [[Asiatic Freshwater Clam]], *[[Corbicula Manilensis]]*, in a natural population in [[Lake Arlington]], [[Tarrant County]], North Central [[Texas]]. The species was introduced to North America and has become an important pest in water-supply and power-plant systems. Sampling ran from September 1974 to January 1976 on the eastern shore of the reservoir.

The population showed two overlapping annual generations, each spawning twice in two reproductive periods. Veliger release ran from late April to late July (spring) and late August to early December (fall), with release peaking at [[Temperature Optimum]] of 25.7°C. The population turned over rapidly and was highly productive: annual net productivity was about 10,444 mg C m⁻² and annual [[Calcium Carbonate Fixation]] was about 317 g CaCO₃ m⁻².

## Background & Description

*Corbicula manilensis* is an Asiatic freshwater bivalve introduced to North America around 1935. It has become a significant component of freshwater systems across much of the United States. Its economic impact on industrial and public water facilities has made it a recognized pest species (see [[Freshwater Clam Pest Species]]). Earlier work on this species came from [[Heinsohn]] (1958) in California, [[Villadolid and Del Rosario]] (1930) in the Philippines, and [[Sinclair and Isom]] (1963) in Tennessee, but few quantitative studies of growth, reproduction, and population biology existed.

**Methods (summary).** Samples were collected by grab sampler and by a Petersen-type grab from a boat, and during the fall 1975 drawdown by a sediment sleeve. Shell length (SL), shell width, and shell height were measured, and size-frequency histograms were used to separate generations. Fecundity was measured by holding adult clams in jars and counting released veligers. Dry tissue, periostracum, and shell CaCO₃ weights were determined, and organic carbon was measured by the [[Russell-Hunter Method]]. Respiration was estimated from oxygen uptake regressions using [[Polarography]] data for this species. Lake water was moderately alkaline (conductivity mean 270 µmho, pH range 7.0-8.2, mean 7.67).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and substrate.** Lake Arlington is an artificial reservoir on [[Village Creek]] (surface area 920.1 ha; storage volume 56 × 10⁶ m³). Sediment was mostly fine sand and silt, similar to other *C. manilensis* habitats.

**Temperature.** Surface water temperatures ranged widely over the study:
- Winter minimum (Dec 1974-Feb 1975): mean 12.2°C
- August maximum: mean 32.3°C
- Spring (March-June 1975) mean: 21.2°C
- Fall (September-November 1975) mean: 22.4°C

**Reproductive timing.** Spring spawning began when surface temperatures rose above 19.0°C and stopped when they exceeded 32.0°C. Fall spawning began when temperatures fell below 32.5°C and ended when they dropped below 13°C.

**Density.** Mean density was 32.1 clams m⁻² (range 17.2 in early June to 94.5 in late December). Densities were lower than in some southern California populations (roughly 5,000-6,000 clams m⁻² at peak), but fell within the range reported for other populations.

## Key Findings

**1. Life cycle and generations**
- Two annual generations, spring (S-75) and fall (F-74/F-75), each spawning in two reproductive periods.
- Spring generation lifespan: about 437 days (~14 months). Fall generation lifespan: about 498 days (~17 months). A few individuals may have survived up to about two years.
- Mean SL at release was 0.2 mm. Estimated sexual maturity was reached at about 10 mm SL.

**2. Fecundity**
- Mean veliger release per adult per day: 381.0 (±129.0 S.D., range 85.3-588.2, n = 6) in spring; 319.8 (±222.3 S.D., range 25.6-735.1, n = 10) in fall.
- Maximum daily output: 588 veligers/clam/day (June) in spring and 735 (6 October 1975) in fall.
- Total veliger release per m²: 388,495 (spring) and 752,325 (fall) in 1975.
- The proportion of adults brooding shelled veligers averaged 73.3% (spring) and 52.0% (fall).

**3. Thermal control of spawning**
- Veliger release fit two linear regressions against surface temperature, intersecting at 25.7°C (the theoretical optimum).
- Midsummer cessation above about 32°C was attributed to temperature stress rather than exhaustion of the clams, since gonad condition remained largely unchanged.

**4. Growth and dry weight**
- Initial growth rates were 42% (April-May, fall generation) and 35% (July, spring generation). Maximum monthly increases exceeded 150%.
- Allometric relations were derived for SL, shell width, shell height, tissue and shell dry weights, and organic carbon (log-log regressions over 2-40 mm SL).

**5. Calcium carbonate dynamics**
- Annual average standing crop of shell CaCO₃ was 95.4 g m⁻².
- Annual CaCO₃ fixation was 317 g m⁻², equal to about 10 g CaCO₃ per clam per year.
- Turnover rate for CaCO₃ fixation was 3.32 per year (turnover time about 110 days).
- The authors estimated fixation could reach 50-60 kg CaCO₃ m⁻² yr⁻¹ in dense freshwater populations, implying rapid sediment deposition.

**6. Bioenergetics and productivity**
- Standing crop organic carbon averaged 2.5 g C m⁻² (spring generation 1.1 g, fall 0.9 g).
- Total annual assimilation was 14,565 mg C m⁻² yr⁻¹. Non-respired assimilation (growth plus reproduction) was 10,443.7 mg C m⁻² yr⁻¹ (about 71%); respired assimilation was about 29%.
- Population productivity was 28.6 mg C m⁻² d⁻¹, described as the highest ever recorded for a freshwater bivalve.
- Net production efficiency was 0.66 (spring generation) and 0.77 (fall generation), exceeding values reported for most other bivalves.
- About 15% of non-respired assimilation went to reproduction.

**7. Turnover**
- Turnover ratios: spring 4.8 (turnover time 91 days), fall 5.5 (95 days), whole population 4.1 (91 days).

**8. Comparative context**
- Lifespan (14-17 months) is shorter than the 5-7 years reported for Tennessee populations and the 2-year claim for California populations.
- The reproductive cycle (two spawning periods) matches the California population studied by Heinsohn, but differs from single-period populations in southern California and the Philippines.
- Productivity exceeded that of most freshwater and marine bivalves compared, though lower than the [[Eastern Oyster]] (*Crassostrea virginica*).

**Conclusion.** The authors argue that the species' high net production efficiency, high fecundity, and rapid growth explain its ability to establish high densities quickly after colonizing a drainage system, and so its potential as a major pest in U.S. water supplies.
