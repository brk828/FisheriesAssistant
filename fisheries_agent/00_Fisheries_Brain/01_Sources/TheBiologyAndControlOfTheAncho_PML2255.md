# The Biology and Control of the Anchor Worm, Lernaea cyprinacea

#parasitology #copepoda #lernaeidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented at an FAO symposium on warm-water pond fish culture, examines the life history and chemical control of the [[Anchor Worm]] ([[Lernaea Cyprinacea]]), a parasitic [[Copepoda|copepod]] of the family [[Lernaeidae]]. Author Wilmer A. Rogers of the [[Auburn University]] Agricultural Experiment Station combined laboratory life-history observations with aquarium and pond control trials using the organophosphate insecticide [[Dylox]] (active ingredient [[Trichlorfon]]). The work concludes that Dylox can eliminate the parasite from aquaria and substantially reduce it in ponds, although repeated treatments are needed, and that low pond concentrations had little apparent effect on non-target zooplankton and phytoplankton.

## Background & Description

The anchor worm is an ectoparasitic copepod that attaches to the skin, fins and gills of freshwater fish, especially [[Goldfish]] and other cyprinids. The paper describes it as a serious pest in warm-water fish culture in the southeastern United States. Control was then limited by the lack of chemicals that were both effective against the parasite and safe for the host fish and pond ecosystem, which motivated the study. ## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life history.** Life-history observations were made on material collected from infected fish and held in aquaria and ponds. Parasite development was followed from egg to adult, and the paper reports that the complete life cycle was observed in the laboratory. Development was temperature-dependent; the paper notes timing at 28°C, and that the female stage developed after hatching. Three larval stages were distinguished before the adult stage. Egg sacs were carried by females attached to a hanging crop and sealed, as is typical for the genus.

**Pond ecosystem.** The ponds studied contained [[Microcrustaceans]], [[Rotifers]] and [[Phytoplankton]], which were monitored as non-target groups during treatments. The author notes that some pond fish harboured anchor worm adults but that fish-stocking and pond management were key variables affecting infestation and treatment success.

**Environmental concerns.** The study emphasises the risk that chemical controls pose to the wider pond community. The paper suggests that the parasite's tolerance of Dylox is greater in the larval stages, and that treatment timing relative to the parasite's life cycle matters.

## Key Findings

- **Laboratory life cycle:** The full life cycle, from egg to adult, was completed in the laboratory, and the parasite's developmental stages were described.
- **Aquarium control:** Complete elimination of anchor worms was achieved in aquaria using two Dylox treatments at low rates (about 0.1 ppm active ingredient) applied two weeks apart.
- **Pond control:** Dylox treatments gave effective control in ponds. Parasitic larvae were eliminated after repeated treatments at about 0.25 ppm active ingredient, but some adult females survived even after four treatments.
- **Dose-response tables:** Table I and Table II summarise average numbers of adult and larval anchor worms on goldfish after Dylox treatments at several concentrations (from 0.1 to 1.0 ppm and above), with infestation categories ranging from heavy to light.
- **Fish toxicity:** Dylox was toxic to goldfish at higher concentrations. In one aquarium trial, goldfish mortality occurred in some tanks treated at a concentration whose value is partly illegible in the scan. Deaths were noted to occur only after a delay of several days, which the author linked to the chemical's mode of action.
- **Non-target effects:** At rates up to 0.2 ppm active ingredient, Dylox had little apparent effect on microcrustaceans, rotifers or phytoplankton. Microcrustacean numbers in treated ponds were not reduced beyond normal variation three days after treatment.
- **Repeated treatment:** Current results suggested that more than two treatments may be needed to eliminate the parasite from ponds, and that a few adult females may survive four treatments.
- **Practical implication:** Dylox offers a workable control option for warm-water pond culture, provided dosing, timing and repeat treatments are carefully managed to protect the host fish and the pond food web.
