# Effects of Human Developments on the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document reviews how dam construction, channelization and water diversion have transformed the Lower Colorado River, once a seasonally erratic, sediment-laden river supporting a small endemic fish fauna. Dams created [[Reservoir Ecosystems]] with clear water and stable levels that favored introduced sport and forage species, while the native fauna declined toward rarity or extinction. The text also summarizes a [[Fish Species Inventory]] of the mainstream and outlines likely future changes linked to continued water development. The authors are affiliated with the [[Arizona Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].

## Background & Description

- **Historical setting:** The river was among the most erratic and silt-laden rivers in the world, with seasonal and annual flow variation that limited invertebrate and fish diversity to specialized forms.
- **Human demand:** Arizona and California populations were reported at 1,707,000 and 19,953,000 in the 1970 census (per the cited Census Bureau data), increases of 35% and 27% over 1960 (values as reported in the source OCR).
- **Water management:** The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] regulated flows from upstream reservoirs for irrigation, municipal supply and power, under 1964 operating guidelines that gave limited consideration to fish ecological needs.
- **Study scope:** The system between Davis Dam and the Arizona-Sonora border was divided into three major segments (reservoirs, channelized mainstream and backwaters).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Natural conditions:** Before dams, monthly discharges varied from under 200 m³/s in fall and winter to more than 1,000 m³/s in summer (Grinnell 1914 reported a maximum of about 1,300 m³/s). Dissolved solids fluctuated inversely with discharge, and suspended sediments often exceeded 30,000 mg/l.
- **Riparian and backwater habitats:** Natural backwaters were lined with cottonwood, willow, mesquite, cattails and arrowweed. Backwaters were warmer and more productive than the mainstream and supported fishes such as the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] because of their clearer water.
- **Invertebrates:** Little was known. Small crustaceans (cladocerans and copepods) were probably abundant, and Grinnell (1914) reported no aquatic insects or amphipods in the area.
- **Post-dam changes:** Clear reservoir water, reduced turbidity and stable temperatures altered productivity. Phytoplankton blooms and detritus-based food webs shifted, and salinity increased downstream as river inflows were reduced.
- **Channelization:** Reductions in stream length, width and channel meanders, plus deepening, increased water velocity and lowered the water table, eliminating much backwater habitat.

## Key Findings

- **Fish fauna before development:** Few species were present. Miller (1961) and Minckley (1973) recognized eight native freshwater species, including the Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker, [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Chub]] and Desert Pupfish-type forms, most of which declined sharply.
- **Shift to exotic dominance:** By the early 1940s, all major mainstream species were exotic, including [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Black Crappie]] and [[Striped Bass]]. Only the Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish persisted, the latter because of clearer water and reduced predatory pressure.
- **Reservoir productivity:** Newly built reservoirs developed plankton-rich conditions and supported sport fisheries, though success was considered satisfactory only in part. Backwater fish production in the early 1940s was poorly known but probably higher than in the mainstream.
- **Table of species (summarized):** A Table 2 compares mainstream fishes above and below Palo Verde Dam (1914–75). It lists roughly 35 entries across about 15 families, mostly exotic species, with presence marked by x.
- **Dam inventory:** The text lists 7 dams and reservoirs (Laguna, Imperial, Palo Verde, Parker, Headgate Rock, Davis, and others) with completion dates from 1909 to 1953 and heights between 3.0 and 42.1 m.
- **Channelization effects:** Approximately 19 km of mainstream and 63 km of reach above Lake Havasu were channelized or dredged, and roughly 2 km of the upper mainstream reach remained largely unaltered.
- **Future outlook:** Continued water development is expected to cause further change, including reduced flows downstream of Parker Dam, greater temperature ranges, higher salinity and loss of habitat for native fish, with a reduction in ecological condition noted by the authors.
- **Sampling and data:** Figures 3–7 show mean total dissolved solids, suspended sediment and discharge (1926–1974), drawn from U.S. Geological Survey and Howard (1929a, 1929b) records.
