# Life History Studies on Ten Species of Rockfish (Genus Sebastodes)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #scorpaenidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game bulletin (Fish Bulletin 126, by Julius B. Phillips) reports life-history data for ten commercially important [[Rockfish]] species of the genus *Sebastodes*. It covers annual growth, size and age at maturity, weight-length relationships, spawning season, [[Fecundity]], and [[Feeding Habits]]. The study was motivated by a lack of basic biological data for the principal species in California's rockfish catch. Only [[Blue Rockfish]] and [[Pacific Ocean Perch]] had been studied in detail before it. Ages were estimated from body scales, growth was modeled with a [[Von Bertalanffy Growth Model]], and the bulletin closes with a review of [[Racial Studies]] and tagging methods.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** Ten species were studied: [[Bocaccio]], [[Chilipepper]], [[Yellowtail Rockfish]], [[Canary Rockfish]], [[Vermilion Rockfish]], [[Widow Rockfish]], [[Dark-blotched Rockfish]], [[Splitnose Rockfish]], [[Stripetail Rockfish]], and [[Shortbelly Rockfish]]. Nine are of considerable commercial value. Shortbelly Rockfish is small and mainly valuable as forage for salmon and similar predators.
- **Diversity:** California waters hold 50 *Sebastodes* species, 2 *Sebastolobus* (channel rockfish) species, and 1 *Scorpaena* (sculpin) species, all in the family [[Scorpaenidae]].
- **Other species of interest:** The author named further species for future study, including shortspine channel rockfish, flag rockfish, speckled rockfish, bronzespotted rockfish, and cow rockfish. The cow rockfish, reaching at least 32 pounds, was not included.
- **Fishery history:** Rockfish landings in California were about 1 to 2 million pounds in 1900, peaked near 18 million pounds in 1958, and fell to about 10 million pounds in 1962. Before 1944 most were taken by [[Longline Fishery]] gear. After the introduction of the "balloon" [[Trawl Fishery]] in late 1943, trawl catches surpassed longline catches by the end of 1944.
- **Regulations (as described):** Commercial setlines and longlines were permitted in most State waters, with specific bay and Santa Catalina Island closures. Trawling was barred within 3 miles of the mainland. Commercial rockfish had no size, bag, or seasonal limits. Sport anglers were limited to 20 rockfish per day.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat and depth:** Rockfish range from shallow rocky inshore habitat to deep offshore waters. Some adults occur only around rocky shores, rarely deeper than 30 fathoms. Others reach at least 300 fathoms. Young of several species occupy shallower water than adults.
- **Early life stages:** Rockfish larvae were taken year-round in routine plankton tows up to 300 miles from shore (1950–1956 data, [[Ahlstrom]]). They occurred with sardine, anchovy, jack mackerel, and Pacific mackerel eggs and larvae, mostly in the upper 100 m.
- **Prey base:** [[Euphausiids]] (especially *Euphausia pacifica*) are the key macroplankton prey. Hyperiid amphipods dominate the widow rockfish diet.
- **Oceanographic events:** From 1957 through at least 1959, a northward redistribution of planktonic forms, [[Pelagic Red Crab]] (*Pleuroncodes planipes*), and sand crabs (*Emerita analoga*) indicated a countercurrent and warming north of their usual ranges. In this "warm water" period, pelagic red crabs were frequent in rockfish stomachs. A mass stranding of [[Blue Lanternfish]] (*Tarletonbeania crenularis*) also occurred south of Monterey Bay in 1952, and rockfish were found gorged on lanternfish.
- **Stock structure:** Ripe bocaccio and chilipepper females were found in the same 3-week period at Monterey, Fort Bragg, and Eureka, about 300 miles apart. This suggests localized spawning rather than a single common spawning area.

## Key Findings

### Sampling and Methods
- Nearly 6,000 fish were processed. Maturity was recorded for all, and weights for 5,600. Between 422 and 783 specimens were examined per species.
- Age analysis used 1,231 fish (92 to 154 per species). Including back-calculations, 8,806 age readings were made (450 to 1,285 per species).
- Samples came from wholesale markets at Eureka, Fort Bragg, and Monterey, plus Department research cruises in southern California. Scales were chosen over [[Otolith Analysis]] because they were easier to collect, although both gave valid readings.
- Total length was used rather than fork length because some rockfish have rounded or truncate tails.

### Age, Growth, and Longevity
- Ages were read from annuli on [[Scale Aging]] (ctenoid scales). Back-calculated lengths assumed a straight-line relationship between scale and fish length.
- Males grew slightly slower than females. Male bocaccio, chilipepper, and stripetail rockfish grew significantly slower. In all species, males failed to reach the lengths or ages of females.
- The von Bertalanffy fit matched observed mean lengths well through age 12 or 13 for all species, and through ages 15 or 16 for some. In shortbelly rockfish, the fit was good to age 10.
- Maximum ages were projected from the fitted curves. All species except shortbelly were estimated at 16 to 30 years. The species account for bocaccio gives "about 80 years," which conflicts with that range and may be a scan error.
- Maximum sizes and depths: bocaccio 34 inches and 175 fathoms; chilipepper 22 inches and 180 fathoms; splitnose 16 inches and 250 fathoms; shortbelly 12 inches and 155 fathoms.

### Weight-Length Relationships
- Weight increases roughly as the cube of total length, with exponents from 2.92 to 3.25 across species. The weight-length curve was fitted as W = aL^b, with sexes combined.
- Maximum weights range from 0.6 pounds (shortbelly) to at least 15.0 pounds (bocaccio).

### Size and Age at Maturity (50% mature)
- Shortbelly rockfish: 6½ inches at 3 years. This is the earliest maturity among the ten species.
- Bocaccio: 16½ inches at 4 years. Chilipepper: 12 inches at 4 years (females).
- Yellowtail: 13 inches at 5 years. Canary: 14 inches at 5–6 years.
- Vermilion: 13 inches at 6 years. Widow: 12½ inches at 4 years.
- Dark-blotched: 12 inches at 6 years. Splitnose: 8½ inches at 5 years.
- Stripetail: 6½ inches (females) at 4 years.

### Reproduction and Fecundity
- Rockfish are [[Ovoviviparity|ovoviviparous]]: fertilization and embryo development occur in the mother's body. "Spawning" in the bulletin therefore means the period when developed eggs are shed (hatching).
- Nine species spawn in winter, from about mid-November to mid-March. Splitnose rockfish spawn from about February through July.
- Fecundity increases with fish size. Most species produce about 0.2 to 2.0 million developing eggs per year. Shortbelly rockfish produce at most about 50,000. Bocaccio produce about 2.3 million in the largest fish.
- The largest fish of a species tend to ripen earlier than smaller ones.

### Feeding Habits
- **Bocaccio:** Feeds primarily on other fishes, switching from plankton before the end of its first year.
- **Splitnose, stripetail, and shortbelly:** Essentially plankton feeders throughout life, with adults switching to [[Euphausiids]].
- **Widow rockfish:** Mainly a macroplankton feeder, with hyperiid amphipods dominant.
- **Chilipepper, yellowtail, canary, vermilion, and dark-blotched:** Feed on macroplankton at times but mostly on smaller fishes.

### Racial Studies and Tagging
- Meristic counts (fin rays, gill rakers, lateral-line pores) may help compare stocks at different latitudes, though vertebral counts are identical across the genus.
- Tagging is difficult because rockfish suffer barotrauma when brought up from depth. Possible approaches include shallow-water sub-adult tagging, gradual ascent, and detachable tagged hooks. The author noted that blue rockfish tagging showed little movement after one year.

### Conclusions
- The bulletin supplies the first detailed life-history baseline for ten major California rockfish species, which is needed for sustainable [[Fisheries Management]].
- Rockfish are slow-growing, long-lived, and late-maturing, with feeding and spawning habits that vary widely by species.
