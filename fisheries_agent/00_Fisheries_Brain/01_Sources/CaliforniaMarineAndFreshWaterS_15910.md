## Executive Summary

This paper reports a 1951 statewide postal-card survey conducted by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] to quantify how California anglers divided their fishing effort among fresh water, the [[San Francisco Bay and Delta]], and ocean/coastal bay waters north and south of Santa Barbara. The study was motivated by disputes between marine and freshwater angling constituencies over the allocation of [[Dingell-Johnson Funds]] (federal excise tax revenue on fishing tackle) between marine and freshwater fisheries research programs. Approximately 3% of all 1951 license buyers (29,464 individuals) were mailed a simple postal card questionnaire; 8,633 usable returns (29%) were obtained. Results showed 56% of angler-days were spent in fresh water, 12% in the [[San Francisco Bay and Delta]], and 32% in ocean and coastal bays (10% north of Santa Barbara, 22% south). The estimated total statewide fishing effort was approximately 25 million angler-days in 1951, notably higher than prior estimates (15 million) from earlier surveys by [[Calhoun 1950]], suggesting either a genuine increase in fishing pressure or methodological differences in questionnaire design. The study validated that samples of 1,000–2,000 returns are statistically sufficient to characterize statewide fishing intensity.

## Background & Description

This is not a species-morphology paper; it is a fishing-effort/creel survey concerned with angler behavior rather than fish anatomy. No morphological data on target species are presented. The [[San Francisco Bay and Delta]] fishery is noted as targeting primarily anadromous species — [[Chinook Salmon]] and [[Striped Bass]] — with secondary catches of catfish, perch, and smelt, but no size, age, or morphometric data are given for any of these taxa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The survey stratified California's aquatic habitats into four functionally distinct fishing zones, reflecting differing ecological and fishery characteristics:

- **Fresh water** — inland lakes, reservoirs, and streams; supported the largest share of fishing effort (56% of angler-days; ~14,221,000 days).
- **[[San Francisco Bay and Delta]]** — a transitional estuarine gradient from salt to fresh water with "no clear cut boundary," supporting a fishery ecologically distinct from either pure marine or freshwater systems, dominated by anadromous fish runs ([[Chinook Salmon]], [[Striped Bass]]) plus catfish, perch, and smelt (12% of effort; ~3,077,000 days).
- **Ocean and coastal bays north of Santa Barbara** — treated as ecologically and fishery-wise distinct from southern California waters (10% of effort; ~2,592,000 days).
- **Ocean and coastal bays south of Santa Barbara** — the largest marine fishing zone, reflecting greater population density and milder climate in Southern California (22% of effort; ~5,473,000 days).

Santa Barbara was selected as the dividing line between northern and southern California marine fisheries due to recognized biological and fishery differences between these regions. The study notes that recent dry years reduced freshwater fishing quality in Southern California inland lakes, potentially driving anglers toward marine environments — an early observation of climate-driven shifts in angler effort distribution between freshwater and marine habitats.

## Key Findings

- **Survey scope**: 29,464 postal cards mailed to a random 3% sample of 1951 California license buyers (four license types: $3 resident annual, $3 nonresident 10-day, $10 nonresident annual, $25 non-citizen annual); 9,168 cards returned (31%), yielding 8,633 usable responses (29%).
- **Distribution of fishing days** (n=218,963 reported days):
  - Fresh water: 56.07% (122,772 days; avg. 18 days/fisherman)
  - [[San Francisco Bay and Delta]]: 12.13% (26,556 days; avg. 14 days/fisherman)
  - Ocean/bays north of Santa Barbara: 10.22% (22,378 days; avg. 10 days/fisherman)
  - Ocean/bays south of Santa Barbara: 21.58% (47,257 days; avg. 16 days/fisherman)
  - Overall average: 25 days/fisherman (elevated because many anglers fished multiple areas).
- **Total estimated effort**: ~25,363,000 angler-days statewide in 1951, extrapolated from ~1,000,000 license sales × 25.36 average days/fisherman.
- **Comparison to prior surveys** ([[Calhoun 1950]], [[Calhoun 1951]]): earlier war-year (1941–1946) estimates averaged only 13–14 days/fisherman (~6,000,000 total days), rising to 15.4 days by 1948 (~15,000,000 days) and 15 days again in 1951 via Calhoun's parallel survey. The present survey's 25-day average is markedly higher; a trend line extrapolated from Calhoun's 1943/1946/1948 data independently predicted ~26,110,000 days for 1951 — closely matching this survey's result despite Calhoun's own concurrent 1951 estimate of only 15 days, indicating unresolved questionnaire design effects (possible over- or under-reporting) on measured intensity.
- **Sample size validation**: Five random subsamples of 1,000 cards each reproduced area percentages within 2.5% of the full 8,633-card sample, and average days-per-fisherman within 1.5 days, statistically confirming that 1,000–2,000 returns are adequate for reliable fishing-intensity estimates despite formally significant chi-square differences (p<0.01).
- **Angler behavior patterns** (based on 5,000-card composite sample):
  - 33% fished fresh water exclusively; 46% fished fresh water plus other areas (78% total fresh water participation).
  - 16% fished ocean waters exclusively; 42% fished ocean plus other areas (87% total ocean participation, combining north and south zones).
  - 3% fished [[San Francisco Bay and Delta]] exclusively; 18% combined Bay-Delta with other areas (21% total participation).
  - Under 1% of anglers fished all four defined areas within the year, indicating that while many Californians engage in multiple fishing environments, full cross-habitat versatility was rare.
- **Policy implication**: The findings directly informed debate over the allocation of [[Dingell-Johnson Funds]] between marine and freshwater fisheries research, shifting the accepted marine-effort proportion from the previously used 25% (per [[Opinion Research Center 1949]]) toward a higher ~32% ocean/bay share (or 44% including Bay-Delta), with total California sport fishing pressure estimated between 15 and 25 million angler-days per year depending on survey methodology.
