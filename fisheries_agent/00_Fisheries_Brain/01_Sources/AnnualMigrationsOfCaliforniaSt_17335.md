# Annual Migrations of California Striped Bass

#ichthyology #marine-biology #roccus-saxatilis

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game report by A. J. Calhoun establishes a well-defined annual migration of large adult [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*) in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] and adjacent waters. Using [[Petersen Disc Tag]]s on roughly 4,200 fish tagged in 1947–1951, the study found a repeating seasonal cycle: a fall movement up into the fresh-water delta, winter residence there, spring dispersal into tributary rivers to spawn, and a return to [[San Francisco Bay]] and salt or brackish water for the summer. The author also compares this pattern with striped bass in Oregon and on the Atlantic coast and explains why the species is not abundant elsewhere on the Pacific coast.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Striped bass (*Roccus saxatilis*). The document also cites *Roccus lineatus* and *Roccus sawatilis* in references, which are OCR or nomenclature variants of the same species.
- **Prior work:** Earlier [[California Tagging Program]] efforts by Clark (1934, 1936) tagged mostly small fish (mostly under 12 inches, virtually all under 20 inches) and concluded that bass dispersed without definite migration. Calhoun argues this result reflects juvenile behavior, which is similar elsewhere (Vladykov and Wallace, 1938; Merriman, 1941). Large adults had not been tagged in sufficient numbers.
- **Tag design:** The Petersen disc tag consists of two plastic discs joined by a wire through the fish's body. Tags were first placed beneath the dorsal fin and later on the caudal peduncle. Materials were tested from 1947 onward (Calhoun, Fry, and Hughes, 1951).
- **Vessels and gear:** Fish were captured with gill nets. Circle netting in shallow, current-free areas such as [[Franks Tract]] used nets about 1,200 feet long and 12 feet deep, with mesh of about nine inches (stretched). Drift nets about 30 feet deep, with four mesh sections, were fished in the lower [[San Joaquin River]]. The research boat *Striper*, a 28-foot [[Fraser River Gill-Netter]] with a powered spool, was used for handling nets and tagging. Two tagging cradles allowed several hundred fish per day when fish were abundant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal distribution (from tag recoveries):**

| Period | Pattern reported |
|---|---|
| Nov 1 – Mar 31 | Fish concentrated in the Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta; recoveries near the tagging area; little movement |
| April | Fish spread into remote delta sections and tributary rivers to spawn |
| May 1 – June 15 | Main body returns downstream toward the bays; fish caught over a wide area |
| June 16 – Aug 31 | Nearly all adult recoveries in salt or brackish water inside the [[Golden Gate]] |
| Sept – Oct | Fish move back up into the delta; angling much better, more recaptures |
| Winter (about one year after tagging) | Pattern repeats the first winter chart, completing the cycle |

**Temperature and spawning:** Spawning is believed to require water temperatures approaching 60°F (Calhoun, 1950). In 1948 the [[Columbia River]] at [[Bonneville Dam]] did not reach this temperature until July 19, after spawning had ended in California. This is offered as one reason bass do not thrive north of Coos Bay.

**Habitat requirements:** The species seems to need extensive tidal estuaries for nursery areas and wintering grounds. Only the San Francisco and [[Coos Bay]] systems provide these between the Columbia River and the south. South of San Francisco, limited rainfall prevents spawning and nursery habitat. Between San Francisco and Coos Bay, the coast is steep and the rivers lack estuaries. Small populations occur where conditions are borderline, such as the Russian and Salinas rivers.

**Range and stragglers:** Striped bass spread from the Sacramento River into Oregon within 20 years of introductions in 1879 and 1882. Bass were caught at the mouth of the Columbia River, about 600 miles north of the Golden Gate, as early as 1906. Stragglers have been taken off Southern California, including a 10-inch fish off Huntington Beach (May 10, 1948) and an eight-pound fish from Mission Bay Bridge near San Diego (May 27, 1947).

**Comparison with other coasts:**
- **Oregon (Coos Bay):** Behavior is similar to California, with a spring spawning run upstream and a fall migration into sloughs (Morgan and Gerlach, 1950). Fish move into the ocean in early summer and return in fall.
- **Chesapeake Bay:** Bass reportedly move from upper to lower bay in fall and back in spring, the reverse of the California cycle (Vladykov and Wallace, 1938).
- **New Jersey rivers:** Behavior resembles California (Pearson, 1938).
- **Atlantic coast:** A mass coastal migration north in spring and south in fall has been reported (Merriman, 1941). No comparable movement is evident on the Pacific coast.

## Key Findings

**Tagged populations**

- **Group 1 (pilot, 1947):** 336 large bass tagged at Franks Tract in January and November 1947. A measured sample of 87 had a mean length of 32.2 inches (SD 2.04; range 25–37). This group contains the only two tags recovered from fish caught in the Pacific Ocean proper.
- **Group 2 (1949–1950 season):** About 1,800 fish, mainly caught in drift nets in the San Joaquin River, with some taken in circle nets at Franks Tract and Sherman Island Lake. The measured sample of 1,799 had a mean length of 25.0 inches (SD 5.07; range 12–42), with many more medium-sized fish than group 1.
- **Group 3 (1950–1951 season):** About 2,000 fish, similar to group 2 in all respects.
- **Returns:** Roughly 400 tag returns with known date and place of capture were available as of January 1952.
- **Juvenile/adult cutoff:** The 20-inch line between juveniles and adults is acknowledged as a simplification, since males mature at smaller sizes.

**Principal conclusions**

- **Annual cycle:** Large adults follow a regular annual migration pattern that repeated across successive years and was observed across four tagging seasons.
- **Fall-winter:** Fish move up into the fresh-water [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]] in fall and remain there through winter.
- **Spawning:** In spring, fish spread into remote delta sections and tributaries to spawn.
- **Summer:** Fish return to San Francisco Bay and salt or brackish waters inside the Golden Gate.
- **Ocean use:** Ocean catches are rare, probably under 1 percent of the total. Two ocean recoveries occurred in 1948, a year of surf fishing south of the Golden Gate. Some fish likely enter the Pacific each summer and go unrecorded, and some individuals "roam great distances."
- **Stock limits:** The species is not abundant on the Pacific coast outside San Francisco and Coos Bay because of limited estuaries, climate, and spawning temperatures.

**Program notes**

- The [[San Francisco Striped Bass Club]] ran a publicity and prize program, with drawings held three times a year over three years, that encouraged tag returns. The author believes nearly all tags were returned, which matters for evaluating fishing pressure.
