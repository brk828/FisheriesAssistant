#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1996 laboratory study by Childs and Clarkson examined how water temperature affects the prolonged swimming performance of larval and juvenile [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a federally endangered large-bodied cyprinid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Using a custom pump-driven swim tube, the authors measured fatigue velocity (FV50 — the velocity at which 50% of fish fail to sustain swimming in a 30-minute trial) across three ecologically relevant temperatures (10°C, 14°C, and 20°C) and three size/age classes (larvae, young juveniles, older juveniles). Swimming performance increased significantly with both temperature and fish length, with the largest relative performance losses occurring at the coldest temperature (10°C). The findings support the hypothesis that cold hypolimnetic dam releases (common in tailwaters of dams such as [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Glen Canyon Dam]]) impair the swimming ability of young Colorado squawfish, contributing to reduced foraging efficiency, increased predation risk, and population decline, with direct implications for species recovery and dam operation policy.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Ptychocheilus lucius* (Colorado squawfish), family Cyprinidae — a large-bodied minnow native to the Colorado River basin.
- **Size classes tested:**
  - **Larval (mesolarval) fish:** 13.0–14.1 mm mean total length (TL); 26–37 days post-hatch. Pre-anal fin fold prominent; most fins except pelvics well developed.
  - **Young juveniles:** 21.5–22.3 mm mean TL; 65–78 days old.
  - **Older juveniles:** 27.7–28.8 mm mean TL; 105–116 days old.
- **Body-shape effects:** One-way ANOVA on young juveniles (range 19.3–24.4 mm TL) found **no significant effect of length, weight, or cross-sectional diameter** on swimming response (pass/fail/failure to swim; *P* > 0.26 for all tests), indicating variation in body shape did not meaningfully affect swimming performance within a size class.
- **Blocking effects:** Considered insignificant, as no test fish had a cross-sectional area exceeding 10% of the swim-tube's cross-section (per Bell and Terhune 1970; Webb 1975).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Historically widespread in major rivers of the [[Colorado River Basin]]; extirpated from the lower basin (downstream of Glen Canyon Dam) by the 1970s, with continued declines in the upper basin.
- **Critical rearing habitat:** Backwaters, eddy return channels, and tailwater–tributary mixing zones are especially important nursery habitats for early life stages of Colorado squawfish and other native fishes (per [[Valdez and Wick 1981]]; [[Tyus and Karp 1989]]).
- **Thermal regime disruption:** Hypolimnetic (cold-water) releases from hydroelectric dams (e.g., [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]]) depress spring–summer tailwater temperatures, rarely exceeding 14°C, versus ~20°C in unimpounded mainstem/tributary reaches during the natural spawning season.
- **Spawning cues:** Spawning is triggered by decreasing flow and rising water temperature, timing reproduction to expose larvae/juveniles to the warmest seasonal temperatures under natural conditions — a cue disrupted by dam-altered thermal and flow regimes.
- **Trophic/predation linkages:** Reduced swimming ability at low temperature is linked to decreased foraging efficiency ([[Blaxter 1986]]), increased vulnerability to predation ([[Coutant et al. 1974]]), and reduced capacity to maintain position in preferred flow habitats ([[Harvey 1987]]).
- **Competing warm-water fauna:** A return to natural (warmer) temperature regimes below dams could inadvertently benefit non-native warm-water fish species, complicating simple thermal-restoration strategies.

## Key Findings

- **FV50 (Fatigue Velocity) increased significantly with both temperature and fish length** across all three size classes (Table 1):

| Life Stage | Temp (°C) | Mean TL (mm) | FV50 (cm/s) | FV50 (BL/s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Larval | 10 | 14.1 | 10.5 | 7.4 |
| Larval | 14 | 13.4 | 13.5 | 10.1 |
| Larval | 20 | 13.0 | 16.1 | 12.4 |
| Young juvenile | 10 | 22.3 | 13.3 | 6.0 |
| Young juvenile | 14 | 21.5 | 14.7 | 6.8 |
| Young juvenile | 20 | 21.7 | 17.4 | 8.0 |
| Older juvenile | 10 | 28.4 | 14.2 | 5.0 |
| Older juvenile | 14 | 28.8 | 15.2 | 5.3 |
| Older juvenile | 20 | 27.7 | 19.2 | 6.9 |

- **Relative swimming ability (BL/s) declined with increasing size**: larvae showed the highest relative swimming performance (7.4–12.4 BL/s) and older juveniles the lowest (5.0–7.0 BL/s) — a pattern consistent with general fish swimming physiology ([[Beamish 1978]]).
- **Temperature sensitivity diminished with size**: differences in swimming ability between 10°C and 14°C became less pronounced as fish length increased; older juveniles showed statistically similar FV50 at 10°C and 14°C, though both remained significantly lower than at 20°C.
- **Large relative performance losses at 10°C vs. 20°C**: swimming ability dropped 40% in larvae, 25% in young juveniles, and 28% in older juveniles when temperature decreased from 20°C to 10°C.
- **Reduced motivation to swim at cold temperatures**: Only 2 of 121 fish failed to swim during acclimation at 20°C, compared to 32 of 101 fish at 10°C and 26 of 104 at 14°C — a significant association between low temperature and failure to initiate swimming (Pearson chi-square, *P* < 0.001 for larvae and older juveniles).
- **Bimodal swim-time distribution**: Most individual fish either failed within the first 5 minutes or successfully completed the full 30-minute trial, consistent with prior larval fish swimming studies ([[Houde 1969]]; [[Meng 1993]]).
- **Behavioral note**: All swimming fish preferred the darkened, styrofoam-covered portion of the swim tube — differing from larval striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*), which prefer lighted areas ([[Meng 1993]]).
- **Peak performance not reached**: Experimental temperatures (10–20°C) did not reach the likely thermal optimum for swimming (~25°C, based on [[Bulkley et al. 1981]] preferenda data); this was intentional, matching realistic river/tailwater conditions rather than physiological maxima.
- **Management implication**: Findings support the conclusion that cold hypolimnetic dam discharges significantly impair prolonged swimming ability in young-of-year Colorado squawfish, plausibly contributing to reduced foraging success, elevated predation risk, and reduced growth/survival — reinforcing calls for epilimnetic (warm-water) release strategies and reduced daily flow fluctuations to aid species recovery, while cautioning that thermal restoration alone may also favor non-native warm-water competitors and that migration corridor restoration remains essential for adult spawning migrations.
