# Crayfish and Freshwater Mussels of the Lower New River, Virginia and West Virginia

#decapoda #cambaridae #unionidae #freshwater-biology

## Executive Summary

This 1980 survey of the [[New River]] drainage, published in the Chicago Academy of Sciences *Natural History Miscellanea* (No. 208), documents the crayfish (Decapoda) and freshwater mussels/clams (Mollusca: Pelecypoda) of the lower New River gorge in Virginia and West Virginia. Fieldwork in summer 1979 yielded six crayfish species and six freshwater clam species. The authors found that the introduced crayfish [[Orconectes Virilis]] has expanded its range, and that the introduced Asian clam [[Corbicula Manilensis]] is well established. They also report that the gorge's falls appear to limit downstream dispersal of some crayfish, not only upstream movement as previously shown for fishes.

## Background & Description

The New River rises in the [[Blue Ridge Mountains]] of North Carolina and flows north through Virginia and West Virginia, joining the Gauley River at Gauley Bridge to form the Kanawha River. The study area spans two physiographic provinces: most of the West Virginia portion lies in the [[Appalachian Plateau]], while the Greenbrier River valley and the small Virginia portion lie in the [[Valley and Ridge Province]].

Across roughly 90 km, the river runs through the New River gorge, incised up to 457 m into the plateau. Falls within or near the gorge include [[Kanawha Falls]] (7.3 m, less than 2.0 km below the Gauley confluence), Wylie, Bull and [[Sandstone Falls]]. Kanawha and Sandstone Falls have long been regarded as barriers to fish dispersal into the upper Kanawha (New) River drainage.

Earlier crayfish and mussel records were sparse. Ortmann (1913) collected five mussel species and two crayfish species along the Alleghenian divide. Newcombe (1929) recorded six crayfish species from New River drainage, and Lawton (1979) described nine crayfish species in the drainage. The stated objective was to document the present distribution of crayfish and freshwater clams in the New River gorge.

**Methods**
- 58 crayfish collections and 11 freshwater clam collections, summer 1979.
- Crayfish were taken with a 1.3 x 3.3 m, 3.6 mm mesh nylon seine during fish collecting, using substrate disturbance (see [[Seine Sampling]]).
- Clams were collected by [[Scuba Sampling]]. High, turbid summer water made this difficult.
- Qualitative representative samples were preserved in 70% isopropanol.
- Nomenclature followed Hobbs (1974) for crayfish and Burch (1973) for clams.
- Localities are mapped in Figures 1 and 2. Crayfish stations span the main river from Lurick, Virginia, through the gorge, to tributaries such as [[Lick Creek]], [[Brush Creek]], [[Bluestone River]] and [[Madam Creek]]. The 11 mussel stations are on the main New River between Glen Lyn, Virginia, and Meadow Creek.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dispersal barriers.** Hocutt (1979) identified the gorge and its falls as a faunal filter for upstream fish dispersal. Lachner and Jenkins (1971) proposed that the same rapids and falls may also hinder downstream movement. This study suggests the latter applies to some crayfish. Several species had very limited populations below Sandstone Falls, while being widely distributed above it.

**Habitat preferences.** Hobbs (1969) notes that *Cambarus robustus* prefers larger streams or quieter water and is not usually found in the cascading reaches typical of the New River drainage. Ortmann (1913) considered *Cambarus bartonii* a species of small creeks rather than rivers.

**Invasive species.** *Orconectes virilis* was previously known only from [[Bluestone Lake]], Summers County (Lawton, 1979). It is tolerant of poor conditions and highly aggressive (Schwartz et al., 1963), and it expanded rapidly after introduction in the [[Patapsco River]], Maryland. *Corbicula manilensis* is an introduced Asian clam. The authors suggest that the apparently disjunct *Orconectes spinosus* population in Lick Creek could result from a bait bucket introduction, since a road parallels the creek.

**Taxonomic uncertainty.** The status of *Orconectes spinosus* and the closely related *O. juvenilis* and *O. putnami* remains unstable. Ortmann (1931) treated *O. spinosus* and *O. putnami* as synonyms of *O. juvenilis*. Hobbs (1974) re-elevated them to species in 1972. H. Hobbs (personal communication) advised identifying West Virginia specimens as *O. spinosus* until ranges and taxonomy are clarified.

## Key Findings

**Crayfish (Table 1)**
- Six species were collected: *Orconectes obscurus*, *O. spinosus*, *O. virilis*, *Orconectes* sp., *Cambarus bartonii*, *C. sciotensis*, *C. robustus* and *Cambarus* sp. (the table lists eight taxa, including two unidentified ones).
- *Cambarus sciotensis* was the most abundant crayfish by numbers and sites. It occurred throughout the drainage but not at downstream main-channel stations. Hobbs (1969) gives it a discontinuous range in the upper Kanawha drainage, but the authors suspect it occurs throughout the Kanawha system.
- *Orconectes spinosus* was the most common Astacidae collected. It was found in only two tributaries below Sandstone Falls, plus throughout Lick Creek.
- *Cambarus bartonii* was found at only two locations below the falls but was widely distributed above them. Only one specimen was taken in the main channel (Station 2).
- *Cambarus robustus* was collected at a single site (Station 39), in Brush Creek.
- *Orconectes virilis* expanded its range upstream and downstream of Bluestone Lake and was recorded in three new counties: Monroe, Raleigh and Fayette.

**Freshwater clams (Table 2)**
- Six species were collected: *Tritogonia verrucosa*, *Cyclonaias tuberculata*, *Elliptio dilatata*, *Actinonaias carinata carinata*, *Lampsilus ovata ventricosa* and *Corbicula manilensis*.
- *Corbicula manilensis* was the most abundant clam, occurring at 9 of 11 stations. It was absent only from stations 5 and 7.
- *Cyclonaias tuberculata* was the most abundant unionid.
- Compared with Ortmann (1913), which recorded four clam species, *Lasmigona subviridis* was not found in this study. *Actinonaias carinata*, *Lampsilus ovata ventricosa* and *Corbicula manilensis* were new additions. All three had been recorded in the Kanawha River (Morris and Taylor, 1978).

**Conclusions**
- Crayfish distribution appears limited below Sandstone Falls. This suggests the gorge filters downstream as well as upstream dispersal for some taxa.
- *Orconectes virilis* is actively spreading and may pose a risk to native crayfish.
- *Corbicula manilensis* is well established across the study area.
- Taxonomic clarification of the *Orconectes spinosus* complex is needed.
