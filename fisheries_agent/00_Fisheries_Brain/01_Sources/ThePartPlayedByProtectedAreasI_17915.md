# Protected Areas and the Conservation of Threatened French Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #threatened-freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This study assesses how threatened freshwater fish species are represented within France's main [[Protected Areas]]. It was carried out as the first phase of a national conservation programme set up by the Ministry of the Environment (MATE), the [[National Museum Of Natural History]] (MNHN) and the French Fisheries Council (CSP). The survey finds that few protected areas were established for fish, that not all threatened species occur in protected areas, and that where they do occur they are rarely monitored or managed specifically for fish. Protected sites also usually cover only part of a catchment basin, which is insufficient to maintain populations. The author recommends integrating protected areas, including [[Fishing Reserves]], into a coherent network linked by river corridors, backed by species inventories, site management plans and national action plans.

## Background & Description

- **Publication:** P. Keith, *Biological Conservation* 92 (2000), pp. 265–273.
- **Context:** Decline and extinction of French freshwater fish prompted a national programme. The Red List was first published by Keith et al. (1992) and revised by Keith (1998) under [[IUCN Red List]] (1995) criteria.
- **Scope:** Of 72 French freshwater fish taxa (primary, secondary, diadromous and vicarious species, following Myers 1938), 27 are on the Red List: 3 Extinct (EX), 4 Critically Endangered (CR), 2 Endangered (EN), 14 Vulnerable (VU) and 4 Low Risk (LR).
- **Rationale:** Protected areas elsewhere have helped restore threatened fish (Maitland and Lyle 1992; Maitland 1995; Moyle 1995). However, most French protected areas were designed to protect habitats, mammals, birds or plants rather than fish.

### Types of Protected Area Examined

Only sites with strong protection, where management could benefit fish, were included:
- **[[National Parks]]:** core areas (strict protection) and peripheral sectors (sustainable development). Established under the 1960 Act.
- **[[Nature Reserves]]:** established under the 1930/1957 and 1976 legislation by ministerial decree. Fishing is allowed in at least part of many.
- **[[Coastal And Lakeshore Conservation Agency]] sites:** land purchased to protect coastal and lakeshore habitats (Act of 10 July 1975).
- **[[Biotope Protection Orders]]:** issued by the Préfet under the 1976 Nature Protection Act. They are quick to issue and restrict activities on critical perimeters.

### Methods

- **Atlas:** The computerised [[French Freshwater Fish Atlas]] (Allardi and Keith 1991; Allardi et al. 1997) holds more than 200,000 species records from over 15,000 sampling stations, drawn from CSP surveys and university or private laboratories (1991–1997).
- **Protected-area file:** The MNHN Natural Heritage Department maintains geo-referenced files on protected areas, linked to the atlas in the SPN/MNHN Flora-Fauna database.
- **Supplementary surveys:** Standardised forms were sent to Conservation Agency managers, followed by targeted additional inventories. Biotope Protection Order data were compiled département by département. Nature reserve data came from published guides (Reille and Bonnin-Luquot 1987; Mosse 1996).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Main threats:** Habitat destruction and anthropisation are the principal causes of decline (Noss 1987; Maitland 1994, 1995).
- **Location of parks:** All mainland national parks lie in high-altitude mountain areas (Alps and Pyrenees) in sparsely populated upper catchments dominated by salmonids. Few threatened species occur there. Parks were chosen for low human presence, and none was established to protect fish.
- **Restocking:** Restocking (for example with introduced [[Lake Trout]]) conflicts with conservation because of predation on native fish, amphibians and invertebrates.
- **Habitat fragmentation:** Streams can act as corridors linking reserves, provided their integrity is maintained along their length (Robinson et al. 1992; Blondel 1995b). The debate over isolated "habitat islands" versus interconnected reserves remains open (Pimm et al. 1988; Noss 1987).
- **Other frameworks:** The [[Natura 2000]] network, the ZNIEFF inventory and the [[Water Framework Directive]] are identified as useful tools for site selection and habitat quality.

## Key Findings

### 1. National Parks (core areas)
- Only one threatened species, the **Arctic Charr**, is recorded in park core areas. It occurs in three of the six national parks (including Vanoise and Mercantour), but was introduced in the 20th century and was not native there.
- None of the six most endangered species (CR and EN) is present in the core areas.

### 2. Nature Reserves
- Of 122 reserves established before 1 June 1997, 84 contain streams, ponds or wetlands that could hold fish. Only a subset is confirmed to contain fish (the OCR-garbled count is not reliable). 33 reserves host threatened species.
- 16 of the 27 threatened species are represented. Only two of the six most endangered (**Apron** and **Atlantic Salmon**) occur.
- The **Allis Shad** spawning reserve at Agen on the Garonne was established specifically for fish. The Camargue is regularly monitored.

### 3. Coastal and Lakeshore Conservation Agency Sites
- Of 322 sites, 261 provided data covering 17 threatened species. Ten species were recorded at fewer than 10 sites.
- The **European Eel** is the only species at more than 100 sites and occurs at about 40% of sites. Pike, sea trout, soufie and barbeau meridional occur at 5–15% of sites.
- Only two of the six most threatened species (**Atlantic Salmon** and **Weatherfish**) are recorded.
- Management of these sites is largely in the hands of local authorities, and fishing limits can only be set with agreement from participating associations, which is rarely given.

### 4. Biotope Protection Orders
- Of 468 orders issued by June 1997, 46 target freshwater fish, covering about 1,200 km of streams (3.4% of French river length).
- 17 fish species are named, 11 of them threatened. Six are not, including Brown Trout (the species covered by 30 orders), Tench, Perch, Rainbow Trout, Roach and Stone Loach.
- Three of the six most endangered species (Apron, Sturgeon and Atlantic Salmon) are covered.
- Most protected length lies in the Garonne basin (Midi-Pyrénées and Aquitaine) and Basse-Normandie coastal rivers (86% of the total length across these three regions).
- 9.6% of orders aim at fish protection, and 4.9% concern threatened species.
- Orders are easy to issue and can be repealed by the Préfet. Enforcement is difficult: illegal works have already destroyed Apron habitat at its only protected site.

### 5. Fishing Reserves (outside the study scope)
- About 1,400 fishing reserves cover roughly 800 km of river and 1,000 ha of lakes and ponds.
- More than 75% are sited on dams, where fishing was prohibited for safety and to limit overfishing of migratory species. The rest were chosen for angler interest, security or restricting professional fishing.
- Many are very short, averaging 500–600 m and sometimes only 50 m. Almost none has a real management scheme.

### 6. Species Coverage and Priority Sites
- Only two threatened species are absent from all protected areas: the **[[Lez Bullhead]]** and the **[[Large Spotted Trout]]**.
- The Lez Bullhead is endemic to a 3–4 km stretch of the Lez River, where its entire life cycle occurs. Protecting this stretch is essential to its survival.
- Other priority sites include the last functional sturgeon spawning grounds (Garonne and Dordogne), the last large apron areas, and the last Alpine ponds with Weatherfish.
- The **Corsican National Park** should include the catchments of the Large Spotted Trout to avoid fragmentation and stochastic extinction. Brown Trout restocking there must stop.

### 7. Overall Conclusion and Recommendations
- French protected areas are currently ineffective for fish conservation. Most lack management plans, population monitoring and research.
- The author recommends:
  1. Compile accurate species inventories for every site, especially reserves and fishing reserves, and monitor population status regularly.
  2. Implement management plans for every site containing threatened species, coordinated across each network.
  3. Coordinate plans and sites at basin and national levels.
  4. Prepare a national action plan for each threatened species, including research and awareness programmes.
- Site boundaries should follow the functional needs of species and their life cycles, including whole catchments and a [[Minimum Viable Area]] for each threatened population.
- Biotope Protection Orders should be extended to more threatened species and strengthened for the most endangered ones.
- Fishing reserves should be redefined, with an inventory and national management plan that shifts the emphasis from fishing to heritage.
