# Fisheries Safety Handbook

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-safety

## Executive Summary

The *Fisheries Safety Handbook* was compiled by the [[American Fisheries Society]] (AFS) Professional Safety Committee and published in December 2008. It is a practical safety reference for [[Aquatic Resource Professionals]] (researchers, educators, managers, and administrators in fishery and aquatic science). The handbook is advisory rather than legal in force and emphasizes U.S. standards, particularly those of the [[Occupational Safety and Health Administration]] (OSHA). It covers eight topics: driving, boating, electrofishing, underwater and diving, first aid and CPR/AED, fish handling, pesticide application and hazardous waste, and laboratory safety. Its central message is that most fieldwork hazards can be prevented through training, risk awareness, and enforced safety protocols.

## Background & Description

**Origin and charge.** The Professional Safety Committee was formed in 2005 under AFS president Chris Kohler, reformed in 2006, and continued through 2008. Its charge was to raise awareness of safety issues in the fisheries profession and to work with the Continuing Education Committee on training opportunities. The charge was justified by Goal 3 (Professional Stature) and Strategy 3.1 of the [[AFS Strategic Plan 2005-2009]], which aims to improve salary, safety, and working conditions for fishery professionals.

**Committee.** Eight members served as authors (Bill Fisher, Chair, OK; Laura Csoboth, IL; Don Dennerline, GA; Casey Harthorn, NM; Chris O'Bara, WA; Larry Olmsted, ID; Dennis Reicke, MS; Roger Rulifson, NC). Fred Heitman (TN) and Steve Sellars (NC) also contributed. Subject-matter reviewers included staff from the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], the [[Nebraska Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit]], and East Carolina University.

**Structure.** The handbook has four general elements for a successful safety program: management leadership and employee involvement, work analysis, hazard prevention and control, and training. It emphasizes the [[Project-Specific Health and Safety Plan]] as an important but often overlooked tool. Each section was written independently and reviewed by subject-matter professionals. The text refers to "six sections," but the contents list eight; this note follows the contents list.

**Scope.** The handbook is intended for general use within the United States. Non-U.S. readers are advised to substitute their own national and state laws while keeping the underlying principles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The handbook is primarily about human safety, but it links safety to aquatic conditions in several places:

- **Polluted or organically enriched waters** raise the risk of disease transmission during fish handling, including [[Mycobacteriosis]] in [[Striped Bass]] in the [[Chesapeake Bay]].
- **Low dissolved oxygen** is usually the cause of fish kills. If the oxygen loss results from chemical pollution, biologists should not investigate the kill themselves.
- **Weather and water conditions** affect risk: thunderstorms, rain, swift, cold, or deep water, and remote wilderness settings more than two hours from definitive medical care.
- **Pesticides and piscicides** (such as [[Rotenone]] and aquatic herbicides) are regulated by the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]] because of risks to the environment, non-target animals, fish, and humans.

## Key Findings

### 1. Driving and Towing
- About 19 million automobile collisions occur each year in the U.S., causing 40,000 deaths and 2,300,000 disabling injuries (National Safety Council 2006). One in nine drivers will be involved in a collision in a given year.
- Vehicles pulling a trailer are four times more likely to be involved in a single-vehicle accident, especially on long trips (Dark 1971).
- Recommended practices include greater following distance, caution when passing, and the "THINK BIG, THINK HEAVY, SLOW DOWN" rule. If a trailer sways, release the gas, hold the wheel straight, and do **not** brake or oversteer.
- Tongue weight should be 10–15% of gross trailer weight. Undersized tires are a common towing problem.
- A vehicle used by a fisheries biologist should carry an emergency kit, and the fuel tank should never fall below a quarter full.
- At least 35 states offer insurance discounts for completing a driving safety course.

### 2. Boating Safety
- Biologists work from large and small motorboats, canoes, and kayaks, and should complete a recognized boating course. The handbook points to the [[America's Boating Course]] and courses from the [[U.S. Coast Guard Auxiliary]] and [[United States Power Squadrons]].
- The U.S. Department of Interior requires personal flotation devices (PFDs) at all times on motorboats under 26 feet, and in open spaces or when directed on boats 26–65 feet long. The handbook states that biologists should wear PFDs at all times on or operating motorboats.
- Boat safety tips cover life jacket use, sobriety (an operator with blood alcohol content above 0.10 is ten times more likely to be involved in a boating accident than one with zero), education (the handbook says 70% of accidents are attributed to inexperience and lack of attention, a figure given in the text), and free vessel checks.

### 3. Electrofishing Safety
- Electrical current can be fatal. People have been killed by currents as low as 0.002 amp, well below the average of 6 amps used in some operations. Death can result from respiratory arrest or cardiac fibrillation.
- Standard boat operations are limited to four people: two netters, one fish handler, and the boat driver. Bank and skiff shocking carry additional risk and should be avoided when alternatives exist.
- The electric field can extend 5–10 meters from the electrodes.
- At least the crew leader and one crewmember must be trained in CPR and AED use.
- Mandatory equipment includes PFDs, electrician's rubber gloves (rated at or above the generator's wattage, such as 5,000 or 7,500 watts), and insulated waders. Breathable waders should not be used for bank or backpack shocking.
- Crews must stop operations during thunderstorms and when a continuous sheen of water develops over equipment. Biologists must never electrofish alone.
- The acknowledgment form requires no alcohol for 4 hours before operations and for 8 hours after an accident, according to the form's text.

### 4. Underwater and Diving Safety
- The section is adapted from the Standards for Scientific Diving of the [[American Academy of Underwater Sciences]] (AAUS), which has more than 100 organizational members.
- OSHA exempted scientific diving from commercial diving regulations in 1982, with final guidelines effective in 1985 (29 CFR 1910.401 Subpart T).
- Exempt programs must have a diving safety manual and a Diving Control Board with a majority of active divers. Scientific divers act as observers and data gatherers, and the buddy system is required for scuba diving.
- OSHA's review found scientific diving to be safer than commercial diving before the exemption.

### 5. First Aid, CPR/AED, and Remote Work
- Most employers recommend basic first aid and CPR/AED training, though enforcement is inconsistent.
- Combination first aid, CPR, and AED courses are the most beneficial and economical. Certifications last 1–2 years for CPR/AED and 3 years for first aid.
- Training options include on-site instructors, community classes, and blended online courses from the [[American Red Cross]] and the [[American Heart Association]].
- AEDs can increase survival in cardiac arrest, with on-site units cited as increasing survival rates by almost 60%. AEDs are not designed for extreme heat or cold or wet conditions, and are not recommended for field or long-term vehicle storage.
- Areas more than two hours from definitive medical care are considered remote, requiring wilderness first aid training. Recommended providers include [[Wilderness Medical Associates]] and the [[Wilderness Medicine Institute]]. A satellite phone may be prudent for work beyond cell coverage.

### 6. Fish Handling Safety
- Fish handling is "one of the greatest and most underestimated" hazards for fisheries biologists, with risks from disease, spines, gill rakers, teeth, and opercula.
- [[Fish Handler's Disease]] (mycobacteriosis) caused severe skin infections and had spread to nearly three-quarters of striped bass in the Chesapeake Bay, according to a March 11, 2006 *Washington Post* report cited in the handbook.
- [[Catfish]] spines can envenomate handlers, causing pain out of proportion to the injury.
- The most effective prevention is appropriate gloves (nylon or Kevlar), with latex gloves worn underneath for open wounds. Wire cutters or hooked picking tools reduce contact with spines.
- Wounds should be cleaned and disinfected promptly. Joint wounds are especially prone to infection. Signs of infection include ascending red marks and joint pain, which require immediate medical attention.

### 7. Pesticide Application and Hazardous Waste
- Pesticide use is regulated under the [[Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act]] of 1972, which requires applicator training and certification. State cooperative Extension services provide the entry point.
- Biologists are often first responders to fish kills. If chemical pollution is suspected, they should **not** enter the area and should contact a supervisor or state emergency response agency.
- Investigators of chemical spills typically complete a 40-hour [[HAZWOPER]] general site worker course, with an 8-hour refresher annually and a medical checkup.
- The [[Hazard Communication Standard]] requires hazard assessments, chemical inventories, [[Material Safety Data Sheets]], labeling, and employee training.

### 8. Laboratory Safety
- Laboratory work falls under [[OSHA 29 CFR 1910.1450]], which governs the use of hazardous chemicals in labs. The handbook argues that most field and lab biologists fall within its scope.
- Employers should maintain a written chemical hygiene plan, a master chemical list, a master file of MSDSs, and an emergency contact list posted on the lab door.
- Key controls include protective clothing, full-toed shoes, no food or drink in the lab, adequate ventilation, and chemical fire extinguishers.
- Formalin should not be poured down the drain untreated. The authors' municipal facility requires formalin to be mixed with bleach to form a precipitate. Chemical wastes must not be mixed, and containers must be labeled and dated.
- The handbook notes that employers can be held personally liable if they fail to provide required protective gear.

### Overall Message
The handbook stresses that safety is everyone's responsibility, that training should come from trusted sources, and that a project-specific health and safety plan is essential. It repeatedly emphasizes that biologists should never work alone, should wear protective equipment, and should avoid investigating hazards they are not trained to handle.
