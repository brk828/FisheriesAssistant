# Fish Population of Deep Lake, Michigan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Michigan Department of Conservation study, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society* (1948), analyzes the fish community of [[Deep Lake]] (Oakland County, Michigan). The entire fish population was killed with [[Rotenone]] (powdered derris root) in September 1941, ending a four-year research program on the spawning, production and survival of four centrarchid species. A total of 27,329 fish weighing 562.7 pounds (38.0 pounds per acre) was recovered. [[Bluegill]] were the most abundant species. Legal-sized game fish made up only 3.2 percent of the number but 56.1 percent of the weight. The authors stress that recovery of young-of-the-year fish was incomplete, so the true population was probably larger than the recovered sample. They also compare the lake's standing crop with natural lakes in Michigan, Wisconsin, Florida and Nova Scotia.

## Background & Description

Deep Lake was privately owned and lightly fished. Its owners asked the Institute for Fisheries Research for a management survey in 1937, believing the lake could support trout. Instead, the lake was used for experiments on [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rock Bass]], [[Bluegill]] and [[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]] from 1938. Before the poisoning, 924 fish were removed by Institute personnel. The only recorded stocking was 1,000 minnows in May 1937 (bluntnose minnows and [[Golden Shiner]]), and neither established.

The lake covers 14.84 acres, is about 1,230 feet long and 660 feet wide, and has two basins: an eastern one 61 feet deep and a western one 51 feet deep. Shoreline development is 1.12, and the drop-off from the shore is steep. Suitable spawning area (shore to 5 feet) is 11.8 percent of the lake, while the whole littoral zone (to the limit of higher submerged plants, which grow to 20 feet) is 37.0 percent. The lake has no outlet or permanent inlet. It is fed mainly by groundwater seepage and receives runoff from less than 10 acres of land. Lake level fluctuations seldom exceed one foot per year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality and stratification.** In late July 1939 the lake was thermally and chemically stratified, with a [[Thermocline]] between 16 and 39 feet. Surface temperature was 79° F and bottom temperature 42.8° F at 61 feet. Dissolved oxygen of 3.0 ppm extended to about 50 feet. Late August and September analyses found 3.3 ppm to 45 feet. Methyl-orange alkalinity was 84 to 93 ppm, and pH ranged from 8.6 at the surface to 6.9 at 61 feet. A Secchi disk was visible to 20 to 24 feet, which is clearer than the average for southern Michigan lakes.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Vegetation was judged fairly abundant relative to suitable depth. Plentiful species included pondweed (*Potamogeton natans*) and cattail (*Typha angustifolia*). Common species included water lilies, bulrush, pickerel weed, several additional pondweeds, and [[Chara]]. Cover was still considered inadequate for all fish species during some seasons, with only a few fallen trees, logs and boulders providing winter shelter.

**Recovery constraints.** Recovery of small fish was difficult. Roiled water and silt hid specimens, emergent vegetation concealed others, and smaller fish died first and were eaten by larger fish. Insects, snakes, turtles and birds also consumed the fish. The authors therefore treat all young-of-the-year counts as minimums.

## Key Findings

**Methods.** Derris root (5 percent rotenone), about 665 pounds, was applied at roughly 0.5 ppm. Some was towed in weighted burlap bags at depth for 18 hours to reach the thermocline and hypolimnion. The remainder was sprayed onto the surface from a motorboat. Caged live fish showed the poison penetrated below 40 feet. Collectors visited daily for 17 days after the poisoning. Scales and lengths were taken from fish over 100 mm, and a random sample was taken from smaller fish. Aggregate weights were recorded for all fish, with individual weights taken from random samples.

**Community composition.** The population had 14 species, comprising game and pan fish, coarse fish, and forage fish. Most were considered native. [[Common Sucker]], [[Mudminnow]] and [[Golden Shiner]] were judged introduced, most likely from bait buckets or, for mudminnows, overflow from an adjoining marsh.

**Totals and legal fish.** Total recovery was 27,329 fish weighing 562.7 pounds (about 1,847 fish and 38.0 pounds per acre). Legal or desirable fish numbered 864 (3.2 percent, 58.4 per acre) and weighed 315.4 pounds (21.2 pounds per acre, 56.1 percent of weight). The five protected species plus hybrids made up 90.7 percent of the number and 87.6 percent of the weight. Green sunfish and coarse fish together made up 9.3 percent of the number and 12.4 percent of the weight.

**Species summaries (number; weight; notes):**

- **[[Bluegill]]:** 16,059 fish; 256.0 lb; 58.8% of number, 45.5% of weight. Legal fish were 544 (3.4% of bluegills, 68.3% of their weight). The legal-to-undersized ratio was nearly 1:30. Growth was comparable to the state average, and legal length was usually reached in the fourth season. The sex ratio was 42% male to 58% female (sample of 365).
- **[[Pumpkinseed Sunfish]]:** 5,935 fish; 68.9 lb; 21.7% of total fish population by number. Only 33 were legal. Growth was slower than the state average, with legal length reached in the fifth or sixth season. Anglers may have removed legal fish, and young-of-the-year counts (1,402) were far lower than bluegill counts.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]:** 785 fish; 106.0 lb. Legal fish (97, 10 inches and over) weighed 79.8 lb, making up 75.3% of the weight. Growth closely matched the Michigan average, with legal length reached in the third or early fourth year.
- **[[Rock Bass]]:** 585 fish (40.0 per acre); 22.9 lb. Growth exceeded the state average, especially in age-groups II to V.
- **[[Yellow Perch]]:** 817 fish; 20.4 lb. Only 26 were legal. Growth was slower than the state average, adding about one year to reach legal length.
- **[[Yellow Bullhead]]:** 1,082 fish; 38.4 lb; 6.8% of weight. About 79.4% of the fish were under 3 inches. Anglers almost never fished for them.
- **[[Mud Pike]]:** 369 fish; 11.4 lb. Scales could not provide reliable ages. About 81.2% of the fish formed one young-of-the-year group.
- **[[Green Sunfish]]:** 1,090 fish; 11.0 lb. Treated as undesirable, since in Michigan it seldom exceeds 6 inches. Only five fish were 6 inches or over.
- **[[Hybrid Sunfish]] (bluegill × pumpkinseed):** 589 fish; 18.4 lb. Only 27 were legal. The hybrids grew faster than either parent, which fits earlier observations on this combination.
- **[[Common Sucker]]:** 5 fish; 8.9 lb; likely introduced by anglers.
- **[[Golden Shiner]]:** 1 fish (2.5 inches); likely introduced by anglers.
- **[[Mudminnow]]:** 2 fish (1.8 and 2.1 inches); likely introduced through periodic overflow from an adjoining marsh.

**Year-class strength.** Age composition showed no major irregularities, but age-group distributions were uneven. The authors judged the 1939 year class weak for bluegill, pumpkinseed, largemouth bass and green sunfish. The 1940 year class was strong for most species, and the 1941 year class appeared weak, though incomplete recovery makes this uncertain. Strong and weak years appeared to alternate, possibly through predation effects, with cyclic trends more prominent among younger year classes.

**Productivity comparison.** Deep Lake's standing crop of 38.0 pounds per acre is less than half the average of [[Walsh Lake]], [[Burke Lake]] and [[Third Sister Lake]] (79.7 pounds per acre), even though these lakes share a similar latitude. The authors conclude that regional location is a poor index of productivity. Deep Lake is comparable to several northern Michigan lakes (21 to 194 pounds per acre) and to Nova Scotian lakes (17.0 to 36.0 pounds per acre), but lower than three northern Wisconsin lakes (92 to 186 pounds per acre). Five Florida lakes ranged from 22 to 110 pounds per acre. Table 9 of the source lists 17 lakes in total.
