# Middle Gila River Aquatic Study (November 1982)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report from the Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Reclamation and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service presents the November 1982 aquatic monitoring survey of the [[Middle Gila River]] in Arizona. The river was long described as nearly devoid of aquatic life, and earlier 1976 surveys lacked quantitative effort. The 1982 survey sampled 12 stations over 78 stream-kilometers (48.5 miles) using benthic invertebrate sampling, water chemistry, discharge measurement and fish seining. Water quality met state and federal standards for protected uses, and the authors conclude that water chemistry no longer appears to be a limiting factor. Fish and invertebrate communities remain depauperate, which the authors attribute mainly to high, sediment-laden flows and habitat homogeneity.

## Background & Description

The [[Middle Gila River]] was described in the early 1970s as depauperate of vertebrates and invertebrates. Minckley (1972) listed two native fishes, [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarkii*), plus 22 non-native species known or suspected to occur, but without abundance data. Stephenson and Ohmart (1974) described the river as virtually devoid of aquatic life and heavily polluted by upstream industrial waste.

A 1976 interagency survey by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation sampled the river in May and August by raft, using gill nets, electrofishing, fyke nets, minnow traps, seines and drop nets. Because effort was not quantified, the 1976 standing crop cannot be compared with later data. Three later surveys were conducted in October 1981, February 1982 and November 1982. This report presents the November 1982 work.

Sampling methods included:
- **Water quality:** a Hydrolab 4041 for temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and specific conductance; a Bausch and Lomb Mini-20 spectrophotometer for orthophosphate, nitrate-nitrogen, alkalinity and hardness; laboratory analysis for sulfate and trace metals.
- **Discharge:** transects at each station and a Marsh-McBirney Model 201 current meter.
- **Benthic invertebrates:** three [[Surber Sampler]] samples per station (500 µm mesh), preserved in 50% isopropanol, sorted, identified to family or lower, and wet-weighed.
- **Diversity:** the [[Shannon-Weaver Diversity Index]] (H'), with each identification level treated as one taxon.
- **Fish:** a 3.1 × 1.2 m (3.2 mm mesh) seine, plus a kick seine for riffles. Specific conductance above about 2000 µmhos/cm precluded electrofishing. Specimens were measured to the nearest millimeter (total length) and weighed to the nearest gram, and retained as museum vouchers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Water was within federal and state standards for wildlife, fisheries, recreation, domestic and agricultural uses. Dissolved oxygen was generally high and pH consistently alkaline. Waters were very hard (>100 mg/l as CaCO₃), largely from calcium, and hardness increased downstream. Nitrate-nitrogen decreased downstream, presumably through [[Periphyton]] uptake. Sulfate and suspended sediment were strongly influenced by the [[San Pedro River]], whose mouth carried roughly five times more suspended sediment than the Gila above the confluence. Trace metals (copper, iron, lead, manganese, zinc) were within criteria and sublethal to aquatic biota, despite a history of heavy metal pollution at [[Mineral Creek]] (Rathbun 1975).

**Hydrology.** [[Coolidge Dam]] (San Carlos Reservoir) has regulated flow above the San Pedro confluence since 15 November 1928. Mean flow below Winkelman during the study was 25.1 ± 1.9 cfs. Nearly all flow reaching the [[Ashurst-Hayden Dam]] is diverted for the San Carlos Irrigation System, except during major floods. The San Pedro River supplies most unregulated inflow.

**Habitat.** High flows and sediment loads flatten the riffle-run-pool heterogeneity that characterizes high-quality habitat. Under normal releases the river averages a much higher discharge (USGS 1979), and USBR (1976) classified about 95% of the river as homogeneous run habitat. Sediment effects include reduced photosynthesis from turbidity, scouring and abrasion that prevent benthic organisms from establishing, and fine sediment burial that homogenizes the substrate.

## Key Findings

**Benthic macroinvertebrates**
- A total of 17 taxa in 10 orders was collected. [[Baetidae]] mayfly nymphs were the most abundant.
- Combined quantitative samples (n = 27) averaged 464.4 ± 626.8 individuals/m² and 3.6 ± 5.9 g/m². The authors describe this as indicative of a depauperate fauna. For comparison, Bruns (1977) reported 12.2 individuals/m² and 6.9 g/m² in nearby Arivaca Creek. (The source text gives "Aravalpa Creek", which is likely an OCR error; the name as written is preserved here.)
- Standing crop was highest at the San Pedro River, while biomass was greatest in the Gila mainstem. [[Mineral Creek]] had the lowest density, biomass and taxa richness of all stations.
- Diversity was lower in tributaries than in the mainstem. H' ranged from 0.347 at the San Pedro mouth (Station 7) to 2.017 below Riverside (Station 6). Mineral Creek had H' = 0.628.
- Baetid mayflies made up 92.8% of the San Pedro fauna, and black flies (Simuliidae) made up 81.2% of the Mineral Creek samples. The authors attribute low diversity in both tributaries to physically stressed conditions (unstable substrate), chemical pollution, or lack of habitat diversity such as cobble and rubble bottoms.

**Fishes**
- The 1976 survey collected 8 species: [[Carp]], Longfin Dace, Desert Sucker, [[Channel Catfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Green Sunflish]] and [[Mosquitofish]].
- Surveys from 1981 to 1982 recorded 10 species, adding [[Sonora Sucker]] and [[Fathead Minnow]]. The authors attribute the difference to greater sampling effort rather than new introductions.
- A review of museum records (Table 7) lists 12 species from the middle Gila since 1976, including [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Threadfin Shad]].
- In November 1982, 106 fish were collected in 44 seine hauls, averaging 2.4 fish per haul. Eleven riffle kick samples yielded 34 fish, averaging 3.1 per sample. A Dripping Spring Wash seine haul netted 91 fish, far above the river average.
- Mosquitofish, Longfin Dace and Desert Sucker were the most abundant species in November 1982. In 1976, the most abundant were Desert Sucker, Channel Catfish, Longfin Dace and Carp, in that order. The authors note that the change may reflect a real community shift or a sampling artifact. Fyke nets captured the most fish in 1976, and the larger seine mesh was ineffective for dace, fathead minnows and juvenile fish.
- Mean measurements of November 1982 specimens included Mosquitofish (n = 91) and Longfin Dace (n = 16). Full size data appear in Table 9.
- Because fish density was too low for depletion sampling at most sites, the authors standardized seine length and recorded haul and riffle counts for future comparison.

**Summary and conclusions**
- The authors conclude that this section of the Gila River has recovered from the water chemistry problems reported in 1972, and that water chemistry no longer appears to be a limiting factor.
- Fish populations remain very low. The probable causes are continuous high flows with suspended sediment, which suppress standing crop and biomass, and reduced habitat heterogeneity. Low releases such as the 25 cfs during November 1982 create better riffle-run-pool ratios.
- Recommendations include recording fish length in future surveys and standardizing seine hauls and kick samples for comparability.
