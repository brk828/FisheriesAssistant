# Endangered Suckers and Squawfish of the American West: Resolutions, Surveys and Larval Ecology (1986 Compilation)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This scanned compilation (OCR text, with some English and Spanish versions) gathers five items on endangered western North American freshwater fishes, mainly from the 1980s. It contains a 1986 resolution from the [[Desert Fishes Council]] calling for [[Endangered Species Act]] protection for the [[Shortnose Sucker]] and [[Lost River Sucker]] of the Klamath Basin. It also includes a proposal to create a sport fishery for the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] in [[Kenney Reservoir]], a 1986 fish survey of [[Cataract Canyon]] on the Colorado River, a laboratory study of food availability and starvation in larval [[Razorback Sucker]], and a study of stocked juvenile Colorado Squawfish dispersal. The unifying theme is that native big-river fishes are declining under dams, competition from non-native species, and habitat change, and that managers need better life-history data to recover them.

## Background & Description

**Klamath Basin suckers (Resolution, December 1986).** The [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*Chasmistes brevirostris*) and [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*) occur in the Klamath Basin of southern Oregon and north-central California. The resolution states that both species had declined drastically, that even the largest reproducing populations in Upper Klamath Lake were in jeopardy, and that the species had long been valued as sport fish and as a dietary staple of the Klamath Tribes. It was passed without dissent and copied to federal, state and tribal officials.

**Colorado Squawfish sport fishery proposal.** The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is a large, endangered cyprinid of the Colorado River system. The author, Patrick J. Martinez of the Colorado Division of Wildlife, discusses a controversial idea first proposed after the species was listed. [[Kenney Reservoir]] was completed in 1984 on the Gunnison River and was stocked with non-native sport fish, which are suspected of contributing to the decline of endangered fishes. The proposal's target is a squawfish of about 450 mm (18 in) to provide a trophy fishery.

**Cataract Canyon survey (1986).** Fish were sampled in [[Cataract Canyon]] of the Colorado River above and below the confluence with the Green River. The work covered six eight-day trips between July 11 and October 6, 1986. Two study reaches were used, one in a 16-mile reach near the confluence and one upstream near Canyonlands National Park.

**Larval razorback sucker study.** Diana Papoulias (Arizona State University) examined whether starvation of newly hatched [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) limits recruitment. The work combined field observations from [[Lake Mohave]] with laboratory feeding trials at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Dexter National Fish Hatchery, New Mexico.

**Squawfish stocking and dispersal study.** Dean A. Hendrickson (Arizona State University) and James Brooks (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Dexter) released hatchery-raised juvenile Colorado Squawfish (roughly 10–100 mm TL) into the Verde River, Arizona, in early October (year garbled in scan). Fish were marked and held in raceways with or without current before release, then monitored downstream with hoop nets, gill nets and trammel nets.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow and habitat:** During the Cataract Canyon survey, river flows varied from roughly 20,000 to nearly 100,000 cfs, with high turbidity. The authors say this hindered sampling efforts.
- **Nursery use:** The presence of young squawfish suggests that fish drift into Cataract Canyon from upstream and use it as a nursery area. Backwaters and eddies were the sites where young fish were found in large, isolated groups.
- **Non-native fishes:** Non-native species dominated the Cataract Canyon catch. The survey recorded 23 species, 15 non-native and 7 native. Hybridization and competition with non-native fishes were concerns in several documents.
- **Hatchery genetics:** The Colorado Squawfish proposal warned that mixing genetic stocks from different river basins could harm wild populations, so fish for any sport fishery should come from the same basin.
- **Thermal cues:** Hatching in the 1986 Cataract Canyon sample was delayed because suitable spawning temperatures (about 20–21 °C) did not occur until late in the season. Hatching dates were inferred from otolith or length-based back-calculation.
- **Larval food web:** In Lake Mohave, zooplankton density appeared low at the time razorback hatching, and stomachs of captured larvae were mostly empty.

## Key Findings

**Klamath Basin resolution**
- Shortnose and Lost River suckers had declined drastically, and even the largest populations in Upper Klamath Lake were in jeopardy.
- The Desert Fishes Council urged immediate Endangered Species Act protection and federal funding for research into the causes of decline.

**Colorado Squawfish sport fishery proposal**
- A sport fishery was proposed as a management alternative when other options seemed unavailable. The author notes that migratory species such as squawfish may not remain in a reservoir.
- Stocking would use squawfish from the same basin to minimize genetic and biological risk. Public education and fishing-regulation enforcement were also recommended to prevent illegal take.

**Cataract Canyon survey (1986)**
- The survey recorded 23 species: 15 non-native and 7 native. Non-natives made up 82.5% of the catch and natives 17.5%.
- Endangered species made up about 4.5% of the total catch. Bonytail and Roundtail Chub were not captured.
- A total of 505 Colorado Squawfish was captured, including 455 young-of-the-year (YOY) and 5 adults. Most of the adults had been tagged in the Green River about 3 years earlier, and most of the YOY came from the 1985 cohort.
- A total of 33 [[Humpback Chub]] was captured, including 21 YOY. Many small chub could not be reliably identified and were classified as "possible Gila cypha".
- Hatching of YOY squawfish in 1986 occurred between mid-summer and September 9, with most hatching in late July to August. Hatching was estimated for each YOY fish using a length-based formula.
- The authors described the 1986 results as preliminary. They planned further years of study to determine the status of squawfish recovery.

**Larval razorback sucker study**
- Mean total length of larval razorback sucker captured in Lake Mohave was 10.6 mm, with empty stomachs and low zooplankton density at hatching. No recruitment to the adult population had been detected.
- Experiments were held at 18 °C. Larvae first fed at 25 or 29 days post-hatch had significantly higher mortality than those first fed at 5, 9, 13 or 17 days.
- At 10 nauplii per liter per 8-hour period, mortality exceeded 70%. At 50 nauplii per liter per period, mortality was below 30%.
- The point of irreversible starvation was between 17 and 21 days post-hatch, and the critical feeding period was between 7 and 17 days post-hatch.

**Squawfish stocking and dispersal study**
- Hatchery-reared juveniles were split into two groups: one held in a raceway with current and the other held in a raceway without current. Fish from the current group were slightly smaller and weighed less at a given length.
- Movement after release was mostly crepuscular (dusk and dawn), with the greatest activity between about 0600 and 0900 hours.
- Most fish from both groups moved downstream past the last hoop net, about 2 km below the release site. Few fish were captured 100 m above the release site, and only one was captured 400 m above it.
- Predators were collected, and stomachs were frozen for analysis. Observations suggested that native predators on squawfish were minor. Fin-marking was judged effective for identifying fish from each treatment group.
