# Revised Report on Fish Toxicity Testing Procedures

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-toxicity-testing

## Executive Summary

This FAO publication (EIFAC Technical Paper, Revision 1, 1983) presents standardized guidance for fish toxicity testing, prepared by the [[European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission]] (EIFAC) Working Party on Toxicity Testing Procedures. The main report was drafted in 1975 and complemented in 1982 by Appendix III on [[Microcosms]] (model ecosystems). The report aims to standardize international toxicity testing with fish and to clarify which test type suits which pollution situation. It classifies pollution scenarios into five groups and defines the corresponding test types: [[Screening Test]], [[Tests to Establish Water Quality Criteria]], [[Effluent Monitoring Test]], [[Legal Test]] and [[River Monitoring Test]].

## Background & Description

The report notes major advances over the previous 25 years in measuring pollution effects on fish, including the influence of chemical and physical variables on toxicity and statistical treatment of results. Earlier standard acute tests (such as the U.S. and U.K. methods) were satisfactory for limited purposes but were often stretched beyond their scope. The report criticizes the assumption that "safe concentrations" equal 10% of a 48 or 96 h [[LC50]], noting this is not a general rule.

The Working Party was formed after the 1970 EIFAC Symposium in Jablonna, Poland, and a 1972 Karlsruhe meeting, with the principle that standardization of fish toxicity procedures should precede standardization of terminology. Members included R. Lloyd (UK, Convener), J.S. Alabaster (UK), K. Besch (Germany), D. Calamari (Italy), M. Grande (Norway), T.B. Hasselrot (Sweden), G. Leynaud (France), A. Lysak (Poland) and J.B. Sprague (Canada). The FAO secretariat included J.L. Gaudet and H. Naeve.

The report contains three appendices:
- **Appendix I:** Standard test techniques (APHA/AWWA/WPCF, UK Ministry of Agriculture, Japanese agricultural chemicals method, ASTM, Polish Institute of Water Economy, Swiss official test, and an Italian method).
- **Appendix II:** Terminology used in fish toxicity testing (poison, toxicant, bioassay, LC50, threshold concentration, synergism, antagonism, application factor and related terms).
- **Appendix III:** The use of microcosms in hazard assessment, with a bibliography of roughly 150 references.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report stresses that laboratory toxicity results must be related to survival of fish populations in natural ecosystems. Key environmental modifiers include:
- **Water hardness, pH, temperature, salinity and dissolved oxygen**, which can alter toxicity. Control limits recommended include pH ±0.1 unit and temperature ±1 °C.
- **Dissociating compounds** such as [[Ammonia]], whose toxicity depends on pH, bicarbonate and free carbon dioxide.
- **Persistent, bioaccumulating substances** (for example [[Mercury]], [[Cadmium]] and [[Organochlorine Compounds]]), for which tissue residue levels and depuration rates are more relevant than LC50 values.
- **Tainting** of fish flesh by phenolic compounds at sub-lethal concentrations.
- **Accidental discharges**, which are isolated and diluted, versus chronic inputs from industry, agriculture and domestic sources, which may persist for long periods.

Field approaches include surveys of fisheries at different pollution levels, caged fish experiments, and artificial ecosystems such as controlled streams and ponds.

## Key Findings

**Pollution scenario classification and matching test types**
1. Accidental discharges require risk information to minimize occurrence.
2. Normal use or misuse may be assessed with a [[Screening Test]]. If the likely maximum environmental concentration is roughly 10^-4 times (or less) the acutely lethal level, the chemical is probably not a pollutional problem. The report notes the safety factor is only an approximate guide.
3. Where environmental levels exceed about 10^-4 of the acute lethal level (persistent or industrial substances), further tests are needed to set water quality criteria.
4. Effluents with hard-to-analyse toxicants need an effluent monitoring test.
5. Legally enforceable standards need a reproducible legal test.
6. Sudden river pollution calls for continuous river monitoring with sentinel fish.

**Screening test (Section 2)**
- Continuous-flow apparatus is preferred, supplying at least six test vessels, with dissolved oxygen above 70% of air saturation in outflowing water.
- Replacement rate of 2–3 litres of water per gram of fish per day is generally sufficient.
- Dilution water hardness should be 75–125 mg/L CaCO3 with pH 7.0–7.5. Temperature should be optimum for the species, controlled to ±1 °C.
- Seven to ten fish per dilution, at least five dilutions at approximately logarithmic spacing, and a control. Control mortality of more than one fish invalidates the test.
- Fish should be held for at least seven days before testing, and discarded if more than 10% of a batch is diseased or dies.
- Observations at 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 and 96 h, with LC50 calculated by probit analysis and 95% confidence limits reported.

**Tests to establish water quality criteria (Section 3)**
- Threshold LC50 is the concentration at which the log concentration–log survival time curve becomes asymptotic to the time axis.
- Sub-lethal tests cover biochemical, histological, physiological, growth, life cycle, behaviour and activity endpoints. No single test is sufficient to set criteria.
- The life cycle test (over a year or longer) is judged the most informative, though it is limited by species use and time and space needs.
- Increased activity raises fish sensitivity only slightly in threshold LC50 terms.

**Effluent monitoring test (Section 4)**
- Uses simple tests performed by unskilled personnel, either diluting effluent with receiving water in aquaria or running serial dilutions in static tests with LC50 at 24 or 48 h.
- Species need not be standardized; the most sensitive common local species is preferred.

**Legal test (Section 5)**
- Example standard: no more than five of ten fish may die when effluent is diluted X times, over a set period.
- Uses static tests with ten test fish and ten controls, continuing for at least 24 h and preferably 48 h.
- Canadian proposal for pulp and paper effluents: 50 rainbow trout, batches of ten, 65% effluent at 15 °C, 96 h continuous-flow test, with failure defined by statistically significant excess mortality relative to controls.

**River monitoring test (Section 6)**
- Sensitive responses are ranked as follows: death (least sensitive), inability to swim (most commonly used and judged superior), altered behaviour pattern, and altered respiratory frequency (possibly most sensitive, based on opercular nerve impulses).
- Inability-to-swim systems use electrical, mechanical or light screens, with optical or sonar detection. An alarm is triggered when several fish fail simultaneously.
- Recommended holding times are 3–4 weeks if fed, or 3–5 days if starved.

**Microcosms (Appendix III)**
- Microcosms include all systems with two or more trophic levels, from laboratory compartments to natural enclosures and artificial streams.
- Increasing complexity raises environmental realism but reduces reproducibility.
- Objectives cover chemical fate (degradation, partitioning, bioavailability), effects on biota, and secondary effects on populations.
- Example: an experimental pond of about 200 m² may be partitioned for plankton studies, but fish inclusion requires the whole pond for one concentration.
- Chemical analysis and monitoring of dissolved oxygen, temperature and pH must accompany all microcosm work.

**Overall conclusion**
The report calls for a comparative testing programme for river monitoring techniques and concludes that no general recommendations on microcosm types can be made because each test must be designed around specific objectives.
