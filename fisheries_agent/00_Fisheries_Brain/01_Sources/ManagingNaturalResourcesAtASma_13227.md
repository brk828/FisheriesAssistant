## Executive Summary
This account, authored by TONT Superintendent Lee Baiza, describes resource-management challenges at [[Tonto National Monument]], a small cultural park unit within the [[Sonoran Desert]] (Arizona Upland subdivision). While the monument's mandate centers on preserving Salado Culture cliff dwellings (c. 1150–1400 A.D.), it also protects a significant tract of desert riparian habitat associated with watered canyons. No fish species, fish surveys, or aquatic community data are reported in this text. Key management concerns include the reconstruction of [[Roosevelt Dam]] and consequent rise in [[Roosevelt Lake]] water levels (15–22 ft), historic grazing legacy, wildland fire recovery, and the need for integrated biological inventory and monitoring programs — all of which have downstream implications for riparian and potentially aquatic ecosystem integrity.

## Background & Description
- **Site**: Tonto National Monument, established 1907, expanded 1937; 1,120 acres.
- **Primary purpose**: Preservation of cliff dwellings and cultural resources of the [[Salado Culture]].
- **Secondary ecological value**: Representative sample of Arizona Upland [[Sonoran Desert]] habitat, including watered canyons and associated riparian plant/animal communities.
- **Land-use history**: Grazing permitted until 1975; monument has experienced 11 wildland fires in the preceding 25 years.
- **Adjacent hydrological feature**: [[Roosevelt Lake]], impounded in 1911 via [[Roosevelt Dam]], borders the monument; dam reconstruction is raising lake levels 15–22 ft, directly affecting shoreline and riparian zones adjacent to monument boundaries.
- **Research collaboration**: Joint inventory and monitoring initiative between TONT staff, National Biological Survey (NBS) units, University of Arizona (UA), and Northern Arizona University (NAU), including wildlife studies (e.g., ringtail research by Bill Shaw, UA School of Renewable Natural Resources).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type**: Desert upland ecosystem with watered canyons supporting riparian vegetation — a rare and ecologically significant feature within the broader Sonoran Desert matrix.
- **Anthropogenic pressures**:
  - Rising reservoir levels from Roosevelt Dam reconstruction altering shoreline/riparian habitat adjacent to the monument.
  - Relocation and development of US Forest Service recreation facilities around the expanded lake, increasing visitor pressure.
  - Urban and mining development in surrounding metropolitan areas, with associated increases in visitation.
  - Historic grazing (pre-1975) likely altered vegetation composition and, by extension, associated faunal communities.
  - Elimination of grazing and lake impoundment (since 1911) are cited as drivers of undocumented ecosystem change.
- **Fire regime**: 11 wildland fires over 25 years, with ongoing ecological recovery — relevant to watershed and riparian corridor health, which can influence downstream aquatic habitat quality.
- **Data gap**: The park explicitly lacks baseline inventory information for biological resources, hindering assessment of potential historical reductions in species diversity and population densities across taxa — implicitly including any aquatic or riparian-dependent fauna, though none are named.

## Key Findings
- **No fish species were identified, surveyed, or discussed in this source.**
- The monument's management team identifies a critical need for **comprehensive biological inventory and long-term monitoring plots**, developed collaboratively with NBS, UA, and NAU — a framework that could eventually encompass aquatic/riparian fauna surveys if canyon watercourses support fish or other aquatic life.
- A recurring institutional challenge is the **communication gap between technical researchers and park management staff**, with scientific findings often left unused or misunderstood due to specialized terminology — a barrier to translating any future aquatic ecology research into actionable management practice.
- **Roosevelt Dam reconstruction** and associated lake-level rise represent the most significant hydrological perturbation affecting habitat adjacent to the monument, though impacts on fish or aquatic communities are not addressed in this text.
- The park's approach emphasizes building foundational baseline data before specific monitoring protocols (potentially including aquatic components) can be effectively implemented.
