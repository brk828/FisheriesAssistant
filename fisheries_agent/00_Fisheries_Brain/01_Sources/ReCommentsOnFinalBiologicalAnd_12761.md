# Comments on Final Biological Opinion for Lower Colorado River Operations and Endangered Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-policy #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #ESA

## Executive Summary

This document comprises formal comments from Defenders of Wildlife on the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Final Biological and Conference Opinion regarding Lower Colorado River operations and maintenance (Lake Mead to Southerly International Boundary). The organization critiques the consultation as severely limited in geographic, temporal, and taxonomic scope, arguing it fails to address the full range of impacts from [[Bureau of Reclamation]] dam operations on endangered and threatened species. The document provides notice of intent to sue under Section 11(g) of the [[Endangered Species Act]] for violations including inadequate consultation, unauthorized take, adverse modification of critical habitat, and failure to take affirmative conservation actions. Key concerns include exclusion of species south of the U.S.-Mexico border (particularly in the [[Colorado River Delta]] and [[Sea of Cortez]]), inadequate protection for native fish species, and insufficient measures for the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]].

## Background & Description

The [[Lower Colorado River]] system has been dramatically altered by dam construction and water management operations conducted by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. These operations serve multiple purposes: flood control, river regulation, navigation, water storage, water delivery to over 30 million people, and hydroelectric power generation. The river historically carved the [[Grand Canyon]] and supported an extraordinary array of endemic fish species adapted to harsh conditions of dramatically changing temperatures and flows. It carried and deposited massive sediment loads—exceeding those of the Mississippi River—creating extensive marsh complexes at its mouth in the [[Sea of Cortez]].

The consultation area extends 412 river miles from Pierce Ferry (upper end of [[Lake Mead]]) to the Southerly International Boundary with Mexico, covering the mainstream and 100-year floodplain. The temporal scope is limited to five years. Species formally addressed in the Biological Opinion include: [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] (*Rallus longirostris yumanensis*), [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*), and [[Flat-tailed Horned Lizard]].

Additional endangered species acknowledged but excluded from formal consultation include: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Brown Pelican]] (*Pelecanus occidentalis*), [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*), [[Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus*), [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), [[Totoaba]] (*Totoaba macdonaldi*), and [[Vaquita]] (*Phocoena sinus*). More than two dozen candidate species are also affected.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Colorado River]] ecosystem has experienced catastrophic degradation. Dam operations have disrupted natural hydrology and severely impaired ecological functions of adjacent riparian habitat. Many native species face extinction or are likely to become endangered. The river's water is over-allocated before accounting for ecological needs, wildlife requirements, or recreational uses.

The [[Colorado River Delta]] and associated wetlands historically supported diverse wildlife assemblages. The delta is being degraded by loss of Colorado River water flow and dramatic decreases in sediment deposition caused by dam operations, compounded by conversion of wetlands to agricultural use. Entire indigenous cultures in the delta region are being lost due to environmental changes.

Southwest riparian ecosystems rank among the most endangered ecosystems on Earth (Noss and Peters 1995). Habitat loss for species like the [[Flat-tailed Horned Lizard]] is primarily driven by conversion of desert to agriculture or urban uses—development made possible only through damming and water delivery by [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. Recent analysis indicates 48.6% of the lizard's entire historic habitat has been lost: 31.1% in Arizona, 50.2% in Imperial Valley California, and 58% in Coachella Valley California.

For the [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], half the population occurs in the consultation action area while the other half resides further south on the Colorado or in the delta. Wetlands south of the project area are declining due to loss of river water.

## Key Findings

**Legal and Procedural Deficiencies:**

The consultation is characterized as "consultation-lite"—extremely limited in geographic and temporal scope, species coverage, and agency actions addressed. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] improperly claimed lack of discretion over many management aspects, confusing the existence of a statutory management scheme with the narrow exemption for purely ministerial acts. The consultation illegally relied on a future Multi-Species Conservation Program (MSCP) to fulfill obligations rather than addressing immediate requirements.

**Geographic Scope Violations:**

Operations dramatically impact the [[Colorado River Delta]], associated wetlands, [[Sea of Cortez]], and numerous species south of the Southerly International Boundary. Limiting consultation scope to north of the U.S.-Mexico border was arbitrary and capricious. Two species covered by the Biological Opinion ([[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] and [[Yuma Clapper Rail]]) also occur south of the border. [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] improperly excused consultation on [[Totoaba]] despite acknowledged impacts.

**Species-Specific Concerns:**

*Native Fish:* The Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) is deficient for [[Bonytail Chub]], lacking minimum population requirements and assuming reintroduction must wait for [[Razorback Sucker]] program completion. No timeline exists for developing native fish habitat impoundments. The RPA uses provisional language ("feasible and within Reclamation's discretion") contradicting the concept of reasonable and prudent alternatives. Operations adversely modify designated critical habitat for [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], with few options to correct deficiencies in constituent elements. The Biological Opinion inadequately addresses severe problems with non-native fish.

*Southwestern Willow Flycatcher:* The Biological Opinion finds the species beyond the jeopardy threshold yet allows substantial direct take and habitat destruction, relying entirely on anticipated protection of other habitat. For the 1997 nesting season on the [[Lake Mead]] delta, only three nests and two eggs were found (dramatically lower than previous year). Failure to maintain water levels similar to the previous successful nesting season demonstrates inability or lack of political will for species conservation. Protective management strategies must be developed by March 15 annually, but this timeline is inadequate given that breeding occurs June through early August and long-term operational planning cycles.

*Flat-tailed Horned Lizard:* Consultation was limited to maintenance activities within the "5-Mile Zone" of wellfield developments along the U.S./Mexico border in Arizona. This excluded the primary threat: habitat conversion to agriculture and urban development enabled by water delivery. The life history and environmental baseline descriptions were artificially limited to Arizona despite 65% of total range occurring in California. Effects analysis was restricted to direct mortality from vehicular traffic rather than addressing habitat loss.

**Continuing Violations:**

Until consultation is complete, [[Bureau of Reclamation]] may not make irreversible or irretrievable commitments of resources (16 U.S.C. § 1536(d)), such as drowning [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] nests in [[Lake Mead]] or constructing/repairing levees in [[Razorback Sucker]] or [[Bonytail Chub]] critical habitat. The organization provides formal notice of intent to sue for failure to consult on full range of activities affecting listed species, unauthorized take, adverse modification of critical habitat, and failure to take affirmative conservation actions.

**Restoration Requirements:**

Adequate recovery will require: (1) substantial financial investment, (2) restoration of significant land areas to cottonwood/willow forests or wetlands/backwater fish refugia, and (3) water allocation for ecological purposes. A primary conservation goal is restoration of yearly spring flows to the river delta and [[Sea of Cortez]] to rejuvenate the biologically rich but seriously degraded system.
