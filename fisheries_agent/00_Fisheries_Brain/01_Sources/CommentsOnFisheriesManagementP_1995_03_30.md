## Executive Summary

This document is a formal comment letter from the Central Arizona Water Conservation District (CAWCD) addressed to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS), responding to a February 1995 preliminary draft "Fisheries Management Prospectus" for four indigenous "big-river" fishes of the Lower [[Colorado River]] Basin: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. Rather than presenting primary biological or ecological data, the letter raises legal, regulatory, and economic objections to the Draft's proposed habitat restoration measures, reintroduction strategies, and a proposed reclassification of experimental Colorado Squawfish populations in the Salt and Verde Rivers from "nonessential" to "essential." The letter is valuable primarily as a historical/regulatory record documenting species status designations, hatchery propagation infrastructure, and population viability assessments made by FWS in the 1980s.

## Background & Description

The source text contains **no morphological, anatomical, or physiological data** on any of the four fish species. As a legal/regulatory comment letter, it does not describe body form, coloration, size, fin structure, or other taxonomic characteristics of [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], or [[Humpback Chub]]. Readers seeking morphological detail should consult primary ichthyological literature on Cyprinidae (squawfish, bonytail, chub) and Catostomidae (razorback sucker).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** All four species are described as "big-river" fishes native to large, free-flowing riverine systems in the arid Southwest — specifically the mainstem [[Colorado River]] and major tributaries: the Salt River, Verde River, Gila River, and Bill Williams River.
- **Habitat dependency:** The Draft prospectus explicitly links species recovery to "restoration of free flowing river reaches," implying that these fishes are ecologically associated with unimpounded, naturally hydrologically dynamic river segments rather than regulated/impounded reservoir systems.
- **Range status:** Colorado Squawfish populations were noted as still viable "in several areas of the upper basin" as of the mid-1980s, indicating a historic upper-basin vs. lower-basin population structure with the lower basin populations largely extirpated or reintroduced.
- **Reintroduction geography:** Colorado Squawfish were experimentally reintroduced into the Salt and Verde Rivers, areas outside the species' designated critical habitat, under an "experimental, nonessential" population designation.
- **Critical habitat:** The Draft raises the possibility of expanding designated critical habitat for Colorado Squawfish to include portions of the Salt and Verde Rivers.
- **Hatchery/captive infrastructure:** [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] is identified as maintaining a sufficient brood stock of Colorado Squawfish to produce fry for reintroduction efforts, indicating an active ex-situ conservation/propagation program supporting wild population augmentation.

## Key Findings

- **Regulatory history:** In 1985, FWS explicitly rejected classifying Salt/Verde River Colorado Squawfish populations as "experimental, essential," determining that species survival was not dependent on these reintroduced populations (50 Fed. Reg. 30188, 30190; 49 Fed. Reg. 14149, 14150).
- **Basis for nonessential designation (1984–85):** FWS cited three factors supporting nonessential status: (1) Colorado Squawfish remained viable in multiple upper-basin locations; (2) Dexter National Fish Hatchery held adequate brood stock for fry production; (3) propagation and rearing techniques for the species were already established and operational.
- **Threshold for reclassification:** According to FWS's own 1985 statement, reclassification from "nonessential" to "essential" would only be warranted by "the loss of all or a significant portion of the wild populations" — a condition the letter's authors state had not occurred as of 1995.
- **Procedural requirement:** Any reclassification to "essential" status would require formal rulemaking, not merely administrative reassessment via a management prospectus.
- **Stakeholder/economic concerns raised:**
  - Ambiguity over what "restoration of free flowing river reaches" entails and whether it requires dismantling dams/diversion structures or mandating specific water releases.
  - Concern regarding unresolved water rights for any mandated environmental flow releases.
  - Precedent of high costs from similar environmental studies conducted for [[Glen Canyon Dam]], flagged as a cost concern if replicated for lower Colorado River dams.
  - Request that economic impacts on existing water projects be evaluated before species reintroduction into new (non-critical-habitat) areas.
  - Recommendation that all reintroductions outside critical habitat retain "experimental, nonessential" status to shield existing water infrastructure projects from jeopardy findings.
- **Scope concern:** Although framed as addressing only the lower Colorado River, the Draft's proposals for the Salt, Gila, and Verde Rivers were noted as having basin-wide implications for central Arizona water users, prompting a request for an additional stakeholder meeting in central Arizona (beyond those planned for Yuma, Lake Havasu City, or Boulder City).
