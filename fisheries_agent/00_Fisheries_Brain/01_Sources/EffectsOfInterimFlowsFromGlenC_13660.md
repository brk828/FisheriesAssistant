## Executive Summary

This is Quarterly Report No. 8 (Trip No. 8, March 30–April 11, 1994) from the Hualapai Aquatic Resources Study, documenting effects of interim flows from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] on aquatic resources of the lower [[Colorado River]] between Diamond Creek (RM 226) and Pearce Ferry/Lake Mead (RM 280). The study employed multi-gear fish sampling (trammel nets, gill nets, boat and backpack electrofishing, minnow traps, seines) across the mainstem, [[Lake Mead]] inflow, and key tributaries — [[Spencer Creek]], [[Surprise Creek]], [[Diamond Creek]], [[Lost Creek]], [[Quartermaster Creek]], and [[Travertine Falls Creek]]. A total of 1,746 fish across 16 species were captured. No [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) or [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) were observed, though notable rare captures included a [[Golden Shiner]] and a second-ever [[Bluehead Sucker]] specimen for this investigation. The report highlights a suspected natural migration barrier limiting non-native fish incursion into upper Spencer Creek, poor body condition in [[Striped Bass]], and tributary-driven fish aggregation patterns.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Mainstem Colorado River (Diamond Creek to Lake Mead):**
- Water temperature: 12.1–15.5°C.
- Turbidity high throughout trip; Secchi depths 0.1–0.4 m.
- Near [[Scorpion Island]]: beach water 14.6°C vs. Scorpion Bay 18.5°C; Secchi depth 0.3 m (island) vs. 0.6 m (bay), indicating localized thermal/turbidity stratification from Lake Mead backwater influence.
- Mainstem catch rates were comparatively low relative to tributaries.

**Tributary habitats:**
- **[[Spencer Creek]]**: Highest fish densities of the trip. Diurnal water temperature ranged 14.8–25.3°C. Three multiple-pass sampling sites (Site 1 near confluence, Site 2 ~0.75 mi upstream, Site 3 ~1.5 mi upstream) showed a gradient from riffle-dominated (Site 1) to greater pool habitat (Sites 2–3). A constricted, high-gradient reach above Site 3 appears to act as a **migration barrier for non-native fish**, with only [[Speckled Dace]] captured above this point. Channel stability has increased markedly since the 1993 flood disturbance, with expanding *Cladophora* algal mats and associated invertebrate production.
- **[[Surprise Creek]]**: Water temperature reached 20.2°C (max) and 14.2°C (min). Fish abundance dropped substantially relative to December 1993, likely due to intervening flash-flood scouring of sand/silt substrate. Fathead minnows were actively spawning here.
- **[[Quartermaster Creek]]**: Least diurnal temperature variation (20.6–21.7°C); minnow trapping yielded no fish.
- **[[Diamond Creek]]**: Seining produced only speckled dace and one plains killifish, suggesting minimal non-native colonization from the mainstem despite apparent accessibility.
- **[[Lost Creek]]**: Water levels low; approximately half the slough was dry, with cattail encroachment on previously inundated habitat.
- Non-native species (carp, striped bass) were observed **staging at tributary mouths** (notably Spencer Creek), apparently blocked by shallow riffles ~30 m upstream of confluences — suggesting flow-dependent access to tributary refugia.

**Trophic/behavioral notes:**
- Fish (particularly non-natives) appear to move into tributaries seeking warmer water and higher food availability relative to the thermally-regulated mainstem (a hallmark effect of dam-regulated flows).
- Striped bass stomach content analysis was pending, but empty stomachs and poor condition point to reduced mainstem foraging success.

## Key Findings

1. **1,746 total fish** captured across 16 species during Trip 8, dominated by [[Red Shiner]] (978), [[Speckled Dace]] (363), and [[Fathead Minnow]] (252).
2. No endangered **[[Humpback Chub]]** or **[[Razorback Sucker]]** were captured or observed during this trip.
3. First-ever **[[Golden Shiner]]** captured in the study (mainstem, RM 249.0) and only the **second-ever [[Bluehead Sucker]]** specimen recorded (RM 230.0).
4. A **high-gradient, constricted reach in upper Spencer Creek (~1.5 mi upstream)** functions as a likely barrier preventing upstream spread of non-native fish, with only speckled dace found above it.
5. **[[Largemouth Bass]]** were documented in Spencer Creek for the first time since multiple-pass sampling began.
6. Fish abundance in **Surprise Creek declined sharply** versus December 1993, attributed to intervening flash-flood scouring and habitat disturbance.
7. **Striped bass and carp aggregated at tributary mouths** (e.g., Spencer Creek), with shallow riffles apparently limiting upstream access — implying that flow modifications from Glen Canyon Dam operations could alter non-native fish access to tributary refugia.
8. Striped bass captured showed **poor body condition** (large heads, slender bodies) and empty stomachs, indicating limited mainstem feeding success during this period.
9. PIT-tagging continued on native catostomids (bluehead and flannelmouth suckers) for long-term movement and growth tracking; no previously tagged fish were recaptured this trip.
10. Water quality and turbidity monitoring (Hydrolab, Secchi disc, thermographs) documented pronounced tributary-mainstem thermal and turbidity gradients, underscoring the ecological role of tributaries as thermal refugia under dam-regulated mainstem conditions.
11. Macroinvertebrate and algal sampling (Surber, Hess samplers, drift nets) indicated increased algal (*Cladophora*) and invertebrate productivity in Spencer Creek following channel stabilization post-1993 flood.

**Recommendations** included continued intensive multi-pass and incidental fish sampling in Spencer Creek, continued mainstem monitoring at PIT-tag sites, ongoing sampling in Diamond Creek near the confluence, and expanded sampling of habitats similar to where humpback chub had previously been collected.
