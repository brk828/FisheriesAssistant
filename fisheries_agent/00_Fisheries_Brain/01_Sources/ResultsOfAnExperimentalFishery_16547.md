# Experimental Fishery on the Crayfish *Orconectes virilis* in Two Michigan Lakes

#crustacean-fisheries #freshwater-ecology #astacidea #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This experimental study, by [[Walter T. Momot]] and [[Howard Gowing]], tested [[Maximum Sustained Yield]] (MSY) as a management strategy for the freshwater crayfish [[Orconectes virilis]] in two small lakes, [[North Twin Lake]] and [[West Lost Lake]], in the [[Pigeon River State Forest]], Michigan. After three years of MSY harvesting, effort in West Lost Lake was increased fourfold and North Twin Lake was overfished. Recruitment declined in both lakes. The authors attribute this mainly to loss of nursery habitat (Carex beds) combined with density-dependent effects of a male-selective fishery. They conclude that MSY-based strategies must be compromised to reduce the risk of catastrophic decline, and that yield in weight is an unreliable indicator of stock impact.

## Background & Description

The study addressed two objectives: to determine the effects of exploitation on a freshwater crayfish population, and to evaluate MSY as a management method. Literature on crayfish fisheries was described as sparse, and the authors noted that fishery science had not modeled freshwater crayfish exploitation in depth. The working hypotheses, drawn from [[Ricker Model]] theory, predicted that harvest of an unexploited stock would shift age composition toward younger animals and increase egg production, growth, recruitment and production.

*Orconectes virilis* was abundant in both study lakes. The lakes were similar in origin, size, morphometry and general water chemistry, and were closed to public fishing as research waters. Study ran from May 1970 to September 1975, building on 1966–69 data. Adults and yearlings were captured with baited wire traps, young-of-the-year with hand nets, and population size was estimated by [[Mark-and-Recapture]] using the [[Schumacher-Eschmeyer Formula]]. Estimates were checked against the [[Leslie Method]]; 95% confidence limits were usually within 10% of the estimate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Nursery habitat:** Young-of-the-year were concentrated in a concentric band of the sedge *Carex aquatilis* var. *substricta* along the shoreline, to about 0.5 m depth. This band provided shelter from predators, especially dragonfly nymphs, and food until the young reached about 20 mm cephalothorax length.
- **Vegetation index (D):** Calculated as band width times number of clumps. In West Lost Lake D fell from 4.94 (1969) to 0.95 (1973); in North Twin Lake from 2.19 to 0.91. Clump density in West Lost averaged 4.8 per metre versus 2.4 in North Twin.
- **Water level:** Water levels rose greatly during 1971–75, inundating emergent vegetation and degrading nursery habitat. Water level did not directly affect recruitment but acted indirectly through microhabitat loss.
- **Sex-dependent distribution:** Adult females migrate to deep, cold water and avoid traps, whereas males remain in shallow, warm water where they dominate the catch. This gave females low fishing mortality.
- **Physical factors:** Temperature and mean monthly precipitation showed no detectable correlation with recruitment.
- **Regional comparison:** Wisconsin commercial catches averaged 7.2–7.5 crayfish per trap, and one Wisconsin site produced an estimated 37.3 kg/ha. Pigeon River lakes have low standing crops that preclude an economically viable fishery.

## Key Findings

**Methods and fishery design**
- Fishing in 1970 used 30 baited traps fished for 5 consecutive days (150 trap days) in each lake. Only crayfish >30 mm carapace length were removed.
- The [[Paulik-Bayliff Model]] (modified Ricker equilibrium yield model) indicated West Lost was at MSY but North Twin was under-harvested. Effort in North Twin was doubled to 300 trap days.
- In 1974–75 West Lost effort rose fourfold to 600 trap days, while North Twin remained at 300.

**Yield and age composition**
- West Lost's highest yield was 25.0 kg/ha in 1970. Yields then stabilized at 8.9–11.5 kg/ha until effort increased in 1974.
- North Twin showed no initial peak. Its yield stayed at 5.2–8.7 kg/ha during 1970–73, then rose to 14.2 kg/ha in 1974 and fell to 2.6 kg/ha in 1975.
- Males dominated catches. In 1975 West Lost sustained high yield through intensive exploitation (71.3%) of age II crayfish.

**Recruitment and fecundity**
- Recruitment declined in both lakes from 1971 to 1975 because of lower fecundity, small brood stocks and reduced nursery area.
- Mean attached eggs per female fell steadily until 1975. Fecundity showed an inverse relationship with adult female density, though correlations were not significant owing to small sample sizes.
- Poor survival of age I females to age II reduced breeding stocks.

**Growth**
- Growth did not increase as predicted. Age I male growth rose with fishing, excluding 1975, but growth of older age classes was variable. North Twin growth was markedly low in 1975.

**Production and biomass**
- Annual production differed significantly between 1966–70 and 1971–75 in both lakes (West Lost t = 3.333; North Twin t = 3.049; P < 0.05).
- Mean summer biomass differed significantly only in North Twin (t = 3.378, P < 0.05).
- The P:B ratio in 1974–75 was 0.75 in West Lost (versus a 1.21 mean earlier) and 0.54 in North Twin (versus 1.11). The authors suggest that a declining P:B ratio may signal overexploitation, but more data are needed.

**Overfishing**
- North Twin was overharvested by about 20% in 1974 and drastically overharvested in 1975. Yield matrices showed that at all multiples of 1975 effort, yields fell below the replacement level of recruitment.
- West Lost gave good yields immediately before its probable collapse.

**Causes of decline (authors' conclusions)**
- Loss of nursery habitat lowered survival of hatchlings in both lakes and altered the stock-recruitment relationship.
- The male-selective fishery increased age I female density, which lowered female survival to age II and reduced egg production per female.
- Stock-recruitment curves for both lakes shifted downward, and collapse under deliberate overfishing was rapid.

**Management recommendations**
- Yield in weight and catch-per-unit-effort data alone are hazardous indicators of stock impact.
- Changes in the annual P:B ratio may be a better early warning of collapse.
- Overfishing studies should run for at least one 3-year crayfish generation cycle.
- Size limits, combined with limited-entry leasing of water areas for commercial harvest, are considered sufficient to protect most crayfish stocks.
