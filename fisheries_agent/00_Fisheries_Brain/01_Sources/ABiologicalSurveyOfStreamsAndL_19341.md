# Biological Survey of Streams and Lakes of Fort Apache and San Carlos Indian Reservations, Arizona

#ichthyology #aquatic-ecology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1935 report from the [[Bureau of Fisheries]], Department of Commerce, presents a biological, chemical, and physical survey of the streams and lakes on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] and [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]] in Arizona. Field work by M. J. Madsen and a party of three assistants ran from August 3 to August 14, 1934. The survey was intended to guide species, size, and number selection for stocking, and it was explicitly described as a condition survey rather than a complete study. Its main conclusions are that the White River drainage holds nearly all permanent waters, that drought dried more than two-thirds of the streams in summer 1934, that trout dominate the cold upper streams, and that systematic stocking, with replacement of angler-removed fish, is the main management recommendation.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The survey aimed to (1) document biological, chemical, and physical conditions of streams and lakes; (2) use these data to select species, size, and number of fish to stock; (3) identify where environmental alterations could increase production; and (4) flag problems needing more intensive study.

**Personnel and timing.** The party comprised M. J. Madsen (biologist in charge), Paul Klingenberg and Larry G. Mosely (assistant biologists), and Wm. Mudspeth (camp attendant). They worked August 3–14, 1934, and covered all permanent and important waters on both reservations.

**Study area.** The two reservations cover approximately 4,500 square miles and contain all headwaters of the [[San Carlos River]] and about 80 per cent of the headwaters of the [[Salt River]]. Elevation ranges from 2,413 feet at [[Coolidge Reservoir]] in the south to 8,000 feet at the Mogollon Rim in the north. Apart from the northeast part of Fort Apache, the area is semi-desert.

**Methods.**
- *Physical:* Stream widths and depths measured by rule and steel tape; lake areas calculated from maps; depths sounded with marked cotton lines; temperatures taken with pocket Fahrenheit and [[Negretti-Zambra Reversing Thermometer]] readings; turbidity measured with a 10 cm white disc.
- *Chemical (field):* pH by colorimetric tubes with bromthymol blue; free carbon dioxide by phenolphthalein titration; [[Dissolved Oxygen]] by the [[Winkler Method]] (organic-matter removal step omitted); alkalinity by titration with 0.02 N sulfuric acid.
- *Biological:* Fish sampled with gill nets, seines, dip nets, wire traps, angling, and observation. Bottom fauna sampled with a square-foot net in streams and an [[Ekman Dredge]] in lakes. Plankton taken with a standard net (11 cm opening, #20 bolting silk).
- *Station definition:* 200 yards of stream, or the area workable from an anchored boat on lakes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream conditions (White River drainage).** Streams are steep upstream (300–500 feet per mile at higher elevations, about 100 feet per mile lower down) and sluggish downstream. Water temperatures ranged from 53°F at [[Williams Creek]] to 70°F in the [[White River]]. Waters above the junction of the East and North forks of the White River were judged suitable for trout; waters below became too warm and suited warm-water fishes only. Pool and riffle proportions were variable, with the better fishing streams having nearly equal areas of each.

**Water chemistry.** Chemistry was very uniform across the White River watershed: waters held considerable dissolved oxygen, were only slightly alkaline, and had small quantities of free carbon dioxide.

**Drought.** The drought of 1934 dried more than two-thirds of the streams; sixteen other streams shown on Indian Service maps were found dry, mostly in the southwest of the reservation. Only the upper White River drainage was unaffected.

**Aquatic vegetation.** Rapid flow limited plant growth, and algae were sparse. Only small, non-fluctuating, spring-fed streams supported abundant vegetation. The report notes that most streams will never produce abundant vegetation because of heavy silt loads or extreme floods.

**Food organisms.** Benthic invertebrates in order of abundance were caddisfly larvae, mayfly nymphs, bugs, snails, scuds ("fresh water shrimp"), and dobsonfly larvae. Caddisflies and mayflies occurred at all stations; scuds and dobsonfly larvae at only one station on each of two streams. Counts ranged from 607 organisms per square foot at the upper White River station to 14 at Diamond Creek, with a mean of 200 per square foot. Caddisflies averaged 91 per station, mayflies 61, and two-winged insect larvae 66. Small headwater streams generally carried more food than the larger lower streams. Terrestrial insects falling into the water were an important summer food for trout and bass, though unquantified.

**Coolidge Lake.** The lake was formed by damming the [[Gila River]] just below its junction with the San Carlos. At survey time it covered about eight square miles, and records showed it had never been filled to capacity; area varies with irrigation demand and erratic rainfall. Dissolved oxygen was the most variable factor and was lowest near the bottom and near the dam, indicating stagnation in quiet areas. Natural food was very scarce, with Ekman samples producing one or two organisms per square foot and plankton also scarce. Fluctuating water levels had eliminated aquatic and shore vegetation.

**Blue Lake.** A spring-fed, rather shallow lake with abundant natural food and no surface outlet; drainage is underground through a rock bed on the west side. The text gives an area of about two acres, although the stocking table lists 12 acres (see Key Findings).

## Key Findings

**Fish community**
- Game fish in streams, by abundance: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Native Cutthroat Trout]], and a few [[Brown Trout]]. Rainbow were the most numerous and were apparently displacing the other trout.
- [[Suckers]] and [[Bonytail]] were very numerous, most abundant in the lower, warmer waters. Sucker fry and minnows occurred in most streams, but game fish were not seen using them as food.
- Natural trout spawning was judged only fair because of poor stream beds and low water.
- Natural enemies, diseases, and parasites were not considered serious in the streams.

**Coolidge Lake fishes**
- Gill-net catches yielded [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill Sunfish]], [[Crappie]], bonytails, and suckers. No small fish were taken, and an additional minnow species was thought likely present.
- All fish except the black bass were in good condition. Bass were heavily infected with a nematode parasite of the viscera; many showed no ill effects, while others were thin.
- The report concludes the parasite does not harm edibility and does not infect species other than black bass. Its life cycle is thought to involve a bird, a crustacean, and the bass, but it is poorly understood, so no practical control was proposed. The only recommendation was to prohibit moving bass from these waters into uninfected waters.

**Water chemistry**
- Chemistry was uniform across White River streams (see [[Stream Chemistry Summary]]).
- At Coolidge Lake, oxygen was highest at the inlet (Station 3) and lowest near the dam (Station 1).

**Stream inventory**
- Table 1 summarizes about eleven surveyed streams, all in the White River and Salt River systems; they include Bog, Big Bonita, Diamond, Gooseberry, Paradise, Pacheto (Pachito), Rock, Williams, and East Fork White River, plus the White River mainstem.
- Paradise Creek and the upper White River had the richest food supply; the lower White River and East Fork had the poorest.

**Management recommendations**
- Improvement work by other agencies was not suggested, since the streams lie within the reservations and are under Indian Service regulation. The Bureau of Fisheries offered to assist if the Indian Service undertook improvements.
- Natural materials for improvement structures were abundant, so labor requirements were small.
- No stocking or improvement work occurred in 1934 because superintendents judged the streams dangerously low and closed fishing for that year.
- Because angling could deplete restocked waters, the survey recommends a stocking system in which angler-removed fish are replaced from nearby hatcheries. Recommended species and numbers appear in [[Stocking Recommendations]] (Table 3), which was based on the planting table of [[Dr. G. C. Embody]].
- Key stocking figures from Table 3 include about 27,700 rainbow annually for Big Bonita Creek (14 miles), 6,860 annually for Paradise Creek, 4,500 annually for Gooseberry Creek, and 1,500 annually for Bog Creek. The table lists 115,200 largemouth bass every five years for the Coolidge Lake area.

**Blue Lake**
- Rainbow trout averaged about three-quarters of a pound. Because the lake produces only a few fish each year, the report recommends annual stocking with legal-size fish and reducing the bag limit to ten fish per day.

**Data caveats**
- The Blue Lake area differs between the text (about two acres) and Table 3 (12 acres).
- Several OCR-damaged table cells, including some stocking numbers and the Williams Creek entries, could not be confirmed.
