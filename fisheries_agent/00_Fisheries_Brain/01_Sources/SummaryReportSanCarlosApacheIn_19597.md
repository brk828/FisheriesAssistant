# Fishery Management Program, San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation (1964 Summary Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries #centrarchidae #salmonidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This summary report from the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]] (Branch of Fishery Management Services, Phoenix, Arizona) describes the 1964 fishery management program on the [[San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation]], Arizona. Under a 1958 agreement with the San Carlos Apache Tribe, the Bureau provided technical assistance for managing and developing Reservation fishery resources and supplied fish for stocking. Work in 1964 focused on bringing all suitable Reservation waters under active management. Most waters are small livestock [[Stock Tanks]], with trout management expanded on many tanks and warm water management on others. The report also covers [[Stream Fishery|stream fisheries]] on the Black, Salt, and San Carlos rivers, fishing pressure estimates, and two proposed dam sites for new fishing lakes. The authors conclude that the Tribe would gain economically from public use facilities and new lakes, but that fishing demand will not grow significantly until access and facilities improve.

## Background & Description

**Program context**
- The program operated under an agreement between the Tribe and the [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]] for technical assistance in managing and developing fishery resources of Reservation waters, and for supplying necessary fish for stocking.
- Fishery work included resurveys of waters to assess the success of 1963 fish stocking, spot checks of fishing pressure distribution throughout 1964, and examination of several dam sites for potential fishing lakes.
- The report was prepared by [[Richard J. Navarre]], Fishery Management Biologist, dated January 29, 1965. The document is marked "Approved:" with no signature shown. It was distributed to the Washington Office, San Carlos Indian Agency, San Carlos Tribe, Phoenix Area Office (BIA), Phoenix Fishery Management Field Office, other Fishery Management Field Offices, and the Regional Office.

**Reservation setting**
- The [[San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation]] covers 1,643,939 acres.
- Most waters are small impoundments built for livestock. Tanks are scattered across the Reservation, mostly in remote areas, and are reached by primitive dirt roads that are sometimes passable only by pickup truck or similar vehicles.
- The report describes the tank fishery as an example of multiple-use management that offers a wilderness-like pond fishing system.

**Fish species referenced**
- Warm water species in tanks before conversion: [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*).
- Trout: [[Rainbow Trout]] were stocked in tanks and in [[Seneca Lake]]. Table 1 also lists [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Golden Shiner]] as present in some waters.
- Other species: [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus natalis*, OCR-garbled as "natalie" in source), [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), and [[Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stock tanks and drought effects**
- Below-normal precipitation in winter 1963–64 caused some ponds to go dry and others to become dangerously low before summer rains in 1964.
- Dry tanks lost their warm water fish populations, which were mainly largemouth bass and bluegill.
- Some tanks refilled by heavy summer run-off were restocked with rainbow trout.

**Black River**
- The Black River becomes the [[Salt River]] at its confluence with the White River. It forms part of the boundary between the San Carlos and [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]].
- The river is 108 miles long on the Reservation.
- The upper 16 miles, which form the Reservation boundary, contain a self-sustaining trout population.
- The section below the trout water, downstream to just below the Bonito Creek confluence, was stocked with 9,106 smallmouth bass in 1964. No major game fish had inhabited this section before.
- Summer limnological data from the past two years indicate that in some years the stream could support trout for at least 20 more miles downstream. In other years, put-and-take rainbow trout stocking would be practical until midsummer water temperatures and pH become too high for trout survival.

**Salt River**
- The Salt River contains a self-sustaining channel catfish population. Intensive management would offer little gain until adequate access is provided.

**San Carlos River and Warm Springs**
- The San Carlos River forms at the confluence of Kiddie Canyon Creek and Ash Creek. It flows southwest for about 20 miles, then south for about 10 miles to [[San Carlos Reservoir]].
- Permanent water runs from a point two miles below the source to a diversion dam one mile north of the Town of San Carlos. At the dam, the entire flow is diverted into an irrigation canal for part of the year.
- [[Warm Springs]] emerge at 85°F from limestone in the Naco formation, with a combined flow of about 3,400 gallons per minute. They supply stable flow along the 17-mile section between the springs and the diversion dam.
- Below the diversion dam, the river becomes intermittent and depends on run-off and return irrigation water. No fishery exists in this reach.
- Sampling found a large population of green sunfish at Warm Springs, with bonytails as the only other species there. Above the diversion dam, green sunfish, bonytails, yellow bullheads, and carp were taken.
- [[Rotenone]] was applied at Warm Springs to reduce green sunfish and bonytail populations.
- Tilapia were stocked there on September 2, 1964, at an average length of 4 inches. By November 23, 1964, they averaged nearly 8 inches. Tilapia cannot survive water temperatures below 55°F, so they cannot overwinter in Reservation ponds.

**Fishing pressure**
- An estimated 12,670 angler-days were recorded across all Reservation waters.
- Seneca Lake accounted for 7,072 angler-days (55.8 percent of the total). Estimates came from direct observation plus tribal concessionaire records.
- Subtracting Seneca Lake leaves an estimate of 5,598 fisherman-days on tanks. Little fishing occurred on Reservation streams.
- Fishing pressure is expected to rise in 1965 as more tanks join the trout program. However, significant growth will depend on lake construction and public use facilities.

## Key Findings

**Management outcomes, 1964**
- Practically all Reservation waters are small stock tanks. Trout-managed Reservation waters increased by over 100 percent from the previous year, mainly through converting drought-affected warm water tanks and stocking newly surveyed tanks.
- Table 1 lists tanks under trout management, including water area, species present, and 1964 plantings. Seneca Lake (27.0 acres, built for put-and-take trout fishing) received 20,850 rainbow trout. Among the tanks, Shortline (10.0 acres) received the largest plant at 1,350 trout. Dry Lake is listed with no fish planted.
- Table 2 lists warm water tanks. Largemouth bass were stocked in six tanks: Tufastone (500), Wob (200), Bar-F-Bar (150), Lower Junction (100), Brushy (200), and Salt (100). Channel catfish stocked in 1963 grew well and appeared regularly in catches by late fall 1964.

**Streams**
- The Black River has a self-sustaining upper trout population and a newly stocked smallmouth bass section. It will be the subject of a future special management report.
- The Salt River's channel catfish population is self-sustaining, but intensive management is not justified until access improves.
- Warm Springs supports green sunfish and bonytails. Rotenone treatment and the Tilapia stocking were used to manage that reach, with Tilapia planned as a seasonal fishery.

**Fishing pressure and recreation**
- Seneca Lake is the main angling site, with 55.8 percent of Reservation angler-days.
- Growth in fishing demand depends on new lakes plus campgrounds, picnic tables, toilets, water supplies, boat rentals, and other goods and services. The report states that Seneca Lake will not become a popular recreation area until such facilities are provided.
- The summary attributes the pond fishery's 5,598 man-days to "1965," though the estimate reflects 1964 data.

**Proposed dam sites**
- **[[Park Creek Dam Site]]:** Located about half a mile below Park Creek Cabins. A 45-foot-high dam with a 330-foot crest would create a lake of about 100 surface acres. Estimated annual use is 25,000 man-days, mainly from Morenci, Clifton, and Safford (all within 30 miles). Using the 1963 Fort Apache expenditure of $4.25 per man-day gives a gross annual income estimate of $106,000.00, assuming facilities and services are provided.
- **[[Sawmill Creek Dam Site]]:** Located one-half mile east of Shorty Tank. A 35-foot-high dam with a crest of about 300 feet would create a 35-acre lake. Estimated annual use is 10,000 fisherman-days, assuming a good road from Highway 60. Gross annual income is estimated at $42,500.00, assuming facilities and services are provided.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The Tribe should provide public use facilities at Seneca Lake and additional goods and services to encourage spending, in order to capture maximum tribal economic benefit.
- New lakes with associated facilities would attract visitors, provide jobs, and generate income for tribal members. The report describes these steps as needed soon if the Tribe wants to pursue recreation seriously.
- Other conditions noted include the new San Carlos Highway under construction, which crosses the Park Creek road about ten miles north of the Park Creek site, and a Phelps-Dodge road that already reaches Morenci.
