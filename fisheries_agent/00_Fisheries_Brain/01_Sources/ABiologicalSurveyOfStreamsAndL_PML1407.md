# Biological Survey of Streams and Lakes of Apache and Crook National Forests, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Fisheries field report, prepared by M. J. Madsen (Temporary Assistant Biologist) and dated May 1955, surveys the streams and lakes of the [[Apache National Forest]] and [[Crook National Forest]] in east-central and southeastern Arizona. The survey was a reconnaissance, not a detailed study, carried out to guide stocking decisions. Field work ran from August 4 to August 16 in the Apache Forest and from July 21 to July 30, 1954, in the Crook Forest. The report concludes that most permanent streams can support [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking, that the lakes are largely unsuitable for fish, and that habitat improvement should accompany regular stocking.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** Pre-stocking surveys aim to (1) document biological, chemical and physical conditions; (2) use these data to choose species, size and number of fish; (3) show how habitat changes can raise fish populations; and (4) flag problems needing further study. The authors state that the work only surveys conditions and is not an intensive study of any lake or stream.

**Survey party.** M. J. Madsen (in charge), Paul Klingenberg, and Gary G. Moseley (Associate Biologists), with a crew member named Hudson. The Arizona State Fish and Game Commission supplied game agents' assistance, and the Forest Service supplied maps.

**Methods** (see [[Stream Survey Methods]]):
- Stations covered about 200 yards of stream or the shoreline a boat could work from an anchor.
- Physical data: width and depth (tape), gradient (USGS maps), and water and air temperatures (pocket thermometers).
- Chemistry, measured in the field: [[Ph Measurement|pH]] (colorimetric, bromthymol blue), free CO2 (phenolphthalein titration against N/44 NaOH), [[Dissolved Oxygen]] (Winkler method, adapted for low organic content), and alkalinity (titration with weak acid, methyl orange and phenolphthalein indicators).
- Biology: plant locations and abundance were sketched and rated dense, medium or scattered. Aquatic insects were sampled with a bottom net and an [[Ekman Dredge]]-type sampler (OCR reads "Elcoan"). Fish were sampled by seine, dip net, angling and observation.
- [[Pool Grading]] scored pool size, type and frequency; the average determined an excellent, average or poor grade.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Apache National Forest.** The forest covers about 800 square miles, with elevations from roughly 11,000 feet down to about 4,500 feet in the southwest along the San Francisco River. Its main drainages are the [[Black River]], [[Blue River]] and [[Little Colorado River]]. The survey took place during a drought, so water conditions were well below average, though the authors judged them still good. Pools and riffles were generally good in permanent streams and fair in drought-affected ones.

- Streams are rapid, averaging about 250 feet of fall per mile.
- Temperatures were generally low and suited to trout. None exceeded about 70°F.
- Water chemistry (August 1954) showed moderately soft to moderately hard, slightly acidic to neutral water typical of streams draining dark mountain soils and rocks.
- Aquatic food was fair in permanent streams. Caddisflies and mayflies were most abundant, followed by midges and two-winged flies. Terrestrial insects (bees, grasshoppers, ants) were also an important summer food source for trout, but were not quantified.
- Fish found: Rainbow Trout and a few [[Cutthroat Trout]] (planted by fish culturists), with [[Suckers]] and bonytails as rough fish. Suckers and bonytails were most common in slow stretches of larger streams. Rainbow trout reproduced in some streams, but spawning potential was judged good only in the larger permanent streams.
- No significant natural enemies of fish or diseases were observed.

**Crook National Forest.** The forest has several separate divisions. Only the [[Graham Mountain Division]] and the [[Clifton Division]] have fisheries potential. The city of Safford is the central base for Forest Service operations.

- **Graham Mountain Division:** a large, steep, isolated mountain (about 4,500 to 10,500 feet, surrounded by desert). It has no lakes and only four permanent streams: Grant, Ask, Big and Frye creeks. Stream flows averaged under 4 cubic feet per second, and in 1954 many streams were dry for half their length. Gradients were steep, at least several hundred feet per mile. Water was cool, rarely above 65°F.
- **Clifton Division:** lies south of the Apache Forest on the White Mountains' south slope, with elevations from about 4,000 to 4,500 feet at the boundaries. Mostly semi-desert. Only the [[Eagle Creek]] drainage was surveyed.

## Key Findings

### Apache National Forest: Streams

- 26 streams were surveyed (Table 1 in the report). Most were classed as dry in drought conditions, and many were classed as roads- and trails-accessible.
- Biological productivity was low during the drought, with food sharply reduced. The authors expected natural food to recover with normal water.
- The Forest Service had already completed about 46 rods (roughly 0.14 miles) of improvements on one stream. The first work was poorly built, and the report recommends correcting it.
- **Recommendations:** (1) focus improvement work on heavily fished streams instead of remote headwaters; (2) stock all permanent streams systematically with desirable game fish (see Table 2 summary below).

### Apache National Forest: Lakes

- **Greer Lakes** (three irrigation storage reservoirs on the Little Colorado River near Greer, surveyed late August after irrigation drawdown): biologically very poor. Bottom samples held no food organisms, no plants were present, and plankton was very scant. Fish matched the tributary streams, but carp were found. Although not well suited to fish, these are the only lakes in the forest easily reached by anglers, so the authors recommend serious consideration for stocking.
- **[[Alpine Reservoir]]**: a New Mexico-owned storage reservoir with no fish, no permanent inflowing streams, and no fisheries potential. Records show it was drained in each of the previous three years, and it is likely to be drained again.
- Numerous small natural lakes in high meadows are very shallow, freeze to the bottom in winter, and lack permanent inlets. None were considered suitable for fish.

### Stocking Recommendations (Apache)

- Table 2 lists stocking recommendations for about 11 stream sections and the Greer Lakes. Most call for [[Rainbow Trout]] stocked annually. Counts in the scanned table are partly illegible; the largest single entries appear to be in the several-thousand range per section.
- A stocking table for lakes is based on the fish-culturist planting formula of Dr. G. C. Embody. The number of three-inch trout per acre depends on average weight and food richness; it is tabulated in the original, and the numbers are not reproduced here.

### Crook National Forest: Graham Mountain Division

- Only four permanent streams (Grant, Ask, Big and Frye creeks) and no lakes.
- No native fish were present. Rainbow trout were introduced and are now found, at least sparingly, in all permanent streams.
- Food supply was scant during the drought. Caddisflies and other insects were very few, and dead caddisfly cases were found stranded on rocks.
- Natural spawning is very unlikely because of rapid flows and a lack of gravel.
- **Recommendations:** build stronger dams that resist spring floods, space them for pool formation, add cover early, and stock legal-size fish every two weeks or every month during the season. The authors expect this to make the streams effective rearing ponds.

### Crook National Forest: Clifton Division (Eagle Creek)

- The survey covered only the Eagle Creek drainage, with the main channel of about 16 miles, of which about 8 miles were recommended for stocking.
- Water was rather warm, and the streams were fed by springs with fairly stable temperatures. Chemistry was similar to the Graham Mountain streams.
- Plants and food were limited. Suckers and bonytails were abundant in some sections, and no natural enemies of fish or disease were seen.
- **Recommendations:** avoid large dams; use small structures to create pools; protect banks and avoid debris accumulation that alters the channel; stock with a desirable game species (Rainbow Trout is recommended, since it has the best chance of sustaining a population in these waters).

### Overarching Conclusions

1. Stocking should be systematic and consistent, not one-time.
2. Habitat improvement must include cover and pool structures alongside the stocking program.
3. Lakes are generally unsuitable, with the partial exception of the accessible Greer Lakes.
4. Further study is needed, especially of natural food, reproductive success and the relationship between water level and fish production.
