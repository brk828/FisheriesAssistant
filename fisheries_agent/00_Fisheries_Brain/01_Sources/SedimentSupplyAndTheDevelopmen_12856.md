> **Note:** This source is a geomorphology paper (Dietrich, Kirchner, Ikeda & Iseya, *Nature* 340, 1989) concerning physical sediment dynamics in gravel-bedded rivers. It does not describe specific fish species or taxa, but it is highly relevant to [[Aquatic Habitat Ecology]] and [[Salmonid Spawning Gravel]] dynamics, since bed surface texture directly governs interstitial habitat quality, spawning substrate suitability, and benthic invertebrate habitat in gravel-bed streams.

## Executive Summary
This study proposes and experimentally tests a quantitative model explaining why the surface layer of most gravel-bedded rivers ("armour" or "pavement") is coarser than the underlying sub-surface sediment or the bedload being transported. The authors argue that this **coarse surface layer** develops when the sediment supply from upstream is *less* than the transport capacity of the flow — a "transport-supply imbalance" — rather than being an inherent property of poorly sorted sediment transport alone. Using flume experiments with progressively reduced sediment feed rates, they demonstrate systematic bed coarsening, changes in bedform behavior (migrating bedload sheets versus stationary coarse patches), and a narrowing of the actively transporting channel zone. A dimensionless transport ratio (**q_r**) is introduced to quantify the degree of surface coarsening relative to sediment supply, with direct implications for land-use impacts (e.g., forestry, damming) on river channel morphology and, by extension, on aquatic habitat including **fish production**.

## Background & Description
*(Substrate/bedform morphology, in place of organismal morphology, since this is a physical habitat study)*

- **Coarse surface layer (armour/pavement):** A surface layer of gravel substantially coarser (higher median grain diameter, D₅₀) than the sub-surface sediment or the bedload transported over it.
- **Grain size shifts with sediment supply reduction** (flume results):
  - High supply (17.4 g min⁻¹ per cm width): surface D₅₀ ≈ 3.7 mm — matching the sediment load, poorly sorted.
  - Moderate supply (6.1 g min⁻¹ per cm width): surface D₅₀ = 4.3 mm, marked reduction in surface sand fraction.
  - Low supply (1.7 g min⁻¹ per cm width): surface D₅₀ = 4.9 mm, further coarsening.
- **Bedload sheet structure:** Thin (1–2 grain diameters), mobile sheets with three sequential zones migrating downstream:
  1. **Congested zone** — coarse, well-sorted leading edge (~4.7 mm median diameter)
  2. **Smooth zone** — fine, poorly sorted (~2.7 mm median diameter)
  3. **Transitional zone** — intermediate (~3.7 mm median diameter)
- **Spatial channel segregation:** As supply decreases, bedload sheets become less frequent/distinct; **inactive coarse zones** expand while active transport becomes confined to a **narrow, fine-textured active channel** bordered by immobile coarse substrate — analogous to thalweg/margin differentiation relevant to physical [[Benthic Habitat Structure]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context
*(Habitat/physical-ecological implications, since no organismal niche data are present in the source)*

- The coarse surface layer is explicitly noted to affect **"river dynamics, morphology and ecology"** — establishing gravel-bed texture as a key structural habitat variable in [[Lotic Ecosystems]].
- Surface armouring below dams (where bedload supply is fully cut off) represents the extreme end of a coarsening continuum; conversely, armour-free, fine surfaces occur on bars fed by landslides or other high local sediment supply.
- The authors explicitly connect their **q_r sediment-supply framework** to land-use/river-use conflicts, citing **forest cutting vs. fish production** as a direct example where altered sediment supply (from accelerated erosion) changes channel morphology and substrate texture — factors known to affect [[Salmonid Spawning Gravel]] quality, redd viability, and interstitial habitat for benthic macroinvertebrates.
- Predicted outcomes of altered sediment supply relevant to aquatic habitat:
  - **Low initial q_r rivers:** increased bedload supply is absorbed via localized increases in q_r and widening of active (fine) zones — moderate substrate change.
  - **High initial q_r rivers:** increased sediment supply leads to net deposition, **pool infilling**, and channel migration — direct degradation of pool-riffle habitat structure important for fish refugia.
  - Increased fine sediment supply specifically promotes **bed fining**, which can mobilize coarse fractions and shift q_r upward — relevant to concerns about fine sediment intrusion into spawning gravels.

## Key Findings
1. **Core hypothesis:** Coarse surface layers form when local bedload transport capacity exceeds sediment supply from upstream (a "transport-supply imbalance"), not merely from inherent grain-sorting mechanics during transport of poorly sorted sediment.
2. **Quantitative model:** A dimensionless transport ratio, **q_r = (transport rate of coarse surface) / (transport rate of a bed as fine as the sub-surface/load)**, decreases toward zero as sediment supply is reduced and the surface coarsens; q_r = 1 indicates supply matches transport capacity (no armouring).
3. **Flume validation:** Progressive reduction of sediment feed (holding water discharge constant) produced systematic surface coarsening (D₅₀ increased from 3.7 → 4.3 → 4.9 mm) and a corresponding decline in observed q_r, closely matching model predictions.
4. **Bedform transition:** Reduced supply caused migrating bedload sheets to become less distinct, with coarse **inactive zones** expanding and active transport confined to progressively narrower **fine-textured active zones** — attributed to feedback between pocket-filling by fines, reduced grain friction, and locally accelerated near-bed flow.
5. **Comparative datasets:** Predictions matched independent flume data from Parker et al. (1982) and Kuhnle & Southard (1988), reframing the latter's results (originally interpreted as support for the "equal mobility" hypothesis of [[Parker-Klingeman Model]]) as consistent with the transport-supply imbalance model, given their large initial excess shear stress (τ/τ_crit > 4).
6. **Alternative to prior theory:** This model contrasts with the classical **equal-mobility hypothesis** (Parker & Klingeman 1982), which attributed coarse surface layers to intrinsic vertical winnowing needed to equalize mobility between coarse and fine load fractions regardless of supply-transport balance.
7. **Applied significance:** The q_r framework offers a field-mappable tool (using bankfull channel geometry, surface and sub-surface grain size sampling) to assess a river's sensitivity to sediment supply changes driven by land use (logging, damming, erosion), with direct relevance to river management balancing land use against [[River Ecosystem Health]] and fish production.
8. **Broader continuum:** Findings support a continuum of bed surface responses — from fully armoured beds below dams (zero supply) to armour-free, high-supply surfaces below landslides — framing sediment supply as the master control variable on gravel-bed river surface texture.
