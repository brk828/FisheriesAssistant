# The Status of the Native Fish Fauna of the Upper Gila River, New Mexico

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Salmonidae #Poeciliidae #southwestern-desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1970s survey by James F. LaBounty documents the historic and contemporary status of the native fish fauna of the upper [[Gila River]] system in New Mexico, one of only two remaining drainages in the American Southwest (alongside [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona) that retains a substantial, largely intact assemblage of endemic and indigenous fishes. Of nine historically native species, seven persist, one ([[Gila Topminnow]]) is extirpated from New Mexico and considered rare/endangered, and two others ([[Gila Trout]] and [[Gila Roundtail Chub]]) are severely reduced or likely extinct from the mainstem. Nineteen introduced species are recorded from the system. The principal driver of native fish decline is competitive displacement and hybridization following intentional introduction of game and bait fishes, compounded by planned dam construction and continued stream stocking practices.

## Background & Description

- The **Gila River** originates in the Mogollon Plateau of southwestern New Mexico; its largest tributary, the **San Francisco River**, arises in the White Mountains, with confluence occurring in Arizona.
- Much of the study area lies within the **Gila National Forest** and **Gila Wilderness Area**, ranging from spruce-fir/aspen forest at headwaters to piñon-juniper woodland and cottonwood-sycamore riparian corridors at lower elevations.
- Elevation range: **3,700 to over 10,000 feet**.
- The river channel is typically **<50 feet wide and <2 feet deep**, with low banks (~3 ft), subject to torrential seasonal flooding to which native fishes are adapted (reproductive prolificacy following floods).
- **Methodology**: Fish were sampled via electric shocker across 100–150 yard stream sections at stations established since 1970 (see [[Figure 1]]), compared against historical collections made by Dr. W. J. Koster and W. L. Minckley prior to 1967 (see [[Figure 2]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Elevational Zonation of Native Fish Communities
- **Highest elevations (headwater streams):** Clear, cool water — historically *Salmo gilae*, *Rhinichthys osculus*, *Pantosteus clarki*.
- **Mid-elevations (deep, precipitous canyons, turbid, higher production):** *Salmo gilae* in rapids/undercut banks; *Gila robusta grahami* near pool heads/riffles; *Tiaroga cobitis* over rocky riffles; *Meda fulgida* over gravelly bottoms; *Rhinichthys osculus* in rocky/weedy pools; *Agosia chrysogaster* in varied habitats; *Pantosteus clarki* and *Catostomus insignis* in deep pools/rocky riffles.
- **Lower elevations (<5,000 ft, mainstem Gila and San Francisco Rivers):** Warm-water, productive, seasonally variable (cold spring flows to warm silty summer flows; agricultural diversion reduces flow). Dominated by suckers, longfin dace, and spikedace among natives; abundant non-native sunfish, catfish, minnows, and mosquitofish. **Speckled dace and roundtail chub are absent** from this zone (lowest dace record: mouth of Hooker Canyon, 4,700 ft).

### Introduction Timeline & Competitive Dynamics
- ~1900: fewer than 4 non-native species present in the Gila Basin.
- 1950: ratio ~1 native : 1 introduced species.
- 1960: ratio ~1 native : 1.5 introduced.
- Present (1970s): ratio ~1 native : 2 introduced.
- Trout (rainbow, brown) introduced since the **late 1800s**; bait minnows introduced primarily around three New Mexico Game and Fish Department fishing lakes; **mosquitofish** introduced deliberately for mosquito control.
- No New Mexico regulations currently prohibit live-minnow bait use, including within the Gila Wilderness Area, facilitating continued introgression of non-native baitfish even in otherwise protected zones.

### Comparative Case Study: Salt River at Tempe, Arizona
Historical data (Minckley & Deacon 1968) from the Salt River (Gila tributary) at Tempe, AZ (1890–1967) illustrate the extreme endpoint of this process: **complete extirpation of all native species** (including *Gila elegans*, *Meda fulgida*, *Plagopterus argentissimus*, *Ptychocheilus lucius*, *Xyrauchen texanus*, etc.) and total replacement by an assemblage of ~19 introduced species (*Gambusia affinis*, *Cyprinus carpio*, *Micropterus salmoides*, *Tilapia mossambica*, and others), following stream desiccation after 1939.

## Key Findings

- **[[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*)**: Historically widespread at higher elevations, potentially down to the Redrock Valley. Now restricted to **three (paper text also notes four) isolated tributary headwater populations** due to introduced rainbow and brown trout, which compete for resources and readily **hybridize** with Gila trout, threatening genetic purity.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*)**: Formerly in pools of the Gila and San Francisco Rivers up to Cliff-Gila Valley. **Last New Mexico record: 20 specimens collected by Dr. W. J. Koster in spring 1948 at Frisco Hot Springs.** Presumed extirpated from New Mexico, likely due to competitive displacement by introduced *Gambusia affinis*.
- **[[Gila Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta grahami*)**: Formerly abundant in the mainstem up to the Gila Cliff Dwellings area through the early 1960s; last specimens collected 1967. Extensive recent (2-year) sampling recovered **zero individuals** — considered likely extinct from the upper Gila drainage, attributed primarily to introduced **smallmouth bass** competition.
- **[[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)**: Historically widespread from the Mogollon Creek–Gila River confluence (4,700 ft) to highest elevations. Now more limited in numbers, confined to higher-elevation streams; decline attributed to introduced baitfish and carnivorous game fish.
- **[[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*)**: Distribution largely unchanged from historical patterns, though loach minnow abundance has **decreased** over time (per comparison of 1940s–50s Koster collections vs. recent data); upper Gila populations represent among the **last remaining populations anywhere** for both species. Localized depletion occurs near heavily stocked fishing lakes.
- **[[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*)**: Distribution similar to historical; abundance has **increased** over the study period.
- **[[Gila Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*) and [[Gila Coarsescale Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)**: Distribution and abundance essentially **unchanged**; considered strong competitors resilient to invasion by introduced species.
- **Refugium site**: A stretch of the **West Fork of the Gila River** near the Cliff Dwellings National Monument supports **five native species together** with minimal introduced-species influence — the only such location identified in the system, flagged as a priority conservation area.
- **Future threats**: Planned dam construction (Hooker Canyon site, with alternative sites on the Gila and San Francisco Rivers, potentially backing water ~15 miles) and continued game-fish stocking (even within the Gila Wilderness Area) are identified as the primary ongoing threats to remaining native fish populations. Environmental/biogeographic studies were planned to continue through construction to inform mitigation.
- **Net species-level outcomes**: Two native species functionally replaced by introduced counterparts — **Gila Topminnow by Gambusia affinis**, and **Gila Roundtail Chub by smallmouth bass** (*Micropterus dolomieui*); **Gila Trout** replaced by other trout species except in isolated headwater refugia.

### Endemism Summary
Endemic to the Gila River system: *Tiaroga cobitis*, *Meda fulgida*, *Agosia chrysogaster*, *Catostomus insignis*, and *Salmo gilae*.
