# Native Fish Decline in Arizona Rivers: Livestock Grazing and Habitat Degradation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1996 newspaper feature from *The Arizona Republic* reports that Arizona's native fishes are in steady decline. Of 31 native species that once lived in the state's waters, five are extinct, 26 are struggling, and 18 are listed as threatened or endangered. The article identifies [[Livestock Grazing]] as the single greatest threat to native fish and wilderness in Arizona, citing a state [[Arizona Department of Environmental Quality]] risk assessment. Other pressures include logging erosion, reservoir evaporation, contaminated irrigation runoff, off-road vehicles, road construction, groundwater pumping, and the spread of non-native sport fish. The piece describes a sequence of riparian degradation, presents the conflicting positions of ranchers, ecologists, and environmental groups, and summarizes ongoing appeals and lawsuits over grazing permits on federal lands.

## Background & Description

The article contrasts the native fauna with familiar sport fish. Native species named include [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Apache Trout]], [[Desert Pupfish]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]], along with [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Loach Minnow]], and [[Little Colorado River Spikedace]]. The author notes that these fish are critical indicators of environmental health "from soil to water." Most surviving native fish occur in isolated upland tributaries high above dams, while imported species such as [[Rainbow Trout]] dominate man-made lakes and feed on native fish.

The feature was reported by Steve Yozwiak, with stream-ecology commentary from [[Robert Ohmart]], professor of ecology at Arizona State University's Center for Environmental Studies. Ohmart served as an advisor to the state risk-assessment project. Art accompanying the article illustrates how healthy and damaged streams differ.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article presents a three-stage model of stream degradation, attributed to Arizona State University researchers:

- **Natural channel:** Riparian zones full of vegetation trap eroded sediment, remove nutrients, and provide shade. Narrow, deep channels with overhanging banks, rocks, and submerged vegetation offer cool hiding and breeding places. Such streams hold high water tables and release floodwaters slowly, so flow persists longer through the year.
- **Enlarged channel:** Overgrazing or timber cutting near streams increases erosion. Added sediment reduces oxygen. Wide, shallow channels warm, and reduced shade further lowers oxygen, making survival and reproduction harder for fish. As the bed widens, floods carry more water in the channel, increasing velocity and erosion, and the water table drops.
- **Blown-out channel:** Over decades, willow and cottonwood riparian forest disappears as cattle eat young trees, water tables fall, and small trees wash away. The forest fragments into tree islands, the water table dries, the forest collapses, and downstream flooding becomes more severe.

Other threats the article lists are erosion from [[Logging]], evaporation from reservoirs, contaminated runoff from irrigated farms, off-road vehicle use, road construction, and groundwater pumping. The article notes that [[Riparian Zones]] are central to this degradation. Mine wastes have also made some fish in favored fishing holes unsafe to eat.

## Key Findings

- **Population status:** Of 31 native Arizona fish species, 5 are extinct, 26 are struggling, and 18 are on the threatened or endangered list. Native fish dominate the state's endangered species list, and their condition is worsening.
- **Primary threat:** Domestic livestock grazing is identified as the single greatest threat to wilderness and native fish, per the 1995 [[Arizona Department of Environmental Quality]] environmental-risk assessment. Ohmart argues that cattle numbers must be reduced and their access to rivers limited.
- **Ecological condition:** Ohmart states that a stream in proper functioning condition could be counted "on one hand" across Arizona.
- **Opposing view:** C.B. "Doc" Lane of the Arizona Cattlemen's Association disputes that cattle pose an environmental threat and argues that environmentalists seek control of public lands.
- **Tonto National Forest appeal:** [[Forest Guardians]] argued in a February appeal that the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] and three endangered birds are harmed by grazing on 167,000 acres of the [[Tonto National Forest]], from Roosevelt Lake to the Sierra Ancha Wilderness Area. Two weeks later, Tonto Basin District Ranger Dennis Roy withdrew five new grazing permits that would have spent $250,000 on water pipelines and cattle troughs. Environmentalists argued that buying out the permits would be cheaper. The five allotments involve miles of riparian habitat along the [[Salt River]] and its tributaries.
- **Proposed grazing regulations:** A 15-member citizens panel created by Interior Secretary Bruce Babbitt drafted new livestock-grazing rules intended to increase vegetation, slow erosion, raise water tables, slow floods, reduce stream temperatures, and improve wildlife habitat. Public meetings were scheduled from 3 to 8 p.m. on a Tuesday and Wednesday in Safford and Phoenix.
- **Apache trout and White Mountains:** In February, the National Wildlife Federation challenged the [[Forest Service]] for reissuing grazing permits in the White Mountains despite advice from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] that no more grazing occur until critical stream areas near Alpine were fenced. The Forest Service said a July congressional rider required automatic reissuance of permits for the same number of cattle, even without completed environmental reviews. Its range specialist, Dave Stewart, said fencing would proceed that year, though environmentalists feared delays.
- **Southwest Center for Biological Diversity actions:**
  - A December appeal challenged a dam about 50 miles southeast of Flagstaff that may reduce flow in East Clear Creek and endanger the Little Colorado River spikedace. The appeal also warned that a pipeline serving a Phelps-Dodge copper mine near Morenci could move non-native fish from the Little Colorado basin into the Gila River basin. A notice of a pending lawsuit followed in February.
  - A January federal lawsuit against the [[Bureau of Land Management]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]] alleged failure to consider grazing effects on 1.4 million acres in six southeastern Arizona counties. Some grazing studies had not been updated in 17 years, and the suit cited possible harm to seven types of rare native fish, plus bats, songbirds, and owls.
  - A January federal lawsuit against the Fish and Wildlife Service alleged inadequate protected river habitat for the endangered loach minnow and spikedace on the [[Gila River]]. The group sought reduced grazing and logging on federal lands in Arizona and New Mexico, and less groundwater pumping along the San Pedro and Verde rivers.
- **Shifting attitudes toward anglers:** Environmentalists once saw sport fishing as a threat, since imported species fed on natives, and wildlife officials poisoned some streams to remove native fish for popular species. Many environmentalists now regard [[Sport Fishing]] enthusiasts as potential allies in protecting native fish and clean water.
