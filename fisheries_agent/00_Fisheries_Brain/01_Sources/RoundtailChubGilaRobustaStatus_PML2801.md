# Roundtail Chub (Gila robusta) Status Survey of the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department technical report (Voeltz, 2002; Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program Technical Report 186) assesses the present status of the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and the [[Headwater Chub]] (*Gila nigra*) in the lower [[Colorado River]] basin. The work was funded by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] to (1) compile existing information, (2) identify information gaps, and (3) survey to fill those gaps. Historically, roundtail chub occurred throughout the Colorado River and its tributaries; both species are now much reduced. Current occupancy is estimated at about 18% of historic range for roundtail chub and 40% for headwater chub. Of 30 streams known to hold either species, 17 are Unstable-Threatened, 12 Stable-Threatened, and 1 Stable-Secure.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** Roundtail chub is one of six recognized *Gila* species endemic to the Colorado River basin. Its taxonomic history is complicated: the subspecies name *grahami* was shown to be a synonym of *robusta*, and the earliest available replacement name for the intermediate form, *Gila nigra* (Cope and Yarrow 1875), was elevated to full species by [[Minckley and DeMarais]] (2000). The report notes that morphological and genetic work (Rinne 1976; DeMarais 1986, 1992) supports a hybrid origin for the intermediate forms, and that the [[American Fisheries Society]] would recognize *G. nigra* in its 2002 names list.

**Morphology.** Roundtail chub is a thick, compressed, moderately streamlined minnow, most often 250–350 mm total length, occasionally 500–600 mm in large rivers. The largest hook-and-line specimen cited was 469.9 mm and 1782.62 g, from the lower Salt River (March 1984). Headwater chub has meristic and morphometric counts intermediate between *G. robusta* and *G. intermedia* and does not grow as large.

**Status history.** The species has been a concern since the late 1970s. The Desert Fish Recovery Team recommended listing, and *G. robusta* was a Category 2 candidate through the 1990s before being dropped when category 2 was abolished. It is listed as endangered in New Mexico and is on the AGFD Wildlife of Special Concern list.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and behavior.** Roundtails occupy cool to warm, mid-elevation waters, often the deepest pools and eddies of middle-sized to large streams, and usually sit near cover such as boulders, undercut banks, or vegetation. In the upper Verde, 60% of observations were in pools. Seasonal movements were documented in Aravaipa Creek. Headwater chubs occupy shallow riffles, runs and undercut banks in smaller streams. Juveniles use shallow, low-velocity, vegetated shorelines.

**Diet.** Both species are generally omnivorous. Roundtail chub diets were described as opportunistic, including filamentous algae, plant debris, aquatic and terrestrial insects, and fish; headwater chub diets at Fossil Creek consisted of aquatic insects, ostracods, plant material, and diatoms, with no fish remains found.

**Reproduction.** Roundtails mature at about age 2–5. Spawning occurs February through June in the lower basin, typically when water temperature reaches about 18 °C; the upper Verde spawning peak occurred in April and May. Eggs are adhesive, and females carry about 1,000–4,300 eggs at 100–260 mm TL in Fossil Creek, and up to 26,903 eggs at 270–427 mm TL in the Verde River. Brouder et al. (2000) found spawning aggregates clearing substrate after Sonora sucker spawning.

**Age and growth.** Growth is about 50 mm/yr through year four, slowing after age 4; age-7 individuals may reach about 300 mm. Otoliths proved a valid aging method, with 100% agreement among readers on known-age hatchery fish. Brouder (2001) found that late-winter and early-spring floods were followed by higher age-0 recruitment.

**Parasites.** Reported parasites include the copepod *Lernaea*, yellow grub (*Clinostomum marginatum*), an Asian fish tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) introduced via [[Mosquitofish]] at a hatchery, and the trematode *Ornithodiplostomum ptychocheilus*.

**Threats.** Key threats are nonnative predatory and competitive fishes (smallmouth bass, flathead and channel catfish, red shiner, green sunfish), nonnative crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii* and *Orconectes virilis*), habitat alteration (diversions, livestock grazing, sedimentation), groundwater pumping that reduces base flows, and mining effluent. Reduced flows below roughly 0.3 m³/s (10 cfs) eliminate chub habitat.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and status**
- Roundtail chub and headwater chub are known from at least 30 streams in the lower Colorado River basin.
- Both species are considered extirpated from 13 streams.
- Population status is unknown for 14 streams, largely due to access restrictions on Reservation lands (White Mountain Apache and San Carlos Apache).
- Estimated historic range: about 5,000 km of stream (4,500 km roundtail; 500 km headwater).
- Estimated current range: about 1,000 km (roundtail 800 km, 18%; headwater 200 km, 40%).
- Estimated reduction: about 3,300 km (66%) of historic range no longer contains chubs.

**Stream status (30 occupied streams)**
- 17 Unstable-Threatened, 12 Stable-Threatened, 1 Stable-Secure ([[Deadman Creek]], South Fork).
- Notable extirpations include the [[Lower Colorado River]] mainstem, [[Little Colorado River]], [[Zuni River]], [[Bill Williams River]], [[Big Sandy River]], [[San Francisco River]], [[Lower Gila River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[Salt River]] upper reaches, and Christopher, Horton, Rye and Dry Beaver creeks.
- The Verde River and Oak Creek populations have declined sharply; the upper Verde showed an alarming decline above Sycamore Creek.
- Lower Verde River mark-recapture estimated over 6,000 fish, but recruitment is very low.

**Surveys (2000–2001)**
- AGFD personnel surveyed streams prioritized by a Gap Analysis, using backpack electrofishing, seines, gill and trammel nets, hook and line, and visual observation.
- Newly documented roundtail populations include Wilder Creek (abundant, the only species sampled) and Rock Creek; a new headwater chub population was reported in Wet Bottom Creek.
- Several populations were found to be dominated by nonnatives (e.g., Trout Creek, Dry Beaver Creek).

**Management recommendations**
- Preserve the existing range of variation among *Gila* populations, regardless of taxonomy.
- Recognize *Gila nigra* in Arizona Game and Fish Commission Order 40 and agency literature.
- Apply the same angling regulations (one chub per day, over 13 inches) to headwater chub as to roundtail chub, since headwater chubs rarely reach legal size.
- Translocate juvenile roundtails from Cherry Creek to Ash Creek and from Chevelon Creek to suitable reaches, and consider the Little Colorado River hatchery for propagation.
- Monitor Aravaipa Creek quarterly above, below and between fish barriers.
- Maintain fences to exclude livestock and burros from riparian areas (Wilder Creek).
- Improve relationships with the White Mountain and San Carlos Apache Tribes to survey Reservation waters.
- Secure water rights and reduce groundwater pumping and riparian degradation.

**Knowledge gaps**
- Status of all Reservation populations is unknown.
- The Middle Fork Gila River population lacks museum collections.
- The possible historic population in Silver Creek (Little Colorado River) remains unverified.
