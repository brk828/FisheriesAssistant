# Endemism in Fishes of the Clear Lake Region of Central California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #embiotocidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This monograph by John D. Hopkirk, a contribution from the [[Museum Of Vertebrate Zoology]] (University of California), examines the freshwater fishes of the [[Clear Lake Basin]] and surrounding [[Clear Lake Region]]. Of the fourteen fish species native to the basin, five (36 percent) are endemic lake-adapted derivatives of lowland, river-adapted forms of the [[Central Valley]]. The fifth is a new genus and species, [[Endemichthys Grandipinnis]], apparently extinct. The paper describes and names these forms, reviews the geologic history that isolated the basin, and argues that the lake is older than previously thought. The study also documents severe declines of native fishes linked to insecticide treatment, introduced species and eutrophication, and warns that proposed water diversions could further threaten the endemics.

## Background & Description

The Clear Lake region extends from the [[Eel River]] basin in the north to the [[San Francisco Bay]] drainage in the south, bounded by the [[Sacramento River]] basin to the east and the [[Russian River]] and coastal basins to the west. Hopkirk built on earlier work on the tuleperch ([[Hysterocarpus Traskii]]), which indicated the Russian River and Clear Lake basins were centers of geographic isolation and speciation. Clear Lake is the largest natural freshwater lake wholly within California, with a surface area of about 40,000 acres and a maximum depth of 50 feet. The lake is shallow, alkaline (pH 7.2 to 9.7), turbid (Secchi visibility under 3 feet), and eutrophic, with a 420-square-mile drainage basin.

Hopkirk notes that the basin's fish fauna had previously been assigned to the Sacramento fauna without recognition of its endemism. He provides new taxonomic descriptions and distribution records, supported by specimen data deposited in the [[California Academy Of Sciences]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and hydrography.** Two hypotheses for the origin of Clear Lake are reviewed: the [[Lava Flow And Landslide Hypothesis]] (Davis, 1933; Anderson, 1936) and the [[Fault Depression Hypothesis]] (Brice, 1953; Hodges, 1966). Hopkirk concludes that fish morphological differentiation implies an age of many thousands of years, not "a few centuries," and that the basin was originally drained into the Sacramento system before isolation by volcanism (Mount Konocti) and tectonic activity. Cache Creek, the present outlet, is considered Recent in age.

**Zoogeography.** Hopkirk uses a provincial framework (after Snyder and Rutter) in which the [[Sacramento Freshwater Fish Province]] contains several subprovinces, including the newly delineated Clear Lake and Russian River subprovinces. Fish provinces align with the Pleistocene uplift and faulting of the North Coast Ranges, which redirected headwater streams westward.

**Longitudinal zonation.** Fishes are arranged into four altitudinal zones: trout, roach, sucker and hitch. Clear Lake's fauna belongs to the hitch zone, with the trout, roach and sucker zones poorly represented because few permanent streams enter the lake.

**Environmental threats.** Multiple factors have reduced native populations: the 1949, 1954 and 1957 DDD insecticide treatments for the Clear Lake gnat (*Chaoborus astictopus*), which were linked to grebe deaths; introduction of exotic species such as [[Bluegill]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and carp; sedimentation and eutrophication; and reduced inflow. A proposed diversion of up to 1 million acre-feet of Eel River water through Clear Lake could alter temperature and favor marginal-water species.

## Key Findings

- **Endemic fishes (5 of 14 native species, 36 percent):**
  - [[Pogonichthys Ciscoides]] (Clear Lake splittail): lake-adapted derivative of [[Sacramento Splittail]], with finer gill rakers, terete body and small fins.
  - [[Lavinia Exilicauda Chi]] (Clear Lake hitch): subspecies of [[Sacramento Hitch]] with more gill rakers (26 or more) and a deeper body.
  - [[Endemichthys Grandipinnis]] (Clear Lake minnow): new genus and species known only from 12 specimens collected in 1939 and 1940; apparently extinct.
  - [[Hysterocarpus Traskii Lagunae]] (Clear Lake tuleperch): subspecies with 26.8 percent broad-barred pattern variants and more gill rakers.
  - [[Cottus Asper]] Clear Lake population: inland derivative subspecies with more fin rays and intense prickling.
- **Tuleperch subspecies:** three subspecies recognized: *traskii* (Central Valley and Monterey Bay), *pomo* (Russian River, new), and *lagunae* (Clear Lake, new). Three color variants occur: narrow-barred, broad-barred and non-barred.
- **Former/extirpated species:** [[Thicktail Chub]] (*Gila crassicauda*), known from only two Clear Lake specimens (1873 and 1938), presumed extinct; [[Sacramento Perch]] much reduced; [[Pacific Lamprey]] recorded only once (Jordan and Gilbert, 1894) and not since.
- **Clear Lake fishery:** dominated by introduced species; bluegill was predominant, with population cycles relative to white catfish. Clear Lake splittail declined from "very abundant" in 1925 to nearly absent by 1946-47.
- **Snails:** three endemic snails (*Physa costata*, *Carinifex minor*, *Valvata virens*), all smaller than widespread cognates, suggesting island-like populations.
- **Speciation model:** the endemic lake forms were produced by [[Fluviolacustrine Speciation]], with lacustrine adaptations (many fine gill rakers, deep dark peritoneum, small fins) contrasted with fluviatile adaptations (few coarse gill rakers, large fins).
- **Conservation:** Hopkirk concludes that Clear Lake's small size and high endemism demand careful consideration of future basin development, and that the endemic fishes are vulnerable to habitat change and introduced species.
