# Food-Fish Farming in the Mississippi Delta

#ichthyology #marine-biology #buffalofish

## Executive Summary

This 1959 article by Malcolm C. Johnson, a former [[Soil Conservation Service]] biologist at Delta Farm Fisheries (Tillar, Arkansas), describes the rapid development of pond food-fish culture in the lower Mississippi valley. The core practice is a rotation of [[Rice-Fish Farming|rice and fish]]: fields are flooded for about two years to control weeds, stocked with [[Bigmouth Buffalo|buffalofishes]], and then returned to rice. The system grew mainly through trial and error by Arkansas rice farmers, with the strongest concentration in Arkansas. The author argues that its value lies in cash fish crops, improved soil and rice yields, and recreation, with fish often treated as a by-product of rice farming.

## Background & Description

The article opens by explaining why U.S. pond food-fish culture received little professional attention:

- **Sport fishing dominates research.** The economic value of commercial freshwater landings is minor compared with the recreational fishery, which receives most research, management, and protective legislation.
- **Consumer preference favors meat.** Americans rely mainly on beef and pork, turning to fish for variety or special occasions. Buyers will not pay much more for fish than for beef or pork, and will not accept an inferior product at a similar price.
- **Market segments.** Buyers fall into three groups: habitual fish-eaters who compete with other meats on quality and price, bargain buyers who prioritize cost, and gourmet buyers who pay a premium. To succeed, a pond producer must offer a choice product, a very cheap product, or good quality at a meat-comparable price.
- **Prior commercial culture.** Apart from [[Rainbow Trout]] hatcheries (the elite market for decades) and a few failed attempts with [[Channel Catfish]] in ponds, no food-fish culture operated in the U.S. Land and construction costs were too high for low-priced species such as [[Common Carp]].

Rice production in the Delta is highly mechanized. Land newly cleared of trees ("[[New-Ground Rice Land]]") yields about 100 bushels per acre in its first year, but yields decline each year. After two to four years the land must be fallowed or planted to pasture or soybeans for one or more years. Weeds and grasses that compete with rice were previously controlled by heavy grazing, repeated mowing, or repeated plowing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Weed control by flooding.** Rice farmers found that flooding fields to 18 or more inches for about two years eliminates most troublesome weeds and grasses, and may also improve soil structure and fertility. Cooperative studies by the University of Arkansas, the Agricultural Research Service, and the [[Soil Conservation Service]] were under way to test whether flooding adds total nutrients or only converts residual nutrients to a more available form. The author notes that rice yields without fertilizer were obtained on land flooded for the previous 24 months.

**Water sources and contamination.** The Arkansas rice area contains many natural lakes, streams, canals, and drainage ditches with wild fish such as green sunfish, shad, and carp. Hatchery and nursery ponds are therefore filled from wells through closed conduits or short open canals. Finishing reservoirs are less sensitive, and surface water as well as well water is used. Reservoirs are reflooded after rice harvest, typically by late fall or early winter.

**Predator and competitor control.** Wild fish that enter reservoirs compete with buffalofish for food and can reduce production below a profitable minimum. [[Largemouth Bass]] are stocked to suppress them, and size differences keep bass from eating the buffalofish.

**Land reuse.** Woodlands flooded for fish die within two to three years, after which the water is drained, the fish are harvested, and dead trees are cleared more cheaply than living ones.

## Key Findings

### Species and Culture Methods

- **[[Bigmouth Buffalo]]** (*Megastomatobus cyprinella* as printed; the standard name is *Ictiobus cyprinellus*) is the main ricefield species. It grows rapidly and can exceed 100 pounds. Its market price is roughly triple that of carp. Its fine intermuscular bones are its main drawback in the eyes of many consumers.
- **[[Smallmouth Buffalo]]** (*Ictiobus bubalus*) and **[[Black Buffalo]]** (*Ictiobus niger*) are produced to a lesser extent. The smallmouth is the best market fish of the three because of its smaller head, higher dressed yield, and palatability. Producers still prefer the bigmouth for its faster growth and higher total production per unit area.
- The method follows the system developed by H. S. Swingle at the Alabama Agricultural Experiment Station, adapted to an extensive Arkansas model. Asian carp culture is intensive, while Arkansas relies on large, low-cost units.
- **Brood fish:** overwintered at 600 to 2,000 pounds per acre in [[Hatchery Ponds]] and fed soybean meal, cottonseed meal, or commercial feed at 1.0 to 2.5 percent of body weight daily. Holding continues until April 1 to avoid freeze losses. Brood fish usually spawn within 24 hours of transfer to freshly flooded spawning ponds.
- **Fry:** with inorganic fertilizer, fry usually reach 2 to 3 inches by June 1. Very high fry numbers require daily concentrate feeding.
- **Nursery ponds:** 18 inches to 4 feet deep, 2.5 to 20.0 acres optimal. Fingerlings are stocked at 10,000 to 20,000 per acre, unfed, and reach 4 to 8 inches by October or November in well-fertilized water.
- **Pond structure:** hatchery and nursery ponds need graded interiors, and finishing reservoirs need drainage systems (field drains, borrow ditches, and outlet pits) for efficient harvest.

### Stocking, Growth, and Yields

- Buffalofish must exceed 5 pounds to be salable. At least 100 fish per acre reach that size in two years in fertile water. Auburn trials recorded about 20 percent mortality over two years.
- The standard finishing-reservoir stocking rate is 125 fingerlings per acre. The author states that the optimal rate is not yet known.
- Mortality in Arkansas usually exceeds 20 percent and sometimes exceeds 90 percent. Brady and Hulsey (1959) identified rough handling of fingerlings by inexperienced personnel as the most critical problem after wild-fish contamination.
- Yields above 1,000 pounds per acre have been recorded on heavy delta soils. Average carrying capacity is about 500 pounds per acre in fertile reservoirs with heavy stubble.
- Feeding buffalofish in reservoirs is unprofitable because organic concentrates are expensive. Natural food is supported by inorganic fertilizer when needed. Some growers accept smaller crops rather than pay for fertilizer.
- Bass yield 20 to 125 pounds of marketable fish per acre in two years, which the author notes is legal to sell only where farm-raised game sales are permitted and regulated.
- The author estimates that 50 to 100 pounds of marketable [[Channel Catfish]] per acre could be grown alongside buffalofish and bass, given adequate fingerlings. A large producer advocates a one-year combination of catfish with [[Fathead Minnow]] as forage. Few Arkansas growers are ready for the costly feeding program outlined by Swingle (1957a).

### Economics

- Reservoir construction costs $10 to $60 per surface acre, depending on levee length and height.
- Flooding and maintaining water levels costs about $8 to $10 per acre per year with deep wells. Surface water is much cheaper.
- Fertilization probably averages under $10 per acre per year in fertile areas.
- Labor is usually provided by year-round general farm workers rather than hired specifically for fish.
- Market prices: buffalofish in the round sell for 12 to 20 cents per pound, and bass for 15 to 25 cents per pound.
- Projected return: 500 pounds of buffalo at 12 cents plus 80 pounds of bass at 15 cents gives $72 per acre per two-year rotation. Net income is probably under 40 percent, or about $13.50 per acre per year. The author considers this modest for high-value Delta land, but comparable to alternate crops. Fish permits for waterfowl hunting or bass fishing often exceed buffalofish income.
- Land flooded for fish can be reclaimed as new-ground rice. Records show rice yields above 100 bushels per acre without fertilizer on land that yielded only 15 bushels per acre with fertilizer before flooding.

### Adoption and Industry Development

- On June 6, 1958, the [[Soil Conservation Service]] estimated 69,982 acres of flooded food-fish land in Arkansas. More than one-third was former rice land, and the rest was woodland or irrigation reservoirs flooded for other purposes.
- A survey suggested that rotation acreage would double by fall 1960 compared with 1958.
- The [[Fish Farmers Cooperative Association]] in Dumas, Arkansas, is an agricultural cooperative that handles only farm-raised fish. Its plant cost about $60,000, funded by member subscriptions, and it owns about $13,000 in trucks and equipment. It can sharp-freeze 10,000 pounds of fish per day at -30°F and store them at -10°F. It began operating in July 1958, stabilized producer returns, scheduled fish flow to specialized markets, and improved quality through trained harvest crews and continuous icing.
- Federal action: in 1958 Congress passed a bill (sponsored by Senator J. W. Fulbright) to establish a warmwater fish pond experiment station in Arkansas, administered by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]]. Construction was expected to begin in 1959, with experiments by 1960.

### Summary of Benefits

The author lists six benefits of the program: (1) increased soil fertility and structure; (2) increased rice yields; (3) cash fish crops; (4) recreational value from hunting and fishing; (5) a long-range increase in human food; and (6) land that stays productive and does not blow away while idle under water.
