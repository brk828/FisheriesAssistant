# Threatened Fishes of the World: *Gila elegans* Baird & Girard, 1853 (Cyprinidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This species account, part of a "Threatened Fishes of the World" series by Paul C. Marsh, describes the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*[[Gila Elegans]]*), a moderate-sized cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species is listed as endangered in the United States. It is now extremely rare, with remnant adults persisting mainly in [[Lake Mohave]]. The author describes it as arguably the most critically imperiled fish in western North America and warns that it will soon be extinct in the wild without augmentation of remaining populations and re-establishment in select areas. Recovery is judged technically feasible but has been constrained by political considerations and perceived needs for further study.

## Background & Description

- **Identification:** Streamlined body with a dorsoventrally flattened skull, very thin caudal peduncle, large falcate fins, and sometimes incomplete scale coverage. Total vertebrae number 47–50 (including urostyle and Weberian apparatus). Pharyngeal teeth are reported as 2,5–4,2. Coloration is dark olive above and light below.
- **Size and life history:** Maximum total length exceeds 521 mm. Growth is rapid early in life, and the species is long-lived, reaching roughly 25 years.
- **Taxonomy and naming:** The common name "bonytail" was until recently applied to almost all *Gila* species, which has contributed to poor knowledge of its habitat and ecology.
- **Distribution:** Endemic to larger Colorado River basin streams in Arizona, California, Colorado, New Mexico, Nevada, Utah, and Wyoming, plus Sonora and Baja California Norte, Mexico. It is now extirpated from Mexico, New Mexico, Wyoming, and the Gila River basin in Arizona.
- **Abundance:** Historically common to scarce. Current populations are too small for reliable estimates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Adults occupy pools and eddies rather than turbulent areas. Young are presumed to use quiet marginal habitats, and limnetic zones of reservoirs are also used.
- **Feeding:** Surface and drift-feeding omnivore, consuming terrestrial and aquatic insects, plant debris, and algae. It acts as a zooplanktivore in reservoirs.
- **Predators and threats to young:** Historically preyed upon by [[Colorado Squawfish]] and aboriginal humans. Larvae and juveniles are now potential prey for a suite of non-native fishes.
- **Reproduction:** Spawns at about 18 °C in June–July in the upper [[Green River]], Utah. In Lake Mohave, May aggregations of about 50 fish were observed, typically one female with 3–5 males broadcasting gametes over gravel at depths to 9 m. Eggs are adhesive and fertilized externally. Incubation takes 5–11 days depending on temperature, and swim-up occurs at roughly 8.1–8.6 mm TL.
- **Recruitment:** Recruitment to wild populations has been undocumented for several decades. The author suggests this may partly reflect difficulty in identifying young fish.
- **Captive propagation:** Readily propagated in hatcheries and ponds that exclude non-native fishes, where volunteer spawning is common.
- **Principal threats:**
  - Habitat degradation and loss from water development.
  - [[Hybridization]] with [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*).
  - Interactions with non-native fishes.

## Key Findings

**Conservation status and actions**
- Current conservation action is limited and lacks a proactive component.
- Recommended actions include:
  - Maintaining refugia populations.
  - Developing propagation and rearing protocols.
  - Augmenting wild populations with hatchery-reared fish.
  - Researching captive, hatchery-produced stocks.
  - Characterizing genetics.
  - Designating critical habitat.

**Recovery recommendations**
1. Obtain an adequate stock of wild fish.
2. Implement an aggressive augmentation and re-introduction program.
3. Evaluate the causes of recruitment failure.
4. Determine critical life history and habitat requirements.
5. Thoroughly document recovery actions.

**Current distribution**
- Now extremely rare outside [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona and Nevada), where remnant adults persist.
- Recent individual captures have been reported from [[Lake Havasu]] (Arizona and California), the [[Yampa River]] (Colorado), the Green River (Utah), and the Colorado River (Utah and Colorado).

**Overall conclusion**
- The species is arguably the most critically imperiled fish in western North America.
- Without measures to augment remaining populations and re-establish the species in select areas, it will soon be extinct in the wild.
- A recovery program is technically feasible but has been delayed by political considerations and calls for further study.
