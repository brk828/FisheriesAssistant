# Diel Drift and Feeding of Channel Catfish Alevins in the Illinois River, Arkansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictalurus-punctatus

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether larval fish enter the drift in order to feed. It examines [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) alevins, which dominated the [[Larval Fish Drift]] assemblage in the [[Illinois River]], a fifth-order [[Ozark Mountains]] stream in [[Benton County Arkansas]]. Alevins were sampled hourly over two 24-hour periods in June and July 1981, and their [[Stomach Content Analysis]] was compared with concurrent [[Invertebrate Drift]] and [[Benthic Invertebrates]] densities. Drift coincided with feeding, but the alevins had eaten mainly [[Chironomidae]] that were rare in the drift. The authors therefore favor the view that drift is a consequence of feeding activity, not a means of obtaining food, while stating that the results are ambiguous.

## Background & Description

Downstream displacement of fish larvae matters both ecologically and practically, especially where water is withdrawn for industrial or other uses. Earlier work described the temporal and spatial distribution of drifting larvae in large rivers, and invertebrate drift has been studied extensively in northern temperate streams. Several basic questions remained open: why drift occurs, how far organisms are displaced, and whether drifting animals are active or passive.

In small streams, fish larvae usually drift with a [[Diel Periodicity]] synchronous with invertebrate drift. Both groups show a strong peak about 2 hours after sunset and a lesser peak 2–3 hours before sunrise, the "[[Bigeminous Drift Pattern]]" described by Waters (1965, 1972). Light intensity is the [[Zeitgeber]] for this rhythm. Fish larvae rarely drift during the day in small streams.

Four explanations for larval drift have been proposed:

- [[Predator Avoidance]]
- Active search for nursery habitat
- Foraging behavior
- Nocturnal disorientation leading to accidental displacement

Some stream fishes, especially salmonids, eat drifting invertebrates and adjust their activity to match drift availability. Elouard and Leveque (1977) found similar drift periodicities in Ivory Coast rivers and proposed that synchrony arises because fish feed on drifting invertebrates. If that is correct, the fish's feeding should follow the diel drift pattern, and the invertebrates eaten should be common in the drift. This study was designed to test that [[Feeding Hypothesis]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** The study took place in a riffle area of the Illinois River, Benton County, Arkansas. Water depth and current velocity differed among net sites:

- June 23–24: depth 37–50 cm, velocity 32–80 cm/s
- July 21–22: depth 22–51 cm, velocity 90–99 cm/s

**Sampling design.** Three [[Drift Net Sampling]] nets (aperture 0.0125 m², mesh 0.4 mm) were placed roughly equidistant along a transect across the riffle. Samples were collected hourly over 24-hour periods on June 23–24 and July 21–22, 1981. The nets sat just below the surface and at least 8 cm above the substrate. Flow was measured with a torpedo-type flowmeter both inside the net aperture and just outside it. Large mesh reduced clogging, and nets were cleaned between collections so internal and external flow rates were essentially equal. The authors note that drift of very small invertebrates may have been underestimated.

**Benthic sampling.** Invertebrate density in the riffle was measured during each sampling period with a 0.1 m², 253 µm mesh [[Hess Sampler]]. Two samples came from the head of the riffle, one from mid-riffle, and one from the tail, and the results were averaged.

**Supplementary daytime collection.** No alevins were captured in drift during daylight. The authors tried kick nets, a 0.4 mm seine, a seine stretched across a swift riffle, and disturbance of the substrate with a garden fork. None of these methods yielded any larval channel catfish during the day.

**Artificial stream.** Two 60-liter aquaria were joined by a 50 cm rectangular glass channel, with a pump producing about 35 cm/s of flow. Live alevins were observed intermittently during the day and photographed at night with flash.

**Diet analysis.** Alevins were measured to the nearest 0.5 mm standard length. Stomach fullness was estimated to the nearest 25% (Marak 1974), and contents were identified and analyzed by the [[Frequency-of-Occurrence Method]] (Hynes 1950).

## Key Findings

**Sample and drift pattern.**
- 317 channel catfish alevins (standard length 14–16 mm) were examined.
- All drifting alevins were 14.0–16.0 mm in standard length. None were collected during daylight.
- Alevin drift and invertebrate drift coincided and followed the typical nocturnal bigeminous pattern.

**Feeding pattern.**
- Stomach fullness rose during the first few hours after drift began each night and again in the 2 hours before sunrise.
- Stomachs were fullest when relatively few alevins were drifting.
- Alevins fed predominantly on chironomid larvae and pupae.
- Chironomids made up only a minor share of the drift (2–6%) and a larger share of the benthos (reported as 14%).
- Several other taxa were far more abundant in the drift and benthos, including [[Simuliidae]], [[Baetis]] sp., [[Stenonema]] sp., and [[Cheumatopsyche]] sp., but few of them were eaten.

**Interpretation.**
- The data are described as ambiguous. Feeding and drift coincide, which supports the feeding hypothesis.
- Selection for rare chironomids would have required intense, visually mediated attacks by weakly swimming larvae on moving prey at night. The authors consider this unlikely.
- Moonlight was limited: the moon was waning toward third quarter and had just begun to rise when maximum drift occurred.
- The authors therefore prefer an alternative model. Alevins hide in the substrate by day, possibly avoiding free-swimming predators. After light fades in the evening they emerge and feed among the benthos. Those that hold position longer eat more and are the last to enter the drift, so they have the fullest stomachs. Alevins that reach satiety shelter in the substrate and drift ends. Alevins swept away by the current are transported passively downstream and leave the drift actively or passively. The cycle repeats toward dawn.

**Caveats.**
- Chironomids are scarce in riffle benthos but often dominate pools (Hynes 1970). Pools were not sampled, and a leaf-detritus study in a pool just upstream found chironomids to be the most abundant organisms there (Petty and Brown, in press). Both larvae and prey may have been concentrated in pools by day.
- No alevins were found in riffle substrates.
- The model is consistent with [[Ploskey and Brown 1980]], who found that the mayfly *Baetis flavistriga* drifts in step with apparent feeding, although that mayfly is an unlikely drifter in search of food.
- The model may not fit all cases. Some invertebrates, such as large mayfly and stonefly instars, may drift to avoid predation. Others drift to colonize new areas. Migrating anadromous larvae may drift as more than a by-product of feeding.
- The authors judge the feeding-consequence model a plausible and parsimonious explanation for why many lotic species drift and for the timing of that drift, and they call for further testing.
