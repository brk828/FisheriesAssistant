# Formation and Destruction of a Gila River Mesquite Bosque Community

#botany #riparian-ecology #fabaceae

## Executive Summary

This paper by W. L. [[Minckley]] and Thomas O. Clark (Arizona State University, *Desert Plants* 6(1), 1984) documents the repeated formation and destruction of a [[Mesquite Bosque]] on a terrace of the [[Gila River]] in eastern Arizona. The terrace formed behind a coarse alluvial cone deposited by an ephemeral tributary, [[Dry Canyon]]. Vegetation then colonized the deposit, culminating in a dense mesquite community. Sustained flooding in the mainstream river removed the bosque in 1978–79. The authors also use the case to discuss how floods, sedimentation, and the water table shape desert riparian plant communities, and why [[Saltcedar]] may fail to invade such terraces.

## Background & Description

- **Historical context:** Extensive mature bosques of [[Honey Mesquite]] (*Prosopis glandulosa*) and [[Velvet Mesquite]] (*Prosopis velutina*) once covered subtropical southwestern floodplains. Most large stands have since been destroyed or degraded by water diversion, pumping, agricultural clearing, cutting for firewood, and invasion by introduced saltcedar.
- **Study site:** The bosque lay on a level terrace on the Gila River, east of Safford, in Graham County. The terrace was about 945 m above sea level in 1976. Its estimated area in 1976 was 10.0 ha. The bosque was roughly 1 km long, about 60 m wide at the downstream (west) end, 275 m at the widest point, and 115 m at the upstream (east) end. Its landward edge was bounded by a cliff.
- **Vegetation:** Mesquite dominated the site and made up about 98.8% of all perennial plants in the study area, according to the authors' earlier work ([[Minckley and Clark 1981]]). Other species were sparse. These included [[Hackberry]] (*Celtis reticulata*), [[Blue Paloverde]], and [[Catclaw Acacia]] (*Acacia greggii*). [[Fremont Cottonwood]] (*Populus fremontii*) and [[Goodding Willow]] (*Salix gooddingii*) occurred along the streamside and in low, moist areas.
- **Size structure:** Trees were dominated by the 2- to 9-m size class (80.7% of trees, 4 to 35 cm diameter). Trees taller than about 9 m (up to about 60 cm diameter) were infrequent and occurred mainly along the bordering cliff, where they likely represent remnants of an earlier bosque.
- **Ecological terms:** The term *bosque* is Spanish for woodland or forest. The study area lies in a zone where *P. glandulosa* and *P. velutina* overlap, and the two species were difficult to separate in the field.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Floods and flood history**

- The Gila River floodplain originally supported diverse riparian vegetation, including broad-leaved trees and mesquite, willow, and cottonwood.
- The 1870–89 period was one of the driest in almost 250 years. Floods followed in 1891–95 and 1896–1904. Large regional livestock herds during this time likely reduced vegetation and increased erosion.
- Between 1875 and 1914, average channel width at a site about 10 km downstream increased sharply. Scoured channel area expanded from 89 ha to 1,207 ha, and vegetated area fell from 1,118 ha to none ([[Burkham 1972]]). Channel widening and clearing were most likely completed by floods in 1905–06.
- Between 1891 and 1979, the flood record for the Solomon–Safford reach lists peak volumes from roughly 490 to 5,390 m³/s, with several events labeled "destructive." Notable floods include the October 1972 flood, which probably eroded the bosque to the configuration observed in 1975–77, and the repeated floods of 1978–79.

**Formation mechanisms**

- The terrace formed behind a partial dam of boulders at the confluence of Dry Canyon and the Gila River. Boulders at least 1.0 m in diameter persisted at the confluence in 1976. A flash flood in 1977 added boulders of similar size and further deflected the river south against a cliff, creating a rapid.
- The authors infer that the obstruction began forming in the early 1900s and grew with later floods. It promoted deposition of coarse and then finer material on its downstream side. Low competence of silt-laden floodflows from 1916 to 1978 probably prevented removal of the dam.
- Mesquite cover stabilized and increased silt accumulation. Mature mesquite in 1978 had trunks buried up to 2.0 m above the major roots. Larger trees were more deeply buried than smaller ones.

**Destruction**

- Removal occurred by undercutting and collapse. The channel was straightened over about 1.0 km. Cavitation may have caused explosion-like sounds and the collapse of large terrace sections.
- About 1.5 ha of bosque disappeared in March 1978. A further 3.5 ha were removed in December 1978–January 1979. The authors estimate that earlier flooding (about 1905–16) removed around 8 ha of an equivalent terrace.
- Winter floods deposited about 0.5 m of silt in the remaining bosque. Deposition on the south side of the river in 1978–79 reached up to one meter of coarse alluvium overlain by sand and silt.
- Seedling mesquite had established on the landward side of the aggrading surface by summer 1982.

**Mesquite biology and site conditions**

- Mesquite does not tolerate waterlogged soils, but its roots penetrate deeply in search of water ([[Minckley and Brown 1982]]). Fine alluvium overlying coarser bedload stays moist by capillarity even without rain. Mesquite uses soil moisture above the water table more than water from the saturated zone.
- Mature mesquite in protected places provides abundant seed. The seed is buried by wind, water, or rodents and germinates readily in moist, well-aerated, sunny sites.
- Once thick stands of young mesquite form, they trap sediment, and the terrace keeps growing vertically. Canopy closure excludes most other plants and produces a bosque. Where alluvial cones protect the site, as at Dry Canyon, the cycle may correspond to flood recurrence intervals of 50 to 100 years, and the bosque may be considered a quasi-climax community.

**Other riparian processes**

- Terraces can form where the water table drops. Vegetation on a new river bar, including [[Bacchasimilar Baccharis]] (*Baccharis salicifolia*) and [[Tessaria]] (*Tessaria sericea*), slows flow, traps sediment, and raises the surface above the water table.
- Saltcedar needs a long period of saturated soil and a high water table to establish. Its windblown seeds probably cannot germinate on terraces more than a few meters from the water's edge, which may explain why it is absent from canyon-bound reaches.

## Key Findings

1. **Repeated formation and destruction:** The bosque formed on a terrace created by a boulder dam from [[Dry Canyon]]. It was then removed by sustained flooding in 1978–79. The authors describe this as a repeated cycle, with the earlier bosque likely removed in 1905–16.
2. **Area loss:** The terrace and bosque lost about 5 ha in 1978–79 (1.5 ha in March 1978 and 3.5 ha in December 1978–January 1979). The authors estimated that an equivalent earlier terrace of about 18 ha had lost roughly 8 ha in the earlier flood.
3. **Alluvial cones as drivers:** Boulder-rich cones from ephemeral side canyons temporarily dam desert rivers. They deflect flow, create rapids, and trap finer sediments, thereby initiating terraces.
4. **Vegetation feedback:** Mesquite and other colonizing shrubs trap silt and raise the surface, which can separate the terrace from the water table.
5. **Mesquite growth:** Mesquite grew to about 25 cm diameter in roughly 60 years, consistent with a [[Gavin 1973]] estimate for floodplains in southeastern Arizona.
6. **Saltcedar exclusion:** The authors hypothesize that the dry, sediment-rich surfaces of high terraces, which lie well above the water table, exclude saltcedar, because its seeds need prolonged saturation to germinate.
7. **Broader implications:** The loss of floodplain bosques in Arizona reflects human activities (diversion, pumping, clearing) and natural events (channel incision, lowered water tables). Understanding these processes is important for managing remaining riparian habitat.
