# Effects of Commercial Fishing on the Population of Smallmouth Buffalo in Elephant Butte Lake, New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1970 Master of Science thesis by Thomas Monroe Moody (New Mexico State University, Wildlife Science) evaluates how commercial removal of [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*) affected the population of [[Elephant Butte Lake]], a large warmwater reservoir on the [[Rio Grande]]. Between August 1963 and December 1969, a licensed commercial fisherman, B. C. Sparkman, removed 95,640 fish weighing 383,639 pounds. Multiple indicators (gill-net density and biomass, catch per net-unit, commercial catch trends, condition, growth, and mark-recapture estimates) all point to a reduced buffalo population, with faster growth and better condition in the survivors. The author concludes that commercial fishing should continue to hold the population at current or lower levels to benefit sport fisheries, while also providing a food fish resource that would otherwise go unused.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Class Actinopterygii, Order Cypriniformes, Family [[Catostomidae]], Genus *Ictiobus*.
- **Range:** Western Hudson Bay to the Ohio River drainage and south through the Gulf states and northeastern Mexico. In New Mexico it occurs only in the lower Pecos and Rio Grande drainages, and has not been recorded north of Elephant Butte Lake in the Rio Grande (Koster 1957).
- **Status in the lake:** Common; one of three major "rough fish" species. Rough fish are defined as species that compete with sport fish for space or food while contributing nothing to sport fisheries.
- **Management context:** Reducing buffalo numbers was a primary objective of a management plan proposed by Jester (1963). Because buffalo are palatable and marketable, the plan established a commercial fishery under a New Mexico Department of Game and Fish permit.
- **Study period:** Field sampling ran April 1968 to March 1970, compared with earlier data (Patterson 1968; Jester et al. 1969).
- **Funding:** New Mexico Department of Game and Fish, Commercial Fisheries Research and Development Project 6-11-R, with the Agricultural Experiment Station, New Mexico State University.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reservoir characteristics (see [[Elephant Butte Lake]])**
- Dam completed 1916 on the main stem of the Rio Grande, operated by the US Bureau of Reclamation for irrigation storage and hydropower.
- Capacity approximately 2,000,000 acre-feet; maximum surface area about 38,000 acres. Storage fluctuated from roughly 180,000 to 600,000 acre-feet (July 1964 to March 1970).
- Depth 70 to 95 feet at the dam, becoming shallower upstream (15 to 35 feet in the upstream third).
- Productivity: classified as marginally oligotrophic (Patterson 1968); temperate, Forel-Whipple Order II; dimictic per Hutchinson and Loeffler (1956). Persistent winds and silt-laden density currents prevent a stable thermocline.
- Temperatures: summer surface up to 85°F, bottom about 70°F; winter surface 32 to 45°F, bottom 40 to 42°F.
- Substrate: mainly sand and gravel with abundant silt; bedrock, boulders, rubble, and organic detritus in limited areas. The upstream third remains turbid.
- Water chemistry relatively stable since 1964.
- Habitat is adequate for buffalo, but growth is generally slower than in several other waters (Patterson 1968).

**Spawning ecology**
- Spawning began in late April 1968 at 68°F and peaked in mid-June at 76°F. In 1969, the earlier spawn began in late March at 56°F, peaking in early May at 64 to 65°F.
- Eggs are deposited over aquatic or inundated vegetation. Spawning coincided with spring rises in water level, which is consistent with earlier literature.
- Gill-net sampling found spawning in all lake areas at 10 to 20 feet depth, with all bottom types used. Commercial fishers found concentrations in recently flooded upstream shoreline vegetation at 4 to 8 feet.

**Community effects**
- Gizzard shad (the main forage fish for predatory species) rose from 6.2% to 20.1% of total catch weight (1964-65 to 1969-70), attributed largely to faster growth after reduced competition. Shad numbers did not show the same rise.

## Key Findings

**Population decline**
- Relative density fell from 13.9% of gill-net numbers (third-ranked, 1964-65) to 2.7% (seventh, 1969-70).
- Relative biomass fell from 36.2% (first) to 12.6% (fourth).
- Catch per net-unit (one unit = 25 ft of net set for 24 hours) fell from 0.40 to 0.29 buffalo per unit across 1964-65 to 1969-70, with a decreasing weight trend.
- Commercial catch per day fell from 169 fish (766 lb) in 1963 to 33 fish (119 lb) in 1969.
- Schnabel mark-recapture estimates (126 recoveries from 1,854 tagged fish; 63,114 total catch) gave a five-year mean of 571,600 buffalo, falling to 461,000 (1966-68) and 413,500 (1967-69). Total estimated reduction was 158,100, of which 87,000 was attributed to commercial harvest and 71,100 to other causes (mortality and reduced reproduction).

**Size structure and growth**
- Larger fish are being cropped. Buffalo 300 to 499 mm made up 88% of the 1969-70 sample, versus 80% at 400 to 549 mm in 1967-68.
- Mean weight of fish in the commercial catch fell from 4.5 lb (1963) to 3.6 lb (1969).
- Only 43% of 1969-70 buffalo exceeded 40 oz (the approximate minimum marketable weight), down from 55% in 1968-69.
- Coefficient of Condition (Kn) rose from 1.37 to 1.44 over the study, interpreted as reduced intraspecific competition.
- Length-weight relationships show that fish 200 to 500 mm were heavier in 1968-69 and 1969-70 than in 1964-68.
- Growth increased since 1966-67. All fish now reach harvestable size (≥400 mm) in Age Group VI rather than Age Group VIII (Patterson 1968). Growth is faster than Patterson's data at every age, with the largest gains at older ages.
- Sixty-four percent of the population is now susceptible to commercial gear, versus 54% in 1966-67.
- Age-growth was based on 90 scale-aged fish (April 1969 to March 1970). Body-scale regression: L = 4.50 + 1.15(S), r = 0.99.

**Reproduction and fecundity**
- Fecundity in 1969 ranged from 79,300 to 323,252 eggs per female, with a mean of 197,371 (n = 20 females, ages IV to IX).
- Fishing during spawning concentrations removed 45.7% of the 1968-69 commercial catch, which removes both individuals and their reproductive output.
- The author concludes that without sustained harvest, buffalo could rebound quickly into a management problem.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Commercial exploitation is an effective means of reducing buffalo numbers and yields a food resource otherwise wasted.
- The author recommends continuing commercial fishing at current or lower population levels to enhance the sport fishery.
- Game fish share of total catch weight rose from 22.3% (1964-65) to 33.7% (1969-70).
