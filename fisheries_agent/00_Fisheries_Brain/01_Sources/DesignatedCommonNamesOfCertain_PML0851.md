# Designated Common Names of Certain Marine Organisms of California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-nomenclature #marine-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game bulletin (Fish Bulletin 161, by Doyle E. Gates and Herbert W. Frey, Marine Resources Region) establishes official [[Common Names]] for marine and anadromous finfishes and selected marine invertebrates in California. The aim is consistent terminology for [[Commercial Fishing Reports]] and sport catch records, since inconsistent names had caused confusion and inaccurate catch statistics. The list covers fishes from the Mexican to the Oregon border and offshore at least 50 miles. It revises earlier official lists and adopts scientific nomenclature from [[Miller and Lea 1972]].

## Background & Description

**Purpose and legal basis**
- California began recording commercial landings in 1916. Early names reflected settlers' comparisons with familiar species in their homelands, producing a chaotic system in which one species had several names and one name covered several species.
- A 1919 state law required that names in landing records be in common usage, with the Fish and Game Commission empowered to decide that usage.
- The law was amended in 1933 and now forms Section 8013 of the [[California Fish and Game Code]]. It limits its application to commercial fish receipts.
- Section 45 of the Code defines "fish" to include wild fish, mollusks, crustaceans, invertebrates, and amphibians, including their parts, spawn, or ova.
- The Department applies the names to recreational catches as well, to promote uniformity in state publications and records.

**Earlier lists**
- [[Walford 1931]]: first comprehensive list of official names for commercially handled and fisher-relevant California fishes, including some invertebrates.
- [[Roedel]] lists: 1948, a revised list in 1953, and a revised, expanded version in 1962. Roedel also explained name changes in 1949.
- [[Shapovalov and Dill]]: names for all California freshwater fishes (1950), revised with Cordone in 1959.
- [[American Fisheries Society]] committee lists of United States and Canadian fish names (1948, 1960, 1970). These are widely used, and the 1970 list agrees with most California marine fish names. A few common and scientific names differ.

**Naming rules**
The authors stated that the guiding principle is accurate, comparable records. Names were chosen by these criteria:
1. Exclusive or widespread usage among sportsmen and commercial fishermen.
2. Usage in adjacent states, especially elsewhere on the Pacific coast.
3. Usage on other common-name lists.
4. Possible effects on market acceptance.
5. Brevity and descriptiveness.
6. Avoidance of names that suggest false relationships.

Where a name departs from Roedel (1962) or the American Fisheries Society (1970), an annotated explanation is given. Names that are not widely accepted are labeled as such.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Geographic scope:** The list covers shallow-water marine and anadromous finfishes within California waters, extending offshore at least 50 miles between the Mexican and Oregon borders.
- **Invertebrate coverage:** Only selected invertebrate groups are included. Species were chosen if they are likely to be taken by sport or commercial fishers, are commonly seen by casual shore visitors, or represent a particular group. Before this list, the Department had named only a few invertebrates, mainly those in Walford (1931).
- **Taxonomic arrangement:** Agnathans (jawless fishes) and sharks and rays follow the American Fisheries Society (1970) order. Bony fish (teleost) families follow Greenwood et al., with genera and species alphabetized. Mollusks and crustaceans follow their own classification sequences.
- **Source text limitations:** The extracted text is largely a garbled, tabular species checklist. Individual species entries, their scientific names, and any count of species are not reliably legible, so this note does not list them.

## Key Findings

1. **Official names are needed for accurate catch statistics.** Consistent common names are essential for the Department to understand the State's fisheries and to record each species' catch.
2. **Legal framework:** Section 8013 of the California Fish and Game Code requires that receipt names be those in common usage, with the Department empowered to designate them.
3. **Revised official list:** The present list adds many species that were absent from earlier official lists of California marine fishes. It also includes a third column of other common names by which each species is or has been known, to aid identification.
4. **Alignment with national usage:** Most California marine fish names agree with the 1970 American Fisheries Society list. Where they differ, the Department provides annotations.
5. **Expanded invertebrate coverage:** Selected marine invertebrates, including mollusks and crustaceans, now receive official common names, extending beyond the few covered by Walford (1931).
6. **Standards for future names:** Names must be commonly used, brief, descriptive, and unlikely to mislead about relationships. Market effects are also considered.
7. **Ongoing revision:** The list is a continuation of a series of revisions from 1931 to 1970. It is meant to be updated as nomenclature changes.
