# Response to Nowak, Phillips and Henry, and Wayne: Use of Molecular Characters in Hybridization Biology

#canidae #conservation-genetics #canis-rufus

## Executive Summary

This letter, published in *Conservation Biology* (Vol. 6, No. 4, December 1992), is a rejoinder by [[Douglas]], [[Marsh]], [[DeMarais]], [[Dowling]] and [[Minckley]] to critiques of their earlier letter. The authors focus on two linked issues: (1) how molecular characters should be used to identify species and their hybrids, and (2) the role of hybridization in conservation. The debate centers on the taxonomic status of the [[Red Wolf]] (*Canis rufus*), whose mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) closely resembles that of the [[Coyote]] (*C. latrans*) and [[Gray Wolf]] (*C. lupus*). The authors argue that mtDNA alone cannot resolve hybrid origin, that morphological and nuclear evidence must be weighed, and that conservation decisions should protect evolutionary entities regardless of species-concept disputes.

The document is not about fish or aquatic systems. It is a taxonomic and conservation-policy exchange, and this note records it as such.

## Background & Description

- **Subject organisms:** The red wolf, a canid whose taxonomic status is disputed. It is considered either a distinct species, a taxon of hybrid origin (coyote × gray wolf), or a descendant of an intermediate wolf-like ancestor.
- **Triggering study:** [[Wayne and Jenks 1991]], an mtDNA analysis published in *Nature* that was interpreted as supporting extensive hybridization in the endangered red wolf. The authors' earlier letter (Dowling et al. 1992) questioned that interpretation.
- **Responding parties:** [[Nowak]] (1992), who argued the red wolf is not a hybrid; [[Phillips and Henry]] (1992), who commented on the red wolf; and [[Wayne]] (1992), who addressed the use of morphological and molecular characters in species-status studies.
- **Legal context:** The [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of the United States, which protects "distinctive forms" of organisms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The letter's arguments draw on evolutionary and conservation biology more broadly:

- **Rate variation in mtDNA:** Wayne and Jenks expected red wolf mtDNA to differ from gray wolf mtDNA according to a "standard" rate calibrated from primates ([[Hillis and Moritz 1990]]). The authors note that reduced mtDNA evolution has been documented in other taxa, including turtles ([[Avise et al. 1992]]).
- **Morphological versus molecular diversity:** Some morphologically distinct taxa show little or no mtDNA divergence, such as certain [[Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon*) species ([[Turner 1974]]). Others are morphologically similar but molecularly divergent, such as [[Plethodontid Salamanders]]. The authors argue that pupfish and plethodontids are equally valid species that diverged at different sets of loci.
- **Hybridization examples:** The letter cites hybridization in cyprinid fishes ([[Dowling et al. 1989]]; [[Meagher and Dowling 1991]]), the [[Gila Seminuda]] case of introgressive hybridization ([[DeMarais et al. 1992]]), and the introduction of South American cougars that introgressed the [[Florida Panther]]'s mtDNA.
- **Mitochondrial lineage sorting:** Random sorting of mtDNA lineages can produce discordance between organismal and gene phylogenies ([[Neigel and Avise 1986]]; [[Pamilo and Nei 1988]]).

## Key Findings

1. **Hybrid origin is not yet demonstrated.** The authors "neither rejected nor accepted" the hypothesis that the red wolf is of hybrid origin. They conclude that available data are insufficient to distinguish competing hypotheses. They concur that the red wolf is not a recent hybrid product, but await more data on the other two alternatives.

2. **Shared mtDNA does not settle the question.** The lack of distinctiveness between red and gray wolf mtDNA is the only current evidence inconsistent with a long-established intermediate taxon. The authors argue that the discordance can be explained by a reduced mtDNA evolutionary rate in wolves.

3. **Morphology matters.** Multivariate analysis of cranial measurements identified the red wolf as distinct from both coyote and gray wolf ([[Nowak 1992]]; [[Wayne 1992]]). The authors criticize Wayne for ignoring this information, and they note that morphological intermediacy is neither necessary nor sufficient to demonstrate hybrid origin.

4. **Nuclear markers are needed.** The most appropriate test of hybrid origin is analysis of codominant, single-locus nuclear gene markers, as suggested by [[Phillips and Henry 1992]]. Joint consideration of additional characters is required.

5. **Hybrid taxa versus hybrids.** The authors distinguish a hybrid taxon, a self-perpetuating group of mixed ancestry following an independent evolutionary trajectory, from an individual hybrid. A hybrid taxon warrants protection, while individual hybrids may not, unless they are part of such a lineage.

6. **ESA protection applies to lineages, not only species.** Wayne's reliance on the phylogenetic species concept is criticized. The authors argue that legislation was designed to protect distinctive forms "irrespective of mode of origin," so the species concept chosen is "irrelevant" to conservation decisions.

7. **Conservation priorities.** Conservation biologists should not let long-standing disputes over species definitions delay efforts to conserve evolutionary entities. The authors state that the red wolf is morphologically distinct and "requires recognition," and they leave the final taxonomic decision to mammalian systematists. They conclude that conservation biologists must continue to protect entities like the red wolf to preserve evolutionary potential.

8. **Hybridization management.** Hybridization can be evolutionarily important but is not always beneficial. When sufficient numbers of a taxon persist to preserve genetic integrity, hybrids may be removed. When a taxon is rare, protecting all individuals, including introgressed ones, may be justified to conserve remaining genetic diversity ([[Echelle 1991]]). The authors state they do not advocate preserving domestic canid hybrids at the expense of wild species.
