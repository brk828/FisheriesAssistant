# Cold Shock Effects on Larval Colorado Squawfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ptychocheilus

## Executive Summary

This document is a manuscript by Charles R. Berry, Jr., submitted with a cover letter to peer reviewer Dr. Paul C. Marsh (Center for Environmental Studies, Arizona State University). The cover letter is from the [[South Dakota Cooperative Fish & Wildlife Research Unit]], South Dakota State University. The manuscript reports a laboratory experiment on how abrupt and gradual temperature drops affect larval [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Abrupt drops of 5, 10, and 15°C were tested in 5 minutes, and gradual drops in 240 to 300 minutes. Mortality was significant only for 14-day-old fish exposed to a 15°C abrupt drop. Older (40-day-old) fish were less susceptible. Behavioral disruption followed the 10°C and 15°C abrupt drops. The author argues that these results can guide water-release and power-plant planning in the [[Colorado River Basin]].

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It was historically widely distributed in the Colorado River and its major tributaries. After water development on the Colorado River system, populations declined. The species is protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The manuscript attributes part of the decline to temperature changes below large dams.

The species appears to perform best at temperatures above 20°C. Adults and juveniles tolerate temperature changes poorly, though a sudden temperature change can be fatal to larval stages. Larvae have been found in backwaters at water temperatures from 19 to 26°C. Mortality rises dramatically at temperatures below 10°C during embryonic development. The author notes that cold reservoir releases could trigger a cold-shock event if larvae drift into a cold-water plume after hatching.

The study tested the response of two age classes to a single cold shock:
- 14-day-old larvae, tested in 1984 and 1985.
- 40-day-old larvae, tested in 1984.

Eggs and sperm were obtained from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Larvae were shipped to Logan, Utah, and held at the [[Utah Water Resources Research Laboratory]] at 20°C in flow-through water. They were fed brine shrimp nauplii.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Test conditions:**
- Aquaria: 17-liter plastic aquaria, with flow of 0.25 liters per minute.
- Photoperiod: 14 hours light to 10 hours dark.
- Water quality: hardness 174 mg/liter, pH 7.1, dissolved oxygen about 7 to 10 mg/liter.
- Replication: 1984 used two aquaria with 100 larvae per treatment. 1985 used three aquaria with 50 larvae per aquarium.
- Temperature control: In 1984, water was mixed to the desired temperature by an in-line mixer, which may have caused partial supersaturation of dissolved gases. In 1985, hot and cold water were mixed by control valves before entering the aquaria.

**Shock protocol:** Temperature was decreased by 5, 10, or 15°C over 5 minutes (abrupt) or 240 to 300 minutes (gradual). Fish were held at the shocked temperature for 19 to 24 hours, returned to 20°C over 96 hours, and then observed for 21 days. Control aquaria were not shocked. Behavior was recorded daily during the shock and then daily afterward.

**Literature context:** The author compares the results with cold-shock studies on other fishes. Cited examples include [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Young American Shad]], [[Northern Pike]], and [[Channel Catfish]]. Studies cited in the manuscript suggest that faster temperature decreases produce greater mortality. Cold-stressed prey also become more vulnerable to predation.

## Key Findings

- **Mortality:** Mortality was statistically significant (P < 0.01) only for 14-day-old fish exposed to an abrupt 15°C decrease in 5 minutes. The manuscript's table reports percent mortality by age, treatment, and year. Individual values are not reproduced in this note.
- **Age sensitivity:** 14-day-old fish were more susceptible than 40-day-old fish. The 40-day-old fish showed no mortality difference from controls after shocks of 5, 10, or 15°C.
- **Behavior:** After a 10°C abrupt decrease, fish showed initial hyperactivity and then lost equilibrium, with fish settling to the bottom. Most mortality in these tanks (reported as 95%) occurred within the first 48 hours. After a 10°C gradual decrease, fish did not lose equilibrium, but surface activity and avoidance of the water column persisted for about 14 hours. Fish shocked by 15°C abrupt decreases showed behavioral changes that were greater than those of the 10°C abrupt group.
- **Timing of deaths:** Deaths in fish exposed to 5 and 10°C abrupt decreases occurred randomly during the experiment, rather than mostly in the first 48 hours.
- **Control mortality:** Control mortality was high in 14-day-old fish in 1984. The author suggests that temperature may have been a factor in this result.
- **Interpretation:** The author links the post-shock behavior to "cold coma," a physiological state associated with osmoregulatory dysfunction and loss of motor function. Fish that recovered from 10 and 15°C shocks probably did not die in the field.
- **Management implications:** The author proposes that the results could provide operating criteria for water-release projects. Discharges from hydroelectric powerhouses could be scheduled to avoid shocking larvae drifting from tributary or backwater areas. The author states that the results may generalize to other endemic Colorado River fishes, including [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Razorback Sucker]], which share similar final temperature preferences.
