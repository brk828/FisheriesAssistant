# Parasites of Fishes from Leavenworth County State Lake, Kansas, and Intestinal Casts in a Niobrara Elopid

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two short studies from the *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science* (1957). The first, by William D. Wilson, reports the degree of parasitism and parasite distribution in 62 fishes of 11 species from [[Leavenworth County State Lake]], Kansas, collected in 1952–1953. Of these fish, 41 (66 per cent) harbored at least one parasite species. The dominant parasite was the strigeid trematode [[Posthodiplostomum Minimum]], which caused heavy infections in sunfishes. The second, by Halsey W. Miller, Jr., describes a Niobrara Formation fossil of [[Pachyrhizodus]], unique among known specimens for preserving internal casts of the stomach and intestine.

## Background & Description

**Study lake.** Leavenworth County State Lake is an artificial impoundment of 175 acres with a capacity of 3,932 acre-feet. It was filled in 1931, opened to fishing in 1933, and drained in 1947 by the Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Department. Some fish survived in a residual pool of 3 to 20 acres, which was seined. The lake was refilled, restocked, and reopened in 1951. Wilson's samples were collected in October and November 1952 and in March and June 1953, when the lake was full. The lake lacked large shallow areas, had few weedy zones, and had low plankton abundance.

**Fish sampled.** Eleven species were examined: [[White Sucker]], [[Carp]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Warmouth]], [[Bluegill]], [[White Crappie]], [[Black Crappie]], and [[Freshwater Drum]]. Fish were measured, weighed, and scale-sampled. External surfaces were checked first, then viscera were examined under a dissecting microscope. Flesh and blood smears were negative for parasites. Parasites were fixed, stained, and mounted using standard helminthological methods. Identifications were confirmed by Professor Ralph V. Bangham of Wooster College, except the Acanthocephala, which he did not examine.

**Fossil specimen.** Miller examined a *Pachyrhizodus* specimen (No. 3018, Fort Hays Kansas State College Museum). It was loaned by curator George F. Sternberg and was collected by Rolli Sanders from the south bank of [[Hackberry Creek]], Trego County, about 14 miles southeast of Collyer, Kansas. Eight species of *Pachyrhizodus* have been described from the [[Niobrara Formation]] in Kansas, though Miller notes that many were based on fragmentary material and only one or two may remain valid.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Netting effort and catch.** Catches were small. In October, November, and March, 102 fish were taken: 66 in the 1-inch gill net (mean 9.4 fish per overnight setting), 14 in the 1½-inch gill net (two per setting), and 22 in hoop nets (2.4 per setting). Most of the 1-inch catch was white crappie (47) and black crappie (7). In June, the 1-inch net yielded 394 fish (290 white crappie, 24 black crappie), and the hoop nets yielded 75 fish (66 black crappie, 8 white crappie). The scarcity of white crappie in the 1½-inch net suggests few large crappie were present. Scale readings indicated slow growth, despite the recent drainage and restocking.

**Factors limiting parasite diversity.** Wilson attributes the low parasite diversity to several factors: the fish population was new (dating largely from the 1947 drainage), lake productivity appeared low, and other hosts in parasite life cycles may have been scarce. Seasonal sampling limits may also have contributed, since parasitism is generally highest in late summer and early fall. Protozoan and arthropod parasites were absent, and trematodes were few compared with other studied lakes.

**Life cycle of P. minimum.** Adults occur in fish-eating water birds, including the Great Blue Heron. Eggs are passed in droppings, hatch into miracidia in about 3 weeks, and infect the snail [[Physa Gyrina]]. Cercariae emerge, penetrate fish, and encyst as metacercariae in the liver, spleen, and mesenteries after 18–21 days. Infected fish must be eaten by birds to complete the cycle.

## Key Findings

**Overall parasitism**
- 41 of 62 fish (66 per cent) had at least one parasite species.
- Parasite taxa recorded include trematodes, cestodes, nematodes, and acanthocephalans. Only six parasite species were involved, and protozoans and arthropods were absent.

**Key parasites**
- [[Posthodiplostomum Minimum]] (metacercariae) was the most important parasite. It occurred in 11 white crappie, 6 black crappie, 3 bluegill, 2 warmouth, 2 largemouth bass, and 1 channel catfish. Many bluegill, white crappie, and largemouth bass had livers in which at least half the volume was cysts. Infections were heavier in October than in June. Its occurrence in channel catfish appears to be a first record.
- [[Proteocephalus Ambloplitis]] (plerocercoids) occurred in white crappie (3), black crappie (3), largemouth bass (1), and channel catfish (2). Infections in white crappie were light.
- [[Contracaecum Spiculigerum]] (larvae) was found in one channel catfish and one largemouth bass. The largemouth bass had three larvae sheathed in the mesentery.
- [[Camallanus Oxycephalus]] was found in black crappie (1), largemouth bass (1, three worms in the large intestine), and freshwater drum (1).
- [[Neoechinorhynchus Cylindratus]] was found in one largemouth bass, which carried 56 worms in the small intestine.
- A likely new cestode species of *Caryophyllaeus* was recovered from carp. Bangham reportedly has specimens and a description was in press.
- An adult copepod of the family Lernaeopodidae found in one carp was probably accidental.

**Comparisons with other lakes**
- The overall infection rate (66 per cent) is close to the 58 per cent from [[Lake Erie]] (Bangham and Hunter, 1939) and the 66 per cent from Buckeye Lake, Ohio (Bangham, 1940b).
- Infection in [[Reelfoot Lake]] reached 95 per cent. A low of 39 per cent was reported from streams and lakes in six central states (Essex and Hunter, 1926).
- Forty of the 62 fish were sunfishes (Centrarchidae), and 78 per cent of them were infected. This is close to the 74 per cent reported for 395 centrarchids from Lake Erie. Note that the species-level counts for centrarchids in this document sum to 41, not 40. The discrepancy is in the source.
- In this lake, 58 per cent of parasitic infections in centrarchids were attributable to *P. minimum*.
- Wilson's study recorded 102 netted fish and 62 examined. Parasite species counts were far lower than those for the Lake Erie (96 species from 2,156 fish), Algonquin Park (75 species from 676 fish), and Reelfoot Lake (approximately 83 species from 588 fish) surveys.

**Pathological effects**
- Hunter (1937) reported that heavy *P. minimum* infections killed common sunfish, *Lepomis gibbosus*. Wilson suggests the heavy infections in this study likely interfered with host metabolism.

**Fossil intestinal casts (Miller)**
- The *Pachyrhizodus* specimen (No. 3018) is unique in preserving internal casts of its stomach and intestine. Such soft-tissue preservation is rare in Niobrara vertebrate fossils, most of which lack soft parts.
- The specimen was found in Trego County, Kansas, and is described as an elopid fish.
- Miller's text ends at the taxonomic heading for *Pachyrhizodus*. Descriptive results and conclusions are not included in the source.
