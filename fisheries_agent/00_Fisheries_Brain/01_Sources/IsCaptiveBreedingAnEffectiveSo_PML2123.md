# Captive Breeding as a Conservation Tool for Endemic and Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review by Jean Claude Philippart (University of Liège) assesses whether [[Captive Breeding]] and reintroduction can effectively preserve endemic and endangered freshwater fishes, with emphasis on the Mediterranean region and examples from southwestern USA. The paper concludes that captive breeding is an effective short-term rescue measure but must remain temporary, while habitat protection and restoration proceed. It stresses strict [[Genetic Management]] constraints, including avoidance of [[Hybridisation and Introgression]], maintenance of founder diversity, and control of unintentional selection, as well as biological quality criteria for released fish.

## Background & Description

The paper defines captive breeding as the propagation of species, subspecies or populations threatened with extinction, followed by reintroduction. Reviews cited include about 35 southwestern US fishes recognized as needing artificial propagation (Rinne et al., 1986) and a European list of endangered taxa identified by [[Lelek]] (1987), spanning [[Sturgeon]] (Acipenseridae), salmonids, cyprinids, [[Northern Pike]], loaches and [[Wels Catfish]].

Three facility types are described:
- Aquaria, used for small tropical ornamentals and endemic cichlids from African lakes.
- Semi-natural ponds, used for small live-bearers, pupfishes and spring cyprinids in North America.
- Hatcheries and farms, the most widely used approach for lake and river fishes.

The main case study is the [[Tihange Fish Breeding Centre]] in Belgium, run by the University of Liège, which holds 15 European and 14 tropical species. It draws on three water sources (natural river, cooling water at 19–40°C, aquifer at 14–16°C) to achieve thermal regimes suited to cold, cool and warm water species. The [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico has bred most endangered southwestern fishes since 1974, with 48 earthen ponds and four raceways.

Three founder-stock methods are outlined: (1) capturing mature wild adults and spawning them immediately or after holding; (2) capturing subadults and maturing them in captivity; (3) rearing wild eggs or juveniles to maturity, which yields well-adapted captive stock but takes long (over 10 years for sturgeons).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors note that threats addressed by priority field actions include pollution, water diversion, overfishing, introduced species and non-native stocking. Translocation to new natural habitats is preferred when feasible; a full relocation to hatcheries is the last resort. The paper stresses that captive breeding must never substitute for habitat conservation.

Genetic context examples:
- [[Brown Trout]] in Corsica (*Salmo trutta macrostigma*) showed introgression from mainland stocking (Guyomard & Krieg, 1986).
- Marbled trout (*Salmo trutta marmorata*) hybridizes with introduced brown trout in northern Italy and Slovenia.
- *Barbus meridionalis* populations in southern France are almost monomorphic (Berrebi et al., 1988).
- *Aphanius iberus* in the Ebro Delta shows 1% genetic diversity within a region but 21% between two regions, with total differentiation of 22% and mean heterozygosity H = 0.029 (Garcia-Marin et al., 1990).

## Key Findings

**Captive breeding success and limits**
- Captive breeding and stocking have produced positive results across fish sizes, habitats and regions.
- The paper argues artificial propagation must not be considered an effective long-term safeguard for most species and strains, and should be used only after in situ options are exhausted.

**Genetic management**
- Key problems are: identifying the genetic identity of the resource, establishing founder stocks with maximum diversity, and preventing drift, inbreeding and unintentional selection.
- Interspecific introgression was documented in the [[Razorback Sucker]] (hybrid with *Catostomus latipinnis*, 0–3% by Buth et al., 1987) and in a cutthroat trout subspecies in Nevada (Bartley, 1992).
- Chevassus (1989) estimated that about 99% of variation could be captured with 90 breeders (15 females, sperm from five males per female).
- Inbreeding depression: a 25% loss of heterozygosity causes about 16% depression in main fitness traits (Gall, 1987); growth in salmonids falls by 0.6–0.8% per 1% heterozygosity loss.
- Guyomard (1989) suggests injecting 50 wild males into 50 captive females per generation to maintain diversity in brown trout.
- Founder stock of *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* with zero heterozygosity failed; replacement with a stock at H = 3.7% succeeded (Quattro & Vrijenhoek, 1989).

**Restocking and reintroduction**
- Three stocking types are defined: restoration, enhancement and translocation.
- Belgian barbel (*Barbus barbus*) restocking in the Meuse: about 85,000 barbel (mostly 3–20 cm) stocked 1983–1989; in one tributary, 11,256 fish were stocked annually from 1983 to 1989, and the population was judged self-reproducing by June 1989 (spawning males, females and fry recorded).
- Landlocked salmon (*Salmo salar* m. *sebago*) in Finland was rescued through captive breeding and fry restocking from 1982; 0.9 million fry were produced in 1985.

**Case histories (southwestern USA)**
- *Ptychocheilus lucius* (Colorado Squawfish): 75 adults taken from the wild, producing over 100,000 fry restocked in 1987 (monitoring to be evaluated in 1994).
- *Gila elegans* (Bonytail): 34 wild individuals captured 1976–1988; 110,000 fish released 1981–1987.
- *Gila nigrescens* (Chihuahua Chub): captive breeding started 1989; reintroduction attempted at only one site.
- *Plagopterus argentissimus* (Woundfin): about 1000 wild fish brought into captivity 1978–1987; no reintroduction yet because of an Asian tapeworm carried by hatchery fish.
- *Cyprinodon bovinus* (Leon Springs Pupfish): founder population of 80 adults in 1976 produced 1000 young after one year in captivity.
- *Gambusia nobilis* (Pecos Gambusia): reintroduction into natural habitat in Texas yielded over 1000 young per year.

**Regulatory and health safeguards**
- The US Endangered Species Act prohibits reintroducing hatchery fish into natural habitats where endangered species still reproduce.
- The Netherlands bans captive breeding of the European catfish (*Silurus glanis*) to protect its relict population.
- Hatchery stocking poses disease and parasite transfer risks; release must follow precise protocols.

**Conclusions and warnings**
- Captive breeding is an effective short-term rescue measure but must be temporary.
- Priority should be given to necessity and feasibility studies for the most endangered species, and to a network of breeding stations under genetic management.
- Funding should treat commercially valuable and non-valuable species equally, with the objective of conserving biodiversity.
- The author warns that the ease of captive breeding may tempt premature reintroductions, and that it may reduce effort on habitat and ecosystem restoration.
