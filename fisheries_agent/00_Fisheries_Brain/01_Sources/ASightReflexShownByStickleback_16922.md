# A Sight Reflex Shown by Sticklebacks

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gasterosteus

## Executive Summary

This short experimental paper by [[Walter E. Garrey]] describes a visually triggered orientation and schooling reflex in [[Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus bispinosus*) held in the aquaria of the [[United States Fish Commission]] at Woods Hole, Massachusetts. Observations were made in the summers of 1900 and 1904. Sticklebacks aligned their bodies and swam parallel to a hand-moved white object, moving opposite to its direction. The author concludes that the reflex arises from an apparent motion of the stationary visual field, produced by the fish fixating the moving stimulus, and links it to the general [[Compensatory Eye and Body Movement]] principle. The paper reports no sample sizes or quantitative statistics.

## Background & Description

- **Study subject:** Schools of sticklebacks displayed annually at the Fish Commission aquaria. Their characteristic swimming drew casual observers' attention.
- **Baseline behavior:** Fish oriented with their long axes parallel and swam in a course parallel to, but opposite to, a walking observer. They continued at a slower rate after the observer stopped, and reversed when the observer retraced their steps.
- **Apparatus:** Aquaria about six feet long. A cylindrical glass vessel suspended within a striped galvanized iron dish on a turntable was used in later tests.
- **Acknowledgement:** The author thanked the research staff of the Fish Commission for access to the aquaria.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stimulus-driven movement:** Fish responded to a horizontally moved white object held by a stationary observer, moving slowly and uniformly opposite to the object. The movement could be arrested or reversed at any instant and was independent of aquarium walls.
- **Other taxa tested:** Two species of *Fundulus* (*F. margalis* and *F. heteroclitus*), small eels and young sea robins did not show the reflex. Young [[Atlantic Mackerel]] behaved similarly to sticklebacks, though less precisely.
- **Field confirmation:** Dr. Gaylord Clark reported positive results for the reflex in open water off the wharves, though the reflex was not easily obtained in small aquaria.
- **Flatfish effect:** A [[Flounder]] swimming on the bottom beneath the sticklebacks also triggered the characteristic reflex.
- **Rheotropism interaction:** Under a gentle circular water current, fish oriented upstream ([[Rheotropism]]). Rotating the visual field opposite to the current increased this effect, while rotation matching the current brought fish to rest. Dr. [[E. P. Lyon]] independently showed orientation in still water driven only by a changing visual field.

## Key Findings

1. **Reflex orientation:** Sticklebacks align with and move parallel to a horizontally moving stimulus, with direction controlled by the stimulus direction. Motion can be reversed or stopped at will, in open water as well as at the walls.
2. **Directional control:**
   - Anterior or posterior placement of the stimulus does not change orientation, though movement is toward or away from the object respectively.
   - Vertical stimulus movement shifts the horizontal path up or down.
   - Horizontal movement above the fish deflects their course left or right, matching the stimulus direction.
3. **Group behavior:** Schools split into two "platoons" that move toward the aquarium ends when stimuli approach from opposite directions. Stimuli moved outward from the center cause the platoons to charge and recombine, which the author describes as having "military precision."
4. **Individual orientation:** Fish headed toward the stimulus can be driven in any direction. Fish at an angle to the motion turn toward the parallel position, with the direction of turn depending on initial heading. Quick stimuli cause turns through the longer arc, slow stimuli through the shorter arc.
5. **Apparent lateral motion:** A stimulus moving along a line appears to move laterally to viewers outside that line, which the author proposes as a cause of platoon formation.
6. **Contrast with compensatory reflex:** Sticklebacks appear to be an exception to the general rule that animals use compensatory movements to hold the visual field constant. However, in turntable tests with a striped field, fish did keep a constant visual field by swimming with the rotation.
7. **Proposed mechanism:** Fixation of the moving stimulus creates an apparent motion of the whole visual field in the opposite direction. The author offers this as a tentative hypothesis explaining the reflex, comparing it to the apparent backward motion of a landscape viewed from a moving train.
8. **Limitations:** The paper gives no sample sizes, counts, or statistical analysis. Results are qualitative and descriptive, and the author notes the reflex was not reliably obtained in small aquaria.
