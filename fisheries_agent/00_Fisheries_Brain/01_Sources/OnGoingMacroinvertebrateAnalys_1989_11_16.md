## Executive Summary

This paper presents preliminary results of an ongoing (initiated 1987) macroinvertebrate and habitat monitoring program at [[Box Canyon Creek]], the eleventh-largest spring in the United States, located in Southern Idaho and designated an Area of Critical Environmental Concern (ACEC) under Bureau of Land Management (BLM) administration. The monitoring program was established in response to a private developer's proposal to divert water from the creek, and builds on baseline data intended to assess impacts of a historic 1973 diversion. A central ecological finding is the detection of the invasive New Zealand mudsnail, [[Potamopyrgus antipodarum]], in Box Canyon Creek more than two years after it was documented in the adjacent [[Snake River]]. Using the [[Biological Condition Index]] (BCI) alongside diversity indices, standing crop measurements, snorkel surveys, photographic transects, and sediment/vegetation sampling, the authors conclude that the ACEC portion of the creek is under significant ecological stress, likely driven by pollution-laden inflow and reduced instream flows, raising concern for protection of Federal Candidate species, including an endemic sculpin and several mollusks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Box Canyon Creek is a large spring-fed stream system, functioning as a stable, cold, nutrient-influenced freshwater habitat — a rare and ecologically significant spring ecosystem in the arid Snake River Plain region.
- **Management context**: The lower (BLM-administered) portion of the creek is designated an [[Area of Critical Environmental Concern]] (ACEC), specifically managed to protect populations of Federal Candidate species, including mollusks and the endemic sculpin.
- **Anthropogenic pressures**: 
  - A historic water diversion occurred in 1973, and a new diversion proposal by a private developer prompted the current monitoring effort.
  - Instream flow reduction from past developments is implicated as a driver of adverse ecological impact.
  - Pollution-laden water entering the spring system is suspected to be contributing to poor biological condition, evidenced by the presence of pollution-tolerant macroinvertebrate taxa.
- **Invasive species dynamics**: [[Potamopyrgus antipodarum]] invaded Box Canyon Creek via connectivity with the [[Snake River]], appearing in the creek over two years after being documented in the river — indicating a lag in colonization/detection and highlighting the vulnerability of isolated spring ecosystems to invasive species originating in connected larger water bodies.
- **Trophic/water quality interactions**: The proposed diversion is hypothesized to potentially reduce dilution of nutrient-rich water, which could exacerbate eutrophication-related stress currently being tracked via BCI indicator species.
- **Monitoring methods** applied to characterize this niche include:
  - Underwater snorkel observation
  - Photographic transects
  - Visual examination of special habitat sites
  - Sediment and vegetative sampling
  - Water quality data collection (in cooperation with the EPA)

## Key Findings

- Box Canyon Creek is the **11th largest spring in the United States**, and its lower reach is an ACEC managed by BLM specifically to protect Federal Candidate mollusk and sculpin species.
- The BLM initiated a **complex, ongoing macroinvertebrate and habitat sampling program in 1987**, partly in response to a new proposed water diversion and to build on data related to a **1973 historic diversion**.
- The invasive snail **[[Potamopyrgus antipodarum]]** was documented in Box Canyon Creek **more than two years after** its known occurrence in the adjacent **[[Snake River]]**, marking a significant biological invasion event within the monitored system.
- The **[[Diversity Index]]** and **Standing Crop** values for the creek were rated **good to fair**.
- In contrast, the **[[Biological Condition Index]] (BCI)** value fell into the **"poor condition"** range, indicating ecological stress despite otherwise moderate diversity/biomass metrics.
- The poor BCI outcome is attributed to **pollution-laden water inflow**, evidenced by the presence of multiple **pollution-tolerant macroinvertebrate species**.
- The findings raise concern that the **existing ACEC management plan**, designed to protect Federal Candidate species, may be insufficient given current stress levels, and that a **proposed new diversion could further reduce instream flow and dilution capacity** for nutrient-rich inputs.
- No comparative monitoring data are currently being collected from the **headwater reaches** of the creek, limiting the ability to contextualize impacts on the lower ACEC segment relative to unimpacted upstream conditions.
- Data from this monitoring program, combined with **EPA water quality data**, are intended to support a future **formal impact analysis** of the proposed diversion project.
