# Fishes of Hunter's Hole, Yuma County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #mugilidae

## Executive Summary

This survey by W. L. Minckley and Randy T. McNatt (Arizona State University) describes the fish and selected aquatic fauna of [[Hunter's Hole]], a young, interconnected set of pools in Yuma County, Arizona, sampled by [[Gill Netting]] in April 5–7, 1974 and combined with [[Electrofishing]] records from 1968–1974. The site supports 17 fish species, dominated by [[Striped Mullet]], [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Common Carp]]. Most native fishes of the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]] are extinct or nearly so here, while several non-native species are established. The authors conclude that netting and electrofishing are highly selective, so their numbers are only a qualitative, relative picture of the fauna.

## Background & Description

- **Origin and setting:** Hunter's Hole lies in an old meander of the [[Colorado River]] and was not present on 1938 aerial photographs, so it formed after that date through river meandering or other physical change.
- **Water source:** It once received inflow from local irrigation drains and the 21-mile wasteway. It is now fed mainly by seepage from the Colorado River or nearby irrigation canals.
- **Size and depth:** About 12 surface acres of water, with a maximum depth of about 19 feet in the larger pond.
- **Vegetation:** Sparse submerged plants (spiny naiad, *Najas marina*, and muskweed, *Chara* sp.) occur in open water. Banks are densely vegetated by large sedge (*Scirpus californicus*), cattail (*Typha domingensis*) and reed (*Phragmites communis*), followed by willows, rare saltcedar and cottonwoods.
- **Native fauna:** The authors list 11 native fishes for the region (Table 1). Seven likely frequented or inhabited habitats like Hunter's Hole, but only two were recently common, and several are known only from single specimens. Examples include the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado River Chub]], [[Woundfin]], [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Gila Topminnow]].
- **Sampling history:** Collections in the lower Colorado River date back before 1900, and Hunter's Hole collections began in 1967.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Historically, collections used seines, gill and trammel nets, trap nets and electrofishing. The authors stress that each gear type is selective by species and size, so absolute abundance or biomass per acre cannot be estimated without in-depth study.

**Salinity and origins.** Three salinity-tolerant species (mullet, threadfin shad and carp) dominated the samples. Mullet are typically marine and catadromous, and the authors argue their presence reflects both gear bias and possible local reproduction.

**Mullet reproduction.** Juvenile mullet (*querimana* stage) were found, and fish up to 3 pounds reached as far upstream as Imperial Dam. Bureau of Reclamation surveys in August 1973 and June 1974 found a land plug about 4 miles south of the Colorado River–Rio Hardy junction, blocking flow to the [[Gulf of California]]. The authors conclude that reproduction in the river, a backwater or an irrigation drain must have occurred, which would be the first record of mullet reproducing outside marine habitats.

**Introduced species.** Several species were introduced or spread widely:
- [[Threadfin Shad]] (introduced to Lake Mead in 1953 and Lake Havasu in 1954) became a major forage fish.
- [[Common Carp]] escaped from ponds before 1885.
- [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) is a "voracious larvivore" that may have contributed to the decline of Gila topminnow and desert pupfish.
- [[Flathead Catfish]] were placed in the lower Colorado River in the 1960s and now form a major part of the fishery.
- [[Mozambique Mouthbrooder]] (*Tilapia mossambica*) was surprisingly rare here.

**Other aquatic organisms and vertebrates.** The invertebrate fauna included the crayfish *Procambarus clarkii*, the introduced Asiatic clam *Corbicula*, odonate naiads and adults, baetid mayflies, culicid and chironomid flies, and aquatic oligochaetes. A plankton pulse, mainly rotifers, was under way in April 1974. Vertebrates included abundant bullfrogs (*Rana catesbeiana*), softshell turtles (*Trionyx spiniferus emoryi*), muskrats (*Ondatra zibethicus*) and beavers (*Castor canadensis*).

| Common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) | 400 | 34% |
| Threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*) | 350 | 3% |
| Yellow bullhead (*Ictalurus natalis*) | 23 | 1% |
| Bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*) | 11 | trace |
| Mozambique mouthbrooder (*Tilapia mossambica*) | 5 | 0.5% |
| Channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*) | 5 | 0.5% |
| Redear sunfish (*Lepomis microlophus*) | 4 | trace |
| Flathead catfish (*Pilodictis olivaris*) | 2 | 1% |
| Black crappie (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) | 2 | trace |
| Sunfish hybrids | 2 | – |
| Largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*) | 1 | – |

Machete (*Elops affinis*), goldfish, red shiner, mosquitofish and sailfin molly were observed but not netted.

**Key species notes:**
- **Striped mullet:** Netted fish were 13–16 inches long, suggesting no more than two year classes. None had developed gonads, and many were very fat, with a high incidence of parasitic cysts and diseased livers.
- **Common carp:** Netted fish were 5–12 inches, with 15 fish over 15 inches and 2 pounds. Electrofishing has taken larger carp.
- **Yellow bullhead:** All 23 netted fish were adults (10–13 inches) from the northeastern pool. Many had darkened mandibular barbels, unlike the typical creamy-white barbels. Fishermen reported declining size and abundance.
- **Channel catfish:** Only five were caught, none over 14 inches. One female extruded ripe eggs at 8.5 inches.
- **Flathead catfish:** A male of about 33 inches (about 20 pounds) was spent, indicating recent spawning. A female of about 24 inches (5.8 pounds) had developing ovaries.
- **Largemouth bass:** 13 of 22 fishermen interviewed sought this species. Only one young-of-year (1973) bass was netted, though electrofishing records indicate a larger population.
- **Bluegill and redear sunfish:** Bluegill ranged from 2 to 6.5 inches. Four redear (3–6 inches) were netted, and two bluegill–redear hybrids (about 5 inches) were found. Most hybrids examined were males without developed gonads, suggesting a sterility barrier.
- **Mozambique mouthbrooder:** Five adults (10–13 inches) were netted, one carrying ova. Its rarity was unexpected and remains unexplained.

**Conclusions and interpretation:**
- Hunter's Hole has relatively high diversity (17 species) but lacks a food base for juvenile game fish such as largemouth bass and black crappie. Small threadfin shad were nearly absent.
- Mosquitofish, mollies and red shiners were unexpectedly scarce compared with other backwaters, possibly because of sampling limits and secretive behaviour.
- Black bullhead and warmouth were expected but not collected, which the authors attribute possibly to sampling error.
- Mullet abundance is probably overestimated by gill netting, and carp likely overestimated by both methods. Largemouth bass and channel catfish are probably underrepresented.
- The authors consider the relative abundance estimates a "valid, albeit somewhat qualitative assessment" of the fauna.
