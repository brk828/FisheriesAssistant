# Results of the 1992–1994 Aravaipa Creek Fish Monitoring Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) technical report (Nongame Branch, Technical Report 73, October 1995) presents results of a two-year fish monitoring project on [[Aravaipa Creek]], a spring-fed tributary of the [[San Pedro River]] in the Galiuro Mountains. The stream supports seven native fishes, two of them federally listed as threatened. The project was designed to track relative abundance and distribution of native fishes and to detect the return of the [[Red Shiner]], an invasive cyprinid whose appearance in September 1990 threatened native species. Sampling from August 1992 to April 1994 combined seining and electrofishing across four reaches. Red shiners were not found in the monitoring collections, though a single individual was recorded in an October 1993 survey. The authors conclude the threat appears to have abated, but recommend continued monitoring and construction of a fish barrier in the lower creek.

## Background & Description

Aravaipa Creek flows nearly 75 km from headwaters near Klondyke (Graham County) to the San Pedro River (Pinal County), through the Galiuro Mountains and the [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness Area]]. It is one of the last streams in the Upper Sonoran Desert with a diverse native fish assemblage.

**Native species (seven):**
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*): federally listed as threatened.
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus (Pantosteus) clarki*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*): candidate species.

**Nonnative species** reported in the 1980s and during the project include fathead minnow, green sunfish, yellow bullhead, common carp, black bullhead, mosquitofish, and largemouth bass. Historical reports are anecdotal (Velasco, pers. comm.).

**Monitoring history:** Fish have been collected since 1965 by students and agencies. Monthly sampling at six sites from August 1991 was the first phase, led by Arizona State University (ASU). The second phase (August 1992–July 1993, then through April 1994) was led by AGFD, with ASU responsible for the remaining trips. Funding came from the Heritage Fund, license revenues, the Nongame Wildlife Checkoff, the [[Bureau of Land Management]], and [[The Nature Conservancy]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Threat:** Native fish declines across Arizona may stem from [[Red Shiner]] predation or competition, or from habitat degradation favoring the invader. Loach minnow and spikedace were thought most affected.
- **Hydrology:** Bimodal flash floods have historically helped keep nonnatives out. A February–March 1993 flood was linked to changes in loach minnow catch rates.
- **Habitat:** Four reaches were defined by geomorphology: East End, Canyon (Turkey Creek and Wagner Ranch), Wilderness Area (Hell Hole, Horse Camp, Painted Cave), and West End (White's Ranch and Sycamore Tree). The West End is flatter and more alluvial, with more shallow backwater and sand substrate.
- **Water quality:** A March 1993 ADEQ investigation found mine tailings in the Klondyke area reaching the creek, with a pond pH of 4.85.
- **Recreation:** The BLM allows 50 visitors per day in the wilderness area, and visitors must hike in the stream channel.
- **Barrier planning:** An inter-agency group including [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], BLM, AGFD, TNC, and ASU has considered a fish barrier since 1988.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Monthly seining at six standard 200 m stations (August 1992–April 1994), with three additional "walk-through" sites in the Wilderness Area sampled quarterly with seining and 100 m of electrofishing.
- Seines were 15' x 4' and 6' x 4' (1/4" or 1/8" mesh). Electrofishing used backpack shockers with block nets and dip nets.
- Fish were counted, measured for adult/juvenile status, and released (nonnatives removed). Habitat parameters were recorded for each subhabitat.
- No statistical tests were applied in the main results, which use qualitative abundance scales.

**Red shiner status**
- Absent from the 1992–1994 monitoring collections.
- One specimen found in September 1990, and a regular monitoring program followed until 1994.
- The October 1993 AGFD-coordinated survey (about 387 person-hours of seining) found only one red shiner, near Sycamore Tree, about 5 km above the San Pedro River.
- A red shiner was detected again in October 1997, according to a later reference in the report's introduction.

**Native fish results**
- Across all sites, 71,041 native fish were collected (adults and juveniles combined). Longfin dace was the most common species (28,193 total, about 40%), followed by desert sucker (about 23%) and spikedace (about 19%).
- **Loach minnow:** Most frequent at the East End; seining averaged 2.0% and electrofishing averaged 14.8% of catch. Juveniles peaked in May and August 1993. Fall spawning was suggested by 20–30 mm young-of-year in December 1992.
- **Spikedace:** Virtually absent from West End stations and highest at the East End. Spawning is suggested in late summer, with recruitment in fall.
- **Roundtail chub:** Rare (<3–5% of catch). Highest at the Wilderness Area stations and absent from the West End. Adults were caught more often than juveniles, and shocking captured more than seining.
- **Longfin dace:** Common throughout. The West End had the highest relative abundance (about 65%).
- **Speckled dace:** The least common native fish (380 fish, 0.5%). Absent from the West End.
- **Sonora sucker:** Adults were very rare (0.8% of catch). Juvenile numbers were high, outnumbering adults over 1:1 on the West End.
- **Desert sucker:** About 23% of catch. Electrofishing captured 68.1 fish per 10 m² versus 10.0 by seining.
- Electrofishing captured far more fish than seining overall (for example, longfin dace: 63.3 adults and 26.6 juveniles per 10 m² shocked, versus 12.5 and 14.5 per 10 m² seined).

**Nonnative results**
- Nonnatives made up 0.2% of the catch (73 yellow bullheads, 38 mosquitofish, 17 green sunfish).
- Counts peaked in August (44 and 43 in the two years) and dropped about 75% by November, possibly from removal, seasonal effects, or flood flushing.

**Habitat**
- Runs covered 98,367 m², riffles 22,890 m², and pools 3,310 m². Pools deeper than 1.5 m could not be sampled.
- Chi-square tests showed significant differences (p<.001) between observed and expected habitat use for most native species.
- Pools per mile ranged from 0 to 44.22 (November 1993, Canyon reach).

**Multivariate analysis (Appendix 6, Kubly)**
- Discriminant analysis and nonmetric multidimensional scaling showed the Canyon groups (Turkey Creek and Wagner Ranch) had the most variable fish assemblages.
- Upper (Guest House and Chimney Rock) and lower (White's Ranch and Sycamore) sites were more similar to each other.
- Recommendation: if sites are reduced, cut them from the exterior reaches. Canyon sites may warrant more sampling per unit length.
- Caveat: the analysis was not built to determine sampling density for detecting rare invaders.

**Management recommendations**
1. Continue monitoring, with an extensive yearly survey of the whole creek when red shiners are most likely to be found, using the October 1993 protocol.
2. Focus on fish data, not habitat parameters, with photo points as the main habitat record.
3. Survey the watershed, including cattle tanks, and the San Pedro River within 5 km of the confluence. Start a nonnative eradication project if warranted.
4. Build a fish barrier in the lower reach of Aravaipa Creek "as soon as possible," since red shiners entered all reaches in 1990–1991 and were found in the lower creek in October 1993.
5. Monitor native fishes before and after any road construction that dewaters the creek. The draft Environmental Assessment for the Aravaipa Creek road repair lacked sufficient runoff and sedimentation data.
6. Study the effects of recreational use in the wilderness area.
7. Expand public education through interpretive displays, brochures, and guided hikes.
