# Integration and Evaluation of Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Research Findings: The Grand Canyon Riverine Ecosystem

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Final Report (February 1998), prepared by Duncan T. Patten for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Center]] under Cooperative Agreement 1425-96-FC-81-05003, evaluates research funded by [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) Phases I and II. It integrates findings on how [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations have altered the Colorado River through Glen and Grand Canyons. The report reviews the strengths and weaknesses of aquatic and terrestrial studies, reviews several synthesis documents, and builds conceptual models linking driving variables (discharge, sediment, light, geomorphology) to response variables (aquatic food base, fishes, waterbirds, riparian vegetation, terrestrial birds).

The central message is that the canyon is a highly managed, disturbance-driven system in which no single factor can be managed in isolation. The report also recommends that future long-term monitoring be reduced in intrusiveness, focused on a few integrative indicators, and conducted conjunctively so that related attributes are measured at the same place and time.

## Background & Description

The report opens with the history of the river below the dam. [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closed in 1963, and [[Lake Powell]] filled by 1980. GCES Phase I (1982-1986) was triggered by concerns that generator upgrades would intensify downstream impacts. Its conclusions that high, uncontrolled flows were destructive were shaped by the wet years 1983-1986. Phase II began in 1989, intended to be integrated and hypothesis-driven, but the short timeline of the [[Glen Canyon Dam EIS]] constrained this goal. Experimental test flows (1990-1991) were followed by [[Interim Flows]] from fall 1991. The 1995 EIS Record of Decision selected [[Modified Low Fluctuating Flows]] (MLFF), which includes periodic high releases for beach and habitat building. A [[Experimental Flood]] was conducted from March 22 to April 8, 1996, at 1274 cms (45,000 cfs) for one week.

The report reviews several integrative documents:

- [[Colorado River Ecology and Dam Management]] (NRC 1991), a symposium volume that presented the flow-diagram concept.
- [[Colorado River Through the Grand Canyon]] (Carothers and Brown 1991), which adds upstream, economic, political and legal factors to the conceptual model.
- The Glen Canyon Dam Final EIS (Bureau of Reclamation 1995), which emphasizes adaptive management.
- The [[Prospectus on Integration of Biological and Physical Data]] (GCES 1995), a Flagstaff workshop report focused on humpback chub and Biological Opinion issues.
- The [[Grand Canyon Data Integration Project]] (SWCA 1997), a synthesis on fish responses to steady flows.
- [[Grand Canyon, A Century of Change]] (Webb 1997), a repeat-photography study.

Dam-related changes documented in these works include near-elimination of sediment transport (reduced by an order of magnitude), clear and cold water year-round, and daily fluctuations that were historically as great as 566 cms (20,000 cfs). Pre-dam flows included spring floods of roughly 1380 to 3396 cms (60,000 to 120,000 cfs).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report organizes the riverine ecosystem into five response attributes: the [[Aquatic Food Base]], native and non-native fishes, waterbirds, riparian and marsh vegetation, and riparian and other terrestrial birds. Their common drivers are light, discharge, sediment transport and deposition, geomorphology, and, where applicable, the aquatic food base and riparian vegetation.

Key ecological contexts include:

- **Clear-water reach.** The reach from the dam to [[Lees Ferry]] is clear, has the highest primary production, and produces more than 50% of the aquatic food base while comprising less than 5% of the river between the dam and Lake Mead.
- **Stair-step sediment.** Suspended sediment rises downstream, with inputs from tributaries such as the [[Paria River]] and the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR).
- **Riparian zones.** The [[New High Water Zone]] (NHWZ) is dominated by non-native [[Tamarisk]]. The [[Old High Water Zone]] (OHWZ) is declining. The [[New Dry Zone]] (NDZ) emerged under Interim Flows.
- **Fishes.** Eight native species were recorded in the late 1800s. Only [[Humpback Chub]], speckled dace, razorback sucker, bluehead sucker and flannelmouth sucker remain in post-dam collections. Nine non-native species have been added, with rainbow and brown trout most common.
- **Temperature.** Pre-dam temperatures ranged from 0 to 26 C, while post-dam temperatures range from 7 to 13 C. This change is considered a likely primary reason for native fish losses.

## Key Findings

**Methodology and integration (Sections II-III)**

- Many GCES studies used non-standard methods. Sampling bias arises from visual versus dip-net detection and unclear definitions of "steady" and "fluctuating" flows.
- Many authors treated repeated samples within transects as independent, inflating degrees of freedom and Type I error risk. The report recommends averaging within transects.
- Catch-per-unit-effort was reported as both catch per area and catch per time, with some reports switching within the same report.
- Long-term monitoring should be limited to a few indicators, with riparian ground monitoring every 3-5 years and annual aerial photo interpretation. Conjunctive monitoring (measuring interrelated attributes at the same time and place) is recommended.
- Avian studies should continue annually in winter and summer. The floating survey method was compared with walking surveys, giving r² = 0.96. The report recommends total vegetation volume (TVV) as a structural metric.

**Aquatic food base (Section V-A)**

- Suspended sediment at 425 cms raised the vertical light attenuation coefficient from about 0.2 to 1.2, compared with 0.2 to 0.6 at 142 cms (Yard et al. 1993).
- Twelve hours of daytime exposure significantly reduced Cladophora biomass, while nighttime exposure had little effect.
- Energy estimates for Lees Ferry and Cathedral Wash showed the latter at about 25% of primary productivity, supporting roughly 37 kg of trout per year versus 113 kg at the clear-water site.
- The 1996 flood scoured benthos and organic matter in the first 48 hours. Benthic recovery was rapid, and Gammarus increased steadily after the flood.

**Fishes (Section V-B)**

- Non-natives made up 81-98% of fish across reaches (1984-1986 data), in some reaches ten to fifty times the native population.
- Humpback chub in eddies were concentrated at night in water under 2 m and during the day at 2-5 m, reflecting predation risk in clear water.
- Of 37 native southwestern species, only four are known piscivores, while 26 of 57 introduced fishes are piscivorous.
- Natives appear to be limited by fluctuating flows that strand young fish, by colder mainstem temperatures, and by predation in clear water.
- The 1996 flood had short-term effects on non-natives and little effect on native populations, and backwaters created by the flood were ephemeral.

**Waterbirds (Section V-C)**

- Waterbird counts are highest in winter and lowest in summer (Stevens et al. 1997; Grahame and Pinnock 1994).
- Total AARE (birds/km/hour) dropped from nearly 10,000 to under 100 between the first and second reaches.
- Mallards and common mergansers were the only regular nesters identified.

**Riparian and marsh vegetation (Section V-D)**

- Stevens and Ayers (1995) documented vegetation expansion in the NDZ after Interim Flows began; marsh patch area increased most in narrow reaches between 1993 and 1995.
- Vegetation on the NHWZ survived the 1983 flood at nearly 60% and most of it survived the 1996 flood. Marsh communities were greatly reduced by high-flow scour and burial.
- Coarse soils supported greater species richness, while fine soils enhanced tamarisk seedling development.

**Terrestrial birds (Section V-E)**

- Avifaunal surveys recorded 36 species in the Glen Canyon reach and 72 species from the dam to Pearce Ferry, with 13 abundant breeding species.
- The endangered [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] nests predominantly in tamarisk and depends on nearby willows and marshes. Brown-headed cowbird parasitism is a key reproductive threat.

**Synthesis of the 1996 flood (Section VI)**

- Hypotheses tested included elevation of sediment to form beaches, scouring of backwaters, scouring of NHWZ vegetation, and minimal effects on fishes and endangered species.
- The flood was smaller than most pre-dam spring floods and about half the 1983 uncontrolled peak.
- The report concludes that the flood did not significantly set back the aquatic food base, and that non-native fish reduction would require much higher, longer discharges and a sediment source that are unlikely under current operations.

**System integration (Section VII)**

- Discharge is the only driving variable with no response function within the ecosystem. Under MLFF, high flows were limited to 556-708 cms, daily fluctuations to 142-226 cms, and ramping rates to 113 cms/hr up and 42 cms/hr down.
- The report concludes that "no two places have the same set of conditions," and that understanding of interactions must precede management of any single factor. Moderate discharge with little fluctuation and wide reaches supports both aquatic and terrestrial organisms.
