# A Botanist in Arizona Territory: Jacob Corwin Blumer's Travels

#botany #plant-ecology #historical-collections #desert-ecology

## Executive Summary

This historical account documents the botanical work of Swiss-born botanist [[Jacob Corwin Blumer]] (1872-1947) in southeastern [[Arizona Territory]] between August 1906 and October 1907. Blumer made the first extensive plant collections in the [[Chiricahua Mountains]], traveling more than 1,600 miles and collecting approximately 1,050 plant species, including 25 new to science. Beyond his taxonomic contributions, Blumer made pioneering observations in plant ecology, particularly regarding altitudinal zonation and the relationship between species distribution, environmental factors, and phenotypic plasticity. Author [[Peter Bennett]] retraced Blumer's travels and recovered a previously overlooked manuscript documenting three horseback trips that formed the foundation of Blumer's ecological insights.

## Background & Description

[[Jacob Corwin Blumer]] conducted his fieldwork during a transitional period in Arizona's history—6 years before statehood and only 20 years after Geronimo's surrender. The region around the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] was sparsely populated. Blumer supported himself by selling plant specimens to herbaria and botanists, though he noted the increasing difficulty of earning a living this way due to rising costs of transportation and supplies.

Blumer was a familiar presence at local ranches, particularly those of Rhoda Riggs and John Greenberry Stephens. Stephens' daughter Betty assisted by collecting plant specimens for the botanist. His work produced solid contributions to knowledge of the southeastern Arizona flora, but his ecological insights remained largely overlooked until recently.

A manuscript titled "Observations on Desert Vegetation Made for Dr. Volney Spalding" was discovered among botanist [[Forrest Shreve]]'s papers in the University of Arizona herbarium archives. This document recorded observations from three horseback trips to or from the Chiricahua Mountains in 1906 and 1907.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Blumer documented a "peculiar inversion of altitudinal zones" in the region east of [[Gleeson]]. The foothills just east of Gleeson (elevation 5,200 feet) were covered by strictly desert species. Descending the long, smooth incline toward the floor of [[Sulfur Springs Valley]], the several-mile incline supported [[Upper Sonoran]] grassland with [[Yucca Elata]] (recorded as Y. radicosa). The immense valley floor was dominated by mesquite and sacaton grass, with typical desert shrubs absent until the surface began rising toward the western base of the [[Swisshelm Mountains]].

At the mouth of [[Leslie Canyon]], Blumer noted the reappearance of [[Fouquieria]] (ocotillo) and limestone formations. This pattern represented an "inversion or rather interruption of altitudinal belts" where desert vegetation occurred at higher elevations than grassland vegetation, contrary to typical elevational gradients.

## Key Findings

**Pioneering Ecological Concepts:**
Blumer was perhaps the first to publish papers demonstrating that at low elevations plant distribution is limited by moisture availability, while at high elevations minimum temperatures impose the primary constraint.

**Species Plasticity and Distribution (1911):**
Blumer articulated a farsighted ecological principle: "species of widest local distribution... are frequently also those possessing... the largest number of individuals per square mile [and] are those exhibiting the largest number and widest range of variations. It is this versatility of comparative plasticity that must enable such a plant to achieve predominance... and to extend or hold its range."

**Altitudinal Zone Inversion:**
Documented unusual vegetation patterns where desert species occurred at approximately 5,200 feet elevation in the foothills, while [[Upper Sonoran]] grassland dominated lower elevations on the valley slopes, with mesquite-sacaton communities on the valley floor. This represented an interruption of typical altitudinal belting.

**Collection Statistics:**
- Duration: 10 months (August 1906 - October 1907)
- Distance traveled: >1,600 miles
- Species collected: ~1,050
- New species described: 25
