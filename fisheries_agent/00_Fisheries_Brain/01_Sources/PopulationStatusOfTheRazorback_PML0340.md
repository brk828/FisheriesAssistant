# Population Status of the Razorback Sucker in the Middle Green River (U.S.A.)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Modde, Burnham, and Wick (1996, *Conservation Biology* 10(1):110–119) assesses the status of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the middle [[Green River]]. The authors analyzed capture data from 1975 to 1992, with population estimates covering 1980–1992. The population is small, and it has no detectable recruitment signal beyond what was observed in tag-loss-biased data, but it shows no significant decline over the study period. The authors conclude the population is "precariously small but dynamic," with survival that depends on whether [[Floodplain Connectivity]] and high spring flows can be restored to support [[Recruitment]].

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, family [[Catostomidae]]) was listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1991 (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1991).
- **Distribution:** The species is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It was once numerous in the mainstem and major tributaries but is now commonly found only in [[Lake Mohave]] and the middle Green River between the [[Yampa River]] and [[Duchesne River]] confluences.
- **Causes of decline:** Declines have been attributed to habitat loss linked to reduced flows, altered temperature regimes, and establishment of [[Nonnative Fishes]] (Carlson and Muth 1989).
- **Longevity:** Individuals may live 30 to 40 years (Minckley 1983), so the persistence of the population is a long-term concern.
- **Study area:** The middle Green River population is the largest remaining wild population in the river reach. Spawning aggregations occur at two locations: upstream of Jensen, Utah, adjacent to the Escalante Ranch (river kilometers 492–501), and at the mouth of the Yampa River (Tyus and Karp 1990).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime and floodplain habitat**
- The authors note that peak spring flows of the Green River between the Yampa and Duchesne mouths have been significantly reduced since the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (Sanford 1994).
- Reduced flows, along with diking associated with floodplain agriculture, have severely reduced the frequency of overbank flooding.
- Flooded bottomlands are considered important habitat for early life stages (Behnke 1980; Tyus and Karp 1990; Modde et al. 1995).

**Nonnative fish and predation**
- Predation on razorback sucker larvae under experimental conditions was attributed to adult red shiners, juvenile green sunfish, and juvenile channel catfish (Robert Muth, personal communication).
- Invertebrate prey density in the Green River was low from June to August: 24 zooplankters/L in riverine backwaters, compared with 402 zooplankters/L in a nearby wetland on the Ouray National Wildlife Refuge (Malde 1991, as cited).

**Juvenile records**
- Larval fish suspected to be razorback sucker were collected below the Escalante site in 1986, with confirmed collections in 1992, 1993, and 1994.
- Juveniles are rare in the middle Green River, and the authors interpret this as a sign of limited recruitment.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**
- Data came from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Colorado River Fish Project. Fish were collected by electrofishing, mainly in April through June, and marked with dorsal Floy tags (1975–1980s) or internally inserted [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (1990–1992).
- Survival was estimated with [[Cormack-Jolly-Seber Model]]s. Model selection used [[Akaike Information Criterion]] (AIC).
- Abundance was estimated with [[Lincoln-Petersen Estimator]]s, a [[Jolly-Seber Model]] comparison, and a modified Jolly-Seber approach (Buckland 1980).

**Capture and growth**
- The dataset included 661 observations of 461 individuals collected from 1975 to 1992. Most observations (all but 10) fell between 1980 and 1992.
- Of these fish, 156 were recaptured, and 213 were sexed.
- Captures ranged from river kilometer 80 to 500, with only 13 observations below river kilometer 368.
- Length-frequency distributions remained fairly constant, with the most frequent modes at 505–515 mm.
- Mean individual growth was slow (1.66, SD = 4.72; units garbled in scan). A regression test for linear trend in mean length across years was not significant (F = 0.03, p = 0.858).

**Survival**
- The best model had time-varying capture probabilities and constant annual survival.
- Estimated apparent annual survival was 0.708 (SE = 0.0245), with a 95% confidence interval lower bound of 0.658 (upper bound illegible in scan).
- The authors state that survival includes tag loss, so this value is an underestimate of true survival if tag loss occurred.
- The authors estimate that tag loss may be a major source of bias. Tyus and Tanigan (1989) observed only 1.6% tag loss in an earlier study.

**Population size**
- Lincoln-Petersen annual estimates were variable, especially in early years, but within expected sampling error.
- The unweighted average estimate was 524 (empirical SE = 85; theoretical SE = 88).
- Adjusting for survival (524 × 0.7) gives an average of about 367 (SE = 61).
- Based on these methods, the authors estimate the 1980–1992 population was between 300 and 600 adult fish on average.
- Earlier estimates by Tanigan and Tyus (1989) were approximately 1,000 adults. The authors consider this the same order of magnitude as their estimates.

**Population trend**
- Linear regression of log abundance on year yielded no significant decline: unweighted slope = −0.694 (SE = 0.0498), one-sided p = 0.1031; weighted slope = −0.0456, p = 0.258; excluding the first estimate, p = 0.4516.
- The authors conclude the data do not support a population decline over time.
- [[Power Analysis]] showed low power to detect small declines. Power ranged from 0.14 at a 2.5% annual decline to 0.97 at a 15% annual decline.
- Without recruitment, survival would need to be 0.875 or higher for the study to detect decline.

**Recruitment and discharge**
- Recruitment was linked to discharge. Numbers of small fish (445–455 mm, F = 5.94, p = 0.035; and a second small-length interval, F = 6.81, p = 0.026) were positively related to discharge five years before capture.
- Larger fish showed no significant relationship with discharge.
- Because most small fish appear to be male, the authors caution that these results may reflect male recruitment only.

**Management implications and conclusions**
- The population is "precariously small but dynamic," and possibly stable or slowly declining.
- Restoring high spring flows and [[Floodplain Connectivity]] may be necessary for sustained recruitment.
- Restoration efforts should focus on defining the relationship between flooded bottomlands and razorback sucker growth and survival, and on managing these areas to reduce the effects of nonnative fishes.
- The authors also recommend improving estimates of tag loss and recruitment, which were not quantified in this study.
