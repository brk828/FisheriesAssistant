# Behavioral Barriers and Guidance Systems at Power Plant Intakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-behavior

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by Charles H. Hocutt (Chapter VII, *Power Plants*, Academic Press, 1980) reviews behavioral stimuli used to attract or repel fish away from power plant intake structures. The stimuli covered are [[Electrical Barriers]], [[Air Bubble Curtains]], [[Illumination]], [[Acoustic Barriers]], louvers and velocity caps (deferred to Chapter VI), and chain and cable screens. The author concludes that behavioral barriers have achieved only marginal success overall. Responses are highly species-, life-stage- and condition-specific, and much of the reported data is contradictory or inadequately tested.

## Background & Description

The chapter frames behavioral guidance as an alternative or supplement to physical screens. Fish respond to stimuli through either preference or avoidance reactions. Stimuli have been drawn from several sensory modalities: electrical fields, air bubbles, light, sound, and changes in current direction and velocity. The author notes that the mechanisms by which fish orient and migrate using various cues "still remain largely a mystery."

Key concepts reviewed include:
- **Electrotaxis:** involuntary movement of fish toward the anode in a direct current (DC) field. In alternating current (AC) fields, classical electrotaxis does not occur; currents only stun or frighten fish.
- **Electronarcosis:** temporary loss of equilibrium caused by high voltage.
- **Rheotaxis:** orientation to water current, dependent on visual (optomotor) cues in most species and on tactile cues in benthic fish and young sturgeon.
- **Diel vertical migration:** light-driven daily movement patterns, which Blaxter (1975) identified as dominated by light.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Behavioral responses determine whether fish are guided away from or drawn into intakes, and so directly affect impingement and entrainment. Several environmental variables modify effectiveness:

- **Light and visibility:** Many fish lose orientation in darkness or turbid water, so they may be entrained or impinged depending on size. Pavlov (1969) reported highest impingement at twilight and at night.
- **Water velocity:** Pugh et al. (1971) found that electrical guidance of four salmonid species decreased with increasing velocity, and was judged feasible only where velocity did not exceed 0.3 m/s.
- **Temperature:** At low temperatures, fish became lethargic. Positive phototaxis may weaken at elevated temperatures.
- **Water conductivity:** Electrical losses limit electrical screens in estuarine and ocean waters.
- **Habituation and background noise:** Fish habituate to sounds quickly, and natural environments contain many sounds (waves, surf, boats, other fish).

Operational and safety limits also apply. Electrical systems can be dangerous to humans and other animals, and artificial light has in some cases caused complete plant shutdowns.

## Key Findings

**Electrical barriers**
- Electrical guiding is species-specific. Electrical threshold response depends on current type, voltage, pulse rate and pulse width (Klima 1972, 1974; Seidel and Klima 1974).
- Yates (1930) found 85% fewer salmon in an electrically protected test channel than in a control channel.
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service ended electrical screen research in 1965 after 15 years of effort (EPA 1973, 1976).
- Sonnichsen et al. (1973) found that alternating strong and weak fields partially guided fish to a bypass, but sustained exposure caused fatigue.
- The author concludes that the advantages of electrical barriers in power plant intakes have been marginal.

**Air bubble curtains**
- Early bubble screens at Indian Point No. 1 (NY) were ineffective. Bubble curtains were also found ineffective at Quad-Cities (IL) and Prairie Island (MN), and have since been removed from these stations.
- Bates and VanDerwalker (1964) reported up to 90% guiding efficiency in daylight at intake velocities not exceeding 0.58 m/s, but the curtain was ineffective at night and in turbid water.
- Bibko et al. (1974) found curtains especially effective with gizzard shad (300–400 mm TL) and striped bass (143–241 mm TL) at 5–11 °C. Fish passed single file through a 5 cm gap and side by side through a 15.2 cm gap.
- Kuznetsov (1971) suggested fish respond to acoustic rather than visual stimuli, with strong inhibiting effects at 6.0–9.0 kHz.

**Illumination**
- Fish responses to light are inconsistent and depend on light type, intensity, wavelength, duration, life stage and physiological state.
- Fore (1969) tested 23 freshwater species. Nearly all families showed positive responses to light, while juvenile and adult black bullhead and channel catfish were negative. Nocturnal fish were negatively phototactic and diurnal fish positively so.
- Fields et al. (1954, 1955) found a 20° light barrier guided salmonids most effectively, at the lowest intake velocity and the highest illumination. A 90° barrier acted as an avoidance barrier.
- Wickham (1973) led fish up to 1 km using continuously operated mobile lamps.
- The author notes that no existing intake was found to have a successfully functioning light barrier.
- Turning off lights near intakes has temporarily reduced impingement, and offshore lights directed toward the plant were proposed as a mitigation option.

**Acoustic barriers**
- Fish produce sound by stridulation (50–10,000 Hz), hydrodynamic noise and swim bladder vibration (usually below 300 Hz).
- Fish with a Weberian apparatus (Cypriniformes and Siluriformes) have greater auditory range.
- Fish can perceive 1–250 Hz via the lateral line (Nikonorov 1975).
- Pulsed low-frequency sounds attract predators such as sharks and serranids and repel prey fish. Rhythm is more important than frequency (Nikonorov 1975).
- Burner and Moore (1962) found fish were startled by low frequencies but showed no response to high ones, and habituated quickly.
- Schuler and Larson (1975) found a pneumatic impact device created a shock wave that kept fish at least 3 m from it, although the effect was not linked to cycle rate.
- Jazz music at a Virginia plant was only marginally effective and was discontinued.

**Louvers, velocity caps, and chain/cable screens**
- Louvers and velocity caps were judged the most promising guidance options and are covered in Chapter VI. Louvers have been used extensively in the Pacific Northwest, and velocity caps are common at Great Lakes power plants.
- Chain and cable barriers have proven ineffective (Ray et al. 1976).

**Overall conclusions**
- Stimulus-based guidance has achieved only marginal success.
- Congleton (1974) argued that any behavioral system must be experimentally validated across a wide range of species, sizes, plant operating conditions, and seasonal and daily conditions before wide-scale adoption.
- Further work is recommended on light wavelength preferences and lighting configurations that minimize attraction near intakes.

**Reference base:** The chapter cites more than 150 references, including EPA development documents (1973, 1976), Fore (1969), Blaxter (1975) and Bibko et al. (1974).
