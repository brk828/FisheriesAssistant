## Executive Summary

This paper documents changes in the commercial fishery of the Alabama portion of the Tennessee River following its conversion from a free-flowing river into a series of impoundments ([[Wheeler Reservoir]], [[Wilson Reservoir]], [[Guntersville Reservoir]], [[Pickwick Reservoir]]) beginning with the completion of Wheeler Dam in 1936. The study traces the interaction between legislative regulation (the [[Sanford Act]] and its 1945 repeal), shifts in fishing gear (baited lines vs. [[Snagline Fishing|snaglines]] vs. nets/seines), and resulting population dynamics of both game and non-game ("rough") fish species. It also documents the unexpected resurgence of the [[Freshwater Mussel Industry]] after impoundment, and evaluates the relative economic value of commercial versus sport fisheries, concluding that regulated commercial harvest of rough fish is compatible with, and potentially beneficial to, sport fisheries.

## Background & Description

This paper is not a taxonomic or morphological study; it is a fisheries economics and management report. Species-specific morphological notes are limited to gear-selectivity observations:

- **[[Paddlefish|Spoonbill (Paddlefish)]]**: Described as a species "thought to be too rare to support a fishery" prior to 1941; its cartilaginous, scaleless body morphology makes it uniquely vulnerable to snagline gear (unlike scaled fishes).
- **Scaled fishes** (buffalo, carp, drum): Their scaled body form renders them largely resistant to snagline capture, explaining their continued population increase despite fishing pressure via this gear type.
- **[[Pigtoe Mussel|Pigtoe Shell]]**: Noted as a "thick shell," commercially valuable for manufacture of shell-shank buttons, distinguishing it from thinner-shelled mussel species in the same beds.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pre-impoundment (riverine) habitat**: Supported a small-scale, seasonal fishery — buffalo and drum taken via nets in late winter/early spring; catfish taken via baited lines in other seasons. Annual harvest (1935–1936 census) totaled approximately **57,000 pounds** of buffalo, drum, and catfish combined.
- **Post-impoundment (lentic/reservoir) habitat**: Following creation of [[Wheeler Reservoir]] (1936), rough fish (carp, buffalo, drum) proliferated rapidly in the new lacustrine environment, while catfish/bullhead catches declined under baited-line-only regulations (1936–1941).
- **Niche partitioning by gear type**: The introduction of the snagline in [[Wilson Reservoir]] (1941) exploited a previously under-harvested niche — the paddlefish — without affecting scaled rough-fish populations, illustrating gear-specific selectivity within the same reservoir ecosystem.
- **Backwater/snag habitat**: Shallow backwaters full of stumps and snags were identified as unfishable by net/seine gear, effectively creating refugia for rough fish populations even after 1945 deregulation.
- **Mussel beds**: Contrary to expectations, [[Unionidae|mussel beds]] persisted and thrived in reservoir conditions post-impoundment, rediscovered in 1945 nine years after Wheeler Dam's completion — indicating unionid mussels are more tolerant of impoundment-induced habitat change (reduced flow, altered substrate) than previously assumed.
- **Tailwater habitat**: Waters immediately below dams were identified as important concentration zones for game and pan fish, warranting special protection from commercial gear.

## Key Findings

1. **Pre-impoundment baseline**: ~57,000 lbs/year commercial catch of buffalo, drum, and catfish on the future Wheeler Reservoir site (1935–1936 census).
2. **Sanford Act (1936)** prohibited all netting in impounded waters, restricting legal commercial fishing to baited lines from 1936–1941, which favored catfish harvest but allowed unchecked increases in carp, buffalo, and drum populations.
3. **Snagline innovation (1941)**: In Wilson Reservoir, 25 snagline fishermen harvested over **600,000 pounds of spoonbill (paddlefish)** between August 1941 and July 1942 — a species previously considered too rare to support commercial harvest. This gear was highly selective for scaleless fish and did little to control rough fish.
4. **Regulatory liberalization (1945)**: Alabama repealed the Sanford Act, permitting nets and seines, increasing rough fish catch — though limited by gear/habitat constraints (deep open water, snag-filled backwaters).
5. **Commercial catch data (1942–1952)**: Total value and poundage of commercial catch (species: buffalo, catfish, drum, carp, sturgeon, spoonbill) across Guntersville, Wheeler, Wilson, and Pickwick Reservoirs rose substantially over the decade, reaching combined total values near $475,703.50 in the 1952 season.
6. **Mussel industry rebirth**: Thought destroyed by impoundment, the mussel fishery was rediscovered in 1945 and rapidly revived, providing part-time employment for ~100 people and generating ~$100,000 in income by 1951 in north Alabama. The pigtoe shell was the dominant commercial species, primarily shipped to Muscatine, Iowa for button manufacturing.
7. **Mussel harvest data (1945–1951)**: White mussel shell tonnage rose from 200 tons (1945) to 3,135 tons (1950), with total valuations increasing from $8,660 (1945) to over $99,640 (1951); total TVA-wide mussel harvest exceeded 10,000 tons by 1951.
8. **Sport fishery economic value**: Estimated at $10–12 million/year in the Tennessee Valley, roughly **15 times** the business value of the commercial fishery, based on angler expenditures on tackle, boats, fuel, lodging, and meals.
9. **White crappie population trend**: Despite sportsmen's claims of decline, unit catch data from 1945–1951 showed a "slow but gradual increase" in [[White Crappie]] abundance, contradicting anecdotal concerns.
10. **Tagging/harvest rate studies**:
    - **Wheeler Reservoir** (1945–1952): 7,157 fish tagged; 116 tags returned → **1.6% harvest rate**.
    - **Guntersville Reservoir** (1948–1952): 7,249 fish tagged; 164 tags returned → **2.3% harvest rate**.
    - Both results indicate persistently poor harvesting of the sport fish crop, suggesting angling pressure alone does not fully exploit available sport fish populations.
11. **Management conclusion**: Regulated commercial netting of non-game (rough) species does not harm — and may benefit — the sport fishery by reducing intraspecific competition for food and space between game and rough fish populations. Recommendations included excluding commercial gear from tailwaters and known angler hotspots, and mandatory release of any incidentally caught game fish.
