# Preliminary Observations on the Ichthyofauna of the Río Matape, Sonora, Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 field survey, presented at the IX Congreso Nacional de Zoología in Villahermosa, Tabasco, inventories the fishes of the [[Río Matape]], an arid-zone drainage in northwestern [[Sonora]], Mexico. Eight localities were sampled in October 1986 and March 1987. A total of 984 specimens representing four species was collected: three native and one introduced. The fauna is dominated numerically by [[Poeciliopsis Occidentalis]] and its unisexual hybrid, while [[Gila Purpurea]] (Yaqui Chub) contributes most of the native biomass. The authors conclude that the basin still supports its native populations, but that habitat modification, groundwater pumping, and introduced [[Green Sunfish]] and other exotic species pose a threat.

## Background & Description

Sonora has a high level of endemism, hybridization, and genetic isolation among its freshwater fishes, yet its fauna is poorly studied, especially in small basins such as the Río Matape. Earlier work covered the [[Río Yaqui]] (Hendrickson et al., 1980) and the Río Mayo (Vrijenhoek, 1978). For the Matape, Hendrickson et al. had reported three native species and suspected a fourth, and Vrijenhoek et al. (1985) cited *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* from the basin.

Sampling used two seines (5 m long, 12–8 and 6.4 mm mesh) and 3 mm mesh cuchara nets. Water temperature and dissolved oxygen were measured at each site with a YSI Model 57 oximeter, and benthic samples were taken with a 9.5 cm diameter acrylic corer. Fish were preserved in 10% formalin, transferred to isopropyl alcohol, and identified using standard keys (Eddy and Underhill, 1957; Lee et al., 1980).

**Gila purpurea (Yaqui Chub, Charalito Yaqui)**: A small cyprinid described by Girard (1856/1857) from the San Bernardino River. It is placed in the subgenus *Temeculina* of *Gila* (Barbour and Miller, 1978). It occupies permanent, often deep microhabitats adjacent to fixed objects such as cliff faces, boulders, and tree roots. It is tied to springs and artesian wells, and it reproduces in spring and early summer. Males develop metallic blue coloration. Diet includes algae, terrestrial insects, and aquatic insects when abundant.

**Poeciliopsis occidentalis (Sonoran Topminnow)**: Widespread in the Matape, Yaqui, and Mayo basins, typically in intermittent, warm, low-elevation streams. It usually co-occurs with *Agosia chrysogaster*, and the association is characteristic of the [[Sonoran Desert]] fauna. Breeding extends from late January to August, pregnant females occur year-round, and males turn black when sexually active. It is declared endangered in the United States (Meffe et al., 1983).

**Poeciliopsis monacha-occidentalis (Unisexual Hybrid)**: An all-female form believed to arise from a cross between *P. monacha* and *P. occidentalis* in the Río Mayo (Schultz, 1977). It reproduces by [[Hybridogenesis]], which depends on sperm from *P. occidentalis* males, and it can be distinguished from its parent species mainly by mandibular tooth structure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Matape drains an arid region of the Sonora Sur hydrological region, covering roughly 2,500 km² between approximately 27°51′ and 28°55′ N and 109°15′ and 110°20′ W. Most of the channel is intermittent. Water flows only in the north during rain events, and a dam (Presa Ignacio L. Alatorre) blocks flow to the southern reach before the river reaches the [[Gulf Of California]] at Bahía de Guaymas. Agricultural and livestock users pump water, which gradually lowers water levels. Contamination is mainly domestic in origin, and the authors detected no industrial sources.

Sampling sites were predominantly lotic (flowing), shallow, and slow-moving. Only two lentic habitats were found, both reservoirs: Presa Ignacio L. Alatorre and Presa La Haciendita. The authors regard the basin as favorable for maintaining native populations, but they noted localized disturbance indicated by Chironomidae and Corixidae, which are bioindicators of water quality. Table 2 lists habitat characteristics for each site, with associated riparian vegetation such as *Baccharis glutinosa*, *Salix*, and *Prosopis*.

Overall, the authors describe the northwestern Mexican freshwater fauna as fragile and poorly documented, with known extinctions and threatened species. Introductions of exotic fishes are not well documented in Sonora, and their effects on native populations are unknown.

## Key Findings

- **Total sample:** 984 specimens of four species were collected from eight localities, with 974 native and 10 exotic individuals.
- ***Gila purpurea*:** The text reports 97 specimens from four collections (localities 3, 4, 7, and 8). The table's totals for this species are partly illegible after OCR. The species showed lower abundance than *Poeciliopsis* but greater biomass among native fishes.
- ***Poeciliopsis occidentalis*:** 477 specimens, found across the sampled sites; the authors describe it as the most abundant native species in number but not in biomass.
- ***Poeciliopsis monacha-occidentalis*:** 403 specimens, found in 87% of collections. It was absent only at locality 3, which is typical *Gila* habitat.
- ***Lepomis cyanellus*:** 10 specimens, all from locality 6 (Presa Ignacio L. Alatorre), where it dominated the habitat and was observed feeding on *Poeciliopsis*. It is the only exotic species collected.
- **Other exotic species:** Residents reported [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and a crappie (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) in Presa Alatorre, plus the same three species and the [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*) introduced at La Haciendita. These records were unverified and lacked introduction dates.
- **Regional context:** Sonora's basins hold roughly 32 native and about 19 non-native freshwater fish species. Its fauna is transitional among the Colorado–Gila, Mayo–Fuerte, and Chihuahuan Desert systems.
- **Threats:** The authors identify habitat reduction, water pumping, and exotic introductions as the main risks. They cite Meffe et al. (1983), who documented declines of *P. occidentalis* in Arizona after habitat modification and exotic introduction.
- **Recommendations:** The authors call for protected areas, controls on exotic introductions, socioeconomic studies of introduced species for food and recreation, and periodic monitoring of fish populations and community structure across the whole river.
