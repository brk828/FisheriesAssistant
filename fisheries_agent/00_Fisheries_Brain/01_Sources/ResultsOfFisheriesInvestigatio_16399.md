# Results of Fisheries Investigations at Twin Lakes, Colorado, 1973-76

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #lake-trout #mysidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation and [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] joint report documents preoperational conditions of the sport fishery in Twin Lakes, Colorado, before the [[Mt. Elbert Pumped-Storage Powerplant]] began operating. It covers creel census data (1973-75, with winter data to 1975-76), gill-net sampling of [[Lake Trout]], and trawl and underwater photographic sampling of [[Opossum Shrimp]]. The study establishes a baseline against which postoperational changes in fish populations, invertebrates and limnology will be compared. The fishery is dominated by angler harvest of [[Rainbow Trout]], with a self-sustaining but modest lake trout population.

## Background & Description

Twin Lakes is a glacially formed, two-basin lake system (lower lake roughly 736 ha, upper lake roughly 263 ha) on Lake Creek, south of Leadville. Combined maximum area is about 1000 ha, and maximum volume is 1.60 x 10^8 m^3. The lakes have been used for irrigation storage since 1901.

The lake is part of the [[Fryingpan-Arkansas Project]], a Bureau of Reclamation water development system. The planned pumped-storage operation would enlarge the lakes and increase annual water-level fluctuation from about 6 m (20 ft) to about 9 m (30 ft), with daily fluctuations possibly exceeding 0.3 m (1 ft).

Historical records list landlocked salmon, greenback cutthroat trout, Yellowfin cutthroat trout, McCloud River rainbow trout, brook trout and lake trout. Today only lake trout and brook trout occur as native-era species; rainbow trout, kokanee salmon, cutthroat trout, Bonneville cisco and American smelt have been stocked, and occasional brown trout are found.

A self-reproducing lake trout population has existed since the early 1900s. Opossum shrimp were introduced in 1957 and are a major component of the lake trout diet. Lake trout management included a 380 mm (15 in) size limit (1961), a 508 mm (20 in) limit (1972), and reinstatement of the 380 mm limit in 1974.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Physical and chemical limnology:** Table 1 (1974 data, Sartoris) reports upper lake surface area 263 ha, maximum depth 28 m; lower lake 736 ha, maximum depth 27 m. Bottom temperatures were 9.0 °C (upper) and 8.2 °C (lower).
- **Winter anoxia:** Severe anaerobic conditions in the upper lake during winter 1974-75 released heavy metals from bottom mud, and spring overturn was associated with a sharp drop in aquatic invertebrates.
- **Lake trout ecology:** Movement was confined largely to early evening and pre-dawn periods, which matters for pumping-cycle exposure.
- **Opossum shrimp ecology:** Shrimp move from bottom to upper strata in the late evening, coinciding with the proposed pumping cycle. Benthic distribution is clumped (negative binomial model; Gregg).
- **Predicted operational risks:** Turbine mortality (literature review by Griest), outlet escapement of trout, entrainment of shrimp, turbidity from resuspended glacial flour, and sedimentation affecting lake trout reproduction.
- **Mitigation plans:** A screened diversion structure to bypass fish from the Otero Canal to a stilling basin, stream improvement of Lake Creek, and federal land acquisition around the lakes.

## Key Findings

**Fishery use and harvest**
- Angling effort exceeded 100 000 man-hours yearly since 1973. Annual harvest was nearly 2000 lake trout and 50 000 rainbow trout.
- Summer 1973: 2073 fishermen contacted, 4042 hours, 1193 rainbow trout and 11 legal lake trout.
- Summer 1974: 2590 fishermen, 8876 hours, 2251 rainbow trout and 105 lake trout.
- Summer 1975: 2578 fishermen, 7762 hours, 3189 rainbow trout and 97 lake trout.
- Comparative 1959 data (Nolting): roughly 850 lake trout and 6800 rainbow trout in 30 000 hours.
- Summer lower-lake pressure was statistically stable across years. Upper-lake pressure fell significantly in 1974.

**Rainbow trout stocking**
- Stocking averaged about 165 000 per year from 1965 to 1973 and was reduced by about 50 percent after 1974.
- Plants made before mid-May showed poor survival. Plants of 75 000 to 100 000 fish at 2-week intervals through summer gave acceptable returns, and this schedule was recommended.

**Lake trout population**
- Gill-net catches: 206 (1974) and 218 (1975) from the lower lake; mean catch per net 4.90 (1974) and 5.19 (1975).
- Catch rose from May to November. Deepwater (>15 m) stations averaged 6.40 fish per net versus 3.25 in shallow water.
- Length range was 177-865 mm. No conclusive trend in numbers or size was found between years.
- Winter catch per man-hour (CPMH) was 0.144 (1974-75) and 0.136 (1975-76), while summer CPMH was about 0.012.
- The authors concluded natural recruitment sustains a stable, modest fishery. Stocked marked fingerlings had not reached legal size and were unlikely to contribute.

**Opossum shrimp**
- Trawl densities generally rose through summer, peaking in August or September, then declined. Juvenile growth to a maximum of 9 mm by August means trawls mainly sample adults.
- Two-year factorial ANOVA showed significant differences among months and sections along the traverse line, but not between years in random areas.
- Underwater photographs (about 0.1 m² per frame) correlated with trawl counts (r = 0.9183; Y = -7.48 + 0.46X). Trawl counts may be up to 60 percent below actual densities.

**Predicted pumped-storage impacts**
- Literature on other pumped-storage plants reports 55-65 percent mortality for fish passing through turbines during pumping and 40 percent during generation.
- Only 150 mm (6 in) trashracks protected the Mt. Elbert forebay intake.
- Over 100 legal lake trout were taken below the outlet in May-June 1974, indicating significant escapement losses.
- Shrimp entrainment is expected at tailrace velocities above 0.15 m/s (0.5 ft/s). The population-level effect remains undetermined.
- Hydraulic models predict little temperature change, but turbidity from resuspended glacial flour could reduce light penetration and primary productivity.

**Conclusions and next steps**
- The baseline establishes current fishery conditions for postoperational comparison.
- Postoperational studies were scheduled to start in early 1981 by the Division of Wildlife, the Colorado Cooperative Fishery Research Unit and the Bureau of Reclamation.
- Fishing pressure and harvest estimates are expected to shift in response to operational changes and proposed access and facility developments.
