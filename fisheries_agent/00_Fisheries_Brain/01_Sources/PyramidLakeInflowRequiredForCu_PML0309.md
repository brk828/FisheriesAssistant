# Pyramid Lake Inflow and Instream Flow Requirements for Cui-ui and Lahontan Cutthroat Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report by Chester C. Buchanan (Great Basin Complex, Reno, Nevada) defines the river flows needed to sustain two endangered or threatened fishes of [[Pyramid Lake]]: the endangered [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*) and the threatened [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki henshawi*). Both are obligatory stream spawners. Channelization of the [[Truckee River]] and out-of-basin diversions to the Newlands Project have blocked access to spawning grounds and degraded spawning and rearing habitat. The report proposes two minimum flow components: a minimum annual lake inflow to keep the lake high enough for spawners to reach the river, and a minimum instream flow to attract spawners and support reproduction. It also outlines a combined regime and a management strategy, and it notes that these flows may need refinement as spawning knowledge improves.

## Background & Description

- **Cui-ui** (*Chasmistes cujus*) is endemic to Pyramid Lake. Its survival depends on successful spawning in the lower Truckee River. Progeny are supplemented by relatively small numbers of hatchery-reared larvae.
- **Lahontan cutthroat trout** (LCT) of Pyramid Lake are listed as threatened. The population is now maintained largely by hatchery-reared fish.
- Successful cui-ui reproduction occurred in some years of the past ten, aided by the [[Marble Bluff Fish Facility]], [[Stampede Reservoir]] releases, and several wet years.
- Since the mid-1970s the authors have tried to define the flows these fishes need. Data from the preceding eight years led to two requirements: (1) a minimum average annual inflow to maintain lake elevation for spawner access; and (2) a minimum instream flow to attract spawners and provide reproductive habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake elevation and passage routes**
- Two routes reach the river: over the delta when water depth allows, or through the [[Pyramid Lake Fishway]]. Delta passage is preferred because runs are larger and earlier and less stressful than fishway passage.
- The minimum lake elevation for [[Delta Passage]] is 3,810 to 3,815 ft above mean sea level. The range reflects seasonal lake fluctuation, sediment deposition at the lake-river interface, and scouring by spring freshets.
- At least 3 ft of water over the delta is needed for unrestricted passage and to reduce bird predation. A shoal about 1.5 ft deep blocks passage.
- The fishway operates between 3,784 and 3,810 ft. It runs at 50 cfs full discharge when the lake is 3,784 to 3,800 ft, and at 15 to 35 cfs above 3,800 ft.
- A minimum average annual inflow of 406,000 to 413,000 acre-feet maintains the required lake elevation.

**Cui-ui cues and constraints**
- Photoperiod likely controls the annual reproductive cycle. Freshwater inflow, instream flow, temperature, and river access modulate it seasonally and daily.
- A freshwater lens in the south end of the lake, or inflowing freshwater, appears to attract and hold the pre-spawning aggregate at the river mouth.
- Water temperature strongly influences daily upstream movement. Warm temperatures correlate with spawner movement in many years at the [[Marble Bluff Fish Facility]].
- Insufficient depth can delay or reduce runs and expose fish to predation by white pelicans and cormorants.
- Constant incubation flows are needed to prevent dewatering or scouring of [[Redd]]s.

**Spawning habitat**
- A [[Instream Flow Study]] between the dams found maximum spawning area at 750 cfs (8,274 ft² per 1,000 linear feet). Area dropped by about 2,200 ft² per 1,000 linear feet at 500 cfs, but only about 600 ft² at 1,000 cfs.
- Flows providing at least 3,000 ft² per 1,000 linear feet are considered sufficient for runs at least as large as 36,000 fish. This assumes about 5 linear miles of suitable substrate and an average of 2 ft² per spawning fish.

**Water temperature**
- The [[Truckee River Temperature Prediction Model]] (Bureau of Reclamation, calibrated May 11, 1984) estimated flows that keep daily maximum temperatures at or below 63°F for cui-ui incubation.
- In May, 1,000 cfs gives 63°F under normal air temperatures, and 2,400 cfs is needed under warm air temperatures. Cold air temperatures keep all flows below 63°F.
- In June, 3,500 cfs is needed under cold air and 4,500 cfs under normal air. Under warm air, 63°F is exceeded at all flows.
- Survival falls sharply above the 63°F critical temperature.
- A large May-to-June flow increase scours embryos and pre-swimup larvae. Holding flows steady reduces scouring but raises temperature-related mortality. For example, 1,000 cfs gives 63°F in May but 69°F in June.

**LCT thermal requirements**
- Gravid LCT tolerate a maximum of 56°F while migrating upstream (February to May). Higher temperatures kill developing eggs even though adults survive.
- Optimum egg incubation is 50 to 56°F. Fry prefer 55 to 60°F and tolerate up to 70°F. Adults prefer 55 to 68°F and survive brief exposure to 80°F.
- Under existing habitat, LCT incubation and fry rearing require flows well above what is usually available. Over 4,000 cfs is needed for 56°F in May, and 4,000 cfs for 70°F in June.

## Key Findings

**1. Lake inflow sets delta access.** A minimum average annual inflow of 406,000 to 413,000 acre-feet is needed to keep the lake at the 3,810 to 3,815 ft delta-passage range.

**2. Attraction flow depends on the passage route.** Attraction volumes are 52,000 to 87,000 acre-feet for delta passage and 176,000 acre-feet for fishway passage (January to April). Increasing attraction flow generally increases run size. Runs from 1984 to 1986, when delta passage was available, were relatively large and early.

**3. Minimum cui-ui regime (Table 2).** Attraction flows total 52,000 acre-feet (January 6,000; February 7,000; March 12,000; April 27,000). Spawning, incubation, and rearing flows of 61,500 acre-feet (May) and 59,500 acre-feet (June) at 1,000 cfs each are included. The total is 197,000 acre-feet.

**4. Alternate minimum regime (Table 3).** Using 87,000 acre-feet for attraction flows gives a total of 232,000 acre-feet.

**5. Optimum cui-ui regime (Table 4).** Based on wet-year hydrographs, attraction flows total 175,000 acre-feet (January to April), with 150,000 acre-feet each in May and June at 2,500 cfs. The total is 507,000 acre-feet. This is 275,000 to 310,000 acre-feet more than the minimum regimes.

**6. Outmigration flows.** Incubation flows must continue until at least 75 percent of larvae emerge. Flows should then ramp down gradually (about 100 cfs per day), giving pre-swimup larvae at least one week to acclimate. In July, a common ramp goes from 1,000 cfs to 500 cfs.

**7. LCT regimes.** Delta passage needs 200 cfs and fishway passage needs 50 cfs. Trout egg collection at the Marble Bluff Fish Facility requires about 900 cfs in April under normal air temperatures. The combined LCT regime (Table 5) totals 478,500 acre-feet. Because 4,000 cfs is needed to maintain incubation habitat, LCT incubation cannot be provided until the lower river's flow-temperature relation improves.

**8. Combined regime (Table 7).** The monthly maximum of each species' requirements forms the combined regime, totalling roughly 224,000 acre-feet. The smaller cui-ui attraction volume is used to conserve water in Stampede Reservoir. The authors expect this conservative approach may leave January and February flows too low for trout passage.

**9. Long-term uncertainty.** Regime attainment depends on Stampede storage, precipitation, and domestic and agricultural diversions. The regime may not be met in many years, possibly for several years in a row. Keeping Pyramid Lake at or above 3,815 ft increases the chance of achieving the instream flow regime.

**10. Management strategy.** Because cui-ui year-class diversity is precariously low and adult numbers are small, managers aim to maximize attraction and instream flows for each run. Stampede releases are used sparingly to conserve water. LCT releases are made only when they will not reduce cui-ui spawning potential. Snowmelt forecasts and storage data are used to set a conservative target regime each year.

**11. Recommendations for LCT restoration.** A ladder over [[Derby Dam]] would give LCT access to upstream habitat, where water is more available and temperatures are less limiting. The LCT reproduction regime should be implemented only when water supply is adequate and does not interfere with cui-ui needs.
