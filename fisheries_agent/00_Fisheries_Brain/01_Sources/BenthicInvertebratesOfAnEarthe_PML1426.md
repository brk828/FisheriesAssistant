# Benthic Invertebrates of an Earthen Canal in the Phoenix Metropolitan Area, Arizona

#benthic-macroinvertebrates #freshwater-ecology #oligochaeta

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Paul C. Marsh]] describes the benthic macroinvertebrate community, abundance, and seasonal dynamics of a 100-m reach of [[Lateral 9.5]], a small earthen canal of the [[Consolidated Canal]] in Mesa, Arizona. Sampling ran for 13 months, from November 1981 to November 1982. The fauna was numerically dominated by non-insects, chiefly [[Oligochaeta]] and the introduced clam [[Corbicula Fluminea]]. Insects, including [[Chironomidae]] and the mayfly [[Callibaetis]], made up a small share of the total. The author notes that the invertebrates could serve as forage for canal fishes, and that canals of this kind are growing in importance as aquatic habitat in arid American Southwest landscapes.

## Background & Description

Artificial waterways such as canals now make up a major proportion of aquatic habitat in American arid lands. Southwestern canals support taxonomically and numerically depauperate but substantial invertebrate communities. Earlier work in the region, cited by the author, includes studies by [[Marsh and Minckley]] (1982), Marsh et al. (1983), and Minckley et al. (1983). This paper is presented as part of a continuing evaluation of the aquatic potential of canal habitats in the Southwest.

The study site was a 100-m reach of Lateral 9.5 of the Consolidated Canal in Mesa, Maricopa County, Arizona. The canal serves primarily as a conveyance for irrigation and floodwater return flows. It is subject to stilling and partial dewatering each autumn as part of an annual maintenance program.

Physical characteristics reported:
- Trapezoidal cross-section with 1.5:1 side slopes
- Mean water width of 7 m and mean channel depth of 0.95 m
- Mean velocity of 0.07 m/s
- Substrate predominantly silt, with minor sand and small gravel
- Mid-day water temperature ranged from 12.5 °C (January) to 30.5 °C (summer)
- Algae abundant in autumn, including [[Chara]] and filamentous forms; aquatic macrophytes [[Myriophyllum Spicatum]] and [[Potamogeton Pectinatus]] present; riparian vegetation dominated by [[Bermuda Grass]]

Methods: Five replicate invertebrate samples were collected on each sampling date using an Ekman dredge (232 cm²). Samples were sieved through 500 µm mesh, preserved in 10% formalin, and returned to the laboratory for sorting, identification, and enumeration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The canal fauna was generally similar in species composition and relative abundance to that of the [[Coachella Canal]] in southeastern California. Both canals, however, have sharply reduced species richness compared with natural streams at similar elevation in Arizona. Natural streams there support more than 100 taxa, particularly mayflies and chironomids (Bruns and Minckley 1980; Gray 1981; Minckley 1981). In those streams, aquatic insects usually make up a much larger share of the fauna than in the canal.

Even Sonoran Desert spring habitats, which are considered taxonomically depauperate (Meffe and Marsh 1983), may contain two to three times as many species as earthen canals. The author suggests that the greater richness of canals than springs reflects structural habitat homogeneity in springs, not physicochemical limits. Earthen canals provide more habitat diversity than natural springs.

Canal management activities, including autumn dewatering and herbicide application, may limit the diversity of canal invertebrates. The author reports no evidence that flow pulses in either canal scour substrates or remove invertebrates, as occurs in natural desert streams after flash floods (Fisher et al. 1982).

Fish observations: Large, main-channel canals often hold many fish species (Marsh and Minckley 1982). Small canals in the Phoenix area, however, frequently contain only introduced [[Mosquitofish]] ([[Gambusia Affinis]]) and [[Red Shiner]] ([[Notropis Lutrensis]]). The author argues that food availability does not explain this difference in species richness. Instead, habitat size, constraints on fish access, unavailability of refugia in smaller canals, and herbicide use are more likely causes.

## Key Findings

**Community composition**
- The fauna was numerically dominated by non-insects. Oligochaeta were the most abundant group, and the introduced clam Corbicula fluminea was also abundant. The text gives a snail value of 288/m².
- Chironomidae (655/m²) and the mayfly Callibaetis together made up only about 4.5% of the fauna.
- Taxa recorded in the table include the damselflies Enallagma praevarum and Enallagma sp., the dragonflies Cordulia shurtleffi and Gomphus oliveaceus, the beetle Peltodytes callosus, the caddisfly family Hydroptilidae, leeches (Hirudinea), and the crayfish Procambarus clarkii.
- Table 1 is only partly legible in the scan, so exact means and ranges for each taxon cannot be fully confirmed.

**Seasonal dynamics**
- Oligochaete abundance peaked in February and July, when mean individual size was small, indicating recruitment.
- Corbicula fluminea density was highest in February, with no secondary peak.
- Chironomidae and Callibaetis reached maximal numbers in November.
- Smaller individuals of most major taxa were present at times of peak population abundance, which suggests a synchronized period of hatching.

**Comparisons and interpretation**
- Species composition and relative abundance were similar to those in the Coachella Canal, but richness was lower than in natural Arizona streams.
- The invertebrate fauna may serve as forage for canal fishes.
- Canals in Arizona are likely to be used for recreation as natural surface waters decline and demand for nearby recreational opportunities rises. The author argues that studies of water quality, biological community structure, and distribution are needed before canal systems can be managed as recreational resources.
