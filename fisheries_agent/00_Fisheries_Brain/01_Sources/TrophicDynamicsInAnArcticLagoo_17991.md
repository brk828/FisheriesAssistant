# Trophic Dynamics in an Arctic Lagoon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coregonidae #arctic-food-web

## Executive Summary

This interdisciplinary study by [[Peter C. Craig]] and colleagues examines the trophic structure of [[Simpson Lagoon]], a shallow arctic coastal lagoon in the central [[Alaskan Beaufort Sea]]. The authors work top-down, from birds and fish (consumers) to their invertebrate prey and finally to the carbon sources sustaining the nearshore food web. The lagoon's birds and fish feed overwhelmingly on epibenthic [[Mysids]] and [[Amphipods]], which are themselves supported mainly by [[Primary Production]] from offshore waters rather than by terrestrial peat carbon. Food is superabundant, with prey standing stocks roughly 50 times the daily requirements of consumers, and the system is characterized as a physically disturbed, early-successional habitat with high diet overlap.

## Background & Description

The study was part of the Beaufort Sea barrier island–lagoon ecological process studies sponsored by the [[Outer Continental Shelf Environmental Assessment Program]] (OCSEAP). It draws on companion studies of birds (Johnson and Richardson, 1981), fish (Craig and Haldorson, 1981), invertebrates (Griffiths and Dillinger, 1981) and primary productivity (Schell et al., 1982).

[[Simpson Lagoon]] is roughly 35 km long, 3–6 km wide and averages 2 m deep (maximum 3 m), with a flat, nearly featureless floor covered by detritus over mud and sand. The ice-free season runs from early July to early October. Summer salinities range from 1 to 32 ppt and temperatures from 0 to 14 °C, usually 4–5 ppt and 2–4 °C within the lagoon. Winter ice thickens to about 2 m, roughly 90% of the lagoon volume freezes by April, and hypersaline conditions reach up to 68 ppt.

Key vertebrate consumers were:
- **Birds:** [[Oldsquaw]] (tens of thousands concentrate mid-July to mid-August during molt), and the [[Red-Necked Phalarope]] and [[Red Phalarope]]. Over 100 bird species have been recorded in the Alaskan Beaufort Sea, but only a small number occupy nearshore lagoons in summer.
- **Fish:** Over 30 species have been recorded, but a few dominate. The anadromous [[Arctic Cisco]], [[Least Cisco]] and [[Arctic Char]], and the marine [[Fourhorn Sculpin]] and [[Arctic Cod]] are the key species, with [[Boreal Smelt]], [[Broad Whitefish]] and [[Lake Whitefish]] also locally abundant.
- **Marine mammals:** negligible; only a few seals seen during aerial surveys.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Invertebrates are grouped as infauna, epibenthos and pelagic forms. No kelp community exists because of the lack of rocky substrate. Infauna, including polychaetes and bivalves, is restricted to the deeper (>2 m) areas, since about one-third of the lagoon freezes solid. The epibenthos is dominated by amphipods (e.g., [[Onisimus glacialis]], [[Gammarus setosus]]), mysids ([[Mysis litoralis]], [[Mysis relicta]]) and the isopod [[Saduria entomon]]. Pelagic forms include copepods (Calanus spp.) and chaetognaths (Sagitta elegans). Epibenthic and pelagic forms are largely absent in winter, recolonizing each spring.

Methods combined shotgun-collected birds, gill- and fyke-net fish (684 specimens of 7 species, 1977–79), stomach content analysis by wet weight, diver-based and drop-net invertebrate sampling, and 14C-uptake primary productivity measurements. Stable carbon isotope ratios (13C/12C) were used to trace carbon sources. Dietary overlap was assessed with the [[Schoener Overlap Index]], where values of 0.6 or greater were considered biologically significant.

## Key Findings

- **Diet convergence:** Mobile epibenthic crustaceans made up over 90% of the diet for arctic cisco, least cisco, arctic char, arctic cod and oldsquaw. Boreal smelt ate fish (41%), phalaropes ate zooplankton (36%), and fourhorn sculpin and arctic flounder fed on a bottom-crawling isopod (30–47%). Glaucous gulls were the most generalist, eating epibenthos, small fish, birds and camp garbage.
- **Unused prey:** Infauna and sedentary epibenthos were about as abundant as mobile epibenthos (biomass 0.59–2.1 vs 0.3–2.5 g AFDW m⁻²) but were little used. Oldsquaw ate infauna only in early summer (about 10% of diet).
- **Short food chain:** Fish and birds feed mainly on mysids and amphipods, which feed on phytoplankton.
- **Seasonal shift:** Summer diets were similar among predators, while winter diets diverged. Summer-to-winter overlap was very low for arctic cisco (C = 0.0), least cisco (0.01), fourhorn sculpin (0.13) and arctic cod (0.26). Phalaropes switched from copepods (1977) to amphipods (1978).
- **Summer overlap:** Only 20% (1977) and 27% (1978) of predator pairs showed overlap above 0.6, but these reflected reliance on the two mysid species.
- **Food superabundance:** Prey supply exceeded consumer requirements by roughly 50 times in both years. Oldsquaw food intake was up to 14 mg AFDW m⁻² day⁻¹ in 1977 and 8.7 in 1978. Fish requirements were generally 1.6 mg AFDW m⁻² day⁻¹, peaking at 8.5 during the August 1978 arctic cod run.
- **Immigration dependence:** Consumers would quickly deplete mysids without continual immigration. Mysis litoralis moves into the lagoon in early summer, and the authors suggest that poorly flushed coastal habitats are weaker feeding areas.
- **Carbon sources:** Primary production, in situ or advected, supplies about 90% of carbon in amphipods and mysids. Peat carbon contributes less than 10% in most samples. Annual lagoon primary production is estimated at 5–7 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹, a small fraction of terrestrial inputs (about 10⁸ kg C yr⁻¹). Secondary consumers ingest about 6.9 g C m⁻² yr⁻¹, which the authors consider balanced with primary production.
- **Advection:** Low phytoplankton growth and a 3–4 day turnover imply a large advective input from offshore; exchange times can be as short as 24 h under strong winds.
- **Disturbance and succession:** Simpson Lagoon is a pioneer, early-successional system. High dietary overlap (50% in 1978 versus 31% in 1977 by the [[Tyler]] method) places it in the "most disturbed" category.
- **Caveats:** Some density estimates are imprecise, and a single August 1978 arctic cod school may have depleted mysids locally. Empty fish stomachs were frequent at some coastal sites, which the authors attribute to feeding periodicity or migration.
