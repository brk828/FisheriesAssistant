# Some Common Taxonomic Errors in Zoology Textbooks

#taxonomy #zoological-nomenclature #trematoda

## Executive Summary

R. E. Blackwelder (Department of Zoology, Southern Illinois University) critiques recurring taxonomic errors in general biology and zoology textbooks, which he attributes to authors who are usually not experienced taxonomists. The article lists thirteen errors and ends with a numbered summary of corrective rules. It covers the naming and hierarchy of organisms, [[Zoological Nomenclature]], [[Binomial Nomenclature]], the use of [[Taxonomic Categories]] and taxa, body symmetry, and the [[Coelom]] classification. A short "Interesting Slides" note on [[Fasciola hepatica]] life history closes the issue. The document is a commentary and contains no original data, sample sizes or statistics.

## Background & Description

The article is a commentary in *Turtox News* (Vol. 46, No. 2, February 1968; © General Biological Supply House). Blackwelder argues that textbook errors persist because they are repeated from text to text, and that published criticism is needed to stop them. He presents each error with an explanation of what is wrong and how to correct it.

The errors discussed are:

1. **Classification lists.** A nested list of groups (e.g., Kingdom Animalia through Species Sapiens) is a hierarchy, not a classification, because it lists rather than groups.
2. **Species names.** The species name must be binomial, e.g., *Homo sapiens*. *Sapiens* alone is not the name of a species. Shortening to the second part is acceptable only as a convenience after the full name has been given (e.g., *Felis leo* and *Felis tigris* shortened to *leo* and *tigris*).
3. **Subdivision of groups.** Genera and families are built by combining lower-level groups, not by dividing higher ones. Subfamilies are an exception formed by dividing a family.
4. **Parenthetical synonyms.** Parentheses between the two parts of a species name are reserved for subgeneric names. A synonym belongs after the correct name (e.g., *Clonorchis sinensis* with *Opisthorchis sinensis* cited as a synonym).
5. **Synonymy.** All names given to one species are synonyms. Only the rejected ones should be called rejected synonyms.
6. **Nomenclatural authority.** The [[International Commission On Zoological Nomenclature]] can interpret, suspend and propose rules but cannot make or enforce them. Rule-making rests with the [[International Congress Of Zoology]], and compliance depends on taxonomists' opinion.
7. **Generic figures.** Labels such as "Hydra" or "Lumbricus" on figures imply a genus, which cannot be drawn. The figure should be explained as a specimen of an unspecified species.
8. **Categories versus taxa.** Categories are levels in the hierarchy and do not change; the groups (taxa) placed at those levels do.
9. **Binomial versus uninominal names.** Zoological names are given at about twenty or more levels. Binomials occur at only one level (species), and one level uses trinomials; all others are uninominal.
10. **"Typical" representatives.** Examples such as [[Ascaris Lumbricoides]], [[Helix Pomatia]] and [[Hydra Oligactis]] are not typical of their groups and should not be described that way.
11. **Symmetry concepts.** Radial, biradial and bilateral symmetry are applied inconsistently and do not yield useful insight, so the concept should be abandoned until it is restated.
12. **"True" and "false."** These adjectives are inappropriate in organ names. The [[Pseudocoel]] is a true body cavity, not a false coelom.
13. **Enterocoely and schizocoely.** These developmental categories were shown to be inconsistent (enterocoely in Brachiopoda, schizocoely in Aves), so they should be dropped from beginning texts unless used as a cautionary example.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The "Interesting Slides" section describes *Fasciola hepatica*, "probably the best known of all flukes," as the first digenetic trematode whose life cycle was fully worked out. Its encysted metacercarial stage occurs on vegetation in water rather than inside a host. The life cycle, as summarised, runs as follows:

- Adult flukes in the liver release eggs into the bile ducts.
- Eggs pass into the alimentary tract and out with the feces.
- After about four days to two weeks in water, the ciliated miracidium escapes from the shell.
- The miracidium swims and penetrates a compatible snail, which is its intermediate host.

The document gives no population data or environmental measurements.

## Key Findings

- Textbook hierarchies are lists of groups, not classifications; the lowest entry of such a list must be a binomial name.
- Genera and families are formed by combining lower-level groups, not by subdividing higher ones (subfamilies are the main exception).
- Parentheses in a species name are reserved for subgenera; synonyms should be cited after the accepted name.
- All names of a taxon are synonyms, and rejected names should be called rejected synonyms.
- The International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature has no power to make or enforce rules; that power lies with the International Congress of Zoology.
- Categories are hierarchy levels; taxa are the groups placed in them.
- Zoological nomenclature is not solely binomial: binomials are used only at the species level among roughly twenty levels.
- Representative or "typical" examples should be avoided; examples should be described as showing the fundamental features of their group.
- Symmetry terms as commonly defined are not useful and should be avoided unless restated meaningfully.
- "True" and "false" should not be used to describe organismal features.
- Enterocoely and schizocoely should not be used as classification criteria, as the facts do not support them.
- The *Fasciola hepatica* life cycle, with its water-borne metacercariae and snail intermediate host, is presented as an unusual example of a digenetic trematode life history.
