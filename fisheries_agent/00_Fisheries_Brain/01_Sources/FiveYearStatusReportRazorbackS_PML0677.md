# Five Year Status Report: Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a Five Year Status Report on the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), prepared by the California Department of Fish and Game (Endangered Species Project) and dated July 1, 1987. The report recommends that the species **retain its Endangered classification**. It attributes the decline of the species within the [[Colorado River Basin]] to dam construction, water diversions, channelization and dredging, and the introduction of exotic fishes, particularly predators such as [[Green Sunfish]] and hybridizing catostomids. The report also summarizes the state's 1985–1995 [[Management Plan]] for the species and the 1986 reintroduction of hatchery-reared fish into the lower Colorado River.

## Background & Description

**Document type and authorship.** The report was prepared by Susan R. Ellis, Fisheries Biologist, and reviewed by Dr. Peter B. Moyle (University of California, Davis) and Robert R. Rawstron, Chief of the Inland Fisheries Division. It was forwarded by the Region 5 office to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service under the Endangered Species Act.

**Physical description.** The species has an elongated head and a laterally compressed body. A sharp-edged hump or keel on the back, which develops with size and age, gives it the alternative name "humpback sucker." Diagnostic counts reported in the document are:

- Lateral line scales: 68–87
- Dorsal fin rays: 13–16
- Anal fin rays: 7
- Gill rakers: 35–50, slender and fuzzy in appearance

The mouth is subterminal, sometimes projecting forward in larger specimens, and the lips are weakly papillose. Adults are dusky brown to olivaceous dorsally and yellowish ventrally, with a dark lateral stripe. During spawning, males become black dorsally with an orange lateral stripe, and develop conical breeding tubercles on the caudal fin, pectoral and pelvic fins, and head. Females remain drab and have fewer tubercles, and their urogenital region is broader and fleshier.

**Identification of larvae.** Larvae are difficult to separate from other catostomid larvae. Useful characters are lip structure (deep median cleft), dorsal fin ray count, and melanophore distribution, following [[Winn and Miller]] (1954). Under laboratory conditions the nuchal hump begins to develop at about 2+ years (approximately 6 in standard length).

**Genetic and taxonomic context.** The report notes hybridization with other catostomids, which is discussed below under Key Findings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.**
- In the upper Colorado River basin, recent collections indicate a preference for stagnant or backwater areas and low-gradient, sandy-bottomed sites of the main stem.
- The species tends to favor warm tributaries (a mean of 72.8°F is cited) entering the colder main-stem waters (35–55°F), such as the mouth of the [[Yampa River]].
- [[Sigler and Miller]] (1963) associate the species with mud, sand, and rock bottoms where aquatic vegetation is scarce.
- In the lower basin, shallow pools and backwaters are considered important for juveniles, while adults appear to have adapted to the colder waters of reservoirs such as [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]], and [[Senator Wash Reservoir]].
- Historically, the species used irrigation canals in the Phoenix area, where individuals often became trapped.

**Threats.** The report identifies three main pressures:

1. **Habitat alteration.** More than 20 major dams have been built on the Colorado River and its tributaries. Each has significantly changed the riverine environment upstream and downstream, leaving few warm, shallow areas needed for spawning, nursery, resting, and feeding.
2. **Predation by exotic fishes.** Predation by exotic species on eggs, larvae, and fingerlings has been documented and may be a significant factor depressing lower basin populations. Green sunfish were cited as feeding heavily on eggs and possibly small larvae.
3. **Hybridization.** Hybridization with catostomids has increased over the same period in which populations declined. Non-native [[Longnose Sucker]] (*Catostomus catostomus*) and [[White Sucker]] (*C. commersoni*) have increased in the Colorado River, raising the potential for hybridization because the species can cross with other catostomids.

**Historic and current distribution.**
- *Historic:* Collections were made throughout the main-stem Colorado River and major tributaries from Wyoming to Sonora and Baja California del Norte, Mexico. The historic range extended into the [[Salton Sea]], most recently filled in 1905–1906, but the species disappeared as salinity rose.
- *Upper basin (current):* Individuals are scattered but concentrated at only two places: the mouth of the Yampa River, Colorado, and a flooded gravel pit (Walker Wildlife Area) near Grand Junction, Colorado. The species is considered extremely rare in the Green River (Utah–Colorado). In the Colorado mainstream it is scarce from Rifle, Colorado, downstream to Lake Powell, Utah. Only one specimen had recently been reported from the Gunnison River. No individuals had been taken in Wyoming since the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962.
- *Lower basin (current):* Records are sporadic and isolated. Large individuals were collected in 1973 by the Arizona Game and Fish Department from Imperial Reservoir. California Department of Fish and Game collected individuals from Davis Dam south to Imperial Dam in the mid- and late 1970s. A netting survey of the lower Colorado River from Parker Dam to the U.S.–Mexico boundary did not locate any Razorback Suckers. (The text reads "1991"; given the report date of 1987, this is likely an OCR error for 1981.)

**Notable events.**
- In 1977, a small irrigation reservoir in Utah connected to the San Juan River was drained, stranding 250 Razorback Suckers.
- Naturally stocked larvae have been found in Senator Wash Reservoir and Lake Mohave, but no recruitment into the adult population has been documented.

## Key Findings

**1. Dramatic decline.** Data indicate a drastic reduction in abundance over the past century, from common in the main river and large tributaries to extreme rarity. Adults now occur only in isolated areas of the basin.

**2. Principal causes.** Physical, chemical, and biological alterations of the Colorado River ecosystem, including dam construction, mainstream channelization and dredging, water diversions, and introduction of exotic species, are cited as critical factors.

**3. Population status by region.**
- *Upper basin:* Remnant and scattered; concentrated at two sites only.
- *Lower basin:* Remnant populations persist in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]], with Lake Mohave holding the largest known lower-basin population (Ulmer 1985). Adults continue to be observed in Lake Mead, but the population is believed to be low, with no documented recruitment.
- *California:* A small remnant adult population exists in Senator Wash Reservoir, Imperial County. Young fish were captured in 1973–1974 from ponds filled from the East Highline Canal near Niland, and seven young fish were collected in 1984 from lateral reservoirs near the Coachella Canal. Water for these canal systems comes from the All-American Canal, diverted from the Colorado River at Imperial Dam.

**4. Exotic predation as a threat to recovery.** Predation on eggs and larvae by [[Green Sunfish]] may limit maintenance of present population levels in the lower river. Mortality of adults by exotic predators is considered likely to result in extinction of the Lake Mohave and Senator Wash populations.

**5. Hybridization.** Rising hybridization with non-native catostomids is viewed as detrimental, since it occurred during the same period the species declined.

**6. Recommendation.** Retain Endangered status. The document concludes that survival depends on areas of the river that remain relatively unmodified. Restoring the species to its original range and abundance is considered impractical, so efforts should focus on protecting existing populations and reintroducing fish into unmodified portions of the historic range.

**7. Management plan (1985–1995).** The California Department of Fish and Game developed a [[Management Plan]] (Ulmer 1985) with the primary goal of improving the species' status in California by securing and perpetuating existing populations and establishing additional self-sustaining populations within its historic range in the state. Under this plan, the species was to be stocked into the lower Colorado River during the 10-year period.
- Fry are propagated at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] to meet stocking requirements.
- Approximately 10 percent of the fry are reared at the California Department of Fish and Game's Niland hatchery, and stocked at various sizes to determine the optimal release size for survival.
- Additional pre-release growth is expected to increase survivability and provide data on age and growth under natural and artificial conditions.
- The plan is to be reviewed and modified as necessary in 1991.

**8. 1986 reintroduction.** Razorback Suckers reared at Dexter National Fish Hatchery were reintroduced into the lower Colorado River in 1986. The success of this program and its impact on population size could not be determined at the time of the report.

**9. Data limitations.** The report states that the historic and current abundance data are based largely on sporadic survey records, and that the success of the reintroduction could not yet be evaluated.
