# Morphogenesis of the Gonopodium in Gambusia affinis affinis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

C. L. Turner (1941) describes the developmental morphogenesis of the anal fin in female and male [[Gambusia Affinis]] (a [[Poeciliidae]] livebearer), from the embryonic bud through the juvenile stage to the mature female fin and the male [[Gonopodium]]. The paper is explicitly a descriptive baseline: it establishes staging, segmentation and growth metrics that later experimental papers were to reference. The central conclusion is that the male gonopodium arises from the same developmental processes as the female fin, but a rapid growth shift toward rays 3, 4 and 5 (the "3-4-5 ray complex"), together with localized differentiation areas, produces the sex-specific pattern.

## Background & Description

- **Structure:** The gonopodium is a modified anal fin used for sperm transfer. Its basic skeleton has nine or ten segmented rays, of which rays 3, 4 and 5 elongate most. Gonopodial patterns are species-specific and have long been used for taxonomic identification within [[Poeciliidae]] (Regan 1913; Hubbs 1924, 1926).
- **Research question:** How much of the final gonopodial pattern is set by specific genetic factors versus endocrine and general organismic factors.
- **Methods:** Five approaches were planned: (1) normal morphogenesis and histogenesis; (2) extirpation of rays and regeneration studies; (3) androgen application to juveniles of both sexes; (4) castration and gonad transplantation; (5) comparative study across species. This paper covers only method 1 and descriptive baseline material.
- **Staging:** Development is staged by the number of segments in the unbranched third ray (the "index ray"), e.g., the twelve-, fifteen-, thirty-three-segment stages. Ray-segment formulas and graphs of ray length and segment number are used to record fin structure compactly.
- **Rearing conditions:** Specimens born in mid-August to mid-September were maintained in laboratory aquaria at about 24 °C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a laboratory developmental study and contains no field ecology data. The author notes that developmental rate depends on temperature and food and therefore varies in natural populations. The early sex-recognition feature (see Key Findings) is highlighted as useful for field workers on Gambusia who need to sex young fish. A brief survey of twelve other poeciliid species found the same early sex difference in all of them. Environmental and endocrine effects are deferred to later papers.

## Key Findings

**Embryonic and juvenile development**
- The anal fin bud acquires rays about 10 days after fertilization; all rays are single except ray 9, which is divided at the base.
- At about 5.8 mm (16 days, roughly 10 days before birth), rays 3, 4 and 5 are longest (about 0.8 mm), and segmentation begins in the longest rays.
- Just before birth (about 6 mm), the longest rays reach about 1 mm, and all rays except no. 1 are segmented.
- The juvenile stage lasts roughly 40 to 55 days, during which the embryo grows from about 6 mm to about 11 mm. The fin keeps its birth symmetry until about the ten-segment stage, when sex differences begin.
- At the nine-segment stage (45 to 55 days after birth), rays 4 to 9 begin terminal bifurcation and basal anchylosis of ray joints starts.

**Sex-specific divergence**
- Early sex recognition: by solidification of basal segments, the female fin shows three to five disappeared or faint intersegmental spaces at the nine or ten segment stage, whereas the male loses only a single one. This allowed hormone experiments on fish of known sex.
- Growth: at maturity, the male's ray 1 has about half the growth of the female's, and ray 2 is a little more than half as long. Rays 3, 4 and 5 are conspicuously longer in males. Rays 6 to 10 are suppressed. The author attributes this to a shift in dominance toward the 3-4-5 complex.
- Bifurcation: in males only one terminal division occurs in rays 4 to 9, whereas females show secondary and tertiary division. The author proposes that the rapid growth of rays 4 and 5 prevents further terminal division.
- Segmentation: rays 6 to 10 segment evenly in both sexes. Rays 3 to 5 in males show marked regional irregularity in segment length, which the author attributes to inequalities in growth and segmentation rates.

**Female fin in maturity**
- Females grow slowly and continuously until senescence, reached at about 50 mm or more in the Chicago area.
- In a 48 mm female, the fin's greatest length at ray 4 is about 7 mm.
- Rays 1, 2 and 3 never divide terminally. Secondary dichotomy appears at about 22 mm; tertiary division occurs in fish of 38 mm or more, mostly in rays 8 and 9.
- Anchylosis of basal joints continues throughout life, and some lateral fusion of branches appears in old females.

**Gonopodium differentiation areas (Areas I to VI)**
- Six localized differentiation areas arise sequentially and add bone to produce spines, hooks, serrae, and fused branches. These include the ray 3 ventral thickening, the ray 3 spines (~26 segment stage), the "elbow" on ray 4 (last to appear), two ventral hooks from rays 4 and 5 (~32 segment stage), serrae on ray 4 (~30 segment stage), and the largest area, VI, which twists ray 5 through 90 degrees and extends to rays 6 to 8.
- Androgens can induce these areas in females; early complete castration prevents them in males (St. Amant, unpublished laboratory work cited).

**Variation and maturity**
- Body length at maturity varies, suggesting early- and late-maturing strains. Figure 12 is based on 1084 randomly selected males, though the exact distribution values are not given in the text.
- Gonopod length and segment number scale with body length; fully differentiated males range from 18 mm to 27 mm standard length.

**Summary conclusions**
- Growth in the female fin and body is continuous and slow. The male fin transforms into a gonopodium in a relatively short time, after which growth stops.
- The male's sex-specific pattern arises from a growth-driven dominance of rays 3-5 and from localized differentiation areas, under partial control by sex hormones.
- The author notes that the species-specific response to hormones is likely common across poeciliid males and that a shared genetic basis probably underlies it. This is inferred, not directly tested.
