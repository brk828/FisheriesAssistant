# Endangered and Threatened Fishes of the West

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

James E. Deacon's symposium paper, published in the Great Basin Naturalist Memoirs (No. 3, 1979), reviews the endangered and threatened freshwater fishes of western North America. It argues that habitat modification is the dominant threat, that western fishes are especially sensitive to biotic interactions, and that many taxa are restricted to single springs or short stream reaches. Case studies cover the [[Virgin River]] fishes ([[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]]), parasitism by *Lernaea* on [[White River Springfish]], goldfish and mosquitofish effects on [[Pahrump Killifish]], and water-level control of [[Devils Hole Pupfish]]. The paper closes with a philosophical argument for saving species and an extended question-and-answer session on flow requirements and the [[Warner Valley Project]].

## Background & Description

The western US freshwater fauna has relatively low diversity and a high proportion of endemic taxa with limited distributions (Miller 1959; Smith 1978). These traits make it vulnerable. The [[American Fisheries Society]] Endangered Species Committee compiled a list of threatened North American fishes (Deacon et al. 1979). Threats were grouped into five categories: (1) habitat destruction or modification; (2) overutilization; (3) disease or parasitism; (4) other factors such as hybridization, introduced species, predation and competition; and (5) restricted range.

Table 5 of the source lists described threatened taxa from western North America, grouped by family, with threat codes and historical distribution. Major groups include trouts (Salmonidae), minnows (Cyprinidae), suckers (Catostomidae), catfishes (Ictaluridae), killifishes (Cyprinodontidae), livebearers (Poeciliidae), sticklebacks, sunfishes, darters, gobies and sculpins. Table 6 lists undescribed threatened taxa, such as Alvord cutthroat trout, Catlow Tui chub and Preston White River springfish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threat patterns.** Habitat modification (Category 1) affects 97.3% of listed western fishes (109 of 112 taxa), compared with the eastern fauna (Table 1). No western species is threatened by overexploitation, whereas about 7% of eastern fishes (6 *Coregonus* ciscoes in the Great Lakes, plus Atlantic whitefish) are. Disease and parasitism affect about 4% of western taxa and none of the eastern ones listed. Biotic interactions affect 54% of threatened western fishes versus 9% of eastern fishes. Restricted range applies to 21% of western and about 7% of eastern taxa.

**Evolutionary sensitivity.** Western fishes evolved in relatively depauperate faunas and have high tolerance of physical extremes but low tolerance of biological interactions (Smith 1978; Deacon and Minckley 1974). Many exist as relict populations in single habitats, a pattern Smith (1978) describes as "extinction resistance."

**Historical habitat change.** Overgrazing between about 1880 and 1900, combined with climatic shifts, produced arroyo cutting, siltation and dewatering. Dam building and irrigated agriculture expanded after about 1930, and reclamation projects came to irrigate about 10 million acres. Native fish declines on the mainstream [[Colorado River]] track these dams (Minckley and Deacon 1968; Holden and Stalnaker 1975).

**Virgin River.** [[LaVerkin Springs]] is a salt spring that marks the upstream limit of distribution for both woundfin and roundtail chub. Irrigation diversions (Hurricane, Washington and Mesquite) have removed essentially all summer flow at their points of diversion since the early 1900s. Woundfin and roundtail chub are restricted to the mainstream below LaVerkin Springs, and the Virgin spinedace occurs both below and above the springs.

## Key Findings

- **Dominant threat:** Habitat modification is the leading threat worldwide and in North America. Western fishes are more affected by biotic interactions, while eastern fishes are more affected by overexploitation.
- **Woundfin and roundtail chub reproduction depends on flow.** In 1977, a severe drought year with some of the lowest flows on record, young-of-the-year made up a very small share of the upper-river woundfin population. Mean length was large, indicating poor reproductive success. In 1973 and 1978, with high winter and spring flows, reproduction was high. Roundtail chub spawned successfully in only one location in 1978.
- **Usable habitat is limited.** Current water practices permit survival of native fishes in about 29% of their remaining potential habitat. Intermittent flows and high summer temperatures make the rest unreliable.
- **Flow threshold:** Reproductive success falls off sharply when mean winter and spring flows drop to about 100 cfs. Mean spring flows above 700 cfs may be slightly poorer than 400–600 cfs.
- **Parasitism is historically understudied.** Parasitism by *Lernaea* on *Crenichthys baileyi* coincided with population decline in some springs (Ash Spring, Hiko Spring) but not in others (Crystal Spring, Mormon Spring, Preston Big Spring). In Hiko Spring, the population went extinct before June 1967.
- **Interactions with exotics:** In Manse Spring, the introduction of goldfish in 1961 was followed by a crash of Pahrump Killifish in winter 1962–63. Killifish numbers fell to fewer than 50 in July 1968 after goldfish removal failed. In Corn Creek Spring, a mosquitofish increase was followed by a killifish decline. The author suggests a cause-effect relationship.
- **Devils Hole population sensitivity:** Small changes in water level strongly affect the minimum population of *Cyprinodon diabolis*. Based on transplant data and monthly counts, the author set a 200-individual minimum and a water level of 2.7 ft needed to sustain it. The court-mandated level is the current management target.
- **Policy conclusion:** Deacon argues that the Endangered Species Act reflects a societal commitment to preserving biodiversity, that ecological and philosophical arguments converge, and that "man's dominion is a call to service, not a license to exterminate" (Nibley 1978).
- **Warner Valley Project:** Deacon's testimony concludes that the project could cause extinction-level impacts if it reduces winter and spring flows below about 110 cfs. Flows near 100 cfs appear necessary for reproduction of woundfin and roundtail chub.
