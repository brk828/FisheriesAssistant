# Propagation of the Creek Chub in Ponds with Artificial Raceways

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1940s study by George N. Washburn (Ozark Fisheries, Incorporated) reports three seasons (1944–1946) of artificial propagation of the [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus atromaculatus*) using a purpose-built [[Spawning Raceway]] inside a hatchery pond at the Drayton Plains facility in Michigan. Spawning succeeded in the raceway, but brood-stock mortality, redd crowding, and fry survival varied with stocking ratios, brood size, and rearing method. The paper gives practical guidance on raceway design, brood selection, egg collection and hatchery incubation, fry transport, and pond stocking with fry of different ages.

## Background & Description

The creek chub is a cyprinid minnow valued as bait and as forage for game fish. Earlier work by Clark (1943) had demonstrated large-scale culture using artificial raceways, but his report lacked important details. Washburn's studies began in 1940; the paper reports data from 1944–1946, while the experiments remained ongoing.

Key biological points drawn from the text:
- Males grow faster than females but mature later in many populations (Greeley 1930 reported maturity in the fourth or fifth summer in natural waters).
- Maturity is better predicted by size than age: a suggested minimum of about 5–6 inches for females and 6–8 inches for males (or 6–7 inches for both sexes).
- Males are identifiable before spawning by nuptial tubercles; females by size and distended abdomen.
- Egg counts averaged about 3,500 for females of about 5 inches and 5,000 for 6-inch chubs.
- Male spawning space: about 1 foot wide and several feet long; completed redds are about 8 inches wide and 2–6 feet long, with roughly 2,500 eggs per running foot.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning season:** Mid-April in Michigan–Ohio border streams, extending to late June in the Upper Peninsula; about 25 days at a single locality.
- **Temperature:** Spawning activity ceased when water temperatures fell below 51°F. Unseasonably warm early April weather in 1945 caused a brief flurry, followed by a two-week lull.
- **Spatial behaviour:** Spawning moved upstream over the season. On April 22, 1946, about 63 percent of redds lay in the lower third of the raceway; by April 25 this fell to 45 percent. By season's end, 4 of 13 sections (about one-third of the area) held 60 percent of redds.
- **Sedimentation:** Silt accumulated over nests, especially in quieter water, and probably caused suffocation-related egg and fry mortality in 1944 and 1945.
- **Predation:** Great blue herons were thought to have contributed to high 1945 losses in an unscreened raceway; netting was used in 1944 and 1946.
- **Fry behaviour:** Fry are negatively phototropic (concentrate in shade) and positively rheotropic (collect in currents), with mass concentrations causing an abnormal die-off.

## Key Findings

**Raceway design (1944–1946 evolution):**
- Initial 1944 raceway: 110 ft × 2 ft × 4 in gravel, flow ¼ ft³/s.
- 1945: 285 ft × 3 ft, gravel increased to 4 in, flow ¾ ft³/s, with check dams.
- 1946: 300 ft × 5 ft, gravel 5 in, flow 1 ft³/s.
- Recommended design for 500–700 fish: at least 300 ft long, minimum 5 ft wide, flow of at least 1¼ ft³/s, shelters about every 25 ft, splash boards for slow pools, gravel of 1–3 in size (ideally about 6 in deep), and netting against birds.

**Brood stock:**
- Overall mortality: 1944 — 80% of males and 50% of females lost (184 fish introduced, 65 survived). 1945 — 78% of males and 62% of females lost. 1946 — 40% of males and 15% of females lost.
- Mortality was higher when males equalled or outnumbered females; more females improved survival.
- Recommended ratio: about two males to three females.
- Fast-growing, pond-reared fish showed higher mortality in 1944 than slower-growing wild fish in 1946, consistent with a shorter life span in fast growers.

**Stocking and spawning results:**
- 1944: 83 males, 101 females; 505,000 potential eggs vs 202 sq ft needed for deposition, 123 sq ft available (overstocked).
- 1945: 60 males, 90 females; 450,000 potential eggs vs 180 sq ft needed, 506 sq ft available (understocked).
- 1946: 164 males, 262 females; 917,000 potential eggs vs 367 sq ft needed, 988 sq ft available.
- Spawning season lengths: 25 days (1944), 41 days (1945), 29 days (1946).
- Late spawners reworked earlier redds, destroying eggs.

**Egg collection and incubation:**
- In 1946, 98 fluid ounces (about 115,000–130,000 eggs per quart) were removed from redds within 24 hours, with an estimated loss of at most 25 percent.
- Jar-tray method: hatch of about 67 percent (3,750 eggs/oz; 5,400 fry/oz); incubation averaged 10 days 6¼ hours at 55°F.
- Eggs from 14 natural redds in Paint Creek (47 oz) produced only 13.8 oz of fry (about 42 percent), attributed partly to uneven hatch timing and possible jarring sensitivity.
- Fry in redds were observed 20 days after first spawning, versus 22–26 days in jar-tray rearing.

**Fry transport:**
- Sac fry (4–8 days old): 95,000 fry moved 110 miles in 10-gallon cans (12,000 per can) without significant loss; a second run of 115,000 was similarly successful.
- Free-swimming fry (8–12 days): 33,000 transferred without appreciable loss; 20,000 moved 60 miles by truck.

**Pond stocking:**
- Eight ponds at Hastings stocked with 8-day-old fry: survival 17 percent or less.
- Two ponds at Drayton Plains stocked with 20-day-old fry: survival 51 and 73 percent.
- Hastings stocking rates ranged from 10,000 to 44,000 per acre; survival tended to be higher at higher rates, though three ponds at 25,000 per acre varied widely.
- Low-density ponds grew fish to over 4 inches in 135 days; high-production ponds reached only about 2½ inches in 115 days, suggesting food as a limiting factor.
- Artificial feeding: a 1.8-acre pond produced 58,000 chubs averaging 2¾ inches in 115 days using 800 lb of food (fish weighed 328 lb). Earlier Clark (1943) feeding rates were higher.

**Conclusions:**
- Advanced fry (20 days old) are the better stocking stage; 8-day-old fry transport well but survive poorly in ponds.
- Artificial feeding is likely needed for greater production of larger fish.
- Further work is needed to determine the spawning space requirements that would allow uniform use of the raceway.
