# Fishes of the Great Basin: A Natural History (Front Matter, Contents, Bibliography and Pahranagat Roundtail Chub Account)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the front matter, table of contents, copyright and cataloging block, and literature-cited section of *Fishes of the Great Basin: A Natural History* by [[William F. Sigler]] and John W. Sigler (University of Nevada Press, 1987). It also contains one complete species account, for the [[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta jordani*), which is described as the most endangered fish in the [[Great Basin]], with fewer than forty adults known from a single population. The book is part of the Max C. Fleischmann series in Great Basin natural history. The excerpted material covers taxonomy, range, morphology and conservation status; the bibliography documents the regional literature on which the book rests.

- The table of contents lists about 36 species accounts, arranged by order and family. Salmonids and the Northern Pike (Esocidae) form the first group, with eight trouts and grayling plus the pike. The Cypriniformes section (carps, minnows, and suckers) comprises the remaining 27 entries.
- Notable entries include the Paiute, Bonneville, and Lahontan cutthroat trouts, Arctic grayling, Alvord chub, Borax Lake chub, Least chub, Desert dace, Relict dace, Lahontan redside, Utah sucker, and the Pahranagat roundtail chub (page 177).
- Species accounts run from about page 110 to page 215 of the printed volume.

**Pahranagat Roundtail Chub: morphology**
- Olive green head and back, with silvery sides in winter.
- More active and streamlined than its associates.
- Moderately forked caudal fin with slightly rounded edges; complete lateral line.
- Dorsal and anal fins each carry about 9 rays.
- Dark markings appear on the sides, mostly above the lateral line.
- Scales: 89 to 94 in the lateral series, 26 to 27 above and 13 to 14 below the lateral line.
- Fins are whitish and may turn orange in preservative. Young are silvery when preserved in alcohol.
- The type description (Tanner 1950) characterizes the body as somewhat elongate and more robust than *[[Gila Elegans]]*, with the back only slightly elevated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range and distribution**
- The range is extremely restricted: the species occurs in a very small area in the outflow of Ash Springs, eastern Nevada.
- It was first collected at [[Hiko Springs]], Nevada, on Mrs. Wipple's ranch in May 1949 (Hardy 1980; La Rivers 1962).

**Threats and management context**
- The species' importance lies in its uniqueness: it is a natural history entity that evolved over thousands of years in a specialized habitat.
- It is listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- Ecological work at Ash Springs (Hardy 1982) examined interactions between introduced and native fishes in the spring outflow, which is central to the species' conservation.
- Adjacent species accounts (Mojave tui chub, arroyo chub) note pressures from expanding human populations, habitat alteration, and predation or competition from introduced sunfish and other predators.

**Literature base**
- The literature cited runs from about page 393 to page 406 and draws on regional fish surveys, state agency reports, and Great Basin paleontology and biogeography.
- Recurring themes include [[Introduced Species]] and their effects on native fauna, [[Pyramid Lake]] fisheries, Pleistocene lake histories (Lake Lahontan and Lake Bonneville), and endangered desert fishes.

## Key Findings

- **Pahranagat roundtail chub is the most endangered fish in the Great Basin.** Fewer than forty adults are known, all from one population at Ash Springs.
- **Its range is extremely restricted.** It occupies a small area in the outflow of Ash Springs, eastern Nevada. It was first documented at Hiko Springs in May 1949.
- **Federal protection.** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service lists the species as endangered.
- **Distinctive morphology.** Its streamlined body, complete lateral line, moderately forked tail, and scale counts (89 to 94 lateral scales) separate it from related *Gila* species.
- **Evolutionary value.** The species is treated as a unique natural history entity shaped over thousands of years in a specialized habitat.
- **Book scope.** The volume covers about 36 species accounts, spanning trouts, pike, carps, minnows, and suckers of the Great Basin, supported by a broad regional bibliography.
- **Conservation reliance on introduced-species research.** Ash Springs studies of interactions between introduced and native fishes are a key source for managing the species.
