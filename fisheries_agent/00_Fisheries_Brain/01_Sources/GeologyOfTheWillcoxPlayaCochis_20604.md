# Geology of the Willcox Playa, Cochise County, Arizona

#geology #paleolimnology #playa-lake #pleistocene #sedimentology

## Executive Summary

This field-guide paper by [[Joseph F. Schreiber Jr.]] (University of Arizona) describes the geology of [[Willcox Playa]], the desiccated bed of [[Lake Cochise]], a shallow late Pleistocene pluvial lake in southeastern Arizona. Results come from three years of field work (1962–65), later work through 1965 onward, and the cited literature. The paper covers the physiographic setting, Pleistocene and Recent fluvial sedimentation, the lake's sediments (clays, sands, beach ridges), its inferred environment, and its chronology from pollen, ostracodes and radiocarbon dates. The main conclusion is that Lake Cochise was a long, very shallow, alkaline and somewhat brackish lake, and that the playa floor is underlain by a late Wisconsin lake bottom.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Willcox Playa lies a few kilometers south of Willcox, in the lowest part of [[Willcox Basin]], the northern end of [[Sulphur Springs Valley]]. Its elevation is 1260 m (4,135–4,136 ft). Much of the central playa is managed by the U.S. Army at Fort Huachuca.
- **Prior work:** [[O. E. Meinzer]] first studied the area in 1910–1911 and named Lake Cochise. Later U.S. Geological Survey work includes a reconnaissance map by J. R. Cooper (1960), a fluoride and salinity study by Kister and others (1966), and a geohydrology report by Brown and Schumann (1969).
- **Funding and output:** The work was supported by National Science Foundation grant G-23746. Results were published in the Playa Lake Symposium volume (Schreiber and others, 1972).
- **Climate:** The area is semi-arid. Willcox (1277 m) records a mean annual precipitation of 28–30 cm (11–12 in) and a mean annual temperature of 14.8°C (58.7°F). Over half the annual rain falls in July–early September convective thunderstorms carrying moist air from the Gulf of Mexico. Winter frontal storms bring rain and snow from the Pacific. Summer temperatures often exceed 38°C (100°F) and average near 32°C (90°F). Winter monthly means range from 4 to 9°C.
- **Tectonic setting:** [[Sulphur Springs Valley]] trends north-northwest between mountain ranges uplifted by mid-Tertiary faulting. The highest peak in the drainage is Mount Graham (3265 m) in the Pinaleno Mountains. Reiley Peak (2336 m) is the highest point of the Winchester Mountains on the west side.
- **Rock types:** The surrounding ranges contain Precambrian granite and gneiss, Paleozoic clastic and carbonate rocks, Cretaceous sediments, Cretaceous–Tertiary intrusives and volcanics, and late Cenozoic sediments. The Galiuro–Winchester Mountains are almost entirely Tertiary volcanics, and the Chiricahua Mountains are mainly rhyolitic volcanics.
- **Basin fill:** Alluvium includes Tertiary conglomerate, sandstone and mudstone; Quaternary–Tertiary gravel, sand, silt and clay; Quaternary stream deposits; and playa lake muds. Wells penetrate more than 300 m of basin fill. Nearby basins suggest the fill may be 900–1500 m thick or more. Black clay and mud reach 146 m depth, with a thickness of 107 m, in a water well on the north edge of Willcox.
- **Basin dimensions:** The basin is 93 km (58 mi) long between divides and covers about 3900 km² (1500 mi²), of which 2500 km² (950 mi²) is valley floor. The playa covers about 130 km² (50 mi²).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainage and sediment transport.** Streams flow intermittently, during winter rains and after summer thunderstorms. Water is lost to streambed infiltration, retention in tanks and dams, and evaporation, so only muddy water reaches the playa at a few locations. Alluvial fans are wider on the east, where the larger range supplied more debris, which pushed the playa and valley axis toward the west boundary. Drainage divides lie to the north (with the [[Arivaipa Valley]]) and south (between the Chiricahua and Dragoon mountains).

**Pleistocene fluvial deposits.** Wide stream meander belts north and south of the playa, exposed in sand and gravel pits, show cut-and-fill deposition, trough cross-stratification, laminated fine sands and graded bedding. One northern pit, active in the early 1960s, yielded Pleistocene camel teeth and bones, horse teeth, and mammoth bones and one tusk. Near the playa, augering and driller's logs show these stream deposits interfingering with clay and mud of Lake Cochise.

**Lake Cochise sediment facies.** Clay and mud accumulated in the deeper middle of the lake. Sand, gravelly sand and sandy gravel were deposited near the north and south stream mouths and along the shores. The study drew on 105 auger holes (3–7 m deep) and a 43-m core from the playa center, with 85 percent core recovery. The material was used for mineralogy, size analysis, pollen chronology, radiocarbon dating and ostracode study.

**The [[Playa Clay]].** The unoxidized clay and mud are black. Downward-moving water has oxidized the upper few meters to brown, and the black material oxidizes rapidly once exposed.
- Over 200 paired Eh and pH measurements were made on the 43-m core. pH was 9.0–9.5 for nearly all samples, and Eh below the oxidized zone ranged from −106 to −326 mV.
- Organic matter ranged from 0.44 to 2.20 percent, averaging about 1 percent.
- The black and greenish-gray color is attributed mainly to a black iron-sulfide mineral, probably ferrous sulfide (hydrotroilite). The authors infer it from color, H₂S evolution with dilute HCl, negative Eh and oxidation behavior, because X-ray diffraction could not identify it.
- Illite dominates the clay fraction, with minor montmorillonite, mixed-layer illite–montmorillonite and vermiculite, and trace kaolinite and chlorite. Authigenic analcime is common and is interpreted as a diagenetic alteration of kaolinite.
- Calcite is ubiquitous, occurring as ostracode valves and fragments and as authigenic crystals. Halite is the only readily soluble salt detected. Gypsum occurs in one auger hole.

**Sand mineralogy.** Quartz and feldspar dominate. The sands are mainly fine to very fine, moderately well sorted where not mixed with clay and silt, and angular to subangular. Heavy minerals include pyroxenes, chlorite, biotite and epidote-group minerals from mafic igneous and metamorphic terrains. Apatite is more abundant where basaltic hornblende occurs. Zircon and tourmaline are absent from many samples.

**[[Beach Ridges]].** These are the major preserved shore features of the lake, on the east and west sides. They support mesquite and yucca and are visible from ground and air. Of the 80 km (50 mi) of shoreline, about 47 km (29 mi) are defined by beach ridges, including a 24 km (15 mi) ridge on the west side. Both sides follow the 1273 m (4,175 ft) contour, with crests 1.5–3 m (5–10 ft) higher. The ridges do not appear to have formed at the north and south ends, where fluvial sediment entered. Their mixed composition (from mud-free gravelly sand to slightly gravelly sandy mud) points to intense storm-wave activity that piled up sand and gravel and moved lake mud shoreward.

## Key Findings

**1. Lake geometry.** Lake Cochise covered about 190 km² (75 mi²) in the northern Willcox basin, extending from a few kilometers north of Willcox to about 6 km south of the present playa's southern tip. Using a beach-ridge elevation of 1274 m, a 3 m drop to the highest lake level, and a playa surface at 1260 m, the lake was about 11 m (35 ft) deep. It was roughly 18 by 32 km in maximum dimensions and was described as a long, wide and very shallow lake.

**2. Water chemistry.** The sediments' sodium chloride content implies somewhat brackish water. The water was not too saline for [[Ostracodes]] to thrive, and it was alkaline enough for calcium carbonate (ostracode valves, and precipitated crystals up to 1–2 mm) to form and persist. Physical or chemical stratification cannot be determined. Organic-rich muds imply low-oxygen to reducing bottom conditions. Anaerobic bacteria metabolized organic matter and converted sulfate to hydrogen sulfide.

**3. Paleoclimate from pollen.** [[P. S. Martin]] (1963) analyzed pollen from the 43-m core. High pine pollen counts indicate cool, wet (pluvial) conditions. Low pine counts, poor preservation and oxidized sediments indicate interglacial or interpluvial conditions. The upper 23 m (77 ft) fall in the Wisconsinan glacial interval. The upper 2 m (6 ft) are barren, probably from oxidation, while the sediment below holds up to 99 percent pine pollen. The 23–29 m (77–96 ft) interval is assigned to the Sangamon interglacial, when the lake dried periodically and oxidation destroyed pollen. The rest of the core represents pluvial conditions of the Illinoisan glacial interval.

**4. Lake-level history from ostracodes.** [[S. P. Cameron]] (1971) found that the ostracode fauna above 15 m confirms a permanent late Wisconsin lake. Between 15 m and about 25 m (82 ft) the fauna records a series of temporary lakes of moderate salinity with good ostracode populations. Below 25 m it records highly saline temporary lakes.

**5. Radiocarbon chronology.** Seven dates were reported for Lake Cochise clays (Table 1, from [[Austin Long]], 1966), with interpretation as follows:
- A-352 (75–84 in depth): 23,000 ± 500 years B.P.
- A-353 (92–101 in depth): 22,000 ± 500 years B.P.
- A-221: > 20,000 years B.P. This is a minimum age, because very low organic content required dilution.
- A-351: 86,154 ± 110 (OCR-garbled in source) is rejected as a minimal age because of modern contamination.
- SI-176, SI-177A and SI-177B: > 30,000 years B.P., from ostracode fragments on the eastern playa edge, where ground-water mixing is possible.

Samples A-352 and A-363 may be mixed by ground-water movement and the very small carbonate crystals. Long (1966) infers pluvial conditions from before 30,000 to about 13,000 years B.P., and perhaps as recently as 11,500–10,500 years B.P.

**6. Overall conclusion.** Lake Cochise was a shallow, alkaline, somewhat brackish lake of late Pleistocene age. Its bed is preserved beneath the playa, and the authors note that the wind-eroded playa surface is a late Wisconsin lake bottom.

**7. Scope limits.** The paper does not reproduce the full radiocarbon discussion, which it states is beyond its scope. The gypsum content is probably underestimated by the sampling. The age of the lake deposits is constrained, but the number of dated samples is small and some are affected by ground-water mixing.
