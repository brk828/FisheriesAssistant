# Using a Dam to Heal a River: Glen Canyon Dam and the 1996 Test Flow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1996 *High Country News* essay by George Sibley (OCR-scanned) examines [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and the controlled "habitat-building test flow" of March 26 to April 2, 1996, released to restore sediment and habitat in the canyons below the dam. The article frames the flood as a shift from a water-management culture rooted in irrigation "religion" toward science-based river management. It documents how the dam altered the [[Colorado River]]'s temperature, flow regime, and food web, which in turn affected native warm-water fishes and non-native trout. The source is a magazine feature, not a research paper, so most figures are journalistic or quoted from agency and scientist sources.

## Background & Description

- **Dam and reservoir:** Glen Canyon Dam began impounding water in 1963, creating [[Lake Powell]]. The dam is about 500 feet high. Its builder was the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BuRec).
- **Pre-dam river:** The article describes a warm, muddy river with large seasonal fluctuations, with summer highs above 100,000 cubic feet per second (cfs) and winter lows below 10,000 cfs. A 1884 flood reached about 300,000 cfs.
- **Post-dam river:** The river became cold and clear, drawn from deep in the reservoir. Seasonal fluctuations ceased, but daily fluctuations sometimes exceeded 30,000 cfs, ranging from about 1,000 cfs to 31,500 cfs during "peaking power" generation.
- **Program origin:** The [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] office was created in 1982, under [[Dave Wegner]], as a result of litigation by the [[Environmental Defense Fund]]. An environmental impact statement (EIS) was ordered in 1989 and completed in March 1995.
- **Policy context:** The [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] (1992) and the National Environmental Policy Act shaped the dam's management. The article traces Bureau culture from the irrigation-era mandate to a shift toward environmental management under Commissioner Dan Beard (1992 to 1995), during which staff was reduced by about 25 percent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish communities**

- The sudden change to cold, clear water had a major impact on the warm-water native fishes. Listed natives include [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and flannelmouth sucker. All were described as officially endangered or under consideration for listing, except [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]], which persist in lower canyons where side-stream inflows warm and muddy the water.
- Non-native cold-water trout, primarily [[Rainbow Trout]], brown trout, and cutthroat trout, have thrived, especially nearer the dam. The Lees Ferry to dam stretch became a renowned trout fishery.
- Warm-water native fishes need warm backwater pools for breeding. The article notes that the sediment deposits from the flood were expected to help restore these pools, but results were uncertain.

**Food web and primary production**

- Before the dam, the canyon food base was mainly decomposing material washed in from the whole basin. After the dam, that material settled in Lake Powell.
- The 16 miles of clear water below the dam let enough light penetrate to support [[Cladophora Glomerata]], a filamentous green alga. It feeds diatoms and aquatic invertebrates, which the article calls "the supermarket for the Grand Canyon."

**Sediment, beaches, and riparian vegetation**

- Pre-dam sediment inflow was about 66 million tons per year. Current inflow from tributaries such as the Paria and Little Colorado rivers is about 20 million tons per year.
- Without high, beach-building floods, sandbars and beaches have shrunk as sand slumps into the channel. Exotic [[Tamarisk]] now dominates the beach plant community, reducing diversity.
- Small animals, including amphibians, lizards, snails, and birds, have benefited. The [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], an endangered bird, is among them. The Kanab ambersnail and leopard frogs were also mentioned.
- About 22,000 overnight campers use the canyon beaches each year, linked to the river-running industry.

## Key Findings

1. **The 1996 test flow was about 45,000 cfs** for roughly one week, combining the power plant's capacity (33,200 cfs) with the four river-outlet tubes. Approximately 150 scientists were deployed to 10 base camps spanning 250 miles of the Grand Canyon.
2. **Stated objectives:** Redeposit sand and silt onto bars and beaches, restore backwater breeding pools for endangered fishes, protect cultural artifacts, and water native vegetation.
3. **Results were mixed.** Sand and silt were stirred up and redeposited, especially from flash-flood deposits at the mouths of the Paria and Little Colorado rivers, but stability was unknown for months.
4. **Limits of the flood:** It did not remove exotic tamarisk, did not flush out non-native fish unable to tolerate floods, did not significantly harm the trout fishery, and did not disrupt the algae-based food web.
5. **Fluctuation control is costly and uncertain.** The preferred EIS alternative limits daily fluctuations to 8,000 cfs or less. This would cost about one-third of the Bureau's marketable power capacity. Early experiments suggested reduced fluctuations made less difference to erosion than hoped. [[Jack Schmidt]] (Utah State University) stated that "erosion in steep narrow canyons can't really be stopped by regulating the flow."
6. **Adaptive management:** The EIS recommended an "Adaptive Management Program," described as openly admitting uncertainty and testing ideas iteratively.
7. **Two management paths** were outlined by Schmidt. One maintains a cold, clear, "naturalized" river with a trout fishery. The other restores pre-dam conditions through larger floods, temperature modification, and possibly large-scale sediment transport. The second path would be expensive and could favor warm-water natives.
8. **Central argument:** The article contends that the flood's most important result was a shift toward science-driven management, with scientists and Bureau managers working together, rather than using science to justify an irrigation-era ideology. [[John Wesley Powell]] is cited as the early advocate of this scientific approach.
9. **Long-term outlook:** The Bureau estimates about 700 years before Lake Powell fills with silt, which would turn the dam into a muddy waterfall.
