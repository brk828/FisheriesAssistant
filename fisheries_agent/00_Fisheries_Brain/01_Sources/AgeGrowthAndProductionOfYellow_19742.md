# Age, Growth, and Production of Yellow Perch in Lake Erie

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Fishery Bulletin (No. 70, Fish and Wildlife Service, Volume 52, 1952) by Frank W. Jobes examines the [[American Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) of [[Lake Erie]]. It combines a 1885–1947 commercial production record with a critical test of [[Scale Method]] age determination, body-scale back-calculation, growth histories, growth compensation, seasonal growth, annual growth fluctuations, length-weight and condition, size at maturity, sex ratio, and year-class strength. The central finding is that the fishery depends on only two or three age groups, so production is highly sensitive to year-class strength and to fishing and size-limit practices.

## Background & Description

- The yellow perch is a common food fish of the northeastern United States and southeastern Canada, important to both hook-and-line and Great Lakes commercial fisheries.
- The study grew out of a broader [[Lake Erie Commercial Fisheries]] investigation begun by the former U.S. Bureau of Fisheries and continued by the Fish and Wildlife Service, in cooperation with Ohio, Pennsylvania, New York, Ontario, the city of Buffalo, and the Buffalo Society of Natural Science.
- The report relies mainly on random samples from 1927–37 (all perch taken by nets). Random commercial-catch samples from 1943–48 (fish 8½ inches or longer) were used only where they added information.
- Earlier work (Jobes 1933; Schneberger 1935; Hile and Jobes 1941) assumed a constant body-to-scale ratio or used direct proportion. This study was needed first to show that scale-based ages are accurate and to choose a sound back-calculation method.
- Sampling sites included Port Clinton, Sandusky, Huron, Vermilion, Lorain, Ashtabula, Conneaut (Ohio), Erie (Pennsylvania) and Dunkirk (New York).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fishing intensity:** Production rose as boats, gear and mesh practices intensified. Power lifters, trap nets, bull nets (100 meshes deep) and finer gill-net thread all raised exploitation.
- **Collapse of the cisco fishery (1925):** Gill-netters switched to perch, which contributed to the 1928–35 peak.
- **Legal size limit:** Ohio cut its minimum from 9 to 8½ inches in July 1929. Lax enforcement in 1934 coincided with the record Ohio catch of about 13 million pounds.
- **Environment:** The author found no extensive heavy pollution harmful to fish, and turbidity was not a factor in young-fish survival or growth (Van Oosten 1948). Causes of year-class variation were described as poorly understood.
- **Temperature:** Growth correlated with air temperature in certain months. Summer temperatures were a plausible influence, but no mechanism was established.
- **Comparison waters:** Lake Erie perch grew faster than most compared populations, and the growth of Saginaw Bay perch was similar.

## Key Findings

**Commercial production**
- Lake Erie production was recorded from 1885 onward. Mean annual United States yield was about 3,000,000 lb in 1885–99, 1,905,000 lb in 1900–27, 7,520,000 lb in 1928–35, and 2,390,000 lb in 1936–47.
- The 1934 United States catch was about 14,218,000 lb. The 1903 low was 873,000 lb.
- Ohio consistently supplied more than half the lake total. Its share of the United States catch rose from 77.8% (1885–99) to 93.1% (recent years).
- The grand average for the entire lake, 1885–1947, was 4,964,000 lb.

**Age validation and scale method**
- Annuli were validated as true year marks. Supporting evidence included the dominance of the 1926 year class across successive years and the coincidence of calculated and observed lengths.
- About 5% of scales were discarded as unreadable.
- Annulus formation could finish as late as July 1 in some years. Annuli are not spawning marks.

**Body-scale relation and back-calculation**
- Key scales from 576 western-basin fish were analysed. The body-to-scale (L/Sc) ratio was constant at about 1.16 for lengths above 4.3 inches, with a sharp break between 4.2 and 4.3 inches.
- Lengths below 4.3 inches required an empirical correction table. The Dahl-Lea direct-proportion method was otherwise used.

**Growth histories and discrepancies**
- Calculated lengths at a given year of life declined with the age of the fish used, a pattern the author found real rather than an artefact.
- The main causes were gear selectivity, selective destruction of fast growers by the fishery (about 14% of undersized trap-net perch were dead at lifting), differential loss at maturity, and legal-size selection. Natural mortality linked to growth was a possible minor factor.
- Annual growth fluctuations were not the source of these discrepancies.

**Growth in length and weight**
- Females grew faster than males from the third year onward. The minimum legal length (8½ inches) was reached on average by the end of the third year.
- Calculated weights: the average at the end of year 3 was 4.2 oz, and at year 6, 9.8 oz. Maximum recorded: male 11.3 in, female 12.9 in, and fish of unknown sex 13.9 in.
- Growth compensation occurred, usually from the third year onward.

**Seasonal growth**
- Estimated cumulative growth was 15% by July 1, 50% by July end, 80% by August end, and 100% by September, with growth continuing through October in 1927.

**Annual fluctuations and temperature**
- Growth varied by 18.3% (range) for sexes combined in 1924–29, and by 17.1% in 1940–48.
- August mean air temperature correlated negatively with growth (r = −0.605, significant).
- May, July and September temperatures, and their combinations, correlated positively. Precipitation, sunshine and wind did not.

**Length frequency and year classes**
- Trap-net length distributions were unimodal each year; bull-net and shoal-net distributions were sometimes bimodal, partly because of entanglement.
- Age group II (second-year fish, typically 8–9 inches) normally dominates the late-season commercial catch. Group III dominates spring and early summer.
- The 1926 year class was exceptionally strong. Probable strong classes included 1936, 1942 and 1944.
- The fishery relies on two age groups, so a failure of two successive year classes could collapse it.

**Length-weight and condition**
- Equation: W = 1.766 × 10⁻⁵ L^3.015 (W in grams, L standard length in mm), fitted to 23,158 fish. Weight increases approximately as the cube of length.
- Mean condition factor K = 1.91 (range 1.13–3.23).
- Trap-net fish had lower K than gill-net fish, so gill-net data were excluded from condition analysis.
- Females lost about 16% of prespawning weight at spawning.

**Maturity and sex ratio**
- Males mature earlier and smaller: 47.4% mature at 6–6.5 in. Females: 48.4% mature at 8–8.5 in, 86.1% at 8.5–9 in, 97% at 9–9.5 in. Any legal minimum should therefore be based on female maturity.
- Overall sex ratio was 96 females per 100 males, excluding April samples. The sexes were segregated in most months, and males declined with age, probably due to differential fishing mortality during the spawning run.

**Management implications**
- Heavy fishing on the spawning run, the size-limit system, and the dependence on two age groups all expose the stock to overfishing.
- Maintaining protective measures to ensure an adequate spawning stock was described as highly imperative.
