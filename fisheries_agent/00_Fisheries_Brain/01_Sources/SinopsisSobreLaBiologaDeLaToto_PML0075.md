# Synopsis on the Biology of the Totoaba (*Cynoscion macdonaldi*) (Gilbert, 1890)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Synopsis No. 108, prepared by Joaquín Arvizu and Humberto Chávez for the Mexican National Fisheries Institute (1972), compiles the known biology, distribution, life cycle, and fishery of the [[Totoaba]] (*[[Cynoscion macdonaldi]]*), a large sciaenid endemic to the Gulf of California. The authors state that the reproductive and life-cycle information available at the time was limited and that it was expected to grow through research by Mexican institutions and the University of Arizona. Much of the content draws on a review of prior literature plus field data from the former Dirección General de Pesca e Industrias Conexas.

## Background & Description

- **Nomenclature:** Described by [[Gilbert]] (1890) from the northern end of the [[Gulf of California]]. The genus *Cynoscion* was synonymised with several earlier names, and the species was later placed as *Cynoscion macdonaldi* in Jordan and Evermann's usage.
- **Taxonomy:** Phylum Chordata, Order Perciformes, Family Sciaenidae, Subfamily Otolithinae. No subspecies are recognised, and the authors consider the species not polytypic.
- **Diagnostic features:** Head length 3.4 and body depth 4.0 times in standard length; dorsal fin IX-I, 24; anal fin II, 7. Canine teeth are not prominent on the jaws. Adults are silvery grey with golden tones and a sometimes bluish back; juveniles have dorsolateral dark blotches.
- **Common names:** Juveniles of both sexes are called "machorros" or "machbrritos"; adult males are "machorros" and adult females "totoaba."
- **Identification:** The authors reproduce a dichotomous key from McPhail (1958) for Eastern Pacific *Cynoscion* species, separating *C. macdonaldi* from congeners by lateral line form, anal and dorsal fin ray counts, and canine size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution:** The range is reduced to the Gulf of California. Important fishing zones lie at the northern gulf: Puerto Peñasco and Santa Clara (Sonora), San Felipe (Baja California), and the Colorado River mouth.
- **Spawning and juveniles:** Spawning occurs in the northern gulf, with larvae and juveniles found close to shore, including in shallow waters near the Colorado River.
- **Adult movements:** Adults are found throughout the range but are most common in the northern gulf, where they are caught year-round, with larger numbers from January onward.
- **Drivers of distribution change:** Warm water in summer, cold northern water in winter, salinity, and reproductive cues appear to drive seasonal migrations, though the authors note that some movements remain poorly understood.
- **Habitat degradation:** Canal construction on the Colorado River and in Arizona, California, and Baja California reduced freshwater inflow. The authors state this has likely affected totoaba by reducing usable habitat and altering salinity, temperature, and dissolved solids.
- **Feeding ecology:** Adults feed on fish and crustaceans, including shrimp, crabs, and small fish. Stomach contents of juveniles from the regional habitat suggest that young totoaba remain in local waters.

## Key Findings

- **Life history:**
  - Reproduction is external, and sexes are not distinguished externally except during spawning, when females show an enlarged abdomen.
  - Spawning begins at the end of February or early March and continues into early June, with peak activity reported in early May.
  - Ovaries contain eggs at multiple developmental stages, and eggs are about 0.1 to 0.9 mm in diameter with a single oil droplet.
  - Juveniles remain in nearshore waters up to about 2 years old.
- **Age and growth:**
  - Berdegué (1955) reported that commercially caught fish were 3 to 15 years old, with the oldest commercial fish measuring 1.27 m and aged 9 years.
  - Nakashima (1916) reported that 1-year-old fish measured about 175 mm and that the longest fish observed were 1.83 m to 1.98 m.
  - Berdegué's growth estimates ranged from 272 mm at the end of the first year to about 1,180 mm by year 5 or 6, with growth slowing thereafter.
  - Males apparently mature earlier than females (Nakashima, 1916).
- **Size and sex structure:**
  - The commercial catch was 66% male and 34% female. Females are reported to reach greater weights and sizes than males (Chart I–III data from San Felipe, 1963).
  - Average length of 190 individuals sampled at San Felipe in 1963 was about 1.38 to 1.55 m for females and 1.38 to 1.44 m for males.
- **Fishery:**
  - Gill nets are highly selective, catching fish older than about 3 years. Hook-and-line gear is used mostly by sport fishers and occasionally by commercial fishers.
  - About 12 large vessels were reported active at Puerto Peñasco, along with smaller launches.
  - Fishing depths range from 10 to 40 fathoms.
  - Catches were reported from 1929 to 1970, with the annual series showing high yields in the 1940s and variable yields thereafter.
- **Management:**
  - A refuge zone was declared from the Colorado River mouth southward to the Ornetepec Bay area and the Santa Clara River mouth, with year-round fishing prohibited.
  - Catching is prohibited each year from April 1 to May 15.
- **Data gaps:** The authors note that no figures were available for mortality and disease, and that age at maturity, sex ratio in the egg-laying season, and the egg's appearance in natural conditions remained unknown.
