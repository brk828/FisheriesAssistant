# Fish Fauna of the Middle Rio Grande: Status, Threats and Research Needs, 1994

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae #freshwater-ecology #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This 1994 status assessment documents the dramatic decline of native fish fauna in the [[Middle Rio Grande]] between Espanola and [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]], New Mexico. Of 45 fish species known historically, only 17 (38%) are native, with 6 (11%) endemic to the system. Seven native species have been eliminated (5 extirpated, 2 extinct), representing 41% loss of native diversity. The [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (*Hybognathus amarus*) is the sole surviving short-lived mainstream endemic cyprinid. [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarki virginalis*) persists in only 5-7% of its historic range, restricted primarily to headwater tributaries. Primary threats include water diversion for irrigation, habitat degradation from livestock grazing and timber harvest, introduction of non-native species (particularly salmonids and centrarchids), and hybridization. The report identifies critical research needs for both warmwater and coldwater native species to inform conservation and restoration efforts.

## Background & Description

The [[Rio Grande]] originates in southwestern Colorado and flows 2,000 km to the Gulf of Mexico, passing through major dams, irrigation diversions, and metropolitan areas. The Middle Rio Grande reach extends from the confluence with the [[Rio Chama]] near Espanola downstream to [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]]. Two major mainstream reservoirs ([[Cochiti Dam]], completed 1975; [[Elephant Butte Dam]], completed 1916) and three diversion structures (including [[Isleta Diversion Dam]] and [[San Acacia Diversion Dam]]) fragment this 177 km reach. Under low-flow conditions, these diversions can completely dewater extensive river sections.

Historic fish collections by Cope and Yarrow (1875) and Dr. W.J. Koster documented a diverse, largely endemic fauna. By the early 1960s, many native species had disappeared from northern mainstream reaches. The last collection of [[Speckled Chub]] (*Macrhybopsis aestivalis*) and [[Rio Grande Bluntnose Shiner]] (*Notropis simus simus*) occurred in 1964 downstream of present-day Cochiti Dam.

Native species composition includes:
- **Mainstream warmwater endemics**: [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (federally endangered as of 1994), [[Rio Grande Shiner]] (*Notropis jemezanus*, under review for listing)
- **Extinct species**: [[Phantom Shiner]] (*Notropis orca*), [[Rio Grande Bluntnose Shiner]]
- **Upper elevation coldwater natives**: [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]], [[Rio Grande Chub]] (*Gila pandora*), [[Rio Grande Sucker]] (*Catostomus plebeius*)

Of 45 total species, 28 (62%) are introduced, including rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), brown trout (*Salmo trutta*), brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), and numerous centrarchids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrologic Regime**: Annual discharge peaks during spring runoff (March-June), with lowest flows July-October coinciding with peak irrigation demand. Summer monsoon storms provide sporadic flow supplementation. Irrigation cessation in late October allows flow recovery. This altered hydrologic regime creates cyclic desiccation and re-wetting of mainstream channels.

**Habitat Fragmentation**: The river system is highly fragmented by dams and diversions. The 1,280 km network of levees, drains, and canals between Algodones and [[Bosque del Apache National Wildlife Refuge]] diverts water extensively. Under low-flow conditions, fish become trapped in isolated pools where they face predation, disease, declining water quality, and potential complete mortality from de-oxygenation or toxic spills.

**Tributary Systems**: Three major tributaries drain upper elevation forested lands: [[Rio Chama]], [[Jemez River]] (entering west of Bernalillo), and [[Santa Fe River]] (below Cochiti Reservoir). These tributaries, primarily on [[U.S. Forest Service]] lands, historically provided coldwater habitat for native trout and associated cyprinids and catostomids at elevations of 2,000-2,600 m.

**Historical Impacts**: Spanish explorations beginning in the 1500s initiated agricultural development and stream diversion. By 1830, wealthy ranchers herded hundreds of thousands of sheep; by 1880, approximately 4 million sheep grazed northern New Mexico. Riparian vegetation, especially cottonwoods (*Populus* spp.), has declined dramatically with flow alterations. Livestock grazing has caused streambank trampling, vegetation removal, loss of undercut banks, elevated summer temperatures, and winter anchor ice development.

**Current Threats**:
- **Water diversion**: Primary threat to mainstream fauna; can completely dewater 177 km reaches
- **Introduced species**: 28 non-native species, including predatory centrarchids and competitive salmonids
- **Hybridization**: Rainbow trout readily hybridizes with Rio Grande cutthroat trout; white sucker (*Catostomus commersoni*) hybridizes with Rio Grande sucker
- **Habitat degradation**: Livestock grazing, timber harvest, loss of streamside vegetation and large woody material
- **Fragmentation**: Loss of stream connectivity isolates populations, prevents gene flow
- **Angling pressure**: Rio Grande cutthroat trout's low aggression and ease of capture may contribute to declines

## Key Findings

**Native Fish Status**:
- Of 17 native species, 7 (41.2%) have been eliminated: 5 extirpated from Middle Rio Grande, 2 extinct
- Only 38% of historically occurring species are native; 62% are introduced
- [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] now restricted to 5-10% of former range (Cochiti Dam to Elephant Butte Reservoir only)
- [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]] distribution declined to 5-7% of historic range
- As of 1992, 77 Rio Grande cutthroat trout populations existed in northern New Mexico: 42 genetically pure, 35 showing hybridization with rainbow trout
- In Colorado, 39 populations exist (34 pure)
- Six new potentially pure populations discovered in summer 1994

**Legal Status**:
- [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]]: Federally endangered (1994)
- [[Rio Grande Shiner]]: Under "notice of review" for federal listing
- [[Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout]]: U.S. Forest Service sensitive species, "management indicator species," AFS "protected" and "special concern"
- [[Phantom Shiner]] and [[Rio Grande Bluntnose Shiner]]: New Mexico Endangered Group 2 (threatened)

**Ecological Mechanisms of Decline**:
- **Mainstream species**: Water diversion causes habitat loss, population concentration below diversions, increased predation and disease from crowding, and catastrophic mortality from de-oxygenation or toxic spills
- **Coldwater species**: Competition and predation from introduced salmonids (particularly brown and rainbow trout); habitat degradation from grazing; hybridization; loss of connectivity; low vagility and ease of capture make native cutthroat vulnerable

**Research Priorities Identified**:

*For warmwater species (Rio Grande Silvery Minnow)*:
- Long-term life history studies initiated 1992
- Reproductive biology and early life-history studies initiated summer 1994
- Habitat association and relative abundance monitoring ongoing

*For coldwater species (Rio Grande Cutthroat Trout)* - six priority areas:
1. Distribution surveys and genetic analyses of populations (highest priority to continue)
2. Habitat evaluation (spawning, rearing, over-wintering requirements)
3. Effects of introduced salmonids (competition, predation mechanisms)
4. Basic life history (reproduction, age-growth, production, parasites, diseases, food habits)
5. Response to land management activities (grazing, timber harvest)
6. Fish-habitat relationships and ecosystem-based research

**Management Context**:
- New Mexico Department of Game and Fish has legislative mandate to "preserve natural diversity and distribution patterns of native ichthyofauna"
- Broodstock program for Rio Grande cutthroat trout initiated 1987, currently being refined
- Draft management plans exist for New Mexico and Colorado
- Research should integrate with U.S. Forest Service land management plans and adopt "ecology-based multiple use management" philosophy
- Cooperative efforts needed among U.S. Forest Service, New Mexico Game and Fish, University of New Mexico, and New Mexico State University

**Data Gaps**:
- No precise data on stream kilometers lost as Rio Grande cutthroat trout habitat
- Effects of grazing on cutthroat habitat unstudied
- Timber harvest impacts undocumented
- De-watering impacts on cutthroat populations not researched
- Angling effects on Rio Grande cutthroat trout not quantified
- Interactions between Rio Grande sucker and introduced white sucker poorly understood
- Mechanisms of competition between native and introduced species require investigation
