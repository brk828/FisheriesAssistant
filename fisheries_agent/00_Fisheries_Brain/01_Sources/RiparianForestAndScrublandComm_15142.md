# Riparian Forest and Scrubland Community Types of Arizona and New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This special issue of *Desert Plants* (Vol. 9, Nos. 3–4, 1989), by Robert C. Szaro of the USDA Forest Service, presents a structural classification of riparian forest and scrubland vegetation in Arizona and New Mexico. Based on 152 field sites sampled from 1979 to 1983 across National Forests and adjacent public lands, the author delineates **28 riparian community types** using cluster analysis of tree and shrub importance values. Each type is described for identification, distribution, vegetation structure, relationships to other types, and prior studies. The study also reviews how grazing, flooding, dam regulation, and recreation shape riparian plant communities, and finds that elevation is the dominant site variable associated with type distribution. The document is a botanical and ecological resource; it does not address fish or aquatic fauna directly.

## Background & Description

The publication is a companion to *Biotic Communities of the American Southwest* (1982), which lacked an on-site riparian classification. The author notes that riparian zones comprise only about 113,000 hectares in Arizona (Babcock, 1968), yet support disproportionately high biological diversity, including many bird and animal species. Riparian vegetation is defined as that occurring in or adjacent to drainageways and floodplains that differs in species or life forms from surrounding vegetation.

The classification uses the **community type** as its fundamental unit, based on existing rather than potential (climax) vegetation. Names reflect dominant overstory tree or shrub species, with codominants joined by hyphens and "mixed deciduous" used where no single species dominates.

Key study objectives were to:
1. Develop a structural classification framework based on actual site data.
2. Determine tree and shrub densities and species composition.
3. Provide herbaceous species lists by type.
4. Summarize physical site characteristics (gradient, bearing, valley cross-sectional area, elevation).
5. Discuss factors affecting composition.

The issue was published by [[University Of Arizona]] at the [[Boyce Thompson Southwestern Arboretum]], and it is a follow-up to the 1982 [[Biotic Communities Of The American Southwest]] volume. Its editor, Frank S. Crosswhite, notes that it is intended as a definitive reference.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Disturbance dynamics.** Riparian systems are characterized by constant change and are rarely at equilibrium. Flooding, scouring, desiccation, grazing, and recreation act as disturbances that structure plant communities. Flood frequency, duration, and timing influence which species establish and persist. Cottonwood regeneration, for example, requires a spring flood around seed dispersal followed by a gradual summer recession of water levels ([[Fremont Cottonwood]]).

**Grazing.** Livestock use is pervasive. Stream bottoms concentrate cattle, and grazing both removes and trampling damages vegetation, lowers floristic diversity, reduces shrub volume, suppresses regeneration, and promotes streambank erosion. Few study sites were ungrazed; notable exceptions included Sonoita Creek, Ramsey Canyon, and Fossil Creek springs. Fencing and exclusion are described as the most effective rehabilitation strategy, though possibly not economically feasible at broad scales.

**Flooding.** Flooding can be destructive or constructive. Floods uproot trees and scour channels, but also deposit sediments and create moist seedbeds. Riparian plants are sorted along flood-tolerance gradients. Saltcedar seedlings are scoured from banks under moderate spring flows, while willows and cottonwoods occupy saturated soils at lower elevations.

**Dams and regulation.** Dams alter downstream flow regimes, sediment loads, and soil moisture. Reported examples include a 87% reduction in suspended sediment below Glen Canyon Dam and near-total water withholding downstream of Conchas Dam. Regulated flows can favor saltcedar over cottonwood, while flow control can also enhance willow stands (e.g., [[Goodding Willow]] at Queen Creek).

**Recreation.** Recreational use per unit area in riparian zones is many times that of other vegetation types. Manning's (1979) seven-step soil impact cycle, from litter scuffing to increased erosion, is cited, along with trampling and vandalism of trees.

**Physical site factors.** Elevation was the single most important variable separating community types (partial r² = 0.83 in stepwise discriminant analysis). Stream bearing and gradient were secondary and more localized, and valley cross-sectional area had the least association with vegetation.

## Key Findings

- **28 riparian community types** were identified from 152 sites (the Table 1 cluster analysis cites 153 sites in one figure caption, a discrepancy in the source). Types are grouped into riparian forest (types 1–20) and riparian scrub (types 21–28).
- **Elevation** is the overriding factor associated with type distribution; gradient and bearing have more limited, local influence; valley area is weakest.
- Between-type variance for all four site characteristics differed significantly from zero (F-test, P < 0.05).
- Key dominant types include [[Populus Angustifolia]] (20 sites), [[Alnus Oblongifolia]] (16 sites), [[Acer Negundo]] (6 sites), and [[Fraxinus Pennsylvanica]] (3 sites).
- Riparian areas comprise an estimated **1.3%** of total lands within the 11 National Forests of Arizona and New Mexico (Szaro and Patton, 1986).
- Cottonwood contributes most strongly to [[Populus Fremontii]] types, which are identified where cottonwood exceeds 70% of tree density and 90% of basal area.
- Grazing has been shown to reduce shrub height to as little as one-tenth of ungrazed levels in some willow stands (Taylor, 1986) and to eliminate cottonwood and willow reproduction along Trout Creek (Reichenbacher, 1984).
- Key recommendations: classification should be used at the level suited to management goals; future workers should add or subdivide types; fencing and grazing management are needed for riparian recovery; and further research on livestock distribution is required.
- The author concludes that floristic detail, not only structural summaries, is needed to link bird and herpetofauna relationships to vegetation ([[Floristic Versus Structural Classification]]).
- The special issue's concluding essay by F. S. Crosswhite stresses that riparian habitats in arid regions have heightened value and support desert organisms, including pollinators of the [[Saguaro Cactus]], and that the 28 types should be husbanded for future generations.
