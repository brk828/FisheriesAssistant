# Some Factors Affecting the Resistance of Rainbow Trout to Acid Waters

## Executive Summary
This 1964 laboratory study by Lloyd and Jordan investigates the physiological and environmental factors governing acid tolerance in [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*). Using controlled acidified aquaria, the authors examined how pH acclimatization history, water hardness, free carbon dioxide concentration, and fish age/size influence survival time in acidic solutions. They further investigated the physiological cause of death via blood chemistry (pH and total CO₂ content), concluding that **acidaemia**, rather than gill epithelial damage or mucus precipitation, was the primary lethal mechanism. The findings have direct implications for setting safe pH and free CO₂ thresholds for acid waste discharge into rivers supporting trout fisheries, and are relevant to understanding [[Freshwater Fish Acid Tolerance]] more broadly.

## Background & Description
- Test subjects: [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii* Richardson), sourced from commercial hatcheries reared in hard water, ranging **4.5–15 cm** in length.
- Comparative species: [[Roach]] (*Rutilus rutilus*), ranging **7.5–13 cm**.
- No visible histological damage was found in gill epithelial cells or other gill tissue of trout killed in acid solution (pH 3.4, survival time 450 min) — ruling out mucus precipitation or cellular necrosis as the lethal mechanism.
- Fish developed a **slightly opaque film** over the body surface (but not the gills) in low-pH solutions (pH 3.0–3.3).
- No "coughing" behavior was observed (a symptom typically associated with heavy-metal-induced gill damage), further supporting the absence of epithelial injury.
- Fish overturned at pH 3.80 after 24 hr recovered fully upon transfer to clean water (pH ~8.2), indicating no permanent tissue damage from sub-lethal acid exposure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- Natural river habitats supporting trout fisheries range from **soft acid streams** (normal pH as low as 5.5) to **hard alkaline chalk rivers** (pH >8.0), establishing broad natural tolerance limits for [[Salmonidae]].
- Acid waste discharges threaten fisheries via two distinct toxic pathways:
  1. **Free CO₂ liberation** from bicarbonate-rich waters — approx. 100 ppm CO₂ is lethal at air-saturation dissolved oxygen (Alabaster et al., 1957).
  2. **Direct hydrogen ion toxicity** in low-bicarbonate waters, with pH <4.0 generally toxic in absence of CO₂ effects (Doudoroff & Katz, 1950).
- Water hardness (total CaCO₃) is an ecological modifier of acid resistance — harder water increases short-term resistance to lethal pH, though the effect diminishes over longer exposures.
- [[Roach]] were found to be markedly more acid-sensitive than [[Rainbow Trout]] at strongly acidic pH (3.0–3.3), converging in sensitivity at milder acidity (pH 4.2) — suggesting differential niche partitioning along acidified river gradients.
- Related species [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) tolerate a natural pH range of 4.1–9.5 (Creaser, 1930), while [[Brown Trout]] are reportedly absent from Norwegian streams with normal pH below 5.2 (Dahl, 1927) — consistent with this study's extrapolated 3-month sub-lethal threshold of pH 5.0.

## Key Findings

### Acclimatization pH Has No Protective Effect
- Trout acclimatized for 5 days to pH 6.55, 7.50, or 8.40 showed **no difference** in resistance to acute lethal acid exposure (≤1 day survival).

### Water Hardness Increases Short-Term Acid Resistance
- Pooled with 1.5 ppm free CO₂, LT50 (4-day) pH values were:
  - **320 ppm CaCO₃**: pH 4.18
  - **40 ppm CaCO₃**: pH 4.22
  - **12 ppm CaCO₃**: pH 4.25
- Resistance increases with hardness for shorter survival periods; differences narrow at longer exposure times.

### Free CO₂ Effects Are Concentration- and Duration-Dependent
- At pH values causing death within ~3 hr to 14 days, log(survival time) vs. pH is **linear**, and this relationship is **unaffected by up to 10 ppm free CO₂**.
- At **≥20–50 ppm free CO₂**, survival time is progressively reduced beyond ~14 days of exposure, producing a "double curve" relationship rather than a single linear one.
- No significant effect on survival was detected across 1.5–48 ppm free CO₂ at fixed pH (3.3 or 3.6) within short (≤1 day) exposures — CO₂ effects manifest primarily over longer time frames.

### Age/Size Positively Correlates with Acid Resistance
- No correlation found between fish **length** and survival time within a single cohort.
- Strong **positive correlation between age (season) and survival time** — a three-fold increase in survival time from June to October corresponded to only ~0.3 pH unit increase in resistance.
- No significant correlation between test temperature and survival time.

### Comparative Species Sensitivity
- [[Roach]] are more sensitive than [[Rainbow Trout]] at pH 3.0–3.3 (rapid loss of equilibrium), but the two species converge in sensitivity near pH 4.2.

### Physiological Cause of Death: Acidaemia
- Blood pH and total CO₂ content were measured via cardiac puncture and micro-diffusion analysis.
- Fish overturned in acid solutions had **significantly lower venous blood pH** than controls:
  - At 1.5 ppm free CO₂: median blood pH 7.00 (acid) vs. 7.20 (control)
  - At 50 ppm free CO₂: median blood pH 6.65 (acid) vs. 7.21 (control)
- Free CO₂ content of blood tracked the free CO₂ content of the water but **did not increase disproportionately in acid conditions**, ruling out CO₂ retention as the lethal factor.
- Bicarbonate concentration in blood dropped sharply under combined acid + high-CO₂ conditions (9.4 mM/kg at pH 4.5/50ppm CO₂ vs. 30.1 mM/kg at pH 7.0/50ppm CO₂), indicating **failure to maintain buffering capacity**.
- These results support a mechanism of **acidaemia** (blood acidification) as the proximate cause of death, consistent with independent findings by Jonas et al. (1962), who found rainbow trout died when venous blood pH fell below 6.85 within 1 minute of acid injection.
- Findings align with prior work on [[Ambloplites rupestris]], [[Micropterus dolomieu]], and [[Perca flavescens]] (Powers et al., 1938) showing bicarbonate compensation for elevated blood CO₂ under normal pH, a mechanism that fails when ambient pH is simultaneously depressed.

### Environmental Management Implications
- Extrapolation suggests exposure to **pH < 5.0 for ~3 months** may harm rainbow trout when free CO₂ is low.
- If acid discharge raises free CO₂ to **≥20 ppm**, mortalities could occur within ~3 months at **pH < 6.0** — a substantially more conservative threshold than pH alone would suggest.
- These thresholds provide a quantitative basis for regulating acid waste discharge into rivers supporting [[Salmonid Fisheries]].
