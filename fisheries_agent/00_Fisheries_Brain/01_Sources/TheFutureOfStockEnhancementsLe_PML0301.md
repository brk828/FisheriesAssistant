# Future of Stock Enhancements: Lessons for Hatchery Practice from Conservation Biology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #stock-enhancement

## Executive Summary

This review examines why hatchery-reared fish released for fisheries restocking and endangered-species recovery so often fail to survive in the wild. The authors argue that fisheries scientists have known of behavioural deficits in hatchery fish and poor post-release survival for over a century, yet hatchery practice has remained focused on husbandry and mass production. Drawing on [[Conservation Biology]] and [[Reintroduction Biology]], the paper reviews three approaches to improving post-release performance: [[Environmental Enrichment]], [[Life-Skills Training]] and [[Soft Release]]. The authors conclude that many of these measures could be implemented by hatcheries with relative ease, at little cost, and could substantially raise post-release survival.

## Background & Description

The paper is a review article published in *Fish and Fisheries* (2002, vol. 3, pp. 79–94) by Culum Brown and Rachel L. Day of the Sub-department of Animal Behaviour, Department of Zoology, University of Cambridge.

Hatcheries breed, rear and release large numbers of fish. Welcomme and Bartley (1998) estimated that more than 300 species worldwide are involved, of which about 290 are freshwater, so marine stock enhancement is comparatively uncommon. Reported release scales include:

- More than 5 billion hatchery-reared Atlantic salmon released each year (figure cited in the text).
- About 8 million Atlantic salmon released in the UK in 2000, of which 98% (rendered "987o" in the scan) went to Scotland.
- Iran in 1996: 72 million sturgeon, 2.5 million perch, 100 million bream and 140 million mahi sephid (*Rutilus frisii*).

Restocking is also used for endangered species. In 1990, 27% of US federal recovery programmes for endangered freshwater fish included captive breeding, and steelhead trout listed under the US Endangered Species Act depend heavily on hatchery-reared individuals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Restocking versus reintroduction.** Fisheries management and conservation biology share the goal of long-term stock viability, but differ in priorities. Conservationists seek self-sustaining wild populations and survival to reproductive age. Fisheries managers seek a stable return to the industry, so survival to a catchable size matters most. The authors stress that both groups should aim to improve post-release survivorship.

**Hatchery shortfalls.** Hatchery rearing techniques have improved, but the proportion of fish surviving to adulthood is declining. Typical figures cited:

- Salmonids: usually less than 5% of hatchery-reared fish reach adulthood (McNeil 1991).
- UK: likely below 3%.
- Chum salmon 1–3%; cod about 0.1% (the scan reads "(lolo", interpreted as 0.1%, per Salvanes 2001).
- Cod released at 6 months to 2 years: mortality at least twice that of wild juveniles (Svåsand et al. 1989).
- Japanese flounder: only about 10% survive to 10 days post-release.

Most mortality occurs in the first few days after release, consistent with predator-mediated losses. Fish that survive the first week or so have a much greater chance of long-term survival.

**Genetic and ecological concerns.** Poor brood-stock choice can cause genetic pollution of wild stocks. The Mary River cod recovery programme in Australia maximizes genetic diversity by regularly turning over brood stock. Introductions outside natural range for fisheries should be strongly discouraged or strictly controlled.

**Behavioural deficits.** Hatchery fish are reared on monotonous pellet diets at high densities, often up to 100 times greater than in nature, in featureless enclosures. Their foraging and antipredator behaviours are poorly developed. Released fish may not feed for days or weeks, take risky surface positions, disperse poorly, and show limited prey choice and weight loss.

## Key Findings

**1. Post-release survival is the critical problem.** The authors argue that research and hatchery practice should shift from husbandry to improving post-release behavioural performance.

**2. Six core behavioural areas matter for reintroduction.** Following Kleiman (1996), candidates must be able to (i) avoid predators, (ii) acquire and process food, (iii) interact socially, (iv) find or construct shelters or nests, (v) locomote in complex terrain, and (vi) orient and navigate.

**3. Environmental enrichment improves survival.** Brief exposure to naturalistic outdoor ponds improved survival; brown trout reared in outside ponds survived better after release than standard hatchery fish (Naeslund 1992). Submerged structure, cover, natural flow and darkbackgrounds reduce aggression and improve growth. Steelhead provided with in-water structure and overhead cover reached higher social rank and growth rate (Berejikian et al. 2000).

**4. Life-skills training can be brief.** Prior exposure to predators strongly improves antipredator responses. Thompson (1965) reported a 50% increase in survival of salmon conditioned with electric shocks. Olla and Davis (1989) found that prior predator exposure substantially increased survival. The authors suggest training need only be initiated shortly before release, with a single exposure sometimes sufficient. Live prey can be introduced periodically or just before release.

**5. Soft release improves acclimatization.** Hard release (dumping fish from drums) contrasts with soft release, which provides an acclimatization period at the release site. Red wolves with longer acclimatization survived longer (Phillips et al. 1995). Vincent (1960) estimated that 10% of released fish were disoriented. Acclimatization for around 3 days helps fish familiarize with new environments, and brown trout held in river enclosures for 6 days showed better growth and recapture rates (Jonsson et al. 1999). Intermediate acclimatization is suggested to reduce post-release mortality, though the optimal duration needs testing.

**6. Transport stress matters.** Elevated stress from transport increases predator susceptibility, impairs learning, and raises infection risk. Physiological recovery takes hours, but behavioural recovery may take days to weeks.

**7. Release site and resident populations.** Success often correlates with existing resident populations. Resident fish hold an advantage in aggressive encounters, so displacement of wild stocks is less likely than assumed. A lack of resident populations may indicate habitat degradation or heavy fishing pressure, where habitat restoration should accompany release.

**8. Size at release is critical.** Larger fish generally survive better. Small cod (<25–30 cm) suffer high mortality. Japanese flounder released at 4–12 cm survive only if larger than 9 cm. Red sea bream over 4 cm contribute to the catch. Grass carp over 10 cm in their second year show better survival (Li 1999). Longer rearing raises costs and worsens behaviour, so a balance is needed.

**9. Economic feasibility is uncertain.** Few restocking programmes are clearly economically successful. Chum salmon and red sea bream in Japan are exceptions, largely because production costs are low. Modelling of Atlantic cod enhancement in the Gulf of Maine indicated a break-even survival rate of 28% for 36-week-old juveniles, far above anything achieved; Kristiansen (1999) reported 23% first-year survival for fish released at 23 cm. Improving survival could offset higher production costs, and Japanese hatcheries are shifting to fewer, higher-quality juveniles.

**10. Conclusion and recommendations.** The smallest improvements in survival to adulthood could yield substantial gains. Many suggestions are cheap and easy to implement, and several can be applied without further experimentation. The authors call for:
- Improved culture methods as a priority for restocking.
- Greater understanding of the behaviour and ecology of commercial species.
- Closer monitoring of hatchery releases.
- Experimental comparison of reintroduction techniques, which fish, given large release numbers, are well placed to provide.
