## Executive Summary
This paper, authored by Senior Fisheries Biologist Brian M. Chan for the Ministry of Environment, Lands and Parks, outlines British Columbia's approach to balancing **wild trout conservation** with **hatchery-augmented ("planted") trout** stocking programs. B.C. supports a large recreational fishery (400,000 licensed anglers, 4.5 million angler-days, ~9.6 million fish caught in 1990) generating ~$450 million annually. The province's Fisheries Branch manages fish stocks under a strategic mandate prioritizing wild [[Wild Indigenous Fish Policy|wild-indigenous fish]] conservation, habitat protection, and public interest. A distinguishing feature of B.C.'s program is its near-total reliance on **wild-sourced eggs** rather than domesticated hatchery broodstock, reflecting a systemic effort to preserve genetic diversity while still supporting put-and-take stocking in barren or limited-spawning lakes.

## Background & Description
This is a fisheries management and policy paper; detailed morphological data on individual species is not provided. However, the text notes physiological/life-history distinctions relevant to hatchery broodstock management:
- **Precocious (early-maturing) male [[Rainbow Trout]]**: Exhibit high post-maturation mortality rates in stocked lakes and slow growth rates, contributing minimally to sport fisheries.
- **Triploid and All-Female Rainbow Trout strains**: Genetically manipulated stocks developed from wild indigenous broodstock specifically to eliminate early sexual maturation issues, used in quality lake fisheries management.
- **Broodstock Classification System**: Rainbow trout stocks are categorized into four genetic/management types:
  1. **Wild Native** — eggs from wild indigenous "brood" stream populations (minimal trapping of run).
  2. **Semi-Wild Native** — 100% wild genetics, hatchery-reared, then used to stock a "brood lake" for subsequent egg collection.
  3. **Mixed-Strain Native** — native strains of mixed origin reared in brood lakes for egg collection (~35% of provincial supply).
  4. **Domestic** — hatchery broodstock maintained for a sub-catchable urban lake program (southern coast).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Context**: B.C. covers ~974,000 km² (comparable to Washington, Idaho, Wyoming, and Montana combined) but only 2% surface water coverage — ranking 5th in Canada for freshwater resources. This includes ~25,000 lakes, 26 major river systems, and 4,600 streams.
- **Regional Variation**: Interior lake/river systems are more nutrient-rich than coastal systems due to a mountain range buffering the interior from coastal precipitation, creating distinct [[Biogeoclimatic Zones|biogeoclimatic zones]] (temperate rainforest, semi-arid interior dry belt, Northern Montane Spruce forest).
- **Stocking Ecology**: The majority of lake stocking targets small lakes (<1000 ha surface area) that are landlocked or have limited/intermittent natural spawning habitat — meaning these populations would not persist ("few or no fish") without hatchery augmentation.
- **Competing Land Uses**: Wild fish habitat is under pressure from agricultural irrigation, hydroelectric development, mining, and forest harvesting — key drivers behind the shift toward ecosystem-based management (see [[Ptolemy 1993]]).
- **Anthropogenic Impact Threshold**: Wild stocks are formally defined as those completing their life cycle without direct human assistance; this includes both indigenous and transplanted exotic populations, contrasted with "augmented" or hatchery-dependent waters.

## Key Findings
- **Angler Effort & Catch (1990 National Survey)**: 400,000 licensed anglers; 4.5 million angler-days; 9.6 million fish caught, with 4.3 million (44.7%) harvested. [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Kokanee Salmon]], and [[Cutthroat Trout]] comprised three-quarters of total catch, with rainbow trout alone accounting for over half.
- **Economic Value**: Total 1990 angler effort generated approximately $450 million CAD for the provincial economy.
- **Management Structure**: Freshwater fisheries (excluding anadromous Pacific salmon, managed federally by [[Department of Fisheries and Oceans]]) fall under provincial jurisdiction via the Ministry of Environment, Lands and Parks.
- **Stock Inventory Gap**: The Fisheries Branch actively manages **less than 10%** of an estimated **30,000 unique fish stocks** in the province — highlighting inventory as the top management priority.
- **Annual Hatchery Output**: A typical year sees release of **5 million rainbow trout**, **2 million kokanee**, and **just under 1 million brook trout** into approximately **1,000 lakes**.
- **Egg Collection Sourcing (Unique to B.C.)**: Unlike most North American hatchery programs, B.C.'s system relies almost entirely on eggs collected from **wild spawning populations** rather than domesticated broodstock:
  - Wild Native + Semi-Wild Native stocks: **55%** of total rainbow egg collection.
  - Mixed-Strain Native stocks: **~35%**.
  - Special (triploid/all-female) and Domestic stocks: **~10%**.
- **Genetic Innovation**: Triploid and all-female rainbow trout strains were developed over the preceding 3 years (relative to publication) specifically from wild indigenous stocks to mitigate precocious male maturation issues and protect wild gene pools.
- **Policy Framework**: The **[[Wild Indigenous Fish Policy]]** (Ministry of Environment, Lands and Parks, 1993) mandates first priority be given to protection, maintenance, and enhancement of wild-indigenous fish stocks; hatchery/exotic releases permitted only where prior stocking history exists or wild/rare stocks are not compromised.
- **Waters Designation Policy (Draft)**: Classifies all lakes/streams into four categories — **wild-indigenous, wild-naturalized, augmented, or hatchery** — with unstocked/uninventoried waters defaulting to "wild-indigenous" status until proven otherwise, and barren lakes classified as "wild-naturalized."
- **Regulatory Oversight**: The **Federal/Provincial Transplant Technical Committee** (established mid-1970s) holds legislative authority to approve/reject fish and aquatic invertebrate transplant/introduction applications based on impact assessments to native populations.
- **Historical Context**: Most major lake stocking and chemical rehabilitation efforts occurred during the 1950s–1960s, concentrated in the southern third of the province, creating many now-famous sport fisheries in originally barren lakes.
