## Executive Summary
This USFWS technical leaflet, though centered on invertebrate husbandry rather than fish biology directly, provides foundational applied knowledge for the [[Freshwater Angling]] industry. It details the biology, collection, and mass-cultivation techniques for earthworms (Lumbricidae), which serve as a primary natural lure for [[Freshwater Fishes]]. The document synthesizes historical angling practices (dating to 1496) with mid-20th-century industrial bait-farming protocols, covering habitat preferences, harvesting mechanics ("fiddling"), and commercial "scouring" preparation to maximize hook-longevity and vitality for anglers.

## Background & Description
While the source does not focus on fish morphology, it notes specific invertebrate biological states relevant to bait viability:
* **Standard State:** Freshly dug worms are robust but have a shorter hook-life.
* **"Scoured" State:** After 2–4 days in damp sphagnum moss, worms become **almost transparent** but remain tough, lively, and more durable as bait.
* **Egg Capsules:** Reproduction is tracked via capsule production; a mature culture box becomes "full" of egg capsules after approximately 21 days.
* **Pest Identification:** Mite infestations (a common pest in cultures) are identified as small, light-grey creatures roughly the size of a pin head.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The paper details the specific environmental parameters required for the earthworm's terrestrial niche, which directly dictates bait availability for [[Aquatic Ecosystem]] anglers:
* **Soil Preference:** Thrives in soil rich in organic matter; highly plentiful in old lawns, wild grasslands, and compost heaps.
* **Behavioral Triggers:** Surfaces during humid seasons and nighttime rainstorms to feed, mate, and migrate. Vibration sensitivity causes surfacing behavior (exploited via "grunting" or "fiddling").
* **Environmental Limiters:** Avoidant of high winds and low temperatures (will not surface via ground-wetting techniques below 40° F). Retreats to deeper strata during dry weather.
* **Culture Ecosystem:** Optimal artificial habitat consists of a substrate mix of stable manure, screened topsoil, and peat moss, supplemented with corn meal, mash, or a rabbit-pellet/soybean/sugar mixture. Requires darkness and consistent moisture (not sogginess) to mimic natural burrow conditions.
* **Predator/Pest Pressure:** Competing organisms in this niche include mites, ants, mice, and rats, which are drawn to the same organic feed sources.

## Key Findings
* **Historical Context:** Use of earthworms as fishing lures for [[Freshwater Fishes]] is documented as early as 1496 in the *Book of St. Albans*.
* **Collection Method ("Grunting"):** Driving a stick ~10 inches into soil and creating vibration (via friction with a board or axe) can attract earthworms to the surface from a radius of 25–30 feet.
* **Population Density Limits:** Culture boxes are self-limiting; worms cease capsule production if overcrowded. Optimal density is capped at 500–600 worms per standard box (14 x 18 x 6 inches).
* **Harvest Cycle:** Initial capsule-loaded boxes are ready for harvest in 60–90 days; subsequent harvests from mature breeding stock occur every 21–30 days.
* **Pest Management:** Mite infestations are treated with sulphur dust; if ineffective, a Metacide 50% W. spray is used, though it carries toxicity risks requiring a 1-week withdrawal period before worms are harvested for use.
* **Bait Preservation:** Field viability is maximized using insulated bait boxes (sheet metal lined with paraffin-coated cork, wrapped in a wetted canvas jacket) to maintain cool temperatures via evaporation.
