# Bureaucratic Mischief: Recognizing Endangered Species and Subspecies

#conservation-genetics #taxonomy #felidae #canidae #emberizidae

## Executive Summary

This 1991 policy essay by [[Stephen J. O'Brien]] and [[Ernst Mayr]] argues that the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of 1973 is being misapplied because of unclear taxonomy and the treatment of hybrids. The authors examine four ESA-listed taxa, the [[Florida Panther]], [[Gray Wolf]], [[Red Wolf]], and [[Dusky Seaside Sparrow]], where molecular results conflict with traditional morphological classifications. They argue that the [[Hybrid Policy]] of the Department of the Interior's Solicitor's Office should be retained for species but dropped for subspecies and threatened populations. They base this on the [[Biological Species Concept]] (BSC) and propose formal criteria for recognizing subspecies.

## Background & Description

The ESA protects three categories of biological taxa: species, subspecies, and populations. The authors state that the operational definitions of these terms, inadequate taxonomy, and periodic hybridization have caused confusion, conflict, and misinterpretation of the Act. Listing has increased research in [[Molecular Genetics]] and field ecology, and in some cases genetic results have contradicted earlier morphology-based taxonomy. Because listing carries legal protection and funding, these taxonomic designations directly affect management.

The essay describes the Hybrid Policy as three Solicitor's Office opinions ruling that hybrids between endangered species, subspecies, or populations cannot be protected. The authors argue that this policy was built on flawed taxonomic assumptions.

The essay defines key terms:

- **Biological Species Concept (BSC):** [[Ernst Mayr]] (1940) defined species as "groups of actually or potentially interbreeding populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups." Reproductive isolation is the central criterion, and the authors note that most species occupy distinct ecological niches.
- **Subspecies:** a geographically defined aggregate of local populations that differ taxonomically from other subdivisions of the species. The authors propose that members share a unique geographic range or habitat, a set of phylogenetically concordant phenotypic characters, and a unique natural history. Subspecies are reproductively compatible and normally allopatric.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The essay treats the four cases as examples of how genetic evidence and legal rules interact:

- **[[Florida Panther]]:** A small population of mountain lions in southern Florida, with fewer than 50 individuals. The population shows physiological and reproductive impairments, likely due to [[Inbreeding Depression]]. Allozyme and mtDNA analysis showed two genetic stocks, one resembling other North American pumas and one more closely related to a South American puma subspecies. Captive animals of mixed origin were released into the Everglades between 1957 and 1967. Today the population contains a mixture of two subspecies.
- **[[Gray Wolf]]:** Populations have declined through habitat loss from agriculture. An mtDNA survey across the northern United States and Canada found coyote mtDNA in wolf populations, but not the reverse, in a restricted region from northern Minnesota to southern Quebec. Farm Bureaus in Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho petitioned to delist the species, and similar reasoning was used to oppose reintroduction in Yellowstone National Park.
- **[[Red Wolf]]:** Its taxonomic status has long been disputed, with some authors treating it as a species and others as a gray wolf subspecies. Molecular analyses of captive animals and museum specimens raised the possibility that the red wolf group is a hybrid of gray wolf and coyote.
- **[[Dusky Seaside Sparrow]]:** A melanistic coastal subspecies from eastern Florida. Its population declined until 1980, when five birds were brought into captivity and crossed with [[Scott's Seaside Sparrow]], a subspecies from the Gulf Coast of Florida. A 1981 Solicitor's Office opinion held that hybridization between subspecies would not serve the ESA. The subspecies became extinct in 1987.

## Key Findings

1. **Taxonomy drives ESA enforcement.** The authors argue that the four cases show the importance of taxonomy to ESA enforcement and the value of molecular data in resolving relationships.
2. **Hybrid Policy is appropriate for species but not subspecies.** The authors recommend keeping the policy to discourage interspecies hybridization, which can disintegrate the genetic organization of a species. They recommend dropping it for subspecies and threatened populations, because subspecies interbreed naturally when in contact and their hybridization does not threaten their status.
3. **Subspecies have four possible fates.** A subspecies may go extinct, exchange genes to form a new "mixed" subspecies, change over time into one or more new subspecies, or become a new species if isolated. The probability of differentiation increases with time of allopatry, and subspecies may carry ecologically relevant adaptations. The authors cite these as reasons to protect them.
4. **Case-by-case application of BSC principles:**
   - The Florida panther clearly qualifies as a subspecies and should keep its protection. The authors suggest the population may benefit from new genes.
   - Gray wolf and coyote hybridization is limited to a narrow Midwestern hybrid zone and does not affect the species elsewhere. The authors find no justification for removing protection.
   - The red wolf's status is difficult because it is extinct in the wild. Captive survivors may be the only descendants of a historic gray wolf subspecies.
   - The dusky seaside sparrow's molecular characters separated it from Gulf Coast populations but not from Atlantic Coast populations. The authors suggest revising the taxonomy to recognize Gulf Coast and Atlantic Coast subspecies, with protection considered if either becomes rare.
5. **Legal opinions need to recognize the species-subspecies distinction.** The authors conclude that biological species do not form hybrids that disintegrate population genetic organization, but subspecies may, and that the Hybrid Policy should not be applied to subspecies because they retain the potential to interbreed freely as part of natural processes.
