# Life History, Ecology, and Management of the Carp (Cyprinus carpio) in Elephant Butte Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This monographic report, based on eight years of investigations (June 1964 to March 1972) by [[Douglas B. Jester]] and graduate assistants at New Mexico State University, examines the [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) in [[Elephant Butte Lake]], a large, warm, morphometrically oligotrophic [[Rio Grande]] mainstream reservoir in southwestern New Mexico. The study covers taxonomy, distribution, age and growth, survival, length-weight relationship, condition, reproduction, feeding, population dynamics, disease, and harvest management. The central conclusion is that carp should be managed as part of a "rough" or commercial-fish unit with [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] and [[River Carpsucker]], because these species interact strongly and harvesting only one tends to be offset by increases in the others.

## Background & Description

**Classification.** Carp belong to Phylum Chordata, Class Osteichthyes, Subclass Actinopterygii, Order Cypriniformes, Family Cyprinidae, Subfamily Cyprininae, Genus *Cyprus*. Systematics have been stable since Linnaeus (1758). Subspecies are considered doubtful because of worldwide culture and introduction. Carp are separated from goldfish by two barbels on each side of the upper jaw, lateral-line scale counts above 32 (versus below 30 in goldfish), and differences in gill rakers and pharyngeal teeth (three rows in carp versus one).

**Appearance.** Carp are robust and compressed, with a dorsal profile more convex than the ventral one. Colour ranges from reddish brown on the back to silver below, with a frequently pale yellow belly. Local fish varied from olivaceous to blackish red. "Leather" and "mirror" varieties arose under domestication.

**Distribution.** Carp are native to Asia and were introduced into Europe. The first US shipment arrived in 1877, and fingerlings were distributed to 38 states by 1879. The species is now the most widely distributed freshwater fish on earth. In New Mexico, carp occur in all five major drainages, from 2,800 ft (845 m) up to 7,000–7,500 ft (2,134–2,287 m), roughly the lower limit of year-round trout waters. The Rio Grande population originated from 6,880 fingerlings stocked near Albuquerque and 2,140 at El Paso in 1884–1885.

**Cultural status.** In the US carp are generally regarded as a "rough" or undesirable fish, but they are valued by some ethnic and food-market groups, including Jewish and African American communities. Local residents fished for carp for food, and some carp are sold as bait or for [[Flathead Catfish]] angling.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Elephant Butte Lake lies at about 4,500 ft (1,646 m) elevation, was first impounded in 1915, and is the oldest reservoir in New Mexico. Its potential capacity is 2,150,000 acre-feet, with a surface area of about 40,000 acres. Storage fluctuates widely, from 30,500 to 600,000 acre-feet during the study. About 35% of the bottom is shoal water shallower than 11 m (35 ft). Mean annual rainfall is about 8 inches, and vegetation is typical of the Lower Sonoran Life Zone.

**Thermal and chemical regime.** The lake is morphometrically oligotrophic and essentially dimictic, although density currents prevent a true thermocline from forming. Mean annual surface temperature is about 15.6 °C. Dissolved oxygen ranged from 4.1 to 13.5 mg/l across the monitoring record, pH from 7.5 to 8.9, and total hardness from 134 to 352 mg/l. Turbidity reached up to 280 Jackson Units in the upstream third during windstorms and spring inflow. No chemical toxicity or correlation with fish movement was found.

**Plankton and benthos.** Plankton and benthos are sparse, attributed to depth, bottom type, and continuous silt deposition. Mean plankton volume was 4.1 ml/m³ in the 1968–69 sampling, and zooplankton made up 96% of plankton organisms. The dominant benthos included Chironomidae, Oligochaeta, and Culicidae. The fish fauna comprises 27 species, including [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[White Bass]].

**Habitat use.** Carp concentrate in the upstream third of the lake, where silt flats with inundated vegetation (such as saltcedar, cattail, and forbs) provide cover in depths of 2.5–4 ft. Catch rates were 6.3 carp per net-unit upstream in June–July, against 1.8 in the middle third and 0.4 downstream. In winter carp disperse over the bottom.

## Key Findings

**Age and growth**
- Scales and opercles were both validated for ageing carp, though opercles must be read with extreme care for early annuli. Scale body regression: L = 34.88 + 0.522(S), r = 0.993. Opercle body regression: L = −3.34 + 13.06(O), r = 0.987.
- In the 1969 sample of 214 fish (100 females, 65 males, 49 immature or undetermined), Age Groups II–XII were represented. Males and females showed no significant difference in growth through Age Group VIII.
- Growth is about average for waters from Tennessee and Iowa to Utah. [[Lee's Phenomenon]] was not evident.
- The largest carp (765 mm, 8,675 g, Age Group XVIII, female) was a unique specimen.

**Survival and longevity**
- Mean life span was 1.30 years, or 475 days, for carp hatched in the lake. Maximum longevity was about eight years for males and 12 for females, with the 18-year-old female as an exception.
- Approximately two million carp must hatch for one to reach Age Group XII.

**Length-weight and condition**
- Length-weight relationship: log W = −4.62025 + 2.87981 log L.
- Grand mean Kₜₗ = 1.23, lower than in six other waters and higher than in Bear Lake, Utah. Condition varied with season, with peaks in winter and spring and a summer low.

**Reproduction**
- Spawning occurs from March to October at 14–23 °C, with peak activity at 21–22 °C. Recently spent ovaries appeared from March to October, and the main spawning period is May to July.
- Carp spawn in water under four feet deep over vegetation, debris, or exposed bottom. Spawning groups of four to 20 fish form around a large female.
- Females outnumbered males in samples older than three years (0.65 and 0.53 males per female in 1969 and 1971). Males matured faster: 82% of males were mature in Age Group II, against 14% of females. All fish of both sexes were mature by Age Group IV.
- Fecundity ranged from 19,578 ova (Age Group IV) to 166,913 (Age Group XII), with a weighted mean of 47,134. Age was the strongest predictor; combined length, weight, and age gave R² = 0.967.

**Diet**
- Carp are versatile omnivores. Chlorophyta and Chrysophyta were the most important food items, with plant material making up 43.5% and animal material 10.2% of identified items.

**Population dynamics and interactions**
- Carp biomass in the 1965–72 Rio Grande rotenone sites was 1,863 lb/mile (1957) and 1,840 lb/mile (1960). At Caballo Lake, carp made up 27% of killed fish biomass.
- Carp rose from an estimated 13,496 fish in 1965–66 to about 59,700 in 1971–72. Over the same period smallmouth buffalo declined by 222,865 fish (26.4%), and river carpsucker rose from 17,412 to 103,419.
- Commercial fish overall declined by 10.4% in number and 14.8% in weight, indicating that the three species compete for shared bottom resources.

**Harvest and management**
- Gill nets outperformed seines, trawls, and traps. Four- and five-inch stretch mesh were most efficient for carp and carpsucker, and brown nets were highly selective for both (carp were 19.1% of the catch against 5.0% of the population).
- Baits (sweet horse feed, dog food, cottonseed meal, gizzard shad, and barley) gave no practical gain in catch rates.
- Carp should be harvested together with buffalo and carpsucker, using the same gear, places, and times. Harvesting carp alone is inadequate, and the authors recommend that carp be exploited to the maximum market and state budgets allow.

**Disease and parasites**
- Only one fungus, one trematode, and one copepod were observed in Elephant Butte Lake from 1964 to 1972. None caused significant mortality.
