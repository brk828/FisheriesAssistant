# Occurrence, Growth, and Food Habits of the Spotted Hake (*Urophycis regia*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #phycidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the [[Spotted Hake]] (*Urophycis regia*) population in the [[Cape Fear Estuary]], North Carolina, and adjacent Atlantic Ocean waters from 1973–1978. A total of 62,867 individuals were collected via trawling, with fish predominantly young-of-the-year (age-0, 25–225 mm SL), plus a smaller cohort of age-1 fish (230–295 mm SL) present in the estuary from January to June. The species exhibits a seasonal shoreward migration, using the estuary as a nursery ground during colder months (February–April), before moving offshore or northward as water temperatures exceed 22°C. Diet analysis identified [[Mysidacea]] (particularly *Neomysis americana*) and decapod crustaceans as dominant prey for juveniles, with a dietary shift toward fish prey (clupeid and sciaenid larvae) in larger individuals. Despite its abundance and relatively large attainable size, *U. regia* remains commercially unexploited in the U.S., though the authors suggest market potential given global trends in hake utilization.

## Background & Description

- **Size range:** 25–295 mm standard length (SL); mean overall length 186 mm SL.
- **Age classes:**
  - **Age-0:** <225 mm SL, mean length 112 mm SL.
  - **Age-1:** 230–295 mm SL (up to 295 mm SL, the largest recorded for this study), mean length 259 mm SL. Age-1 fish comprised <1% of the sampled population.
- **Length-weight relationship:** Curvilinear regressions calculated per year (1973–1978), generally consistent across years:
  - 1973: W = 0.00003L^2.97 (N=2835, r=.95)
  - 1974: W = 0.00001L^3.14 (N=81, r=.99)
  - 1975: W = 0.0005L^2.11 (N=1341, r=.86)
  - 1976: W = 0.00003L^3.00 (N=522, r=.98)
  - 1977: W = 0.00002L^3.07 (N=1733, r=.97)
  - 1978: W = 0.00003L^3.00 (N=6072, r=.97)
  - (W = weight in grams, L = SL in mm)
- **Functional morphology (feeding-related):** Subterminal mouth, chin barbel, well-developed air bladder, and chemoreceptive [[Pelvic Fins]] used to detect and test palatability of prey items on the substrate — consistent with benthic foraging behavior described for related [[Urophycis]] species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Estuarine nursery habitat (Cape Fear Estuary) used by juveniles during late winter through late spring; adults spawn offshore, as no ripe adults or eggs have ever been collected in estuarine waters.
- **Temperature range:** Most abundant in waters 13–20°C; sharply declines above 22°C, with only 801 of 62,867 individuals (1.3%) collected above this threshold — suggesting temperature-driven offshore/northward migration.
- **Salinity tolerance:** Collected across a broad salinity range (0–36 ppt), with the majority in 9–34 ppt; no clear distributional relationship with salinity was found in this system (contrasts with [[York River]] populations, where <7 ppt limited upriver movement).
- **Seasonality:** Peak occurrence February–April (particularly March–April, accounting for ~79.5% combined monthly presence); virtually absent June–January in the estuary.
- **Habitat preference within estuary:** Power plant intake canal, deep channel stations, and shoal stations south of buoy 19.
- **Trophic role:** Benthic feeder relying on chemoreception via pelvic fins to test prey palatability, though primary prey detection is visual. Diet composition shifts ontogenetically:
  - Juveniles (30–140 mm): dominated by [[Mysidacea]] (highest IRI).
  - Larger individuals: increasing reliance on fish prey (clupeid and sciaenid larvae), consistent with patterns noted in [[Urophycis Chuss|Red Hake]] and other regional hake studies.
- **Comparative biogeography:** Growth rates and maximum first-year size are smaller than in more northern populations (e.g., [[York River-Lower Chesapeake Bay]] fish reaching up to 253 mm SL in their first year vs. 230 mm SL in Cape Fear), suggesting a latitudinal cline in growth potential.

## Key Findings

- **Abundance:** *U. regia* ranked 7th in numerical abundance and 8th by weight among the 15 most common fish species in the Cape Fear Estuary; comprised 3.2% of all fish collected (2,013,983 total) over the study.
- **Interannual variability:** Catches varied widely by year (702 in 1974 to 28,885 in 1978), with larger catches associated with lower average water temperatures (7–8°C).
- **Growth rates:** Average monthly growth increments ranged from 12–26 mm SL (mean 18 mm/month across 6 years); the largest recorded increase was 92 mm SL between January and June 1977.
- **Food habits:** Based on 2,416 stomachs examined (87% containing food):
  - Arthropods present in 78.4% of stomachs (54.6% by volume) — dominated by [[Mysidacea]] (63.1% frequency, 34.8% volume), especially *Neomysis americana*.
  - Fish prey present in 34.9% of stomachs (33.0% by volume), dominated by clupeid and sciaenid larvae; Atlantic croaker (*[[Micropogonias undulatus]]*) larvae were most common in 1973, *Anchoa* sp. in 1976.
  - Decapods were the second most important arthropod prey group (24.9% frequency, 12.4% volume).
  - Index of Relative Importance (IRI) analysis confirmed Mysidacea as dominant prey 1973–1976, with fish becoming more important (surpassing Mysidacea) in 1977–1978.
- **Commercial potential:** Despite currently having no commercial value in the U.S., the species' abundance and attainable size (up to 350 mm SL) suggest unexplored market potential, paralleling increased global commercial reliance on other [[Hake]] species (per Whitaker 1980).
- **Comparative regional studies:** Findings align broadly with prior studies from [[Chesapeake Bay]] (Barans 1969, 1972), [[Georgia Estuaries]] (Sikora et al. 1972), the [[Gulf of Maine]] (Bigelow and Schroeder 1953), and the Mid-Atlantic Bight (Sedberry 1983; Bowman and Michaels 1984), all confirming crustacean- and fish-dominated diets and estuarine nursery use during cooler months.
- **Life history note:** This is the only study to document age-1 *U. regia* persisting within an estuarine system (rather than solely offshore), documented in 1973, 1976, 1977, and 1978 samples.
