# Your Stake in Wetlands

#ichthyology #marine-biology #wetland-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1962 public circular from the [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]] explains what wetlands are, why they matter to a wide range of users, how they have been lost, and what conservation programs are in place. It frames wetlands as a national "resource estate" whose main purpose is preserving [[Waterfowl Habitat]], while noting that this protection also supports most other wetland-dwelling plants and animals, including fish. The circular argues that most wetlands must stay in private hands, so public purchase alone cannot save them. It asks readers to support preservation.

## Background & Description

- **Legal basis:** On October 4, 1961, the "wetlands bill" became [[Public Law 87-883]]. It authorized Congress to appropriate up to $105 million as a loan fund to accelerate the Bureau's acquisition of wetlands by purchase or lease.
- **Funding:** The loan fund is repayable from [[Duck Stamp]] sales.
- **Definition:** Wetlands are lowlands covered, even temporarily, by water no more than 6 feet deep, long enough to support moist-soil plants. Examples include pasture ponds, [[Coastal Marshes]], mountain beaver meadows, southern river bottoms, [[Prairie Potholes]], and marshes around [[Great Salt Lake]].
- **Extent:** In 1956 the United States had 74 million acres of wetlands.
- **Economic value:** Nearly $4 billion was spent annually on hunting and sport fishing. Some people earn their livelihood from wetland wildlife and fish, including trappers, commercial fishermen, oystermen, hunting guides, and resort owners.
- **Recreational value:** The circular notes that the value of intangible recreation (picnicking, hiking, camping, photography, bird watching) cannot be estimated, and that no technique yet exists to express it in monetary terms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Waterfowl and birds.** The [[Prairie Potholes]] of the North Central United States and South Central Canada produce more than half of the continent's ducks. Adult ducks use small temporary water bodies for resting, while deeper ponds are used to raise broods. Other breeding grounds across the United States, though individually less productive, collectively sustain the autumn flights. Many upland game birds, such as ruffed grouse and pheasants, depend on wetland edges and cover. Sora rails, snipe, and woodcock also depend on widely scattered wetland types.

**Fish and shellfish.** Some of the most productive [[Sport Fisheries]], particularly in the South, occur in shallow wetlands. The text cites [[Bluegill]] in weedy pond margins and [[Largemouth Bass]] near marshy estuary edges. [[Oysters]] from salt marshes and shallow bays are an important food source. Many marine fishes, including [[Atlantic Menhaden]] and [[Shrimp]], use tidal estuaries and marshes as nursery areas during crucial life-cycle stages.

**Mammals.** Wooded swamplands in the North and Southeast provide [[White-Tailed Deer]] and turkey range. [[Muskrat]] and [[River Otter]] are major furbearers that depend on wetlands.

**Ecosystem balance.** The circular stresses that productivity in coastal wetlands depends on an "intricate balance" between wet and dry areas and between fresh and salt water.

## Key Findings

1. **Wetlands are a shared national resource.** They support hunters, fishermen, photographers, trappers, farmers, and many other groups, whether for sport or livelihood.

2. **Wetland value is widely distributed.** Waterfowl depend on many scattered wetland types, so the preservation of varied, widely spaced wetlands is essential.

3. **Wetland losses have been severe.**
   - Wetlands were reduced by 45 percent in the 100 years before 1955.
   - More than a million acres of prairie potholes had been drained since 1943.
   - The text notes that drained lands often produce good crops, and that preservation costs are high.

4. **Major threats are identified:**
   - **Dredging:** channels cut for mosquito control or navigation disrupt the wet-dry and fresh-salt balance of coastal wetlands.
   - **Siltation:** dredged spoil fills marshes, and silt destroys the productivity of downstream estuaries.
   - **Farm drainage:** agricultural drainage, supported by subsidies, has removed large areas.
   - **Stream channelization:** straightened channels stop floodwaters from reaching bottom lands used by ducks.
   - **Pollution:** near urban areas, pollution reduces or destroys the value of wetlands for fish and wildlife.

5. **Conservation responses:**
   - The [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]] runs a combination of purchase and easement programs in the prairie pothole region, plus field contacts with landowners and drainage agencies. The circular notes that drainage subsidies limit the effectiveness of this approach.
   - Stock pond construction in the western Dakotas and eastern Montana creates some habitat but does not compensate for natural wetlands lost.
   - State agencies and the [[Soil Conservation Service]] purchase or create other threatened wetlands, including through the small watershed program. When properly designed and managed, these areas benefit fish and wildlife.

6. **Conclusion:** Public ownership can protect only a nucleus of the best remaining wetlands, so most must stay in private trust. The circular calls for public support for these preservation programs, especially for small prairie potholes whose national importance is harder to appreciate than that of large marshes.
