# Water Quality Trends in the Las Vegas Wash Wetlands

#limnology #wetland-ecology #nutrient-cycling

## Executive Summary

This conference paper (Symposium on the Aquatic Resources Management of the Colorado River Ecosystem, Las Vegas, 1981) examines water quality and nutrient removal in the [[Las Vegas Wash]], a constructed wetland that receives treated effluent before it enters [[Lake Mead]]. The wash is fed almost entirely by perennial flows from sewage treatment plants and is valued as a low-cost alternative to [[Advanced Wastewater Treatment]]. Monitoring from July 1979 to December 1980 at five stations shows that the wetland removes substantial total nitrogen and phosphorus on average, while a net nitrate contribution from contaminated groundwater offsets part of that benefit. The authors conclude that the wash behaves seasonally as a nitrogen and phosphorus trap, but that channelization and erosion are reducing its efficiency.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The wash lies in the northern [[Mojave Desert]], receiving an average of only about 10 cm of rain annually. It is the terminus of the Las Vegas Valley drainage basin and empties into [[Las Vegas Bay]] of Lake Mead on the [[Colorado River]].
- **Water source:** About 90% of the water is secondarily treated wastewater from the City of Las Vegas and Clark County [[Sewage Treatment Plant]]s. [[Vegas Creek]], the last natural stream in the valley, dried up in the late 1940s.
- **Loads to Lake Mead:** Flows contribute on average 1.7 t of nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus) and 4 t of oxygen-consuming organic material (BOD5) per day. Nitrate (2.7 t/day) and total dissolved solids (603 t/day) are derived mainly from groundwater inputs, which originate in large underground salt mounds formed by industrial effluent discharged into unlined evaporation ponds until 1978.
- **Discharge growth:** Total wastewater discharge averages 2.8 m³/s (100 cfs), twice the 1970 flow measured at [[Northshore Road]]. The region has nearly half a million permanent residents and about nine million tourists annually.
- **Vegetation:** Increased flows transformed desert shrub and mesquite woodland into dense stands of [[Cattail]] (*Typha domingensis*) and [[Common Reed]] (*Phragmites communis*), bordered by the introduced [[Salt Cedar]] (*Tamarix pentandra*).
- **Policy context:** Conflicting municipal, recreational and downriver interests make the wash a focus of legal disputes over advanced treatment. [[Public Law 92-500]], Section 208 (parts d and f), encourages water reclamation and recycling and revenue-producing uses such as aquaculture, silviculture and agriculture combined with open space.
- **Study aim:** To describe historical and current water quality and quantify the nutrient removal occurring in the wash.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and stations.** Five sampling stations (W1 to W5) run from the confluence of the STP effluents to Northshore Road, about 6 km from Lake Mead. The wash has two morphological parts divided at [[Pabco Road]]. The upper reach has a gradual gradient (about 7 m drop over 4 km), supports the largest cattail extent, and drains through culverts into irregular ponds. The lower reach is steeper (about 8.5 m drop over 4 km) and receives the main saline groundwater inflows between W3 and W5.

**Hydrology and retention.** A [[Hydraulic Retention Time]] study (November 1980) found travel times from the STPs to Lake Mead of less than 20 hours. Channelized flows increase velocity above and below the wetland, and the longest residence times occur in the wetland and ponds.

**Wetland change and erosion.** A 1975 [[Channelization]] program in the upper reach (to 1.6 km downstream of the STPs) steadily reduced wetland area from a 1969–1975 maximum of about 730 ha to 120 ha in 1979. Increased velocities and unstable soils have accelerated [[Headcutting Erosion]] over the past five years. Sediment transport was particularly large in 1979 and 1980. The principal headcut advanced about 1.5 km upstream during a single storm in February 1980, draining another 50 ha and creating riparian habitat with channel depths often exceeding 6 m. Current wetland vegetation covers 55 ha, including 6 ha of shallow ponds.

**Nitrogen dynamics.**
- Groundwater-derived nitrate was a major input, but its loads declined after industrial discharges to unlined ponds ended in the mid-1970s.
- Ammonia loads at Northshore Road rose steadily and, according to the authors, now exceed 60% of total nitrogen load.
- As water slows in the cattail stands, [[Denitrification]] by anaerobic bacteria converts nitrate to nitrogen gas, reducing nitrate concentrations in the upper marsh. No rate measurements were available.
- Ammonia concentrations rose slightly in the marsh, probably from bacterial decomposition of organic nitrogen.

**Phosphorus dynamics.** Upgraded treatment plants have reduced total phosphorus loads compared with earlier years. Spring 1980 showed a net contribution of total phosphorus from headcut sediments, while dissolved phosphorus apparently adsorbed to eroded sediments during high-sediment periods.

## Key Findings

- **Total nitrogen removal:** Total nitrogen fell by 27% between W1 and W5 in summer 1980, reached 47% on some occasions, and averaged 15.4% over the entire study. The mean removal was about 1,000 kg/day, mostly as ammonia.
- **Nitrate net gain:** Despite overall nitrogen removal, the wash had a net contribution of 697 kg/day of nitrate, mainly from groundwater in the lower wash.
- **Phosphorus:** Total phosphorus loads were reduced by approximately one-third, and soluble phosphorus showed a slight decrease.
- **Pollution events:** Nitrate reached 12 mg/L on three occasions. A leaking pipe carrying industrial wastes to lined evaporation ponds on 4 March 1980 caused six- to seven-fold increases in nitrate loads to Lake Mead, so the authors describe mean removal values as conservative.
- **Ammonia trend:** Ammonia removal declined during the study, likely because STP ammonia loads were lower late in the study period, headcutting increased, and lower loads reduced removal efficiency. [[Ammonia Adsorption]] to clay particles was proposed as a mechanism, but eroded sediments may have less clay than historical headcut zones, a point needing further analysis. Comparisons with earlier Northshore Road data show ammonia reductions of about 90% under historical conditions versus much lower reductions in this study.
- **Seasonal trap:** Although retention is brief, the ecosystem behaves seasonally as a nitrogen and phosphorus trap, improving the quality of water discharged to Lake Mead.
- **Controlling factor:** Efficiency depends on nutrient loads and the degree of contact between water and wetland. Channelization limits [[Sheet Flow]] across the wetland, which reduces spatial and temporal contact and therefore nutrient uptake. A related [[Lake Tahoe Basin]] study found that removal was greatest at higher loads.
- **Declining efficiency:** Removal rates reported by URS and Clark County (1978) appear to be declining because of changes in flow regimes.
- **Management recommendation:** Improving nutrient removal through proper management of the wetland appears feasible. The authors recommend a dedicated [[Nutrient Removal]] management program, combined with flow distribution and erosion control, to maintain wetland wildlife habitat, as earlier proposed by Goldman and Deacon. Further studies are needed to identify specific removal mechanisms.
