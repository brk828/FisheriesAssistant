# Arnett Creek Native Fish Re-Establishment Project, Phase I: Barrier Design, Cattle Guard and Habitat Inventory

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This final report from the [[Tonto National Forest]] describes Phase I of the Arnett Creek Native Fish Re-Establishment Project, funded by Heritage Grant #193W7 (awarded August 1993). The project had three parts: designing a fish barrier to block upstream invasion by non-native fish from [[Queen Creek]], installing a cattle guard to limit livestock access, and inventorying fish habitat in the perennial reach to select native species for reintroduction. The selected barrier site (Site 1) is a confined canyon reach with bedrock and boulder anchoring, and the barrier was designed as a [[Gabion Structure]]. The habitat survey found a degraded but restorable channel, and [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Desert Sucker]] were identified as the strongest reintroduction candidates.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Arnett Creek lies south of the Town of Superior at the base of Picketpost Mountain, within the Globe Ranger District. It is a tributary of Queen Creek, which eventually joins the [[Gila River]]. Riparian vegetation is a rare cottonwood-willow community, described as [[Bonpland Willow-Goodding Willow Community]], a rare type within the [[Lower Sonoran Desert]].
- **Flow regime:** The creek is perennial and intermittent within the project area but ephemeral for most of its length, with a few permanent reaches on private land upstream.
- **Problem species:** [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Mosquitofish]] are known from Queen Creek. Both are aggressive non-native fish that negatively affect native Arizona fishes. The barrier also restricts downstream movement of fish from Arnett to Queen Creek.
- **Organization of report:** Part I covers site selection and barrier design; Part II covers the cattle guard; Part III covers the fish habitat inventory.
- **Collaborators:** Technical input came from Tonto National Forest staff, [[Arizona State University]] researchers, the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Streamflow has been measured since August 1991 about 2 miles upstream of the barrier site. The estimated 25-year flood is 5,194 cfs, and the barrier was designed to withstand it. Estimated flood magnitudes (cfs) by return interval were:

| Return interval (yr) | 2 | 5 | 10 | 25 | 50 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Magnitude (cfs) | 356 | 1,640 | 2,886 | 5,194 | 9,290 | 13,882 |

Mean annual rainfall is 14–22 inches, mainly from winter storms and summer thunderstorms. Steep, tilted bedrock and sparse upland ground cover produce flashy runoff.

**Cultural resources.** None are present in the construction area. Because the site lies within an active stream channel and materials will be moved by helicopter, no overland roads are needed and no cultural resources are expected to be affected.

**Riparian pasture and cattle guard.** Before the [[Arnett Creek Riparian Pasture]] was built, up to 314 livestock plus 174 yearlings had access to the creek for 150 days per year. Afterward, access is limited to 3–15 days, usually in winter, and does not exceed 30 days per year. A cattle guard was installed in summer 1994 on FS Road 2412.

**Habitat condition.** Banks were rated stable (average 3 on the [[Platts Habitat Rating System]]), and average embeddedness was 3.5. Undercut banks covered only 3.5% of bank length, which the authors considered surprisingly low given the riparian condition. Myers (1994) described the creek as healthy, with vigorous vegetation and a narrow, stable channel.

## Key Findings

### Barrier Site Selection
- Seven selection criteria were applied: floodprone width, anchoring substrate, gradient, height and stability of the adjacent terrace, amount of habitat protected from reinvasion, access, environmental effects of access, and funding.
- **Site 1 (selected):** Floodprone width about 30 ft, bedrock and boulder anchoring on both banks, and a terrace at least 7 ft above the channel. Access is by helicopter only. Preliminary cost was $32,428 against $50,000 available. The Bureau of Reclamation added $23,781.
- **Site 2 (rejected):** Floodprone width about 95 ft, making a costly, maintenance-heavy barrier.
- **Sites 3 and 4 (rejected):** Floodprone widths of about 100 and 150 ft, very low gradient, and low, unstable terraces that could not withstand floods. Site 3 needed a temporary trail, and Site 4 needed a temporary road. Both sites would have protected more habitat.
- Site 1 was preferred because structural reliability outweighed protecting extra habitat from reinvasion.

### Barrier Design
- A [[Gabion Structure]] was chosen over a [[Concrete Barrier]] because of cost, access limits and ecological impact. A concrete wall would have created an upstream impoundment and required a road.
- Design standards: withstand the 25-year flood; create a 3–5 ft vertical drop; be permeable, avoiding a permanent impoundment; have a 50–100 year lifetime; be angled downstream if feasible; include a downstream apron to prevent undercutting and reduce jump-over risk.
- A [[Two-Barrier Control Reach Design]] was considered but rejected for funding reasons.
- Specifications (gabion mesh of at least 0.120 in galvanized wire, rock at least 1.5 times the mesh size and up to 12 in) and contract documents are in Appendices A–C. The cost estimate was $72,710 and is kept confidential for the public bid process.

### Fish Habitat Inventory (Part III)
- Surveyed length: 21,732 ft, of which 10,338 ft (about 48%) was dry, mostly in the lower reach near Queen Creek.
- Habitat composition: riffles were the largest share of the wetted channel, followed by 39.7% runs and 19.6% pools.
- Median residual pool depth was 0.8 ft; median wetted width was 7 ft (9 ft in the pasture, about 4.5 ft downstream).
- Discharge was 0.58 cfs on March 11, 1994; summer 1995 flow was too low to measure.
- Width/depth ratios at bankfull for three cross sections were 14, 12 and 28.
- Instream cover was scarce. 83% of units had no small woody debris cover, and large woody debris was virtually absent. 62% of units had no terrestrial vegetation cover. Substrate, mostly boulders, was the main cover type. Algae provided the most aquatic cover.
- Backwater pools covered about 3% of the channel and side channels about 8%.

### Reintroduction Assessment
- Gila chub is the only known native fish record for the Arnett Creek drainage. Surveys by AGFD and the Forest Service have found only green sunfish and mosquitofish.
- Reintroduction requires an Environmental Assessment by the Forest Service and AGFD's 12-step reintroduction process. AGFD is best placed to determine stocking sources and methods.
- **[[Longfin Dace]]:** Recommended as an excellent candidate. It tolerates flash floods, uses sand, gravel and small cobble substrate, and favors riffles and shallow pools (about 60% of habitat).
- **[[Desert Sucker]]:** Recommended as a good candidate. Riffles and shallow pools could provide adequate, though not optimal, habitat. Deeper pools would improve it.
- **[[Sonora Sucker]]:** Not recommended for now because of the lack of deep, rocky pools and large cover.
- **[[Gila Chub]]:** Doubtful for now because the creek lacks undercut banks and deep, covered pools. Monitoring is recommended for 5–10 years.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]:** Not preferred. It is not highly resistant to downstream displacement in flood, and the creek lacks cover.

### Monitoring and Recommendations
- A later monitoring plan should track physical and biological habitat, fish populations and barrier integrity.
- Six permanent photopoints were established in the riparian pasture and will be moved into the exclosure if it is built.
