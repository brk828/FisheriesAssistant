# Interest Group Participation in the Upper Colorado River Recovery Program

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #ColoradoRiverBasin #ConservationPolicy

## Executive Summary
This document is primarily an administrative and policy-oriented report from the **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service** concerning the **[[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]]**. Rather than presenting new morphological or physiological fish science, it outlines the institutional framework, stakeholder engagement strategies, and hydrological management actions designed to recover populations of endangered fishes native to the Upper Colorado River Basin. Key scientific/management content includes river flow regulation (Flaming Gorge, Aspinall Unit), fish passage engineering (Redlands Diversion Dam), and policy governing nonnative fish stocking, all of which directly impact the ecological recovery of the region's endangered native fish species, notably the **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]** (historically termed "Colorado squawfish") and the **[[Razorback Sucker]]**.

## Background & Description
The source text does not provide direct morphological, meristic, or anatomical data on the fish species involved. It is a program-management document rather than a species-level biological study. However, it implicitly references two federally listed endangered fish taxa:
- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) — referred to in the text by its historical common name, "Colorado squawfish"; the largest native cyprinid (minnow) of the Colorado River system, historically a top predatory fish in the basin.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — a large-bodied catostomid characterized by a pronounced dorsal keel ("razorback"), adapted to riverine and floodplain habitats.

No length, weight, coloration, or fin-ray count data are provided in this source.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Basins Discussed:**
  - **[[Green River]]** (including the **[[Flaming Gorge Dam]]** tailwater reach and **[[Yampa River]]** confluence) — critical habitat requiring managed flow regimes mimicking natural hydrographs to support endangered fish spawning and rearing.
  - **[[Yampa River Basin]]** — one of the few remaining basins with a largely natural, unregulated flow regime; central to recovery efforts via instream flow protection (e.g., **[[Juniper Water Rights]]**, potential **[[Elkhead Reservoir]]** enlargement).
  - **[[Gunnison River Basin]]** — regulated by the **[[Aspinall Unit]]**, undergoing a 5-year (1992–1996) research program to evaluate dam operations' effects on endangered fish habitat.
  - **[[Redlands Diversion Dam]]** (lower Gunnison River) — site of fish passage barrier; a fish ladder is being constructed to restore access to historic habitat for the **[[Razorback Sucker]]** and **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]**, while also excluding nonnative fish species.

- **Threats to Native Fish Populations:**
  - **Altered flow regimes** from dam operations (Flaming Gorge, Aspinall Unit) disrupting natural hydrology needed for spawning cues and habitat maintenance.
  - **Nonnative warmwater fish stocking** in the upper basin, identified as a major threat requiring new interim regulatory procedures among Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming.
  - **Habitat fragmentation** due to diversion structures (e.g., Redlands Dam) blocking migratory access to historic riverine habitat.

- **Interacting Stakeholder/Management Framework:**
  - Federal and state agencies ([[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Western Area Power Administration]]) manage flow regimes to balance water use with endangered species conservation.
  - Nonnative fish stocking is governed by interim interstate procedures aimed at reducing competitive/predatory impacts on native fish assemblages.

## Key Findings
- A **Biological Opinion (1991)** on Flaming Gorge Dam operations established flow recommendations for the Green River at Jensen, Utah (July–October), with experimental test flows for remaining months; full revision expected by 1997 pending completion of technical studies (targeted 1996).
- Efforts are underway to **legally protect instream flows** below Flaming Gorge Dam down to the confluence with the **[[Duchesne River]]** (July–October flows), with additional protections (November–June) planned through **[[Canyonlands National Park]]** after 1997.
- In the **Yampa River Basin**, recovery strategy centers on acquiring **conditional Juniper water rights** (potentially controlling ~75% of river flow) and converting them to protected instream flows to sustain the basin's natural flow regime — critical for endangered fish recovery.
- The **Aspinall Unit** (Gunnison River) is subject to a 5-year interdisciplinary research plan (1992–1996) assessing dam operation effects on endangered fish habitat, culminating in a **Biological Opinion by 1997**.
- A **fish ladder** at the **Redlands Diversion Dam** is being designed to restore fish passage for the **Colorado Pikeminnow** and **Razorback Sucker**, while selectively excluding nonnative species — a key structural intervention for reconnecting fragmented habitat.
- **Interim Nonnative Fish Stocking Procedures** were finalized and adopted by Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming (completed by 4/94), representing a coordinated tri-state regulatory response to reduce nonnative fish impacts on native endangered species; these procedures are scheduled for evaluation, revision, and incorporation into state aquaculture permitting systems through 1996.
- The Recovery Program formally recognized the need for broader **stakeholder inclusion** (sportsman groups, agricultural interests, Indian tribes, aquaculture industry, local/federal governments) in recovery decision-making, reflecting the socio-political complexity of managing endangered fish recovery alongside water resource development in the Upper Colorado River Basin.
