# Importance of the 15-Mile Reach to Colorado River Populations of Endangered Colorado Pikeminnow and Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) position paper, a synthesis of current biological information dated March 2000 and authored by [[Douglas B. Osmundson]] of the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]], argues that the quality and quantity of habitat in the [[15-Mile Reach]] of the upper [[Colorado River]] must be protected and enhanced if populations of two endangered species, [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], are to be maintained and recovered. The reach runs from the Gunnison River confluence in Grand Junction to the Grand Valley Diversion Dam at Palisade, Colorado. The report concludes that the reach, and the [[Grand Valley]] generally, is critical habitat for adult pikeminnow and is central to razorback sucker recovery.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The 15-Mile Reach is roughly 15 miles (24 km) long and lies in the upper half of the Grand Valley, an alluvial valley of about 35 miles (56 km) from Palisade to Loma containing Colorado's largest urban area and one of its largest farming areas.
- **Water use:** Withdrawals since the turn of the century, plus transbasin diversions to the Front Range, have reduced flows. Future depletions are expected because Colorado intends to use its full apportionment under the 1922 Colorado River Compact and the 1948 Upper Colorado River Compact, agreements that predate the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- **Regulatory context:** A [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] programmatic biological opinion stipulates recovery actions to offset past and future depletions. Additional depletions are compliant only if these actions are implemented in a timely manner and a positive fish population response is detected.
- **Species:** [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] is the river's top piscivore. [[Razorback Sucker]] is nearly extirpated, with only rare captures in the late 1970s and early 1980s, and persisted in the reach until recent times.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow alteration:** Upstream diversion dams have significantly reduced summer flows since the turn of the century, and headwater dams have reduced spring runoff since mid-century.
- **Life history:** Pikeminnow adults migrate to spawn, larvae drift long distances downstream, and subadults and young adults disperse from nursery areas to adult habitat. Razorback suckers show long-range spawning migrations, and their larvae drift downstream from spawning sites.
- **Nursery habitat:** Young pikeminnow rear in backwaters below Moab, Utah, where silt and sand create bars and still-water habitats. They feed on zooplankton and chironomid larvae first, then on small prey fish.
- **Food and temperature trade-off:** Upstream reaches have higher periphyton and invertebrate standing stocks, supporting more forage fish, but main-channel temperatures decline upstream. Warmwater species therefore face a balance between food supply and preferred thermal regimes.
- **Floodplains:** Historically, bottomlands were inundated more often and for longer each spring. Floodplain habitats are critical to razorback sucker reproduction, and alluvial valleys were likely centers of distribution.
- **Diversion barriers:** The [[Price-Stubb Dam]] has blocked upstream fish movement since its construction in 1911. The 15-Mile Reach and the 3-mile reach just upstream currently form the upstream limit of the pikeminnow range in the mainstem.
- **Thermal thresholds:** Spawning begins when river temperatures exceed 18°C and lasts 3 to 8 weeks. Hamman (1981) reported no spawning in raceways below 20°C. Marsh (1985) found the highest hatching success and embryo survival at 20°C.
- **Broader ecology:** Core populations in large alluvial reaches are considered critical for metapopulation persistence, and floodplains function as centers of biophysical organization.

## Key Findings

1. **Adult habitat concentration:** The Grand Valley (33 miles) supports the greatest concentrations of adult Colorado pikeminnow. Prime adult habitat is restricted to the upper 60 miles of the 185-mile occupied reach above the Green River confluence.
2. **Nursery habitat:** The reach below Moab, Utah, contains the best nursery habitat in the Colorado River.
3. **Thermal balance:** The Grand Valley, including the 15-Mile Reach, may represent the best balance between food supply and preferred temperatures. Below Westwater Canyon, food limits adults. Above the Grand Valley, temperature limits them.
4. **Potential range extension:** Based on Yampa River comparisons (Nesler 1995), suitable temperatures may extend about 14 miles into canyon habitat upstream of the Grand Valley, toward De Beque Canyon, if passage is provided.
5. **Razorback sucker:** Floodplain habitats in the Grand Valley and De Beque-to-Rifle reach are critical; both should be focus areas for recovery. The species may historically have centered its distribution in the Grand Valley.
6. **Population status:** The subadult and adult Colorado pikeminnow population in the river is estimated at only about 600–800 individuals (Osmundson and Burnham 1998; Osmundson 1999b). Stronger year classes are needed to increase it.
7. **Recovery requirements:** Razorback sucker will require stocking before naturalized reproduction can sustain the population. Passage restoration and preservation of Grand Valley habitat are required for both species.
8. **Policy position:** The USFWS position is that prime adult habitat in the Grand Valley, including the 15-Mile Reach, must be preserved and enhanced, and that all historic upper-basin reaches must be restored for long-term viability.
