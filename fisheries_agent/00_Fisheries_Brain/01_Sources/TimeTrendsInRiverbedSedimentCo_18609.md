# Time Trends in Riverbed Sediment Composition in Salmon and Steelhead Spawning Areas: South Fork Salmon River, Idaho

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Platts and Megahan (1975, [[U.S. Forest Service]], Intermountain Forest and Range Experiment Station) report a 1966–1974 time series of riverbed surface sediment in four major [[Chinook Salmon]] spawning areas of the [[South Fork Salmon River]] (SFSR), Idaho. Accelerated erosion from [[Logging and Road Construction]] on steep, erodible lands of the [[Idaho Batholith]] had buried spawning gravel in fine sand. After a [[Forest Service Moratorium]] on logging and road construction in the upper watershed, and a [[Watershed Rehabilitation Program]] begun in 1965, the river began flushing fines. The percentage of fines in the spawning areas fell from an estimated 55 percent in 1966 to 21 percent in 1974. By the end of the study, Krassel (12 percent) and Glory (13 percent) were within the optimum range of 10 to 20 percent fines, while Poverty (26 percent) and Stolle (23 percent) slightly exceeded it.

## Background & Description

### Study Watershed
- **Drainage:** The SFSR is a major tributary of the Salmon River and drains a 1,270-square-mile watershed within the 16,000-square-mile Idaho Batholith.
- **Elevation:** 9,000 feet at the headwaters to 2,100 feet at the mouth.
- **Terrain:** Steep, with most hillslope gradients between 40 and 70 percent.
- **Channel:** About 100 miles long, averaging 75 feet wide and 2 feet deep. Roughly two-thirds riffles and one-third pools (Platts 1970).
- **Water chemistry:** Low mineral content, averaging about 60 ppm total dissolved solids, due to granitic bedrock.
- **Soils:** Highly erodible; the eroded material is mostly coarse sand, classified as fines in this report.

### Land Use History
- Before 1950, logging and road construction were largely confined to gentle, low-erosion-hazard slopes.
- By 1965, 15 percent of the watershed above (south of) the Secesh River confluence was within logging sale boundaries, and 622 miles of roads had been built.
- 78 percent of logging and 69 percent of road construction were on fluvial lands, the least stable lands in the drainage.
- Severe storms in 1962, 1964, and 1965 greatly accelerated erosion on newly disturbed lands, especially logging roads.
- Arnold and Lundeen (1968) estimated that accelerated erosion had increased river sediment loads by 350 percent by 1967 relative to pre-1950 conditions.

### The Fishery
- The SFSR historically supported Idaho's largest salmon run, composed entirely of summer chinook salmon. Before the mid-1960s, the river produced about 30 percent of Idaho's chinook harvest and accounted for 20 percent of chinook redds counted in Idaho.
- Steelhead trout are anadromous; chinook spawn mainly in the main stem, while steelhead spawn in the main stem and tributaries.
- Rainbow trout, cutthroat trout, Dolly Varden, and mountain whitefish also occur in the river, with light fishing pressure.
- Heavy fishing pressure on chinook and steelhead has occurred in open-season years since 1940.

### Sampling Procedures
- Cross sections were placed in the four spawning areas (Glory, Krassel, Poverty, Stolle) to measure riverbed surface materials, using a method similar to [[Herrington and Dunham Method]] with increased sample sizes.
- Sections were spaced 50 feet apart at Krassel and Glory, and 300 feet apart at Poverty and Stolle.
- Ten cross sections were used at Krassel, Glory, and Poverty; 20 at Stolle.
- Composition was assessed visually, bank to bank at the waterline, by assigning each 1-foot tape division to one of four size classes (see Table 2 below).
- Sections were read annually in late summer at low flow, except 1969 (no data) and 1966 (Krassel and Glory only).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Sediment Effects on Spawning
- Channel bottom materials should contain no more than 10 to 15 percent fines for successful spawning (McNeil 1964).
- Excess fines blanket or infiltrate spawning gravel and cause embryo, alevin, and fry mortality by:
  - decreasing gravel permeability and cutting off oxygen,
  - concentrating metabolic wastes to toxic levels,
  - blocking emergence from the gravel to surface water.
- Reduced permeability from fines has been documented by Cooper (1959), Cordon and Kelly (1961), Phillips and Campbell (1962), Peters (1962), and Vasil'en (1964).

### Sediment Transport Principles
- Deposition occurs when sediment supply exceeds the transport capacity of river energy.
- Material is flushed out when transport energy exceeds supply.
- Pre-1965, supply exceeded transport because erosion expanded across the watershed. After 1965, supply dropped and the river began to cleanse itself.

### Fish Population Decline
- Returns of adult summer chinook and steelhead have declined steadily over 17 years (U.S. Army Corps of Engineers 1965–1974).
- Runs leveled off from 1969 to 1973, but 1974 returns were the lowest on record.
- Most of the decline is attributed to stress on migrants passing downstream hydroelectric structures and impoundments in the Snake and Columbia Rivers. Upriver environmental degradation also contributed (Mallet 1974).

## Key Findings

1. **Sediment loading was severe before 1965.** Accelerated erosion from logging and roads, mostly coarse sand, buried chinook and steelhead redds. Fines in 1965 were very high, and sediment loads were estimated at 350 percent above pre-1950 levels by 1967.

2. **Fines declined over time.** The fitted hyperbolic regression ($y = a + b/t$, with $t$ = years since 1965) estimated fines at 55 percent of surface materials in 1966 and 21 percent in 1974. The gain was mainly in gravel, with smaller gains in rubble.

3. **Trends were statistically supported.** The regression coefficients *a* and *b* were significant at the 95 percent level in all cases. Explained variance (r²) was low, reflecting wide error bands. For the combined spawning areas, reported r² values include Gravel 0.09, Rubble 0.02, and Boulder 0.11.

4. **All four spawning areas improved.** Each showed declining fines and rising gravel and rubble. The curves had similar shapes but different elevations. Poverty and Stolle had significantly higher *a* coefficients than Krassel and Glory.

5. **Spatial differences were linked to sediment supply and transport energy.** The higher curves at Poverty and Stolle likely reflect greater initial sediment supply, including erosion from a burned area near Poverty, and less stream power. Stream power at bankfull flow, ranked ascending, was Poverty, Stolle, Glory, Krassel.

6. **Present conditions:**
   - Krassel: 12 percent fines
   - Glory: 13 percent fines (both within the optimum 10–20 percent range)
   - Stolle: 23 percent fines
   - Poverty: 26 percent fines (both slightly above optimum)

7. **Fines in 1966 ranged from 45 to over 80 percent** across the four individual spawning areas.

8. **Logging restriction was the dominant factor in recovery.** The [[Forest Service Moratorium]] eliminated new sources of accelerated surface and landslide erosion. Surface erosion on logging roads dropped rapidly after disturbance (Megahan and Kidd 1972), and the watershed rehabilitation program (initiated 1965 or 1967 per different passages) and favorable climate also helped.

9. **High flows flushed sediment.** Considerable flushing occurred even in low-flow years 1966–1970, with further flushing during high flows in 1971, 1972, and 1974.

10. **Recovery is possible but conditional.** Streams like the SFSR can recover if sediment inputs from watershed erosion fall below the stream's capacity to flush fines.

11. **Management recommendation.** Land use in sensitive areas must be planned across both time and space, using programmed land uses to prevent sediment supply from exceeding transport capacity. Otherwise, the aquatic degradation seen in the SFSR is likely to recur.

**Limitations noted in the source:** The data show considerable variation among cross sections in any year. The text also reports 1974 water yield as missing and includes some OCR-garbled regression equations, so only legible coefficients are cited here.
