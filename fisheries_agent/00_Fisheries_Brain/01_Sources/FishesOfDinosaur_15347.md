# Fishes of Dinosaur National Monument

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ichthyofauna

## Executive Summary

This museum publication, prepared by [[Robert Rush Miller]] (Curator of Fishes, Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan) with contributions credited to Paul C. Marsh, surveys the fishes of [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Water for fish is supplied mainly by the [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]], which are part of the [[Colorado River]] system. The author emphasizes that the native fauna is specialized for an extreme, silty, fast-flowing and temperature-variable environment, and that these native species are a unique part of American wildlife heritage under pressure from development. Roughly half of the known species are introduced, and the document describes each species, marking non-native ones.

## Background & Description

The Monument's permanent water is largely confined to the Green and Yampa rivers. Most tributaries flow only after heavy rain. [[Jones Creek]], which enters the Green from the north between Echo Park and Island Park, is the notable exception with permanent flow.

The rivers cut spectacular canyons, with cliffs rising up to about 3,000 feet above the stream bed. The Green drops 650 feet over its 60-mile course through the Monument, an average of nearly 11 feet per mile, and reaches 21 feet per mile through steeper sections such as Split Mountain. Historically, water ran at speeds up to nearly 20 miles per hour and flash floods raised the river more than 30 feet within short periods. The rivers are silty, with wide flow variation and considerable temperature change.

The fauna comprises about half introduced species (baitfishes, game and food fishes) and only eight native kinds. Of the natives, three were eaten by Indigenous peoples and the [[Colorado Squawfish]] is now a sport fish. The document notes that the [[Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Mountain Whitefish]] of the Green drainage never reached the Monument's waters, probably because of the silt load.

The species accounts are grouped by family:

- **Salmonidae** (trouts, whitefishes, charrs): [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]], both introduced.
- **Cyprinidae** (minnows): about 2,000 species worldwide, freshwater only. Accounts cover the [[Carp]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Redside Shiner]], [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Fathead Minnow]].
- **Catostomidae** (suckers): about 80 species, nearly all in North America. Accounts cover the [[Flannel Mouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Humpback Sucker]].
- **Ictaluridae** (catfishes): introduced to western North America before 1900. Accounts cover the [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Black Bullhead]].
- **Cottidae** (sculpins): about 25 US species of *Cottus*. The account covers the [[Colorado Mottled Sculpin]].

Six further introduced species were recorded but are scarce or absent: cutthroat trout (planted in Jones Creek, last reported 1956), creek chub, white sucker, walleye, smallmouth bass (accidentally planted at the head of Lodore Canyon, July 1963) and green sunfish (Island Park, September 1962).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Green River was altered by [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], located about 45 miles above the Monument and closed in November 1962. Below the dam the river now carries a fraction of its former flow, is consistently cold enough for introduced trout, and no longer shows its earlier dramatic rises and falls. The effects on fish life had not been determined at the time of writing.

Several species show adaptations to the swift, turbulent gorge sections. The [[Humpback Chub]] has a hump that the author believes deflects water toward the bottom, along with reduced scales and large sickle-shaped fins, and it withstands flows of up to 18,000 cubic feet per second. The [[Bonytail]] and [[Flannel Mouth Sucker]] have streamlined bodies and expansive fins suited to fast current. Suckers have scraping lips and chisel-like jaw edges for feeding on algae.

Temperature preferences shape distribution. The [[Rainbow Trout]] prefers 55° to 65° F and tolerates water above 70° F. The [[Brown Trout]] tolerates warmer, less clear water and so has been more common in the big rivers. The author suggests that if the Green becomes consistently cold, the rainbow may replace the brown. The [[Colorado Mottled Sculpin]] prefers cool mountain streams with summer temperatures of 55° to 65° F.

Human pressures named in the document include dam building, water diversion, introduction of competing game fishes, and changes to river character. The [[Colorado Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]] are declining, and the [[Colorado Squawfish]] is described as increasingly scarce.

## Key Findings

- **Native diversity is low.** Only eight native fish species occur in the Monument, and about half of the known fauna is introduced.
- **Native species are specialized.** Their adaptations (streamlined forms, reduced scales, hump, scraping mouths) match the extreme silt, flow and temperature conditions.
- **Colorado Squawfish.** The largest North American minnow and native only to the Colorado River system. Historical weights reached about 80 lb and lengths of 5 ft or more. Recent catches were mostly 4 to 15 lb, and one taken below Flaming Gorge Dam in September 1962 weighed 13 lb and was nearly 3 ft long.
- **Humpback Chub.** Known only from the Colorado River and its major swift tributaries. Adults reach about 16 inches. It was first discovered in Grand Canyon National Park in 1942.
- **Humpback Sucker (razorback sucker).** Reaches about 3 ft and 10 to 14 lb, and was taken commercially near Phoenix, Arizona, as recently as 1949.
- **Fathead Minnow.** The male guards the nest and strokes eggs with a dark spongy pad to keep them free of silt. Up to 12,000 eggs may be laid per nest; they hatch in 4 to 6 days, with spawning at about 64° F.
- **Brown Trout.** First stocked in the United States in 1883. It grows larger (4 to 7 lb) than the Rainbow Trout (1 to 4 lb).
- **Carp.** Introduced from Asia. The largest recorded Iowa specimen weighed nearly 60 lb. Carp are regarded in the West as a nuisance because they muddy water and compete with game fishes.
- **Flow regime change.** Flaming Gorge Dam has reduced the Green's flow, stabilized its temperature and removed its flood pulses. The biological consequences were still unknown.
- **Conservation priority.** The author argues that the native species deserve conservation as unique components of American wildlife heritage.
