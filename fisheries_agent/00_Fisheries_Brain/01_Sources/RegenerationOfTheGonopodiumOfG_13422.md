# Regeneration of the Gonopodium of Gambusia During Morphogenesis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1941 experimental study by [[C. L. Turner]] (Department of Zoology, [[Northwestern University]]) examines how the anal fin of [[Gambusia]] develops, regenerates, and is reshaped into a [[Gonopodium]] under the influence of genetic factors and a testicular [[Androgenic Hormone]]. Over 100 successful operations removed or cut back single rays and groups of rays at known developmental stages, and the regenerated fins were tracked to maturity. The results support a model in which genetics set the fin's basic pattern, testicular hormone concentration drives the sequence of [[Differentiation Areas]], and growing differentiation progressively limits the fin's capacity to regenerate.

## Background & Description

The gonopodium is an [[Anal Fin]] that is nearly identical in juvenile males and females. At sexual maturity, the male fin undergoes [[Metamorphosis]] into a copulatory organ, while the female fin keeps a typical fin structure. The author notes that the study builds on earlier work on fin morphogenesis (Turner '41a) and on castration and hormone experiments by [[St. Amant]] ('41), [[Grobstein]] ('40), [[Eversole]] ('39, '41), and [[Regnier]] ('38).

Key structural features described in the text:

- **Rays:** Female fins have ten or eleven rays that lengthen over life. Some rays bifurcate secondarily and tertiarily, and the basal joints solidify by [[Anchylosis]].
- **Male development:** Basal joints stay unsolidified, and bifurcation is limited to the primary division in all rays except rays 1, 2, 3 and 10. Rays 3, 4 and 5 (the "3-4-5 ray complex") elongate rapidly.
- **Differentiation areas:** Six areas (I to VI) carry spines, plates, serrae and hooks. Area I thickens ray 3, and area VI thickens and fuses branches of rays 5 to 8. Areas II, V, IV and III develop in that sequence.
- **Index ray:** The undivided third ray serves as the staging index. Stage is expressed as its segment count (for example, the "16-segment stage").

The author identifies three groups of factors shaping the fin: genetic factors, hormonal stimulation, and interactions among rays and areas within the developing fin field.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The text is a laboratory study and does not describe natural habitat, field populations, or water conditions. The one environmental variable reported is temperature. At approximately 23°C, a normal male completes gonopodial differentiation in 30 to 50 days, moving from the 15-segment stage to the 42-segment stage, where development is complete. The author states that the rate of development depends on the temperature at which the animal is kept.

Hormonal context comes from laboratory manipulations. Females treated with androgens (ethinyl testosterone) develop atypical gonopodia that resemble male structures, indicating that females carry the genetic potential for the gonopodium but need androgen to express it. The study also cites similar work in other poeciliid species, including [[Platypoecilus Maculatus]], [[Lebistes Reticulatus]] and [[Xiphophorus Helleri]].

## Key Findings

**1. Hormone dose controls the sequence of metamorphosis.**
- Complete castration of juvenile males prevents fin metamorphosis.
- Regeneration of a testicular mass about one-forty-fifth the size of a mature testis is enough for complete gonopodial development.
- Very small amounts of regenerated testis trigger only the first stages (elongation of the 3-4-5 complex and areas I and VI). Larger amounts are needed for each later area.
- Females given increasing concentrations of ethinyl testosterone show that each area requires a definite minimum concentration.

**2. Regeneration depends on developmental stage.**
- Before metamorphosis, excised rays regenerate completely, and the mature fin architecture is normal.
- Extracting a ray removes it permanently because no basal stub remains for a [[Blastema]] to form on.
- During early metamorphosis, ray 3 cut back to seven segments at the 21-segment stage regenerates rapidly. Its growth outpaces thickening, so the regenerated ray is thin at first. By maturity, area II has formed normally and ray 3 has thickened.
- Excising ray 3 at the 32-segment stage yields little or no regeneration, and the same is true of ray 4. Regeneration is restored when all other rays are removed at the same time, indicating that the intact, developing fin suppresses it.
- Once the gonopodium is fully differentiated, excising rays 3, 4 or 5 produces no regeneration at all. Rays 7, 8 and 9 regenerate considerably, and ray 6 slightly.

**3. Dominance within the fin field.**
- Removing ray 3 allows ray 2 to grow longer than normal, showing that the rapid growth of ray 3 normally subordinates ray 2.
- Removing ray 5 at the 15-segment stage causes ray 6 to take its place in the dominant complex. Ray 6 grows to the length of ray 5 and forms a terminal hook under the influence of area IV. This substitution happens whenever ray 5 is destroyed or delayed.
- Areas III, IV and V are not dependent on ray 3, and area II is not dependent on the presence of ray 4, but removing ray 4 prevents the terminal hook on ray 5 from forming in the normal way.

**4. Bifurcation depends on ray length and growth rate.**
- Rays 1, 2, 3 and 10 stay undivided. Rays 4 to 9 are bifurcated.
- Rays 6 to 9 do not divide a second time in the normal male because they fail to reach the length needed for division.
- High growth rate during late regeneration delays or prevents bifurcation. The author proposes that bifurcation occurs only when the high metabolic rate at the ray's apex falls.

**5. Anchylosis is linked to slow growth.**
- Anchylosis starts soon after birth. It affects all basal segments in females but only one or two basal segments per ray in males.
- The author suggests a low growth rate accompanies anchylosis and that its scarcity in the rapidly growing male fin may be due to this.

**6. Differentiation areas are positionally fixed but hormone-dependent.**
- Area positions are constant and resist experimental change, suggesting genetic control.
- The order of area origin can be interrupted by castration and then restored by androgen treatment, showing that hormone concentration sets the sequence.
- Excessive hormone produces abnormal structures in areas, so both a minimum concentration and a continued slight increase are needed for normal development.

**7. Duplicate structures can form.**
- Removing the middle of ray 3 at the 28-segment stage, while leaving the basal and terminal parts intact, led a new ray to regenerate from the basal stub. The terminal portion continued to develop normally, and both portions produced normal duplicate structures.
- The author concludes that specific structures arise in a ray at an appropriate stage, and that an existing differentiated character does not block a second normal character if a new ray arises from the same source.

**8. Combined operations interact.**
- Extraction and excision effects are cumulative only when the operated rays are adjacent.
- Excising all or nearly all rays and then extracting ray 5 leads to longer, more segmented short rays, a result seen at both early and late stages (for example, figures 7, 9, 15 and 16).

**Overall conclusion:** The author concludes that the position of differentiation areas is genetically determined, that hormone from the testis controls the sequence in which areas arise, and that genetic factors specify the structures within each area, which need an increasing hormone concentration to develop normally. The study ends with a call for further work on how these factors combine in the molding of the fin.
