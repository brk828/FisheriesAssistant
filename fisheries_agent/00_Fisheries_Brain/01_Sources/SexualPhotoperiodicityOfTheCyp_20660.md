# Photoperiod Control of the Sexual Cycle in the Bridled Shiner, *Notropis bifrenatus*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 experimental study by Robert Whiting Harrington, Jr. tests how daylength controls the annual reproductive cycle of the [[Bridled Shiner]] (*Notropis bifrenatus*), a cyprinid minnow. Wild-caught adults were held at about 18.9 °C under either natural autumn daylight (short photoperiod) or 17 hours of light per day (long photoperiod) from September 26 to January 4. Long days induced spawning on January 1, roughly five months ahead of the natural June spawning season. Short-day fish showed no sexual behavior and only limited ovarian growth. The author defines a **prespawning period** of about 44–46 days, identifies a refractory (unresponsive) period from mid-July to mid-November, and compares these results with data for the [[Banded Sunfish]] (*Enneacanthus obesus*).

## Background & Description

*Notropis bifrenatus* is a small cyprinid with a black lateral band, which gives the species its common and specific names. Males develop a brassy-yellow wash along the flanks and coffee-colored pectoral fins during the breeding season. The author notes that this nuptial coloration is a reliable field indicator but developed only faintly in the captive fish, possibly because of a carotenoid deficiency in the aquarium diet.

The study builds on earlier work by the same author on this species (reproductive behavior, life cycle, food, and preseasonal breeding) and on comparisons with the [[European Minnow]] (*Phoxinus phoxinus*), the [[Bitterling]] (*Rhodeus amarus*), and the [[Three-Spined Stickleback]] (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*). Earlier experiments on *N. bifrenatus* had started the long-day treatment near the winter solstice. This experiment began it near the autumnal equinox.

The paper also introduces provisional terminology for the fish annual cycle, including the prespawning, spawning, responsive, refractory, interspawning, and vegetative periods, and discusses how these relate to mammalian and avian reproductive terms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and origin of fish:** Adults were collected September 24–26 from Wash Brook, a tributary of the [[Park River]] in Bloomfield, Connecticut. Experiments were conducted at [[Trinity College]], Hartford, Connecticut (latitude 41° N).

**Experimental conditions:**
- Water temperature: 18.9 ± 1.1 °C, thermostatically controlled.
- Short photoperiod: natural autumn daylight, declining from about 12.0 to 9.0 hours per day.
- Long photoperiod: 17 hours per day (daylight plus artificial illumination).
- Diet: commercial dried aquarium food, with algal growth encouraged for small organisms in some tanks.

**Natural cycle context:** In the wild, the first breeding color was seen on April 22 (daylength about 13.3 hours), breeding behavior on May 22, and first spawning on June 7. The natural prespawning period was 46 days.

**Comparative species:** The [[Banded Sunfish]] (*Enneacanthus obesus*), a centrarchid, was used as a comparison. In the earlier experiment on this species, a long photoperiod imposed October 4 led to first spawning 45 days later, on November 17.

## Key Findings

**Sample and mortality**
- 178 adult Bridled Shiners were collected; morbidity was 2.8 %.
- 173 effective subjects: 94 females, 76 males, and 3 additional males (text only).
- Two males hatched from eggs fertilized during the experiment were also reared.

**Spawning and behavior under long days**
- Long-day fish showed the first intimations of sexual behavior on November 17, 52 days after the long photoperiod began.
- Nuptial behavior (pursuit, nudging, prodding) occurred sporadically afterward, without clear intensification.
- Male breeding color was first recognized November 24.
- Seven spawning acts were observed on January 1 starting at 1400 hours EST. Fertile eggs were obtained each time.
- Larvae found on January 10 indicated further spawning after adults were removed on January 4.
- Short-day fish showed no sexual behavior throughout the experiment.
- Long-day spawning began 45 days after the first sexual behavior and about 97 days after the photoperiod was imposed.

**Ovarian response (94 females)**
- Egg diameter distributions were recorded from the largest 50 eggs per ovary and compared across dates and treatments.
- Short-day fish showed a small, steady increase in minimum, mean, and modal egg diameters. Maximum diameters stopped increasing after October 26, at about 336 µm (16 micrometer divisions; 1 division = 21 µm). This is the **critical maximum diameter**.
- Long-day fish showed a skewed distribution on October 26 (skewness g = −0.497; t = 4.78), with the same maximum as the short-day fish but a mode already reached by short-day fish only on January 4.
- By December 23, long-day fish showed significant increases in maximum, mean, and modal egg diameter. They were in the "penultimate" maturation stage three days before spawning began.
- Two mechanisms were identified: an earlier one that grows eggs to the critical diameter without needing long days (but which long days accelerate), and a later one that depends on long days and remains refractory until about mid-November.

**Testicular response (76 males)**
- Testicular changes in long-day males were absent until after October 26.
- The first sign of spermatogenesis appeared in one male killed November 16 (patches of spermatozoa, spermatids, and secondary spermatocytes).
- Short-day males showed no consistent change between September and January. Their testes stayed inactive, dominated by primary germ cells and spermatogonia.
- By January 4, long-day males had testes containing all spermatogenic stages, from primary germ cells to spermatozoa.
- Ovarian and testicular maturation appeared to be synchronized, though minor differences were noted.

**Natural and experimental fish in June**
- Five fish kept under natural daylight from September 26 reached functional maturity by May 31.
- The two males spawned in the aquarium on January 1 were fully mature by June 17. They measured 32 mm standard length and had the brightest nuptial coloration of any male in the study.

**Annual cycle model**
- Responsive period: mid-November to mid-July (at least eight months), when long days can initiate the prespawning period.
- Refractory (postspawning) period: mid-July to mid-November (four months or less).
- The **prespawning period** is the interval from the onset of supraliminal (effective) photoperiods to the first spawning. It lasted 44–46 days in both [[Notropis bifrenatus]] and [[Enneacanthus obesus]]. The author proposes that this period is the phase of the annual cycle "set right each year by the celestial clock."
- In *N. bifrenatus*, the prespawning mechanism controls spermatogenesis, which stalls at early stages outside the normal breeding season. In *E. obesus*, spermatogenesis finishes independently of photoperiod, and the prespawning mechanism governs nuptial attire, reproductive behavior, and spermiation.

**Conclusions**
- A long daily photoperiod imposed during the responsive period promptly starts the prespawning period.
- Before mid-November, a long photoperiod does not activate the prespawning mechanism or affect spermatogenesis, but it does accelerate egg growth up to the critical diameter.
- The refractory period appears to set the end of the spawning season. The author suggests the refractoriness may lie in the gonads rather than the pituitary, unlike in birds.
- Enforced darkness did not prevent ultimate gonadal maturation in the [[European Minnow]] (Bullough, 1941). This suggests an internal sexual rhythm in fishes that can operate without external cues.
