# Fossil Fish and Diatomite of the Lompoc Quarries, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fossil-fish

## Executive Summary

This travelogue-style feature describes a family field trip to the [[Grefco Diatomite Quarries]] near [[Lompoc]], California, where Miocene-Pliocene marine diatomite preserves abundant fossil fishes. The article reports that roughly 42 fossil fish species have been found in the Lompoc area, with the [[Fossil Herring]] (*Xyne grex*) the most common. It explains how the diatomite formed in a deep, restricted marine basin, why its purity is unusual, and how amateurs can split and preserve specimens. The piece also includes advertising and classified material for gem, mineral and fossil suppliers, which is not scientifically relevant.

## Background & Description

The fossil-bearing sediment is [[Diatomite]], a rock made of the silica tests of marine [[Diatoms]]. Deposits of this kind were first quarried in the 1880s as building stone; today they are processed for filtration, filler, abrasive and insulation uses. Diatomite is reported to be as little as 10% solid matter and up to 90% open space, and one cubic inch may contain more than 60 million individual diatoms.

Fossils in the quarries include:
- **Fish:** about 42 species reported from the Lompoc area, ranging from about 1 inch to a couple of feet long. Taxa named include the fossil herring (*Xyne grex*), other herrings, tarpons, half beaks, codfishes, opahs and groupers. Specimens are often preserved as mahogany-brown carbonaceous bones against white matrix.
- **Marine mammals:** occasional whale remains. In April 1988 a loader operator found a sperm whale skull, reported as 90% intact, and a baleen whale skull; both were donated to the [[Natural History Museum of Los Angeles County]].
- **Birds and other taxa:** cormorants, boobies and shearwaters from the Calabasas Miocene deposits; lanternfish, shad and pipefish, a ray impression and turtles from the Santa Monica Mountains and Palos Verdes hills.

The article describes an 8-inch *Xyne grex* specimen found during the visit as the first of several fish found that day.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Monterey Formation]] (middle-to-upper Miocene) and overlying [[Sisquoc Formation]] (upper Miocene/lower Pliocene) span roughly 12 to 2 million years ago. During the middle Miocene, deep, elongated near-shore basins similar to today's [[Gulf of California]] developed along coastal California. The Lompoc area is postulated to have been a narrow-mouthed bay about 1,400 feet deep, north of the Sierra Santa Ynez. Sediment accumulated at roughly 1 millimeter per year. Diatom abundance reflects high organic productivity near the surface, and fine diatom "snow" settled over fish carcasses. Mass death assemblages, where fish are concentrated in certain layers, are interpreted from the abundance of specimens.

Diatomite near Lompoc is among the purest in the world because the shoreline was far enough away that little terrigenous sediment reached the basin. Elsewhere, the Monterey Formation is often mixed with clay and volcanic ash. Volcanism during the Miocene, linked to the origin of the [[San Andreas Fault]] system, produced abundant ash layers. Tectonic uplift, including the "Coast Range orogeny," elevated these basins.

The quarries run reclamation planting with conifers, coast live oaks, erosion-control grasses and wildflowers. Operators also suppress dust with water.

## Key Findings

- Approximately 42 fossil fish species have been recorded from the Lompoc diatomite; the fossil herring (*Xyne grex*) is the most common.
- Fossil fish preservation is described as "spectacular," with fine fin and scale detail, comparable in quality to the [[Green River Formation]] of Wyoming.
- Modern relatives of the Lompoc fishes inhabit warm-temperature coastal waters, such as the Atlantic coast of North America, out to about 0.5 mile offshore and 150-300 feet deep.
- The diatomite formed in a deep, restricted basin with high productivity, and the purity reflects distance from shore.
- Deposition spanned about 12 to 2 million years ago, covering the late Monterey and Sisquoc formations.
- Whale skulls (sperm and baleen) were recovered in April 1988 and donated to a museum.
- Collecting guidance: split sheets along bedding planes, wrap specimens promptly in paper towels and newspaper because they flake when dry, and consolidate with a dilute white glue (one part glue to three parts water), Gliptol or clear lacquer on fully dry material.
- The quarries are active commercial operations; visits are a courtesy, must be arranged in advance, and property must be respected.
