# Lower Virgin River Project: Preliminary Findings Report (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation memorandum transmits the Preliminary Findings Report (PFR) on a proposed dual-purpose [[Lower Virgin River Project]], prepared jointly with the [[Las Vegas Valley Water District]] (LVVWD). The project would divert saline water from the lower [[Virgin River]] in Nevada, desalt it, and supply municipal and industrial (M&I) water to the Las Vegas Valley, while removing salt that would otherwise flow into the [[Colorado River]]. The report is a screening-level assessment of whether the project appears viable and merits feasibility-level study; it does not select a final plan. A 30-day public review followed distribution of the draft, with comments requested by April 30, 1993.

The preferred preliminary plan (Alternative 2, Riverside Diversion) targets an average annual diversion of 70,000 acre-feet, yields about 63,000 acre-feet per year of desalted water at 500 mg/L TDS, and removes about 210,000 tons of salt annually. Estimated costs are $709 per acre-foot for water and $299 per ton of salt removed under the Separable Cost-Remaining Benefits method. Key unresolved issues include upstream depletions, State Engineer water right allocations, endangered fish impacts, and the institutional framework of the Colorado River Compact.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The PFR assesses technical, environmental, and economic feasibility at a preliminary level. It explicitly does not present all alternatives considered, and the plan shown is not guaranteed to be the one built.

**Project setting.** The project would extend roughly 75 miles from the Lake Mead area southwest to Las Vegas, with the Virgin River drainage of about 5,980 square miles upstream of [[Halfway Wash]]. Mesquite and Bunkerville are the main river towns. Irrigation companies divert water to irrigate about 2,400 acres near the flood plain.

**Project features (preferred plan).** The plan includes:
- [[Riverside Diversion Dam]]: a 400-foot concrete spillway, radial-gated sluiceway, and 900-foot earth dike (about 30 feet high).
- [[Riverside Pumping Plant]]: 390-foot dynamic head, 3.1-mile, 135-inch steel discharge pipeline.
- [[Halfway Wash Dam and Reservoir]]: zoned earthfill embankment about 1 mile long, operating capacity about 113,000 acre-feet.
- [[Desalting Plant]] at Dry Lake (sized for 80,000 acre-feet annual feedwater), using lime softening, membrane processes, electrodialysis, and reverse osmosis.
- [[Evaporation Ponds]]: 53 individual ponds of 20 acres each for brine disposal.
- Feedwater and product pipelines (about 34 miles and 48 miles), a 4-million-gallon product storage tank, and 230-kV power facilities.

**Alternatives.** Four alternatives were examined: (1) diversion near Mesquite with a 24-mile concrete canal; (2) downstream diversion with pumping to an offstream reservoir (preferred); (3) subsurface diversion with pumping; and (4) underground infiltration galleries with groundwater recharge in Dry Lake Valley before desalting.

**Institutional context.** The project would be a component of the [[Colorado Water Project]] (CWP), and LVVWD has applied for surface and groundwater rights across eastern and southern Nevada.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and hydrology.** The lower Virgin River lies in the eastern [[Mojave Desert]]. Annual precipitation averages 3 to 6 inches, while potential evaporation averages about 80 inches. Flows vary with snowpack and sporadic flash floods. Between 1930 and 1991 the average annual discharge at Littlefield, Arizona was about 120,000 acre-feet per year. Summer flows at the Nevada-Arizona border are roughly 70 cubic feet per second, sourced mainly from Littlefield springs and Beaver Dam Wash.

**Water quality.** Total dissolved solids (TDS) in the river are driven by natural saline springs (Littlefield Springs above 2,500 mg/L; Riverside Springs near 10,000 mg/L), phreatophyte consumption, irrigation return flows, and sediment-laden flood flows. One ton of salt per acre-foot equals about 735 mg/L TDS.

**Aquatic habitat.** The river is wide, shallow, and meandering over a sand bed, with high turbidity and flashy flows. Downstream of Mesquite it runs intermittently and is often dry below the Riverside Bridge. Five native and nine introduced fish species are reported. Of the native species, [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) and [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*) are federally listed as endangered. The introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) has become dominant downstream of Mesquite and is considered a major factor in native fish decline, with populations often exceeding 90 percent of the fauna in low-velocity reaches.

**Riparian and wetland habitat.** Riparian corridors support a high proportion of regional wildlife: 69 bird species, 22 mammals, 18 reptiles, and 4 amphibians are recorded in riparian and wash communities. The Overton Wildlife Management Area lies just downstream of the proposed diversion. Exotic [[Salt Cedar]] (*Tamarix chinensis*) is now dominant in the lower river corridor.

**Terrestrial and special status species.** Creosote bush scrub covers the uplands. [[Desert Tortoise]] (*Gopherus agassizii*), listed as threatened by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and the State of Nevada, occurs mainly on the pipeline route and near Halfway Wash. Several special status plants and cacti are present, including some Federal Category 2 candidates.

**Principal environmental concerns:**
- Effects on riparian and wetland habitat at the dam and pipeline sites.
- Loss of woundfin and Virgin River chub habitat, eggs, larvae, and young fish at the diversion inlet.
- Loss of desert tortoise habitat from pipelines and ponds.
- Attraction of waterfowl to evaporation ponds.
- Reductions in nutrients and sediment entering the Overton Arm of [[Lake Mead]], with possible effects on sport fish.

## Key Findings

**Feasibility and cost**
- Preferred plan: 70,000 acre-feet target diversion; average yield about 63,000 acre-feet per year at 500 mg/L, with annual diversions ranging from 35,000 to 140,000 acre-feet.
- Average diverted salinity about 735 mg/L; diversion removes about 210,000 tons of salt per year.
- Cost of water: $709 per acre-foot. Cost of salt removal: $299 per ton, which is considered too high to compete with many Colorado River salinity projects unless the salt allowance rises to roughly $700 per ton.
- Total project construction cost: $816.0 million (October 1992 dollars), with a capitalized total of about $1.8 billion.
- Cost effectiveness for the least expensive alternative tested: $299 per ton of salt removed.

**Water supply and rights**
- Southern Nevada is projected to need additional water as early as 2007. Intensifying demand may be deferred if conservation measures are implemented.
- Future upstream depletions: Utah projects 84,091 acre-feet (2040), up from 15,900 in 1990. Arizona projects 72,540 acre-feet (2040) with an unknown share from groundwater.
- The State Engineer's allocation of water rights is still uncertain, and no hearing date has been set.
- Future depletions create the largest source of uncertainty regarding water availability.

**Sediment and salinity**
- Sediment would be diverted with the water. The estimated sediment load to Halfway Wash Reservoir is considered manageable through design (sluice operation, bypass, and an offstream reservoir).
- Salinity is projected to increase gradually as upstream diversions and return flows grow.

**Environmental and regulatory**
- Consultation with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] is required under the [[Endangered Species Act]] for woundfin, Virgin River chub, and desert tortoise.
- The Virgin River Native Fishes Recovery Plan provides management direction for the conservation plan.
- Other concerns include cultural resources (about 150 recorded sites) and potential Moapa River Indian Reservation impacts, which will require a social accounting in the next phase.

**Recommended next steps and schedule**
- The project proceeds to a feasibility study and environmental impact statement under NEPA.
- Planned milestones: Draft EIS (September 1994), Final EIS (June 1995), and Record of Decision (October 1995).
- Critical issues to resolve include equitable cost sharing with the federal government, validation of desalting and pretreatment costs, the Colorado River Compact question, and the handling of ecological impacts on native fish.
