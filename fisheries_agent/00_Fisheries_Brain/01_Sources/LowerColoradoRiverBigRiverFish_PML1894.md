# Lower Colorado River Big River Fishes: 1999 Stewardship Accomplishment Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This USFWS report, prepared by C.O. Minckley and Mitch Thorson for the [[Arizona Fishery Resources Office]]-Parker (AZFRO-Parker), documents FY1999 stewardship work on two federally endangered big river fishes of the lower [[Colorado River]]: [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Stewardship funding supported the [[Achii Hanyo Project]], a native fish grow-out facility on Colorado River Indian Tribes (CRIT) lands, grow-out ponds on National Wildlife Refuges, repatriation of both species into the lower Colorado River, collection of wild broodstock and larvae, and public education. The report is an accomplishment summary rather than a research study, and its numbers are operational counts.

## Background & Description

- **Bonytail (*Gila elegans*)** and **Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)** are long-lived native fishes of the lower Colorado River. Bonytail may reach 49 years and razorback sucker 45 years.
- Razorback sucker were once abundant near Yuma, where in the early 1900s they were harvested to feed swine and fertilize fields.
- Both species declined after hydroelectric dams, cold-water releases, and introduced fishes. Razorback Sucker were listed as Endangered by the USFWS in 1990, with Critical Habitat designated in 1994 and a recovery plan finalized in 1998. Bonytail were listed as Endangered in 1980, with a recovery plan in 1990 and Critical Habitat in 1994.
- The report describes bonytail as the most endangered big river fish and one of the most endangered fishes in the United States.
- The governing goal, from the [[Lower Colorado River Ecoregion Plan]] (USFWS 1995), is to prevent extinction and stabilize native fish populations through restoration, maintenance, and enhancement of populations and habitat.
- AZFRO-Parker is a satellite of the lead station, AZFRO, for recovery of these species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Mohave** (Arizona-Nevada) holds the largest razorback population, estimated at about 10,000 wild adults. No recruitment has been documented in 25 years, and these adults are expected to be extirpated early in the new millennium.
- Bonytail persist in Lake Mohave at numbers too low for a population estimate. It took 20 years to collect the first 40 wild bonytail, and no recruitment or spawning has been documented in the last 40 years.
- **Lake Havasu** (about 40 miles long) is a repatriation site. The report notes that small recapture numbers reflect the lake's size, the small number of fish released, and limited monitoring.
- **Native fish habitats** include backwaters, coves, and beaver ponds (for example, a beaver pond on the [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]]) that were evaluated or stocked.
- Figure 4 in the source shows the population structure of razorback sucker in Lake Mohave, 1992-1999; its data are not reproduced here.
- The report lists future work on [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and nonnative fishes such as channel catfish, to develop native fish habitat and predator-prey experiments.

## Key Findings

**1. Grow-out facilities (Deliverable 1)**
- Seven ponds totaling about eight surface acres were developed at the Achii Hanyo Project on CRIT lands, with ponds of 0.8-1.2 acres created by subdividing a larger pond. Four ponds were watered and used to raise bonytail for release into Lake Havasu.
- The project is a cooperative effort with CRIT, the [[Bureau of Land Management]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], and the Central Utah Project.

**2. Backwaters (Deliverable 2)**
- No new backwaters were developed in FY99. Earlier work had identified all suitable coves on NWR and CRIT lands, and the three existing facilities are being stocked and monitored until hatchery production makes them unnecessary.

**3. Propagation and production (Deliverable 3)**
- Office Cove (Bill Williams River NWR) produced 86 bonytail and 69 razorback sucker. High Levee Pond (Cibola NWR) produced 38 bonytail. Emerald Canyon Golf Course produced 1 bonytail and 132 razorback sucker.
- The Achii Hanyo Project repatriated 1,776 bonytail and 743 razorback sucker. Razorback sucker were also returned to Lake Havasu from [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]] stock, which had been held there since 1997.
- Total FY99 repatriation: 1,044 razorback sucker and 1,301 bonytail. The text also gives 1,097 and 1,136 for AZFRO-Parker activities, so the figures differ across sections.
- [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] raised about 250,000 razorback sucker fry, with stewardship funds paying part of two staff salaries.
- All released fish were tagged with PIT (Passive Integrated Transponder) tags.

**4. Tagging and monitoring (Deliverable 4)**
- Razorback sucker were released into Lake Havasu, the Colorado River mainstream (Cibola NWR), and a beaver pond on the Bill Williams River NWR. Bonytail were released into Lake Havasu.
- Monitoring used 19 hoop-net nights in CRIT and Lake Havasu waters. Three razorback suckers were recaptured (two from Lake Havasu, one from the beaver pond).
- Cumulative repatriation since 1993: 2,362 razorback sucker and 1,716 bonytail into Lake Havasu.
- **[[Lake Mohave Razorback Roundup]]:** The spring roundup began about 30 years ago and now involves four camps and 50 or more people. USFWS collected 195 adult razorback sucker and about 2,000 wild larvae, and the fry went to Willow Beach. The program has returned about 20,000 ten-inch razorback sucker to Lake Mohave.
- **[[Lake Mohave Bonytail Roundup]]:** In FY99 a total of 11 adult fish were collected by all camps, of which two were wild. Fourteen bonytail were taken and placed at Willow Beach. A wild fish captured in 1998 was included in the 1999 propagation effort at Dexter National Fish Hatchery, increasing broodstock genetic diversity by 25%.

**5. Education and outreach (Deliverable 5)**
- Six types of informational brochures were provided at USFWS offices, along with aquariums holding bonytail and razorback sucker, the Native Fishes of Arizona poster, and school and community talks.
- Stewardship funds supported attendance at meetings and symposia and coordination with stakeholders, including the Desert Fishes Council, the Multi-species Conservation Plan Biology Committee, CRIT, the Hualapai and Quechan tribes, and the states of Arizona, California, and Nevada.

**6. Recommended future work (Additional Opportunities)**
- **Bonytail:** Establish large adult populations in Lake Havasu and Lake Mohave, determine whether recruitment occurs, and test species combinations in native fish habitats.
- **Razorback Sucker:** Maintain target population sizes through stockings and build native fish habitats with bonytail, Colorado Pikeminnow, and other species.
- **Colorado Pikeminnow:** Establish populations in native fish habitats and Lake Havasu and Lake Mohave, using the Achii Hanyo facility to raise fish.
- **Humpback Chub:** Develop and maintain two broodstocks and determine what monitoring level is sufficient.
