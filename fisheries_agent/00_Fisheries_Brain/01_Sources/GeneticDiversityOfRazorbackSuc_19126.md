# Genetic Diversity of Razorback Sucker as Determined by Restriction Endonuclease Analysis of Mitochondrial DNA

#ichthyology #catostomidae #conservation-genetics #endangered-species #mtDNA-analysis

## Executive Summary

This 1994 report to the [[US Bureau of Reclamation]] documents mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) diversity in [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) populations across the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The study examined both natural populations and hatchery-produced stocks at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH). Natural populations exhibited remarkably high genetic diversity in [[Lake Mohave]] (haplotype diversity h=0.97, 0.67 haplotypes per individual), with diversity declining progressively upstream. Analysis of 146 specimens using eight restriction endonucleases revealed 38 composite haplotypes. The study found limited geographic differentiation among populations, suggesting recent connectivity or ongoing gene flow. Hatchery year classes showed variable success in maintaining genetic diversity, with the 1990 year class exhibiting significantly reduced diversity (h=0.71) compared to the source population. The authors strongly recommend direct capture and husbandry of wild-spawned larvae over exclusive hatchery culture to maximize preservation of existing genetic variation.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], historically widespread and abundant throughout the system including the [[Gila River]] drainage. By 1994, the species was federally listed as endangered, with the largest remaining populations in the upper [[Green River]] (fewer than 1,000 adults) and [[Lake Mohave]] (approximately 55,000 adults). Natural populations elsewhere were either extirpated or too small for reliable size estimates.

The species exhibits extreme recruitment failure throughout its range. While adults continue to spawn and produce larvae in rivers and reservoirs, all populations consist only of adults averaging 25 to more than 40 years of age. Juveniles are virtually unknown and recruitment to spawning stocks is largely undetected. The [[Lake Mohave]] population is estimated to have hatched around 1954, coincident with impoundment by [[Davis Dam]]. No verified recruits have been found among almost 12,500 fish tagged or handled since 1973.

[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] established a captive breeding program beginning in 1981-1982 using wild-caught adults from [[Lake Mohave]] as foundation broodstock. Initial broodstock consisted of 79 females captured in 1981-1982 (81+82-WILD), with most F₁ progeny derived from six to eight females (two of which contributed 95.6% of the 29,815 larvae produced in 1981).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and Distribution**: Razorback suckers historically occupied large rivers and floodplain habitats throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species tends to be localized in wide, non-canyon reaches of the mainstem Colorado and larger tributaries. [[Lake Mohave]] is a mainstem impoundment created in 1954, consisting of an upstream riverine reach (~60 km) grading into a wider lower basin (48 km long, maximum width 6.4 km). The reservoir has short retention time (0.24 year) with average depth of 10.5 m.

**Threats and Decline**: Water management practices have reduced and extensively modified aquatic habitats. Non-native species introduced as sport- or baitfish have seriously affected indigenous species through competition, predation, and hybridization. More than 20 native taxa became extinct in the past century. Twenty-three species have been introduced into [[Lake Mohave]], with at least three-fourths known or predicted to consume eggs or larval fishes.

**Recruitment Failure Hypotheses**: Three hypotheses examined include: (1) transport of larvae by water operations, (2) insufficient zooplankton resources, and (3) predation by non-native fishes. Evidence suggests predation is a major factor—larval razorback suckers fail to survive to juvenile stage when stocked with predatory fishes but typically mature in fishless arenas without food augmentation.

**Hybridization**: Limited introgression detected between razorback suckers and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*). Only one of 119 fish from Lakes Mohave, Mead, and Powell or Green/Yampa rivers exhibited flannelmouth mtDNA. Higher incidence in upper [[Colorado River]] (2 of 29 specimens) and [[Little Colorado River]] (5 of 12 individuals). Analysis also revealed considerable introgression between flannelmouth and [[White Sucker]] (*C. commersoni*) in the upper Colorado River.

**Paleoclimatic Context**: The last full glacial period peaked 18,000 years ago with warm season temperatures on the [[Colorado Plateau]] averaging 6.3°C cooler than present. Such thermal conditions would have negatively affected spawning, hatching, and growth of razorback suckers upstream but not downstream, suggesting lower basin populations served as thermal refugia and source populations for post-glacial recolonization.

## Key Findings

**Natural Population Genetic Diversity**:
- [[Lake Mohave]] exhibited exceptionally high mtDNA diversity: h=0.97, with 32 haplotypes among 48 individuals (0.67 haplotypes per individual)—among the highest recorded for any organism
- Progressive decline in diversity moving upstream: [[Lake Mead]] (h=0.82, n_h=0.46), [[Lake Powell]] (h=0.73, n_h=0.35), [[Green River]] (h=0.80, n_h=0.14), upper [[Colorado River]] (h=0.00, n_h=0.04)
- Analysis of 146 specimens with eight restriction endonucleases produced approximately 160 fragments per individual, yielding 38 composite haplotypes
- Enzymes used: BanI, BstEII, HinfI, HinPI, MboI, NheI, ScrFI, and TagI

**Population Structure**:
- Monte Carlo simulation detected significant haplotype frequency differences among populations (χ²=239.5 for 4+6 bp data, χ²=104.7 for 6 bp data, both P<0.001)
- Theta (θ) estimates indicated 20-25% of variation distributed among all populations, but only ~10% when upper Colorado River sample excluded
- Gene diversity analysis: total nucleotide diversity 0.447%, with most variation within populations (0.35%) rather than among populations (0.097%)
- Limited phylogenetic resolution due to low sequence divergence (maximum 0.7% among haplotypes)
- Haplotypes did not cluster geographically, indicating recent connectivity or ongoing gene flow

**Biogeographic Interpretation**:
- High diversity and lack of differentiation consistent with recent panmixia across the entire [[Colorado River Basin]]
- Most examined razorbacks are old individuals likely representing remnant offspring of a single, essentially panmictic population
- Clinal reduction in diversity upstream best explained by [[Lake Mohave]] region serving as stable source population, with serial bottlenecks during post-glacial upstream colonization
- Alternative "sink" hypothesis (downstream accumulation from multiple upstream sources) rejected based on lack of upstream differentiation and paleoclimatic evidence

**Hatchery Population Analysis**:
- Three year classes examined: 1987 (10 individuals, 55 females spawned), 1989 (11 individuals, 14 females spawned), 1990 (10 individuals, 17 females spawned)
- 1987 year class: h=0.89, 6 haplotypes, 0.60 haplotypes per individual
- 1989 year class: h=0.91, 7 haplotypes, 0.64 haplotypes per individual
- 1990 year class: h=0.71, 4 haplotypes, 0.40 haplotypes per individual—significantly lower than source population
- Bootstrap resampling of [[Lake Mohave]] sample (N=25) yielded mean h=0.91 (95% CI: 0.80-0.98); only 1990 year class fell outside this range

**Hatchery Diversity Dynamics**:
- Haplotype diversity (h) is relatively insensitive to broodstock size above 15-20 females when source diversity is high
- Simulation showed h plateaus at ~0.95 with broodstock sizes near 20 individuals, even though 100 females would maintain far more actual genetic variation
- 1990 year class exhibited dramatically lower survivorship to swim-up (11.1%) compared to 1982-1989 average (40.1%, range 22.5-71.4%)
- Reduced diversity likely due to over-representation of some females and/or unequal viability among matings
- Potential inbreeding effects as F₁s may have been interbred with close relatives (parents, siblings)

**Broodstock History Complications**:
- Original broodstock (81+82-WILD): maximum 79 females from [[Lake Mohave]]
- F₁ broodfish derived primarily from 6-8 females in 1981 (two contributed 95.6% of larvae)
- 71 wild females spawned in 1982 produced 1.7 million F₁ larvae (82YC-F₁)
- By 1990, broodstock consisted of mixed 81+82YC-F₁s (likely >50%), 85YC-WILD (likely <20%), and 176 87YC-F₂s (33.5%)
- Lack of documentation on which specific year classes contributed to 1990 production prevents definitive determination of inbreeding effects

**Management Recommendations**:
1. **Direct capture and husbandry of wild-spawned larvae** (strongly recommended): Collect larvae directly from [[Lake Mohave]] using light traps during December-May spawning period. Sample across multiple time periods and geographic locations over multiple years. Rear in diverse "grow-out" habitats (hatchery ponds, cutoff bays, livestock ponds, urban lakes). This approach maximizes genetic diversity preservation as larvae represent far more parents than hatchery or bay-culture can accommodate.

2. **Cutoff bay culture** (intermediate option): Remove predators from isolated backwaters, stock adults to spawn unattended, release adults and progeny back to lake. Five pilot bays used 145 females between 1984-1992 with 40% success rate. Expansion to 10 bays could involve ~120 different females annually. Maintains more diversity than hatchery but less control over reproduction and parental contribution equalization.

3. **Hatchery culture** (least preferred for genetic conservation): Can produce spectacular numbers (100 females could theoretically yield 1.02 × 10⁶ larvae at maximum recorded values) but dramatically reduces genetic variability due to space limitations and limited broodstock. Under ideal conditions maintains only small proportion of natural population variability. Current inbred DNFH broodstock should be replaced with wild-caught individuals; progeny should not be released to replace existing [[Lake Mohave]] population.

**Critical Urgency**: Natural recruitment cannot maintain existing genetic variability. Population crash will result in significant loss of diversity. The old, wild population averaging 25-40+ years of age will soon disappear. Immediate action required to capture and rear wild-spawned larvae before opportunity is lost. Hesitation may preclude maintenance of genetic variation into the future.

**Cost-Effectiveness**: Direct larval capture is most cost-effective and biologically desirable. Hatchery culture is likely most expensive and least desirable for genetic diversity. Cutoff bays intermediate in cost and diversity maintenance, but may be expensive if heavy equipment required for construction.
