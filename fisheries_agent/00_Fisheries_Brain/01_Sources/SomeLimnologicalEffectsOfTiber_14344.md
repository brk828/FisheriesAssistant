# Limnological Effects of Tiber Reservoir on the Marias River, Montana

#limnology #aquatic-ecology #plankton #reservoir-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1963 limnological study by Quentin J. Stober, published in the *Proceedings of the Montana Academy of Sciences*, examines how [[Tiber Reservoir]], an impoundment on the [[Marias River]] completed in 1956, altered water temperature, turbidity, and plankton in the river. Field work ran from June to September 1960 and 1961, with stations above the reservoir, in the reservoir, and at eight sites below the dam. The main results were: the reservoir stratified thermally and developed a density current in 1961; releases lowered river temperatures by about 12°F on average in 1961; turbidity was reduced immediately below the dam but was overwhelmed by storm runoff and high releases; and plankton was extremely sparse in the reservoir, with the river plankton largely produced locally rather than carried from the reservoir.

## Background & Description

The study was designed to measure physical and biological effects of an impoundment on a river. The document places it within earlier work on temperature, turbidity, and plankton below reservoirs, citing studies on [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] mainstream reservoirs, Tenkiller Reservoir, Elephant Butte Reservoir, Lake Mead, and Atwood Lake, and plankton work on the Missouri, upper Mississippi, Pymatuning, and White Nile systems.

Tiber Dam is 196 feet high and was completed by the [[United States Bureau of Reclamation]] in 1956 for irrigation. The reservoir was used only for flood control and recreation. It covers about 15,000 acres. Water levels were held at 2983 ft (1960) and 2984 ft (1961) msl during the study, with a drawdown of about 10 ft in between for spring runoff storage.

The river above the reservoir was sampled for about 10 miles and the river below for about 80 miles, to the mouth of the [[Teton River]]. The river is sluggish, with a gradient of 2.8 to 3.4 ft per mile and an average width of about 200 ft. Its only rooted plant was sparse [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water chemistry data were limited. pH ranged from 7.5 above the reservoir to 7.6 below on August 2, 1960. Dissolved oxygen in the reservoir depths and at the outlet never fell below 7.8 ppm in July and August 1960 and 1961. Total dissolved solids averaged 395 ppm above the reservoir, 350 ppm in the reservoir, 429 ppm one mile below, and 392 ppm 80 miles below. Total hardness was 300 ppm above and 320 ppm immediately below the dam.

Releases came from different depths in different periods. In 1960 releases were drawn from the [[Hypolimnion]] from June 12 to September 1. In 1961 four release periods were recognized, and the water came from the epilimnion, the hypolimnion, or both, depending on the period. Flows ranged from a spring peak of 2170 cfs (1960) and 3071 cfs (1961) down to August lows of about 110 to 122 cfs.

The river's riparian zone includes sagebrush, cottonwood, and willow. [[Cladophora]] grew in dense mats in riffles in the first six miles below the dam. Carp and suckers were present, and the author suggested they may have contributed slightly to turbidity during low flow in August.

## Key Findings

**Temperature**
- The reservoir stratified thermally on the first sampling date each year. In 1961 a density current of cold inflow ran about 20 miles through the lower reservoir.
- Surface temperatures in the reservoir reached a maximum of 70°F (1960) and 74°F (1961).
- In 1961 daily river temperatures below the dam averaged 12°F cooler than above.
- Below the dam, maximum and minimum temperatures rose about 1.2°F per mile over 11 miles.
- The reservoir had a marked effect on river temperature at least 24 miles downstream.
- Low flows were associated with higher maximum and minimum temperatures at stations farther downstream.

**Turbidity**
- Above the reservoir, turbidity averaged 16.6 ppm (1960) and 16.5 ppm (1961), with maxima during spring runoff.
- Immediately below the dam, average turbidity was 14.1 ppm lower than above in 1960 and 10.3 ppm lower in 1961.
- Secchi transparency was lowest at the head of the reservoir and increased toward the dam.
- A storm-driven runoff event on July 24 raised turbidity to 465 ppm, the maximum recorded in the study.
- Shore erosion during wave action turned the water milky-brown, with samples of 127 to 158 ppm near shore.

**Plankton**
- Plankton was extremely scarce in the reservoir in both years. Diatoms were the most common phytoplankton, and [[Ceratium]] was the most numerous single taxon.
- Diatoms (Chrysophyta) made up over 90% (1960) and over 85% (1961) of phytoplankton at river stations.
- The average total phytoplankton in the river below the reservoir was lower in 1961 than in 1960, which the author linked to high water, turbidity, and scouring releases.
- Zooplankton was generally greater below the reservoir than above, and it declined at successive downstream stations.
- The author concluded that most river phytoplankton originated in benthic communities of the river, not in the reservoir.

**Overall Conclusion**
The reservoir cooled the downstream river and reduced turbidity near the dam, but storm runoff, scouring releases, and river-bed production dominated the downstream plankton and turbidity patterns.
