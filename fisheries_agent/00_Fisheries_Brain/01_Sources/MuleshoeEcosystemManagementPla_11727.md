# Muleshoe Ecosystem Management Plan and Environmental Assessment (Draft, 1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This September 1996 draft from the [[Bureau Of Land Management]] (BLM) Arizona State Office and Tucson Field Office presents the **Muleshoe Ecosystem Management Plan (EMP)** and its accompanying Environmental Assessment (EA-AZ-046-96-001). The plan covers about 57,500 acres in the [[Galiuro Mountains]] of southeastern Arizona, jointly managed through the [[Muleshoe Cooperative Management Area]] by the BLM, the [[US Forest Service]] (FS), and [[The Nature Conservancy]] (TNC). It is intended to be the primary management guide for BLM lands (including wilderness) and provides guidance for TNC lands within the CMA.

The plan follows an [[Ecosystem Management Approach]] built on interagency partnership, inventory data, a shared vision, six resource objectives, monitoring, and scheduled evaluation. The proposed action emphasizes riparian and upland vegetation, native fish and wildlife, wilderness, cultural resources, and social environment values while permitting compatible uses.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** The planning area lies in the Basin and Range physiographic province. Approximately 26,500 acres are BLM land, 22,000 acres are FS land, 6,000 acres are private, and 3,000 acres are Arizona state land. Major drainages include [[Redfield Canyon]], [[Hot Springs Canyon]], and [[Cherry Springs Watershed]], all tributaries of the lower [[San Pedro River]]. Elevations range from about 3,250 ft at the west end of Redfield Canyon to 7,650 ft at Bassett Peak.

**Governance and legal framework.** The plan implements the [[Safford District Resource Management Plan]] (1994) and integrates the functions of wilderness, ACEC, habitat, allotment, recreation, and cultural activity plans. It complies with [[FLPMA]] (1976), the Wilderness Act, the [[Endangered Species Act]], the [[Sikes Act]], and several recovery plans. Designated areas include the [[Redfield Canyon Wilderness]] (designated 1990), the [[Hot Springs Watershed ACEC]] (16,763 acres), and a portion of the [[Galiuro Wilderness]] (76,317 acres, administered by the FS).

**Planning team.** An interdisciplinary team from the BLM, Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), FS, TNC, Soza Mesa Ranch, Saguaro-Juniper Association, and Bayless-Berkalew Company prepared the plan. Public input came from a 1990 scoping open house, a 1993–94 scoping mailing to more than 500 recipients, and two 1994 field trips.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and geology.** Annual precipitation ranges from 10–12 inches at the lower San Pedro margin to 16–20 inches at higher elevations, with bimodal summer thunderstorms and winter frontal storms. Mid-Tertiary volcanism and [[Basin and Range Extension]] shaped the terrain; the [[Muleshoe Fault]] has displaced the Allen Flat basin more than 3,200 ft. Mineral potential for gold, silver, and copper is rated low.

**Soils and watersheds.** Soils are generally shallow with rock outcrops; about 40% of the Muleshoe Allotment has slopes above 50%, and ground cover averages nearly three-fourths rock and gravel. Watershed condition on BLM lands in the Redfield and Hot Springs watersheds is rated fair (1989 Safford RMP analysis). Hot Springs Canyon contributes a significant share of base flow to the lower San Pedro River.

**Hydrology.** The planning area has seven perennial streams totaling about 23.3 miles. Average flows in Table 1 include Hot Springs Canyon at 5.4 cfs, Bass Canyon at 3.02 cfs, Redfield Canyon at 3.9 cfs, and Wildcat Canyon at 0.08 cfs. Flows are highly variable and respond flashily to storms. The BLM holds an instream flow permit for Hot Springs Canyon, and a Federal Reserve Right of 1,659.06 acre-feet per year was claimed for Redfield Canyon Wilderness.

**Vegetation.** The area spans Sonoran desert scrub, semi-desert grassland, riparian deciduous woodland, evergreen woodland/chaparral, and montane forest. Riparian canopies are dominated by Velvet Ash, Sycamore, Arizona walnut, and willows, with Fremont cottonwood and Goodding willow in wider bottoms. Historic [[Livestock Grazing]] and fire suppression shifted much of the grassland toward shrub dominance (mesquite, whitethorn, juniper, and amole).

**Riparian and aquatic habitat.** Riparian areas are the most biodiverse habitat on the Muleshoe. Pools, runs, and riffles shape which fish species can persist. [[Gila Chub]] and Sonora sucker depend on pool habitat, while undercut banks and woody debris enhance quality. Flooding is a key process: non-native fishes in canyon-bound reaches are often eliminated by floods, so a natural flood regime supports native fish.

**Fish and wildlife.** The area supports 29 special status species: five fishes, four reptiles, one amphibian, eight birds, 10 mammals, and one plant. The five native fishes (longfin dace, speckled dace, desert sucker, Sonoran sucker, and Gila chub) are all biologically imperiled and candidates for federal listing. Gila chub occurs at only 13 locations worldwide. Green sunfish, an exotic, has been found in Redfield Canyon.

**Cultural resources.** Human occupation may extend back about 12,000 years. Only limited survey has been done. Eleven prehistoric sites are documented, and Hooker's Hot Springs is the only listed National Register site. The Aravaipa Band of Western Apache identified medicinal and edible riparian plants for protection.

**Land use.** Livestock grazing has been suspended on the Muleshoe Allotment since TNC acquired the ranch in 1982, while Soza Mesa has been grazed under a 44-cattle yearlong stocking rate. Recreation draws an estimated 1,700–1,800 visitors per year (sign-in based and likely conservative).

## Key Findings

**Management objectives (six total):**
1. **Riparian:** Achieve or maintain proper functioning condition and high seral states. Tree densities and sapling-to-tree ratios are targeted (Redfield Canyon benchmark: ratio of 3:1 and more than 425 trees/acre). Recovery is expected within five years of a major flood, defined as one reducing tree density by at least one-third.
2. **Upland:** On the Muleshoe portion, convert shrub-invaded grassland so that 80% of acreage is in States I and II (64% State I, 16% State II) within 30 years. On Soza Mesa, maintain PNC or High condition on at least 80% of acreage.
3. **Fish and wildlife:** Maintain and enhance native biodiversity. Evaluate reintroduction or supplementation for seven species, including Gila topminnow, desert pupfish, loach minnow, spikedace, Gila chub, desert bighorn sheep, and turkey.
4. **Cultural:** Protect and preserve sites while making them available for research and tradition. Actions include a Class II inventory and an ethnoecology study by the end of 2000.
5. **Wilderness:** Maintain naturalness and solitude, with a group size limit of 15 persons and natural-ignition-only prescribed fire.
6. **Social environment:** Maintain the four recreation opportunity settings (rural, semi-primitive motorized, semi-primitive non-motorized, and primitive).

**Key management actions:**
- Pursue instream flow water rights on Hot Springs, Bass, Redfield, and Wildcat canyons.
- Eliminate livestock grazing in riparian areas and build fencing to separate uplands and riparian zones.
- Close Hot Springs Canyon riparian area (about 140 acres) to vehicles and limit Bass Canyon to day use.
- Implement a prescribed fire program of 15 designated units, with each unit burned on a 5–10 year cycle and no more than 20% of acreage treated annually in the first five years.
- Reduce the Muleshoe Allotment (No. 4401) to the Pride Basin, with an initial 346 AUMs in suspended nonuse until upland objectives are met.
- Run a four-pasture deferred-rotation grazing system on Soza Mesa (No. 4402, 502 AUMs).

**Monitoring and evaluation:** Monitoring covers monthly flows, biennial riparian vegetation and aquatic habitat sampling, annual fall fish surveys (AGFD protocol), and winter and June avian surveys. Formal evaluation is required at least every five years, with informal annual reviews. Plan amendments require a 45-day public review.

**Environmental assessment alternatives:** The No Action Alternative would continue full fire suppression, indefinite livestock suspension, and uncoordinated activity plans. Alternatives rejected include winter-only grazing (about 1,563 AUMs) and a riparian-pasture yearlong strategy (86 cattle, 671 AUMs) because of anticipated streambank and riparian damage.

**Open items:** The plan notes that the Implementation and Cost Estimates section (Section IX) "will be developed in the final plan." The 1996 document is a draft.
