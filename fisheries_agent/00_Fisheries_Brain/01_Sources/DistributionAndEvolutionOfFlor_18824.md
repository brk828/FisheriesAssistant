# Distribution and Evolution of Florida's Troglobitic Crayfishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This 1982 review by [[Richard Franz]] and [[David S. Lee]] synthesizes taxonomic literature and the authors' roughly 17 years of field observations on Florida's cave-adapted crayfishes. Florida hosts the most diverse troglobitic crayfish fauna known, with eleven species in the genera *Cambarus*, *Procambarus* and *Troglocambarus*. The authors argue that geology, especially the [[Crystal River Formation]], controls where these crayfishes occur, and that the availability of organic food energy controls which species groups succeed in a given cave system. They propose that multiple invasions of surface crayfishes into the aquifer, linked to stream capture by sinkholes, produced the modern fauna.

## Background & Description

- **Fauna:** Eleven troglobitic species were recognized. Before Hobbs's work in the late 1930s, only [[Procambarus acherontis]] was known from the state.
- **Taxonomic history:** [[Horton H. Hobbs Jr.]] described most species between 1940 and 1972. Later additions were [[Procambarus erythrops]] (Relyea and Sutton, 1975) and [[Procambarus franzi]] (Hobbs and Lee, 1976). One further undescribed species, from Alexander Springs in Lake County, was known only from two specimens.
- **Groups used by the authors:**
  - **Group A:** *Cambarus cryptodytes*, a relict species related to Tennessee and Ozark taxa. It is the only Florida troglobite known outside the state.
  - **Group B:** *Procambarus acherontis*, apparently unrelated to any other living *Procambarus*. It is known from one Orange County site and three Seminole County sites.
  - **Group C:** *Procambarus milleri*, known only from its type locality, a well in Miami, and closely related to the epigean *P. alleni*.
  - **Group D:** The five-member [[Procambarus lucifugus Complex]], related to the stream-dwelling *P. pictus*.
  - **Group E:** The three-member [[Procambarus pallidus Complex]] (*P. pallidus*, *P. horsti*, *P. orcinus*), related to *P. lepidodactylus*.
  - **Group F:** *Troglocambarus maclanei*, a highly specialized species derived from the Pictus group and related to *P. ancylus*.
- **Study context:** Fieldwork was partly sponsored by a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service contract to study the impact of phosphate mining on the Osceola National Forest and the upper Suwannee River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology and overburden.** Troglobitic crayfishes are restricted to carbonate rocks that have light or no clastic overburden. Areas with moderate to heavy sediment cover lack them, because the sediment filters detritus and limits trophic input to cave entrances and sinkholes. Several limestones that appear suitable, notably the [[Suwannee Limestone]] and Tampa limestone, host no troglobitic crayfish. The authors suggest that their hard, dense zones limit solution, sinkhole formation and detritus movement.

**Physiographic setting.** The [[Northern Highlands Escarpment]] runs northwest to southeast, roughly along the 30 m contour, and is marked by extensive sinkhole activity. Streams flowing off the highlands are captured by sinks. Species distributions track the age of karst: *P. pallidus* favors newly developing systems along the escarpment, while *P. lucifugus alachua* occupies more mature systems below it. Caine's (1974) suggested link to the 15 m contour was not supported; localities ranged from 3 m (Manatee Springs) to 46 m (Fort Clark Church).

**Food energy.** Food arrives as plant or animal debris washed through sinks or as guano under bat roosts. Two bats, *Myotis austroriparius* and *M. grisescens*, use caves in sufficient numbers to build guano deposits. Estimated nursery colonies of *M. austroriparius* numbered 14,000 at Sunday Sink and 17,000 at Orange Lake Cave. At Sunday Sink, more than 1,000 crayfish congregated under the roost for about two months after the bats left, which suggests guano is a short-term resource.

**Two dispersal patterns.** Where subterranean water moves considerably, food is carried far and crayfish spread widely through the aquifer. Where water movement is static, populations cluster around the organic source.

**Energy-group relationships.** The *P. lucifugus* complex occurs mostly in collapse sinks and under bat roosts, and it has the largest populations (about 400-500 *P. erythrops* at Sims Sink; more than 1,000 intergrade *P. lucifugus* at Sunday Sink). *P. pallidus* and the other Group E species usually number under 50 at energy input points and occur in blue-hole sinks and spring mouths. *T. maclanei* is linked to the lowest energy zones, where particles are small. The authors observed little co-occurrence of these groups and report only three syntopic cases.

**Interconnected aquatic subterranean system.** The cave-dwelling yellow bullhead (*Ictalurus natalis*) and the cavefish *Notropis harperi* occur in north-central Florida caves, often many kilometers from epigean habitat. The authors infer that much of the aquatic subterranean system is interconnected.

**Physiology.** [[Caine]] (1978) and [[Dickson and Franz]] (1980) found lower metabolic rates in hypogean crayfish than in related surface species. Respiration and ATP data suggest that *P. pallidus* is more physiologically adjusted to low-energy caves than *P. erythrops*, *P. franzi* or *P. lucifugus*.

## Key Findings

1. **Geology drives distribution.** The [[Crystal River Formation]], a highly soluble Eocene limestone forming the upper part of the Ocala Group, is the most important geological control. *P. lucifugus*, *P. erythrops*, *P. franzi*, *P. pallidus* and *T. maclanei* are almost entirely confined to it.
2. **Formation-specific species.** *P. acherontis* and an undescribed *Procambarus* sp. occur in the Hawthorne Formation; *P. milleri* in the Miami Oolite; *P. horsti* and *P. orcinus* in the St. Marks Formation; *C. cryptodytes* near the Marianna Limestone and Crystal River contact.
3. **Energy dictates colonization.** Species most dependent on large organic accumulations are more isolated and show more speciation, as in the *P. lucifugus* complex. Low-energy specialists such as *P. pallidus* and *T. maclanei* disperse more widely and today have the largest ranges.
4. **Population fragmentation.** The *P. lucifugus* complex contains five allopatric populations. The authors propose barriers such as the Withlacoochee River and the floodplain of an extinct river, and suggest *P. franzi* may have excluded *P. lucifugus* from an intermediate area.
5. **Sea level and age.** Pleistocene sea levels probably never exceeded about 20 m above present m.s.l. Florida has existed as at least a short, truncated peninsula since the Pliocene. Some sinks may have remained open for at least 2,000,000 years (Webb, 1974), supporting long colony histories.
6. **Multiple invasions.** The authors conclude that at least six surface crayfish lineages invaded the aquifer, possibly from the late Miocene or Pliocene, after stream capture by sinkholes. Fossil streams at the [[Love Bone Bed]] (late Miocene) support this model.
7. **Revision of earlier hypotheses.** The authors disagree with Caine (1974) that *P. milleri* and *P. acherontis* are recent arrivals. They argue that a southern refugium likely persisted long enough for *P. milleri* to predate the 1920s aquifer lowering.
8. **Unique cryptodytes distribution.** *C. cryptodytes* ranges widely over west Florida and southwest Georgia because lateral detritus transport is extensive, and it is absent from the nutrient-rich Bat Cave and largely absent from Geromes Cave.
9. **Records questioned.** The authors doubt a *P. pallidus* record from Eickelburger Cave in Marion County, 40 km from the nearest known locality, and suspect a sample mix-up.
10. **Research gaps.** Form I males are still needed to resolve the affinities of some populations. Chemical properties of uninhabited aquifers and ecology of *T. maclanei* and *P. milleri* remain unstudied.
