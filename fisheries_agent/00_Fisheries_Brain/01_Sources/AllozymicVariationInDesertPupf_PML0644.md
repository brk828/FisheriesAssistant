# Genetic Variation in Desert Pupfish from Natural and Captive Habitats: Genetic Conservation in Fluctuating Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares patterns of allozymic (protein-electrophoretic) variation among wild and captive populations of the endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), a small fish native to the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] of northwestern Mexico and the southwestern United States. The authors, Jason B. Dunham and W. L. Minckley of Arizona State University, asked whether genetic differences among captive populations reflect founder effects, bottlenecks, or admixture, and whether current recovery stocking preserves genetic diversity. Results indicate significant genetic differences among captive and wild populations, which the authors attribute largely to founder effects from few founding events, with reduced gene flow after hydrological isolation. They recommend managing captive stocks in ways that mimic natural conditions and treating remaining wild populations as a conservation priority.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Cyprinodon Macularius]], a short-lived desert cyprinodontid fish. Its original range exceeded 40,000 km² at elevations of roughly 50 to 1,500 m (Minckley, 1973).
- **Taxonomic context:** Populations in Quitobaquito Spring and nearby sites were once recognized as *C. m. eremus*, a subspecies now considered synonymous with the main form. A third form occurs in the Laguna Salada region (Hubbs & Miller, 1948 cited in text).
- **Status:** The species has declined through habitat destruction and introduced non-native fishes, has been extirpated from much of its range, and is listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS, 1986). A recovery plan exists (Marsh & Sada, 1993).
- **Management context:** Recovery relies heavily on translocations and captive propagation, whose effectiveness for maintaining genetic integrity is uncertain (see [[Translocation as Conservation Tool]]).

**Study populations:** Captive populations were held at sites including the Dexter National Fish Hatchery, the Living Desert, Desert Botanical Garden, Thompson Arboretum, Howard Wells, Simone/McCallum pond and the Quitobaquito-linked Organ Pipe stock. Wild samples came from Salton Sink localities (e.g., Salt Creek, Cleveland Street Drain, shoreline pools, San Felipe Creek, Palm Canyon) and Colorado Delta sites (e.g., Flowing Wells, Santa Clara). Captive histories were reconstructed from unpublished notes and personal communications.

**Methods:** Proteins were analyzed by starch-gel electrophoresis from eye, muscle and liver tissue. Four to six polymorphic loci were scored (e.g., [[sAh-A]], [[Gpi-A]], [[Gpi-B]], [[Ldh-A]]) using the BIOSYS-1 program. Heterozygosity was computed following Nei (1978), Hardy-Weinberg deviations were tested with chi-square with Levene's correction, and population differentiation was tested with Fisher exact tests (GENEPOP). Mantel tests related genetic variation to number of founders, relative habitat size, time since founding and number of founding events. Heterozygosity change was standardized against source populations to control for source variability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical hydrology and boom-bust cycles:** Before 1905, pupfish occupied extensive desert springs and streams of the Salton basin. Flooding of the Colorado River in 1905–1907 formed the present [[Salton Sea]], after which pupfish formed large shoreline populations that declined sharply from the mid-1900s with the introduction of non-native fishes and rising salinity from irrigation return flows. A similar boom-and-bust cycle is inferred for the [[Colorado River Delta]], where upstream diversions from the 1900s and the construction of Hoover Dam (1935) led to desiccation. Rewatering of the delta in 1982–1983 increased pupfish distribution and abundance, although some previously occupied sites could no longer be located in later surveys.

**Lineage structure:** Four genetic lineages were identified: the San Felipe Creek–Palm Canyon lineage, the Canal Sánchez–Taboada lineage (including Dexter NFH and Desert Botanical Garden refugia), the Terrace Springs lineage (Howard Wells and Flowing Wells JHS refugia), and the Salt Creek lineage.

**Threats and stresses:** Isolation, sudden expansions and reductions in habitat, and hybridization or interbreeding of populations are major stresses. Hydrological control of the Colorado River now precludes gene flow among populations, and the species is at risk of further loss of diversity.

## Key Findings

- **Captive vs. wild differences:** Significant genetic differences exist between captive and wild desert pupfish populations, with founder effects playing a major role in shaping captive genetic patterns.
- **Low overall variation:** Only four polymorphic loci were resolved, and levels of allozymic variation were generally low. Mean heterozygosity values in the 13 populations ranged from roughly 0.07 to 0.14, with 1 to 4 polymorphic loci per population.
- **Chi-square deviations:** Significant deviations from Hardy-Weinberg expectations occurred in only two of 39 tests, with an excess of heterozygotes for sAh-A at one site.
- **Founder effects:** Heterozygosity loss in descendant populations was strongly associated with founder population size, but not with time since founding. Populations founded from multiple sources (e.g., Howard Wells, from a mixture of two populations) showed more variation.
- **Source-stock influence:** Genetic patterns in some captive stocks may not be fully explained by founding alone. Some differences, such as in the Thompson stock and Wells stock, reflect a poorly characterized history.
- **Lineage differentiation:** Significant population differentiation occurred within lineages for certain loci (e.g., sAh-A and Gpi-A in some comparisons), while Salton Sink wild populations were remarkably homogeneous across a large geographic scale.
- **Gene flow and translocation:** Translocation can reduce founder effects only when sufficient gene flow is maintained. Wild Salton Sink populations were genetically homogeneous, which the authors attribute to historical gene flow and abundance rather than current connectivity.
- **Comparison with prior work:** Earlier studies (Turner, 1984; Edds & Echelle, 1988) found fewer genetic differences between captive and wild desert pupfish, which the authors attribute to limited sampling.
- **Recovery recommendations:** The authors conclude that wild populations should be preserved as units, captive populations should be managed to mimic natural conditions, and local adaptation and genetic diversity should be considered. Efforts should prioritize maintaining wild populations, as few such populations remain and genetic diversity is limited in captivity.
- **Broader implication:** Desert pupfish are a standard model for conservation of desert fishes, and their perpetuation should be a high priority because they represent desert aquatic ecosystems.
