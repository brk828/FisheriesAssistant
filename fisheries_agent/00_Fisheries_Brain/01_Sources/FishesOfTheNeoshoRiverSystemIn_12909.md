# Fishes of the Neosho River System in Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes #faunal-survey

## Executive Summary

This 1967 faunal survey by [[Branley A. Branson]] (Department of Biology, Eastern Kentucky University) reports 103 fish species from the Oklahoma portion of the [[Neosho River]] and its tributaries, equal to 66% of the fish species known from Oklahoma. The study is based on field collections made in 1955 to 1956, supplemented by published and museum records. The author argues that the Neosho acts as a biogeographic barrier between eastern (Ozarkian, clear-water) and western (turbid, Arkansas River) faunas, and proposes that some Ozark species, notably [[Fundulus Catenatus]], entered the system through stream capture from the [[White River]] drainage.

## Background & Description

The [[Neosho River]], known as the Grand River downstream of the [[Spring River]] mouth, rises in the Flint Hills of Morris County, east-central Kansas, and flows about 478 miles southeast to its confluence with the [[Arkansas River]] near Fort Gibson, Oklahoma. The watershed covers about 12,660 square miles: 6,285 in Kansas, 2,965 in Oklahoma, 2,995 in Missouri and 415 in Arkansas. The basin is underlain by gently westward-dipping Cretaceous, Permian and Pennsylvanian formations. Headwater elevation is about 1,500 ft (460 m), falling to 477 ft (145 m) in Arkansas.

Three major tributaries drain about 43% of the watershed:
- **[[Cottonwood River]]**: heads in McPherson County, Kansas, and runs 383 miles to its mouth near Emporia.
- **[[Spring River]]**: the Neosho's largest feeder, rising near Aurora, Missouri.
- **[[Elk River]]** (locally Cowskin Creek): formed by Big and Little Sugar creeks near Pineville, Missouri.

Land use has affected water quality. Many small streams, once clear, are turbid from strip mining and poor soil practices. The region receives about 40 inches of annual rainfall, which percolates quickly into rocky soils, so natural lakes and swamps are scarce.

The fauna is based on collections made by [[Allan D. Linder]], R. M. Sutton, W. G. Greer, G. H. Wallen and the author, plus records from the University of Oklahoma and Oklahoma State University. Roughly 100 collecting stations are listed by number (e.g., A1 to A25, B1 to B5, C1 to C8, J1 to J17), with localities keyed to township, range and section.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat partitioning.** The Neosho system divides into three habitat groups: clear eastern tributaries (Ozarkian streams such as [[Spavinaw Creek]] and [[Shoal Creek]]), turbid western tributaries, and the main channel. Many species are restricted to one group. For example, [[Notropis Pilsbryi]] is the most common cyprinid in eastern tributaries but is absent from the main stream, while [[Notropis Lutrensis]] is the dominant minnow in the quiet waters of the main stream and western tributaries.

**Biogeographic barrier.** Table 1 of the source tallies the distribution of species among these groups:
- 23 species restricted to clear eastern tributaries
- 2 species found only in western tributaries
- 29 species found in eastern tributaries, the main channel and western tributaries
- 12 species restricted to the main channel
- 11 species shared by the main channel and eastern tributaries only
- 6 species shared by the main channel and western tributaries only
- 1 species found in both tributary types but not the main channel

The author concludes the Neosho "is very definitely acting as a barrier" to east-west dispersal.

**Drought and faunal exchange.** During the 1952 to 1956 drought, the longest on record, the Neosho became creek-like. Small-stream fishes moved into the river, which the author suggests may have served as a southern escape route. Floods and stream capture are invoked to explain faunal exchange with the [[Illinois River]] and [[White River]] systems.

**Introductions.** Several species, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Banded Tetra]], [[Walleye Pike]] and [[Redear Sunfish]], were introduced or escaped from hatcheries or bait buckets. The bait trade is a recurring source of introductions, for example [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Golden Shiner]].

## Key Findings

- **Species count:** 103 species are reported from the Oklahoma Neosho drainage, about 66% of the state's known fish fauna.
- **Expected but unrecorded:** The author lists 13 additional species (e.g., [[Silver Chub]], [[Johnny Darter]], [[Brindled Madtom]], [[Freckled Madtom]], [[Yellow Perch]]) as likely occurring in the system but not yet confirmed.
- **Notable Oklahoma records:**
  - [[Northern Studfish]] (*Fundulus catenatus*): 7 specimens from Spavinaw Creek, 31 from Buffalo Creek, and 1 from Woodward Hollow, Grand Lake; its habits are unlike any other Oklahoma *Fundulus*.
  - [[Sauger]] (*Stizostedion canadense*): a single Oklahoma locality, represented by a partially decayed head and anterior trunk.
  - [[Plains Topminnow]] (*Fundulus sciadicus*): first Oklahoma records since 1929.
  - [[Arkansas Darter]] (*Etheostoma craigini*): found only in spring-fed pools and creeks no farther south than Mayes County, at the western limit of its range.
- **Economic fishes:** [[Flathead Catfish]] is the largest food fish, often exceeding 60 lb, and dominates commercial catches from large impoundments. [[Buffalofish]] species compose a large share of commercial catches in Grand Lake.
- **Forage:** [[Gizzard Shad]] and [[Threadfin Shad]] are key forage in reservoirs, and the author stresses the need for predators such as garfishes to limit shad numbers.
- **Hybridization:** Sunfish hybrids (*Lepomis macrochirus* × *L. megalotis*, × *L. cyanellus*, × *L. humilis*) occurred in intermittent turbid pools, and the author calls for further study of introgression in Kansas and Oklahoma strip-mine pits.
- **Stream capture hypothesis:** The Neosho probably captured Spring and Elk rivers from the White River system. Evidence includes:
  - Headwaters of Shoal and Elk creeks lie within about one mile of White River tributaries.
  - [[Hellbender]] (*Cryptobranchus alleganiensis*) specimens typical of the eastern form were recorded from Kansas.
  - The fish fauna of the White River drainage, such as large populations of *Notropis rubellus* and *Fundulus catenatus*, matches species that are rare in the Neosho.
- **Origins:** The primary influence on the Neosho fauna has been from the Arkansas River, while some species, such as *Fundulus olivaceus*, reflect possible exchange with the Illinois River system.
- **Future work:** The author was preparing a comprehensive study of the Spring River Drainage, and recommended legislation against indiscriminate introduction of exotic fishes.
