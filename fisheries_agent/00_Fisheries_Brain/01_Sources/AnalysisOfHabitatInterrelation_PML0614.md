# Spikedace and Red Shiner Habitat Interaction: First Annual Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is a first annual progress report from [[Arizona State University]] to the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] under contract G-70001. It addresses habitat interactions between the federally listed [[Spikedace]] and the introduced [[Red Shiner]] in streams of the [[Gila River Basin]]. The report states that native and non-native fishes have been widely separated in recent decades, but the nature and magnitude of their habitat relationships had received little quantitative study. Field work at five stream systems compared the habitat occupied by spikedace, [[Loach Minnow]], and red shiner, using seine and electrofishing samples with measured stream variables. Data were analyzed with ANOVA and [[Weighted ANOVA]] procedures. The authors conclude that the habitat preferences of spikedace and red shiner differ and that the interaction is still unquantified, so management for recovery of the native species cannot be fully effective without further study.

## Background & Description

The native fish community of the Gila River basin includes spikedace and loach minnow, both of which are rare and restricted to specific stream habitats. Spikedace is a small cyprinid listed by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. Its historic and present distribution, abundance, and habitat requirements had been poorly documented before this work. Red shiner is non-native to the study area and has spread across the region.

The report notes that the distribution declines of native fishes have been documented in earlier studies, but the relationship between native and introduced species had received little quantitative attention. Earlier work described interactions such as competition, which the authors suggest may be subtle rather than overt. Because these interactions were unquantified, the authors argue that an understanding of habitat use is needed before effective recovery planning can proceed.

The project was designed as a multi-year study. The first year included field surveys of habitat and fish distribution at several reaches, with the aim of informing later experimental work. The report describes its approach as intended to assess the [[Habitat Partitioning]] between the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Five stream systems in Arizona and New Mexico were surveyed (Figure 1):
- [[East Aravaipa Creek]] (upper, east) and [[West Aravaipa Creek]] (lower, west), Graham County, Arizona
- [[Sycamore Creek]], Maricopa County, Arizona
- [[Verde River]], Yavapai County, Arizona
- [[Blue River]], Greenlee County, Arizona
- Gila River at Gila (Gila-Gila) and at Redrock (Gila-Redrock), Catron County, New Mexico

Two to three sites were sampled in each stream so that variation in habitat and species composition could be evaluated within streams.

**Fish species recorded.** Native species occurring alongside spikedace included [[Loach Minnow]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Speckled Dace]], Sonora sucker, and [[Desert Sucker]]. The report notes that red shiner was present in several reaches, sometimes in isolation from spikedace and sometimes in syntopy (co-occurrence) with it. Species checklists and specimen records are provided in tables and are summarized here rather than reproduced in full.

**Sampling methods.** Fish were collected in standardized replicates using a fine-mesh seine at each locality. Seining for loach minnow was targeted to riffles. Electrofishing was used at some sites. Captured fish were identified, counted, measured for length and weight, and released, with specimens retained where required. Habitat was characterized at each seine haul by:
- Current velocity
- Stream depth
- Cover (aquatic vegetation, overhanging vegetation, canyon shade, and similar)
- Substrate (silt, sand, gravel, cobble, boulder, and others)

**Analysis.** Initial comparisons used ANOVA and t-tests in statistical software. Habitat variables that were not normally distributed were transformed before analysis. The authors used:
1. Unweighted ANOVAs comparing habitat at occupied sites with habitat available across each stream.
2. Weighted ANOVAs comparing presence versus absence of each species, with weights based on the number of individuals captured at each site, so that more abundant samples carried more influence.
3. Comparisons of spikedace and red shiner habitat within syntopic sites and in isolation.

## Key Findings

- **Spikedace habitat in isolated streams.** Spikedace in streams where it occurred alone (East Aravaipa, Gila-Gila, and West Aravaipa) occupied habitats that differed from what was available. Results varied by stream. East Aravaipa showed no clear preference for a particular velocity, while spikedace in other reaches occupied currents that were significantly swifter on average than available habitat. Overall, the authors report that spikedace occupied significantly swifter water and smaller substrates than were available.

- **Red shiner in Sycamore Creek.** In Sycamore Creek, where red shiner occurs apart from spikedace, the species was captured at 89 of 100 sampled sites with habitat data, compared with 11 absences. Red shiner occupied water that was shallower and slower than the stream average. This pattern is interpreted as a habitat preference distinct from that of spikedace.

- **Shared habitat where syntopic.** In the Verde River and Gila-Redrock reaches, where spikedace and red shiner co-occurred, the two species showed substantial overlap in habitat. The report states that the species occupied similar habitat when alone or together in some comparisons, which suggests that the presence of red shiner does not necessarily displace spikedace from its preferred conditions.

- **Differences between species.** Weighted analyses at the Verde River and Gila-Redrock suggested that spikedace and red shiner differ in current velocity and substrate use. Red shiner tended to occupy slower water. The authors note that these differences were not always statistically significant, and that sample sizes in some syntopic comparisons were small (for example, 20 spikedace-only hauls and 17 red shiner-only hauls at the Verde River; 14 each at Gila-Redrock, as read from the tables).

- **Stream-specific variation.** Habitat relationships differed among streams. The authors note that the same species occupied different habitats in different systems, and that the habitat available to spikedace in one stream was not the same as in another. They caution that these habitat measures should be interpreted in light of these differences and that extrapolation across streams is limited.

- **Implications for management.** The report concludes that because co-occurrence interactions between red shiner and spikedace were not quantified, recovery actions for native fishes cannot be fully designed without understanding these interactions. The authors recommend continued study, including further habitat characterization and experimental work in the second year, to evaluate the potential for red shiner to affect spikedace through competition or habitat use.

- **Study status.** This is a progress report. Conclusions are described as preliminary, and the authors state that the first-year results are the basis for planned second-year work. Final conclusions are not yet available.
