# Position Statement on Recreational Fishery Resources and Native Species Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #conservation-policy #invasive-species #nonindigenous-species #endangered-species-act

## Executive Summary

This 1994 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service working draft establishes agency policy balancing two potentially conflicting mandates: sustaining **recreational fishery resources** and ensuring **conservation/recovery of native aquatic species**. The document acknowledges that ~150 years of habitat alteration, fishery management, species introductions, and biological experimentation have produced "mixed aquatic communities" across the United States that must now be actively managed. It affirms that healthy [[Aquatic Ecosystems]] are the best indicator of ecological integrity and underpin both native species persistence and fisheries (subsistence, recreational, commercial). The statement formally adopts the [[American Fisheries Society]] (Kohler and Courtenay 1986) guidance on introduction of aquatic species as Service policy (Appendix A), and lays out supporting legal, partnership, ecological, outreach, and future-outlook principles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Nonindigenous species introductions**: [[Pacific Salmonids]] were successfully introduced to the [[Great Lakes]]; Eastern species — [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[American Shad]] — were introduced to Western U.S. waters and now support significant recreational fisheries.
- **Niche context of introductions**: Some nonindigenous species were introduced into *natural, healthy ecosystems*, where they directly competed with native species. Others were introduced into *altered habitats* (e.g., large Western/Midwestern reservoirs) where they occupied newly created ecological niches within communities already disrupted by:
  - Altered flow and temperature regimes
  - Degraded water quality
  - Habitat modification
- **Impacted native taxa**: [[Anadromous Salmonids]] in the Pacific Northwest and native [[Suckers (Catostomidae)]] and [[Minnows (Cyprinidae)]] in the Southwest have been particularly impacted by habitat alteration and species introductions — some populations are now near extinction and protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- **Ecosystem carrying capacity**: Aquatic ecosystems' ability to support or produce fish biomass is finite and directly a function of ecosystem quality (habitat, water quality/quantity, flow regime).
- **Risk of introduction**: Ecological risk associated with many nonindigenous species remains "generally not well understood"; new species/hybrid introductions can produce unanticipated impacts on native fish communities and should be ecologically justified prior to release.
- **Causal link between habitat alteration and invasion success**: The proliferation of nonindigenous species was, in many cases, enabled by habitat alteration and by recreational-fishing demand tied to reservoir/project purposes.

## Key Findings

1. **Dual mandate**: The Service commits to maintaining/restoring recreational fisheries *consistent with* conservation and recovery of native species — not as competing goals but as jointly managed objectives.
2. **Legal framework**: Management operates under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973, as amended), 50 CFR implementing regulations, Section 6 State cooperative grant agreements, and the [[Nonindigenous Aquatic Nuisance Species Prevention and Control Act]]. States hold primary management authority for most fish/wildlife; the Service holds specific federal legislative responsibilities.
3. **Adoption of AFS introduction guidance (Appendix A)**: The Service formally adopts the [[American Fisheries Society]] position (Kohler & Courtenay 1986) on introduction of aquatic species, which recommends a multi-stage evaluation protocol before any release:
   - Rationale
   - Search/comparison of candidate species
   - Preliminary impact assessment (on target ecosystems, game/food fishes, waterfowl, aquatic plants, public health)
   - Publicity and expert review
   - Experimental research in confined waters
   - Evaluation/recommendation with published results
   - Monitored introduction with published outcomes
   - Recommends international/national/regional oversight bodies with veto authority, prioritizing quality of introductions over quantity.
4. **Differentiated management emphasis**: Primary fishery management emphasis in *relatively unaltered* habitats should favor conservation/recovery of native fish; in *significantly altered* habitats, use of nonindigenous species remains a valid tool for sustaining recreational fisheries.
5. **Hatcheries**: Recognized as valuable management tools only when operated under approved fisheries management plans incorporating sound genetics, native fish conservation, disease control, and socio-economic objectives.
6. **Partnerships**: Effective conservation depends on trust-based partnerships among States, Tribes, Federal agencies, and private/recreational fishing organizations, including coordination with Federally recognized [[Native American Governments]] early in resource decision-making (per the Service's Native American Policy).
7. **Public outreach**: The Service commits to educating the public on causes of fishery/ecosystem degradation (loss of water quality/quantity) and to fostering public participation in identifying solutions.
8. **Future outlook**: Anticipated expansion of the National Wildlife Refuge System is expected to increase recreational fishing opportunities where compatible with refuge purposes and operational funding; the Service will pursue challenge grants and private funding to support this.
9. **Historical legacy**: ~150 years of cumulative habitat alteration, introductions, and management experimentation have created mixed native/nonindigenous aquatic communities nationwide that require ongoing, adaptive, collaborative management rather than a return to a purely "native-only" baseline.
