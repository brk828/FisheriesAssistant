# Introduction of Pond Smelt from Japan into California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Osmeridae

## Executive Summary
This 1962 California Fish and Game report documents an experimental introduction of the [[Pond Smelt]] (*Hypomesus olidus*), imported as eggs from Japan, into six California lakes and reservoirs between 1959 and 1961. The goal was to establish a self-sustaining forage fish population to improve food chains for [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) in waters with underutilized plankton resources. Eggs were air-shipped from [[Suwa Reservoir]], Japan, and planted in tributary streams of six test waters. By August 1961, only [[Freshwater Lagoon]] (Humboldt County) showed a robust, self-propagating smelt population, while other sites showed limited or no survival, illustrating the challenges of establishing introduced forage fish via egg transplantation.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: *Hypomesus olidus*, the [[Pond Smelt]] (also called freshwater smelt)
- **Maximum size**: Up to 5 inches under optimum conditions, though typically smaller
- **Recovered specimen**: A 4.3-inch individual collected from [[Dwinnell Reservoir]] in April 1961
- **Eggs**: Small, adhesive; adhere naturally to rocks and gravel in spawning streams (and to artificial palm fiber mats in hatchery propagation)
- **Fry**: Begin exogenous feeding at approximately 7 mm in length

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range**: Distributed along the North Pacific rim — as far south as [[San Francisco Bay]] on the North American coast and as far south as Japan on the Asian coast
- **Habitat preference**: Primarily a freshwater species, though tolerant of brackish water; ecological requirements closely parallel those of [[Rainbow Trout]], including similar preferred water temperatures
- **Spawning behavior**: Spring spawner in lake/reservoir tributaries; eggs attach to substrate (rocks, gravel) in flowing water
- **Incubation**: Hatching time ~12 days at 58°F
- **Diet**: Primarily [[Zooplankton]] consumer, functioning as a trophic link between plankton and piscivorous [[Salmonids]]
- **Life history**: Reaches sexual maturity at the end of first year; typically semelparous (does not survive to spawn a second time)
- **Trophic role**: Intended to fill a vacant forage-fish niche in California lakes/reservoirs where native plankton consumers were absent or inadequate, thereby strengthening the food chain supporting trout fisheries
- **Established precedent**: Successfully introduced into reservoirs in Japan as trout forage prior to the California effort

## Key Findings
- **Source material**: Approximately 3,600,000 eggs were air-shipped from Tokyo (originating at [[Suwa Reservoir]], ~70 miles east of Tokyo) to San Francisco on March 18 and 31, 1959; eggs were spawned artificially onto palm fiber mats and shipped unrefrigerated in plastic-lined fiberboard boxes
- **Egg mortality**: Significant losses occurred in transit due to drying, premature hatching, and bacterial decomposition, though enough survived for viable plants
- **Six introduction sites**: [[Dodge Reservoir]] (Lassen Co.), [[Dwinnell Reservoir]] (Siskiyou Co.), [[Freshwater Lagoon]] (Humboldt Co.), [[Spaulding Reservoir]] (Nevada Co.), [[Jenkinson Lake]]/Sly Park Reservoir (El Dorado Co.), and [[Big Bear Lake]] (San Bernardino Co.) — chosen for suitable ecology and feasibility of chemical eradication if smelt proved undesirable
- **Establishment success**: By August 1961, only [[Freshwater Lagoon]] showed confirmed self-propagating reproduction (first/second generation fish), based on seine sampling by Professor John DeWitt and Humboldt State College students; predation impact by trout at this site remained unquantified
- **Big Bear Lake**: Chemical treatment in fall 1960 to eradicate stunted [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*) and [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*) incidentally killed smelt, with dead specimens suggesting a population in at least the hundreds — indicating some prior establishment success before eradication
- **Dwinnell Reservoir**: Only a single 4.3-inch smelt was recovered (April 1961) despite observed hatching and fry drift from initial plants in tributary streams, suggesting poor survival
- **Overall outcome**: Egg-transplant method yielded inconsistent results across sites, with viable fry numbers presumed low in several plants; Freshwater Lagoon identified as the most promising source population for potential future transplantation to other California waters
- **Institutional collaboration**: Research Institute for Natural Resources (Tokyo) provided ecological data and facilitated procurement through a Tokyo commercial supplier
