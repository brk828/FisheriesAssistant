# Extraspecific Imposition of Social Grouping among Surgeonfishes (Acanthuridae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #acanthuridae

## Executive Summary

This 1974 paper by [[George W. Barlow]], reprinted from the *Journal of Zoology*, examines why the reef-grazing [[Manini]] (*Acanthurus triostegus*) switches between solitary feeding and dense schooling. Field work at [[Kealakekua Bay]], Hawaii, in July and August 1971 indicates that schooling in Hawaii is a tactic for reaching the substrate when it is held by territorial competitors, especially the [[Lavender Tang]] (*Acanthurus nigrofuscus*). In atoll reef flats, where competitors are absent, the same species school mainly as an antipredator response. The paper argues that extraspecific aggression can impose social grouping, a mechanism less often considered than predation in studies of fish schooling.

## Background & Description

- **Surgeonfishes (Acanthuridae):** Among the commonest fishes on coral reefs. Almost all are herbivorous, and reef grazers that rasp small algae from hard surfaces include the greatest number of species.
- **Manini (*Acanthurus triostegus*):** One of the most widely distributed surgeonfishes, found in the Red Sea, Indian Ocean, and tropical Western and Eastern Pacific. It is the least aggressive reef grazer, with the most weakly developed caudal knife, and is often attacked by congeners. Jones (1968) suggested it is probably at the bottom of any dominance order among *Acanthurus* species.
- **Lavender Tang (*Acanthurus nigrofuscus*):** A territorial reef grazer near or at the top of the extraspecific dominance hierarchy. Only full-grown adults hold territories where it is most abundant.
- **Study conditions:** Kealakekua Bay (12°28'N, 155°57'W). Waves 25 to 100 cm over shallow reefs, underwater visibility about 10 to 30 m, and water temperature 26° to 27°C. About 25 hours of underwater time were devoted to the research, mostly from the surface with supplementary SCUBA observations.
- **Habitat zones:** Lava shore with scoured basins at canyon mouths; reef tops at 1 to 2 m depth with abundant live brain corals; open reef at about 4 to 15 m; and steep outer reef face where surgeonfishes are rare.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Competition and territory:** Lavender tangs excluded Maninis from favoured habitats. Maninis were almost never encountered where Lavender tangs were most abundant (reef tops).
- **Attack rates:** Solitary Maninis received about one attack per minute (58 attacks in 12 Maninis over one hour of observation). Of these attacks, 69% came from Lavender tangs, 15.5% from other surgeonfishes, 12.1% from the damselfish *Pomacentrus jenkinsi*, and 3.4% from conspecifics.
- **Habitat partitioning:** Hawaii lacks shallow reef flats, so surgeonfishes mix considerably. In atolls with extensive reef flats (Line Islands, Marshall Islands), Maninis occur mainly and as the only surgeonfish in very shallow water.
- **Mixed-species assemblages:** Other herbivores joining Manini schools likely benefit similarly. Predatory wrasses feed on small animals stirred up by the grazing mass, forming a mutualistic feeding community.
- **Selfish herd:** Both in avoiding attacks and predation, each Manini takes refuge among its fellows, consistent with Hamilton's "selfish herd" model.

## Key Findings

1. **Two modes of existence:** Maninis are either solitary, spaced feeders or dense column-forming schools. Both can be observed on the same day at different reef locations.
2. **Abundance pattern (Table I):** Where Manini were sighted, they were 4.15 times as abundant as Lavender tangs (P<0.001). In regular-interval samples, the Manini-to-Lavender tang ratio was 0.21 (P<0.001). Regular-interval Lavender tang counts (18.2 per 100 m²) did not differ significantly from adjacent-Manini samples (21.7 per 100 m²), so the adjacent samples served as valid controls.
3. **Paired comparison (Table II):** Sight-Manini samples contained fewer Lavender tangs in 16 pairs, more in only one, and were tied in 18 (P<0.001). Manini were more abundant than Lavender tangs within these samples (P<0.001). Manini thus fed where Lavender tangs were less likely to occur.
4. **Attack rate on schools:** In five descents of a school of about 100 Maninis, attacks per Manini averaged about 0.12 per descent from one Lavender tang. In eight descents of a school of about 150 Maninis, the figure was about 0.22 per descent. Under worst-case assumptions (four Lavender tangs, 0.22 attacks), the estimate was 0.44 attacks per minute, about 30% less than the solitary-Manini mean of 0.65 per minute.
5. **Schooling as a response:** Schools descend en masse to swamp territorial Lavender tangs. Where Lavender tangs are at very high densities, this tactic fails and Maninis are absent. Where Lavender tangs are scarce, Maninis feed alone, and the risk of attack for solitary feeders is higher where schooling occurs.
6. **Other imposed grouping:** Pairs of Achilles tangs drove Manini schools away. Adult Lavender tangs formed Manini-like schools in response to territorial *Pomacentrus jenkinsi*. Subadult Lavender tangs schooled under attack from adults, and Yellow tangs formed schools of 200 to 500 fish.
7. **Schooling function:** Hawaiian schooling is primarily a response to dominant competitors; atoll reef-flat schooling is primarily antipredator. Standard antipredator interpretations of fish schooling (Cushing & Jones, 1968; Hamilton, 1970; Williams, 1964) may apply only secondarily in Hawaii.
8. **Conclusion:** Grouping in the Manini is determined by external factors. The solitary mode prevails when conditions permit, and extraspecific aggression can impose social grouping.
