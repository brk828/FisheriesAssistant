## Executive Summary
This paper (Rinne & Janisch, 1995) documents the extensive history of nonnative coldwater fish stocking in the Little Colorado and Black river drainages of Arizona's White Mountains and its detrimental effects on two native, threatened species: the [[Apache Trout]] and the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]]. Since the 1930s, nearly 70 million fish across 17 nonnative species—dominated by [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Brook Trout]]—were introduced into these watersheds. These introductions, combined with fish-toxicant stream renovations, baitfish introductions, and land management practices (timber harvest, livestock grazing), drove both native species toward endangerment through hybridization, competition, and predation. The paper outlines a shift in Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) policy beginning in the 1980s toward integrated native/nonnative fish management, habitat restoration, and stream renovation projects aimed at reestablishing native trout populations.

## Background & Description
- **[[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*)**: Native salmonid distinguished from introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] via morphometric analysis (Rinne and Minckley 1985); pure populations identified in streams lacking sustained rainbow trout stocking. Susceptible to hybridization with rainbow trout, producing F1 hybrids (Rinne et al. 1986) that compromise genetic purity.
- **[[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*)**: A small-bodied native cyprinid (minnow) endemic to the Little Colorado River drainage; classified as a rare, narrow-range endemic (Miller 1963).
- **Native trout complex**: Broader regional native trout taxa include [[Gila Trout]] (*O. gilae*) and various [[Cutthroat Trout]] subspecies (*O. clarki*), both historically sympatric with introduced salmonids in the Rocky Mountain/southwestern region.
- Stocked nonnative salmonids included nine documented species/strains: rainbow trout, brown trout, brook trout, Arctic grayling (*Thymallus arcticus*), kokanee/lacustrine sockeye salmon (*O. nerka*), coho salmon (*O. kisutch*), plus warmwater species (black crappie, bluegill, channel catfish, largemouth bass, northern pike, redear sunfish, threadfin shad, walleye, yellow perch).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historic native range**: Apache trout ranged throughout headwaters of the Black, White, and Little Colorado rivers; Little Colorado spinedace occurred throughout the Little Colorado River drainage (Miller 1963; Minckley and Carufel 1967).
- **Habitat type**: High-elevation montane coldwater streams and associated impounded lakes/reservoirs (e.g., Big Lake, Crescent Lake, Lee Valley Reservoir, Lyman Lake, Nelson Reservoir, Becker Lake, Colter Reservoir) within the [[White Mountains]] of east-central Arizona.
- **Interspecific interactions**:
  - Rainbow trout hybridize with and prey upon native Apache trout and Little Colorado spinedace, respectively.
  - Brook and brown trout reduce Apache trout distribution/abundance through competitive exclusion for food and habitat (Rinne et al. 1981).
  - Spinedace abundance is inversely related to rainbow and brook trout abundance (Blinn et al. 1993).
  - Other native taxa (speckled dace *Rhinichthys osculus*, desert sucker *Catostomus clarki*) co-occurred and were incidentally affected by toxicant-based fish removals.
  - Bullfrog tadpoles (*Rana catesbeiana*), introduced as bait, are opportunistic predators potentially impacting native fish fry/larvae (Clarkson and Devos 1986).
- **Anthropogenic habitat pressures**: Dam construction (beginning with Roosevelt Dam, 1911) altered natural flow regimes; by 1980, ~2,300 river kilometers in Arizona had been modified by impoundments. Timber harvest and unregulated livestock grazing (pre-Taylor Grazing Act 1934) degraded riparian vegetation and increased sedimentation in spawning/rearing habitat.

## Key Findings
- **Stocking magnitude**: Since the 1930s, ~61 million nonnative sport fish stocked into White Mountain lakes; ~8 million more into streams/tributaries. Combined lake and stream introductions total ~41 million fish into the Black River system and ~28 million into the Little Colorado River system.
- **Species composition of stocking**: Rainbow trout comprised 60% of all stocked individuals, followed by cutthroat trout (12%), brook trout (7%), and brown trout (3%).
- **Legal status**: Both Apache trout and Little Colorado spinedace are listed as **Threatened** under the U.S. Endangered Species Act of 1973.
- **Toxicant use**: Rotenone and toxaphene were used extensively for stream/lake renovations; 135 km of streams treated with fish toxicants during 1958–1968 alone. A 1950s toxaphene treatment of Lyman and Becker lakes killed Little Colorado spinedace downstream, affecting ~23 km of prime habitat between Springerville and Lyman Lake.
- **Restoration efforts (Table 4)**: 47.18 km of stream renovated to date to reestablish Apache trout (e.g., Bear Wallow Creek, Lee Valley Creek, Home Creek, Wildcat Creek, Hayground Creek); an additional 49.8 km proposed (West Fork Black River, Thompson Creek, Burro Creek, Stinky Creek, Snake Creek, Bear Wallow Creek).
- **Policy shifts**:
  - 1982: AGFD policy banned stocking of any additional nonnative species and prohibited transplanting species into watersheds where they do not currently exist.
  - 1984: Native fish species formally incorporated into AGFD's sport-fish management program.
  - All Apache trout reintroduction stock are first-generation progeny of pure-strain cultured fish from the type locality (East Fork of the White River).
- **Land acquisitions (1993)**: U.S. Forest Service acquired a 2.4-km reach of Nutrioso Creek (critical spinedace habitat) upstream of Nelson Reservoir; AGFD acquired 3.2 km of the Little Colorado River near Springerville and the Rudd Creek watershed (3 km), securing habitat for both native species.
- **Socioeconomic context**: Trout fishing represents a major economic driver—over 130,000 anglers target trout in Arizona; 380,000 anglers overall contribute an estimated $355–561 million annually to the state economy. In 1988, over 5 million trout were stocked at a cost of US$1.2 million.
- **Public sentiment**: Nearly two-thirds of Arizona residents supported prioritizing native fish management even if it meant restricting nonnative trout stocking (Behavior Research Center 1992); 26% of anglers favored maximal native fish preservation efforts.
- **Future management strategy**: Proposed use of spatial separation (natural/artificial barriers) between native and nonnative fish populations, non-reproducing or genetically altered nonnative strains (e.g., sterile rainbow trout, Arctic grayling, kokanee) for continued sport fisheries, and expanded use of Apache trout (and potentially [[Gila Trout]]) as sport species to build angler-driven conservation support.
- **Core tension**: Restoration of the non-recreational [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] is identified as a more difficult conservation test than Apache trout restoration, given the latter's dual status as both a threatened native and a potential sport fish.
