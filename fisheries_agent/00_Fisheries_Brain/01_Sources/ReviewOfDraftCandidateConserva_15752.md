## Executive Summary

This document is **not a species-specific scientific paper** but a 1993 draft policy memorandum from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] establishing guidance for **Candidate Conservation** under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). It addresses the conservation of species — including fish, wildlife, and plants — that are official "candidates" for listing as threatened or endangered but have not yet been formally listed. The guidance outlines the rationale, legal authorities, priority-setting framework, and mechanisms (particularly **Conservation Agreements**) intended to stabilize or improve the status of candidate species early enough to reduce or eliminate the need for formal ESA listing. At the time of writing, approximately **3,600 Federal candidate species** were recognized, underscoring the scale of the conservation challenge and the need for proactive, partnership-based management before species reach critical status.

No morphological, taxonomic, or population-specific fish data are present in this text; the document is purely administrative/regulatory in nature, applicable across all taxa including [[Fish and Wildlife Conservation]] categories.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While no specific habitat or niche data for individual species are provided, the document establishes an **ecosystem-based conservation framework** with the following ecological principles:

- **Prelisting recovery concept**: Conservation action taken *before* a species is formally listed is framed as ecologically and economically preferable, since "by the time species are listed, the ecological situation is critical and available recovery options are limited."
- **Ecosystem management emphasis**: The guidance promotes management across a "broad land base" to reduce the need for individual species listings — an early articulation of ecosystem-level conservation strategy, relevant to aquatic systems supporting candidate fish species.
- **Category 1 vs. Category 2 species**: Candidate species were ranked by [[Listing Priority System|listing priority]], with Category 1 species (facing more substantiated threats) receiving priority for conservation funding and action over Category 2 species (data-deficient or lower documented threat).
- **Habitat-based prioritization criteria** for effective conservation agreements included:
  - Habitat controlled by a small number of land/water managers
  - Well-understood relationship between habitat management and species persistence
  - Habitat that remains relatively intact
  - Conservation actions involving *cessation* of harmful activity rather than costly restoration
  - Availability of staff and funding to implement agreements

## Key Findings

- **Purpose of Candidate Conservation**: To reduce threats to candidate species and their ecosystems sufficiently to make ESA listing unnecessary, or to stabilize declining species while listing of higher-priority species proceeds.
- **Legal Authorities Leveraged**:
  - **Section 6** — grants to States for candidate species conservation activities (post-1988 ESA Amendments).
  - **Section 7(a)(1)** — requires the Secretary of the Interior to use existing authorities to further ESA purposes, interpreted to support candidate conservation.
  - **Section 7(a)(2)** — technically applies only to listed species, but creates an indirect "umbrella of protection" for co-occurring candidates.
  - **Section 10** — Habitat Conservation Plans (HCPs) for listed species create secondary opportunities to conserve associated candidate species.
- **Seven Categories of Conservation Actions** identified:
  1. Habitat protection (fencing, land acquisition, access control)
  2. Habitat management/restoration (stream rehabilitation, erosion control, prescribed burns, water management changes — directly relevant to [[Freshwater Fish Habitat Restoration]])
  3. Species management (anti-overharvest measures, disease control, law enforcement, reintroduction of extirpated populations)
  4. Education and information transfer
  5. Use of existing legal/economic incentives (e.g., State conservation easements, tax breaks)
  6. Interagency and stakeholder coordination (including [[Bureau of Land Management]] and [[U.S. Forest Service]] policy alignment)
  7. Research (taxonomic, genetic, and habitat characterization studies)
- **Funding Mechanism**: Beginning FY1994, a dedicated **50:50 cost-sharing fund** between the Service and non-Federal entities was established specifically to support Conservation Agreements aimed at eliminating the need to list candidate species.
- **Conservation Agreements**:
  - Function similarly to a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU)/Memorandum of Agreement (MOA) but are specifically termed "Conservation Agreements" for candidate species work.
  - Must include explicit milestones, funding mechanisms, annual review and reporting requirements, and administrative record-keeping.
  - Default duration is **3 years**, with mandatory reviews at 12 and 24 months and a final decision (modify/renew/terminate) by month 36.
  - If threats remain unresolved despite an agreement, the Service retains authority to pursue proposed or **emergency listing** under Section 4 of the ESA.
  - All signed agreements must be reported in the **Endangered Species Technical Bulletin**, with press releases and conservation awards encouraged, particularly for private landowners.
- **Evaluation and Tracking**: Field offices are required to maintain record-keeping systems tracking monitoring results and compliance, with highlights reported to the Director for inclusion in annual Congressional budget justifications — linking candidate conservation performance directly to [[Endangered Species Program]] funding.
- **Strategic Rationale**: Early, voluntary, and cooperative conservation action is positioned as reducing regulatory conflict, retaining management flexibility, and lowering long-term recovery costs compared to post-listing intervention — a foundational rationale still cited in modern [[Species at Risk Conservation Agreements]] frameworks (e.g., Candidate Conservation Agreements, CCAs, and CCAAs).
