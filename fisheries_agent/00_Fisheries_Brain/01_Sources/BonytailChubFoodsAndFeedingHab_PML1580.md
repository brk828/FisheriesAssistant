# Diet and Feeding Ecology of Bonytail Chub in Cibola High Levee Pond, Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report presents part of an ongoing study of the diet of [[Bonytail Chub]] (*[[Gila Elegans]]*) in the [[Cibola High Levee Pond]], a small, reclaimed remnant of the [[Lower Colorado River]] on the [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]]. Using non-lethal stomach flushing on 72 fish collected at evening and night in 2003 and 2004, the authors examined food use by fish size and feeding time. Empty stomachs were more common in evening samples (about 33%) than at night, and gut contents were dominated by amorphous organic matter, invertebrates, and plant material. Invertebrate matter rose and plant matter fell with fish size. The authors conclude that the pond's bonytail feed mainly at night, consistent with [[Telemetry Studies]] showing nocturnal activity. The study also recorded the first wild-habitat record of [[Asian Tapeworm]] in bonytail from the lower Colorado River.

## Background & Description

The [[Bonytail Chub]] is a native cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The Cibola High Levee Pond is a remnant of the lower Colorado River channel, lying between the river and inland high levees within the [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] in La Paz County, Arizona, and Imperial County, California. The pond was reclaimed to remove non-native fishes and was then restocked with native species. It has served both management and research roles, as documented in earlier work by [[LaBarbara and Minckley 1999]], [[Marsh 2000]], and [[Mueller et al. 2003]].

The study had three goals:

1. Document the food items used by bonytail.
2. Examine food use as a function of fish size.
3. Investigate temporal aspects of feeding (day versus night).

### Methods

- **Sample collection:** A combined sample of 72 bonytail was taken with [[Trammel Net]] sets on 7 May 2003 and 4–5 May 2004. Nets were set to sample two feeding periods: evening (1800–2400 hrs) and night (0100–0545 hrs). A distinct size class was sampled each year:
  - 2003: total length above 376 mm (n = 28)
  - 2004: total length below 375 mm (n = 44)
- **Handling:** Fish were held briefly in a floating live car, then measured for total length (nearest mm) and weighed (nearest gram).
- **Stomach flushing:** Stomach and intestinal (GI) contents were removed non-lethally with a [[Stomach Flushing Method]]. A one-way rubber squeeze bulb and tubing of several sizes were used, matched to fish gape size, to flush contents through the esophagus with clear site water into a sieve. Fish with empty tracts were noted. Samples were preserved in 10% formalin and later transferred to 70% [[Ethanol]].
- **Laboratory analysis:** Samples were washed through a 500 µm sieve, and solids were wet-weighed to 0.001 g. Contents were sorted under a stereo-microscope and the percent of total quantity estimated for six categories: amorphous organic matter (AOM), inorganic matter, plant, fish, invertebrate, and other. Prey were identified to family level where possible.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Bonytail in the Cibola pond fed on benthic and drifting aquatic invertebrates and terrestrial insects, a pattern similar to observations from natural streams. Telemetry at the pond showed that adults are active at night and spend daylight hours resting under cover among large downed material. The authors link this to the high proportion of empty stomachs in evening samples and suggest that intense feeding occurred at night.

Previous diet studies of related fishes include:

- Stream bonytail and [[Roundtail Chub]] in Utah, which ate mostly chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs when small, shifting to floating terrestrial insects as they grew ([[Vanicek and Kramer 1969]]).
- Adult bonytail in the [[Green River]], which fed mostly on terrestrial insects taken from the surface, with no evidence of piscivory.
- Bonytail in [[Lake Mohave]], which preyed on small (64 mm TL) rainbow trout fingerlings ([[Wagner 1955]]).
- Bonytail in [[Lake Mead]] and other reservoirs, which ate plankton, insects, algae, and organic detritus ([[Jonez and Sumner 1954]]).

Asian tapeworm (*[[Bothriocephalus acheilognathi]]*) had previously been reported in [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) from the Little Colorado River and Grand Canyon. The present study is the first record of it in wild bonytail from the lower Colorado River. The authors note that the source of infection is unknown and could involve hatchery stocks or illegally moved fish. They recommend monitoring the species in bonytail and other fishes.

## Key Findings

### Sample Characteristics

- Of the 2003 sample (n = 28), total length ranged from about 370 to 500 mm and weight from 305 to 1,136 g (mean weight 565 g).
- Of the 2004 sample (n = 44), total length ranged from 271 to 509 mm (mean 325 mm) and weight from 129 to 710 g (mean 222 g).
- Length–weight relationships formed a continuum from smaller to larger fish, with some variation among the larger individuals.

### Empty Stomachs and Feeding Time

- 13 of 72 GI tracts (about 18%) were empty.
- The frequency of empty tracts was more than four times higher in evening samples (about 33%) than at night. The night value is partly illegible in the scan and appears to be about 7%.
- The composition of stomach contents was similar between day and night feeding periods.

### Diet Composition

- **Amorphous organic matter (AOM):** The dominant material, a brownish, unidentifiable "gruel." It likely included stomach lining, mucus, and heavily digested food.
- **Inorganic matter:** Pebbles, rock, sand grains, and insect larval cases (caddisflies, including Hydroptilidae) built from sand and pebbles.
- **Plant matter:** Various aquatic macrophytes.
- **Fish:** Fish remains were found in 6 of 72 samples (8%), all from fish larger than 425 mm.
- **Invertebrates:** Microcrustaceans (copepods, ostracods, *Daphnia*), crayfish, Corbiculidae (clams), dipteran larvae and adults, Notonectidae (backswimmers), and Odonata nymphs and adults.
- **Parasites:** One specimen of Asian tapeworm was positively identified. Tapeworm proglottids, presumably of the same species, were found in 1 of 72 samples (about 1%).
- **Other:** Both identifiable (bullfrog, *[[Rana Catesbeiana]]*) and unidentifiable vertebrate remains.

### Size-Related Diet Shifts

- Plant matter decreased and invertebrate matter increased as fish size increased.
- Gut sample weight had no clear linear relationship with fish body weight or total length.
- Mean stomach sample weight was 1.544 g, with a standard deviation of 2.642 g. The range was from about 0.06 g to a higher value that is illegible in the scan.

### Methodological Limitations

The authors identify several sources of uncertainty:

1. **Staged net sets:** On some occasions nets were run in stages, so early catches were held in a live car for a longer period before processing. Digestion time may have affected gut contents, and the effect on food consumption results is unknown.
2. **No control samples:** No controls were available to test the [[Stomach Flushing Method]] against surgical extraction. Earlier studies on [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]] suggest flushing is nearly 100% effective, and the authors assume it is similarly effective for bonytail.
3. **Unexpected weight relationship:** A linear relationship between fish weight and GI sample size was not observed. This may reflect variation in the time between capture and processing.
