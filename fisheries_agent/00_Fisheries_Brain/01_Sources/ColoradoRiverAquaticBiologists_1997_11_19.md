# Colorado River Aquatic Biologists Annual Meeting — 1997 Minutes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Catostomidae #river-management #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This document summarizes the January 8–9, 1997 annual meeting of the [[Colorado River Aquatic Biologists]] in Laughlin, Nevada, attended by ~40 biologists from five states. The meeting covered river-wide reporting from the Mexican border upstream through [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Havasu]], and [[Lake Powell]] to the Grand Canyon reach below [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Key themes included ongoing [[Razorback Sucker]] recovery efforts (radio and sonic tagging), management tension between native fish recovery and popular non-native sport fisheries ([[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]]), the ecological aftermath of the 1996 experimental flood release from Glen Canyon Dam, selenium and heavy-metal contaminant monitoring, and a proposed multi-million dollar selective withdrawal (temperature control) structure at Glen Canyon Dam. The document is primarily a management and monitoring synthesis rather than a controlled scientific study, reflecting a multi-agency, multi-state collaborative framework for Colorado River fisheries.

## Background & Description

While this is a management minutes document rather than a morphological study, several taxon-specific notes emerged:

- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Native catostomid distinguished by a pronounced dorsal keel ("razorback"); juveniles/wild-recruited individuals were distinguished from hatchery releases via size and capture context. Larval razorbacks were captured, reared, and released — indicating early developmental stages are identifiable and viable for rearing programs.
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]**: Found in low numbers below [[Davis Dam]]; population apparently derived from fish transplanted from the Paria River, suggesting a genetically traceable translocated lineage persisting in the lower river.
- **[[Flathead Catfish]]**: Behaviorally and physiologically distinct from [[Largemouth Bass]] in electrofishing response — requires a special low-amperage electrofishing setting to effectively "turn" (stun) individuals, implying differing electroreceptive/neuromuscular thresholds compared to centrarchids.
- **[[Common Carp]]**: Heavy metals study identified an endocrine imbalance in the sampled population, tentatively linked to selenium or other metal bioaccumulation — an internal physiological/endocrinological finding rather than gross morphology.
- **[[Striped Bass]]** (Lake Powell): Individuals were reported in "excellent condition," supporting an unprecedented young-of-year (YOY) recruitment event in 1996.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat partitioning**: Radio-tagged [[Razorback Sucker]] preferentially used backwater habitats, while [[Flathead Catfish]] were most frequently detected in the main channel — indicating clear niche segregation between native and invasive benthic/pelagic species in the lower Colorado River.
- **Non-native establishment and spread**: [[Tilapia]] were documented in [[Lake Havasu]] and [[Lake Mead]], likely originating from [[Alamo Lake]] and transported downstream via the Bill Williams River arm. [[Smallmouth Bass]] appeared in Lake Mead, likely via angler live-well introductions — an anthropogenic vector for range expansion.
- **Predator-prey dynamics**: In [[Lake Powell]], an unprecedented 1996 YOY striped bass class was enabled by high forage (shad) availability, and this cohort was expected to cause a collapse of the pelagic shad population through intense predation, illustrating rapid trophic cascade potential in reservoir systems.
- **Flood-driven ecosystem disturbance and recovery** (1996 Glen Canyon Dam experimental flood, Grand Canyon reach):
  - Macroinvertebrates (esp. *[[Gammarus]]*) were flushed downstream but recolonized to pre-flood densities as macrophytes recovered.
  - Macrophyte succession: scoured vegetation was recolonized first by *Potamogeton*, returning eventually to pre-flood dominance by *[[Chara]]* — a shift from the *[[Cladophora]]*-dominated regime typical of 1970s peaking-flow operations.
  - Increased plankton abundance was inversely correlated with macrophyte cover.
  - Native and sport fish showed resilience to flood disturbance, with only minor downstream displacement of trout.
  - Small-bodied non-native fish (*[[Fathead Minnow]]*, *[[Red Shiner]]*) declined in abundance post-flood, suggesting flow disturbance disproportionately impacts smaller exotic competitors relative to natives and larger sport fish.
- **Selenium and contaminant ecology**: Selenium was documented across most Colorado River fish species at sub-threshold levels for public health concern; a proposed link between selenium exposure and reduced trout reproduction in the [[Lee's Ferry]] reach was refuted, since ~80% of the local trout population is sustained by natural reproduction.
- **Thermal regime and dam operations**: Discussion of proposed selective withdrawal structures at Glen Canyon Dam (to warm hypolimnetic release water by 5–7°C at the dam, up to 18°C some 240 miles downstream) raised concerns that eliminating the current cold-water barrier could facilitate upstream invasion of warm-water non-native competitors and reduce suppression of parasites/tapeworms currently limited by cold discharge temperatures.

## Key Findings

- **Razorback Sucker recovery progress**: Sonic and radio telemetry studies in Lake Mead (led by [[Paul Holden]]/BioWest) captured 12 wild razorback suckers, with 8 surgically implanted with sonic tags; some relatively young wild fish suggest possible natural recruitment in the lake population. PIT tagging of wild-caught individuals in Lake Mead is ongoing.
- **Large-scale habitat restoration**: The multimillion-dollar [[Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Program]] (partly funded by Anglers United) was ~30% complete, including boat ramps, docks, and artificial cover structures; a planned stocking of 10,000–15,000 razorback suckers was part of this effort, though the direct benefit of artificial structure to sport fish recruitment remained unconfirmed (bass fry were observed using plastic cover extensively).
- **Tagging policy debate**: Cost-benefit discussions on PIT-tagging stocked native fish concluded that large-scale stockings (e.g., 15,000 razorbacks into Lake Havasu) may be cost-prohibitive to individually tag; alternative approaches proposed include treating any post-extirpation fish detections as presumptively hatchery-origin unless YOY are found during a no-stocking year (proof of natural recruitment).
- **Hydroacoustic survey results** ([[Gordon Mueller]], USGS): Dual-beam sonar surveys of Lake Powell and Lake Mead (ground-truthed with plankton sampling and vertical gill nets) found Lake Powell had unusually high shad abundance while Lake Mead was largely shad-depleted except in Vegas Wash — setting a baseline expected to shift dramatically due to 1996 striped bass predation pressure.
- **Flood management insight**: The 1996 Glen Canyon Dam flood release produced immediate, dramatic geomorphic benefits (new beaches/backwaters), though beach erosion and backwater loss resumed under subsequent steady high flows; similar floods are estimated to recur naturally/operationally every 7–10 years.
- **Dam infrastructure economics**: BOR was pursuing ~$160 million in funding for selective withdrawal structures at Glen Canyon Dam per a Record of Decision on dam operations; many biologists expressed concern about committing large capital to an ecologically uncertain experiment, favoring cautious, evidence-based implementation (e.g., testing concepts first at [[Hoover Dam]], which already has unused surface-release capacity).
- **Lake Powell fishery status**: A banner year for water levels, cover, and forage supported strong sport fish populations; a cooperative Lake Powell fish management plan was finalized, formally prioritizing native and sport species in habitats most suited to each.
- **Contaminant monitoring**: Selenium levels in Colorado River fishes remained below public-health advisory thresholds even as some AZ hospital studies promoted dietary selenium supplementation (200 μg/day) as a potential cancer-prevention measure — highlighting a public-health vs. ecological-risk contrast. Endocrine imbalance detected in [[Common Carp]] warrants further heavy-metals research.
- **Institutional coordination**: The meeting closed with updates on the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] and the native "big river fishes" recovery teams, reflecting ongoing multi-state, multi-agency coordination on Colorado River native fish recovery.
