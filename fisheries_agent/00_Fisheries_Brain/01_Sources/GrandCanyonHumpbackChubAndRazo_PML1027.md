# Grand Canyon Humpback Chub and Razorback Sucker Monitoring Project: 1990 Scope of Work

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1990 proposal from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) to monitor and research two endangered native fishes, the [[Humpback Chub]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]], in the Colorado River and [[Little Colorado River]] within [[Grand Canyon National Park]], Arizona. It was submitted to the National Park Service and the Navajo Fish and Wildlife office by Dean A. Hendrickson and Dennis K. Kirby. The proposal argues that [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations, especially hydropeaking flows, threaten the remaining native fish populations, and that the Grand Canyon humpback chub population needs better demographic, age, and movement data for management and Section 7 consultation. Fieldwork was proposed for roughly 21 to 45 days between April 15 and July 15, 1990, with an overall requested window of April 15 to December 1990.

## Background & Description

**Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*)** was formally described in 1946 but was known to early river runners, who occasionally ate it. Historically it ranged through the Colorado, Green, Yampa, and White rivers and their tributaries. It is unique to the Colorado River Basin and was listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1967. Post-Glen Canyon Dam collections come from the mainstream, Little Colorado River, Bright Angel Creek, Shinumo Creek, and Havasu Creek. Successful reproduction appears concentrated in the Little Colorado River.

**Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)** is also an endangered species of the Colorado River system. A single individual was taken at the mouth of the Little Colorado River. It was first identified as a possible hybrid with *Catostomus latipinnis* (flannelmouth sucker), but morphological and scale data later indicated no introgression. This was a female measuring 515 mm total length (ASU catalog #12161), and its characteristics were confirmed by [[Dr. W. L. Minckley]].

The proposal also draws on prior work, including the Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (1984-1987), the [[Biological Assessment]] and draft Conservation Measures, and the [[Humpback Chub Recovery Plan]] (USFWS 1979, 1989 draft).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dam effects:** Glen Canyon Dam operation has produced adverse effects on Colorado River natural resources. Of the native fish species in Grand Canyon, four have been extirpated and a fifth (humpback chub) is endangered. Causes include altered natural flow regimes, reduced water temperature, loss of spawning and rearing habitat, restricted movement, and isolation from other populations.
- **Fluctuating flows:** Hydropower-driven fluctuating flows may affect humpback chub during spawning and post-spawning periods and in mainstream backwaters. The extent of these impacts is not yet determined.
- **Backwaters:** Backwaters are important rearing habitats for immature humpback chub, but little is known about how they respond to mainstream flows.
- **Temperature and recruitment:** Mainstream water temperatures are thought to preclude successful natural reproduction, so reproduction has probably been restricted to tributaries, while larval and young fish of the year are found in the Little Colorado River.
- **Growth and age:** Previous studies show individuals reach sexual maturity at 250-300 mm body length and grow slowly.

## Key Findings

**Age and growth**
- A single otolith from a fish estimated at 22 years old was analyzed by microprobe at 260 points along a focus-to-edge transect. Elemental chemistry is being used to test whether otolith chemistry can reconstruct individual movement between the Little Colorado River and mainstream habitats. Preliminary data show Little Colorado River chemistry is very different from the mainstream.
- A razorback sucker otolith was estimated at 16 to 22 years, implying that natural reproduction and recruitment occurred after the closure of both Hoover and Glen Canyon dams, probably in tributary habitat.
- Preliminary age estimates from otoliths and scales were compiled for 50 specimens collected in 1989.

**Tagging**
- Previous studies reported 99% retention and survival of fish with injected tags, and PIT tags were judged less disruptive and better retained than Carlin or other ring tags.
- The 1990 plan combines coelomic PIT tags with external Carlin tags to compare relative growth and mortality rates between tag types.

**Monitoring plan (tasks)**
1. Determine age-size relationships and growth history using scales, otoliths, and recaptures of previously marked fish.
2. Use otolith chemistry to reconstruct movement and birth sites.
3. Observe humpback chub schooling, spawning, and predator response in the Little Colorado River, using shoreline observation and snorkeling.
4. Quantitatively sample humpback chub and other fishes in mainstream and Little Colorado River backwaters, along with plankton and benthos, and water-quality variables.
5. Install a larval fish drift net in the confluence region to measure larval drift into the mainstream.
6. Remove live humpback chub or reproductive material for transport to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], to support a refugium population.
7. Track movement of tagged fish into and out of backwaters during flow fluctuations.

**Logistics and timing**
- Mainstream work would use a river party launching at Lee's Ferry, joining the Little Colorado River group for about 5 days, then continuing to Diamond Creek.
- Razorback sucker captures would be photographed, tagged, and released unharmed. Incidental mortalities would be preserved for museum collections.
- The initial six personnel would descend the Hance/Navajo trail on April 29, depending on Little Colorado River discharge.

**Conclusions**
- Existing knowledge is insufficient to answer key questions about humpback chub ecological requirements and the effects of Glen Canyon Dam operations.
- The proposed work is intended to support the draft Conservation Measures in the Section 7 Biological Assessment.
