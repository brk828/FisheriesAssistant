# The Use of Copper Sulphate to Increase Fyke Net Catches

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #centrarchidae #percidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary
This study by the [[Massachusetts Division of Fisheries and Game]] investigates the use of **copper sulphate (CuSO₄)**, traditionally known as an algacide and fish poison, as a non-lethal irritant to increase [[Fyke Net]] catch efficiency during pond thinning operations. Field trials conducted in 1952–1954 across five Massachusetts water bodies — [[Lake Quannopowitt]], [[Jordan Pond]], [[Duck Pond]], [[Massapoag Pond]], and [[Wedge Pond]] — demonstrated that sub-lethal concentrations of CuSO₄ (0.05–1.0 ppm) reliably triggered increased fish movement, leading to dramatically higher fyke net yields across multiple species including [[White Perch]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]], and bullheads. The technique was subsequently adopted as a standard operational tool in Massachusetts pond management by 1954, with negligible cost and no observed long-term harm to fish populations or productivity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The species examined occupy the typical warm-water pond and lake community structure of eutrophic, soft-water Massachusetts habitats:

- **[[Lake Quannopowitt]]** (Wakefield, MA): A productive, historically CuSO₄-treated lake (annual algacide treatments since 1927 at 0.12–1.0 ppm) dominated by [[White Perch]], with substantial [[Black Crappie]] and sunfish populations.
- **[[Jordan Pond]]** (Shrewsbury, MA, 21 acres): High populations of [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[White Perch]], and [[Yellow Perch]].
- **[[Duck Pond]]** (Groton, MA, 52 acres, mean depth 6 ft): Extensive aquatic weed growth; dominated by bullheads, chubsuckers, and [[Yellow Perch]].
- **[[Massapoag Pond]]** (Lunenburg, MA, 38 acres): Sunfishes, brown and yellow bullheads, and yellow perch comprised 86% of total catch.
- **[[Wedge Pond]]** (Winchester, MA, 21 acres): Sunfish-dominated (81% of catch), with minor populations of chubsuckers, white suckers, golden shiners, black crappie, yellow perch, brown bullhead, chain pickerel, and largemouth bass.

**Mechanism of Action**: Copper sulphate is understood not as a true systemic toxicant at sub-lethal concentrations, but as a physical/chemical irritant. Copper salts bind with mucous secreted by skin, mouth, and gill tissues, forming an insoluble precipitate. At lethal concentrations this precipitate coats the gills, causing respiratory failure; at sub-lethal concentrations, increased mucous secretion continuously sloughs off the precipitate, producing irritation that stimulates fish movement without causing mortality (Ellis 1937).

**Water Chemistry Context**: Massachusetts waters in this study were characterized as soft-water systems with pH and hardness ranges typical of the region, reducing the likelihood of acute toxicity at the tested concentrations (≤0.5 ppm).

**Carrying Capacity Context**: Broader Massachusetts pond surveys (142 reclaimed ponds, 1,132 acres total) found fish carrying capacities ranging from 17–280 lbs/acre, with 95% of ponds in the 30–140 lbs/acre range — providing ecological context for the productivity of the study ponds.

## Key Findings

- **Increased Total Catch**: At Lake Quannopowitt, a single application of 1.0 ppm CuSO₄ increased daily catch from 11 lbs/net to 173 lbs/net (peak), representing an estimated 36–45% increase in total harvest for the operation.
- **Species-Specific Post-Treatment Harvest**: Species already less available in nets prior to treatment ([[Black Crappie]], [[Bluegill]]/[[Pumpkinseed]]) showed the largest relative increases post-treatment (94.1% and 88.0% of their total catch, respectively, taken after CuSO₄ application).
- **Effective at Very Low Concentrations**: Increased catches were achieved with cumulative concentrations as low as 0.05–0.125 ppm across the four 1953 experimental ponds, far below classically lethal thresholds.
- **Dramatic Yield Multipliers**:
  - [[Wedge Pond]]: 0.10 ppm application increased poundage 4.5× over the previous 24-hour period.
  - [[Duck Pond]]: 0.05 ppm application produced a 12-fold increase in poundage harvested, notably demonstrating effectiveness for **bullhead** populations.
  - Both [[Jordan Pond]] and [[Wedge Pond]] yielded over 125 lbs/acre removed during netting operations.
- **Temporal Response Pattern**: Netting success dropped sharply approximately 24 hours after CuSO₄ application regardless of concentration used; this decline could be counteracted via repeated applications of minimal concentrations on successive days.
- **No Differential Species Response**: All species tested responded to treatment; differences in post-treatment catch composition reflected pre-existing species availability rather than differential physiological sensitivity to copper sulphate.
- **Application Method Matters**: Full-surface or all-area-adjacent spraying produced the best results; marginal (band) spraying (as tested in [[Massapoag Pond]]) did not preferentially enhance sunfish catches as hypothesized.
- **No Evidence of Long-Term Harm**: Despite near-annual copper sulphate treatment of Lake Quannopowitt since 1927, the lake remained above-average in productivity for Massachusetts waters. Citing [[Moyle 1949]], the authors note treated Minnesota lakes showed equal or higher commercial and angler fishing yields compared to untreated lakes over a 24-year period.
- **Operational Efficiency & Cost**: By 1954, Massachusetts adopted a standard operational concentration of 0.15 ppm using gasoline-powered centrifugal pumps and rotenone-style spray booms. Treatment cost was approximately **4 cents per acre-foot** (commercial CuSO₄ purchased at 10.7 cents/lb), with minimal time investment relative to catch gains.
- **Broader Applications Suggested**: The authors propose potential extensions of this technique to gill net surveys, farm-pond bluegill trapping, and rapid population estimation studies, given CuSO₄'s effectiveness at concentrations below lethal thresholds for most planktonic organisms.
