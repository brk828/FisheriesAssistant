# Feeding Dynamics, Nitrogen Budgets, and Ecosystem Role of Longfin Dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), an omnivorous cyprinid of Sonoran Desert streams, feeds, how much it ingests daily, and how its nitrogen budget affects the stream ecosystem. Feeding was measured in [[Sycamore Creek]] on two dates, 24 May and 3 November 1983, when food quality differed but total algal and invertebrate standing crops were similar. The fish ate mainly insects (chiefly [[Baetid Mayflies]]) when mayflies were abundant and mainly algae when they were scarce. Daily dry-mass ingestion was roughly twice as high in November, but daily nitrogen ingestion was similar on both dates. The fish's excretion of ammonia, which primary producers can use directly, is presented as a significant source of recycled nitrogen in this nitrogen-limited stream.

## Background & Description

*Agosia chrysogaster* is the most common fish in Sycamore Creek. It is native to the Sonoran Desert in Arizona and northern Mexico and is found in uniform coarse sand to fine gravel runs, shallow pools, and, less commonly, cobble-boulder riffles. Introduced minnows (*Notropis lutrensis* and *Pimephales promelas*) are minor but persistent members of the fauna. Earlier work on the species includes diel feeding chronologies by [[Fisher Et Al. 1981]] and feeding interrelationships in Aravaipa Creek by [[Schreiber And Minckley 1981]].

The study was part of a larger investigation of consumer roles in [[Nitrogen Cycling]] in desert streams (Grimm 1985). Its objectives were to assess the importance of these fish to stream nitrogen dynamics by measuring:

- food habits
- diel feeding periodicity
- daily dry-mass and nitrogen ingestion
- individual and population nitrogen budgets

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Food quality versus quantity:** Chlorophyll and invertebrate standing crops were similar in the two months. What changed was composition. *[[Cladophora Glomerata]]* was present in May and absent in November, and mayflies made up a much smaller share of invertebrate biomass in November.
- **Algal food quality:** Algae are nitrogen-poor. Mean N content was 1.82% of dry mass for diatoms, 2.47% for filamentous green algae, and 8.78% for insects. Thick-walled *Cladophora* may be hard to digest unless it carries diatom epiphytes, which may explain why the fish avoided it.
- **Nitrogen limitation:** The stream is nitrogen-limited ([[Nitrogen Limitation]]; Grimm & Fisher 1986), so nutrients released by consumers have disproportionate value to primary producers.
- **Chironomids:** Chironomids were abundant but were not a major food item, possibly because of their interstitial habitat.
- **Temperature and size effects:** Gut evacuation rates are temperature-sensitive, so ingestion estimates apply to the 20–25 °C range and body sizes sampled.

## Key Findings

**1. Opportunistic omnivory.** The fish preferred mayflies but switched to algae when mayflies were scarce. In May, diatoms, filamentous Chlorophyta, and insects made up 30%, 14%, and 56% of contents of fish with food. In November, insects were 53% of contents and algae made up 21% diatoms plus 26% filamentous chlorophytes. The source text describes the November insect share as "significantly higher" than May, which conflicts with the May value of 56% reported in the same passage.

**2. Individual feeding patterns.** Many fish ate only one food type. Insects were the sole gut contents in 11 of 28 fish (39%) in May and 4 of 27 (15%) in November. Algae were the sole contents in 17 of 27 fish (63%) in November and 9 of 28 (32%) in May.

**3. Diel periodicity.** Ingestion, which the study found to be a better measure of feeding periodicity than gut fullness, was diurnal on both dates. The pattern was much stronger in November. In November, ingestion between 1000 and 1800 h was significantly higher than at night and early morning (*F* = 15.95, *p* < 0.001). In May, ingestion between 1415 and 1815 h was significantly higher than only the 2200–0615 h rate (*F* = 7.84, *p* = 0.009).

**4. Ingestion rates and compensation.**

| Measure | May | November |
|---|---|---|
| Daily dry-mass ingestion (mg fish⁻¹ d⁻¹) | 111.4 | 215.4 |
| Weighted mean N content of food | 5.81% | 3.77% |
| Daily N ingestion (mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹) | 6.47 | 8.12 |

Daily N ingestion was nearly the same on both dates even though dry-mass intake nearly doubled. The author interprets this as compensation: fish eating low-N algal food increased total intake to maintain nutrient intake. The author notes this has not previously been reported for fishes.

**5. Gut evacuation.** Evacuation followed the exponential model of [[Elliott And Persson Model]] (1978). Three estimates of the instantaneous evacuation rate *R* were 0.496 h⁻¹ (gut clearance, mass-specific), 0.630 h⁻¹ (gut clearance, dry mass), and 0.758 h⁻¹ (fecal production). The combined estimates were used for ingestion calculations.

**6. Nitrogen assimilation.** Food N fell from 4–6% of dry mass to under 1% in feces. Egestion was 22–28% of ingestion, giving an apparent [[Assimilation Efficiency]] for nitrogen of 72–78%. This is higher than for organic matter overall. The author notes that the herbivorous *Sarpa salpa* showed 81% protein assimilation on algae (Gerking 1984), and that longfin dace's refractory algal and detrital nitrogen makes slightly lower efficiencies reasonable.

**7. Ammonia excretion.** Field excretion was measured as ammonia-N, with urea assumed to add 10%. Individual excretion was 3.11 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ in May and 1.65 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ in November. Population excretion was 24.9 and 13.2 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹. This represented 5–10% of total ecosystem nitrogen retention over the 20-day successional period (Grimm 1987). The source's abstract describes this figure as 5–10% of algal nitrogen uptake, while the Discussion describes it as a share of total ecosystem retention. The Discussion wording is used here.

|---|---|---|
| Ingestion | 51.8 | 65.0 |
| Egestion | 14.3 | 14.3 |
| Excretion | 24.9 | 13.2 |
| Utilization (growth) | 10.1 | 36.2 |

Utilization efficiency was 19% in May and 56% in November. These values were calculated from the table's utilization figures. The text gives slightly different individual utilization values (1.41 and 4.67 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹), and the source does not reconcile the two.

**9. Ecosystem significance.**

- Fish nitrogen stored in biomass was 3–6% of the stream's total nitrogen standing crop.
- Egestion was approximately equal to net particulate export by the stream on day 27 of the succession study.
- Feces may enter the benthic organic pool, where they can be reingested or mineralized, releasing ammonia.
- Excretion is ecologically important because ammonia can be used immediately by autotrophs. The author argues that these abundant consumers may matter to desert stream nitrogen economies well beyond what energy-flow measures would suggest.

**10. Methodological limits.** Population budgets used a mean density of 8 fish m⁻² (unpublished, n = 4, SE = 1.9). Egestion estimates varied widely among samples collected at different times and are described as rough approximations. The author stresses that ingestion rates apply only to the temperatures and body sizes sampled.
