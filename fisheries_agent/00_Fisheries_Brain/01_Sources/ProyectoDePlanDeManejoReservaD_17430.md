# Sierra San Luis Protected Area Management Plan (Sonora–Chihuahua, Mexico)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is a 1994 draft management plan (*Proyecto de Plan de Manejo*) for the proposed Zona de Protección de Flora y Fauna Silvestres y Acuáticas "[[Sierra San Luis]]," a 59,000 ha protected area on the northern edge of the [[Sierra Madre Occidental]] straddling the municipalities of Agua Prieta (Sonora) and Janos (Chihuahua), Mexico. The plan was prepared under the [[Sistema de Áreas Naturales Protegidas del Estado de Sonora]] (SANPES) by the [[Centro Ecológico de Sonora]] (CES) with SEDESOL. The accompanying cover letter (August 23, 1996, from a St. Louis law firm to a local ranch owner) transmits the Spanish-language draft plan and a book, *Treasured Earth*, to the ranch owner, Joe Austin, of El Coronado Ranch, Pearce, Arizona. The document is primarily a land-use and conservation planning text; its aquatic content concerns a fish fauna of nine species.

## Background & Description

- **Area and zoning:** 59,000 ha, divided into a Protection and Recovery Zone (14,400 ha) and an Extensive Use Zone (44,600 ha).
- **Setting:** Northern extreme of the Sierra Madre Occidental on the US–Mexico border, near Arizona and New Mexico. Elevations range from 1,300 to 2,520 m, with a temperate climate.
- **Watersheds:** Split between the [[Río Yaqui]] basin (Sonora, western slopes, draining via Cajón Bonito to the Bavispe) and the [[Río Casas Grandes]] basin (eastern divide, with no significant runoff in the reserve).
- **Biotic communities:** Four communities after Brown and Lowe (1980): Madrean montane conifer forest, Madrean evergreen woodland, Plains and Great Basin grassland, and semi-desert grassland.
- **Legal basis:** The Ley General del Equilibrio Ecológico y la Protección al Ambiente (LGEEPA, articles 44–78) and the Mexican Constitution (articles 25–27) provide the framework. The protected-area category is "Zona de Protección de Flora y Fauna Silvestres y Acuáticas."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biodiversity counts (as given):**
- Plants: 157 species in 49 families. Only [[Juglans Major]] has federal protection; [[Coryphantha robbinsorum]] and [[Talinum marginatum]] are rare.
- Vertebrates: 395 species, of which 70 have some protection status.
- Mammals: 76 species in 19 families; four protected.
- Birds: up to 248 species; 30 federally protected.
- Reptiles: 51 species in 11 families (38.93% of the state's reported reptiles); 22 with protection concerns.
- Amphibians: 11 species (29.73% of Sonora's total); five federally listed.
- Fish: nine species (eight native, one introduced), about 14.75% of Sonora's total; seven of the eight natives are federally listed (87.5%).

**Threats identified in the diagnostic (síntesis de la problemática):** overgrazing (especially in oak and conifer communities), loss of ground-layer vegetation from trampling, wildfire losses, introduced exotic species, population declines of critical species, incomplete knowledge of flora, and irresponsible or illegal hunting of deer, black bear, and wild turkey. The authors note that the problem load is relatively small, so the strategy is mainly preventive.

**Land use:** Cattle ranching on natural grassland (nine ranches fully within the area, about 18,071 ha) and palmilla (*Nolina microcarpa*) harvesting for brooms, mostly exported to the US (about 90% of production).

## Key Findings

- **Reserve status:** The area is proposed, not yet decreed, and the plan is a draft under restricted circulation for landowners.
- **Aquatic fauna:** Nine fish species are recorded. Seven of the eight native species are federally listed, which the plan calls an urgent conservation priority.
- **Exotic fish:** The Río Yaqui basin has seen expanding exotic fish distributions with documented devastating effects on natives. The plan prohibits permanently the introduction of fish into the reserve.
- **Cajón Bonito:** Described as one of the most intact desert watercourses in the US Southwest (Rinne and Minckley, 1991). The plan includes continuous monitoring of this stream for exotic fish.
- **Amphibian decline:** The [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] and [[Yavapai Frog]] have declined sharply in the region over 15 years. The plan calls for urgent status assessment.
- **Management structure:** A governing body led by SEDESOL/INE, a Council of Landowners (the primary implementers and beneficiaries), a technical-scientific advisory council, and a reserve administration with an onsite liaison.
- **Programs:** Conservation; sustainable use; research and monitoring; administration and operation. Each has short-, medium- and long-term actions.
- **Finance:** Initially public and private grants, moving toward self-financing through landowner activities over the medium to long term.
- **Limiting factor:** The plan identifies landowner interest as the only structural constraint capable of blocking implementation.
- **Context:** The document places the reserve within Mexico's national and international biodiversity policy, including CITES accession in 1993 and the Programa Fronterizo Ambiental under NAFTA. It cites Mexico as one of five megadiverse countries with about 2,000 freshwater fish species.
