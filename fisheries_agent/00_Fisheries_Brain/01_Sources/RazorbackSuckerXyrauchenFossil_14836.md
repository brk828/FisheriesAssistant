# Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen) Fossils from the Anza-Borrego Desert and the Ancestral Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented in the San Bernardino County Museum Association Special Publication 93-1 for the 1993 Desert Research Symposium, reports the first documented fossil records of the genus [[Xyrauchen]] (razorback sucker) in the [[Anza-Borrego Desert State Park]] region of southern California. The authors, J.D. Stewart and Mark A. Roeder, argue that these fossils indicate a sizeable fluvial-tributary system of the ancestral [[Colorado River]] reached the [[Borrego Badlands]] during part of the [[Pleistocene]] (Ocotillo Formation), and that a nearly complete specimen from the San Felipe Hills extends the lineage back to the early [[Pliocene]] (Diablo Formation).

## Background & Description

[[Xyrauchen Texanus]], the razorback or humpback sucker, is one of the few fishes endemic to the Colorado River drainage and one of the largest catostomid fishes in North America. Its historic range lies entirely within the Colorado system, including the main river and major tributaries such as the Gila and Green rivers. Adults reach up to about 1 m. The species was once a major food source for Native Americans along the Colorado River, and it is now endangered in California because of water-flow manipulation on the lower river.

Before this study, the only published reference to a fossil of the genus was a mention by Minkley, Hendrickson, and Bond (1986) of a "well-preserved Pleistocene fossil Xyrauchen" from the Colorado Desert west of the Salton Sea, identified by Roeder. The San Felipe Hills specimen, SBCM A768-1, was collected by Ruth Coyle and donated to the San Bernardino County Museum. Its beds were originally mapped as Palm Spring Formation by Dibblee (1954), but Remeika (1991) showed they belong to the informal Diablo Formation, a set of Colorado River-derived fluvial sandstones.

The San Felipe Hills specimen is a foot-long skeleton preserved in an oval arenitic sandstone concretion. It is nearly complete and was under study by Dr. Kenneth Gobalet (California State University, Bakersfield) and Mark Roeder.

Morphology: the authors follow Nelson (1948) for osteological nomenclature. The Weberian apparatus of Xyrauchen resembles that of the Catostomini and Erimyzonini, because the neural spine of the third vertebra does not join the posterior margin of the neural complex. In [[Catostomus]], the neural complex is attenuated and supported by at least two vertebral neural arches behind it. In Xyrauchen, the supraneural sits directly above vertebrae one through four, with the fifth vertebra's neural arch extending forward to make contact. The neural complex and its supraneurals together form the conspicuous hump of the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Ocotillo Formation in the Borrego Badlands consists of locally derived sediments and probably does not represent Colorado River sediments directly. The authors interpret it as deposits of a Colorado River tributary system large enough to support big Xyrauchen. Dating places the formation at approximately 0.37–1.25 Ma (Remeika and Jefferson, this volume). Most of it falls within the Irvingtonian North American Land Mammal Age. The Irvingtonian/Rancholabrean boundary is poorly dated, with estimates of 0.2–0.55 Ma (Lundelius and others, 1987). A volcanic ash in the upper Inspiration Wash Member is tentatively correlated with the [[Bishop Tuff]] (about 0.74 Ma; Izett and others, 1988), and some fossil localities lie above this tuff and some below. The Diablo Formation, in contrast, is an early Pliocene unit that records the deltaic plain of the ancestral Colorado River along the western side of the Salton Trough.

## Key Findings

- **First fossil record of Xyrauchen in the region:** The genus is documented from the Ocotillo Formation (Pleistocene) and the Diablo Formation (lower Pliocene).
- **Pliocene specimen:** SBCM A768-1, from the San Felipe Hills, is interpreted as early Pliocene (Remeika, pers. comm.). If confirmed, it pushes the large river-adapted lineage back more than three million years. Gobalet and Roeder are assessing whether it is an earlier species or an extended range record for *X. texanus*.
- **Pleistocene material:** Six LACM specimens (LACM 130319, 130320, 130321, 130322, 135364, 135467) from the Ocotillo Formation are assigned to *X. texanus*. Table 1 lists 4123, 4124, 6763, 6775, 67113 and 136427 across the Inspiration Wash and Los Playas members. The most common element is the neural complex of the Weberian apparatus, which is the osteological basis of the hump. Five neural complex specimens come from four Borrego Badlands localities.
- **Large body size:** All identifiable LACM material comes from individuals well above 50 cm standard length, judging by comparison with LACM 43613-1. This suggests that a sizeable tributary system supported large fish.
- **Biogeographic significance:** The findings support a long history of large, river-adapted Xyrauchen in the ancestral Colorado River system, and show how fossil fish can help date and interpret desert stratigraphy.
- **Future work:** The near-complete San Felipe Hills skeleton may clarify whether it represents a new earlier species or only an extended range for *X. texanus*.
