# Federal Register Endangered Species Listings: Ouachita Rock Pocketbook and Razorback Sucker

#endangered-species #federal-register #unionidae #catostomidae #freshwater-conservation

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register document (Vol. 56, No. 205, October 23, 1991) announces two final rules listing endangered species: the [[Ouachita Rock Pocketbook]] (*Arkansia wheeleri*, syn. *Arcidens wheeleri*), a freshwater mussel, and the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a native fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The [[Ouachita Rock Pocketbook]] is listed as endangered in Oklahoma and Arkansas. The [[Razorback Sucker]] is listed as endangered throughout its range (Arizona, California, Colorado, New Mexico, Nevada, Utah, Wyoming, and Mexico). Both listings were made under authority of the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. Critical habitat was not designated for either species at the time of listing; for the [[Razorback Sucker]], critical habitat was deemed "not determinable" due to insufficient data on precise habitat requirements essential for recruitment and recovery.

## Background & Description

### Ouachita Rock Pocketbook (*Arkansia wheeleri*)

The [[Ouachita Rock Pocketbook]] is a freshwater mussel (Family [[Unionidae]]) first described by Ortmann and Walker in 1912. It inhabits the [[Kiamichi River]] in Oklahoma and historically occurred in Arkansas. The species has been the subject of status surveys by Clarke (1987), Mehlhop-Cifelli and Miller (1989), and the [[Oklahoma Natural Heritage Inventory]] (1989). Archaeological remains from the Bug Hill Site in Pushmataha County, Oklahoma, document historical presence (Dogan and Bogan 1983). The species is part of the tribe Pleurobemini within subfamily Unioninae (Branson 1983).

### Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large-river native fish (Family [[Catostomidae]]) once abundant and widely distributed throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Adults are characterized by a distinctive dorsal keel or "hump." The species now inhabits approximately 25% of its original range. The largest remaining population occurs in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona/Nevada), though these fish are old individuals with virtually no recruitment. Small numbers persist in the upper [[Green River]] (Utah), lower [[Yampa River]] (Colorado), mainstem [[Colorado River]] near Grand Junction, and the Colorado, Dirty Devil, and San Juan arms of [[Lake Powell]]. The species is considered one of the rarest of four endangered large-river native Colorado River basin fishes, with only the [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) considered less common (McAda 1987).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Ouachita Rock Pocketbook

Limited ecological information is provided in the references cited section. The species occurs in the [[Kiamichi River]] system and was subject to status surveys examining distribution and population status. Vaughn (1991) studied habitat use and reproductive biology in the Kiamichi River. The species co-occurs with other unionid mussels documented in Oklahoma fauna surveys (Branson 1983, Isely 1925).

### Razorback Sucker

The [[Razorback Sucker]] evolved in the highly turbid, turbulent, warm waters of the natural [[Colorado River]] system. Adults prefer water temperatures between 22-25°C (71.6-77°F) and avoid temperatures below 14.7°C (58.5°F) and above 27.4°C (81.3°F) (Bulkley and Pimentel 1983). Spawning has been observed in several reservoirs and river reaches. Early investigators reported spawning concentrations in small tributaries in the lower basin (Jordan 1891, Hubbs and Miller 1953). Recent observations document spawning in [[Senator Wash Reservoir]], tailwaters of [[Hoover Dam]], [[Lake Mead]], and [[Lake Mohave]] (Jonez and Sumner 1954, Ulmer 1980, Minckley 1983, Langhorst and Marsh 1986, Mueller 1989). Radio-tracking demonstrates that some fish migrate considerable distances to spawn (Tyus 1987a).

Larval razorback suckers consume zooplankton. Papoulias (1986) found that food items needed to be present at a density of 10 organisms per liter within 10 days of yolk sac absorption; death occurred at 20-30 days if insufficient zooplankton were present. Marsh and Langhorst (1988) found only 1.5 zooplankters per liter in [[Lake Mohave]] and reported disappearance of larvae at about 20 days of age.

The species co-occurs with three other listed Colorado River fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. However, its life history and habitat requirements differ from these species (Tyus and Karp 1989).

## Key Findings

### Listing Determinations

**Ouachita Rock Pocketbook**: Listed as endangered (E) under "CLAMS" with historic range USA (AR, OK). Entire population listed. No critical habitat designated (NA). Special rules: NA. Listed reference: 448 NA NA.

**Razorback Sucker**: Listed as endangered (E) under "FISHES" with historic range USA (AZ, CA, CO, NM, NV, UT, WY) and Mexico. Entire population listed. No critical habitat designated (NA). Listed October 23, 1991.

### Threats to Razorback Sucker (Five Factors Analysis)

**Factor A - Habitat Destruction/Modification**: Since 1910, 15 dams constructed on lower [[Colorado River]] and major tributaries ([[Gila River]], [[Verde River]], [[Salt River]]) have dewatered, cooled, or impounded most of the lower basin. [[Glen Canyon Dam]] reduced water temperatures for 384 km (238 mi) through the [[Grand Canyon]]. [[Lake Powell]] and [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] impounded 500 km (310 mi) of habitat and lowered temperatures in another 105 km (65 mi). Storage and diversion reduced mean annual discharge by 18% at Green-Colorado confluence; spring runoff reduced by 28% (Green River) and 37% (Colorado River) during May-June (USGS flow records 1906-1982). Peaking power operations at [[Flaming Gorge]] produced 400% increase in daily flow fluctuations at Jensen, Utah. Reduction of spring flooding has eliminated off-stream bottomland habitats historically important for spawning (Osmundson and Kaeding 1991, Tyus and Karp 1989). Dams obstruct migration and access to spawning areas.

**Factor B - Overutilization**: Once extensively used for food and supported commercial fishery in lower basin (Hubbs and Miller 1953), but all States now protect species from harvest. Not currently a threat.

**Factor C - Disease or Predation**: No evidence disease is significant factor, though Minckley (1983) reported many old Lake Mohave individuals blind or showing signs of disease/injury. Predation on eggs and larvae observed by carp, [[Channel Catfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], bluegill, [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Redear Sunfish]] (Jonez and Sumner 1954, Ulmer 1980, Langhorst 1989, Marsh and Langhorst 1988). Predation hypothesized as major cause of recruitment failure (McAda and Wydoski 1980, Minckley 1983, Tyus 1987a). Marsh and Brooks (1989) demonstrated channel catfish and [[Flathead Catfish]] were major predators of stocked razorback suckers in [[Gila River]]. Recent establishment of [[Walleye]] and [[Northern Pike]] in Green River adds additional predators (Tyus and Beard 1990).

**Factor D - Inadequacy of Existing Regulatory Mechanisms**: State regulations protect from take/possession but do not address habitat destruction or nonnative species introduction. [[Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin]] has goal of managing species to avoid need for ESA protection, but is not regulatory mechanism—it is cooperative agreement that can be amended, terminated, or withdrawn from. Program only applies to upper basin (excluding [[San Juan River]]), does not protect species throughout range. Without listing, Federal Agencies not mandated to consider razorback sucker in their actions.

**Factor E - Other Natural or Manmade Factors**: **Critical finding**: Significant recruitment has not been evident for at least 30 years. Existing populations composed primarily of old individuals slowly dying off (McCarthy and Minckley 1987, Tyus 1987a). Only a few naturally reproduced juveniles reported from Lake Mohave, Colorado River, and off-stream canals in past 15 years. In upper Colorado River, last juvenile collection was eight individuals 90-115 mm length downstream of Moab, Utah, during 1962-1964 (Taba et al. 1965). Only four juveniles (33-54 mm) collected from Lake Mohave since 1950s (Marsh and Minckley 1989).

**Nonnative Species**: 42 nonnative species established in upper basin (Tyus et al. 1982), 37 in lower basin (Minckley 1979). Several are serious competitors or predators. [[Triploid Grass Carp]] legalized for importation into California and Arizona. Two tilapia species and flathead catfish have become dominant in lower Colorado River. [[Rainbow Smelt]] proposed for introduction into Lake Powell (Gustaveson et al. 1999).

**Hybridization**: Intercrossing with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) reported by multiple investigators. Vanicek et al. (1970) found ratio of 16 intercrosses to 73 razorback suckers; Holden (1973) found 40 intercrosses to 53 razorback suckers. However, electrophoretic analyses of Lake Mohave razorback suckers revealed less than 5% incidence of flannelmouth sucker genes, considered insignificant by Buth et al. (1987). Premature to conclude intercrossing is significant threat pending additional data.

### Public Comment Summary

62 written and 18 oral comments received. 41 comments supported listing, 24 neutral, 9 opposed. Comments received from 11 Federal agencies, 7 State agencies, 10 local governments, and 47 private organizations/companies/individuals. Public hearing held August 14, 1990, in Farmington, New Mexico, with approximately 60 attendees and 18 oral statements.

**Key Issues Raised**:
- Bureau of Reclamation and State of Nevada recommended threatened rather than endangered status
- Concerns about critical habitat designation and economic impacts
- Questions about impact on [[Animas-LaPlata Project]] and future water development
- Concerns about stocking of nonnative fishes
- Questions about State-Federal partnership in protection

**Service Response to Threatened Status Recommendation**: Species clearly in danger of extinction throughout range due to greatly reduced range (now 25% of original), extensive habitat alteration, apparent inability to recruit successfully, and nonnative species introduction. Therefore qualifies as endangered, not threatened.

### Critical Habitat Determination

Critical habitat **not designated** and deemed **not determinable**. Service unable to adequately determine precise constituent elements within specific areas essential to survival and recovery. Limited information on specific habitat needs. Questionable whether currently occupied areas adequately meet life history needs given little/no recruitment. Species cannot perpetuate if no recruitment to adult population. Not in species' best interest to identify existing habitat characteristics as basis for critical habitat when unable to identify specific areas and precise characteristics needed to bring about recruitment. Section 4(b)(6)(C) allows postponement of critical habitat determination for 1 additional year when prompt endangered status determination is essential to conservation.

### Temperature and Incubation Requirements

Incubation time to hatching varies inversely with water temperature:
- 19.4 days at 10°C (50°F) (Bozek et al. 1984)
- 11.1 days at 15°C (59°F) (Bozek et al. 1984)
- 9 days at 15°C (59°F) (Marsh 1985)
- 6.8 days at 20°C (68°F) (Bozek et al. 1984)
- 5.5 days at 20°C (68°F) (Gustafson 1975)
- 3.5 days at 25°C (77°F) (Marsh 1985)

Most investigators reported poor hatching success below 15°C (59°F) and total egg mortality below 10°C (50°F). However, Bozek et al. (1984) noted only slightly lower survival at 10°C than at 15 and 20°C.

### Water Development Impacts

Over 100 section 7 consultations conducted since 1980 on federally funded/regulated projects in upper basin involving water depletions. Several transbasin diversions planned or under construction. Most prominent is [[Central Utah Project]], which would divert 185,060 acre-feet from Green River to [[Bonneville Basin]]. Continued habitat alteration likely as major reservoirs and diversions are planned (e.g., [[Animas-La Plata Project]], [[Muddy Creek Reservoir]], [[Sandstone Reservoir]]).

### Historical Context

Pre-impoundment poisoning project in Green River where [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] now located often cited as partial cause for loss of native fishes downstream. Comparison of fish species composition in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] before and after program (Binns et al. 1963, Vanicek and Kramer 1989, Vanicek et al. 1970) supports premise that effect was short-term and not responsible for current status. Similar program conducted on [[San Juan River]] at [[Navajo Reservoir]] site; no razorback suckers collected before or after treatment (Platania 1990).
