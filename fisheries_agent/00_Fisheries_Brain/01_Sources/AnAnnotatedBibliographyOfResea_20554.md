# Annotated Bibliography of Research in Economically Important Species of California Fish and Game

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-bibliography

## Executive Summary

This document is the front matter and marine-fishes section of a 1958 annotated bibliography prepared for the California Assembly Interim Committee on Fish and Game. Compiled by [[William F. Scheuermann Jr.]], a legislative intern, it was commissioned under House Resolution No. 124 (1957) to list research on roughly 144 economically important California species, so the Legislature could evaluate existing research, consider future management proposals, and identify gaps. The annotations summarize content rather than judge its reliability. The marine fishes section, which is the portion present in the source, covers sharks, sardines, herring, anchovy, smelts, flatfishes, sea basses, barracuda, yellowtail, jack and Pacific mackerel, tunas, bonito, swordfish, croakers, surfperches, rockfishes, sablefish, lingcod and cabezon. Invertebrate sections (crustaceans, mollusks) are also included in part. Several sections list dozens of references each.

## Background & Description

- **Commissioning:** House Resolution No. 124 (1957), introduced by Assemblyman Vincent Thomas of San Pedro; the Assembly Interim Committee on Fish and Game was created under House Resolution No. 210 (1957). The report was published August 1958.
- **Method:** References are arranged chronologically, with authors alphabetical within each year. Coverage extends to material published before July 1958, including unpublished agency reports and trade or popular magazines. Excluded are purely morphological handbook descriptions, processing, marketing, folklore and literary items. Emphasis is on life history, habits, habitat and management.
- **Scope:** Species were included when they bore on California's economy. About 144 species were targeted in the full bibliography.
- **Author context:** The compiler was a Public Administration graduate student with wildlife curriculum training, serving through the California Legislative Internship Program, sponsored by the Assembly, the Ford Foundation and five graduate universities.
- **Structure of full volume:** Marine fishes (pp. 11-77), crustaceans, mollusks, anadromous fishes, inland fishes, migratory waterfowl, upland game and fur-bearers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The annotations repeatedly link fishery fluctuations to environmental drivers. Among the most frequently cited are:

- **Sea temperature and hydrography:** Temperature was linked to sardine abundance and spawning (Lewis 1929; Tibby 1937; Ahlstrom 1954), albacore distribution (Thompson 1917), and British Columbia pilchard failures (Hart 1934b; Foerster 1941).
- **Upwelling and salinity:** Walford (1946) correlated year-class size of sardines with summer surface salinity, interpreted as an index of upwelling and plankton supply.
- **Plankton and food webs:** Sardine food was linked to microplankton (Lewis 1929; Parr 1930), and tuna and albacore diets were surveyed across regions (Hart 1942; McHugh 1952; Reintjes and King 1953).
- **Spawning areas:** Sardine spawning centred off central Baja California and southern California (Ahlstrom 1954); herring spawning concentrated in San Francisco and Tomales Bays (Miller and Schmidtke 1956).
- **Red water and mortality events:** A 1945 mass mortality in Santa Monica Bay was attributed to dinoflagellate blooms (Summers and Clark 1946).
- **Fishing-induced decline:** Multiple species (soupfin shark, Pacific mackerel, Pismo clam, sardine) were examined for overfishing, with conclusions ranging from clear depletion to no evidence of depletion.

## Key Findings

**Sharks (pp. 12-14)**
- Ripley (1946a) concluded that landings of soupfin shark had declined since 1939 and that catch per unit effort indicated serious decline.
- Tagging showed a soupfin shark tagged at Ventura in 1943 recovered in British Columbia in 1945, supporting homogeneity of the west coast population (Ripley 1946b).
- Razum (1952) analysed 307 stomach samples from San Francisco Bay: anchovy made up 17.3% of dogfish food volume, and shrimp 38.3% of smoothhound diet.

**Pacific Sardine (pp. 15-32)**
- This is the largest section, with roughly 130 references. Early studies covered sampling, length frequency and age (Thompson 1926a; Walford and Mosher 1943a, 1948b).
- Scofield (1934) reported spawning concentrated in an area 200 miles long and 100 miles wide, about 100 miles offshore between San Diego and Point Conception.
- Clark (1934) found spawning from February through August, peaking in April and May, and that larger fish spawn more eggs per batch.
- Walford and Mosher (1948b) concluded scale-based age determination was 80% to 90% accurate.
- Ahlstrom (1954) estimated a minimum larval survival of 1 in 1000.
- McHugh and Ahlstrom (1951) concluded the species was not disappearing; the decline reflected poor spawning seasons and changed availability or adult mortality.
- Clark (1936a) concluded that fishing on younger adults could lead to serious depletion.
- Clark (1939a) recommended a 250,000-ton limit to rebuild supply; the 1947 Pacific Fisherman reports a suggested 150,000-ton quota.

**Pacific Herring (p. 33)**
- Miller and Schmidtke (1956) concluded the California population was small, relied mainly on San Francisco and Tomales Bays for spawning, and fluctuated because of sporadic spawning.
- Merkel (1957) found herring made up 12.7% of king salmon food in the San Francisco area.

**Anchovy (pp. 34-36)**
- McHugh (1951) concluded the northern anchovy ranged from British Columbia to southern Baja California, probably matched or exceeded sardines in numbers, and was a relatively unexploited resource.
- Miller (1956) found no measurable effect of fishing on the population, and proposed two populations divided near Cape San Martin.
- Merkel (1957) found anchovy made up 29.1% of king salmon food.

**Flatfishes (pp. 36-42)**
- Hagerman (1952) studied 3,200 Dover sole specimens and concluded the stock had great survival potential under heavy exploitation.
- Orcutt (1950) found a stable starry flounder population able to sustain much higher fishing pressure.
- Arora (1951) found sanddab spawning peaked in August.

**California Halibut (p. 40)**
- Clark (1931) concluded depletion in the San Pedro area was evident from falling catch per unit effort, and recommended protection of spawners.
- Holmberg (1949) noted that since 1936 landings had been adjusted to a 9:1 ratio of Pacific to California halibut.

**Sea Basses and Kelp Bass (pp. 43-44)**
- Clark (1933) found that closing rock bass to commercial fishing would give little hardship to industry and little conservation benefit.
- Collyer and Young (1953) tagged 4,172 kelp bass and recovered 500; they concluded the fishery was probably based on a series of independent populations, with spawning in May through August.

**Barracuda (pp. 45-47)**
- Walford (1932) recommended a slight increase in size limit to 30 inches to protect four-year-olds.
- Craig (1927) found that the 1927 purse-seine ban reduced total catch and made monthly catch and prices more variable.

**Yellowtail (pp. 48-49)**
- Collyer (1954) tagged 8,147 fish, with 103 recovered; only 13 moved more than ten miles, and the maximum movement was 240 miles.
- Greenhood (1949) noted that peak years of 1918, 1936 and 1947 contradicted any claim of depletion.

**Jack Mackerel (pp. 50-51)**
- Roedel (1953) concluded the California fishery was healthy, while noting a serious gap in knowledge and no measure of fishing effort.

**Pacific Mackerel (pp. 52-55)**
- Fitch (1952) concluded the Pacific mackerel fishery was declining under constantly increasing fishing pressure and recommended a closed season and size limits.
- Fry and Roedel (1949) concluded that tag returns were inaccurate and dubious as abundance measures.
- Roedel (1952a) found a Pacific coast population that was extremely heterogeneous, though this conflicted with some tagging results.
- Fitch (1955) reported a 1954-55 landing of 7,613,000 pounds, the lowest since 1928.

**Tunas and Related Species (pp. 56-65)**
- The section lists about 90 references covering albacore, bluefin, yellowfin and skipjack. Godsil (1948) concluded that yellowfin in the eastern Pacific formed a single intermingling population.
- Schaefer (1952) concluded the west coast yellowfin fishery was tapping an essentially virgin resource.
- Shimada and Schaefer (1956) concluded that fishing had a real effect on eastern Pacific yellowfin stocks but little effect on skipjack.
- Ganssle and Clemens (1953) reported the first authentic evidence of transpacific albacore migration.

**Swordfish (p. 66)**
- Scofield (1930) suggested protection might be needed because the fish were never abundant and fishing was intensive.

**Croakers and White Seabass (pp. 67-68)**
- Clark (1930) recommended minimum size protection of 39.5-40 inches and 28-24 pounds for white sea bass.
- Whitehead (1930a) concluded that trends in boat catch from 1918 to 1928 were decidedly downward and the fishery needed protection.

**Surfperches (p. 69)**
- Clark (1930) found white perch was 82% of San Pedro landings, walleye perch 14% and eight other species 4%.
- Rechnitzer and Limbaugh (1952) concluded gestation lasted about 5 to 6 months, with young numbers highest at 12.

**Sheepshead (p. 70)** and **Rockfishes (pp. 71-73)**
- Phillips (1939) found the most important Monterey rockfish were bocaccio (39.4%), chilipepper (30.8%), yellowtail rockfish (7.9%), vermilion rockfish (6.8%) and orange rockfish (6.4%).
- Wales (1952) concluded blue rockfish spawn November to January, and commercial catch was mostly fifth, sixth and seventh year groups.
- Phillips (1957) provided the comprehensive review of California rockfish species.

**Sablefish (pp. 74-75)**
- Phillips and Imamura (1954) concluded there was no evidence of depletion; annual fluctuations were linked to demand.
- Phillips et al. (1954) tagged 9,511 fish, recovering 243 (2.55%), and concluded the stocks were local.
- Phillips (1958) concluded there was no evidence of depletion in California waters and proposed regional regulations.

**Lingcod (p. 76)**
- Hart (1943) found 36% of recovered tagged lingcod had migrated extensively, while most did not migrate more than a nautical mile.

**Cabezon (p. 77)**
- O'Connell (1953) concluded crabs made up half the total cabezon diet, spawning was October to March, and the stock would support further fishing effort.
- Hubbs and Wick (1951) found cabezon roe toxic in feeding experiments; they recommended not eating it.

**Crustaceans (pp. 79-86)**
- Market crab: Dahlstrom (1957) found shoulder width and weight increased in San Francisco and Eureka during 1956-57 compared with previous seasons.
- Spiny lobster: Lindberg (1955) analysed about 2,000 specimens, found circumstantial evidence of overfishing, and concluded closed seasons and size limits had negligible value.
- Ocean shrimp: Calif. Bureau of Marine Fisheries (1955) recommended that the shrimping season begin in mid-May instead of May 1.

**Mollusks (pp. 87-94)**
- Abalone: Cox (1955b) found no north coast area investigated could support a commercial fishery. Bonnot (1948) found tagging indicated delicate adjustment to environment, no extensive migration, and night foraging with return to a home spot.
- Pismo clam: Herrington (1930) concluded the 1926 census showed the greatest scarcity since records began. Weymouth (1923) concluded protection of adults was adequate and poaching on young was the main cause of depletion.

**Overall Conclusion**
The bibliography does not assess reliability or make recommendations of its own. It is intended to give the Legislature a comprehensive list for evaluation by competent technical authorities, and to point to areas that need further research.
