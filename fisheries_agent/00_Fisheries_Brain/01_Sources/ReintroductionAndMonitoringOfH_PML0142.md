# Reintroduction and Monitoring of Hatchery-Reared Bonytail in the Colorado and Green Rivers, 1996–2001

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Utah Division of Wildlife Resources final report (Publication 03-13, Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program, Project #25) evaluates the reintroduction of hatchery-reared [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) into the upper [[Colorado River]] Basin (UCRB) between 1996 and 2002. Bonytail are considered functionally extirpated from the upper basin and are federally listed as endangered. The study stocked fish from [[Wahweap State Fish Hatchery]] (broodstock from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]) into the Colorado and Green rivers, monitored recaptures and habitat use, and radio-tracked adult fish to assess dispersal and survival.

Recaptures were low (about 0.8% of Colorado River releases and 0.4% of Green River releases), but survival and movement data guided recommendations to stock fish near 300 mm TL in March to early April, mark fish with PIT tags, and monitor primarily with [[Electrofishing]].

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Bonytail (*Gila elegans*), a large, rare cyprinid of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Listed as federally endangered in 1980. Only a few adult captures have been recorded in the upper basin since listing, with no documented recruitment.
- **Declines attributed to:** habitat loss, altered flow regimes, [[Hybridization]] with other Gila species, and interactions with [[Nonnative Fish]] (predation and competition).
- **Management framework:** The [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]] required an approved stocking plan. The Bonytail Reintroduction Plan (Lentsch et al. 1996) set a six-year stocking and monitoring guideline. The interim management objective for the UCRB stock was 4,118 (± 817) bonytail per population, with the three stocking locations (Green, Colorado, Yampa) treated as separate populations.
- **Broodstock origin:** A relict population in Lake Mohave (Minckley et al. 1989).
- **Rearing:** Fry were transferred from Dexter to Wahweap and reared in ten ponds to release size. Early target size was about 120 mm TL after one summer, and about 300 mm after a second growing season. Higher production after 1998 increased density, reduced growth, and compromised the design.
- **Marking:** 1996–1998 fish were PIT tagged and individually measured. From 1999 to 2001, fish received coded wire tags (CWT), which cannot reveal individual codes without killing the fish, so cohort identity was lost for later recaptures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area:** Three reintroduction sites were chosen on the basis of habitat availability, hybridization potential, historical occupation, predation potential, concurrent sampling, and logistics:
- [[Colorado River]] near Dewey Bridge (RM 94.3; initially RM 96.2 below the [[Dolores River]] mouth), monitored from RM 100 to the [[Green River]] confluence.
- [[Green River]] at Green River State Park (RM 120.0), just below [[Gray Canyon]] and [[Desolation Canyon]], monitored from RM 136 to the Colorado confluence.
- [[Yampa River]] stocking by the Colorado Division of Wildlife (2000–2001).

**Hybridization and nonnative fishes:** Hybridization with [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] occurs to some degree in the wild, and spatial rather than temporal isolation is thought to separate spawning. Nonnative predation on native fishes is documented in the Colorado system. Alder and Crowl (1995) modeled gape-limitation and suggested bonytail become largely beyond predation risk at about 150 mm TL.

**Hydrology and temperature:** Flow and temperature were recorded at USGS gauging stations 09163500 (Colorado River, near the Utah–Colorado border) and 09315000 (Green River at Green River, Utah). Declining water temperature after fall releases (from about 12 °C to 5 °C within weeks in 1997) was associated with negative growth in fall-stocked fish.

**Habitat use:**
- Juvenile bonytail were most often recorded in flooded tributaries (55% of habitat-recorded captures), where fish were small (mean TL 95 mm).
- Larger juveniles and adults were associated with flowing habitats such as runs, riffles, eddies, and shorelines.
- Other Gila species (291 captures) were mostly in flowing habitats (60%), and were also smaller in flooded tributaries and backwaters (mean TL 82 mm) than in flowing habitats (mean TL 221 mm). The authors infer that flooded tributaries and backwaters are important rearing habitat for juvenile Gila species.

**Dispersal:** More than 98% of bonytail were captured downstream of release sites. On the Green River, 85% were within 40 RM below the release site; on the Colorado River, 94% were within 30 RM downstream. Juveniles did not generally enter higher-gradient canyon sections, which were presumed to constrain movement.

## Key Findings

**Stocking totals and sizes**
- Colorado River: 84,603 bonytail released (1996–2001), 65–354 mm TL in the first three years. 82% were under 140 mm and only 1.6% were over 200 mm.
- Green River: 127,714 bonytail released (1998–2001).
- Only three releases averaged over 150 mm TL in six years of stocking. Fish averaging 316.2 mm (104 fish, 1998) and two 2000 releases (169.0 and 187.4 mm) were the largest.

**Recaptures**
- Colorado River: 663 recaptures (0.8% of released fish). Seventeen (0.02%) had overwintered and persisted more than six months, mostly in 2001.
- Green River: 523 recaptures (0.4%) between 1999 and 2002. Fifty-nine (about 11%) overwintered for more than six months, with 31 in 2001.
- Green River catch rates for all methods were lower than on the Colorado River, which suggests lower survival in the Green.

**Monitoring methods**
- [[Electrofishing]] produced the most recaptures (385 Colorado, 391 Green) and was judged the most effective method for river miles covered, habitats sampled, and labor.
- Seining yielded 242 (Colorado) and 118 (Green) recaptures. Fyke and hoop nets yielded 36 and 14.
- Trammel nets were tested but caught few small bonytail.

**Size and survival**
- A positive regression of electrofishing relative catch rate (RCR) on stocking TL was found on the Colorado River (R² = 0.85), but this was driven by one 316 mm cohort. Without it, no relationship was found (R² = 0.01). No relationship was found on the Green River (R² = 0.08).
- Only one cohort (1998, >300 mm) was large enough to test a size-survival effect. Its higher return rate was consistent with razorback sucker results.

**Season of stocking**
- No significant difference was found between fall and spring stocking in short-term RCR (fall mean 0.42, spring mean 0.47).
- Spring-stocked fish grew (mean 6.3 mm after 2–9 days; 10.4 mm after 58–70 days). Fall-stocked fish showed mean growth of −0.3 mm after 2–20 days, possibly indicating too short an acclimation period before winter.

**Adult telemetry**
- 49 adult bonytail (>300 mm TL) were implanted with radio tags. Only one was suspected dead before tag failure. This contrasts with the 74.4% confirmed mortality reported by [[Chart and Cranney 1993]].
- The most likely cause of survival differences was age at release and handling history.
- Radio-tagged fish showed a sedentary period of one to two months after release, then moved with spring runoff, and finally returned upstream to eddy/run habitats. Maximum downstream movement was up to 113 river miles, but most remained within 20 RM and within the release flat-water section.
- Intensive tracking found adults in deep eddies or runs 15–30 m from shore by day, moving into areas less than 5 m deep at dusk.

**Flow training**
- Laboratory experiments (Lentsch et al. 1996; Crowl and Rivera 2000) gave mixed results. Flow training did not clearly improve survival, and field testing had not occurred as of FY2001.

**Recommendations**
1. Stock hatchery-reared bonytail in the Colorado and Green rivers at about 300 mm TL. Also stock about 200 mm fish to evaluate optimal size.
2. Mark all hatchery fish with PIT tags before release.
3. Stock in March to early April to allow acclimation.
4. Monitor from mid-April to early June, using electrofishing as the primary method, with three spring passes for annual population estimates.
5. Monitor for successful reproduction (larval and juvenile surveys, light trapping, or larval drift sampling).
6. Conduct any further flow-training work at Wahweap.

**Conclusions**
- Overall return rates were consistent with juvenile razorback sucker and Colorado pikeminnow reintroductions, but long-term recapture rates were lower than in other native fish reintroductions.
- Return rates and survival were highest for fish released at 300 mm or greater.
- Juveniles under 150 mm favored flooded tributaries and backwaters.

**Open questions**
- What are the short- and long-term survival rates of stocked bonytail?
- What is the relationship between size and survivorship in the wild?
- Do stocked reaches contain adequate spawning and rearing habitat to support independent subpopulations?
- Can flow training produce measurable changes in condition and survivorship?
