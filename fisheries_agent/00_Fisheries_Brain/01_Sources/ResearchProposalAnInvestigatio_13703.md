## Executive Summary

This research proposal outlines a comparative field and laboratory study designed to investigate the causes of population decline in two endemic southwestern U.S. minnows, the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), both monotypic genera restricted to the Gila River basin. Both species have suffered severe range and population contractions attributed to stream habitat modification (damming, flow regulation) and biotic interactions with the introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*). The study proposes a multi-site comparative sampling design across four categories of stream reaches (native-only, long-term coexistence, active competitive displacement, and shiner-only/post-extirpation sites) to quantify dietary and microhabitat niche shifts, evaluate reproductive success, and test causal mechanisms via field manipulative experiments (split-habitat enclosures and reach-level introductions/removals).

## Background & Description

- **Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)**: Monotypic genus; benthic body form adapted to hold position in swift, shallow riffle currents with gravel/cobble substrate.
- **Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*)**: Monotypic genus; midwater body form suited to turbulent, eddying currents at riffle-pool transitions.
- **Red Shiner (*Notropis lutrensis*)**: Generalized, stream-adapted cyprinid morphology; considered aggressive and highly invasive relative to the specialized native forms.

*Note: The source document is ecological/proposal-based and does not provide detailed morphometric data (fin ray counts, body proportions, coloration) for any of the three species.*

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Loach Minnow**: Occupies shallow riffles with gravel/cobble substrate and dense algal growth; a bottom-dwelling riffle specialist.
- **Spikedace**: A midwater inhabitant of turbulent/eddying currents where riffles enter pools; occupies a distinct hydraulic niche from the loach minnow despite habitat overlap zones.
- **Red Shiner**: Introduced circa 1950s–1960s as a baitfish/forage species into the mainstream [[Colorado River]] and subsequently into the [[Gila River]] system; a generalist, aggressive invader that progressively displaces native species upstream into headwater refugia.
- **Range overlap dynamics**: Ranges of red shiner and the two native species are described as "almost mutually exclusive," with native species being forced into progressively higher (upstream) watershed positions as red shiner expands.
- **Site categorization for study** (see [[Gila River Basin]] drainage system):
  1. Natives present, red shiner absent (e.g., [[Aravaipa Creek]], White/Blue rivers, East Fork Gila River)
  2. Long-term coexistence (e.g., upper [[Verde River]] for spikedace/red shiner)
  3. Active detrimental interaction (mainstream Gila River, New Mexico)
  4. Red shiner present, natives extirpated (middle Verde or Gila rivers)

## Key Findings

*(Note: This document is a research proposal; findings represent background rationale and hypothesized mechanisms rather than completed experimental results.)*

- **Historical decline pattern**: [[Minckley]] and Deacon (1968) first documented the disappearance of loach minnow and spikedace correlating with red shiner abundance, though the causal mechanism remains unresolved.
- **Proposed interaction mechanisms** (4 categories driving native decline):
  1. Competition for trophic/spatial resources
  2. Lowered reproductive success (spawning interference or egg/larval predation by red shiner)
  3. Interference with foraging/microhabitat use (agonistic behavior)
  4. Community-level niche compression forcing native species into shared "refuge niches," potentially increasing intra-native competition
- **Study focus**: The proposal prioritizes (A) direct resource competition and (B) reproductive success impacts, treating agonistic interference and community-level shifts as secondary/opportunistic lines of inquiry.
- **Proposed sampling scale**: 12 primary field sites per native species (18 total stream reaches accounting for overlap), with fish sampled for stomach contents, growth rates (via size-frequency analysis), and fecundity.
- **Analytical approach**: Combination of niche breadth/overlap indices ([[Moyle 1977]] methodology) and multivariate statistics (Principal Component Analysis, clustering; per [[Matthews 1985]]) to detect dietary and microhabitat niche contraction or shifts in presence of red shiner.
- **Microhabitat assessment**: Underwater snorkel transects and point-sampling (seining/electrofishing) to quantify fish position relative to depth, current speed, substrate, turbidity, and cover — modeled on [[Moyle and Baltz 1985]] methods.
- **Manipulative experiments proposed**:
  - Split-habitat designs using buried plastic screen fencing to isolate paired pool/riffle habitats, comparing native-only vs. native+red-shiner treatments under controlled density conditions.
  - Whole-reach manipulations: baseline microhabitat assessment → red shiner introduction → red shiner removal, to test reversibility of niche shifts.
  - Reproductive output comparisons (larval drift for spikedace; nest counts/egg numbers for loach minnow) between red-shiner-present and red-shiner-removed pools.
- **Underlying conservation implication**: Both species were proposed for federal listing as Threatened under the Endangered Species Act (1985), motivating urgency for mechanistic understanding of the [[Red Shiner]] invasion's role in their decline.
