# Size and Morphological Variation in Males of the Sailfin Molly (*Poecilia latipinna*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines size and morphological variation in mature male [[Sailfin Molly]] (*Poecilia latipinna*) from four [[Poeciliidae|Poeciliid]] populations in east-central Florida, sampled monthly from 1973 to 1982. Males did not fall into discrete classes. Instead they formed a continuous size and morphological gradient in which coloration and fin development were strongly correlated with standard length (SL). Size distributions were stable over time within a site but differed markedly among sites. The author attributes this to an interaction of environmental, genetic, and social factors, and proposes a three-class division (Types I–III) purely as a descriptive convenience.

## Background & Description

Adult male [[Livebearer|Livebearers]] vary greatly in size within and between populations. In *P. latipinna*, mature males from Florida range from 16 to 103 mm SL. Large males display a [[Sailfin Phenotype]]: bright blue and gold coloration, pigment markings, and an enlarged dorsal fin. Small males resemble females in color and fin shape. Previous authors used different names for the same morphs (for example, "high," "dominant," or "sneaky" males), and this study adopts a neutral three-class scheme:

- **Type I (small):** less than about 30 mm SL, with no distinctive coloration or morphological development.
- **Type II (intermediate):** about 30–45 mm SL, with incomplete development of secondary sexual traits.
- **Type III (large):** greater than about 45 mm SL, with full sailfin expression.

Males were classified as mature using [[Gonopodium]] characters. Specimens were measured to the nearest 0.1 mm SL.

**Environmental conditions.** Water levels fell from November to a low in April or May each year. Fish stress during these drawdowns was inversely related to the availability of deep-water refugia. Submerged vegetation was mainly *Chara* sp. at VABI and TOWY and *Ruppia maritima* at BDGST. VABI experienced severe desiccation, BCHRD very severe, and TOWY minor. Temperature averages were similar among sites that were measured (daily minimum 19.6–21.3 °C; daily maximum 23.5–24.6 °C).

**Predators.** Predator assemblages differed among sites. TOWY supported several [[Centrarchidae|Centrarchid]] sunfishes and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), and a [[Longnose Gar]] (*Lepisosteus platyrhincus*) was present at TOWY and VABI. BDGST had [[Fundulus grandis]] and *Elops saurus*. Predatory wading birds were about equally abundant at all sites.

## Key Findings

**1. Size and morphology form a continuum, not discrete classes.**
- Male size was continuously distributed at every population studied.
- Coloration and morphology were linearly related to SL, with no breaks or steps in the graphs.
- Morphological traits showed strong allometry.

**2. Strong among-site differences; stable within-site distributions.**
- Within a site, monthly SL distributions were generally stable over time. Mean shifts at VABI and TOWY were under 1.2 mm.
- Between sites, nearly all pairwise comparisons in 1978 were significantly different. The exceptions were TOWY and BCHRD in June and July.
- VABI males ranged from 16 to 40 mm SL, and Type III males were absent. BDGST males ranged from 18 to 60 mm SL (Table 2 lists a maximum of 59.1 mm for May 1978), and Type II males were present in small numbers through the summer.
- Between 1973 and 1982, VABI showed a subtle upward shift in male size. The shift reflected the whole distribution moving rather than a change in its shape.
- The BDGST May–June 1978 change was caused mainly by heavy mortality of small males.

**3. Allometric growth of secondary sexual traits.**
- Dorsal fin length, dorsal ray length, dorsal fin basal length, caudal fin length, and caudal peduncle depth increased allometrically with size.
- Gonopodium length and predorsal length decreased allometrically with size.
- SL predicted 86–98% (mean 95%) of the variation in most morphometric characters, the exception being PDIV, where it predicted 55%.
- Dorsal fin rays ranged from 12 to 15 (mean 13.3, n = 149) and were not correlated with SL.
- Subdorsal vertebrae ranged from 10–12 in small males to 14–17 in large males. The dorsal fin base expanded mainly through forward migration of its origin. The r value between transformed DFBL and PDL was −0.98.

**4. Population differences in morphology were small.**
- VABI and BDGST regression lines were very similar over the shared SL range (20–38 mm).
- Significant differences were found for dorsal fin length (F = 10.37), caudal fin length (F = 100.10), and caudal peduncle depth (F = 24.52). The gonopodium showed no significant difference.
- The dorsal fin lines cross at about 28 mm SL. VABI males are slightly shallower-bodied at all sizes.
- The author concluded that VABI males are about as well developed as BDGST males at any given size, and that Type III morphs would probably appear at VABI if males grew larger.

**5. Coloration tracks size, with social influence.**
- Small males are colored much like females, with faint yellow wash and little caudal pigment.
- Large males have blackened dorsal fin membranes, orange and iridescent blue colors, and 4–8 vertical bars on the trunk.
- Intermediate males vary substantially in color at the same size.
- In laboratory tanks, socially dominant small males developed more pigment and lateral bars. Field samples showed this development less dramatically.

**6. Hypotheses on the control of male size.**
- Luckner (1979) proposed a [[Sex-Linked Maturation Gene|P-locus]] model with early (P^E) and late (P^L) maturation alleles, which would correspond to Types I, III, and II (heterozygotes). The data neither confirm nor reject this model.
- Environmental factors (food, light, temperature), social interactions among males, and multiple alleles could all blur any discrete genetic morphs.
- The absence of Type III males in some populations may reflect growth limitation from the environment, not a lack of the genotype.

**7. Factors not supported as drivers.**
- Estuarine versus freshwater habitat did not explain size differences.
- No clear relationship was found between predation pressure and male size.
- Male size varied inversely with population density in general, but with exceptions (large Type III males were rare in suboptimal habitats).
- Male and female size covary, suggesting a shared environmental control such as food availability.

**8. Possible genetic divergence.**
- Hubbs (1936, 1942) suggested geographic variation in adult size. Southwestern populations reportedly show the sailfin phenotype at smaller sizes. Northeastern populations were described as "poorly developed."
- The author suggested that the small VABI–BDGST regression differences might indicate subtle genetic divergence, though the differences were small.

**Conclusion.** Male size and morphology in *P. latipinna* form a continuous, allometrically controlled gradient. Each population keeps a characteristic size distribution that changes little over time. Local environmental conditions, probably including growth limitation and density, appear to set the maximum male size, and that limit determines whether Type III males appear at all. The precise mechanisms remain poorly understood.
