# Spring-Summer Movements of Bonytail in a Colorado River Reservoir, Lake Mohave, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Open-File Report (99-103), prepared jointly with [[Arizona State University]] and authored by Paul C. Marsh and Gordon Mueller, documents two short-term sonic telemetry studies of [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) in [[Lake Mohave]] during spring-summer 1996 and 1997. The species is federally listed as endangered and Lake Mohave holds the largest remaining population. Twenty fish were implanted with sonic transmitters and tracked roughly weekly through August of each year. Most fish moved downstream into the lower [[Reservoir Canyon]] zone and remained there, while pond-reared fish showed more variable or upstream movement. The study also found that unmarked adult bonytail could be captured in areas frequented by tagged fish, suggesting telemetry may support brood-stock acquisition for the [[Repatriation Program]].

The document also includes a letter from Gordon Mueller (August 31, 1999) noting that Figure 1 (the waypoint map) was omitted from the printed report and enclosing a replacement copy, plus a mailing list of agency recipients.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Gila elegans* is a large-river cyprinid endemic to the Colorado River system. Its biology is poorly understood and its movements little studied.
- **Conservation status:** Federally listed as endangered. Bonytail persists in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona and Nevada) and [[Lake Havasu]] (Arizona and California), but is reliably captured only in Lake Mohave.
- **Native big-river fauna:** Three endemic minnows (bonytail, [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]) and one sucker ([[Razorback Sucker]]). Historically widespread, all four are now declined or restricted.
- **Drivers of decline:** Dam construction, diversions, impoundments and channelization, plus introduced non-native fishes. Main-stem dams and non-native interactions are considered the most significant impacts.
- **Recruitment problems:** Bonytail and razorback sucker rarely recruit to reproductive size in the wild. Humpback chub recruits only in a few reaches of the Grand Canyon region, and Colorado pikeminnow recruitment is limited to parts of the upper Green River basin.
- **Study fish:** 20 bonytail were implanted in total: 10 in May 1996 (5 wild-caught, 5 cove-reared) and 10 in April 1997 (7 wild-caught, 3 pond-reared). Pond-reared fish were 1994 or 1995 year-class spawn from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], reared at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] or [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]].
- **Tags and surgery:** Cylindrical sonic tags (Sonotronics MT-95-2; about 32 mm long, 8 mm diameter, 5 g) were surgically implanted under MS-222 anesthesia. Each fish also received a passive integrated transponder (PIT) tag. Surgery never exceeded 8 minutes of immersion. Tags were programmed to a 70 kHz range, with pulse interval extended to 17 s to extend nominal battery life from 47 to 90 days.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area:** Lake Mohave was divided into 5 zones: Black Canyon (Hoover Dam to WP 13), Interface (WP 13–24), Castle Cliff Basin (WP 24–40), Cottonwood Basin (WP 40–61), and Reservoir Canyon (WP 61 to Davis Dam). Monitoring used 71 numbered listening stations (waypoints) at about 1.6 km spacing, covering roughly 85% of the reservoir surface.
- **Hydrology (1996–1997):** Surface elevation varied slightly under 2 m (194.65–196.52 m MSL). Hoover Dam discharge averaged 471–500 m³/s; Davis Dam release averaged 462–498 m³/s.
- **Thermal regime:** In the deep Reservoir Canyon reach (up to about 40 m deep), summer surface temperatures exceeded 25 °C and may approach 30 °C, while water below 10 m may remain near 15 °C.
- **Habitat use:** Most locations were in shallow shoreline water, sometimes within inundated riparian vegetation. Daytime contacts were mostly in deep water along the steep Nevada shore, while nighttime contacts were in shallow water along gently sloping Arizona shorelines.
- **Detection limits:** Typical sonic detection range was at least 500 m and sometimes approached 1000 m under ideal conditions. Obstructions, turbulence, boat traffic and dam power generation reduced range.

## Key Findings

1. **Dispersal after release:** In 1996, eight of ten fish dispersed immediately. Five wild-caught fish moved down-lake as much as 40+ km (one stated as 4 km in the text) and four of them stayed in the lowest 5 km. Two cove-reared fish moved up-lake 15 and 43 km; a third moved 4 km down-lake. Two fish were never recontacted.
2. **1997 movements:** Of three pond-reared fish, two were not recontacted and one was lost 12 days after release after moving about 1.4 km. Of seven wild-caught fish, five were tracked 62 to 119 days, and all moved downstream into the lower Reservoir Canyon.
3. **Net displacement and cumulative distance (1997 wild-caught, tracked fish):** Net displacement averaged 5.4 km (range 4.7–6.9 km), all downstream. Cumulative distance averaged 28.9 km (range 19.9–33.0 km).
4. **Timing:** Most wild-caught fish reached their final range within 4 to 14 days of release.
5. **Diel habitat pattern:** Wild-caught bonytail spent most daylight hours in deep, open water (73% of deep-water contacts were during the day) and most night or low-light hours in shallow shoreline water (69% of shore contacts were at night or at crepuscular times). Among all contacts, about 76% were near shore and 24% in deeper water. Cove-reared fish were always near shore, while about 70% of wild-caught contacts were along shore.
6. **Repeated use of sites:** Fish in both years used the same areas, where they may remain for weeks.
7. **Association with unmarked fish:** Two or more tagged fish were contacted close together several times, and visual contact was made once. In June 1996, two telemetered fish guided trammel-net sets, which captured **4 unmarked adult bonytail** in shore-side nets near Arrowhead and Yoke Coves.
8. **Management recommendations:** Future wild-collection efforts should target shallow points and gravel bars along the Arizona shore in the lowermost Reservoir Canyon, as well as other lake areas. Post-stocking dispersal, distribution and habitat use of hatchery-produced bonytail remain key knowledge gaps. The authors suggest that external tagging techniques developed for [[Juvenile Razorback Sucker]] might reduce handling stress while focusing sampling on congregation sites.
9. **Program context:** The study was intended to replicate and corroborate the 1996 work, improve understanding of wild bonytail life history in the reservoir, and inform the ongoing repatriation program.
