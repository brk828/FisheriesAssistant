# Recent Developments in Conservation Genetics

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Philip W. Hedrick]] surveys how molecular genetics is applied to endangered species conservation. It argues that new molecular data (highly variable loci, large marker sets, extensive sequence data) greatly increase statistical power, but that statistical significance does not always equal biological meaning. The review covers four themes: neutral genetic variation as an indicator of biological importance, long-term [[Effective Population Size]] in the endangered [[Colorado River Big Fishes]], [[Inbreeding Depression]] and detrimental variation (with the [[Florida Panther]] as a case study), and adaptive variation, illustrated by [[Pathogen Resistance]] in [[Winter-Run Chinook Salmon]] and [[Major Histocompatibility Complex]] (MHC) variation in [[Red Wolf]]s. Most examples are mammals and birds; the author notes the same approaches should apply to endangered plants.

## Background & Description

The review traces the field from early population-genetics theory, which offered general rules (avoid inbreeding, maintain large population sizes, avoid prolonged low numbers), to the molecular era. Earlier recommendations assumed a typical neutral polymorphic locus model. Modern work uses [[Microsatellite Markers]], [[Mitochondrial DNA]] sequences and genome-project data to address questions such as past effective population size, population bottlenecks, gene flow, adaptive divergence and genetic load.

The author stresses that markers reflect neutral processes (mutation, drift, gene flow), whereas conservation depends on fitness-related traits. Neutral and adaptive variation can be correlated but the association is often weak.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Neutral versus biological significance.** Two kinds of mismatch are described:
- *False negative type:* In [[Scots Pine]] from Finland, neutral markers showed little differentiation between northern and southern populations (Karhu et al., 1996), yet adaptive quantitative traits differed strongly in common-garden experiments. Strong selection overcame gene flow; the markers were correct for neutral loci but missed adaptive differences.
- *False positive type:* With many highly variable markers, very small, real differences become statistically significant but may lack biological meaning.

**Colorado River big fishes.** Four endangered species were reviewed: [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]. The last wild Colorado pikeminnow in the lower river was caught in 1975. Bonytail are critically imperiled, with fewer than 1,000 wild fish (in Lake Mohave, possibly Lake Havasu), augmented by hatchery stock. Humpback chub persist in one viable population in the Little Colorado River–Grand Canyon complex, which declined to around 200 adults. Razorback sucker annual adult estimates fell to about 900 in 1999 and are thought to be around 400 now.

**Cheetah.** Low allozyme variation raised concern, but microsatellite surveys later showed substantial variation, and captive cheetahs show inbreeding depression in juvenile survival (Wielebnowski, 1996).

## Key Findings

### 1. Long-term effective population size of Colorado River fishes
- Garrigan et al. (2002) used mtDNA sequences and the maximum-likelihood program FLUCTUATE to estimate long-term Ne in three species (Colorado pikeminnow lacked samples).
- Samples and haplotypes: humpback chub 5 haplotypes from 18 fish; bonytail 3 haplotypes from 16; razorback sucker 10 haplotypes from 49.
- Mean pairwise divergence: about 1.5 nucleotides (humpback and razorback); 2.8 nucleotides (bonytail).
- Assumptions: mutation rate of 2 × 10⁻⁸ per nucleotide in mammals, about five times lower in fishes, giving 4 × 10⁻⁹ per year. Generation length was 10 years for chubs and 15 years for razorback sucker.
- Constant-size Ne estimates: humpback chub 48,800; bonytail chub 44,800; razorback sucker 223,000.
- Variable-size estimates with trend: humpback chub 74,400 (stable); bonytail chub 31,000 (declining); razorback sucker 313,600 (expanding).
- Conclusion: all three species were numerous until recently. Present census numbers are orders of magnitude below long-term Ne.

### 2. Effective size, census size and the 500 rule
- Franklin (1980) suggested Ne of about 500 to balance mutation and drift for neutral variation.
- Frankham (1995) reviewed published estimates and found Ne/N often near 0.10 (Ne about 10% of adult numbers). Maintaining variation may require census sizes near 5,000 adults.
- Lande (1995) estimated that up to 99% of new mutational variance may be unconditionally deleterious. Lynch and Lande (1998) noted that disease-resistance genes may mutate about 100-fold more slowly than quantitative traits, raising the required numbers further.

### 3. Inbreeding depression and purging
- Captive and laboratory estimates usually underestimate inbreeding depression in nature. In semi-natural conditions, inbred male mice achieved only about 20% of the reproductive success of outbred males (Meagher et al., 2000).
- Bijlsma et al. (1999) found inbreeding depression in *Drosophila melanogaster* increased greatly under stress.
- Purging by breeding relatives is inconsistent. Wang (2002) concluded it is not justified as a conservation strategy for populations of concern.
- Inherited disorders occur in captive endangered populations. Removing a recessive allele for chondrodystrophy in the [[California Condor]] would require about half the population to stop breeding (Ralls et al., 2000). Hereditary blindness in captive gray wolves could be reduced without greatly affecting the rest of the genome (Laikre et al., 1997).

### 4. Florida panther and genetic restoration
- The last remaining Florida panther population shows drift-fixed traits absent from other puma subspecies: high cryptorchidism, a kinked tail (last five vertebrae), a cowlick, and the poorest semen quality recorded in any felid (Roelke et al., 1993). Molecular variation is lower than in other North American pumas (Culver et al., 2000).
- Hedrick (1995) modelled gene flow of 5% in the first generation and 2.5% thereafter. Fitness improved quickly, and locally adapted alleles were only slightly reduced.
- Eight [[Texas Cougar]] females were released from 1995. Five produced offspring with resident males. About 20% of overall ancestry now comes from Texas cougars.
- Kinked tail fell from 88% (no Texas ancestry) to 1% in animals with Texas ancestry (progeny of backcrosses to Florida cats). Cowlick fell from 93% to a much lower level. Cryptorchidism was reduced to 0% among five evaluated males. One F1 male's semen quality matched or exceeded the average Texas cougar.

### 5. Adaptive variation and genome-wide scans
- Liberles et al. (2001) estimated adaptive evolution in 280 of 5,305 vertebrate genes examined (5.3%) and 123 of 3,385 plant genes (3.6%).
- Non-synonymous to synonymous substitution ratios indicate past selection, not necessarily current selection. Garrigan and Hedrick (2003) showed the signal is generated much faster than it is lost.
- Selective sweeps leave low variation and high linkage disequilibrium at tightly linked markers. Tishkoff et al. (2001) used this pattern to date adaptive alleles at the human G6PD locus.

### 6. Pathogen resistance in winter-run chinook salmon
- Winter-run chinook of the Sacramento River declined from over 100,000 spawners in 1969 to about 200 by the early 1990s. A refuge population was established at Bodega Marine Laboratory.
- Arkush et al. (2002) tested families for resistance to *Vibrio (Listonella) anguillarum* (bacterium), infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus (IHNV), and *Myxobolus cerebralis* (parasite).
- No difference in overall survival by MHC genotype was found for the bacterium. For IHNV, survival of MHC heterozygotes (0.82) exceeded that of homozygotes (0.25) in a significant subset of the 10 families.
- The selection coefficient estimated from these values was s = 0.085.
- Loss of MHC variation may increase disease susceptibility and mortality in small populations.

### 7. MHC variation in red wolves
- Red wolves declined through eradication, habitat loss, hybridization with coyotes and parasites. They were listed as endangered in 1967.
- A captive breeding program began in 1974 from 14 remnant individuals. Wild reintroduction in eastern North Carolina began in 1987.
- Four red wolf DRB alleles were compared with coyote, gray wolf and Mexican wolf alleles. Red wolf sequences were widely dispersed across the tree with high bootstrap support, consistent with shared ancestry with coyotes and balancing selection (Hedrick et al., 2002).

### 8. Neutral variation as an indicator of detrimental and adaptive variation
- Table 3 (summarized) predicts that bottlenecks and small populations often lower neutral, detrimental and adaptive variation, but the relationship depends on scenario:
  - *Bottleneck, shortly after:* low neutral, low adaptive variation.
  - *Bottleneck, some time after:* purging reduces detrimental variation; retention depends on mutation rate.
  - *Metapopulations with extinction and recolonization:* high neutral variation and fixation of different alleles across populations.
- The correlation between neutral and adaptive variation is often weak. High neutral variation can indicate adaptive potential, but low neutral variation does not necessarily mean low adaptive variation.

### 9. Overall conclusions
- Genetics will play a larger role in conservation as genome-project data accumulate.
- High statistical power from large data sets requires an evolutionary perspective to judge biological importance.
- Neutral markers are useful for inferring past non-selective processes (bottlenecks, gene flow), but predictions about adaptive variation require great caution.
- Endangered species with small histories may face combined threats from low MHC variation, high inbreeding and pathogen exposure, which could present a serious threat in coming decades.
