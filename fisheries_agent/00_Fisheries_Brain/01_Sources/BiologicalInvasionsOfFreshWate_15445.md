# Biological Invasions of Fresh Water: Empirical Rules and Assembly Theory

#ichthyology #marine-biology #invasion-biology

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Peter B. Moyle]] and [[Theo Light]] (Biological Conservation, 1996, pp. 149–161) examines how non-native fishes and other aquatic organisms invade freshwater and estuarine systems. Drawing on case histories from California streams, Sri Lankan rainforest streams, and the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary]], the authors propose a conceptual model of invasion (arrival, establishment, integration) and a set of empirically derived rules. They conclude that environmental factors are generally more decisive than biotic resistance, that existing [[Community Assembly Theory]] predicts invasion outcomes poorly, and that predictive capacity remains limited, so "the Frankenstein Effect is the one firm rule."

## Background & Description

Freshwater and estuarine biotas are changing rapidly worldwide, often with extirpation of native species. Notable examples include the loss of roughly 200 haplochromine cichlid species after the introduction of [[Nile Perch]] into [[Lake Victoria]], and changes to the [[Laurentian Great Lakes]] caused by [[Sea Lamprey]] and [[Zebra Mussel]]. The authors frame invasion biology around two approaches: case-history analysis and the application of [[Community Assembly Theory]].

Assembly theory holds that communities are built by continuing sequences of invasions and extinctions. Five generalizations from this literature are listed, including that invasion success declines with species richness and resident time, that residents enjoy priority effects, and that final community states depend on invasion order. The authors argue that assembly theory largely neglects abiotic conditions and environmental disturbance.

The conceptual model divides invasion into three phases: arrival, establishment, and integration. Establishment is governed by "ecological resistance," composed of environmental, biotic, and demographic elements. The authors view environmental resistance as the most critical factor in aquatic systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Case histories** (all led by the senior author over 10–15 years) include:

- **[[Eel River]], California:** Forty-plus years of invasions into a drainage historically dominated by anadromous fishes. Of ten invading species, only American Shad, Sacramento Squawfish, and California Roach were abundant in the river itself. Invaders from [[Pillsbury Reservoir]] were largely confined to the reservoir.
- **[[Deer Creek]], California:** The largest unregulated tributary to the Sacramento River, with intact native assemblages. Invasions were blocked mainly by hydrologic regime, and non-natives persisted only in human-altered reaches.
- **[[Martis Creek]], California:** A Sierra Nevada coldwater stream where [[Brown Trout]] came to dominate after replacing native cutthroat trout. Shifts were tied to spawning timing (fall for brown trout, spring for natives), and the assemblage was predicted to oscillate between states.
- **Sri Lankan streams:** Four species introduced from species-rich to species-poor streams all became widespread within five years, without major niche shifts in native fishes.
- **[[Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary]]:** The largest Pacific Coast estuary in North America, repeatedly invaded since 1850. The 1989 flood, which briefly converted the estuary to freshwater, was followed by an invasion of the [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Potamocorbula amurensis*). Exotic fishes made up 27 species, or 60% of the fish fauna of the low-salinity zone.

The Eel River and Deer Creek contrast illustrates that invasion success depends largely on whether a system's hydrologic regime is naturally variable and predictable.

## Key Findings

**Conceptual and theoretical conclusions**

- Environmental resistance is generally more important than biotic resistance in determining invasion outcomes in aquatic systems, except perhaps in early establishment when invader numbers are small.
- Predictions from [[Community Assembly Theory]] have limited usefulness for aquatic invasions. Invasion outcomes depend more on environmental regime and invader traits than on invasion sequence or community structure.
- Community structure "generally seems to have little to do with the success or failure of invasions."

**Empirical rules (six from Moyle and Light, 1996; six added in this paper)**

The authors state that these rules provide a basis for testable hypotheses. Key rules include:

1. **Most invaders fail to become established.** Failures are rarely recorded, and invasion is a function of the frequency of introduction and narrow windows of suitable conditions.
2. **Most successful invasions integrate without major community effects.** Mixed native–introduced assemblages often resemble co-evolved ones, though piscivores are a notable exception.
3. **All aquatic systems are invasible, and invasibility is not strongly related to resident diversity.** The authors acknowledge this conflicts with the widely held view that low-diversity communities are most susceptible.
4. **Major community effects are most often observed where species richness is low.** Western North American streams with five to 10 native species frequently show extinctions following invasion, whereas eastern streams with 20 or more species show invasions without equivalent losses.
5. **Piscivores and omnivores/detritivores are most likely to succeed in minimally disturbed systems, but piscivores alone most often alter invaded communities.** Omnivores and detritivores rarely limit food but often show success through life-history traits.
6. **Long-term success requires a close physiological and life-history match** between the invader and the system, as shown by [[California Roach]] and [[Sacramento Squawfish]] in the Eel River.
7. **Invasions succeed most when native assemblages are depleted or disrupted,** exemplified by the Sacramento-San Joaquin Estuary and the Great Lakes salmonids following earlier invasions.
8. **Long-term integration is more likely in permanently human-altered systems,** such as reservoirs and estuaries, which resemble one another across regions.
9. **Invasibility depends on environmental variability, predictability, and severity.** Invaders are least likely to establish in highly variable but predictable environments and most likely where variability is low or variability is moderate but unpredictable (Table 2).
10. **Invaders are most likely to extirpate natives in systems with either extremely high or extremely low variability or severity,** such as desert springs and tropical lakes.

**Specific case-based results**

- [[Lake Victoria]]: Nile Perch eliminated about 200 endemic cichlids within a decade. Four tilapiine species were introduced at the same time, with *Oreochromis niloticus* persisting in large numbers.
- [[Eagle Lake]], California: A highly alkaline lake (pH 8–9) with four native fishes and no established exotics. Largemouth Bass, once a notable fishery, disappeared during the 1930s drought when alkalinity rose.
- [[Lake Tahoe]]: Neutral pH, successfully invaded by at least four non-native fishes and two macroinvertebrates.
- Sepik River, New Guinea: Three detritivorous species introduced for food production were judged low risk after extensive surveys.

**Overall conclusion**

The authors consider a predictive model feasible at a regional scale but doubt it will be useful for local managers, where patterns are "idiosyncratic and complex." Until predictive ability improves, they advise managers to assume that new invasions will have unexpected consequences.
