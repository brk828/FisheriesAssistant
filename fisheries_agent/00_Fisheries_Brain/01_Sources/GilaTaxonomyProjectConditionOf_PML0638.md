# Gila Taxonomy Project: Condition of Received DNA and Alcohol-Preserved Samples

#ichthyology #marine-biology #molecular-systematics

## Executive Summary

This is a 1993 letter from [[Thomas Dowling]] of Arizona State University's Department of Zoology to [[Wayne Starnes]] at the [[National Museum Of Natural History]], [[Smithsonian Institution]]. It reports on a shipment of samples for the [[Gila Taxonomy Project]] that arrived from [[Brigham Young University]] on June 23, 1993. The shipment came in eight coolers holding both [[DNA Samples]] and [[Alcohol-Preserved Tissue]]. Dowling judged most of the material to be of doubtful value because of unknown contents, mislabelling, contamination and poor storage. He will not base his studies on the DNA samples. He also objects to the way some museum specimens were sampled, which he describes as destructive.

The document does not give species names, habitat or geographic data. It is a correspondence item, so its main points are operational findings and recommendations rather than research results.

## Background & Description

- **Project:** The [[Gila Taxonomy Project]] is a systematics study that required tissue and DNA material from multiple sources.
- **Shipment:** The material was sent from [[Brigham Young University]] and received at Arizona State University on June 23, 1993. The letter is dated June 25, 1993.
- **Contents:** Eight coolers held two kinds of material:
  - *DNA extracts*, stored on small pieces of filter paper, some in liquid and some dry, in individually labelled tubes. The tubes may contain total DNA, amplification products or clones, and the labels do not say which.
  - *Alcohol-preserved tissues* in glass vials with loose-fitting caps. Each vial had a cap label, and the vials were placed in ziploc bags labelled by box of origin.
- **Correspondence:** The letter was copied to L. Crist.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document does not describe the organisms, their habitat, their distribution or any environmental variables. The only ecological content is indirect: the samples are museum or field tissue, and the letter discusses how specimen handling and storage affect the quality of molecular data.

Relevant points for future sample handling include:

- **Storage:** DNA samples were placed in a -20°C freezer. Dowling notes that long-term DNA storage is normally done in buffer, and he is unsure how to handle samples stored this way.
- **Preservation:** Alcohol-preserved samples should be held in tightly closing containers or sealed with parafilm to stop evaporation. Dowling had a student refill the vials with 95% alcohol and refrigerate them.
- **Specimen damage:** Some tissue from [[Museum Specimens]] was removed carelessly, which Dowling says seriously damaged valuable historical material.

## Key Findings

1. **DNA samples arrived in apparently good physical condition.** The coolers still held dry ice, and the tubes were individually labelled. However, the labels do not reveal what each tube contains.
2. **Sample identity cannot be verified.** Without knowledge of each tube's contents and handling history, screening every tube would be futile. Dowling says the labelling system will need the sender's knowledge to decode.
3. **DNA samples are considered unusable.** The letter states that the DNA samples were contaminated and that their storage conditions are unusual. Dowling will not base his results on them.
4. **Alcohol-preserved samples were in poor condition.**
   - More than half of the vials arrived with no alcohol, because the caps were loose.
   - At least one sample was desiccated.
   - Alcohol leaked from the vials and smeared the cap labels and bag labels, making them hard to read.
   - Some samples were pink, possibly from ink or dye that leaked from the paper.
5. **Repackaging caused possible mix-ups.** Samples were removed from their storage boxes for shipment. Dowling sorted them into new boxes using the paperwork in each bag, which was meant to link each sample to its box. He notes that the labels are not always distinctive, so sample identity may have been compromised.
6. **PCR suitability is doubtful.** [[Polymerase Chain Reaction]] analyses depend strongly on DNA quality. Many samples are very small and are unlikely to yield much DNA even under good conditions, so Dowling is not convinced they will meet the project's needs.
7. **Destructive sampling of museum material is objectionable.** Dowling calls the handling of some museum specimens unacceptable. He suggests that curators may start excluding molecular systematists from their collections to prevent further damage.

## Recommendations and Next Steps

- Ask the sender to document the contents, type and handling of each DNA tube, since the letter says this is needed to interpret the samples.
- Decide how to handle DNA samples stored dry or in liquid on filter paper, and whether to re-extract or discard them. This is an open question in the letter.
- Store alcohol-preserved samples in sealed containers or with parafilm, and check fluid levels regularly.
- Have the sending lab's labelling system explained before any further analysis, and link each sample to its paperwork.
- Set clearer standards for tissue removal from museum specimens, so that molecular sampling does not damage historical material.
