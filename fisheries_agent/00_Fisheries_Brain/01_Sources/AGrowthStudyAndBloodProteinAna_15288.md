# A growth study and blood protein analysis of two subspecies of largemouth bass; the Florida bass, Micropterus salmoides floridanus (LeSeuer) and the Northern bass, Micropterus salmoides salmoides (Lacepede), in San Diego county, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #freshwater-fisheries #[[Largemouth Bass]]

## Executive Summary

This 1965 California Department of Fish and Game administrative report (Lee W. Miller) presents a comparative growth study and immunogenetic (blood protein) analysis of two subspecies of largemouth bass — the [[Florida Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides floridanus*) and the [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides salmoides*) — introduced together into [[Red Mountain Reservoir]], San Diego County, California. A total of 380 fish (189 Florida bass, 191 northern bass) were tagged or fin-clipped and released to assess whether growth rates differed between subspecies in an identical environment. No statistically significant difference in growth rate was found between the two subspecies. However, recapture data showed Florida bass were caught significantly more often than expected, suggesting differential catchability, mortality, or survival rather than growth superiority. Immunogenetic (heteroimmune and isoimmune) blood-typing experiments revealed that antigenic differences between the two subspecies were minimal, indicating the subspecies are immunologically very closely related — more so than comparably studied strains of [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Golden Trout]], or [[Brown Trout]]. The study concluded there was insufficient evidence to justify preferentially extending the range of Florida bass over northern bass for fisheries management purposes.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic subjects:** Two subspecies of largemouth bass (family [[Centrarchidae]]):
  - *Micropterus salmoides floridanus* (LeSueur) — Florida Bass
  - *Micropterus salmoides salmoides* (Lacepède) — Northern Bass
- **Historical context:** Florida largemouth bass were first introduced to San Diego County at [[Upper Otay Reservoir]] in 1959 by the San Diego County Fish and Game Commission, on the premise that this subspecies would grow faster and larger than the native northern subspecies (Sasaki, 1961).
- **Research questions posed:**
  1. Are Florida bass phenotypically/physiologically distinct enough (growth, catchability) to warrant separate fisheries management?
  2. Should Florida bass distribution be expanded or restricted relative to northern bass?
  3. Do the two subspecies hybridize?
- **Study design:** Two parallel experimental programs:
  1. A controlled growth-rate comparison of both subspecies stocked together in [[Red Mountain Reservoir]] (a small, 10-acre impoundment near Fallbrook, CA), closed to public fishing.
  2. An immunogenetic (blood protein) study using **heteroimmunization** (rabbit antisera) and **isoimmunization** (cross-injection between the two bass subspecies) to test for antigenic/genetic distinctness and potential for hybridization.
- **Identification methods:** Fish were marked with numbered disk-dangler tags (per [[Kimsey 1956]] methodology) or fin-clipped — right pelvic fin removed for northern bass, left pelvic fin removed for Florida bass.
- **Recovery methods:** Angling, gill netting, and electrofishing across multiple sampling dates (1962–1963).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Red Mountain Reservoir — a small, closed, 10-acre freshwater impoundment, selected specifically to control for environmental variability between the two subspecies during growth comparison.
- **Growing season:** Defined as May 1 – November 1, used as the standardized time base for calculating weekly growth increments.
- **Confounding environmental/anthropogenic factors:**
  - **Poaching** at the reservoir hampered accurate evaluation of growth and survival.
  - Northern bass were stocked in two separate batches at different times (A-12 lot of 111 fish and a later B-30 lot of 106 fish), introducing size and timing biases.
  - Late-season stocking of one northern bass group may have adversely affected their survival.
- **Comparative ecological note:** Clugston (1964) found in Florida that northern bass fingerlings transplanted from Iowa grew comparably to native Florida bass, suggesting Florida bass' typically rapid growth is linked more to **favorable native climate/growing season** than to intrinsic genetic superiority — consistent with this study's conclusions.
- **Broader relevance:** Findings bear on inland fisheries management practices regarding stocking non-native bass subspecies and potential ecological/genetic consequences of hybridization in reservoir "balance" (fecundity, genetic fitness).

## Key Findings

### Growth Comparison
- 189 Florida bass and 191 northern bass tagged/fin-clipped and released; overall tag return rate was low (**13.4%**).
- Mean fork length at stocking: Florida bass = 6.56 in; Northern bass = 7.32 in (per Table 2 summary).
- Usable growth comparison limited to fish with reliable tag data: **13 Florida bass** vs. **14 Northern bass**.
- **Mean growth rate:**
  - Florida bass: **0.089 inches/week** of growing season
  - Northern bass: **0.098 inches/week** of growing season
- Student's t-test (27 df, P=0.10): difference in mean growth rate **not statistically significant**.
- F-test (f1=13, f2=12, P=0.01): F = 1.77 — **no significant difference in variance** between the two samples.
- One marked Florida bass recovered at Lower Otay Reservoir (April 1965) weighed **9 lb 1 oz**, though comparably large northern bass are also caught occasionally in San Diego reservoirs.

### Recapture Frequency / Catchability
- Marked-fish recaptures across the study period totaled **48** (33 Florida bass, 15 Northern bass) — see Table 3.
- **July 24, 1963:** 22 fish recaptured (17 Florida bass, 5 Northern bass); chi-square = **5.50** (1 df, P=0.05) — significant.
- **September 27, 1963:** 11 fish recaptured (9 Florida bass, 2 Northern bass); chi-square = **4.54** (1 df, P=0.05) — significant.
- **Total across study:** chi-square = **6.74** (1 df, P=0.05) — significant.
- Florida bass were recaptured disproportionately more often than expected given near-equal stocking numbers — possible explanations include differential catchability, differential mortality (natural vs. fishing, especially poaching), or handling/timing differences, though the study could not isolate the definitive cause.

### Immunogenetic (Heteroimmunization) Studies
- Nine rabbits were immunized with pooled, washed erythrocytes from each bass subspecies (Florida bass from [[Upper Otay Reservoir]] and [[Miramar Reservoir]]; Northern bass from [[Yorba Linda Reservoir]] and [[Hodges Reservoir]]).
- Antisera agglutination tested via serial dilution (to 1/2048) using capillary tube method; scored 0 (–) to 4 (strongest clumping), with "tr" (trace) as weakest positive.
- Rabbit antisera showed strong agglutination reactions with **both** subspecies' erythrocytes at low dilutions, with reactions fading similarly for both at higher dilutions (Tables 4–6) — indicating **little antigenic distinction** detectable by this method.
- **Absorption experiments** (Table 7): antisera were completely exhausted of agglutinating activity by absorption with erythrocytes of **either** subspecies, confirming that heteroimmune antisera could not reliably discriminate between Florida bass and Northern bass antigens.

### Immunogenetic (Isoimmunization) Studies
- 110 fish were isoimmunized via intraperitoneal/subcutaneous cross-injection (Florida bass injected with Northern bass cells and vice versa); optimal protocol = 0.25 cc weekly for 4 weeks (lowest mortality).
- Isoimmune sera revealed **some immunological differences** between individuals, but **few differences were characteristic of one subspecies versus the other** (Table 8) — i.e., variation appeared more individual than subspecies-diagnostic.
- Comparison to other salmonid work (Calaprice, unpublished) showed this isoimmune technique **can** distinguish even closely related strains (e.g., two brown trout strains separated only ~10 years) — implying that the Florida bass/Northern bass immunogenetic closeness reflects a genuinely **minimal degree of genetic divergence**, not a limitation of the method.

### Conclusions & Management Implications
- Phenotypic and immunogenetic differences between Florida bass and Northern bass are **not substantial**.
- Florida bass' reputation for rapid growth is likely attributable to **favorable native environmental conditions** rather than intrinsic genetic superiority (consistent with [[Clugston 1964]] Florida transplant study).
- The two subspecies are likely to **hybridize freely** without apparent adverse genetic effects, given their close immunogenetic relatedness.
- **Recommendation:** Insufficient evidence exists to justify preferential range expansion of Florida bass over Northern bass in California inland waters at that time. Further study needed on comparative fishing mortality and natural mortality rates between subspecies.
