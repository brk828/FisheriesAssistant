# Sonic Telemetry of Bonytail in Lake Havasu, Arizona-California (2005)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report by C.O. Minckley (Arizona Fishery Resources Office, Parker, AZ, July 2006) describes a 2005 sonic telemetry study of adult [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) in [[Lake Havasu]]. Twelve hatchery-reared adults from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] were surgically tagged, released, and tracked from February to October 2005. The study aimed to quantify immediate post-release dispersal, seasonal and diel movement and habitat use, and possibly home range. Eleven fish survived surgery. Most moved within two weeks of release and then remained largely stationary in coves and along shorelines. Tracking yielded no usable seasonal movement data because fish had stopped moving by June.

## Background & Description

**Species status.** The bonytail is the most endangered cyprinid in North America. It was once one of the most abundant native fishes of the lower [[Lower Colorado River]] but became increasingly rare after the mid-1930s. The last stronghold was [[Lake Mohave]], where wild fish were collected in the 1980s to establish a broodstock. That broodstock still supports stocking programs across the Colorado River basin, but no wild fish have been successfully added to it since. The species was listed as endangered in 1991, and recovery goals followed in 2004 (USFWS 2004).

**Morphology and life history.** Bonytail is a "big-river" fish reaching about 60 cm total length and 1 kg. It has embedded scales, a narrow caudal peduncle, and a large caudal fin for swift swimming. It is an omnivore, eating invertebrates, small fishes, and drifting material. Spawning occurs between March and May, depending on the year. Eggs are adhesive and are scattered over the substrate without a nest. Eggs hatch in 7-10 days depending on water temperature, and the young disperse into shoreline habitats that offer riparian vegetation and debris for cover.

**Management context.** The Lake Havasu Fisheries Improvement Program began in 1991. One goal was to stock 30,000 bonytail and 30,000 [[Razorback Sucker]] (>25 cm) to increase endangered fish populations, and this goal was met for both species by 2003. Understanding how stocked adults disperse and use habitat was intended to guide future stocking strategies.

**Prior studies.** Earlier telemetry work on bonytail movement and habitat use was carried out in Lake Mohave (90 days) and at High Levee Pond on [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] (60 days). Both were of short duration.

**Study area.** [[Lake Havasu]] was formed by the closure of [[Parker Dam]] (river mile 1) in 1938. The main body of the lake is about 28 miles long, and the lake shares its shoreline between Arizona and California. Depths reach about 70 ft near the dam, with shallow sand-silt areas (about 6 ft) on the refuge and in the north end. The tagged fish were mainly found between river mile 5 ([[Steamboat Cove]]) and river mile 15 ([[Mallard Cove]]), in water up to 30 ft deep. This area is characterized by open water and many shallow coves that extend well into the shoreline.

**Methods.**
- Twelve adults (6 females, 6 males) were held 24 hours in a floating live car, surgically implanted, then released about 2 miles from the surgery site into a cove near [[Havasu Palms Resort]].
- Fish were tracked continuously for the first week using a single [[Sonic Telemetry]] receiver (2-person boat crew with hydrophone). Locations were recorded as UTM coordinates with a handheld GPS and classified as open water, backwater, shoreline, cove, or down-lake.
- After week one, tracking shifted to twice weekly through March, then weekly from April, then biweekly from June to September, alternating day and night.
- Net and gross displacement were calculated. A linear regression of distance against days post-release was planned to predict per-day travel distances. Seasonal habitat use was described as percent frequency of occurrence, and a [[Chi-Square Analysis]] (alpha = 0.05) was planned to compare habitat use within and among seasons. Movement was mapped using [[Home Range Extension]] in ArcView.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Havasu is a regulated reservoir on a river once controlled by hydroelectric dams, and nonnative fishes have been introduced. The study used shoreline coves with cattails, riparian vegetation, cobble-boulder substrate, and cliff faces as the main habitats. Cover is important for adult bonytail during daylight hours, and cove habitats were heavily used. Several tagged fish settled in coves that contained cattails or stands of *Potamogeton* (pondweed), and some were found near dead trees or in crevices along the shoreline.

Habitat availability data were not available, so habitat use could not be assessed as selection or preference. Results were therefore descriptive only.

## Key Findings

**Survival and tag performance**
- Of 12 fish released, 11 survived anesthesia and surgery and swam away when returned to the cove. Fish 69 likely voided its tag immediately or died.
- Tracking ran from February 2005 to 16 October 2005. Tags were recovered from 5 fish, and contact was lost with the remaining 7 of 12 tags over the summer and into September.

**Dispersal and movement**
- Eleven fish moved after release. Four (36%) initially moved up lake, five (45%) initially moved down lake, and two (18%) stayed within 0.5 km of the release site.
- At the end of the study, 5 fish (45%) were up lake of the release site, 4 (36%) were down lake, and 2 (19%) remained relatively stationary.
- All movement occurred within the first two weeks. Fish then remained in place, with only local movement within their coves.
- Maximum up-lake displacement was 2.54 km (Fish 80). Maximum down-lake displacement was 2.65 km (Fish 70). Maximum cumulative distance was 7.2 km (Fish 70).
- No seasonal movement could be determined because all fish had stopped moving by the end of June.

**Habitat use**
- Most contacts were in shoreline or cove habitat (mean 82%, range 62-95%). The remainder were in open water (mean 16%, range 4-36%).

**Individual highlights**
- Fish 70 (female) traveled farthest, moved into [[Whipple Bay]], and was contacted 36 times.
- Fish 71 (male) settled in [[Balance Rock Cove]] after moving up lake, with 8 recorded moves and 71 contacts.
- Fish 75 (female) moved into [[Pilot Rock Cove]] and was contacted 65 times.
- Fish 73 (female) settled along a cliff face with 5 m of water over cobble and boulder.

**Study limitations and conclusions**
- The study was limited by high tag loss (7 of 12 tags) and by the sharp drop in movement after the first two weeks, which prevented seasonal analysis.
- Results suggest that hatchery-reared adult bonytail disperse actively in the first two weeks after release and then settle into coves and shoreline cover. This information is relevant to managers considering the stocking of non-conditioned, hatchery-reared bonytail into Lake Havasu.
