# Nutritional Limitation of Recruitment in the Razorback Sucker (Xyrauchen texanus)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This research proposal, authored by Michael J. Horn (July 1992), tests whether nutritional constraints during the early larval phase act as a key factor regulating recruitment to the adult population of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]], the largest remaining population of the species. The proposal frames the problem through population regulation theory and [[Life-History Strategy]] concepts, arguing that the larval stage is the main bottleneck because adult populations show no successful recruitment. It proposes a physiological and bioenergetic approach, combining field sampling in Lake Mohave and an enclosed backwater, [[Yuma Cove]], with laboratory feeding experiments and a [[Growth Model]].

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is an iteroparous catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Historically abundant and formerly present across most major basin rivers and tributaries, it was harvested for food and fertilizer. Populations declined from the early twentieth century; extirpation was nearly complete in the Salt and Gila River drainages by the late 1950s. The species was federally listed as endangered in November 1991 (USFWS 1991).

Lake Mohave formed after [[Davis Dam]] closed in 1954. The lake holds an estimated 60,000 razorback suckers, apparently the survivors of pre-closure populations (Paul Marsh, pers. comm.). Females of 45.0–70.0 cm length produce roughly 1,700–1,800 ova/cm of body length, equating to about 100,000 eggs per fish. Larvae disappear at about 10.6 mm total length and 2–3 weeks of age, and no recruitment into the adult population has been documented for more than 30 years. Adults are estimated to live 40–50 years, and the species was predicted to disappear within a decade without augmentation (Minckley et al. 1991).

The species has a J-shaped survivorship curve, with mortality highest in larval and juvenile stages. The author argues that the early post-hatching phase, not egg production, is the principal regulatory point, because eggs are produced by adults in condition set by environmental factors largely uncorrelated with larval survival.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document identifies three principal agents of larval/juvenile mortality (Nikolskii 1969): adverse abiotic conditions, predators (including parasites and disease), and lack of food. Three alternative hypotheses explain larval loss in Lake Mohave:

- **Transport out of the system:** larvae may be entrained by subsurface currents; sampling below Davis Dam found no individuals that could have passed through the dam.
- **Predation:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] were probably historic predators; introduced [[Common Carp]] and [[Striped Bass]] are now abundant. A backwater breached by rising lake levels lost its larvae within weeks to predators, including bluegill.
- **Nutritional limitation:** zooplankton densities are typically low, highly variable, and sometimes unavailable because of larval gape limitation. Larvae in the lake often have empty stomachs and are smaller at comparable sizes than hatchery fish from Dexter National Fish Hatchery.

Yuma Cove, a 0.95–2.1 ha bay separated from Lake Mohave by a gravel berm, is hydrologically connected and limnologically similar to the lake. Ichthyocide renovation rendered it predator-free. In January 1992, 27 females and 60 males from Parker Bay were stocked there for spawning.

## Key Findings

- **Central hypothesis:** mortality from nutritional constraints during the early larval phase is a key factor regulating adult recruitment in Lake Mohave.
- **Recruitment failure:** no documented recruitment for at least 30 years (McCarthy and Minckley 1987; Minckley 1983).
- **Critical period:** during the switch from endogenous to exogenous feeding, inadequate food combined with low energy reserves may cause irreversible starvation. Papoulias (1988) found mortality from feeding delays shorter than 15 days to be minimal.
- **Food items:** larvae shift from diatoms and phytoplankton to cladocerans, copepods and microchironomids. Lake Mohave larvae had fewer food items than comparably sized Dexter hatchery larvae.
- **Cui-ui comparison:** in [[Pyramid Lake]], the related [[Cui-ui]] suffers similar recruitment failure. Small changes in larval survivorship produce several-hundred-percent variation in adult population size, and the USFWS survival value of 0.001–0.003 translates to about ±100,000 adults per year class.
- **Biggest bottleneck:** survival from hatching to age one (25–35 cm TL) is the largest barrier to recruitment, since fish at that size have outgrown most predators.

**Methods proposed:**
- Weekly larval collections from Lake Mohave and Yuma Cove (February–April), with diel sampling and otolith aging.
- Condition indices: morphometrics (discriminant analysis), histology (grading of gut, notochord and muscle tissues), and lipid content (diethyl ether extraction). The author expects lipid analysis may be of limited use early in life, since protein is more affected by starvation.
- Zooplankton, benthos, chlorophyll, dissolved oxygen, temperature and pH sampling.
- Four-treatment laboratory feeding experiment (starvation, delayed feeding after 15–18 days, suboptimal ration of 4–6 nauplii per fish per feeding, and ad libitum feeding at about 120 nauplii per fish per feeding), each in triplicate and run at five temperatures from 10 to 20 °C.
- A bioenergetics model (Hewett and Johnson 1982 program) to predict foraging requirements, growth, survivorship and the duration larvae spend at predation-vulnerable sizes.

**Conservation framing:** the author distinguishes stocking, a "half-way technology" that adds individuals without addressing causes of decline (Frazier 1992), from identifying life-history bottlenecks and their environmental drivers. Stocking is acknowledged as a short-term goal to maintain a viable population while a management plan is developed.

**Timeline:** two spawning seasons (January–April 1992–1993), with analysis and writing in 1993.
