# Habitat and Biology of the Loach Minnow (Tiaroga cobitis) in New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #tiaroga-cobitis

## Executive Summary

This study examines the status, life history, and microhabitat requirements of the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) in the warmwater reaches of the [[Gila River]] and [[San Francisco River]] drainages of southwestern New Mexico. The species has declined sharply across its historic range, and by the early 1990s it persisted mainly in a few comparatively undisturbed streams. The authors combined field surveys at four sites, microhabitat measurements, age and growth analysis, reproductive assessment, and diet analysis. They found that the species is a specialized, benthic riffle dweller that spawns briefly in spring and depends on natural flow regimes and native fish communities. Its persistence is tied to flow dynamics, and human alterations of streams are identified as the main threat.

## Background & Description

*Tiaroga cobitis* was once widespread in low- to mid-elevation streams of the Gila River drainage in Arizona, Sonora, and New Mexico. Its range declined over roughly 50–75 years because of altered flow regimes, habitat destruction, and introduction of non-native species. By the study period it was found mainly in four relatively undisturbed streams: [[Aravaipa Creek]] and the [[Blue River]] in Arizona, and portions of the Gila and San Francisco drainages in New Mexico. The species is federally listed as threatened (U.S. Department of the Interior, 1986), yet its biology was poorly known.

The study had three stated objectives:
1. Document the status of *T. cobitis* in New Mexico.
2. Describe its life history and microhabitat associations.
3. Identify threats to its continued existence.

Study sites:
- **[[Tularosa River]]:** mean width 3 m; shallow runs with short riffles and pools 0.5–1.2 m deep; cobble and rubble riffles; mean mid-day water temperature 9.6 °C in winter and 24.3 °C in summer.
- **Forks Site ([[West Fork Gila River]] and [[Middle Fork Gila River]]):** mean widths of about 12 m; pools 0.5–2.0 m deep at boulder, cliff, and tree bases; mean winter temperatures of 9.1 °C (West) and 10.0 °C (Middle); mean summer temperatures of 19.7 °C (West) and 23.8 °C (Middle); riparian vegetation including cottonwood, Arizona alder, and box elder.
- **[[Cliff-Gila Valley]] (Gila River):** mean width 22–45 m; runs with riffles and pools 0.5–1.0 m deep, often associated with uprooted trees and debris; mean winter temperature 12.8 °C and summer 22.1 °C; cottonwoods and Arizona sycamore in the riparian zone.

The study area was the warmwater reaches of the Gila and San Francisco drainages in Catron, Grant, and Hidalgo counties. Fieldwork relied on [[Electrofishing]], seining, and dip-netting. Specimens were preserved and deposited in the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish Laboratory and the University of New Mexico Museum of Southwestern Biology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream geography and flow.** Populations were concentrated in reaches with a stream gradient of roughly 3–6 m/km, which implies suitable [[Riffle Habitat]] availability. The exceptions were the Tularosa River and Dry Blue Creek, with gradients of 10.9 and 20.9 m/km. No populations were found above about 1980 m elevation. Reaches were classified as "V-canyon," canyon, or valley. V-canyon reaches had large substrates, faster water, and deep run and cascade riffles. Canyon reaches had a mix of runs, riffles, and pools. Valley reaches were dominated by runs.

**Microhabitat preferences.** The preferred habitat of *T. cobitis* was riffles with substrates from sand to rubble, water velocities of about 0–100 cm/s, and depths of 6–40 cm. Each life stage showed distinct preferences:
- **Embryos:** large gravel to rubble, water velocity of 30–40 cm/s, depth around 15 cm.
- **Larvae:** slightly deeper and slower water, with smaller substrate particles.
- **Juveniles:** shallower, faster water with larger substrates than larvae.
- **Adults:** velocities mostly 24–80 cm/s, gravel to cobble substrate, depths of 12–27 cm.

Geographic differences in microhabitat use among juveniles and adults were minor and were probably related to habitat availability.

**Natural disturbance.** The region has unpredictable precipitation, prolonged droughts, and severe floods. Floods can scour and reshape riffles, and low flows can shrink riffle area. Two major floods occurred in the Gila Basin during the study period, but no appreciable negative impacts on *T. cobitis* populations were detected.

**Associated fishes.** Six native species occurred within the New Mexico range: [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*, Gila River only), *Rhinichthys osculus*, *Catostomus insignis*, and *C. clarki*. Only *R. osculus* and *C. clarki* regularly used the same riffles, and their microhabitats differed from that of *T. cobitis*. Of 15 recently documented non-native species, only three were found in habitats occupied by *T. cobitis*: fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), and [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), which preyed on the species.

**Threats.** Water diversion for agriculture dewatered sections of the San Francisco River, fragmenting populations. Stream regulation that eliminated natural flooding reduced habitat quality. Non-native fishes showed little direct evidence of negative effects, and the species persisted only where native fishes dominated.

## Key Findings

**Status and distribution**
- No populations were found above about 1980 m elevation.
- Current populations are patchy and uncommon across most of the range. The species remains comparatively common only in streams with relatively natural flow regimes, low water withdrawal, and native fish dominance.
- The authors concluded that *T. cobitis* was probably never abundant historically, because riffle habitat is patchily distributed and population densities in optimal habitat appeared low.

**Life history and reproduction**
- Spawning occurred for a brief period in April 1984 at daytime water temperatures of 16–20 °C.
- Eggs were deposited on the undersides of cobbles in moderate-velocity riffles.
- Males and females were sexually mature by their second spring (Age I). Age-I fish made up about 60–70% of the spawning population, and Age-I females produced most of the year's eggs.
- Larval growth was rapid. By autumn, Age-0 fish were 30–35 mm SL. Growth slowed afterward, and few fish exceeded 60 mm SL or reached Age II.
- Largest specimens: a female of 56 mm SL (Age II, 13 April 1984) and a male of 62 mm SL (Age II or III, 14 March 1984).
- Females were significantly larger than males in the 1982 year-class (t = 2.41, P < 0.05) but not in the 1983 year-class (t = 0.72, P > 0.05).
- Male and female length–weight relationships did not differ significantly during spawning (ANCOVA, F = 1.73, P > 0.05).
- Maximum longevity was estimated at about 36 months, but most individuals did not survive beyond 18 months.

**Age structure**
- The Cliff-Gila population comprised two to three age groups throughout the study.
- In 1984, Age-I fish made up 66–77% of the population before spawning, 79% during spawning, and 7% in August.
- Age-0 fish made up 63–92% of the population from June to August 1984.
- Other populations (Tularosa and Forks) had similar age structures.

**Diet**
- All life stages were exclusively insectivorous in all seasons.
- Larvae and juveniles fed mainly on chironomid larvae and ephemeropteran naiads (mainly Baetidae).
- Adults also consumed Plecoptera, Trichoptera (mainly Hydropsychidae), and Simuliidae.
- Ephemeropteran naiads were the most frequent and volumetrically most important food item. Dipteran larvae were numerous but made up a small volume of stomach contents.
- Diet in the Gila River did not differ notably from that in Aravaipa Creek.

**Behavior and adaptations**
- The authors attribute the species' resistance to flood displacement partly to its position in riffles, between the slow shoreline and fast midstream. It moves toward the center as flows decline and away from it as flows rise.
- Adhesive eggs on rock undersides in well-oxygenated, moderate-velocity water keep embryos in riffles.
- The benthic, cryptic habits and early maturity reduce predation risk. The only potential native predator, *Gila robusta*, is a pool dweller and is unlikely to encounter the species.
- Similar traits occur in other small freshwater fishes, but no other American Southwest fluvial species was documented to have this full combination.

**Conservation conclusions**
- Traits that help the species survive desert stream variability are not sufficient to persist in human-altered streams. Diversion and water withdrawal cause fragmentation and riffle loss.
- Canyon reaches may act as refugia from which the species recolonizes degraded areas.
- The current distribution provides strong evidence that human alterations of lotic systems harm the species. Its remaining strongholds are [[Aravaipa Creek]], the Blue River, and portions of the Gila–San Francisco drainage in New Mexico.
