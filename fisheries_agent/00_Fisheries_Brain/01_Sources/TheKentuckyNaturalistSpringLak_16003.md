# The Kentucky Naturalist, Spring 1952: Lake Productivity, Copepods, and Captive Bats

#ichthyology #marine-biology #copepoda #chiroptera

## Executive Summary

This issue of *The Kentucky Naturalist* (Vol. VI, No. 2, April 1952), published by the [[Kentucky Society of Natural History]], is a mixed-content journal with four main research or feature articles and several society news items. The principal scientific paper, by Donald Distler, examines why [[Tom Wallace Lake]] produces few "keeper" [[Largemouth Bass]]. The author attributes this to overstocking, a eutrophic lake with summer oxygen depletion, low total alkalinity, and limited shallow-water habitat. Other articles cover the copepod nauplius larva, notes from a captive-bat observer, and profiles of two Kentucky ornithologists. The issue closes with chapter and program news.

## Background & Description

**Tom Wallace Lake** is a small impoundment in Jefferson County, Kentucky. Its dam was completed in fall 1946, and the lake was stocked with 1500 three-week-old Kentucky bass fry on May 4, 1947. Reported complaints of "no keeper bass" prompted University of Louisville ecology and limnology students to investigate. Basic data come from the Department of Biology's records of work on the lake.

**Morphometry** (as reported):
- Surface area: 5.81 acres
- Maximum length 1080 ft; maximum width 540 ft
- Shoreline: 2,900 ft; [[Shoreline Development Index]] 1.6
- Mean depth about 15 ft; western end about 3 ft deep
- Deepest point "approximately 294 feet" as printed. This is implausible against the stated mean depth and is likely a transcription error.
- Volume Development above 1, indicating a U-shaped rather than V-shaped basin

**Geology:** The lake lies in a basin of Rosewood Shale, with knobs of Kenwood and Holsclaw sandstone around it.

**Captive bat notes:** Roberta Burckhardt kept a female [[Big Brown Bat]] (*Eptesicus fuscus*) from October 20, 1949, to January 13, 1950, when it escaped. Later she kept a probable [[Myotis]] (*M. keenii*) and a [[Georgian Bat]] (*Pipistrellus subflavus*).

**Copepod article:** Gerald Cole uses a photomicrograph of the nauplius larva of *[[Cyclops]]* (a freshwater copepod) as a starting point for a discussion of Crustacea and Copepoda.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nutrients and alkalinity.** The lake sits on sandstone and shale and contains little calcium. Total alkalinity was measured at 30 to 44 parts per million (ppm) during spring and summer. Moyle (1946), cited in the paper, reported that surface waters below 40 ppm total alkalinity are poor fish producers. The author states that this may apply in Kentucky but cannot yet be confirmed. Alkalinity rises in fall and early winter as bicarbonates from the deep water mix through the lake, then falls again as outflow over the dam resumes.

**Fertilization.** Two tons per year of commercial 6-8-6 fertilizer are added. Phosphorus and nitrogen were not analyzed because of lack of equipment, so their levels are assumed to be adequate. The lake loses most of its fertile water during fall and winter outflow, so it must be fertilized every year.

**Light and plankton.** Secchi disk readings ranged from 3 to 4 ft, and rarely up to 6 ft. The author notes that low transparency limits the rooted-plant (littoral) zone to roughly the 4 ft contour. Filamentous [[Spirogyra]] mats occur in shallows to about 4 ft.

**Zonation.** Thermal and chemical stratification places the upper hypolimnion about 12 ft below the surface in summer. The sublittoral zone runs from about 4 to 12 ft. The profundal zone below 12 ft is blackened organic ooze, and it is anaerobic in summer.

**Trophic state.** No dissolved oxygen was found below 13 ft in summer, and the only animals present were small species tolerant of anoxia, typical of eutrophic sediments. The author concludes that [[Tom Wallace Lake]] is [[Eutrophic]] and not [[Oligotrophic]]. Eutrophic lakes deplete deep oxygen through decomposition of surface production, while oligotrophic lakes usually keep oxygen-rich cold bottom water.

**Fish sampling.** Seine hauls yielded bass, bluegills, and catfish in an approximate ratio of 6:3:2, and all fish were small. Minnows were seen but not captured by seine. The author calls sampling non-optimal and conclusions tentative.

**Age determination.** Ages were estimated from scale circuli. Annuli form during slow growth, mainly in winter. The author cautions that injury, disease, spawning, and starvation also affect growth marks.

## Key Findings

**Bass growth and condition (Distler):**
- **August 18, 1951:** 22 bass taken on worms, 4⅝ to 5⅝ in. total length. Four more were taken the next day, similar in size. Scales indicated third-year fish. Of five stomachs examined, only three contained food: midge larvae and pupae, insect chitin, ostracods, a centipede, a fish scale, and Spirogyra.
- **October 16, 1951:** Five bass, 4⅝ to 6 in. Four had two annuli (assumed third year). The 6 in. fish had four annuli (assumed fifth year) and was "better formed." Carlander (1950) gives 11 to 17 in. as the expected length range for that age. No food was found in these fish.
- **Bluegills (October 20, 1951):** One fisherman's eight bluegills ranged from 5 to 6½ in. (scan ambiguous). Scales indicated fourth-year fish, and Carlander (1950) gives 4 to 8 in. for that age. The author takes this as evidence that bluegills have reached a balance and grow at a normal rate despite overstocking.

**Conclusion on bass:** The bass are stunted, which the author takes to mean there are too many bass for the available food. The total poundage of bass is unknown.

**Stocking error:** The lake was first assumed to cover 12.5 acres, and it was already known to hold about 500 too many fingerling bass. Kentucky Division of Game and Fish ratios (80 bass fry plus 40 adult bluegills per surface acre) for the actual 5.8 acres would call for about 460 bass fry and 230 bluegills. The lake received 1500 bass fry and 300 bluegills, an excess of more than 1000 bass and about 70 bluegills.

**Habitat restriction:** Below 13 ft the bottom is anaerobic in summer, leaving foraging bass only the narrow band of sparse vegetation at the margin. Bottom samples show more animals in deep sediments than in the shallows, which the author links to heavy predation by fish in the shallows.

**Management conclusion:** Stocking by surface area alone, without regard to physical characteristics, chemical stratification, and food availability, is unlikely to produce "keeper bass" in this lake.

**Copepods (Cole):** The nauplius of *Cyclops* has three pairs of appendages, and the lake's copepods are described as major links in food chains supporting fish, including [[Northern Pike]]. Copepods serve as intermediate hosts for parasites such as the broad fish tapeworm and the Guinea worm. Most freshwater crustaceans have lost the free-swimming larval stage that remains in marine relatives, which the author suggests reflects the recent colonization of inland waters. The 12 moult stages of *Cyclops* lead to the adult, and the paper notes an origin in trilobite-era biramous appendages, with fossils around 500 million years old.

**Captive bats (Burckhardt):**
- The female Big Brown Bat roosted in consistent places and was most active at 65°F and above.
- Liver was the staple diet. The maximum intake recorded was twenty-one pieces. She refused liquid squirted into her mouth.
- The Big Brown Bat was poor at detecting thin wires but avoided them after the first night.
- The Myotis was a better flyer but could not be fed.
- The Georgian Bat escaped within twenty minutes through a three-quarter-inch gap under the door, a route the Big Brown Bat had not found in three months.

**Society news:**
- The [[Berea Knobs Chapter]] was formally accepted on February 21, 1952, with headquarters at Berea College. Its 1951-52 program included geology, forestry, and nature photography talks, and a joint meeting with the Ohio Falls Chapter on April 25-27, 1952.
- The summer issue will be dedicated to [[Bernheim Forest]], with a paper by Frank H. Bunce.
- Profiles of [[Burt L. Monroe]] (State Ornithologist of Kentucky) and his son Burt L. Monroe, Jr. (author of a key to Kentucky fishes) highlight the family's natural-history work. The museum described holds about 700 bird skins.
