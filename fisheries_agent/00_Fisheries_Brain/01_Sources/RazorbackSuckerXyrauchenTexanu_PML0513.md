# Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish Monitoring, Verde and Salt Rivers, Arizona, 1991–1993

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This special report by the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] documents the monitoring of reintroduced [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the [[Verde River]] and [[Salt River]], Arizona, from 1991 to 1993. A total of 3,552 razorback suckers and 15 Colorado squawfish were stocked into the Verde River, mostly as adult-sized fish intended to reduce predation by introduced species. No fish were stocked into the Salt River. Monitoring relied on electroshocking and seining, with recaptured fish providing growth, condition and survival information. Introduced fishes continued to dominate the Verde River, while native fish dominance in the Salt River in 1993 followed a major flood. Reintroduced fish generally lost weight after release, and the parasitic copepod [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] was observed on several recaptured individuals.

## Background & Description

Native fish populations in Arizona have declined, been extirpated, or gone extinct, largely due to habitat alteration and the introduction of exotic species (Minckley and Deacon 1968, 1991). Species extirpated from the Gila River basin include the federally listed Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker, along with bonytail, desert pupfish and other taxa. Federally listed species in the region include the Gila topminnow, loach minnow and spikedace, and the Arizona endemic Apache trout is also listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

Key program history:

- **1981:** A razorback sucker reintroduction program for the Gila River basin began under a 10-year memorandum of understanding between the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]].
- **1985:** Colorado squawfish reintroduction began under the 1984 amendment to the Endangered Species Act. Squawfish stocked into the Salt and Verde rivers were designated "experimental, non-essential."
- **By 1990:** Over 600,000 squawfish and 11,000 razorbacks had been stocked into the Verde, Salt and Gila rivers, most as juveniles.
- **Predation:** High predation on small fish by introduced species led to stocking primarily adult-sized fish from May 1991 onward.

Funding came from the Arizona Heritage Fund and Section 6 of the Endangered Species Act.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Verde River:** The Verde retains one of the most diverse remnant native fish communities in Arizona, with seven native species. Native fishes include longfin dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker, Gila chub, roundtail chub, speckled dace and spikedace. Native fishes are most common in upper reaches, while non-native fishes are more common downstream.

**Salt River:** Only five native fishes remain in the mainstem: longfin dace, Sonora sucker, speckled dace, roundtail chub and desert sucker. Eighteen or more non-native species occur in the river. The same pattern of greater native diversity in upper reaches holds for the Salt River.

**Study reaches:**
- Salt River: Highway 60 bridge (river kilometer [RK] 90) to Highway 288.
- Verde River: Morgan Ranch (RK 225) to Sheep Bridge, sampled in late winter to early summer 1991 and autumn 1991, and in 1992 and 1993 in the same periods as noted below.

**Flood disturbance:** A flood of approximately 47,000 cubic feet per second passed through the Salt River canyon in January 1993. Introduced fishes are poorly adapted to high-velocity currents and scouring in canyon-bound reaches, and the flood appears to explain the shift from introduced to native dominance in the Salt River between 1992 and 1993.

## Methods

- **Collection:** Primary methods were [[Electroshocking]] (from a canoe or raft, using a pulsed DC shocker at 35 m, with output currents from 4 to 8 amps) and [[Seining]] of shorelines and backwaters. Trammel, hoop and fyke nets were used on a more limited basis.
- **Marking:** Fish were checked for external tags and [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging]] (PIT tags). Fish were weighed to the nearest gram and measured to the nearest millimeter.
- **Health:** Parasites, diseases and injuries were recorded.
- **Analysis:** Weight changes in recaptured fish were compared using a [[Wilcoxon Matched-Pairs Signed-Ranks Test]].
- **Effort:** Verde River longitudinal monitoring ran February 23 to May 21, 1991, and September 21 to November 9, 1992. Salt River monitoring occurred June 15–18, 1992, and June 2–6, 1993. Sporadic sampling near Perkinsville, the primary stocking site, took place from July 1991 through July 1992.

## Key Findings

### Stocking
- **Verde River:** 3,552 razorback suckers and 15 Colorado squawfish were released at five locations, mostly at RK 187–188 and Perkinsville. The largest single release was 1,920 razorbacks on January 14, 1991, at RK 220. Most razorbacks exceeded 300 mm total length, and squawfish ranged from 562 to 669 mm.
- **Salt River:** No fish were stocked, due to limited availability of hatchery fish and because the Verde offered better sampling access and electroshocking conditions.

### Fish Community Monitoring
- **Verde River, 1991:** Relative abundance of native fishes declined from 44% in the uppermost reach to less than 1% in the lowermost reach. Mosquitofish and red shiner numerically dominated the introduced catch. Sonora sucker and desert sucker were the most abundant natives. Spikedace were absent from 1991 catches.
- **Verde River, 1992:** Native relative abundance declined from 50% to less than 1% downstream, similar to 1991.
- **Salt River, 1992:** Introduced species dominated. Electroshocking was relatively unsuccessful because of rapidly decreasing flows and high water conductivity.
- **Salt River, 1993:** Native species dominated. Red shiners were rare, and young-of-year native suckers made up most of the catch.
- **Spikedace:** Five were captured above the Sycamore Creek confluence in April 1992. No squawfish were captured in 1992.

### Recaptures and Movement
- Three razorback suckers were recaptured in 1991. They probably originated from the January 14, 1991, stocking. No Colorado squawfish were captured in 1991.
- One stocked razorback was taken at the Sycamore Creek confluence in 1992.
- Most razorback recaptures occurred within one kilometer of their release site, and most were at large for about 22 days.
- One razorback was recaptured 20 km downriver, six months after stocking.
- All three squawfish recaptures occurred within one kilometer of their release site.
- Radio-tagged squawfish that were never recaptured moved up to 30 km downstream (Clarkson et al. 1993).

### Condition and Growth
- Recaptured razorbacks and squawfish generally lost weight after release, a pattern also reported by Hendrickson (1993). Weight loss is likely linked to transferring hatchery pond-reared fish into wild river environments.
- Razorbacks that gained weight were recaptured 145 and 168 days after release.
- Weight changes in recaptured razorbacks were significant (p < 0.001).
- Several recaptured razorbacks appeared to have been PIT-tagged or injected with radio transmitters before release.

### Parasitism
- [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]], a copepod parasite, was found on the body and gills of recaptured fish.
- Two razorbacks and two squawfish were heavily infested.
- At least five to seven other razorbacks and one squawfish showed varying levels of infestation. The source gives both counts.
- Parasite levels were recorded only as present or absent.
- Lernaea was rare in Arizona before the 1930s and increased significantly from the 1930s through the 1960s. Its increase may have contributed to reduced condition in razorbacks, and it was documented as common on razorbacks in Lake Mohave.

### Conclusions and Management Implications
- Introduced fishes continue to dominate the Verde River fish community. Native fishes made up about half of the community in the upper third of the river in 1991 and 1992.
- Native fish dominance in the Salt River in 1993 likely reflects the January 1993 flood. Red shiner numbers dropped after the flood, although a similar 1984 event was followed by recovery within a year.
- Recapture success differed between rivers. Factors likely include more stocking and monitoring effort in the Verde, and lower sampling efficiency in the Salt.
- Reintroduced fish are largely sedentary near release sites, but some move long distances.
- Stocking adult-sized fish aims to reduce predation losses, but post-stocking weight loss and parasitism may limit reintroduction success.
