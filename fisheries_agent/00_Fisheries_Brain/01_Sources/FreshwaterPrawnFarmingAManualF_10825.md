# Freshwater Prawn Farming: A Manual for the Culture of Macrobrachium rosenbergii

#ichthyology #marine-biology #palaemonidae

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Technical Paper (No. 225, Rome, 1982), written by M.B. New and S. Singholka, is a practical extension manual for culturing the giant freshwater prawn [[Macrobrachium Rosenbergii]]. It is based mainly on Thai practice, where the species was the world leader in production at the time. The manual covers general biology, [[Hatchery Operation]] (larval rearing, feeding, post-larval harvest and transport), [[Rearing Site Requirements]], and [[Pond Culture]] to market size, with annexes on water filtration, brine shrimp nauplii, prepared feeds, larval stage identification, stock estimation, pond feeds, seine design and a glossary. The authors state that the manual is narrational rather than a "cookbook", because successful farming involves as much art as science.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and identity.** The document treats "shrimp" and "prawn" as synonyms. Only *M. rosenbergii* is covered in detail; other members of the genus are mentioned only in passing. The genus includes more than 100 species, over a quarter of them in the Americas. *M. rosenbergii*, together with *M. americanum* and *M. carcinus*, is among the largest species known. It is indigenous to South and Southeast Asia, northern Oceania and western Pacific islands.

**Introduction and spread.** The species was imported into Hawaii from Malaysia in 1965-66. Work by [[Ling 1969]] and later mass-production methods by Fujimura and Okamoto (1972) led to its spread. It is now farmed in Hawaii, Honduras, Mauritius, Taiwan and Thailand, and is being established in Costa Rica, Indonesia, Israel, Malaysia, Mexico, the Philippines and Zimbabwe.

**Life history.**
- Four phases: egg, larva, post-larva and adult. Growth occurs through moulting.
- Females carry eggs in a brood chamber under the abdomen, for normally no more than three weeks.
- Fecundity: 80,000 to 100,000 eggs per spawning in fully mature females; first broods of 5,000 to 20,000.
- Egg incubation averaged 20 days at 28°C (range 18-23 days).
- Larvae are planktonic and require brackish water. Larval life lasts several weeks; the fastest recorded was 16 days.
- Larvae feed on zooplankton and other small invertebrates. Post-larvae become crawling and omnivorous, and can be cannibalistic.

**Morphology.**
- Eggs are slightly elliptical (long axis given as 0.6-0.7 m in the source, most likely mm, since the OCR is ambiguous), bright orange, turning grey-black 2-3 days before hatching.
- Larvae pass through 8 to 11 stages before metamorphosis. The first stage is just under 2 mm, and the eleventh over 7 mm.
- Adults are blue, becoming red only when cooked. The second walking leg is enlarged in males.
- Males are larger, with the genital pores between the bases of the fifth walking legs. Females have a broader abdomen, and their genital pores lie at the base of the third walking legs.
- Macrobrachium can be separated from penaeid prawns by the smooth dorsal abdomen (penaeids have a ridge) and by pleuron overlap.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hatchery water.**
- A coastal site with access to well water is preferred. Surface supplies are discouraged because of pollution and salinity variation.
- Target pH is 7.0-8.5 and temperature 28-31°C. Hydrogen sulphide must be absent.
- Suggested maximum hatchery intake levels: nitrite 0.1 ppm and nitrate 20 ppm (NO3-N). Sublethal nitrite effects on larvae were reported at 1.8 ppm.
- Total hardness below 100 ppm CaCO3 is associated with better results.
- Brackish water for larvae is 12 ppt. A 10 m³ larval tank uses 4-6 m³ per day. Peak pumping of about 170 L/min per tank is needed for emergency water changes.

**Rearing water.**
- Provisional criteria: pH 7.0-8.5; total hardness below 150 ppm (preferably below 100); temperature 18-34°C (optimum 29-31°C); dissolved oxygen above 75% saturation.
- Temperatures below 14°C or above 35°C are usually lethal.
- Growth is much slower in hard water.
- Acid sulphate soils (pH 4.5 or less) should be avoided for ponds.

**Pond design.**
- Average depth about 0.9 m (range 0.75-1.2 m). Pond sizes are most manageable at 0.2-1.6 ha, with most around 0.2-0.6 ha.
- Clay content should not exceed 60%. Sandy soils and peat are unsuitable.
- Water requirements: about 1,800 m³ to fill a 0.2 ha pond. Maintenance flow of 140-560 L/ha/min.

**Predators and pests.** Main predators are snakeheads (*Ophiocephalus* spp.), catfish (*Clarias batrachus*), *Mystus planiceps*, crabs, birds and humans. Poaching is described as the greatest source of loss.

**Water quality hazards.** Pesticides (MIREX above 100 µg/L lethal) and phytoplankton blooms that cause high pH or night-time low dissolved oxygen are the main concerns.

## Key Findings

- **Site selection** is the most critical factor. Groundwater is preferred, and sites should be screened for pesticides and watershed pollution. Market access and road access are also key.
- **Larval rearing** uses a "clearwater" technique with rectangular tanks (1 m deep, 2 m wide, 7 m long, about 10 m³). Tanks are stocked with berried females at about 3 per m³ (30-50 larvae/L). Expected post-larval output is 100,000-200,000 per 10 m³ tank, or 10-20 per litre.
- **Feeding** uses brine shrimp nauplii (BSN) and a prepared egg/mussel feed (PF). Density of 1-5 BSN/mL is maintained. Overfeeding is a principal cause of water pollution.
- **Water exchange** of 50% per day is the core management practice. Tanks are siphoned daily, and the aeration must stay on.
- **Metamorphosis** typically starts on days 16-18, with most larvae transformed by days 25-28.
- **Post-larvae transport** is by aerated trash cans for short journeys, or sealed plastic bags with 1/3 water and 2/3 oxygen for long trips (up to 16 hours by road).
- **Pond stocking** in Thailand is typically 5 post-larvae/m² (50,000/ha), with higher densities of up to 20/m² used on some farms. Continuous culture uses 16-22/m² per year.
- **Harvest methods:** batch culture (drain and total harvest after about 6-8 months) and continuous culture (cull harvest by seine after 5-7 months). Thai farms commonly combine both. Output reported: 2,500-4,000 kg/ha/year for continuous culture; 3,000 kg/ha/year in Honduras.
- **Feeding in ponds:** initial rate about 6.25 kg/ha/day, rising to about 37.5 kg/ha/day before batch harvest of 1,250 kg/ha. Food conversion ratio for compounded diets is 2:1 to 3:1; for wet trash fish, 7:1 to 9:1. Feed is given at dusk.
- **Stock assessment** is inexact. Multiple seine samples are recommended, with the same method each time. Harvest records (weight and average size) are the most reliable productivity measure.
- **Alternative techniques** discussed include high-density larval culture, green water culture, conical tanks (over 50 post-larvae/L, up to 110/L reported in Indonesia), recirculation systems (25-35 post-larvae/L in Honduras), nursery ponds, cage and pen culture, polyculture with carps and mullet, tank culture, and open-water restocking.
- **Site-specific recommendations:** the authors stress that farm design and management must be adapted to local conditions, and that farmers should keep detailed written records of each pond.
