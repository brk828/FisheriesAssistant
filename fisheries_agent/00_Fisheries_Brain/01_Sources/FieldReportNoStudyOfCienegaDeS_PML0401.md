# Field Report No. 1: Study of Cienega de Santa Clara and Environs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

Field Report No. 1 documents preliminary field studies of the Cienega de Santa Clara, a brackish-to-saline wetland in the Colorado River Delta of Sonora, Mexico, conducted October 13–18, 1992, with supplementary flora surveys on November 22–23, 1992. The work was undertaken to document hydrology, vegetation, fauna and the status of endangered species, and to build a base for future monitoring. The cienega is fed by the [[Main Outlet Drain Extension]] (M.O.D.E., also known as the [[Wellton-Mohawk Canal]]) and the [[Riito Drain]], and receives tidal seawater from the [[Colorado River Estuary]] (Estero Colorado). Surveys confirmed significant populations of [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] and [[Desert Pupfish]], and the report sets out recommendations for equipment, survey protocols and long-term monitoring.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** The cienega lies in the delta of the Colorado River in northwestern Mexico. Its wetland was enlarged by the construction of the M.O.D.E. (Wellton-Mohawk Canal) in 1961, and the Riito Drain was built in 1950s-era agricultural drainage works (as stated in the report).
- **Sponsorship and partners:** The study was coordinated among the Centro Ecologico de Sonora (CES), the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), the Environmental Research Laboratory at the University of Arizona, the Advanced Resource Technology Program, the Drylands Institute, the Comision Internacional de Limites y Aguas, and the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, with U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Conservation International support.
- **Size:** A July 1992 overflight estimated an upper vegetated zone of about 3,000 ha and a lower unvegetated water surface of about 6,000 ha. In September 1992 a visible tidal connection to the Gulf of California was present, and the water surface was larger than in July.
- **Team:** Thirteen named participants, including [[James J. Riley]] (compiler), Guadalupe Morales Abril, Francisco J. Abarca, Dave Weedman, Mike Ingraldi, Robert Henry, Richard S. Felger and Alberto Burquez.
- **Methods:** Aerial and oblique/vertical video and photography with [[Global Positioning System]] landmark referencing (ten GPS points, Table 2); 1:24,000 aerial photo mosaic for a base map; seine, dip net and minnow trap collections; refractometer salinity readings; surveying-staff depth readings; and estimated flow rates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and water regimes.** The report identifies two primary regimes: a lower tidal regime dominated by seawater exchange with the Colorado Estuary, and an upper regime fed by fresher discharges from the M.O.D.E. and Riito Drain. Estimated inflow is 115,600 acre-ft/yr from the M.O.D.E. (1.43 x 10⁸ m³/yr), within the Bureau of Reclamation range of 106,000–140,000 acre-ft/yr, plus 6,300 acre-ft/yr from the Riito Drain (7.8 x 10⁶ m³/yr), a combined total of 121,900 acre-ft/yr (1.5 x 10⁸ m³/yr). The authors hypothesise a mixing zone of brackish water between the two regimes defines the southern vegetation boundary. The Colorado Estuary is hypersaline (44,000 ppm) compared with the open Gulf (37,000 ppm), and tidal heights of 14 ft occur on 6 to 16 days per month.

**Salinity gradient.** Measured salinities ranged widely: the M.O.D.E. was about 2,000 ppm (refractometer; the Bureau reports an average of 3,200 ppm); the upper vegetated area near El Campo was 2,000–2,500 ppm, with about 5,000 ppm at the shore; water in the lower cienega and near the Gulf was higher, and one puddle on the Colorado Estuary reached 90,000 ppm.

**Vegetation.** The northern zone is dominated by [[Cattail Marsh]] (*Typha* spp.) with some [[Common Reed]] (*Phragmites communis*), in water about 65 cm deep. Cattails reached about 4 m near the Hunter's Camp and were shorter near the shore at higher salinity. The transition (ecotone) between salt flats and marsh is dominated by [[Salt Grass]] (*Distichlis* spp.), saltbush and possibly glasswort (*Salicornia* spp.). Algal mats covered much of the bottom. The report estimates turnover times of 41 to 124 days (Table 7), and notes that evapotranspiration could concentrate salts by roughly a factor of three if the vegetated area is around 4,000 ha.

**Birds.** The [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] and other marsh birds were recorded, with bird lists summarised by section (M.O.D.E., cattail marsh, strip, open cienega). Abundant species included [[American Coot]], [[Great Egret]], [[Snowy Egret]], [[Yellow-Headed Blackbird]], [[American Avocet]], [[Killdeer]], [[California Gull]] and [[American White Pelican]]. The open cienega could not be surveyed.

**Fish.** Collections used seines, dip nets and minnow traps. Species recorded include [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), tilapia (*Tilapia* sp.), carp, and largemouth bass (*Micropterus*, identification unconfirmed). Pupfish occurred in shallow, vegetation-free areas, and were relatively abundant only in an isolated pool about 200 m north of the M.O.D.E. end bridge that appeared disconnected from the main cienega. Predators and competitors (mosquitofish, tilapia, largemouth bass) likely exclude pupfish from the main water body.

**Invertebrates and other fauna.** Bottom samples were dominated by fly larvae (Diptera, possibly Simuliidae), with dragonfly and damselfly nymphs, adult aquatic beetles and an unidentified shrimp in seine hauls. Mammal tracks, coyote and lizards were observed.

**Human influence.** Raw sewage discharge near the end of the Flor del Desierto road, burned cattail areas, shrimp farm debris and waste from the Hunter's Camp were noted. Local ejido members reported that the upper cattail wetland did not exist before the canal's construction and that the water level tracks canal flow.

## Key Findings

- The cienega is a diverse aquatic mosaic from fresh to hypersaline, with an upper canal-fed cattail marsh and a lower tidally influenced zone.
- Water inflow of about 121,900 acre-ft/yr from the M.O.D.E. and Riito Drain drives the upper regime; turnover times are 41–124 days.
- Ground crews could not penetrate the cattail zone or reach the unvegetated lower cienega during this trip, so the open water zone remains largely unsurveyed.
- Significant populations of Yuma Clapper Rail and Desert Pupfish were confirmed; pupfish are restricted to isolated, shallow pools.
- Tidal exchange with the Colorado Estuary may transport salts in and out of the cienega; net salt balance is unknown and requires salinity measurements of incoming and outgoing water.
- Recommendations include a corrected base map from aerial photos, four-wheel-drive vehicles, a canoe with a 2–3 hp engine, minnow traps for small fish, and a Yuma Clapper Rail point-count protocol with repeated visits over several breeding seasons.
- Long-term monitoring should record weekly salinity, continuous flow and stage data for the M.O.D.E. and Riito Drain, daily tide and water level records, and salinity and flow during several high-tide periods.
- Water and sediment samples were to be analysed by the Bureau of Reclamation; results were pending at the time of the report.
