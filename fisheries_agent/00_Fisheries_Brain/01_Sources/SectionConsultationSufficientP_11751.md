# Recovery Implementation Program Recovery Action Plan (RIPRAP) for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) memorandum and attached document transmit the revised [[Recovery Action Plan]] (RIPRAP) for the four endangered fishes of the Upper [[Colorado River]] Basin, together with the [[Section 7 Agreement]] it supports. The Recovery Program aims to recover [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]] to naturally self-sustaining populations while allowing water development to proceed in compliance with the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The plan sets out recovery actions, schedules, and budgets across subbasins, and defines how the program serves as a reasonable and prudent alternative (RPA) for Section 7 consultations on water depletions and [[Critical Habitat]].

The memorandum states that the RIPRAP will be revised annually, with the next cycle beginning January 1998. Comments are collected in an open file at the Program Director's office.

## Background & Description

The four species are large-bodied native fishes of the Colorado River system. The recovery plans cited in the document give the following priorities:

- **Colorado Squawfish:** Goal is to establish and maintain natural self-sustaining populations and habitat.
- **Humpback Chub:** Goal is to establish and maintain natural self-sustaining populations and habitat.
- **Bonytail:** Because of its critical status in the upper basin, the immediate goal is preventing extinction.
- **Razorback Sucker:** The first priority is preventing extinction in the wild, since successful recruitment of young fish has been limited. The recovery plan for this species was still in development at the time.

The document structure is as follows:

- **Part One:** The Section 7 Agreement (originally finalized October 15, 1993) clarifies how consultations on depletion impacts of new projects and impacts of historic projects will be conducted.
- **Part Two:** The RIPRAP, which contains an introduction, subbasin priorities, Recovery Action Plan tables (Section 4.0), budget projections (Section 5.0), literature cited, and a [[Critical Habitat Analysis]] appendix.

The plan is organized around six recovery elements, plus program management:

1. [[Instream Flow Protection]]
2. [[Habitat Restoration]]
3. [[Nonnative Fish Management]] and sportfish management
4. [[Genetics Management]] and augmentation or restoration stocking
5. Monitoring and research
6. Public information and education
7. Program planning and support

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat loss and fragmentation.** Upper basin floodplains were once inundated by spring runoff. Today, levees, dikes, rip-rap, and tamarisk channelize much of the river, and reduced peak flows from upstream impoundments limit fish access to bottomlands. Passage barriers have confined the fishes to 20 percent of their former range. Flooded bottomlands contain large numbers of zooplankton and benthic organisms, and razorback suckers and squawfish use them for feeding.

**Flow and temperature.** Cold releases below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] are presumed to exclude endangered fishes from the Green River reach between the dam and the Yampa confluence. Flow regimes, sediment transport, and temperature are all treated as important habitat parameters.

**Contaminants.** The document identifies selenium, petroleum derivatives, heavy metals, and uranium as potentially harmful, with suspected contaminant hot spots. Contaminant remediation is conducted independently of the Recovery Program. Uranium processing on the Dolores River in the late 1940s through the 1960s is cited as a possible contributor to squawfish decline there.

**Nonnative fishes.** Of 52 fish species in the upper basin, only 13 are native. Competition and predation by nonnative species are widely assumed to have contributed to the decline of the endangered fishes, though direct evidence is difficult to obtain. [[Northern Pike]] are a particular concern in the Yampa and Green rivers.

## Key Findings

**Program structure and legal framework**
- The Section 7 Agreement applies only to the four Colorado River endangered fishes in the Upper Basin, excluding the San Juan River.
- Sufficient progress is judged by the FWS based on measurable population responses, habitat improvements, legal flow protection, or reduced immediate extinction risk.
- Depletion impacts of new projects and past impacts of historic projects are covered, except discharge of pollutants such as trace elements, heavy metals, and pesticides.
- If progress is insufficient, biological opinions will specify RIPRAP actions that must be completed to avoid jeopardy.
- Critical habitat was designated by a final rule published March 21, 1994, effective April 20, 1994. Subsequent RIPRAP modifications added instream flow adjudication, the Ruedi Reservoir interim agreement, floodplain restoration, entrainment screens, and nonnative fish removal.

**Budget**
- Estimated total budget for FY 1997 to FY 2003 is about $116 million.
- Annual operating budget is about $3 million, contributed by [[Bureau of Reclamation]], FWS, and the states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming, totaling about $24 million through FY 2003.
- Water development depletion fees could provide about $1 million through 2003. The rate was $13.41 per acre-foot as of October 1, 1996, adjusted annually for inflation.
- Operation and maintenance for refugia, hatcheries, and fish passage is about $9 million through FY 2003.
- Full recovery scope would require about $83 million: roughly $42 million for water acquisition and instream flows, and about $41 million for capital construction such as fishways, hatcheries, and bottomland restoration.

**Subbasin priorities**
- **Green River:** Supports the largest known riverine population of razorback sucker. It is the highest-priority area for squawfish recovery and is important for humpback chub and bonytail. Refinement of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations is a central action.
- **Yampa and Little Snake Rivers:** The Yampa has one of two confirmed squawfish spawning areas in the Upper Basin. The Little Snake supplies about 28 percent of the Yampa's flow and 60 percent of its sediment. A Yampa River Operations and Management Plan and Environmental Impact Statement (1996 to 1998) are planned.
- **Duchesne River:** Squawfish and razorback suckers use the lower 15 miles, especially during spring runoff. Initial flow recommendations were developed in 1995.
- **White River:** Adult squawfish occupy the river below [[Taylor Draw Dam]] in relatively high numbers. Taylor Draw Dam, built in 1984, blocked upstream migration. Interim flow recommendations are scheduled for March 1997.
- **Colorado River (Rifle to Lake Powell):** Holds several important populations. The upper reach between Palisade and Rifle is unoccupied, blocked by three diversion dams. In 1993, 67 adult razorback suckers were collected from ponds near Debeque. No evidence of successful razorback reproduction was found in the mainstem.
- **Fifteen-Mile Reach:** Squawfish catch rates are about double other mainstem areas. Instream flows here are heavily reduced by late-summer agricultural diversions. The Colorado Water Conservation Board filed for a 581 cfs instream flow right for July to September in 1992, and a junior right for the rest of the year in December 1995. Ninety-five statements of opposition were filed.
- **Gunnison River:** Occupied by squawfish, with historic razorback use. The 10-foot [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] blocks migration; passage there was restored, with a fish ladder completed in 1996 and designed to exclude nonnative fishes.
- **Dolores River:** Historic squawfish habitat with limited recent use, and recovery actions are restricted to preventing nonnative sportfish escapement from McPhee Reservoir.

**Water acquisition and flow protection**
- The Bureau of Reclamation has provided 20,000 acre-feet from [[Ruedi Reservoir]] since 1990. An additional one-year contract for up to 21,650 acre-feet covers 1996 and 1997, with a longer-term agreement targeted by December 1997.
- The Yampa filings drew 48 statements of opposition.
- Colorado uses two levels of state acceptance of flow recommendations, one unqualified and one expressly subject to modification.
- Utah relies mainly on conditioning new and pending water right approvals on predetermined streamflow levels, adopted as State Engineer policy.

**Habitat and passage**
- A 1994 inventory identified floodplain habitats on 870 miles of the Colorado, Green, Gunnison, Yampa, and White rivers. Site restoration began in 1994 and will continue to at least 1999.
- Fish passage has been determined necessary at Redlands, Hartland, Grand Valley Irrigation Company, Price-Stubb, and Government Highline (5 sites). Passage at Redlands is complete. Restoring passage at GVIC is under way, which would open about 50 miles of historic habitat.
- Old Charlie Wash on the Ouray National Wildlife Refuge has been restored, with 5 to 10 more Green River sites planned for 1996.

**Nonnative fish and stocking**
- Stocking procedures were finalized and a memorandum of understanding was signed by the states and FWS.
- A study found young squawfish made up 5 percent of the diet of northern pike, while squawfish were a much smaller share of available food. One pike could consume 100 or more squawfish per year. Removal of pike from certain Yampa reaches is expected to begin in 1998.
- Razorback sucker stocking began experimentally in 1994 on the Colorado River near Rifle and Grand Junction and on the Gunnison River near Delta.
- Colorado squawfish and humpback chub populations were judged stable, so initial stocking focuses on razorback suckers and bonytails.

**Monitoring and management**
- Four planning documents guide genetics and propagation: Genetics Management Guidelines, Genetics Management Plan, Annual Propagation Operations Plan, and Coordinated Hatchery Facility Plan.
- Interim Management Objectives are to be developed for each species and stock to gauge biological response.
- Recovery success will ultimately be measured by population response, which may take many years to become evident.
