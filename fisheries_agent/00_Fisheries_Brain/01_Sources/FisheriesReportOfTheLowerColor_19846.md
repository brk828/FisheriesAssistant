# Fisheries Report of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #fisheries-management #multi-species-assemblage #reservoir-ecology #river-ecology #Centrarchidae #Salmonidae #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 report by the Fisheries Task Group of the [[Colorado River Great Basin Field Committee]] presents a comprehensive survey of the Lower Colorado River fishery from [[Hoover Dam]] to the Mexican border. The study divides the river into seven sections, each assessed individually for present and potential fishery resources. The report documents a diversified fishery dominated by [[Rainbow Trout]] in cold-water reservoir sections and [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Bluegill]] in warm-water areas. Field surveys conducted over the past year by biologists from Nevada, Arizona, and California fish and game departments, plus the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, reveal that the fishery is generally underutilized despite increasing recreational pressure from Southwest anglers. The report emphasizes that [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]] represent critical recreational resources, particularly as drought conditions have eliminated many alternative fishing waters. Native species including [[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]] persist but are declining, while introduced game fish dominate the sport fishery.

## Background & Description

The Lower Colorado River system encompasses approximately 250 river miles and multiple large reservoirs created by dam construction. [[Lake Mohave]] (Section I), formed by [[Davis Dam]], extends 67.5 miles through Black, Eldorado, and Pyramid Canyons with maximum width of four miles and operating level at 643 feet elevation. The lake experiences four-foot maximum fluctuations and strong seasonal winds. [[Lake Havasu]] (Section III), created by [[Parker Dam]], covers 25,000 surface acres extending 46 miles with highly irregular shoreline and average depth of 22 feet. The river sections between dams vary from confined canyon reaches to wide alluvial valleys with extensive bottomlands.

Water temperatures define distinct thermal zones: cold-water areas below [[Hoover Dam]] maintain 53-56°F from hypolimnetic releases, supporting trout populations, while downstream sections reach 84-90°F in summer, favoring warm-water species. The report documents 18 fish species including seven game fish (rainbow trout, largemouth bass, smallmouth bass, black and white crappie, bluegill, green sunfish, redear sunfish, brown and yellow bullhead, channel catfish) and four native species (bonytail, razorback sucker, Colorado squawfish) plus introduced [[Carp]]. Aquatic vegetation includes [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), spiny naiad (*Najas marina*), widgeon grass (*Ruppia maritima*), and emergent species (cattail, bulrush, tule).

The fishery receives heavy use particularly in winter months (October-May) when air temperatures are moderate. In 1950, [[Lake Mohave]] recorded 68,235 fishermen, with 61% increase in first five months of 1951. Approximately 80% of anglers originate from California. A special use permit system requires anglers to hold licenses from both states when fishing boundary waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Dam construction has profoundly altered the Colorado River ecosystem, creating stratified reservoirs with distinct thermal regimes and eliminating natural flow patterns. [[Hoover Dam]] releases create a cold-water tailrace supporting trout for approximately 10-15 miles downstream, with thermal separation occurring between Cave Rapids and Emery's Landing where 53°F bottom water undercuts warmer surface layers reaching 76°F. This thermal stratification enables coexistence of trout and bass populations in [[Lake Mohave]], with trout occupying upper canyon sections and bass dominating lower rocky shoreline habitats.

Water level management critically affects fish reproduction and habitat quality. The report emphasizes that bass and bluegill spawning (March 15-August 30) requires water fluctuations limited to less than two feet vertical change. Greater fluctuations destroy redds and strand fry. [[Lake Havasu]] experienced detrimental 11-foot fluctuation in August 1950, though typical variations remain under six feet. The Metropolitan Water District contract limits fluctuations to less than ten feet.

Aquatic vegetation abundance directly correlates with fishery productivity. [[Lake Havasu]] supported extensive spiny naiad beds until approximately 1946-1947, after which submerged aquatics nearly disappeared from coves. This vegetation loss coincided with declining bass catch rates, suggesting reduced forage fish (bluegill, green sunfish) production and diminished cover for young fish. The report attributes reduced fertility to unknown factors but recommends vegetation restoration through planting programs.

Native fish populations face multiple stressors including habitat alteration, competition from introduced species, and barriers to migration. [[Colorado Squawfish]], historically reaching over six pounds, has become extremely rare with last capture in 1949. [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] remain common in upper sections but provide no sport fishery value. Dams create absolute barriers to fish movement, fragmenting populations.

Turbidity varies dramatically by section: water released through [[Parker Dam]] turbines is relatively clear (silt-free), while dredged channels and areas below irrigation returns carry heavy sand loads exceeding 150 ppm. High turbidity inhibits aquatic plant establishment and reduces invertebrate productivity. The sterile sand bottom characteristic of many river sections supports minimal food organisms compared to stable gravel substrates.

Water quality degradation occurs in lower sections, particularly below Yuma where raw sewage increases coliform bacteria over 1500-fold. [[Mittry Lake]] experiences extreme salinity fluctuations (600-10,000 ppm total dissolved salts) due to evaporation exceeding 10 feet annually, periodically exceeding fish tolerance limits.

## Key Findings

**Section I - Lake Mohave (Hoover Dam to Davis Dam):**
- Rainbow trout comprise 95% of catch; creel census of 625 anglers over six months yielded 3.25 fish per angler, 1.1 fish per hour, average weight 1 pound
- Record rainbow: 18 pounds in 1950, 15 pounds in 1951
- Natural trout spawning confirmed in area above Ringbolt Rapids; deeper water from reservoir filling should improve spawning success
- Largemouth bass had excellent 1951 spawn in lower lake; sizeable adult population present from pre-impoundment river
- Thermal stratification creates natural separation: trout waters above Emery's Landing (Cave Rapids), bass waters in lower lake
- Growth rate of aquatic insects, freshwater shrimp (*Gammarus fasciatus*), and snails expected to increase with establishment of aquatic vegetation
- Recommendations: manage upper section as trout fishery with continued fry/fingerling stocking; close spawning area below Hoover Dam December-February; restrict live bait to species already present (prohibit Utah Chub introduction); establish egg-taking station; improve parallel access roads

**Section II - Davis Dam to Needles:**
- Limited utilization by local Bullhead City population and dam personnel
- Upper portion may support trout fishery if tail race dredging removes sterile sand areas
- Cold water releases from Davis Dam lower outlet valves maintain suitable trout temperatures
- Recommendations: designate area between Davis Dam and Nevada tip as trout water; maintain trout fry plants; retain public land strips for river access; improve Arizona-side access between Bullhead City and Fort Mohave

**Section III - Needles to Parker Dam (Lake Havasu and Topock Swamp):**
- Lake Havasu: 24,851 man-days angling effort in 1950; one angler-day per acre per year (light use)
- Creel census (1,035 anglers, 9 months): 0.37 largemouth bass, 0.23 channel catfish, 2.67 bluegill, 0.08 black crappie per angler-day
- Total harvest: 0.91 pounds fish per acre per year (low productivity)
- Bass fishing declined from 1938-1947 peak; attributed to reduced fertility and near-complete failure of aquatic weeds since 1947
- 91% of anglers targeted bass; bluegill, catfish, crappie capable of greater exploitation
- Topock Swamp: estimated 6,000 angler-days per year; better quality fishing for bass, catfish, crappie than main lake
- Needles-Topock channel dredging (completion July 1951) will drain marginal waters but create 6,000 acres flooded swamp at Topock opening
- Recommendations: limit water drawdown to one foot March 15-August 30 for bass/bluegill spawning; introduce golden shiner or other forage fish as bass/crappie food and buffer for young game fish; improve Arizona-side access to Site 6 and Bill Williams Arm; continue study of vegetation restoration and brush shelter construction

**Section IV - Parker Dam to Headgate Rock Dam:**
- 15-mile section with abundant sago pondweed; much greater fish productivity than other river sections due to less turbid water and stable substrate
- Estimated 15,000-20,000 angler-days in 1950; heavy use by trailer residents (150 trailers, 120 boats counted November 18, 1950)
- Creel census (259 anglers): 1.3 channel catfish, 4.3 bluegill per angler-day; bass and crappie fishing poor
- Bluegill and catfish are fishery mainstay; both species could sustain greater harvest
- Parker Dam releases vary 500-3,500 c.f.s. daily; wide seasonal fluctuation (11,000-20,000 c.f.s.)
- Water temperatures: 49°F January, 84°F August; reach 65°F in April, 70°F in May, 80°F in June
- Recommendations: minimize water fluctuations to less than two feet during spawning seasons; construct anchored brush shelters; retain public lands between road and river for access/camping; complete Parker-to-Parker Dam road on Arizona side; study channel catfish and bass populations; evaluate new forage/game fish introductions

**Section V - Headgate Rock Dam to Palo Verde Drain:**
- 81-mile section; estimated 11,890 man-days river use (146 angler-days per river mile), plus 4,200 man-days at Lost Lake/Alligator Slough, 7,300 man-days at Palo Verde Slough
- Channel catfish is fishery mainstay; catch records show 2.7-4.9 catfish per angler-day at Palo Verde Slough, 1.4 at Palo Verde Weir
- Largemouth bass and bluegill most successful in lentic waters (Lost Lake, Bass Lake, Miller's Pond); limited in river except where submerged cover present
- River characterized by nearly total absence of rooted aquatics and invertebrate bottom fauna; sterile shifting sand bottom
- Lost Lake (58 acres): horseshoe-shaped oxbow formed 1942; moderate sago pondweed stands; heavily used, producing maximum fish consistent with size/fertility
- Palo Verde Slough: 12 miles navigable; 77 permanent homes, 89 boats, five concessions with 45 rental boats at town of Palo Verde; high turbidity (150 ppm) precludes bass/bluegill success; catfish-dependent fishery receiving maximum use
- Cibola dredging project will drain seven miles of river, drain Miller's Pond, lower Palo Verde Slough 2-3 feet, increase current velocity—total fishery loss in drained areas
- Emory's soft-shelled turtle abundant (specimens over 20 pounds common) but little utilized; bullfrogs numerous in irrigation districts
- Recommendations: minimize water fluctuations to less than two feet during spawning; construct brush shelters; assure free public access via retained public lands and key river bank sections; obtain Colorado River Indian Reservation access agreement; study river catfish/bass populations; evaluate forage/game fish introductions and brush shelter program expansion

**Section VI - Palo Verde Drain to Imperial Dam:**
- 49 miles river plus 4,045 surface acres backwater lakes (largest: Cibola 1,500 acres, Adobe 600 acres, Martinez 785 acres, Fergeson 570 acres)
- Total use approximately 35,540 angler-days; seven man-days per surface acre per year (light use); 90% of use concentrated between Imperial Dam and Castle Dome Landing
- Castle Dome Landing: estimated 30,000 people used area in 1950; 72 private boats counted March 31, 1951; Calizona Sportsclub operates 20 rental boats, 10 cabins, restaurant
- Creel census (Castle Dome Landing, February-June 1951, 1,035 anglers): 1.2 largemouth bass, 0.23 channel catfish, 2.67 bluegill, 0.08 black crappie per angler-day; 2.63 hours per fish; 1.52 total fish per angler-day
- 90% of anglers targeted bass; fair fishing success considering water nature/use
- Bluegill small (5-6 inches); channel catfish 0.75-1.5 pounds average, 10-15 pounds common, up to 22 pounds reported
- Black crappie present in significant numbers for first time spring 1951; may develop into highly desirable crappie fishery
- Bass adequately harvested; bluegill, catfish, crappie capable of greater exploitation
- Marginal lakes relatively clear; main channel turbid; most lakes silting in, rate dependent on inlet size
- Cibola dredging will drain Davis Lake, Three-Finger Lake, most of Cibola Lake
- Recommendations: retain public access; construct Ehrenberg-to-Cibola access route with camping ground; improve Martinez Lake north access; develop California-side Fergeson Lake access; continue general fishery study; analyze new forage/game fish introductions

**Section VII - Imperial Dam to International Border:**
- 79 miles divided into Zone 1 (Imperial Dam to Laguna Dam, 44 miles) and Zone 2 (Laguna Dam to border, 35 miles)
- Zone 1: shifting sand bars, few dead-end sloughs; Zone 2: wide shallow meandering areas, sometimes too shallow for boat navigation in summer
- Turbid waters carry tremendous sand but little dissolved organics; adverse physical conditions inhibit aquatic life and fishery
- Air temperatures: 32-128°F, average 72°F; water temperatures: 45-90°F
- Channel catfish support sport fishery; some bass, bluegill, crappie, yellow bullhead fishing
- Striped mullet and ten pounder present (unique to this section)
- Laguna Lake and marsh: over 1,500 acres; Yuma Boat Club facilities; popular for bass, bluegill, catfish, bullfrogs
- Mittry Lake: 2,000 surface acres, average depth 8 feet; highly turbid, no direct river connection; seepage-fed with small Gila Canal flushing
- Mittry Lake salinity: 10,000 ppm maximum to 600 ppm minimum (spring); evaporation exceeds 10 feet/year, concentrating salts beyond probable fish tolerance at times
- Laguna Dam flushing (Thursdays 8 AM): four-foot water drop strands fish including large spawning channel catfish (some over 25 pounds); over 3,000 catfish 6-8 inches observed returned to water on different occasions; over 50% stranded fish by weight are rough fish (carp, mullet)
- Yuma raw sewage discharge increases coliform bacteria over 1,500-fold; serious public health problem
- Recommendations: do not stock warm-water fish already present; study and modify Laguna Dam flushing to benefit fishery; develop Mittry Lake with proper water flow and aquatic plant establishment; study new forage fish introduction desirability; continue general fishery study; evaluate submerged aquatic introduction and cost-effective management techniques

**Overall Conclusions:**
- Lower Colorado River fishery represents valuable recreational resource for increasing Southwest angler population
- Fishery generally underutilized except at specific high-use locations
- Research program by three states plus federal agencies should continue for effective management
- Dam construction generally aided rather than hindered sport fisheries; channel dredging and extreme water fluctuations detrimental
- Stocking of warm-water fish already present serves no useful purpose
- Need uniform regulation and administration across three states; lack of Arizona-California reciprocal license arrangement problematic
- Recommend formation of permanent interstate management body similar to Columbia River or Pacific Coast marine fisheries models
- Control undesirable fish introductions primarily through live bait restrictions
- Retain public lands for access routes and river bank sections to assure future public access
- Work with other agencies to obtain water regulation beneficial to fish life consistent with other river uses
