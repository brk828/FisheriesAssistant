# Biological Opinion for Rerouting an Existing Powerline on the Upper Blue River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This is a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service) biological opinion, dated March 24, 1997, on a proposed rerouting of an existing [[Navopache Power]] powerline across and along the upper [[Blue River]] in Greenlee County, Arizona. The action agency is the [[U.S. Forest Service]] (Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests), and the powerline was being moved because a section was collapsing. The Service concluded the action is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of [[Loach Minnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], or [[Bald Eagle]], and not likely to destroy or adversely modify the presently enjoined critical habitat of loach minnow. An incidental take statement sets a trigger-based limit on take of loach minnow, and the document also includes a set of later section 7 concurrence letters for other Forest Service projects.

## Background & Description

**Proposed action.** The project would relocate a line that lies within the right-of-way of [[Forest Road 281]]. Key construction steps were:
- Crossing the river on foot (two to three people, at least three crossings, one work day) with no machinery entering the river or floodplain.
- Placing three to four new poles within the right-of-way.
- Trimming or, if necessary, felling a large cottonwood overhanging the river, with the trunk dropped into the floodplain.
- Removing old wire from the river and floodplain while leaving down poles in place; no roadbed or bank material to enter the river.

**Species accounts.**
- **Loach minnow** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): listed threatened in 1986. A small, slender, bottom-dwelling fish of shallow, swift water over gravel and cobble, which is rare where fine sediments fill interstitial spaces. Lifespan is about two years, and it feeds exclusively on aquatic insects. Its range has declined by about 85 percent, mainly from non-native fish competition and predation and habitat destruction. Critical habitat, including the Blue River from Campbell and Dry Blue Creeks to the San Francisco River confluence, was designated in 1994 but has been set aside and enjoined pending [[National Environmental Policy Act]] compliance after a 1996 Tenth Circuit decision.
- **Razorback sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): listed endangered in 1991, with critical habitat designated in 1994 in the Gila basin. It can exceed a metre in length and lives up to about 50 years. No historic records exist from the Blue River, but the species is presumed to have occurred there. Hatchery fish from Lake Mohave were stocked in 1986 and 1989. Only three recaptures have been recorded, and no razorback suckers were found in 1994–95 sampling.
- **Bald eagle** (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*): originally listed endangered in 1967 and reclassified threatened in 1995. Southwestern birds are treated as a distinct population, with about 38 occupied breeding areas recorded in recent years. No nests are known on the Blue River; the nearest is at [[Luna Lake]], about 20 air miles away. Wintering eagles use the area, and perching trees such as large cottonwoods are important.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Environmental baseline.** The Blue River is described as a seriously degraded ecosystem. [[Aldo Leopold]] (1922) called it "crufted" and an extreme example of erosion. Channel erosion was driven mainly by the 1900–1906 floods, which were enabled by overgrazing, timber and fuelwood harvest, field development, roads, and flood-control works. The river now has a wide, shallow profile, high sediment, braided channels, and depauperate riparian vegetation. Current pressures include timber harvest, roads, grazing, low-water ford crossings, a private fish hatchery diverting much of the base flow, and unauthorized off-road vehicle use.

**Fish community.** Non-native species include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Channel Catfish]]. Four other native fishes persist: speckled dace, longfin dace, desert sucker, and Sonora sucker. The Service estimated that up to nine native species may have been extirpated from the Blue River over the past century. Recent [[Arizona State University]] surveys (1995–1996) found loach minnow throughout suitable habitat.

**Cumulative effects.** Small, accumulating impacts were of serious concern. The Forest Service road along the upper river (about 25 miles, much of it on the floodplain) was judged a major adverse impact. The Service also reviewed three earlier formal consultations and two informal concurrences on Blue River actions.

## Key Findings

- **Direct effects.** The project is expected to have only small, localized, short-term adverse effects. Avoiding in-channel machinery and keeping roadbed material out of the river reduce mortality and sediment risk. Loach minnow and their eggs are particularly vulnerable to substrate sedimentation because of their benthic habit.
- **Cottonwood removal.** The Service supported the tiered approach (trim first, fell only if necessary) and dropping any felled tree into the floodplain, since the Blue River lacks large woody debris.
- **Bald eagle.** Removing the cottonwood is an adverse effect because it removes potential perching habitat, but the Service accepted the tiered approach.
- **Toxic spills.** Introduction of petroleum products during repair work was judged minimal.
- **Incidental take.** Take of loach minnow cannot be quantified directly, so it is indexed to the fish community and project parameters. Take is exceeded if more than **20 dead fish of any species** are found in or within **500 yards downstream** of the project area, or if any toxic spill occurs. Bald eagle and razorback sucker take was not anticipated.
- **Reasonable and prudent measures.** Four measures cover minimizing direct mortality of loach minnow, minimizing habitat loss, monitoring the fish community and habitat, and keeping records.
- **Terms and conditions.** Mandatory terms include minimizing activity in the wetted channel, preventing pollutants from entering surface water, minimizing riparian loss, monitoring for dead or dying fish (with telephone notification for more than 20), and submitting a written report within 60 days of completion.
- **Reinitiation.** Consultation must be reinitiated if take limits are exceeded, new information emerges, the action is modified, or a new species is listed.
- **Not a Migratory Bird Treaty Act exemption.** The incidental take statement does not exempt the project from the Migratory Bird Treaty Act.

**Later concurrence letters (1997).** The appended letters give Service concurrence with Forest Service "may affect, not likely to adversely affect" determinations for about 27 batch-consulted and individual projects. These cover prescribed burns (East Castle, East Eagle, McKibben, Badger Knoll), timber sales (Bear Canyon, Little, Sundown, Wiggins, Linle/Little Timber, Blue Ridge Morgan), trail and road projects (KP Trail, Tutt Creek, Three Oaks, Forest Road 504), and a Campbell Blue Creek erosion control project. Most concerned the [[Mexican Spotted Owl]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], bald eagle, loach minnow, or Apache trout. Several are conditioned on seasonal restrictions (for example, no work during the peregrine breeding season of March 1–July 31) and buffer or survey requirements. One Service letter (August 1996) found the Campbell Blue Creek erosion control project not likely to adversely affect loach minnow, because the stream was dry or near dry and sediment reaching critical habitat would be insignificant. Its conditions required fish relocation upstream and confirmation that spoil disposal sites were cleared.
