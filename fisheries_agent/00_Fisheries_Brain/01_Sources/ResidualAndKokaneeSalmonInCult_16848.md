# Residual and Kokanee Sockeye Salmon in Cultus Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1938 paper by W. E. Ricker of the [[Pacific Biological Station]] describes three forms of [[Oncorhynchus Nerka]] in [[Cultus Lake]], British Columbia: the normal [[Anadromous Sockeye]] stock, a non-migratory "residual" group that is largely or perhaps wholly the progeny of anadromous fish, and non-anadromous [[Kokanee]] with no known present connection to the other two. Residual sockeye are predominantly male, mature at younger ages and smaller sizes than anadromous fish, and have dull breeding colouration, heavy [[Salmincola Gibber]] infestations, and autumn spawning. Kokanee are scarce, mostly female-normal in sex ratio, brightly coloured, lightly parasitised, and spawn in late summer. The author proposes that growth rate and sex drive the split of each spawning into migrant and residual groups.

## Background & Description

**Study context.** The work is Paper No. 5 in a series on factors affecting sockeye survival during their lake life in Cultus Lake. Residual sockeye samples were mostly collected incidentally during other investigations. [[Gill Netting]] began in 1932, was limited until 1934, and was intensive from June 1935 to the end of 1937, with the goal of reducing certain fish populations.

**Defining the forms.**
- **Anadromous sockeye:** young migrate to sea after one (occasionally two) years in the lake and return in the second or third year after.
- **Residual sockeye (proposed name):** non-migratory, but progeny of anadromous parents. Breeding colour differs from kokanee, and spawning time is different.
- **Kokanee:** non-migratory, whole life in fresh water, with parents of several generations doing the same. Kokanee are known from many British Columbia lakes.

The author stresses the fundamental similarity of all three forms. Anadromous and residual fish are two life strategies within one population. Intergradation between kokanee and residual types may occur elsewhere, and some anadromy among young kokanee at Cultus Lake has not been ruled out.

**Sample scale.**
- 1935: 72 maturing residual specimens, including two females.
- 1936: 952 fish caught, including 63 females.
- 1937: 20 individuals, including one immature female.
- Kokanee: 13 specimens in 1935, two in 1936, none in 1937 and none in the 1932 or 1934 experimental netting. One male was angled on September 18, 1934.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sex segregation.** Residual sockeye were predominantly male. No females were found among maturing fish before 1935. In 1936 the female fraction was 0.066, or about one in fifteen. Mesh selectivity was judged to accentuate the imbalance only slightly. Sex ratio among maturing residuals (June to September) was 0.11, compared with 0.062 among mature spawners (October to December). Anadromous sockeye at Cultus Lake usually show a female surplus, with a sea-fish maximum observed at 0.75 female. The author proposes that sex is determined during the seaward migration, when the anadromous stock segregates by size and sex.

**Age at maturity.** Male residuals mature earlier than females. In the 1936 autumn sample, males were 14% second-year (II), 76% third-year (III) and 10% fourth-year (IV). Females were 43% III and 57% IV. Anadromous sockeye mature mainly as III, IV and V fish, so residuals mature younger.

**Feeding.** Residuals in their second and third years eat the same food as fingerling sockeye, mainly pelagic Crustacea such as [[Daphnia]], with some insects. Fourth-year residuals also eat small fish, including sockeye fry and *Cottus asper* fry. Obtaining Daphnia in summer requires feeding near the thermocline, at 9 to 15 m depth.

**Habitat and behaviour.** Residuals taken in spring and summer were usually found between 15 and 20 m down to about 40 m, with a preferred level just off the bottom. As maturity approached they moved to lower levels, then to shallower water and inshore around the whole lake margin. A few ran up tributary creeks, with about 150 taken in a hatchery tail-race in 1934. Ripe residuals were also taken from the same redds as large anadromous fish in 1932, when natural spawning was permitted. Whether they interbred with sea-run fish is unknown.

**Parasites.** Residuals almost always carried medium to heavy loads of the copepod *Salmincola gibber*. In 1936 very few residuals had fewer than 20 parasites, and 40 was not unusual. Gill filaments were shortened and fins frayed, yet pre-spawning residuals were fat and apparently in good condition. Kokanee carried light or no infestations.

## Key Findings

**1. Residuals and kokanee are distinct from each other and from anadromous fish.**
The summary table of characteristics (residual vs. kokanee) reports:
- Sex ratio: predominantly male vs. normal
- Male age at maturity: mostly II, III, occasionally IV vs. III and IV
- Size at maturity: 16 to 38 cm vs. 22 to 41 cm
- Male colour: dull olive-grey vs. green head, red body
- Scale margin absorption: little or none vs. always advanced
- Salmincola infestation: usually heavy vs. light or none
- Spawning time: October to December vs. August and September
- Abundance: usually common vs. scarce

**2. Growth rates separate residual classes from smolts.**
- Second-year residuals grew faster in their first year than most other residuals or smolts. In 1930 they averaged 13 mm longer than yearling smolts, and in 1934 they averaged 7 mm longer.
- Third- and fourth-year residuals grew more slowly in year one than yearling smolts. The deficits were 14 mm (1931 spawning) and 11 mm (1933 spawning).
- Fourth-year residuals grew more slowly in year two than third-year residuals.

**3. Size relationships.**
- The largest residual measured 382 mm; the smallest anadromous sockeye of 1936 measured 445 mm, though the residual was a year older.
- Residual weights ranged from 38 g (162 mm, 1932) to 636 g (382 mm, 1934).
- Kokanee in 1935 averaged 339 mm and about 500 g. A 1936 male weighed 737 g after preservation.

**4. The migratory impulse hypothesis.** The author proposes that migration pressure rises with size and acts more strongly on females at a given size. Maturation suppresses it more strongly in males, so large males stay as residuals. Supporting observations include:
- Larger fish of both sexes migrate earlier on average.
- In 1937 females were in excess early in the run and males late.
- In the 1934–35 pond-raised experiment, the largest released group produced only 60% of the previous group's smolts. Its smolts averaged 4 mm shorter than at release, and the difference between migrants and non-migrants was at least 18 mm.
- A marked migrant sample of 128 had a male fraction of 0.438. A sample of 140 from the earlier marking had 0.505.

**5. Spawning timing.** Residuals were first ripe around October 6, with nearly all ripe by October 21. Kokanee died about three weeks before the first residual became ripe, so interbreeding is considered negligible.

**6. Parentage.** Residuals are regarded as the non-migratory progeny of anadromous parents. Evidence includes a marked fish released in 1933 that matured as a III residual in 1934. Survival to smolt of artificially released fish was 63%, 66% and 33% in 1931, 1933 and 1935. Residual females produced eggs of normal appearance, but their fertility was not tested.

**7. Economic position.**
- As game fish, residuals take a spoon and are worth some regard, and their flavour is considered superior to that of other lake fish.
- For commercial sockeye fisheries, residuals are a loss, since each was a potential smolt. They also compete with fingerlings for food and sometimes prey on small fingerlings.
- Mature residual males might help attract returning adults, based on White (1935) work with Atlantic salmon. This has not been shown for Pacific salmon.

**8. Historical records.**
- Ward (1932) described landlocked-type sockeye in the Baker River system, Skagit River, Washington, after a dam was built. These were judged residuals, with a male excess and dull colour at maturity.
- Babcock (1903) described a small dark form in Seton and Anderson lakes on the upper Fraser River. These fish died at the surface in October and November and were gathered by the Indians. Babcock's description suggests residuals, in large numbers. The author suggests that excess residual production might have limited anadromous runs earlier in the century.

**9. Management recommendation.** Kokanee are scarce, so they currently have little effect on Cultus Lake. If kokanee increased, through deforestation, predator removal or new introductions, they would compete for the same niche as young anadromous sockeye. The author recommends that any increase be sockeye rather than kokanee, and that kokanee be controlled economically if they begin to increase.

**Summary conclusions (from the paper's own summary).**
- The progeny of a single anadromous spawning are split into residual and migrant groups by size and sex.
- Residuals and anadromous sockeye overlap in spawning time, and their smolts are often similarly infested with *Salmincola*. Kokanee resemble anadromous fish in breeding colour, but residuals do not.
- Kokanee may have originated from residuals, but they are now a population distinct from both residuals and anadromous fish.
