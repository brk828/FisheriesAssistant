# Fish Distribution and Abundance Correlated with Stream Gradients as a Consideration in Stocking Programs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Centrarchidae #StreamGradients #Petromyzontidae

## Executive Summary
This 1942 study by Milton B. Trautman investigates the correlation between stream gradient (elevation change per mile) and fish population size/distribution in Ohio streams, using 20 years of survey data. The paper's central case study is the [[Smallmouthed Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), Ohio's most prized game fish, whose "puzzling" patchy distribution within otherwise suitable streams is explained via gradient analysis using USGS topographic maps. The author establishes that stream gradients between approximately 4.0–19.9 ft/mile generally support moderate-to-high bass populations, while very low (<2.0 ft/mile) or very high (>25 ft/mile) gradients correlate with low or absent populations. Secondary case studies examine [[Spotted Bass]] habitat preference for low-gradient, silty waters, and the discovery of a relict population of the [[Big-headed Darter]] in an anomalous high-gradient stretch of the Walhonding River. The paper also documents how channel dredging can override gradient-based predictions, converting bass-associated fish communities to "creek chub and brook associate" communities. Findings on [[Nonparasitic Lampreys]] reveal that gradient must be considered alongside underlying geology (bedrock permeability) to explain presence/absence patterns. The work has direct management applications for fish stocking programs, stream improvement prioritization, and reservoir siting.

## Background & Description
This paper is primarily ecological/distributional rather than morphological, but it references several key taxa by name and diagnostic habitat affinity:

- **[[Smallmouthed Bass]]** (*Micropterus dolomieu*): Ohio's most prized game species; builds nests (observed up to 12 nests in a <100-yard pool); high fecundity documented (est. 30,000 fry/mile in optimal Beaver system habitat).
- **[[Spotted Bass]]** (*Micropterus punctulatus*): Congener of smallmouth bass; ecologically adapted to "sluggish and silty waters," contrasting with smallmouth's preference for moderate gradients.
- **[[Big-headed Darter]]** (*Hadropterus macrocephalus*): Normally an inhabitant of large, high-gradient northwestern Appalachian streams; found as a relict colony in an anomalous high-gradient stretch of the Walhonding River — the only Ohio record of this species at time of writing.
- **Brook/headwater associate species** displacing bass after dredging: [[Black-nosed Dace]] (*Rhinichthys atratulus meleagris*), [[Red-sided Dace]] (*Clinostomus elongatus*), [[Red-bellied Dace]] (*Chrosomus erythrogaster*), [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*), [[Silver-jawed Minnow]] (*Ericymba buccata*).
- **[[Nonparasitic Lampreys]]** (*Lampetra aepyptera* and others): Adults spawn in small, high-gradient headwater streams; ammocoetes (larvae) require flowing water in streams with permeable, sediment-conducive substrates — a life-history bottleneck independent of adult habitat suitability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Gradient-Population Relationship (Table 11 summary):**
  | Gradient (ft/mile) | Smallmouthed Bass Population |
  |---|---|
  | 0.0–1.9 | Absent or very low |
  | 2.0–3.9 | Low |
  | 4.0–4.9 | Moderate |
  | 5.0–6.9 | High |
  | 7.0–19.9 | Moderate to high |
  | 20.0–24.9 | Low |
  | 25.0+ | Low or absent |

- **Habitat Determinants Beyond Gradient:** Population outcomes are also strongly modified by (1) **dredging** (channelization removes pool-riffle heterogeneity, converting bass streams to dace/chub-dominated brook fauna — documented dramatically in the [[Mad River]]); (2) **siltation/pollution** (heavy cornfield silt load implicated in low bass numbers in the [[St. Marys River]] despite historically favorable gradient); (3) **stream size** (very small headwater streams cannot support large bass populations regardless of favorable gradient); and (4) **underlying geology/soil permeability** (governs run-off character and ammocoete habitat for lampreys).

- **Species Segregation by Gradient:** The [[Spotted Bass]] dominates the extremely low-gradient (1.1–1.7 ft/mile) lower [[Scioto River]] reaches (334 spotted bass vs. 8 smallmouthed bass trapped over one year at a station ~14 miles upstream), while [[Smallmouthed Bass]] becomes relatively more numerous as gradient rises modestly (2.8 ft/mile), though absolute numbers of both remain low — the sharp transition zone remains unexplained by the author.

- **Regional Geomorphic Control (Flushing Escarpment):** East of the Flushing escarpment in eastern Ohio, shale-dominated (~70%) surface/subsurface geology produces uniform, high "flash" runoff gradients and seasonally intermittent, pool-fragmented streams; nonparasitic lampreys are broadly absent here (e.g., [[Sunfish Creek]]) despite suitable adult spawning gradients, because ammocoetes cannot survive the lack of consistent flow. West of the escarpment, more impervious sandstone/limestone substrates yield lower-gradient, perennially flowing lower stream courses (e.g., [[Beaver Creek]], Pike County) that do support lamprey ammocoete habitat.

- **Relict Colonies:** Stream gradient mapping revealed previously unknown or unexpected relict populations, notably the [[Big-headed Darter]] colony in a short 8.3 ft/mile high-gradient stretch of the otherwise low-gradient [[Walhonding River]].

## Key Findings
1. **Gradient as a Predictive Tool:** Stream gradient (derived from USGS topographic contour data) is proposed as a powerful, broadly applicable variable for predicting fish species presence/absence and relative abundance across Ohio's 170 recorded fish species, and specifically validated in detail for smallmouthed bass across at least 10 major stream systems (Beaver/Little Beaver/West Fork, Blacklick Creek, Big Walnut Creek, Olentangy River, Upper and Lower Scioto River, Chagrin River and East Branch, Auglaize River, St. Marys River, Mad River, Walhonding River).

2. **Optimal Gradient Range:** Across multiple independent stream systems, the best smallmouthed bass populations consistently occur in reaches with average gradients roughly between 4.5 and 17.3 ft/mile; both very flat (<2 ft/mile, e.g., lower Scioto, St. Marys) and very steep (>20–25 ft/mile, e.g., East Branch Chagrin at 75.0 ft/mile) gradients correlate with sparse or absent bass.

3. **Historical Decline Case Studies:** Both Blacklick Creek and the Olentangy River were noted by fishermen as prime bass streams from 1890–1915, with subsequent declines attributed to reduced summer stream flow — an example of gradient-suitable habitat degraded by hydrological/anthropogenic change rather than gradient itself.

4. **Dredging Overrides Gradient Suitability:** In the Mad River, a reach with favorable gradient (8.0 ft/mile average) but dredged channel morphology exhibited extremely low bass abundance, with community composition shifting toward brook-associated cyprinids — demonstrating that channel modification can nullify otherwise favorable gradient conditions.

5. **Species-Specific Gradient Optima Differ:** Spotted bass reach peak Ohio abundance at gradients much lower than the optimum for smallmouthed bass, indicating gradient-driven niche partitioning between congeners.

6. **Lamprey Distribution Requires Dual-Stage Habitat Assessment:** Nonparasitic lamprey presence depends on suitable high-gradient spawning habitat for adults AND suitable low-gradient, perennial flowing habitat for ammocoetes — gradient alone is insufficient without considering underlying lithology and hydrologic permanence.

7. **Management Applications:** The authors argue gradient data should directly inform (a) site selection for bass stocking (avoiding unsuitable extremes as in St. Marys River or upper Chagrin system), (b) prioritization of stream improvement projects, and (c) siting of headwater reservoirs, particularly where maintaining constant summer flow is critical.

8. **Caveats:** The gradient-population classification (Table 11) is explicitly stated to be valid only for undredged, unpolluted Ohio streams with typical soil/substrate conditions, and is not necessarily generalizable outside Ohio or to modified streams.
