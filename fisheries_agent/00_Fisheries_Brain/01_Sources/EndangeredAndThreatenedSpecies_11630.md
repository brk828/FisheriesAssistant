# Endangered and Threatened Species of Arizona and New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-status

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) summary, compiled by the Albuquerque Office, lists the plants and animals federally designated as endangered or threatened in Arizona and New Mexico. It covers 56 listed taxa and 6 proposed species. Of the listed taxa, 18 are fishes, the largest group, followed by 23 plants, 9 birds, 4 mammals, 1 reptile and 1 invertebrate. Each account gives federal status, description, habitat, historic and present distribution, reasons for decline, recovery actions and references, with distribution maps. The document is a reference summary for general knowledge, not a research study.

## Background & Description

The document is framed by the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), which was passed to halt the decline of native wildlife and plants. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] identifies species facing extinction from habitat alteration and protects them. Federal agencies must ensure their actions do not jeopardize listed species or adversely modify [[Critical Habitat]].

Only federally listed species appear in the summary. Both states also protect additional rare species under state law, which the document does not list. Contact offices are given for the Albuquerque headquarters and the Albuquerque and Phoenix Ecological Services Field Offices, and for the state game and fish departments in Santa Fe and Phoenix.

Each species account follows a standard format:
- **Status:** the Federal Register citation for listing, and whether critical habitat is designated
- **Species description**
- **Habitat and distribution:** historic and present
- **Reasons for decline**
- **Other information:** recovery teams, plans, captive breeding and reintroduction
- **References**

The summary covers these taxa:
- **Mammals (4):** [[Black-footed Ferret]], [[Mexican Gray Wolf]], Mt. Graham red squirrel, and Sonoran pronghorn.
- **Birds (9):** American peregrine falcon, Arctic peregrine falcon, [[Aplomado Falcon]], [[Bald Eagle]], California brown pelican, [[Interior Least Tern]], [[Masked Bobwhite]], [[Whooping Crane]] and Yuma clapper rail.
- **Reptile (1):** New Mexican ridge-nosed rattlesnake.
- **Invertebrate (1):** [[Socorro Isopod]].
- **Fishes (18):** Apache trout, beautiful shiner, [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Chihuahua Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Gila Trout]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Loach Minnow]], Pecos bluntnose shiner, [[Pecos Gambusia]], [[Sonora Chub]], [[Spikedace]], [[Woundfin]], [[Yaqui Catfish]], [[Yaqui Chub]] and [[Yaqui Topminnow]].
- **Plants (23):** Mostly cacti (e.g., [[Knowlton Cactus]], [[Lee Pincushion Cactus]], [[Nichol's Turk's Head Cactus]]), along with [[Arizona Cliffrose]], [[Navajo Sedge]], [[Tumamoc Globeberry]] and others.

Six further species are proposed for listing in 1985–1987: Hualapai vole, Kearney's blue star, Little Colorado spinedace, Sacramento prickly poppy, Sanborn's long-nosed bat and Virgin River chub.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The listed taxa occupy a range of habitats:
- **Aquatic:** Desert springs and marshes (e.g., [[Desert Pupfish]], [[Pecos Gambusia]]), warm turbid mainstream rivers of the [[Colorado River]] basin (e.g., [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Woundfin]]), the [[Gila River]] system with its riffles and cobble bottoms (e.g., [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]]), and high-elevation trout streams (e.g., [[Apache Trout]], [[Gila Trout]]).
- **Terrestrial:** Desert grassland and scrub, including the [[Sonoran Desert]] and [[Chihuahuan Desert]], spruce-fir forest, and pinyon-juniper woodland.
- **Breeding habitat:** Sandbars and playa lakes for terns, cliffs for falcons and eagles, and marsh vegetation for the Yuma clapper rail.

Dams, impoundments, water diversion and groundwater pumping alter river flow and habitat. Several accounts note that recurrent flooding is necessary to maintain habitat quality, for example for loach minnow and spikedace. Introduced predators and competitors are another major threat. Exotic fishes, such as [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and nonnative trout, compete with or prey on native fishes and hybridize with native trout. Habitat destruction from overgrazing, mining, road construction, urbanization and off-road vehicle use affects many plants and desert species. Over-collection is a major cause of decline for many cacti. Persistent organochlorine pesticides caused reproductive failure in several raptors and pelicans.

## Key Findings

- **Scope:** 56 federally listed species (plus 6 proposed) across Arizona and New Mexico. The 18 listed fishes are the largest group.
- **Mexican gray wolf:** Extirpated from the U.S. The last known specimen was collected in 1970. About 50 wolves remained in Durango and Chihuahua, Mexico, in 1980. Wild populations probably no longer exist. A captive-breeding program had 30 animals in four U.S. facilities, and reestablishment in the wild is being investigated.
- **Black-footed ferret:** No verified post-1960 sightings in New Mexico. Loss of prairie dogs, the primary food source, and burrow habitat drove the decline. A captive-breeding program is underway in Wyoming.
- **Mt. Graham red squirrel:** Fewer than 300 estimated to exist, mostly above 9,400 feet in the Pinaleno Mountains. Logging and competition with the introduced tassel-eared squirrel are the main threats.
- **Whooping crane:** The experimental Rocky Mountain population numbered 25 birds in fall 1986 and winters in the middle Rio Grande Valley, New Mexico. The only self-sustaining wild population (Wood Buffalo/Aransas) had 110 birds in winter 1986–87. Whooping cranes were not sighted in New Mexico from 1938 until reintroduction began in 1975.
- **Bald eagle:** 20 known breeding territories in Arizona. An average of 16 young fledged per year over 1981–1986. No bald eagles nest in New Mexico. Arizona's population is expanding, with new nests on the Gila, Bill Williams and Agua Fria drainages.
- **Aplomado falcon:** No U.S. nests verified since 1952. Recovery efforts include obtaining nestlings from Mexico for captive breeding.
- **Interior least tern:** Terns persist only as small remnant colonies, with colonies of about 5 to 75 nests. In New Mexico, the species is found on playa lakes at Bitter Lake National Wildlife Refuge.
- **Masked bobwhite:** At most a few hundred birds remain, in one population in central Sonora, Mexico. Reintroduction at Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge is underway, with releases planned through 1988.
- **Sonoran pronghorn:** Found in southwest Arizona and northern Sonora. Threats include destruction of desert grassland and riparian areas and competition with livestock.
- **Socorro isopod:** Restricted to thermal springs in Socorro, New Mexico, with a population estimated at under 2,500. Capping and diverting spring water caused the loss of the historic springs. The species is now known only from less than 100 feet of drainpipe from Sedillo Spring, with a captive population at the University of New Mexico.
- **Gila topminnow:** Reintroduced at more than 100 sites on National Forest Service lands in Arizona, with a survival rate of about 50%.
- **Bonytail chub:** Rarest Colorado River fish. A few very old (40+ year) specimens in Lake Mohave, with no known wild reproduction. About 80,000 fingerlings from Dexter National Fish Hatchery were stocked in Lake Mohave in 1981, with restocking in 1985 and 1986.
- **Chihuahua chub:** Restricted to about 3 miles of the Mimbres River, New Mexico. Upstream channelization and irrigation dewatering threaten the habitat.
- **Gila trout:** Candidate for downlisting to threatened in 1987. Barriers were built at Iron Creek (1981) and Little Creek (1982), with stream renovations.
- **Pecos gambusia:** Survives at Bitter Lakes National Wildlife Refuge and Blue Spring, New Mexico, and in Texas springs. Competition with mosquitofish is a key threat.
- **Desert pupfish:** Two natural U.S. populations remain, one in California's Salton Sea tributaries and one at Quitobaquito Springs, Arizona.
- **Pecos bluntnose shiner:** Reduced in numbers within its present range on the Pecos River, New Mexico. Threats include reservoir flow restrictions, irrigation diversions and siltation.
- **Plants:** Most listed cacti are threatened mainly by over-collection and habitat loss. Knowlton cactus survives in a single natural population in San Juan County, New Mexico. Todsen's pennyroyal has fewer than 750 plants. Sacramento Mountains thistle has twenty known populations. Cochise pincushion cactus occupies about 100 acres at its only verified Arizona locality. Gypsum wild buckwheat's critical habitat covers nearly 1 square mile.
- **Recovery:** Most taxa have a recovery team and a recovery plan, with many approved in 1984–1986. Several plans remained in draft or were to be completed in 1987.
