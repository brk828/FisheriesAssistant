# Windbreaks May Increase Water Yield from the Grassland Islands in Arizona's Mixed Conifer Forests

#hydrology #plant-ecology #pinaceae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 paper, by J. R. Thompson, O. D. Knipe, and Phil M. Johnson of the [[USDA Forest Service]] Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, examines whether tree windbreaks can hold blowing snow on the high-elevation [[Grassland Islands]] of eastern Arizona and thereby raise [[Water Yield]]. The authors estimate that holding snow in place could add 1.5 to 2 inches of spring runoff, roughly 10,000 acre-feet per year across about 80,000 acres. A two-year planting trial of conifer seedlings showed adequate survival, supporting the idea that windbreaks could be established in the grassland.

## Background & Description

The high grasslands (9,000 feet and above) cover about 80,000 acres in large blocks, surrounded by or interspersed with [[Mixed Conifer Forest]]. The study focuses on two paired watersheds at [[Seven Springs]], East Fork (748 acres) and West Fork (482 acres), elevation about 9,200 to 9,765 feet. One watershed is to receive a vegetation windbreak treatment and the other serves as control. Data from the instrumented [[Burro Mountain]] watershed (730 acres, gaged since 1960) provide the forest comparison.

**Geology and soils:** The area sits on 4,000 to 8,000 feet of [[Tertiary Basalt]], with volcanic cinders and [[Cinder Cones]] prominent in the landscape. Grassland soils belong to the Bandera, Cinder, and Big Lake series, 12 to 32 inches deep. Adjacent forest [[Sponseller Soils]] range from 12 to 48 inches. Differences in chemistry between grassland and forest soils were statistically significant but probably too small to affect vegetation growth. Mycorrhizal fungi were absent from grassland soils, confirmed by soil cultures and visual examination.

**Climate:** Monthly mean temperatures are similar in grassland and forest. However, the 10-year mean winter wind speed at Seven Springs is 10 mph, with peak gusts of 50 to 65 mph. Average minimum temperatures from December to March are 11 to 15 °F, making the grassland harsher than the forest. Wind blows predominantly from the southwest quadrant, with the strongest winds from the south and southwest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Precipitation and gage bias:** Seven Springs receives 22.3 inches annually, compared with 28 inches at Thomas Creek (8,350 ft) and 31.7 inches at Burro Mountain (9,350 ft). Nearly half of the precipitation falls in June through October (56% at Seven Springs, 52% at Burro Mountain). The Seven Springs gage does not follow the elevation trend seen in the forest gages. The authors suspect it under-catches by about 30 percent because of constant exposure to wind.

**Runoff:** Burro Mountain yields 17% of recorded precipitation as streamflow and Seven Springs 14%. After correcting for likely gage undercatch, grassland yield efficiency is nearer 10%. Snowmelt produces 96% of streamflow at Seven Springs and 80% at Burro Mountain.

**Snow and frozen soil:** Grassland snowpack is only one-half to two-thirds of the forest pack at peak accumulation, yet it yields about three-fourths as much water. The authors attribute this to [[Frozen Soil]] ("concrete-type frost"), which limits infiltration so meltwater runs off as surface flow. Snow on windward slopes is swept into leeward forest and topographic depressions. Standard snow fences were judged visually unacceptable in this heavily used recreation zone.

**Tree encroachment:** Slow tree spread into grasslands appears driven by soil-moisture relations: summer evaporation, winter sublimation, and snow blown across open terrain. Three grassland-forest interface types were identified: a sharp boundary on the windward edge and on the lee side of steep hills, and diffuse continuum boundaries on the lee border and around low timbered hills. Trees and seedlings found beyond these stands suggest that wind-modified microclimates support self-perpetuating advance.

**Sublimation model:** Using [[Schmidt's Sublimation Model]] (1972), the authors estimate that snow particles fully sublimate after about 1,800 feet of air travel under average snow-season conditions at Seven Springs.

## Key Findings

- **Snow loss is substantial:** Precipitation on windswept grasslands is probably 6 to 8 inches higher than exposed gages show, much of it lost to sublimation of blowing snow.
- **Windbreak potential:** Tree windbreaks could trap enough blowing snow to increase spring runoff by 1.5 to 2 inches, roughly matching adjacent forest levels. Across 80,000 acres this could add about 10,000 acre-feet of streamflow annually.
- **Planting design:** A [[Randomized Complete Block Design]] with three replications was used. Each replicate had three rows of three transplants per species, with 6 ft spacing and rows 30 ft on the lee side of 4-ft snow fences.
- **Species planted (July 1974):** Engelmann spruce, blue spruce, Scotch pine, Austrian pine, and limber pine, chosen for windbreak growth traits and site suitability. Spruce and one limber pine variety are native to adjacent forests.
- **Survival:** Overall first-season survival was 76%, ranging from 48% (Engelmann spruce) to 93% (limber pine). Survival after two growing seasons was 60%, considered adequate.
- **Growth lag:** Seedlings had not grown in size by the end of the second season, due to many dead buds (cold damage or drought) and low vigor. The authors expect 2 to 3 years for root development and acclimatization before growth accelerates.
- **Mortality cause:** [[Pocket Gophers]] caused 19% of seedling mortality from planting through September 1975, a more manageable threat than climate.
- **Management implication:** Windbreaks planted along the upwind edge of natural depressions were judged the most acceptable way to hold snow, and the authors suggest introducing [[Mycorrhizal Fungi]] with seedlings and altering the microclimate during establishment.
