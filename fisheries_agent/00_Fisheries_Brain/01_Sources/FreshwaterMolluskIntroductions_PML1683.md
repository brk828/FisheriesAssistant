# Freshwater Mollusk Introductions and Type-Locality Records in the American Southwest

#malacology #freshwater-biology #unionidae

## Executive Summary

This short scientific note, published in *The Nautilus* (Vol. 85, No. 4, April 1972), reports several freshwater mollusk records from New Mexico, western Texas, Oklahoma, and Montana. The notes document likely human-mediated introductions of a freshwater mussel, a clam, and a snail, and they clarify the type locality of a North American snail. The authors reason through the origin of a mussel population and track the northward spread of an introduced clam. The note contains no fish-specific data; the fish role is limited to a probable vector for mussel larvae.

## Background & Description

The note is a collection of regional malacological notes, each contributed by a different author:

- **Anodonta stewartiana** (catalogued as *A. grandis* Say in the heading): a freshwater mussel found in a pond in El Paso, Texas. Specimens have low umbones and a slightly sinuous hinge line. These features differ from [[Anodonta Stewartiana]] from central Texas, which has an inflated umbonal area and a straighter hinge line.
- **Corbicula fluminea** (Miller): an Asiatic clam, identified by Dr. J. P. E. Morrison of the U.S. National Museum. Earlier records had called it *C. manilensis*. See [[Corbicula Fluminea]].
- **Radix auricularia** (L.) s.l.: a Palearctic lymnaeid snail, catalogued as *R. japonica* Say in the U.S. National Museum. See [[Radix Auricularia]].
- **Stagnicola montanensis** (Baker, 1913): a snail whose original type locality is clarified. See [[Stagnicola Montanensis]].

The notes are grounded in museum catalogue numbers (USNM) and field records from 1963 to 1971.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Anthropogenic introductions.** Three of the four taxa (*Anodonta*, *Corbicula*, *Radix*) are considered established through human activity. Introduction pathways include:

- Stocking of fish in a private fishing club pond (*Anodonta*).
- Irrigation drainage ditches, which served as the original *Corbicula* range in the Mesilla Valley ([[Mesilla Valley]]).
- Ranch ponds and reservoirs.

**Fish as vectors.** The authors reason that the El Paso mussel population most likely resulted from fish stocking. Freshwater mussels release larvae called [[Glochidia]], which must attach to a host fish. The note cites a 1966 report of an *Anodonta* in an Arizona lake, which the authors describe as the only known case in western states of a mussel introduced along with fishes. Stocking records state that the El Paso pond was stocked in the 1930s with catfish, bass, and perch.

**Hydrological barriers.** The [[Rio Grande]] was judged unlikely to have seeded the El Paso population for three reasons:

1. The nearest known Rio Grande drainage records lie about 700 miles southeast.
2. The river is often dry or only a trickle between El Paso and Presidio, Texas.
3. No unionid mussels, living or fossil, have been recorded in the drainage above Presidio.

**Reservoirs and range expansion.** Corbicula was not observed in surveys from 1963 to 1965 but was found in later years in several northern waters. This suggests dispersal between 1966 and 1970.

## Key Findings

**Freshwater mussel (*Anodonta*), El Paso, Texas**
- Collected in "Cement Lake," a pond built in 1910 and owned by the Southwestern Portland Cement Company, in 1968 and 1971 (USNM 681635).
- The author concludes the population most likely came from stocked fish carrying glochidia.
- The shell morphology separates it from the central Texas *A. stewartiana*.

**Asiatic clam (*Corbicula fluminea*), range expansion**
- Originally known from two irrigation drainage ditches in the Mesilla Valley (Doña Ana County, New Mexico) and El Paso County, Texas.
- Later found northward in Sierra County, New Mexico, in the Rio Grande and in [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]] and [[Caballo Reservoir]] (USNM 706608, 706607, 706606).
- Earlier surveys from 1963 to 1965 did not detect it in these waters, so dispersal likely occurred between 1966 and 1970.
- A separate record from [[Lake Overholser]] near Bethany, Oklahoma, was collected on August 25, 1969, by Louise Cassel. She described it as new to the lake.

**Palearctic lymnaeid snail (*Radix auricularia*)**
- Recorded at [[Lake Roberts]] on the [[Gila River]], Grant County, New Mexico, by Carl Olsen in 1966 (USNM 706609).
- Recorded in a ranch pond about eight miles southeast of Deming, Luna County, New Mexico, by Kay S. Samson and G. I. Wilson in 1970 (USNM 706610).

**Type locality of *Stagnicola montanensis***
- Baker (1913) described the species from "Hayes Creek, near Ward, Montana," at 3825 feet, collected by L. E. Daniels in April 1912.
- Local inquiry showed the Ward post office, which operated about 1910, was seven miles south of Hamilton, Ravalli County. The old brick building remains about half a mile north of the Jim Ward Ranch on Hayes Creek, at 3780 feet elevation.
- The site is in the south ½ of section 3, T.4N., R.21W. (USGS Hamilton South Quadrangle, 1:24,000, 1964).
- Taylor et al. (1962) were correct in identifying this area as the type locality.
- The species is abundant in boggy spring-seepage areas on both sides of Hayes Creek.
