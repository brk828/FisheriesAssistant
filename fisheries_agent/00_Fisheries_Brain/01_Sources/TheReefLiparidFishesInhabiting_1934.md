# The Reef Liparid Fishes Inhabiting the West Coast of the United States

## Executive Summary
This 1934 taxonomic and biogeographic study by Hubbs and Schultz examines four species of snailfish (family [[Liparidae]]) collected from rocky intertidal and shallow subtidal reefs along the coasts of Washington, Oregon, and California. Building on Victor Burke's 1930 revision of the Liparidae, the authors correct several taxonomic errors — most notably re-validating **[[Liparis florae]]** as distinct from **[[Liparis mucosus]]**, a synonymy erroneously proposed by earlier workers (Gilbert and Burke, 1912; Burke, 1930). The paper extends the known geographic ranges of three species substantially southward, documents detailed life-color patterns (including a striped color phase seen in three of the four species), and provides morphometric descriptions based on field-collected specimens from tide-pool poisoning surveys.

## Background & Description

### General Family Traits ([[Liparidae]] / Snailfishes)
- Soft, scaleless, often loose or "leathery" skin
- Reduced/fused pelvic fins forming an adhesive **pelvic disk** used for clinging to rock substrate
- Elongate dorsal and anal fins, often confluent with the caudal fin
- Presence/absence of a distinct **first dorsal fin lobe** is a key diagnostic character across species

### [[Liparis rutteri]] (Gilbert and Snyder)
- Very well-marked species; no separated/elevated first dorsal rays observed in West Coast specimens (contra original type description)
- Two chief color phases occur together in the same tide-pool:
  1. Uniformly dark/blackish with an abrupt light gray bar at the caudal base and posterior fin margins
  2. Bold, wavy-striped body pattern with a less abrupt pale caudal bar
- Distinctive pale bar at caudal base present in all specimens examined

### [[Liparis florae]] (Jordan and Starks)
- Distinguished from *L. callyodon* by:
  - Gill slit extending opposite 3–5 pectoral rays (vs. ending above first ray)
  - First dorsal fin indented behind (vs. rounded)
  - Anus slightly nearer anal fin than disk (vs. much nearer disk)
  - Peritoneum pale, unspotted (vs. spotted with black)
  - Pectoral rays 31–33 (vs. 28–31)
  - Dorsal rays 31–33 (vs. 33–35)
- Skin generally less lax, more leathery than congeners
- Never exhibits striped or blotched color phases (contra Burke 1930)

### [[Liparis mucosus]] Ayres
- Morphometrics (based on 3 specimens: 1 California, 2 Washington):
  - Dorsal rays: 30 (32)
  - Anal rays: 24 (25)
  - Pectoral rays: 30–31 (28, 32)
  - Caudal rays: 16 (16, 17); 10 principal rays
  - Disc length: 1.7–1.8 in head
  - Head: 3.5–3.8 in standard length
  - Eye: 5.5–6.0 in head
  - Snout: 2.8–2.9
  - Gill slit extends in front of 3–6 pectoral rays
  - First dorsal fin low, broadly rounded, no incised notch, ~4 rays, less than half as high as long
  - Body heavy forward; greatest depth below origin of first dorsal fin
  - Prickles scarcely developed
- Distinguished from *L. florae*: larger disk (>half head length vs. less), larger eye (5–7 vs. 8–9 in head), non-incised first dorsal, less leathery/more lax skin, presence of striped color phase, distinct blackish bar at caudal base
- Shares two features (body depth position, disk size) with *[[Liparis micraspidophorus]]* from the Aleutians

### [[Liparis fucensis]] Gilbert
- Nearly all specimens show a somewhat elevated first dorsal fin with partially free rays
- Synonymized taxonomically with *Neoliparis fissuratus* Starks (1896)
- All specimens examined were young to half-grown, collected from rock-pools; adults presumed to inhabit deeper water
- Exhibits striped color phase at times, like several congeners

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: All four species occupy rocky intertidal tide-pools and shallow subtidal reef zones along exposed, open-coast shorelines. Collection methods included tide-pool poisoning and hand-net scraping during extreme low tides (levels as low as −1.7 ft).
- **Substrate/microhabitat associations**: Pools with rock, sand, stone, and gravel bottoms; often associated with dense algal cover including eel-grass (*Phyllospadix*), kelp (*Laminaria*, *Macrocystis*), other brown algae, red algae, and encrusting sponges.
- **Depth stratification**: *[[Liparis florae]]* dominates shallow tide-pools and is often the only liparid present; *[[Liparis mucosus]]* appears restricted to the poorly-explored subtidal fringe below the lowest tide limits, explaining its rarity in tide-pool collections despite extensive poisoning surveys.
- **Color mimicry hypothesis**: The authors propose that color variation in these species (red, lavender, kelp-brown, olive) reflects mimicry of associated substrate biota — e.g., *L. mucosus* red morphs matching encrusting red sponge, lavender morphs matching coralline algae, and *L. florae*'s kelp-brown coloration matching large brown algae/kelp beds to which it is more ecologically restricted.
- **Co-occurrence**: Multiple species and color phases were frequently collected together within the same tide-pool, indicating fine-scale niche overlap despite morphological and color differentiation.

## Key Findings

1. **Taxonomic correction**: *[[Liparis florae]]* is re-validated as a distinct, valid species after having been erroneously synonymized with *[[Liparis mucosus]]* by Gilbert and Burke (1912) and Burke (1930); Burke's "mucosus" concept in his 1930 revision actually corresponds to *L. florae*, with only one true *mucosus* specimen apparently examined by Burke.
2. **Range extensions**:
   - *[[Liparis rutteri]]*: first recorded from Washington and Oregon (previously known only from SE Alaska, Aleutians, and Vancouver Island).
   - *[[Liparis fucensis]]*: first recorded from Oregon and California (previously known from Washington and SE Alaska).
   - *[[Liparis mucosus]]*: range extended south to Pacific Grove, California (Monterey Bay), with additional new records from northern Washington; previously known definitively only from San Francisco.
   - *[[Liparis florae]]*: confirmed as the dominant reef liparid across the entire outer coast of Washington, Oregon, and northern California (south to Point Lobos, Monterey County).
3. **Color phase distribution**: Striped color morphs are documented in *[[Liparis rutteri]]*, *[[Liparis mucosus]]*, and *[[Liparis fucensis]]*, while *[[Liparis florae]]* is shown to lack this phase entirely, contradicting Burke's (1930) claims.
4. **Ecological rarity of *L. mucosus***: Despite extensive tide-pool poisoning across the entire West Coast, only three specimens were recovered, suggesting the species primarily occupies the subtidal zone rather than the intertidal tide-pool habitat favored by congeners.
5. **Comparative morphology**: Detailed meristic and morphometric comparisons (dorsal/anal/pectoral ray counts, disk size, eye size, gill slit extent) are provided to differentiate *L. mucosus* from closely related North Pacific species including *[[Liparis micraspidophorus]]*, *[[Liparis curilensis]]*, *[[Liparis callyodon]]*, and *[[Liparis cyclopus]]*.
