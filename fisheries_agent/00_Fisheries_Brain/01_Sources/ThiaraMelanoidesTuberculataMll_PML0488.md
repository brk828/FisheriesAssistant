# Thiara (Melanoides) tuberculata and Its Probable Ecological Impact in Mexico

#malacology #gastropoda #thiaridae #freshwater-ecology #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This study assesses the probable ecological impact of the exotic, operculate freshwater snail [[Thiara Tuberculata]] (syn. [[Melanoides Tuberculata]]) in Mexico. The species was apparently introduced in the 1960s, most likely through the [[Aquarium Trade]], and has since spread to springs, streams, rivers, and reservoirs across at least 12 states. The authors combine historical malacofaunal lists with recent collections to document local declines in native snail and bivalve faunas at several sites, particularly in northeastern Mexico. Impact is inferred from temporal and spatial co-occurrence rather than experimental proof. Reported losses reach 100% at some localities, but many sites lack pre-invasion baselines, so the authors stress the need for further monitoring.

## Background & Description

**Species and introduction.** *T. tuberculata* is a prosobranch gastropod native to Asia and described as a "traveling snail" because of its ability to colonize new habitats. Its operculum allows it to survive desiccation during transport. The first Mexican record was made by Abbott (1973) near Veracruz. Subsequent reports followed from Parras de la Fuente, Coahuila (under the names *Oncomelania* sp. and *Tarebia* sp.), and from Pointier and McCullough (1989), based on material from Parras. The species was later recorded in a tributary of the Río La Silla, Guadalupe, Nuevo León, where it was judged to be a recently introduced but well-established species not coexisting with other subbasin species.

**Reproduction and dispersal.** The species has high reproductive capacity and reproduces parthenogenetically, which the authors identify as a barrier to eradication. Dispersal occurs through the [[Aquarium Trade]] and by adventitious transport.

**Study objective.** To evaluate the probable impact of *T. tuberculata* on native malacofauna in Mexico, including the relationship between snail density and co-occurring species.

**Methods.** Snails were collected using sieves, hand nets, and manual searches. Ecological parameters were recorded at sites in the Río Salado de los Nadadores (Sacramento and Santa Gertrudis, Coahuila), the Río La Silla and Presa Rodrigo Gómez "La Boca" (Nuevo León), and other states including Durango and Veracruz. Results were compiled in five tables, separating native from exotic species and recording the percentage of faunal loss.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Contreras-Arquieta et al. (in press, cited) documented *T. tuberculata* in 65 localities across 12 Mexican states. Of the sites considered, 23.43% had the snail alone, and 68.76% had it coexisting with other gastropod species. The authors note that the snail is generally found alone, and that when it co-occurs with native species, those species tend to be rare or scarce.

**Habitat relationships.** Pointier and McCullough (1989) listed environmental factors favorable or unfavorable to thiarid densities, including temperature, salinity, hardness, and dissolved oxygen. Observed Mexican populations mostly matched these patterns, with notable exceptions:
- In the [[Río Salado de los Nadadores]], snails were buried up to 1.0 cm in silt.
- At the thermal Ojo de Agua La Concha, Durango, which is low in oxygen, snails reached abundant densities at depths of up to 4 m.
- In Veracruz, snails occurred in streams rich in decomposing organic matter, which the authors say contradicts the favorable factors in Pointier and McCullough (1989) for some populations.

**Regional context.** The [[Cuatro Ciénegas Valley]] contains endemic gastropods (*Nymphophilus minckleyi*, *Durangonella coahuilae*, and *Mexipyrgus* sp.). The authors warn that the snail is advancing upstream from the Río Salado, and that the valley's endemic fauna could be threatened as valley water is diverted for agriculture and connected to the Río Salado.

**International comparison.** At [[Lake Yojoa]], Honduras, mollusc diversity was 20 species in 1980. By 1985, *T. tuberculata* had reached several hundred individuals per square metre, while [[Biomphalaria Havanensis]] and *Pachycheilus largillierti* had become very rare (Clark, 1987). The authors cite this as evidence of apparent competitive exclusion.

## Key Findings

**1. Río Salado de los Nadadores, Cañón de la Montaña (Sacramento, Coahuila).**
- Taylor (1966) recorded *Cochliopina riograndensis*, *Physa virgata*, *Heliosoma anceps*, *Gundlachia radiata*, and *Pisidium compressum* here.
- In 1987 only empty shells of these species were found, apart from one living *H. anceps*, giving an estimated 80% loss. The site was reported to have 100% loss of native fauna by 1996, when *T. tuberculata* was the only snail found 10 km downstream.
- Empty shells of endemic Cuatro Ciénegas species were also found, suggesting that the invasion may be moving upstream toward the valley.

**2. Parras de la Fuente, Coahuila.**
- Before 1974, the snail fauna included *Physa virgata*, *Fossaria* sp., and *Gundlachia radiata*.
- *T. tuberculata* is now the only species found, with 100% loss at the pond localities. The authors attribute this partly to falling spring and water-table levels.

**3. Río La Silla, Guadalupe, Nuevo León.**
- Four native species were recorded between 1936 and 1990. In 1990 only *T. tuberculata* was collected (100% loss). In 1991 three native species were found without the exotic, giving a loss of 25%, but these species occupied different microhabitats and were isolated in pools where *Thiara* was absent.
- The authors describe the native species as being displaced toward extinction at the site.

**4. Presa Rodrigo Gómez "La Boca" (Santiago, Nuevo León).**
- One juvenile *T. tuberculata* was collected in 1985 and not found in later surveys from 1987 to 1991.
- In September 1992, densities reached up to 300 individuals per m² at the mouths of the Arroyos Dolores and Cristalinas, where only *Anodonta imbecilis* and the exotic [[Corbicula Manilensis]] coexisted with it.
- Before the invasion, at least eight native gastropod species and *Sphaerium* sp. were present. The authors estimate a local loss of 75% in the southern part of the reservoir and about 36% overall, with native populations persisting mainly in the northern and northeastern areas.

**5. Laguna Escondida and Lago de Catemaco (Los Tuxtlas, Veracruz).**
- In the Laguna Escondida (28 March 1990), a sparse *T. tuberculata* population was found alongside other gastropods and bivalves.
- At Lago de Catemaco (30 March 1990), four gastropod and three bivalve species were recorded. In August 1992 *T. tuberculata* occurred at 30–50 individuals per m², but only at Puerto de los Pescadores, while other species remained abundant.

**6. Other states.** In Durango, densities reached up to 1500 individuals per m². For Quintana Roo, Tamaulipas, and other states, the authors had almost no baseline data, so losses could not be determined.

**7. Conclusions.**
- Apparent impact on native malacofauna has been recorded at several sites, with losses from 25% to 100%, but the evidence is largely circumstantial, based on co-occurrence and missing historical data.
- Control is difficult because of the species' high reproductive capacity and parthenogenesis; the authors recommend monitoring recently invaded sites such as Los Tuxtlas to evaluate impact.
- Environmental conditions in Mexico vary widely, and the species occupies habitats that do not fit the patterns described by Pointier and McCullough (1989).
