# Flora Representativa de Chipinque: Arboles y Arbustos

#botany #dendrology #flora-of-chipinque #plant-taxonomy

## Executive Summary

This 1995 field guide, published by the [[Consejo Consultivo Estatal]] (Subcomision de Flora) with the [[Universidad Autonoma de Nuevo Leon]] and the [[Patronato del Parque Ecologico Chipinque]], documents the representative trees and shrubs of [[Chipinque Ecological Park]] in the [[Sierra Madre Oriental]], near Monterrey, [[Nuevo Leon]]. It presents a checklist of 48 woody taxa, detailed botanical descriptions of each (habit, leaves, flowers, flowering period, fruit, distribution and uses), a glossary of botanical terms, and a bibliography. The guide is written for both technical users and the general public, and it frames habitat protection as the most effective route to conserving native flora.

## Background & Description

The guide is organized in sections: a prologue by the Governor of Nuevo Leon, an introduction, a physiography and vegetation overview, species descriptions, a floristic list, an illustrated botanical glossary and a bibliography. The authors are listed as Glafiro J. Alanis Flores, Marcela Gonzalez Alvarez, Marco Antonio Guzman Lucio and Geronimo Cano Cano, with photography and layout credited to members of the consultative council and students of the Faculty of Biological Sciences.

Each species account follows a standard format:

- **Aspecto y tamano:** height, trunk diameter and bark.
- **Hojas (leaves):** arrangement, shape, margin and texture.
- **Flores (flowers):** colour, inflorescence and size.
- **Epoca de floracion (flowering season)** and **fruto (fruit)**.
- **Area de distribucion (range)** and **observaciones** (uses, habitat and notes).

The species described include ornamentals, medicinal plants, timber and fodder species, and taxa of conservation concern. Examples include Anacahuita (*Cordia boissieri*), which is considered representative of Nuevo Leon, and Barreta (*Helietta parvifolia*), listed as endangered in Texas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography and climate.** The guide attributes the local distribution of temperate subhumid forest and submontane scrub to:

- The topographic relief and altitude of the Sierra Madre Oriental.
- Moist air penetrating from the Gulf, bringing rainfall.
- Soils with good water-retention capacity.

**Vegetation structure.** Three main controls on community distribution are identified: soil depth, aspect (exposure) and human disturbance.

- Sheltered sites with less insolation and high soil moisture carry tall, closed vegetation.
- Exposed or disturbed sites carry open, less vigorous and more spiny vegetation.
- On shallow, rocky, calcareous soils, spiny species such as [[Huizache]], Hierba del Potro (*Caesalpinia mexicana*) and [[Mezquite]] dominate. Where soils are deeper than 40 cm, in less exposed and moister sites, these species can exceed 6 m in height with very closed cover.

**Conspicuous species.** The guide highlights [[Barreta]], Anacahuita, [[Tenaza]] and [[Chaparro Prieto]] as the most conspicuous species of the more sheltered communities.

**Habitat associations.** The guide notes associations with pine-oak forest, [[Encino Roble]] woodland, riparian corridors ([[Alamo de Rio]], [[Palo Blanco]]), canyons and limestone slopes.

**Conservation framing.** The authors state that "lo que no se conoce no se puede conservar" (what is not known cannot be conserved). They argue that habitat protection, under a effective park administration, is among the most efficient strategies for conserving native flora. The text also notes that human activity has changed floristic diversity in some regions, contributing to species extinction.

## Key Findings

**Floristic inventory**
- The checklist ("Lista Floristica") records **48 woody species** from Chipinque, spread across a wide range of families. The most represented families include Leguminosae (acacias, mesquites and related legumes), Fagaceae (oaks) and Rutaceae.
- Among the oaks, the guide describes [[Encino Roble]] (*Quercus polymorpha*), [[Encino Blanco]] (*Quercus laeta*), [[Encino Molino]] (*Quercus virginiana* var. *fusiformis*) and several others, most of which are used in rural construction or as firewood and charcoal.

**Conservation status**
- [[Barreta]] (*Helietta parvifolia*) is listed as endangered in Texas.
- [[Manzanita]] (*Colubrina greggii*) is reported as endangered in Texas.
- [[Lantrisco]] (*Pistacia mexicana*) was rare, with only a few known populations, and was rescued from possible extinction by a nursery. It is now common in submontane scrub.
- The guide cites the work of Voevides (1988) on rare or endangered Mexican plants as a reference for this status.

**Uses and hazards**
- **Medicinal and food uses:** Aguacatillo, Laurel and Anacahuita are used in traditional medicine. Several species have edible fruits, including [[Granjeno]], Cerezo Negro and Anacua.
- **Toxicity:** Cerezo Negro (*Prunus serotina*) contains prussic acid in its dry twigs and leaves, toxic to humans and animals. Monilla (*Ungnadia speciosa*) has toxic seeds. Colorin (*Sophora secundiflora*) has toxic flowers and seeds. [[Madrono]] (*Arbutus xalapensis*) fruit can cause intoxication if eaten in large quantities. Guavillo (*Acacia berlandieri*) can cause poisoning in livestock if eaten excessively.
- **Timber and fuel:** Many species provide timber, fuel or charcoal, including [[Mezquite]], [[Encino Roble]], Encino de Asta and [[Pino Blanco]] (strong, durable timber used in construction, and turpentine production).

**Ornamental and landscape value**
- Several species are recommended for urban greening and gardens near the mountain, including [[Chaquira]], Corona de San Pedro (*Cornus florida*), Pata de Vaca (*Bauhinia macranthera*, drought-tolerant and suited to small gardens) and [[Palo Blanco]].
- [[Anacahuita]] is considered the representative flower of Nuevo Leon.

**Notable structural and taxonomic details**
- [[Pino Blanco]] (*Pinus pseudostrobus*) has five needles per fascicle. [[Pino Colorado]] (*Pinus teocote*) has three.
- Pata de Vaca is recorded at 750 to 1,300 m altitude.
- Tenaza is noted as a winter forage for goats and sheep.

**Glossary and illustrations**
- The guide includes a glossary with illustrated plates of plant structure, leaf types, leaf apices, bases and margins, flower diagrams, inflorescence types and fruit types, intended to help non-specialist visitors identify species.
