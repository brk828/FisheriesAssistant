# Spatial and Temporal Aspects of Bonytail Movement and Habitat Use, Cibola High Levee Pond, Lower Colorado River, Arizona and California, 2003–2004

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-elegans

## Executive Summary

This report presents part of an ongoing study of [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) at the [[Cibola High Levee Pond]] (HLP), a small backwater on the [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]]. Using [[Sonic Telemetry]], the study tracked 10 large adult bonytail in 2003 and 9 smaller sub-adult bonytail in 2004 to examine their movement and habitat use. Adult bonytail sheltered in interstices of large [[Riprap]] during daylight and moved into open water at dusk, presumably to feed. Sub-adults stayed in open water and did not seek cover, but nearly all of them appear to have died or lost their tags early in the study, so their data are of limited reliability. The authors conclude that cover availability may limit bonytail habitat carrying capacity and recommend that cover be considered when designing [[Bonytail Management]] areas.

## Background & Description

The Cibola HLP is a roughly 5-acre remnant of the lower [[Colorado River]] channel, lying between the river (low) levee and the inland (high) levee. The pond was reclaimed to remove non-native fishes and first stocked with native species in 1993. Since then it has served both management and research roles (LaBarbara and Minckley 1999; Marsh 2000; Mueller et al. 2003).

The study's purpose was to describe temporal and spatial patterns of movement and habitat use by bonytail. Relatively larger fish were studied in 2003 and relatively smaller fish in 2004.

**Fish capture and tagging**
- **2003:** 10 relatively large adult bonytail (mean TL 45.3 cm), collected by trammel net on 17 March 2003.
- **2004:** 9 smaller sub-adult bonytail (mean TL 28.4 cm), collected in April 2004 and weighed (nearest 2 g).
- Fish were measured, sexed, checked for health, scanned for a [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag]] (PIT tag), tagged with a PIT tag if none was present, and fitted with a sonic tag.
- **2003 tags:** Model 18T:96-2 with a nominal 60-day life expectancy, attached to the caudal peduncle with cable ties. Some ties included a fusible metal link designed to corrode and release the tag.
- **2004 tags:** Model SMT-01-376 with a nominal 4-day life expectancy, attached directly to the caudal peduncle with vinyl tape. One of the 10 tags malfunctioned when first activated, leaving 9 usable fish.

**Tracking equipment and protocols**
- Equipment included DH-2 directional and omnidirectional hydrophones, a USR-5W ultrasonic receiver, and headphones or external speakers. Neoprene shielding was added to directional hydrophones to reduce interference, and a compass was used to estimate bearings to within about 30 degrees.
- Three protocols were used: (1) simultaneous directional bearings from two fixed listening stations, one at the north end of the high levee and one on a spit at the north end of the river levee, nominally every 15 minutes from sunset to dawn; (2) point data from tracking individual fish; and (3) general directional data outside the synchronized schedule.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Cover and daytime behavior.** Adult bonytail spent daylight hours under cover in the high levee's interstices, mostly among large riprap boulders. They emerged into open water after dark. The authors note that daytime cover use is consistent with stomach-content evidence that feeding peaks at dusk and night.

**Pond habitat.** The high levee appeared relatively uniform among zones, so the authors assumed bonytail occupied interstices within the riprap. Two distinct areas were noted:
- **Southeastern pond:** submerged aquatic vegetation in moderately deep water (1–2 m), a relatively open shoreline, and woody riparian vegetation. Several fish were located here.
- **Northwestern pond:** little submerged vegetation, shallower water (<1 m), a gently sloping shoreline with dense cattail, and woody riparian vegetation. Other fish were located here.

**Comparison with other sites.** Adult bonytail in [[Lake Mohave]] showed a similar pattern, remaining in deep water during the day and moving into shallow near-shore habitat at night (Marsh and Mueller 2000). Related species of *Gila* in Arizona streams also use deep pools, undercut banks, and shadows during the day. The authors suggest this reflects protection from sight-feeding predators, while also supporting other life functions such as feeding and reproduction.

**Equipment limitations.** Aquatic vegetation dampened acoustic signals, and reflections off large riprap sometimes produced false bearings. [[Lotek Radio Tags]] with nano-technology were unsuitable here: detection radius was under 10 m for a tag suspended 1 m below the surface (Marsh and Mueller, unpublished data).

## Key Findings

**1. Adult bonytail were nocturnal and occupied cover by day.**
- Of 2,947 separate recorded bearings, 1,649 and 1,299 came from the high levee and river levee stations, respectively.
- Simultaneous contacts (≈945) clustered after sunset. Most fish appeared shortly after sunset, and contacts declined through the mid-hours of the night. Signals from fish returning to the high levee were recorded near dawn.
- Directional data were used almost exclusively for nighttime activity, because marked fish spent daylight hours under cover.

**2. Zone use was non-random.**
- The high levee was divided into 26 five-metre zones (A–Z). A chi-square test of contacts per zone gave χ² = 697.9 (df = 26, p < 0.001).
- No signals were recorded in 7 zones, 1–2 contacts were recorded in 6 zones, 5 zones had moderate use (5–15 contacts each), and 6 zones had high use. The six highest-use zones occurred in three nearest-neighbour pairs separated by low-use zones.

**3. Individual fish showed strong site fidelity.**
- Most fish were found frequently in only one or two zones, with occasional contacts elsewhere.
- Fish usually returned each morning to the same zone, often to the same location. A few fish shifted from one area to another and then stayed there consistently.
- Two or more fish rarely occupied the same zone at the same time, though different fish used the same zones on different occasions.
- Only the 10 marked fish were monitored, so unmarked fish may have occupied "empty" zones.

**4. Sub-adult bonytail appeared to avoid cover and may have died early.**
- Sub-adults were detected in open water throughout day and night, with no apparent use of riprap cover.
- Within a few days of release, most fish became stationary, which the authors interpreted as either tag loss or mortality.
- Two carcasses, parts of a third, and two recovered tags were found. Tags were assumed to have expired at the end of fieldwork, and most or all fish were likely dead at least a week before then.
- Hatchery experiments (Mueller et al. 2004) found mortality within 14 days across all treatments, including caudal-tagged and control groups. The authors attribute this to sensitivity to capture, handling, and tag attachment.

**5. Management recommendations.**
- Small bonytail tolerate tagging poorly, so methods that do not harm or kill fish must be identified before reliable telemetry data can be collected.
- Cover is probably a limiting factor for larger bonytail. If cover is scarce, fish may be forced into open water, which exposes them to predators and other mortality factors. Cover could therefore limit population levels.
- Selection or design of bonytail management areas, including grow-out and refugium sites, should consider cover requirements for larger fish.
- Experimental work to test these effects is recommended, but replicate ponds like the Cibola HLP do not currently exist.
