# Native Piscifauna of Utah Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic review by A. Gaylon Cook (Bioconservation International, Provo, Utah) re-examines the native fishes of [[Utah Lake]], a 38,800-ha freshwater body in central Utah, by compiling the descriptive and systematic literature and winnowing the tangled synonymies that have accumulated since the 1800s. The author concludes that many published synonymies are unsupported, that several nomenclatural assignments are wrong, and that the native fauna cannot be reliably delimited without new chemical taxonomic work, such as [[DNA-DNA Hybridization]]. The paper's central practical aim is to provide morphological characterizations that let biologists distinguish species during periodic surveys and during [[June Sucker]] recovery-plan activities.

## Background & Description

Utah Lake lies beside Provo, measures 23.5 by 38.0 km, has a mean depth of 2.9 m and a maximal depth of 4.2 m, and formerly received a flow of about 740 million m³/yr. The first ichthyofaunal report was by [[Jordan and Gilbert]] (1881). Later treatments include [[Hatton]] (1932), [[Hayes]] (1935), and [[Heckmann et al.]] (1981), the last of which offered only a cursory, unreferenced annotated list of native fishes.

The review identifies the principal source of confusion as the broad species lumping by [[David Starr Jordan]] and [[Barton Warren Evermann]] (1896–1898). Of 11 piscine forms treated as species by Robins et al. (1991), 8 were considered polytypic by Mayden et al. (1992). Ages-related morphological differences are cited as a major cause of inflated synonymies.

The author treats each taxon with a detailed synonymy and morphological comparison. Taxa covered include:

- **Cyprinidae:** [[Leatherside Chub]] (*Gila copei*), [[Utah Chub]] (*Siboma atraria*), [[Least Chub]] (*Jotichthys phlegethontis*), [[Longsnout Dace]] (*Rhinichthys cataractae*), [[Western Dace]] (*Rhinichthys nubilus*), and [[Bonneville Redside]] (*Richardsonius hydrophlox*).
- **Catostomidae:** [[Exceptional Sucker]] (*Catostomus generosus*), [[Mountain Sucker]] (*Catostomus platyrhynchus*), [[Utah Sucker]] (*Catostomus ardens*), [[Webug Sucker]] (*Chasmistes fecundus*), and [[June Sucker]] (*Chasmistes liorus*).
- **Salmonidae:** [[Mountain Whitefish]] (*Prosopium williamsoni*) and [[Bonneville Cutthroat Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus clarki utah*).
- **Cottidae:** [[Prickly Sculpin]] (*Cottus semiscaber*).

Two unrelated but connected threads run through the text: the historical provenance of specimens collected during the [[Gunnison-Beckwith Survey]] of 1853–1854, and the taxonomic dispute over Utah Lake sucker species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is primarily taxonomic and historical rather than ecological, but it provides several habitat and life-history details:

- Mormon settlers called the Utah Chub "chub"; it was abundant in the Provo River and Utah Lake, reportedly spawned in muddy streams in May and June, and was considered destructive to trout eggs and fry.
- The Utah Chub's range is limited, according to Sigler and Sigler (1987), to watersheds of ancient [[Lake Bonneville]] and the Snake River.
- Pigmentation and body colour of several cyprinids were reported to be paler in alkaline or turbid waters than in clear streams (Hayes 1935).
- The June Sucker is endangered (Lowe et al. 1990); the Utah Lake sculpin population is believed extirpated (Robins et al. 1991).
- Spawning timing differs among suckers: June Sucker in April and June, Webug Sucker probably in June, Utah Sucker in March to May.
- Provo River was the main spawning and sampling area for lake fishes; the [[Utah Lake Fish Management Advisory Team]] (1994) is cited for management context.

## Key Findings

- **Synonymy errors are widespread.** The author documents "an elaborate web" of synonyms with nomenclatural and species-assignment errors, many linked to age-related morphological variation and to Jordan's lumping of species.
- **Leatherside Chub (*Gila copei*):** Earlier assignment to *Gila gracilis* is rejected. The taxon ranks among the slenderest cyprinids of the basin (BD/BL 22.4 ± 1.5%, n = 55). The name *Gila copei* was ultimately adopted after a series of reassignments.
- **Utah Chub (*Siboma atraria*):** The genus *Tigoma* is judged inappropriate; *Siboma* is the first valid genus assigned to the species. Several names (*obesa*, *lineata*) are judged not to be Utah Chub. The single type specimen of *S. atraria* was most likely collected at Cedar Spring, near present-day Holden, Utah, in October 1853. The author also argues that placing the species in *Gila* is misleading, as *Gila* is paraphyletic (Coburn and Cavender 1992).
- **Least Chub (*Jotichthys phlegethontis*):** Cladistic analysis by Coburn and Cavender (1992) suggests it may be a sister species of the Utah Chub. If confirmed, it would be *Siboma phlegethontis*. The Utah Chub and Least Chub share a delayed closure of the posterior cranial fontanelle in juveniles, a trait the author regards as needing more juvenile specimens.
- **Longsnout Dace and Western Dace:** Several putative synonyms are judged distinct. *Rhinichthys dulcis* and *R. luteus* are judged distinct from the Longsnout Dace, and the Provo River dace may represent a distinct subspecies of *R. transmontanus*. The Western Dace is considered polytypic, with some putative synonyms (e.g., *novemradiata*) judged distinct.
- **Bonneville Redside:** *Richardsonius balteatus* is judged not to be the Bonneville Redside, which is considered a separate species, *R. hydrophlox*, with its synonyms (*taenia*, *montanus*) requiring further study.
- **Sucker identification:** Lowder (1951) claimed three sucker species in Utah Lake; Miller and Smith (1981) argued the Webug Sucker is a hybrid of Utah and June suckers and that the June Sucker is an intergeneric hybrid. The author finds these hybrid hypotheses unsupported, noting non-intermediate traits (e.g., Stubbs 1966) and that scale and fin-ray counts overlap heavily among taxa. Stubbs (1966) found the [[Weberian Mechanism]] more useful for distinguishing the three suckers.
- **June Sucker size trend:** Because recruitment has been low in recent years, the average size of June Suckers has been rising. Mean mass of spawners was 2.4 kg (1991, n = 35) and 2.3 kg (1992, n = 57).
- **Mountain Sucker and June Sucker larvae:** Larvae reported as June Suckers from Provo River in 1982–1984 were identified as Mountain Suckers, so the author stresses that larval identification is critical for recovery surveys.
- **Mountain Whitefish:** The paper notes its broad range and that *Prosopium couesii* may be distinct. A 1970 sample from Provo and Weber rivers ranged from 190 to 425 mm.
- **Bonneville Cutthroat Trout:** Its name has shifted through many combinations, and it is now *O. clarki utah*. The author argues that Girard's *Salar virginalis* was collected in Utah Creek, Colorado, rather than in Utah.
- **Prickly Sculpin:** The Utah Lake form (*Cottus echinatus*) is considered valid only if it differs from *Cottus semiscaber* from Fort Hall, Idaho. The author notes that Bailey and Bond (1963) did not compare the two, and that skin roughness varies widely.
- **Conservation implication:** Because it is unknown whether certain taxa are endemic species or subspecies deserving endangered status, the author calls for [[DNA-DNA Hybridization]] and other molecular studies of Utah Lake's native fishes and their putative conspecifics.
