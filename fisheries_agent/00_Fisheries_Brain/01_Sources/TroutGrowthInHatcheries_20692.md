# Trout Growth in Hatcheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from the *New York Fish and Game Journal* (Vol. 6, No. 2, July 1959) contains three items: a short pharmacology note on drug use in handling [[Black Bear]]s, the main article "Trout Growth in Hatcheries" by [[David C. Haskell]], a New York State hatchery foreman, and the volume index. The Haskell article is the substantive scientific content. It proposes a working definition of hatchery trout growth (constant rate of length increase under constant temperature and adequate food) and derives practical tools from it: a length-weight relationship, daily feeding rates, hatchery carrying capacity, a temperature-unit growth model, and guidance on statistical design of growth trials.

## Background & Description

The article is written for hatchery managers and experimental researchers. Its data are mainly from [[Brook Trout]] under 1½ years old, supplemented by [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] comparisons. The author notes that the New York State feeding chart system, started in 1933 by A. V. Tunison and C. R. Deuel, produced a large body of production records that had not yet been fully analyzed.

The black bear item is a fragment of a separate paper. It describes drug-based handling of [[Black Bear]]s, with respiratory support given priority over stimulation of the respiratory center when breathing stopped. It reports that the drug response was not dramatic and that its therapeutic role is hard to assess. The literature cited (about 20 entries, e.g., Erickson 1957 on trapping and handling black bears; Hall et al. 1953 on [[Flaxedil]]-induced paralysis in white-tailed deer) is not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature** is identified as the most important growth factor. Growth is near zero near freezing and rises with temperature. For brown trout, growth increases up to about 60°F, declines at higher temperatures, and is very small above 70°F. Brook trout grow better than brown trout in the 40s°F, and their upper growth limit appears lower.

**Other growth factors** named in the article are:
- **Care** (feeding practice, thinning, aeration, removal of metabolic wastes, cleanliness). The author says care "will pay off in increased growth."
- **Species and strain.** Usual hatchery strains show similar growth potential. Wild trout introduced to hatcheries generally grow slowly.
- **Water chemistry.** Alkalinity and pH have little effect over a broad range. Trout from very soft water do not grow well in normal hatchery water.
- **Diet.** Cod liver oil increased growth in some trials. Over 30 years of diet development produced only slight growth changes in controlled tests, and hatchery gains are attributed mainly to better management.
- **Health and sexual maturity.** Disease slows growth. Spawning alters metabolism, and egg production may slow growth in female trout during their spawning years.

**Hatchery sites** discussed include the Cortland laboratory, [[Rome Hatchery]] (monthly water temperatures 34–75°F), Van Hornesville, Chateaugay, Bath, Randolph and [[Lily Lake Hatchery]]. Temperature-unit calculations were run for four State hatcheries using 1955–1956 records.

## Key Findings

1. **Growth definition.** Under constant temperature and adequate food, trout rate of length increase is constant, except when metabolism is altered by disease, spawning, or similar factors.

2. **Length-weight relation.** The condition factor concept gives W = KL³. A near-constant value of 0.0004055 (weight in pounds, length in inches) fits the Tunison/Deuel hatchery table for brook, brown and rainbow trout. Lake trout required a different table. The 1-inch and 3/4-inch groups showed discrepancies, attributed to difficulty removing water from the fish.

3. **Growth form.** The cube root of mean weight plotted against time gave a nearly straight line in earlier Cortland records (Haskell 1948). Cube root of weight is proportional to length for hatchery brook trout.

4. **Percentage gain.** For constant length growth, the instantaneous daily percentage gain in weight is inversely proportional to length. A 2-inch fish gains at half the daily percentage of a 1-inch fish, and a 6-inch fish at one-sixth.

5. **Feeding rate.** The daily ration in percent of body weight equals the conversion factor × daily percentage gain (equation 9). A conversion factor of 3.0 was assumed for all-meat diets. Expected brook trout daily length increases are tabulated by temperature (e.g., 0.0069 to 0.0317 inches/day across the table's temperature range). Suggested feeding levels for brook trout range from about 0.6% to 14% of body weight per day depending on size and temperature. The author notes that 1-inch fish could not consume the ration within an 8-hour day at Rome Hatchery, so 24-hour feeding was used until fish reached about 1,000 per pound.

6. **Carrying capacity.** Daily food weight serves as a single criterion of pond carrying capacity across species, sizes and temperatures. At a given temperature, permissible fish weight is proportional to length: a pond holding 100 lb of 2-inch fish could hold 200 lb of 4-inch fish or 300 lb of 6-inch fish.

7. **Temperature units.** Growth is linear with temperature between 42° and 52°F for brook trout, extrapolating to a growth threshold of 38.6°F. One temperature unit (TU) equals the monthly average water temperature minus 38.6 (e.g., 46°F for one month = 7.4 TU; 42.3°F for two months = 7.4 TU). Brook trout require about 21 TU per inch of growth in production hatcheries.

8. **Management target.** New York aims to produce 7-inch brook trout by the opening of the fishing season in the second year. Growing from 3½ inches (January 1) to 7 inches (April 1) requires about 130 TU. The article also calculates that a transfer from hatchery A to hatchery B on October 1 would supply 96.1 TU at A and 32.9 TU at B, totaling 129.

9. **Statistical design.** Fish in successive periods are not independent, so statistical tests should be applied only at the end of a trial, using final weight or mean growth. Lots must be randomly assigned to diets. Re-randomizing troughs each period increases error degrees of freedom (from 4 to 14 in the three-period example with 18 items), which improves test sensitivity. Using Tunison et al. (1939) data (six lots, 24 weeks), the article shows the experimental variation is large and the test is insensitive.

10. **Volume index.** The index shows other papers in the volume: rainbow trout biology and vital statistics in the Finger Lakes region (Hartman, pp. 121–178), effects of five herbicides on farm pond plants and fish (Dance and others), mink food habits in New York (Hamilton), and waterfowl potential for mass production (Holm and Bauer).
