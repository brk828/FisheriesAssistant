# Post-Stocking Dispersal, Habitat Use, and Behavioral Acclimation of Juvenile Razorback Suckers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined the movements, habitat use, and short-term behavioral acclimation of stocked juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered [[Colorado River]] fish, in [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Mohave]] during summer 1997. Juvenile razorbacks are rarely found in the wild, and earlier telemetry work had focused almost entirely on adults implanted with transmitters surgically. To avoid surgical stress, the authors externally attached [[Sonic Telemetry]] transmitters to the [[Dorsal Keel]] of 55 fish (27 released in Lake Mohave, 28 in Lake Powell). Half of each group was held for three days in blocked coves to test whether site acclimation influenced short-term dispersal.

Post-stocking dispersal was rapid and widespread. Movement was greatest immediately after release and declined over time. After the first week, fish were typically found in backwaters and coves, and more than 50% of detections were in cover. Preliminary data suggest that [[Behavioral Acclimation]] may influence dispersal: 64% of the Lake Powell fish that remained in the study area for more than 30 days had been held in coves, a sample too small to be conclusive. The authors judged [[Sonic Telemetry]] less effective than expected because fish used shallow, vegetated backwaters that attenuate the signal. They recommended expanded monitoring of shallow flooded habitat, stocking of fish over 30 cm, winter rather than year-round stocking, study of reservoir fluctuation effects on predators, and radio rather than sonic transmitters for future juvenile work.

## Background & Description

**Species and life stage.** The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a native [[Colorado River]] catostomid with a pronounced dorsal keel in adults that is less developed in juveniles. Information on post-stocking movement and habitat use is mostly limited to adults. Juveniles are rarely encountered in the wild, and wild populations are dominated by old individuals (Minckley 1983; Minckley et al. 1991; Gutermuth et al. 1994; Modde 1996). Reintroduction efforts have largely failed. In one ten-year program in Arizona, nearly 12 million razorback suckers were stocked with no measurable results. Most were stocked as fingerlings or fry, and young fish moved downstream en masse and were rapidly eaten by [[Channel Catfish]] and flathead catfish (Brooks 1985; Marsh and Brooks 1989).

**Why external attachment.** Surgically implanted transmitters are not feasible for small fish. Earlier surgical attempts on juveniles produced high transmitter shedding and possible mortality. Saddle-mounted transmitters tested on surrogate species (white sucker and [[Largemouth Bass]]) caused severe fungal and bacterial infection at the attachment point (Winter et al. 1978). Galvanic-corrosion release mechanisms using steel, aluminum, and copper wire harnesses were unreliable.

**Study fish.**
- **Lake Powell:** About 300 surplus "Green River" stock fish from [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]] (Utah) were held in golf-course ponds at Page, Arizona, from May 1996. On May 3, 1997, 30 fish averaging 358 mm TL (335-402 mm) were captured and released at Castle Creek Canyon (RK 72), San Juan Arm. Of 30 transmitters, 28 functioned properly.
- **Lake Mohave:** Fish from [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], averaging 241 mm TL (201-290 mm per text; appendix range 210-290 mm). Fish were also [[PIT Tagging|PIT-tagged]]. Released May 20, 1997, at Owl Cove (RK 45), Tequila Cove (RK 32 or 34, as reported), and Mesa Cove (RK 29 or 24, as reported).

**Transmitters.** Sonotronics MT-95-2 ultrasonic transmitters measured 9 × 36 mm, weighed 3 g in water, had an estimated battery life of 90 days, and had a normal detection range of 500 m. Ultrasonic units were chosen over radio because their signal is detectable regardless of depth, which in Lake Powell can exceed 100 m.

**Attachment method.** Earlier external attachment used two sutures and a plastic backing plate, similar to Carlin tags. For this study, a single suture was passed through the dorsal keel about 6-8 mm below the keel, with a monofilament line tied over the keel with three square knots. The procedure was modified to lack a backing plate so that transmitters would eventually shed and wounds would heal quickly. Processing took under a minute per fish, and the whole procedure took about 1.5 hours. Fish showed no obvious stress or swimming impairment.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake Powell.** The San Juan Arm is narrow. Survey coverage extended from RK 51 to RK 85 (about 34 km) initially. A 3 m waterfall blocked upstream access at release until reservoir rise inundated it by June 10. Rising water also flooded hundreds of hectares of tamarisk that could not be monitored effectively. Late-season surveys extended upstream to Slickrock Rapids (RK 106), and a week-long search of the full 106 km reach (RK 0-106) found no additional fish.

**Lake Mohave.** The reservoir (about 5 km wide) was surveyed on a GPS grid with 71 listening stations spaced 1,600 m apart, later increased to more than 125 stations, with intensified 100 m-interval monitoring of flooded shoreline vegetation. A full survey of the 106 km length normally took two days.

**Cover and flooded habitat.** Cover types used in Lake Mohave were flooded tamarisk (*Tamarix pentandra*, 48-86%), submergent vegetation (*Najas* or *Potamogeton* spp., about 40%), and rock ledges or rubble (12-14%). Lake Powell had similar cover types except for emergent vegetation. Submerged tamarisk and dense [[Pondweed]] stands reduced detection range to under 40 m, and broad flooded tamarisk bands were impenetrable to boats.

**Predators and reservoir fluctuation.** Shallow backwaters are also habitat for known predators, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Striped Bass]]. In stable Lake Mohave, territorial predators are less affected by elevation changes. In larger reservoirs such as Lakes Powell and Mead, annual elevation can fluctuate up to 20 m, shifting inflow habitats as much as 15 km in a few weeks. The authors suggest that this fluctuation could influence predator distribution and reproduction and could be manipulated to regulate predator densities. The lower 100 km of the San Juan, Green, and Colorado rivers have little nursery habitat, so natural recruitment may depend on habitat in [[Lake Powell]] itself.

**Habitat change.** The authors attribute reduced cover availability to channelization, loss of backwaters and oxbows, fluctuating hydroelectric releases, and loss of large snags and woody debris.

**Temperature.** Surface water in shallow backwaters was 1-3 °C warmer than the main lake. Body temperature data for 10 adults and one two-year-old juvenile in Lake Mohave suggested that juveniles occupy a thermal regime equal to or warmer than the upper range used by adults ([[Reservoir Fluctuation]] and temperature data from Mueller et al. 1998).

## Key Findings

**1. Dispersal was rapid, widespread, and declined over time.**
- Lake Mohave: mean daily movement 347 m/d (3-1,500 m/d).
- Lake Powell: mean daily movement 480 m/d (3-1,800 m/d). The executive summary also reports that maximum daily distance averaged over 2 km/d (0 to over 7 km/d) for Powell fish. Results report a 21-fish average maximum of over 2,080 m/d (400-5,000 m/d). These figures differ by source and are reported here as given.
- Fish left release sites within 5 days. In Powell, fish moved uplake, with 21 of 23 fish (92%) moving upstream. In Mohave, 84% moved downlake.
- About 50% of stocked suckers emigrated from the 46 km Powell study area during the first month. Some fish that were disturbed moved more than 100 m and then returned to their original location.
- The most mobile Mohave fish (#238) traveled about 20.5 km in one day. Its transmitter then moved nearly 63 km over four days in pelagic zones, which suggests it was eaten by a [[Striped Bass]].

**2. Contact and retention were limited.**
- Lake Powell: contact made with 23 of 28 fish initially.
- Lake Mohave: 17 of 27 fish detected initially, with only 7 remaining in contact after the second week.
- Attrition was high in Mohave; nearly 80% of transmitters disappeared. Causes considered were transmitter failure, predation, and fish entering impenetrable flooded habitat.
- Initial transmitter failure was 2.5% (2 of 80).

**3. Habitat use was strikingly similar between reservoirs.**
- Fish were within 10 m of shore 62% of the time in Lake Mohave (N = 60) and 65% in Lake Powell (N = 159). Detections beyond 50 m were 15-20% of sightings.
- More than 50% of observations were in cover, used for concealment.
- Juveniles were seldom found in narrow, deep canyon habitat. They favored the backs of coves and flooded washes, typically within 10 m of shore and at depths under 2 m.
- Juveniles strongly preferred flooded backwater complexes at Castle Creek Canyon and Mike's Canyon (RK 73-79). The average observed range in Powell was 2.2 km (0.1-7.4 km), but actual dispersal range could not be determined because contact was lost with 50% of fish.

**4. Behavioral acclimation may affect dispersal.**
- Of the 14 Powell fish that remained in the study area more than 30 days, 64% had been cove-held, twice the proportion of immediately released fish.
- The sample was small and may reflect normal variability. The authors concluded that holding did not deter fish from staying in the area and might encourage it.
- Lake Mohave data were inadequate to draw conclusions.

**5. External attachment to the dorsal keel is viable for short-term studies.**
- Dissection showed a cartilaginous ridge and thin, poorly ossified bony plate, with no major arteries, veins, or musculature at the attachment site.
- Tank test 1 (five fish, about 23 cm TL): average transmitter retention 3.8 weeks (2 to over 8 weeks); all fish recovered; wounds healed in 2-4 weeks with no infection.
- Tank test 2 (ten larger fish, about 29 cm, two sutures, plus one control): five fish including the control died of unknown causes. The first transmitter was lost in week 5, and the remaining five retained tags through week 9.
- Lake Mohave fish (about 241 mm) retained transmitters for less than 2 weeks, shorter than tank fish or Lake Powell fish (over 4 weeks). Possible causes are snagging on aquatic macrophytes (one fish trailed pondweed) and incomplete ossification of the keel in younger fish. Single sutures were less intrusive but less permanent.
- The authors consider external attachment less stressful than dart tags or surgery for behavioral studies, and a non-lethal option for all age classes.

**6. Recommendations for stocking and research.**
- Test the three-day holding effect further in both reservoir and riverine habitats.
- Stock fish larger than 30 cm, and consider 40 cm for the San Juan River, per [[Ryden 1997]].
- Delay stocking until winter when predators are dormant (Marsh and Brooks 1989), and investigate autumn-winter rather than year-round stocking.
- Expand monitoring to shallow, flooded habitat (under 2 m depth).
- Study reservoir fluctuation zones and their effect on predator density and colonization.
- Use radio rather than sonic transmitters for future juvenile telemetry, and consider external attachment for short-term studies.
- Focus research on reducing predation in early-life habitats, since stocking larger fish does not solve early-life survival problems.
