# The Black Basses of Alabama

#ichthyology #marine-biology #micropterus

## Executive Summary

This 1954 reprint from *Alabama Conservation* by J. M. Lawrence, Assistant Fish Culturist at the [[A.P.I. Agricultural Experiment Station]], is a field identification guide to the four [[Black Bass]] species found in Alabama's ponds, lakes, and streams. It gives external and internal diagnostic features, typical coloration, habitat notes, and size records for each species, so that anglers can identify their catch. The guide places the [[Largemouth Bass]] as the most commonly caught species in ponds and some large impoundments, and it separates the three remaining species, which are classed as smallmouth basses.

## Background & Description

The article covers four species of the genus *Micropterus*:

- **[[Largemouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus salmoides* Lacepede)
- **[[Northern Smallmouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus dolomieu* Lacepede)
- **[[Spotted Bass]]** (*Micropterus punctulatus* Rafinesque)
- **[[Redeye Bass]]** (*Micropterus coosae* Hubbs & Bailey)

The author states that the last three are all classed as smallmouth bass. The article notes that the Alabama spotted bass is a subspecies of the northern spotted bass, but the two are treated together for practical purposes. Each species entry includes a sketch, a description of distinguishing marks, typical coloring, and typical habitat.

The largemouth has long been regarded as the prize catch among southern anglers. Its popularity has grown with the [[Farm Fish Pond Program]]. The average catch weighs 1 to 2 pounds, though individuals over 10 pounds have been taken from some Alabama waters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and distribution**

- The largemouth is the species most often taken from ponds and some large impoundments.
- The northern smallmouth is native to the [[Tennessee River Drainage]]. Where it occurs in the [[Alabama River System]], it was introduced by stocking. It is caught occasionally in Alabama tributaries of the Tennessee River and in large [[Tennessee Valley Authority Impoundments]].
- The spotted bass occurs in the larger streams and impoundments throughout the state.
- The redeye bass is native only to the Alabama and [[Chattahoochee River Drainage]]. It has not been introduced into the Tennessee River drainage. It is most abundant in upland tributaries and also grows in ponds built on small streams.

**Water-dependent coloration**

The author links coloring to water type:

- Largemouths from brackish water are bright green.
- Largemouths from swamp water range from brown to almost black.
- Largemouths from muddy water are almost white.
- Largemouths from fertilized ponds range from green to black.
- In all conditions the belly of the largemouth is white.
- Smallmouth coloring also varies with water type.

**Pond culture trials**

- At the [[A.P.I. Agricultural Experiment Station]] in Auburn, the northern smallmouth was tested in pond culture with [[Bluegill Bream]]. It was not voracious enough to control the bluegill population.
- In separate trials with smallmouth alone in a pond, the fish reproduced and reached 6 to 8 ounces within one growing season. The largest smallmouth from these ponds weighed 4 pounds.
- The spotted bass had not been tested as a pond fish when the article was written.

## Key Findings

**Largemouth Bass**

- Upper jaw extends well behind the hind margin of the eye.
- Cheek scales are large and easy to see at a glance.
- The spiny and soft-rayed portions of the dorsal fin look separated, although they are joined by a narrow membrane.
- The side markings are usually little more than a lateral stripe. The stripe is very pronounced in young fish and may become indistinct with age.
- Internally, the [[Pyloric Caeca]] are usually branched. In smallmouths most are simple, unbranched tubules.

**Northern Smallmouth Bass**

- Upper jaw does not extend behind the rear margin of the eye.
- Cheek scales are small and hard to distinguish at a glance.
- Spiny and soft portions of the dorsal fin are well joined.
- Sides bear dark, irregularly sized vertical markings. A distinct horizontal lateral stripe is never present, in either young or old fish.
- Coloring is somewhat lighter than the largemouth, typically brownish-green on the sides and back with darker blotches.

**Spotted Bass**

- Head is more elongate and pointed than in the other smallmouths. It flattens between the eyes with age.
- A dark, practically straight horizontal stripe runs from the eye to a dark caudal spot at the base of the tail fin.
- Dark markings appear on the scale rows of the belly and tail.
- Dark blotches appear on the back above the horizontal stripe.
- Teeth are present on the tongue near its tip.
- Coloring is similar to the largemouth. The horizontal stripe and side bands range from brown to black.
- Average size and weight are not given in the article.

**Redeye Bass**

- Body is more robust and rounded than the northern smallmouth or spotted bass.
- No horizontal stripes on the sides, and no dark spots at the base of the tail fin.
- Small scales are evident on the base of the soft dorsal and anal fins and on the base of the tail fin.
- Dark markings appear on the scale rows of the sides and tail, with scattered dark marks on the soft fins.
- Faint bar-like markings on the back fade out near the mid-line. They are more distinct in smaller fish.
- Eye color varies from brown to red.
- Individuals over 3 pounds have been recorded, but the average catch is about 12 ounces.

**Identification summary**

- The three smallmouth species are separated mainly by side markings and body shape: the northern smallmouth has irregular vertical markings, the spotted bass has a horizontal stripe with a caudal spot, and the redeye has a rounder body with faint bars and no horizontal stripe.
- The largemouth is separated from all smallmouths by jaw length and cheek scale size, and internally by branched pyloric caeca.
