## Executive Summary

This 1984 report, adopted by the American Fisheries Society (AFS) [[Exotic Fish Section]] at its 1983 Milwaukee business meeting, addresses the lack of standardized terminology for describing [[Introduced Species|introduced organisms]] in fisheries science. Authored by Paul L. Shafland and William M. Lewis, the paper traces the historical evolution of terms like [[Exotic Species]] and [[Transplanted Species]] since the 1969 "Invitational Conference on Exotic Fishes and Related Problems" and the 1973 AFS position statement. The authors argue that political (national-boundary-based) definitions, while imperfect, should be retained for regulatory consistency, while descriptive terms (established, possibly established, localized, reported) should be used to characterize population status. The paper is foundational to standardizing invasion biology nomenclature within North American fisheries science and calls for new, non-political terminology to be developed via peer review rather than redefinition of existing terms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

While no specific species or habitat is analyzed, the paper establishes a conceptual framework for classifying the ecological status of any [[Introduced Species]] relative to its native and non-native ranges:

- **[[Introduced]]**: Any plant or animal moved from one locale to another by human action (individual, group, or population present due to man's actions).
- **[[Exotic Species]]**: An organism introduced from a foreign country — its entire native range lies outside the country where it is now found (based on political/national boundaries, following Webster's Dictionary and the 1969 Invitational Conference definition).
- **[[Transplanted Species]]**: An organism moved outside its native range but within the same country where it naturally occurs (i.e., moved between watersheds within country of origin).

The authors critique the political-boundary-based classification as sometimes ecologically uninformative — e.g., an "exotic" species moved a short distance across a national border into a similar biotic community may be less ecologically disruptive than a "transplant" moved across a major zoogeographic barrier (e.g., Florida to Hawaii or Alaska) within the same country. They propose that future terminology consider **degree of community similarity** between an organism's native and introduced ranges as a more ecologically meaningful criterion than nationality alone.

Population status descriptors relevant to niche establishment and management prioritization:

- **[[Established Species|Established]]**: Introduced organism with a permanent population unlikely to be eliminated by man or natural causes.
- **[[Possibly Established]]**: Reproducing population lacking permanent status, but impractical to eliminate.
- **[[Localized Population]]**: Confined, reproducing population that can be eliminated via standard control methods.
- **[[Reported]]**: An introduced organism collected with no evidence of reproduction.

## Key Findings

- The first formal AFS attempt to categorize introduced fish occurred at the 1969 "Invitational Conference on Exotic Fishes and Related Problems," co-sponsored with the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists (ASIH), which defined exotic and transplanted species based on national/watershed boundaries (Sport Fishing Institute 1969; Lachner et al. 1970).
- The 1973 AFS Position Statement attempted an ecosystem-based (rather than political) definition of "exotic," but this was not widely adopted due to the ambiguous scale of "[[Ecosystem]]" (ranging from oceans to temporary ponds).
- The **fish nationality concept** (political boundary-based classification) remains the prevailing standard due to its simplicity, despite criticism that it conflates biologically and economically distinct types of introductions under a single political criterion.
- The authors recommend maintaining existing political definitions for "exotic" and "transplanted" to preserve regulatory consistency (many state/federal regulations are built around these terms), while calling for **new, original, non-political terminology** — rather than redefinition of existing terms — to capture ecologically meaningful distinctions (e.g., community similarity between native and introduced ranges).
- Only two subcategories of "introduced" are formally recognized in this framework (exotic, transplanted); other introduction types (e.g., supplemental stocking within a species' native range) still lack standardized terminology.
- Permanence of introduced populations is emphasized as ecologically and managerially significant — neither reproduction alone nor duration of persistence is sufficient to establish permanence, since some populations remain unstable for years before disappearing.
- This report serves as a historical anchor point for later, more nuanced invasion biology terminology (e.g., [[Naturalized Species]], [[Invasive Species]]) that developed in subsequent decades of the field.
