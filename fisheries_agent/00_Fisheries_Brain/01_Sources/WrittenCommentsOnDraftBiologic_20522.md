# Defenders of Wildlife Comments on Bureau of Reclamation Draft Biological Assessment for Lower Colorado River Operations

#conservation-policy #endangered-species #river-management #ESA-section-7

## Executive Summary

This document is a formal comment letter from [[Defenders of Wildlife]] to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] regarding the Draft Biological Assessment (DBA) of Operations, Maintenance, and Sensitive Species of the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Submitted May 15, 1996, the letter represents input from 135,000 members nationwide and provides extensive criticism of the Bureau's consultation process under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The organization expresses deep disappointment with the DBA's legal and biological adequacy, arguing it cannot serve as an adequate basis for formal consultation with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. The letter identifies fundamental flaws in geographic scope, temporal scope, operational descriptions, species effects analyses, and critical habitat assessments. [[Defenders of Wildlife]] contends that decades of Bureau operations have caused severe ecological degradation along the [[Lower Colorado River]] and that current consultation procedures perpetuate rather than remedy these impacts on threatened and endangered species.

## Background & Description

The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] prepared a Draft Biological Assessment covering operations and maintenance activities on the [[Lower Colorado River]] mainstem, defined as the reach from Pierce Ferry (upper [[Lake Mead]]) to the Southerly International Boundary with Mexico. The DBA was developed initially as a "biological document" for the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] (LCRMSCP) Steering Committee, then transformed into a biological assessment only after [[Defenders of Wildlife]] threatened litigation for unreasonable delay in ESA consultation.

The document addresses multiple listed species including [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and other native fish species. The Bureau limited consultation scope to a five-year "interim period" or until completion of the long-term Multi-Species Conservation Program. Operations covered include water delivery, reservoir operations, river maintenance (dredging, levee repair, bank stabilization), and quarrying activities.

Historical context reveals that the [[Lower Colorado River]] has been transformed from a natural river system into what experts describe as a "plumbing system" through construction of major dams ([[Hoover Dam]], [[Parker Dam]]), canals, levees, and channels. Native riparian habitat dominated by cottonwood-willow forests has been largely replaced by nonnative saltcedar, fundamentally altering habitat for native species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Lower Colorado River]] ecosystem has experienced severe degradation from decades of Bureau operations. Historic cottonwood-willow riparian forests that once supported diverse native species have been eliminated or severely reduced. Natural spring flooding that historically regenerated native vegetation and flushed salts from floodplain soils has been eliminated by dam operations and levee systems.

Current conditions favor nonnative species over natives. Exotic fish species thrive in reservoir and mainstem conditions created by Bureau operations, while endangered native fish like [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] cannot successfully reproduce and recruit. The [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], listed as endangered, was historically more common when native riparian habitats were extensive and undisturbed with suitable nesting structure.

Water operations have fundamentally altered flow regimes. Annual flows and seasonal peak flows have been dramatically modified from pre-dam conditions. Rapid drawdown conditions in reservoirs and regulated river reaches create stranding hazards for spawning native fish and their eggs. Sediment transport has been altered, with sediment trapped behind dams rather than aggrading the riverbed and maintaining shallow groundwater conditions that historically created marsh-like habitat throughout the floodplain.

Critical habitat designated for [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] includes currently unoccupied river reaches considered vital for species reestablishment and recovery. However, current Bureau operations perpetuate conditions that render this critical habitat unsuitable for native fish recovery.

Impacts extend beyond the U.S. border. Bureau water management affects the [[Colorado River Delta]] in Mexico by withholding water, changing discharge timing, and increasing salinity, with consequences for listed and candidate species including [[Desert Pupfish]], totoaba, and cochito (vaquita).

## Key Findings

**Geographic Scope Deficiencies**: The DBA artificially limits analysis to the mainstem, excluding Bureau operations on tributary rivers ([[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], [[Gila River]]) and activities in Mexico that affect the same species. ESA Section 7(a)(2) requires comprehensive analysis of all agency actions potentially affecting listed species throughout their range. The DBA provides only satellite-level perspective without detailed maps precisely delimiting consultation boundaries or showing where specific maintenance activities occur.

**Temporal Scope Problems**: The five-year consultation limitation has no basis in the ESA and creates confusion about effects determinations. The ESA requires agencies to ensure actions do not jeopardize species here and now, not in some future timeframe. The Bureau cannot delay consultation by hoping for a future Multi-Species Conservation Program. If activities affect species currently, formal consultation must proceed immediately regardless of potential future conservation plans.

**Inadequate Operational Descriptions**: The DBA provides insufficient detail about Bureau activities to support intelligent effects determinations. River maintenance descriptions (pages 44-45, 134-140) lack site-specific information, maps, or details about work locations, magnitudes, frequencies, and recent history. For example, dredging descriptions state only that "dredging has continued in the basin since 1982 on an intermittent basis" without specifying where, when, how much, or potential nexus to listed species habitat. Reservoir operations descriptions similarly lack plain English explanations of effects on river flows and species.

**Flawed Species Effects Analyses**: The DBA applies incorrect legal standards throughout. For [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], the conclusion of "no effect" is indefensible. The proper standard is whether species "may be affected" (50 CFR § 402.14), not whether Bureau intends to change operations. Bureau activities prevent regeneration of native cottonwood-willow habitat essential for Flycatcher recovery. The Fish and Wildlife Service recognizes that for species on the brink of extinction, adverse effects to recovery potential constitute jeopardy under Section 7(a)(2). The DBA ignores that Bureau operations preclude habitat regeneration, thereby affecting the species' continued existence.

For [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], the DBA inappropriately compares effects to a hypothetical "uncontrolled river system" rather than assessing whether current operations may affect the species. For native endangered fish, the DBA incredibly omits that Bureau activities create conditions favoring exotic fish that harm native species—the main obstacle to native fish recovery. The analysis contains unsupported statements contradicting expert knowledge, such as claiming native fish would avoid rapid drawdown impacts by spawning in "more permanently watered gravel bars," when these species are not tidal species evolved to handle fluctuating water levels.

**Critical Habitat Analysis Failures**: The DBA's determination regarding designated critical habitat for [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] is cursory and biologically indefensible. Bureau operations render critical habitat useless for native fish recovery because habitat conditions favor exotic fish, preventing successful native fish reproduction and recruitment. The analysis ignores impacts on recovery potential, though critical habitat was designated specifically to help recover fish in currently unoccupied river stretches.

**Section 7(a)(1) Non-Compliance**: The Bureau has substantially failed to implement ESA Section 7(a)(1), which requires federal agencies to "utilize their authorities in furtherance of the purposes of [the ESA] by carrying out programs for the conservation of endangered species." Rather than recovering species, Bureau operations perpetuate degraded conditions, causing species to "tread water" or continue declining.

**Section 7(d) Concerns**: The DBA fails to specify which activities will be stopped pending consultation completion. ESA Section 7(d) forbids "any irreversible or irretrievable commitment of resources" that may foreclose reasonable and prudent alternatives. Activities like levee repair or replacement may need to be halted if consultation determines structures should be removed to facilitate species protection and recovery.

**Scientific Data Inadequacies**: The DBA fails to use best available scientific data from experts including [[W.L. Minckley]], [[Paul Marsh]], and [[Robert Ohmart]]. Critical baseline data is missing, including reliable assessment of pre-dam riverbed elevations and floodplain conditions. All data is based on post-1904 flood conditions, distorting the historical baseline. The 1904 flood was a channel-clearing event; prior conditions may have featured clear water with tremendous primary productivity for native fish in the mainstem, contradicting DBA assumptions about backwater habitat requirements.

**Procedural Deficiencies**: The DBA was not prepared as a true biological assessment but rather as a descriptive "biological document" for the LCRMSCP, with cursory two-sentence "effect determinations" added only after litigation threat. This history demonstrates insufficient time was spent making effects determinations for any species. The document merits zero judicial deference if finalized in substantially current form.

**Mexican Species Exclusion**: The DBA's conclusion of "no direct effect" on species in Mexico is inconceivable. By withholding water, changing discharge timing, and increasing salinity, the Bureau affects survival and recovery of listed and candidate species in the [[Colorado River Delta]]. The ESA applies to U.S. agency actions in foreign countries and domestic actions affecting listed species abroad. The species list for Mexico should not be limited to [[Desert Pupfish]], totoaba, and cochito.

**Recommendations**: [[Defenders of Wildlife]] insists the Bureau completely rewrite the DBA to address all identified concerns before submitting to the Fish and Wildlife Service for formal consultation. The organization reserves the right to litigate for continuing unreasonable delay. The consultation should become a vehicle for designing and implementing effective recovery actions for the [[Lower Colorado River]] ecosystem, including habitat regeneration through modified flow regimes, native vegetation restoration, and exotic species control.
