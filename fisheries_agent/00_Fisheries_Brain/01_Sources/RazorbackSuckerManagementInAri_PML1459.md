# Razorback Sucker Management in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This document reviews a [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) restoration program in Arizona, begun in 1981 under a ten-year memorandum of understanding between the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. By 1985, more than 10 million juveniles, fingerlings, and fry had been stocked at 28 sites on the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]], and [[Verde River]] and their tributaries. Post-stocking studies in 1984 and 1985 found that stocked fish moved downstream at night and that introduced catfishes posed a significant predation threat. The author recommends stocking strategies that reduce predation, staged introductions over a long period, and concentrated monitoring on a single river system.

The author is Paul C. Marsh of the Center for Environmental Studies and Department of Zoology, Arizona State University, Tempe. ## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking and distribution**
- More than 10 million juveniles, fingerlings, and fry were stocked by 1985.
- Stocking occurred at 28 sites across the Gila, Salt, and Verde river systems and their tributaries.
- Stocking sites and downstream riverine reaches were monitored annually. Reservoirs on each system were sampled in 1985.
- Few stocked fish were recaptured more than a few weeks after introduction.

## Key Findings

1. **Scale of effort:** Over 10 million fish were stocked at 28 sites by 1985, and monitoring was conducted annually across three river systems.

2. **Low persistence:** Few stocked fish were recaptured beyond a few weeks, indicating poor short-term retention.

3. **Downstream dispersal:** Juvenile fish moved downstream at night. This behavior was documented in the 1984–1985 post-stocking studies.

4. **Predation risk:** Introduced catfishes present a significant threat to stocked razorback suckers.

5. **Recommended stocking practices:**
   - Stock at upstream localities where predator populations are absent or small.
   - Stock in winter, when predation may be reduced.
   - Stock where predators have been removed or at least depleted.
   - Introduce a few individuals over a wide time span rather than releasing large groups at once.

6. **Monitoring strategy:** Restrict intensive monitoring to a single river system to make better use of limited resources and obtain clearer information on successes and failures.

7. **Open research questions:**
   - Whether raceway-reared and pond-reared fish differ in survival.
   - Whether fish can be trained in predator avoidance before release.
