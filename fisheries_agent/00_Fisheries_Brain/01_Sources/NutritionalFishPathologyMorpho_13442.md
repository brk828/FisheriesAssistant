# Nutritional Fish Pathology: Morphological Signs of Nutrient Deficiency and Toxicity in Farmed Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #nutritional-pathology

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fish Technical Paper (No. 330, 1992, 75 p.) by [[Albert G.J. Tacon]] is a synthesis of published literature on the gross morphological signs of dietary nutrient deficiency and toxicity in farmed fish. It was adapted from lecture material for an FAO training course on fish disease diagnosis held in Coyhaique, Chile, in late 1992. The paper is aimed at aquaculture practitioners who feed nutritionally complete diets in intensive, clear-water systems, and it argues that [[Nutritional Fish Pathology]] needs closer collaboration between fish pathologists and nutritionists. Disorders are organized under protein, lipid, mineral, and vitamin nutrition, plus endogenous plant anti-nutritional factors and adventitious feed contaminants.

## Background & Description

- **Motivation:** When sick fish show no recognisable pathogen, the cause is often diet or water quality. Nutritionally related "diseases" remain poorly charted, and feed sales staff and veterinarians often lack the expertise to diagnose them.
- **Scope:** Extensive and semi-intensive systems obtain some nutrients from natural pond food. Intensive systems depend entirely on a nutritionally complete feed. Commercial feeds were often developed without reliable published nutrient requirements for many species.
- **Format:** The document is a literature synthesis with tables listing deficiency and toxicity signs per species, extensive reference lists, and an annex of scientific and common names (about 37 species).
- **Caveat:** The author notes that few studies have systematically characterised these conditions under practical farming conditions, so many findings come from laboratory trials.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses farmed systems rather than wild ecosystems, but it covers several environmental interactions:

- **Water temperature:** Reported incidence of scoliosis and cataract increased with decreasing and increasing water temperature, respectively (tryptophan deficiency in chum salmon, Akiyama, Mori & Murai, 1986).
- **Water hardness and calcium:** Environmental calcium interacts with dietary zinc in channel catfish fry growth and survival (Scarpa & Gatlin, 1992).
- **Leaching into water:** Water-soluble feed nutrients are lost before ingestion. Up to 50-70% of residual vitamin C leached within 10 seconds of immersion in one study. In shrimp feed, one hour in seawater removed 98% of thiamine and 89% of ascorbic acid (Cuzon, Hew & Cognie, 1982).
- **Light, heat, and metals:** Oxidation of lipids is accelerated by light, temperature, haeme compounds, and trace elements (Fe, Cu, Zn).
- **Gut microflora:** Antibiotic use may reduce gut bacterial synthesis of B vitamins, which contributes to requirements in omnivorous species such as carp, tilapia, and channel catfish.
- **Stress:** Vitamin requirements may rise under stressful or adverse environmental conditions.

## Key Findings

### 1. Protein nutrition
- Essential amino acid deficiency is linked to signs including scoliosis, lordosis, cataract, renal calcinosis, and fin erosion. Methionine, tryptophan, and leucine/isoleucine imbalances are the most notable.
- Common feed-formulation pitfalls include methionine deficiency in plant proteins and lysine deficiency in oilseeds. Compared with [[Fish Meal]], which has a well-balanced amino acid profile, most alternative protein sources are imbalanced.
- Excessive heat in fish meal manufacture reduces digestibility, and heat-treated fish meals containing histidine and histamine may yield [[Gizzerosine]], which causes gizzard erosion in chicks and gastric changes in trout.
- Dietary leucine at 13.4% of diet caused scoliosis, deformed opercula, and scale loss in rainbow trout.
- [[Leucaena Leucocephala]] (mimosine), [[Canavalia Ensiformis]] (L-canavanine), and [[Sesbania Grandiflora]] are toxic plant protein sources.

### 2. Lipid nutrition
- Essential fatty acid deficiency causes reduced growth, swollen pale livers (fatty liver), and high mortality across more than 15 species in Table 3.
- Excess essential fatty acids reduced growth and feed efficiency in rainbow trout and coho salmon.
- Cyclopropenoic acid in cottonseed products reduced growth in rainbow trout, caused liver damage, and acted as a synergist for aflatoxin carcinogenicity.
- Oxidised, unprotected polyunsaturated fats (fish oil, fish meal, rice bran) are the major lipid hazard. Signs in fish fed oxidised fish oil include lordosis, exophthalmia, muscular dystrophy, ceroid liver deposits, and anaemia. In most trials, dl-alpha-tocopherol acetate (vitamin E) prevented these effects. The one exception was a six-week *O. niloticus* trial in which vitamin E prevented only lordosis.

### 3. Mineral nutrition
- Mineral deficiency signs include cataract, short body dwarfism, scoliosis, anaemia, and reduced growth. Deficiency is linked to missing premixes and to imbalances.
- White fish meal (high ash) has lower zinc, manganese, and magnesium bioavailability than brown low-ash meal.
- Phytate in plant protein binds zinc, iron, copper, and other trace elements. Stomachless fish cannot dissolve bone phosphorus well, so soluble phosphate salts must be supplied.
- Excess dietary calcium antagonises phosphorus, zinc, iron, and manganese absorption. Excess phosphorus (1.8% of diet) also reduced growth and zinc availability in rainbow trout, so a balanced Ca:P ratio matters.
- Toxic metals in feed ingredients include copper, lead, cadmium, mercury, arsenic, selenium, and fluoride. Lead at dietary levels of the study caused scoliosis, lordosis, and anaemia in rainbow trout.

### 4. Vitamin nutrition
- Table 8 lists deficiency signs for about 15 vitamins across more than 30 species. Vitamin C deficiency produces the most common and severe signs (scoliosis, lordosis, haemorrhage, fin erosion, and renal granuloma).
- Water-soluble vitamins are unstable during processing and storage. Vitamin C losses of 90-95% have been reported in practical feeds. Coated or phosphorylated forms such as [[Ascorbyl-2-Polyphosphate]] retain far more activity than free ascorbic acid.
- Fat-soluble vitamins accumulate, so hypervitaminosis A (2.2-2.7 million IU/kg in salmonids) is a possible toxicity risk, though unlikely under practical conditions.
- Anti-vitamin factors include avidin in raw egg white, thiaminase in raw fish and shellfish, and anti-vitamin factors in raw soybean.

### 5. Plant anti-nutritional factors and contaminants
- Table 12 lists anti-nutritional factors for more than 40 plant feedstuffs (cereals, root tubers, legumes, and oilseeds). Heat treatment inactivates many of them, but toxicological data exist for only a minority.
- Gossypol in cottonseed reduced growth in rainbow trout at 0.05% of diet and caused liver necrosis at 0.009%.
- Fungal toxins are the most important adventitious contaminants. More than 200 mycotoxins have been identified in feed ingredients.
- [[Aflatoxin B1]] causes liver tumours in rainbow trout after one year at 0.4-1.0 ppb. Trout are much more sensitive than channel catfish. A US limit of 20 ppb applies to maize and peanut products for fish and other immature animals.
- Ochratoxin A killed trout at an LD50 of 4.67 mg/kg. Cyclopiazonic acid at 100 ppb reduced growth in channel catfish.

### 6. Major pathology summary (Table 13)
The concluding table links six major conditions to dietary causes:
- **Scoliosis/lordosis:** deficiencies of tryptophan, magnesium, phosphorus, vitamin C, and essential fatty acids; toxicities of lead, cadmium, leucine, vitamin A, and oxidised fish oil.
- **Cataract:** deficiencies of methionine, tryptophan, zinc, magnesium, copper, selenium, manganese, vitamin A, riboflavin, and choline.
- **Fin erosion:** deficiencies of lysine, tryptophan, zinc, riboflavin, inositol, niacin, and vitamin C.
- **Fatty liver:** deficiencies of choline and essential fatty acids; toxicity of oxidised fish oil.
- **Exophthalmia:** deficiencies of pantothenic acid, niacin, folic acid, vitamins A and E; toxicity of oxidised fish oil.
- **Fin/skin haemorrhage:** deficiencies of riboflavin, pantothenic acid, niacin, thiamine, inositol, vitamin C, vitamin A, and vitamin K; toxicity of oxidised fish oil.

### 7. Research gaps
The author notes that apart from eye cataract research in US salmonids, no systematic attempt has been made to characterise these six conditions individually in fish. The paper calls for further studies under practical farming conditions.
