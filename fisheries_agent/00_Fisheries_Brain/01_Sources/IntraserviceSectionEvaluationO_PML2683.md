# Intra-Service Section 7 Evaluation of Fish Stocking in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1992 memorandum from the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 2, Fishery Resources, Phoenix Ecological Services Office) transmits intra-Service [[Section 7 Consultation]] evaluations under the [[Endangered Species Act]] for ongoing recreational fish stocking in Arizona. Rather than evaluating each stocking separately, the evaluations were organized by drainage: the Gila, Salt, Black, White, Verde and Agua Fria, Little Colorado, and lower Colorado systems. Each evaluation covers listed, proposed and candidate species, known and potential impacts, and the effect determination requested from the reviewing office. Most stocking is non-native trout, largemouth bass and channel catfish, mainly on tribal lands and state waters. The memo asks for formal consultation on two actions (trout effects on [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] and [[Spikedace]]) and concurrence on the rest.

## Background & Description

The memo covers the stocking program run by the [[Arizona Fishery Resources Office]] in Pinetop and San Carlos, with the Parker office covering the lower Colorado. Stocking is carried out each year, generally from March through September in the Gila, Salt, Black and White drainages and November through April in the lower Colorado. Stocking is intended to sustain consistent, high-quality recreational fishing for tribal members and visitors.

Service staff reported that the evaluation format differed from earlier ones. Because of the large number of waters stocked, stockings were assessed by drainage, with individual stockings noted only where specific problems were identified. Each drainage package includes a range map, a stocking-site overlay and a site table in Appendix 1. The memo also states that some stocking had already been discontinued, notably [[Channel Catfish]] stocking on the Colorado River.

Stocked and managed species include:
- Native or introduced salmonids: [[Apache Trout]] (native, East Fork strain), [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]] and [[Arctic Grayling]].
- Warmwater species: [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Listed and proposed species.** The memo's species lists are the core of each evaluation:
- Gila drainage: [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]], Gila chub and roundtail chub are the species that may be affected. Bald eagle, Gila topminnow and desert pupfish are treated as unaffected or not likely to be adversely affected.
- Salt drainage: loach minnow and Gila topminnow may be affected. Roundtail chub is a proposed or candidate species of concern.
- Black drainage: [[Apache Trout]] and loach minnow may be affected. Roundtail chub and [[Arizona Willow]] are proposed or candidate species of concern.
- White drainage: Apache trout and loach minnow may be affected. Roundtail chub is a candidate species of concern.
- Lower Colorado drainage: [[Razorback Sucker]], humpback chub and bonytail chub may be affected. Roundtail chub and flannelmouth sucker are candidates of concern.
- Little Colorado drainage: [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] is the key listed species. Roundtail chub and Arizona willow are also noted.
- Verde and Agua Fria drainages: spikedace, razorback sucker and Gila topminnow may be affected.

**Key threats.** The memo identifies several recurring threats:
- Decline of [[Gila Topminnow]] is attributed largely to [[Mosquitofish]] introduction, which can replace small topminnow populations quickly through predation on young and sometimes adults.
- Introduced trout, especially rainbow trout, have caused the most significant impacts on Apache trout through introgression, because spawning periods overlap.
- Channel catfish move onto swift riffles at night to forage, which could affect loach minnow.
- Largemouth bass prefer quiet, warm, vegetated waters, so their establishment in flowing mainstems is considered less likely.
- [[Striped Bass]] in Lake Mohave may forage on hatchery rainbow trout, creating possible indirect effects on native fishes.

**Management setting.** Much of the stocking takes place on tribal lands, including the San Carlos, Fort Apache, Ft. McDowell, Ft. Mohave, Hualapai, Havasupai, Chemehuevi and Colorado River Indian reservations. Stocking is linked to the [[Apache Trout Recovery Plan]], which requires 30 self-sustaining discrete populations before delisting.

## Key Findings

- **Apache trout recovery framework.** The recovery plan (USFWS 1983) calls for 30 self-sustaining populations. No non-native stocking occurs in streams designated critical to delisting. Only isolated, noncritical impoundments are stocked. The Apache trout was listed as endangered in 1969 and moved to threatened status in 1974. It now occupies fewer than 30 streams and impoundments.
- **Hatchery production targets.** A draft Apache Trout Implementation Plan (Appendix 2) calls for rising Apache trout fingerling production, from roughly 80,000 in 1987 to about 385,000 in 1991 (values read from degraded OCR). Non-native trout stocking is to be replaced with Apache trout as production grows.
- **Channel catfish.** The Arizona Fishery Resources Office discontinued channel catfish introductions into areas with direct access to the Colorado River in 1992. The memo cites evidence that resident catfishes limit reintroduced razorback sucker survival.
- **Rainbow trout and native fishes.** Rainbow trout have been shown in microcosm experiments to eat native fishes, including Little Colorado spinedace (Runck et al. 1992). However, no evidence links them to a significant limit on native fish recovery in larger systems.
- **Formal consultation requests.** The memo asks for formal consultation on:
  - trout stocking effects on the Little Colorado spinedace, where rainbow trout stocking into Blue Ridge and Knoll lakes may conflict with recovery goals because of their proximity to spinedace; and
  - trout effects on spikedace in the Verde system, where stocking into White Horse Lake may conflict with the Pinkinsville spikedace population.
- **Concurrence requests.** The Service requests concurrence on the remaining stockings. Stocking into Ashurst, Long and Kinnickinick lakes does not appear inconsistent with spinedace recovery, and the winter rainbow trout fishery on the Ft. McDowell and Salt River reservations is judged unlikely to seriously threaten roundtail chub.
- **Loach minnow in the White River.** Since 1985, loach minnow have been regularly collected in the East Fork and mainstem White River over about 14 miles. Summer water temperatures often exceed lethal limits for trout, so the memo concludes that trout stocking elsewhere in the White River will not adversely affect the existing population.
- **Largemouth bass and catfish risk.** Largemouth bass stocking is limited to isolated tanks, and no largemouth bass were found in some surveyed reaches. Channel catfish remain the species of greater concern because of escapement potential. Many stock tanks dry completely each year or are uninhabitable to fish, so escapement is considered a rare event.
- **Funding.** The memo includes a cost table for a proposed Apache trout recovery effort on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation, with an additional-funding line item shown for each year. Possible sources include the Region 2 Endangered Species program, a University of Arizona cooperative unit, the Tribe, and the Fisheries Resources program.
