# Biological Assessment on Threatened and Endangered Species for Hoover Dam Powerplant Modification Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This biological assessment evaluates potential impacts of the proposed [[Hoover Dam]] powerplant modification project on five federally listed or candidate species: [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*), [[Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus*), and [[Desert Tortoise]] (*Gopherus agassizii*). The project would add 500 MW of generating capacity through construction of a subsurface powerhouse, increasing maximum releases to 62,000 cubic feet per second during peak generation. The Bureau of Reclamation concluded that consultation under Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act is not required, as the project would have no foreseeable adverse impacts on listed species. The assessment was prepared in March 1991 following coordination with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

## Background & Description

### Project Context

[[Hoover Dam]] was constructed 1931-1935 pursuant to the Boulder Canyon Project Act of December 21, 1928, creating [[Lake Mead]], the nation's largest manmade lake. The existing powerplant has 17 generating units with capacity of approximately 1,340 MW. A routine replacement and maintenance program (uprating) was scheduled to increase capacity to 1,800 MW by the early 1990s through improved technology and materials, with each uprated unit generating an additional 30-50 MW.

### Proposed Modification

The proposed project involves constructing a subsurface powerhouse approximately 50 feet downstream from the present Arizona powerhouse, containing two 250-MW generators. This would increase total plant capacity to 2,300 MW (including the 1,800 MW from uprating). Maximum releases during peak power generation would reach 62,000 cubic feet per second. Public Law 94-156 (December 16, 1975) authorized feasibility investigations for this modification.

### Project Need

The southwestern United States experienced some of the nation's fastest population growth, with Arizona and Nevada ranking first and third respectively in the continental United States. The Western Systems Coordinating Council (43 interconnected western utilities) estimated need for an additional 3,200 MW of peaking capacity in the near future to meet demands of increasing population.

### Species Evaluated

Five species were identified by U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (February 1, 1990) as potentially affected:

- **Endangered**: [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*), [[Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus*), [[Desert Tortoise]] (*Gopherus agassizii*) Mohave population
- **Proposed for listing**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- **Candidate**: Desert Tortoise Sonoran population

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Bald Eagle

**Distribution**: Large predatory and scavenging bird associated with rivers, lakes, and seashores of North America. Most nesting occurs north of Latitude 40° N. In the southwest, primarily winter residents associated with unfrozen aquatic habitats.

**Local Abundance**: National Wildlife Federation's 1990 wintering bald eagle count documented minimum of 14 bald eagles for [[Lake Mead]] area. Annual counts since 1979 ranged from 14-24 eagles. No known roost sites within project area. No breeding records for Lake Mead/Lake Mohave area; no sightings between May and late September. Current breeding population concentrated in central Arizona, with potential for expansion to Lake Mead and [[Lake Mohave]].

**Life Requisites**: Opportunistic feeders; important food items include fish (especially [[Carp]] *Cyprinus carpio* and [[Channel Catfish]] *Ictalurus punctatus*), waterfowl, carrion, and small mammals. Fish are primary prey during breeding season. Foraging on dead and dying fish in evaporating pools correlated with breeding success in Florida. Perch sites usually in trees or cliffs near open water with wide flight clearances. Night roost sites close to food sources and protected from adverse weather. Breeding activities begin November-December; eggs laid January-March, hatching February-April. Nest sites in tops of large trees or cliffs, typically within 200 feet of perennial water bodies. Isolation from human disturbance important; shore anglers and motorboating can disrupt activity, though eagles can become conditioned to land traffic.

### Peregrine Falcon

**Distribution**: Historically common in Arizona; population declined during 1950s with only three active nests known in Arizona in 1975. Few historic records in southern Nevada. Recent increase in sightings and active nest locations in southwest. Ongoing study from Fortification Hill (upstream of Hoover) to approximately 4 miles below Willow Beach identified five active nest sites; nearest approximately 1.5 miles north of proposed project site.

**Life Requisites**: Potential habitat composed of cliffs adjacent to productive hunting habitat associated with wetlands, rivers, and lakes. Eyries typically located within cliffs overlooking preferred habitat. Diet consists mainly of birds including waterfowl, shorebirds, songbirds, and gallinaceous birds. Nesting birds can be very sensitive to human disturbance, generally locating nests far from human populations, though have nested on skyscrapers and bridges in large cities.

### Desert Tortoise

**Distribution**: Extremely long-lived reptile with nonuniform distribution throughout Mohave and Sonoran Deserts. Found mainly in mountainous, rocky desert terrain in southern and central Arizona. Elsewhere most common in flatlands, bajadas, and washes. In southern Nevada occasionally found on slopes or rugged terrain.

**Life Requisites**: Soil composition and texture are key habitat features for cover and egg laying. Burrows constructed in soils ranging from sandy-loam to light gravel-clay. Food items include annual forbs, grasses, cacti, and shrubs; diet highly variable and determined by plant availability. Sensitive to grazing by livestock and off-road vehicle use, which have contributed to decline throughout habitat.

### Bonytail Chub

**Distribution**: Once ranged throughout mainstream [[Colorado River]] and principal tributaries. Still abundant in [[Lake Mead]] after closing of [[Hoover Dam]]; by 1950 considered rare. Presently believed extirpated in Colorado River from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to Hoover. Small populations exist in Upper Colorado River Basin (above Glen Canyon Dam) and [[Lake Mohave]] in lower basin. Some doubt exists about genetic purity of Upper Colorado River population. Lake Mohave population near point of extinction.

**Stocking Attempts**: Initial attempts (1982-83) involved release of bonytail fry from Dexter National Fish Hatchery into small backwater area; follow-up sampling failed to locate any fish. More recent releases with larger sub-adult fish; some recaptured by anglers and researchers.

**Life Requisites**: Morphological characteristics appear adapted for survival in turbid and swift flows of pre-dam Colorado River. Prefer eddy and pool habitats in canyons. Optimum habitat: shifting sand substrate, water depths 3-4 feet, relatively constant moderately swift current. Feeding occurs mainly on surface for terrestrial insects, plant debris, and algae. In Lake Mohave, mostly found between mid-lake and Davis Dam. Frequent angler capture near Katherine's Landing and Davis Dam during summer. Recent netting surveys (May) located bonytail adults using shallow rock points and shoals along Cottonwood Basin (mid-lake).

### Razorback Sucker

**Distribution**: Once abundant throughout 3,500 miles of [[Colorado River]] basin, primarily in mainstream and major tributaries in Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming, and Mexican states of Baja California Norte and Sonora. Many accounts of abundance during early settlement of lower basin; significant commercial fishery existed in southern Arizona in early 1900s. Jordan (1891) reported razorback suckers very abundant at Green River, Utah, in 1889. Residents near Clifton, Colorado observed several thousand during spring runoff in 1930s and early 1940s.

**Current Status**: Distribution reduced to about 750 miles in upper basin and 200 miles of lower Colorado River now impounded by Hoover, Davis, and Parker Dams. Estimated 65,000 adult razorback suckers in [[Lake Mohave]]; 758-1,138 estimated in upper Green River. Observations elsewhere spotty and inconsistent, generally viewed as incidental captures. No significant recruitment documented in recent years.

**Life Requisites**: Information on behavior and habitat needs quite limited. Adults apparently migrate considerable distances to specific areas to spawn. Spawning occurs in lower basin late January through April. Water temperatures during spawning ranged 52.7°-64.4°F. Spawning usually accomplished with several males accompanying single female over gravel bars swept free of silt by currents. In Lake Mohave and Senator Wash Reservoir, spawning takes place on gravel bars swept clean by wave action. Eggs apparently settle onto gravel and into interstices swept clean by spawning activity. Larvae appear to remain in gravel until swim-up.

**Recruitment Failure**: Multiple investigations collected viable fertilized eggs and larvae in spawning areas, but few collected larvae larger than 14 mm in wild, indicating little or no successful recruitment. Marsh and Langhorst (1988) recovered larvae up to 20 mm total length in isolated backwater in Lake Mohave where predators had been eradicated; fish disappeared within month following reinvasion by predators. Most investigations reported concentrations of [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) in razorback sucker spawning areas. Larvae and larger razorback suckers found in stomachs of predatory fishes including green sunfish, warmouth (*Lepomis gulosus*), channel catfish, flathead catfish (*Pylodictis olivaris*), and threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*).

**Habitat Needs of Juveniles**: Largely unknown because rarely encountered by researchers, particularly in native riverine habitats. Marsh and Langhorst (1988) observed larval razorback suckers in Lake Mohave remained near shore after hatching but either disappeared or migrated to depths exceeding 49 feet within few weeks.

**Food Habits**: Larvae from reservoirs selected *Bosmina* spp. (Cladocera) as food items and avoided Copepoda; larvae from backwaters avoided Rotifera. Information not available on food habits from natural riverine habitats. Marsh (1987) examined stomachs of 34 adult specimens from Lake Mohave; food selection included benthic fauna and flora and inorganic materials from bottom. Jonez and Sumner (1954) reported algae as most common food item from Lake Mead, followed by plankton, insects, and decaying organic matter. Chironomids most prominent food item in Lake Mohave specimens. Studies confirm morphology indicates bottom feeding plankton consumer.

**Reintroduction Efforts**: FWS and Arizona Game and Fish Department undertook 10-year cooperative effort to reintroduce young razorback suckers into historic habitats. Approximately 9.5 million small fish introduced in Verde, Salt, and Colorado Rivers. Despite efforts, very few believed to have reached adulthood. Monitoring showed predation by ictalurid catfishes primary deterrent to reestablishment of hatchery-reared suckers.

**Yuma Cove Experiment (1985-1986)**: Biologists placed sexually active razorback suckers in naturally isolated portion of Yuma Cove to determine if habitat conditions existed for successful spawning and production of viable young in absence of exotic fishes. Prior to introduction, backwater was renovated, removing all living fish. Experiment proved successful: adult suckers spawned and swim-up fry grew to 22 mm (more than 50% greater than maximum sizes achieved in unprotected lake). Unfortunately, rising reservoir levels in May 1986 reclaimed backwater and young razorback suckers disappeared within month following incursion of non-native fishes. This demonstrated existing habitat conditions in Lake Mohave support razorback sucker spawning and growth and that primary limiting factor is predation.

## Key Findings

### Impact Determinations

**Bald Eagle**: Analysis concluded project would have no foreseeable impact. Implementation would not result in direct mortality or displacement; possible perch sites, nest sites, or foraging areas would neither be created nor destroyed. Only conceivable impact to prey would be to fish in first 12 miles below Hoover. Impacts to fish in river, especially carp and catfish, expected to be minor to nonexistent. Therefore, no impacts expected to wintering bald eagles.

**Peregrine Falcon**: Implementation not expected to adversely affect availability of nest or perch sites, nor reduce food source. Increased human activity during construction could disturb individual peregrine falcons using specific site. Potential disturbance not thought significant due to small area involved and low density of peregrine falcons.

**Desert Tortoise**: No change in present status expected from implementing proposed action. Habitat immediately adjacent to river's edge from Hoover to just downstream of Willow Beach very marginal at best. Terrain mostly consists of steep canyon walls, especially near Hoover where greatest degree of water fluctuation (approximately 16 feet) will occur. Potential that 5 surface-acres of desert wash associated habitat will be required as materials borrow site. Specific site not yet identified. Once identified, environmental clearances including desert tortoise surveys will be required. These clearances not expected to result in major problems but may require additional funding for surveys and report preparation.

**Bonytail Chub**: Fish surveys suggest bonytails rare if not absent in cold water (54°F) areas of [[Black Canyon]]. If they existed, cold water coupled with unsuitable habitat would make reproduction and viable population impossible. Changes from implementing proposed project would not adversely impact further existence of bonytail chub.

**Razorback Sucker**: Although razorback suckers spawn in Lake Mohave producing swim-up fry, no documented natural recruitment to adult population has occurred. Primary cause for lack of young razorback suckers throughout basin for last 30 years attributed to predation from introduced species (carp, green sunfish, channel catfish, largemouth bass). Considerable evidence that remaining razorback population composed primarily of old individuals slowly dying off.

Natural recruitment primarily hampered by predation. Increased reservoir elevation fluctuations may possibly reduce number of razorback suckers being hatched; however, since no evidence of successful recruitment to adult population exists, only impact would be to predators which feed on razorback sucker larvae. Project could only be viewed as detrimental to sucker recruitment if Lake Mohave's fishery could be reverted to all native species and exotic predation factor eliminated. Removal of reservoir's current sportfishery would not be desirable nor technically feasible.

**Senator Wash Reservoir Comparison**: Similar conditions reported for Senator Wash Reservoir, California. Adult razorback suckers spawn in wave wash zone and produce larvae. Senator Wash Reservoir experiences daily elevation fluctuations of nearly 8 feet, fluctuations significantly greater than conditions expected from project.

### Recovery Initiatives

Bureau of Reclamation recently took initiative to organize effort to examine if backwater facilities could be established for propagation and rearing of razorback suckers and other native fish in Lake Mohave. This action appears most feasible way of securing and maintaining continued existence of these species in lower Colorado River. Currently, Reclamation working with National Park Service, Arizona Game and Fish Department, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and Nevada Department of Wildlife to initiate this recovery concept.

### Consultation Conclusion

Bureau of Reclamation evaluated potential impacts of proposed project to species listed as potentially affected. Consultation as outlined in Section 7 of Endangered Species Act of 1973, as amended, determined not required. Assessment requested letter of concurrence or non-concurrence from U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.
