# Recovery Goals for Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) – Draft 1

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft supplement to the [[Razorback Sucker Recovery Plan]], prepared by SWCA, Inc. for the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]] and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (April 1, 2000), develops objective, measurable criteria for downlisting and delisting the endangered razorback sucker across its Colorado River Basin range. The species once occupied about 5,600 km of the basin, but now persists on roughly 1,200 km of riverine habitat plus remnant reservoir populations. Its decline is attributed mainly to lack of recruitment caused by high mortality of young fish, driven by dam construction, altered flows, cold releases, habitat fragmentation and [[Nonnative Fishes]].

The document sets site-specific management actions for each subbasin and proposes two sets of population thresholds: 4,500 adults (genetic effective-size basis) for downlisting and 5,800 adults (minimum viable population) for delisting, each sustained over defined monitoring periods (5 years and 15 years, respectively).

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid, the only species in the genus *Xyrauchen*. Adults are typically 400–700 mm total length and weigh less than 3 kg, with a maximum of about 1 m and 5–6 kg. A bony dorsal keel behind the head gave rise to the older name "humpback sucker." It was listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] on October 23, 1991; critical habitat was designated March 21, 1994, and the recovery plan was approved December 23, 1998.

The species is one of four big-river fishes listed under the ESA, alongside [[Humpback Chub]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Bonytail]]. It belongs to a fish assemblage of 35 species with 74% endemism. Two programs coordinate recovery: the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Program]] (initiated January 22, 1988) and the [[San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program]] (established 1991). The [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] was initiated following a biological opinion of April 30, 1997.

The document follows a defined process: assimilating life-history data and population estimates, evaluating demographic and genetic viability, identifying threats under the five ESA listing factors, then setting management actions and criteria. Recovery is considered for the full range; the distinct population segment concept was not applied because of insufficient distinctiveness among populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population status by subbasin (from Table 1 and Appendix A):**
- **Lake Mohave:** largest remaining population in the basin. The wild stock fell from about 60,000 adults in 1991 to about 9,000 in 2000 (estimates from Pacey and Marsh, cited with T. Burke, pers. comm.). Repatriated juveniles numbered an estimated 3,104 (about 25% of adults).
- **Lake Mead:** about 400 adults estimated at Vegas and Echo bays; very limited recruitment.
- **Middle Green River:** the only population with some evidence of recruitment. Estimates were 948 adults (Lanigan and Tyus 1989) and 524 adults (Modde et al. 1996; 95% CI 351–696).
- **Upper Colorado River:** essentially extirpated from the Gunnison River; only 12 individuals captured in Grand Valley 1984–1990; no young fish since the mid-1960s.
- **San Juan River:** very low numbers; no wild fish found during 1991–1997 monitoring; hatchery-reared fish have reproduced.
- **Gila River and Verde/Salt rivers:** repatriated fish showed little or no long-term survival, mainly from predation.

**Life history and habitat (Appendix A):** Adults use low-velocity currents, backwaters and flooded bottomlands. Spawning occurs in spring over cobble or gravel at about 6–19°C, with some aggregations in reservoirs. Fecundity averaged 46,740 eggs/fish (McAda and Wydoski 1980). Reported maximum age is 24–44 years (McCarthy and Minckley 1987). Adult annual mortality is about 29% (Modde et al. 1996). Diet varies by life stage, from zooplankton and chironomids in larvae to benthic invertebrates in riverine adults and planktonic filter-feeding in Lake Mohave.

**Threat-related context:** Peak spring discharge in the upper Colorado River declined by 29–38% since 1950 (Van Steeter and Pitlick 1998). Coldwater releases hold temperatures at about 4–10°C, and selenium concentrations in Ashley Creek water ranged from 59 to 78 µg/L. Hybridization with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] has occurred since at least 1889, with Lake Mohave showing under 5% introgression.

## Key Findings

**Threats (five ESA listing factors):**
1. **Habitat modification:** streamflow depletion, altered flow regimes, coldwater releases and fragmentation by at least 15 major dams in the lower basin and numerous diversions. Flow recommendations packages (5 in total) call for high spring flows to restore flooded nursery bottomlands.
2. **Overutilization:** not currently a threat; the species has no commercial or recreational value and is protected by state law.
3. **Disease or predation:** no evidence that disease is a significant factor. Predation by at least 67 introduced nonnative fish species is the primary cause of recruitment failure.
4. **Inadequate regulatory mechanisms:** existing programs are cooperative rather than regulatory. No stocking agreements exist among lower basin states.
5. **Other factors:** selenium is hypothesized to impair reproduction but has not been conclusively demonstrated; there is little evidence that other contaminants are significant.

**Viability thresholds:**
- A minimum effective population size (Ne) of 1,000 was adopted (sex ratio 3:1, Ne/N ratio 0.30), giving a genetic minimum of about 4,433 adults, rounded to 4,500 for downlisting.
- Adding 30% for annual adult mortality gives a minimum viable population of 5,763, rounded to 5,800 for delisting.

**Downlisting criteria (endangered to threatened):**
- Lake Mohave: maintain at least 50,000 adults for 5 years as a wild genetic refugium.
- Green River, Upper Colorado, Gila/Lower Colorado (two populations each): at least 4,500 adults for 5 years, with the lower 95% CI above 4,500, a non-declining trend, and age-3 fish between 20% and 30% of adults.
- San Juan: continue activities toward a self-sustaining population.

**Delisting criteria:**
- Lake Mohave: maintain at least 50,000 adults for 15 years.
- Green River, Upper Colorado, Gila/Lower Colorado: at least 5,800 adults for 15 years, using the same CI, trend and age-structure tests.
- San Juan: maintain a wild refugium of at least 4,500 adults for 5 years after delisting.

**Monitoring and statistics:**
- A 5-year monitoring period applies to downlisting and a 15-year period to delisting. Fifteen years approximates two generations (about 7 years).
- At least three annual estimates are required for downlisting and at least seven for delisting. Ages 0, 3 and 4+ are monitored.
- Closed-population mark-recapture estimators are proposed, with lower 95% CI at or below 30% of the estimate (about 15% CV) and no more than four sampling events.
- Trend tests: linear regression slope significantly below zero (p<0.10), and a criterion failure if each annual estimate is below the previous one.

**Site-specific actions highlighted:** regulate Lake Mohave elevation; secure and protect flows for the Yampa, White, Duchesne, Price and Gunnison rivers; maintain fish passage at Grand Valley Diversion, Price-Stubb Dam, Government Highline Dam and Redlands Diversion; control nonnative fish; investigate selenium effects; and evaluate Aspinall Unit modifications to allow warmed releases.
