# Limnological Survey of Butler and McAllister Lakes, Imperial National Wildlife Refuge

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 2007 report from the [[Lower Colorado River Multi-Species Conservation Program]] presents a one-year limnological survey (December 2005 to December 2006) of two isolated floodplain backwaters, [[Butler Lake]] and [[McAllister Lake]], on the [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]] in Arizona. The survey sought the physical, chemical and biological constraints that limit these lakes as habitat for native fish, primarily [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Both lakes were found to be unsuitable in their current state. Butler Lake is extremely hyper-eutrophic, with a dense cyanobacterial bloom, severe oxygen swings, high mercury and arsenic, and anatoxin-a at 14.3 ng/L. McAllister Lake is constrained mainly by high salinity, and its only fish is [[Mosquitofish]]. The report recommends sediment removal, re-established open-water connection to the river with fish-exclusion screens, and managed salinity control.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Identify environmental constraints on native fish survival and recommend feasible remedial actions.
- **Historical context:** Lower Colorado River backwaters formed through scouring floods and once served as rearing habitat for juvenile native fish. Flow regulation by large dams and channelization removed much of this habitat.
- **Native fish of concern:** Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*), Bonytail Chub, and Flannelmouth Sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*) use backwaters as nursery habitat.
- **Non-native fish threat:** Non-native species prey on, compete with, or damage native fish and spawning beds (Minckley 1979).
- **Butler Lake:** 43 surface acres (17.2 ha), at river mile 61.5, about 160 river miles from Lake Havasu/Colorado River mouth reference point as stated in the source. Average depth 0.9 m, most of the lake between 0.9 and 1.2 m. Total volume 175 acre-feet; shoreline 2,906 m-equivalent with development index 1.90. No surface connection to the river; relies on groundwater.
- **McAllister Lake:** 32.3 total backwater acres (26.5 open water, 5.8 marsh), mean depth 4.5 feet, shoreline 8,077 feet, development index 1.924. Roughly 1,200 feet east of the river (about river mile 61), seepage-driven, with no known surface connection.
- **Climate:** Arid and hot; open-water evaporation about 2.2 m per year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Butler Lake**
- Thermal stratification appeared only in spring 2006; the lake is too shallow to hold stratification for long, and wind mixing helps keep temperature uniform.
- Spring 2006 algal biomass was extraordinary, producing surface supersaturation (over 20 mg/L and over 200% saturation) and anoxia below the surface.
- Summer dissolved oxygen was below 2.0 mg/L at all depths in the sampled period, often under 0.5 mg/L near the bottom.
- BOD and COD were very high, indicating far more oxygen sinks (sediments, decomposing algae) than sources.
- Cattail (*Typha domingensis*) encroachment and hummock formation are in-filling the lake; it is more marsh-like than lacustrine.
- Sediment and water contain high arsenic and mercury, and hydrogen sulfide odor was strong, indicating sulfate-reducing bacteria that favor methylmercury formation.
- Zooplankton was sparse (large cladocerans and calanoid copepods only). Benthic invertebrates included chironomid midges (most abundant), damselflies, dragonflies, true bugs, and glass shrimp (*Palaemonetes*).

**McAllister Lake**
- No thermal stratification; temperatures were uniform year-round.
- Dissolved oxygen generally higher than Butler but anoxic at times, mostly at night, and probably not severe enough to prevent survival of native fish, though it could produce a seasonal fish kill.
- Salinity is likely the major constraint. Target specific conductivity of 4,000 to 6,500 µS/cm (from Walker et al. 2007) was exceeded during summer and winter 2006 after a December 2005 draw-down.
- Three Pond 1® wind-powered aerator/mixers were installed on July 14, 2004 (as printed).
- Site ML4, the "Western Lobe," is more eutrophic, saline, and marsh-like, with dense macrophytes and lower dissolved oxygen (mean about 1.34 mg/L versus 6.78 mg/L for the main body, as printed).
- The only fish found was [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).

## Key Findings

**Butler Lake (current status: not viable for native fish)**
- Extreme hyper-eutrophication; nutrients are far above levels needed to limit algal growth, so the lake is not nutrient-limited. Total P 0.033 mg/L comparison was taken from a Tucson reference lake.
- Anatoxin-a found at 14.3 ng/L (July 2006), a fast-acting neurotoxin from *Anabaena* and *Anabaenopsis*, considered a threat to wildlife.
- Total mercury ranged from non-detectable (12/07/05) to 27 ng/L (07/26/06), a very high level well above the US EPA acute (1.4 µg/L as stated) and chronic (0.77 µg/L as stated) criteria.
- Arsenic 0.016 to 0.020 mg/L in water, above the 10 µg/L drinking-water criterion cited; bioaccumulation concerns remain without speciation data.
- Selenium low (0.33 to 0.35 µg/L), not a constraint.
- Dissolved iron and manganese rose during anoxia, consistent with reducing conditions releasing metals from sediment.

**McAllister Lake (current status: salinity-constrained)**
- Specific conductivity was significantly reduced after the draw-down but rebounded within months.
- Organic carbon and sediment nutrients were significantly lower than in Butler, which may explain part of the productivity difference.
- Total N and total P were significantly lower than in Butler (one-way ANOVA, P < 0.0001 for total P and total N).

**Recommendations**
- Butler: substantial sediment removal and lake deepening, steepened margins to limit macrophyte encroachment, and re-establishment of an open-water connection to the Lower Colorado River using cylindrical wedge-wire screens to exclude non-native fish (screens evaluated at Beal Lake, Nonnandeau Associates 2006).
- McAllister: managed salinity control through automated or repeated draw-down and recharge, with long-term monitoring of conductivity, dissolved oxygen, and trophic state.
- Both: a long-term adaptive management framework and habitat creation guided by sound ecological principles, since historic backwater structure and function are poorly known.
