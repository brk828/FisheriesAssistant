## Executive Summary

This 1994 working draft position paper, produced by the Sport Fishing and Boating Partnership Council's Special Task Force, addresses the tension between conserving [[Federally Threatened Endangered And Proposed Species]] (Federal T/E/P species) and sustaining/enhancing recreational fisheries programs in the United States. Rather than presenting primary field data on a single species or family, the document synthesizes national-scale conservation statistics on imperiled fish, crayfish, and freshwater mussel taxa, and proposes policy actions under existing federal legal frameworks (the [[Endangered Species Act]], [[Fish and Wildlife Act Of 1956]], [[Federal Aid In Sport Fish Restoration Act]]) to reconcile habitat conservation with continued recreational angling opportunities. The paper emphasizes an ecosystem-based management approach, the role of nonindigenous species in altered habitats, and the need for federal-state-tribal-private partnerships.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Fishery resources and aquatic ecosystems are characterized as an "integral component" of the national heritage, with fish identified as the "main biological component" without which aquatic ecosystems are not functional.
- **Habitat degradation and loss** are identified as the primary drivers of fish population declines across North America.
- Compromised habitat integrity has historically led to management shifts favoring different species assemblages, sometimes forcing tradeoffs between **indigenous** and **nonindigenous fish species**.
- [[Nonindigenous Fish Species]] are now naturalized in most U.S. aquatic ecosystems and provide social/economic benefits, but their introduction has been linked to declines of indigenous species due to poorly understood ecological risks.
- The paper distinguishes management priorities by habitat condition:
  - In **significantly altered habitats**: nonindigenous species remain an important tool for sustaining recreational fishing.
  - In **relatively unaltered habitats**: management emphasis should prioritize conservation, use, and recovery of **indigenous fish species**.
- Recognizes that aquatic ecosystems have finite carrying capacity for fish production, dependent on overall ecosystem quality — reinforcing a call for **multi-purpose, ecosystem-based fisheries management**.
- Introduction of new species or hybrids into a drainage basin requires ecological justification and evaluation of impacts on [[Federal T/E/P Species]], guided by the American Fisheries Society's Position Statement on the Introduction of Aquatic Species.

## Key Findings

- **Economic scale of recreational fisheries (1991):** Over 50 million anglers spent $24 billion on tackle, equipment, food, lodging, and related expenditures; this generated over $2.1 billion in federal income tax revenue and supported 1.3 million jobs.
- **Species imperilment trend:** In 1989, 364 species and subspecies of fish in North America were identified as needing special management consideration (Williams et al., *Fisheries* 14(6): 2–20) — an increase of 139 taxa compared to just 10 years prior.
- **Listing status (as of August 1994):** 103 fish species and 56 clam/mussel species were listed as threatened or endangered under 50 CFR 17.11 & 17.12.
- **Imperilment rates by taxon** (Sport Fishing and Boating Partnership Council, 1994):
  - ~20% of North American fishes ranked as extinct or imperiled.
  - ~36% of crayfishes ranked as extinct or imperiled.
  - ~55% of freshwater mussels ranked as extinct or imperiled — the highest imperilment rate of the groups assessed.
- **Primary cause of decline:** Habitat loss and degradation, generally recognized as the leading cause of fish population declines nationally.
- **Policy framework:** The paper references multiple federal statutes relevant to fisheries and species conservation, including the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), the [[Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act]], the [[Federal Water Project Recreation Act]], the [[Reclamation Act]], the [[Dingell-Johnson Act]] (Federal Aid in Sport Fish Restoration Act of 1950), and the [[Fish and Wildlife Act Of 1956]].
- **Action framework proposed** across six domains: (I) pertinent laws/federal policy, (II) partnerships, (III) ecology and recreational fisheries, (IV) public outreach/education, (V) funding, and (VI) mitigation — each with specific action items assigning responsibility to [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], [[National Marine Fisheries Service]], states, tribes, and private/recreational fishing organizations.
- **Mitigation principle:** When recreational fishing opportunities are curtailed to conserve Federal T/E/P species, compatible alternative fishing opportunities should be developed to offset losses, supporting calls for a national recreational fishing mitigation policy.
