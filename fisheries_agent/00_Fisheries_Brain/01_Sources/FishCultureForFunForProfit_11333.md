# Fish Culture for Fun and Profit in Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Department of Game and Fish information pamphlet, written by W.R. Seaman, Superintendent of Fish Culture, gives practical guidance for private landowners and small commercial operators who want to raise fish. Its central premise is that water (its temperature, quantity, and quality) is the limiting factor for any fish culture effort. The pamphlet covers water sources, pond and raceway construction, [[Trout Hatchery Operations]], feeding, stocking, warm water pond management, and chemical control of weeds and unwanted fish. It presents fish as a crop that must be harvested every year, and it directs readers to the state for water testing, licensing, and further references.

## Background & Description

The pamphlet was issued as Information Pamphlet Number Six by the Education Division of the Colorado Department of Game and Fish. Its author notes growing interest among Colorado landowners in raising fish for sport, food, or income, and it was written to answer the department's frequent requests for information.

Two principles frame the text:

- **Water is the limiting factor.** The amount and suitability of water determine which species and culture methods are practical.
- **Fish are a crop.** Like grain or livestock, they must be harvested each year. The author notes that farmers rarely fail to harvest wheat or herd increase, but failing to harvest fish crops is common.

The guidance is aimed at both commercial operations and family farm ponds. The author advises that even small operations study the fundamentals first.

The text describes two broad approaches:

- **[[Trout Culture]]** relies on cool, well-oxygenated, constant-flow water, mainly [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Cutthroat Trout]] (the species named in the spawning section).
- **[[Pond Fish Culture]]** relies on natural food production in still or slow-changing water, mainly warm water species such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill Sunfish]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water sources**

- **[[Spring Sources]]** are the ideal supply. Their flows are relatively constant, temperatures are more stable than in streams, and murky runoff is avoided. Most Colorado springs flow at one cubic foot per second or less, and flows above three cubic feet per second are rare.
- **[[Constant-Flow Streams]]** can be diverted into ponds or raceways. Their temperatures swing with weather and season, which limits commercial use but suits a private fishing pond.
- **[[Intermittent Streams]]**, especially on the plains, can be dammed to form ponds for warm water species. Adequate spillway design is needed for flood protection.
- **Undesirable waters** may be too acidic, too alkaline, polluted, or heavily silted. Mountain waters in highly mineralized drainages or peat bogs can be too acidic, and waters below steel mills, sugar factories, and cities may be chemically altered or depleted of oxygen by organic wastes.

**Temperature and chemistry thresholds**

| Parameter | Value given in text |
|---|---|
| Ideal trout temperature | 50–60 °F |
| Upper limit for trout (sustained) | 70–75 °F |
| Water colder than 50 °F | Permissible, but curtails growth |
| Stream temperatures below 40 °F or above 65 °F | Virtually halt trout growth |
| Lowest pH for appreciable growth | 6 (7 is neutral) |
| Best pH range | Slightly alkaline, up to pH 8.5 |

Dissolved oxygen tests require an on-site visit by a field fishery technician. Water samples for alkalinity and acidity are analyzed free of charge by the [[Colorado Game And Fish Department]].

**Food chain**

The pamphlet describes pond productivity as a [[Food Chain]] that starts with mineral salts and [[Pond Fertilization]]: algae and aquatic plants, then protozoa, water fleas and other small crustaceans, and insects, then young game and forage fish, then larger predators. Bacteria return dead fish to inorganic salts, completing the cycle. The author describes a pond as "a living world in miniature." Ponds should operate as self-sustaining systems, similar to a balanced aquarium, rather than through continuous water flow.

## Key Findings

**Pond and raceway construction**

- Most ponds are built as [[Earth-Fill Dams]]. Rock rip-rap is needed on the water side of the dam, and a valved bottom-tube outlet that drains the lake is "quite valuable" for management.
- Where ice covers the water in winter, pond depth at the dam should be at least 8–10 feet.
- Soils must have enough clay to hold water. The soils department of Colorado A&M College can test compaction and water retention.
- A dam higher than 10 feet, or an impoundment larger than 20 acres, requires prior approval by the State Engineer. Landowners should file for a non-consumptive water right on diverted stream water.
- [[Raceway Ponds]] suit intensive commercial culture. They are typically 6–10 feet wide, with dam boards and screens or checks every 50 or 100 feet. Grate screens with 1/2-inch or 3/4-inch bar spacing need less upkeep than mesh screens, which clog.
- Large ponds need a catch basin at the outlet tube and a bottom graded toward the outlet so fish do not strand in pockets.

**Trout hatchery operations**

- Hatching is traditionally done in troughs about 12 feet long, 1 foot wide, and 8 inches deep, with even water delivery.
- Commercial operators also need dry feed storage, freezer space for meats, and a food preparation area.

**Trout propagation**

- Trout spawn in the fall (brook and brown) or spring (rainbow and cutthroat). Female ripeness is judged by body softness and eggs flowing under slight pressure.
- Stripping is done with a gloved hand and gentle pressure toward the vent. One male can fertilize the eggs of two or three females, and a ripe male yields roughly 1/4 to 1 teaspoonful of milt (the OCR text is ambiguous here).
- Eggs are washed of milt, hardened in water for 2–3 hours, and kept out of sunlight. They become tender after about 36 hours.
- Dead, white, opaque eggs should be removed two or three times a week because fungus spreads from them to healthy eggs.
- Eggs are "eyed" when the eyes are visible through the shell. At this stage they can be moved or shipped.
- Fry absorb the yolk sac before feeding, and finely ground beef liver is the best early food.
- Taking eggs from wild fish requires a class A lake license or other permit.

**Feeding**

- Small fry are fed 6–8 times a day with fine particles. Commercial dry feed alone lacks an "unknown" requirement found in raw meat, so it should be supplemented about weekly with ground raw meat.
- Meats are trimmed of fat and connective tissue. Common sources are condemned packing-house by-products such as spleens, lungs, and livers.
- Beef spleen mixed with 14 per cent salt keeps a rubbery texture that limits nutrient leaching.
- A rule of thumb is a daily ration of about 5 per cent of body weight. Fish that do not clean up food within 10–15 minutes are overfed.
- Small pond owners are advised to use commercial pellets rather than install meat-processing equipment.

**Grading and growth**

- Grading at frequent intervals is critical. Without it, some trout reach 2–3 times the size of same-age siblings. The larger fish monopolize food, and size differences can cause cannibalism.
- Sorting boxes with bar-grate bottoms let small fish pass through.
- In raceways, water changes matter more than depth. Depths of 10 inches to 1 foot are adequate for small trout.

**Stocking and trout ponds**

- Trout prices quoted in the text range from $25.00 per thousand for fry to $1.50 per pound for large fish. The state may not sell fish or eggs to private parties.
- In a productive, not overstocked pond, fingerlings reach 8–9 inches in about a year on natural foods. A stocking rate of about 500 or fewer 2–3 inch trout per surface acre is considered adequate on natural food alone.
- Understocking is preferred to overstocking.
- Trout mature by their third year. If they cannot spawn, females reabsorb their eggs and decline, so these fish should be removed by angling or netting.
- After 6–8 years, drain the pond and restock, retaining young, vigorous fish.
- Put-and-take trout ponds, where fish cannot spawn, are the easiest private fisheries to manage.

**Warm water pond management**

- Ponds from 1/4 acre to 60 acres can be managed, but the best size is 1 to 3 acres.
- Ponds should be fenced against livestock. They should be at least 8 feet deep, with a steep slope to about 3 feet of depth to discourage cattails.
- A stable water level is important for spawning and food production.
- A [[Largemouth Bass]] to [[Bluegill Sunfish]] stocking of roughly 1:8 to 1:10 is suggested, or about 30–50 bass and 400 bluegills per acre. This ratio only works if harvest removes fish to keep it in balance.
- [[Crappie]] do poorly in small ponds and are best limited to ponds over 5 acres with deep areas. Catfish and perch should be avoided unless intensive harvest is maintained.
- Bluegill and green sunfish are the only sunfish that reach a useful size in Colorado. Pumpkinseed are generally too small.
- Stocking many species, a "garbage can" pond, leads to management problems.
- Conventional angling rarely removes more than 50 per cent of fish. Frequent fishing and keeping the catch are needed. Seines, traps, and gill nets correct imbalances faster.

**Fertilization and weed control**

- Fertilizer is often overused. Sheep manure is the best organic fertilizer. Ammonium sulfate and superphosphate are the most common inorganic options, at about 100 pounds of commercial fertilizer per acre of water.
- Mechanical removal works in small ponds but becomes impractical in larger ones.
- [[Copper Sulfate]] controls scum algae. The text gives limits of 1 part per million in hard water and 0.3 part per million in soft water for trout, and a maximum of 2 parts per million for warm water fish. It also gives a prescribed rate of no more than 2 pounds per million pounds of water.
- [[Sodium Arsenite]] controls rooted aquatic plants at 4 parts per million or less. It is caustic, so protective clothing is needed. The best time to apply it is spring or early summer. Livestock must be kept away from sprayed vegetation and treated water for several days.

**Fish eradication**

- Complete removal may be needed when rough fish (carp, suckers) or overabundant sunfish and catfish prevent growth.
- [[Derris]] is the faster-acting and safer option, at 1 part per million (2.7 pounds per acre-foot). Killed fish are edible, treated water is safe for warm-blooded animals to drink, and restocking is usually safe within a month.
- [[Toxaphene]] is slower and cheaper, at 1/10 part per million. It is practical only in ponds with little or no flow, and its residual toxicity can last months. Rubber gloves and a respirator are advised.
- Poisons may not be used in waters containing fish without clearance. Legal use of poisons is prohibited by law except with clearance from the department, granted by a letter of authority with time limits.
- A [[Class A Lake License]] allows harvest without regard to seasons, bag limits, or angling method restrictions, if the impoundment meets the conditions.
