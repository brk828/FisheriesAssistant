# Effects of ambient temperatures on development, oxygen consumption, and hatching success of the razorback sucker

## Executive Summary
This study (Bozek, Paulson & Wilde, submitted to *Environmental Biology of Fishes*, 1988) examines how ambient water temperatures in [[Lake Mohave]] affect the reproductive success of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). Combining field collections of larvae (1982–1983) with controlled laboratory incubation experiments (8–20°C), the authors found that razorback suckers spawn and hatch successfully across a broad thermal range (10–22°C in the field; 10–20°C in the lab), with no viable hatching at 8°C. Development rate and oxygen consumption scaled positively with temperature. The findings suggest that although razorback suckers consistently spawn at temperatures below the previously reported laboratory optimum (20°C), this pattern may reflect an evolved reproductive strategy tied to natural, cooler riverine conditions rather than a strictly maladaptive response — a conclusion the editorial correspondence notes was novel and merited further emphasis by the authors.

## Background & Description
- **Species**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), family [[Catostomidae]], a large-bodied endangered catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] basin.
- **Context**: Cold hypolimnetic discharges from dams (e.g., [[Hoover Dam]]) have altered the historic thermal regime of the Colorado River system, raising winter temperatures and lowering summer temperatures relative to pre-dam conditions (Paulson et al. 1980a).
- **Hypothesis under test**: Reduced/altered incubation temperatures downstream of dams may cause direct egg mortality or prolong embryonic/larval exposure to other mortality sources (water level fluctuation, predation, wave action), contributing to the species' documented **recruitment failure** despite adult spawning activity.
- **Study site**: [[Lake Mohave]], a reservoir on the lower [[Colorado River]] (Arizona/Nevada), hosting the largest known population of razorback suckers. Sub-sites include Yuma Cove, Arizona Bay, Six-Mile Area, North Six-Mile Area, and Tequila Cove.
- **Prior work referenced**: Marsh (1985) reported an optimal incubation temperature of 20°C for razorback sucker eggs (using hormonally-induced, formaldehyde-treated eggs), with reduced hatching at 15°C and total mortality at 5–10°C — findings this study directly re-examines and largely contradicts using more naturalistic experimental protocols.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Spawning temperature range in situ**: 10–22°C (up to >25°C reported seasonally), spanning a ~7-month spawning season in Lake Mohave (Bozek et al. 1984).
- **Thermal stability of riverine habitat**: The riverine portion of Lake Mohave below Hoover Dam remains relatively constant at **11–13°C year-round**, suggesting minimal risk of temperature-driven reproductive failure in that specific reach based on this study's data.
- **Larval collection methods**: Nighttime hand-netting using dive lights in spawning areas; larvae staged per [[Snyder 1981]] and [[Fuiman 1979]] criteria, cross-validated against laboratory-reared razorback sucker larvae.
- **Environmental disturbance event**: Severe wind and wave action in the Six-Mile-Coves area (March 1983) apparently destroyed redds and terminated spawning activity at that site for the remainder of the season, while spawning continued in more protected areas (e.g., Tequila Cove) — highlighting **wave action/storm disturbance** as an additional, temperature-independent mortality factor for egg/larval survival.
- **Diel and spatial temperature variation**: Diel temperature swings of up to 2°C were recorded at collection sites; larvae were most abundant in shallow water (~1.1 m depth) and declined in abundance with increasing depth over spawning areas.

## Key Findings

### Field Observations (Lake Mohave, 1982–1983)
- Larvae were collected at water temperatures ranging from **9.5 to 15.0°C**, confirming successful natural development within this range.
- Highest larval abundance recorded: **10 larvae/minute (739 total)** in the Six-Mile-Coves area, February 1983, at 1.1 m depth (day temp 12.0°C, night 10.5°C).
- Mean larval total lengths ranged from **10.4 ± 0.5 to 11.1 ± 0.5 mm**; maximum length recorded was 12.6 mm (early mesolarval stage). No larvae collected exceeded early mesolarval developmental stages.
- Larvae collected in January 1982 were likely spawned in **late November/early December**, coinciding with the coldest seasonal water temperatures — indicating spawning (and successful incubation) occurs even during the coldest part of the year.

### Laboratory Hatching Success (Table 2)
| Incubation Temp (°C) | % Viable Hatch (range across experiments) |
|---|---|
| 8 | **0.0%** (no viable hatch) |
| 10 | 22–57% (high variability) |
| 12 | 36.0 ± 10.8% |
| 15 | 32–65% |
| 20 | 34–65% |

- Hatching success was statistically similar across **12–20°C**.
- Reduced (but non-zero) hatching occurred at **10°C**, with high inter-experiment variability, including one anomalously high result (57%).
- **Complete mortality occurred at 8°C**, indicating this temperature is likely near or below the threshold for embryonic development.
- Greatest egg mortality at 10°C occurred prior to the morula stage of development.

### Development Rate (Table 3)
- Time to 50% hatch was strongly temperature-dependent:
  - **10°C**: 420–581 hours
  - **15°C**: 256–298 hours
  - **20°C**: 158–168 hours
- Time to 50% swim-up stage at 10°C took **3.0–4.4× longer** than at 20°C.
- Full yolk depletion was frequently not reached by 95% of individuals at 10°C before experiments were terminated, underscoring markedly slower ontogeny at low temperatures.

### Oxygen Consumption (Figure 1)
- Oxygen consumption (µl O₂ · 15 individuals⁻¹ · hr⁻¹) increased **positively with both incubation temperature and developmental stage** (morula/blastula → pre-tailbud → pre-hatch → post-hatch → swim-up → yolk depletion).
- The **largest increase in oxygen consumption** occurred at the **swim-up stage**, indicating heightened metabolic demand coinciding with active larval swimming behavior.

### Comparison with Prior Studies
- This study's hatching success at comparable temperatures was **equal to or better** than prior investigators:
  - Inslee (1982): 22.5% hatch (hatchery).
  - Marsh (1985): 19%, 35%, 29% hatch at 15, 20, 25°C respectively.
- Development rates in this study were **slightly slower** than those reported by Minckley & Gustafson (1982, at 20°C) and Marsh (1985, at 15/20°C), possibly due to lower initial spawning/preacclimation temperatures used here.
- Methodological differences likely explain improved low-temperature performance relative to Marsh (1985) and Toney (1974):
  - Use of **naturally ripened eggs** (no hormonal induction) — egg diameter 3.1 ± 0.4 mm vs. 1.8 mm (hormone-induced) vs. 2.9 mm (naturally ripened, per Minckley & Gustafson 1982).
  - Avoidance of **formaldehyde** fungicide treatment (potential synergistic toxicity with cold stress).
  - More **gradual thermal acclimation** (1°C increments) vs. steeper gradients used by prior researchers.
  - Larger, more genetically diverse broodstock.

### Ecological Interpretation
- Despite reduced hatching efficiency at 10°C, larval emergence still reached as high as **55%** under lower-temperature conditions, suggesting direct temperature-driven mortality alone is unlikely to fully explain the species' documented recruitment failure in the Colorado River system.
- The protracted spawning season (10–22°C+) likely **buffers population-level reproductive success**, since substantial spawning occurs at warmer temperatures where hatching success is not reduced.
- However, **slower development at low temperatures** prolongs egg/larval exposure to indirect mortality sources (predation, wave action, water level fluctuation) — a mechanism the authors propose as more significant to recruitment failure than direct thermal mortality.
- The stable, moderate temperature (11–13°C) of the riverine reach below Hoover Dam appears within the range supporting adequate hatching success, per this study's results — a finding that nuances earlier claims (e.g., Johnson & Rinne 1982; Marsh 1985) implicating cold hypolimnetic discharge as a primary driver of reproductive failure via direct thermal effects on embryos.

### Editorial Context (Notable)
- Correspondence from the reviewing editor (Zoology Dept., Arizona State University) highlights the paper's key unaddressed significance: razorback suckers **consistently spawn at temperatures lower than the laboratory-determined hatching optimum**, both in Lake Mohave and in remnant natural riverine habitats upstream — a pattern the reviewer felt warranted further speculative discussion regarding potential adaptive significance, but which the original manuscript did not fully explore.
