# Die Fettflosse der Knochenfische (Morphology, Function, Phylogenetic Significance)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Characidae #Siluriformes #Teleostei

## Executive Summary

This 1965 German-language monograph by Curt Kosswig (with Bertram Rudolph), published in *Zeitschrift für zoologische Systematik und Evolutionsforschung*, examines the dorsal adipose fin (Pinna adiposa) of bony fishes. It covers external form, morphogenesis from the larval fin fold, internal anatomy, and the supporting elements, notably [[Actinotrichia]] (elastoidin filaments) and, in some catfishes, spines and segmented soft rays. Functional tests used [[Astyanax Mexicanus]] (amputation, regeneration, larval fin-fold destruction). The author concludes that the fin has no detectable function in most species and proposes that it represents a persistent, polygenically controlled, phylogenetically old structure that is homologous to the posterior part of a dorsal fin.

## Background & Description

- **Definition:** The adipose fin is a fleshy, rayless dorsal fin between the dorsal and caudal fins. Historically it was named for its fleshy appearance in European salmonids. Later it was defined as a fin lacking an artspecific supporting skeleton.
- **Distribution across orders:** Of 45 recent actinopterygian orders (Berg, 1958), four contain species with a dorsal adipose fin: Clupeiformes (Salmonoidei), Stomiatoidei (Clupeiformes, mostly bathypelagic), Scopeliformes (partly bathypelagic), Cypriniformes (Characinoidei, Siluroidei), and Percopsiformes (Percopsidoidei). The author stresses that these groups are not closely related.
- **Size:** Adipose fin area is roughly 1/30 to 1/10 of the dorsal fin area in Salmonidae and Characidae. In *Astyanax mexicanus* (90 mm body length), the base was 1.8 mm long and 4.0 mm high, with a width of 0.45 mm at the base and 0.22 mm at mid-height.
- **Enlarged forms:** Large adipose fins occur in some Characinoidei (*Metynnis*, *Citharinus*, *Distichodus*) and especially in many Siluroidei. In *Synodontis membranaceus*, a 355 mm specimen had a fin 125 mm long, larger in area than the dorsal or anal fin (after Sanders, 1956).
- **Materials and methods:** Histology used 7–10 µm sections stained with haematoxylin/eosin, orcein, and Mucicarmine. Specimens were fixed in 4% formalin or Bouin's solution. Anaesthesia used MS 222.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Adipose-fin-bearing taxa occupy a wide range of habitats: cold freshwater and seawater (Salmonoidei), bathypelagic marine (Stomiatoidei, Scopeliformes), warm freshwater (Characinoidei, Cypriniformes), and warm freshwater and marine (Siluroidei).
- The enlarged adipose fins of [[Metynnis]] and [[Citharinus]] are linked to a compressed, disc-shaped body form, which the author associates with schooling in calm waters and with high manoeuvrability. The author notes this link is only a possibility, not a demonstrated function.
- Many large-finned catfish (*Synodontis*, Bagridae, Pimelodidae) lead concealed lives and swim little, which makes a locomotor role for the enlarged fin doubtful.
- The electric catfish *Malapterurus electricus* has a fleshy adipose fin and no dorsal fin. Garstang (1931) suggested the fin acts as an accessory organ for the electric organ, but no evidence was given.

## Key Findings

1. **Distribution is irregular.** The adipose fin occurs in unrelated orders, and it can be absent in close relatives. Within the Characidae, genera can differ only by the presence or absence of the fin (e.g., *Poecilobrycon* vs. *Nannostomus*). Presence or absence can even vary within a single brood in some Characidae (*Piabucina*, *Lebiasina*).

2. **Ontogeny.** The fin forms from the larval median fin fold, the same source as all unpaired fins. In *Astyanax*, the adipose fin appears last among the unpaired fins, at about 13 mm total length, when it is only about 0.2 mm high. In *Thymallus vulgaris*, the adipose fin primordium is much earlier and larger than in *Astyanax*.

3. **Supporting elements.** The fin is supported by [[Actinotrichia]] (elastoidin filaments), which have the same structure in fish and sharks. Their chemistry matches the description by Krukenberg (1886): they resist pepsin only partially and are not keratin or collagen. Histochemistry shows staining like elastic fibres and reticulin, while the embryonic form shows different thermal shrinkage (Garrault, 1936).

4. **Directed and undirected actinotrichia.** In *Astyanax*, directed actinotrichia run parallel from base to outer margin (about 16–20 µm diameter near the base in adults, 1–2 mm long). Undirected actinotrichia are interwoven with them, mostly near the base, and run across the fin.

5. **Bony spines in catfish.** A bony spine occurs at the anterior edge of the adipose fin in Callichthyidae (all genera), in Loricariidae (all genera except Arginiinae), in *Trachyglanis* and *Andersonia* (Amphiliidae), and in *Clarotes* (Bagridae). In *Corydoras paleatus*, the spine is a modified dermal bony scute with no segmented rays or internal skeleton, and it is movable in Callichthyidae and Loricariidae. Arginiinae, which lack armour, have a spineless adipose fin, which the author interprets as supporting a scute origin.

6. **Segmented soft rays.** Rays occur in the adipose fin of *Pygocentrus piraya*, *Piaractus nigripinnis*, *Myletes* (?) *bidens*, *Clarotes laticeps*, *Mochocus niloticus* (11 unbranched segmented rays in a 9.5 mm fin), and *Phractocephalus hemiliopterus*. None of these rays are attached to an internal skeleton. The only documented exception is a teratological *Synodontis membranaceus* with seven bony rays on a fused pterygiophore complex.

7. **Function.** Amputation of the adipose fin in 16 juvenile and 16 adult *Astyanax* had no evident effect on swimming or feeding, and a pair spawned 12 hours after total amputation. Three *Synodontis* also showed no change after removal. Regeneration depended on the cut level: 10 fish with partial amputations regenerated fully in 50–90 days. Of 12 fish cut at the base, 2 regenerated fully in about 120 days, 7 formed only a stump, and 3 showed no regeneration after 8 months. In 10 fish cut deeper (removing about 0.5 mm of underlying tissue), none regenerated. Regeneration of the dorsal fin in the same fish was much faster (about 30 days). The author concludes that an underlying "border layer" controls regeneration.

8. **Larval fin-fold destruction.** Of 112 *Astyanax* larvae (5.8–9.0 mm) with the fin fold scraped away, 43 formed an adipose fin and 69 did not. Three larvae developed a second, smaller adipose-like fin between the dorsal and normal adipose fins, which the author attributes to splitting of the fin-forming zone.

9. **Homology.** The author argues the adipose fin is homologous to the posterior part of a segmented dorsal fin, since both arise from the same dorsal fin fold and have similar early structure. Its loss in many lineages is interpreted as slow reduction of a polygenically controlled, functionally unimportant trait, possibly sustained by pleiotropic genes.

10. **Hypothesis.** Kosswig proposes six linked points: (1) a potency for actinotrichia formation is present across the dorsal fin fold; (2) a second potency for lepidotrichia overlies it; (3) the lepidotrichia potency may extend more caudally; (4) where it is absent, an adipose fin forms; (5) the exact pattern is set by hereditary constitution, which varies among groups; (6) in exceptional cases, such as hybrids or old age, segmented-ray fins may form behind the dorsal fin. The author applies this to explain the variability of catfish dorsal fin systems.

11. **Colour.** In most species the fin matches the body colour. The exception is the African characin *Alestes imberi*, whose adipose fin is bright red, while the other fins are nearly transparent. Reisinger reports that red adipose fins are common in young trout of the Trutta group.

12. **Conclusion.** The author states that the adipose fin has no function in at least most cases, that its structure is similar in unrelated groups, and that its persistence reflects an ancient developmental trait rather than an adaptive advantage that evolved independently several times.
