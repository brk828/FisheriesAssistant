# Dexter National Fish Hatchery Endangered Fish Die-Off, March 1992

#ichthyology #aquatic-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

In late March 1992, a sequence of failures at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center in New Mexico killed most of the endangered upper Colorado River fish held there for [[Recovery Program]] broodstock. The documents are internal [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] memoranda, a chronology, and press coverage. Together they describe the die-off, its proximate cause (chlorinated municipal backup water flowing into the incubation and holding systems after valves were left open), the staff response, and the internal and public-relations fallout. Of the upper basin [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] held at Dexter, only three of each survived by late March, according to the press account.

## Background & Description

The hatchery is a roughly 60-year-old federal station that maintains endangered fishes for recovery of species listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The fish involved were:

- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): listed as endangered in 1979, grows to about 3 feet and 13 pounds.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): listed as endangered since 1967, grows to about 6 feet and 80 pounds, once known as "Colorado salmon." Estimated at roughly 10,000 remaining in the wild per the press account.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*): held in the holding house; six died on March 24.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*): listed as endangered since 1974, grows to about 20 inches, with a distinctive hump behind the head. Fifteen were placed in a pond on October 29, 1991.
- **[[Cutthroat Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus clarkii*): held as natural food for the squawfish.

Since 1988 biologists had captured 16 razorback suckers, 10 Colorado squawfish, and 20 humpback chubs for propagation. Some of the fish were gathered over several years from the wild and were, according to press accounts, rare and possibly past breeding age. The [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] and regional U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service staff were involved in the program.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water system.** The hatchery's primary water supply came from a [[Colorado River Basin]]-linked source that fed 43 ponds and the incubation system. A backup supply came from the Dexter [[Municipal Water Supply]], a 2-inch line with enough pressure to displace the primary supply. The municipal supply was periodically dosed with chlorine flushes required by the State, and the town also supplied the Visitor Center aquaria and the holding house.

**Water quality on March 24.** Dissolved oxygen measured 6 mg/l and pH 7.8. Staff ruled out [[Gas Supersaturation]] because the die-off was acute and no gas bubbles were seen under the skin of the fish. The backup supply contained slightly over 1 mg/l of chlorine, which was identified as the likely toxic agent (see [[Chlorine Toxicity]]).

**Disease.** The humpback chubs were infested with [[Anchor Worm]] (*Lernaea* sp.) and were held in the holding house pending treatment. The fish also showed signs of stress before the die-off.

**Genetic stakes.** Fish were captured from several locations to provide [[Broodstock Genetic Diversity]], which the program considered essential to survival in the wild. The loss of wild-origin, rare individuals was described as a serious setback.

## Key Findings

**1. Sequence of events (March 23 to April 1, 1992).**
- **March 23:** An electrical contractor repairing the incubation heater shut off the regular water supply. When he reopened it, no water flowed, so he opened a different valve (the backup municipal line) and left both valves open. Water flowed through the egg incubators and holding tanks. Four razorback suckers (one female, three males) died that day. Staff removed the female's ovaries and oviducts and spawned the eggs with milt from three males in a three-way combination in hopes of fertilization. The eggs were then lost to the chlorinated water.
- **March 24:** The staff shut off incoming water, unplugged aerators, and sampled water. The overnight toll was one more upper basin razorback sucker, four upper basin squawfish, five domestic razorback suckers, six bonytail chubs, and the neutered squawfish. The memo later noted that the eggs from the dead female were also lost.
- **March 25:** Two more Colorado squawfish died. Four razorback suckers and three Colorado squawfish remained for the upper basin at Dexter.
- **March 30–April 1:** A further razorback sucker (PIT-tagged) was found dead on a weekend. By April 1, three razorback suckers and three Colorado squawfish were reported to be doing well.

**2. Root cause.** The chlorinated backup supply was opened and left running, while the regular supply was found to be "not operating properly." The electrician had shut off the primary water to work on the heater and, after finding no water on the backup line, opened a second valve and left both open. The backup line was isolated from the holding house's east side, where fish were held and spawned. Staff "eliminated" supersaturation as the cause and concluded that the chlorine was the principal factor.

**3. Losses.** The press reported that of the upper basin fish, only three razorback suckers and three Colorado squawfish survived. The memos say seven of ten squawfish died. The memo's total razorback-sucker loss is garbled in the scan and cannot be reliably reconstructed. The press also reported that the earlier chronology lists 15 humpback chubs placed in the pond, and that these fish were recorded as absent by June.

**4. Prior warnings.** The project leader had recommended replacing the municipal backup supply with a well pump and installing a standby generator. Those recommendations were not funded because other priorities were given precedence on this 60-year-old station. The memo states the risk was known and that "a warning was [not] acted on."

**5. Institutional and public response.** Regional personnel had long known that multiple species were maintained in a single pond, which the Dexter director said had been discussed "on numerous occasions." Staff documented that Region 5 had brought additional fish and a new species to Dexter under pre-existing conditions. The regional offices disputed the account, and coordination problems, including the lack of formal annual coordination meetings, were acknowledged.

**6. Media coverage.** The [[Rocky Mountain News]] (April 1992, reporter Deborah Frazier) and the Roswell Daily Record (April 8, 1992) carried stories quoting Tom Smylie (Assistant Regional Director, New Mexico), Galen Buterbaugh (Director, Denver), Kelly Drake (Colorado Wildlife Federation), and Angela Kantola (Colorado biologist). Quotes included "a comedy of errors" and "a major catastrophe." A Colorado Division of Wildlife public affairs officer prepared a correction release, and Dexter staff provided background materials to limit distortion. Dexter staff said they received national media calls from ABC, NBC, and CBS.

**7. Outcome.** The episode is described as a major setback, not an extinction. Staff said the species were not "going to put them into extinction," but the loss sharply reduced genetic diversity and the program's recovery prospects.
