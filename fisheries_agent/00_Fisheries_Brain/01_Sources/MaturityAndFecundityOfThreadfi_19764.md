# Maturity and Fecundity of Threadfin Shad in Central Arizona Reservoirs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[James E. Johnson]] (Arizona State University, 1971) examines reproductive maturity and fecundity in [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*) from three [[Salt River Reservoirs]] in central Arizona, sampled April 1966 through January 1969. The author shows that the [[Maturity Index]] (ovary weight/body weight) reliably describes maturity stages when combined with ovum size-distribution, and that females release mature ova only once the index reaches 19% or more. Shad spawn once a year in these reservoirs, young-of-the-year do not spawn in their first summer, and older fish begin spawning about two months before age-I fish. A substantial share of older fish fail to reproduce, with fatty tissue replacing gametes in the gonads.

## Background & Description

- **Fecundity** (number of eggs spawned per year) is determined by genotype and modified by nutrition, population density, temperature, and photoperiod. Standard fecundity techniques are problematic, particularly in fish that produce several overlapping cohorts of ova per season.
- **Atresia** (resorption of ova not destined for spawning) complicates counts. Ova diameter-frequencies are the most informative maturity measure but are time-consuming, so the maturity index is used as a practical alternative.
- Threadfin shad were widely introduced into warm-water reservoirs as forage for piscivorous game fish, and their rapid establishment in the [[Lower Colorado River]] prompted the need for a fecundity study of this freshwater clupeid.
- Study lakes lie at 450 to 635 m above sea level, are monomictic (stratifying June to November), and had open-water surface temperatures of 8 to 31 °C.
- **Methods:** Fish were mainly collected by [[Electrofishing]], with seines and rotenone used occasionally. Specimens were preserved in formalin, then stored in 50% isopropyl alcohol. Ova were measured microscopically (ocular micrometer, 35X), with 15 largest ova per fish for mean diameter and 200 consecutive ova >0.16 mm for size-distribution. Total ova were estimated from weighed ovary subsamples, with estimates within ±5.0% of total counts for four fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Immediately after spawning, ovaries are thin and translucent with maturity indices below 1%, and primary oocytes (≤0.16 mm) are present.
- Recruitment ova grow from about 0.2 mm in September to a second mode of about 0.4 mm by spring. Maturation begins in late February in age-III and older fish, and mature ova reach 0.74 to 0.94 mm.
- Spawning aggregations occupy a different lake niche than non-mature females, which explains why mature females are rarely caught in general nightly electrofishing.
- Spawning is linked to seasonal light and temperature. Maturity indices decline just before peak summer temperatures, and high summer temperatures may suppress second spawns (as suggested by [[Shelton]] in Lake Texoma).
- Reservoir dynamics matter: [[Saguaro Lake]] was drained in 1966 and repopulated by a "population explosion," with indications of a limited late-summer 1967 spawn. Reduced competition may also influence growth and reproduction.
- Threadfin shad are a key forage species, and their reproduction influences predator populations such as [[Piscivorous Game Fish]], though this is not quantified in the study.

## Key Findings

- **Maturity threshold:** Females are incapable of releasing mature ova until the maturity index reaches 19% or more, although mature ova may occur at lower indices (as low as 12%) in partially spawned fish. Fish above 19% are designated mature.
- **Spawning success:** Of 105 females (of 1,972 fish seined) from a spawning school in Apache Lake on 10 April 1968, 95.7% were reproducing (the source text reads "957%", interpreted here as 95.7%). Maturity indices of fish with mature ova ranged 9.4% to 24.9% (mean 18.3%).
- **Spawning frequency:** Threadfin shad spawn once per year in the Salt River reservoirs. Age-I fish produce only one complement of mature ova in their first season, and young-of-the-year do not spawn in their first summer, despite reaching 49 mm by autumn.
- **Age-related timing:** Age-groups III–IV reached maturity by 1 April 1968, with a peak around 10 April. Age-II peaked in early May, and age-I peaked in late May (observed spawning 23 May to 6 June 1968), more than a month later.
- **Fecundity:** Mature ova per gram averaged 2,308 ± 185 (two standard errors) in 25 fish with indices above 19%. Estimated mature ova ranged from 923 (age I, 49–55 mm, 0.4 g ovary) to 8,540 (age III, 93 mm, 3.7 g ovary). Fecundity was not related to length, weight, or age per gram.
- **Comparison:** [[Finucane]] (1965) counted 6,210 and 21,007 ova in fish of 126 and 168 mm SL from Tampa Bay, but these eggs were opaque and therefore immature; they are lower than Arizona estimates for equivalent sizes.
- **Gonadal aberrations:** Three types were recorded, all more common in large fish. Least common was unilateral ovary failure. Second was empty post-spawn ovaries with only residual ova. Third, most important, was fatty gonads with no germ cells. Fatty gonads occurred in about 2% of fish under 80 mm and 77.3% of fish over 99 mm. Weighted estimates indicate 6.7% of the Apache Lake population and 2.3% of Roosevelt Lake were non-reproductive over the three years.
- **Males:** Males are mature throughout the April–June spawning season, with testis indices of 3.6% to 7.2% (mean 5.0%) in spawners. Indices fall sharply by late June, and no male after June had an index above 0.5%. A changing sex ratio in older fish suggests higher male mortality.
- **Interpretation:** The authors favor the view that older females occasionally produce a second complement of eggs under suitable environmental conditions, but that these eggs are usually resorbed rather than spawned.
