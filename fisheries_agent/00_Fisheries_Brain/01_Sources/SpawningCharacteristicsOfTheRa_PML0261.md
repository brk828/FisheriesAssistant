# Spawning Characteristics of the Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, AZ-NV, During 1982-1983

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document bundles a peer-review solicitation from [[Southwestern Naturalist]] with the manuscript it concerns (F-05-87), a field study of [[Razorback Sucker]] spawning in [[Lake Mohave]] during 1982-1983. The authors (Bozek, Paulson, Wilde and Deacon) report a protracted, seven-month spawning season in a reservoir whose population appears to be reproducing despite heavy egg and larval losses. The letter, dated 27 March 1982 (OCR-uncertain year), asks reviewer [[W. L. Minckley]] to assess originality, literature, organisation, statistics and figures, and to recommend acceptance, minor or major revision, or rejection.

## Background & Description

- **Editorial correspondence:** Edie Marsh, Associate Editor for Fishes, requests a review within a set number of weeks. Reviewers are asked to use a standard form with nine guiding questions and four recommendation categories: publish without revision, publish with minor revision, reconsider after revision, or do not publish. The journal states that its standard should match that of national or regional journals.
- **Study species:** The Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was once widespread throughout the [[Colorado River]] system. It declined sharply during the twentieth century alongside other native fishes. Lake Mohave holds the largest known spawning population in the Colorado River system, and the species appears to tolerate reservoir conditions better than other native fishes.
- **Study area:** [[Lake Mohave]] formed after [[Davis Dam]] was built in 1951 and extends about 100 km from [[Hoover Dam]] to Davis Dam. Its shoreline is long, wind-exposed and rich in gravel and cobble substrates, with flooded shoreline vegetation providing cover.
- **Methods:** Cove surveys were conducted from a boat in February 1982, and trammel netting ran monthly from January through May 1982 and in 1983. Fish were measured for total length and weight, tagged, and checked for condition, disease and parasites. Population sizes were estimated with a [[Modified Schnabel Estimator]] following [[Ricker Method]] procedures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning habitat:** Spawning occurred mainly over gravel and gravel-sand substrates in shallow water, often on steeper slopes and in coves, and was associated with wave-washed gravel and sand terraces created by reservoir drawdowns. Good spawning habitat in the reservoir is maintained by wave action that replenishes gravel and removes fine sediment.
- **Major spawning areas:** The authors identified 19 spawning areas. The largest concentrations were at [[Six Mile Cove]] and [[Little Cove]] (the Six Mile and Little Coves area), and the second largest were at [[Arizona Bay]] and adjoining Yuma and Gold Coves. Unique flat gravel terraces at Yuma Cove were also used for spawning.
- **Temperature:** Water temperatures rose from roughly 10 to 15 °C during peak spawning in January-March and exceeded 20 °C by May. Spawning began when temperatures were near 10 °C (the OCR text for the exact range is partly illegible).
- **Threats:** Egg and larval mortality from wave action, siltation, desiccation from reservoir drawdowns and predation by introduced fishes all appear significant. Reservoir drawdowns desiccate eggs and larvae and can destroy redds. Introduced species, including [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]], were common in the spawning areas. Carp was the second most abundant species caught in Arizona Bay.

## Key Findings

**Spawning season and timing**
- Spawning is protracted, lasting about seven months, from November through May in both study years.
- Peak spawning occurred from January through March, with more than 50% of individuals ripe at peak.
- Early spawners were in ripe condition in November. The authors believe a cyclical, staggered replacement of spawned fish by newly ripened fish sustains spawning throughout the season.

**Population and mark-recapture**
- Over the two-year study, 636 males and 467 females were netted. The authors cite a population estimate of about 13,860 individuals in the Arizona Bay area, derived from the [[Modified Schnabel Estimator]] (the 95% confidence interval is partly illegible).
- Only a few recaptures were made, so the estimate is imprecise. The Six Mile and Little Coves area yielded no usable estimate because no recaptures were obtained there.
- Movement between spawning areas appears limited, with a single documented movement from Six Mile Cove to Arizona Bay in 1983.

**Size and sex structure**
- Mean total length combined for both years was about 554.6 mm for males and 627.3 mm for females. Females were significantly longer than males (Mann-Whitney U test).
- Males were usually the dominant sex in the spawning season. The highest male-to-female ratios occurred in November and May, and the authors suggest males arrive earlier.

**Condition, health and reproduction**
- Condition factor stayed high through February and dropped in March, marking the end of spawning.
- Bacterial infection and eye damage were recorded. Infection was significantly more common in males than females, and eye affliction affected about 37% of males (other percentages are partly illegible).
- Eggs from wild females were artificially fertilised and hatched normally, and larvae were abundant in spawning areas in January-March, indicating successful reproduction.

**Recruitment and conservation implications**
- Young fish were rare in the samples, and the authors suspect recruitment is low or intermittent. The large, long-lived adult population could persist for decades even without recruitment.
- Successful spawning is partly attributed to life-history adaptations such as a long spawning season, high fecundity, long life and rapid maturation, which allow the species to exploit variable reservoir conditions.
- Wave action, drawdowns and predation are major threats. The authors recommend further work on aging techniques and on early-life survival and migration.
