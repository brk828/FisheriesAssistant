# Utah Division of Wildlife Resources Proposal for Rainbow Smelt Introduction to Lake Powell

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #Moronidae #Osmeridae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 correspondence from the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] defends a proposal to introduce [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) into [[Lake Powell]] and the [[Colorado River]] system as a biological control mechanism for stunted [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) populations. The author argues that smelt would provide deep-water forage diversity, improve striped bass growth rates, and create a self-regulating predator-prey system without threatening endangered native species. The proposal addresses concerns about impacts on endemic fish, potential for illicit species transfers, and compares biological versus mechanical control approaches for managing overpopulated striped bass.

## Background & Description

[[Lake Powell]] and the broader [[Colorado River]] system face fisheries management challenges stemming from introduced species, particularly [[Striped Bass]]. The reservoir has experienced population imbalances since filling in 1963, with conditions remaining relatively static. Current forage base consists primarily of [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*), which has proven insufficient for the striped bass population.

[[Rainbow Smelt]] have established populations in the lower [[Mississippi River]] and over 15 years in the [[Missouri River]] system, providing working models for their potential performance. Smelt are described as planktivorous during migration, not eating while spawning, and young smelt (less than 150 mm) are non-piscivorous. The species coevolved with striped bass in the North Atlantic.

Endemic species including [[Gila]] spp., *[[Xyrauchen texanus]]*, and other native fish are threatened or endangered primarily due to habitat alteration from [[Hoover Dam]], [[Davis Dam]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]], and other impoundments rather than from introduced species predation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The current [[Lake Powell]] ecosystem exhibits inefficient energy transfer, with juvenile striped bass competing directly with adult striped bass for shad and maintaining themselves through direct plankton consumption. This creates a population structure dominated by numerous small, stunted fish rather than fewer, larger individuals. Juvenile striped bass currently crop the open-water shad population and limit shad recruitment.

The proposal notes that native fish recruitment is minimal or nonexistent, requiring populations to be augmented by hatchery-reared fish. Management practices dictate introducing hatchery fish larger than 40 mm, while smelt do not prey on fish larger than 40 mm.

Habitat alterations from dam construction have fundamentally changed the river system. Smelt are not riverine fish and would not establish in flowing river sections. In the [[Mississippi River]] and [[Missouri River]] systems, smelt passing through river sections have been beneficial as forage organisms without establishing populations or causing problems for riverine fish.

## Key Findings

**Rationale for Smelt Introduction:**
- Provides biological control that is self-perpetuating versus mechanical removal requiring continuous funding and effort
- Introduces deep-water forage fish to complement existing shad populations
- Creates more efficient food chain with planktivorous forage fish consuming plankton instead of juvenile striped bass
- Coevolved relationship with striped bass provides natural predator-prey balance

**Expected Ecological Outcomes:**
- Open water plankton primarily consumed by efficient planktivorous forage fish (smelt or shad) instead of striped bass
- Striped bass growth rates increase on fish diet, retreat to depths to forage on smelt
- Juvenile striped bass recruitment potentially limited by smelt predation on eggs and larvae
- Reduced plankton availability to juvenile striped bass
- Adult striped bass may limit juvenile recruitment through predation and competition
- Sustained yield of striped bass remains same by weight but fewer by number, producing larger individual fish

**Management Philosophy:**
- Proactive introduction preferred over reactive response to illicit transfers (citing [[Grass Carp]] example present throughout Colorado River basin despite prohibition)
- Biological control provides self-regulating mechanism without continual maintenance and funding
- Larger striped bass will attract anglers without additional education or regulatory coercion
- Addresses current reality of introduced species (striped bass) as permanent fixtures requiring active management

**Risk Assessment:**
- Smelt will not run upriver to spawn or disturb altered river habitat
- Smelt do not eat while actively spawning
- Smelt passing through river sections will not be present in large numbers and will be benign
- Will not further impact threatened/endangered native species beyond existing habitat alterations
- Young smelt (less than 150 mm) are not piscivorous

**Alternative Approaches Rejected:**
- Mechanical removal requires perpetual control regardless of equipment breakdown or budget cuts
- Education and regulation to encourage harvest of small stripers lacks sufficient precedent for success
- Small striped bass in marginal condition have questionable commercial value
- Failure of any mechanical/regulatory approach returns system to stunted bass problem
