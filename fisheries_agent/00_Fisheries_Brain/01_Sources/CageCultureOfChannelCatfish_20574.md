# Cage Culture of Channel Catfish

#ichthyology #aquaculture #Ictaluridae #cage-culture #intensive-fish-farming

## Executive Summary

This document presents research findings from [[Auburn University]] Agricultural Experiment Station (begun 1966) on developing cage culture systems for raising [[Channel Catfish]] ([[Ictalurus punctatus]]) from fingerlings to marketable size. The system allows intensive fish culture in previously unused aquatic environments including reservoirs, rivers, irrigation canals, and mining pits. Key advantages include simplified harvest operations, elimination of pond-draining stress, and flexible stocking schedules. Production potential reaches approximately 2,000 lb per acre in ponds, with fish growing from fingerlings to 0.75-1.0 lb market size. The method addresses major limiting factors of intensive culture: nutrition, parasites/disease, and waste disposal through proper cage design, placement, and water exchange.

## Background & Description

### Historical Context
Cage culture of fish has existed in rivers of [[Thailand]] since before the 20th century, practiced in riverine areas of Thailand and [[Cambodia]] and sewage streams of [[Indonesia]] where intensive culture feeds were available. Recent years have seen expansion to freshwater and saltwater environments in other countries, though existing literature provided minimal usable information for developing [[Channel Catfish]] cage culture systems.

### Cage Construction and Materials
Cages at [[Auburn University]] were constructed using 2 × 2 inch pine or spruce frames enclosed with ¼ or ½ inch galvanized hardware cloth. Standard dimensions were 47½ × 36 × 36 inches deep (1.32 yd³ or 1.0 m³). Hardware cloth was attached to wooden frames with steel staples. Cages were suspended in ponds with tops 4-8 inches above water level, either by direct pier attachment or flotation using styrofoam.

**Cost and Durability**: Construction cost approximately $20 per m³ unit including materials and labor. Cages remained satisfactory for up to 3 years with maintenance between cultures. By the third year, hardware cloth became severely weakened by rust, especially along stapled edges. Wood became watersoaked, creating undesirable weight for handling and flotation. Hardware cloth caused cuts and abrasions around fish mouths, potentially inducing fatal bacterial infections.

**Cage Covers**: Opaque covers constructed of 2 × 2 inch boards covered with aluminum sheeting were essential. Covers prevented predation, fish escape by jumping, and potential theft (when locked). Importantly, opaque covers reduced fish stress—within 10 days of installation, fish ceased swimming into cage sides when approached and began feeding immediately even with human presence.

**Feeding Rings**: Rectangular enclosures built into cage covers with surface area of approximately 280 in² for 1-m³ cages. Typical dimensions: 14 × 20 × 20 inches deep with open top and bottom. Approximately 12 inches positioned below water level to prevent feed dispersal by fish-induced currents; remaining 8 inches above water prevented feed splashing.

### Mesh Size Effects
Mesh size significantly affected production. In 93-day experiments comparing ¼-inch versus ½-inch mesh in 1-m³ cages (four replicates each):
- **¼-inch mesh**: 4.1-fold weight increase
- **½-inch mesh**: 6.5-fold weight increase

No algae or growth developed on either mesh size. Production differences resulted directly from differential water circulation frequency—smaller mesh accumulated more waste products due to less frequent water exchange.

**Recommendations**: Fingerlings larger than 4½ inches should be grown in 1-inch mesh cages. Smaller fingerlings (<4½ inches) should be cultured in ½-inch mesh and transferred to ¾-inch mesh upon reaching 4½ inches total length. Larger mesh sizes might further improve production.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Limiting Factors in Intensive Culture
Three major categories regulate cage culture success, parallel to intensive culture of terrestrial animals:

1. **Nutrition**: Nutritionally complete feed necessary since confined fish cannot access natural foods
2. **Parasites and Disease**: Confinement and crowding create high potential for epizootics (epidemics)
3. **Waste Disposal**: Crowding creates serious waste elimination problems. Carbon dioxide, ammonia, urea, and feces accumulate rapidly and must be eliminated before reaching concentrations threatening fish well-being

### Water Quality and Exchange
Proper water quality within cages can only be maintained by frequent replacement and dilution with surrounding environmental water. **Rate of high-quality water exchange is a major limiting factor** in cage culture, comparable to raceway culture of trout.

### Cage Placement Strategies

**Depth Considerations**: Unknown if water depth beneath cage bottom affects production. Cage bottoms should use same material as sides to allow solid waste passage. Fish should be separated from solid wastes and bottom mud by minimum 1 foot, potentially important for preventing parasite and disease problems.

**Nonflowing Water (Ponds)**: Cage position relative to other cages and water currents affects production, reflecting water exchange and quality. Best production obtained in cages with greatest surface area exposed to open water, maximizing exposure to convection (wind) currents.

**Placement Guidelines**:
- Position cages in open water away from obstructions preventing water current passage from any direction
- Space individual cages minimum 3 feet apart to prevent waste contamination between cages
- Maximum 6 m³ of cages per surface acre of pond

**Flowing Water and Large Reservoirs**: No data yet available for [[Channel Catfish]] in cages suspended in flowing water or large reservoirs. Theoretically, production per cage unit could increase considerably in running water over ponds, as wastes flush before accumulation inhibits production. Slow current of approximately 3-10 yards per minute considered optimum. Large impoundments would likely provide greater water exchange than ponds due to gravity flow and/or greater convection currents, with much greater dilution potential.

### Stocking Density

**Pond Systems**: At [[Auburn University]], 500 fingerlings stocked per 1-m³ cage to raise fish to ¾-1 lb average size. Higher densities appropriate for cages in large reservoirs, flowing water, or ponds with agitators. The "space factor" from crowding reflects waste disposal more than confinement itself—fish densities may be increased without diminishing production as long as waste products are eliminated and good water quality maintained.

**Per-Acre Calculations**: Expected production of [[Channel Catfish]] in cages suspended in ponds at [[Auburn University]]: approximately 2,000 lb per acre, plus additional 200-400 lb of fish loose in pond (unfed, growing on natural food organisms).

**Example Stocking Plan**: To raise fish to average 0.80 lb, stock per acre:
- Five 1-m³ cages with 500 catfish fingerlings each
- Additional 500 fingerlings loose in surrounding pond (optional)

If pond already contains fish population, total weight should be considered as part of carrying capacity.

**Timing**: Fingerlings should be stocked before water temperature reaches 55°F. Feed daily at 1.0% of total fish weight per cage until water temperature averages 58-60°F over 3-day period, then begin planned feeding program.

### Feed Management

**Feed Type**: Nutritionally complete feed required—containing all essential vitamins, amino acids, and other nutrients. Floating pelleted feeds most desirable for cage use: easily contained within feeding rings, available to fish with minimal feed loss. Non-floating feeds less expensive but difficult to use without loss.

**Feeding Rate Schedule**: Feed at least once daily, 7 days per week. Dividing daily ration into multiple feedings results in faster growth and better feed efficiency. Adjust feeding rate every 20-30 days:

| Culture Days | Daily Feeding Rate (% total weight) | Mean Individual Weight (lb) | Weight Range (lb) |
|--------------|-------------------------------------|----------------------------|-------------------|
| 1-30         | 3.50*                               | 0.07                       | 0.05-0.08         |
| 31-60        | 3.00                                | 0.15                       | 0.08-0.20         |
| 61-80        | 2.75                                | 0.25                       | 0.20-0.32         |
| 81-100       | 2.50                                | 0.40                       | 0.32-0.50         |
| 101-120      | 2.00                                | 0.60                       | 0.50-0.70         |
| 121+         | 1.50                                | 0.80                       | 0.70-0.90         |

*Fingerlings <0.05 lb average should be fed at 4.0% until reaching 0.05 lb each.

**Feeding Guidelines**: At each feeding, fish should totally consume feed within less than 5 minutes. Failure indicates over-feeding, poor water quality (low dissolved oxygen), or parasite-disease infestation. In ponds, total feed in all cages should not exceed 30 lb per acre per day.

### Water Quality Monitoring

**Dissolved Oxygen**: Greatest danger of fish kills in ponds comes from low oxygen, usually resulting directly or indirectly from organic decay of feed residue. Dissolved oxygen analyses should be made daily at 1-foot increments from top to deepest pond point. Portable measurement equipment available at $20-$500 (more expensive units typically more accurate and easier to use).

**Fertilization**: Neither organic nor inorganic fertilizers should be added to ponds where fish are raised in cages.

## Key Findings

1. **System Viability**: Cage culture of [[Channel Catfish]] demonstrates great potential and promises to significantly affect commercial fisheries, enabling intensive fish culture in previously unused aquatic environments (reservoirs, rivers, irrigation canals, mining pits, natural lakes, bays, lagoons).

2. **Harvest Advantages**: Cage systems facilitate harvest operations requiring less labor and equipment than pond harvest. Cages serve as storage units, eliminating need for separate storage equipment. Partial harvests possible without affecting other cages. Harvest can be manipulated for continuous marketable-size production by staggered stocking. Stress and mortality associated with pond draining greatly reduced or eliminated.

3. **Mesh Size Critical**: ½-inch mesh cages produced 58% greater weight gain (6.5× vs 4.1×) than ¼-inch mesh over 93 days due to improved water circulation and waste removal. Fingerlings <4½ inches should start in ½-inch mesh, transfer to ¾-inch mesh at 4½ inches length.

4. **Cage Cover Benefits**: Opaque covers essential for reducing fish stress. Within 10 days of installation, fish ceased panic swimming and began feeding immediately even with human presence nearby.

5. **Production Potential**: Expected production in pond-suspended cages at [[Auburn University]]: approximately 2,000 lb [[Channel Catfish]] per acre in cages, plus 200-400 lb from unfed fish in surrounding pond utilizing natural foods.

6. **Stocking Density**: 500 fingerlings per 1-m³ cage appropriate for raising fish to ¾-1 lb market size in ponds. Higher densities possible in flowing water or large reservoirs with better waste removal.

7. **Cage Placement**: In ponds, position cages in open water with maximum exposure to wind-driven convection currents. Space individual cages minimum 3 feet apart. Maximum 6 m³ cages per surface acre.

8. **Water Exchange Paramount**: Rate of high-quality water exchange is major limiting factor, comparable to raceway trout culture. "Space factor" from crowding reflects waste disposal capacity more than physical confinement.

9. **Feed Management**: Floating pelleted feeds most effective. Feed at least once daily at rates declining from 3.5% body weight (days 1-30) to 1.5% (day 121+) as fish grow. Total feed should not exceed 30 lb per acre per day in ponds. Fish should consume feed within 5 minutes.

10. **Water Quality Monitoring**: Daily dissolved oxygen measurements at 1-foot depth increments essential. Low oxygen from organic decay of feed residue represents greatest fish kill danger. No fertilizers should be added to cage culture ponds.

11. **Cage Economics and Durability**: Construction cost approximately $20 per m³. Cages satisfactory for up to 3 years with maintenance, though hardware cloth rust and wood waterlogging present limitations. Hardware cloth may cause mouth abrasions leading to bacterial infections.

12. **Flowing Water Potential**: Though untested for [[Channel Catfish]], flowing water (3-10 yards per minute) theoretically could substantially increase production per cage unit by flushing wastes before inhibitory accumulation occurs.
