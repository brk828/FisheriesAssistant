# Age Determination of Walleye Based on the Examination of Eight Different Structures

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Fisheries and Marine Service Technical Report (No. 849, April 1979) by J.S. Campbell and J.A. Babaluk, Western Region, Winnipeg, compares [[Age Determination]] methods for [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum vitreum*) from [[Wapun Lake]], a small boreal lake in northern Manitoba. The work was a sub-study of a longer [[Walleye Population Study]] at [[Heming Lake]]. Using 103 fish, the authors tested [[Scale Reading]] against seven calcified structures. Scales consistently underestimated the ages of fish older than about eight years. [[Dorsal Fin Spines]] were judged the most practical structure, and [[Opercle]] and [[Otolith]] annuli were the most readable. Scales should be used only after they have been validated against a bony structure.

## Background & Description

- **Study context:** Begun in 1977 in northern Manitoba, the program was planned to run at least six years. Its aims were to understand the factors controlling walleye biology and productive capacity, and to test how resilient walleye are to controlled "cropping" (harvesting). Associated species studied were [[Northern Pike]], [[Yellow Perch]] and [[White Sucker]].
- **Study site:** Wapun Lake is a small eutrophic lake on the Precambrian Shield at the headwaters of the Nelson River system, at 54°51'N, 101°12'W. Surface area is about 272 ha and maximum depth about 8.5 m.
- **Sample:** 103 walleye were captured in the experimental netting program between May and September 1977. Fork length, weight, sex and sexual maturity were recorded. Sampling aimed to represent most size classes. The catch used for aging was 806 walleye (Fig. 2 shows the length-frequency distribution).
- **Structures collected:** Scales; dorsal fin spines; left pelvic fin rays; left pectoral fin rays; sagittal otoliths; left branchiostegal rays; left opercle; and precaudal and caudal vertebrae.
- **Background rationale:** Walleye ages had traditionally been assigned from scales. Earlier work had shown difficulty in distinguishing annuli on scales of older fish from unexploited populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Unexploited, slow-growing population:** The authors stress that aging error matters most in such populations. Underaging of fish past first maturity could bias estimates of growth, mortality, year-class strength and production.
- **Historical stocking and age composition:** In 1951 and 1953, [[Lake Whitefish]] (*Coregonus clupeaformis*) eggs and fry were introduced into Wapun Lake. Walleye captured in 1950–52 (n = 153), aged from scales, had a modal age of five and a maximum of ten. Walleye caught in 1977 (n = 122), aged from dorsal spines, had a modal age of eleven and a maximum of seventeen. The authors attribute much of this difference to scale underaging, since the population had been only lightly angled between the two periods.
- **Methodological environment:** Scale and bony-structure preparation involved formalin and ethanol preservation, hot-water treatment of opercles, epoxy embedding and thin sectioning with a Thin Sectioning Machine, and viewing under transmitted light. Xylene and epoxy hardener were noted as toxic and required fume-hood use.

## Key Findings

**Scales versus bony structures**
- Scale and bony-structure counts agreed well up to ages 8–9. Beyond that, scales generally underestimated age, sometimes substantially.
- Pelvic and pectoral fin rays showed no clear advantage over scales until about ages 12–13.
- Sectioned otoliths, vertebrae, dorsal fin spines and opercles made annuli easier to delineate.

**Reader agreement between structures (two readers, Table 1)**

| Structure | Complete agreement, all ages | Within ±1 yr, all ages | Complete agreement, ≥8 yr |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dorsal spines | 80.6% | 94.2% | 52.8% |
| Branchiostegal rays | 79.6% | 97.9% | 54.6% |
| Opercles | 75.3% | 94.1% | 52.8% |
| Scales | 59.8% | 89.2% | 20.0% |

- Dorsal spines were best for complete agreement overall (80.6%). For fish under eight years, they reached 95.5%.
- Branchiostegal rays gave the most consistent results for fish ≥8 years (54.6%).
- Scales had only 59.8% complete agreement overall and 20.0% for fish ≥8 years.
- Pectoral fin rays were the least consistent structure overall (48.9%, less than scales).

**Statistical comparisons**
- Student's t-tests showed mean ages were mostly not significantly different between readers. Only vertebrae (for ages ≥8 and all ages) and pectoral fin rays (for ages <8 and ≥8) differed significantly.
- Regression analysis (Appendix 1) found that slopes for pelvic rays, opercles, branchiostegal rays and otoliths did not differ significantly from 1. Otoliths, branchiostegal rays and perhaps opercles were most consistent.
- Opercles and otoliths showed the best between-structure agreement, with r² = 0.95, although the sample was small.

**Agreement between scale readers**
- Four scale readers and a fifth reader from an outside aging agency were compared on 35 walleye.
- Complete agreement was 18.0% for all ages and 3.8% for fish ≥8 years. Agreement within ±4 years was 100% in both cases.
- Including the outside agency, complete agreement for all ages fell to 14.3%.
- ANOVA showed no significant difference among readers for fish <8 years (F = 2.5). Significant differences appeared for fish ≥8 years (F = 20.34) and for all samples combined (F = 13.34). The outside agency assigned ages up to 6 years away from the other readers' means.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
1. In an unexploited population such as Wapun Lake, scales appear to underestimate the ages of older walleye.
2. Annuli are clearer in sections of opercles, dorsal spines, otoliths, branchiostegal rays and vertebrae.
3. Pelvic and pectoral fin ray sections appear unreliable for older fish.
4. Dorsal fin spines are recommended for practical reasons: they are easy to collect, and removing only the second spine may allow the fish to be released alive.
5. Scales should be used only after their ages have been validated against corresponding bony-structure ages.

**Methodological guidance (Appendices 3)**
- Sections were about 0.30 mm wide, cut on a Thin Sectioning Machine. Three to four sections were taken per structure.
- Dorsal spines should be sectioned about 1/8 inch from the base, using the second spine.
- Vertebrae should be sectioned sagittally, and otoliths transversely through the centrum. Saturating otoliths in glycerine before embedding may reduce them falling out of the section.
