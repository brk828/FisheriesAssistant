# Cuatro Ciénegas: A New World for Biologists

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This early account by [[D. W. Taylor]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] introduces the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], a small, closed desert basin in central [[Coahuila]], Mexico, as a biologically exceptional site. Despite its modest size (about 20 by 30 miles), the basin combines a rich, largely endemic aquatic fauna with long evolutionary isolation. The authors argue that the area offers a natural laboratory for studies of ecology, evolution and biogeography, and that conservation under a multiple-use plan is urgently needed because canal construction and water diversion threaten the fauna.

## Background & Description

The town of Cuatro Ciénegas lies about 150 airline miles from the Big Bend of Texas. Until recently, road access was very poor, and the area had attracted neither mining nor tourism. Biological exploration began with [[E. G. Marsh Jr.]], who collected vertebrates in 1939 during a survey of northern Coahuila. His material was studied late, owing to [[World War II]] and other circumstances. [[Carl L. Hubbs]] recognized unusual fishes but did not describe them at that time. The first new animal named from the area was the [[Coahuilan Box Turtle]] (1944), later found to be unusual in the genus *Terrapene* for its aquatic habits, with algae growing on its shell.

Visually, the valley appears to be a typical creosote-bush desert. Its springs, ponds, lakes and streams, however, differ strongly in chemistry and habitat, and several drainage systems are present. The authors compare its spring landscape with [[Ash Meadows]], Nevada.

The authors note that published records cover only a few of the known vertebrates and almost none of the invertebrates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Desert perennial springs usually support only one or two fish species, several insects, sometimes a shrimp and one or two snails. The Cuatro Ciénegas basin is an exception. It has about 20 fish species in 8 families and 13 genera, half of them found nowhere else. Of about 20 snail species, only one was known before the current collecting. The authors describe the aquatic fauna as isolated ("islanded") but not impoverished, with representatives of nearly all families known from northern Mexico occupying most of the regional ecological roles.

The large warm springs rise into clear pools called [[Lagunas]], with soft ooze and broken shells over mud, reaching 20 to 30 feet deep at travertine-lined "pipes." Water lilies (*Nymphaea*) grow in shallow areas, while leaves at greater depth remain submerged. Some lagunas are probably old, given a unique snail species.

Subterranean fauna is poorly known. The isopod genus *Speocirolana*, whose species are blind and unpigmented, is represented by a species known from one specimen. One underground stream held at least eight fish species, and an isolated stream lies 114 miles from the nearest surface water. None of the fishes examined showed subterranean adaptations.

Water chemistry varies widely. One *Cyprinodon* species tolerates temperatures from 8 to 42 °C and dissolved solids from 0.2 to 15 g/L. Irrigation water for local farming comes from a mountain stream, because the springs holding the unique fauna are too mineralized.

Major threats include canal construction, which drains marshes, lowers spring water levels and may admit non-native species, as happened on the [[Galápagos Islands]], in [[Hawaii]] and in [[Australia]].

## Key Findings

- **Endemic fishes:** About 20 fish species occur in the basin, and half are endemic. Representatives of nearly all northern Mexican families are present.
- **Endemic mollusks:** Preliminary collections yielded five new snail genera found only in the basin. Three of these are placed in three new subfamilies, a concentration of high-rank endemics unmatched in the Western Hemisphere, comparable to [[Lake Tanganyika]] and [[Lake Baikal]].
- **Antiquity:** Following the assumption that taxonomic rank reflects isolation time, the authors suggest that some snail lineages date back to the Tertiary or possibly the Mesozoic.
- **Sympatric speciation in cichlids:** The genus *Cichlasoma* has four species in the basin. *C. cyanoguttatum* is widespread in the Rio Grande drainage, while three new species are endemic. They differ in feeding: one crushes snails, one feeds on soft detritus and one preys on fishes. The authors infer local evolution into adjacent niches.
- **Banded spring snails:** Snails from seven localities represent six distinct types. Differences are localized and not correlated with geographic distance; two sites a quarter-mile apart differ markedly. The authors link this to the snails' restriction to the soft bottoms of large springs.
- **Pupfish hybridization:** Two *Cyprinodon* species occupy different habitats (thermal spring pools vs. marsh pools) and rarely hybridize naturally. Stable hybrid populations, with their own reproduction and behavior, have persisted in man-made canals for several years, which the authors compare to a hybrid-origin species.
- **Subterranean life:** Underground waters are barely studied. Only a single *Speocirolana* specimen is known from one unique species.
- **Conservation:** The authors call for multiple-use planning that protects representative habitats while allowing water development. They describe biological wealth as a renewable resource that grows with research, unlike minerals.
