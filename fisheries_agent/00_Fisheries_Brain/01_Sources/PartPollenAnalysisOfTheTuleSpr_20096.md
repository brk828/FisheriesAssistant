# Pollen Analysis of the Tule Springs Area, Nevada

#palynology #paleoecology #pleistocene-vegetation #mohave-desert

## Executive Summary

This Nevada State Museum Anthropological Paper (No. 13, Part 3, October 1967) by Peter J. Mehringer, Jr., reports a fossil and modern pollen study of the [[Tule Springs Site]] in the [[Las Vegas Valley]], southern Nevada. The arid sediments yielded pollen only sparsely, but the work reconstructs a sequence of vegetation change over Wisconsin and Recent time. It also includes a methodological guide for pollen analysis in bogless Southwestern settings and proposes a possible Pleistocene [[Woodland Corridor]] across the eastern [[Mohave Desert]].

## Background & Description

The study was supported by [[National Science Foundation]] grants and was a contribution of the [[Geochronology Laboratories]], University of Arizona. The paper has three broad parts:

- **Methods and theory:** the value of [[Pollen Analysis]] for [[Southwestern Archaeology]], sample collection, a chemical extraction procedure ("HCl swirl" followed by hydrofluoric acid treatment), and the interpretive limits of fossil pollen.
- **Study area and modern pollen rain:** vegetation zones from the lower Mohave Desert to bristlecone pine and alpine zones along the east slope of the [[Charleston Mountains]] and Spring Range, documented with 29 modern surface and spring samples (Table 3).
- **Fossil record:** pollen profiles from alluvium, a playa lake, organic spring mats and spring mounds at Tule Springs, with radiocarbon-based stratigraphic ages.

Pollen is extracted from roughly 50 g of sediment by treatment with hydrochloric acid, hydrofluoric acid, nitric acid and potassium hydroxide, then mounted on slides with basic fuchsin. Counts were made to at least 200 grains per sample.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Modern vegetation zones (elevation, as given in the text):**
- Lower Mohave Desert (150–1,220 m): [[Creosote Bush]], bursage, hop sage and saltbush; riparian species such as cottonwood and willow around springs.
- Higher Mohave Desert (1,220–1,800 m): [[Blackbush]], [[Joshua Tree]] and Mohave yucca on bajadas; sagebrush appears near 1,600 m.
- Juniper-Pinyon Woodland (1,800–2,200 m): pinyon pine and Utah juniper.
- Ponderosa Pine-White Fir (2,200–2,750 m): pine pollen exceeds 30% in this zone.
- Bristlecone Pine (2,750–3,500 m) and Alpine (above 3,500 m).

**Pollen behaviour and bias:**
- Wind-pollinated types dominate the record. Pine is strongly over-represented in deserts and large lakes because local pollen production is low; at [[Mono Lake]], pine pollen values resemble those of coniferous forest rather than the shrub-dominated shoreline.
- Local over-representation is common: cheno-am and low-spine Compositae (for example bursage) can exceed 80% of counts in desert grassland, reflecting edaphic and water-table conditions rather than regional climate.
- Preservation is poor in oxidizing alluvium. [[Populus]] pollen is fragile, and [[Juniper]] pollen is best preserved in aquatic settings.
- Cattail ([[Typha]]) tetrads suggest cooler, moister and less saline conditions. Greasewood ([[Sarcobatus]]) pollen may indicate high soil salinity.

**Dating context:** The fossil record covers roughly 70,000–50,000 years ago to the present. Unit ages in Table 4 range from 300–1,000 B.P. (Unit Gy) to 16,000–30,000 B.P. (Unit D).

## Key Findings

1. **Sample yield:** Of 225 fossil samples extracted, only 73 (32.4%) yielded enough pollen for a minimal 200-grain count. Unidentified types never exceeded 2.3% in a single sample.
2. **Unit Bg (Early Wisconsin/Altonian):** Sagebrush-dominated Great Basin Desert shifted to moister, cooler conditions with fir and pine expansion. The author estimates a 1,000–1,200 m lowering of vegetation zones, followed by a return to sagebrush desert.
3. **Unit D (buried lake sediments, about 31,300–22,000 B.P.):** Pine dominance with low but consistent [[Abies]] pollen suggests a vegetation lowering of at least 1,000 m. Fluctuating lake level, rather than climate alone, likely drove the replacement of pine by Typha.
4. **Unit E1 (about 14,000–13,000 B.P.):** Juniper pollen and Cupressaceae wood indicate junipers grew on the valley floor. *Shepherdia* and *Sium* pollen signal a southward vegetation extension.
5. **Around 12,000 B.P.:** A major change from juniper-sagebrush to sagebrush-shadscale, coinciding with the Two Creeks interstadial. Trend toward warmer and drier conditions from about 12,000 to 7,000 B.P.
6. **By about 7,000 B.P.:** Vegetation probably resembled the present lower Mohave Desert. Cooler, moister reversals are suggested at about 8,500–8,000 B.P. and 10,500–10,000 B.P., the latter tied to a Panamint Valley organic mat dated 10,435 ± 100 B.P.
7. **Minimum vegetation lowering:** At the Pleistocene maximum, a lowering of at least 1,000 m (about 3,300 ft) is the author's conservative estimate, based on juniper occurring at 703 m against a present lower limit of about 1,700 m. Surface-sample comparison suggests up to about 1,220 m.
8. **Pleistocene woodland corridor:** A mapped, roughly continuous woodland belt linking the Spring Range to the [[San Bernardino Mountains]] is proposed for about 22,000–12,500 B.P., supported by woodrat middens and juniper remains. The author stresses that this is a hypothesis requiring fossil or biogeographic confirmation.
9. **Methodological conclusions:** Double fixed-sum counting (excluding local types) can separate regional from local signals. Spring mound deposits are identified as the most promising untapped pollen source in southern Nevada, and a transect of modern samples from northern Idaho to southern California is recommended.
