# Anadromous Fish Law in the Pacific Northwest, 1979–1990: Decade Review of Salmon and Steelhead Policy

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This final issue of the [[Natural Resources Law Memo]] (Issue 50, August 1990), published by the [[Natural Resources Law Institute]] at Lewis and Clark Law School, reviews a decade of law and policy affecting the recovery of anadromous fish, principally [[Pacific Salmon]] and [[Steelhead Trout]], in the Pacific Northwest. The authors conclude that although anadromous fish law has grown substantially since 1979, the fish themselves are not clearly better off. Some runs have rebounded from 1970s lows, but upriver spring, summer, and fall runs, and many wild stocks, remain in serious trouble, and several stocks have gone extinct. The document argues that the [[Northwest Power Act]] and its [[Columbia Basin Fish and Wildlife Program]] failed to secure biologically based mainstem fish flows, and that the [[Endangered Species Act]] is likely to supersede them as the principal legal driver of change.

## Background & Description

The Memo covers species that migrate from mountain streams in Idaho to Alaskan waters and back. The authors describe anadromous fish as an indicator of aquatic ecosystem health, comparable to a canary in a mine. The issue is a retrospective and agenda-setting survey, organized into seven sections:

- **Section II:** Prospect of [[Endangered Species Act]] listing for Columbia Basin stocks.
- **Section III:** [[Indian Treaty Fishing Rights]], including the [[Boldt Decision]] and its habitat implications.
- **Section IV:** Development of the [[Columbia River Hydroelectric System]] and the [[Non-Treaty Storage Agreement]] with British Columbia Hydro.
- **Section V:** Evaluation of the [[Columbia Basin Fish and Wildlife Program]].
- **Section VI:** Analysis of [[California v. FERC]] (1990) on state fish-flow conditions.
- **Section VII:** Concluding observations on the future of anadromous fish law.

The issue ends with a bibliography of all 50 issues and a list of other relevant publications.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stock status.** The authors cite several data points on decline:

- Snake River spring chinook: Grande Ronde spawners fell from about 3,000 to fewer in 1989; Imnaha subbasin counts fell from about 1,500 to 412 in 1988.
- Salmon River summer chinook: 1989 count of 501 fish, against annual escapement averaging about 20,000 in the 1960s and 1970s.
- Lower Granite Dam: about 4,200 adult summer chinook passed in 1989, against a peak of nearly 31,000 twenty years earlier. Fall chinook counts were below 1,000 in most years from 1983 to 1989.
- Sacramento River winter chinook: the run stabilized near 2,000 spawners after two decades of decline, having once numbered some 117,000 spawners as late as 1969.
- Sockeye: Idaho-bound stocks are virtually extinct. Counts at Ice Harbor Dam fell from 127,600 in 1964 to 4 in 1989; Redfish Lake spawners were 26, 50, and 22 in recent years.
- Coho: wild runs above Bonneville Dam have essentially disappeared. Columbia tributary rearing capacity declined from 19 juveniles per pool to 4 by 1985.
- Oregon Trout estimated wild stocks in the basin at 2% of historic levels. BPA acknowledged that 13 Columbia Basin runs are in critical condition.

**Causes.** The authors attribute much of the coho decline to harvest management and hatchery operations rather than hydropower. Hydropower is identified as a major cause for upriver salmon, through cumulative juvenile and adult mortality across up to nine dams. A 1986 staff estimate put average annual system losses at 7–14 million fish, of which 5–11 million were attributed to hydropower.

**Habitat and flows.** Mainstem flow regime is central. Coordinated reservoir operation stores water in spring and summer for fall and winter power sales, while fish need high spring and summer flows for downstream migration and lower fall flows for upstream passage. Mainstem dam survival at the 90% standard is described as producing only about 40% of upriver Snake River smolts surviving to Bonneville Dam.

## Key Findings

1. **Stock decline persists despite legal growth.** Pacific salmon law has expanded significantly, but several runs have gone extinct and many others are depressed. The authors describe the national goal of doubling Columbia Basin runs over 20 years as distant.

2. **Endangered Species Act listing is likely.** NMFS listed the Sacramento River winter chinook as threatened in 1989–1990 under emergency rules, and designated critical habitat in April 1990. Petitions were filed for Snake River sockeye, Snake River spring/summer/fall chinook, and lower Columbia coho. The authors judge that further listings are probable.

3. **Indian treaty rights are a key protection.** The [[Boldt Decision]] (1974, affirmed 1979 by the Supreme Court) established a moderate-living standard and tribal shares of the harvestable fish. Later rulings extended protection to habitat. The authors note tribal treaty rights as a significant regional constraint on development.

4. **The Columbia Basin Program failed on flows.** The program's "Water Budget" was never fully implemented on the Snake River and supplied less than half its intended amount. It provided enough water for only about 8–10 days of flow across a migration of more than 3 months. On The Dalles Dam, 1990 flows of 176,000 cfs fell short of the 220,000 cfs the tribal biologists consider the minimum necessary.

5. **Institutional structure favors power over fish.** The Northwest Power Planning Council, the Bonneville Power Administration, and the Corps of Engineers have consistently prioritized power production. The Council rejected fishery-agency proposals and placed excessive proof burdens on the agencies and tribes. The authors argue that the "co-equal partnership" the Act promised has not materialized.

6. **Spill agreement and BPA's Non-Treaty Storage Agreement.** A 1988 Fish Spill Memorandum of Agreement, reached after a lawsuit, provided a ten-year spill program at four Corps dams. BPA decided in June 1990 to proceed with its Non-Treaty Storage Agreement without guaranteed fish flows, but it agreed to an 80-day negotiation period.

7. **The Fishery Agencies' Flow Proposal (March 1990).** The Columbia Basin Fish and Wildlife Authority proposed a comprehensive flow regime with flows treated as "hard constraints," plus full fishery representation in Coordination Agreement planning. The authors expect these flows to be central in ESA proceedings and non-treaty storage negotiations.

8. **California v. FERC (1990).** The Supreme Court unanimously upheld FERC's preemption of state minimum-flow conditions for the Rock Creek project, relying on [[First Iowa Hydroelectric Cooperative v. FPC]] (1946). The authors argue the decision may be narrower than it first appears, and that state conditions protecting proprietary water rights may survive. They urge an amendment to the Federal Power Act.

9. **Wildlife mitigation.** In late 1989 the Council adopted a basin-wide wildlife mitigation process with an interim goal of mitigating about 35% of lost wildlife habitat over 10 years.

10. **Recommendation for the future.** The authors call for biologically justified mainstem flows to be treated as a cost the region can afford, with the Council and state energy offices, not BPA, leading cost-effective implementation. They conclude that the ESA is the likely means to break institutional inertia and restructure hydropower operations around fish.
