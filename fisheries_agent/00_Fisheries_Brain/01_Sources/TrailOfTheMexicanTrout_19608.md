# Trail of the Mexican Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This first-person account, written by the author (named in the running head as Dean Hendrickson), traces decades of efforts to find, collect, and transplant trout from mainland and peninsular Mexico. Beginning with reports by [[Edward Cope]] (1886) and [[Seth Meek]] (1904), the narrative centers on [[Nelson's Trout]] (*Salmo nelsoni*), first collected by [[E. W. Nelson]] from the [[Rio Santo Domingo]] in Baja California. Two attempts to bring these fish into hatchery culture in the United States ended in losses. The author then documents later reconnaissance trips across the [[Sierra Madre Occidental]], which yielded rainbow-type trout from many drainages, a new golden trout form, and trout from the [[Rio Casas Grandes]]. The article closes with a hypothesis for how Mexican trout reached their current isolated populations. A short, unrelated piece on [[Meteor Crater]] appears at the end of the document.

## Background & Description

- **Early reports:** Dr. Edward Cope reported trout in high streams of southern Chihuahua in 1886. Seth Meek reported trout from the head of the Rio Yaqui and Pacific-draining streams west of Durango in 1904. E. W. Nelson observed trout at the headwaters of the Rio Sinaloa and Rio del Presidio in 1898.
- **Type collection:** Nelson's 1,000-mile horseback trek down the Baja California peninsula yielded the first specimens from the Rio Santo Domingo, at Rancho San Antonio about 35 miles above the mouth and about 160 miles south of Ensenada. [[Barton Evermann]] described the species as *Salmo nelsoni* in 1908.
- **Taxonomic placement:** All Mexican trout collected to that point belonged to the rainbow trout series.
- **Research motivations:** The author lists three reasons for interest in southern trout:
  1. The evolutionary and biogeographic question of how salmonids spread southward from northern centers.
  2. A hypothesis that Mexican rainbows might be less migratory than Pacific Coast rainbows and less prone to moving downstream after hatchery planting.
  3. A hypothesis that Mexican rainbows might tolerate higher water temperatures than native U.S. stocks.
- **Fish appearance (Rio Casas Grandes and Yaqui fish):** Heavily spotted, with orange-tipped dorsal fins, orange-and-white-tipped pelvic and anal fins, a strong rainbow stripe, and dark vertical parr marks in smaller individuals. They lacked the basibranchial (hyoid) teeth characteristic of cutthroat trout.
- **Mexican golden trout:** The Rio Verde fish were described as golden on the ventral surface and opercles, with the color fading dorsally, and were heavily spotted and deep-bodied. The author compares them to the California golden trout, *Salmo agua bonita*, but notes they lack its brilliant coloration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat conditions:** Trout were found in high mountain streams, barrancas, and headwater tributaries. Collection sites include Rancho San Antonio on the Santo Domingo, the Rio Hondo, the Rio Tabacatiado (a tributary of the Presidio), and the Rio Truchas near San Miguel, Durango. A site on the Rio Chinatu near Turguachic, Chihuahua, is at an elevation of 6,200 feet; the upper Rio Verde (Arroyo Pedernales) camp is at 7,500 feet; Arroyo Soldado, south of Guadalupe y Calvo, is at 7,500 feet.
- **Latitude contrast:** The Santo Domingo lies at about 31° N latitude, while the Rio del Presidio enters the Pacific at about 24° N. The author describes the Santo Domingo as a northern stream in comparison.
- **Barriers:** Desert barriers and warm ocean areas would now prevent trout movement from the Santo Domingo around Cape San Lucas, into west-coast Mexican streams, or into the Colorado River.
- **Water conditions during collecting:** High, muddy water from summer rains hampered collecting on the Rio Hondo and Rio Truchas. Sampling on the Rio Verde was carried out during low water before afternoon rains.
- **Regional geography:** Guadalupe y Calvo is a mining town about 75 miles southwest of Parral. Nearby Mount Mohinora rises to 10,335 feet and forms the headwaters of several major drainages, including the Yaqui, Fuerte, Verde, Sinaloa, Culiacán, and Nazas.
- **Drainage coverage:** By the final expedition, the author had trout from every major Pacific drainage from the Rio del Presidio north to and including the Rio Yaqui. The Rio Casas Grandes is the only eastward-flowing stream in Mexico from which trout were collected, and it was formerly connected with the Rio Grande.

## Key Findings

### Hatchery Introduction Attempts

- **1936 reconnaissance:** The team, including Fred W. Johnson of the U.S. Forest Service, found Nelson's trout abundant and easy to collect. They preserved about 30 specimens.
- **1937 transport:** The team collected 50 yearling specimens and delivered them alive to the Forest Home State Fish Hatchery near Redlands, California, after 36 hours of driving, with no losses. A photo caption states 55 yearling trout were transported, which differs from the 50 given in the text.
- **1938 loss:** A cloudburst in March 1938 on Mill Creek destroyed the Forest Home Hatchery and its ponds, including the Mexican trout.
- **1938 third trip:** The team returned in May and collected 325 fingerlings, 2 to 4 inches long, with no losses. They were placed at the Clackamas Fish Hatchery in Oregon.
- **Later loss:** About 40 survivors, 10 to 14 inches long and approaching breeding condition, died two years later. A frog blocked the 2-inch supply pipeline, and a loose standpipe drained the pond. The author states no one was at fault.

### Later Collections and Discoveries

- **1952 expedition (Durango):** Collectors Jack Lattin, Stan Weitzman, and Aurelio Solórzano of the Mexican Fisheries Division worked the Rio Hondo, Rio Tabacatiado, and Rio Truchas.
  - Rio Hondo: eight trout, 3 to 7 inches.
  - Rio Tabacatiado: 23 trout preserved, with none eaten to meet a minimum series quota of 30.
  - Rio Truchas: no fish caught by rod, seine, or chemicals; 12 trout were supplied from a local resident's freezer (6 to 8 inches), and his three sons caught six more (9 to 13 inches).
- **Rio Verde golden trout:** Lattin and Weitzman collected 12 specimens of a new Mexican golden trout from a Rio Verde tributary. Flechsig and Moller later collected from three headwater tributaries of the Rio Verde. Based on studies of about 40 characters, the author concludes the Rio Verde form is new to science and was being described at the time of writing.
- **Flechsig and Moller dates:** The text places this work in 1953 in one passage and in summer 1958 in another.
- **Rio Yaqui and Rio Casas Grandes (latest expedition):** Bob Rupp and the author collected trout from the headwaters of both the Rio Yaqui and the Rio Casas Grandes, closing the last geographic gap in their collections.

### Taxonomic Conclusion on Casas Grandes Trout

- Trout from the Rio Casas Grandes were historically thought to be cutthroats (*Salmo virginalis*) in the United States.
- The author found them to be rainbows, lacking basibranchial teeth and matching the rainbows from the Yaqui headwaters at Black Canyon.
- The author suggests Casas Grandes and Yaqui populations may result from stocking of transplanted forms, given early English settlement and known trout planting in the region.
- The author considers Nelson's trout from the Santo Domingo and the bulk of trout from the Culiacán, Sinaloa, and Verde drainages to be normal, natural populations little affected by fish culture.

### Biogeographic Hypothesis

- The author adopts the theory first proposed by [[Barton Evermann]] in 1908, later elaborated by other workers. Salmonids are thought to have originated in cold Northern Hemisphere waters and were circumpolar.
- Southward movement into Mexico would have occurred through sea-run (steelhead-type) migration during glacial periods, when ocean cooling allowed passage.
- As the glaciers retreated and the ocean and lower streams warmed, populations were landlocked in cold upper stream reaches. Non-migratory relict groups were isolated in the middle and upper courses of Pacific-draining streams in the Sierra Madre Occidental.
- Supporting points include:
  - A relict Pacific salmon, *Oncorhynchus formosanum*, on the island of Formosa, far south of the salmon's main range.
  - Trout collected above high falls that would be impassable to upstream migration today, suggesting they arrived before geological uplift or erosion changed access.
- The author notes that the Colorado River cutthroat (*Salmo clarkii pleuriticus*) probably reached Colorado River headwaters by stream capture from the east slope.

### Conclusions

- The author views the introduction attempts as failures, but believes trout from mainland Mexico offer greater promise for a hardier, non-migratory rainbow.
- The article ends with the hope that trout would be exchanged between the United States and Mexico for their mutual benefit.

### Unrelated Appended Piece: Meteor Crater

- A short, separate article describes [[Meteor Crater]] in northeastern Arizona, formed by an impact perhaps 50,000 years ago.
- The crater is more than three-fourths of a mile in diameter and 570 feet deep. The surrounding ridge rises 150 feet above the plain and is visible for 20 miles.
- The rim is 5.5 miles by paved road from Meteor City (population 2) on U.S. 66.
