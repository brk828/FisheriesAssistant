# Trichoptera of a Stony Stream: Caddisfly Life Histories and Distribution in Ford Wood Beck

#entomology #freshwater-ecology #trichoptera

## Executive Summary

This paper, by [[Jean C. Mackereth]] of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]], reports the caddisfly (Trichoptera) fauna of [[Ford Wood Beck]], a small stony moorland stream that drains into [[Blelham Tarn]] north-west of [[Windermere]]. Monthly collections over three years at five main stations, plus one year at a sixth, recorded 15 species excluding the Limnephilidae. The study is based on preserved material from a survey whose primary aim was the mayflies (Ephemeroptera). It describes emergence periods, life-cycle lengths and the upstream-downstream distribution of the larvae, and it documents an unusual overwintering in the pupal case by [[Rhyacophila Dorsalis]].

## Background & Description

Ford Wood Beck is a stony stream with a bed that is stony almost throughout its length. Station 1 and station 2 sit on the lower reach, which runs through a valley, and the stream there is nearly 2 m wide and 10–20 cm deep. Flow rises sharply after heavy rain. A steeper gradient above the valley leads to station 3, and station 4 lies just below a spring that has not been known to dry up in 50 years. The main beck drains a plateau through underground channels. Two tributaries were sampled: [[Sykeside]] (station Ss), which is dry in drought and joins just below station 4, and Outgate (station Og), which has slower flow and rises in a bog.

Sampling used a pond net for ten-minute collections. Stations 1, 2, 3, Ss and Og were visited monthly for three years, and station 4 monthly for one year. In 1952 a cage placed near station 1 caught emerging adults. Live animals were sorted in the field and all but the Ephemeroptera were preserved. The author notes that a study focused on Trichoptera would have needed collecting periods longer than ten minutes, since caddisflies were less numerous than mayflies.

Identification was a major constraint. Breeding of undescribed larvae was needed to link larvae to known adults. All British species in three genera were described in earlier work, but identification of larvae in the family [[Limnephilidae]] could not be reached reliably because of the number of species. Specimens of [[Hydropsyche]] were named by Miss R. M. Badcock.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The stream community changes along its length. Stations 1, 2 and 3 on the main beck form a fairly uniform lower-reach assemblage. Above that, the fauna becomes distinctly sparser, and only six of the 15 species were found at station 4, Ss and Og. The author notes that this contrast is larger than those reported for the Plecoptera (stoneflies, [[Mackereth 1957]]) and the Ephemeroptera ([[Macan 1957]]).

Several species show clear habitat preferences:

- [[Hydropsyche Fulvipes]], [[Hydropsyche Instabilis]] and [[Diplectrona Felix]] occurred only in the main stream. Hydropsyche fulvipes was most common at station 1 and H. instabilis at station 3, while D. felix was concentrated at station 4.
- [[Plectrocnemia Conspersa]] increased upstream, particularly at Sykeside and Outgate, where hydropsychids were absent.
- [[Wormaldia Occipitalis]] was markedly more abundant at Outgate than elsewhere.
- Cased species such as [[Silo Pallipes]], [[Sericostoma personatum]], [[Odontocerum albicorne]] and [[Glossosoma boltoni]] were scarce and found mainly in the main stream. Unidentified limnephilids were sparse in the main stream but abundant at Sykeside in some months.
- [[Agapetus Fuscipes]] was the most abundant species and was most numerous at Outgate, with fair numbers at stations 1 and 2 and few at Sykeside and station 3.

Parasitism was recorded: the ichneumon fly [[Agriotypus Armatus]] was found parasitising Silo pallipes, averaging one specimen per month in 1952.

## Key Findings

**Community composition**
- Fifteen caddisfly species were recorded, excluding the Limnephilidae, listed by abundance. Totals across the three years include 4,593 larvae and 411 pupae of Agapetus fuscipes, and 334 larvae and 143 pupae of Silo pallipes.
- Limnephilids totalled about 464 larvae and 15 pupae, but could not be named to species.
- Uncased species included Plectrocnemia conspersa (374 larvae, 13 pupae), Wormaldia occipitalis (369 larvae, 2 pupae), Hydropsyche instabilis (94 larvae), Diplectrona felix (76 larvae), Rhyacophila dorsalis (72 larvae, 21 pupae), Hydropsyche fulvipes (63 larvae), Rhyacophila obliterata (17 larvae) and Philopotamus montanus (10 larvae, 1 pupa).
- Several species were rare, with one to four specimens in total, including Glossosoma vernale and Crunoecia irrorata (one larva each).

**Life cycles and emergence**
- Agapetus fuscipes emerged from early summer to autumn and completed one generation per year.
- Silo pallipes also had a one-year life cycle. Pupae were found for only three months in each of the two seasons, and a one-month lag between pupae and adults suggests about one month as a pupa.
- Plectrocnemia conspersa showed all size groups every month, so its life history could not be determined. Pupae were found in May and adults in September, which may indicate a four- to five-month egg-laying period.
- Wormaldia occipitalis suggested a two-year life cycle: larvae hatch in late summer, grow little in the first winter, grow through the following summer, pause in the second winter, pupate in late spring and emerge from August. This was the fourth-largest group among the uncased species.
- Rhyacophila dorsalis emerged from early spring to late autumn. A quantity of pupal-case larvae found in late October in the [[River Brathay]] overwintered in their cases and metamorphosed from early spring, with the first adult emerging in early June. The author offers two possible explanations, both involving delayed development.
- Rhyacophila obliterata emerged in autumn, mainly from station 1.

**Growth and sampling notes**
- In 1950–51, Agapetus larvae appeared to grow steadily through winter, with no 1–2 mm larvae after December. In 1951–52 growth seemed nearly absent in winter, and 1–2 mm larvae persisted until April, for which no reason was found.
- Many more Agapetus larvae were collected in 1950–51 than in 1951–52. A check by Macan (1957) indicated this was not caused by over-collecting.
- Breeding data showed that only a small share of collected larvae can be reared to adults, so comparisons with other streams based on bred adult lists were judged unreliable.

**Conclusions**
- Caddisfly composition in Ford Wood Beck shows a clear upstream decline in species richness, with six of 15 species at the three upper sites.
- Life-cycle lengths differ among species, from one year (Agapetus, Silo) to two years (Wormaldia, suggested), with at least one case of prolonged pupal overwintering.
