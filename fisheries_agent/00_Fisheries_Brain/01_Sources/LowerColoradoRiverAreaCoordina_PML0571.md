# Lower Colorado River Native Fish Research: Symposium Overview, Upper Basin Trend Zone Monitoring, and Prehistoric Weir Fishing at Lake Cahuilla

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document has three parts. The first is a symposium front page and area coordinator report from the Lower Colorado Region of the Water and Power Resources Service (Boulder City, Nevada), which summarizes ongoing and planned research on several native fishes of the lower Colorado River. The second is a conference abstract on the Colorado Division of Wildlife's trend zone monitoring of two endangered fishes in the upper Colorado River basin. The third is an archaeological paper (with a Spanish-language abstract) on prehistoric weir fishing at [[Lake Cahuilla]] in the [[Salton Basin]]. Together they address endangered and imperiled native species, their distribution and reproduction, and the long-term ecological history of Colorado River fish fauna.

## Background & Description

The symposium front matter introduces native fishes of the lower Colorado River that are the subject of current research. Most information was to be presented in eight individual symposium papers, with additional data available in two reports from the Water and Power Resources Service and in research proposals under review by the Fish and Wildlife Service, National Park Service, and Water and Power Resources Service.

The species highlighted include:

- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*): Four papers concerned this species. Its status is tied to proposed agricultural development near the Salton Sea, California, and steps were initiated to consider listing it under the Federal List of Threatened and Endangered Wildlife.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Growing interest in its future in the lower Colorado River area. A paper by Terry McCall (Arizona Game and Fish Department) covers recent observations and collections in [[Lake Mead]].
- **[[Bluehead Mountain Sucker]]** (*Catostomus discobolus*): Two reports by C. R. Smith and R. K. Koehn on the Zuni Mountain sucker in New Mexico and Arizona recommended that it be classified as a subspecies of the bluehead mountain sucker.

Other symposium papers covered fishes in Grand Canyon National Park (C. O. Minckley, Museum of Northern Arizona), the mainstream Virgin River (James E. Deacon et al., University of Nevada, Las Vegas), and the Upper Bill Williams Drainage (Bill Kepner, Arizona State University).

The coordinator report notes that a cooperative one-year study of razorback distribution and movement in Senator Wash Reservoir, California, was planned to begin in January 1980 by the California Department of Fish and Game, Bureau of Land Management, and Water and Power Resources Service. A University of Nevada, Las Vegas proposal to study razorback biology in [[Lake Mohave]] was under review.

The upper basin abstract describes the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) trend zone monitoring program, begun in July 1977 by the Colorado Division of Wildlife with Colorado State University's Larval Fish Laboratory and regional Division of Wildlife staff.

The prehistoric paper by Philip J. Wilke (Department of Anthropology, University of California, Riverside) describes stone fish weirs and associated fish remains. Bones of *Xyrauchen texanus* and *Gila elegans* (the [[Bonytail]]) were recovered from house ruins at the site.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Upper Colorado River trend zones.** Monitoring covers five trend zones that together track the distribution and status of two endangered species:

1. Yampa River from Maybell to Sunbeam, plus special sites at Juniper Springs, Cross Mountain Canyon, and Lily Park
2. White River from Rio Blanco Lake to the County Road 65 Bridge
3. Colorado River from Palisade to the old Clifton Bridge
4. Gunnison River from Whitewater to Redlands Diversion Dam
5. Colorado River from Loma to the Colorado-Utah State Line

Generalized habitat types were identified at each collection site and sampled as independently as possible. Fish were collected mainly by seine, dipnet, and electrofishing, with hook and line occasionally effective for squawfish and humpback chub. Habitat preferences of early life-history stages and reproductive success are central objectives. [[Ruby Canyon]], including the Black Rocks area, was an important location for captures.

**Lake Cahuilla ecology.** Lake Cahuilla formed intermittently during the upper Pleistocene and Holocene when Colorado River inflow filled the Salton Basin for extended periods. The resulting lake was over 175 km long and overflowed at an elevation of +12.7 m across the Colorado River delta into the Gulf of California. It maintained a stable freshwater lake with a fish fauna resembling that of the Lower Colorado River. Rerouting of the river at the head of its delta around A.D. 1500 ended the most recent stand of the lake. It then dried by evaporation, apparently as a single progressive decline over about 55 to 60 years.

## Key Findings

**Research and conservation priorities (symposium front matter)**
- Desert pupfish research is linked to proposed agricultural development near the Salton Sea, and listing consideration was initiated.
- Razorback sucker interest is increasing, with studies planned or proposed for Senator Wash Reservoir and Lake Mohave, and recent collections reported from Lake Mead.
- Zuni Mountain sucker studies recommended reclassifying it as a subspecies of the bluehead mountain sucker.

**Upper basin trend zone results (August 1979 and program to date)**
- On 24 August 1979, two adult Colorado squawfish (lengths partly unreadable in the scan) and one 79 mm yearling were collected 2 km above Black Rocks in Ruby Canyon. This indicates limited reproductive success in the area for two successive years.
- Two humpback chub yearlings were collected at Black Rocks in August 1979, indicating recent reproductive success for this species.
- A federal endangered fish tagging program, coordinated by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, was incorporated in 1979:
  - Yampa River trend zone: 14 Colorado squawfish and one sucker tagged. The scan reads "humpback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)," but that binomial belongs to the razorback sucker, so the common name is probably a transcription error.
  - Lower Colorado River trend zone: 29 squawfish and 66 humpback chub tagged, mostly from the Black Rocks area in Ruby Canyon.

**Prehistoric weir fishing at Lake Cahuilla**
- Evidence of prehistoric weir fishing was found on the recessional shorelines of Lake Cahuilla.
- The west-side archaeological site includes some 650 stone weirs built in 15 levels at elevations from -7.8 m to 29.7 m. Each level represents a separate construction episode.
- Successive weir levels differ in elevation by a little over 1.5 m, slightly less than the measured annual evaporative water loss from the nearby Salton Sea.
- Weirs are U- or V-shaped stone structures, one to several courses high, with openings about 0.5 m wide at the apexes. They resemble brush weirs used historically by Lower Colorado River tribes.
- Weirs were probably used with dip nets placed at the apex openings to catch schooling fish that entered along the shore. Their regular level spacing indicates annual construction at the same time each year, during a very short period.
- Fish remains from house ruins identify *Xyrauchen texanus* and *Gila elegans* as the probable species taken. The interpretation suggests the fish were spawning in quiet water within the weirs and that construction occurred in March and April.
- The ecological conditions attracting fish to the rocky shore lasted only about one to one and one-half months each year.
- Decline of the weir fishery is linked to the demise of the native fish fauna, probably caused by increasing salinity or other unfavorable conditions as Lake Cahuilla receded.
