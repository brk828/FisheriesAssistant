# Visceral Retia Mirabilia of Tuna and Sharks: Annotated Translation of Eschricht & Müller (1835)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #scombridae

## Executive Summary

This Guelph Ichthyology Reviews volume (Number 4, February 1996) by [[Douglas Fudge]] and [[E. Don Stevens]] provides an annotated English translation of the 1835 paper by [[Johannes Müller]] and D.F. [[Eschricht]] on the visceral anatomy of the [[Atlantic Bluefin Tuna]] (*Thunnus thynnus*), the first comprehensive description of visceral retia mirabilia in a fish. The volume adds a translation of the authors' supplement on the [[Common Thresher Shark]] (*Alopias vulpinus*), translations of two papers and abstracts by [[John Davy]] on the first body-temperature measurements of warm fishes, plates from [[Kishinouye]] (1923), dissection photographs, and facsimiles of the original German texts. The editors argue that the thresher and tuna possess analogous visceral circulations, implying that endothermy evolved independently at least twice within elasmobranch and bony fish lineages.

## Background & Description

The volume is a scholarly review rather than a field study. Its core is the translation of Müller's report, presented to the Berlin Academy of Sciences on 29 June 1835, which describes the liver and gut vasculature of a tuna sent from Montpellier and a fresh male tuna of roughly 180 kg (270 cm total length) received in Copenhagen. Eschricht's contributions, built on injected specimens prepared by Mr. Ibsen, form a major section.

Key anatomical features described in the original text:

- **Liver radiating vessels:** Large venous reservoirs on the convex liver surface give rise to radiating liver vein branches; the largest are 1–2 mm in diameter.
- **Visceral retia:** Five major retia (right-side stomach, caecum-stomach, left stomach near pylorus, caecum, spleen-intestine) connect the digestive veins to the liver through the hepatic portal vein. Retia are 5 cm long and 13–51 mm thick; the largest contain hundreds of vessels.
- **Dual arterial supply:** Eschricht found that some retial vessels are arterial, fed by the coeliac artery and leading to the digestive organs, so the retia have a double connection to the liver and the gut.
- **Thick-walled artery:** A large, nearly cartilaginous artery in the hepatic portal area supplies both the liver and the digestive retia.
- **Enlarged visceral nerves:** Nerves accompanying the visceral vessels are unusually large and pass through and branch within the retia.
- **Kidney and ureter:** The tuna kidneys lie over the anterior body cavity, with an unpaired ureter unknown in other fishes.

Müller's historical contribution is substantial, but the editors note that he and Eschricht never linked retia to endothermy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The volume emphasises the comparative physiology of warm fishes. The editors organise research into four questions: how warm fishes are, how they generate and maintain heat, what adaptive advantages they gain, and how endothermy evolved.

Comparative anatomy distinguishes the three warm species by retial position relative to the liver:

- **Tuna and thresher:** Retia lie between the liver and the digestive organs, so the liver is outside the warm circuit and is cold relative to the viscera. Temperature differences of up to 6 °C between liver and caecum are cited from [[Carey et al. 1984]].
- **Porbeagle ([[Porbeagle Shark]], *Lamna nasus*):** Suprahepatic retia lie between the liver and the heart, so the liver is warm, roughly equal to the other viscera ([[Carey et al. 1981]]).

The radiating liver structure appears only in fishes whose livers are outside the warm retial circuit. The authors hypothesise that these vessels act as a crude countercurrent rete conserving liver metabolic heat.

Conservation concerns frame the volume. Müller's bluefin were obtained under a special government permit, the species is now endangered, and the giant bluefin is prized in sashimi markets where single fish reach US $50 000. Large sharks are described as subject to largely unregulated harvest.

The editors also discuss the taxonomic confusion of 1835 nomenclature. A table in the volume lists 22 outdated species names and 20 outdated genera with their modern equivalents, along with the caveat that these are approximate guides.

## Key Findings

- **Historical priority:** [[John Davy]] (1835) reported a bonito (*Thynnus pelamys*) muscle temperature of 37.2 °C in water at 26.9 °C, a difference of 10.2 °C. This is the first scientific report of elevated body temperature in a fish. Davy's data are taken to refer to skipjack tuna, and the editors note that his bonito identification is questionable.
- **Davy's 1841 measurements:** Davy recorded thermometer readings of 23.9 °C in muscle and 22.8 °C in the abdomen of Pelamides (*Sarda sarda* as named by Davy) in water near 16.7 °C at depth, roughly 7.1 °C above the medium.
- **Müller's visceral retia:** The tuna has five major visceral retia that mediate the liver's portal supply. Eschricht showed that retial vessels also carry arterial blood, creating a double connection between the digestive organs and the liver.
- **Convergent systems:** Eschricht & Müller's descriptions suggest three convergent visceral systems supporting endothermy: tuna, thresher and porbeagle. Thresher retia are analogous to tuna retia in position relative to the liver.
- **Thresher identification:** A liver with radiating vessels, previously of uncertain origin, is assigned in the supplement to *Alopias vulpinus*, which the supplement shows also possesses visceral retia.
- **Nomenclature:** Squalus vulpes was reassigned to the new genus *Alopecias* (now *Alopias*), a name that today is a valid synonym-corrected genus.
- **Countercurrent heat exchange:** Müller's comments on blood flow and the function of retia anticipate the countercurrent mechanism, which was not elucidated for over a century.
- **Bypass question:** The original text suggests the bluefin lacks a vascular bypass of the gut retia, unlike the porbeagle, a point the editors plan to test in a subsequent publication.
- **Nerve size:** Visceral nerves in tuna are described as among the largest in the animal world. Davy compared branchial nerves in tuna with the electric nerves of the torpedo, and the editors confirmed in dissections that they are roughly an order of magnitude larger than analogous mammalian nerves.
- **Wider significance:** The editors conclude that Müller's and Eschricht's work "continues to have real scientific value," and that the 1835 papers remain a foundation for research on warm fishes.
