# The Recent Terrestrial Gastropoda of Oklahoma: Pupillidae, Carychiidae, Strobilopsidae and Oligyridae

#malacology #gastropoda #pupillidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic revision covers the terrestrial (land) snails of Oklahoma in four families: [[Pupillidae]], [[Carychiidae]], [[Strobilopsidae]] and [[Oligyridae]]. It is the terrestrial companion to a paper on the state's freshwater snails. The author reports that Oklahoma has a large land snail fauna, with 15 families, 38 genera, 95 species and 40 subspecies recorded since 1884. Many of the recorded names are misidentifications or synonyms, and the paper proposes dropping a number of subspecies and varieties. The fauna has both eastern and western components, with the eastern influence stronger. Several taxa are known only from Pleistocene or Pliocene fossils.

## Background & Description

The paper begins with a comparison of regional richness. Kansas has about 65 species, while Texas has upwards of 200 forms. This fits the general pattern of more species toward the tropics and subtropics.

Land snails are tightly tied to moisture. They are active only at night or during precipitation, and they aestivate in drought, closing the shell with an epiphragm. Dispersal is mostly by the snails' own movement across slime tracts. Barriers include mountain ranges, dry areas, rivers, oceans and areas lacking limestone for [[Calciphilic Species]]. The author stresses one dispersal route for the Great Plains: flood debris. During near-annual floods, logs and bark carry living snails into streams. When the water recedes, the debris forms drift piles. Because most land snails are hermaphroditic, a single transport event could establish a new population.

Taxonomic groups are defined largely by shell features. The paper describes apertural armature, including folds (plicae) and lamellae, which are the key characters for separating [[Pupillidae]] and [[Strobilopsidae]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biogeography.** The Oklahoma fauna has eastern affinities, most strongly in the Ozarkian region, especially the Carolinian area set off by the [[Neosho River]]. Some eastern species likely arrived by Tertiary migration from the Appalachians. A southeastern element entered via the Austroriparian zone of McCurtain County. Tropical and subtropical intrusions include *Oligyra orbiculata* (Oligyridae) and *Bulimulus dealbatus*. Western forms, such as *Gastrocopta cristata*, *Pupilla* and *Columella*, reached the state by another route. Living *Pupilla* may survive as Pleistocene relicts near the [[Black Mesa]] region of Cimarron County.

**Habitat and moisture.** Several species depend on wet microhabitats:
- *Vertigo milium* lives near springs and permanent water, even in arid counties. It can survive near-desiccation for several months by aestivation.
- *Vertigo ovata* is found near ponds, marshes and streams. It also occurs in crevices of porous Ozark soils where moisture is retained.
- *Carychium exiguum* is hydrophilic and found under logs, leaves or rocks near water. It occasionally enters water.
- *Gastrocopta tappaniana* is hydrophilic and occupies a different station from *G. pentodon*.

**Substrate and chemistry.** Many pupillids are [[Calciphilic Species]] (lime-dependent) and occur where limestone is available. *Vertigo ovata* appears to prefer a slightly basic to neutral pH. *Gastrocopta pentodon* prefers slightly alkaline soils. *Pupoides albilabris* is highly tolerant of hot, dry periods. It is the most widely distributed pupillid in Oklahoma, found near grass roots on the plains and under litter or rocks in the Ozarks.

**Fossil record.** Pleistocene and Pliocene deposits in central and western Oklahoma contain abundant fossil snails, especially *Gastrocopta*. The author advises collectors to treat dead shells with caution. Fossils include *Columella edentula* (the Oklahoma records are fossils), *Pupilla muscorum* and *Vertigo ventricosa* in some sources.

**Host relations.** *Vertigo ovata* (and possibly other species) can serve as an intermediate host for the rabbit trematodes *Hasstilesia tricolor* and *H. texensis*. These flukes parasitize *Sylvilagus* and *Lepus*.

## Key Findings

- **Diversity:** Oklahoma's land snail fauna includes 15 families, 38 genera, 95 species and 40 subspecies recorded since 1884. The four families treated here contain 25 pupillid taxa (about 18% of the state's terrestrial fauna), along with *Carychium*, *Strobilops*, *Oligyra* and *Columella* species.
- **Pupillidae** (5 genera in Oklahoma): *[[Vertigo Ovata]]*, *[[Vertigo Milium]]*, *[[Vertigo Tridentata]]*, *[[Vertigo Rugosula]]* and *[[Vertigo Ventricosa]]*. The genus *[[Gastrocopta Cristata]]* and related species are the most diverse, with 9 recent and 9 fossil *Gastrocopta* species.
- ***Pupoides albilabris*** is the most widespread pupillid in Oklahoma. It was previously recorded as *P. marginatus*, but that name is a homonym, so the replacement name *albilabris* by Adams (1841) is used.
- ***Pupilla blandi*** is the only Oklahoma *Pupilla* with three aperture teeth. Its living occurrence is uncertain, and most state records are of Pleistocene shells. *P. sinistra* was elevated from subspecies to full species by Hibbard and Taylor (1960).
- **Invalid taxa proposed for removal:**
  - *Vertigo ovata diaboli* (Pilsbry, 1919): the author expresses "grave doubt" about its validity, since its characters appear throughout the *V. ovata* range.
  - *Gastrocopta armifera abbreviata*: not a valid race (Franzen and Leonard, 1947).
  - *Gastrocopta procera* forms *mcclungi* and *sterkiana*: all three forms occur together, so they are considered invalid.
  - *Gastrocopta holzingeri agna*: of doubtful validity.
  - *Strobilops texasiana*: should be suppressed as it cannot be separated from *S. labyrinthica*.
  - *Strobilops labyrinthica parietalis*: without nomenclatural sanction, so dropped.
  - *Strobilops aenia spiralis*: considered dubious, with no *S. aenia* shells found in Oklahoma.
- **Synonymy:** *Carychium exile* is treated as a synonym of the older name *C. exiguum*, based on continuous variation across Oklahoma and Texas samples. *Vertigo decora* is a synonym of *V. modesta* and has not lived in Oklahoma since at least the Pleistocene. Walker's (1915) record was either a fossil or a misidentification of *V. ventricosa*.
- ***Vertigo ventricosa*** is rare in Oklahoma and absent from Kansas, which suggests a relictual distribution. The author had not seen a living specimen.
- ***Vertigo tridentata*** is restricted to the eastern part of the state and was found only in Mississippian–Pennsylvanian outcrop areas. The author suspects it is a calciphile.
- ***Oligyra orbiculata*** is associated with limestone-rich areas. It is seldom seen alive except during rain and must be sought under deeply embedded rocks and logs during droughts.
- ***Strobilops labyrinthica*** is most common under decaying logs and litter in forested areas but is not restricted to them.
- ***Gastrocopta procera*** is very abundant in the Carolinian region. It approaches *G. rupicola* in the Austroriparian region of McCurtain County, and the author suggests the latter may be a southern form of *G. procera*.
- **Dispersal:** Flood-driven transport of land snails on drift debris is an important dispersal mechanism in the Great Plains.
- **Overall conclusion:** The Oklahoma fauna is of composite origin, with both western and eastern components, and the eastern influence is stronger.
