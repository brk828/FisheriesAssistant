# Population Estimation and Depletion of Yellow Pikeperch in Spirit Lake, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This study estimated the adult [[Yellow Pikeperch]] population of [[Spirit Lake]], Iowa, after gill-net and angler catches declined over roughly a decade. Biologists from the [[Iowa Conservation Commission]] tagged 550 adult fish in spring 1947 and then used a [[Creel Census]] (May 15 to November 1, 1947) to compare tagged and untagged catches. The [[Peterson Mark-Recapture Estimate]] gave **30,544** adult fish as of May 15, 1947, with 95% limits of 26,119 to 35,733. The authors concluded the stock was depleted and changed management, including removing Spirit Lake from the [[Fry Stocking]] program and loosening regulations on competing species.

## Background & Description

The yellow pikeperch (*Stizostedion v. vitreum*) was one of the principal game fishes of Spirit Lake, and the lake had long been known for its sport fishing. Catches declined progressively over the 10 years before the study, and creel records from the preceding four years showed few pikeperch in anglers' catches.

Spirit Lake is Iowa's largest inland water body, covering 5,684 acres apart from many connected smaller lakes and sloughs. It is a shallow, [[Eutrophication|eutrophic]] glacial lake with a maximum depth of about 25 feet and an average depth of about 12 feet. The water is usually clear, with occasional late-summer blooms of blue-green algae. The littoral zone supports submerged pondweeds (*Potamogeton* spp.) and emergent bulrush (*Scirpus acutus*), both reduced in recent years by [[Carp]] and other factors.

The [[Iowa Conservation Commission]] has operated a yellow pikeperch hatchery at Spirit Lake for about 40 years. Spawn is obtained mainly from spawning-run fish taken by [[Gill-Netting]] in Spirit Lake and the adjoining [[Okoboji Lakes]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Gill-net records (1936 onward).** Hatchery crews kept catch records since 1936, complete except for 1943 and 1944. Crews usually numbered four or five each spring, using 800 feet of 2 to 2¼-inch bar-measure mesh set in shoal areas at dusk. Because crew numbers varied, the authors used [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] (average pikeperch per night per crew) as the index. The 1936 catch of 176 per night per crew was described as phenomenally high. The 1939 catch suggested a strong year class entered the run, though no supporting data exist.

**Creel census (1945–1948).** Angler records are incomplete before 1945. Table 1 summarises the yellow pikeperch and yellow perch catches:

| Year | Yellow Pikeperch Taken | Yellow Perch Taken |
|------|------------------------|--------------------|
| 1945 | 876 | 17,038 |
| 1946 | 19,508 | (not reliably transcribed) |
| 1947 | 8,719 | (not reliably transcribed) |
| 1948 | 6,520 | 56,804 |

The 1945 angler catch of pikeperch was poor, yet the 1945 gill-net catch exceeded 1946. The authors attributed the difference to a very large 1944 [[Yellow Perch]] year class, which served as prey for pikeperch and other predators until 1946 and entered the angler catch in 1947 and 1948. The authors warned that abundant forage can make fishing poor and lead to the false conclusion that a lake is "fished out," so creel data alone should be interpreted with caution.

**Competing and predatory species.** Populations of [[Black Crappie]], yellow perch, [[Black Bullhead]], [[White Bass]], and [[Largemouth Bass]] had increased significantly in the preceding years. These increases may have limited pikeperch reproduction and survival. [[Carp]] and black bullheads were reported to prey heavily on pikeperch spawn, and white bass were considered voracious predators on young pikeperch, though no direct evidence of predation was found.

## Key Findings

**1. Population estimate.** Tagging was carried out April 6 to April 26, 1947. The authors report 556 tagged fish (in the Procedures section), of which 6 were later found dead; calculations assume **550** tagged fish at the start of the season. The creel census recorded **8,719** untagged pikeperch and **157** tagged recaptures. The Peterson estimate was:

*P* = (8,719 × 550) / 157 = **30,544** adult yellow pikeperch as of May 15, 1947.

**2. Confidence limits.** Using the Poisson-based approach of Ricker (1937), the 95% limits on recaptures (C) were 134 and 183, giving population limits of **26,119** (lower) and **35,733** (upper).

**3. Exploitation.** The authors report that **28.6%** of tagged fish were accounted for in the creel census, and they assumed the same proportion of untagged fish was caught. They called the harvest "not extremely heavy" but warned that continued intensive harvest of the breeding stock could reduce the population to a level from which recovery would be difficult. Summer surveys indicated recruitment since 1944 had been meager, roughly balancing mortality and angler take.

**4. Tagging method.** Fish were tagged with number 3 metal tags attached at the corner of the upper jaw, and standard length, total length, weight, and scale samples were recorded. Recaptures were made up to 5 miles from release points, and one fish moved 5 miles in 8 hours. Recapture percentages by size group (Table 2) ranged from 0% to 38.3%, and were fairly uniform except in the 371 to 430 mm standard-length classes. The overall recapture rate was 28.2%.

**5. Sources of error.** The authors identified three main concerns: the accuracy of the creel census (A), the assumption that tagged and untagged fish are equally vulnerable to angling (C/A ratio, which was fairly uniform across 10-day periods), and the assumption that tagging does not change survival or behaviour. Any difference in reporting rates between tagged and untagged fish would bias the estimate.

**6. Effects of tagging.** Stomach analyses showed no difference in feeding between tagged and untagged fish, so tagging did not appear to inflate recaptures by stimulating feeding. Some tagged fish showed tag irritation and weight loss in late July 1947, but most showed no ill effects. Growth records from 1948 (14 fish) indicated that tagging retarded growth in most individuals.

**7. Management changes.**
- Spirit Lake was removed from the [[Fry Stocking]] program. The authors' evidence supported other studies (Carlander 1945; Hile 1937) that fry stocking has little effect on later abundance.
- The lake receives a small annual allotment of [[Fingerling Stocking|fingerlings]] from nursery lakes. All are fin-clipped, and survival was high, so the authors suggested expanding the nursery program.
- 1948 regulations removed size limits on yellow perch, crappie, and white bass, moved the crappie opening to May 15, removed the bullhead bag limit, and reduced the daily pikeperch limit from 8 to 5.
- Large-scale [[Carp]] removal was underway, and a possible fry-stocking trial was planned for spring 1949 as part of a research project on artificial propagation.
