# Nutrient Management with Hydroelectric Dams on the Colorado River System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Technical Report by Larry J. Paulson (Lake Mead Limnological Research Center, University of Nevada, Las Vegas, May 1981) examines how the depth of water discharge at [[Hoover Dam]] controls nutrient losses from [[Lake Mead]] and, through that, the reservoir's fertility and trophic state. The report argues that the present hypolimnion (deep) discharge strips large quantities of nitrogen and phosphorus, protecting the reservoir from wastewater-driven eutrophication but likely contributing to declining productivity and a serious decline in the [[Largemouth Bass]] fishery. Modeling suggests an epilimnion (surface) discharge would retain nutrients and raise fertility. The paper is a version submitted to the Journal of the American Society of Limnology and Oceanography.

## Background & Description

Phytoplankton growth in lakes and reservoirs is usually controlled by diverting wastewater or applying [[Advanced Wastewater Treatment]] (AWT), but reuse requirements and high AWT costs have limited these options. The contemporary nutrient-loading framework derives from [[Vollenweider Model]] studies of natural lakes with long retention times; in high-flushing reservoirs, outflow strongly controls nutrient concentrations. Selective withdrawal, long recognized as a tool for managing tailwater quality, is proposed here as a means of managing reservoir nutrient status.

Lake Mead, formed in 1935 by [[Hoover Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]], lies in the Mojave Desert and extends 183 km from the mouth of the Grand Canyon to Black Canyon. [[Boulder Canyon]] divides it into an Upper Basin and a Lower Basin. Key characteristics reported:

- Surface area 660 km²; volume 36 × 10⁹ m³ at maximum operating level of 374 m.
- Intake gates at 319 m and 272 m elevations; the dam has been operated from the lower gates since 1954.
- Colorado River (via [[Lake Powell]]) supplies 98% of annual inflow; the Virgin and Muddy Rivers and [[Las Vegas Wash]] supply the remainder.
- Monomictic: surface temperatures 10–12 °C (winter) to 28–30 °C (summer); stratification at 10–15 m from late May through October; alkaline (pH 7.5–8.6) and saline (750–800 mg l⁻¹).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nutrient sources and limitation.** The Colorado River provides 85% of annual inorganic nitrogen, while Las Vegas Wash contributes 60% of phosphorus. The Upper Basin is phosphorus limited and most of the Lower Basin nitrogen limited in summer. On chlorophyll-a, the Upper Basin is oligotrophic and most of the Lower Basin oligotrophic–mesotrophic; the inner [[Las Vegas Bay]], near the sewage outflow, is mesotrophic–eutrophic.

**Oxygen.** A metalimnetic oxygen minimum develops at the thermocline in summer, often falling below 4 mg l⁻¹; hypolimnetic oxygen usually exceeds 6 mg l⁻¹.

**Impoundment effects.** Formation of [[Lake Powell]] in 1963 reduced nitrate loading and retained phosphorus and suspended sediments (Lake Powell retains 70% of dissolved phosphorus and 80% of suspended sediment), making Lake Mead more phosphorus limited. Sewage effluent from Las Vegas Wash rose steadily, and an elevated phosphorus-rich inflow confined to the Lower Basin fueled dinoflagellate blooms.

**Methods.** Historical nitrate data came from monthly [[USGS Monitoring]] records (since 1935); nutrient and chlorophyll data came from the Lake Mead Monitoring Program (1972–1977) and the authors' 1977–1980 investigations, including epilimnion (0–5 m) and hypolimnion (70 m) sampling near the intake towers.

## Key Findings

- **Present-day losses are large.** Hypolimnion discharge produced annual nutrient losses of 6273 × 10² kg N and 169 × 10² kg P (July 1979–July 1980, Table 1). For October 1977–September 1978, losses were 2896 × 10³ kg NO₃-N and 91 × 10³ kg PO₄-P.
- **Epilimnion discharge would cut losses.** Inorganic losses would fall to 750 × 10² kg N and 49 × 10³ kg P, which are 74.1% and 46.2% lower than hypolimnion losses. Total N and P losses would still be 2154 × 10² kg N and 68 × 10² kg P higher from the hypolimnion, so organic entrainment does not offset the difference.
- **Long-term fertility decline.** Average epilimnetic nitrate fell from 300 to 50 mg m⁻³ (stratified period) and from 400 to 150 mg m⁻³ (unstratified period) since 1960–1962. Sediment analysis indicates reservoir-wide productivity has decreased by as much as 78% of pre-Lake Powell levels.
- **Bloom decline in Las Vegas Bay.** Spring chlorophyll-a bloom diminished after 1972 to about half its historic level. The 1972 bloom was dominated by dinoflagellates (Glenodinium, Hemidinium) and Carteria; Hemidinium was absent in spring 1975 and 1976 samples.
- **Modeling.** A nitrate model reproduced history well (predicted 4.47 × 10⁹ kg N/yr vs. observed 4.42 × 10⁹ kg N/yr). Under an epilimnion discharge, steady-state unstratified nitrate would reach about 650 mg m⁻³, roughly three times current levels, with phosphate estimated at 13.6 mg m⁻³.
- **Trophic implications.** Vollenweider's model predicts "dangerously" eutrophic conditions for the Lower Basin at 2.9 g P m⁻² yr⁻¹, but measured status is oligotrophic–mesotrophic because about 70% of phosphorus input is lost in hypolimnion discharge (reduced to 40% under epilimnion discharge).
- **Management recommendations.** Dam operating criteria should explicitly account for nutrient and trophic impacts. Epilimnion discharge may sustain fertility in multi-reservoir systems, whereas hypolimnion discharge is preferable where flooding replenishes nutrients or wastewater loading is high. Trade-offs between warm-water fisheries and cold-water tailwater trout fisheries must be weighed.
- **Supporting evidence.** Comparable patterns were reported on the Madison River (Martin and Arneson 1978) and in Kortowskie Lake, where nitrogen retention fell from 36.2% to 28% and phosphorus from 65.9% to –10% after hypolimnion withdrawal was installed.
