# U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Opinion on Central Arizona Project Water Delivery to Gila River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-biology #endangered-species #Cyprinidae #Poeciliidae #Cyprinodontidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This biological opinion addresses the February 12, 1991 request by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] for formal consultation under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] regarding delivery of [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water to the [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona and New Mexico. The consultation focuses specifically on the potential for CAP water delivery to introduce and spread non-native aquatic species into habitats occupied by listed fish species. The opinion concludes that the proposed delivery of CAP water is likely to jeopardize the continued existence of threatened [[Spikedace]] ([[Meda fulgida]]) and [[Loach Minnow]] ([[Tiaroga cobitis]]), and endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] ([[Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis]]) and [[Razorback Sucker]] ([[Xyrauchen texanus]]). The opinion finds no jeopardy for [[Desert Pupfish]] ([[Cyprinodon macularius]]), [[Colorado River Squawfish]] ([[Ptychocheilus lucius]]), or [[Bald Eagle]] ([[Haliaeetus leucocephalus]]). The document proposes a comprehensive Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) including physical barriers, electrical barriers, monitoring programs, and substantial funding for conservation and research over the 100-year project life.

## Background & Description

The [[Central Arizona Project]] was constructed to provide long-term, non-groundwater sources for municipal, industrial, and agricultural users in Arizona, representing Arizona's allocation of [[Colorado River]] flow. Water is taken from Lake Havasu and conveyed across the state through large open aqueducts. As of 1990, 744,506 acre-feet of water was delivered to 38 agricultural and municipal users under interim contracts.

**Listed Species Status:**

- **[[Spikedace]]** (threatened, listed July 1, 1986): Small silvery fish with well-developed dorsal spine. Originally throughout [[Gila River]] drainage above Phoenix; currently found in only four areas: [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona), upper [[Gila River]] (Grant and Catron Counties, New Mexico), [[Verde River]] (Yavapai County, Arizona), and [[Eagle Creek]] (Greenlee County, Arizona). Proposed critical habitat includes portions of Verde and Gila Rivers and Aravaipa Creek.

- **[[Loach Minnow]]** (threatened, listed June 15, 1986): Small, elongated, olive-colored fish. Once common in Gila River above Phoenix; now found in six locations: Aravaipa Creek, upper Gila, San Francisco and Tularosa Rivers (New Mexico), White River and Blue River (Arizona). Proposed critical habitat includes portions of Gila, San Francisco, Blue and Tularosa Rivers and Aravaipa, Campbell Blue, and Dry Blue Creeks.

- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (endangered, listed March 11, 1967): Small livebearing fish, once among most common species in Gila River and tributaries. Nine naturally occurring populations remain, all but one in [[Santa Cruz River]] basin. One natural population exists in three small adjacent spring systems near Bylas, Graham County, Arizona, on [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]].

- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (endangered, listed March 31, 1986): Small fish historically common throughout lower Gila, lower Colorado, and Sonoyta River systems. Natural populations now exist in three Imperial County, California locations; Quitobaquito Spring, Arizona; Rio Sonoyta, Mexico; and scattered sites in lower Colorado River delta.

- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (endangered, listed October 23, 1991): Grows over two feet in length with distinctive abrupt, sharp-edged dorsal ridge behind head. Once common throughout Colorado River basin; now exists sporadically in approximately 750 miles of upper basin and 200 miles of lower Colorado River. Proposed critical habitat within Gila River basin includes portions of Verde, Gila, and Salt Rivers and multiple tributaries.

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (endangered, listed March 11, 1967): Large silvery minnow growing up to six feet long. Once common throughout Colorado River system including Gila River basin; natural populations now found only in scattered areas of upper Colorado River system. Salt River from Roosevelt Dam upstream to U.S. Highway 60 bridge and Verde River from Horseshoe Dam upstream to Perkinsville designated as experimental non-essential population locations.

- **[[Bald Eagle]]** (endangered, listed March 11, 1967): Large fish-eating eagle. Currently nests along Bill Williams, Agua Fria, Verde, Salt, and Gila Rivers and tributaries in central Arizona.

Primary causes of decline for all fish species include habitat destruction and competition/predation from introduced non-native fish species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The aquatic ecosystems of the [[Gila River Basin]] have undergone severe deterioration. Of the 14 native fishes remaining in or restocked into the basin, 71% are Federally listed as endangered or threatened (8 species) or are Federal candidates (2 species). Several native fish species have already been extirpated. [[Minckley and Douglas]] (1991) believe the pattern of decline and extinction in western river basins is rapidly moving beyond loss of individual fish species to collapse of entire aquatic faunas.

**Habitat Degradation:** Impoundments such as [[San Carlos Reservoir]], [[Roosevelt Dam]], and [[Horseshoe Dam]] directly removed habitat and modified or dried flows in long downstream stretches. Major portions of the Gila River system now flow only intermittently, including the [[San Pedro River]], lower [[Salt River]], and Gila River near Virden, New Mexico, near Safford, Arizona, and below [[Ashurst-Hayden Dam]]. Habitat alterations have created new habitat types more favorable to non-native species than native species.

**Non-native Species:** The Gila River basin presently supports several non-native fish species with substantial adverse impacts to native fishes, including [[Rainbow Trout]] ([[Oncorhynchus mykiss]]), [[Channel Catfish]] ([[Ictalurus punctatus]]), [[Mosquitofish]] ([[Gambusia affinis]]), and [[Smallmouth Bass]] ([[Micropterus dolomieui]]). Non-native species already present exert continuing competitive and predatory pressure on native fishes.

**Baseline Condition:** The pre-project baseline represents a deteriorated system where native fishes persist in isolated stream sections that have not yet undergone sufficient perturbation to cause extirpation. Fragmentation of aquatic habitats has disrupted historic patterns of recolonization or augmentation of damaged or destroyed native fish populations. Project effects would be cumulative to this existing degraded baseline.

**CAP Connection Pathways:** The CAP creates perennial waterway connections from the Colorado River to major streams of the Gila River basin, reconnecting rivers and canal systems currently more isolated by dams and intermittent stream reaches. Specific connection points include:

- **[[Hassayampa River]]**: CAP-Granite Reef Aqueduct crosses approximately 24 miles upstream of Gila River confluence
- **[[Agua Fria River]]**: CAP crosses downstream of Lake Pleasant's Waddell Dam; CAP water will be stored in enlarged Lake Pleasant
- **[[Salt River|Salt]] and [[Verde River|Verde Rivers]]**: CAP crosses Salt River below Granite Reef Dam (below confluence); connections via Salt River Project canals with electric fish barriers
- **[[Gila River]]**: 21 CAP water delivery turnouts between Salt River siphon and Picacho Pumping Plant; direct connection via Florence-Casa Grande Canal to Gila River above Ashurst-Hayden Dam

## Key Findings

**Jeopardy Determinations:**

The proposed delivery of CAP water to water users in central Arizona is **likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of:
- Threatened [[Spikedace]] ([[Meda fulgida]])
- Threatened [[Loach Minnow]] ([[Tiaroga cobitis]])
- Endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] ([[Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis]])
- Endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] ([[Xyrauchen texanus]])

The proposed action is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of:
- Endangered [[Desert Pupfish]] ([[Cyprinodon macularius]])
- Endangered [[Colorado River Squawfish]] ([[Ptychocheilus lucius]])
- Endangered [[Bald Eagle]] ([[Haliaeetus leucocephalus]])

**Critical Habitat:** The proposed delivery is likely to adversely modify proposed critical habitat for spikedace, loach minnow, and razorback sucker.

**Non-native Species Threat Analysis:**

Five species specifically identified as potential CAP introductions with adverse impacts: [[Striped Bass]] ([[Morone saxatilis]]), [[White Bass]] ([[Morone chrysops]]), [[Blue Tilapia]] ([[Tilapia aurea]]), [[Rainbow Smelt]] ([[Osmerus mordax]]), and triploid [[Grass Carp]] ([[Ctenopharyngodon idella]]). [[Matter]] (1991) concluded four of five would likely spread into areas between Ashurst-Hayden and Coolidge Dams and San Pedro River system.

Non-native fishes already documented in CAP and interconnected Salt River Project canals include: [[Threadfin Shad]] ([[Dorosoma petenense]]), rainbow trout, [[Brook Trout]] ([[Salvelinus fontinalis]]), [[Goldfish]] ([[Carassius auratus]]), [[Common Carp]] ([[Cyprinus carpio]]), [[Red Shiner]] ([[Cyprinella lutrensis]]), [[Fathead Minnow]] ([[Pimephales promelas]]), [[Black Bullhead]] ([[Ameiurus melas]]), [[Yellow Bullhead]] ([[Ameiurus natalis]]), channel catfish, [[Flathead Catfish]] ([[Pylodictis olivaris]]), mosquitofish, [[Sailfin Molly]] ([[Poecilia latipinna]]), [[Yellow Bass]] ([[Morone mississippiensis]]), striped bass, [[Green Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis cyanellus]]), [[Bluegill]] ([[Lepomis macrochirus]]), [[Redear Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis microlophus]]), [[Largemouth Bass]] ([[Micropterus salmoides]]), [[Black Crappie]] ([[Pomoxis nigromaculatus]]), [[Walleye]] ([[Stizostedion vitreum]]), and blue tilapia.

**Entry Points:** Non-native species may enter CAP aqueduct from Lake Havasu (documented source of nine species), Lake Pleasant (once connected), and through "bait bucket" introductions by private citizens. The proximity of CAP and SRP canals to high-density human populations in Phoenix-Tucson area and easy availability to anglers creates prime conditions for unauthorized introductions.

**Time Frame:** CAP will operate for at least 100 years, providing continuous access for non-native species into Gila River basin. Over this timeframe, probability is high that one or more non-native fish species will colonize habitats occupied by listed species with major adverse consequences. Effects of non-native species on native species will continue far beyond the 100-year project life.

**Sub-basin Specific Effects:**

- **Hassayampa River**: Moderate to high probability of introductions; moderately adverse consequences. Listed species present include stocked populations of desert pupfish, Gila topminnow, and Colorado squawfish at The Nature Conservancy Hassayampa Preserve.

- **Agua Fria River**: Introduction virtually certain; moderate consequences. Fish movement from CAP into Lake Pleasant and up Agua Fria River will be unrestricted. Barrier constructed on Tule Creek protects large stocked Gila topminnow population.

- **Salt and Verde Rivers**: Low probability of introductions but severe consequences if they occur. Multiple dams and electric barriers limit upstream movement. One-third of remaining spikedace habitat is in upper Verde River; one of five remaining loach minnow populations in White River (upper Salt basin). Addition of more non-native species could preclude recovery of razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish.

- **Gila River below Coolidge Dam and San Pedro River**: High risk area. No effective barrier to upstream fish movement exists between Ashurst-Hayden and Coolidge Dams. Non-native species have open access to San Pedro River system including Aravaipa and Redfield Creeks and Babocomari River. Matter (1991) concluded four of five studied non-natives would likely spread into these areas. Aravaipa Creek supports spikedace and loach minnow populations. Upper San Pedro River basin considered important recovery habitat for multiple listed species.

- **Gila River above Coolidge Dam**: Less likelihood of CAP-mediated introductions but most severe consequences. Largest remaining populations of spikedace and loach minnow found in upper Gila, San Francisco, and Blue Rivers. Transfer of non-natives over Coolidge Dam would not result directly from CAP but becomes substantially more likely as result of CAP introduction of non-natives below dam. San Carlos Reservoir supports heavily-used warm water sport fishery creating high probability of bait bucket transfer.

**Species-Specific Impacts:**

- **Spikedace and Loach Minnow**: Both species particularly vulnerable due to short life span (approximately 2 years). Loss of two consecutive years of reproduction would result in extirpation. Aravaipa Creek populations at high risk and would probably be extirpated by cumulative impacts over project life. Upper Gila River and Eagle Creek populations of spikedace and San Francisco, Blue, and upper Gila populations of loach minnow would probably suffer substantial losses.

- **Gila Topminnow**: Particularly vulnerable to adverse impacts from non-native fish. Springs near Bylas supporting natural populations separated from Gila River only by short stream stretches; barriers only partially successful. Cow Creek and Humbug Creek stocked populations will probably be extirpated. Tule Creek population may survive if barrier successfully prevents upstream movement.

- **Desert Pupfish**: Effects expected to be minor. Most populations are small stocked populations in springs or short perennial stream segments generally isolated from mainstream rivers.

- **Razorback Sucker**: Monitoring of ten-year stocking effort indicates razorback suckers do not survive well in presence of non-native fish populations. Combined effects of existing and CAP-introduced non-natives will most likely prevent successful reestablishment in Verde, Salt, and Gila Rivers.

- **Colorado Squawfish**: Stocked populations designated experimental nonessential; loss cannot result in jeopardy by definition. Combined effects of existing and additional non-native species expected to severely limit successful reestablishment.

- **Bald Eagle**: Change in fish fauna over project life may or may not have significant effects to prey base. Evidence suggests bald eagles are not prey specific and will take whatever fish species are available. Unless newly introduced species eliminate significant portions of forage base and are themselves not available as prey, effects to nesting territories may not be significant.

**Cumulative Effects:** Introduction and spread of non-native fishes identified as major factors in decline of native fishes throughout North America, particularly in southwest. [[Miller et al.]] (1989) report non-native species were second most common causal factor in recent extinctions of North American fishes, playing a part in 68% of 40 known fish taxon extinctions in past 100 years.

Multiple or continuous introduction of individuals of each non-native species into Gila River system represents long-term cumulative effect. Impacts may be additive, multiplicative, or synergistic. Effects of any given non-native may be tolerable in absence of other non-natives but result in serious losses in their presence. Adverse effects would not necessarily require successful reproduction of non-natives in Gila River; successful reproduction in CAP aqueduct or irrigation canals combined with continuous movement into river system may give non-natives advantage by insulating them from effects of limiting natural events.

**Recovery Implications:** Major recovery strategy for endangered and threatened southwestern fishes is reestablishment within historic range. Suitable reestablishment sites are rare due to habitat destruction/alteration and non-native fish presence. Upper [[San Pedro River]] basin (above Saint David) considered among most promising recovery habitat for native Gila River fishes. Thirty-six miles of upper San Pedro River corridor now protected by [[Bureau of Land Management]] as [[San Pedro Riparian National Conservation Area]]. CAP connection would substantially increase likelihood of additional non-native fish establishment, potentially contaminating area to point where successful reestablishment of native fishes is no longer feasible.

**Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA):**

Implementation of following measures necessary to remove jeopardy threat (all portions must be implemented):

1. **Physical Barriers**: Construct drop structures acting as barriers to upstream fish movement at:
   - Aravaipa Creek between Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness and mouth (2 barriers, completed within 3.5 years)
   - San Pedro River between Redington and Fairbank (2 barriers, completed within 6 years)
   - Barriers anticipated to rise 4-10 feet above stream bottom; may not provide 100% probability of blocking upstream movement at all flows
