# Woundfin Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This is the completed 1983 revision (dated March 1, 1985) of the Recovery Plan for the endangered woundfin, *Plagopterus argentissimus*, prepared by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) with a [[Woundfin Recovery Team]] of state, federal, and consultant biologists. It supersedes the original plan approved in July 1979. The stated primary objective is to prevent the extinction of the woundfin and restore it to unlisted status. Recovery centres on protecting and enhancing the last viable population in the [[Virgin River]] and establishing at least two additional self-sustaining populations in historic range outside that river. The plan includes a step-down action outline, an implementation schedule with priorities and cost estimates, population monitoring procedures, reintroduction site criteria, and the public comment record from 1977–1979 drafts.

## Background & Description

The woundfin is a streamlined silvery minnow with a flattened head and a sharp dorsal spine from which its common name derives. The type specimen was described by Cope in 1874. It was first listed as endangered on October 13, 1970. Adults rarely exceed 75 mm standard length and show a light-yellow wash at the pectoral and pelvic fin bases during breeding. Adaptations to swift, shallow, sand-bottomed streams include a torpedo-shaped body, expanded falcate fins, lip barbels, reduced eyes, and sensory buds on the head and pectoral rays. The fish is essentially scaleless.

Taxonomically, the woundfin is the most highly specialised member of the cyprinid tribe Plagopterini (subfamily Leuciscinae). The tribe is endemic to the lower [[Colorado River]] basin and its ancestral tributary, the White River, and comprises three genera.

The historic range extended from the Salt River near Tempe, Arizona, to the mouth of the Gila River near Yuma, the lower Colorado River, and upstream through the Virgin River and LaVerkin Creek. Transplants into four Arizona localities at the range periphery failed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Adults are found in runs and quiet water beside riffles, at a mean velocity of 0.48 m/s (±0.23) and depth of 0.34 m (±0.18), over sand or sand/gravel substrate. Juveniles favour similar but slower, deeper runs (0.43 m/s; 0.38 m). Fry occur in backwaters associated with spawning areas (0.08 m/s; 0.21 m). Adults tolerate summer temperatures reaching 37.6°C, and an adult thermal preference of 19.5°C was reported.

**Associated species.** As many as 9 exotic and 6 native fishes are known from the Virgin River system. The woundfin is intimately associated with four native fishes and one introduced species. Competition with the introduced [[Red Shiner]] is a key concern: the red shiner dominates degraded lower reaches where woundfin are rare. Potential predators include piscivorous birds, soft-shelled turtles, [[Largemouth Bass]], green sunfish, channel catfish, and mosquitofish.

**Food and reproduction.** The species is omnivorous, feeding on filamentous algae, detritus, tamarisk seeds, and aquatic insects. Spawning is triggered by rising water temperature, lengthening daylight, and declining spring runoff, with peak activity in late May and early June. Survival of young is reduced at flows below 200 CFS and above 800 CFS.

**Threats.** The dominant limiting factor is habitat modification and loss, from dams, reservoirs (including [[Lake Mead]]), diversions, canals, and dewatering, together with exotic species introductions. Woundfin habitat was lost in the mainstream Colorado River after Hoover Dam, and flows below the Mesquite and Washington–St. George diversions are frequently diminished.

## Key Findings

- **Recovery criteria:** Downlisting from endangered to threatened is proposed once Virgin River habitat for all life stages is secured, marginal habitat is upgraded, and one additional population is established. Delisting requires a third self-sustaining population. A self-sustaining transplanted population is defined as over 100,000 adults persisting for at least 10 years.
- **Current status:** The Virgin River population is the only viable one. It is locally abundant in permanent reaches, and the 1942 collection at Bunkerville is cited as a baseline for declines below Mesquite.
- **Failed transplants:** Hassayampa River (reproduction in 1972, population lost in a September 1972 flood), Salt River, Sycamore Creek (no collections after 1973), and Paria River (no fish found in 1974 or 1975).
- **Monitoring:** Six stations on the Virgin River are sampled twice yearly, in late April and in September, using seines. Monitoring is contracted to Dr. James Deacon under FWS.
- **Reintroduction:** Preliminary criteria favour permanently flowing medium to large low-desert streams with sand or sand/gravel substrate. Priority sites include the Gila River above Safford and the Verde River above Horseshoe Reservoir, Arizona. Stocking is planned at a minimum of 1,000 fish per site annually until populations are established, with monitoring for up to 10 years.
- **Hatchery:** Propagation at Dexter National Fish Hatchery targets 10,000 fish per year, though early hatchery survival of fry was poor.
- **Critical habitat:** The Virgin River from LaVerkin Springs to Lake Mead was proposed in November 1977 but withdrawn on March 6, 1979, pending re-proposal.
- **Priorities and cost:** Priority 1 covers actions essential to prevent extinction; Priority 2 maintains current status; Priority 3 provides for full recovery. Cost estimates were disputed in comment letters as too low.
- **Public comment:** State agencies, the Bureau of Reclamation, the Bureau of Land Management, and the Forest Service reviewed drafts. Key objections concerned the feasibility of reintroduction before habitat requirements are known, the vagueness of habitat management plans, critical habitat designation for transplant sites, and the role of the Recovery Team in monitoring. The Arizona Game and Fish Department and the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources raised similar concerns. Several comments were incorporated into the final plan.
- **Management needs:** Long-term recovery requires cooperative management agreements with landowners and agencies to protect woundfin habitat and other Virgin River fishes.
