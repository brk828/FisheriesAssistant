# Determining Conditions That Promote Razorback Sucker Recruitment in Lake Mead: A Summary of the 2000–2002 Pilot Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This report summarizes a three-year pilot study (2000–2002) commissioned by the [[Southern Nevada Water Authority]] and conducted by BIO-WEST, Inc., to identify environmental conditions associated with [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) recruitment in [[Lake Mead]]. Razorback sucker larvae and juveniles are rarely recruited into the adult population in Lake Mead, while the Lake Mohave population recruits poorly as well. The study compared water quality (nutrients, total dissolved solids, conductivity, turbidity, total phosphorus), zooplankton density and composition, and aquatic vegetative cover among five Lake Mead sites (Las Vegas Bay, Echo Bay, Trail Rapids Bay) and Lake Mohave sites (Arizona Bay, Tequila Cove). The cover-and-turbidity hypothesis, rather than nutrients or food, emerged as the most plausible explanation for recruitment at Las Vegas Bay and Echo Bay.

The transmittal letter (June 27, 2003) from Rebecca D. McArthur, Environmental Biologist I, to Ross Haley of the [[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]] forwarded the finalized June 2003 report.

## Background & Description

*Xyrauchen texanus* is a large catostomid sucker native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its populations in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]] are remnants: a remnant population was discovered in Lake Mead in the early 1990s. Although research indicates the adult population is actively recruiting in Echo Bay and Las Vegas Bay, a much larger adult population derived from earlier rearing and stocking efforts by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and [[Bureau of Reclamation]] shows little documented recruitment. Predation on larval and juvenile razorback sucker by [[Nonnative Fishes]] is widely considered a principal reason for recruitment failure, although predator abundance in Lake Mead appears not to be unusually high.

The 2000 study was designed to test suspected factors, including nutrient levels, zooplankton abundance, and available cover, at sites in both reservoirs. Sampling occurred in March and May each year, when larval razorback sucker are typically available.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites.** Five locations were sampled: Las Vegas Bay (LVB, the influence of the [[Las Vegas Wash]] is a key factor), Echo Bay (EB), and Trail Rapids Bay (TRB) in Lake Mead, and Arizona Bay (AZB) and Tequila Cove (TQC) in Lake Mohave. Sites were 200 m × 20 m shoreline rectangles. Several sites in Lake Mead had to be relocated in 2002 because falling lake elevations left earlier sites dry.

**Methods.** Temperature, dissolved oxygen, nitrate, ammonium, and total dissolved solids were measured with a [[Hydrolab Datasonde]] 4a and Surveyor 4. Turbidity and Secchi depths were measured in 2001 and 2002. Total phosphorus was analyzed from water grab samples at NEL Laboratories. Zooplankton was collected with a 30 × 90 cm net with 80 µm mesh (changed from 153 µm), towed horizontally for about 6 m at 0–0.5 m depth, sampling about 430.5 liters per tow, and preserved in 10% formalin. Aquatic cover was mapped visually from the boat, with an underwater video camera and snorkeling in deeper areas. Statistics used [[Analysis of Variance]] (ANOVA) with [[Tukey's HSD]] post hoc tests in SYSTAT.

**Hydrologic context.** Lake Mead elevations declined substantially over the three years because of drought, exposing shoreline vegetation and reducing cover. Lake Mohave fluctuates more on an annual basis, so its cover remained relatively stable.

## Key Findings

- **Nutrients:** Las Vegas Bay consistently had the highest nitrate-nitrogen, ammonium-nitrogen, total dissolved solids, and conductivity values. Nitrate was significantly higher at LVB than at other locations in March 2002 (p < 0.005) and in May 2002 (p < 0.001), and LVB had the highest mean NO3-N over all years. However, nutrient levels did not differ among years, and LVB total phosphorus was not highest in 2002.
- **Zooplankton:** Densities were generally low at all locations, particularly for small zooplankton preferred by larval razorback sucker. LVB had significantly higher mean zooplankton density than all other sites in March 2002 (p < 0.001), and May 2002 samples had the highest densities overall. Densities at LVB and AZB were not consistently associated with recruitment.
- **Community composition:** Lake Mohave sites had significantly higher percentages of Bosminidae, which larval razorback sucker reportedly select, whereas Lake Mead sites (especially EB and LVB) had more adult copepods.
- **Turbidity:** LVB and EB had significantly higher turbidity than other sites in March and May 2002. LVB site 1 and EB site 1 were significantly higher than all AZB, TQC, and TRB sites (p < 0.001). Secchi depths at LVB ranged from 0.9–8 m in March and 0.85–1.5 m in May.
- **Cover:** In 2000, EB and LVB had markedly greater vegetative cover than other sites, but by May 2001 this difference was no longer significant. Cover declined at Lake Mead sites in 2002; AZB had the highest average percent cover and LVB and TQC the lowest, although differences between locations were not significant in 2002.
- **Water level link:** Cover at EB and LVB appears tied to inundated terrestrial vegetation that developed when Lake Mead levels were higher. Vegetation on the recently dewatered shoreline of EB in 2002 supported this interpretation.
- **Recruitment history:** Figure 36 indicated that a large proportion of the current adult population recruited during the 1980s, a period of relatively high reservoir elevation; subadult fish collected in 2003 were likely spawned in 1997–1998.
- **Larval surveys:** Lake-wide larval surveys in spring 1999 and 2000 found larvae only in EB and LVB and one additional area (Las Vegas Wash/Grand Wash Bay inflow area, with 11 larvae in 2000 and 22 in 2001).

**Conclusions and recommendations.** The authors concluded that higher nutrient levels and food availability may help increase razorback sucker recruitment at Las Vegas Bay, but these factors are not responsible for the recruitment at Echo Bay. Vegetative cover that protects young fish from predation by nonnative fishes, and perhaps turbidity, is the most likely common factor. They recommended:

1. Examining Lake Mead water level records and turbidity measurements to identify favorable cover years.
2. Continuing to collect and age adult and larval razorback sucker at LVB and EB.
3. Comparing cover types and turbidity at EB and LVB with other coves lacking a population.
4. Gathering cover and turbidity information lake-wide to guide future repatriation of hatchery-reared fish or locating additional populations.
