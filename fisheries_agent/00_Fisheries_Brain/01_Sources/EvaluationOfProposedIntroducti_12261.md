#ichthyology #marine-biology #Ariidae

## Executive Summary

This 1968 California Department of Fish and Game administrative report by Emmitt L. McClendon evaluates a proposal to introduce "bagre" (sea catfish, family [[Ariidae]]) into the [[Salton Sea]] as a potential forage or sport fish addition. The author concludes that the introduction should **not** proceed, citing insufficient taxonomic clarity, unknown salinity tolerance, unstudied venom pharmacology, and the likelihood that bagre would function as a high-level carnivore competing with existing top predators (e.g., [[Orangemouth Corvina]]) rather than serving as a suitable forage species. The report highlights the poor taxonomic resolution of the Ariidae as a central obstacle to risk assessment.

## Background & Description

- **Family-level trait**: Members of [[Ariidae]] (sea catfishes) possess **venomous spines** at the base of the pectoral and dorsal fins.
- **Venom apparatus**: Venom glands located at the spine bases; venom is released into puncture wounds inflicted by the spines.
- **Size/lethality variance by genus**:
  - *[[Selenaspis]]* and *[[Pimelodus]]* (Brazilian coastal genera): capable of producing violent pain lasting 48+ hours.
  - *[[Galeichthys felis]]*: larger individuals can inflict severe lacerations and extremely painful wounds.
- **Taxonomic ambiguity**: The generic identity of "bagre" as used in Latin America is unresolved — the term is applied colloquially to both freshwater and marine catfishes, complicating species-level morphological description.
- Only one true species is confirmed in the genus *[[Bagre]]*: **Bagre marinus**, ranging from Cape Cod to Panama.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preferences (documented in source species)**:
  - *Bagre marinus*: prefers intermediate salinities (5–30 ‰).
  - *Galeichthys felis*: prefers warm water of high salinity (≥30 ‰).
  - General Ariidae behavior: congregate around river mouths and in brackish water (Starks, 1913).
- **Commercial role**: Marine catfishes play only a minor role in commercial fisheries of the United States and Mexico.
- **Target ecosystem — the Salton Sea**:
  - Described as a "marine-like" environment; salinity approximates seawater, but **ionic composition differs** due to evaporative concentration of Colorado River water — a potential source of physiological stress for introduced marine nekton.
  - Maximum depth recorded: 45.1 ft (13.75 m) as of September 9, 1968.
  - Greater temperature fluctuation than open ocean due to shallow depth.
  - **Anoxic bottom conditions** occur during summer months.
  - **Primary food chain**: nutrient salts → plankton → detritus → [[Neanthes]] → [[Bairdiella]] → [[Orangemouth Corvina]].
  - **Secondary food chain**: nutrient salts → plankton → [[Threadfin Shad]] → [[Orangemouth Corvina]].
- **Introduction criteria (per Walker, 1961)**: Any additional fish species introduced to the Salton Sea should be a **plankton feeder**, **no more than 3 inches long**, and available as prey to the existing top carnivore ([[Orangemouth Corvina]]).

## Key Findings

1. **Taxonomic uncertainty**: The Ariidae (sea catfish) taxonomy is "badly in need of review"; the generic identity of the proposed import "bagre" cannot be confidently assigned.
2. **Venom risk**: Most or all sea catfish species can inflict severe, prolonged pain via venomous spines; the specific venom pharmacology of candidate bagre species is unknown.
3. **Trophic mismatch**: Bagre are likely high-level carnivores, not forage fish, and would probably **compete with the existing top carnivore** ([[Orangemouth Corvina]]) for food rather than support the food chain.
4. **Environmental uncertainty**: The Salton Sea's unusual ionic composition (despite marine-like salinity) creates unknown physiological risk for any introduced marine species.
5. **Recommendation**: The report explicitly **discourages importation of bagre into the Salton Sea** based on (i) insufficient life history/ecological/salinity tolerance data, (ii) unknown venom action, and (iii) probable trophic competition with the sport fishery's apex predator.
6. **Management guidance**: Reinforces Walker's (1961) prior recommendation that any new species introduced to the Salton Sea should be a small (<3 in), plankton-feeding forage fish — a criterion bagre does not meet.
