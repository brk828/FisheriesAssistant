# Factors Influencing Poststocking Dispersal of Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether site acclimation and rearing environment affect the postrelease dispersal of hatchery-reared [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]*). Dispersal from the stocking sites was rapid and declined over time in all tests. Downstream drift was greatest from a small backwater and was significantly lower for fish conditioned to flow before release. The authors concluded that postrelease dispersal may decrease if razorback suckers are preconditioned to flow. Site acclimation effects were inconclusive.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker, once common in the [[Colorado River Basin]], declined in distribution and abundance and is now represented mainly by small populations of old adults. It was federally listed as endangered in 1991. Over 12 million hatchery-raised fish, mostly fry or juveniles, have been stocked in the basin. Reintroduction efforts have been hampered by downstream drift and poor survival, which are commonly attributed to stress, disorientation, starvation, and [[Predation]].

Management responses have included releasing larger fish (>25 cm) to reduce predation, stocking farther upstream to allow for drift, and terminating stocking programs. Researchers had recommended testing [[Site Acclimation]], [[Physical Conditioning]], and winter releases, but these had not been tested in reintroduction programs. The authors therefore examined how site acclimation and rearing environment affected dispersal of hatchery-reared fish released at three locations.

Transmitters were attached externally to 120 razorback suckers. The attachment took about 30 s and avoided the stress and recovery associated with abdominal surgery. Anesthesia was not used because it could impair swimming. Transmitters were conical, measured 8 mm in diameter and 35–50 mm in length, weighed 4–7 g, and had a battery life of 45 d. Sonic transmitters (70 kHz) were used in the reservoir test and [[Radio Telemetry]] transmitters (40–41 MHz) in the backwater tests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Reservoir (Lake Powell, Utah).** This 65,000-ha mainstem reservoir has annual water-level fluctuations typically exceeding 15 m, which prevent submergent vegetation. Underwater cover is limited to rock talus, *Tamarix* sp., and sparse flooded salt cedar. Fish were released in Castle Creek Canyon on 3 June 1997.

**Small backwater (Millard Canyon, Green River, Utah).** This side canyon seasonally floods. Backwater habitat is rare, consisting mainly of small side eddies and side-canyon entrances. Channels are confined by steep walls and are typically less than 100 m wide. Velocities were about 0.5–1.0 m/s, and the release site was 53 km upstream of the Green–Colorado confluence. Downstream lies [[Cataract Canyon]], a 26-km white-water reach. Thirty fish were released on 16 June 1998.

**Large backwater ([[Laughlin Lagoon]], Nevada).** This 30-ha backwater lies 12 km downstream of Laughlin, Nevada, on the Colorado River below [[Davis Dam]]. It connects to the river through a navigational channel and paired culverts. About 75% of its area is shallow (<1 m) and supports submergent pondweed (*Potamogeton* sp.) and emergent bullrush (*Scirpus* sp.). River channels are typically less than 150 m wide, with currents below 1 m/s.

**Fish sources.** Fish came from the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], where they were reared in raceways with recirculating flow, and from the [[Boulder City Municipal Ponds]], where they were kept in lined earthen ponds. Hatchery fish were placed in a concrete raceway (1.5 × 10 m) on 16 November 2000 and held under flow for about 2 months. Flows from the 10-cm inlet pipe ranged from 0.1 to 1.0 m/s.

## Key Findings

### Experimental Design
- Eight test groups of 15 fish each were followed for 28 d.
- Reservoir and small-backwater tests each used 30 fish, split into two 15-fish subgroups (reference versus 3-d site acclimation behind a 6-mm barrier net).
- The large-backwater test used 60 fish, split into four 15-fish subgroups: pond-reared released immediately, pond-reared site-acclimated for 7 d, flow-conditioned released immediately, and flow-conditioned site-acclimated for 7 d.
- Surveys were weekly, by boat, raft, or jet boat. Positions were triangulated and located by GPS.
- Contact was lost for several fish, so analyses included only fish tracked for at least 14 d.

### Downstream Drift and Dispersal Distances
- Mean poststocking dispersal of pond-reared fish was 3.7 km in the reservoir (N = 19), 69.5 km in the small backwater (N = 20), and 7.6 km in the large backwater (N = 28).
- A [[Kruskal-Wallis Test]] showed that dispersal differed among the three test groups (χ² = 39.5, df = 2, P < 0.01). Dispersal was greatest in the small backwater, where flow and a lack of adequate cover aggravated downstream drift.
- Drift from the small backwater was described as "pronounced and prolonged," at more than 68 km per month.

### Site Acclimation
- No statistical difference in dispersal was found between reference and site-acclimated fish at any release site.
- The authors judged that acclimation periods of less than 7 d had negligible influence on short-term dispersal and that the site-acclimation results were inconclusive. They noted that small sample sizes or short acclimation periods may have contributed.

### Flow Conditioning
- Flow-conditioned fish dispersed significantly less than pond-reared fish. A [[Wilcoxon Two-Sample Test]] gave Z = −2.298, with P = 0.0216 (N = 30) in the Results.
- The abstract reports the same comparison as P < 0.01, with mean dispersal of 1.9 km for flow-conditioned fish versus 7.7 km for pond-reared fish. These values match the average of the two flow-conditioned and two pond-reared subgroups in the large-backwater test.

### Reservoir Results
- 145 detections were recorded during the first month. All fish had left the release site within 5 d.
- By 3–4 d, about 72% of fish were within 10 m of shore, in vegetative or rocky cover.
- Movement was minimal (<0.1 km/week) by week 3.
- Mean dispersal was 3.3 km for acclimated fish and 4.3 km for reference fish. Maximum distances were 11.1 km (acclimated) and 12.4 km (reference).

### Small-Backwater Results
- All fish left the release site within 5 d. Downstream movement averaged about 5 km/d during the first week.
- Twenty fish (8 acclimated, 12 control) were detected 2 weeks after release, with 70 contacts.
- Six fish (4 acclimated, 2 control) moved upstream into the Colorado River. Nine of the 11 fish detected downstream of the confluence (82%) were controls.
- Mean distance was 66 km for site-acclimated fish (maximum 126 km) and 71 km for controls (maximum 122 km).

### Large-Backwater Results
- 58 fish were followed for a month, yielding 1,121 contacts.
- 22 fish (16 pond-reared, 6 flow-conditioned) moved downstream, and 6 (5 flow-conditioned, 1 pond-reared) moved upstream. 21 fish remained in the backwater for at least a month.
- By the end of week 4, 95% of fish entering the river had been found in small backwaters.
- Mean dispersal was 8.8 km (site-acclimated pond-reared), 6.6 km (reference pond-reared), 3.6 km (site-acclimated flow-conditioned), and 0.2 km (reference flow-conditioned).
- The maximum movement recorded by week 4 was 49 km downstream and 9 km upstream.

### Interpretation and Conclusions
- Pond-reared fish showed greater downstream drift, consistent with earlier work on physiological fatigue in pond-reared trout.
- The authors proposed that flow conditioning for 2 months before release improved swimming performance, reduced overall stress, and lowered the risk of chronic fatigue. They assumed this would improve survivability. Flow-conditioned fish remained closer to the release site and performed similarly to wild-captured fish in earlier telemetry studies (McAda and Wydoski 1980; Valdez and Masslich 1989).
- The authors concluded that poststocking dispersal may decrease if razorback suckers are preconditioned to flow. They noted that flow conditioning is common in salmonid culture but had not been applied to catostomids or other warmwater fish.
