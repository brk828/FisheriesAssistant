# Use of Surrogate Species in Assessing Contaminant Risk to Endangered and Threatened Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. EPA-funded final report, prepared by the [[National Biological Service]] Midwest Science Center, tests whether the standard surrogate species used in aquatic toxicity assessments, [[Rainbow Trout]] (coldwater) and [[Fathead Minnow]] (warmwater), adequately protect federally listed fishes. Under [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7, federal agencies must ensure that authorized actions do not jeopardize listed species. Static acute 96-h toxicity tests were run on three listed salmonids, two listed cyprinids and one listed catostomid, exposed to five chemicals: [[Carbaryl]], [[Copper]], [[4-Nonylphenol]], [[Pentachlorophenol]] and [[Permethrin]]. The authors conclude that surrogates often perform similarly but that listed species are sometimes more sensitive, so an additional margin of safety may be needed for listed salmonids.

## Background & Description

The report reviews the regulatory framework that governs chemical risk to aquatic life: the [[Federal Insecticide, Fungicide and Rodenticide Act]], the [[Toxic Substances Control Act]] and the [[Clean Water Act]]. It notes that the FWS 1989 Biological Opinion covered 165 listed species (primarily aquatic) and evaluated 113 chemicals. Standard acute water-quality criteria require data from several taxonomic groups, with salmonids and a warmwater fish family as key fish representatives.

The listed species studied, each with a [[Species Profile]] in Appendix 1, were:
- **Coldwater:** [[Apache Trout]] (Threatened), [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (Threatened), [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] (Threatened)
- **Warmwater:** [[Bonytail Chub]] (Endangered), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (Endangered), [[Razorback Sucker]] (Endangered)

Fish were obtained as eyed eggs (spring and summer 1992 and 1993) from National Fish Hatcheries and other sources, then cultured in well water at the Midwest Science Center. Tests followed [[EPA Acute Toxicity Test Methods]] and ASTM (1988) procedures, with 15 L glass jars, six exposure concentrations, three replicates per concentration and 10 fish per replicate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report stresses that listed species have limited distributions, so further adverse effects could lead to extinction. Environmental protection procedures typically target populations or communities rather than individuals, which the authors argue is insufficient for listed species.

Species declines cited in the profiles include:
- **Apache trout:** habitat reduction, introduced species, competition, predation and hybridization with cutthroat trout; pure strains occupy about 5% of former range.
- **Greenback cutthroat trout:** irrigation, mining pollution, grazing and introduced brown and brook trout; pure populations persist in Colorado streams.
- **Lahontan cutthroat trout:** dams (Derby Dam 1905, Lahontan Dam 1915) blocked migration; most populations lost except Summit Lake (NV) and Independence Lake (CA).
- **Bonytail chub, Colorado squawfish, razorback sucker:** dam construction, water withdrawal and non-native species competition and predation in the Colorado River basin.

Chemical release context is also given: the EPA Toxics Release Inventory reported releases of 2.6 billion kg from 22,650 facilities for 320 chemicals in 1989, with 1,807 million kg released to air and 86 million kg to surface waters. About 70% of the 540 million kg of pesticides sold annually are used in agriculture.

## Key Findings

- **Surrogate performance:** Standard organisms often had similar sensitivity to listed species. For 30% (8 of 27) of possible surrogate/listed comparisons at 96 h, the listed species' LC50 was lower than the surrogate's.
- **Warmwater species:** At 96 h, listed warmwater species were more sensitive than fathead minnows 33% of the time, but were always less sensitive than rainbow trout. Rainbow trout hazard assessments were therefore considered protective of the warmwater species tested.
- **Coldwater species:** At 96 h, listed salmonids were more sensitive than rainbow trout in 25% of comparisons. The authors recommend an additional margin of safety for listed salmonids.
- **Chemical toxicity ranking:** After 96 h, permethrin was most toxic and carbaryl least toxic overall. For coldwater species the order was permethrin > copper > pentachlorophenol > 4-nonylphenol > carbaryl. Rainbow trout 96-h LC50s were lower than fathead minnow LC50s for all five chemicals.
- **Carbaryl:** Apache trout (LC50 3.29 mg/L at 12 h) and Lahontan cutthroat trout (4.38 mg/L) were more sensitive than rainbow trout (6.76 mg/L) at 12 h. Bonytail chub (3.49 mg/L) and Colorado squawfish (3.07 mg/L) were more sensitive than fathead minnow (5.21 mg/L) at 96 h, but not more than rainbow trout.
- **Copper:** Listed coldwater species did not differ significantly from rainbow trout. Bonytail chub (0.22 mg/L) and razorback sucker (0.27 mg/L) were more sensitive than fathead minnow (0.47 mg/L) at 96 h.
- **4-Nonylphenol:** Apache and Lahontan cutthroat trout were more sensitive at 24 h (0.24 and 0.25 mg/L vs 0.30 mg/L for rainbow trout). By 96 h, all coldwater species had similar LC50s.
- **Pentachlorophenol:** Apache trout (0.11 mg/L) was significantly more sensitive than rainbow trout (0.16 mg/L) at 96 h. No warmwater listed species differed from fathead minnow.
- **Permethrin:** Apache (1.71 µg/L) and Lahontan cutthroat trout (1.58 µg/L) were about half the rainbow trout value (3.31 µg/L) at 96 h. Razorback sucker was more sensitive than fathead minnow at 96 h. Bonytail chub and Colorado squawfish were much less sensitive.
- **Quality control:** Control survival exceeded 90% in all tests. Dissolved oxygen dips occurred in some rainbow trout runs, but LC50s were not affected, so no tests were excluded. Measured chemical concentrations varied widely from nominal values (for example, 4-nonylphenol 160% in 1993), and LC50s are based on nominal concentrations.
- **Biochemical screening:** Muscarinic cholinergic receptor number decreased in fish brains after carbaryl and permethrin exposure, with no change in affinity. This was a preliminary study and suggests a compensatory response.
- **Recommendations:** Test additional listed species or FWS-identified surrogates, and conduct chronic toxicity studies, before definitive policy decisions. The authors note that water-quality criteria derived by halving the rainbow trout Species Mean Acute Value may not protect listed salmonids.
