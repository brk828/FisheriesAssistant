# A Distributional Study of the Fishes of Missouri

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #biogeography #missouri-fish-fauna #zoogeography

## Executive Summary
This dissertation by William Leo Pflieger (University of Kansas, 1969) presents a comprehensive distributional and biogeographic analysis of the fish fauna of Missouri, encompassing 191 species. Rather than focusing on the morphology of a single species, the work classifies Missouri's fishes into faunal groups based on shared distributional patterns tied to four recognized **fish faunal regions**: [[Ozark Faunal Region]], [[Lowland Faunal Region]], [[Prairie Faunal Region]], and [[Big River Faunal Region]]. The study integrates ecological factors (turbidity, current velocity, substrate, water permanence) with historical/Pleistocene glacial events to explain contemporary distribution patterns, disjunctions, and relict populations. The work also addresses the role of interspecific competition, climate, and anthropogenic environmental change (agriculture, impoundment, species introductions) in shaping the modern Missouri fish fauna.

## Background & Description
This study is primarily biogeographic and ecological rather than morphological; no specific anatomical or morphometric data for individual species are presented in the abstract. Instead, species are grouped taxonomically by **faunal affiliation** rather than by morphotype:

- **[[Ozark Faunal Group]]** (65 species) — largest group; associated with limestone geology.
- **[[Lowland Faunal Group]]** (38 species) — associated with lentic/low-gradient habitats.
- **[[Prairie Faunal Group]]** (18 species) — tolerant of highly fluctuating environments.
- **[[Big River Faunal Group]]** (30 species) — adapted to large-river turbidity/current regimes.
- **[[Ozark-Lowland Faunal Group]]** (7 species) — intermediate, quiet-pool specialists.
- **[[Ozark-Prairie Faunal Group]]** (6 species) — require stony substrate but broad tolerance otherwise.
- **[[Wide-Ranging Faunal Group]]** (23 species) — broad tolerance, often favored by anthropogenic habitat change.
- **Rare/unrecorded species** (4 species) — absent from analyzed collections.

Morphological differentiation is implied indirectly through references to **geminate species pairs** — sister taxa on either side of the Mississippi River believed to derive from a common ancestral stock isolated during early Pleistocene glaciation (5 species pairs cited), suggesting incipient morphological divergence associated with vicariant speciation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The study defines faunal groups by their environmental tolerances and habitat associations rather than individual species niches:

- **[[Ozark Faunal Region]]**: Clear, permanently flowing streams with stony bottoms; limestone-dominated karst geology; high habitat stability.
- **[[Lowland Faunal Region]]**: Lentic environments and low-gradient streams; likely swamp/slough-associated habitats of southeastern Missouri.
- **[[Prairie Faunal Region]]**: Streams with wide fluctuations in turbidity, temperature, and water level — highly variable, unstable environments requiring euryecious adaptations.
- **[[Big River Faunal Region]]**: Missouri and Mississippi mainstem rivers; niche partitioning driven by turbidity, current velocity, and bottom substrate type.
- **Ecotones**: Transitional zones are formally recognized, including the **[[Ozark Border]]** ecotone and additional "lines of best fit" marking zones of abrupt faunal turnover between regions.

Key ecological drivers identified:
- **Interspecific competition** is presented as significant in controlling contemporary fish distribution patterns.
- **Climatic effects** on distribution are largely obscured/masked by other more proximate environmental factors (e.g., substrate, hydrology).
- **Faunal diversity** varies markedly across the state, linked in part to differential **aquatic environmental stability** among regions (stable Ozark streams vs. unstable prairie streams).
- **Anthropogenic factors** — agriculture, impoundment construction, and species introductions — have measurably altered fish distributions within the last two centuries, disproportionately favoring the wide-ranging/lentic-tolerant group.

## Key Findings
- **191 fish species** documented for the state of Missouri, organized into a formal faunal-group classification system.
- **Four primary faunal regions** identified (Ozark, Lowland, Prairie, Big River), each anchoring a distinct species assemblage.
- **Two secondary/transitional faunal groups** (Ozark-Lowland, Ozark-Prairie) documented, reflecting shared habitat requirements across regional boundaries.
- **Pleistocene glaciation** is identified as a primary historical determinant of current distribution patterns:
  - **22 species** thought to have been localized preglacially in drainages north of the Mississippi Valley.
  - **5 northern species** persist as disjunct **glacial relict populations** in Missouri.
  - **10 species** show **east–west disjunctions** across the Mississippi River, attributed to alternating erosion/sedimentation cycles in the [[Mississippi Embayment]] that alternately allowed dispersal and isolation.
  - **17 species** are hypothesized to have colonized the central Mississippi Valley via eastward diversion of central/southern plains drainage through the lower Arkansas and Missouri rivers.
  - **11 species** in the upper Arkansas River system may have dispersed southwestward via historical stream connections with the Missouri River system.
  - **2 species** show disjunct populations in the Ozark Uplands and the [[Driftless Area]], possibly due to failure to reoccupy areas following glacial retreat.
  - **5 species** are represented by **geminate (sister) forms** east of the Mississippi River, interpreted as products of early-glacial vicariant isolation from a shared ancestral stock.
- **Post-Pleistocene climatic fluctuations** have caused only minor range adjustments in some species.
- **Anthropogenic change** over the past two centuries (agriculture, impoundments, introductions) has driven the most rapid and dramatic shifts in Missouri fish distribution, especially benefiting the 23-species wide-ranging/lentic-tolerant faunal group.
- The dissertation totals **589 pages**, representing one of the most comprehensive state-level ichthyofaunal distributional analyses of its era.
