# The Genetics of the Colorado Squawfish: Considerations for a Population Viability Analysis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This August 1992 draft by Michael Gilpin and Andrew Soulé (University of California, San Diego) for comment and discussion, not citation, examines how [[Population Genetics]] bears on the viability and recovery of the [[Colorado Squawfish]]. The authors state that very little is known empirically about squawfish genetics, so the analysis is explicitly speculative and requires empirical confirmation. Genetic data are framed as serving three roles in a [[Population Viability Analysis]]: delineating distinct population subunits, inferring dispersal and other dynamical parameters, and detecting erosion of variability that can drive populations into [[Extinction Vortex]] dynamics. The conclusion is that genetics does not appear to be of overriding importance to the viability or recovery of the species at present.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), a potamodromous cyprinid restricted to freshwater migrations, and the closely related [[Sacramento Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus grandis*), used as a comparator.
- **Genetic status:** Little published genetic information existed; genetic work at the University of Arizona was described as ongoing.
- **Key concept:** [[Heterozygosity]] (H), the fraction of gene loci with two distinguishable alleles, usually measured by [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]]. Typical fish values range from 0% to 20%. The authors stress that H alone is not diagnostic because many ecological and genetic forces can produce the same value.
- **Population size concepts:** The [[Effective Population Size]] (Ne) is central, distinguished from census size (N) and corrected for unequal sex ratios and fluctuating numbers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Demography:** Squawfish appear to be K-selected with low adult mortality, a life-history profile associated with low heterozygosity (Mitton and Lewis 1989).
- **Mobility and fragmentation:** Adults are mobile and distributed across different selection regimes, which could raise H through mixing. However, the species has already disappeared from upper reaches above dams and from some lower reaches, producing fragmentation and possible [[Metapopulation]] structure with recolonization bottlenecks.
- **Altered environment:** The species now experiences modified water flow regimes, a different prey base, and different competitors and predators, all of which could drive selection on heritable traits. Selection tends to reduce H at the loci involved.
- **Hatchery influence:** No immediate plans existed to stock large numbers of hatchery fish in occupied areas; small past introductions were judged unlikely to have altered local gene composition because of selection against inappropriate gene combinations.
- **Historical sampling:** Early explorers such as [[John Wesley Powell]] collected no systematic genetic samples, so no "before" baseline exists to quantify genetic change.

## Key Findings

**1. Heterozygosity estimate**
- The authors predict low H for Colorado squawfish, in the range of 1% to 3%, citing the comparable Sacramento squawfish (H of 1%), life-history correlations, and possible effects of migration.

**2. Drift and time scale**
- Generation time is likely greater than 10 years under pristine conditions. With adult population sizes above 1,000 and only about 10 generations since dam construction, expected drift-driven heterozygosity loss is minimal (about 0.5% loss, calculated from H(t) = H0(1 - 1/(2N))^t).

**3. Current effective size**
- Adult numbers are estimated at 10^3 to 10^4 (Tyus 1991). A proposed viability threshold of 500 (Franklin 1980; Soulé 1980) was questioned by Lande (1987).
- Observed sex ratios of 5:1 to 15:1 (males to females; Tyus 1990) reduce Ne to roughly 50% to 75% of census size, bringing the estimate "dangerously close" to the 500 threshold, although the authors caution these ratios cannot be accepted uncritically.

**4. Inbreeding threshold**
- Soulé (1980) suggested Ne of 50 as a level below which inbreeding depression may become a problem. The [[Florida Panther]] (about 35 adults) illustrates severe inbreeding effects, including defective heart valves, cross-eyed individuals, and malformed sperm.
- The "magic number" of 1 migrant per generation is sufficient to alleviate local inbreeding. Fidelity to natal areas is unproven, so gene flow may be adequate.

**5. Gene flow and population structure**
- Squawfish show spawning-site fidelity (Wick et al. 1983; Tyus 1990), but natal fidelity is not proven. Occasional straying (Tyus 1990) could maintain a single panmictic population across the upper basin.
- Current distribution is centered on the Green River, based on 1978–1989 CRFP data (Chuck McAda), and the authors consider genetic differentiation there unlikely.

**6. Recommended methods**
- [[PIT Tagging]] is expected to improve data on adult movements.
- Otolith microstructure analysis (Muth and Bestgen 1991) may help study the movements of age-0 fish.
- Appendix: [[Slatkin Private Allele Method]] (Slatkin 1985) could estimate Nm from the proportion of private alleles, p(1). A p(1) of about 0.07 or less would indicate safety from inbreeding. Estimation requires at least 3 to 5 populations, with roughly 20 or more private alleles. A one-dimensional stepping-stone pattern may raise the threshold above that of an island model, so a p(1) above 0.07 would be inconclusive.

**7. Overall conclusion**
- Genetics is important to understanding squawfish viability and recovery, but the authors conclude it is not of overriding importance at present. Ongoing University of Arizona analysis is expected to clarify key uncertainties, particularly gene flow and population structure.
