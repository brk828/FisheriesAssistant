# Exploratory Fishing for a Source of Non-Ciguatoxic Sport and Food Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #lutjanidae #serranidae #ciguatera

## Executive Summary

This Virgin Islands Ecological Research Station (VIERS) special report (Contribution No. 2, July 15, 1970) documents exploratory deep-water hook-and-line and trap fishing along the [[Shelf Slope]] of the Virgin Islands and nearby [[Saba Bank]], [[Anguilla Bank]] and [[Barbuda Bank]]. The work had two linked goals: to identify deep-water snapper and grouper stocks that might be free of [[Ciguatera]] and to develop a fishery for them. The report also covers the search for a practical [[Ciguatoxin]] bioassay, which the authors did not achieve. Fishing yielded 303 to 304 fish weighing 1357 lb (617 kg) over 78 hours on the grounds. [[Silk Snapper]] was identified as the most promising commercial species. The report concludes that a small native fishery could be productive, but that a reliable ciguatera test remains undeveloped.

## Background & Description

**Project context.** The work was run by the [[Virgin Islands Ecological Research Station]] (VIERS), Caribbean Research Institute (CRI), College of the Virgin Islands (CVI), under Project Leader Dr. Arthur E. Dammann. It was funded by the US Bureau of Commercial Fisheries (Project 2-96-R) and the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife (Project F-5), with matching funds from the Virgin Islands government. A preliminary survey in 1965 had identified ciguatera as a major barrier to marketing shallow-water food fish.

**Rationale.** The authors cited Halstead (1965) and Randall (1958) to argue that ciguatera might be limited to the euphotic zone (above about 600 ft). If so, fish caught deeper should be ciguatera-free. Earlier private fishing efforts with electric reels had found deep snappers and groupers but suffered from ciguatoxic catches and from insufficient capital.

**Vessels and gear.**
- [[Reef Sampler]]: 34 ft diesel single-screw vessel, 160 hp Perkins engine, Ross Model 200-A Fine Line depth recorder. Slow (8–10 knots) and rolled heavily when not underway.
- [[Fish Hawk]]: privately owned 43 ft hard-chined boat used on several snapper trips with a local captain.
- Two 12-volt standard [[Electric Reel]] snapper reels, each filled with about 200 fathoms of wire.
- Terminal rigs: in-line vertical rig and triangle rig. Triangle rig with dropper assembly of #15 Malin stainless steel wire.
- Hooks: #9 Mustad-Tuna Circle Hooks (best performer), Kirby Sea Hooks, Eagle Claw, Mustad-Sea Master and Sea Demon.
- Baits: squid (excellent), fresh false albacore (*Euthynnus alletteratus*, excellent when fresh), frozen conch (*Strombus gigas*, unsatisfactory).

**Chemical and biological assay methods.** Three approaches were tried: [[Mongoose Bioassay]] and [[Mouse Bioassay]] (following Halstead 1967), [[Human Consumption Bioassay]], and [[Invertebrate Bioassay]] using crabs and mixed protozoan cultures. A separate [[Bacterial Bioassay]] was carried out by Dr. Adolf Spandorf.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Depth zonation.** Species were sampled between about 50 and 200 fathoms along the shelf edge. The report describes the following depth patterns:
- Silk snapper: abundant around rocky ledges in 60 to 150 fathoms; most abundant at 105 to 130 fathoms, with a second peak at 65 to 75 fathoms under intensive trap effort.
- Blackfin snapper: 30 to 110 fathoms, most successfully caught at 50 to 60 fathoms.
- Red grouper: 40 to 85 fathoms.
- Warsaw grouper: deeper water; traps averaged 92 fathoms and hook-and-line averaged 133 fathoms.

**Diel patterns.** Weighted averages suggested that silk snapper and red grouper are caught more often by day, while blackfin and vermilion snappers and Warsaw grouper are more often caught at night. The authors state this needs more data.

**Weather.** Weather was the main barrier to fishing. Only 53 fish were caught over 38 hours during 10 days of moderate-to-heavy seas (wind above 10 knots). From April to June, 14 to 16 possibly favorable days occurred. Fish caught in "rolly" weather were few, possibly because of bait motion or sinker bouncing.

**Diet.** Of about 200 silk snappers from 60 to 90 fathoms, nearly 80% had empty stomachs. Remaining stomachs held bait (mackerel from traps or squid from hooks) or natural prey: two shrimp species, spider crabs, rock crabs and isopods. Juvenile snappers up to 5 in long and a 5-in porcupinefish were also found, along with brittle stars, tunicates and polychaete worms.

**Population structure.** Silk snappers appear to aggregate by size. Mean fork length was 28.4 cm on Saba Bank and 30.1 cm on Anguilla and Barbuda Banks. Hook-caught silk snappers from Virgin Islands waters averaged 46 cm.

**Migration and mortality.** A migration study was judged impractical because of irregular bottom terrain and near-certain mortality of fish brought up for tagging.

## Key Findings

**Fishing effort and yield**
- Between January and June 1970: 27 trips in Reef Sampler and 2 in Fish Hawk, totaling 215 boat hours. 17 trips produced useful catch data; 12 were shakedown or problem-solving cruises.
- 78 hours spent on fishing grounds (10 days south slope, 7 days north) yielded 303 fish, 1357 lb (617 kg), for a combined average of 17.4 lb per hour. Table 2 lists 304 total fish.
- Table 2 CPE (fish per hour): silk snapper 3.9; all species 5.5. Silk snapper catch was 214 fish in 55 hours of effort.
- Silk snapper catches of over 100 per trap in an overnight set were recorded. Ten or more per hour were taken on two electric reels.

**Species assessments**
- [[Silk Snapper]] (*Lutjanus vivanus*): the most promising commercial species. Of 214 tested by controlled human consumption, none caused ill effects. Large Alcyon catches were marketed regionally with no reported poisonings.
- [[Blackfin Snapper]] (*Lutjanus buccanella*): caught in appreciable numbers. A few ciguatera cases have been reported, possibly from fish migrating into reefs. None of the blackfins tested in this study were ciguatoxic. The average hook-and-line depth (91 fathoms) reflected limited effort in its optimum range.
- Other snappers (*Pristipomoides macrophthalmus*, *Rhomboplites aurorubens*, *Etelis oculatus*, *Apsilus dentatus*, *Ocyurus chrysurus*): too infrequently caught to judge toxicity.
- [[Red Grouper]] (*Epinephelus morio*): 25 caught in traps averaged 15 lb at 67 fathoms. Untested for ciguatoxin. Red hind (*E. guttatus*) occurs in a similar range but is usually shallower.
- [[Warsaw Grouper]] (*Epinephelus nigritus*): 20 eaten, with two of the fish (both caught on the shelf edge due south of Flanagan Island) linked to illness. Ten trap-caught fish averaged 12.5 lb from 92 fathoms; 20 hook-caught averaged 16 lb from 133 fathoms.
- [[Jacks]] (Carangidae, four species): notorious ciguatera carriers, but valued as sport fish and bait.

**Human consumption results**
- Of 289 fish released for consumption from VIERS demersal catches, 2 produced toxic reactions. Both were Warsaw grouper from the same location.
- Symptoms were unclear. None showed the hallmark ciguatera signs (lip or mouth paresthesia, paradoxical temperature sensation).
- Authors could not confirm ciguatera because preparation and cooking were uncontrolled.

**Bioassay results**
- [[Mongoose Bioassay]] and [[Mouse Bioassay]]: highly variable. Mouse controls died, and the same fish gave conflicting results in mongoose and mice. Literature conflicts were noted (Helfrich et al. 1968: 7.2% toxicity in mongoose; Halstead 1963: 72% in mice, same Line Islands fish). Ciguatera can persist in fish for up to 3 years (Banner and Helfrich 1966).
- Mongoose became trap-shy in 1970, reducing testing to 2 or 3 bioassays per week of trapping.
- Only jacks and barracudas showed ciguatera-like toxicity in the present tests. A cat fed 2–3 oz of horse-eye jack (*Caranx latus*) reacted, and 4 of 5 mongoose fed 200 g portions also reacted.
- Crab injection and feeding (Portunus, Cancer, Coenobita) and protozoan culture tests produced inconclusive or non-reproducible results.
- [[Bacterial Bioassay]] (Spandorf): extracts of 20 g muscle tissue were tested on *E. coli* and *S. aureus*. Extract growth-enhancement was confounded by added nutrients, and *S. aureus* plates became overgrown with fungal contamination. The method was judged preliminary, with recommended next steps including filtration (0.45 µm), protozoan motility assays and toxin identification.

**Previous work cited:** 58 of 280 large specimens (19 of 32 species) had toxic livers (Dammann ed. 1969).

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Antillean fishery resources are sparse, but a small native shelf-slope fishery could be productive and profitable with government support.
- Licensing of local fishermen, a co-operative fish store and ice-making facility, and low-interest or revolving credit funds were recommended.
- Trap and hook-and-line effort should concentrate on 50 to 75 fathoms if blackfin and red grouper prove non-ciguatoxic in outlying areas.
- Gear recommendations: 12-volt reels operated at 16 volts with a 50 ft warning marker; push-button switches; single-strand #12 stainless main line; triangle terminal rig with #15 Malin dropper assemblies and #9 circle hooks; squid or fresh oily fish as bait.
- Distant banks (Saba, Anguilla, Barbuda) lie 60 to 90 miles from the Virgin Islands and receive little fishing pressure. A 40–60 ft boat could reach them in 6 to 12 hours.
- Ciguatera remains unresolved. No reliable rapid test exists, the toxin's chemistry and life cycle are unknown, and true prevalence in local fish has not been established.
