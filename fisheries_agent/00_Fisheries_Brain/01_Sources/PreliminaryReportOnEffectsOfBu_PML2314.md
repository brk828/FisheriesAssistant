# Bullfrog Predation and Declines of Native Wetland Herpetofauna in Southeastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ranidae #herpetology #colubridae #kinosternidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary report, presented by [[Cecil R. Schwalbe]] and [[Philip C. Rosen]] at a 1988 symposium on amphibian, reptile and small mammal management, tests whether the introduced [[American Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*) drives declines of native wetland herpetofauna in southeastern Arizona. Survey data show bullfrogs are negatively correlated with leopard frogs and garter snakes, and [[Sonoran Mud Turtle]] populations are sharply depressed at the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]. Diet and injury evidence support predation, and a bullfrog removal experiment was initiated to test causation. The authors conclude that bullfrogs have negatively affected native amphibians and reptiles, but that removal effects remain inconclusive.

## Background & Description

The American bullfrog is North America's largest frog and one of its most widely distributed anurans. It is native from Florida to Nova Scotia and west to central Texas, Oklahoma and Kansas, and has been introduced widely into permanent western waters. Bullfrogs are known as voracious, opportunistic predators and have been implicated in declines of native anurans in earlier studies. Less is known about their effects on other vertebrate classes.

A prior study ([[Rosen and Schwalbe 1988]]) linked decline of [[Mexican Garter Snake]] populations to bullfrog predation. [[Hayes and Jennings 1986]] questioned bullfrog predation as a cause of western ranid declines, favouring predation by introduced fish. The present authors aim to document distributional and natural history data implicating bullfrogs and to begin a removal experiment.

The study species include the bullfrog, two [[Leopard Frog]] species (*R. yavapaiensis* and *R. chiricahuensis*), the Mexican garter snake, the checkered garter snake (*T. marcianus*), and the Sonoran mud turtle.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (SBNWR) covers 984 ha in the San Bernardino Valley on the Mexican border, at elevations of 1134 to 1183 m. Chihuahuan desertscrub occupies higher slopes and mesas, while lowlands contain mesquite (*Prosopis velutina*) bosques, sacaton grasslands, four spring-fed ponds and [[Black Draw]], the headwater of the [[Rio Yaqui]]. Cottonwood and willow thickets and cienega pools up to about 2 m deep occur along the drainage.

**North Pond.** The focal site for removal work contains 0.1 ha of open water surrounded by earthen levees, fed by an artesian well, with cattail spreading and submerged macrophytes.

**Survey scope.** Over 50 localities were sampled across central and southern Arizona during 1985–1987, using foot surveys of lotic reaches (2–6 mile reaches) and lentic habitats, with mark-recapture at four intensive sites.

**Native fishes.** Two endangered native fishes, the [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) and the [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*), were found in bullfrog stomachs, indicating predation on native fauna beyond herpetofauna.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution:** Leopard frogs are significantly less common where bullfrogs abound (Spearman rank correlation, p < 0.025). SBNWR was the only site where both bullfrogs and native leopard frogs co-occurred at high density in the survey tables reported. Garter snakes are also significantly less abundant where bullfrogs are present (rank correlation r = -0.420, p < 0.001).
- **Garter snake population structure:** Snakes at SBNWR were dominated by large adults, contrasting with bullfrog-free areas where yearlings and small adults predominate (Mann-Whitney U test, p < 0.001). Garter snakes comprised 57% of garter snakes seen on the refuge in 1985–1988.
- **Injury evidence:** 67.9% of Mexican garter snakes at SBNWR had damaged tails that bled when handled, suggesting unsuccessful bullfrog predation. This injury was not seen elsewhere.
- **Mud turtle scarcity:** Only four turtles were captured in 29 trap-nights at SBNWR (0.14 captures per trap-night). Across 17 other Arizona localities, the mean trap success was 4.32 ± 0.23 captures per trap-night. Quitobaquito Pond, at 0.20, was the only comparable low site, and its population is known to have been reduced by human activity. Only six mud turtles were found on the refuge overall, all large adults born before 1981, whereas juveniles made up over 20% of samples elsewhere.
- **Bullfrog diet:** Invertebrates formed the majority of food items. Vertebrates occurred in 14.6% of stomachs containing food, mostly other frogs (including bullfrogs). Prey also included native fishes, small mammals, a checkered garter snake neonate, a spiny lizard and a probable red-winged blackbird nestling.
- **Bullfrog density:** After removing 74 adult bullfrogs in 1987, the authors estimated a minimum density of 22 adults/ha at North Pond, and a total of 552 bullfrogs (approximately 8 frogs were removed on average) were recorded, with about 55–80% of adult bullfrogs on the refuge estimated removed by that time.
- **Preliminary experimental results:** Leopard frogs bred successfully at a spring source in Black Draw in early 1987, an area open enough that predators could reduce bullfrogs. The first confirmed leopard frog in North Pond was found in a bullfrog stomach in May 1988, and no further increase was observed. The first juvenile Mexican garter snake was captured in fall 1987 after removal.
- **Regulation:** Effective January 1, 1988, the Arizona Game and Fish Commission allowed year-round bullfrog harvest with unlimited bag and possession limits statewide, except La Paz, Mohave and Yuma counties, to discourage accidental release. The three western counties retained a July 1 to November 30 season with a limit of 12 per day.
- **Conclusions:** Bullfrogs have negatively impacted native amphibian and reptile populations in Arizona. Preliminary removal data are inconclusive about recovery of leopard frogs, garter snakes and mud turtles, and more intensive effort will be needed to eliminate bullfrogs from structurally complex habitats.
