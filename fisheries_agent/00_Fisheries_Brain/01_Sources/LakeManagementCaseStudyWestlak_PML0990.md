# Lake Management Case Study: Westlake Lake, Westlake Village, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This Urban Land Institute (ULI) Technical Bulletin, written largely by the lake's homeowner managers, is an in-depth case study of [[Westlake Lake]], a 150-157 acre man-made lake created in the 1960s within the [[Westlake Village]] development in southern California. It traces the lake from planning through a homeowner take-over in 1974, and documents how management shifted from heavy chemical treatment to a biologically based approach. The report covers planning and design, finance, lake privacy, public relations, drainage and silting, chemical versus biological control, aeration, and the laboratory. It is offered as a model for developers, public officials, and lake managers, and it stresses that managing a lake costs money and requires an organized, funded program.

## Background & Description

- **Publisher and authorship:** The Urban Land Institute (ULI), a nonprofit research organization founded in 1936. The bulletin is credited to William A. Hanson and Frans Bigelow, with Bo Bigelow identified as the lake manager and a key author. It is a companion to ULI Technical Bulletin 72, *Lakes and Ponds*.
- **Development:** The land was the former Russell Ranch of about 18 square miles, bought by [[D. K. Ludwig]] in the early 1960s at $2,500 per acre. Bechtel Engineers prepared the planning. The developer was American-Hawaiian Land Company, later partnered with [[Prudential Insurance Company]] of America. The development grew to about 18,000 residents by 1976.
- **Lake physical features:** A dam built from about 1965 on [[Potrero Creek]] (reinforced concrete, anchored in rock) forms the lake, with a crest at 870 feet above sea level. The lake area is 150-157 acres, average depth about 9 feet (23 feet near the dam), and it has about 9 miles of shoreline, 18 inlets, and a central island. Inlets average 90 feet wide, and many are about 600 feet long.
- **Ownership transfer:** The homeowners took over management on January 4, 1974. Of about 1,200 lots, 1,198 voted to accept ownership. A board of 33 elected representatives took over management and operations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Climate:** Semi-desert, with about 15 to 22 inches of rain, mostly falling in November to March in intense storms. Surface water reaches about 80-84°F for roughly four summer months.
- **Stratification and oxygen:** The lake stratifies from mid-April to mid-October, with a thermocline around 10 feet. Anaerobic conditions produce hydrogen sulfide in the bottom layer. Fall turnover can cause fish kills, and only the 15 deepest acres near the dam required aeration.
- **Eutrophication:** Nutrient inputs from street drains (lawn fertilizer and detergent phosphates) accelerated aging of the lake. The report notes nitrates of about 1.4 mg/l N and phosphates of about 1.0 mg/l P, well above the European minima cited (0.3 mg/l N and 0.01 mg/l P).
- **Heavy metals:** Mud near the main drainage input near the marina had 29 ppm lead and 3.4 ppm cadmium, versus 5.9 and 1.2 ppm in control areas.
- **Biota:** Dominant organisms included filamentous shoreline algae (*Cladophora*), blue-green *Aphanizomanon*, the weed *Potamogeton*, and diatoms (*Tabellaria*). Fish included [[Bluegill]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Crappie]], and [[Mosquito Fish]]. Midge larvae and [[Crawfish]] (*Orconectes causeyi*) are also important. Waterfowl included ducks and [[Coots]].

## Key Findings

**Planning and design**
- A 1:2 ratio was adopted: one acre of water to two acres of lake-oriented residential land, giving about 300 acres of lake-oriented living around the lake.
- Potrero Dam, designed by the planners, was chosen over a flood control channel. The lake concept was economically favored over a storm drain of about $3 million.
- Authors advise against placing developments across county lines, which forced a two-bridge design and cost time and money.
- Depth guidance: a minimum of about 8 feet controls weed and algae growth, and a maximum of 12-15 feet limits oxygen-depletion problems. A sloping soil-cement shelf (about 4:1) improves safety and provides spawning habitat.
- Shoreline length, not surface acreage, drives maintenance costs.

**Finance**
- The first-year expenses were close to $100,000 and income was just under $110,000, close to the budget.
- Costs were underestimated by the developer: insurance (later $6,000-$8,000 per year), security (about $4,000 within three years), and accounting were all understated.
- A deficiency assessment of $70,000 for 1974-1976 was set. Average assessments were about $80 per year for lakefront homes and about $24 for off-lake properties.
- Chemical costs fell from $38,800 in 1973 to about $12,000 in 1976 and about $10,000 under the new approach.
- Advice: set aside at least $500 per year for consultants, and expect legal and collection fees to be significant.

**Lake privacy and permits**
- The deed approach was to "take all rights from everyone, then grant them back under permit only."
- Permit income included private dock fees ($72 in 1976), public slip rentals ($240 or $220 per year), boat stickers ($20 per year), and fishing permits.
- Privacy depends on keeping written logs of enforcement, posting signs every 300 feet, and securing title protection.

**Public relations**
- The developer and the homeowners were initially hostile. A 1972 slide presentation of lake conditions pushed the developer toward cooperation, and a test homeowner group was formed before the transfer.
- A 1974 newspaper story headlined "Fish Die from Poisons" damaged sales and reputation. The authors describe the lesson as honest, ongoing public education.
- A newsletter and annual meetings helped secure public support, including approval for a roughly $6,000 aeration program.

**Chemical versus biological control**
- Early chemical use (Hydrothol, Diquat, Paraquat, Weedtrine) created a biologically sterile lake, and resistant *Cladophora* strains developed.
- The summer 1974 fish kill, following a large chemical application, led the board to ban Hydrothol, Paraquat, and Diquat. Durisban and Abate were restricted and later banned in 1976.
- Copper-sulfate crystals and copper-sulfate/citric-acid "tea bag" treatments replaced the harsher chemicals. [[Casoron]] G-10 was used for pre-emergent weed control.
- Algae grid counts, taken twice weekly, were used to time treatments, and chemical costs fell from $38,000 to about $10,000 per year.

**Aeration**
- A destratification system with four 3/4-horsepower piston air pumps (about $3,429 total) was installed in 1975. It was run from 9:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m. during the stratified months.
- After aeration, the 3-week hydrogen sulfide smell faded, and a dam gate opening caused no complaints within 6 hours.

**Laboratory and monitoring**
- The lab was established with equipment costing about $3,000 to $4,000, and it required about 10-15 hours per week.
- The authors recommend a year of baseline dissolved oxygen and temperature data before installing any aeration system.

**Later problems and outlook (1976)**
- A 2-4 inch rainstorm after 10 months of drought caused an early turnover, and a fish kill occurred near street drains. Herbicide and pesticide runoff was suspected but not proven.
- The authors plan basins at major drain inputs with chlorinators, copper sulfate, and alum treatments, and they cite water savings of about $65,000 per year from street drainage.
- Other management notes include limits on the duck population (200 ducks relocated), coot control efforts, and a 1976 insurance cancellation that raised liability insurance quotes up to $30,000 per year.
