# Dispersal Patterns of Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the dispersal, distribution, body condition, forage availability, and diet of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the [[Colorado River]] of Utah and Colorado. Mark-recapture data collected from 1991 to 1995 show that adults generally moved upstream, that dispersal distance declined with fish size, and that fish moved from the lower reach to the upper reach but almost never in the reverse direction. The authors attribute these patterns mainly to a search for better food resources, with temperature trade-offs also playing a role. The lower reach offers warmer water and abundant small non-native minnows, but larger fish there show declining body condition. The upper reach offers cooler water and better prey for larger adults. The authors propose that upstream dispersal is a response to food resource gradients.

## Background & Description

*Ptychocheilus lucius* is a large, endangered cyprinid native to the Colorado River basin. Understanding its life history, including the movements needed to reach spawning, nursery, and feeding habitats, is central to its recovery. The study was conducted by researchers from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Colorado River Fishery Project), the Colorado and Utah Divisions of Wildlife, and Utah State University.

Adult squawfish are piscivorous and are thought to prefer soft-rayed, fusiform prey. Spined and spiny-rayed fish are considered costlier to ingest and riskier to swallow. Prey size is assumed to be limited to about half the predator's length, a rule drawn from related piscivores such as [[Northern Pike]] and [[Muskellunge]].

### Study Area and Methods

- **Study area:** The river was divided into seven strata (three downstream and three upstream of [[Westwater Canyon]]). A 19-km section of Westwater Canyon was excluded for logistical reasons.
- **Fish capture:** Fish were captured by [[Electrofishing]] and [[Trammel Netting]]. Each fish was measured, weighed, and tagged with a [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging|PIT tag]] if untagged.
- **Movement criteria:** Only consecutive captures at least one year apart were used. Displacements under 10 km were classed as localized and excluded. Spawning-period captures were excluded.
- **Condition:** [[Relative Body Condition]] (Kn) was calculated using month-specific length-weight regressions.
- **Forage:** Relative abundance of prey was estimated from seine, electrofishing, and trammel-net [[Catch Per Unit Effort]] indices.
- **Diet:** Stomach contents from 1994 fish were collected with a stomach sampler, preserved, and analyzed by volume.
- **Temperature:** Main-channel temperatures were recorded at several sites, and annual [[Thermal Units for Growth]] were calculated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Temperature and Growth

The most downstream thermograph site recorded the most days at or near 25°C, the preferred temperature for yearling and adult squawfish. Daily averages there never exceeded 26°C during four years of monitoring, and this site had the greatest annual thermal units for growth. Thermal units declined upstream. At Rulison, mean daily temperatures never reached 20°C in three of the five years studied. Laboratory work cited by the authors found that yearlings held at 20°C grew only about 54% as fast as those at 25°C.

### Prey Community

Small fish (<100 mm) were denser in lower-reach backwaters, at 5.71 versus 3.31 fish per area seined (SE 0.4 for each). Three introduced cyprinids, [[Red Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and sand shiner, made up 84–99% of fish in annual reach samples. Larger soft-rayed fusiform fish (>100 mm) were generally more abundant in the upper reach. Spined species such as [[Common Carp]] and suckers were less important prey.

### Barriers

The reach above the Grand Valley, which includes Rulison and Cameo, has been blocked by diversion structures since the turn of the century. The authors suggest it may mark the fringe of the species' former range in the Colorado River.

## Key Findings

### Distribution and Size Structure

- Mean squawfish length increased progressively upstream. In 1991–1992, lengths in several upper strata differed significantly from the stratum 1 mean, and regressions of length on river kilometer were significant (r² = 0.59 in 1991; r² = 0.60 in 1992; P < 0.0001).
- Adults (≥500 mm) were scarce in the lower reach in 1991 (CPUE 0.02 per net, N = 47 net sets) compared with the upper reach (CPUE 0.30, N = 139).
- Adult CPUE rose to 0.20 per net (N = 115) in the lower reach in 1993. The authors link this increase to the recruitment of two or three strong year-classes produced in the mid-1980s.

### Movement

- **Upper reach fish:** Of 69 recaptured fish, 16 of 92 recaptures (17%) were more than 10 km from the previous capture site. Twelve of these 16 displacements were upstream, three downstream, and one both ways.
- **Lower reach fish:** Of 37 recaptured fish, 25 of 43 recaptures (58%) moved more than 10 km. Seventeen of these 25 (68%) were upstream. For movements over 25 km (N = 23), 74% were upstream (P = 0.037).
- Mean upstream displacement was 108.2 km (SE 16.5), compared with 35.1 km (SE 6.9) for downstream displacements (P = 0.0005).
- **Lower-to-upper transfers:** Eleven of 25 long-distance lower-reach movements (44%) ended in the upper reach. The share of lower-reach recaptures found in the upper reach rose from 0 of 1 in 1992 to 5 of 12 in 1995.
- **Upper-to-lower transfers:** No upper-reach fish were recaptured in the lower reach.
- **Size and dispersal:** Mean displacement between captures fell with size. It was 43.8 km for fish under 500 mm (N = 19 pairs cited in the text), 13.3 km for 500–599 mm, and 3.1 km for fish 600 mm and longer. Long-distance displacement (>10 km) was 50% or more for fish under 500 mm and 6–7% for fish 600 mm and longer.

### Body Condition

- **Lower reach:** Relative condition declined as length increased. Fish 500–699 mm had significantly lower Kn than smaller size-classes.
- **Upper reach:** Relative condition increased with length, peaking in fish 700–799 mm.
- Reach differences were significant for 500–599 mm and 600–699 mm fish (P < 0.0004).

### Diet

- Stomach samples were collected from 1994 fish. Fathead minnows were the most common prey. Roundtail chub and white suckers averaged 85 mm and 70 mm (SL), respectively.
- Feeding rate appeared lower before 26 May, when a higher share of empty stomachs was recorded.
- Anecdotal records include a 687-mm fish with a 235-mm sucker (34% of its length) and an 860-mm fish with a 310-mm sucker hybrid (36% of its length). These suggest that large adults take much larger prey than the small fish sampled.

### Interpretation and Hypotheses

- Upstream dispersal is interpreted as a search for food. Bluegill studies suggest that fish select lower temperatures when rations are low. The authors propose that similar temperature selection, combined with food searches, drives upstream movement against the current and away from the warmest reach.
- The authors considered four hypotheses for movement timing: hunger-driven exploration, an innate directional tendency, movement tied to spawning, and a combined hunger-spawning model. They judged the combined model most plausible. In this model, young adults learn food gradients during their first spawning migration and then explore upstream after spawning.
- Adult squawfish are not strongly territorial. They concentrate in backwaters during spring runoff and occasionally occur side by side during base flows.

### Conclusions

- Dispersal is directed upstream and declines with fish size. Upper-reach habitats appear to offer a better balance of temperature and food for adults.
- Growth potential is highest in the lower reach, but adults there may face food shortages under warmer, metabolically demanding conditions.
- Recruitment of strong year-classes raised adult abundance river-wide during the study.
