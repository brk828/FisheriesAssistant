# The Introduced Aquatic Macrophyte *Myriophyllum spicatum* as Habitat for Fish and Their Invertebrate Prey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how the Eurasian macrophyte [[Myriophyllum Spicatum]] (Eurasian watermilfoil), which spread through [[Lake Opinicon]] after 1974, affects fish and their invertebrate prey. The study compares fish and invertebrate distribution before and after the invasion (1972 vs. 1979–1980) and compares milfoil beds with native [[Potamogeton-Vallisneria Community]] beds in the summer of 1980. Fish were sampled with gill nets, and invertebrates with Ekman grabs, plant-foliage sampling and emergence traps. The milfoil dominated the 2.0–3.5 m zone, which had been mostly open water, but penetrated the native shallows only marginally. Within the milfoil zone, fish and prey invertebrates declined, and native shallows supported markedly more prey. The author concludes that net impact on fish populations has so far been modest, but replacement of native shallow beds would be a concern.

## Background & Description

*Myriophyllum spicatum* colonized Canadian lakes in the 1960s and 1970s. Its compact growth form clogs waterways, reduces water flow, may alter summer temperature profiles, and may increase sedimentation, all of which could affect fish. Before this study, only [[Robinson 1981 Okanagan Study]] had assessed fish effects, and it found little impact.

The study site, Cow Island Bay, lies on the north side of Lake Opinicon, a eutrophic lake of 890 ha with a maximum depth of 11 m in the central [[Rideau System]]. The lake had been the subject of earlier long-term fish studies, which made it a suitable site. Milfoil reached the central Rideau in 1974. In the 2.0–3.5 m zone it formed stands from bottom to surface, in places approaching monoculture. The native shallows retained diverse beds of *Potamogeton* and *Vallisneria*, with the milfoil present in scattered clumps.

Methods included:
- **Plant sampling:** ten 1 m² quadrats per depth, with dry-weight biomass and [[Simpson Diversity Index]] calculations.
- **Substrate and water chemistry:** [[Ekman Grab]] sampling, organic content, temperature and dissolved oxygen profiles.
- **Fish sampling:** [[Gill Net Sampling]] in five depth zones (1.0–1.3, 2.5, 3.5, 5.0 and 7.0 m) in May and late July, with twelve 24-hour settings per net section. Comparisons used the [[Wilcoxon Signed Ranks Test]].
- **Juvenile fish:** [[Minnow Traps]] for young-of-year centrarchids in August 1980.
- **Benthos:** 50 Ekman grab samples (225 cm²) per series.
- **Foliage fauna:** ten plants of each species collected from late May to August.
- **Emergence:** [[Insect Emergence Traps]] from 1 May to 15 September 1980.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat change.** In 1972 the 2.5–3.5 m zone held six major low-growing species, with few tall columns of *Myriophyllum exalbescens*. By 1979–1980, milfoil made up 94.4% and 88.6% of dry-weight biomass at 2.5 m (May and July 1980), and 88.8% and 80.6% at 3.5 m. In the 1.0–1.3 m shallows it made up only about 60–61% (Table 2). Shallow native biomass changed little between 1972 and 1980.

**Temperature and oxygen.** Summer temperature profiles did not differ between milfoil and open-water areas, so the milfoil had no effect on late-summer temperatures. Dissolved oxygen did change. At the substrate–water interface inshore, oxygen was 0.8–1.6 ppm. At 3.0 m, dense milfoil held 1.6–5.6 ppm, compared with 5.8–7.0 ppm in open water.

**Substrate.** Beneath milfoil, the substrate was an organic ooze 0.2–0.4 m deep, with high clay content and 40–70% organic carbon, resembling gyttja. Beneath native beds it was firm fine sand with 4.8% average organic carbon. In 1972 an organic ooze was found only in the deeper parts of the lake.

**Fish community.** Of the ten species netted, bluegill, pumpkinseed, rock bass, black crappie, pike, alewife, golden shiner and yellow perch were caught in sufficient numbers to show trends. Largemouth bass and brown bullhead were rare and mostly inshore.

**Spawning.** Pumpkinseed nest counts were 130 and 145 active nests per 500 m of shoreline in 1971 and 1972, and 119 in 1980. Rock bass nests were 11, 9 and 10, and largemouth bass nests 7, 5 and 8. Bluegill colonies, previously 40–150 nests per year, moved out of the milfoil zone. A 150-nest colony persisted in a channel 2.4 m deep with good water movement and no milfoil.

## Key Findings

**Offshore (2.0–3.5 m) changes, 1972 vs. 1979–1980**
- Bluegill were significantly fewer in the milfoil zone, particularly in July at 2.5 m and in May and July at 3.5 m.
- Black crappie, golden shiner and alewife were more numerous in 1979–1980, suggesting the milfoil favoured these species.
- Five invertebrate taxa were significantly lower at 2.5 m, and three at 3.5 m, in 1979 than in 1972. Isopods, chironomid larvae, Trichoptera larvae and Ephemeroptera nymphs were the main reductions.
- Changes at 1.0–1.3 m and 5.0 m were minor, which the author takes to indicate that the invader, rather than time, caused the shifts.

**Shallow (1.0–1.3 m) comparison, summer 1980**
- By day in May, eight times as many bluegill and twice as many pumpkinseed were netted in the native beds. In July, three to six times as many bluegill, pumpkinseed and yellow perch were netted in native beds by day.
- Rock bass, black crappie and pike were more numerous in milfoil at night, which the author attributes to dusk dispersal and feeding movements.
- Five major benthic prey taxa were three to seven times more abundant beneath native beds. Isopods and chironomid larvae were the most affected.
- Foliage of *Potamogeton* plus *Vallisneria* carried about four times as many invertebrates as milfoil in May, twice as many in June and August, and three times as many in July.
- Insect emergence was almost twice as high over native beds: at Cow Island Bay, 4,476 vs. 2,980 insects/m², and at Rabbit Island, 3,271 vs. 1,260 insects/m² (Table 8).

**Large bloodworms**
- In July, milfoil benthos held significant numbers of large *Chironomus* larvae, which were previously restricted to deeper suspended-sediment areas. The author links this to the changed substrate.

**Mechanisms**
- The author proposes that the milfoil slows water movement and traps fine material, producing a poorer substrate and lower oxygen. Dense foliage with simple leaves may also support fewer invertebrates, consistent with [[Dvorak and Best 1982]], who found milfoil had the poorest invertebrate fauna of eight macrophytes.
- The fish response may also reflect predation risk. Werner et al. (1983) found that young bluegill prefer weedy habitat despite lower forage returns in open water.

**Overall conclusions**
- Five years after invasion, the net effect on Lake Opinicon's fish populations was limited. The milfoil has affected distribution, not overall abundance, and bluegill appear to be most affected.
- Most of the milfoil-invaded area had previously been open water. Invasion of the native shallows has been marginal.
- The author warns that if milfoil replaced the native shallow beds, the resulting prey loss would be a serious concern for fish populations.
