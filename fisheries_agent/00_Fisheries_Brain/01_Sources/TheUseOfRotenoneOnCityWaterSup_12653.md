## Executive Summary

This 1962 Illinois Department of Conservation mimeo report documents the operational use of **[[Rotenone]]**, a piscicide, as a fisheries management tool on municipal water supply lakes across Illinois between 1956 and 1961. The document is not a species morphology study but a management case-report describing how chemical renovation was used to eliminate or reduce populations of undesirable and "stunted" fish—principally **[[Common Carp]]**, **[[Bullhead]]** species, and **[[Gizzard Shad]]**—which were degrading water clarity and sport fishery quality in these impoundments. Following treatment, lakes were typically restocked with desirable sport species, primarily **[[Largemouth Bass]]** and **[[Redear Sunfish]]**. The report catalogs 11 named lakes (plus 2 watershed-only treatments) with treatment type (partial, selective, complete), rotenone dosage, area treated, cost, and fish biomass removed.

## Background & Description

The source contains no morphometric or anatomical data on individual fish. Instead, it characterizes species by their **ecological behavior and functional role** within the impounded lake systems:

- **[[Common Carp]] / [[Bullhead]] species** — described as "rough fish" exhibiting a "mud rooting" habit that keeps lake water chronically turbid throughout the year.
- **[[Gizzard Shad]]** — classified functionally as a forage fish; it does not enter the pole-and-line (angler) catch and can reach high population densities.
- **[[Largemouth Bass]] / [[Bluegill]]** — described as **sight feeders**, whose foraging efficiency and growth are impaired directly by turbid water conditions caused by rough fish activity.
- Fish biomass removed during treatments was recorded in aggregate pounds by species/group (e.g., carp, black bullhead, white crappie, bluegill, green sunfish, warmouth bass, gizzard shad), providing indirect standing-crop estimates for these systems (see Key Findings table).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report frames lake fish communities in terms of **trophic imbalance** rather than individual habitat preference:

- **Turbidity-driven habitat degradation**: Benthic-foraging rough fish (carp, bullheads) resuspend sediment, creating chronically turbid conditions that structurally disadvantage sight-feeding predators and panfish ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]]).
- **Forage base overpopulation**: Excessive **[[Gizzard Shad]]** populations were noted to shift bass foraging effort away from cropping small panfish, contributing to stunted panfish populations (bluegill, crappie) and an "out-of-balance" community structure. Contemporary studies cited in the source suggested large gizzard shad populations exert a depressing effect on panfish growth rates, likely via food/space competition.
- **Density-dependent stunting**: Overpopulation of otherwise desirable species (e.g., bass with poor reproduction/survival) was identified as a self-limiting population imbalance correctable via partial density reduction, after which survivors exhibited improved growth rate and size.
- **Post-treatment restocking niche**: Treated lakes were deliberately reset via stocking with **[[Largemouth Bass]]** and **[[Redear Sunfish]]**, indicating a managed succession strategy favoring predator-sunfish sport assemblages over rough-fish/forage-fish dominated communities.

## Key Findings

**Regulatory/Historical context**: Rotenone has been used in U.S. fisheries management since 1934; its first application to an Illinois city water supply was **Canton Lake, Fulton County (1956)**. By 1962, 11 Illinois city water supply lakes had received partial, selective, or complete rotenone treatments (plus 2 additional watershed-only treatments), all conducted under fishery biologist supervision with prior [[Illinois Department of Public Health]] approval.

**Restocking practice**: Lakes subjected to complete treatment (e.g., West/East Twin Lakes, Virginia Lake, Lake Eureka, Spring Lake) were consistently restocked with **[[Largemouth Bass]]** and **[[Redear Sunfish]]**, reflecting a standardized Illinois management protocol of "chemical reset + sport fish restocking" for underperforming or rough-fish-dominated water supply impoundments.

**Disposal logistics**: Removed fish biomass was typically collected and disposed of either by municipal crews, by volunteer sportsmen, or jointly by both, indicating a community-cooperative operational model for large-scale rotenone renovations.
