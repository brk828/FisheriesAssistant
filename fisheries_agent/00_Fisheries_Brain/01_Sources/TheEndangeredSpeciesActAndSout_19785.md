# The Endangered Species Act and Southwest Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #salmonidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Fisheries bulletin article by [[James E. Johnson]] and [[John N. Rinne]] (Fisheries, Vol. 7, No. 4, July-August 1982) reviews the status of native fishes of the American Southwest under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The authors argue that the region's fauna, marked by high endemism and specialized adaptations, is facing severe and progressive decline driven mainly by habitat loss from dams, diversion, pumping, and pollution. They review the Act's listing, protection, and recovery components, then examine five recovery cases in detail. They conclude that recovery depends on habitat protection, reintroduction, and interagency cooperation, and they propose policy changes, including an "experimental" listing category and removal of the critical habitat requirement.

## Background & Description

The native fish fauna of the Southwest is characterized by [[Endemism]] and unique physiological, morphological, and behavioral traits. Representative species range from the large [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] to the minute [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]] and the [[Arizona Trout]].

The paper defines the American Southwest, following [[Brown and Lowe]] (1980), as Arizona, New Mexico, west Texas, and southern portions of California, Nevada, Utah, and Colorado. The authors note that Johnson worked with desert fishes from 1965 and joined the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Office of Endangered Species in 1976; Rinne has studied desert fish habitats and distribution with the [[U.S. Forest Service]] since 1976.

The [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973) defines an endangered species as one in danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of its range, and a threatened species as one likely to become endangered within the foreseeable future. The Act was amended in 1978 and 1979, and the authors note reauthorization was due in 1982.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Scale of decline.** Of 44 U.S. fish species listed under the Act, 27 (61%) are endemic to the Southwest. Of 251 North American taxa identified as likely candidates for listing (Deacon et al. 1979), 94 (47%) are native to the Southwest. Among native species, 22 of 27 (81%) in Arizona and 31 of 73 (42%) in New Mexico are listed as nearing extinction. Extirpation has affected 19% of Arizona's native fishes and 16% of New Mexico's. In California, 7 of 20 desert-region native taxa are extinct and 6 are endangered.

**Habitat loss.** Flow regulation began with the [[Roosevelt Dam]] (1911) on the Salt River and with [[Boulder Dam]] (closed 1932) on the [[Colorado River]], which the authors describe as perhaps the most regulated river in the world. Dams produce cold tailwaters (about 10 °C) favoring more than 100 introduced species over the 30+ natives. Remaining semipristine reaches in canyons such as Desolation, Grand, and Westwater still hold some natives but face cold water, non-native fishes, proposed dams, and dewatering. The [[Rio Grande]] has suffered similar impacts.

**Desert springs.** Springs range from seeps to hundreds of cubic meters per minute. Endemic fish and invertebrates have evolved in these isolated "aquatic islands." Groundwater pumping may cause "the greatest faunal loss in North America" within 20 years. Texas illustrates the trend: of 281 major historic springs, only Comal and San Marcos remain among the four originally very large springs, and 63 have completely failed.

**Competition and replacement.** Non-native species, including [[Mosquitofish]] and black bullheads, have replaced natives in many refugia.

## Key Findings

**Legislative framework**
- The Act requires federal agencies to conserve listed species and prohibits private taking without a permit.
- Citizens may bring civil suits to enforce the Act.
- As of 1982, 148 vertebrate species were listed as endangered and 33 as threatened in the U.S.; 32 and 12 of these, respectively, are fishes.
- Section 7 consultation: 1,632 consultations were reviewed in Arizona, New Mexico, Texas, and Oklahoma over the decade; only 13 were judged likely to jeopardize a listed species, and alternatives were proposed in each such case.
- Enforcement emphasizes deterrence over arrest and conviction.
- About 15 approved recovery plans were operating for Southwestern fishes.

**Recovery case studies**
- **[[Gila Trout]]** (*Salmo gilae*, endangered): fewer than 10,000 individuals persist in eight headwater streams. Hatchery rearing began in 1923; the first reintroduction was in McKnight Creek in 1970. Recovery requires replicating five natural populations to reach threatened status.
- **[[Arizona Trout]]** (*Salmo apache*, threatened): occupies about 5% of its former range. The recovery goal is 30 self-sustaining populations before delisting. Hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]] and genetic swamping are key threats.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): a 1978 threatened proposal was withdrawn in 1980. In 1981 Arizona and the Service signed a memorandum of understanding for reintroduction. About 20,000 fingerlings were produced at Dexter in 1981; nearly one million larvae were produced in 1982, with 700,000 stocked into the Salt River. The authors call it the first major cooperative non-game reintroduction in the Southwest.
- **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*, endangered since 1967): persists in about 60 km of the Virgin River and 7 km of LaVerkin Creek. The 1979 recovery plan's stocking of the upper Gila River had not occurred at the time of writing, because the Bureau of Land Management questioned potential impacts. The proposed Allen-Warner Valley Project threatens flows.
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*, endangered): once the most abundant fish in the Gila basin. By 1977 only ten wild and three stocked populations remained; by 1981, five wild populations were dominated by mosquitofish. A 1981 three-way memorandum set a goal of 50 new self-sustaining populations, with reintroductions starting in May 1982 and 72 sites stocked by July 1982.

**Authors' projections and recommendations**
- Four assumptions underlie the outlook: reauthorization of the Act in 1982, continued southwestern human migration, intensifying water competition, and continued habitat deterioration.
- Recovery efforts will likely favour birds and mammals (e.g., [[Whooping Crane]], [[California Condor]], [[Black-footed Ferret]]); fish recovery will be overshadowed by habitat loss unless the trend is reversed.
- Recommended priorities: (1) setting goals for protecting specific habitats, (2) reintroducing extirpated species, and (3) increasing agency cooperation.
- Habitat protection options include federal management, prioritized selection of the most valuable habitats, and ecosystem-based recovery teams (e.g., the San Marcos River plan covering four listed species).
- The authors propose an **experimental** listing category, first recommended by the [[Desert Fishes Council]] more than four years earlier, for reintroduced populations.
- They propose eliminating the critical habitat requirement from the 1978 amendment, which they say has caused more conflict than protection; the San Marcos River example, with a lawsuit by the Edwards Underground Water District, is cited.

**Epilogue**
- Over 42,000 bonytail chub were stocked into Lake Mohave on November 9, 1981.
- The Tecopa pupfish (*Cyprinodon nevadensis calidae*) was declared extinct in 1982.
- In April 1982 the Service purchased San Bernardino Ranch in Arizona to protect the last U.S. Rio Yaqui aquatic habitat.
- The authors conclude that habitat protection, and public awareness of it, is the long-term answer, and that some recovery failures should be expected.
