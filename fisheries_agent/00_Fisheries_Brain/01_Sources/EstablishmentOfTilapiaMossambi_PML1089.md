# California Fish and Game Notes: Tilapia Establishment, Octopod Records, and Desert Fish Literature

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This scanned document is a compilation of short notes and reference pages from a California Fish and Game publication (late 1960s), with a few partly illegible pages. It covers four topics: (1) the establishment of [[Mozambique Tilapia]] in drains of Bard Valley, Imperial County; (2) a [[Scrub Jay]] apparently feeding on ectoparasites of a [[Columbian Black-Tailed Deer]]; (3) morphometric measurements of a male [[Ocythoe Tuberculata]] octopod and its occurrence in salps; and (4) a page of cited literature on [[Sonoran Desert]] stream fishes. The main concern is the unauthorized spread of a non-native cichlid in southern California.

## Background & Description

- **Tilapia introduction:** [[Mozambique Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica*) is a non-native cichlid. Its first documented California records were in Bard Valley. Tilapia were stocked by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] in several drains near Yuma, and the Yuma canal system population is described as self-supporting for some six years.
- **Scrub jay observation:** A [[California Scrub Jay]] was seen on a three-point buck in Marin County on August 1969 and was presumed to be removing ticks or [[Deer Keds]].
- **Octopod specimen:** A male *Ocythoe tuberculata* was measured and compared with published reports. The octopod is pelagic and was recorded in salps (gelatinous tunicates).
- **Literature page:** The final reference pages cite work on [[Desert Stream Fishes]], [[Longfin Dace]], and a poeciliid topminnow (name garbled in OCR), along with studies of flood effects on stream fish communities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Bard Valley drains:** Tilapia were found in the Araz Drain and the Reservation Main Drain. Water temperatures in these ditches seldom drop below 60 °F, which the authors compared with the lower temperature limits reported for *T. mossambica* (Kelly, 1957). This thermal regime supports year-round establishment.

**Spread pathways:** The authors attribute Bard Valley populations to either natural migration from Yuma-area introductions or unauthorized introductions. Because tilapia are popular sport fish, the authors expected further dispersal in southern California through angler introductions.

**Parasite interaction:** The scrub jay's behavior suggests a commensal or mutualistic relationship with deer, in which birds remove ectoparasites. Dixon (1944) reported similar behavior in Sequoia National Forest, and local residents considered it common.

**Desert streams:** The literature cited in the final pages describes Sonoran Desert stream ecology. Key themes include habitat use, flood disturbance and differential selection, and hydrologic budgets after cloudburst floods. These studies relate to the native fish communities that non-native species could affect.

## Key Findings

1. **Tilapia established in Bard Valley:** Presence was confirmed in July 1968 by [[Franklin Hoover]] and [[Marschall Stevens]] of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]]. Identification was verified by [[Ethelwynn Trewavas]] at the British Museum (Natural History). The only previously known California record was a small ditch near the Hot Mineral Spa in Imperial County, about several miles northwest of Bard.
2. **Collection record:** Tilapia were collected from the Araz Drain in July 1968, and from the Reservation Main Drain in July 1968 and in February, May, and June 1969. Exact mileage of occupied drains is illegible in the scan.
3. **Introduction history:** The population is probably from Yuma-area stocking, natural migration, or unauthorized introduction. Further spread by anglers is expected.
4. **Thermal tolerance:** Ditch temperatures seldom drop below 60 °F, which suits *T. mossambica* (Kelly, 1957).
5. **Scrub jay ectoparasite feeding:** Observed in Marin County on August 1969. The jay removed parasites from a deer's head and back for two to three minutes. The deer stayed motionless and was not disturbed.
6. **Octopod morphometrics:** Measurements from the specimen include:
   - Length of hectocotylus arm: 61 mm
   - Mantle length (ventral): 54 mm
   - Mantle breadth: 15.5 mm
   - Length of ventral arm: 21 mm
   - Ventral length of first left arm: 13.5 mm
   - Diameter of largest sucker, fourth left arm: 1.2 mm (OCR-uncertain)
7. **Salp occurrence:** The note cites [[Jatta]] (1896), who recorded a male *Ocythoe tuberculata* in a salp, and an 1880 record by Schmidtlein of a male *Argonauta argo* in a salp.
8. **Literature base:** The final pages list references on [[Desert Fishes]] ecology, flood effects on [[Stream Fish Communities]], and habitat use of desert stream fishes.
