# The Use of Sedatives to Reduce Manipulative Error in Measuring Fish

#ichthyology #fishery-science #salmonidae #percidae #methodology

## Executive Summary

This 1958 study by Wilbur L. Hartman investigated whether sedating fish with [[Urethane]] reduced measurement error and improved accuracy when recording fork and total lengths. Four groups of fish were measured repeatedly while normal and while drugged: yearling [[Brown Trout]], yearling [[Lake Trout]], adult [[Pikeperch]] (walleye), and adult [[Lake Trout]]. Although statistically significant reductions in manipulative error occurred in half the comparisons (both measurements for lake trout yearlings and adults), the practical value was negligible—requiring only one additional normal fish per 100 to match the accuracy of drugged samples. Drugged fish did not measure significantly longer than normal fish, fork and total length measurements showed essentially equal accuracy, and anesthetization did not appreciably reduce measurement time under the study conditions.

## Background & Description

The study examined manipulative error, defined as variation among repeated measurements of fork and total lengths on the same fish with temporal effects removed. Sample lots consisted of five fish each from four groups:

- **Lot 1**: [[Brown Trout]] yearlings (7.7 to 9.3 inches total length) from U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Hatchery, [[Cortland, New York]]
- **Lot 2**: [[Lake Trout]] yearlings (5.4 to 7.5 inches total length) from New York State Fish Hatchery, [[Bath, New York]]
- **Lot 3**: [[Pikeperch]] adults (15.7 to 16.8 inches total length) from [[Oneida Lake]], held at New York State Fish Hatchery, [[Constantia, New York]]
- **Lot 4**: [[Lake Trout]] adults (25.4 to 28.1 inches total length) from [[Cayuga Lake]], gill netted by Cornell University

Fish were measured five times each while normal, then drugged in 0.5% [[Urethane]] solution until completely relaxed (3-5 minutes), and measured five more times. Yearling fish were measured to the nearest millimeter; adults to the nearest 0.1 inch. The author deliberately avoided measuring more accurately than typical field conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was prompted by discussion following DeRoche and Bond (1955), who noted that fish recaptured after tagging appeared shorter than when originally tagged while drugged. This raised concerns about potential bias in length measurements used for [[Growth Studies]], [[Population Dynamics]], and fishery management.

Previous research had demonstrated various advantages of sedatives in fishery work:
- Griffiths et al. (1941): easier spawning of adult [[Steelhead Trout]] with lower egg mortality
- Johnson (1954): time savings taking eggs from [[Brook Trout]] with no adverse hatch effects
- Foster (1941): greater speed and accuracy in fin-clipping [[Cutthroat Trout]]
- Eschmeyer (1953): time savings marking fingerling [[Lake Trout]]

The study was conducted at [[Cornell University]] Department of Conservation with fish provided by New York State Conservation Department, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and Cornell University.

## Key Findings

**Manipulative Error Reduction:**
- Statistically significant reductions (p < 0.01) occurred in four of eight comparisons: fork and total lengths for both [[Lake Trout]] yearlings and adults
- For [[Lake Trout]] yearlings: fork length F = 3.48**, total length F = 4.96** (16 degrees of freedom)
- For [[Lake Trout]] adults: fork length F = 4.17**, total length F = 9.33** (16 degrees of freedom)
- [[Brown Trout]] yearlings and [[Pikeperch]] adults showed no significant reductions
- **Critical finding**: Even highly significant reductions had negligible practical value—a sample of 101 normal fish provided the same accuracy as 100 drugged fish

**Measurement Bias:**
- No significant differences found between lengths of drugged versus normal fish (F = 0.26 for [[Lake Trout]] yearlings, p > 0.05)
- Drugged fish did not measure significantly longer despite being relaxed
- No evidence of systematic bias introduced by sedation

**Fork vs. Total Length Accuracy:**
- Standard deviations for [[Pikeperch]] adults: fork length 0.056 inches, total length 0.050 inches
- Standard deviations for [[Lake Trout]] yearlings: fork length 0.956 mm, total length 1.168 mm
- F-tests showed no significant differences between fork and total length measurement variances in any of the four groups
- Conclusion contradicts Carlander and Smith (1945) who found total length generally more accurate for [[Perch]], [[Crappies]], and [[Pikeperch]]

**Time Requirements:**
- Total time for 100 fork and 100 total length measurements: 25 minutes 47 seconds (normal fish) vs. 20 minutes 18 seconds (drugged fish)
- Apparent 20% time reduction negated by 5+ minutes required for anesthetization
- Net time savings negligible under good working conditions
- Oregon Game Commission (1950) reported 31% tagging time reduction with anesthesia, plus slightly higher survival rates

**Statistical Methods:**
- Analysis of variance used to partition variation between fish, between trials, and manipulative error
- One-tailed F-tests compared manipulative error variance for normal vs. drugged fish
- Sample size calculations (adapted from Snedecor 1946) determined practical significance
- Manipulative error variances for [[Brown Trout]] yearlings: 0.925 (normal) vs. 0.825 (drugged) for fork length

**Practical Implications:**
- Under good working conditions, sedatives provide minimal benefit for simple length measurements
- Sedatives may prove more valuable when field conditions are adverse or when extensive handling is required (stripping, tagging, weighing)
- [[Urethane]] noted as now recognized carcinogen with curtailed use, though principle remains valid for other sedatives
- Study conditions deliberately replicated typical field measurement accuracy rather than maximum precision
