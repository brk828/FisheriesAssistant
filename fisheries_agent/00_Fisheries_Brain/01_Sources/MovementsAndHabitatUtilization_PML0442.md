# Movements and Habitat Utilization of Reintroduced Razorback Suckers and Colorado Squawfish in the Verde River, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department special report (September 1993) describes radiotelemetry of hatchery-raised, adult-sized [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] released into the upper [[Verde River]] as part of the [[Reintroduction Program]] for these endangered fishes in the [[Gila River Basin]]. Fifteen squawfish and 24 razorback suckers were implanted with radiotransmitters and tracked by weekly [[Aerial Telemetry]] and intensive [[Ground Telemetry]]. Both species selected slow currents, pools, and sand substrates, and avoided riffles. Movements were generally small and sedentary, with night-time activity higher than daytime. The authors judged the success of the adult-sized reintroductions "equivocal," because no implanted fish was confirmed to survive a complete summer, and heavy infestation by the exotic parasitic copepod [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] may have contributed to mortality.

## Background & Description

The two species are large, endangered native fishes of the Colorado River system. Their declines have been attributed in part to habitat alteration and to competition and predation by non-native fishes. In 1981, the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] and the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] began a 10-year Memorandum of Understanding to reintroduce razorback suckers into historic habitats in the Gila Basin. Earlier efforts mostly used small, hatchery-raised fish, which were heavily preyed upon. Later stockings favored larger fish, which are less vulnerable to predators. This study was designed to test whether released adult-sized fish move, use habitat, and survive in a way that supports long-term recovery.

Fish were implanted with radiotransmitters at the Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center (Dexter, New Mexico) and the Page Springs Hatchery (Arizona). Transmitters were attached by [[Surgical Implantation]] using a ventral-incision method with antibiotic and antiseptic treatment, followed by biweekly healing checks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The study reach of the upper Verde River extended from about river kilometer (RK) 204 to RK 179 (scan partly illegible) and flowed from Perkinsville to its confluence with the Salt River. Base flow is near 21 cubic feet per second and is supplemented by perennial spring inputs, with peaks from winter floods and summer convection storms. Native fishes make up a small proportion of the fish community in the reach. Introduced species, including channel catfish, largemouth bass, and green sunfish, dominate abundance.

**Water quality.** Monthly 24-hour measurements were taken with a Hydrolab datasonde. Parameters included temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity, and alkalinity. Dissolved oxygen varied most from September through the high-temperature season. The widest daily pH range occurred in June, associated with stagnant summer flows. Daily temperature ranges were largest in late spring and early summer.

**Parasitism.** Heavy infestation by [[Lernaea Cyprinacea]] (anchor worm) was observed on reintroduced fish during late summer and early autumn. This parasite is considered rare in the upper basin but was reported in other Arizona waters.

## Key Findings

### Study Design and Sample
- **Fish tracked:** 15 sub-adult Colorado squawfish (three groups of five, plus additional releases) and 24 adult or sub-adult razorback suckers.
- **Methods:** Weekly aerial telemetry flights; ground telemetry for 24 or 48 consecutive hours per fish each week.
- **Habitat measurements:** Current velocity, depth, and dominant substrate were measured in 5 m long cells across the study reach. Habitat availability was assessed at both reach-wide and within-habitat-type scales.

### Movement
- **Aerial telemetry, squawfish:** Mean absolute movement was 166 m/day (n = 169), with a net upstream movement of 20 m/day. Twenty-eight percent of observations showed no movement. Most fish stayed within 5 km of release, but two moved more than 30 km downstream and one moved more than 45 km upstream, both associated with flooding.
- **Aerial telemetry, razorbacks:** Mean absolute movement was reported as a garbled value (scan illegible; 277 aerial observations), with a net downstream movement of 60 m/day. Forty-six percent of observations showed zero movement. Two razorbacks moved large downstream distances during February 1992 flooding, and two traveled more than 60 km downstream.
- **Ground telemetry, squawfish:** Mean absolute longitudinal movement was 17.1 m/hr (SD = 33.45, n = 713). Thirty-four percent of locations showed no movement. There was no significant difference between upstream and downstream hourly movement (Kruskal-Wallis, P > 0.05).
- **Ground telemetry, razorbacks:** Mean absolute longitudinal movement was 14.5 m/hr (SD = 25.34, n = 1,191). Twenty-two percent of locations showed no movement.
- **Diel pattern:** Both species moved more at night. Squawfish moved 19.5 m/hr at night versus 14.6 m/hr by day (P < 0.05). Razorbacks moved 18.2 m/hr at night versus 10.5 m/hr by day (P < 0.001).

### Habitat Availability
- Reach-wide habitat was 59% run, 32% pool, and 9% riffle.
- Current velocities below 0.1 m/s were the most common velocity class. Velocities above 1.0 m/s made up less than 2% of available habitat.
- The modal depth class was 20–39 cm (43%), and 81% of available depths were less than 60 cm.
- Sand was the most common substrate (32%), followed by cobble (23%) and boulder (16%). Bedrock, clay, and detritus were rare.

### Habitat Selection (Reach-wide)
- **Velocity:** Both species used currents below 0.1 m/s more than their availability. They used 0.20–0.29 m/s currents less than available. Razorbacks also under-used 0.30–0.39 m/s currents.
- **Depth:** Both species avoided depths below 60 cm and selected depths of 60–119 cm.
- **Substrate:** Both species selected sand. Both avoided boulder and cobble. Razorbacks also selected detritus and gravel.
- **Riffles:** Neither species was found in riffles.
- **Pool use:** 71% of squawfish and 61% of razorback locations were in pools.

### Habitat Selection (Within Habitat Types)
- **Pools, squawfish:** Used velocities averaged lower (mean 0.05 m/s) than available (0.06 m/s), and used depths averaged deeper (mean 71 cm versus 58 cm available). Sand (54%) was the most used substrate.
- **Runs, squawfish:** Used faster flows (mean 0.31 m/s versus 0.22 m/s available), deeper water (92 cm versus 60 cm), and sand (56%).
- **Pools, razorbacks:** Used slower velocities (mean 0.04 m/s), deeper water (mean 77 cm versus 58 cm), and silt (65%).
- **Runs, razorbacks:** Used deeper water (76 cm) and silt (57%) more than available.

### Telemetry Accuracy
- In 24 single-blind trials, the 95% confidence interval for location error was 2.73–4.27 m longitudinally and 2.69–4.17 m laterally.
- No significant difference was found among three observers (ANOVA, F = 0.0720, df = 2,21, P > 0.05).

### Survival and Tag Retention
- Contact with tagged fish lasted from 6 to 266 days.
- For squawfish, the final status of 10 of 15 fish was unknown. Fish stocked October to January were tracked longer (mean 153 days) than those stocked in other months (mean 14 days).
- For razorbacks, the final status of 18 of 24 fish was unknown, mostly from tag expulsion or death. Tagged fish expired after about 266 days in the best cases.
- Not one implanted fish of either species was confirmed to survive a complete summer.

### Conclusions and Recommendations
- The authors consider the success of recent adult-sized reintroductions in the Verde River "equivocal." Larger fish may reduce predation losses and extend post-stocking survival compared with small-fish releases.
- Lernaea infestation may be an additional obstacle to reestablishing populations. The authors recommend a dedicated study of its incidence and effects on native fishes in the Verde River and across the Gila Basin.
- Continued stocking of adult-sized fish should proceed as part of the recovery strategy, paired with a well-funded monitoring program.
- Future stocking should use grow-out environments that more closely resemble natural conditions. Hatchery rearing may have shaped the higher use of slow currents seen in the Verde compared with upper-basin populations.
- Resurveying adult fish in the Gila Basin and the Verde River is recommended to evaluate reintroduction outcomes.
