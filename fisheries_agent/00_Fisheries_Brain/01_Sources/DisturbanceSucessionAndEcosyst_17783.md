# Disturbance, Succession, and Ecosystem Processes in Streams: A Case Study from the Desert

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #periphyton

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[Nancy B Grimm]] (Arizona State University) uses a long-term case study of [[Sycamore Creek]] in the northern [[Sonoran Desert]] to examine how flood and drying disturbances shape stream succession and ecosystem processes. The chapter argues that [[Nitrogen Limitation]] is a central constraint on successional pattern and rate, that this constraint operates at three spatial scales (particle, subreach, reach) and three temporal scales (interflood, annual, decadal), and that the nature of the controlling factor changes with scale. The document addresses algae, macroinvertebrates, and hydrology; it does not discuss fish.

## Background & Description

The chapter defines a **disturbance** as a discrete event that causes significant mortality and changes organism-to-resource ratios. **Stability** comprises resistance (capacity to avoid change) and resilience (ability to recover rapidly). **Succession** is defined simply as the sequence of changes at a site after disturbance, which does not require a change in community structure.

Disturbances are classed as:
- **Autogenic** (generated within the system, often biotic, smaller in scale), such as invertebrate grazing and sloughing of algal mats.
- **Allogenic** (imposed from outside, usually abiotic, larger in scale), such as drying, [[Spates]], debris torrents, fire ash inputs, and catchment logging.

Hydrologic extremes dominate desert lotic systems. Two are contrasted:
- **[[Flash Floods]]** arise because intense, brief, localized rainfall runs off rather than infiltrating soils, producing flashy hydrographs in ephemeral channels. Peak flows may be four to five orders of magnitude above base flows.
- **[[Drying]]** is a protracted disturbance that begins with boundary shrinkage, proceeds to loss of surface water, and ends with rewetting, which initiates post-drying successional sequences. Persistent [[Hyporheic Zone]] flow can continue during drying, but surface-confined organisms are killed.

Sycamore Creek (catchment area 500 km²) is a typical intermittent southwestern desert stream. Modal discharge is 0.01-0.05 m/s in summer and 0.1-0.5 m/s in winter. Substrata range from coarse sand to boulders, arranged in [[Riffles]] (high-gradient cobble-boulder reaches) and [[Runs]] (low-gradient sand-gravel reaches). Near the Verde River confluence, the channel is dominated by runs, and surface flow occurs only above 10 m³/s. The study has been conducted since 1978.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Primary producers** are patchy assemblages dominated by more than 100 diatom species and/or epilithic filamentous [[Cyanobacteria]] (Calothrix sp.), with filamentous green algae (chiefly Cladophora glomerata plus Zygnemataceae), cyanobacterial mats (mainly Anabaena sp.), and macrophyte beds (Potamogeton sp., Rorippa nasturtium-aquaticum, Zannichelia palustris, Chara sp.). Late-succession algal standing crops are high and productivity is substantial.

**Macroinvertebrates** are dominated by collector-gatherers (especially Fallceon quilleri and several [[Chironomidae]] species). Collector-filterers (Hydropsyche sp.) and scrapers (Helicopsyche mexicana, Physella virgata) are also common.

**Nitrogen sources** are limited between floods. Catchment inputs are minimal during interflood periods, so nitrogen comes from in-system regeneration, release of stored nitrogen (mainly from the hyporheic zone), or [[Nitrogen Fixation]]. Storm runoff delivers inorganic nitrogen from desert soils, and precipitation and flood waters are both nitrogen-rich.

**Hyporheic exchange** links surface and subsurface subsystems. Upwelling zones (positive vertical hydraulic gradient, VHG) bring nutrient-rich interstitial water to the surface, while downwelling zones (negative VHG) are where surface water infiltrates. Interstitial flow can reach 0.15 cm/s, which keeps oxygen rarely depleted and favours nitrification. Streamwater nitrate is positively correlated with VHG.

## Key Findings

**Scale framework and definitions**
- Succession is treated as a component of resilience. Succession can increase biomass or productivity without any change in community structure.
- Spatial scales of disturbance are positively correlated with temporal scales or recurrence intervals.

**Particle scale (periphyton on artificial substrata, summer 1989, 3-month experiment)**
- Nitrate addition produced a pronounced enrichment effect after 12 days, with no grazer effect.
- Enrichment differences disappeared after 3-4 weeks. By day 40, little difference remained among treatments, except that chlorophyll a stayed significantly higher in enriched treatments.
- Unenriched substrata were dominated by N-fixers. Calothrix reached 95% of biovolume by day 90. On enriched substrata, N-fixers reached 65% of biovolume by day 90.
- The grazer Petrophila jaliscalis was the only taxon excluded by malathion. Its density peaked on day 55, the only date with a significant biomass increase in grazer-exclusion treatments.
- Flash floods act as resetting disturbances. Late succession is dominated by autogenic constraints such as blocked nutrient diffusion through thick mats.

**Subreach scale (upwelling and downwelling zones)**
- Upwelling zones have high late-succession algal standing crops and rapid post-flood biomass accrual. Downwelling zones are N-depleted and often dominated by Cyanobacteria.
- Thick algal crusts can block infiltration, producing perched surface water over a dry hyporheic zone at a stream sink. Such clogging is judged unlikely to operate over large areas because of patchy distributions.
- Drying reduces or reverses positive VHG at upwelling zones, cutting off nitrogen supply until flow resumes.

**Reach scale (runs and riffles)**
- Fisher et al. (1982) described a 63-day successional sequence for a single run. Lateral export of stranded assemblages accounted for 6% of organic matter export during that interflood period.
- Across 18 successional sequences (1984-1987), five sequence types (A-E) were identified. In types D and E, nitrogen flux was low and cyanobacterial coverage increased late in succession. Precipitous declines in macroinvertebrate density occurred in the same sequences.
- A preliminary calculation for one sequence in which cyanobacterial cover reached 60% suggested nitrogen fixation rates higher than those reported for other streams, on the same order as whole-system daily nitrogen retention.
- In types D and E, chlorophyll a standing crop increased linearly throughout sequences, illustrating that algal biomass can keep rising under nitrogen limitation when autogenic fixation adds nitrogen.

**Temporal scales**
- **Interflood (under about 3-4 months):** Nitrogen retention in biomass may be offset by nitrogen fixation.
- **Annual:** Peak flood flows dominate. Time since flood, discharge, and flood magnitude together predicted algal biomass (multiple r² = 0.68, Fisher and Grimm 1988), outweighing nitrogen availability. Biggs (1988) found discharge and time since flooding significant across 66 New Zealand rivers.
- **Decadal:** Floodwater inorganic nitrogen reached up to 3-5 mg/L in events following about 3 years of dryness (1978 and late 1980s). Floodwater N was low in the mid-1980s after the wet 1978-1980 years. Over the decade, floodwater N concentration was inversely correlated with the number of floods in the prior 3 years. The chapter suggests that climatic variability at the decadal scale can link terrestrial inputs to aquatic productivity.

**Conclusions**
- Nitrogen availability controls rate and pattern of succession, productivity, autotroph and heterotroph biomass, and assemblage structure.
- Flash floods drive succession at annual scales and are also a major nitrogen source. The magnitude of that input varies with decadal climate.
- The author argues the mechanisms are not unique to arid streams. Because desert sunlit channels have high algal biomass, processes may occur quickly, compressing the time scales needed for study.
