# Fishes of Leslie Creek, Cochise County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department job progress report, prepared under the Federal Aid in Fish Restoration Act (Federal Aid Project F-7-R-17, Job No. VI, Segment 2), documents field surveys of the fishes of [[Leslie Creek]] in summer 1974 and 1975. Leslie Creek is one of the few U.S. aquatic systems tributary to the [[Rio Yaqui Drainage]] of Mexico. Earlier literature gave conflicting accounts of its native fish fauna, and the survey set out to resolve them. Three species were confirmed: [[Yaqui Chub]], [[Longfin Dace]] and [[Yaqui Topminnow]]. A fourth, the [[Mexican Stoneroller]], was not collected, and its previously known pool was relocated for a future survey. The report also recommends caution about the proposed [[Leslie Canyon Lake]] impoundment, which would inundate most of the permanent aquatic habitat.

## Background & Description

Leslie Creek rises from a spring near the entrance of Leslie Canyon and flows about 16 miles southwest to [[Whitewater Draw]]. It crosses the [[Sulfur Springs Valley]] west of the [[Swisshelm Mountains]]. The permanent flowing regime is limited to the area just below the spring source, and the system undergoes extreme annual fluctuation. Only during unusual high flows does water reach Whitewater Draw.

The report treats the system as the only U.S. site with an aquatic fauna related to northwestern Mexico, alongside [[Astin Spring]] and [[Rucker Canyon]]. Formerly, the Yaqui drainage in Arizona supported six native fish species. Earlier authors disagreed about the native status of several fishes, partly because of introductions. Reported introductions include the Yaqui chub (1969) and the Yaqui topminnow (reportedly 1969).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Two 250-yard stations were sampled in the flowing headwater, covering about two-thirds of the permanent aquatic regime at the time.
- Station 1 ran from an existing rock dam downstream.
- Station 2 ran from the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] weir to the stream terminus.
- Collections were made on August 22, 1974, and June 18–19, 1975. Supplemental observations were made May 3–4, 1972.
- The 1974 survey used a 12-volt, 200-watt battery-powered [[Electrofishing]] unit, plus a seine. Seining was limited by debris, so the 1975 survey used a 115-volt, 350-watt AC gasoline shocker to avoid [[Seining]] selectivity.
- In 1975, Station 1 was limited to pools because its riffles were completely overgrown.

**Hydrology.** Stream gradient in the permanent headwater averages 48 feet per mile. Measured stream lengths of permanent flow declined across survey years: 3,400 ft (1972), 2,700 ft (1974) and 2,200 ft (1975). U.S. Geological Survey records for Station 09537200 show the same reduction in flow volume. Peak flows occur in July–August and October–November, and low flows in April–June. The authors attribute the permanent flow to the impervious volcanic strata capping the Swisshelm Mountains, with water dissipating into fractured limestone below. Over 60 years of observations indicate the system supports only finite population levels.

**Habitat.** The water is clear, with turbidity only near the lower terminus, which is affected by livestock grazing. Organic debris and litter make up much of the sediment. The overstory is dominated by *Fraxinus*, *Salix*, *Populus* and *Baccharis*. Station 1 riffles are covered by watercress (*Rorippa nasturtium-aquaticum*), and its pools have dense emergent borders. Station 2 has less understory and exposed pool and riffle habitat.

## Key Findings

**Species status and identification**
- Previous authors listed five fish species for Leslie Creek. This survey verified three: Yaqui chub, longfin dace and Yaqui topminnow.
- The 1972 report of [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish) is attributed to misidentification of the Yaqui topminnow, which is very similar in morphology and habits.
- The Mexican stoneroller was not collected. Earlier records came from a single deep pool, and that pool was located in the midsection of the headwater between survey stations. A future survey will check for the species.

**Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*)**
- Status classifications vary: "Undetermined" (1966, 1973) and "Extinct Peripheral" (Minckley 1973b).
- The study confirmed a substantial, well-distributed population in the headwater, in both stations.
- Specimens 80 mm standard length or larger were rare (3 specimens) and secretive. Smaller fish (20–60 mm SL) were numerous and found in shallow pools and intermediate riffles as well as deep pools.
- Length-frequency data show four age classes in 1974 and three in 1975, consistent with a population present since about 1970.
- Only two U.S. populations are known (Leslie Creek and Astin Spring). The Leslie population is the most prolific, but limited habitat likely keeps it at or near maximum density for Arizona.

**Longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*)**
- Status: localized large populations (Minckley 1973b).
- The species is widespread in Arizona drainages below the Mogollon Rim, and it tolerates exotic species better than most native fishes.
- It is the dominant species in the Leslie Creek fauna and prefers deep pools.
- Four distinct age classes were found, with sizes from 25 to 63 mm SL.
- Yaqui chub and longfin dace coexist. Dietary studies suggest niche separation: longfin dace feed heavily on detritus, while Yaqui chub prefer algae. Both are opportunistic on invertebrates.
- A distinct "Yaqui form" of longfin dace was proposed by McNatt (1974), but no definitive taxonomic work has confirmed it as a separate entity.

**Yaqui topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*)**
- Status: "Endangered, possibly extinct, peripheral" (Minckley 1973b), and "Endangered" (1974 federal list).
- No reference specimens were collected, due to possible restrictions under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973.
- Observed sizes ranged from about 20 to 50 mm total length, suggesting substantial reproduction and a viable population.
- Occurrence was confined to the lower sector, with none recorded at Station 1. It was most common in pools, though it was also found in intermediate riffles.
- The species is known only from the Astin Spring complex and Leslie Creek in Arizona. Its population density is probably at its maximum within the United States.

**Conservation and management**
- The authors conclude that the origin of the Yaqui chub and Yaqui topminnow populations, and whether they predate the 1969 introductions, cannot be fully resolved.
- The proposed Leslie Canyon Lake (about 250 surface acres) would overlie and inundate most of the permanent aquatic regime, and the report frames the controversy around it.
- Leslie Creek supports all remaining native species of the Arizona Yaqui drainage, which has declined from six native species to four. Astin Spring and Rucker Canyon each support two.
