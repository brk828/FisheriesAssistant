# Limnological Features of Weber Lake, Cheboygan County, Michigan

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #plankton

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Frank F. Hooper]] (Institute for Fisheries Research, Michigan Department of Conservation) reports the physical, chemical and biological features of [[Weber Lake]], a small, 31-acre seepage lake in southwestern [[Cheboygan County]], Michigan. Surveys in 1950, 1951 and 1952 found two unusual features. First, a dichotomous (double-minimum) stratification in which pH, alkalinity, conductivity and most major electrolytes are lowest in the thermocline. Second, very low alkalinity compared with the surrounding ground water and with other lakes of lower Michigan. The author attributes the chemistry to a basin sealed from regional ground water, with inputs from precipitation, runoff and seepage from an adjacent [[Sphagnum Bog]]. The lake also has sparse rooted vegetation and a poor plankton flora, and its bottom fauna is dominated by midge larvae and fingernail clams.

## Background & Description

The study was part of [[Michigan Lake Surveys]] run by the [[Institute for Fisheries Research]], with sponsorship from the [[University of Michigan Biological Station]]. Fieldwork took place in August 1950, July 1951 and August 1952, with a more intensive physical, chemical and quantitative biological inventory in August 1952.

Weber Lake lies in a belt of high moraine. The surrounding land rises 20 to 30 feet above the water, and the land is entirely state-owned. The lake has a surface area of 31 acres, a mean depth of 20 feet and a maximum depth of 41 feet. Its basin drops steeply between the surface and the 15-foot contour, so the shoal area is small. Shore sediments are fine sand, sometimes less than an inch thick over soft ooze, and deep-water sediments are soft reddish-brown ooze.

Stocking history is relevant to the fishery. Trout were planted after a 1943 biological survey, but growth was poor where stunted [[Bluegill]] and [[Yellow Perch]] populations were present. The lake was treated with [[Rotenone]] and restocked with trout in 1948.

Methods included an electrical-resistance thermometer, an underwater photometer (Greenbank 1945), a portable conductivity bridge, a Beckman pH meter, a Beckman flame photometer for Na, K, Ca and Mg, a Klett-Summerson colorimeter, a [[Foerst Plankton Centrifuge]], a [[Juday Plankton Trap]] and a 6 × 6 inch [[Ekman Dredge]]. Chemical methods followed [[Standard Methods for the Examination of Water and Sewage]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal and oxygen conditions.** Epilimnion temperatures never exceeded 72 °F. The upper thermocline boundary lay at 14 to 20 feet and the lower boundary at 26 to 32 feet. Each summer contained a stratum suitable for trout (below 70 °F with more than 4 ppm dissolved oxygen), at 13 to 22 feet in 1950, 16 to 25 feet in 1951, and 20 to 29 feet in 1952. An anaerobic zone lay below the thermocline.

**Light and color.** The Secchi disk read 19 to 21 feet in August 1950 and 19 feet 8 inches on August 18, 1952. Ten per cent of surface light reached 21 feet, a high-medium transparency by the [[Birge-Juday Transparency Classification]]. Water colour rose from 12 at the surface to 81 at the lower thermocline and 93 in the bottom water (platinum-cobalt scale), because the hypolimnion carried organic colouring matter.

**Alkalinity and conductivity.** Epilimnion methyl orange alkalinity was 3 to 4 ppm, and bottom-water alkalinity was 10 to 15 ppm. Specific conductance was 19 to 21 mho in the epilimnion and 24 to 32 mho in the bottom water. Alkalinity and conductivity were both at their lowest in the middle thermocline.

**Ground-water isolation.** Lake water is notably soft, and local farmers use it for domestic purposes. Wells within 150 feet of the lake had alkalinities of 150 ppm (Conservation Department cabin) and 180 ppm (camp ground). The author argues that the basin is sealed by clays and organic deposits, following [[Broughton (1941)]]. If so, the lake's water comes mainly from precipitation, surface runoff and seepage.

**Bog influence.** The northwest end of the lake connects to a [[Sphagnum Bog]] through a road-crossed strip of peat, which was once a lake arm. Seepage was seen in June 1950 and had stopped by August. Sphagnum peat releases acids and removes bases (Ruttner 1953), which the author links to the thermocline electrolyte minimum.

**Biota.** Rooted vegetation is sparse. Eighteen aquatic plant species were recorded, and most grew along the shore in water less than 6 inches deep. Only [[Nuphar Variegatum]], [[Vallisneria Americana]] and a Sagittaria species grew in deeper water, mainly in a small bay at the southeast corner. Phytoplankton was dominated by dinoflagellates and chrysophytes, and diatoms were rare or absent.

## Key Findings

1. **Dichotomous pH stratification.** The epilimnion pH ranged from 6.1 to 6.7. The pH minimum, 4.9 to 5.5, occurred at 22 to 26 feet in the thermocline, and bottom-water pH was 5.7 to 5.9. The minimum was at roughly the same depth each year, but its position relative to the anaerobic zone varied.

2. **Electrolyte minimum in the thermocline.** On August 18, 1952, minimum concentrations of sulphate, potassium, sodium, calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate (all except iron) were found at 26 feet. Sulphate fell from 6.0 ppm at the surface to 2.0 ppm at 26 feet, then rose to 5.0 ppm at 32 feet. Magnesium, iron and bicarbonate increased steadily below the thermocline, so the hypolimnion alkalinity increase appears to come mainly from magnesium and iron bicarbonates rather than calcium.

3. **Mechanism of the pH increase.** The hypolimnion pH increase was largest in 1950, when the anaerobic zone was thickest and hypolimnion alkalinity was highest. This supports [[Yoshimura (1932)]], who proposed that free carbon dioxide dissolves bottom-deposit carbonates and releases bicarbonate.

4. **Cause of the electrolyte deficiency.** Plankton and seston were low at 26 feet, so biological uptake is ruled out as the main cause. The author suggests either chemical removal at the mud surface or subsurface inflow of electrolyte-poor water. Hydrogen sulphide was absent at 26 feet but present at 36 and 39 feet, so sulphate-reducing bacteria are a less likely cause.

5. **Basin isolation.** The contrast with ground-water alkalinity (150 to 180 ppm) indicates that the lake is effectively sealed from regional ground water, apart from bog seepage.

6. **Poor plant and plankton communities.** Eighteen aquatic plant species were recorded, mostly along the shore. Average epilimnion phytoplankton volume on August 25, 1952 was 923,000 standard cubic units per litre. Seston ash-free dry weight averaged 1.31 mg per litre across surface, 10 ft and 20 ft samples.

7. **Zooplankton.** Rotifers dominated, with 12 genera recorded. Six Entomostraca species appeared in June 1950, mainly [[Polyphemus Pediculus]] and [[Holopedium Gibberum]]. By August 25, 1950 only three species were recorded, averaging fewer than 10 individuals per litre. A single *Holopedium* specimen turned up in 80 litres of water sampled on August 14, 1952.

8. **Bottom fauna.** Volume ranged from 0.06 ml per square foot (39 ft) to 1.48 ml per square foot (3 to 6 ft). Gomphine dragonfly nymphs made up 85 per cent of the volume at 3 to 6 ft. The sublittoral was dominated by [[Tendipes Plumosus]] and *Pisidium* sp., and below 26 ft the important taxa were *T. plumosus* and [[Chaoborus Punctipennis]].

9. **Fishery implications.** Thermal and oxygen habitat for trout existed in every summer studied, but the paper does not report fish population data. The earlier poor trout growth was attributed to stunted bluegill and perch populations.
