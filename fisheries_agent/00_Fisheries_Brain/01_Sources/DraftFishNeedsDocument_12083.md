# Artificial Propagation Plan: Fish Needs 1993-1998 for Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This October 1992 draft from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service), prepared by Propagation Coordinator J. Holt Williamson, is a skeletal operational artificial propagation plan for four endangered big-river fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin: [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. It stems from a [[Fish Needs Work Group]] meeting held September 1, 1992, in Grand Junction, Colorado. The plan defines five-year fish needs (1993-1998) for artificial genetic refuges (AGRs), back-up refuges, broodstocks, research/development, and stocking, and it flags facilities capacity as a separate, later phase. The document is explicitly preliminary: many numbers are minimum estimates, population status and priorities are not yet formally established, and attrition assumptions remain disputed.

## Background & Description

The Recovery Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin calls for captive breeding of four target species. The plan's core framework is a [[Di-allele Cross]] breeding design using a 5 x 5 parental matrix, which yields 25 unique [[Family Lot]]s per population. Each wild donor adult is [[PIT Tagging|PIT-tagged]], sampled nonlethally for genetic analysis, and acclimated for about a year before spawning. Wild fish are returned to the wild after contributing progeny and replaced with new wild fish, a cycle described as capture, spawn, release, replace.

Key operational terms used in the document:
- **AGR / Refuge**: captive population founded to avoid immediate extinction and preserve unique genetic material.
- **Back-up refuge**: secondary captive population held at a separate site.
- **Broodstock**: captive population maintained to produce fish for stocking and research.
- **Surplus fish**: hatchery fish exceeding explicitly defined program needs; see [[Surplus Fish Disposition]].

The companion documents are the 14 May 1992 "Refuge and Broodstock Development" matrix (Appendix One), the 20 July 1992 "Propagation Roadmap" memo (Appendix Two), the [[Priority Ranking of Populations]] draft from the Service's Endangered Species and Environmental Contaminants office, and a [[Genetic Risk Assessment]] discussion of hatchery-type development and selection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses hatchery and genetics management rather than wild ecosystem dynamics, but it identifies several environmental and biological pressures:

- **Status and extirpation**: The Service memo notes that many populations have become extirpated since the turn of the century. Within the last 10 years, the Gunnison River squawfish and several razorback sucker populations were lost, and the remaining riverine bonytail was noted as at risk. Populations named as at risk within the next few years include the Yampa razorbacks, San Juan razorbacks, Lake Powell razorbacks, Cataract humpbacks, and San Juan squawfish.
- **Habitat and interactions**: All populations were considered affected, in varying degrees, by habitat change and interactions with introduced taxa.
- **Genetic degradation**: Risks named include drift, selection, inbreeding, and hybridization. Hatchery selection arises mainly from variation in fish numbers among family lots. Divergence between captive and wild stocks over time is termed "hatchery type."
- **Disease and contamination**: Surplus fish are described as a potential source of disease and genetic contamination to wild and captive stocks.
- **Facilities context**: Current capacity is 13 ponds totaling 5.5 acres, 75 assorted tanks, and 27 rearing cages across four facilities (Ouray EFCES, Horsethief Ponds, Bellvue, Wray). Facilities at Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center (NFH&TC) supply most research fish.

## Key Findings

**Scope and status**
- The plan is a draft "skeleton" to be reviewed annually; it is not yet approved.
- No formal population status assessment and priority document existed at the time; the Service identified this as the main missing link in the recovery process.
- Fish needs for stocking and research beyond 1993-1994 are poorly defined.

**Attrition and survival assumptions (main unresolved dispute)**
- The plan assumes a 20% annual attrition rate after the first year. Utah representatives felt 5% was more realistic. The Service recommended 20% as a conservative value pending experience.
- The first-year attrition assumption is 50% from egg to Age I in some model estimates; this is flagged as very uncertain.
- Mean annual attrition for mature hatchery fish was given as 10-20% (Table 7 of the document).

**Founder and family-lot design**
- Donor population: 5 male and 5 female wild adults per high priority population (10 adults total), a preliminary number chosen for practicality. The literature cited suggests 20 to 4000 individuals for a broodstock.
- Family lots: 5 x 5 matrix produces 25 family lots per population.
- Production goal per family lot: 200 Age V fish (100 males, 100 females); 500 Age I fish (markable, about 100 mm TL); 500 to 1,000 YOY fish. The work group recommended two refuges per population, with 250 Age I fish per family lot in each.
- Under a 20% annual attrition model, about two fish from each family lot of 200 would remain in the twentieth year after maturation, or six after 15 years.

**Population numbers**
- Razorback sucker: all five populations designated highest priority for AGRs. Model total of 125 family lots x 400 Age I fish = 62,500 Age I fish.
- Humpback chub: highest priority at Cataract, Yampa, and Black Rocks canyons.
- Colorado squawfish: highest priority is the Upper Mainstem Colorado River population (RM 110-185).
- Bonytail chub: Lake Mohave population received relatively high priority.
- Upper cap suggested: 12,500 F1 fish per population in the captive program, with about 50 fish left after 20 years.

**Five-year production totals (Table 5, not additive where noted)**
- Razorback sucker: 47,200 YOY (1994 refugia), plus research and augmentation/restoration fish.
- Humpback chub: 30,000 YOY (1994 refugia).
- Bonytail chub: 53,200 YOY (1994 refugia, shown in the source with uncertainty marks).
- Colorado squawfish: 53,200 YOY (1994 refugia).
- Total fish needs listed in Table 5: 196,900 Young-of-Year, 65,800 age 1+, and 16,500 adults (with some figures marked "?").
- Table 2 totals for refugia production: 130,400 YOY, 65,200 age 1+, and 16,400 adults.

**Broodstock needs (Table 1)**
- Spawning lots and adult brood needs are listed per population for 1993 and 1994. For example, 50 males and 50 females of bonytail chub were listed for 1994 (values as given in the source table).

**Research/development**
- Table 3 lists research fish needs for 1993-1994, with approximate totals of about 600 plus age 1+ fish and about 3,000 YOY. Major studies include chemoreception work on razorback sucker at Ouray, nursery habitat and contaminant studies, Colorado squawfish morphology studies at LFL, and a bonytail study at USU.
- Long-term research needs could not be identified.

**Stocking and augmentation (Table 4)**
- Proposed razorback sucker stocking in the Upper Colorado River (250-300 mm fish, 100 fish in 1994, with a proposed Gunnison-area plan tied to chemoreception work).
- Colorado squawfish: 45,000 Y-O-Y at the Gunnison River (1994).
- Bonytail: 3,000 YOY in 1995, 3,000 in 1996, and 1,700 age 1+ in 1997 at the Green River.
- Proposed totals: about 52,700 plus fish and about 100 additional fish.
- Stocking categories defined: experimental, supplemental, augmentation, and restoration. The terminology debate was not resolved.

**Facilities**
- Horsethief Ponds (six earthen ponds, four 0.75 ac and two 0.25 ac) are committed to Colorado populations of the four species.
- Ouray EFCES: 12 lined 0.2 ac ponds being built by the Bureau of Reclamation (three by fall 1992, the rest in spring); the schematic notes about 12-14 additional ponds in 1993.
- Bellvue and Wray are interim sites; Wray holds 3 wild adult razorback suckers in an AGR.
- A Colorado Water Conservation Board hatchery feasibility study is pending; a new facility was estimated to be about five years away.
- The Ouray fish inventory (10 August 1992) recorded about 5,497 estimated razorback sucker fry (about 5,264 in the San Juan arm plus 233 in the Colorado River arm), and 446 actual Colorado squawfish fry (counts confirmed).

**Surplus fish policy**
- Surplus fish will be euthanized with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222) unless claimed by a permitted requester who pays all costs.
- Surplus fish will not be released into the wild.
- Surplus endangered fish become the property and responsibility of the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

**Recommendations and next steps**
- The Biology Committee should review the plan, resolve the attrition dispute, and form a facilities-capacity phase with the Propagation Subcommittee.
- Ranges of production may be preferable to exact fish numbers, as the program is not yet advanced enough for exact targets.
- The work group requested formal ad hoc status, and the Propagation Coordinator was asked to write a planning and guidance document covering genetic risks and methods.
- The numbers should be treated as minimum estimates and scrutinized, as they rest on untested assumptions.
