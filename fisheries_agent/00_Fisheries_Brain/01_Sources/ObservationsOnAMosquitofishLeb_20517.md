# Guppy (Lebistes reticulatus) as a Larvivore for Culex pipiens fatigans Control in Polluted Bangkok Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This WHO/EBL report (WHO/Vector Control/99.64, authors Manabu Sasa, Takeshi Kurihara and Ophas Dhamvanij, with co-authors listed as an affiliation in the source) examines how the [[Guppy]] (*Lebistes reticulatus*), a Caribbean poeciliid, became established in sewage-fed pools in [[Bangkok]] and whether it can suppress [[Culex pipiens fatigans]], a major vector of [[Bancroftian Filariasis]]. Guppies reached high densities in polluted pools, and mosquito larvae were absent where adult densities exceeded about 10 per square metre. Laboratory tests showed high larval consumption and that certain organophosphorus insecticides, notably [[Sumithion]], combine larvicidal action with low fish toxicity. The authors conclude that guppies are promising but insufficient alone, and that integrated control with environmental sanitation is needed. The work is explicitly preliminary.

## Background & Description

*Lebistes reticulatus* (the guppy, or "Barbados millions") is a viviparous member of the [[Poeciliidae]] family, widely kept in aquaria and used in genetic and physiological research. It is known for eating mosquito larvae, which has led to its introduction across warmer regions.

- **Introduction to Bangkok:** The first wild colony was discovered at Wat Tevaraj, Sao Sao quarter, in January 1961, in a shaded ground pool about 70 × 50 m with a maximum depth of about 30 cm, fed by rainwater and domestic sewage. The route of introduction is unknown; the authors suggest the pet trade, possibly via Hong Kong. Local people call it "pla nog-yung" (peacock fish), "pla hang nog-yung" (peacock tail fish) and "pla kin yung" (mosquito-eating fish).
- **Spread:** Municipal mosquito control teams transplanted fish to more than 50 areas across the city over about three and a half years. Populations typically needed two to three months after release to reach densities that eliminated mosquito larvae.
- **Identification:** Professor S. M. Rose (Tulane University) noted that Bangkok specimens were smaller and less showy than American strains, but showed no differences in non-selected traits such as mouth, gonopodium and fin characters.
- **Study context:** The senior author was a WHO consultant at the Faculty of Tropical Medicine, University of Medical Sciences, Bangkok, with support from a Public Health Service Research Grant and the Colombo Plan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat requirements.** Guppies rarely thrived in large open ditches covered with water plants or in rice paddies, probably due to food scarcity and predatory fish. Optimal habitats were shallow, polluted ground pools beneath or between houses in low-income, low-lying quarters lacking adequate sewage disposal. Many of these pools remain filled through the dry season (December–April), while some dry out.

**Mosquito context.** Sewage pools under houses supported large larval counts of *C. p. fatigans*. One example (Huay Kwang Quarter, 15 July) recorded averages of 295.2 larvae, 125.4 pupae and 2.2 egg rafts per five net dips (24 cm diameter).

**Population density.** Density depended on environmental conditions and time since release. Methods included visual counts (low densities, under 10 per m²), bamboo frames floated on the surface, and fish-net sampling (24 cm diameter) for high densities. Maximum populations with balanced age structure were estimated to occur about six months after introduction, though this was not directly confirmed. Notable values:

- Din Dang open ditch (about 15 × 450 m, released 12 months earlier): mean 24.5 fish per net sample, or 542 per m², suggesting roughly 700,000 fish in the ditch. Authors describe this as likely below maximum due to limited food.
- Another ditch under a house: mean 655 per m², mostly immature fish.
- Eight pools in three quarters (five samplings each): mean counts per sample ranged from 5.4 to 47.4, higher where younger age groups dominated (Table 1).

**Age composition.** Newborns measure 4–5 mm. Under favourable conditions (about 28°C), fish reached young adulthood in about six weeks. Males matured at about 15 mm and were identified by colour patches and a modified anal fin (gonopodium). Adult/total ratios varied: E-1 25.2%, E-2 31.8%, D-2 5.4%, A-1 0%, A-2 1.7%; E-3 and D-1 were largely adult.

**Natural enemies.** The main threats in Bangkok were children, ducks and larger fish. Public education reduced losses to the first two. Thai fighting fish and catfish ate guppies in aquaria. A fungal disease of the family Saprolegniaceae caused sluggishness and white fin patches. In one outbreak, 25 of 952 fish died within a week, and the epidemic largely subsided in the affected pool by 30 August, although the source notes that fish in another pool remained sick.

## Key Findings

### Field observations
- In sewage pools with roughly 10 or more adult guppies per m², mosquito larvae were never found. Fish and larvae co-occurred where densities were below one per m².
- Surveys during the rainy season (June–August) classified pools into four types: fish only, fish and larvae, larvae only, and neither.
- At Din Dang, the largest pool (450 × 15 m) had no mosquito larvae. A pool of 200 × 20 m beside an 80-family barrack, with highly polluted water, also had none, attributed to guppies. A third pool (about 90 × 60 m, 50 cm deep) still held many larvae at a guppy density averaging 0.5 per m², but was expected to clear within two to three months.
- Small pools under houses were invaded by fish after floods, and larval presence depended on guppy density.

### Laboratory feeding
- Starved fish were isolated in glass vessels. Females averaged 123.3 third/fourth-instar *C. p. fatigans* larvae consumed per day; males averaged 135.4 per the Results section. The Conclusions give males as "13.4," which conflicts with the Results and appears to be a typographical error.
- Neither sex ate pupae.
- Guppies preferred mosquito larvae at all life stages and also ate egg rafts and dead adult mosquitoes.
- About 150 adult guppies consumed all edible parts of 2 g of human faeces in about five minutes. The authors suggest guppies may also aid natural purification of sewage pools.

### Density experiment
- Cylinders with 10, 33, 100 and 333 fish (plus a fish-free control) were run from 21 July at 27–29°C.
- Three weeks later, survivors were 3, 23, 62 and 11 fish respectively. The control and the lightly stocked (3-fish) cylinder had large numbers of larvae and pupae.
- Egg rafts: 27 in the control and 35 in the most crowded cylinder.
- Conclusion: mosquitoes could not develop where guppies reached high density, though both could coexist at low fish density.

### Chemical susceptibility
- All chlorinated hydrocarbons tested were more toxic to fish than to mosquito larvae.
- Certain organophosphorus compounds were effective larvicides at very low concentrations with little harm to fish.
- [[Sumithion]] killed larvae at 0.006 ppm, and was toxic to fish only above 4 ppm.
- A field test with a 10% emulsion at about 0.1 ppm killed *C. p. fatigans* larvae without harming guppy colonies.
- Detergent (FAB, alkylbenzene sulfonate) killed all fish above 40 ppm, with almost no deaths below 5 ppm. Toilet soap (Swan) showed similar toxicity.

### Cold tolerance
- In the senior author's Tokyo experience, the species was the most cold-susceptible of common tropical pet fish, unable to survive below 13°C.
- At 14°C, the source text reports 45 of 81 fish dead after six hours (87.1%) and none of 54 surviving 48 hours. Table 4 gives 55.5% for six hours; the discrepancy is in the source.
- At 6°C, survival was practically nil beyond one hour.
- In gradual cooling, knock-down reached 9.4% at 18°C, 37.5% after 20 minutes at 15°C, and total immobilization after 30 minutes at 13°C.
- The authors conclude the Bangkok strain is unsuitable for temperate biological control.

### Discussion and conclusions
- Guppies are efficient larvivores in semi-permanent sewage pools, with more than 90% mosquito reduction possible in many parts of Bangkok.
- Limits: guppies rarely colonize swamps, open canals or streams that are important habitats for anophelines and *Mansonia*; they need several months to show effect; and they tolerate only tropical conditions.
- Insecticides that spare fish remain necessary where guppies cannot survive.
- The authors recommend integrated control (biological, chemical and other methods) with environmental sanitation as the ultimate goal, and further research before wider use.

### Addendum
A Japanese strain from wild colonies near Shimoda City, Shizuoka, appears cold-resistant and larger, with more colourful males. Investigations at the University of Tokyo were underway, with potential application in temperate mosquito control.
