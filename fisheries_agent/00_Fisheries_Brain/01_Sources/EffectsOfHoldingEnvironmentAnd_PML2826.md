# Effects of Holding Environment and Exercise Conditioning on Swimming Performance of Southwestern Native Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This experimental study by [[Ward and Hilwig]] (2004) tests whether [[Exercise Conditioning]] in flowing water can restore the swimming performance of endangered fish reared in standing water at hatcheries. Four [[Native Fish of the Colorado River Basin]] were compared: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Spikedace]]. Fish were held in standing water, exercised in flowing water, or wild-caught, and their [[Failure Velocity]] was measured in a [[Swim Tunnel Testing]] apparatus. Exercise increased swimming performance in all four species, and exercised fish performed at levels close to wild fish.

## Background & Description

Endangered fish are commonly propagated in hatcheries and stocked into streams where populations are depleted or extirpated. Hatchery fish are usually grown in ponds or tanks with standing water. The authors argue that this rearing can reduce the stamina needed to hold position in swift currents. Fish raised this way may show [[Downstream Displacement]] after release, are rarely recaptured, and suffer high [[Predation]] mortality. Poor physical condition, together with handling and transport stress, may add to these losses.

The study species are stream-oriented fishes that are typically raised in standing-water facilities. The authors aimed to determine whether swimming performance differed among treatment groups and whether exercise would improve the performance of hatchery fish reared in standing water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods**

- **Swim tunnel:** A centrifugal pump circulated water between two reservoirs through a clear acrylic swim chamber. Pilot trials set start velocities for each species. Two fish were tested at a time, following [[Bulkley et al. 1981]], who found no differences in swimming performance between fish tested singly or in pairs. After a 10-min settling period, water velocity increased by 4 cm/s every 5 min until fish could no longer hold position. Failure was defined as being pinned against the downstream screen for 10 s.
- **Exercise apparatus:** Fish were exercised in a 530-L fiberglass oval raceway at the U.S. Forest Service Laboratory in Flagstaff, Arizona. Water velocity was 10–100 cm/s and temperature was held at 20 ± 2 °C. Fish unaccustomed to flow were impinged on the downstream screen, so a 20 cm river rock was added for 2 d as a refuge.
- **Flannelmouth Sucker:** Tank-reared fish (about 90 mm TL) were split into an exercised group and a standing-water group in a 1,900-L tank. Fatigue velocity was tested after 14 d.
- **Bonytail:** Fish of 115 mm TL came from [[Bubbling Ponds Fish Hatchery]] and originated from [[Wahweep Fish Hatchery]]. Twenty fish were held in standing water for 10 d, and 20 were exercised for 10 d. A third group was tested within 48 h of removal from a 0.16-ha pond.
- **Razorback Sucker:** Pond-reared fish (146 mm mean TL) were tested within 48 h of removal from the pond. The same group was then held 2 months in standing water, retested, exercised for 10 d, and retested. Repeated testing of one group allowed direct measurement of holding and exercise effects.
- **Spikedace:** Wild fish were seined from the upper [[Gila River]]. Captive fish were held in 38-L aquaria for 265 d, and half were exercised for 10 d. A separate wild group was tested within 48 h of capture.
- **Statistics:** The analysis used [[Analysis of Variance]] with a Tukey-Kramer correction. Spikedace were analyzed with [[Analysis of Covariance]] to adjust for length differences. Two-sample t-tests compared exercised and nonexercised fish of the same species.

## Key Findings

**Flannelmouth Sucker.** Exercised fish (66.7 cm/s) had a failure velocity 6.7 cm/s higher than standing-water fish (60 cm/s; P = 0.045). Mean TL did not differ significantly between groups (P = 0.83).

**Bonytail.** Exercised fish (87.4 cm/s) outperformed standing-water fish (74.4 cm/s) by 13 cm/s and fish tested immediately after pond removal (76.0 cm/s) by 11.4 cm/s (P < 0.0001, F = 39.23). Mean TL did not differ significantly among groups (P > 0.10).

**Razorback Sucker.** Exercised fish (71.6 cm/s) performed 18.6 cm/s better than the same fish after 2 months in standing water (53 cm/s) and 5.6 cm/s better than the same fish tested after pond removal (66 cm/s; P < 0.0001, F = 19.07). Mean TL did not differ significantly (P > 0.24).

**Spikedace.** Mean TL differed between wild fish (60.2 mm) and captive groups (p = 0.014). After correction for size, wild spikedace (86.0 cm/s) outperformed unexercised fish (50.6 cm/s) by 35.4 cm/s and exercised fish (84.1 cm/s) by 1.9 cm/s. The difference between wild and exercised fish was not significant (P = 0.15; ANCOVA F = 37.5, P < 0.0001 overall).

**Reported performance gains.** The abstract reports swimming performance increases of 10%, 15%, 26%, and 40% for Flannelmouth Sucker, Bonytail, Razorback Sucker, and Spikedace after exercise in flowing water for as little as 10 d. The velocities given in the Results imply different percentages for some species, so the table values should be checked against the abstract.

**Discussion and conclusions.**

- Fish reared in standing water (ponds or tanks) had significantly reduced swimming performance compared with exercised or wild fish.
- Exercise conditioning raised swimming performance close to wild levels.
- Poor condition of pond-reared fish may contribute to the limited success of [[Repatriation Stocking]] of [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes, with millions of hatchery fish stocked and few recaptured.
- Exercise conditioning is known to improve feeding efficiency, growth, muscle development, recovery from fatigue, and escape response, yet it is rarely used in hatchery programs for nonsalmonid fishes.
- The authors recommend that [[Exercise Conditioning]] before stocking be evaluated as a way to increase survival of repatriated lotic fishes, and that hatchery success be measured by long-term survival and species recovery rather than numbers stocked.

**Internal inconsistencies noted in the source.** The Methods describe holding razorback suckers in a 378-L tank, while the Results cite a 138-L tank. The Table 1 sample sizes are partly garbled in the scan and were not reproduced.
