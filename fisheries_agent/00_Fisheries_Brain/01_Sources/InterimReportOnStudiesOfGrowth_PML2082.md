# Razorback Sucker Larval Growth and Survival Under Experimental Fertilization Regimes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This interim report from the [[Arizona State University]] Department of Zoology, prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], describes preliminary pond experiments on larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*[[Xyrauchen Texanus]]*). The species has been extirpated from much of its range, and the only substantial remaining population is in [[Lake Mohave]]. Otolith analysis showed adults aged 24 to 44 years, suggesting little or no recruitment since the 1950s. The study tested whether fertilization-driven pond productivity affects larval survival and growth, using twelve ponds at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].

Survival did not differ significantly among fertilization treatments. Larval growth, measured as individual weight and total biomass, did differ significantly and increased with fertilization rate. The report also notes a possible confounding effect from the [[Tiger Salamander]] (*[[Ambystoma Tigrinum]]*), which established itself naturally in the ponds.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid fish native to the Colorado River basin. Its wild recruitment problem has made larval biology a management priority. Reproduction has been observed in Lake Mohave, and larvae are produced, yet no individuals larger than 18 mm total length (TL) had been collected from the wild. Researchers have hypothesized that young razorbacks cannot survive, which would explain the absence of recruits. Predation was considered an unlikely full explanation (Langhorst 1985), and the effects of environmental and food factors had not been seriously investigated.

The study addressed three factors important to larval fish survival: predation, environment, and food. Four experiments were planned to examine:

1. Growth and survival in ponds at three fertilization rates.
2. Growth and survival under laboratory conditions at six food levels.
3. The point at which larvae must begin feeding or die.
4. The time required for death by starvation.

This report covers only the pond experiment (Experiment 1). Later reports were to document the other experiments.

**Methods (pond experiment):**
- **Ponds:** Twelve ponds at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], each about 0.1 ha in surface area, were assigned to high, medium, or low fertilization treatments. Assignment followed a stratified block design where possible, though uneven distribution among localities prevented full blocking.
- **Fertilization:**
  - High: 341 kg/ha alfalfa pellets and 57 kg/ha PO4-P
  - Medium: 170 kg/ha alfalfa pellets and 28 kg/ha PO4-P
  - Low: no fertilizer
  - Applications were made at two-week intervals throughout the experiment.
- **Larvae:** Produced by [[Artificial Fertilization]] from [[Lake Mohave]] brood stock. Stocked about two days after hatching on 23 February 1985 at 250,000 individuals/ha.
- **Harvest:** Ponds were drained and harvested 17–19 April 1985. All fish were weighed en masse to estimate total biomass. Subsamples were weighed and counted to derive number per gram. Survival was calculated as the product of total biomass and number per gram, expressed as a percentage of fish stocked.
- **Water temperature:** Mean temperature rose from 12 °C in February to 17 °C in April.
- **Voucher specimens:** Preserved samples are housed in the Arizona State University collection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The main ecological context concerns predation, competition, and food supply within the ponds.

- **Predator presence:** [[Tiger Salamander]] larvae established through natural reproduction in three of four ponds in each treatment (Appendix B). Salamander larvae are predaceous, feed directly on razorback larvae, and also consume [[Zooplankton]], which larval suckers use as food (Collins and Holomuzki 1984).
- **Salamander patterns:** A Mann-Whitney test showed a trend toward larger salamanders at low fertilization than at high and medium fertilization. Salamander numbers per hectare were greater at high and medium fertilization than at low. No statistical correlation was found between salamander numbers and fish numbers.
- **Zooplankton:** Zooplankton was plentiful, according to unpublished data cited in the report. No reduction in razorback survival was clearly linked to the presence of larger salamanders, so direct competition appeared unlikely.
- **Predation risk by size:** At low fertilization, salamanders were few but large, so predation could have been a factor. At high and medium fertilization, salamanders were numerous but small, so predation was expected to be minimal. In one high-fertilization pond (Pond 1), salamanders were both abundant and large, so predation may have reduced razorback survival there.

## Key Findings

**Survival**
- No statistically significant differences in survival were found among high, medium, and low fertilization treatments (Figure 2).
- Mean survival in the low treatment was 67.4%. The values for high and medium treatments are partly illegible in the source, but the discussion states that survival in both was greater than, or near, the 73% level predicted from earlier experience at DNFH (B. L. Jensen, pers. comm.).
- Lower survival at high-treatment ponds compared with medium may partly reflect salamander influence, but the authors describe this as confounded.

**Growth**
- Mean larval growth, whether measured as total biomass or individual weight, differed among treatments. Except for the high and medium treatments, pairwise comparisons for the last two data sets showed significant differences.
- Individual and total biomass varied significantly and positively with fertilization rate. A least-squares regression gave r = 0.661 between fertilization treatment and larval razorback biomass. Adding salamander biomass to give total planktivore biomass raised the correlation only slightly.

**Interpretation**
- Larger, presumably stronger, larvae may result from higher fertility conditions. This could influence survival in nature.
- Competition from salamanders appeared unlikely to harm razorbacks directly.
- Predation by salamanders may have affected survival in some ponds, particularly where large salamanders were abundant.

**Data and reporting**
- The report presents preliminary results only, and all data are in Appendices A and B.
- Appendix A lists per-pond stocking, recovery, and growth values for each treatment. Appendix B lists salamander numbers and biomass for each pond.
- Subsequent reports were planned to cover the laboratory food-level experiment, the point of starvation onset, and time to starvation death.
