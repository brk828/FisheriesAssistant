## Executive Summary

This interim report by Diana Papoulias (Arizona State University, 1985) documents experimental pond studies investigating growth and survival of larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The species has been extirpated from most of its historical range, with the only substantial remaining population in [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada) consisting entirely of aging adults (24–44 years old as of 1980-81), indicating **no successful recruitment since the 1950s**. While reproduction and larval production still occur, wild larvae disappear before reaching sizes larger than 18 mm TL, suggesting a critical early-life-history bottleneck. This study tested whether pond fertilization rate (a proxy for food/productivity) affects larval growth and survival, as part of a larger four-experiment research program addressing predation, environmental, and food-resource hypotheses for recruitment failure.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Xyrauchen texanus* — Razorback Sucker, family Catostomidae.
- **Stocking size:** Larvae stocked at 16 mm total length (TL), approximately 5 days post-hatch.
- **Size threshold in wild populations:** No wild-collected razorback sucker larvae have been found larger than 18 mm TL, implicating an early mortality bottleneck shortly after this size.
- **Recovered individual weights (mean, mg/fish) by treatment:**
  - Low fertilization: ~95.7 mg/fish (SE = 5.45)
  - Medium fertilization: ~132.3 mg/fish (SE = 15.5)
  - High fertilization: ~149.6 mg/fish (SE = 17.7)
- **Age structure of wild adult population (Lake Mohave, via otolith analysis):** 24–44 years old in 1980–81 (McCarthy 1986), confirming a single aging cohort with no juvenile recruitment for decades.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native habitat:** Lower Colorado River basin; primary extant population in the lentic, reservoir-like habitat of [[Lake Mohave]].
- **Experimental habitat:** Twelve ~0.1 ha earthen ponds at DNFH, treated with three fertilization regimes (high, medium, low/none) using alfalfa pellets and phosphorus (P₂O₅-P) fertilizer, applied in early February and at half-dose biweekly intervals thereafter.
- **Stocking density:** 250,000 individuals/ha, derived from artificially fertilized eggs from wild [[Lake Mohave]] broodfish ([[Hamman 1985]] spawning protocol).
- **Water temperature regime:** Increased from 12°C (February) to 17°C (April) over the ~7-week grow-out period (25 Feb–18/19 Apr 1985).
- **Food web interactions:**
  - Larval razorback suckers feed on zooplankton, which is shared with larval [[Tiger Salamander]] (*Ambystoma tigrinum*), an unplanned colonist in most experimental ponds via natural reproduction.
  - Tiger salamander larvae are confirmed predators of razorback sucker larvae (via stomach content analysis) and also compete for zooplankton resources.
  - Zooplankton was reported as plentiful throughout the experiment, reducing the likelihood of significant food competition between salamanders and sucker larvae.
- **Predation dynamics:** Salamander predation impact appeared size- and density-dependent rather than uniformly negative — large salamanders at low densities (low fertilization ponds) posed potential predation risk despite low competitive pressure, while abundant but small salamanders in high/medium fertilization ponds posed minimal predation risk except in one outlier pond.

## Key Findings

- **Survival was NOT significantly different among fertilization treatments** (p > .1):
  - Low: 67.4% (also reported as 67.6%/67.3% in appendix tables)
  - Medium: 89.8%
  - High: 77.0% (appendix: 76.6%)
  - All values were at or above the ~75% survival benchmark previously observed at DNFH (per B. L. Jensen, pers. comm.), except the low-fertilization group, which was somewhat lower (though not statistically significant).

- **Growth (biomass & individual weight) DID differ significantly among treatments** (p < .1):
  - Total biomass (g/ha, thousands) and individual weight (mg/fish) increased with fertilization level.
  - Pairwise comparisons showed high vs. low and medium vs. low were significantly different; high vs. medium was not significantly different.
  - Mean individual weights: Low ≈ 95.7 mg, Medium ≈ 132.3 mg, High ≈ 149.6 mg.

- **Fertilization–biomass correlation:** A positive correlation was found between fertilization treatment and larval razorback sucker biomass (r = 0.64), which increased only marginally (r = 0.68) when larval tiger salamander biomass was included as total planktivore biomass — suggesting fertilization primarily benefits sucker larvae growth directly rather than through a shared planktivore compartment.

- **Salamander confound:** Tiger salamanders established naturally in 3 of 4 ponds per treatment. No statistical correlation was found between salamander numbers and sucker fish numbers overall. However:
  - Mann-Whitney tests indicated a trend toward **larger** salamanders at low fertilization and **more numerous** (but smaller) salamanders at medium/high fertilization.
  - One exceptional pond (Pond 1, high fertilization) had unusually abundant AND large salamanders (27,500/ha at ~2.0 g each), coinciding with the **lowest survival value in the high-fertilization group**, suggesting localized predation impact.

- **Interpretation:** Results suggest fertilization rate does not strongly govern larval survival directly but does significantly enhance growth/biomass. Since larger larvae are presumed more robust, higher fertility conditions could indirectly promote survival in the wild by producing sturdier young, even though direct survival percentages were statistically similar across treatments in this controlled setting.

- **Broader implications:** Findings support the hypothesis that environmental/food-resource factors (rather than predation alone) may shape larval condition and, by extension, recruitment potential — though this study did not find fertilization to be a decisive direct driver of survival under experimental pond conditions. This aligns with related work ([[Langhorst 1985]]) suggesting predation intensity alone is insufficient to explain high wild larval mortality, redirecting focus toward environmental and nutritional factors as documented in this and subsequent planned experiments (laboratory feeding-level trials, starvation-onset trials).
