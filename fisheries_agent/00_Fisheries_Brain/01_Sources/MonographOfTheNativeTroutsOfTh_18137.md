# Native Trouts of the Genus Salmo of Western North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This monograph by [[Robert J. Behnke]] (1979, published by the Rocky Mountain Region of the USDA Forest Service in 1980) reviews the native trouts of the subgenus *Parasalmo* in western North America. It covers identification, taxonomy, distribution, status and management value. The work argues that taxonomic boundaries among western trouts are a "gray area" because no sterility barriers separate them, and that the most management-relevant traits (migration, temperature tolerance, feeding specialization) vary among local populations rather than among named taxa. The author treats these local genetic stocks as a resource to protect and use, and presents evidence that native-trout special-regulation fisheries and wild-stock management outperform hatchery stocking. The document is a monograph whose table of contents runs to the Addenda, but the supplied text ends partway through the account of the Yellowstone cutthroat trout.

## Background & Description

**Scope and authorship.** The monograph was funded by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], the [[Bureau Of Land Management]] and the [[U.S. Forest Service]]. It builds on a 1963 manuscript co-written with [[Paul Needham]], which the author withdrew after Needham's death in 1964. The author states that the work deliberately emphasizes biology and ecology over taxonomic technicalities.

**Taxonomic framework.** Following [[Vladykov]] (1963), the cutthroat and rainbow trouts are placed in the subgenus *Parasalmo*. The author proposes three series:
1. The **cutthroat trout**: one species, *Salmo clarki*, with 15 subspecies listed in the systematic section (the text elsewhere cites 14 interior subspecies).
2. The **rainbow and redband trouts**: two provisionally recognized species, *S. gairdneri* (coastal rainbow) and *S. newberryi* (interior redband).
3. The **lower Colorado and Gulf of California trouts**: three species with no subspecies recognized, *S. chrysogaster*, *S. gilae* and *S. apache*.

The author notes that about 50 species were described between 1792 and 1972 and that many were invalid products of the "typological" era. He also explains that the [[Redband Trout]] is a distinct evolutionary line rather than a hybrid of rainbow and cutthroat trout, a conclusion reached after collections from 1967 to 1972.

**Key diagnostic traits.**
- Spotting pattern and coloration are the most consistent subspecies characters. Coastal cutthroat have profuse, irregular spots; interior cutthroat such as [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] have fewer, rounded spots.
- [[Basibranchial Teeth]] are the main field indicator of hybridization. The author states that more than 90 percent of a pure cutthroat population should have them.
- Chromosome numbers differ: coastal cutthroat 68–70, interior cutthroat 64, and rainbow trout 58–60 (ranging 58–64 in coastal rainbow).

**Fossil and ancestry notes.** The subgenus *Parasalmo* is thought to have diverged roughly 10 million years ago or more. The earliest salmonid fossil cited is *Eosalmo* from the Middle Eocene (about 40 million years old). The Kamchatkan *S. mykiss* is estimated to have separated from *S. gairdneri* no more than 11,000–12,000 years ago.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Cutthroat trout have the widest range of the western trouts. The [[Coastal Cutthroat Trout]] runs from Prince William Sound to the Eel River, California, and typically lies less than 100 miles inland. Interior forms occupy the Columbia, upper Missouri, Yellowstone, Snake, Colorado, Rio Grande and Great Basin systems, often above barrier falls. Coastal and interior cutthroat ranges do not overlap. Coastal rainbow trout extend from the Kuskokwim River, Alaska, to the Rio del Presidio, Mexico. Redband trout are native to the Columbia Basin east of the Cascades, the Oregon desert basins, and the upper Sacramento system.

**Hybridization and introductions.** The author identifies rainbow trout introductions and hatchery stocking as the main drivers of native-trout loss. Brook trout and brown trout have also replaced cutthroat in many waters. Documented examples include:
- Black Hollow Creek, Colorado: after a 1967 rotenone treatment and 1968 greenback stocking, brook trout reached dense numbers and no cutthroat were found in 1977.
- The Estes Park stockings of 1922 and 1923 (about 270,000 fish) that confused the status of the Big Thompson greenback population.
- Crescent Lake, Washington, where heavy stocking of hatchery rainbow and Yellowstone cutthroat produced common hybrids by the 1950s.

**Habitat and land use.** The author argues that stream habitat, not water chemistry or invertebrate diversity, is usually the limiting factor. Slower, deeper water (over 1 foot) with cover supports more and larger trout. Livestock overgrazing that removes riparian vegetation triggers channel degradation, and the author reports that trout biomass was three to four times higher in protected stream sections than in grazed sections. The [[Douglas Creek]] watershed in western Colorado, historically home to *S. c. pleuriticus*, is cited as a case of grazing-driven arroyo cutting.

**Life history variation.** The author stresses that life history traits such as migration, age at maturity and growth are expressed at the population level, so they cannot be generalized from one population to a whole subspecies. Kootenay Lake, British Columbia, provides an example: two redband races differ sharply in feeding, size and maximum age.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomy is provisional.** About 50 species were described from 1792 to 1972. The author proposes a reduced, lineage-based scheme and deliberately leaves several forms unnamed to avoid invalid "nomen nudum" names.
- **Redband trout are a distinct line.** Electrophoretic, chromosomal and morphological evidence support a separate lineage from coastal rainbow trout, though some forms cannot be assigned with certainty.
- **Management value lies in local stocks.** Natural selection makes populations uniquely adapted; for example, the Lahontan cutthroat tolerates high alkalinity, and redband trout from Chino Creek survive 83 °F water.
- **Special regulations work for cutthroat.** On the upper St. Joe River, Idaho, catch-and-release regulations (13-inch size limit, 3-fish bag limit, artificial lures) increased abundance fourfold (214 to 878) and catch per man-hour sixfold (0.8 to 4.8). Kelly Creek abundance rose from 27 to 355. Angling pressure above about 50 hours per acre per year is identified as the threshold for overexploitation.
- **Hatchery stocks underperform wild stocks.** Wild trout fisheries were valued at 1.8 to 1.0 relative to catchable-trout fisheries on the Poudre River, Colorado. A Lake Berryessa test showed Eagle Lake wild trout producing 50 percent more creel biomass than the Coleman rainbow.
- **Hooking mortality varies.** Bait-caught trout suffer 30–50 percent mortality after release, while artificial-lure-caught trout suffer about 5 percent.
- **Angler harvest can be extreme.** At Dutch Creek, Alberta, an estimated minimum of 78 percent of catchable cutthroat were harvested, prompting alternate-year closures.
- **Subspecies extinctions and declines.** Of 14 interior cutthroat subspecies recognized, the author reports 2 extinct (Yellowfin trout of Twin Lakes, Colorado, and the Alvord Basin form), 10 with catastrophic declines, and 2 still stable (the fine-spotted Snake River form and an undescribed form from Willow and Whitehorse creeks, Oregon).
- **Predatory variation.** Lahontan cutthroat (*S. c. henshawi*) were the only large predators in Pleistocene Lake Lahontan; the Pyramid Lake population was credited with a 41-pound world record, possibly over 60 pounds. Lewisi cutthroat, by contrast, specialize on invertebrates in lakes where kokanee are abundant.
- **Stocking history confounds identification.** Stocking records showed that "Salmo evermanni" was a Lahontan cutthroat synonym, and that a Green River lake population was introduced Yellowstone cutthroat (stocked 1937 by the [[Civilian Conservation Corps]]).
- **Conservation recommendation.** The author urges protecting remaining native genetic diversity, avoiding mitigation by hatchery stocking where native fisheries are lost, and cautions that the Yellowstone Lake cutthroat, a widely propagated form, is not representative of the species as a whole.
