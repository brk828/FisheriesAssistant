# Genetic Effects of Cultured Fish on Natural Fish Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review, by [[Kjetil Hindar]], [[Nils Ryman]] and [[Fred Utter]] (published in the *Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences*, 1991), assesses the genetic consequences of [[Aquaculture]] on natural fish populations, focusing on [[Salmonids]]. Driven by rapidly rising numbers of intentionally ([[Sea Ranching]]) and accidentally ([[Net-Pen Escapees]]) released cultured fish, the authors compile empirical observations from the literature. Outcomes range from no detectable effect to complete [[Introgression]] or displacement. Where performance effects were documented, all were negative for the indigenous population relative to the unaffected native stock. The authors argue that the genetic future of many natural populations is threatened, and recommend strong restrictions on gene flow from cultured to wild stocks, along with effective monitoring.

## Background & Description

- **Scope of the problem:** Cultured salmonids are often genetically exogenous, or altered by inbreeding, selective breeding, or [[Domestication]]. In many spawning populations, released fish now outnumber wild fish (Gausen and Moen 1991).
- **Why salmonids:** The authors cite three reasons. Salmonids are well studied genetically and ecologically; local populations are strongly differentiated, largely through [[Local Adaptation]]; and they have been reared and released for more than a century, often into areas with indigenous conspecifics.
- **Framing:** The paper treats escapes and intentional releases alike, since both bring exogenous fish into contact with native stocks. It extends [[Ricker 1972]] by including molecular genetic data developed after that review.
- **Three impact categories:** Interbreeding (direct genetic effect), competition (possible indirect genetic effect through reduced population size), and contamination by pathogens (for example, [[Gyrodactylus Salaris]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Release scale and intent**
- Sea-ranched smolts range from 8 million Atlantic salmon in North Atlantic and Baltic drainages to more than 4 billion Pacific salmon in North Pacific drainages (Isaksson 1988).
- Intentionally released fish made up as much as 80% of local spawners for coho salmon and rainbow (steelhead) trout in some cases.

**Norwegian escape data**
- Norwegian aquaculture production doubled roughly every second year after 1970, reaching 115,000 tons in 1989. Wild Atlantic salmon yield was stable at 1,200 tons in Norwegian home waters and 300 tons in rivers.
- Storms in December 1988 and January 1989 released an estimated 1.2 million salmon (over 1,200 tons), equal to the total annual catch of wild Atlantic salmon in Norway.
- Farmed proportions among spawners in southern Norwegian rivers averaged 13% (1987) and 28% (1988); up to 80% in areas of intense culture.

**Escapes elsewhere**
- Burrishoole River, Ireland: farmed fish rose from 4% (1986) to 28% (1988) of ascending Atlantic salmon.
- River Lochy, Scotland: escapees were almost 20% of catches in 1988.
- Puget Sound, Washington: Atlantic salmon escapees observed over a broad range; experimentally released coho produced an estimated 700–4,000 spawners.

**Reproduction of released fish**
- Sea-ranched Atlantic salmon and hatchery steelhead reproduce successfully in nature.
- In two Norwegian rivers, redds containing eggs of pen-reared fish were 15% and 38%. In the River Oselven, 38% of redds (N = 8) contained eggs from escaped females, against 56% escaped females among spawners (N = 52).

## Key Findings

**1. Genetic structure (Table 1 and text)**
- Outcomes range from displacement to no detectable effect. In the Naiba River (Sakhalin), mass transfer of chum eggs displaced donor-population gene frequencies in the first generation, and total returns fell sharply relative to the undisturbed Kalininka River.
- Introgression with native stocks was documented in brown trout (Northern Ireland, France), rainbow trout (Washington), and cutthroat trout (western USA).
- Some native populations (for example, southern Idaho "redband" trout) show no detectable introgression despite heavy planting.
- Hybridization rates increase with releases beyond natural range, for example brown trout with Atlantic salmon in Newfoundland.
- Chinook hatchery populations in the lower Columbia River showed similar allele frequencies across 25 polymorphic loci (Utter et al. 1989).

**2. Performance traits (Table 1, 38 observations in total across trait categories)**
- Survival: wild fingerlings and fry survive better than hatchery or hybrid fry. Natural offspring of hatchery fish survive less well.
- Behavior: hatchery offspring show weaker territorial behavior and less concealment. Hatchery steelhead swimming stamina was lower in domestic-parent offspring (brook trout).
- Homing and straying: recapture of transplanted coho was negatively related to transfer distance. Recapture of transplanted stock was about one-tenth of local stock in one case, and one-fifth or less in another.
- Disease: foreign stocks were more susceptible to *Ceratomyxa shasta* than local stocks or hybrids. Baltic salmon were resistant to *G. salaris*, while two Norwegian stocks were highly susceptible.

**3. Indirect and stock-size effects**
- Hatchery coho stocking in Oregon produced higher juvenile density in stocked streams during the stocking summer, but returning adult density did not differ. The following generation had fewer juveniles in stocked streams.
- *G. salaris* introduced via Baltic-origin hatchery fish has threatened Norwegian Atlantic salmon, with declines in more than 30 rivers.
- In the Skagit River, steelhead harvest assumptions had to be revised; all wild fish caught must now be released.
- In the Yaquina River, Oregon, coho decline was linked to predators attracted by a sea-ranching facility.

**4. Synthesis (five conclusions)**
- Growing net-pen escapes increase the number of exogenous spawners, and many of them spawn successfully.
- Direct genetic consequences vary from complete introgression to no detectable change.
- Populations may be indirectly threatened through reduced size from harvest or predation in mixed fisheries.
- Local salmonid populations appear adapted to their environments, so exogenous gene influx is generally harmful. Wherever a difference was detected, indigenous fish performed better.
- Outcomes cannot be predicted for any specific case.

**5. Theoretical basis**
- Predictions are difficult because key parameters (population sizes, selection, migration, genetic basis of traits) are poorly known.
- Hybridization can break down locally adapted gene complexes ("outbreeding depression"). Hybrid populations may lose the distinctness of parental populations, a process the authors describe as producing "mongrel" populations.

**6. Recommended strategies**
- *Closed culture:* reduce escapes through legislation and relocate marine operations inland (one coho operation in Idaho relocated for economic reasons).
- *Sterilization:* hormonal or triploid sterility is feasible, but sterile males can still court, and sterile fish may spread pathogens. Large-scale release is not yet justified.
- *Localization:* limit culture and release sites, release smolts in large rivers, and set aside protected areas. Washington State reserved 25% of Puget Sound for hatchery-based management.
- *Local stock origin:* base aquaculture on local populations to reduce exotic gene and pathogen introduction.
- *Monitoring:* [[Genetic Marking]] of cultured stocks is the authors' most important step. Fluctuating asymmetry can complement biochemical monitoring, and baseline data are essential.

**7. Eight-point recommendations (affirming the 1980 Stockholm Fish Gene Pools symposium)**
- Minimize gene flow from aquaculture, and locate and design facilities to prevent escape.
- Select rearing and release sites with minimal impact; protect some areas completely.
- Restrict movements of fish that may carry pathogens.
- Catalogue genetic characteristics of potentially affected natural populations as baseline data.
- Require genetic marks on cultured stocks.
- Run experimental releases of genetically identifiable fish before large-scale operations.
- Encourage sterile fish, but study their ecological and genetic effects first.

**Core message:** The authors state that the primary issue is "immediate action based on everything that is already known," not more research.
