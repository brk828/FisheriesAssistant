# Threatened Native Wildlife in Arizona (1988 TNW List)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #multi-taxa #wildlife-conservation

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department publication, approved by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Commission]] at a public meeting on July 21, 1988, presents the state's official Threatened Native Wildlife (TNW) list. The list covers 116 taxa (species or subspecies) across fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals. Most listed taxa have suffered significant habitat loss, and many are also federally listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The document explains the listing philosophy, defines four status categories, and gives a species-by-species account of threats, distribution and recovery efforts. Its central message is that habitat conservation must act before populations collapse, because extinct species cannot be recovered.

## Background & Description

The list was first developed in 1975 under authority implicit in Arizona Revised Statute 17. It was revised in 1976 and 1982. Earlier versions listed some species solely for their limited distribution, which the 1988 revision corrects. Under the current approach, the focus is on the degree to which habitats or populations are threatened and on the probability that a taxon will be extirpated from Arizona. Documented population declines and identified threats are the most important factors. Where data are lacking, listing decisions depend on professional inference and informed opinion.

Listing conventions:

- **Species versus subspecies:** If only one subspecies in a multi-subspecies animal is threatened, only that subspecies is listed. If only one form occurs in Arizona, the species as a whole is listed.
- **Four categories:**
  - *Extinct:* gone forever, with no possibility of recovery.
  - *Endangered:* extirpated from Arizona, or extinction or extirpation is highly probable without conservation.
  - *Threatened:* continued presence could be in jeopardy in the near future; populations are lower than historical levels or extremely local and small.
  - *Candidate:* threats are known or suspected, but substantial declines have not been documented.
- **Extirpated species:** Taxa eliminated from Arizona but surviving elsewhere, which may be reintroduced.

The list is intended to guide land management priorities. The department acknowledges that earlier "unique" listings sometimes diverted resources to species whose biological status did not warrant priority attention.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Arizona's varied topography, climate and geologic history create many habitat types, and the state hosts more than 800 vertebrate species. The document identifies human-caused habitat fragmentation, degradation and destruction as the primary drivers of wildlife decline, replacing natural factors. Water diversion, channelization, groundwater depletion, grazing, road building, urban development and introduced species, especially nonnative fishes and bullfrogs, recur throughout the species accounts.

Hunting and fishing were discussed as a contrast. Some game animals face harvest pressure but remain abundant, so they are not listed. A species qualifies only when it is both subject to excessive take and faces significant threats or declining populations. The Arizona ridge-nosed rattlesnake is cited as an example of a listed species, while the closely related rock rattlesnake is not, because its habitat is more extensive and less threatened.

## Key Findings

**Scope and summary table**
- The list includes 116 taxa. The summary table's totals (5, 48, 24, 39) sum to 116. The scan garbles the column headers, so the per-category breakdown cannot be fully confirmed.
- Groups represented: fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

**Fish (Colorado River and Gila basin)**
- Extinct: [[Monkey Springs Pupfish]], eliminated by 1971 through habitat alteration and predation by introduced [[Largemouth Bass]].
- Endangered taxa include the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (extirpated before the mid-1960s; reintroductions began in 1985), the [[Razorback Sucker]] (reintroductions since 1981, long-term success unknown), the [[Desert Pupfish]] (reintroduced in 1983 at four localities), the [[Yaqui Chub]] (re-established at several sites from 1967 to 1986) and the [[Humpback Chub]] (survives mainly in Grand Canyon and the lower Little Colorado River).
- Gila topminnow: reduced to 10 sites by 1982, with recovery efforts underway since then.
- Gila trout: reintroduced to Cap Creek in the Verde River drainage, where it is reproducing.

**Amphibians**
- Huachuca tiger salamander and barking frog are among the most at-risk taxa. The barking frog is known from only two specimens, and the Tarahumara frog's last Arizona individual was found dead in 1983.
- Several leopard frogs are threatened by bullfrogs, habitat loss and nonnative predators.

**Reptiles**
- Desert tortoise: a candidate species. A federal listing petition was found "warranted but precluded" by higher-priority listings. The department did not believe federal listing was justified for the population south and east of the Colorado River.
- Massasauga: restricted to a few local populations in southeastern Cochise County, with road mortality a concern.
- Fringe-toed lizards are threatened by agriculture, pesticide drift, urbanization and off-road vehicles.

**Birds**
- [[California Condor]]: extirpated; last reported near Williams around 1924.
- [[Bald Eagle]]: endangered; threats include lead poisoning, timber harvest, and loss of foraging perches and riparian habitat.
- [[Yuma Clapper Rail]]: federally endangered, threatened by channelization and drying or flooding of marshes.
- Northern aplomado falcon and masked bobwhite: extirpated, with the bobwhite reintroduced on Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge in 1985.
- Thick-billed parrot: a release program started in 1986 in the Chiricahua Mountains.

**Mammals**
- [[Sanborn's Long-Nosed Bat]]: endangered and federally listed. Both the number of occupied roosts and the number of individuals per colony have declined sharply. Threats include agave harvest in Mexico and human disturbance of roosts.
- [[Mexican Gray Wolf]]: extirpated from Arizona; roughly 35 animals were in captive-breeding programs.
- Intermountain gray wolf: extinct; the last resident population likely disappeared before the mid-1930s.
- [[Grizzly Bear]]: extirpated; the last confirmed Arizona record was from 1939.
- [[Sonoran Pronghorn]]: endangered; a small remnant population in southwestern Arizona and adjacent Mexico, threatened by poaching.
- Black-footed ferret: possibly extirpated; escaped domestic ferrets may threaten reintroduction efforts.
- Mount Graham red squirrel: endangered and restricted to high-elevation spruce-fir forest in the Pinaleño Mountains. A proposed astrophysical complex poses an additional threat.

**Management principles**
- The list's purpose is to prompt early action. Candidate species are highlighted so that habitat protection can begin before populations decline further.
- Public input on additional species is requested through the department's Endangered Species Coordinator at the Nongame Branch in Phoenix.
- The department acknowledges that many species are still poorly known and that listing decisions often rely on informed opinion.
