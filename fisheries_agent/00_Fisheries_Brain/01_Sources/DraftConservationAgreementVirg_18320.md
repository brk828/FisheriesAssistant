# Draft Conservation Agreement for Virgin Spinedace

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #conservation #endangered-species #virgin-river

## Executive Summary

This draft Conservation Agreement for the [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*) establishes a comprehensive framework to ensure the continued existence and recovery of this endemic minnow in the [[Virgin River]] drainage basin spanning Utah, Arizona, and Nevada. The species has experienced a 37-40% reduction in historic range (approximately 83.9 km/52.1 mi of 226.4 km/140.4 mi historic habitat) due to water development, agriculture, mining, urbanization, and nonnative fish introductions. Proposed as threatened by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service in May 1994, the species currently occupies approximately 60-63% of historic habitat, nearly all in Utah. The agreement involves eight signatory agencies including state wildlife departments, federal land management agencies, and water districts, committing to a 10-year implementation period with estimated costs exceeding $3,000,000.

## Background & Description

The [[Virgin Spinedace]] belongs to the endemic western cyprinid tribe Plagopterini, which includes three genera: *Meda*, *Plagopterus*, and *Lepidomeda*. The species derives its name from the fusion of two anterior hardened spiny rays in the dorsal and pelvic fins, with the specific epithet referring to the soft-tipped second dorsal spine. Adults typically range 60-120 mm (2.4-4.7 in) standard length with silvery bodies exhibiting brassy sheen and occasional sooty blotches dorso-laterally. During breeding, paired fin bases become reddish-orange. The species has 77-91 lateral line scales and pharyngeal teeth typically numbering 2,5-4,2.

Life history studies by Rinne (1971) established a three-year lifespan with sexual maturity at one year. Populations consist primarily of young-of-year and one-year-old fish. Annual spawning occurs April through June at mean daily water temperatures of 13-17°C and day lengths around 13 hours. One-year-old females average 459 eggs, while two- and three-year-olds show 42% and 34% increases in mean relative fecundity. The species is primarily insectivorous, feeding on drifting prey in midwater and at the surface in a manner similar to drift-feeding salmonids.

Historic distribution included most clearwater tributaries and several mainstem reaches of southwestern Utah, northwestern Arizona, and southeastern Nevada. Museum records from University of Nevada Las Vegas, Brigham Young University, University of Michigan Museum of Zoology, and the United States National Museum document early occurrence. The earliest surveys (Tanner 1932, 1936) indicated the species was common in the [[Santa Clara River]] and [[North Fork Virgin River]], but probably less common in the mainstem Virgin River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Virgin River]] basin encompasses approximately 15,600 km² (6,000 mi²) with elevations ranging from 3,300 m (10,000 ft) above mean sea level near headwaters to less than 700 m (2,000 ft) at Littlefield, Arizona. Mean annual precipitation ranges from 20 cm (8 in) at low elevations to 100 cm (40 in) at higher elevations. The diverse topography provides habitat for over 366 wildlife species, of which 81 are identified as sensitive. Six fish species are indigenous to the basin: [[Woundfin]], [[Virgin River Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Desert Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Virgin Spinedace]]. Five of these have been listed or are candidates for federal listing under the Endangered Species Act.

[[Virgin Spinedace]] typically inhabit clear, cool, swift streams with interspersed pools, runs, and riffles. Upper thermal preferences are reported as 23.1°C. Habitat preferences vary by location: in [[Beaver Dam Wash]], fish utilize narrow, shallow runs with large amounts of emergent vegetation, while in [[North Fork Virgin River]], they occupy quiet pools. The species also prefers shear zones between high (100 cm/sec) and low (10 cm/sec) velocities containing cover. Nursery habitat preferences remain unclear.

Current populations exist in the mainstem Virgin River and eleven tributaries: [[East Fork Virgin River]], [[Shunes Creek]], [[North Fork Virgin River]], [[North Creek]], [[La Verkin Creek]], [[Ash Creek]], [[Santa Clara River]], [[Beaver Dam Wash]], [[Coal Pits Wash]], [[Moody Wash]], and [[Magotsu Creek]]. The largest populations occur in the upper mainstem above [[Quail Creek Diversion]] and in drainages of the [[Santa Clara River]] and [[Beaver Dam Wash]]. Small populations exist in [[Ash Creek]], [[La Verkin Creek]], and the lower mainstem below [[Pah Tempe Springs]].

Shoreline land ownership within floodplains is approximately 38% federal, 3% state, 5% Paiute Tribe managed, and 54% private. This mosaic ownership complicates regulation, monitoring, and basin-wide protection. The diverse ownership includes Bureau of Land Management, National Park Service, U.S. Forest Service, Paiute Indian Tribe, States of Utah, Arizona, and Nevada, and private landowners.

## Key Findings

**Habitat Loss and Degradation:**
Approximately 6.8 km (4.2 mi) of historic habitat has been inundated by reservoirs including [[Quail Creek Reservoir]], [[Gunlock Reservoir]], and [[Schroeder Reservoir]]. Approximately 59.9 km (37.1 mi) of historic habitat has been dewatered by diversions, and diversions have depleted water in approximately 30.9 km (19.2 mi) of currently occupied habitat. Twenty-four percent of currently occupied habitat has experienced some degree of adverse modification. Dams and diversions function as migration barriers causing fragmentation of habitat and populations.

**Nonnative Species Threats:**
Several nonnative fish species occupy the same habitat as [[Virgin Spinedace]], including [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*), [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*), and various other species. Crayfish (Astacidae) may prey on larval and young-of-year life stages in lower reaches. The Asian Tapeworm has been introduced into the basin. These species affect [[Virgin Spinedace]] through predation, competition for limited resources (food and space), and disease transmission.

**Conservation Strategy Objectives:**
The agreement focuses on two primary objectives: (1) eliminate significant threats or reduce those that cannot be completely eliminated, and (2) stabilize and enhance specific reaches of occupied and unoccupied historic habitat. The goal is to ensure the species occupies at least 80% (181.1 km/112.3 mi) of its historic range through five management actions: re-establishing and maintaining required flows, enhancing and maintaining habitat, selectively controlling nonnative fish, maintaining genetic viability, and monitoring populations and habitat.

**Flow Management:**
Two critical hydrograph components are identified: (1) channel-maintenance flows (high flows preventing vegetation growth in channels and removing sediments), and (2) fish maintenance flows (flows required during low-flow periods to maintain self-sustaining populations). Fish maintenance flows currently occur in approximately 15 occupied stream reaches. Interim fish maintenance flows will be established in approximately 38.7 km (24 mi) of historic habitat to reduce fragmentation and restore populations. Priority areas include 30.6 km (19.0 mi) of [[Santa Clara River]] between [[Gunlock Reservoir]] and the Virgin River confluence, and 4.5 km (2.8 mi) of Virgin River between [[Quail Creek Diversion]] and [[Pah Tempe Springs]]. Flow responses will be evaluated for five years before finalizing permanent requirements.

**Habitat Enhancement:**
Enhancement procedures will be implemented in approximately 25.4 km (15.8 mi) of occupied habitat, focusing on agricultural activities, recreational use of riparian zones, and water quality factors. Projects include boundary-line fence maintenance and construction between federal and private parcels, intensive grazing management programs for federal lands, and conservation easements within the Virgin River floodplain. Mitigation for acceptable future projects in occupied habitat will generally be based on one-to-one replacement of historic habitat.

**Nonnative Fish Control:**
Basin-wide procedures for controlling stocking, introduction, and spread of undesirable aquatic species will be implemented. Stocking of native species will follow Conservation Agreement protocols. Salmonid stocking will be restricted to areas with existing populations where they will not conflict with native species of special concern. [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking to establish new self-sustaining populations in association with native fishes of special concern is prohibited. [[Brown Trout]] stocking is generally prohibited except for replacing lost populations above 5,000 feet elevation where no conflicts with native species occur. Prohibited species include nonnative minnows (Family: Cyprinidae), [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieni*), [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Crappie]] (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*), all crayfish species, and other non-indigenous aquatic species.

**Specific Eradication Projects:**
Nevada Division of Wildlife will lead efforts to remove [[Rainbow Trout]] from upper [[Beaver Dam Wash]] below [[Schroeder Reservoir]] and re-introduce [[Virgin Spinedace]] in early summer 1995. Utah Division of Wildlife Resources will evaluate chemical renovation feasibility in the [[Santa Clara River]] drainage to control or eliminate [[Green Sunfish]] and other exotic fishes. Chemical treatments to eradicate [[Red Shiner]] from the Virgin River between [[Washington Fields Diversion]] and [[Mesquite Diversion]] will be implemented in 1995 using rotenone at approximately 20 drip stations, with detoxification using potassium permanganate. Temporary fish barriers will be constructed at strategic sites.

**Monitoring and Administration:**
A monitoring plan will assess [[Virgin Spinedace]] population and habitat responses to management actions. A minimum of 10 stations will be chosen as monitoring points throughout the basin, with annual fall sampling using 15' x 6' mesh seines until depletion. All native fish will be identified, counted, measured, and returned to capture sites. The Virgin Spinedace Conservation Team (VSCT) will administer the agreement, consisting of designated representatives from each signatory agency, technical advisors, and other members as necessary. The team will meet annually to develop yearly conservation schedules and quarterly to report implementation progress.

**Implementation Timeline and Costs:**
Full implementation is anticipated over 10 years, with significant survival-ensuring actions implemented within the first five years. Estimated total costs exceed $3,000,000, with the greatest expense (approximately $2,000,000) associated with re-establishment and/or enhancement of flows during the first three to five years. Additional costs include: determination of flow requirements ($200,000), maintenance of existing flows ($30,000), formalization of flow protection ($200,000), and annual review of activities ($100,000). Funding will be provided by federal sources (FWS, BLM, Land and Water Conservation funds, Soil Conservation Service), state sources (direct legislative appropriation, Community Impact Boards, Water Resources Revolving funds, State Department of Agriculture, State Resource Management Agencies), and local sources (Habitat Conservation Plan, Water District, cities and towns, Washington County, local irrigation companies).

**Agreement Duration and Compliance:**
The Conservation Agreement duration is five years following the last signature date. Prior to the end of the fifth year, a thorough analysis of implemented actions will be conducted. If all signatories agree that progress toward implementation is acceptable, the agreement will automatically extend for an additional five years. Quarterly progress assessments will be provided to FWS by Utah Division of Wildlife Resources. Annual assessments of conservation accomplishments will determine agreement effectiveness and whether revisions are warranted. If threats to [[Virgin Spinedace]] survival cannot be resolved through the Conservation Agreement, FWS will initiate actions to list the species under Section 4 of ESA through an emergency rule.

**Ecosystem Benefits:**
While the primary focus is [[Virgin Spinedace]] conservation, 366 species of fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals co-exist in the same or adjacent habitat. Eighty-one of these species are listed as sensitive in Utah, Arizona, or Nevada and/or are listed under ESA. An additional eight sensitive plant and seven sensitive invertebrate species also co-exist in the same or adjacent habitat. Using an ecosystem approach, the agreement could reduce or eliminate threats for several of these species, potentially precluding their need for federal listing pursuant to ESA.

**Signatory Agencies and Responsibilities:**
Eight agencies signed the agreement: Utah Department of Natural Resources Division of Wildlife Resources (team leader, oversees administrative responsibilities, consults on water protection, assists in securing water rights and land, plans and implements nonnative eradication projects, leads population and habitat enhancements in Utah); U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (advises on ESA and NEPA compliance, maintains Virgin River fishes database, assists in funding); Bureau of Land Management Utah and Arizona (cooperates in habitat enhancement and monitoring, assists in funding, Arizona leads in developing migration barriers for [[Red Shiner]] eradication); National Park Service (leads funding and implementation within [[Zion National Park]]); Nevada Department of Conservation and Natural Resources Division of Wildlife (leads agency for funding, monitoring, and nonnative control in upper [[Beaver Dam Wash]]); Arizona Game and Fish Department (assists in nonnative control in lower basin reaches); Washington County Water Conservancy District (obtains water rights and land, assists in planning and funding nonnative fish barriers and diversion enhancements).
