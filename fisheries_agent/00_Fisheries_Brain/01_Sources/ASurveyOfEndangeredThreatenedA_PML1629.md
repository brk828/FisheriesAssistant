# Survey of Endangered, Threatened and Unique Fish in Southeastern Utah Streams Within the Coal Planning Area

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by the [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Research Unit]] at Utah State University for the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (contract YA-512-C26-257) with the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, surveys the fish, invertebrates, water quality and habitat of streams on federal coal-lease Public Lands (PL) in southeastern Utah. Fieldwork ran February to June 1977, supplemented by a review of historical records. The study covers seven major rivers (Green, Price, San Rafael, Dirty Devil, Escalante, Paria and Virgin) and addresses the potential aquatic effects of coal mining. Errata on the first pages correct several species identifications (Gila robusta complex and Gila copei/Gila atraria).

## Background & Description

Coal reserves in the region are large: Western reserves total about 198 billion minable tons, and about 82% of Utah's coal reserves are owned or controlled by the federal government, mainly administered by the [[Bureau of Land Management]]. Coal seams in the West are typically 50 to 75 ft thick, against a 10 ft exceptional thickness in the East. Because the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), the [[National Environmental Policy Act]] and the Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act require wildlife values to be considered, the study aimed to document fish distribution and abundance on coal-lease lands, emphasizing the six threatened or endangered species present.

The study area runs diagonally across Utah from the northern border of Carbon County to the Arizona border, bounded east by the Green and Colorado Rivers and west by the Wasatch Mountains. Rivers are typically turbid, carry high sediment loads, and show drastic seasonal flow fluctuation. Tributaries in headwater regions are cool and clear and often support trout; lower-elevation tributaries rarely contain gamefish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish community:** Native species (e.g., flannelmouth and bluehead suckers, speckled dace, roundtail chub) dominate headwater reaches, while introduced warm-water species (carp, red shiner, fathead minnow, channel catfish) dominate downstream areas near river termini. This pattern is especially apparent in the San Rafael, Fremont and Escalante rivers.
- **Rare species:** Endangered fish were found only in the Green/Colorado system and the Virgin River. The four large-river fish ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]]) are found in the Green and Colorado rivers; the [[Woundfin]], [[Virgin River Spinedace]] and [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] occur in the Virgin system.
- **Invertebrates:** Qualitative kick-screen sampling found a poor to moderate standing crop of benthic invertebrates. Common taxa included caddisflies, mayflies, stoneflies, midges, damselflies and dragonflies.
- **Water quality:** Historic flows ranged from yearly averages of about 7,000 cfs (Green River) down to about 5 cfs in smaller streams. Each river had a pH near 8.0, temperatures from 0 to about 30 °C, and dissolved oxygen suitable for fish. Heavy sediment load caused high turbidity, hardness and conductivity.

## Key Findings

**Historical distribution**
- Endangered fish were recorded historically only in the Colorado/Green rivers (squawfish, humpback chub) and the Virgin River (woundfin).
- In Desolation Canyon, Holden (1973) found four rare or endangered species; native fish (seven species) still dominated the system.
- The Virgin River is the best-known drainage; Cross (1975) recorded six native and 13 introduced species there.

**1977 field sampling**
- Adult [[Colorado Squawfish]] were collected throughout Desolation Canyon. Juvenile squawfish in June indicated ongoing reproduction, and a young-of-year squawfish was collected in October from the San Rafael River, the first reported squawfish collection there.
- Juvenile squawfish and a squawfish fry were found in collections during this study; the ranges of other rare fish were not extended.
- No specimens of unique cutthroat trout subspecies were found.
- Woundfin were collected in large numbers at one Virgin River station, with roundtail chub from the same pool.
- Each rare fish range was mapped on BLM land-status maps.

**Rare-fish life histories** (summarized from the literature)
- [[Woundfin]]: prefers mobile sand bottoms and turbid, swift water; spawns April–May; now restricted to the Virgin River below La Verkin.
- [[Virgin River Spinedace]]: found mainly in pools or runs with rubble/cobble bottoms; primarily in tributaries and near their mouths; over 90% of Cross's specimens came from unaltered stream reaches.
- [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]]: Cross (1975) collected only 154 specimens; diet dominated by filamentous algae (>90% of gut contents in 11 specimens).
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: largest native North American cyprinid, reported to reach 45 kg; reproduction has declined drastically in the last 12 years.
- [[Razorback Sucker]]: successful recent reproduction undocumented in the basin; adults may be up to 22 years old.
- [[Humpback Chub]]: unusual nuchal hump; little known of habitat or reproduction.
- [[Bonytail Chub]]: most streamlined form; drastic decline; not collected in two summers (1974–1975) of McAda and Seethaler sampling.

**Critical habitat**
- Critical habitat has so far been published only for the woundfin (Federal Register, 1976). Proposed critical habitat for the Virgin River roundtail chub is similar to that of the woundfin, with spinedace habitat expected to be larger.
- Critical habitat for the Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub and razorback sucker is recommended along the Colorado, Green, Yampa, Gunnison and San Juan rivers; a significant portion lies on or near Public Lands.

**Stream classification**
- Wydoski and Berry (1976) rated habitat for endangered fish: 551.4 miles of PL stream were critical habitat and 344.7 miles were high priority, while PL streams were generally only minor gamefish waters (classes 3–5).

**Water quality and mining effects**
- Water quality was broadly similar across basins. Sediment load produced high turbidity, hardness and conductivity.
- The main potential contaminants from mining are trace elements, sediment and salts. Mine effluent limits for Utah include pH 6.5–9.0, total suspended solids 25 mg/l maximum and oil and grease 10 mg/l.
- Sediment is the principal non-point problem; roads and disturbed areas can yield up to 30,000 tons of silt per square mile of mined land.
- Mine water is often of better quality than local water and could benefit fish and wildlife; one Tongue River (Montana) demonstration produced 50–60% northern pike hatching success in mine effluent.
- Stream dewatering from aquifer disruption is a site-specific risk; the authors note that acid mine drainage is uncommon in the West due to alkaline buffering.

**Conclusions**
- Impacts of mining on rare fish are expected to be minimal for sediment, given naturally high sediment loads and the fish's evolved tolerance, but the effects of trace metals and chronic toxicity remain unknown.
- Impacts must be evaluated site by site, ideally with pre-mining baseline surveys.
