# Review of the State of World Fishery Resources (FAO Fisheries Circular No. 710, Revision 6, 1989)

#fisheries-management #marine-biology #global-assessment #stock-status #FAO

## Executive Summary

This document is the sixth biennial review of global fishery resources prepared by FAO's Marine Resources Service for the Committee on Fisheries (COFI), covering statistics through 1986 with some 1987 data. Total recorded landings of marine and freshwater fish reached 90.9 million tons in 1987, down slightly from the record 92.1 million tons in 1986—the first year-to-year decline since 1976-77. The review provides regional assessments of fish stocks across all FAO statistical marine and inland areas, evaluating exploitation status and management frameworks. Most demersal stocks globally are now fully exploited or overexploited, while recent increases in total catch have come primarily from volatile pelagic species ([[Peruvian Anchovy]], [[South American Sardine]], [[Japanese Sardine]]) and [[Alaska Pollock]]. The document emphasizes that many fisheries show signs of biological degradation and economic waste, with climatic fluctuations often driving stock changes more than management interventions. Environmental degradation—pollution, eutrophication, habitat destruction—increasingly threatens fishery productivity and requires integrated management approaches beyond traditional fishing regulations.

## Background & Description

This circular updates FAO's regular assessment of world fish stocks, building on the original 1970 review by [[J.A. Gulland]] that estimated global biological potential at approximately 100 million tons—a figure still considered valid. The document serves dual purposes: providing information on development possibilities and offering early warning when catches approach resource limits. Since 1970, total world landings increased from approximately 60 million tons to over 90 million tons, with growth patterns showing three distinct phases: steady increase through the 1960s, stagnation through the 1970s following the [[Peruvian Anchovy]] collapse in 1970-71, and renewed increase in the 1980s driven primarily by pelagic species.

The review covers 17 marine FAO statistical areas ([[Northwest Atlantic]], [[Northeast Atlantic]], [[Western Central Atlantic]], [[Eastern Central Atlantic]], [[Mediterranean and Black Sea]], [[Southwest Atlantic]], [[Southeast Atlantic]], [[Western Indian Ocean]], [[Eastern Indian Ocean]], [[Northwest Pacific]], [[Northeast Pacific]], [[Western Central Pacific]], [[Eastern Central Pacific]], [[Southwest Pacific]], [[Southeast Pacific]], and [[Southern Oceans]]) plus inland water regions across all continents. Regional fishery bodies provide scientific advice: [[ICES]] for Northeast Atlantic, [[NAFO]] for Northwest Atlantic, [[ICSEAF]] for Southeast Atlantic, [[CECAF]] for Eastern Central Atlantic, [[GFCM]] for Mediterranean, [[IOFC]] for Indian Ocean, [[ICCAT]] for Atlantic tunas, and others.

The document emphasizes that Maximum Sustainable Yield (MSY) estimates must be viewed with caution as orders of magnitude rather than precise values. For most fully-fished stocks, the average catch over the last 10 years may provide a reasonable MSY estimate with its variance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Environmental Threats to Fishery Resources:**

The review identifies multiple non-fishing factors critically affecting fishery productivity:

- **Chemical Pollution**: Heavy metals (cadmium, mercury), synthetic compounds (PCBs, DDT, pesticides), and radioactive wastes enter food chains or reduce juvenile survival in coastal nursery areas. [[Baltic Sea]] cod liver shows high contamination levels rendering it unfit for consumption.

- **Oil Pollution**: Crude oil and tar contamination clogs nets and taints catches in areas of oil exploitation and heavy naval traffic, though oil is biodegradable.

- **Eutrophication**: Nutrient loading from agricultural runoff, detergents, and sewage can enhance production in limited quantities but causes oxygen depletion, mass mortalities, and toxic phytoplankton blooms (red tides) in enclosed waters like the [[Baltic Sea]], [[Black Sea]], and [[Laurentian Great Lakes]]. Shellfish poisoning requires seasonal closures.

- **Pathogen Contamination**: Domestic sewage discharge contaminates filter-feeding bivalve molluscs with pathogenic organisms, particularly dangerous when consumed raw.

- **Siltation and Habitat Loss**: Terrestrial runoff from poor watershed management, coastal dredging, and land reclamation harm commercially important species like oysters and destroy coral reefs. River damming for irrigation reduces marine production and alters species composition. Mangrove deforestation eliminates critical nursery habitat.

- **Marine Debris**: Non-biodegradable plastic materials (nets, sheeting, strapping bands, litter) kill or disable resources through entanglement or ingestion. Evidence shows this problem is increasing but overall impact remains difficult to assess.

**Regional Environmental Issues:**

The [[Mediterranean Sea]] shows increased pelagic and demersal production over recent decades, possibly related to eutrophication documented in the [[Adriatic Sea]] and other coastal areas. The [[Black Sea]] has experienced significant decline in species diversity, with biomass now dominated by [[Anchovy]] and [[Sprat]], likely related to eutrophication from various inflow sources.

Climate variability significantly affects stock productivity. The 1982-83 [[El Niño]] severely impacted the [[Southeast Pacific]], reducing catches from over 10 million tons to 6.3 million tons in 1983. A milder El Niño in 1987 again reduced catches. Long-term climatic fluctuations (warm/saline versus cold/diluted periods typically lasting 3-5 years) influence recruitment patterns, particularly for species near range extremes like [[Northern Cod]] stocks.

International conventions address marine pollution: the [[London Dumping Convention]], [[Paris Convention]], [[MARPOL Convention]], [[Helsinki Convention]] for Baltic Sea, and UNEP Regional Seas Programme conventions for Mediterranean, Kuwait, West/Central Africa, Southeast Pacific, Red Sea/Gulf of Aden, and Wider Caribbean.

## Key Findings

**Global Catch Trends:**

- Total 1987 landings: 90.9 million tons (marine and freshwater), down from 92.1 million tons in 1986
- Marine landings increased at over 3% annually since 1980
- 98.8% of 1980s marine increase came from [[Pacific Ocean]], with 81.8% from [[Northwest Pacific]] (Area 61) and [[Southeast Pacific]] (Area 87)
- Four species account for 70% of 1980s increase: [[Peruvian Anchovy]], [[South American Sardine]], [[Japanese Sardine]], and [[Alaska Pollock]]
- Distant-water fleet catches declined from 12% of total (1978) to 8.7% (1986) following 200-mile EEZ establishment

**Regional Stock Status:**

**Northwest Atlantic (Area 21):** 3.0 million tons (1987). [[Atlantic Cod]] stocks show mixed conditions: potential rebuilding in Subarea 1, continued low biomass in Subareas 2-3, relatively stable in Subarea 4. [[Haddock]] stocks fragile with heavy dependence on few age groups. [[Georges Bank]] cod at second-lowest catch since 1977 despite 24% effort increase; spawning stock near historic low. [[Yellowtail Flounder]] at record-low abundance. [[Atlantic Herring]] stocks recovering from 1970s collapse; [[North Sea]] stock increasing due to very strong 1985 year class. [[Gulf Menhaden]] landings 907,000 tons (1987), down from 983,000 tons (1984); biologists predict unsustainable harvest levels.

**Northeast Atlantic (Area 27):** 10.1 million tons (1987). Most demersal stocks show downward trends from excessive fishing, undesirable exploitation patterns, and poor recruitment. [[North Sea Cod]] and [[Haddock]] at record lows with high fishing mortality and poor recent recruitment. Heavy discarding of haddock and [[Whiting]]. [[North-East Arctic Cod]] and [[Haddock]] near historic lows despite strong 1985 year class; reduced growth rates coincided with [[Barents Sea]] [[Capelin]] collapse (spawning stock dropped from 1.8 million tons in 1981 to 16,000 tons in 1987). [[Pacific Ocean Perch]] depleted by 1960s fishing, remained at low abundance through 1980s. [[Norwegian Spring-Spawning Herring]] recovering from virtual collapse (zero in 1972 to 1.3 million tons spawning biomass in 1988) due to strong 1983 year class. [[North Sea Herring]] stock increasing to 1.7 million tons expected spawning biomass (1989). [[Mackerel]] stocks declining since 1973-74; [[North Sea]] spawning stock extremely low (37,000 tons in 1988 versus 1.2 million tons in 1973); western spawning stock dropped from 3.7 million tons (1974) to 1.5 million tons (1988). Agreed TACs consistently exceed ICES recommendations; actual catches exceed agreed TACs; enforcement inadequate.

**Western Central Atlantic (Area 31):** 2.0-2.2 million tons. [[Gulf Menhaden]] continues as major species by weight. Most demersal resources on island shelves fully or overexploited; figures underestimate true catch due to unreported sport fishing and shrimp trawler discards. Greatest expansion potential from smaller pelagics, large oceanic pelagics, and squid. [[Queen Conch]] and [[Spiny Lobster]] heavily exploited throughout Caribbean. Shrimp landings stable at 230,000-280,000 tons over past decade; management improving in Mexico, USA, Venezuela, Guyana using economic and biological criteria.

**Eastern Central Atlantic (Area 34):** 3.0 million tons (1986), recovering from 2.9 million tons (1985) after peak of 3.8 million tons (1977) and subsequent [[Horse Mackerel]] crash. Non-African long-range fleet share declined from 68% (1977) to 45% (1986). Northern sector: most demersal stocks fully exploited or overexploited, including cephalopods, [[Hake]], [[Seabream]]. Fishing effort reduction of 21-65% needed for cephalopods MSY, 25% for [[European Hake]]. [[Sardine]] stocks returned to historical levels with improved coastal fleet accessibility. [[Mauritania]]-[[Senegal]] sector: all traditionally exploited stocks at least fully fished; demersal and pelagic abundance seriously reduced since early 1970s. Southern sector: most species in depths <50m fished at maximum exploitation; 50-200m stocks still below full exploitation but economically unattractive. [[Pink Shrimp]] (Penaeus notialis) exploited at maximum biological production. [[Sardinella aurita]] stock ([[Côte d'Ivoire]]-[[Ghana]]-[[Togo]]-[[Benin]]) recovered to record highs mid-1980s after collapse from fishing pressure and climate.

**Mediterranean and Black Sea (Area 37):** 2.0 million tons (1987), record year. Increases slowed 1982-87 (1-6% annually). Western Mediterranean: all stocks very heavily exploited except possibly [[Mackerel]] and [[Horse Mackerel]]. [[Adriatic]]: evidence of overfishing for [[Hake]] and [[Red Mullet]]. Central Mediterranean: demersal resources fully to heavily overexploited; Italian demersal stocks very heavily fished with fish density one-fifth that of Tunisian waters. Despite heavy exploitation, demersal landings increased 3-8% annually (1975-76 to 1985-86) for most species, possibly from higher effort, better statistics, or increased coastal productivity. Small pelagic stocks highly variable; [[Sardine]] stock increased since 1976, [[Anchovy]] declined in [[Gulf of Lions]]. Combined Mediterranean-Black Sea anchovy catches increased ~2% annually, but Mediterranean-only catches showed slight decline. Large pelagic landings increased over last decade (8-10% annually for [[Bonito]] and [[Swordfish]]); local declines in mean swordfish size reported in Eastern Mediterranean indicate need for coordinated management. [[Black Sea]]: significant diversity decline, biomass dominated by anchovy and sprat, possibly related to eutrophication.

**Southwest Atlantic (Area 41):** 2.1 million tons (1987), increasing steadily from 1.5 million tons (1982). [[Argentine Hake]] (Merluccius hubbsi) catches recovered to 381,000 tons (1987) after 1984 drop; stock moderately to fully exploited. [[Red Shrimp]] (Pleoticus muelleri) catches dropped from 22,000 tons (1984) to 7,000 tons (1987) following natural abundance decrease possibly related to environmental changes and/or heavy fishing. [[Squid]] catches increased rapidly from 31,000 tons (1980) to 600,000 tons (1987), primarily [[Short-fin Squid]] (Illex sp.) and [[Common Squid]] (Loligo sp.) in southern Patagonian shelf. Short-fin squid considered fully exploited (1986); common squid probably fully to heavily exploited. [[Southern Blue Whiting]] stable around 100,000 tons annually since 1984; lightly to moderately exploited. Management prospects encouraging: Brazil has 10 interstate working groups; Argentina-Uruguay established Common Fishing Zone and Joint Technical Commission (1974); offshore Patagonian management measures adopted for some fishing grounds though high seas fishing largely unrestricted.

**Southeast Atlantic (Area 47):** 2.6 million tons (1987), up from ~2.1 million tons (1984-86). Distant-water fleets took 59% of 1986 catch. [[Horse Mackerel]] stocks doubled 1970-1980, stabilized since; 1987 landings 633,000 tons. [[Pilchard]] stocks off [[Namibia]] and [[South Africa]] at very low levels (101,000 tons total 1987). [[Anchovy]] stocks showed major 1987 increase: record catches of 376,000 tons off Namibia, 596,000 tons off South Africa—first suggestion of species composition shift from pilchard to anchovy as occurred on North American west coast. [[Hake]] quota off Southern Angola-Namibia: 411,000 tons set, 270,000 tons estimated catch (1987). Management continues primarily through annual catch quotas with national allocations.

**Western Indian Ocean (Area 51):** 2.7 million tons (1987). India accounts for 46% of area catch, Pakistan 13%, Sri Lanka 5%, Iran 5%. Indian landings 1.2 million tons (1986); shrimp stocks heavily exploited with shift toward smaller, less valuable species. Pakistan landings 330,000 tons (1986); shrimp fully exploited or overfished. Sri Lanka landings dropped from 180,000 tons (early 1980s) to 140,000 tons (1986) partly due to political situation. Acoustic surveys in [[Oman]] (1984) indicated 1.2 million tons pelagic biomass (scads, mackerels, sardines) and 345,000 tons demersal biomass; maximum yield estimated 270,000 tons pelagic, 77,000 tons demersal; only 80,000 tons pelagic and 20,000 tons demersal landed (1986). Small pelagics in [[Red Sea]] and [[Gulf of Aden]] generally unexploited. Foreign fleets caught 11% of area landings (1986), primarily tuna and tuna-like species; fishery started 1981.

**Eastern Indian Ocean (Area 57):** 1.9 million tons (1986), record catch; levelled off 1983-87 (1.8-1.9 million tons). Burma accounts for 27% of catch, India 28%, Thailand 16%, Bangladesh 11%, Indonesia 9%. Over 50% of total landings classified only as miscellaneous fish. Indian catch stable at ~500,000 tons annually. Australian prawn fisheries subject to strict controls; western rock lobster landings ~10,000 tons. Trawl surveys in [[Bay of Bengal]] indicated limited deep-water fish and shrimp resources; rough bottom and costs suggest alternative gear needed. Burma shrimp evaluation: heavy exploitation along [[Tenasserim]] coast, no clear overexploitation in [[Rakhine]] area. Assessment and management generally at low level throughout region; many coastal pelagic stocks believed lightly exploited but inadequate information. Cephalopod resource information lacking.

**Northwest Pacific (Area 61):** 25.4 million tons (1987), down from 25.8 million tons (1986) but levelled off 1983-87 (23.8-25.8 million tons). [[Alaska Pollock]] and [[Japanese Pilchard]] dominant, together accounting for 40% of total. Alaska pollock: 5.0 million tons (1987), highest yield for any single demersal species globally; most caught by USSR and Democratic People's Republic of Korea; stocks considered fully exploited despite rising catches. [[Pacific Cod]] catch increased to 200,000 tons in recent years from ~80,000 tons (early 1980s), primarily incidental to pollock and flatfish fisheries. Serious overfishing evident in [[Yellow Sea]] and [[East China Sea]]; demersal stock abundance estimated at one-fifth to one-tenth of previous levels with little recovery sign. [[Salmon]] stocks generally good condition except USSR river chum salmon; intensive USSR restocking should aid recovery. Coastal pelagic stocks very productive but exhibit large fluctuations and dramatic species dominance changes. [[Japanese Sardine]] catch exceeded 5.1 million tons, up from ~9,000 tons (mid-1960s)—one of world's largest single-species catches. [[Pacific Saury]] declined moderately; [[Japanese Jack Mackerel]] and [[Pacific Herring]] seriously declined; [[Japanese Chub Mackerel]] in descending phase. [[Japanese Anchovy]] fishery fairly constant, indicating stability. [[Japanese Flying Squid]] landings declined from >400,000 tons (early 1970s) to 150,000 tons (1987); Japan prohibited fishing during major spawning seasons. No functional multilateral organization covers entire area; five bilateral agreements partially manage shared stocks.
