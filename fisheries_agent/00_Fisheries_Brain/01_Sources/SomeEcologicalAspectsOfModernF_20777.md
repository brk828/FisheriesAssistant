# Some Ecological Aspects of Modern Fresh-Water Stromatolites in Lakes and Streams of the Cuatro Ciénegas Basin, Coahuila, Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyanobacteria #stromatolites #diatoms

## Executive Summary

This 1990 doctoral dissertation by Barbara Madden Winsborough (University of Texas at Austin) describes the ecology of modern freshwater [[Stromatolites]] and related microbial deposits forming in the spring-fed desert [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], Coahuila, Mexico. The study distinguishes four stromatolite types that differ in morphology, community architecture and habitat, and links their formation to light, current, depth, detrital sediment input and lithification rate. Water chemistry is similar across sites and dominated by calcium sulfate, and the precipitated mineral is low-magnesian calcite. Diatoms are shown to play a role in mat formation and diagenesis, and benthic animals, including fish grazers, are found to modify mat fabric rather than prevent stromatolite growth. Fish are mentioned mainly as grazers and sediment disturbers; the document is not a fish-focused study.

## Background & Description

Stromatolites are defined as lithified, often laminated, organo-sedimentary structures built by microbial communities through sediment trapping and binding and/or carbonate precipitation. The term was introduced by [[E. Kalkowsky]] in 1908. The author replaces "blue-green algae and bacteria" with the broader term "microbial communities," which includes associated plants and animals. Stromatolites first appeared about 3,500 million years ago and declined with the evolution of higher plants and metazoans, persisting in restricted settings.

The study has three stated goals: (1) describe water chemistry, habitat, macrostructure, community composition, architecture, fabric and microstructure of the four stromatolite types; (2) identify relationships between associated organisms, lithified structure and biological versus environmental influence on morphogenesis; and (3) describe the role of diatoms in freshwater stromatolite growth. The work was conducted over eleven years of field and laboratory effort, with [[Diatoms]] used as paleoecological indicators because their assemblages respond to environmental change.

The basin is a small intermontane desert valley, about 30 km by 40 km, at roughly 740 m elevation. It is one of only two large desert spring systems in North America with high endemism, the other being [[Ash Meadows]] in Nevada. Springs reach densities of 12–15 per km², most are warm (up to 35°C), and the mean annual air temperature is 23°C with annual rainfall under 200 mm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry.** Spring waters are dominated by Ca²⁺ and SO₄²⁻, with lesser sodium, magnesium and chloride. Sulfate is unusually high relative to alkalinity for water with so little chloride. Salinities range from 0.45 to 1.52 ppt, and the four stromatolite sites are described as oligohaline with total dissolved solids of 2265–2498 mg/l and circumneutral pH. Phosphate appears to be limiting, and the author describes the lakes as oligotrophic. Nitrate at three sites ranged from 5–6 to 6.8 mg/l, and phosphate from 0.002 to 0.01 mg/l.

**Geology.** The basin floor consists of carbonate and evaporitic sediments. Lower Cretaceous formations include gypsum and dolomite units, and the sulfate signature reflects Cretaceous coastal lagoons where calcium sulfate was deposited. Structures include the [[San Marcos Fault]].

**Vegetation.** Aquatic plants include water lotus (*Nymphaea ampla*), pondweed, muskgrass (*Chara*), bladderwort (*Utricularia*) and sedges. The basin has 25 bird species and 66 amphibian and reptile species, 10% of which are endemic.

**Four stromatolite types:**
- **Type 1** (El Mojarral system): laminated stromatolites and oncoids built by *Homoeothrix balearica* and *Schizothrix lacustris*, with horizontal lobate ledges or microterraces.
- **Type 2** (Río Mesquites): bulbous cushions at the ends of digitate branches, built mainly by the green alga *Gongrosira calcifera*, with cyanobacteria and diatoms.
- **Type 3** (Los Pozos Azules, notably Pozo Azul): large, globose, non-laminated structures with a sinuous, brain-like incised surface, built by *Dichothrix bonetiana*, *Schizothrix lacustris* and diatoms.
- **Type 4** (Garabatal system): laminated, domed structures built by alternating *Scytonema cf. mirabile*, *Schizothrix cf. affinis* and diatom layers, responding to a wet-dry seasonal cycle.

**Physical settings.** Sites differ in light, current, depth, detrital input and desiccation. Pozo Azul is about 13 m deep, El Mojarral has a mean depth of 1–5 m, and the Río Mesquites flows at 0.4–0.6 m/s in narrow reaches. Garabatal experiences seasonal drying.

## Key Findings

- **Four stromatolite morphologies** are distinguished. Their best development occurs at the four study sites, though the types recur in other localities in the basin.
- **Water chemistry** is similar across all stromatolite sites; sulfate dominates, and calcite is favored over aragonite because Mg/Ca ratios are about 1:2 at low salinity and temperature.
- **Mineralogy:** all structures are built of low-magnesian calcite (micrite and sparry calcite). Crystals are "gothic arch" and "edge-guttered," likely from lattice interference by Mg²⁺ or SO₄²⁻. Average sparry calcite length at El Mojarral is 34 µm (range 20–50 µm).
- **Vertical zonation:** all mats show a vertically differentiated microbial community, with an oxygenic surface layer above a reduced layer containing purple sulfur bacteria and sulfate reducers.
- **Diatoms** are the most diverse group of stromatophilic microorganisms, with over 100 species identified in the basin. A count of 99 species and varieties was tabulated from 20 sites. Diatoms are first colonizers of fresh surfaces at Pozo Azul and at Garabatal.
- **Diatom preservation and quartz:** where diatoms are well preserved (porous laminated structures), quartz crystals are few; where diatoms are dissolved (dense laminae in cave and oncoid interiors), quartz crystals are abundant. This suggests diatom opal dissolves to feed local quartz growth.
- **Benthos:** a diverse animal community occurs at all sites, including chironomids, amphipods (*Hyalella*), oligochaetes, nematodes, ostracods and endemic hydrobiid snails. The author argues that grazers do not prevent stromatolite growth in this basin, contrary to some earlier hypotheses.
- **Fish grazing** removes mat patches, particularly on the down-current side, and can bevel stromatolites and leave parallel tooth-marked grooves.
- **Growth rates:** a labeled Río Mesquites stromatolite showed an estimated growth rate of 1–3 mm per year. At Garabatal, a 2 cm thick Scytonema colony formed within a few months.
- **Phytoplankton** in Pozo Azul was nearly absent; no measurable productivity was detected in six replicate light-dark bottles, consistent with 10 years of routine sampling.
- **Current** controls morphology: El Mojarral's ridges require steady current, and Río Mesquites Gongrosira dominance reflects shade plus swift flow.
- **Conclusions:** the basin extends known ranges of modern non-marine stromatolites to continental waters rich in calcium sulfate. The author proposes that stromatolite associations are shaped by light, current, depth and desiccation, and that calcification is a competitive and possibly anti-predation strategy.
