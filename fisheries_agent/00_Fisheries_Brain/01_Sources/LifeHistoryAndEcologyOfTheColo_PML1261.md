# Life History and Ecology of the Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 Utah State University Master of Science thesis by Karl Seethaler synthesizes the state of knowledge on the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), once abundant throughout the [[Colorado River System]] and now an endangered species restricted to parts of the upper basin. The study combines a literature-based review of distribution with field work (1974–1976) concentrated in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], the Yampa and Green rivers, and the [[Walter Walker Wildlife Area]] near Grand Junction, Colorado. It covers systematics, larval development, age and growth, reproduction, food habits, movement, parasites, and causes of decline, and closes with management recommendations centred on the [[Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan]].

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** *Ptychocheilus* (Cyprinidae, subfamily Leuciscinae) contains four species: *P. lucius* (Colorado River system), *P. oregonensis* (northern squawfish), *P. grandis* (Sacramento squawfish), and *P. umpquae*. The species was first described by Charles Girard in 1856 from a specimen collected in the Colorado River in California. It is the largest cyprinid in North America.
- **Common names:** Local names included "salmon," "white salmon," "Colorado salmon," "pike," and "Gila trout." The name "squawfish" was applied to *P. lucius* only later (Dill, 1944); locally it was often confused with the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) or whitefish.
- **Morphology:** The body is compressed and fusiform, with small scales, a large mouth, and pharyngeal teeth (four on the main row). Adults are bluish grey dorsally and silvery golden ventrally.
- **Size:** Specimens exceeded 1.5 m (about 5 ft) and 20–30 lb in historical records; the Colorado River catch of 52 lb (23.6 kg) was reported in Vernal lore.
- **Systematics:** Electrophoretic and meristic data showed no intraspecific differences between fish from the Yampa/Green and Grand Junction sites, supporting a single population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical distribution.** The species occurred throughout the Colorado River system, from Wyoming to the Gulf of California. Fossil records include Pliocene material from the [[Bidahochi Formation]] and archaeological remains from Arizona and California sites dating from about 300 B.C. to 1700 A.D. It was abundant in the lower basin into the early twentieth century, with reports of fish being pitchforked from irrigation ditches and "salmon runs" at Phoenix.

**Decline.** The thesis identifies a three-to-four stage decline:
1. Dewatering of the [[Gila River]] system in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
2. Sharp reduction in 1930–35, coinciding with Hoover Dam construction, the 1934 drought, and exotic introductions.
3. Impacts of Glen Canyon and Navajo dams, and especially the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962, which ended a viable population in the upper Green River.

A proposed fourth stage was further dams on the Green River or its tributaries.

**Current status.** Known populations were restricted to the Yampa River (Craig to Echo Park), the Green River (Echo Park to Canyonlands National Park), the Colorado River (Grand Junction to Lake Powell), and some tributaries. Recent natural reproduction was documented only in the upper Green River system, in the Desolation Canyon reach.

**Habitat.** Young-of-the-year use quiet backwaters (depth 0.3–0.6 m, velocity below 0.15 m/s, silt bottom). Juveniles use shallow pools and backwaters (0.3–1.8 m deep). Subadults and adults occupy eddies, runs, and backwaters, typically over sand. Habitat use shifts seasonally with flow. Dams and reduced spring peak flows eliminated the scoured backwaters that young fish require.

**Threats.** Dewatering (Upper Colorado Basin consumptive use projected to reach 6.5 million acre-feet by 2020), dams and reservoirs, altered flow and temperature, water quality degradation (salinity, turbidity, pesticides), and exotic competitors and predators, notably [[Channel Catfish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Redside Shiner]], and [[Common Carp]].

**Parasites and diseases.** The most common parasite is the copepod *Lernaea* sp. Other recorded organisms include *Ichthyophthirius* sp., the tapeworm *Proteocephalus ambloplites*, and the protozoa *Myxosoma* sp. and *Myxobolus* sp.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution:** The species was once found throughout the basin, but the thesis documents sightings from 1825 onward. It is now confined to parts of the upper basin, with only small numbers. It is essentially extinct in Arizona, with no lower-basin records since 1967.
- **Systematics:** Electrophoretic analysis (27 fish: 22 from the Yampa, 5 from the Colorado at Grand Junction) and meristic analysis (37 fish) found no differences, indicating one population.
- **Larval development:** 15 detailed camera-lucida drawings describe the stages from egg through protolarva, mesolarva, metalarva, and juvenile. Eggs measured 1.97–2.08 mm; hatching occurred about 3 days after spawning.
- **Age and growth:** Growth rates were similar between the Yampa–Green and Colorado river fish (1974–76) and the upper Green River (1964–66).
- **Length–weight:** For Colorado River fish, log W = 3.17 log TL − 5.39; for Yampa–Green fish, log W = 3.16 log TL − 5.54. The combined regression (n = 130) had r² = 0.97.
- **Maturity:** Fish matured at about 428–503 mm total length and 6–8 years of age. All 34 fish in the 428–503 mm range were aged by scale method. Of 147 specimens examined, all over 503 mm were mature and all under 428 mm were immature.
- **Sex ratio:** Overall, the Utah State collection held 22 females and 125 males, which the author notes frustrated the hatchery broodstock program.
- **Spawning:** Spawning occurs from early July to mid-August, after temperatures exceed 19 °C for about a month. Hatchery fish spawned at 22–24 °C. Spawning behaviour was inferred from observations and related species, not observed directly.
- **Food habits:** Young fish eat cladocerans, copepods, and chironomid larvae. Fish over 200 mm consumed only fish, mainly redside shiners, and 39% of stomachs from these larger fish were empty.
- **Movement:** Of 70 fish tagged with Floy anchor tags (1974–75), only seven were recaptured. Sonic tracking of several fish provided limited data, and the author concluded that the stress of tagging was not justified by the results.
- **Habitat use in Desolation Canyon:** Juveniles were found between river kilometres 16.5 and 147.9 upstream from the Green River confluence, and reproduction was documented there.
- **Recommendations:** The author recommends preservation as the goal, artificial propagation to "buy time," a public education program, a ten-year development moratorium on the upper Colorado (Kidd, 1977), and strong restrictions on water projects. The Central Utah Project and proposed White River dams are identified as incompatible with recovery.
- **Overall conclusion:** The thesis states that the prospects for survival are "not favorable" and that the preservation of the species and further water development in the Upper Colorado Basin are incompatible.
