# Fisheries and Watershed Management in Arizona: Looking Into the Future

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) technical report (NGTR 169, June 2001, by [[Linda J. Allison]] and [[Dennis M. Kubly]]) proposes a watershed-based strategic framework for managing native fish and sportfish in the [[Little Colorado River Basin]]. The authors argue that native fish recovery and sportfish opportunities are best addressed through habitat restoration, and that the watershed, rather than the individual stream reach or lake, should be the landscape unit of management. The approach is illustrated with the [[Nutrioso Creek Watershed]], selected because it holds part of a population of the threatened [[Little Colorado Spinedace]]. The report accompanies the [[Integrated Fisheries Management Plan]] for the basin (NGTR 146) and draws on reviewer comments to set out key issues and a management structure built on focal, nodal, adjunct and grubstake habitat categories.

## Background & Description

The report is a companion to the Integrated Plan and was revised after internal and external review, with reviewer comments summarized rather than incorporated into a single overarching modification. Its stated premise is that AGFD must first rescue native fishes from ongoing declines in distribution and abundance before sportfisheries can be expanded without creating conflicts.

AGFD manages two groups with different goals:

- **Sportfish** are managed to provide recreational angling experiences. Stocking from hatcheries is routine, and some native species, such as [[Apache Trout]], are managed as sportfish in reservoirs where natural reproduction is rare.
- **Native fish** are managed to maintain self-sustaining, genetically diverse populations. Management must protect habitats for all life stages, maintain dispersal corridors, and guard against founder effects, inbreeding and outbreeding depression, genetic drift and hybridization.

A third group of introduced species (bait fish, aquarium releases and other non-target fish) and crayfish are also considered because of their documented effects on native fish. The Arizona Game and Fish Commission has restricted live baitfish in areas where they threaten federally listed native fish (A.A.C. R12-4-316).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Causes of decline.** Native fish declines were originally driven by habitat loss and degradation and further eroded by conflict with introduced fish, including predation and competition documented in several studies.

**Watershed concepts.** Watershed management in the West has roots in forested regions, where effects of land use on fisheries were first recognized. The report treats watersheds as spatially nested hierarchies in which downstream conditions depend on upstream land and water management. A temporal dimension is added by comparing a system's present condition with its potential capacity, separating natural variability from human-caused disturbance.

**Little Colorado River Basin.** The authors consider the basin sufficiently degraded that watershed restoration is needed to resolve native fish and sportfish conflicts. Recent headwater restoration followed retirement of livestock grazing and riparian fencing.

**Nutrioso Creek watershed.**
- Area of 446 km²; land ownership is 77.2% federal (USFS and BLM), 11.4% AGFD, 3.2% state trust land and 18.2% private.
- [[Nelson Reservoir]] (831 ac-ft capacity, created in 1890) divides the watershed and acts as a barrier between spinedace subpopulations. Since 1985 its outlet has been closed, so water passes only when the reservoir spills, predicted about once every six years.
- Gaging records (1967–1982) show snowmelt peaks in March–May. Outflow matched inflow best during snowmelt (about 70% of inflow) and fell to about 25% during summer monsoons.
- Four stream segments totaling 46 miles are on the 1998 303(d) list for turbidity.
- Five managed sportfish streams total 38.7 km (24.2 mi), featuring rainbow and brown trout. Angler use is unquantified for streams but is suspected to be low.
- Hulsey Lake (3–5 acres) is an intensive-use rainbow trout fishery with estimated use of about 2,430 anglers in 1981 and 11,080 in 1998.
- The [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]] is a species of concern. Recent surveys failed to find it, though suitable habitat is extensive.

## Key Findings

- **Strategic premise:** Native fish recovery and improved sportfishing are best achieved through habitat restoration at the watershed scale. Spatial separation of native and sportfish is only a short-term tactic.
- **Watershed classification:** Management areas are ranked by priority:
  - **Focal watersheds** (highest priority): protected refugia for threatened native fish, with reduced human disturbance and connectivity to sources of colonists.
  - **Nodal habitats:** areas outside focal watersheds that sustain target populations, such as the reach below Nelson Reservoir, which could serve as a drought refuge.
  - **Adjunct habitats:** degraded areas adjacent to focal or nodal areas, prioritized for restoration because of proximity.
  - **Critical contributing areas:** upstream zones that supply water, organic matter and geomorphic structure to downstream habitat, though they may not currently support target species.
  - **Grubstake habitats** (lowest priority): heavily degraded lower-elevation areas with high restoration costs and largely private ownership.
- **Focal watershed selection:** Two focal watersheds were identified in Nutrioso Creek: Rudd Creek (largely AGFD and USFS land) and Nutrioso Creek above Nelson Reservoir (mostly private land, containing one of a handful of isolated spinedace groups).
- **Rudd Creek management:** Remove nonnative fish above the reservoir barrier to secure Little Colorado spinedace, then manage the drainage as a native-focused focal area.
- **Repatriation caution:** Apache trout should not be introduced to spinedace refugia until research clarifies interactions. Rainbow trout at some sizes and densities reduce spinedace through predation and behavior change, and spinedace respond differently to Apache trout.
- **Monitoring:** Success depends on fish population data. Recommended actions include estimating population size across seasons and years, documenting habitat use by life stage, and sampling focal and nodal areas to compare outcomes.
- **Stakeholder cooperation:** Watershed-scale working groups are essential, since many native populations lie in downstream, multi-impacted areas. Examples include the Timber, Fish and Wildlife agreement in Washington and the Henry's Fork Watershed Council in Idaho. ADEQ's Watershed Management Teams were cited as an Arizona model.
- **Key issues raised by reviewers:** Consistent data standards; a management effectiveness evaluation method; clarity on the roles of AGFD and other agencies; criteria for when reintroduction or fish removal is appropriate; and the economics of fisheries decisions.
- **Institutional commitment:** AGFD's *Wildlife 2006* plan calls for watershed-based fisheries management in at least two watersheds by 2006, and the authors propose a Verde River aquatic wildlife technical committee and a Little Colorado River working group to support this.
