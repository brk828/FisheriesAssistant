# Pit Digging, a Behavioral Feeding Adaptation in Pupfishes (Genus Cyprinodon)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1969 study by [[W. L. Minckley]] and Elena Trotzel Arnold (Arizona State University) describes "pit digging," a behavior in which pupfishes of the genus [[Cyprinodon]] excavate circular depressions in soft sediments of shallow water. The authors compare excavation across many populations, with detailed quantitative observations on [[Cyprinodon Atrorus]] in a semi-natural outdoor pool. Pits are primarily linked to feeding and territorial defense rather than spawning, with the exception of possible reproductive roles in [[Cyprinodon Bifasciatus]] and [[Cyprinodon Variegatus]].

## Background & Description

Earlier interpretations of pit building varied. [[Raney et al. 1953]] considered pits made by [[Cyprinodon Variegatus]] to be nests, though spawning in them was rare and possibly fortuitous. [[Barlow 1961]] proposed that crowded populations of [[Cyprinodon Macularius]] restricted male territories, causing them to dig repeatedly in one spot and produce small pits. A related species, [[Jordanella Floridae]], also builds pits not obviously tied to reproduction (Peters, 1941).

Excavation uses three basic motor patterns defined by Barlow (1961):
- **Digging**: excavation with the mouth.
- **Plowing**: pushing sediment with the head and body.
- **In-place-swimming**: violent undulation of the belly and fins against the bottom, often used first to create a small depression that is then enlarged.

Additional patterns described by others include **chafing** (lateral contact with the substrate, [[Baerends and Baerends-Van Roon 1950]]) and **looping** ([[Itzkowitz 1967]]). Pits typically reach 20 cm in diameter and 2 to 5 cm deep under ideal conditions. Pits are often elongated downflow in streams of [[Cyprinodon Rubrofluviatilis]]. Fish smaller than 15 mm standard length rarely excavate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Pit excavation occurs only in soft, flocculent sediments or fine sand in shallow, quiet water. Field observations are limited to early spring through early autumn, matching major activity seasons, while laboratory digging continues year-round. Pits last about 3 to 5 days under continuing activity and are often produced by several individuals in succession. Tracks of small mammals were excluded unless excavation was observed.

Study sites span several drainages:
- **Death Valley system**: Pit digging noted in marshes near Saratoga Springs (up to 10 cm wide) but not in the spring pool; not reported in [[Cyprinodon Nevadensis]] at Zzyzx, [[Cyprinodon Salinus]] (laboratory pits only), or [[Cyprinodon Diabolis]].
- **Lower Colorado River basin**: Three *C. macularius* populations dig 5 to 20 cm pits in aquaria and ponds; one isolate from Monkey Spring was seen digging in the field. Quitobaquito Spring stock is a prolific pit builder in aquaria, although [[Cox 1966]] did not record this in the field.
- **Guzmán Basin, Chihuahua**: A few pits noted at two localities (June 1964 and June 1968).
- **Rio Grande system**: Pits were absent in a *C. eximius* stock from Rio Florido over four years. An isolated population at the mouth of the Devil's River likely went extinct after the 1968 filling of Amistad Reservoir.
- **Pecos River basin**: [[Cyprinodon Elegans]] did not dig; two other forms were vigorous excavators with pits observed in natural habitat.
- **Red River system**: *C. rubrofluviatilis* digs in nature, mostly by in-place-swimming and plowing.
- **Cuatro Ciénegas basin, Coahuila**: The site of the most detailed behavioral work, with extensive excavation in *C. bifasciatus* and hybrids.
- **Atlantic coastal waters**: *C. variegatus* from Solomons, Maryland, excavates regularly in tanks and ponds.

The study pool at Arizona State University (about 25 m²) held a population of *C. atrorus* from Laguna de San Pablo, Cuatro Ciénegas, from March 1964 until April 1967. Its water temperatures exceeded 20 °C during digging periods.

## Key Findings

- **Behavioral scope**: Pit digging is widespread across *Cyprinodon* but varies in intensity and quality among species, sexes, times of day, and populations. Its apparent absence in some species may be spurious because field observation time control was poor.
- **Feeding function**: In most species, pit excavation and defense are simple and food oriented. In *C. atrorus*, nine fish with pit sampling had sand grains, chironomids, and ostracods in their foreguts, while ten fish from a deeper area without pits had mainly algae and sediment (over 80 percent of foregut content).
- **Pit size in the study pool**: Pits ranged from less than 2 cm wide and 0.5 cm deep to maxima of 16 cm wide and 4 cm deep in the field and artificial pond.
- **Territorial defense**: On 9–11 April 1967, twelve active pits occupied about two square meters. A male defended one pit for 38 minutes. Fifteen males averaged 17 minutes (range 3–38) across two pits over 240 minutes of observation. Eleven females averaged nine minutes (range 2–13).
- **Sexual and age differences**: Males involved in excavation were often non-reproductive and remained peripheral to spawning areas. Females defended pits primarily by charging. Active defense was shown only by adults or sub-adults, and young fish moved into pits with impunity.
- **Reproduction**: Only one spawn was realized out of 17 female encounters with males (nine initiated breeding repertoires), and it occurred outside the pit. In *C. bifasciatus*, breeding territories corresponded to pit areas where substrate allowed, but very few spawns occurred near pits.
- **Evolutionary hypothesis**: The authors propose that in *C. bifasciatus*, defense of temporary feeding depressions may have developed into defense of permanent feeding and breeding territories, a possible model for the evolution of territoriality. The authors describe this as speculative, and the data as "adequate only for speculative discussion."
- **Contrast with sticklebacks**: There is no evidence that digging acts as a displacement activity in cyprinodonts, unlike in sticklebacks (Tinbergen, 1951).
- **Future work**: The authors call for refined analysis of quantitative variation between species and studies that specifically address pit building.
