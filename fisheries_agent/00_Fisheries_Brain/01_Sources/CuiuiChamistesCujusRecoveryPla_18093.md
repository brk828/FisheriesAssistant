# Cui-ui (Chasmistes cujus) Recovery Plan, Second Revision

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This second revision of the [[Cui-ui Recovery Plan]] was prepared by the Cui-ui Recovery Team for Region 1 of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service). The plan covers *Chasmistes cujus*, a large lakesucker endemic to [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada, listed as endangered on March 11, 1967. The species persists only in Pyramid Lake and the lower [[Truckee River]], where it depends on spawning runs that are constrained by upstream water storage and diversion, dams, and a delta barrier.

The plan replaces the earlier 1978 original, 1980 update and 1983 revision. It sets a quantified objective based on probabilistic modeling: reclassification to threatened at a 0.85 probability of persistence for 200 years, and delisting at 0.95. Recovery is tied to acquiring about 5,000 acre-feet per year of equivalent water benefits and to habitat, passage, water quality, research, hatchery and protection actions. The cost of implementation and recovery date are stated as uncertain, since they depend on task funding.

## Background & Description

Cui-ui is the largest living member of *Chasmistes*, a robust sucker with a long, broad, deep head and a blackish-brown dorsal surface fading to creamy-white ventrally. Breeding males develop tubercles on the anal and caudal fins. Weights reach up to 3.5 kg. Documented lengths are 695 mm for a female and 662 mm for a male (personal communications, 1991). The species was first described by Cope in 1883, and its taxonomy has been stable.

The genus is resident in three basins. The other members are the [[Shortnose Sucker]] (Klamath Basin), the [[Utah Sucker]] (Utah Lake) and the recently extinct Snake River sucker. Cui-ui is considered the only member of the genus not to have hybridized extensively with syntopic catostomids.

Historically, cui-ui occupied all of [[Lake Lahontan]] during the Pleistocene. Fossils are confined to the Lahontan Basin. At the start of the twentieth century the species used both Pyramid and [[Winnemucca Lake]], and it spawned as far as about 25 miles upstream. It was extirpated from Winnemucca Lake when that lake dried in the 1930s, following unrestricted diversion from the Truckee River.

Life history is summarized as follows:
- **Obligate river spawner.** Adults migrate into the lower Truckee River in April to June.
- **Lake rearing.** Adults and juveniles live in Pyramid Lake year-round, mostly in littoral waters at 60 to 100 feet.
- **Fecundity.** 25,000 to 186,000 eggs for females of 430 to 657 mm.
- **Longevity.** Individuals of 40+ years are documented. Females generally live longer than males, and they grow faster after maturity at 6 to 12 years.
- **Diet.** Larvae eat zooplankton and chironomid larvae. Adults eat mainly zooplankton (cladocerans and copepods). Juveniles also eat algae and ostracods.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pyramid Lake** is the terminus of the Truckee River. It is saline (>4.1 ppt), alkaline (pH 9.1 to 9.3) and oligotrophic to mesotrophic. It is monomictic, usually stratified from about May until December, with a thermocline at 35 to 85 feet. Elevation was 3803.5 feet in November 1990. Average annual evaporative loss is 4 feet.

**Lower Truckee River** is a low-gradient, braided, unstable channel descending about 1.5 m/km. It has been straightened and lowered, and it has built a broad delta at the mouth. [[Marble Bluff Dam]] acts as a hydraulic control and creates spawning habitat upstream. Discharge depends on upstream storage and diversions at Derby Dam. Water quality at Nixon (1968 to 1989) shows a temperature range of 32.9 to 85.2 °F, dissolved oxygen of 6.0 to 15.5 mg/l, and total dissolved phosphate of 0.002 to 0.45 mg/l. Point and nonpoint pollution from municipal, agricultural and industrial sources creates high nutrient loading.

**Passage** depends on lake elevation. The [[Pyramid Lake Fishway]] works above 3,784 feet, and delta passage is generally possible above 3,812 feet. The fishway is described as less effective than anticipated, and it has caused stress and mortality.

**Spawning** occurs in April to May in gravel, at depths of 0.8 to 4.0 feet and velocities of 1 to 2 ft/s. Eggs hatch in 1 to 2 weeks. The optimum range is 58 to 63 °F, and survival drops sharply above 63 °F. Spawning habitat between Marble Bluff and Numana dams is estimated at 109,000 ft² at 2,500 cfs and 202,000 ft² at 1,000 cfs.

**Predators and competitors** include [[Lahontan Redside Shiner]] (eggs and emergent larvae), [[Tui Chub]] and [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] (larvae and yearlings in the lake), and white pelicans and cormorants (adults in the river).

**Water management** is central. The [[Newlands Project]] diverted about 250,000 acre-feet per year (roughly 50% of average runoff) from 1918 to 1970. The [[Stampede Reservoir]] was built on the Little Truckee River in 1970 and holds 226,000 acre-feet of storage. Its water is dedicated to Pyramid Lake fishes. The 1990 [[Truckee-Carson-Pyramid Lake Settlement Act]] (P.L. 101-618) and the 1988 OCAP both affect allocations.

## Key Findings

**Status and causes of decline**
- The primary cause of listing was reduced Truckee River inflow from upstream storage and diversions. Channelization, grazing and timber harvest added to habitat loss.
- Catch fell sharply from 1956 to 1968. Only three year classes (1942, 1946 and 1950) were produced before 1966. In 1983 a single year class (1969) made up 97% of the spawning run.
- Spawning runs averaged 12,470 fish in the 1980s (range 5,000 to 36,300). Insufficient water from 1988 to 1991 prevented spawning runs.

**Population estimates**
- Prespawning aggregations were 90,000 to 200,000 adults (1982 to 1986 tagging).
- Preliminary estimates indicate about 300,000 adults (age 9+) and one to several million juveniles.

**Recovery objectives and water needs**
- Reclassification to threatened: population probability 0.85 of persisting 200 years. Delisting: 0.95.
- Acquiring 70,000 acre-feet per year would permit delisting consideration immediately. Acquiring 40,000 acre-feet per year would permit reclassification consideration.
- Acquisition of at least 5,000 acre-feet per year (or equivalent benefit) is treated as the minimum for progress. Preliminary examination suggests more than 100,000 acre-feet equivalent may be available.
- Recovery from water purchase alone at 3,000 acre-feet per year is unlikely because 120,000 acre-feet would be required.

**Recovery strategy (five elements)**
1. Secure and maintain habitat: purchase water rights, reduce Newlands diversions, operate upstream reservoirs, rehabilitate the river channel and riparian canopy, improve water quality, and improve fish passage.
2. Research: population dynamics, survival, genetics and lake habitat.
3. Operate the [[David Koch Cui-ui Hatchery]] as a refuge, with a rotating brood stock.
4. Translocation: rejected. Service policy forbids moving the species outside its historic range, and the Truckee-Pyramid Lake system is the only suitable habitat.
5. Update the recovery plan and computer models as conditions change.

**Genetics**
- A 1988 concern about hybridization with Tahoe suckers was resolved by allozyme analysis: all 1988 putative hybrids were genetically identical to older cui-ui. Brussard et al. (1990) confirmed no hybridization and very low heterozygosity.
- A possible genetic bottleneck is linked to the loss of the 1950 year class, which is predicted to hold the highest variability. A study is recommended immediately.

**Hatchery history**
- Propagation began in 1972 at Hardscrabble Creek. Operation was transferred to the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe in 1977.
- From 1972 to 1990 millions of larvae and several thousand juveniles were stocked. Their contribution to the adult population is unknown. The program was redirected toward extended rearing.

**Implementation schedule**
- The schedule lists tasks by priority (1 to 3), lead agency, duration and cost, in millions of dollars. Tasks include water rights purchase (about $2 million per year), the upper reservoir operating agreement, Newlands efficiency improvements, delta passage, channel rehabilitation, water quality studies and population monitoring.
- Totals and some costs are unknown. The schedule excludes costs of P.L. 101-618 actions.
- Funding for the 8-year population dynamics study was not assured beyond FY 1992.

**Related studies**
- The University of California, Davis Limnological Research Group nutrient loading study (completion 1993) is intended to predict hypolimnetic dissolved oxygen.
- The DSSAM water quality model for the Truckee River is being developed by Washoe County, the Nevada Division of Environmental Protection and the EPA.
