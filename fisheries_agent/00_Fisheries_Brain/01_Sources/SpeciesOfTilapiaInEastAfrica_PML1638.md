# Tilapias of East Africa: Identification, Breeding and Pond Culture

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This note combines two articles from the [[East African Agricultural Journal]]: a 1955 identification key to the common [[Tilapia]] species of East Africa, and a 1952 article, "The Tilapias of Kenya Colony", on their breeding, feeding, growth and value for [[Farm Dam]] fish culture. The authors argue that identification is difficult on morphology alone, that species differ in breeding strategy and diet, and that uncontrolled breeding in small ponds causes [[Stunting]]. The 1952 article also reports on the fishery of [[Lake Victoria]] and proposes stocking strategies for farm ponds. The 1955 key covers about 10 common species, plus species that have been introduced or described from the region.

## Background & Description

The 1955 article addresses the difficulty of separating East African tilapias. Species look alike, and their differences are mainly in counts and proportions. The key therefore uses:

- **Gill rakers** on the lower part of the anterior gill arch (for example, 8–12 versus 14 or more) (see [[Gill Rakers]]).
- **Scale counts**, including lateral line scales, scales around the caudal peduncle, and scales on the cheek.
- **Fin spines and rays**, such as the number of anal fin spines (three or four) and soft rays.
- **Pharyngeal teeth** of the lower pharyngeal bone, the most useful diagnostic character, which requires killing the fish (see [[Pharyngeal Teeth]]).
- **Colour patterns**, which the authors describe as subjective but useful, especially in live fish. These include the "Tilapia mark" (a black blotch at the rear of the dorsal fin), the opercular spot, and breeding colours.

Standard body length (snout tip to base of the tail fin, excluding the tail) is used for proportions. Sexes are told apart by the genital papilla. In males it is conical with one opening. In females it is broader, with the oviduct and urethra as separate openings.

The 1952 article is more general. It discusses the value of tilapias as a food fish, their introduction to dams, and their growth. It also mentions the introduction of gill nets to [[Lake Victoria]] by a Norwegian named Aarup in 1903. The author of the 1952 article is not legible in the OCR text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and introductions.** Several species are native to particular lakes and rivers and have been moved widely:

- [[Tilapia Melanopleura]] is native to Lake Tanganyika in East Africa and was introduced to Kenyan and Tanganyikan dams. In 1951 some escaped into Kenyan waters of Lake Victoria.
- [[Tilapia Zillii]] came from Lake Albert and was stocked in Teso dams in Uganda in April 1951. It may have escaped from the Kijansi ponds into Lake Victoria.
- [[Tilapia Nilotica]] is the basis of the large [[Lake George]] fishery. It has been introduced to many Ugandan lakes, including [[Lake Nakivali]].
- [[Tilapia Esculenta]] and [[Tilapia Variabilis]] occur naturally only in Lakes Victoria and Kyoga. Both are now widespread in dams in Uganda, Kenya and Tanganyika.
- [[Tilapia Mossambica]] is native to the eastward-flowing rivers. It forms the basis of extensive culture in the Far East and has been moved to the West Indies and elsewhere.
- [[Tilapia Macrochir]] is important commercially in Lakes Bangweulu and Mweru.

**Habitat and predators.** The authors contrast [[Lake Rudolf]] and [[Lake Baringo]]. Lake Rudolf has predators ([[Nile Perch]] and [[Tiger Fish]]) and Tilapia there reach about 12 lb. Lake Baringo has no predators, abundant food (blue-green algae, *Microcystis*) and a pH of 8.2–8.5. Its Tilapia average 9 oz. The authors attribute this to over-reproduction.

**Food and feeding.** Most tilapias feed on phytoplankton, but some species differ:

- *T. nigra* lives on molluscs, underwater insects and fish fry in the [[Athi River]] and [[Tana River]], where phytoplankton is scarce.
- *T. melanopleura* and *T. zillii* eat aquatic weeds and help control vegetation in dams. *T. melanopleura* eats weeds only after reaching 5–6 inches.
- *T. leucosticta* and *T. galilaea* feed on algae. The fine pharyngeal teeth of *T. galilaea* suggest it is a phytoplankton feeder, and it apparently did not survive in the Teso dams.

**Breeding.** Tilapias are either mouth brooders or nest makers. Mouth brooding is the main strategy. The female carries eggs and young, and the male is more brightly coloured. Two species, *T. melanopleura* and *T. zillii*, guard eggs in a nest and do not brood in the mouth. Nests are groups of 5–10 small holes on pond banks (*T. nilotica* and *T. zillii*). Egg colour is useful for identification: *T. zillii* has green eggs, and *T. galilaea* also has green eggs. Females may spawn every few weeks, or continuously in the tropics, but spawning is less frequent in cold or dry seasons.

**Environment and disease.** Clearing weeds from dams reduces [[Malaria]] (weeds create wave action that deters mosquito larvae) and [[Bilharzia]] (weed clearance reduces snail habitat). [[Gambusia]] can be introduced into shallow fringing edges to control mosquito larvae. Removing weeds also increases sunlight and phytoplankton, which benefits *T. nigra*.

## Key Findings

**Identification**
- Morphology alone rarely identifies species. The key combines gill raker counts, scale counts, fin counts, pharyngeal teeth and colour patterns.
- Tilapias from the same size range should be compared when identifying fish from mixed samples.
- The 1955 key separates about 10 common species and is based on gill rakers, scales, and fin spines and rays.

**Breeding and sexing**
- Most species are mouth brooders. *T. melanopleura* and *T. zillii* are the exceptions and guard eggs in nests.
- Males can be separated from females at about 5–7 cm (the basis of [[Mono-Sex Culture]]) in some species. In *T. nigra*, males grow faster than females even when young. In many East African species, the sexes grow at the same rate.
- Breeding at small sizes is the main obstacle to pond culture. Females of *T. zillii* bred at 7 cm in overcrowded ponds, and *T. leucosticta* bred at 7 cm in Kisumu fishponds.

**Growth and stunting**
- Overproduction creates runts. The authors propose several remedies: introducing a predator (which may eat out the Tilapia in small dams), stocking one sex only, and sterile hybrids. These remain untested.
- The authors found that early spawning cuts growth by about one-third (the growth rate fell to one-third of the previous rate). The cause is unknown, and temperature and pH were ruled out.
- A stocked 4-acre dam with *T. nigra* from the Athi River gave fish averaging about 2 oz after two years (as stated in the OCR text).

**Economic value**
- Lake Victoria: in 1951, about 2,400,000 Tilapia (approximately 3,000,000 lb) were exported from Kisumu. Value to fishermen was 182,500, and retail value 127,500 (currency units not clearly stated in the OCR text).
- Roughly 13,000 tons of Tilapia were caught in Lake Victoria each year, with about 9,000 gill nets set nightly in the Kavirondo Gulf.
- Farm ponds could yield 400–500 lb of economically sized fish per acre per year. The authors considered 1,000 lb per acre unrealistic.
- *T. macrochir* introduced to Kenya in 1930s–1950s gave disappointing results, and work on it was abandoned. A *T. Macrochir*–black bass combination was tried and showed promise in some areas.

**Recommendations**
- New dams should be stocked only with species already present in the drainage area. Exceptions may be made for the weed-eating *T. zillii* and *T. melanopleura*.
- Records of the origin of all Tilapia used for stocking should be kept.
- A future dam might combine *T. melanopleura* (weeds), *T. nigra* (phytoplankton) and [[Gambusia]] (mosquito larvae). A predator such as [[Clarias]] or black bass may be needed.
