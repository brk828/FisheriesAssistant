# *Gambusia heterochir*: A New Poeciliid Fish from Texas, with an Account of Its Hybridization with *G. affinis*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This paper describes *Gambusia heterochir*, a new species of mosquitofish (family [[Poeciliidae]]) known only from the headsprings of [[Clear Creek]] in [[Menard County]], Texas. The species is reported to be separated by some 800 miles from the ranges of its nearest relatives. The study also documents a natural hybrid swarm between *G. heterochir* and [[Gambusia Affinis]], which the author describes as the first documented natural hybridization among gambusiine fishes. Analysis of four collections indicates that introgression into *G. heterochir* is negligible and that introgression is not evident in nearby *G. affinis* populations. The author attributes the restricted range of *G. heterochir* to competitive exclusion by *G. affinis*.

## Background & Description

The paper opens by noting that a number of Texas freshwater fishes have been described in recent years, some long known but previously undescribed, and others only recently recognized. The new *Gambusia* is described from a restricted headwater system, and the author compares its morphology with the subgenera and species groups established by [[Carl L. Hubbs]] (1926, 1929).

**Type material and collections:** The holotype is a 24.5 mm specimen (University of Michigan Museum of Zoology No. 170936), accompanied by 125 other specimens measuring 17 to 45 mm. All were seined from the headspring of Clear Creek, 10.4 miles west of Menard, on two occasions: February 22, 1953, and February 20, 1956. Material is deposited across several institutions, including the Stanford University, United States National Museum, Museum of Comparative Zoology (Harvard), Chicago Natural History Museum, and Texas Natural History Collection.

**Diagnosis:** *G. heterochir* is a stocky species. It is distinguished from all known gambusiines by a deep indentation on the upper margin of the male pectoral fin. It also has the largest gonopodial elbow and the bluntest gonopodial tip among known gambusiines. The name *heterochir* derives from the Greek *heteros* (different) and *cheir* (hand), referring to the distinctive pectoral fin shape.

**Meristics and morphometrics:**
- Dorsal fin rays 7 or 8 (rarely 9); branched caudal rays 14 (rarely 13 or 15); anal rays 9 (rarely 10); pelvic rays 6 (rarely 5); pectoral rays 13 to 15.
- Lateral line scales 30 or 31; caudal peduncle scales 16; predorsal scale rows 15 or 16.
- Head length enters standard length 3.7–3.8 times in adult males and 3.3–3.7 times in adult females.
- Descriptions were based on a minimum of ten adult males and ten adult females; up to 86 specimens were examined for hybridization characters.

**Gonopodium and pectoral fin:** The male gonopodium has an anterior branch of ray four that reaches almost to the tip, and a two- to four-segmented elbow longer than the longest modified spine of ray three. Ray three has short distal spines, a feature shared with the *G. nicaraguensis* species group. Both male pectoral fins are modified, with a flattened region on rays two through five; the upper margin has a deep incision near the tip, which the author suspects steadies the gonopodium during intromission.

**Coloration:** Males and females differ in color pattern. *G. heterochir* lacks a middorsal stripe and a diffuse lateral band. Females have large black anal crescents that do not meet across the midventral line; these are absent in young and in adult males.

**Relationships:** The author places *G. heterochir* with the *G. nicaraguensis* group on gonopodial characters and fin-related features, but notes that its female anal markings resemble the *G. nobilis* group. He proposes that *G. heterochir* evolved from the ancestral stock of *G. nicaraguensis* shortly after that stock separated from the ancestral stock of *G. nobilis*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range:** *G. heterochir* is known only from the headsprings of Clear Creek and from one locality near the shore 350 yards east of the headspring. Extensive collections in San Saba River headwaters in Menard and Schleicher counties, including habitats similar to the Clear Creek headsprings, did not yield the species. The author believes the species once had a wider distribution in Central Texas and that its range has contracted because it cannot compete with *G. affinis*. The author also suggests that *G. nobilis* group members have restricted ranges for similar reasons, citing unpublished work by Clark Hubbs and Springer.

**Habitat:** The headsprings of Clear Creek are now impounded by three dams; the middle dam holds most of the water. Banks are covered with cattails except in collection areas. Submerged and emergent vegetation is prolific, including dense *Ceratophyllum* sp. beds (with *Ludwigia* sp.) in the headspring and at the 350-yard-east site, along with *Jussiaea*, *Distichlis*, *Typha latifolia*, and *Hydrocotyle umbellata*. *Myriophyllum*, *Potamogeton*, *Chara*, and *Conium maculatum* were present elsewhere, but no *G. heterochir* were collected in those areas. The author suspects that *G. heterochir* populations are correlated with factors associated with *Ceratophyllum* beds.

**Temperature:** Daytime surface water temperatures ranged from 18.3 to 20.6 °C in February and from 27.0 to 31.0 °C in July. Temperatures near 21 °C occurred at the bottom (6 inches below the surface) in both seasons, but the author judged this to have little effect because the fish stayed near the surface unless disturbed. Night temperatures were presumed to be lower.

**Hybrid swarm location:** The hybrid swarm occurs in the Clear Creek headsprings, and the lower Clear Creek collection lies 2.4 miles downstream. The Middle Valley Prong collection is 1 mile west of Ft. McKavett, 17 stream miles from the swarm.

## Key Findings

1. **New species described:** *G. heterochir* is distinguished by the deep pectoral fin indentation in males, the largest gonopodial elbow among known gambusiines, and the bluntest gonopodial tip.

2. **Restricted range:** The species is known from a single headspring system and one nearby locality, separated by some 800 miles from its nearest relatives. The author attributes the restriction to competition with *G. affinis*.

3. **Natural hybrid swarm:** Numerous hybrids between *G. heterochir* and *G. affinis* occur in the Clear Creek headsprings. The hybrids grade into *G. affinis* and may grade into *G. heterochir*, suggesting F₁ and/or backcrosses and gene flow between the species. The author describes this as the third documented example of natural hybridization in the genus, and the first among gambusiines.

4. **Hybrid index analysis:** Scatter plots of morphological indices (for males, five gonopodial characters; for females, four color characters) show two main clusters, one for *G. heterochir* (upper right) and one for *G. affinis* (lower left), with intermediate plots interpreted as hybrids. Males from the 1953 collection (six individuals) and the 1956 collection were compared; the author considers the apparent grouping in the 1953 sample to be more likely due to chance. The female hybrid pattern did not differ significantly between 1953 and 1956.

5. **Limited introgression:** Backcrosses with *G. heterochir* appear to be scarce. The author suspects a sterility mechanism inhibits such crosses, since other isolating mechanisms did not prevent crosses between the more distinct parental forms. The constitution of the hybrid swarm did not change noticeably between February 1953 and February 1956.

6. **No introgression downstream:** No hybrids were noted among the 17 plotted individuals from lower Clear Creek or among the unplotted immature individuals there. The author concludes that the downstream *G. affinis* populations show no indication of introgression, though morphological evidence indicates potential gene flow into them.

7. **Abstract conclusion:** Introgression with *G. heterochir* appears negligible, and introgression is not found in nearby *G. affinis* populations.

8. **Future research:** The author notes that the hybrid swarm may be valuable for studying the selective advantages of hybrids, and that if hybrids are at a selective advantage, *G. heterochir* influence should become noticeable in downstream collections over time. The paper also cites related work by Clark Hubbs and Springer on the *G. nobilis* species group, and by Hubbs and Strawn on darter hybrids.
