# Survey of Fishes in the Unlined Reach of the Coachella Canal, Imperial County, California (April 1-5, 2002)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This survey assessed the fish community of the unlined (earthen) reach of the [[Coachella Canal]] before the [[Coachella Valley Water District]] (CVWD) proposed concrete-lining the remaining 53.4 km (33.2 mi). The main goal was to determine whether the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was present, since its presence would change [[Canal Lining Project]] mitigation requirements. Sampling used [[Electrofishing]] at night and [[Hoop Net]] sets over April 1-5, 2002.

No razorback sucker was captured. Electrofishing yielded six fish species, dominated by [[Channel Catfish]], [[Common Carp]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]]. Hoop nets (255 net-hours) caught no fish, only a single [[Spiny Softshell Turtle]]. The authors concluded that the canal provides little habitat for razorback sucker maintenance or recovery, that predatory non-native fishes pose the greatest threat to native species, and that the lining project would likely reduce habitat for native turtles. They also noted that the canal breach upstream of the study area on April 2 may have affected catch results.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The CVWD planned to concrete-line the remaining earthen reach of the Coachella Canal, which runs from siphon 7 near Camp Dunlap ("Slab City") to siphon 32, about 3.2 km (2.0 mi) north of the [[Salton Sea]] State Park. The reach between siphons 14 and 15 was already lined and excluded from sampling. Razorback sucker is known from the [[Colorado River]] near the canal's point of diversion and has been found in distributary habitats (St. Amant et al. 1974). A positive capture would have affected mitigation plans. A negative capture would not prove absence but would still inform assessment of possible adverse effects from lining.

**Physical setting.**
- The unlined canal is incised from several to more than 10 m (values partly illegible in scan; original text describes depths from several to more than one-tenth of the reach) deep, with slopes averaging about the same ratio as access roads.
- Earthen banks were nearly vertical from sloughing and averaged about 1 m in height.
- Substrates ranged from fine-grained material to angular cobble or boulder, but clays predominated except immediately below siphons.
- Current velocities were uniformly swift (approaching 1 m/s), slowing or eddying only near rare shoreline obstructions.
- Depths were under 1 m below swift-flowing siphons, and presumably more than 2 m in a few deeper inter-siphon reaches that were not sampled.

**Field team.** The study was conducted by [[Paul C. Marsh]] of Arizona State University and [[Robert W. Clarkson]] of the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. The report was issued as a technical report in May 2002.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and water quality (April 1-5).**
- Flow at the Parshall flume at siphon 7 (head of the unlined reach) was 400-500 cubic feet per second (cfs) on April 1.
- A canal breach upstream caused flow to rise abruptly on April 2 (value partly illegible in scan, reported near 900 cfs).
- Discharge then fell to about 350 cfs over the next 10 hours and held near 400 cfs through April 5.
- Stage marks suggested that check gates downstream were opened, lowering water levels 1.0-1.5 ft below normal after April 2.
- At 2000 hrs, conductivity was 1050 µS/cm and water temperature 22 °C.

**Habitat and fish community.** The authors described the habitat as monotonous, with swift currents and little instream structure, which they considered unfavorable for native fishes. Clarity declined downstream, reducing electrofishing efficiency there.

**Species of concern and non-native fishes.** Razorback sucker has never been recorded in this reach during this survey. The authors noted that catostomids (suckers) are susceptible to capture by electrofishing, so their absence is more meaningful than it would be for other taxa. Non-native predators were identified as the most serious threat to native fishes.

## Key Findings

**1. Sampling design.**
- Four paired inter-siphon reaches were chosen at random from 24 available. In each pair, one reach was electrofished and the other was hoop-netted.
- Electrofishing reaches: 16-17, 20-21, 25-26, and 30-31 (reaches 1-4).
- Hoop-net reaches: 15-16, 19-20, 24-25, and 29-30 (reaches 1-4).
- Other gear, including seines and mid-channel nets, was not practicable because of swift currents and steep banks.

**2. Electrofishing effort and catch.**
- Total electrofishing effort was 5,357 seconds across reaches 1-4 (1,017, 2,068, 1,220, and 1,052 seconds).
- Six species were captured, plus one soft-shelled turtle in reach 2 or 3 (the report text is inconsistent).
- Average relative abundance:
  - Channel Catfish: 31-55%
  - Common Carp: about 21%
  - Smallmouth Bass: about 20%
  - Largemouth Bass: about 6%
  - Flathead Catfish: about 3%
  - [[Grass Carp]]: about 2%
- Catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) was highest in reach 1 (2.83 fish/min), where water was clear. Values for reaches 2-4 were lower (partly illegible in scan, roughly 0.5-1.2 fish/min). CPUE declined downstream with reduced clarity.

**3. Hoop-net results.**
- 255 net-hours of effort were expended over 3 nights (87, 84, and 84 hours by reach; none in reach 4).
- No fish were captured. A single [[Spiny Softshell Turtle]] (*Trionyx spiniferus*) was taken in reach 3.
- Hoop netting in reach 4 was dropped because of these results.
- Nets were 3 ft in diameter, 0.5 in mesh, about 8 ft long, single-throat, and baited with canned cat food.

**4. Gear and electrical settings.**
- Boat: 14-ft, launched by CVWD crane, with an 18 hp outboard and a [[Smith-Root GPP 5.0]] electrofisher.
- Pulsed DC output: 0-500 V (exact voltage could not be determined), 60 pulses/s, and 10-18 amps measured output.
- Electrofishing began after nightfall and covered all habitat types (below riffles, along both banks, and mid-channel).

**5. Interpretation and conclusions.**
- Although few fish were captured, the consistent species composition across reaches suggests that the sample represents the common fish fauna present.
- The authors suspected that the April 2 breach and flow changes reduced both clarity and capture efficiency, and may have flushed a portion of the fish population downstream.
- Razorback sucker absence was not confirmed. The authors judged that the canal is unlikely to be a regular habitat for this species. Occasional entrainment from the Colorado River system is possible, but the canal does not provide habitat suitable for maintenance or recovery.
- Predatory non-native fishes were judged the greatest threat to native fishes in the canal.
- Lining the canal would remove the swift-water, earthen-bank habitat used by [[Soft-Shelled Turtle]]s, which need earthen banks for reproduction. Loss of these banks could cause their local disappearance.
- Fish distribution downstream may have shifted following flow changes, and the authors recommended further monitoring to determine whether abundance changed.
