# Systematics, Status, and Life History of the Ashy Darter, *Etheostoma cinereum*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 study by [[Thomas E. Shepard]] and [[Brooks M. Burr]] redescribes the [[Ashy Darter]] (*Etheostoma cinereum*), a large, poorly known endemic of the [[Cumberland River]] and [[Tennessee River]] drainages. The species was historically recorded from 15 tributary systems but has been regularly taken in only four in recent years. The authors recognize no subspecies, but identify three somewhat distinct regional populations (Cumberland, Duck, and upper Tennessee). They also report on habitat, age and growth, reproduction, diet, and parasites, and review its conservation status, which is driven by habitat loss from impoundment, siltation, coal mining, and pollution.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** Described by [[D. H. Storer]] in 1845 from specimens collected near Florence, Alabama, by Charles A. Hentz. No type specimens survive; the description apparently rested on drawings and notes. The species is the only member of the monotypic subgenus *Allohistium* as diagnosed by [[Page 1981]].
- **Earlier work:** [[Kirsch 1893]] rediscovered it in the Obey River, Tennessee. Electrophoretic work by [[Page and Whitt 1973]] found it to be the only *Etheostoma* species with LDH-B1 isozyme mobility, a trait otherwise seen in *Percina*.
- **Diagnosis:** A large darter (maximum 100 mm SL) with thick, papillose lips, a long snout (32% of head length), reduced gill rakers in 7-9 clusters per arch, unscaled breast and nape, and no palatine teeth. It typically has 6 branchiostegal rays.
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Mature males have the most elongate second dorsal fin of any darter (mean 32% of SL) and bright iridescent blue and red breeding coloration. Breeding males show small white tubercles on the pelvic and anal fins. Only 1 of 83 specimens showed tubercles, a 77 mm nuptial male (SIUC 7456).
- **Meristics:** Modal counts include 11 dorsal spines, 12 dorsal rays, 2 anal spines, 8 anal rays, 5 pelvic rays, and 16 principal caudal rays. Lateral-line scales range from 50 to 63 (mode 57). Vertebrae range from 39 to 44.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and status**
- Historically collected from 15 tributary systems. Not collected in seven of these since 1969.
- Regularly taken only in the Rockcastle, Big South Fork Cumberland, Buffalo, Little, and Elk river systems in recent years.
- Status designations: extirpated in Alabama, last collected in Georgia in 1955 and Virginia in 1964. Endangered in Kentucky, of special concern in Tennessee, and of special concern nationally per the [[American Fisheries Society]] Endangered Species Committee.
- Principal threats: impoundments (e.g., [[Dale Hollow Lake]], [[Norris Lake]], [[Watts Bar Lake]]), siltation, coal and strip mining, acid and pesticide pollution, and domestic and industrial discharges.

**Habitat**
- Pool-inhabiting, so siltation is thought to affect it more than riffle-dwelling darters.
- Usually found in water 0.50-1.75 m deep over silt-free sand or gravel, close to shore and near cover.
- Standard seining is inefficient. One pool of about 10 m² yielded four individuals (SIUC 4048), and nine fish came from a 30 m pool in one hour (SIUC 7456).

**Diet**
- Of 83 stomachs examined, 72% contained food.
- Main items: chironomid larvae, *Ephemera* (burrowing mayfly) larvae, and oligochaetes.
- Cumberland drainage fish ate more *Ephemera* and oligochaetes. Upper Tennessee fish ate more chironomids (84% vs. 52% of diet).

**Parasites**
- External: fluke metacercariae in 76% of individuals (27% had 10 or more; maximum 75 cysts).
- Internal (of 83 dissected): encysted nematodes in 9 and acanthocephalans in 3.

## Key Findings

1. **Range contraction:** The species has disappeared from most historical localities. The authors note that low recent catches may partly reflect inadequate sampling.
2. **Morphological variation:** Three somewhat distinct populations (Cumberland, Duck, upper Tennessee) are recognized, but no subspecies. The authors suggest genetic rather than environmental causes for much of the variation. Little River fish had significantly fewer lateral-line scales (modally 54-55).
3. **Discriminant analysis:** The combined analysis correctly classified 97.2% of specimens, and 97.9% to the correct major drainage. Meristic-only analysis classified 85.6% correctly.
4. **Age and growth:** Oldest specimen was a 52-month-old male (100 mm SL) from the Obey River. Most males (68.2%) and females (79.7%) were under two years old. Growth decelerates with age. Duck drainage fish grew fastest.
5. **Sex ratio:** 1.5 females to 1 male overall (62:42 of 104 sexed; X² = 3.85, P < 0.10), possibly due to sampling bias.
6. **Reproduction:** Males mature by age 1 (88% of 1-year-olds) and 2-year-olds are all mature. Females mature at 55 mm SL or more. Spawning likely runs from late January to mid-April, peaking in mid-March. Fecundity averaged 53 mature ova per gram adjusted body weight, with the two ripest females carrying 150 and 249 ova. The authors classify the species as a possible egg burier or attacher.
7. **Conservation recommendations:** Additional collecting is needed to clarify status at several localities. Protection is recommended for the Rockcastle River (partly within [[Daniel Boone National Forest]]) and the Buffalo River, one of the least disturbed southeastern rivers. Coal mining, siltation, and impoundment remain the major threats.
