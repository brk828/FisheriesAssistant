# Fishes of the Recent and Pliocene Lakes of the Western Great Basin and the Idaho Pliocene Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

E. D. Cope's 1883 paper, published in the *Proceedings of the Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia*, describes the fishes of the modern lakes of the northwestern [[Great Basin]] and of the late-Tertiary (Pliocene) lake deposits of Oregon and Idaho. It proposes [[Lake Idaho]] as a distinct Pliocene lake, names several new species and genera, and compares faunal distributions among the [[Lake Lahontan]], [[Klamath Lakes]] and [[Lake Bonneville]] systems. Fossil material from [[Fossil Lake]] and the Idaho beds is described in detail, mainly from pharyngeal bones and skull fragments.

## Background & Description

The paper opens by placing the lakes in a geological frame. Cope notes that the region has undergone relatively recent tectonic disturbance and that the lakes were formerly more extensive and interconnected, as shown by the [[Fortieth Parallel Survey]]. Three former lake systems are discussed:

- **[[Lake Bonneville]]** (Utah): represented today by Salt Lake, Utah Lake and Sevier Lake.
- **[[Lake Lahontan]]** (western Nevada): including Walker's, Carson, Humboldt, Pyramid and Winnemucca lakes.
- A probable Oregon lake north of Lahontan, now represented by the Warner, Abert, Summer and Silver lakes, with Harney and Malheur lakes possibly included.

Cope also argues against the conventional explanation for Great Basin desiccation (rain shadow from the Sierra Nevada and Rocky Mountains). He cites the persistence of lakes in Miocene and Pliocene times, when the ranges were as high or higher, and reports King's estimates of tertiary faulting (up to 30,000 ft on the eastern fault, 3,000–10,000 ft on the western).

Methodology is largely morphological. Cope uses meristic counts (scales, dorsal and anal rays, teeth in the pharyngeal series) and proportional body measurements, typically given in inches or as ratios of head length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry:** Lakes nearer the Sierra Nevada are fresher, because mountain stream inflow dilutes accumulated salts. Lakes farthest from the mountains (Summer and Christmas lakes) are intensely alkaline and not habitable by fish, supporting only crustaceans and insect larvae. Pyramid Lake, fed by the [[Truckee River]], is too alkaline to drink. A King/Allen analysis of its water (in parts per 1000) is cited; the total dissolved solids sum to 3.2365.
- **Upper Klamath Lake:** Described as the most biologically productive water Cope knew. Its alkalinity is considered favourable to life. It supports abundant mollusks (*Planorbis (Carinifex) newberryi*, *Limnaea*), a hydroid newly named *Rhizohydra flavitincta*, and dense fish populations that sustain large flocks of fish-eating birds.
- **Fossil Lake, Oregon:** A shallow depression of about 100 acres with a sand and clay substrate supporting *Artemisia*. Fossils are mixed with worked flints and shells of *Carinifex newberryi*. Nearby sand dunes up to 100 ft high are migrating northeast under prevailing southwest winds.
- **Predator–prey links:** Pelicans, gulls and terns gather when schools of *Leucus* ripple the surface. Pyramid Lake trout (*Salmo purpuratus*) are thought to feed heavily on *Leucus dimidiatus*. The Myloleucus parovanus constitutes a major food source for birds at Goose and Klamath lakes.
- **Sustained use:** Klamath and Modoc Indians dried large numbers of *Chasmistes luxatus* taken during spring runs.

## Key Findings

**Modern lake fauna (Great Basin and Oregon):**
- The author lists 22 species across the fossil and recent faunas, all but a few new to science or newly determined.
- **Distributional patterns:** *Leucus* replaces *Myloleucus* in Pyramid Lake. Of the Pyramid Lake species, all are endemic except *Catostomus tahoensis*, which also occurs in the third Warner Lake about 150 miles north. *Myloleucus formosus* is characteristic of the eastern lakes (Warner, Abert, Silver), while *M. parovanus* occupies the western lakes (Goose, Klamath). *Catostomus labiatus* dominates Goose and Klamath, while *C. tahoensis* is the eastern form.
- **New taxa described:** *Apocope* sp. nov. *Agosia novemradiata* (nine dorsal rays, complete lateral line, Weber River, Utah); *Myloleucus thalassinus* (Goose Lake, a single specimen); *Leucus olivaceus* and *L. dimidiatus* (Pyramid Lake); *Siphateles vittatus* (Pyramid Lake; 17 longitudinal scale rows versus 23–24 in the local *Leucus*); *Squalius galtiz* (Pyramid Lake, with a crimson lateral band); *Chasmistes cujus* (Pyramid Lake; 18 inches; Indian name "Couia"); *Catostomus batrachops* (Silver Lake, Oregon); *Rhizohydra flavitincta* (a new hydroid genus from Klamath Lake).
- **Chasmistes complex:** Cope reports that the Klamath Lake species *C. luxatus* attains nearly three feet and migrates up tributaries in thousands each spring. *C. brevirostris* grows to 14–16 inches. Cope concludes that the pharyngeal bones of these species do not justify a separate genus (he had earlier proposed *Lipomyzon*).

**Idaho Pliocene fauna ([[Lake Idaho]]):**
- The author records **22 species** in total. Most are extinct or unassigned, and all differ from the recent species of the Oregon lakes.
- The fauna is dominated by **Cyprinidae**. Cope recognises carnivorous (*Squalius*), molluscivorous (*Leucus*, *Myloleucus*) and crushing forms (*Mylocyprinus*).
- *Mylocyprinus robustus* is the most abundant Idaho fish. Cope proposes three species (*M. kingi*, *M. robustus*, *M. longidens*) but notes intergradation and suggests age and subspecific variation.
- Remaining Idaho fishes include *Squalius* (four species, including *S. reddingi*, named after B. B. Redding), *Diastichus* (an extinct genus related to Leucus), *Cliola*, *Catostomus shoshonensis*, and the salmonid *Rhabdofario lacustris* (a head comparable in size to *Salmo salar*).
- **Cobitidae** are reported from the Idaho beds, supported by a vertebral element. Cope calls this the most interesting result: it implies Asiatic fish types reached North America before the glacial epoch, suggesting land continuity between the continents.
- **Cottidae:** four sculpin species are described from preopercular bones (*Cottus divaricatus*, *C. pontifex*, *C. cryptotremus*, *C. hypoceras*).
- Other records include a stingray (*Raia pentagona*), a silurid catfish (*Amiurus* sp.), and dorsal spines of an unidentified percid.
- Three crayfish (*Astacus subgrundialis*, *A. chenoderma*, *A. brevifrons*) were found in the same formation. Mollusks indicate a freshwater, lacustrine setting.

**Conclusions:**
- Cope concludes that the Idaho Pliocene formation is distinct from any previously known formation, and that it is older than the Oregon Fossil Lake deposit.
- Of the eight families recorded, four (Percidae, Siluridae, Cobitidae, Ratidae) are absent from the modern Pacific-draining rivers of North America, with the exception of one percid species in California.
