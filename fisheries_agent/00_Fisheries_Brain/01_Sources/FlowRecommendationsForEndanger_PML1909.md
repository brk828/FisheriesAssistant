# Flow Recommendations for Endangered Fishes in the Yampa River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (final, November 1995, prepared by Tim Moddel and George Smith under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]]) updates the 1990 interim flow recommendations for the [[Yampa River]], the last major unregulated tributary of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. It integrates biological data on endangered fish distribution, spawning, and migration with a comparison of historical and estimated [[Virgin Flow]] conditions, and it treats natural variability as central to the recruitment and survival of [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Humpback Chub]].

The core premise is that recruitment failure, driven by habitat alteration and interactions with [[Nonnative Fishes]] in moderated environments, is the main cause of decline. The report recommends maintaining annual, seasonal, and daily variation through a natural hydrograph rather than stair-stepped monthly means, with baseflows set between 20% and 80% exceedance of virgin flow.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The document defines hydrological needs (measured at the Maybell gage) for recovering the four listed species: Colorado Squawfish, Humpback Chub, [[Bonytail]], and Razorback Sucker. It also notes populations of [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] in the basin. It does not prescribe mechanisms for achieving the flows; those are deferred to a separate report.

**Study Area.** The Yampa River drains about 7,600 square miles and provides an average annual flow of 1.2 million acre-feet, of which roughly 20% comes from the Little Snake River. The greatest abundance of endangered fishes occurs downstream of the canyon reaches, including [[Yampa Canyon]] and [[Cross Mountain Canyon]]. Mean spring discharge is about 5,493 cfs; the maximum recorded flow was 33,200 cfs at Deerlodge Park.

**Hydrology.** Annual water depletion was estimated at roughly 100,000 acre-feet in 1989, disproportionately taken from baseflow months. The river transports 1.5 to 2.0 million tons of sediment annually.

**Biological Background.** All four listed species were once common in the Upper Colorado River Basin. The Yampa River still supports some of the last abundant populations, and all three of Colorado Squawfish, Humpback Chub, and Razorback Sucker spawn there, with reproduction closely linked to hydrograph changes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Endangered fish distribution and habitat.**
- *Colorado Squawfish:* Found between Craig and the Green River confluence. The upper Yampa (RM 50.7–123.3) is an overwintering concentration area. Adults use backwaters, runs, and eddies in winter and flooded bottomlands in spring. Larvae drift to backwater nursery habitats in the Green River.
- *Humpback Chub:* Adults collected in canyon-bound areas, mostly in shoreline eddies and runs. Juveniles were found between RM 0.1 and 40 in Yampa Canyon.
- *Razorback Sucker:* Adults collected between RM 13 and RM 0.1. Only one juvenile has been recorded (RM 39, June 1994). The main spawning site lies just above the Green River confluence.
- *Bonytail:* Largely absent; a few individuals recorded since the 1960s.

**Spawning requirements.** Spawning is cued by the shape of the hydrograph and temperature more than by absolute flow magnitude. Colorado Squawfish post-spawn migrations occurred at flows of about 324 cfs or greater through Cross Mountain Canyon and about 177 cfs through the [[Maybell Diversion]]. Spawning temperatures ranged from 14.5 to 27.5 °C across studied years. Razorback Sucker spawning occurred at roughly 14 °C, and Humpback Chub spawning averaged about 19 °C.

**Nonnative fishes.** Native fish were more abundant than nonnative fish in Yampa Canyon, while nonnative fish dominated the lower reach and the Deerlodge to Craig reaches. [[Northern Pike]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] increased in abundance as releases from [[Elkhead Reservoir]] (13,700 acre-feet capacity) began, suggesting that reservoir releases can disperse nonnative fish into the river.

**Flow alteration.** Compared with virgin flows (1950–1982), historical flows show lower peaks (March –5.3%, April –15.2%, May –13.5%, June –8.6%) and higher baseflows (August +4.7%, September +25.6%, October +34.3%). Annual one-day minimums increased by 79.2%, and annual one-day maximums decreased by 8.4%. Standard deviations were similar, indicating reduced variability in the mean.

**Geomorphology.** O'Brien (1984) estimated the effective discharge near Cleopatra Couch at 11,500 cfs, the bankfull discharge at 21,500 cfs, and the channel-scouring flow at about every 1.5 years. Mussetter and Harvey (1994) estimated bar-formation flows at 17,500 cfs (Cleopatra Couch) and 25,000 cfs (Mathers Hole).

## Key Findings

- **Recruitment failure** is the major factor in decline of large-river fishes in the Upper Colorado Basin, driven by habitat alteration and nonnative interactions.
- **Virgin flow conditions** are used as the best-available standard for recovery because the species evolved under them and were abundant then.
- **Spring high flows** are required to cue migration, spawning, and bar and channel maintenance. The recommendation is to provide existing flows minus the baseline depletion (Table 3) during April–July.
- **Baseflows (August–March)** should vary between 20% and 80% exceedance of virgin daily flow, depending on the year type. Under ice conditions, flows should not fall below 80% exceedance.
- **Daily ramping** should not deviate materially from virgin conditions; [[Stanford 1994]] suggested natural daily variation of about 5% per day.
- **Baseline depletions** above Maybell total 142,824 acre-feet (Table 3), including 81,669 acre-feet of agricultural and municipal uses plus reservoir evaporation.
- **Estimated 2040 depletions** average about 171,700 acre-feet per year, with an enlarged Elkhead Reservoir proposed for summer augmentation.
- **Information gaps** include long-term monitoring of the lower Yampa, habitat-flow relationships, native–nonnative interactions, and fish passage limits at low flows (passage observed at 177–324 cfs, but failure at lower flows was not tested).
- **Recommended approach** is adaptive management: implement the recommendations, monitor responses, and revise if low-flow years produce detrimental native–nonnative interactions.
