# Colorado Nonnative Fish Stocking Regulation Evaluation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This final report from the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] (CDOW) and the [[Wyoming Geographic Information Science Center]] (Project No. 106, July 2004) evaluates whether Colorado's 1999 restrictions on stocking nonnative, nonsalmonid fishes west of the Continental Divide protect endangered fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin (UCRB). The study combined GIS mapping, pond sampling, stocking records, and backwater fish surveys within the Grand Valley reach of the Colorado River.

The 6,500-foot elevation demarcation held up broadly. [[Triploid Grass Carp]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] were the most widely stocked species. [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] posed the highest threat to critical riverine habitat. The study could not determine whether the regulations were effective. Private stocking data were incomplete, a vendor response rate was poor, and no spatial pattern linked stocking or floodplain ponds to backwater densities. The authors recommend keeping and clarifying the regulations as a preventive strategy.

## Background & Description

The [[Procedures for Stocking Nonnative Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin]] were adopted in 1996 by Colorado, Utah, Wyoming and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. They required states to ensure that all state and private stocking of nonnative fishes complied with the Procedures. Colorado responded in January 1999 by restricting nonnative stocking in waters below 6,500 feet in the Colorado, Gunnison, White, Yampa and Green river basins. The Wildlife Commission required an evaluation of the regulation's biological effectiveness.

Nonnative fishes are suspected of harming the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Backwaters in the Colorado and Gunnison rivers were reported to be dominated by nonnative fishes, which made up 90% or more of species composition in earlier studies. Shallow backwaters and floodplain depressions serve as nursery habitat for these endangered natives. The study's objectives covered: (1) whether stocking administration reduced riverine abundance of target nonnatives; (2) trends in riverine abundance versus public and private stocking; and (3) a risk analysis of stocking as a source of nonnatives entering critical habitat.

The study area had four nested levels: the general area west of the Continental Divide; the regulatory area below 6,500 feet (excluding the San Juan basin); the primary study area along critical habitat of the Colorado and Gunnison rivers (river miles 240–152 and the Gunnison's lower reach); and the Intensive Study Area (ISA), the Grand Valley reach between river miles 185 and 152 with its 50- and 100-year floodplains.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Standing waters.** Of 3,616 standing waters compiled west of the Continental Divide, 1,104 (31%) lie at or below 6,500 feet. Of 896 suspected ponds in the primary study area, about 59% lie within the 100-year floodplain and roughly 50% within the 50-year floodplain. Fish composition data existed for 307 ponds (34%).

**Pond reclamation.** Of the 896 ponds, 86 (9.5%) had been treated for nonnative control. Chemical reclamation was the most common method (45 ponds). [[Martinez]] (2004) reported about 30% reinvasion of treated ponds by nonnatives.

**Species threat.** The [[Index of Threat]] scales from 0 (no threat) to 1 (maximum threat), combining species prevalence with the proportion of ponds that remain uncontrolled. Green sunfish and largemouth bass had the highest threat indices, mainly because of their widespread occurrence. Common carp and white sucker had similar prevalence, but control reduced carp's index more, because about 30–40% of carp ponds were controlled versus 26% for white sucker. Channel catfish and western mosquitofish showed low pre-control threat and little change after control, so their untreated ponds could become sources.

**Backwater communities.** Fathead minnow comprised more than 50% of nonnative backwater catches across sampling efforts. Electrofishing by [[Osmundson]] (2003) favored larger centrarchids. Nonnative species dominated backwaters throughout 1997–2001, with no clear change in native versus nonnative proportions.

**Spatial structure.** Green sunfish showed a weak positive spatial correlation. The semivariogram range was about 9,908 m, approximately the distance between high-density clusters. Largemouth bass and fathead minnow showed no spatial pattern, which the authors suggest may reflect habitat selection or displacement by green sunfish.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking data:** Vendor responses were poor. Only 3 of 10 vendors replied, and 1 reported no nonnative nonsalmonid sales. The roughly 200 stocking incidences from those three respondents suggest about 400 more from the six non-respondents, but this is an estimate, not a count.
- **Spatially mappable records:** Stocking events with location precise to one square mile numbered 21 (1998), 60 (1999), 132 (2000) and 106 (2001). Of 336 mapped events, the authors assumed many unmapped events were similarly distributed, mostly below 6,500 feet and likely within the 100-year floodplain.
- **Most stocked species:** [[Triploid Grass Carp]] had the most stocking locations (89 west of the Continental Divide). [[Fathead Minnow]] reached up to 250,000 individuals per event. Triploid grass carp was the only private-sector species stocked in designated floodplains (12 events in the 100-year and 11 in the 50-year floodplain).
- **Public stocking:** CDOW stocked bluegill, channel catfish, black crappie, triploid grass carp and largemouth bass below 6,500 feet. Bluegill and largemouth bass were stocked in the 100-year floodplain, and only bluegill in the 50-year floodplain.
- **Importation:** Of 415 importation events (1998–2000), black crappie, bluegill, channel catfish, fathead minnow, largemouth bass and grass carp each made up 8–12%. Most destinations were recorded as "statewide," so the final locations are unknown.
- **Denied permits:** Six permits were denied in western Colorado in 2000, mainly for missing screening requirements or species not permitted for the site.
- **Outcome:** No reduction in riverine abundance of target nonnatives was observed. Spatial pattern analyses showed "no spatial pattern" across nearly all comparisons (Table 15).
- **Conclusions on regulation:** The 6,500-foot demarcation was broadly supported by the data. Whether the regulations were effective remains inconclusive because of data limits and the short timeframe.
- **Index of Threat:** Green sunfish and largemouth bass pose the highest risk of reaching riverine habitat because of their widespread presence in floodplain ponds.
- **Recommendations (CDOW):** Compare private stocking records with CDOW permits to measure compliance; require explicit site coordinates (UTM, latitude/longitude) and water codes; develop methods to track out-of-state direct sales; verify pond connectivity and escapement screens; and require reporting for stocking above 6,500 feet.
- **Recommendations (Recovery Program):** Develop a standardized backwater monitoring protocol; identify the provenance of largemouth bass and green sunfish in backwaters; and keep existing critical-habitat protections (no direct stocking into rivers or connected habitats, prohibition of flathead catfish, sterile fish for grass carp, tiger muskie and hybrids).
- **Alternative methods:** The authors suggest isotopic analyses of water and biota (Martinez et al. 2001) to trace the origins of nonnative fish in critical habitat.
