# Temperature, Humidity and Wind Profiles over the Great Lakes

#physical-limnology #meteorology #great-lakes

## Executive Summary

This 1961 preliminary report from the [[University Of Michigan Great Lakes Research Division]] describes a new shipboard meteorological boom on the research vessel [[Porte Dauphine]] for measuring vertical profiles of temperature, humidity and wind in the lowest atmosphere over the lakes. The authors, J. P. Bruce, D. V. Anderson and G. K. Rodgers, aimed to improve estimates of [[Air-Lake Interaction]] friction and [[Lake Evaporation]]. They conclude that the instrument still needs work before its profiles are reliable, mainly because of timing errors in gusty winds and a ship "hull effect" on wind readings. Mean profiles under near-neutral stability gave drag coefficients broadly consistent with other investigators, so the authors expect the boom to be useful as an operational tool for mapping low-level air-mass climatology.

## Background & Description

**Scientific motivation.** Knowledge of friction between atmosphere and lakes, and of evaporation from them, is important in both meteorology and [[Physical Limnology]]. The authors note that the friction term is poorly constrained despite many experiments, as emphasized by [[Portman]] (1960) at the Third Conference on Great Lakes Research. Earlier work included a summary of evaporation estimates by [[Bruce And Rodgers]] (1959) and an energy-budget estimate of average monthly evaporation for [[Lake Ontario]] by [[Rodgers And Anderson]] (1961).

**Measurement platform.** In August 1960 the research vessel C.M.S. Porte Dauphine was fitted with a boom carrying a remote-indicating anemometer and wet- and dry-bulb thermometers.
- The boom is mounted at the bow. Measurements are taken on an arc of 25 ft radius (the boom length) centred on the bow.
- Profiles span 1 ft (in light winds) to 44 ft above the water, with the ship hove to a few points off the wind.
- Profiles are collected routinely alongside regular meteorological and limnological observations.

**Instrument.** A small blower draws air through a shielded orifice across wet and dry bulbs, whose temperatures are read by thermistors. Wind speed comes from a commercial hot-wire anemometer (Hastings-Raydist type B15A). The sensing head weighs 10 lb.

**Boom construction.** The boom is rectangular aluminum tubing (2 × 4 × 3/16 in), cantilevered and counterbalanced by two torsion springs, and raised by a small cable winch. The post can be swung inboard when not in use. The boom has survived winds up to 50 mph, but pitching restricts good measurements to about 0–30 mph.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses only physical processes at the air-water interface. It contains no biological data (no fish, plankton or invertebrate observations) and no water-chemistry measurements. Its relevance to aquatic ecology is indirect: wind stress and evaporation drive lake circulation and the energy balance of the water body, which in turn shape the thermal and physical habitat of aquatic organisms. The authors do not draw any such biological connection.

**Platform effects.** The ship is 125 ft long with a 25 ft beam. Its hull can distort wind measurements. Citing [[Deacon Et Al]] (1956), the authors note that 5 m forward of a 70 ft schooner the hull effect reaches 2–3%. They expect low-level winds to be affected, but the error should be small at heights of 20 ft or more above the forward deck.

**Field conditions.** Trials were conducted on [[Lake Ontario]] in November 1960, in strong, gusty winds with showers and hail. Steady-wind profiles were later analyzed from [[Lake Erie]] off Port Burwell, Ontario, in late September 1960.

## Key Findings

**1. Instrument performance and limitations**
- Wind-tunnel calibration showed that the boom head must be kept oriented into the wind for consistent results between levels and between observation sets.
- Rapid wind fluctuations were tracked by the instrument needle, making 5- or 10-second mean winds hard to read. A damping circuit was being added.
- Ship-induced perturbation is still a concern. The authors plan an independent wind measurement, or ship runs at known speeds, to quantify the windage correction.

**2. November 1960 Lake Ontario test run**
- Hot-wire readings at 38 ft at the bow averaged about 25–30% higher than the ship's mast-mounted flashing anemometer (36 ft). The discrepancy was not resolved by calibration.
- Ten-second averages fluctuated sharply in gusty conditions. In one case, winds fell from 37 to 29 mph within one minute.
- Taking a series of observations from 2 to 44 ft takes 2–3 minutes. The authors conclude that under such gusty conditions the boom profiles cannot be relied upon.
- At an anchor station about 2 miles offshore from Hamilton, Ontario, 4 ft and 40 ft wind differences fluctuated rapidly between 1700 EST on 3 November and about 0130 on 4 November 1960. The authors could not determine whether these reflected real profile-shape changes or timing effects during profiling.

**3. Mean profiles and drag coefficients (Lake Erie, 26–27 September 1960)**
- Only steady-wind periods with near-neutral lapse rate were analyzed. Two ten-hour mean profiles were obtained off Port Burwell.
- Table 1 reports two periods: 26–27 September (1715–0410) and 27–28 September (1430–0007). Frictional velocities were 8.5 and 8.8 cm/s, with shear stresses of about 0.86 × 10⁻¹ and 0.93 × 10⁻¹ g cm⁻¹ s⁻², respectively.
- Drag coefficients were recorded as 5.8 and 2.5 (×10⁻⁴, as inferred from the stated formula Cd = (U*/U₁₀)²). The OCR-scanned exponents are not fully legible, so these values should be checked against the original print.
- The authors use von Kármán constant k = 0.40 and air density ρ = 1.2 × 10⁻³ g cm⁻³.
- Drag coefficients were "close to" those reported by Portman over [[Lake Michigan]], though at somewhat higher wind speeds. Portman's values averaged roughly 4 × 10⁻³ for winds of 200–350 cm/s (exponent as scanned).

**4. Conclusions and planned use**
- Keeping the same instrument at every level avoids inter-calibration problems. However, the roughly two-minute interval between low- and high-level readings can distort results in gusty winds and unstable air.
- Ship windage must be determined before the profiles can be considered reliable.
- Under neutral-stability mean conditions, the drag coefficients are not radically different from those of other investigators. The authors therefore expect the boom to be valuable for light-to-moderate winds and stable to slightly unstable lapse rates.
- Its main intended value is as an operational tool for building a low-level air-mass climatology over the lakes, supplementing routine observations from the [[Porte Dauphine]].
