# Fisheries Problems Associated with the Development of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review by Stephen J. Nicola (Senior Fishery Biologist, Endangered Species Program, Inland Fisheries Branch, California Department of Fish and Game) traces how water development on the [[Lower Colorado River]] altered the river and its fishes. The river's native fishes were adapted to a turbulent, turbid channel and declined after [[Hoover Dam]] and other large storage structures were built, although early studies are lacking to pinpoint the main cause. Storage reservoirs created conditions favoring exotic sport fisheries, while dredging and channelization destroyed the backwater spawning and nursery habitat that native species needed. Mitigation has not fully compensated for lost fishery values. Since 1973, renewed interest in restoring native fishes under the [[Endangered Species Act]] has grown, creating a dual challenge: restoring natives while sustaining existing sport fisheries.

The paper was prepared under a federal Endangered Species Act grant-in-aid project ("California E-F-1, Endangered, Threatened, and Rare Fish") and reprinted from proceedings of a 1977 conference on Alternative Strategies for Desert Development and Management.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] is the fifth-longest river in the United States, about 2,250 km long, draining roughly 6.21 x 10^5 km² (about 7.7% of the conterminous US surface area). It rises in the Rocky Mountains at about 40° N and enters the Gulf of California at 31° 40′ N. Its average pre-development discharge was 17,500 hm³, far less than the [[Nile River]] (6,690 km; 93,000 hm³), and it has not reached the Gulf of California except in 1961. Nearly 100% of its average annual flow is now appropriated for agricultural, domestic, and industrial use.

The lower river, defined as the 584 km reach from Hoover Dam (Lake Mead) to the Gulf, forms much of the California–Arizona border. Under natural conditions its gradient averaged about 0.3 m/km, and it carried heavy silt loads; peak flows once transported up to roughly 1,800 ppm of dissolved solids in flood periods, falling below 300 ppm during larger floods. Water temperatures ranged from about 12 to 23 °C.

Before European settlement, Native American tribes fished the river and hunted along its banks. Early settlers recorded the Colorado Squawfish as a major sport and food fish, and large migratory spring spawning runs of squawfish and suckers were common in tributaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sixteen native fish species (eight families) were reported from the Colorado River, eight of which occurred in the lower river. Native fishes showed morphological adaptations to swift flows, including a nuchal hump and enlarged muscle mass, which helped them hold position in strong currents. Squawfish could reach 185 cm and 45 kg. Table 2 lists about 30 native and introduced species, with status codes for native, introduced, endangered, and extirpated taxa.

Dams changed the river's temperature regime: water released from deep reservoir layers kept Below Hoover and Davis dams near 10 °C year-round, and flow shifted from winter floods to summer irrigation releases. Sediment deposition then caused aggradation, flooding at Needles, and channel degradation. Salinity increased as Colorado water was used for irrigation, with salts concentrating in return flows. Exotic plants such as saltcedar (Tamarix spp.) replaced cottonwood-willow riparian habitat.

Dredging and channelization removed backwater and marsh nursery habitat. The Cibola Diversion channel now reaches flow velocities too high for effective fishing, and unplanned deep scouring is lowering the water table in the adjacent floodplain, drying out backwater lakes and marshes.

## Key Findings

- **Native fish decline:** The squawfish was extant below Lake Mead, with its last reported capture in 1967. The humpback chub has not been taken since [[Hoover Dam]] was built. The bonytail chub may persist in small numbers in Lake Havasu and Lake Mohave, with the last verified capture in 1975. The humpback sucker is doing somewhat better; adults are occasionally taken, and juvenile reproduction has been observed only once.
- **Exotic species expansion:** Channel catfish, carp, largemouth and other black basses, sunfishes, and rainbow trout became dominant sport fishes. Cold-water releases from Hoover Dam created ideal habitat for [[Rainbow Trout]], supported by a hatchery below the dam. Catch rates at Lake Havasu fell from 0.5 fish per angler-hour in the 1940s to under 0.2 by 1965.
- **Introductions:** Striped bass and flathead catfish were introduced in the late 1950s. Mozambique tilapia were introduced into irrigation canals in the Palo Verde and Imperial valleys to control aquatic weeds, but with only partial success.
- **Habitat loss:** Dredging of the reach from Davis Dam to Topock (1949–1960), channelization below Topock (from 1967, halted after about 2.5 km), and work at Gadgets Rock Dam (37 km in 1967) altered the river. Plans to extend work 34 km toward Palo Verde Diversion Dam were blocked by state and federal wildlife agencies. Only about 128 km of unmodified floodplain habitat remains.
- **Mitigation limits:** Artificial backwater lakes can take nearly a decade to mature, and so mitigation has not fully compensated for lost fishery values.
- **Future outlook:** The author expects continued pressure from water demands in the American Southwest, the upper basin states, Mexico, and energy developments. Native fish restoration programs by Recovery Teams are underway, but future fisheries management will need to balance restoring native species with maintaining exotic sport fisheries.
