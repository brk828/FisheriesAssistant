# Surveys Through 1951 of the Distribution and Abundance of Young Sardines

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This California Department of Fish and Game Bureau of Marine Fisheries bulletin (Fish Bulletin No. 87, 1952) by [[Julius B. Phillips]] and [[John Radovich]] reports fall surveys of young-of-year [[Pacific Sardine]] (*Sardinops caerulea*) along the Pacific coast from Bodega Head, California to Cape San Lucas, Baja California, with a brief look at the northern Gulf of California. Surveys ran in 1938–1940 and again in 1949–1951. The aim was to measure the strength of incoming year classes before they reached the commercial fishery, so that catch could be forecast. The 1938–1940 surveys were judged only partly successful: the 1938 class came out about ten times as abundant as the 1939 class, but the 1939 class proved the strongest of the three. The 1950–1951 surveys tracked the 1948 year class as the dominant group and projected a weak fishery for 1952–53 and 1953–54.

## Background & Description

The sardine population of the Pacific coast of North America fluctuates strongly with spawning success, producing very abundant, sparse and intermediate year classes. Older fish once buffered these swings, but by the late 1930s they were largely gone from the fishing grounds, leaving the industry dependent on fish one to three years old. The [[California Cooperative Sardine Research Program]] supplied egg and larval distribution data to set survey ranges, and the [[Walford and Mosher Age Determination Method]] (scales and otoliths) made aging of year classes possible.

Survey methods included:
- **Lampara nets** (small: 100 fathoms; large: 150 fathoms) operated from skiffs.
- **Ringnet** trials, found impractical for counting school size.
- **Dynamite** charges, requiring roughly one-quarter to one-half stick per school and typically under 100 fish per sample.
- **Night light attraction** and plankton tows for larvae.
- **Recording echo-sounder and sonar**, the latter judged unsuccessful in shallow water.
- Vessels used: the [[ALBACORE]], [[BLUEFIN]], [[N. B. Scofield]] (sic, *N. B. Scofield*, research vessel launched 1938) and the [[YELLOWFIN]] (114 ft, used 1950–1951).

In the 1950–1951 program the coast was divided into areas A–E, with subdivisions B-1, B-2, C-1, C-2, D-1, D-2 and D-3, and surveys were scheduled from south to north to follow the northward progression of spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature limits:** Sardines were not found above 26.1 °C (79 °F) in 1938–1940 surveys or above 25.7 °C in the later surveys. Sardines were not found below 11.4 °C. The overall surveyed range was 10.4 °C to 28.3 °C. Sardines were found at 79 °F near Santa Maria Bay, but only as fish of the previous year's spawning. Japanese sardine (*Sardinops melanostictus*) laboratory data (7–29 °C) were cited as a comparison.
- **Spawning range:** Main spawning was expected off Southern California from Pt. Conception to San Diego. No sardines were found south of [[Magdalena Bay]] in the fall 1939 and 1940 cruises, which is taken to define the southern nursery limit.
- **Shoaling behavior:** Young sardines did not usually reach inshore flats until about 55 mm standard length. Sardines were found in albacore stomachs offshore at Monterey (36–75 mm) before inshore appearance.
- **Schooling and mixing:** Young sardines mixed with young [[Northern Anchovy]] (*Engraulis mordax*), jack smelt, top smelt, round herring and thread herring.
- **Predation:** Observed predation by barracuda on anchovy schools and by Pacific mackerel on a sardine pod.
- **Other fishes in 1950–1951:** Sardines, jack mackerel (*Trachurus symmetricus*), Pacific mackerel (*Pneumatophorus diego*) and anchovies were the principal species. Sauries (*Cololabis saira*) were abundant offshore.
- **Gulf of California:** Found to contribute little to the main stock, based on vertebral counts.

## Key Findings

**1938–1940 surveys**
- Schools of young sardines observed: 818 (1938), 63 (1939), 204 (1940).
- 1938: 91% of schools in Southern California, 8% in Baja California, 1% in Central California.
- 1939: 49% Central California, 2% Southern California, 49% Baja California. Later evidence showed extensive 1939 spawning north of the surveyed area.
- 1940: 30% Central California, 22% Southern California, 48% Baja California.
- Final estimates: the 1938 class about 10 times as abundant as 1939 and four times as abundant as 1940. Commercial catches were roughly 800,000 tons from the 1939 class and about 500,000 tons each from the 1938 and 1940 classes, so the 1939 class proved strongest. The survey-based ranking was therefore wrong.
- Surveys were judged inadequate because they missed northern spawning, which was linked to outbreaks and mortality in the Pacific Northwest.

**1950–1951 surveys**
- 1950 survey: the 1948 year class averaged 88.2 fish per scouting night (48.3% of fish measured). The 1950 class averaged 37.7 (20.7%); the 1947 class 14.1%; the 1949 class 10.5%.
- 1951 survey: the 1948 class was still dominant (about 39.6% of fish). Older fish declined sharply.
- Mortality between surveys: 1950 class 45.1%; 1949 class 25.0%; 1948 class 39.5%; 1948-and-younger combined 39.0%; 1946–1947 classes 70.9%; 1945 and older 89.3%.
- Size: fish of the same year class were larger in Southern California than in Sebastián Vizcaíno Bay (region D-1). In 1950 the 1948 class averaged 19.74 cm (C-1) vs. 17.92 cm (D-1), a difference of 1.82 cm (14 times the standard error). In 1951 the difference was 1.68 cm (10.5 times standard error).
- Commercial comparison: the 1948 class made up 77% of fish three years and older in the survey and 72% in the 1951–52 commercial catch. The 1951–52 season ranked with 1947–48 as the poorest in 29 years.

**Forecasts and conclusions**
- The 1948 class could not sustain a fishery alone; the 1952–53 season was expected to be weaker than 1951–52.
- The 1950 and 1949 classes were weak; the 1951 class was stronger than the 1950 class in D-1 and D-2 but its future strength remained uncertain.
- The San Francisco–Monterey area was not expected to contribute much to the catch; success was expected mainly in Southern California.
- Factors controlling sardine movement remained undetermined, limiting predictive accuracy.

**Methodological notes**
- Equal weighting of samples (each standardized to 100 fish) was adopted because school size bore no relation to the number of fish counted.
- The scouting night was used as the unit of effort.
- Gill nets and mid-water trawls were rejected for selectivity or inefficiency. Dynamite was judged the best sampling method.
