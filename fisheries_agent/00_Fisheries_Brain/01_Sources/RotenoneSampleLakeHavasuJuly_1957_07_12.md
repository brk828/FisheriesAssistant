# Rotenone Sample, Lake Havasu, July 7, 1957

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #Dorosomatidae #Cyprinidae #Poeciliidae #Ictaluridae #FisheriesManagement

## Executive Summary
This report documents a fisheries survey conducted by the California Department of Fish and Game on July 7, 1957, in three small coves within the Bill Williams Arm of [[Lake Havasu]]. Using [[Rotenone]] (a piscicide) as a total-recovery sampling method, biologists G.W. McCammon and field aids Cuneo and Bray collected a total of 1,664 fish across five species. The sample was overwhelmingly dominated by juvenile [[Threadfin Shad]] (93.4% of total catch), indicating a successful spawning/recruitment event for this forage species. The data was intended to characterize the size-class structure of the shad population via an attached length-frequency histogram, alongside incidental catch data for accompanying species.

## Background & Description
The following length data (in inches) were recorded for each species collected during the rotenone sample:

| Species | Count (N) | Mean Length (in) | Range (in) |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Threadfin Shad]] | 1555 | 1.17 | 0.6 – 3.2 |
| [[Plains Red Shiner]] | 68 | 1.0 | 0.6 – 2.0 |
| [[Gambusia]] (Mosquitofish) | 33 | 0.9 | 0.7 – 1.2 |
| [[Channel Catfish]] | 7 | 4.3 | 4.1 – 4.7 |
| [[Common Carp]] | 1 | 1.1 | N/A (single specimen) |

- **Threadfin Shad** exhibited the widest size range (0.6–3.2 in), consistent with a mixed cohort of recent hatchlings and slightly older juveniles/young-of-year.
- **Channel Catfish** specimens were notably uniform in size (4.1–4.7 in), suggesting a single age class or cohort was present in the sampled coves.
- **Carp** representation was minimal (n=1), captured at a small juvenile size (1.1 in), suggesting incidental presence rather than established breeding population in these specific coves.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **[[Threadfin Shad]]**: Serves as the dominant forage fish species in this reservoir system. Their overwhelming numerical dominance (1555 of 1664 total fish, ~93%) in the shallow cove habitat suggests these areas function as critical nursery/rearing habitat for pelagic forage fish. The small mean size (1.17 in) indicates the sample captured a recent age-0 cohort.
- **[[Plains Red Shiner]]** and **[[Gambusia]]**: Both are small-bodied cyprinid/poeciliid species typically associated with littoral, vegetated, or slow-moving shoreline habitats — consistent with the shallow cove environments targeted by the rotenone treatment.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]**: Represented by only 7 juveniles, likely opportunistic occupants of the cove rather than primary littoral residents; catfish typically use such areas for early rearing before moving to deeper or more complex benthic habitat.
- **[[Common Carp]]**: Single juvenile specimen suggests either transient use of the cove or very low localized recruitment in the sampled area.
- The cove habitat within the [[Bill Williams Arm]] of [[Lake Havasu]] appears to function primarily as a forage-fish nursery, supporting the broader reservoir food web (e.g., providing prey for game fish such as bass or catfish elsewhere in the lake).

## Key Findings
- Total catch across three rotenoned coves: **1,664 individual fish**, spanning 5 species.
- **[[Threadfin Shad]]** dominated the catch by an overwhelming margin (93.4% of total sample), indicating a strong or successful recent spawning event.
- A **length-frequency histogram** was generated specifically for the Threadfin Shad population (referenced as an attachment), intended to visualize size-class distribution across the 0.6–3.2 inch range.
- Species diversity in the cove was low, with only one predatory/game species ([[Channel Catfish]]) represented, and in very low abundance (n=7).
- The presence of [[Common Carp]] was negligible, represented by a single juvenile.
- This report exemplifies standard mid-20th century fisheries management practice of using **rotenone sampling** to quantitatively assess forage fish populations and age-class structure in reservoir nursery habitats.
