# Gastropods of Cowley County, Kansas

#malacology #gastropoda #pulmonata

## Executive Summary

This regional faunistic survey, published in the *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science* (1962), records **29 species of gastropods** (snails and slugs) from Cowley County, southern Kansas, based on collections made between November 1956 and December 1961. The study classifies collecting stations into wooded, prairie and aquatic habitats (56 stations in total) and documents habitat preferences for each species. It also reports several range extensions and first county records, and includes a detailed taxonomic discussion of variation in [[Physa Gyrina]] shell morphology along a gradient from flowing to standing water.

The document contains no fish data. Its ecological focus is on snails and slugs, their habitat associations, and environmental drivers such as moisture, intermittency and rainfall.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Cowley County lies on the southern boundary of Kansas, roughly 100 miles west of the state's eastern border. Most of the county falls within the [[Flint Hills]], with cuesta scarps formed on Early Permian limestones. The southwestern part has low-relief topography developed on the Middle Permian Wellington Formation.
- **Sampling:** Collections spanned about five years (1956–1961). Roughly half were made in November and December 1958. Identifications were assisted by Professor A. Byron Leonard and Mr. Tong-Yun Ho of the University of Kansas.
- **Habitat classification:**
  - **Wooded (Stations W 0–24; 25 stations):** Three types: creekside fringe forests (oaks, red elm, black walnut, hackberry), Arkansas River floodplain fringe forest (willow, cottonwood), and a sandy upland blackjack oak (*Quercus marilandica*) stand that yielded almost no snails.
  - **Prairie (Stations P 25–38; 14 stations):** Predominantly tall-grass bluestem prairie with limestone ledges and rocky slopes.
  - **Aquatic (Stations A 39–55; 17 stations):** Ephemeral pools, creeks and rivers, impoundments and oxbow lakes, and a few springs.
- **Taxonomic approach:** The author follows [[Hibbard And Taylor]] (1960) and Leonard (1959) for physid and other nomenclature, while noting unresolved disagreements with [[Branson]] (1961) for Oklahoma physids. Several taxa are assigned conservatively because of clinal variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Woodland and floodplain systems.** Wooded habitats hold the greatest snail diversity. Along creeks, [[Anguispira Alternata]] was the most common large snail, *Polygyra dorfeuilliana* favored dry, rocky wooded slopes, and *Stenotrema leai aliciae* was abundant on wooded floodplains under leaf litter and logs. Fringe forests on the [[Arkansas River]] floodplain had few snails near the river, where sorghum-dominated, alluvial sand supported little, but richer humus farther from the channel supported many.

**Prairie and dry uplands.** Dry, rocky grassland and hillsides were dominated by a small group: [[Gastrocopta Armifera]], *Gastrocopta procera*, *Pupoides albilabris*, *Retinella indentata* and *Hawaiia minuscula*. Moist sites, such as upland brooks and seepage areas, added *Gastrocopta contracta*, *Vallonia parvula*, *Zonitoides arboreus*, *Helicodiscus parallelus* and *Quickella vagans*.

**Aquatic gradients.** Aquatic snails followed a clear moisture and flow gradient. *Physa gyrina* was abundant in flowing brooks with rubble and bedrock and in ephemeral pools, while *Lymnaea bulimoides techella* was common in ephemeral pools and upland brooks. *Helisoma trivolvis lentum* dominated impoundments, farm ponds and roadside ditches. No aquatic snails were found in the sandy, braided lower Walnut River or in the sandy Arkansas River.

**Drought and rainfall effects.** Abundance shifted with rainfall. *Euconulus chersinus* and *Gastrocopta contracta* were uncommon during the drought years 1956 and 1957, but were much more common in the wetter years 1960 and 1961. *Gastrocopta contracta* was far more abundant than *G. armifera* in seepage-moistened sites in 1961.

**Hydrologic regime and shell form.** Shell morphology tracked the permanence of water. Physids from intermittent upland brooks and ephemeral pools had thicker shells with deeper striae and more pronounced rest marks. Snails from lakes and spring-fed pools had thinner, shinier shells with fewer rest marks.

## Key Findings

1. **Species inventory:** 29 gastropod species were recorded, with habitat notes for each. Wooded habitats had the highest species richness.

2. **Range extensions and new records:**
   - *Mesodon thyroidus thyroidus* was recorded at stations W 9, 13 and 23, providing a slight westward extension of its known Kansas range.
   - *Euconulus chersinus* records provide a southwestward extension of its known Kansas range.
   - *Strobilops labyrinthica* was previously unrecorded west of Greenwood and Montgomery counties. The westernmost record is at the Arkansas River and Grouse Creek confluence, 4 miles southwest of Silverdale.
   - *Gastrocopta tappaniana* is the first record for Cowley or adjacent Kansas counties.
   - *Carychium exile exile* is based on only two specimens (November 24, 1961). The nearest previous Kansas locality was in Cherokee County, about 100 miles east.
   - Cowley County marks the western recorded limit of *Anguispira alternata alternata* in Kansas, though the species is often abundant there.

3. **Physa taxonomy:** Cowley County physids are best assigned to *Physa gyrina* Say. The author treats the variation as a single variable species, possibly a "microgeographic mosaic," rather than separate taxa. Specimens from the Cowley County State Lake (A 42) and another lake (A 52) could be referred to *P. halei* under Branson's criteria, but the author did not do so. No specimens were clearly *P. anatina*, though a few from spring-fed pools (A 55) and a dead shell near the Arkansas River (W 9) approached it.

4. **Shell variation along a flow gradient:** Shell height/width means for *P. gyrina* declined from 1.78 at station A 50 (upstream creek) and 1.74 at A 45 (stream) to 1.61 at A 42 (Cowley County State Lake; n = 50). Shells became thinner, shinier and less sculptured as conditions shifted from lotic to lentic.

5. **Subspecies question in *Stenotrema*:** Cowley County specimens are assigned to *Stenotrema leai aliciae*. Northern Kansas populations show slight intergradation, which suggests a cline from *S. l. leai* in northern Nebraska to *S. l. aliciae* in southern Kansas. The author proposes that the Nebraska Sand Hills may mark the boundary.

6. **Euconulid and Helicodiscus uncertainty:** Euconulid assignment to *E. chersinus* is provisional because species limits in the complex remain unresolved. *Helicodiscus parallelus* is conservatively assigned as a single species, although southern specimens show broader umbilici, larger palatal denticles and reddish periostracum relative to northern ones.

7. **Relict and historical signals:** Dead *Mesodon* shells embedded in floodplain sediments along Cedar and Beaver creeks suggest the species was more common in the county in the past. The author also suggests *S. l. aliciae* may have replaced the typical subspecies in post-Pleistocene time.

8. **Survey limitations:** The author notes that the blackjack oak sand stand and the sandy lower Walnut River and Arkansas River yielded almost no snails, and that the euconulid and *Helicodiscus* taxonomic questions remain unresolved for want of a regional revision.
