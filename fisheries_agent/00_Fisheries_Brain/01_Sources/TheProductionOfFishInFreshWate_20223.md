# The Production of Fish in Fresh Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This reprinted 1958 paper by E. D. Le Cren of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] argues that British freshwater fish production is mainly a sport-fishing problem, not a food or commercial one. The author separates *production* (total growth of a fish population) from the *crop* actually taken by anglers. Published production estimates show that large amounts of fish are generated, but anglers harvest only a small share, and most production occurs in young fish that die from natural mortality before they become catchable. The paper concludes that management should focus on improving the efficiency of reproduction, rearing and cropping, rather than on manipulating primary or secondary production. A discussion section follows, in which Le Cren and M. E. Solomon consider density-dependent regulation and sustainable yield, using insect and blowfly experiments as analogies.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with a comparison of yields. Intensively managed fish farms in Africa and Java produce up to five tons per acre per year (9,500 kg/ha). In Germany, under climatic conditions similar to Britain's, nearly 700 lb of [[Carp]] were produced from one acre of fresh water. The author argues these figures show that fresh waters can compete with agriculture in both yield and economics.

Freshwater fish have little food or commercial importance in Britain. Commercial freshwater landings, mainly of [[Salmon]] and sea trout (which grow mostly at sea), are about 0.2 per cent of sea-fish landings. The [[Eel]] is commercially fished, especially in Northern Ireland, but eaten by only a small part of the population. The author attributes this neglect to prejudice and poor culinary skill, and suggests freshwater species may be less palatable than marine fish.

Trout farming is an exception. It is primarily a Danish development, with farms now also in England. The economics depend on a luxury market that pays about ten times the cost of glut marine white fish. Roughly one pound of [[Rainbow Trout]] is produced from eight pounds of white fish, after mortality and other losses. Deep-freeze distribution supports an export trade to Germany, Britain and North America.

The main value of British freshwater fish is recreational. The number of anglers is not known. The author estimates that more than 250,000 people fish regularly and perhaps a million or more angle at some time. Angling also supports the tackle industry, fishing-rights values and tourist spending. The practical goal, the author argues, is to produce as large a total weight of fish as possible.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author describes production as a chain of linked processes:

- **Primary production**, which in many British waters is often reused organic matter from the drainage basin or sewage works rather than in-lake photosynthesis.
- **Food-web conversion** into fish food by a complex animal food web. Plant matter forms part of the diet of only a few British fishes.
- **Assimilation and loss**. Most food is lost during conversion, and the food that is assimilated supports maintenance and growth.
- **Natural mortality and predation**, which remove much of the production before it can be harvested.
- **Reproduction**, which replenishes the stock.

Freshwater fisheries have traditionally been managed for three aims: maximizing reproduction and population density, sharing the catch among those legally entitled to fish, and reconciling fishing with water supply and sewage use. Some managers have tried fertilization, habitat improvement and predator control. Water abstraction, pollution and legal questions are noted but are outside the paper's scope. The author states that the biological basis of fish production remains poorly understood.

Competing demands on freshwater are strong. The author argues that recreational fishing is unlikely to outweigh other uses in a crowded island, so the challenge is to use nutrient-rich inputs, such as sewage, for sporting fish production.

## Key Findings

**Few production estimates exist.** Le Cren knew of only two published estimates of fish production:
- [[Ricker And Foerster]] (1948) estimated young [[Sockeye Salmon]] production in [[Cultus Lake]] across several years, including a predator-control experiment.
- Allen (1951) estimated brown trout production in the [[Horokiwi River]], New Zealand.

The author also made tentative estimates for [[Perch]] and [[Pike]] in [[Windermere]], but the survival data for young fish were too limited for more than conjectural production values in the young stages.

**Production is large, but anglers harvest little.**
- Horokiwi trout production was 570 kg/ha. Perch production in Windermere was probably more than 200 kg/ha.
- Anglers harvested only about 6 per cent of the Horokiwi trout production.
- The author concludes that angling is often the least efficient part of the system.

**Most production occurs in young fish, and most is lost early.**
- 80 per cent of Horokiwi trout production occurred in the first year of life, before fish were large enough to catch.
- By the end of that year, 73 per cent of this production had been lost to natural mortality.
- Cultus Lake salmon showed a similar first-year survival rate.
- Cyprinids are expected to show an even greater share of early-life production, given their higher fecundity.

**Reproduction absorbs much of the food supply.** The author estimates that about 40 per cent of adult Windermere perch production goes to gonad products. Rearing young fish to catchable size may use most of the remaining production, yet the resulting recruits have a standing crop no greater than the total weight of the eggs they came from. Much of the food supply is therefore spent on gametes and fry that die before capture.

**Predator and density control can improve survival.** At Cultus Lake, reducing predatory fish density by about one-third had little effect on total productivity. It did, however, markedly raise the share of salmon production that survived the first year, and it increased production per egg. Production per egg was highest at lower egg and fry densities. Salmonid fry density appears inversely related to survival, so spawning could be regulated and maximum yields obtained from small numbers of spawners ([[Stock And Recruitment]]).

**Artificial hatching may be harmful.** The planting of artificially fertilized eggs or hatchery fish, where it makes a difference, may be harmful rather than beneficial in many natural populations.

**Management principles.**
- The primary management aim should be to increase the efficiency of reproduction and rearing. Natural mortality should be minimized, and the proportion of catchable fish that is caught should be increased.
- The author warns that the links between algae, invertebrates and fish are so complex that trying to manage primary or secondary production to increase catches is rarely worthwhile.
- The author expects that much British freshwater production goes into eggs, milt and fry that die, while anglers catch only a small fraction and often return fish to the water to protect the spawning stock.

**Discussion: density-dependent regulation.** M. E. Solomon links the sustainable yield question to density-dependent population control. He cites [[Kenneth Watt]]'s [[Flour Beetle]] (*Tribolium*) experiments, in which the optimal monthly cropping fraction was about 90 per cent, and [[Nicholson]]'s [[Blowfly]] (*Lucilia*) experiments, where removing young adults increased adult emergence up to a cropping level of about 90 per cent. Solomon argues that potential yield must be found through trial and error or ecological analysis, not from the size of the standing crop. Le Cren agrees that fisheries theory is moving toward predicting optimum fishing rates and net mesh sizes for maximum sustained yield. He notes that the persistence of heavily exploited fisheries is evidence of natural population regulation, and that the inverse relation between density and growth also affects fecundity, because egg number depends on female size.
