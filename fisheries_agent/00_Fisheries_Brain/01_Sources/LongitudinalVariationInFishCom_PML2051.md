# Longitudinal Variation in Fish Community Structure and Water Temperature in the Upper Colorado River: Implications for Colorado Pikeminnow Habitat Suitability

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service final report (March 1999, Recovery Implementation Program Project No. 48, author Douglas B. Osmundson) examines why the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] population in the upper [[Colorado River]] may be limited by carrying capacity. Electrofishing, body condition, diet and thermal data were collected in 1994–1995 across 12 strata of the Colorado River and the lower [[Gunnison River]]. Forage-size fish and temperature were combined to estimate the adult population the system can support, and the potential gains from range expansion through fish passage and temperature augmentation. The report found that carrying capacity is probably far lower than generally assumed, and that range expansion plus productivity improvements are needed for long-term viability.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, formerly "squawfish") is an endangered, warm-water cyprinid restricted to the upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. The upper Colorado River population persists with little reproduction and recruitment, and the study was motivated by the observation that native fish abundance and pikeminnow body condition changed sharply along the river.

Three native forage species were the focus of body-condition and diet analyses:
- [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)

Relative condition (Kn) was used instead of Fulton's condition factor because it is comparable across fish lengths. Length-weight regression coefficients (slope b, y-intercept, r²) were derived for each species from pooled data (see Table 1 of source).

Major historical changes cited as reducing carrying capacity include diversion structures blocking access to habitat, flow regulation by dams, reduced spring flood pulses and sediment dynamics, and irrigation projects reducing seasonally preferred mesohabitats. The Aspinall Unit dams on the Gunnison River were highlighted as cooling downstream temperatures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and design.** The Colorado River study area extended from the Moab, Utah area to the Cameo/Palisade region, stratified by average channel gradient, landform and canyon/alluvial type into strata 1–11, with [[Westwater Canyon]] as a key break. Stratum 12 was the Gunnison River reach between Redlands and Hartland diversion dams. Sampling occurred in spring (March–April) and fall (September–October) of 1994 and 1995, during base flow; spring 1994 was a pilot covering only strata 4, 6, 9 and 11.

**Methods.** Shoreline [[Electrofishing]] was used with a Coffelt WP-15 unit; catch per unit effort (fish/min) was log-transformed for ANOVA and Tukey-Kramer comparisons. Fish under 300 mm TL were treated as potential prey, on the assumption that predators can consume prey up to half their own length. Stomach contents were collected with a [[Seaburg Stomach Sampler]] and analysed by volume using the aggregate percent method. Temperature was recorded at six Colorado River sites and two Gunnison River sites, and converted to [[Annual Thermal Units]] (ATU) following Kaeding and Osmundson (1988), with an optimum growth temperature of 25 °C.

**Temperature gradient.** Main-channel temperatures declined in an upstream direction. ATU values for pikeminnow growth at the Colorado River sites were roughly double near Cameo compared with the Stratum 1 site, and the report concludes that about 40 ATU appears needed to support year-round residence.

**Gunnison River.** Stratum 12 was sampled only through the Burdick (1995) data set, with the same stratification and equipment but a different sampling design. Fish densities there were high, driven largely by bluehead suckers.

**Other environmental context.** The report notes that [[Introduced Fish Species]] (red shiner, fathead minnow, sand shiner) make up most small backwater fish, and that nonnative fish such as common carp and channel catfish were present in downstream strata.

## Key Findings

**Fish abundance and distribution**
- Mean catch rates upstream of Westwater Canyon were about 2–5 fish/electrofishing minute, versus under 1 fish/min downstream.
- Stratum 1 had the lowest catch rate (0.1 fish/min).
- For forage-size fish (100–300 mm), upstream catch rates were about three times those downstream. Stratum 12 had the highest rates, mostly bluehead suckers.
- Downstream forage was dominated by bluehead suckers that were largely confined to riffles; runs were largely unproductive.
- Catch rates in fall 1994 were significantly higher than in spring or fall 1995 across the river as a whole.

**Body condition**
- Body condition of bluehead suckers, flannelmouth suckers and roundtail chubs increased in an upstream direction within the Colorado River.
- Condition was significantly higher in spring than in fall; flannelmouth suckers and roundtail chubs declined significantly by fall of each year.
- Gunnison River suckers had higher condition than Colorado River strata in fall 1994 and spring 1995.

**Diet**
- Organic debris was the largest diet component for all three natives: bluehead sucker 83%, flannelmouth sucker 55%, roundtail chub 43%.
- Invertebrates were the second item for suckers (flannelmouth 41% vs. bluehead 16%); chironomids dominated the bluehead invertebrate fraction.
- Roundtail chubs ate fish (29% of volume), and ephemeropteran larvae were important in strata 10–11.
- Colorado Pikeminnow 400–550 mm TL ate mostly small nonnative minnows (fathead minnow, red shiner, sand shiner); introduced species made up about 40% of diet volume.

**Temperature suitability**
- ATU for pikeminnow growth decreased upstream; at Stratum 10 it was about 84% of Stratum 1 and at Stratum 11 about 77%.
- Adult pikeminnow density increased upstream but dropped sharply above sites where ATU fell below about 40. A Yampa River comparison (rk 188, 41.3 ATU) supported this threshold.
- The Gunnison River temperature regime at Delta averaged about 32 ATU, below Craig (36 ATU), but the Gunnison River ATU at Delta could rise to 46 ATU through selective-withdrawal retrofitting of the Aspinall Unit dams.

**Range extension and carrying capacity**
- Suitable habitat for year-round home ranges was estimated to extend upstream to about Debeque on the Colorado River and about 53 km upstream of the confluence on the Gunnison River.
- Redlands fish passage (1996) opened about 50 km of Gunnison habitat; further temperature augmentation could add about 40 km.
- Planned passage at Price-Stubb, Government-Highline and Grand Valley diversion dams would add about 9.7 km (Price-Stubb), about 8.7 km at the next upstream site, and about 9 km at Government-Highline (figures as reported).
- Preliminary carrying capacity for adult pikeminnow was about 1–5 fish/km (about 3–7 fish/mile) on the Colorado River downstream of Westwater Canyon; the overall system may have supported only about 300 adults before diversion passage. With passage at all diversion dams the estimate rose to about 1,000 adults, and about 1,200 with temperature augmentation.
- The report stresses these estimates are preliminary and should be validated with bioenergetic modeling.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The upper Colorado River system's carrying capacity for adult pikeminnow is probably much lower than generally assumed, limiting population viability.
- Recommended actions: provide passage at Price-Stubb and Government-Highline diversion dams; conduct a feasibility study on increasing Gunnison River temperatures near Delta by modifying Aspinall Unit outlet structures; and carry out bioenergetic studies to refine carrying-capacity estimates.
- The report also recommends management actions to raise river productivity, including reducing fine sediment input and providing flows for substrate flushing and bottomland flooding.
