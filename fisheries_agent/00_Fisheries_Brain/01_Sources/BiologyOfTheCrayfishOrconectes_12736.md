# Biology of the Crayfish *Orconectes causeyi* and Its Use for Aquatic Weed Control in Trout Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This special report from the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, Division of Sport Fisheries (Gallup, New Mexico), by fishery management biologist Jack L. Dean, examines the life history, ecology and weed-control value of the introduced crayfish [[Orconectes Causeyi]]. Submerged aquatic plants limit trout management in many shallow Southwestern lakes, and the crayfish's feeding on these plants was found to restore open water. The study ran from May 1963 to March 1967 and drew on roughly 20,000 crayfish captured from 22 water bodies. The report concludes that *O. causeyi* is an effective weed controller but is not the ideal species for trout lakes; the [[Great Lakes Crayfish]] (*Orconectes propinquus*) is proposed as a promising alternative.

## Background & Description

*Orconectes causeyi* is a large, omnivorous freshwater crayfish. Its introduction to the upper [[Rio Grande]] occurred in 1931, when Sinesio Torres stocked crayfish from the Dry Cimarron River (Arkansas River watershed) into Atrisco Drain south of Albuquerque. By 1966 the species was reported over 150 miles upstream and 200 miles downstream of Albuquerque, and it had also been stocked in the Colorado River watershed of western New Mexico and northeastern Arizona.

Methods included seining, wire and burlap baited traps, hand and dip netting, gill netting, burrow excavation and lake draining. Collections were made monthly except January. Primary sites were [[Nutria Lake]] (70 surface acres) and [[Ramah Lake]] (250 surface acres) in McKinley County, plus [[Mesa Hill Lake]] (55 acres) on Acoma Pueblo. Body length was measured on 4,780 crayfish, carapace length on 716, and weight on 460.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and habitat.** The species occurs from 4,000 ft (Caballo Lake, New Mexico) to 8,400 ft (Horseshoe Lake, Fort Apache Indian Reservation, Arizona). It is established in Morgan Lake, a cooling reservoir for a thermoelectric plant on the Navajo Reservation. Adults tolerated 33–90°F in heated aquaria, and immatures withstood temperatures in the low 90s°F. A single specimen was taken in 70 ft of water at Conchas Lake, so the species is not confined to shallow water. Abundant populations occur where predaceous warm-water fishes such as [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) and [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) are absent or scarce. Rocky bottoms are the preferred substrate, though substantial populations occur in sandy and mud-bottomed streams. Populations do not persist in lakes subject to severe winterkill.

**Density.** A seine estimate at Nutria Lake in September 1965 gave 9,000 crayfish (140 lb/acre) in the littoral zone.

**Water chemistry.** Across eight lakes with *O. causeyi*: pH 7.6–10.73; total dissolved solids 122–1,440 ppm; calcium 5–230 ppm; magnesium 2–73 ppm; sodium 2–239 ppm; bicarbonate 26–308 ppm; chloride 5–230 ppm; sulphate 3–514 ppm. Upper tolerance limits are unknown.

**Diet.** Stomachs from 110 crayfish (five lakes, one stream) were examined; 54% were empty. Contents of the rest were 51% algae, 27% vascular plant fragments, 18% detritus and 4% animal matter (snails, young crayfish, fish). Fresh meat is the most effective bait. Cannibalism occurs when food is scarce.

**Weed control.** Controlled species include slender pondweed (*Potamogeton pusillus*), floating pondweed (*P. natans*), leafy pondweed (*P. foliosus*), coontail (*Ceratophyllum demersum*), white water buttercup (*Ranunculus aquatilis*), elodea (*Elodea canadensis*), water milfoil (*Myriophyllum exalbescens*), muskgrass (*Chara* sp.) and filamentous algae. Smartweed (*Polygonum*), bulrush (*Scirpus*) and cattail (*Typha*) were not controlled. Control was proportional to crayfish density and proximity of rocky substrate.

**Turbidity.** The species does not roil the bottom. Increased turbidity in many lakes was attributed mainly to plankton algae blooms that follow nutrient release after weed removal.

**Burrowing.** *O. causeyi* is a [[Tertiary Burrowers|tertiary burrower]] (Hobbs, 1942 classification). Its burrows are mostly tunnels under rocks, with winter and egg-hatching holes and above-water burrows dug by young of the year. It is not a chimney builder, and it caused no reported damage to dams or irrigation ditches.

## Key Findings

**Weed control case histories**
- *Nutria Lake:* crayfish were scarce in 1961 and the lake was nearly choked with slender pondweed. Crayfish became common by 1963, pondweed was cut loose and eliminated from shallow water by mid-August 1963, and the lake was essentially weed-free by August 1966.
- *Ramah Lake:* the lake was nearly choked with pondweed in 1963. Weed-free shoals appeared by 1964, and by 1966 the south end was kept free of pondweeds while the north end retained reduced beds.
- *Mesa Hill Lake:* a long history of weed infestation ended as crayfish became moderately abundant in 1965. Fertilization to produce shading algae failed to control weeds.
- Five lakes without crayfish served as controls; their submerged weeds stayed abundant except where early algal blooms intervened.

**Reproduction and life history**
- Eggs are laid late March to early May and hatch late May to June after 6–9 weeks. Young remain attached for 7–12 days.
- Sexual maturity occurs at 14–15 months, between mid-August and mid-September of the second summer.
- Ovarian egg production averages 588 per female (range 142–1,237), greater than any other North American crayfish cited (see [[Comparative Ovarian Egg Counts]]). Fertile pleopod eggs averaged 415, implying about a 29% loss.
- Sex ratio is 1:1 for immatures, but adult males outnumber females 1.5:1 (sex-ratio collection data give 150 males to 100 females).
- Most females die at about 24–25 months after their first brood. About 8% survive to produce a second brood; maximum female lifespan is about 37 months. Maximum male lifespan is about 37–38 months, and only about 12% of males survive their third summer.

**Size**
- The largest male and female each measured 4.9 in body length. The largest male had a 63 mm carapace and weighed 0.22 lb; the largest female weighed 0.16 lb.
- Stunting was noted at River Reservoir, Apache County, Arizona, where the population was abundant.

**Burrowing and hibernation**
- Young hibernate from 1 September to 11 May. Burrowing intensifies in September, and adults begin about a month after the young.

**Recommendations**
- The ideal trout-lake crayfish would be primarily vegetarian, small to moderate in size (carapace ≤45 mm), highly reproductive, able to persist under trout predation, and non-burrowing.
- *O. causeyi* meets the weed-control and population goals, but its large size (many young are too large for trout at 3–4 months) and burrowing (which leaves few young for trout in cooler months) are main disadvantages.
- *Orconectes propinquus* appears promising: it is vegetarian, does not exceed 40 mm carapace, is a non-burrower except in winter, and reportedly was the most common crayfish in rainbow trout stomachs in Michigan. Its main drawback is that it does not occur in small ponds.
- Combining two or more species may best balance weed control and trout forage.
