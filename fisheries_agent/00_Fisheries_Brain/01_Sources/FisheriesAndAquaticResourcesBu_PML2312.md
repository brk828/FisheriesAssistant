# USGS Fisheries Aquatic Ecosystem Research Program Newsletter

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-science

## Executive Summary

This issue of the [[Fisheries and Aquatic Ecosystem Research]] (FAER) program newsletter, produced with the USGS [[Assistant Program Coordinator]], combines outreach announcements with short research summaries. The main items are: a call for outreach contributions for the upcoming [[American Fisheries Society]] annual meeting; fish passage studies of [[American Shad]] at the [[Turners Falls Fishway]] on the [[Connecticut River]]; the parasitic [[Asian Fish Tapeworm]] and its effects on endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]]; a laboratory model of [[Columnaris Disease]] in several fish species; the calcein marking technique; mussel safety testing of aquaculture drugs; new publications; funding opportunities; and staff updates.

## Background & Description

The newsletter is aimed at FAER scientists and their partners. It asks researchers to contribute project posters, fact sheets, CDs, and bookmarks for distribution at the American Fisheries Society meeting and at science centers. It also points to [[USGS Science Centers]] product websites as examples for outreach materials. The issue is an informational bulletin, not a research report, so its items vary in scope and depth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Connecticut River migration:** Researchers at the [[USGS S.O. Conte Anadromous Fish Research Center]] in Turners Falls, Massachusetts, have studied shad migration and passage for about 15 years. Historic data indicate that about half of the fish passing through the fish lift at [[Holyoke Dam]] reached a distance of about 40 miles upstream. Less than 25 percent of shad that reach Turners Falls on average successfully pass the dam. Shad that ascend the lowermost fishway also use the [[Power Canal]], where they may exit the canal and move past the dam.
- **Colorado River fishes:** The Asian fish tapeworm was introduced into the US and is linked to disease and death in hatchery-raised carp. It infects endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] in the Colorado River basin.
- **Thermal shock:** Small fish swept from the warm Little Colorado River (23.5 °C) into the cold Colorado River (9.5 °C) during monsoon flooding showed stress responses similar to those from tapeworm infection.

## Key Findings

### Fish Passage at Turners Falls
- Shad passage studies have run for about 15 years.
- Less than 25 percent of shad reaching Turners Falls on average pass the dam successfully.
- Researchers are working with utilities to design and test fishway lighting, hydraulics, and a prototype fishway entrance near the Turners Falls gatehouse and dam. Permanent improvements are expected within several years.

### Asian Fish Tapeworm in Endangered Chub
- Infection of humpback chub (10 months old and older) produced no negative effects in the initial trials.
- In smaller juvenile fish (2.5 months old), infected fish reduced growth by as much as roughly 9 percent compared with controls by the end of a 24-week study. (The percentage is hard to read in the scan.)
- When food was restricted, infected bonytail chub began dying 20 days earlier and at nearly twice the rate of uninfected fish on a low ration. Infected fish also had significantly lower visceral fat reserves.
- Larger chub can become infected by eating infected smaller fish (postcyclic infection), in contrast to the conventional route through infected copepod intermediate hosts.
- These results are intended to inform resource managers and ecological models of parasite transmission and possible impacts if the tapeworm invades other ecosystems.

### Columnaris Disease Model
- Researchers at the [[USGS Upper Midwest Environmental Sciences Center]] and the USFWS [[La Crosse Fish Health Center]] are developing a disease exposure model. It is supported by a Multistate Conservation Grant from the US Fish and Wildlife Service, coordinated through the [[International Association of Fish and Wildlife Agencies]].
- *Flavobacterium columnare* is a gram-negative bacterium that causes acute to chronic mortality in nearly all cultured freshwater fish. No successful therapy has yet been developed.
- An isolate from a channel catfish outbreak at Rathbun State Fish Hatchery (Iowa) was used to challenge rainbow trout, walleye, and channel catfish. Fish were abraded and then immersed in bacterial suspensions.
- Mortality and time to death decreased as challenge concentration and duration increased for all three species (P < 0.01).
- Therapy must start within 24 hours of challenge to be effective. Delays beyond 24 hours may allow infections to become too advanced for treatment to work.
- Contact: [[Mark Gaikowski]].

### Calcein Marking
- Calcein binds to calcium phosphate in calcified tissue and can be detected under proper light wavelengths without lethal sampling.
- Calcein has an [[Investigational New Animal Drug]] (INAD) exemption for immersion marking of fish under 2 g.
- Compared with tetracycline, calcein has many similar properties but is not an antibiotic.
- Dietary calcein has produced single and multiple marks in brook trout, Atlantic and coho salmon, and yellow perch. Two marks can be seen after two doses. Species differences and non-specific binding to feed components are noted.
- Research objectives: determine feed formulations and dose levels, how long marks last as fish grow, and mark resistance to ultraviolet degradation.
- Contact: [[Dale Honeyfield]], USGS Northern Appalachian Research Laboratory.

### Freshwater Mussel Drug Safety
- USGS and USFWS are evaluating five common water-borne aquaculture drugs for safety to freshwater mussels.
- Preliminary results on three drugs suggest they can be used to prevent disease in host fish during mussel culture without harming larvae.
- Contact: [[Jeff Rich]].
