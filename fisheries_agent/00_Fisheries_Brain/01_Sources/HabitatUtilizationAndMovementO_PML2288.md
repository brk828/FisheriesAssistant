# Habitat Utilization and Movement of Fish as Determined by Radio-Telemetry

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1983 field study used [[Radio Telemetry]] to track the habitat use and movements of four fish species in [[Cedar Lake]], Illinois: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Pumpkinseed]]. Fish were located by [[Triangulation]] at five-minute intervals and assigned to five habitat types. Largemouth bass and bluegill showed significant habitat selection, with largemouth bass favoring the [[Ecotone Habitat]] and bluegill favoring heavy [[Macrophyte Beds]]. Habitat use by pumpkinseed and yellow perch was not significantly different, mainly because sample sizes were small. Movements were oriented roughly perpendicular to the shore and macrophyte beds, a pattern the authors linked to visual landmark use. The authors suggest that available forage and predator-prey interactions, rather than predator avoidance, were the main drivers of habitat use.

## Background & Description

**Objective.** The authors aimed to define habitat utilization in a manner similar to studies of mammals and birds, and to test whether fish movements differed among habitats. Earlier work by [[Savitz and Stein 1982]] found that largemouth bass moved more in open habitats than in dense vegetation, reflecting a shift from searching and following prey to ambush predation in dense cover.

**Study species and sampling.** Fish were captured by seine in Cedar Lake and fitted with external transmitters, then released at their capture site after one or two days. Tracking was carried out between May and December, with the monitored periods varying by species:

| Species | Individuals tracked | Tracking period | Length range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Largemouth Bass | 10 (movement data for 8) | May to December | 21.5–37.6 cm |
| Bluegill | 9 (movement data for 7) | May to November | 15.5–19.0 cm |
| Yellow Perch | 4 (movement data for 3) | May to December | 19.8–22.5 cm |
| Pumpkinseed | 4 | August to November | 13.8–16.0 cm |

The fish were not reproductively active. Transmitters weighed 4.5 g or 2.6 g, depending on the size of the mercury battery. To avoid problems with buoyancy, transmitters were positioned on the fish's exterior so that they approached neutral buoyancy. Earlier observations had shown that yellow perch fitted with whip antennas became entangled in vegetation; the present transmitters lacked such antennas and no entanglement was observed.

**Tracking methods.** Two observers on shore, at pre-selected stations, took compass bearings on each fish at five-minute intervals. Average triangulation error was 0.7 m at 30 m from shore and 1.7 m at 100 m. Most fish with movement data were within 30 m of shore. When fish could not be located from shore, observers used a boat positioned directly over the fish; boats were used mainly in water deeper than about 2 m or in macrophyte beds, so their effect on fish behavior was probably minimal. A loop antenna produced no detectable error in fish location, but it was used only for habitat data, not movement data.

**Study area.** An enlarged, scaled map of Cedar Lake was built from a lake survey. It included 32 shore stations with measured distances between them, and the dimensions of four habitat types were recorded and mapped.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat classification.** Five habitat types were defined using depth and macrophyte criteria: heavy macrophyte (dense growth), light macrophyte (sparse growth), sand, open water (roughly 6 m with no macrophyte growth), and [[Ecotone Habitat]]. The ecotone was the transition zone between macrophyte beds and open water or sand. The authors noted that the average triangulation error at 30 m (about 0.7 m) was smaller than half the ecotone width, so fish could reliably be placed in or out of the ecotone.

**Lake habitat composition.** Across the lake, the proportion of each habitat was approximately: heavy macrophyte 8.7%, light macrophyte 27.2%, sand 31.9%, open water 23.3%, and the mixed macrophyte/sand or open-water ecotone 8.9%.

**Behavioral context.** The authors suggest that visual landmarks, such as shore and macrophyte edges, guided fish orientation. Earlier work on minnows, green sunfish and largemouth bass also showed visual landmark orientation, and this study's movement patterns were consistent with that idea. Fish did not appear to orient toward a particular compass direction, since largemouth bass in the western and eastern parts of the lake moved in different directions.

**Prey and predators.** [[Johnny Darter]] (*Etheostoma nigrum*) was common, especially at the edges of macrophyte beds. Yellow perch habitat use may have been shaped by competition with largemouth bass, since both fed heavily on darters. [[Northern Pike]] predation could also have influenced yellow perch movement and habitat use, as reported in earlier studies, but the other species were large enough to reduce that risk.

**Interpretive caveats.** The authors emphasized that habitat use measured as the proportion of locations in each habitat cannot show whether a habitat is used more than its availability would predict. They recommended using available habitat (within a home range or the whole lake) as the baseline for comparisons across lakes or seasons. Habitat use may vary with season, temperature, time of day and fish size, so the results apply specifically to the sizes and conditions studied.

## Key Findings

### Habitat utilization

- **Largemouth bass:** Habitat use differed significantly (χ² = 18.30, p < 0.05). The [[Ecotone Habitat]] was ranked highest overall and differed significantly (p < 0.05) from all other habitats, although the ecotone and sand were not separated at the 5% level. Individual variation was large: the ecotone was the top-ranked habitat for seven fish, heavy macrophyte for two, and sand for one.
- **Bluegill:** Habitat use also differed significantly (χ² = 16.11, p < 0.05). Heavy macrophyte was ranked highest, but its use was not significantly different (p > 0.05) from light macrophyte, sand or ecotone. For seven bluegill heavy macrophyte was top-ranked, while for two it was sand or ecotone.
- **Pumpkinseed (χ² = 7.14) and yellow perch (χ² = 6.65):** Neither showed significant habitat differences (p > 0.05), largely because of small sample sizes. Pumpkinseed showed large individual variation, and yellow perch were found more often in macrophyte habitats.

### Tracking effort

- Largemouth bass: 138 tracking days (404 locations)
- Yellow perch: 59 tracking days (146 locations)
- Bluegill: 67 tracking days (174 locations)
- Pumpkinseed: 38 tracking days (154 locations)

### Movement speed

- Speeds varied widely within every habitat for all four species. No correction was made for triangulation error, which was small relative to the speed variation.
- Average speeds across all habitats were approximately 20 body lengths/min for bluegill, 14 body lengths/min for pumpkinseed and 14 body lengths/min for largemouth bass. Bluegill were considered the most active species on this basis. The yellow perch average was not legible in the scanned text.
- Highest average speeds occurred in heavy macrophyte habitat: largemouth bass 19.2 body lengths/min, bluegill 22.5 body lengths/min, and pumpkinseed 33.8 body lengths/min. Pumpkinseed moved significantly faster in heavy macrophyte than elsewhere (p ≤ 0.05), although only two pumpkinseed were located there.
- Speed differences among habitats were not significant (p > 0.05) for largemouth bass, yellow perch or bluegill.

### Movement direction

- All four species moved predominantly in directions nearly perpendicular to the shore and macrophyte beds.
- Movement directions differed significantly between the eastern and southwestern parts of the lake (chi-square tests, p < 0.001 in all sectors reported), consistent with a landmark-based orientation rather than a fixed compass heading.

### Turning angles

- Largemouth bass turns were concentrated between 160° and 180°, meaning the fish often reversed direction. This differed significantly from other turning angles (p < 0.05). The authors suggested that this search pattern kept bass near their previous search area and may relate to predation on Johnny Darter.
- Bluegill, pumpkinseed and yellow perch showed no significant differences among turning angles (p > 0.05).

### Interpretation

- Largemouth bass use of the ecotone suggests an ambush strategy at the edge of cover, consistent with earlier work by [[Savitz and Stein 1982]] and with [[Predator-Prey Interaction]] at vegetation edges.
- Available forage, especially prey density, was considered the main driver of habitat use. Predator avoidance was judged less likely to explain habitat choices, since the fish were large enough to limit predation risk. The authors also suggested that largemouth bass would prefer more profitable prey over the small fish and surfish available.
- Similar habitat patterns were reported in other studies of the same species, though the authors emphasized that habitat use patterns depend on the relative area of each habitat in each lake.
