# Predicting Depth Distribution of Fish in Three TVA Storage-Type Reservoirs

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #limnology #Centrarchidae #Percidae

## Executive Summary
This 1948 study by Jack S. Dendy (TVA) details a predictive service developed to inform anglers of the vertical (depth) distribution of [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Walleye]], and [[Sauger]] in three Tennessee Valley Authority reservoirs: [[Norris Reservoir]], [[Douglas Reservoir]], and [[Cherokee Reservoir]]. Utilizing gillnet data collected from 1943–1945, researchers correlated fish depth distribution with water temperature and dissolved oxygen (DO) concentrations. Weekly/bi-weekly predictions were published in local Knoxville newspapers during the summer of 1946. The study confirmed that while initial conditions might be similar across reservoirs, differing thermal stratification and oxygen depletion rates caused fish distribution to diverge over the season. Feedback from fishing dock managers indicated the service significantly improved catch success, particularly for anglers unfamiliar with storage reservoir dynamics.

## Background & Description
This paper is ecological/limnological in focus rather than morphological. However, key physical data points relevant to capture methodology were noted:
*   **Gear Selectivity:** Standard 8-foot gill nets were used for sampling.
*   **Sauger/Walleye Vertical Position:** Over half of the [[Walleye]] and [[Sauger]] captured were found in the lower third of the 8-foot gill net, suggesting a strong benthic (bottom-oriented) affinity for these Percid species compared to Largemouth Bass.
*   **Angling Implication:** Due to this benthic tendency, the paper notes that trolling or still-fishing lures must be positioned within 2-3 feet of the bottom to effectively target these species, regardless of the general depth prediction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The study establishes distinct thermal and chemical niches for the three target species based on gillnet distribution data (Fig. 2, 1943 Norris data):

*   **[[Largemouth Bass]] (Huro salmoides):**
    *   Niche: Epilimnion (upper warm water layer).
    *   Thermal Preference: Found in the upper layer where temperatures were 80° F. or above during late summer.
    *   Behavior: In spring, they feed actively near the surface; as the epilimnion thickens through summer, they distribute throughout this warm layer, requiring a shift from surface lures to deep trolling.

*   **[[Walleye]] (Stizostedion vitreum):**
    *   Niche: Intermediate depths (Metalimnion/Thermocline).
    *   Thermal Preference: Generally found in water around 77° F. in late July.
    *   Anomaly: Occasionally found in unusually warm water (e.g., Aug 16, Sept 6) due to oxygen shortages displacing them from their preferred cooler strata.

*   **[[Sauger]] (Stizostedion canadense):**
    *   Niche: Hypolimnion (deep, cool water).
    *   Thermal Preference: Found in cooler water, around 65° F. in late July.
    *   Oxygen Tolerance: Notably tolerant of low dissolved oxygen concentrations, surviving in levels of 1.5 p.p.m. or less in cool water (60-65° F.), allowing them to occupy deep strata often avoided by other species.

**Environmental Drivers:**
The primary drivers of fish distribution were **Thermal Stratification** and **Dissolved Oxygen (DO)** availability. In reservoirs with ample DO throughout the water column, fish distribution was purely a function of temperature preference. However, in reservoirs experiencing oxygen depletion in the hypolimnion (common in [[Douglas Reservoir]] and [[Cherokee Reservoir]]), fish were forced into higher, warmer strata than their physiological optimum, or concentrated in specific oxygen-rich pockets, complicating simple temperature-based predictions.

## Key Findings
*   **Divergent Predictions:** Although the first prediction (June 2, 1946) showed identical depth distributions for all three reservoirs, subsequent predictions diverged significantly due to varying rates of thermal stratification and inflow/discharge volumes specific to each reservoir.
*   **Density Currents:** The research built upon earlier work (Wiebe, 1940; Eschmeyer & Tarzwell, 1941) demonstrating that "density currents" of oxygen-poor water significantly influence fish distribution, explaining previously "unexpected" poor fishing quality in specific areas/depths of [[Norris Reservoir]].
*   **Spring Fishing Correlation:** Water temperature closely dictates fishing quality in early spring; upstream reservoir areas warm faster, leading to better fishing earlier in the season compared to downstream/deep areas. Optimal angling activity begins around 60° F.
*   **Methodology:** Data collection relied on standard limnological methods, including resistance thermometers for temperature and the Winkler method for dissolved oxygen, sampled at 5 or 10-foot intervals primarily from bridges.
*   **Efficacy of Public Outreach:** A survey of 6 fishing docks on Norris Reservoir revealed that 5 managers/guides believed the newspaper-published predictions improved angler success. The one dissenting dock catered primarily to out-of-state (Kentucky) anglers who lacked access to the local Knoxville publications, highlighting a distribution/access gap rather than a flaw in the scientific methodology.
*   **Inter-Lake Variability:** The paper acknowledges (citing Hile and Juday, 1941) that the relationship between temperature, DO, and fish distribution is not universally consistent across all lakes, cautioning that this specific TVA model requires refinement before broader application to other water bodies.
