# Razorback Sucker Early Life History and Survival Study Plan (National Biological Service, 1996-1998)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1996 draft study plan from the [[National Biological Service]] (NBS) Midcontinent Ecological Science Center, submitted to Dr. Paul Marsh (Arizona State University) for peer review, with a comment deadline of May 17, 1996. The plan, titled *Advancing Knowledge on Critical Early Life History and Survival of the Endangered Razorback Sucker*, aims to determine how river flow regulation and nonnative predators affect the survival and growth of larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a federally endangered species listed in 1990. Work is planned for FY1996-FY1998 with a total study budget of $89.0K (NBS) plus $69.0K (NPS/other outside funding).

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a large-river catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It has disappeared from most of its native range; the largest and possibly only reproducing population was documented on the Green and Yampa Rivers in Utah and Colorado (Tyus 1987; Tyus and Karp 1990). Historically, larval husbandry and sampling methods were poorly developed. Fine-mesh seines and drift nets caused high larval mortality, so the [[National Park Service]] (NPS) funded testing of non-lethal sampling, notably [[Light Trap]] designs. Trials at the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] (LFL), [[Colorado State University]], found that floating, low-intensity, quatrefoil-style traps were most effective.

Captive-rearing work at [[Lake Mohave]] has been substantial. Larval collections rose from 12,000 (1993) to 21,000 (1995) and 68,000 in the current year. Larvae are reared at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], where survival to stocking size is approximately 25%. Fish support augmentation of an aging reservoir population and supply brood stock to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Reservoir operations have altered natural spring hydrographs in the upper Colorado system. [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] on the Green River and [[Aspinall Unit]] releases on the Gunnison River reduce spring peak flows, shorten flow durations, and shift peak timing. Lower peaks limit river-floodplain connectivity in most years, reducing nursery habitat. Nonnative predators compound the problem. The most abundant nonnative predator, the [[Red Shiner]], has a mouth gap that larvae outgrow at about 26 mm TL. Larvae at this size also gain swimming speed and sub-terminal mouths suited to benthic prey such as chironomid larvae.

Hydrology shapes nursery conditions. Green River flows usually peaked 10-14 days after the Yampa River, lengthening the flow duration. The authors hypothesize that longer inundation (six to seven weeks) in backwater habitats improves growth and recruitment. Planned floodplain restoration in the Jensen and Ouray reaches (beginning Fall 1996) depends on this information. The [[Biological Opinion]] on Flaming Gorge re-operation, due in 1997, adds urgency.

## Key Findings

**Background and results from prior work**

- Larval sampling in 1993 documented successful reproduction near spawning areas in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] and downstream in [[Canyonlands National Park]] and [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]]. A total of 122 larvae were collected from flooded tributary mouths near Anderson Bottom in Canyonlands; 15 larvae were collected from the Colorado River inflow to Lake Powell, indicating downstream transport.
- In the following year, 47 larvae were collected from flooded tributary backwaters in Canyonlands, with a size range of 11-16 mm TL, suggesting some larvae survived into their second or third month.
- Larval captures in 1995 were poor. Cold weather and water temperatures may have delayed spawning or flushed larvae downstream. No larvae were collected in Canyonlands, and only 47 were taken on the Green River, compared with 1,200+ the previous year.

**Objectives (FY1996-1997)**

1. Document spatial and temporal distribution and abundance of razorback larvae through standardized light-trap surveys in the Lake Powell inflow and Canyonlands.
2. Evaluate size- and time-dependent survival and growth of larval native suckers (razorback, flannelmouth, and bluehead) under predation by red shiners, food availability, and flow conditions. Test field mark-recapture techniques.
3. Assess the feasibility of blocking nonnative fish access to nursery habitats with 2-mm mesh block nets.
4. Transport and rear larvae for genetic analysis and selection for brood stock, stocking, and telemetry research stock.
5. Implant juvenile suckers (35 cm) with radio/sonic tags to track habitat use and movement (telemetry deferred to FY1998 and a refined FY1997 addendum).

**Methods**

- Light traps: at least ten set each night, supplemented by high-intensity halogen underwater lights (Lake Mohave method) and fine-mesh seining as a last resort.
- Captured larvae are anesthetized with tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222). Handling follows IACUC (1988) guidelines.
- Enclosure experiment: six 1 m³ enclosures (500-micron mesh) at Millard Canyon and Anderson Bottom-Bonita Bend, each stocked with 50 tetracycline-marked larvae per trial and 5-10 adult red shiners in three enclosures per site. Trials last seven days; otolith marks are analyzed to age larvae.
- Statistical analysis includes chi-square tests, non-parametric multiple comparisons, and time-series regression.

**Budget and schedule**

- FY96 total $30.0K; FY97 total $19.0K; FY98 $40.0K. Total study $89.0K (NBS) and $69.0K (NPS/other), with $34.0K outside funding for NPS support.
- Progress reports are planned for 1996 and 1997, with a final report and oral presentations to the Recovery Program.
- Principal investigators are [[Gordon Mueller]] (NBS), [[Ed Wick]] (NPS), and [[Robert Muth]] and Steven Meismer (LFL). Recommended users include NPS superintendents, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery staff, Bureau of Reclamation regional offices, and the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources.

**Review status**

- The document is an evaluation request. The reviewer must rate the plan on objectives, relevance, methods, scientific soundness, data analysis, budget, and time frame, and provide an overall rating of Good, Fair, or Questionable. Review comments go to the Center Director.
- Internal approval forms show that the plan still needed IACUC review and statistician review before final approval, and the telemetry phase was not yet fully defined.
