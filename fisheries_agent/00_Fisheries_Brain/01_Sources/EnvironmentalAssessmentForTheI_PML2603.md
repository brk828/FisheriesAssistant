# Environmental Assessment: Wiltbank Grazing Permits on the Sprucedale-Reno Allotment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This draft Environmental Assessment (EA) by the USDA Forest Service, Southwestern Region, evaluates whether to issue a 10-year livestock grazing permit to W. Wiltbank (and to analyze E. Wiltbank's co-permit) on the [[Sprucedale-Reno Allotment]] of the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]], Alpine Ranger District, Arizona. The allotment covers approximately 38,000 acres. Three alternatives are analyzed: [[Alternative A]] (no grazing), [[Alternative B]] (current-season permit with livestock numbers exceeding estimated capacity), and [[Alternative C]] (stocking balanced with capacity, the preferred alternative). The EA addresses soils, watershed, vegetation, riparian condition, water and air quality, threatened, endangered and sensitive wildlife including [[Apache Trout]], economics, social factors and cultural resources.

## Background & Description

- **Permittees:** W. Wiltbank (typically 280 cow/calf pairs, May to October, deferred grazing) and E. Wiltbank (typically 8 cow/calf pairs plus 47 horses, year-long use).
- **Proposed action (modified):** Original 8/95 scoping proposal (July 15 to October 31 season) was modified to retain the current season, via Alternative B.
- **Purpose and need:** Authorize grazing at a level and timing that maintains satisfactory resource conditions and improves unsatisfactory ones, consistent with the [[Endangered Species Act]], the [[Clean Water Act]] and other legal mandates.
- **Decision:** The Forest Supervisor decides whether to issue a 10-year term permit. A separate [[Allotment Management Plan]] analysis would follow.
- **Significant issues:** (1) permitted use must be in balance with grazing capacity, and (2) grazing season must suit site-specific conditions, since early-season grazing overuses small areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Soils and watershed.** Soil stability was assessed with the Universal Soil Loss Equation from the Terrestrial Ecosystems Survey. Satisfactory-condition soils cover about 91% of the allotment currently, projected at 100% under Alternative A, 96% under Alternative B and 100% under Alternative C. Litter from dense canopy protects soils, and meadows and riparian areas make up only about 11% of the allotment. Watersheds (Upper and Lower Black River 5th-code, and 6th-code watersheds of the Apache trout streams) are rated satisfactory.

**Vegetation.** Stocking was based on allotment-wide production, but use concentrates in riparian areas and meadows. Spring-early summer grazing has shifted composition from sedges and native cool-season grasses to [[Kentucky Bluegrass]], rushes and forbs, and has heavily utilized woody riparian species. Desirable species such as Arizona fescue and mountain muhly can sustain about 40% (by weight) allowable use under a deferred system. Vegetation changes in dense ponderosa pine and mixed-conifer stands further reduce forage.

**Riparian condition.** Of nine drainages (about 30 miles) in the allotment, 95% of riparian habitat is in unsatisfactory condition. Alternatives B and C are expected to improve conditions gradually, with C recovering faster; full satisfactory condition is considered unlikely under any alternative.

**Water and air quality.** The [[Arizona Department of Environmental Quality]] 1994 assessment classed the [[Black River]] as "partially limited," with nitrates, pH and turbidity as limiting factors; Reservation and Conklin creeks had no limiting factors, and Double Cienega Creek had too few parameters to assess. Air sheds in the Alpine District are in attainment.

**Threatened, endangered and proposed species.** Five listed species were identified: [[Mexican Gray Wolf]] (endangered, extirpated from Arizona), [[American Peregrine Falcon]] (endangered; one known eyrie within the allotment), [[Bald Eagle]] (threatened; key wintering habitat along the Black River), [[Mexican Spotted Owl]] (threatened; 21 PACs covering about 7,300 acres, and 33,535 acres, about 71% of the allotment, designated critical habitat), and [[Jaguar]] (proposed endangered; transient only).

**Apache Trout.** The allotment contains seven of thirty recovery populations (about 20 stream miles), including Conklin, Corduroy, Double Cienega, Fish, Reservation, Snake and Soldier creeks. Habitat improvement fencing is planned for critical reaches.

**Sensitive species.** Of 39 sensitive species that may occur, six were flagged as potentially trending toward listing under current grazing: [[Northern Water Shrew]], [[Northern Goshawk]], [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]], [[Northern Leopard Frog]], [[Narrowheaded Garter Snake]] and [[Roundtail Chub]].

**Economics.** Estimated direct and indirect jobs are 3.7 under Alternative B and 3.5 under Alternative C. Payments to counties are about $813 (B) and $458 (C) before PILT offset. Actual use has averaged 86% of permitted use over ten years. Greenlee County received $432,187 in 1994 Forest Service receipts, of which these five permits accounted for about 1%.

**Social and cultural.** Ranching is a long-standing lifestyle in the Southwest. About 2,260 acres of the [[Bear Wallow Wilderness]] lie within the allotment, which receives about 3,000 visitors a year, and livestock presence is a recreation conflict. No known standing ruins or historic structures would be affected; Section 106 consultation applies to future range structures.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking exceeds capacity under current permit.** Alternative B keeps numbers that exceed estimated capacity; utilization standards would therefore shorten the season, and the draft notes that Alternative B's economic benefit is roughly equal to the current permit.
- **Alternative C is the preferred alternative.** It balances stocking with capacity, adds a July 15 start, and yields the greatest long-term habitat recovery short of no grazing. It reduces jobs and county payments relative to Alternative B.
- **Alternative A (no grazing)** would best serve soils, riparian recovery and wildlife, but it eliminates the grazing-dependent ranch operation and its jobs and tax base.
- **Apache trout effects.** Alternative B is judged likely to adversely affect Apache trout and its habitat because excess livestock would continue to reach unfenced reaches. Alternative C is also judged likely to adversely affect the species, with habitat improvement less than under Alternative A but greater than under B.
- **Mexican spotted owl and peregrine falcon.** Both alternatives B and C are judged not likely to adversely affect the species or its critical habitat, provided 40% upland and 45% riparian use standards and PAC protections (no mineral sites or gathers in PACs) are implemented.
- **Northern water shrew, roundtail chub, and other sensitive species.** Alternative B could contribute to a trend toward federal listing in combination with similar ASNF permits. Alternative C would maintain or improve habitat.
- **Monitoring.** Paired exclosure plots, utilization cages in key areas within about half a mile of water, and movement triggers at maximum utilization form the monitoring plan (Appendix B).
