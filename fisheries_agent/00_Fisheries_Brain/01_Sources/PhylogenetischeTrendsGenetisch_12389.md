# Phylogenetic Trends from a Genetic Perspective

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This is a 1959 theoretical essay by [[Curt Kosswig]] (Zoologisches Staatsinstitut und Museum, Hamburg), written from a colloquium presentation on phylogenetic trends. It argues that the genetic basis of evolutionary change can be explained by ordinary mutation, polygenic inheritance and [[Pleiotropy]], without invoking special "orthogenetic" forces. The main case study is the sexual characters of live-bearing toothcarps (Poeciliidae), especially the sword of [[Xiphophorus Helleri]] and the [[Gonopodium]]. The essay also engages with [[Stammer]]'s claim that parasites evolve under reduced selection, and with [[Remane]]'s demand for rapidly appearing complex structures. The document is a discussion piece, not an empirical study, and reports no new sample sizes or statistics.

## Background & Description

Kosswig frames genetics as an empirical tool for evolutionary questions. Two sources of data are used: laboratory "domestic" organisms such as [[Drosophila Melanogaster]], and crosses between natural populations, subspecies, species or genera. Crosses between natural forms rarely give simple Mendelian ratios because most differences depend on many genes acting together (polygenic or polymeric inheritance). Mutations are treated as undirected changes at a locus. Most "large" mutations are loss-of-function mutations that halt a reaction chain early. The essay argues that the differences between natural forms are quantitative, with each gene contributing a small effect.

Kosswig also discusses the concept of selective value. Selective value can only be measured relative to an alternative allele under the same conditions. Apparent neutrality requires excluding drift, population bottlenecks and balancing selection. Selective value should be attributed to genes or allele combinations, not to traits as such.

The essay identifies two categories of group:
- **Progressive groups** are rich in species and adaptable to varied ecological conditions. Examples given include teleost fishes.
- **Regressive (relict) groups** have few species and occupy stable habitats. Examples given include the crossopterygians.

Parasites, sessile animals and passively dispersed plankton are described as degraded groups, following [[Sewertzoff]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Selection and environment.** Kosswig notes that the selective value of a gene changes when the environment changes or when mutations elsewhere in the genome alter developmental conditions. For the [[Drosophila Pseudoobscura]] inversion heterozygotes, the heterotic advantage is linked to a [[Chromosomal Inversion]] that prevents [[Crossing Over]] within a block of co-adapted genes. The selective conditions appear to correlate with seasons, but the physiological mechanism is unknown.

**Parasites.** Stammer's argument is that parasites, which the essay describes as about one quarter of all known animal species, evolve under reduced selection and so can follow freer morphological paths. Kosswig doubts that the host environment imposes less demanding requirements on parasites, since these requirements are poorly understood. He instead attributes orthoevolution to directed mutation probabilities and Wright-type chance.

**Relict forms and stability.** A relict form may be so well adapted to a long-constant environment that new mutants cannot improve on it. Genes also differ in mutation rate, and some mutation rates depend on the genotype itself, giving a second route to genetic stability. In progressive groups, greater mutability is expected to arise mainly from internal causes.

**Canalization by the genetic background.** Following [[Baltzer]], the essay argues that developmental pathways and the adult organization of the ancestor channel evolutionary change, so that novelties must relate to the existing lineage. Kosswig calls this a "restricted directedness" (*beschränkt zielbedingt*). Genes that act early in development are strongly bound to the [[Restgenotype]], which limits the directions available for change.

**Speciation.** Sexual barriers can arise after adaptive divergence, as in ring species, or can appear first in a panmictic population. In the latter case, the split groups are expected to diverge because it is unlikely that identical mutations occur in both.

## Key Findings

**1. Polygenic architecture and mutation.** Natural differences are mostly polygenic and quantitative. The essay argues that mutation alone can produce the genetic differences found between natural forms, and no special type of inheritance is needed.

**2. Sword of Xiphophorus helleri is not a mating requirement.**
- The sword is a Xiphophorus male secondary sexual trait, inherited from several additively acting genes, and passed on by females.
- Males with the sword amputated still mate, and one successful pairing can yield several monthly broods, since sperm remain viable in the oviduct for months.
- In nature, *X. helleri* and *P. maculatus* are sympatric and often ecologically overlapping, yet do not hybridize readily. The natural barrier is attributed to the lower viability of heterospecific sperm in the oviduct, not to the sword.
- Hybrids can produce males without a sword that court as effectively as sworded males.
- Sword development and sword use are inherited independently (Gerschler).
- Sword length varies among species: long in *X. helleri*, shorter with guanophores in *X. montezumae*, absent in *X. pygmaeus*. Reduced caudal extensions occur in *P. xiphidium* and *P. variatus*.
- Testosterone induces a sword-like extension in *P. maculatus* females and males (Grobstein; Dzwillo). The normal hormone level is insufficient to activate the sword genes in this species.

**3. Gonopodium evolution.** The gonopodium is a modified anal fin, and its structure varies gradually among species, providing a graded system for reconstructing phylogenetic relationships (Regan). Individual gonopodial features are polygenic with no clear adaptive value, which the essay presents as evidence that polygenic systems stabilize traits and support phylogenetic irreversibility.

**4. Independent origin of viviparity.** Following [[Hubbs]], gonopodia in cyprinodontiform fishes arose independently at least four times among American families (Poeciliidae, Jenynsiidae, Anablepidae, Tomeuridae). Viviparity itself has arisen separately in these families, and the nutrition of embryos differs between Anablepidae/Poeciliidae and Jenynsiidae. The Indian Horaichthyidae have a separately derived gonopodium that is used only for sperm transfer. Hubbs proposed that oviparous cyprinodontids retain the potential to evolve viviparity, which is supported by clasping structures in many oviparous species.

**5. Pre-adaptation and novelty.** Kosswig cites REMANE's example of tits, chaffinches and mountain finches at feeders, and the House Sparrow hovering to take insects. Such already present but rarely used abilities can open new evolutionary directions. He argues that "pre-adaptation" (Cuénot) is a legitimate concept, not a mystical one.

**6. Stable and reversible traits.** Multiple non-additive polygenes make a trait stable and contribute to phylogenetic irreversibility. Gene substitution (where one gene replaces another for the same function) allows a trait to persist while its genetic basis changes.

**7. Conclusions.** Kosswig concludes that gradual, multi-step restructuring of a systematic unit, shaped by polygenic, pleiotropically linked genotypes, is the main constraint on undirected evolutionary change. He expects that the trends debated for parasites may be more easily tested experimentally in other animal groups, which could help reconcile genetic and systematic-phylogenetic findings.
