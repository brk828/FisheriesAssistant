## Executive Summary
This 1955 paper by Ricker and Jupp documents a small collection of twenty-one [[Stoneflies]] (order Plecoptera) gathered by P. M. Mann in 1953 near [[Fort Simpson]], Northwest Territories, Canada. Specimens were collected along the lower 52 miles of the [[Keele River]] and on the banks of the [[Mackenzie River]] near their confluence. The collection, though small, is notable for range extensions and taxonomic clarifications of four species, including the first confirmed identification of *Isogenus zionensis* outside its type locality in Utah. The work highlights the biogeographic connectivity between northern Canadian drainages and other North American regions for aquatic insect fauna.

## Background & Description
As a taxonomic/faunistic note rather than a morphological study, detailed anatomical descriptions are not provided in this excerpt. However, key taxonomic distinctions are noted:

- **[[Isogenus Zionensis]]** vs. **[[Isogenus Elongatus]]**: The females of these two closely related species are described as morphologically indistinguishable based on current taxonomic keys at the time. Only males could be reliably differentiated.
- **[[Isogenus Frontalis Colubrinus]]**: Similarly, female specimens of this subspecies could only be identified to the species level (*Isogenus frontalis*), as subspecies-level identification required male specimens for confirmation.
- Sex ratios were recorded for each collection event (e.g., 2 males/9 females for *Pteronarcys dorsata*; 5 males/5 females for *Isoperla decolorata*), though no morphometric data (size, wing venation details, etc.) are given in this text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat Type**: All specimens were collected from lotic (flowing water) freshwater habitats — specifically riverbanks and adjacent riparian zones of the [[Keele River]] and [[Mackenzie River]] in the Mackenzie River Basin.
- **Collection Period**: Specimens were collected during a narrow window in early-to-mid summer (June 15–26, 1953), consistent with typical adult stonefly emergence timing in northern latitudes.
- **Biogeography**:
  - *[[Pteronarcys Dorsata]]* is described as a **transcontinental species**, occurring throughout most of Canada except British Columbia and the high Arctic.
  - *[[Isoperla Decolorata]]* has a northern distribution, previously recorded in Alaska and the Mackenzie District.
  - *[[Isogenus Frontalis Colubrinus]]* was previously known from Manitoba to Alaska.
  - *[[Isogenus Zionensis]]* was previously known **only** from its type series in Zion National Park, Utah — making this Northwest Territories record a very significant range extension (thousands of km northward).
- **Ecosystem Role**: Stoneflies (Plecoptera) are well-established bioindicators of clean, oxygen-rich flowing water and serve as an important food source for fish species in these northern river systems, though this trophic link is not explicitly discussed in the text.

## Key Findings
1. **Species Inventory**: Four stonefly taxa were identified from the 1953 Fort Simpson collection: *[[Pteronarcys Dorsata]]*, *[[Isoperla Decolorata]]*, *[[Isogenus Frontalis Colubrinus]]*, and *[[Isogenus Zionensis]]*.
2. **Major Range Extension**: The discovery of *Isogenus zionensis* in the Northwest Territories represents a dramatic range extension from its only previously known location (Zion National Park, Utah), confirmed via specimen comparison by the species' original describer, Dr. J. F. Hanson.
3. **Taxonomic Ambiguity Flagged**: The authors note that because females of *I. zionensis* and *I. elongatus* cannot be distinguished, prior records of *I. elongatus* from Alberta and Manitoba (based solely on female specimens per Ricker 1947, 1952) may actually represent misidentified *I. zionensis*, warranting re-examination of museum records.
4. **Regional Faunal Documentation**: This paper contributes to the sparse baseline knowledge of Plecoptera fauna in Canada's Northwest Territories, an understudied region for aquatic entomology at the time of publication.
5. **Methodological Note**: The paper underscores a recurring taxonomic challenge in Plecoptera systematics — reliable species/subspecies-level identification often depends on male genitalic or morphological characters, leaving female-only collections taxonomically ambiguous.
