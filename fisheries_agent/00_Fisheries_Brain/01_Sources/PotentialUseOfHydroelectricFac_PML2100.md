# Potential Use of Hydroelectric Facilities for Manipulating the Fertility of Lake Mead

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented as a technical report from a national workshop on mitigating losses of fish and wildlife habitat in mountain and range reservoirs, argues that the nutrient status of [[Lake Mead]] can be managed by altering the depth and seasonal pattern of water discharge at [[Hoover Dam]] hydroelectric facilities. Nitrate concentrations in the reservoir's upper layer (epilimnion) and deep layer (hypolimnion) differ during summer stratification, so withdrawing water from different depths can remove nutrient-rich or nutrient-poor water. The authors propose that this could raise the fertility of the reservoir and, through it, support higher [[Largemouth Bass]] production, whose catch declined from about 800,000 in 1953 to 125,000 by the late 1970s.

## Background & Description

- **Reservoir:** Lake Mead, formed by [[Hoover Dam]] on the [[Colorado River]]; the report compares it with [[Lake Mohave]] and other reservoirs.
- **Morphometrics (Table 1):** maximum operating level 374.0 m; maximum depth 180.0 m; mean depth 55.0 m; surface area 660.0 km²; volume 36.0 × 10⁹ m³; maximum length 183.0 km; maximum width 28.0 km; shoreline development 9.7; discharge depth 83.0 m; annual discharge (1977) 9.3 × 10⁹ m³; storage ratio 3.9.
- **Data sources:** U.S. Geological Survey water-quality records (1946–1976), Lake Mead Monitoring Program nutrient profiles (1975–1976), and unpublished data from J.E. Deacon.
- **Context:** Angler use of the lake has grown significantly in recent years, and the Arizona and Nevada Fish and Game Departments are investigating the decline in the bass fishery.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Nitrate dynamics:** Epilimnetic nitrate and phosphate are depleted in spring and early summer after phytoplankton blooms. By summer, nitrate falls below 20 µg/L in the euphotic zone while it accumulates in the hypolimnion. Phosphate accumulates less strongly. Both nutrients become uniform with depth as the lake mixes in fall and remain so through winter.
- **Concentration trends:** Epilimnetic nitrate ranged from 200–350 µg/L during 1946–1952, then rose to about 600 µg/L in the mid-1950s. It fell sharply in 1957, rose again around 1960, and increased slightly after [[Lake Powell]] formed in 1963 before declining after 1969.
- **Sources:** The [[Colorado River]] supplies most (80–90%) of the inorganic nitrogen (NO₃) entering Lake Mead, and this input has declined in recent years. Nitrate increases in the mid-1950s and early 1960s were attributed to increased runoff and high-nitrate loading from the river. Loss of nitrate from Lake Powell accounted for the 1965–1969 increase.
- **Thermal effects:** Epilimnetic discharge tends to dissipate heat, while hypolimnetic discharge stores it. Oxygen in the epilimnion does not vary appreciably with discharge depth, but hypolimnetic oxygen is typically lower.
- **Downstream effects:** The reach of the Colorado River immediately below Hoover Dam is highly productive due to nutrient enrichment from the hypolimnion. Downstream environmental uses may limit how discharge can be altered for nutrient management.
- **Nitrogen–phosphorus interaction:** Without additional nitrogen input, phosphorus cannot be used efficiently by phytoplankton, so retaining more nitrogen in the reservoir may increase productivity.

## Key Findings

1. **Mechanisms of nitrate accumulation:** Sinking phytoplankton cells, which displace nitrogen from the epilimnion to the hypolimnion, are the main process accumulating nitrate in the hypolimnion. Hypolimnetic loading from the Colorado River causes periodic increases (for example in 1962 and 1967).
2. **Fertility manipulation model:** The authors developed a simple model (Paulson and Baker 1979) showing that discharging from the nutrient-poor epilimnion lets the reservoir accumulate nutrients, while discharging from the nutrient-rich hypolimnion progressively depletes them.
3. **Experimental support:** Experiments at Lake Mortosskie, Poland (Mientki and Mynaska 1977), under different discharge regimes, produced trends consistent with the model. Nitrogen and phosphorus retention was 29% and 10% for hypolimnetic discharge, but increased to 37% and 57% for epilimnetic discharge.
4. **Seasonal discharge effects:** At Lake Mead, nitrate concentration in the hypolimnion peaks in late summer and fall. A year of relatively high discharge produced 15.0% more annual nitrate loss than a year of relatively low discharge.
5. **Fishery implications:** Fish yield is closely related to primary productivity and standing crop (McConnell 1963; Erbacek 1969; Melack 1976). Retaining more nutrients in the reservoir could increase the largemouth bass population. The authors suggest that the bass fishery could be improved by fertilizing Lake Mead, but this has never been done directly.
6. **Conclusion:** Changes in the depth or seasonal pattern of discharge from hydroelectric facilities could serve as a practical fisheries management tool for Lake Mead and other large reservoirs. The authors recommend further investigation of how these discharge changes would affect downstream environments and other physical and chemical factors before implementation.
