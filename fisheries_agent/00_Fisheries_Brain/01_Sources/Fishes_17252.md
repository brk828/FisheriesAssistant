# Classification of Fishes: Reprinted Encyclopedia Articles on Bony and Jawed Fish Taxa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osteichthyes

## Executive Summary

This booklet, compiled by [[Reeve M. Bailey]] and [[Ted M. Cavender]] of the Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan, reprints 51 encyclopedia articles on fish classification from the McGraw-Hill Encyclopedia of Science and Technology (third edition, 1971). Most articles focus on bony fishes ([[Osteichthyes]]); five orders of fossil bony fishes were written by Cavender. The arrangement is stated to be in phylogenetic sequence. The text summarizes the classification of fishes as a whole, reports estimated species counts for living fish groups, and describes the evolutionary history and anatomy of the major bony fish lineages, from the primitive [[Actinopterygii]] (rayfin fishes) and [[Sarcopterygii]] (lobefin fishes and lungfishes) to the dominant [[Teleostei]].

## Background & Description

**Scope and authorship.** The 51 included articles are scattered across 15 volumes of the Encyclopedia (volumes 5, 8 and 15 excluded). Comparable accounts for [[Agnatha]], [[Chondrichthyes]] and their subgroups were written by Robert H. Denison, Alfred S. Romer and Bobb Schaeffer.

**Terminology.** The term [[Anamnia]] groups vertebrates lacking an amnion in development: the Recent classes Agnatha, Chondrichthyes, Osteichthyes and Amphibia, plus the fossil class Placodermi. The term [[Pisces (Zoology)]] is used here as a convenience name for all fishlike vertebrates, although it has no strict taxonomic status. The text proposes four well-defined classes of fishes: Agnatha, Placodermi, Chondrichthyes and Osteichthyes.

**Major taxa described:**
- [[Cyclostomata (Chordata)]]: jawless, eel-like vertebrates (lampreys and hagfishes) with a cartilaginous skeleton, a single median nostril and no true teeth or paired fins.
- [[Batoidea]]: skates and rays, one of two Recent orders of Elasmobranchii, with about 300-350 species in 16 families.
- [[Holocephali]]: chimaeras, one order, one family, 4 or 5 genera and about 24 species, all marine.
- Actinopterygii infraclasses [[Chondrostei]], [[Holostei]] and [[Teleostei]], plus the many orders assigned to each.
- Sarcopterygii: [[Crossopterygii]] (lobefins, including [[Coelacanthiformes]] and [[Osteolepiformes]]) and [[Dipnoi]] (lungfishes).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Diversity of living fishes.** Estimates of Recent species vary widely (18,000 to 40,000), and the text suggests most earlier estimates are too high. The working count is just under 18,300 recognized living species:
- Agnatha: 2 families, about 11 genera, approximately 45 species.
- Chondrichthyes: 31 families, some 132 genera, roughly 575 species.
- Osteichthyes: 32 orders, about 357 families, 3570 genera and about 17,600 species. Within this, about 5100 species fall in 29 orders, nearly 2000 in [[Siluriformes]], 3000 in [[Cypriniformes]] and 7500 in [[Perciformes]].

The text cautions that deep-sea and tropical groups are imperfectly known.

**Habitat adaptation.** Fishes occupy almost all permanent waters. Examples include mountain torrent fishes with attachment organs, Antarctic fishes with physiological adjustments to sub-freezing water, deep-sea fishes with their own light sources, and East Indian fishes that skip over mud flats or forage on land. The [[Anglerfish]] male is attached parasitically to the female.

**Size range.** A Philippine goby, *Mistichthys*, reaches only 1/2 in. and about 70,000 individuals make 1 kg. The Samoan *Schindleria* weighs about 6 mg. At the other extreme, the [[Whale Shark]] is reputed to reach 60 ft, and a 38-ft individual weighed more than 13 tons.

**Adaptive radiation.** Water support permits body forms impossible on land, including pancake-thin bodies, eel-like forms evolved independently many times, boxlike trunkfishes and deep-sea fishes with eyes on elongate stalks.

**Feeding and reproduction.** Diet ranges from filter feeding with gill rakers to crushing mollusks and crustaceans, scraping vegetation and parasite removal by wrasses. Most fishes are oviparous, with nest-building and parental care ranging from pebble piles to oral incubation and the male pipefish brood pouch. Viviparity and ovoviviparity arose independently.

**Human relevance.** Per capita fishery consumption in the United States is about 10 lb annually. Fishing is a major recreational activity, and home aquarium keeping engages millions. Shark attacks cause few deaths, ciguatera poisoning from fish flesh causes more, and venomous fishes take a limited toll.

## Key Findings

1. **Fish are not a single natural group.** Early recognition of several classes is supported, since at least five lineages with modern descendants were established before tetrapods appeared. Pisces is retained only as a convenience term.
2. **Osteichthyes arose from primitive bony gnathostomes.** The oldest typical bony fishes appear in the Lower Devonian. The ancestral stock is most plausibly linked to acanthodians, and the earlier idea that bony fishes evolved from cartilaginous fishes has been abandoned.
3. **Three subclasses are recognized** for Osteichthyes: Actinopterygii (rayfin fishes, about 97% of modern fish), Crossopterygii and Dipnoi. Some authors combine the latter two as Sarcopterygii.
4. **Actinopterygii infraclasses are evolutionary grades, not natural groups.** Chondrostei gave rise to Holostei, and Holostei in turn gave rise to Teleostei, which dominate modern seas and fresh waters.
5. **Teleost radiation.** Teleosts first appeared in the Middle Triassic as Leptolepiformes. Rapid radiation occurred in the Cretaceous, producing 15 or more orders. Teleosts constitute about 96% of living fishes in roughly 26 orders.
6. **Diagnostic teleost traits** include homocercal caudal fins, cycloid scales, a supraoccipital bone and a hydrostatic swim bladder function.
7. **Lobefin fishes and lungfishes are relict groups.** Today only three families, four genera and seven species remain. The lobefins gave rise to tetrapods through the Osteolepiformes. The coelacanth *Latimeria chalumnae*, captured off East Africa in 1938, was described as the most publicized zoological discovery of the 20th century.
8. **Gars and bowfin** are the last surviving holosteans, and the Recent sturgeons and paddlefishes are among the largest actinopterygians. A Volga River sturgeon (*Huso huso*) reached 14 ft and 2250 lb.
9. **Major orders described** include [[Gadiformes]] (cod family, with about 730 species), [[Salmoniformes]] (about 950 species in 8 suborders), [[Cypriniformes]] (about 3000 species), [[Siluriformes]] (about 2000 species), and [[Perciformes]] (about 7500 species, roughly 41% of all fishes).
10. **Electrogenic fishes.** African mormyrids and gymnarchids generate electric pulses for detection, a convergent trait with South American gymnotids. Mormyrids have the largest brain of any fish.
11. **Ostariophysi** share the Weberian apparatus linking the swim bladder to the inner ear. This superorder dominates freshwater faunas on every continent except Australia.
12. **Economic importance** is high for the anchovy, menhaden and herring fisheries, and for sport fisheries of trout, salmon, pike and bass. Gars are considered pests and their roe is toxic to warm-blooded animals.
13. **Limited taxonomic certainty.** Several groups, including [[Cetomimiformes]] (41 species, mostly known from one or a few specimens), are regarded as polyphyletic and may be dismembered. Classification of Teleostei is described as still actively reconstructed.
