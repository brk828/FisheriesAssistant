## Executive Summary

The [[Moapa Dace]] (*Moapa coriacea*) is a critically endangered cyprinid **endemic** to the thermal headwaters of the [[Moapa River]] in Clark County, Nevada. It is the sole species in the monotypic genus *Moapa*, making its extinction risk taxonomically significant — loss of the species would eliminate an entire genus. Once common at the time of its description (Hubbs and Miller, 1948), the population had declined to fewer than 1,000 individuals by 1979. The primary drivers of decline are **habitat destruction/modification** of its thermal spring habitat and the **introduction of the [[Shortfin Molly]]** (*Poecilia mexicana*) around 1965, compounded by introduced parasites and predation by exotic species. This 1981 USFWS technical draft recovery plan outlines habitat acquisition, restoration, exotic species removal, reintroduction, monitoring, and life-history research needed to delist the species.

## Background & Description

- **Distinguishing coloration**: distinctive black spot at the base of the caudal fin; wide, black, mid-dorsal stripe — the most reliable field character separating it from co-occurring native cyprinids.
- **Mouth/premaxillary**: non-protractile premaxillary with a slight frenum hidden in the groove.
- **Pharyngeal teeth**: uniserial, asymmetric count — five on the left arch, four on the right.
- **Barbels**: absent.
- **Scales**: deeply embedded, giving the skin a "leathery" texture — the basis for the specific epithet *coriacea* ("leathery").
- **Size at maturity**: males sexually mature at 32 mm standard length (SL); females at 33 mm SL (Hubbs and Miller, 1948).
- **Similar species**: closely resembles two other native Moapa River cyprinids, the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta* ssp.) and the [[Moapa Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus moapae*), from which it is most easily distinguished by coloration rather than meristic characters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: warm thermal spring pools, spring feeders, small outflow streams, and the mainstem river channel within roughly one mile below the spring inflow.
- **Thermal requirements**: strongly associated with water temperatures of 28–32°C; the species is largely restricted to these thermal refugia, though it has tolerated temperatures as low as 19.5°C briefly in the wild and 21°C for several months in captivity (Deacon and Bradley, 1972).
- **Flow regime**: found in areas with slight current; recorded in swift water of 0.8–0.9 m/sec (Cross, 1976).
- **Dissolved oxygen**: tolerant of a range of 3.4–8.3 ppm (La Rivers, 1962; Deacon and Bradley, 1972).
- **Substrate**: variable — spring-deposited gravels or flocculent organic/silt in pools; sand, gravel, pebbles, cobbles, or mud in outflow streams.
- **Vegetation associations**: *Najas*, *Potamogeton*, *Nasturtium*, *Eleocharis*, *Chara*, and other algae; riparian tamarisk (*Tamarix* sp.) and mesquite (*Prosopis juliflora*); the only native Nevada palm, [[Washingtonia filifera]], occurs in the Warm Springs area.
- **Diet/trophic role**: primarily carnivorous, feeding mainly on insect remains with some vegetable matter; short intestine morphology supports a carnivorous strategy (La Rivers, 1962).
- **Social behavior**: described as a "rather methodical schooler," remaining conspicuously segregated from other similarly sized co-occurring fish species.
- **Reproduction**: likely a year-round spawner with a spring/summer peak, presumably triggered by increasing photoperiod, a pattern shared with other desert spring-endemic fishes; gravid females recorded in nearly every month sampled (January, February, April, June, September, October, December).
- **Threats within niche**: competitive interactions and habitat overlap with the introduced [[Shortfin Molly]]; also affected by introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) impacts documented in analogous Nevada desert-spring systems (e.g., Pahrump killifish decline).

## Key Findings

- **Population decline**: Once abundant at time of description (1948); reduced to <1,000 individuals by 1979 (USFWS, 1979). Steepest decline followed habitat modification in the late 1950s–early 1960s and shortfin molly introduction in 1965.
- **Primary cause of endangerment**: Physical destruction/alteration of spring habitat identified as the single greatest factor — consistent with broader patterns of decline among western North American desert fishes.
- **Habitat loss specifics**: All springs on the former Desert Warm Springs Resort and 7-12 Resort properties (now USFWS-owned) have been altered beyond suitability for the species. Springs on the Church of Latter Day Saints (LDS) property are similarly modified with no known extant populations. Only the Apcar property retains sufficient flow to support a small remnant population.
- **Exotic species impacts**: Parallel case studies cited include extirpation of the Monkey Springs pupfish and White River springfish by largemouth bass, and extinction of the Ash Meadows killifish following introduction of exotic fish, snails, frogs, and crayfish — supporting the causal link between exotic introductions and desert fish decline.
- **Parasite introduction**: Exotic fishes and snails may have introduced parasites; heavy parasite infestations documented in other native Moapa River fishes (Deacon and Bradley, 1972; Wilson et al., 1966).
- **Failed translocation**: In September 1972, 20 individuals were transplanted to [[Shoshone Ponds]] (BLM endangered fish facility near Ely, NV); survived at least one month but eventually died for unknown reasons.
- **Legal/conservation status**: Listed as Endangered under the U.S. Federal Endangered Species list in 1967 (FR 32:4331). USFWS purchased ~12 acres in the Warm Springs area in 1970 specifically for species preservation, establishing the basis for the Moapa National Wildlife Refuge.
- **Recovery plan structure**: Prime objective is to establish a self-sustaining, non-endangered population on the Moapa National Wildlife Refuge sufficient to allow delisting, via: (1) habitat acquisition (7-12 Resort, Apcar property, Plummer Springs, LDS trespass rights), (2) habitat restoration and exotic species removal, (3) reintroduction of *Moapa coriacea*, (4) population monitoring, (5) ecological/life-history research (reproduction, feeding, competition, age/growth), (6) physical protection (fencing, signage), and (7) public interpretation/education programs.
- **Knowledge gaps flagged for research**: fecundity, spawning season/substrate, egg incubation time, nursery habitat, food items across life stages, optimal population density (alone and with [[Moapa White River Springfish]]), effects of exotic fish on population dynamics, growth rates, and longevity.
