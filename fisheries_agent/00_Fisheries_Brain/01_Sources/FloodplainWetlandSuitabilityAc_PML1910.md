# Floodplain Wetland Suitability, Access, and Use by Juvenile Razorback Sucker in the Middle Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether off-channel floodplain wetlands in the middle [[Green River]], Utah, provide suitable nursery habitat for juvenile fishes, including age-0 [[Razorback Sucker]]. The work centered on [[Old Charley Wash]], a floodplain wetland on the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]], during the high-water years of 1995 and 1996. The authors found that floodplain depressions offered warmer water, adequate oxygen, depth, cover, and zooplankton prey, while main-channel habitats did not. Access to these wetlands, however, depended on flow timing and duration, which were reduced by [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. The wetland also produced a large biomass of [[Nonnative Fishes]], which raises unresolved questions about their effects on native recruitment.

## Background & Description

Razorback sucker is a large catostomid of the [[Colorado River Basin]] that has declined substantially and is federally listed as endangered (USFWS 1991). Earlier studies had linked the species to low-gradient floodplain reaches, yet few juveniles had been collected, and those came from main-channel habitats. The authors argued that reduced spring flood flows had cut the river's connection to floodplains that may serve as early life-history habitat. Spawning occurs on the ascending limb of the spring hydrograph, so newly emerged larvae depend on high flows to reach inundated floodplains (Tyus and Karp 1990; Modde and Irving 1998).

Old Charley Wash covers about 147 ha. Only its main unit (80 ha) was evaluated. Water enters through a gravity-fed inlet canal at flows of 240 m³/s, and through a levee notch at approximately 396 m³/s. Dominant vegetation included smartweed (*Polygonum* sp.) and sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), with bulrush and cattail along the margins. The wetland was drained each winter and refilled by spring floods. A modified outlet structure allowed efficient fish capture during draining.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and floodplain loss.** Dams have reduced the magnitude and duration of high flows in the Upper Colorado River Basin, reducing floodplain connection. Large reservoirs in the Lower Basin have permanently inundated historical floodplain (Carlson and Muth 1989). Since Flaming Gorge Dam was built in 1963, middle Green River floodplain inundation has been dramatically reduced (Flo Engineering 1996).

**Access thresholds.** Floodplain wetlands near Ouray were used as a reference flow of about 575 m³/s for connection. Most floodplain habitat in the area is dewatered below about 147 m³/s (as written in the source). Main-channel backwaters generally do not form until flows recede to roughly 109 m³/s (Pucherelli et al. 1983).

**Prey and habitat quality.** The authors compared prey densities across habitats. Mabey (1993) reported zooplankton of 7.1/L and 1.5/L in a riverine backwater, and 1.3/L and 0.3/L in high-velocity main-channel habitat in July and August. Old Charley Wash values for the same period were reported as 311.4/L and 690.2/L (OCR-corrected). Laboratory work found significant starvation mortality of larval razorback sucker when [[Artemia]] densities fell below 50 organisms/L (Papoulias and Minckley 1990). Pond studies found no survival differences between prey densities of 13 and 43 organisms/L, but growth did differ (Papoulias and Minckley 1992).

## Key Findings

**1. Wetland conditions supported age-0 fishes.**
- Water temperatures were consistently warmer in the wetland than in the main channel during spring and summer of both years.
- Surface [[Dissolved Oxygen]] never fell below 4.0 mg/L in 1995. In 1996, stratification appeared in late June and early July as aquatic macrophytes became abundant, but bottom oxygen did not appear limiting.
- Maximum depth exceeded 2.0 m in both years when the wetland was connected to the river.

**2. Zooplankton peaked in the wetland.**
- Peak mean zooplankton density (mainly cladocerans and copepods) was 54.3 per liter in 1995 and 42.8 per liter in 1996, both occurring around the third week of June.
- Zooplankton peaks came just after the wetland disconnected from the river at about 396 m³/s.

**3. Larval access depended on flow regulation.**
- [[Larval Fish Sampling]] with [[Light Traps]] recorded 292 (1993), 1,277 (1994), 32 (1995), and 174 (1996) larval razorback sucker, over sampling periods of 51, 32, 39, and 24 days.
- Under regulated flows, larvae generally lacked access to most floodplain wetlands (flows above 575 m³/s). Under unregulated flows, larvae would have had access in every study year except 1994.
- Flows would have allowed access for as much as 17 days in 1993.
- In 1995 and 1996, larvae appeared in the river after floodplains were isolated, so connectivity lasted too briefly for all larvae to reach nursery habitat.

**4. Razorback sucker used the wetland.**
- Razorback sucker was the most abundant native fish when the wetland was drained.
- In 1995, 28 age-1 razorback sucker averaged 94 mm total length (range 74–125 mm) and 9.5 g (range 3–18 g). Two adults were also collected.
- In 1996, 45 age-1 razorback sucker were collected earlier in the drain, averaging 65.9 mm (range 54–83 mm) and 3.8 g (range 1–6 g). No adults were collected.
- Juvenile numbers were the greatest reported for the Upper Colorado River Basin from a single site, according to the authors.

**5. Nonnative fishes dominated the wetland community.**
- Fifteen species were collected in 1995 and 16 in 1996. Standing stock was reported as 126.7 kg (1995) and 71.4 kg (1996).
- Carp and fathead minnow together made up 95.5% of weight and 79.4% of fish collected in both years.
- Carp accounted for 91.2% of biomass, and fathead minnows were the most numerous (60.6%).
- Fish from the river made up 80% (1995) and 77% (1996) of the biomass, though fathead minnows and red shiners were classed as wetland-produced.
- Other notable species included green sunfish, black bullhead, channel catfish, and red shiner. Colorado pikeminnow, flannelmouth sucker, and roundtail chub were also present as immature fish.

**6. Open questions and management implications.**
- The authors suggest that [[Floodplain Connectivity]] be maintained through levee removal and flood flow management. This would increase access to nursery habitat for larval razorback sucker.
- Two open questions remain. First, what impact would releasing large numbers of wetland-produced nonnative fishes into the Green River have? Second, if the impact is negative, does it offset the recruitment benefits to razorback sucker?
- The authors argue that when natural floodplain habitat is present, nonnative predation may be a secondary rather than primary threat. They cite the success of razorback sucker in large lower-basin impoundments as partial support.

**Data note.** The text reports standing stock of 126.7 kg and 71.4 kg, but the table totals (10,133.6 g in 1995 and 5,710.3 g in 1996) differ by roughly an order of magnitude. This discrepancy likely reflects a unit or transcription error and should be checked against the original data.
