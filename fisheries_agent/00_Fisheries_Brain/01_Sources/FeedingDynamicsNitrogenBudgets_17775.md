# Feeding Dynamics, Nitrogen Budgets, and Ecosystem Role of a Desert Stream Omnivore, Agosia chrysogaster (Pisces: Cyprinidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #desert-ecology #nutrient-cycling #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study examines the feeding ecology, nitrogen budgets, and ecosystem role of [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) in [[Sycamore Creek]], a Sonoran Desert stream in Arizona. The research demonstrates that this cyprinid is an opportunistic omnivore that shifts diet composition based on prey availability, consuming primarily insects (especially [[Baetid Mayflies]]) when abundant but switching to algae when mayfly densities decline. Despite similar standing crops of algae and macroinvertebrates between sampling periods (May and November 1983), fish compensated for lower nitrogen content in algal diets by increasing daily ingestion rates. The population plays a significant role in stream nitrogen dynamics, with fish biomass comprising 3-6% of total ecosystem nitrogen storage and ammonia excretion representing 5-10% of total nitrogen uptake by algae.

## Background & Description

[[Agosia chrysogaster]] (Longfin Dace) is a cyprinid fish native to the [[Sonoran Desert]] in Arizona and northern Mexico. The species is the most common fish in [[Sycamore Creek]], inhabiting uniform coarse sand-fine gravel runs and shallow pools, though also occurring in cobble-boulder riffles. The study site experiences spatially intermittent flow with low summer-autumn discharge (0.01-0.05 m³ s⁻¹) and higher winter-spring discharge (0.1-0.5 m³ s⁻¹).

The research was conducted during two contrasting periods: May 1983 (discharge 0.36 m³ s⁻¹, temperatures 15-25°C) when benthic material consisted of [[Cladophora glomerata]] mats and diatoms with high macroinvertebrate production, and November 1983 (discharge 0.063 m³ s⁻¹, temperatures 19-23°C) when *Cladophora* was absent and benthic material consisted primarily of diatoms and flocculent detritus. Other fish species present include the native [[Pantosteus clarki]] (Desert Sucker) and introduced minnows [[Notropis lutrensis]] and [[Pimephales promelas]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was conducted within a nitrogen-limited desert stream ecosystem where nutrient cycling processes are critical to primary production. [[Sycamore Creek]] exhibits typical desert stream characteristics with variable discharge, high temperatures, and distinct seasonal patterns in algal and invertebrate communities.

In May 1983, mayflies ([[Baetis quilleri]] and [[Tricorythodes dimorphus]]) and [[Chironomidae]] comprised >90% of macroinvertebrate biomass. Chlorophyll a standing crop was 96.5 mg m⁻², with 50% [[Cladophora glomerata]] cover and 50% diatoms. Macroinvertebrate density was ~4,600 individuals m⁻² with biomass of 2.2-3.3 g m⁻² and secondary production of 669.3 mg m⁻² d⁻¹.

In November 1983, chlorophyll a was similar (99.0 mg m⁻²) but community composition differed markedly: 78% diatoms, 19% flocculent material, only 9% *Cladophora*. Ephemeroptera comprised only 9% of macroinvertebrate biomass (compared to 41% in May), while Chironomidae increased to 61%. Total invertebrate density was 4,900 individuals m⁻² with biomass of 3.07 g m⁻².

## Key Findings

**Food Habits and Opportunistic Omnivory:**
- Of 39 fish examined in May, 28% had empty intestines; of those with food (n=28), mean diet composition was 30% diatoms, 14% filamentous Chlorophyta, 56% insects
- In November, 54% of 50 fish had food; mean composition was 53% diatoms, 21% filamentous Chlorophyta, 26% insects
- Individual fish often specialized: 39% consumed only insects in May vs. 15% in November; 32% consumed only algae in May vs. 63% in November
- [[Baetid Mayflies]] were the preferred insect prey when abundant; [[Chironomidae]], though abundant, were rarely consumed, possibly due to interstitial habitat

**Feeding Periodicity:**
- Feeding was diurnal on both dates but more markedly so in November when algae dominated the diet
- Ingestion rate provided better measures of feeding periodicity than gut fullness
- In May, midday ingestion (1415-1815 h) was significantly higher than early morning (2200-0615 h)
- In November, ingestion rates between 1000-1800 h were significantly higher than nighttime and early morning rates (ANOVA: F=15.95, p<0.001)

**Ingestion Rates and Compensation for Food Quality:**
- Daily dry mass ingestion in November (215.4 mg fish⁻¹ d⁻¹) was nearly twice the May rate (111.4 mg fish⁻¹ d⁻¹)
- Weighted mean nitrogen content of food: 5.81% in May, 3.77% in November
- Despite large differences in dry mass ingestion, daily nitrogen ingestion was similar: 6.47 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (May) vs. 8.12 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (November)
- Fish compensated for low nitrogen content in algal diets by increasing ingestion rate—a pattern not previously reported in fishes

**Gut Evacuation:**
- Gut evacuation conformed to exponential model of [[Elliott & Persson]] (1978)
- Three methods yielded instantaneous evacuation rates (R) of 0.496, 0.630, and 0.758 h⁻¹
- Mass-specific gut clearance rates did not differ significantly between samples
- Evacuation rate appeared unaffected by body size or food type, consistent with literature

**Nitrogen Assimilation and Excretion:**
- Nitrogen content of food decreased dramatically during digestion: 4-6% (dry mass) to <1% (feces)
- Nitrogen assimilation efficiency: 72-78% (nitrogen assimilated/nitrogen consumed)
- Mean egestion rate: 1.79 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (22-28% of ingestion)
- Laboratory ammonia excretion: 0.89 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (May), 0.45 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (November)
- Field excretion rates were much higher: 3.11 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (May), 1.65 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (November)
- Excretion increased linearly with body mass: E = 13.2W - 2.11 (r² = 0.786, n=36)

**Nitrogen Utilization:**
- Nitrogen deposited in growth: 1.41 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (May), 4.67 mg N fish⁻¹ d⁻¹ (November)
- Utilization efficiency (utilization/ingestion): 19% (May), 56% (November)
- Values within literature ranges for other fish species (8-70%)

**Ecosystem Significance:**
- Population density: mean 8 fish m⁻² (SE=1.9)
- Nitrogen stored in fish biomass: 3-6% of total ecosystem nitrogen standing crop
- Population ingestion: 51.8 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹ (May), 65.0 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹ (November)
- Population egestion: 14.3 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹ (approximately equal to net particulate export on day 27 of succession)
- Population excretion: 13.2-24.9 mg N m⁻² d⁻¹, representing 5-10% of average total nitrogen retention by the stream ecosystem
- Fish feces may be reingested or enter benthic organic matter pool; as feces decay, organic nitrogen is mineralized to ammonia available to primary producers
- Rapid recycling of ammonia to primary producers is significant in this nitrogen-limited ecosystem, suggesting fish play important roles beyond energy flow considerations
