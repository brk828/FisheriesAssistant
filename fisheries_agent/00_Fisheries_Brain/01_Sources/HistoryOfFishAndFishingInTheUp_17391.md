# A History of Fish and Fishing in the Upper Mississippi River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-history #freshwater #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #polyodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 historical monograph by Harriet Bell Carlander, sponsored by the [[Upper Mississippi River Conservation Committee]], traces how fish and fishing shaped and were shaped by the Upper Mississippi River from St. Paul, Minnesota, to Caruthersville, Missouri (951 miles). It draws on Indian, explorer, settler and government sources, and on interviews with fishermen and dealers conducted in 1949-1952. The narrative covers early fishing and food use, the decline of the river's fishery under navigation engineering and impoundment, state and federal fish-propagation and fish-rescue programs, the [[Freshwater Pearl Button Industry]] and its mussel resource, the [[Fairport Biological Station]], the commercial fishery, sport fishing, and the evolution of interstate fishery law.

## Background & Description

The study was commissioned by the Upper Mississippi River Conservation Committee, formed in December 1943 at Dubuque, Iowa, by the conservation agencies of Minnesota, Wisconsin, Iowa, Missouri and Illinois, the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. The author notes that sources on river fishing are scattered and brief, because the river's commerce, especially [[Steamboating]], overshadowed fishing in the historical record.

Major historical periods covered:
- **Beginnings:** Mound-builder and Indian fishing, including shell middens at Sabula and Bellevue, Iowa, and accounts by [[John Long]], [[Black Hawk]] and early explorers.
- **Explorers and settlers:** Radisson and Groseilliers, [[Father Marquette]], La Salle, [[Zebulon Pike]], Stephen Long and Carver, with notes on fish species and angling.
- **Management era:** Fish commissions (federal 1871; Iowa 1874), artificial propagation, and fish rescue from overflow ponds (Iowa from 1876; federal Bureau of Fisheries from 1917 onward).
- **Industrial era:** Pearl button mussel fishery (began about 1889 at Muscatine, Iowa).
- **Modern era:** Creel census, commercial statistics and legislation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The river originally consisted of deep pools separated by shallow bars and rapids. Settlement, deforestation, drainage, levees, dredging and channel straightening increased flood peaks, reduced dry-season flow and increased silt. Between 1878 and the 1930s, navigation works progressed from a 4.5-foot to a nine-foot channel. By the 1930s there were 26 dams between Minneapolis and the Missouri River.

Key habitat effects:
- **Impoundment:** Keokuk Lake covered 60 square miles at low water, versus 36 square miles of river surface before the dam. The difference between high and low water areas was reduced from 18 to 4 square miles.
- **Current and silt:** Reduced current let silt cover sand and gravel bars needed by fish, mussels and invertebrate food organisms.
- **Pollution:** The 1927 Minnesota survey found fish avoided grossly polluted, low-oxygen water; Platner later identified siltation as the most serious pollution problem for river fisheries.
- **Water-level fluctuation:** Winter drawdowns in navigation pools stranded and killed fish, and the Minnesota Game and Fish Commissioner (1924) argued that stable water levels were needed to perpetuate wall-eyed pike.

Migration barriers at Keokuk Dam (no effective fishway) affected paddlefish, American eel, skipjack, Ohio shad, buffalo, and several catfish species. The skipjack was the required host for the glochidia of the valuable niggerhead mussel.

## Key Findings

**Fish and mussel history**
- Early accounts describe catfish, paddlefish, sturgeon and buffalo as the river's most notable fishes. [[De Soto]] found Indians netting catfish and paddlefish.
- Shell middens at Sabula and Bellevue, Iowa, contained 14 species of Unios and one Paludina, all still living in the river in 1868.
- Commercial catch changed dramatically. Carp, not present before about 1880, became over half the commercial catch. Buffalo catches fell from about 6 million pounds (1894) to about 2 million (1947-50).
- The American eel catch fell from 63,000 pounds (1894) to about 1,000 pounds in the 1940s. Lake sturgeon has practically disappeared. Paddlefish catches in 1947-50 were about one tenth of 1894-99 levels.
- Coker predicted that the exclusion of American eel, blue catfish and paddlefish above Keokuk would cost fishery products worth several thousand dollars annually.

**Fish propagation and rescue**
- The U.S. Commissioner of Fish and Fisheries was established February 9, 1871. Iowa's Fish Commission began in 1874.
- Iowa's rescue work began in 1876, with 1,574,200 fish taken on the first trip. The U.S. Bureau of Fisheries ran rescue stations; the 1917 season saved 54,957,830 fish by the close of the season (November 18, 1918). The 1921 season was the peak, with 176,000,000 fish rescued.
- Rescue crews collected fish from drying overflow ponds. Most biologists later questioned its value for maintaining river populations. Work was largely halted when the nine-foot channel stabilized water levels (1937-1940s).
- Stocking of American shad (1872) was judged a failure by 1886. Carp planting was the most successful introduction of the period.

**Mussel fishery and pearl button industry**
- Mussel fishing began about 1889. [[J. F. Boepple]] started the first button plant at Muscatine, Iowa, in 1891. In 1899 there were 60 button factories (41 in Iowa), employing 1,917 people.
- In 1899, 11,300 tons of mussel shells were used. Shell beds declined rapidly: Lake Pepin produced 3,000-4,000 tons in 1914-15 but not over 150 tons in 1929. Only 39 species were found in a 1931 survey.
- Dr. Hugh Smith's 1899 recommendations (minimum size, closed spawning season January 1 to May 1, restrictions on shipment) were not acted upon.
- Artificial propagation via fish hosts succeeded in part: Lake Pepin "fat muckets" rose from 36.5% (1911) to 56.3% in closed sections (1924). The 1924 reopening yielded strong catches.
- Ellis's 1931 work found no niggerhead mussels under nine years old. Erosion silt and pollution were identified as main causes.

**Fish Technical and historical management**
- Interstate cooperation was driven by jurisdictional disputes. In *State v. Meyers* (1912), the Iowa Supreme Court held Iowa could enforce its fishing laws on the Illinois side of the channel.
- Iowa's first Mississippi River license law was passed in 1909. Minnesota and Wisconsin passed the first reciprocal interstate fishing law in 1913.
- In 1923, Iowa, Illinois and Wisconsin closed mussel areas for five years.
- The Federal Water Pollution Act (Public Law 845) was passed in 1948.

**Commercial fishing**
- Commercial catch data were collected in 1894, 1899, 1902, 1922 and 1931, then 1947-1950. Total catch: 13,728,000 lb (1894) to 8,015,000-9,131,000 lb (1947-50).
- In 1949, seines took 63.7% of the Wisconsin catch and 8.8% of the Missouri catch.
- Fishermen numbered 5,807 in 1949-50 (about 3.6% of U.S. total), yet the Upper Mississippi produced only about 0.2% of U.S. fishery poundage.
- Value of fish: $678,812 (1947), $701,631 (1948), $782,545 (1949), $971,764 (1950).
- Catfish brought 22-25 cents per pound versus carp at 4-5.5 cents (1947-50).
- Lake Pepin produced 87.8 pounds per acre, compared with 16 pounds per acre for the whole river (Greenbank). The author cites Greenbank's tentative conclusion that the commercial species were not seriously over-fished.

**Sport fishing**
- Creel census (1944-45 and 1945-46) interviewed 40,000 fishermen. Twelve warm-water species appeared in creels; bluegill and crappies made up about half the summer catch.
- Summer fishing pressure rose from about 60,000 fishermen (1945) to 250,000 (1948). Summer catch rose from about 120,000 fish (1945) to about 1,000,000 (1948-49).
- In 1878, [[George Barton Berrell]]'s diary records about 10.6 fish per man-day and 1.4 fish per man-hour in river-bottom lakes near St. Louis; the author compares this to 1.3 fish per man-hour in modern warm-water lakes and 0.155 at Weber Lake (1950).

**Conclusions**
- The author concludes that man's changes to the river have "had their effect both on fish and on fishing methods," and that it is "almost impossible to separate the effects of the various changes."
- Historical accounts show that early fishery information is scarce and often anecdotal, so quantitative baselines are difficult to establish.
