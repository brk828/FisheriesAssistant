# Control and Treatment of Parasitic Diseases of Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-parasites

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife leaflet (FDL-28, July 1970, Division of Fishery Research), prepared by [[Glenn L. Hoffman]] of the [[Eastern Fish Disease Laboratory]], compiles the most effective chemical and non-chemical methods for controlling external and internal parasites of freshwater fishes. It is a revision of Fishery Leaflet 486 (April 1959). The guidance stresses prevention first, with [[Chemical Treatment]] reserved for cases where other controls fail. Dosages are given as active-ingredient concentrations, generally in parts per million (ppm), except formalin.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** A practical compilation of treatments for parasitic diseases of freshwater fish, organized by parasite group.
- **Diagnosis:** The first step is identifying the causative organism. The keys cited are Bykhovskaya-Pavlovskaya (1962), Davis (1953), and [[Hoffman]] (1967).
- **Regulatory notice:** Fish used for human or animal food may be treated with drugs and chemicals only under current laws. Relevant federal agencies are the [[Food And Drug Administration]] (DHEW) and the Department of Agriculture. State and local regulations may also apply.
- **Reference dosing convention:** Amounts refer to active ingredients rather than commercial preparations, which are often diluted with inert ingredients. A [[Chemical Synonyms]] list follows the treatments.

The compilation is organized as:
1. **External parasites** (body, fins, gills): fungi, protozoa ([[Ichthyophthirius]]), monogenetic trematodes, parasitic leeches, glochidia, and parasitic copepods.
2. **Internal parasites:** protozoa ([[Hexamita]], [[Myxosporida]], [[Eimeria]]), and helminths (cestodes, trematodes, nematodes, acanthocephalans).
3. **Miscellaneous:** predacious insects, crayfish, small crustacea (intermediate hosts), and molluscicides.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Prevention through water management:** Infected fish must not be placed in uninfected waters, and parasite-free fish must not be placed in water drawn from lakes, ponds, or streams with infected fish. If such water must be used, adequate filters are required. Filtered water can be exposed to sterilizing radiation such as [[Ultraviolet Light]] to remove very small parasites, bacteria, and viruses.
- **Temperature dependence:** Formalin dosage for [[Ichthyophthirius]] is tied to water temperature.
- **Oxygen and algae:** Oxygen depletion may occur in warm weather if formalin kills algae.
- **Water chemistry:** Copper sulfate toxicity and efficacy depend on water carbonate and methyl orange alkalinity. Hard-water ponds may use citric acid with copper sulfate.
- **Host and non-target toxicity:** Several treatments are toxic to non-target organisms. [[Malachite Green]] is highly toxic to catfish under 10 cm (4 inches) and to bass. [[Bayluscide]] is toxic to fish. [[Baytex]] kills crayfish but not fish. Some fish species may be killed by malachite green.
- **Biological control:** [[Redear Sunfish]] sometimes reduce snail populations.
- **Hatchery and pond management:** Good pond sanitation (removal of detritus) can reduce snail populations. Drained ponds can be disinfected.

## Key Findings

### Prevention and General Principles
- Prevention is the best treatment for parasitic diseases.
- Chemical treatment should be used only if other control methods fail.
- Filtration and [[Ultraviolet Light]] sterilization can protect water supplies.

### External Parasites

**Fungi** (*Saprolegnia, Achlya, Aphanomyces, Leptomitus, Pythium*)
- Fish eggs: [[Malachite Green]] at 5 ppm as a daily flush; or [[Formalin]] at 1:500 for 15 minutes.
- Live fish: malachite green dip at 66 ppm (1:15,000) for 10 to 30 seconds; or malachite green at 1 to 3 ppm for 1 hour (use cautiously).

**Protozoa: [[Ichthyophthirius]] ("Ich")**
- Treatment must be applied daily, or the chemical must remain in contact until "Ich" disappears (5 to 10 days). If all fish are heavily infected, usually no known treatment will save them. Examine fish regularly and begin treatment at the first sign of "Ich."
- Transfer method (aquariums): move fish from contaminated to clean aquarium, repeating daily until "Ich" disappears. Rapid water flow can help flush free-living forms.
- [[Formalin]]: 1:4000 for 1 hour daily at 10 °C or colder; 1:5000 for 1 hour daily at 10 to 15 °C; 1:6000 for 1 hour daily above 15 °C. In ponds or aquariums, 15 to 25 ppm every other day until "Ich" disappears (usually 5 to 7 days). Can also be used at constant flow at the same concentration.
- [[Malachite Green Oxalate]]: 0.1 to 0.15 ppm twice weekly in aquariums and ponds; or 2 ppm for half an hour daily for 5 to 10 days. Highly toxic to catfish under 10 cm and to bass.
- [[Copper Sulfate]]: if water carbonate is under 50 ppm, use less than 1 ppm with extreme caution; at 50 to 200 ppm carbonate, do not exceed 1 ppm; over 200 ppm carbonate, try 2 ppm (control may be difficult). A ratio of 3 parts citric acid to 2 parts copper sulfate (at 2 ppm copper sulfate) has been used in hard-water ponds.
- Temperature: for tropical fish that resist high temperatures, raise water to 90 °F for 6 hours daily for 3 to 5 days.

**Other external protozoa** (*Costia, Chilodonella, Oodinium, Trichodinids, Ambiphrya, Apiosoma, Epistylis, Trichophrya*)
- Ich methods are usually satisfactory; one application ordinarily suffices.

**Monogenetic trematodes** (*Gyrodactylus* and dactylogyrids)
- [[Formalin]]: use as for ichthyophthiriasis; one application usually suffices.
- [[Potassium Permanganate]]: 5 to 10 ppm for 1 to 2 hours; or 3 to 5 ppm in ponds and aquariums.
- [[Dylox]]: 0.25 ppm in ponds and aquariums. [[Bromex-50]] at 0.18 ppm is reported as better (Lahav, Sarig, and Shilo, 1966).

**Parasitic leeches**
- [[Dylox]]: 0.5 to 1 ppm for 5 days.

**Glochidia**
- No chemical treatment known. Remove larvae from the water supply with sand filters or 200-mesh wire screens.

**Parasitic copepods** (*Argulus, Lernaea, Ergasilus*; *Salmincola* resistant to chemicals)
- [[Dylox]]: 0.25 to 0.5 ppm five times at weekly intervals; 1 ppm when temperature exceeds 29 °C. Apparently kills adult *Argulus* and larval *Lernaea*, but not adult *Lernaea*.
- [[Bromex-50]] at 0.12 ppm is considered better than Dylox (Lahav and Sarig, 1967).
- Copepods can be removed with forceps from individual valuable fish.

### Internal Parasites

**Protozoa**
- *[[Hexamita]] (Octomitus):* Some strains are not pathogenic, but some culturists consider it harmful.
  - [[Enheptin]]: 0.2 percent of food for 3 days.
  - [[Cyzine]]: 20 ppm in dry food for 3 days.
  - [[Carbarsone Oxide]]: 0.2 percent of food for 3 days (arsenicals must not be used on fish intended for human or livestock food).
- *[[Myxosoma Cerebralis]] (whirling disease), [[Ceratomyxa Shasta]], and other Myxosporida:*
  - No practical treatment with systemic drugs is documented.
  - Do not transfer infected fish to waters lacking these organisms.
  - Destroy infected fish, disinfect the hatchery, and ensure the water supply is free of infected fish.
  - [[Quicklime]] at 1 ton per acre of drained, moist pond bottom is the best-known pond disinfectant (Hoffman, Dunbar, and Bradford, 1962).
  - The European method of rearing trout in spore-free water until 7 cm long, then transferring them to contaminated waters, is considered dangerous because it creates asymptomatic carriers.
- *[[Eimeria]] (coccidiosis):*
  - Drugs: [[Stovarsol]] at 1 mg/g of food and [[Furoxone]] show promise.
  - Pond disinfection: [[Calcium Hypochlorite]] is used in Russia at 500 kg/hectare (446 lbs per acre) on drained ponds.

**Helminths**
- *Cestodes (tapeworms):*
  - [[Di-N-Butyl Tin Oxide]]: 250 mg/kg of fish or 0.3 percent of food for 5 days.
  - [[Dibutyl Tin Dilaurate]] may be used as above.
  - Population management: increasing fishing pressure on adult pike (*Esox*) reduced *Triaenophorus* larvae.
  - Food control: bass tapeworm can be controlled by removing infected forage fish from the diet. *Eubothrium* and *Diphyllobothrium* in trout were controlled by removing infected copepods from the water supply (sand-gravel filtration) and treating the fish as above.
- *Trematodes (flukes):*
  - [[Di-N-Butyl Tin Oxide]] or dibutyl tin dilaurate may be used for adult trematodes.
  - Filtration of water supplies removes fish trematode cercariae.
  - Snails (intermediate hosts) can be controlled (see [[Molluscicides]]).
- *Nematodes:* [[Santonin]] at 0.04 g/fish has been used for sturgeon. [[Piperazine Citrate]] might be effective for intestinal nematodes.
- *Acanthocephala:* [[Di-N-Butyl Tin Oxide]] shows promise.

### Miscellaneous Controls
- **Predacious insects:** Floating oils (kerosene, diesel fuel, motor oil, vegetable oil) at about 5 gallons per acre kill air-breathing beetle larvae. [[Dylox]] at 0.5 ppm kills predacious aquatic insects.
- **Crayfish:** [[Baytex]] at 0.23 ppm killed crayfish but not fish.
- **Small crustacea** (intermediate hosts of helminths): Chlorophos (Dylox) at 0.8 ppm is effective. Calcium hypochlorite (HTH) is probably effective for most parasites but requires caution.

### Molluscicides (Snail Vectors of Trematode Cercariae)
- **Physical removal:** Pond sanitation (removing detritus by shovel or high-pressure fire hose) sometimes reduces snail populations.
- **Biological control:** [[Redear Sunfish]] sometimes reduce snail populations (Avault and Allison, 1965).
- **Copper compounds, by methyl orange alkalinity:**
  - Under 50 ppm: 2 lbs copper carbonate per 1,000 square feet of pond bottom, or copper sulfate under 1 ppm with great caution.
  - 50 ppm or greater: 2 lbs copper sulfate plus 1 lb copper carbonate per 1,000 square feet of pond bottom, or 1 ppm copper sulfate in water.
- **[[Bayluscide]]:** 0.5 to 1 ppm in water controls all snail stages but is toxic to fish. Restock 3 weeks after treatment. The granular (sinking) formulation can be used where fish will not eat the granules, but may be toxic to bottom-dwelling fish.
- **[[Dylox]]:** 0.1 to 2 ppm killed *Lymnaea* and *Planorbis* snails. 0.25 ppm has been used to control parasitic copepods without damaging fish.
