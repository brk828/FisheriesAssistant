# Birds From Coahuila, México

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ornithology

## Executive Summary

This is a 1959 University of Kansas Publications, Museum of Natural History report (Volume 11, No. 8, pp. 443–516) by Emil K. Urban. It is a systematic summary of the birds of [[Coahuila]], a northern Mexican state, based primarily on about 500 specimens held in the [[University of Kansas Museum of Natural History]] supplemented by previously published records. The report documents at least 312 named kinds representing 249 species, with a further estimated 100 species possibly to be reported, and at least 154 species probably breeding in the state. It is not a fish or aquatic study; the aquatic content is limited to waterbird and waterfowl records.

## Background & Description

The paper begins with an introduction, acknowledgments, and a list of collectors (about 25 individuals, including [[Rollin Harold Baker]], [[Harrison Bruce Tordoff]], [[James Smith Findley]], and [[Robert Lewis Packard]]). A gazetteer gives latitude and longitude for roughly 60 localities, such as Saltillo, Cuatro Ciénegas, Piedras Negras and Sierra del Carmen. The main body is an annotated species account following the [[American Ornithologists' Union Check-list]] nomenclature. Asterisks mark breeding species, and double asterisks mark probable breeding.

The front and back matter include scanned pages from the museum's publication series listing other volumes (mammals, birds and reptiles). These are catalogue debris and are summarized here only as context.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Topography and climate.** Coahuila is arid. Most mountains are below 6,000 ft, but several peaks (Sierra del Carmen, Sierra del Pino, Sierra de la Madera) exceed 9,000 ft, and some exceed 10,000 ft in the [[Sierra Madre Oriental]]. The Gulf Coastal Plain of the northeast lies at 700–1,800 ft. Rainfall averages about 23 in in the [[Tamaulipas Biotic Province]] and about 10 in in the western state.

**Biotic provinces.** Following [[Goldman and Moore]] (1945), the state has three provinces, with [[Merriam's Life Zones]] as supplements:
- **Tamaulipas Biotic Province:** northeastern lowlands, thorny shrub, yucca, agave and cactus, similar to southern Texas. Eastern-flyway migrants pass through.
- **Sierra Madre Oriental Biotic Province:** southeastern mountains with the highest rainfall, boreal conifers (spruce, pine, aspen), oak and grassland lower down. It holds the Thick-billed Parrot and several western-associated species that are disjunct here.
- **Chihuahua-Zacatecas Biotic Province:** arid western desert with island-like "pine islands." Isolation of these islands is treated as a driver of ecological release and local variation (for example, the Ladder-backed Woodpecker replacing the Hairy Woodpecker in the Sierra del Carmen).

**Origin of the avifauna.** Urban relates the fauna to climatic change from the late Pliocene through the Ice Age, when southward withdrawal and desert formation shaped present ranges. Of the breeding populations, 43% breed mainly in the western United States, 6% mainly in the eastern United States, 80% in both, 20% are restricted to Mexico plus the southern U.S. border states, and 1% (Aztec Thrush and Rufous-capped Atlapetes) is endemic to Mexico. By family-level origin, 37.0% are North American, 24.0% South American, 2.4%(as printed, "2A,7%") Old World, and 14.8% unanalyzed.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness:** at least 312 named kinds of 249 species are recorded for Coahuila, with about 100 more possible; at least 154 species probably breed in the state.
- **Ecological release:** where species are absent (for example, Hairy Woodpecker in the Sierra del Carmen), others expand their niches, and gene flow limited by isolation may favour rapid local infraspecific differentiation.
- **Disjunct western species:** Steller's Jay and Scrub Jay are absent from the Sierra del Carmen but occur in southeastern Coahuila, which is interpreted as a relict of a formerly wider distribution before aridification.
- **Range records and first state records:** the paper lists many first or new records, including the Eared Grebe (first record, 10 mi E Hacienda La Mariposa, 1952), Anhinga (first record, same locality), Goshawk (first record from northeastern Mexico), Broad-winged Hawk (first record, Packard 1957), Prairie Falcon (first specimen), Thick-billed Parrot records, and the Yellow-throated Vireo (first specimen record).
- **Subspecies intergradation:** many taxa are shown to intergrade, including Scaled Quail (pallida/castanogastris/squamata), Red-tailed Hawk, Violet-green Swallow (thalassina/lepida), Golden-fronted Woodpecker (aurifrons/incanescens), and Brown-throated Wren (cahooni/compositus).
- **Breeding evidence:** breeding is inferred from gonad size, ovum diameter, brood patches, juveniles and nests, for example Bobwhite (testes 11–16 mm), Scaled Quail (ova 14 mm; egg 23 mm long), and Band-tailed Pigeon (egg 38 mm long).
- **Notable waterbird records:** Least-Sandpiper-level detail aside, the report records Black-bellied Tree Duck, Blue-winged Teal (several spring records along the Rio Grande), American Avocet (small breeding colony, 8 mi E and 2 mi S Americanos, with a nest of four incubated eggs), and Wilson's Phalarope on the Rio Grande.
- **Parrot and migratory context:** the Thick-billed Parrot is "fairly common" in the southeast, and the report notes a flock of about 300 at 9,345 ft.
- **Field methods:** Urban uses specimen measurements (wing, tail, tarsus, culmen, weight), testes dimensions, and ovum sizes for breeding inference. Specimen counts are reported per subspecies.

**Limitations:** The document is a systematic and distributional report on birds. It contains no fish species, no freshwater or marine data, and no water-chemistry information. Its aquatic relevance is restricted to waterbirds and waterfowl, and the requested fish-specific fields are set to N/A where appropriate.
