# Anomalies of the Genus Paralichthys: Pigment, Skeletal and Double-Tail Abnormalities in North Carolina Flounders

#ichthyology #marine-biology #bothidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports pigment anomalies (ambicoloration), associated structural abnormalities, and one double-tailed specimen in [[Paralichthys Lethostigma]] (southern flounder) and [[Paralichthys Dentatus]] (summer flounder) collected in [[North Carolina Waters]] between 1960 and 1972. The double-tailed *P. lethostigma* is described in detail as the first paralichthid flounder with this condition reported in the literature. Radiographic analysis showed no skeletal abnormalities in fish with partial ambicoloration, but fish with total ambicoloration, incomplete [[Eye Migration]] and a [[Hooked Dorsal Fin]] showed a distinctive modification of the orbital region. The authors also review competing theories of how ambicoloration is induced.

## Background & Description

**Ambicoloration and pigment anomalies.** Pigment anomalies and deformities are widely reported in [[Pleuronectiformes]] (flatfishes). Earlier explanations of [[Ambicoloration]] included prolonged light exposure on the blind side, a prolonged pelagic stage, germinal factors, disrupted embryonic transformation, and mutation. [[Norman 1934]] argued that ambicoloration represents variation toward the original bilaterally symmetrical condition of flatfish ancestors. [[DeVeen 1969]] summarized further hypotheses, including light intensity at [[Metamorphosis]], total darkness during larval hatching, population density, temperature during larval development, and low food levels during larval rearing. [[Houde 1971]], working with *Achirus lineatus*, found that pre-metamorphic environmental conditions can cause pigment abnormalities, whereas DeVeen reported that abnormal coloration in plaice may develop throughout life.

**Prior North Carolina records.** Ambicolorate *P. lethostigma* and *P. dentatus* had been reported from North Carolina before this study. Earlier records included specimens with a hooked dorsal fin and ambicolorate fish without associated anomalies.

**Study material.** Seven individuals were examined, all from [[Bothidae]] genus *Paralichthys*, collected by [[Trawl Fishing]] and [[Gill Net Fishing]]:

**The double-tailed specimen (UNC 6154).** [[Chabanaud 1935]] described an atypical bothid, *Zeugopterus punctatus*, with an unusual tail whose caudal fin appeared singular. [[Okada 1950]] reported a double-tailed field-caught coffer-fish, *Ostracion farnasini* (Ostraciidae), in which both tails contained the usual 10-ray complement with three extra bony pieces between them. The present *P. lethostigma* condition is described as more extreme.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame the anomalies within the early life-history hypotheses for flatfishes. Most proposed causes act before metamorphosis, when the larva is still pelagic and its eyes have not yet migrated, so the specimens provide evidence on the relation between skeletal development and pigment pattern rather than on habitat conditions directly. The collections were made in coastal North Carolina trawl and gill-net fisheries, and the paper does not report water chemistry, temperature, or food conditions for the sampling sites.

## Key Findings

**1. Caudal structure of the double-tailed *P. lethostigma* (UNC 6154)**

- *Upper caudal fin:* The penultimate vertebra had a distorted hourglass shape. Its neural spine, normally modified into the first epural supporting a fin ray, formed a massive structure of five separate elements divided by fissures. It supported eight to nine epaxial fin rays. The haemal spine was unaffected.
- *Upper caudal ray count:* 16 rays, of which eight to nine were supported by the penultimate neural spine and seven to eight were free or articulated with the dorsal edge of the lower caudal's first epural.
- *Lower caudal fin:* Superficially normal, but it lacked the clear division between epural and hypural elements found in typical *P. lethostigma*. Typical fish have six epurals supporting six epaxial rays and five hypurals supporting five hypaxial rays. The anomalous fin had about nine epural-hypural elements supporting 12 to 13 rays, with about 17 epaxial and hypaxial rays in total.

**2. Classification of ambicoloration (Dawson 1962 scheme)**

- *Type B (almost total ambicoloration):* Two specimens (UNC 4792, *P. dentatus*; UNC 6153, *P. lethostigma*). Blind-side pigmentation was similar in both and resembled that of an anomalous *P. dentatus* described by [[Gudger 1935]]. In UNC 4792 the blind-side pattern mirrored the eyed-side pattern in shape and location. In UNC 6153 the right pectoral fin lacked pigment, while about 20 per cent of the basal portion of the right pectoral fin was pigmented in *P. dentatus*.
- *Type D (total ambicoloration, incomplete eye migration, hooked dorsal fin):* Four specimens (UNC 3495, UNC 4794 (2), UNC 6195). The blind sides were totally pigmented except for distal portions (15–80 per cent) of the right pectoral fin. Fin lengths showed no variation toward symmetry, and ray counts fell within ranges given by [[Gutherz 1967]].

**3. Pectoral fin length.** In both Type B species the eyed-side pectoral fins were longer, with no trend toward symmetry, consistent with [[Norman 1934]].

**4. Radiography**

- Radiographs of the Type B fish and the normal fish showed no abnormalities in body skeletal structures.
- In all Type D fish, the oblique crest of the left [[Frontal Bone]], which forms the posterior boundary of the right orbit, had a distinct dorsal rearward protrusion, unlike the normal fish. Because the frontals form in their final position only after eye migration is complete, this modification is attributed to the abnormal orbit position.
- The authors could not resolve the outlines of the frontals and prefrontals well enough to assess symmetry in Type D fish.
- In Type D fish, the [[Pterygiophores]] terminated at the oblique crest instead of continuing anteriorly past the orbit.

**5. Dorsal fin counts**

- Two "normal" flounders (UNC 2765, 4670) had 10 and 11 pterygiophores, each supporting one ray.
- Type D specimens had 9 or 10 pterygiophores, with 9 to 10 rays: 10 pterygiophores with 10 rays (*P. lethostigma*, UNC 4794, 6195); 9 pterygiophores with 10 rays (*P. lethostigma*, UNC 4794); and 9 pterygiophores each with a ray (*P. dentatus*, UNC 3495).
- Pterygiophores were crowded, suggesting that median fin ray number was fixed before eye migration was complete. Forward growth of the dorsal fin in Type D fish was blocked by the protruding frontal crest, but ray counts appeared unaffected.

**6. Conclusions**

- Skeletal damage does not cause ambicoloration or the hooked condition in adult *P. dentatus* or *P. lethostigma*.
- The authors support the rule of [[Gudger And Firth 1936]]: when the lower surface is colored like the upper, or when roughly one-quarter to one-third of the blind-side head surface is colored, the eye will not complete migration and the anterior dorsal fin will be hooked.
- The paper concludes that the abnormal orbital bone structure is a consequence of, not a cause of, the pigment and eye-migration anomaly.
