# Herpetofauna of the San Gabriel Mountains, Los Angeles County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #herpetofauna

*Note on scope:* The source is a freshwater and terrestrial herpetological study, not an ichthyological one. The fish tag above is a required header; the content concerns amphibians and reptiles.

## Executive Summary

This 1976 monograph by Allan A. Schoenherr (University of Southern California), published as a Special Publication of the [[Southwestern Herpetologists Society]], documents the amphibians and reptiles of the [[San Gabriel Mountains]] and relates their distribution to nine [[Plant Communities]]. Based on 242 field stations established in summer 1959, supplemented by museum and published records, the study lists 14 amphibian and 36 reptile species. The author ranks plant communities using the [[Holdridge Evapotranspiration Ratio]] and traces the herpetofauna's origins to [[Arcto-Tertiary Geoflora]] and [[Madro-Tertiary Geoflora]] lineages, with dispersal routes shaped by the [[San Joaquin Embayment]] and by the mountains' steep, flatland-poor terrain.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The range is about 102 km long, averages 32 km wide at its base, and rises to 3066 m on San Antonio Mountain (Mount Baldy). It lies at the junction of the Coast Range, Sierra Nevada and peninsular ranges.
- **Prior work:** Earlier studies were mainly checklists (Grinnell and Grinnell 1907; Bogert 1930; Dixon 1967). This paper links fauna to plant communities and ecological limiting factors.
- **Methods:** Locality records were mapped and analyzed against plant community, elevation, soil, temperature and rainfall. The author favored collecting at as many localities as possible over the sample-habitat method. Amphibians were best found by driving roads at night during rain. Lizards were taken by noosing or by shooting with .22 cal. number twelve shot. Snakes were collected by shooting and by dusk or dawn road driving.
- **Climate:** The range blocks Pacific air masses, so the desert slope is about 3 °C warmer in summer and about 6 °C cooler in winter. Annual precipitation is 689.2 mm at the mouth of San Antonio Canyon and 185.5 mm at Llano. Most rain falls in winter, and chaparral fires are common in September and October.
- **Soils:** Soils are shallow, rarely over 15 cm deep outside alluvial fill. Soil pH ranges from acid (pH 5.0) in yellow pine forest to alkaline (pH 8.0) in Joshua tree woodland.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Limiting factors.** Five principal factors affect herpetofaunal distribution: temperature, precipitation, vegetation, soil type and available food. Temperature and precipitation drive the others. Potential evapotranspiration is 58.93 × mean annual temperature (°C).

**Plant communities and moisture balance** (from the study's Holdridge ratios and deficits):

| Community | Evapotranspiration ratio | Moisture status |
|---|---|---|
| Joshua tree woodland | 4.9 | Deficit of 727.9 mm (most arid) |
| Juniper woodland | 3.14 | Deficit of 602.45 mm |
| California chaparral | 1.5 | Deficit of 321.7 mm (intermediate) |
| Coastal sage scrub | 2.03 | Deficit of 172.7 mm more than chaparral |
| Yellow pine forest | 0.79 | Surplus of 239.3 mm (Big Pines) |

Riparian woodland has a large water surplus because of permanent streams. Sagebrush scrub and pinyon pine woodland lacked climatic data.

**Community associations:**
- **California chaparral:** The largest and most diverse fauna, but no species is restricted to it. Western fence lizard, side-blotched lizard, striped racer, gopher snake and western rattlesnake are common.
- **Coastal sage scrub:** A reduced chaparral fauna with burrowing forms such as [[Western Spadefoot Toad]] and [[Legless Lizard]].
- **Yellow pine forest:** Few amphibians; [[Mountain Kingsnake]] and [[Sagebrush Lizard]] are characteristic.
- **Riparian woodland:** The richest amphibian and reptile assemblage ("gallery forest"), including most anurans and [[Western Pond Turtle]].
- **Southern oak woodland:** Salamander-dominated (slender salamanders, [[Arboreal Salamander]]), with ring-neck snake and alligator lizard.
- **Joshua tree and juniper woodland:** Desert-derived fauna, with [[Sidewinder]], [[Mojave Rattlesnake]], [[Yucca Night Lizard]] and [[Leopard Lizard]] near their upper limits.

**Dispersal and biogeography.** Amphibian lineages are mostly Arcto-Tertiary, while lizards and snakes are mostly Madro-Tertiary. Routes into the range included the [[Coast Range]], the [[Sierra Nevada]], the [[Southern Chaparral Corridor]] and the [[Mojave Desert]] via [[Soledad Canyon]] and [[Cajon Canyon]]. The author notes that the absence of flat terrain (oak savannah and pine flats) likely excludes some species.

**Human impact.** The red racer has apparently disappeared from the Pacific slope since 1930, likely due to cultivation and habitation. Gopher snake numbers are described as declining, "most likely a direct manifestation of encroaching civilization."

## Key Findings

- **Species richness:** 14 amphibians (5 salamanders, 6 frogs, 3 toads) and 36 reptiles (1 turtle, 16 lizards, 19 snakes). Two further snakes (rubber boa and Pacific garter snake) are predicted but unrecorded, which would bring the total to 38 reptiles.
- **Diversity pattern:** Chaparral has the largest fauna. Joshua tree and riparian woodlands, the two ecological extremes, have the most restricted faunas.
- **Most widespread taxa:** Western toad (*Bufo boreas*) and Pacific treefrog (*Hyla regilla*) among amphibians; western fence lizard and side-blotched lizard among lizards; gopher snake and striped racer among snakes.
- **Biogeographic origin:** Two Arcto-Tertiary coast range immigrants (*Bufo boreas*, *Rana aurora*); four Arcto-Tertiary subgroups; four Madro-Tertiary subgroups.
- **Ensatina controversy:** A spotted-form *Ensatina* was reported from [[Coldbrook Camp]] (Stager, pers. comm.) but not verified. Its presence would complete the [[Ensatina Rassenkreis]] pattern around the Central Valley.
- **Sympatry of yellow-legged frogs:** *Rana boylii* and *Rana muscosa* were confirmed sympatric in the San Gabriel River drainage, with *R. muscosa* favouring upper turbulent reaches and *R. boylii* the slower downstream sections.
- **Relict populations:** Arroyo toad (*Bufo microscaphus*) in Big Tujunga Canyon and *R. boylii* are interpreted as relicts of once wider distributions.
- **Rarity drivers:** Scarcity of flatlands and oak savannah is linked to the rarity of [[Gilbert's Skink]] and yellow-bellied racer. Steep slopes are linked to the absence of rubber boa and spotted *Ensatina*.
- **Collecting findings:** Salamanders and frogs appear on roads during rain and stop soon after. Desert lizards and snakes are best sampled at dusk or by shooting.
- **Record sizes:** Largest rattlesnake measured 1250 mm (Chilao Flats). Largest striped racer 113 cm. Largest garter snake 860 mm.
- **Conservation implications:** The author stresses protecting the chaparral and yellow pine mosaic and warns that drought and encroachment alter amphibian and snake populations.
