# Succession, Scale, and Hypothesis Testing in Streams

#benthic-invertebrates #aquatic-insects #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This Letters and Comments exchange in the *Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences* (1987, Vol. 44) debates whether [[Succession]] occurs in stream benthic communities after disturbance. [[Stuart G Fisher]] argues that [[Peckarsky 1986]], a study of [[Colonization]] by [[Aquatic Insects]] on [[Denuded Substrates]] in a New York woodland stream, tested the wrong spatial scale and used a trivially falsifiable null hypothesis. [[Barbara Peckarsky]] replies that her [[Micro-Level]] study was valid for the process it examined and that it did test the alternative null hypothesis Fisher proposed. The exchange turns on [[Spatial Scale]], the falsifiability of ecological hypotheses, and the role of [[Partial Correlation Analysis]] in separating time since disturbance from changing abiotic conditions.

## Background & Description

The document contains three items:

- **Fisher's comment**, which restates the concept of succession as the sequence of changes after a disturbance. Fisher first documented succession after [[Flash Flooding]] in [[Desert Streams]] (Fisher et al. 1982). He then proposed (Fisher 1983) that succession may not occur in [[New England Streams]], where floods are more frequent, the biota is adapted to them, and substrates are more stable.
- **Peckarsky's reply**, which defends her 1986 study and reiterates its results.
- **Fisher's rejoinder**, which is contained in the same letters section and refers back to the 1983 review, the [[Stream Ecology]] volume edited by Barnes and Minshall.

Peckarsky's study placed denuded natural substrates in enclosures in a small woodland stream and monitored colonization by benthic insects over a 48-day autumn period. The stated aim was to examine colonization and later changes in species dominance on these substrates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Disturbance scales.** Fisher characterized floods as the main disturbance in desert streams, causing high biotic mortality by scouring fine gravel. Peckarsky's manipulation caused no appreciable mortality in the stream as a whole. Fisher therefore argued that her denuded patches were smaller than the scale at which floods and ecosystem succession operate. Peckarsky countered that rock rolling and other non-flood causes create similar gaps, and that these small disturbances are ecologically relevant. She cited work on [[Rock Rolling]] by McAuliffe (1983, 1984), [[Gap Dynamics]] in forests, and [[Patch Dynamics]] in the marine rocky intertidal (Paine and Levin 1981). Sousa (1984) was cited on the general role of disturbance in natural communities.

**Scale framework.** Both authors draw on the micro-, meso-, and macro-level scale terms of [[Sheldon 1984]]. Peckarsky agrees that large-scale disturbances matter, and she argues that the best studies examine processes at several spatial and temporal scales.

**Hypothesis structure.** Fisher argued that Peckarsky's design made the hypothesis of "no site-specific succession" nearly unfalsifiable, because depopulated substrates in a stream full of mobile organisms are unlikely to stay empty. He proposed a non-trivial [[Null Hypothesis]]: time since flooding is an insignificant predictor of biological state variables such as chlorophyll and the numbers and kinds of animals. Peckarsky argued that her study tested this hypothesis by examining the separate and interactive effects of season, time since disturbance, and abiotic regime.

**Temporal limitation.** Both authors agree that the 48-day study was too short to assess seasonal effects. Fisher suggested that Peckarsky may have committed a [[Type Ii Error]] when rejecting a seasonal effect. Peckarsky accepted that the study should have run for at least one year.

**Methodological limits.** Fisher acknowledged that correlation analysis is weaker than a well-designed manipulative field experiment. He argued, however, that a planned experiment at the whole-ecosystem scale would be impractical, expensive, and possibly impossible.

## Key Findings

**Fisher's critique (Comment)**

- Succession in desert streams is well documented after flash floods (Fisher et al. 1982). Succession in New England streams may be weaker because of more frequent floods, adapted biota, and more stable substrates (Fisher 1983).
- Peckarsky's denuded patches are smaller than the disturbance scale. The manipulation caused no appreciable mortality in the stream as a whole, so it did not change the pool of potential colonists.
- The hypothesis as originally framed was trivially falsifiable. Fisher proposed a better null: time since flooding is an insignificant predictor of biological state variables.

**Peckarsky's reply**

- Her study examined "redistribution of the benthos in response to availability of newly opened habitats" at the micro-level scale. It was not designed to test ecosystem-level succession after catastrophic scouring.
- Disturbance is relevant at multiple spatial scales, and small patches are biologically relevant. She points to Paine and Levin (1981), who showed that patch size governs colonization and succession.
- Two-way ANOVA found that time since disturbance had a highly significant effect on total numbers of individuals and species colonizing cages. Season (date of cage retrieval) was nonsignificant, and season × time interactions were not significant (Peckarsky 1986, table 2).
- Simple correlations showed that colonist numbers were associated with changes in depth, current velocity, and temperature over each cage's time in the stream. Partial correlations, holding those abiotic factors constant, made the time-since-disturbance association nonsignificant (Peckarsky 1986, table 3).
- Peckarsky concluded that the time effect largely reflects the covariation between time since disturbance and changing abiotic regime. She rejected the original null hypothesis, but her reply stresses that this result applies only to the small scale and short season.

**Points of agreement**

- Large-scale disturbances are important in desert streams and are a precursor to succession.
- The study was limited in both spatial and temporal scale, and an annual study would be needed to address seasonal effects.
- Both authors see value in studying colonization and species replacement at several spatial scales.

**Overall significance**

The exchange shows that ecological conclusions about succession depend on the spatial and temporal scale of the study, and on how the null hypothesis is formulated. Its central methodological lesson is that partial correlation can reveal whether a time effect is independent of co-varying abiotic conditions. It cannot by itself settle whether a time effect is causal.
