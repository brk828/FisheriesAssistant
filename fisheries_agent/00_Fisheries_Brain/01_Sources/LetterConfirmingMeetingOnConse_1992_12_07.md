## Executive Summary

This document is administrative correspondence from The Klamath Tribe to Dr. W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University, Department of Zoology), confirming logistics for a January 8, 1993 meeting on the **conservation genetics of endangered suckers** in the **Upper Klamath Basin**. While not a primary data-bearing scientific paper, the letter is historically significant as evidence of early 1990s collaborative efforts between tribal, academic, and federal (USFWS) stakeholders to address the genetic conservation status of endangered catostomid fishes. It also references a prior, apparently unreported, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) study on hatchery-reared **Shortnose Suckers**.

## Background & Description

The letter originates from The Klamath Tribe (Chiloquin, Oregon) and is addressed to a prominent ichthyologist specializing in North American freshwater fishes. The core subject is the planning of a scientific meeting focused on **[[Conservation Genetics]]** of sucker species native to the **[[Upper Klamath Basin]]**, an inland freshwater ecosystem spanning Oregon and California known for its endemic and endangered fish fauna.

Key species implicated (based on regional context and species status) include:
- **[[Shortnose Sucker]]** (*Chasmistes brevirostris*) — explicitly named in the postscript.
- **[[Lost River Sucker]]** (*Deltistes luxatus*) — a co-occurring endangered catostomid typically studied alongside the Shortnose Sucker in Klamath Basin conservation contexts.

Both species belong to the family **[[Catostomidae]]** and were listed as endangered under the U.S. Endangered Species Act in 1988, prompting coordinated genetic and ecological research efforts such as the one this letter documents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: The suckers under discussion inhabit lakes, rivers, and tributary streams of the Upper Klamath Basin, a high-desert lake and wetland system historically vital to indigenous Klamath Tribe communities and regional biodiversity.
- **Conservation Status**: Both referenced sucker species are recognized as endangered, reflecting historical population declines linked to habitat degradation, water diversion, altered hydrology, and water quality issues (e.g., eutrophication) common to the Upper Klamath Lake ecosystem.
- **Institutional Collaboration**: The meeting reflects an interagency and inter-institutional approach involving:
  - **[[The Klamath Tribe]]** (local ecological knowledge and hatchery operations)
  - **[[Arizona State University]]** (academic genetics expertise via Dr. Minckley)
  - **[[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]** (federal regulatory and conservation authority, hosting the meeting at the Sacramento Field Office)

## Key Findings

- A formal meeting on the **conservation genetics of endangered suckers** in the Upper Klamath Basin was scheduled for **January 8, 1993**, at the USFWS Sacramento Field Office.
- The correspondence confirms active, multi-institutional interest (tribal, academic, federal) in genetic research to inform conservation strategy for the **[[Shortnose Sucker]]** and related **[[Endangered Suckers]]**.
- A postscript reveals that approximately **4–5 years prior (circa 1987–1988)**, Shortnose Sucker specimens from a tribal hatchery were sent to Dr. Minckley's lab for **mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) analysis**, but no results or data had been communicated back to The Klamath Tribe as of this letter's writing.
- This suggests a historical data gap or lost/delayed genetic dataset that the Tribe sought to recover and integrate into contemporary (early 1990s) conservation genetics efforts — highlighting the importance of long-term data continuity and institutional communication in endangered species genetic monitoring programs.
- No quantitative genetic, morphological, or population data are presented directly in this letter; its scientific value lies in documenting the initiation of a formal conservation genetics research collaboration for these endangered catostomids.
