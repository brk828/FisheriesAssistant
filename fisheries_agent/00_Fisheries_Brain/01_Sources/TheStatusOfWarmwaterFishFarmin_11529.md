# Report to the Fish Farmers: The Status of Warmwater Fish Farming

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catfish

## Executive Summary

This 1970 report from the [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]] (Resource Publication 83) reviews the state of warmwater fish farming in the American South, focusing on [[Channel Catfish]] and baitfishes. It presents the industry as a fast-growing agricultural enterprise, with about 68,000 acres under intensive culture by the late 1960s, up from 16,870 acres in 1963. It covers culture methods (broodstock, spawning, hatching, rearing), feed and nutrition, disease and parasite control, pond management and water chemistry, stock improvement through hybridization and crossbreeding, farm economics, and federal hatchery programs. It closes with a list of research needs. The report's central message is that fish farming is profitable and sound, but that its growth depends on sustained applied and basic research.

## Background & Description

**Origins.** Private warmwater culture began in the late 1920s and early 1930s with minnow raising for bait. After World War II, farm pond construction boomed, and by 1953 many farmers were also trying [[Buffalo Fish]], bass and crappie. Many of these ventures failed because of inexperience, poor pond construction, or stocking of low-value species.

**Federal research.** From 1955 to 1959 the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, using [[Saltonstall-Kennedy Act]] funds, sponsored channel catfish research at the University of Oklahoma. Later the Bureau established the [[Southeastern Fish Cultural Laboratory]] at Marion, Alabama, the [[Fish Farming Experimental Station]] at Stuttgart, Arkansas, and the Fish Farming Development Center at Rowher, Arkansas (1963). Together these are the [[Warmwater Fish Cultural Laboratories]].

**Species.** The report focuses on three catfishes:

- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*): the most desirable species for the Grand Prairie and Delta areas. Native to eastern U.S. streams and lakes, now mostly hatchery-raised. Channel and [[Blue Catfish]] both have forked tails, but channel catfish have 24 to 29 anal fin rays with a rounded margin, versus 30 to 36 rays with a straight margin in blue catfish.
- **[[Blue Catfish]]:** grows more uniformly and dresses out better, but is harder to culture and transport.
- **[[White Catfish]]:** more tolerant of crowding, high temperature and low oxygen, but dresses out poorly.

The baitfishes most cultured in the South are the [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*), [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) and [[Goldfish]] (*Carassius auratus*). The report notes that at least 20 species have been raised commercially for bait in the U.S.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water and land.** Fish farming is presented as a conservation practice that holds water longer than rice farming, improves subsoil moisture and soil texture, and can supply irrigation water. Aeration of pond water removes iron from well water, and vegetation helps desalt water. Nitrogen-rich reservoir soils (up to 224 lb N/acre, mostly stable ammonium) benefit following rice and soybean crops. Ponds also provide habitat for ducks, frogs, and other wildlife, enhancing recreational and aesthetic values.

**Water chemistry and temperature.** Water temperature is called the single most important factor for fish welfare. Water at 80°F holds 8 ppm dissolved oxygen at equilibrium, versus 12.5 ppm at 40°F. In summer, ponds stratify, and bottom water can lose all oxygen. Fish need more oxygen as temperatures rise. Channel catfish hatch best in the high 70s°F, grow best near 84°F, and feed markedly less below 60°F or above 95°F.

**Oxygen depletion.** The report identifies [[Dissolved Oxygen]] depletion as the greatest fish farming problem. Kills usually follow a [[Phytoplankton]] die-off, aggravated by decaying uneaten feed and fecal waste. Recommended responses include draining bottom water, refilling, halting feeding, and in emergencies applying up to 6 ppm potassium permanganate. Phosphate fertilizers should not be used to correct depletion.

**Chemical contamination.** Persistent pesticides (endrin, toxaphene, dieldrin) may make some abandoned cotton land unusable for ponds because these chemicals degrade slowly in aquatic and arable environments.

**Predators.** Snakes, bullfrogs, tadpoles, kingfishers, herons, grebes, mergansers and "snapper" turtles can prey on or compete with fingerlings. Sliders eat feed rather than fish. Aquatic insects can cause total losses without control.

## Key Findings

### Industry scale and economics
- About 68,000 acres under intensive warmwater culture, including 39,300 acres of catfish. In 1963 the total was 16,870 acres, with 2,370 in catfish.
- The 1969 wholesale value of the crop exceeded $33 million, roughly a $75 million retail business.
- In Arkansas, intensive catfish produced $450 gross and $150 net per acre in 1968, compared with $240 gross and $142.60 net for rice and $89.30 gross and $50.05 net for irrigated soybeans. The report notes that the "net" figures do not subtract land costs (about $27 per acre).
- Arkansas has about 3.5 million acres that could be used for fish farming.
- More than 70% of fish products consumed in the U.S. are imported.
- Lenders initially refused fish operations; the Production Credit Association reported no default by fish farmers, and the Farmers Home Administration began financing catfish farmers in Jasper, Texas.
- A typical Louisiana cost-return report (1969 prices, two 10-acre ponds) showed average net returns of $242.37 per acre to land and management, with a feed conversion of 1.53 to 1 and a production cost of $0.27 per pound. Total harvest was 48,980 pounds.

### Rice-fish-bean rotation
- Fish farming is presented as completing a rice-fish-bean rotation. Farmers report higher rice and bean yields following fish.

### Production and culture
- Some farmers have produced up to 2,000 pounds of catfish per acre. Average yield in the Louisiana report was 1,600 pounds per acre per season at 1,500 yearlings per acre.
- Broodstock of 2 to 10 pounds are preferred and can be reliably spawned in 3 years.
- Females of 1 to 4 pounds produce about 4,000 eggs per pound of body weight; larger females about 3,000.
- Eggs number 450 to 700 per ounce; yolk-sac fry number 750 to 1,800 per ounce.
- Channel catfish spawn between 70°F and 85°F, with incubation of 5 to 10 days. The male cares for the eggs.
- Three spawning methods are described: the pond method (cheapest, fish pair themselves), the pen method (greater control), and the aquarium method (hormone-induced, highest control).
- Fingerlings should be at least 2 inches before stocking grow-out ponds.
- Stocking rates for fingerlings: 1,500 per acre yields about 1 pound average in 180 days; 3,000 per acre yields about 0.7 pound.

### Feeds and nutrition
- Catfish require ten essential amino acids. Good growth occurred on purified diets with 28 to 40% protein.
- The practical formula, a 2,000-pound batch, includes fish meal, soybean meal, rice bran, distillers solubles, and alfalfa meal, among others. Fingerlings fed this diet in static water over 6 months gained over a pound at a feed conversion of 1.3.
- Guidelines call for more than 30% crude protein, more than 7% fish meal protein, and about 1,200 metabolizable Calories per pound.
- Fish meal below 10 to 12% of the diet causes slower growth and poorer feed conversion; 10 to 12% is an economic minimum.
- A 30% protein feed gives the lowest cost per pound of gain.
- Pellet types include sinking hard pellets (most common), floating expanded pellets, crumbles for small fingerlings, blocks, semi-moist feeds and agglomerates.
- Feed conversion for 1-, 2- and 3-year-old fish ranged from 0.9 to 3.2 across studies.

### Disease and parasites
- Only about 5% of fish farmers are likely to face critical disease problems in any one year.
- A [[Channel Catfish Virus]] caused major losses of fingerlings in summer 1968. It was found in five states and is highly contagious. No drug is known to treat it; quarantine and destruction are the only controls.
- [[Ichthyophthirius Multifilis]] ("Ich") is the major threat to fingerling producers and remains hard to control. Malachite green has worked in some areas.
- Plistophora ovariae, an ovarian parasite of golden shiners, has no known cure; culling and disease-free broodstock are required.
- Anchor worm (Lernaea) was controlled by [[Dylox]] (trichlorfon), which replaced benzene hexachloride (BHC) as the standard treatment.
- Only copper sulfate is cleared by the Food and Drug Administration for food fish.
- Water chemistry strongly affects chemical toxicity: copper sulfate above 2 ppm was safe in water with carbonate hardness above 200 ppm, while 0.02 ppm could kill fish in 20 ppm hardness.
- An Arkansas minnow producer lost all treated minnows after switching from a wettable-powder to an emulsifiable-oil formulation of BHC at the same rate.
- Preventive measures include excluding wild fish, treating broodstock before spawning (reported to increase fry survival by up to 100%), and maintaining good water management.

### Pond management
- Ponds should maintain at least 3 feet of water in shallow areas.
- Filling should be rapid, and surface water must be filtered (saran sock or box filters) to bar wild fish.
- Rotenone (0.5 to 2.0 ppm) kills wild fish but can take up to two weeks to lose toxicity. Antimycin A (Fintrol) selectively kills scaled fish without harming catfish.
- Algal blooms can shade out rooted plants. Over-fertilization, however, can cause night-time oxygen crashes.
- Aerating water over baffles or with agitators or compressed air increases dissolved oxygen.

### Stock improvement
- Hybrid vigor can yield up to 100% growth gains, though second-generation hybrids seldom retain it.
- In a 1966 Guidice study, channel × blue catfish hybrids grew 22% faster than channel catfish alone and 57% faster than blue catfish alone over 18 months. At 1,500 per acre they reached 1.1 pounds, versus 0.9 for channel and 0.7 for blue catfish.
- Inbreeding depression reduced carp growth by 20% in Israel (Moav, 1968). The report argues that channel catfish stocks show similar signs, including crooked spines, missing tails and eyes, and malformed mouths.
- Crossbreeding unrelated strains is recommended as the most promising immediate step, requiring little expense.

### Hatchery programs
- Of the 100 [[National Fish Hatcheries]], about 25 produce warmwater species, mainly largemouth bass and bluegill.
- In 1968 the hatcheries produced 9-1/3 million channel catfish (9 million fingerlings, 1/3 million larger fish), weighing about 125,000 pounds. Private farms produced 35 to 36 million pounds in the same year.

### Research needs
The report calls for expanded research on:
- Low-cost nutrition, including replacing fish meal with vegetable proteins plus synthetic amino acids, since feed can account for up to 40% of production cost.
- Virology, especially rapid diagnosis of carriers and whether the virus is transmitted through eggs.
- Selective breeding and hybridization, and sperm preservation.
- Drug and herbicide clearance, since no drug of significant efficacy is cleared for food fish.
- Culture in heated power-plant cooling water, confined culture (raceways, silos, cages) and recirculating water systems.
- Marketing, economics, processing waste use, and water reclamation.

The report concludes that the future of fish farming depends on the research being done, its success, and its adoption by the industry, which has historically used technical information eagerly.
