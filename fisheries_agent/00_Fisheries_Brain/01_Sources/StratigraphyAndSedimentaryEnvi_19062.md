# Stratigraphy and Sedimentary Environments of the Miocene-Pliocene Verde Formation, Southeastern Verde Valley, Arizona

#geology #sedimentology #stratigraphy #verde-formation #lacustrine-deposits

## Executive Summary

This Northern Arizona University M.S. thesis by Jason Harris Donchin (1983) examines the [[Verde Formation]], a late Miocene to early Pliocene sequence of carbonate, clastic and basaltic rocks deposited near the eastern margin of the Verde depositional basin in central Arizona. Six measured stratigraphic sections, petrographic analysis of thin sections and field mapping were used to define lithofacies and three depositional environments: lacustrine, marginal-lacustrine and alluvial fan. The study also separates spring-deposited [[Calcareous Tufa]] from lacustrine limestones and interprets [[Montezuma Well]] as a collapse sink within a Quaternary tufa mound. The document is a geological study rather than a fish or aquatic-fauna report; aquatic organisms are discussed only as fossil invertebrates, algae and plants that indicate depositional conditions.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** Southeastern margin of the Verde Valley, about 80 km south of Flagstaff and 145 km north of Phoenix, roughly 16 km north of Camp Verde. The valley is about 50 km long and 17 km wide, with its floor slightly over 1 km above sea level.
- **Objective:** Determine depositional environments, sources and stratigraphic relations of Verde Formation units near [[Montezuma Well]], and build a depositional model. A secondary aim was to distinguish local spring deposits from lacustrine limestones.
- **Formation extent:** The Verde Formation covers about 760 km²; its composite thickness is at least 950 m. It was dated by potassium-argon methods and magnetostratigraphy, with onset near 7.2 m.y. and cessation near 2.5 m.y. (Bressler and Butler, 1978).
- **Methods:** Six stratigraphic sections measured with a Jacobs staff and Brunton compass; samples taken at each lithologic change; petrographic thin sections stained for calcite (Friedman, 1959); geologic mapping at 1:24,000 from aerial photographs.
- **Thesis context:** Acknowledgements credit committee chair J. Dale Nations, committee members Larry Middleton, George Ulrich and Richard Hevly, and invertebrate identifications by Jerry J. Landye.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geologic framework.** The basin formed through a sequence of events summarized in seven steps: Laramide uplift, ancestral Verde Valley erosion, alluviation, re-excavation, Tertiary basaltic flows, damming of the Verde River by [[Hackberry Mountain Volcanism]] that created ancient [[Lake Verde]], and Quaternary re-excavation. The Verde Fault has displaced alluvial fans by up to 450 m since the Miocene (Wolfe, 1981, pers. comm.).

**Underlying units.** The Verde Formation was deposited on an irregular erosional surface cut into the Permian Schnebly Hill Formation, pre-Verde Tertiary sedimentary gravels, and pre-Verde Tertiary basalt, with relief of roughly 150 m across the depositional surface in one correlation.

**Climate and hydrology.** Pollen evidence suggests evergreen forest in the surrounding highlands and scattered desert vegetation in the valley, similar to present conditions. Perennial flow of Wet Beaver Creek is strongly controlled by springs, including Montezuma Well and Soda Springs. Summer temperatures in the valley average 74–84 °F.

**Carbonate production.** Carbonate precipitation in ancient Lake Verde is attributed mainly to photosynthetic removal of CO₂ by blue-green algae and aquatic plants, with temperature and evaporation also playing roles. The lake depth did not exceed the photic zone in the study area. The analogy to temperate hardwater lakes is described as imperfect because semi-arid conditions may have prevailed.

**Fauna and flora (fossil record).** Invertebrate fossils reported include the bivalve *Pisidium* sp. and gastropods such as *Physa virgata*, *Fossaria obrussa*, *Gyraulus parvus*, *Tryonia* sp. and *Vertigo ovata*, with unidentified ostracodes. Vertebrate fossils indicate a Blancan (late Pliocene) age for the upper formation. Charophytes, algal material and vascular plant remains are present. Fossil gastropods were interpreted as indicating a spring environment enriched in calcium carbonate. No fish fossils are reported.

## Key Findings

**Stratigraphy and age**
- The Verde Formation contains three stratigraphic units: basal fluvial conglomerates (Tvc), lacustrine mudstones and limestones (Tvl), and interbedded basalt flows (Tvb).
- Two interbedded basalt flows were dated at **5.45 ± 0.18 m.y.** and **5.66 ± 0.17 m.y.** (unpublished K-Ar dates, Damon and Shafiqullah, 1981). They lie in the upper third of the formation, just below the 5.0 m.y. Miocene-Pliocene boundary as defined by Berggren and Van Couvering (1974), and are separated by a 45 m sequence of fluvial and lacustrine sediments.
- Stated regional K-Ar ages range from 4.5 m.y. to 8.3 m.y. in earlier reports.

**Measured sections**
- Six sections were measured: Interchange Butte (69.5 m), Sacred Mountain (22.8 m), Hog Canyon (69.1 m), Beaver Creek School (54.6 m), Lake Montezuma (86.2 m) and McGuireville (52.1 m).

**Lithofacies (20 in total)**
- Carbonate lithofacies include Structureless Micrite (the most widespread quiet-water facies), Fossiliferous Micrite, Horizontally-Laminated Micrite, Sandy and Silty Micrite (the most abundant lithofacies, up to 90% of the Lake Montezuma and Beaver Creek School sections), Peloidal Micrite and Sparite, Intrasparite, Domal and Planar Cryptalgal Laminated Micrite, and Horizontally Bedded Plantiferous Micrite.
- Clastic lithofacies include Mudstone, Structureless Coarse Sandstone, several stratified and structureless conglomerates, Trough and Planar-Tabular Cross-Stratified Conglomerate, Channel-Form Cross-Stratified Conglomerate, and Structureless Upward-Fining Sandstones and Conglomerates.

**Depositional environments**
- **Lacustrine:** quiet-water, photic-zone carbonate mud precipitation, plus shallow agitated cryptalgal benches.
- **Marginal-lacustrine:** vegetated, clear-water marshes with meandering fluvial channels, spring-associated plantiferous micrite, and possible debris-flow or deltaic conglomerates at Interchange Butte.
- **Alluvial fan:** the Montezuma Well assemblage, a wedge-shaped deposit interpreted as debris flow and sheet-flood deposits; Hog Canyon deposits reinterpreted as alluvial-fan channel deposits.

**Controls on sedimentation**
- Lake-level fluctuations are attributed mainly to volcanic and tectonic damming and breaching, which had greater influence than climate. Fault movement along the Verde Fault may also have shifted environmental boundaries.

**Deformation**
- Penecontemporaneous slump structures were observed, along with a post-lithification, down-dropped anticline adjacent to Interchange Butte. The anticline (axial trend N 44° E; western limb dip up to 42°, eastern limb up to 65°) is described as the first such structure reported in the Verde Formation, with vertical displacement possibly exceeding 15 m.

**Tufa and Montezuma Well**
- Tufa deposits of spring origin range from Miocene to Recent in age. They are mapped as Tertiary-Quaternary because of dating uncertainty. Miocene-age tufa is inferred palynologically along Red Tank Draw.
- Tufa at Montezuma Well, dated as Quaternary, rests in erosional contact on basal Verde conglomerate. Its well is interpreted as a collapse sink formed by dissolution within a spring-deposited mound, with the well roughly 140 m in diameter and sounded to 16.7 m depth in 1947.
- Spring tufa is distinguished from lacustrine limestone by mound shape, fenestral texture, porosity (estimated 5–50%), and bedding parallel to a depositional surface dipping up to 80°.

**Summary of conclusions**
- The Verde Formation was deposited in lacustrine and fluvial environments near the basin margin, with frequent lake-level shifts driven mainly by volcanic and tectonic events.
- The formation contains an unconformable relationship with underlying pre-Verde units, and the Montezuma Well tufa is Quaternary in age.
