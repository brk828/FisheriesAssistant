# The Role of Stocking in the Reestablishment and Augmentation of Native Fish in the Lower Colorado River Mainstream (1998-2002)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey open-file report, prepared by [[Gordon Mueller]] in cooperation with the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Arizona State University]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], assesses whether stocking can reestablish and augment native fishes in the 60-km reach between Davis Dam and [[Lake Havasu]]. The Colorado River has been channelized, dammed and invaded by more than 80 nonnative species, and native fishes have declined sharply. Over three decades, managers stocked more than 12 million small razorback suckers (1991 and earlier), and since 1995 nearly 18,000 bonytail and 30,000 large razorback suckers into Lake Havasu.

The [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], stocked once in 1976, has become the only native species thriving in the study area, with an estimated population exceeding 4,000 large adults. The [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] show poor stocking survival (under 12% initially) and no detectable recruitment. The authors attribute this contrast mainly to habitat preference and predation: flannelmouth suckers use channel habitat with fewer predators, whereas razorback young depend on slack-water backwaters dominated by nonnative predators. They recommend preconditioning hatchery fish to flow, stocking only fish of at least 30 cm, predator removal, and creation of isolated refuge communities.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River, once the fifth largest river in the USA by volume, now seldom reaches the sea. Lower-basin storage reservoirs cover 36% of the historic channel, and levees, dredging and erosion have isolated much of the river from its floodplain. Native fish communities were reduced to remnants by the 1960s and 1970s. Wild bonytail and razorback suckers are extremely rare, and the study area has been the main refuge for remnant wild fish.

The three focal species are:

- **Flannelmouth sucker** (*Catostomus latipinnis*): historically rare in the lower basin; a successful stocked population now exists near Bullhead City.
- **Razorback sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): a federally listed species whose stocked fish have shown poor survival.
- **Bonytail** (*Gila elegans*): a federally listed species with very few captures in the river.

Study methods included passive netting, visual surveys from a boat (15-minute counts within 10 m of each bank), larval light trapping (2000-2002), PIT tagging, radio telemetry and mark-recapture analysis. The project was reduced by funding rescissions of about 60%, which focused effort on the lower 60 km.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The upper 40-km reach below Davis Dam is a degraded, braided channel with gravel and rock substrates and large, shallow backwaters, subject to hydropower fluctuations. The lower 40-km reach, from Fort Mohave to Topock Gorge, was dredged in the 1950s and 1960s and is a narrow, sandy channel with many backwaters and dense cattail marsh.

**Predator community.** Predators (bullhead, catfish, bluegill, largemouth bass, striped bass) were typically more than twice as abundant in the lower reach, where razorback suckers were concentrated. Flannelmouth suckers were concentrated in the upper channel reach, where predators were fewer.

**Fish assemblage.** Passive netting recorded more than 8,000 fish of 19 species (3 native, 16 introduced) over four years. Native species made up about 40% of the catch in the study area, largely due to flannelmouth suckers. Common carp dominated visual surveys (56.3% by number in August 2000).

**Threats.** Dredging deepened Laughlin Lagoon, which now holds more predators; a planned marina and urban development threaten a known spawning area. Turbine passage at Davis Dam, and diversions at Parker Dam, the Central Arizona Project and Metropolitan Water District pumping plants, may kill suckers.

## Key Findings

**Fish surveys**

- Flannelmouth suckers were the most numerous native fish (1,355 caught in passive netting, 766 PIT-tagged). Estimated channel density was 8.6 fish/ha, giving a population of about 4,129 in the 32-km upper reach; the mark-recapture estimate was 2,296 (95% CL 1,847-2,998).
- Razorback suckers (238 captured) were rare; visual survey estimates were fewer than 100 in the main channel (81 fish, 0.06/ha), most being in backwaters that were not sampled. Mark-recapture estimates ranged from 1,600 to 3,600 in Lake Havasu, with roughly 12% stocking survival.
- Only one bonytail was captured in the river during the study.

**Larval fish**

- Razorback sucker larvae were found in 2000, 2001 and 2002, in the lower 40-km reach (Park Moabi, Blankenship Bend, Pulpit Rock). Flannelmouth larvae were confined to the upper 16 km. Of about 3,554 larvae in 2000, 98.7% were cyprinids.
- Razorback larvae emerge earlier than other species, leaving them vulnerable to predators; flannelmouth emerge later, in the spawn of other species.

**Life history**

- Flannelmouth suckers grew 4.5 mm/month at 450 mm, declining to 0.35 mm/month for the largest fish. Ages ranged mostly from 8 to 13 years, with a maximum of 24, and annuli were hard to read.
- Ripe females averaged 57 cm and males 52 cm. Spawning occurred in March and April, at depths of 2-3 m over cobble and gravel.
- Razorback suckers recaptured after about one year grew 31.3 mm/year (smallest male) to 16.8-14.4 mm/year for larger fish. Males predominated among spawners (ripe males 28, ripe females 1 in one sampling).

**Telemetry and stocking risk**

- Post-stocking dispersal was rapid: 69.5 km/month in the Green River, 3.73 km/month in Lake Powell and 7.77 km/month in Laughlin Lagoon. Downstream dispersal was significantly lower for flow-conditioned fish (1.89 km/month) than for pond-reared fish (7.73 km/month).
- Fish held in backwaters for one year returned at nearly three times the rate of fish released directly into the river (Modde, unpublished).
- A GIS-based [[Stocking Risk Assessment]] ranked reaches from 1 (poor) to 6 (best); Lake Mead and upper Lake Mohave scored 6, the Davis Dam reach 5-6, and lower Lake Havasu 2.

**Recommendations**

1. Expand the Lake Havasu round-up to include an April bonytail survey and larval collection.
2. Mechanically remove predators from Park Moabi Lagoon and other spawning areas.
3. Stock only native fish of at least 30 cm, since survival of smaller fish was virtually undetectable.
4. Physically condition and site-acclimate hatchery fish to flow and backwaters for several weeks to months before release.
5. Refine the GIS stocking risk model and build at least a dozen small (<5 ha) refuge ponds.
6. Investigate the feasibility of establishing flannelmouth sucker in tailraces of downstream dams.

**Conclusion.** The authors conclude that recovery of razorback suckers and bonytail within the main river is unlikely while nonnative predators persist. Off-channel refuge communities, managed for isolation from predators, offer the most practical route to self-sustaining populations. The flannelmouth sucker's success suggests that channel-oriented native species may be able to re-establish in the modified river.
