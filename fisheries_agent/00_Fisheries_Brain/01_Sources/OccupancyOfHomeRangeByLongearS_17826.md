# Occupancy of Home Range by Longear Sunfish and Bluegill

#ichthyology #marine-biology #lepomis

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Gunning]] and [[Shoop]] (Department of Zoology, Tulane University) tested whether [[Longear Sunfish]] (*Lepomis megalotis*) and [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) occupy a defined stream segment continuously over about one year. Work was done in [[Talisheek Creek]], a tributary of the [[Pearl River]] in Louisiana, where year-round field work was possible. Fish were marked by fin-clipping, captured with an [[Electrical Fish Shocker]], and recaptured in two study areas. The authors estimated the home range of longear sunfish at about 70 linear feet of stream and that of bluegill at about 125 feet. They concluded that short-term occupancy of a home range is easy to demonstrate, but continuous occupancy over several months cannot yet be proven because recaptures decline sharply over time.

## Background & Description

**Home range concept.** Home range is defined as the area over which an animal normally travels. Earlier work by [[Gerking]] on stream sunfishes in Indiana reported that longear sunfish, [[Rock Bass]], and [[Green Sunfish]] moved very little from year to year. Some individuals stayed in one stream section for up to four years, close to their life span. Estimated ranges were 100 to 200 linear feet for the sunfishes and 200 to 400 feet for [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Spotted Bass]].

**Prior longear sunfish work.** [[Gunning]] (1959), studying the [[Richland Creek]] population, reported three main conclusions:
- The home range concept, as applied by Gerking, was valid.
- Longear sunfish showed [[Homing Behaviour]], returning to a home range after displacement 200 to 350 feet away.
- The olfactory mechanism was believed to mediate homing.

**Gap addressed.** All earlier longear sunfish experiments were run in summer. The mid-western climate prevents year-round study, so occupancy over a full year had not been demonstrated.

**Aims.** The authors set three aims:
1. Study home range occupancy of longear sunfish and bluegill over roughly one continuous year.
2. Compare the two species for home range size and the extent of straying beyond defined limits.
3. Determine whether longear sunfish home range limits are smaller than previously reported.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Two study areas were set up in Talisheek Creek. Section C of Study Area I and Section B of Study Area II served as marking areas. Study Area I was divided into sections A through D. The two areas were separated by a stream segment about 0.4 mile long.

**Marking.** Fish were removed, given a distinctive fin-clip (pectoral or pelvic), and returned to the segment where they were caught. Fish under 2 inches total length were not marked because the shocker does not sample them adequately. Each fin-clip was specific to a date, so time at large could be calculated.

**Sampling method.** A portable generator (230 V AC) fed a shocker with 200 feet of cable and hand electrodes. Two-person teams sampled along banks and across the channel in 20-foot segments. Blocking nets prevented fish from moving between sections during sampling. Recaptured fish were released at the centre of the section where they were taken. Some fish escaped by swimming beyond the electrical field.

**Limitations.** Flooding prevented sampling in February and March 1961. Behavioural effects of shocking were not determined. Metal tags were not tested for effects on movement.

## Key Findings

### Longear Sunfish
- **Study Area I:** 97 fish marked (20 February to 15 July 1960). Sampling on 13 occasions (18 April 1960 to 20 January 1961) produced 152 recaptures. Of these, 125 (82%) were in marking section C and 27 (18%) strayed into sections A, B, and D.
- **Study Area II:** 71 fish marked (21 July to 7 September 1960). Sampling on six occasions (28 July to 2 December 1960) produced 91 recaptures. Of these, 81 (89%) were in the marking section and 10 (11%) strayed into sections A and C.
- **Home range estimate:** About 70 linear feet of stream. This was set from Study Area I, which had the shorter marking section (70 ft versus 125 ft in Study Area II).
- **Inter-area movement:** One longear sunfish moved from Study Area I to Study Area II. Three fish marked in Study Area II were taken about 100 feet below its downstream boundary.

### Bluegill
- **Study Area I:** 84 fish marked (20 February to 15 July 1960). 70 recaptures were taken. Of these, 30 (42%) remained in section C and 40 strayed into sections A, B, and D. Almost as many moved into section B as stayed in section C.
- **Study Area II:** 68 fish marked (21 July to 7 September 1960). 44 recaptures were taken. Of these, 39 (about 89%) remained in the marking section.
- **Home range estimate:** About 125 linear feet of stream, since more than 80% of individuals in both areas stayed within this limit.
- **Direction:** Bluegill strays showed a tendency to move upstream.
- **Inter-area movement:** One stray bluegill moved from Study Area I to Study Area II, a distance of about 0.4 mile.

### Cross-Species and Temporal Patterns
- **Time at large and straying:** For both species, fish recaptured farthest from the marking section had been at large longer. The longer a fish was at large, the more likely it was to stray outside a restricted segment.
- **Body size and range:** Stray fish of both species were longer on average than fish remaining in the marking section. The authors concluded that older individuals have larger home ranges. Longear sunfish strays matched the pattern reported by [[Gerking]] (1953).
- **Habitat preference:** Bluegill movement from section C to section B may reflect better habitat. The authors treated this with caution because longear sunfish did not respond the same way.

### Short-Term versus Long-Term Occupancy
- **Short-term experiment:** 35 longear sunfish marked in section B of Study Area II on 21 July 1960 were released there. On 28 July, 27 were recaptured in section B. On 9 August, 18 were recaptured in section B. No marked fish were found in sections A or C. This supports short-term occupancy.
- **Long-term comparison:** [[Gunning]] (1959) marked 66 longear sunfish in August 1956. Only 12 (18%) were recaptured in 1957 and 1958.
- **Decline in recaptures:** Recaptures fell over time in both species and in both study areas. Possible causes were natural mortality, fishing mortality, shock mortality, and emigration. The authors judged natural mortality the main cause, since many strays would be expected near marking sections if emigration were large.
- **Mortality reference:** [[Ricker]] (1945) reported annual natural mortality of bluegill of 46% in Muskellunge Lake and 56% in Shoe Lake, Indiana. The authors noted these rates must be considered when interpreting multi-month experiments.

### Critique of Prior Work
- [[Funk]] (1957) recorded longear sunfish moving several miles, but those data came from a few anglers' tag returns. The authors argued that this does not disprove restricted ranges.

### Summary Conclusions
1. Estimated home range: about 70 ft for longear sunfish and about 125 ft for bluegill.
2. Longer time at large after marking increases the chance a fish strays from its segment.
3. Older longear sunfish and bluegill appear to have larger home ranges.
4. Bluegill strays tend to move upstream.
5. Space and time aspects of home range must be considered together.
6. Short-term occupancy is easy to show. Continuous occupancy over several months is hard to prove because recaptures fall sharply with time.

### Authors' Position
The authors regard the home range concept as valid for these populations. They noted that the bulk of a population emigrating could not be accepted without adequate recapture data. They also noted that some individuals remain in one segment, although they could not show that these form most of the population.
