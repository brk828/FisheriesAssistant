# Efficacy of Passive Integrated Transponder Tags to Determine Spawning Site Visitations by a Tropical Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pomacentridae

## Executive Summary

This study evaluates [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tags]] (PIT tags) as a non-invasive way to quantify small-scale space use in the coral reef damselfish *Pomacentrus amboinensis* ([[Ambon Damselfish]]). Across a 47-day laboratory tank trial and a 63-day field study at [[Lizard Island]], PIT-tag implantation in the body cavity of fish as small as 5.2 g did not measurably affect mortality, growth, body condition, gonadosomatic index or plasma cortisol. The tags were then used in a preliminary field application that recorded the diel timing of female visits to male nest sites, showing strong periodicity, with most time in the nest spent before dawn but most visits occurring after dawn. The authors present the approach as a template for studying activity rhythms, patch dynamics and social interactions in marine organisms.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** *P. amboinensis*, a protogynous hermaphrodite common on Indo-Pacific coral reefs. Males guard a demersal nest (an upturned clam shell or plate coral) during the austral summer, and females are attracted from the neighboring reef to spawn. Females mature at approximately 45 mm standard length (SL).
- **Study site:** Lizard Island (14°38'S, 145°28'E) on the northern [[Great Barrier Reef]]. Laboratory work ran December 2000 to February 2001; field work ran December 2002 to February 2003, at a shallow reef-edge site (about 4 m at high tide) within Blue Lagoon.
- **Tag technology:** PIT tags contain an electromagnetic coil, tuning capacitor and microchip in a glass tube. The reader used a table-top loop [[Antenna]] linked by a 6 m twisted-pair cable to a waterproof [[Datalogger]]. Housings were pressure-tested to 50 m. The smallest available tag (11.5 mm long, 2.1 mm diameter, 134.2 kHz) was used, estimated to occupy 10–15% of the body cavity of 39–62 mm SL fish.
- **Rationale:** Individual identification is essential for space-use and social studies. Earlier marking methods (acrylic paint, elastomer tattoos, fin clipping, heat branding, colored beads) required recapture or were labor-intensive. PIT tags were previously used mainly in salmonid hatcheries and in marine tag-release programs, and this study is described as the first automated use of the technology to track resource use in a marine species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Most variability in abundance of shallow marine fishes occurs at the within-site scale (below about 10 m), where organisms interact and site-attached species make behavioral choices. PIT tags suit this scale, whereas [[Satellite Telemetry]] and [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] address larger movements.
- Male nest-site quality affects reproductive success. Females appear to assess male quality through repeated visits, and the number of egg clutches a male receives is likely limited by how many reproductive females are nearby.
- The study addresses a basic gap: the social system of *P. amboinensis* was poorly known, and daily timing of nest-site activity could not previously be recorded without prohibitive effort.

## Key Findings

**Tagging effects (laboratory, 47 days)**
- 35 adult fish (40–65 mm SL, collected with clove oil and fence nets) were randomly assigned to PIT-tagged or non-tagged treatments. Twenty fish received PIT tags. Groups were held in two 1,000 L outdoor tanks (10 tagged, 7–8 untagged each).
- One tagged fish jumped from its tank. No other tag-induced mortality occurred. No tags were rejected among the remaining 19 fish, and the tag site healed within 2–4 days.
- Tagged and untagged fish showed the same growth trajectory (Figure 2).
- Tags were effective down to 5.2 g wet weight. Capture, sexing, measuring and tagging took 3–5 minutes underwater.

**Physiological condition (field, 63 days)**
- 12 PIT-tagged and 12 untagged females were compared using ANOVA (Table 1):
  - Standard length: no significant difference (p = 0.493).
  - [[Fulton's Condition Factor]] (K): no significant difference (p = 0.161).
  - Gonadosomatic index (GSI): significantly higher in tagged females (3.49 ± 0.51 vs 0.90 ± 0.66; p = 0.008). The authors attribute this to the non-random choice of nests with high reproductive success.
  - Plasma cortisol: lower in controls (40.55 vs 71.40 ng/ml) but not significant (p = 0.066).
- The authors conclude that tagging had no adverse effect on physiological condition.

**Diel visitation patterns (preliminary field application)**
- Two adjacent, reproductively successful males were fitted with readers. Forty females (39–62 mm SL) were tagged. Nests were monitored for 58 days, with a 3 s logging interval.
- Over a 9-day period with eggs present, females spent a total of 4 h 45 min in the nest. Visits started at 03:20 h, and 88% of that time occurred before dawn.
- Most visits (81%) occurred after dawn. These were brief, averaging 17 s. The 30 min after dawn had the most entries (17 females), with an average stay of 1 min 56 s.
- Females spent the longest time in the nest between 04:00 and 04:30 h, with two females staying 24 and 21 min.
- No visits occurred between 22:00 and 03:00 h.
- Twenty-four different females visited. Nine females accounted for 90% of female time in the nest; the other 15 accounted for 10%. The authors describe this as a polygynous mating system.
- The high number of short post-dawn visits is interpreted as females assessing egg content and male guarding ability.

**Conclusions and outlook**
- PIT tagging is a viable, minimally disruptive method for small marine fishes, but the authors stress that tagging protocols should be tested for each species before large-scale use, because tag-induced mortality varies among taxa.
- Pairing PIT readers with infrared cameras and a flat-plate PIT detector could expand the method. The authors suggest it could be adapted to other marine vertebrates and large invertebrates.
