# Dissolved Oxygen and Water Temperature Criteria to Protect Fish and Aquatic Life

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a two-part reprint on setting water quality criteria for aquatic life. The first paper, by [[Peter Doudoroff]] and [[Dean L. Shumway]] (Oregon State University), argues that fishery biologists should not recommend any single numerical [[Dissolved Oxygen]] standard for regulatory use until clear guidelines and defined terms are available. The second paper, by J. A. Mihursky and V. S. Kennedy (Chesapeake Biological Laboratory, University of Maryland), addresses [[Water Temperature]] criteria, focusing on thermal loading from electric power plants. The text of the second paper is truncated in the source, so its conclusions are not included.

## Background & Description

**Paper 1: Dissolved Oxygen Criteria.** The authors frame the question of how much dissolved oxygen fish need as analogous to a difficult economic question: it requires value judgments that biology alone cannot settle. They note that oxygen-depleting organic wastes from domestic and industrial sources are a major reason the question matters, because aquatic life is often affected before other beneficial uses of water are impaired.

**Paper 2: Temperature Criteria.** The second paper examines the ecological significance of temperature in aquatic habitats. It is concerned with thermal loading of ecosystems by steam electric stations (S.E.S.) and describes standards for three ecosystems: cold-water salmonid streams, warm-water [[Centrarchidae]] environments, and [[Estuaries]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Oxygen supply limits:** Activity, growth and production of cold- and warm-water fishes in unpolluted waters may be limited by oxygen supply, even near air saturation (about 9 mg/liter at 20 C). Growth limitation depends on food availability and other factors such as temperature.
- **Tolerance thresholds cited:** Salmonids, among the most sensitive fishes, have repeatedly shown effects near 3 mg/liter in laboratory studies. Concentrations not much below 2 mg/liter are probably tolerable for long periods by most fishes at moderate temperatures, and many species survive near and below 1 mg/liter.
- **Warm-water records:** Fish communities of moderate abundance and normal variety have been found in warm waters with recorded dissolved oxygen as low as 5 mg/liter (Ellis, 1937).
- **Temperature and oxygen solubility:** Oxygen solubility decreases as temperature rises, so percent-saturation standards applied across temperatures are biologically questionable, since they allow the lowest oxygen concentrations where the highest are needed.
- **Thermal loading (Paper 2):** Steam electric stations are identified as the greatest threat to altered thermal regimes because they have the largest non-consumptive industrial demand for water as a heat transfer medium. Power plants may require up to 0.5 million gallons of water per minute for cooling.
- **Nuclear vs. fossil plants:** Nuclear units are less efficient than fossil fuel plants, so they produce more waste heat per kilowatt of electricity. Planned nuclear units of 1,000 to 3,000 MW compare with 100 to 700 MW for current units.

## Key Findings

### Dissolved Oxygen (Doudoroff and Shumway)

- **No single numerical standard should be recommended yet.** Biologists should decline to specify a minimum dissolved oxygen level for warm- or cold-water fish populations until terms such as "pollution," "criteria," and "standards" are defined.
- **Only full-protection standard:** Virtually no reduction below natural levels would fully protect fishery resources, but this is usually unrealistic and unnecessary for waste regulation.
- **Units:** Any simpler standards applied across temperatures should be expressed in mg/liter, not percent saturation.
- **Diurnal fluctuations matter:** Wide daily swings between low and high oxygen should be avoided. Repeated exposure to low levels, even for moderate durations, can be nearly as harmful as continuous exposure. Average oxygen values have no clear biological significance and no proper place among standards.
- **Food and growth interaction:** Growth of fish fed unrestricted rations is more strongly affected by oxygen reduction than that of fish on restricted rations. Oxygen concentrations far below saturation may not impair growth of fish on low rations.
- **Swimming performance (Figure 1):** For wild juvenile [[Coho Salmon]] in groups of five, swimming failure velocities at 10, 15 and 20 C were compared against oxygen concentration. At 10 and 20 C, equal percentage reductions in sustained swimming speed occurred at nearly the same oxygen percent saturation, not the same concentration. The authors flag this as needing verification.
- **Embryo development (Figure 2):** Dry weights of newly hatched coho salmon alevins were measured against oxygen concentration during incubation at 9–10 C and 300 cm/hour water velocity.
- **Growth on diurnal cycles (Figure 3):** Underyearling coho salmon on restricted and unrestricted rations were tested at constant and fluctuating oxygen levels at 18–19 C for 18 and 21 days. The figure reports weight gains as percentages of dry weight.
- **Steelhead alevin findings:** At very low water velocity (10 cm/hour), growth of [[Steelhead Trout]] alevins was markedly inhibited by low oxygen after hatching. Exposure of embryos to low oxygen before hatching had no marked effect on later growth.
- **Physiological effects:** [[Bouck and Ball]] (1965) found changes in serum protein electrophoretic patterns in [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] exposed to 3 mg/liter for 8 hours on each of 9 successive days.
- **Marine data gap:** Very little data exist on dissolved oxygen requirements of marine fishes.
- **Existing standards:** Minimum values of 5 mg/liter for warm-water and 6–7 mg/liter for cold-water fish habitats are widely accepted. Although not initially based on firm evidence, the authors consider them intermediate and defensible.
- **Policy critique:** The 1966 Federal Water Pollution Control Administration guidelines (12 pages) were judged vague. They allowed variable, economically and technologically based standards and did not clearly separate effluent standards from receiving-water standards. The authors call for a joint, multidisciplinary study of water quality control principles.

### Water Temperature (Mihursky and Kennedy)

- **Projected demand:** Projected 1980 power needs were expected to use one-fifth to one-sixth of total U.S. freshwater runoff for cooling. Discounting flood flows, which occur about one-third of the year and account for two-thirds of runoff, cooling would require about half of total runoff for the remaining two-thirds of the year.
- **Local pressure:** In some heavily industrialized northeastern watersheds, over 100% of flows may pass through power stations during low-flow periods.
- **Growth in electricity demand:** U.S. electricity needs were said to double every 10 years, and in some areas every 6 years. Projected from 1960, this implies a 30- to 256-fold increase by 2010.
- **Documented discharge temperatures:** A Pennsylvania advisory committee (1962) recorded S.E.S. discharges of 100–115 F and river temperatures up to 95 F almost 5 miles downstream of a plant.
- **Planned scope:** The paper states its aim is to use ecological principles to judge temperature regulations for preserving aquatic life. Its full analysis of standards for each of the three ecosystems is not included in the source text.
