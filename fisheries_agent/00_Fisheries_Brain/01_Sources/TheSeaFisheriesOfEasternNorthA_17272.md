# The Sea Fisheries of Eastern North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-science

## Executive Summary

This historical report surveys the sea fisheries of Eastern North America north of Delaware Bay, covering the principal food and bait fishes, the methods and grounds of capture, the utilization and economic value of catches, and the natural history, migrations, and mortality factors affecting fish stocks. It was prepared in the context of the [[Treaty of Washington]] and the [[Halifax Fisheries Commission]] of 1877. The author notes that US fishery statistics were incomplete, relying on [[Customs Records]], state inspection reports, and special correspondence, while [[Canada]] maintained a more systematic government survey. The report documents heavy historical declines in anadromous fish and inshore species, while cod, mackerel, bluefish, and herring were judged not to have declined essentially in numbers. It also emphasizes that natural predation and mortality far exceed human catches.

## Background & Description

**Scope and structure.** The work is organized into five divisions: (I) natural history or biology, (II) methods of capture, (III) utilization of products, (IV) maintenance and improvement of fisheries, and (V) general political considerations. The author acknowledges that the material is incomplete and calls for further cooperative research. Southern fisheries are noted only briefly, with [[Menhaden]], [[Bluefish]], and [[Striped Mullet]] highlighted as important there.

**Data sources.** Information came from:
- Reports of the [[Massachusetts Commissioners of Inland Fisheries]] and the Maine commissioner
- Manuscript records of weirs and pounds, supplied by Colonel Lyman
- Correspondence with fishing captains and port officials, including those at [[Gloucester]]
- Printed circulars of questions distributed to observers
- Testimony and conferences at the Halifax convention
- Manuscript notes from [[Dr. H. C. Yarrow]] and contributions from [[G. Brown Goode]]

**Principal species checklist.** The checklist is headed "18 principal food and bait marine fishes," but its numbering runs to 76 entries, covering flatfishes, cods, hakes, scombrids, carangids, sciaenids, sparids, serranids, clupeids, sharks and rays, and sturgeons, among others. Common names and ranges are given for each, for example Atlantic Cod (polar regions to Cape Hatteras), Atlantic Mackerel (Greenland to Cape Hatteras), and Menhaden (Nova Scotia to Brazil). Invertebrates used as food or bait are also listed, including squids (*Loligo pealii*, *Ommastrephes illecebrosa*), Quahog (*Venus mercenaria*), Lobster (*Homarus americanus*), and Blue Crab (*Callinectes hastatus*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Temperature.** Water temperature is identified as the most important factor in fish distribution. Herring were typically taken at temperatures not exceeding 50–55°F, and cod at lower temperatures. On the New England coast, [[Cape Cod]] functioned as a practical boundary between two faunal regions. Winter bottom temperatures were nearly uniform, which pushed delicate species offshore or toward the edge of the [[Gulf Stream]].

**Migration and movement categories.** The author sorts fishes into groups:
- *Anadromous* (shad, salmon, alewife, sturgeon, smelt, striped bass)
- *Catadromous* (eel, the only example cited)
- *Inshore* (scup, tautog, cunner, sea bass, and others)
- *Offshore* (cod, haddock, hake, halibut, pollock)
- *Pelagic* (herring, bluefish, menhaden, mackerel, swordfish, bonito)
- *Deep-sea* (poorly known; some taken at 100 to 2,900 fathoms during the [[Challenger Expedition]])

**Food webs.** Diatoms and small crustaceans, especially the copepod *Temora longicornis*, were described as the base of the food chain. Herring feed heavily on copepods, and whales, mackerel, and sea birds feed on herring. Predation is intense throughout: bluefish drive menhaden ashore, porpoises attack mackerel schools, dogfish appear in huge schools and disrupt fishing, and sharks, seals, and gannets are major consumers. The stomach analyses cover more than 50 fish species.

**Natural mortality and disasters.** The author cites several sources of mortality:
- Very low egg-to-juvenile survival (for salmon or shad, "not five eggs out of one thousand," per calculation)
- Cold snaps that killed large quantities of fish along the Gulf coast and at [[New River]], North Carolina
- Volcanic and chemical events, such as the 1876 Cocos Islands lagoon die-off
- Storms that stranded or buried herring at [[Anderson's Cove]], New Brunswick
- Mass stranding of [[Capelin]] on beaches during spawning

**Human influence.** Dams that block rivers cause decline in anadromous runs within about six to eight years. Removal of obstructions, fishways, and replanting are proposed as remedies. The author cautions that removing predators such as belugas or bluefish can have unintended effects on prey and other predators.

## Key Findings

**Fishery economics and utilization**
- Mullet ([[Striped Mullet]]) was described as a staple of the Southern fishery. Yarrow reported about 12,000 barrels taken at Fort Macon alone and 70,000 barrels in Carteret County, North Carolina. Two-thirds of the North Carolina coastal population was said to be employed in the fishery.
- On 20 November 1872, 3,000 bluefish averaging 12 lb (36,000 lb total) were shipped from one North Carolina station.
- Gloucester losses from 1830 to 1873 included 1,252 men and 280 vessels, or about 28 lives and 6 vessels annually, with property losses of $1,145,500.
- The 1865 products table for fifteen countries (codfish, herring, whale oil, mackerel, sardines, cod-liver oil, seal oil, pilchards) totaled $59,606,218, which the author considered an underestimate.
- Norway's fisheries averaged about $12,710,000 in 1866, according to Baars.
- The Caspian Sea catch was estimated at 68,000,000 lb (about $10,500,000) by Schultz.
- Potomac shad and herring catches were compared with the Scottish fishery. The author noted that six months of Potomac barreling equaled the entire 1873 Scottish yield. In 1873, the Potomac yielded 8,541,851 lb of shad, herring, and bunker, and in 1874 about 16,122,533 lb.
- Refrigerated rail and ice transport allowed salmon to reach New York from the Restigouche in thirty hours.

**Stock status**
- Shad, freshwater herring (alewife), salmon, and striped bass showed the most certain declines, along with striped bass and scup on the coast.
- Cod, mackerel, bluefish, and sea herring were judged not to have declined essentially.
- Historical accounts of abundance (such as 950,000 herring in one seine haul at the Potomac in 1832) were cited to illustrate past productivity.

**Major fishing grounds.** The report describes more than 30 banks and grounds, with dimensions, depths, bottoms, and target species. Key entries include:
- [[George's Bank]]: the largest US-coast ground, which hosts winter cod spawning aggregations and a Gloucester fleet of about 100 vessels.
- [[Grand Bank]]: the largest fishing ground in the Western Atlantic, mainly cod and halibut.
- [[Western Bank]]: a major cod and halibut ground around Sable Island.
- Other grounds include Brown's Bank, Jeffrey's Ledge, Bankquereau, Saint Pierre Bank, and Flemish Cap.

**Stomach-content findings.** Stomach analyses were compiled from 1871–1872 work at Wood's Hole, Eastport, and Great Egg Harbor. Some examples:
- Cod ate large numbers of brittle-stars, crabs, shrimp, and bivalves, and Trumbull's Stonington samples added many shell species.
- Bluefish fed on mackerel, menhaden, and scup. One bluefish held 47 scup.
- Menhaden were found to ingest bottom mud, apparently for microorganisms.
- Whales were noted to feed heavily on copepods.

**Recommendations and plans.** The author called for:
- A systematic national statistics program, such as Canada's
- Protection, artificial propagation, and legislation for anadromous fish
- Cooperative research and wider questionnaires to fishermen
- Caution about fishing regulations, because natural predation removes far more fish than human fisheries
