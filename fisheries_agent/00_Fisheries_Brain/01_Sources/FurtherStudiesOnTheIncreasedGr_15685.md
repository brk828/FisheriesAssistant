# Increased Growth Rate of Rock Bass Following Reduction in Population Density

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports follow-up work on [[Rock Bass]] (*[[Ambloplites Rupestris]]*) in the south basin of [[Booth Lake]], Michigan. In 1937 the fish of the south basin were poisoned to reduce the population, and the author, [[William C. Beckman]] of the [[Michigan Department of Conservation]], tracked the response. Growth increased in every age group, too much to be explained by normal year-to-year fluctuation. Additional 1941 and 1942 collections allowed the change in weight, not just length, to be analyzed, and the mean [[Coefficient of Condition]] (K) also rose, from 3.07 before poisoning to 4.10 after. The author concludes that reducing numbers is one solution to lakes overrun with [[Stunted Fish Populations]].

## Background & Description

The study builds on an earlier report by [[Beckman (1940)]] on the same lake, which first documented the growth increase after the south basin was treated. The earlier report relied on the [[Age and Growth Analysis]] method, with ages determined by counting [[Scale Annuli]]. The present paper adds two data sets:

- **July 20, 1941:** 25 rock bass, collected by [[Fly Fishing]].
- **May 24–25, 1942:** 37 rock bass, collected by [[Fly Fishing]] plus four experimental [[Gill Netting]] sets. Each net was 5 × 125 feet with five mesh panels ranging from 1½ to 4 inches (stretched measure; the scan reads "11%").

Rock bass were a legal sport fish at 6 inches. At the time of poisoning, 5 of 1,233 rock bass recovered were legal size. After poisoning, 289 rock bass were taken, of which 20 were legal size. Fishermen reported that the lake was regaining some of its former popularity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pre-treatment condition.** Before poisoning, the rock bass population was dense and growth was stunted, especially in older fish. The author attributes this to food limitation for older fish. Young-of-the-year fish are mainly [[Plankton Feeding]] predators, and the lake's plankton appears to have supplied them adequately. That explains why first-year lengths barely differed before and after treatment.

**Post-treatment food supply.** With fewer fish competing for food, the remaining individuals grew better. Post-1937 growth was judged better than the tentative state average for the species (unpublished data).

**Stocking after treatment.** Several fish were introduced after the poisoning:

- 1937: 25 adult [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*)
- 1938: 200 [[Largemouth Bass]], 5,000 [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and 1,500 [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*)

No bluegills had been recovered at the time of writing. Perch were already present, so their stocking success could not be judged. The insufficient perch sample also prevented any conclusion about their growth. Black bass were successfully established and reproducing. The author suggests that [[Predation]] by bass and [[Interspecific Competition]] for food could hold rock bass numbers down, while rock bass prey on young bass, potentially allowing both species to grow normally.

## Key Findings

**1. Length growth increased after density reduction.**
- Growth in length rose markedly in all age groups after the 1937 poisoning and stayed high for four years.
- Males grew slightly faster than females, and the male advantage was more evident after poisoning.
- [[Grand Average Calculated Lengths]] (Table 3, mm, males/females):

**2. Weight growth and condition increased.**
- The [[Length-Weight Relationship]] for post-poisoning fish is W = 3.597 × 10⁻⁵ L^3.025 (W in grams, L as standard length in mm; log W = −4.4440488 + 3.025 log L).
- The exponent of 3.025 is close to 3, consistent with the [[Cube Law]]. Comparison exponents were 3.003 for [[Nebish Lake]] rock bass (Hile 1941) and 2.969 for combined Michigan samples (Beckman, unpublished).
- Mean K before poisoning was estimated from 13 fish (79–99 mm, collected August 1937) at **3.07**. After poisoning it was **4.10**.
- Estimated weights at equal length:
  - 50 mm: 4 g before vs. 5 g after
  - 75 mm: 13 g before vs. 17 g after
- Because no individual weights were taken before treatment, pre-poisoning weights were calculated from the mean K rather than the post-poisoning equation. The author judged this acceptable given how close the exponents are to 3.

**3. Management implication.**
- Reducing population density improved growth in length, weight, and condition.
- The author concludes this is "one solution to the problem of improving fishing in lakes overrun with stunted fish," since treated lakes recovered legal-size fishing.

**4. Open questions.**
- Whether black bass will keep rock bass numbers low, and whether predation will stabilize both species, remained to be seen. The author planned continued observation of the lake.
