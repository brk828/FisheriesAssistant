# Fisheries Problems Associated with the Development of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Stephen J. Nicola (as recorded in the document's header, which reads "Stephen J. Nicol3" under OCR), reviews the development history of the [[Lower Colorado River]] and its effects on the native fish fauna and sport fishing. Nearly 100% of the river's average annual flow is appropriated for agricultural, domestic and industrial use. Dams, dredging, channelization and salinization have altered habitat, favoring non-native species while native fishes declined. The paper concludes that maintaining sport fishing and restoring native species will remain a formidable challenge for managers, with the future shaped by rising water demand and continued habitat alteration.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] is the fifth longest river in the United States, rising in the Rocky Mountains at about 40° N latitude and emptying into the [[Gulf of California]] at about 31° 40′ N. It drains a watershed of 6.27 × 10⁵ km², roughly 7.7% of the continental United States surface area. Its length is 2,250 km, compared with 6,690 km for the [[Nile River]], which discharges far more water. Pre-development average annual discharge was 17,500 hm³. The river has not reached the Gulf since 1951.

The lower Colorado (584 km from [[Hoover Dam]] to the Gulf) forms most of the Arizona–California border. Its gradient is about 0.3 m/km, and it carries a heavy silt load. Peak sediment transport past Yuma was estimated at 130 × 10⁶ m³ per day during peak flows.

Sixteen native fish species (eight families) occur in the Colorado River. Eight of these were believed to have occurred in the lower river. Two marine species, the machete (ten-pounder) and threadfin shad, enter the lower river on brief forays. Native fishes such as the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Sucker]] show specialized adaptations to swift, turbulent water, including a large nuchal hump in the chubs and large body size in the squawfish, which reportedly reached 185 cm and 45 kg.

The full introduced and native species checklist (Table 2) lists roughly 40 species; the most significant introduced species are [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Common Carp]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Tilapia]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and temperature.** Impoundment by Hoover, Davis and Parker dams shifted peak discharge from winter floods to summer irrigation releases. Water temperature below Hoover and Davis dams stays near 13 °C year-round, compared with a pre-impoundment range of 10–30 °C. Parker Dam partly restores the natural temperature regime because [[Lake Havasu]] is shallower.

**Sediment and channel change.** Reduced floods caused silt to accumulate in the channel, raising water tables on the floodplain. Summer releases that raise water levels up to 5 feet scour the bed below reservoirs. Silt deposition then reduces backwater habitat. The town of Needles was flooded by rising waters from Lake Havasu after Parker Dam was completed.

**Dredging and channelization.** Since 1949 the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] has dredged and channelized sections of the river. Dredged silt is deposited along the shoreline and in backwater marshes, lagoons and lakes. Current deflectors narrow the channel, and the resulting stable shorelines reduce the backwater habitat fishes use as spawning and nursery areas. Dredging below the Cibola Diversion Dam was over-deep, causing "natural" scouring that is lowering the water table and drying out backwater lakes.

**Riparian vegetation and salinity.** Introduced [[Salt Cedar]] (Tamarix spp.) transpires large quantities of water. The Bureau is attempting to re-establish [[Cottonwood]] (Populus fremontii) to suppress it. Irrigation leaching in the Palo Verde and Imperial valleys steadily increases dissolved solids in the main channel downstream. Salinity rose from about 1,150 ppm (pre-development) to higher values downstream.

**Aquatic weeds.** More than 5,600 km of irrigation canals in the Palo Verde and Imperial valleys host aquatic macrophytes. Control by dredging and herbicide has been costly, and the introduced [[Grass Carp]] has been only partly successful.

## Key Findings

- **Development intensity:** Nearly 100% of the Colorado's average annual flow is appropriated; the river is one of the most developed in the world.
- **Native fish decline:** Squawfish were last verified captured in 1967 and are considered nearly extinct below Lake Mead. The humpback chub has not been taken since dam construction. The bonytail chub persists in small numbers in lakes Havasu and Mohave, with the last verified capture in 1975. Adult humpback suckers occur occasionally, and only one juvenile has been recorded.
- **Exotic species expansion:** Changed conditions favored catfish, carp, black bass, sunfish, and trout (the latter supported by a hatchery below Hoover Dam). Blue catfish, carp and channel catfish suited the changed environment.
- **Fishing trends:** Catch rates at Lake Havasu fell from about 0.5 fish per angler-hour in the 1940s to less than 0.2 fish per hour by the 1960s. Biologists then introduced new sport species, including striped bass, white bass and flathead catfish.
- **Mitigation limits:** Constructed backwaters take nearly a decade to become ecologically functional and never fully compensate for lost fishery values.
- **Remaining habitat:** Of the 584 km of river below Lake Mead, only 128 km remains essentially unmodified flood-plain habitat.
- **Future pressure:** Rising demand from upper-basin states, Mexico, nuclear and fossil power plants, and tribal claims may leave insufficient water by the turn of the century. The author expects some sport fishing to persist, but its quality depends on further habitat alteration, the extent to which modified habitat can be made productive, and whether biologists can develop new fish communities.
- **Conservation policy:** The U.S. Endangered Species Act of 1973 spurred recovery efforts and reintroduction studies for the squawfish and bonytail chub, which may also help maintain game fish populations.
