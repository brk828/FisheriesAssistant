# Bylas Springs and the Endangered Sonoran Topminnow: Long-Term Relationships to Changes in Climate

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 Annual Report from the [[Arizona Fishery Resources Office]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) documents the first year of a long-term monitoring program on the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) isolated in three small springs of the [[Bylas Springs Complex]] on the [[San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation]], Arizona. The study was funded under the [[Fishery Resources Monitoring Program]] climate change component and asks how localized, persistent climatic change affects marginal spring habitats that hold the species. Thermographs recorded stream temperatures hourly, water quality and discharge were measured monthly, and topminnow abundance and distribution were sampled seasonally. Topminnow were found in two of the three springs and remained absent from the middle spring (S2).

## Background & Description

The Sonoran topminnow is a poeciliid fish endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico and to the Yaqui, Mayo, and Sonora basins of Sonora, Mexico. It was listed as endangered in 1967 under the [[Endangered Species Act]] because of predation by introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and habitat degradation. Of the 10 known natural stocks remaining in the Gila River Basin, two occupy the Bylas springs, which lie adjacent to the Gila River floodplain. Base flows range from a few liters to a few tens of liters per minute, and the lower reaches become intermittent as water desiccates or percolates into the terrace bordering the floodplain. The complex sits in upland Sonoran Desert at about 859 m elevation, with average annual precipitation of about 25 to 28 cm.

The springs also support two endemic hydrobiid snails, *Tryonia gilae* and *Apachecoccus arizonae*. Management has focused on controlling introduced mosquitofish, while surface water depletion and loss of habitat remain the primary threats.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The three springs are named by convention: S1 is the westernmost, S2 the middle, and S3 the easternmost. Each spring has distinct hydrology:

- **S1:** The only spring that originates from two locations; it showed the greatest temperature variation. Its barrier site and east spring head held the highest topminnow concentrations.
- **S2:** Characteristically reduced discharge; temperatures were consistently higher than in S1 and S3. Low discharge increased habitat homogeneity, and topminnow were absent.
- **S3:** Relatively high discharge (tens of liters per minute) that buffered within-month temperature variation.

Water quality, measured monthly at spring heads and a downstream locality, showed high variability. Conductivity was high across the complex, ranging from roughly 1,290 to about 5,900 micromhos depending on site, and dissolved oxygen was often low (below 4 ppm at several heads). Air temperatures peaked in June and July 1993, and rainfall was most prevalent in January and August. Climatological data came from the [[Arizona Meteorological Network]] station at Safford Agricultural Center.

The authors propose that the interaction of discharge, ambient temperature, and rainfall regulates the quantity and quality of habitat needed to sustain the species. Sustained discharge reductions, such as those expected under increased regional aridity, may raise base-flow temperatures and reduce shallow pool habitat. Apart from competition and predation by mosquitofish, which may limit juvenile survival, these changes are the main concern.

## Key Findings

- **Temperature sensitivity:** All springs responded to changing ambient temperatures. The S2 spring recorded consistently higher temperatures than S1 and S3, while S3 showed minimal variation.
- **Thermograph network:** Five thermographs were deployed by the end of the reporting period (two each in S1 and S3, one in S2), after two additional units were added in spring 1993.
- **Habitat quality:** Habitat did not differ appreciably from 1992. Topminnow were found only on pool margins and in deeply incised spring heads.
- **Abundance:** The highest counts came from the pool at the East spring head of S1, ranging from 14 (June 1993) to 47 (July 1993), averaging 32 fish per sampling date.
- **Distribution:** Topminnow were collected at the S3 barrier on two of seven sampling dates and at the S3 road crossing on one of five dates. None were captured at the S1 barrier. Mosquitofish made up the majority of fish captured at the S1 East spring head.
- **Water chemistry ranges:** pH ranged from about 6.3 to 10.3 across sites; dissolved oxygen from 0.4 to 11.8 ppm; conductivity from about 1,290 to 5,900 micromhos. The S3 barrier had the highest conductivity (4,400 to 5,900 micromhos).
- **Management implication:** Survival of Sonoran topminnow may ultimately depend on the resilience of marginal habitats to global climate change, and multi-year discharge-frequency and flow-duration curves are needed to predict population viability under different climate scenarios.
- **Study objectives:** Hourly temperature monitoring; monthly water quality and discharge; seasonal abundance and distribution surveys; vegetation mapping; quantifying effects of rainfall events; and compiling historical data.
