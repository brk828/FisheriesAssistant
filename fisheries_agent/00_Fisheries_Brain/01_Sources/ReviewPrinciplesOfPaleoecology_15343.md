# Preliminary List of Rare, Restricted, and Threatened North American Freshwater Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a preliminary conservation inventory of rare, restricted, and threatened North American freshwater fishes, compiled by [[Robert Rush Miller]] of the [[Museum of Zoology]], [[University of Michigan]]. It covers roughly 65 taxon entries, arranged alphabetically by U.S. state, with brief notes on foreign occurrences. Each entry gives the known distribution, the threats, and, in many cases, a specific recommendation for habitat protection. The author states that a more expanded treatment is planned for publication and solicits additions.

## Background & Description

The list addresses species whose ranges are small, whose populations are isolated, or whose numbers have fallen sharply. Many are relict forms, endemic to single springs, streams, or basins. Examples include:

- **Monotypic or single-genus relicts:** [[Eremichthys Acros]] (desert dace), [[Moapa Coriacea]] (Moapa dace), and [[Novumbra Hubbsi]] (Olympic mudminnow), which the author describes as one of three relict genera in western North America.
- **Highly specialized forms:** [[Gila Cypha]] (Humpback Chub), adapted to very swift water, and [[Ptychocheilus Lucius]] (Colorado Squawfish), described as the largest minnow in the New World.
- **Cave and subterranean fishes:** [[Satan Eurystomus]] (Widemouth Blindcat) and [[Trogloglanis Pattersoni]] (Toothless Blindcat), each known from only three specimens taken from artesian waters near San Antonio, Texas.
- **Unique all-female reproduction:** [[Poecilia Formosa]] (Amazon Molly), which occurs naturally in the U.S. only in Cameron, Willacy, and Hidalgo counties, Texas.

Several entries are salmonids or game fishes. Among these are [[Salmo Gilae]] (Gila Trout), [[Thymallus Arcticus]] (Arctic Grayling), and [[Micropterus Treculi]] (Guadalupe Bass).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document repeatedly identifies the same threats:

- **Habitat destruction and alteration:** Stream impoundment, spring modification, filling of river mouths, and "rehabilitation" of trout streams. The Texas [[Tidewater Goby]] habitat is threatened by filling of estuaries and domestic pollution.
- **Chemical poisoning:** Rotenone and other poisons used in fish eradication or management killed populations of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] in the Colorado River system (September 1962), of [[Gambusia Nobilis]] near Toyahvale, Texas (February 1962), and of [[Cyprinodon Elegans]] and related stocks in the Balmorhea-Toyahvale area.
- **Introduced and exotic species:** Competition and predation by [[Gambusia Affinis]] (mosquitofish), [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bullfrog]], and other exotics. These are cited as threats to Ash Meadows pupfish and killifish, Clear Creek gambusia, Comanche Springs pupfish, and Gila topminnow.
- **Genetic swamping:** Hybridization with introduced *Gambusia affinis* is a possible threat to [[Gambusia Heterochir]] (Clear Creek Gambusia).
- **Water withdrawal:** Winnemucca Lake has been dry for many years, and the lower Truckee River flow is insufficient for the spawning migration of [[Cui-ui]].
- **Restricted range:** Many species survive in one or two localities, so a single disturbance could eliminate them.

The author frequently suggests protected areas, including [[Dinosaur National Monument]], [[Death Valley National Monument]], [[Ash Meadows]], [[Big Bend National Park]], and state or federal reserves, along with management measures such as banning chemical treatment and restricting collecting.

## Key Findings

**Most seriously threatened species**
- The author identifies [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] as the most threatened Arizona species. It survives only in a small section of East Clear Creek, a fraction of its former range. A five-mile reserve with no chemical treatment is recommended.
- [[Gila Crassicauda]] (Thicktail Chub) may already be extinct. Only six specimens have been collected since 1936.
- [[Maryland Darter]] (*Etheostoma sellare*) was thought extinct after 1912 and was rediscovered in 1962, when a single young individual was seined from Swan Creek. It is described as one of the rarest American freshwater fishes.
- [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*) lives in a single spring in Nye County, Nevada. Its home range is smaller than a backyard swimming pool. The author advises trying to establish it in another suitable habitat.
- [[Ash Meadows Killifish]] (*Empetrichthys merriami*) may already be extinct, and the author judges survival unlikely because of competition from exotic animals.
- [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*) is one of three species in its genus, and all three are threatened with extinction.

**Possibly extinct or unverified**
- [[Modoc Sucker]] (*Catostomus microps*) was taken only in 1898 and 1933. A 1961 electrofishing survey on Rush Creek found none.
- [[Owens Valley Pupfish]] has not been taken since 1939. A 1963 report of survival was being investigated.
- [[Sharphead Darter]] (*Etheostoma acuticeps*) is evidently extinct. Its type locality was inundated by impoundment.
- [[June Sucker]] (*Chasmistes liorus*) may be extinct, and its status needs evaluation.

**Small populations and key localities**
- The only large population of [[Ozark Cavefish]] (*Amblyopsis rosae*), estimated at about 100 individuals by T. L. Poulson (1963), lives in the Cave Springs town water supply, Benton County, Arkansas.
- [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) now survives in only two Arizona areas and in spring-fed ditches about 8 miles south of Safford.
- [[Okaloosa Darter]] (*Etheostoma okaloosae*) has a very limited range, but it lies mostly within Elgin Air Force Base, which lowers the risk of modification.
- [[Olympic Mudminnow]] (*Novumbra hubbsi*) is known from three Washington sites, and the author recommends a sanctuary.
- [[Blotched Gambusia]] (*Gambusia senilis*) is known in the U.S. only from Devils River, Val Verde County, Texas.

**Recommendations for protected areas**
- A five-mile section of East Clear Creek for the Little Colorado Spinedace.
- The upper part of Soldier Meadows, Nevada, to protect [[Desert Dace]].
- Several specified springs in the White River system, Nevada, for the [[White River Killifish]].
- Devils River, Texas, which holds five endangered species: *Dionda diaboli*, *Notropis proserpinus*, *Cyprinodon* sp., *Gambusia senilis*, and *Etheostoma grahami*.
- The San Marcos spring area, Texas, to protect [[Fountain Darter]] and other Edwards Plateau fishes.
- The Pine Hills, Union County, Illinois, state recreation area, to protect [[Spring Cavefish]] and a relict bantam sunfish population.

**Other notes**
- The [[Tule Perch]] and [[Sacramento Perch]] could be restored to Thurston Lake, California, after removal of exotic species.
- [[Suwannee Bass]] (*Micropterus notius*) is threatened by illegal spearing and by a proposed cross-state barge canal in northern Florida.
- [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) is restricted to swift sections of the Virgin River system in Utah, Arizona, and Nevada.
- The [[Least Chub]] (*Totichthys phlegethontis*) is the only endemic fish genus of the ancient Lake Bonneville basin, and it has served as a mosquito control agent.
- The Gila trout population in Diamond Creek is protected by federal and state law. A three-year biological study began in New Mexico on April 1, 1962.
