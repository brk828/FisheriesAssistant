# Age and Growth of Pike (Esox lucius) in Windermere

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 reprint from *Salmon and Trout Magazine* by Frost and Kipling reports the age and growth of [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) in [[Windermere]], England. Ages were determined from [[Opercular Bone]] readings rather than [[Scale Analysis]], which the authors found unreliable without correction. The paper also qualifies earlier English growth data from [[Hartley 1947]] and compares Windermere pike with Irish and Scottish populations. Long-term [[Gill Netting]] has reduced the size and age structure of the population.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius* L.), the main piscivorous fish of British and Irish freshwaters.
- **Prior record:** Hartley (1947) provided the first British and Irish growth record, based on 94 pike from small rivers and dykes in East Anglia (River Cam and Shepreth Brook). Hartley used scales for age only and did not back-calculate growth. The authors believe he overestimated ages, so true growth was likely faster than published.
- **Windermere study:** Earlier data were published by Frost and Kipling in 1959 (Table 6) and 1961 (Figure 4). This article summarises that work for accessibility.
- **Methods:** The authors used [[Opercular Bone]] reading as the main method, which they describe as a first for this species. Scales were used only in conjunction with bones. Opercular annuli appear as milky white rings, but in pike of 50 cm (20 in) and over, discoloured spongy tissue can obscure the first or second ring. Operculars therefore determine growth but not age alone.
- **Sampling:** Since 1944–1945 an annual winter [[Gill Netting]] programme has removed pike over 50 cm and 3 lb. Nearly 7,500 opercular bones were read over 20 years. The 1946–1947 season (75 males, 117 females) forms the basis of Tables I and II.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Growth pattern:** Males and females grow equally for the first two years. Thereafter males grow more slowly, and the gap widens each year. This applies to both length and weight.
- **Size and age records:** The largest fish recorded weighed 35 lb, measured 43¼ in, and was aged 14 (female). The largest male weighed 15 lb. No large males were recorded.
- **Effects of netting:** Netting initially reduced numbers, but populations recovered to near pre-netting levels. Pike over seven years, which once formed about a third of adults, are now scarce. Ages 3–5 dominate catches. Mean catch weights fell: females from 11 lb (1944–45) to 7 lb (1963–64), males from 6 lb to 4 lb.
- **Maturity and reproduction:** Most males (80–83%) and females (92%) mature in their third year. Pike spawn annually. The authors infer very high early mortality, possibly in the first weeks of life, citing Swedish studies.
- **Diet:** Pike begin eating fish at about 2 in long and continue throughout life. At age three, a pike weighs about 3 lb, while a [[Brown Trout]] of the same age weighs about 3¼ lb.

## Key Findings

1. **Sex-based growth divergence:** Female pike grow larger than males from year 2 onward. Mean back-calculated lengths at age 12 are 95 cm (females) and 79 cm (males). Mean weights at age 12 are 9,220 g and 4,530 g.
2. **Opercular method validated:** Opercular bones gave reliable growth data, while scale back-calculation required correction.
3. **Population shift from netting:** Gill netting has shifted the population toward younger fish and reduced the number of large individuals.
4. **Comparison with other waters:**
   - [[Hartley 1947]] (East Anglia): slower growth, likely underestimated because of age overestimation.
   - Irish loughs (Healy 1956): Lough Rea grew fastest of the three; Barnagrow was slowest.
   - Loch Choin, Scotland (Munro 1957): slower than Windermere and the Irish loughs, with females larger than males.
5. **Life history:** Early maturity, high fecundity, long life, and few predators (apart from humans) make pike highly successful in freshwater systems, according to the authors.
