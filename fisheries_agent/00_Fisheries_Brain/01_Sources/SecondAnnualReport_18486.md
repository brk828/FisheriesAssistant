# Windy-Gap Fishes Study: Second Annual Report (1983 Field Season)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This second annual report from the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] covers 1983 field work on endangered endemic fishes of the Upper [[Colorado River]] Basin. Work was conducted by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service under a cooperative agreement with the Northern Colorado Water Conservancy District. The study focuses on the first-year survival bottleneck of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]], and on the value of river backwaters and [[Gravel Pits]] as nursery habitat. Its main results cover spawning-migration routes, spawning-site fidelity, larval and young-of-the-year (YOY) production, and the first tests of hatchery stocking into backwaters. Results are preliminary, and the report notes that final conclusions are deferred to a later report.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River fish community was historically dominated by endemic species, which are vulnerable because of their restricted ranges. The [[Endangered Species Act]] listing of Colorado squawfish, humpback chub and [[Bonytail Chub]] means federal water projects must not jeopardize these species, which has made the fishes central to water-development disputes.

Three objectives drove the program:

- Identify and describe reproductive habitats, and quantify rearing areas, for young Colorado squawfish and humpback chub.
- Identify the major factors that affect first-year survival.
- Evaluate natural and modified backwaters and gravel pits as habitat, and determine how far adults move within the river system.

The work is organized into two elements: movement, spawning and rearing studies, and backwater and gravel pit investigations.

A taxonomic concern also runs through the study. Chubs (*Gila*) with intermediate morphology suggest that gene exchange between [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] may be occurring, possibly because habitat change has eroded reproductive barriers. The bonytail chub, a close relative, was described as nearly extirpated from the Upper Basin.

Study areas included the Colorado River from Palisade, Colorado to the upper part of Lake Powell, the lower 3.5 miles of the [[Gunnison River]], the lower 20 miles of the [[Yampa River]], and the [[Green River]] from its confluence with the Yampa downstream. The main Colorado River section (about 200 miles) was split into upper, middle and lower reaches. Humpback chub work was concentrated at [[Black Rocks]], a narrow, deep-water igneous-rock reach, with [[Westwater Canyon]] about 15 river miles downstream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Adult Colorado squawfish over 450 mm TL were implanted with radio transmitters following [[Radiotelemetry]] procedures described by Tyus (1982). Battery life was 6 months in 1982. In 1983, transmitters with 10- and 18-month theoretical lives were used. Humpback chub were radio-tagged with about 9 g transmitters (approx. 90-day battery). Larval fish were collected from backwaters and shoreline with 0.5 mm mesh nets and preserved in 5% formaldehyde ([[Larval Fish Collection]]). YOY surveys used 3 and 6 mm seines, sampled September through October, and were averaged within 10-mile reaches. Adult chub were taken by trammel nets and angling and assessed for nuptial tubercles, gonadosomatic index (GSI), relative condition (K) and ovum diameter. Morphometric and meristic characters were measured to separate *Gila* taxa ([[Gila Speciation]]).

**Backwater studies.** Natural backwater populations were too sparse for study, so hatchery fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico) were stocked. These fish were about 3 months old and 50-100 mm long, and each was marked with a coded-wire microtag.

**Hydrology and habitat.** The 1983 flood breached many dikes between the Colorado River and adjacent gravel-pit ponds. This created many new backwaters, which the authors expected to benefit endangered fishes. Spring floods also pushed Colorado squawfish into shallow flooded alfalfa fields in the upper Yampa and lower Green rivers, a habitat use not previously documented.

**Discharge.** Green River YOY survival is linked to flow. Cold water and increased discharge from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] are suspected in the 1983 year-class failure.

## Key Findings

### Adult movement and spawning

- **Green River and Yampa River.** Twenty-nine adult Colorado squawfish (reported TL = 636 mm) were radio-implanted in 1983: 15 in the lower Green and 14 in Dinosaur National Monument and the upper Yampa. Contact was made with 28 of 29 fish in late summer. Fifteen moved 50 miles or more, and 11 migrated to known spawning areas.
- Eight fish from the Yampa and upper Green migrated to the lower 20 miles of [[Yampa Canyon]] and apparently spawned there. The pattern closely matched 1981 (FWS) and 1982 (NPS/CSU) observations.
- Three fish from the lower Green migrated to the [[Three Fords Rapid]] area of [[Gray Canyon]] and apparently spawned. Two adults successfully swam upstream over the Tusher Wash Diversion Dam to reach these grounds.
- Two adults moved about 80 miles into [[Labyrinth Canyon]], where both were located near RM 40 in August. No ripe fish were collected there. One fish moved into [[Cataract Canyon]], but spawning there is unconfirmed.
- Netting at spawning grounds captured 20 ripe or spent Colorado squawfish in the Yampa River (13 at RM 18-18.2, 7 at RM 16.4-16.5). Twelve ripe fish were taken at Three Fords Rapid. Fidelity was demonstrated: a fish tagged on the spawning ground in 1981 and another tagged in 1982 were recaptured there in later years.
- A fish radio-tagged in 1982 that did not migrate then did migrate and spawn in 1983. The authors suggest immaturity, alternate-year spawning, or surgical stress as possible causes.

### Colorado River movement

- Five of six radio-tagged fish that moved after release returned to near their release site within 2-3 months.
- Transmitter failure appears to be common. Fall 1982 fish with 18-month transmitters lost contact after about 9 months, suggesting real battery life was well below the manufacturer's rating.
- A mature Colorado squawfish in the Gunnison River showed no extensive movement during its contact period.

### Humpback and roundtail chub

- Radio contact was maintained with only 4 of 10 implanted adult humpback chub. Two fish could not be tracked after release, and contact with four fish lasted no more than 2 weeks.
- Of the four tracked fish, two showed no displacement, one moved about 0.3 mile, and one moved more than a mile downstream outside Black Rocks. No definitive spawning site was identified.
- Three of four radio-tagged humpback chub present in late June were near their release points, in deep-water areas among igneous rocks, suggesting spawning nearby.
- Controlled tests showed radio signals could not be detected from transmitters deeper than about 5 m, while the area has 10-15 m water. Some fish may have been missed for this reason.
- Nuptial tubercles were present on pectoral fins of nearly all male roundtail and humpback chub from May to late July. Female humpback chub showed pectoral tubercles only in the last week of June. Tubercle occurrence was poorly correlated with gonad data, except for female humpback chub pectoral tubercles, which may indicate spawning. The decline in K was associated with spawning, but other physiological processes may confound it.

### Larval fish

- **Yampa River:** 104 larval Colorado squawfish were collected from 17 samples (about 10% of all fish specimens taken from the Yampa). Spawning occurred by 10 August, with larvae most abundant 19-30 August.
- **Green River:** Only 7 samples were taken. Larvae first appeared on 15 September (7 larvae, 13.8-22.2 mm TL). Shallow backwaters below the Gray Canyon site were largely absent because of high water.
- Colorado River larval collections had not been analyzed at the time of writing.

### Young-of-the-year (YOY) surveys

- **Colorado River:** Mean catch per unit effort in September and October 1983 was generally higher than in 1982. However, the mesh sizes differ (6 mm in 1982, 3 mm in 1983), so the years are not directly comparable. Age-0 fish appeared in the upstream reach in 1983, where none were found in 1982, possibly due to finer mesh.
- **Green River:** Data from 1979-82 indicated good nursery habitat below Jensen, with abundance peaks in the Ouray and Labyrinth Canyon areas. In 1983, very low catch per unit effort indicated poor YOY survival. The cause is unknown but is probably linked to cold water and high discharge from Flaming Gorge Dam, and is being investigated.

### Backwater and gravel pit experiments

- About 55,000 hatchery Colorado squawfish were stocked on 13 October 1983 into the [[Walter Walker Wildlife Area]] (WWWA) backwater, a former gravel pit breached by the 1983 flood. Ten fish were found 27 km downstream 6 days after stocking. Most later captures were near the stocking site, and relative densities declined as fish dispersed beyond seine coverage.
- CPUE at WWWA was 0.252 CS/m² (17-21 Oct), 0.098 (24-28 Oct), 0.174 (1-4 Nov) and 0.009 (14-18 Nov). Upstream and downstream reaches yielded much lower catches, and the stocked fish were essentially absent from the downstream reach.
- Five gravel-pit ponds received 31,000 hatchery fish in October 1983 (2,625 to 9,750 per pond). Ponds differed in predator-prey fish communities, with centrarchid predators present in four and absent in Fish Chalet.
- A two-way fish trap at WWWA caught one adult Colorado squawfish before the flood destroyed it. A replacement trap near Clifton, Colorado is planned.
- Stocked fish collected later were 110-120 mm, about 50 mm longer than at stocking.

### Planned work for 1984

- Radiotelemetry of Colorado squawfish confined to the upper and middle Colorado River, and reduced in the Green River to Dinosaur National Monument through the Ouray reach, focusing on Split Mountain (about 10 adults).
- Tagging of 18 humpback and roundtail chub at Black Rocks.
- Larval sampling at suspected spawning sites in the Green River, and river-wide Colorado River larval sampling.
- Fall YOY surveys in both rivers, linking spawning success and recruitment to 1984 discharge.
- About 70,000 hatchery YOY Colorado squawfish from Dexter NFH for backwater and gravel-pit studies, and a possible new gravel-pit trap near Clifton.
- Discharge-habitat studies below Flaming Gorge Dam, funded by the Bureau of Reclamation.
