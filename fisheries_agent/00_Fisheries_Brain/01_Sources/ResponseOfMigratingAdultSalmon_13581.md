# Response of Migrating Adult Salmonids to Vertical and Horizontal Rectangular Orifices at Two Depths

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Special Scientific Report (No. 547) by Clark S. Thompson, William Spencer Davis, and Emil Slatick tests how migrating adult salmonids respond to [[Fishway]] entrance orifices that differ in alignment and depth. The experiments were run from August 24 to September 20, 1962, at the [[Fisheries-Engineering Research Laboratory]] at [[Bonneville Dam]], as part of a [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] program to develop design criteria for [[Fish Passage]] facilities on the [[Columbia River]]. The three species tested were [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Steelhead Trout]], and [[Coho Salmon]]. The main result is that vertical orifices generally drew more fish than horizontal orifices, and shallow orifices (3 ft submergence) generally drew more fish than deep ones (9 ft).

## Background & Description

**Study species.** Chinook salmon (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*), steelhead trout (*Salmo gairdneri*; printed as "airdneri" in the scan), and coho salmon (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*) are anadromous salmonids. All were adults actively moving upstream when tested.

**Engineering context.** At Columbia River dams, the [[Powerhouse Fish-Collection System]] is a long channel with several entrances. Migrants attracted by turbine discharge have to find entrance ports above the turbine draft tubes. Earlier Corps of Engineers work had shown:
- Salmonids entered the collection system more readily through a submerged orifice than over an overfall weir (U.S. Corps of Engineers, 1948).
- Shallow orifices, submerged 3.8 to 6 ft, were more effective than intermediate (14.8 ft) or deep (30 ft) orifices (1952 and 1954 studies; U.S. Corps of Engineers, 1960b).
- More fish used vertical than horizontal orifices in 1960, but the results were not conclusive (U.S. Corps of Engineers, 1960a).

**Experimental facility.** The [[Laboratory]] could deliver and discharge up to 200 cubic feet per second (c.f.s.). Lighting came from 1,000-watt mercury-vapor lamps giving about 700 foot-candles at the water surface, similar to natural light in the main Bonneville fishway on a bright cloudy day. Test fish came from the Washington shore fishway, passed through the orifices, and were counted as they left upstream transportation channels.

**Orifices.** Each orifice was a 2- by 5-ft rectangular port. Panels could be turned 90° to switch between vertical and horizontal alignment. Solid panels filled the remaining space.
- Deep setting: centerline 9 ft below the surface (elevation 49.75 ft).
- Shallow setting: centerline 3 ft below the surface (elevation 55.75 ft).
- Water depth immediately downstream of the orifices was 19 ft.

**Hydraulics.** A constant 1-ft head on each orifice produced a calculated flow of 49 c.f.s. and an average velocity of about 8 ft/s. Shallow orifices created surface turbulence in the introductory pool, while deep orifices produced little or none.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Approach behavior.** Fish were released into the [[Introductory Pool]] as they arrived, so several could be in the area at once. The authors inferred that migrants approached the test panel at depths between 3 and 9 ft. Fish entered an orifice if it lay in their plane of travel. If none did, they apparently searched upward and laterally for the first passable opening, and when searching downward, they stopped at the first passable opening.

**Laboratory limits.** The approach area was 19 ft deep, but orifice depth was limited to 9 ft by the existing sill of the transportation channel. Deeper submergence might have given clearer results, particularly for vertical orifices placed at shallow versus deep settings.

**Prototype considerations.** In real powerhouses, turbine discharges come from depths beneath the collection-channel ports. Competing attraction flows could therefore change the vertical distribution of fish approaching a collection channel. This effect was absent in the laboratory, so the results may not transfer directly to the field.

**Behavioral observation.** The authors stressed that all orifices, regardless of shape or location, were entered by at least some members of every species on every test day.

## Key Findings

### Experimental design
- Four test conditions were run in two blocks. Block 1 compared deep horizontal vs. deep vertical and shallow horizontal vs. shallow vertical. Block 2 compared shallow vs. deep horizontal and shallow vs. deep vertical.
- Test periods lasted 2 days, with 3 or 4 replications per condition. Orifices were swapped between the north and south channels halfway through each period to offset channel bias.
- Responses were reported as average percentages per orifice and tested against 50% with t-tests on arcsine-transformed values (Snedecor, 1957).

### Test condition 1: deep horizontal vs. deep vertical
- Chinook salmon: 69.4% entered the vertical orifice (significant at 95%).
- Steelhead trout: 62.6% entered the vertical orifice (significant at 95%).
- Coho salmon: the vertical share did not differ significantly from 50%.
- Table totals (OCR order): 807, 2,737, and 202 fish.

### Test condition 2: shallow horizontal vs. shallow vertical
- No species differed significantly from 50%.
- Chinook salmon and steelhead trout favored the vertical orifice in three of four replicates. The reversal in the second replicate contributed to the nonsignificant mean.
- Only two replicates were run for coho salmon. They showed more fish in the vertical orifice, but the difference was not significant.

### Test condition 3: shallow horizontal vs. deep horizontal
- Chinook salmon: 78.6% entered the shallow horizontal orifice (significant).
- Steelhead trout: 69.7% entered the shallow horizontal orifice (significant).
- Coho salmon: 61.8% entered the shallow orifice, but the two replicates (136 fish) were not significant.
- Table totals (OCR order): 720, 2,131, and 136 fish.

### Test condition 4: shallow vertical vs. deep vertical
- No species departed significantly from 50%.
- Chinook salmon tended toward the shallow orifice in all replicates, with variable strength.
- Steelhead trout in the first two replicates tended toward the shallow depth. In the third, they were nearly evenly split.
- Coho salmon tended toward the 9-ft depth.

### Lateral (north–south) bias
- In nearly all tests, a given orifice drew a larger share when placed on the south side of the introductory pool. The only exception was steelhead trout in test 2, replicate 2.
- Mean differences were significantly above zero for all three species. Coho salmon had the largest mean difference (32.9 percentage points).
- Because of this bias, the authors ran a second analysis that excluded south-side entrants. Only the coho salmon result in test condition 3 changed, becoming a significant preference for the shallow horizontal orifice.

### Conclusions
- Vertical orifices generally suited migrants better than horizontal ones.
- Shallow orifices generally suited migrants better than deep ones, at 3 and 9 ft centerline submergence.
- Unequal use appeared when both orifice types sat at 9 ft (vertical preferred, since it extended farther above and below mean depth) and when horizontal orifices sat at 3 and 9 ft (shallow preferred).
- No unequal use appeared when both orifice types sat at 3 ft, or when vertical orifices were compared at two depths.

### Limitations
- Only 9 ft of orifice depth range was testable, which limits generalization to deeper submergence.
- Results from the four test conditions could not be combined into one statistical analysis, so the general pattern was inferred by inspection.
- The design assumed similar approach patterns on both days of each 2-day test period.
- Turbine-induced attraction flows, absent from the laboratory, could alter vertical distribution at operating dams.
