# Sustainable Development for the Rio Grande/Rio Bravo Basin: 1994 El Paso Conference Statement

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This bilingual (English/Spanish) conference statement records the outcome of a three-day meeting held in [[El Paso]], Texas, on May 19-22, 1994. About 100 participants from Mexico and the United States, including residents, government agency staff, NGOs and business representatives, discussed sustainable development across the [[Rio Grande/Rio Bravo Basin]]. The statement reflects general agreement, but no participant was asked to sign it, and participants spoke for themselves rather than for their institutions.

The statement identifies population growth, water scarcity and poor water quality as the basin's most common problems. It argues that ecological boundaries, not political borders, should guide planning. Its four main recommendations are to strengthen local and sub-basin working groups, plan a basin-wide [[Rio Grande/Rio Bravo Coalition]], create a basin-wide public institution, and launch a sustainability education program.

## Background & Description

The conference was the culmination of two years of planning under the [[Sustainable Development Initiative]], founded by five institutions: [[El Colegio de la Frontera Norte]], [[Houston Advanced Research Center]], Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León, University of New Mexico and University of Texas at El Paso. Major funding came from the [[Ford Foundation]], with additional support from Big Bend National Park, the General Services Foundation and the Jamail Foundation. Five working groups (New Mexico, El Paso/Ciudad Juárez, the Central Border, the Lower Rio Grande Valley/Reynosa/Matamoros and the San Juan River) prepared reports beforehand. The statement is signed for the convening institutions by René Franco and [[Jurgen Schmandt]], director of the [[Center for Global Studies]].

The basin drains about 185,000 square miles and is one of the largest in North America. Roughly 10 million people depend on the river. Its waters rise in the snow-capped mountains of Colorado and New Mexico, and downstream flow is sustained only by Mexican and US tributaries. The river forms about 2,000 km of the international border. The basin is arid or semi-arid, with fragile ecology, and is described as "a region at risk."

The participants framed two central questions: how to build a viable future that respects both people and the environment, and what kinds of organizations are needed to plan and implement sustainable development.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water and hydrology**
- Water is scarce, especially during recurrent, often long-lasting droughts. Streamflow is below safe norms, causing decline of wildlife and wetlands.
- Many reservoirs and dams were built during the 20th century. Major new dams are planned only in the [[Rio Conchos]] basin. The last large reservoir, [[La Cuchillo Dam]] on the Río San Juan, was nearing completion.
- Groundwater levels are falling and major aquifers are overpumped. The Paso del Norte aquifers are projected to run out of potable water within 30 years.
- The Lower Basin's groundwater is too saline for most uses.

**Pollution and health**
- Sewage treatment is absent or outdated in many places, and illegal dumping of solid and hazardous waste is widespread.
- Water-related diseases are common near several urban centers. Toxic discharges occur but are poorly documented.
- More than 2,000 [[Maquiladora]] plants operate in the Mexican border region, some contributing hazardous wastes.

**Habitat and biodiversity**
- The Upper Basin in New Mexico is where four major biomes meet: Great Basin shrub steppe, Great Plains grassland, conifer forest and [[Chihuahuan Desert]]. Disturbance can disrupt interactions in these transition zones.
- In the Central Border, riparian greenways have been bulldozed for warehouses, roads and Army Corps of Engineers projects. This increases erosion and salinity.
- In the Lower Basin, nearly 95 percent of the unique brushland ecosystem has been destroyed. Lush wetlands below Matamoros and Brownsville face a proposed channel dam that local residents fear would damage them.
- Many species are threatened in the Lower Valley, including the castor, jaguarundi and various fishes. Many species protected in the United States are not protected on the Mexican side of the San Juan region.

**Fisheries and coastal links**
- The fish population has declined from decade to decade in the basin.
- About 25 percent of fish and shrimp harvests from the [[Gulf of Mexico]] come from lagoons and coastal areas near the river mouth. These fisheries depend on healthy seagrass beds, which are threatened by river pollution. The lagoons are also polluted by two river branches used to divert wastes from the main stem.
- Adequate streamflow is needed for the river-sea interface.

**Climate and development**
- Economic development is constrained by limited water. Climate change could reduce availability further. Erosion, desertification and biodiversity loss are of particular concern.

## Key Findings

**Regional key issues**
- Population has doubled over the last 30 years, and similar growth is predicted. Only the Pecos River tributary has a stable population, and San Miguel County there is growing fast.
- The poorest counties in the United States lie along the river. Unsewered substandard housing ([[Colonias]]) exists on both sides of the border.
- Indigenous communities struggle to secure water rights despite the [[Winters Doctrine]]. Farming for the Cochiti tribe ceased because of dam construction.

**Sub-basin findings**
- **[[Upper Rio Grande Basin]]:** Open-pit mining near Questa threatens water supplies. Irrigation and competition among urban, rural and agricultural users strain limited water.
- **[[Paso del Norte]]:** El Paso and Ciudad Juárez have nearly two million people, many in extreme poverty. Irrigation and low rainfall leave the river as "nothing but a trickle." Illegal hazardous and solid waste dumping and severe air pollution damage health and desert ecosystems.
- **[[Central Border]]:** Between [[Amistad Reservoir]] and [[Falcon Reservoir]], Mexican cities lack modern water infrastructure. Nuevo Laredo planned a new state-of-the-art water treatment plant. Piedras Negras and Ciudad Acuña lack water treatment and sewage facilities.
- **[[Rio San Juan]]:** Water scarcity persists even with the nearly completed La Cuchillo dam. Distribution of resources is inequitable, and erosion and waste discharge into the Río Pesquería are concerns.
- **[[Lower Basin]]:** Close to two million people live in the Lower Valley and Reynosa/Matamoros. Irrigation water rights are being sold to cities. Matamoros and Reynosa urgently need wastewater treatment plants.

**Vision and principles**
- Sustainable development must address social, economic and ecological components while respecting cultural diversity and ensuring an adequate standard of living.
- Planning should use ecological boundaries rather than political borders, with the basin as the central unit for assessment, information sharing and research, while still attending to local and sub-basin conditions.
- Citizen participation is essential. Surface water management has been weak in environmental and economic coordination, and groundwater and biodiversity protection is largely absent.

**Recommendation I: Strengthen local and sub-basin working groups**
- Working groups now exist in the Upper Rio Grande, Paso del Norte, Central Border, Lower Basin and San Juan. Groups are encouraged to create groups in the Río Conchos and Pecos River sub-basins.
- Each group should prepare a 1-year work plan and a 3-year general plan.
- Work has relied mainly on volunteers. Modest funding is recommended for staff, office costs and travel.

**Recommendation II: Plan a basin-wide Rio Grande/Rio Bravo Coalition**
- The coalition would be a nongovernmental network open to Mexico, the United States and Native American tribes. It would share information, facilitate local work and, over time, partner with governmental and international agencies.
- Guiding principles include developing a new basin identity; restoring the river so people can drink and swim in it; giving high priority to ecosystem protection; and building consensus across sectors.
- Proposed activities include networking by email or newsletter, education, sponsoring research, monitoring change, publishing "State of the River Reports," advocacy and fundraising.
- The [[Steering Committee]] was to be broadened, with each working group nominating a representative, and to complete its work by May 30, 1995.

**Recommendation III: Create basin-wide public institutions**
- No truly basin-wide resource management institution exists. The statement calls for an advisory intergovernmental organization with tasks including a natural resources inventory, an information clearinghouse, identification of areas needing critical management, and policy recommendations. It should be transparent and governed by a representative board.
- The International Border and Water Commission (IBWC) was debated. Some wanted expanded authority over conjunctive surface and groundwater management, and others wanted it abolished. No agreement was reached, but participants agreed it should be more open to citizen input.
- Members of the [[Border Environmental Cooperation Commission]] (BECC) should be knowledgeable about the border region. BECC and the [[North American Development Bank]] (NADBank) were expected to fund infrastructure improvements.
- Governments should ease restrictions that hinder cooperation between federal, state and local staff across the border.

**Recommendation IV: Develop a sustainability education program**
- Broad public education on citizens' options and their consequences is the most important step, using volunteer work, public service announcements and mass communication.
- Existing public education programs should be reviewed for consistency with sustainability. The Valley Proud Environmental Council and Vecinos del Río Bravo are cited as binational examples.
- Business and labor should participate in defining sustainability with economic feasibility in mind. The Business Council on Sustainable Development and the Rio Grande Workers' Association are given as models.
- Youth education and exchange of technologies and community principles between cultures are also recommended. The Rio Grande International Study Center is cited as an example.
