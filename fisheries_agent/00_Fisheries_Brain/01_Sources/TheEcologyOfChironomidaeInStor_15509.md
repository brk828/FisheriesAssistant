# The Ecology of Chironomidae in Storage Reservoirs

#entomology #freshwater-ecology #chironomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by J. H. [[Mundie]] (Imperial College, London; communicated by P. Freeman, received 5 September 1956, read 5 December 1956) reports a three-year survey (February 1950 to February 1953, with a 1954 extension) of the non-biting midges of storage reservoirs supplied by the River Thames in Middlesex. The main study site was [[Kempton Park East Reservoir]], with [[Staines South Reservoir]] and [[Queen Mary Reservoir]] as comparison sites. The work combined [[Emergence Traps]] (submerged funnel traps), [[Jenkin Surface Mud Sampler]] core sampling of larvae, and oxygen and temperature measurements to address species distribution by depth, emergence phenology, voltinism, standing crop and annual production. The survey concludes that the profundal chironomid community of eutrophic reservoirs is controlled largely by benthic oxygen supply, which is itself linked to nutrient status and morphometry, and that trapping efficiency is a major, unresolved limitation.

## Background & Description

The Chironomidae (= Tendipedidae) are a large family of nematocerous Diptera, with c. 3000 known species according to the paper, and are the predominant insects of lake sediments. Their larvae take part in nutrient exchange between sediments and overlying water. Prior lake research had linked profundal chironomid genera to productivity classes ([[Oligotrophy]] to [[Eutrophy]]), and the author argues that general short-term faunal surveys of lakes had become insufficiently intensive to add much new knowledge.

Reservoirs were chosen because their uniform, sloping benthic zones simplify lake problems, they are accessible, and little was known about reservoir midges. Morphometric data (Table I):

- Kempton Park East Reservoir: 16.18 ha, maximum depth 7.0 m, capacity 909,000 m³. A standing reservoir, with a maximum depth of 7.2 m in 1950 and 6.8 m from March 1951.
- Staines South Reservoir: 99.55 ha, maximum depth 9.0 m, capacity 7,980,000 m³. A standing reservoir; depth fluctuated between 8.8 and 9.1 m.
- Queen Mary Reservoir: 292.59 ha, maximum depth 10.5 m, capacity 30,700,000 m³. In constant use, with a level maintained by pumping from the river; only adult observations were made here.

Zones were defined as littoral (water's edge to the limit of rooted vegetation, about 4 m), sublittoral (gravel, beyond the concrete slabs) and profundal (below about 6 m, with deep deposits and midsummer oxygen deficiency).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling and methodology.** Emergence traps had a mouth area of 0.25 m² and were set about 1 m above the substratum. Kempton Park East had 27 traps on six lines; Staines South had 12 traps on a single line, placed in a wave-exposed corner whose catches the author regards as unrepresentative. Traps were emptied twice weekly. Larvae were sampled with the Jenkin sampler (core area 36.29 cm²), sieved at 0.25 mm and separated by a benzene flotation procedure. Sieve losses were acknowledged: a 0.25 mm mesh retains some first-instar larvae, and an 80-mesh gauze was impracticable.

**Vertical distribution in the mud.** In March 1952, about 95 per cent. of larvae lay in the top third of the core, and in April 1952 fewer than 1 per cent. were found below 10 cm.

**Oxygen and temperature.** Oxygen was estimated from water sampled near the mud surface and titrated by the Winkler method. Strong, lasting anaerobic conditions did not develop at Kempton Park East in 1951, owing to slight, intermittent stratification (maximum surface–bottom difference of 2.5 °C). Staines South, being deeper and more stable, is inferred to have lower midsummer oxygen.

**Substrate.** Organic carbon at Kempton Park East averaged 5.63 per cent. at 1 m and 3.92 per cent. at 6–8 m. Loss on ignition at 6–8 m was 36.94 per cent. in June.

**Water chemistry.** Thames water at Walton (1951) had total hardness of 81 p.p.m., a pH of 7.9, and BOD of 2.3 p.p.m. The reservoirs are alkaline, hard, and rich in silicate, phosphate and nitrogen from sewage and industrial discharges.

**Wider context.** [[Chaoborus Flavicans]] and [[Caenis Horaria]] (Ephemeroptera) were also recorded, and Trichoptera were occasionally trapped. Fish (mainly perch and minnows) are considered a predation source affecting catches, but no fish population data were available.

## Key Findings

1. **Species inventory.** 62 species of Chironomidae were recorded across the reservoirs (Table IV). Kempton Park East has 57 species, of which 38 were trapped and 20 were caught only in swarms. Staines South yielded 26 species. The Procladius choreus, Tanytarsus lugens, Chironomus plumosus and C. anthracinus profundal community occurs at Staines South and Queen Mary.
2. **Novel record.** [[Cryptochironomus Redekei]] was identified by P. Freeman as new to Britain, having been found only at Kempton Park East.
3. **Depth zonation.** The Tanypodinae, Procladius choreus and Psilotanypus rufovittatus dominate most zones. Orthocladiinae are confined to the littoral and sublittoral, and Tanytarsus holochlorus is nearly five times more abundant than any other species in the profundal. The reservoir was therefore described as a "Tanytarsus reservoir".
4. **Phenology and voltinism.** Procladius choreus is bivoltine in the profundal. Psilotanypus rufovittatus is univoltine. Tanytarsus lugens is interpreted as four generations. Cladotanytarsus atridorsum is trivoltine. Chironomus plumosus is bivoltine in shallow water and univoltine in deep water.
5. **Thermal summation.** Developmental zeros were estimated, for example 1.9 °C for C. plumosus, 1.8 °C for T. lugens and 4.2 °C for Cladotanytarsus atridorsum. These estimates are described as descriptive only.
6. **Trapping efficiency.** Estimates varied from 1 to 79 per cent. across species. The efficiency of Chironomus plumosus was 6–11 per cent. (95 per cent. limits), and that of Polypedilum nubeculosum was 2 per cent. The maximum possible efficiency for Endochironomus albipennis was 17 per cent.
7. **Larval standing crop.** At 6–8 m in April 1951, the count was 5,565 larvae per m² (20.2 per core). In June 1951 it was 25,940 per m² (12 double cores). In March 1952 it was 33,874 per m², and in April 1952 42,762 per m². Staines South had about 10,000 per m² on 10 May 1951. Littoral counts at 1 m in March 1952 were about 46,000 per m².
8. **Winter and spring fluctuations.** Larval numbers at 6–8 m changed markedly between sampling dates, rising from 5,565 to 10,193 per m² between April and May 1951. In 1954 they fell from about 36,000 to 12,000 per m² between February and April. The author suggests wind-driven water movement disturbs the mud and moves larvae into the water column, a hypothesis supported by an inverse correlation with wind velocity (Table XI) that is not fully proven.
9. **Taxon composition.** The larval population at 6–8 m in June and September was about 73 per cent. Tanytarsus and 22 per cent. Tanypodinae. Emergent Tanypodinae, however, were nearly five times more numerous than all other taxa together. Either Tanytarsus larval mortality is high or the traps favour Tanypodinae; the author considers the second more probable.
10. **Biomass and production.** The dry weight of larvae at 6–8 m in March 1952 was 5.25 g/m² (46 lb per acre; about 346 kg wet/ha). The total annual emergence at 6–8 m in 1951 was 3.0 g/m² (60 per cent. of the late-winter standing crop). Figures are described as minimal because of sampling bias.
11. **Anomalous emergences.** Irregular emergences of Procladius choreus (littoral in 1950) and Tanytarsus holochlorus (littoral in 1950, profundal in 1951) remain unexplained.
12. **Oxygen as the controlling factor.** The author argues that oxygen deficit, rather than productivity directly, determines which chironomid predominates in the profundal. Eutrophy and high oxygen deficit usually coincide, giving the correlation between lake type and chironomid type.
13. **Biogeographic comparison.** Ringsjön (Sweden) shares 16 species with Kempton Park East, and the Grosser Plöner See shares several others.
14. **Limitations and future work.** The author identifies key uncertainties: trap selectivity and efficiency, the cause of spring larval fluctuations, the life-cycle timing of Tanytarsus lugens, and the role of water movement in fauna distribution. He recommends core processing of fresh material and sampling of washed-ashore pupal skins, and notes that a survey mainly defines problems rather than solving them.
