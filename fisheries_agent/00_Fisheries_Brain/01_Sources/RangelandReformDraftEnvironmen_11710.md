# Rangeland Reform '94 Draft Environmental Impact Statement Executive Summary

#rangeland-management #policy #environmental-impact #livestock-grazing #riparian-habitat #upland-habitat #ecosystem-management

## Executive Summary

This document is the executive summary of the Rangeland Reform '94 Draft Environmental Impact Statement (EIS), prepared by the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM) and [[Forest Service]] to evaluate proposed changes to federal rangeland management programs across 270 million acres in the western United States. The proposal addresses ecosystem management, rangeland restoration, grazing fee reform, and administrative streamlining. Five management alternatives and seven fee formula alternatives are analyzed for their ecological, economic, and social impacts. The document emphasizes the need to restore rangeland ecosystems—particularly riparian areas, which occupy only 1 percent of rangelands but are in their worst historical condition—while maintaining sensitivity to communities dependent on livestock grazing. The analysis covers 16-17 western states and evaluates impacts on vegetation, watersheds, wildlife, special status species, wild horses and burros, recreation, cultural resources, and socioeconomic conditions.

## Background & Description

### Purpose and Need

The Rangeland Reform '94 initiative responds to decades of debate over rangeland conditions in the American West. While management improvements since the 1930s have brought progress, significant problems persist: rangeland ecosystems are not functioning properly in many areas, riparian zones are widely depleted, some uplands produce far below potential, and soils are becoming less fertile. The reform aims to: (1) manage public rangelands compatibly with ecosystem management principles; (2) accelerate restoration and improvement of public rangelands; (3) streamline BLM and Forest Service grazing administration and reduce costs; and (4) establish fair and equitable grazing fees. The proposal operates under authorities including the [[Federal Land Policy and Management Act]] (FLPMA) of 1976, [[Taylor Grazing Act]] of 1934, [[Public Rangelands Improvement Act]] (PRIA) of 1978, and [[National Forest Management Act]] (NFMA) of 1976.

### Management Alternatives

**Alternative 1 - Current Management (No Action):** Continues existing policies with no comprehensive national requirements or rangeland management standards for BLM. Permits issued for 10 years. BLM has grazing advisory boards; Forest Service does not. Inconsistencies between agencies persist in leasing, prohibited acts, appeals, water rights, and range improvement ownership.

**Alternative 2 - BLM-Forest Service Proposed Action:** Establishes national requirements for managing rangeland ecosystems on BLM lands. State or regional standards and guidelines would be developed within 18 months meeting national requirements; fallback standards apply if not completed. Multiple resource advisory councils replace BLM grazing advisory boards. Allows base property and pasture leases with surcharges (20% for base property, 50% for pasture, 70% for both; sons and daughters exempted). Both agencies could authorize conservation use for up to 10 years. Federal government would hold title to all future permanent range improvements. Range Betterment Funds expanded to cover monitoring, planning, environmental assessments. Appeals no longer automatically stayed; 75-day maximum before decisions take effect. Proposed fee formula uses $3.96 base value updated annually by Forage Value Index, phased in 1995-1997 ($2.75 in 1995, $3.50 in 1996, $3.96 in 1997).

**Alternative 3 - Livestock Production:** Standards and guidelines developed regionally by permittees and grazing advisory boards. Allows base property and pasture leases without surcharge. Prohibits foreign corporations from holding permits. Permit tenure 10-20 years (20 years for good stewardship). Up to 5 years nonuse for personal convenience. Permittees could file for water rights on public land. 50% of grazing fees returned to district of origin. Grazing advisory boards have expanded roles in planning, decisionmaking, monitoring.

**Alternative 4 - Environmental Enhancement:** Regional and national standards and guidelines established. No leasing allowed. Forage not allocated above current levels even after desired conditions reached. Ten-year permits only to permittees with substantial compliance records. Suspended nonuse eliminated from BLM permits. BLM would assert water rights claims for livestock watering on public lands. Livestock grazing limited to areas in proper functioning condition; permanently excluded from designated/recommended wilderness, critical habitat for threatened/endangered species, developed recreation sites. Grazing advisory boards eliminated; joint BLM-Forest Service resource advisory councils established on ecoregion basis.

**Alternative 5 - No Grazing:** Eliminates grazing on public lands over 3-year phase-out. Livestock used only where needed to achieve vegetation management objectives via temporary permits. All range improvements federally owned. No Range Betterment Fund. Appealed decisions not automatically stayed.

### Fee Alternatives

Seven fee formulas analyzed: (1) **Current PRIA** - $1.23 base updated by three indexes (forage value, beef cattle prices, selected production costs), ±25% annual change limit; (2) **Modified PRIA** - same base, uses all production costs index; (3) **BLM-Forest Service Proposal** - $3.96 base updated by Forage Value Index, phased 1995-1997, ±25% annual change limit; (4) **Regional Fees** - different base values for six regions from 1983 Federal Land Forage Appraisal (updated 1992), annually updated by FVI; (5) **Federal Forage Fee Formula** - 3-year average private lease rates for 16 states, multiplied by cash receipts differential; (6) **PRIA with Surcharges** - PRIA fee plus surcharge covering local administration costs, capped at 2× PRIA fee; (7) **Competitive Bidding** - market-based fees set through competitive bidding process.

### Affected Environment

**Rangelands:** Span nine climatic zones across 15-17 western states, including hot deserts, sagebrush plateaus, grasslands, and Rocky Mountain coniferous forests. Two basic vegetation types: upland communities (99% of rangelands) and riparian communities (1%). BLM manages 176 million upland acres and 1.0 million riparian acres; Forest Service manages 144 million upland acres and 2.2 million riparian acres.

**Historical Context:** Livestock grazing began in southwest in 1600s, expanded throughout West by late 1800s. Millions of cattle, sheep, horses grazed vegetation never before intensively grazed. Adverse effects apparent before 1900. Grazing reduced native grasses and palatable shrubs, decreased plant litter, exposed bare ground, heightened erosion. Reduction in grasses and litter disrupted natural fire regimes. Fire suppression began; fires decreased except in hot desert Southwest. Shrub-dominated areas (sagebrush, pinyon-juniper) expanded as grasslands depleted. Native bunchgrasses and quaking aspen diminished. Nonnative annual grasses invaded, crowding out natives, providing less value for livestock/wildlife, burning more readily—permanently changing large rangeland areas.

**Current Upland Conditions:** Since mid-1930s, upland vegetation shows improvement in many areas due to historical management emphasis and Range Betterment Fund use favoring pasture configurations and developments benefiting uplands. Once altered, upland communities change gradually. Native grasses revegetate slowly; annual grasses cannot be removed once established; disturbed/eroded soils require long time to rebuild. Areas receiving >12 inches annual precipitation show improvement within 20 years when management improves; drier areas generally have not improved. BLM estimates: 13% proper functioning condition, 30% functioning at risk, 57% not functioning properly. Forest Service: 80% meeting/moving toward objectives, 20% not meeting objectives.

**Current Riparian Conditions:** Riparian communities make up only 1% of rangelands but provide far-reaching values. Healthy riparian areas stabilize streambanks, filter sediments, improve water quality, reduce flooding, recharge groundwater, maintain streamflow. Most biologically productive and diverse habitats on public land; all fish and nearly all terrestrial wildlife depend on riparian areas. Amount and quality severely reduced since settlement. While uplands improved since 1930s, riparian areas continued declining and are in worst historical condition. Most affected by livestock grazing: livestock spend extensive time in riparian areas due to lush vegetation, shade, water; remove protective vegetation, trample streambanks, defecate near streams degrading water quality. Streambank erosion increases, channels widen/deepen, streams lose ability to absorb, retain, steadily release water. Nonfunctioning riparian communities cannot provide watershed values or habitat needed by fish/wildlife. Once nonfunctioning, usually will not recover without major management changes. With moisture present, most respond relatively rapidly once disturbance removed—many improve within 5 years; some restored habitats reestablish perennial streamflow in formerly intermittent streams. BLM estimates: 34% proper functioning condition, 46% functioning at risk, 20% not functioning properly. Forest Service: 78% meeting/moving toward objectives, 22% not meeting objectives.

**Wildlife and Special Status Species:** More than 3,000 mammal, bird, reptile, fish, amphibian species inhabit public rangelands. Changes since settlement generally favored brush-dominated upland species (mule deer, black-tailed jack rabbits, javelina). Most big game populations abundant and stable. Many species associated with native grassland and riparian communities declined. More than 100 rangeland species listed as federally threatened or endangered (desert tortoise, Utah prairie dog, bald eagle, [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]]). Many others designated sensitive. Decline in riparian-dependent species especially extensive and alarming: many native fish, upland birds, neotropical migratory birds, raptors greatly affected. More than 100 special status riparian species in Arizona and New Mexico; most salmon stocks using rangeland streams at risk due to poor habitat. Additionally, 75 plant species federally endangered/threatened; more than 1,100 other plants protected due to viability concerns.

**Wild Horses and Burros:** Approximately 46,000 wild horses and 8,000 burros inhabit public rangelands, protected under Wild and Free Roaming Horse and Burro Act of 1971. Use same forage as livestock; often directly compete with livestock and wildlife for food and water. Horses concentrate in and damage riparian areas, particularly during drought. BLM routinely gathers and removes excess animals to maintain suitable populations.

**Economic Conditions:** Analysis addresses 16 western states (Washington, Oregon, California, Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado, Wyoming, Montana, Idaho, Nevada, Utah, North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, Kansas, Oklahoma); Texas excluded due to small federal grazing. Western economy highly diversified. 1982-1990: employment grew 11 million workers; percentage increased in service, finance, insurance, real estate, construction, retail; decreased in government, manufacturing, agriculture, transportation, communications, utilities, mining. Income increased $350 billion 1982-1990; agriculture income fell back to 1982 level by 1990 while all other industries grew. About 38% of land nationally used for raising cattle is leased. In western states, nearly 70% of cattle raisers own all land they operate. Federal permittees: ~3% of 907,000 beef producers in 48 contiguous states; 22% in 11 western states. Sheep producers with federal permits: ~19% of total in 11 western states. Permitted use: ~12% of forage consumed by beef cattle in 16 states, ~25% in 11 states; BLM lands ~5% of annual feed for sheep, Forest Service ~6%. Average dependency on federal forage highest in Arizona, lowest in Montana. Operations with federal permits larger on average: permittees average 221 cows vs. 93 for nonpermittees; 112 sheep vs. 13. Grazing fee expenses represent ~3% of total cash costs. Average per-cow costs for permittees significantly lower than nonpermittees (~$40 per cow differential). Permittees spent more per cow for breeding stock, fences, hired labor; nonpermittees spent more for capital items, machinery, buildings, equipment, feed, pasture rental, purchased stock cattle.

**Permit Value:** Ranching operations with grazing permits worth more than similar operations without permits. Real estate market recognizes difference in value reflecting benefits of federal permit. Court cases hold ranch owners with permits cannot recover from United States for losses in ranch value due to permit modifications. Recognition of permit value would allow permittees to retain capitalized value of public resource never conveyed to them. Theoretically, permit value at least partially reflects capitalized difference between grazing fee and competitive market rate. Raising fee to competitive market rate could eliminate permit "value." Altering permit (length, AUMs authorized) might also have this effect.

**Social Conditions:** Many rural areas experiencing significant population increase after decades of stability/decline. Other areas continue losing population due to outmigration of young people. West has major cities with significant growth; residents concerned about environment, use rangelands for recreation. Movement of people/jobs into rural areas began 1970s, expected to continue into 21st century. In scenic areas, ranches sold for recreation or subdivided for homes. Western rural areas moving from long-term economic dependence on agriculture/mining to recreation/tourism. Trends may cause rural natives to feel loss of community control. Montana survey (Saltiel 1991, n=1,084): 67% opposed raising grazing fees, 85% said increases would harm them; but 56% of ranchers without federal permits favored raising fees, two-thirds said increase would not affect them, 10% said would benefit them. Key point: most western ranchers lack federal permits, would not be affected by fee increases. 1991 data from 3,853 ranchers in 11 states: average rancher 55 years old, worked same ranch >31 years, family ranched same state 68 years. Long-time ranchers perceive themselves as embodying fair play, honesty, independence. Take pride in independence but neighborly when needed. Most face increasingly stressful social situations balancing traditional lifestyles with changing communities. 1993 national study: two-thirds said ranchers should pay more for federal grazing; at least three-fourths said wildlife should be better protected; about two-fifths said economic vitality of local communities should be priority in federal rangeland decisions, similar proportion disagreed. Environmental groups believe current fee system does not account for all costs to public resources, undervalues grazing privilege, encourages overemphasis of grazing at expense of other uses. Many recreationists want stricter policies on fragile/damaged lands. Some want cattle removed believing they are destructive, fees should cover damage. Others concerned about ranchers selling to outside interests; many depend on ranchers opening land to recreation, concerned new interests will close land. Others believe ranching compatible with other uses if livestock properly managed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document evaluates rangeland ecosystems across multiple scales, from local sites to regional landscapes. Rangelands encompass diverse ecological zones with two fundamental habitat types: upland communities (dry sites, 99% of area) and riparian communities (wet areas, 1% of area but disproportionately important). The analysis adopts ecosystem-based evaluation methods recognizing that traditional vegetation measurement techniques do not adequately assess whether ecological processes function properly and meet watershed/wildlife needs.

BLM implements a functioning condition system with three categories: (1) **Proper Functioning** - vegetation and ground cover maintain soil conditions sustaining natural biotic communities; (2) **Functioning but Susceptible to Degradation** - proper functioning capabilities threatened by livestock grazing activities; (3) **Nonfunctioning** - vegetation and ground cover not maintaining soil conditions sustaining natural biotic communities. Forest Service uses resource objectives system assessing whether conditions meet fundamental ecosystem health needs for given sites.

The document emphasizes biodiversity management as essential for reversing broad species declines. Biodiversity refers to total amount and variety of plants and animals in an area (local site, watershed, region, or larger). Biologically diverse areas function at highest potential, provide most stable and productive habitat. Managing for biodiversity entails identifying natural processes not functioning properly and changing responsible actions to slow and reverse undesirable ecological processes. For riparian communities, this might involve changing livestock grazing to enable vegetation to shade and protect streams, allowing streams to deposit sediment, repair eroded banks, restore watershed functions, thereby improving wildlife and fisheries habitat and stabilizing/recovering species.

Watershed functions are critical: healthy uplands with adequate plant cover and litter increase water infiltration, reduce runoff and erosion. Healthy riparian areas act like "giant sponge" filtering sediments, improving water quality, reducing flooding, recharging groundwater, maintaining streamflow. When riparian areas become nonfunctioning, they lose these watershed capabilities. Restoration can reestablish perennial flow in streams that became intermittent.

The analysis recognizes historical ecological disruptions: pre-settlement fire regimes maintained patchwork of shrub- and grass-dominated communities through frequent lightning and Native American-set fires. Post-settlement livestock grazing reduced grasses and plant litter, making fires less frequent. Combined with aggressive fire suppression, this allowed shrub expansion (sagebrush, pinyon-juniper) and native grassland/aspen decline. Nonnative annual grass invasion represents permanent ecological change in large areas. These altered states are difficult to reverse: native grasses revegetate slowly, annual grasses cannot be removed once established, disturbed soils require long rebuilding periods. Arid lands receiving <12 inches annual precipitation show minimal improvement even with management changes; areas receiving >12 inches can improve within 20 years.

## Key Findings

### Projected Impacts by Management Alternative

**Current Management (No Action):**
- **Livestock Use:** BLM forage declines 18%, Forest Service 19% over 20 years due to monitoring-based stocking adjustments, declining economic feasibility, threatened/endangered species recovery plans
- **Uplands:** BLM proper functioning increases 30% to 117 million acres; functioning at risk decreases 55% to 22 million acres; nonfunctioning decreases <5% to 20 million acres. Forest Service: 82% meeting/moving toward objectives (60 million acres), 18% not meeting objectives (13 million acres)
- **Riparian:** BLM proper functioning decreases 3% to 33%; functioning at risk decreases <1% to 45%; nonfunctioning increases 7% to 21%.
