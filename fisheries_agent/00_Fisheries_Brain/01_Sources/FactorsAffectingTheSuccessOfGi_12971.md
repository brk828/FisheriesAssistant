# Factors Affecting the Success of Gila Topminnow (Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis) Introductions on Four Arizona National Forests

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Poeciliidae #endangered-species #reintroduction #habitat-assessment

## Executive Summary

This report documents the first-year monitoring results of [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*) reintroductions conducted in 1982 across four [[Arizona]] National Forests under a tripartite Memorandum of Understanding between the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, U.S. Forest Service, and Arizona Game and Fish Department. Of 64 sites stocked with topminnow from May-June 1982, 58 were monitored in June-July 1983. Overall success rate was 48.4% (31 successful sites). The study employed both qualitative habitat assessments (habitat type, cover, substrate) and quantitative measurements (drainage area, elevation, dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature, conductivity) to identify factors contributing to introduction success. Discriminant analysis identified four key variables—drainage area, elevation, cover type, and dissolved oxygen—that correctly classified 80% of sites as successful or unsuccessful. The most significant predictor was drainage area: successful sites averaged 0.8 ± 0.5 km² versus 5.3 ± 1.8 km² for unsuccessful sites (P < .001). Flooding in large-drainage streams and desiccation were primary causes of failure. Revised site selection criteria recommend drainage areas < 1.0 km², elevations < 1600 m, presence of aquatic vegetation, and perennial water sources.

## Background & Description

The [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis o. occidentalis*), also called [[Sonoran Topminnow]], is an endangered livebearer (family [[Poeciliidae]]) native to the [[Gila River]] drainage of southern [[Arizona]]. Historically widespread in "almost all kinds of aquatic habitat present in southern Arizona" (Minckley 1973), the species has experienced severe range contraction. The recovery effort described here represents a systematic attempt to establish new populations in suitable habitats on federal lands.

Source fish came from two locations: the [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]] pond in [[Pinal County]] (original genetic stock from [[Monkey Springs]], [[Santa Cruz County]]) and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico (transplanted from Arboretum stock). Sixty-four sites were stocked in 1982 with 200-2,000 individuals per site, depending on habitat size. An additional 24 sites were stocked in 1983 but not included in the primary analysis.

The topminnow is omnivorous with detritus as a major food source. As a livebearer, spawning substrate is not a reproductive requirement. Previous studies documented the species' ability to tolerate highly variable environmental conditions and withstand flooding events, though populations have also been lost to severe floods (Collins et al. 1981).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Types**: Five basic habitat categories were identified: (1) spring-fed or overland-flow-fed pond, (2) spring-fed trough, (3) artesian well-fed habitat (marsh/pond), (4) spring stream, and (5) stream (spring and runoff fed). Spring streams were most commonly stocked (26.0% of sites) with 65.0% success rate. Spring-fed ponds/tanks showed 70.6% success (12 of 17 sites). Artesian well sites had 100% success (3 of 3 sites). Streams had only 22.2% success (2 of 9 sites), primarily due to flooding.

**Cover**: Aquatic vegetation was present at 32 sites, with 71.8% success rate (23 sites). Sites with terrestrial vegetation overhang showed 50.0% success (1 of 2). Abiotic cover (undercut banks, boulders, pools) yielded 44.4% success (4 of 9). Sites with no cover had only 14.3% success (2 of 7). Aquatic plants, including algal mats, appear critical for successful establishment.

**Substrate**: Silt substrates showed highest success (65.2%, 15 of 23 sites), followed by sand (61.5%, 8 of 13), bedrock (60.0%, 6 of 10), and gravel (50.0%, 1 of 2). Concrete troughs and metal stock tanks both failed (0 of 2 total). The importance of silt may relate to detritus availability as a food source.

**Water Quality**: Successful sites showed mean dissolved oxygen of 8.4 ± 0.6 ppm (range 6.4-15.0), pH of 8.0 ± 0.2 (6.4-10.0), temperature of 24.1 ± 0.7°C (17.0-33.0°C), and conductivity of 514 ± 39 µmho/cm (112-1000). Temperature was significantly higher at successful sites (P < .001) compared to unsuccessful sites (21.0 ± 0.9°C). However, single-point sampling limits interpretation of temperature effects. pH, dissolved oxygen, and conductivity did not differ significantly between successful and unsuccessful sites.

**Drainage Area**: The most significant predictor of success. Successful sites averaged 0.8 ± 0.5 km² (trace to 6.1 km²) versus 5.3 ± 1.8 km² (trace to 17.5 km²) for unsuccessful sites (P < .001). Thirteen successful and four unsuccessful sites had zero drainage area (artesian wells, windmill-fed ponds, perched springs). Large drainage areas correlated with evidence of severe flooding: debris piling, scouring, and bank instability.

**Elevation**: Successful sites averaged 971 ± 45 m (599-1465 m) versus 1071 ± 60 m (587-1682 m) for unsuccessful sites (P < .001). Both ranges fall within the 695-1600 m range documented for other natural and introduced populations (Meffe et al. 1983).

**Causes of Failure**: Flooding was the primary cause in larger streams with extensive drainage areas. Desiccation (complete or seasonal reduction) contributed to many failures. Other factors included water development activities (vegetation removal, deepening), livestock trampling when water was limited, and presence of predatory non-native fishes in pond habitats.

## Key Findings

1. **Overall Success Rate**: Of 58 monitored sites from 1982 introductions, 31 (48.4%) were successful, 27 (42.2%) failed, and 6 (9.4%) were not monitored. Including [[Mesquite Spring Stream]] (confirmed later), 32 sites were ultimately successful.

2. **Discriminant Analysis Results**: A stepwise discriminant analysis identified four variables that adequately discriminated between successful and unsuccessful sites: drainage area, elevation, cover type, and dissolved oxygen. The canonical correlation was 0.73. Classification functions correctly predicted outcomes for 80% of all sites (40 of 50 analyzed sites).

3. **Critical Habitat Variables**:
   - **Drainage area** < 1.0 km² strongly associated with success (P < .001)
   - **Elevation** < 1600 m recommended based on natural population ranges
   - **Aquatic vegetation** present at 71.8% of successful sites
   - **Temperature** significantly higher at successful sites (24.1°C vs 21.0°C, P < .001), though wide tolerance observed

4. **Habitat Type Performance**:
   - Artesian wells: 100% success (3/3 sites)
   - Spring-fed ponds/tanks: 70.6% success (12/17 sites)
   - Spring streams: 65.0% success (13/20 sites)
   - Streams (mixed sources): 22.2% success (2/9 sites)
   - Troughs: 0% success (0/1 site)

5. **Population Establishment**: Successful sites supported estimated populations ranging from 10 to 20,000 individuals within one year of stocking. Larger populations (>5,000) established in spring streams and ponds with optimal conditions.

6. **Revised Site Selection Criteria**: Recommended criteria include drainage area < 1.0 km², elevation < 1600 m, stream flow < 1 m³/sec, stream gradient < 3%, pond surface area < 2 ha, pond depth < 2 m, minimal channelization, complex heterogeneous habitat, presence of aquatic vegetation, native non-predatory fishes only, perennial water source (indicated by presence of *Physella*, *Planorbella*, and/or hydrobiid snails), low developmental potential, and location within [[Gila River]] drainage.

7. **Interacting Factors**: Success depends on interactions among stream gradient, topography, habitat complexity, and watershed use—not drainage area alone. Natural populations persist in [[Cienega Creek]] despite large drainage and seasonal flooding, demonstrating that refuge habitats and habitat complexity can mitigate flood impacts.

8. **Management Implications**: The study demonstrates that small, stable spring-fed systems with aquatic vegetation provide optimal reintroduction sites. Large streams require careful evaluation of flood refugia and habitat complexity. Continued annual monitoring with standardized data collection (Appendix A survey form) is essential for refining predictive models and adaptive management.
