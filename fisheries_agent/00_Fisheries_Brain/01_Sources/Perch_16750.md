# Yellow Perch (Perca flavescens) in New York State

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #percidae #game-fish

## Executive Summary

The [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) is one of the most widely distributed food and game fishes in [[New York State]], eliciting mixed reactions from anglers. While valued as a pan fish for its palatability and ease of capture, it can become a nuisance species, particularly in [[Adirondack]] ponds and streams where it competes with native [[Trout]] populations. The species exhibits schooling behavior, feeds primarily on smaller fishes and invertebrates, and serves as important forage for larger game fish. Growth rates vary dramatically depending on environmental conditions and competition, with stunted populations common in overpopulated waters.

## Background & Description

The [[Yellow Perch]] is distinguished by its yellowish coloration and two spines in the anal fin. It should not be confused with the [[White Perch]] (*Morone americana*), which belongs to the sea bass family ([[Serranidae]]), lacks yellowish color, and possesses three anal fin spines rather than two. Both species may occur together, though [[White Perch]] are more common in brackish estuaries.

The species has expanded its range significantly in [[New York State]] and now occupies nearly every major river system division. Historical introductions, often as bait fish, have resulted in establishment in many upland ponds, frequently with negative ecological consequences.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Yellow Perch]] spend most of their life span in schools of 50 to 200 individuals of similar size, inhabiting weedy areas at shallow to medium depths. Studies in [[Lake Mendota]], [[Wisconsin]], using divers, gill nets, and electric eye technology documented daily movement patterns, with schools appearing over certain bars during afternoon and evening hours throughout summer.

The species has become problematic in many upland ponds following introduction. Initial success is typically followed by reduction of native forage fish populations, leading to stunted perch growth, grub parasite infestation, and decline in native [[Trout]] populations through competition. Despite these issues, [[Yellow Perch]] inhabit vast acreages of water and support both recreational and commercial fisheries.

Spawning occurs near shore in April and early May at water temperatures of 44 to 54°F. Each gravid female is followed by 15 to 25 males that fertilize the eggs.

## Key Findings

**Feeding Ecology**: Adult [[Yellow Perch]] feed primarily on fishes, including conspecifics. Stomach content analysis of several thousand individuals over three inches in length revealed predominance of minnows, darters, sticklebacks, and sunfish. Invertebrate prey includes many kinds of insect larvae.

**Role as Forage**: [[Yellow Perch]] serve as important prey for numerous game fish species. In [[Oneida Lake]], 53 of 460 young [[Pike-Perch]] (walleye) stomachs contained [[Yellow Perch]]. Adult [[Pike-Perch]] from the same lake consumed more [[Yellow Perch]] than any other fish species. Other predators include [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Pike]], [[Pickerel]], and [[Crappie]]. In [[Lake Champlain]], [[New York Conservation Department]] surveys found [[Yellow Perch]] were eaten four times as frequently as the next most common identifiable fish food ([[Johnny Darter]]). Perch were a major food item for every important game fish in the [[St. Lawrence River]].

**Growth Rates**: Growth varies dramatically with food conditions, competition (especially intraspecific), water body size, and other factors. Stunted populations from [[Gilbert Lake]] averaged 1.75 ounces at 6 years old, while same-age fish from [[Bradley Brook Reservoir]] weighed nearly six times as much. Under better than average conditions, [[Yellow Perch]] reach approximately 12 inches and nearly one pound in 9-10 years. Fast-growing females may attain 12 inches in 5 years. A two-pound perch is considered very large; the all-time record is 4.25 pounds, taken from the [[Delaware River]], [[New Jersey]], in 1865 by Dr. C. C. Abbott.

**Fishery Value**: As a pan fish, [[Yellow Perch]] has few equals, providing accessible angling opportunities for youth and less skilled anglers. No expensive lures or equipment are required. Minnows are favored bait, but perch readily rise to flies, especially during large insect hatches. The species remains active throughout winter and supports ice fishing.
