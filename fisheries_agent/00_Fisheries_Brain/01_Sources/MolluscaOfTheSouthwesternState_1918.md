## Executive Summary

This 1918 Academy of Natural Sciences of Philadelphia report (Pilsbry & Ferriss, "Mollusca of the Southwestern States—IX, X") is primarily a **malacological survey**, not a fish-focused study. It documents land snail (and a few freshwater mollusk) faunas across a series of "sky island" mountain ranges in southeastern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico: the [[Santa Catalina Mountains]], [[Rincon Mountains]], [[Tortillita Mountains]], [[Galiuro Mountains]], [[Pinaleno Mountains]] (Graham Mountain), and the [[Blue Mountains]], [[White Mountains]], and [[Mogollon Mountains]] of the Gila River headwaters. Fish are mentioned only incidentally — as habitat features (e.g., "Fish Creek") and as passing ecological notes on native mountain trout populations in remote canyon streams — while the bulk of the scientific content concerns terrestrial gastropod taxonomy, distribution, and habitat ecology, plus a few freshwater bivalves and gastropods (Physidae, Unionidae).

## Background & Description

The survey covers two large geographic units:

- **Article IX**: The Santa Catalina, Rincon, Tortillita, and Galiuro Mountains (Pima, Pinal, and Graham Counties, Arizona), forested with yellow pine, quaking aspen, cork bark fir (*Abies arizonica*), Douglas spruce (*Pseudotsuga mucronata*), and Arizona cypress at higher elevations, transitioning to open desert scrub and rocky talus at lower elevations.
- **Article X**: The [[Gila River]] headwaters region, including the Blue and White Mountains of Arizona and the Mogollon Mountains of New Mexico, surveyed by Ferriss (1913) and Ferriss & J.H. Daniels (1914).

Collecting focused on rock slides, talus, canyon banks, and forest litter, using specialized digging tools to extract snails from dry substrate. The authors note strong correspondence between forest tree associations (yellow pine, quaking aspen, Douglas spruce, cork bark fir) and snail diversity, with the wettest, most continuously forested slopes (e.g., north slope of Mount Lemmon) yielding the richest snail faunas.

Aquatic/freshwater fauna recorded incidentally alongside the terrestrial mollusks includes:
- *Physa virgata* and unidentified *Physa* sp. (Blue River, Reservation Creek)
- *Anodonta dolecta* (Black River, southern Apache Co.), with subfossil valves also found in banks of the Santa Cruz River at Tucson
- *Pisidium* sp. (noted alongside *Physa* at Big Springs)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper frames its mollusk data within a broader description of montane and riparian habitat across the Gila headwaters:

- **Canyon streams of Big Dry Canyon (Mogollon Mountains)** are explicitly noted to support "the mountain trout, friendly and well conditioned, but... not the cut-throat trout of the Colorado" — an important, if brief, ecological observation distinguishing local native trout populations from Colorado River cutthroat trout (*Oncorhynchus clarkii*) stocks. This suggests the presence of a distinct, possibly Gila trout–related (*[[Oncorhynchus gilae]]*) or otherwise locally adapted trout lineage in these isolated headwater canyons in the early 20th century, prior to extensive habitat disturbance.
- **Flood disturbance**: The San Francisco and Blue Rivers are described as having been "seriously torn up by floods in recent years," with many streamside farms swept away — indicating substantial hydrological volatility in these watersheds, a factor relevant to both mollusk and fish habitat stability.
- **Water chemistry/substrate effects**: The authors note unexplained absences of snails in certain short river stretches, hypothesizing effects of "soil, the chemistry of the rocks, or something not traced" — an early acknowledgment of substrate/water chemistry controls on aquatic and riparian invertebrate distribution.
- **Elevation gradients**: Collecting stations range from ~4,000 ft (Little Blue River mouth) to over 12,000 ft (rim of Blue Mountains, pasture stations), spanning desert scrub, oak-pine woodland, and subalpine spruce-fir forest — a strong elevational habitat gradient relevant to both terrestrial and aquatic faunal zonation.

## Key Findings

- **No dedicated fish taxonomy or fish-specific data** are presented in this source; all fish-related content is incidental to the malacological survey.
- **Mountain trout** in Big Dry Canyon (Mogollon Mountains, Socorro Co., New Mexico) are described as abundant, "friendly and well conditioned," and explicitly distinguished from Colorado cutthroat trout — an ecologically notable but taxonomically unresolved reference to a native or historically stocked trout population in these isolated Gila headwater canyons.
- **"Fish Creek"** (Apache Co., Arizona, tributary crossed en route to Reservation Creek) is used as a place name/collecting station for mollusks (e.g., *Cochlicopa lubrica*, *Gastrocopta pilsbryana*), with no direct fish survey data reported for this stream.
- Freshwater mollusk records (*Physa virgata*, *Anodonta dolecta*, *Pisidium* sp.) from the Blue River, Black River, and Reservation Creek provide indirect evidence of perennial, chemically favorable freshwater habitat in these drainages — of potential relevance to reconstructing historic aquatic community composition alongside fish populations.
- The report's extensive collecting station lists (Stations 1–102, compiled by Daniels and Ferriss, 1913–1918) provide precise georeferenced locations along the Blue River, San Francisco River, Black River, and Mogollon Mountain drainages that could serve as valuable historical baseline sites for later fish or aquatic ecosystem surveys in the [[Gila River]] headwaters system.
- Overall, the source is best classified as a **historical malacological baseline survey** with tangential ecological value for reconstructing early 20th-century montane stream conditions in the Gila River headwaters region, rather than a fish-focused ichthyological study.
