# Proposed Rule to List the Southwestern Willow Flycatcher as Endangered with Critical Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #tyrannidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 Federal Register proposed rule from the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (Service) proposes listing the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*) as an endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, and designating its critical habitat. The subspecies breeds in dense riparian vegetation in the southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico, and is endangered by extensive habitat loss and modification, [[Brood Parasitism]] by the [[Brown-Headed Cowbird]], and inadequate regulatory protection. The Service found the petitioned action warranted following a 12-month finding on a petition. The document covers the taxonomy, distribution and status by state, threats, a proposed critical habitat designation totalling approximately 1,038 km (643 miles) of stream and river, and the regulatory and economic framework for the proposal. Comments were due by October 21, 1993, and hearing requests by September 7, 1993.

## Background & Description

- **Morphology:** A small bird of about 15 cm (5.25 in), with grayish-green back and wings, whitish throat, light gray-olive breast and pale yellowish belly. Two wingbars are visible; the eye ring is faint or absent. The song is a "fitz-bew" and the call "whit."
- **Taxonomy:** A subspecies of one of the 10 North American species of the genus *Empidonax*, a group notoriously difficult to identify by sight. The [[Willow Flycatcher]] and [[Alder Flycatcher]] were formerly treated as a single species, the Traill's Flycatcher, and the taxonomy remains under revision. The two species differ in morphology, song, habitat use, nest structure and placement, ecological segregation and genetic distinctness.
- **Breeding biology:** Nests are compact cups of fiber, grass and fine plant material, built about 4–7 m (13–23 ft) high in a fork or horizontal branch, typically in a medium-sized bush or small tree with dense foliage. The nesting cycle is about 28 days; three or four eggs are laid at one-day intervals, and the female incubates for about 12 days. Young fledge about 13 days after hatching, and usually one brood is raised per year, with a second brood known only occasionally.
- **Migration and wintering:** Present on breeding territories from May, with birds leaving in August or September. Winter range is thought to be Central America and perhaps northern South America.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Breeds in riparian (streamside) communities with dense willows (*Salix* sp.), baccharis, arrowweed (*Pluchea* sp.) and tamarisk (*Tamarix* sp.), often with a scattered cottonwood (*Populus* sp.) overstory. Such habitat is rare, widely separated, small and/or linear across arid lands.
- **Patch size:** Occupied patches in the Grand Canyon in 1991–1992 ranged from 0.2 to 1.5 ha (0.5 to 3.7 acres), with a mean of 0.8 ha (2 acres). The Service stresses these figures are indications of suitable patch size, not minimum requirements.
- **Habitat loss:** Estimated wetland losses between 1780 and the 1980s were 52% in California, 30% in Nevada, 36% in Arizona, 33% in New Mexico and 52% in Texas. As much as 90% of lowland riparian habitat has been lost in Arizona.
- **Livestock grazing:** Overgrazing degrades riparian structure and species composition. A study on the [[Willow Flycatcher]] reported counts seven times higher in an ungrazed area than in comparable grazed plots, and an increase of 61% after grazing was reduced.
- **Tamarisk invasion:** Introduced from the Middle East in the late 1800s, tamarisk spread along southwestern watercourses, replacing native vegetation and simplifying the multilayered community. Bird species strongly associated with cottonwood-willow habitats, such as the [[Yellow-Billed Cuckoo]], [[Summer Tanager]] and [[Northern Oriole]], are lost when tamarisk replaces native cover.
- **Water development:** Dams, diversions, channelization and groundwater lowering have reduced flows and flood regimes essential for native riparian communities, favouring tamarisk.
- **Cowbird parasitism:** The brown-headed cowbird spread with agriculture and livestock. Parasitism rates reported include 50–80% in California (1987–1992), where the estimated population declined from 44 to 28 pairs; 50% average in the Grand Canyon (1982–1987); and 68% of nests on the Kern River in 1987. Parasitism reduces nest success and delays fledging.
- **Other threats:** Possible effects of pesticides and herbicides, recreation during the breeding season, predation by the [[Great-Tailed Grackle]] and logging in upper watersheds. Tamarisk and parasitism are noted as possible but not confirmed causes.

## Key Findings

- **Status:** The subspecies has declined throughout its range. About 70 pairs and 8 single birds were known in California, and about 120 to 150 pairs in Arizona (earlier estimates 24–30 pairs). The Service estimated the total population at approximately 230 to 500 nesting pairs; a population of about 1,000 pairs was considered possible but less likely.
- **Extirpation:** The subspecies no longer nests on the lower Colorado River and is absent from much of its former range. Three recent reviews considered extirpation from California possible or likely in the foreseeable future.
- **State listings:** California, Arizona and New Mexico classify the willow flycatcher as endangered.
- **Listing decision:** The Service concluded that endangered (not threatened) status was appropriate, because historic habitat loss already constitutes extinction across a significant portion of the species' range.
- **Critical habitat:** Approximately 1,038 km (643 miles) of stream and river across California, Arizona and New Mexico are proposed, including reaches of the [[Santa Ana River]], [[San Pedro River]], [[Gila River]], [[Verde River]], [[Colorado River]], [[Kern River]] and [[San Diego River]]. Designated areas include surface water and riparian thickets within 100 m of the water's edge.
- **Protection of habitat:** The Service found critical habitat designation prudent, reasoning that the species is not sought by birdwatchers and that the proposed areas are remote. Habitat primary constituent elements include riparian thickets, surface water, nesting grounds and space for population growth.
- **Regulatory context:** The [[Migratory Bird Treaty Act]] protects the subspecies but does not protect habitat unless nests are destroyed or birds directly taken. Section 7 consultation would apply to federal actions affecting the species or its critical habitat, including grazing, water diversion and recreational development.
- **Economic and procedural notes:** The Service will consider economic and other impacts before final designation, and a public hearing is planned in San Diego, Tucson and Las Cruces. An Environmental Assessment was determined not to be required under NEPA.
