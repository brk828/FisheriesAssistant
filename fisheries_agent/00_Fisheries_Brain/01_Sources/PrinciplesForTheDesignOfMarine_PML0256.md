# Principles for the Design of Marine Reserves

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This synthesis paper, published in *Ecological Applications* (Supplement, 2003), distills existing theoretical and modeling results into general principles for designing [[Marine Reserves]]. The authors, from the University of California, Davis and the University of Pisa, note that marine reserve theory is "in its infancy" and that empirical data comparing reserve designs are largely unavailable. They therefore derive four principles from models: two comparing reserves with conventional fishery management, and two addressing how movement and larval dispersal shape reserve configurations for sustainability of fisheries and biodiversity. The paper also reviews sources of uncertainty and possible ways to manage it, including [[Decision Analysis]], [[Meta-Analysis]] and [[Adaptive Management]].

## Background & Description

The document is a review-style scientific article rather than a species study. It frames the central design questions as:

- The size of reserves.
- The fraction of coastline to be covered.
- Where reserves should be sited.

The answers depend on the reserve's goal (improving [[Fishery Management]] or protecting [[Biodiversity]]), on the life history and dispersal characteristics of target species, and on existing fishing pressure.

Existing empirical work is limited. Most studies compare abundance between reserve and control sites after implementation, rarely before-and-after, and few examine spillover to areas outside reserves. Examples cited include [[Philippines Marine Reserve]] (spillover of commercial fish) and reserves in [[Kenya]], where spillover was not detected. Qualitative siting criteria (maximizing biogeographic representation, habitat heterogeneity, rare and endemic species, and avoiding frequent disturbance) are widely used but do not answer quantitative design questions.

Modeling is the main route forward, using concepts such as [[Yield Per Recruit]], [[Spawning Potential]] (eggs per recruit), and [[Larval Dispersal]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dispersal and movement.** Species differ in juvenile and adult movement and in larval dispersal distance. Sessile or sedentary species rarely leave reserves and so gain little beyond larval transport. Species with high movement rates spend much time outside reserves. Dispersal characteristics are known for only a handful of short-distance dispersers (e.g., Grantham et al. 2003; Shanks et al. 2003).

**Retention zones.** Areas that accumulate larvae before settlement are considered important for reserve placement (e.g., Wing et al. 1998; Warner et al. 2000). The [[Exuma Cays Land and Sea Park]] in the Bahamas is cited as a recent example of empirical attention to movement and its effect on reserve success.

**Fraction of lifetime reproduction.** Persistence is often assumed to require about 35% of unfished lifetime reproduction, though empirical estimates range from 20% to 70% (Mace and Sissenwine 1993).

**Case study.** Much of the modeling concerns the [[Northern California Red Sea Urchin Fishery]], where reserves were modeled to maximize catch under uncertainty.

## Key Findings

**Four design principles:**

1. **Principle 1 (yield per recruit):** Adding reserves has essentially the same effect on yield per recruit as increasing the age (or size) at first capture, because both reduce the effective fishing mortality on the stock. This follows from growth overfishing being the primary issue. The effect is illustrated in Beverton and Holt (1957) analyses.
2. **Principle 2 (yield):** Adding reserves has essentially the same effect on yield as reducing fishing effort (fishing mortality). Both approaches make yield less sensitive to increasing effort. Figure 1 (a model with 24 subpopulations, Quinn et al. 1993) shows this similarity: at low harvest rates, yield is reduced by increasing the fraction of area in reserves, but this fraction is necessary to sustain populations at high harvest rates.
3. **Principle 3 (movement and biodiversity vs. fishery):** Reserves for preserving biodiversity are most effective for species with low rates of juvenile and adult movement. Reserves for fishery management are most effective for species with intermediate rates of juvenile and adult movement, where the yield benefit from capture within the reserve is greatest. The first principle is largely obvious; the second describes an important trade-off.
4. **Principle 4 (larval dispersal):** Species with longer larval dispersal distances require a larger fraction of coastline in reserves to persist. The authors estimate that sustaining a population by reserves alone would require 35% or more of the coastline for some species. If that is not feasible, effort could be controlled to offset the larval shortfall in fished areas, or larger reserves with a lower fraction of coastline could be used.

**Uncertainty:**

- Reserves are more sensitive to uncertainty in dispersal, while conventional management is more sensitive to uncertainty in harvest rate. The authors suggest combining reserves and conventional management as a "portfolio" approach.
- Fraction of lifetime reproduction needed for persistence is an uncertain input for both management types.
- Fishers' responses to reserves (effort shifts) add further uncertainty (Wilen et al. 2002).

**Coping strategies:** Decision analysis (Botsford et al. 1999; Morgan and Botsford 2001), meta-analysis to link poorly known dispersal and reproduction parameters to life history traits, and adaptive management with monitoring of species responses to specific reserve configurations.

**Research gaps:** Little existing data from reserves is useful for design. Future work should test how persistence depends on reserve size and spacing, flow fields and community structure, and should compare reserve and conventional management outcomes under uncertainty.
