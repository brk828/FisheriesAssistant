# Natural Radioactivity in the Food Web of the Banded Sculpin *Cottus carolinae*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary study from the 1961 [[Radioecology]] symposium examines the life history, feeding ecology, growth, and [[Bioaccumulation]] of naturally occurring radionuclides in the [[Banded Sculpin]] (*Cottus carolinae*) in [[Doe Run]], a spring stream in Meade County, Kentucky. The banded sculpin is the principal vertebrate carnivore in the stream. Data come from two stations, [[Station I]] (spring source, stable temperature) and [[Station IV]] (mid-stream, temperature tracks air temperature). The authors found that most radioactivity in the system derives from natural decay of [[Uranium-238]] and [[Thorium-232]], with small fallout contributions from [[Strontium-90]] and [[Cesium-137]]. Sculpins at the two stations differed in diet and growth rate, and the study linked these differences to metabolism and temperature. Retention of ingested radioactivity was about 2% of the total over most of the fish's life.

## Background & Description

**Study setting.** Doe Run originates as a torrent spring near Ekron, Kentucky, and flows 9.7 miles to enter the [[Ohio River]] near Brandenburg, with an average gradient of about 21 feet per mile. Five permanent stations were established, but only Stations I and IV supplied data. Station I had a relatively constant temperature of 13.3 ± 2.0 °C, rubble-sand bottoms, and abundant aquatic vegetation. Station IV had marl-cemented rubble riffles, bedrock-sand-silt pools, and little vegetation.

**Study species.** *Cottus carolinae* is secretive as an adult, concealed beneath stones and vegetation. Young fish occupy quiet areas with silt-detritus bottoms and move into swifter water as they grow. Fish over 40 g are most often found in deep chutes, downstream pools, and fast eddies. The species has a large mouth, esophagus, and stomach capacity, which the authors cite as specializations for predation.

**Methods overview.**
- Fish were collected by seine, by hand, and with an electrical shocker. Invertebrates were sampled with a Surber sampler, Ekman dredge, bobbinet seine, and hand picking.
- Stomach contents were examined under a binocular microscope and weighed volumetrically or by number multiplied by average organism weight.
- Gross beta radioassay used nitric acid wet digestion, planchet counting on [[Proportional Gas-Flow Counter]] scalers, and results expressed in micromicrocuries per gram wet weight.
- Radiochemical analyses were performed by the Robert A. Taft Sanitary Engineering Center and Radiochemistry, Inc.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Radioactivity sources.** Most radioactivity in Doe Run samples came from natural decay of uranium-238 and thorium-232, identified by [[Bismuth-214]] and [[Actinium-228]] on a gamma spectrometer. Fallout nuclides ([[Strontium-90]], [[Cesium-137]]) were detected but usually made up a secondary share of total activity. Water contained Sr-90 at 0.0003 and Cs-137 at 0.0009 micromicrocuries per mL. The marl-rubble substrate contained Bi-214 at 5.7 micromicrocuries per gram. Uranium and thorium are widely distributed in carbonate sediments.

**Food web structure.** At Station I, sculpins act as secondary consumers, feeding mainly on herbivores and detritivores. Their food web is simpler, built on cyanophytes, chlorophytes, chrysophytes, and diatoms. At Station IV the web is more complex. Sculpins eat a wider range of primary consumers, predaceous insect larvae, crayfish, and other fishes, including [[Notropis Spilopterus]] and [[Etheostoma Flabellare]].

**Diet.** Sculpins eat nearly any animal they can swallow. Key prey included [[Gammarus Minus]], [[Asellus Bivittatus]], [[Chironomid Larvae]], [[Tipula Larvae]], [[Hydropsyche Bettenii]], and [[Paragnetina Media]]. Small sculpins (under 0.5 g) relied on chironomids at both stations. Mid-sized sculpins (0.5–1.7 g) fed mainly on amphipods at Station I and mayflies at Station IV. Crayfish became more important as fish grew.

**Seasonal and temporal patterns.** Seasonal differences in diet were not obvious. Small invertebrates were abundant year-round, and only extreme floods reduced the benthic fauna. Feeding peaked in the early night (6:00 P.M. to midnight). Daytime stomach contents mostly reflected hard parts of prey eaten the previous night.

## Key Findings

**Feeding and food intake**
- In a 24-hour study of 30 sculpins fed pre-weighed *Orconectes rusticus*, the authors tracked digestion hourly.
- The 302 fish used in the diet analysis were collected between 9:00 A.M. and 7:00 P.M.
- A 2-gram sculpin ingested about 16% of its body weight (0.32 g) per feeding period. The ratio of ingested food to daytime stomach contents was about 4:1.
- Smaller fish ate a greater proportion of their body weight per day than larger fish. Above about 4 g the daily ratio became nearly constant.

**Growth**
- Length-frequency data were collected from October 1959 through January 1961.
- Length-weight relationships were the same at both stations, but growth rates differed.
- Fish at Station I took about 7 months to reach 1 g. Fish at Station IV reached 1 g in about 3.5 months and weighed nearly 3.6 g at 7 months.
- At 12 months, Station I fish weighed about 3.5 g and Station IV fish nearly 10 g.
- Lifespan was 28 to 30 months. Few individuals survived a third summer.
- Relative growth differed markedly up to about 5 g. Beyond that, relative growth was similar when weight alone was considered.

**Reasons for slower growth at Station I** (the authors suggest three factors)
1. A lower percentage of ingested food was converted to growth at Station I, possibly because low, constant temperature slowed metabolism. The authors caution that these estimates are orders of magnitude rather than precise values.
2. Invertebrates were individually smaller and more abundant at Station I, and dense vegetation may have forced sculpins to expend more energy foraging.
3. The lack of silt-detritus bottoms at Station I may have reduced the supply of chironomid larvae, a major food for young fish.

**Radionuclide accumulation**
- Gross beta concentrations were reported for multiple taxa. Examples include *Cottus* and crayfish, which were compared across size classes in Figure 6.
- Concentration factors (food to water) ranged from 170 to 1,800. These were much lower than values reported for phosphorus in phytoplankton on the Columbia River and for green algae in White Oak Lake. The authors note that the naturally occurring radioelements studied are not known to be metabolically utilized, so direct comparison is limited.
- Small invertebrates (primary and secondary consumers) held 0.5 to 2.0 times the radioactivity of their food. [[Goniobasis]] discriminated against radiomaterials.
- Smaller vertebrates and invertebrates contained more radioactivity per unit weight than larger ones. Crayfish curves resembled the sculpin curve.
- Younger, faster-growing fish accumulate more radioactivity than mature, slow-growing fish. The authors attribute this to higher metabolic rates in young animals.
- Seasonal temperature effects on accumulation were minor because Doe Run temperatures are relatively constant.

**Retention**
- The percentage of ingested radioactivity retained in the body was about 6.5% early in life.
- It fell to near 1.5% just before maturity at 8 to 10 g, then rose slowly.
- Retention remained near 2% of total ingested radiomaterial over most of life. The authors interpret this as a fairly constant metabolic accumulation.
- Changes in body burden did not track changes in food radioactivity, so the authors conclude that metabolic change matters as much as dietary intake in determining retention.

**Limitations.** The authors describe this as a preliminary account. They rely on composite data from both stations for some calculations and state that several estimates are approximate. Some fish-growth and food-intake figures are derived from fitted curves rather than direct measurement.
