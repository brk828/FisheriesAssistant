## Executive Summary

This short field paper by Whitfield (1979) presents rare **in situ underwater observations** of the foraging behaviour of [[Terapon jarbua]], a lepidophagous (scale-eating) teleost, in the clear waters of the Bulolo and Mtumbane estuaries in southern Africa. Prior work by [[Whitfield & Blaber 1978]] on this species in [[Lake St. Lucia]] was limited to laboratory specimens due to high turbidity. The present study confirms and extends those findings under natural conditions, describing coordinated shoal-hunting behaviour, prey species targeted, and the ecological implications of scale-feeding as a "modified form of predation" rather than simple ectoparasite removal. The paper concludes that *T. jarbua* is physiologically adapted to a wide range of estuarine conditions (tidal open estuaries and temporarily closed systems), suggesting broad ecological tolerance across tropical and subtropical estuaries.

## Background & Description

- No new morphological data presented in this observational note; species identity and feeding morphology (jaw structure enabling scale removal via "lunging with open jaws") are referenced from [[Whitfield & Blaber 1978]].
- Body size is relevant to feeding mode: **smaller prey fish are swallowed whole**, while **larger fish have only their scales removed**, indicating a size-dependent switch in feeding strategy tied to jaw/mouth gape relative to prey body size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat range**: *T. jarbua* was recorded in the upper, middle, and lower reaches of both the Bulolo estuary (subject to tidal salinity and temperature fluctuation) and the Mtumbane estuary (temporarily in a closed phase), indicating tolerance of variable and fluctuating estuarine conditions.
- **Population proportion**: *T. jarbua* comprised only **2% of the observed fish sample (n = 401)** in the Bulolo estuary, closely matching the **1.2%** composition previously recorded at [[Lake St. Lucia]] ([[Whitfield 1977]]).
- **Ecological implication**: Because *T. jarbua* represents such a small fraction of the fish community, **over 90% of the estuarine fish fauna represents a potential and constantly renewable food resource** in the form of scales, supporting a sustainable lepidophagous strategy without necessarily depleting prey populations.
- **Prey species observed being attacked**:
  - [[Sarotherodon mossambicus]]
  - [[Monodactylus falciformis]]
  - [[Mugil cephalus]] (grey mullet)
  - [[Myxus capensis]] (freshwater mullet)
  - [[Rhabdosargus holubi]]
- **Predator-prey recognition**: Large *Mugil cephalus* and *Myxus capensis* displayed fleeing responses even when approached by *smaller* T. jarbua, suggesting **learned or innate recognition of predatory shape/colour pattern**, with one case of *M. cephalus* leaping out of the water to escape.
- **Turbidity as a mediating factor**: Higher turbidity in most South African estuaries (compared to clear-water Bulolo/Mtumbane) likely **facilitates closer approach** by *T. jarbua* schools to prey shoals, enhancing ambush success under natural (turbid) conditions.

## Key Findings

1. **Shoaling behaviour in foraging context**: *T. jarbua* forms schools of **2–7 individuals** while foraging. Leadership and inter-individual spacing within the school **changed continuously**, with no fixed hierarchy.
2. **Coordinated attack strategy**: Upon sighting prey, the school forms a **broad front**, with each fish attacking independently but simultaneously — creating confusion in the prey shoal and driving individuals toward other members of the hunting school (a cooperative/emergent hunting strategy, consistent with [[Radakov 1973]]'s finding that predator schools hunt more efficiently than solitary predators).
3. **Size-dependent prey handling**: 
   - Small prey (fish fry) → **swallowed whole**.
   - Larger prey → **scales removed only**, via lunging with open jaws.
4. **"Hit and retreat" hunting pattern**: After attacking a shoal, *T. jarbua* schools **leave the area** and return only after prey resume normal behaviour, preserving the **element of surprise** for repeated attacks.
5. **High attack frequency**: In a single **10-minute observation period**, a school of 5 *T. jarbua* attacked **17 different fish shoals**, explaining why stomach contents of wild-caught *T. jarbua* from Lake St. Lucia contain scales from **multiple fish species** ([[Whitfield & Blaber 1978]]).
6. **Reinterpretation of feeding origin**: Scale removal from large fish and whole ingestion of fry both represent a **modified form of predation**; consumption of ectoparasites incidentally present on scales is **incidental**, not the primary driver of this feeding mode (revising earlier hypotheses of ectoparasite-cleaning origin).
7. **Low population density supports sustainability**: Consistent low proportion (~1–2%) of *T. jarbua* in two independent estuarine systems (Bulolo, St. Lucia) supports the idea that lepidophagy is ecologically viable without decimating prey populations, since scales are a **renewable resource**.
8. **Broad estuarine tolerance**: Presence across tidal, fluctuating, and temporarily closed estuarine systems suggests *T. jarbua* is **physiologically well-adapted** to a wide range of estuarine salinity/temperature regimes, implying this predatory mode could operate across **tropical and subtropical estuaries** throughout its Indo-Pacific range.
