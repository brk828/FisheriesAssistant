# Geochronology, Geology, and Listric Normal Faulting of the Vulture Mountains, Maricopa County, Arizona

#geology #geochronology #tectonics #listric-normal-faulting

## Executive Summary

This paper is a geology and geochronology study, not an aquatic or fish study. It contains no ichthyological, aquatic-ecosystem, or water-chemistry content, so the ichthyology-specific frontmatter fields are marked N/A. The authors (Rehrig, Shafiqullah, and Damon, 1980) combine geologic mapping with 12 potassium-argon (K-Ar) and Rb-Sr isotopic analyses in the Vulture Mountains near [[Wickenburg, Arizona]]. They identify a 68.4 Ma [[Wickenburg Batholith]], a sequence of late Oligocene to Miocene silicic volcanics (about 26 to 16 Ma), and overlying late Miocene basalts (13.5 Ma). They argue that listric normal faulting in the 18 to 16 Ma interval marks a regional shift from low-angle to high-angle [[Basin and Range Extension]] at about 16 to 14 Ma.

## Background & Description

The Vulture Mountains lie southwest of Wickenburg in the northwest corner of Maricopa County, in the [[Basin and Range Province]]. Earlier maps showed Cretaceous volcanics, Precambrian gneiss and granite, and Quaternary basalt. The authors reassign much of this to Miocene volcanics and a Laramide basement, and propose the name Wickenburg batholith for the granodiorite body underlying the town.

Key rock units:

- **Precambrian basement:** coarse porphyritic granite, gneissic granite, and mafic schists. Foliation is steep, with strikes from west-northwest to northeast.
- **Wickenburg batholith:** an elongate granodiorite body with at least three intrusive phases (early diorite, main granodiorite, and porphyritic [[Quartz Monzonite]]).
- **Basal conglomerate:** reddish-brown, up to about 30 m thick, deposited on an erosion surface.
- **Silicic volcanic sequence:** [[Ultrapotassic Rhyolite]] flows, welded tuffs, ash-flow tuffs, and agglomerates, with minor basaltic andesite. Measured sections are about 470 m (old Highway 60) and about 300 m (Vulture Peak ridge).
- **Porphyritic dike swarm:** north-trending dikes of ultrapotassic rhyolite and quartz-biotite alkali rhyolite, typically 10 to 30 m thick.
- **Late basalts:** blocky, vesicular basalt flows with iddingsite after olivine, dipping less than 10 to 15 degrees, resting in angular unconformity on the tilted volcanics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The setting is a semi-arid mountain landscape of low relief, with dirt roads and jeep trails and historic gold mining at the Vulture mine. The paper's "environment" is tectonic and magmatic rather than biological.

Regionally, the Vulture Mountains sit within a north-northwest to northwest-trending zone of intense normal faulting, which the authors project about 20 miles south to the White Tank Mountains. Similar tilted Tertiary volcanics extend northeast through the Buckhorn, Big Horn, Eagle Tail, Kofa, and Castle Dome ranges to the Colorado River.

The authors place the Vulture rocks within the broader Southwest tectonic framework, including:

- Laramide convergence between the North American and Farallon plates (80 to 40 Ma, after Coney, 1976, 1977).
- The [[Harcuvar Metamorphic Core Complex]], northwest of the Vulture Mountains, with mylonitic rocks and tilted Tertiary cover.
- The [[Big Horn-Vulture Antiform]], a broad northwest-trending arch roughly 100 miles wide with little structural relief.

## Key Findings

**Geochronology (K-Ar, 12 samples)**

- Main granodiorite of the Wickenburg batholith (sample 71-34): **68.4 ± Ma**, overturning earlier assumptions that all range granites were Precambrian.
- Biotite granite north of the batholith (UAKA-75-55): **65.6 Ma**, interpreted as Laramide and possibly reset. The authors tentatively assign Precambrian age on compositional grounds.
- Vermiculitized biotite (71-14): anomalously young **62.9 Ma**.
- Biotite from the lowest rhyolite tuff (75-54): **26.0 Ma**, the oldest silicic volcanic date.
- Biotite porphyritic rhyolite dike truncated by fault (72-44): **18.2 Ma**.
- Rhyolite cataclasite at the sole of the low-angle fault (75-53): **17.1 Ma**.
- Rhyolite about 2 m above the fault (76-16): **16.1 Ma**, with 16.3 Ma as a minimum age for a whole-rock tuff (72-13).
- Basaltic andesite (72-60): **16.7 Ma**.
- Postdeformational dark dikes (71-35): about **16.2 Ma**.
- Untilted olivine basalt at Black Mountain (72-43): **13.5 Ma**.

**Fault timing**

- Listric normal faulting is bracketed between about **18 and 16 Ma**, based on truncated pre-fault dikes and undeformed post-fault dikes.
- Tilting in the silicic volcanics dips up to 70 degrees. The best-exposed fault dips 30° W, flattening to 10° W at the south end of Vulture Peak ridge.
- Tilted silicic rocks dip 30 to 40° NE beneath the flat basalts. The petrologic-tectonic transition from tilted silicic volcanics to untilted basalts occurred between about **16 and 14 Ma**.

**Petrology and geochemistry (whole-rock, 18 analyzed samples)**

- Silicic rocks average **8.1 wt% K₂O**. Fifteen of 18 samples exceed 69 wt% SiO₂.
- K₂O/Na₂O exceeds 3 in most silicic samples, which the authors classify as [[Ultrapotassic Rhyolite]]. Five are peralkaline, with normative acmite.
- Most samples belong to the subalkaline series (Irvine and Baragar, 1971). The rhyolites are described as the first reported ultrapotassic alkali rhyolites in Arizona's basin-range.
- The Black Mountain basalt (72-43) has lower alkali content, Na > K, and high CaO and MgO.

**Strontium isotopes**

- Initial ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr for pre-15 Ma silicic and intermediate rocks: **0.7071 to 0.7089, mean 0.7081**.
- Wickenburg granodiorite: **0.7069**.
- Black Mountain basalt: **0.7054**, distinctly lower, consistent with a subcrustal source.
- The authors rule out direct derivation from partial melting of Precambrian basement (ratios above 0.71) and direct mantle derivation (much lower ratios). They favor a late melt of a residuum from fractional crystallization of a more basic magma, derived from a fairly uniform lower-crustal source.

**Tectonic interpretation**

- Laramide intrusion was controlled by northeast-trending structures, reflecting northeast-southwest compression.
- Some 40 Ma later, extension rotated roughly 90 degrees, producing north-northwest dikes (about 18 to 16 Ma) and listric faulting.
- The authors propose that northeast-southwest crustal spreading at shallow levels, facilitated by dike emplacement, produced listric faulting and the [[Big Horn-Vulture Antiform]].
- The deformation persisted from about 20 to 15 Ma and was closely tied to maximum Basin and Range extension and a mid-Tertiary magmatic pulse.
- After about 14 Ma, extension shifted to high-angle normal faulting, the crust cooled and became more brittle, and relatively uncontaminated subcrustal melts were tapped.

**Regional correlation**

- Along the Colorado River between Davis Dam and Lake Mead, the structural-petrologic boundary falls at about 14 to 13 Ma (Anderson and others, 1972).
- McKee (1974) placed the petrologic switch from calc-alkalic to basaltic magmatism between 17 and 15 Ma across much of the Great Basin.
- The authors note the boundary's timing varies locally, from about 18 to 10 Ma, but most commonly falls between 16 and 14 Ma.
