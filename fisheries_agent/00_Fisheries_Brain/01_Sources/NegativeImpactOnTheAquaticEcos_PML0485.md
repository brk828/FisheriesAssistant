# Negative Impact of Introduced Aquarium and Commercial Fish on the Aquatic Ecosystems of Morelos, Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Contreras-MacBeath, Mejía Mojica and Carrillo Wilson (published in *Aquarium Sciences and Conservation*, 1998) documents how introduced fish have reshaped the freshwater fish fauna of the Mexican state of [[Morelos]]. Of the 22 fish species now present, 14 were introduced, mainly for [[Aquaculture]] and the [[Ornamental Fish Trade]]. The authors identify [[Fish Introductions]] as the most damaging pressure on local aquatic ecosystems, ahead of pollution and eutrophication. They attribute this to a lack of knowledge about aquatic resources and to fragmented, uncoordinated government management, and they propose an integrated, sustainable approach built on [[Artisanal Fisheries]].

## Background & Description

**Study area.** Morelos lies in central Mexico, south of the Neovolcanic Axis. Its waters drain mainly into the [[Balsas Basin]], which flows to the Pacific. The state has seven major rivers, six lakes, numerous ponds and about 50 springs. The principal river is the [[Amacuzac River]] (68.8 km within the state). The largest lake is [[Tequesquitengo Lake]], which is used mainly for tourism. The state spans temperate highlands in the north and subtropical lowlands in the south, and lies within the [[Mexican Transition Zone]], a historical dispersal route linking Nearctic and Neotropical fish groups.

**Methods.** Field surveys ran from 1994 to 1995 at a number of sites across the state (the exact count is garbled in the scan). Fishing effort was standardized at each site, covering all observed microhabitats. Specimens were fixed in formalin, preserved in isopropyl alcohol and deposited in the [[Centro de Investigaciones Biológicas]] collection. Historical comparison drew on [[Meek 1904]] (the first comprehensive survey of Mexican freshwater fish), the collection of the Escuela Nacional de Ciencias Biológicas (ENCB), and earlier literature. The authors define an "exotic" species as one introduced from another country. Releases between drainages within Mexico are termed [[Translocation]]s.

**Historical baseline.** Meek (1904) recorded ten species in the state, all native. Two of these have not been collected since: *Agonostomus monticola* (possibly the "trucha de río," a local name) and *Chonophorus tajasica* (now considered a synonym of *Awaous tajasica*, a goby that was once common in local rivers). The authors doubt the record of *Melaniris balsanus*, which has not been confirmed by any specimen.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Dynamic baseline vs. human impact.** Natural species successions occur through changes in food, refuge and water availability. The authors argue these differ in character from the changes caused by human activity.
- **Habitat loss and degradation.** Water extraction for agriculture and domestic use, deforestation and eutrophication have reduced ecosystem capacity. [[Lake Coatetelco]] shrank steadily after water extraction and soil erosion caused nutrient overload, and it dried up completely in 1989.
- **Urbanization and pollution.** Fish have been locally exterminated near the cities of Cuernavaca, Cuautla, Yautepec and Puente de Ixtla. Rivers near Zacatepec and Jojutla have become sewers.
- **Type localities lost.** Many type localities (sites where species were first described) can no longer be relocated, so these species cannot be sampled at their original sites.
- **Governance.** Several agencies manage aquatic resources (including SARH, SEPESCA, PDP, SEDESOL and CNA) with little coordination. The authors note that the 1990s creation of SEMARNAP is a step forward but insufficient on its own.

## Key Findings

**1. Species composition has shifted toward introduced fish.**
- 22 species are now present, spanning 17 genera and 8 families.
- 8 species are native and 14 were introduced (about 64%, consistent with the abstract's "more than half"; the abstract's "6.4%" appears to be an OCR error).
- Meek's ten native species were joined by new arrivals, while two native species disappeared.
- The earlier stable count of species in the 1960s masked significant faunal replacement. Three species were replaced by *Girardinichthys multiradiatus* (first recorded in the 1960s and thought to be indigenous to the Zempoala lakes).

**2. Introductions by purpose.**
- **Aquaculture (food and sport fish):** [[Rainbow Trout]] (cold lakes and springs), [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill Sunfish]], the Texas cichlid (stocked in Tequesquitengo for sport fishing), [[Common Carp]], [[Grass Carp]] and [[Tilapia]]. Tilapia has been stocked in nearly every water system since 1964, with variable success.
- **Ornamental fish (about 35.7% of introductions):** Almost all are poeciliids, mainly [[Guppy]] (*Poecilia reticulata*), [[Platy]] (*Xiphophorus variatus*) and [[Swordtail]] (*X. hellerii*), with distributions tied to aquaculture centers. Other ornamental species are a barb (*Barbus* sp., not established) and the [[Convict Cichlid]].
- **Unknown origin, probably escapees:** *Heterandria bimaculata* and *Poeciliopsis gracilis*.

**3. The Convict Cichlid is the most damaging introduction.**
- A stock was spilled from an ornamental farm in Cuautla into the [[Chalma River]].
- By 1990, it had spread to the Amacuzac, Chalma and Tembembe rivers and made up approximately 50% of the total fish community by biomass and abundance.
- It displaced the native [[Mojarra Criolla]] (*Cichlasoma istlanum*), caused the near-total disappearance of tilapia, and eliminated *Astyanax*-type natives (the complete absence of *Atherinella balsana*-related species was noted). The authors warn it could upset one of the country's most important inland fisheries because the state's rivers are part of the Balsas Basin.

**4. Tilapia stocking had unintended costs.**
- Stocking began in 1964 without any prior impact assessment.
- Tilapia's high reproductive rate and fast growth displaced other species, especially in closed pond systems.
- Fishery decline followed from unregulated harvest based on specimen size, which encourages stunting, and from degraded genetic lines used for restocking.

**5. Root causes are management and knowledge gaps.**
- Fragmented governance and a lack of coordination among agencies.
- A national policy that promotes tilapia in tropical waters and trout and carp in temperate waters, without regard to regional ecosystem integrity.
- Assumptions that native-species fisheries have little value, despite their importance to rural diets. The authors argue rural people eat a wide range of small native fish, which is culturally acceptable.

**6. Recommendations.**
- Adopt an integrated, ecosystem-based approach to aquatic resource management that involves all relevant sectors.
- Reevaluate artisanal fisheries to promote sustainability and protect native species, in line with the principles of the 1992 Rio de Janeiro Earth Summit ([[Rio Earth Summit 1992]]).
- Develop selective capture methods that reduce ecosystem damage and revive traditional methods.
- Improve regional ecological knowledge, including predator–prey and species relationships, and identify which species are productive under which conditions.
- Conservation alone is justified only in specific cases, with the aim of protecting native species.
