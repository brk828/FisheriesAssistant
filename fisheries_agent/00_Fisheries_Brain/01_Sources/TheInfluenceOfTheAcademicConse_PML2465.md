# The Influence of the Academic Conservation Biology Literature on Endangered Species Recovery Planning

#conservation-biology #endangered-species #recovery-plans

## Executive Summary

This study asks whether [[Academic Conservation Biology]] has a measurable effect on the content of U.S. [[Recovery Plan]]s for threatened and endangered species, which are mandated under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The authors analyzed a national database of 136 approved plans covering 181 species, compiled with the [[National Center for Ecological Analysis and Synthesis]], the [[Society for Conservation Biology]], and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. They tested four literature themes: [[Metapopulation Dynamics]], [[Population Viability Analysis]], [[Conservation Corridors]], and [[Conservation Genetics]].

The authors found a mixed picture. Plans approved after each topic's literature median were often more likely to contain data-collection and monitoring tasks on that topic. However, these tasks were rarely given high priority, and corridors showed no detectable influence. The authors conclude that the "ivory tower" stereotype is not strictly true, but that the flow of ideas into recovery planning is uneven.

## Background & Description

The paper responds to a published debate. [[Whitten Et Al 2001]] argued that conservation biology may be a "displacement behavior" for academics unable to affect large conservation problems. [[Kinnaird And O'Brien 2001]] countered that academic work supplies reserve design, population theory, and recovery science, and that science must be carried into practice by others.

Recovery plans are the first formal management step after a species is listed. They gather information, set recovery goals, and list recovery tasks in an Implementation Schedule. The authors define several key terms (Table 1):

- **Data collection:** gathering any information on a population or species.
- **Monitoring:** direct measures to determine whether recovery is occurring.
- **Recovery criteria:** requirements for down-listing or de-listing.
- **Recovery task:** a specific activity to promote recovery, each ranked on a 1–3 [[Task Priority]] scale, with 1 as high priority.

The data come from a survey developed jointly by the Society for Conservation Biology and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Plans were approved by the USFWS or the National Marine Fisheries Service. Because a plan, not a species, is the unit of analysis, a yes for any species in a multi-species plan counted for the whole plan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors frame the work as a test of communication between researchers and practitioners. The "ivory tower" idea holds that researchers debate conservation practice without reaching the managers of rare species. The authors did not aim to prove or disprove this stereotype. They aimed to identify which literature themes practitioners find useful, and why some are not adopted.

The four themes were chosen because each drew considerable attention, enough survey data existed for each, and each rose in prominence during the database period (1980–1998). Searches were run in [[Biological Abstracts]] using key terms, and the team acknowledged that [[Publication Bias]] could distort the literature index. Conferences, workshops, and working groups also spread information but were not measured.

## Key Findings

**Method.** The analysis compared plan content before and after each topic's literature median year. Likelihood-ratio [[Chi-Square Test]]s were used when every expected cell count was at least 5 and at least 20 plans were approved after the median. Otherwise the right-hand [[Fisher's Exact Test]] was used. Topics with fewer than 20 post-median plans were compared against the plan median year instead.

**Literature and plan timing.**
- Median publication years: PVA and conservation genetics 1995, corridors 1995, metapopulations 1996.
- Median plan approval year: 1991.
- Of 136 plans, 100 were approved before the 1995 median for PVA, genetics, and corridors, and 110 were approved before the 1996 metapopulation median.

**Population viability analysis (strongest trend).**
- Plans approved after the median addressed PVA more often (42% after vs. 22% before).
- Tasks to collect PVA information or models rose (46% after vs. 26% before), but the share of high-priority tasks did not differ.
- Use of monitoring data in predictive models rose (47% after vs. 17% before).

**Conservation genetics.**
- Information on genetics rose only slightly (69.4% after vs. 58.6% before), which was not significant.
- Tasks to collect genetic data rose significantly (73.5% after vs. 54.1% before), with marginal support for higher priority (p = 0.06).
- Genetic monitoring tasks rose (41.7% after vs. 15.3% before), but only 30 of 136 plans called for genetic monitoring, and priority did not follow.

**Metapopulations.**
- Spatial structure or migration information was already common before the median (87.5%), and every later plan included it (marginal, p = 0.08).
- The share of plans with monitoring tasks for population number, turnover, or movement rose (95% after vs. 79% before; marginal, p = 0.06), but high-priority monitoring did not change significantly (57% vs. 42%).
- Using the number or trend of subpopulations as a recovery criterion rose significantly (68% vs. 41%).

**Conservation corridors.** Corridors showed no detectable influence. Plans after the plan median were no more likely to recognize a need for corridor data or call for their acquisition or maintenance. Corridors had the lowest response rate of the four topics.

**Overall.** Seven of the 20 tests in Table 2 were significant. The authors note that the sample sizes after each median were consistently smaller, which may have limited statistical power.

**Interpretation.**
- The most consistent pattern is a mismatch: more plans assign tasks on PVA, genetics, and metapopulations, but task priority does not rise accordingly. The authors suggest task priorities may be a "zero-sum game," or that the literature lacks consensus on which topic matters most.
- Corridors may be unused because they are expensive and politically difficult, and because the literature disagrees about their value.
- Metapopulation processes were already widely recognized, likely reflecting earlier work in [[Island Biogeography]] and habitat fragmentation.

**Recommendations.**
- Academics should identify when one factor, such as genetics, should take precedence over others, and provide guidance on relative priorities.
- Journals such as [[Conservation Ecology Journal]] and [[Conservation Biology in Practice]] can help communication.
- Academic scientists are rarely involved in plans. Only 5% of plans were authored by academics, and 34% included academics on the recovery team. The authors encourage the USFWS and NMFS to invite more academics onto teams and encourage academics to accept such invitations.

**Limitations.**
- The survey records plan content at approval, not actual recovery actions.
- The study cannot establish causation, because both literature and plans may respond to a common external factor such as easier DNA analysis.
- The 1988 amendments to the Endangered Species Act may have influenced plans, but this would predate the literature medians and so would make the results conservative.
- A historical method using interviews and planning documents could detect subtler influences and is likely complementary to this coarse-scale approach.
