# Issues in Defining, Detecting, and Quantifying Rare Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #cyprinidae #rare-species #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This report, submitted by Francisco J. Abarca and Linda J. Allison of the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program) to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] on May 12, 2000, examines how rare species should be defined, detected, and quantified. Its practical motivation is the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP), a canal system that may carry non-native fishes from the [[Colorado River]] into the [[Gila River]] Basin. The authors argue that endangered native fishes and recently introduced non-native fishes might be studied under a unified framework of rarity. The discussion concludes that this unification is only partly justified: rare and invasive species show almost opposite characteristics, and monitoring for either is more complex than simply deciding how many samples to take.

## Background & Description

**Desert fish fauna.** About 170 species make up the native fish assemblage of the western United States (Dowling and Childs 1992). The western fauna is depauperate relative to ichthyofaunas east of the Rocky Mountains and contains many endemic subfaunas, shaped by geologic and climatic events that reduced habitat diversity and reliability. Human habitat destruction and introduced non-native species have driven most desert fishes in the Southwest toward listing as threatened or endangered.

**Central Arizona Project.** The CAP diverts Colorado River water through pipelines and aqueducts from Lake Havasu to near Tucson. It was completed in 1993, largely by the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation. A [[Biological Opinion]] by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] concluded that CAP water delivery to the Gila River Basin would jeopardize four federally listed fishes: [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The opinion directed the Bureau to establish baseline fish community data and monitor non-native impacts.

**Rarity concepts.** Rarity is defined in many ways, typically using one to a few variables: abundance, range size, habitat specificity, temporal persistence, threat, gene flow, genetic diversity, endemism, or taxonomic distinctness. The most widely used framework is [[Rabinowitz's Rarity Scheme]] (Rabinowitz 1981; Rabinowitz et al. 1986), which classifies species by geographic range (large or small), habitat specificity (wide or narrow), and local abundance (somewhere large or everywhere small). [[Rey-Benayas Expanded Model]] (1999) adds a fourth criterion, habitat occupancy.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Connectivity and invasion risk.** The CAP canal links to the Lower Colorado River, the Salt River Project canals, and the Salt River during floods. Fishes in these connected waters may alter the abundance of native and non-native species in the San Pedro and Gila rivers. Common, widespread fishes can be monitored with conventional techniques, but uncommon species are hard to detect, let alone estimate in density.

**Vulnerability.** The authors list several factors that make the Gila and San Pedro rivers highly vulnerable to invasion: extensive habitat modification, an already depauperate fish fauna, the presence of alien species, proximity to urban development, and the CAP as a potential corridor. Detection of alien fishes in the CAP and parts of these rivers does not necessarily mean they have established; detections may reflect a continuous supply of individuals from the canal, which makes successful recruitment more likely if conditions become favorable.

**Biological invasion.** Biological invasion is defined as the geographic expansion of a species into an area it previously did not occupy (Mack et al. 2000). Invasions have produced notable impacts, such as [[Zebra Mussel]] damage to shipping and power plants in the Great Lakes, and [[Sea Lamprey]] decline of [[Lake Trout]] populations.

## Key Findings

**Survey data (Table 1 and Table 2):**
- Fifteen non-native or otherwise absent species were detected in CAP canals or the Salt River since 1970 but were not recorded during AGFD monitoring of the San Pedro and Gila rivers (1995 to 1998). Examples include [[Oscar]], [[Grass Carp]], [[Walleye]], and [[Rainbow Trout]]. [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] are native fishes found in the Salt River.
- Table 2 summarizes 17 species detected in the San Pedro and Gila rivers from 1995 to 1998 across seven monitored reaches. Densities are reported as counts per 200 m. Examples: [[Red Shiner]] 14.90, [[Desert Sucker]] 7.04, [[Longfin Dace]] 7.82, [[Mosquitofish]] 5.36, and [[Fathead Minnow]] 0.11.
- Several species are spatially restricted or occur at low density, including [[Smallmouth Bass]] (1 reach, 0.12), [[Black Crappie]] (2 reaches, 0.26), and [[Flathead Catfish]] (0.68).
- None of the four federally listed fishes was detected in AGFD surveys from 1995 through 1998.

**Definitions of rarity:**
- Rabinowitz's three-criteria scheme yields eight combinations; only one (wide range, broad habitat specificity, locally large abundance) is considered truly common.
- The Rey-Benayas et al. (1999) expansion yields 16 combinations; only one is common, requiring frequent habitat occupation in addition to the other traits. The three added endangerment categories are locally endangered, potentially endangered, and endangered.
- Operational definitions of rarity from state and national sources (e.g., Miller 1972; Heintzelman 1971) treat rarity as a third threat level after endangered and threatened, describing species that are not directly threatened but are vulnerable because of small numbers or restricted habitat.

**Attributes of rare species:** Gaston and Kunin's synthesis suggests rare species tend to have less reliance on outcrossing, lower reproductive investment, poorer dispersal, higher homozygosity, narrower resource use, higher trophic position, and body size patterns that depend on range. For freshwater fishes, the relationships between body size, range size, and density are weak (Gaston and Lawton 1990a). Fishes may resemble plants in showing three forms of rarity: widespread but sparse, restricted but locally common, and restricted and locally rare.

**Detection and sampling:**
- The negative binomial distribution is appropriate for rare, clumped organisms, while the Poisson distribution suits randomly distributed counts. At low densities (below about 0.1 per sampling unit), the Poisson approximation performs adequately (Green and Young 1993).
- Under a Poisson model, detecting a species at 0.1 per sampling unit with a 5% failure rate requires about 30 sampling units (95% detection probability).
- Estimating density to ±10% for a rare species at density 0.1 requires about 3,841 samples (Kovalak et al. 1986; Zar 1996), showing strong diminishing returns when moving from detection to quantification.
- Bayesian methods (Nicholson and Barry 1995) incorporate prior probabilities, illustrated with the invading [[Manila Clam]].

**Monitoring intensity:** Menges and Gordon (1996) describe three levels: occurrence mapping, quantitative abundance assessment, and demographic monitoring. These can be mixed within one study.

**Invasion process:** Invasion proceeds through arrival, establishment, and integration (naturalization), with a lag phase often preceding rapid growth. Most extinctions of immigrant populations occur during the lag phase, when they are hard to tell apart from future invaders. [[Brown's Invasion Patterns]] (1989) describe five patterns in vertebrates, including the role of disturbance and human association.

**Attributes of successful invaders (Table 5):** Successful invaders tend to have large geographic range, high abundance in their original range, high mobility and dispersal, short generation times, high genetic variability, gregarious social patterns, large body size relative to relatives, strong human association, and wide adaptability. Unsuccessful invaders tend to show the opposite traits.

**Central conclusions:**
- Rare and invasive species show almost opposite characteristics, so a single framework for both is only partly justified.
- Endangered and rare species share traits that limit range expansion and population growth, making them more similar to each other than to invasive species.
- Management should treat each potential invader case by case, considering spatial and temporal scales, each invasion stage, and the role of each life stage (larvae, juveniles, adults).
- Experiments on invasion conditions could complement the monitoring framework.
- Knowledge of alien fish ecology in desert aquatic systems is limited, and a systematic approach is needed.
