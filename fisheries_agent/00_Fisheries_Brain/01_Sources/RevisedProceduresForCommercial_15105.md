## Executive Summary
This 1957 paper by H.S. Swingle (Auburn University, Alabama) presents revised commercial pond-culture procedures for the [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*Megastomatobus cyprinellus*, now classified as *Ictiobus cyprinellus*) in the Southeastern United States. Building on five years of experimental production at Auburn, the study details brood fish selection, disease treatment protocols, spawning induction, fingerling rearing, and market-size growout, along with economic analysis of stocking density versus fish size and dressed-weight yield. The work represents an early and detailed applied aquaculture protocol for a native North American catostomid species raised as a food fish, comparable in scope to management guides for [[Channel Catfish]] and other pond-cultured species of the era.

## Background & Description
- **Sexual dimorphism**: Males identifiable in spring by ability to express milt under light pressure on the body sides; male cheeks are rough to the touch, while female cheeks are smooth.
- **Size classes referenced**: Brood fish selected at 1+ years old, weighing 1 pound or more, with 2–4 pound individuals preferred for higher fecundity/production of young.
- **Growth benchmarks**: Fingerlings reach 4–5 inches by October after ~8 months of rearing; market fish range from 1.3 to 3.6 pounds depending on stocking density and grow-out duration (6–18 months).
- **Dressed yield**: At 2.4 pounds live weight, dressing (removal of head, skin, viscera, and body fluids) results in ~47% weight loss, yielding approximately 53% dressed weight — an important morphological/processing metric for [[Fish Dressing Yield]] comparisons across species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Managed earthen pond systems; wintering ponds require 4–6 feet water depth without constant freshwater inflow to avoid diluting a hypothesized "repressive factor" tied to fish excretion that suppresses reproduction.
- **Reproductive cues**: Spawning is temperature-triggered, occurring at water temperatures ≥60°F, with heaviest spawning activity between 60–65°F. Eggs hatch in ~5 days at 65–70°F.
- **Spawning substrate**: Spawning ponds are kept dry beforehand to grow a grass crop, which — once flooded — serves as an egg-laying substrate, echoing the paper's title concerning vegetative substrates for egg deposition.
- **Density-dependent reproduction**: High stocking densities (≥1,000 lbs/acre) in wintering ponds create a "repression" effect (likely linked to excretory metabolite buildup) that inhibits spawning until diluted by heavy rainfall or fresh water inflow at temperatures above 60°F.
- **Interspecific pond community management**: [[Largemouth Bass]] fingerlings (25–50 per acre) are co-stocked with buffalo to control wild fish competitors, reflecting a predator-based biological control strategy within the pond ecosystem.
- **Trophic/feeding inputs**: Brood fish supplemented with "Auburn No. 1" feed (35% soybean oil meal, 35% peanut oil meal, 15% fish meal, 15% distillers dried solubles) at 1% body weight/day when water temp exceeds 55°F; growout ponds fertilized with 8-8-2 fertilizer (100–200 lbs/acre) to stimulate natural productivity, linking to broader [[Pond Fertilization]] and plankton-based food web management practices.

## Key Findings
- **Disease/parasite control protocol**: Brood and fingerling fish treated with potassium permanganate (10 ppm, 1 hr — toxic beyond this exposure), formalin (15 ppm, 6 hrs), and acriflavine (1 ppm, 12 hrs) to control external protozoan parasites, gill flukes, and surface pathogens; these treatments are ineffective against internal parasites/disease. External ulcers from scale loss are treated by direct swabbing with 20% potassium permanganate solution, avoiding gill contact.
- **Reproductive productivity**: Spawning stock of 50 brood fish/acre typically spawns within 24–48 hours of pond stocking; if not laid within 1–2 weeks, pond draining/refilling with fresh water is required to trigger spawning. Fry production averaged 30,000–100,000 young per acre.
- **Stocking density vs. growth trade-off**: 
  - 432 fish/acre (5-inch fingerlings) over 9 months yielded 586.2 lbs/acre at 1.3 lbs average size (6.2¢/lb fertilizer cost).
  - Lower-density trials (120 vs. 240 fish/acre) over 18 months showed 120/acre produced larger fish (3.6 lbs avg, 10.4 lbs fertilizer efficiency metric) vs. 240/acre (2.4 lbs avg, 8.3 lbs metric) — demonstrating the classic aquaculture trade-off between total biomass yield and individual fish size, comparable to stocking studies in [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Tilapia]] pond culture.
- **Economic modeling**: Market value calculations show that premium pricing for larger fish (e.g., 3.6 lb fish needing a 5¢/lb premium at 25¢/lb vs. 20¢/lb for 2.4 lb fish) can equalize per-acre returns (~$63/acre) despite lower total production — underscoring that optimal stocking rate depends on market price structure for different size classes.
- **Processing yield economics**: Dressed fish yield of 53% live weight nearly doubles effective production cost per pound (10¢/lb live → 19¢/lb dressed, plus labor), a critical consideration for [[Commercial Fish Processing]] economics.
- **Complete revised management protocol** (10-step) consolidates brood selection, disinfection, wintering, spawning pond preparation, egg incubation, fry/fingerling handling, fertilization schedule, co-stocking with predatory bass, and harvest timing — establishing a replicable commercial production system for the species.
