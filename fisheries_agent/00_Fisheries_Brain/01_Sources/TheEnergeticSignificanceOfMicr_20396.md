# Energetic Significance of Microhabitat Use in Two Stream Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 University of Georgia doctoral dissertation by Jennifer Hill, directed by Gary D. Grossman, examines how stream fishes in Coweeta Creek, North Carolina use microhabitat. It combines four components: a [[Subcutaneous Marking Technique]] for individually identifying fish, a [[Home Range Estimates|home range]] mark-recapture study, and a mechanistic [[Energetic Cost-Benefit Model]] of current-velocity selection by [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Rosyside Dace]]. The model predicts that fish hold positions where net energy gain is maximized, and its predictions closely matched observed velocity use. Capture success was identified as the most important model component.

## Background & Description

The dissertation has five chapters: an introduction, a marking study (Chapter 2), a home range study (Chapter 3), a microhabitat energetics model (Chapter 4), and conclusions (Chapter 5). Chapters 2 and 3 were published as Hill and Grossman (1987) in *Copeia*. Chapter 4 was prepared for submission to *Ecological Monographs*.

The study focused on the numerically dominant fishes of Coweeta Creek, a fourth-order stream in the Blue Ridge Mountains. The dominant assemblage included [[Mottled Sculpin]], [[Longnose Dace]], and [[Rosyside Dace]]. Rainbow trout were the focal salmonid of the model. Mottled sculpin and longnose dace are benthic, while rosyside dace are water-column inhabitants. The author notes that the three dominant species together made up more than 80% of numerical abundance.

Study site characteristics (Chapter 3 and Chapter 4):
- Stream reach of 177 m, comprising 79 m riffles, 46 m runs and 52 m pools.
- Stream width 2–11 m, with most sections 5–8 m wide.
- Depth about 20 cm in riffles and 40–70 cm in runs and pools.
- Average current velocity about 25 cm/s; velocities ranged from about 0 to 100 cm/s.
- Elevation approximately 700 m; riparian vegetation dominated by Rhododendron, dogwood and mountain laurel.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and microhabitat.** Current velocity was selected as the key niche dimension because it strongly influences microhabitat use in rainbow trout and rosyside dace. The author argues that these two species are potential competitors because they use similar microhabitats ([[Grossman and Freeman 1987]]).

**Storm effects.** Storms of about 17 cm rainfall in May 1984 increased flow about five-fold. Storms of this intensity occur at Coweeta about every 1–2 years. Benthic species showed minimal change in movement after the storm, while rosyside dace movements increased.

**Diel and seasonal behavior.** Both species fed almost entirely during daylight and shifted to shallow or substrate-associated resting areas at night. In winter, when temperatures were at or below 2 °C, fish sheltered in cobble substrate. Drift density and caloric value were lowest in fall, consistent with earlier Coweeta studies.

**Drift and prey.** Seven invertebrate taxa dominated the drift: Copepoda, Diptera, Ephemeroptera, Plecoptera, Trichoptera, Annelida and Acarina. Most drifting organisms were below the 2 mm minimum prey size used for the energy model.

**Relevance to management.** The author notes that the models may help predict effects of [[Flow Diversion]] and [[Hydropower Facilities]] on fish energy budgets and of species introductions on microhabitat overlap.

## Key Findings

**Subcutaneous marking (Chapter 2).**
- Subcutaneous injection of acrylic paints was tested on 90 laboratory fish across six species, using nine body positions and five colors, over 25 weeks.
- Marking did not significantly affect growth in standard length (N = 16, T = 50.0, P > .05) or weight (N = 15, T = 58.5, P > .05).
- Mortality of mottled sculpin (27 experimental, 12 control) was linked to [[Saprolegnia]] infection, not to marks.
- About 50% of marks remained identifiable for 25 weeks in all species except Notropis spp. Marks around pectoral and pelvic fins, adjacent to the anal fin, between the pelvics, and in the gular region retained best.
- In the field, anal and gular marks gave high recapture rates. Dorsal and caudal marks gave low rates. Hooker's Green gave poor recapture in several species.

**Home range (Chapter 3).**
- Mean movement between captures: mottled sculpin 12.9 m, longnose dace 13.4 m, rosyside dace 19.3 m.
- Mean time between captures was 128 days (maximum 458 days). 86% of recaptures were in the section of previous capture.
- Over 18 months, 94 of 365 marked individuals were recaptured (about 26%). Recapture rates were 28% (sculpin), 33% (longnose dace) and 19% (rosyside dace).
- Because fish may not have used the whole section (mean 10.4 m), the author states these values likely overestimate true home range. The maximum home range was 19.3 m, and 25 m sections were used in the microhabitat study.
- Electrofishing of nearby sections found almost no marked fish, supporting a sedentary interpretation.

**Energetic model (Chapter 4).**
- Net energy gain was defined as E = B − C. Costs were standard and active metabolism from respirometry (Facey 1987). Benefits were the product of 0.68 (utilization efficiency, with 32% lost to specific dynamic action, egestion and excretion), prey capture success, and drift caloric availability.
- Sixteen separate models were derived: 2 species × 2 size classes × 4 seasons.
- Dace size classes were 47–52 and 53–70 mm SL. Trout size classes were 53–70 and 71–125 mm SL.
- Capture success was tested at 0–40 cm/s in 5 cm/s steps, at 5 °C and 15 °C.
- Trout and dace held positions about 4.6–5.6 cm/s slower than their strike positions. A correction was applied to the model.
- Predicted velocities matched observed velocities with an average deviation of 2.6 cm/s for the full energetic model.
- Using capture success alone (point of maximum deceleration in capture success curves) gave an average deviation of 1.8 cm/s.
- The full model fell within the 10% acceptance criterion for all eight trout models and six of eight dace models.
- Fall velocity use was lower than predicted in all cases, probably related to a drought that reduced velocity availability.
- Dace and trout held positions near their energetic optima, leading the author to conclude that competitive exclusion from optimal velocity sites was unlikely in Coweeta Creek, and that velocity use appeared guild-specific.

**Management implications (Chapter 5).**
- A five-fold increase in flow may displace fish beyond their normal home ranges, with greatest effects on water-column species in streams with few refuges.
- For trout, flow increases from 20 to 30 cm/s may cause up to 50% net energy losses, depending on size and season. Flows up to 40 cm/s could make a positive energy budget impossible.
- Structural refuges can offset these effects by providing low-cost, low-velocity positions close to moderate-velocity feeding areas.
- Because the velocity of maximum net energy gain can be approximated by capture success, competition might be assessed by comparing capture success curves of different species. This needs testing in other systems.

[[Coweeta Creek]] and [[Optimality Models]] are the central contexts for these findings.
