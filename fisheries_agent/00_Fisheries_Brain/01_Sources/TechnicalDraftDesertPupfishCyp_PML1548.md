# Desert Pupfish (Cyprinodon macularius) Technical Draft Recovery Action Plan

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This is a 1991 technical draft of the recovery action plan for the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), prepared by Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University) and Ronald W. Sada for U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Region 2 under contract to the Tonto National Forest. It was reviewed by the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]] and transmitted to the Service with a cover letter dated July 16, 1991. The species is listed as endangered by the United States, the IUCN and the states of Arizona and California.

Two subspecies are recognized: the [[Colorado River Pupfish]] (*C. m. macularius*) and the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*C. m. eremus*). Natural populations of the Colorado River subspecies are extirpated from Arizona and restricted to two natural localities in California and the Colorado River delta in Sonora and Baja California. The Quitobaquito form persists in a single modified spring and pool at [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], Arizona. Both natural and transplanted populations are threatened by habitat loss and interactions with non-native fishes.

The plan calls for habitat acquisition, water security, eradication of non-native fishes on a case-by-case basis, genetic protection, re-establishment in a three-tier system, monitoring, and public education. Downlisting of the Colorado River subspecies is considered feasible about ten years after the plan is completed, while delisting is considered infeasible in the foreseeable future. Recovery is estimated to take five years of initial implementation.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The species was described by Baird and Girard (1853) from specimens collected in the San Pedro River, Arizona. Two recognized subspecies are *C. m. macularius* and *C. m. eremus*, plus an undiagnosed form in the Rio Sonoyta, Sonora. The name *C. m. californiensis* is no longer recognized as valid, and *Lucania browni* was synonymized with the species.

**Genetics.** Allozyme analysis by Turner (1983) of six populations found mean heterozygosity within the range reported for the euryhaline *Aphanius dispar*, and low but detectable differentiation among the three geographic areas and among four Salton Sink populations.

**Morphology.** The body is deep and laterally compressed in adults, with a superior, highly protractile mouth and pharyngeal jaw teeth. Ground color is silvery in females and juveniles, with narrow vertical dark bars. Breeding males are bright yellow to orange on the caudal fin and caudal peduncle and iridescent light-to-blue. The Quitobaquito form differs mainly in male head and body dimensions and in reduced pelvic fins.

**Life history.** Pupfish can become sexually mature at about six weeks at 1.5 cm total length. Breeding occurs from early spring into winter when water temperature exceeds about 20 °C. Territorial males defend 1 to 2 square meters of bottom. Territoriality is the common breeding system; dominant-pair breeding occurs in low-productivity habitats. Females spawn a single relatively large egg (about 1 mm) at a time, which is fertilized immediately. Incubation takes about 10 days at 20 °C. Wild life span is under one year for some populations, two years for others, and up to three years for Quitobaquito fish.

**Diet.** Larvae feed on tiny invertebrates. Adults are opportunistic omnivores eating algae, plants, invertebrates (ostracods, copepods, insects, snails), and detritus. Quitobaquito fish have been reported to eat their own eggs and young.

**Tolerance.** Desert pupfish tolerate water temperatures to 45 °C, dissolved oxygen as low as 0.1–0.4 mg/L, and salinity about twice that of seawater. They also tolerate abrupt changes in salinity and temperature that are lethal to most fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic distribution.** Pupfish historically occupied the Gila and Salt basins below about 1,500 m elevation, the lower Colorado River and its delta, the Rio Sonoyta, the Laguna Salada basin, and the Salton Sink. Their distribution was widespread but patchy, with populations in stable springs persisting for long periods and river populations fluctuating with drought and flood.

**Co-occurring fishes.** Harsh habitats were often free of other fishes. In cienegas and springs, the [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) was a common co-inhabitant. Other native species included Gila chub, speckled dace, desert sucker, longfin dace, Sonora sucker and roundtail chub in deeper stream waters, and bonytail, razorback sucker, Colorado squawfish and roundtail chub in large rivers.

**Threats.** Declines were driven by habitat loss (groundwater pumping, spring and stream dewatering, channel erosion), habitat modification (impoundment, channelization, diversion, livestock grazing), pollution, and non-native species. Key non-native threats include [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), sailfin molly, cichlids (*Oreochromis* and *Tilapia*), [[Black Bullhead]], and the [[American Bullfrog]]. Largemouth bass caused extinction of the Monkey Spring pupfish. Golden shiner stocking threatened the Quitobaquito population in 1968–1969. Agricultural pesticide drift and mercury detected near the Cerro Prieto geothermal field (up to 0.44 µg/g dry weight in fish) are additional concerns. Introduced saltcedar may reduce water supply to small habitats.

**Protection context.** Major sites include Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, the Reserva de la Biosfera El Pinacate, and a natural area for totoaba in the lower Colorado River delta. Management plans and coordination involve the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the [[Bureau Of Land Management]], the National Park Service, the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the California Department of Fish and Game, and [[Centro Ecologico De Sonora]].

## Key Findings

- **Current status:** The Colorado River subspecies is extirpated from Arizona as natural populations. Current natural populations are limited to a few localities in California, Sonora and Baja California.
- **Transplants:** At least 8 and as many as 17 Arizona transplant sites supported pupfish in spring 1991, with populations ranging from fewer than 100 to more than 1,000 individuals. Roughly 40 transplanted populations were documented across the species' range, and about 30 of them had failed by 1987, mostly from habitat desiccation (eight populations) or unknown reasons (nine).
- **Quitobaquito Pupfish:** The population in Quitobaquito Spring fluctuated from about 500 to 1,000 fish in normal conditions. Captive stocks exist at hatcheries and universities. Some Quitobaquito stocks released outside the historic range are of questionable genetic purity and should be destroyed.
- **Recovery criteria for downlisting:**
  1. Natural populations in the U.S. and Mexico are secure, comprising six metapopulations at 14 known locations.
  2. Re-established populations are secure within probable historic range.
  3. A protocol for genetic exchange among re-established populations is developed and implemented.
  4. A secure Quitobaquito refugium population is established near Quitobaquito Spring.
  5. Monitoring plans are in place.
- **Viable population definition:** Not fewer than 500 overwintering adults, or existing numbers if greater, with a normal sex ratio and self-sustaining reproduction.
- **Security standard:** In the U.S., protection requires legal possession of water rights and habitat by a conservation-oriented agency or organization, adequate statutory authority, regulations, and at least 10 years of demonstrated capability, or a legally binding long-term agreement of at least 25 years. Locally adjacent populations are considered separate only if a discrete catastrophic event is likely to affect just one population.
- **Three-tier re-establishment:** Tier 1 consists of natural populations (the most valuable genetic reservoir). Tier 2 consists of re-established populations in the best available natural habitats. Tier 3 consists of populations in natural or quasi-natural refugia. Tier 3 populations must persist for 10 years to count toward recovery. The target is 3 Tier 2 populations in Arizona (6 in the Colorado delta and Rio Sonoyta in combination with other tiers, per the table) and a total of 17 Tier 3 populations specified by region.
- **Monitoring:** Populations should be monitored twice annually (before spring spawning and in late summer–early autumn). Genetic monitoring is recommended every five years, using 4–5 diagnostic loci with about 10 males and 10 females per population.
- **Research priorities:** A biochemical-genetics study (including mitochondrial DNA), habitat criteria, biological criteria, life history studies and population persistence factors are secondary but considered essential.
- **Cost and timeline:** Recovery is expected to take five years to plan and implement, followed by a ten-year period before downlisting. The estimated total cost of downlisting excludes property and water rights acquisition and is given in the plan as an approximate annual figure over the five-year period, with a lower figure thereafter for biannual monitoring and reporting. The exact dollar amounts are obscured in the source text.
- **Public education:** Public and agency education programs, bilingual materials, and training in the Endangered Species Act are recommended.
