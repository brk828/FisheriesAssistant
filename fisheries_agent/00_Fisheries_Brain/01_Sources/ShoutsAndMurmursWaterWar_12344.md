# Water War: Fossil Groundwater in the Great Basin and Las Vegas

#hydrogeology #water-resources #groundwater #great-basin

## Executive Summary

This is a literary essay by John McPhee on the groundwater of the [[Great Basin]] and the conflict over its transfer to [[Las Vegas]]. The document does not report fish or other aquatic species data. Its subject is the aquifer system that sustains desert springs and seeps, and the proposal to pump that stored "fossil" water hundreds of miles south to a fast-growing city. The author frames the dispute as the "Water War," a fight in which ranchers and environmentalists find themselves on the same side, and the final decision rests with the state engineer, not the legislature or the governor.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Eastern Nevada's long, dry valleys ("waterlogged basins" or "saturated valleys") lie between high mountain ranges. The landscape is dominated by bunchgrass, buffalo grass, shadscale, and white and black sage, with scattered windmills and cottonwood clusters marking settlement.
- **Water source:** The subsurface holds essentially no fresh recharge. The stored water is described as [[Fossil Water]], accumulated during the [[Pleistocene]] when glacial-era rainfall was heavy enough to fill two freshwater lakes "about the size of Lake Michigan and Lake Erie" in the Great Basin. The author estimates storage at about ten thousand years.
- **Expression at the surface:** This water reaches the surface only in small quantities as spring creeks and seeps, which sustain the valleys' wildlife and settlement.
- **Legal framework:** In Nevada, groundwater can be bought and transported between basins within the law, provided the transfer does not impinge on existing rights and is in the public interest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Wildlife at risk:** The author lists [[Mountain Sheep]], antelope, deer, coyotes, eagles, badgers, and bobcats as species that would "forever disappear" if permanent springs go dry under the proposed pumping.
- **Land subsidence:** Groundwater extraction around Las Vegas has caused the land surface to subside six feet. The essay notes that well casings now "stand in the air."
- **Urban water demand:** Las Vegas is described as having less rain than some places in the [[Sahara]], yet it has nearly a million residents, with four thousand new residents arriving each month. Its water uses include eight lakes in one new subdivision, twenty-two golf courses (at sixteen hundred gallons a divot), green lawns along the Strip, and water attractions such as Wet 'N Wild and an outdoor water display at the Mirage.
- **Precedents for interbasin transfer:** The essay cites [[Owens Valley]], where Los Angeles diverted water, Colorado water sent through tunnels under the Continental Divide to communities north of Denver, and proposed undersea and overland pipelines from Alaska to California and Texas.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed pumping:** Las Vegas has applied to drill the saturated valleys, collect the fossil water at a central site, and pipe it south. The author compares the process to collecting maple sap in Quebec.
- **Decision authority:** The decision rests with R. Michael Turnipseed, the state engineer, rather than the legislature or governor.
- **Scale of application:** The city's claim is described as renewable, but the author notes that the annual application equals "the equivalent of a one-acre pond eight hundred and sixty thousand feet deep" (as stated in the text).
- **Shifting alliances:** Ranchers and environmentalists, historically opposed over federal land use, are now united against the transfer under the "Water War" banner.
- **Core conflict:** The essay contrasts the cost of depleting a finite, Pleistocene-era aquifer with the city's ability to purchase and move water across basins, raising the question of whether a renewable claim holds for non-renewable fossil water.
