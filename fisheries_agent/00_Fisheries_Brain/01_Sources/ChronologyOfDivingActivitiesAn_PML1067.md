# Chronology of Diving Activities and Underground Surveys in Devils Hole, Nevada (1950–86)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey open-file report by Ray J. Hoffman compiles a chronology of SCUBA diving, underground exploration, and hydrologic monitoring in [[Devils Hole]] and the adjoining [[Devils Hole Cave]] in Nye County, Nevada, covering 1950–1986. The record recognizes past underwater explorers, scientists, and observers and provides a historical baseline for present and future investigations. It also summarizes the water-level history of the site, which is central to the survival of the endangered [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*[[Cyprinodon Diabolis]]*).

## Background & Description

Devils Hole is a planar fissure formed by faulting in carbonate rock (limestone and dolomite) in [[Ash Meadows]], near the [[Amargosa Desert]]. The site is a roughly 40-acre tract within [[Death Valley National Monument]], about 30 miles east of the main monument area. The fissure intersects the regional water table, creating a pool about 50 feet long and 10 feet wide, located nearly 50 feet below the general land surface at an altitude of about 2,360 feet above sea level. The water is a constant 34 °C (about 93 °F), and the pool offers a warm, weightless entrance to a deep, dark network of subterranean chambers and passageways. Some chambers are air-filled, and one is extensive. The maximum depth of the water-filled fissure is unknown and possibly unmeasurable.

The upper 80 feet of the pool (the main chamber) is the core habitat of the Devils Hole pupfish, an endemic and endangered species that evolved from a Pleistocene-age ancestral stock. Adult fish are small (about 1 inch). Females and juveniles are olive-drab, while adult males are much darker and sometimes show an iridescent sheen. The diet consists mainly of microscopic plants and small aquatic invertebrates. Spawning occurs through spring and summer, with a peak in April and May. The fish are active and playful, which gives them their common name.

[[Devils Hole Cave]] lies about 500 feet northeast of Devils Hole. It is a nearly vertical hole about 3 feet in diameter at the entrance that widens abruptly to 20–25 feet and descends about 130 feet to the water table. There it forms four crescent-shaped pools and widens to more than 50 feet, giving the cave the shape of a tall boot. The pools may be extensive enough to connect with Devils Hole. [[Figure 2]] of the report shows that the fissure is extensive vertically and in a northeast–southwest direction, with an average width of less than 6 feet, and that its maximum depth exceeds 300 feet. Breakdown rock fragments have wedged into the narrow fissure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Devils Hole ecosystem is a groundwater-fed, isolated habitat. The [[U.S. Geological Survey]] has collected water-resource data at the site intermittently from 1953 to 1962 and almost continuously since 1962. Most of these data are water-level measurements made under a [[Groundwater Monitoring Program]] managed with the [[National Park Service]] to ensure the survival of the pupfish.

Water levels changed significantly during the period. Beginning in 1969, increased irrigation pumping in Ash Meadows caused a dramatic decline. The falling level threatened to expose the submerged shelf where the pupfish feed and breed, a slightly submerged area about 10 by 20 feet. Pumping was halted in the vicinity in 1973, which apparently reversed the decline. By 1985 the water level had risen to near its pre-1969 level. The report cites [[Dudley and Larson]] (1976) on the effects of irrigation pumping on desert pupfish habitats.

In late 1984 the Geological Survey began collecting broader earth-science data at Devils Hole. A central goal is to estimate the isolation time of the pupfish using [[Uranium Series Dating]] and other techniques. Many of these data can only be obtained by [[SCUBA Diving]]. The underwater surveys indicate that Devils Hole is a unique natural laboratory for hydrogeology, paleoclimatology, geochemistry, microbial geochemistry, and evolutionary biology.

Water temperatures recorded in November 1954 showed near-uniform values across the main chamber and [[Brown's Room]], ranging from about 33.2 to 34.0 °C at depths below the surface.

## Key Findings

The report organizes the chronology into a series of dives, surveys, and observations:

- **February and June 1950:** Early reconnaissance and diving activity in the fissure, recorded in the first entries of the chronology.
- **August 1953:** A three-man team led by William M. Brown from the [[National Speleological Society]] (NSS) made SCUBA dives in Devils Hole and nearby features. A depth of about 150 feet was reported during these dives, and the team noted the atmospheric and water-surface conditions and related support logistics.
- **November 1954:** Further dives and water-temperature measurements were made, with temperatures recorded in the main chamber and Brown's Room.
- **May 1960:** A team of eight divers, including NSS members, conducted a four-day reconnaissance, including photographic and geological work and a survey of underwater features and water movement.
- **February 1961:** A survey team surveyed Devils Hole Cave and Devils Hole, including photographic documentation of subterranean chambers, and estimated the depth of the cave passages.
- **April 1961:** A follow-up diving expedition was conducted.
- **January 1964:** [[James Houtz]] led a team of divers on a photographic expedition, and the chronology notes a magazine account (Jacobs, 1964) describing a descent to 284 feet.
- **June 1965:** Two divers went missing in Devils Hole. A search involved 44 searchers making round-the-clock dives over three days, mostly at depths not exceeding 200 feet for safety reasons. Houtz reportedly descended to 315 feet and reported seeing 30–40 additional feet below without reaching bottom. No trace of the missing divers was found.
- **August 1966:** A handwritten note on a water-sample analysis states that the sample was collected by a skindiver 180 feet below the water surface, possibly in Devils Hole Cave. The author could not verify this feat, and the diver remains unidentified.
- **April 1972:** The first official [[Pupfish Census]] using SCUBA was conducted by William Acree and Howard Dimont (National Park Service) and Charles Osborn (U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife). They counted 38 fish at depths of 10 to 70 feet and reported that the fish appeared at ease, even curious, in the presence of divers.
- **June 1972:** A two-day geological field trip commissioned by the [[Desert Research Institute]] examined the main chamber to 30 feet. Divers found no obvious evidence of water levels lower than the 1972 level (Mehringer and Haynes, 1972).

The chronology concludes with an epilogue and a reference list of about 15 citations, including works on the pupfish's diet (Minckley and Deacon, 1975), its population dynamics (Chernoff), and irrigation impacts (Dudley and Larson, 1976). The report's main value is as a historical baseline for interpreting the 1969–1985 water-level cycle and for planning future diving and isotopic research on the pupfish's isolation history.
