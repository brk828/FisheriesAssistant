# Trophic Interrelations Among Introduced Fishes of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study, by W. L. Minckley of the Arizona State University Department of Zoology, examines food habits and trophic structure among the introduced fishes of the mainstream [[Lower Colorado River]] in the southwestern United States. Stomach analyses were conducted as part of an ecological survey from 1974 through 1976. The document finds that the introduced fish fauna depends largely on [[Detritus]] and primary production, with [[Chironomid Larvae]] and other benthic invertebrates as major secondary foods. [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Red Shiner]] are the principal forage fishes, and [[Asiatic Clam]] and crayfish are important prey for larger species. The author concludes that the introduced fish community has relatively simple food interrelations, and that its food web rests on autochthonous rather than terrestrial inputs.

## Background & Description

The river is one of the most highly modified channels in western North America. Its flows are essentially under control of mainstream and tributary impoundments, and its native fish fauna, though highly endemic, has been reduced to about nine species in the mainstream. An exotic fauna is becoming established. Earlier work included faunal surveys, fishery summaries, identification keys and reviews of species introductions, but published data on food relations were limited. This study outlines the trophic structure of the introduced fishes and also summarizes information on [[Striped Bass]] food habits.

The study area extends from [[Davis Dam]] to the International Boundary, a reach of 453 km. Historically, the only perennial tributaries entering this reach were the Gila and Williams rivers, and these are now largely dammed or diverted. Flow is maintained by return flows from agriculture and domestic wastewater. Local precipitation seldom exceeds 12 cm per year, summer air temperatures often exceed 40 °C, and winter temperatures rarely drop below freezing. The channel ranges from steep, eroded canyons in the north to broad floodplains and terraces in the south.

Sampling used gill, trammel and hoop nets, and stomachs were preserved and examined in the laboratory. Food items were assessed by frequency of occurrence and volume. The author notes that frequency of occurrence tends to under-represent highly digestible prey, such as soft-bodied invertebrates, which are digested rapidly.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food web basis.** Detritus formed the dominant food category for many fishes, consisting of fine organic matter with associated bacteria, algae and small invertebrates. The river's food web is described as based on autochthonous materials, in contrast to many large rivers that rely on terrestrial inputs. In the Missouri River, for example, 54% of ingested material was of terrestrial origin. The sparse vegetation of the Colorado River basin and the narrow, water-limited riparian zone reduce allochthonous inputs.

**Dams and reservoirs.** Impoundments trap much of the organic and inorganic load, but plankton and suspended debris pass through penstocks and sustain downstream production. [[Lake Mohave]] supplies plankton and detritus through [[Davis Dam]], while cold hypolimnetic releases exclude many temperate and tropical species from the reach just below the dam. In [[Lake Havasu]], fish distribution tracked food supply: planktivorous threadfin shad concentrated near nutrient inputs, and piscivorous largemouth bass were found near them. Warm epilimnetic releases from Parker Dam enhance habitat for warmwater fishes downstream.

**Backwaters and productivity.** The few remaining backwaters are highly productive, supporting plankton, rooted vegetation and fishes, and these are flushed into the channel by hydroelectric and irrigation-related fluctuations. In-stream productivity is locally high because of strong insolation and nutrient additions from irrigation return flow.

**Temperature and distribution limits.** Upstream temperature limits (too cold in winter or near Davis Dam) and the distance from the sea, together with intervening barriers, constrain the distribution of detritivores such as sailfin molly, mouthbrooder and [[Striped Mullet]] more than food availability does.

## Key Findings

**Overall food habits**
- Detritus was the major food for many species, and [[Detritivory]] became a major feeding mode in the lowermost reaches.
- Benthic insects, mainly [[Chironomid Larvae]] (Chironomidae), comprised major parts of the diet of all species present. Ephemeropteran, trichopteran and simuliid larvae were also common.
- Threadfin shad and red shiner, the lowest trophic-level fishes, were the most abundant species in the river and were eaten by other fishes most frequently.
- Empty stomachs were absent among detritivores and facultative planktivores. Species with broader food bases had low empty-stomach percentages. Large piscivores had high empty-stomach rates, with [[Flathead Catfish]] at 39.1%. [[Striped Bass]] had 45% empty stomachs in 100 adults (Edwards 1974). Smallmouth bass, warmouth and green sunfish each exceeded 20%. [[Largemouth Bass]] had a broad diet, with only 11.6% of stomachs empty.

**Predator and prey relationships**
- Largemouth and smallmouth bass fed heavily on ephemeropteran nymphs, megalopteran larvae and odonate naiads. [[Bluegill]] relied strongly on chironomids together with cladocerans, and [[Green Sunfish]] fed opportunistically on small carp and unidentified fishes.
- [[Asiatic Clam]] was eaten by carp, channel catfish, yellow bullhead, redear sunfish, largemouth bass and mouthbrooder. Redear sunfish crush shells with pharyngeal teeth, and clam shells passed intact through other species' guts. Clams eaten by carp and channel catfish exceeded 2.5 cm across the valves, but most were under 1.0 cm.
- Introduced [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] was a major food of almost all large carnivores, especially catfish and smallmouth bass. Crayfish were also found in striped bass stomachs.
- [[Threadfin Shad]] was the main prey of striped bass and was the only fish eaten by rainbow trout. It was also consumed by largemouth bass and [[Channel Catfish]].
- [[Red Shiner]] was eaten by flathead catfish, warmouth, black crappie and green sunfish.
- Other prey included the decapod shrimp *Palaemonetes paludosus*, Tiger Salamander (*Ambystoma tigrinum*, a common bait animal), and terrestrial insects in mouthbrooder stomachs.

**Spatial variation**
- Near Davis Dam, filter-feeding invertebrates such as simuliids dominated fish stomachs, reflecting plankton and detritus passing through the dam.
- Topock Gorge appeared relatively devoid of piscivore food, with red shiners rare and threadfin shad almost absent.
- Benthic invertebrates in Lake Havasu were locally dominated by Asiatic clams. Phytoplankton in the lower river was partly derived from clam pseudofeces.

**Conclusions**
- Food habits of introduced fishes in the Colorado River do not differ substantially from those of the same species in their native ranges (Calhoun 1966).
- Introduced forage species that succeeded in the mainstream depend on detritus, primary producers or secondary consumers such as zooplankton and chironomids. Large fishes feed mainly on detritus-dependent clams or crayfish, so the introduced fish fauna has relatively simple food interrelations.
- Many introduced forage species have high reproductive rates and form dense monospecific populations. These populations may explode in transitory habitats before stabilizing at low levels or disappearing.
