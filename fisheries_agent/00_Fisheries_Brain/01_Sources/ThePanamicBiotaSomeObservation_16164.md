# Panamic Biota: Observations Prior to a Sea-Level Canal

#ichthyology #marine-biology #coral-reef-ecology

## Executive Summary

This 1972 symposium volume of the [[Biological Society of Washington]] (Bulletin No. 2, edited by Meredith L. Jones) compiles the state of knowledge on the marine and terrestrial biota of Panama ahead of a proposed [[Sea-Level Canal]]. The contributions cover canal history and engineering, hydrography, coastal ecology, corals, molluscs, crustaceans, sea grasses and algae, fishes, and museum data management. The central theme is that the biota of the Caribbean and Eastern Pacific sides of the isthmus are poorly documented, so the biological consequences of joining the oceans cannot yet be predicted reliably. Several authors argue for baseline surveys before construction, while differing on the degree of biological risk.

## Background & Description

The volume was produced by a symposium sponsored by the Biological Society of Washington, The Conservation Foundation, and the [[National Museum of Natural History]] (Smithsonian Institution). The Introduction by Meredith L. Jones frames the problem: the Central American isthmus separated marine faunas for one to five million years, producing likely geminate species pairs, and the 1914 [[Panama Canal]] added a freshwater barrier for terrestrial forms and a partial barrier for marine forms.

Key contributions include:
- **Background for a New, Sea-Level Panama Canal** (David Challinor): political and engineering history, the 1970 Atlantic-Pacific Interoceanic Canal Study Commission, and treaty disputes over sovereignty.
- **Ecology of the Caribbean and Pacific Coasts** (Peter W. Glynn): contrasts in climate, upwelling, tides, reefs, and communities.
- **Physical Characteristics** (John P. Sheffey) and **Exchange of Water** (Donald R. F. Harleman): tidal range, currents, and net water transfer.
- **Museums as Environmental Data Banks** (Richard S. Cowan): curatorial and data-management issues.
- **Marine Plants** (Sylvia A. Earle), **Coral Reefs** (James W. Porter), **Molluscan Origins** (Axel A. Olsson), and **Decapod Crustacea** (Lawrence G. Abele).
- Companion reviews on echinoids, coastal fishes (C. Richard Robins), marine fisheries, terrestrial plants, insects, herpetology, birds, mammals, and anthropology.

Tributes to [[Waldo Lasalle Schmitt]] (crustacean systematist and Antarctic collector) open the volume.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and hydrography.** Both coasts share a seasonal ITCZ-driven regime, but the Pacific side has strong dry-season upwelling in the Gulf of Panama, with sea surface temperature dropping from 27°C to as low as 15°C within 24 hours in parts of the Gulf. The Caribbean shelf stays stratified and is more exposed to high seas and rainfall. Caribbean tides are small (under 0.5 m diurnal range in Glynn's account; about 0.7 m absolute range at Cristóbal per Porter) while Balboa's maximum daily range reaches 6.3 m.

**Biotic contrasts.** Porter and Glynn describe a fundamental difference: the Caribbean has abundant reef-building corals, sea-grass beds (Thalassia testudinum), and mangroves with epibiota, whereas the Pacific has lava rock coasts, sparse reefs dominated by Pocillopora, and little sea grass. Fish predation on corals and sponges is markedly heavier on Pacific shores.

**Introduced or range-extended species.** The crown-of-thorns starfish *Acanthaster* is an important coral predator in southwestern Panama, and the sea snake *Pelamis platurus* approaches shore with drift lines. Several Indo-Pacific reef fishes were newly recorded from the eastern Pacific.

**Transisthmian corridors.** The Third Lock Lagoon has been colonized by Atlantic gobiid and blenniid fishes. Fouling studies found that only a small fraction of isopod and bryozoan species are shared between oceans.

## Key Findings

- **Canal engineering (Challinor, Sheffey).** Route 10, about ten miles west of the existing canal, was chosen by the 1970 Commission for conventional excavation. The Commission spent about $22 million, of which $17.5 million went to nuclear route evaluation and about $3 million to conventional routes. Sheffey reports a Pacific mean sea level about one foot higher than the Atlantic, and Pacific tidal range up to about 20 feet at Balboa versus about two feet on the Caribbean side.
- **Water exchange (Harleman).** Modelling of 88 cases gives a mean net transport of roughly 40,000 cubic feet per second from Pacific to Atlantic for the 35-mile, 600 ft × 60 ft canal considered. Organisms entering from the Pacific would take about two and a half days to transit. Tidal gates could largely eliminate interocean flow.
- **Biogeographic contrast (Glynn).** The Caribbean has 49 hermatypic corals versus 20 on the Pacific coast; 14 of the Pacific species occur at single localities in southwestern Panama. A Caribbean Millepora-bearing reef was found in the Contreras–Secas area of the Pacific.
- **Baseline needs (Glynn, Jones, Cowan).** Authors agree that Panama's biota is inadequately inventoried and that seasonal, long-term ecological studies are required.
- **Faunal risk (Voss, Olsson).** Voss et al. (Miami Survey) analysed 1,878 species from the 1966–1967 cruises and concluded there was no valid biological reason to oppose a conventional sea-level canal, since roughly 50% of species are similar or identical across the two sides. They recommended a low-salinity/thermal barrier, and the Miami group reported that the NAS committee omitted their research. Olsson concludes that the Pacific mollusc fauna is about five times richer than the Caribbean (about 4,500 Pacific Pelecypoda and Gastropoda), and that molluscan damage seems negligible, with the greatest risk from Indo-Pacific invaders.
- **Corals (Porter).** Shannon diversity on San Blas reef transects peaks near 3.2 at about 5 m and falls off by 25 m, with species count the best single predictor of diversity (r = 0.9028). Maximum Pacific values approach only 1.81. Porter urges experiments on competition between Indo-Pacific and Caribbean corals, and flags *Acanthaster* as a serious potential threat.
- **Marine plants (Earle).** About 195 species are recorded from Panama, including 125 from the Atlantic and 90 from the Pacific, with 20 shared. Earle notes the Atlantic flora is more abundant and diverse, though this may partly reflect sampling bias.
- **Decapods (Abele).** Undescribed species are common; about 20% of Caribbean shrimps from recent expeditions were undescribed. Habitat similarity indices range from 25% (sandy beach) to 60% (freshwater stream) for Pacific-Caribbean comparisons.
- **Fouling and ballast (Glynn, Cowan).** Shipping transfer already occurs through the existing canal; the authors argue the new canal would expand rather than create such exchanges.
- **Data management (Cowan).** The Steere Report estimates a 40% increase in systematic collection space (3.5 to 5 million square feet) and at least 100 new professional researchers, and Cowan advocates computerized biological data banks as baselines for detecting change.

Overall, the volume concludes that a sea-level canal's biological risk cannot be assessed reliably without substantial baseline surveys, while the contributors disagree on the level of concern.
