# The Improvement of Trout Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trout

## Executive Summary

This early article by [[Carl L Hubbs]], written for *American Forests*, argues that trout fishing in Michigan can be increased by improving stream habitat, not only by introducing trout, protecting them, and stocking hatcheries. The author sets out four means of improving trout waters: introduction of non-native trout, protection during breeding and in nursery waters, hatchery and rearing-station stocking, and environmental control of the stream itself. He argues that only the last can increase the supply beyond maintaining current levels. The work described was carried out by the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]] at the [[University Of Michigan]] with the [[Michigan Department Of Conservation]]. The article is the first of two parts; the second, by [[John R Greeley]] and [[Clarence M Tarzwell]], was to give fuller methods and results.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Reduce the distance anglers walk between productive pools, summarized as "less time between bites."
- **Four methods of trout improvement:**
  1. Introduction of trout to waters where they are not native. Michigan's best-known brook trout streams (e.g., [[Pere Marquette River]], [[Manistee River]], [[Au Sable River]]) reportedly had no trout until introductions in the 1870s and 1880s. Rainbow trout (native to the Pacific Coast) and brown trout (native to Europe) were also introduced. The author considers this method largely exhausted in Michigan.
  2. Protection during the breeding season and in nursery waters, plus regulation to prevent serious depletion.
  3. Hatchery and rearing-station stocking.
  4. Environmental control: providing adequate spawning beds, shelter, and food. The author calls this the method most truly "improvement of trout streams."
- **Key habitat requirements:** adequate spawning gravel, sufficient cover, and sufficient food.
- **Lessons from elsewhere:** British stream management is valuable but differs from Michigan. British streams are mostly under intensive private control, and the British climate allows more sun exposure than Michigan streams can safely tolerate. Methods from New York's Catskill streams (described in Mr. Hewitt's *Better Trout Streams*) must be adapted before use in Michigan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Human impacts:** Forest cutting and agriculture have warmed streams and washed sand over food-producing gravel. Streams were cleared of snags to float logs or pulpwood, and some were stripped of cover because people thought it improved appearance.
- **Temperature:** High water temperature is identified as a major climate-related hazard for Michigan streams.
- **Sand and gravel:** Trout food grows poorly on soft sand but abundantly on gravel. Sand also smothers spawning beds.
- **Cover and food:** Log and brush shelters support insect hatches. Weed beds provide both food and shelter, and can be increased where scarce.
- **Regional value:** The author links better trout fishing to health, happiness, and income in northern Michigan, whose economy depends on "pure air, open spaces, and wild life."

## Key Findings

- **Environmental control is the only method that can grow the trout supply** beyond what conservation and stocking maintain. Stocking and protection cannot by themselves produce fish.
- **Large-scale work began in 1930** on the [[Little Manistee River]], likely the first large-scale improvement on any public waters in the country. A crew of three log-handling men worked with conservation officers, and the [[Institute For Fisheries Research]] planned and studied the work.
- **In 1931**, the Department's [[Pigeon River Forest Project]] engaged the Institute for similar work. Two privately funded projects on controlled streams were also carried out, and some experimental devices were built directly by the Institute in publicly fished waters.
- **Scale of observation:** 875 numbered improvement devices were under observation across six streams: Little Manistee River (Lake County), Pigeon River (Otsego and Cheboygan counties), East Branch of the Black River (Montmorency County), Gamble Creek (Ogemaw County), Huron River (Washtenaw County), and River Rouge (Wayne County). All except the Huron River are trout waters.
- **Spawning gravel:** Gravel was created by hauling it into gravel-poor feeder streams, and by using log and stone structures to speed the current, wash out sand, and expose the original gravel. Trout were already spawning on the exposed gravel.
- **Cover and pools:** Log deflectors on a previously troutless bend of the Little Manistee River (improved in 1930) dug holes that trout used. Good catches were made there in spring 1931. The author states the approach has been general and that hundreds of miles of troutless or nearly troutless stream in Michigan could be made habitable.
- **Stone and log structures:** Because stone is used as well as logs, the term "re-snagging" was judged inadequate. [[Dr Jan Metzelaar]] began log re-snagging studies earlier, and his log shelters created good pools that stayed in place.
- **Methods to be published:** Detailed designs were to appear in the Institute bulletin *Methods for the Improvement of Michigan Trout Streams*.
- **Conclusion:** The author states that trout production can be increased at low cost, in a lasting way, and that nearly all Michigan streams could be made more productive if properly improved.
