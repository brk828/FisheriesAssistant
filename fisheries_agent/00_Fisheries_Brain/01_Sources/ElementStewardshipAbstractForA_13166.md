# Element Stewardship Abstract: Longfin Dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #freshwater-fish #desert-fish #Southwestern-US

## Executive Summary

The [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) is a small, hardy cyprinid minnow native to the Gila River basin and associated Sonoran Desert drainages of Arizona, New Mexico, and Mexico. Renowned as one of the most adaptable native fishes of the American Southwest, it persists in extreme desert stream conditions—including intermittent flow, high temperatures, and drought—better than most co-occurring native and non-native species. Its life history includes nest-building reproduction in sand/gravel substrates, near year-round spawning capability, and rapid recolonization following flooding or drying events. Primary threats stem from habitat alteration (impoundment, diversion, groundwater pumping, channelization) and introduced predatory and competitive fish species. Conservation of natural flow regimes and permanent-flow stream reaches is identified as essential to the species' persistence.

## Background & Description

*Agosia chrysogaster* (family [[Cyprinidae]]) is a small minnow characterized by:

- **Body form:** Fusiform with a thick, blunted head
- **Fin ray counts:** 9 dorsal rays, 16–17 pectoral rays, 7–9 pelvic rays, 8 anal rays
- **Sexual dimorphism:** Males have relatively longer fins overall; females have a noticeably longer anal fin. In males, the pelvic fin tip overlaps the anal fin origin—a reliable field character for sexing
- **Scales:** Small, 75–90 along the lateral line
- **Mouth:** Subterminal, upper jaw extends past the lower; small paired barbels present on the upper lip
- **Pharyngeal teeth:** Single row, 4-4 formula
- **Coloration:** Dark silvery to olive-gray dorsum, white ventrum, often with a dark lateral band terminating in a caudal spot; gold flecking sometimes present
- **Breeding males:** Develop yellow ventral and paired-fin coloration and extensive tuberculation on head, body, and fins (occasionally giving a "frosty" appearance). Kepner (1982) found 1.5% of 780 females also exhibited restricted head tuberculation

### Species Differentiation

Longfin dace can be confused with the introduced [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*) and native [[Gila Chub|Gila sp.]] chubs, but are distinguished by their small subterminal mouth, small barbels, and a triangular dorsal fin lacking a dark spot near the leading edge. Fathead minnows have fewer (<50) and larger lateral line scales, plus crowded nape scales.

### Taxonomic Notes

- Woodman (1987) proposed subsuming *Agosia* into *[[Rhinichthys]]* based on muscle/bone morphology; this reclassification remains unadopted pending molecular verification.
- Hendrickson (1987) identified two morphs:
  - **Northern morph:** Bill Williams, Gila, Sonoyta, and de la Concepción basins (AZ, NM, Sonora)
  - **Southern morph:** Willcox Playa (AZ) and Rios Sonora, Yaqui, Mayo, Fuerte, Sinaloa (Sonora/Sinaloa, Mexico)
  - Southern morph is smaller-bodied with longer pre/postdorsal lengths; males differ more between morphs than females; southern males lack extreme tuberculation and no nests have been documented in their range.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Distribution & Habitat

Native range spans the **Gila River basin** of Arizona and New Mexico, with populations in the Yaqui, Magdalena, Sonoyta, Gila, and Bill Williams drainages. Introduced populations occur in the Virgin River and Hualapai drainages (NV/AZ) and portions of the Rio Grande, Mimbres River, Rio Hondo, and Largo Creek (NM). Mexican range includes the Rio Yaqui, Rio Concepción, Rio Sonoyta, south to Rio Sinaloa.

- **Elevation range:** Up to ~1500 m, though typically low, hot, sandy desert streams
- **Microhabitat:** Nests built along stream margins or backwater mouths in areas of slow flow (mean 0.07 m/s); average nest depth 8.5 cm (range 2.9–17.9 cm) at mean water temperature 23.4°C (16.8–29.7°C) (Kepner 1982, Aravaipa Creek)
- **Depth/velocity preferences (Hardy et al. 1990):** Modal depth for adults/juveniles ~18.3 cm; modal velocities 30.5 cm/s (adults) and 6.1 cm/s (juveniles)
- **Drought survival:** Persists under algal mats during stream drying; occupies residual pools and rapidly recolonizes as flows resume
- **Flood response:** Moves into current, then laterally as flow increases; rarely trapped in flood pools/backwaters
- **Thermal tolerance:** Withstands 28–33°C
- Notably **avoids springs and headwaters** in some systems (e.g., Sonoita Creek basin)

### Associated Native Fish Community

Co-occurs with [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), and [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*). Historically associated with now-extirpated (from the Gila basin) [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Colorado Pikeminnow|Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*).

### Interspecific Interactions

- Increasing longfin dace abundance associated with declining [[Speckled Dace]] populations at intermediate elevations (competitive displacement, LaBounty & Minckley 1972)
- Inverse relationship between longfin dace abundance and both other native species abundance and stream flow rate (Minckley data in Hardy et al. 1990)
- Uniquely among native minnows, longfin dace maintains high populations alongside the invasive [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*)

### Predation & Parasitism

- Predation by kingfishers and herons, facilitated by the species' tendency to occupy open shallow water
- Juveniles/larvae can become fatally entangled in the water-net alga *Hydrodictyon*
- Susceptible to sunburn in shallow, clear, drying streams
- Parasite infestations documented by Mpoame (1982) and Mpoame & Rinne (1983) in Aravaipa Creek; *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* recorded as a mortality source (Lewis 1978)

## Key Findings

### Reproduction

- **Age/size at first spawning:** ~12 months, 32–45 mm SL
- **Sex ratio:** Approximately 1:1 (female:male)
- **Nest construction:** Saucer-shaped conical nests in sand-to-coarse gravel (0.063–31 mm grain diameter)
  - Aravaipa Creek: mean diameter 13.5 cm, depth 2.3 cm (Kepner 1982)
  - Bonita Creek: mean depth 3.7 cm, diameter 20.3 cm (N=85; Vives unpubl.)
  - Nest densities up to 50/m²
- **Spawning behavior:** Non-territorial tuberculate males patrol nest areas; 1–4 males flank/follow an entering female; spawning obscured by a sand "flurry"; described in detail by Minckley (1973)
- **Fecundity-length relationships:**
  - Lewis (1978): Log fecundity = -0.4961 + 3.693 Log SL (r=0.87, N=43)
  - Kepner (1982): Fecundity = -507.603 + 11.986(SL) (r=0.51, N=41); also related to weight: Fecundity = -94.739 + 77.469(weight)
- **Egg characteristics:** Mean nest egg count 87.1 (Bonita Creek, N=19 nests); egg diameter ~3.5 mm; mature ova 1.4–2.0 mm diameter
- **Spawning season:** Extended, December–July/September (Minckley 1973) to January–November (Lewis 1978; Constantz 1981); peak reproductive condition in February with secondary September peak (Aravaipa Creek); potentially year-round given suitable temperature/flow
- **Flood-cued spawning:** Flooding after low-flow periods may trigger new breeding peaks, paralleling patterns in [[Speckled Dace]]
- **Incubation:** Hatching in <4 days at temperatures >24°C

### Growth

- Fry averaging 5.2 mm SL in February reach 35.1 mm SL by August (same year)
- Weight-length relationship: log W(g) = -2.116 + 3.341 Log SL (r=0.98) (Lewis 1978)
- Fish >3 years or >100 mm SL are rare
- Adults (12–24 months): mean 3.3 g, 51.1 mm SL
- Largest recorded specimen: male, 10.4 g, 83.2 mm SL

### Diet

- **Adults:** Primarily detritus and algae; also zooplankton and aquatic insects
- **Juveniles:** Microscopic crustaceans, detritus, algae
- **Diurnal feeders** (contrasting with crepuscular feeding by co-occurring Gila mountain sucker; Fisher et al. 1981)
- Diet dominated by *Cladophora glomerata* algal mats/epiphytes and associated invertebrates (Sycamore Creek study)
- In Aravaipa Creek, primary food was ephemeropteran nymphs; 38 other invertebrate taxa recorded; algal proportion of diet ranged 9.8–53% (peak in July)

### Threats

- Habitat loss/alteration: impoundment, water diversion, groundwater pumping, channelization, unnatural flow stabilization
- Pollution: domestic sewage, pesticide contamination
- Invasive species impacts, particularly from stocked gamefish near reservoirs (depletes all native fish, LaBounty & Minckley 1972)
- Documented extirpation: Quitobaquito Spring population lost following pond draining/rotenone treatment for invasive [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*) removal
- Natural flow regime disruption reduces the species' competitive advantage over non-natives and may interfere with flood-cued breeding (Meffe & Minckley 1987; Minckley & Meffe 1987)

### Conservation & Land Protection Recommendations

- Maintenance of low-elevation desert stream reaches with at least some permanent, unpolluted flow is essential
- Habitat should be kept free of introduced predators, notably [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) and black basses (*Micropterus* spp.)

### Research Needs (Identified 1990)

1. Taxonomic clarification of *Agosia*'s relationship to other cyprinid groups (molecular verification of Woodman's 1987 *Rhinichthys* hypothesis)
2. Direct observation of nest-building and spawning behavior (never directly observed as of this abstract)
3. Identification of environmental/physiological spawning triggers
4. Quantification of dispersal capabilities
5. Larval biology studies
6. Characterization of interactions with introduced species, especially [[Red Shiner]]
7. Quantification of physical habitat modification effects on life cycle completion
