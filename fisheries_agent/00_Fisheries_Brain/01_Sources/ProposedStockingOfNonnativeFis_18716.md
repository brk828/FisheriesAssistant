## Executive Summary

This 1992 Utah Division of Wildlife Resources proposal outlines a plan to introduce sterile or non-reproducing hybrid game fish — [[Hybrid Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis* x *M. chrysops*) and [[Tiger Musky]] (*Esox masquinongy* x *E. lucius*) — into **Mill Meadow Reservoir** and **Forsyth Reservoir** in the headwaters of the Fremont River, Utah. The primary objective is disease control, not sport fishery enhancement: these predatory hybrids are intended to suppress reinvasion of salmonids (and thus interrupt the life cycle of [[Whirling Disease]], caused by the myxosporean parasite *Myxobolus cerebralis*) following rotenone eradication treatments conducted in 1991–1992. A secondary goal is to preserve limited sport fishing opportunity during the multi-year disease containment period. The project is explicitly temporary, with plans to eventually restore native [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] and mottled sculpin populations once whirling disease is controlled or eradicated.

## Background & Description

Data on the introduced hybrids and forage species focus on stocking size and reproductive biology rather than detailed morphology:

- **Hybrid Striped Bass**: Stocked as 2-inch fingerlings; cross of striped bass (*Morone saxatilis*) x white bass (*M. chrysops*, historically *Roccus chrysops*).
- **Tiger Musky**: Stocked as 3-inch fingerlings; cross of muskellunge (*Esox masquinongy*) x northern pike (*E. lucius*).
- **Reproductive sterility/limitations**:
  - Tiger musky are functionally sterile in the wild — males are largely incapable of producing viable sperm (Beyerle 1973), and induced spawning in captivity has met with high fry mortality (Black & Williamson 1947; Eddy 1944 held females 4 years before egg production, still without natural spawning).
  - Hybrid striped bass sterility is less absolute. Successful backcrossing and F2 production have been documented in some reservoirs (Cherokee Lake, TN; Lake Palestine, TX), often requiring hormone induction (HCG) for spawning success (Smith & Jenkins 1984), with lower survival/hatch rates in F2 generations. Wild F2 hybrids without backcrossing were found in Lake Palestine, TX (Forshage et al. 1986).
- **Native species affected by whirling disease**: Rainbow trout (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) examined during rotenone treatments showed skeletal deformities characteristic of whirling disease in **23% (~1/4) of individuals**.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainage overview**: The Fremont River (upper Colorado River Basin, via Dirty Devil River → Lake Powell) originates near Fish Lake (8,843 ft) and Johnson Reservoir (8,819 ft), flows through Mill Meadow Reservoir (7,681 ft) and Forsyth Reservoir (7,989 ft), then through agricultural lands, a narrow canyon, [[Capitol Reef National Park]], and eastward to the Dirty Devil River near Hanksville before entering Lake Powell.

**Native fish community (pre-disturbance)**:
- Above Mill Meadow Reservoir: historically only [[Mottled Sculpin]] and [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] (Sigler 1953).
- Near Capitol Reef National Park: [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], and mottled sculpin (absent below Sulphur Creek confluence).
- Downstream of the park: native [[Roundtail Chub]] also present (McAda et al. 1978).

**Nonnative fish community (established)**:
- Reservoirs/Fish Lake: [[Utah Chub]], [[Utah Sucker]], [[Redside Shiner]], [[Yellow Perch]] (illegally introduced to Fish Lake pre-1965), and recently detected [[Common Carp]].
- Mid-river reaches: rainbow trout, brown trout (*Salmo trutta*), brook trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*), introduced cutthroat trout (*O. clarki bouvieri*), lake trout (*Salvelinus namaycush*), and hybrid splake.
- Downstream of Capitol Reef: nonnative [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Red Shiner]] (McAda et al. 1978), though red shiner has not been detected in recent surveys.

**Isolation/barriers relevant to risk assessment**:
- No direct hydrological connection between Mill Meadow Reservoir and the lower Fremont River — 7 miles permanently dewatered year-round, plus an additional 5 miles seasonally intermittent, effectively isolating upstream reservoirs from Colorado River mainstem habitats.
- The Fremont/Dirty Devil drainage holds **no listed threatened or endangered fish**; the nearest documented federally listed species (Colorado Pikeminnow, Razorback Sucker) occur >150 miles downstream near the Dirty Devil–Lake Powell confluence, separated by Lake Powell itself.
- Environmental extremes (flow ranging ~10 cfs to >8,800 cfs; temperatures from 30s°F winter to 80s°F summer; high turbidity variability) are considered unfavorable for establishment of Esocids and Percichthyids, which typically favor lentic or large-river habitats.
- Notably, even well-adapted local nonnatives (Utah chub, Utah sucker) and invasive species common elsewhere in the Colorado River Basin (sunfish, catfish, red shiner) have failed to establish within Capitol Reef National Park despite open water connectivity — used as evidence for low invasion risk downstream.

**Category 2 (candidate) species** documented in the lower drainage: [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Roundtail Chub]], located several miles downstream of the proposed stocking sites, separated by the permanently dewatered reach.

**Herptiles**: [[Boreal Toad]] (*Bufo boreas*), a candidate species, historically documented near Bicknell, Fruita, and Loa; population status survey planned for spring 1993.

## Key Findings

- **Disease-driven stocking rationale**: This is a biological control strategy — hybrid predators are stocked specifically to prevent salmonid reestablishment (via predation on trout attempting to recolonize) and thereby interrupt the *Myxobolus cerebralis* life cycle, rather than as a conventional sport-fishery enhancement.
- **Stocking quantities/timing**: 2,000 two-inch hybrid striped bass and 200 three-inch tiger musky planned for **each** reservoir (Mill Meadow and Forsyth) in July 1993, repeated in July 1994 — approximately 10 hybrid bass/acre and 1 tiger musky/acre.
- **Forage base**: Locally occurring nonnative Utah chub, redside shiner, and yellow perch (already present in Fish Lake headwaters and unable to be excluded) will serve as forage, with stocking planned for 1993 following 1992 rotenone renovation.
- **Reproductive containment as safeguard**: Tiger musky are essentially non-reproductive in the wild; hybrid striped bass reproduction, while documented in a few U.S. reservoirs under specific conditions, has not been shown sufficient to sustain wild populations — but project success does **not depend** on sterility, only benefits from it as an added safeguard.
- **Hydrological isolation as primary risk mitigation**: Permanent and seasonal dewatering below Mill Meadow Reservoir functions as a physical barrier preventing downstream escapement into occupied habitat of category 2 and listed species.
- **Precedent of failed establishment**: Parental species (striped bass, northern pike) already exist in Lake Powell and the Green/Colorado Rivers without evidence of significant reproductive success or ecological disruption beyond current baseline, suggesting the hybrids pose no meaningfully greater incremental risk to listed species such as [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]].
- **Active monitoring plan**: Annual rotenone treatments (1993–1994) between Mill Meadow Reservoir and Capitol Reef National Park will double as an escapement monitoring/control mechanism — crews will document any hybrid fish killed during these treatments.
- **Planned endpoint**: The stocking program is explicitly temporary; by 1996 the program's effectiveness at excluding salmonids will be evaluated, informing a transition back to native/sport trout fisheries, including reintroduction of Colorado River Cutthroat Trout into UM Creek and possibly Forsyth Reservoir. No long-term stocking of hybrid striped bass or tiger musky is planned beyond the whirling disease control period.
- **No impact to federally listed species** is anticipated given geographic isolation (>150 miles from nearest listed fish populations) and lack of direct hydrological connectivity outside of Lake Powell.
