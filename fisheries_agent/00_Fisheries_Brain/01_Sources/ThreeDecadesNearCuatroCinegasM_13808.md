# Three Decades Near Cuatro Ciénegas, Mexico: Photographic Documentation and a Plea for Area Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[W. L. Minckley]] compares paired photographs taken in 1960 with rephotographs from 1988–89 and 1991 to document ecological change in the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], an intermontane desert valley in Coahuila, Mexico. The basin floor and remote mountain slopes changed little, while human-influenced habitats changed considerably. Aquatic habitats were degraded by [[Canalization]], declining local water tables and intensified [[Recreational Use]]. The author argues that the basin's endemic biota retain a high degree of integrity and calls for national and international conservation action.

## Background & Description

The Cuatro Ciénegas basin is a spring-fed, saline desert valley of roughly 1200 km² (about 30 by 40 km), surrounded by limestone mountains rising above 2500 m. Most surface water rises as springs near the mountains and evaporates in saline lakes on the valley floor. Individual springs range from seeps to discharges above 1.0 m³·sec⁻¹, and at least 80 springs flowing more than 4.0 L·sec⁻¹ have been visited.

Vegetation grades from a [[Saltgrass-Bunchgrass Association]] on the basin floor, through [[Chihuahuan Desertscrub]] on bajadas, to chaparral, woodland and coniferous forest on the highest peaks. The biota is highly endemic and includes more than 50 taxa of plants, invertebrates, fishes and reptiles known nowhere else. The basin was first studied by biologists in the late 1930s, and its diversity was only fully recognized after sampling resumed about 20 years later.

The author, [[Charles B. Stone]], took the original 1960 photographs during an August field trip. Rephotography relied on trial-and-error alignment with distant peaks rather than surveyed positions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Human land use, 1960 versus 1988–91:**
- In 1960, the basin had about 2500 residents, with firewood cooking, no electricity, and gravity-fed water. By 1988–89, Cuatro Ciénegas town had more than 15,000 inhabitants, with electricity, paved roads and propane.
- Irrigated agriculture at least tripled, and groundwater from five or more electric pumps now supplies it.
- Goat herds were greatly reduced, and mesquite ([[Prosopis]] spp.) expanded near settlements.
- A [[Gypsum Mining]] operation opened in the 1970s and depleted unconsolidated dunes in the southwestern lobe.

**Aquatic habitat change:**
- Canalization lowered spring surfaces, dried outlets and marshes, and invited terrestrial plant invasion. Former marshes at Pozos de la Becerra were reduced, and the surface there dropped about 2.0 m.
- Río Cañón's base flow fell from ~0.25 m³·sec⁻¹ in the 1960s to ~0.1 m³·sec⁻¹, which the author attributes largely to pumping and population growth.
- Canals connect previously isolated habitats, allowing biota to intermix, hybridize, and increasing the risk of exotic invasion.

**Stable areas:** Río Mesquites at Puente Orosco showed no detectable habitat change, with discharge estimated at 1.3–1.5 m³·sec⁻¹ across the 30-year period. Laguna Tío Candido changed little in extent.

**Persistence factors:** Basin-floor soil salinity, aridity and high total dissolved solids (magnesium sulfate and sodium chloride) have limited agriculture and large-scale pumping so far.

## Key Findings

- **Change is confined to human-affected habitats.** Basin floor and remote slopes remained much the same, while canalized springs, mined dunes and recreation sites changed markedly.
- **Aquatic losses are the most serious.** Canalization and pumping lowered water tables and reduced marsh area, and several formerly permanent marshes are now dry or only seasonally wet. Only a few springs were fully destroyed.
- **Recreation and over-harvest are degrading the system.** Bathing and fishing intensified after highway completion in the 1970s, and fishing success declined. Fishing and collection are now prohibited in parts of the basin.
- **Exceptional biodiversity.** The basin reportedly supports 15 fishes (12 endemic), four endemic reptiles, six crustaceans, at least 10 molluscs and more than 20 endemic plants. At least 64 amphibian and reptile species occur below 1000 m, more than a third of the 170 Chihuahuan Desert herpetofauna species in [[Morafka]]'s (1977) analysis.
- **Comparison with other desert sites.** [[Ash Meadows]] (five endemic fishes, one extinct) and [[Dalhousie Springs]] (six fishes, four endemic) have less aquatic diversity concentrated in one place.
- **Recommendations.** The author does not advocate a static preserve. He recommends maintaining natural fluctuation of springs and marshes, permanently established access roads, concentrated and managed recreation areas, and designating the basin as a long-term natural laboratory. He notes that the Mexican government has already moved to protect parts of the basin and urges that these efforts be expanded.
