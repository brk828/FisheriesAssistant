#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Swift (1965) investigates the effect of water temperature on egg mortality and developmental rate in the [[Windermere Char]] (*Salvelinus alpinus willughbii*), a landlocked salmonid found in Windermere, England. Eggs from both the autumn-spawning (shallow water) and spring-spawning (deep water) populations were incubated at temperatures ranging from 2–16°C. Results showed excessive mortality above 8°C, with total mortality above 12°C, and hatching time ranging from ~45 days at 10°C to ~95 days at 4°C. The findings suggest that while ambient lake temperatures do not limit adult char distribution, the low upper lethal temperature threshold of their eggs likely constrains the species' geographic range — contrasting with the author's earlier work (Swift, 1964) on adult thermal tolerance.

## Background & Description

- Species studied: *Salvelinus alpinus willughbii*, a subspecies/form of [[Arctic Char]] endemic to Windermere.
- Two reproductively distinct populations exist within Windermere, differentiated by spawning habitat and season rather than distinct morphology:
  - **Autumn-spawning population**: spawns in shallow water.
  - **Spring-spawning population**: spawns in deep water.
- Egg development stages tracked: fertilisation → blastopore closure period → embryonic development → hatching (alevin emergence).
- No morphological differences noted in hatching times or mortality patterns between the two populations at equivalent temperatures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Habitat: Windermere, a freshwater lake in Westmorland, England — represents the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] research context.
- Thermal ecology: Adult [[Windermere Char]] grow well between 12–16°C (per Swift, 1964), indicating naturally encountered lake temperatures do not limit adult geographic distribution.
- However, egg-stage thermal tolerance is far more restrictive: incubation above 8°C produces excessive mortality, and above 12°C mortality is total (or near-total, 3% survival in autumn-spawned eggs).
- This creates an ecological bottleneck: the **egg/incubation life stage**, not the adult stage, is likely the limiting factor for the species' geographic range — a decoupling between adult thermal tolerance and embryonic thermal tolerance.
- Comparable dual-population reproductive strategy (spring vs. autumn spawning within one water body) is an adaptive mechanism potentially reducing risk from variable thermal conditions, as noted by [[Frost 1951]].
- Comparative context: other salmonids studied (Ainsworth 1859 on American brook trout; Embody 1934 on brown trout, [[Lake Trout]], and rainbow trout; Hayes 1949 review) show similar temperature-dependent developmental patterns, situating the Windermere char within broader salmonid thermal biology.

## Key Findings

- **Mortality thresholds**: Excessive egg mortality occurred above 8°C; total mortality occurred above 12°C.
  - At 12°C: 0% hatch in spring-spawned eggs; only 3% hatch in autumn-spawned eggs.
  - At 8°C: total mortality only 15%, with most deaths occurring at hatching.
  - Under experimental conditions, no temperature above 8°C produced more than a 32% hatch rate.
- **Developmental rate**: Eggs took an average of 45 days to hatch at 10°C versus 95 days at 4°C — confirming the general teleost pattern of faster development in warmer water (within viable range).
- **Hatching time table** (days ± S.E. mean):

- **Mortality pattern**: Two distinct peaks of mortality identified:
  1. **Early stage** (5–15 days post-fertilisation, timing dependent on temperature) — likely due to asphyxiation from insufficient oxygen diffusion through the egg capsule, especially at higher temperatures (per [[Hayes 1949]]).
  2. **Hatching stage** — deaths at or immediately after hatching, attributed to **precocious hatching** driven by a disproportionately high temperature coefficient of the hatching mechanism (per [[Gray 1928]]), rather than oxygen deprivation.
  - At 4, 6, and 8°C, deaths occurred randomly throughout development (no distinct pattern).
  - At 14°C, all eggs died during the early developmental stage.
- **No inter-population difference**: Hatching times did not differ significantly between spring- and autumn-spawned eggs at equivalent temperatures.
- **Hatching period duration**: Unlike rainbow trout (Embody 1934), where hatching period duration varies strongly with temperature (14 days at 5.1°C vs. 3 days at 17.6°C), the Windermere char showed no strong temperature-dependence in hatching period duration, varying between 21–38 days across temperatures.
- **Ecological implication**: The low upper lethal temperature limit for char eggs (above 8–12°C) likely constrains the species' geographic distribution, even though adult thermal tolerance (12–16°C) does not — a key distinction from the conclusions of [[Swift 1964]].
- **Comparative salmonid context**: Two-stage mortality pattern parallels findings in [[Brown Trout]] (Steuert, 1906 — susceptibility to shock 10–17 days post-fertilisation) and [[Atlantic Salmon]] (Hayes & Armstrong, 1942 — mortality peaks at blastopore closure and hatching).
