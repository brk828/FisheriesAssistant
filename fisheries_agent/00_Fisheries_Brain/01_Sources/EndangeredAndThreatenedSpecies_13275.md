# Ash Meadows Species Recovery Plan (Agency Review Draft)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #endangered-species #recovery-plan

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) agency review draft sets out a recovery plan for twelve federally listed endangered and threatened species of [[Ash Meadows]], an isolated spring-fed oasis in the Mojave Desert straddling the Nevada–California border. The plan covers four fishes, eight plants and the naucorid bug. It states that the ecosystem as a whole, not single species, must be the recovery target, because the species share tightly linked habitats. The draft is explicitly not an approved document; goals depend on appropriations and review by cooperating agencies.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Ash Meadows lies at about 670 m (2,200 ft) elevation, roughly 64 km (40 mi) east of [[Death Valley National Monument]] headquarters and 90 mi northwest of Las Vegas. It covers about 20,500 ha (50,000 acres). Spring discharge sustains lowland soil moisture; uplands receive under 7 cm of rain per year, and annual evaporation exceeds 2.5 m.

**Origins and endemism.** The area is described as the sole habitat for roughly 25 unique plants and animals. Historically it held five distinct fishes, eight plant taxa, at least 12 aquatic rissoacean snails, one mammal and two aquatic insects. Development caused the extinction of one fish, at least one snail and possibly the endemic [[Ash Meadows Vole]]. Persistent water since the late Pliocene/early Pleistocene allowed relict taxa to differentiate in isolation during pluvial periods.

**Human history.** [[Shoshone]] people used the springs; the first biological inventory was the 1891 [[Death Valley Expedition]]. Private settlement followed statehood in 1864. Large-scale disturbance began in the early 1960s, including peat mining that destroyed about 800 ha of upper Carson Slough marsh. A 1960s–1970s cattle and alfalfa ranch (Spring Meadows Ranch, Inc.) pumped groundwater heavily. In 1979 the land was sold for a planned municipality of about 55,000 people, which was never completed.

**Species accounts (summary).**
- *Devil's Hole pupfish* (*Cyprinodon diabolis*): restricted to a thermal pool; its population fell below 150 under pumping; a minimum water level is enforced by court order.
- *Warm Springs pupfish* (*C. nevadensis pectoralis*): the smallest subspecies in its complex; School Spring counts in Novembers 1972–1974 ranged 136–231 individuals.
- *Ash Meadows Amargosa pupfish* (*C. n. mionectes*): nine critical-habitat spring areas; counts of 568 and 1,189 (Jackrabbit Spring, June 1982 and July 1983) and 1,189 and 1,822 (Big Spring).
- *Ash Meadows speckled dace* (*[[Rhinichthys osculus]] nevadensis*): total population estimated at 500; persists only in Bradford, Big and Jackrabbit springs.
- *Ash Meadows naucorid* (*[[Ambrysus Amargosus]]*): known from Point of Rocks Springs only; demersal eggs; highly vulnerable to extirpation.
- Plants: Spring-loving centaury, Ash Meadows gumplant, Ash Meadows ivesia, Ash Meadows blazing star, Ash Meadows milk-vetch, Ash Meadows sunray, and Amargosa niterwort (found in alkaline soils and salt crusts; sensitive to disturbance).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat types.** Listed and candidate species occupy aquatic/riparian and terrestrial habitats that are intimately connected. Disturbance in one type is likely to affect the other, which is why critical habitats overlap substantially.

**Biogeography.** Fish and mollusk relatives lie to the south and east, while the speckled dace is linked to northern dace. Plant relatives (e.g., *Astragalus phoenix*, *Centaurium namophilum*) occur in montane areas of the [[Intermountain Region]], and their ancestors likely arrived during cooler, wetter pluvial periods.

**Ecosystem processes.** Spring outflows that combine locally account for over 90 percent of total spring discharge. Many spring-dependent taxa depend on the [[Ash Meadows Aquifer]], so groundwater pumping directly reduces habitat. Essential habitat (about 8,806 ha for Devil's Hole pupfish) is defined by the zone where groundwater removal most affects spring discharge.

**Threats.** The plan identifies:
- Groundwater pumping and drawdown.
- Diversion, channelization and impoundment of springs.
- Introduced fishes, crayfish, bullfrogs, turban snails and salt cedar.
- Wild horse and livestock trampling.
- Off-road vehicle travel, road construction and soil compaction.
- Past fish farming and game fish introductions.

**Management status.** Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge was established in 1984 after The Nature Conservancy bought 5,121 ha of private land and resold it to USFWS. Table 1 records federal and state listings, and critical habitat is designated for several taxa.

## Key Findings

**Recovery goal.** Restore the listed species to non-listed status within native habitats across their historic ranges. Delisting may be considered only after populations and habitats have been stable for at least 10 years without further threats. The plan is ecosystem-oriented, and the Area of Management Concern (about 9,505 ha) is the primary focus.

**Criteria examples.**
- Devil's Hole pupfish: protect the aquifer so water level returns to 1.4 ft; maintain at least 300 fish in winter and 700–900 in late summer and early autumn; keep refugium populations at Hoover Dam and the Amargosa Pupfish Station. The current interim minimum level is 2.7 ft.
- Warm Springs and Amargosa pupfish: maintain current (January 1986) spring discharge, keep each population within historic habitat, acquire habitat for the refuge and eliminate introduced species.
- Speckled dace and naucorid: protect the aquifer, determine historic distribution, and reconstruct habitat throughout native range.
- Plants: protect soil moisture, withdraw habitat from mineral entry, survey historic distribution and eliminate introduced species.

**Priority actions in the narrative.**
- Acquire roughly 600 ha of remaining private land in the Area of Management Concern, prioritizing areas with the most listed species.
- Withdraw about 1,085 ha of BLM land from mineral entry.
- Maintain certified water rights and buy remaining private surface waters.
- Remove salt cedar and wild horses; eliminate competitive or predatory exotic fishes and crayfish.
- Restrict hunting to steel shot, end fishing and reservoir use in the refuge, and prohibit off-road vehicles, swimming and new roads through critical habitat.
- Establish a Devil's Hole refugium programme with 10 fish per year transferred from the parent population to limit inbreeding.

**Monitoring and cost framework.** Monitoring covers Devil's Hole (monthly from 1976–1983, quarterly from 1983), water levels (USGS since 1962), refugium populations, plant transect surveys and exotic species. Part III of the plan contains an implementation table with priority ratings (Priority 1 to 3) and agency responsibilities; the detailed cost figures are not reproduced here.

**Status of the draft.** The document is an unapproved Agency review draft. Its funding, timetables and goals depend on appropriations and comments from cooperating agencies.
