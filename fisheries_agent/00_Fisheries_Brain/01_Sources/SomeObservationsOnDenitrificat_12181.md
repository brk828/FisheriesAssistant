# Some Observations on Denitrification in Rivers and Estuaries

#water-quality #nitrogen-cycle #bacterial-ecology #Thames-Estuary

## Executive Summary

This 1959 paper by [[A. B. Wheatland]], [[M. J. Barrett]], and [[A. M. Bruce]] from the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] investigates bacterial denitrification in polluted rivers and estuaries, with particular focus on the [[Thames Estuary]]. The authors demonstrate that nitrate serves as an oxygen reserve in polluted waters, delaying anaerobic conditions and sulphate reduction. Through laboratory experiments and field observations, they show that denitrification can occur in the presence of low dissolved oxygen concentrations (up to 0.5–0.8 ppm), with nitrogen gas as the primary end-product. The study estimates that nitrate reduction contributes approximately 56 tons of oxygen per day to the Thames Estuary. Temperature significantly affects denitrification rates, which approximately halve with each 10°C decrease. The presence of dissolved oxygen inhibits denitrification, but once established, the process can continue at oxygen levels up to 0.75 ppm under certain conditions.

## Background & Description

### Historical Context

Nitrate has long been recognized as beneficial in polluted waters, preventing putrefaction and particularly inhibiting bacterial sulphate reduction. The nitrate acts as an oxygen reserve utilized before highly anaerobic conditions develop. [[Regan]] noted nitrate presence in upper [[Thames Estuary]] reaches and its reduction further seaward when dissolved oxygen falls near major sewage outfalls. [[Gameson and Preddy]] estimated that nitrate entering over [[Teddington Weir]] provides approximately 56 tons oxygen per day to the estuary through reduction processes.

### Bacterial Mechanisms

Multiple bacterial species reduce nitrate through different pathways:

- **[[Escherichia coli]]**, **[[Clostridium welchii]]**, and **[[Agrobacterium radiobacter]]**: reduce nitrate to nitrite, then to ammonia
- **[[Pseudomonas denitrificans]]**: produces nitrogen gas as sole end-product
- **[[Pseudomonas stutzeri]]**: produces primarily nitrogen (99%) with trace nitrous oxide

[[Verhoeven]] demonstrated two-stage reduction: nitrate → nitrite + nitrous oxide, then → nitrogen gas. Aerobic spore-forming bacilli producing ammonia did not form appreciable gaseous products and vice versa.

### Oxygen Effects on Denitrification

Literature presented conflicting views on dissolved oxygen effects:

- [[Sacks and Barker]]: oxygen suppresses enzyme formation and interferes with enzyme action; 4% oxygen in gas phase reduced denitrification rate to ≤50% of anaerobic rate
- [[Stickland]]: 1.65% oxygen in gas stream (0.32 ppm in solution) reduced denitrification to 12% of anaerobic rate with *E. coli*
- [[Korsakova]]: reported oxygen has no influence when organic nutrients are not limiting
- [[Watson and Twine]]: observed no nitrate reduction until dissolved oxygen reached zero in sewage-effluent mixtures
- [[Marshall et al.]]: reported denitrification in vigorously aerated 2% glucose solutions with gaseous end-products

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Thames Estuary Conditions

Field surveys (1953–1954) revealed characteristic nitrogen distribution patterns. On 14 September 1954, a pronounced dip in total inorganic nitrogen occurred approximately 5 miles below [[London Bridge]], coinciding with oxygen-depleted zones. This pattern indicates nitrogen gas loss rather than conversion to ammonia.

**Seasonal Variations:**
- **Summer**: dip in total inorganic nitrogen curve visible when large estuary sections lack oxygen
- **Winter**: dip not observed because nitrate disappearance coincides with ammonia peak near sewage outfalls

### Laboratory Microcosm Studies

**Estuary Water Incubation (44 days at 20°C):**
- Initial total inorganic nitrogen: ~10 ppm (constant)
- After sodium lactate addition (100 ppm): ammonia and nitrite increased, nitrate and total inorganic nitrogen decreased
- Approximately 1/7 of disappeared nitrate converted to ammonia, 6/7 to nitrogen gas
- Nitrite reduction occurred in presence of dissolved oxygen

**Carboy Experiment (25°C):**
- 5.3 ppm nitrate nitrogen added to estuary water + sewage + mud mixtures
- Gas evolved: 98% nitrogen, 1.1% CO₂, 0.85% oxygen
- Nitrogen equivalent to 2 ppm N in gas phase, ~3 ppm remained dissolved
- Ammoniacal nitrogen increased 1.1–1.5 ppm but continued increasing after nitrate depletion
- Nitrogen gas confirmed as chief end-product

## Key Findings

### Batch Culture Experiments

**Experimental Design:**
- Medium: standard BOD dilution water + 20% [[Stevenage]] settled sewage + 40 ppm nitrate-N
- Temperatures: 25°C, 18°C, 12°C
- Air-nitrogen mixtures: 5–50% air
- Duration: 48 hours

**Results:**
- Nitrate reduction and nitrite formation greater under anaerobic conditions than aerobic
- Reduction rates decreased with time, virtually ceasing after 2 days in some experiments
- No significant temperature effect when plotted against dissolved oxygen concentration (ppm)
- Temperature appeared to increase oxygen inhibition when plotted against % saturation

### Continuous-Flow Experiments

**Experiment 1:**
- Feed: diluted settled sewage (3-min permanganate value 10 ppm) + 10 ppm nitrate-N
- Retention time: 24 hours
- Temperature: 20°C
- Gas flow: 3 L/min nitrogen or air-nitrogen mixture
- Oxygen exchange coefficient: 26 ppm/hr/ppm deficit

**Key Results:**
- Anaerobic conditions: ~73% nitrate reduced
- 0.12 ppm dissolved oxygen: reduction fell to ~1/3 of anaerobic value
- Denitrification occurred up to 0.45 ppm oxygen (5.5% saturation)
- 0.53 ppm oxygen (6.24% saturation): nitrate loss equaled nitrite formation (no nitrogen gas production)
- 0.8 ppm oxygen: nitrification commenced
- pH 8.4: ammonia swept out in gas stream

**Experiment 2:**
- Higher sewage proportion: 3-min permanganate value 15 ppm
- Oxygen increased to 1.0 ppm over 42 days
- Ammonia absorption column added

**Key Results:**
- First 7 days (anaerobic): nitrate nearly completely removed, no nitrite in effluent
- Increasing oxygen: decreased total oxidized nitrogen loss
- 0.85–1.0 ppm oxygen: some nitrification occurred while nitrate reduced to nitrite
- Organic carbon content significantly lower with dissolved oxygen present vs. anaerobic
- Ammonia loss accounted for by gas displacement
- Net loss of ammoniacal + organic nitrogen: 3.7 ppm (assumed lost as nitrogen gas)

**Experiment 3:**
- Started aerobic, oxygen reduced in stages
- Never completely anaerobic

**Key Results:**
- Denitrification persisted only when dissolved oxygen ≤0.2 ppm
- Once actively denitrifying, process continued (more slowly) with up to 0.75 ppm oxygen
- Isolated organism identified as *[[Pseudomonas stutzeri]]*

### Tank Experiments (Artificial River)

**System:**
- Ten 20-gallon tanks in series
- Retention time: 2 days
- Feed: tap water + nitrate + 15 ppm peptone
- Mixing: paddles at 6 rpm

**Results (15°C):**
- Tank 1: 3.4 ppm nitrate reduced in presence of 1.5 ppm dissolved oxygen
- No conversion to ammonia (no increase in total ammonia + organic nitrogen)

### Temperature Effects

**Experimental Design:**
- Diluted settled sewage (1/5 strength) + nitrate
- Temperatures: 5°C, 12°C, 18°C, 25°C
- Anaerobic conditions (nitrogen sweep)
- Inoculum: denitrifying bacteria from activated sludge

**Results:**
- Denitrification rate decreased with temperature
- 5°C: very low rate, ~1/3 of 25°C rate after 3 days
- Rate approximately halved per 10°C decrease
- Temperature coefficient similar to carbonaceous oxidation
- Denitrification does not cease at low temperatures

### Critical Oxygen Thresholds

**Denitrification Cessation:**
- Experiment 1: 0.53 ppm (6.5% saturation at 25°C, ~4.9% at 10°C)
- Experiment 2: 0.85 ppm (10.5% saturation at 25°C, ~7.8% at 10°C)

**Nitrification Initiation:**
- 0.8 ppm (9.9% saturation)
- [[O'Shaughnessy and Hewitt]]: reported ~7% saturation in activated sludge effluent
- Border between nitrification and denitrification: ~7% saturation

### Practical Implications

**Thames Estuary Modeling:**
- Initial assumption: denitrification occurs below 5% oxygen saturation
- Nitrate oxygen utilized proportional to excess carbonaceous oxygen demand
- Predictions agreed with observations during high temperature/low flow periods
- Poor agreement during high flows (3,000 mgd) and low temperature (10°C)
- Predicted oxidized nitrogen exceeded observed values in central/seaward reaches

**Effluent Standards:**
- [[Mersey River Board]] proposed ≥10 ppm oxidized nitrogen in sewage effluents (except activated sludge plants)
- Nitrate beneficial: provides oxygen reserve before complete anaerobiosis
- Unlikely bacterial utilization before oxygen falls below fish-supporting levels
- Plant assimilation possible at higher oxygen concentrations

**Fish Toxicity Considerations:**
- Slightly alkaline waters: low ammonia concentrations toxic to fish
- Toxicity increases with decreasing dissolved oxygen
- pH 8.0, 4 ppm oxygen, 18 ppm ammoniacal nitrogen: complete rainbow trout mortality in 7 days ([[Merkens and Downing]])
- Un-ionized ammonia proportion increases with pH
- Desirable to minimize ammonia, especially when photosynthesis elevates pH

**Water Supply Implications:**
- Excessive ammonia or oxidized nitrogen objectionable for potable water
- Nitrate preferable: potential nitrogen loss during storage/treatment
- No oxygen demand during slow sand filtration
- May increase purification degree
- Artificial recharge: nitrate prevents anaerobic conditions, avoiding iron/manganese dissolution
