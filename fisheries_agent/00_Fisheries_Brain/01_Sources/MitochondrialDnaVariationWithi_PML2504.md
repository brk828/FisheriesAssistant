# Mitochondrial DNA Variation in Little Colorado Spinedace

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1993 report from [[Arizona State University]]'s Department of Zoology to the [[Coconino National Forest]] on mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variation in the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*[[Lepidomeda Vittata]]*), a federally threatened cyprinid. The study found substantial population structure, with most genetic variation lying among samples rather than within them. Remaining populations fall into three groups: [[Chevelon Creek]], [[East Clear Creek]], and the upper [[Little Colorado River]] (including the mainstem at St. John, [[Nutrioso Creek]] and [[Rudd Creek]]). The cover letter and invoice that accompany the report are summarized briefly below.

## Background & Description

The Little Colorado spinedace is a cyprinid fish native to the Little Colorado River drainage in Arizona. It typically inhabits high-elevation streams across a range of habitats (Miller 1963). Habitat degradation and interactions with non-native fishes have caused its decline within its former range (Minckley and Carufel 1967). It persists only in the upper Little Colorado River, Nutrioso Creek and Chevelon Creek, and the species is federally listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (U.S. Department of the Interior 1987).

The study's goal was to measure levels of mtDNA variation within and among remaining populations, so that unique stocks could be identified and genetic diversity preserved. mtDNA was chosen as a [[Genetic Marker]] because it evolves rapidly, is usually polymorphic, and is easily isolated from a variety of tissues.

**Document components:**
- **Cover letter (14 September 1993):** Thomas E. Dowling of Arizona State University sent a draft report to Tom Cain, Fisheries Biologist, Coconino National Forest. He noted that analysis of additional enzymes on Verde-area samples from Chevelon Creek was still pending and was not expected to change the results significantly.
- **Invoice (10 September 1993):** A final invoice under Cooperative Agreement/Contract #030492-D17 for chemicals and supplies, listed at $1,292.65 (the OCR text is partly garbled, so this figure should be checked against the original).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling sites and sample sizes** (133 individuals in total):

| Locality | Sample size | Collection year |
|---|---|---|
| Nutrioso Creek at MP 408, Apache Co. | 29 | 1989 |
| Little Colorado River at St. John, Apache Co. | 30 | 1992 |
| Chevelon Creek, Coconino Co. | 5 + 24 = 29 | 1992 and 1993 (combined) |
| East Clear Creek at Dines Tank, Coconino Co. | 19 | 1992 |
| Rudd Creek, Coconino Co. | 26 | 1992 |

Fish were collected by seining or electroshocking, frozen on dry ice in the field, and stored at -70 °C. The sites cover all known regions where the species persists.

**Laboratory methods:** mtDNA was extracted following Dowling et al. (1990) and assayed with restriction endonucleases. An initial survey of five randomly chosen individuals per population used 15 six-base-recognizing enzymes. Fragments were end-labeled with ³²P dNTPs, separated on agarose and polyacrylamide gels, and visualized by autoradiography. Polymorphic fragment patterns were given letter codes, and each individual's combined pattern defined a composite haplotype. For the remaining animals, only five polymorphic enzymes were used (BglII, BstEII, MluI, SacI, XbaI), with double digests where needed to resolve fragment combinations.

**Analytical approach:** Sequence divergence among haplotypes was estimated from shared restriction sites (Nei and Tajima 1983), and haplotype networks were built with PAUP (Swofford 1990). [[Nucleotide Diversity]] was computed with REAP, and population subdivision was estimated as F-statistics (θ/GST) with HAPLOID, with variance from jackknifing across populations.

## Key Findings

**Genetic structure and diversity**
- The 15 enzymes produced 44 restriction sites. The mtDNA genome was estimated at 16.5 kb.
- The initial screen of 25 individuals identified six distinct haplotypes. All differed from one another by single restriction-site changes, and all were less than 0.7% divergent.
- Chevelon Creek and East Clear Creek each contained a single, unique haplotype, so their nucleotide diversity was zero.
- The upper Little Colorado River at St. John and Rudd Creek each held two haplotypes. Nutrioso Creek held three.
- Average nucleotide diversity within samples was very low, and about 65% (0.6528) of total diversity lay among localities. The authors interpret this as most variation being found among samples.

**Relationships among localities**
- Nutrioso and Rudd creeks were nearly identical, sharing haplotypes at similar frequencies.
- The mainstem Little Colorado River sample differed from Nutrioso and Rudd creeks by about 0.05%.
- Chevelon Creek differed from the upper Little Colorado River cluster by about 0.25%, and East Clear Creek differed from Chevelon Creek by about 0.2%.

**Population subdivision (θ / GST)**
- Across all samples, overall θ was 0.5542, indicating substantial population structure. Haplotypes 2 and 6 were fixed in East Clear and Chevelon creeks and not shared, so their θ values were 1.0.
- Excluding Chevelon or East Clear Creek lowered overall θ to about 0.4, so neither sample alone accounted for the structure.
- Excluding both, the remaining localities showed lower θ values, with haplotypes 5 and 3 restricted to the Little Colorado River and Nutrioso/Rudd samples respectively.
- For Nutrioso and Rudd creeks alone, overall θ was approximately zero, indicating these two samples were effectively identical.
- Only the first analysis (all samples) had a jackknifed mean significantly different from zero. The authors note that jackknifing cannot be performed accurately with two populations.

**Interpretation**
- The pattern suggests current populations descended from a stock that became restricted to particular geographic regions and differentiated through stochastic events. Divergence among haplotypes is too small to indicate long isolation, and the timing of isolation cannot be estimated. The authors caution against attributing the pattern to recent human-caused events without better dating.
- Similarity between the upper Little Colorado River and Nutrioso/Rudd samples may reflect higher gene flow in the upper basin than farther downstream. Distance and habitat quality may limit dispersal between the lower Little Colorado tributaries (Chevelon and East Clear creeks) and upstream localities.

**Management recommendations**
- Management should treat the three genetically distinct regions (Chevelon Creek, East Clear Creek, and the upper Little Colorado River) as separate units.
- Each region has distinctive mtDNA variants, so differences in gene flow among them could, in an extreme scenario, lead to locally adapted variants. At minimum, each region represents an independent sample of accruing genetic variation.
- The authors conclude that populations from each region should be conserved to maintain existing genetic variation, whether or not that variation is adaptive.
