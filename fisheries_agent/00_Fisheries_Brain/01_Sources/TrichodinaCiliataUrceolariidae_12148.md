# Trichodina of Freshwater Fishes of the Southeastern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #urceolariidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic study revises the peritrich ciliate genus [[Trichodina]] (Urceolariidae) as it occurs on freshwater fishes of the southeastern United States. The author examined 939 fish of 46 species from 36 localities in 10 states, prepared 279 permanent slides, and described eight new species. Nine previously described species are redescribed, and a dichotomous key to the North American species is provided. Trichodinids were previously poorly documented in North America: before this work only 10 species had been reported from 16 fish species in 5 families, from six states. The paper also addresses host specificity, the value of silver impregnation for adhesive-disc taxonomy, and the nomenclatural confusion surrounding [[Trichodina Pediculus]].

## Background & Description

Trichodinids are small, disc-shaped ciliates that live on the skin, fins and gills of fishes. They are considered among the most important ectoparasites of freshwater fishes, yet they were largely ignored because of their small size, the apparent lack of specific characters, and the difficulty of removing them from hosts. Fish culture literature refers to them frequently but rarely names them.

The taxonomic history is complicated:

- [[Mueller]] first described a North American trichodinid as *Cyclochaeta renicola* (1931), transferred it to *Trichodina* (1932), and later placed it in a new genus, *Vauchomia*, based on the number of turns made by the adoral groove.
- [[Davis]] (1947) described nine species and redescribed *T. truttae*. [[Lom]] and Hoffman later moved two of Davis's species to [[Tripartiella]] and recognized that *T. symmetrica* comprised species from two genera, [[Trichodinella]] and Tripartiella.
- [[MacLennan]] (1939) redescribed *T. domerguei* and described *T. guberleti*. Both were treated as nomina nuda by Lom and Hoffman because the descriptions were inadequate.

Over 90 trichodinid species had been described worldwide, many of them only because they were found on a new host or in a new locality. The author adopts [[Lom Scheme of Specific Characteristics]] (Lom, 1958), which standardizes measurements of the denticle, adhesive disc, macronucleus and micronucleus, and builds on the earlier work of Dogiel and Fauré-Fremiet.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling.** Fish were collected mainly with seines, and from holding tanks at fish hatcheries. Specimens were either transported live in water, or fixed in the field in 1:4,000 formalin, which was brought to 5% after 30 to 60 minutes. Frozen hosts were found unsuitable because freezing breaks down the trichodinid pellicle.

**Examination.** Gills and fins were examined by light microscopy at 8x and 60x, and then with a compound microscope. Infected tissue was smeared and air-dried for silver and hematoxylin staining. Sediment from collecting jars was concentrated by light centrifugation.

**Preservation and staining.** Two variants of [[Silver Impregnation Technique]] were applied: a modification of the Chatton-Lwoff method (Corliss, 1953), in which specimens were embedded in cold salinated gelatin before silver nitrate treatment and UV exposure; and a modification of Klein's technique on albuminized slides. Harris-Lillie's or Ehrlich's hematoxylin was used for nuclear details, and formalin-hardened specimens were examined by phase microscopy.

**Host specificity.** The author disagrees with Lom and Hoffman that trichodinids lack host specificity, while agreeing that new species should not be erected solely for a new host or locality. Field evidence supports host specificity for *T. reticulata*, which was known only from goldfish and bream (both Cyprinidae). Fathead minnows and golden shiners in ponds with heavily infected goldfish were free of it, as were [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]] in the same pond. Only three North American species (*T. discoidea*, *T. fultoni* and *T.* sp. (?*T. nigra*)) were reported from more than one fish family.

## Key Findings

**Scope and novelty**

- The study examined **939 fish of 46 species** from **36 localities** in 10 states, with **279 permanent slides** produced.
- Eight new species were described, all from specimens collected in the study region:
  - *T. davisi* from [[Striped Bass]] (*Roccus saxatilis*), North Carolina hatchery ponds
  - *T. funduli* from Starhead Topminnow (*Fundulus notti*), Alabama
  - *T. globosa* from Orangebelly Darter (*Etheostoma radiosum*), Arkansas
  - *T. hoffmani* from Brown Darter (*Etheostoma edwini*), Alabama
  - *T. hypsilepis* from Highscale Shiner (*Notropis hypsilepis*), Alabama
  - *T. microdenticula* from Threadfin Shad (*Dorosoma petenense*), Louisiana, described as very abundant on every host examined
  - *T. noturi* from Speckled Madtom (*Noturus leptacanthus*), Alabama
  - *T. salmincola* from [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), North Carolina
- Nine species were redescribed according to the synopsis. The Results section treats 18 taxa in total: 8 new and 10 previously described (*T. californica*, *T. discoidea*, *T. fultoni*, *T. pediculus*, *T. platyformis*, *T. reticulata*, *T.* sp. (?*T. nigra*), *T. truttae*, *T. tumefaciens*, *T. vallata*).
- The Methods section states that "four known and 8 new species" were found. The source does not reconcile this with the ten previously described taxa in the Results, so the discrepancy should be checked against the original paper.
- Prior to this work, 10 species had been reported from 16 North American freshwater fish species in five families, and only from California, Florida, Iowa, Pennsylvania, Oregon and Washington.

**Diagnostic characters**

- Species separation relies on denticle structure (ray, blade, central part and aperture), number of radial pins per denticle, adhesive disc diameter, denticular ring diameter, the position of the contractile vacuole (central or eccentric), and micronuclear position (-y, +y, -y¹ and similar).
- Radial pin counts range from 5 (*T. microdenticula*) to 20 (*T. truttae*). *T. truttae* has the largest body (130–140 µm), and was not available for study; its data are from the literature.
- *T. reticulata* is the only species with a reticulated adhesive disc center, visible in both living and silver-impregnated specimens.

**Taxonomic decisions**

- *T. pediculus* is re-erected for the form on largemouth bass. Mueller's 1937 description differed from Davis's concept of *T. fultoni*, and Davis had synonymized the two. Key differences include ray stoutness and curvature, denticle number (22–26, mean 23, versus 25–30, mean 28 in *T. fultoni*), and the step in the central part of the denticle.
- The form from largemouth bass and bluegill is designated *T.* sp. (?*T. nigra*), since it differs biometrically and in ray shape from European *T. nigra* forms, and the author considers comparison with European material still necessary.
- The name *T. pediculus* is kept for the form on the three hosts (Hydra, Necturus and largemouth bass), although the author doubts they are one species, until silver-impregnated material from all three can be compared.

**Key and host records**

- A dichotomous key to North American species, based on radial pin number, denticular ring and denticle counts, and body and disc diameter, is provided.
- Notable host and locality records include *T. reticulata* on goldfish at ten National Fish Hatchery ponds across Alabama, Mississippi, Arkansas, Georgia, Kentucky, Louisiana and South Carolina, and *T. vallata* on channel catfish at Cohutta, Georgia.

**Methodological recommendations**

- Silver impregnation of formalin-preserved specimens is important for disc detail; the author notes that two methods were used.
- Freezing hosts damages the pellicle and makes specimens unsuitable for taxonomy.
