# Production in Coastal Salt Marshes of Southern California

#marine-biology #salt-marsh-botany #macrophyte-production

## Executive Summary

This EPA Project Summary (EPA-600/S3-81-023, May 1981) by H. Peter Eilers reports a one-year study of macrophyte (plant) production in [[Salt Marsh]] systems of southern California. Harvests and substrate measurements were made at four sites between October 1977 and September 1978. The study asked four questions: what production levels plant species and communities reach; how production varies within and between marshes, especially with and without regular tidal inundation; which physical factors explain that variation; and how southern California production compares with other Pacific and Atlantic coast marshes.

Net aerial production averaged roughly 2,160 to 3,787 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ across sites. The author concludes that habitat, particularly drainage and salinity, controls production more strongly than tide level alone, and that southern California marshes match or exceed Atlantic marshes at similar latitudes.

## Background & Description

Coastal salt marshes are widely regarded as among the most productive natural systems. They sit between the limits of tidal inundation, where nutrients from terrestrial runoff and coastal waters are delivered to marsh soils by tidal flux. Yields still vary markedly between marshes. Earlier explanations focused on tidal factors such as the frequency and duration of inundation, tidal range, waterlogging, drainage density, and soil salinity. Soil nutrient levels, especially [[Nitrogen Availability]], are also known to influence yield.

Most knowledge of North American salt marsh production comes from the Atlantic and Gulf coasts. Pacific marshes, particularly those on the California coast, had not been well characterized before this work.

**Study sites**

- **[[Sweetwater River Estuary]]:** 83 ha, in southern [[San Diego Bay]]; floristically diverse; perennially open to tidal fluctuation.
- **[[Los Penasquitos Lagoon]]:** 95 ha; seasonally closed to tidal contact; depauperate flora dominated by [[Salicornia Virginica]].
- **[[Upper Newport Bay]]:** 58 ha; about 110 km north of Los Penasquitos Lagoon; continually open to the Pacific Ocean; floristically diverse and low in stature.
- **[[Bolsa Bay]]:** Northern Orange County; highly disturbed by dredging and diking; floristically poor; artificially closed to tidal fluctuation since 1899.

**Methods**

- Vegetation was harvested from a 2.0 × 2.5 m frame at six- to eight-week intervals along transects parallel to the elevation gradient.
- Samples were sorted to species and dried to constant weight.
- Net production was estimated with a modified [[Smalley Method]] that incorporated decomposition rates measured with [[Litter Decomposition]] bags placed in each marsh.
- At each harvest, substrate salinity (interstitial and gravity water), pH, redox, ammonia, water content, and temperature were measured. Tidal elevation was surveyed, and each macroplot was assigned a habitat type.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Production depends on the position of plants along the tidal gradient and on the drainage and salinity regime. Habitat types ranged from creek levee and upland transition zones to back levee depressions, fore levee slopes, and pans. The pattern suggests that well-drained, aerobic soils with moderate salinity and moderate soil ammonia favor high plant growth. The author links these conditions to the optimal growth environment for marsh plants.

The study also points to a latitudinal gradient. Production appears to increase toward lower latitudes on the Pacific Coast, and southern California yields are similar to or higher than those of low-latitude [[Atlantic Coast]] sites. The author notes that methods used by other investigators vary, which makes direct comparison difficult.

## Key Findings

**Production levels**

- Mean annual net aerial production (g m⁻² yr⁻¹), with the reported values as given:
  - Sweetwater River Estuary: 3,196
  - Los Penasquitos Lagoon: 3,787

  - Bolsa Bay: 2,494
- Overall site mean was reported as 2,980 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ (partly garbled in the scan).
- Individual macroplot values ranged from 164 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ at a low-elevation Bolsa Bay site to 6,369 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ at a mid-elevation Los Penasquitos Lagoon site.

**Species and habitat patterns**

- Los Penasquitos Lagoon was clearly the most productive marsh.
- Production was highest in [[Frankenia Grandiflora]], [[Salicornia Virginica]], and [[Spartina Foliosa]] at Los Penasquitos Lagoon. Reported species means of 1,483 and 716 g m⁻² yr⁻¹ appear in the text, but the scan does not reliably assign them to species.
- Production generally increased with elevation at all sites except Upper Newport Bay.
- Creek levee and upland transition habitats had the highest production. Back levee depressions, fore levee slopes, and pans had the lowest.

**Conclusions (as stated by the author)**

1. Macrophyte production varies within and between southern California salt marshes.
2. Production in some species may be increased by reduced tidal contact, as suggested by high values for Salicornia virginica and Frankenia grandiflora at Los Penasquitos Lagoon.
3. Habitat, especially drainage and [[Soil Salinity]], exerts greater control over production than tide level alone.
4. The estimates support an increase in salt marsh production with decreasing latitude along the Pacific Coast.
5. Southern California production is comparable to that of the same latitude on the Atlantic Coast, and is equal to or greater than it.

**Scope note:** The study covers macrophytes and marsh substrates, not fish or other fauna. The text is a project summary; the full report is cited as a separate document.
