# Morphology of the Post-Pharyngeal Alimentary Canal of the White Bass, *Morone chrysops* (Rafinesque)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #morone-chrysops

## Executive Summary

This 1956 paper by [[Mary Smith Sublette]], reprinted from *The Texas Journal of Science* (Vol. VIII, No. 4), describes the gross and histological anatomy of the post-pharyngeal digestive tract of the [[White Bass]] (*Morone chrysops*). The study examined 27 fish by gross dissection and six by histology. It describes the [[Esophagus]], [[Stomach]], [[Pyloric Caeca]], [[Intestine]], and [[Rectum]], and reports tissue layer structure, cell types, and measurements. The work was a portion of a Master of Science thesis at the [[University Of Oklahoma]].

## Background & Description

Earlier anatomical work on fishes focused mainly on external features used for taxonomy. Histological studies of the gut were limited, and pre-late-nineteenth-century work lacked refined techniques (Dawes, 1929). The author cites more than 30 gut histology studies on other teleosts. Only two species in the family [[Serranidae]] had been studied for gut histology: *Serranus hepatus* (Edinger, 1877; Oppel, 1896) and [[Centropristes Striatus]] (Blake, 1930), with Blake's work the only detailed one.

The white bass is described as:
- A carnivorous fish that feeds mainly on small fishes, insects, and crustaceans.
- Limnetic and gregarious, often feeding in large schools on other gregarious species.
- Maximum length of about 20 inches and weight of about 4 pounds. It is a rapid-growing, short-lived fish, so its average size is much smaller.

Specimens were fixed in Bouin's and Zenker's solutions, embedded in paraffin, and stained with Delafield's hematoxylin, eosin Y, Mallory's triple connective tissue stain, and, for stomach tissue, Harris' hematoxylin and mucicarmine. Sections were cut at six to ten micra.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Natural distribution:** The major parts of the Great Lakes and Mississippi drainages.
- **Range expansion:** The distribution has recently been filled in and extended by introduction, particularly into reservoirs of the southwestern United States.
- **Feeding ecology:** The diet of small fishes, insects, and crustaceans is the basis for the author's comparisons of gut structure with fishes of different feeding habits, such as the bottom-feeding *Labeo* and *Mulloides* and the plankton-feeding *Atherina*, drawn from the cited literature.
- **Habitat use:** Limnetic, schooling, and gregarious. The document does not give details on water chemistry or habitat measurements.

## Key Findings

**Sample and scope**
- 27 white bass (mature and immature) were collected by gill nets and a bag seine.
- Six fish (standard length 98 to 307 mm) were used for histology. Five were sectioned from every gut region, and one small fish was sectioned as an incomplete serial (four sections taken, four omitted).
- No differences apart from size were found between large and small fish.

**Gross morphology**
- **Esophagus:** A short, muscular region, wider than long. In a 323 mm fish it was about 14 mm long and 17 mm in diameter. It joins the cardia and gives the stomach its characteristic shape.
- **Stomach:** A caecal-type stomach that starts at the cardia and runs almost the length of the coelom, ending in a blind caecum. The cardia and caecum distend with food, and the rugae flatten. The pylorus is short and is never as distended as the cardia or caecum.
- **Pyloric caeca:** Muscular, finger-like projections, usually four (sometimes five), with four more frequent. In the 323 mm fish the caecum measured 31 mm. Caeca are never distended with food.
- **Intestine:** A long, thin-walled tube with three convoluted "limbs." It ends at the intestino-rectal valve.
- **Rectum:** Smaller in diameter and more muscular than the intestine, leading to the anal opening.

**Esophageal histology**
- Anterior mucosa is intensely folded (primary, secondary, and tertiary folds).
- Stratified epithelium is present through most of the esophagus. It contains saccular mucous cells, triangular intercalated cells, and supporting cells. Near the stomach it changes to simple columnar epithelium, with one layer in the posterior esophagus.
- Taste buds occur in the anterior esophagus.
- No muscularis mucosae was found. The tunica propria is dense.
- The circularis muscularis is striated, with a maximum thickness of 280 micra in the anterior esophagus. The author notes that the submucosa is thicker than the circular muscle layer by a maximum of about 100 micra.
- The intercalated cells may be superficial squamous epithelial cells pushed apart by mucous cells. This is the author's conjecture.

**Stomach histology**
- The surface epithelium is a single layer of tall columnar cells, lacking saccular mucous or goblet cells.
- The gastric glands are simple tubular glands. They open through gastric crypts whose bottoms are lined with mucicarmine-positive neck cells.
- The pylorus lacks gastric glands.
- The stomach has inner circular and outer longitudinal smooth muscle layers.

**Intestinal histology**
- Folds are tallest in the anterior first limb (up to 2,280 micra), 760 micra midway down the first limb, and 360 micra just before the intestino-rectal valve.
- Goblet cells first appear beyond the pyloric caeca. They are most numerous at the fold crests and become very abundant in the rectum.
- A stratum granulosum of lymphocytes and eosinophilic granular cells lies beside the epithelium.
- The submucosa is absent.
- Muscle thickness in the first region: circularis 158.4 micra and longitudinalis 52.8 micra before the pyloric caeca. After the caeca, these fall to 29.7 and 19.8 micra. In the rectum, the circularis averages 115.5 micra and the longitudinalis 56.1 micra.
- The rectum has an additional oblique muscle mass between the tunica propria and the circularis.

**Pyloric caeca and rectum**
- Caecal mucosa resembles the first intestinal limb but has more goblet cells and closer folds.
- The rectum has many more goblet cells than the intestine.

**Anal region**
- Stratified squamous epithelium with a stratum granulosum.
- The basement membrane is thickened to 3 micra.
- Circular muscle increases and the longitudinal muscle disappears.

**Comparisons with other species**
- The author's findings differ from several cited species. For example, goblet cells were absent from the rectum of the plaice (Dawes, 1929), the racemose glands reported in *Labeo rohita* (Sarbahi, 1940) were absent in the white bass, and the white bass has no blood-vessel layer between the epithelium and tunica propria (unlike Jacobsen, 1939).
