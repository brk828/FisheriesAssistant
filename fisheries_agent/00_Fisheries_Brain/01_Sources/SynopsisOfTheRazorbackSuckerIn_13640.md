# Synopsis of the Razorback Sucker in Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Catostomidae #endangered-species #Colorado-River

## Executive Summary

This synopsis documents all known captures and observations of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]] from 1944 to 1995. Only 10 adult razorback suckers were reported from 472 km of river between [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Pearce Ferry]] in upper [[Lake Mead]]. All captures occurred in or near tributary inflows during spring/early summer (April-July), suggesting attraction to warm tributaries during spawning season. Many hybrid forms showing phenotypic characteristics of razorback sucker and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) have been documented, with genetic analyses confirming introgressive hybridization. The population is estimated at fewer than 100 pure razorback suckers, with possibly 1,000 or more hybrids. Habitat conditions appear unsuitable for completing all life stages, primarily due to cold water releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] limiting spawning, egg incubation, and larval rearing. The downstream-most 70 km in the [[Lake Mead]] inflow area may provide the only suitable habitat for all life stages, though high densities of non-native predatory fish pose significant threats.

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is an endangered catostomid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Paleontological records indicate presence in central Arizona near ancient Lake Bidahochi 2-3 million years ago, though remains were notably absent from 4,000 year-old flood deposits in Stanton's Cave (74 km downstream of [[Glen Canyon Dam]]) where other native species were present.

Adult razorback suckers are characterized by a distinctive dorsal keel (hump). In other basin regions, adults function as habitat generalists, occupying deep runs, eddies, pools, and tributary inflows in both alluvial and canyon-bound areas. Water depths range from <1 m to >2 m, velocities from 0 to 0.75 m/sec, over sand or silt substrates. Spawning occurs on large cobble/gravel bars in riverine environments and rocky shoreline shoals in reservoirs. Larval fish rely heavily on flooded bottomlands during spring runoff in riverine systems. The species exhibits approximate longevity of 40 years.

Hybrid forms between *Xyrauchen texanus* and *Catostomus latipinnis* are commonly described as "low-keeled fish" with poorly developed dorsal keels and intermediate characteristics including scale size, lateral line counts, dorsal fin rays, head/mouth morphology, and coloration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Conditions in Grand Canyon

The [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]] presents a steep, canyon-bound system with limited potential for flooded bottomland development. Channel geometry from [[Lees Ferry]] (River Mile 0) to [[Diamond Creek]] (RM 225) shows narrow canyons constraining the river. Cold water releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (8-10°C) limit spawning and early life stage survival, though water warms 5-8°C by summer in downstream reaches.

Four distinct fish assemblages occur longitudinally:

1. **Cold-Water Species** (Glen Canyon Dam to [[Little Colorado River]]): Low diversity, high biomass; dominated by [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]
2. **Mixed Cold/Warm-Water Species** (LCR to [[Havasu Creek]]): Moderate diversity and biomass; mixture including flannelmouth suckers, bluehead suckers, [[Humpback Chub]], carp, trout
3. **Warm-Water Species** (Havasu Creek to Diamond Creek): High diversity, very low biomass; warm-water assemblage with Lake Mead invaders
4. **Inflow Species** (Bridge Canyon RM 235 to Pearce Ferry RM 280): High diversity and biomass; braided channel with flooded bottomlands, seasonally-warmed tributaries

Primary and secondary production show "stairstep" longitudinal decreases downstream of major tributaries ([[Paria River]], [[Little Colorado River]]) due to sediment interference with photosynthetic processes, correlating with decreased fish biomass.

### Lake Mead Inflow Area

The downstream-most 70 km (Bridge Canyon to Pearce Ferry) exhibits substantially reduced gradient with vast sediment deposits creating a braided channel system with secondary channels and small flooded bottomlands lined with willows, tamarisk, and cottonwoods. This area contains seasonally-warmed tributaries including [[Spencer Creek]] (RM 246), [[Separation Canyon]] (RM 239.5), [[Surprise Canyon]] (RM 248.3), and [[Quartermaster Canyon]] (RM 259.9). Large cobble/gravel bars suitable for spawning occur at Spencer Creek, Separation Canyon, and RM 242.5.

### Non-Native Fish Assemblage

Twenty-two fish species occur from Glen Canyon Dam to Pearce Ferry, with only four native species (not including razorback sucker). Fifteen species occur upstream of the Lake Mead inflow; seven additional non-natives inhabit the inflow area. Potential predators include [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Walleye]], [[Largemouth Bass]], and [[Carp]]. Egg predators include carp, [[Red Shiner]], [[Plains Killifish]], and [[Fathead Minnow]]. The Lake Mead inflow contains high densities of potentially competing and predaceous species.

## Key Findings

### Capture Records (1944-1995)

Ten adult razorback suckers documented from 472 km of river:
- **1944**: 1 adult from [[Bright Angel Creek]] (angler capture)
- **1958**: 2 specimens (~37 cm) from [[Glen Canyon]], classified as "humpback sucker"
- **1963**: 1 adult from [[Paria River]] inflow (post-dam impoundment)
- **1978**: 1 gravid female (spawning condition) from Paria River, ~100 m upstream of Colorado River; 2 additional fish captured but escaped
- **April 1984**: 1 adult near [[Lower Bass Camp]], ~1 km upstream of [[Shinumo Creek]] inflow (Floy-tagged, electrofishing)
- **May 1989**: 1 female (TL=555 mm, 1,860 g) from [[Little Colorado River]] inflow (trammel net)
- **April 1990**: 3 adults captured, 2 recaptured at LCR inflow (TL range 475-588 mm, weights 1,211-2,049 g); 1 specimen preserved (PIT tag 7F7F456647, TL=482 mm, 1,249 g)

All captures occurred April-July, indicating attraction to warm tributaries during typical spawning period. All captures associated with tributary inflows: 3 from Paria River, 5 from Little Colorado River, 1 from Bright Angel Creek, 1 near Shinumo Creek.

### Anecdotal Observations

Four unverified sightings reported:
- 1985-86: 1 fish at the Aisles
- 1987: 1 fish in Bright Angel Creek
- 1989: 1 fish at Lees Ferry; 1 fish at Bass Camp near Shinumo Creek

Three of four observations occurred near tributaries where razorback suckers were previously captured.

### Hybridization

From October 1990-November 1993, BIO/WEST captured 2,197 adult flannelmouth suckers and 5 adult "hybrid" specimens (Lees Ferry to Diamond Creek). Summer 1994: 12 specimens with phenotypic characteristics of both species captured from lower 1,200 m of Little Colorado River. Genetic analyses (Dr. Tom Dowling, Arizona State University) showed 7 of 12 possessed DNA of both *Xyrauchen texanus* and *Catostomus latipinnis*; 5 had primarily *C. latipinnis* DNA, indicating introgressive hybridization from multiple matings.

### Population Estimates

- **Pure razorback suckers**: Probably fewer than 100 individuals
- **Hybrid forms**: Possibly 1,000 or more fish with introgressive hybridization characteristics
- Intensive sampling since 1984 yielded very few captures, indicating extremely low population density
- Absence from recent collections at historical capture sites suggests persistence only as very low numbers of adults

### Habitat Limitations

**Adult habitat**: Variable and apparently suitable in many Grand Canyon areas (deep pools, eddies, canyon regions)

**Critical limitations**:
1. **Spawning and egg incubation**: Limited by cold water releases from Glen Canyon Dam
2. **Larval rearing**: Limited by cold temperatures and lack of flooded bottomlands
3. **Nursery habitat**: Absence of sheltered, highly-productive habitats critical for larvae to ~25 mm TL (when mouth transitions from terminal to subterminal position)

**Potentially suitable area**: Downstream-most 70 km (Bridge Canyon to Pearce Ferry) may provide suitable conditions for all life stages due to:
- Seasonally-warmed tributaries for spawning
- Braided channel with flood vegetation for nursery areas
- Sufficient distance from dam for suitable temperatures
- Large cobble/gravel bars for spawning substrate
- However, high densities of non-native predatory fish pose significant threat

### Historical Context

[[Lake Mead]] supported large razorback sucker numbers until mid-1970s, with dramatic decline indicating aging population with little/no recruitment. Approximate 40-year longevity suggests mortality of cohort established when [[Hoover Dam]] was constructed (1935). Increased captures in mid-1960s suggest influx from Grand Canyon following [[Glen Canyon Dam]] construction (1963).

Construction of Glen Canyon Dam (1963) and Hoover Dam (1935) fragmented the 250-mile Grand Canyon reach, blocking exchange of potamodromous species and isolating populations. The region apparently lacks full complement of habitat conditions necessary for razorback sucker life cycle completion. Historically, upstream and downstream regions likely provided suitable nursery conditions while adults used deep Grand Canyon pools/eddies as holding areas during non-spawning periods.

### Recommendations

1. **Evaluate Predator Load**: Assess species composition, predator sizes, locations, and seasonal occurrence in Lake Mead inflow area; develop predator-prey models relating gape size to prey body dimensions to determine risk for different razorback sucker size classes

2. **Release Juvenile and Adult Razorback Suckers**: Release large PIT-tagged juveniles and radiotagged adults in selected areas/times to maximize survival; [[Spencer Creek]] mouth identified as suitable release location; chemoreception studies indicate young fish imprint well with penethyl alcohol and morpholene; releases could augment population or establish new population while providing habitat use data

3. **Hydrology Study of Lake Mead Inflow**: Investigate channel behavior and riparian area dynamics (RM 235-280) in relationship to river flows and lake elevations; understand formation and maintenance of critical habitats including channel braiding and flooded bottomlands under varying hydrologic conditions
