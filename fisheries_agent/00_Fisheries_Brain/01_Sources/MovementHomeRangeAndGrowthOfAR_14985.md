# Movement, Home Range, and Growth of Gila Trout in Improved and Unimproved Habitats

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study by John N. Rinne (USDA Forest Service, Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station) examines how [[Gila Trout]] (*[[Salmo Gilae]]*), an endangered southwestern salmonid, move and grow in headwater streams with and without [[Log Dam Structures]]. Fish were tagged and recaptured in three streams of the [[Gila National Forest]] in 1978 and 1979. Most recaptured fish stayed close to their release points, and the few that moved upstream were largely blocked by log structures of 0.5 m or more in height. Growth was slower in streams with high fish densities and in streams with many log structures. The author recommends that future introductions occur in headwater reaches and that any habitat improvement use few, low, single-log structures.

## Background & Description

The Gila trout is listed as endangered (U.S. Department of the Interior 1980). It occurs naturally in five small headwater tributaries of the [[Gila River]] system in southwestern New Mexico. The [[Gila Trout Recovery Plan]] calls for introducing the species into additional streams, eventually delisting it, and allowing a sport fishery. The success of this plan depends on the growth and dispersal of introduced fish in headwater streams, many of which are habitat-limited, with few pools and little cover.

[[Stream Habitat Improvement]] for salmonids began in the 1930s, when Civilian Conservation Corps workers built thousands of structures across the United States. Tarzwell's earlier work showed that improvement increased trout numbers, weights, and bottom-organism standing crops. Tarzwell also cautioned that it is much easier to install an improvement than to determine whether it is beneficial. Rinne (1981) had previously reported that log dams improved physical habitat and increased Gila trout size, numbers, and biomass.

The study asked how movement and growth relate to the presence or absence of improved habitat, using one introduced and two natural populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study streams.** Three small headwater tributaries were studied: [[Main Diamond Creek]], [[South Diamond Creek]], and [[McKnight Creek]]. Mean flows range from 0.3 to 4.0 m³/minute. Drought and flooding are common, and South Diamond Creek becomes intermittent each year within the study area. Main Diamond Creek is the type locality for the species and supplied the stock introduced into McKnight Creek. These three streams support about 80% of the estimated total population of the species (Mello and Turner 1980).

**Habitat structure.** Main Diamond Creek had 150 log dam structures and McKnight Creek had 18. Each structure consisted of one or two logs placed across the flow. South Diamond Creek had none. Adult Gila trout prefer pools to riffles. Pools were similar in volume and area across streams, but McKnight Creek had larger riffles. Estimated fish densities in riffles were significantly lower in McKnight Creek than in the other two streams.

**Population densities.** Density and biomass data were summarized per unit area and volume of riffles and pools in each stream (Rinne's Table 2). Biomass was similar across streams, while the number of fish per unit area differed. The author interpreted this as evidence that individual behavior, including territoriality and density, influences mobility.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sample**
- Fish were captured by [[Backpack Electrofishing]], weighed, measured, and marked with numbered sew-on tags. The release site was the same pool where each fish was captured.
- A pilot tagging in McKnight Creek (March 1978) was recaptured after 3 months. The main tagging took place in October 1978 and was recaptured after 8 months, in June 1979.
- Of 427 Gila trout tagged across the three streams, 129 (30%) were recaptured.
- A recapture within 10 m of the release point counted as "home." Age was estimated from length-frequency data from Mello and Turner (1980).

**Home range and movement distance**
- Only 11 recaptured fish were more than 300 m from their release site, and most of these were in South Diamond Creek.
- After 8 months, more than half of recaptured fish were within 50 m of their tagging sites, and 75% were within 100 m.
- Overall, 75% of the fish that moved were less than 0.1 km from their release sites.
- About 30% of fish recaptured after 8 months were sedentary.
- Mean movement was below 0.1 km (per the abstract).

**Direction of movement**
- In McKnight Creek, after 3 months, significantly more fish moved downstream (P < 0.005). After 8 months, no directional difference was found.
- In Main Diamond Creek, movement was either significantly downstream (P < 0.025) or nonexistent.
- South Diamond Creek was the only stream where most recaptured fish moved upstream (60%, P < 0.005). Upstream movement there was largely by age-1 and age-2 fish.
- Downstream movers were primarily smaller fish, consistent with nocturnal movement and flood-driven displacement.
- Mean downstream distance in McKnight Creek after 8 months was 604 m, compared with 51 m for upstream movers (P < 0.05).

**Effect of log structures**
- Log structures strongly limited upstream movement. Of 30 fish released just below structures in McKnight Creek, only one moved upstream, ascending four structures of 0.50 to 0.85 m and traveling more than 1 km.
- In Main Diamond Creek, of 20 recaptured fish released below structures, one moved upstream over a 0.55 m structure.
- Across all streams, 2 of 60 recaptured fish (about 3%) moved upstream over log structures averaging about 0.5 m high. The abstract reports this as fewer than 2%.
- Upstream movement was 10% in Main Diamond Creek, 32% in McKnight Creek, and 57% in South Diamond Creek, which lacks structures.

**Orientation to permanent water**
- In intermittent South Diamond Creek, fish oriented toward permanent surface-water reaches. Of nine fish released at the lower end of one permanent reach, 56% moved upstream. Of fish released at the head of another permanent reach, 63% moved downstream.
- Recapture rates were 40% in South Diamond Creek compared with 28% in each of the other streams. This suggests fish were not simply lost during flooding or drying.

**Growth**
- All age groups grew significantly faster in McKnight Creek than in Main Diamond Creek (P < 0.05). Growth declined with age.
- Example age-I growth over 8 months (October 1978 to June 1979): McKnight Creek 42 mm (range 37–47, n = 2); South Diamond Creek 21 mm (range 1–40, n = 9).
- Trout in South and Main Diamond creeks gained an average of 8.9 g and 6.5 g over 8 months. McKnight Creek fish gained 10 g in 3 months.
- Growth was reduced in Main and South Diamond creeks by reduced summer flows and, in Main Diamond Creek, likely by excessive numbers of structures promoting overpopulation and stunting.
- Mean length and maximum size in each stream paralleled population density.

**Discussion points**
- Movement was greater in McKnight and South Diamond creeks than in Main Diamond Creek, even though McKnight had fewer fish. The author attributes this to the presence of log structures, density, and territoriality.
- Regan (1966) suggested slow growth in Main Diamond Creek came from seasonal flow reductions. Mello and Turner (1980) reported smaller size and poorer condition in Main Diamond Creek compared with South Diamond Creek. The author's data partly support Regan but conflict with Mello and Turner on relative growth.
- The rapid growth in McKnight Creek suggests its density was at or below stream carrying capacity.

**Management implications**
- Reintroductions should be made in upper reaches of candidate streams, especially if young-of-the-year or age-1 fish are stocked.
- If log structures are used, they should be:
  1. Less than 0.5 m in height.
  2. Single-log rather than double-log.
  3. Kept few in number to prevent overpopulation and stunting, which would undermine a quality sport fishery.
- Structures could be counterproductive in South Diamond Creek because of its intermittency and comparable fish densities to Main Diamond Creek.
- The author cautions against the view that "bigger is better" or that more structures are better. Objectives must be set before any improvement is installed. Improvements are cheaper to build than to evaluate, and the total cost may be 5–10 times the construction cost once longevity and maintenance are included.
- Hydrologists, engineers, economists, and landscape architects should work with fishery biologists when planning and evaluating structures.
