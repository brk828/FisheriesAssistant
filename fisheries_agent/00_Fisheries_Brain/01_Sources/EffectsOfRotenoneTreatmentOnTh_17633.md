# Effects of Rotenone Treatment on the Fauna of the Green River, Wyoming

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #benthic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This M.S. thesis by Niles Allen Binns (Oregon State University, 1966) documents the September 1962 rotenone treatment of roughly 445 miles of the [[Green River]] and its tributaries above [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. The treatment was intended to suppress a rapidly expanding coarse fish population (notably [[Common Carp]]) before impoundment, so that trout could be established in the new reservoirs. The study ran from July 1962 to September 1964 and sampled fish and selected macroinvertebrates before, during and after treatment. Rotenone sharply reduced invertebrates and virtually eliminated fish from the mainstream. Recovery was driven mainly by downstream dispersal and survival in sloughs and backwaters, with invertebrate communities regaining diversity more slowly and with a different composition. The author concludes that the rehabilitation achieved its fishery management goals, while cautioning against indiscriminate use of such projects.

## Background & Description

The thesis was motivated by the planned closure of the [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] and [[Fontenelle Reservoir]] under the Colorado River Storage Project. Managers faced a choice between establishing trout after impoundment amid abundant coarse fish, or reducing those fish beforehand. The pre-impoundment option was chosen because the area had strong trout-fishery potential and post-closure treatment would have been prohibitive.

The treatment used Chem-Fish Regular, an emulsified product guaranteed at 5 percent [[Rotenone]] (70 percent active ingredients, including 55 percent methylated naphthalene and 10 percent other cube extractives). Target concentration was 5.0 ppm, reduced to 2.0 ppm at the last six stations and detoxified with potassium permanganate near Brown's Park. A sudden undetected drop in river flow caused overdosing, with actual concentrations of 9.4 ppm at station #1 and 2.5 ppm at station #22.

The literature review summarizes conflicting earlier reports: several workers found five-percent rotenone at about 0.5 ppm spared most food organisms, while others documented high invertebrate toxicity. Mechanistic work (Lindahl and Oberg, 1961; Oberg, 1962) indicated that the primary action is blockage of cellular metabolic pathways, with gill histolysis and vasoconstriction as secondary effects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area drains about 10,000 square miles above Green River City. The river originates in the [[Wind River Mountains]], passes through the [[Wyoming Range]] and [[Uinta Mountains]] drainages, and crosses the [[Little Colorado Desert]]. Water quality varies by tributary: Wind River streams are dilute and clear, while desert tributaries add silt and alkalinity. Recorded alkalinity ranged from 64 to 228 ppm CaCO3 and pH from 7.25 to 8.8 across sections. Minimum water temperature was 31°F and maximum recorded was 74°F.

The river was divided into four sections: I, the New Fork River; II, the Upper Green River; III, the Middle Green River; and IV, the Lower Green River. Historic pollution from towns, railroad yards and oil fields had reduced species such as the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], which had declined markedly. The Intermountain Chemical Company removed about 1,000,000 gallons of water daily from the river for its sodium carbonate plant.

Invertebrates were sampled at nine permanent stations (A to I) plus temporary lower-river stations, using a Surber square-foot bottom sampler and a one-foot-square drift net. Station A (New Fork, upstream) and Station C (Green River, upstream) served as untreated controls. Emergence traps were used in 1963 and 1964.

## Key Findings

**Treatment operation and concentrations**
- Rotenone was released from introduction stations spaced about ten miles apart, with sequential main-stream discharges over seven hours.
- Field bioassay on 4 September 1962 gave concentrations from 3.9 to 8.8 ppm half a mile below station #1; the calculated value there was 6.4 ppm.
- The [[Post Colorimetric Method]] gave erratic results and was judged unreliable; laboratory bioassays were considered the best available method.
- The 24-hour median tolerance limit (TL) for the mayfly *Siphlonurus* sp. was 0.012 ppm pure rotenone; the 48-hour TL was 0.0045 ppm.
- Rotenone arrived at stations 30 minutes to nearly four hours earlier than predicted, implying a velocity above the assumed 1 mph in some reaches.
- Rotenone reached Green River City at least 131 hours (5.5 days) after first presence.

**Fish**
- Pre-treatment, [[Rocky Mountain Whitefish]] dominated the upper river, followed by [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]; brown trout was the most common trout.
- Carp had spread upstream in the New Fork River, threatening the trout fishery.
- Whitefish, the first species to appear in distress, appeared at the surface within minutes; carp and suckers moved downstream or shoreward.
- Fish were largely eliminated from the mainstream. Survivors were concentrated in sloughs and backwaters; one 1963 retreatment of an oxbow pond yielded carp up to 9.5 pounds.
- Whitefish re-entered at about 1.5 miles per week; brown trout dispersed from untreated tributaries near Big Piney.
- Flannelmouth suckers reached three distinct year-classes by late 1964. Carp of the 1962 year-class survived in untreated sloughs.
- Restocking used roughly 8,000,000 rainbow trout in Flaming Gorge Reservoir alone; in late 1964, 720,000 brown trout fingerlings were stocked below Green River City.
- No squawfish, humpback suckers or channel catfish were found after treatment; squawfish recruitment was blocked by Flaming Gorge Dam.

**Invertebrates**
- Rotenone sharply reduced invertebrate numbers at all treated stations. Survivors included gomphid dragonfly naiads and rhagionid snipefly larvae at station D and station E.
- At station B, the post-treatment fauna was dominated first by Tubificidae, then by midge-mayfly and later by Simuliidae; Hydropsychidae did not return until summer 1964.
- At station D, the fauna recovered rapidly: 12,746 invertebrates per square foot were counted in summer 1964, with Tendipedidae and Oligochaeta most numerous.
- Station E recovered in numbers within two weeks, but the composition stayed skewed toward midges for about two years.
- At station F, Lepidostomatidae first dominated, and Corixidae was the only group still absent at study end.
- At station G, Tubificidae, Lymnaea and Tendipedidae dominated early; Simuliidae erupted in summer 1963. Hydropsychidae and Tipulidae had not regained pre-treatment abundance.
- At station H, the mayfly families Pentagenia and Ephemera had not returned by the end of the study.
- At station I, Tubificidae became abundant after treatment; Hexagenia and Tipulidae were missing at study end.
- A post-treatment check near the Jarvie Ranch showed that mayflies appeared languid, caddisfly occupants hung dead from cases, and no midge larvae could be found.

**Recovery and ecology**
- Re-population relied on downstream drift, survival of eggs and untreated refugia, and in some groups, pre-treatment egg deposition (e.g., [[Tricorythodes]] and Ephemerella).
- Recovery rates varied: Tendipedidae returned quickly, Ephemerella within a year, Hydropsychidae last.
- Clarity increased after the removal of bottom-feeding fish, and algal growth (Cladophora, a diatom bloom) increased.
- The author concludes that the Green River rehabilitation project achieved its basic objectives. Coarse fish populations in the lower treated area may rebound, and brown trout may replace rainbow trout as the dominant trout without continued heavy stocking.

**Management recommendation**
- The author argues that rehabilitation projects should be carefully scrutinized before implementation and not used indiscriminately.
