# Effects of Pesticides on Fish and Wildlife (1964) — Commercial Fishery Investigations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #estuarine-ecology #toxicology #Ostreidae #Penaeidae #Sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 Fish and Wildlife Service Circular (No. 226) documents the Bureau of Commercial Fisheries' research into the impacts of synthetic organic pesticides on marine and estuarine ecosystems in the United States. Work was centered at three laboratories: the **Technological Laboratory** (Pascagoula, Mississippi), the **Biological Laboratory** (Auke Bay, Alaska), and the **Biological Laboratory** (Gulf Breeze, Florida). Studies encompassed acute and chronic toxicity testing across phytoplankton, mollusks, crustaceans, and fish, as well as the development of monitoring methodologies using [[Eastern Oyster|Oysters]] and resident fish populations as bioindicators of pesticide pollution. Key findings established that chlorinated hydrocarbons (especially [[DDT]] and its metabolites [[DDE]] and [[DDD]]) bioaccumulate to extraordinary levels in marine organisms — up to 70,000-fold above ambient water concentrations in oysters — while highlighting species-specific and life-stage-specific sensitivity thresholds critical for fishery management.

## Background & Description

The Bureau of Commercial Fisheries' 1964 pesticide program aimed to determine the extent to which fishery products were being damaged by synthetic organic pesticide production and use. Roughly half of the research projects continued efforts to establish toxic concentration thresholds for representative marine animals, while expanded staffing and budget enabled a broader monitoring initiative across environmental compartments (water, biota, and processed food products).

Three primary research sites contributed distinct lines of investigation:

- **Technological Laboratory, Pascagoula, MS**: Initiated July 1964 to survey pesticide residues in fish, shellfish, and other fishery products at first point of landing, and to assess whether processing techniques alter residue levels.
- **Biological Laboratory, Auke Bay, AK**: Participated in a 4-year cooperative study on the effects of aerial [[DDT]] application (1/4 lb/acre) for [[Spruce Budworm]] control on watershed biota, including fish tissue residue accumulation.
- **Biological Laboratory, Gulf Breeze, FL**: The primary site for acute and chronic toxicity testing across trophic levels (phytoplankton, mollusks, crustaceans, fish) and for developing pesticide-monitoring methodology using bioindicator organisms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Test organisms were exposed to pesticides under flowing seawater conditions (except phytoplankton assays), with salinity fluctuating naturally between 17–27 parts per thousand across experiments. The estuarine environments studied — including the [[Pensacola Bay]] estuary and the Mississippi River delta — represent critical nursery habitat for commercially important species such as [[Brown Shrimp]], [[Pink Shrimp]], [[White Shrimp]], and juvenile finfish.

Key ecological vulnerabilities identified:

- **Estuarine dependency**: Shrimp species (*Penaeus aztecus*, *P. duorarum*, *P. setiferus*) spend critical life stages in estuaries highly susceptible to pesticide runoff, and their phylogenetic relationship to insects renders them acutely sensitive to insecticides.
- **Bioaccumulation via filter feeding**: [[Eastern Oyster|Oysters]] (*Crassostrea virginica*) proved highly efficient at concentrating chlorinated hydrocarbons from water, functioning as sentinel organisms for environmental pesticide monitoring.
- **Trophic and seasonal residue variation**: Fish residue levels varied significantly by year-class (2x higher in older cohorts) and season (4x higher in midsummer vs. winter), reflecting temperature-dependent uptake and food-web dynamics.
- **Persistent low-level contamination**: Even a decade after direct application, DDT and metabolite residues persisted in forest-stream fish tissue following spruce budworm spray programs.

## Key Findings

### Acute Toxicity Testing
- **Phytoplankton**: Radiocarbon (C¹⁴) uptake assays measured pesticide interference with primary productivity. Herbicides like **2,4,5-T (polyglycol butyl ether ester)** caused an 89.4% reduction in productivity within 4 hours; **Silvex** caused 77% reduction; **Zytron** caused 58.8% reduction.
- **Mollusks (Oysters)**: Growth-rate depression (shell deposition) was used as an EC50 endpoint over 96 hours. Organophosphorus compounds such as **Phorate (Thimet)** (EC50 = 0.64 ppm) and **Shell 4072** (EC50 = 0.60 ppm) showed measurable toxicity; recovery to normal growth rates occurred within 1–3 weeks post-exposure.
- **Crustacea (Shrimp)**: Insecticides were disproportionately toxic due to shrimp's phylogenetic affinity to insects. **Zytron** was extraordinarily potent (24-hr EC50 = 0.0015 ppm); **Zectran** and **Stauffer N-2790** also showed sub-ppm toxicity.
- **Fish**: Juvenile [[Spot (Fish)|Spot]] (*Leiostomus xanthurus*), [[Sheepshead Minnow]] (*Cyprinodon variegatus*), [[Longnose Killifish]] (*Fundulus similis*), and [[Striped Mullet]] (*Mugil cephalus*) were tested. Standout toxicants included:
  - **Endrin**: 24-hr EC50 = 0.00079 ppm (extremely toxic)
  - **Telodrin**: 24-hr EC50 = 0.0024 ppm
  - **DDT**: 24-hr EC50 = 0.028 ppm (killifish); highly toxic to sheepshead minnow at 0.1 ppm within 24 hrs
  - **Stauffer R-5092**: 0.020 ppm (both 24- and 48-hr)

### Chronic Toxicity
- **Shrimp & Endrin**: 0.6 ppb killed 50% of a population in 24 hours; at 0.06 ppb, shrimp survived 10 days; at 0.025 ppb, 15% survived 2 months with body residues <5.0 ppb.
- **Blue Crabs & DDT**: Survived 5-month exposure at low concentrations; 1.0 ppb was lethal within 8 days, but 0.25 ppb allowed normal survival and growth. A crab that died after 4 months at 0.5 ppb carried a 5.36 ppm body residue.
- **Oysters & DDT bioaccumulation**: Oysters concentrated DDT up to **70,000-fold** over a 40-day exposure at just 0.1 ppb ambient concentration. Approximately 65% of stored DDT localized in the digestive tract, associated glands, and gonads — with a major fraction specifically in gonadal/gamete tissue (14–20 ppm in eggs and sperm after 12-day exposure to 1.0 ppb). This raised concern for impaired larval viability, consistent with external reports of 100% larval mortality at 1.0 ppm DDT exposure within 6 days.
- **Fish resistance experiments**: Juvenile [[Spot (Fish)|Spot]] exposed long-term to sublethal Telodrin (0.01 ppb for 5 months) or endrin (0.05 ppb for 8 months) showed no growth/histological changes, but paradoxically exhibited **increased** sensitivity to lethal re-exposure doses (2x more sensitive in the endrin trial) — suggesting acclimation does not confer resistance. Breeding experiments with DDT-selected [[Sheepshead Minnow]] survivors produced offspring **more** sensitive than parent stock, indicating large population sizes are likely necessary for genuine resistance to evolve via natural selection.

### Field Monitoring & Residue Levels
- **Fishery products (Gulf & Atlantic coasts)**: DDT and metabolite residues detected in fishmeal, oysters, shrimp, catfood, lobster, cod, haddock, and tuna, ranging from 0.014 to 1.65 ppm (highest in Atlantic cod waste). Processed oyster samples also contained dieldrin (0.170–0.230 ppm) and endrin (0.025–0.180 ppm).
- **Auke Bay spruce budworm study**: One year post-spray, fish tissue retained DDT (max 1.4 ppm), DDE (max 2.6 ppm), and DDD (max 0.23 ppm), despite insect populations rebounding to prespray abundance within 14 months.
- **Pensacola Bay fish monitoring**: 152 samples across 19 species showed DDT/metabolite residues from 0.01–13.7 ppm in healthy fish; fish-kill specimens contained 0.30–5.72 ppm. A related laboratory test found that [[Pinfish]] (*Lagodon rhomboides*) exposed to just 0.01 ppb DDT in water suffered 100% mortality within 48 hours — despite field-caught healthy fish tolerating tissue residues of 13 ppm or more, illustrating the disconnect between tissue burden and direct water toxicity.
- **Cholinesterase biomarker studies**: Organophosphorus pesticide exposure (as low as 0.5 ppb) produced measurable, reproducible depression of brain cholinesterase activity in fish, persisting a week or longer — proposed as a promising bioindicator for organophosphate pollution monitoring.
- **Monitoring organism comparison**: Oysters outperformed mussels and crustaceans as bioindicators due to superior pollutant retention and salinity tolerance; crustaceans were deemed unsatisfactory as monitoring organisms due to low residue retention.

### Broader Implications
The report identifies three forward-looking considerations:
1. Many marine animals exhibit sharply defined toxicity thresholds, potentially allowing localized pesticide use without broad non-target harm.
2. Sub-lethal pesticide residues may cause subtle, long-term reproductive or developmental effects warranting further research.
3. Oyster-based monitoring networks should be expanded across U.S. shellfish-producing coasts to track chlorinated hydrocarbon pollution trends.
