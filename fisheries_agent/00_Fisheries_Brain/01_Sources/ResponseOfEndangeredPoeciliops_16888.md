# Response of Endangered Yaqui Topminnow to Introduced Mosquitofish in the Rio Yaqui Drainage, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study examined whether the endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*) can coexist with the introduced [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) at [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]], southeastern Arizona. Fishes were collected on 11 occasions between May 1985 and October 1987 from springs, ciénegas and streams. Persistence differed by habitat. In a [[Spring Pool]] (West Border Spring) where mosquitofish were present, topminnows never exceeded 12% of poeciliids, yet they were larger and more fecund than topminnows in spring pools without mosquitofish, which the authors attribute to compensatory reproduction. In [[Black Draw]] stream, topminnows declined to zero by July 1987 but rebounded to more than 60% of poeciliids after flash flooding in August 1987. The authors conclude that recurrent flooding and thermally uniform springhead refugia, rather than an intrinsic biotic resistance, are required to sustain topminnow populations alongside mosquitofish.

## Background & Description

*Poeciliopsis occidentalis* (Sonoran topminnow) was once widespread in streams, springs and ciénegas of the Sonoran Desert. Its populations were reduced by habitat loss and predation by *Gambusia affinis*, and it was listed as federally endangered in 1973. Two subspecies are recognized: *P. o. occidentalis* in the Gila River drainage, and *P. o. sonoriensis*, which extends into the United States only in the Rio Yaqui drainage of extreme southeastern Arizona. Mosquitofish were first collected in Arizona in 1926 but were not recorded in the U.S. portion of the Rio Yaqui drainage until 1979.

Earlier work by [[Hendrickson et al. 1980]] found mosquitofish at two Mexican Yaqui localities, where they were rare but coexisted with *P. o. sonoriensis*, suggesting the subspecies might resist replacement. [[Meffe 1984]] showed that recurrent flooding allowed *P. occidentalis* to persist with mosquitofish outside headwater streams, and [[Minckley and Meffe 1987]] proposed a differential-selection-by-flooding hypothesis. The study had two objectives: (1) to test coexistence or replacement under this hypothesis, and (2) to compare size-related fecundity and reproductive effort between species and habitats.

**Study area.** The refuge supports six endemic Rio Yaqui fishes, including the Yaqui chub, Mexican stoneroller, beautiful shiner, Yaqui catfish, Yaqui sucker and the topminnow. Topminnows occur without mosquitofish in several limnocrenes and impoundments (Bunting, Cottonwood, Middle, Ciénega and Upland springs, North and Tule Ponds). Mosquitofish are syntopic with topminnows only at West Border Spring and in parts of Black Draw. A second population at [[Leslie Creek]] lacks mosquitofish. Limnocrene water temperatures are about 27–29°C; Black Draw-Border is cooler (12–16°C in March).

**Methods.** Fish were collected by dipnetting and seining (3.2 mm mesh). Females were measured for standard length (SL), body and ovary wet weight, and fecundity. Reproductive effort (RE) was calculated from dry weights of reproductive tissue relative to total body dry weight. Statistics included Student's t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, regression, and analysis of covariance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predation and size:** Mosquitofish prey mainly on juvenile topminnows, which likely explains why topminnows were larger at West Border Spring than at Bunting and Cottonwood springs. Intraspecific crowding without predator control probably limited size at the latter sites.
- **Non-predatory interactions:** Mosquitofish and the native longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*) and Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*) feed largely on algae and invertebrates; only one of over 200 Yaqui chub stomachs contained topminnow remains, so competition for food may influence size.
- **Photoperiod and temperature:** Reproduction in both species was infrequent in autumn and winter in constant-temperature habitats, suggesting photoperiod is a stronger control than temperature. Temperature nonetheless triggered March breeding at the warm Black Draw-Source spring, where over 90% of females carried broods, compared with none at cool Black Draw-Border.
- **Springhead refugia:** Persistence of native poeciliids in springheads while downstream populations are displaced by mosquitofish has been widely documented. Black Draw-Source had higher alkalinity (7.2 meq l⁻¹) than Black Draw-Border (4.6 meq l⁻¹) or West Border Spring (4.9 meq l⁻¹), a pattern consistent with, but not proof of, a chemical preference explanation.
- **Flooding:** Monsoon storms in August 1987 (12.4 cm of rain) produced flash floods with depths of about 1.5 m in Black Draw, which removed mosquitofish more effectively than topminnows.

## Key Findings

- **Topminnow decline in a stream:** Proportion of topminnows at Black Draw-Border declined about 2% per month until none were collected in July 1987 (R² = 0.68, p = 0.012). After the August 1987 flash floods, the proportion rose to more than 60%.
- **Persistence in a spring with mosquitofish:** At West Border Spring, topminnows made up 1.4–12.4% of poeciliids (mean 4.84 ± 4.4%) and were never absent from collections (N = 9). The ratio to mosquitofish ranged from 1:7 to 1:65. No significant trend occurred over time (p = 0.789).
- **Size and fecundity:** Females at West Border Spring were significantly larger than at Bunting and Cottonwood springs, and reproductive output was higher. Topminnow fecundity was higher than mosquitofish fecundity in March 1987 and reproductive effort was higher at West Border Spring than in mosquitofish. The authors interpret this as compensatory reproduction.
- **Fin damage:** Caudal fin damage was 76% at West Border Spring (mean severity 0.76), compared with 5% at Cottonwood and 0% at Bunting springs. At Black Draw-Border it was 17.5%, versus 2 of 195 fish at Leslie Creek.
- **Recolonization:** After topminnows were reintroduced to Black Draw-Source (about 200 females and 50 males), mosquitofish arrived by July 1986 and made up 13% of poeciliids by September 1986. Mean female body weight fell 43% and ovary weight 72% between March 1986 and March 1987, consistent with density-dependent effects.
- **Reproductive timing:** In March 1987, 24% of topminnows at West Border Spring were unsexed immatures versus 3% of mosquitofish, suggesting earlier reproductive activity in topminnows. All gravid topminnows showed superfetation.
- **Conclusion:** The authors found little difference between the responses of *P. o. sonoriensis* and *P. o. occidentalis* to mosquitofish. No biotic mechanism of resistance was identified; persistence depends on recurrent flooding and uniform-temperature springhead refugia.
- **Management recommendations:** Conservation of U.S. populations depends mainly on controlling mosquitofish through (1) physical barriers to reinvasion from Mexico, (2) eradication of existing mosquitofish, and (3) public education on native poeciliids as mosquito control agents.
