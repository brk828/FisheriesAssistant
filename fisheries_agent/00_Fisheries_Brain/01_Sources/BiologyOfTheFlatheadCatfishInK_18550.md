# Biology of the Flathead Catfish in Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 study by [[W. L. Minckley]] and [[James E. Deacon]] (State Biological Survey, University of Kansas) describes the life history of the [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) in two contrasting Kansas streams: the turbid, sand-bottomed [[Big Blue River]] and the clearer, gravel-bottomed [[Neosho River]]. Fish were mainly collected by [[Electrofishing]] (A.C. electric shocker). The authors examine size-related habitat use, nocturnal activity, diet, growth, length-weight relationship, sexual maturity, fecundity, spawning timing, and parasites. Diet and growth differed between the two rivers, which the authors attribute to differences in food availability and habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** Flathead catfish, *Pylodictis olivaris* (Rafinesque). Its range extends from South Dakota to Pennsylvania, south through the [[Mississippi River]] Valley to the Gulf Coastal Plain, and into northeastern Mexico.
- **Kansas distribution:** Present in major streams and many impoundments. Less abundant in small streams and absent from intermittent streams.
- **Big Blue River:** Watershed of 9,600 square miles, 283 miles long, sand-bottomed, highly turbid (to 14,000 p.p.m. and more), never intermittent. Mean flow at Randolph, Kansas, 1,690 cfs.
- **Neosho River:** Drains 6,285 square miles in Kansas, flows 281 miles, and has more gravel bottom and less turbidity. Its upper two-thirds became intermittent during the 1953–1956 drought. Mean flow at Parsons, Kansas, 1,774 cfs (Oct 1956–Sep 1957; maximum 24,900, minimum 0).
- **Study site:** Neosho Falls, Woodson County, Kansas. A pool about 1 mile long, 150 feet wide, and 8 feet deep at maximum, with six cover drifts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Relative abundance:** In the Big Blue, flathead catfish made up 1.8% of all fish taken by combined methods and 15.1% of the electrofishing catch (Big Blue and Little Blue). In the Neosho, they made up 3.7% of all fish taken and 10.5% of the electrofishing catch.
- **Size-related habitat use:**
  - Under 4.0 in: in riffles, often under stones.
  - 4.0–12.0 in: dispersed through the stream.
  - 12.0–16.0 in: associated with cover at intermediate depths.
  - Over 16.0 in: solitary, near large logs and drifts in deep pools.
- **Movement:** Funk (1957) classed the species as "semi-mobile" in Missouri streams. In tagging studies, 90.7% of recaptures were within 25 miles of the tagging point, and none moved more than 50 miles. Recapture rates in the [[Coosa River]] (32.2% and 77.7%) support low mobility.
- **Nocturnal activity:** Catch rate was 1.72 fish/hour in daylight and 2.77/hour at night (10.5 h daylight, 23.2 h night shocking). Shallow-bank and riffle use also varied with time of day.
- **Behaviour when shocked:** Fish rose to the surface, mouth open, then sank or recovered. Channel catfish and bullheads instead stiffened and sank.
- **Invertebrate fauna:** The Big Blue mainstream had a sparse invertebrate fauna and no rooted vegetation. Invertebrates were concentrated around drifts and debris. The Neosho had abundant invertebrates under riffle stones, but also no rooted vegetation.

## Key Findings

**Diet**
- Analysis of 258 stomachs (69 empty) showed that fish under 4.0 in ate almost only insect larvae. Fish of 4.1–10.0 in ate insect larvae, fishes and crayfish. Larger fish ate mostly fishes and crayfish.
- Big Blue (93 stomachs with food, 77.5%): Fish under 4.1 in were dominated by Ephemeroptera (54% of volume) and Trichoptera (29%). Fish over 10 in consumed fishes (79% of volume), notably [[Notropis lutrensis]] (61.3% of identified fishes; 43.5% of all fish in 1957 collections).
- Neosho (96 of 138 with food, 69.5%): Fish over 10 in consumed decapods (43% of volume) and fishes (37%). Insect larvae made up 11% versus 3% in the Big Blue.
- Authors conclude diet reflects prey availability.
- Feeding peaks were at night and early morning. Empty-stomach percentages did not show clear diurnal trends.

**Growth**
- Young-of-the-year averaged 2.8 in (Big Blue, August 1957) and 2.7 in (Neosho, August 1957).
- Five-year-old fish averaged 24.8 in (Big Blue) and 17.0 in (Neosho) total length.
- Neosho fish grew 3.1 and 3.3 in/year less than Big Blue fish in 1957 and 1958, respectively.
- Growth was fastest in turbid, shallow areas and lower stream reaches, comparable to Grand Lake, Oklahoma.

**Length-weight relationship**
- Log W = −5.3873 + 3.0990 Log L, calculated from 36 Big Blue and 26 Neosho fish, with no meaningful difference between streams.

**Reproduction**
- Sex ratio: 108 males to 104 females (about 1:1) from 212 sexed fish.
- Males matured at 15–18 in (about 17 in average), at 3–4 years in the Big Blue and 4–5 years in the Neosho.
- Females matured at 18–20 in, at 4–5 years in the Big Blue and 5–6 years in the Neosho.
- Fecundity estimates: 11,300 eggs (24.0 in), 6,900 (23.0 in), and 9,300 (21.0 in) from three Big Blue females.
- Spawning occurred from late June to late July (late June in the Neosho and mid-July in the Big Blue, 1957).

**Parasites**
- 27 of 57 fish examined had cestodes (mean 5.2 per fish; 141 total), mostly in the duodenum. Most were identified as *Marsipometra* sp.
- Nematodes were found in only three fish.

**Ageing**
- Ages were estimated from pectoral spine sections (154 fish), with annuli read as narrow translucent bands. No sex-related difference in growth was found.
