# Spawning and Hatching of the Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report, prepared at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (NFH) in New Mexico, documents two years (1981 and 1982) of induced spawning, egg incubation, and fry rearing trials for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]]. Broodstock came from [[Lake Mohave]]. The work aimed to support a plan to stock 100,000 hatchery-reared fingerlings per year for 10 years.

Key outcomes were:
- [[Chorionic Gonadotropin]] (HCG) injected into the muscle at 100 units/lb per day, followed by [[Egg Stripping]] every 12 hours, gave the best results.
- [[Heath Incubator]] trays were the superior incubation method.
- A ten-minute [[Formalin Treatment]] at 1:100 controlled [[Fungus Control|fungus]] on eggs.
- Fish held in a hatchery pond for a year spawned as well as or better than wild-caught fish, and they were more predictable.
- Overall egg viability at 96 hours averaged 30.5%, and the hatch was 22.5%. Both fell well short of the 50–60% target.

## Background & Description

The [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973 shifted fishery work toward declining species such as the razorback sucker. Reintroduction into its historic range required hatchery production, but spawning and hatching techniques were poorly developed. Earlier adult spawning at Willow Beach NFH (Toney, 1974) had been a pilot project.

The study identified major problems to solve:
- obtaining large quantities of reproductive products from a "suspected" intermittent spawner
- determining hormone type and dosage
- accurately enumerating eggs
- selecting an effective incubation method
- developing stripping techniques
- controlling fungus on incubating eggs
- enumerating fry and holding them until swim-up

Broodstock were captured with [[Trammel Net]] and later [[Seining]] in Lake Mohave and transported to Dexter NFH in January 1981 and January 1982. Fish were treated with a 1:6000 formalin bath for one hour on arrival.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Wild versus hatchery broodstock.** Capture stress, injury, and transport caused high mortality. In 1981, 62 fish (46.3%) died before or during spawning. In 1982, 51 of 104 females (49%) died, and females were more vulnerable than males. Net marks were found on 34 of the 60 dead fish examined, all females, representing 66.67% of females captured by netting. Mortality from seining was estimated at 32%.

**Holding time and reproduction.** Fish spawned on arrival, after one week, or after two weeks had mortality rates of 0%, 22.7%, and 42.1%, respectively. Eggs per female followed the same pattern: 103,245 (no hold), 99,825 (one week), and 67,365 (two weeks). The author concluded that stress, injury, and handling strongly affected reproductive performance.

**Hatchery pond effects.** Fish held one year at the hatchery had no losses. Their colder pond temperatures delayed spawning by about 30 days compared with fish taken directly from the lake. The author argued this delay would benefit culture, because [[Razorback Sucker]] fry rely heavily on plankton as food. Pond temperatures from February 1 to 16 ranged from 50 to 62°F.

**Pond spawning.** In 1981, nest-building was observed by day 12 in three 0.1-acre [[Pond Spawning|pond]] trials and continued for 40–60 days. Viable eggs were found on nine occasions, but no live fry were found during the 85-day period. Continuous nest-building in a silt or clay bottom likely limited fry survival, since newly hatched fry cannot swim and silt can cause suffocation. The author judged this method unpromising.

**Lake Mohave population.** Of the 1982 females that died, some had atrophied ovaries. Eight females (11.5%) were judged non-producing from their lack of secondary sex characteristics and failure to spawn after repeated injections. The author suggested that Lake Mohave's population is reproductively old and that sterility will likely increase as the population declines.

## Key Findings

**Spawning methods**
- Circular tanks: females injected with [[Carp Pituitary]] did not ovulate eggs that could be stripped, and spawning was unsuccessful in rectangular tanks. [[Hormone Injection Site|Injections]] of CG at 300 units/lb for four days also gave negative results in circular tanks.
- Stripping after CG injection was the most effective method in 1981. Doses of 500–800 USP units/lb were excessive and caused premature release of immature eggs. Doses of 300–500 USP units/lb released mature eggs.
- In 1982, the best dose was 100 units/lb per day, given until spawning was complete (usually 3 to 5 days). Females at 300, 200, and 100 units were stripped an average of 1.4, 1.7, and 2 times, respectively.
- Stripping every 12 hours reduced eggs lost from overholding in the ovary. Females stripped daily gave an average of 1.5 spawns, and those stripped every 12 hours gave 2.4.
- Muscle injection was preferred over body-cavity injection. It caused less struggling and a lower risk of hitting a vital organ. The sample was too small for firm conclusions on survival.

**Egg handling and viability**
- Eggs were mixed with sperm from at least two males, coated with a [[Bentonite Coating|bentonite slurry]] to reduce adhesiveness, and then water-hardened.
- Egg viability at 96 hours rose from 27.7% to 61.1% when eggs were stripped at 12-hour intervals and moved to incubator trays after 45–60 minutes of [[Water Hardening]]. Water-hardening periods of 1.5 to 6 hours reduced viability. In the 12-hour trial, morning eggs (held longer) had 23% and 33% viability, versus 61.8% and 59.2% for evening eggs.
- Egg viability was 30.5% (mean at 96 hours). Viability at 96 to 110 hours ranged from 0% to 82% across spawns.

**Egg enumeration**
- The [[Gravimetric Enumeration]] method was judged most practical and realistic. Counts from 100 trays averaged 130.2 eggs per gram (range 100–185).
- [[Water Displacement Method|Water displacement]] estimates were close to gravimetric ones. The [[Von Bayer Method]] gave estimates averaging 28% higher.
- Egg diameter averaged 0.0827 inch (73 samples; range 0.0640–0.0948 inch).
- Eggs placed in Heath trays ranged from 101,500 to 119,978 per tray in 1982. The author recommended no more than 50,000 eggs per tray. Trays with more than 35,000 eggs were considered crowded, and crowding was directly correlated with fungal proliferation.

**Incubation**
- Heath incubators were the best method. Stripped eggs coated with bentonite were used in 1982. Coating gave little or no viability advantage but made eggs easier to count and treat for fungus.
- McDonald hatching jars caused premature hatching on day 3 (five trials), with most embryos dying or malformed.
- Tank incubation on rock substrate and flat net suspension were both unsatisfactory because of uneven egg distribution, fungus, and low oxygen. Water depth above 20 inches reduced oxygenation, which was partly fixed with a standpipe holding depth at 8–12 inches.

**Fungus control**
- Formalin at 1:75 to 1:200 for ten minutes controlled fungus without killing developing embryos.
- In Heath trays, a ten-minute 1:100 formalin dip, applied daily for the first four days, fully controlled fungal growth.
- Constant-flow treatment through a stack of eight trays failed because the chemical was diluted in lower trays.
- Fungal growth was delayed but not fully killed at 1:300 and above.

**Fry and hatch**
- The 1982 hatch was 22.5% (1,660,652 fry from 7,366,565 eggs). The author expected 50–60% with improved handling.
- Water displacement counts averaged 233.4 fry per ml (67 samples; range 198–276).
- Abnormal fry, many with bent or circular swimming behavior, were common. They ranged from nearly none to 80% per sample in Heath trays and reached 20% in holding tanks. Possible causes include low incubation temperature, handling injury, forced early ovulation, and weak eggs from old brood fish.
- Fry losses from hatching to swim-up averaged 34.2%, with 29% lost in seven lots.

**Production and recommendations**
- 1982 production from 71 females was 7,366,565 eggs, averaging 103,754 eggs per female, or 15,460 per pound of brood fish.
- Projected expectations: about 100,000 eggs per female, or 15,000 eggs per pound, with an assumed spawning rate of roughly 80%.
- Summary recommendations included: use hatchery-held fish; delay induced spawning until most females are ripe; inject females intramuscularly with CG at 100 units/lb daily until spawning is complete; strip every 12 hours; coat eggs with bentonite and water-harden for no more than 45 minutes; enumerate eggs gravimetrically at about 130 eggs per gram; incubate in Heath trays at no more than 50,000 eggs per tray; treat eggs daily with 1:100 formalin for 10 minutes for the first four days; use a catch tank below the trays for migrating fry; enumerate fry at 230 per ml of water displaced; and hold fry in a well-aerated trough until swim-up.

**Research gaps**
The author stated that much remains to be learned about culturing razorback suckers. Hatchery-reared brood fish had not yet been tested, so egg-number predictions for them were unavailable.
