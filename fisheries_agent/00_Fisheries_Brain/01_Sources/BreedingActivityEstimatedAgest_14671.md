# Breeding Activity, Estimated Age-Structure, and Growth in Sonoran Desert Anurans

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anura

*Note: the source concerns amphibians (anurans), not fish. The tag line follows the system format for a non-fish taxon.*

## Executive Summary

This study examines a community of four desert-adapted anurans at two flood control sites in north-central [[Phoenix, Arizona]], monitored for breeding activity from 1990 to 1995 and analyzed for age structure and growth in 1994 and 1995 using [[Skeletochronology]]. Breeding was restricted largely to 1990 and 1992, years with repeated significant rainfall events (SREs; more than 25 mm within 24 h). The authors estimate that all four species were young, with average ages of two to four years, and that growth was fastest in the second year for two species. The results suggest rapid maturation and do not support the idea that these toads and spadefoots are long-lived with extended adult reproductive lives.

## Background & Description

- **Study taxa:** [[Colorado River Toad]] (*Bufo alvarius*), [[Great Plains Toad]] (*B. cognatus*), [[Red-spotted Toad]] (*B. punctatus*), and [[Couch's Spadefoot Toad]] (*Scaphiopus couchii*).
- **Study sites:** Adobe Dam site (534 ha, Skunk Creek) and Cave Buttes site (737 ha, Cave Creek), both maintained by [[Maricopa County]] as flood control land. Both sites were at the outset of urbanization as the city of Phoenix expanded northward.
- **Rationale:** Little was known about the population biology of most desert amphibians, and the sites offered baseline data before urban development.
- **Skeletochronology principle:** Concentric [[Lines of Arrested Growth]] (LAGs) in bone sections correspond to winter growth depression. Resorption of inner bone can erase early LAGs, which complicates age estimates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Breeding trigger:** Chorusing and egg-laying occurred only after rainfall of more than 25 mm within 24 h. Carefree, about 10 km northeast of Cave Buttes, served as the rainfall reference station (see [[Monsoon Rainfall]]).
- **Rainfall variability:** Summer (July–August) totals ranged from 8.89 mm (1994) to 143.5 mm (1992). SREs occurred in 1990 (3), 1992 (3), and none in 1991, 1993, 1994, or 1995.
- **Habitat:** The species breed in rain-formed pools in low-lying areas and along low-gradient floodplains. Dike construction across washes may have created analogous habitat, but its effect is unknown.
- **Other species:** [[Woodhouse's Toad]] (*B. woodhousii*) was recorded at Adobe Dam in 1992 but likely was not detected because it breeds earlier in permanent water.
- **Potential threat:** The sites were beginning to experience urban pressure, which the authors framed as a reason for baseline monitoring.

## Key Findings

**Breeding activity**
- All four species bred during the summer monsoon, with simultaneous chorusing only after intense storms (>25 mm in 24 h).
- Total chorusing nights across 1990–1995: *B. alvarius* 12, *B. cognatus* 17, *B. punctatus* 20, *S. couchii* 19.
- No breeding occurred in 1991 or 1993. In 1994, only *S. couchii* bred (one large aggregation of 65 toe-clipped males on 4 September). In 1995, only *B. alvarius* bred, on a single night, with a mixed chorus recorded on 20–21 August at Cave Buttes.
- Chorus sizes: *B. alvarius* at Adobe Dam averaged 21.1 ± 6.05 males per chorus (n = 5) and at Cave Buttes 13.0 ± 3.88 (n = 9). A single *B. cognatus* chorus at Cave Buttes on 30 July 1992 included 54 marked males, roughly 25% of males present.

**Age structure**
- In 1994, *B. alvarius*, *B. cognatus*, and *S. couchii* averaged about 2.5 LAGs (ages 2–4 years).
- In 1995, the same three taxa averaged three to four LAGs. The authors interpret this as roughly a one-year increase, consistent with recruitment from the successful 1992 breeding season.
- *B. punctatus* was apparently younger in 1995, with an average of about two LAGs.
- Femurs yielded clearer LAGs than digits, so femurs were used for age estimates (fewer than 1–2% were unscorable).

**Growth**
- Growth in the second season was significantly higher than in other seasons for *B. alvarius* (ANOVA F = 12.66, P < 0.001) and *B. cognatus* (F = 12.09, P < 0.001).
- *B. punctatus* (F = 6.76, P < 0.001) and *S. couchii* (F = 3.56, P < 0.003) showed a monotonic decline in growth with age.
- Growth across calendar years showed no clear pattern; *B. punctatus* showed no significant variation.

**Size–age relationships**
- SVL and body mass were strongly positively correlated within each sample (for example, *B. alvarius* males in 1994: r = 0.93).
- SVL was not significantly related to LAG number for any species in either year, which the authors take as evidence against size and age being tightly linked in anurans.

**Conclusions and caveats**
- The authors conclude that the species reach reproductive maturity at about two years, do not support a long-lived, extended-reproduction life history, and regard their age estimates as conservative underestimates.
- Resorption could not be evaluated because nearly all sampled individuals were adults. Validation would require longitudinal studies of known-age individuals.
- Populations persisted through most seasons without successful breeding across at least a six-year span.
- Comparative data: [[Southeastern Arizona Spadefoot Population]] (Tinsley and Tocque, 1995) had older breeders (majority ≥5 years), possibly due to a more mesic, higher-elevation habitat.
- Most temperate bufonids studied by skeletochronology mature at about two to three years, while *B. bufo*, *B. calamita*, and *B. raddei* mature later (median 3–5 years), likely reflecting colder environments.
