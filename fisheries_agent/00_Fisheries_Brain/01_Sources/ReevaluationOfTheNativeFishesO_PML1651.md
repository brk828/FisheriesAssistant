# Re-evaluation of the Native Fishes of the Rio Yaqui in the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, presented at the 1974 Annual Conference of the Association of State Game and Fish Commissioners in Albuquerque, New Mexico, re-evaluates the native fishes of the upper [[Rio Yaqui]] system within the United States. The Yaqui drainage in Arizona originally contributed roughly a quarter of the native ichthyofauna of the state, and several of its fishes are differentiated from adjacent populations at least to the subspecific level. Surveys in spring 1974 found new populations of some species but confirmed that the fauna is in a precarious state. Three extinctions (counting the Yaqui catfish as native) are attributed largely to habitat modification, drought and water over-use. The author, Randy H. McNatt of the [[Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory]] and Arizona State University, stresses that accurate, preserved voucher specimens in museum collections are essential for documenting such change.

## Background & Description

The Rio Yaqui is the largest river of Sonora, Mexico, with its uppermost headwaters in extreme southeastern Arizona. Two US portions are recognized:

- **Whitewater Draw system**: an intermittent drainage from the Mule Mountains and the Sulphur Springs Valley, draining toward the Willcox Playa (Pluvial Lake Cochise), with remaining habitats in Whitewater Draw, Rucker Canyon (Chiricahua Mountains) and Leslie Creek (Swisshelm Mountains).
- **San Bernardino Creek**: an incised channel entering from New Mexico and quickly entering Sonora, where it becomes the Rio de San Bernardino. Remaining fish habitat comprises artesian wells, natural springs and artificial ponds.

Historical landscape change includes arroyo cutting in the late 1880s, mining, pumpage and decline of artesian heads, and minor impoundments for livestock and sport fishing.

The species accounts cover six taxa:

- [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*): described from [[U.S. and Mexican Boundary Survey]] collections; the type locality is generally considered San Bernardino Creek.
- [[Yaqui Shiner]] (*Notropis formosus mearnsi*): last recorded in numbers until 1968; only two very young fish were found in 1969–70.
- [[Mexican Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma ornatum pricei*): juveniles taken in 1974 from Leslie Creek; abundant in Rucker Canyon.
- [[Yaqui Sucker]] (*Catostomus bernardini*): formerly in San Bernardino Creek; not found in the 1974 survey and considered extinct in the United States.
- [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*): all US specimens are from an introduced population in the Santa Cruz River basin, now extirpated.
- [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis sonoriensis*): once nearly as widespread as the local [[Desert Dace]] (*Agosia*) form; now in small populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most surviving populations are small, isolated remnants separated by long dry reaches or by barriers created by dams and other human activity. Whitewater Draw and San Bernardino Creek have been dried out, and artesian waters on the San Bernardino Ranch have been physically altered. Large-scale [[Groundwater Pumpage]] in the San Bernardino valley, in both the United States and Mexico, is increasing and continues to reduce surface water.

Introduced predators and competitors, including [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) and [[American Bullfrog]] (*Rana catesbeiana*), may have contributed to the decline of the Yaqui Shiner by eliminating escape areas in ponds. Flash flooding and intermittency characterize Leslie Creek and Rucker Canyon, and a persistent leak from a fishing lake in Rucker Canyon maintains downstream flow during drought, which may help sustain Mexican Stoneroller populations.

Yaqui Topminnow reproduction shows a marked seasonal cycle with little or no reproduction in winter, according to [[Schoenherr]] (1973). The species was transplanted to Leslie Creek, where a thriving population now lives alongside three other native species, despite expectations that its higher elevation and cooler climate would be unfavourable.

## Key Findings

- **Extinctions:** Three extinctions have occurred in the Yaqui fauna within the United States if the Yaqui catfish is counted as native. Two were very recent and are attributed to habitat modification and/or drought, possibly with livestock water over-use.
- **Rediscoveries (spring 1974):** A reproducing population of Yaqui Chub was found in a small artesian bore on San Bernardino Creek, with a large population collected from a second site. The bore population was estimated at no more than a few tens of individuals. Yaqui Chub was thought extinct after 1969.
- **Mexican Stoneroller:** Previously reported extinct in Arizona (1971 collections), the species was found abundant in Rucker Canyon, with at least three age classes where trout were absent.
- **Yaqui Shiner:** Not found in the 1974 survey and probably extirpated, although it may persist in larger ponds.
- **Yaqui Topminnow:** Taxonomic status was retained at the species level, based on morphology, biogeography and distinct breeding strategies, including female aggression and cannibalism of offspring, which the author notes is practically unknown in *Poeciliopsis*.
- **Conservation message:** Accurate records require knowing where and when to sample, and preserved voucher specimens are essential. The author argues that museum collections function as libraries that provide baselines for future assessment and legislation.
