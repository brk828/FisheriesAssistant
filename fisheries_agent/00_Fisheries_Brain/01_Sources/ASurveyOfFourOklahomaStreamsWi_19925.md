# A Survey of Four Oklahoma Streams with Reference to Production

#limnology #aquatic-ecology #benthos

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared at the [[Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory]] under Director Robert M. Jenkins and supported by the Oklahoma Wildlife Conservation Department and the University of Oklahoma Biological Survey, presents the first limnological survey of Oklahoma streams. The author, Lothar E. Hornuff, studied four streams from June 1955 through August 1956 to determine which physical, chemical, and biological factors control production in running water. Production was assessed with three indices: [[Benthos]] standing crop, [[Periphyton]] accumulation, and [[Primary Production]] measured as community metabolism.

The main conclusion is that rate of flow and bottom type, shaped by stream gradient, precipitation, and water-level stability, control benthic production more than chemistry does. Riffles with rocky or bedrock bottoms, swift current, and stable levels supported the highest benthos. Sandy, slow, or fluctuating reaches were the least productive. The document does not analyze fish populations, although it cites earlier fishery work.

## Background & Description

The study was motivated by the lack of a stream classification based on productivity. The author noted that most prior work had been done in lentic (lake) environments and that chemical indices had not been applied to lotic systems. Benthos was chosen because these animals sit near the middle of the food chain, feeding on plankton and serving as prey for higher animals. Periphyton was used following [[Newcombe 1949]], and primary production followed [[Odum 1956]].

Four streams were selected to represent a range of conditions:

- **[[Mountain Fork River]]**: about 90 miles long, watershed about 1,000 square miles, mean discharge 1,400 cfs (range 6 to 9,490 cfs). Mean annual precipitation 49.95 inches. Flows through sandstone and shale; vegetation is oak and pine forest.
- **[[Blue River]]**: about 110 miles long, watershed about 800 square miles. The text gives mean discharge as 37.14 cfs, while Table 1 lists 34.8 cfs. Mean annual precipitation 38.09 inches. Vegetation is mixed post oak, blackjack forest, and tall grass prairie.
- **[[Little River]]** (tributary of the Canadian River): about 100 miles long, watershed about 2,000 square miles, mean discharge 3.66 cfs in the text (Table 1 lists 17.9 cfs). Mean annual precipitation 33.4 inches. Vegetation is almost entirely post oak and blackjack forest. Only one station was sampled because the stream dried up in summer.
- **[[Honey Creek]]** (tributary of the Washita River): about 15 miles long, watershed about 20 square miles, with a gradient averaging 49 feet per mile. Flows over Turner Falls, a travertine formation. Vegetation is mainly tall grass prairie.

Sampling used 1 to 5 stations per stream. Methods included float-method flow classification (slight at 1 ft/s or less, moderate at 1 to 3 ft/s, swift above 3 ft/s), dissolved oxygen and alkalinity analyses following [[Welch 1948]], phosphorus determinations, dip-net and rock-surface benthos sampling, submerged-slide periphyton sampling for at least two weeks, and [[Dark-and-Light Bottle Method]] oxygen measurements.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and land use.** Mountain Fork River fluctuates widely and may drop to 6 cfs in dry weather, though it keeps flowing. The author attributes this to improper timber cutting early in the twentieth century, which reduced the soil's capacity to hold moisture. Local residents reported that flows were sustained until about 1928, followed by gradual decline. Only the major streams (Mountain Fork, Little, and Glover rivers) were said to flow year-round.

**Precipitation.** Blue River received less rainfall than Mountain Fork River but kept a steady water level. The author explains that much of the precipitation was retained in the watershed and released gradually through springs. Blue River's water-level stability is therefore linked to groundwater and spring inflow rather than rainfall alone.

**Water chemistry.** Blue River, Little River, and Honey Creek had very similar chemical properties. Little River showed high chlorides and specific conductance near Tecumseh, which the author attributes to pollution. Mountain Fork River had lower overall concentrations than the other streams, except for comparable chloride, nitrate, and phosphorus levels. The author concluded that chemistry was not the main driver of production.

**Plankton.** Filtering 150 gallons of water through No. 25 bolting silk revealed no plankton. Dark-and-light bottle oxygen changes over 19 hours were small: a decrease of 0.2 ppm in Mountain Fork River and 0.5 ppm in Blue River. The author interpreted these as non-photosynthetic, probably bacterial, organisms. The author's summary states there was virtually no plankton in any stream.

## Key Findings

**Periphyton**
- Slides in slow-flow (slight) regions yielded about 0.25 x 10⁻⁴ mg C/slide/day regardless of stream.
- A higher reading at Station 3 in Mountain Fork River was attributed to chironomids on the slides.
- Moderate-flow regions averaged 0.72 x 10⁻⁴ mg C/slide/day.

**Primary production (gross, g/m²/day)**
- [[Honey Creek]] Station 3: 2.661 (highest)
- [[Mountain Fork River]] Station 3: 2.17
- [[Blue River]] Station 2: 1.032 (lowest)
- Production could not be measured at Blue River Station 1 because of numerous springs, or in Little River because of low water.

**Benthos**
- Of all animals collected, about three-fourths were insects. Mollusks made up 15 percent and crustaceans about 7 percent. Ninety-five percent of crustaceans were [[Hyalella Azteca]], from one collection at Blue River Station 1.
- Excluding that collection and a few [[Campeloma Decisum]] collections, over 90 percent of animals were insects.
- Riffle fauna were typical swift-water forms, mainly Ephemeroptera, Trichoptera, and Plecoptera.
- Pool debris and silt held Odonata nymphs, Coleoptera, Diptera larvae, Oligochaeta, Hirudinea, Amphipoda, and Isopoda.
- Riffle standing crops (organisms per square foot): Mountain Fork River Station 1 gave 56.3 and 85.0; Blue River Station 1 gave 108.0 and 43.0; Honey Creek Station 3 gave 39.0, 36.0, and 29.0.
- Pool habitats generally yielded under 20 organisms per square foot. The exception was 104 at Blue River Station 1, a spring-fed, watercress-lined margin with reduced velocity.
- Sandy, moderate-flow reaches in Little River and central Blue River were nearly devoid of life, dominated by burrowers, bivalves, and [[Progomphus Obscurus]] nymphs. The author cites [[Gersbacher 1937]], who reported similar scarcity in shifting sand streams in Illinois.

**Controlling factors**
- Consistently high values occurred in riffles and low values in pools for all three indices.
- Edaphic (chemical) factors did not correlate with production. Two Blue River stations with similar chemistry differed in benthos. Blue River Station 1 and Little River Station 1 had similar nutrients but different benthos. Blue River Station 1 and Mountain Fork Station 1 had different chemistry but similar benthos.
- Rate of flow and bottom type were identified as the most important factors, though they were often inseparable.
- Precipitation and watershed retention, through springs and runoff, were the ultimate controls on discharge and velocity.

**Summary conclusions**
1. Primary production, periphyton, and benthos standing crops were comparable indices of production.
2. Greatest benthic production occurred in habitats with rubble or bedrock bottoms, swift current, and stable water levels.
3. Sandy bottoms, slight flow, and extreme water-level fluctuation produced the lowest benthos.
4. The author suggests that in running water, low nutrients may be offset by a steady flow of materials to organisms.

**Limitations.** The author notes that streams are mosaics of habitats, that standing crop does not capture turnover rates, and that only selected components of flora and fauna were measured. Stream production was therefore estimated from a few representative habitats.
