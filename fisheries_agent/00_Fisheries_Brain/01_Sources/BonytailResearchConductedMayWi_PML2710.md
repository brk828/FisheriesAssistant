# Bonytail Chub Research and Handling Protocol Review, Lake Mohave, May 1996

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This memorandum from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Parker Fish and Wildlife Office reports a multi-agency field effort on [[Lake Mohave]] from 12 to 17 May 1996 to capture, transport, and radio-tag [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), an endangered native fish of the [[Colorado River]] system. The trip was judged successful. Several bonytails were captured and moved to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], and nine fish were radio-tagged and released into the lake. The memo also critiques the handling protocol used during the trip and proposes changes to chemical dosing, oxygenation, pickup logistics, size-based decisions, and capture inspection.

## Background & Description

The bonytail is a [[Cyprinidae|cyprinid]] native to the Colorado River basin. It is listed as endangered, and the memo treats its recovery as a coordinated [[Interagency Recovery Program]] effort. The research sought wild adults in Lake Mohave for radio-tagging, along with fish suitable for holding and spawning. Some captured fish were "cove" fish (previously PIT-tagged and stocked), while others were "lake" fish with no tags.

Participants in the field camps included:

- **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS):** 6 personnel, Parker Fish and Wildlife Office, led by [[Stuart Leon]] (Project Leader)
- **Arizona State University (ASU):** 5 personnel, who ran radio-tagging surgery, led by [[Paul Marsh]]
- **National Biological Service (NBS):** 2 personnel, including [[Gordon Mueller]]
- **Bureau of Reclamation (BOR):** a separate camp, with staff including [[Tom Burke]] and [[Glen Gould]]
- **Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD):** a camp near Katherine's Landing, with [[Tom Liles]]
- **Pinetop Fish Health:** [[Ron Majors]], who attended to fish health

Total camp personnel reached 13 (6 FWS, 5 ASU, 2 NBS) by 15 May.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The work took place in Lake Mohave, a reservoir on the Colorado River, and in a backwater known as North AJ. Field sampling used trammel nets, which were expanded from four to eight. Netting sites included:

- Four nets at Cottonwood East
- One net between Cottonwood East and Carp Cove
- One net upstream of Carp Cove
- Two nets near Teguila Cove, in Nevada waters

Nets were checked every six hours, except on one night when weather prevented safe operation. From 15 May, nets were pulled during daylight, so daytime runs were unnecessary. Nets were typically cleared of native fish by 0630 and of all other fish by 1300.

**Bycatch (estimated by the author, not verified counts):**

## Key Findings

### Capture and Transport Results

- Four bonytails were collected from North AJ by ASU and Parker FRO personnel. All were radio-tagged and released.
- The first "lake" bonytail was caught by BOR on the night of 12 May. It was transferred to [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] via a rendezvous with [[Jin Hanson]] on 14 May at 1145.
- The Carp Cove camp captured three more bonytails: one PIT-tagged cove fish (released), one radio-tagged fish (released), and one untagged fish (radio-tagged and released).
- AGFD captured six bonytails, the largest share of the catch, and transported three to Willow Beach. Those fish were PIT-tagged cove fish that had not been free in the lake for any length of time.
- At least three bonytails remained at Willow Beach at the end of the trip.
- Nine fish were radio-tagged and released into Lake Mohave: four cove fish and five lake fish.

### Coordination

Interagency coordination was achieved but had to be adjusted. Cell phone contact between camps often failed because of equipment problems and missed call times. Contact with Willow Beach worked reliably. Mueller visited the BOR camp to resolve a communication gap, and the landline to the AGFD camp worked well.

### Recommended Protocol Changes

1. **Chemical dosing:** Add the chemicals to the water and turn on oxygen when the fish is captured and placed in the holding boat. This takes about one minute and reduces chemical waste.
2. **Oxygenation:** Use supplemental oxygen rather than aeration in small containers. Strong aeration overworks and abrades fish during extended holding. A smaller airstone was considered but would not supply enough oxygen.
3. **Pickup logistics:** The Parker pontoon served as the central pickup boat for bonytails. The other net boats carried containers with a 0.58 percent NaCl solution rather than the full chemical suite. This was judged the most logical arrangement because the boats could reach the pontoon quickly, but it departs from the standard protocol and should be reviewed.
4. **Undersized fish:** The protocol did not specify what to do with a small fish unsuitable for implanting or spawning. The fish was scanned and released after a second opinion from Willow Beach, which declined it.
5. **Brief inspection on capture:** One radio-tagged fish was recaptured, held about two hours, re-anesthetized, and prepared for surgery before the team realized it had been tagged the previous day. The "no handle" rule was followed, but the fish was placed at risk of injury from repeated capture and surgery. The author argues that a quick scan for tags takes only seconds and should be permitted. PIT-tagged bonytails should be scanned, measured, and released in the same way as small razorbacks. The handling time for this step is left to the Region to determine.

### Conclusions

The author judged the trip a success: several bonytails were captured and transported, nine were radio-tagged and released, the chemical protocol was followed with slight modifications, and the cooperation among agencies, especially between the Carp Cove crew and the BOR camp, was a good model for future interagency work.

Comments on the protocol were requested from all camp leaders and interested parties, to be sent to [[George Divine]], Recovery Coordinator, by 1 June 1996. The mailing list included Liles, Burke, Gould, Mueller, and Marsh.
