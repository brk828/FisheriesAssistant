# Sonora Chub (*Gila ditaenia*): Proposed Threatened Listing and Critical Habitat

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of 1984 U.S. regulatory materials, including an Arizona Clearinghouse notice, a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (USFWS) cover letter, and a *Federal Register* proposed rule (Vol. 49, No. 110, June 6, 1984). It proposes listing the [[Sonora Chub]] (*Gila ditaenia*) as a **threatened** species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of 1973, with designation of [[Critical Habitat]]. The only known U.S. population occurs in [[Sycamore Creek]] and [[Yank's Spring]] on the [[Coronado National Forest]]. The species is also known from the [[Rio Magdalena]] in Sonora, Mexico. Its very small range, the threat of exotic fish and parasites, and possible [[Uranium Mining]] are the main justifications. The proposal includes a [[Special Rule]] for take, a critical habitat description, and a request for public comment.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** A member of the minnow family (Cyprinidae). First collected in 1893 from Sycamore Creek, Arizona, by E. A. Mearns. It was first described from specimens collected by R. R. Miller from the Rio Magdalena near La Casita, Sonora.
- **Morphology:** Usually under 125 mm total length. It is moderately chubby and dark-colored, with two prominent black lateral bands and a dark oval spot at the base of the tail. In breeding males the lower fins are red at the base, with some orange on the belly.
- **Behavior and life history:** A pool dweller that is highly secretive. Little is known of its behavior or habitat preferences. Available information is limited to feeding observations on a few individuals and spawning inferred from the presence of young in collections (Minckley, 1973).
- **Prior status:** Included in USFWS Category 2 in the December 30, 1982 Vertebrate Notice of Review. Category 2 covers species thought to need listing but needing more information. Arizona lists the species as threatened (Arizona Game and Fish Commission, 1982).
- **Status report:** In November 1982 the USFWS contracted C. O. Minckley to prepare a status report. It recommended threatened status with critical habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**U.S. habitat.** The species occurs only in Sycamore Creek and its headwater, Yank's Spring. Yank's Spring has perennial flow but has been impounded in a concrete tank for many years. Sycamore Creek begins about ½ mile below the spring and flows about 3.7 miles through a series of pools and small riffles over bedrock and rubble. The creek is intermittent in dry periods, when it becomes a series of pools maintained by underground flow in shaded areas. In heavy rain years, water reaches the International Border about 5 miles downstream.

**Mexican habitat.** The Rio Magdalena near La Casita was a clear stream 4 to 5 feet wide and about 1 foot deep, with fairly swift current over sand and gravel. Watercress was the main vegetation. Specimens were collected at Campo Carretero and Cienega La Atascosa as recently as 1981. Current habitat condition and the species' biology in Mexico are largely unknown.

**Threats (five ESA factors, as assessed):**

- **A. Habitat destruction or curtailment:** Its tiny range makes it very sensitive to siltation, runoff, stream-flow depletion, and pollution from mining or other activities. It could be extirpated from its U.S. range quickly. Sycamore Canyon is largely unaltered. Grazing is in a livestock allotment but has little direct effect due to steep, rocky banks. Exploration for uranium occurred in 1981 on mining claims on the upper eastern slopes of the drainage, and the claims are maintained, though no active mining was planned at the time.
- **B. Overutilization:** No indication of overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific or educational purposes.
- **C. Disease or predation:** Introduced exotic game fish could cause disastrous predation. Green sunfish occur in small numbers in the lower creek, and removal plans were being considered. Parasites introduced with exotic fish are a further threat.
- **D. Inadequate regulatory mechanisms:** Arizona designation offers no habitat protection and no management plan exists. Mexico provides no protection for the species or its habitat.
- **E. Other natural or human factors:** Natural drought could extirpate the population if the water supply failed. Watershed disturbance (grazing, mining, roads, off-road vehicles) and water manipulation (stock tanks, groundwater pumping) can reduce flow.

**Associated species and hybridization.** The [[Tarahumara Frog]] disappeared from Sycamore Canyon after a catastrophic die-off in 1974, possibly due to environmental toxicants. It shares the same waters and may be relevant to the chub's survival. In the Rio Magdalena, the [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) is present alongside the Sonora chub. Collections suggest hybridization may be occurring at least at one site, which could cause extensive losses of Sonora chub if the Yaqui chub spreads.

## Key Findings

1. **Proposed status:** Listing as **threatened**, rather than endangered, because the U.S. population is currently stable and some populations exist in Mexico. The U.S. population has some protection from state laws and Forest Service management policies.
2. **Critical habitat:** The proposal covers the entire known U.S. range. It includes Sycamore Creek from Yank's Spring to the International Border in Santa Cruz County, plus a 25-foot riparian zone around the spring and along each side of the creek. All of the proposed area lies within the Coronado National Forest. The riparian zone is considered essential to the spring and creek ecosystem.
3. **Primary constituent elements:** Clean permanent water with pools and intermediate riffles, and/or intermittent pools maintained by subsurface flow in areas shaded by canyon walls.
4. **Activities of concern:** Collecting or mining during intermittent flow; excessive groundwater pumping, impoundment, or water diversion; chemical or biological pollutants; and introduction of exotic fish or parasites. No federal activities were then planned for the area, so critical habitat designation was not expected to have near-term effects.
5. **Federal protections under the ESA:**
   - Federal agencies must ensure their actions are not likely to jeopardize the species or adversely modify its critical habitat. Formal consultation with the USFWS is required where a federal activity may affect the species.
   - Take, import, export, interstate commerce, and sale are prohibited, as are possession or transport of illegally taken specimens.
6. **Special Rule (Section 4(d)):** The state collecting permit system was judged adequate. Take is allowed without a federal permit for education, scientific, propagation or survival enhancement, zoological exhibition, and other consistent conservation purposes, provided a valid state collecting permit is held. Incidental take by licensed anglers is allowed if the fish is immediately returned to its habitat. Violation of state wildlife laws on this species is also a federal violation.
7. **Economic analysis:** Section 4(b)(2) requires consideration of economic and other impacts. The USFWS was preparing an economic analysis for the final rule and requested data from all interested parties.
8. **Environmental review:** No NEPA documentation was prepared, following a Council on Environmental Quality recommendation based on a Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals decision (*PLF v. Andrus*, 1981).
9. **Public comment:** Comments were due by August 6, 1984. Requests for public hearings were due by July 23, 1984. Comments were sought on biological data, additional populations, current and planned activities, and economic impacts.
10. **Related actions:** Once listed, the USFWS would consider whether the species should be placed on the annex of the Convention on Nature Protection and Wildlife Preservation in the Western Hemisphere.
