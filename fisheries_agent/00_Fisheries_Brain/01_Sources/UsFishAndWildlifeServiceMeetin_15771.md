## Executive Summary
This 1994 working draft from the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] addresses the tension between conserving **native aquatic species** (many protected under the [[Endangered Species Act]]) and maintaining **traditional recreational fisheries**, which are often sustained by [[Nonindigenous Fish Species]]. The document does not represent official Service policy but is a "straw man" position statement intended to guide a facilitated stakeholder workshop ("Achieving Balance") hosted by the [[Sport Fishing And Boating Partnership Council]] in Phoenix, Arizona (September 20–21, 1994). The core thesis: healthy aquatic ecosystems are the best indicator of ecological health and the foundation for both native species conservation and sustainable recreational fishing opportunities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historical introductions**: Eastern game fish species (striped bass, black bass species, American shad) were introduced into Western ecosystems, some into healthy native systems (creating competition) and others into anthropogenically altered habitats such as large reservoirs.
- **Altered habitat niches**: Reservoirs in the West and Midwest, with modified flow/temperature regimes, degraded water quality, and habitat modification, created "newly created niches" exploited by nonindigenous species rather than native fish assemblages.
- **150-year legacy**: Cumulative habitat alteration, fisheries management practices, species introductions, and biological experimentation have produced mixed native/nonindigenous aquatic communities across the U.S. that require active management.
- **Vulnerable native taxa**: Western anadromous salmonids (Pacific Northwest) and native suckers/minnows (Southwest) have been disproportionately impacted, with some species approaching extinction and receiving [[Endangered Species Act]] protection.
- **Recreational economy niche**: Despite ecological costs, modified habitats now support economically valuable recreational fisheries for introduced species, generating local jobs and community revenue via the recreational fishing industry.

## Key Findings

### Guiding Policy Principles
1. **Ecosystem quality limits fish production** — the aquatic ecosystem's capacity to support/produce fish is finite and dependent on ecosystem quality (Principle 6).
2. **Management tool diversity** — recreational fisheries are sustained by a mix of native and nonindigenous species; risks of many nonindigenous introductions remain poorly understood (Principle 7).
3. **Introduction risk assessment required** — new species/hybrid introductions must be ecologically justified and evaluated for impact on native fishes, following [[American Fisheries Society]] guidance (Kohler and Courtenay 1986) (Principle 8; Appendix A).
4. **Habitat alteration as root cause** — habitat loss, combined with nonindigenous species proliferation, is a primary driver of native fish decline; recreational fishing demand has sometimes justified further habitat modification (Principle 9).
5. **Differentiated management by habitat condition** — nonindigenous species remain an important recreational tool in *significantly altered* habitats, while native species conservation/recovery should be the primary emphasis in *relatively unaltered* habitats (Principle 10).
6. **Hatchery role** — hatcheries are valuable management tools only when consistent with fisheries management plans incorporating sound genetics, native fish conservation, disease control, and socio-economic objectives (Principle 11).

### Legal & Institutional Framework
- The [[Endangered Species Act Of 1973]] and its implementing regulations (50 CFR) govern Service obligations for threatened/endangered species conservation, including Section 9 and Section 10 responsibilities.
- [[Nonindigenous Aquatic Nuisance Species Prevention And Control Act]] provides additional legislated authority to the Service.
- States hold primary management authority for most fish and wildlife species; Section 6 grant agreements formalize State-Service cooperation on recovery of listed species.
- Partnerships among States, Tribes, Federal agencies, and private organizations are identified as essential to reconcile conservation and recreational fishing goals.

### AFS Position on Species Introduction (Appendix A; Kohler & Courtenay 1986)
A seven-step evaluation protocol is recommended before any aquatic species introduction:
1. **Rationale** — clear justification for why the import is preferable to native species.
2. **Search** — survey of alternative candidate species with pros/cons documented.
3. **Preliminary Impact Assessment** — evaluation of effects on target ecosystems, game/food fishes, waterfowl, aquatic plants, and public health.
4. **Publicity and Review** — open review process with expert input before proceeding.
5. **Experimental Research** — confined testing (e.g., experimental ponds) prior to open release.
6. **Evaluation/Recommendation** — publication and circulation of research findings.
7. **Introduction** — release only following favorable evaluation, with ongoing monitoring and public reporting.
- The AFS position also recommends restricting importation authority to accountable ornamental fish dealers, prohibiting release of research/display fish into natural systems, and involving international/national/regional regulatory oversight given that aquatic species do not respect political boundaries.

### Outlook & Unresolved Tensions
- The Service explicitly acknowledges the appearance of **internal policy inconsistency**: simultaneously working to conserve endangered native fish while supporting nonindigenous introductions for recreational purposes.
- Future direction includes expanding the National Wildlife Refuge System's recreational fishing opportunities where compatible with refuge purposes, increased funding advocacy, coordination with [[Native American Governments]] per the Service's Native American Policy, and prioritizing issue resolution at the lowest feasible organizational level.
