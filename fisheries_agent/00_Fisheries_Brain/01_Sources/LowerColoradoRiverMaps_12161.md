# Colorado River Front Work and Levee System Operation and Maintenance Program

#river-ecology #habitat-management #sediment-dynamics #water-resources #fish-and-wildlife

## Executive Summary

This 1969 Bureau of Reclamation document describes the comprehensive Operation and Maintenance (O&M) Program for the [[Lower Colorado River]] below [[Davis Dam]], implemented under the Colorado River Front Work and Levee System Act of 1927. The program addresses channel instability, sediment deposition, flooding risks, and water loss resulting from dam construction ([[Hoover Dam]], [[Imperial Dam]], [[Parker Dam]], [[Davis Dam]]). The river below Davis Dam is divided into ten operational divisions: [[Mohave Valley Division]], [[Topock Gorge Division]], [[Havasu Division]], [[Parker Division]], [[Palo Verde Division]], [[Cibola Division]], [[Imperial Division]], [[Laguna Division]], [[Yuma Division]], and [[Limitrophe Division]]. The program employs multiple-purpose planning to achieve channel stabilization, water salvage, sediment control, and enhancement of fish and wildlife habitat and recreational opportunities.

## Background & Description

Before regulation, the [[Colorado River]] flowed unchecked from the Rocky Mountains to the Gulf of California, alternating between devastating spring floods and minimal late-fall flows. Construction of major dams (Hoover, Imperial, Parker, Davis) regulated flow but created secondary problems. Each reservoir trapped sediment, releasing clear water that scoured downstream channels and redeposited sediment at the head of the next reservoir. [[Topock Gorge]] and the reach above Imperial Dam experienced extensive sediment accumulation, raising riverbeds. At [[Needles]], portions of the city became inundated, prompting emergency legislation in 1946 that expanded the Colorado River Front Work and Levee System Act authority, enabling the Bureau of Reclamation to initiate active O&M operations.

Initial goals focused on preventing flooding and land destruction from river meandering and sediment deposition. Objectives expanded to include water salvage, stabilization and enhancement of fish and wildlife habitat, and improvement of recreational opportunities. The program employs comprehensive multiple-purpose planning to maximize overall public benefits rather than single-purpose objectives.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The regulated river system created dynamic ecological challenges. Sediment transport patterns changed fundamentally: clear water released from reservoirs eroded downstream channels, transporting material to depositional zones in reservoir backwaters. [[Topock Marsh]], formed after Parker Dam closure and Lake Havasu filling in 1938, encompasses approximately 4,000 acres of open water at elevation 455 feet, managed as part of [[Havasu National Wildlife Refuge]].

Backwater areas throughout the system provide critical fish and wildlife habitat. However, sediment accumulation progressively fills or isolates these areas, reducing depth to 2-3 feet and allowing cattails and tules to overrun open water. Natural dike formation isolates some backwaters from freshening flows, risking stagnant, saline conditions. The program addresses these issues through selective dredging to deepen backwaters (e.g., 300 acres in Topock Gorge, 200 acres in [[Mittry Lake]], 72 acres in [[Yuma Island]] area, 500 acres in Parker Division), installation of culverts for freshening flows, and construction of inlet/outlet structures.

[[Lake Havasu]] presented unique hazards from submerged trees (snags) not cleared before Parker Dam closure in 1938. As recreational use intensified, particularly boating and waterskiing, snags contributed to accidental deaths. A snag removal program initiated in 1964 was scheduled for completion in 1970.

Fish species mentioned in supplementary material include [[Walleye]], [[Largemouth Bass]] (Largemouth Black Bass), [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Bluegill]], indicating warm-water sport fishery management objectives.

## Key Findings

**Mohave Valley Division**: Channel stabilization initiated 1949 with dredging between Needles and Topock, plus levee construction. A settling basin above Topock traps sediment using a 12-inch dredge. Work achieved channel stability and controlled water levels at Needles. Benefits: 109,000 acre-feet annual water salvage, 121,000 tons/year sediment reduction into Topock Gorge. [[Topock Marsh]] maintained at 4,000 acres for [[Havasu National Wildlife Refuge]]. [[Park Moabi Marina]] developed below Needles.

**Topock Gorge Division**: Heavy sediment deposits in gorge and upper Lake Havasu required channel dredging. Modified plan (August 1967) limited initial work to upper 1.7 miles and lower 4.0 miles. Work suspended June 1968 due to opposition from outdoor groups; further ecological evaluation ordered. Full plan benefits: permanent channel stability Needles to Lake Havasu, 28,000 acre-feet annual water salvage, assurance of 109,000 acre-feet salvage in Mohave Valley, lowered water levels at Topock for marsh management, sediment control in backwaters, deepening of 300 acres of backwaters for fishery habitat.

**Havasu Division**: Newly established division covering Lake Havasu and river from Parker to Headgate Rock Dams. Primary focus: snag removal program to eliminate hazards from submerged trees, scheduled for completion 1970.

**Parker Division**: Active bank erosion, meandering, braiding, and sediment deposition create shallow, unstable conditions. Erosion reaches 45 feet/year laterally in some areas. Approximately 73% of sediment load trapped in [[Imperial Reservoir]], remainder removed by [[All-American Canal Desilting Works]] and [[Laguna Settling Basin]]. Channel improvement work above [[Alligator Bend]] completed 1967, enabling recreational development by [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]]. Below Alligator Bend: planned dredging for deeper channel, improved alinement, stabilized banklines. 500 acres of backwater to be deepened for fish/wildlife/recreation; 100 acres of isolated channel preserved for fish spawning/feeding. Benefits: 24,200 acre-feet annual water salvage, 788,000 tons/year sediment reduction. Comprehensive plan approved September 19, 1969, but remaining work deferred due to uncertainty over Colorado River Indian Reservation western boundary.

**Palo Verde Division**: Channel stabilization essentially complete using earthfill training structures and bank protective riprap. Backwater areas behind structures improved for fish and wildlife with culvert installation for freshening flows. Dredge "Little Colorado" deepening off-channel areas per fish and game agency plans. Benefits: 10,000 acre-feet annual water salvage, 310,000 tons/year sediment reduction.

**Cibola Division**: Shallow natural channel from sediment deposition. Active dredging and levee construction program. Major fish and wildlife features include inlet/outlet structures for [[Oxbow Lake]] south of Palo Verde Townsite (completed), with plans for [[Three Fingers Lake]] and [[Cibola Lake]]. Benefits: 36,000 acre-feet annual water salvage, 350,000 tons/year sediment reduction into Imperial Division.

**Imperial Division**: Diversion pool and backwater areas behind Imperial Dam receive sediment from Parker, Palo Verde, and Cibola Divisions. 73% deposits on sandbars or in backwater lakes, 27% diverted at Imperial Dam. Diverted sediment removed by Desilting Works, returned below dam, dredged to permanent storage near Laguna Settling Basin. Backwater areas progressively filled or isolated by sedimentation; trap efficiency decreasing. Short reach immediately upstream from dam full of sediment, creating diversion difficulties for [[Gila Canal]]. Comprehensive planning not yet initiated; focus remains on reducing sediment inflow. Reconnaissance studies indicate 110,000 acre-feet/year water losses, potentially reducible by 15,000 acre-feet/year.

**Laguna Division**: Receives sediment bypassed by Imperial sluice gates and from All-American Canal Desilting Works. Settling basin constructed where sediment trapped and pumped to dry land storage by dredge. Removal necessary for years but will diminish as upstream work takes effect. Benefits: 10,000 acre-feet annual water salvage from covering marshy areas with dredged material.

**Yuma Division**: Channel formed by moderate flows no longer hydraulically efficient for typical 100-1,000 cfs flows. Plans call for dredging to lower riverbed, provide flatter gradients, narrower section, reducing water surface area and increasing average depth. Fish and wildlife improvements at Mittry Lake (200 acres deepened) and Yuma Island (72 acres deepened), plus boating, picnicking, and sanitary facilities. Benefits: 22,000 acre-feet annual water salvage from lowered water table reducing phreatophyte water use and enabling clearing. Comprehensive plan approved August 15, 1969 by Acting Secretary Dole; work to begin when funds available.

**Limitrophe Division**: River no longer important for irrigation conveyance but inadequate flood capacity requires future action. Planning not undertaken due to higher upstream priorities. River forms International Boundary; all work must be coordinated with Mexico through [[International Boundary and Water Commission]].

**Total Program Benefits**: Cumulative annual water salvage across divisions: 348,200 acre-feet. Cumulative annual sediment reduction: 1,569,000 tons. Extensive fish and wildlife habitat preservation and enhancement through backwater deepening, freshening flow provision, and marsh management. Recreational facility development including marinas, boat access, picnicking, and camping areas.
