# The Fishery at Sutherland Reservoir, San Diego County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] Inland Fisheries Branch documents the sport fishery of [[Sutherland Reservoir]], a warmwater impoundment completed in 1953 on [[Santa Ysabel Creek]] near Ramona. It covers 1955 to 1960 and combines a [[Creel Census]], [[Tag-Recapture Method|tag-recapture]] mortality estimates, and [[Age and Growth]] analyses for [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]. Contrary to the typical reservoir pattern of an early peak followed by decline, angling success and total catch rose steadily through 1960. The authors conclude that overall catch per hour is not a reliable index of bass population size and that the pre-impoundment [[Rotenone Pretreatment]] of the drainage had doubtful value.

## Background & Description

**Reservoir setting.** Sutherland is part of the City of San Diego's domestic water supply. It lies at about 2,000 ft elevation in the Upper Sonoran Life Zone, surrounded by chaparral. The reservoir has a maximum area of 660 acres and a maximum depth of 162 ft. During the study, its greatest area and depth were about 280 acres and 94 ft. Limited inflow and release kept annual level fluctuation small, except after two heavy-rain releases.

**Climate and thermal regime.** Summer daily maxima are about 95 °F, with occasional periods over 100 °F. Maximum water temperature is about 80 °F in late July and early August, and minimum is about 50 °F in December and January. The reservoir stratifies in summer and early fall, with the [[Thermocline]] at 15 to 25 ft. The hypolimnion is oxygen-depleted and contains hydrogen sulfide, while the epilimnion holds more than 4.0 ppm oxygen. Secchi readings were 18 to 52 inches in late fall 1960.

**Recreation and management.** The City of San Diego permits angling, boating, picnicking, and waterfowl hunting, with angling the main use. A $1 daily fee per adult angler helps cover costs. Regulations changed over time:

- 1955: angling restricted to half the shoreline, no boat fishing.
- 1957: entire lake opened; rental boats available late 1957.
- 1958: private boats permitted.
- 1959: 10-inch minimum length for bass.
- Daily bag limits: 5 bass and 10 catfish per angler.

**Stocking and introductions.**

| Year | Introduction |
|---|---|
| Fall 1953 | Drainage treated with rotenone before impoundment |
| Spring 1954 | About 15,000 largemouth bass fry, 1,500 golden shiner fry, 900 adult golden shiners |
| Fall 1956 | 160 adult bluegills |
| Spring 1957 | 2,450 adult [[Threadfin Shad]] |
| February 1960 | 800 [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] yearlings (evaluation) |

Green sunfish and brown bullheads survived the rotenone treatment and reproduced. Bass fry stocked in 1954 showed excellent growth, and about one-third were eventually caught.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species interactions.** Green sunfish dominated catches in 1955 and 1956, then declined rapidly and were insignificant by 1958. The authors judged this more likely due to inability to compete with [[Largemouth Bass]] in warm reservoirs than to competition with bluegills, since the decline was already under way before bluegills were added. Bluegills entered the fishery in 1957, became dominant by 1958, and comprised most of the catch in 1959 and 1960. The effects of bluegills and threadfin shad on bass cannot be separated, because both were introduced between the 1956 and 1957 seasons. The faster growth of the 1958 bass year-class may reflect a larger food supply from these introductions or from generally larger reservoir size that year.

**Angler population.** San Diego County residents made up 91 to 96 percent of anglers each year, with most others from the Los Angeles area, about 100 miles away. About 20 percent of anglers were under 16 years old. In 1960, roughly 35 percent fished exclusively for bass, 30 percent exclusively for panfish, and 35 percent for both. Bass anglers caught 0.8 bass and 0.1 bluegill per day, while panfish anglers caught 0.01 bass and 7.6 bluegills per day.

**Limnological influences.** The authors propose that bass vulnerability varied with water temperature and other limnological conditions, which contributed to differences in success between seasons and years. Low water turnover may have retained nutrients and sustained high yields.

## Key Findings

**Fishery trends (1956 to 1960)**
- Angler days rose from 5,732 in 1956 to 21,304 in 1960.
- Catch per hour: 0.32, 0.15, 0.49, 0.68, and 0.87 for 1956 through 1960.
- Pounds of fish per angler day: 0.69, 0.47, 1.05, 1.20, and 1.67.
- Total catch in 1960 was 114,522 fish (about 224 lb/acre harvested), including 11,993 largemouth bass and 101,009 bluegills.
- The bass share of the catch was 10.5 percent in 1959 and 1960 and reached 54.8 percent in 1957.

**Largemouth bass population and mortality**
- Annual total mortality averaged almost 0.7, with exploitation about 0.4 from 1957 through 1959 and only 0.2 in 1956.
- The authors concluded that these rates were not harmful, since the population expanded and 1960 had the greatest catch and average size.
- Instantaneous fishing mortality roughly followed angling pressure through 1958 but did not rise with the 1959 and 1960 increases; the authors offered no full explanation for the decline in the April to September harvest rate in those years.
- Mean bass weights for 1956 to 1960 were 1.22, 1.03, 0.78, 1.06, and 1.38 lb.
- Age I and II fish dominated most years, with Age III fish exceeding one-third of the catch only in 1957.

**Population size and catch per unit effort**
- Overall catch per hour and early-season bass catch per hour were not correlated with estimated population size (Table 10).
- Daily success during the first 10 fishing days showed no consistent trend. This conflicted with midwestern studies that reported consistent declines attributed to bass becoming wary.
- The authors attribute the weak link to changing angler type, short-term differences in availability, and temperature-linked vulnerability.

**Growth**
- Bass length–scale relation: Y = 0.62 + 0.062X (linear from 7 to 18 inches).
- Mean bass fork lengths at annulus formation: Age I 11.4, Age II 14.3, Age III 16.3, Age IV 18.1 inches.
- Bluegill mean lengths at the end of years 1, 2, and 3: 2.2, 5.2, and 7.3 inches.
- Growth is better than average but not exceptional compared with other waters and well below maximum Oklahoma rates.

**Comparisons and conclusions**
- Bass total mortality at Sutherland exceeds most reported values (Table 13), yet the population expanded.
- The green sunfish decline and the incomplete bullhead kill cast doubt on the value of [[Rotenone Pretreatment]] before impoundment.
- The study concludes that heterogeneous sport-fishery catch per unit effort is not a meaningful population index. The authors also judged the 1956 bass year-class and the 1958 year-class differences to be linked to introductions and food supply.

**Overall**
- The fishery improved over six years instead of following the common early-peak-then-decline reservoir pattern.
- The bass population expanded despite high mortality and exploitation and a growing bluegill population.
