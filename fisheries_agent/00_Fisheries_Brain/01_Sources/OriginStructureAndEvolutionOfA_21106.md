# Origin, Structure, and Evolution of a Reattachment Bar, Colorado River, Grand Canyon, Arizona

#sedimentology #fluvial-geomorphology #reattachment-bar

## Executive Summary

This sedimentological study by [[David M. Rubin]], [[John C. Schmidt]], and [[Johnnie N. Moore]] examines a [[Reattachment Bar]] in a channel expansion of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona. The paper describes how [[Recirculation Zone]] flow deposits sand at the point where flow reattaches to the bank, how the internal structure of the bar develops, and how the bar evolves over time. It also argues that the same processes shape alluvial channels, including [[Point Bar]] and [[Concave Bench]] deposits. The paper is a research article published in the *Journal of Sedimentary Petrology* (1990). It contains no fish or aquatic-biology data. The aquatic content is limited to flow hydraulics and sediment processes.

## Background & Description

Recirculation zones form in channel expansions where flow separates from the bank and then reattaches to it. High-velocity flow from the constricted channel strikes the bank at the reattachment point, decelerates, and deposits sand. Deposition is also induced near the center of the main eddy, in secondary eddies, and in nearly stagnant flow just downstream of the separation point.

The authors use the following terms for deposits within these zones:

- **Separation deposits:** mantle the debris fan near the separation point.
- **Reattachment deposits:** centered on or highest at the reattachment point.
- **Eddy-center deposits:** highest near the center of the eddy.
- **Eddy bar:** a composite bar, following [[Baker]]'s usage.

Only the reattachment component is treated in detail. Previous work on flood deposits in bedrock gorges includes [[McKee]] (1938), [[Howard and Dolan]] (1981), and [[Baker]] et al. (1983). Recirculation deposits in alluvial settings were described by [[Taylor]] et al. (1971), [[Leeder and Bridges]] (1975), and [[Page and Nanson]] (1982).

The study site lies about 1 km upstream of Kwagunt Rapids, downstream of a debris fan deposited by a small ephemeral tributary. The fan constricts the main channel. The bar has existed since at least 1965, although its surface has been eroded at times.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The Colorado River flows about 400 km through Grand Canyon, from Lees Ferry to Lake Mead. The average slope is 1.5 m/km, and Froude numbers are commonly below 0.1 except in major rapids.

**Dam effects.** [[Glen Canyon Dam]] closed in 1963, reducing natural high flows and sediment transport:

- 1963–1965: flows were very low while the reservoir filled.
- Until May 1983: daily fluctuations from lows of 30–150 m³/s to highs of 400–900 m³/s.
- Summer 1983: peak flow of about 2,800 m³/s.
- 1984–1986: highs of roughly 1,500 m³/s.
- 1987: daily fluctuations driven by dam releases.

**Regional context.** The authors identified 91 reattachment bars in the first 170 km downstream of Lees Ferry. The study bar is larger than average but otherwise representative.

**Vegetation.** Aerial photos from 1980 show a marsh with [[Tamarisk]] trees 1–2 m above the present surface. High flow in 1983 eroded the marsh.

## Key Findings

1. **Origin of the bar.** Sand deposition at reattachment points creates the bar. Deposition is strongest where velocity drops, in the eddy center and along the eddy line.

2. **Discharge controls geometry.** Recirculation zones lengthen as discharge increases, mainly because reattachment points migrate downstream rather than because separation points migrate upstream. At very low discharge, reattachment points retreat upstream and the bar's downstream end is eroded. Flow direction at any single point on the bar therefore changes with stage.

3. **Bar topography.** A transect across the bar shows four features: an [[Eddy-Return Channel]], a linear ridge, a main platform, and an accretionary bank. The main platform projects upcurrent near the reattachment point. Its advance direction is opposite to that of a normal spit.

4. **Cross-bedding.** Foresets are up to 1.5 m thick in trenches, and sets up to 4 m thick could form if the bar advanced into deeper water. Two kinds of foresets occur:
   - Avalanche foresets dip at the angle of repose and contain internal shear surfaces.
   - Fluctuating-discharge foresets contain mud or organic drapes and dip at lower angles. Their regular thickness reflects daily discharge cycles.

5. **Reversing-flow ripples.** Symmetrical ripples with crests trending normal to the bank form where the reattachment point shifts up and downstream. Observed excursions ranged from a few meters to tens of meters, and their duration ranged from a few seconds to more than ten minutes. These ripples behave like [[Oscillation Ripples]] and should not be mistaken for wave ripples. Low migration rates combined with rapid deposition produce steep climb angles.

6. **Lateral pattern of ripples.** Ripple migration directions change systematically with position:
   - Upstream of the reattachment point, near the bank: asymmetric ripples migrating upstream.
   - At the reattachment point: symmetrical, reversing, or bankward-migrating ripples.
   - Downstream of the reattachment point: asymmetric ripples migrating downstream.

   Upstream-climbing ripples are very common in these bars because the upstream part of the recirculation zone has high preservation potential.

7. **Recognition criteria.** Reattachment bars are identified by rotary flow patterns (including upstream flow) and by symmetrical, reversing, vertically climbing ripples. The authors argue that these deposits should be an identifiable subfacies of alluvial channels.

8. **Grain size.** Dune and bar foresets average 0.28 mm, while climbing-ripple beds average 0.20 mm.

9. **Depositional history.**
   - 1983: high flows deposited coarse cross-bedded sand (unit R1) and built a higher central platform. That platform was later eroded to form a truncation surface.
   - 1983–1986: the ridge was overtopped during late-spring floods. The authors identified three subaqueous-eolian couplets, although four were expected for 1983–86.
   - 1986–1987: low flows deposited muddy rippled sediment. About 15 cm accumulated on the central platform between April and October 1987.

10. **Idealized sequence and its limits.** Under steady conditions, bars evolve from a scoured surface, to massive suspension beds, to dune foresets, then to ripples, mud, and marsh. Restricted flow eventually replaces dunes with ripples and fines the grain size. Actual evolution on the Colorado River is more complicated because discharge has decreased since 1983, and seasonal and daily fluctuations further complicate the pattern.

11. **Relevance to alluvial rivers.** The same processes operate on [[Point Bar]] surfaces in meandering channels, on concave benches, in the lee of emergent dunes, and at the mouths of abandoned channels and tributaries. Recirculation deposits trap sediment and can be preserved in the rock record, unlike bars in bedrock canyons, which are rarely preserved.

12. **Conclusions.** Recirculation deposits are characterized by rotary flow with upstream components. Reattachment bars are diagnosed by their climbing ripples, which are symmetrical, reversing, and vertically climbing at the reattachment point and grade laterally into asymmetric ripples. Under steady conditions, reattachment bars fine upward as flow becomes more restricted.
