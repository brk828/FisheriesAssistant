# Lower Colorado River Basin "Big River Fishes" Management Prospectus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This preliminary draft (February 1995) from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Arizona Fishery Resources Office is a management prospectus for the four endangered "big river" fishes of the lower [[Colorado River]] basin: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], and [[Humpback Chub]]. It was written to complement the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Recovery Implementation Plan]], because the lower basin holds comparatively larger populations of three of the species and because development there has largely stabilized. The prospectus proposes a cooperative interagency, intergovernmental, and water-user management framework built on ecosystem-level habitat improvement, genetic management, refugia and captive propagation, and evaluation of management actions. The letter accompanying the draft requests written comments and announces at least two stakeholder workshops in Yuma and Lake Havasu City or Boulder City.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] drains about 650,000 square kilometers of western North America, roughly one-twelfth of the contiguous United States, across portions of seven states and a small part of northwestern Mexico. Historically a river of extremes, with torrential floods alternating with prolonged drought, it was once a mix of slow, turbid valley reaches and turbulent canyon rapids to which native fishes were adapted. The watershed is divided at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] into upper and lower basins. The lower basin comprises the Colorado mainstem and the Paria, Little Colorado, Virgin, Bill Williams, and Gila river sub-basins.

The four big river fishes evolved under these conditions and attain large sizes and great ages. Colorado squawfish is a near-shore ambush predator, with a large head, raptorial pharyngeal teeth, and solitary habitat. Its prey species show high reproductive potential, rapid growth, and adaptations for escaping predation, such as hydrodynamic shapes, falcate fins, and powerful musculature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document describes the river as one of the most altered aquatic ecosystems in North America. Hydroelectric power generation, irrigation, and livestock grazing have reshaped its ecology. Key pressures identified include:

- **Dams:** they block historic flood corridors, release water too cold for indigenous spawning, and remove natural hydrologic variability, which is described as the likely most significant impact.
- **Non-native predators:** predation on eggs, larvae, and juveniles by introduced fishes is identified as a major factor in the decline of indigenous species.
- **Habitat loss:** most dam sites are in use and few free-flowing reaches remain. Floods that once created oxbows and backwaters no longer occur, so habitat must be created and maintained artificially.

Native fish are also threatened by hybridization and genetic erosion. Refugia such as the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], and [[Bubbling Ponds Fish Hatchery]] hold captive broodstock.

## Key Findings

**Species status and distribution**

- All four species are federally listed as endangered. Critical habitat is designated for three of them downstream of Glen Canyon Dam. Colorado squawfish has none in the lower basin, having been extirpated there in the 1960s.
- **Colorado squawfish:** present in the lower basin only as captive broodstock at Dexter and as "experimental, nonessential" populations in the Salt and Verde rivers. These populations cannot directly contribute to recovery under the Endangered Species Act.
- **Bonytail:** occurs infrequently in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. Its rarity requires first acquiring germplasm and broodfish. Captive broodstock is held at Dexter and Willow Beach.
- **Humpback chub:** occurs in the Colorado River and lower Little Colorado River. It is far more common than bonytail. Management is partly addressed by an environmental impact study under the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]].
- **Razorback sucker:** the largest population occurs in Lake Mohave, with a further population in Lake Mead. Individuals are also captured occasionally in the mainstem and downstream of Davis Dam.

**Goals and objectives**

- Goal 1: establish and maintain genetically diverse populations of big river fishes throughout the lower basin.
- Goal 2: stabilize and maintain self-perpetuating populations of all four species.
- Objectives run from preventing extinction and perpetuating genetic variability through stabilizing, expanding, and self-sustaining populations, to downlisting or delisting.

**Management actions**

- A management team and coordinator will be established. Its members will come from federal, state, and private organizations.
- **Genetic management:** maximize the number of individuals contributing to recruitment. Collect wild-caught razorback sucker larvae at different times and places in Lake Mohave, using their phototactic response. Where wild larvae are unavailable, use strict pedigree breeding. Preserve germplasm through cryopreservation, and verify donor genetics with genomic analysis.
- **Fish health:** examine natural and semi-natural habitats for pathogens before renovation and stocking. Monitor both captive and stocked fish.
- **Refugia:** manage isolated coves and embayments by removing resident fishes and installing exclusion barriers. Stocked fish are removed at about 300 mm total length for augmentation of wild populations or for release into the reserve.
- **Habitat:** periodic dredging and vegetation control will be needed, and fish habitat maintenance should be integrated into [[National Wildlife Refuge]] management plans.
- **Hydrographs:** study the effects of controlled water releases on target and non-target fishes.
- **Expansion:** create large isolated refugia (80 ha or larger) on national wildlife refuges within five years. Restore dispersal corridors and free-flowing reaches.

**Evaluation and partners**

- All management actions will be tracked, monitored, and evaluated as an integral part of study plans.
- Specific study protocols must be approved by the Arizona Fishery Resources Office before implementation.
- Appendix A lists indigenous fishes across five bio-regions of the lower basin, with their listing status. Appendix B lists potential partner agencies, including federal, state, tribal, academic, and non-governmental organizations. **Document status**

- The prospectus is a preliminary draft, offered as a starting point for stakeholder collaboration. It has not been finalized.
