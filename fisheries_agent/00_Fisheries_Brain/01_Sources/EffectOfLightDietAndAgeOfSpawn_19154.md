# Effect of Light, Diet, and Age of Spawning Brown Trout Upon Certain Characteristics of Their Eggs and Fry

#ichthyology #aquaculture #Salmonidae #hatchery-biology #egg-development

## Executive Summary

This experimental study investigated how light exposure, diet composition, and spawner age affect the physical and chemical characteristics of [[Brown Trout]] ([[Salmo trutta]]) eggs and fry at the [[Cortland Fish Hatchery]]. Four groups of spawning trout were maintained under different conditions: (1) first-spawning fish on standard diet under natural light (control), (2) first-spawning fish on pellet-and-meat diet under natural light, (3) first-spawning fish on standard diet under controlled light (advanced spawning by one month), and (4) second-spawning fish on standard diet under controlled light. Results demonstrated that light, nutrition, and spawner age all influenced egg weight and diameter, but only nutrition and spawner age affected chemical composition. Eggs from second-spawning fish were heaviest and largest, while eggs from light-controlled first-spawners were smallest. The pellet-and-meat diet produced eggs with significantly higher fat content and lower protein content compared to the standard hatchery diet.

## Background & Description

[[Brown Trout]] egg development has long been of interest to fisheries workers, though experimental evidence on physical changes during incubation remained limited. Previous work by Tingley (1941) reported slight volume increases in [[Atlantic Salmon]] eggs during incubation, while Phillips et al. (1955) described weight decreases and chemical changes in trout eggs. This study addressed gaps in understanding how brood stock history affects offspring characteristics.

Four experimental groups were established using brown trout spawners:
- **Group 1 (Control)**: First-spawning trout, standard hatchery diet (27% each beef liver and spleen, 11% each whitefish meal, distiller's solubles, wheat middlings, cottonseed meal, 2% salt), natural light
- **Group 2**: First-spawning trout, pellet-and-meat diet (Cortland pellets five days/week, beef liver-spleen mixture two days/week), natural light
- **Group 3**: First-spawning trout, standard diet, controlled light (spawning advanced ~1 month)
- **Group 4**: Second-spawning trout, standard diet, controlled light

Replicated populations of 6,000 eggs each were incubated at constant 47°F. Weekly samples (300 eggs for weeks 1-7, 300 fry for weeks 8-10) were collected starting one week post-spawning. Eggs reached "eyed" stage at 4 weeks and began hatching at 7 weeks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study was conducted entirely in hatchery conditions at the [[Cortland Fish Hatchery]], New York, representing controlled aquaculture rather than natural ecosystem conditions. Water temperature was maintained at constant 47°F throughout incubation. The research addresses practical hatchery management questions relevant to [[Salmonid]] production.

Light manipulation was used to advance spawning timing by approximately one month, demonstrating the role of [[Photoperiod]] in reproductive timing. This technique has applications for hatchery production scheduling.

Fungus control was accomplished manually (picking dead eggs) rather than chemical treatment to avoid confounding effects on egg chemistry—an important methodological consideration for water quality in hatchery systems.

## Key Findings

### Physical Characteristics

**Egg Weight and Diameter Stability During Incubation:**
- No significant changes in weight or diameter occurred within populations during the 7-week incubation period
- Fluctuations between weekly samples were equal to or less than fluctuations within replicate samples
- This contradicts some earlier hypotheses about developmental size changes

**Between-Group Differences (Highly Significant):**
- **Group 4** (second-spawning, controlled light): Heaviest and largest eggs
  - Weight: 17.8-18.8 grams per 300 eggs
  - Diameter: 0.182-0.186 inches
- **Group 1** (first-spawning, standard diet, natural light): Intermediate
  - Weight: 16.0-17.2 grams per 300 eggs
  - Diameter: 0.175-0.178 inches
- **Group 2** (first-spawning, pellet-meat diet, natural light): Smaller
  - Weight: 14.2-15.7 grams per 300 eggs
  - Diameter: 0.169-0.171 inches
- **Group 3** (first-spawning, standard diet, controlled light): Smallest
  - Weight: 12.6-13.7 grams per 300 eggs
  - Diameter: 0.162-0.166 inches

The smallest eggs from light-controlled first-spawners may reflect shortened egg growing period (one month less) forcing premature maturation.

### Chemical Composition

**Diet Effects (Statistically Significant):**
- Pellet-and-meat diet (Group 2) vs. standard diet (Group 1):
  - **Higher fat content** (wet and dry basis)
  - **Lower protein content** (dry basis)
  - Similar ash percentages (wet and dry basis)
  - Similar water content

**Light Control Effects:**
- Controlled light (Group 3) vs. natural light (Group 1) showed **no differences** in chemical composition when both received standard diet
- Light exposure affected physical size but not chemistry

**Spawner Age Effects:**
- First-spawning (Group 3) vs. second-spawning (Group 4) fish on same diet and light regime:
  - Differed only in water and fat percentages (wet basis)
  - Fat percentages similar on dry basis
  - Water content higher in second-spawners, accounting for apparent fat difference

### Dietary Analysis and Fat Deposition Mechanism

**Carbohydrate Hypothesis:**
- Pellet-and-meat diet: 23.0% total carbohydrate (15.0% available)
- Standard diet: 17.7% total carbohydrate (11.3% available)
- Digestible carbohydrate: 7.0-8.3% (pellet-meat) vs. 4.3% (standard)
- Higher digestible carbohydrate plus higher protein may drive increased fat deposition in eggs

**Protein Considerations:**
- Pellet-and-meat diet slightly higher in protein than standard diet
- Excessive dietary protein or poor-quality protein may convert to fat
- Total protein in pellet-meat diet: 28.5% vs. 26.8% in standard diet

**Carbohydrate Digestibility:**
- Lactose (from dried skim milk): 60% digestible by trout
- Uncooked starch: 38% digestible
- Cooked starch: 47% digestible
- Pelleting process may cook starch, increasing digestibility

### Management Implications

The study demonstrates that brood stock history significantly affects offspring characteristics:
1. **Light exposure, nutrition, and spawner age** all determine relative egg weight and size
2. **Nutrition and spawner age** (but not light) affect chemical composition
3. Diet formulation could potentially be optimized to produce eggs with specific chemical profiles for improved survival and hardiness

### Methodological Notes

- Egg diameter measured using [[Von Bayer]] method (1910) with conversion table
- Three replicate measurements per sample, averaged
- Surface water removed by rolling on filter paper before weighing
- Chemical analyses: water (vacuum oven drying), fat (continuous ether extraction), ash (ignition at 600°C), protein (micro-Hengar method)
- All chemical values represent averages of duplicate analyses on duplicate samples
- Statistical significance established by analysis of variance
