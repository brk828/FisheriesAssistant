# Exotic Fish Species in the Fresh Waters of Iran

#ichthyology #marine-biology #exotic-fishes

## Executive Summary

Coad and Abdoli (1993, *Zoology in the Middle East* 9) review records of exotic and transplanted fishes in Iranian fresh waters. At least 24 species are reported, and the paper gives the first Iranian records of the [[Pseudorasbora Parva]] (Stone Moroko) and the [[Threespine Stickleback]]. Introductions were made for food, sport, research, ornamentation, weed control and health (mosquito control), or occurred accidentally during these activities. The authors argue that exotics threaten native fishes through parasites, disease, competition, predation and habitat change, and they recommend tighter import controls, education, monitoring and an end to open-water stocking.

## Background & Description

The paper defines an **exotic** as a species introduced from another country, and a **transplant** as a native Iranian species moved into a drainage where it did not naturally occur. Both are treated as hazards. The authors expect the number of exotics and transplants to rise with more efficient transport, growing demand for protein, government and commercial fish farms, and the aquarium trade. Petr (1987) documented a rapid expansion of Iranian fish farming and the distribution of fingerlings, especially [[Chinese Major Carps]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], across the country.

The species accounts are grouped by order and family. Identification notes are given for the salmonids, which are difficult to separate. For example, *Coregonus lavaretus* has fewer than 100 lateral-line scales and a strongly forked tail, while other salmonids have over 120 scales and a truncate tail. Among the Pacific and Atlantic salmonids, *Oncorhynchus keta* has 13–17 anal-fin rays, against 12 or fewer in other salmons and trouts.

The species treated, roughly 28 in total, include:

- **Cyprinids:** [[Grass Carp]], [[Common Carp]], [[Silver Carp]], [[Bighead Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Common Sawbelly]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Pseudorasbora Parva]].
- **Salmonids:** [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Common Whitefish]], and [[Chum Salmon]].
- **Predatory and other species:** [[Pike]], [[Pike-Perch]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Indian Stinging Catfish]], and [[Brown Bullhead]].
- **Mosquito-control and marine-origin species:** [[Mosquitofish]], [[Threespine Stickleback]], [[Nile Tilapia]], and the Mugilidae and Pleuronectidae species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors stress that the risk of exotics is greatest in the desert regions of Iran, where species diversity is low and a single introduction can alter the whole ecosystem. The Caspian basin, the [[Namak Lake]] basin, the [[Sistan Basin]], and the [[Tigris River Basin]] are the main areas of introduction. Impacts described in the text include:

- **Disease and parasites:** Grass Carp carries the tapeworm [[Bothriocephalus Acheilognathi]], which has spread to other cyprinids in Europe and the former U.S.S.R. Pike and Pike-Perch carry [[Diphyllobothrium Latum]] in their muscle tissue, a parasite that infects humans who eat poorly cooked fish.
- **Predation:** Eels, Pike, Pike-Perch, Chum Salmon, Brook Trout, Largemouth Bass and sticklebacks all prey on native fishes or their eggs and young.
- **Competition and habitat change:** Grass Carp removes vegetation that native fishes need for spawning and feeding. Common Carp muddies the water and uproots plants. Goldfish compete for food and space.
- **Genetic and reproductive effects:** Female Goldfish in Europe use the sperm of other cyprinids to trigger development of their eggs (gynogenesis), which lowers the reproductive success of those species. Bighead Carp and Silver Carp hybridise in the [[Enzeli Mordab]], threatening genetic purity in fish farm stocks.
- **Native alternatives:** The native cyprinodonts [[Aphanius]] dispar and *Aphanius sophiae* eat mosquito larvae and tolerate a wide range of temperatures and salinities. They were not evaluated as control agents before [[Mosquitofish]] was introduced.

## Key Findings

1. **New records:** *Pseudorasbora parva* was recorded from over 200 specimens along the Caspian coast of Mazanderan Province, over a distance of about 300 km. Sex ratio was 1 male : 1.16 females (n = 80), and mean standard length was 42.8 mm (n = 115, SD = 12.56). Breeding individuals were found in April 1991. Its presence at [[Arak]] and [[Mashhad]] suggests escape from fish farms, probably with carp fingerlings.
2. ***Gasterosteus aculeatus* first record:** One fully plated specimen, 58 mm standard length, was collected on 5 March 1992 at the Neka Power Plant near Behshahr. Its origin is unknown but it was probably introduced with food fishes.
3. **Grass Carp stocking:** 50,000 fingerlings from the U.S.S.R. were released into the Enzeli Mordab in October 1970, and 1150 into the Dez Irrigation Project in April 1974. Escape was considered probable, but the authors note that reproduction requires large rivers with rising water, warming, turbidity and flood plains. Triploid Grass Carp are recommended for canal weed control because escapees cannot breed.
4. **Mosquitofish:** Introduced in a malaria-control programme begun in 1966. Over 3000 permanent water bodies were stocked, and it is now "the most widely distributed fish in Iran" and cannot be eradicated. It is also aggressive and preys on the eggs of other fish.
5. **Rainbow Trout:** Almost two million fingerlings were released to 20 locations over about four years, many in high mountain streams believed to be fishless. Breeding populations were reported in several places, but the stocking success has not been properly evaluated.
6. **Salmonid transplants:** Brook Trout escaped in large numbers during a 1968 flood on the Jajehrud. Some *Salmo trutta* in Iran are of uncertain subspecies, and European Brown Trout plantings threaten genetic swamping of native *S. trutta caspius*. A transplant of 500 Lighvanchai Brown Trout to the Ab-e Bazuft in 1975 has an unknown outcome.
7. **Marine-origin species:** Liza aurata, Liza saliens and Mugil cephalus were transplanted from the Black Sea to the Caspian between 1930 and 1934. *Liza aurata* produced 960,282 kg in commercial catches in 1959/1960, with no deleterious effects recorded. The [[European Flounder]] was introduced in 1902 and 1934 and is no longer caught.
8. **Failed or unconfirmed introductions:** [[Nile Tilapia]] did not survive winterkill in Iraq. [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] populations in the Yengi Kand Reservoir perished through winterkill. The *Ameiurus nebulosus* record is from Turkey, not Iran, and the Crucian Carp record is unconfirmed.
9. **Recommendations:** The authors advise (a) a licensing system for high-risk imports, with illustrated identification guides; (b) education for hatcheries, aquarists and agencies, including a check of the biology of any species before import; (c) discontinuing open-water stocking; (d) a nationwide survey of exotic localities, with eradication where feasible; (e) monitoring of established populations; and (f) bilateral agreements with neighbouring countries sharing drainages. The authors note that 421 fish-culture licences were issued during 1984–1986 (Petr 1987).
