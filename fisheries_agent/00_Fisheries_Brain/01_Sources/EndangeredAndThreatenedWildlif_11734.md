# Federal Register Notice: Endangered Species Listing Program Restart and Priority Guidance (May 16, 1996)

#government-policy #endangered-species-act #regulatory-framework #conservation-priorities

## Executive Summary

This Federal Register notice from May 16, 1996, documents the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's restart of the endangered species listing program following a 13-month moratorium and severe funding constraints. The Service announces final listing priority guidance to allocate limited appropriations ($4 million for fiscal year 1996) among competing demands, including a backlog of 243 proposed species listings. The guidance establishes a three-tiered priority system emphasizing emergency listings and completion of pending proposed listings over new proposals, petition findings, and critical habitat designations. This policy framework aims to maximize conservation benefits for species facing the most imminent threats despite inadequate funding and extensive litigation pressures.

## Background & Description

The [[Endangered Species Act]] listing program operates under section 4 of the Act (16 U.S.C. 1531 et seq.). Beginning April 10, 1995, Public Law 104-6 imposed a moratorium on final listing and critical habitat designations while rescinding $1.5 million from the listing appropriation. From October 1, 1995, through April 26, 1996, the Department of the Interior operated under thirteen continuing resolutions that effectively shut down the listing program, providing minimal funding (as low as $43,000 for six weeks, then zero funds for extended periods).

The Acting Director ordered reassignment of all listing personnel to other activities from mid-November 1995 through April 26, 1996, to prevent reductions-in-force that would have caused permanent loss of scientific expertise. The final fiscal year 1996 appropriation enacted April 26, 1996, provided approximately $4 million for the entire fiscal year, with $3,767,000 remaining after prior expenditures. President Clinton waived the moratorium provisions on April 26, 1996.

The Service faced a backlog of 243 proposed species, 182 candidate species requiring status resolution, approximately 60 pending civil suits, and 300 Notices of Intent to Sue related to listing activities. The 1983 Listing Priority Guidelines (48 FR 43098-43103; September 21, 1983) established priorities based on threat magnitude and taxonomic distinctiveness but did not prioritize among different types of listing activities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The primary purposes of the [[Endangered Species Act]] are "to provide a means whereby the ecosystems upon which endangered species and threatened species depend may be conserved, to provide a program for the conservation of such endangered species and threatened species" (16 U.S.C. 1531(d)). The Service's long-standing policy prioritizes species facing the greatest extinction threats.

The vast majority of the 243 proposed species face high-magnitude threats requiring immediate protection. Only listed species receive full conservation benefits and substantive protections of the Act, including section 7 consultation requirements and section 9 take prohibitions. Proposed species receive limited procedural protection through the section 7 conference requirement (16 U.S.C. 1536(a)(4)) but lack substantive protections.

Critical habitat designation, while required by statute, generally provides limited conservation benefits beyond listing itself because protections apply only to Federal actions. Situations where critical habitat provides additional protection beyond the jeopardy prohibition are rare, making it a lower priority during severe funding constraints.

## Key Findings

**Four Guiding Principles for Program Restart:**

1. Highest priority to species most in need based on biological threat assessment
2. Biological need, not litigation preferences, drives the listing process
3. Sound science, including peer review, forms the foundation of all listing decisions
4. Enhanced public comment and participation with complete explanations and careful evaluation of all input

**Three-Tiered Priority System (Final Guidance):**

**Tier 1—Emergency Listings:** Immediate processing under section 4(b)(7) for species facing imminent extinction risk. All petitions and status information screened for emergency situations.

**Tier 2—Final Decisions on Proposed Listings:** Processing 243 pending proposed listings receives highest priority after emergencies. Within Tier 2, priorities determined by:
- Immediacy and magnitude of threats (highest listing priority assignments processed first)
- Multi-species packages prioritized based on most urgent component species
- Proposals requiring minimal additional work processed before those needing extensive revision
- Oldest proposals processed first when other factors equal

Regional offices will reevaluate threat immediacy and magnitude for all proposed species and revise listing priority assignments accordingly.

**Tier 3—All Other Actions:** Including new proposed listings, reclassifications, delistings, petition findings, and critical habitat designations. The Service anticipates no Tier 3 actions will be undertaken before September 30, 1996, given the backlog and limited appropriations.

**Implementation Timeline and Constraints:**

- 45 days estimated to fully reengage all listing personnel reassigned during shutdown
- 30-120 days for Field Office completion of packages depending on remaining steps
- 30-60 days for Regional Office policy review and concurrence
- 30-60 days for Washington Office technical and policy review
- Additional time required for critical habitat rules requiring Office of Management and Budget review

**Litigation Management:**

The Service will assess relative priority of all section 4 activities subject to litigation using this guidance and 1983 guidelines. Through the Office of the Solicitor and Department of Justice, the Service will request appropriate court relief to address highest biological priorities first. The Service will not elevate priorities simply because species are subjects of litigation, as this would allow litigants rather than biological judgments to control priorities.

The [[Fund for Animals]] v. Lujan settlement agreement (Civ. No. 92-800, D.D.C., December 15, 1992) requires resolution of 443 candidate species by September 30, 1996. The Service published proposed listings for 359 candidate species during the agreement period but notes that full compliance would consume the entire $4 million appropriation while leaving 243 proposed species without final protection. The Service will seek appropriate court relief to modify this agreement.

**Rules Near Completion:**

Headquarters will promptly process fewer than ten draft final rules, proposed listings/delistings, petition findings, critical habitat determinations, and withdrawal notices that were in the Washington Office prior to this notice but delayed by funding constraints or moratorium.

**Guidance Duration:**

This guidance remains effective until September 30, 1996, unless extended by future notice. The Service anticipates returning to typical implementation of listing responsibilities to concurrently process petition findings, proposed and final listings, reclassifications, delistings, and critical habitat determinations after backlogs are reduced.
