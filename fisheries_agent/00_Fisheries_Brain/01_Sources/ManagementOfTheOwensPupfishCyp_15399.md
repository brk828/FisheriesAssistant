# Management of the Owens Pupfish (Cyprinodon radiosus) in Mono County, California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1971 paper by [[Robert Rush Miller]] and [[Edwin P. Pister]] documents the decline, rediscovery, and conservation management of the [[Owens Pupfish]], a killifish endemic to the Owens River drainage of [[Owens Valley]], California. The species was believed extinct when described in 1948, but a population was found in [[Fish Slough]] in 1964. Following near-loss of the population in 1969, the authors describe the creation of the [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]], a cooperative project between the [[City of Los Angeles]] (Department of Water and Power) and the [[California Department of Fish and Game]], along with additional refugia at BLM Spring and Warm Springs.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** [[Cyprinodon radiosus]] Miller (1948) is a member of the killifish family Cyprinodontidae. It was initially confused with the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*), which occurs in California only in the Salton Sea basin (Miller, 1943).
- **Range:** Historically from near Lone Pine, Inyo County, north to the source springs of Fish Slough, Mono County, about 10 to 12 miles north of Bishop.
- **Historical abundance:** Early 1900s populations were abundant in springs, sloughs, swamps, irrigation ditches, and bog pastures along the Owens River. Kennedy (1916) noted its potential for mosquito control.
- **Morphology (Figure 1 specimens):** Maximum size about 60 mm total length. Breeding males show pinkish tan to orange dorsal fins, turquoise anal fins, a blue predorsal stripe, and brilliant metallic blue vertical bars. Unlike other *Cyprinodon*, the caudal fin of breeding males lacks a conspicuous terminal black border (though it was observed in life by [[Robert K. Liu]] in 1964), and the bars are not narrowed ventrally. Females are olive-brown with narrower, irregular brownish bars.
- **Behavior:** Breeding and aggressive individuals of both sexes engaged in "leapfrogging" over a fallen *Scirpus* stem.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Wide, flat, shallow, marshy pools with dense [[Chara]] on mud bottoms and stands of [[Scirpus]]; water 2 to 6 inches deep. At the "Rediscovery Pools" on July 10, 1964, air was 91 °F and water 92 °F at 3 PM; water was 79 °F the next morning.
- **Threats:**
  - Diversion of water to the [[Los Angeles Aqueduct]], which reduced marshes.
  - Introduced exotic fishes, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), which competed with or preyed on pupfish.
  - Predation by largemouth bass was the chief suspected cause of complete disappearance from Fish Slough springs by 1942.
  - Declining spring flow in August 1969, possibly from natural summer decrease combined with transpiration by riparian vegetation after heavy precipitation in winter 1968-69.
- **Associated fauna:** [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), chubs (*Gila*), and suckers (*Catostomus*), all native to the Owens Basin.

## Key Findings

- **Decline:** Hubbs found only a few fishless pools in 1934. In 1937, pupfish persisted at the upper Fish Slough springs. On August 31 and September 1, 1942, no pupfish were found at any of the three Fish Slough springs where they had previously lived. Subsequent surveys in Bishop and between Fish Slough and Big Pine also failed to find them, leading to a 1948 description as likely extinct.
- **Rediscovery:** In 1956, Richardson and Beck collected a few fish (about one per three seine hauls) from shallow ponds near Fish Slough. A 1963 survey failed to relocate them. On July 10, 1964, the population was rediscovered in an elongate pool with an estimated 200 individuals.
- **Sanctuary creation:** Approved by the California Fish and Game Commission in April 1968 and completed in October 1969 by inmate labor from the Inyo-Mono Conservation Camp. The refuge covers 5.6 acres within a 21-acre enclosure, with an exotic fish barrier based on a Nevada design (used for the White River springfish in 1963). In April 1970, the refuge was drained and treated with calcium hypochlorite and rotenone (repeated June 3).
- **Near-extinction event:** In August 1969, spring flow declined and the marsh supporting the last known population dried. About 800 individuals were rescued by Robert E. Brown and placed in live cages.
- **BLM Spring transplant:** On August 18, 1969, 400 pupfish were introduced after chemical removal of resident fish. Mosquitofish survived treatment. The population was estimated at 1,000 by late June 1970, making it the only fully successful transplant. A single female was seen in the northern spring on May 15, 1970.
- **Sanctuary stocking:** On June 30, 1970, 428 fish were introduced into the Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary (males 142, females 249, immatures 37), with water at 74 °F. Only *C. radiosus* was stocked initially to secure establishment before adding the other three native fishes.
- **Warm Springs refugium:** Built in spring 1970 at the base of the White Mountains, about five miles north of Big Pine, to test interspecific relationships among the four Owens Valley native fishes.
- **Policy and coordination:** A Pupfish Task Force was established by the U.S. Department of the Interior in May 1970. A symposium on Death Valley system fishes was held at Furnace Creek in November 1969, and the species was classified as rare and endangered (U.S. Dept. Int., 1968).
