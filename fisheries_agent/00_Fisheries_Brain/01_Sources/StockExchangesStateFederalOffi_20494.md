# Non-Native Fish Stocking Review on Colorado's Western Slope

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This *Denver Post* outdoors article (dated January 26; the year is not stated, but it references events through March 1992) reports a dispute between the [[Colorado Division Of Wildlife]] (DOW) and the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) over stocking non-native game fish on the Western Slope. The [[Tiger Muskie]] is expected to be the first "test case" for a new federal/state review procedure. USFWS fears that muskies and other non-native game fish may prey on four [[Endangered Fish Of The Upper Colorado River]]. Critics on both sides argue that the procedure is slow, costly and may halt warm-water sport fishing.

## Background & Description

- **Tiger muskie:** A hybrid of [[Muskellunge]] and [[Northern Pike]]. It can grow to 35 pounds or more but cannot reproduce. It has vertical bars along the body and a duck-bill snout typical of the pike family. It had not existed in Colorado until recent years and had been stocked in reservoirs east of the [[Continental Divide]], but not on the west side.
- **Proposed stocking:** DOW proposed stocking six mountain-area reservoirs: Green Mountain, Rio Blanco, Harvey Gap, Hallenbeck, Juniata and Sanchez. All but Sanchez lie in the upper Colorado River basin.
- **Endangered fish:** Four native minnow-family species (Cyprinidae) are protected in the upper Colorado River system:
  - [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): olive-green and gold back, silvery belly, longer mouth with thick folded lips.
  - [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*): brown or olive sides, prominent hump behind the head.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
  - [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)
- **Legal basis:** USFWS relies on the [[Endangered Species Act]], and in particular Section 9, which prohibits "taking" (including mortality) of listed fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Officials and biologists cite the introduction of non-native fishes as one of the principal causes of decline in endangered species, second only to habitat alteration such as dam construction. Most popular Colorado sport fish are non-native, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] and [[Walleye]].

Key habitat issues:

- **Critical habitat definition:** USFWS wants critical habitat defined as the 100-year [[Flood Plain]] along the river. State officials proposed a different, more restrictive flood-plain definition (the scanned figure is partly unclear). Under the federal definition, an unconnected gravel pit could be classed as unsuitable for stocking.
- **Species covered by review:** Tiger muskies, walleyes, northern pike, yellow perch, wipers, striped bass and other warm-water species. Striped bass would be allowed only in [[Lake Powell]]. Trout are exempt because USFWS does not consider them a threat.
- **Environmental advocacy:** The [[Sierra Club]] and other groups supporting [[Biodiversity]] and [[Ecosystem Management]] are watching closely and may file lawsuits. At least one group urged USFWS to include trout in the review.

## Key Findings

1. **Review procedure:** The draft includes 10 stages of rules and review. Any stocking plan must be evaluated by state and federal officials, opened to public review and approved by the [[Colorado Wildlife Commission]]. USFWS in effect holds veto power if (1) federal hatcheries are involved, or the fish would be stocked in a federally built reservoir, or (2) the Endangered Species Act is invoked in legal action. The state commission has final say in theory, but federal authorities could challenge state decisions.
2. **Presumption of guilt:** Under the procedure, non-native fish are "presumed guilty until proven innocent"; the state must prove no threat to the endangered fish.
3. **Tiger muskie analysis:** A 73-page DOW analytical report, prepared by biologist [[Jim Satterfield]], found no likelihood that tiger muskies would escape the six reservoirs and prey on the endangered fish. DOW aquatic manager [[Eddie Kochman]] noted that zero risk cannot be demonstrated.
4. **Stocking halt:** Stocking of all warm-water, non-native species on the Western Slope has effectively stopped. Bob Caskey, DOW northwest region manager, said this may mark "the beginning of the end" for the Western Slope warm-water program, which includes walleye and smallmouth bass in Rifle Gap Reservoir and largemouth bass, crappie and smallmouth bass in McPhee Reservoir.
5. **Staff time:** Kochman has devoted 60 to 70 percent of his time to endangered-species matters and only 30 to 40 percent to unrelated sport-fishing issues.
6. **Costs:** Federal recovery plans call for spending up to $134 million over the next 10 years. DOW estimates it spends the equivalent of $750,000 a year from state fishing and hunting license revenue on the four endangered species, in manpower and other costs, even though most funding is federal.
7. **Stakeholder positions:** [[Jim Smith]], founder of the Colorado chapter of [[Muskies, Inc.]] and a former state wildlife commissioner, opposes the restrictions. [[John Hamill]], USFWS director of the endangered fish recovery program, argues that non-native fish are the central issue, not the tiger muskie itself.
8. **Etter Pond fiasco:** In the prior year, USFWS and DOW paid $20,000 (split evenly) to the owner of an oxbow pond near Grand Junction, Jimmie Etter, for access to take adult razorback suckers as [[Broodstock]]. Four adults died. Tissue analysis by a [[University Of California, Los Angeles]] geneticist found contamination with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] genes, so the fish should not be used for recovery. A second expert disagreed and suggested they might adapt better to the river. No official decision had been made. Tom Nesler, DOW fish biologist and Colorado's representative on the biological subcommittee, stated that the fish were still considered usable.
9. **Hatchery losses at Dexter, New Mexico:**
   - Spring 1991: about 50,000 young hatchery razorback suckers suffocated after a dust storm cut power.
   - Fall 1991: wild humpback chubs were lost after being placed in a pond with adult Colorado squawfish, which ate them.
   - March 1992: 12 razorback suckers, eight squawfish and eight bonytail chubs were lost when a repairman released chlorinated water into a holding tank. The eight bonytails were the last captured from the Colorado River.
10. **Conservation status:** Razorback sucker numbers are very low, and bonytail chub is believed to be close to [[Extirpation]].
