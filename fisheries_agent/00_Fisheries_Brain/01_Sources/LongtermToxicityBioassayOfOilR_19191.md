# Long-Term Toxicity Bioassay of Oil Refinery Effluents

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 doctoral dissertation by Richard James Graham (Department of Zoology, [[Oklahoma State University]]) tests whether [[Oil Refinery Effluent]] harms fish when exposed for extended periods, a question that standard short-term tests cannot answer. Using [[Fathead Minnow]] as the test organism, the study compared 96-hour [[Routine Bioassay]] results with 16- or 32-day [[Long-Term Bioassay]] results under continuous renewal of test solutions. Effluents came from four refineries (A–D): A and B were lightly treated and highly toxic, while C and D were extensively treated in oxidation ponds and had low initial toxicity.

The main finding is that highly toxic effluents did not cause measurable harm at sub-acute concentrations, whereas low-toxicity effluents produced [[Chronic Toxicity]] that was invisible in short-term tests. Fish survival was lower and body condition poorer after prolonged exposure. The author concludes that dilution of at least 60 percent is needed to prevent chronic toxication from treated effluents.

## Background & Description

The dissertation opens by noting that the [[Routine Bioassay]] of the American Public Health Association (1960) measures acute toxicity over 24, 48 and 96 hours. It cannot detect the cumulative effects of low concentrations, which can impair growth and reproduction or increase susceptibility to disease. The study was designed to fill that gap and to test whether treatment methods reduce long-term hazard.

Earlier work cited in the introduction includes:
- Ludzack, Ingram and Ettinger (1957), who reported adverse effects on aquatic life for about 30 miles below a refinery outfall.
- Clemens and Crawford (n.d.), who found that most of 14 Oklahoma refinery effluents tested had a [[Median Tolerance Limit]] below 30 percent at 96 hours.
- Turnbull, DeMann and Weston (1954), and Wallen, Greer and Lasater (1957), who tested individual refinery chemicals.
- Ongoing Oklahoma Oil Refiners' Waste Control Council cooperation with the Aquatic Biology Laboratory since 1957.

### Methods Overview

- **Test fish:** Fathead minnows reared in farm ponds, typically 42–51 mm total length. Fish were held 8–10 days, treated with terramycin for bacterial control, and not used if disease or mortality exceeded 10 percent. Less than 15 percent of fish about 45 mm long were mature females.
- **Dilution water:** Stillwater tap water, aerated and held in 600-gallon vats. Hardness was 138–153 ppm and pH 7.8–8.4.
- **Temperature:** Test solutions were kept at 22–24 °C, with an extreme range of 21–26 °C.
- **Apparatus:** A [[Constant-Flow Apparatus]] using metering pumps replaced 10 liters of test solution every 5.5 hours (about 30 cc/min). Test concentrations were 9, 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 percent effluent.
- **Sub-acute concentrations:** Only concentrations where more than 50 percent of fish survived the routine test were used for long-term work.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Measured by the [[Winkler Method]] (Alsterberg modification). Oxygen was bubbled in when levels fell toward 2 ppm.
- **Condition index:** K = weight (g) × 100,000 / length (mm)³, following [[Condition Factor]] methods of Carlander (1953).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The four refineries differed in processing and treatment:
- **Refinery A:** Skimming, aeration and pond settling; sour waters stripped separately.
- **Refinery B:** Oil separation, sour-water stripping, and settling in a holding basin; high toxicity.
- **Refinery C:** Three oil settling ponds, aeration, and four [[Waste Stabilization Ponds]] with 60 days total holding.
- **Refinery D:** Activated sludge pond, 2 miles of ditch, then 10 oxidation ponds.

Chemical characteristics of effluents (1960–62) show a clear gradient. Ammonia averaged 105–122 mg/L in the treated effluents (C and D) against 261–356 mg/L in the less treated effluents (A and B). Median tolerance limits (TL) at 96 hours were 4–61 percent for A and 4–70 percent for B, and mostly above 50 percent for C and D.

Effluent components examined included:
- [[Ammonia]], [[Phenol]] and sulfides, which the author links to acute toxicity.
- [[Carbon Dioxide]] and alkalinity, which accumulated where algae were abundant in ponds.
- Trace metals and anions in Experiments 16 and 17: [[Copper]] (trace to 0.13 ppm), [[Cyanide]] (maximum 0.008 ppm, as CN), arsenic (0–0.011 ppm), thiocyanate, total chromium, and lead (not detected).

Effluent storage also mattered. High-toxicity effluent did not change much during storage, while low-toxicity effluent lost toxicity over 6–16 days. The author concluded that [[Effluent Storage]] biased results, and that bioassays should ideally be run at the refinery.

## Key Findings

### Results by Effluent Type

**High-toxicity effluents (Refineries A and B; 7 experiments: 3 of 16 days, 4 of 32 days)**
- 96-hour routine TL ranged from 6.5 to 16.5 percent.
- Survival during 96 hours was about the same in renewed and non-renewed tests.
- Fish survived 16 days in all but one experiment. In Experiment 1, mortality reached 35 percent by day 16 at 9 percent concentration.
- No differences in behavior or appearance were seen in long-term controls. Mean condition factors in three 32-day experiments were not significantly different from controls.
- Effluent toxicity showed little change in storage, with ammonia falling from 132 to 75 ppm in Experiment 6.

**Low-toxicity effluents (Refineries C and D; 11 experiments: 4 of 16 days, 1 of 24 days, 6 of 32 days)**
- Routine 96-hour mortality was 0–10 percent at 100 percent concentration in most experiments.
- Survival after 16 days was significantly lower than in routine tests in six experiments (8, 9, 10, 11, 12 and 15). Routine mortality was 0–10 percent, while prolonged mortality ranged from 25 to 100 percent.
- Across all eleven experiments, eight showed appreciably lower survival after prolonged exposure than in routine tests.
- Condition factors of exposed fish were significantly lower than controls in Experiments 13, 14 and 16 (for example, Experiment 13 at 100 percent: 0.45 vs. control 0.64, using [[Duncan's Multiple Range Test]]).
- In Experiment 15, all fish were dead by day 25.
- In Experiment 17, raising dissolved oxygen from about 3 to 7.5 ppm reduced total mortality by 15 percent at both concentrations. The 40 percent dilution cut mortality by about 40 percent.
- Experiment 18, from a period when the catalytic crackers were shut down, was the only low-toxicity effluent with no adverse effects after prolonged exposure. Its first batch contained 5.5 ppm ammonia.

### Dissolved Oxygen and Carbon Dioxide

- Dissolved oxygen in test solutions ranged from 1.7 to 10.3 ppm. Low values were limited to the first 3–4 days and did not persist.
- Low-toxicity effluents needed artificial oxygenation nearly always, because high organic and algal loads raised oxygen demand.
- Carbon dioxide reached a maximum of 26 ppm. Values under 30 ppm are generally not considered harmful to most fish (Tarzwell, 1957), but the author notes that CO₂ may interfere with respiration in combination with other stressors.

### Conclusions

1. Fish were not adversely affected by extended exposure to sub-acute concentrations of high-toxicity effluents.
2. Prolonged exposure to sub-acute low-toxicity effluents caused significantly lower survival than short-term bioassays predicted.
3. Condition factors of surviving fish were significantly lower than controls.
4. Dilution of at least 60 percent appears necessary to prevent chronic toxication from treated effluents. The author warns that greater dilution may be needed if growth or reproduction is considered, or if exposure is longer.
5. The specific toxicants responsible for chronic effects are unknown. Copper, cyanide, ammonia and low oxygen are the leading candidates discussed, with copper possibly sufficient to cause chronic harm at the highest measured levels.
