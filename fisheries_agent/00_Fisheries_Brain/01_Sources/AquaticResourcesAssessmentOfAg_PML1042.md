# Fish and Aquatic Resources of Irrigation Drains in the Palo Verde, Coachella and Imperial Valleys (1975)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

A May 1975 survey by [[California Department of Fish and Game]] staff examined fish and other aquatic life in irrigation drains operated by the [[Palo Verde Irrigation District]] (PVID), the [[Coachella Valley County Water District]] (CVCWD) and the [[Imperial Irrigation District]] (IID). Seven sites were sampled in each valley using chemical knockdown with chlorine, because [[Electrofishing]] equipment failed in the drains. The survey collected 19 fish species, including game and nongame species, and found evidence that several fish were reproducing in the drains. The authors concluded that these drains can support fish populations and important invertebrate communities and that they may have potential as fishery resources, provided that management is sound. The sampling was limited, so the results are a partial picture of each drain's fauna.

## Background & Description

The report opens by noting that surface water in a desert climate supports aquatic life far out of proportion to its volume. Water bodies that persist long enough to support plankton and invertebrates can serve as nursery habitat. For example, some game fish spawn in small drains, and their young feed on plankton before moving to permanent water.

The study was designed to determine whether significant aquatic resources exist in drains that carry [[Irrigation Return Flow]] from agricultural fields in the desert valleys. Sampling took place on the following dates:

- **PVID drains:** May 25–26, 1975
- **CVCWD drains:** May 27–28, 1975
- **IID drains:** May 29–30, 1975

Each site was photographed and field notes were recorded. Block nets were set at the upstream and downstream ends of each area. Chlorine solution was poured into the drain near the upstream net, and stunned fish were collected with dip nets. The report describes two formulations:

- **PVID:** a 65% active chlorine product, described as soluble and used to sanitize swimming pools.
- **CVCWD and IID:** "Desert Granules," described as 8% active chlorine that dissolves less readily in water.

Plankton samples were preserved in formalin for later reference. Other notable organisms were collected or recorded when observed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report stresses several limitations. Chlorine was used because the electroshocking equipment was inoperable in the drains and chlorine was available only in limited quantities. The required dose was uncertain, and species differ in their susceptibility to the toxicant. Other constraints included:

- Physical inaccessibility of most drains
- Very turbid water
- Extreme variation in depth and flow
- Limited time for sampling

The authors state that the samples do not necessarily represent all species present at each site. However, the data show that fish of several species, sizes and age classes were present in the drains studied.

Chemical control with [[Rotenone]] was rejected because it persists in the environment and could kill organisms outside the study area. Electrofishing was also limited by steep, sloped banks, soft bottoms, dense vegetation and high [[Total Dissolved Solids]] content in the water.

Aquatic invertebrates were abundant and important. They included [[Crayfish]], freshwater snails, shrimp, damselflies, dragonflies, water boatmen (Corixidae), backswimmers (Notonectidae) and other insects. These organisms serve as food for fish and birds, help recycle nutrients, and compete with game fish for resources. The report also observed [[Bullfrog]] tadpoles and adults and [[Muskrat]] activity in several drains.

## Key Findings

### Fish Community and Collection Results
- **Species diversity:** Fish collected represented 19 species, including nine game species and ten nongame species, from 12 genera and 7 families. (OCR of the figures for genera and families is uncertain.)
- **Species per site:** Each site yielded between one and ten species. The reported averages were approximately 14.7 species per site for PVID, 11.6 for CVCWD and 2.1 or more for IID. The first two figures are inconsistent with the stated maximum of ten, so they may be misread from the scan and should be verified against the original.
- **Species restricted to one district:** Six species were found in only one of the three districts. These included [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Desert Pupfish]] and three species of mollies.
- **Most common game fish:** [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]] were the most frequently encountered game fish throughout the study.
- **Most common nongame fish:** [[Common Carp]], [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and [[Golden Shiner]] (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*) were the most frequently collected nongame fish.
- **Diversity pattern:** The PVID drains had the most diverse fish community and a healthy balance between game and nongame species. The IID drains had the lowest abundance and diversity of both groups, and their fish were usually represented by only a few individuals.

### Evidence of Reproduction
- Largemouth bass from Olive Lake Drain included at least three age classes: yearlings and a single three-year-old.
- Several age classes of channel catfish were collected. Young-of-the-year catfish were found in Lincoln Street Drain and in an IID drain, and Lincoln Street Drain also produced a large number of young catfish.
- Carp of various age classes were collected frequently.
- In one drain, observers saw [[Tilapia]] (*Tilapia mossambica*) and their distinctive nests. Fish of various sizes and ages were also present. The authors concluded that these drains provide suitable conditions for reproduction and population growth if managed properly.

### Habitat Associations
- **Tilapia-dominated drains:** The best example was a slow-moving, turbid drain in which tilapia were the dominant fish. Few aquatic plants were present, and the authors suggest that tilapia grazing removed vegetation. Other species were scarce in these drains, possibly because of competition.
- **Catfish-bass drains (PVID):** These drains were dense with vegetation and had clear water. Prey, including shrimp and crayfish, were abundant.
- **Mosquitofish-carp drains (CVCWD):** These were smaller, fast-flowing drains with little overhanging vegetation and moderate aquatic plant growth. Game fish were usually absent in this group.

### Food Web and Ecological Roles
- Largemouth bass stomach contents from Olive Lake Drain contained mainly small crayfish.
- Crayfish are a critical food source for large game fish, and nongame fish such as threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*) and mollies provide food for larger predators.
- Nongame fish and invertebrates play important ecological roles and are not merely competitors of game fish. Their presence indicates that a drain can potentially support a game fishery.

### Management Implications and Hypotheses
- The authors noted that a drain's fish community could be predicted from its physical characteristics and visual inspection, but the actual diversity and abundance of fish exceeded what visual inspection suggested.
- Differences between the three districts may reflect several factors: possible natural recruitment from [[Colorado River]] waters, the persistence of some drains as remnants of natural river channels, and management practices that degrade water quality through high turbidity, low dissolved oxygen, high salinity, sedimentation and habitat alteration.
- Absence of a species from a single sample does not prove it is absent from the drain. For example, the lack of fish in Holtville Main Drain does not rule out their presence there.
