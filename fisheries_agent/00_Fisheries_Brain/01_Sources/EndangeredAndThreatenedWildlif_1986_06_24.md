# Virgin River Chub (Gila robusta seminuda) — Proposed Endangered Listing (1986)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Federal Register notice proposes listing the **Virgin River Chub** (*Gila robusta seminuda*), a subspecies of the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), as an **Endangered Species** under the Endangered Species Act of 1973, along with designation of approximately 50 miles of the [[Virgin River]] as critical habitat. The subspecies is endemic to the Virgin River in southwestern Utah, northwestern Arizona, and southeastern Nevada, and is identified as the **rarest native fish** in that river system. Principal threats include habitat destruction from water diversion, impoundment, desalinization projects, urban growth, sedimentation, pollution, and competition/predation from introduced exotic fishes such as the [[Red Shiner]]. The naturally extreme conditions of the Virgin River (high salinity, turbidity, temperature, and flow fluctuation) have historically acted as a barrier to exotic species invasion, but proposed water development and desalinization projects threaten to remove this protective barrier.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic history**: First collected and described from the Virgin River near Washington, Utah, by the Wheeler Survey (Cope and Yarrow, 1875), originally considered intermediate between *Gila robusta* and *Gila elegans* ([[Bonytail]]). Later treated as a subspecies of *G. robusta* (Ellis 1914; Miller 1946; LaRivers and Trelease 1952). Holden and Stalnaker (1970) restricted the name *seminuda* specifically to the Virgin River population; validity as a distinct subspecies was supported by Minckley (1973) and confirmed via extensive taxonomic analysis by Smith et al. (1977).
- **Morphology**: A very silvery, medium-sized minnow, generally less than 15 cm (6 in) total length. The back, breast, and part of the belly bear small, deeply embedded scales that are difficult to see and may be absent in some individuals — the source of the subspecific name *seminuda* ("half-naked").
- **Related taxon**: An undescribed subspecies of *G. robusta* occurs in the [[Moapa River]], Nevada — originally a Virgin River tributary, now tributary to Lake Mead via the Colorado River. This Moapa form has also experienced population decline and reduced range, though its taxonomic status does not affect the listing status of *seminuda*.
- **Historic vs. current range**: Historically abundant, ranging from near Riverside, Nevada, upstream to La Verkin Springs near Hurricane, Utah (Cope and Yarrow 1875). Major range contraction occurred largely between 1860–1900 with construction of water diversions and agricultural modification of the valley/riverbanks. Current distribution: mainstream Virgin River from Mesquite, Nevada, upstream to La Verkin Springs, Utah.
- **Habitat association**: Found exclusively in the mainstream of the Virgin River (only one tributary record ever documented, Cross 1975). Most common in deeper, swift (but non-turbulent) waters associated with boulders or other cover (Minckley 1973). Typically occurs over sand and gravel substrates in water below 90°F (32°C); highly tolerant of high salinity and turbidity (Deacon and Holden 1977).
- **Diet**: Omnivorous — consumes algae, aquatic and terrestrial insects, organic detritus, and crustaceans (Cross 1975).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Co-occurring listed species**: Shares habitat with the federally endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*); the Woundfin Recovery Team recommended chub listing in April 1983.
- **Natural environmental barriers to exotic fish invasion**: The Virgin River's naturally high salinity, elevated temperature, high turbidity, and highly fluctuating flow (extreme summer lows punctuated by thunderstorm floods) have historically limited establishment of exotic predatory fish (e.g., green sunfish, black bullhead, largemouth bass) over the past ~70 years.
- **La Verkin Springs**: Contributes ~11 cubic feet per second of highly saline (9,650 mg/L), hot (100–109°F / 35–39°C) water to the river, substantially elevating salinity below the confluence — a natural habitat feature considered ecologically important as a deterrent to exotic species.
- **Water diversions**: Five major diversions remove all flow from long stretches of the river during peak summer irrigation; three are within the chub's present range. Below these diversions, summer flow often consists only of groundwater accretions and spring inputs (La Verkin, Littlefield Springs).
- **Habitat modification extent**: Cross (1975) found the chub occupying unmodified habitat 80% of the time, slightly modified habitat 20% of the time, and rarely in extensively modified habitat — indicating strong habitat specificity/sensitivity to alteration.
- **Population growth pressure**: Washington County, Utah population grew from 9,269 (1940) to 13,669 (1970) to 26,065 (1980), driving anticipated future water demand shifts from agricultural to municipal/industrial use, projected to further threaten instream flows.
- **Exotic species threat**: [[Red Shiner]] has been moving progressively upstream from Lake Mead into Utah, implicated in declines of other native fishes and considered a threat to both the Woundfin and early life stages of the Virgin River Chub.
- **Parasitism**: Minor infestations of black grub and *Lernaea* documented in Virgin River Chub (Radant and Coffeen 1983); more severe parasite problems (likely introduced via exotic fish from Lake Mead) documented in the related Moapa River *Gila robusta* form (Wilson et al. 1966).
- **Regulatory landscape (pre-listing)**: Listed as endangered by the American Fisheries Society (Deacon et al. 1979); Arizona Group 2 endangered (1982); Utah threatened (1982); Nevada sensitive (1981). None of these state designations provided habitat protection, and neither Utah nor Nevada water law at the time allowed acquisition of instream water rights for fish/wildlife preservation.

## Key Findings

- **Recruitment failure**: Cross (1975) documented very few young-of-year fish and very few adults exceeding 7 inches standard length, indicating poor recruitment as a central concern for subspecies viability.
- **Interannual reproduction variability**: Woundfin Recovery Team reported good chub reproduction in 1983 (attributed to high water) but no evidence of successful reproduction in 1984; however, Hickman (1985) reported young-of-year comprising 14% of total 1984 catch — indicating conflicting or inconsistent survey results and the need for standardized long-term monitoring.
- **Population age-structure concern**: Surveys indicated the population dominated by fish from the single successful 1983 spawning cohort (~3 years old at time of report), an unhealthy demographic pattern (ideal populations should have distributed age/size classes including older fish, sexually mature smaller fish, and young-of-year).
- **Sampling methodology caveat**: Electrofishing yields higher catch rates than seining but does not by itself provide information on population trends/stability; catch-per-unit-effort comparisons across years are needed.
- **Land ownership**: In Arizona, 80–90% of riverside lands are BLM-administered (private lands concentrated near Littlefield); in Utah, ~13 miles are BLM with some state parcels, remainder private; in Nevada, lands above Mesquite are entirely private.
- **Regulatory history**: Initially proposed for listing in 1978 (43 FR 37668) but withdrawn in 1980 for failure to finalize within the then-required 2-year window (45 FR 64853). Re-added to the Vertebrate Notice of Review as Category 1 in December 1982 (47 FR 58454), leading to this 1986 re-proposal.
- **Critical habitat proposal**: ~50 miles of the Virgin River proposed, from the Mesquite diversion dam (Nevada) upstream to the Utah SR-9 crossing north of Hurricane, Utah, excluding a ~14-mile segment of the Virgin River Narrows. This area was identified as supporting the only known self-perpetuating population.
- **Primary constituent elements of critical habitat**: Deeper pools and runs with cover in the mainstream channel; warm, saline, turbid water; rock-sand-gravel substrates; few or no exotic fish species; and periodic flooding necessary to maintain habitat quality.
- **Threat magnification factor**: The subspecies' naturally limited range makes it acutely vulnerable — a single adverse action (e.g., major diversion failure, desalinization project, or exotic species introduction) could potentially drive it to extinction.
- **Key uncertain/pending projects assessed**: Quail Creek Water Reclamation Project (assessed as unlikely to jeopardize the Woundfin given guaranteed 86 cfs minimum release, with probable but unconfirmed low impact on chub); Warner Valley Energy System (proposed then abandoned in 1982); Dixie Project and La Verkin Springs Unit/Lower Virgin River Unit of the Lower Colorado River Water Quality Improvement Program (desalinization projects under study, deemed uneconomical as of 1964 study but potentially resurrected).
