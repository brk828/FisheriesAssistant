# Regulation of the Abundance of Pink Salmon Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, a [[Symposium on Pink Salmon]] lecture by [[W. E. Ricker]] (Fisheries Research Board of Canada, Nanaimo), examines why [[Pink Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus gorbuscha*) populations often show a regular alternation of strong and weak years, known as the odd-year and even-year "[[Line Dominance]]" pattern. Ricker frames the problem through [[Stock and Recruitment]] theory, defines dominance quantitatively, reviews fourteen observational points from North America and Asia, and evaluates seven hypotheses (plus one supplementary hypothesis). He concludes that no single cause explains dominance, that several mechanisms may operate in each stock, and that management strategy depends on which mechanism applies.

## Background & Description

Pink salmon are the most numerous and most uniform of the Pacific salmon: practically all individuals mature at age II (two years of life). Because each fish is exposed to capture only near the end of its life, recruitment is easier to measure than in most fishes. The document notes that reliable spawner-recruit data for pinks remained scarce in 1960, with the [[Skeena River]] and [[Fraser River]] data becoming available over time.

Key terms defined in the paper:

- **Stock:** a homogeneous group of pinks spawning in one tributary.
- **Line:** all stocks are split into an odd-year line and an even-year line.
- **Run:** adjacent stocks arriving and spawning at about the same time.
- **Assemblage (natio):** a group of runs in one region, distinctive in morphometric/meristic characters or in dominance pattern.
- **Dominance:** one line at least twice the other for at least 8 years (4 generations of each line). Ratios are graded as weak (2:1 to 4:1), moderate (4:1 to 8:1), marked (8:1 to 16:1), and extreme (above 16:1).

The working assumption is that pinks return to natal streams with fidelity, with straying probably rarely above 10% for indigenous stocks. Commercial catches serve as the main abundance index, usually plotted on logarithmic scales.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Density dependence and depensation.** Ricker defines *depensatory mortality* as a cause of death that kills a greater percentage of fish when the stock is small, the opposite of *compensatory mortality*. Depensatory predation on fry is observed at [[McClinton Creek]], [[Hooknose Creek]] and Karimai Spring Creek, where [[Dolly Varden]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Coho Salmon]], and sculpins eat a larger share of small fry populations. At McClinton Creek in 1931 and 1933, predator stomachs held about the same number of pink fry per stomach even though the fry migration was about twice as large in 1931.

**Freshwater and marine links.** Fry from one line usually leave fresh water before adults of the other line return, but the outbound and inbound migrations overlap at sea. Manzer (1956) found many age 0 pinks in coastal British Columbia waters when age 1+ pinks passed through, and Dvinin (1952) recorded age 0+ pinks in Sakhalin trap nets into autumn.

**Climate and population shocks.** Neave noted abrupt declines across extensive regions of British Columbia caused by widespread unfavourable climatic conditions. Such events can initiate dominance where none existed and can bring whole regions into phase.

**Spawning-ground effects.** Large spawnings may foul redds with dead eggs persisting into following years, or may favour egg predators in the gravel.

**Marine geography.** Ricker estimates that in the odd years early in the twentieth century, the Strait of Georgia and Puget Sound area held upwards of 60 million adult pinks, producing about one billion fry in even years (an estimate based on a 2% fry-production assumption). The [[Masset Inlet]] streams drain into a sea basin of about 200 square miles, which Ricker suggests may favour salt-water depensation.

## Key Findings

**Dominance patterns across the range**
- Dominance occurs in all degrees, from near-complete absence of one line to near-equality.
- Examples: [[McClinton Creek]] (near-complete alternation); [[Fraser River]] (extreme, via Point Roberts and Boundary Bay traps); [[Cook Inlet]] (marked, up to 1927, average ratio about 10:1); [[Rivers Inlet]] (moderate, 1919-1931); [[Skeena River]] (weak, 1921-1931); southern Clarence Strait (absence of dominance).
- Dominance often existed before commercial fishing began (Fraser, northern British Columbia, Cook Inlet, Bristol Bay, Amur estuary 1902-1914, where even-year catches averaged about 2:1).
- Dominance intensified or first became recognizable after fishing developed. The [[Amur River]] odd-year closure in 1923 lasted 4 generations.
- Dominance can disappear when the big line declines (the Skeena River after 1932; the Amur region 1949-55), but no weak line has been observed to reach parity while the dominant line stays abundant.

**Reversal and spread**
- Most rivers bordering the [[Sea of Okhotsk]] reversed dominance in the 1930s, beginning in east Sakhalin in 1933, followed by the Bolsheretsk region and west Kamchatka (1939). Ricker interprets this as a wave spreading between regions.

**Transplant failures**
- At [[McClinton Creek]], only 1 adult returned from 878,000 transplanted fry (1931) and 6 from 506,000 (1935).
- At Wahleach (Jones) Creek, a Fraser tributary, transplanted fry returned at 0.26% (1956) and 0.031% (1958), versus 0.89% and 0.59% for native stock (1957, 1959). Native returns at four other British Columbia localities ranged from 0.82% to 3.74%.
- Transplants from Sakhalin to the [[Barents Sea]] and [[White Sea]] rivers returned in large numbers despite some wandering.

**Size and abundance**
- At [[Bolsheretsk Region]] (Bolshaia River), catch and average length correlated at r = -0.705 (1930-49) and catch and weight at r = -0.785. Weight versus catch for 1925-49 gave a second-degree regression with multiple R = 0.884 and R² = 77%.
- In British Columbia, odd-year pinks were 0.5-1.5 lb (0.2-0.7 kg) larger than even-year pinks (Hoar 1927-42; Godfrey 1944-58), the reverse of the Asian pattern.
- No size-abundance alternation was found in America.

**Hypotheses evaluated**
1. **Depensatory predation in fresh water:** flattens the left limb of the reproduction curve, slowing recovery but probably not generating dominance by itself.
2. **Depensatory mortality in salt water:** plausible for areas like Masset Inlet and the Amur estuary, with no direct observational support yet.
3. **[[Cannibalism]] between lines:** Ricker's simulation (20% cannibalism mortality, starting ratio 2:1) produced 16:1 after 10 years. At 90% mortality, the weak line went extinct within two generations. Cannibalism predicts inverse changes between lines, which Ricker sees on the Amur and in west Kamchatka.
4. **Redd fouling by large egg depositions:** plausible at local scales (Hunter 1959, Hooknose Creek), but not demonstrated clearly.
5. **[[Depensatory Fishing]]:** fishing once took a larger percentage of small runs (e.g., during poor years with limited cans and barrels), intensifying dominance. Modern fishing is much less depensatory.
6. **Constant-percentage fishing:** slows recovery of depressed lines and maintains inequality from natural origins, but cannot initiate dominance.
7. **Food competition between lines:** considered unlikely, though within-line competition affects size.

**Supplementary hypothesis (marine separation):** no evidence for marine separation of adjacent runs in America. In Asia, Birman (1956) suggests the Maritime Province pinks (Tumnin River) share the Sea of Japan with the Amur pinks, and Appendix I discusses the migration route through Laperouse Strait.

**Management implications**
- Under hypotheses 1, 2, 5 and 6, managers should aim for two lines both at maximum productivity, using selective exploitation. Stocking an absent line is feasible.
- Under hypothesis 3, dominance may be impossible to correct by regulation, and starting an off-year run would probably be impossible under active cannibalism. Extreme inequality may give the maximum average catch.
- Under hypotheses 5 and 6, flexible fishing effort aimed at a fixed optimum escapement each year is recommended, the stated objective of most Canadian pink regulations.

**Conclusion:** Ricker argues for multiple causation. He suggests that research should identify which causes operate in each stock, and asks why some stocks never show dominance.
