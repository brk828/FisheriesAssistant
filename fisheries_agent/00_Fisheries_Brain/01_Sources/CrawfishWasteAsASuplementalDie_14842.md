# Crawfish Waste as a Supplemental Diet for Channel Catfish in Louisiana

#ichthyology #aquaculture #Ictaluridae #channel-catfish #feed-development #pond-culture

## Executive Summary

This 1967 Master's thesis investigated the feasibility of using crawfish processing waste as a supplemental feed for [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) in shallow Louisiana ponds. The study compared raw ground crawfish waste and pelleted crawfish waste meal against a commercial fish ration. Nine experimental ponds (0.1-0.25 acres each) were stocked at 1,455 fish per acre and fed for 105 days at 3% body weight daily. Pelleted crawfish waste produced reasonably good growth (mean gain 0.345 lbs) but was inferior to the commercial ration (0.447 lbs), while raw waste performed poorly (0.232 lbs). The crawfish waste contained approximately 35% crude protein on a dry matter basis. The study also demonstrated that [[Channel Catfish]] continued feeding at surface temperatures up to 103°F in shallow (2-foot mean depth) ponds, contrary to traditional recommendations.

## Background & Description

The crawfish processing industry in Louisiana discards 85-90% of each crawfish as waste, retaining only the tail for human consumption. This represents a substantial volume of material with no commercial application. Previous research had shown that processing wastes from crab and shrimp industries could be converted into high-quality protein meal through steam drying and grinding, successfully replacing menhaden fish meal in poultry and swine rations.

Multiple food habit studies (Sibley 1929, Bailey and Harrison 1943/1948, Clemens 1952, Parsons 1963, Walden 1964, Gresham 1965) demonstrated that crawfish were prevalent in the natural diet of adult [[Channel Catfish]], particularly in southern waters where these crustaceans are more abundant. This natural dietary preference suggested potential for crawfish waste as a feed ingredient.

The experimental site consisted of nine earthen ponds constructed in 1962 on [[Ben Hur Farm]], East Baton Rouge Parish, Louisiana. Ponds ranged from 0.10 to 0.25 acres with mean depths of approximately 2 feet (13-27 inches). Water was supplied from a 2,400-foot deep artesian well with 85 psi pressure when pumped. Well water had dissolved oxygen of 0.6 ppm and contained primarily sodium and calcium, with minor amounts of boron and silicon.

Three experimental rations were prepared:

1. **Raw crawfish waste**: Fresh waste from [[Naquin's Seafood Processing Plant]] in Breaux Bridge, Louisiana was frozen, then thawed and ground through a 3/8-inch die meat grinder. Material had approximately 70% moisture content.

2. **Pelleted crawfish waste**: Waste was dried 24 hours at 176°F, ground into meal in a Wiley mill, then mixed with 1.5% bentonite clay and ~12% water and pelleted into 1/8" × 1/4" pellets using a California Pellet Mill. Final moisture content was 2-4%.

3. **Commercial control**: Purina Farm Pond Fish Chow containing 32% crude protein, 2.5% crude fat, and multiple ingredients including fish meal, soybean meal, brewer's dried yeast, and vitamin/mineral fortification.

Analysis of dried crawfish waste meal revealed:
- Moisture: 3.4%
- Crude protein (apparent): 40.2%
- True protein (corrected for chitinous nitrogen): 35.8%
- Ether extract: 4.8%
- Crude fiber: 14.2%
- Calcium: 18.1%
- Phosphorus: 1.2%
- Inorganic matter: 29.0%

[[Channel Catfish]] fingerlings (3-14 inches length) were obtained from Delhi Fish Farm, Monroe, Louisiana on March 23, 1966, treated with formalin in transit and 3 ppm potassium permanganate prophylactically, then held in a single pond until stocking. On June 1, 1966, fish were counted, weighed, dipped in 500 ppm potassium permanganate for 6 seconds, and stocked at 1,455 fish per acre. The experiment used a randomized block design with ponds blocked by size and treatments randomly assigned within blocks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The shallow experimental ponds presented extreme thermal conditions. Surface temperatures reached 103°F while maximum bottom temperatures (at ~28 inches depth) reached 88°F. Recording thermometers in pond M-6 from June 15 to July 13 documented this thermal stratification. Despite these high temperatures, [[Channel Catfish]] continued feeding on all rations throughout the experimental period, as confirmed by examination of feeding trays placed on pond bottoms.

Traditional aquaculture recommendations suggested reducing feeding rates by half when air temperatures exceed 90°F due to increased fish respiration rates and decreased oxygen saturation. This study challenged that conventional wisdom, demonstrating active feeding at temperatures previously considered limiting. Harry DuPree at the Southeastern Fish Cultural Laboratory in Marion, Alabama confirmed similar observations of channel catfish feeding at 94-98°F.

The shallow pond depth (mean 2 feet vs. standard recommended 5 feet) may have provided advantages for earlier spring warming. Water temperatures reached 70°F (optimal for feeding response) two weeks earlier than in a nearby 17-acre pond with 4-foot average depth. However, shallow water also increased vulnerability to predators.

Dissolved oxygen was measured at critical times (4:00-6:00 a.m.) in each pond. A 28-hour monitoring period documented oxygen and carbon dioxide pulses, pH, and temperature fluctuations. One complete fish kill occurred in pond M-9 on July 21, 1966 due to oxygen depletion following die-off of a heavy algae bloom after three overcast days.

The pond ecosystem included various organisms beyond the stocked catfish:
- Water snakes present in ponds M-5, M-6, M-8, and M-10
- Turtles in ponds M-5, M-8, and M-10
- Bullfrog tadpoles numerous in first month
- [[Green Sunfish]] appeared in M-6 during August sampling (up to 64 fish, 1-3 inches, per seine haul)
- Evidence of raccoon and nutria predation (tracks observed)

Stomach analysis of captured turtles indicated they consumed raw crawfish waste. Green sunfish were known to feed on the commercial ration, likely increasing feed conversion values and reducing catfish production in affected ponds.

## Key Findings

**Feed Performance and Growth:**

Mean weight gains over 105 days were:
- Raw crawfish waste: 0.232 lbs
- Pelleted crawfish waste: 0.345 lbs  
- Commercial ration: 0.447 lbs

Mean harvest weights were:
- Raw crawfish waste: 0.332 lbs
- Pelleted crawfish waste: 0.452 lbs
- Commercial ration: 0.552 lbs

Analysis of variance showed differences between replications were insignificant, but differences between treatments approached significance. Orthogonal comparisons revealed the two pelleted rations together varied significantly (P < 0.05) from raw crawfish waste. No significant difference existed between crawfish pellets and commercial pellets.

**Feed Conversion (S values):**

S conversion values (pounds feed added / total pounds fish produced) ranged from 2.2 to 7.9:
- Raw crawfish waste: mean 4.6
- Pelleted crawfish waste: mean 2.8
- Commercial ration: mean 2.3

One extreme value (7.9 for pond M-10) influenced overall analysis. When analyzed without this outlier, no significant differences were found among treatments.

**Survival Rates:**

Mean survival percentages were poor across all treatments:
- Raw crawfish waste: 62.2%
- Pelleted crawfish waste: 76.1%
- Commercial ration: 79.4%

Poor survival was attributed to multiple factors: non-uniform fingerling sizes at stocking (3-14 inches), predation by snakes (inversely proportional to survival in three ponds), raccoons, nutria, bullfrogs, and turtles, competition with [[Green Sunfish]] in pond M-6, and shallow water increasing predator access.

**Feed Characteristics and Handling:**

Raw crawfish waste presented significant practical problems:
- Rapidly dispersed upon water contact, creating a red "cloud" of suspended material
- Only shell material reached bottom feeding areas; liquids and solubles carried off in surface waters
- Messy and troublesome to prepare and handle
- Required 3 hours to thaw 1-pound frozen blocks
- Objectionable odor from volatile substances released during enzymatic and bacterial breakdown
- Red pigment stained materials on contact
- Approximately 70% moisture content meant only ~1/3 the solid material of pelleted forms at same feeding rate

Pelleted crawfish waste was superior in handling:
- Easy to store in sealed metal cans
- No noticeable swelling at 119°F with high humidity (commercial pellets enlarged 50%)
- Maintained structural integrity (commercial feed became crumbly after one week)
- Slower breakdown rate: 20-35 minutes vs. 10-15 minutes for commercial feed
- Additional availability time may have allowed smaller fish to feed

**Feed Acceptance:**

Aquarium observations revealed [[Channel Catfish]] behavior with crawfish materials:
- Fish immediately detected and consumed rations
- Whole pellets or ground material taken into mouth
- After 2 seconds of chewing, red shell chunks (2-3 mm long × 1 mm wide) were rejected
- Shell material found in feeding trays and lower digestive tracts
- Crawfish shell appeared to have little nutritional value

**Protein Content Variation:**

Protein percentage in crawfish waste varied from 30-40% across samples, attributed to:
- Efficiency of processing workers (amount of meat remaining with waste)
- Approximately 10% protein loss when waste left unpreserved for 24 hours (waste in this study exposed 8-10 hours)
- Variation in crawfish size
- Processing technique differences

**Sampling Challenges:**

Mean weights of catfish varied greatly between sampling intervals, with values increasing and decreasing at least once during the experiment in all but one pond. Fluctuations were attributed to:
- Schooling behavior of [[Channel Catfish]] (contagious rather than random distribution)
- After several seine hauls with few fish, subsequent hauls captured up to 20% of assumed population
- Size selectivity of seines
- Bottom type variations (soft mud caused lead line to roll up; hard areas allowed small fish to escape)
- Snags and obstructions affecting seine efficiency

**Shallow Pond Feasibility:**

The study demonstrated that [[Channel Catfish]] can be successfully cultivated in shallow (2-foot mean depth) ponds despite extreme surface temperatures. Benefits included earlier spring warming and faster temperature optimization. Primary disadvantages were increased predator exposure and potential oxygen depletion during algae die-offs. The critical depth for dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide concentrations in summer stratification (normally 5 feet in 2-acre ponds per Byrd 1942) was not applicable to these shallow systems.

**Rice-Fish Farming Context:**

The shallow pond design relates to rice-fish farming practices common in southwestern Louisiana, where rice fields undergo 1-3 year wet fallow periods. Benefits of fish-rice rotation include: reduced irrigation water costs, fish crop production on otherwise unproductive land, domestic or sporting fish supply, and erosion prevention through maintained flooding.

**Recommendations:**

The study concluded that crawfish waste is a potential feed for catfish when fed in pelleted form but not when fed raw. Work should progress toward formulating a low-cost catfish ration substituting crawfish meal for fish meal as the animal protein source. The 3% feeding rate for raw waste could theoretically be increased to 9% to match solid content of pelleted forms, but this would rapidly foul water and cause oxygen depletion from bacterial breakdown.
