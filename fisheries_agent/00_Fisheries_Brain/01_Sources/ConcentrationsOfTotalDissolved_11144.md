## Executive Summary

This 1983 study by Pimentel and Bulkley (*Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*) investigated the preferred and avoided concentrations of **total dissolved solids (TDS)** in juveniles of three endangered/threatened Colorado River fishes: the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]]. Using a laboratory salinity-gradient chamber, researchers determined that the Colorado squawfish and humpback chub preferred TDS concentrations closely matching present-day conditions in the Green and Colorado rivers, whereas the bonytail preferred concentrations roughly four times higher than ambient river levels. All three species avoided TDS concentrations far exceeding regulatory or agricultural thresholds, suggesting that increased salinization from oil-shale processing, mineral extraction, and water development in the upper [[Colorado River Basin]] would harm municipal, industrial, and agricultural water users before it would directly threaten these fish. The findings support hypotheses about historical habitat selection, migratory cueing via salinity gradients, and possible predator-avoidance functions of TDS preference in the bonytail.

## Background & Description

While this paper is primarily physiological/behavioral rather than morphological, key biological descriptors of the study organisms include:

- **Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)**: The largest minnow endemic to North America; historically ranged throughout the entire Colorado River system, now restricted to the main stems of the Green and upper Colorado rivers. Test subjects were yearlings, 2.5–5.0 cm long.
- **Humpback chub (*Gila cypha*)**: Not described scientifically until 1946; distinguished by apparent habitat specialization for deep, swift water in steep canyon reaches. Test subjects were young-of-the-year, 2.5–5.0 cm long.
- **Bonytail (*Gila elegans*)**: Once the most abundant fish in the main channels of the Colorado River system; now the rarest, found in the study period only in Lake Mohave and Desolation Canyon of the Green River. Test subjects were young-of-the-year, 2.5–5.0 cm long.

All experimental fish were juveniles/yearlings, chosen because preliminary trials showed juveniles were more sensitive to TDS than larger, older fish, and because smaller body size permitted larger sample sizes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat range**: The [[Green River]], [[Little Colorado River]], and upper [[Colorado River]] constitute the present-day range for these three species, with the humpback chub restricted to deep, swift canyon reaches and the bonytail now confined largely to reservoir (Lake Mohave) and Desolation Canyon habitats.
- **Ambient TDS conditions**: Green River TDS ranges 212–756 mg/liter; upper main stem Colorado River ranges 285–1,330 mg/liter (USGS 1978).
- **Trophic/predatory context**: The Colorado squawfish is noted as the historical primary piscivorous predator within this river system, with the paper proposing that bonytail's unusually high TDS preference may function as a spatial refuge from predation.
- **Anthropogenic pressures**: Reservoir construction, altered flow regimes, agricultural diversion, desalination management, and emerging oil-shale/mineral extraction activities are identified as key threats increasing TDS concentrations in the upper Colorado River Basin, with the potential to eliminate historic backwater nursery habitats.
- **Species status context**: All three species were federally recognized as endangered/threatened at time of writing; the [[Bonytail]] was noted as nearest to extinction, with no documented survival of reservoir-spawned young.

## Key Findings

### TDS Preference and Avoidance Concentrations (juveniles, pooled data)

| Species | TDS Preferred (mg/L) | TDS Avoided (mg/L) | Test Temperature (°C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Colorado Squawfish]] | 560–1,150 | >4,400 | 14–16 |
| [[Humpback Chub]] | 1,000–2,500 | >5,100 | 12 |
| [[Bonytail]] | 4,100–4,700 | <560 or >6,600 | 16–18 |

- **Colorado squawfish and humpback chub** selected TDS concentrations closely matching present-day river conditions, suggesting no significant behavioral avoidance issue under current salinity regimes.
- **Bonytail** preferred TDS levels ~4x higher than current ambient river concentrations, and uniquely avoided *low* TDS (<560 mg/liter) as well as very high TDS (>6,600 mg/liter) — a distinctive bimodal avoidance pattern not seen in the other two species.
- Chi-square "goodness of fit" tests confirmed all three species' treatment distributions diverged significantly from uniform distribution (p < 0.001), indicating genuine behavioral grouping around preferred TDS zones rather than random dispersal.
- Schooling tendency affected statistical independence: humpback chub showed the strongest schooling behavior (control χ² = 48.0), while Colorado squawfish showed the weakest (control χ² = 12.8), affecting the reliability of chi-square independence tests.

### Ecological and Management Implications

- **Migratory/nursery cue hypothesis**: Following McInerney (1964) and Reynolds & Thomson (1974), the authors propose that juvenile bonytail historically used high-TDS gradients as an orientation cue toward warm, shallow backwater nursery habitats — analogous to salinity-guided migration in Pacific salmon (*Oncorhynchus* spp.) and the grunion *Leuresthes sardina*. These backwater habitats have been lost or degraded due to reservoir construction and flow regulation.
- **Predator-avoidance hypothesis**: The high TDS preference of bonytail may have served to spatially isolate juveniles from Colorado squawfish (their primary historical predator), since squawfish avoid the TDS range preferred by bonytail.
- **Ontogenetic considerations**: The TDS preference for bonytail may decline as fish mature, consistent with documented ontogenetic shifts in salinity preference among other fish taxa (Baggerman 1960; Houston 1957; Otto & McInerney 1970).
- **Temperature-salinity interaction**: Because temperature affects osmoregulatory capacity, TDS avoidance thresholds measured at the study's fixed temperatures (12–18°C) may not reflect winter conditions in the Colorado River system, where temperatures can drop to 1.0°C while TDS remains elevated — flagged as a research gap.
- **Human water-use context**: TDS avoidance thresholds for all three species (4,400–6,600+ mg/liter) far exceed the regulatory salinity standard for the Colorado River at Imperial Dam (879 mg/liter, EPA 1976) and the US–Mexico treaty limit (~115±30 mg/liter above ambient). The paper concludes that agricultural, municipal, and industrial water users would suffer economic harm (estimated $450,000/year per 1 mg/liter TDS increase, USDI 1981) long before TDS increases would directly threaten juvenile survival of these endangered fishes.
- **Localized risk**: Despite low basin-wide risk, tributaries receiving oil-shale process waters (TDS 1,750–24,500 mg/liter, averaging 6,800 mg/liter; Fox et al. 1978) could exceed avoidance thresholds and cause localized habitat loss for these species.
- **Caveat**: Findings apply only to juvenile life stages; the authors note that eggs and larvae may be more sensitive to TDS and were not assessed in this study, representing a key area for future research within the broader [[Colorado River Fishery Project]].
