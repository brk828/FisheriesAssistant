# Distribution of Aquatic Macroinvertebrates in Three Sonoran Desert Springbrooks

#freshwater-ecology #aquatic-macroinvertebrates #desert-springs

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Meffe and Marsh (1983)]], published in the *Journal of Arid Environments*, documents the occurrence and distribution of aquatic macroinvertebrates in three springbrooks near Bylas, south-east Arizona: [[First Spring]], [[Second Spring]], and [[Salt Creek]]. The authors report that taxonomic richness was low at spring sources and rose in middle and lower reaches. They attribute this pattern to harsh physico-chemical conditions at the sources that ease downstream. A comparison with desert streams shows that springs support markedly poorer faunas, a pattern the authors suggest may be general for springs in desert and temperate regions.

## Background & Description

Aquatic macroinvertebrate communities of the [[Sonoran Desert]] were described as poorly known. Earlier work mostly addressed individual taxa, range extensions, or taxonomic revisions, and few studies had examined invertebrates of Sonoran Desert springs. Springs are described as distinctive habitats. Their sources are characterized by stable temperature, discharge, and dissolved gas and solid content. Many springs show high temperature, high dissolved solids, free [[Carbon Dioxide]], and low [[Dissolved Oxygen]] and pH, all of which are stressful to some aquatic organisms. Physico-chemical gradients usually develop downflow, so variability increases with distance from the source.

The three study systems lie at about 800 m elevation, north-east of Bylas, Arizona:

- **First Spring** has two sources, Bos Spring and [[Medicine Spring]], which join into one brook. Discharge was about 1.0 l/s, and the lower reach disappears into distributaries on the [[Gila River]] floodplain. The maximum observed flow distance during 1980–1982 was 0.8 km.
- **Second Spring** had discharge below 0.1 l/s and disappears through evapotranspiration and percolation into alluvium. Its maximum flow distance was 0.4 km.
- **Salt Creek** had discharge of about 2.6 l/s, with a maximum flow distance of 1.0 km. It was the most intensively studied site.

Channels were 0.2 to 2.0 m wide, with current velocities up to 0.5 m/s. Major plants were similar across systems and included sedges (*Eleocharis* spp., *Scirpus americana*), naiad (*Najas guadalupensis*), pondweed (*Zannichellia palustris*), and the alga *Vaucheria* sp. Stonewort (*Chara* sp.) seasonally choked many channel sections. Riparian vegetation included lizardtail (*Anemopsis californicum*), velvet mesquite (*Prosopis velutina*), and introduced saltcedar (*Tamarix chinensis*). The surrounding landscape is typical Southwestern desertscrub dominated by creosotebush.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Temperature, dissolved oxygen, and pH were measured intermittently from August 1980 to March 1982. Water samples were analysed in the laboratory for conductivity (at 25 °C) and total alkalinity. Dissolved carbon dioxide was calculated from pH and alkalinity. Macroinvertebrates were collected with dip nets, aquarium nets, and an [[Ekman Dredge]] on 26 March, 16 April, 29 May, and 10 July 1982. Sampling covered each springhead and middle section, plus lower reaches of First Spring and Salt Creek. Samples were sieved (0.991 mm and 0.244 mm), preserved in 10% formalin, and examined macro- and microscopically. The lower First Spring reach dried after initial sampling, so it was sampled only once.

**Physico-chemical gradients.**

- *Temperature:* 29.0–32.2 °C at the four sources, decreasing downflow except during high summer ambient temperatures. Extremes at downstream sites were 17.0 °C and 36.7 °C, both in Second Spring.
- *pH:* Most sources had low pH (about 7.6), rising to 8.5–9.0 within 20–30 m downstream. Medicine Spring stayed at pH 8.8–9.0 along its entire length.
- *Dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide:* Carbon dioxide was high at three sources and fell quickly downstream. Dissolved oxygen showed the opposite pattern in all four systems.
- *Conductivity:* High at most sites, at 3400–5050 µmhos/cm, with no consistent longitudinal trend. Medicine Spring was distinctly lower, at 1350–1700 µmhos/cm, which the authors interpret as a different water source.

## Key Findings

### Richness and Distribution

- A total of **53 macroinvertebrate taxa**, including **46 insects**, were recorded across the sites (Table 1 covers nine collecting localities).
- Four non-insect taxa occurred at all permanent sites: a planarian, the amphipod [[Hyalella Azteca]], and two hydrobiid snails ([[Fontelicella]] sp. and [[Tryonia]] sp.).
- In contrast, **29 insect taxa** were found at only one or two sites. Roughly **55%** of total taxonomic richness consisted of rare taxa.
- Headsprings held fewer species than downstream reaches. The extra richness downstream came mainly from Coleoptera and Diptera, with some Hemiptera and the mayfly *Baetis* sp., which was never found at springheads.
- Three rare taxa (two elmid beetles and one hydrophilid beetle), each known from a single specimen, occurred only at springheads. The authors call them possible "springhead endemics," but the sample sizes are too small to support firm conclusions.

### Abundance

- The most abundant taxa, by visual estimate, were *H. azteca* and the two hydrobiid snails. All were concentrated near the sources.
- Amphipods were especially abundant in filamentous algal mats and *Chara*. The snails reached thousands per square metre on both hard and soft substrates.
- Odonates and planarians were widespread, and the physid snail was most numerous at lower sites. Burrowing forms such as chironomid larvae were not abundant at any locality.

### Spring Versus Stream Comparison

- Comparison with [[Monkey Spring]] (a spring in southern Arizona), [[Sycamore Creek]] (an intermittent Verde River tributary), and [[Aravaipa Creek]] (a permanent San Pedro River tributary) showed that **springs support roughly one-half to one-quarter as many species as streams**. This matches the pattern reported from springs elsewhere.
- Springs were poorly represented in Ephemeroptera, Hemiptera, Trichoptera, Coleoptera, and Diptera, at both the familial and specific levels. Hirudinea, Collembola, Plecoptera, Megaloptera, and Lepidoptera were absent from springs but contributed little to the overall difference.
- Non-insect richness was similar between habitat types. Hydrobiids and amphipods, however, were found almost exclusively in springs.
- Table 2 totals are partly garbled in the source scan. The legible totals for the spring systems are 46 and 27 species, and for the stream systems 103 and 104+ species.

### Interpretation

- Low richness at springheads is attributed mainly to the unusual abiotic conditions at sources. This is consistent with earlier work by [[Brues]] (1932), [[Mason]] (1939), [[Sloan]] (1956), and [[Abbott and Hoese]] (1960), who linked spring diversity to temperature, low dissolved oxygen, and high carbon dioxide.
- [[Island Biogeography]] constraints, such as small colonization area and isolation, seem unlikely to be the main cause, because springs are close to streams and most aquatic groups are highly vagile.
- Streams offer more microhabitat diversity, which may support a greater variety of invertebrates.
- The authors call for further work on the effects of abiotic constraints, size-area relationships, and microhabitat selection in springs and streams within the same region.
