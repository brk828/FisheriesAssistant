# Chinook Salmon Pond Rearing Studies (Decker Flat, Idaho)

## Executive Summary
This Idaho Fish and Game report (Job Completion Report, DJ 49-R-5/6, Job 7; Feb. 1968) documents an experimental pond-rearing program for [[Chinook Salmon]] (spring-run, *Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) near Stanley, Idaho. A five-surface-acre pond was created from an old gravel borrow pit, equipped with concrete inlet/outlet structures and electric self-cleaning rotary drum screens, and supplied by both [[Salmon River]] diversion water and groundwater seepage. The study tracked two brood-years (1966 and 1967) of chinook salmon eggs from the [[Decker Flat]] trapping/spawning weir through incubation at the [[Hayden Creek Experimental Facility]], fingerling rearing, and pond stocking. The 1966 brood-year experienced heavy losses from bacterial gill disease and a raceway screen failure, leaving only 12,000 fingerlings to stock the pond in July 1967. These fish were fed through fall but "disappeared" from observation as water temperatures dropped below 42°F, presumed to have moved into substrate interstices to overwinter. Winter physical-chemical monitoring (Table 1) suggested pond conditions (dissolved oxygen, ice/water depth, temperature) remained adequate for survival. The 1967 brood-year suffered catastrophic (>98%) mortality attributed to malachite green fungicide toxicity during incubation, effectively ending that year's rearing trial.

## Background & Description
- **Stocking size (1966 brood-year, July 28, 1967):** Average fork length = 65 mm; average weight = 3.3 g (approximately 138 fish per pound).
- **Marking:** 7,500 of the 12,000 stocked juveniles received a right ventral fin-clip prior to transfer, enabling future identification/recapture.
- **Developmental stage effects (1967 brood-year):** Malachite green exposure during incubation caused coagulated yolk tissue and internal organ damage; mortality concentrated at the point fry "buttoned up" (yolk sac absorption/emergence stage).
- **Disease morphology (1966 brood-year):** Bacterial gill disease diagnosed during February–April 1967 rearing period at Hayden Creek raceways, prior to pond stocking.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Life stage examined:** Egg → eyed egg → swim-up fry → fingerling, spanning hatchery incubation, raceway rearing, and pond grow-out — a critical freshwater rearing phase preceding smoltification and outmigration for this [[Anadromous Fish|anadromous]] species.
- **Habitat structure:** A converted gravel borrow pit pond (~5 surface acres), depth maintained at ~6 ft during spring/summer/fall via river diversion, dropping to ~34 inches in winter when reliant solely on groundwater seepage.
- **Seasonal ice dynamics:** Thick ice and snow cover from late November to mid-April; by January 1968, ice reached 19 inches over only 15 inches of water.
- **Overwintering behavior:** Juvenile chinook ceased surface feeding response and became undetectable via boat and SCUBA survey once water temperature fell to ≤42°F, coinciding with a hypothesized retreat into substrate/rock interstices — an inferred cold-water refuge/dormancy behavior rather than mortality.
- **Water chemistry as habitat constraint:** Dissolved oxygen ranged 7–11 ppm and free CO₂ 4–5 ppm across the monitoring period (Table 1), with groundwater seepage cited as the primary oxygen source sustaining fish life under ice cover.
- **Trophic/feeding ecology:** Fed a formulated diet (Idaho dry diet, No. 3 pellet size), hand-distributed at the pond inlet; feeding response was noted as inconsistent, with only a small proportion of fish observed responding to feed.
- **Predation/loss pathways:** Population loss mechanisms identified included raceway screen failure (downstream escapement of ~148,000 fingerlings), disease, and rotenone treatment used to eradicate resident competitor/predator fish in the pond prior to stocking.

## Key Findings
- **Pond construction:** A ~5-acre rearing pond was built near Stanley, Idaho (10 miles south, upper [[Salmon River]]) from an old gravel pit; concrete headworks with electric rotary drum screens were installed in 1966–1967.
- **1966 brood-year survival trajectory:**
  - ~250,000 eggs collected at [[Decker Flat]] weir, eyed at Sweetwater Eyeing Station.
  - ~230,000 survived to swim-up stage (Jan. 1967, Hayden Creek raceways).
  - Bacterial gill disease outbreak (Feb–Apr 1967) treated with 1:500,000 Hyamine solution (1 hr/day, 3 days).
  - ~160,000 fingerlings remained by May 1967.
  - Raceway screen failure (late May 1967) reduced surviving stock to just 12,000 fish.
  - These 12,000 were stocked into the pond on July 28, 1967 (7,500 fin-clipped).
  - ~600 lbs of feed distributed Aug–mid-Oct 1967.
  - Fish became undetectable by mid-October 1967 as temperatures dropped; presumed overwintering in substrate.
  - Survival outcome to be assessed in 1968.
- **1967 brood-year failure:** ~286,000 eggs from Decker Flat transported to Hayden Creek; malachite green prophylactic treatment caused coagulated yolk and organ damage, producing >98% mortality by January 20, 1968 — mortality occurred predominantly at fry emergence ("buttoning up"). This loss jeopardized continuation of 1968 rearing experiments due to lack of alternative Idaho chinook stock.
- **Winter physical-chemical monitoring (Table 1, Oct 1967–Jan 1968):**
  | Date | Water Depth | Ice Thickness | Dissolved O₂ | Free CO₂ | Water Temp |
  |------|-------------|---------------|--------------|----------|------------|
  | 10/31/67 | 72" | – | 10 ppm | 5 ppm | 44°F |
  | 12/1/67 | 30" | 5" | 10 ppm | – | 33°F |
  | 12/20/67 | 24" | 10" | 11 ppm | 4 ppm | 33°F |
  | 1/22/68 | 15" | 19" | 7 ppm | 4 ppm | 33°F |
  - Air temperatures reached as low as -40°F in January 1968, disabling rotary screens and cutting off river water supply, dropping pond depth from 6 ft to ~34 inches.
  - Despite extreme ice buildup, dissolved oxygen remained adequate (7–11 ppm) throughout winter, supporting the hypothesis that groundwater seepage sustains fish survival under ice.
- **Management recommendation:** Continue measuring pond rearing capacity, chinook growth, and survival rates in 1968 and 1969; pursue replacement 1967 brood-year fry from another agency given the malachite green–related loss.
- **Unresolved question:** Direct confirmation of chinook survival through winter under ice could not be obtained; conclusions on overwintering success remain inferential, pending 1968 recapture/return data to the pond and downriver fisheries.
