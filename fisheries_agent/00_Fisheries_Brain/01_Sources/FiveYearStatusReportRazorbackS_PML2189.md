# Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) Status Review and Recovery Documentation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This compilation of California Department of Fish and Game (CDFG) and cooperating agency documents (1986 to 1991) reviews the status of the [[Razorback Sucker]], a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The species was listed as endangered; the 1987 five-year status review recommends retaining that classification. Declines follow dam construction, water diversion, channelization and the introduction of [[Exotic Fish Species]] that prey on eggs, larvae and fingerlings. Remnant populations persist in [[Lake Mohave]], [[Senator Wash Reservoir]], Lake Mead and the upper basin, but natural recruitment is largely absent. The documents also describe a [[Lake Mohave Backwater Rearing Facility]] plan, a predator-free rearing and tagging program intended to augment the adult population.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and common names:** Razorback sucker, also called humpback sucker, for a sharp-edged dorsal hump that develops with size and age.
- **Size:** One of the largest suckers, reaching sizes of roughly 10 to 16 lb (figures as given in source).
- **Morphology:** Elongated head, laterally compressed body, subterminal mouth, lightly papillose lips, dorsal fin with 13 to 16 rays, anal fin with 7 rays, and 36 to 50 gill rakers.
- **Coloration:** Dusky brown to olivaceous on the back, yellowish on the belly, with a dark lateral stripe. Spawning males develop breeding tubercles; spawning females become darker with a broader, fleshier urogenital region.
- **Larvae:** Difficult to separate from other catostomid larvae. Diagnostic traits are a deep median cleft in the lip, dorsal fin ray count and melanophore distribution.
- **Maturity and growth:** Maturity begins around 5 to 7 years of age. Under laboratory conditions the nuchal hump begins to develop at about 2+ years (6 in standard length).
- **Spawning:** Occurs at 12 to 18 °C over gravel substrates, with reported eggs and larvae drifting downstream.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preference:** In the upper basin, adults favor stagnant or backwater areas and low-gradient, sandy-bottomed mainstem locations. They prefer warm tributary mouths (summer mean of 72 °F), such as the mouth of the Yampa River, entering colder main-river waters (35 to 55 °F).
- **Lower basin:** Shallow pools and backwaters are critical for juveniles. Adults have adapted to reservoirs such as Lake Mead, Lake Mohave and Senator Wash Reservoir.
- **Habitat alteration:** More than 20 major dams on the Colorado River and its tributaries have altered flow, temperature and substrate, reducing warm shallow spawning, nursery and feeding areas.
- **Exotic predators:** [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*) and catfish prey heavily on eggs, larvae and small fish. Stocked larvae were rapidly consumed in Marsh and Brooks' predation studies.
- **Hybridization:** Hybrids with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[White Sucker]], [[Longnose Sucker]] and [[Gila Sucker]] have been documented. Hybridization is suggested as a possible contributor to the gradual decline, particularly where exotic catostomids were introduced.
- **Recruitment failure:** Lake Mohave contains an estimated 60,000 individuals, mostly 25 to 45 years old. Larvae disappear at about 10 to 12 mm, and recruitment is described as extremely limited or absent.

## Key Findings

**Status and distribution**
- The 1987 five-year status review (Ellis, March 10, 1987) recommends retaining Endangered status.
- Upper basin: fish concentrated in only two places (10 to 25 individuals) near the Yampa River mouth and Walker Wildlife Area, Grand Junction, Colorado. No fish reported in Wyoming since closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1962.
- Lower basin: records are sporadic. A few large fish came from Imperial Reservoir in 1973. A 1980s CDFG netting survey of the lower river found none. Seven young fish were collected from reservoirs near the Coachella Canal in 1994 (as stated in the document).
- Senator Wash Reservoir holds the remnant population in California; the Mohave population is the largest known in nature.

**Historic abundance**
- Once among the most abundant large fishes. Harvested commercially in southern Arizona and collected "by the wagon-load" from canals near Phoenix. Declines became precipitous after the early 1950s.

**Management and recovery**
- The [[Ulmer 1985 Management Plan]] (Region 5 Informational Bulletin 0013-10-1985) set a primary goal of improving status by securing existing populations and establishing new self-sustaining populations. It covers 1986 to 1995.
- Propagation is at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]].
- Marsh (1986) recommended avoiding stocking into predator-occupied habitats, stocking larger fish (about 150 mm TL), stocking in cool seasons, preserving at least 25 specimens, and considering rotenone or antimycin sampling.
- The 1990 Native Fish Work Group (NFWG) achieved its goals. Yuma Cove was modified into a spawning cove in less than 8 months. In January 1990, 90 sexually ripe fish were stocked for on-site production.
- Experiments showed that larvae could grow to the juvenile stage in predator-free backwaters. Growth of 6 to 8 mm per day was achieved in hatcheries (approximately 12 to 18 months).
- The NFWG goal is to release a minimum of 10,000 fish of 25 cm length into Lake Mohave over five years. Anticipated output is 2,000 or more young fish annually.

**Tagging and monitoring**
- PIT (passive integrated transponder) tags were judged superior, with 100% tag verification for razorback suckers. Estimated cost for 1,000 fish was about $7,600 (PIT) or $16,500 (coded wire tag).
- Earlier tagging (1988 to 1990) marked 2,152 suckers, with 116 returns and 62 recaptures from earlier years (2.8% return rate).
- Monitoring is planned bi-annually in Lake Mohave (November and March).

**Estimated cost and timeline**
- Proposed schedule runs from 1990 to 1992, including NEPA compliance, barrier construction, stocking cycles and progress reports. Costs are listed for barrier construction (about $15,000 to $20,000 range as stated) and tagging equipment ($5,000 to $15,500).

**Concluding recommendations**
- Protect existing populations and reintroduce fish to unmodified historical areas.
- Define egg and larval survival requirements and maintain habitat before reintroduction, as stated in the 1990s-era recovery document.
- Conduct electrophoretic comparison of Parker Dam, Lake Mohave and Senator Wash stocks before selecting broodstock.
