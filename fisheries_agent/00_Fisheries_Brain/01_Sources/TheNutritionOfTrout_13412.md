# The Nutrition of Trout: Cortland Hatchery Report No. 12 (1943)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Fisheries Research Bulletin (No. 5) reports the 1943 work of the [[Cortland Hatchery]], a cooperative facility run by the [[New York State Conservation Department]], the United States Fish and Wildlife Service, and [[Cornell University]]. The research covered trout anemia and vitamin requirements, red blood cell variation among four trout species, carbohydrate digestion, protein utilization, practical hatchery diets, fry feeding, and mass production of house fly maggots as a feed.

The main result is that trout anemia can be cured by a combination of [[Riboflavin]], [[Pantothenic Acid]], and [[Pyridoxine]]. The study also found that [[Alewife]] is a good trout food but contains a vitamin B1-destroying enzyme. House fly maggots satisfied the fresh-meat factor but were linked to spinal curvature whose cause remained unresolved.

## Background & Description

The report is the twelfth in the Cortland Hatchery series and follows earlier nutrition reports (Reports No. 8 to 11). Work was conducted under wartime conditions, when hatcheries had limited access to fresh meat and dry feed ingredients were rising in price, which made practical, low-cost diets a priority.

Trout species studied were [[Brook Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], and [[Lake Trout]]. Most experiments used brook trout. Feeds were tested on yearlings, fingerlings, and fry, and the experiments ran for periods of 12 to 24 weeks, often in replicated groups.

The authors were A. V. Tunison and D. R. Brockway (New York State Conservation Department), H. B. Shaffer and J. M. Maxwell (United States Fish and Wildlife Service), and C. M. McCay, C. E. Palm, and D. A. Webster (Cornell University). The maggot production note was written by C. E. Palm and D. A. Webster.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Experiments were run under controlled hatchery conditions. Fry feeding trials were held at 47°F. Maggot rearing used Cornell Insectary rooms held at 70–80°F, with adult flies at 80°F and 80% humidity.

The report notes that certain fish species contain an enzyme that destroys vitamin B1 (thiamin). Deutsch and Hasler (1943) listed species with and without this enzyme. Species without it included [[Lake Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], perch, and several marine fishes. Species with it included whitefish, carp, sucker, and saltwater herring.

## Key Findings

**Trout anemia and B-complex vitamins**
- Healthy hatchery brook trout have a red blood cell count of 900,000–1,200,000 cells per cubic millimeter. Counts well below 800,000 indicate anemia with increased mortality.
- Anemia was induced with a synthetic casein–yeast–starch–dextrin diet. Riboflavin, pantothenic acid, and pyridoxine together restored a normal count within about two weeks.
- Omitting any one of these three vitamins caused failure in every series. Thiamin, choline, and nicotinic acid were not required for red cell regeneration.
- Pure vitamins matched fresh beef liver at 14 days. Fish fed pure vitamins relapsed sooner than fish fed fresh liver (about 10 days earlier, per the authors' account of the liver-fed group). The authors suggest vitamins are stored better from natural sources.
- These were short-term, preliminary experiments. The authors tentatively suggest these three vitamins may be the solution to "factor H" in trout.

**Red blood cell variation among species**
- Average counts (thousands per mm³) across the study were: rainbow 1,220; brook 1,032; brown 766; lake 642.
- All fish were fed beef liver (50%), pork spleen (48%), and salt (2%). Blood counts were taken from April 23 to September 23.

**Carbohydrate digestion (brook trout)**
- Digestibility: raw corn starch 40%, dextrin 65%, sucrose 75%.
- The raw starch and sucrose values agreed closely with earlier reports. Only one experiment used dextrin.

**Protein utilization (nitrogen balance)**
- Protein intake drops as trout grow, from about 20 g/kg/day in the first hatchery year to about 4 g/kg/day.
- Yearlings (about 30 g): optimum intake is no more than 2.9 g protein/kg/day.
- Fingerlings (8–16 g): the data suggest 5 g protein/kg/day is the maximum for efficient utilization.
- Carbohydrates interfered with protein digestion and did not materially improve protein utilization.
- Pork spleen was used as the basic test diet because it binds feeds well when mixed with 2% salt. The authors note that a large part of the fingerling meat-diet protein is probably used for energy.

**Practical diets**
- Dried whey was a satisfactory dry-feed supplement. Skim milk, buttermilk, and whey were equal in value, so the choice depends on market price. "Milkmalt" was inferior.
- [[Distillers' Dried Grains]] were not a good trout food, with higher mortality than the standard diet.
- Dried egg and dried liver were good feeds but not recommended for hatchery use because of cost. Regenerating dried egg had no benefit.
- Argentine liver meal equaled three parts of fresh beef liver. The Wilson liver meal performed worse. White fish meal was about equal to dried liver.
- Alewife produced growth equal to a good meat diet. Cooked alewife gave poor physical consistency, and oatmeal as a binder caused enlarged livers from abnormal glycogen deposition. The authors do not recommend oatmeal.
- Alewife contains the B1-destroying enzyme, and microbiological assays showed B1 deficiency in fish fed alewife diets. Hatchery managers should cook alewife or feed it on alternate days.

**Fry feeding**
- Brook, rainbow, brown, and lake trout fry were fed in a 12-week comparison of meats.
- Sheep and horse liver were about equal to each other and inferior to beef liver. Replacing half the liver with pork spleen gave nearly the same results and lowered cost.
- Adding 25% dry feed mixture to the fry diet improved results once fish reached about one inch.
- Gelatin as a binder improved the diet but was labor-intensive.

**House fly maggot production**
- Life cycle (egg to adult): 13–15 days. Eggs hatch in 18–24 hours, larvae are full-grown in 4–5 days, and pupae last 4–5 days.
- Media consisted of bran or middlings, alfalfa leaf meal, malt syrup, live yeast, and water, fermented for 12–24 hours. Sodium benzoate (0.01%) controlled mold.
- Yield was 1¼ to 2 pounds of maggots per flat. Labor was the largest cost. Maggots were harvested by washing them through 16-mesh screen and then quick-frozen.
- Maggot diets satisfied the fresh-meat factor. No anemia developed, even with only 15% maggots in the diet. Maggot-fed fish were yellow from alfalfa xanthophyll.

**Spinal curvature**
- Fish fed maggot diets developed spinal curvature starting around week 10 (Group 727) and week 16 (Group 726). Some curvatures approached 180 degrees.
- Tocopherol (vitamin E) and Viosterol (vitamin D) did not correct the condition. Body analyses showed no vitamin D deficiency, and liver B2 levels were normal. The cause was not determined.
