# Management and Recovery of the Razorback Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen

## Executive Summary

This document has two parts. The main text is Chapter 17 of a volume on recovery of long-lived species, which reviews the decline of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endemic fish of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It covers the historic record, status and biology, the political steps that led to a recovery strategy, and the choice of propagation and reintroduction as recovery tools. The appended material is 2003 correspondence and data sheets about PIT-tagged fish records and stocking at the [[Cibola High Levee Pond]], a [[USFWS]] memo on a bonytail release, and scanned field data sheets that are largely illegible in the OCR text.

## Background & Description

- The razorback sucker is a large, uniquely shaped member of a monotypic genus, endemic to the Colorado River basin. Its range once extended from Wyoming to northwestern Mexico, but it has largely disappeared.
- Early naturalists described it as large, edible and abundant in parts of the basin. Lower-basin [[Native American]] groups named it (*tsa'xnap* by Yumans, *suxyex* by Cocopahs).
- The species was long neglected because it was considered inconsequential or detrimental to sport fisheries. Concern rose only in the 1960s, when most basin fishes had already declined.
- The authors note that about half of the more than 350 works they cite are [[Gray Literature]] (agency reports), which were essential because journal coverage was thin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic decline**
- Early abundance estimates are anecdotal and sampling was limited to river crossings and camps, so exaggeration is likely.
- Many lower-basin records describe abundance in the early 1900s, and the species was lost from the [[Salton Sea]] after salinity rose.
- Decline followed impoundment. Reservoirs such as [[Lake Mead]] (filled by [[Hoover Dam]] in 1935), [[Lake Mohave]] (formed by [[Davis Dam]]) and [[Lake Havasu]] (formed by [[Parker Dam]]) changed habitat, and nonnative competitors and predators such as [[Common Carp]] and centrarchids affected eggs and larvae.
- Upper-basin work, including the 1962 [[Rotenone Poisoning]] of the Green River system before [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] filled, eliminated many fishes, and [[Hybridization]] with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] was documented.

**Current status (as summarized in the chapter)**
- Lake Mohave held the last major population in existence as of 1990, and the chapter describes it as the only large population with reproduction.
- Lower-basin remnants were scattered, and the chapter reports no evidence of recruitment to reservoir populations between 1963 and 1990.
- Upper-basin populations were small. Adults occurred in the [[Green River]], the [[Upper Colorado River]], the [[Gunnison River]] and the [[San Juan River]], and juveniles were rarely found.

## Key Findings

**Historic and status data**
- Lake Mohave, 1967: 8 razorbacks among 810 fish electrofished (about 1%). Reported lengths were almost certainly in error.
- Lake Mead: 3 fish among more than 10,000 recorded at 57 sites in 1978–1979. Eighteen were caught between 1976 and 1980, and none between 1981 and 1988.
- April 1990: an adult male was caught, and about fifteen others were seen in an apparent spawning aggregation. Two ripe males (54.8 and 61.0 cm) and a gravid female (59.2 cm) were captured in May 1990.
- Lake Mohave age structure: [[McCarthy and Minckley]] aged 70 fish sacrificed in 1981–1983. None was under 24 years old, the oldest had 44 apparent annuli, and 88.6% hatched before or during construction of the reservoir.
- Lake Powell (San Juan arm): twelve adults were tagged in 1987 and ten adults were taken in 1988.
- Green River: [[Lanigan and Tyus]] (1989) estimated 978 ± 232 adults (95% confidence limits) above Desolation Canyon from 50 recaptures of 360 fish. None was taken in Desolation or Gray canyons.

**Recruitment and juveniles**
- Only about 24 wild-looking juveniles were recorded between 1973 and 1986. Sixteen were captured before stocking or from places reintroduced fish could not have reached.
- Canals near Parker yielded 68 juveniles (38 in 1987, 3 in 1988 and 27 in 1990). Ten of 21 specimens aged by otoliths were two to seven years old, which indicates natural recruitment.
- Larvae: Razorbacks occurred in 13 of 42 larval samples (31%) from the Green River in 1984. Larval production was documented in Lakes Mohave and Havasu in the mid-1980s.

**Recovery program**
- Three problems hindered planning: fragmentary life history data, inferred rather than demonstrated causes of decline, and doubt that the species could be reestablished where it had vanished.
- The authors chose propagation and reintroduction, with Lake Mohave's population considered large enough to support research and as a source of broodstock.
- Political steps included public education, development of a conservation ethic and institutional commitment, all summarized in the chapter.

**Appended correspondence and records (2003)**
- Schooley's emails classify the Cibola High Levee Pond as disconnected from the main channel (fed through a semi-permeable levee inaccessible to fish). [[Senator Wash]] is an elevated reservoir that is filled and emptied for hydropower. All other lower-river backwaters and lakes are considered connected.
- Release records for Cibola High Levee Pond are inconsistent. About 684 fish were sampled from 1995 to 2002, and for most the data sheets do not state where they were released. Some field data came from a colleague who never sent the sheets. The summary totals include 878 fish from rearing sites to the main channel and 103 from the CRIT and Cibola releases.
- Stocking records note about 14,000 fish stocked into Cibola High Levee Pond in 1995 and an additional 14,000 in 1996. The number of PIT-tagged releases is uncertain and estimated at roughly 1,000 by 2003.
- A USFWS memo (12 December 2003) reports 134 **bonytail** (not razorback suckers) released into Lake Havasu on 5 December 2003 at the [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]] dedication. The fish were produced at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], raised at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and tagged with PIT tags.
- Scanned lower Colorado River collection datasheets could not be reliably transcribed and are not summarized.
