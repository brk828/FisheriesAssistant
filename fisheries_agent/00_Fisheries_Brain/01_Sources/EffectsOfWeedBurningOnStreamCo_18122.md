# Effects of Weed Burning on Stream Conditions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 study by [[Colbert E. Cushing Jr.]] and P. A. Olson, published in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, examines whether the burning of [[Russian Thistle]] mats in a small stream on the [[Hanford Reservation]] harms native fish. The stream was cleared by spraying the thistles with diesel oil and burning them on May 5, 1962. Observations of temperature, pH, ash deposition, water chemistry, and caged or free-ranging fish were made before, during, and after the burn. The main finding is that the burn raised stream pH to about 11.3, mainly from [[Ash Deposition]], and that this alkaline shift, rather than temperature, was lethal to juvenile [[Chinook Salmon]]. The authors describe the work as a cursory study of a single burn.

## Background & Description

The study site was a ditch on the [[Atomic Energy Commission]]'s Hanford Reservation that is periodically cleared of *Salsola kali* accumulations by [[Weed Burning]]. The work was performed under a contract between the Atomic Energy Commission and the General Electric Company.

**Study reach**
- Section of stream: 245 yards long; the thistle mat over the upper 180 yards was burned.
- Stream in the burned section: about 7 ft wide, 10 in deep, negligible water velocity.
- Volume of flow: 1.5 c.f.s.
- Thistle mat: up to 25 ft wide and 7 ft deep, completely obscuring the stream.

**Stations**
- **Station 1:** immediately below the burn area.
- **Station 2:** 173 yards below the burn. Width 7 ft 4 in, average depth just over 3 in, velocity 0.8 ft/sec.
- **Station 3:** 245 yards below the burn.

**Methods**
- Burning began at 1007 hours and lasted 40 minutes.
- Ash was estimated by weighing deposits caught in upturned garbage-can lids placed under the thistles.
- Water temperatures and samples for chemical analysis and [[Bioassay]] were collected frequently at each station.
- Native fish were present in low numbers. Live-boxes holding yearling [[Rainbow Trout]] and several native species, collected near the stream mouth, were placed at Stations 2 and 3.
- Laboratory bioassays used juvenile Chinook salmon (three fish per test, averaging 1 g) held for 16 hours in water cooled to 12 °C.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The burn produced three overlapping stressors: a thermal pulse, a sharp rise in alkalinity, and a deposit of ash. Temperature peaks showed a downstream lag and a flattened peak, and the authors suggest that latent heat absorbed from the banks may have raised post-burn temperatures. The pH shift was the more dangerous change. Ash from the estimated 20 kg deposited in the stream raised pH and dissolved minerals. Calcium and magnesium concentrations roughly doubled, potassium rose threefold, and sodium increased only slightly.

Fish behaviour showed the effect of these conditions. Caged fish in the live-boxes were visibly distressed during the temperature and pH peaks but did not die. Free-ranging native Chinook salmon fry moved downstream to escape, reaching Station 1 at 1040 hours (33 minutes after ignition) and Station 2 at 1100 hours (53 minutes). Some of these fry died. Their movement likely prolonged their exposure, which may explain why they suffered mortality when the caged fish did not.

## Key Findings

**Water temperature and pH**
- Temperatures showed a downstream lag and a flattened peak at all three stations. The authors attribute the rise in post-test temperatures partly to heat stored in the stream banks.
- pH at Station 2 rose from 7.8 to 11.1 alongside the temperature increase.
- pH at Station 1 reached 11.3 when the fire was about 15 ft away.

**Chemistry**
- Calcium and magnesium in high-pH water were about twice pre-burn levels.
- Potassium increased threefold; sodium increased slightly.
- The authors attribute these changes to the estimated 20 kg of ash deposited in the stream.

**Fish response in the field**
- Caged rainbow trout and native fish in live-boxes showed marked distress at peak temperature and pH, with no mortalities.
- Native Chinook fry escaped downstream at 1040 h (Station 1) and 1100 h (Station 2). Some native fish mortality was recorded.

**Laboratory bioassay (Table 1 summary)**
Juvenile Chinook salmon were exposed to water cooled to 12 °C. Results are reported as the time after burn start (1007 h) at which water was collected.

| Station | Collection time (minutes after start) | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1040 (33) | None died |
| 1 | 1042 (35) | All dead in 20 min |
| 1 | 1043 (38) | All dead in 30 min |
| 1 | 1044 (37) | All dead in 6 hr |
| 1 | 1046 (39) | All dead in 14 hr |
| 3 | 1034 (27) | None died |
| 3 | 1055 (48) | None died |
| 3 | 1113 (66) | All dead in 2 hr |
| 3 | 1130 (83) | Two dead in 15 hr |
| 3 | 1150 (103) | None died |

- The greatest mortality came from water collected at the times of highest temperature and pH. Because temperature was held constant in the bioassay, the authors conclude the deaths were due to chemical factors.

**Neutralization experiment**
- Water from Station 1 collected at 1042 h was cooled to 12 °C and then neutralized with [[Hydrochloric Acid]].
- Fish held in the neutralized water for 16 hours were unaffected.
- Fish in cooled, non-neutralized water from the same sample all died.
- The authors conclude that high pH is strongly lethal to juvenile Chinook salmon.

**Overall conclusion**
The burn caused alkaline stress, produced by ash deposition, that was toxic to juvenile Chinook salmon and killed some native fish. Temperature was a secondary factor. The study was limited to one burn event.
