# Castle Lake Investigation — Second Phase: Eastern Brook Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #freshwater-ecology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This study documents the second phase (1947–1954) of a long-term investigation into trout population dynamics at [[Castle Lake]], California, following a 1946 rotenone treatment that eradicated all fish species present during the first phase. The research isolated [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) as the sole stocked species from 1947–1951, then introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] beginning in 1952, allowing comparison between a single-species brook trout fishery and a multi-species assemblage. Key outcomes included dramatically improved fingerling survival (from ~1.9% to ~35%) following removal of predatory fish, establishment of a self-sustaining wild brook trout population, and economic analysis of stocking strategies (fingerling vs. yearling plants). The study concluded that a brook trout–rainbow trout combination likely yields the highest total production per acre.

## Background & Description

**Site Description — Castle Lake:**
- Location: North-central California (Siskiyou County)
- Elevation: 5,200 ft
- Surface area: 48 acres
- Maximum depth: 120 ft
- Water volume: ~1,800 acre-feet
- Origin: Glacial cirque formation with abrupt southern cliffs and a low northern moraine
- Bathymetry: Northern third shallow (12–15 ft); remainder a deep bowl
- Water source: Snowmelt and bottom springs; snow cover for ~6 months annually
- Water clarity: Secchi disk visibility 40–55 ft
- Substrate: Nearly complete silt cover; sparse aquatic vegetation due to poor soil fertility
- Bottom fauna: Moderately abundant in plant beds, scarce elsewhere
- Thermal regime: Surface temps never exceed 75°F; winter/early spring bottom temps (100 ft) drop to 40°F; thermocline develops ~20 ft in June, deepens to ~85 ft by September
- Oxygen regime: Highly variable; dissolved oxygen as low as 1.0 ppm recorded at 100 ft depth in September
- Plankton: Zooplankton moderately abundant; phytoplankton scarce; copepods historically present but reduced by 1946 rotenone treatment (one species reportedly eliminated)

**Historical Context:**
During the First Phase (1938–1946), four trout species (brook, rainbow, brown, lake trout) and two minnow species coexisted. On October 9, 1946, the lake was treated with [[rotenone]], eliminating all fish; toxicity persisted until July 1947.

**Stocking Record (Table 1):**

| Date | Quantity | Size | Fin Marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| July 3, 1947 | 20,000 brook (yearlings) | 13.3/lb | Not marked |
| Sept 19, 1947 | 20,000 brook (fingerlings) | 5.0/oz | RV |
| Aug 12, 1948 | 10,000 brook (fingerlings) | 12.8/oz | Adipose (Ad) |
| Aug 12, 1948 | 10,000 brook (fingerlings) | 12.5/oz | Adipose-Left Ventral (Ad-LV) |

No brook trout were planted 1949–1951 due to overpopulation relative to food supply. Rainbow trout stocking began in 1952, initiating the "third phase" of the broader investigation.

**Methodology:**
- Complete creel census conducted annually (except 1954, partial/estimated census)
- Census taker stationed at lakeside cabin; contacted nearly all anglers, measured fish, recorded gear/time/angler data
- Missing weekday catch data estimated via averaging of adjacent weeks' equivalent days

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predation Dynamics:**
Prior to the 1946 treatment, the lake supported large predatory [[Brown Trout]] and [[Lake Trout]], which heavily preyed upon brook trout fingerlings. This predation:
- Suppressed fingerling survival to <2% (average 1.9%, range 0.4–3.2%)
- Limited brook trout longevity to a maximum of 3 years (none survived to age 4)

Following predator removal via rotenone treatment:
- Fingerling survival to creel rose to ~35%
- Marked hatchery brook trout survived to at least 7 years of age
- The authors conclude predation was the dominant historical mortality factor limiting brook trout fingerling survival

**Food Web Effects:**
- Rotenone treatment reduced zooplankton (notably copepods), with at least one copepod species apparently eliminated
- Overstocking of 20,000 same-age/size yearlings in 1947 likely created intraspecific food competition, slowing growth and lowering condition factor
- Brook trout are largely nonpiscivorous, reducing intraspecific/interspecific predation mortality once established as a monoculture
- Diet overlap between brook and rainbow trout is substantial but not identical — likely explaining why the two-species system supports greater total biomass than brook trout alone

**Population Self-Sustainability:**
Wild (naturally spawned) brook trout began appearing in the creel starting in 1951 and increased yearly thereafter, despite no additional hatchery stocking after 1948. By 1954, 84% of the total catch was brook trout, nearly all wild-spawned — demonstrating a robust self-sustaining population even amid rainbow trout introduction.

## Key Findings

**Survival & Yield:**
- 1947 yearling plant (20,000 fish, 13.3/lb): 48.3% total return (9,669 fish) over 4 years
- 1947 fingerling plant (20,000 fish, RV mark): 34.6% return (6,923 fish) over 7 years
- 1948 fingerling plant, Ad mark: 34.1% return
- 1948 fingerling plant, Ad-LV mark: 38.7% return
- Fin-clip comparison (adipose alone vs. adipose+left ventral) showed no significant difference in survival or growth — ventral fin removal is not more harmful than adipose removal alone

**Historical Comparison (Pre- vs. Post-Treatment Fingerling Survival):**

| Metric | Pre-treatment (1943) | Post-treatment (1947–48) |
|---|---|---|
| Fingerling survival to creel | 1.9% avg (0.4–3.2%) | ~34.6–36.4% |
| Max lifespan | 3 years | 7+ years |
| Cost per fish in creel (fingerling) | $0.525 | $0.03 |
| Cost per fish in creel (yearling) | $0.375 | $0.35 |

**Growth Rates:**
- Average fork length of all brook trout caught (1947–1954): 7.0 inches
- Largest brook trout recorded: 18.4 inches fork length; heaviest: 2 lb 14 oz
- Fingerlings showed no significant post-stocking growth retardation; yearlings did show retardation, attributed to overstocking and reduced food post-rotenone
- Growth trajectory for typical fingerling plant (~12/oz): ~4.5 in (age 1+) → ~6 in (age 2+) → ~9 in (age 3+) → growth plateau near 9 in (age 4+/5+) → resumes to 10+ in following year
- 1948 fingerling cohorts reached by year 4 what the 1947 cohort reached by year 5 — attributed to improved food availability over time

**Production (Poundage):**
- Total planted (1947–48): 1,853 lbs
- Total removed by anglers (1947–54): 3,767 lbs
- Yearling plant (1947): 1,503 lbs planted → only 978 lbs removed (unprofitable; 0.65× return)
- Fingerling plant (1947): 250 lbs planted → 889 lbs removed (3.5× return)
- Fingerling plant (1948): 100 lbs planted → 923 lbs removed (9.2× return — attributed to improved food conditions, not survival differences)
- Wild fish contribution: 977 lbs

**Yield per Acre:**
- Average annual yield (brook trout only, pre-1952): 493 lbs total = 10.3 lbs/acre
- Average yield 1947–54 (including rainbow years): 9.8 lbs/acre
- Yield increased further after 1952 rainbow introduction, suggesting brook + rainbow combination outproduces brook trout alone
- Pre-1946 (4-species) yield: 10.4 lbs/acre — comparable to brook-alone, but with older piscivorous brown/lake trout reducing angler-accessible biomass

**Angling Quality Metrics:**
- Catch per hour: High (declined from 2.1 in 1947 to 0.9 in 1951 as fingerling stock aged out)
- Food quality: High (especially in lakes with red copepods, yielding pink flesh)
- Size of fish: Medium
- Gameness: Low
- Zero-catch angler-days: 42% (1941–45, 4-species) vs. 36% (1947–51, brook-only)
- Catch distribution became more equitable among anglers after brook-trout-only restocking
- Lure preference shifted from spinners (pre-1947) to flies (1947–49) and back to spinners/bait as fishing quality declined (1950–54)

**Management Recommendations:**
1. Lakes with adequate spawning springs: stock brook trout alone or with rainbow trout for cost-effective natural reproduction
2. Lakes lacking spawning habitat: consider rainbow trout alone
3. Lakes with spring-fed spawning tributaries: use spring-spawning rainbow strain
4. For trophy-size demand: stock brown trout alone
5. Avoid introducing fingerlings where brown trout already established (poor survival)
6. Yearling brook trout stocking is economically unjustified vs. fingerling stocking
7. Optimal fingerling stocking density remains lake-specific (depends on food, predators, fishing intensity)
8. Combined brook + rainbow trout stocking likely maximizes total yield per acre compared to single-species management
