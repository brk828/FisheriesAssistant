# Black and Salt Rivers Fishery Management Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #Ictaluridae #Catostomidae #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 progress report from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, Division of Fishery Services, Phoenix) summarizes preliminary fishery investigations on the [[Black River]] and [[Salt River]] in Arizona, which form the common boundary between the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] and the [[San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation]]. The survey ran from June 1963 to December 1965, in cooperation with the [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]]. The authors found a good channel catfish population in the lower Black River and parts of the Salt River, and a fair to good trout population restricted to the upper 16 miles of the Black River by excessive summer water temperatures. A gap with no significant game fish lay between these sections, so [[Smallmouth Bass]] were stocked to fill it. The report concludes that the upper Black River has high recreational fishing potential, contingent on road and campground development.

## Background & Description

The Black and Salt Rivers are effectively one stream; the Salt is formed by the confluence of the Black and White Rivers. The rivers flow west through canyons of 300 to 2,000 feet in depth. The Salt River Canyon at Highway 60 is described as the "second Grand Canyon of Arizona," with walls rising more than 3,000 feet. The Black River originates at 10,500 feet in the [[White Mountains]].

Along the 108 river miles that form the reservation boundary (76 miles of Black River and 32 miles of Salt River), the stream bed drops from 6,800 to 3,000 feet in elevation. An additional 22 miles of Salt River form the southern boundary of Fort Apache.

Vegetation changes with elevation, from [[Ponderosa Pine]] forest in the upper canyons to scrub oak, juniper, and desert-type vegetation downstream. The terrain is rugged, with few access points. Survey access relied on pickup trucks, four-wheel-drive vehicles, trolleys, and hiking.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime:** Flows peak in spring (March to April) from snowmelt and are lowest in June and July. USGS records at the gauge one mile below the Black River pumping station give a mean discharge of 153 cfs over an 11-year period ending September 1964. For October 1963 to September 1965, monthly low daily mean flows ranged from 36.6 cfs (January) to 407 cfs (April), with daily minimum and maximum flows of 21 cfs and 960 cfs.

**Water clarity:** Flows are clear at low stages. At high flow, the Black River near the pumping station becomes moderately turbid and the Salt River becomes very turbid.

**Water diversion:** The Black River is diverted at the [[Black River Pumping Station]] to Willow Creek (Gila River drainage) for mining, ore processing, and domestic supply near Morenci, Arizona. The remaining water flows to [[Roosevelt Reservoir]], which supports irrigation for the [[Salt River Project]] near Phoenix. Annual diversions ranged from 1,600 acre-feet (1963) to 5,000 acre-feet (1960 and 1962), and the report warns that excessive pumping under low water could endanger downstream fish populations.

**Water temperature:** Daily temperatures at the pumping station (May 21 to September 17, 1965) fluctuated by as much as 15°F. Maxima occurred near 5:00 p.m. and minima near 8:00 a.m. Peak temperatures occurred during the first week of July, then declined. Temperatures increased gradually downstream and decreased upstream from the pumping station. These excessive summer temperatures are cited as the limit on trout distribution.

**Tributaries:** North-side tributaries draining Fort Apache include Reservation Creek, Pacheta Creek, Big Bonito Creek, White River, Cibeque Creek, and Canyon Creek. South-side tributaries draining San Carlos include Freezeout Creek, East and West Sawmill Creeks, Deer Creek, and Cienega Creek.

## Key Findings

**Fish community (sampled by seining, gill netting, electrofishing, hook-and-line, and rotenone):**
- **Channel catfish:** good to excellent population in most reaches below the Black River crossing, and in portions of the Salt River.
- **Rainbow trout:** fair to good population in the upper 16 miles of the Black River. Winter drift carries trout downstream, where they are collected almost anywhere on the river at that time.
- **Bonytail, flannelmouth sucker, and Gila sucker:** the most numerous species, distributed throughout the system.
- **Green sunfish:** distributed throughout the system.
- **Black bullhead:** found only in the Salt River.
- Very few game fish occurred in the middle Black River, between the trout and catfish sections.

**Smallmouth bass stocking:**
- Recommended as the best-fitting sport fish for the middle section of the Black River, with three consecutive years of plants and then an evaluation period.
- First plant: August 27, 1964, 9,106 four-inch fish at three locations.
- Second plant: July 16, 1965, 7,000 two-inch fingerlings at two locations.
- Evidence of partial success: two bass (7 and 6 inches) caught August 10, 1965; three observed at Military Crossing on October 13, 1965; a 9-inch bass caught above the Highway 60 bridge in early September 1965. The bass appear to have spread over a large section of the rivers.

**Recreational potential:**
- Angling pressure is very low, mainly because of poor access, in contrast to heavy use of other Arizona streams.
- The upper 50 miles of the Black River are suitable for fee campgrounds. The section upstream of the pumping station to the reservation boundary could support an estimated 75,000 angler-days annually with road and campground development.
- Trout anglers in Arizona spend $13.68 per day, which the report expects to stimulate tribal economies.

**Recommendations and planning:**
- Development of the upper Black River should be prioritized.
- A proposed highway across the San Carlos Reservation (Barlow Pass to Highway 666) has two route options. The Eagle Creek route would not open the Black River to fishing. The northerly route, crossing the Black River near Paddy Creek and reaching Maverick, would give canyon-bottom access and serve Big Lake, and is therefore preferred.
- The two tribal councils must agree on common fishing regulations and an orderly road and campground development plan.

**Conclusion:** The survey judges the fishery potential of the Black and Salt Rivers to be high. Realizing it depends on roads, campgrounds, and tribal agreement.
