# Size at Release, Size-Selective Mortality, and Age of Maturity of Willamette River Hatchery Yearling Chinook Salmon

#ichthyology #salmonidae #chinook_salmon

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) from the [[Willamette River]] exhibit both yearling (migrate to sea after first winter) and subyearling (migrate before first winter) life histories, though this study focused exclusively on hatchery-produced yearlings. Age at maturity ranged from 3 to 6 years, with ages 4 and 5 predominating in adult returns. Maturity timing was significantly related to size at ocean entry and growth during the first ocean year. Upper-Willamette River spring Chinook salmon originate from three primary populations: [[McKenzie River]], [[North Fork Santiam River]], and [[South Fork Santiam River]]. The study examined 527,340 hatchery yearlings released 2002–2005 from [[Blind Slough]], Oregon, approximately 40 km upstream from the Columbia River mouth. Smolt-to-adult ratios (SARs) varied dramatically: 1.8 for 2002 releases versus 0.1–0.2 for 2003–2005 releases. Age-4 fish were significantly larger at ocean entry (median >155 mm FL) than age-5 and age-6 fish. Fecundity, specific growth parameters, and natural spawning characteristics were not reported.

**Habitat Associations**: Hatchery juveniles were reared at [[Willamette Hatchery]] and transported to [[Blind Slough]] for 1-week acclimation before release into the lower [[Columbia River]] estuary. Yearling migrants are thought to migrate rapidly through the estuary (estimated <2 days from Blind Slough to ocean based on PIT tag detection rates of 620 km in 16.6–17.3 days). During their first ocean summer, juveniles from Willamette River spring populations were captured on the continental shelf primarily north of the Columbia River between 44° and 48.5°N latitude in May and June, but had migrated out of the survey area by August–September. Early marine distribution overlaps with mid- and upper-Columbia River spring Chinook and Snake River spring/summer Chinook based on coded wire tag recoveries and juvenile sampling 1998–2009. Adult returns were captured in terminal gill-net fishery at [[Tongue Point]] and [[Youngs Bay]] in the Columbia River.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Yearling Chinook salmon released at Blind Slough in April–May (six release groups spaced ~10 days apart annually) migrated rapidly to the ocean, with estimated transit time <2 days based on distance and known migration rates. Release timing (early April through late May) showed no significant relationship to age at maturity (Kruskal-Wallis test: P = 0.06). Juveniles entered the ocean at 120–240 mm FL depending on release group and year. Marine distribution during first summer was concentrated on continental shelf waters north of the Columbia River mouth. Migration out of the coastal survey area occurred by late summer. Adult returns occurred 2–4 years after ocean entry, with the majority returning at ages 4 and 5.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Predation was hypothesized as the primary mechanism driving size-selective mortality, consistent with the critical-size and critical-period hypothesis. Potential predators in the Columbia River estuary and nearshore marine environment include piscivorous birds (Caspian terns, cormorants), piscivorous fishes ([[Pacific Hake]], [[Northern Pikeminnow]]), and adult salmonids. Piscivorous fishes off the Columbia River mouth primarily consume fish prey <150 mm total length. Juveniles <150 mm FL at release were consistently underrepresented in adult returns for 2002–2004 cohorts, suggesting size-selective predation during early marine residence. The study noted that trophic interactions among juvenile migrants and avian predators, piscivorous fishes, forage fishes, and adult salmonids are important mortality factors, though specific predation rates were not quantified.

**Environmental Drivers**: Ocean conditions, prey abundance and quality, and inter- and intraspecific competition were identified as probable influences on mortality and maturation, though specific environmental correlates were not analyzed in this study. The dramatic decline in SAR from 1.8 (2002 releases) to 0.1–0.2 (2003–2005 releases) occurred despite similar release sizes and intensity of size-selective mortality, indicating that factors beyond size-selective predation drive overall marine survival. First-ocean-year growth conditions appeared to influence age at maturity: fish with greater circulus spacing (indicating faster growth) during the first ocean year were more likely to mature at age 4. Oceanographic conditions during the first ocean year have been shown in other studies to influence growth, size attained, and subsequent age at maturity.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Not reported. The study focused on hatchery-produced fish rather than wild population trends. However, smolt-to-adult survival varied by an order of magnitude among cohorts (SAR = 1.8 for 2002 versus 0.1–0.2 for 2003–2005), indicating high interannual variability in marine survival independent of size-selective mortality effects.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Size-selective mortality was observed for yearling Chinook salmon released 2002–2004 but not 2005 (though 2005 sample size was limited). Juveniles entering the ocean at <150 mm FL were underrepresented in returning adult populations, suggesting elevated mortality risk for smaller individuals. However, the relationship between intensity of size-selective mortality and overall smolt-to-adult survival remains unclear, as survival declined dramatically from 2002 to 2003–2004 despite similar size distributions at release and similar intensity of size selection. Predation by marine and estuarine piscivores was hypothesized as the primary threat mechanism. The study noted that hatchery fish may have reduced predator avoidance abilities compared to naturally reared salmon due to rearing without predator interactions. Diet restrictions used to standardize release sizes among groups could potentially affect fish health and condition, though no clear evidence of pathogen infection was observed.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Managers should consider releasing hatchery yearling Chinook salmon at sizes >150 mm FL to reduce size-selective mortality during early marine residence, though this recommendation must be balanced against the observation that size-selective mortality does not explain variance in overall survival among years. The study suggests that rearing smolts larger than wild populations may increase occurrence of precocious (age-3) male life histories, which could have fitness implications for supplementation programs. Repeated sampling of migrants during juvenile migration could help disentangle how size and growth at various stages relate to marine survival. Future research should investigate naturally reared Chinook salmon to determine whether size-selective mortality patterns differ from hatchery fish. The interaction of size at release and post-release growth in the marine environment may be more important than size at release alone. Managers aiming to understand interconnections among size at release, survival, and age at maturity should consider that both larger size at ocean entry and larger size at end of first ocean year relate to earlier age at maturity.

**Biological Reference Points**: Juveniles <150 mm FL at ocean entry experienced elevated mortality for 2002–2004 cohorts. Age-4 returning adults had significantly larger median size at ocean entry (>155 mm FL) compared to age-5 and age-6 fish. Fork length at end of first ocean winter showed significant negative relationship with age at return: younger returning fish were larger after first year of ocean growth. The scale radius–fork length relationship for yearling Chinook salmon was: FL = 167.82 × SR + 44.10 (n = 217, R² = 0.95), where FL = fork length (mm) and SR = scale radius (mm). This equation was derived from juveniles and subadults 120–550 mm FL collected in ocean surveys off Oregon/Washington (1998–2002) and Columbia River estuary (2008). Age-4 fish had significantly greater average circulus spacing during first ocean year (OG1) than age-5 and age-6 fish, indicating faster growth rates. No significant difference in circulus spacing was observed between age-5 and age-6 fish.
