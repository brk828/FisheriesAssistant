# Recovery Program for Endangered Fishes Newsletter (Fall 1996)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Fall 1996 newsletter from the [[Recovery Program]] for Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin reports on efforts to recover four endangered fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]]. The lead story covers the $1 million [[Redlands Fish Ladder]] on the Gunnison River, which opened about 50 miles of upstream habitat blocked for nearly a century by the [[Redlands Diversion Dam]]. The issue also covers hatchery expansion at the [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]], the first successful rearing of wild razorback suckers to the fingerling stage, water-release agreements for flows, a [[Selenium Contamination]] study, and program accomplishments and policy updates from Program Director [[John Hamill]].

## Background & Description

- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): Once grew to more than 5 feet and was common in the basin. It is a [[Living Fossil]] that has existed in the [[Colorado River Basin]] for more than 3 million years, according to [[Ralph Morgenweck]].
- **Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Evolved in the Colorado River Basin more than 4 million years ago and exists nowhere else on Earth. Adaptations for turbulent water include depressed skulls, keeled or humped nape, buttressed fins, slim bodies, and reduced scales.
- **Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*): Now found only in a few isolated canyons of the Colorado River and its tributaries, after habitat changes from dams and non-native species.
- **Bonytail** (*Gila elegans*): Described as nearly extinct in the wild.
- **Native suckers**: Bluehead and flannelmouth suckers are among the native fish using the Redlands ladder.

The [[Colorado River Recovery Program]] is a multi-agency effort to re-establish self-sustaining populations of these four species while allowing for future water development. Participants include the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[Western Area Power Administration]], the states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming, and several environmental, water-user, and energy groups.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow alteration**: Dams have cut stream flows in the Colorado River Basin by one-third, according to Morgenweck. Water development projects have reduced flows on the Colorado and its major tributaries by at least one-third.
- **Contaminants**: Pesticides and [[Selenium]], a naturally occurring element, have entered the basin. In the Grand Valley, irrigation leaches selenium from soil into the river and adjacent wetlands, where it accumulates in organisms and fish.
- **Non-native species**: More than 40 non-native fish species have been introduced. They compete with and prey on native fish for food, space, and survival.
- **Lost habitat**: Much [[Flood Plain]] habitat has disappeared over the last century. Dams, levees, and flood-control structures prevent the river from overflowing into surrounding areas. Wetland losses in the upper basin reach tens of thousands of acres.
- **Wetland value**: Wetlands provide warm water, abundant food, and submerged vegetation for young fish, and they filter pollutants, control flooding, and support hundreds of wildlife species.

## Key Findings

### Redlands Fish Ladder (Gunnison River)
- The ladder was dedicated Aug. 14 and operates full-time. The fish enter downstream and climb a 350-foot U-shaped channel around the 12-foot-high Redlands Diversion Dam to a holding area.
- Biologists sort native from non-native fish. Non-natives are removed or returned downstream, and all native and endangered fish are sent upstream.
- About 7,000 native fish had used the ladder by press time.
- On Aug. 28, technicians found the first endangered fish at the top: a 22.5-inch, 3-pound [[Colorado Squawfish]] estimated to be at least 8 years old. It was tagged electronically and released upstream.
- In an experiment, two of six endangered razorback suckers swam to the top of the passageway.
- The ladder will operate through a complete spawning season the following year.
- Colorado State University's [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] confirmed that at least five of about 2,000 larval fish collected from the Gunnison River were Colorado squawfish. This was the first time squawfish larvae had been found upstream of the dam. Less than one-tenth of 1 percent of the water was sampled.

### Ouray National Fish Hatchery (Utah)
- The facility began as an experimental site in 1987 and was designated one of 72 national fish hatcheries in the U.S.
- It has 22 ponds totaling 4.4 acres and is the largest endangered fish hatchery in the upper basin. The annual budget is $250,000.
- Staff of four, led by [[Tom Pruitt]], manage 17 wild adult razorback suckers, more than 6,000 young razorbacks, nearly 1,400 young Colorado squawfish, and a few adult bonytails.
- Over four years, 50 or more adult endangered fish were removed from the wild and held temporarily in the ponds.
- Plans include a new hatchery building and 14 more ponds, bringing the total to 36 within two years.
- Research focuses on preventing deformities, maximizing growth, imprinting fish to spawn in the wild, and improving tagging.

### Wild Razorback Rearing
- Biologists captured 27 wild razorback suckers and raised them to the 4-inch fingerling stage, described as the "missing link" for survival. Wild razorbacks are rarely found at this adolescent stage.
- The larvae (about ¼ inch) were collected in light-traps on the Green River near Jensen, Utah. Of 92 sucker larvae identified as razorbacks, 27 survived.
- Only about 500 adult razorbacks remain in the Green River, but millions of larvae may be spawned there each year.
- The fish are expected to produce offspring in about five years, after which adults will return to the river. The fish were captured near the primary known spawning site, so they may imprint on the area.

### Other Program Actions and Accomplishments
- **Steamboat Lake releases**: A five-year agreement among Colorado State Parks, the Colorado Water Conservation Board, and the Service allows up to 3,300 acre-feet annually to benefit Yampa River fish. The Service pays $30,000 annually for up to 25 cubic feet per second (cfs) between July 1 and Aug. 15, and up to 200 cfs between Aug. 16 and Oct. 31. The Director's message separately cites a 2,000 acre-foot contract for late-summer Yampa flows. Yampa flows have dropped to 20 cfs in some falls.
- **Orchard Mesa Check settlement**: After five years of litigation and negotiation, about 30,000 acre-feet of Green Mountain Reservoir water will be made available to the 15-mile reach of the Colorado River in late summer and early fall.
- **Ruedi Reservoir contract**: 31,650 acre-feet of water will benefit endangered fish in the 15-mile reach.
- **Old Charlie Wash**: 42 wild young-of-the-year razorback suckers were collected from this Green River wetland near Ouray, suggesting some young razorbacks can survive despite non-native fish.
- **Lower Green River**: More than 170 razorback larvae were found near Canyonlands National Monument, confirming spawning and suggesting another population.
- **Bonytail stocking**: The Utah Division of Wildlife Resources plans to stock several thousand bonytails in the lower Colorado River near Moab this fall, starting efforts to re-establish the species in the upper basin.
- **Biological opinions**: The Service has issued favorable biological opinions on 176 projects in the upper basin. None has been stopped or litigated on endangered species grounds.
- **Funding and legislation**: FY 1997 funding appears secure. Legislation on cost-sharing for the upper Colorado and San Juan recovery programs was put on hold to resolve water-user concerns about section 7 consultations under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

### Selenium Contamination Study
- The Service, Bureau of Reclamation, and National Biological Service are studying how [[Selenium]] affects razorback sucker eggs and larvae. Selenium is essential in trace amounts but can kill all larvae at high concentrations.
- Three Grand Valley sites are under study: a pond at Walter Walker State Wildlife Area (the most contaminated), a side channel near Adobe Creek, and ponds at Horsethief Canyon State Wildlife Area.
- Contamination is a limiting factor in choosing flood plain restoration sites.

### Public Engagement
- Morgenweck argued that recovery benefits people by protecting water for crops, drinking, fishing, and rafting, and by cleaning pollutants and preserving wetlands.
- A world-class aquarium planned for downtown Denver will feature Colorado squawfish and other endangered Colorado River fish in its Rocky Mountain display.
- An electronic mailing list for participants was being tested, with full operation expected in October.
