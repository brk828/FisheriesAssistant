# The effects of Glen Canyon and Hoover Dams on Lotic Community Processes and in the Lower Colorado River of the Grand Canyon

## Executive Summary
This 1992 preliminary proposal, jointly submitted by the [[Hualapai Tribe]] and the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] to the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]], outlines a four-year research program to study the aquatic ecosystem of the lower Grand Canyon reach of the [[Colorado River]], from Diamond Creek (river mile 225.8) to Pierce Ferry (river mile 279.5). The study seeks to extend existing Glen Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) research upstream into this understudied 54-mile reach, which forms a critical transition zone between the riverine environment shaped by [[Glen Canyon Dam]] releases and the lentic (lake) environment of [[Lake Mead]] influenced by [[Hoover Dam]]. The project's central concern is characterizing how altered flow regimes and thermal/chemical gradients affect native, threatened, and endangered fish species relative to expanding populations of predatory introduced fishes.

## Background & Description
- **Study Area**: The Colorado River reach in Grand Canyon between Diamond Creek (RM 225.8) and Pierce Ferry (RM 279.5), spanning approximately 54 river miles.
- **Ecological Gradient**: The reach transitions from a fully riverine system near Diamond Creek to a lacustrine (lake) system near Pierce Ferry. The area from Diamond Creek to Bridge Canyon (RM 235) is considered the least dam-impacted portion of the Colorado River in Grand Canyon due to tributary inputs, attenuation of dam-driven flow pulses, and natural seasonal thermal equilibration. Below Bridge Canyon, [[Lake Mead]] backwater influences increasingly dominate the physio-chemical environment.
- **Historical Context**: AGFD has led aquatic research for GCES since 1983, with prior Phase I studies concentrated between [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and Diamond Creek. Independent studies of native and sport fishes in the lower Grand Canyon and Lake Mead predate GCES (McCall 1980; Brooks & Morgensen 1981; Morgensen 1983).
- **Rationale**: The lower reach remains virtually unstudied despite being critical to understanding the full aquatic ecosystem response to dam operations, particularly regarding the fate of [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and possibly relict [[Bonytail Chub]] populations, and the encroachment of non-native predators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Physio-chemical Drivers**: Cold, fluctuating hypolimnetic releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] shape upstream conditions; downstream, [[Lake Mead]] pool elevation and Hoover Dam operations impose a distinct set of lentic conditions (temperature stratification, reduced current, altered turbidity).
- **Species Composition Gradient**: Native fish assemblages (humpback chub, razorback sucker, bonytail chub, flannelmouth sucker, bluehead sucker) become increasingly rare moving downstream, while introduced piscivores and competitors — striped bass and red shiner — become increasingly dominant.
- **Predation Pressure**: [[Striped Bass]] is identified as an obligate piscivore likely exerting strong negative pressure on smaller/younger native fish; [[Channel Catfish]] is a facultative piscivore with potentially significant but less understood impacts.
- **Habitat Types Targeted**: Tributary confluence zones (notably [[Diamond Creek]] and [[Spencer Creek]]), shallow-slow shoreline habitats, backwaters, and main-channel mainstem sites — all sampled across variable flow regimes and Lake Mead pool elevations.
- **Trophic Considerations**: The project explicitly links lower trophic level dynamics (algal standing crop, benthic and planktonic communities, chlorophyll *a*, dissolved organic carbon, nutrients) to the food base supporting larval and juvenile native fishes, treating primary/secondary production as a leading indicator of ecosystem condition.

## Key Findings
*(Note: This document is a research proposal, not a completed study; "findings" below represent proposed hypotheses, target species, and identified knowledge gaps rather than empirical results.)*

- **Target Native Species** (>175 mm TL adults and early life stages):
  - [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) — federally endangered; distribution and habitat use in the lower reach are unknown; believed to become rare/extirpated in the lowest portions.
  - [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — proposed endangered at time of writing; appears sporadically throughout the canyon.
  - [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) — federally endangered; a small relict population possibly persists in the lowest reaches.
  - [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] — native catostomids targeted for distribution/habitat use assessment.

- **Target Introduced/Non-native Species**:
  - [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) — obligate piscivore, capture via trammel nets predicted to be most effective; stomach content analysis planned to determine food preferences.
  - [[Channel Catfish]] — facultative piscivore, baited hoop nets predicted most effective capture method.
  - [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) — introduced cyprinid noted as a potential competitor/predator threat to native fish populations.
  - [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) — referenced in prior Lake Mead spawning studies (Morgensen 1983).

- **Proposed Methodological Approach**:
  - Multi-gear fish sampling: electrofishing (using non-damaging pulse-shape pulsators for T&E species), trammel nets, hoop nets, minnow traps, seines, and larval drift nets.
  - Age determination via otoliths or pectoral spines for subsampled fish.
  - Binary coded micro-tags (0.5 mm) proposed as the preferred mark-recapture method for small (<150 mm) native fishes.
  - Habitat characterization via plane-table mapping, Total Station surveys, and remote water-chemistry data loggers (dissolved oxygen, specific conductance, turbidity, pH).
  - System productivity assessed via chlorophyll *a* (cryopreserved via liquid nitrogen for field stability), benthic metabolism chambers, and nutrient chemistry (DOC, phosphorus, nitrogen species, hardness, alkalinity).
  - Tributary confluence zones (Diamond Creek, Spencer Creek) identified as priority sites for larval/juvenile fish and drift studies, sampled at least three times annually.

- **Nine Core Work Items** structured the proposed research:
  1. Distribution/abundance/food habits of introduced predatory fishes.
  2. Distribution/habitat use of large adult native fishes (>175 mm TL).
  3. Extension of AGFD's long-term native fish monitoring program (established 1987) into the lower reach.
  4. Spatio-temporal distribution of larval/juvenile fishes in tributaries and mainstem.
  5. Environmental condition changes in tributary mouths/shoreline habitats under variable flow and Lake Mead pool elevation.
  6. System productivity and algal standing crop assessment.
  7. Behavioral responses of early life stage native fish to changing habitat conditions (e.g., dewatering, stranding risk).
  8. Quantification of rearing habitat availability under different flow/pool regimes.
  9. Longitudinal changes in benthic and planktonic community composition.

- **Logistics**: Proposed at 56 river days/year (eight 7-day trips), requiring a 33' J-rig boat, a support/sport boat, and crews of 4–6 biologists, coordinated with concurrent upper-river GCES trips to reduce shuttle costs.

- **Significance**: The proposal frames the Diamond Creek–Pierce Ferry reach as essential to a complete understanding of Grand Canyon aquatic ecosystem dynamics and to informing Glen Canyon Dam operational decisions under the Environmental Impact Statement and Endangered Species Act Section 7 consultation process.
