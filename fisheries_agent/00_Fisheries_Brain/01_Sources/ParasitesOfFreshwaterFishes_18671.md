# Parasites of Fresh-Water Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-parasitology

## Executive Summary

Economic Circular No. 42 (Department of Commerce, Bureau of Fisheries, revised February 1, 1929), written by H. S. Pratt of Haverford College, reviews the animal parasites of freshwater fishes. It classifies them as external parasites (lampreys, leeches, fish lice, flukes, and freshwater mussel larvae) and internal parasites (threadworms, spiny-headed worms, tapeworms, and flukes). The paper stresses that heavy infections can kill fish or make them commercially unsalable, that epidemics are most severe in small, enclosed, warm waters, and that infected but edible fish are harmless to humans, with condemnation resting on aesthetic prejudice rather than danger.

## Background & Description

The circular is a general consideration of parasitism in freshwater fish, addressed to fisheries managers, fish dealers, and the public. Parasites are grouped into two classes:

- **External parasites** cling to the skin, gills, mouth, or vent, feeding on blood or slime. Four groups are described:
  - **Lampreys**: eel-shaped, half a foot to over a foot long, with suckerlike mouths that rasp flesh; they often kill hosts or leave ulcerous sores. The illustrated species is the [[Lake Lamprey]] (*Petromyzon marinus unicolor*).
  - **Leeches**: worms an inch or more long with extreme contractility and a sucker at each end; large species are described as the fiercest bloodsuckers.
  - **[[Fish Lice]]**: crustaceans related to crabs and crayfish, usually under half an inch long, with a rigid, noncontractile body. Free-moving forms make new incisions when feeding; others remain fixed to gills or skin.
  - **[[Flukes]]**: small, semitransparent worms, about one twenty-fifth to one fourth of an inch long, that infect gills and can cause significant mortality.
- **Internal parasites** infest the digestive tract, liver, kidneys, body cavity, muscles, and other organs. Four classes are described: [[Threadworms]], [[Spiny-Headed Worms]], [[Tapeworms]], and flukes.

The circular also notes that freshwater mussel larvae attach to gills, scales, or fins for several weeks, apparently causing little harm, and may reduce infection by fish lice.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Transmission and seasonality:** Free-swimming larvae spread infection between fish. Infection spreads most readily in lakes and other small or enclosed waters, where fish cannot migrate, and in warm summer conditions that accelerate parasite multiplication. Some parasites show periodicity, peaking in spring and early summer.
- **Host specificity:** Parasitic worms are highly selective about host species and the organ they inhabit. Internal parasites are tied to specific environments within the host, shaped by local chemistry (for example, alkaline mouth or pharynx versus acidic stomach).
- **Life cycle:** Most internal parasites have an adult stage inside a host with open connection to the outside, a free-living larval stage (often lasting only hours, yet critical for dispersal and finding a new host), and sometimes a second intermediate host in which larvae encyst until eaten by a predator. This free stage is described as the most vulnerable point for control measures.
- **Organ preference:** The digestive tract is the most frequently infected site, followed by the gills and other respiratory organs. Muscles, liver, and fat often contain encysted larval grubs.
- **Sublethal effects:** Light infections appear harmless, but heavy infections may reduce reproductive capacity, a process compared to parasitic castration in crustaceans and other invertebrates.

## Key Findings

- **Epidemics:** At Lake Mille Lacs, Minnesota, for several successive summers, thousands of wall-eyed pike, yellow perch, rock bass, and black bass killed by parasites washed onto the beach. At the New York State Fish Hatchery, Cold Spring Harbor, Long Island, practically all rainbow trout a year or more old were infected by flukes, with gills shriveled and nonfunctional in many cases.
- **Economic impact:** Grubs in fish flesh, such as flukes in cysts about a quarter inch long beneath the skin of yellow perch, rock bass, and sunfish (most common in late summer and fall), make fish unsaleable. Fishermen usually discard such fish.
- **Human safety:** None of the parasites found in American freshwater fish live in the human body. The European broad tapeworm is the only exception and is considered practically negligible in America. Cooked or cleaned fish with parasites is safe to eat.
- **Regulatory position:** Citing [[Dr. C. W. Stiles]] of the Public Health Service, the circular states that larval threadworms in fish flesh are neither infectious nor toxic to humans. Any condemnation of wormy fish should be based on aesthetic grounds, not on fear of infection. The case involved butterfish condemned by a city inspector for threadworms.
- **Microbial diseases:** Bacteria, fungi, and protozoans also cause disease, with popular names including ulcer disease, spot disease, red plague, scale disease, and fungus disease. These also cause epidemics, especially in small enclosed waters.
- **Reporting protocol:** Those seeking identification should report size, shape, appearance, and location of the parasite in the fish, and send preserved specimens in alcohol. Diseased fish should be wrapped in alcohol-soaked cloth, then cotton waste and thick paper, for safe mailing.
