# Gambusia, The Fish Destroyer

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Poeciliidae #invasive-species #conservation

## Executive Summary

This article by Dr. George S. Myers examines the ecological dangers posed by [[Gambusia affinis]] (mosquitofish), a small livebearer from the southeastern United States that was widely introduced worldwide for mosquito control beginning around the turn of the 20th century. Despite its effectiveness against mosquito larvae, [[Gambusia]] has proven highly destructive to native fish populations, causing local extinctions of smaller species and reducing recruitment of larger game fish. The author argues that the uncritical acceptance of [[Gambusia]] as a mosquito control agent, without adequate ecological research, has resulted in significant and often irreversible damage to aquatic ecosystems globally.

## Background & Description

[[Gambusia affinis]] and its subspecies [[Gambusia affinis holbrooki]] are small livebearing fish native to the southeastern United States. Early in the tropical fish hobby, these fish were kept in aquariums but were soon abandoned due to their aggressive behavior toward other species. The species gained worldwide prominence following the discovery that mosquitoes transmit malaria and yellow fever. Public health officers began introducing mosquito-eating fish into local waters, with [[Gambusia]] becoming the most widely distributed species for this purpose.

The initial research on [[Gambusia]] as a mosquito control agent was inadequate in several respects. Researchers correctly noted that [[Gambusia]] does not penetrate thick aquatic vegetation and that the species cannot survive in temporary ponds that dry seasonally. However, they failed to recognize that other species, such as [[Heterandria formosa]] (a close relative in southeastern states) and [[Rivulus]] species in tropical America, could complement [[Gambusia]] by accessing dense vegetation. The research also overlooked the existence of annual killifish species that survive pond desiccation as buried eggs. Most critically, the destructiveness of [[Gambusia]] to other fish species was not recognized.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Gambusia]] exhibits extreme predatory behavior toward both fish of similar size and the young of much larger species. The species has been introduced to numerous locations worldwide, including [[Malaya]], [[Thailand]], the [[Philippines]], and the [[Nile River]] region. In its native range in the southeastern United States, [[Gambusia]] exists in ecological balance with natural predators and prey species that have evolved defensive behaviors. However, when introduced to new environments lacking these natural checks, [[Gambusia]] becomes a destructive invasive species.

The author provides detailed observational data from a garden pond in [[San Jose, California]], monitored over seven consecutive years. The pond measured approximately 10 by 15 feet, 2.5 feet deep at center, with heavy growth of [[Elodea]] and pond lilies. Initial stocking included 11 goldfish and approximately 250 [[Gambusia]]. The goldfish biomass exceeded [[Gambusia]] biomass in a ratio of approximately 2:1. Over five years with [[Gambusia]] present, goldfish recruitment averaged one surviving young fish every two years. After [[Gambusia]] removal, goldfish population increased to 30 individuals by year's end and approximately doubled the following year. By the seventh year, goldfish biomass was approximately three times greater than when [[Gambusia]] was present.

The California State Fish and Game Department's black-bass hatchery at [[Friant]] discontinued using [[Gambusia]] as forage fish after discovering it was destroying a large proportion of young bass, despite the bass being much larger than [[Gambusia]].

## Key Findings

**Global Impact on Native Species:**
- In southwestern United States streams, native [[Poeciliopsis]] has disappeared following [[Gambusia]] introduction
- In canals of [[Bangkok, Thailand]], native [[Aplocheilus panchax]] is now rare and the endemic [[Phenacostethus]] has disappeared; [[Gambusia]] is common
- In creeks around [[Laguna de Bay, Philippines]], [[Gulaphallus]] is extinct and [[Gambusia]] dominant
- In the lower [[Nile River]], native [[Micropanchax schoelleri]] cannot be found; [[Gambusia]] is common

**Predatory Behavior:**
- [[Gambusia]] destroys fish of its own size and young of much larger species
- In controlled pond observations, [[Gambusia]] presence reduced goldfish recruitment by approximately 95% (from normal reproduction to one surviving young per two years)
- [[Gambusia]] caused young bass mortality at California hatchery despite bass being much larger

**Ecological Mechanism:**
- In native range, [[Gambusia]] is controlled by evolved predator-prey relationships and defensive behaviors of native species
- In introduced ranges, absence of natural enemies and naive prey species allows [[Gambusia]] to become dominant
- Pattern mirrors other invasive species such as house sparrows in North America and rabbits in Australia

**Mosquito Control Alternatives:**
- [[Guppy]] (known as "millions fish" in [[Trinidad]]) was among first species used for mosquito control
- Other species including [[Heterandria formosa]] and [[Rivulus]] can access thick vegetation that [[Gambusia]] cannot penetrate
- Annual killifish species can survive seasonal pond desiccation as buried eggs
- Multiple less destructive species can control mosquito larvae as effectively as [[Gambusia]]

**Conservation Implications:**
- Hundreds of small fish species face potential extinction in areas where [[Gambusia]] has been introduced
- Many threatened species are potential aquarium fish or scientifically unique
- Many species may already be extinct
- Damage assessment has not been systematically conducted despite 60+ years of introductions

The author concludes that uncritical acceptance of [[Gambusia]] as a mosquito control panacea, based on inadequate ecological research, represents a case study in how human ignorance can destroy what nature took millions of years to produce. The continued introduction of [[Gambusia]] into new waters poses ongoing threats to global freshwater biodiversity.
