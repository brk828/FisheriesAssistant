# Special Regulation Trout Ponds in Michigan (1944–1947)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1948 report by [[Albert S. Hazzard]] and K. G. Fukano, of the [[Institute for Fisheries Research]] in the [[Michigan Department of Conservation]], describes the [[Special Regulation Trout Ponds]] program. Because large cold springs and cold streams are scarce near Michigan's population centers, the Department converted or built small ponds to provide trout angling close to cities, especially during wartime travel restrictions. The ponds were stocked with [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] and managed under strict angling rules. Creel census data from 1944 to 1947 showed good catch rates and strong angler approval, and the authors concluded that the ponds met a real need, at least in southern Michigan.

## Background & Description

**Origin.** The concept arose from a survey by the Institute for Fisheries Research in summer 1943 of a 12-acre rearing pond near the headwaters of Portage Creek in the [[Waterloo State Recreation Area]]. The pond had been built by the National Parks Service and was no longer needed for raising [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]]. Two small streams and several springs fed it. Surface water exceeded 80°F, but the bottom layer stayed in the sixties, which suited trout. An initial planting of 300 each of brook, brown and rainbow trout was made in fall 1943, and the pond opened to fishing in 1944 (see [[Portage Creek Pond]]).

**Special restrictions.** Modeled partly on Pennsylvania's experience with the "Fisherman's Paradise," the Conservation Commission imposed the following rules:
- Artificial flies only
- No boats or rafts
- Fishing only from one hour before sunrise to one hour after sunset
- Eight-inch minimum size limit
- Daily creel limit of two trout

**Pond network.** The program expanded steadily:
- **1944:** Portage Pond opened.
- **1945:** [[Wilder Creek Pond]] (Marshall), [[Bates Pond]] (near the [[Hastings Fish Hatchery]]) added.
- **1946:** [[Cook Pond]] (Hastings), a Hillsdale rearing pond, and the [[Sylvan Ponds]] (Waterloo Area, built solely for trout fishing).
- **1947:** Nine ponds in use, including a second [[Hillsdale Ponds]] unit and the [[Bald Mountain State Recreation Area]] pond. A pond at Sumnerville was under construction.
- **Northern trial (1947):** Provemont, Solon and Manistee ponds in the northern Southern Peninsula, plus the Harrisville millpond. These had a five-trout daily limit, and the Harrisville pond had no method or hour restrictions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal requirements.** Trout need cold water, preferably below 70°F. Most southern Michigan streams are too small or warm because they flow through cleared agricultural land. Pond bottoms stay cool because inflowing spring water is colder and denser than the pond surface. The bottom layer forms a cool retreat during hot summer weather, though inlet streams may approach 70°F on the hottest days. Adequate oxygen in the springs is essential.

**Food and behavior.** [[Midge Larvae]] and other food organisms live in or near the pond bottom. Trout therefore avoid the warm surface layer in midsummer, which explains the seasonal drop in catch. Trout respond quickly to cooling and feed at the surface again after the first cold nights or cool, rainy days late in the season.

**Pond construction.** Ponds can be built on springs in suitable depressions at low cost, since floodwaters are rarely a problem and high dams are unnecessary. Existing dams range from about 6 to 10 feet high. Ponds should be fully drainable so managers can check the population and remove unwanted fish. A flow of about 200 gallons per minute can support one acre of pond. The authors note that one acre of lake surface equals roughly a mile of stream about eight feet wide, so small streams can support worthwhile angling if dammed.

**Reproduction and stocking.** Natural reproduction occurs but is limited by scarce spawning streams. Regular stocking is therefore needed to maintain supply. Southern ponds were planted each fall with trout 6 to 8 inches long, in numbers meant to restore the population to about 75 legal trout per acre.

**Survival and carryover.** At the Hillsdale Station, where fish were checked in and ponds could be fully drained, the 1946 data for Pond 2 showed:
- Brown trout: 70 planted the previous year, 32 caught by anglers, 33 found on drainage (loss of 5).
- Rainbow trout: 70 stocked, 43 caught, 20 found (loss of 7).
- Brook trout: 68 caught, but 162 found in the pond, which indicated natural spawning in the stream above.

In 1947 anglers caught 91 brook, 72 brown (15 from the 1945 planting) and 83 rainbow trout (12 from the 1945 planting). Significant carryover occurred, and natural brook trout reproduction was sustaining that species.

## Key Findings

**Catch and effort (1944–1947):**
- **1944:** 1,247 fisherman-hours; catch of 74 brook, 164 brown and 152 rainbow trout; 0.31 legal trout per fisherman-hour (figures estimated about 80 per cent complete).
- **1945:** 3,217 hours in the compulsory-reporting ponds; 0.35 to 0.46 legal trout per hour. Browns and rainbows dominated Portage Pond, while brook trout gave the best returns at Bates and Wilder.
- **1946:** 4,963 hours of fishing.
- **1947:** 5,754 hours across nine ponds; 0.21 to 0.55 legal trout per hour.
- **Averages across the period:** 0.23 to 0.50 legal trout per hour. Catch rates compared favorably with northern streams (Little Manistee, Pigeon, Pine, North Branch of the Au Sable, Hunt Creek), which ranged from 0.21 to 0.77.

**Harvest and release:**
- Anglers removed about 40 per cent of plantings. The authors estimated the true total return at about 50 per cent, because creel records were incomplete outside Hillsdale.
- Anglers released 14 to 37 per cent of legal trout taken. Some releases were fish over the daily limit, and others were barely legal fish returned in hope of larger ones later.

**Fish size and quality:** Average catch size was close to 10 inches. Brook trout over one pound and brown trout up to four pounds were taken. Fish were in good condition and "full of fight." Spring feeding was the best time to fish. Later in the season, smaller flies (sizes 16 and 18) on fine leaders worked best.

**Management changes:** For the 1948 season the eight-inch size limit was dropped, so a seven-inch trout became legal, as elsewhere in Michigan. Creel census records were required only at the Hillsdale ponds, which remained permit-regulated for experiments on stocking rate, fish size at planting and fish marking.

**Use patterns:** Use was heaviest near cities, though nonresident anglers increasingly appeared after the war. Fishing day length ranged from just over an hour to more than two. Proximity to towns, ease of wading and fishing quality determined popularity.

**Angler sentiment:** Comments on creel slips were overwhelmingly positive. Anglers valued the chance to fly fish close to home during gas rationing and to practice fly casting. Some called the ponds a "step in the right direction."

**Conclusions:** The authors concluded that the ponds "are filling a real need, at least in southern Michigan." They saw them as a conservation project that encourages sport over meat fishing, promotes artificial flies and light tackle, and makes better use of hatchery fish than continuous "put and take" planting of legal-sized trout. Whether the model would work in better northern trout country remained an open question for further study.
