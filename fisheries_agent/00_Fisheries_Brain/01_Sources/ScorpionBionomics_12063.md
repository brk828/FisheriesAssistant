# Scorpion Bionomics: A Review of Life History, Reproduction, Feeding, Habitats, Cycles and Predators

#arachnology #invertebrate-biology #scorpiones

## Executive Summary

This 1987 review by S.C. Williams of [[San Francisco State University]] summarizes recent research on the biology of scorpions (order Scorpiones), covering systematics, life history, reproduction, feeding, habitat use, activity cycles and predation. The document describes scorpions as an ancient and conservative order, with roughly 1500 described living species in 112 genera across eight living families. Its central themes are low species diversity per habitat, intermittent feeding enabled by low metabolic demand, viviparous reproduction with maternal association, and strongly nocturnal, moonlight- and temperature-sensitive surface activity. The review concludes that research has concentrated on only three to four dozen species and that taxonomic knowledge remains far from complete.

## Background & Description

Scorpions are described as among the oldest known terrestrial metazoans, with fossils from the Silurian, and have changed little in external morphology over geologic time. The body is metameric, with a prosoma under an unsegmented carapace, a seven-segmented [[Mesosoma]], a five-segmented [[Metasoma]], and a telson modified into a stinger. The sclerotized exoskeleton limits water loss, and scorpions excrete guanine, uric acid or xanthine as primary nitrogen products, which aids water retention.

Systematics: eight living families (Bothriuridae, Buthidae, Chactidae, Chaerilidae, Diplocentridae, Iuridae, Scorpionidae, Vaejovidae) and 14 extinct families are recognized. Living diversity is about 1500 described species in 112 genera. The review notes that scorpion family-level diversity is lower than in insects, mites or spiders.

Reproductive mode: females are viviparous and maintain association with their young. Mating involves a courtship "promenade a deux" and indirect sperm transfer via a spermatophore, since males lack a penis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Community diversity.** Single-species communities occur in extreme environments such as high latitudes and elevations (e.g., [[Paruroctonus boreus]], [[Vaejovis gertschi]], [[Uroctonus mordax]]). Favorable sites hold more species: up to eight sympatric species in Sonoran Desert habitats in Arizona and an intermontane basin in Coahuila, Mexico; six in a California dune community; five in a Sonoran coastal dune; four in a seasonally wet Jalisco forest. Three to five species are typical in favorable regions.

**Habitat range.** Scorpions are most diverse in arid lower temperate latitudes. [[Baja California]] holds 61 species in 11 genera and 5 families. Some species occupy moist tropical canopy, bromeliads and caves, while others (e.g., [[Serradigitus deserticola]]) tolerate habitats that receive rain only every few years. Populations reach 2133 m in the Sierra Nevada and sea level along shores, and Paruroctonus boreus extends to about 52° N.

**Burrowing.** Burrows range from simple surface cells to deep, elaborate structures. Anuroctonus phaiodactylus builds permanent burrows up to 42 cm deep. Burrows buffer temperature and humidity, and Crawford & Riddle reported that Diplocentrus spitzeri can survive without free water if prey is available. Lamoral found soil hardness and texture to be the key factor partitioning all 19 Opisthophthalmus species.

**Activity cycles.** Only a small fraction of a population surfaces on a given night: about 5% of Paruroctonus utahensis, about 15% of Uroctonus mordax, and about half of a Paruroctonus mesaensis population on one evening. Surface activity runs roughly March through October in the western United States. Critical surfacing temperatures include 10 °C for P. boreus, 20 °C for adult and 9 °C for juvenile P. mesaensis, and 4 °C for U. mordax and V. gertschi.

**Predators.** At least 124 vertebrates and 26 invertebrates prey on scorpions, suggesting vertebrates are the more important group. Listed predators include elf owls, burrowing owls, barn owls, grasshopper mice, desert shrews, lizards, [[Black Widow Spiders]], and other scorpions.

## Key Findings

- **Life history:** Many species mature in the first year (Centruroides gracilis), while Paruroctonus mesaensis takes 19–24 months and Urodacus abruptus takes 3 years. Buthotus minax matured fastest (164–307 days) and Tityus trivittatus slowest (426–810 days) among 10 species. Maximum longevity estimates range from 4 yr (C. gracilis) to 10 yr (U. abruptus).
- **Reproduction:** Litter sizes range from 6 to 105 young. Gestation is one litter per year in many temperate taxa and 2–4 months intervals in some tropical taxa. Birth mortality in U. abruptus was about 27%.
- **Maternal care:** Larvae ride the mother's dorsum through the first instar. Tritium experiments on Euscorpius flavicaudis showed transfer from mother to larvae, suggesting a reexamination of the adaptive scope of mother–larval association. Three hypothesized benefits are antipredator defense, microclimate selection and trophic exchange.
- **Courtship:** In Paruroctonus mesaensis, courtship lasted about 22 min of initial contact plus a 9.3 min dance, covering about 8 m. Insemination took 3–5 seconds. Parthenogenesis is known but uncommon (e.g., Tityus serrulatus, Liocheles australasiae).
- **Prey detection:** Paruroctonus mesaensis locates prey by low-frequency compressional vibrations in sand, detected by basitarsal slit sensillae, at up to 50 cm.
- **Feeding and engorgement:** Scorpions ingest 8–97% of prey biomass (Anuroctonus phaiodactylus) and 5–87% (Uroctonus mordax). Weight gains of 9.6–16.5% (Vaejovis gertschi) and 9–36% in other species were recorded. Vaejovis gertschi returned to pre-feeding weight in about 13 days, implying feeding roughly every two weeks.
- **Diet:** Paruroctonus mesaensis ate 95 prey species, with tenebrionids (42%) and orthopterans (17%) dominant, and cannibalism contributed about 9% of diet in one California dune population.
- **Circadian biology:** Light sensitivity of the median eyes of Androctonus australis was about 1000 times higher during the subjective night. Extraocular light reception through the metasomal exoskeleton was documented in Urodacus.
- **Conclusions:** Of about 1500 species, only three to four dozen have been studied extensively, and recent ultraviolet sampling suggests the known fauna is incomplete. The review calls for systematic and biogeographic reinvestigation and for further use of scorpions in circadian and neurophysiological research.
