# Red Shiner (Cyprinella lutrensis) Literature Review and Stewardship Considerations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a literature review prepared by [[Steven P. Vives]] (Georgia Southern University) as a stewardship document for [[The Nature Conservancy]] on the [[Red Shiner]], *[[Cyprinella Lutrensis]]*. It covers taxonomy, morphology, native and introduced distribution, physiological tolerances, reproduction, diet, threats, and management and research needs. The species is a small, highly adaptable cyprinid native to the south-central United States that has spread widely through bait and aquarium releases. Its spread is linked to declines of several native [[Southwestern Fishes]] such as [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]. A 1993 review letter from [[Paul C. Marsh]] (Arizona State University) accompanies the draft, offering editorial comments and recommending that the [[Virgin River]] eradication experience be treated as a case study.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and names.** The subgenus *Cyprinella* was elevated to genus status by Mayden (1989). The American Fisheries Society uses "red shiner"; other names, mainly from the aquarium literature, include rainbow shiner, rainbow dace, redhorse minnow, redhorse shiner, and Asian fire barb.

**Morphology.** Adults reach 25 to 75 mm standard length. Typical meristic counts are:

- Dorsal rays: 7 (range 6–9)
- Pectoral rays: 14 (range 9–16)
- Pelvic rays: 9 (range 8–10)
- Anal rays: 9 (range 7–13)
- Lateral line: complete, with 32–35 scales (range 30–40)

The body is deep and laterally compressed, the mouth terminal and oblique, and the snout blunt. Pharyngeal arches carry four teeth in a single row. Scales are diamond-shaped and edged with melanophores, producing a cross-hatched pattern. Breeding males develop red-orange coloration on the head and fins, along with tubercles on the head, chin, scales, and fin rays. Tubercles are sometimes present in females to a lesser degree. Meristic and morphological characters vary geographically.

**Similar species.** The [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Cyprinella formosa*) has eight anal rays and an incomplete lateral line with 37–39 scales. The [[Gibbous Shiner]] (*Cyprinella garmani*) usually has ten anal rays. Diagnostic characters for separating *Cyprinella* species are given by Mayden (1989).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** The type locality is Otter Creek, a tributary of the North Fork Red River, Tillman County, Oklahoma. The native range covers the western Mississippi River basin and Gulf of Mexico drainages. Introductions are documented in:

- **Colorado:** Native to plains reaches of the Arkansas, Republican, Rio Grande, and South Platte; introduced to the Colorado, Dolores, Green, and White river basins.
- **New Mexico:** Native to the Rio Grande, Pecos, and Canadian drainages; introduced to the San Juan, San Francisco, and Gila drainages.
- **Arizona:** Introduced to the Gila and Colorado river basins.
- **California:** Introduced in the early 1930s as one of the state's three legal bait fishes; established in the [[Colorado River]] and Imperial Valley irrigation ditches.
- **Nevada and Utah:** Introduced to the Colorado River basin.
- **Northern range expansion:** Extending north into Wisconsin, possibly at the expense of *Cyprinella spiloptera* populations.

**Physiological tolerances.** The species tolerates a wide range of conditions:

- pH from 5 to 10
- Salinities up to 10 g/L
- Dissolved oxygen down to 1.5 mg/L
- Thermal tolerance: Matthews and Maness (1979) reported a mean critical thermal maximum of 38.99 °C (range 38.7–39.2 °C, n = 10). Brues (1928) collected red shiners at 39.5 °C in New Mexico.

**Habitat associations.** Red shiners are most common where few other species occur, especially in variable streams. Minckley and Meffe (1987) found them reduced or depleted by floods but quickly re-established from re-invaders or survivors. Cross and Moss (1987) found them abundant in residual pools of intermittent streams. Pflieger and Grace (1987) hypothesized that they may become more abundant in clear-water conditions because they are sight-feeding carnivores.

## Key Findings

### Reproduction and Life History

- **Age and size:** Fish are usually in their second or third summer at maturity (Pflieger 1975). Laser and Carlander (1971) found all males longer than 40 mm TL were in breeding condition.
- **Spawning season:** Varies by region:
  - Kansas: peak in June and July, at 15.6–29.4 °C (Cross 1967)
  - Missouri: late May to early September (Pflieger 1975)
  - Oklahoma: April to September (Farringer et al. 1979)
  - Wisconsin: June to August (Becker 1983)
- **Substrates:** Spawning occurs over gravel riffles, gravel in pools, aquatic plant beds, sunfish nests, and crevices formed by rubble, branches, and logs. Vives (1993) showed that red shiners use a variety of substrates in aquaria, and crevices are not exclusively preferred. The author suggests this substrate plasticity may explain part of the success of *Cyprinella* species and the rapid spread of red shiners in the western United States.
- **Fecundity (Laser and Carlander 1971):** Of 50 females examined, 485 to 684 eggs per female. No correlation was found between egg number and female length or weight.
- **Gale (1986) outdoor pools:** Nine pairs produced 5 to 19 clutches each, totaling 4,701 to 8,248 eggs (mean 6,177). Spawning occurred from 13 June to 28 August. Total length correlated poorly with total egg number but significantly with mean clutch size (r = 0.95). Interclutch intervals ranged from 2 to 12 days (mean 5, mode 3). Seventeen of 20 clutches in one nine-day period were spawned between 0700 and 1200 h.
- **Egg development:** Eggs hatched in four days at 26–29 °C (Gale 1986).
- **Growth:** Laser and Carlander (1971) gave average lengths of 29 mm TL at age 0, 50 mm at age I, and 72 mm at age III. Missouri fish averaged 23 mm TL at the end of the first summer and 40 mm at the end of the second (Pflieger 1975). Individuals in Illinois live beyond two years (Lewis and Gunning 1959).

### Diet and Behavior

- Red shiners are omnivorous. In Oklahoma, algae made up a third of total food volume (Hale 1963).
- Missouri pond studies (Divine 1968) showed diet shifts with size: small fish ate collembola, plant material, copepods, rotifers, and protozoans, while fish over 51 mm TL ate insects almost exclusively in June through August.
- Laser and Carlander (1971) found plant material made up 67.8% and insects 32.2% of July diet volume.
- Schooling is common, and individual metabolism is reduced while swimming in a group (Delco and Beyers 1963).

### Quality Occurrence Criteria

A quality occurrence is one within the native range. Because of the species' rapid increase and colonization ability, a quality occurrence in introduced areas is defined as several red shiners collected in one localized area. If red shiners are collected at the same site in two or more consecutive years, the population should be considered established there or to have ready access to the site.

### Threats and Conflicts

- **Status:** Wisconsin lists the species as a "watch" species, though its status designation is questioned because its recent range extension is probably linked to declines of *Cyprinella* species.
- **Introduction pathways:** Bait-bucket release (Hubbs 1954) and release by pet fish enthusiasts (Richter 1989). Early records from the Colorado River in 1953 were attributed to bait use or releases from rearing facilities. Some Colorado River fish may have originated from Lake Buchanan, Texas.
- **Native fish declines:** The inverse relationship between red shiners and native fishes such as loach minnow, spikedace, and woundfin is strong circumstantial evidence that red shiners are a causal factor (Minckley and Deacon 1968; Minckley 1973, 1995; Propst et al. 1986). The author notes that in some situations they may be a contributing rather than primary cause, as in the San Francisco River, where water diversion and low flows were also implicated in spikedace declines (Anderson 1978; Bestgen and Propst 1987).
- **Displacement evidence:** Marsh et al. (1999) found direct evidence that red shiners displace spikedace in natural situations and laboratory experiments. Spikedace moved to slower currents when red shiners were present in an artificial stream. In field collections, however, spikedace occurred at higher current velocities where red shiners were present. Marsh et al. attributed this contradiction to differences in available velocities between laboratory and field conditions.
- **Aggression:** Red shiners are aggressive minnows, and males become territorial during spawning.
- **Hybridization:** Red shiners readily hybridize with *C. callitaenia* (Wallace and Ramsey 1982), *C. formosa* (Vives, unpublished), *C. spiloptera* (Page and Smith 1970), and *C. venusta* (Hubbs and Strawn 1956; Buchanan 1976; Robison and Buchanan 1988). Introgression could become critical for range-restricted species such as *C. formosa*.

### Aravaipa Creek Invasion

Red shiners were discovered in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, in October 1990, probably invading from the [[San Pedro River]] (Minckley 1990; Marsh 1990). They were not present at the time of the review and were believed to have been removed by flooding (Stolzenburg 1992). The author stresses the importance of a natural flow regime for southwestern streams (Propst et al. 1986; Meffe and Minckley 1987). Marsh's letter notes that the [[Virgin River]] (Arizona, Nevada, and Utah) may foreshadow Aravaipa Creek's future. Measures there have included repeated eradication attempts, barrier construction, and emergency funds, making it a valuable case study that should receive fuller treatment.

### Conservation and Management Recommendations

- **Land protection:** Lands do not need protection for red shiners within their native range, as they are common in human-altered streams.
- **Recovery potential:** Not applicable.
- **Biological monitoring:** Populations should be monitored at the periphery of native and introduced ranges. When red shiners appear in a new location, a monitoring program should include stations in the affected stream and adjacent tributaries, spaced according to habitat variation and flow permanency. Seines with small mesh and electroshocking are effective for capture. Monitoring in Aravaipa Creek is conducted by [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Arizona State University]], The Nature Conservancy, the Bureau of Land Management, and the Fish and Wildlife Service.
- **Management procedures:** Regulations should discourage transport and stocking of non-native fishes. Angler education is recommended. Natural barriers to invasion should be maintained or enhanced, provided barrier designs do not significantly alter stream flow and are approved by the appropriate agencies and the Desert Fishes Recovery Team.
- **Research needs:** Six priorities are identified: (1) colonization rates after floods or new introductions; (2) competition for food with native species, using diets of all life stages; (3) reproductive potential in introduced locations compared with natives; (4) displacement of native species in field studies; (5) the feasibility of eradication in the Virgin River; and (6) comparison of sites where red shiners and native species have coexisted for years with sites where they do not coexist.
- **Expert contacts:** [[Walter J. Matthews]] (University of Oklahoma Biological Station) is identified as the best single authority on red shiner ecology. Mayden is cited as an authority on *Cyprinella* taxonomy.

### Review Letter Comments (Paul C. Marsh, 29 January 1993)

Marsh describes the review as well written and comprehensive, with few comments. His suggestions include:

- Double-spacing and page numbering to ease editing and cross-referencing.
- Consistent use of "red shiner" as singular or plural.
- Acknowledgment of all monitoring entities, which Marsh suggests may reflect redundancy, funding competition, or a combination of motives, but in Minckley's case represents long-term conservation commitment.
- Inclusion of the Virgin River case study.
- Consideration of several published works listed on the back of page 7 of the draft.
