# Native and Non-Native Fish Interactions in the Lower Colorado River: Habitat Overlap and Management Recommendations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #native-fish-conservation

## Executive Summary

This final report (30 April 1998), submitted by W.L. Minckley and colleagues at the Arizona State University Department of Biology to the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation, reviews the literature on [[Lower Colorado River]] fishes to determine whether habitat management alone can allow native and non-native fishes to coexist. It was prepared as a preliminary step to a research program under Reasonable and Prudent Alternative #4 of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion (1997). The authors conclude that native and non-native fishes overlap broadly in habitat and resource use, that no physical habitat attribute favours one group, and that the presence of established non-native fishes precludes successful life-cycle completion by native species. They recommend segregated management, with native fishes held in habitats free of non-natives.

## Background & Description

The mainstream lower Colorado River historically supported only ten freshwater fish species (Table 3A), dominated by cyprinids and catostomids. Five large-bodied endemics, [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], are now extirpated, depleted, or federally listed as endangered (flannelmouth sucker excepted). [[Woundfin]] and [[Desert Pupfish]] are also extirpated from the lower river. The introduced fauna now includes 29 established species (Table 3B), among them [[Striped Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Red Shiner]] and several tilapias.

The river is heavily regulated by a chain of dams: [[Hoover Dam]] (Lake Mead), [[Davis Dam]] (Lake Mohave), [[Parker Dam]] (Lake Havasu), and several diversion structures. The report notes that no natural segments remain along the mainstream.

Literature was gathered from three bibliographic databases (Biological Abstracts, Wildlife and Fisheries Review, Zoological Record), the USFWS web site and dissertation indices. The search returned 95,447 keyword hits; more than 8,500 were screened and 415 citations form the final reference list (1957–1997). Data were arranged into 2 x 2 matrices for larval, juvenile and adult stages, and a meta-analysis approach was considered for future work.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Lake Mead is mildly mesotrophic; Lake Mohave is clear year-round with a productive upper reach; Lake Havasu is shallow, warm and often isothermal in summer. Substrates range from bedrock and gravel in armored reaches to sand in channelized segments. Hiebert and Grabowski (1985, 1987) reported that about 40% of banks in the upper Parker I reach were riprap versus about 3% in Parker II (Table 2).

**Hydrology and chemistry.** Water chemistry is generally not limiting; dissolved oxygen usually exceeds 50% saturation, and nitrate and phosphate concentrations range from about 0.05 to 0.20 mg/L. Currents range from 0.5–1 m/s in channelized reaches to over 1 m/s below dams at high discharge.

**Biota.** Aquatic macrophytes are largely absent except in deltas and reservoirs (cattail, tules, pondweeds, tamarisk). Benthic invertebrates are richest on stable substrates below dams, and oligochaetes and chironomids can reach 10,000 to over 100,000 per m² in soft reservoir sediments. The exotic Asiatic clam (*Corbicula fluminea*) and crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii*) occur locally.

**Temperature.** Adult thermal optima were about 24°C for bonytail and humpback chub, 23–25°C for razorback sucker, and 25°C for Colorado squawfish. Non-native adults mostly prefer 10–30°C. Spawning temperatures overlap broadly, with bonytail near 18°C and razorback sucker from 10–24°C.

## Key Findings

- **Habitat overlap.** Only 7 of 133 habitat descriptors were exclusive to native fishes, and those were not truly exclusive. About one-third of habitat types were exclusive to non-natives, so nearly two-thirds were shared.
- **Feeding overlap.** Diets overlap extensively, and no resource limitation was evident. Non-natives include a much larger share of piscivores; only [[Colorado Squawfish]] was a piscivorous native adult. Fourteen non-native species were documented preying on native fishes (Table 17).
- **Cannibalism.** Colorado squawfish and desert pupfish consume their own young, and non-native cannibals include common carp, largemouth bass, green sunfish and striped bass.
- **Territoriality.** Desert pupfish was the only native territorial species; 19 of 22 non-native species showed some form of territoriality.
- **Spawning.** Timing and temperature overlap broadly. Razorback sucker is the only native that spawns in winter, when no other warm-water species is reproducing.
- **Reproductive mode.** All native freshwater fishes except desert pupfish are broadcast spawners with no parental care. Most non-native fishes build nests or guard young, which gives them an early-life survival advantage.
- **Case studies.** Stocked native fishes grew and reproduced in predator-free ponds (ASU Golf Course, ASU Research Park, Buenos Aires NWR, Dexter NFH, Emerald Canyon, Page Golf Course). Where non-natives were present, survival was dramatically reduced, as at the Davis Cove and Yuma Cove backwaters on [[Lake Mohave]]. Some ponds produced multiple generations of razorback sucker (Rock Tank, Buenos Aires NWR).
- **Conclusion.** The authors infer that non-native predation on early life stages is the single most important factor limiting native recruitment, though they acknowledge that the mechanism is not fully elucidated.
- **Recommendation.** Manage native fishes in segregated, off-channel habitats free of non-natives, using genetically defined stocks. The report urges immediate action, citing continued decline of the native fauna. Management of non-native fishes was beyond the report's scope.
