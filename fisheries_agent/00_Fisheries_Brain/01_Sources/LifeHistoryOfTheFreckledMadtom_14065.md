# Life History of the Freckled Madtom (*Noturus nocturnus*) in Mill Creek, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Occasional Paper from the [[University Of Kansas Museum Of Natural History]] describes the life history of the [[Freckled Madtom]] ([[Noturus Nocturnus]]) in [[Mill Creek]], a small tributary of the [[Cache River]] in [[Pulaski County]], [[Illinois]]. Authors [[Brooks M. Burr]] and [[Richard L. Mayden]] studied growth, reproduction, development, diet and demography between 27 August 1978 and 25 June 1981, using 145 preserved specimens aged 1 to 54 months. Little was previously known about the species despite its wide distribution in the lower and central [[Mississippi River Basin]] and other [[Gulf of Mexico]] tributaries. The study reports the first detailed observations of nest-guarding behavior in beer cans and bottles, and documents a short egg incubation period compared with related madtoms.

## Background & Description

*Noturus nocturnus* is a small catfish in the family [[Ictaluridae]] (madtoms). Its range covers Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana and Texas, and extends into the lower and central Mississippi River basin. Prior knowledge was limited: [[Taylor 1969]] revised the genus [[Noturus]] and described external morphology, habitat and associated species; [[Pflieger 1975]] recorded females with fully developed eggs in late May in southeastern Missouri; and [[Cross And Collins 1975]] reported an insectivorous and small-crustacean diet in Kansas.

The authors frame the work as part of a broader call for life-history data on warmwater fishes (Larimore 1981). It is closely linked to parallel studies of the [[Brindled Madtom]] ([[Noturus Miurus]]), the most common madtom at the site, and the [[Slender Madtom]] ([[Noturus Exilis]]).

Breeding males and females of *N. nocturnus* developed tiny white abrasive structures over the head and body. Histology showed these to be taste buds, which the authors suggest may function like breeding tubercles and stimulate courtship or spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Mill Creek is a 2 to 4 m wide stream, 0.5 to 1.5 m deep, with rocky or brushy riffles and mud, silt and rock pools. Banks were tree-lined until recent channelization. The site is surrounded by cultivated fields and is a common trash dumping ground. Accumulated cans and bottles likely add suitable nesting cover. Water temperature ranged from 1 °C in January to 30 °C in July.

**Fish community.** Twenty-three fish species occur at the site. *N. miurus* is the most common madtom, *N. nocturnus* is less abundant but regularly present, and the tadpole madtom (*N. gyrinus*) is rarely captured. Associated species include stonerollers, shiners, sunfishes, darters and suckers. A single mudpuppy (*Necturus maculosus*) was collected with *N. nocturnus*.

**Habitat use.** In fall and winter, young fish occurred mainly in mixed gravel and sand riffles with sticks, leaves and debris, often at the heads or bases of riffles with strong current. Adults were mostly in riffles through the year, though some were in mud-bottomed pools. During the breeding season (mid-May to July), adults favored shallow pools but also shallow riffles where velocity was reduced. Across its range, the species is typically found in moderate to fast current over gravel or debris in medium to large streams, and clear water is probably preferred.

**Nesting habitat.** Nests were in shaded areas of the creek with some current, about 0.5 m wide and 10 to 15 cm deep, at 25 °C. Nest cans usually contained tightly packed mud or silt.

## Key Findings

### Reproduction

- **Males:** Testis weight followed a curvilinear relation with standard length (1000T = 22.300 − 1.328L + 0.019L², r = 0.802). Testis weight was negligible until males exceeded 60 mm SL or 15 months of age. Gonadosomatic index (GSI) rose from a mean of 2.6 (August to May, adults) to a maximum of 9.1 (mean 6.2) in June and July. Males are sexually mature at about 2 years, in their third summer.
- **Females:** Females began preparing for spawning in May and were ripe with mature oocytes through July. Ovaries reached 9.0% of adjusted body weight in a 1-year-old, 69 mm female, compared with 24.8% in breeding *N. miurus* from the same creek. Five mature females (67 to 75 mm SL) carried 85 to 116 mature oocytes (mean 102.2). Some females reached maturity by age 1.
- **Breeding morphology:** Breeding males developed swollen lips, genital papillae and swollen head muscles from May to July. Breeding females had more conical, partially concealed papillae.

### Nesting Behavior

- The first nest was a male in a narrow-mouthed glass bottle on 9 May 1979. A pair was then found in a 355 ml beer can on 17 May 1979.
- On 25 June 1981, three nests were found, each in a 355 ml beer can with a single guarding male and a clutch of embryos. Clutch sizes were 47, 139 and 154 eggs. Nesting males measured 73.0, 75.3 and 75.4 mm SL, and all nesting males were 2 or 3 years old with empty stomachs.
- Males guard nests head-first at the can opening, which blocks most potential predators. Breeding males averaged 21.7 mm head width and 13.3 mm head depth, while typical can openings measured 23.7 mm long and 16.2 mm wide, leaving little room to spare.
- The spawning and nesting seasons of *N. nocturnus* and *N. miurus* overlap from mid-June to late July. Because both species use cans and bottles, the authors suggest competition for nest sites may occur.

### Development

- Eggs were spherical, lemon-yellow and adhered to each other in a mass. Diameters ranged from 3.6 to 4.5 mm (mean 3.9, N = 5).
- At 25 °C, eggs hatched in 139 to 161 hours (5.8 to 6.7 days), faster than *N. exilis* (187 to 210 hours) and *N. miurus* (189 to 215 hours). Hatching success was 100% in 60 incubated eggs.
- Hatchlings measured 7.5 to 8.0 mm TL. Larvae reached adult-like form by day 15 (14.8 to 15.5 mm TL), with the yolk sac fully absorbed.
- The smallest seine-collected specimen from Mill Creek was an 11.8 mm SL fish taken in July.

### Growth

- Length growth rate declined with age. Half of the first year's length growth occurred in about 8 weeks.
- Adjusted body weight increased at a constant rate. Half of the first year's body weight was reached in about 3 months (the Summary states about 12 weeks).
- Males and females grew at nearly the same rate, but males lived slightly longer (54 versus 52 months). Linear body-weight equations did not differ significantly between sexes.
- The largest Mill Creek specimen was a 101.5 mm female that weighed 19.6 g. The largest recorded specimen was 122.6 mm SL, from Nolin Reservoir, Kentucky.

### Demography and Survival

- Of the 145 specimens, 61.4% were up to 1 year old, 22.8% were 1 to 2 years old, 10.3% were 2 to 3 years old and 5.5% were 3 years or older.
- Sample composition was 79 males and 66 females. The sex ratio did not deviate significantly from 1:1 in the 0 or 1+ year classes. Sampling was biased toward males in older classes.
- Survival was 54.4% of males and 69.7% of females up to 1 year old, with the oldest male and female aged 54 and 52 months.
- The authors note that *N. miurus* lived up to 36 months (males) and 25 months (females) in the same creek, and that several congeners live at least 4 years.

### Diet

- Among 41 stomachs examined across all seasons, aquatic insect larvae were the main food. Ephemeropterans, trichopterans and chironomid larvae formed the bulk of the diet in every season.
- Simuliids were eaten in fall, winter and spring. Crustaceans, nematodes, oligochaetes and arachnids were minor components.
- Clark (1978, unpublished thesis) reported that three *N. nocturnus* from Black Creek, Mississippi, contained remains of three speckled madtoms (*Noturus leptacanthus*).

### Parasitism

- Hoffman (1967) listed no parasites for the species. In this study, the endoparasitic fluke *Crepidostomum* was found in four specimens. The ectoparasitic copepod *Lernaea* was found on two specimens, one from June and one from July.

### Conclusions

Individuals grow in length at a decreasing rate and in body weight at a constant rate for at least 4 years. Males mature at 2 years, and females can mature by age 1. Nesting in beer cans is a conspicuous reproductive behavior. The authors conclude that the species spawns from late June to July in this region, not in spring, and that its short incubation time and male nest guarding contribute to its life-history strategy.
