# Status Report: Lilaeopsis schaffneriana subspecies recurva

#botany #aquatic-plants #wetlands #conservation #Umbelliferae #cienega

## Executive Summary

This status report documents [[Lilaeopsis schaffneriana]] subspecies recurva, a rare semi-aquatic perennial plant endemic to cienega wetlands in southeastern Arizona and adjacent Sonora, Mexico. The taxon is a USFWS Candidate Category 2 species and USFS Sensitive Plant. Surveys conducted 1988-1990 by [[The Arizona Nature Conservancy]] documented the species at fewer than twenty locations between 3,700-6,500 feet elevation. The plant has experienced documented population losses at historic sites including the [[Santa Cruz River]] near Tucson (type locality), [[San Pedro River]], Zinn Pond, Monkey Spring, and House Pond. Current threats include water diversions, groundwater pumping, pond dredging, and flood-related habitat destruction. The species serves as an indicator for vulnerable cienega communities that support other sensitive taxa including [[Spiranthes delitescens]], [[Pyrgulopsis thompsonii]], [[Gila intermedia]], [[Ambystoma tigrinum stebbinsii]], [[Rana chiricahuensis]], and [[Thamnophis eques]].

## Background & Description

[[Lilaeopsis schaffneriana]] subspecies recurva is a rhizomatous herbaceous perennial in the [[Umbelliferae]] (Parsley family, subfamily [[Apiaceae]]). First described by A.W. Hill in 1927 as *Lilaeopsis recurva* based on material collected by Pringle in 1881 along the [[Santa Cruz River]] near Tucson, the taxon was reduced to subspecific status by Affolter (1985) following revision of the genus. Affolter found extensive overlap in characters distinguishing *L. schaffneriana* from *L. recurva* and concluded fruit shape provided the primary basis for distinction. He recognized subspecies recurva only from southern Arizona, while Mexican populations east of the continental divide were included in subspecies schaffneriana.

The plant produces slender erect cylindrical hollow leaves 1-3 mm diameter and 3-20 cm tall (depending on water depth) from nodes of creeping white rhizomes 0.6-4.5 mm diameter located 1-2 cm underground. Rhizomes branch freely and may form dense mats in sand or mud streambeds. Leaves are pale green, semi-succulent, somewhat flexuous, with septa at irregular intervals, typically borne 2-3 per node. Tiny 3-10 flowered umbels arise from root nodes on peduncles 1-7 cm tall, always shorter than leaves. Flowers are 1-2 mm wide with maroon-tinted petals. Fruits are globose to ellipsoid, 1.6-2.3 mm long, 1.2-1.9 mm broad, with spongy cells in all 5 ribs of the mericarp.

The genus [[Lilaeopsis]] contains 13 species of perennial rhizomatous herbs inhabiting temperate and alpine regions of North and South America and Australasia. [[Lilaeopsis schaffneriana]] is one of 4 strictly freshwater species, occurring in southeastern Arizona, central and northern Mexico, and northwestern South America. The subspecies are separated by the continental divide, with subspecies recurva on the western side and subspecies schaffneriana on the eastern side in central Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Lilaeopsis schaffneriana]] subspecies recurva is endemic to cienega habitats—marshy or meadow-like wetlands surrounded by relatively arid expanses. Following Hendrickson and Minckley (1984), cienegas are delineated by elevation into three types, with this taxon occurring mainly in mid-elevation (3,000-6,000 feet) sites but also found in low (<3,000 feet) and high (>6,000 feet) elevation zones.

**Low Elevation Sites** (subtropical marshes) occur along major rivers in backwaters and floodplain seeps buffered from high-energy main channel flows. These sites have large drainage areas with high flood potential and have experienced the most human disturbance. Groundwater pumping and diversion have eliminated perennial flows requisite for wetland formation. Grazing has damaged stabilizing riparian vegetation, increasing vulnerability to erosive flooding. Current low-elevation sites include [[Black Draw]] on [[San Bernardino Creek]] (3,700 feet) and [[Rio San Bernardino]] near Mexico Highway 2.

**Mid-Elevation Sites** (true cienegas, 3,000-6,000 feet) have smaller drainage areas with lower flooding potential and gentler gradients than high-elevation sites. These support unique wetland communities with permanent water. Sites include [[Bear Canyon]] tributaries (5,520-5,750 feet), [[Sonoita Creek]] at Cottonwood Spring (4,550 feet), [[San Rafael Valley]] springs (4,700-4,740 feet), [[Turkey Creek]] in [[Canelo Hills]] (4,500 feet), and three Sonora locations: [[Ojo de Agua]] southeast of Cananea, [[Rio San Rafael]] north of Cananea, and [[Arroyo Los Fresnos]] at Los Fresnos Cienega.

**High Elevation Sites** (>6,000 feet) are marshy to bog-like alpine and cold temperate meadowlands, typically stream headwaters or depressions in mountain canyons with steep gradients. Sites include [[Garden Canyon]] (6,150 feet) and [[Scotia Canyon]] (5,700-6,000 feet) in the [[Huachuca Mountains]].

Physical requirements include perennial water, gentle stream gradients, small-to-medium drainage areas, and mild winters. Sites occur in the Sonoran Desert Section of Basin and Range physiographic province. Climate data from representative stations show mean annual precipitation 12.16-17.06 inches, mean annual temperature 57.2-61.6°F.

The species requires intermediate flooding frequency to manage competition but is locally extirpated when floods are too frequent and intense—a pattern matching the model developed by Bowles and Apfelbaum (1989) for [[Plantago cordata]] in Illinois streams. Increased runoff from loss of native vegetation brings flooding and scouring intolerable to local populations.

Cienega plant communities vary by elevation. Low-elevation sites support large reeds, cattails, bulrush, 3-square, willows, cottonwoods, and halophytes in saline areas. Mid-elevation sites have low shallow-rooted semi-aquatic sedges, rushes, grasses (rarely cattails), locally common dicots (watercress, water-pennywort), locally common macrophytes, few trees (willows most common), and halophytes in adjacent saline areas. High-elevation sites contain low semi-aquatic and terrestrial grasses, cold-resistant rushes and sedges, and low woody shrubs (alder, willows).

## Key Findings

**Distribution and Population Status:**

Prior to 1988 surveys, the taxon was known from only 10 herbarium records representing 7 southern Arizona sites. Surveys 1988-1990 failed to relocate populations at 4 historic sites but discovered 8 new locations (4 in Arizona, 4 in Sonora, Mexico). The known range extends from [[Rio San Bernardino]] on the east to [[Sonoita Creek]] on the west, and from Tucson on the north (historically) to Cananea on the south.

**Extirpated Historic Populations:**
1. [[Santa Cruz River]] near Tucson (type locality)—collected by Pringle 19 May 1881, river now dry
2. Zinn Pond, St. David—collected by Goodding 1951-1953, pond dredged and deepened
3. [[San Pedro River]], St. David—collected by Goodding 16 May 1958, habitat no longer suitable
4. Monkey Spring, [[Sonoita Creek]]—collected by Minckley 6 Mar 1965, not relocated despite apparently suitable habitat
5. House Pond at Slaughter Ranch adjacent to [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]—discovered 1981, destroyed by recent pond dredging

**Current Arizona Populations (11 sites):**
- [[Bear Canyon]], [[Huachuca Mountains]]: Two tributaries (Wakefield Camp tributary at 5,750 feet, [[Lone Mountain Canyon]] at 5,520 feet) plus two small patches higher in Bear Creek
- [[Garden Canyon]], [[Huachuca Mountains]]: Half-mile stretch at 6,150 feet, low density mixed with grasses and sedges
- [[Scotia Canyon]], [[Huachuca Mountains]]: Two sections (5,700-6,000 feet) separated by dry reach; upper section dense, lower section medium-to-low density
- [[Sonoita Creek]]: Two separate stretches; upper part low density for half-mile; lower part from Cottonwood Spring downstream, experienced 95% loss from August 1988 flood but recolonized by August 1989
- [[San Rafael Valley]]: Three springs (Sheehy Spring, Sharp Spring, Heron Spring) at 4,700-4,740 feet, low densities
- [[Turkey Creek]], [[Canelo Hills]]: Approximately half-mile surveyed at 4,500 feet, extremely low densities
- [[Black Draw]], [[San Bernardino Creek]]: Four small patches totaling ~20 m² at 3,700 feet

**Current Sonora Populations (4 sites):**
- [[Rio San Bernardino]] near Mexico Highway 2: Two patches <1 m² each found May 1988, both destroyed by flood August 1988
- [[Ojo de Agua]] southeast of Cananea: Spring system, uncommon among sedges and rushes along pool banks
- [[Rio San Rafael]] north of Cananea: Scattered patches at medium density
- [[Arroyo Los Fresnos]]: Two sites—Los Fresnos cienega and Rancho Casa Blanca downstream; cienega has extensive sub-irrigated meadow, downstream site has scattered spots along eroded stream

**Quantitative Population Data (1989 baseline):**

Permanent transects established at 4 sites with detailed monitoring:

*Coverage of Available Aquatic Habitat:*
- Bear Canyon tributary (Wakefield Camp): 11.5% (81.3 m² of 707.6 m² potential habitat)
- Cottonwood Spring: 14.5% (72.6 m² of 500.5 m² potential habitat)
- Upper Scotia above pond: 43.6% (370.9 m² of 851.2 m² potential habitat)
- Upper Scotia below pond: 58.3% (308.8 m² of 529.6 m² potential habitat)

*Density Distribution:*
- Upper Scotia above pond: 75.3% of covered area ranked density ≥2.0
- Upper Scotia below pond: 45.5% of covered area ranked density ≥2.0
- Cottonwood Spring: 10.4% of covered area ranked density ≥2.0

*Stem Densities by Rank Score:*
- Cottonwood Spring, Bear Creek, upper Scotia below pond: 7.7-80.9 stems per 0.01 m² (ranks 0.5-3.0)
- Upper Scotia above pond: 5.8-130.6 stems per 0.01 m² (ranks 0.5-3.0), significantly different from other sites

**Reproductive Biology:**

Affolter (1985) observed flowering specimens June-August, fruiting specimens May-September. Field observations found abundant flowering only once (Cottonwood Spring, April 1988 under drying conditions); low-frequency flowering observed March-October. Self-pollination suspected. Seeds germinated within 1-2 weeks after ripening in aquarium observations. Vegetative reproduction via rhizomatous spreading and dispersal of dislodged clumps clearly important.

**Threats to Survival:**

1. **Documented habitat loss**: Five historic populations extirpated
2. **Water supply diminishment**: Groundwater pumping, diversions (Ojo de Agua supplies Cananea; Santa Cruz and San Pedro Rivers dewatered)
3. **Direct habitat alteration**: Pond dredging (Zinn Pond, House Pond)
4. **Flooding from degraded watersheds**: Loss of stabilizing riparian vegetation increases erosive flood impacts (Santa Cruz River, San Pedro River, Rio San Bernardino, Sonoita Creek—95% population loss at Cottonwood Spring from single August 1988 flood)
5. **Small population sizes**: Widely disjunct populations subject to stochastic events
6. **Limited suitable habitat**: Restricted to rare cienega wetlands in arid region

**Microhabitat Requirements:**

Detailed observations at [[Garden Canyon]] illustrate requirements: plant found in low-gradient cienega habitat with stable, non-eroded stream channel at 6,150 feet. Stream continues perennially for miles below but at higher gradient with rockier, heavily scoured bed—no [[Lilaeopsis]] found despite perennial flow and upstream immigration source, except one high-density patch immediately upstream of water diversion dam where flow is slowed.

**Land Ownership:**

Many sites privately owned. Five populations on U.S. government property:
- [[Garden Canyon]]: Fort Huachuca (Department of Defense)
- [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]: U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service
- [[Bear Canyon]], [[Scotia Canyon]], [[Lone Mountain Canyon]]: [[Coronado National Forest]] (Department of Agriculture, listed as Sensitive Species)

**Conservation Significance:**

Population health may serve as indicator of habitat condition for other sensitive cienega species: [[Spiranthes delitescens]] (Canelo Lady's Tresses orchid), [[Pyrgulopsis thompsonii]] (Huachuca Springsnail), [[Gila intermedia]] (Gila Chub), [[Ambystoma tigrinum stebbinsii]] (Huachuca/Sonora Tiger Salamander), [[Rana chiricahuensis]] (Chiricahua Leopard Frog), [[Thamnophis eques]] (Mexican Garter Snake).

**Recommendations:**

1. **Critical Habitat Designation**: All known sites should be considered critical habitat given rarity and habitat specialization
2. **Habitat Protection**: Protect cienega habitat supporting known populations through:
   - Acquiring Instream Flow water rights
   - Managing watersheds to reduce flood intensity
3. **Further Research**:
   - Exclosure/control experiments to evaluate role of competition and herbivory
   - Examination of seed dispersal mechanisms
   - Relationship between population dynamics and hydrologic factors

**Assessment Conclusion:**

[[Lilaeopsis schaffneriana]] subspecies recurva is a rare plant in unusual and vulnerable habitat. Small, widely disjunct populations subject to natural and human disturbances causing drastic local fluctuations. Species especially sensitive to watershed conditions. Documented loss of historic populations and clear future threats. Extensive surveys suggest unlikely many new populations will be found beyond currently known areas.
