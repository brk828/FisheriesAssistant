# Recovery Program for the Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fishes

## Executive Summary

This newsletter-style document describes the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for four rare native fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. A cooperative agreement was signed in January 1988 by the Secretary of the Interior, the governors of Colorado, Wyoming and Utah, and the Western Area Power Administration. The program was built to resolve conflict between protecting endangered fishes under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and future water development. Its stated goal is to recover and delist the species within 15 years. The document covers program elements, funding, institutional structure, congressional support, and several project updates, including monitoring, reservoir management, the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] biological opinion, and water rights acquisition.

## Background & Description

The program applies to three species federally listed as endangered in 1973 (Colorado squawfish, humpback chub and bonytail chub) and to the razorback sucker. The razorback sucker was a candidate for federal listing and is protected under state law in Colorado and Utah.

The program arose from conflict. In March 1984, the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS), Region 6 in Denver, organized the Upper Colorado River Basin Federal/State Coordinating Committee. It included high-level representatives from Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, a broad steering committee, and technical subcommittees. Program details and NEPA compliance were completed in late 1987.

Endorsing organizations include the Colorado Water Congress, Utah Water Users Association, Wyoming Water Development Association, Environmental Defense Fund, National Audubon Society, Colorado Wildlife Federation and Wyoming Wildlife Federation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and flow needs.** The program seeks to protect habitat under state water law and interstate compacts, including the [[Colorado River Compact]]. Much of the water needed below the Gunnison and on the Green River is expected to come from operational changes at federal reservoirs such as [[Blue Mesa Reservoir]] and [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. Where no federal reservoir exists, water rights will be bought from willing sellers and converted to [[Instream Flows]].

**Water development and Section 7.** Water projects remain subject to [[Section 7 Consultation]]. Indirect impacts from new water depletions are offset by a one-time contribution of $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion, adjusted annually for inflation. The FWS applies this case by case, depending on progress toward instream flow protection. Projects causing direct habitat impacts, such as migration barriers or physical alteration of occupied habitat, receive reasonable and prudent alternatives from the FWS to avoid jeopardy.

**Non-native species.** More than 30 non-native species are established within the current Upper Basin range of the four rare fishes. Some prey on larval and young fishes with severe effects. Stocking of non-native species is therefore restricted to areas where conflict with the rare fishes can be ruled out. The FWS and states will also assess selective removal of non-natives from essential areas. An information and education program and a law-enforcement program aim to reduce accidental take by anglers.

**Historical movement.** Historically, Colorado squawfish ranged up the White River to about Rio Blanco Reservoir, roughly 45 river miles above Taylor Draw Dam. Adults wintered in upstream reaches and made spawning migrations of 200 miles or more to lower Yampa River sections or downstream Green River reaches.

## Key Findings

**Program structure and goals**
- The program has five elements: habitat management, habitat development and maintenance, native fish stocking, non-native species and sport fishing, and research, data management and monitoring.
- The goal is recovery and delisting within 15 years.
- A Recovery Implementation Committee guides the effort. It includes representatives from the FWS, [[Bureau of Reclamation]], Western Area Power Administration, the three governors' appointees, and water user and environmental groups. Management and Technical Groups meet monthly or bimonthly.
- Habitat development may include grow-out (rearing) ponds, spawning and backwater habitats where these limit recovery, and fish passages.
- Native fish stocking will proceed only after a genetics management plan, survival research on hatchery stock, hatchery evaluation, and a monitoring program are in place.

**Funding**
- Annual funding in 1988 dollars totals $2.3 million: FWS $600,000, Reclamation $1.5 million, and the three states $200,000 combined.
- Water project sponsors add funds through the $10-per-acre-foot depletion charge.
- Congress appropriated $1 million in Fiscal Year 1988 for water rights acquisition. Participants had requested a $10 million commitment.
- Participants requested that Congress restore $200,000 of Section 6 funding and add $124,000 to FWS base funding, to meet the FWS annual commitment of $624,000 in Fiscal Year 1990. The Senate and House Appropriations Subcommittee on Interior reportedly approved the package, though final decisions were pending.
- Congress indicated it would consider additional funds once actual water rights purchases were arranged.

**Monitoring**
- The [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP), begun in 1986, aims to determine when Colorado squawfish and humpback chub populations are large and self-sustaining enough to be delisted.
- Its three components are spring sampling of late juvenile and adult squawfish, fall surveys of post-larval Colorado squawfish, and humpback chub monitoring.
- A common database holds all field data.

**Kenney Reservoir experiment**
- The 53-foot Taylor Draw Dam closed in October 1984, impounding about 13,800 acre-feet on the [[White River]] about 6 miles above Rangely, Colorado.
- The dam posed a barrier to native fish movement. The Colorado Division of Wildlife drafted a Lake Management Plan due to concern over warmwater predators.
- Colorado squawfish stocking began with 17,000 3–5 inch fish in 1988, followed by 35,000 in 1989 and up to 50,000 planned for 1990. Trout are stocked to maintain angling.
- Channel catfish stocking, possibly of sterile fish with permanent marks or tags, was under evaluation.
- The experiment aims to build a non-essential population that gives anglers exposure to the squawfish.

**Flaming Gorge biological opinion**
- High late summer and early fall releases from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] were flooding out backwater nursery habitat for young squawfish.
- Six biological and four hydrologic studies began in 1986, covering seasonal flow needs, reproduction, and species interactions. Tailwater trout studies were run concurrently.
- The Draft Biological Opinion deadline moved from June 1989 to March 1990 because of low Green River flows in 1988. An Integrated Technical Report was due by November 1989, and the opinion was to be drafted by March 15, 1990.

**Water rights acquisition**
- Highest-priority rivers are the [[Yampa River]], the White River, and a 15-mile reach of the Colorado from the Grand Valley Diversion to the Gunnison River confluence.
- Accomplishments include a cooperative agreement for 10,000 acre-feet of stored water from [[Ruedi Reservoir]] for release into the 15-mile reach below the Grand Valley Powerplant, a Yampa Basin water availability study by The Nature Conservancy, and a contract to evaluate instream flow options for the 15-mile reach.
- Transactions will follow state water law and involve willing sellers only.

**Program progress**
- The program began its first full fiscal year on October 1, 1988. More than 40 research and management projects totaling about $2.3 million were included in the Fiscal Year 1989 work plan.
- The Fiscal Year 1990 work plan was approved by the Implementation Committee in August 1989.

**Stakeholder views**
- Tom Pitts (Colorado Water Congress, Utah Water Users Association and Wyoming Water Development Association) described the program as a way to avoid conflict between water development and fish protection, and called it proactive rather than merely conflict-resolving.
- Bob Weaver (environmental groups) described it as precedent-setting because it forces water planning, Colorado River Compact consideration, and environmental needs into water resource decisions, which water developers have historically avoided.
