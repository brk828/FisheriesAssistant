# Explosive Speciation in Cichlid Fishes of the African Great Lakes: A Dynamic Model of Adaptive Radiation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Christian Sturmbauer]] (University of Innsbruck) synthesises evidence from the [[East African Great Lakes]] cichlid species flocks into a generalised model of [[Adaptive Radiation]]. The author argues that rapid, "explosive" [[Speciation]] in these lakes reflects a dynamic process in which abiotic modulators (especially [[Lake Level Fluctuations]]) and intrinsic biological traits of the radiating fishes interact, and in which the balance between these drivers shifts over time. The model proposes four successive stages of radiation, from initial sympatric diversification to tertiary allopatric diversification with little further morphological change. Stated as a platform for future research rather than a settled theory, the model is tested against the present-day condition of several lakes.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** The East African Great Lakes contain the world's most diverse freshwater assemblages of cichlids, with the [[Lake Tanganyika]] flock described as the most morphologically, ecologically and behaviourally diverse of the African lakes.
- **Key anatomy:** A second set of pharyngeal jaws, functionally decoupled from the oral jaws, is credited with enabling phenotypic plasticity and rapid adaptation to new resources.
- **Reproductive traits:** All cichlids show brood care, and most flocks (except Tanganyika) are maternal [[Mouthbrooding]] lineages, so [[Sexual Selection]] via mate choice is proposed as an important promoter of speciation.
- **Foraging specialisation:** Tanganyikan scale-eaters (*Perissodus*-type feeders) show a 50:50 split of left- and right-turned mouths, an example of frequency-dependent selection (Hori, 1993).
- **Lineage structure:** The Tanganyika flock comprises 12 ecomorphologically distinct tribes; at least six seeded the radiation and the remaining six (the H-lineage) underwent a rapid species explosion. Lake Malawi contains a tilapiine sub-flock (five species) and a haplochromine sub-flock divided into two to five clades.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake level fluctuations.** Water-level changes, driven by climate or geology, occurred repeatedly in the three great lakes. They fragment lakes into sub-lakes, drive cycles of isolation and secondary contact, and can cause extinctions:
- Lake Victoria dried c. 12,400 years ago; some species survived in refugia.
- Lake Tanganyika split into three sub-lakes about 25,000 years ago.
- Lake Malawi's southern fauna was nearly eradicated, with a drop of about 150 m some 200 years ago leaving sediment layers of fish bones.
- The major drop of c. 500 m in Lakes Malawi and Tanganyika is proposed to have caused mass extinction and hybridisation, a "species amalgamation" that may explain mitochondrial polymorphism in Mbuna.

**Lake age and diversity.** Species numbers correlate roughly with lake age and size:

| Lake | Approx. age | Endemic cichlids (as reported) |
|---|---|---|
| Nabugabo | <4,000 yr | 4 |
| Kivu | <4,000 yr | c. 20 |
| Victoria | 12,000–200,000 yr | c. 300 |
| Malawi | c. 700,000 yr | 500–1,000 |
| Tanganyika | 9–12 Myr | 197 described (likely more) |

**Habitat and dispersal.** Sand shores and pelagic habitats are less affected by level changes than rock habitats. Sand beaches act as dispersal barriers for specialised rock cichlids. Tanganyikan rock cichlids tend to have wider ranges, while Malawian rock species are younger and more locally restricted. The genus *Tropheus* illustrates allopatric diversification, with over 100 populations and six nominal species, each shoreline population a distinct geographic race.

**Water chemistry and turbidity.** Lake chemistry isolates lacustrine habitats from surrounding rivers. Increased water turbidity can break down visual mate-recognition cues, and experiments show species status can be reversed by environmental change.

## Key Findings

1. **Four-stage model of adaptive radiation:** (1) initial sympatric primary radiation followed by rapid ecomorphological differentiation; (2) primary allopatric diversification among moderately specialised, poorly dispersing species; (3) secondary allopatric diversification driven by repeated lake-level cycles, termed a "[[Species Pump]]" (Rossiter, 1995); (4) tertiary diversification with little morphological change, where geographic races become sister species differing mainly in neutral traits such as colouration and fin morphology.
2. **Stage assignment of present lakes:** Okavango Delta, Lake Kivu, Lake Chohoha and small shallow East African lakes are placed in stage 1; Lake Victoria in stage 2; Lake Malawi in stage 3; and Lake Tanganyika in stage 4.
3. **Tanganyika origin:** Lake Tanganyika developed from a river-swamp-lake sequence over about 20 million years. The model links three major habitat transitions (riverine to swamp, formation of protolakes, and a truly lacustrine habitat) to three successive radiation events. The lake merged into a single body about 1.6 million years ago (the source text gives "c. i6 million", read here as 1.6 Myr, which is an OCR-uncertain value).
4. **Speciation modes:** Both allopatric and sympatric speciation are considered to occur. Sympatric models may better explain the speed of speciation in these lakes, and sexual selection on male colour may operate alongside disruptive selection on niche.
5. **Species concept:** The author adopts a reproductive-cohesion definition of species rather than reproductive isolation, to separate spatial isolation from intrinsic isolating barriers.
6. **Morphological divergence:** Lake Victoria's flock (younger) shows intermediate morphotypes, while Tanganyika's older flock shows large morphological gaps, consistent with Greenwood's (1984b) age-divergence correlation and with the [[Red Queen Hypothesis]] (Van Valen, 1973).
7. **Rates of speciation:** Speciation rates are expected to be moderate early in a radiation and to rise almost exponentially once intralacustrine allopatric speciation dominates, particularly in large lakes.
8. **Limitations:** The author states that models necessarily generalise, that radiations are not purely deterministic, and that the framework is intended to be refined by future data, including phylogenetic reconstructions from DNA sequences.
