# Distribution of Pure Populations of the Native Arizona Trout (*Salmo apache*): Management Report for Recovery of a Threatened Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by [[John N. Rinne]] of the Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Office of Endangered Species, assesses the genetic and morphological purity of the native [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*) across streams in east-central Arizona. The species had been degraded by hybridization with the introduced [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). Populations were sampled by electrofishing and classified as pure, pure-impure, or impure using morphometric, meristic and [[Karyotype Analysis]] data. The report is explicitly a working summary intended for management agencies, not a final published study, and its classifications are described by the author as conservative and provisional.

## Background & Description

*Salmo apache* was described by [[Miller 1972]] and is a native salmonid of the White Mountains region. Its listing history was unusual: it was listed as endangered in 1975 and downlisted to threatened the same year, despite an undefined distribution of pure stocks in the wild. The main threat identified is the introduction of rainbow trout, whose hybridization with the native form greatly reduced its range of pure stocks ([[Harper 1978]]).

The study was conducted under an interagency agreement between the [[Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station]] and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and it supports agencies such as the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], the [[U.S. Forest Service]], and the [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]].

Four diagnostic characters separated the species in the analysis:
- Vertebrae count
- Pyloric caeca count
- Scales above the lateral line
- Karyotype (chromosome number)

Spot density was examined but judged non-diagnostic. Six morphometric characters were chosen for their minimal range overlap and large mean differences between rainbow and native trout: snout length, dorsal fin base, dorsal fin depressed length, anal fin base, anal fin depressed length, and adipose fin depressed length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Populations were sampled at point localities by electrofishing, with specimens preserved in 10% formalin and radiographed for vertebral counts. Most samples came from streams on the Fort Apache Indian Reservation and the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest; Grant and Ash Creeks on the Coronado National Forest were the exceptions. Sampled drainages included the [[Black River]], the [[East Fork of the White River]], the [[North Fork of the White River]], the [[Little Colorado River]] system, the [[Graham Mountains]] streams, and [[Buckaloo Creek]] in the Blue River drainage.

Hybridization patterns track stocking history. Streams on the Reservation appeared less influenced by rainbow genes than those on the National Forest, which may reflect differences in when regular rainbow stocking ended on each land area. The author also suggests that rainbow genotypes may be selected against in harsh headwater environments. Reservoirs with rainbow stocking and downstream connectivity pose ongoing risks; [[Nelson Reservoir]] is cited as a likely source of rainbow introgression into [[Paddy Creek]].

## Key Findings

**Sample scope and methods**
- Specimens were collected across many streams in the White Mountains, Graham Mountains, Little Colorado system, and Blue River drainage.
- Comparative rainbow trout samples came from hatchery and lake populations, including the White River Hatchery and [[Luna Lake]].
- Meristic and morphometric values for rainbow trout were drawn largely from [[Needham and Gard 1959]].

**Classification outcomes (based on the 10-character combined assessment)**
- **Pure:** The report designates nine streams as pure, including [[Grant Creek]] (Graham Mountains), [[Mineral Creek]], the mainstream [[East Fork of the White River]], [[Ord Creek]], [[Sun Creek]], [[Boggy Creek]], [[Crooked Creek]], [[Soldier Creek]], and [[Centerfire Creek]]. Several of these are flagged as tentative because of small samples or missing karyotypes.
- **Pure-impure:** Streams such as [[Big Bonita Cienega]], [[Little Bonita Creek]], [[Flash Creek]], [[Little Diamond Creek]], and [[Mamie Creek]] show non-native morphometric traits but native meristics.
- **Impure:** Notable examples include [[Ash Creek]], [[Paddy Creek]], [[Deep Creek]], [[North Fork of Diamond Creek]], [[Paradise Creek]], [[Hughey Creek]], and [[Squaw Creek]]. Paddy Creek is described as one of the most contaminated populations studied.

**Meristic and karyotype patterns**
- Of 425 specimens, 29 (7%) had vertebrae counts below 58. A count of 57 was the most frequent low value.
- Only 2 of 433 specimens had pyloric caeca counts below the native lower limit.
- Among 421 specimens, 11 (2.6%) had scale counts above the upper limit (40).
- In Black River tributaries on the National Forest, 65 of 191 specimens (34%) had scale counts below 32, compared with 6 of 154 specimens (6%) across the East and North Fork and Black River tributaries combined.
- Meristic and karyotype classifications disagreed in 24 of 121 fish (about 20%), so the author treats the combined meristic set as a single unit when interpreting results.

**Interpretive caveats**
- Small sample sizes (often fewer than six specimens) and point-locality sampling limit confidence. Corduroy Creek illustrates this: a larger sample from Arizona Game and Fish Department changed the vertebrae result.
- Historical stocking of "black spotted mountain trout," possibly [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*), may have influenced vertebrae counts of 62 that were attributed to rainbow influence.
- The genetics of hybrid expression and backcrossing remain poorly understood, and the author calls for artificial hybridization studies.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Treat the classifications as provisional and working documents.
- Sample at least 25 fish per locality where feasible, and add localities within streams.
- Prioritize population estimates for the East Fork of the White River, and resample Big Bonita Creek at two to three localities.
- Continue suppressing non-native trout stocking, and carefully review timber, grazing, recreation, mining, and road-building in pure-population areas.
- Address unsampled or unresolved streams, including [[Grant Creek (Blue River)]], Home Creek, Bear Wallow Creek, Snake Creek, and Pacheta Creek.

**Management implications**
- Several "pure" populations are small or introduced, so their conservation value is fragile.
- Soldier Creek is a stock source for hatchery rearing and introductions, so a larger, fully analyzed sample is needed before its purity is confirmed.
- The [[Christmas Tree Lake]] population, stocked from Ord, Firebox, and Deep Creeks, may carry impurity from the latter two streams.
