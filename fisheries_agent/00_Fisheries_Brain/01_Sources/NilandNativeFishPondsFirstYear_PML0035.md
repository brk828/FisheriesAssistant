# Niland Native Fish Ponds: First-Year Experimental Operations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 interoffice memorandum from the California Department of Fish and Game's Desert Area fishery staff sets out the first-year plan for the [[Niland Native Fish Ponds]] in [[Imperial County]]. The first year is treated as experimental. Operations are limited to rearing [[Razorback Sucker]] fry, with one pond reserved for testing the holding and rearing of [[Bonytail]]. The plan covers staffing and responsibilities, pond water quality, fertilization and plankton monitoring, fry sampling, feeding, pond rotation, overwintering, and draining procedures. Its stated aim is to keep labor to a minimum while learning how to rear native fish in a hatchery setting.

## Background & Description

The memo describes 1986 as the first attempt at rearing native fish in a hatchery setting, so it is classed as an experimental year. Its main points are:

- **Staffing:** The Niland area biologist ([[Nicol]]) manages the facility. Supervision comes from [[Glenn Black]] (Associate Fishery Biologist) and field supervisor [[Wayne Parker]], who advises as needed. [[Jim St. Amant]], Desert Area Fishery Supervisor, coordinates construction and maintenance funding and decides the disposition of reared fish. The program will be reviewed in July 1986.
- **Expected output:** About 50% of the razorback sucker fry are expected to survive, reaching 7–8 inches by November 1986. The scanned text gives the starting fry count only partially; the memo later refers to 25,000 fry originally stocked in pond #2.
- **Planning input:** Parker and [[Buddy Jensen]] (Fish Culturist, [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Dexter Hatchery]], New Mexico) contributed to the first-year strategies.
- **Stocking destinations:** Depending on growth, numbers, and site conditions, fish are to be stocked in the Topock, Parker, and Imperial divisions of the [[Colorado River]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality and disease risk**
- The swimming-pool sand filters used for the native fish ponds will not remove bacteria or protozoans.
- Jensen and Parker expect [[Ich]], a cool-season parasite that affects fish in the existing fish pond, could also appear in the native fish ponds. The memo hopes it will not be a problem if the native fish are removed and the ponds drained by November.
- Once fish reach about 2 inches, 6–10 fish per pond will be removed every six weeks and sent for a disease check.

**Thermal stratification and aeration**
- Parker expects a [[Thermocline]] to form at about 4½ feet.
- Pond conditions should stay good in the new ponds during the first year, but problems could appear as early as the second year.
- The older [[Grass Carp]] ponds may need [[Aeration]] during the first few weeks. Work continues on a permanent aeration system and a backup pump for emergencies.

**Monitoring schedule**
- [[Dissolved Oxygen]] and temperature are recorded daily.
- [[pH]] is measured every two weeks, since it appears stable in the CCF ponds.

**Fertilization and [[Plankton]]**
- [[Fertilization]] rates are listed in Table I. Stocking rates will stay roughly constant so that different fertilization rates can be tested in the first year.
- Plankton tows are made weekly in each pond to decide whether extra nitrogen treatments are needed. Samples are preserved in 10% formalin or stored in labeled jars for later study of primary productivity under different fertilization rates.

## Key Findings

**1. Fry sampling regimes (three size classes)**
- **A. About ½ to 1½ inches (about 5 weeks):** Young razorbacks show [[Phototaxis]]. Lights are used at night (three times per week) to draw larvae to the surface for survival and abundance estimates. A significant drop in larval numbers would signal a serious problem, and Jensen would be contacted.
- **B. 1½ to 2 inches (about 8 weeks):** Each pond is sampled every two weeks with a 6–10 ft bagless [[Fry Seine]]. Fish are counted and weighed to determine fish per pound. Other methods, including minnow traps and cast nets, will also be tested.
- **C. 2 inches and up:** Each pond is sampled monthly with a 20–25 ft [[Bag Seine]]. Growth rates can be projected between sampling dates.

Feeding rates are adjusted to match each sampling result.

**2. Feeding**
- Food is hand-applied so it spreads evenly over the pond surface. Jensen advised against using automatic feeders, which can degrade pond conditions quickly, especially on weekends.
- Feed size and amount follow a size-based table. Fry of about 1 to 1½ inches are fed enough to cover the surface about three times a day. The table's feeding frequencies (2–3 times daily) are partly illegible in the scan.
- Parker will work with Nicol to develop feeding charts in pounds of feed per day for fish larger than 1½ inches.

**3. Pond rotation and density management**
- Razorbacks held in pond #2 are moved to a second pond about one month after stocking. Half of the 25,000 fry originally stocked will either go to the Colorado River or be moved to one of the 0.25-acre grass carp ponds, when available.
- This cycle repeats every six weeks until November.
- Pond #2 is the critical pond because of its large fry density. Dissolved oxygen readings of **2–3 ppm** mean conditions are deteriorating fast, and fish must be moved immediately.

**4. Overwintering**
- In November, a subsample of 50–100 razorbacks from each pond will be moved to the supply pond. They will be held through winter to assess growth and variation within the cohort.
- These fish will be held until they reach 12 inches and then stocked in [[Senator Wash Reservoir]].

**5. Draining procedure**
- If a thermocline has formed (based on temperature profile data), a pump will remove the anaerobic [[Hypolimnion]] before the pond is drained.
- Fresh water is pumped in at the surface at the same time. When the pond is fully mixed, it is drained.

**6. Research and labor goals**
- The first experimental year will test rearing methods for razorbacks. Techniques and equipment will also be developed to reduce labor, such as monitoring methods that keep staff time to a minimum.
