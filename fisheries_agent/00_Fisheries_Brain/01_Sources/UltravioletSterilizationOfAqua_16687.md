# Ultraviolet Sterilization of Aquarium Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquarium-husbandry

## Executive Summary

This 1970 report by [[Earl S. Herald]], [[Robert P. Dempster]] and [[Marjorie Hunt]] of the [[Steinhart Aquarium]] evaluates [[Ultraviolet Sterilization]] as a way to reduce bacterial loads in crowded, recirculating aquarium systems. Test and production units were installed between 1960 and the late 1960s on several large systems. The authors found that UV units markedly lowered bacterial counts and reduced infection in some animal systems, but did not lower [[Fish Mortality]] rates. They conclude that UV sterilization is a specialty tool for systems with valuable exhibits where chemical treatment is not an option, and that healthy fish in uncrowded, well-filtered tanks do not require it.

## Background & Description

The authors trace UV germicidal knowledge to 1877 and note that UV sterilization of municipal water supplies began in 1909 (3,000,000 gallons per day). Its first application to large aquarium water systems came in 1962. Earlier uses included seawater purification for [[Shellfish Culture]] and commercial practice in England, Japan and the United States. Small home-aquarium UV units had been marketed but were not widely adopted.

Steinhart's program progressed through several units:

- **Initial test unit:** A stainless steel, four-tube [[Aquafine]] unit rated at 50 gpm, designed for swimming pools. Results were reported at the 1961 International Aquarium Symposium in Monaco.
- **1963–1964 PVC units:** Inert polyvinyl chloride sterilizers were installed on:
  - Tropical seawater system (75°F; 23,717 gallons): 150 gpm, 12-tube
  - Tropical freshwater system (82°F; 26,971 gallons): 150 gpm, 12-tube
  - Alligator system (80°F; 11,132 gallons): 6-tube (later described at 82°F)
- **Dolphin system:** A 116 gpm, 8-tube stainless steel unit, installed later on the freshwater dolphin system.

Other facilities cited include [[Sea World]] (two 55,000-gallon seawater systems, at 75°F and 68°F, using PVC Aquafine units), [[Aquarama]] in Philadelphia (now closed; tropical seawater, 2,000 gallons; tropical freshwater, 1,700 gallons, with Ultradion units) and the [[La Jolla Fishery-Oceanography Center]], which used a 250 gpm, 26-tube wall-mounted Aquafine unit for experimental laboratories.

Funding came from the [[National Fisheries Center]] and Aquarium of the U.S. Department of the Interior and the [[Office of Naval Research]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physics of UV action.** The authors note that the most biologically active UV wavelengths lie between 1900 and 3000 Å, with the most lethal effect at about 2600 Å, the wavelength most effective against [[Deoxyribonucleic Acid]]. Sterilizing tubes peak at 2537 Å. About 16% of tube output lies at longer wavelengths (2652–5780 Å). Ozone-producing tubes operate at 1950 Å, and the 2900–3100 Å band is antirachitic (stimulates [[Vitamin D]] production). Long-wave [[Black Light]] tubes (maximum at 3660 Å) were reported by Bevan and Zeiller to have therapeutic effects on fishes and turtles, but the authors say further tests are needed.

**Chemical side effects.** Following [[William Shipman]], the authors explain that UV, ozone and radiation all form peroxy compounds and free radicals in water, which contribute to lethal action. UV peroxide formation can be shown by testing water next to the tube.

**Temperature dependence.** UV penetration increases with water temperature. A tube in direct contact with water works best at about 105°F. At 70°F it operates at only about 50% of maximum efficiency. Manufacturers therefore place a quartz or vycor jacket around the tube, with an insulating air gap, so the tube operates near 105°F regardless of water temperature.

**Penetration and limiting factors.** Under optimum conditions UV penetrates about 1½ inches. The authors identify three limits:

1. **Turbidity:** Suspended matter reduces penetration, so the UV unit must follow the filter, not precede it.
2. **Tube aging:** Tubes are guaranteed for at least 7,500 hours, but glass becomes opaque with use. A UV-meter test rack is recommended.
3. **Sleeve fouling:** Scum builds up on quartz sleeves. The Ultradion unit has a built-in push-pull cleaner. The Aquafine unit requires disassembly every three months (8 man-hours for a 12-tube unit). Sea World's David Powell used 20% hydrochloric acid, left for half an hour and then flushed for at least 15 minutes, which avoided breakage of quartz sleeves costing $15 each.

**Tank design.** Treated water must replace all tank water. Dead spots or static zones reduce or negate the benefit, since UV-treated water is the opposite of chlorinated or copper-treated water, which retain residual disinfectant.

**Parasite limitations.** UV kills free-swimming stages of [[Oodinium]] and free-swimming ciliates as water passes through the sterilizer, but not adult stages attached to fish gills. By contrast, free copper ions can remove these parasites from fish.

**Bacterial flora.** Common bacteria in the systems included [[Pseudomonas]], [[Aerobacter]], Micrococci, [[Proteus]], [[Alkaligenes]], [[Bacillus Subtilis]] and [[Escherichia Coli]]. The authors caution that constant UV use might select for UV-resistant strains, analogous to DDT-resistant flies and copper-resistant diatoms.

**System notes.** The Steinhart alligator system (11,132 gallons) contains 7 alligators (7–10 ft), 11 smaller crocodilians of seven species, and 125 turtles of 16 species. It also feeds six diorama tanks with large snakes and lizards. The freshwater dolphin system is an 8,500-gallon display tank at 82°F with its own heater and reverse-flow filter (90 gpm). Its maximum animal weight was 725 pounds, and daily food reached about 35 pounds.

## Key Findings

**Bacterial reduction (Table 1, one-week UV shutdown).** Counts in bacteria per cc, UV on and off, are summarized as typical values:

- Seawater after UV sterilizer: 1,600 (with UV off, 7 days); after filter 330
- Freshwater head tank: 860; cistern reservoir: 5,500 (after filter 290; cistern 500 as recorded)
- Seawater system: 23,717 gallons, 419 animals, 788 pounds, 11 pounds of food daily, 28 gallons of water per pound of animal
- Freshwater system: 26,971 gallons, 589 animals, 782 pounds, 12 pounds of food daily, 35 gallons of water per pound of animal

The authors note that counts did not always follow a logical sequence through the system.

**Shutdown and restart (Table 2).** During a five-month UV outage in the heated seawater system (75°F; 419 fishes, 788 pounds; about 21,000 gallons as stated in the table background), counts at seven checkpoints rose to 26,000–40,000 bacteria per cc. The first day of renewed UV operation caused a sharp drop, and a sample ten days later showed the effect was maintained. Medium was Reutzer's with blood added.

**Disease control.** In the alligator system, one alligator had been in sick bay almost constantly before UV installation, sometimes requiring amputation. After installation, the animals still fought, but wounds no longer became infected and gangrene disappeared.

**Dolphin system.** A new 116 gpm, 8-tube stainless steel Aquafine unit effectively removed the high bacterial population. Chlorine compounds cannot be used in this tank because the system alternates water with the freshwater fish system (26,971 gallons). Ammonia removal in the reverse-flow filter is less effective than in a sand-and-gravel gravity filter.

**Mortality.** Despite the bacterial reductions, mortality did not fall. Across populations of 419 to 814 acclimated fishes (30 days or more in captivity), the average mortality rate was about 2½% per month. During the five-month UV outage the seawater system's mortality was not appreciably higher than this norm. The authors conclude that fish in good condition (no parasites, abrasions, or gill damage) can live in high-bacteria systems.

**Handling practice.** Steinhart does not net valuable fishes (except sharks, triggers and moray eels). Valued fish are moved with [[Quinaldine]] anesthetic, bucketed into a reserve tank without anesthetic, and returned the same way. This protects body mucus and natural immunity.

**Recommendations.**
- UV sterilization is recommended for systems with valuable living exhibits, where bacterial control is essential and chemical treatment cannot be used.
- Healthy fish in uncrowded aquariums with adequate aeration and filtration can live without UV, though UV adds protection against outside contamination.
- Effective UV use requires proper tank design, filtration before UV, temperature-appropriate tube operation, and regular cleaning and tube testing.
