# Recolonization of a Montane Stream by Mountain Galaxias After Rainbow Trout Eradication

#ichthyology #marine-biology #galaxiidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus mykiss*) could be eradicated from a small montane stream using the piscicide [[Rotenone]], and whether the native [[Mountain Galaxias]] (*Galaxias olidus*) would recolonize the stream naturally. Trout were removed from 2.4 km of [[Lees Creek]] upstream of a stream-gauging weir in March 1992. The weir was then fitted with a steel grill to form a 1.75 m vertical barrier. Galaxias returned to the trout-free section above the barrier, and a breeding population was established by 1995, three years after eradication. Galaxias never appeared in the downstream section where trout remained. The authors describe this as the first documented recovery of a native Australian fish following eradication of an exotic fish from a stream, and they conclude that trout are the main cause of the fragmented distribution of *G. olidus*.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** *Galaxias olidus* is a small native galaxiid. It is not listed as threatened in any Australian State or Territory, but it has declined in abundance and become fragmented in the presence of trout. Earlier evidence for this decline was largely inferred from distribution patterns rather than measured directly.

**Introduced salmonids.** [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) and rainbow trout were introduced to Australia more than a century ago, before native fish distributions were documented. Trout were first introduced into the Cotter River catchment in 1888. Predation by trout is considered a major threat to galaxiids. Predation by rainbow trout has also been implicated in declines of galaxiids and other fish.

**Management options.** Two responses to trout predation are relocating threatened species to trout-free waters and eradicating trout from critical habitats. At the start of this study, no complete trout eradication from an Australian stream had been documented. The authors also note that [[Piscicides]] are used in at least 30 countries, and that [[Rotenone]] is among the most commonly used.

**Rationale for the approach.** Studies of introduced species usually begin after the effects have occurred and are confounded by habitat degradation. Experimentally introducing an exotic species was judged ethically questionable, so the authors instead removed the exotic species and observed the response of the native fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The study streams lie in the lower [[Cotter River Catchment]] in the [[Australian Capital Territory]]. Key features are:

- **Lees Creek:** a perennial fourth-order stream rising on the eastern slopes of the Brindabella Ranges. Catchment altitude ranges from 600 to 1320 m, and mean annual precipitation is about 990 mm. Stream width is generally 1-2 m, with average depth of 100-300 mm. The catchment is mostly dry and wet sclerophyll forest, with a commercial *Pinus radiata* plantation in the lower catchment.
- **[[Blundells Creek]]:** a third-order perennial tributary of Lees Creek, averaging 1 m wide and 100-300 mm deep. In 1990 it held *G. olidus* but no trout.
- **[[Coree Creek]] and [[Bushrangers Creek]]:** perennial fourth-order streams, 2.5-3 m wide and 100-400 mm deep. Both held trout and were used as reference sites.

**Natural barrier and dispersal route.** Blundells Creek enters Lees Creek through a 15 m long piped outlet, 300 mm in diameter, beneath a management road. Fast, unbroken flow in the pipe prevents trout from moving upstream from Lees Creek into Blundells Creek. The pipe does not stop downstream movement, so *G. olidus* could be displaced from Blundells Creek into Lees Creek.

**Trout distribution before treatment.** A 1990 survey recorded trout in Lees, Bushrangers and Coree creeks, and brown trout also in Coree Creek. Trout occurred above and below the Lees Creek weir. *G. olidus* was absent from all three streams.

**Forest and channel structure.** Several v-notch stream-gauging weirs were built in the lower Cotter catchment between 1964 and 1972. One of these is on Lees Creek. The weir pool was relatively deep and is thought to have concentrated trout.

## Key Findings

### 1. Rotenone eradication of trout from Lees Creek

- **Treatment:** 2.4 km of Lees Creek upstream of the weir was treated over two days, 23-24 March 1992. Treatment proceeded upstream in sections of about 500 m. Mesh stop-nets were placed after each section to stop trout re-invading from downstream.
- **Application:** About 300-350 mL of a 5% rotenone emulsion was added over 15 minutes per section, together with a fluorescent dye (sodium fluorescein) to track the rotenone slug. Flows were low (0.37-0.53 m/s) and water temperatures high, conditions under which rotenone is more effective and breaks down faster.
- **Neutralization:** About 350-500 g of [[Potassium Permanganate]] was added at the downstream limit of each section to neutralize the rotenone.
- **Concentration correction:** The original estimate of about 1 part per million was corrected in an erratum to 0.05 part per million.
- **Catch:** 30 *O. mykiss* and 5 *G. olidus* were collected during treatment. Dead fish that drifted into vegetation or debris were probably missed. Four of the five galaxias came from the most upstream site on the western arm, suggesting this very small, shallow reach was becoming marginal for trout.
- **Trout size:** 76-222 mm long, most under 170 mm.
- **Comparison:** The 1990 survey recorded 35 *O. mykiss* from only 40 m of stream just above the weir. The authors suggest the higher 1990 density may reflect low rainfall and concentration in the weir pool, or incomplete local recovery from the 1990 depletion sampling.

### 2. Barrier performance

- Both arms of Lees Creek were treated up to natural or built barriers: a road culvert on one arm and an underground section on the other. Electrofishing above these barriers found no trout.
- The weir was augmented with a heavy steel grill to form a 1.75 m vertical barrier. No trout re-invaded the treated section during the study. No maintenance was needed.

### 3. Annual monitoring, 1993-1996

- **Design:** Fish were sampled each autumn at three sites by backpack electrofishing (Smith-Root Model 12). Sites 1 and 2 lay in the untreated stream below the weir, 1750 m and 750 m downstream. Site 3 lay in the treated section just above the weir. Sampled lengths were 40 m, 70 m and 170 m at Sites 1, 2 and 3. Greater effort was directed at Site 3 to confirm that trout had not re-invaded.
- **Trout:** *O. mykiss* was never eradicated from the untreated sites below the weir and was present in every sample there. It was absent from the treated section (Site 3) in all years after 1992.
- **Galaxias at Site 3 (treated section):** counts rose over time, from 10 (1993) to 8 (1994), 19 (1995) and 75 (1996).
- **Galaxias downstream:** *G. olidus* was never detected at Sites 1 or 2.
- **Reproduction:** Ripe males and gravid females in 1995 and 1996 confirmed that recruitment was occurring in Lees Creek.

### 4. Reference streams

| Species | Coree Creek 1990 | Coree Creek 1996 | Bushrangers Creek 1990 | Bushrangers Creek 1996 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *O. mykiss* | 36 | 8 | 65 | 54 |
| *S. trutta* | 17 | 9 | 0 | 0 |
| *G. olidus* | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

- Species composition did not change at either reference site between 1990 and 1996, although fish numbers were lower in 1996. *G. olidus* was absent from both sites in both years. This indicates that the Lees Creek recovery was not part of a wider regional increase in galaxias.

### 5. Recolonization pattern and size structure

- The first colonists in 1993 were almost all small fish, 46-61 mm long, corresponding to the 0+ age group. The authors suggest these were displaced downstream from Blundells Creek by high winter flows, since juveniles are more vulnerable to displacement than adults.
- The 1994 sample contained two size classes, small adults and juveniles. It is unclear whether the juveniles reflected limited breeding in Lees Creek or further displacement from Blundells Creek.
- By 1995, ripe adults and juveniles together indicated successful reproduction within Lees Creek.

### 6. Interpretation and management implications

- **Causal link:** Recovery occurred only in the stream from which trout were removed. Galaxias did not recover where trout remained, and did not recover in the reference streams. The authors therefore attribute the recovery directly to trout removal.
- **Predation hypothesis:** The small size of the colonists may make them vulnerable to trout predation. Trout are opportunistic and usually take the largest accessible prey. The authors cite the lack of predator-avoidance behaviour in *G. olidus* as a possible reason for its decline, and hypothesize that trout predation is the most likely cause of its failure to recolonize trout-inhabited waters.
- **Barriers:** Relatively small structures can exclude trout. The authors suggest natural materials such as boulders or logs would work as well as concrete. Natural barriers such as waterfalls need no construction. A piped road crossing, although not built as a barrier, also blocked trout movement for the long term. Barriers can also block native fish, so land and water agencies must take care when building them. Ongoing vigilance is needed against deliberate reintroduction of trout by anglers.
- **Cost-effectiveness:** The authors conclude that rotenone treatment of small streams is rapid and cost-effective for removing trout, and is a useful tool for conserving threatened native fish. They note the broader pattern of fragmented galaxiid distribution in the upper Cotter catchment, where barriers to trout included waterfalls, weirs, piped crossings, underground stream sections and bogs.
