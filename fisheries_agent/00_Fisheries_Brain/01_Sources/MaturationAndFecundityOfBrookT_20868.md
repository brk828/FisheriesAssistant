# Maturation and Fecundity of Brook Trout from Infertile Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 study by [[Wydoski and Cooper]] (Pennsylvania State University, published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, vol. 23, no. 5) examines the reproductive biology of [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) living in nutrient-poor, low-productivity streams in Pennsylvania. The authors combined mark-and-recapture population estimates, age and growth analysis, sex-ratio testing, maturity indices, oocyte size-frequency tracking and fecundity regressions across eight to twelve streams. They found slow growth, late maturation, and modest egg production, and they assembled ecological life tables for two intensively studied populations, [[Tomtit Run]] and [[Lingle Stream]], to show how survival and fecundity trade off to maintain population equilibrium.

## Background & Description

The [[Brook Trout]] is widely distributed across eastern North America and is often found in small, infertile streams of the [[Appalachian Mountains]]. Trout in these streams grow slowly and occur at low densities, yet the populations are resilient to angler harvest and even heavy reduction by fish toxicants (Cooper et al., 1962). The study aimed to measure the reproductive ability of these populations and compare them with previously reported populations elsewhere in the species range, including [[Lawrence Creek]] (Wisconsin) and the [[Pigeon River]] (Michigan).

Study streams were in two areas: the [[Allegheny Plateau]] (1961 collections) and the ridge-and-valley region of central Pennsylvania (1963 and 1964 collections). Bedrock was sandstone and shale, with second-growth northern hardwood forest. Streams were 5 to 10 ft wide, with gradients of 110 to 520 ft/mile, total alkalinity of 12 to 38 ppm, and pH of 6.8 to 7.5. Turbidity was low, and aquatic plants and bottom organisms were scarce. Sixteen fish species were collected across the streams, but only brook trout and sculpins (*Cottus bairdi* and *C. cognatus*) were abundant; [[Brown Trout]], blacknose dace, longnose dace and creek chub were commonly collected, and the remainder were rare.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water quality and productivity:** Streams of this water chemistry were previously shown to be unproductive of fish in the region (McFadden and Cooper, 1962). Low alkalinity and scarce food organisms were linked to slow growth.
- **Associated fauna:** The fish community included lampreys (*Ichthyomyzon* sp.), brown trout, several cyprinids, suckers, a sunfish (*Lepomis gibbosus*), a darter (*Etheostoma flabellare*) and two sculpin species. Brook trout and sculpins dominated.
- **Sampling constraints:** Electrofishing was ineffective at water temperatures below 45 °F, so population estimates were not made for February, March, April or December samples.
- **Environmental control of reproduction:** The authors argue that nutrition, growth rate, territorial behaviour and feeding behaviour all regulate maturation, egg production and young-of-year survival in these small streams.

## Key Findings

**Population density and growth**
- Standing crops across eight streams ranged from 14 to 41 lb/acre; brook trout were the dominant species.
- No trout in their 5th year were found, and age-group III trout were rare.
- Mean lengths at the end of the growing season were approximately 3.0 in (age 0), 4.5 in (age I), 5.5 in (age II) and 6.5 in (age III). Most trout matured, spawned and died before reaching 6 in.
- The length-weight relationship for 1088 trout was W = 0.15 L^3.0 (weight in g, length in inches).
- Males grew slightly faster than females in large samples (Tomtit Run and Lingle Stream), but differences were small.

**Sex ratio**
- Across all streams the sex ratio was close to 1:1. Only age-group II showed a female preponderance (58.2%, p < 0.001).

**Maturation**
- Most trout reached sexual maturity at the end of their 3rd year, though many were mature at 2 years and one male was mature at 1 year.
- Size influenced maturity; in age-group II, the percentage of mature fish rose with total length.
- Development of gonads began in June and peaked in September. Peak testes reached about 1.5% of body weight, and peak ovaries about 10.0%.

**Oocyte development and ripe egg size**
- Maturing ova grew from about 0.9 to 1.0 mm (June) to 1.75 mm (July), 2.5 mm (August), 3.0 mm (September) and 4.0 mm (October).
- Maturing females could be distinguished from immature ones by late August or early September.
- Mean diameter of ripe ova from 67 females was 4.05 mm (range 3.37 to 5.01 mm), with a regression of Y = 2.71 + 0.23X linking ova diameter (mm) to total length (inches). The correlation between ripe ova size and female length was 0.645.

**Spawning season**
- On [[Tomtit Run]], spawning began in late September and extended into November, with mean spawning-female length declining from 6.03 in (September 22 to October 14) to 5.57 in (October 28 to November 4).

**Fecundity**
- Egg number increased with length: about 18 eggs for a 3.6-inch female and about 213 eggs for a 7.7-inch female (Tomtit Run, 63 females, r = 0.87).
- Equation for Tomtit Run: log Y = −0.5361 + 3.23 log X (where X = total length in inches).
- Fecundity was also related to body weight (about 30 eggs at 13 g, about 200 at 70 g) and ovary weight.
- Egg number varied among streams: Bobb's Creek and Cherry Creek produced nearly twice as many eggs as Tomtit Run and Lingle Stream at equivalent length.
- Left and right ovaries did not differ significantly in egg number.

**Ova production per acre**
- Tomtit Run: 9,050 ova/acre. Lingle Stream: 13,620 ova/acre.
- Age-group contributions (Lingle/Tomtit context): age I about 9%, age II about 54%, age III about 37%.
- These values are low compared with [[Lawrence Creek]] (28,300 to 82,500 ova/acre) but within the range for the [[Pigeon River]] (2,200 to 26,800 ova/acre).

**Atresia**
- Only a small fraction of the recruitment stock matured. In most wild populations losses to atresia likely exceed 50%, and in the small mature females of this study probably exceed 50% as well.

**Life tables and population equilibrium**
- Ecological life tables were built for Tomtit Run and Lingle Stream. Lingle Stream showed high ova production with low first- and second-year survival and higher survival later. Tomtit Run showed lower production with higher early survival and a sharp decline in year 3.
- Both populations reached a similar number of mature fish and reproductive rates sufficient for equilibrium (Table IX). The authors conclude that environmental control acts through multiple concurrent parameters.

**Conclusions**
- Brook trout are capable of maturing at the end of their first year, but slow growth in these streams delays maturation and reduces fecundity.
- Interpretation of maturity from gonad appearance alone can be misleading. Unripe fish may be mistaken for immature ones, which may partly explain apparently delayed maturation elsewhere.
