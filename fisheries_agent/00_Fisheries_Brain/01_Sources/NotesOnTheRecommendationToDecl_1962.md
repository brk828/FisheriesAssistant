## Executive Summary

This 1962/1963 special report by Howard M. Bassett addresses a fisheries management crisis on [[Oak Creek]], Arizona, driven by rapidly increasing human population and recreational fishing pressure. The core issue is the unsustainable demand for hatchery-reared catchable-sized [[Rainbow Trout]] required to maintain "satisfactory" fishing rates on a limited stream resource (under 40 surface acres). The report uses three years of creel census data (1959, 1961, 1962) to demonstrate declining fisherman success despite increasing stocking numbers, quantifies the severe financial loss associated with stocking Oak Creek, and evaluates a 1962 pilot "artificial lure only" zone as evidence for a proposed seasonal (May 1–Labor Day) artificial-lure-only restriction across the entire creek. The stated goals are to reduce fishing pressure, more equitably distribute catch success throughout the week, and curb runaway hatchery stocking commitments, while explicitly rejecting several alternative management strategies (bag limit reduction, reduced planting size/frequency, split-stream zoning, closed periods after stocking).

## Background & Description

This report does not describe individual specimen morphology. Relevant biological/management-size data include:

- **Target stocking class**: "Catchable sized" rainbow trout — the standard hatchery-reared size class released for put-and-take recreational fishery management.
- **Egg sourcing constraint**: Arizona imports all trout eggs from external sources, and egg arrival timing constrains the department's ability to control final stocked fish size, limiting attempts to reduce average planted trout size as a cost-saving measure.
- No length, weight, or age-class data for wild or stocked individuals are provided in the source text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: [[Oak Creek]] is a narrow montane freshwater stream system within Oak Creek Canyon, Arizona, comprising fewer than 40 surface acres of trout water accessible to the public — a comparatively small habitat under disproportionately intense fishing pressure.
- **Population management context**: The fishery is not sustained by natural [[Trout Reproduction]] but almost entirely by put-and-take hatchery stocking of catchable-sized rainbow trout, a classic artificial "catchable trout program" designed to bridge gaps between natural production and angler demand.
- **Comparative habitat contrast**: [[Ashurst Lake]] (~200+ surface acres, ~60 miles from Oak Creek) is cited as a contrasting habitat where stocked trout can be provided far more economically (a "small fraction of a cent" per fish vs. at least $0.30 per fish at Oak Creek) and where additional fishing pressure could be more sustainably absorbed.
- **Anthropogenic pressures**: Rapid Arizona population growth, increased leisure time, and canyon congestion (private landowner conflicts, U.S. Forest Service waste/facility burdens) form the anthropogenic backdrop degrading habitat quality and the "quality outdoor recreation" experience (referencing [[Aldo Leopold]]'s framework).
- **Behavioral ecology of stocked fish**: Data show stocked trout exhibit a temporally patterned vulnerability to angling — catch rates peak sharply in the 1–2 days post-stocking and decline thereafter, indicating rapid depletion/habituation dynamics of hatchery fish following release, with movement between "artificial lure only" and "any lure" zones observed within the stream (fish and anglers both crossed the boundary in the 1962 pilot zone).

## Key Findings

### Fishing Pressure & Stocking Trends (Creel Census Data)

| Year | Fishing Trips | Catch Rate (fish/man-hour) | Catchable Trout Planted |
|------|--------------|----------------------------|--------------------------|
| 1959 | 60,000 | 0.57 | 129,000 |
| 1961 | 92,000 | 0.34 | 117,600 |
| 1962 | 83,000 | 0.43 | 139,400 |
| 1963 (est. need) | — | 0.50 (target) | ~170,000 (needed); ~160,000+ estimated to hit 0.50 target |

- The commonly accepted minimum catch rate for "good fishing" in a catchable-trout put-and-take fishery is **0.50 fish per man-hour**.
- Despite planting ~140,000 catchable trout in 1962, this failed to meet satisfactory catch rates, and hatchery capacity cannot realistically scale to meet projected 1963 demand of ~170,000 fish.
- Competing hatchery demands from newly developed waters ([[Lynx Lake]] near Prescott, [[Kaibab Lake]] near Williams, and future [[Blue Ridge Lake]] on East Clear Creek) further strain the state hatchery system's capacity to serve Oak Creek.

### Economic Findings

- Estimated annual financial loss from rearing/stocking Oak Creek: **~$40,000/year**, rising proportionally with any increase in stocking.
- Estimated annual license revenue attributable to Oak Creek fishing: **<$20,000/year**.
- Estimated annual cost of providing fish to Oak Creek: **~$60,000/year** (excluding enforcement/administration costs).
- Oak Creek receives nearly **1 in 3** of the state's catchable trout despite hosting **less than 1 in 10** statewide trout fishing trips.
- Cost per stocked fish: Oak Creek ≥ $0.30/fish vs. a "small fraction of a cent" per fish at Ashurst Lake — illustrating extreme relative inefficiency of small-stream catchable-trout stocking.

### Weekly Catch Distribution Pattern (Pre-Restriction)

- Across all three census years, catch rates on the day of and day after stocking (typically Friday stocking → Friday/Saturday fishing) were consistently **~2x** higher than during the following five days.
- Little significant variation in catch rate was found among the latter five days themselves.
- A 1959 experiment with Thursday stocking shifted the "good fishing" window earlier, disadvantaging weekend anglers — reinforcing Friday as the optimal stocking day for balancing weekday/weekend angler access.

### 1962 "Artificial Lure Only" Pilot Zone Results (1.1-mile section)

| Metric | Artificial Lure Area | Any Lure Area |
|---|---|---|
| Fishing pressure (relative reduction) | −38% (vs. projected unrestricted) | baseline |
| Overall success (fish/man-hour) | 0.466 | 0.430 |
| First 2 days success | 0.51 | 0.63 |
| Next 5 days success | 0.40 | 0.26 |
| Drop in success (2-day → 5-day period) | −18% (−59% in 1962 alone) | −50% average |
| % juvenile (unlicensed) anglers | 24.6% | 28.1% |

- Fishing pressure was substantially reduced in the lure-only zone without a statistically significant drop in overall angler success.
- Success was distributed far more equitably across the week in the lure-only zone, directly addressing the "feast or famine" post-stocking pattern.
- Juvenile participation was **not meaningfully reduced** by the artificial lure restriction, addressing a key equity concern in policy design.
- Confounding factors (easy angler and fish movement between zones due to small pilot area size) limit direct extrapolation of magnitude, but directional inferences were considered robust.

### Management Recommendation

- Recommend declaring the entire Oak Creek trout fishery **"artificial lure only"** from **May 1 through Labor Day** (~4-month peak season), on a **one-year trial basis** (with anticipation that two years of data may be needed for fair evaluation due to a "novelty effect" of first-year adopters).
- Rationale: uniquely capable among considered options of simultaneously (1) reducing fishing pressure, (2) improving weekly equity of catch success, and (3) arresting runaway demand for catchable trout — without the drawbacks of alternative measures.

### Alternative Measures Considered and Rejected

- **Reduce fish planted**: Would only reduce pressure by making fishing unacceptably poor; not a proactive solution.
- **Reduce bag limit**: Unenforceable unless applied statewide; unnecessary given current lake fisheries status.
- **Reduce size of planted trout**: Partially helps economics but not congestion; constrained by egg-supply logistics (all eggs imported, hatchery scheduling conflicts with fingerling programs).
- **Plant twice weekly**: Increases cost without extending duration of good fishing (splits same total fish across two smaller plantings).
- **Close stream post-planting**: Cited external studies show no effect on ultimate catch — fish are caught at the same rate once reopened; also reduces total "fishing days" available.
- **Change planting day from Friday**: Disadvantages either weekday or weekend anglers; Friday planting was empirically found (1959) to best balance both groups.
- **Split stream (half lure-only, half any-lure)**: Rejected as concentrating all bait anglers into half the stream would worsen congestion in that half.

### Concluding Risk Assessment

- The report's author identifies the primary risk of the artificial-lure-only policy as being **insufficiently drastic** rather than overly restrictive, given that more severe alternative measures exist but each individually cannot deliver the full suite of benefits achievable via the lure restriction.
