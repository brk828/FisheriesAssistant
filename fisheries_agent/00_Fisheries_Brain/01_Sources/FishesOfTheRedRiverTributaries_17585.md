# Fishes of the Red River Tributaries in North Dakota

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 study by [[Fredrick A. Copes]] and [[Richard A. Tubb]], published by the [[Institute for Ecological Studies]] at the University of North Dakota, documents the fish fauna of eastern North Dakota tributaries to the [[Red River]]. Fish were collected at 90 stations on 11 tributaries during spring, summer and fall 1964. The survey recorded 63 species in 14 families. The Cyprinidae were the best represented family, with 24 species. A secondary aim was to compare water chemistry between the [[Forest River]] (North Dakota) and the [[Snake River]] (Minnesota) to explain why Minnesota streams appear to hold more diverse fish faunas. The authors framed the work as a baseline before the planned diversion of [[Missouri River]] water into the Red River system.

## Background & Description

- **Prior work:** The earliest survey was by [[Woolman]] (1896), followed by [[Hankinson]] (1929), checklists by Carufel (1958) and Dotson (1964), and stream-specific studies by Wilson (1950), Feldman (1963) and Tubb et al. (1966). Neighboring Minnesota, South Dakota and Manitoba faunas were better documented (e.g., [[Eddy and Surber]] 1947; [[Underhill]] 1957).
- **Study rationale:** Record original distributional data for eastern North Dakota and identify gross differences in water quality or habitat that influence fish distribution.
- **Methods:** Collections used minnow seines, fyke nets, minnow traps, gill nets and, where access was difficult, fish poisons. Nomenclature followed [[American Fisheries Society]] standards (Bailey et al. 1960). Specimens were deposited in the University of North Dakota Museum.
- **Physical setting:** Tributaries arise as intermittent streams on the [[Glacial Till Plain]]. Most are under 100 miles long; the Sheyenne and Pembina are longer. Watersheds are intensively cultivated (small grains on the till plain; potatoes and sugar beets in the [[Red River Valley]]), producing heavy silt loads near stream mouths. Low-water dams are common; only Baldhill and Lisbon dams (Sheyenne), M-4 (Tongue) and Homme (Park) dams presented physical barriers to upstream fish migration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water chemistry (Forest vs. Snake River, June–September 1965):** Bicarbonate (as CaCO3) varied widely without a consistent pattern, likely reflecting differing photosynthetic uptake of CO2. Turbidity (Secchi readings) increased downstream and was of similar magnitude in both streams.
- **Chlorides:** Forest River chlorides were strikingly higher downstream, ranging from 722 to 963 mg/liter at station A-1, always above any other station on either stream. Chlorides rose during low runoff, suggesting release from a saline aquifer; the [[Dakota Sandstone]] subcrop lies between stations A-2 and A-1. Lower Park, Turtle, Goose and Sheyenne rivers also showed elevated chlorides.
- **Pollution and disturbance:** Some stations were judged polluted (e.g., Park River North Branch, Baldhill Creek near Hannaford, Wild Rice River near Cayuga). Potato and sugar beet processing effluent enters lower reaches only seasonally; minimally treated sewage from small towns was considered more consequential.
- **Winter fish kills** are frequent in shallow upstream pools, and several streams stop flowing upstream in dry periods.

## Key Findings

- **Species richness:** 63 species from 14 families across 90 stations; at least 32 species showed a preference for one or more environmental factors.
- **Dominant species:** The 10 most abundant and widespread species (Fathead Minnow, Common White Sucker, Black Bullhead, Brook Stickleback, Common Shiner, Creek Chub, Northern Pike, Johnny Darter, Carp, Blacksided Darter) made up over 98% of the catch.
- **Most widespread:** [[Fathead Minnow]] occurred at 85% of stations; [[Common White Sucker]] at 70%; [[Black Bullhead]] at 55%; [[Brook Stickleback]] at 47%; [[Common Shiner]] at 46%.
- **Richness pattern:** Species number increased downstream from headwaters, indicating that elevated chloride near tributary mouths does not act as a complete barrier.
- **Chloride-limited species:** Fewer than 6 specimens were found of Hybopsis storeriana, Notropis rubellus, Hiodon tergisus, Percina caprodes and Umbra limi, which may reflect the high chloride levels.
- **Notable records:** Notropis rubellus ([[Rosyface Shiner]]) was a first North Dakota tributary record (3 specimens). Esox masquinongy was stocked in Lake Ashtabula but is not naturally reproducing. A [[Smallmouth Bass]] population was reported self-sustaining above Lisbon (per personal communication).
- **Introduced and managed species:** Rainbow trout and brown trout persist only through stocking in reservoirs; rainbow trout summerkilled at stations 9 and 62 in July 1964. [[White Bass]] was introduced to Lake Ashtabula in 1952 and maintains itself through natural reproduction. Golden shiner populations were largely eradicated by the state.
- **Absent or uncollected species:** Several Minnesota-reported species were not collected (16 listed in Table 2, including Acipenser fulvescens, Lepisosteus osseus and Cottus bairdi). Some absences may reflect sampling timing: Campostoma anomalum was abundant in early spring but absent in July–August, and no winter collections were made.
- **Hybridization:** Only one group of sunfish hybrids (Lepomis humilis × gibbosus, 12 specimens) was recorded.
- **Conservation caveat:** The authors note that selective poisoning and stocking to support sport fisheries may have eliminated native species such as Notropis heterolepis. The study is described as a preliminary, not definitive, inventory.
- **Forward outlook:** The planned Missouri River diversion was expected to alter the Red River fish fauna, making this baseline valuable.
