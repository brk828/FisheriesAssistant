# Genetics, Metapopulations, and Ecosystem Management of Fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by [[David Policansky]] and [[John J. Magnuson]] (Ecological Applications, 1998 Special Issue), argues that [[Genetic Diversity]] deserves far more weight in [[Ecosystem Management]] of fisheries than it usually receives. The authors use [[Pacific Salmon]] of the Pacific Northwest as a case study. Salmon declines, they note, were attributed in the [[Upstream Report]] (NRC 1996a) to many interacting factors, and that report placed special emphasis on maintaining the diversity and genetic structure of salmon populations. The paper extends this reasoning to marine fisheries, in which mixed-stock harvest and selective effects can erode genetic variability.

## Background & Description

**Life history and structure.** Salmon are unusual because they are [[Anadromy|anadromous]], spawning in freshwater and maturing at sea, and they [[Homing Behavior|home]] to the streams where they hatched. Because stream networks are dendritic (tree-like), salmon populations tend to form a similar branching arrangement termed a [[Metapopulation]]. Within this structure, populations are linked by low levels of straying and by [[Local Adaptation]], which produces genetic differences in traits such as spawn timing, body size, and age at maturity.

**Policy context.** In 1992 the U.S. Congress funded a [[National Research Council]] (NRC) study of options for long-term survival of anadromous salmonids. The resulting report, *Upstream: Salmon and Society in the Pacific Northwest* (NRC 1996a), was one of several recent efforts, alongside the [[Independent Scientific Group]] (1996) and the [[Snake River Salmon Recovery Team]] (1994), to provide a scientific basis for salmon recovery.

**Authors and publication.** The authors are affiliated with the Board on Environmental Studies and Toxicology of the NRC and with the [[Center for Limnology]] at the [[University of Wisconsin-Madison]]. The manuscript was received 20 February 1996 and accepted in February 1997.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Human-caused loss of habitat.** Human activities have eliminated salmon metapopulation structure by removing [[Deme|demes]] (subpopulation units). Salmon have been eliminated from about 40% of their breeding habitat in Washington, Oregon, California, Idaho, and Montana over the past century. In the [[Columbia River]] watershed, dams have eliminated about one-third of habitat by blocking passage or flooding.

**Hatchery and introduction effects.** Hatchery outplanting has caused [[Hybridization]] between hatchery and wild stocks and, in some cases, outright replacement of wild populations. Non-native fishes can reduce genetic variability through hybridization, displacement, or predation. The authors note these effects are probably less significant for marine species, which have wider distributions and exposure to migrants from other marine areas.

**Selective pressures.** Fisheries and habitat change act as selective forces. Fishing tends to select larger fish, and [[Habitat Alteration]] can select for tolerance of higher water temperatures. Hatcheries exert [[Domestication Selection]], favoring traits that boost production at the expense of wild-type characteristics.

**Marine species.** Although marine species are generally less spatially structured than salmon, the authors cite [[Walleye Pollock]] (*Theragra chalcogramma*) in the [[Bering Sea]] as having distinct populations with considerable genetic differentiation (Bakkala 1993; Dawson 1994). This suggests that marine metapopulation structure may be more important than commonly recognized, though probably less fine-scale than in salmon.

## Key Findings

1. **Genetics is undervalued in fishery management.** The authors believe genetic considerations "have not generally been accorded enough priority" in practice, despite extensive discussion in the literature.

2. **Salmon are a model system for genetic conservation.** Their anadromy and homing behavior make them suitable for developing genetic conservation concepts. [[Waples]] (1991, 1995) developed the [[Evolutionarily Significant Unit]] (ESU) concept, which provides objective criteria for population distinctiveness under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The authors note that deciding where to set this threshold is a policy judgment as well as a scientific one.

3. **Mixed-stock fisheries are problematic.** Most fisheries harvest multiple populations, often through [[By-catch]]. Protecting a low-productivity population is difficult when it is mixed with a more productive one, because fishing mortality will probably continue to reduce the less productive stock. Larger, more productive populations also attract more fishers.

4. **Selective effects can be genetic.** Mixed-population fishing is selective when productivity differences among populations have a genetic basis. Traits such as [[Fecundity]], fertility, mating behavior, growth rate, and maximum size can be genetically influenced (Policansky 1993a). Any genetically based trait that wild stocks do not share with hatchery stocks will be selected against.

5. **Hatcheries have larger effects on anadromous species.** Hatchery and other nonfishing activities probably have fewer and smaller selective effects on fish that spend their entire lives in the ocean, especially those with pelagic eggs, than on freshwater or anadromous species.

6. **Ecosystem approaches must include genetics.** The authors argue that genetic structure and variability are integral aspects of marine ecosystems. Mixed-stock problems therefore should be considered in any [[Ecosystem-Based Fisheries Management]] framework. They hope that the Upstream report, by emphasizing genetics within an ecosystem context, can serve as a model for other fisheries.

7. **Fishing can cause evolution.** The authors cite [[Fishing-Induced Evolution]] (Policansky 1993a) and note that the selection fishing causes is "at least as important" as other factors, though less appreciated, citing studies with time scales of over 35 years (e.g., Ricker 1981).
