# Lower Colorado River Basin Management Plan for "Big-River" Fishes

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species #Colorado-River

## Executive Summary

This revised draft management plan, submitted by the [[Parker Fishery Resource Office]] to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Region II in November 1996, addresses conservation of four federally endangered "big-river" fishes in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]: [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). These species historically comprised more than half the fish fauna of the Colorado mainstem below [[Lees Ferry]]. The plan establishes a six-level framework from preventing extinction (Level I) through delisting (Level VI), emphasizing off-channel habitat development, genetic management, and ecosystem-level manipulations to enable recruitment success. The approach differs substantially from upper basin recovery plans due to the lower basin's more extensive modifications, longer time-frame of impacts, larger accessible populations, and extensive site-specific research base.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River Basin]] drains approximately 650,000 km² of western North America, divided administratively at [[Lees Ferry]] near [[Glen Canyon Dam]] into upper and lower basins. The lower basin encompasses the Colorado mainstem and the Paria, Little Colorado, Virgin, [[Bill Williams River|Bill Williams]], and [[Gila River]] subbasins across Arizona, California, Nevada, New Mexico, Sonora, and Baja California Norte. Originally inhabited by only 34 native fish taxa, the basin exhibits 62% endemism (21 of 34 taxa)—the highest percentage of any western North American watershed. Of these, two species have become extinct in the past century and 20 are federally listed as threatened or endangered.

The four "big-river" fishes evolved under extreme hydrologic conditions: torrential spring floods from Rocky Mountain snowmelt alternating with low summer-winter flows, high turbidities, and extreme temperature variations. All four species display powerful musculature, hydrodynamic body shapes, falcate fins, leathery skins, and high temperature tolerances. [[Colorado Squawfish]] was the only native piscine predator; the other three species exhibit adaptations suggesting coevolution as prey (high reproductive potential, rapid growth, predator-avoidance morphology).

Current status varies by species: [[Bonytail]] are infrequently caught in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]; the largest [[Humpback Chub]] population persists in [[Grand Canyon]], mostly in the lowermost [[Little Colorado River]]; the largest [[Razorback Sucker]] population remains in [[Lake Mohave]]; [[Colorado Squawfish]] are extirpated from the lower basin but reintroduced experimentally in [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat loss and modification coupled with negative interactions with non-native species drive the decline of these taxa. River control through dams blocks movement corridors, releases water too cold for spawning, and suppresses natural flow variance. The [[Gila River]] now exhibits long dry reaches due to upstream impoundments and groundwater mining. Predation on eggs, fry, and juveniles by non-native fishes (ictalurid catfishes, [[Striped Bass]], sport fish) is identified as a major biological factor in native species depletion.

River stabilization created two interrelated fatal effects: (1) physical environment changes and (2) biological changes favoring lake-adapted non-native species. Artificial habitats ranging from mainstem reservoirs to livestock-watering tanks, combined with sport-fish stocking, ensure constant sources of alien predators and competitors. Non-native fishes pose problems throughout the system, even in less-controlled river reaches.

The lower basin differs from the upper basin in having: (1) substantially longer time-frame of modifications with relative stabilization (most damsites used, major alterations completed); (2) larger, more accessible populations of three species; (3) extensive site-specific research base; and (4) recognition that great longevity of these fishes (decades to potential century-scale lifespans) allows extended time-frames for management actions but requires long-term commitment beyond typical 5-10 year planning cycles.

## Key Findings

**Management Framework**: The plan establishes six hierarchical levels:
- **Level I**: Prevent extinction (immediate priority for [[Bonytail]])
- **Level II**: Understand and acquire maximum genetic variability
- **Level III**: Stabilize population size, structure, and genetic variability
- **Level IV**: Reintroduce and re-establish additional stocks
- **Level V**: Promote self-sustaining status
- **Level VI**: Downlist to threatened or delist

**Species-Specific Status**:
- [[Bonytail]]: Enters at Level I (extinction prevention); genetic data partially known; requires immediate stabilization efforts
- [[Humpback Chub]]: Lower extinction danger; considerable genetic information available; stabilization and expansion underway
- [[Razorback Sucker]]: Apparent onset of [[Lake Mohave]] stock collapse raises Level I concerns; genetic data available; stabilization and expansion efforts active
- [[Colorado Squawfish]]: No wild fish in lower basin; reintroduced as "experimental, nonessential" in [[Salt River|Salt]] and [[Verde River|Verde]] rivers (plan recommends changing to "experimental, essential")

**Implementation Strategy**:

*Natural and Semi-Natural Habitats*:
- Construct or identify isolated habitats amenable to non-native species exclusion/eradication
- Initially focus on [[National Wildlife Refuge|National Wildlife Refuges]] ([[Bill Williams River NWR]], [[Cibola NWR]], [[Havasu NWR]], [[Imperial NWR]]) and other federal lands
- Stock year-classes; when fish reach ~300 mm TL (predation-resistant size), use for augmentation, translocation, or broodstock

*Hatchery Operations*:
- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH) serves as primary production facility
- Satellite facilities needed for grow-out, quarantine, disease treatment
- Annual fish health monitoring mandatory before site-to-site transfers

*Genetic Management* (Level II priorities):
1. Maximize founder numbers contributing to recruitment
2. For [[Razorback Sucker]]: collect wild larvae (positive phototactic response) across multiple years, times, and locations in [[Lake Mohave]]
3. Alternative: isolated bays with non-native removal, wild breeding fish, natural reproduction
4. If wild larvae unavailable: strict pedigree-based hatchery propagation
5. Broodstock management: maximal wild fish use, infusion of wild fish with F1 "domestic" stock, strict pedigree protocols to minimize inbreeding

*Habitat Development* (Levels III-V):
- Create large (≥80 ha) isolated habitats on [[National Wildlife Refuge|NWRs]] and federal lands
- Design to encourage natural reproduction and self-sustaining populations
- Manage in ecosystem framework including waterfowl, neotropical migratory birds, riparian taxa
- Expand within decade to non-federal lands, especially tributaries
- Exploit reservoir drawdowns (maintenance, drought) by stocking adults and larvae during refilling to capitalize on predator absence

*Habitat Maintenance*:
- Periodic renovation to control non-native fish infiltration
- Evaluate native/non-native interactions, especially large predators
- Replicate natural flood-created habitats (oxbows, backwaters) through dredging/excavation
- Merge fish management with wetland and riparian values management

**Program Coordination**:
- Establish lower-basin Coordinator position
- Form lower-basin Recovery Team (biologists with species/ecosystem expertise)
- Coordination Team with agency representatives
- Recovery Team to examine, revise, and integrate existing plans; provide biological information to [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service|USFWS]] Region II
- Maximum 50% of team from agencies affecting taxa
- Annual working sessions to: (1) consolidate best information through consensus; (2) determine broodstock acquisition/production protocols; (3) develop population stabilization methods
- Coordinator meets with [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service|USFWS]] administration minimum twice yearly

**Rationale**: Off-channel habitats offer highest probability of success with minimal disruption to current/future water use. The approach emphasizes recruitment problem resolution, exploiting availability of wild larvae ([[Lake Mohave]]), [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery|DNFH]] production capacity, suitable natural/semi-natural habitats, and dedicated expertise. All four species responded similarly to human interventions (became rare early 1950s through recruitment failure), allowing collective management approach. Experimental data indicate all four taxa can survive to maturity in habitats free of non-native fishes during early life stages.

**Critical Success Factors**:
- Long-term budgeting and operations commitment (results may take decades)
- Planning conforming to time scales of major water development projects
- Ecosystem-level manipulations enabling recruitment success
- Integration of piece-meal conservation efforts into consolidated program
- Coordination with upper basin efforts (genetic protocols, broodstock management)
- Flexibility and adaptability to species-specific, habitat-specific, and water-use situations
