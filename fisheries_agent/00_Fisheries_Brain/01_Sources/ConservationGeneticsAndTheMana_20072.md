# Conservation Genetics and the Management of Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-genetics

## Executive Summary

Gary K. Meffe's 1986 review in *Fisheries* (Vol. 11, No. 1) argues that [[Endangered Fishes]] management must incorporate [[Conservation Genetics]] from the outset of recovery programs. The paper is a primarily theoretical treatise. It identifies forces that erode genetic variation in small populations, including small size, [[Genetic Bottleneck]] events, [[Genetic Drift]], [[Inbreeding Depression]], artificial selection in captivity, and mixing of distinct stocks. It then proposes five management actions intended as first approximations for minimizing genetic deterioration. The author notes that the literature on endangered fish genetics is small, that the work is biased toward southwestern U.S. fishes, and that it ignores economic, political and social constraints.

## Background & Description

The paper opens with a quotation from Frankel (1983) stating that wild species need a pool of genetic diversity to survive environmental pressures beyond the limits of developmental plasticity. Meffe notes that the [[Endangered Species Act]] (passed 1973) prompted fisheries scientists to address declining populations of rare fishes. These fishes have been studied in natural settings and reared in captivity for restocking, yet the field is "new and still largely experimental." The text states there was only one hatchery facility for endangered fishes, the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, established in 1974.

The author states three caveats: the genetics literature on endangered fishes is small, so principles were drawn from other taxa and fields such as aquaculture and zoo care; 61% of U.S. fishes originally listed as endangered are endemic to the southwest (Johnson and Rinne 1982); and the paper addresses genetics only. The author describes the work as presenting a "best of all possible worlds" reference guide.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Management goals.** Three goals are identified (Soulé 1980): short-term viability and avoidance of extinction, maintenance of the capacity to adapt to changing environments, and maintenance of the capacity for continued speciation. The paper argues that genetic adaptation is the primary focus of conservation genetics and that conservation efforts must be planned on an evolutionary timescale.

**Central problem.** Loss of genetic variation erodes evolutionary flexibility, reducing the match between organism and environment. Total genetic variation has two components: variation within populations (demes), on which natural selection acts, and variation among populations. Both should be maximized.

**Effective population size (Ne).** Ne is the size of an idealized population with the same inbreeding or drift as the population under study. It is usually smaller than the census size (N) because of:
- **Sex ratio imbalance.** Ne = 4NmNf / (Nm + Nf). For a census of 100 fish, a 50:50 ratio gives Ne = 100, while 10 males and 90 females gives Ne = 36.
- **Unequal progeny distribution.** Disproportionate reproduction by a few individuals lowers Ne. In the worked example, one male producing 1001 offspring among 2000 offspring from 1000 females reduces Ne from 2000 to 238.
- **Population fluctuation.** Ne is the harmonic mean of population sizes across generations. A series of 100, 10, 300, 10, 80 fish (arithmetic mean 100) gives Ne = 22, a 78% reduction relative to a stable population of 100.

**Bottlenecks.** A bottleneck is a sudden, sharp decline in numbers. Retained quantitative variation after one bottleneck is approximately 1 − 1/(2N). Two individuals retain about 75% of quantitative variation and 10 individuals about 95%. Loss of rare alleles is more serious. In the worked example, a bottleneck of 10 causes an average loss of more than three of six alleles at a locus.

**Genetic drift.** Drift is a chronic bottleneck. Variance is retained at 60% in a population of 10 after 10 generations and 95% in a population of 100. Over 100 generations in a population of 100, retention drops to 60.6%, and over 1000 generations to 0.67%. In a hatchery stock of Montana west-slope cutthroat trout (*Salmo clarki lewisi*) derived from about 60 wild fish 14 years earlier, polymorphic loci (P) fell 57%, mean alleles per locus (Ma) 29%, and heterozygosity (H) 21% (Allendorf and Phelps 1980).

**Inbreeding depression.** Inbreeding increases homozygosity without directly changing overall variance. Fitness-related traits such as fecundity, fertility, growth and survivorship are most affected. Data from domesticated animals suggest a ΔF of 10% causes a 5–10% decline in individual reproductive traits and up to about 25% in total reproductive performance. Franklin (1980) and Soulé (1980) consider 1% inbreeding tolerable in the short term (Ne ≈ 50) and recommend populations above 500 for the long term. Documented fish examples include the convict cichlid, carp, zebrafish, brook trout and rainbow trout.

**Captive selection.** Captivity can produce domestication. Frankel and Soulé (1981) describe four types of selection: for increased productivity, for a type, for tractability, and non-selection of sick or abnormal individuals. Each can reduce variation or introduce maladaptive traits.

**Among-population variance.** Isolated populations diverge through selection and drift, which can produce a range of genetically distinct stocks for recovery. Mixing isolated stocks can break up [[Coadapted Gene Complexes]] and cause outbreeding depression. Convergent selection in common hatchery environments can erase between-stock differences.

**Case study: Sonoran topminnow.** Vrijenhoek et al. (1985) partitioned genetic variation in *Poeciliopsis occidentalis* as follows: 21% within localities, 26% between localities within drainages, and 53% between three major genetic groups equivalent to subspecies. The author concludes that representatives of all three groups must be preserved.

## Key Findings

- **Genetic management must start early.** The author argues that long-term conservation will likely fail if genetic aspects are ignored at the outset.
- **Ne is the key parameter.** Short-term survival requires Ne ≈ 50 (an inbreeding rate of about 1% per generation). Long-term health requires a minimum Ne of at least 500 (Franklin 1980), with an inbreeding coefficient of about 0.1%.
- **Recommended actions** (Table 1):
  1. Monitor genetics of field and captive populations, using non-lethal sampling such as fin, blood or mucus.
  2. Maintain the largest feasible Ne in captive stocks.
  3. Avoid inbreeding in small captive populations through selective mating, including the outcrossing scheme in Figure 5 (starting from three males and three females).
  4. Keep stocks in hatcheries for as short a time as possible, and periodically replace hatchery fish with wild fish.
  5. Maintain separate stocks of distinct populations to preserve among-population variance.
- **Proactive monitoring is needed.** Electrophoretic analysis of brood stocks should be done at founding and periodically thereafter.
- **Stock structure must be known.** The FAO/UNEP (1981) report is quoted: "A good knowledge of the population structure in the management of fisheries cannot be exaggerated."
- **Trade-offs with realities.** Budget, facility and population-size limits may prevent full adherence. The author argues that approximations are still better than none, and that recovery programs should not be abandoned because the guidelines cannot be fully met.
- **Caveat from the Devil's Hole pupfish.** *Cyprinodon diabolis* has persisted for thousands of generations near several hundred individuals, which conflicts with classical inbreeding models. The author notes that the models are incomplete.
- **Example of extreme loss.** The entire world population of *Gambusia gaigei* once consisted of two males and one female (Hubbs and Brodrick 1963).
