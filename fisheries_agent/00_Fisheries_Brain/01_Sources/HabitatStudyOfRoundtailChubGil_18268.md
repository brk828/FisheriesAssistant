# Habitat Study of Roundtail Chub and Loach Minnow in Gila National Forest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #habitat-assessment

## Executive Summary

This habitat study, conducted by Ecology Audits, Inc. from April to October 1979, assessed the distribution and habitat requirements of two State of New Mexico endangered cyprinid species: [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta grahami*) within the [[Gila National Forest]] of New Mexico and one site in the [[Apache National Forest]] of Arizona. The study documented 120 man-days of field operations across the [[Gila River]], [[San Francisco River]], and [[Blue River]] drainages. Sampling was significantly impacted by December 1978 flooding and heavy spring 1979 runoff, which scattered fish populations and delayed spawning activities. Despite these challenges, both species were located in multiple tributaries, with distinct habitat preferences identified: loach minnows favored shallow (3-11 inches), swift riffles with algae-covered substrate, while roundtail chubs occupied deeper pools (20-33 inches) with slower flow. The study concluded that both river systems provide adequate habitat for species survival, provided flow regimes and water quality remain unaltered by impoundment.

## Background & Description

### Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)

The [[Loach Minnow]] is a diminutive cyprinid in a monotypic genus, with adults reaching approximately two inches in length. The body is elongated, subfusiform, and ventrally flattened. Males possess thicker builds than females. The mouth is small, downward-angled, without barbels. Body coloration is olive with darker blotches; white spots surround the dorsal fin base, caudal peduncle, and caudal fin base. A black spot often appears at the caudal fin base. Breeding males develop small tubercles on the head, pectoral fins, and caudal peduncle, with bright red to red-orange coloration at fin bases, mouth, lower head, and abdomen. Breeding females display yellowing of lower body and fins.

Historically, *Tiaroga* was endemic to much of the [[Gila River Basin]] in Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora, Mexico. First described by Girard in 1851 from specimens collected in the [[Rio San Pedro]], the species was periodically collected from that river between Benson and Fairbank, Arizona, up to 1950. Historical sites included: [[Salt River]], [[White River]], [[Eagle Creek]], and [[Aravaipa Creek]] in Arizona; [[Gila River]] in Arizona and New Mexico; and [[Rio San Pedro]] in Sonora, Mexico. By 1966, Barber and Minckley considered the species essentially extirpated from all streams except [[Aravaipa Creek]], the Gila River in New Mexico, and possibly the Rio San Pedro in Sonora.

### Roundtail Chub (*Gila robusta grahami*)

The [[Roundtail Chub]] is a larger cyprinid, with adults capable of reaching substantial size. Literature indicates the species is largely omnivorous, consuming algae, aquatic insects (particularly hellgramites), crayfish, and fish. The species exhibits spring to early summer spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Characteristics

All literature sources agree on loach minnow habitat requirements: the species is "found only on and near rocky riffles on which green algae are common." In [[Aravaipa Creek]], Barber and Minckley (1966) captured fish in areas with coarse gravel and small rubble bottoms, no more than 8 inches (20 cm) deep. Minckley (1965) collected *Tiaroga* from the Gila River near Cliff, New Mexico, in shallow (less than 6 inches) turbulent riffles with 50% bottom coverage by vegetation (*Zannichellia palustris*, *Potamogeton foliosus*, and *Cladophora* sp.). Water temperature ranged 52-65°F daily.

The loach minnow rests near the bottom, supporting itself on pectoral fins, watching for food items at the water surface. [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*) is usually found in association with *Tiaroga cobitis*, but remains at higher water levels.

### Water Chemistry and Physical Parameters

Water chemistry showed relatively uniform values across sampling sites:

**Loach Minnow capture sites:**
- Temperature: 16°-27°C (mean = 20.8°C, σ = 3.49)
- pH: 7.6-8.2 (mean = 7.8, σ = 0.21)
- Dissolved oxygen: 6.0-10.0 mg/l (mean = 8.2, σ = 1.11)
- Depth: 3-11 inches (mean = 6.6", σ = 2.75)
- Water velocity: 0.503-1.976 ft/sec (mean = 1.174, σ = 0.55)
- Discharge: 2.55-47.43 ft³/sec (mean = 14.89, σ = 15.34)

**Roundtail Chub capture sites:**
- Temperature: 15°-22°C (mean = 19.1°C, σ = 2.33)
- pH: 7.2-8.0 (mean = 7.6, σ = 0.25)
- Dissolved oxygen: 5.0-10.0 mg/l (mean = 7.7, σ = 1.70)
- Depth: 20-33 inches (mean = 28.4", σ = 7.04)
- Water velocity: 0.959-1.691 ft/sec (mean = 0.683, σ = 0.51)
- Discharge: 1.47-29.17 ft³/sec (mean = 12.28, σ = 10.31)

### Elevation Limits

Elevation appeared as a limiting parameter. No *Tiaroga* specimens were captured above 7,100 feet elevation, and no *Gila* above 7,500 feet. This may reflect physical barriers such as waterfalls or physiological constraints.

### Food Habits

Loach minnow stomach contents included plecopteran (stonefly) nymphs, baetid mayfly nymphs, simuliid dipterans, and algae. Most collected specimens had empty or unidentifiable stomach contents. Minckley (1973) stated that *Tiaroga* feeds extensively on aquatic insect larvae, with diet shifting based on prey availability.

Roundtail chubs proved largely omnivorous. Stomach contents included: algae (often filling stomachs), hellgramites (most common insect), crayfish, and Dipteran larvae. No fish were found in stomach contents, though chubs were readily caught using small spinners and speckled dace as bait.

### Reproductive Biology

**Loach Minnow:** Literature indicates spawning occurs in late winter and early spring. However, two gravid females were collected June 19-20, 1979, containing 93 and 173 ova respectively. Ova were approximately 0.5 mm diameter, well-developed, and probably near spawn. These late dates likely resulted from high runoff holding water temperatures below normal during spring. Fish do not spawn until the second summer of life; females develop only one egg set per season. Ova counts vary from 250-1,200 per female depending on size.

Young-of-the-year loach minnows predominated in October collections from smaller streams such as [[Dry Blue Creek]] and upper [[San Francisco River]], suggesting these areas serve as spawning grounds and/or refuges for juveniles.

**Roundtail Chub:** Literature indicates spawning occurs in spring and early summer. Gravid females were captured May 8-10 from [[Taylor Creek]] and [[Beaver Creek]] on the [[East Fork Gila River]], and June 21 from [[Harden Cienega Canyon]] off the San Francisco River. Ova counts from Beaver-Taylor area ranged 3,080-15,893 eggs, well-formed and 0.75-1.0 mm diameter. Young-of-the-year chubs collected in October were found in swifter, shallower waters than adults, contrasting with adult habitat preferences.

### Impact of 1978-1979 Flooding

The December 1978 flood and heavy spring 1979 runoff significantly impacted fish distribution. Early spring sampling proved difficult and fruitless in headwater areas. As water receded, isolated individuals and populations were located, but populations of both species appeared declined and widely scattered through mid-summer. Spawning activities were delayed compared to historical data, attributed to prolonged runoff delaying water temperature rise. October sampling revealed predominance of young-of-the-year for both species, indicating good population recovery.

### Competitive Interactions and Threats

Introduction of exotic and game species into the [[Gila River Basin]] poses major threats. The [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) introduction resulted in competitive displacement of *Tiaroga*. Stocking of game fish reduced native species populations, including loach minnow. Concurrent increases in [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) populations accompanied *Tiaroga* declines in the Gila River. Mechanisms of competitive displacement remain unestablished.

Additional threats include habitat deterioration or elimination through excessive silt load, reduced or eliminated flow from dams and irrigation projects, and climatic drying.

## Key Findings

### Distribution

**Present Range - Loach Minnow:**
- Gila River system: from near Red Rock (southern Gila National Forest) north to confluence of East, Middle, and West Forks
- Southernmost capture: near Bill Evans Lake (northern edge of southern Gila National Forest segment)
- Northernmost capture: near confluence of three Gila River forks
- San Francisco River drainage: from San Francisco Hot Springs (south of Glenwood) north to approximately 6 miles upriver from Reserve (near Bill Lewis Springs)
- Also captured in [[Tularosa River]] (2 miles upstream from San Francisco River confluence) and Dry Blue Creek (Apache National Forest, 9 road miles south of Highway 180)

**Present Range - Roundtail Chub:**
- Gila River system: from near Turkey Creek confluence north to [[Beaver Creek]] and Taylor Creek (headwaters of East Fork)
- Collected 8 miles upstream from Gila Visitor's Center on Middle Fork, 6 miles upstream from [[Gila Cliff Dwellings]] on West Fork
- Mike Hatch (New Mexico Game and Fish Department) collected specimen near Red Rock in summer 1979
- San Francisco drainage: only in Harden Cienega Canyon (historical records exist from near San Francisco Hot Springs and Reserve, but no specimens collected in this study)

### Habitat Requirements Summary

The two most significant factors affecting distribution were **water depth** and **instream flow**. Loach minnows preferred fast-moving, shallow, riffling water; roundtail chubs occupied deeper pools with slow-moving water. Streamside vegetation showed great variation between capture points and did not appear significant. Elevation, water flow, and food availability may represent the most limiting factors.

### Population Status

Despite temporary scattering and isolation from 1978-1979 flooding, both species demonstrated strong population recovery by October 1979, with predominance of young-of-the-year indicating successful reproduction. The Gila and San Francisco Rivers and tributaries afford adequate habitat for survival and production of both species, provided river systems remain unimpounded and existing habitat unaltered.

### Management Implications

Careful management techniques are necessary to prevent total extirpation of *Tiaroga cobitis*. Key recommendations include:
- Maintain natural flow regimes; avoid impoundment
- Preserve existing habitat quality (substrate, algal growth, riffle-pool structure)
- Control introduction of exotic and game species
- Protect smaller tributary streams serving as spawning grounds and juvenile refuges
- Monitor instream flow requirements: loach minnow requires mean velocity ~1.17 ft/sec; roundtail chub ~0.68 ft/sec

### Associated Species

Species commonly found with loach minnow and roundtail chub included: [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Gila Mountain Sucker]], [[Sonoran Sucker]], [[Spikedace]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[River Carpsucker]], and [[Carp]].
