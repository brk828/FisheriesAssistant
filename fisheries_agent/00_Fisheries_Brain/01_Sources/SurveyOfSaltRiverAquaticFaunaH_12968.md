## Executive Summary

This 1981 inter-office memo from the Arizona Game and Fish Department documents a baseline fish community survey of the Salt River between Horseshoe Bend and Roosevelt Lake, Arizona, conducted May 27–29, 1981. The survey was undertaken as a pre-reintroduction assessment ahead of planned stocking of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). A cooperative team from AGFD, USFWS, and the U.S. Forest Service used gill nets (set overnight in pools) and seine hauls (~10 m, at randomly selected sites) to characterize the existing fish assemblage. The survey documented a fish community strongly dominated by exotic species, particularly the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), which comprised the large majority of individuals collected. Native catostomids (Gila sucker, Gila mountain/desert sucker) and the [[Roundtail Chub]] were also present but in far lower numbers. A key management concern raised was the recent (October 1980) discovery of flathead catfish at the Pinal Creek/Salt River confluence — a predatory, non-native species with potential to jeopardize razorback sucker reintroduction success.

## Background & Description

The memo is a field survey record rather than a morphological study, so detailed anatomical descriptions are not provided. However, taxonomic identifications and common-name associations were recorded for each species collected:

- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*) — native cyprinid, occurring across all seine and gill-net sites.
- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*) — small native cyprinid, present at several seine sites.
- **[[Gila Sucker]]** (*Catostomus insignis*) — native catostomid, occurring in both adult and larval ("sucker larvae") forms, the latter suggesting active spawning in the survey reach.
- **Gila Mountain Sucker** (*Pantosteus clarki*, referred to in-text as "Gila mtn. sucker," now generally recognized as the [[Desert Sucker]]) — native catostomid captured in gill nets and some seine hauls.
- **[[Red Shiner]]** (*Notropis lutrensis*) — small-bodied, non-native cyprinid; overwhelmingly the most numerically abundant species in nearly every collection.
- **Fathead Minnow** (*Pimephales promelas*) — non-native cyprinid, present in low numbers.
- **Green Sunfish** (*Lepomis cyanellus*) — non-native centrarchid, low abundance.
- **Channel Catfish** (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and **Black Bullhead** (*Ameiurus melas*) — non-native ictalurids; catfish were notably absent from gill-net catches despite known presence, but were captured via seine and dip net.
- **Common Carp** (*Cyprinus carpio*) — non-native cyprinid captured only in gill nets.
- **Flathead Catfish** (*Pylodictis olivaris*, implied but not formally listed in the species table) — noted as newly documented in the drainage (October 1980, Pinal Creek confluence), not captured on this survey but flagged as ecologically significant due to its predatory habits.

"Sucker larvae" were recorded as a separate abundance category (not speciated), indicating recent native catostomid reproduction in the river reach.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** Desert mainstem river (Salt River) with pool-riffle morphology, sampled between Horseshoe Bend and the confluence area near Roosevelt Lake; also included a small tributary, Coon Creek, sampled via dip net.
- **Thermal regime:** Water temperatures during sampling ranged from 21–26°C across sites and dates (late May), consistent with a warm desert lotic system.
- **Sampling methods and micro-habitat implications:**
  - **Gill nets** (set overnight in pools) selectively captured larger-bodied fish — [[Roundtail Chub]], Gila mountain sucker, Gila sucker, carp, and green sunfish — but failed to capture catfish despite their known presence, suggesting gear bias or catfish avoidance behavior in pools.
  - **Seine hauls** (~10 m, run-riffle-margin habitat) captured overwhelmingly cyprinid-dominated assemblages, with [[Red Shiner]] numerically dominant at every station.
  - **Dip netting** in Coon Creek (a tributary) targeted black bullhead specifically, reflecting the species' preference for slower, off-channel tributary habitats.
- **Species interactions / community structure:** The native assemblage (*Gila robusta*, *Catostomus insignis*, *Pantosteus clarki*, *Agosia chrysogaster*) co-occurs with an exotic-dominated assemblage (*Notropis lutrensis*, *Pimephales promelas*, *Cyprinus carpio*, *Lepomis cyanellus*, *Ictalurus* spp.). The overwhelming numeric dominance of red shiner suggests strong competitive or trophic pressure on native cyprinids and young suckers sharing similar shallow-water nursery habitat.
- **Conservation relevance:** The survey was explicitly conducted as a baseline prior to reintroduction of the federally listed [[Razorback Sucker]]. The discovery of non-native, piscivorous **flathead catfish** in the drainage introduces a predation risk to stocked razorback suckers, a major management concern noted in the memo.

## Key Findings

- **65% of all fish collected during the survey were exotic (non-native) species**, with natives comprising only about 35%.
- **[[Red Shiner]]** was by far the most abundant species overall, totaling **1,177 individuals** across all sample sites — exceeding the combined total of all native sucker species.
- Native sucker total (*Catostomus insignis* + *Pantosteus clarki* + sucker larvae) was substantial in larval form (372 "sucker larvae" recorded), indicating active native spawning, but adult native sucker numbers (Gila sucker: ~46; Gila mountain sucker: ~5) were far outnumbered by exotics.
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** was the most abundant native adult fish species (216 individuals total across sites), found at nearly every seine and gill-net location.
- Catfish (channel catfish, black bullhead) were **absent from gill net catches** but were captured in seine hauls and dip nets, revealing a gear-dependent detection bias for benthic ictalurids.
- The **flathead catfish** (*Pylodictis olivaris*), first documented in the Salt River/Pinal Creek confluence area in October 1980, was **not captured during this survey** but was flagged as a serious potential threat to the success of the planned razorback sucker reintroduction due to its predatory ecology.
- Species-specific totals recorded across the survey:
  - Red shiner: 1,177
  - Roundtail chub: 216
  - Sucker larvae (unspeciated): 372
  - Gila sucker: 46 (approx., adult)
  - Longfin dace: 65 (approx.)
  - Channel catfish: 14
  - Green sunfish: 6
  - Fathead minnow: 6
  - Black bullhead: 4
  - Gila mountain sucker: 5
- This survey serves as an important **pre-reintroduction baseline** for evaluating future changes in the Salt River fish community following [[Razorback Sucker]] stocking efforts, and highlights the ecological challenge posed by an exotic-dominated fish assemblage in the Gila River Basin.
