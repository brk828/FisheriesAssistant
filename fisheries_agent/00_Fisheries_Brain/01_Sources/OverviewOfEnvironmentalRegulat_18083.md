# Environmental Regulation of the Domestic Copper Mining Industry

#environmental-law #mining-regulation #industrial-ecology #policy-analysis

## Executive Summary

This legal analysis examines the comprehensive federal and state environmental regulatory framework governing the domestic copper mining industry in the United States as of the early 1990s. The document focuses on major environmental statutes including the [[Resource Conservation and Recovery Act]] (RCRA), [[Comprehensive Environmental Response Compensation and Liability Act]] (CERCLA), [[Clean Water Act]], [[Clean Air Act]], and state groundwater protection programs, with particular emphasis on [[Arizona]]'s aquifer protection permit program. The author argues that escalating environmental compliance costs, estimated in the billions of dollars, place domestic copper producers at a severe competitive disadvantage relative to foreign operations facing minimal environmental regulation. The analysis details the complex regulatory history of mining waste classification, the expansion of hazardous waste definitions, and emerging requirements for storm water management, air toxics control, and groundwater protection that threaten the economic viability of domestic copper mining operations.

## Background & Description

The domestic copper mining industry faced unprecedented environmental regulatory pressure beginning in the 1970s. Federal and state laws evolved through three distinct phases: initial focus on air and water pollution prevention (1970s), toxic waste site cleanup (1980s), and comprehensive waste management regulation (1990s). The industry spent millions of dollars on compliance during the first two phases, with closures occurring in the copper smelter sector during the 1980s attributed partly to regulatory costs.

The [[Bevill Amendment]] to RCRA temporarily excluded certain mining wastes from hazardous waste regulation pending EPA studies. Mining wastes were categorized as extraction, beneficiation, or processing wastes, each subject to different regulatory treatment. EPA's 1985 Report to Congress examined extraction and beneficiation wastes but found insufficient evidence to classify them as hazardous, leading to development of a separate Subtitle D mining waste program rather than Subtitle C hazardous waste regulation.

The [[Eagle-Picher Industries v. EPA]] decision (759 F.2d 905, D.C. Cir. 1985) significantly expanded CERCLA liability for mining operations by ruling that mining wastes containing any substances listed under referenced environmental statutes qualified as "hazardous substances," even if the mining-specific Bevill Amendment exclusion applied under RCRA. This interpretation exposed mining operations to liability for releases of thirteen naturally occurring inorganics (antimony, arsenic, beryllium, cadmium, chromium, copper, lead, mercury, nickel, selenium, silver, thallium, zinc) and their compounds, as well as naturally occurring radionuclides—substances present in virtually all mining wastes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The regulatory framework addresses multiple environmental pathways and receptors affected by mining operations:

**Groundwater Quality**: [[Arizona]]'s Environmental Quality Act established an aquifer protection permit program requiring demonstration of [[Best Available Demonstrated Control Technology]] (BADCT) and compliance with drinking water standards at designated points of compliance. All aquifers are presumed to be drinking water aquifers unless reclassified through a difficult process. Primary drinking water numeric standards under the federal [[Safe Drinking Water Act]] serve as aquifer water quality standards.

**Surface Water Quality**: The [[Clean Water Act]] regulates point source discharges to "waters of the United States," broadly interpreted to include non-navigable tributaries, intermittent streambeds, normally dry arroyos, gullies, ditches, and wetlands. Mining operations in desert regions far from substantial rivers face NPDES permit requirements if overburden placement or waste pile erosion creates discernible channels conveying pollutants during precipitation events. Water quality standards often require compliance with aquatic and wildlife criteria (e.g., 0.5 mg/L copper) even in ephemeral desert drainages lacking aquatic life.

Storm water discharges received specific attention in 1987 CWA amendments. Congress exempted precipitation runoff not contaminated by contact with overburden, raw materials, intermediate products, finished products, byproducts, or waste products. EPA adopted a "contact test" in 1990, but discharges "associated with industrial activity" require NPDES permits covering industrial plant yards, access roads, material handling sites, refuse sites, process wastewater application areas, storage areas, and areas where past industrial activity left significant exposed materials.

**Air Quality**: The [[Clean Air Act]] establishes [[National Ambient Air Quality Standards]] (NAAQS) for criteria pollutants including sulfur dioxide and particulate matter. Primary copper smelters face particular challenges with sulfur dioxide standards (80 µg/m³ annual arithmetic mean; 365 µg/m³ maximum 24-hour concentration). EPA consideration of more stringent one-hour standards could require expensive retrofitting at smelters already achieving approximately 90% sulfur dioxide removal.

Particulate matter regulation shifted from total suspended particulates (TSP) to PM-10 (particles ≤10 micrometers) in 1987, with standards of 150 µg/m³ (24-hour) and 50 µg/m³ (annual arithmetic mean). EPA advanced notice of rulemaking addressed potential secondary standards for fine particles <2.5 micrometers to address visibility impairment.

The 1990 CAA Amendments established a comprehensive air toxics program listing 189 hazardous air pollutants requiring [[Maximum Available Control Technology]] (MACT) at major sources (≥10 tons/year single pollutant or ≥25 tons/year combined). Preliminary EPA lists included primary copper smelters, secondary copper operations, zinc smelting, mineral beneficiation operations, ore flotation, solvent extraction processes, and mineral dryers/calciners.

## Key Findings

**RCRA Waste Management Complexity**: Mining wastes fall into multiple regulatory categories: (1) wastes subject to Subtitle C hazardous waste regulation (spent solvents, mineral processing wastes exhibiting hazardous characteristics); (2) wastes subject to future Subtitle D mining waste program (extraction and beneficiation wastes from 1985 Report, 18 of 20 conditionally-retained processing wastes, non-Bevill processing wastes without hazardous characteristics); (3) potentially unregulated wastes (non-mining wastes not classified as hazardous, extraction/beneficiation wastes not studied in 1985 Report, excluded mining wastes, exploration wastes).

EPA's September 1, 1989 rule established "high volume" and "low hazard" criteria for mineral processing wastes eligible for Bevill Amendment exclusion. Five mineral processing wastes were retained within the exclusion pending completion of studies. On June 13, 1991, EPA determined Subtitle C regulation inappropriate for all 20 conditionally-retained wastes, proposing to address 18 under Subtitle D and two (phosphogypsum, phosphoric acid process wastewater) under the [[Toxic Substances Control Act]].

**Subtitle C Hazardous Waste Requirements**: Generators producing >1000 kg/month hazardous waste may accumulate waste on-site in containers or tanks for ≤90 days without permits prior to off-site transportation. Requirements include EPA identification numbers, manifests, pre-transport compliance (packaging, labeling, marking, placarding), recordkeeping, biennial/annual reports, personnel training, proper container/tank management, and contingency plans.

On-site treatment, storage, or disposal facilities require hazardous waste permits unless using exempt methods (elementary neutralization, totally enclosed treatment, certain wastewater treatment plants, exempt recycling). Permitted TSD facilities must meet minimum technology requirements: two or more liners, leachate collection systems above and between liners, groundwater monitoring. EPA ruled that RCRA § 3004(x) relaxation provisions do not apply to mineral processing wastes outside the Bevill Amendment exclusion.

TSD facilities must establish financial assurance for closure, post-closure care, liability coverage (sudden and non-sudden accidental occurrences), and corrective actions. Corrective action applies to all releases from any solid waste management unit at TSD facilities regardless of waste placement timing, including units receiving only Bevill-excluded wastes ([[American Iron and Steel Institute v. EPA]], 886 F.2d 309, D.C. Cir. 1989).

The "derived-from" rule classifies any solid waste from treatment, storage, or disposal of listed hazardous waste (sludge, spill residue, ash, emission control dust, leachate) as hazardous waste. The "mixture" rule treats any mixture of solid waste and listed hazardous wastes as listed hazardous waste, regardless of whether resulting material exhibits hazardous characteristics. The D.C. Circuit upheld extension of the derived-from rule to leachate from wastes not considered hazardous when disposed and application of the mixture rule to contaminated soil/groundwater under the "contained-in rule" ([[Chemical Waste Management Inc. v. EPA]], 869 F.2d 1526, D.C. Cir. 1989).

Land disposal restrictions prohibit placement of untreated hazardous waste in landfills, surface impoundments, waste piles, or injection wells. Wastes must be treated to meet EPA waste-specific treatment standards. RCRA does not apply retroactively to wastes finally disposed before effective dates of hazardous waste regulations unless solid waste disposal units are "actively managed." However, EPA interprets leachates and residues as subject to land disposal restrictions if wastes are actively managed after effective dates of underlying waste prohibitions.

**Subtitle D Mining Waste Program Development**: EPA's "Strawman Process" began in June 1988 with Strawman I, receiving criticism for essentially applying hazardous waste regulations despite the determination not to do so. Strawman II (May 1990) proposed regulating new and existing facilities containing regulated mining materials (excluding closed/abandoned units), covering extraction and beneficiation wastes from the 1985 Report, non-hazardous mineral processing wastes, and certain non-waste "regulated materials."

Strawman II technical criteria would establish performance goals for constituents in groundwater, surface water, air, and soils, with design and operating criteria to meet goals. Additional requirements include monitoring and verification, corrective action, closure and post-closure care, and financial responsibility. EPA postponed spring 1991 proposed rule publication, forming a Policy Dialogue Committee with industry, state government, EPA, and public interest representatives. EPA estimates proposed rule publication in 1992 or 1993.

**CERCLA Liability Expansion**: The [[Eagle-Picher]] decision rejected mining industry arguments that the Bevill Amendment parenthetical exclusion applied to the entire CERCLA "hazardous substance" definition. The court ruled mining wastes qualify as hazardous substances if they contain substances listed under any referenced environmental statute, and mining sites could be regulated for releases of "pollutants or contaminants" even without imminent and substantial danger findings at the NPL listing stage.

This interpretation exposes mining operations to liability for releases of naturally occurring substances and natural resource damages. Mining necessarily results in exploitation (loss) of natural resources, creating inherent liability exposure. The [[Superfund Amendments and Reauthorization Act]] (SARA) 1986 provided limited protection, exempting EPA removal/remedial actions for naturally occurring substances "in unaltered form, or altered solely through naturally occurring processes or phenomena, from a location where it is naturally found." This protects undisturbed mineralized deposits but not man-disturbed deposits.

EPA's December 14, 1990 [[Hazard Ranking System]] revisions assess relative risk by examining quantity, toxicity, and concentration of hazardous constituents in "special wastes" (including mining wastes), extent and potential for environmental release, and degree of risk to human health and environment.

**Economic Impact Projections**: According to the [[Bureau of Mines]] Methodology to Measure the Economic Impact of Copper Mining and Mineral Processing Waste Regulations (May 1990), waste management regulations could impose billions of dollars of compliance costs on the domestic copper mining industry. Two domestic mines were estimated to have 1991 operating costs above product prices. Six other mines had projected 1991 costs within 5-10 cents of forecasted prices, placing them at competitive disadvantage with foreign markets and threatening their existence.

**State Groundwater Regulation**: [[Arizona]]'s aquifer protection permit program exemplifies increasing state regulation. The program prohibits discharges without individual or general permits. "Discharge" means adding pollutants to aquifers directly or to land surface/vadose zone with reasonable probability of reaching aquifers. "Pollutant" includes any liquid, solid, gaseous, or hazardous substance. "Aquifer" means any geologic unit containing sufficient saturated permeable material to yield usable water quantities to wells or springs.

Individual permits require demonstrating facilities are designed, constructed, and operated to ensure greatest discharge reduction achievable through BADCT, including where practicable technology prohibiting all pollutant discharge. BADCT determination considers site-specific hydrologic and geologic factors, water conservation/augmentation opportunities, industry-wide economic impacts of alternative technologies, presence of acutely toxic or carcinogenic organic substances, and whether facilities are existing or new.

Applicants must show discharged pollutants will not cause or contribute to aquifer water quality standard violations at applicable points of compliance or further degrade already-violated aquifers. Permits must prescribe monitoring, recordkeeping, reporting, contingency plans, discharge limitations, compliance schedules, post-closure plans, and alert levels triggering permit condition adjustments or contingency plan actions.

**Surface Water Regulatory Expansion**: Broad interpretations of "waters of the United States" require NPDES permits for mining operations in desert regions. Dumping overburden into gullies or arroyos constitutes discharge of pollutants (rock) from point sources (trucks, railcars) into waters of the United States (gullies, arroyos) if linked to river/stream drainage systems or containing intermittent water. Erosion from spoil piles creating discharges via ditches, gullies, or similar conveyances constitutes point sources even if miners only collected rock and materials ([[Sierra Club v. Abston Construction Co.]], 620 F.2d 41, 5th Cir. 1980).

NPDES permittees must comply with water quality standards prescribing maximum pollutant levels in receiving waters. [[Arizona]] applies aquatic and wildlife standards (0.5 mg/L copper) to virtually every river system, requiring compliance even for discharges to dry desert arroyos lacking aquatic life or wildlife.

Storm water discharge regulation expanded significantly. The 1987 CWA amendments allowed mining operations to divert storm water runoff around operations and discharge downstream without NPDES permits if not contaminated by contact with overburden, raw materials, intermediate products, finished products, byproducts, or waste products. EPA's November 16, 1990 "contact test" triggers permit applications only if storm water contacts these materials.

Storm water "associated with industrial activity" requires NPDES permits covering industrial plant yards, immediate access roads and rail lines, material handling sites, refuse sites, process wastewater application/disposal sites, material handling equipment storage/maintenance sites, residual treatment/storage/disposal sites, shipping/receiving areas, manufacturing buildings, raw material/intermediate/finished product storage areas (including tank farms), and areas where past industrial activity left significant exposed materials. Office buildings and parking lots are excluded unless storm water combines with drainage from industrial activity areas.

Individual storm water permit applications were due November 18, 1991, with EPA proposing extension to May 18, 1992. EPA published draft general permits for storm water discharges associated with industrial activity on August 16, 1991, covering all new and existing qualifying discharges.

**Air Quality Regulatory Intensification**: Sulfur dioxide NAAQS (80 µg/m³ annual arithmetic mean; 365 µg/m³ maximum 24-hour concentration; 1300 µg/m³ maximum 3-hour secondary standard) already required several hundred-million dollar smelter reconstructions. EPA consideration of one-hour standards for asthmatic protection and acid rain concerns could require additional expensive retrofitting at facilities already eliminating approximately 90% of sulfur dioxide emissions. Additional incremental controls for one-hour standards would be most costly relative to sulfur dioxide amounts controlled.

The 1990 CAA Amendments established time frames and requirements for PM-10 NAAQS implementation (150 µg/m³ 24-hour, 50 µg/m³ annual arithmetic mean), expected to have major impacts on mining, mineral processing, and smelting facilities. EPA advanced consideration of secondary welfare-based NAAQS for fine particles <2.5 micrometers addressing visibility impairment.

**Hazardous Air Pollutants Program Expansion**: Pre-1990 NESHAP for inorganic arsenic (August 4, 1986) required most copper smelters to comply with sampling, analysis, recordkeeping, and reporting. Some smelters with higher inorganic arsenic levels in copper matte required secondary hood installation and work practice requirements. November 20, 1990 asbestos NESHAP amendments added inspection and notification requirements for demolition/renovation activities, requiring thorough facility inspections before commencing activities.

Title III of 1990 Amendments significantly increased air toxics regulatory authority. "Major sources" emitting or potentially emitting ≥10 tons/year of a hazardous air pollutant or ≥25 tons/year combined must obtain operating permits requiring MACT, residual health risk control, and accidental release rules compliance. The initial list contains 189 air pollutants.

EPA's preliminary draft list (June 21, 1991) of major and area hazardous air pollutant source categories included primary copper smelters, secondary copper operations, zinc smelting, primary metals-miscellaneous, secondary metals-miscellaneous, secondary metals process heaters, process heaters, mineral dryers/calciners, ore flotation, solvent extraction processes, mineral wool production, and refractories production.

Title V operating permits are required for: (1) major sources; (2) sources subject to air toxics standards; (3) sources subject to new source performance standards; (4) affected sources under acid rain provisions; (5) sources requiring preconstruction review under PSD or nonattainment area programs; (6) EPA-designated sources. Permits must incorporate all federally enforceable applicable CAA requirements, with permit compliance deemed CAA compliance.

**Industry Survival Concerns**: The domestic copper mining industry faces critical development decisions as production costs increase, ore grades drop, and environmental requirements become more demanding and complex. Foreign governments allowing mining industries to operate with minimal environmental regulation create enormous competitive disadvantages for domestic operations absorbing ever-increasing compliance costs. Industry survival in the 1990s requires development policies balancing economic, social-cultural, and environmental protection measures, acknowledging the importance of integrating environmental and economic considerations in decision-making through interaction between industry, government, non-governmental organizations, and the public, directed toward optimizing economic development while minimizing environmental degradation.
