# Historical Overview of the Genus Craterocephalus and Mammals of the Dalhousie Springs Area

#ichthyology #marine-biology #atherinidae #mammalogy

## Executive Summary

This extract comprises two chapters from a study of the Dalhousie Springs area in central Australia. Chapter 14 (Crowley & Ivantsoff) reviews the evolutionary history of the freshwater atherinid genus [[Craterocephalus]] (hardyheads) and compares the two hardyhead species at Dalhousie Springs. Chapter 15 (Smith) reports a mammal survey of the same area, including pitfall, baited-trap, predator-scat and mist-net methods. The text is truncated during the mammal results, so only two dunnart records are available. Both chapters stress the uniqueness of the springs and warn that water extraction, tourism and pollution threaten their fauna.

## Background & Description

**Chapter 14: Hardyheads (Craterocephalus)**

- Atherinids (silversides) are a worldwide family with marine and freshwater genera. Most are euryhaline, but the Australian genus [[Craterocephalus]] is mostly freshwater: all but three or four species are found only in fresh water.
- The authors propose that the ancestors of the genus entered inland waters when the mid-Cretaceous sea withdrew (about 95 million years ago), and that isolated populations then diverged.
- The oldest known atherinid fossils date to the mid-Eocene (about 45 million years ago) and are nearly identical to living northern-hemisphere species. This suggests slow morphological differentiation in the family.
- The authors estimate that hardyhead speciation took millions rather than thousands of years, in contrast to the rapid speciation of the [[Desert Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon) in Death Valley.
- Two Dalhousie species are compared: [[Craterocephalus Dalhousiensis]], endemic to the springs, and a second species resembling [[Craterocephalus Stercusmuscarum]] (subspecies C. s. stercusmuscarum and C. s. fulvus). The authors note that the second species' taxonomic status is unresolved.

**Chapter 15: Mammals**

- Central Australian mammals were summarised by Finlayson (1961). Strahan (1983) distribution maps suggest one monotreme, eleven marsupials, six native rodents, numerous bats, the dingo and seven introduced mammals could occur near Dalhousie Springs.
- The South Australian Museum holds collections of fifteen marsupial species, the dingo, and ten species each of bats and rodents from within 300 km of the springs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat range of hardyheads**

- Hardyhead habitats span Lake Kutubu in New Guinea (808 m above sea level) to Lake Eyre (10–14 m below sea level).
- They occupy acidic lakes on Fraser Island, hard springs at Dalhousie (up to 115 %o CaCO3 hardness, as given in the source), saline ephemeral creeks in the Lake Eyre drainage, and both permanently warm thermal springs and very cold central Australian winter nights.
- Many species have wide distributions. C. stercusmuscarum has the widest range. C. eyresii occurs across South Australia and the Murray-Darling system, and in the Goulburn-Hunter system of New South Wales, which has been separate from western-flowing streams since the mid-Miocene (about 12 million years ago).
- C. dalhousiensis has the narrowest range, restricted to the Dalhousie springs. The authors suggest it may be a relict population or a species that evolved rapidly through ecological pressure and isolation since the springs began in the Pleistocene.

**Feeding and food**

- Desert pupfish in thermal springs feed mainly on algae.
- Earlier work (Ivantsoff & Glover 1974) reported that Dalhousie hardyheads ate mostly gastropods. In 1985, the gut contents of 54 fish examined were mainly algae, blue-green algae and unidentified crustaceans. Only two fish contained gastropods and two contained snail eggs.
- The authors conclude that hardyheads are opportunistic rather than selective feeders. C. dalhousiensis was seen darting into water at a known lethal temperature to feed on blue-green algae.

**Predation and limiting factors**

- Unlike in Death Valley, where food shortage limits pupfish, Dalhousie fish do not appear to be short of food. Predation by large fish and water birds, plus natural wastage, appear to be the main limits on populations.

**Threats**

- Pupfish populations in America have been lost through drainage, resort use and introduced predators. At least eight populations were lost from the early 1940s, about half of them after 1970 (Brown 1971).
- Possible threats at Dalhousie include falling artesian water levels, unregulated bore flow, irrigation, pollution from detergents, soaps, plastics and food refuse, and tourist camping. Discharge from the springs is known to have declined since they first became active (Smith, this report).

**Chapter 15 survey sites**

- Pitfall traps were set in a line 4.3 km east of the main Dalhousie Spring. Most lay in partly stabilised windblown sand dune, and seven lay in a damp loamy area with samphires.
- Predator scats came from twelve areas within 5 km of the main spring, plus one at the Dalhousie ruin. Most scats were probably from dingoes.

## Key Findings

**Chapter 14: Hardyheads**

- Hardyhead speciation is estimated to span millions of years. The authors argue that the constancy of small morphological differences rules out recent speciation for this group.
- Meristic counts show C. stercusmuscarum (Dalhousie) is closer to C. dalhousiensis, while morphometric proportions fall midway between C. dalhousiensis and C. s. fulvus.
- Osteology shows the Dalhousie pair differ in the upper mouthparts and several other bones. Table 2 gives these differences; for example, the premaxilla has 3–4 rows of strong teeth in the Dalhousie pair versus 1–2 rows of small teeth in the C. s. subspecies pair.
- Biochemical analyses are needed to decide whether the second species is a C. stercusmuscarum subspecies, a variant of C. dalhousiensis, or a hybrid resulting from flood-borne C. s. fulvus entering the springs. The authors note the Finke River records and flood history make entry in the last two million years possible.
- Breeding is not fully known. A mature male caught in November had sperm, and a mature female caught in August had no ripe oocytes. In May–June 1985, larvae under 8.0 mm total length were found at several sites, and the size range (larvae to 61.1 mm standard length adults) suggests year-round breeding at Dalhousie. Elsewhere, C. s. fulvus breeds from September to October in south-eastern Queensland.
- The authors recommend that some pools and outflows at Dalhousie be closed to tourists but kept open to scientists to study ongoing development and degradation.

**Chapter 15: Mammal Survey**

- Methods: 25 pitfalls (45 cm metal cylinders with 30 cm fly-wire barriers) run for five nights; 125 baited collapsible live traps (peanut butter, rolled oats and honey) run for four days; predator scat analysis (dried, oven-sterilised at 80 °C, then broken up in water); two mist nets run for two nights near a pool at Spring Cat.
- [[Fat-tailed Dunnart]] (Sminthopsis crassicaudata): one juvenile male captured in a pitfall in the sand dune.
- [[Stripe-faced Dunnart]] (Sminthopsis macroura): two dentaries from one predator scat were identified by a high entoconid on the third molar. Tooth wear showed they came from different individuals. Two maxillae from another scat were attributed to a Sminthopsis species that could be S. macroura.
- The mammal results are cut off in the source, so the survey totals are not available.
