# Delaware Fresh Water Fisheries Survey (1950)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Delaware Board of Game and Fish Commissioners publication (Fisheries Publication No. 1, 1952) reports a 1950 summer survey of nine warm-water impoundments in Delaware. Crews combined fish sampling, a creel census of anglers, age determination from scales, bottom fauna and plankton sampling, water chemistry, and contour and vegetation mapping. The authors conclude that each pond is a separate biological problem with its own productivity limit, and that management must be tailored to each water body. The main game species are [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Chain Pickerel]], while [[Bluegill]], [[Yellow Perch]], and [[Black Crappie]] are the main panfish. The report stresses stable water levels, watershed soil conservation, careful stocking, and the sportsman's cooperation.

## Background & Description

The survey was financed by state funds and carried out by a four-man summer crew working in two units, one in northern and one in south-central Delaware. The work was planned by [[Lyle M. Thorpe]] of the Connecticut State Board of Fisheries and Game, with scale-reading training from the [[Solomons Island Research Laboratory]] in Maryland. Water samples were analyzed by the Water Pollution Commission of Delaware. The Foreword, written by Director of Conservation Norman G. Wilder, states that rising outdoor recreation demand justified a scientific basis for management.

The check list is based on Maryland Eastern Shore collections, published Delaware records, and standard ichthyological works, and includes only species actually taken or seen by the crews. It lists **27 species** in total. Those most relevant to the ponds include:

- **Game fish:** [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*, present in all ponds surveyed), [[Chain Pickerel]] (*Esox niger*), and [[Redfin Pickerel]] (*Esox americanus*, uncommon).
- **Panfish:** [[Bluegill]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Redbelly Sunfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Blue-spotted Sunfish]] (one specimen), [[Black-banded Sunfish]] (rare, one pond), [[Black Crappie]], [[White Crappie]], and [[Yellow Perch]].
- **Forage and other species:** [[Golden Shiner]], [[Tricolor Shiner]], [[Eastern Mosquitofish]], [[Eastern Banded Killifish]], [[White Perch]], [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Common Carp]], [[American Eel]], and several catfish and bullheads.

The report cautions that the bass and pickerel are "the most attractive drawing card" for anglers, and that stocking has often been haphazard.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors describe food chains in which plankton support insects, crustaceans, and bottom fauna, which in turn support fish. Plankton volumes were reported in cubic centimeters per cubic meter of water, but the authors say these figures should carry little weight because daily variation is so large. Bottom samples were taken with an [[Ekman Dredge]] (one haul = 36 square inches). Insects, molluscs, and oligochaete worms were counted.

Key environmental factors noted across the ponds include:

- **Water-level fluctuation:** Several impoundments dropped by feet within short periods. The report says such drops could wipe out a year's production of species that spawned at that depth.
- **Siltation:** Periodic dredging of the Chesapeake and Delaware Canal deposits silt in [[Lum's Pond]]. Heavy silt could smother eggs, destroy vegetation, and in extreme cases wipe out fish.
- **Vegetation:** Waterweed (*Anacharis*) choked [[Haven Lake]] in midsummer, while [[Noxontown Pond]] had a spring clogging by *Potamogeton*. The authors argue vegetation control should be weighed against public benefit and that chemicals are hazardous to fish.
- **Pollution:** No pond was judged harmful at the time. Concerns included cabin sewage, cannery waste, and trash dumps.
- **Parasites:** Yellow grub cysts (via herons and snails), black and white grubs, and red roundworm were common, making many fish unattractive or inedible. Parasitism was described as an unsolved problem.

## Key Findings

**Methods and scope**
- The creel census was the most intensive part of the survey. Fish were weighed, measured, and aged from scales, and stomachs were examined.
- The survey covered nine ponds: [[Lum's Pond]], [[State Pond]], [[Noxontown Pond]], [[Red Mill Pond]], [[Haven Lake]], [[Blair's Pond]], [[Waple's Pond]], [[Cedar Creek Pond]], and [[Silver Lake]] (Milford).

**Combined creel census (June 25 to August 31, 1950)**
- 507 parties, 976 people, and 3,684 hours fished.
- 1,183 fish removed. Largemouth bass 282 (23.83%), chain pickerel 255 (21.55%), bluegill 254 (21.47%), common sunfish 149 (12.59%), crappie 74 (6.25%), yellow perch 13 (1.09%), other species 56 (4.73%).
- Overall catch rate was 0.321 fish per man-hour.
- Total weight of fish in the survey was 668.4 pounds. By weight, largemouth bass made up 57.27%, chain pickerel 17.64%, bluegill 9.23%, yellow perch 7.48%, crappie 6.90%, and common sunfish 1.47%.
- The authors state that management should aim to increase the weight of desirable fish per hour, not just the number.

**Pond-level results**
- [[Noxontown Pond]] had the heaviest fishing: 175 parties, 1,424 hours, and 246 fish removed.
- [[Lum's Pond]] had large bass, but few fish under 1.5 pounds were caught, which may point to poor recent bass reproduction.
- [[Haven Lake]] had the highest catch per hour among the larger samples (1.605 fish per successful hour), with many out-of-state anglers.

**Growth, stunting, and forage**
- Average growth curves were prepared for six Delaware fishes (1950).
- [[Golden Shiner]] was judged the most important forage fish. It matures in one to two years and can reach 8 to 9 inches in its first full year.
- Stunting in overpopulated [[Yellow Perch]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Pumpkinseed]] stocks was a recurring concern, with small, humped-back fish as the visible sign.
- [[Common Carp]] were judged detrimental. Their feeding uproots vegetation and muddies the water. A Maryland study cited an estimated productivity of about 500 pounds per acre, of which about 450 pounds were carp.

**Management recommendations**
- Raise the minimum legal size for [[Largemouth Bass]] to 12 inches and for [[Chain Pickerel]] to 14 inches. This was recommended for several ponds.
- Stabilize water levels by repairing dams and building permanent control structures.
- Protect bass spawning areas during April, May, and June.
- Control watershed siltation through soil conservation.
- Stock only under a planned program. Do not introduce brackish or salt water species without prior study. The white perch was considered likely to over-populate.
- Study the effects of carp and turtles on fish productivity.
- Encourage panfishing through incentives such as prizes for record bluegills and crappies.
- Maintain the [[Creel Census]] every few years to measure the effects of management.

**Authors' conclusions**
- No two ponds are alike, so each needs its own plan.
- Sportsman understanding of regulations is essential to their success.
