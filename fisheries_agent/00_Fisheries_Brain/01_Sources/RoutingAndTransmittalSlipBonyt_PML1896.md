# Routing and Transmittal Slip: Bonytail, Dexter NFH

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 Colorado Division of Wildlife newsletter article, reproduced on an OPTIONAL FORM 41 routing and transmittal slip, covers three linked topics: the recovery of endangered [[Bonytail]] and other "big river" fishes in Colorado, the radio tracking of [[Colorado Squawfish]], and a 1985 capture and disease-treatment operation for [[Rocky Mountain Bighorn Sheep]]. The bonytail material is the core. A bonytail found in Colorado waters was the first of its kind recorded there in twenty years, and a planned reintroduction is being weighed against unknown genetic and ecological risks. The bighorn sheep item is terrestrial and only loosely connected to the aquatic theme.

## Background & Description

**Bonytail.** The article reports that the bonytail had declined sharply. Citing the 1983 bulletin *Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin* by Robert Behnke and Del Benson (Colorado State University Cooperative Extension), the article notes that the species' disappearance is so rapid that it would be "almost beyond belief" without the example of the [[Passenger Pigeon]].

**Big river fish survey.** The [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] set out to determine the distribution, abundance, and habitat needs of all four endangered big river fishes in the state. Since 1978, researchers have sampled hundreds of miles of the [[Yampa River]], [[White River]], and [[Colorado River]]. They have inventoried nursery habitats and handled and identified "literally thousands" of larval fish.

**Funding and tracking.** The project is funded in part by the [[Colorado Nongame Income Tax Checkoff]], through which taxpayers donate to protect nongame species. It has also involved implanting small radio transmitters into Colorado squawfish, allowing biologists to monitor fish movements through [[Radio Telemetry]].

**Bighorn sheep operation.** On Jackass Flats near Ouray, wildlife biologist Jim Olterman of the Division of Wildlife used a net-capture method to take 39 Rocky Mountain bighorn sheep. The animals were injected with [[Oxytetracycline]] for pneumonia and given [[Fenbendazole]], an oral treatment for lungworm. The stated goal was to prevent a die-off from [[Pneumonia]]. The article cites the last state die-off, in the herd at [[Waterton Canyon]] near Denver, as the reason for the precaution.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Bonytail status:** The article describes a remnant population of older bonytail in [[Lake Mojave]] on the California-Arizona border and in the [[Lake Havasu]] area. Spawning efforts there have been unsuccessful.
- **Stocking:** Thousands of bonytail fingerlings were recently stocked in a California reservoir, where the effort is still being assessed.
- **Source stock:** The reintroduction stock came from a captive population at a New Mexico federal hatchery.
- **Risk assessment:** Biologists say they must first understand how reintroduction could affect any wild populations and learn more about the genetics of the fish being proposed for release. Learning more about habitat requirements is also needed before reintroduction can proceed.
- **Recovery coordination:** The [[Colorado River Fish Recovery Team]] is helping coordinate efforts to spare the species from extinction.
- **Sheep health:** The bighorn capture was a preventive response to pneumonia and lungworm risk in a herd whose neighboring population had previously suffered a die-off.

## Key Findings

1. A bonytail found in Colorado waters was the first of its kind discovered there in twenty years. It was tagged, measured, and released unharmed.
2. Division of Wildlife research since 1978 has covered hundreds of miles of three major Colorado rivers and identified thousands of larval fish, with the goal of mapping the distribution, abundance, and habitat needs of four endangered big river fishes.
3. Radio transmitters implanted in Colorado squawfish allow direct monitoring of their movements.
4. Bonytail reintroduction is planned, but managers say the effect on any wild populations and the genetic background of the stock must be understood first.
5. Spawning efforts with the remnant bonytail population at Lake Mojave and Lake Havasu have been unsuccessful, and recently stocked fingerlings in a California reservoir are being evaluated.
6. The article presents the bonytail's decline as a serious conservation case, and the Colorado River Fish Recovery Team is involved in recovery work.
7. A preventive operation captured and treated 39 Rocky Mountain bighorn sheep on Jackass Flats for pneumonia and lungworm, aiming to avert a die-off like the one previously recorded in the Waterton Canyon herd.
