# Initial Discoveries of Fish Faunas on Seamounts and Offshore Banks in the Eastern Pacific

#ichthyology #marine-biology #seamount-ecology #Triglidae #Sebastidae #Scombridae #Pleuronectidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 paper by Carl L. Hubbs presents some of the earliest documented records of fish faunas inhabiting isolated [[Seamounts]], [[Guyots]], and offshore banks across the eastern Pacific, from the Gulf of Alaska to far off Chile. Hubbs frames the work around fundamental zoogeographic questions: how species colonize isolated submarine rises, whether these features act as "stepping stones" across the vast eastern Pacific faunal barrier separating Indo-Pacific and American fish faunas, and whether elevated productivity around seamounts (possibly via current-induced [[Upwelling]]) supports commercially exploitable fisheries. Key case studies include a significant range extension of the deep-sea sole [[Embassichthys bathybius]] on Pratt Seamount, taxonomic clarification of a supposed new rockfish species (actually juvenile [[Sebastodes ruberrimus]]) from Cobb Seamount, a newly discovered and rapidly exploited tuna fishery on Stranger ("Hurricane") Bank, and the discovery of the Indo-Pacific/Australasian sea robin [[Pterygotrigla picta]] on an unnamed guyot 800 miles off Chile — a major biogeographic extension supporting the "stepping stone" hypothesis for antitropical Indo-Pacific taxa.

## Background & Description

The paper responds to the mid-20th-century discovery of numerous submerged mountains in the Pacific Ocean (following Hess 1946; Menard and Dietz 1951; Menard 1955), distinguishing between:
- **Banks**: submerged rises reaching depths of less than 100 fathoms
- **Seamounts/Guyots**: flat-topped or peaked submarine mountains with minimum depths greater than 100 fathoms

Hubbs poses a structured set of research questions that anchor the study, including species composition and abundance on individual seamounts, dispersal mechanisms, the role of these features as biogeographic "stepping stones" across ocean barriers, the potential for relict fauna reflecting seamount subsidence history, degrees of isolation-driven speciation, fisheries potential, and the physico-chemical/biotic drivers (e.g., current-induced upwelling) of elevated productivity around these structures.

Four principal case studies are documented:

**1. Pratt Seamount (Gulf of Alaska)** — An adult specimen (242 mm SL) of the deep-sea sole [[Embassichthys bathybius]] was dredged at 510 fathoms on the seamount's slope (Lat. 56°20′N, Long. 142°30′W), ~210 nautical miles offshore. This represented a substantial northward and seaward range extension for a species previously known mainly from off southern and northern California and Washington.

**2. Cobb Seamount (off Washington)** — A rockfish originally described as the new species *Sebastodes bilineatus* (Welander and Alverson 1954) was re-examined by Hubbs and determined to be a striped juvenile of [[Sebastodes ruberrimus]] (the commercially important "red rockfish"), not a distinct species, though slight local differentiation was not ruled out.

**3. Stranger Bank / "Hurricane Bank"** (Lat. 16°52′N, Long. 117°30′W, ~200 nm SW of Clarion Island) — Discovered in 1957 by Adrian F. Richards (Scripps), this shallow bank (~15 fathoms) rapidly became a major yellowfin tuna ([[Neothunnus macropterus]]) fishery, yielding over 1,000 tons within a year (projected to exceed 2,000 tons), alongside skipjack tuna ([[Katsuwonus pelamis]]) and rainbow runners ([[Elegatis bipinnulatus]]). Associated fauna included the labrid [[Bodianus diplotaenius]], hammerhead sharks (*Sphyrna* sp.), wahoo ([[Acanthocybium solandri]]), and manta rays (presumed [[Manta hamiltoni]]). Tuna stomach content analysis revealed heavy predation on the spiny trunkfish [[Lactoria diaphana]] (487 specimens in one stomach sample) and juvenile triggerfish [[Xanthichthys lineopunctatus]].

**4. Unnamed Guyot off Chile** (Lat. 25°44′S, Long. 85°25′W, ~800 miles off Chile) — A juvenile specimen (54 mm SL) of the triglid [[Pterygotrigla picta]] was dredged at 129 fathoms during the IGY Downwind Expedition. This species was previously known only from Juan Fernández, New Zealand, and Australia — never from the American mainland — making this discovery a critical biogeographic link supporting dispersal via submarine ridges extending toward Easter Island.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Upwelling hypothesis**: Hubbs proposes that bottom topography of seamounts and banks disturbs deep current flow, inducing upwelling and nutrient enrichment of surface waters, thereby explaining elevated biological productivity and fish/squid aggregations detected via echograms around seamounts between California and Hawaii (per Henry W. Menard's observations).
- **Biogeographic barrier crossing**: The eastern Pacific is characterized by a broad deep-water barrier separating Indo-Pacific and American fish faunas (per Ekman 1953). Seamounts and banks are proposed as potential "stepping stones" facilitating faunal transgression across this barrier.
- **Antitropical Indo-Pacific distribution pattern**: [[Pterygotrigla picta]] and its relative [[Chelidonichthys kumu]] exemplify an "antitropical Indo-Pacific" distribution — occurring in temperate zones of both hemispheres of the Indo-Pacific/Australasian region but absent from continental Chile — contrasting with endemic American triglids (*Prionotus*, *Bellator*). This pattern is linked to submarine ridge topography: a deep trench lies off continental Chile, while numerous ridges and peaks rise between Juan Fernández/San Felix and westward toward Easter Island and New Zealand, potentially providing dispersal corridors.
- **Depth-driven relictism**: The paper raises (without resolving) the possibility that deep seamount faunas include relict populations that adapted to increasing depth as seamounts subsided or became flooded over geological time.
- **Trophic linkages**: Tuna stomach contents at Stranger Bank demonstrate a forage-fish assemblage (trunkfish, triggerfish, flying fish) sustaining a major pelagic tuna fishery, illustrating bank-associated productivity supporting higher trophic levels.

## Key Findings

- **Range extension**: [[Embassichthys bathybius]] documented far north (Gulf of Alaska) and far offshore (Pratt Seamount, 210 nm out) of its previously known southern California–Washington range, at 510 fathoms depth.
- **Taxonomic correction**: *Sebastodes bilineatus* Welander and Alverson, 1954, described from Cobb Seamount, is a junior synonym or misidentified juvenile color phase of [[Sebastodes ruberrimus]], not a valid new species.
- **New fishery discovery**: Stranger ("Hurricane") Bank, discovered 1957, produced >1,000 tons of yellowfin tuna within less than a year of exploitation (projected >2,000 tons, valued at >$500,000), demonstrating substantial and rapid commercial fisheries potential from newly found offshore banks.
- **Major biogeographic record**: [[Pterygotrigla picta]], previously known only from Juan Fernández, New Zealand, and Australia, was found on a guyot ~600 miles northwest of Juan Fernández and ~800 miles off continental Chile — the first American mainland-region record and strong support for seamount "stepping-stone" dispersal across the South Pacific.
- **Taxonomic/systematic revisions**: Hubbs argues for retaining *Pterygotrigla* (containing *P. picta* and *P. polyommata*) as distinct from the Japanese genus *Otohime*, based on differences in vomerine teeth, anal spine structure, and dorsal buckler size, despite superficial similarity. He also clarifies that *Trigla guttata* Philippi, 1896, is a synonym of *P. picta*, while a separately figured Juan Fernández gurnard (misattributed as *Trigla punctata*) is more likely [[Chelidonichthys kumu]].
- **Detailed meristic and morphological data** for the Chilean guyot specimen of *P. picta*: dorsal spines VIII (vs. VI in type), dorsal soft rays 12, anal rays 11, pectoral rays 15 (formula i, 9, ii+3), caudal rays 6+5=11; elongated nuchal spine and double preopercular spine attributed to juvenile stage.
- **Call to action**: Hubbs emphasizes the need for systematic, comprehensive faunal surveys of seamounts and banks, citing the ongoing Revillagigedo fish fauna study (Boyd W. Walker) as a model, and highlights untapped scientific value regarding zoogeography, speciation, and fisheries potential of these isolated features.
