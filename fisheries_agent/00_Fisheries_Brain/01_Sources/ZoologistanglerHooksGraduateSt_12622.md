# ASU Insight: Zoologist W.L. Minckley, Native Arizona Fishes, and Campus News (Sept. 23, 1991)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This Arizona State University (ASU) newsletter issue profiles [[W.L. Minckley]], an ASU zoologist and fish specialist who mentors graduate students through intensive field training. It reports his comments that only 30 of the 80 fish species in Arizona waters are native, nearly all of them on endangered lists, and that the [[Colorado River]] south of [[Lake Mohave]] has no native fish left. The issue also covers an Arizona Board of Regents budget approval for ASU, a pilot beer-sales plan at [[Sun Devil Stadium]], a jazz concert, an architecture lecture, and a school-age child-care conference. Only the Minckley profile contains ecological data.

## Background & Description

The lead article, by Steve Koppes, describes Minckley's career and teaching philosophy:

- **Field-based teaching:** Minckley believes students should get into the field as much as possible. His view is that "if you can't smell it and feel it and lick it and taste it, it's not going to work."
- **Early training:** He grew up in Ottawa, Kansas, where he fished for catfish, crappie and bass. He earned an undergraduate degree in wildlife and fisheries biology at [[Kansas State University]], then did graduate study at the universities of Kansas and Louisville.
- **Arrival at ASU:** He became an assistant professor at ASU in 1963, a few years after the teachers college gained full-university status. He described ASU's "proximity to a diversity of environments" as ideal for biological instruction and research.
- **Recognition:** In May 1991 the Graduate College and the ASU Foundation gave him a $1,500 Outstanding Teacher of Graduate Students Award.
- **Breadth:** Former student [[John Rinne]] recalled that Minckley was "a biologist first and then a specialist," knowledgeable about birds, mammals, reptiles and amphibians as well as fishes. Minckley taught students to see how different life forms fit together within ecosystems.
- **Collaboration:** Minckley and Rinne were writing a general-audience book on the fishes of the Southwest. Minckley was also co-editing *Battle Against Extinction: Native Fish Management in the American West*, which includes papers by five former ASU students.

The profile also covers the growth of ASU and its students. Minckley has seen enrollment rise from 15,400 to more than 43,000 during his 28 years at the university. He credits students with much of the university's intellectual growth: "They do a lot of the work and publish a lot of the papers."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Minckley says human impacts make it harder to find undisturbed nature, even in remote areas. He once studied fishes of the Colorado River, but has had to move into its tributaries to find native Arizona species: "That's where they are, that's where they're left. There aren't any in the main streams."

Key conservation statements from the article:

- **Fish diversity:** Of the 80 fish species in Arizona waters, only 30 are native.
- **Conservation status:** "All but about five" of the native species are on some kind of endangered list.
- **Colorado River:** The reach south of Lake Mohave is described as the only major river in North America that essentially has no native fish left.
- **Approach:** Minckley said that with increasing human impacts, he has "emphasized an ever-increasing conservation approach."

The article also describes how research methods have changed. Earlier researchers counted and measured organismal features and compiled data on hand calculators. Current methods include photographic and video imagery, [[Protein Electrophoresis]], and analysis of [[Mitochondrial DNA]].

## Key Findings

**1. Native fish decline in Arizona (as reported by Minckley)**
- 30 of 80 fish species in Arizona waters are native.
- Nearly all native species (all but about five) are listed as endangered in some form.
- The Colorado River south of Lake Mohave is described as having no remaining native fish.
- Remaining native populations are mainly in Colorado River tributaries.

**2. Mentorship and graduate training**
- Since 1963, Minckley has supervised almost 50 master's and doctoral degrees.
- Former students have held posts such as chief of biological research at the U.S. [[Bureau of Reclamation]] in Denver, director of the Pine Run Ecology Laboratory in Lewisburg, W.Va., and professorships at universities nationwide, including a former dean at [[SUNY Stony Brook]].
- Rinne, who received his doctorate in 1973 and has worked as far afield as [[Lake Victoria]] in Africa, now works for the [[U.S. Forest Service]] in Tempe, near Minckley's office in the Life Sciences Center.
- Minckley calls his former students his "accomplices in inquiry."

**3. Teaching philosophy**
- Field experience is central to training.
- Minckley says he has no distinct teaching style and prefers one-to-one interaction, adjusting his approach to each student's needs.

*Arizona Board of Regents budget (by George Cathcart)*
- The regents approved ASU's 1992-93 budget request on Sept. 13, forwarding $266.4 million for ASU and $42.4 million for ASU West to the governor's budget office.
- Requested increases are 11.2 percent for ASU and 35.48 percent for ASU West over the current year.
- The system request includes $19.4 million for salary increases (2.85 percent over the current salary base).
- The regents defeated, 6-2, regent Donald Pitt's amendment to delete a $623,200 item for planning ASU III, a proposed branch campus. ASU President Lattie Coor proposed the campus to serve the eastern Valley, accommodating a projected enrollment increase of up to 30,000 students by 2010, while capping Tempe enrollment.
- Other requests included $3.86 million and 70 positions for undergraduate education, $1.4 million for Engineering Excellence, $746,000 for economic development, $732,800 for extended education, and $307,300 for cultural diversity.
- The regents also approved, 6-2, an experimental plan to sell beer at Sun Devil Stadium during two October [[Phoenix Cardinals]] games. Beer must be bought and consumed on the lower concourse.

*Campus events*
- Grammy-nominated singer and pianist [[Judy Roberts]] and trumpeter Fred Forney open the Kerr Cultural Center Jazz Showcase Series on Oct. 1 at 7:30 p.m. General admission is $10.
- Manhattan architect [[Jack Travis]], a 1977 ASU graduate, will lecture on Oct. 3 at 6:30 p.m. He reports that of the 50,000 registered U.S. architects, only 771 are black. His forthcoming book covers 35 individuals in 22 firms.
