# Introgression among Apache, Cutthroat, and Rainbow Trout in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study used 10 diagnostic [[Isozyme Electrophoresis|isozyme]] loci to measure hybridization between [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*), [[Rainbow Trout]] (*O. mykiss*) and [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*O. clarki*) across the species' historical range in Arizona. The authors analysed 645 fish from 31 wild populations. Only 11 populations appeared unhybridized. Rainbow trout introgression was found in 19 of 31 populations, and at least two populations contained only hybrids. Backcrossing toward Apache trout was the dominant pattern. Because of pervasive hybridization, the genetic variability of the Apache trout genome could not be reliably estimated. The authors conclude that recovery will be complicated by variable introgression, habitat deterioration and barriers to rainbow trout immigration. They recommend strict prevention of further rainbow trout stocking, protection of the least-admixed populations, and a tiered management strategy for hybridized streams.

## Background & Description

**Species and history.** The Apache trout was known from 1873 and formally described by [[Miller 1972]]. It historically occupied most streams of the [[White Mountains]] of Arizona, including the upper [[Salt River]] (Black and White rivers), the [[San Francisco River]] (Blue River), and the headwaters of the [[Little Colorado River]]. Loss of habitat, overfishing and competition from introduced trout sharply reduced the species. By 1950 the only known populations were on the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]].

**Regulatory status.** Management milestones include:
- 1955: the White Mountain Apache Tribe closed all fishing for Apache trout.
- 1969: the species was listed as endangered.
- 1973: it came under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- 1974: it was downlisted to threatened, and all Arizona waters were closed to Apache trout fishing.
- Currently: it occupies fewer than 30 stream systems and impoundments.

The [[Arizona Trout Recovery Team]] (White Mountain Apache Tribe, Arizona Game and Fish Department, U.S. Forest Service and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) agreed that additional recovery steps are needed. It also agreed that Apache trout should, where feasible, regain dominance as the game fish in its historical waters.

**Introduced trout.** Many former Apache trout waters were stocked for more than 50 years with rainbow, cutthroat, [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) or [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*). Competition from brown and brook trout was identified as a major cause of decline (Rinne and Minckley 1985). Earlier biochemical work (Loudenslager et al. 1986) had already flagged localities with high hybrid proportions.

**Stocking history.**
- Cutthroat trout were reportedly moved into Apache trout streams by mule train in the late 1800s. Hatchery records show stocking from about 1920 to 1942.
- Rainbow trout were widely stocked from 1934 to 1954, and some stocking continues in marginal habitat outside the main protected range.
- Recovery stocking of "purportedly pure" Apache trout began in 1965. Some of these fish were later judged probable Apache–rainbow hybrids.
- Streams such as Ord Creek were renovated with the piscicide [[Antimycin A]] and restocked. Several others, including [[Hurricane Creek]], [[Bear Wallow Creek]] and [[KP Creek]], received fish from various source populations and hatcheries.

The authors note that the success of renovation and restocking had not been systematically evaluated.

**Objectives.** The study aimed to (1) increase the number of informative gene loci for detecting hybrids among the three species, (2) estimate the types and frequencies of hybrids across the range, and (3) draw management implications.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling.** Fish were collected from the Fort Apache Indian Reservation and the Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest by electrofishing and angling between fall 1987 and spring 1989. Sites were selected by the Arizona Trout Recovery Team as places where Apache trout could feasibly be collected. Reference samples comprised:
- 25 Apache trout from the [[Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]] (East Fork of the White River origin, the type locality)
- 25 Eagle Lake strain rainbow trout
- 28 Yellowstone strain cutthroat trout
- 29 Apache × rainbow F1 hybrids

Tissues were analysed by [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]] using buffer systems described by Morizot and Schmidt (1990).

**Habitat pressures.** The authors identify three factors that complicate recovery: introgression, habitat deterioration, and the variable effectiveness of natural barriers. Barriers to rainbow trout migration may be transient, so they may not fully prevent admixture.

**Hybrid classes.** Hybrids were assigned to cross types using expected Mendelian proportions at diagnostic loci. The categories were F1, F2, and backcrosses to each parent. Because only a small number of diagnostic loci were available, the study could not resolve hybrids beyond the second generation, so hybridization is likely underestimated.

## Key Findings

**Genetic markers.** Of roughly 55 locus products resolved, 10 were polymorphic at the 99% criterion. Several loci were fixed for different alleles between species. These differences made it possible to identify hybrids. Segregation in F1 hybrids was largely as expected, with two anomalies (CK-B* and PGM*) that were attributed to sampling error, shared alleles, or polymorphism in the rainbow trout reference strain. The authors note that shared alleles would lead to underestimation of rainbow trout contribution.

**Extent of hybridization.**
- Of 31 sampled localities, 11 had apparently pure Apache trout. Only 11 potentially unhybridized populations were found.
- Rainbow trout introgression was documented in 19 of 31 populations, including at least two in which every fish sampled was a hybrid.
- Cutthroat introgression occurred in four populations. Three-species individuals (carrying diagnostic alleles of all three species) were found at two localities. Apache–cutthroat hybrids occurred at one locality without any rainbow alleles.
- No pure rainbow or pure cutthroat trout were sampled from the wild.
- More than 60% of individuals sampled were interspecific hybrids.

**Hybrid composition.** Across the 645 wild fish (Table 3):
- 274 were classified as Apache trout.
- 371 were hybrid-derived individuals. The largest class was backcrosses to Apache trout (RA × A, N = 265). Other classes were RA × RA (N = 82), RA × R (N = 12), A × R × C (N = 2), AC × AC (N = 2), AC × A (N = 6), and R × A F1 (N = 2).
- Only 10 fish carried diagnostic cutthroat alleles, usually heterozygous at one locus. This points to several generations since the original hybridization.

**Directionality.** In 18 of the 19 rainbow-hybridized populations, backcrosses toward Apache trout far outnumbered backcrosses toward rainbow trout. The abstract reports this pattern as 19 of 20 hybridized populations. The exception was KP Creek, where backcrosses toward rainbow trout predominated. The authors attribute the directional bias mainly to stocking practices, including high age-specific mortality of stocked rainbow trout and infrequent stocking relative to native numbers.

**Population purity gradient.** Hybridized populations ranged from about 90% pure Apache trout in Sun and Moon Creeks to 0% in Snake Creek. Four populations were classed as Apache-predominant (>50% pure): [[Little Diamond Creek]], [[Sun and Moon Creeks]], [[Hughey Creek]] and [[Paddy Creek]]. Paddy Creek was about 50% hybrid.

**Genetic variability.**
- The hatchery reference stock showed no variability, apart from uncommon CK-B* variants.
- Within non-hybridized wild populations, rare variation occurred at PEPB-1*, PGK* and PGM*. The PEPB-1* variation probably reflected cutthroat contributions. This left three loci with detectable variability in Apache trout populations.
- Only one of seven alleles detected exclusively in hybridized populations, sMDH-B1,2*e, could be assigned with confidence, to rainbow trout. Attribution of the other alleles was limited by biased sample composition.

**Recommendations.**
1. Stop all further rainbow trout stocking into streams where Apache trout predominate.
2. Give highest priority to the 12 populations with no detected rainbow introgression. Increase sample sizes to at least 50 fish each and maintain barriers. Hatchery trout should not be introduced unless these populations become critically small.
3. Consider Crooked Creek, Deep Creek, Boggy Creek-1, Firebox Creek, Coyote Creek and the East Fork of the White River as donor sources for introductions or hatchery stock. Resample small-sample sites (Flash Creek, Elk Canyon Creek, Hurricane Creek, Soldier Creek, Loafer Creek) and Reservation Creek.
4. For the four Apache-predominant populations, maintain barriers and increase sampling. Resample Paddy Creek in five years to test a do-nothing approach.
5. For the 14 hybrid-predominant populations, use a tiered approach. Renovate and restock Paradise and Snake Creeks (no Apache trout sampled), one with hatchery fish and one with wild spawn for comparison. Leave at least two streams untreated to track rainbow allele loss. Consider renovation, "swamping" by Apache trout, or selective removal of hybrids, depending on habitat condition.
6. Establish parallel hatchery stocks to preserve variability. Three alleles absent from the current hatchery strain (CK-B*a from Coyote Creek, PGM*d from Deep Creek and PGK*a from Boggy Creek-1) could be introduced by hatchery crosses. The authors warn that inbreeding depression is a risk regardless of measured heterozygosity effects.

**Conclusion.** The authors argue that the scale of present-day introductions far exceeds early mule-train transfers, so recovery will require a comparably massive effort to prevent the extinction of Apache trout.
