# Limnological Study of Four Lakes near Rotorua

#limnology #freshwater-ecology #trout-fishery

## Executive Summary

This publication (Fisheries Research Publication No. 140, 1970) reports seven years of limnological observations, 1960–66, on four lakes near Rotorua, New Zealand: [[Lake Okataina]], [[Lake Rotoehu]], [[Lake Ngapouri]] and [[Lake Okaro]]. The study was undertaken as part of a trout population experiment. It documents water temperature regimes, [[Heat Budget]]s, [[Dissolved Oxygen]] dynamics, [[Hypolimnetic Oxygen Deficit]]s, and nutrient and sulphur chemistry. Trophic status ranges from oligotrophic (Okataina) to highly eutrophic (Ngapouri and Okaro), and lake differences are largely explained by altitude, area and depth. The author argues that the depth-independent oxygen deficit fails to separate lake types unless depth is incorporated, and that Lake Okataina's unusually large heat budget is a notable departure from lakes elsewhere.

## Background & Description

The trout fishery of the Rotorua district is valuable and well known. Lakes are the main trout habitat, and the study notes that lake physical and biological characteristics strongly affect resident fish population size. The four lakes lie within 20 miles of Rotorua City in a volcanic region of the North Island.

**Origins and morphometry**
- Okataina and Rotoehu occupy a divided volcanic caldera, older than the hydrothermal explosion craters holding Okaro and Ngapouri.
- Okataina is the largest lake (surface area 1,049.419 ha; mean depth 45.48 m; maximum depth 78.5 m; volume about 477,250,210 m³), with three well-defined basins.
- Rotoehu has the greatest shoreline development (3.871) despite a smaller area (791.178 ha). Its digitate arms formed by erosion into softer sedimentary rock. It is the shallowest lake (maximum depth 12.0 m; mean depth 8.16 m).
- Okaro (32.954 ha; maximum depth 15.0 m) and Ngapouri (22.336 ha; maximum depth 25.0 m) have smooth, nearly circular outlines, low shoreline development (1.135 and 1.515), and are much younger.
- Shoreline development is emphasised as a better index of trout potential than area or volume, because it measures the littoral zone where most trout food is concentrated.

**Methods**
- Lakes were visited at least once, and usually twice, a month from late 1960 to the end of 1966.
- Temperature was measured with a thermistor; water samples were collected with a Ruttner bottle.
- Dissolved oxygen was estimated by a modified [[Winkler Method]]; other chemistry followed Mackereth (1963).

**Altitudes:** Okataina 309.1 m; Rotoehu 293.8 m; Okaro 412.4 m; Ngapouri 477.0 m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime**
- All four lakes are classed as "warm" lakes because they stay well above 4°C, so there is no winter inverse stability.
- Winter conditions are isothermal (roughly May to October). Winter minima are controlled mainly by lake volume and altitude.
- Okataina has the highest winter temperatures, with a lowest recording of 10.0°C (10 August 1965) and a mean annual minimum of 10.3°C over five winters.
- Rotoehu responds most quickly to cold; its mean annual minimum was 8.0°C, reached 3–4 weeks before Okataina's.
- Ngapouri (mean minimum 8.2°C) is cooler than Okaro (8.7°C), consistent with the altitude lapse rate of 0.5°C per 100 m attributed to Richter (quoted by Hutchinson 1957). Ngapouri lies 65 m higher than Okaro, giving an expected difference of about 0.3°C.
- Rotoehu is rarely stratified in summer (third-class lake). The other three are second-class and become thermally stratified.
- The annual cycle is a single mixing period in winter followed by summer stratification, so the lakes are classed as [[Monomictic Lakes]].

**Stratification and overturn**
- At maximum summer stratification the [[Thermocline]] depth was 16–21 m (mean 18 m) in Okataina, 7–10 m (mean 8.4 m) in Okaro and 8.5–12 m (mean 9.0 m) in Ngapouri. Lake area was judged the main controlling factor.
- Okataina's epilimnion reached 21.8°C (2 March 1966). Okaro's reached 23.8°C (3 March 1961).
- Hypolimnetic mid-summer temperatures were 10.75–11.5°C (mean 11.0°C) in Okataina, 9.8–11.8°C (mean 10.7°C) in Okaro and 9.6–11.1°C (mean 10.4°C) in Ngapouri.
- [[Overturn]] occurs in June in Okataina (1962–66). In Ngapouri and Okaro it occurs in May, with the shallower Okaro overturning first.

**Seiches**
- Underwater seiches probably affect Okataina's sub-thermocline isotherms (clearly shown in November–December 1962).
- A surface seiche of small amplitude and roughly 3-minute period was noted on 6 December 1962.
- Seiches in Ngapouri and Okaro were small and heavily damped.

**Local climate**
- Okataina's large thermal mass releases heat over winter, so frosts were absent near the shore although severe a few hundred yards inland. Pohutukawa (*Metrosideros excelsa*) fringes the shores.

**Biology and oxygen**
- Ngapouri and Okaro are highly eutrophic. Their plankton (dominated by [[Ceratium Hirundinella]] and [[Anabaena]] in Okaro; diatoms in Ngapouri) caused Secchi disc depths of 2–3 m.
- Rotoehu's oxygen is driven largely by [[Elodea Canadensis]] in its extensive shallow littoral weedbeds.
- Okataina's sparse plankton is still enough to deplete hypolimnetic oxygen steadily.
- Samples from deoxygenated hypolimnia smelled of hydrogen sulphide.
- Smelt (*Retropinna lacustris*) deaths were found after overturn in 1965.

## Key Findings

**1. Heat budgets**
- Okataina has the largest heat income, with a mean of 57,000 cal/cm² over several years. Few lakes elsewhere exceed 40,000 cal/cm².
- Per metre of depth, the heat absorbed is about 1,300 cal/cm²/m in Okataina, 1,100 in Okaro and 800 in Ngapouri.
- Altitude has a large effect: about 2,000 cal/cm² annually per 50 m of altitude, based on Hutchinson's (1957) comparison.
- No clear relationship was found between meteorological variables (sunshine, wind, rainfall) and heat budgets, possibly because Rotorua City weather data do not represent the lakes' local climate.
- Rainfall in 1963 was very high and may be linked to Okataina's low budget that year.

**2. Dissolved oxygen**
- Winter maxima (mean): Okataina 10.3 ppm (near saturation, so production was low); Rotoehu 11.2 ppm (slightly supersaturated); Ngapouri 11.9 ppm; Okaro 12.0 ppm (up to 2.0 ppm above saturation).
- Okataina's lowest recorded levels were 6.34, 6.74, 6.76 and 7.60 ppm for 1963–66, just before overturn.
- Okataina shows a positive heterograde oxygen maximum at 20–22 m, probably from photosynthesising microorganisms below the thermocline.
- Okaro and Ngapouri show clinograde distributions, with the hypolimnion completely deoxygenated by 1966. In 1962 it still held 1–2 ppm.
- The sequence of events on 27 February 1963 in Okaro suggests that an algal population sank through the water column and accumulated on top of the thermocline.

**3. Hypolimnetic oxygen deficits**
- Mean seasonal deficits (mg/cm²): Okataina 8.8; Ngapouri 7.8; Okaro 7.6. Rotoehu's deficit was 2.25 mg/cm².
- Relative (daily) hypolimnetic deficits: Okataina 0.042 mg/cm²/day; Ngapouri 0.044; Okaro 0.034.
- Comparing lakes by area alone obscures differences, because Okataina is much deeper. Expressing deficits in g/m² incorporates depth: Okataina 2.3 g/m² seasonal (0.013 g/m²/day); Ngapouri 9.7 g/m² (0.05 g/m²/day); Okaro 10.0 g/m² (0.054 g/m²/day).
- The author concludes that the proposed [[Mortimer Oxygen Deficit Thresholds]] (up to 0.025 for oligotrophic and 0.055 for eutrophic) do not work here, but depth-corrected grams per cubic metre separate Okataina (oligotrophic) from Ngapouri and Okaro (eutrophic).

**4. Chemistry**
- pH: Okataina's epilimnion reached 8.3, over 1.0 unit above the bottom. Ngapouri's surface rarely exceeded pH 7.0. Okaro's surface was generally above 8.0 and reached over 9.0 in algal blooms. Hypolimnetic pH was about 6.6 in Okaro and Ngapouri.
- Alkalinity: bicarbonate was greatly increased in the bottom waters of Okaro (8.7 vs 5.8 at surface, April 1964) and Ngapouri (8.25 vs 3.7).
- Nutrients: Okataina and Rotoehu had very low phosphate and ammonia. Okaro's hypolimnion reached 0.85 ppm phosphorus and 40.0 ppm nitrogen; Ngapouri's reached 1.7 ppm phosphorus and 64.0 ppm nitrogen. These high values are attributed partly to reduction of [[Iron Sulphide]] and phosphate release from deoxygenated sediments ([[Mortimer 1941]] and [[Mortimer 1942]]).
- Sulphur: only 3–18% of the sulphate reduced to sulphide. The remaining sulphide probably came from bottom deposits. Dissolved iron was immobilised as black iron sulphide.

**5. Overturn effects**
- The overturn of Okaro on 4 May 1965 mixed hypolimnetic nutrients through the water column. Surface oxygen fell to 4.97 ppm, near the critical level for fish.
- About 30 gulls were seen feeding over the lake, and two dead smelt were found. Water was undrinkable by 21 May 1965.

**6. Wider comparisons**
- Okataina's heat budget is much greater than most lakes elsewhere. The author suggests its large epilimnion may explain this, but geothermal input (thermal springs at Tikitere) was not supported by the data.
- The oxygen deficits (about 0.04 mg/cm²/day) resemble those of mesotrophic lakes in Europe and America. The very soft water of the Rotorua lakes suggests low dissolved nutrients may dampen the difference between high- and low-production lakes.
- Other workers' findings are cited: fish productivity inversely related to depth (Hayes and Anthony 1964; the author's own result, Fish 1968); primary production proportional to heat budget (Reimers and Combs 1956); plankton standing crop correlated with hypolimnetic oxygen deficit (Sparrow 1966).

**7. Conclusions and recommendations**
- Lake metrics such as heat budget, depth-corrected oxygen deficit and shoreline development are useful for comparing lakes, but more data are needed.
- The Southern Hemisphere's warm monomictic lakes are poorly studied, and the Rotorua lakes may provide a comparison with lakes at similar latitudes.
- The author cautions that interpretation depends on local conditions and that the critical limiting factor may change quickly.
