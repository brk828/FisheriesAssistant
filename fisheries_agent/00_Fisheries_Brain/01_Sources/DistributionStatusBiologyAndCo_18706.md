# Distribution, Status, Biology, and Conservation of the Spikedace (Meda fulgida) in New Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1986 final report from the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] (Propst, Bestgen, and Painter) documents the status of the [[Spikedace]], a minnow endemic to the Gila Basin of New Mexico and Arizona. Surveys from December 1982 through September 1985 found the species has been extirpated from the [[San Francisco River]] and greatly reduced in the [[Gila River]]. The largest and most stable surviving population is in the [[Cliff-Gila Valley]]. The report attributes the decline to habitat modification combined with predation and competition from [[Non-Native Fishes]], especially the [[Red Shiner]]. It recommends federal listing as threatened, critical habitat designation, and an end to non-native fish introductions.

## Background & Description

The spikedace was first collected in 1851 from the San Pedro River, Arizona, and belongs to the tribe Plagopterini, a group of spiny-rayed minnows endemic to the lower [[Colorado River]] basin. The genus *Meda* contains only this species. In New Mexico it was first documented in 1872 by E.D. Cope from the San Francisco River.

The species prefers low-to-moderate gradient, intermediate-sized streams with sand, gravel, and cobble substrates and slow-to-moderate velocities. It moves in aggregations, feeds on drifting macroinvertebrates, and shifts into slow water in autumn and winter. Historically it co-occurred with the piscivorous [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]], which entered shallow habitats to feed.

Funding came from the Department's Nongame Wildlife Studies project (FW-17-R) and, from September 1983, the "Survey and Management of the Spikedace and Loach Minnow" project (E-3), a federal aid project funded 75% through Section 6 of the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats.** Habitat loss followed European settlement and was driven by irrigation diversion, livestock grazing, timber cutting, groundwater pumping, channelization, dams, and water pollution. The authors note that habitat degradation and non-native fishes often acted together, so the two effects are hard to separate.

**Non-native fishes.** Among the introduced species, [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]] are piscivorous and likely prey on spikedace. The [[Mosquitofish]] is considered to have only a minor impact because it occupies different habitat. Red shiners were implicated in declines elsewhere in the Southwest and are now common in the lower Gila.

**Hydrology.** Flows vary with spring snowmelt, late-summer rains, and drought. The Gila River is perennial, while the San Francisco River often dries in lower reaches because of irrigation. Floods are most common from October through March. The report argues that high flows create and renew spawning and foraging habitat.

**Associated natives.** Six native species co-occurred with the spikedace in New Mexico: [[Longfin Dace]], [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Sonoran Sucker]].

## Key Findings

### Distribution and Status

- **San Francisco River:** Extirpated. The last New Mexico records were three specimens collected in 1948 and 1950, and recent surveys in Arizona, including the Blue River, also failed to find it.
- **East Fork Gila River (headwaters):** Historically common (14.8% of pre-1960 collections). Declined sharply after 1960, with only five specimens in 1961–1980. Absent in 1983 but found at low-to-moderate numbers at several sites in September 1985 after the 1983 and 1984 floods and reduced grazing. Its long-term persistence is not assured.
- **Forks area (Middle and West forks):** Moderate populations persisted. The lower East Fork population, which Anderson (1978) found to be comparatively large, was eliminated within about seven years, probably by habitat degradation after the 1978 flood.
- **Cliff-Gila Valley:** The largest population in New Mexico, and likely the largest surviving population anywhere in the range. Its relative abundance was 21.1% in 1983–1984 and 29.3% in 1961–1980 samples. Density was highest downstream of the village of Gila.
- **Redrock and Virden valleys:** Not viable. Age 0 fish appeared after the 1983 spring runoff, probably displaced from the Cliff-Gila Valley, but could not be found by late 1984. The population at the mouth of the [[Middle Box]] (the [[Conner Site]]) is small and tenuous.
- **Basin survey:** Across the warmwater basin, seven native and 15 non-native species were collected. The spikedace was found only in the areas described above.

### Microhabitat Use

- **Larvae (<25 mm TL):** Mostly in slow water near stream margins. Mean velocity was 8.4 cm/s, and most were in water shallower than 32 cm. Most (60%) were over sand.
- **Juveniles (26–35 mm TL):** Used a wider range of velocities than larvae. Most were in gravel (46%) and sand (45%).
- **Adults:** Used velocities up to 74.7 cm/s (mean 29.6 cm/s in the text), mean depth 19.5 cm, mostly over gravel (47%), cobble (32%), and sand (19%).
- **Geographic differences:** Adults in the Forks area used water below 29 cm/s, while those in the Cliff-Gila Valley used 29–59.5 cm/s. The authors attribute this mainly to habitat availability rather than preference.
- **Seasonal shifts:** In the Forks area, cold-season fish used shallower water. In the Cliff-Gila Valley, cold-season fish used slower velocities, in the lee of gravel bars and riffles.

### Reproduction and Life History

- **Spawning:** Occurred in shallow, sand and gravel riffles. In 1984 spawning began in early April and probably ended by late May. The authors found evidence of fractional spawning, with females spawning several times within a few days.
- **Fecundity:** Age I females may produce up to about 200 ova, and Age II females up to 300. Average fecundity is lower for both.
- **Age structure:** Two age classes contributed to reproduction, but Age I fish did most of it. Maximum longevity is about 24 months, though most individuals live no more than 13 months.
- **Growth:** Little growth occurs from September through April. Age 0 fish averaged 38 mm SL at the end of their first summer, and Age I fish averaged 50 mm SL at the end of their second. The largest specimen recorded in New Mexico was about 58 mm SL.
- **Larval drift:** Downstream displacement is driven mainly by high discharge. Larvae may drift up to about 45 km from the Conner site and survive for a short time.

### Food Habits

Of 88 spikedace examined from June 1983 to June 1984, 76 contained food. Mayflies (Ephemeroptera) occurred in 71% of stomachs, true flies (Diptera) in 34%, and caddisflies (Trichoptera) in 25%. Empty stomachs were common in winter (40% of 24 fish, December–March) but rare the rest of the year (8% of 64 fish, April–November). The authors conclude the species depends on riffle-produced aquatic insects, so riffle habitat is essential.

### Floods and Drought

- The October 1983 flood (peaks of 237.6 m³/s near Gila and 356.8 m³/s near Redrock) increased spikedace at the [[Cliff Dwellings Site]] from 2.7% to 12.2% of the community, likely through recruitment. At the [[Cottonwood Site]] it rose from 11.0% to 63.2%.
- Levees on the Gila near the Indian Ruins site contained the flood, which incised the channel and removed habitat. The authors attribute native-fish losses there to these human modifications rather than the flood itself.
- Non-native fishes were more vulnerable to flood displacement than natives, which tended to stay near stream margins.
- Drought was not studied directly. Droughts are expected to desiccate floodplain habitat and isolate populations.

### Predation and Competition

- **Predation:** Evidence is largely circumstantial. Native fish remains were found in stomachs of channel catfish, flathead catfish, and smallmouth bass in the basin. In the upper East Fork, adult predators were absent after the floods and native fishes recovered.
- **Competition:** The red shiner was first found in the Redrock valley in 1979 and became dominant there (47.8% of collections in 1983–1984). Observers saw red shiners repeatedly disrupt feeding aggregations of suckers. A similar behavior was reported for spikedace in Arizona.

### Recommendations

1. List the spikedace as federally threatened, as proposed by the U.S. Department of the Interior (1985).
2. Designate critical habitat in the Cliff-Gila Valley and the lower West and Middle forks.
3. Keep the species listed as endangered in New Mexico under the state Wildlife Conservation Act.
4. Develop cooperative agreements among landowners and agencies to protect occupied and potential habitat.
5. Prepare a recovery or management plan for New Mexico and Arizona.
6. Discontinue introductions of non-native warmwater fishes, and carefully evaluate and halt activities that harm spikedace habitat.
7. Continue research on human-caused stream modification and the effects of introduced fishes.
8. Secure management areas, including the Cliff-Gila Valley, the East Fork, and the Redrock Valley.
9. Evaluate the feasibility of reintroduction, noting that [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] would be a suitable facility for hatchery stock.
10. Establish guidelines and an agency framework to advise entities proposing instream or riparian modifications.

The authors conclude that the Cliff-Gila Valley population is critical: if it is lost or diminished, the species' overall survival is doubtful.
