# Degeneration of the Eyes in Typhlogarra widdowsoni (Cyprinidae, Pisces)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 study by Nicolaus Peters and Gabriele Peters (Zoologisches Staatsinstitut, Hamburg) describes the progressive degeneration of the eyes in [[Typhlogarra Widdowsoni]], a blind, depigmented cave cyprinid from limestone caves near Haditha, Iraq. Using histological sections of juvenile and adult specimens, the authors compare the eye of the closely related surface fish [[Garra Rufa]] and trace the reduction sequence from the first eye primordium to the adult eye remnant. They conclude that Garra already had a relatively poor, twilight-type eye, which predisposed its descendants to cave life. In the cave form, degeneration follows a pattern similar to that described for [[Anoptichthys]] (the cave form of [[Astyanax Mexicanus]]), and it produces marked left-right asymmetry and individual variability. The authors attribute this variability to the degeneration process itself, which amplifies natural variation in the lens-inducing organizer.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** [[Typhlogarra Widdowsoni]] Trewavas 1955, described from Iraq. Trewavas considered [[Garra Rufa]] (formerly *Discognathus rufus*, type locality Aleppo, Euphrates) its nearest relative.
- **Geological context:** The limestone deposits holding the caves date to the early Miocene. The caves themselves must be younger. The authors argue that freshwater fish could not have colonized them before the Pliocene, when Garra is thought to have entered the region after the retreat of the Syro-Iranian sea (after Kosswig 1955). This makes Garra a probable near-unchanged ancestral form.
- **Morphological differences from Garra:** Typhlogarra differs mainly in eye reduction, near-total loss of pigment, and reduction of scales to those of the lateral line.
- **Material:** 14 specimens in total.
  - Four juveniles preserved in 4% formalin, total length 27–35 mm.
  - Adults, 51–63 mm total length, kept alive in an aquarium for two years or more without further growth before fixation.
  - Heads of 13 specimens (including all juveniles) were paraffin-embedded and cut into 10 µm serial sections, stained with Azan or hematoxylin-picric acid/thiazine red.
  - One specimen was cleared using the modified Lundvall method for whole-head observation.
- **Comparative material:** Several adult Garra rufa (94–121 mm total length) from captive stock of the Istanbul Zoological Institute, originally wild-caught in Asia Minor.
- **Embryology:** A single partial breeding success allowed in vivo observation of embryonic development, but the entire brood died overnight, so no larvae were fixed for histology.
- **Acknowledgements:** Material was obtained through Prof. Dr. C. [[Kosswig]], with support from the Deutsche Forschungsgemeinschaft.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Garra rufa (surface reference):** A bottom-dwelling omnivore that grazes stony substrate with a subterminal scraping mouth bearing horny denticles and a suction apparatus. Its eye is typical of teleosts, with a few departures (the external limiting membrane of the retina is not prominent). The authors judge its eye to be mediocre or even poor, comparable to that of a twilight fish. Because Garra does not depend heavily on visual orientation, reduced vision would have been tolerable, which the authors see as a pre-adaptation for cave life.

**Astyanax mexicanus (comparative reference):** A river fish that feeds on plankton in open water, forms schools, and relies mainly on vision. Its eye is much larger relative to body size, and its retina is densely packed with photoreceptors and strongly stratified. Its cave form, Anoptichthys, is the best-studied model of cave eye regression.

**Ecological interpretation:** The contrast between a visually dependent open-water fish and a grazing bottom fish suggests that eye size and receptor density track lifestyle. The authors argue that Garra's eye, already not highly specialized, offered little resistance to regression once the fish entered the dark cave environment.

## Key Findings

**1. Eye of Garra rufa compared with Astyanax**
- Astyanax eye diameter: 5.10 mm. Garra eye diameter: 3.93 mm (each measured on three specimens and averaged).
- Garra's total length exceeds Astyanax's, yet its eye is relatively and absolutely smaller.
- Per unit retinal area, Astyanax has about twice as many photoreceptors as Garra. The difference lies mainly in rod numbers, as both have roughly equal cone densities.
- In Garra, the cone-rod distinction is blurred, so cones must be counted using ellipsoids as well as nuclei. The cone-to-rod ratio remains uncertain, but total receptor number is reliable.
- Garra's retinal layers are not strictly parallel; the outer nuclear layer shows early folding. The authors interpret folding in a cave population of *Poecilia sphenops* as an early sign of degeneration.
- Garra's ganglion-cell nuclei are scattered, and its nerve-fiber layer is loosened. The inner limiting membrane is often torn.

**2. Eye development in Typhlogarra**
- The eye primordium forms with the somites and central nervous system during early development, as in Astyanax and other fishes. It is noticeably smaller than in Garra.
- A newly hatched larva already has a clearly formed lens but no iris.

**3. Juvenile eye (35 mm specimen)**
- The eye lies deep in the orbit with no open or solid epidermal connection to the skin surface.
- The lens has degenerated completely, leaving no remnant.
- The anterior chamber is filled with a vesicular tissue (the **[[Spongium]]**) derived from the **[[Ligamentum Annulare]]**.
- The iris closes the pupil and contains fine pigment.
- Retinal photoreceptors and the outer nuclear layer have been eliminated. The inner nuclear layer and multilayered ganglion-cell layer remain.
- Concentrated pigment clusters in the choroid, probably a choroidal pigment body, obscure underlying tissue.
- Extraocular muscles are present but insert irregularly, including on the rudimentary sclera.
- The optic nerve runs to the brain.

**4. Adult eyes (51, 61 and 62 mm specimens)**
- The eye sinks deeper beneath the skin than in the juvenile. The distance from the surface is roughly three to four times the largest eye diameter in adults, compared with a smaller fraction in the juvenile.
- In the 61 mm specimen, the left eye retains the juvenile arrangement in reduced form (cornea remnant, spongium, closed iris, retinal nuclear layer without distinct layers, and a temporal choroidal pigment body). The right eye is much more reduced and lacks recognizable eye structure.
- In the 51 mm specimen, both eyes lack recognizable organization. The left eye has a partly ossified sclera, a retinal ring, and two blood spaces. An extraocular muscle appears to enter through a scleral opening, and three nerves pierce the sclera. The right eye is about half the size of the left, with four nerves crossing its sclera.
- In the 62 mm specimen, the left eye is a tangled mass of retinal, muscular, nerve and connective tissues. The right eye is a hollow retinal sphere with only faint pigmentation.
- Pigment is absent in both eyes of the 51 mm adult.

**5. Variability and asymmetry**
- Individual variability ranges from rudiments that still resemble an eye to completely disorganized tissue masses.
- Left-right asymmetry is pronounced. Eyes of the same individual can differ greatly in form and degree of reduction, unlike normal paired eyes.
- Structures are generally lost bilaterally: if a structure (sclera, spongium or choroidal body) is missing on one side, it is usually missing on the other.
- Some features are shared between the two eyes of one individual, such as multiple nerves piercing the sclera.
- The authors judge individual variability to be far greater than left-right variability. Because both eyes share the same genetic basis, they attribute the asymmetry to the degeneration process.
- The rudimentary eye may also rotate about 180° in the transverse plane, with the retina and choroid lying distally.

**6. Proposed mechanism**
- Eye development depends on a chain of dependent inductions, with the optic cup inducing lens formation.
- If the organizer falls below a critical size or differentiation level, the induced structures react more strongly. In a normal eye, minor changes in the organizer produce only proportional changes in the lens.
- Degeneration reflects a major inhibition of growth and differentiation. Within the chain of dependent steps, the normal variability of the organizer is amplified, producing the strong individual and asymmetric variation seen in adults.

**7. Conclusions**
- The eye of Typhlogarra regresses along the same pathway described for Anoptichthys: the lens disappears early, the iris closes the pupil, the spongium fills the anterior chamber, and photoreceptors are lost first.
- Each ontogenetic stage of the juvenile and adult eyes can be read as a stage in the phylogenetic transition from a Garra-like eye to the blind Typhlogarra eye.
- Garra's eye, being already a poor twilight-type eye, provided the predisposition for cave life.
