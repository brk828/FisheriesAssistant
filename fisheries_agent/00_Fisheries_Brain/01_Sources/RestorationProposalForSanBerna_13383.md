## Executive Summary

This 1998 restoration proposal for the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] addresses the degraded habitat conditions currently threatening a suite of threatened and endangered [[Yaqui Fishes]] endemic to the Río Yaqui drainage system straddling the U.S./Mexico border in Southeast Arizona. Although these fishes persist within their historic geographic range, they no longer inhabit their historic habitat type. Since 1982, populations have survived only in artificial, artesian well-fed, human-maintained ponds rather than in naturally flowing ciénega or stream habitat. The report argues that true recovery and eventual **downlisting** of these species under the Endangered Species Act cannot occur without holistic, landscape-level restoration of hydrology, vegetation, and soil — not simply fish-focused intervention. The document outlines 13 restoration goals and describes early-stage grassland restoration, vegetation monitoring, and Restoration Management Unit (RMU) delineation efforts already underway on the refuge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historic Habitat Type:** [[Ciénega]]-like habitat — shallow, low-velocity, perennial streams, as documented in historical literature for [[Black Draw]] and [[Hay Hollow]].
- **Current Habitat:** Since 1982, the Yaqui fishes have been confined to **mud-bottomed, artesian well-fed, human-maintained ponds** — an artificial refugium lacking natural flow regime, substrate complexity, or self-sustaining hydrology.
- **Hydrological Deficiency:** No suitable habitat "consistently exists" in Black Draw at present; perennial flow has been lost, and water table levels are insufficient to maintain interconnected pools between flow events.
- **Landscape Context:** The refuge spans ~2,300 acres and comprises 8 distinct vegetation communities (per [[Lanning 1981]] vertebrate survey), all of which show some degree of degradation. Aquatic, vegetative, and soil resources are described as **interrelated systems** — restoration of fish habitat is treated as inseparable from broader riparian and upland ecosystem repair.
- **Threats to Habitat:**
  - Erosion along steeply cut banks of Black Draw and Hay Hollow
  - Bare, erodable ground lacking vegetative cover
  - Invasive/exotic species, notably **non-native bullfrogs**, identified as a priority eradication target
  - Mesquite encroachment into grasslands and Chihuahuan desert habitat (though mature mesquite bosques are recognized as valuable habitat and are not slated for removal)
- **Associated Terrestrial Indicator Species:** Restoration of Sacaton grassland draws is also linked to habitat needs of other refuge wildlife, including [[Botteri's Sparrow]], indicating the fish recovery plan is nested within a broader multi-taxa restoration framework.

## Key Findings

1. **Habitat-Species Mismatch:** The Yaqui fishes occupy their historic *range* but not their historic *habitat type* — a critical distinction driving the restoration rationale.
2. **Artificial Life Support:** Since 1982, survival of these ESA-listed fish populations has depended entirely on artesian well-fed, human-maintained ponds, which the author explicitly states are **not** the "evolutionary end" state for these species — implying an unsustainable long-term conservation strategy.
3. **Recovery Plan Gap:** An existing Yaqui Fishes recovery plan exists but does not provide guidelines for physical *habitat* restoration — a gap this proposal seeks to fill.
4. **Restoration Goals (13 total)** were established, key aquatic-relevant goals including:
   - Restore perennial flow to [[Black Draw]] to sustain a high water table and interconnected pool systems between flow events.
   - Reestablish ciénega-like shallow, low-velocity stream habitat.
   - Reduce bank erosion in Black Draw and Hay Hollow.
   - Eradicate non-native fauna, particularly **bullfrogs**, a known predator/competitor of native desert fishes and amphibians.
   - Implement long-term monitoring (vegetation, erosion, groundwater, and faunal) coordinated with treatment units to evaluate restoration efficacy.
5. **Restoration Management Units (RMUs):** A spatial framework using roads and ecotones to delineate management zones was initiated, intended to be refined via GPS/GIS mapping and on-the-ground vegetation monitoring, to allow triaged, prioritized restoration interventions.
6. **Pilot Project (RMU-1):** In summer 1997, 12,000 plugs of seven locally-sourced native grass species were planted in a degraded, non-vegetated area adjacent to Black Draw, representing the refuge's first active restoration action tied to this plan.
7. **Multidisciplinary Precedent:** Prior refuge studies — vegetation surveys ([[Marrs-Smith 1983]]), vertebrate surveys ([[Lanning 1981]]), fish studies ([[Mais 1995]]), and hydrologic studies ([[Davis 1997]]) — provide baseline data, but no prior project had attempted to **integrate** these floral, faunal, and hydrologic datasets into a unified restoration strategy until this proposal.
8. **Conservation Framework:** The ultimate refuge goal is framed explicitly as the **downlisting** of the affected Yaqui fishes under the Endangered Species Act, achievable only through coordinated, ecosystem-wide (not species-isolated) restoration.
