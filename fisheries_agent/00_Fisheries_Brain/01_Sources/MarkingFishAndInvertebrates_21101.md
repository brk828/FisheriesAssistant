# Marking Fish and Invertebrates

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Commercial Fisheries leaflet (Fishery Leaflet 545) by George A. Rounsefell, of the [[Biological Laboratory]] in Galveston, Texas, is a practical guide to marking fish and invertebrates. It explains why marking is needed, describes the main [[Mark Types]] (mutilation, vital stains, fluorescent dyes, tattooing and tags), and summarizes how tags are classified by material, [[Attachment Methods]] and [[Recovery Methods]]. It also gives guidance on choosing a mark for a given experiment and on how capture and handling affect survival and recovery. The document is a methods review rather than a single-species study.

## Background & Description

The leaflet is part of the [[Department Of The Interior]] fishery literature and is written for fishery biologists. Its central premise is that natural marks (such as growth bands on scales or parasite differences) are too uncertain for most work, so biologists must mark and release fish themselves. The abstract states that the report covers the reasons for marking, the characteristics of marks, the more successful tags in current use, and how to select a mark for a specific species or experiment.

The leaflet is organized around the following sections:
- Why fish are marked
- Types of marks (mutilation, vital stains, fluorescent and phosphorescent dyes, tattooing, tags)
- Materials used for tags
- Methods of recovery and attachment
- Tags in current use (Table 1)
- Selection of marks for specific experiments (Tables 2 and 3)
- Importance of methods of capture and handling
- References (about 30 entries, many from the [[ICNAF Fish Marking Symposium]], Woods Hole, May 1961)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Marking studies depend on the behaviour and biology of each target organism. The leaflet illustrates several species-specific points:

- **Salmon:** Spawning and migration routes are studied by marking, including king salmon migrating from Southeastern Alaska to the Columbia River and pink salmon using several straits to reach natal streams in the [[Alexander Archipelago]]. Salmon are also a central example in the discussion of [[Spawning Frequency]] and [[Migration Speed]].
- **Halibut:** Mature [[Pacific Halibut]] were reported to migrate from the Aleutian Islands to the Gulf of Alaska off Yakutat to spawn.
- **Shrimp:** Shrimp shed their exoskeletons, so scales and other hard parts cannot be used for ageing. Marking and recapture are therefore a key tool for [[Age And Growth]] estimates. Shrimp in the Gulf of Mexico are marked by injection of biological stain.
- **Skipjack tuna:** Fast, large fish that are sensitive to handling time.
- **Small schooling fish (herring, clupeoids):** Individuals released singly are vulnerable to predation, so marked fish should be released as a school.
- **Invertebrates:** Lobsters, clams, scallops and starfish are marked by notching, etching, filing or staining.

## Key Findings

**Why mark fish.** Marking answers questions that natural marks cannot. The leaflet lists ten uses:
1. Species distinction. Early British tagging tested whether immature salmon and sea trout returned as the same species.
2. Frequency of spawning. Atkins (1873, Penobscot River) found by marking kelts that most Atlantic salmon spawned only every second year.
3. Race distinction. Tests whether groups in different localities intermingle.
4. Geographical distribution. Shows where fish are found, provided recapture effort covers the range.
5. Age and growth. Especially useful for shrimp and for tropical fish without clear annuli.
6. Spawning migrations. Revealed long migrations of king salmon and halibut.
7. Migration routes. Continuous marking at straits showed routes used by pink salmon; early and late runs to the same stream can use different routes.
8. Speed of migration. Important for judging the effect of barriers on salmon, which must reach spawning beds before exhausting energy reserves.
9. Mortality rates. Recapture of tagged versus untagged fish allows estimates of fishing and natural mortality, under stated assumptions.
10. Rearing methods. Compares hatchery feeding, handling and release methods by adult recaptures.

**Types of marks.**
- **Mutilation:** Used for large numbers of small fish. Pink salmon fry were marked by excising fins. Barnaby (1944) found fewer sockeye smolts survived to return with a pectoral fin excised, so pectoral excision is not recommended. Excising two fins gives 10 two-fin combinations. Half-fin clips tend to regenerate. Other methods include opercle or maxillary notching, punched holes (LeCren and Kipling, 1961: 4 mm holes in char fins, usable for under 6 weeks), and telson or uropod notching in lobsters.
- **Branding:** Hot and cold branding (Nesbit, 1920s) became illegible as fish grew. Watson (1961) branded sea herring with heated resistance wires, but the marks were distinguishable for only 2 to 3 days.
- **Vital stains:** Shrimp are injected with dye, which concentrates in the gills within about 24 hours so the thorax is brightly coloured. Marks are unaffected by moulting and work on very small individuals. Immersion staining suits short experiments, such as pink salmon fry used to estimate outmigration.
- **Fluorescent and phosphorescent dyes:** Allow discrimination from naturally coloured organisms. Very small quantities can be measured with a fluorometer.
- **Tattooing:** Kask (1936) injected India ink into halibut, but the marks were unidentifiable after 3 months. Colored latex injections lasted a few months. Electric tattooing is used for rapid marking of large numbers of small salmonids.
- **Tags:** The most common method. Rounsefell and Kask (1945) listed 18 tag types and Rounsefell and Everhart (1953) listed 21.

**Materials.** Plastics, nickel, monel, silver, aluminium, stainless steel, titanium, tantalum and magnetic steel are in use. Aluminium corrodes and suits only short experiments. Calhoun et al. (1951) found cellulose nitrate disks superior to vinylite or cellulose acetate, and stainless steel or tantalum superior to nickel, monel or silver pins. Non-corrosive material is essential for long-term external tags.

**Recovery methods.**
1. By sight: visible external tags, often oversized and colored (e.g., Petersen disc tags with a contrasting spot).
2. Underwater sound transmission: battery-powered transducers track individual fish for several hours, useful around fishways and dams.
3. Electromagnet or electronic detector: for small species processed in bulk (magnetic body-cavity tags).
4. Radioactivity: very low-level sources detected on conveyor lines.

**Attachment methods.** Eight methods are described: to muscle (most widely used; Atkins, Petersen, hydrostatic and spaghetti tags), to bone, to fin membranes, anchored by barbs or hooks, encircling (collar, jaw), body cavity, internal anchor, and elasticity-dependent (rubber collar). Method 8 is unsuitable for long-term experiments.

**Table 1 tags.** About 20 types are listed as the most successful, including Petersen, dart, serrated spear, smelt, body cavity, magnetic, radioactive, Atkins, spaghetti, herring, internal anchor, bachelor button, strap and jaw tags.

**Selection guidance.** Design the experiment around a specific question. Fin clipping is permanent and suits small fish but gives low individuality. Tattooing suits very large numbers. Individual identification requires tags. Key details include:
- Red external tags seem most attractive to predators, but evidence conflicts. Watson found yellow plastic tubing gave five times the returns of green, and no red tags were recovered.
- Stainless steel pins are superior to nickel or silver for Petersen tags.
- Hydrostatic Atkins tags attached by a bridle outperform those on a curved loop. Braided nylon is superior to a single nylon filament.
- Dart tags gave several times the recoveries of spaghetti tags for skipjack. Marr (1961) attributed this to a 7-second operation versus 20 seconds.
- Hartt (1961) found Petersen-tagged salmon were held more easily by gill nets, retaining smaller fish. This biases results, because marked fish must not differ from the population in their chance of capture.

**Capture and handling.**
- Iversen and Idyll (1960) obtained 22 percent recoveries of Tortugas pink shrimp from 15 to 20 minute hauls with a small otter trawl, but only 14 percent from 1 hour hauls with a large commercial otter trawl.
- Hartt (1961) reported poor high-seas recoveries from gill-netted salmon. Baited longlines did better, and purse seines gave the highest proportion of returns.
- Large, agile fish should be held in a padded cradle. Elapsed time from capture to release can be critical.
- Narcotizing solutions are used by some workers, but opinion remains divided.
- Costello's release box (Galveston) lowers shrimp to the bottom and opens by messenger. Divers observed shrimp burrowing rapidly, which reduces predation.

**Conclusion.** The leaflet concludes that no single tag suits all purposes. Choice depends on the aims, the size of the organism, the expected time before recovery, and the recovery method.
