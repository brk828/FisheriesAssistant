# Aggressive Display in the Paradise Fish, *Macropodus opercularis*

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anabantidae

## Executive Summary

This Turtox News article by [[Charles H. Southwick]] and [[Ronald W. Ward]] describes the stereotyped aggressive display of the [[Paradise Fish]] (*Macropodus opercularis*), an [[Anabantidae]] labyrinth fish. The authors present the display as a practical teaching and research model for visual communication and ritualized social interaction. The encounter sequence, its components, and a quantitative comparison of male-male aggression after 1, 6, and 12 days of isolation are described. The authors also outline laboratory methods and the wider research program, which is supported by an [[National Science Foundation]] grant.

## Background & Description

- **Labyrinth organ:** Anabantids possess a [[Labyrinth Organ]] in the upper gill chamber that allows air breathing. This lets them survive in oxygen-poor water. The paradise fish can survive 6 to 12 hours out of water if kept moist.
- **Genus overview:** *Macropodus* contains four species, all native to Asia. *M. opercularis* is the only one selectively bred for the aquarium trade, having been introduced to France in 1869. Related species include *M. cupanus* (Indian spike-tailed paradise fish), *M. dayi*, and *M. chinensis* (round-tailed paradise fish).
- **Size and habitat:** Adults are small, usually 2 to 3 inches in total length. Natural habitats include village ponds, wet rice paddies, and slow shallow streams. The diet consists of mosquito larvae, aquatic crustaceans, annelids, and small snails.
- **Reproduction:** Males are territorial [[Bubble Nest]] builders. In *M. opercularis*, eggs float up into the nest, whereas in *M. cupanus* they sink and are retrieved by both sexes. Fry hatch within two to four days and are guarded by the male. The nest typically breaks down after 6 to 8 days.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Aggressive displays can occur at any stage of nest building, mating, or parental care, whenever an intruder enters a male's territory. They can also be evoked by placing two fish together that have been isolated for 24 hours or more. Two adult males of similar size show the full display most consistently. Females also show most display components, and fish do not need to be in breeding condition.

In nature, the authors suggest, fighting would be rare because display establishes social relationships without injury. In aquaria, display often fails to settle dominance, especially when fish are evenly matched in size, color, and condition, and direct fighting follows.

The authors describe the females' aggressive repertoire as a possible [[Ecological Support System]]. If a territorial male is removed, the female takes over nest defense and parental care.

## Key Findings

**Display sequence.** A full aggressive encounter follows five stages: (1) frontal display; (2) lateral or mutual lateral display; (3) flank attack; (4) frontal attack; (5) jaw locking. Dominance can be established during lateral display, which ends the encounter before any attack. The subordinate fish turns pale and holds a 45° head-up posture with collapsed fins, which inhibits further attack.

**Display components.**
- *Frontal display:* gill covers and branchiostegal membranes are erected to enlarge the apparent profile. This display is especially well developed in the [[Siamese Fighting Fish]] (*Betta splendens*).
- *Mutual lateral display:* four components are usually present: head-to-tail orientation, full dorsal and caudal fin expansion, S-shaped body posture, and body quiver with rapid pectoral fin beating. Fish often rotate 90 to 180° around a vertical axis.
- *Flank attack:* glancing bites to the flank, often at the caudal peduncle, which can tear scales and damage the caudal fin.
- *Frontal attack:* bites to the mouth or head. Jaw locks can last more than one minute; one lasted nine minutes.

The authors report that males fought more readily and vigorously after 6 days of isolation. Latency to display was shorter (P < 0.05), latency to jaw lock was shorter, and there were more attacks and jaw locks per 10 minutes. The authors state these data do not fully support [[Lorenz]]'s concept of "specific action potential," which predicts that motivation to perform a behavior grows when the appropriate situation is unavailable.

**Sex differences.** Female-female threat phases are shorter than male-male threat phases, while female attack phases are longer or more intense.

**Research program.** Completed or ongoing studies compare males and females, wild-trapped and domestic fish, isolation durations, and opaque versus transparent dividers. Other topics include sensory modalities, fin patterns, model releaser shapes and colors, early experience, and population density. Drug, hormone, and CNS ablation studies have not yet been done. The authors note that the fish are hardy, breed readily, and are unlikely to be disturbed by observers, which makes them well suited to classroom and laboratory teaching.
