# Sea Lamprey in New York Waters: Status, Biology and Control Options (1956)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyclostomata

## Executive Summary

This 1956 article from the *New York State Conservationist* by Chief Aquatic Biologist John R. Greeley reviews the [[Sea Lamprey]] (*Petromyzon marinus*) and related lampreys in New York. It argues that New York's lamprey situation is less acute than the crisis in the upper [[Great Lakes]], where sea lampreys caused a sharp decline in [[Lake Trout]] production. New York has long-established lamprey populations, and the author concludes that most of these waters are relatively stable, that lamprey damage can be offset in part by heavy stocking, and that no method of complete eradication is in sight. The piece also contains an unrelated feature on ash bat manufacture at Dolgeville and a directory of Conservation Department and farm service offices for Ontario, Livingston, Monroe, Wayne and Yates counties, which carry no ecological content.

## Background & Description

- **Lamprey biology:** Lampreys are jawless vertebrates (Cyclostomes), not eels and not true fishes. They have no jaw bones or paired fins. Their sucking disc has teeth arranged in concentric rows, and the name derives from Greek roots meaning "circle" and "mouth."
- **Diversity in New York:** Six lamprey species occur in New York. Four are non-parasitic and two are parasitic: the [[Sea Lamprey]] and the [[Silvery Lamprey]] (*Ichthyomyzon concolor*). The silvery lamprey is rare, with only a few distribution records from [[Lake Erie]] to [[Lake Champlain]]. It rarely exceeds one foot and is not considered troublesome.
- **Brook lampreys:** Non-parasitic [[Brook Lamprey]] species seldom exceed eight inches as breeding adults and do not feed after transformation. The article notes they cause no harm to fish life. Breeding adults found from April to June that are very small probably belong to a non-parasitic species.
- **Life cycle:** All species pass through a larval stage called ammocoetes. Larvae are eyeless, toothless and worm-like, and they live for several years in mud banks, straining small organisms from the current (see [[Larval Ammocoetes]]). At about pencil-diameter size they transform, developing eyes and a sucking disc. Parasitic species are immature at transformation and feed on fish blood until they mature. All species spawn in gravel riffles and die after breeding.
- **Landlocked versus sea-run forms:** Landlocked sea lampreys in lakes are usually one to two feet long. Sea-run adults can reach about three feet. Sea-run populations occur in the Delaware, lower Hudson tributaries and Long Island streams. The Susquehanna had a similar run until dams eliminated it.
- **Historical records:** Biologists at Cornell noticed the lamprey in Cayuga Lake in 1875. Lampreys are believed to have been present in Seneca, Ontario, Oneida and Champlain for a very long time. The article suggests the species moved west from New York waters, with the [[Welland Canal]] (built 1833) providing access to [[Lake Erie]]. The first recorded Lake Erie specimen was in 1921.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Great Lakes invasion:** Sea lampreys spread rapidly through Lakes Michigan, Huron and Superior, and their spread correlated with a crash in lake trout production. Lake Erie is shallow with a small area of deep, cold water, and it never supported many lake trout, so the same correlation was not evident there. Angling and commercial fisheries in Lake Erie showed no overall drop attributable to lampreys.
- **[[Lake Ontario]]:** Lampreys have long been numerous here, but their effect is hard to assess. Smallmouth bass fishing is productive, so lampreys do not appear to be a general threat to game fish. Lake trout are particularly vulnerable, and a co-operative stocking program with Ontario showed encouraging survival.
- **[[Seneca Lake]]:** Lake trout and lampreys have both maintained high populations. Royce (1949) found 88 per cent of trout scarred, with no evident effect on weight. The authors suggest rapid growth helps fish tolerate blood loss, and heavy annual stocking supports trout numbers. Seneca supplies the principal spawn-taking for the state hatcheries.
- **[[Cayuga Lake]]:** Earlier efforts to reduce lampreys with a trap weir, reported in 1904, produced little lasting result. The lake responds well to lake trout planting despite some lamprey damage, and it supports a productive smallmouth bass fishery.
- **[[Lake Champlain]]:** This lake has considerable deep, cold water and is an outstanding producer of smelt. It is not a lake trout lake, though a few were reported in early times. It has a fair number of lampreys.
- **[[Oneida Lake]]:** This shallow, productive lake supports walleye, bass and other warm-water species. Lampreys are not scarce but draw little complaint. Adams and Hankinson (1928) reported eleven species showing signs of lamprey attack, including tullibee (a whitefish) often found dead with scars.
- **Pepacton and Rondout Reservoirs:** The [[Pepacton Reservoir]] dam blocked the historic sea lamprey spawning run in the East Branch of the Delaware. Transforming lampreys dropped into the reservoir and attacked large brown trout. Some moved through the tunnel into the [[Rondout Reservoir]]. No fatal attacks had been documented, and the author considers permanent landlocking unlikely, citing dammed rivers elsewhere where no resident populations formed.
- **Larval period and recruitment:** The larval period is believed to be four or five years in some waters, though this is uncertain in the East Branch of the Delaware. Several annual year-classes may therefore be produced even without new spawning.

## Key Findings

1. **Overall status:** Most New York lamprey waters have had lampreys for a long time, and their situation is relatively stable. Populations have decreased in some areas and increased in others, with no general increase.
2. **Damage varies by water body:** Lake trout are most vulnerable. Heavy stocking appears to offset damage in lakes such as Seneca, while smallmouth bass and warm-water fisheries show little sign of general harm.
3. **Extermination is not feasible:** The article stresses the "defense in depth" of multiple age groups. Removing the entire breeding population would still leave immature lampreys and several larval year-classes in the mud. Cutting breeding numbers in half would not halve survival, because a female can produce about 25,000 eggs.
4. **Control methods assessed:**
   - *Electric weirs* at spawning streams can block lampreys with complete success over time, but they are subject to power failures and are dangerous without foolproof safeguards. They also threaten game fish, such as the rainbow trout run in Catharine Creek.
   - *Killing immature lampreys* before they feed offers proportionate reduction. However, downstream migration of recently transformed lampreys is spread over Fall to Spring, and no effective trap has been developed.
   - *Larval poisons:* The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service has found several promising chemicals toxic to lampreys at concentrations safe for fish, but they are currently very expensive.
   - *Electric shockers* stir larvae out of mud for netting, but they are labor-intensive.
5. **Recommended approach:** Local evaluation of lamprey damage and the cost of reducing it should come before any control program. Lampreys should be viewed as one phase of overall fisheries management, where good fishing remains the primary objective. Partial control implies an indefinite expenditure, so economical methods are worth developing.
6. **Current actions:** Concentrated control research is underway in the upper Great Lakes. New York is studying how much damage local lampreys cause and seeking ways to minimize it.
7. **Historical note:** Large sea lampreys fresh from the sea were considered a delicacy in the Old World. James E. De Kay's *Natural History of New York* (1842) described them as highly esteemed by epicures. Today, lamprey larvae are used as lively, durable bait for game fish.
