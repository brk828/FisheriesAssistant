# Livestock Grazing Effects on Western Streams and Riparian Ecosystems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This review, by Belsky, Matzke and Uselman, synthesizes the major effects of livestock grazing on streams and riparian areas in the arid and semiarid western United States. It draws mainly on peer-reviewed experimental and observational studies, with secondary attention to comparative studies of grazed versus historically or currently protected areas. The authors conclude that livestock grazing damages stream and riparian systems through effects on [[Water Quality]], [[Stream Channel Morphology]], [[Riparian Vegetation]], and aquatic and terrestrial wildlife, and that there is no credible evidence that grazing benefits these systems. Recovery is possible but slow, typically requiring long periods of exclusion.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The review covers local-scale impacts (Table 1) and landscape- and regional-scale consequences (Table 2) of grazing on [[Riparian Ecosystems]] in the arid West.
- **Extent of riparian land:** Riparian and stream ecosystems represent only 0.5 to 1% of the surface area of the 11 western states, yet they are disproportionately important for biodiversity and water.
- **Historical context:** Riparian habitats were viewed as "sacrifice" areas until the late 1960s. The Taylor Grazing Act of 1934 began regulating grazing on the public domain. Early grazing practices were described as highly destructive.
- **Current status:** A joint [[Bureau of Land Management]] and U.S. Forest Service report (USDI 1994a) estimated that 70% of BLM-managed riparian areas were "non-functioning" and 16% were "functioning at risk." Less than 20% of potential riparian habitat in the western United States still exists in functioning condition.
- **Livestock numbers:** Cattle in the western United States increased from 21,500,000 in 1940 to 54,400,000 in 1990, according to the source.
- **Biodiversity:** An estimated 60 to 70% of western bird species, and up to 80 to 90% of wildlife species in Arizona and New Mexico, depend on riparian habitats.
- **Methods:** The authors searched the literature for empirical studies and reviews. They prioritised refereed papers with paired or controlled designs and excluded conclusions drawn from unrefereed reports or anomalous studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality (Table 1):**
- Runoff from disturbed banks, direct manure and urine deposition, and resuspension of sediments increase nutrient, bacterial and protozoan loads, and sediment and turbidity.
- Fecal coliform and fecal streptococci are monitored as indicators of pathogens such as *Cryptosporidium*, *Giardia* and *Salmonella*. Bacterial levels tend to rise with grazing pressure.
- Excess nutrients stimulate algal blooms, whose later decomposition lowers dissolved oxygen.

**Stream channel morphology and hydrology:**
- Streambank trampling, loss of vegetation, and compaction lead to channel widening, deepening (downcutting), loss of pools, and reduced large woody debris.
- Grazing lowers groundwater tables, increases peak flows and flood velocity, and reduces summer base flows.

**Fish and aquatic life:**
- Sediment blankets spawning gravel and suffocates embryos. Higher temperatures, low dissolved oxygen, and lower summer flows reduce survival of [[Salmonid Fishes]] and other cold-water species.
- Loss of thermal refugia and cover reduces salmonid productivity. Shifts from salmonids to non-game fish are reported.

**Vegetation and wildlife:**
- Selective grazing favours unpalatable and weedy species. Loss of trees and shrubs reduces shade and detritus inputs, and degrades habitat for birds, amphibians and mammals.
- Riparian zones provide flood attenuation, sediment trapping and bank stabilisation.

**Landscape-scale effects (Table 2):**
- Degraded streams reduce recreational fishing, swimming and commercial fishing, and increase costs for water treatment, reservoir sedimentation and irrigation canal maintenance.
- Upland grazing increases runoff and erosion, which delivers sediment and nutrients to streams.

**Other stressors:** Urban development, mining, dams, road construction, logging, beaver removal, and water diversions also damage riparian systems. Livestock grazing is still considered the most pervasive source of upland and riparian degradation in the arid West.

**Humid regions:** Fewer studies exist, but available evidence suggests similar impacts in wetter climates.

## Key Findings

- **Predominantly negative effects:** Nearly all scientific studies, observational and experimental, found that livestock grazing damages streams and riparian areas. The authors found no peer-reviewed empirical studies reporting a positive impact of cattle on riparian areas when compared with ungrazed controls.
- **Causes of inconsistent results:** Authors attribute non-significant results to design problems. These include high variability among plots, short recovery periods after grazing removal, heavy browsing by native herbivores, unplanned disturbances such as floods, and prior grazing history. Some studies also used a probability threshold of P < 0.10 rather than the conventional P < 0.05.
- **Grazing systems:** Rotational, seasonal and deferred systems improve conditions relative to season-long grazing at high stocking rates. However, none of these systems were compared with no grazing, so they do not show that grazing benefits riparian zones.
- **Fish declines:** Grazing is identified as a major cause of degraded fish populations across the arid West. A U.S. Forest Service review (Flather et al. 1994) ranked livestock grazing as the fourth major cause of species endangerment in the United States, and as the leading cause in some regions.
- **Recovery time:** Herbaceous and woody vegetation may recover soon after grazing stops, but channel form may take hundreds of years. Stream recovery after livestock exclusion may take 10 to 50 years, and some authors estimate 10 to 15 years of exclusion is needed to initiate recovery. One study (Sarr et al. 1996) found 10 full years of exclusion were needed to reverse negative trends.
- **Conclusion:** Livestock do not benefit stream and riparian communities, water quality, or hydrologic function in any way. Their damage can be reduced by improved grazing methods, herding or fencing livestock away from streams, reducing stocking rates, or increasing rest periods. Livestock exclusion produced the most rapid recovery of ecosystems.
