# Structure and Dynamics of a Turtle Community over Two Decades

#ichthyology #marine-biology #emydidae #chelydridae #kinosternidae #trionychidae

## Executive Summary

This chapter reports a 42-year mark-recapture study (1953–1994) of the turtle community on the [[E. S. George Reserve]] (ESGR), a protected research site in southeastern Michigan. Three common species, [[Midland Painted Turtle]], [[Blanding's Turtle]], and [[Common Snapping Turtle]], make up over 98% of resident turtles. The authors used repeated captures to estimate population sizes and asked how community structure changed over time. Over the 20-year window (1975–1994), the painted turtle population grew significantly, snapping turtles grew slowly, and Blanding's turtles remained stable. The [[Stinkpot Turtle]] was the only species with substantial instability, ranging from extirpation in dry years to small populations in wet years. A sharp decline in the southwest painted turtle population between the 1950s and 1970s is also documented. The authors attribute it to a catastrophic rather than gradual event, probably linked to the drying of wetlands in 1971.

The text is truncated in the source at the end of the Discussion, so the Summary and References are not included.

## Background & Description

**Research framing.** The authors frame community stability as dependent on the persistence of the component populations. Long-lived organisms such as turtles often require multi-decade studies to document variation in life-history traits. The ESGR studies were originally designed to investigate life histories and ecology rather than community dynamics, so the authors used the data to infer whether interspecific interactions shaped community structure.

**Study site.** The 615-ha ESGR was set aside as a game preserve in 1927–1928 and given to the [[University of Michigan]] in 1930. Access is limited by fencing and locked gates. Primary aquatic areas include the Southwest Swamp, Fishhook Marsh, and Crane Pond complex (7.3 ha, southwest population) and the East Marsh and Cattail Marsh complex (5 ha, southeast population). Smaller ponds and wetlands are not considered permanent residences for many turtles.

**Study history.** Captures were made over several overlapping programs:
- Owen Sexton marked 913 painted turtles and 92 Blanding's turtles in 1953–1957 ([[Sexton 1959]]).
- Henry Wilbur marked about 600 painted, 60 Blanding's, and 12 snapping turtles in 1968–1973 ([[Wilbur 1975]]).
- Donald Tinkle and Justin Congdon marked 1216 painted, 281 Blanding's, and 356 snapping turtles in 1975–1979.
- Congdon and colleagues marked 2687 painted, 469 Blanding's, and 1291 snapping turtles in 1980–1994.
- Overall, approximately 8100 individuals of the three common species were marked on the ESGR, with 1105 more marked in adjacent marshes.

**Natural history of the three common species.**
- **Sex determination:** Sex is determined by incubation temperature rather than genetically, so nest-site selection and weather can shift hatchling sex ratios.
- **Painted turtles:** Smaller than the other two species, they mature at 7–12 years and produce one or two clutches of about seven eggs per year. Males mature earlier and at smaller size. Hatchlings typically overwinter in the nest.
- **Blanding's turtles:** Gentle and intermediate in size, they mature at 11–20 years and have a cohort generation time of about 37 years. They produce about 10 eggs per clutch, one or fewer clutches per year, and are primarily carnivorous. They make extended overland movements.
- **Snapping turtles:** The most widely distributed and largest of the three. They are aggressive, mature at 11–16 years (females), lay about 28 eggs per clutch, and clutch frequency is less than annual. They are harvested for meat across their range.

**Table II (morphometrics):** Mean carapace length was 136.6 mm (painted), 195.2 mm (Blanding's), and 253.1 mm (snapping). Mean body mass was 371.8 g, 1171.9 g, and 4085.4 g, respectively. Mean clutch sizes were 6.96, 10.0, and 27.8 eggs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Capture methods.** Turtles were captured with baited hoop traps, basking traps, fyke nets, dip nets, seines, and by hand from boats or land. The present study relied heavily on drift fences: a 0.9-km fence enclosing Crane Pond (1975–1982) and a 1.3-km fence enclosing East Marsh (1983–1994). Intensive aquatic trapping used 75 to 140 traps each year from early May to early September in 1975–1986 and 1991–1994. Nesting-season observers walked fences and searched nesting areas, recording more than 2500 nests. Turtles were individually marked by notching or drilling the carapace margins.

**Population estimation.** Population sizes were estimated from annual captures adjusted by catchability indices calculated over rolling 3-year periods. Repeated Lincoln indices from sequential years were also used. Only individuals greater than 60% of minimum adult size were included (n = 5447) to avoid biases from catchability differences among young juveniles. A change was judged significant only if it exceeded two standard errors.

**Drought and wetland drying.** The authors link population fluctuations to hydrologic events. The 1971 drying of Southwest Swamp, Crane Pond, and Fishhook Marsh is a key candidate for the southwest decline. The dredging of Crane Pond margins in 1966 is another candidate. The 1980s dry years coincided with peaks in East Marsh, probably because turtles moved from drying temporary habitats into marshes that still held water.

**Interspecific responses to drying.** Literature cited in the chapter shows that species respond differently to drying. Painted turtles and sliders emigrated from a drying lake while snapping turtles did not. In South Carolina, sliders and cooters left a drying bay, but mud turtles and chicken turtles did not. These differences could alter community structure during drought.

## Key Findings

**Community composition**
- Painted turtles, Blanding's turtles, and snapping turtles make up over 98% of resident turtles on the ESGR.
- Totals in Table I are 3515 painted, 1679 snapping, and 911 Blanding's turtles marked over the full study. Painted turtles were about two-thirds of all marked turtles.
- The total number of turtles of the three common species grew from about 1200 to 1600 during 1975–1994. Relative proportions of each species stayed fairly constant. Most of the increase came from painted turtles, which rose from about 850 to 1150 individuals.
- Six species were captured in 42 years. The stinkpot is present in low numbers. The spiny softshell and common map turtle were rarely captured with no recaptures, so they are not considered residents.

**Population trends, 1975–1994 (individuals/year and Lincoln estimates)**

| Species | Mean estimate | Trend | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Painted turtle | 959 / 986 | Increase of about 300 | Significant |
| Snapping turtle | 198 / 220 | Increase of about 40 | Significant |
| Blanding's turtle | 176 / 186 | Increase of about 10 | Not significant |

- Blanding's turtles remained stable.
- Snapping turtle growth was slow and was partly attributed to improved trapping experience and technique in the early years.

**Southwest and southeast painted turtle populations**
- The southwest population fell from about 1000 individuals (1950s) to fewer than 200 (1968–1972). A 1975–1979 estimate was about 460.
- Reductions were estimated at 80% (Wilbur's data) and 55–60% (this study). The authors concluded the reduction was less than 60% and that the decline was catastrophic rather than gradual.
- Over the past 20 years, both southwest and southeast populations increased by about 100 individuals, a growth rate under 1% per year. The authors suggest the southwest population may not be recovering at all.
- The East Marsh population rose by 90–220 individuals above the regression mean in 1981–1985, then returned near pre-peak levels by the late 1980s.
- Movement between southwest and southeast populations involved less than 5% of studied individuals over 15 years.

**Sex ratios**
- Painted turtle sex ratio shifted from about 58.8% female (1950s) to 67.8% male (1980s–1990s).
- Male-to-female ratios among newly marked painted turtles were 1.94 (1975–1979), 1.41 (1980–1984), 3.38 (1985–1989), and 2.48 (1990–1994).
- Blanding's turtles stayed female-biased (77.4% female) throughout the studies.
- Snapping turtles maintained an approximately equal sex ratio.
- The authors suggest environmental sex determination may partly explain the painted turtle shift, but field sexing of hatchlings is not possible.

**Stinkpot turtles**
- Stinkpots were the only species with substantial instability. About 46 were marked over 20 years.
- Roughly 32 were present in 1975–1980 (wet years), none were captured in 1980–1989 (mostly hot, dry years), and about a dozen were captured in 1990–1994 (wet, cool years).
- They constituted less than 2% of the community when present. Their presence may depend on open water of a critical minimum depth.

**Nest survival**
- Nest survival was highest in painted turtles, intermediate in Blanding's, and lowest in snapping turtles. Among-year nest survival ranged from 0 to 67% for all species.

**Community regulation**
- The authors could not explain why two species stayed near constant levels while painted turtles fluctuated. Snapping turtle males are aggressive, which may regulate their density. Blanding's turtles are not aggressive, so social structure is unlikely to be their main density control.
- Blanding's and snapping turtles are primarily carnivorous, whereas painted turtles are omnivorous. The authors note this difference but do not link it to the population patterns.

**Conclusions**
- Painted turtle populations declined sharply in the southwest between the 1950s and early 1970s, probably after the 1971 wetland drying. They have not recovered to 1950s levels.
- The relative composition of the ESGR turtle community was probably altered during the drought, but no pre-decline composition data exist for comparison.
- Long-term data are essential for detecting such community-level shifts, which short-term studies could miss.
