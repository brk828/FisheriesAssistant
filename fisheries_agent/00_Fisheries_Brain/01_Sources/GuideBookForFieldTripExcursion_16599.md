# Gila Conglomerate: Definition, Age and Groundwater Problems

#geology #hydrogeology #stratigraphy #gila-conglomerate

## Executive Summary

This is a pre-field-trip review paper by L. A. Heindl (Tucson, Arizona), prepared for the Arizona Geological Society guidebook for the Cordilleran Section meeting of the [[Geological Society of America]] (April 10–14, 1952, Tucson). It concerns the [[Gila Conglomerate]], a body of poorly to well-consolidated alluvial and lacustrine sediment that underlies much of southern Arizona. The document contains no fish, invertebrate, or aquatic-biology data. Its aquatic relevance is limited to groundwater: the sediments store and transmit water, and the paper discusses them as an auxiliary aquifer. The main conclusion is that the term "Gila conglomerate" has been applied so broadly that it obscures the depositional and structural history of the basins.

## Background & Description

**Origin of the term.** [[Gilbert]] (1875) first applied "Gila conglomerate" to coarse fluvial conglomerates along the [[Gila River]] east of the Arizona–New Mexico border, tentatively assigning them to the Pleistocene. [[Ransome]] (1904, 1919) extended the name westward to the Ray, Arizona area and redefined it to include lacustrine deposits, because the conglomerates grade laterally and vertically into silt. Later workers, including [[Meinzer]] (1913) near Willcox, [[Schrader]] (1915) as far south as Nogales, and [[Darton]] (1925) near the Vekol Mountains and at Peach Springs, extended the name further, including beds in New Mexico.

**Current usage.** As used today, the term covers poorly to well-consolidated alluvium deposited in the structural valleys of the Gila River and its tributaries, under conditions similar to those of the present. It excludes early Tertiary conglomerates and obviously Recent terrace and stream deposits.

**Three depositional environments** are generally recognized within the formation:
1. **Fanglomerate** along basin margins (cobble and pebble conglomerates with interbedded pebbly sandstone).
2. **Playa deposits** in the valley centers (coarse sand to clay, with fine sand and silt predominating).
3. **Transition zone** between the two.

Basalt flows are locally intercalated with the sediments.

**Structural setting.** The paper describes a region of at least 30,000 square miles crossed by seven roughly parallel mountain ranges separated by seven broad intermontane troughs. The ranges are generally interpreted as uplifted blocks and the troughs as down-faulted, alluvium-filled basins. The [[Structural Basins of Southern Arizona]] are central to the discussion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses the depositional environment and hydrology of the sediments rather than any living community. The relevant environmental points are:

- **Depositional setting.** The Gila conglomerate is interpreted as deposited under conditions similar to today's, in an arid to semi-arid desert basin setting with desert washes and intermittent lakes (playas). Fossils from the lake beds show that deposits in several basins were contemporaneous.
- **Lacustrine and evaporitic deposits.** Playa deposits include extensive [[Gypsum Deposits]], thin freshwater limestone units, and tuffaceous and diatomaceous beds. Some gypsum is mined commercially.
- **Vertebrate fauna.** A few vertebrate fossils from essentially horizontal lake beds south of [[Benson Arizona]] date the formation (see Key Findings). The paper does not describe fish or other aquatic fauna.
- **Surface recharge.** Infiltration from runoff in desert washes is greatest in the fanglomerate zone, which makes that zone the main recharge area for the basins.

## Key Findings

**1. Age.** Based on a few vertebrate fossils from essentially horizontal lake beds south of Benson (Gidley 1922, 1926; Gazin 1942), the Gila conglomerate is dated as [[Late Pliocene]] to [[Early Pleistocene]]. No environmental change separates the two epochs. Because drill data show a further 1,200 to 1,600 feet of similar sediment beneath the surface exposures, deposition may have begun before the late Pliocene.

**2. Thickness.** Estimates range from a minimum of not less than 1,000 feet (Schwennesen 1919) to a maximum of 8,000 feet (Ransome 1919). The author notes that the great thicknesses are probably explained by contemporaneous faulting, and that faulting may have duplicated beds. No estimate defines a bottom boundary, and the base of the formation has not been identified in the centers of any structural valley.

**3. Fanglomerate characteristics.** Some fanglomerates have initial dips of up to 10 degrees and contain boulders up to 12 feet in diameter, which indicates local sources. Granitic boulders are generally rounded, while volcanic clasts are angular.

**4. Transition zone.** In most places the transition zone is less than 200 yards wide. Where sediment is almost wholly granitic, it can reach a mile. Abrupt changes locally mean that faulting must be excluded by tracing the gradation step by step.

**5. Geomorphology.** In most Arizona valleys, the formation is eroded into a series of graded surfaces sloping gently toward valley centers. Three main terraces, separated by distinct slope steepenings, are generally recognized. Some terraces are veneered by up to 100 feet of younger alluvium.

**6. Definitional problem.** The term is applied to alluvial deposits across at least 30,000 square miles. The author argues that textural similarity, common lake beds, and some contemporaneity of fossil-bearing upper members do not prove areal continuity, which has not been demonstrated at the surface or at depth. The passes between basins are not known to be underlain by Gila conglomerate. The assumption of a single structural pattern across the region is also unproven. The author suggests the broad usage has hindered understanding of basin history.

**7. Late Tertiary deformation.** Steeply dipping, tightly cemented conglomerates within the basins, with local sources that are not clearly demonstrable, are often lumped with the flat-lying Gila beds. Ross (1925) and Knechtel (1936) separated them. The latest regional deformation is assumed to be late Miocene or early Pliocene (Knechtel 1936; McKee 1951), but [[Gilluly]] (1949) has questioned whether a single tectonic episode applies over such large areas. The author suggests the steeply dipping beds in each basin may be contemporaneous with flat-lying beds in that basin and in others, and that these deformed areas may be key to the region's structural history.

**8. Groundwater significance.** Basin water supply is derived mainly from groundwater, and increasing agricultural and municipal demand makes the Gila conglomerate an important auxiliary source. Key findings on its hydrology are:
- Relatively few wells in the fanglomerate are successful, even at depths of 500 to 600 feet. Water has not been reached everywhere because drilling has not always gone deep enough.
- Playa lake beds contain important [[Artesian Aquifers]] made of permeable sand and gravel tongues that extend basinward into impermeable quiet-water deposits.
- Recharge to these artesian aquifers occurs by lateral movement from the marginal fanglomerates into the permeable central sediments.
- Extensive lake deposits containing locally important deep aquifers occur in nearly all basins. Their extent, continuity, and relations to other deposits are central to evaluating the [[Ground Water Resources of Southern Arizona]].

**9. Research agenda.** The paper identifies three problems: (1) defining the Gila conglomerate; (2) the depositional and structural history of basin alluvium; and (3) groundwater resources of the basins. Investigation of storage and transmission capacity, and of structural relations within and between the formation and adjacent units, was in progress at the time of writing.
