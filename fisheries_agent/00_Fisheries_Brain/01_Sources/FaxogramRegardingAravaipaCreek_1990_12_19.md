## Executive Summary

This document is an internal faxogram from the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Lower Colorado Region, Boulder City, Nevada) addressed to Dr. W. L. Minckley, a prominent ichthyologist specializing in Sonoran Desert fishes, dated December 1990. The correspondence does not present primary fish survey or biological data; rather, it documents an administrative and interagency conflict surrounding the construction of a **fish barrier on [[Aravaipa Creek]]**, a critical habitat stream for native Sonoran Desert fishes. The memo describes delays attributed to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) in fulfilling regulatory compliance obligations (Endangered Species Act Section 7 consultation and Clean Water Act Section 404 permitting) necessary before the Bureau of Reclamation can proceed with barrier construction. While lacking direct ichthyological data, the document is ecologically significant as a historical record of the regulatory and logistical challenges faced in protecting native desert fish assemblages from invasion by nonnative species in the early 1990s.

## Background & Description

[[Aravaipa Creek]] is a perennial, spring-fed tributary of the San Pedro River within the Gila River drainage of southeastern Arizona. It is widely recognized in the ichthyological literature (including extensive work by [[W. L. Minckley]]) as one of the last strongholds of the native Sonoran Desert fish fauna, historically supporting seven or more native species, several of which are federally listed as threatened or endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

The correspondence centers on a proposed **fish barrier**, a structure typically designed to physically block the upstream movement of nonnative, invasive fish species (e.g., green sunfish, largemouth bass, channel catfish) into reaches of stream occupied by native desert fish populations. Such barriers are a standard conservation tool used by resource agencies to maintain refugia for imperiled native fishes.

The memo indicates that the barrier project required cooperative action between the **Bureau of Reclamation** (funding and construction authority) and the **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service** (regulatory review and biological compliance), operating under a cost-share agreement. The author expresses frustration that FWS had not completed statutory environmental compliance processes — namely **Section 7 consultation under the Endangered Species Act** and **Section 404 review under the Clean Water Act (CWA)** — despite these being the FWS's stated responsibility, resulting in construction delays extending from an estimated three months to nine months.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat significance**: Aravaipa Creek is considered critical habitat for multiple native Sonoran Desert fish species and is frequently cited in desert fish conservation literature as a reference site for relatively intact native fish assemblages, in contrast to most other Gila River basin streams that have been heavily invaded by nonnative fishes.
- **Threat driving barrier construction**: The implicit ecological rationale for the fish barrier is protection of native fish populations from upstream invasion by nonnative, often predatory or competitively superior fish species — a well-documented driver of native fish decline throughout the American Southwest.
- **Regulatory framework**: The delays described illustrate the intersection of aquatic conservation actions with federal environmental law, specifically:
  - [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7 consultation (required when a federal action may affect listed species or their habitat)
  - [[Clean Water Act]] Section 404 (permitting for actions affecting waters of the United States, including in-stream barrier construction)
- **Interagency dynamics**: The memo highlights operational friction between the Bureau of Reclamation (an engineering/water-management agency) and the FWS (the regulatory/biological agency), a recurring theme in mid-20th-century desert fish conservation efforts where infrastructure projects intended to benefit native species were nonetheless subject to the same compliance timelines as harmful development projects.
