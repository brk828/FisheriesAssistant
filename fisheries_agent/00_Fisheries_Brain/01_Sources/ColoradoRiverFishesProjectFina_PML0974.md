# Colorado River Fisheries Project: Spawning and Culture of Humpback Chub (Report No. 3, Section IV)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the front matter and contents of the *Colorado River Fisheries Project Final Report* (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Bureau of Reclamation, Salt Lake City, Utah, April 1982), with one full section reproduced: Report No. 3, Section IV, *Spawning and Culture of Humpback Chub*, by Roger L. Hannan of [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], Boulder City, Nevada (1982). The project aimed to develop techniques for spawning and rearing endangered Colorado River fishes and to supply hatchery-reared test fish for research. The section reports successful induced and natural spawning of wild [[Humpback Chub]] broodstock in 1981, with egg, hatching and early growth data across four incubation temperatures. The broader report covers instream flow modelling, [[Bonytail]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]] culture, [[Razorback Sucker]] spawning, disease surveys, habitat tolerance, striped bass food habits and toxicity testing. Only the contents and this humpback chub section are substantively present in the source.

## Background & Description

The [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) was one of the last freshwater fishes discovered in North America (Miller 1946). It is endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], was known only from the Grand Canyon of Arizona before 1950, and is now found sporadically in canyon reaches of the upper and lower basin. The species is listed as endangered by Arizona, Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, and by the U.S. Department of the Interior (1980).

The 1981 study tested spawning and culture techniques and produced hatchery-reared fish for scientific work. Broodstock comprised:

- **Little Colorado River (LCR):** 14 wild adults, collected May 17 1978, October 17 1979 and June 13 1980 near the confluence with the Colorado River, Grand Canyon National Park, Arizona.
- **Colorado River (CR):** 16 wild adults, collected November 5 1979 at [[Black Rocks]], near Grand Junction, Colorado.

Males showed orange to red-orange breeding colour on the fins and lower sides; females were normally larger and possessed fine tubercles on the head and fins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Declines were attributed to habitat loss from channelization and impoundment, reduced flows below dams, flow fluctuation from dam operations, and [[Hybridization]] with related species (Holden and Stalnaker 1970, 1975; Joseph et al. 1977; Minckley 1973). The author noted that cold water released below dams may contribute to decline, since most eggs and larvae incubated at 12–13 °C died. Hybridization may also occur in nature because [[Bonytail]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] co-occur with humpback chub.

Spawning temperature was a central variable. Spawning was believed to begin near 18 °C (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1979), and the recirculating raceways were warmed to at least 18 °C. Earlier authors suggested June–July spawning in Grand Canyon (Suttkus and Clemmer 1977) and May–June in Desolation Canyon (Holden 1973). This study found that LCR fish spawned earlier (May, at 19–20 °C) than CR fish (June, at 21–22 °C), so lower-basin humpback chub appear to spawn earlier than upper-basin fish. The author estimated spawning temperatures of roughly 19–22 °C.

## Key Findings

**Broodstock and induced spawning (hand stripping)**
- Sixteen wild CR humpback chub were used. Ovulation was induced with [[Carp Pituitary Hormone Induction]] (40 mg acetone-dried carp pituitary, mixed with oxytetracycline) at 4 mg/kg body weight at 24-h intervals (Hannan 1981).
- Fish were anaesthetised with tricaine methanesulfonate (50–100 mg/L) before intraperitoneal injection and held in a tank with flow-through water at 21–22 °C.
- Eight females ovulated 20 h after the first injection; one female failed to ovulate after three injections.
- Eggs averaged 2.7 mm in diameter (range 2.6–2.8 mm), with 51–58 eggs/mL (mean 55).
- Total fecundity was 201,185 eggs, or 51,262 eggs per kg of body weight.

**Natural spawning**
- Fourteen injected CR females were placed in a concrete raceway with cobble substrate (water depth 10–76 cm). Nine females spawned on May 5–6, 1981, within 24 h of injection, at 19–20 °C.
- An estimated 301,000 eggs were spawned naturally. Egg diameters were 2.6–2.9 mm (mean 2.8 mm). Eggs adhered to the cobble and were allowed to hatch on the substrate.

**Incubation and hatching (by water temperature)**

| Incubation temperature | Fertilisation | Hatching began | Hatching completed | Hatch rate | Fry length |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 21–22 °C | Artificial | 102 h | 146 h | 79% | 6.9 mm |
| 19–20 °C | Natural | 115 h | 160 h | 84% | 7.1 mm |
| 16–17 °C | Artificial | 167 h | 266 h | 62% | 6.9 mm |
| 12–13 °C | Artificial | 340 h | 475 h | 12% | 6.9 mm |

- Newly hatched larvae were about 6.9–7.4 mm total length, weighing 2.9–4.2 mg.
- Water quality in the hatchery stayed acceptable (dissolved oxygen about 8.5–10.6 mg/L; pH mostly 8.0–8.7), except pH above 8.5 on several occasions.

**Culture outcomes**
- Natural spawning produced 23,194 swim-up fry and 19,857 fingerlings (83% survival).
- Artificial spawning produced 12,381 swim-up fry and 9,318 fingerlings (75% survival).
- Over 56 days, fish increased 5.2 to 6.9-fold in total length and gained 584.6–883.9 mg.
- Fry fed on [[Zooplankton]] initially, then on a dry commercial trout starter from day 28 onward. Schooling behaviour was observed throughout.

**Recommendations and limitations**
- The author established two propagation methods: natural spawning on cobble substrate and injection followed by hand stripping and artificial fertilisation.
- Cold-water incubation below dams was identified as a likely cause of low hatch rates.
- The author suggested the spawning temperature range of 19–22 °C should guide future broodstock management.
