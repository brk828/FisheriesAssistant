# Swimming Performance of Three Rare Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study measured the prolonged swimming performance of three endemic, threatened [[Colorado River]] fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Using a [[Stamina Tunnel]], the authors determined the [[Fatigue Velocity]] (FV50, the speed at which 50% of test fish fatigue) at 14, 20, and 26 °C. Humpback and bonytail chubs swam better than similarly sized Colorado squawfish. Small squawfish were stronger swimmers than large ones in body lengths per second, but large fish swam faster in absolute terms. The authors conclude that these rare fishes have prolonged swimming abilities similar to other species, and that the data are useful for designing [[Fish Ladders]], [[Culverts]], and [[Water Intakes]], although they are only one part of the information needed for engineering.

## Background & Description

The three species are endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system and are threatened with extinction. The main causes of decline are habitat modification by dams, water depletion, and introduction of exotic species (Minckley 1973; Hickman 1983 as cited in the text). Earlier work covered their biology and habitat preferences (Holden and Wick 1982; Valdez and Clemmer 1982), along with field studies, laboratory studies, and symposia. No previous study had measured their swimming ability, which is needed for designing water projects.

Prolonged swimming speeds are those a fish can sustain for 15 s to 200 min, as distinct from burst speeds or speeds maintainable indefinitely (Webb 1975). The experiment was designed to reproduce the sudden velocity increases a fish meets when entering a culvert or fish ladder. The authors note that swimming data from other species may not reliably predict the capabilities of unstudied species.

**Study animals and rearing**
- Fish were hatched and reared at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (Arizona), except bonytail chubs, which were reared at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico).
- Fish were held at the [[Utah Water Research Laboratory]] in circular tanks with no appreciable current.
- Photoperiod was 14 h light : 10 h dark. Fish were fed commercial trout pellets ad libitum twice daily.
- Water quality: dissolved oxygen 90–100% saturation, pH about 8.0, ammonia below 0.2 mg/L.
- Temperature acclimation to 14, 20, and 26 °C followed [[Richards et al. 1977]] procedures; fish were held at each temperature for at least one week before testing.

**Test groups (mean total length)**
- Colorado squawfish: large (mean 432 mm) and small (mean 104 mm; range 78–138 mm).
- Humpback chub: mean 95 mm (range 87–107 mm).
- Bonytail chub: mean 99 mm (range 90–107 mm).

All fish were subadults or subyearlings, and sex was not determined. The authors reason that sex probably did not affect performance at this stage (Brett 1965).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Engineering relevance**
- Hydroelectric plants and industrial facilities withdraw water through structures that can entrain and impinge young fish. Recommended approach and face velocities at intakes are about 15 (units truncated in the scan; presumably cm/s; Barnes 1976).
- The 120-min FV50 for small squawfish exceeded 35 cm/s at all temperatures, so the tested fish should be able to avoid entrainment. Larvae may be the most vulnerable stage, and no published data exist on their swimming ability.
- Fish passage is needed for the potamodromous Colorado squawfish, which makes spawning migrations of several hundred kilometres (Tyus 1983). Fish ladders have been proposed as a conservation measure because the northern squawfish (*Ptychocheilus oregonensis*) readily uses ladders on the Columbia River (Clay 1961; Zimmer and Bretsch 1960s citation as given in the text).
- Data of this kind help define one of a dozen biological criteria for designing an experimental fish ladder (Trueblood and Drooker 1982), and are needed for culvert design formulas (Watts 1974; Evans and Johnston 1980).

**Comparative species**

- [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*), about 2.1–3.2 BL/s (Kutty and Saunders 1973)
- [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), roughly 2.3–3.0 BL/s (Beamish 1970)
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), about 3.3–5.4 BL/s (Hartman and Dever 1968 as cited)
- [[Coho Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*), about 4.6–5.4 BL/s (Brett et al. 1958)
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*), about 4.1–7.1 BL/s (Bisson and Milleman 1971)

**Temperature context**
Final temperature preference for these species is about 25–27 °C (Bulkley et al. 1981). The authors assume this is near the optimum for most fish functions. Maximum prolonged swimming of other eurythermal species usually occurs between 25 and 30 °C (Beamish 1978).

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- A [[Stamina Tunnel]] was built from two 500-L reservoirs joined by pipes, with a 2 m long Plexiglas swimming chamber of about 20 cm diameter (Thomas et al. 1964 design).
- An electrified screen (5–10 V alternating current) at the upstream end encouraged tiring fish to move back to the test section. An observer shocked tiring fish as needed.
- Tests used 2–4 fish at first; later groups were 9–14 small fish or 4–6 large fish, depending on tunnel size. Fish were acclimated for 5 min, then exposed to a sudden test velocity. Fatigue times were recorded, with tests continuing until fish fatigued or until a chosen period (2 or 120 min) ended.
- FV50 values were calculated by linear interpolation of fitted dose-response curves for percent fatigued versus velocity, using a covariance approach. 95% confidence intervals came from standard deviations and the t statistic.

**Main results**

| Species (mean TL) | FV50 range (BL/s) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| [[Humpback Chub]] (95 mm) | 4.6–5.7 | Higher than squawfish of similar size |
| [[Bonytail Chub]] (99 mm) | 4.7–5.8 | Higher than squawfish of similar size |
| Small Colorado Squawfish (104 mm) | 4.0–4.5 | |
| Large Colorado Squawfish (432 mm) | 2.0–2.3 | Absolute speed about 2.4 times that of small fish |

- 2-min FV50 values were slightly larger than 120-min values for each group. This agrees with [[Brett 1967]] for [[Sockeye Salmon]].
- Velocity significantly affected mean fatigue time for all species (P < 0.01). Length ranges did not significantly affect fatigue time (P > 0.01).
- Temperature significantly affected the performance of small fish (analysis of covariance, P < 0.01) but not of large fish.
- Small squawfish performed better at 26 °C than at 20 °C. Their intermediate result at 14 °C confounded the trend.
- Large squawfish did not improve with temperature. The authors suggest this may reflect a small sample size, or inaccuracy from estimates made without fatigue data between 0% and 100%.
- Humpback and bonytail chubs showed the expected rise in performance with temperature.
- Individual variation was large, especially at intermediate velocities. This likely reflects differences in motivation, stress, and ability within each species.
- Only about 40% of fish fatigued within 2 min at the highest velocity tested (OCR reading; check the original table footnote).

**Interpretation**
- The narrow caudal peduncle and high caudal fin aspect ratio of humpback and bonytail chubs suggest superior swimming ability (Lighthill 1969).
- Prolonged swimming at 20 °C was similar to that of several other fishes (Table 2).
- The authors consider their data conservative for two reasons: the test apparatus is limited, and the fish were not exercised before testing (Webb 1975). Downward bias is therefore likely.
- The main engineering conclusion is that these fish are no weaker than other fishes of similar size. Existing data for other species can supplement this study for fish-passage and entrainment problems.

**Limitations**
- The data are incomplete for most engineering and construction planning. Other aspects of swimming performance and behavior at each life stage still need study.
- Larval swimming is unknown.
