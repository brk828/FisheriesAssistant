# Correspondence Regarding Land Development Threat to Cuatro Ciénegas Valley

#conservation #environmental-policy #endemic-biodiversity #cuatro-cienegas

## Executive Summary

This document contains two pieces of correspondence from September 1987 regarding the proposed "Fraccionamiento Campestre Las Tortugas" (Las Tortugas Country Subdivision) development near [[Cuatro Ciénegas]], [[Coahuila]], [[Mexico]]. The first letter (September 10, 1987) from Bertha Laura G. de Narvéez to Roberto Finep at the Department of Invertebrates, Smithsonian Institution, offers land parcels for sale at $1 USD per square meter, with average lot sizes of 3,600 m². The second letter (September 19, 1987) is a protest to the SEDUE (Secretaría de Desarrollo Urbano y Ecología) Subdelegado opposing this development, citing environmental protection laws and the ecological fragility of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Valley]].

## Background & Description

The [[Cuatro Ciénegas Valley]] is described as a fragile ecosystem with significant biological value. The proposed development site is located 6 kilometers from Cuatro Ciénegas city. The subdivision received authorization from SEDUE (Secretaría de Desarrollo Urbano y Ecología) for land parceling. The development is being marketed to both national and international buyers, including foreign universities and research institutions. The valley is characterized by soils of low fertility and high instability, with its primary value residing in high levels of biological endemism and unique hydrological characteristics.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Cuatro Ciénegas Valley]] is identified as a unique habitat in Mexico with exceptional scientific, educational, and recreational value. The ecosystem contains numerous endemic species and represents part of Mexico's national patrimony. The area's biological resources have potential importance for medicine development, bioinsecticides, and other applications. The valley's hydrological characteristics and biological resources are described as irreplaceable. The fragile soils make construction difficult and environmentally damaging. The ecosystem is under pressure from development despite its ecological significance and the instability of its substrate.

## Key Findings

**Development Proposal Details:**
- Land parcels averaging 3,600 m² offered at $1 USD per square meter
- First sector lots already calculated with drainage areas
- Some lots already sold or reserved (marked in red on accompanying map)
- Development authorized by SEDUE despite environmental concerns

**Legal and Regulatory Issues:**
- Article 7 of the Federal Environmental Protection Law requires environmental impact studies for projects that may cause contamination or environmental deterioration
- Article 16 empowers SEDUE to promote limitation or suspension of developments through technical and scientific justification
- Articles 15 and 38 may provide mechanisms for project suspension
- Environmental impact assessments may have been incomplete or failed to consider critical factors

**Conservation Arguments:**
- Development would cause irreversible changes to a fragile ecosystem
- Valley contains high biological endemism of national importance
- Soils are unsuitable for construction due to fragility and instability
- Tourism potential exists as sustainable alternative to land sales
- Scientific value documented in multiple publications (bibliography referenced but not included)
- Resources should be preserved for present and future generations
- Proper management plan could provide economic benefits to local community through controlled tourism rather than land sales

**Recommendations Made:**
- Halt or suspend the development project
- Conduct proper environmental impact assessment
- Inform potential buyers through press about unsuitability of land for construction
- Develop sustainable tourism plan under SEDUE coordination
- Engage sympathetic institutions for planning and economic support
- Recognize valley as part of national patrimony requiring protection
