## Executive Summary
This news article reports on a landmark agreement to demolish the **Condit Dam**, a 125-foot hydropower dam on the **White Salmon River** in Washington state, which has blocked anadromous fish from historic spawning grounds since 1913. Under the plan, PacifiCorp (the dam's owner) will contribute $17 million toward decommissioning and fishery restoration in exchange for operating the dam for seven more years before removal begins in 2006. The dam's elimination is expected to reopen access to cold, gravel-bedded upstream reaches for **[[Chinook Salmon]]**, **[[Steelhead Trout]]**, **[[Bull Trout]]**, **[[Coastal Cutthroat Trout]]**, and **[[Pacific Lamprey]]**, potentially allowing 5,000–10,000 fish to spawn annually once the river is restored.

## Background & Description
- **[[Chinook Salmon]]** and **[[Steelhead Trout]]**: Reported to reach **40–60 pounds** in this system, indicating a historically large-bodied, robust anadromous population capable of substantial growth prior to blockage of migratory routes.
- Fish currently observed below the dam are largely **hatchery-raised** (released from net pens) rather than wild-origin fish; these hatchery fish are described as **more sickly** than wild counterparts, suggesting morphological/physiological differences in condition, robustness, or disease resistance compared to naturally-spawned populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat blocked**: Cold, pebble/gravel-bedded stretches of the **White Salmon River** upstream of the dam — classic **[[Salmonid Spawning Habitat]]** characterized by clean, oxygenated, cool-water substrate essential for egg incubation.
- **Migratory behavior**: All target species are anadromous or migratory life-history fish that historically moved between the Columbia River system and upstream spawning tributaries; the dam has functioned as a nearly complete barrier for ~90 years.
- **Current fish assemblage**: The river still supports Chinook and steelhead populations below the dam, but these are hatchery-supplemented fish attempting (and failing) to reach ancestral spawning grounds — indicating a fragmented, human-subsidized population rather than a self-sustaining wild run.
- **Broader ecosystem impact**: Dam removal will convert an artificial lentic reservoir back into a lotic river ecosystem, altering hydrology, sediment transport, and littoral habitat (affecting lakeshore property but restoring riverine **[[Fish Passage]]** corridor).
- **Multi-species benefit**: Restoration is expected to benefit not just salmonids but also the ecologically distinct **[[Pacific Lamprey]]**, a jawless anadromous fish with different life-history and substrate requirements, underscoring the dam's role as a barrier for a taxonomically diverse fish community.

## Key Findings
- **Dam specifications**: Condit Dam is 125 feet high, built in 1913, produces 15 megawatts of electricity, and impounds a reservoir that drains via a controlled blast-and-drill removal method at ~10,000 cubic feet per second.
- **Removal timeline**: Demolition begins in 2006 (drilling and blasting), with the dam structure fully removed and lake converted to river within approximately 10 years (by ~2007–2010s per cottage-owner impact timeline).
- **Economic tradeoff**: PacifiCorp opted for a $17 million removal/restoration package over a $28 million fish-passage retrofit (e.g., fish ladders) that regulators would have required to keep the dam operational — illustrating a case where dam removal was economically preferable to fish-passage mitigation.
- **Stakeholder consensus**: Notably, the **National Hydropower Association**, traditionally opposed to dam removal, did not contest this agreement, reflecting a rare alignment of industry, government, and environmental interests.
- **Projected ecological gain**: Estimated 5,000–10,000 fish (spawning individuals) expected to gain access to previously blocked upstream habitat post-removal.
- **Affected species list**: [[Chinook Salmon]], [[Steelhead Trout]], [[Bull Trout]], [[Coastal Cutthroat Trout]], and [[Pacific Lamprey]] are explicitly named as beneficiaries of restored passage.
- **Human impact**: Removal will eliminate the reservoir lake, negatively affecting owners of lakefront vacation properties, who will lose water frontage as the reservoir reverts to a narrower river channel — a socioeconomic consequence tied directly to the ecological restoration effort.
- **Case relevance**: This event is an early, prominent example of the broader **[[Dam Removal]]** movement in the Pacific Northwest aimed at restoring **[[Anadromous Fish]]** runs impacted by 20th-century hydropower infrastructure.
