# Water Balance of the Great Lakes System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1962 paper by J. P. Bruce and G. K. Rodgers (Meteorological Branch, Canada Department of Transport; Great Lakes Institute, University of Toronto) examines the [[Water Balance]] of the [[Great Lakes]] system. Gross annual budgets indicate that about one-third of precipitation over the basin eventually leaves through the [[St. Lawrence River]]. The authors critically assess three methods for estimating precipitation onto the lakes ([[Water Budget Method]], [[Island Gage Networks]], and [[Weather Radar]]) and compute [[Lake Ontario]] evaporation by [[Mass Transfer Method]] and [[Energy Budget Method]]. They adopt a "best estimate" of mean annual evaporation of 2.8 feet. The paper closes with a call for far greater hydrometeorological data collection over the lakes, especially in winter.

## Background & Description

The paper is a hydrometeorological review rather than a biological study; no fish species, aquatic invertebrates or plankton are documented. Its subject is the physical components of lake water budgets, which the authors argue matter for several reasons:

- **Forecasting and regulation:** lake levels and outflows are already measured, but forecasts and gate operation require understanding of the physical factors behind them.
- **Diversions:** the effects of artificial diversions into and out of the system cannot be assessed without knowing natural inflow and outflow.
- **Energy-water linkage:** evaporation is tied to water temperature and therefore to ice formation and extent, relevant to shipping.

Water supplies are inflow from upstream lakes, land runoff, and precipitation on the lake surface. Losses are outflow through outlet channels and evaporation. Groundwater exchange is ignored, following earlier authors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Basin water balance**
- Mean annual precipitation over the 296,000 sq mi drainage basin: 31.6 in (1870–1957, Canadian records); U.S. Lake Survey gives 31.3 in (1900–1958).
- Basin runoff at the International Rapids section of the St. Lawrence: 10.7 in/yr.
- Precipitation ranges from 28–29 in (Lake Superior watershed) to 34–35 in (Lake Erie Basin). Net yield ranges from 12.6 in (Superior) to 10.1 in (Erie).

**Lake Ontario annual budget (Table 1)**
- Runoff from land: 18 in; land precipitation: 33 in.
- Precipitation on lake surface: 28–33 in; evaporation from lake surface: 32 in.
- Inflow from Lake Erie: 205,000 cu ft/s; outflow via St. Lawrence: 241,000 cu ft/s.

**Trends and interrelationships (Lake Erie)**
- Mean Erie water temperature rose from 50.9 °F (decade ending 1928) to 53.2 °F (decade ending 1938), more than twice the rise in air temperature. The authors link this to reduced cloud cover and increased [[Insolation]] during a period of falling precipitation and lake levels.
- Precipitation rose by more than 6 in (about 20%) between the decades ending 1941 and 1951, with rising lake levels while temperatures stayed roughly constant.

**Physical controls on lake precipitation**
- Summer: cool, uniform lake surfaces inhibit shower development, so clouds often ring the lakes while the lake surface stays clear.
- Winter: cold air heated and moistened over open water yields lake-effect snow on lee shores (cited examples: Buffalo and Owen Sound). Flat land next to warm water can receive up to 0.2 in/day of snow.

**Controversy over lake precipitation**
- Earlier gage studies found island precipitation at 84–94% of shore-station values, depending on lake and season.
- Hunt (1958a) reported Lake Ontario precipitation at 79% of shore values (60% in August to 91% in November).
- Kohler (1959) argued that island gage catch correlates with windiness and largely explains the differences.
- Morton and Rosenberg (1959) found shore-station annual precipitation 1.68 in lower than the whole land-area average.

## Key Findings

1. **Basin partition:** about one-third of basin precipitation reaches the St. Lawrence River; the rest is lost to evaporation and transpiration.
2. **Precision problem in water budgets:** a 1% bias in inflow or outflow produces about 0.7 ft of error over Lake Ontario per year, roughly 25% of total precipitation and about one-quarter of annual evaporation. Water-budget methods therefore cannot reliably separate lake precipitation from evaporation.
3. **Gage limitations:** gage catch errors are of the same order (10–15%) as the signal sought, and all of them are negative, so they do not cancel over time. Island gage data show summer catches of 89% of shore values on average; small wind-swept islands caught about 85%.
4. **Weather radar:** a 3-cm Decca radar at Malton Airport (Toronto) gave a standard error of monthly rainfall of 22% at 20–40 miles, 66% at 40–60 miles, and about 94% at 60–80 miles. For October 1959 it could not resolve the lake-versus-land precipitation ratio. The authors judge that more powerful equipment is needed.
5. **Lake Ontario mass transfer:** using the Lake Hefner-based equation and lake/land wind ratios (R_spring = 1.56; R_fall = 1.86; Main Duck Island check 1.68 and 1.97), the annual total is 3.05 ft. The authors consider this at least 6–7% too high, partly because the equation overestimates by about 5% at Lake Mead.
6. **Lake Ontario energy budget:** heat-storage data from 1958–1959 surveys were combined with 1937–1946 surface temperature averages for winter months. The annual total is 2.88 ft, which the authors also judge high (mainly due to a probably overestimated July value).
7. **Best estimate:** mean annual evaporation from Lake Ontario is about **2.8 ft**, reconciling the water budget, mass transfer and energy budget results.
8. **Lake Ontario comparison:** Morton and Rosenberg's water budget with a precipitation ratio of 1 would give 2.71 ft. The Hunt and Kohler mass transfer values are lower, probably because they used land-station data.
9. **Recommendations:** the main gap is the absence of over-water meteorological data. The authors call for recording or telemetering weather buoys and a research vessel large enough for winter work (the Porte Dauphine, a 125-ft ex-Canadian Navy gate-class vessel, had to seek shelter in November gales).
