# Life History of the Black Redhorse (Moxostoma duquesnei) in Missouri

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study describes the life history of the [[Black Redhorse]] (*Moxostoma duquesnei*) in two south-central Missouri Ozark Highland rivers, the [[Niangua River]] and the [[Big Piney River]], using collections made from 1951 to 1957. Scale samples from 1,314 Big Piney and 1,559 Niangua fish were analysed for age, growth and length-weight relationships. Abundance appeared linked to stream discharge, with the greatest proportion of redhorse in streams averaging 500–600 cfs. The paper also documents spawning behaviour on [[Spawning Shoal]] habitat, sexual dimorphism, feeding habits, seasonal movements and population differences linked to harvest intensity.

## Background & Description

The black redhorse is a catostomid sucker restricted to Atlantic and Gulf drainages of North America. It is described as an inhabitant of moderate-sized, cool, clear streams that disappear in excessively silty waters. In Missouri it occurs in the major streams of the south, is absent from the small Ozark Highland streams, and is generally unsuited to impoundments. Little had previously been published on the species, which the author notes is numerically large in these streams.

The study streams rise in the [[Ozark Highland]] and drain northward into the [[Missouri River]] system. The Niangua has a mainstream length of 170 km and a watershed of 1839 km²; the Big Piney has 243 km of mainstream and a watershed of 1989 km². Both have a gradient of about 0.93 m/km and a succession of riffles and pools. Mean annual discharge was 19 m³/s (688 cfs) for the Niangua and 15 m³/s (561 cfs) for the Big Piney.

Fish were collected by drag seine and boat-mounted electric shocker. Lengths are standard length, with conversion factors given for total length.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Abundance and flow:** Across Ozark Highland streams, the proportion of redhorse weight that was black redhorse was highest in streams averaging 500–600 cfs. Ruhr's Tennessee data showed a similar pattern, with peak densities near 550 cfs.
- **Habitat use:** Young-of-the-year were most common among beds of water willow (*Justicia americana*) in quiet pools. Adults fed in schools immediately above or below riffles.
- **Spawning substrate:** Spawning shoals were used intensively only where bottom composition was about 70% fine rubble, 10% coarse rubble and 20% sand and gravel, in water 0.1–0.6 m deep. Low water in 1954 reduced usable shoal area, and a flood on a Tennessee shoal eliminated spawning until bottom sorting restored it.
- **Thermal refuges:** Spring inflows at Bennett Spring Branch (Niangua) and Boiling Springs (Big Piney) warmed downstream reaches, which held redhorse in winter when adjacent areas did not.
- **Competition:** Stomach analyses showed little dietary overlap with [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*). Similar foods in bass fingerlings may reflect bass feeding on drift from sucker activity.
- **Harvest:** Angling removed about 5% of adult fish in the Big Piney versus 2% in the Niangua. Spearing scars were found on 50% of Big Piney fish and 16% of Niangua fish.

## Key Findings

**Spawning and reproduction**
- Spring spawners, with spawning occurring over 4 days in late April; start dates varied by up to 2 weeks. Water temperatures were 13–22 °C during spawning.
- Males defended territories averaging 0.45 m in diameter in 1953 and about 0.2 m in low-water years. Females drifted into the shoal and were clasped between two territorial males.
- Spawning trios lasted 2.0–3.8 s (mean 3.4 s, n = 25). Adults spawned more than once, and there was no parental care of eggs.
- Males were sexually mature at 200 mm (Big Piney) and 150 mm (Niangua); females at 210 mm and 170 mm. No fish younger than age group II were mature.
- Males developed a pinkish mid-lateral band and nuptial tubercles during spawning; females showed no colour change.
- Eggs per female were correlated with body weight, from about 1,357 eggs at 50–99 g to 6,055 at 600–649 g (Niangua, November 1957 sample).

**Age and growth**
- Scale methods were validated for age determination. False annuli occurred in 2.6% of Niangua and 1.6% of Big Piney scale samples.
- The body-scale relationship was best described by two straight lines, with breaks at 170 mm (Niangua) and 230 mm (Big Piney), which the author links to attainment of sexual maturity (170 and 232 mm).
- Big Piney fish grew slightly faster in length through age group III, while the Niangua population showed better growth in older age groups (V–IX).
- Length-weight equations: Niangua log W = −4.58 + 2.94 log L; Big Piney log W = −4.59 + 2.95 log L. Big Piney fish were heavier up to 350 mm; beyond that, Niangua fish were heavier.

**Feeding**
- Black redhorse are bottom feeders that suck in substrate, leaving a trail of silt termed "drift." Stomachs contained many fine sand grains.
- Fish from 65 mm and smaller fed mainly on phytoplankton. Larger young shifted to aquatic insects, with some Cladocera, Copepoda and Nematoda.
- Only 14% of night-collected stomachs were empty, against 36% of day-collected stomachs. No fish eggs were found in 775 stomachs examined.

**Movement and population structure**
- Movements were greatest in spring and fall, with little movement in summer and winter. Tagged adults moved up to 6 miles upstream or about 2 miles downstream to spawning riffles.
- Adults did not return to the same spawning shoal in subsequent years, but tagged fish showed fidelity to summer home pools.
- Fish moved to deep wintering holes (6–15 ft deep) from October through November.
- Sex ratios did not differ from 1:1 overall (240 specimens), but males predominated among sexually mature fish at spawning time.
- The author concludes that black redhorse within a river form a rather homogeneous population.

**Conclusions**
- Stream discharge of 500–600 cfs appears to support the largest black redhorse populations.
- Harvest intensity appears to explain differences in population structure between the two rivers.
- Impoundments and gravel infilling of tributaries are identified as likely threats to population persistence.
