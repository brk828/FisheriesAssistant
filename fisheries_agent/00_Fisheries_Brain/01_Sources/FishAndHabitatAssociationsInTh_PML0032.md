# Fish and Habitat Associations in the Gila River Basin: Improving Monitoring Protocols

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department Technical Report (NGTR 187, January 2002, by [[Linda J. Allison]]) evaluates whether monitoring data on fish assemblages in the [[Gila River]], [[Salt River]] and [[San Pedro River]] can detect changes in species abundance and relate them to shifts in habitat availability. Data from 1995 to 1999 were used to describe macrohabitat availability, species–habitat associations (logistic regression), canonical correspondence ordinations, and pairwise species associations. The authors conclude that habitat measurements collected alongside fish sampling add little explanatory power for most species, that stratifying analyses by macrohabitat will not meaningfully improve trend estimates, and that discharge should be tracked annually using USGS gage data. The work was prepared for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] under a cooperative agreement related to the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) and its [[Biological Opinion]].

## Background & Description

The [[Central Arizona Project]], operating since 1993, allowed potential transport of non-native fishes and other aquatic organisms from the [[Colorado River]] into central and southern Arizona. The 1994 [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion found that CAP water delivery would jeopardize four threatened or endangered fishes: [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Reasonable and Prudent Alternative No. 2 required the Bureau of Reclamation to develop a monitoring plan to determine baseline composition and distribution, monitor impacts of non-native fishes, and describe movement of non-natives from CAP canals.

A draft plan (Clarkson 1996) was adopted as the [[Long-Term Monitoring Protocol]]. A prior power analysis (Allison 2000, NGTR 170) found that count variability could be reduced by stratifying counts by macrohabitat. The protocol calls for single-pass quantitative sampling at three 200 m stations per reach, with macrohabitats (pools, riffles, runs, isolated pools) as sampling units. Eight reaches were sampled: three on the San Pedro, four on the Gila and one on the Salt. Sampling was centred on early November each year. ## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fish assemblages in the basin face two main pressures: non-native fishes moving from CAP canals and changes in habitat due to upstream dams and water withdrawals. Discharge in the Salt River is regulated by [[Stewart Mountain Dam]] and the Gila by [[Coolidge Dam]], and groundwater pumping also alters flows. Flows were measured at four USGS gages (San Pedro at Charleston; Gila below Coolidge Dam; Gila at Kelvin; Salt below Stewart Mountain Dam), with each gage assigned to a set of reaches. Substrate was classified from clay (<0.24 mm) to bedrock (>4096 mm) using categories that were treated as an interval variable.

The Salt River had few riffles and more pools than the Gila or San Pedro, and the frequency of macrohabitat types was consistent within reaches across years but not across reaches within a river. Native species were more associated with pools and non-pool habitats such as riffles, while several non-natives were more common in runs and deeper habitats.

## Key Findings

- **Macrohabitat availability:** 379 macrohabitats were visited in 1995–1999 (Table 4: San Pedro 191, Gila 155, Salt 33). Only 8 isolated pools were recorded, so they were excluded from statistical tests. Macrohabitat frequency differed between rivers (p<0.0005), driven by fewer runs and more pools on the Salt River. Within rivers, relative frequency was consistent between years (p = 0.287, 0.466, 1.000 for San Pedro, Gila and Salt).
- **Effect of discharge (10-fold increase):** The number of pools fell to about one-fifth of its previous level (p<0.0005), riffles fell by about one-half (p = 0.026), and runs rose about 1.6-fold (p = 0.097). Pool depth increased 56%, and substrate diameter was about one-fifth as large. Run length increased 88% and run depth 53%.
- **Species–habitat associations (logistic regression):** Longfin dace, Sonora suckers, common carp and desert suckers were more common in runs than pools. Largemouth bass, green sunfish and black bullheads were most common in pools. Red shiners were more prevalent in shallower macrohabitats. Longfin dace and fathead minnows occurred more often in macrohabitats with large substrate.
- **Canonical correspondence analysis:** The full model explained 18.8% of total variance on Axis 1 (environment–species correlation R = 0.90). Axis 1 was linked to Reach 2 on the San Pedro. Axis 2 was linked to Reach 1, and Axis 3 to Reach 8 on the Salt River, where [[Mosquitofish]] (MISA) dominated. Reach information alone captured most of the species pattern, so adding macrohabitat measurements did not improve predictions for most species.
- **Model fit:** Biserial correlations ranged from 0.320 (GAAF) to 0.724 (CYCA). Nagelkerke R² ranged from 0.138 (GAAF) to 0.606 (CYCA). Habitat features poorly predicted the occurrence of some species, including GAAF and ICPU.
- **Pairwise associations:** Of 12 common species, [[Sonora Sucker]] (AGCH) had the most significant negative associations (five), while several co-occurrence correlations were positive. Odds of finding uncommon species were modestly predicted by the presence of common species.
- **Monitoring recommendations:**
  - Stratifying analyses by macrohabitat will not improve trend estimates. Coefficients of variation dropped by 15% or more for some species, but those species were not among the hard-to-estimate trend species.
  - Annual flow descriptions from USGS gages should be developed to track long-term changes in discharge.
  - Models should be built at different units of analysis (reach, station, macrohabitat) for discharge, species counts and habitat dimensions.
  - The author suggests that separate models of discharge and species abundance, and a designed experiment with high- and low-flow years, would better isolate effects.
  - Habitat measurements are expensive and of limited value for species trends. Continued collection should be weighed against the value of the information gained.
