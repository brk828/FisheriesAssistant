# Golf Course Refuge: Emerald Canyon Lakes Shelter Endangered Colorado River Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This magazine feature from *Golf Course Management* (November 1995) describes how Emerald Canyon Golf Course, a county-owned course beside the [[Colorado River]] near Parker, Arizona, was converted into a temporary habitat for two endangered native fish, the [[Bonytail Chub]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]]. The project was a joint effort between the course and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] through the Parker Fisheries Resource Office. The article explains why the "big river fish" of the Colorado River are declining, outlines the steps taken to prepare the lakes (screening, fish removal, and [[Rotenone]] treatment), and reports early growth results for stocked fish.

## Background & Description

**Emerald Canyon Golf Course** sits in the mountains and canyons cut by the river, and several holes have views of the Colorado River. General manager and head pro Steve Benton described the course's lakes as a good fit for native fish habitat once the Fish and Wildlife Service made contact in summer 1994.

The article focuses on four "big river fish" that the Parker office considers endangered:

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)**: Could reach 6 feet and 100 pounds. The last known wild individual was caught in 1972 in the Grand Canyon.
- **[[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)**: Named in the article, but no quantitative data are given.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)**: Normal adult size is 14 to 16 inches. The article states it is "essentially extinct" in the wild.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)**: Can reach about 3 feet in length.

Because of their large size and distinctive appearance, these fish would be noticed if a sport angler caught one.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Per the article, the Colorado River was originally a harsh system where few fish species could adapt. Federal wildlife officials identified four main threats:

- **Dams**: Block historic spawning runs.
- **Controlled water releases**: Have destroyed spawning sites.
- **[[Hypolimnion Release]]**: Cold water drawn from the bottom of reservoirs interferes with reproduction. [[Chuck Minckley]] said the fish need warmer water to spawn successfully and for normal egg and larval (fry) development.
- **Non-native fish**: Many exotic and introduced species eat the eggs and young of native fish.

Minckley also described the historic abundance of bonytail: about 50 years earlier they were abundant in the lower Colorado, but it took 25 years to catch 30 wild individuals. Razorback sucker numbers in the lower basin also fell sharply. The article gives two counts: about 66,000 in 1984 and about 22,000 by 1994.

### Lake Conditions and Preparation

Emerald Canyon's lakes receive water from the Colorado River, so screens were installed on the intake pipes to keep other fish out. The preparation sequence was:

1. Installation of intake screens, directed by superintendent Florencio Carrillo.
2. Removal of existing fish, to create a predator-free environment for the endangered species.
3. Electrofishing by Fish and Wildlife Service staff, who stunned fish from a boat and netted them for relocation to the Colorado River.
4. Seining (dragging a large net) to catch remaining fish.
5. Application of Rotenone, which removes dissolved oxygen from the water and suffocates fish. Minckley noted it is toxic only to gill-breathing organisms and is not harmful to people or other animals. It was used to eliminate minnows and very small fish that could prey on the stocked species.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and growth results**

- **Bonytails**: Young fish were supplied by the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Because the species is so rare, the fingerlings came from descendants of only six bonytail chubs brought to Dexter in 1981. Fish stocked in October 1994 reached 10 to 12 inches, and some were released into Lake Harrasu.
- **Razorbacks**: Stocked in July (less than 2 inches long). By September 1 they measured 5 to 7 inches. Once large enough, they are to be harvested and fitted with a [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag]] (a 10-digit implanted ID) in the body cavity.

**Conservation strategy**

- The Fish and Wildlife Service has been producing large numbers of fingerlings in hatcheries.
- Wild razorback larvae were recently found to be harvestable from [[Lake Mohave]], a large reservoir on the Arizona-Nevada border.
- Earlier attempts to stock fingerlings in the Verde and Salt rivers failed: the fish "just disappeared," apparently eaten by other fish.
- Minckley concluded that rearing fish in predator-free habitat lets them grow larger before release, improving survival in the wild. The stated aim is to maintain large populations and prevent extinction.

**Stakeholder collaboration**

- Agreement required approval by the La Paz County manager and board of supervisors, because the course is county-owned. Benton described the process as "quite a bit more complex than you might imagine."
- Benton stated the course feels a duty to protect the river's beauty and biological diversity.

**Limitations of the source**

- The article is a popular-press piece. It reports no formal sample sizes, controlled experiments, or survival statistics for the stocked fish, and only gives early growth observations.
- The article notes the stocked fish were kept in the lakes as a predator-free nursery, but it does not report their final outcome.
