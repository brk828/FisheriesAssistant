# Pelleted Dry Food as a Total Diet for Trout

#ichthyology #aquaculture #Salmonidae #fish-nutrition #hatchery-management

## Executive Summary

This experimental study compared an all-pellet dry food diet with a traditional pellet-plus-meat diet for [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] in New York State hatcheries from November 1957 through April 1958. Nine pairs of hatchery ponds across six facilities were used to evaluate growth, mortality, food conversion, and cost-effectiveness. The all-pellet ration (based on [[Cortland Dry Meal]] formula No. 6) produced statistically equivalent results to the meat-supplemented diet in terms of weight gain, mortality, and conversion, while offering substantial economic advantages through reduced labor and food costs. The pellet diet cost $8.22 per 100 pounds versus $10.50 for the meat-supplemented diet, with food cost per pound of fish gained at 32.9 cents versus 65.2 cents respectively.

## Background & Description

Prior to the 1950s, hatchery trout were fed ground meat or ground meat with fine-ground dried cereals and animal products—rations that were expensive to purchase and prepare and cumbersome to handle. [[New York State Conservation Department]] had been using dry meals as components of trout diets for over 20 years before 1951, when pellet usage began. However, no agreement existed among hatchery foremen regarding optimal pellet percentages in total diet composition.

The experimental pellet formula (Table 1) contained: 24% wheat flour middlings, 24% whitefish meal, 16% cottonseed meal, 7% dry skim milk, 10% dry brewers yeast, 17% distillers solubles, and 3% cod liver oil. Following the study, the formula was modified to increase distillers solubles to 21% and reduce dry skim milk to 3% to achieve 60% total grain products for cheaper freight rates without nutritional loss.

Test subjects were yearling and 2-year-old [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) and [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) held at normal densities (not exceeding 2 pounds of fish per cubic foot of water) in 18 ponds at six hatcheries: [[Adirondacks Hatchery]], [[Crown Point Hatchery]], [[Johnstown Hatchery]], [[Lake George Hatchery]], [[Rome Hatchery]], and [[Warrensburg Hatchery]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Fish were maintained under standard hatchery conditions with temperature variations among facilities. The experiment was conducted during winter months (November through April) when water temperatures were predominantly below 40°F, a period when food conversions tend to be higher than annual averages. Fish were fed according to the [[New York State Feeding Chart]], with meat fed at 100% level and pellets at 50% level.

Control groups received varying meat-supplemented diets depending on hatchery: combinations of pellets (26-63%), frozen beef liver (0-19%), pork spleen (21-53%), and frozen shrimp heads (0-28%). Salt (1-2%) was added as a binder to meat portions. Shrimp was specifically fed to brook trout to improve coloration.

Stocking densities and pond conditions reflected normal operational parameters, ensuring results would be applicable to standard hatchery practices rather than experimental conditions.

## Key Findings

**Weight Gain Performance:**
- Sum of percentage gains for control lots: 447.1%
- Sum of percentage gains for experimental (all-pellet) lots: 435.9%
- Analysis of variance showed difference not significant (F = 0.05)
- Wide variations in percentage gain between hatcheries occurred primarily due to temperature differences
- Within-hatchery differences between diet types were relatively small

**Mortality Rates:**
- Total percentage mortality for experimental lots: 46.9%
- Total percentage mortality for control lots: 50.0%
- Analysis of variance showed difference not significant (F = 0.06)
- Mortality values represent 4-5 month period
- Three lots at [[Rome Hatchery]] showed inventory overruns indicating counting errors

**Critical Finding - Extended Feeding Limitation:**
At [[Lake George Hatchery]], when the experiment was extended into May, the all-pellet lot began to break down with rapidly rising mortality on May 11. When switched to a meat diet (50% pork spleen, 50% beef liver), response was immediate—mortality halted and fish showed good gain for remainder of month. This suggests a temporal limit for continuous pellet feeding exists, though broader conclusions require experimental repetition. Fish pathology examination found no specific tissue breakdown; possible anemia in dying specimens was suspected.

**Food Conversion:**
- Average conversion for pellet-fed lots: 4.0
- Average conversion for control lots: 6.2
- Analysis of variance showed difference not significant (F = 2.55)
- Both conversions exceeded the annual statewide hatchery conversion of 3.5 for 1957-58
- Poor conversions attributed to winter feeding during sub-40°F temperatures and suspected overfeeding based on [[New York State Feeding Chart]] values

**Economic Analysis:**
- All-pellet diet cost: $8.22 per 100 pounds
- Meat-supplemented diet cost: $10.50 per 100 pounds
- Food cost per pound of fish gained (pellet): 32.9 cents
- Food cost per pound of fish gained (meat-supplemented): 65.2 cents
- All-pellet diet achieved 50% cost reduction per pound of fish gained
- Additional labor savings realized with pellet-only feeding

**Operational Implications:**
The experiment demonstrated that New York State trout hatcheries could successfully feed an all-pellet diet to yearling [[Brook Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]] from November through April, with substantial economic benefits through reduced food costs and labor requirements. The formula used was based on publicly available specifications suitable for competitive bidding, unlike proprietary commercial pellets with "closed" formulae.
