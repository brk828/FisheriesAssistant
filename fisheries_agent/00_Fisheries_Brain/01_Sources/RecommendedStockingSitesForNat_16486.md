## Executive Summary

This 1987 internal memorandum from W.L. Minckley and Paul C. Marsh to the Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge (NWR) file documents recommended stocking locations for three federally significant native fish taxa: the [[Bonytail Chub]], the [[Razorback Sucker]], and the [[Desert Pupfish]]. Recommendations stem from field surveys conducted in April and October 1986 across a network of artificial stock tanks and small impoundments on the refuge, located in southern Arizona. The document is a management/conservation planning record rather than a primary morphological or ecological research paper, but it provides valuable site-specific habitat allocation data for three imperiled [[Sonoran Desert Fishes]] undergoing early reintroduction efforts.

## Background & Description

The memo itself contains no direct morphological descriptions or measurements of the fishes. However, life-stage stocking designations imply size/age class considerations relevant to each species' biology:

- **[[Bonytail Chub]]** (*Gila elegans*) — stocked as **adults**, consistent with the species' known vulnerability as juveniles to predation and competition from non-native fishes; adult stocking is a common strategy to bypass high early-life mortality bottlenecks in [[Cyprinidae]].
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — stocked as **fry**, the early larval/juvenile stage, suggesting these sites were selected as rearing habitats free of predatory competitors, a critical need given the species' characteristic humped nuchal morphology develops with age and does not confer protection at early stages.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*) — stocked at **all size classes**, reflecting the species' short generation time, high fecundity, and tolerance of small, harsh, isolated habitats typical of [[Cyprinodontidae]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type:** All recommended stocking sites are artificial or semi-artificial **stock tanks** ("tanks") and small reservoirs on rangeland within the refuge — shallow, often turbid, thermally variable, and subject to seasonal drying, mimicking historic ephemeral desert aquatic habitats.
- **Site distribution:**
  - *Bonytail chub*: Agua Hito Tank 2 North, Hito Tank
  - *Razorback sucker*: Rock Tank, Suffrido Tank, Tequila Tank (alternate), Mormon Lake
  - *Desert pupfish*: Little Mormon Tank, two unnamed clear tanks near refuge Headquarters
- **Habitat suitability rationale:** Site selection presumably reflects water permanence, absence of predatory nonnative fishes (e.g., [[Gambusia affinis]], centrarchids), and appropriate physicochemical conditions (temperature, salinity tolerance) matching each species' ecological requirements.
- **Conservation context:** All three species are native to the lower Colorado River basin and Gila River drainage and were, by the 1980s, in serious decline due to habitat alteration, damming, and introduction of nonnative competitors/predators — a broader pattern affecting many [[Southwestern Native Fishes]].
- The use of isolated, fishless refugial tanks represents an early example of **"ark" site management**, a conservation strategy still used today for endangered desert fish species to establish insurance populations outside their historic mainstem ranges.

## Key Findings

- Three federally recognized native fish species — [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Desert Pupfish]] — were recommended for translocation stocking into isolated refuge tanks based on 1986 field surveys.
- Stocking life stages were tailored to species-specific vulnerability: adults for bonytail chub, fry for razorback sucker, and all sizes for desert pupfish.
- A total of at least **8 distinct water bodies** across the Buenos Aires NWR were identified as suitable refugia, spanning multiple sections/townships (T21S–T22S, R11E–R13E approx., per Arizona PLSS notation).
- This record reflects early (1980s) proactive conservation stocking efforts predating many later formalized recovery plans for these species under the [[U.S. Endangered Species Act]].
- The memo underscores the historical role of the Buenos Aires NWR as a refugium network for imperiled Sonoran Desert ichthyofauna.
