# The Upper St. Lawrence River Ice Fishery

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Esocidae #fisheries-management #creel-census

## Executive Summary
This paper presents findings from a two-winter (1958-59 and 1959-60) creel census conducted on the upper 45 miles of the [[St. Lawrence River]] in New York State, focusing on the ice fishery for [[Northern Pike]]. The study was initiated to address local complaints regarding alleged overharvesting of pike, particularly large females, by non-resident anglers. Researchers checked 1,280 anglers who fished 5,471 hours and caught 673 legal pike (one fish per 8.1 hours fished). The data indicated that fishing pressure, while increasing due to greater leisure access, was relatively light across the study area as a whole, largely due to winter inaccessibility of most habitat. The study found no evidence supporting the claim of overharvest, validating the five-tip-up regulation limit in place at the time.

## Background & Description
Data collected focused on legal-sized specimens (minimum legal length 20 inches) captured via hook-and-line/tip-up methods.

*   **[[Northern Pike]] (Esox lucius):**
    *   **Females:** Averaged 25.5 inches in length across both seasons, with a maximum recorded length of 36.0 inches (1958-59) and 34.0 inches (1959-60). Minimum catch length was 20.0 inches (legal limit).
    *   **Males:** Averaged 22.9–23.1 inches in length, ranging from 20.0 to 29.0 inches in both seasons.
    *   **Sexing Difficulty:** Researchers noted that external sexing was unreliable via visual inspection alone; dissection of gonads was required as females in poor physical condition often externally resembled males.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The study area comprises the upper 45 miles of the St. Lawrence River, characterized by the "Thousand Islands" region and numerous large, weedy bays that provide prime pike habitat.

*   **Habitat Accessibility:** A significant portion of the total pike habitat was inaccessible to ice fishermen due to winter conditions (deep snow, unsafe ice), naturally limiting exploitation pressure regardless of angler demand.
*   **Species Interaction:** The paper raises unresolved questions regarding interspecific competition and population dynamics between [[Northern Pike]], [[Smallmouth Bass]] (Micropterus dolomieu)—noted as the primary game fish—and [[Muskellunge]] (Esox masquinongy). Specifically, it questions whether a large pike population suppresses "pan fish" (preventing stunting) while simultaneously limiting the population/success of the more prized Muskellunge through resource competition.
*   **Population Structure:** It remains unknown whether the pike constitute a single homogenous population moving freely throughout the river system, or if discrete, localized sub-populations exist within specific bays (which would render those specific bays more vulnerable to localized overfishing).
*   **Forage Base:** [[Yellow Perch]] (Perca flavescens) were the only other species targeted, though this was typically incidental to primary pike fishing efforts using hand lines.

## Key Findings
*   **Total Catch:** 673 legal pike were caught by checked anglers over the two-year period, translating to a catch rate of 1 pike per 8.1 hours of fishing effort.
*   **Sex Ratio Skew:** A strong skew toward females was observed in the catch. The overall Male:Female ratio was 1:2.4 (150 males to 363 females), suggesting females are either more abundant or more susceptible to capture during winter months.
*   **Seasonal Sex Ratio Stability:** Contrary to the initial complaints driving the study, the data did not support the hypothesis that female pike were caught in significantly higher proportions toward the end of the season (March) compared to earlier in the season. The ratio during the last month (1:2.3) was statistically similar to the ratio for the rest of the season.
*   **Angler Origin:** The fishery is not primarily local. 63% of anglers checked drove more than 40 miles one-way to fish the area, with many traveling from neighboring states.
*   **Regulatory Efficacy:** The legal limit of five tip-ups per angler (reduced from 15 prior to 1951) appeared effective in preventing localized overcrowding/monopolization of prime bays and keeping the harvest sustainable.
*   **Bait Preference:** Minnows were the standard and most effective bait; the condition and size of the minnow bait appeared to directly influence catch success rates.
*   **Management Implication:** Based on the light fishing pressure relative to the total pike habitat and the stable sex ratios, the study concluded that existing regulations were satisfactory and current ice fishing pressure did not constitute a conservation threat to the overall Upper [[St. Lawrence River]] pike population.
