# Native Fish Strategy for the Murray-Darling Basin 2003–2013

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-freshwater-fish

## Executive Summary

The [[Native Fish Strategy]] is a ten-year framework, published by the [[Murray-Darling Basin Commission]] (MDBC) in May 2003, for rehabilitating native fish communities across the [[Murray-Darling Basin]]. Its overall goal is to return native fish populations to **60 per cent of estimated pre-European settlement levels after 50 years** of implementation. The Strategy argues that native fish populations, currently estimated at about **10 per cent** of pre-European levels, are unsustainable and will fall to about 5 per cent without intervention. It proposes six driving actions, 13 objectives and an integrated, catchment-by-catchment investment plan, supported by monitoring, auditing and review. The document is a policy and planning statement, not a research paper, and its quantitative claims are largely expert estimates.

## Background & Description

The Murray-Darling Basin covers more than one million square kilometres, about one-seventh of Australia, and is one of the largest catchments in the world. Its unique fauna evolved under long periods of drought and sudden floods, and many native fish depend on specific flow regimes. The health of fish populations is treated as a primary indicator of the health of the Basin's rivers.

The Strategy is part of the [[Integrated Catchment Management]] (ICM) Policy and was developed through consultation with agencies, interest groups and regional communities. It builds on the 1991 MDBC Fish Management Plan for the Murray River, extending its principles Basin-wide. Its vision is a Basin that "sustains viable fish populations and communities throughout its rivers." It targets the causes, not just symptoms, of decline and provides a framework rather than prescribing specific practices.

The document also gives species-level context. [[Murray Cod]], [[Trout Cod]], [[Macquarie Perch]] and [[Silver Perch]] are flagship species, and [[Golden Perch]] and [[Short-Headed Lamprey]] illustrate long-distance migration. Table 2 lists the status of the native fish fauna (about 35 native species; roughly 15 to 16 classed as threatened under state or national lists, with inconsistent counts across sections). Alien species, including [[Carp]], [[Redfin Perch]], [[Gambusia]], goldfish, trout and [[Tilapia]], are a major pressure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Strategy identifies **eight key threats** (Table 1): flow regulation; habitat degradation; lowered water quality; barriers; alien species; exploitation; diseases (including [[Epizootic Haematopoietic Necrosis Virus]]); and translocation and stocking.

- **Flow regulation:** Dams, weirs and diversions have changed natural flood and drying cycles, reduced seasonal flows and favoured carp and other alien species. Environmental flows ("any managed change in a river flow pattern intended to maintain or improve river health") are central to the response.
- **Thermal pollution:** Cold water released from storages affects spawning, growth and survival of warm-water native species. Sudden temperature changes can kill eggs, larvae and juveniles. The problem is considered greater in the southern Basin, south of the Lachlan River.
- **Barriers:** Agencies have recognised more than 1,600 barriers in the Basin, and only a small number have mitigation in place. About 80 per cent of Basin river flow is diverted, and an estimated two-thirds of native fish are lost onto farmland and farm dams.
- **Habitat:** Billabongs, wetlands and riverbanks are degraded, and river snags important for breeding have been removed. Resnagging is a key remedy.
- **Water quality:** Salinity, turbidity, nutrients, pesticides and blue-green algal blooms interact with flow changes.

Fish also link to human values. Declines affect recreational angling, commercial fisheries and [[Tourism]]. The Strategy notes that fish decline acts as a "canary in the coal-mine" warning for river ecosystem function.

## Key Findings

**Status of native fish**

- Native fish populations are estimated at about **10 per cent** of pre-European levels. An [[Expert Panel]] concluded that abundance and diversity are at roughly 10 per cent of their status 200 years ago and still declining.
- Without intervention, levels are likely to fall to **5 per cent** over the next 40 to 50 years.
- Localised extinctions include trout cod, Murray cod and Macquarie perch from the Mitta Mitta River for 100 km downstream of Dartmouth Dam.
- Silver perch passage through Euston Weir fell by **93 per cent** between 1940 and 1990.
- Commercial Murray cod catch in NSW fell from about 74 tonnes per year (late 1940s) to 9.5 tonnes per year (early 1990s).
- Native fish make up just **4.4 per cent** of the total recreational catch in the River Murray region.
- Alien species make up about a quarter of the Basin's fish species. Carp make up an estimated **60 to 90 per cent** of fish biomass at many sites, reaching densities of up to one carp per square metre of river surface.
- The Strategy warns that anecdotal improvements in angler catches for large species (Murray cod, golden perch) do not indicate overall community health, as small species such as gudgeons, galaxiids and hardyheads are declining rapidly.

**Strategic response**

- **Goal:** 60 per cent of pre-European levels after 50 years, covering abundance, biomass and distribution of all species. The goal does not relate to diversity; diversity is implicitly 100 per cent (no extinctions).
- **Modelling:** Single interventions are questionable beyond about **25 per cent** of pre-European levels. Integrated action, including environmental flows, habitat rehabilitation, cold water mitigation, land-use improvement, fish passage, Habitat Management Areas and alien fish control, is judged necessary to reach 60 per cent.
- **Timeframe:** Cold water abatement may take 5 to 10 years to deliver benefits. Habitat recovery may take decades.
- **Investment:** Funding should balance quick-return actions (fish passage, cold water mitigation) with long-term actions (habitat restoration, environmental flows).

**Six driving actions** (addressing 13 objectives)

1. Rehabilitating fish habitat (objectives 1–8)
2. Protecting fish habitat (objectives 1–8)
3. Managing riverine structures (objectives 4–8)
4. Controlling alien fish species (objectives 8–9)
5. Protecting threatened native fish species (objectives 6 and 10)
6. Managing fish translocation and stocking (objectives 9–12)

All actions contribute to objective 13: community and partner ownership.

**Management actions and examples**

- Establish [[Riverine Management Zones]] with management plans, and prioritise them for action.
- Establish [[Demonstration Reaches]] and a Basin-wide network of [[Habitat Management Areas]].
- Implement a Basin-wide [[Fish Passage]] program, with [[Tomrmbarry Fishway]] cited as a model: it opened 350 km of river and passed over 20,000 native fish in its first two years.
- Build thermal mitigation (such as variable-level outlets) into new and existing dam infrastructure.
- Investigate [[Daughterless Carp Technology]], which is expected to take at least 7 years to refine and another 20 to 30 years to take effect.
- Prepare recovery plans for threatened species, such as silver perch and freshwater catfish.

**Documented successes**

- [[Resnagging]] at 14 sites on the River Murray added over 300 river red gum snags, with native species such as Murray cod, trout cod and golden perch favouring the snags while carp did not.
- After [[Googong Dam]] was built in 1978, 57 adult Macquarie perch were transported upstream past a waterfall, and a breeding population is now established.
- At [[Pilby Creek Wetland]] (about 17 ha, South Australia), community-led carp removal and screening since 1993 improved water quality and returned some bird, insect and plant species.

**Targets (indicators by 2013)**

- Aquatic structural habitat values increased by 20 per cent.
- 80 per cent of remaining wetland habitats with restored floodplain links.
- Water quality meeting 90 per cent of biological requirements.
- 50 per cent of migratory pathways and habitat reinstated.
- Distribution and abundance of all exotic species reduced by 30 per cent.
- 90 per cent of key partners actively engaged; 50 per cent of the general community aware and supportive.

These targets have not yet been assessed for economic or social impacts.

**Funding and governance**

- The Basin's [[The Living Murray Initiative]] includes a $150 million program of structural and operational measures.
- About **10 per cent** of the total Strategy budget is allocated to monitoring, evaluation and review.
- Recommended: a new inter-State management and science committee; an external audit in 2007 and 2012; and planning for a 2014–24 strategy.

**Data caveat**

The document's counts are internally inconsistent. For example, the status section states that 8 of 15 species are nationally threatened, while the summary table and foreword cite 16 of 35 species threatened under state listings and eight of 35 nationally threatened. Readers should check these figures against primary sources before reuse.
