# Johnny Darter (*Etheostoma nigrum*) Spawning in Captivity: A Hobbyist Account

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This short hobbyist article by Nancy Garcia of the [[Darter Study Group]] describes the capture and aquarium spawning of the [[Johnny Darter]] (*[[Etheostoma Nigrum]]*), a small percid collected from [[Druce Lake]], a 75-acre glacial lake in northeastern Illinois. Twelve wild-caught darters were kept in a 20-gallon tank from fall 1978. Through winter and spring, two dominant males guarded caves and the females laid eggs on the undersides of rocks. Spawning continued for at least six weeks, and fry were reared to about 2 inches by seven months. The piece is followed by an editorial note recommending further reading and an outline for writing breeding articles for the newsletter *American Currents*.

## Background & Description

- **Origin of the fish:** The author began keeping [[Tropical Fish]] for eight years before deciding in fall 1978 to "go native." She collected small bottom fish with a 7 x 9 inch aquarium net while observing [[Game Fish]] in the lake. She later identified the unfamiliar fish as Johnny darters.
- **Appearance:** Females and juveniles were pale tan with black saddles on the back, small "w"-shaped side markings forming blotches along the [[Lateral Line]]. Dominant males turned warm yellow-brown with dark vertical side bands, a black head, and black fins.
- **Lake setting:** Druce Lake is mostly shallow, reaching about 60 feet at its deepest point. It is fairly weedy, with some silt over an original sand and gravel bottom. Its game fish include [[Largemouth Bass]], sunfish, [[Yellow Perch]], yellow bass, and [[Northern Pike]]. Minnows, carp, gar, and bowfin are also present. In late fall, when the bass moved to deeper water, darters were visible resting in the open in shallow water.
- **Aquarium setup:** The 20-gallon long tank sat on the floor, with natural gravel, flat rocks spaced several inches apart with a spanning rock to form caves, a few plants, and a large box filter. The author used little filtration and aeration because she judged the darters came from a quiet lake.
- **Tankmates:** Two small [[Bluegill]], two [[Redbelly Dace]], four bluntnose minnows, a pair of [[Stickleback]], and a small white sucker.
- **Diet:** Live [[Brine Shrimp]], [[Tubifex Worms]], and [[Chaoborus Larvae]] (glass worms).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water chemistry and temperature**
- Tank water came from a well at pH 7.8 and 10 dH, described as similar to the lake water.
- The fish room was only partially heated. Water temperature fell to a low of 48°F over winter and rose to the mid-50s°F by late February, when territorial behavior and spawning began.

**Habitat and behavior**
- Darters held territories in caves. Spawning took place on the underside of rocks, where the author observed eggs attached to cave roofs. The rock undersides were only about 3/4 inch above the gravel.
- The sticklebacks in the same tank spawned twice in early winter. The male guarded and mended the nest and fanned the eggs, but each nest disintegrated at about two weeks, when hatching was expected. The author suspected bad eggs or cannibalism by the male. The sticklebacks were removed once the darters became territorial, because their territories overlapped.
- Other tankmates sometimes ate the fry, so the author moved egg-bearing rocks to separate containers.

**Relevant literature cited by the author and editor**
- Bob Goldstein's article on [[Rainbow Darter]] spawning (FAMA, Nov. 1978) describes gravel spawning. The author notes that Johnny darters differ by spawning on rock undersides.
- The editor cites "Life History of the Eastern Johnny Darter" (Trans. Am. Fish. Soc. 101(1): 80–88) as a source on the species' life history in cold and polluted water.

## Key Findings

1. **Spawning site:** Johnny darters spawned on the underside of rocks, not in gravel as the author initially expected. Eggs were attached to cave roofs.
2. **Spawning pattern:** Both dominant males spawned, and each female appears to have spawned more than once. Spawning continued for at least 6 weeks. The author did not establish the exact number of spawns.
3. **Spawning behavior:** Spawning pairs were observed upside down against the cave roof. The male left to defend the cave while the female remained on her back on a protruding rock.
4. **Incubation:** Eggs were transparent, so developing embryos were visible. Hatching took at least two weeks at the prevailing temperature (mid-50s°F).
5. **Fry rearing:** Fry were first fed green water and frozen baby brine shrimp (live food was preferred) and grew slowly at first. A mystery snail was added to clean uneaten food. Water changes of one-third were made weekly.
6. **Growth and diet shift:** Fry were gradually given small live tubifex, finely ground flake food, and finely scraped frozen brine shrimp. Some fry later refused flakes, which the author attributed to a diet of frozen daphnia, brine shrimp, and live tubifex.
7. **Growth result:** At seven months, most surviving fry were 2 inches or longer.
8. **Follow-up:** The author collected [[Iowa Darter]] in the same lake in summer 1978 and planned to attempt spawning them the following spring.
