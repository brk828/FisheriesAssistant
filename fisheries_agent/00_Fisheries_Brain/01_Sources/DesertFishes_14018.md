# Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[James E. Deacon]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] reviews the inland, indigenous fishes of deserts, with emphasis on the hot deserts of the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. It covers desert rivers, springs and marshes, and closed-basin lakes, then examines the physical and chemical stresses these waters impose (current and flooding, drought and desiccation, light, temperature, salinity, dissolved gases) and the biological responses of fishes to them. The chapter concludes that desert fish faunas are depauperate, that many native populations have been lost to dams, diversion, and introduced species, and that behavioral, physiological, and reproductive adaptations allow persistence in extreme habitats.

## Background & Description

Desert fishes are heterogeneous. Some are unique and isolated, while others range across complex drainage patterns. The authors define desert broadly, following [[Cole (1968)]], since many waters span multiple biomes. Aquatic habitats in arid lands depend on surface flow or subsurface infiltration from more mesic zones, except for springs fed by subterranean water.

Fish body form tracks current and habitat. Torrential streams favor fishes with sucking discs or hooks, deep-bodied fishes dominate slow reaches, and small generalized, rounded fishes characterize intermediate creeks. Fishes successful in desert waters are mostly generalized in shape, with few adjustments.

The authors note that the western North American freshwater fauna comprises about 200 native fishes, and that the speckled dace ([[Rhinichthys osculus]]) is the only widespread species, consisting of many local differentiated forms. The chapter is based largely on the authors' unpublished data from the southwestern United States and northern Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rivers and creeks.** Desert rivers such as the [[Colorado River]] and [[Murray-Darling River]] system are highly variable. The Colorado at Yuma ranged from a peak discharge of about 4.25 × 10⁶ m³/min in 1916 to 20.5 m³/min in 1934. Water temperatures in large desert rivers rarely drop below 10–12°C in winter and rarely exceed 30°C in summer, while air temperatures often reach 40–50°C. Small Arizona creeks reached maxima of 24–40.3°C, and backwaters 33–42.7°C. Flash floods can change water from clear to turbid within seconds and drop temperatures by more than 15°C.

**Historical change.** Large streams have been beheaded by impoundment and diverted for irrigation. Overgrazing in southern Arizona (1870–1890) preceded a drought in 1891–1894 that killed about 75% of the cattle herd and altered streams. Channelization and impoundment have largely eliminated the indigenous fauna of the largest streams.

**Springs and marshes.** Springs range from rheocrenes (creek sources) to limnocrenes (quiet pools), and many are thermal. Constancy of temperature and chemistry is common, but springs are sensitive to climatic shifts and to well pumping. [[Posos de la Becerra]], Cuatro Ciénegas basin, held about 15 fish species and fed marshes covering more than 200 hectares before drainage in 1964. Desert marshes are highly fluctuant, saline in low water, and resemble estuarine environments.

**Lakes.** Closed-basin (endorheic) lakes depend on balances of inflow, evaporation, and seepage. Few natural lakes persist in deserts, and many are too saline for fishes. [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada, is a remnant of pluvial Lake Lahontan; spawning runs of Lahontan cutthroat trout were destroyed by 1929 after a diversion dam. The inadvertent [[Salton Sea]] formed in 1905 and reached 25 m deep and about 1334 km² in area.

## Key Findings

**Physical stresses**
- **Flooding:** Floods rarely eliminate fishes entirely. In [[Sycamore Creek]], a September 1970 flood scoured the channel, yet longfin dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*) young were hatching within one week and filled the creek by one month. A 1965–1966 flood in the Salt River killed many introduced fishes (e.g., threadfin shad, sunfishes) while native suckers survived.
- **Sorting by size:** High water may affect young fish more than adults; juvenile speckled dace were carried farther in floods than adults.
- **Turbidity and sediment:** Suspended solids can suffocate fishes. Some species have barbels, reduced eye exposure, and leathery skin as responses to abrasive, turbid water.
- **Drought:** Longfin dace persisted beneath algal mats in Sycamore Creek during a 14-day period in 1965, while many fish species died. Some fishes can burrow into moist substrates, and pupfishes may survive up to 10 days in moist mud.
- **Light:** Intense sunlight can cause sunburn and tissue damage, and ultraviolet light is lethal to killifish embryos.

**Temperature tolerance**
- The maximum constant temperature for reproducing populations rarely exceeds 35°C, and tolerances above 45°C are considered doubtful.
- *Cyprinodon atrorus* was confirmed at 47.2°C in August 1968 near Cuatro Ciénegas. Adults of *C. macularius* died at 40.6°C, though a critical thermal maximum of 44.6°C was reported experimentally.
- Many eurythermal desert fishes tolerate water near 2°C, while stenothermal species such as the Devil's Hole pupfish (*Cyprinodon diabolis*), which lives near 33°C, tolerate 8–38°C for short periods.
- Acclimation to warm water is faster than to cold, so rapid cooling is more dangerous than gradual heating.

**Chemistry**
- Most desert waters have pH 7.2–9.0; values above 8.6 are linked to high salinity or photosynthesis.
- Fishes have persisted in salinities 2–3 times that of seawater (~35 g/L). *Cyprinodon variegatus* survived 142.4 g/L, *C. atrorus* up to 95 g/L, and recently dead fish were found at 240 g/L.
- Dissolved oxygen is one of the most important limiting factors. The lowest recorded minimum is 0.13 mg/L for *C. macularius*. Springfishes (*[[Crenichthys]]* spp.) tolerated oxygen below 0.7 mg/L at 32°C only with reduced activity.
- Free carbon dioxide reached 600 mg/L in [[Montezuma Well]], Arizona, where introduced fishes died quickly.
- Hydrogen sulfide tolerance was high in *Aphanius* from a sulfur-producing Cyrenaican marsh (12–200 mg/L recorded), suggesting physiological coping mechanisms.

**Dispersion and population dynamics**
- Fish distributions track stream gradient and current. Colorado River fishes, such as the [[Gila]] chubs, show strong morphological parallelism, including streamlined bodies that substitute for holdfast organs.
- In spring systems, faunal diversity increases with decreasing constancy in large springs, while sumps of desiccating pools support only a single or few highly tolerant species.
- Populations fluctuate sharply. In Aravaipa Creek, species composition shifted over 1963–1970 with changing flow.
- In the Cuatro Ciénegas basin, 10–14 fish species coexist in large springheads. In Aravaipa Creek, seven native species coexist, five minnows and two suckers.

**Reproduction and development**
- Reproduction in many desert fishes is protracted and asynchronous, with some populations spawning year-round under constant conditions and others showing spring–summer surges.
- Day length, temperature, food supply, and density-dependent factors all influence spawning. Density effects may outweigh physical factors in some desert fishes.
- Eggs of several cyprinodontids (*Cynolebias*, *Nothobranchius*) show delayed hatching, which buffers populations against unreliable filling of ephemeral waterholes.

**Threats and conservation**
- Introduced species, especially mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*), are implicated in the decline or extinction of many native desert fishes, including the Gila topminnow and desert pupfish in Arizona.
- Two extinctions of desert fish populations resulted from largemouth bass introduction, documented by the authors in Nevada.
- The authors conclude that most of the large-river fauna of the Colorado system has been severely reduced, and that some large native fishes face extinction "within a few years" under continued impoundment.
- The authors argue that climatic and density-dependent factors, rather than competition, often shape desert fish populations, and that behavioral interactions may allow species to coexist.

**Addendum:** The authors note that new literature published after completion of the chapter, including the six-volume *Fish Physiology* series, should be consulted.
