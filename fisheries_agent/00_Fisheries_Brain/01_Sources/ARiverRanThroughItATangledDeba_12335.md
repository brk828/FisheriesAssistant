# San Pedro River: Water, Land Use and the Fight Over a Living River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This New Times feature (June 1-7, 1994, pages 23-26, by Gregory McNamee) examines the [[San Pedro River]] in southeastern Arizona and the competing claims on its water and land. The article is primarily a socioeconomic and historical account rather than a biological study. It documents heavy municipal and agricultural water withdrawal, a long history of human-driven habitat loss, and a political conflict among environmentalists, the [[Nature Conservancy]], the [[Bureau of Land Management]], ranchers and Indian water-rights claimants. The author frames the river's future as a choice between a boom-town economy and a living river.

## Background & Description

The San Pedro is presented as a desert river whose flow has been heavily reduced. Municipal water use and irrigated agriculture take nearly 22,000 acre-feet (about eight billion gallons) per year, so that travelers crossing on I-10 usually see a dry bed. Sierra Vista alone has 14 private and municipal water companies, and the river's water rights remain unresolved.

Historical context given in the article:

- Sobaipuri Indians, described as distant cousins of the [[Hohokam]], lived in small settlements along the river.
- Two woolly mammoth kill sites near the river date to about 12,000 years ago, including the [[Murray Springs Mammoth Kill Site]] along Highway 90.
- Spanish cattle introduction in the mid-1600s caused small changes; mining smelters in the following centuries consumed thousands of cords of wood daily, stripping oak and mesquite from the hills.
- Millions of cattle arriving from southern Texas in the 1880s converted the river from a ciénaga to a channel that was either dry or flooded.
- U.S. Geological Survey records cited in the article describe the channel near the Gila confluence at about 10 feet deep in 1870 and nearly 80 feet deep about 40 years later.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article reports that Arizona lost nearly 85 percent of its riparian habitat within roughly half a century. Listed wildlife impacts include:

- [[Willow Flycatcher]]: reduced to 50 to 100 nesting pairs.
- [[Gray Hawk]]: only 55 pairs known.
- [[Bald Eagle]]: fewer than ten pairs along the watershed.

Cottonwood-willow bosques are described as among the most endangered plant associations in the world, with an estimated biomass comparable to an equal area of Amazonian rain forest. Groundwater depletion has produced a subsidence "cone of depression" beneath [[Sierra Vista]], the county's largest town.

Regional growth pressure is significant. Sierra Vista's population is given as 35,000 and rising, with a projected position among Arizona's largest cities by 2000. Nearby [[Fort Huachuca]] has about 22,000 people. Landowners are no longer required to disclose water-supply uncertainty to buyers, and the Whetstone Ranch annexation by Benson is projected to support a 7,000-home retirement community with three golf courses.

## Key Findings

- **Water demand exceeds sustainable supply:** municipal and agricultural use of about 22,000 acre-feet per year removes much of the river's flow.
- **Habitat loss:** the article cites an 85 percent loss of Arizona riparian habitat in about 50 years, with cattle, mining and wood cutting as drivers.
- **Species decline:** willow flycatcher, gray hawk and bald eagle populations are all described as critically small.
- **Nature Conservancy strategy:** the group has acquired land (including the 55,000-acre Muleshoe Ranch and 14,761 acres of streamside land), leased 80,000 acres from the BLM, and helped establish the nation's first federal riparian conservation area in May 1989 between Hereford and St. David. The river was named one of the world's eight "Last Great Places" in 1989.
- **Conflict with ranchers:** critics such as [[Howard Post]] argue the Conservancy and BLM reduce private land and tax base. The Conservancy's Andy Laurenzi acknowledged that the group put too much emphasis on land ownership and now seeks voluntary private conservation.
- **BLM grazing moratorium:** the BLM halted cattle grazing in the riverbed in January 1988. The Natural Resource Conservation District blocked a proposed BLM addition of 43,000 acres of streamside land.
- **Indian water rights:** the January 1992 [[Gila River Adjudication]] involves 65,000 individual claims by Apache, Akimel O'odham, Yavapai and Tohono O'odham nations, seeking rights to about two million acre-feet, nearly the whole Gila system flow. Proposed leasing at $1,200 per acre-foot is cited as prohibitive for agriculture and ranching.
- **Legal backdrop:** the article describes the Wyoming Doctrine (first-come, first-served) and the [[Winters Doctrine]] (reserved Indian water rights) and notes that Congress was upheld in 1903 in abrogating treaties.
- **Proposed dams:** politicians and developers call for more dams, including one upstream of Sierra Vista and a new dam at Charleston. The author warns this would undo restoration work.
- **Local preservation goals:** the Friends of the San Pedro River seek to restore the San Pedro House, upgrade the Murray Springs site and promote river awareness.
- **Central conclusion:** the article frames the outcome as a choice between a boom-town economy with a cattle industry and a considered approach to growth that preserves a living river, with the author noting that consensus is unlikely in the near term.
