# Status Review of Gila Chub (Gila intermedia) in the United States and Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department technical report (Technical Report 91, March 1996) reviews the status of the [[Gila Chub]], *Gila intermedia*, a small, deep-bodied minnow endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], the review compiles historic and current distribution, taxonomy, life history, and stream-by-stream site accounts to assess range-wide status. Gila chubs are now confined to 24 isolated populations in central and southern Arizona and northern Sonora, Mexico, and are considered extirpated in New Mexico. Of the 24 populations, 9 have unknown status, 6 are unstable-threatened, 8 are stable-threatened, and 1 is stable-secure. Principal threats are [[Nonnative Fish Species]], especially [[Green Sunfish]], together with habitat loss from water diversion, groundwater pumping, arroyo cutting, and mining.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The species was first described by Baird and Girard (1854) as *Gila gibbosa* and has a complicated nomenclatural history. Populations once labelled *G. robusta grahami* were reclassified by DeMarais (1995) as phenotypically intermediate between [[Gila Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*), the result of ancient introgressive hybridization. For this report, these populations are treated as *G. robusta*, though the term "grahami" is retained when citing older records. Identification of individual fish remains difficult; field identification relies on one or two characters.

**Diagnostic features.** Adults are small-finned, deep-bodied, and darkly colored. Males average about 150 mm total length and females can exceed 200 mm. Lateral-line scales usually number 61 to 79, with eight (rarely seven or nine) dorsal and anal fin rays and a head length to caudal peduncle depth ratio of 3.0 or less. Barbels are absent. By comparison, [[Roundtail Chub]] are larger (250-350 mm typical), have more than 80 lateral-line scales, usually nine dorsal, anal, and pelvic rays, and a head-to-peduncle ratio of 3.3 to 4.3.

**Growth and diet.** Females grow larger than males, which complicates age analysis. Griffith and Tiersch (1989) found four age groups in Redfield Canyon, with back-calculated lengths of 90, 135, 160, and 183 mm after years one through four. Gila chubs are omnivorous: adults are principally carnivorous, while juveniles eat organic debris and filamentous algae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Populations occupy pools in small streams, cienegas, and impoundments. Associated riparian vegetation includes willows, tamarisk, cottonwoods, and ash. Aquatic plants include watercress, horsetail, rushes, and speedwell. Monkey Spring water temperatures average about 82°F, with pH 6.8 to 7.0 and dissolved oxygen of 2.3 to 2.6 mg/l.

**Reproduction.** In [[Cienega Creek]], Nelson (1993) found that breeding color intensity increased with warmer water (20-24°C). Fish of 75 to 80 mm total length or more appear capable of spawning.

**Native and nonnative fish communities.** Native associates include [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Longfin Dace]], and [[Gila Topminnow]]. Nonnative species in the historic range include [[Green Sunfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Mosquitofish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Rainbow Trout]], among others.

**Historic distribution.** Gila chubs were recorded from approximately 30 rivers and spring-fed tributaries across the Gila basin, including the Salt, Verde, Santa Cruz, San Pedro, San Simon, San Francisco, and Agua Fria drainages. Cienega loss from [[Arroyo Cutting]] in the late 1800s and early 1900s likely eliminated undocumented populations.

**Population status categories.** Populations were ranked as Stable-Secure, Stable-Threatened, Unstable-Threatened, Extirpated, or Unknown, using qualitative criteria because quantitative data were largely lacking.

## Key Findings

- **Current range:** Gila chubs inhabit 24 isolated systems in Arizona and northern Sonora. Mexican populations are limited to [[Cienega Los Fresnos]] and [[Cienega La Cienegita]], within 2 km of the Arizona-Mexico border.
- **Status tally:** 24 populations: 9 unknown (2 recently established, survival uncertain), 6 unstable-threatened, 8 stable-threatened, and 1 stable-secure.
- **Extirpation:** The species is considered extirpated in New Mexico, including the San Francisco, Gila, and San Simon drainages. Arizona systems listed as formerly occupied include Cave Creek/Seven Springs, Fish Creek, Sonoita Creek (Monkey Spring), Santa Cruz River, Turkey Creek, Big Chino Wash, and Post Canyon.
- **Nonnative predation:** Dudley (1995) found green sunfish as small as 51 mm preyed on young chubs of 20-25 mm. Chubs were absent under 40 mm where green sunfish co-occurred in [[Sabino Canyon]], and chubs found there resulted from upstream dispersal rather than local recruitment.
- **Smallmouth bass threat:** Smallmouth bass, first recorded in Rye Creek in 1995, pose a serious threat there given limited habitat.
- **Water rights:** Safford, Arizona was considering additional withdrawals from Bonita Creek in 1995, which could threaten the population and the BLM's reserved water right.
- **Parasites and water quality:** Chubs in lower Bonita Creek were heavily parasitized by *Lernaea*. A March 1993 salvage yielded 67 Gila chubs out of 17,500 fish, many with skin infections. Minckley and Sommerfeld (1979) reported summer oxygen deficits in lower Bonita Creek.
- **Recent discoveries:** Gila chubs were found in Red Tank Draw (Verde basin) in October 1995, with 32 chubs captured in December 1995. Indian Creek chubs (n=18 in May 1995) made up 29% of the fish fauna.
- **Recommendations:** Remove green sunfish from lower Sabino Canyon and O'Donnell Canyon, conduct intensive surveys of Red Tank Draw, Harden Cienega Creek, and Dix Creek, evaluate the Silver Creek waterfall barrier, consider augmentation of Post Canyon from O'Donnell Canyon, and collect additional specimens for genetic analysis.
