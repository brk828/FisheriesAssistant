# Recreating the Cambrian Explosion: Cuatro Cienegas as a Living Time Machine

#microbiology #cyanobacteria #stromatolites

## Executive Summary

This ABCNews.com science column by Lee Dye describes a research program to explain the [[Cambrian Explosion]], the rapid appearance of multicellular plants and animals about 570 million years ago. The study uses [[Cuatro Cienegas]], a remote Mexican site with living [[Stromatolites]] built by [[Cyanobacteria]], as a modern analogue of early Earth environments. The program is led by [[James Elser]] of [[Arizona State University]] and [[Jack Farmer]], and is funded by a $750,000 grant from the [[NASA Astrobiology Institute]]. The article presents two competing explanations for the event: rising atmospheric oxygen and a shift in the nutrient supply, specifically [[Phosphorus]].

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** For about 3 billion years, life on Earth was made up only of single-celled organisms. The Cambrian Explosion then produced multicellular life in what may have been only a few million years.
- **Naming:** The event is called the "Cambrian Explosion" by some researchers and the "Cambrian Transition" by others.
- **Core puzzle:** Why did microorganisms dominate for so long without major grazers, and why did grazing organisms then appear so rapidly?
- **Study site:** Cuatro Cienegas lies in an isolated area of Mexico, about an eight-hour drive south of the U.S. border. Its streams and ponds host a simple biological system that the researchers describe as having diversified dramatically in only 40,000 years.
- **Stromatolites:** These bacterial structures resemble coral reefs. Before the Cambrian, they covered much of Earth. They declined once grazing animals appeared. Living examples are rare and mostly inaccessible elsewhere, whereas at Cuatro Cienegas they can be reached by wading in streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Grazing dynamics:** The researchers plan to observe snails and other organisms feeding on stromatolites and algae in the ponds, which they regard as a view of conditions that existed millions of years ago.
- **Active growth:** Elser describes the stromatolites as "very active" and growing, not fossilized.
- **Oxygen hypothesis:** The most widely accepted explanation holds that atmospheric oxygen reached levels sufficient for multicellular organisms. Early oxygen is thought to have been absorbed by iron in oceans and on land ("oxygen sinks") until those sinks filled, after which atmospheric oxygen rose. Elser considers this "not entirely unsatisfactory."
- **Nutrient hypothesis:** Complex organisms need carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus. Phosphorus controls growth, and stromatolites may have been phosphorus-poor, like a "marshmallow" diet. Fossil evidence reportedly supports low phosphorus in ancient stromatolites. Phosphorus leaching from rocks into seas shortly before the Cambrian may have enriched the algae, making them nutritious enough to support grazers, which in turn could feed snails and eventually fish.

## Key Findings

- **Research plan:** The three-year project will collect samples in the field and manipulate environmental conditions, such as nutrient levels, in the laboratory. Researchers will also observe grazing directly by snorkeling in the ponds.
- **Threshold model:** Elser suggests that once a threshold was crossed, whether in atmospheric oxygen, algal phosphorus, or a combination of factors, the transition could have occurred relatively quickly.
- **Limitations:** Elser states that findings from this single project are unlikely to be conclusive. Similar sites would need to be located to test whether results are repeatable.
- **Broader significance:** The work is expected to improve understanding of this period in Earth's history and of how a species could evolve to ask such questions.
