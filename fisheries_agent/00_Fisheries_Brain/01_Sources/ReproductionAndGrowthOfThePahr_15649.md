# Reproduction and Growth of the Pahrump Poolfish (*Empetrichthys latos latos* Miller)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Empetrichthyinae #endangered-species #desert-fishes

## Executive Summary

This study documents the reproductive biology and early growth of the **[[Pahrump Poolfish]]** (*Empetrichthys latos latos*), an endangered cyprinodontiform fish endemic to isolated desert spring systems in Nevada, USA. The species was extirpated from its type locality, **[[Manse Spring]]**, in 1975 and now persists only as introduced populations at three transplant sites: **[[Corn Creek Springs]]**, **[[Spring Mountain Ranch State Park]]**, and **[[Shoshone Ponds]]**. Laboratory spawning trials using wild-caught broodstock from Corn Creek Springs demonstrated that eggs hatch in 7–10 days at 24°C, with larvae averaging 6.2 mm total length (TL) at hatching and growing to an average of 21.1 mm TL by 112 days post-hatching. Larger females produced significantly more eggs than smaller females, both in the laboratory and in the historical natural population at Manse Spring. Ovarian maturity index data from Manse Spring (1967–68) indicate a prolonged spawning season from February through July, peaking in April. Handling stress was identified as a confounding factor that likely suppressed growth rates in experimental groups relative to an undisturbed control group.

## Background & Description

- **Length at hatching:** Mean 6.2 mm TL (range 5–7 mm)
- **Length at 112 days post-hatching:** Mean 21.1 mm TL (range 14–29 mm)
- **Growth pattern:** Relatively even/constant growth rate across the 112-day trial period, with growth increments generally increasing with age (attributed to differential growth between sexes)
- **Sexual size dimorphism:** Adult females are consistently larger than adult males across all sampled populations (Corn Creek, Shoshone Ponds, Spring Mountain Ranch)
  - Example: Corn Creek Upper Pond (June '84) — Males mean TL 35.3 mm (n=203); Females mean TL 47.5 mm (n=738)
  - Example: Shoshone Pond 5 (Aug '84) — Males mean TL 39.8 mm (n=2); Females mean TL 51.7 mm (n=162)
- **Mean female TL used in spawning trials:** 46.6 mm
  - Females averaging 45.9 mm TL (n=11) failed to produce eggs
  - Females averaging 47.3 mm TL (n=12) produced an average of 13 eggs each

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** Endemic to isolated desert spring systems and spring-fed impoundments in the Mojave Desert region of Nevada; part of the broader **[[Death Valley System]]** pupfish/poolfish radiation
- **Thermal ecology:**
  - Laboratory culture temperature (24.5°C ± 2.5°C) selected to approximate the main pool conditions at Corn Creek
  - Field observations at Spring Mountain Ranch State Park indicate fish become inactive from November through April at temperatures below ~13°C
  - Thousands of fry observed at Spring Mountain Ranch in June 1984 at a surface water temperature of 16.4°C
  - Cooler, variable-temperature habitats (e.g., irrigation impoundments) likely delay spawning to early summer compared to spring-fed source habitats
- **Trophic/diet inputs in captivity:** TetraMin® staple food and frozen brine shrimp (*Artemia* sp.) for adults; larvae fed a staged diet (Liquifry No. 1 → Liquifry + Artemia nauplii → Artemia nauplii only → Artemia + crushed TetraMin) at set intervals post-hatching
- **Conservation context:** Species survival is entirely dependent on translocated, managed populations following loss of the type locality; relevant to broader **[[Endangered Desert Fishes]]** conservation efforts in the Death Valley System, alongside related taxa such as **[[Pahrump Killifish]]** (a synonym used historically) and other **[[Cyprinodontiformes]]**

## Key Findings

1. **Hatching success:** Ranged from 40–68% across trials; low hatching success in Trial 1 (40%) attributed to fungal loss of 20 eggs. Trials 2, 3, and the control group showed consistent hatching rates (~66–68%).
2. **Egg production:** Varied from 0–28 eggs per female and 19–93 eggs per trial.
3. **Survivorship to 28 days:** Ranged from 15.5% to 84.6%; low survivorship in Trials 1 and 2 suggested handling stress contributed to fry mortality, while Trial 3 achieved the highest survival, indicating handling was not the sole cause of mortality.
4. **Growth suppression by experimental handling:** The non-concurrent control group (measured only once, at 28 days) was significantly larger than any of the repeatedly handled experimental groups, indicating that frequent measurement/handling negatively affected growth rates.
5. **Fecundity–size relationship:** Both laboratory and wild data (Manse Spring, 1961–63) confirm that larger females produce substantially more mature eggs (>1.5 mm diameter) than smaller females — e.g., on 23 April 1962, females in the 60+ mm size class averaged 74.2 eggs compared to 6.1 eggs in the 30–39 mm class.
6. **Reproductive seasonality:** Maturity Index data (% body weight as ovary weight) from Manse Spring (1967–68) show ovarian development beginning in late December, sustained spawning condition from February through at least July, and a peak Maturity Index (9.56) in late April.
7. **Growth trajectory (Table 5):** Grand mean sizes progressed from 6.2 mm (day 0) to 7.0 mm (day 14), 8.7 mm (day 28), 10.3 mm (day 42), 12.5 mm (day 56), 14.7 mm (day 70), 17.0 mm (day 84), 19.6 mm (day 98), and 21.1 mm (day 112), with growth increments generally stable to slightly increasing over time.
8. **Sex-based size divergence:** Field data from all three transplant populations confirm females attain larger adult body sizes than males, consistent with patterns observed in laboratory-reared cohorts as they age.
