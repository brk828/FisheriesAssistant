# Kansas Farm Ponds: Fish Production, Limnology and Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Kansas Agricultural Experiment Station bulletin (Bulletin 488, by Otto W. [[Tiemeier]]) reports roughly a decade of studies (1953 to 1963) on stock ponds in the [[Flint Hills]] region of Kansas. The work covered water chemistry, soils, turbidity, thermal and oxygen stratification, stocking and species combinations, fish growth, spawning, survival, escape over spillways, harvest, and daily movements of [[Channel Catfish]]. The central conclusion is that pond fish management depends on water clarity and oxygen conditions: [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] succeed in clear ponds, while channel catfish tolerate both clear and muddy water. The bulletin closes with practical management suggestions for farm pond owners.

## Background & Description

- **Scale of pond construction:** The Agricultural Stabilization and Conservation Service provided cost-sharing on approximately 55,500 stock watering ponds in Kansas from 1940 to 1964, and on about 15,000 erosion control dams that impounded water suitable for fish.
- **Prior surveys:** King (1960) surveyed 1,000 farm ponds stocked by the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife. Of these, 82 percent provided excellent or satisfactory fishing, at a rate of 64 fisherman-days per acre. Fishermen were 52 percent men, 23 percent women and 25 percent children.
- **Early work:** Dyche (1910 to 1914) addressed pond construction, pond fish habits and culture. Schneberger and Jewell (1928) found that both very dense and absent vegetation reduced fish production, and that production rose as turbidity fell below 100 ppm.
- **Study area:** The Flint Hills cover about 4,000,000 acres, between the 96th and 97th meridians, with elevations of 850 to 1,550 feet. Rainfall ranges from 30 to 38 inches, and about 75 percent falls during the growing season. Residual soils derive from Permian limestones and shales.
- **Study ponds:** Forty-five ponds were studied, and 44 were stocked. Surface areas ranged from 0.3 to 5 acres and depths from 6 to 18 feet, though pond size and depth varied widely with drought and rain.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Soils and nutrients.** Soil fertility around ponds was generally moderate to high. Of 18 ponds sampled, organic matter (an index of nitrogen) was very low or low in 13, and available phosphorus was very low or low in 15. Potash was generally very high. Phosphorus was identified as the key limiting nutrient, following Zeller (1953).

**Rainfall.** Manhattan's average annual precipitation is about 32 inches. During the 10-year study period it averaged 31.6 inches, ranging from 21.64 inches (1956) to 44.39 inches (1958). About 66 percent of annual rain falls from May through September.

**Turbidity.** Ponds were classified by average summer turbidity (Tiemeier and Moorman, 1956): clear at 40 ppm or less, intermediate at 41 to 100 ppm, and muddy at 101 ppm or more. Of 27 ponds sampled for 2 to 8 years, 7 were clear, 11 intermediate and 9 muddy. [[Turbidity]] was driven largely by cattle wading and trampling, heavy grazing and clay colloids. Muddy water reduced [[Largemouth Bass]] reproduction, though studies by Wallen (1951) found natural turbidities not directly lethal to adult fish.

**Thermal and chemical stratification.** Ponds stratified thermally and chemically by early June and remained stratified through summer. In 1957, surface water reached 93°F, with 9-foot readings as low as 56°F. Fall mixing was earlier in 1957 than in 1958.

**Oxygen and carbon dioxide.** [[Dissolved Oxygen]] was often 0.0 ppm at depths of 6 feet or more during June through August. Tarzwell (1957) recommended that dissolved oxygen not fall below 5 ppm for more than 8 hours per day, and never below 3 ppm. [[Free Carbon Dioxide]] reached 115 ppm at 9 feet in 1957 readings, and no free CO₂ was found at the surface. The combination of low oxygen and high CO₂ limited use of deep water by stocked species.

**pH and alkalinity.** Surface pH ranged from 7.2 to 9.6 in 1957. Average phenolphthalein alkalinity ranged from 1.5 to 8.5 ppm, and methyl orange alkalinity from 55 to 162 ppm at the surface. Pond owners reported fish kills at pH 10.6, associated with high surface evaporation.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and fish species**
- Fingerlings were usually stocked at 100 per acre, or 50 per acre in ponds under 1 acre.
- Of 44 stocked ponds, 23 contained only stocked fish after 1 to 10 years. Wild fish were found in 21, largely hitched by pond movements or unknown sources.
- Unmanaged stocking, including fish seined from creeks, "almost always" produced unbalanced populations within a few years.

**Species performance**
- **Largemouth bass and bluegill:** Both grew and reproduced in clear ponds. Bass failed to reproduce in muddy water. Bluegills spawned in muddy ponds but became stunted when bass numbers were low.
- **Channel catfish:** Grew well in clear or turbid water and tolerated muddy conditions. Fish reached 14.5 inches and 397 grams by age group III in the pooled sample.
- **White bass:** Grew more slowly in Horne ponds than in New York or Oklahoma populations. Ten dead fish and a pH of 10.6 were recorded in 1956, with no spawning. The authors judged the species unsuitable for Flint Hills ponds.
- **White crappie:** One pond reached 3,818 crappies (252.25 pounds) at drainage in 1960. The authors advise against stocking crappies.

**Deep-water survival experiments (Tiemeier, 1962)**
- Caged bluegill, largemouth bass, black crappie and channel catfish died or were compromised within about 45 minutes at depths of 6 to 12 feet.
- Bullheads were much hardier than carp.

**Channel catfish survival and escape**
- In 5 ponds stocked 8 times, 1,459 of 1,678 fish (87 percent) were recovered, a loss of about 7 percent per year.
- In 3 other ponds under adverse conditions, loss averaged about 13 percent per year.
- At Fear No. 2, 89 of 1,016 fingerlings (8.76 percent) moved downstream into Fear No. 1 during a flood.

**Growth and feeding**
- Supplemental pellet feeding at 5 percent of body weight per day produced large gains. Fish fed through September gained 94.8 percent of weight.
- Data on stunted populations showed poor growth from overpopulation, with Dugan pond catfish weighing less than 3 ounces at age group V.
- Fish stocked at 1,600 per acre grew no faster than fish stocked at 100 per acre without feeding in ponds with abundant natural food.

**Condition and liver weight**
- The coefficient of condition (R = W × 10⁵ / L³) was graded as poor (1.20 or less), normal (1.21 to 1.40) or excellent (1.41 or above).
- Liver weight correlated highly with body weight in 98 channel catfish (t = 19.256; r² = 0.7942, or 79 percent of variation explained).

**Spawning**
- Young channel catfish were found in muddy ponds without visible cavities. Cream cans placed in Pickering pond No. 4 served as spawning containers in 1959 to 1961.
- Largemouth bass did not spawn until the second spring after fingerling stocking. Bluegills spawned the summer after fall stocking.

**Harvest**
- Piner (1952) estimated annual harvest in 42 Manhattan-area ponds at 15 to 75 pounds per acre, averaging 41 pounds. Avery pond No. 1 (1953) yielded 119.7 pounds of fish from about 1 acre.
- Seining and fishing removed 300 bluegills and crappies against 32 bass, prompting a 12:1 bluegill-to-bass removal goal.

**Movement**
- Channel catfish tagged in a 1.8-acre pond moved from 55 to about 920 feet in 25 minutes. Fish were most active at dusk and before dawn.

**Management suggestions (Tiemeier)**
1. Stock bass and bluegills only in clear water.
2. Stock channel catfish in clear or turbid water for rapid growth.
3. Avoid stocking crappies, bullheads and white bass.
4. Remove 12 bluegills for each bass, and seine young-of-the-year bluegills.
5. Fence ponds and pipe water to tanks to reduce silt and extend pond life.
6. Fish primarily for sport rather than meat.
