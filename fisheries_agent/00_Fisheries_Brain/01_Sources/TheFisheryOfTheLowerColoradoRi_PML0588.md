# The fishery of the lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #catostomidae #cyprinidae #mugilidae

## Executive Summary

This 1944 report by William A. Dill of the [[California Division of Fish and Game]] compiles existing knowledge of the fishery of the lower [[Colorado River]], from [[Boulder Dam]] to the Mexican boundary and including [[Lake Havasu]], [[Imperial Dam]], and associated canals. It combines a physical and biological survey made in 1942 with compiled data from federal, state, and private agencies. The author states the work is a compendium and guide, not a conclusive study.

The river was transformed by the [[Boulder Canyon Project]] dams: silt was removed, flow was regulated, and water became clearer and more constant. A sport fishery developed, dominated by introduced [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]], while native fishes declined. The report concludes that the fishery is healthy, recommends no stocking, a year-round open season, uniform Arizona-California regulations, and specific bag and size limits.

## Background & Description

- **Basin:** The Colorado Basin covers about 1/13 of the area of the western United States plus parts of northwestern Mexico. The lower basin is arid, hot, and bordered by deserts. The river ends in a delta before the [[Gulf of California]], after a course of 1,700 miles through seven states and Mexico.
- **Historical silt load:** Before dam construction the Colorado was described as the greatest silt-carrying stream in the world. Fortier and Blaney (1928) estimated the annual silt load at Yuma at about 138,000 acre-feet, roughly 20 per cent of it bed silt.
- **Salton Sink:** The [[Salton Sea]] lies below sea level (deepest point about 275 feet below), formed by an accidental diversion of the river in 1905-1907. It covered 445 square miles at formation and had shrunk to 287 square miles, about 244 feet below sea level, at the time of writing.
- **Reservoirs:** [[Lake Havasu]] (full elevation 450.54 feet, about 25,000 acres, storing 717,000 acre-feet) is a run-of-the-river lake. Operating range is limited to 10 feet of fluctuation between 440.54 and 450.54 feet under a contract with the [[Metropolitan Water District of Southern California]].
- **Fish fauna:** Before 1942 the known fauna was based on Gilbert and Scofield (1898), who recorded four families. The 1942 survey added records of at least 14 species in five additional families, including Elopidae, Salmonidae, Catostomidae, Cyprinidae, Ameiuridae, Cyprinodontidae, Poeciliidae, Mugilidae, and Centrarchidae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical conditions**
- **Flow:** Historically unpredictable, ranging from about 3,000 to 150,000 cubic feet per second below the proposed dam site. Boulder Dam allows storage of enough flood water to hold back the entire average annual flow for two years.
- **Air and water temperature:** Yuma has a mean annual air temperature of about 72 °F (rendered as "2° F" in OCR, but the text indicates a high mean with a maximum of 120 °F). Water temperature typically bottoms near 50 °F in January and peaks at 80-90 °F from June to September. Largemouth bass stop feeding below about 50 °F, and spring spawning begins near 65 °F, typically in March or April.
- **Water chemistry:** Dissolved oxygen in 1942 surface samples ranged from 7.6 to 11.4 ppm. Water is alkaline, and total dissolved solids are high, around 700 ppm after dam control. Drainage ditches had markedly higher solids (1,578 ppm versus 860 ppm in river intake water on May 31, 1940).
- **Oxygen crisis:** A flash flood on the [[Bill Williams River]] in September 1939 caused an oxygen demand and loss of fish below [[Parker Dam]].

**Biological factors**
- **Vegetation:** Riparian plants include willow, cottonwood, seepwillow, arrowweed, and [[Salt Cedar]] (tamarisk), which provide shelter and detritus.
- **Food:** Bottom samples were sparse. Snails, chironomid larvae, and small crustacea were present. Stonefly, amphipod, and clam/mussel records were absent. Forage fishes include bluegill, green sunfish, and [[Western Mosquitofish]], which are abundant.
- **Predators and competitors:** Piscivorous birds (herons, pelicans, cormorants), the [[Pallid Coon]], and bullfrogs and turtles were considered of minor impact.
- **Parasitism:** Only nematodes were noted, with no unusually harmful parasites.

**Dams and diversions**
- Dams create fishing water and have little effect on native migration because migratory game fishes are not present in the lower river.
- The [[Colorado River Aqueduct]] (242 miles) pumps fish from Lake Havasu. In February 1940 about 1,000,000 green sunfish, 30,000 largemouth bass, and 30,000 channel catfish were rescued from Gene Wash Reservoir.
- The All-American Canal has a maximum capacity of 15,155 cubic feet per second, and the Gila Canal an ultimate 6,000 cubic feet per second.

## Key Findings

- **Native fish decline:** The [[Humpback Sucker]], [[Bony-tail]], and [[Colorado River Squawfish]] were once abundant but were scarce or absent by 1942–1944. Decline was noticeable shortly before or after 1930, coinciding with increases in exotic species.
- **Dominant game fishes:** [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]] are the major game fish. Channel catfish is considered the most numerous and most caught species in the area as a whole. The largest recorded channel catfish in the 1940–1943 contests was 20 lbs 8 oz.
- **Bass growth:** Bass averaged 13.0 inches at age 3 (1942 sample of 83 fish). Most bass spawn first at three years of age.
- **Introduced sport fishes:** [[Bluegill]] (abundant, roughly 10:1 over green sunfish), [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Crappie]], and [[White Crappie]] were introduced or spread after 1938.
- **Ten-pounder:** The [[Ten-Pounder]] (*Elops affinis*), first reported from the river in 1941, is scarce but established in the [[Salton Sea]] and was declared a California game fish in 1943.
- **Trout:** Trout are not permanently established. Records are limited to occasional downstream migrants in winter. Moffett (1942) considered only 25–30 miles below Boulder Dam suitable for trout.
- **Mullet:** The [[Common Mullet]] is abundant and was recognized as a potential sport fish. Salton Sea mullet averaged 5.6 lbs in October 1942 samples. Commercial fishing there was reestablished in May 1942.
- **Silt reduction:** Annual silt at Yuma dropped to 2,071 acre-feet in 1940, down from about 138,000 acre-feet before dam control.
- **Fisheries impact of stocking:** Largemouth bass and crappie were stocked by California in 1938–1939 (about 18,000 bass in 1938). The author recommends no further stocking.
- **Turtle:** [[Emory's Soft-shelled Turtle]] is probably introduced, spread from the upper Gila drainage. Collected at Headgate Rock Dam on May 19, 1942.
- **Fishing pressure:** Considered light. Yuma, Needles, and Blythe had a combined population of 11,804 (1940 census).

**Recommendations**
- **Stocking:** None, either game or forage fishes.
- **Season:** Open year-round for all fish. The California closed season for bass, crappies, and sunfishes (outside May 29–October 31) is recommended for removal.
- **Bag limits:** Largemouth bass and channel catfish: 10 fish, not to exceed 15 pounds, one per day. Other game fishes: 40 in aggregate, not to exceed 20 pounds. Mullet and carp: no limit when taken by angling.
- **Size limits:** Largemouth bass and channel catfish: 10 inches minimum (total length). No limits on sunfishes.
- **Gear:** Angling only, with one line or pole and no more than three hooks per bait line. Nets, traps, spears, explosives, and poisons prohibited.
- **Closed areas:** None.
- **Uniform district:** Dual Arizona and California licensing for the Colorado River District.
