# Mortality Rates and Movement in the Channel Catfish Population of the Sacramento Valley

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by [[George W. McCammon]] and [[Don A. LaFaunce]] (California Department of Fish and Game) examines a [[Channel Catfish]] population that became a major sport fishery in the lower [[Sacramento Valley]] by about 1954. In spring 1955, 797 fish were tagged with [[Disk-Dangler Tag]]s at four river and slough sites. Anglers returned 228 tags (29 percent) over four years. Recoveries showed a spatially restricted population with strong attraction to [[Sutter Bypass]]. Using [[Ricker Methods]] of [[Mark-Recapture Tagging]], the authors estimated annual survival at 0.379 uncorrected and 0.440 after adjusting for an assumed 40 percent [[Angler Nonresponse]] and differences in vulnerability.

## Background & Description

Channel catfish were introduced into the [[Central Valley]] in 1891, with an unauthorized introduction between 1925 and 1930, both in the [[Sacramento Valley]] (bounded by Redding and Stockton). The first authenticated capture came in 1942. Through 1950 the Valley's sport catfish catch was dominated by [[White Catfish]], while [[Brown Bullhead]] and [[Black Bullhead]] contributed little.

Channel catfish became common in the lower Valley's sport catch around 1950. By 1954, a major fishery was evident from newspaper reports and departmental observations. Anglers frequently caught 5 to 10 lb fish, with occasional fish of 15 to 20 lb. The fishery area is roughly triangular, with [[Marysville]] on the [[Feather River]], [[Colusa]] on the [[Sacramento River]], and their confluence at the apexes. Angling is heaviest along the Bypass sloughs.

The study had two objectives: (1) to determine fish movements, and (2) to obtain reliable mortality estimates. It was funded under Dingell-Johnson Project California F-2-R.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tagging sites and methods.** Fish were captured with [[Fyke Net]]s fished for four to five days per set, since longer undisturbed periods were needed for optimal catches. Tagging occurred in April and May 1955 at four sites:

- Sacramento River at Sacramento Slough (199 fish)
- Sacramento River at Butte Creek mouth (200)
- Feather River about five miles below Marysville (200)
- West Cut of Sutter Bypass at Highway 40A causeway (198)

Fork length was recorded for 794 fish: mean 12.1 ± 0.1 in, range 6.8 to 22.1 in. Fish tagged in Sutter Bypass were significantly larger (mean 14.9 in) than those in the river sites. A minimum tagging length of 7.0 in was set, though one 6.8 in fish was tagged by oversight.

**Thermal regime.** The completion of [[Shasta Dam]] in 1944 cooled the Sacramento River downstream. Daily maximum temperatures at Hamilton City, about 30 miles above Colusa, had never exceeded 68 °F since power generation began. Mean maximum summer temperatures at Colusa and downstream consistently exceed 70 °F, the range the authors describe as preferred by this warmwater species. This likely explains the absence of catfish upstream.

**Salinity.** Late-summer and early-fall reduced river flows allow brackish intrusion from San Francisco Bay into the lower [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]], with chlorides exceeding 2,000 ppm at Antioch Bridge in dry years. Channel catfish appear much less tolerant of brackish water than [[White Catfish]], which are abundant in the lower Delta. The few Delta catches, near the [[Pacific Gas and Electric Company]] outfall at Antioch, occurred only in late fall or winter.

**Barriers.** [[Sutter Butte Diversion Dam]], about six miles below Oroville, obstructs upstream movement. A fish ladder exists, but the authors doubted channel catfish would use it.

**Hydrology.** A major flood in December 1955, about seven months after tagging, sent roughly 106,000 cubic feet per second through Sutter Bypass. This may have altered both movement and mortality.

## Key Findings

**Movement**

- Of 226 returns with length data, most recaptures occurred within the triangle defined by Colusa, Marysville, and the confluence. Only 29 (13 percent) were reported outside it.
- Of all returns, 150 provided both direction and distance, 59 gave direction only (54 of these from Sutter Bypass), and 26 were of no value.
- The authors found little emigration upstream or downstream from the area, and no evidence of regular seasonal migration, though the data were limited by summer-weighted angling.
- Sixty-three recoveries in the Bypass sloughs came from river-released fish, representing 37 percent of returns from the three river sites. Larger fish were significantly more likely to move into the Bypass (t-test, 5 percent level). The reason is unknown; warmer water or food supply were proposed.
- Fish must pass through underground conduits to enter the Bypass, which the authors considered evidence of a strong attraction.

**Returns and size bias**

- Annual recoveries were 146 (first year), 54, 20, and 8 (fourth year). Sutter Bypass tags had the highest first-year return rate.
- A chi-square test (5 percent level) found a significant size difference between all tagged fish and returned fish, driven by fewer first-year returns under 9.0 in and more than expected over 15.0 in. Of 149 fish 15.0 in or longer, 104 (70 percent) were tagged in Sutter Bypass, where angling pressure was highest.
- Fish under 9.0 in were judged incompletely recruited to the fishery, and 78 such fish were excluded from mortality computations.

**Growth**

- Growth was not formally estimated. One angler-reported fish, tagged at 13.0 in fork length on May 10, 1955, was recaptured on July 17, 1957, at 21 in total length and 5 lb 11 oz. The authors judged this excellent growth.
- Brood stock from this population was often immature at 20 to 21 in total length, whereas maturity elsewhere is typically reached near 15 in total length.

**Survival and mortality (uncorrected)**

- Weighted survival: 0.373. Unweighted survival from regression on log recoveries (slope −0.42151): 0.379. Close agreement indicated survival did not vary significantly among years.
- Total annual mortality (a): 0.621.
- Annual expectation of death from fishing: 0.182. From natural causes: 0.439.
- Instantaneous total mortality (i): 0.970. Instantaneous fishing mortality (p): 0.284. Instantaneous natural mortality (q): 0.686.
- Annual conditional fishing mortality (m): 0.248. Annual conditional natural mortality (n): 0.497.

**Nonresponse correction**

- Comparison studies gave nonresponse estimates of 15 to 60 percent. The authors adopted 30 to 50 percent, with 40 percent as the best guess. Striped bass data from [[Chadwick]] (47 percent in 1958, 35 percent in 1959) were given the most weight.
- At 40 percent nonresponse, the corrected estimates were: a = 0.298 (fishing), v = 0.323 (natural). Table 3 summarizes the 30, 40, and 50 percent scenarios.

**Final adjusted estimates (40 percent nonresponse, Bypass fish excluded)**

| Parameter | Estimate |
|---|---|
| Mean annual survival | 0.440 |
| Mean annual total mortality | 0.560 |
| Annual expectation of death from fishing | 0.304 |
| Annual expectation of death from natural causes | 0.256 |
| Instantaneous mortality rate | 0.821 |
| Instantaneous fishing mortality rate | 0.445 |
| Instantaneous natural mortality rate | 0.376 (Summary lists 0.3876, likely an OCR error) |
| Annual conditional fishing mortality rate | 0.359 |
| Annual conditional natural mortality rate | 0.314 |

Bypass fish were excluded because their fishing rate (about 0.388 at 40 percent nonresponse) was far higher than the river rate (about 0.304). The stock fraction present in the Bypass at tagging was unknown, so proper weighting was impossible.

**Management conclusion**

- Because growth rates were unknown, the fishing rate for maximum sustained yield per unit weight of recruits could not be determined. Using [[Tester]]'s yield curve for Type B growth, the authors concluded that the current fishing rate provides a sustained yield close to the maximum.
- The authors recommended repeating the experiment, since the 1955 flood may have affected results, and using a method to measure angler nonresponse directly.
