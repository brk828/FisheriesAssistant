# Fishery Management Summary Report, San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation (1964)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This annual summary, issued January 29, 1965 by the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]] Branch of Fishery Management Services (Phoenix, Arizona), reports on fishery management of the [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]]'s reservation waters in 1964. Under a 1958 agreement, the Bureau provided technical assistance to the Tribe, and the year's main goal was to bring all suitable waters under an active fishery management program. Most managed waters are small [[Stock Tank Fishery|stock tanks]], supplemented by a few streams, a large reservoir-type lake ([[Seneca Lake]]), and candidate dam sites for new fishing lakes. The report estimates 12,670 angler-days on reservation waters in 1964, about 56% of them on Seneca Lake, and recommends expanding lakes and public-use facilities to support tribal economic development.

## Background & Description

The reservation is largely rugged, remote canyon country with limited access. Most aquatic habitat consists of small, man-made impoundments (stock tanks) built for livestock, scattered across the reservation and mostly in remote locations reachable only by poor roads. Fishery work in 1964 included:

- Resurveys of waters to assess the 1963 fish stocking and spot-checks of reservation waters for fish distribution.
- Surveys of several dam sites for suitability as fishing lakes.
- Stocking, population checks, and reclassification of tanks under trout or warm-water management.

Under the stock tank program, rainbow trout were stocked in roughly 32 tanks plus Seneca Lake, and warm-water species were managed in about 46 other tanks (Table 2) and 14 additional tanks (Table 3). Tables list surface area, species present, and numbers stocked in 1964 for each water body.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stock tanks.** Many tanks dried or became dangerously low during the 1963-64 winter and before summer rains in 1964. Some tanks lost their warm-water fish populations, including [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Common Carp]]. Dry tanks were restocked with [[Rainbow Trout]]. Converting warm-water tanks to trout production produced a reported increase of over 100 percent in trout management compared with the prior year. Channel catfish in warm-water tanks showed good growth and were regularly caught by late fall.

**Black River.** The Black River becomes the [[Salt River]] at its confluence with the White River and forms part of the boundary with the Fort Apache Indian Reservation. Along 108 river miles on the reservation, the upper 16 miles hold a self-sustaining trout population. Below the trout water, the river was stocked with [[Smallmouth Bass]] in 1964 (the stocked count is garbled in the scan), and no major game fish had been present there before. Summer data over two years suggest the stream could support trout roughly 20 miles further downstream in some years. Intensive management is deferred until access and public-use facilities are provided.

**Salt River.** Contains a self-sustaining channel catfish population. Intensive management is not recommended until access is improved.

**San Carlos River.** Rises from two intermittent creeks and flows about 20 miles southwest, then south about 10 miles to [[San Carlos Reservoir]]. Permanent water runs roughly 17 miles from the headwaters to a diversion dam, where the entire flow is diverted into an irrigation canal during part of the year. Stable flow comes from springs, including [[Warm Springs]] (about 85°F, emerging from Naco Formation limestone). Below the diversion dam, the river flows across a floodplain as an intermittent stream with no fishery.

**Warm Springs.** Fish sampling found [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Bonytail]] (an endangered species). [[Rotenone Treatment|Rotenone]] was applied to reduce green sunfish and bonytail populations. In August-September 1964, [[Tilapia]] were stocked; by late 1964 they averaged nearly eight inches. Because tilapia cannot tolerate cold water (the scan suggests about 55°F), they cannot overwinter in ponds, so the plan was to collect brood fish from Warm Springs each spring and stock them in reservation ponds.

**Seneca Lake (27.0 acres).** Stocked with catchable-size rainbow trout in 1964, with [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Golden Shiner]] also present. It provided 7,072 angler-days, and the report expects more angling if public facilities are developed.

## Key Findings

- **Angling pressure:** An estimated 12,670 angler-days on reservation waters in 1964. Seneca Lake accounted for about 7,072 (55.8%) and tanks about 5,598. Stream fishing was negligible.
- **Stocking:** Trout were the main stocked species; largemouth bass stocked in six tanks; channel catfish, bluegill, and golden shiners distributed in several warm-water tanks.
- **Trout expansion:** Conversion of tanks to trout and the addition of seven tanks to the trout program were expected to increase angling in 1965. Major increases will depend on tribal construction of lakes designed for fishing.
- **Public facilities:** Campgrounds, picnic areas, toilets, and water supplies are needed at Seneca Lake and other sites to attract visitors. Without them, the Tribe will not receive significant economic benefit.
- **Park Creek dam site:** A proposed dam would create a lake of about 100 surface acres, with a crest length of 330 feet. Estimated potential is 25,000 angler-days annually, all within 30 miles of Phoenix. The report projects a gross annual income for the Tribe based on about $9.25 per angler-day (the expenditure figure is partly garbled in the scan).
- **Sawmill Creek dam site:** A dam about 35 feet high would create a 35-acre lake, with an estimated 10,000 angler-days annually and a projected gross annual income of $42,500.00 to the Tribe, contingent on road access and services.
- **Management priorities:** Black River trout expansion is planned for a future special report. Salt River and San Carlos River management are deferred until access and facilities improve.
- **Overall recommendation:** Build additional fishing lakes and public facilities to meet growing recreation demand, create jobs, and generate income for tribal members if the Tribe pursues recreation as an enterprise.
