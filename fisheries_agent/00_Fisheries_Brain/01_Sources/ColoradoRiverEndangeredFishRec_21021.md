# Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program: FY1990 Research Proposal Guidance

#ichthyology #marine-biology #colorado-river-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is a solicitation and guidance packet issued by the [[Technical Group]] of the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for endangered fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin. It asks researchers, managers and interested individuals to submit proposals for Fiscal Year 1990 (FY-90) research and recovery activities, using standardized forms (a two-page summary, Form 1, and an eight-page scope of work, Form 2). The program expects to have almost $2.4 million available from October 1989. The guidance covers five recovery elements: habitat management, habitat development and maintenance, stocking of native fishes, non-native control and sportfishing, and research, monitoring and data management. The stated objective is to recover and delist the [[Colorado Squawfish]], the [[Humpback Chub]] and the [[Bonytail Chub]], and to manage the [[Razorback Sucker]] so that it does not need listing under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Background & Description

The packet describes the four rare species of the upper basin. Recovery goals are set in species-specific recovery plans prepared by the [[Fish And Wildlife Service]] (FWS), and the Recovery Program document is cited throughout for its objectives, priorities and monitoring definitions. Parties to the program include three Federal agencies and three States, each with differing missions, which the packet identifies as a reason coordinated research and monitoring is essential.

The solicitation process is scheduled as follows:

- **Dec. 15, 1988:** FY-88 annual reports and FY-89 scopes of work due; FY-90 needs defined.
- **Feb. 1989:** Technical Group meetings and researcher meetings.
- **March 21, 1989:** Solicitation letter issued by Jim Bennett, Chairman of the Technical Group.
- **May 1, 1989:** FY-90 proposals due to the Technical Group chairman.
- **June 15, 1989:** Technical Group ranks existing projects and new proposals.
- **Aug. 1989:** Management Group and [[Implementation Committee]] approve the FY-90 Annual Work Plan (AWP) and budget.

Form 2 requires a title, relation to the program, goals and end products, study area, methods, task descriptions, a schedule, a budget by task (labor, travel, equipment, other, total), and a budget summary. Multi-year studies need only brief (one to two paragraph) summaries for FY-91 and beyond. Submission does not guarantee funding; selected work must also comply with State and Federal procurement rules.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The packet identifies the following ecological concerns:

- **Predation and competition** have been hypothesized as major limiting factors for the rare fishes. The packet states that physical and chemical habitat alone may not lead to recovery without consideration of biological interactions.
- **Non-native fishes** may compete with or prey on the rare species. In higher-than-average flow years such as 1983 and 1984 in the [[Yampa River]], the reproductive success of native species exceeded that of non-native species.
- **Instream flow** links habitat to fish populations. The [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) is considered useful, but the packet notes unresolved uncertainty in correlating fish populations with flow rates and volumes, so research and new data are to be continually integrated.
- **Habitat development** is directed at increasing habitat quantity and overcoming quality limits such as water temperature, water chemistry, turbidity and predatory fish.
- **Disease and genetic variability** are recognized as key concerns for hatchery propagation. The packet notes an Asian tapeworm concern in both hatchery and wild stocks.
- **Water allocation** is central. Priority reaches for instream flow protection are the [[Yampa River]] first, then the [[White River]] and the 15-mile reach of the [[Colorado River]] between Palisade and the [[Gunnison River]].

## Key Findings

**Research, monitoring and data management**

- Research goals: identify recovery criteria, test management strategies, examine the needs of the rare fishes, and clarify relationships among program elements.
- FY-90 research priorities: (1) complete research in the Gila taxonomy work plan (due February 16, 1990); (2) evaluate effects of tagging and handling on mortality; (3) assess inter- and intraspecific interactions, including which species and life stages compete or prey on one another, which variables influence interactions, and how interactions might be manipulated.
- Monitoring is defined as an ongoing program to determine status and population trends of rare species, tracking progress toward recovery. FY-90 monitoring priorities include developing quantitative, scientifically based recovery goals, maintaining the interagency standardized monitoring program with statistically valid results, and using video image interpretation to document channel morphology and habitat conditions.
- Data management priorities: maintain and update existing systems, and ensure all researchers follow data collection and management guidelines. The Recovery Program document calls for a centralized data management system with high initial priority.

**Non-native and sportfishing control**

- Test whether competition with non-native fishes affects endangered fish abundance and distribution, using controlled laboratory and field research.
- Assemble existing data to build testable hypotheses on how flows and temperatures could be managed to favor native fishes, then test them experimentally.
- A Colorado squawfish experimental sportfishery based on hatchery-reared fish is being implemented at [[Kenney Reservoir]] on the White River near Rangely, Colorado, as a substitute for potentially harmful species such as bass and pike.
- Colorado has restricted stocking of non-native fishes in the upper basin where conflicts with endangered fishes are possible; similar measures are recommended for Utah.
- Utah and Colorado began a creel survey in FY-89 to determine the nature and extent of incidental take.
- Recommended actions include mapping sources of exotic fishes, an information and education program (a five-year plan to be developed by an ad hoc subcommittee in 1989), a deterrent enforcement program, removal of exotic fishes from grow-out ponds and nursery areas, and an exotic fishes issues paper.

**Habitat development and maintenance**

- Habitat development has not been practiced for rare fishes in the upper basin, though it has been applied to introduced sportfish. Research on [[Grow-Out Ponds]] has been conducted (Kaeding and Osmundson, 1987), and a 1986 FWS report examined fish passage at the [[Redlands Diversion]]; the packet notes that report did not address the biological benefits of range expansion. FWS planned to complete its fish passage assessment in FY-89.
- FY-90 priority tasks include: (1) an issue paper and literature review on the role of habitat development in recovery (priority 1); (2) non-flow alternatives for adult habitat in the 15-mile reach (priority 2); (3) life stage limitations for young squawfish and razorback sucker below the Gunnison River (priority 3); (4) backwater habitat, designed to exclude exotic fishes, with a demonstration project (priority 4); (5) spawning habitat expansion with a demonstration project (priority 5); and (6) evaluation of grow-out ponds as rearing sites (priority 6).

**Habitat management**

- Habitat management has two objectives: identify habitat requirements and flow needs for all life stages, and legally protect instream flows consistent with State water law.
- Research on the four species is expected to continue throughout the 15-year recovery period. Future emphasis is on empirical data that quantify incremental relationships between species response (growth, survival, catch rate, movement) and habitat, including flow.
- Section 7 consultation on federal reservoirs sets release volume and timing. [[Flaming Gorge]] consultation is scheduled to conclude in June 1990. Studies on the [[Aspinall Unit]] are to begin in FY-90 and continue through FY-92, focusing on humpback chub year-round flows in Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon and flows that enhance young squawfish growth and survival.
- Approximately 10,000 acre-feet of stored water from [[Ruedi Reservoir]] is earmarked for supplementing deficient habitat.
- About $1 million is available for purchasing water rights from willing sellers on the Yampa and Colorado rivers. The packet states that acquiring "wet" water is essential by the end of FY-90 to show material progress.
- Preliminary flow recommendations for the Yampa River and the 15-mile reach are scheduled for FY-90, with validation studies possibly needed. Studies on the [[San Juan River]] depend on its 1989 summary report and Management Plan, and on New Mexico's participation.

**Stocking native fishes**

- Stocking research aims to produce enough fish for life history research, augment populations with disease-free and genetically healthy stock, and establish refugia, with emphasis on bonytail.
- Bonytail have been reintroduced into the Green River system; further research is needed to evaluate reintroductions and set criteria for future releases.
- Grow-out ponds have shown rapid Colorado squawfish growth when competition is absent and forage is adequate, but optimal rearing conditions remain to be determined.
- FY-90 tasks include: concurrent food habit studies of larval fishes and competitors in hatchery, grow-out and backwater nursery settings; refugia criteria and site evaluation; marking and tagging methods for augmentation; culture research (loading densities, feeding, stress management, reproductive physiology); an Asian tapeworm management protocol; razorback sucker research at the Ouray National Wildlife Refuge facility; a genetics management plan; and systematics of wild and hatchery razorback sucker.
