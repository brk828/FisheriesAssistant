# Solar-Heated Brine Shrimp Hatchery and Environmentally Controlled Seawater Systems for Marine Finfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae #sciaenidae #serranidae #centropomidae #carangidae

## Executive Summary

This document contains two technical notes on aquaculture infrastructure. The first describes a low-cost, solar-heated [[Brine Shrimp]] hatchery built at the [[Fayetteville State Fish Hatchery]] in North Carolina to supply live food for newly hatched [[Striped Bass]] fry. The second describes duplicate, environmentally controlled recirculating seawater systems at the [[Florida Bureau of Marine Research]] for holding and spawning large marine finfish. Both notes focus on husbandry engineering, water heating and water quality rather than on wild populations, and neither reports field survey data.

## Background & Description

**Solar-heated hatchery (Fayetteville, NC).** Striped bass fry (4–7 days old) must be fed large numbers of brine shrimp nauplii (*Artemia* sp.) to initiate feeding before stocking into production ponds. Brine shrimp eggs require incubation water at roughly 21–27 °C (70–81 °F) to hatch. Mean April–May water temperature at Fayetteville is 20 °C (68 °F), so supplemental heat is needed during the striped bass fry production season. Oil furnaces, electric heaters and aquarium heaters were judged costly, inconvenient and unreliable, which motivated a simple solar design.

The system is built around:
- **Solar panel:** a 122 × 244 cm box frame on styrofoam insulation, holding two 60 m coils of flexible black plastic waterline under a clear fiberglass cover, facing south at 45° and placed beside the greenhouse.
- **Greenhouse:** a 3 × 3 × 2.4 m clear-fiberglass structure housing the hatchery.
- **Hatchery:** one 114-L (30-gal) water bath containing eight 5-L (1-gal) hatching jars, plus a buried 159-L (41-gal) hot water tank outside the greenhouse and a 159-L greenhouse storage tank beneath the bath.
- **Controls:** a repeat-cycle timer, a thermostat with probe, a water pump and five gate valves.

Reported materials cost was approximately US$660 for the greenhouse, solar panel and hatchery combined (1980 prices).

**Marine finfish culture facility (Florida).** Experimental facilities at the Florida Department of Natural Resources Bureau of Marine Research were expanded so that replicate studies of large marine finfish could run concurrently. Three of nine planned duplicate environmentally controlled systems were completed in 1982. Design criteria were: (1) the largest feasible tanks within a 24.4 × 18.3 × 3.0 m (80 × 60 × 10 ft) wet lab, (2) duplicate systems for statistical comparison of concurrent environmental regimes, and (3) full control and monitoring of environmental parameters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Thermal regime (hatchery).** During sunny days the water bath is held at 27–38 °C (81–100 °F) through greenhouse solar gain, while water in the solar panel reaches 43–49 °C (109–120 °F). The timer activates the pump every 15 minutes, moving about 19 L (5 gal) of heated water from the panel to the underground tank, with an equal volume returned through the greenhouse storage tank, forming a closed loop. The system stores about 433 L (114 gal), enough to incubate eight 5-L jars for three days. At night the panel is isolated to limit heat loss. When the bath falls below 27 °C (81 °F) the thermostat runs the pump from the underground tank until the bath reaches 28 °C (82 °F).

**Recirculating seawater system (Florida).** Each system pairs two fiberglass tanks (total water volume 20,000 L, 5,284 gal) with artificial seawater, recirculation and biological filtration inside an environmentally controlled room. The principal components are:
- **Holding tank:** 4.3 × 3.0 × 1.2 m (14 × 10 × 4 ft), 13,000 L (3,435 gal), with rounded corners and airlifts at vertical corners to prevent debris accumulation and maintain flow. A 7.6 cm (3 in) bottom drain with an outside standpipe controls water depth and directs pelagic eggs onto a collecting screen.
- **Settling tank:** 510 L (135 gal), using baffles to remove large particulates before they reach the biological filter.
- **Biological filter tank:** 4.9 × 3.0 × 0.8 m (16 × 10 × 2.6 ft), supporting the holding tank. A false bottom holds 30–46 cm (12–18 in) of 0.9 cm lime rock, which provides substrate for nitrifying bacteria and pH buffering.
- **Pumping:** a 1 HP pump on a 3.8 cm (1.5 in) network.

Rectangular tanks were preferred because they use floor space efficiently and can be stacked. The design is reported to maintain high water quality with minimal disease problems.

## Key Findings

- **Solar heating reduced cost.** At the Fayetteville hatchery, the solar system reduced heating costs by approximately 50% compared with an oil furnace and electric aquarium heaters.
- **Hatch rate improved.** The percent hatch of brine shrimp eggs reportedly increased by about one-third after conversion, possibly because eggs receive direct sunlight. This is an informal observation, not a controlled comparison.
- **Hatchery performance targets were met.** Water bath temperatures stayed within the required range during sunny days, and the stored volume supported three days of incubation for eight jars.
- **Replicate marine systems were built.** Three of nine planned duplicate environmentally controlled systems were completed in 1982, enabling statistical comparison of concurrent environmental regimes.
- **Recirculation supports marine species work.** The system has been used to maintain and spawn [[Florida Pompano]] and [[Red Drum]], and current work targets conditioning and spawning of [[Gag]] and [[Snook]].
- **Filtration design.** Settling baffles, a lime-rock biological filter and airlift-assisted flow are the main water-quality measures described.
- **Limitations.** The notes report engineering and husbandry results only. They give no field population data, no detailed water-quality parameters (for example, ammonia, pH or dissolved oxygen values), and no statistical testing of hatch rates or cost savings.
