# Management Plan for the Razorback Sucker in California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This 1985 informational bulletin from the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (CDFG), Region 5, sets out a [[Management Plan]] for the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a State-listed endangered fish. The species was once abundant across the [[Colorado River]] drainage but has declined to a handful of individuals in California. The plan's primary goal is to improve the species' status in the state by securing existing populations, establishing new self-sustaining populations within its historic range, and supporting a long-term [[Reintroduction Program]]. It relies on a decade of research and on Arizona's active recovery effort.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was first described as *Catostomus texanus* from the Colorado and New rivers of California (Abbott 1861). Historically it was found throughout the mainstream Colorado River and major tributaries, from Wyoming to Sonora and Baja California del Norte, Mexico. Its range also extended into the [[Salton Sea]] until it disappeared as salinity increased.

The species was once among the most abundant large fishes in the basin. It was an important food for Indian tribes and was harvested commercially in southern Arizona. It was common enough to be collected "by the wagon-load" from canals and the Salt River near Phoenix. Concentrations in the lower basin were reported until the early 1950s, after which numbers declined precipitously.

Its status is tied to other endemic Colorado basin fishes that are also listed: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The razorback sucker is the fourth large endemic fish of the system to decline in this way.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Upper basin (Colorado, Utah, Wyoming).** The species was never abundant here. Recent collections show further decline, and surviving populations consist of large, presumably old individuals. Surveys of the [[Green River]] in 1979–81 found only 92 razorbacks, and the species is considered extremely rare there. Two gravel pits near Grand Junction, Colorado, including Walker Wildlife Area, yielded 37 of the 52 razorbacks collected in one study. Only one specimen has recently been reported from the Gunnison River. In 1977 a San Juan River reservoir was emptied, stranding 250 razorbacks. No razorbacks have been taken in Wyoming since [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closed in 1962.

**Lower basin (Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico, California).** Fishery surveys since the early 1970s confirm a continued decline. Remnant populations persist in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Mead]], and Lake Mohave holds the largest known natural population. These populations are dominated by senescent adults, with no evidence of recruitment. Larval razorbacks are commonly found in Lake Mohave, but they do not reach the adult population. Exotic fishes, principally [[Green Sunfish]], prey heavily on recruitment. Razorbacks have not been recorded in New Mexico, although they likely once occurred in the San Juan River.

**Reasons for decline.** The main causes are:
- Physical, chemical, and biological alteration of the river through dam construction (1930s–1950s).
- Mainstream [[Channelization]] and dredging, which removed quiet backwaters and oxbows needed for spawning, nursery, and refuge habitat.
- Introductions of exotic fishes by agencies and through accidental or incidental releases. Predation on eggs, larvae, and fingerlings is documented and may significantly suppress lower-basin populations.

Vanicek et al. (1970) reported altered seasonal flow and temperature regimes along 104 km (65 miles) of the Green River below Flaming Gorge Dam, severe enough to exclude native fishes, including razorback.

**California populations.** Razorbacks in California persist in [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] (Imperial County), [[West Pond]], and possibly the mainstream river at undetectably low numbers. Senator Wash is associated with [[Imperial Reservoir]].

## Key Findings

**Regulatory status**
- Listed as endangered by California in January 1974.
- Protected in Nevada, and classed as "Group 3" in Arizona.
- Federal endangered listing was proposed in 1978 and withdrawn; the species was dropped from consideration in 1980.
- After a 1981 petition by the American Fisheries Society (Bonneville chapter), it was designated a federal [[Candidate Species]] in December 1982, under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
- The Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team recommends listing as threatened, at least in the upper basin.

**Senator Wash Reservoir population**
- A [[Mark-Recapture Estimation]] in August 1980 gave an adult population of 54 ± 22 (95% confidence limits).
- Larval razorbacks were collected over three years (1980–83), but no young were taken in subsequent sampling. There is no recruitment beyond the larval stage.
- A [[Telemetry Study]] (1980–81) documented seasonal and daily movements and depth and habitat preferences.
- Preferred spawning substrate data suggest historic spawning sites along the Colorado River.

**Lower mainstream surveys**
- A 1981 CDFG netting survey from Parker Dam south to the U.S.–Mexico boundary caught no razorbacks in 2,500 hours of effort, despite gear known to be selective for the species.
- Young razorbacks were captured in 1973–74 from ponds filled from the East Highline Canal near Niland, and seven young were collected in 1984 from lateral reservoirs near the Coachella Canal.
- Adults were taken sporadically at scattered sites, including Lake Havasu, Topock Gorge, and the mouth of Bright Angel Creek in the Grand Canyon.

**Propagation and stocking**
- [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico) has propagated razorbacks since 1981; more than 10 million fry and fingerlings had been stocked in Arizona by the time of the report.
- Stocking targets include 25–30 cm (10–12 inch) fish for Senator Wash Reservoir, and 10–15 cm (4–6 inch) fish for the mainstream river.
- Grow-out sites include the [[Niland Facilities]] and the [[Blythe Golf Course Pond]].
- Fry and fingerlings from Senator Wash spawning showed excellent growth and low mortality in rearing trials.

**Recovery actions planned**
1. Secure self-sustaining populations in Senator Wash Reservoir and West Pond, and reestablish a viable population in the lower Colorado River (Parker, Imperial, and Topock divisions).
2. Obtain written concurrence from the Arizona Game and Fish Department, the [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]], and the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] to stock the mainstream.
3. Establish West Pond as a native fishes refugium, including through a Bureau of Land Management habitat plan and renovation funded under the Sikes Act.
4. Conduct annual or biannual monitoring of Senator Wash and the mainstream to track movement, habitat use, recruitment, and losses to exotic predators.
5. Cease stocking exotic fishes in river reaches, reservoirs, and backwaters targeted for razorback reestablishment.

**Arizona and Nevada context**
- A 1981 Memorandum of Understanding between the Arizona Game and Fish Department and USFWS Region 2 set a 10-year reintroduction period. Federal listing would be pursued if populations had not reestablished by 1991.
- Nevada has protected the species, and razorbacks from Dexter are being reared in ponds to support recovery.
