# The Use of Instream Habitat Improvement Methodology in Mitigating the Adverse Effects of River Regulation on Fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by Stephen Swaies (pp. 186–208 of a regulated-river management volume) reviews [[Instream Habitat Improvement]] methodology and evaluates its actual and potential use in mitigating the effects of [[River Regulation]] on fish populations. It contrasts two regulation pathways: [[River Channelization]] (straightening, widening, dredging, clearance), where natural recovery is possible but often interrupted by maintenance, and [[Impoundment]] (dams and reservoirs), where flow control is chronic and natural recovery is much less likely. The chapter argues that habitat improvement structures can restore pool-riffle habitat, bank cover and substrate, and that these tools are increasingly shifting from fisheries enhancement toward mitigation of channel modification. It concludes that such methods should be designed into regulation schemes at the outset, with engineers and biologists negotiating acceptable compromises.

## Background & Description

The chapter is a review rather than a primary field study. It states its aim as reviewing stream habitat improvement methodology, describing the main techniques, and discussing their use in mitigating regulation impacts. Historically, conventional mitigation for impounded salmonid fisheries has relied on [[Fishways]], artificial spawning channels and hatcheries, while channelized rivers have had few mitigation attempts until recent decades. Habitat improvement devices were first pioneered in the midwestern United States in the early 1930s, and the field has since been applied to waters from small headwater streams to large lowland rivers such as the [[Mississippi River]].

Three categories of improvement structures are described:
- **Structures that impound or modify flow:** [[Current Deflectors]], [[Low Dams]] and weirs, and [[Bank Protection Devices]] (groynes, jetties, revetments).
- **Structures providing direct cover:** artificial bank overhangs, floating or platform cover, [[Boulder Placement]], and [[Half-Log Cover]] devices.
- **Structures or treatments modifying channel substrate:** deflectors and dams that scour sediment, gravel reintroduction, and bed disturbance.

The chapter notes that fish habitat is limited by five components: space, water quality, flow, cover and food. It also stresses that habitat needs differ across life stages (spawning, juvenile rearing, adult rearing), so improvement programs should target the limiting factor for the stage in question.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Scale of impacts.** A U.S. study of 42 channelization projects in 18 states found more than 3000 km of stream severely affected. In Britain, approximately 25% of lowland rivers in England and Wales were affected by channelization since the 1930s. A review of 81 impounded Pacific Northwest case histories found that 76% showed decreased salmonid populations, with reductions most often linked to reduced post-project flows.

**Channelization effects.** Straightening reduces channel length and carrying capacity; in one Iowa study more than 1000 mi of stream were lost. Straightened channels have steeper gradients and higher velocities, causing scouring and deepening. Widening reduces water depth and removes bankside cover, increases bank erosion, and can turn a stream into a uniform canal.

**Impoundment effects.** Dams act through barriers to migration, altered flow regimes (reduced low flows, increased high flows, changed fluctuation amplitude), and altered water quality (temperature, suspended sediment, dissolved oxygen). Flow velocity strongly influences salmonid behavior and dominance among species.

**Recovery.** Benthic invertebrates often recover first, in some cases within 5 months after dredging, though some populations had not recovered after 52 years. Fish recovery is slower because of longer generation times and more diverse habitat needs. Fish populations in some channelized areas had not recovered after 33 years (Kentucky) or 40 years (North Carolina), and a Victorian (Australia) channelized river (1888) still supported lower fish abundance than natural sites. Recovery is faster where unmodified refuge reaches exist.

**Sediment and dredging.** Bank erosion was estimated to supply 55% of the sediment load of a Michigan trout stream. Dredging can replace stable gravel with shifting sand or silt, reducing benthic food and spawning habitat.

## Key Findings

- **Channelization is more damaging to habitat diversity than to flow alone.** Loss of the pool-riffle pattern drives reduced fish diversity and abundance.
- **Habitat improvement can produce measurable fish gains.** In the St. Regis River (Montana), trout populations rose by up to 200% one year after installing deflectors and rock clusters. In England, cyprinid density and biomass in channelized reaches increased by up to 75% and 31%, respectively, one year after low dams, deflectors and cover devices were installed.
- **Mitigated sections can match natural ones.** Barton and Winger (Weber River, Utah) found invertebrate and fish communities in improved channelized reaches similar to natural areas within 6 months.
- **Gravel restoration can work but needs stabilization.** At Clear Creek, Oregon, spawning chinook salmon increased to 137 after gravel introduction, compared with 34 in remaining gravel. Gravel restoration failed in some high-sediment Danish rivers.
- **Impoundment mitigation is partial.** The Norwegian Weir Project installed nearly 400 weirs or low dams since 1964. Benefits included improved scenic and biological quality, but impacts could not be fully separated from regulation itself. In the Skjoma River, juvenile Atlantic salmon declined while brown trout increased, suggesting a possible shift in species composition.
- **Design caveats.** Double-wing deflectors should be limited to channels narrowed by no more than about 50%. Low dams can block fish movement, so keeping them low (submerged at high flows) is advised. Structures should be installed parallel to flow and kept low enough to drown out at high flows.
- **Conclusion.** Habitat improvement should be integrated into regulation design from the start, with empirical monitoring and engineer-biologist negotiation defining acceptable compromises. This is framed as a valuable tool in alternatives to traditional regulation.
