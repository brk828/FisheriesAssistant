# Some Effects of Plants and Animals on the Conditions in Fresh-Water Streams with Particular Reference to Their Oxygen Balance

#aquatic-ecology #stream-ecology #oxygen-dynamics #macrophytes #benthic-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This paper describes research conducted at the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] in Stevenage, England, examining how rooted aquatic plants and benthic macro-invertebrates affect the oxygen balance in small streams. The study develops and applies methods for measuring photosynthesis, respiration, and oxygen exchange in shallow rivers where bottom-dwelling organisms significantly influence water quality. Key findings demonstrate that in streams like the [[River Ivel]], plants contribute approximately 3500 g oxygen/m²/year through photosynthesis while consuming about 3000 g oxygen/m²/year through respiration and decay. Macro-invertebrates, particularly [[Chironomus]] larvae and tubificid worms, indirectly affect oxygen dynamics through irrigation of sediments, stabilization of deposits, and feeding activities that alter decomposition processes.

## Background & Description

Dissolved oxygen concentration serves as a critical index of water quality in rivers and lakes. Understanding the processes governing oxygen supply and utilization is essential for assessing the organic load a water system can receive without oxygen depletion that would kill fish and reduce recreational value. In shallow rivers, rooted plants and attached algae flourish, and bottom deposits become significant when current velocities permit fine organic material deposition.

The research employs methods developed by [[Vinberg]] and [[Odum]] for calculating photosynthesis and respiration from diurnal changes in dissolved oxygen concentration. The oxygen balance between upstream and downstream stations depends on: (1) oxygen release through photosynthesis, (2) oxygen uptake through respiration by plants, animals, and aerobic bacteria, and (3) oxygen exchange with air depending on saturation state.

The fundamental equation is: Q = P + D - R, where Q = rate of oxygen gain/loss per unit surface area, P = photosynthesis rate, R = oxygen utilization rate, and D = diffusion rate. During darkness: Q = D - R, allowing calculation of exchange coefficients and respiration rates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[River Ivel]] in Bedfordshire, England, serves as the primary study site—an unpolluted chalk stream with pronounced diurnal oxygen rhythms. Water velocities rarely exceed 6 cm/sec, making surface oxygen exchange relatively unimportant compared to benthal community metabolism. The river supports dense growths of aquatic macrophytes including [[Callitriche]], [[Hippuris vulgaris]], [[Berula erecta]], [[Ranunculus]], and [[Potamogeton pectinatus]].

Studies extended to other hard-water streams: [[River Test]], [[River Yare]], and [[River Chess]] in southern England, plus polluted systems including [[River Colne]], [[River Lark]], [[River Hiz]], and the [[Thames Estuary]]. These sites represent gradients from unpolluted to organically enriched conditions with varying plant and invertebrate communities.

Temperature affects respiration rates with a Q₁₀ of approximately 2 for benthal communities within normal environmental ranges. Oxygen consumption by plants varies with oxygen concentration following the relation R' = aC^b, where R' is respiration per unit dry weight, C is oxygen concentration, and a and b are constants. Laboratory studies show [[Callitriche]], [[Hippuris]], and [[Ranunculus]] exhibit oxygen-dependent respiration between 1.5 and 16.0 ppm dissolved oxygen.

## Key Findings

**Stream Productivity Measurements:**
- Exchange coefficients determined using "opaque-sheet" and "sulphite" techniques showed good agreement (TABLE 1), ranging from 4.2 to 8.9 cm/h in the River Ivel
- Photosynthetic efficiency averaged 1.5% (±0.4%) of surface radiation between 3000-30,000 Å
- Annual photosynthetic oxygen production in River Ivel: approximately 3500 g O₂/m²
- Annual oxygen consumption: approximately 3000 g O₂/m²
- Plants responsible for ~70% of community oxygen consumption during summer

**Plant Growth and Oxygen Balance:**
- Net productivity of macrophytes ranged 1.44-2.30 g organic carbon/m²/day across four rivers (TABLE 2)
- Equivalent to 3.8-6.1 g oxygen/m²/day net release
- Assimilation efficiency averaged 0.45% of total solar radiation
- About two-thirds of energy fixed during growth dissipated through respiration
- Maximum standing crop in River Ivel: 181 g organic carbon/m² (September 1958)
- Emergent plants (7% of Berula crop above water) lose photosynthetic oxygen directly to atmosphere

**Effects of Pollution on Plant Communities:**
- Sewage effluent increased ammonia (0.15 to 0.24 ppm N), nitrate (7.8 to 9.6 ppm N), and phosphate (0.22 to 0.54 ppm P)
- Similar maximum crops in unpolluted (34 g C/m²) and polluted shaded reaches (42 g C/m²)
- Much higher crop in unshaded polluted reach (70 g C/m²)
- Growth limited by solar radiation, not nutrients, in these systems

**Macro-invertebrate Effects:**

*Direct Respiratory Impact:*
- [[Chironomus riparius]] densities of 100,000/m² consume ~0.45 g O₂/m²/h at 20°C above 4 ppm dissolved oxygen
- Thames Estuary tubificid populations (3.9 × 10⁵/m²) show oxygen consumption of ~0.45 g O₂/m²/h
- Mud oxygen consumption dependent on dissolved oxygen concentration above 1 ppm (FIG. 7)
- River Ivel mud respiration: 2.2 g O₂/m²/day at 20°C

*Indirect Effects - Irrigation:*
- Chironomus larvae at 12,500-50,000/m² increased depth of oxidized sludge layer
- Redox potential isopleths drifted deeper after larval introduction (FIG. 9)
- Effect reversed after pupation and emergence (~25 days)
- Larvae draw water currents for respiration, increasing oxygen supply beyond surface diffusion

*Indirect Effects - Stabilization:*
- Tube-dwelling chironomid larvae produce silk threads preventing sediment scour
- Deposits with low larval densities more resistant to erosion than uninhabited deposits
- Effect diminished after pupation and emergence
- Critical scour velocity substantially affected by larval presence

*Indirect Effects - Feeding:*
- Thames Estuary tubificids (3.9 × 10⁵/m²) ingest 100-200 g dry mud/m²/day
- [[Potamopyrgus jenkinsi]] snails (50,000/m²) produce up to 140 g dry faeces/m²/day
- Chironomus emergence represents organic matter loss from system
- River Colne: estimated 160 g dry weight midges/m²/year lost through emergence (five emergences March-October)
- This loss exceeds maximum macrophyte standing crop (120 g dry weight/m²) in same river

**Particle Translocation:**
- Tubificids transfer particles from depth to surface much more effectively than Chironomus larvae
- Radioactive tracer experiments: ~100× more activity reached surface with tubificids
- Difference related to feeding habits: tubificids ingest below surface and deposit faeces on top; Chironomus primarily surface feeders

**Methodological Advances:**
- Opaque plastic sheets suspended over 100-yard stretches prevent photosynthesis for exchange coefficient determination
- Sodium sulphite addition with cobalt catalyst creates controlled deoxygenation for measuring re-aeration
- Polarographic respirometers measure oxygen consumption of intact mud cores
- Scandium glass particle tracers track sediment mixing by invertebrates
