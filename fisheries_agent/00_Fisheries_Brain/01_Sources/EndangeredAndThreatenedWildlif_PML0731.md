# Spikedace (Meda fulgida) Threatened Status Final Rule, 1986

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) published a final rule in the Federal Register on July 1, 1986 (effective July 31, 1986) listing the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) as a threatened species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, as amended. The species has been eliminated from more than 95 percent of its historic range in the [[Gila River Basin]] because of dams, channel alteration, riparian destruction, water diversion, groundwater pumping, and competition and predation from exotic fishes. Designation of [[Critical Habitat]] was postponed to no later than June 1987. The rule also includes a [[Special Rule]] allowing certain take under State collection permits and incidental take by recreational anglers.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** First collected in 1851 from the Río San Pedro in Arizona and described in 1859 (the source gives "1850"; the OCR text is ambiguous). It is the only species in the genus *Meda*.
- **Morphology:** A small (under 75 mm, about 3 in) slim fish with very small scales and spines in the dorsal and pelvic fins. Breeding males develop a brassy golden color.
- **Habitat preference:** Moderate to large perennial streams, shallow riffles over gravel and rubble, moderate to swift currents, and swift pools over sand or gravel.
- **Life history:** Resurrent flooding is important to its life history; the species evolved with floods, moving outward with spreading water to avoid the heaviest flows, while non-native fishes lack this adaptation.
- **Historic range:** Once common throughout much of the Verde, Aqua Fria, San Pedro, San Francisco, and Gila rivers (upstream of Phoenix), up to about 1,800 m (5,900 ft) elevation. The historic range is about 2,500 km (1,600 miles) of stream.
- **Current range:** About 190 km (118 miles) of presently occupied habitat, roughly 6 percent of the historic range (other passages state approximately 9 percent). Occupied sites include about 24 km (15 miles) of [[Aravaipa Creek]], about 108 km (67 miles) of the upper [[Gila River]], about 57 km (35 miles) of the upper Verde River, and a small section of [[Eagle Creek]].
- **Land ownership:** Mixed. The [[Bureau of Land Management]] administers much of Aravaipa Canyon (designated Wilderness), the Defenders of Wildlife owns the George Whittell Wildlife Preserve, and the [[U.S. Forest Service]] manages much of the upper Gila River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats to habitat:**
- Conversion of flowing water to still water by impoundment; altered flow regimes; altered water temperatures, silt and bed loads; loss of marshes and backwaters; and channel changes from defined, vegetated channels to deeply incised arroyos with uniform substrate.
- Causes include dams, water diversion, channel downcutting, groundwater pumping, channelization, riparian vegetation destruction, erosion, mining, grazing, and watershed disturbance.
- Remaining habitat faces further loss from [[Central Arizona Project]] water exchanges, diversions on the upper Verde River, and dams proposed for the upper Gila River (see [[Conner Dam]]).

**Biological interactions:**
- The introduced [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) has a competitive advantage in altered habitats, and its spread is facilitated by impoundments and flow control.
- Predatory exotics include channel catfish, flathead catfish, black and yellow bullheads, green sunfish, largemouth and smallmouth bass, and brown trout. Native roundtail chub is also a potential predator.
- Reservoirs supply a source of exotic species and increase their habitat.

**Regulatory context:**
- New Mexico lists the species as endangered (Group 2); Arizona lists it as threatened (Group 3), with little habitat protection.
- Neither New Mexico nor Arizona water law historically provided instream flow rights for fish.
- The State of New Mexico's bait-minnow policy allows red shiner use, which encourages its spread.

## Key Findings

- **Listing decision:** The species is classified as threatened, not endangered, because it is still locally abundant across about 190 km of stream and is not in danger of extinction. Its range has nonetheless been drastically reduced, and threats to remaining populations are imminent.
- **Range reduction:** Roughly 95 percent of the historic range has been lost. The Conner Dam alternative would inundate about 14 km (8.5 miles) in the small-dam option, or up to 29 km (18 miles), with a loss of 27 percent of the existing Gila River range.
- **Critical habitat:** Designation was postponed and must be made by June 1987 under Section 4(b)(6)(C)(ii). A concurrent designation was not required because prompt listing was judged essential.
- **Take regulation:** A special rule (Section 17.44(p)) permits take for educational, scientific, propagation, and conservation purposes under State permits, and incidental take by anglers who immediately return fish.
- **Public comment:** 107 people attended three public hearings (Silver City, Phoenix, Thatcher), with 38 oral or written statements in total: 16 in support, 12 in opposition, and 5 neutral (figures vary across the text). Opposition came from the Town of Silver City, the Hooker Dam Association, the Arizona Cattle Growers' Association, the Arizona Mining Association, and the Soil Conservation Service, among others.
- **Corrected figures:** The planned City of Prescott/Yavapai-Prescott Indian Community diversion was corrected to 10.5 cubic feet per second, with an average median monthly discharge of 10.8 cubic feet per second.
- **Petition history:** The Desert Fishes Council petitioned for listing in March 1985. Their July 1985 letter also supported the Little Colorado spinedace and the loach minnow.
- **Federal consultation:** Section 7 consultation is required for federal actions affecting the species, including Central Arizona Project and Bureau of Reclamation water projects, and federally funded flood control and irrigation projects.
