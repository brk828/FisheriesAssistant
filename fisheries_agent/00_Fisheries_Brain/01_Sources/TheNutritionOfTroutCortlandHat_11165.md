# The Nutrition of Trout: Cortland Hatchery Report No. 9 (1940)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1940 report from the [[Cortland Hatchery]] summarizes a year of cooperative research by the New York State Conservation Department, the [[United States Fish and Wildlife Service]] and [[Cornell University]]. The work covers nutritional anemia, vitamin A storage, carbohydrate absorption, the use of house fly larvae as natural food, hatchery feed formulation, growth variability, madder marking and protein requirements in [[Brook Trout]] and related salmonids. Its central message is that dry-feed mixtures are more economical than fresh meat for hatchery production, while several nutritional questions, including the identity of the anti-anemic factor, remain unresolved.

## Background & Description

The report is one of a numbered series (Report No. 8 for 1939 and No. 7 for 1938 are cited) and follows the work of [[A. V. Tunison]], [[A. M. Phillips]], [[C. M. McCay]], [[A. H. Fenn]] and [[C. R. Mitchell]]. Experimental animals were mainly fingerling brook trout, usually 5 to 25 grams, with brown and rainbow trout used in some trials. Hatchery diets were built from hog spleen, beef liver, cottonseed meal, dry skim milk, fish meal and wheat middlings, with salt added. Acknowledged donors of liver concentrates included [[Eli Lilly Company]], [[Chappel Bros.]], [[E. R. Squibb and Sons]] and [[Armour and Company]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report links hatchery nutrition to wild stocking success. Vitamin A stores in hatchery fish may determine whether released trout can catch food and avoid predators. Natural food production was also reviewed, from 19th-century pond-rotation schemes to shrimp (Palaemonetes) and blow fly maggot cultures. The authors note that most hatcheries of the period were still using outdated feeding practices, and that many feeds were low in vitamin A. Water temperature (18 °C in absorption trials; 47 °F in growth trials) and water quality were treated as experimental variables.

## Key Findings

**Anemia and factor H**
- Trout on a synthetic diet deficient in "factor H" died when red cell counts fell to about 700,000.
- Liver fractions from Squibb, Chappel, Lilly and Armour, plus fresh beef liver, all checked the anemia. House fly maggots were equal or superior to every fraction tested.
- The authors conclude the larvae must synthesize the essential factor or obtain it from bacteria, because their plant-based rearing media lacked it.
- Whether brook trout will serve as a test animal for anti-anemic compounds is still unknown.

**Vitamin A**
- A simplified micro-method based on the antimony trichloride colorimetric test was developed for use in any biological laboratory.
- Most body vitamin A is stored in the pyloric caeca, liver and intestinal wall. In well-fed trout the caeca equal good-grade cod liver oil weight for weight.
- Trout fed liver-rich diets had 20 to 40 times the vitamin A stores of those fed spleen.
- Beef liver was the best source. Cod liver oil and carotene at moderate levels gave poor results over three months.
- Spleen and horse meat contain so little vitamin A that they give no colour test. Liver contains roughly fifty to a hundred times more.

**Carbohydrate absorption**
- Absorption rates were ranked: glucose and sucrose (well absorbed), then cooked starch, then raw starch.
- After 36 hours about one-third of raw starch had disappeared from the gut without being excreted, while more than half of cooked starch had been digested and absorbed.
- Cooked starch was about 20% better absorbed than raw starch at equivalent intervals. Raw starch was partly excreted.
- No glucose excretion was found.

**Carbohydrate utilization**
- Sucrose, dextrin and cooked starch diets produced swollen, pale, enlarged livers and significant rises in liver glycogen. Raw starch produced a slight rise. Cellulose and low cooked starch diets showed no significant rise.
- The production diet (about 14% carbohydrate, mostly raw starch) caused no increase in liver glycogen.

**Growth variability**
- Eight groups (four meat-fed and four meat plus dry feed) were compared over 24 weeks.
- Within-group differences were not significant, except for group 407 over the last 12 weeks.

**Feed mixtures**
- The common dry mixture is equal parts cottonseed meal, dry skim milk, white fish meal and wheat middlings, plus 4% salt, fed with an equal weight of fresh hog spleen.
- Dry-feed mixtures cost about half as much per pound as meat diets and grew trout as well in ponds.
- Soybean meal (44% protein, Archer-Daniels-Midland) equalled cottonseed meal.
- Frozen herring caused high mortality. Buckeye shiners caused failures at 12 weeks, and smelt and herring caused listlessness without death.
- Mixture No. 433 (semi-frozen spleen plus dry feed with 30 lb water per 100 lb) can be fed from a potato ricer.

**Brown trout strains**
- Three egg lots from the Randolph Hatchery showed no growth difference over 32 weeks.

**Madder marking**
- Eimer and Amend madder was more potent than S. B. Penick product.
- A 5% dietary level gave distinct pink bone colouration in fingerlings within two months; 1% did not.
- Colour persisted for six months to a year or more.
- Madder cost about three dollars per thousand three-inch fingerlings.

**Protein requirements**
- About twenty diets were tested at 10 to 20% protein. High-fat (Crisco, up to 80%) diets caused heavy mortality and edema.
- Half-spleen, half-casein diets caused high mortality within six weeks.
- Results were inconclusive because vitamin, carbohydrate and fat requirements were not yet understood.
