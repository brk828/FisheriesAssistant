# Status of Unlisted Native Fishes of the Gila River Basin, with Recommendations for Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #elopidae #mugilidae

## Executive Summary

This 2004 report by the [[Desert Fishes Team]] reviews the status of seven native warm-water fishes of the [[Gila River Basin]] (Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora) that are not listed under the federal [[Endangered Species Act]]. The species are [[Longfin Dace]], [[Sonora Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Machete]], [[Striped Mullet]], [[Desert Sucker]], and [[Speckled Dace]]. The report covers post-1967 conservation actions and provides species-specific recommendations.

Key conclusions: one species (flannelmouth sucker) is extirpated from the basin; four freshwater species are widespread but show moderate decline; two marine-derived species (machete and striped mullet) are occasional visitors from the [[Gulf of California]] that rarely reach the basin. Distribution and abundance of all resident species have declined, and the trend is accelerating. Conservation actions have been few over the 37-year period assessed, and most have targeted listed species, with benefits to non-listed species incidental.

The authors' central recommendation is control and removal of [[Nonnative Fishes]] and other nonnative aquatic species, followed by conservation planning, habitat restoration, protection and monitoring of existing populations, and repatriation. They argue that proven techniques already exist and that management does not depend on further biological research, although innovative approaches should still be explored.

## Background & Description

The Gila River basin supports 21 native fish species. Machete was added to the basin fauna in this report; two native trouts are not addressed. Like the broader fauna of the American Southwest, the native warm-water fishes are described as critically imperiled.

The seven species assessed differ in habitat and origin:

- **Freshwater species:** longfin dace, Sonora sucker, flannelmouth sucker, desert sucker, and speckled dace.
- **Salt-water species:** machete and striped mullet, which enter the lower Gila River only when flows connect the lower [[Colorado River]] with the Gulf of California.

Longfin dace is described as probably the most successful and adaptable native cyprinid in the Southwest. Sonora sucker is an obligate riverine species that does not persist in impoundments. Flannelmouth sucker historically inhabited large, strongly flowing Colorado River system rivers. Desert sucker occupies small to medium mountain streams and achieves greatest abundance in hard-bottomed streams. Speckled dace occurs in rivers, streams, cienegas, and spring streams.

Data were drawn primarily from the [[SONFISHES Database]], supplemented by literature, agency reports, and field experience. Records were grouped at the stream-within-county level. Historical records span ca. 1840–1979 and modern records 1980–2003. Decline was measured as the percentage of sites remaining occupied in modern times, and the authors state these estimates are conservative.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report identifies several interacting drivers of decline:

- **Nonnative species:** introduction and spread of nonnative fishes is described as a major factor in displacing natives, including predatory fishes that prey on native species.
- **Habitat alteration:** dewatering, reservoir construction, water development, and watershed alteration. Habitat destruction is described as an equal and interactive factor with nonnative species.
- **Fragmentation:** isolated populations are at risk of extirpation with little potential for recolonization from adjacent sources ([[Metapopulation Dynamics]]).
- **Drought:** [[Longfin Dace]] can persist through short droughts by sheltering under filamentous algal mats and moist debris, but populations of other species were lost where streams dried, including failed transplants.
- **Conservation conflicts:** efforts to restore listed species have occasionally caused losses of non-listed species, and nonnative control has been limited by lack of coordinated effort.

The [[Lower Colorado River]] is central to the marine species. Restoring flows and connectivity to the Gulf of California is identified as necessary for machete and striped mullet. Striped mullet spawns in the sea, and juveniles move into brackish water; some evidence suggests freshwater spawning as salinity rises from agricultural runoff.

## Key Findings

**Status by species** (historical 1840–1979 vs. modern 1980–2003 records, from Table 1):

- **[[Longfin Dace]]:** 214 of 257 historical locations (83%) retain the species. Low probability of local extirpation. Transplanted successfully to three sites in the basin; one transplant was lost when the site dried. Recommended for listing as threatened.
- **[[Sonora Sucker]]:** present at 93 of 127 locations (73%). Low probability of local extirpation, but fragmentation is a risk. Repatriated into O'Donnell Creek after nonnative removal; a single stocking into Arnett Creek failed during drought. Recommended for listing as threatened.
- **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]:** no longer occurs in the Gila River basin; disappeared from the Gila and San Pedro by the late 19th century and from the Salt River by the 1960s. High probability of local extirpation. No reintroductions have been made in the basin, though an unplanned Colorado River stocking established a population. Recommended for listing as endangered.
- **[[Machete]]:** occurrence in the Gila River is tied to access from the Gulf of California, now blocked in most years. One Gila River record from 1963. No evidence of distributional change, though abundance may have declined. No conservation actions. Benefits from restored lower-river flows.
- **[[Striped Mullet]]:** enters the lower Gila system relatively frequently when flows allow; recorded as far upstream as Painted Rock Reservoir. No evidence of range reduction. No conservation actions. Benefits from restored migration routes.
- **[[Desert Sucker]]:** present at 137 of 186 locations (74%). Low probability of local extirpation. One repatriation attempt failed due to stream desiccation. Recommended for listing as threatened.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]:** historically recorded at 215 locations; currently at 153, a 29% decline. Low probability of local extirpation. One transplant recorded; lost from one system during listed-species recovery work. Recommended for listing as threatened within the basin.

**Overall conclusions and recommendations:**

1. Extend [[Endangered Species Act]] protection to longfin dace, Sonora sucker, flannelmouth sucker, desert sucker, and speckled dace.
2. Convert streams and watershed complexes into refuges through barriers, nonnative removal, and repatriation.
3. Modify anthropogenic factors that harm habitat.
4. Protect existing populations and implement systematic monitoring.
5. Consider innovative tools, including species-specific [[Piscicides]] and transgenic approaches to control nonnative fishes, subject to licensing and appropriate deployment.
6. Develop a multi-agency, holistic management program covering the whole native assemblage and associated aquatic fauna and flora.

**Conservation activity (Tables 2–3):** repatriation has been the primary management tool but applied to few species with limited success. Most actions (e.g., [[Aravaipa Creek]] barriers, [[Fossil Creek]] renovation planning, nonnative threat control, land acquisition by The Nature Conservancy, and livestock grazing changes) were tied to listed species. Monitoring has been ongoing in several Arizona and New Mexico rivers.

**Data limitations:** the authors note that presence/absence analysis likely understates rarity in modern times, and that absence records may reflect sampling gaps. Declines are therefore described as conservative.
