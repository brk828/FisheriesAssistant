# Irish Salmon Disease — An Interim Report (1966)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #fish-pathology #Saprolegnia #Columnaris-Disease

## Executive Summary

This 1966 interim report by Dr. Margaret E. Brown and Dr. Vera G. Collins documents an emergent, highly lethal disease affecting [[Atlantic Salmon]] populations in southern Irish rivers, first reported in the Waterville area of Co. Kerry in autumn 1964 and subsequently spreading to the Feale, Shannon, Cork rivers (Bandon, Lee, Blackwater), Suir, Slaney, and Liffey by 1966. The disease is characterized grossly by white, fungal skin patches but the authors argue that the primary pathogenic agents are bacteria—most likely of the **columnaris group** (*Chondrococcus/Cytophaga columnaris*)—with the visible fungus (*Saprolegnia parasitica*) acting as a secondary, opportunistic invader following bacterial debilitation and skin damage. The authors propose a **marine transmission hypothesis**: infection is contracted by migrating adult salmon in coastal/estuarine waters and spread progressively along the coast from estuary to estuary, rather than via overland vectors such as birds. The disease shows strong seasonality, being most virulent in autumn, winter, and spring, and receding in summer. The report explicitly notes disagreement with Irish Government scientists regarding both the causative agent and the transmission pathway, with confirmatory infection experiments pending at time of writing. Concerns are raised about the potential for disease spread across the [[St. George's Channel]] to Wales and southwest England.

## Background & Description

### Gross Pathology of Diseased Salmon
- **White patches ("fungal patches")**: dirty white, raised lesions observed in water; collapse and turn brown when exposed to air.
- **Common lesion sites**: snout, operculum, area around dorsal fin, behind adipose fin, on/behind pectoral fin, along the belly (up to nine patches recorded on a single fish).
- **Fin damage**: ragged dorsal, anal, and pelvic fins.
- **Lesion severity spectrum**:
  - Mild: single small spot (e.g., between dorsal and adipose fin).
  - Moderate: multiple patches as described above.
  - Severe: fish almost entirely covered with white patches.
- **Early-stage lesions**: small whitish, slime-covered areas with a "woolly" appearance underwater; may develop a reddish tinge/ring as they expand.
- **Deep tissue involvement**: some patches extend past superficial skin layers into underlying muscle (which turns white/necrotic), with haemorrhaging that can penetrate to the body cavity or kidney.
- **Estuarine presentation**: diseased fish caught in estuaries often show raw, bleeding patches rather than white fungal coverage — attributed to fungal sloughing exposing underlying tissue.
- **Behavioral/locomotor sign**: anecdotal reports of salmon "running along the water on their tails," hypothesized as an early clinical sign of infection.

### Causative/Associated Microorganisms
| Organism | Type | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| *Saprolegnia parasitica* | Fungus (Oomycete) | Secondary invader; produces swimming and resting spores; cannot survive in seawater; penetrates skin into muscle causing necrosis |
| *Chondrococcus columnaris* | Myxobacterium (slime bacterium) | Slender gliding rods; forms "columns"; produces resting microcysts; tolerates higher temperatures |
| *Cytophaga columnaris* | Myxobacterium (slime bacterium) | Gliding motion; does not produce microcysts; possibly synonymous with *C. columnaris* |
| *Aeromonas liquefaciens* | Flagellate bacterium | Associated with "boils," spottiness; can affect gut, liver, kidney |
| *Pseudomonas fluorescens* | Flagellate bacterium | Similar pathogenic profile to *Aeromonas* |

Historical comparator: **Bacillus salmonis pestis**, described by [[J. Hume Patterson]] (1903) in a nearly identical 19th-century British salmon disease epidemic — cold-water tolerant, seawater-viable, invaded lymph spaces and muscle, and required fungal co-infection (*Saprolegnia ferax*) to produce the classic white-patch symptomology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Host range**: Primarily migratory adult [[Atlantic Salmon]]; parr and smolts in freshwater were, at time of writing, unaffected. Credible but limited evidence of infection in [[Pike]], [[Roach]], occasional [[Brown Trout]], and possibly [[European Eel]].
- **Habitat zones affected**: Freshwater rivers and pools above weirs (e.g., Fermoy, Careysville on the Blackwater), tidal estuaries, and coastal marine waters.
- **Seasonality**: Disease prevalence peaks in the colder months (autumn–spring) and recedes in summer, plausibly due to temperature-dependent bacterial virulence — notably the **inverse** of the American columnaris disease pattern, which is a summer disease.
- **Geographic distribution (as of June 1966)**: First identified in Waterville, Co. Kerry (autumn 1964); spread to the Feale River (near Shannon estuary), Cork rivers (Bandon, Lee, Blackwater), Suir, Slaney, and Liffey. No confirmed cases north of the Shannon (west coast) or north of the Liffey (east coast).
- **Proposed transmission ecology**: Coastal/estuarine spread among migrating adult salmon, rather than overland transmission via birds or anglers (deemed unlikely since candidate bacteria cannot survive drying or passage through gut acid). Infected fish falling back into estuaries are proposed as reservoirs/foci of infection for subsequent incoming migratory cohorts, producing a progressive estuary-to-estuary spread around the coastline correlating with migratory routes and landfall/coastal-travel behavior of returning salmon.
- **Transboundary risk**: The authors flag potential spread across [[St. George's Channel]] to rivers in Wales, southwest England, and the Bristol Channel, contingent on the proportion and speed of Irish-origin salmon transiting those waters, especially in autumn when migratory movement is most rapid.

## Key Findings

- The disease's most conspicuous sign — white fungal patches — is caused by *Saprolegnia parasitica*, but this is considered a **secondary** opportunistic pathogen exploiting a primary bacterial infection and/or physical injury.
- Four bacterial species were consistently cultured from diseased salmon: *Chondrococcus columnaris*, *Cytophaga columnaris*, *Aeromonas liquefaciens*, and *Pseudomonas fluorescens*; the authors favor **columnaris-type bacteria** (in one or both forms) as the true causative agent based on symptom correlation with existing literature.
- A major inconsistency with the American columnaris disease model is the **seasonal mismatch**: American columnaris disease is a summer disease, whereas Irish salmon disease flourishes in winter.
- Confirmatory experimental infection of salmon with pure bacterial cultures had not yet been completed at time of publication; Irish Government scientists disputed both the causative agent identification and the proposed transmission theory.
- Historical precedent exists: a nearly identical epidemic devastated British salmon populations ~100 years prior (documented by [[J. Hume Patterson]], 1903), attributed to *Bacillus salmonis pestis* combined with secondary *Saprolegnia ferax* fungal infection — raising hope that, like the historical outbreak, the current epidemic may be self-limiting over a period of years.
- Mortality data: Irish Government reported diseased salmon comprised 8% of the total healthy catch on the River Feale (1965); by April 1966, nets at Lismore caught almost entirely diseased fish (up from 0% in the prior three months); no healthy kelts were caught in 1966, and spawning redd counts were markedly reduced in the Bandon, Lee, and Blackwater in 1965.
- The overland spread hypothesis (e.g., via fish-eating birds) was largely rejected because none of the four candidate bacteria survive desiccation or gut-acid exposure required for that transmission route.
- The marine/estuarine spread hypothesis is supported by the bacteria's ability to survive in salt water and by the observed pattern of disease progression correlating with salmon coastal migration routes and homing behavior (navigation via sun/stars, final river selection via olfaction).
- The disease's geographic and temporal spread pattern (Kerry → Shannon/Feale → Cork rivers → Suir/Slaney → Liffey) is consistent with a coastal, estuary-to-estuary infection model tied to the timing of salmon runs.
- Practical conservation implication: rivers lacking an early salmon run appear less likely to be affected, suggesting run-timing as a risk factor for disease exposure.
