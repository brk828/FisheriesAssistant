# Long-term Patterns of Fish Community Structure and Decline in Native Species in a Colorado River Reservoir, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #Catostomidae #community_ecology #endangered_species

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) were the only two remaining native fishes in [[Lake Mohave]] at study initiation (1980). Four of six historically present native species ([[Speckled Dace]], [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]) were extirpated prior to sampling. Colorado Pikeminnow was last detected in 1962. Both Bonytail and Razorback Sucker were listed as endangered in 1980 and 1991, respectively. Historical Razorback Sucker population estimates ranged from hundreds of thousands in the 1960s-1970s to 60,000-70,000 individuals in the 1980s, declining to fewer than 4,000 individuals currently. Razorback Sucker successfully spawn annually but experience complete recruitment failure; larvae are eliminated by predation before reaching juvenile stage. **Age, Maturity, Fecundity, Growth Parameters**: Not reported. **Habitat Associations**: Lake Mohave is approximately 108 km in length with steep canyons and broad structural valleys (maximum depth 43 m). Substrate along shore is gravel and cobble formed by wave action; deeper areas dominated by sand and silt. Water released from [[Lake Mead]] hypolimnion creates cold, clear riverine section upstream of deeper, summer-stratified downstream reach. Reservoir experiences little discharge variation with seasonal drawdown of ~5 m (August-December), considered a stable reservoir. Study area (Carp Cove vicinity and Cottonwood East Area) ranges 1.1-4.2 km wide, vulnerable to seasonal wind action from south.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Not reported for specific species movements. **Trophic & Community Interactions**: Nonnative fishes dominated throughout 40-year study, never comprising less than 52% of total catch (native fishes averaged 15% annually). [[Common Carp]] and Razorback Sucker had highest abundance across all years. Razorback Sucker larvae experience complete predation before reaching juvenile stage. Adult Razorback Sucker are susceptible to [[Striped Bass]] predation, likely facilitated by clear water conditions. [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking (discontinued 2014, resumed 2017) may have augmented food base for Striped Bass, enhancing predator survival and growth. Three nonnative species increased in abundance: [[Gizzard Shad]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]]; nine decreased; three showed no relationship. Striped Bass rapidly increased from 1980s to mid-1990s following 1983 heavy rains and Hoover Dam floodway releases allowing downstream movement from Lake Mead. [[Largemouth Bass]] was most abundant centrarchid in early samples but gradually declined; Smallmouth Bass became dominant centrarchid in 2011. **Environmental Drivers**: Reservoir formation in early 1950s after Davis Dam construction transformed riverine to lentic habitat, facilitating establishment of lentic-associated nonnatives. Possible trophic upsurge period after dam closure followed by slow nutrient decline may explain general CPUE decline. Fluctuating water levels strand and desiccate Razorback Sucker eggs. [[Quagga mussel]] (*Dreissena bugensis*) established in Lake Mohave ~2006 (first recorded Lake Mead 2007, likely established lower Colorado River basin 2003); role in community shift uncertain but may have caused indirect effects through habitat alteration. Clear water conditions facilitate visual predation on native species.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: Razorback Sucker abundance declined precipitously through time (Spearman's rs = -0.774, p < 0.001). Bonytail, never common, was functionally extirpated by late 1990s; last wild individual captured 2005. Total CPUE summed across all species showed negative relationship with time (rs = -0.853, p < 0.001), whereas total species richness showed no relationship (rs = -0.080, p = 0.626). Across 40 years, 20,431 individuals captured representing 17 species. Fish community composition exhibited low stability and moderate turnover (DCA first axis length 1.96 SD, indicating 49% turnover from 1980 to 2020). Community trajectory was directional in multivariate space (NMDS 2D stress = 0.10) with no return to former structure. SIMPROF analysis identified three possible alternative states in community composition, averaging 13 years duration each, with shifts in 1991 and 2006. **Threat & Risk Assessment**: Predation by nonnative piscivores identified as main reason for native fish decline. Recruitment bottleneck following aging wild Razorback Sucker population that recruited in 1950s. Larvae nutritional limitation potentially exacerbates recruitment failure. Habitat alteration from reservoir formation facilitated nonnative establishment. Biotic homogenization from nonnative introductions. Contemporary fish community shows little resemblance to historical endemic ichthyofauna. Both wild native populations are gone.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: Continued adaptive management required to preserve most genetically diverse Razorback Sucker population in Colorado River system, especially while coexisting with unstable nonnative fish community. Repatriation program initiated 1991 prevented Razorback Sucker extirpation but has not achieved self-sustaining population. Nearly 300,000 Razorback Sucker repatriated to Lake Mohave; current population remains below 4,000 individuals. Current strategy relies on capturing larvae in Lake Mohave, rearing in hatcheries and backwaters, then releasing; limited success due to size-survival relationship and Striped Bass predation on even largest released individuals. Repatriation cannot mitigate larval predation precluding successful recruitment past larval stage. Bonytail reintroduction efforts have failed. No management strategy conceived to allow Razorback Sucker to complete life cycle in nature. Rainbow Trout stocking suspended 2014 for economic reasons, resumed 2017 at Arizona Game and Fish Department behest; practice may augment Striped Bass food base. Ongoing monitoring and assessment necessary to understand dynamic nature of aquatic ecosystems. **Biological Reference Points**: Not reported.
