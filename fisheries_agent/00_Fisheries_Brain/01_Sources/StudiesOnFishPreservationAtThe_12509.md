# Studies on Fish Preservation at the Contra Costa Steam Plant

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Moronidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Fish Bulletin No. 92 (California Department of Fish and Game, 1953), by James E. Kerr of Bechtel Corporation, reports a 17-month research program (ending October 1952) into protecting fish drawn into the condenser cooling water of the [[Contra Costa Steam Plant]] on the San Joaquin River near Antioch. The plant's intake, which began full operation in 1951, trapped large numbers of small [[Striped Bass]] and [[King Salmon]] in the screen structure, causing mortality at the traveling screens. The study combined emergency rescue, controlled flume and condenser experiments, and the development of a [[Fish Collector]] that returns fish alive to the river. The report concludes that the collectors recover fish with about 98 percent survival and that the plant will not significantly affect the Delta striped bass population.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** The plant sits on the south bank of the San Joaquin River, about 2.5 miles upstream from Antioch, in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin Delta]], where the two rivers enter Suisun Bay. Tides range about eight feet at the site, and summer salinity reaches 2,000 to 3,000 ppm.

**Fisheries.** The Delta holds anadromous species (king salmon, striped bass, shad, steelhead, Pacific lamprey, white and green sturgeon) and many resident species (about 19 listed, including catfishes, black bass, carp, splittail, and sculpins). Striped bass and king salmon were judged the most important. Bass have been protected from commercial fishing since 1935; about 200,000 anglers catch roughly 1,500,000 bass a year, with about $10,000,000 spent annually on bass fishing (Calhoun, 1952).

**Life stages.** The authors defined working classes: bass eggs and larvae (4 inch or less), juveniles (over 1/4 inch to under 12 inches, with yearlings in the 3 to 6 inch range), and adults (12 inches or more). Males mature at about 12 inches and females at about 22 inches. A single female may spawn 250,000 to 1,250,000 eggs per season. King salmon were divided into juveniles (about 1 to 5 inches, migrating seaward January to April) and adults.

**Plant design.** The first three units (100,000 kW each) started in 1951; Units 4 and 5 were scheduled for 1953. River water requirements were 623 cu. ft./sec. initially and 868 cu. ft./sec. at five units. Water is drawn from near the river bottom through a headworks 410 feet offshore, carried through two 12-foot tubes to an onshore screen structure with five traveling screens, and returned through twin tunnels. The original design used 3/8-inch mesh, sized after a compromise between impingement on fine mesh and passage of small fish. Condenser temperature rise was designed as a weighted mean of 16.2° F. at full five-unit load.

**Early failure.** The one-tube, three-screen start-up doubled tube velocity and raised screen approach velocities. The original vertical hydraulic fish lift performed poorly. Fish built up in front of the screens, and state biologists recovered dead bass in the discharge after a 12° F. rise during full-load testing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Fish behavior.** Bass and salmon consistently swim headed into the current, sense screen obstructions several inches ahead, and move laterally to low-velocity refuges. Impingement occurs mainly when fish are exhausted. Fish avoid abrupt velocity differentials in vertical stream lines, which made [[Velocity Barrier]] concepts possible.
- **Vulnerability by size.** Larval and small yearling bass have little swimming ability and die quickly when impinged. Yearling salmon tolerate impingement well.
- **Stratification.** Bass eggs, larvae and small yearlings were found at all depths with no clear stratification, so the proposed hanging curtain wall was not supported as a sole measure.
- **Tides and seasons.** Bass recruitment builds from zero to maximum and back to zero over about 90 days; juvenile bass collections peaked in summer. Shad and splittail runs also affected collection.
- **Water chemistry.** City chlorinated water was no worse than river water for test fish. Zinc from galvanized surfaces at 2 ppm or less was not injurious to small fish; all galvanized tanks were painted in any case.

## Key Findings

**Rescue phase (1951).**
- Hand dip netting from August 19 to December 21, 1951 (24-hour, 7-day operation at peak times) rescued about 12,000 bass, 2 to 16 inches long, between August 30 and November 1.
- Noise devices, lights, traps, cups on screens, and metal frames gave no useful results. Continuous screen operation cut mortality by less than 10 percent.
- A mechanical dip netter matched hand netting and was kept in use.

**Research findings.**
- Velocity: Approach velocities of 1.14 to 1.52 ft./sec. (overall average 1.32 ft./sec.) were measured in front of the four operating screens. A design approach velocity of 1.5 ft./sec. was judged reasonable.
- Endurance: Yearling bass (3 to 5.5 inches) survived 2 ft./sec. for 10 minutes at 100 percent after correction for poor condition; 5 to 7 inch bass resisted 2 ft./sec. and only one of about several dozen was impinged at 2.75 ft./sec.; one-inch bass held 80 percent at 1 ft./sec. but all impinged fish died; king salmon of 1.25 to 1.89 inches showed no deaths when impinged.
- Mesh: Screens of 0.159 to 0.375 inch clear opening were tested. Bass of 1.18 inches were stopped by a 0.159-inch opening, and 1.97-inch bass by a 0.375-inch opening, while substantial numbers smaller than 1.18 inches were still recovered by collectors.
- Temperature: Yearling salmon survived an instantaneous 25° F. rise and a maximum tolerance of about 83° F. Condenser tests on yearling salmon (100 fish, 10 tests) showed no fatalities at a 16° F. rise. Yearling bass tolerated a 16° F. rise with about 94 percent survival after five days in condenser tests on 0.83 to 1.81 inch fish; bass maximum tolerance was 90° F. or more.
- Intermittent versus continuous operation: Survival was 9.9 percent (continuous) and 9.6 percent (intermittent), a non-significant difference.
- Fish collectors: One collector recovered 80 fish/hr (all species, 77 percent survival) compared with 33 fish/hr from three traveling screens; in a comparison, the collector took 58 percent of all fish and screens 35 percent. The final design achieved 98 percent or more survival and reduced screen recovery to under 1 percent.
- Pump comparison: The 8-inch pump took 85 percent of recovered fish over 5 inches and outperformed the 5-inch pump.
- Velocity barriers: Vanes set in a V-pattern deflected 100 percent of experimental fish with Type 1 arrangement; a 30° deflection angle with 10° vanes at 1.85 ft./sec. gave 92 percent recovery; a pipe barrier gave 93 percent. Results were laboratory-scale only.
- Electric screens were tested at the headworks (7.5 kVA, 4 to 10 pulses/sec, about 1,400 V) and abandoned as impractical under changing salinity.

**Population impact estimate.**
- The Delta was estimated to hold 35,000,000 fingerling bass (about one inch) in mid-July 1951. Normal natural mortality between fingerling and adult stages was taken as 95 percent, based on an equilibrium catch of about 1,500,000 adults.
- Entrainment at 868 cu. ft./sec. was estimated at 3,476,000 bass fry (maximum). With 48,800 fish losses after the 6 percent loss allowance, about 2,200 fry were expected to reach the fishery.
- Collectors were estimated to return 98 percent of juvenile bass; 560,000 were collected annually, leaving about 560 adults.
- Total estimated annual loss: 2,760 fish, or 0.18 percent of the estimated 1,500,000 annual catch. The authors concluded the plant would not significantly affect the Delta striped bass population.

**Pittsburg Steam Plant.** The planned [[Pittsburg Steam Plant]] (Suisun Bay, 900 cu. ft./sec.) will use 3/8-inch (2-mesh) screens with open forebays, velocities below 0.5 ft./sec. in the forebay, and approach velocities well below 1.5 ft./sec. No fish collectors are planned. Maximum temperature rise at full load is 15.6° F., and passage time is estimated at about five minutes.

**Caveats.** The author notes that experiments were conducted on confined, often weakened fish, and that results were "extremely conservative." Collector survival figures exclude fish killed during counting, and the population estimates rest on assumptions about fish density and mortality.
