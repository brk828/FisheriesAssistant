# Internal Consistency in Endangered Species Recovery Plans

#conservation-biology #endangered-species #recovery-plans

## Executive Summary

This study assesses whether [[Recovery Plan|recovery plans]] for federally listed endangered species are internally consistent, meaning their recommendations stay aligned across the planning process. The authors built a database of recovery plans and answered nine questions grouped into three topic areas: [[Threat Abatement|threats]], [[Information Gaps]], and [[Monitoring]] of recovery. Internal consistency was generally high, with mean consistency above 50% for eight of the nine comparisons. Monitoring was the clear weak point. The authors conclude that internal consistency is necessary for a clear plan of action but is not sufficient to guarantee a high-quality plan.

## Background & Description

Recovery plans are the primary management tool for endangered species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (NRC 1995). Their content varies widely because there is no set format, and authorship ranges from single [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) biologists to species experts and multi-institution recovery teams. Earlier work found variation in the amount, quality, and type of information plans provide (Tear et al. 1993, 1995).

Internal consistency is a standard metric in psychology and medicine, where it is used to judge the reliability of surveys and assessments. The authors apply the same logic to planning. For example, if a plan identifies a threat but has no task to address it, the chance of recovery may fall. Consistency is assessed within specific topic areas: threats, information gaps, and monitoring.

The analysis used a database of recovery plans collected in 1999 and described in Hoeksema et al. (2002). According to the source, the database includes more than 180 plans. Each plan was checked against a survey of nine yes/no questions, producing 38 comparison categories per plan. Consistent versus inconsistent responses were assigned by simple accounting based on the database, not by judgment calls.

Plans were compared by taxon (plants, animals, vertebrates, invertebrates), by plan type ([[Single-Species Plans]] versus [[Multi-Species Plans]]), and by year of plan approval. Mean consistency and standard error were calculated for each question. Trends were tested with a two-way ANOVA including a taxon-by-plan-type interaction. Consistency values were arcsine-transformed to meet normality assumptions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study covers recovery planning for endangered species in general rather than one ecosystem. The taxa named include plants, vertebrates, and invertebrates, and it discusses common threats such as habitat loss and [[Exotic Species Invasions]]. Because monitoring is often difficult in recovery programs, the authors note that weak consistency in monitoring may be a factor in whether recovery actions succeed.

The paper treats consistency as a planning property rather than an ecological measurement. It does not evaluate the biology of any particular species. Its aim is to show whether planners carry threats, information needs, and monitoring through the plan in a coherent way.

## Key Findings

**Overall consistency**
- Consistency was relatively high overall but varied by topic.
- The highest mean was for Question 1 (agreement between listing documents and plans on threats), reported as 89.9 ± 3.35% (mean ± SE).
- The lowest was for Question 3 (whether plans include tasks to monitor identified threats). The abstract reports 25.7 ± 1.76%, while the body text's OCR reads as "53 ± 1.76%". The source should be checked to confirm which value is correct.
- Consistency on success criteria for monitoring (Question 7) was reported as 51.3 ± 3.35%.
- Questions on threat identification (Q1, Q2), prioritization by threat level (Q4), and tasks to collect missing information (Q6) appeared consistent across plans.

**Strengths**
- Plans consistently recognize threats from the listing process through planning.
- Recovery actions are prioritized in line with the degree of threat.
- Once a plan calls for more information, it usually lists the tasks needed to obtain it.

**Weaknesses (monitoring)**
- Monitoring showed the lowest consistency (Questions 3, 7, 8, and 9).
- Plans that identify threats often do not call for monitoring them.
- Monitoring tasks often do not deliver the type of information needed to assess recovery criteria.
- The authors argue that weak monitoring undermines [[Adaptive Management]] and the ability to defend delisting decisions.

**Taxon and plan-type effects**
- Multi-species plans were more consistent than single-species plans for monitoring. The authors describe this as the largest area of neglect in the planning process.
- Multi-species plans were less effective than single-species plans in generating the information needed to assess recovery for animals.
- Plant plans were more consistent than animal plans overall.
- Overall consistency did not change with time for most questions.

**Temporal trends**
- Recent plans were less likely than older plans to request missing information, although they remained highly consistent.

**Interpretation and recommendations**
- Internal consistency is necessary but not sufficient for plan quality. A consistent plan can still contain incorrect or missing information.
- The authors suggest that the USFWS is working to improve monitoring in recovery plans, including staff training in monitoring.
- Because no fixed consistency threshold has been established for recovery planning, the authors focus on overall trends rather than specific cutoffs.
