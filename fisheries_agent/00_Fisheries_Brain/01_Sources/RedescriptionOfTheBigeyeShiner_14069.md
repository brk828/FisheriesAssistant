# Redescription of the Bigeye Shiner, *Notropis boops* (Pisces: Cyprinidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Burr and Dimmick (1983) redescribed the [[Notropis Boops]] (bigeye shiner), a wide-ranging but poorly known cyprinid of the central [[Mississippi River Basin]]. The study examined 567 specimens from throughout the species' range and measured 20 morphological characters. Significant geographic variation was not found in any character. Lateral-line scale and caudal vertebra counts were slightly higher in the northern and eastern parts of the range. The authors also documented severe population declines in Ohio and Illinois caused by excessive [[Siltation]] from poor agricultural practices.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** [[Notropis Boops]] Gilbert, 1884, originally described from Indiana. Its closest relative is [[Notropis Xaenocephalus]], which it resembles in body shape and pigmentation.
- **Prior work:** Before this study, little systematic work had been published beyond atlases, checklists, and state fish books. Earlier papers included Gilbert (1978) on type material, Smith and Hocutt (1981) on pharyngeal tooth formulae in Missouri, and Lehtinen and Echelle (1979) on the reproductive cycle in Oklahoma.
- **Methods:**
  - Counts and measurements followed Hubbs and Lagler (1964).
  - Cephalic lateral-line terminology followed Snelson (1971).
  - Vertebral counts were made from radiographs.
  - Data were pooled into two groups: populations east and west of the Mississippi River.
- **Type material:** Syntypes were collected from Salt Creek, Brown County, Indiana, and Flat Rock Creek, Rush County, Indiana. A lectotype (45.6 mm SL, USNM 34982) was selected from the Salt Creek specimens.
- **Diagnosis:**
  - Moderate-sized species; largest specimen measured was 66 mm SL.
  - Peritoneum black.
  - Intestine short (76.5–98.0% SL) with a single lengthwise loop.
  - Eye large (less than 3.5 times into head).
  - Anal-fin rays usually 8.
  - Pharyngeal teeth usually 1,4–4,1.
  - No breeding colors.
- **Description:**
  - Dorsal-fin rays: 7(1), 8(324).
  - Anal-fin rays: 7(5), 8(488), 9(24).
  - Lateral-line scales: 34–40.
  - Predorsal scale rows: mean 13.2.
  - Total gill rakers on first arch: mean 9.7.
  - Distinct black lateral stripe, about 1–2 scale rows wide.
  - Breeding males develop [[Nuptial Tubercles]] from April through August, peaking in May.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution:** The species occurs in the central Mississippi basin, from the Scioto River drainage in Ohio west to the lower Red River drainage in Oklahoma, south to the Ouachita River drainage in Louisiana, and north to the Illinois River drainage in Illinois. A reported occurrence in the Cache Creek drainage, Oklahoma, is not verified by specimens.
- **Habitat and threats:** The species formerly inhabited clear, gravel-bottomed streams. Agricultural practices have degraded many of these streams through siltation and turbidity, particularly in western Ohio and east-central Illinois.
- **Conservation status:**
  - Listed as endangered or rare in Mississippi (Clemmer et al. 1975).
  - Of special concern in Alabama (Ramsey 1976).
  - Common and abundant across most of Arkansas, eastern Oklahoma, southern Missouri, southeastern Kansas, and central Kentucky and Tennessee.
- **Reproduction:** Breeding males show tubercles and creamy white fin membranes. Females have an enlarged, protruded urogenital papilla during spawning.

## Key Findings

- **Sample base:** 567 specimens examined from throughout the range, with 20 morphological characters analyzed.
- **Geographic variation:** Minimal. No significant geographic variation was found in any of the 20 characters.
- **Variable characters:** Only lateral-line scales and vertebrae varied, with a similar pattern in both.
  - Lateral-line scales: east of the Mississippi River mean 36.5 (n = 221); west mean 35.9.
  - Caudal vertebrae: east mean 17.4; west mean 16.8.
  - Specimens from the east and north averaged slightly higher counts. The authors relate this to the general northward increase in meristic elements in many fishes (Barlow 1961).
- **Sexual dimorphism:** No significant sexual differences in meristic characters. Males in breeding condition had significantly greater values for:
  - Anal fin length (p < .01)
  - Pelvic fin length (p < .005)
  - Pectoral fin length (p < .005)
  - Preanal length (p < .005)
- **Pharyngeal teeth:** Smith and Hocutt (1981) examined 100 individuals from Big River, Missouri. Of these, 64% had a 1,4–4,1 count and 19% had 1,4–4,0. Tooth counts across the range agreed with these results. A tooth is occasionally lost from the minor row.
- **Intestine:** In 10 adults (48–59 mm SL), the total intestinal length was 76.5–98.0% SL (mean 87.8%).
- **Comparison with N. xaenocephalus:** The two species are very similar in body shape and pigmentation. *N. xaenocephalus* differs in having pharyngeal teeth usually 2,4–4,2, anal-fin rays usually 7, a silvery peritoneum with scattered melanophores, a well-developed caudal spot, and less extensive tuberculation.
- **Population decline:** Populations in Ohio and Illinois have been severely decimated by siltation and turbidity from poor agricultural practices. The species remains common across the central portion of its range.
- **Taxonomic action:** A lectotype (USNM 34982) was designated from the Salt Creek syntypes. Remaining syntypes became paralectotypes.
