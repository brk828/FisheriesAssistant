# Status of Cyprinodon macularius eremus, a New Subspecies of Pupfish from Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 technical report by Robert Rush Miller and Lee A. Fuiman, issued by the [[Cooperative National Park Resources Studies Unit]] (University of Arizona) under a [[National Park Service]] contract, formally describes *Cyprinodon macularius eremus* as a new subspecies of [[Desert Pupfish]] endemic to the spring-fed pond at [[Quitobaquito]], [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]. Morphometric analysis (26 measurements, principal components analysis across 12 populations) separates it from other populations, especially those of the nearby [[Rio Sonoyta]]. The report also reviews the species' sharp decline across its range, its endangered status, and management options for the Quitobaquito population, which is the only Arizona population of the species and one of very few surviving anywhere.

## Background & Description

The Quitobaquito fish was assigned to the desert pupfish, *Cyprinodon macularius* Baird and Girard. The species historically inhabited the [[Gila River]] basin, the lower [[Colorado River]], the [[Salton Sea]] and [[Laguna Salada]] basins, and the Rio Sonoyta basin. The authors describe the subspecies as follows:

- **Males:** longer, wider and deeper head; broader and deeper body.
- **Females:** deeper head, slightly deeper body, longer dorsal-fin base, shorter anal fin.
- Differences are not explained by allometric growth.
- **Holotype:** UMMZ 162661, a nuptial male, 40.0 mm SL. Paratypes were collected 14 April 1950 by R. R. Miller, F. H. Miller and H. E. Winn, with additional paratype series from other institutions.
- **Meristics (15 males and 15 females):** dorsal-fin rays mostly 10; anal-fin rays mostly 10–11; pelvic rays mostly 7; lateral scale series mostly 25–26; vertebrae total mostly 26.
- **Coloration:** nuptial males are metallic blue to turquoise with yellow-olive caudal fins; females are golden yellow to brownish olive with dark lateral blotches.

The name *eremus* derives from the Greek *eremos* ("solitary" or "lonely"), reflecting the isolation of the population.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** In April 1950 the Quitobaquito pond was a shallow, man-made marsh of about 70 × 70 m, fed by two warm springs. Summer shallow-water temperatures reached 39.6–41.0 °C. The pond supported aquatic vegetation including *Chara*, *Eleocharis*, and *Scirpus*. Pupfish were not found in the spring sources themselves, only in the outflow ditch and pond. The pond was impounded around 1860 for irrigation and dikes were added about 1900; it was enlarged and deepened in 1962.

**Water chemistry.** Table 1 compares Quitobaquito pond and spring water with Rio Sonoyta water (Cole and Whitehead 1965; Kunzmann 1982). Quitobaquito had a 1983 pond salinity of 0.7 ppt and conductivity of 1,113 µS; Rio Sonoyta had a 1983 salinity of 1.8 ppt.

**Diet.** Cox (1972) found the subspecies to be an unselective omnivore, with digestive tracts rich in detritus and including plants, insect larvae, water mites, ostracods, [[Daphnia]], and mollusk eggs.

**Physiological tolerance.** The fish survive dissolved oxygen as low as 0.13 mg O₂/L (Lowe et al. 1967) and tolerate laboratory temperatures up to 44.6 °C (range 44.2–45.4 °C; Lowe and Heath 1969).

**Life history.** Kynard and Garrett (1979) reported a maximum life span of three years. Mean lengths were 29.6 mm TL at age 1, 40.2 mm at age 2, and 48 mm at age 3. Breeding starts in April or May and ends in late summer. Adults are dormant in the pond in winter. A nematode parasite was found in 13 of 58 fish (22%) (Cox 1966).

**Population.** Estimates were 7,986 (October 1975), 3,592 (June 1976), 4,558 (November 1976), and about 3,143 (fall 1980). A 1976 fish kill, linked to lethal levels of m-parathion, reduced numbers to under 3,000 by fall and under 1,800 the next spring.

**Threats.** Threats at Quitobaquito include the introduction of [[Golden Shiner]] (eliminated 18 December 1969), habitat modification, possible loss of water from groundwater pumping in Mexico, and airborne pesticides from Mexican agriculture.

**Range decline.** The species has been lost from most of the Gila basin and Arizona, most of the [[Salton Sea]] basin, and the Laguna Salada basin (extirpated by 1970). Surviving populations are small and precarious: juveniles were found in San Felipe Creek in April 1986, and 70 fish were captured in Salt Creek in April–May 1986. Exotic species, including [[Tilapia]], are a major cause of decline.

**Floods.** Floods in the Rio Sonoyta basin in 1981 and 1982 scoured the channel. Pupfish were almost absent in April 1983 but had repopulated the stream by May 1986, with over 100 caught in three short seine hauls.

## Key Findings

- **Subspecies status:** the Quitobaquito population is distinct in shape from all 12 populations studied and is designated *Cyprinodon macularius eremus* n. subsp. The authors treat it as a subspecies rather than a full species because of overlap with other populations.
- **Analyses:** four separate analyses (male/female, two population groups, two measurement techniques) gave consistent results. Five characters define its shape: body, head and mouth widths, depressed anal-fin length, and pelvic-fin length.
- **Allometry:** no significant allometric differences from the Rio Sonoyta population were found. Apparent sex-specific differences in some features were attributed to larger body size in the Quitobaquito sample.
- **Origin:** the authors suggest the population arose from selection for a localized niche after a small initial population was isolated, with genetic drift possible. Rio Sonoyta was probably colonized from the Colorado River delta within the past 100,000 years, or earlier.
- **Conservation status:** the species is listed as endangered (Federal Register, 30 April 1986). Quitobaquito is the only Arizona population and may be the only large, healthy natural population remaining.
- **Management recommendations:** reduce pond depth to no more than about 15 cm in part of the pond, retain deeper refuges, and do not manage the pond solely as a bird-watching site.
- **Refugia:** artificial refugia (e.g., [[Arizona State University]], [[Anza-Borrego Desert State Park]], [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]]) are described as temporary. Turner (1984) found allozyme polymorphism and heterozygosity essentially identical between natural and cultured stocks.
- **Taxonomy:** *C. m. californiensis* is not accepted as a valid subspecies, because repeated mixing of stocks through Colorado River flooding into the Salton Sink left no evidence for distinct taxa.
