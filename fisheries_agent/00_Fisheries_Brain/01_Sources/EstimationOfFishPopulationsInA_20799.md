# Estimation of Fish Populations in a Fluctuating Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 study by [[Donald E Wohlschlag]] (Stanford University), published in *California Fish and Game*, estimates the fish populations of [[Searsville Lake]], a 98-acre, weed-choked reservoir in San Mateo County, California. The lake had received negligible fishing pressure for about a decade. The author tested a low-equipment field method using [[Wire Traps]] combined with a [[Schnabel Method]] mark-recapture estimate. Three species were estimated with adequate precision: [[Brown Bullhead]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Black Crappie]]. [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Hitch]], and [[Green Sunfish]] were too scarce for reliable estimates. The study also evaluated how to express fish densities per acre in a fluctuating reservoir, where area changes with water level.

## Background & Description

The study addresses a declining trend in angler catch in California. The author attributes this partly to the limited future extent of cold-water habitat and the growth of warm-water [[Fluctuating Reservoirs]]. Population estimates for larger waters were scarce, because direct enumeration by poisoning or draining is impractical there.

Searsville Lake is an impoundment of part of the [[San Francisquito Creek]] drainage, on the northeast edge of the [[Santa Cruz Mountains]]. Key characteristics:

- **Area and volume at high water:** 98 acres (39.7 ha); about 105 × 10⁶ gallons.
- **Dam height:** 90 feet; the lake was impounded in 1891 and altered later.
- **Water level:** Drained after the 1923–24 drought. In the decade before the study, levels usually stayed within 10 feet of spillway level. The 1950 drawdown is reported as "about 64 feet," which may be an OCR error for 6.4 feet.
- **Silting:** Very heavy during the rainy season.
- **Vegetation:** Bulrush (*Scirpus* sp.), cattail (*Typha* sp.), and smartweed (*Polygonum* sp.) line the shore. Water milfoil (*Myriophyllum* sp.) grows profusely from shore to about 12 feet deep.

The fish fauna was dominated by brown bullheads, black crappies, bluegills, and largemouth bass. Green sunfish and hitch were rare, and mosquitofish were abundant in shallow shoreline waters. Common carp disappeared during the 1924 drainage. The lake was restocked in 1924 with 500 bass and an unknown number of bluegills and black crappies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishing pressure and mortality:** Fishing had been virtually absent for years, so existing populations were subject only to natural mortality. This made the lake a baseline for an unfished reservoir.

**Vegetation and habitat:** Dense plant growth restricted usable habitat. Traps placed inside dense growth rarely caught fish, while traps in open patches between weed beds caught fish successfully. The author estimated that the average annual open-water area roughly equaled the area within the 2-meter (6.5-foot) contour. Dense vegetation was maintained ahead of the retreating shoreline during summer drawdown to about 18 feet (4 m) depth.

**Seasonal drawdown:** The lake stayed near spillway level for six to eight months per year. The remaining four to six months involved drawdown, causing further loss of usable space. Area declined faster in the shallow south section than in the deeper north section.

**Thermal regime:** The author describes the lake as relatively warm, with rapid growth and mortality of species.

**Prior limnological work:** Macrobenthos and limnetic plankton, with physical and chemical data, were described by [[Felin Benthic and Plankton Study]] (1940) and [[Scott Limnology Study]] (1927).

## Key Findings

### Study design
- **Sampling period:** April 2 to May 14, 1950.
- **Method:** The [[Schnabel Method]], a modification of the [[Petersen Method]] mark-recapture technique, summing estimates across five to seven marking periods of one week or less.
- **Gear:** [[Wire Traps]] made of one-inch galvanized poultry mesh. Each trap was triangular, 3 ft on each side and 4 ft long, with a six-inch funnel entrance. Traps were set unbaited parallel to depth contours in 4–12 feet of water.
- **Marking:** Fish were marked by clipping the left pectoral fin. Each fish was weighed and measured.
- **Sections:** The north section (deeper) was sampled with 8–16 traps over five marking periods. The south section (shallow and heavily weeded) was sampled over 6–7 one-day periods. The more isolated west section was not studied.
- **Trap susceptibility control:** Traps stayed in one location no more than 24 hours, and several days passed between samplings in any one place.
- **Confidence intervals:** 0.95 limits were computed using the [[Poisson Confidence Limits]] procedure of Chapman (1948).

### Population estimates

|---|---|---|---|---|
| North | Brown bullhead | 10,554 | 9,733–11,493 | 134 |
| North | Bluegill | 4,119 | 1,928–7,743 | 99 |
| North | Black crappie | 4,542 | 2,023–8,870 | 83 |
| South | Brown bullhead | 5,693 | 3,631–8,422 | 123 |
| South | Bluegill | 665 | 102–3,752 | 99 |
| South | Black crappie | 1,201 | 184–6,776 | 76 |

- **Largemouth bass:** One recapture in the north section gave an estimate of 85, with 0.95 limits of about 18–1,657. The author considered the upper limit more realistic than the point estimate, and bass were too scarce for reliable totals.
- **Bullheads:** Dominant in both numbers and weight in both sections.
- **Size ranges sampled:** Bullheads 6–14.5 in total length; bluegills 4–9.5 in; crappies 4–14 in; bass 6–21 in. Population estimates apply only to these ranges.

### Standing crop
- Standing crops were calculated per acre for bullheads, bluegills, and crappies, using high-water area (north 34.9 ac; south 39.9 ac) and area at a drawdown of 6.5 ft (2 m).
- On a low-water basis, the total standing crop was about **162 lb/acre** in each section.
- On a high-water basis, the shallow south section appeared disproportionately low. The author judged the per-acre figures using total area to be unrealistic for that section.
- Relative to the [[Carlander Handbook]] (1950) list of standing crops, these values were high. Most bodies of water above 150 lb/acre were small managed ponds or lakes with intensive removal or large forage populations.

### Methodological issues
- **Non-random sampling:** Larger fish, especially bullheads, became scarcer in traps after four to five weeks. This was attributed to abnormal mortality or to a shift into deeper or more pelagic areas during spawning. This bias forced the census to end.
- **Trap bias:** Traps sample mainly demersal fish, which likely explains the abundance of bullheads and the scarcity of bluegills, crappies, and bass.
- **Marked versus unmarked fish:** No marked bullhead was found dead, suggesting marking did not cause excess mortality.
- **Recruitment:** Yearling fish grew during April and May, suggesting little recruitment into the sampled sizes at that time.
- **Precision:** Wide limits for bluegills and crappies resulted from few recaptures. The author recommended marking these species more intensively.
- **Effort:** One person handled 8–16 traps daily, for no more than 30 days at under six hours per day. The author estimated that 40 traps could be handled twice daily by one person if sampling intensity was increased.

### Conclusions and recommendations
- Wire traps are recommended for sampling weedy reservoirs where nets are difficult to use and manpower is limited.
- Mark-recapture work should be confined to about one month during the spring spawning season to limit bias from growth, mortality, and movement.
- Density should ideally be expressed per unit volume, not per unit area. Area-based figures are arbitrary in fluctuating reservoirs. More data on fish distribution and space use are needed to replace area-based densities.
- Studies of unfished reservoirs are valuable baselines for understanding natural balance among species in waters affected by angler removal.
