# Little Colorado Spinedace (Lepidomeda vittata) – Proposed Threatened Species Determination

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #threatened-species #conservation

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed in 1985 to list [[Lepidomeda vittata]] (Little Colorado spinedace) as a threatened species under the Endangered Species Act. This small cyprinid fish, endemic to the upper [[Little Colorado River]] drainage in eastern Arizona, has experienced severe range contraction from its historic distribution. The species now persists in only five stream segments: portions of [[East Clear Creek]], [[Chevelon Creek]], [[Silver Creek]], [[Nutrioso Creek]], and the upper [[Little Colorado River]]. Primary threats include habitat destruction from impoundments, water diversion, exotic predatory and competitive fish introductions, and use of piscicides. The proposal includes critical habitat designation for three stream segments and a special rule allowing regulated take under Arizona State permits.

## Background & Description

[[Lepidomeda vittata]] was first collected by the U.S. Topographical and Geographical Survey west of the 100th meridian and described by E.D. Cope in 1874. The type locality was listed as "Chiquito Colorado," later defined as the Little Colorado River between the mouth of the Uni River and Sierra Blanca (White Mountain). The species is a member of the family [[Cyprinidae]] and typically measures less than 10 centimeters (4 inches) in total length.

The species is endemic to the upper portions of the [[Little Colorado River]] and its north-flowing permanent tributaries on the [[Mogollon Rim]] and northern slopes of the [[White Mountains]] in eastern Arizona. This naturally restricted historic range has been significantly reduced over the past 50 years.

**Current Distribution (as of 1985):**

1. **[[East Clear Creek]]** and tributaries (Coconino County): Approximately 35 stream miles from confluence with Clear Creek upstream to headwaters near Potato Lake; flows through Apache-Sitgreaves and Coconino National Forests. [[Leonard Canyon]] at Dines tank is the only tributary currently known to harbor spinedace.

2. **[[Chevelon Creek]]** (Navajo County): Lower 8 stream miles from confluence with Little Colorado River near Winslow upstream; primarily privately-owned lands with small portion as Arizona Game and Fish Department's Chevelon Creek Wildlife Area.

3. **[[Silver Creek]]** (Navajo County): Approximately 20 stream miles from confluence with Little Colorado River upstream to headwaters near town of Silver Creek; primarily privately-owned lands.

4. **[[Little Colorado River]]** (Apache County): Sporadically throughout approximately 46 miles from St. Johns upstream to headwaters in White Mountains near Greer; mix of private, State, and Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest lands.

5. **[[Nutrioso Creek]]** (Apache County): Approximately 12 stream miles from confluence with Little Colorado River upstream to near town of Nutrioso; primarily private lands with approximately 5 miles through Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat Characteristics:**
[[Lepidomeda vittata]] inhabits very small to moderate-sized streams, characteristically found in pools with flowing water over fine gravel and silt-mud substrates. During drought periods, spinedace persist in intermittent streambed pools. During flooding, they distribute throughout the stream with no apparent habitat preferences.

**Reproductive Biology:**
The species apparently spawns primarily in early summer but continues at reduced levels until early fall.

**Population Dynamics:**
Populations fluctuate dramatically year-to-year, historically reflecting drought and rainfall patterns. Human water use has adversely altered habitat, accentuating population lows and reducing population highs. During extreme population lows, extensive collection efforts may fail to detect spinedace at locations that formerly supported healthy populations. These same locations may later support populations again. No population size estimates have been made.

**Historic Ecosystem Changes:**
In the 1880s, the Little Colorado River above Grand Falls was a perennial stream with extensive riparian areas of grasses, cottonwoods, and willows. Extensive swamps and marshy areas existed above Winslow. The river now has perennial flow only in the uppermost 10-15% of its length.

**Native Fish Community:**
Historically, the spinedace was associated with few if any fish predators. Of native species, only the [[Roundtail Chub]] ([[Gila robusta]]) was a potential predator.

## Key Findings

**Threats to Species (Section 4(a)(1) Analysis):**

**A. Habitat Destruction and Modification:**
- Approximately 150 impoundments now exist in Little Colorado basin, ranging from small stock tanks to reservoirs up to 1,400 surface acres
- Reservoirs have inundated previously occupied stream habitat
- Impoundments result in total or partial dewatering of downstream reaches
- Riparian destruction, urban growth, mining, timber harvest, road construction, livestock grazing, and watershed disturbances have caused: dewatering, erosion and channel downcutting, chemical and organic pollution, altered flow regimes, altered stream temperature, and excessive siltation

**Proposed Future Threats:**
- [[Wilkin's Dam]] at confluence of Clear and East Clear Creeks (Bureau of Reclamation project, part of larger Mogollon Mesa Project) would inundate approximately 8 miles of stream and significantly decrease downstream flows; currently inactive but pressure exists to reactivate
- Arizona Public Service Corporation conducted test drilling in 1977 to tap groundwater in Chevelon Creek drainage for Cholla Lake generating facility (water quality too poor for their needs)
- Arizona Game and Fish Department identified nine potential sites within existing spinedace range for future recreational impoundments
- Residential, recreational, and urban growth near Snowflake, Taylor, Springerville, and Saint Johns increases water demand and contributes pollution and sediments

**B. Overutilization:**
No evidence of overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific, or educational purposes.

**C. Predation:**
Exotic piscivorous fish are a major factor in decline. Introduced predatory species include:
- [[Black Bullhead]] ([[Ictalurus melas]])
- [[Channel Catfish]] ([[Ictalurus punctatus]])
- [[Yellow Bullhead]] ([[Ictalurus natalis]])
- [[Green Sunfish]] ([[Lepomis cyanellus]])
- [[Largemouth Bass]] ([[Micropterus salmoides]])
- [[Rainbow Trout]] ([[Salmo gairdneri]])
- [[Brown Trout]] ([[Salmo trutta]])

Reservoir construction exacerbates exotic fish introductions since reservoirs are desirable habitat for predatory game fishes purposely introduced for recreation. Parasites introduced with exotic fish may also adversely affect spinedace.

**D. Inadequacy of Existing Regulatory Mechanisms:**
Arizona lists this species under Group-3 of Threatened Wildlife of Arizona ("Species or subspecies whose continued presence in Arizona could be in jeopardy in the foreseeable future"). Taking is regulated and allowed only under collecting permit or by licensed angling. However, no habitat protection is included and no management plan exists.

**E. Other Factors – Competition:**
Introduction of exotic competitive fishes poses major threat:

- [[Golden Shiner]] ([[Notemigonas chrysoleucas]]) present in large numbers in Chevelon Creek since late 1950s; Arizona Game and Fish Department treated stream with pesticide in 1965 in unsuccessful eradication attempt. Golden shiner competes with young trout; since spinedace is "troutlike in its behavior and habitat requirements," golden shiner is likely significant competitor.

- [[Red Shiner]] ([[Notropis lutrensis]]) and [[Redside Shiner]] ([[Richardsonius balteatus]]) pose additional threats. Redside shiner has displaced [[Virgin River Spinedace]] ([[Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis]]) in Virgin River. Red shiner is apparently displacing [[Spikedace]] ([[Meda fulgida]]) in portions of Gila River system. Red shiner is widespread in Arizona and commonly used for bait, increasing probability of introduction.

- Spinedace is generally rare or absent where exotic fish other than trout are present.

**Piscicide Use:**
Use of fish toxicants (rotenone and toxaphene) in attempts to rid streams of "trash" fish (carp, suckers, chubs, shiners) to improve game fishing has significantly reduced populations and range:
- Little Colorado River treated from Lyman Reservoir downstream approximately 10 miles in 1951
- Chevelon Creek treated twice in 1965 and again several years later

**Proposed Critical Habitat:**
Three stream segments designated as critical habitat:

1. **[[East Clear Creek]]**, Coconino County: Approximately 18 miles from confluence with Leonard Canyon upstream to Blue Ridge Reservoir dam, and approximately 13 miles from upper end of Blue Ridge Reservoir upstream to Potato Lake

2. **[[Chevelon Creek]]**, Navajo County: Approximately 8 miles from confluence with Little Colorado River upstream to spring source

3. **[[Nutrioso Creek]]**, Apache County: Approximately 5 miles from Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest boundary upstream to Nelson Reservoir dam

**Constituent elements:** Clean, permanent flowing water with pools and fine gravel or silt-mud substrate.

**Rationale:** These stream portions presently support healthy self-perpetuating populations providing all ecological, behavioral, and physiological requirements. Silver Creek and Little Colorado River populations are currently spotty and/or difficult to locate but may change with population fluctuations.

**Proposed Special Rule (Section 4(d)):**
Since primary threat stems from habitat disturbance rather than direct taking, a special rule is proposed allowing taking without Federal permit if:
- State collecting permit obtained
- All State wildlife conservation laws and regulations satisfied
- Taking limited to: educational purposes, scientific purposes, enhancement of propagation or survival, zoological exhibition, and other conservation purposes consistent with ESA
- Incidental take by State-licensed recreational fishermen allowed if fish immediately returned to habitat
- Piscicide application precluded unless in accordance with approved conservation plan

**Conservation Status:**
Threatened status proposed (rather than endangered) because species still extant in several locations and threats are generally localized, though severely reduced range and multiple threats warrant protection. Without listing, species could reasonably be expected to become endangered within foreseeable future.
