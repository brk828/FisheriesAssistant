# The Usumacinta River: Troubles on a Wild Frontier

#freshwater-ecology #river-ecosystem #riverine-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This feature-length article describes the [[Usumacinta River]] as a large tropical river system on the Mexico-Guatemala border that drains the largest surviving rain forest in North America. The author's raft expedition documents three intertwined pressures: rapid settlement and [[Deforestation]] of the [[Lacandon Forest]], political and armed conflict that has displaced tens of thousands of people, and planned [[Mexico-Guatemala Hydroelectric Project]] dams that would flood archaeological sites and up to 500 square miles of forest. The document contains no fish survey or species data. The only fish mentioned is the *robalo*, a river fish caught for food. Its ecological value lies in the watershed as a whole.

## Background & Description

The Usumacinta drains a basin of about 41,000 square miles. The region receives rain for over half the year, up to 200 inches annually, and supports a towering rain forest with a 120-foot canopy. Unusually for North American rivers, the Usumacinta flows northward to the [[Gulf of Mexico]], passing through the Tabasco coastal plain and mangrove swamps before joining the Grijalva River.

The basin's main tributaries include the [[Chixoy River]] (called the Salinas in Mexico), the [[Pasión River]], the [[Lacantún River]], and the San Pedro. Upstream, the river cuts through a mountain spur beneath 1,000-foot cliffs and then runs placid through lowland meanders.

The region was home to the [[Maya Civilization]], whose Classic period (A.D. 250-900) produced major cities including [[Yaxchilán]], [[Piedras Negras]], [[Palenque]], and Altar de Sacrificios. After the Classic collapse, the cities reverted to forest. Maya groups survived in the wilderness, and the largest remained independent until 1697. Their descendants, the [[Lacandon Indians]], are a few hundred people in Chiapas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biodiversity.** The author reports more than a thousand plant species in a single square mile of forest, most never studied. Wildlife observed includes crocodiles, turtles, scarlet macaws, parrots, howler monkeys, brocket deer, and jaguars. Mexican zoologist Miguel Álvarez del Toro warns that "almost any animal whose natural habitat is the rain forest of Chiapas is now in danger of extinction." Animals are hunted for food, sport, and skins, including ocelot and jaguar pelts.

**Soils and land use.** Much of the region sits on [[Karst Landscape]] with porous limestone. Once forest cover is removed, fertility is lost quickly, slopes erode, rivers silt and flood, and rainfall becomes more erratic. At Arroyo Jerusalén, Chol farmers reported corn yields falling from one metric ton per hectare in the first year to one-fifth of a ton in the second. Many fields are now abandoned. Chiapas forestry chief Higinio Padilla estimates that 10,000 hectares (about 24,000 acres) of Lacandon Forest are converted to cattle pasture each year. The region's clay soils "crack and turn as hard as concrete" under pasture use.

**Forest regeneration.** Ecologist Nicholas Guppy notes that virgin rain forest is "exceptionally delicate and the product of at least a 400-year-long growth cycle," so cleared forest is unlikely to regenerate under current exploitation.

**Hydrology.** The river rises 50 feet or more in yearly floods. The Pasión and Lacantún flood spectacularly each year, and deforestation near dam basins would increase runoff.

## Key Findings

- **Settlement pressure.** Mexican officials estimate about 28,000 families occupy the Lacandon Forest, most arriving in the past ten years. Settlement names reflect migrants' hopes, such as Dreams of Gold, Abundance, and Free America.
- **Refugee crisis.** Up to 90,000 Guatemalan expatriates may be in the region, with estimates varying widely. In 1985 the UN aided 42,000 people, of whom 18,000 had been resettled, and the number of camps in Chiapas had fallen from 99 to 75. Mexico relocated many refugees to Campeche and Quintana Roo, often against their will.
- **Logging.** The Mexican Chancala mill can process nearly 17 million board feet annually and runs near full capacity. About 30 million board feet were cut in Mexico's part of the forest in 1984, mostly mahogany and red cedar. Domestic use consumes nearly twice that amount, and more than 200 million board feet is burned or left to rot during clearing.
- **Roads and oil.** Pemex exploratory drilling in the [[Marqués de Comillas Zone]] led to the region's main road, which opened the forest to logging, pasture clearing, and resettlement. A perimeter road is under construction, and a similar road may encircle the Petén. Oil drilling has increased in both countries.
- **Protected areas.** A 1977 Mexican decree set aside 1,278 square miles as the [[Montes Azules Biosphere Reserve]]. Seven years later, the proposal was still "mired in controversy," with large cut areas, communities inside the reserve, and no established boundaries.
- **Projected loss.** Conservative projections put the Lacandon Forest at about 1,500 square miles within the coming years, less than a third of its 1960 size. Gertrude Blom believes encroachment could "destroy the forest in five years."
- **Hydropower.** Mexico has 16 hydroelectric projects in planning and Guatemala has two under study. The joint project would dam the river in two to four places to generate up to 2,890 megawatts, more than Egypt's Aswan High Dam produces. Estimated cost is $2 billion to $3.7 billion in 1982 dollars. Depending on dam height, it could flood more than 500 square miles and alter at least 320 miles of river. Porous limestone could allow major leakage and flooding in Guatemala's Petén.
- **Heritage at risk.** The lowest dam heights would submerge most of Piedras Negras, leaving only its highest buildings as an island. Yaxchilán could lose its ceremonial plaza. The [[Planchón de las Figuras]] carvings, which include 68 counted figures, might vanish.
- **Local impacts.** Farming communities on rich alluvial soils would be displaced. A Bethel cooperative member said, "No one has talked to us about the dams."
- **Looting.** Organized looting for the international antiquities market has visibly plundered every site the author visited, though enforcement has reduced the trade in recent years.
- **Official position.** Guatemala's National Electrification Institute head Renato Fernández stated, "We must control the waters or lose them." Engineer Efraín Aguilera said Piedras Negras's fate "will have to be decided by the national interest."
- **Conservation view.** Guatemalan environmentalist Marta Pilón de Pacheco argued that "the environment can be utilized, but what should not be done is to abuse, wear out, and destroy." Mexican ecologist Moisés Morales called for viewing the Usumacinta as a single geographic area.
- **Security.** Guerrilla forces of the Fuerzas Armadas Rebeldes (FAR) operate in the region and have vowed to sabotage government-sponsored dams. The army is pursuing a campaign of road-building and civilian outreach. Democratic elections were scheduled for November 1985.
