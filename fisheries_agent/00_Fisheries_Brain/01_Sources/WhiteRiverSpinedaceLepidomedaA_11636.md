# White River Spinedace Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #desert-aquatic-ecosystems #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

The [[White River Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda albivallis*) is a federally endangered cyprinid fish endemic to northern [[White River Valley]] in [[Nevada]]. As of June 1991, the species persisted in only one of seven historically occupied habitats—the northern spring of the [[Flag Springs]] complex—with an estimated population under 50 individuals. This Technical/Agency Review Draft recovery plan, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in October 1992, outlines actions required to recover the species through habitat enhancement, population reestablishment, and long-term monitoring. The recovery objective is delisting when five self-sustaining populations exist for 5 consecutive years in historic habitats secured from all threats. Downlisting to threatened status may occur when three self-sustaining populations exist for at least 3 consecutive years. The species has declined due to habitat destruction from channelization and water diversion, and introduction of nonnative fishes that compete with and prey upon native species.

## Background & Description

The [[White River Spinedace]] belongs to the Plagopterini tribe of cyprinid fishes, which includes the monotypic genera *Meda* (spikedace) and *Plagopterus* (woundfin), and the polytypic genus *Lepidomeda* (spinedace). Members of this tribe are distinguished by spinelike pelvic, pectoral, and anterior dorsal fin rays; membranous connection between innermost pelvic fin rays and belly; bright silver coloration; and absent or diminutive body scales. [[White River Spinedace]] differ from other spinedace by pharyngeal tooth formula of 5-4 in the main row, typically less than 90 lateral line scales, moderately oblique mouth, moderately high dorsal fin, and melanophores extending well below the lateral line. They are the largest spinedace, commonly exceeding 4 inches total length.

Postnuptial males collected in 1934 displayed bright brassy green to olive coloration above, brassy over bright silvery on sides, silvery white below with sooty splashes. Dorsal and caudal fins showed pale olive-brown to pinkish brown coloration with deep-olive rays and rosy-flushed interradial membranes. Pectoral fins were yellowish with orange-red axils; anal and pelvic fins bright orange-red. Coppery-red to red coloration appeared on the face, gill opening, preorbital region, and upper preopercle. Females exhibited similar but less intense coloration.

The species was described by Miller and Hubbs (1960) following taxonomic review of spinedace populations. Plagopterin fishes are restricted to the [[Colorado River]] drainage system and represent relict populations from late Pleistocene pluvial stages when the [[White River]] flowed south approximately 200 miles through [[Pahranagat Valley]] and [[Moapa Valley]], joining the pluvial [[Carpenter River]] (now [[Meadow Valley Wash]]) before emptying into the [[Virgin River]]. As the pluvial system dried, native fishes became isolated in springs and disjunct river sections.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Historic habitats occupied by [[White River Spinedace]] included cool (65°-71°F), clear spring systems with source pools ranging from 15 by 25 feet to 60 by 80 feet. Bottoms comprised mostly gravel and sand with some mud. Emergent aquatic vegetation was common and often dense. Spring outflows and river sections exhibited swift to moderate current. Water temperatures among springs were relatively similar (66°-73°F), but discharge rates varied substantially from 630 to 5,700 gallons per minute.

Seven historic habitats are recognized: [[Preston Big Spring]], [[Nicholas Spring]], [[Arnoldson Spring]], [[Cold Spring]], [[Lund Spring]], [[Flag Springs]], and the [[White River]] near its confluence with [[Ellison Creek]]. All historically supported [[White River Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki intermedius*), [[White River Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus* ssp.), and [[Preston White River Springfish]] (*Crenichthys baileyi albivallis*) in various combinations. These candidate species (Category 2) require consideration in recovery planning to ensure no recovery action is detrimental to their long-term status.

Life history and habitat requirements remain poorly known. Spawning has never been observed, though remnants of spawning tubercles were present on postnuptial males collected in August 1938. Related species spawn May through August. Food preferences are unknown, though related [[Virgin River Spinedace]] feed primarily on aquatic insect larvae but consume algae and plant material when insects are unavailable. [[Preston Big Spring]] supports diverse algal flora (74 species) and invertebrate fauna (over 40 taxa of crustaceans and insects).

Habitat manipulation and nonnative fish introductions have altered native fish distributions. Early settlers diverted and impounded streams and spring outflows, removed riparian vegetation, and introduced nonnative fishes beginning in the mid-1800s. Five populations were extirpated by 1985. The [[Lund Spring]] population was extirpated after listing when improvements to an irrigation diversion structure eliminated water leakage, causing periodic desiccation of outflow ditches. [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) from downstream reservoirs currently persist in lower gradient areas of [[Flag Springs]] outflows and may limit spinedace distribution through predation.

## Key Findings

**Population Status and Distribution:**
- Only one population remains at the northern [[Flag Springs]] pool, estimated at under 50 individuals (June 1991)
- Six of seven historic populations extirpated: [[White River]], [[Preston Big Spring]], [[Nicholas Spring]], [[Arnoldson Spring]], [[Cold Spring]], and [[Lund Spring]]
- Species collected from [[White River]] (429 individuals in 1934), four disjunct spring systems in [[White Pine County]], and one spring complex in [[Nye County]] during the 1930s
- [[Lund Spring]] population extirpated after 1985 listing due to irrigation structure improvements
- No evidence that 1951 [[Colorado River]] bait fish releases or 1957 [[Railroad Valley]] transplants established populations

**Critical Habitat Designation:**
- [[Preston Big Spring]] and outflow (approximately 4.0 acres) in T. 12 N., R. 61 E., NE¼ sec. 2, [[White Pine County]]
- [[Lund Spring]] and outflow (approximately 1.3 acres) in T. 11 N., R. 62 E., NE¼ NE¼ sec. 4 and T. 12 N., R. 62 E., S½ SE¼ sec. 33, [[White Pine County]]
- [[Flag Springs]] and outflows (approximately 3.0 acres) in T. 7 N., R. 62 E., E½ NE¼ sec. 32, SW NW sec. 33, [[Nye County]]
- Constituent elements include consistently high quality and quantity of cool (55°-70°F) springs and outflows, surrounding land areas providing vegetation for cover and habitat for invertebrate prey

**Recovery Objectives and Criteria:**
- **Delisting criteria:** Five self-sustaining populations in historic habitats secured from all threats, maintained for 5 consecutive years
- **Downlisting criteria:** Three self-sustaining populations in historic habitats secured from all threats, maintained for 3 consecutive years
- Recovery priority: 2C (high threat, existing conflicts, high recovery potential)
- Estimated recovery completion: 2008
- Total estimated cost: $185,000 over 16 years (1993-2008)

**Recovery Actions Needed:**
1. **Maintain and enhance extant population** ($35,000): Enhance [[Flag Springs]] habitat, install and maintain fish barriers, remove nonnative species, develop habitat management plan, establish captive breeding program
2. **Identify ecological parameters** ($71,000): Determine life history and habitat requirements, species interactions, conduct population viability analysis
3. **Reestablish populations** ($37,000): Select suitable habitats, secure habitats through conservation agreements or acquisition, prepare management plans, rehabilitate habitats, establish populations
4. **Monitor populations and habitats** ($31,000): Continuous monitoring of population size, age-class structure, distribution, habitat quality
5. **Public information program** ($11,000): Inform local residents and stakeholders of protected status and recovery efforts

**Immediate Conservation Needs:**
- Northern [[Flag Springs]] pool undergoing natural vegetative succession that threatens pool integrity
- Fish barrier installed in 1985 on combined [[Flag Springs]] outflow requires effectiveness evaluation
- [[Largemouth Bass]] present above barrier in outflows
- [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]] installed habitat improvement structures in spring 1992 and removed excess watercress
- [[Flag Springs]] complex and water rights owned by State of Nevada on [[Wayne E. Kirch Wildlife Management Area]]

**Coexisting Species Considerations:**
- Recovery plan oriented toward aquatic ecosystem recovery, not single-species focus
- All recovery actions must avoid detriment to [[White River Desert Sucker]], [[White River Speckled Dace]], and [[Preston White River Springfish]]
- Relative composition of native fishes has shifted over time as species were extirpated from individual habitats
- Research on species interactions necessary before reintroduction to determine effects on resident native fishes

**Legal Protection:**
- Listed as federally endangered with critical habitat designation in 1985 (50 Federal Register 37194)
- Nevada protected species under Nevada Revised Statutes 503.065
- American Fisheries Society classified as endangered (1987)
- Section 6 funding allocated 1991-1992 for status survey, ecological investigations, and habitat evaluation
