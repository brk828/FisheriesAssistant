# Little Colorado Spinedace Monitoring Project: 1993–1995 Summary Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) technical report summarizes three field seasons (1993, 1994, 1995) of monitoring for the federally threatened [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda vittata*) across its disjunct range in the [[Little Colorado River Basin]]. Surveys sampled 97 site-visits across the Little Colorado River drainage, catching more than 35,000 fish. The spinedace remains depressed throughout most of its range, has apparently been extirpated from [[Silver Creek]] and much of the [[Clear Creek]] drainage, and persists at low levels in a few refugia such as [[Dines Tank]]. Nonnative predators, drought-driven habitat loss, and habitat alteration are identified as the principal threats. The report closes with monitoring protocol changes and stream-specific management recommendations.

## Background & Description

The Little Colorado spinedace is a small (<10 cm), silvery, often solitary cyprinid endemic to the Little Colorado River Basin. It was federally listed as threatened, with about 71 km of stream designated as [[Critical Habitat]] along East Clear Creek, Chevelon Creek, and Nutrioso Creek.

Habitat and behavior:
- Prefers clear, relatively open pools with fine gravel or silt-mud substrates, overhanging banks, or large rocks for cover.
- Spawns in late spring and early summer.
- Avoids the deepest or heavily shaded pools in warm seasons and shallow open water.
- Young-of-the-year are most abundant on uniformly turbulent riffles.
- Persists in deep pools and spring heads during drought; redistributes throughout the stream after flooding.

Diet: predominantly aquatic arthropods, especially caddisflies, with terrestrial insects in smaller proportion. Stomach analyses from [[Nutrioso Creek]] and [[Chevelon Creek]] (May–June 1990) recorded chironomid larvae, mayfly nymphs, cladocerans, detritus, and filamentous algae. Behavior and habitat resemble those of the [[Virgin River Spinedace]], which has been studied in more detail.

Documented threats: habitat loss and alteration, use of fish toxicants, and introduction and spread of nonnative predatory and competitive fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study objectives** (four management objectives, plus a baseline-establishment goal on AGFD properties):
1. Monitor trends in relative abundance and distribution through time.
2. Monitor gross changes in stream habitats and riparian conditions.
3. Document occurrence of nonnative fish species.
4. Document predation on spinedace by nonnative predators.

Laboratory work cited a negative association between [[Rainbow Trout]] and spinedace, and predation by rainbow trout and [[Green Sunfish]].

**Methods (summary):**
- 53 sites sampled over three years; sites were designated "permanent" (annual) or "random" (drawn from a pre-existing list), but designations shifted after 1993 because some sites were dewatered or landowners denied access.
- Sites were at least 200 m long and divided into run, riffle, and pool macrohabitats.
- Gear included backpack electrofishers (Coffelt BP-4, Smith-Root Model 12 POW), dip nets, and 1/8-inch mesh seines.
- At permanent sites, at least 30 fish per site were measured for total length; fish ≥100 mm were weighed.
- Habitat data included water temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity, substrate (modified Wentworth scale, Table 1), cover, velocity, and bank stability.
- Nonnative trout stomachs were examined for native prey.
- 1995 protocol change: sites without spinedace received an extra 200 m of survey length, up to a maximum of 600 m.

**Study area:** divided into an western section (Clear and Chevelon creeks and tributaries) and an eastern section (upper Little Colorado River, Nutrioso, Rudd, and Silver creeks), with 20 and 16 sites respectively in 1993–1994.

**Species assemblage:** four native species (Little Colorado River sucker, Little Colorado spinedace, [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]]) and eleven nonnative species were captured, including [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Red Shiner]].

## Key Findings

**Overall results**
- 97 sampling efforts: 36 sites in 1993 (23 permanent, 13 random), 36 in 1994 (19 permanent, 17 random), and 25 in 1995 (all treated as permanent).
- More than 35,000 fish collected: about 25,000 in 1993, 5,400 in 1994, and 5,100 in 1995.
- Hugo Meadow on Chevelon Creek yielded the most fish in every year (over 80% of the 1993 catch; about 30% in 1994 and 1995).
- Spinedace was present at 11 of 30 sites with fish in 1993, 13 sites in 1994, and was present at 19 of 25 sites in 1995 (all 25 sites had fish; all but six yielded spinedace).

**Stream-level status**
- **Clear Creek drainage:** spinedace found at 3 of 16 sites in 1993, 1 of 18 in 1994, and 4 of 7 in 1995 (within East Clear Creek and Leonard Canyon). Status described as "precarious." No records from Clear Creek since 1960 and none from Hamilton Crossing since 1990.
- **Dines Tank (Leonard Canyon):** population maintained but greatly reduced; 171 spinedace in August 1983 (about 57% of catch) versus 2 in July 1995 (1.1%). Recommended for protection.
- **Chevelon Creek:** Above the Steps, 4 (1990), 3 (1994), 2 (1995) individuals; at the Steps, relative abundance 1.7% (1993), 6.0% (1994), 1.8% (1995), down from 54.8% in 1990. Hugo Meadow spinedace rose to 3.1% (46 fish) in 1995 from 0.2% in 1994.
- **Little Colorado River:** spinedace remained a minor component (generally ≤11% at most sites). At The Corral, relative abundance rose from 4% (1990) to 16.7% (1995). At South of St. Johns near Little Reservoir, the population fluctuated from 46.1% to 76.9% and was 90.0% in 1991.
- **Nutrioso Creek:** Rudd Creek Confluence declined from a historical high of 76.9% (1983) to 1.2% (1995); Correjo Crossing 11.2% (1993), 12.8% (1994), 9.8% (1995).
- **Rudd Creek:** first documented in 1992 (over 77% at Lower Sipe White Mountain Wildlife Area); fell to 22% in 1994 and 6.3% in 1995. Only five sites surveyed.
- **Silver Creek:** no spinedace found; considered extirpated (last record 1965).

**Threat evidence**
- Brook trout (97 specimens, 16% of catch) at Little Colorado River Highway 260 Bridge in 1993 coincided with no spinedace captured there in the following two years.
- Speckled dace increases often corresponded inversely with spinedace declines.
- Blue Ridge Reservoir acts as a barrier to recolonization within East Clear Creek.
- Stomachs of 56 nonnative predators were examined across 1993–1995 (23, 19, and 14 fish respectively); fish remains were found in only a few.

**Management recommendations**
- Continue long-term annual monitoring with 200 m minimum sample length, extended as needed to a 600 m maximum, and validate protocols per stream.
- Adopt a watershed-based approach to fisheries management covering native and nonnative sportfish.
- Conduct research on spinedace life history, interactions with nonnative fish and crayfish, and habitat alteration.
- Stream-specific actions include evaluating [[Potato Lake]] as a refugium, creating deep pool refugia in East Clear and Leonard canyons, protecting Dines Tank, assessing barriers to nonnative fish, ensuring minimum flows in Chevelon, Nutrioso, and Little Colorado reaches, and establishing refugia (e.g., Winema, Slade, Hugo Meadow, Sipe White Mountain Wildlife Area) and display aquaria.
- Evaluate reintroduction options in Silver Creek only after nonnative predator control is considered.
