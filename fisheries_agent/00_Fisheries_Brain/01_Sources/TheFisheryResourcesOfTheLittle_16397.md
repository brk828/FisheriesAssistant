# The Fishery Resources of the Little River System, McCurtain County, Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #stream-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1955 field survey by the [[Oklahoma Fisheries Research Laboratory]] (Finnell, Jenkins and Hall) documents the fish populations, habitat, growth rates and standing crops of the [[Little River]] system in southeastern Oklahoma. Sampling took place June to August 1955 using rotenone, seines, gill nets and hook and line. The survey recorded 87 species, and the known total for the system rose to 98 species, with 112 possible once expected species are included. The stream fauna is dominated by forage fishes and sunfishes. The survey also evaluates the proposed [[Broken Bow Reservoir]] on the [[Mountain Fork River]] and predicts the future fishery of impounded waters.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** The Little River drains approximately 2,200 square miles of southeastern Oklahoma. Its main tributaries are the [[Mountain Fork River]] (842 square miles, 96 miles long), the Glover River (about 110 miles) and the Black Fork River (about 40 miles). The Little River is 150 miles long in Oklahoma and flows 80 miles in Arkansas to join the Saline River.
- **Terrain and geology:** The region lies in the [[Ouachita Mountains]], with steep, rocky slopes and sandstone and chert ledges. Elevations range from 750 to 1,250 feet, with some peaks above 1,400 feet. Streams are mostly in youthful or early mature condition, with V-shaped valleys, rapids and boulder shoals.
- **Land use:** Over three-fourths of the watershed is forested, but most commercial pine, cypress and oak timber was cut over the previous 45 years. Less than 10 percent is cultivated. Grazing, burning and erosion are the main land problems.
- **Climate and hydrology:** The climate is humid and mesothermal. Average annual temperature is 64°F and average precipitation is 44 inches (range 28 to 67 inches). The Little River at Idabel averages 2,070 cfs, and the Mountain Fork at Eagletown averages 1,400 cfs. Mountain Fork flow usually falls below 300 cfs from July to September. Annual peak discharges since 1925 ranged from 18,200 cfs (1931) to 91,500 cfs (1950).
- **Water quality:** Water is soft (total hardness 11 to 18 ppm) and slightly acid (pH 6.2 to 7.2) in the upper reaches. It is neutral to slightly basic (pH 7.0 to 8.0) in the lower reaches and bottomlands. Specific conductance and mineral content are very low.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat structure:** Upper tributaries have bedrock and boulder bottoms, few gravel pools and little aquatic vegetation. Most tributaries stop flowing in summer and retain disconnected pools. The lower Little River has a gentler gradient, deeper water and finer silt bottoms, and it supports extensive [[Cutoff Lakes]] in its floodplain (0.5 to 30 acres, typically with buttonbush fringes).
- **Flow regime:** Flow is erratic and ranges from flood to near-zero. This instability limits bottom fauna and gravel spawning habitat and is considered a major constraint on fish production.
- **Historic pressures:** Logging, grazing, forest fires, erosion and flash floods have altered the biology of the streams. Municipal and industrial pollution was described as practically nonexistent in the lower river.
- **Fish community zones:** The upper Mountain Fork is dominated by smallmouth bass and sunfish. Gizzard shad, golden redhorse and sunfish dominate the lower reaches, and cutoff lakes carry crappie, bluegill and gizzard shad.
- **Biological benchmarks:** The survey standing crops for the three Mountain Fork tributaries were 63.2 lb/acre (Boktuklo Creek), 166.9 lb/acre (Cow Creek) and 286.7 lb/acre (Otter Creek). The mean of the three was 172.3 lb/acre. Six Little River cutoff lakes averaged 182.9 lb/acre across 10.8 acres.

## Key Findings

**Survey scope and catch**
- 87 species were collected during June to August 1955 from 62 sites (28 Mountain Fork drainage sites, 34 Little River drainage sites).
- Two species, mooneye and brown bullhead, were recorded for Oklahoma for the first time.
- A total of 23,854 fishes were processed. Forage fishes made up 49.4 percent, pan fishes 31.9 percent, coarse fishes 14.1 percent and sport fishes 4.6 percent.

**Distribution patterns**
- Smallmouth bass dominated the upper Mountain Fork sport catch (93.2 percent of sport fishes), declining to 15.2 percent in the lower reaches.
- Spotted bass (2.7 to 21.7 percent) and largemouth bass (4.1 to 29.4 percent) increased downstream, indicating a preference for deeper, more stable water.
- In the Little River drainage, largemouth bass (35.1 percent) and spotted bass (31.6 percent) were the most abundant sport fishes. In cutoff lakes, white crappie (53.1 percent) dominated.
- Green and longear sunfish were the most abundant pan fishes throughout. Redear sunfish were absent from the Mountain Fork collections, despite about 441,900 individuals stocked from 1941 to 1955.
- Golden redhorse dominated the upper and middle coarse fishes. Gizzard shad dominated the lower Mountain Fork (29.1 percent) and the cutoff lakes (52.5 percent).
- The bigeye shiner was the most numerous forage fish system-wide. The taillight shiner made up 40.0 percent of forage fishes in cutoff lakes.
- The leopard darter had the most restricted distribution, found only in Cow and Boktuklo creeks.

**Standing crop and growth**
- Standing crop in the three Mountain Fork tributaries compared favorably with other warm-water streams and exceeded figures cited for trout streams.
- Growth of largemouth bass, channel catfish, and longear and green sunfish was faster in the southeastern streams than in the Illinois River. Smallmouth and spotted bass grew more slowly after the third year.
- Channel catfish were the fastest-growing game fish, reaching 10 inches early in their third summer.
- Smallmouth bass rarely exceeded six years of age. Legal length (10 inches) was reached in the fourth summer. Spotted bass needed almost four growing seasons to reach 10 inches.
- Scale annuli were poorly defined, so only sport and pan fishes were aged.

**Management recommendations**
- The survey found no need for regulatory size limits or closed seasons. Angling pressure is moderate, and present use could roughly double without endangering smallmouth bass.
- The drying of cutoff pools each summer probably kills more legal-size bass than anglers catch.
- Stocking unsuited species is considered futile, since hatchery-reared species were extremely rare in the samples.
- The main management need is restoring watershed condition: intensive reforestation, reduced overgrazing and fewer scouring floods. Low-water dams are judged only a temporary fix.

**Predicted impoundment effects (Broken Bow Reservoir)**
- Expected to resemble [[Tenkiller Reservoir]] in its early fish community, with explosive early growth of sunfish and bass followed by declines in stream-adapted species.
- Predicted to be classed as a largemouth bass, crappie, white bass and channel catfish fishery, with gizzard shad expected to constitute about 75 percent of a predicted standing crop of about 300 lb/acre.
- Bluegill expected to dominate pan fishes. White crappie expected to outnumber black crappie by about 3 to 1.
- Possible rainbow trout fishery below the dam if aerated tailwater releases are provided.
- Predicted annual fishing use of 20,300 man-days, while the Fish and Wildlife Service estimate was 13,800 fisherman-days per year for the affected river reaches. The authors suggested use could approach 100,000 man-days per year in the first decade.

**Environmental risks**
- A pronounced summer thermocline was observed in [[Broken Bow City Lake]] on 21 July 1955. Temperature fell 20°F from the surface to 10 feet. Dissolved oxygen dropped from 11.2 ppm at the surface to 0.0 ppm at 10 feet, and pH fell from 7.2 to 6.4.
- Aquatic plant growths (Chara, Potamogeton, Nuphar, Nymphaea) could become profuse in waters under 6 to 8 feet deep if reservoir levels stay stable. The authors suggest water-level fluctuation as a control.

**Conclusion**
- The upland streams are classed as smallmouth bass–green sunfish waters, with longear sunfish, golden redhorse and minnows as companion species. The lower Little River is a more typical large-stream fishery with largemouth bass, channel catfish and white bass.
- The authors advise that improved land management, more stable stream flows and better fish habitat are needed to raise productivity in the coming quarter-century.
