# Genetic Monitoring of Repatriated Populations of Endangered Bonytail and Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #conservation-genetics #catostomidae #cyprinidae #endangered-species #repatriation #mtDNA

## Executive Summary

This unsolicited proposal to the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] outlines a three-year genetic monitoring program for endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) populations in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. Submitted by researchers from [[Arizona State University]] (Paul C. Marsh, Thomas E. Dowling, and W.L. Minckley) in September 1996, the proposal seeks to assess genetic diversity in remnant wild populations and evaluate the genetic representativeness of ongoing hatchery-based repatriation programs. The study employs mitochondrial DNA analysis using cytochrome b gene sequencing, SSCP (single-stranded conformational polymorphism) analysis, and PCR amplification to characterize genetic variation within and among populations. Preliminary results indicate high genetic diversity in razorback sucker adults (32 haplotypes via restriction site analysis, 13 via SSCP) and concerning differences between hatchery and wild bonytail populations. The proposed budget totals $400,940 over three years, with field sampling coordinated through the multi-agency [[Native Fish Work Group]].

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] are federally listed endangered species endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Both species have experienced catastrophic population declines throughout their historic ranges, with [[Lake Mohave]] representing one of the few remaining strongholds. The razorback sucker population in Lake Mohave consists of aging adults with limited natural recruitment, while bonytail numbers are estimated at only a few hundred to a few thousand individuals. Wild, naturally-produced bonytail are extremely rare, and current policy mandates that any captured wild-spawned individuals be transferred to the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] for broodstock development.

Since 1981, over 190,000 hatchery-produced bonytail have been stocked in Lake Mohave to augment the dwindling wild population, though most were unmarked and relatively small (10-150 mm). The [[Native Fish Work Group]] has implemented a repatriation program for razorback sucker that involves harvesting larvae directly from the lake, rearing them in hatcheries and lake-side backwaters, and restocking at approximately 30 cm with individual [[PIT Tags]] (Passive Integrated Transponders).

The molecular genetics approach focuses on mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), specifically the cytochrome b (cytb) gene, which offers several advantages: maternal inheritance without recombination, relatively rapid evolution allowing detection of population-level variation, ease of isolation and characterization, and proven utility in fish population studies. The cytb gene was selected because it contains both conserved and rapidly evolving regions, evolves more slowly than most mitochondrial genes (appropriate for hypervariable species), and has been successfully used in previous cyprinid and catostomid studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Lake Mohave]] is a reservoir on the [[Lower Colorado River]] created by [[Davis Dam]]. The lake supports a complex fish community including both native and non-native species. For razorback sucker, three primary staging and spawning locations have been identified: [[Arizona Bay]] (Arizona), [[Tequila Cove]] (Nevada), and [[Nine Mile]] (Nevada). Spawning activity occurs primarily between November and March, with peak spawning in January.

Field sampling protocols involve trammel nets (6 x 50 to 300 feet, with ⅝ to 1⅛ inch inner-meshes and 10 or 12 inch outer-meshes) set along shorelines, in coves and embayments, and on wave-washed points at depths from less than 3 feet to more than 60 feet. Nets are examined at overnight intervals of no more than 6 hours, with highest catch rates occurring between dusk and dawn. Sampling typically occurs during March, May, and November of each year, with additional collections as needed.

Larval razorback suckers are collected at night using hand-held aquarium dip nets after attraction to lights suspended in the water. This method has proven effective for obtaining representative samples of the larval cohort.

The study recognizes that genetic diversity of the remnant adult razorback sucker stock is known to be high, and it is imperative that repatriation programs adequately represent this diversity. The research aims to evaluate whether genetic makeup of repatriated populations remains stable as individual cohorts age and experience natural mortality.

## Key Findings

**Preliminary Razorback Sucker Results:**

- Restriction endonuclease analysis of adult razorback sucker gametes identified 32 distinct haplotypes with high genetic diversity (haplotype diversity h = 0.82, number of haplotypes per individual nh = 0.67)
- SSCP analysis detected 13 haplotypes with somewhat lower but still substantial diversity (h = 0.69, nh = 0.29)
- Sequence analysis of four haplotypes revealed relatively limited variation (0.0-0.5% sequence divergence)
- Analysis of 600 bp of the cytb gene using two primer sets (LA-HE and LE-HA) identified 21 composite haplotypes across three sample groups
- A single haplotype (AA) predominated at approximately 50% frequency across all samples (1987-1988 adults, 1993 adults, and 1994 larvae)
- Monte Carlo simulations failed to detect significant heterogeneity in allele frequencies among temporal samples, suggesting these may represent random draws from a single population
- No evidence of spatial or temporal sub-structuring was detected in preliminary analyses

**Preliminary Bonytail Chub Results:**

- Analysis of 19 F1 individuals from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] broodstock revealed only a single haplotype
- Analysis of 16 individuals recently captured in Lake Mohave ("wild" fish) identified two haplotypes at high frequency, differing by two mutations
- The presence of unique genotypes in Lake Mohave samples but absent from hatchery stock has significant management implications
- Two hypotheses could explain this pattern: (1) F1 samples may not adequately represent broodstock variation, or (2) additional bonytail persist in Lake Mohave that have not contributed to existing broodstock

**Proposed Research Activities:**

- Three-year study period (January 1997 - December 1999) with field sampling during three consecutive spawning seasons
- Target sample sizes: 25-30 adult razorback suckers per locality per date (up to 270 fish total); 30-50 larval razorback suckers per locality per date (up to 450 specimens); comparable numbers of repatriated individuals
- Non-lethal sampling methods: muscle biopsy with 2.0 mm biopsy needle, fin clips, and gamete collection from expressing individuals
- All fish to be PIT-tagged for individual identification and released near point of capture
- Additional genetic samples to be obtained from federal facilities ([[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]]), state hatcheries, and cooperating agencies
- Laboratory analyses using PCR amplification, SSCP screening, and direct DNA sequencing of cytb gene variants
- Statistical analyses to assess spatial and temporal heterogeneity using programs HAPLOID and REAP
- Genetic diversity to be partitioned into within- and among-sample components
