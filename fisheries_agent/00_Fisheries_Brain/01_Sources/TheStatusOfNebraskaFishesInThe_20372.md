# Status of Nebraska Fishes in the Missouri River: Flathead Catfish, Blue Catfish and Sauger

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This note combines two Nebraska Game and Parks Commission status reports by Larry W. Hesse (Dingell-Johnson Project F-75-R-11, 1993). Part 4/5 covers [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Blue Catfish]]. Part 6 covers [[Sauger]]. All three species are described as severely reduced in Nebraska's Missouri River. The author attributes the declines to overharvest, [[Channelization]], impoundment, reduced turbidity, removal of [[Large Woody Debris]], and altered [[Hydrograph Alteration|hydrograph]] and sediment transport. The reports recommend ending commercial harvest, restricting sport harvest, listing Blue Catfish and Sauger as endangered in Nebraska, and restoring habitat, including floodplain connection and woody debris.

## Background & Description

**Flathead and Blue Catfish.** Flathead catfish were historically large. Reports from the period include a 1862 newspaper account of fish weighing "two and three hundred pounds," and a 1960 snagging trip that landed more than 50 large flatheads in one day. Blue catfish were common before the turn of the century. The [[Lewis and Clark Expedition]] journal of 1804 called them "white catfish." Both species were harvested heavily by net, set-line and trot-line gear. The [[Telephone Generator]] electrofishing method was the main sampling tool for flatheads and was highly specific to these two catfish.

**Sauger.** Sauger are closely related to [[Walleye]] and are often confused with them by anglers. They can be told apart by the dark spots on the membranous webbing of the spinous dorsal fin, which walleye lack. Sauger also have 5 to 8 pyloric caeca, while walleye have three. Sauger hybridize with walleye. Hatchery-produced hybrids, known as [[Saugeye]], were planted in Nebraska and adjacent states, and the author warns they could cross with native sauger.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use.** Flatheads favored deep scour holes formed by large tree snags. Radiotagged adults in the [[Platte River]] were found in complex cover associated with large log snags 60% of the time (Peters et al. 1989). Blue catfish used deep areas for winter resting and swift water near complex habitat the rest of the year. Sauger used side channels and backwaters seasonally for feeding and maturing, and the main channel for breeding.
- **Turbidity.** Water clarity was an important factor. Flathead density was strongly and inversely related to [[Secchi Disk Transparency]] (r = -0.9636).
- **Spawning.** Sauger likely spawned in March and April at 7.2 to 10.0 °C before dams altered the temperature regime. Spawning now extends to late May in tailwaters. Eggs are broadcast over rock or rubble bottoms, and sauger spawning sites had velocities of 42 to 144 cm/s.
- **Diet.** Sauger fed mostly on fish once larger than about 50 mm. In a 1974–1975 channelized-reach sample of 165 stomachs, fish occurred in 80% of stomachs, aquatic insects in 40%, and crustaceans in 5%.
- **Snag removal.** More than 17,000 large trees were removed from the lowermost 800 km of the river in 1901 alone. The author links this to declines in catfish and "most all native Missouri River species."
- **Flow management.** Dam releases, peaking operations at [[Fort Randall Dam]], and flood-control storage at [[Gavins Point Dam]] dewatered off-channel habitat and disrupted spring spawning and nursery conditions.

## Key Findings

### Flathead Catfish

- **Size structure.** The percentage of flatheads longer than the 457 mm legal commercial size limit was 2.6% (1974), 4.0% (1978), 3.0% (1989), 2.3% (1990), 3.4% (1991), and 1.6% (1993). For comparison, 100% of flatheads sampled from the Cape Fear River, North Carolina, were longer than 457 mm, and 27% from the Flint River, Georgia.
- **Relative stock density.** The Nebraska population had RSD-quality 6% and RSD-preferred 0%. The Cape Fear River had 49% and 28%, and the Flint River had 48% and 21%.
- **Density.** The upper unchannelized reach held an estimated 417 to 900 flatheads during 1990–1991, or about 9 per km over 72 km. The abstract puts the population below 1,000. Upper unchannelized density is described as 6 to 10% of the lower unchannelized reach, and channelized density as six times the unchannelized density.
- **CPUE comparison.** Mean electrofishing CPUE was 0.189 in the unchannelized reach and 1.301 in the channelized reach (P = 0.0001, F = 49.62). Unchannelized density was 14.5% of channelized density.
- **Tagging.** 225 tagged transplants were released in 1990. In a later sample, 6 of 11 flatheads (55%) were tagged transplants. One year later, 6 of 24 (25%) were tagged.
- **Upper reach collections.** No flatheads were collected upstream of the [[Niobrara River]] confluence in nearly three hours of effort in recent years. From 1977 to 1987, 11 flatheads were caught in 2,267 minutes of electrofishing, all downstream of the confluence.
- **Commercial harvest.** Mean annual harvest fell from 9,074 kg (1944–1953) to 6,876 kg, 3,251 kg (-26%), and 5,116 kg (-38%) in later decades. Flatheads made up 7.6% of reported commercial catch on average before 1978 and 15.8% after. Harvest was 16,581 kg in 1984, 8,872 kg in 1986, 19,014 kg in 1990, and 8,117 kg in 1991.
- **Length change.** Mean sample length rose from 221 mm TL (1986–1990) to 271 mm TL (1991–1993), a significant difference.
- **Regulation.** Commercial flathead fishing was approved for closure in October 1990 and took effect in January 1992. Sport limits were combined with channel catfish, at 10 per day. Other basin states also closed commercial fishing.

### Blue Catfish

- **Near extirpation.** More than 51,000 catfish were collected in Nebraska from about 1958 to the present. Only 16 were blue catfish, and 15 of those were young-of-the-year captured downstream of Omaha in 1991.
- **Missouri comparison.** Missouri surveys found 2% (1,350) blue catfish among 63,191 catfish.
- **Historical decline.** Nebraska commercial blue catfish harvest fell from 5,846 kg in 1944 to 654 kg in 1966, and was stopped in 1967.
- **Failed stocking.** More than 1.2 million fingerlings (40–80 mm TL) were stocked in 1980–1982. None were recovered.
- **Reporting concern.** Sport anglers reported 250 blue catfish in 1992, but none were verified by biologists. Commercial blue catfish reports may also include misidentified channel catfish.

### Sauger

- **Historical abundance.** Before channelization and impoundment, sauger made up 10 to 65% of the main-channel big river fish group. Since then, sauger have declined by as much as 98% in some areas.
- **Channelized reach electrofishing.** In 1974–1975 in revetment habitat, sauger were 2.38% of the catch at 2.44/hr. In 1986–1990, they were 0.39% at 0.21/hr, a drop of 84% and 91%.
- **Winter collections.** Winter sauger composition fell from 3.42% (1974–1975) to 0.24% (1979–1986), a 93% decline.
- **Unchannelized reach.** Sauger were 13.3% of the catch in 1983 (2.82/hr) and zero in 1990. Night electrofishing at the Boyd County spawning bank fell 90%, from a 1963–1965 peak of 36/hr to a 1982–1989 mean of 3.7/hr.
- **Gill netting.** Sauger CPUE declined steadily in off-channel habitat near the Niobrara from 1983 to 1991, and the decline was highly significant. At Desoto Bend, catch fell from 30 per net-night in 1961 to 2 in 1965, after the bend was cut off.
- **Larval sauger.** More than 112,000 larval fish have been collected since 1983. Mean larval sauger density was 0.9/1,000 m³ in the upper unchannelized reach, compared with 10.6/1,000 m³ reported by Nelson in 1965. Sauger larvae were 3.0% of fish larvae in the channelized reach in 1974 and 0.2% in 1986–1991, a 93% decline.
- **Stocking.** Of 123,082 sauger stocked into mainstem reservoirs since 1963, only 7,333 went into Nebraska's portion of the river. Stockings are judged to have failed.
- **Tailwater sport harvest.** Sauger were 26.7% of the 1,512 fish harvested in 1956 and 30% of the 239,976 harvested in 1958. The 1956 Gavins Point tailwater fishery overshadowed the Missouri River resource that supported it. By 1992, sauger were 0.2% (106 fish) of the tailwater harvest.
- **Possible recovery.** Anglers reported many young-of-the-year sauger in 1993 below Gavins Point Dam. The author suggests this may reflect the 1993 flooding, but notes that the result cannot be confirmed until sauger enter gill nets in a year or so.

### Management Recommendations

- **Blue catfish:** fully protect the species. Release all fish caught, list it as endangered in Nebraska, and do not stock Missouri River waters until the effects of hatchery genetics on wild stock are known.
- **Flathead catfish:** close the upper unchannelized reach to harvest. Set a maximum size limit downstream of [[Gavins Point Dam]], releasing flatheads over 500 mm TL. Prohibit harvest during the June pre-spawn period, and reduce set-line and trot-line pressure.
- **Sauger:** list as endangered in Nebraska and stop all harvest.
- **Habitat (all three):** restore a [[Floodplain Restoration|floodplain]] that floods seasonally, recover large woody debris in the channel, reconnect cut-off chutes and backwaters, restore sediment transport through [[Sediment Bypass]] at mainstem dams, and restore a more natural hydrograph.
