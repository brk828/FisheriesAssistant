# Phylogeny of Pyrgulopsis Springsnails: Systematics, Biogeography, and Conservation

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrobiidae

## Executive Summary

This is a two-year funding proposal to the [[Scholarly Studies Program]] of the [[Smithsonian Institution]] (start date September 1, 1998; total request $62,506). It asks for support to build a molecular phylogeny of [[Pyrgulopsis]], the largest genus of nonmarine snails in North America, and to use that phylogeny for taxonomic revision and [[Vicariance Biogeography]] analysis. The work continues a prior project on [[Tryonia]], which used [[Mitochondrial COI Gene]] sequences to overcome a long-standing obstacle in hydrobiid systematics.

The principal investigator is [[Robert Hershler]] of the [[National Museum of Natural History]]. The co-principal investigator is [[Hsiu-Ping Liu]] of [[Montclair State University]], who provides molecular expertise.

## Background & Description

**Study group.** Hydrobiid springsnails are small (Pyrgulopsis shells are 1–8 mm) and have smooth or weakly sculptured shells. The proposal argues they are suitable for evolutionary and biogeographic work for several reasons:

- They are obligate to water and disperse slowly, which links them closely to drainage history.
- They show more local endemism than fishes.
- They often persist in basins without fish and are less affected by introgression.

**Taxonomic background.** The genus Pyrgulopsis includes 131 recent species, 123 of them extant (per the proposal). Its systematics are poorly resolved. Morphology-based trees were weakly supported, many characters are homoplasic, and [[Penial Morphology]] varies more than in any other nymphophiline genus. Several widespread or variable species are suspected to contain cryptic species, including *P. gibba*, *P. kolobensis*, *P. micrococcus*, and *P. wongi*.

**Prior work.** Hershler has published a monographic synthesis (1994), a descriptive paper (1995), and a Great Basin treatment (1998) that described 58 new species. The earlier [[Tryonia]] project found that the genus as then constituted was polyphyletic, and it supported the [[Amargosa River Basin]] as a combined biotic and geologic unit.

**Distribution and biology.** The Great Basin holds the greatest diversity (80 species), followed by the Colorado River drainage (20 species). Most species are endemic to single springs or spring systems. Snails graze periphyton, lay eggs in capsules on hard substrates, and have direct development with crawl-away young. Fossil records date the genus to at least the [[Miocene]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Biogeographic setting.** The proposal frames the American West as a region of isolated aquatic habitats, shaped by desert and mountain barriers and by complex Cenozoic geologic history. It cites [[Minckley et al. 1986]] for a vicariance model of western areas.
- **Fish comparison.** The [[Cyprinodon Pupfish]] of [[Death Valley]] show distribution patterns partly congruent with those of *Tryonia*. Early fish-based studies emphasized late Pleistocene pluvial drainages, while later work stressed great antiquity and vicariance.
- **Dispersal.** Some Pyrgulopsis species also live along montane slopes, which the authors suggest may allow upland headwater transfers across drainage divides.
- **Conservation.** Many species are at risk. Some occur on private land, and their owners have been hostile to field biologists. The authors plan to share taxonomic and distribution information with land managers under a Memorandum of Understanding on [[Springsnail Conservation]] with the National Museum of Natural History.
- **Eastern "Pyrgulopsis."** Twelve eastern American species are currently allocated to the genus. Morphological studies suggest they form a distinct clade unrelated to the western Pyrgulopsis.

## Key Findings

**Stated goals of the proposed research:**

1. Test monophyly and phylogenetic structure of Pyrgulopsis using mtDNA sequences, and map morphological characters onto the resulting trees.
2. Revise the genus's taxonomy. The authors expect non-conspecificity across major drainage divides and numerous previously unrecognized species.
3. Conduct a vicariance biogeographic analysis based on the new phylogeny, and compare it with geologic history and with other regional biota.

**Preliminary results (pilot study):**

- About 600 bp of mtCOI were sequenced from seven Pyrgulopsis congeners, four eastern nymphophilines (*Cincinnatia*, *Notogillia*, *Spilochlamys*), and two Australian outgroups (*Ascorhis*, *Phrantela*).
- Total aligned length was 584 bp, of which 152 were parsimony-informative.
- Maximum parsimony yielded two trees of 502 steps (CI = 0.65). Taxa were well resolved with high [[Bootstrap Analysis]] support.
- The results were congruent with morphology in placing eastern "Pyrgulopsis" apart from western snails.
- The authors infer that the ancestral Pyrgulopsis had a highly glandular penis, and that simpler forms (e.g., *P. owensensis*, *P. amargosae*, *P. stearnsiana*) are reductive.

**Sampling plan:**

- About 80 described congeners, selected to span morphological variation and all occupied drainages.
- 15–20 unreported species from [[National Museum of Natural History]] holdings.
- Representatives of the other seven nymphophiline genera, plus Australasian outgroups.
- About 200 populations sampled in total. Roughly 250 specimens will be sequenced.
- The existing USNM collection (more than 1,400 lots, formalin-fixed) will serve morphology. Fresh ethanol-preserved samples (about 10 individuals per population) are needed for DNA.

**Molecular and analytical methods:**

- Full COI (about 1500 bp) and a second gene, mtCOII (about 1000 bp), with COIII, 16S, or ND genes as backups.
- Analysis in PAUP* with maximum parsimony, maximum likelihood, and bootstrapping, followed by morphological character mapping in MacClade.
- Vicariance analysis following [[Platnick and Nelson]] and [[Rosen]]. A broadly distributed ancestral fauna is assumed, possibly linked to the Eocene–Oligocene erosion surface.

**Deliverables:**

- (a) A paper on phylogeny and monophyly, including congruence with morphology.
- (b) A vicariance biogeography paper.
- (c) A taxonomic revision with formal descriptions of new species.
- (d) An informal report for land management agencies.

**Timetable:**

- Months 1–3 of year 1: protocol development for COI and COII.
- Months 4–12: COII sequenced for all taxa.
- Months 1–6 of year 2: COI sequenced.
- Final 3 months: analysis and manuscript preparation.
- Field collecting and fossil and literature review occur during the first 15 months.

**Personnel and effort:** Both investigators will devote about 33% of their time. Hershler will handle most fieldwork and morphology. Liu will handle laboratory work with one undergraduate assistant.
