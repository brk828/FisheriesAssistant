# Responses to Light and Microhabitat Selection in Mayfly Nymphs

#ephemeroptera #freshwater-ecology #stream-invertebrates

## Executive Summary

This 1966 behavioural study by D. A. Hughes tests whether the light responses of two mayfly nymphs, [[Baetis Harrisoni]] and [[Tricorythus Discolor]], help determine where they live within a stream. Field work showed that Baetis favours sunlit reaches and Tricorythus favours shaded reaches. Laboratory and outdoor [[Model Stream Experiments]] show that both species respond strongly to light and shade, through different mechanisms. Baetis is positively [[Phototaxis|phototactic]] and is also suppressed in activity by shade. Tricorythus shows a weak negative phototaxis but a strong [[Skototaxis]] toward dark objects. The author argues that these responses, combined with [[Orthokinesis]] and [[Rheotaxis]], are sufficient to explain the observed [[Microhabitat Selection]].

The file also contains a separate 1966 paper by Margaret A. Vince on potential stimulation between avian embryos. It concerns birds, not aquatic fauna, and is outside this note's scope.

## Background & Description

**Study organisms.** Both species are stream mayfly nymphs (order Ephemeroptera) collected in large numbers, 300-400 at a time, from nearby streams and rivers.

- *Baetis harrisoni* is described as agile and streamlined. It swims in short darts and also runs and walks. It occurs in faster current, mainly on the upper surfaces of stones.
- *Tricorythus discolor* is less motile. It never swims and moves only by walking over the substrate. It occurs in fast water, but only on the under-surfaces of stones.

Nymphs in flowing water always face upstream. The author cites Neave (1930), who suggested that this orientation is controlled by body shape rather than rheotaxis.

**Apparatus.** The main experimental stream was built from three 6 ft lengths of 6 in. diameter asbestos guttering, fed from a tap. Water speed was held at 30 cm/s and depth at 5-6 cm. An 18 ft outdoor stream was divided into three sections with white, black, and alternating black-and-white substrates. Light was set by shading, and intensities were recorded in foot-candles.

**Design.** The two species were always tested separately. Observations were mostly made between 11.00 and 14.00 hours, when sunlight was strongest and most uniform. Nymphs were allowed to settle for an hour or more before trials began.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author notes that light and shade are usually thought to affect stream fauna indirectly, through factors such as debris, algal growth, and temperature. The present work instead proposes that the distribution of these two mayflies is a direct function of their responses to light stimuli.

Field evidence came from an earlier survey (Hughes, in press). Harrison & Elsworth (1958) on the Berg River had also recorded more Baetis at an open station than at a shaded tributary at the same altitude.

The author also considers life-cycle links. Baetis (positively phototactic) emerged during the day, while Tricorythus (negatively phototactic) emerged at night or in late evening. Earlier literature on emergence timing supports a link between nymphal habitat and adult behaviour. Adult habitat choice and oviposition site choice could therefore reinforce nymphal distribution. Upstream flight by egg-bearing females (Roos, 1957) is noted as a further factor.

## Key Findings

1. **Strong light-related distribution in the artificial stream.** Under uniform light, nymphs aggregated at the upstream gauze partition. In tests with alternating sunlit (~8000 ft-candles) and shaded (~150 ft-candles) segments, both species avoided the light. Each trial used 100-200 individuals and counts were taken every 5-10 minutes.

2. **Incident light, not reflected light, is the main stimulus.** On a black substrate, the contrast in reflected light was largely removed, yet the photonegative distribution persisted. The author concludes that reflected light is at most a minor component. For Tricorythus, a black-and-white substrate gave a weaker and less clear pattern, but a tendency toward higher counts on black segments.

3. **Threshold-independent aggregation.** Tricorythus accumulated in shade even at low illumination (about 2000 ft-candles on open segments). Baetis showed little response below about 3500 ft-candles and accumulated less than Tricorythus.

4. **Strong orthokinetic response.** Locomotory activity was measured as the percentage of nymphs moving per 5-second period in each 10 cm segment. Activity almost stopped at low light and was very high at high light. The author describes this as sufficient to explain the shade aggregations, since nymphs slow down in shaded patches as they move upstream.

5. **Reflected light and activity.** Under 7500 ft-candles, activity on white and black substrates did not differ significantly for either species (Student's t-test). Baetis: 70.5% (white) vs 65% (black). Tricorythus: 64.7% (white) vs 58.8% (black).

6. **Under-stone aggregation.** In artificial stones under 100 ft-candles of shade, the percentage of nymphs under stones was:

| Species | Condition | White substrate | Black substrate |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Baetis harrisoni* | Sunlight | 83% | 87% |
| *Baetis harrisoni* | Shade | 42% | 36% |
| *Tricorythus discolor* | Sunlight | 92% | 95% |
| *Tricorythus discolor* | Shade | 86% | 73% |

   Baetis was less confined to under-stone sites than predicted by activity alone. The author therefore suggests that Tricorythus has an additional directional response to shade.

7. **Weak negative phototaxis in Tricorythus, strong skototaxis.** Within a light beam, Tricorythus showed only a weak negative response. When a black object was placed in the path, nymphs were strongly attracted to it. Under high light, 100% of late-stage, 92% of middle-stage, and 96% of early-stage nymphs made contact with a black object within 90 s. Under 150 ft-candles, only 7 late-stage, 4 middle-stage, and 5 early-stage nymphs out of 50 per group reached the object in 90 s, though most would eventually do so. The author names this response skototaxis, because it is a movement toward a defined dark area that cannot be explained by negative phototaxis alone.

8. **Baetis does not respond to black objects.** Only 8% of Baetis made contact with a black object, probably by chance.

9. **Positive phototaxis in Baetis.** In a light beam, most Baetis swam or crawled toward the source. In a trial of 100 nymphs per instar group, responses increased in strength with development. The ultimate instar responded rapidly and directly, with no somersaulting or hesitation. Somersaulting was attributed to a dorsal light response (Hughes, 1966). Some individuals kept a consistent negative response across repeated trials, while most reverted to positive.

10. **Illuminated stones attract Baetis.** Two equal stones at the inflow were compared, one sunlit and one shaded. Counts over ten readings gave 112 nymphs on the sunlit stone and 14 on the shaded one. On an illuminated stone, 17 of 20 individuals stayed longer than 1 minute, versus 3 of 20 on an equally lit area of substrate. Total time was over 125 minutes on the stone and 9 min 35 s on the substrate.

11. **Proposed mechanism.** Baetis is drawn into sunlit areas by positive phototaxis. The orthokinetic response there would normally disperse it, but current conditions near the stone surface can suppress that response and hold the nymphs in place. Tricorythus is guided under stones by the combined action of its orthokinetic response, weak negative phototaxis, and skototaxis.

12. **Limits of the conclusions.** The author states that the exact position of nymphs within the microhabitat is only partly explained. The current factor that suppresses orthokinesis in Baetis is undefined, and may relate to the boundary layer (Ambühl, 1959). Whether Baetis spends more time in sunlit than in shaded areas remains uncertain.
