# Public Comments on Proposed Critical Habitat for Colorado River Endangered Fishes (1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This is a January 10, 1994 comment letter from the [[Sierra Club Legal Defense Fund]] to the Utah State Supervisor of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] on the proposed rule designating [[Critical Habitat]] for four endangered fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Filed on behalf of six groups, the letter supports the proposal in principle but argues that it is legally and biologically deficient. Its central demands are that the final rule (1) specify water quantity, water quality and sediment through defined [[Flow Regime]] targets that approximate the natural hydrograph; (2) address predation and competition by [[Non-Native Fish]]; (3) protect the 100-year [[Floodplain]]; (4) add several river reaches; and (5) exclude no proposed habitat on biological or economic grounds.

## Background & Description

The letter argues that the four fishes are "on the verge of extinction." Its key background points are:

- **Listing history:** The species were listed for periods of 14 to 27 years, and the letter states that the humpback chub and Colorado squawfish have been declining for more than 25 years since listing. It also notes that the bonytail, humpback chub and squawfish are "even more imperilled" than at listing.
- **Status of the razorback sucker:** The letter quotes a federal district court that the razorback is "dangerously close to extinction" (*Colorado Wildlife Federation v. Turner*, 1992), and the Service's own description of it as "moving toward extinction" (BSD at 39).
- **Commenting coalition:** The Colorado Wildlife Federation, Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance, Four Corners Action Coalition, Colorado Environmental Coalition, Taxpayers for the Animas River and Sierra Club. The letter incorporates earlier comments by Drs. W.L. Minckley and Paul Marsh (Arizona State University) and Dr. Michael Kavanaugh.
- **Governing documents:** The comments refer to the Biological Support Document (BSD), the Draft Economic Analysis (Vol. II, Ch. 6), the original 1993 critical habitat proposal, and several biological opinions, including those for the [[Animas-La Plata Project]], the [[NIIP]] and the operation of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The letter frames the fishes' decline in ecological terms:

- **Flow dependence:** Flows cue spawning, provide nursery habitat, flood areas that supply nutrients, and influence the success of non-native competitors. The letter argues that daily base-flow fluctuations pose substantial risk to young and larval fish and to the food webs that sustain them.
- **Water quality and sediment:** Water must be of sufficient quality (temperature, dissolved oxygen, nutrients, turbidity). The letter calls for reservoir release structures that deliver water at appropriate temperatures, and for sediment inputs such as the [[Paria River]].
- **Fluctuating ecosystem:** The fishes evolved in a variable system, so critical habitat "need not be occupied year round or even annually" to be essential.
- **Fragmentation and genetics:** Habitat fragmentation and reduced genetic exchange are named as principal causes of decline. Isolated populations may hold unique genetic stock.
- **Upstream–downstream linkage:** Because water flows, excluding any reach would harm all downstream reaches.
- **Non-native species:** Predation and competition from introduced fish are described as well documented, and the letter cites studies of [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]] predation.

## Key Findings

**1. Flow and water quantity**
- The proposed rule does not specify water quantity, which the letter calls a mandatory constituent element of critical habitat under 50 C.F.R. § 424.12(b).
- The letter requests flows that approximate the natural, historic hydrograph: a spring peak, a gradual descent, and low stable flows through summer, fall and winter. It acknowledges the hydrograph varies between flood, drought and average years.
- It asks that flow levels developed for the economic analysis be used in the biological definition, and it cites the [[Concho Water Snake]] critical habitat as precedent for flow-based designation.
- It asks that daily base-flow fluctuations be prohibited.

**2. Non-native fish**
- The final rule should specifically eliminate predation and competition from non-native species, should not stock non-native fish, and should implement flows that favor endangered fishes over non-natives.

**3. Floodplains and wetlands**
- The letter agrees with the BSD that the 100-year floodplain is the appropriate area for critical habitat benefits, and it asks that lands within that boundary not be adversely modified.
- It asks the final rule to identify wetland and floodplain sites for acquisition or management, including the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]], and to provide flows that flood those sites and improve fish access to them.

**4. Additional reaches proposed for inclusion**
- **San Juan River:** extend upstream of [[Navajo Reservoir]] and include the reach from Navajo Dam to [[Hogback Diversion]], New Mexico.
- **Animas River:** extend up to the New Mexico border for squawfish and razorback sucker. The letter cites historic spawning (Jordan 1891) and says the Animas is now the largest source of water and a natural hydrograph to the San Juan.
- **Dolores River:** include based on a recent squawfish record near its mouth and past observations.
- **Humpback chub, Lower Basin:** extend upstream to [[Glen Canyon Dam]], include several miles of the Paria River (where an adult humpback chub was captured in March 1993), and extend the [[Little Colorado River]] to the Blue Spring outflow, 13 miles upstream from its mouth.
- **Razorback sucker, Lower Basin:** include [[Lake Havasu]], which the letter says supports a population of razorbacks. It cites the [[Lake Mohave]] populations as evidence of the value of lacustrine habitat.
- **Yampa system:** expand to include Cross Mountain Canyon and the Little Snake River, where a viable humpback chub population was identified (Tyus and Karp 1989).

**5. No exclusions**
- The letter argues that no segment should be excluded on biological grounds, because each remaining population is extremely important and the loss of any one population threatens the species as a whole. It cites the Animas-La Plata opinion that the San Juan is an essential buffer against extinction.
- On economics, it argues that impacts are within the margin of error of the data, so no measurable economic benefit exists to justify exclusion. It also contends that the [[Economic Analysis]] overestimates costs, ignores substitution and non-market values, and uses units (states, basin, nation) too coarse to capture flowing-water effects.
- It states that failing to give notice of specific exclusions would violate the [[Administrative Procedure Act]].
- Footnote 3 states that the [[Endangered Species Act]] does not allow economic exclusion for species listed before 1978, so no exclusions are legally permissible for the squawfish and humpback chub.

**6. Water quality**
- The BSD does not identify the specific factors that influence water quality. The letter asks the final rule to review the biological links to fish vitality, identify relevant actions, and specify remedial measures.

**7. Recovery and the Recovery Implementation Program**
- The letter says the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP) is not an appropriate forum for setting measures. It is riddled with conflicts of interest, has not shown significant recovery progress, and is geographically too limited. The RIP should instead implement measures in the final rule and expedite the RIPRAP.

**8. Existing projects and activities**
- Section 7 consultation with parties responsible for existing projects should begin immediately, because the RIP cannot offset impacts from existing and planned Upper Basin projects and does not address the Lower Basin or the San Juan.
- The final rule must summarize activities that may adversely modify critical habitat (16 U.S.C. § 1533(b)(8)).
