# Report of Investigations Concerning Shad in the Rivers of Connecticut

#ichthyology #marine-biology #clupeidae

## Executive Summary

This 1925 report from the [[State Board Of Fisheries And Game]] presents a multi-year investigation of [[Alosa Sapidissima]] (American shad) in the [[Connecticut River]] and its tributaries. Catches had collapsed from tens of thousands per year to record lows (13,350 in 1923). The investigation combined biological, chemical, and age studies to determine whether shad restoration was possible. Its main conclusion is that the rivers are generally suitable for shad migration and propagation, with pollution confined mainly to deep holes near [[Hartford]], [[Rocky Hill]] and [[Middletown]]. Decline is attributed mainly to dams, loss of spawning habitat, and overfishing, with artificial propagation identified as the necessary remedy.

## Background & Description

**Life history.** Shad are anadromous. Adults migrate from salt water into fresh water to spawn in fairly swift water over sandy or gravelly bottoms, then return to the sea. Fertilization relies on chance contact between eggs and sperm in the water, so reproduction depends on access to suitable spawning grounds.

**Embryo and larval stages.** Eggs were photographed from fertilization to hatching using material from the [[State Shad Hatchery]] at Windsor. Embryo movement appears when development is a little over half complete. Hatching takes five to nine days depending on temperature. The newly hatched larva (about 10 mm) carries a yolk sac that supplies nourishment for about three days, after which larvae must feed. Hatching rate rises with temperature, so development stage cannot be tied to elapsed time without controlled temperature.

**Historical catches.** Shad were once abundant. Recorded [[Connecticut River]] catches were:
- 1892: under 19,000 fish
- 1897–1906 average: 114,890 per year (maximum 176,085 in 1903)
- 1907–1916 average: 44,300 per year
- 1922 and 1923: 13,821 and 13,350, the lowest ever recorded for Connecticut
- 1924: 25,316

**Investigation staff and organization.** A conference in Hartford on April 28, 1923 planned the study. [[Philip H. Mitchell]] served as Director. Staff included [[Nicholas Borodin]] (Ichthyologist), Dr. [[Emmeline Moore]] (Biologist), and assistants for chemistry, bacteriology, plankton, and otolith work. Parts II–IV contain separate reports on scale age determination, otolith confirmation, and helminth parasites.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Barriers and habitat change.** Dams restricted spawning access, and the [[Fishways]] built at them failed to pass shad. Forest loss, dam-induced warming, and dredging at river mouths were also cited as possible contributors.

**Sewage and sludge.** Sewage and trade wastes deposited sludge in deep holes. These are the same spots where juvenile shad feed and where fertilized eggs collect. Sludge smothers eggs and, through fermentation, depletes oxygen and reduces the small invertebrates that young shad eat.

**Plankton and food base.** [[Plankton]] surveys across the [[Connecticut River]], [[Farmington River]], [[Salmon River]] and [[Scantic River]] found varied, healthy communities including crustacea, diatoms and green algae. Pollution forms ([[Oscillatoria]], [[Beggiatoa]], [[Tubifex]]) dominated only in deep, sludge-laden holes near Rocky Hill and Middletown. Quantitative counts (Sedgwick-Rafter method) ranged from about 64 to 704 organisms per cc, with the Farmington's low-water summer counts being poor and spring flood counts high.

**Dissolved oxygen.** The 110 Farmington River samples mostly showed 70–94% saturation. One sample at a sewer mouth read 35%, which the authors considered unrepresentative. In the Connecticut, values were low at South Glastonbury, Rocky Hill and Middletown. Fish can generally avoid low-oxygen channels because oxygen-poor water was confined to deep holes. Rooted aquatic plants raised oxygen by day through photosynthesis; on a sunny day, Farmington River oxygen rose from 6.6 ppm (75%) at 8 A.M. to 8.4 ppm (97%) at 2 P.M.

**pH.** Most sites were neutral to faintly alkaline. The Salmon River was slightly acid (pH 6.6–6.8 on a falling tide) from tannic and organic acids leached from woodland and swamp soils. The authors judged that pH posed no barrier to migration or propagation.

**Predators and competitors.** [[Pickerel]] stomachs (87 examined) contained no shad. However, when Joshuatown ponds were drained, pickerel in crowded water consumed shad (4 of 12 pickerel). Eels (27 examined) contained no shad, instead feeding mainly on minnows, insect larvae and crustacea, so they were considered competitors rather than major enemies.

## Key Findings

- **Decline driven by multiple factors.** Dams, reduced spawning areas, pollution, and overfishing combined to reduce shad runs. The fall from 1897–1906 (average 114,890) to 1907–1916 (average 44,300) is one key marker.
- **Water quality is mostly adequate.** Biological, chemical and pH data indicate conditions are favorable for shad migration and propagation across most of the river system, except for specific polluted deep holes.
- **Juvenile habitat is deep holes.** Young shad, even at larval stage, stay in holes 9–20 feet deep and seldom appear in shallow water. Seining of several hundred shallow-water hauls in 1923 found no larvae.
- **Diet.** Larval shad feed on [[Glochidia]] of river mussels, [[Chironomidae]] larvae, and small crustacea ([[Cypris]], [[Bosmina]], [[Cyclops]]). Little or no plant matter was found. Food intake rises fast: average organisms per larva went from 13 (12.5–16.5 mm) to 36 (17.5–21.5 mm), a food-capacity increase of nearly 200% accompanying roughly 85% body-length growth.
- **Growth.** Young shad may grow about one inch per month. The largest specimens (118, 98 and 98 mm) were taken in late August and September. The 74 river specimens taken between August 23 and September 15 averaged 60 mm, while pond fish averaged 35 mm.
- **Retaining ponds are overstocked.** Joshuatown ponds were stocked from 1896–1912 and again in 1923–1924. Pond fish were stunted and emaciated, and food was depleted within about six weeks. One pond stocked with 630,000 fry yielded an estimated 30,000 survivors (about 5%). The authors recommended either earlier release (six or seven weeks after metamorphosis) or much lower stocking densities.
- **Enemies and parasites are not serious.** Of 65 adult shad examined, only 2 were free of parasites, yet parasitized fish appeared healthy. The authors judged natural enemies to pose no serious menace.
- **Age structure (Borodin scale method).** Of about 250 shad examined, 80% were five to eight years old and the youngest adults were four years old. Females were at least seven years old, and eight-year-old roe were most numerous. Scale transverse grooves divided by two give age.
- **Otolith confirmation (Barney).** A fish of about 14 cm, estimated at one year two months, had a single otolith annulus at 0.8 mm. Fish aged three, four, five, six, seven, nine and ten years showed matching annuli, with the first and fourth annuli serving as reliable landmarks (0.8–0.9 mm and 1.5–1.7 mm from the nucleus). This supports the scale method.
- **Parasites identified.** Twelve immature and one adult [[Ascaris Adunca]] (roundworms), and larval cestodes identified as [[Scolex Polymorphus]], were recorded from shad.
- **Artificial propagation is necessary.** Natural reproduction is insufficient, so artificial propagation must be increased. Fry planted in 1896–1898 (about 37.6 million, averaging over 12 million per year) were followed by catches above 100,000 during 1900–1905. Correspondence between fry and catches is rough, and overfishing, pond losses and fishing intensity complicate the link. The 1924 propagation effort (3,751,000 fry) was the largest in twenty years.
- **Recommendations.** The State Board recommended measures to: (1) help adults reach the best spawning areas; (2) favor natural spawning; (3) avoid set nets that prevent conservation of ripe spawn; (4) secure fishermen's cooperation in spawn-taking; and (5) increase artificial propagation. The authors stated that shad could recover if overfishing is restricted and pollution does not worsen.
