# Cumulative Effects of Research-Induced Stress on the Little Colorado River Humpback Chub

#ichthyology #marine-biology #humpback-chub

## Executive Summary

This document is a July 1992 Bureau of Reclamation memorandum from the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program, with a meeting notice for the [[Aquatic Coordination Team]] (ACT). The memo assesses whether the combined sampling, handling, tagging and surgery by several research groups might be harming the native fish of the [[Little Colorado River]] and the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon]]. The focus is the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Data come from first-year annual reports, not peer-reviewed work, so the authors label the analysis provisional. They find that incidental mortality stayed within permitted limits for every group, but that humpback chub condition declined and the causes remain unexplained. They recommend better cumulative tracking, shared reporting, and a review of radio telemetry and otolith aging work.

## Background & Description

- **Trigger:** The [[National Park Service]], the Navajo Nation and other entities had raised concern at past ACT meetings about the number of researchers and their combined effect on native fish.
- **Objectives:** (1) outline the scope of endangered fish research and its effects; (2) identify areas of concern; (3) recommend actions for the ACT.
- **Concern metrics:** incidental mortality, total fish handled, recaptures of previously handled fish, and fish taken for experimental or reproductive (propagation) purposes.
- **Native species noted:** [[Humpback Chub]] is the focal species. The memo also mentions the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), a category 2 species, and the [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Pantosteus discobolus*). Both are native fish in need of life-history research, and the memo recommends that work begin soon.
- **Permitting:** Incidental take limits are set by the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) under research permits. Research groups are coordinated through the ACT.
- **Meeting notice:** The ACT was scheduled for 28 July 1992 in Flagstaff. Agenda items included research updates, expansion below Diamond Creek through the [[Hualapai Tribe]], [[Little Colorado River]] mapping, the stress memorandum, report content, and PIT tag needs for the next year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Research groups and effort (first-year data, 1991 to early 1992):**

| Group | Area | Effort | Humpback Chub Handled |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Arizona State University]] (ASU) | Little Colorado River | 10 trips, 99 field days (through Jan 1992) | 8,432 (of 10,144 total fish) |
| [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) | Little Colorado River | 80 research days | 2,025 (of 5,386 post-larval fish) |
| USFWS | Little Colorado River | 6 trips, 61 days | Most of about 1,4xx fish (count garbled in scan) |
| AGFD | Mainstem Colorado River | 5 trips, 92 days | 111 (pre-adults under 150 mm) |
| [[Bio/West Inc]] | Mainstem Colorado River | 14 trips, about 210 days | 964 (77 juveniles, 506 adults) |

**Field methods:**
- **Capture:** hoop nets, trammel nets, weirs, minnow traps, seines, drift nets, dip nets and [[Electrofishing]] (Coffelt systems). Nets were checked on 2- to 12-hour cycles.
- **Marking:** [[PIT Tagging]] for fish over about 100 to 150 mm; fin clips for smaller natives; [[Radio Telemetry]] for the largest adults.
- **Anesthesia:** Used only during surgery under protocol, not for routine handling.

**Environmental and biological factors noted:**
- A December 1991 night flood sedimented fish held in nets, causing three ASU mortalities.
- Bio/West reported a general decline in humpback chub weight averaging about 9% over the year. Possible causes include normal population fluctuation, effects of interim flows on food availability, or infection by the [[Asiatic Tapeworm]]. Tapeworm infestation level has not been quantified, though it is presumed present.
- Electrofishing effects are generally short-lived for small fish; larger fish such as trout showed extended narcosis.

## Key Findings

**Incidental mortality stayed within permitted limits.**
- ASU: under 0.1% of humpback chub handled. Permits allowed up to 35 mortalities. Four died in a 2.5 cm mesh trammel net, since replaced with 3.4 cm mesh, and three in the December flood. Twenty expected weir mortalities did not occur, and weirs were discontinued for lack of success.
- Bio/West: four mortalities (about 0.4%) against a USFWS limit of 10.
- AGFD mainstem: no incidental mortalities reported; seven chubs under 100 mm were taken for aging, within the USFWS limit of 100.
- Total: 8 humpback chub incidental mortalities reported across all research, under 0.1% of chubs handled.

**Fish taken for propagation or study:**
- AGFD removed 15 adult humpback chub from the Little Colorado River for the [[Bubbling Springs Hatchery]]. All 15 died there and were reported as incidental mortalities.
- Growth and aging studies took about 100 chubs under 99 mm, 42 over 100 mm, and 127 larvae.

**Recapture rates were high.**
- ASU: 1,464 recaptures (recapture rate garbled in scan), with a substantial share previously PIT-tagged by ASU and 43 [[Carlin Tags]] or Floy tags from earlier researchers.
- AGFD Little Colorado River: 512 chubs PIT-tagged; 166 recaptured (32.42%); 62 of those (37.32%) carried tags from other researchers.
- Across all four GCES groups: 12,115 humpback chub handled; 1,824 were recaptures; 1,165 (9.6%) were recaptured by the same group that originally tagged them; 559 carried tags from earlier research or from a different group.

**Radio telemetry.**
- 53 adult chubs were implanted with radio transmitters (about 11 g; under 2% of body weight; minimum 550 g body weight). This represents 82% of the implants allowed by USFWS for the first year, and 227 eligible fish were captured.
- 14 radio-tagged fish were recaptured and examined for suture healing and general health. Surgical protocols and materials were changed in response.
- Implanted fish showed normal behavior and returned to spawning areas in the Little Colorado River, with no displacement from capture sites. Bio/West reduced implants from ten to five per trip because recontact rates were higher than expected.

**Electrofishing effects.**
- Amperage was first set at 15 to 20 amps and was lowered to 12 amps after burn marks appeared on rainbow trout. Efficient capture of target small chubs continued at the lower setting.
- Three of 340 captured chubs showed extended effects; all but one recovered and were released. One was recaptured several miles downstream the next day and never recovered.

**Concerns and open questions.**
- Humpback chub condition factor declined on the mainstem over the year. The cause was not explained.
- Population estimates were unreliable, based on one year or less of data. ASU did not attempt an estimate in its first report, and Bio/West's estimates ranged from 919 to 6,564 (95% CI, September).
- USFWS recapture data for its first year are unusable because scanning for previous PIT tags did not start until December 1991.

**Recommendations to the ACT.**
1. Compile cumulative humpback chub handling and mortality annually and share it with permitting agencies and cooperating researchers before permit renewal, while leaving enough time for annual reports.
2. Require USFWS permit holders to file monthly reports, distributed to ACT members, to support decisions and information exchange. Quarterly reports should be reviewed at ACT meetings.
3. Examine the status of the otolith growth and aging project. Set a completion date and decide whether more fish should be taken for it.
4. Review the radio telemetry program in conjunction with the next annual report. Evaluate whether the data being collected are needed and whether the program can be modified to fill data gaps with less effect on the chub.
5. Study the life history and ecology of the flannelmouth and bluehead suckers soon, before those populations decline further, and coordinate sampling to reduce duplicated stress.
6. Quantify the degree of Asiatic tapeworm infestation in the Grand Canyon humpback chub population.

**Governance note:** The memo states that GCES has no jurisdiction over changes to permit limits, which rest with USFWS, the National Park Service and the Navajo Nation. The memo is intended only as an aid to those agencies, and the authors call their analysis provisional.
