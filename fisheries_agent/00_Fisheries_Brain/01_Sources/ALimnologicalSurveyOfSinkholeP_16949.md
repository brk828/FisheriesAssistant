# A Limnological Survey of Sinkhole Ponds Near Doe Run, Meade County, Kentucky

#limnology #marine-biology #plankton

## Executive Summary

This 1960–1961 limnological survey by [[Geiling]] and [[Krumholz]] (Department of Biology, University of Louisville) characterizes shallow [[Sinkhole Ponds]] in the karst landscape around [[Doe Run]], Meade County, Kentucky. The study had two aims: a limnological reconnaissance of an unstudied area, and a comparison of sinkhole pond (lentic) waters with the spring-fed stream Doe Run (lotic). Two ponds (Stations A and B) were sampled 22 times each, and 93 further ponds were sampled once. The ponds are small, shallow, eutrophic and alkaline, and their plankton is dominated seasonally by dinoflagellates, rotifers, cladocerans and copepods. Pond chemistry differs sharply from Doe Run, mainly in alkalinity, and the authors found little correlation between water chemistry and plankton occurrence.

## Background & Description

**Study area.** The study area is a rectangle of about 8.4 by 5.5 miles, bounded by the Doe Run drainage basin (east and west), the [[Ohio River]] (north) and U.S. Highway 60 (south). Doe Run falls 9.7 miles from 575 ft to 374 ft above mean sea level. The area is entirely [[Karst Hydrology|karstic]], and all main tributaries of Doe Run arise from caves or springs. Roughly 75% of the land is cleared; the remainder is western mixed mesophytic forest.

**Geology and climate.** The area is underlain by upper [[St. Louis Limestone]] of Mississippian age, about 400 ft thick in the study area and more than 90% calcium carbonate. Joint and bedding planes favor [[Sinkhole Formation]]. Mean annual rainfall is 41.5 inches, mean annual temperature 56.5 °F and mean annual relative humidity 79%. Soils are iron-rich and orange-red, and are deficient in lime.

**Sinkhole ponds.** Sinks were classed as simple or compound (after Scott, 1909). Most ponds occupy simple sinks on cleared farmland, where erosion carries silt into the depression and plugs it, cutting the pond off from groundwater. Ponds are small and shallow: the maximum size is under 3 acres and the maximum depth 8 ft. About 80% are under 1 acre and under 5 ft deep, and more than a third are under 2 ft deep. Siltation is rapid; one pond fell from 8 ft to 4 ft in 24 years.

**Focal stations.**
- **Station A:** a simple sinkhole of about 2.0 acres, maximum depth 7 ft 11 in, in pasture used by hogs and sheep. It has a firm clay-mud bottom and sparse vascular flora. It is judged relatively young geologically.
- **Station B:** a compound pond of about 2.3 acres, maximum depth 3 ft 6 in, much shallower and more eutrophic. It has a rich submerged flora, dominated by [[Ceratophyllum demersum]], and a thick black ooze layer. Its catchment was plowed and planted to corn in March 1961.

**Methods (summary).** Physical and chemical variables included depth, temperature, [[Turbidity]], pH, [[Alkalinity]], free carbon dioxide, total iron, nitrate and nitrite nitrogen, and phosphate phosphorus. Dissolved oxygen was measured by the Winkler method. Plankton was collected with a No. 25 silk net in single vertical tows, preserved in four per cent formalin, concentrated by centrifuge and counted in a Sedgwick-Rafter chamber.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pond hydrology.** Ponds are cut off from groundwater and their levels track precipitation. Evaporation was the main water loss in a comparable pond studied by [[White]] (1930). Residents reported that some long-standing ponds drained suddenly during heavy rain, when the plug was removed. In spring 1961, heavy rain raised levels at Stations A and B by more than two feet, and the maximum depth of Station B increased by almost 66%.

**Temperature and stratification.** Water temperatures closely followed air temperatures. Stratification was temporary and occurred only on hot sunny days, because the ponds are too shallow for thermoclines. On August 15, 1961, Station A showed a 4 °C difference between surface and bottom at seven feet. Ice covered the stations for about a week in winter 1960–1961.

**Turbidity and iron.** Turbidity peaked during heavy rain and was raised by livestock. Station A ranged from 95 turbidity units to 4; Station B from 188 to 2. Across the 93 survey ponds, values ranged from 594 to 6. Total iron was positively correlated with turbidity, both at the stations and across the survey ponds.

**Carbonate chemistry.** Dissolved oxygen was highest in winter and lowest in summer, and it showed diel cycles (maxima late afternoon, minima before dawn). Free carbon dioxide reached 219.9 mg/L at Station B on December 10, 1960, under low temperature, low pH and high alkalinity. Photosynthesis drove pH up to 8.7 in late summer at the stations, and to 10.4 in Pond 29 during an [[Anabaena Spiroides]] bloom.

**Nutrients.** Phosphate was undetectable in 24 of the 93 survey ponds. Pond 79, in a barnyard, had 0.84 mg/L. At Station A, surface phosphate on July 27 rose from 0.01 mg/L at 6 PM to 1.40 mg/L 18 hours later. Nitrite exceeded nitrate in nine survey ponds, though nitrite concentrations were generally below 0.1 mg/L. Station B's nitrate stayed low, probably because of its abundant vascular flora.

**Comparison with Doe Run.** Doe Run's source water had an average alkalinity of about 230 mg/L at low flow, compared with a modal value of about 50 mg/L in the sinkhole ponds. Phosphate and nitrate were much higher in Doe Run (Minckley, 1962). Only three typical pond plankters (*Difflugia lobostoma*, *Mesocyclops edax* and *Keratella cochlearis*) were found in Doe Run, in very small numbers, and were thought to have been washed in from ponds during heavy rain. Man-made and modified ponds, used for sport fishing and sometimes fertilized, had chemistry similar to natural ponds.

## Key Findings

**Survey scope.** About 95 ponds were sampled between October 1960 and December 1961. Of the 93 survey ponds, 4 were man-made, 3 were modified natural basins and 86 were natural sinkholes. More than 140 plankton taxa were identified. Station A and B data were collected 22 times each over one year.

**Phytoplankton.**
- **Chlorophyta:** more than 40 species, 20 of them desmids. *Cosmarium reniforme* and *Cosmarium* spp. dominated at Station B.
- **Chrysophyta:** 14 taxa, nine of them diatoms. *Fragilaria crotonensis* produced a spring bloom at Station A (3,200 organisms per foot of tow). *Botryococcus* spp. appeared in late summer and autumn.
- **Pyrrophyta:** only two species, but they dominated net plankton from midspring to late autumn. [[Ceratium Hirundinella]] was present in 52 of 81 sampled ponds and [[Peridinium Cinctum]] in 48.
- **Cyanophyta:** 13 taxa, not as abundant as expected for eutrophic waters. [[Microcystis Aeruginosa]] was abundant in 4 of 16 ponds where it occurred, mainly late summer and autumn.

**Zooplankton.**
- **Rotifera:** 21 taxa, the most diverse group. [[Keratella Cochlearis]] was the most abundant zooplankter, occurring in 76 of 81 ponds. *Kellicottia bostoniensis* exceeded 9,000 individuals per foot of tow on June 21 at Station A.
- **Cladocera:** 19 taxa. [[Bosmina Longirostris]] reached more than 2,000 individuals per foot of tow by mid-October and was present in 31 of 81 ponds. [[Daphnia Ambigua]] was a vernal species, peaking in mid-March, and it occurred with Bosmina in only three ponds.
- **Copepoda:** 11 species (five calanoid, six cyclopoid). [[Diaptomus Pallidus]] was the most common copepod, present in 57 survey ponds. *Mesocyclops edax* had two generations in the ice-free season.
- **Anostraca:** [[Streptocephalus Seali]] (spiny-tailed fairy shrimp) was found at Pond 31 and in great numbers at Pond 34, which the authors thought probably lacked fish.
- **Protozoa and other groups:** one ciliate (*Codonella cratera*), two rhizopods (including *Difflugia lobostoma*), and an unidentified nematode.

**Seasonality.** The authors arranged species into four groups:
1. Present year-round: *Codonella cratera*, *Kellicottia bostoniensis*, *Keratella cochlearis*, *Polyarthra* sp., *Bosmina longirostris*, *Daphnia ambigua*, *Diaptomus pallidus*.
2. Absent in only one season (mostly winter): *Ceratium hirundinella*, *Peridinium cinctum*, *Phacus longicauda*, *Brachionus havanaensis*, *Filinia longiseta*.
3. Present in only one or two seasons (mostly summer and autumn): for example *Eudorina elegans*, *Microcystis aeruginosa*, *Anabaena spiroides*, *Diaphanosoma brachyurum*.
4. Scattered throughout the year: *Closterium turgidum*, *Cosmarium* spp., *Dinobryon cylindricum*, *Cyclops vernalis*.

Species richness and abundance were highest in summer and fell in winter. The authors reported little correlation between plankton occurrence and water chemistry, and considered most fluctuations unexplained.

**New state records for Kentucky.** Eight taxa are reported as new records: *Daphnia ambigua*, *D. laevis*, *D. parvula*, *Alona karua*, *Diaphanosoma leuchtenbergianum*, *Diaptomus leptopus*, *D. birgei* and *Mesocyclops tenuis*. The *M. tenuis* record extends the known range by more than 1,000 miles.

**Conclusions.** Sinkhole ponds in Meade County are shallow, alkaline and eutrophic, and have plankton communities characterized by *Cosmarium* spp., *Ceratium hirundinella*, *Peridinium cinctum*, several rotifers, *Bosmina longirostris*, *Daphnia ambigua* and *Diaptomus pallidus*. Cold-water forms such as *Synchaeta* were absent, and diatoms were rare. Ponds differ strongly from Doe Run, mainly in alkalinity, and cannot be readily linked to it by chemistry or biota.
