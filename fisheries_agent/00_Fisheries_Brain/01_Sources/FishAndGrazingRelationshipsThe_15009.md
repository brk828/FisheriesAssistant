# Fish and Grazing Relationships: The Facts and Some Pleas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[John N. Rinne]] of the [[Rocky Mountain Research Station]] examines the relationships between [[Livestock Grazing]] and fishes in the western and southwestern United States. The author finds that the direct effects of grazing on vegetation are well documented, but the indirect effects on fishes and their habitats are poorly studied. Most available literature is not scientifically derived and addresses only salmonids and domestic livestock. Rinne argues that [[Cypriniform Fishes]] and large wild ungulates, especially elk, must be included in future [[Fish-Grazing Research]] and management. He calls for [[Adaptive Management]], interagency partnerships, and data collection in place of opinion and repeated literature summaries.

## Background & Description

Livestock grazing has shaped southwestern landscapes for centuries. Cattle and sheep were widespread in Arizona and New Mexico by the time the [[U.S. Forest Service]] was established in 1905. Several laws, including the 1897 Organic Act, the 1934 Taylor Grazing Act, the Multiple Use and Sustained Yield Act of 1960, the National Forest Management Act of 1974 and the National Forest Renewable Resources Research Act of 1978, shaped management and research on public lands.

Native fish diversity in the Southwest is low. Fewer than 60 fish species are native to the region, compared with the eastern United States. Nonnative introductions began around the turn of the century, mainly for sportfishing. More than 100 nonnative species have been introduced into Arizona alone, and almost 50 have become permanently established. Damming, irrigation and groundwater pumping have also reduced native fish ranges and numbers. Most native southwestern fishes are now federal- or state-listed.

The paper has four stated aims: to summarize general knowledge of fish, habitat and grazing relationships; to discuss them for the Southwest; to outline how Forest Service research and management should interact; and to call for cooperative partnerships and integrated monitoring and research.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Literature base.** Most grazing-fish research comes from the northern Rocky Mountains, Intermountain and Pacific Northwest regions. [[William S. Platts]] was the principal researcher in the 1980s, and his 1991 review on salmonids is the key synthesis. Platts concluded that grazing had harmed fishes and habitats, but also that controversy persists because valid evaluations of grazing strategies are lacking.

**Riparian linkages.** Riparian-stream areas are indicators of watershed condition. In the arid Southwest, isolated riparian and aquatic areas are critical fish habitat. Water quantity and quality determine where fishes can survive. Because grazing affects vegetation, streambanks, channels, instream substrate and water quality, it can influence fishes indirectly and in complex, nonlinear ways.

**Study sites.** Rinne's own case studies include:
- [[Rio de las Vacas]], northern New Mexico (Santa Fe National Forest): montane stream with grazing exclosures in place for 10 years before study.
- [[West Fork Allotment Study]], Apache-Sitgreaves National Forest, Arizona: three montane streams, initiated after a Biological Opinion on the threatened [[Apache Trout]].
- [[Verde River]], Prescott National Forest, Arizona: upper reach with six native fishes, including the threatened [[Spikedace]], and an equal number of common nonnative species.

## Key Findings

**Literature review of grazing-riparian-fish studies**

- Rinne reviewed almost 200 references on riparian areas, grazing and fish. About two-thirds addressed riparian ecosystem components (channel, instream structure, vegetation, streambanks), and only one-third (66 papers) included fish.
- Of the 66 fish papers, 47 (72%) addressed salmonids and 19 (28%) addressed nonsalmonids.
- Ten papers (15%) were general, often hypothetical, treatments of fish effects. Eleven (17%) were opinion, summary or review papers presenting no new data.
- Only eight papers came from the Southwest, and only half of these were data-based.
- Platts (1991) reported that 15 of 21 studies indicated grazing harmed fish populations. Rinne's review adds 151 further documents. Platts's 1982 review reportedly found that 35 of the same 21 papers reported negative fish impacts. That figure exceeds the 21 papers, so it is likely an OCR error for 15.

**Design weaknesses identified**

- **Pre-treatment data:** Ideally, several years of baseline data are needed. Such data are rarely collected because grazing plans and fencing changes are not foreseen. Knapp and Matthews (1996) could not obtain it because of a remote study area.
- **Controls:** Linear treatment-control arrangements along one stream are not independent, since upstream and downstream reaches influence each other. Water quality flows across boundaries, and fish movement cannot be fully bounded. No paired-watershed design with pre-treatment data had been achieved. The West Fork study partly addresses this, but elk could still reach the upper headwaters.
- **Temporal replication:** Platts (1978) recommended at least three years of replication to define variability. Among the 10 multi-year studies, only a second year of sampling was normally done. Pseudoreplication is also a risk ([[Hurlburt 1984]]).
- **Data type:** Measured quantitative data are more repeatable than qualitative classifications of substrate or bank stability.
- **Statistics:** Many studies reported differences such as "percent greater fish numbers or biomass" inside exclosures without temporal variability or statistical testing. Expression of fish response also varies with species, numbers versus biomass, and units of area versus stream length.
- **Peer review:** The author argues that research should undergo peer review and statistical scrutiny, because flawed unreviewed studies can harm both the grazing industry and fish.

**Confounding factors identified from Southwestern case studies**

Rinne lists five factors that can dramatically change conclusions (in no particular order):
1. Fish species (salmonid versus nonsalmonid).
2. Temporal and spatial variation in fish populations. Year-to-year variation is rarely addressed, and single-year snapshots are common.
3. Habitat influences. Gradient, substrate, velocity and channel type affect community structure. At the Rio de las Vacas, grazed reaches lay more than 4 km downstream of exclosures and differed in channel type. [[Rio Grande Sucker]] habitat occurs in lower-gradient (under 3%) pool-and-glide reaches.
4. Fishery management influences, such as the Arizona Game and Fish Department's practice of managing watersheds for either sport or native species. Most Arizona streams contain both.
5. Natural factors such as geology and historic and current flow regimes. Southwestern fish communities are dynamic and often difficult to separate from land-use effects.

**Research needs**

Rinne identifies three requirements for valid grazing-fish research: (1) defining linkages between grazing, habitat and fish; (2) cooperative management and research; and (3) collecting scientific data rather than reiterating reviews and opinions. He stresses that linkages are likely complex and nonlinear. Vegetation responses are well documented, but fish population responses have not been adequately studied.

**Cooperative models**

- The six-year West Fork ungulate study involved Forest Service personnel, the grazing permittee, the Arizona Game and Fish Department (which funded elk-proof and standard fencing) and the Rocky Mountain Research Station, which designed the study and analyzed the data. Analyses at years two and four led to management adjustments.
- The 1998 [[Upper Verde River Adaptive Management Program]] brings together the Forest Service, grazing permittees, environmental and public interests, the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. It aims to coordinate research, management, monitoring and two-year adaptive changes.

**Monitoring and funding**

- Monitoring of land-use activities is "woefully lacking" because there is no budget line item for it, unlike research. Legislation requires research on the effects of management and the collection of scientific information, but the funding is not provided. By law, management funds cannot be used for research, or the reverse.
- Monitoring and research both collect resource data and can be combined. Nearly 17,000 fish were collected at 7 sites over the upper 60 km of the Verde River over five years, and the Rio de las Vacas research produced a fish population database after about six years.

**Closing conclusions**

- The analysis shows a lack of reliable data but does not show that grazing has no effect on fishes.
- Little new data are being collected, while literature is repeatedly restated. The author calls on managers, researchers and environmental groups to stop promoting opinion and litigating, and to collect defensible data from well-designed research and monitoring. As he puts it, "Without data, one is just another person with an opinion."
