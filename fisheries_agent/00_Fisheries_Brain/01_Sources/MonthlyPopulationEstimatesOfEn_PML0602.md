# Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) Population Dynamics and Distribution in Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gila-cypha

## Executive Summary

This document contains two conference abstracts from the Proceedings of the Desert Fishes Council, both on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*[[Gila Cypha]]*) in Grand Canyon, Arizona. The first reports seasonal movement and population estimates for chubs in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) based on monthly mark-recapture sampling from July 1991 onward. The second reports the present mainstem distribution of the species in the [[Colorado River]] through [[Grand Canyon]], compared with its historic range. A third, partly illegible fragment appears to describe effects of electrofishing on fish. Abstracts are bilingual (English and Spanish).

## Background & Description

*Gila cypha* is described in the abstracts as a species with a restricted distribution, confined to narrow stretches of canyon rapids in the Colorado River and its tributaries. Its greatest abundance occurs in the lower basin, where it uses the [[Little Colorado River]], a tributary entering the mainstem in northern Grand Canyon, for spring spawning and summer residence. The authors note that the timing of its reproductive movements and its seasonal variation in population density had not been quantified, which they describe as an obstacle to managing this endangered species.

Study work was carried out by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] field office in Flagstaff, Arizona. The abstracts list the author team but the author names are not fully legible in the scan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Sampling areas were defined by river mile (RM) and by side-canyon reaches. The mainstem survey covered RM 56 to 226, and the LCR study covered three reaches upstream of the confluence:

- **[[Confluence Reach]]:** 0.0 to 1.4 km upstream of the confluence.
- **[[Powell Canyon]]:** 1.5 to 7.0 km upstream, within the Navajo Indian Reservation.
- **[[Salt Canyon]]:** 8.0 to 15.0 km upstream, within the Navajo Indian Reservation.

Mainstem concentrations were associated with tributary inflows and canyon features, including [[Bright Angel Creek]] (RM 87-88), [[Shinumo Creek]] (RM 108-109), the area above Blacktail Canyon (RM 119-120), Middle Granite Gorge (RM 126-127), [[Havasu Creek]] (RM 155-157), and the area near [[Pumpkin Spring]] (RM 213-214).

## Key Findings

**Seasonal movement and population dynamics (LCR study)**
- Fish were trapped and seined at monthly intervals from July 1991 to the time of writing, and were marked and given standard monitoring data.
- Population estimates were derived from tag-recapture data by selecting the best-fitting of seven models in the [[CAPTURE Program]]. Each reach was treated as a closed population, an assumption justified by the short 6-8 day monthly sampling periods.
- During 1992, density at the confluence peaked in early March, when adults staged for upriver movement, and fell by May.
- At Powell Canyon, density peaked in late March and declined gradually through July.
- At Salt Canyon, density peaked in April and stayed high through the summer months.
- The authors conclude that chubs migrate into the LCR in spring and remain in upper reaches through summer.

**Present mainstem distribution**
- Of 1,392 humpback chubs captured in the mainstem Colorado River in Grand Canyon from October 1990 to June 1992, 95% (1,324 fish) were found in a single 13-km reach between RM 57 and RM 65. This reach lies roughly equidistant upstream and downstream of the LCR confluence (RM 61.3). This concentration is the study's main result.
- Bimonthly sampling between RM 56 and RM 226 showed that chubs were distributed from RM 57.0 to RM 221.8.
- The present distribution is compared with historic records in the paper.

**Electrofishing and fish condition (partly legible)**
- A fragmentary abstract, which appears to describe pulsed direct-current electrofishing in the Colorado River, reports necropsy and X-ray examination of fish. Fin damage was reported in about 5% of fish, and hemorrhages were noted. The text is too degraded to confirm the methods or sample sizes, so these details should be checked against the original source.

**Management implications**
- The authors state that understanding the timing of spawning movements and seasonal density is essential for managing this endangered species. The seasonal LCR data and the mainstem distribution data are presented as the basis for that work.
