# Status of the Zuni Mountain Sucker

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1979 report, transmitted by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) Albuquerque office to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under a Memorandum of Agreement, reviews the taxonomic status, distribution, ecology and conservation needs of the Zuni mountain sucker (*Catostomus discobolus yarrowi*). Authors G. R. Smith and R. K. Koehn recommend that the Zuni form be classified as a subspecies of the bluehead mountain sucker, based on [[Multivariate Morphological Analysis]] and [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]]. The populations are interpreted as a stable, true-breeding combination of *C. discobolus* and *C. plebeius* characters, likely produced by hybridization following an ancient [[Stream Capture]]. The report argues the subspecies is scientifically significant and recommends protecting it when evaluating proposed dams in the Zuni watershed.

## Background & Description

The memorandum (August 15, 1979) accompanies a report that is the second of three FWS products on the species. The first, "Fishery Management Activities Affecting the Zuni Mountain Sucker," was distributed in July; a third, on distribution of the Zuni mountain sucker and other fishes within the Zuni River drainage, was in preparation. Copies were sent to state and tribal wildlife agencies, the [[Cibola National Forest]], and FWS offices, along with two specialists.

The fish was first described by E. D. Cope in 1874 as *Minomus jarrovii* from the Zuni River headwaters, based on specimens with nine dorsal rays. Cope gave the type locality as Provo, Utah, which Cope and Yarrow corrected in 1875 to the Zuni River, New Mexico. The types (USNM 15783) were collected by H. W. Henshaw in 1873. Smith (1963, 1965) found New Mexico populations resembled *C. discobolus* in gill raker counts but *C. plebeius* in vertebrae, dorsal rays, jaw shape and pigment. The native range is restricted to the upper Zuni drainage (Nutria Creek, Rio Pescado) and waters of the Little Colorado River system, which are threatened by possible reservoir inundation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and ecology.** Most fish were found in late-summer pools of small, permanent, high-gradient streams, usually with hard substrate (larger than sand) present or nearby. Riffles are probably needed for spawning, which likely occurs in spring. Stomach contents of 54 October-collected individuals showed a sparse diet of particulate organic matter, detritus, diatoms, filamentous algae, midge, caddisfly and mayfly larvae, flatworms, occasional terrestrial insects, fish scales, gastropods and insect eggs (see [[Stomach Content Analysis]]). Growth was good in Rio Pescado, intermediate in Nutria Creek and poor in Radosevich Creek. Age, growth and maturity analyses were incomplete because scale annuli were hard to read.

**Threats.** Proposed impoundments were the main concern. The Yellowstone dam site would inundate most of the Rio Pescado habitat, while the alternative Coal Mine Dam and Burned Dam sites were judged to have minimal effect on known populations. Siltation and exotic species competition are also identified threats.

**Sampling sites.** Samples came from Nutria Creek, Rio Pescado, Radosevich Creek, Kin-li-Chee Creek, East Clear Creek, Whiskey Creek (*C. discobolus* comparison) and Wells Spring (*C. plebeius* comparison).

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic conclusions**
- *C. d. yarrowi* is intermediate between *C. plebeius* and *C. discobolus* in morphology and biochemistry. It is diagnosed morphologically by usually 25 or more first-row gill rakers, 35 or more back-row rakers, more than 50 predorsal scales, more than 40 post-Weberian vertebrae, and mean dorsal rays below 10.
- Shared discobolus-type alleles at [[N-Acetylglucosaminidase]], [[Malate Dehydrogenase]]-A1 and A2 separate it from plebeius. The report describes this as the strongest support for subspecies status.
- Upper Zuni populations are the extreme forms, and downstream populations are intergrades. The clinal pattern is why species-level diagnosis was not recommended.

**Morphological analysis**
- 247 specimens were analyzed using principal component and canonical axes analyses.
- Component I explained 56% of variance; Component II explained an additional 18%.
- The Nutria population overlapped broadly with plebeius, supporting partial plebeius ancestry in Nutria Creek headwaters.

**Biochemical analysis**
- Of about 35 loci, 18 were monomorphic and identical across populations.
- Three loci gave exclusive evidence linking yarrowi with discobolus.
- Five loci (including [[Malate Dehydrogenase]]-M, Superoxide Dismutase-1 and Lactate Dehydrogenase-B2) indicated plebeius-derived alleles restricted to Nutria Creek, consistent with hybridization there.
- Four loci gave equivocal evidence, and four showed local differentiation unrelated to the hybridization history.

**Stability and origin**
- Morphology matched 1873 type material and 1948, 1960 and 1978 collections in most characters, supporting long-term stability.
- The Little Colorado barrier falls appear to block further upstream introgression of discobolus genes.
- Kin-li-Chee Creek, about 250 miles by water from Nutria Creek, shows only trace introgression.

**Recommendations**
- Protect the Zuni populations, since the subspecies is scientifically significant as a record of hybridization and gene flow over several thousand years.
- Ensure a permanent minimum discharge of at least 1 c.f.s. through the protected stream reach, with temperature and oxygen levels suited to native fish.
- Control siltation and exotic competitors.
- The report concludes that the total effect of the alternative dams would be favorable, provided these conditions are met.
