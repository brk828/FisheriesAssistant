# Low-Cost Meal-Based Diets for Culturing Trout in Ponds (1965)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquaculture #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1965 article from the *Boletín de Piscicultura Rural*, published by the Mexican [[Secretaría de Industria y Comercio]] through the [[Dirección General de Pesca e Industrias Conexas]] and the [[Instituto Nacional de Investigaciones Biológico-Pesqueras]], reports preliminary work on a cheap, nutritious, easily made feed for [[Trout]] raised at the [[El Zarco Fish Station]]. Traditional fresh foods such as beef, horse meat and crayfish were costly, spoiled quickly and were hard to obtain. The authors formulated two dry-meal diets, Diet A (meat meal and soybean meal) and Diet B (fish meal and rice meal), and tested them against fresh meat in a four-month [[Growth Trial]]. Diet A produced the greatest weight gain, Diet B matched the fresh-meat control, and both diets cost less per kilogram than fresh meat.

## Background & Description

The authors note that [[Formulated Feed]] containing meals and [[Vitamin Premix]] had solved the problem of raising trout on a large scale. Natural foods were expensive, decomposed rapidly and had uncertain supply. Trout are carnivorous, so animal-origin ingredients should dominate any mixed diet. Among available meals, the authors rejected rice bran, alfalfa and oat meals on the basis of [[Chemical Composition]], availability, appearance and price. The ingredients tested were:

- A meat meal from the Rastro de Ferrería, which contained bone and hair fragments.
- A fish meal, which contained spines about 1 cm long and debris.
- Vegetable meals of rice and soybean.

Raw meals were ground finer and sieved through a screen of 49 orifices per square centimetre. The vegetable meals were used as obtained.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Trout were held in rectangular ponds with running water. Water temperature ranged between 10 and 12 °C in most cases. The authors stress that uneaten feed sinking to the pond bottom decays and produces substances harmful to fish, so feed should be given at the rate fish consume it. The article also notes that a single diet fed for a long period may cause [[Nutritional Disease]], which is why alternation between diets is recommended. Microbial growth in wet feed was a central problem, addressed through [[Feed Preservation]].

## Key Findings

**Diet formulations (% of the mixture as reported)**

- **Diet A:** [[Meat Meal]] 69, [[Soybean Meal]] (defatted) 29, salt 2, vitamin mix 171 mg.
- **Diet B:** [[Fish Meal]] 78, [[Rice Meal]] 20, salt 2, vitamin mix 171 mg.

The vitamin premix contained vitamins A (50,000 IU), D (5,000 IU) and E (10 units), plus B-complex vitamins, niacinamide, choline bitartrate and monobasic calcium phosphate as a dispersant. The full list is in the source table.

**Preparation**

- Vitamins were first mixed with a small portion of the meal, then the remaining meals and salt were added and stirred to a homogeneous mix. Water (40–50% by weight) was added, containing [[Gum Arabic]] at 2% solution, until the dough reached a tortilla-like consistency. Diet B did not need gum arabic, because boiled rice meal formed an atole that gave good binding.
- Dough was extruded through a hand "churrera" and later a meat grinder with 2 mm holes, producing strands about 3 mm in diameter. These were cut into pieces about 5 mm long. See [[Pellet Preparation]].

**Feeding behaviour**

- Trout preferred feed that was moving, so feed was given at the speed at which it was eaten. Fish showed initial indifference but accepted the diets after a few days.

**Growth trial (four lots of 70 trout each, 4 months)**

| Lot | Diet | Initial mean weight | Final mean weight | Weight gain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | Fresh meat (control, donkey/horse) | 11.7 | 42.2 | 30.5 |
| II | Diet A | 9.6 | 54.2 | 44.6 |
| III | Diet B | 7.3 | 38.1 | 30.8 |
| IV | A + B alternated day by day | 6.7 | 41.5 | 34.8 |

Weight units are not specified in the source table. Diet A gave the greatest gain. Diet B equalled the control. The alternating regime fell between Diets B and A.

**Late-trial observations**

- By the end of the fourth month, Lot III ate less. The authors attributed this to increasing spine and impurity content and lower [[Protein Content]] as the fish meal sack was used up. They stress that fish meal is not inherently inferior and that meal quality was the limiting factor.
- Lots I and II continued for four more months. Diet A remained the best, though growth declined relative to the first period.

**Cost**

- Diet A costs $3.00 per kg (wet). Diet B costs $1.85 per kg (wet). Horse or donkey meat costs $3.50 per kg.
- Trout consume roughly half as much dry-meal feed as fresh meat, so the prepared diets were cheaper overall. Bulk purchase of raw materials could lower costs further. See [[Cost Comparison]].

**Preservation**

- Adding [[Aureomycin]] at 20 mg per 100 g of dry feed kept wet feed usable for about 48 hours, compared with controls. Refrigerated storage was also recommended.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

1. Juvenile trout can be fed economically on meat-meal or fish-meal diets.
2. Avoid one diet for long periods. Alternate diets in three-month blocks, for example Diet A with fresh meat. Fish-meal diets may be alternated if the fish meal has at least 60% protein.
3. Diets A and B suit adult trout, but newly hatched fish did not accept them well.
4. Store wet feed up to 48 hours only with added aureomycin; otherwise feed soon after preparation.
5. Feed only what trout eat so that nothing sinks to the pond bottom.

**Open problems**

- Trials on [[Fry Feeding]] were unsatisfactory. No good substitute was found for the ground beef liver used to feed fry.
