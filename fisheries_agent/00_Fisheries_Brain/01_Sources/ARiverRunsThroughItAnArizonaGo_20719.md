# Endangered Fish Habitat at Emerald Canyon Golf Course

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This magazine feature (*Golf Course Management*, November 1995) describes a cooperative project in which [[Emerald Canyon]], a county-owned public golf course in Parker, Arizona, provides predator-free lake habitat for two endangered native fish of the [[Colorado River]]: the [[Bonytail Chub]] and the [[Razorback Sucker]]. The project is run with the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] through the Parker Fisheries Resource Office. The article frames the four "big river fish" (Colorado squawfish, humpback chub, bonytail chub, razorback sucker) as all listed as endangered, and attributes their decline to dams, altered flows, cold hypolimnion releases, and non-native predators. It reports the population decline of razorback suckers and the rarity of bonytails, then documents the stocking, fish removal, and maintenance steps taken at the course. The article presents the project as a "win-win" for conservation and golf course management.

## Background & Description

**The four "big river fish."** According to project coordinator [[Chuck Minckley]], the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]] are all on the endangered species list. The article notes that the last known wild Colorado squawfish was caught in 1972 in the [[Grand Canyon]].

**Size and appearance.** The article states:
- Colorado squawfish could reach 6 feet in length and weigh 100 pounds.
- A grown bonytail chub is normally 14 to 16 inches long.
- A razorback sucker can grow up to 3 feet long.

Because of their large size and distinct appearance, these fish would be noticed if a sport angler caught one.

**Why they are rare.** Federal wildlife officials attribute the decline to:
- Construction of dams, which stopped historic spawning runs.
- Reduced river flows and controlled water releases that destroyed spawning sites.
- [[Hypolimnion Release]] of very cold bottom water from reservoirs, which impairs reproduction because the fish need warmer water to spawn and for normal egg and fry development.
- Competition from and predation by non-native and introduced fish, which eat eggs and young.

The article also describes the pre-development Colorado River as a harsh environment in which few fish species could adapt.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Bonytail decline.** Minckley states that about 50 years before the article, bonytail chubs were abundant in the lower Colorado. He says the species is now "essentially extinct," and that it took 25 years to catch 30 of them in the wild. The project's young bonytails came from the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], where the fingerlings descended from only six bonytail chubs brought to Dexter in 1981.

**Razorback decline.** The article reports that razorback sucker numbers in the lower basin dropped sharply, from approximately 66,000 in 1984 to 22,000 in 1994.

**Recovery approach.** The [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] has produced large numbers of fingerlings in hatcheries and found that wild razorback larvae could be harvested from [[Lake Mohave]]. Earlier attempts to place fingerlings in the Verde and Salt rivers failed; Minckley reports the fish "just disappeared," apparently eaten by other fish. The team concluded that raising fish in predator-free habitat allows them to grow larger and survive better once released. The stated goal is to maintain large populations and prevent extinction.

**Water source and pest control.** The course's lakes are filled from the Colorado River, so screens were installed in the intake pipes to keep other fish out. The article describes [[Rotenone]] as a natural piscicide that suffocates gill-breathing organisms by removing oxygen from water, used here to eliminate minnows and tiny fish that could prey on the endangered species. Minckley states it is not harmful to people or animals.

## Key Findings

**Project setup and fish removal**
- Minckley contacted [[Steve Benton]], general manager and head professional at Emerald Canyon, in summer 1994. Because the course is county-owned, the proposal required approval from the La Paz County manager and board of supervisors.
- Once the agreement was approved, screens were installed on intake pipes under the direction of superintendent [[Florencio Carrillo]].
- Before stocking, all existing fish were removed. Fish and Wildlife staff used a boat-mounted electrical shocker to stun fish, netted them and released them into the Colorado River, seined the lake, and then applied Rotenone. The article says every attempt was made to save and relocate the resident species.

**Stocking and growth**
- Bonytail fingerlings were placed in the lakes in October 1994 and grew to 10 to 12 inches. Some were released into Lake Havasu.
- Razorback suckers were introduced in July (under 2 inches long) and reached 6 to 7 inches by the first of September.
- The project has raised fish to 12 inches. Staff plan to harvest larger fish and implant a [[PIT Tag]] in each body cavity to track origin and growth.

**Tagging method.** A PIT (passive integrated transponder) tag carries a 10-digit identifying number. If a tagged fish is caught, the tag reveals information such as its origin and growth. The article compares this to microchipping dogs.

**Course management and maintenance**
- The course provides labor to maintain the lakes. A grant helped cover the cost of attaching the screens.
- Aerators were installed to control algae.
- Maintenance is mechanical only, with no chemicals: trimmers and rakes remove cattails, and a large custom rake removes algae.
- Fish and Wildlife staff visit often to test water and capture fish, but the course reports no major added workload or restrictions.
- Parker High School plans an interpretive kiosk on the course to inform the public about the fish.

**Stakeholder assessments**
- Benton: "The project has been a success so far," and the course plans to continue.
- Carrillo: "I'm happy with the project," and the lakes must be kept in good condition for the fish.
- Minckley says the fish are "like aquatic eagles," noting that people recognize eagles and wolves on endangered species lists but rarely notice fish.
- [[Hans Stuart]], natural resource specialist for the Southwest region of the Fish and Wildlife Service, encourages golf course managers to pursue similar partnerships and states the agency is "always looking for partners."

**Overall conclusion.** The article concludes that the results were worth the effort and presents the Emerald Canyon arrangement as a model for collaboration between golf courses and federal conservation programs.
