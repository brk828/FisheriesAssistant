# Ecology of Stream and Riparian Habitats of the Great Basin Region: A Community Profile

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Report (No. 85(7.24), September 1989) is a reference profile of the ecology of [[Stream Habitat]] and [[Riparian Habitat]] across the [[Great Basin]], a largely closed interior drainage covering most of Nevada and western Utah and parts of California, Oregon and Idaho. The authors (Minshall, Jensen and Platts) state that these systems have received less study than comparable habitats elsewhere in the United States, so the profile compiles available information on geography, geomorphology, riparian classification, [[Aquatic Habitat]] chemistry, biota, organic matter production, human interactions and management. The document is a review and synthesis, not a primary field study. The excerpt provided ends partway through the fish section (Table 22), so later fish, management and recommendation content is not included.

## Background & Description

**Scope and definitions.** The report defines riparian habitat as land with hydrophytes and/or soils saturated by groundwater for part of the growing season, within the rooting depth of potential native vegetation. This includes vegetated wetlands (per [[Cowardin Classification]]) and more mesic riparian zones. Aquatic habitat covers riverine (stream), lacustrine (lake) and palustrine (marsh) systems, but streams are the main focus because they are more numerous and have been studied more.

**Physiography.** The term "Great Basin" was first applied by John C. Fremont after his 1843–1844 expedition. The region contains more than 75 elongated basins separated by north-to-south mountain ranges. Two basins held large Pleistocene lakes: [[Lake Bonneville]] (nearly 52,000 km² at maximum) and [[Lake Lahontan]] (about 22,442 km² at maximum, per Morrison 1965). The region is bounded by the [[Basin and Range Province]] and is subdivided into five hydrographic subregions: Central, Bonneville, Lahontan, Northwest and Southern.

**Climate.** The region is semiarid to arid and lies in the rain shadow of the Sierra Nevada. Mean annual precipitation ranges from less than 10 cm in the lower basins to more than 76 cm on high summits. Evapotranspiration accounts for all water loss from the closed drainage.

**Geology and soils.** Block faulting that began in the middle Tertiary produced the present topography. Soils are mostly aridisols, entisols and mollisols, and salt and mud flats are extensive in the Bonneville and Lahontan subdivisions.

**Vegetation.** Riparian vegetation is considered a reduced relict of an Arcto-Tertiary geoflora. Common riparian genera include willow (Salix), cottonwood (Populus), alder (Alnus) and sedges (Carex).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian gradients and classification.** Riparian ecosystems vary along three gradients: a north–south latitudinal gradient (about 35° to 44° N), an elevational gradient from headwaters to basin sink, and a lateral transriparian gradient from channel to upland. The authors propose a hierarchical classification (hydrologic unit, geomorphic valley form, water regime, physiognomy, community type, descriptors). Preliminary valley forms are glacial, fluvial canyon, alluvial and [[Lacustrine Basins]]. Water-regime classes are permanently flooded, semipermanently flooded, saturated, seasonally flooded and subirrigated. The authors note that existing riparian classifications are inconsistent in method and resolution across the region.

**Hydrographic systems.** Key drainages include the [[Bear River]] (about 59% of annual inflow to the [[Great Salt Lake]]), the [[Provo River]] (about 70% of Utah Lake's inflow), the [[Truckee River]] (draining more than 5,000 km² from [[Lake Tahoe]] to [[Pyramid Lake]]), the Humboldt, Carson and Walker rivers, and the [[Amargosa River]] in Death Valley. Numerous springs, including those at [[Ash Meadows]] and Ruby Marsh, sustain aquatic habitats.

**Water chemistry.** Closed-basin lakes are shaped by evaporation and inflow composition. The Great Salt Lake is hypersaline and dominated by sodium chloride. Pyramid Lake's TDS rose 29% from about 3,750 mg/L (1933) to 5,250 mg/L (October 1977), largely because of water diversions. Streams are mostly intermediate in dissolved solids (about 150–600 mg/L), with calcium/magnesium carbonate as the main constituent. Nitrogen is often the limiting nutrient in lakes such as Pyramid Lake and Lake Tahoe.

**Biota.** The report describes these systems as "islands of freshwater in a sea of land," with limited taxa and extreme physical conditions. Benthic and planktonic communities, algae and macrophytes are described for specific sites (see Key Findings). Ephemeral streams are described as biologically depauperate. Many endemic species occur in isolated springs and lakes, and several have declined or gone extinct.

**Organic matter and carbon flux.** The report stresses allochthonous inputs (plant litter, dehydrated vegetable matter and feces carried in by runoff) as food sources for temporary waters. Overgrazing could cut off this input and limit completion of aquatic life cycles.

**Human pressures.** Water withdrawals, diversions, channelization, grazing, mining and groundwater pumping have degraded or destroyed many habitats. Examples include the channelization of the Truckee River in the early 1960s and the decline of Owens Valley wetlands after groundwater pumping by Los Angeles beginning in the 1920s and 1930s.

## Key Findings

- **Limited knowledge base.** Few studies exist on Great Basin algal, vascular hydrophyte and invertebrate communities, "fewer than a half dozen" for each. Research is concentrated near a few universities and much of it remains in unpublished theses and reports.
- **Riparian classification is fragmented.** Classification efforts (Utah community types, Malheur National Forest streamside types, Southwest biogeographic systems) differ in method and resolution, which limits integration.
- **Wetland significance for waterfowl.** Shaw and Fredine (1956) identified 1,008 km² of high-quality waterfowl wetlands in Utah, 442 km² in Nevada and 996 km² in Oregon, mostly associated with lakes.
- **Ephemeral streams.** Ephemeral streams are biologically poor, but temporary waterbodies respond rapidly after wetting because stored plant material and feces provide nutrients for bacteria, algae, protozoa and crustaceans.
- **Great Salt Lake.** Normal-level surface area is about 4,370 km² (average depth 4 m). Lake level has varied as much as 6.1 m, and record flooding since 1983 damaged industries, highways and waterfowl areas.
- **Provo River invertebrates.** Gaufin (1959) found 95.2% of bottom fauna in five insect orders. Upper stations averaged 776 organisms/m² (Upper Bridge) and 733 (Soapstone), while lower stations reached 4,538 (Lemon's Grove), 5,088 (Deer Creek Park), 3,676 (Vivian Park) and 3,094 (Highway 89) organisms/m². Over 275 invertebrate species and 165 genera have been reported from the river.
- **Diatoms of the Provo River.** Lawson and Rushforth (1975) identified 38 genera, 156 species, 45 varieties and 4 forms, arranged in three floristic zones.
- **Pyramid Lake zooplankton.** *Diaptomus sicilis* made up 66% of net zooplankton, and chironomids were the most abundant macroinvertebrates (Chironomus averaging 582/m²).
- **Lake Tahoe nutrients.** Nitrate was low in the upper 100 m, and nitrogen was likely the main limiting nutrient. Epilithic periphyton in the littoral zone may contribute more than 60% of primary production there, though the littoral zone is only 19% of lake area.
- **Ward Creek nitrogen.** Alder stands (about 30 ha of riparian alder) release nitrate in fall and early winter, estimated at about 500 kg/yr of NO₃-N. Most of the watershed's annual nitrate flux is attributed to nitrogen fixation.
- **Phosphorus retention.** Dingle Marsh (Bear Lake) was a net annual sink for TSS, TP and phosphate, retaining about 10%–62% of TSS. Detritus was heavily colonized by periphyton, which made it a major phosphate sink.
- **Pine Grove Creek.** This small Beaver County stream supported few invertebrate taxa, dominated by hardy species such as *Baetis*, *Hydropsyche* and Chironomidae, and was attributed to historical grazing, road construction and mining.
- **Blacksmith Fork shading.** Open-site invertebrate production was about twice that of the shaded site for most taxa (excluding black flies), probably from higher-quality food or phototactic attraction.
- **Fish and endemism.** Great Basin fishes evolved in an isolated inland drainage with large salinity fluctuations. Ash Meadows, with more than 30 springs, supports endemic organisms including 5 fish species, and the Ash Meadows Wildlife Refuge was established in 1984 with a 4,525 ha unit, expanding toward a planned total of 9,513 ha.

- **Management conclusion.** Overgrazing, diversion and channelization threaten the allochthonous inputs and habitat structure that sustain Great Basin aquatic life. The authors call for urgent study of these systems before further alteration.
