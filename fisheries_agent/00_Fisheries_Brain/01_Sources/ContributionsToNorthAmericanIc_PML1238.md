# Contributions to North American Ichthyology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic section covers North American [[Cyprinidae]] (minnows) in three genera: [[Ptychocheilus]] (the pikes), [[Gila Genus]] and [[Squalius Genus]]. It gives diagnoses, meristic counts, body proportions, colouration and known ranges for about 40 named taxa, drawn from the original descriptions and the authors' examination of type material. The authors state that many North American species were poorly defined, and that larger series are needed before species can be reliably separated. The text is an OCR scan and ends partway through the account of the last species, [[Squalius Gibbosus]].

## Background & Description

The section follows the systematic arrangement of a series of contributions to North American ichthyology. Each entry gives:

- Body form, head and caudal peduncle proportions.
- Mouth position and maxillary reach relative to the eye.
- Fin placement, for example dorsal fin position relative to the ventrals.
- Meristic counts: dorsal (D), anal (A), lateral-line scales (Lat. l.), scale rows, and pharyngeal teeth in the formula 2, 5-4, 2 style.
- Maximum length, usually in inches.
- Colour in life, including spring breeding colours in males.
- Synonyms and original citations.

**Genus Ptychocheilus.** This group contains the largest North American cyprinids. [[Colorado Pike]] (*P. lucius*) reaches more than 4 feet and is described as abundant in the [[Colorado River]]. [[Sacramento Pike]] (*P. oregonensis*) reaches 3 to 5 feet, is confined mainly to Pacific slope rivers west of the [[Sierra Nevada]], and is the largest cyprinid in the region. The two species differ mainly in the pharyngeal bones and teeth, lateral-line curvature and scale counts. *P. rapax* (Monterey, California), *P. harfordi* and *P. lucius* are also described.

**Genus Gila.** The genus is diagnosed by an elongate body, a very slender, widely forked caudal peduncle, small scales, and close-set, compressed, hooked pharyngeal teeth with no grinding surface. The authors note that the genus grades into *Squalius* through intermediate forms. Its only consistent character is the vertebral count of about 46, which is 5 or 6 more than usual in *Squalius*. The species treated are:

- *G. elegans* (Colorado and Gila rivers, 12 inches), described as the extreme form of the type, with a hump-like region before the dorsal fin.
- *G. robusta* (Colorado and Rio Gila), with a stouter caudal peduncle than *G. elegans*.
- *G. grahami* (Rio Gila and Colorado basin), with larger, more closely imbricated scales.
- *G. affinis* (Kansas and Platte rivers), known from Abbott's description only.
- *G. gracilis*, *G. emorii* (Rio Gila), *G. nacrea* (Green River), and *G. seminuda* (Rio Virgen, Utah). *G. seminuda* is described as a transition form to normal *Squalius*.

**Genus Squalius.** This is described as a very large genus with numerous species in Europe, Asia and North America. Most North American species were poorly defined. The authors based most descriptions on Girard's preserved type specimens. The genus is divided by:

- Tooth form (with or without a grinding surface).
- Caudal peduncle depth.
- Mouth size and position.
- Anal fin basis length (ray count 10 to 12 versus 8 or 9).
- Lateral-line scale count (50 to 65 versus 65 to 80).

Subgenera or groups *Clinostomus*, *Tigoma*, *Siboma* and *Cheonda* are used as informal divisions. Notable species include:

- [[Red-Sided Shiner]] (*S. elongatus*): dark bluish body, crimson lateral band in spring males, 5 inches, Great Lakes and upper Mississippi Valley.
- *S. vandoisulus*: 5 inches, Virginia to Georgia, abundant.
- *S. estor*: the largest mouth of the American cyprinids, 4 inches, Cumberland and Tennessee rivers.
- [[Leather-Sided Minnow]] (*S. tenia*): 3.5 inches, Salt Lake Basin, abundant in the [[Provo River]].
- *S. copei*: 4 inches, Rio Grande and Utah basin, described as very abundant.
- *S. cruoreus*: 4 inches, Utah Lake.
- *S. margaritus*: 3 inches, Susquehanna River.
- *S. gula*: 7 inches, Mexico.
- *S. pulcher*: 6 inches, Chihuahua River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The species occupy freshwater rivers and lakes across western North America, from the [[Colorado River]], [[Gila River]] and [[Sacramento River]] drainages to the [[Great Basin]] and Utah basins. Eastern species occur in the [[Great Lakes]], the [[Upper Mississippi River]], and the Cumberland, Tennessee and Susquehanna rivers. Several species extend into northern Mexico, including the Chihuahua, Nazas and Mimbres drainages.

The text reports habitat only in general terms, such as "abundant in Provo River", "very abundant" in Utah basins, and "Great Basin" for lake- and river-dwelling forms. It contains no water-chemistry, flow, or invertebrate data. Several entries record only "exact locality unknown" or "probably Nevada". Breeding colouration, with red or crimson fins and sides in spring males, is a recurring trait in the descriptions.

## Key Findings

1. **Largest taxa.** [[Ptychocheilus Lucius]] and [[Ptychocheilus Oregonensis]] are the largest North American cyprinids, reaching 3 to more than 4 feet. The authors describe *P. oregonensis* as the largest cyprinid in the USA.
2. **Diagnostic characters.** The pharyngeal bone and tooth form distinguish *Ptychocheilus* from *Gila* and *Squalius*. The vertebral count, about 46, is the only consistent character for *Gila* from *Squalius*.
3. **Taxonomic uncertainty.** The authors report that *Gila* and *Squalius* intergrade, that several species may be synonymous (for example *P. rapax* and *P. oregonensis*, and *S. pulcher* whose tooth count differs from Girard's account), and that larger series are needed.
4. **Dentition-based key.** The *Squalius* key separates species first by the presence or absence of a grinding surface on the pharyngeal teeth, then by caudal peduncle depth, mouth size, anal ray count and lateral-line scale count.
5. **Regional richness.** The largest number of species is reported from the Gila, Colorado and Utah basins. Many species are known from one or two localities.
6. **Spring colouration.** Spring males of several species (for example *S. elongatus*, *S. estor*, *S. tenia*, *S. montanus*) develop red or crimson sides or fins, which the authors use as a diagnostic feature.
7. **Incomplete text.** The OCR text ends in the account of [[Squalius Gibbosus]] (the Mullet Chub), so the section is incomplete. The source does not cover ecology, life history, or conservation status.
