# Relationship of Limnological Features to Growth of Fishes in Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 paper by [[Kenneth Dixon Carlander]], presented at the Verhandlungen of the Internationale Vereinigung für Theoretische und Angewandte Limnologie (Stuttgart, December 1966), argues that environmental factors rarely correlate directly with the average growth rate of fishes in lakes. Carlander proposes a conceptual model in which environmental factors act mainly on [[Biomass Growth]] and [[Carrying Capacity]], while [[Average Growth Rate]] of individuals depends largely on [[Population Density]] relative to that capacity. The paper is followed by a discussion section in which several researchers raise objections and offer supporting field observations.

## Background & Description

Efforts to link environmental variables with fish growth in lakes have generally been unsatisfactory, even though controlled experiments show that such factors can modify growth. Carlander attributes part of the difficulty to the impossibility of holding all factors constant in natural waters. Multiple regression could help if enough factors were recorded at once, but the more basic need is to separate biomass growth from the growth of individual fish.

The paper defines the key terms as follows:

- **Carrying capacity** is the maximum biomass an environment can support, set by environmental factors.
- **Potential biomass growth** depends on how far the current biomass (P) lies below carrying capacity (Pm).
- **Average growth rate (G)** equals the annual biomass growth (Pm − P) minus the biomass of [[Recruitment]] (R), divided by the number of individuals (N) among which it is shared:

  G = [(Pm − P) − R] / N

Carlander notes that in stabilized populations, biomass usually reaches carrying capacity each growing season, so potential annual growth is governed by annual removal through harvest and natural mortality. He acknowledges that this formula simplifies reality but considers it a more valid conceptual model than assuming simple correlations between environmental factors and average growth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper identifies several complications in applying the model:

- **Carrying capacity cannot be predicted accurately.** The best estimates come from measuring biomass after it stabilizes, yet fish biomass methods remain inefficient. Correlation with a single environmental factor is expected only if that factor limits all or most populations.
- **Populations are heterogeneous.** Each species has its own carrying capacity, which is not independent of other species' biomass. Size and age groups within a species also differ in requirements.
- **Overpopulation can damage habitat,** though this has rarely been shown for fish. Cann (1929) described habitat destruction by an overpopulation of [[Common Carp]].
- **Stunted populations** are usually of one size class and do not use all available niches, so total biomass is likely below the carrying capacity of a better-balanced population. Occasional larger, faster-growing individuals in stunted stocks (Escumeyer, 1937, on [[Stunted Perch Populations]]) suggest little competition for the niches used by these odd-sized fish.

Carlander explains apparent conflicts with the model in several ways:

- **Growth not yet at carrying capacity:** In newly stocked waters, in experiments with controlled fish numbers, or over short studies while biomass is still rising, environmental factors may act directly and correlate well with growth.
- **Young fish outside the controlled biomass:** Small fish often occupy niches not shared with larger fish, so their growth may track environmental factors until their own biomass reaches capacity.
- **Environmental change affects capacity:** When a factor changes growth, it may be altering carrying capacity rather than growth directly. Unless recruitment absorbs the entire biomass change, average growth will shift too. The same factor may show no correlation once populations stabilize.

**Temperature and growing season length** deserve special attention because they often correlate with average growth. Higher temperature increases metabolic turnover of nutrients and usually shortens fish lifespans. Longer growing seasons can raise annual fishing and natural mortality if instantaneous rates are higher during the growing season. The gap between biomass and carrying capacity therefore widens with higher temperatures and longer seasons. For species that spawn only at the start of the season, a longer season may shift more of the potential biomass growth toward individual growth rather than recruitment.

## Key Findings

1. **The central claim:** Environmental factors mainly control carrying capacity and biomass growth. Their effect on average individual growth is indirect, so significant simple correlations are not to be expected in stabilized populations.
2. **Population density matters in relation to carrying capacity.** Average growth depends on how many individuals share the potential biomass growth, not on absolute density alone.
3. **Apparent exceptions can be explained.** Most published correlations between growth and environmental factors fit the model when the timing of measurements, the niche use of young fish, and effects on carrying capacity are considered.
4. **Temperature and growing season length** affect the annual gap between biomass and carrying capacity, with consequences for mortality and the balance between recruitment and individual growth.
5. **Stunted populations** use fewer ecological niches, so their biomass is typically below what a balanced population could support.
6. **Discussion points:**
   - [[Le Cren]] asked whether "biomass growth" equals ecological production and suggested that food is produced continuously through the growing season rather than only at the start. Carlander agreed that biomass growth equals biological production but avoided the term because it is often confused with yield or harvest. He agreed that longer seasons increase both biomass and average growth, which he treated as annual variation in carrying capacity.
   - Bond reported that, for adult [[Largemouth Bass]] in southern Oregon and Willamette Valley ponds, the area under "growth capacity" curves (based on temperature converted to digestion rate) was directly proportional to average individual growth. Carlander suggested temperature may also affect nutrient overturn and mortality.
   - A discussant reported that annual growth of [[Common Carp]] correlated well with the number of days at or above 20 °C, with little or no correlation with average temperature.
   - Baton proposed four combinations of food reserve, biomass, growth rate and spawning size (A/B for food reserve, X/Y for biomass, a/b for growth rate, x/y for spawning size) and referred to a 1963 paper for detail. Carlander suggested that a large food reserve may indicate biomass below carrying capacity, which would produce rapid growth.
   - Ouiva reported over ten years of rotenone sampling (2–8 treatments) in small backwaters of the Labe River floodplain, Bohemia. Biomass was about 400 kg/acre at maximum, with roughly 40,000 fish over 5 cm per acre. Growth in all cyprinids was extremely poor and correlated with early sexual maturity, and carnivorous fish were almost entirely absent. The discussant argued that carrying capacity was the main control on growth.
7. **Research implications:** The author recommends keeping the biomass–individual growth distinction in mind when assessing published work on growth factors and when designing new studies.
