## Executive Summary
The [[Electric Eel]] (*Electrophorus electricus*) is a South American freshwater fish notable for its eel-like elongated body and its powerful bioelectric organ. Despite its common name and superficial resemblance to true eels, it is taxonomically distinct, belonging to the order [[Cypriniformes]] (also referenced as [[Ostariophysi]]), the largest order of freshwater fishes, which also includes minnows, carps, and catfishes. It is placed in the family [[Gymnotidae]], alongside the aquarium-favored [[Knifefishes]]. The species is renowned for producing substantial electric discharges—exceeding 500 volts at 2 amperes (1,000 watts)—primarily used to immobilize prey and deter predators.

## Background & Description
- **Body shape:** Greatly elongated, almost round in cross-section, superficially resembling a true eel.
- **Size:** Commonly exceeds 3 feet (0.9 m) in length; reportedly reaches 8–9 feet (2.4–2.7 m), with a body thickness comparable to a man's thigh.
- **Fins:**
  - Pelvic fins: absent.
  - Dorsal fin: rudimentary.
  - Caudal fin: rudimentary.
  - Anal fin: extends along the entire length of the body, running forward to the anus, which is located under the throat (an unusual anatomical placement).
- **Electric organ:**
  - Occupies approximately four-fifths of the total body length (the tail region).
  - Composed of longitudinal columns, each functioning like a battery.
  - Each column is made up of an immense number of stacked electric plates.
  - The organ is a modification of muscle tissue, an evolutionary trait convergent with other electrogenic fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat:** Freshwater rivers of South America.
- **Behavior:** Described as a relatively sluggish creature, consistent with other electrogenic fish groups.
- **Predation strategy:** Uses electric discharge primarily to immobilize prey; the shock is delivered voluntarily via extensive innervation of the electric organs by branches of the spinal nerves.
- **Defense:** The discharge is powerful enough to stun a human and potentially kill a horse, serving as a formidable anti-predator defense.
- **Convergent evolution:** Electrogenesis has evolved independently in several unrelated fish lineages, including:
  - [[Electric Ray]] (*Torpedo* spp.) — warm/temperate seas.
  - [[Electric Stargazer]] (*Astroscopus* spp.) — warm American shores.
  - [[Electric Catfish]] (*Malapterurus electricus*) — Nile River.
  - In all cases, despite independent evolutionary origins, the electric organs derive from modified muscle tissue.

## Key Findings
- The Electric Eel is **not** a true eel; it belongs to the Cypriniformes/Ostariophysi order and the family Gymnotidae, related to knifefishes rather than to Anguilliformes.
- The electric organ generates an **alternating current**, flowing from the electrically positive tail to the negative front (head) end.
- Maximum shock intensity occurs when the head and tail simultaneously contact well-separated points on a victim's body, maximizing the current path across the target.
- Measured discharges can exceed **500 volts at ~2 amperes (1,000 watts)** for very brief durations.
- Shock severity is influenced by the individual fish's size and health condition.
- The evolution of electrogenic organs represents a striking case of **convergent evolution** across phylogenetically distant fish groups (Gymnotidae, Torpedinidae, Astroscopidae, Malapteruridae), each independently repurposing muscle tissue for bioelectric function.
