# Observations on the food habits of the flathead and blue catfish in Alabama

## Executive Summary
This study by Brown and Dendy (1961) examines stomach contents of 34 [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) and 152 [[Blue Catfish]] (*Ictalurus furcatus*) collected via telephone-magneto electroshocking from the [[Alabama River]], [[Tensaw River]], and [[Tombigbee River]] between 1951–1954. The research documents an ontogenetic dietary shift from invertebrate consumption to piscivory, with flathead catfish transitioning at approximately 10 inches total length, and blue catfish transitioning across the 8–18 inch size range. Notable site-specific variation in diet composition was observed among the three river systems, suggesting that local prey availability (e.g., [[Anchovy|Anchovies]], [[Threadfin Shad]]) strongly influences feeding patterns. The findings challenge prior assumptions (Swingle 1954) that catfish become "predatory" only at 15.5+ inches, suggesting piscivory begins much earlier.

## Background & Description
- **Species examined:**
  - *Pylodictis olivaris* ([[Flathead Catfish]]) — n=34, size range 3–42 inches (inch-group classification)
  - *Ictalurus furcatus* ([[Blue Catfish]]) — n=152, size range 3–28 inches
  - *Ictalurus punctatus* ([[Channel Catfish]]) — incidental catch, n=6, all under 7 inches (undersampled due to gear bias)
- **Size-based dietary transition points:**
  - Flathead Catfish: invertebrate feeding dominant below 11 inches; no invertebrates found in stomachs >11 inches; fish prey first appears at 11-inch group.
  - Blue Catfish: fish prey documented in individuals as small as 4 inches; general dietary transition (invertebrate → fish/macro-invertebrate) occurs between 8–13 inches.
- Sampling gear (telephone-magneto shockers) was relatively ineffective for capturing *Ictalurus punctatus*, introducing a likely sampling bias against that species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat sampled:** Large river channels several hundred feet wide, 5–30 ft deep, with variable current (0–2.0 ft/sec) and tidal influence in the Tensaw and lower Tombigbee Rivers.
- **Trophic ecology:**
  - [[Flathead Catfish]] consistently piscivorous in larger size classes (62% of stomachs 12–42 in. contained fish), consistent with earlier work by Eddy & Surber (1947) and Forbes & Richardson (1908).
  - [[Blue Catfish]] displayed **omnivorous, site-dependent feeding**:
    - **Tombigbee River:** Fish-dominated diet across nearly all size classes; prey included [[Anchovy]] (*Anchoa mitchilli*, ~300 individuals from 15 stomachs in 1954), [[Threadfin Shad]] (*Dorosoma petenense*, 63 individuals/14 stomachs), [[Skipjack Herring]] (*Alosa chrysochloris*), and [[Logperch]] (*Percina caprodes*).
    - **Tensaw River:** Diet dominated by debris and invertebrates (notably [[Crayfish]] and chironomids); fish prey mainly minnows, occasional [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) and catfish.
    - **Alabama River:** All 7 examined blue catfish (15–24 in.) fed exclusively on invertebrates (mayflies, mussels, hellgramites) — no fish prey detected despite large body size.
  - Diet composition correlated with local forage fish abundance (per concurrent rotenone population surveys), e.g., anchovies and threadfin shad were abundant only in the 1954 Tombigbee sample and were correspondingly prevalent in stomach contents that year.
  - Notably, despite being the most abundant small fish in rotenone samples, young catfish and [[Freshwater Drum]] (*Aplodinotus grunniens*) rarely appeared in stomach contents — suggesting possible avoidance, differential capture efficiency, or rapid digestion.
- **Invertebrate prey documented:** chironomids, caddisflies, termites, fingernail clams, freshwater mussels, freshwater shrimp (*Palaemonetes* sp.), dragonfly/odonate nymphs, mayfly nymphs (ephemerid), hellgramites, and crayfish.
- **Predator-prey/cannibalism note:** One flathead catfish had consumed a blue catfish (Tensaw River); one blue catfish consumed a smaller catfish (Tensaw River).

## Key Findings
1. **Flathead Catfish dietary shift:** Invertebrate feeding dominant in fish <11 inches; transition to predominant piscivory (62% fish occurrence) occurs at ~10–11 inches TL, with debris present in 75% of stomachs in the 12–42 inch range.
2. **Blue Catfish dietary shift:** More gradual and geographically variable; general transition to fish/macro-invertebrate feeding occurs in the 8–18 inch range, but the [[Alabama River]] population showed exclusive invertebrate feeding even at 15–24 inches, contradicting the general size-based trend.
3. **River-specific diet divergence:** Sharp contrast between Tombigbee (fish-dominated) and Tensaw (invertebrate/debris-dominated) blue catfish diets within the same 8–18 inch size range, despite similar habitat structure — implicating prey availability over intrinsic size-driven preference as the primary driver.
4. **Comparison to literature:** Findings align with [[Darnell 1958]] (Lake Pontchartrain blue catfish diet shifts: zooplankton <4in → bottom organisms 4–9.5in → fish/macro-organisms >9.5in) but contrast with [[Lambou 1961]], who found large Louisiana blue catfish fed exclusively on blue crabs.
5. **Management implication:** The study challenges [[Swingle 1954]]'s threshold of 15.5 inches for classifying catfish as "predatory" (C-species) in pond/river balance models, proposing that both blue and flathead catfish may function as effective predators well before reaching 16 inches.
6. **Gear limitation:** Telephone-magneto shocking was an ineffective method for sampling channel catfish, limiting comparative conclusions for that species.
