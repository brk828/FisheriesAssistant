# Biological Studies of the Delta-Mendota Canal, Central Valley Project, California, II

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyrenidae #Ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by the [[California Academy of Sciences]] under [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] Contract No. 14-06-200-7762A (April 1975), describes a year-round biological study of the [[Delta-Mendota Canal]] from August 1972 to November 1974. The work sought to identify the canal's organisms and their ecology, with a focus on the introduced [[Asiatic Clam]] (*Corbicula manilensis*), whose dense populations foul the canal and reduce its capacity through sedimentation. The study found that the clam was the most prominent organism in the canal, occurring at densities much higher than reported elsewhere, and that the tube-building [[Corophium Amphipod]] (*Corophium spinicorne*) provides a nursery habitat for juvenile clams. The authors conclude that complete eradication is unlikely under operating conditions and recommend a multiple-use approach combining sport fishing, a limited clam fishery, control of encrustations, and limited seasonal chemical treatment.

## Background & Description

The Delta-Mendota Canal is one of the larger concrete-lined canals in California. It has a long history of unusual and widespread sedimentation that significantly reduced canal capacity. Sedimentation correlated with the presence of Asiatic clams and tube-building amphipods, so the report treats these biological fouling organisms as the primary targets for possible control. The authors note that control efforts require a thorough understanding of canal ecology first.

Earlier biological information came from [[Prokopovich]] (1954, 1965, 1967, 1968), [[Hanna]] (1956), and [[Zullo]] et al. (1972). That work relied mainly on fall and winter sampling during dewatering, so little was known about canal life during normal operation or in other seasons. The present study filled that gap.

The canal is described as large, steep-sided, relatively deep, swiftly flowing, and concrete-lined, which required specially designed sampling equipment. Sampling covered:

- **Biweekly collections** from 15 stations while the canal was in operation.
- **Two dewatering periods:** November to January 1972–73 and November to January 1974–75.
- **Total samples analyzed:** 5,239, covering species composition, seasonal and geographic abundance, ecology, and life history.

The author was Larry L. Eng, Research Biologist, with assistance from David W. Behrens, Barbara Weitbrecht, Michael Daigian, and Victor Granchukoff.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The canal has three major benthic habitats, each with characteristic inhabitants:

1. **Concrete side-lining and vertical concrete structures.** Eighteen species were collected here. Sponges (including *Eunapius fragilis*), the entoproct *Urnatella gracilis*, and the barnacle *Balanus improvisus* occurred mainly on vertical concrete and bare side-lining, while most other species were associated with Corophium tube incrustations. The incrustations are strengthened by the hydrozoan *Cordylophora lacustris* and the bryozoan *Fredericella sultana*. Incrustations are concentrated in Pools 1 to 3.
2. **Clam-bearing sediments on the canal invert (bottom).** The Asiatic clam dominated, often at densities above 1,000 individuals per square foot. More than twenty species were identified from these sediments. Sediment width fluctuated markedly with water velocity: it generally increased during late fall and winter, when pumpage was minimal, and sometimes disappeared entirely in summer, when velocity peaked.
3. **Earth-lined canal.** This section, which is partly tule-lined, had lower water velocity and generally much lower densities than the concrete-lined habitats.

Plankton populations were also sizeable. Twenty-one phytoplankton species were identified, with diatoms the most abundant and an important food for filter feeders including the Asiatic clam. Fourteen zooplankton species were identified; zooplankton communities were seasonally dominated by rotifers, cladocerans, copepods, and clam larvae.

Water velocity and temperature were the two most obvious factors influencing variation in species occurrence. Most invertebrates varied seasonally in abundance, related to water velocity, temperature, and reproductive cycles. Pumping operations were also found to either depress or enhance natural periodicity, and annual variation was observed.

Fish use the canal as well. Fish are unevenly distributed, congregating where water turbulence increases, near checks and siphons, and more abundantly near the canal head in association with amphipod encrustations. The canal offers an opportunity for fish and invertebrates to extend their ranges upstream in the San Joaquin River drainage. Most fish appear to enter through the [[Tracy Fish Collecting Facility]].

## Key Findings

### Species Composition
- Five to six hundred? No: the document reports **23 fish species** identified in the abstract (plus 9 from earlier studies), while the summary states **32 fish species** have been identified from the canal. The discrepancy is in the source text.
- More than 20 invertebrate species were recorded in clam-bearing sediments, and 18 in the concrete side-lining habitat.
- A sponge, bryozoan, entoproct, and barnacle complement occupies the concrete surfaces.

### Asiatic Clam (*Corbicula manilensis*)
- The most prominent organism in the canal, at densities much higher than reported elsewhere.
- Likely important in building and maintaining sediment beds because of its density and filter-feeding habit.
- Enters the canal from the Delta, O'Neill Forebay, and through reproduction of clams already established in the canal, and at all life stages.
- Reproduction runs primarily March through October, with peaks in spring and late summer. Some evidence suggests a minor third reproductive period in winter.
- Several fish species feed on clams, but none are known to preferentially select them.

### Corophium Amphipod (*Corophium spinicorne*)
- Builds extensive mud-tube incrustations on concrete side-lining in Pools 1 to 3.
- Tube incrustations function as a "nursery area" for juvenile Asiatic clams.
- Appears to be the primary food of most canal fish.

### Fish and Fishery
- [[Channel Catfish]], [[White Catfish]], and [[Striped Bass]] support a moderate sport fishery.
- The most frequently caught species were white catfish, channel catfish, and striped bass.

### Management Recommendations
- Complete eradication of the Asiatic clam appears unlikely under operating conditions.
- A multiple-use concept could be beneficial, including enhancement of the existing sport fishery, a limited clam fishery, and continued primary use for water delivery.
- Additional clam reduction might come from controlling side-lining encrustations (the clam nursery area) and limited seasonal chemical treatment of canal waters.
- The report notes that pesticide analyses of clams from the 1972–73 dewatering were also conducted, and that further study areas could lead to reduced clam fouling.
