# Frederic Morton Chamberlain's 1904 Survey of Arizona Fishes, with Annotations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #historical-ecology

## Executive Summary

This annotated publication by W. L. Minckley reproduces the manuscript of [[Frederic Morton Chamberlain]], a U.S. Bureau of Fisheries aquatic biologist who surveyed Arizona fishes from 15 January to 28 April 1904, with about 30 field days concentrated in the [[Gila River Basin]]. Chamberlain's handwritten, fifty-two-page report was held in the Smithsonian Institution Archives. Minckley's annotations place the 1904 observations against modern conditions, showing that the aquatic systems Chamberlain sampled have been drastically reduced. The report also identifies the causes of decline he observed: vegetation destruction by grazing, flooding and erosion, irrigation diversion, mining pollution and sediment, and the use of explosives in fishing.

## Background & Description

- **Historical value:** Chamberlain's report is one of the few pre-1900 documents describing southwestern aquatic biota. Its significance rests on its precision and on its voucher specimens, which were deposited in the [[National Museum of Natural History]] (USNM) and have served as comparative material for later systematic work.
- **Author's career:** From 1897 to 1914 Chamberlain worked for the [[U.S. Fish Commission]], later the [[U.S. Bureau of Fisheries]]. His assignments included river fish surveys in the Gulf of Mexico drainages, salmon studies in California and Alaska, naturalist duties aboard the USS Albatross, and fur seal assessments in the Pribilof Islands.
- **Commission:** [[Barton Warren Evermann]] commissioned the 1904 Arizona survey.
- **Sites:** Chamberlain recorded collections and observations at more than 20 locales, numbered Stations I to XXII in Minckley's system. The survey was organized along the Colorado, Santa Cruz, Sonoita, San Pedro, Gila, San Francisco, Salt and Verde drainages.
- **Editorial method:** Minckley reproduced the text essentially verbatim, added bracketed clarifications, placed footnotes, converted discharge from miner's inches to cubic feet per second (1 miner's inch ≈ 0.025 cfs), and matched Chamberlain's names to modern taxonomy using [[Common and Scientific Names of Fishes]] (Robins et al. 1991).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish fauna at the time of the survey**
- Of the 15 native fishes then recognized from the Gila basin, 12 had type localities that Chamberlain visited. A total of 18 freshwater species is now known from the basin.
- Chamberlain collected or reported 16 native species. Of these, one is extinct, eight are listed as Threatened or Endangered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and eleven are formally listed in Mexico.
- Chamberlain recorded only seven non-native species. More than 40 non-native fishes are now known from the Gila basin, and 84 translocated species were tallied for Arizona as a whole.
- Averages across sites: 5.7 native species were recorded or expected per site (range 1 to 11); Chamberlain caught or noted 3.2 species (range 1 to 10) at sites he physically sampled; in the 1990s only 2.3 species (range 0 to 5) remained at the same localities.
- Minckley notes that 14 of 22 sites or reaches visited in 1904 are now dry except in flood or so altered that native fishes are greatly reduced or extirpated.

**Habitat and water conditions**
- Chamberlain recorded water temperatures in °F, and most sites were 55 to 82 °F. The Monkey Spring outflow was 80 °F, and the Salt River warm spring outlet reached 108 °F.
- Groundwater in the Tucson area was reported to have fallen about 6 ft over the previous two to three years. Minckley notes that pumping has since lowered groundwater beneath Tucson by 200 ft or more.
- Several sites were artificially impounded, such as [[Silver Lake]] on the Santa Cruz River and the artesian ponds near Safford, and many irrigation diversions were temporary brush-and-sand dams.

**Causes of decline (Chamberlain's Discussion and Summary)**
1. **Vegetation destruction** from cattle ranching and overgrazing, leading to increased floods, erosion, sedimentation, temperature extremes, and possible climatic change.
2. **Irrigation** diverting water, creating migration barriers, and sending fish into unscreened ditches.
3. **Mining:** sediment from concentrating mills, use of explosives in fishing, and possible toxic ore-processing releases.
4. **Overfishing and stocking** of non-natives in ponds.

**Modern annotations**
- Minckley argues that predatory and competing non-native species are the most important factor in the continuing decline of native fishes in the American Southwest.
- Arroyo cutting after about 1880 lowered floodplain water tables, and later groundwater pumping and surface-water diversion caused the loss of perennial baseflow. Minckley's footnotes describe the [[Gila River]] and [[San Pedro River]] as drying with the loss of groundwater.
- Surviving native populations are now mainly in isolated headwater or spring habitats, such as [[Monkey Spring]] and [[Fossil Creek]].

## Key Findings

- **Historic baseline:** Chamberlain's 1904 collections document native fish occurrence at 22 stations, and Minckley's Table 1 compares those records with the 1990s samples.
- **Severe decline:** Native fish richness fell from an average of 3.2 species at sampled sites in 1904 to 2.3 in the 1990s, and more than half of the sites visited in 1904 are now dry or greatly degraded.
- **Conservation status:** Eight of Chamberlain's native species are federally listed as Threatened or Endangered, and one is extinct. Eleven are listed in Mexico.
- **Non-native introductions:** Chamberlain recorded seven non-native species in 1904. The Gila basin now holds more than 40 non-native fishes.
- **Speciation and taxonomy:** Chamberlain's voucher specimens were used to revise several taxa. The headwater chub was not recognized until Minckley's 1999 revision, and Chamberlain misidentified speckled dace as loach minnow in the East Verde River.
- **Documented causes:** Grazing, flood and sediment regimes, irrigation diversion, mining effluent, and explosive fishing were all recorded as causes of loss. Minckley agrees that these stressors largely persist.
- **Colorado squawfish and bonytail:** Chamberlain caught bonytail in large numbers in the lower Colorado River, and Minckley reports that the bonytail is now extirpated from the Gila basin and largely restricted to Lake Mohave. Local reports of Colorado squawfish up to 35 lb are supported by archaeological remains.
- **Non-native fish history:** Common carp were established in Arizona by the 1880s. The Yaqui catfish was introduced into the lower Colorado in 1899 and the Monkey Spring reservoir was stocked with it. Black bass and crappie were stocked in ponds around Safford in 1903.
- **Management recommendation:** Chamberlain concluded that restocking of natural streams was unlikely to succeed under existing conditions, and that pond culture offered the only hope, provided ponds were not overstocked.
