# Nearshore Fish Habitat Utilization and Species Associations in Lake Opinicon (Ontario, Canada)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1978 study by Keast, Harker and Turnbull, published in *Environmental Biology of Fishes* (Vol. 3, No. 2), examines how fish use the physically diverse inshore margin of [[Lake Opinicon]], a small eutrophic warm-water lake in Ontario. Using [[Strip Count Method]] snorkel and SCUBA census techniques, the authors quantified fish densities, species composition, and diel movements across 11 inshore habitat types over the 1975 open-water season. The inshore zone held most of the lake's fish biomass; each habitat type supported a characteristic assemblage that remained relatively constant through the season, even as fish biomass declined in late summer when fish moved offshore. Weedbeds were the richest and most abundant habitat, and day–night shifts were marked in several species.

## Background & Description

Fish habitat specialization had received limited study compared with birds and mammals. The authors note that face masks, snorkels and SCUBA had recently made direct underwater counts practical, citing [[Northcote and Wilkie]] (1973) for stream counts comparable to poisoning estimates and [[Werner et al.]] (1977) for depth and habitat zonation of fish in small Michigan lakes. This paper is a companion to an earlier methodological paper on census repeatability (Keast & Harker 1977a).

[[Lake Opinicon]] (890 ha, maximum depth 11 m, half the lake less than 5 m) is ice-covered from December to April. Feeding and growth are largely confined to the May–October period, so the study was restricted to spring through autumn. The inshore margin consists of alternating segments of weedy inlet, sandy shallows, rocky shelf, gravel, boulders, vertical cliff and clay, whereas the central basin is homogeneous with low fish biomass.

Eleven habitat categories were defined (Table 1) by substratum, shoreline exposure, percent vegetated bottom, water characteristics and plant species number. These included weedy shallow, weedy deep (2–3 m, added to capture [[Bluntnose Minnow]] and [[Golden Shiner]] habitat), mixed bottom, Myriophyllum shelf, vertical cliff, rocky shelf, big stones, gravel, sandy sheltered, sandy exposed and exposed clay. The study area covered about 71,360 m² of inshore habitat (1,327.7 m of shoreline strip), with offshore depth zones ranging from 2–3 m to 5.5–7.0 m.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Censuses used parallel strips marked with yellow nylon ropes set at depths of 1.2–1.8 m along a 1,400 m shoreline from Birch Bay to Cow Island, subdivided into 50 sub-strips (10–60 m each). Counts were made at 0800–1000 and 1600–1700 h (day) and 2200–2400 h (night), with the full strip traversed 24 times per 10-day sampling period. Four periods were sampled: early spring (May 15–24), early weed growth/spawning (June 15–25), late summer (August 5–15) and autumn (September 5–15). Results were expressed as fish per 1,000 m², and selective seining confirmed abundance ratios.

**Seasonal and habitat patterns.** Numbers peaked in May in nearly all marginal habitats, typically halving by August, with September lower still. [[Bluegill]] set the overall pattern; only exposed clay, with uniformly low numbers, lacked a seasonal drop-off.

**Offshore shift.** The 2–3 m zone (about 70,000 m², where Myriophyllum beds develop) roughly matched the entire marginal strip in area, and the 3–4 m zone covered about 109,000 m². The loss of fish inshore in late summer was closely matched by a gain offshore.

**Diel behaviour.** Several species changed distribution between day and night, including [[Pomoxis nigromaculatus]] (Black Crappie) and [[Ictalurus nebulosus]] (Brown Bullhead), which fed in deeper or shallow areas after dark respectively.

**Statistical approach.** Habitat differences were tested with Friedman two-way ANOVA and a paired-observations comparison of means for the five most distinct habitats (weedy shallows, weedy deep, gravel, rock shelf, sandy sheltered). Diversity was measured with the [[Shannon-Wiener Index]].

## Key Findings

- **Biomass concentration:** The bulk of Lake Opinicon fish biomass lies in the inshore zone, and each habitat type supported a characteristic, seasonally stable assemblage.
- **Weedbeds are richest:** Weedy shallow areas held the highest numbers and greatest species and year-class diversity (1,055 fish per 1,000 m² in May, falling to 263 in September). Weedy deep areas were initially highest (1,199 per 1,000 m² in May).
- **Habitat specialists vs generalists:** [[Notropis heterodon]] (Blackchin Shiner) was almost entirely restricted to weedy shallows (7–27% of fish there). [[Lepomis macrochirus]] (Bluegill), the commonest species, occurred in all inshore habitats and made up 45–93% of the fish at most sites. Bluegill of 40, 60 and 80 mm were confined to weedbeds.
- **Diversity:** Shannon-Wiener values were highest in weedy areas (weedy deep 2.44–2.61; weedy shallow 2.75–3.21 in June and August) and lowest on exposed sites such as sandy exposed (about 1.54) and vertical cliff (about 1.55–1.62).
- **Diel shifts:** In June, bluegill numbers were lower at night in weedy areas but higher in rocky and sandy areas, indicating movement from cover to exposed areas after dark. By August, most habitats showed no day–night difference, reflecting a permanently offshore fraction of the population.
- **Species-specific diel behaviour:** Rock bass dispersed to sand and clay areas to feed at night; black crappie rested in shallow weedy areas by day and fed in deeper water at night; brown bullhead moved from deeper water into shallows after dark.
- **Pumpkinseed breeding:** Sandy sheltered areas were a major pumpkinseed habitat, where they made up nearly half of fish in June during spawning, although they were only 2–15% of the total inshore elsewhere.
- **Offshore compensation:** Fish numbers offshore increased as inshore numbers fell seasonally, indicating a near-complete redistribution of the population.
- **Habitat distinctiveness:** Comparable habitats (gravel, stones, rocky shelf) had similar assemblages, whereas weedy deep and sandy sheltered differed significantly in most comparisons (Table 7).
- **Conclusions:** The authors conclude that inshore margins form a mosaic of habitats whose complexity of community and seasonal utilization is shaped by cover needs for small fish, spawning requirements, and predation risk. They note that whether these associations are cohesive enough to recur in other lakes remains untested.
