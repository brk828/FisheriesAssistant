# Snail Control by Fish: An Explanation for Its Failure

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

Fish are widely proposed as biological control agents for freshwater snails that host schistosomes, but field trials have been rare and inconsistent. This review focuses on the East African cichlid [[Astatoreochromis Alluaudi]], the most studied snail-eating fish, and argues that its poor performance in snail control is largely explained by its foraging behavior and by plasticity of its [[Pharyngeal Jaws]]. In aquaculture settings, fish feed on abundant, more profitable food and so rarely switch to snails. Fish with reduced jaws, such as those reared in ponds, cannot crush hard-shelled snails efficiently. The authors conclude that research should shift toward integrated snail control rather than searching for a single fish predator that will eradicate snail hosts.

## Background & Description

An estimated 200 million people are infected worldwide by the five known species of human [[Schistosomiasis]] parasites, which are transmitted by freshwater snails. Several other trematode parasites affect fish and cattle, causing serious economic losses. Although effective medicines exist, rapid reinfection after treatment and the high cost of repeated medication limit the long-term value of treatment campaigns, so snail host control often remains necessary.

Three main approaches to [[Snail Control]] are recognized:

1. **[[Molluscicides]]**: expensive and toxic to fish and other useful organisms.
2. **Habitat modification**: such as removing vegetation or concrete-lining ponds and canals. This is expensive and usually limited to man-made environments.
3. **[[Biological Control]]** by fish: often proposed for [[Aquaculture]] ponds, irrigation canals, and [[Rice-Fish Culture]].

Early research on snail-eating fish was unsystematic, so no consistent body of knowledge developed. Field trials with species such as [[Black Carp]], [[Shellcracker Sunfish]] (*Lepomis microlophus*, which successfully controlled snails in [[Puerto Rico]]), and African cichlids including *Astatoreochromis alluaudi* and [[Serranochromis Mellandi]] have reported varying success.

### Astatoreochromis alluaudi

This haplochromine cichlid is common in [[Lake Victoria]] and several other East African lakes and rivers. In Lake Victoria it lives mainly in the littoral zone and feeds on the thick-shelled snail [[Melanoides Tuberculata]], crushing the shells with its pharyngeal jaws.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Jaw development and diet.** Fish from a small lake near Lake Victoria, where *M. tuberculata* was absent, had less developed pharyngeal jaws and had fed on insects. Laboratory fish raised on [[Biomphalaria Glabrata]] snails, which have thin shells, also failed to develop heavy jaws. Thus both the consumption of snails and the hardness of the shell determine jaw development. Jaw width, measured as the width of the lower pharyngeal elements' horns (Fig. 1), is a practical index for separating heavy-jawed from light-jawed fish.

**Reproductive capacity of snails.** Snail populations are limited more by food availability than by the number of offspring produced, which complicates control. Most schistosomiasis vector snails are pulmonate and depend on oxygen-rich water, typically found on the fringes of reservoirs or on floating vegetation. Snails of the genus *Oncomelania*, the oriental schistosomiasis hosts, are bottom feeders, and the spatial distribution of snails further reduces the efficacy of snail-eating fish.

**Other fish.** Non-cichlid snail-eaters may also be unsuitable. Omnivorous or bottom-feeding species may not forage for snails, so their usefulness in control programs is limited to permanent habitats and to combination with other control measures.

## Key Findings

### Field trials and their outcomes

- **Kenya (Nyanza Province), 1955:** *A. alluaudi* was introduced into earthen dams. It initially reduced some snail species, but results were not conclusive.
- **Kenya, 1986/87:** Eight dam sites were revisited. The fish was recaptured at five sites and was the most abundant species at one. Snails were still abundant at all sites. All fish examined had reduced-type pharyngeal jaws.
- **Cameroon (field experiment, date garbled in scan):** Eight wild-caught *A. alluaudi* imported from Uganda reduced snails in pond trials and could be cultured with [[Nile Tilapia]]. The trial covered only one production cycle. A 1987 reconnaissance found no remaining *A. alluaudi* in Cameroonian aquaculture stations.
- **Gounougou, Cameroon (trial started 1988):** In combination with [[African Catfish]] (*Clarias gariepinus*) and Nile tilapia (*Oreochromis niloticus*), neither the catfish, a known snail eater, nor *A. alluaudi* affected resident snail populations. A minor but significant reduction in snail numbers occurred only in the presence of adult Nile tilapia, an omnivore. This reduction was insignificant for parasite transmission control.
- **Zaire:** Convincing field evidence exists that the riverine cichlid *Serranochromis mellandi* was successful in controlling molluscs in fishponds, irrigation canals, and ricefields.

Further trials in drainage canals and a [[Rice-Fish Culture]] experiment provided additional evidence that the fish could not control snails.

### Three reasons for failure

1. **Foraging behavior and prey choice.** Under the [[Optimal Foraging Model]], a predator ranks prey by profitability, defined as energetic yield divided by handling time. Prey are accepted based on energy content, handling time, and encounter rate. Insect larvae have higher yield than snails, so fish eat snails only when insect encounter rates fall below a threshold. In aquaculture, food is abundant and fish are raised for maximum growth, so they rarely switch to snails. The authors suggest the apparent specialization of Lake Victoria fish on snails occurs only under heavy competition from other fish.
2. **Plasticity of the pharyngeal jaws.** Fish without heavy jaws can still eat snails, but handling times increase, especially for larger snails. Figure 2 shows that pond-reared fish fall within the range of reduced jaw types, and jaw reduction in reared fish is a general trend.
3. **Introduction of wild-caught fish.** The early Kenyan introduction used wild-caught fish from Lake Victoria with heavily developed jaws, so the initial snail reduction probably reflected this wild generation. Later generations, which were pond-reared, had reduced jaws and did not control snails. The Cameroonian trial followed the same pattern: after initial success, the fish was never heard of again, probably because the next generation did not eat snails.

### Conclusions and recommendations

- Snail-eating fish can be effective only when snails are the best available food. Their use in aquaculture is therefore unlikely to succeed where better food is provided.
- Research on [[Astatoreochromis Alluaudi]] and related snail-eating cichlids suggests further investment in this group is not advisable.
- Fish may contribute to snail control in integrated programs, especially where water management reduces snail breeding habitats and where natural or introduced competitors and predators add pressure on snail populations.
- The most promising approach is an [[Integrated Snail Control]] strategy that includes aquaculture, water management, and knowledge of fish foraging, rather than searching for a single fish that will eradicate schistosomiasis intermediate hosts and other nematode parasites.
- Further research on the [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) in irrigation systems is suggested, since its introduction reduces the food available to snails and exposes snail populations to stress. Its impact in Israel was apparently considerable where the environment was made more hostile to snails.
