# Predatory Fish Removal and Native Fish Recovery in the Colorado River Mainstem

#ichthyology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered_species_management

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: The Colorado River mainstem historically supported 10 native freshwater species (Table 1), including three large-bodied species that are the focus of recovery efforts: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, the largest North American cyprinid reaching nearly 2 m), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, both reaching less than 1 m). All three species exhibit longevity exceeding 30 years. Currently, 7 of 10 native species are federally endangered, one is state-listed, and one is of special concern. The native component now comprises <2% of the basin's mainstem fish fauna. [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) is also federally endangered and declining. Wild bonytail are believed extirpated; the last individual was captured from Lake Mohave in the late 1990s. Wild razorback sucker populations have declined to <1,000 individuals basin-wide: approximately 100 in the Green River, 300 in Lake Mead, and 500 in Lake Mohave. Recruitment failure has persisted for nearly 4 decades for both bonytail and razorback sucker.

**Habitat Associations**: Native species were historically widely distributed throughout the mainstem river and associated floodplain habitats. Razorback sucker and bonytail populations temporarily rebounded when Lakes Mead, Roosevelt, and Mohave initially filled, suggesting reservoir habitats can support these species under certain conditions. Aging studies indicate sucker recruitment occurred before predator populations fully established and ceased when predators became abundant. The species historically utilized oxbow communities and floodplain wetlands. Current successful recruitment has been documented only in small (<3 ha) ponds where predators are absent or in larger ponds that were drained and predator populations completely removed.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Not extensively reported. Razorback suckers utilize spawning areas in reservoirs, and repatriated individuals show up on these spawning grounds, indicating site fidelity or homing behavior.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Predation by nonnative species is the primary factor preventing native fish recruitment. Initial releases of razorback suckers were lost to resident [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) within hours. The majority of introduced fish species (>80 species introduced basin-wide), including their young, plus nonnative crayfish and frogs, contribute to predation losses. Predation experiments comparing larval razorback sucker survival (44,000/ha) among three sunfish densities (14, 71, 354/ha) showed complete mortality after 4 months at high predator densities, even with supplemental feed. Suckers survived only when predator numbers were reduced by >80%. Razorback suckers are being lost when only a few days old, implying predation by small or intermediate-sized predators, not just large adults. Studies suggest predation occurs from a much broader host of species and life stages than currently acknowledged.

**Environmental Drivers**: Spring run-off is now captured by upstream storage reservoirs (basin water storage exceeds 5× annual flow), fundamentally altering historic flow regimes. These reservoirs flood 1,750 km² of floodplain. Flow modification and reservoir construction created conditions favoring nonnative predators. Channel catfish and [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) were introduced in the late 19th century; by 1935, [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), and other centrarchids were common while natives had become rare. Physical habitat destruction combined with predation drove native fish declines beginning in the lower mainstem by the mid-1930s, progressing upstream over subsequent decades.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: All mainstem native fishes continue to decline despite nearly 3 decades of preventative programs. Colorado pikeminnow were extirpated from the lower basin by 1975; small populations persist in the upper basin but are declining. Wild bonytail are believed extinct in the wild. Razorback sucker populations declined from >100,000 in Lake Mohave to <1,000 basin-wide. The population was predicted to die off near the turn of the century based on longevity studies and recruitment failure. Humpback chub and Colorado pikeminnow populations are also declining. Native fish in the lower mainstem became rare by the mid-1930s; their decline progressed upstream with brief resurgences when mainstem reservoirs initially filled.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Predation by nonnative species is the primary threat. After 10 years and removal of >1.5 million predators, no positive response from the native fish community has been observed. Recruitment failure has persisted for nearly 4 decades. Stocking survival is extremely poor: <200 of 12 million razorback sucker fry stocked from 1981-1991 were ever recaptured. Recent bonytail returns averaged only 1.2 fish/year from 205,000 stocked. Razorback sucker repatriation success in Lake Mohave is approximately 2% (1,400 of 58,000 stocked by 2002). Lake Havasu stocking shows 5-12% survival (1,600-3,600 of 30,000 stocked). A 58% predator biomass removal in a 1.3-ha backwater resulted in only 0.09% survival of 10,000 stocked 7-cm razorback suckers after 1 year. The Colorado River has the dubious distinction of being one of the largest rivers in the world with a totally displaced fish community.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: The author provides specific recommendations for future recovery efforts:

1. **Prioritize removal activities** based on likelihood of reducing and maintaining unwanted community densities by >80%, as studies suggest this threshold is necessary for measurable native fish response.

2. **Construct small (<2 ha) or drainable refuge communities** for dual purposes of conservation and predator/prey research, with size increases based on biological success rather than institutional mandates.

3. **Measure program success** using parameters directly linked to species biology and community response (e.g., stocking goals based on survival rather than hatchery production; habitat alterations based on community response rather than acreage developed).

4. **Develop conceptual models** linking relevant ecosystem and biological components to identify, plan, and measure future removal actions.

5. **Convene independent expert panel** to review what has and has not worked in predator removal programs worldwide and develop basin-wide strategies.

6. **Increase minimum stocking size** to >30 cm for bonytail and 35 cm for razorback sucker to improve survival, though other mortality factors may be at play.

7. **Embrace integrated approach** examining ways to benefit specific species both in and out of the mainstem, using small refuge communities to provide research opportunities while rebuilding riverine stocks.

The document questions whether mechanical predator removal in large (>100 cfs base flow) western streams is technically or politically feasible, suggesting recovery for some mainstem fishes may require innovative management strategies beyond conventional approaches to prevent extirpation or extinction.

**Biological Reference Points**: Predator removal thresholds of >80% reduction appear necessary to facilitate measurable native fish recruitment, based on experimental evidence. One study showed razorback sucker survival only when predator numbers were reduced by >80%. Other studies suggest reductions >90% may be required, with some indicating even greater reductions necessary. Carrying capacity in predator-free Cibola High Levee Pond was estimated at 4,350 fish/ha or 635 kg/ha for fish >15 cm. Davis Cove backwater contained initial predator densities of 1,460 fish/ha (181 kg/ha); 58% biomass removal was insufficient to prevent near-total mortality of stocked razorback suckers.
