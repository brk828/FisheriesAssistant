# Fishes of the Big Blue River Basin, Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 survey by W. L. Minckley, published by the [[University Of Kansas Museum Of Natural History]], documents the fish fauna and angling success of the [[Big Blue River]] Basin in northeastern Kansas. The work was done from March 30, 1957, to August 9, 1958, before the [[Tuttle Creek Dam]] closed and impounded the river. Across 59 stations, the author recorded 48 species, with five more known from literature or museum specimens. One species, *Carpiodes forbesi*, was recorded in Kansas for the first time. The study also includes a [[Creel Census]] of anglers, and it ends with management recommendations for the coming reservoir.

## Background & Description

**Study context.** The Tuttle Creek project sparked debate over the value of the reservoir compared with farmland and settlements to be flooded. The Big Blue supported a catfish fishery "notable throughout most of the State of Kansas." The study's goals were to record fish species and their relative abundance, and to measure angler success before the dam closed. The data were intended as a baseline for future work.

**Tuttle Creek Dam and Reservoir** (data from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Kansas City District):
- Earth-fill dam, 7,500 feet long, maximum height 157 feet above valley floor.
- Two release tunnels, 20 feet in diameter, capacity 45,000 cubic feet per second. Releases above 25,000 c.f.s. were not planned.
- Maximum pool capacity 2,280,000 acre-feet, surface area 53,500 acres, shoreline 368 miles.
- Conservation pool: 15,700 acres, 112 miles of shoreline, 20 miles long.

**Basin.** The Big Blue and its tributaries drain about 9,600 square miles, of which 2,484 are in Kansas. The Big Blue runs 283 miles from Nebraska to its confluence with the [[Kansas River]] near Manhattan. The [[Little Blue River]] is its largest tributary, at 208 miles. The basin is subhumid continental. Annual precipitation is 22 inches in the northwest and 30 inches in the southeast, while evaporation exceeds precipitation by about 30 inches. The mean annual temperature is 53°F. Land use is mainly tilled crops (wheat, sorghum, corn), with beef cattle in some areas.

**Geology.** Permian limestones and shales of the [[Flint Hills]] were deeply incised by the rivers. Pleistocene glacial till, outwash and loess cover much of the watershed. The Big Blue's history is tied to the Kansan glaciation and to later drainage changes involving the Platte River Basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stream types.** The author recognized three kinds of streams:
1. **Sand-bottomed rivers** (Big Blue and Little Blue; typically 150–300 feet wide). These have deep, uniform channels with mostly fine sand bottoms. Gravel occurs rarely on riffles and below dams.
2. **Mud-bottomed streams** (10–60 feet wide), such as the Black Vermillion River and Mill Creek. They have V-shaped channels and clay or mud bottoms. Heavy algal blooms occurred in 1957 and 1958.
3. **Gravel-bottomed streams** (5–30 feet wide, deeply incised). These consist of large pools connected by short riffles. Gradients are steep (Carnahan Creek 33 feet per mile). Flow is usually under 5 c.f.s., and some streams become intermittent in summer.

**Physical and chemical conditions:**
- Big Blue gradient 2.8 feet per mile; Little Blue 5.3 feet per mile.
- Big Blue at Barnston averaged 603 c.f.s.; the Little Blue at Waterville averaged 601 c.f.s.
- Turbidity ranged from 27 parts per million (January 1958) to 14,000 p.p.m. (July 1958). Gravel-stream turbidity was usually under 25 p.p.m.
- pH ranged from 7.2 to 8.4 in summer 1957.
- Surface temperatures ranged from 38°F (January 1958) to 90°F in backwaters (July 1957). Gravel streams averaged 79.5–81.0°F in July and August 1957.

**Riparian and historical factors.** Narrow riparian forests of elm, cottonwood, sycamore and willow line the rivers. Hills are largely bluestem prairie. The author notes a severe drought from 1952 to 1956, which likely reduced fish populations. Fish hatched in 1956 therefore faced low competition when flows returned in 1957, and grew faster than later year-classes.

## Key Findings

**Methods.** Sampling used hoop and fyke nets and wire traps (288 trap-net hours in 1957), experimental gill nets, haul seines, [[Rotenone Sampling]] and an [[Electric Shocking]] unit (115 V, 600–700 W). Relative abundance came from 290 seine-hauls, 26 hours 15 minutes of shocking, and seven rotenone samples. Age was determined from scales and pectoral spines.

**Species richness.** Forty-eight species were collected, and five more were known from earlier records or museums. The annotated list covers the most important taxa, including:
- Gars: [[Longnose Gar]] (abundant in mainstream, usually evaded capture) and [[Shortnose Gar]].
- [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]]: found only in the lower Big Blue. Ripe fish were seen on April 20, 1957.
- Buffalo and suckers: [[Smallmouth Buffalo]], [[Bigmouth Buffalo]], [[Black Buffalo]] (only four specimens), [[River Carpsucker]], and [[Carpiodes Forbesi]] (first Kansas record; 430 mm standard length).
- Minnows: [[Red Shiner]] (most widespread species), [[Sand Shiner]], [[Topeka Shiner]], [[Plains Suckermouth Minnow]], and [[Common Shiner]].
- Catfishes: [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Yellow Bullhead]] and [[Stonecat]].
- Sunfishes: [[Green Sunfish]], [[Orangespotted Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[White Crappie]] and [[Largemouth Bass]].
- Non-native or introduced: [[Carp]], [[White Bass]], and [[Goldfish]] (seen as bait).

**Relative abundance.** The most abundant species was [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), followed by [[Sand Shiner]] and [[Channel Catfish]]. The most abundant sport fishes were channel catfish, black bullhead and flathead catfish. Stream habitat strongly shaped communities:
- Sandy rivers were dominated by channel catfish, river carpsucker, carp and sand-loving species.
- Muddy streams supported fathead minnow, red shiner and creek chub.
- Gravel creeks held upland specialists such as the common shiner, redbelly dace and Topeka shiner.

Faunal composition shifted gradually from downstream to upstream along Carnahan Creek, with a decline in "large-river fishes" and a rise in "upland fishes."

**Spawning behavior.** The author described the spawning of *Notropis lutrensis* in detail. Observations on June 29, 1958, at 78.4°F showed males chasing females in open water, with rival-male conflicts and nest-raiding over sunfish nests.

**Notable records:**
- *Notropis buchanani* (ghost shiner) was the first published record from the Kansas River Basin (two specimens, August 14, 1957).
- *Notropis umbratilis* (redfin shiner) was recorded from one specimen, though it is otherwise common in nearby Kansas River tributaries.
- *Hybognathus nuchalis* from the Big Blue was morphologically intermediate between Walnut River and Wisconsin populations, but closer to the former.
- Two hybrids were found: *Notropis cornutus frontalis* × *Chrosomus erythrogaster*, and *Lepomis cyanellus* × *L. humilis*.

**Age and growth.** Of 438 channel catfish sampled by rotenone in August 1958, 265 were age-0 (average 2.5 inches) and 178 were age-I (average 4.5 inches). Age-II fish from the 1956 year-class averaged 7.3 inches at the second annulus, which the author attributed to reduced competition after the drought.

**Creel census.**
- Sampling covered 53 fishermen in 1957 and 152 in 1958.
- In 1958, pressure averaged one fisherman per 7.9 miles of stream (one per 15.7 miles of shoreline).
- Average catch in 1958 was 0.33 fish per fisherman-day and 0.14 fish per man-hour. Success was 15.8 per cent of fishermen.
- In Area I, catch dropped from 0.91 fish per fisherman-day (1957) to 0.26 (1958). The author linked this to above-normal summer flows.
- Most anglers fished from the bank (93 per cent). The most common baits were worms, doughballs and minnows.
- Seven species were identified from creels, in order: channel catfish, carp, freshwater drum, flathead catfish, shovelnose sturgeon, smallmouth buffalo and river carpsucker.

**Recommendations.**
1. Continue the study of the reservoir and river above and below the dam to track impoundment effects.
2. Expect increases in backwater, creek-mouth and borrow-pit fishes, such as gars, shad, buffalo, carp, sunfish and white bass, plus channel and flathead catfish, as in Oklahoma reservoirs.
3. Do not stock species already present as brood-stock (channel and flathead catfish, bullhead, bluegill, crappie, largemouth bass). White bass is an exception.
4. Introduce [[Walleye]] and possibly [[Northern Pike]] immediately, since these were probably native and may have been extirpated.
5. Allow commercial harvest of non-game food fishes such as "coarse fish" to control populations and benefit sport fishing.
