# Gila Chub (*Gila intermedia*): Status, Habitat, and Threats

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a conservation petition that asks for federal protection of the [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*), a minnow endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]]. The species has been extirpated from New Mexico and survives in only a small number of Arizona sites, probably in decline since the 1950s. The authors attribute the decline to cumulative human impacts: loss and degradation of [[Cienega]] and small-stream habitat, and predation and competition from introduced fishes. They review the taxonomy, natural history, habitat requirements, distribution, and threats, and they recommend listing with designation of [[Critical Habitat]]. The petition is an advocacy document, and its numbers come largely from grey literature and personal communications.

## Background & Description

**Morphology.** The Gila chub is a dark steel-grey, chunky-bodied fish. It is sometimes lighter on the belly or has faint lateral bands, and it lacks a basicaudal spot. Breeding males turn bright red or orange on the lower cheek, lips, fin bases, and caudal peduncle. Adults are reported as reaching 8–10 inches, averaging 5–8 inches. The scales are large and thick, and the fish usually has 8 or fewer dorsal fin rays, 8 or fewer anal fin rays, and 8–9 pelvic fin rays. Total vertebrae number 38–45, usually fewer than 42. Large females occasionally develop a fatty nuchal hump. The species is distinguished from the closely related [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) by its chunkier build, fewer than 80 lateral-line scales, and a head-length/caudal-peduncle-depth ratio of 3.0 or less.

**Taxonomy.** [[Baird and Girard]] (1854) first described the species as *Gila gibbosa* from the Santa Cruz River. The name *gibbosa* is unavailable because of homonymy, and the species was later described as *Tigoma intermedia* (1856). The American Fisheries Society lists it as a subspecies of *G. robusta*, but most authors treat it as a full species. The case for species status rests on [[Rinne 1976]] and [[DeMarais 1986]]. Their arguments cite morphological differences, including scale counts, basal radii, and fin-ray counts, and the fact that contiguous populations of the two forms have maintained their independence. The petition notes that phenotypic intermediates of unknown origin exist, and that no current zones of sympatry are known.

**Natural History.**
- *Phylogeny:* Chubs of the genus *Gila* date to the Mid-Miocene. Rinne (1976) hypothesized that *G. intermedia* arose from southern relatives such as *G. ditaenia*, *G. nigrescens*, and *G. pandora*, and reached the Gila system through stream captures.
- *Reproduction:* Breeding occurs in late spring and summer and may continue into autumn. Large females are attended by smaller males among aquatic vegetation. Egg-to-adult development is not described.
- *Age and growth:* Griffith and Tiersch (1989) measured 113 fish from the Redfield Canyon population, ranging 45–222 mm total length. Scale-based estimates for ages 1–4 were 90, 135, 160, and 183 mm. Four age groups were present, suggesting a lifespan of about 3–4 years and maturity in the second or third year.
- *Diet and behavior:* The species is an omnivore that eats small fish, aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, and filamentous algae. It is highly secretive. Young fish use shallow vegetated or woody cover, subadults use swifter riffles and are active all day, and adults occupy deep pools with undercut banks and overhanging vegetation. Adults feed mainly in morning and evening.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** [[Cienega]] habitat is the most favorable setting for the species. Cienegas are spring-fed, depositional wetlands with stable, relatively warm flows and up to several meters of organic substrate. They lie in the Basin and Range Province, in valleys such as San Simon, Sulphur Springs, San Pedro, and Santa Cruz. Bank vegetation includes sedges, rushes, grasses, and water purslane, and aquatic plants include watercress and water pennywort.

**Water chemistry at one site.** The only detailed habitat data in the document describe the Spring Creek population (Coconino County, 3,300–3,500 ft elevation), taken from Minckley (1983):

**Associated native fishes.** Fish that co-occur with the Gila chub include [[Loach Minnow]], [[Spikedace]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Longfin Dace]], Sonora sucker, and desert sucker. Several species once associated with the chub, including [[Woundfin]], bonytail, [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and Gila topminnow, have been extirpated from the Gila basin.

**Exotic species.** Fishes now found in occupied habitat include yellow bullhead, [[Green Sunfish]], red shiner, and fathead minnow. The document also lists [[Largemouth Bass]], channel catfish, flathead catfish, [[Western Mosquitofish]], and smallmouth bass as predatory or competitive exotics.

## Key Findings

**1. Status and distribution.**
- The species is extirpated from New Mexico. Former New Mexico sites include Duck Creek, San Simon Cienega, and the Tularosa River.
- Current populations are limited to fewer than 10–15 streams in central and southeastern Arizona. Sources differ: the abstract says fewer than 10 sites, while Minckley (1983) says fewer than 15 streams. The Nature Conservancy considers the species abundant in no more than 10 locations.
- Table 1 lists 13 recent survey records across several basins, including Silver Creek, Cienega Creek, Bonita Creek (189 and 224 fish), Redfield Creek (533 fish in 1991), and Sheehy Spring (107 fish in 1977). The document says these numbers are not population estimates. Hot Springs Creek had only 2 fish, and Rye Creek had 1.
- Table 2 lists 20 sites within the historic range where the species was not found in recent surveys. They include O'Donnell Creek, several Agua Fria tributaries, eleven Tonto Creek sites, Double R Creek, and Sonoita Creek.
- Historically the species was widespread in southeastern Arizona, including the Santa Cruz, San Simon, Salt, Gila, Agua Fria, San Pedro, and Verde systems.

**2. Scale of decline.** Of the 41 strictly freshwater fishes native to the American Southwest, 28 (68%) were listed as threatened, endangered, or of special concern by the American Fisheries Society in 1989. Three species were listed as extinct. The Gila chub has been declining since about the 1950s.

**3. Causes of decline.** The authors say no single cause can be identified. They attribute declines to cumulative effects of multiple human impacts, and they state that these causes may be reversible.
- *Habitat degradation and loss:* This is cited by many experts as the leading cause, going back to the 1960s. Key drivers are grazing, water diversion, groundwater pumping, timber harvest, mining, reservoirs, and roads. [[Beaver Trapping]] beginning in the 1830s also reduced productive low-velocity habitat.
- *Grazing:* Cattle grazing has spread from the 1600s onward and is described as triggering overgrazing, loss of riparian vegetation, and arroyo cutting. Grazing continues in drainages where the last populations occur. Effects include compaction, loss of undercut banks, siltation of pools and spawning vegetation, and intense flood-related mortality.
- *Water diversion and groundwater pumping:* Irrigation diverts water and can carry fish into ditches. Dams isolate populations genetically. Lowered water tables have desiccated springs, cienegas, and riparian zones, including the Santa Cruz River.
- *Timber harvest:* Harvest in headwaters increases erosion, sedimentation, flood intensity, and downcutting.
- *Exotic predators and competitors:* The Nature Conservancy named exotic invasion as one of two principal causes of decline. Largemouth bass decimated the Monkey Spring, New Mexico population, and green sunfish were associated with the disappearance of chub from the San Carlos River. In lower Bonita Creek, chub show poor condition, ectoparasites, and disease, while upper-creek fish appear healthy, though some of the same exotics occur in both reaches.

**4. Regulatory inadequacy.** Arizona lists the species as threatened, and New Mexico lists it as endangered. Neither state has protected habitat or created a management plan. The petition criticizes a proposal by the Bureau of Land Management and the Arizona Game and Fish Commission to stock Agua Fria streams with "seed fish" from Silver Creek. It argues that handling wild fish can cause mortality and that transplants without a hatchery program or prior habitat restoration are questionable.

**5. Recommendation.** The petitioners strongly recommend designating critical habitat at the time of listing. This would cover all areas where the species now lives and key unoccupied areas that need restoration for its conservation.

**6. Expert opinion (Appendix A).** Quoted experts agree that loss of habitat, especially spring and cienega modification, arroyo cutting, and dewatering, has been the main problem. They also agree that predation by introduced bass and catfish is a serious threat. Minckley (1985) concluded that listing as threatened was appropriate. A 1992 Desert Fishes Recovery Team recommended listing as threatened or endangered. The Nature Conservancy warned that continued presence in Arizona "could be in jeopardy in the near future."
