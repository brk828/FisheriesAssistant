# Young Fish Distribution in Backwaters and Main-Channel Borders of the Kanawha River, West Virginia

## Executive Summary
This 1987 field study (Scott & Nielsen, 1989) examined habitat partitioning of larval and juvenile fishes between backwaters and main-channel border zones of the impounded [[Kanawha River]], a sixth-order tributary of the [[Ohio River]] in West Virginia. Using 0.5-m plankton nets and 1-m² dropbox quadrat samplers, the authors sampled three dominant taxa — [[Lepomis]] spp. (sunfishes), [[Emerald Shiner]] (*Notropis atherinoides*), and [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) — across 174 plankton samples and 120 dropbox samples from 25 May to 13 July 1987. Results demonstrated strong, taxon-specific habitat partitioning: nest-building *Lepomis* larvae were overwhelmingly concentrated in backwaters (20× denser than main-channel borders), while pelagic-spawning emerald shiners and litho-pelagophilic gizzard shad favored main-channel borders. Within backwaters, *Lepomis* larvae exhibited an ontogenetic habitat shift from open-silt substrata to vegetated substrata as they grew. The study underscores the disproportionate ecological value of scarce backwater habitats in geomorphically constrained (backwater-poor) river systems, drawing a parallel to the functional importance of floodplains in tropical rivers.

## Background & Description
The study did not focus on adult morphology but on **larval developmental staging**, using standardized length-class categories to track ontogenetic shifts in habitat use:

- **Length classes used:**
  - 0–10 mm total length (early larvae)
  - 10+ mm total length (later larvae)
  - Juvenile (specimens with a complete set of fins)
- Identification followed [[Auer 1982]] larval fish identification keys for the Great Lakes Basin, identifying specimens to the lowest feasible taxonomic level.
- **Lepomis spp.:** Smaller larvae (0–10 mm) were morphologically distinguished from later-stage larvae (10+ mm) and juveniles based on fin completion; size-class distribution corresponded strongly with substratum use.
- **Emerald shiner:** Smaller larvae (0–10 mm) dominated catches (~90–100%) in both backwaters and main channel; 10+ mm larvae were rare in both habitats.
- **Gizzard shad:** Larger larvae (10+ mm) constituted roughly one-third of backwater catches but were captured at low densities in the main channel, suggesting differential retention/growth by habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
### Habitat Classification
Three riverine zones were distinguished based on physical characteristics (following [[Balon 1975]] reproductive guild framework):
- **Backwaters:** static, shallow, fine-substrate (e.g., [[Little Guano Creek]], [[Bill's Creek]])
- **Main-channel borders:** low-velocity, shallow, structurally complex
- **Main channel:** high-flow, deep, structurally simple

### Species-Specific Habitat Use
- **[[Lepomis]] spp.** (nest-building spawners): Overwhelmingly dependent on backwaters (70% of catch vs. 5% in main-channel borders); classic lentic-habitat dependence for nesting substratum, depth, and flow conditions. Exhibited an **ontogenetic microhabitat shift**: early larvae used open-water backwater areas, later dispersing to vegetated (*Justicia* spp.) substrata as juveniles — a pattern consistent with reduced predation and increased food availability.
- **[[Emerald Shiner]]** (*Notropis atherinoides*, pelagic spawner): More than twice as dense in main-channel borders as backwaters; consistent with pelagophilic reproductive strategy requiring higher flow/pelagic drift conditions for egg and larval development.
- **[[Gizzard Shad]]** (*Dorosoma cepedianum*, litho-pelagophil): 2.5× denser in main-channel borders, with a bimodal seasonal density pattern (early June and late June peaks); larger larvae disproportionately retained in backwaters, suggesting mixed spawning/nursery habitat use across zones.

### Comparative Riverine Context
The Kanawha River, part of the ancient, narrow-floodplain Ohio River basin, is structurally "backwater-poor" compared to multi-channel, lake-like systems such as the Upper [[Mississippi River]] or seasonally flooded tropical floodplain rivers. Despite scarcity, backwaters in the Kanawha appear to fulfill a disproportionately critical ecological role for nest-building species, functionally paralleling the floodplain-dependent nursery function documented in tropical systems ([[Welcomme 1979]]).

## Key Findings
1. **Strong habitat partitioning by reproductive guild:** *Lepomis* spp. (nest builders) were 20× denser in backwaters than main-channel borders (*P* = 0.0001); emerald shiners (pelagic spawners) were >2× denser in main-channel borders (*P* = 0.01); gizzard shad (litho-pelagophils) were 2.5× denser in main-channel borders (*P* = 0.03).
2. **Ontogenetic substratum shift in *Lepomis*:** Smaller larvae (0–10 mm) occurred almost exclusively over open-silt substrata, peaking in late May; juveniles shifted to vegetated substrata, where densities were 4× higher than over open silt (*P* = 0.002), reaching 35× higher by July.
3. **Seasonal peak timing differed by habitat and taxon:** *Lepomis* peaked in mid-June in backwaters vs. late June in main-channel borders; emerald shiner densities peaked in late June in main-channel borders (14.2 larvae m⁻³) while remaining low and stable in backwaters; gizzard shad showed a bimodal main-channel pattern (early and late June peaks).
4. **Backwaters function as critical nursery habitat despite scarcity:** In backwater-poor river systems like the Kanawha, isolated impoundment-created backwaters (e.g., flooded creek mouths) appear to serve as the primary spawning/nursery habitat for nest-building species, analogous to the ecological role of floodplains in tropical rivers.
5. **Vulnerability of backwater habitats:** Backwaters in navigated rivers are highlighted as especially susceptible to degradation from fine sediment resuspension and boat-wake turbulence, with implications for reduced reproductive success, fish diversity, and abundance if such habitats are lost or degraded ([[Nielsen et al. 1986]]).
6. **Management implication:** The authors call for identification of specific physical/ecological attributes that make backwaters and other atypical riverine habitats effective spawning/nursery areas, citing the Upper Mississippi River backwater rehabilitation program as a model for river systems with fewer, more ecologically essential backwaters.
