# Genetics Management Plan for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This [[Genetics Management Plan]], prepared by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Region 6, Denver) in August 1994 under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]], covers four [[Endangered Species Act]]-listed fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The plan (1) justifies the identification of endangered fish stocks, (2) explains genetics management decisions, (3) sets priorities for species and stocks, and (4) recommends management actions.

Twelve [[Presumptive Stock]]s were identified: three of [[Razorback Sucker]], five of [[Humpback Chub]] and four of [[Colorado Squawfish]]. No wild [[Bonytail]] stock was identified, so restoration will rely on broodstocks derived from [[Lake Mohave]] fish. Assignments were deliberately conservative because every stock is considered potentially important to recovery. The Biology Committee will review the plan annually under adaptive management.

Species propagation priorities were: razorback sucker 1, bonytail 2, humpback chub 3, and Colorado squawfish 4 (1 = highest). Immediate broodstock development covers four stocks, and captive-reared fish are held at facilities such as the [[Horsethief State Wildlife Area]] and [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. The program estimates that about 10.3 acres of active ponds and 144 acre-feet of water per year are needed to maintain these broodstocks.

## Background & Description

The four species are endemic to the Upper Colorado River Basin and were listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act of 1973. Declines were attributed to altered streamflow, habitat change, water temperature shifts, water depletions, barriers to passage, and predation or competition from nonnative fishes.

The plan defines a stock as a randomly breeding group with spatial, temporal, or behavioral integrity from other such groups (Kutkuhn 1981). Stocks are treated as Ecological Significant Units or evolutionary significant units. Stock identification relied on:

- Geographic distribution and abundance, including spawning-season distribution
- Identified spawning sites (ripe adults or larvae collected downstream)
- Known movements and interchange between stocks
- Genetic characterization, using [[Electrophoresis]] (allozymes) and DNA analysis such as [[Mitochondrial DNA]] restriction analysis

Genetic management follows the program philosophy: maintain the genetic integrity of wild and captive-reared stocks and keep captive broodstocks similar in diversity to their founders. The plan is one of three Program documents, alongside the *Genetics Management Guidelines* (Williamson and Wydoski 1994) and case-by-case *Stocking Plans*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Dams and diversions have fragmented habitat, so some stock separation reflects altered rather than natural conditions. Limited interchange occurs, but the plan holds that even small amounts of mixing during spawning can quickly homogenize genetic diversity, so stocks with limited interaction are kept separate.

Movement patterns differ among species:

- **Colorado Squawfish:** the most mobile. The average one-way migration to Yampa Canyon spawning sites was 124.8 km. Green River subbasin migrations averaged 140.7 km (range 32–372.8 km).
- **Razorback Sucker:** moderate movements, up to 86 river miles, with spawning-site fidelity documented.
- **Humpback Chub:** localized movements only, typically a few kilometres.
- **Bonytail:** movements unknown in riverine habitat.

Several stocks are in deep canyons and face specific threats. The [[Black Rocks Canyon]] and [[Westwater Canyon]] stocks may be at catastrophic risk because a railroad runs close to the river, and a chemical spill could eliminate a stock. Irrigation diversion dams, such as [[Redlands Irrigation Diversion Dam]] on the [[Gunnison River]], block upstream movement. Hybridization with [[Roundtail Chub]] and introgression with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] were also identified as risks.

## Key Findings

**Stock status**

- **Razorback Sucker:** critical. Old individuals in current stocks are declining rapidly. The Middle Green/Yampa stock was estimated at 948 fish (95% CI 758–1,138) in 1988, falling to 250–550 in 1993. Some recruitment occurs but is probably insufficient for self-sustaining populations. In the Upper Colorado River reach (RM 152.8–185.1), 206 fish were captured in 1974, none in 1989–1992, and three in 1993.
- **Bonytail:** nearly extirpated from the upper basin. The last reported captures include one specimen in Black Rocks Canyon in 1984 and one adult near the Green River confluence in 1993. Broodstocks must come from Lake Mohave fish.
- **Humpback Chub:** apparently stable but small, isolated populations. Black Rocks Canyon: 597 Gila examined in 1983–1985, of which 45% were positively identified as humpback chub and about 9.5% could not be classified. Cataract Canyon (1985–1988): 22 adults, 56 juveniles, 19 young-of-the-year and 11 larvae.
- **Colorado Squawfish:** generally stable. The Yampa stock had 145 adults captured in 1981–1988. The Gunnison stock is small and declining, with 5 wild adults captured above and 5 below the diversion dam in 1993. Upper Colorado River catch rates averaged about 0.2 fish per hour (1987–1992). Gilpin (1993) concluded the species is not threatened by stochastic forces over 100 years, assuming environmental conditions remain stable.

**Genetic findings**

- Allozyme heterozygosity was 3.1% (Green River) and 5.3% (Colorado River) for Colorado squawfish, compared with a 3.78% average for nine other cyprinid genera (Ammerman and Morizot 1989). Deviations from Hardy-Weinberg proportions suggested reduced gene flow.
- Allozyme analysis of razorback suckers from Etter Pond showed hybridization with flannelmouth suckers. Two of 29 Upper Colorado River razorback suckers carried flannelmouth mtDNA.
- Introgression with flannelmouth sucker was estimated at 0–5%, and with razorback sucker at 0–3% (Buth et al. 1987).
- About 270 suspected razorback larvae from the Green and Colorado rivers were confirmed by DNA analysis (Muth 1994, personal communication).

**Priorities and recommended actions**

- **Razorback Sucker (priority 1):** all stocks assigned priority 1. Middle Green/Yampa stock: continue broodstock development using 25 paired matings (50 fish) to keep inbreeding near 1%. Upper Colorado River stock: refuge broodstock for augmentation and restoration. Colorado River Arm of Lake Powell: monitor at least every five years.
- **Bonytail (priority 2):** develop a broodstock from 25 paired matings of Lake Mohave fish for restoration. Broodstock fish were originally derived from five pairs collected in 1976–1981.
- **Humpback Chub (priority 3):** Black Rocks Canyon and Westwater Canyon stocks assigned priority 1 within the species. The plan recommends placing 25 adults from Black Rocks Canyon in a genetic refuge at Horsethief. The Executive Summary states 30 fish, an inconsistency with the body text. Yampa River stock: priority 2, evaluate hybridization with roundtail chub.
- **Colorado Squawfish (priority 4):** the Upper Colorado River stock (upstream of Westwater Canyon) is the top stock priority. Its broodstock comprises 17 family lots, with 8 further matings planned to complete a 25-lot 5 × 5 di-allele cross. The Redlands fishway, scheduled for 1995, is expected to allow Colorado River squawfish to repopulate the Gunnison River, with augmentation considered if it fails.

**Monitoring**

Ongoing monitoring relies on the [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]]. The plan recommends three-year or five-year cycles for several stocks, and genetic reports were scheduled for completion in December 1994, with *Gila* mtDNA work due in 1997.
