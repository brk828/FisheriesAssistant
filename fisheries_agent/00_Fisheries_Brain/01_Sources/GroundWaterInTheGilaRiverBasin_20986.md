# Ground Water in the Gila River Basin and Adjacent Areas, Arizona: A Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hydrogeology

(Note: this document is a hydrological report, not an ichthyological study. Fish are not discussed; the tag line follows the required format while the content concerns groundwater.)

## Executive Summary

This 1952 U.S. Geological Survey open-file report, prepared with the [[Underground Water Commission of Arizona]] and the [[Arizona State Land Department]], summarizes ground-water conditions in fifteen areas of the [[Gila River Basin]] plus several adjacent basins. Ground water is held mainly in alluvial fill deposited in structural troughs between mountain ranges. Groundwater use in Arizona rose from 2,400,000 acre-feet (1946) to 3,750,000 acre-feet (1951), a gain of more than 50 percent. Water tables are declining in heavily pumped areas, most severely in [[Pinal County]] and [[Maricopa County]], at up to 10 feet per year. Deep wells have been drilled to compensate, but the deep aquifers are part of the same supply and often yield less water per foot of drawdown. Water quality is generally suitable for irrigation, with local exceptions where salts or fluoride are high, and salt balance is an emerging concern.

## Background & Description

The report is an open-file summary (Tucson, October 1952) by L. C. Halpenny and others, not reviewed for editorial or stratigraphic conformance. It draws on Geological Survey work from 1890 to 1952; systematic ground-water investigations began in 1939 under cooperation with the State of Arizona, with the State Land Department as cooperating agency from 1940.

The report is organized in three parts:

- **Part I, General description:** regional hydrology, geology, occurrence, quality of water, and data collection methods (well records, water-level measurements, pumpage inventories, pumping tests, seepage measurements).
- **Part II, Individual areas:** detailed chapters on about two dozen basins and areas, each with geology, hydrology, problems and a summary.
- **Part III, Problems:** interrelation of ground-water areas, ground-water and surface-water interactions, transpiration by [[Phreatophytes]], industrial and irrigation use, and additional studies.

The fifteen areas in the Gila River system are Duncan, Safford, San Simon, Upper San Pedro, Lower San Pedro, Aravaipa Creek, Upper Santa Cruz, Lower Santa Cruz, Salt River Valley, Rainbow Valley-Waterman Wash, McMullen Valley, Harquahala Plain, Gila Bend, Palomas Plain and Wellton-Mohawk. Data are also given for Ranegras Plain, [[Willcox Basin]] and [[Douglas Basin]], and for other irrigated areas such as the St. Johns, Hunt and Springerville-Round Valley areas, Big Sandy Valley and Virgin River Valley. The report includes about 26 maps and 30-plus illustrations, plus tables of well records, logs, and water analyses, which are summarized here rather than reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Recharge sources.** Ground water in these basins comes mainly from infiltration of runoff along mountain fronts and seepage from irrigation water on cultivated land. Other sources include underflow from tributary washes, seepage from the [[Gila River]] (which can reverse direction under heavy pumping), upward leakage from older alluvium along faults, and minor direct rainfall.

**Natural discharge and phreatophytes.** Native phreatophytes, notably [[Saltcedar]] and [[Mesquite]], consume large volumes of shallow ground water. In the Safford basin about 9,300 acres of bottom-land vegetation used about 28,000 acre-feet per year (1944 study); the total bottom-land use in the valley was estimated at 50,000-60,000 acre-feet per year. In the Willcox basin mesquite transpiration was estimated at 10,000-15,000 acre-feet per year, and in the Lower San Pedro basin evapotranspiration was estimated at about 35,000 acre-feet per year.

**Water quality and salinity.** Most ground water is suitable for irrigation, but the older alluvium is often highly mineralized. Hot springs add large salt loads (for example, Clifton Hot Springs at about 9,000-25,000 tons of salt per year in the Safford basin). Fluoride above 1.5 parts per million is common in several areas and can cause dental fluorosis in children. Nitrate (47 ppm in one Safford well) and boron (1-6 ppm in places) are noted as local hazards.

**Ground-water and surface-water links.** The Gila River and its tributaries are intimately connected with ground water. Perennial streams and large-seepage streams show no sustained water-table decline, whereas pumping can reverse gradients and alter river flow.

## Key Findings

**Regional trends**
- Arizona ground-water use rose from 2,400,000 acre-feet (1946) to 3,750,000 acre-feet (1951).
- Water tables are declining in the heavily pumped areas of Pinal and Maricopa Counties, up to 10 feet per year at the peak.
- Deep wells were drilled to offset falling yields; their water is part of the same basin supply, and they generally yield less per foot of drawdown.
- The ground-water areas are interrelated, although subsurface barriers limit movement in places.

**Area-specific results**
- **[[Duncan Basin]]** (Greenlee County): Recent alluvium averages about 90 feet thick; underlying storage estimated at about 165,000 acre-feet; pumpage rose to 44,000 acre-feet by 1951; no persistent net decline found. Fluoride is a concern.
- **[[Safford Basin]]** (Graham County): about 300,000 acre-feet in underlying storage in Recent alluvium. Irrigation pumpage reached 125,000 acre-feet in 1951, or about 250,000 tons of salts at 2 tons per acre-foot. Irrigation wells numbered 680 in 1952. The report recommends studies of salt balance and hot-spring salt removal.
- **[[San Simon Basin]]** (Cochise County): artesian aquifers beneath a confining blue clay (about 400 feet thick). Estimated available storage 8,840,000 acre-feet. Artesian head declined about 10 feet between 1940 and 1951, and about 6,000 acre-feet was discharged from flowing and pumped wells in 1951. About 1,100 acre-feet of artesian flow was wasted.
- **[[Upper San Pedro Basin]]** and **[[Lower San Pedro Basin]]**: Lower basin surface inflow about 45,000 acre-feet per year at The Narrows; about 20,000 acre-feet pumped from Recent alluvium in 1952; latent storage in Recent alluvium about 200,000 acre-feet; older alluvium storage about 4,000,000 acre-feet (up to 12,000,000). Fluoride is locally above 1.5 ppm.
- **[[Upper Santa Cruz Basin]] and [[Lower Santa Cruz Area]]** (Pima, Pinal, Santa Cruz Counties): heavy pumping with progressive water-table decline; separate district analyses for Avra-Marana, Eloy, Casa Grande-Florence-Sacaton and Maricopa-Stanfield.
- **[[Salt River Valley]]** (Maricopa and Pinal Counties): cumulative net water-level change tracked against pumping; salt-balance analysis started. Surface water diverted and water pumped were tabulated for 1946-61.
- **[[Willcox Basin]]** (Cochise and Graham Counties): about 14,000 acres irrigated with 38,000 acre-feet pumped in 1951; water table declined about 18 feet in the Stewart district between 1946 and 1951; underlying storage in the Stewart district estimated at about 440,000 acre-feet; annual net draft on storage about 20,000 acre-feet.
- **[[Douglas Basin]]** (Cochise County): water-table decline mapped for spring 1952; recharge and storage data limited.
- **[[Gila Bend Basin]]** and **[[Wellton-Mohawk Area]]** (Maricopa and Yuma Counties): summarized with well yields, water-level fluctuation and quality; additional study recommended for Gila Bend.

**Management and problems identified**
- Lining canals could increase surface-water efficiency but may reduce recharge.
- Eradication of nonbeneficial phreatophytes was not yet shown to be economically feasible; replacement by low-water-use plants was proposed.
- Need for further study of surface-water and ground-water relationships, salt balance, artesian aquifers, fluoride sources and phreatophyte water use.
- Flood-control dams on the San Pedro River should be evaluated for ground-water effects before construction.
- Uncontrolled artesian flow should be capped or beneficially used, and artesian wells should be properly cased.
