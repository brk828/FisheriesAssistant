# The Ecology of Interfaces: Riparian Zones

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-ecology

## Executive Summary

This draft review by [[Robert J. Naiman]] and [[Henri Decamps]] (University of Washington and CNRS, Toulouse), submitted to the *Annual Review of Ecology and Systematics*, treats [[Riparian Zones]] as a class of ecological interface (ecotone) between terrestrial and aquatic systems. It defines interfaces through the rate of change, the contrast between adjacent patches, and within-interface homogeneity; it then synthesizes physical controls (hydrology, geomorphology, light, temperature, fire), life history strategies of riparian plants, biotic patterns, the role of woody debris, litter and nutrient processing, biodiversity and exotic invasion, wildlife habitat, human alteration, and management tools. The text is an ecosystem and vegetation review; fish are mentioned only indirectly (as consumers affected by riparian plant communities and instream habitat), so no fish species are reported as subjects of study.

## Background & Description

- **Scope:** The review focuses on riparian zones associated with streams and rivers because they encompass most characteristics considered important for interfaces ([[Ecotone]], [[Interface Theory]]).
- **Terminology:** "Riparian" derives from the Latin *riparius* ("of or belonging to the bank of a river"). Transition zone, ecotone and boundary are used synonymously in the review.
- **Definition:** The riparian zone comprises the stream channel and the terrestrial landscape from the high water mark toward the uplands, where vegetation is influenced by elevated water tables, flooding, or soil water-holding capacity.
- **Width variation:** Riparian zones are small in numerous headwater streams embedded in forest; wider as a distinct band in mid-sized streams (width set by >50 yr channel dynamics and annual discharge); and largest on large streams with floodplains, oxbow lakes and lateral migration.
- **Management delineation:** [[Riparian Buffer Zones]] are defined distances from streams within which land use is restricted; delineation increasingly uses [[Geographic Information Systems]] and Digital Elevation Models.
- **Plant functional types:** Four categories are proposed: Invader (wind-disseminated propagules colonizing alluvial substrata), Endurer (resprouts after stem or root breakage), Resister (withstands flooding or desiccation), and Avoider (lacks adaptation to specific disturbances).
- **Example species:** [[Sitka Willow]] (*Salix sitchensis*), [[Scouler's Willow]] (*Salix scouleriana*), [[Sitka Spruce]], [[Cottonwood]] (*Populus*), [[Bald Cypress]] (*Taxodium distichum*), [[Water Tupelo]] (*Nyssa aquatica*), [[Coast Redwood]], [[River Red Gum]] (*Eucalyptus camaldulensis*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical controls**
- Hydrology, interacting with local geology, is described as hierarchically the most important control.
- [[Brinson Model]] (1990): flood power and frequency are inversely related. High-power, low-frequency floods shape features persisting hundreds to thousands of years (oxbow lakes, relic levees); medium-power floods shape structures lasting tens to hundreds of years; low-power, high-frequency annual floods drive seed germination and seedling survival.
- Cottonwood (*Populus angustifolia*) in southwestern Colorado: good seedling years occur about every 3.4 years versus stand-origin years about every 10–15 years.
- Older cottonwood, river red gum and other mature riparian trees use groundwater rather than creek water, probably because it is more reliable in drought.
- Riparian evapotranspiration can alter discharge, producing diel flow fluctuations.
- Sediment erosion and deposition rates range from a few millimeters to several meters annually; lateral channel migration ranges from centimeters to tens of meters per year.
- Understory light is often <2% of full sunlight in the forest interior.
- Fire is rare in humid riparian zones but significant in dry regions; in the lower Colorado River nearly 40% of riparian vegetation burned in 12 years.

**Biotic patterns and productivity**
- Forman (1995) recognizes eight common shapes for riparian corridors.
- Aboveground riparian forest biomass: 100–300 t/ha, with leaves 1–10%. Belowground biomass: 12–190 t/ha, ranging 5–120% of aboveground.
- Aboveground production: 6.5–21.4 t/ha/yr; litterfall averages 47% of annual production.
- Forests in the southeastern United States and humid tropics tend toward greater stem density and basal area than arid or northern regions.

**Woody debris**
- Debris piles reach up to 160 per km of stream bank in temperate forests; density is substantially reduced in tropical regions where termites are prevalent.
- Some wood is very old: radiocarbon dates on logs in a Tasmanian stream ranged from present to 17,000 years BP.
- [[Woody Debris]] piles provide energy dissipation, trapping of materials, and habitat formation.

**Organic matter and decomposition**
- Litter sources to temperate streams: ~500 g AFDM/m² in small and medium streams to ~50 g AFDM/m² in larger rivers; above 1000 g AFDM/m² in tropical streams.
- Coarse particulate organic matter (CPOM, >1 mm) declines with stream size. In a 673 km² undisturbed Quebec watershed, annual litterfall declined from 307–539 g AFDM/m² (first order) to 15–17 g AFDM/m² (sixth order).
- Breakdown rates (k, d⁻¹) for [[Beech]] (*Fagus silvatica*) and [[Willow]] (*Salix alba*) leaves in the Garonne basin, France: beech 0.0052 and willow 0.0147 in a third-order mountain stream; beech 0.0027 and willow 0.0094 in a seventh-order lowland river.
- Fungal biomass may account for about 95% of total microbial (fungi + bacteria) biomass associated with organic matter.

**Nutrient filtering and biogeochemistry**
- Riparian buffers can remove 80–90% of sediments leaving agricultural fields (North Carolina).
- Grassy areas trap more than 50% of upland sediments when overland flow is <5 cm deep.
- Denitrification rates of 30–40 kg N/ha/yr were measured in natural riparian forests in the United States; Lowrance et al. (1984) estimated an average of 31 kg N/ha/yr in the top 50 cm.
- Riparian denitrification varied from 1.4 to 295 kg N/ha/yr across sites.
- In an Illinois experimental marsh study, phosphorus concentrations decreased 64–92% (low-flow) and 53–90% (high-flow) over three years; inflow averaged 176 µg P/L.

**Biodiversity and invasions**
- Exotic plants made up 24% of about 1400 riparian species along the Adour River (France) and 30% of about 850 species along the Mackenzie River (Oregon).
- *Tamarix* spp. and *Impatiens glandulifera* are identified as invasive exotics; the review states clear data supporting or refuting their impacts do not exist.
- Mimosa nigra in northern Australia: areal doubling time 1.2 years (1980–1986) versus 6.7 years at the regional scale.
- Riparian forests in Central America average 52 tree species/ha (trees >10 cm dbh); Amazonia and southeast Asia reported 225 and 283 tree species/ha respectively.

**Wildlife and habitat**
- Lower Mississippi River riparian habitats are seasonally associated with >60 mammal species, about 190 reptile and amphibian species, and about 100 bird species.
- 82% of breeding birds of northern Colorado occur in riparian vegetation.
- Bird communities respond to woody strip width: 2 m strips permit only reduced populations; widths >25 m on each bank are needed for sensitive species. Keller et al. (1993) surveyed 117 corridors (25–800 m wide) and found probability of occurrence increased most rapidly between 25 and 100 m.
- White-tailed deer use riparian zones almost twice as much as non-riparian areas.
- Beaver, moose, elk, brown bear, hippopotamus and warthog modify riparian structure through damming, browsing, trailing and digging.

**Human alteration**
- Flow regulation favored redgum invasion along the River Murray, Australia, expected to cause complete extinction of grass plains in the near future.
- Declines of cottonwood forests across western North America are linked to drought-induced mortality from abrupt flow reductions and lowered water tables.
- After the Garrison Dam (completed 1953), poplar-willow regeneration sites progressively disappeared downstream on the Missouri River; on the Platte River, poplar-willow communities expanded after dam construction.

## Key Findings

1. **Interfaces defined:** Interfaces are transition zones where structural or functional properties change discontinuously; they are characterized by rate of change, contrast between adjacent patches, and within-interface homogeneity.
2. **Riparian zones are complex:** They are among the most diverse, dynamic and complex biophysical habitats on the terrestrial part of Earth, and serve as a framework for understanding fluvial community organization.
3. **Hydrology dominates:** Hydrology (with local geology) is hierarchically the most important physical control on riparian vegetation.
4. **Plant strategies:** Riparian plants are classified as Invader, Endurer, Resister, or Avoider; hydrochory (water dispersal) is important, with bald cypress and water tupelo relying more on water than wind.
5. **Woody debris role:** Large woody debris redirects flow, traps materials, increases residence time and provides habitat; removal experiments increased erosion and export rates.
6. **Litter inputs:** Riparian litter is a major nourishment source for streams, with input declining as stream size increases; laterally transported litter may be qualitatively important despite forming ≤10% of total input.
7. **Nutrient filtering:** Riparian zones act as sinks for sediment-bound nutrients and nitrate through denitrification and uptake, but can become saturated or act as sources of dissolved phosphorus under some conditions.
8. **Corridors and invasions:** Riparian corridors facilitate exotic plant movement, though evidence for terrestrial animal dispersal is limited.
9. **Management:** A multi-species buffer model is proposed with three zones: a permanent forested zone (~10 m), a shrub-and-tree zone (up to 4 m, managed for biomass), and a herbaceous zone (up to 7 m). Management effectiveness depends on catchment-level land practice and hydrology.
10. **Future needs:** The authors call for linear extensive mapping (10–20 m resolution), site-specific intensive research, catchment-scale studies, and flexible adaptive management to address thresholds and synergistic stresses.
