# Effects of Heated Discharges on the Temperature of the Thames Estuary

#marine-biology #estuarine-hydrography #thermal-pollution

## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper uses a theory of tidal mixing to estimate how heat from [[Power Stations]] and other sources has raised the water temperature of the [[Thames Estuary]]. The authors reconstruct a "[[Basic Temperature]]" for the estuary, meaning the temperature expected without artificial heating. They do this by relating historical water temperatures to air temperatures at [[Greenwich]], [[Teddington Weir]] and [[Crossness]]. Observed temperatures are then compared with those calculated from measured heat inputs, using an empirically derived [[Heat Exchange Coefficient]]. The result is a set of [[Unit Input Curves]] that estimate the temperature rise caused by a given heat input at any point in the estuary. The document does not address fish or other organisms directly. Its relevance to aquatic ecology lies in the temperature and dissolved-oxygen context it provides.

## Background & Description

The work began during an investigation of [[Thames Estuary]] pollution. The authors recognised that temperature might affect water quality, because several electricity generating stations take cooling water from the estuary and return heated water to it. Assessing new plant, or changes in existing discharges, required knowing the rate of heat exchange between the estuary and the air above it.

The study had five parts:

- Measurement of estuary water temperature over several years.
- Estimation of the temperatures that would have existed without heated discharges.
- Estimation of the rate of heat entry.
- Calculation of the rate of loss of excess temperature.
- Prediction of temperature distribution for unit heat inputs at points throughout the estuary.

**Measurement methods.** Between 1949 and 1954, the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] took weekly temperature readings at 6 ft depth, mid-stream, at fifteen or more points over about 50 miles. Early readings used a mercury thermometer in a [[Casella Sampler]]. From November 1952, electrical [[Thermistor]] measurements replaced them. In a November 1952 comparison of forty-eight readings, the thermistor read on average 0.09 °C higher than the mercury thermometer, with a standard deviation of 0.14 °C.

**Tidal and averaging treatment.** The average tidal excursion in the relevant reach is 8 to 9 miles. The authors standardised readings to half-tide positions, then averaged them into quarterly distributions. A three-month averaging period was judged most suitable.

**Historical records.** Several long records were used:

- Kingston-on-Thames, 1855 to 1857, daily at 9 a.m.
- Greenwich and Deptford, 1844 to 1891, from several vessels and stations.
- Teddington, 1915 to 1921 and 1944 onward, from the [[London County Council]].
- Crossness, from February 1885, twice daily.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's framework is physical: heat budgets, mixing and cooling. Biological effects are not measured. Several points are relevant to aquatic conditions:

- **Heat budget.** The total average heat entering the estuary was about 306 × 10⁶ B.Th.U. per day (see Key Findings). The largest share came from power stations.
- **Thermal lag.** Seaward stations responded more slowly to air temperature changes, because the sea has a large thermal capacity. The regression coefficient on air temperature rose from about 1.07 at Kingston to about 1.10 at Crossness, which indicates a larger lag effect seaward.
- **Oxygen.** Temperature data were originally collected partly to calculate the solubility of [[Dissolved Oxygen]]. The authors also noted that oxidation of organic matter releases heat, estimated at about 0.014 × 10⁶ B.Th.U. per ton of oxygen consumed. Biochemical heating was small and was largely omitted from later calculations.
- **Cross-channel gradients.** Near the power stations upstream of [[London Bridge]], heated water hugged the banks, producing corrections of up to about 1 °C in mid-stream readings.
- **Pollution context.** The paper notes that the degree of pollution in the Thames had changed over the past century, which affected how biochemical heat was treated.

## Key Findings

**Historical warming.** The regression models suggest the observed temperature difference between recent years and the late nineteenth century is consistent with the calculated heating. The authors state that for steady air temperatures, the excess of water over air temperature is independent of the existing temperature within the normal range.

**Basic temperature models.** Air-to-water regression equations were derived for each station:

- **Teddington (Kingston data, 1855–57):** standard error of estimate 0.56 °C, compared with 0.68 °C for a simple air-plus-constant model. Observed water temperatures had a standard deviation of over 5 °C.
- **Greenwich/Deptford (1856–66, 1871–79, 1883–91):** standard error of 0.40 °C across seventy-eight quarters, with the form T′w = a + 1.101 Ta − 0.151 A, where A is the monthly change in air temperature.
- **Crossness (1885–95):** T′c = a + 1.098 Ta − 0.272 A, standard error 0.57 °C monthly, or about 0.36 °C for quarterly averages across sixty-two quarters.

**Seasonal excess.** Average observed excess of water over air at Teddington was 0.7, 1.8, 1.3 and 1.4 °C for the four quarters, respectively.

**Heat inputs (1951–54 averages, × 10⁶ B.Th.U. per day):**

| Source | Heat input | Share of total |
|---|---|---|
| Power stations | 228 | about 75% |
| Sewage effluents | 27 | about 9% |
| Industrial effluents | 19 | about 6% |
| Fresh water discharges | 20 | about 6% |
| Biochemical activity | 12 | about 4% |

The total was 306 × 10⁶ B.Th.U. per day. Power stations contributed over 75% of the total heating and more than 80% in summer. Summer power generation was lower than in winter, with quarterly relative values of 114, 90, 85 and 111 percent of the mean.

**Heat exchange.** The authors modelled heat loss from the water surface as proportional to the temperature increment. The estimated average exchange coefficient was 4.0 cm per hour for a three-month period. Trial calculations at 3.7 and 4.5 cm per hour were compared with observations, and 3.7 cm per hour gave the closer fit across ten quarters from 1951 to 1954. The authors concluded there was insufficient evidence that the coefficient varied with temperature, fresh-water flow, season or position. A coefficient of about 2 cm per hour was reported from a brief survey of a river.

**Mixing and cooling.** Roughly one-fifth of excess heat was lost by cooling over three tides. A mixing length of 9 miles was used for two-tide periods.

**Model validation.** The predicted distributions agreed reasonably well with observed quarterly profiles. The largest discrepancy occurred near the temperature maximum, and the authors attributed it partly to cross-sectional variation and to fluctuating flow.

**Unit input curves.** Distribution curves were calculated for a reference input rate at fresh-water flows of 170, 500 and 1500 million gallons per day. For a 500 m.g.d. flow, the rise at the point of input at Teddington was 1.1 °C. The rise from a given input is proportional to the heat rate. Upstream reaches respond roughly in proportion to flow, while downstream reaches are less sensitive.

**Relocation example.** The paper modelled moving the heat discharge from [[Battersea Power Station]] (average 25.5 × 10⁶ B.Th.U. per day in the third quarter of 1954) from 3.6 miles above London Bridge to 20 miles below it. This was done by interpolating and scaling the unit input curves.

**Method limits.** The authors stated that the approach may not transfer well to strongly stratified estuaries. It also needs data from before heating began, which are scarce for most estuaries.
