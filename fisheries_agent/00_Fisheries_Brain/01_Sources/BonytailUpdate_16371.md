## Executive Summary
This document is an internal hatchery management memo (dated 9 June 1998) from Chester R. Figiel to colleagues at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, reporting on the husbandry status of four **Bonytail Chub (BTC)** — *[[Gila elegans]]*, an endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The fish were collected/transported between 19–21 May 1998, pit-tagged, measured, and transferred to an outside recirculating raceway at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] on 5 June 1998. The memo addresses acclimation status (net-mark healing, feeding behavior, disease treatment) and requests coordination for future muscle-tissue sampling and transport to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], a key facility for endangered fish propagation and genetic archiving.

- **Physical condition post-handling**: Net-induced marks/abrasions on the body surface were observed to be healing ("disappearing"), though residual **white scar tissue** persisted at time of reporting — an indicator of handling stress recovery in captive-held broodstock.
- Size range (420–540 mm) suggests these are **subadult to adult** bonytail, consistent with broodstock or reintroduction-candidate individuals rather than juveniles.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- ***Gila elegans*** is a highly endangered, large-river cyprinid historically native to the mainstem [[Colorado River]] and its major tributaries, adapted to swift, turbid, historically unregulated flows.
- The species has suffered drastic population declines due to habitat alteration (damming, flow regulation), thermal regime changes, and predation/competition from introduced non-native fishes — necessitating intensive **captive propagation and refuge programs** such as the one documented here.
- In this managed setting, fish were held in an **outside recirculating raceway**, a controlled semi-natural environment used to simulate riverine conditions while enabling close monitoring, disease control, and conditioning prior to translocation.
- Dietary ecology in captivity: fish were maintained on **live red shiners (*Cyprinella lutrensis*)** as forage; notably, the bonytail had **not yet transitioned to prepared/pelleted trout feed**, indicating a persistence of natural predatory/foraging behavior over formulated diet acceptance — a common challenge in early-stage conservation aquaculture of wild-caught cyprinids.
- Health management: A **formalin/malachite green treatment** was administered, standard prophylactic/therapeutic protocol against external parasites and fungal infections (e.g., *Saprolegnia*) common during post-transport stress periods.

## Key Findings
- Four wild or hatchery-sourced bonytail chub were successfully transported, tagged, and integrated into a recirculating raceway system at Willow Beach between 19 May and 5 June 1998.
- All four fish were individually identifiable via PIT tags, with one confirmed **recapture** (1F777A125E), indicating ongoing longitudinal tracking of this small founder/broodstock group.
- Fish exhibited physical recovery from net-capture trauma but retained visible scarring, an important welfare/health indicator for future handling decisions.
- Fish had not yet acclimated to artificial feed, remaining dependent on live prey (red shiners) — a critical husbandry consideration for long-term captive maintenance and eventual muscle-tissue biopsy procedures.
- A disease treatment (formalin/malachite green) was proactively administered, suggesting standard quarantine/health protocols following transport stress.
- The memo flags an **unresolved logistical need**: coordination of a timetable for **muscle plug (tissue biopsy) collection** — likely for genetic/genotyping purposes — and subsequent transport of these fish to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], underscoring the role of this event in a broader **genetic management and captive breeding program** for this endangered species.
- No weight data were collected during this transport event, representing a gap in the morphometric dataset for these individuals.
