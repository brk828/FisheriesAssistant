## Executive Summary

This 1970 annual report documents the fishery management program on the 1,643,939-acre [[San Carlos Indian Reservation]] in Eastern Arizona for the 1969 season. The reservation supports roughly 100 warm- and cold-water tanks and lakes across an elevation gradient from 2,400 to 8,000 feet, managed by the [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]] in cooperation with the San Carlos Apache Tribe. During 1969, 56,000 trout were stocked by the [[Williams Creek National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Alchesay National Fish Hatchery]], generating 28,891 angler man-days — a 75% increase in fishing pressure over 1968. Poor water conditions (low water levels, high temperatures, low dissolved oxygen) caused significant fish kills at several sites, including a complete kill at [[Deadman Tank]] (trout) and [[Ash Grove Tank]] (catfish), while [[Seneca Lake]] experienced a large non-game fish die-off linked to ash accumulation from emergent vegetation burning. The report emphasizes expansion of stocking programs ([[Brown Trout]], catchable [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]]), aquatic vegetation control needs, and the growing importance of the [[Black River]] fishery for smallmouth bass and catfish.

## Background & Description

- **Brown Trout**: Stocked as fingerlings in spring 1968; demonstrated growth rates exceeding 12 inches within 12 months in most tanks, though growth was inconsistent or fish disappeared entirely in some locations. Noted to be a more wary/difficult species to catch than [[Brook Trout]] or [[Rainbow Trout]], contributing to higher survival under angling pressure.
- **Channel Catfish**: Fingerlings stocked at 2–3 inches in fall 1968 grew to an average catch length of 14 inches by the report period. Older cohorts stocked in 1963 and 1965 were yielding trophy-sized fish up to 12 pounds.
- **Brook Trout**: Growth performance in [[Point Of Pines Lake]] was poor, hypothesized to result from interspecific competition with stocked catchable-sized rainbow trout.
- **Non-game fish** (unspecified species) at Seneca Lake: introduced by anglers over several years, occupying shallow-water margins; largely eliminated in an accidental kill event.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal/oxygen stress**: Multiple tanks experienced critically low water levels in late summer 1969, producing high water temperatures and low dissolved oxygen that drove complete fish kills (e.g., [[Deadman Tank]], [[Ash Grove Tank]]) and partial kills in northwestern tanks above the Nantes Rim (e.g., [[Dry Lake]], where ~50 large trout were trapped and died).
- **Habitat drying**: [[Lower Windy Tank]], [[Tufa Stone Tank]], and [[Quail Mesa Tank]] dried completely during the drought period, illustrating the ephemeral nature of many reservation impoundments.
- **Aquatic vegetation dynamics**: Heavy emergent and submergent plant infestations degraded habitat quality in numerous tanks; at Seneca Lake, Tribal efforts to burn emergent vegetation caused ash accumulation in shallow margins — the likely proximate cause of the non-game fish kill, incidentally benefiting sport fish management.
- **Species compatibility**: Channel catfish were found to be ecologically compatible with trout across most reservation waters, enabling co-stocking in all tanks except [[Point Of Pines Lake]] and [[Dry Lake]] (the latter reserved for catfish-only stocking beginning spring 1970 due to marginal trout conditions in summer).
- **Riverine fishery**: The [[Black River]] (including a portion of the upper [[Salt River]]) sustains a longitudinal gradient of fish communities — upper perennial reaches support trout, while lower reaches host [[Smallmouth Bass]] and channel catfish populations within an undeveloped, deep-canyon wilderness environment.
- **Point of Pines Lake**: A constructed 30-acre lake maintained exclusively as a trout fishery (rainbow and brook trout), with consistently excellent water conditions throughout 1969.

## Key Findings

1. **Fishing pressure surge**: Permit-based estimates show man-days of fishing rose from 16,439 (1968) to 28,891 (1969), a 75% increase, generating $12,295 in permit income (partial-year figures, Jan–Oct).
2. **Fish kill events**: Two complete kills documented — 113 dead trout at Deadman Tank and 132 catfish at Ash Grove Tank — both attributed to low water/high temperature/low oxygen conditions typical of the southern reservation.
3. **Brown trout program success**: Brown trout stocking (spring 1968) proved successful overall, with fast growth and reduced vulnerability to angling, supporting continuation of the program.
4. **Catfish program expansion**: Growing angler enthusiasm and strong growth performance (up to 12 lb specimens) justify continued and expanded channel catfish stocking reservation-wide.
5. **Expanded catchable rainbow trout stocking**: Previously limited to [[Point Of Pines Lake]], [[Ginmie Tank]], and [[Upper Highway Tank]], the program will expand to include [[Phillips Park Tank]], [[Brush Basin Tank]], [[Stove Tank]], [[B.S. Gap Tank]], [[IDT Tank]], [[Cox Corral Tank]], and [[Upper Cienega Tank]], pending evaluation.
6. **Seneca Lake management shift**: Accidental eradication of undesirable non-game fish presents an opportunity to redesign the fishery with catchable trout, catfish, and low-reproductive-potential sunfish, potentially paired with "lure only" regulations to prevent future undesirable introductions; a $500,000 tourism development nearby will increase demand.
7. **Black River/Salt River growth potential**: Increasing angler use of smallmouth bass and catfish fisheries in lower reaches signals need for controlled access road development, campgrounds, and an inter-tribal management agreement with the White Mountain Apache Tribe.
8. **Management infrastructure gaps**: Absence of a systematic creel census (due to funding/staffing limitations) restricts area-specific management; report recommends hiring a dedicated creel census agent and a full-time Seneca Lake caretaker.
9. **Vegetation control needs**: Recommends maintaining an on-site supply of USDI-approved aquatic herbicides (e.g., copper sulfate, Aquathol) for rapid deployment against emergent/submergent vegetation in larger impoundments such as [[Seneca Lake]] and [[Point Of Pines Lake]].
