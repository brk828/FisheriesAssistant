## Executive Summary

This 1992 field study documents a successful on-site propagation and grow-out effort for the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in an isolated backwater at Yuma Cove, [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona/Nevada. Adult broodstock (28 females, 60 males) were stocked into a predator-free, berm-isolated backwater in January 1992. Natural spawning produced larvae that were tracked from ~11 mm total length (TL) in February through sub-adult size (mean 35.4 cm TL) by November — exceptional growth attributed partly to a nutrient pulse from a flash flood. Mark-recapture (multiple-census) methods estimated juvenile abundance at 251–472 fish (Schumacher-Eschmeyer estimate: 329), closely matching the actual harvest of 296–298 fish transferred for further grow-out. Of 88 adults stocked, 94% were recovered alive and returned to Lake Mohave. The study validates on-site "cove-rearing" as a viable augmentation strategy but identifies scale, mortality, water quality, and vegetation management as key constraints to achieving recovery-level production targets (6,000–12,000 sub-adults/year).

## Background & Description

- **Species**: [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), family [[Catostomidae]] — an endangered fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Population context**: Lake Mohave holds the largest remaining razorback sucker population (~60,000 adults per Marsh 1992), composed almost entirely of old individuals ([[McCarthy And Minckley 1987]]) with no detectable recruitment in ~40 years due to predation on early life stages by non-native fishes ([[Marsh And Langhorst 1988]]).
- **Genetic significance**: The Lake Mohave stock has extraordinary genetic diversity (Dowling and Minckley, in press) making it a focal point for species recovery.
- **Management rationale**: Because larval/juvenile stocking elsewhere fails due to predation, and because sub-adults (≥30 cm TL) are relatively predator-immune but costly to hatchery-rear and transport (nearest facility: [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], NM, >1200 km away), on-site (Lake Mohave) propagation and grow-out was explored as an alternative.
- **Site history (Yuma Cove backwater)**:
  - 1984–1987: Three prior attempts at isolating the backwater with a berm and stocking adult broodfish; only one attempt (1986) produced a small surviving cohort before berm breaches allowed predator invasion and fish escape.
  - 1990: [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG) formed (multi-agency: AZGFD, ASU, NPS, NVDOW, BR, FWS) to formalize the on-site augmentation concept, pairing Yuma Cove (spawning/early rearing) with [[Davis Cove]] (netted-off grow-out site).
  - Winter 1990-91: Berm reinforced and backwater deepened; larvae were produced but lost to unknown causes before reaching juvenile stage.
  - 1992: Fourth attempt — the subject of this report — succeeded.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: A naturally isolated backwater at Yuma Cove, adjacent to Arizona Bay, hydrologically connected to Lake Mohave through coarse gravels of a separating berm.
- **Water quality stressors**: Declining backwater water level (tracking falling Lake Mohave elevation), high water temperature (approaching 30°C), and low dissolved oxygen (<1 mg/L in deeper areas) during mid-summer caused significant juvenile stress and mortality.
- **Vegetation**: Dense growths of sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*) and spiny naiad (*Najas marina*) hampered sampling/harvest and contributed to the decision to move fish to [[Davis Cove]].
- **Predation pressure**: Light trapping revealed unexpectedly high densities of predatory odonate (dragonfly) naiads in the backwater, prompting a follow-up experimental study on their impact on razorback sucker recruitment ([[Horn Et Al. In Preparation]]).
- **Nutrient pulse event**: A flash flood on 10 August 1992 introduced substantial organic material, including feral burro (*Equus asinus*) faeces, into the backwater — likely boosting primary and secondary (zooplankton/benthic invertebrate) production and food availability for young razorback suckers.
- **Reclamation**: Ichthyocide (rotenone) treatments (autumn 1984, 1985, winter 1990-91, and 25 November 1992) were used to remove non-native predatory fish and reset the system between propagation cycles.

## Key Findings

### Larval Production & Growth
- First post-larval razorback suckers captured 26 February 1992, averaging 11.0 mm TL (~12–15 days post-fertilization).
- Mean TL increased exponentially through spring; fish transitioned from late post-larval to juvenile stage during May.
- Growth stalled (~nil) for ~2 weeks in mid-July, coincident with falling lake levels, rising temperature, and low dissolved oxygen.
- Growth resumed in August, proceeding approximately linearly through November.
- **Average TL at 9 months post-hatch: 35.4 cm** (≈4 cm/month or 0.028 cm/day linear growth rate); largest individual reached **39.1 cm TL**.
- Cove-reared growth substantially exceeded hatchery-reared growth (Dexter NFH) and was roughly double that observed in central Arizona urban lakes.
- At least 5 ripe males were found among fish removed in November — indicating **sexual maturity at 9 months of age**, notably earlier than the previously youngest documented male maturation age of 2 years.

### Population Abundance Estimates (Mark-Recapture)
- Fin-clip marking conducted 16 June–22 July 1992; three independent multiple-census ([[Ricker 1975]]) methods applied to data from 16 June–29 July:
  - Original Schnabel estimate: **306 fish**
  - Modified Schnabel estimate: **276 fish**
  - Schumacher-Eschmeyer estimate: **329 fish** (95% CI: 251–472)
- Actual total harvest to Davis Cove/reclamation: **296 fish** (298 total including 2 post-poisoning mortalities).
- Mark-recapture estimates predicted **93–111% of actual harvest** — deemed highly accurate.
- Only **64% (34 of 53)** of marked fish were recovered after 22 July, attributed to mortality during poor water quality conditions (high temp/low DO) in June–July; affected fish showed emaciation and heavy *Lernaea* parasite infestation.
- Adjusting the 29 July estimate (329 fish) for 64% subsequent survival predicted a final harvest of ~211 fish (140% of actual 296) — within extended 95% CI (161–302 fish).

### Adult Broodstock Recovery
- 88 adult razorback suckers stocked 27-28 January 1992 (28 females, 60 males); 29% of females and 88% of males were ripe at stocking.
- 87 adults (or their remains) recovered over the season; **83 (94%) captured alive and returned to Lake Mohave**.
- 4 (5%) confirmed natural mortalities.
- 4 individuals recovered without detectable PIT tags were re-tagged.
- Only **1 adult (1%)** remained fully unaccounted for at final reclamation — presumed natural mortality.

### Production Metrics & Recovery Implications
- Backwater production **augmented the stocked adult cohort by a factor of 2.5× (females) to 5× (males)** in terms of sub-adults produced (assuming 1:1 sex ratio among juveniles).
- This level of production is **inadequate** to replace the existing ~60,000-fish adult population within a 5–10 year sustainability window; NFWG would need to produce **6,000–12,000 sub-adults annually** (20–40× the 1992 output).

### Identified Constraints for Scaling Up
1. **Space limitations** in Yuma Cove backwater limit juvenile production capacity; additional habitats need identification.
2. **Mortality between hatching and juvenile stage** represents the largest population loss; poor water quality (June–July) reduced numbers by ~a third, compounded by capture/handling stress.
3. **Aquatic vegetation overgrowth** hampers sampling and harvest efficiency.
4. **Growth disparity**: Hatchery-produced fish stocked into Davis Cove grew markedly slower than Yuma Cove on-site–produced fish through November — cause undetermined (habitat, initial size, non-native competitors/predators in Davis Cove, or other factors).
5. **Lake level fluctuations**: Monthly Lake Mohave water level changes may exacerbate poor water quality in backwaters even at higher elevations; stabilization strategies are recommended.
