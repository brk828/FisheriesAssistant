# Overfishing in a Small Artificial Lake: Onized Lake Near Alton, Illinois

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1945 Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin by [[George W. Bennett]] documents the only case of [[Overfishing]] ever observed by Survey aquatic biologists in Illinois. The study covers [[Onized Lake]], a 2-acre artificial pond near [[Alton Illinois]] that was intensively angled by members of a company fishing club from 1938 to 1941. A [[Creel Census]] recorded hook-and-line catches, [[Hoopnet Sampling]] was done in 1938 and 1940, and a [[Poison Census]] in June 1941 counted and measured the remaining fish. The author concludes the lake was severely overfished, yet most species persisted. Surviving fish grew rapidly after an initially slow first season, and the population was judged able to recover to its carrying capacity once fishing pressure eased.

## Background & Description

The study was produced by the [[Illinois Natural History Survey]], Section of Aquatic Biology. The author argues that the common angler belief that intensive fishing causes declining catches is seldom proven. Of 22 Illinois artificial lakes censused, only one showed a yield decline tied to intensive fishing. Under the strict definition used here, overfishing means removal of the whole fish population's yield faster than natural food can replace it, pushing standing stock below carrying capacity. This is "extremely rare" because it requires very great angling intensity.

Onized Lake lies on a 23-acre recreation tract about 8 miles northwest of Alton, leased from C. W. Terry and used by the Owens-Illinois Glass Company for picnics, softball and fishing. The lake was built in 1933 by damming a steep-sided ravine. It is roughly V-shaped, covers about 2 acres, has a maximum depth of 21 feet near the dam, and an average depth of about 8 feet. Its drainage area is 15 to 20 acres. Aquatic plants, including American lotus and coontail, covered nearly all the limited shallow water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lake behaves like many small, wind-protected ponds. It stratifies from late spring to late fall. Organic matter creates high oxygen demand, so during summer little or no oxygen is present below 3 to 4 feet. On 29 August 1938 surface water was 78.0 °F and dissolved oxygen was 6.0 ppm. At 5 feet and below the oxygen reading was 0.0 ppm, and temperatures fell to 54.7 °F at 21 feet. Water chemistry included total hardness of 91.5 ppm and methyl orange alkalinity of 76.0 ppm.

Drainage from a barnlot raised fertility but produced recreational problems. Blooms of subsurface algae and floating bluegreens (*Oscillatoria*) followed heavy rains, and large bullfrog tadpole die-offs (*Rana catesbeiana*) produced odors that bothered the public. The steep basin and small shoal area also limited habitat for bottom-dwelling bullheads.

Competition is central to the author's argument. Predators of small fish were relatively scarce, and under crowded conditions the young of desirable species could not compete with the young of other species. [[Interspecific Competition]] for food and space was therefore identified as a likely driver of population decline, alongside the effects of angling.

## Key Findings

**Fishing pressure and yield (creel census, 1938–1941)**
- The creel census recorded 3,997 fish weighing 1,152.43 pounds, requiring 7,526.9 man-hours for an average catch of 0.531 fish per man-hour.
- Fishing intensity was 1,414.9 man-hours per acre in 1939, 1,647.3 in 1940, and 634.0 in April–June 1941.
- Hook-and-line yield was 349.95 pounds per acre in 1939, 142.5 in 1940, and 70.9 for three months of 1941. The author states the 1939 yield exceeded any single hook-and-line year recorded for another US artificial lake.
- Average catch weight fell steadily: 0.92 pound (1938), 0.32 (1939), 0.24 (1940), and 0.22 (1941).
- Nine species were caught. Largemouth bass, [[Black Crappie]] and [[Bluegill]] each made up more than 10 per cent of the catch by weight. Bluegills comprised 60.9 per cent of the number and 48.1 per cent of the weight.

**Hoopnet comparison (1938 vs. 1940)**
- Crappies were taken at 23.78 per net-day in 1938 and 6.50 in 1940. Bluegills dropped from 6.00 to 2.75 per net-day.
- Total fish numbers per net-day were 3.37 times higher in 1938 than in 1940, indicating fewer desirable-size fish in 1940.

**Final census (poison census, 24–28 June 1941)**
- The lake held 9,171 fish weighing 442.17 pounds: 4,586 fish and 221.09 pounds per acre.
- Every species known from the creel census was present, along with one carp, one common sucker and one blunt-nosed minnow, probably escaped bait.
- Only 481 fish (5.2 per cent by number) were of desirable size, but they made up 44.4 per cent of the weight.
- The 1941 yield-per-acre figure was lower than the 1939 yield, yet the lake still held a large number of fish.

**Species-level results**
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: 275 fish, of which 47 (17.1 per cent) were legal length, over 23 legal fish per acre despite heavy fishing. Six annual broods (1936–1941) were present. Growth was poor in the first summer but rapid afterward, and legal length (10 inches) was reached in the second summer.
- [[Black Crappie]]: reduced to 22 fish in the final census, "nearer extermination than any other species known to be numerous in the past." Growth was unusually fast for the 1940 brood. The author says the decline was apparently not due to hook-and-line fishing, and a natural die-off in summer 1940 is suggested.
- [[Bluegill]]: 6,545 fish, 71.4 per cent of all fish present. Only 5.0 per cent were of desirable size. Bluegills thrived under intensive fishing.
- [[Warmouth Bass]]: 1,638 fish weighing 28.6 pounds, with only 13 of desirable size. Warmouth was the only species that did not show rapid later growth.
- [[Green Sunfish]]: 245 fish, of which 9 measured 6 inches or more. In Michigan waters, the comparable ratio was 1 in 32; here it was 1 in 27.
- [[Yellow Bass]]: four fish, all of desirable size. Stocking occurred in 1934–1935 and a successful spawn is not documented after 1936.
- [[Bullheads]]: 347 yellow bullheads and two black bullheads in 1941. The bullhead catch was 89 fish weighing 63.4 pounds, nearly all probably yellow bullheads.
- [[Golden Shiner]]: 90 fish, 37 of which were at least 7 inches. Shiners were kept by fishermen, despite not usually being considered hook-and-line fish.

**Growth and age**
- Across species, first-year growth was slow, but most species grew exceptionally fast in later years. The author links this to reduced adult predation and food competition in later life stages, with early food competition among young fish from heavy spawning.
- The oldest fish were in their sixth summer, mostly largemouth bass. Most fish were caught in their third summer.
- Age was determined by the [[Scale Method]]; bullheads were grouped by length because their age determinations were too slow to be practical.

**Carrying capacity and conclusions**
- The author estimates the 1939 yield at about three-fourths of the lake's carrying capacity, which he places between 450 and 500 pounds per acre. The average for Onized Lake and 21 other small Illinois lakes was 446.5 pounds per acre.
- Every species present was still present in the lake, which the author attributes to fishing pressure falling across all species rather than focusing on one.
- The author concludes that the natural spawn was sufficient to replace fish removed, and that under reduced fishing the lake would have returned to its former carrying capacity.
