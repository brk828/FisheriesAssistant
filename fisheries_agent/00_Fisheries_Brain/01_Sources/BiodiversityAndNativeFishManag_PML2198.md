# Biodiversity and Native Fish Management in the Southwestern United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This review, written by John N. Rinne of the [[Rocky Mountain Research Station]] and Arizona State University, examines the status and management of the native fish fauna of the arid American Southwest in relation to the levels of [[Biodiversity]] (genetic, species, ecosystem, and landscape). The paper argues that conservation has been effective at the species level, notably through [[Endangered Species Act]] listings and species-by-species recovery plans, but that genetic and ecosystem levels are only now receiving attention and landscape-level biodiversity is unlikely ever to be fully achieved. Its central conclusion is that all levels of biodiversity must be conserved together, with acquisition of aquatic habitats proceeding on an ecosystem basis.

## Background & Description

The paper defines [[Biodiversity]] as the totality of life on earth and the interactions among its biotic components. It divides biodiversity into at least four levels: genetic, species, ecosystem, and landscape diversity. Early conservation efforts traced to the Endangered Species Preservation Act of 1966, followed by the [[Endangered Species Conservation Act]] of 1969 and the 1973 [[Endangered Species Act]]. Threatened, endangered, and sensitive (TES) species programs have been in place for over two decades, but the author notes these represent only the "tip of the iceberg."

The U.S. [[Forest Service]], established in 1905, has a mandate to manage ecosystems and conserve major components of biodiversity, further clarified by the [[National Forest Management Act]] of 1976 and the [[Forest Land and Resource Management Plan]] regulations. The paper focuses on the native fishes of the American Southwest, with much of its discussion drawn from Arizona.

The region's fish diversity is low relative to eastern United States faunas, yet about a little over two dozen native fishes historically inhabited Arizona's rivers and streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The two principal causes of decline in many Southwestern fish species are habitat alteration and introduction of non-native species, with biological control of vegetation also cited. Damming of major rivers has fragmented aquatic systems into disjunct reaches, with stabilized flows, reservoirs, and tailraces replacing natural flood-and-drought cycles. Irrigation diversions and groundwater pumping have dried springs, reduced streamflows, and desiccated streams.

Several large-river species, including the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Sucker]], lost spawning and range access because of mainstream dams on the Colorado and Salt-Verde rivers. Non-native species such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia) compete with and prey on natives. Some small spring fishes, including the Monkey Springs pupfish and a unique form of chub, have become extinct.

Among the species most affected by introductions are the [[Apache Trout]] and the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], harmed by land management activities and by introductions of non-native salmonids for sport and cyprinids for bait. Upper-elevation montane fish assemblages fare better because of sufficient precipitation and their location above major dams.

## Key Findings

**Overall status**
- Of Arizona's native fishes, large river species are the most endangered. Diminutive spring-dwelling species and those restricted to isolated spring habitats are also endangered.
- Intermediate-elevation species in degrading canyon streams fare comparatively better because aquatic habitats persist there.
- If state-listed and sensitive species are included, the fauna must be labeled endangered.

**Species-level conservation**
- Most conservation since the Endangered Species Act has been species-by-species, with individual recovery plans.
- Federal and state listings and Forest Service Region 3 sensitive species lists are said to address perpetuation at the species level, at least on paper.

**Genetic diversity cases**
- [[Gila Trout]]: Once widespread in the Gila River headwaters, reduced to five small headwater streams through rainbow trout introduction, habitat alteration, and overfishing. Genetic studies supported treating each population as distinct. A genetically pure population at Main Diamond Creek was threatened by wildfire but partly recovered from surviving fish.
- [[Apache Trout]]: Once widespread in the White Mountains but reduced to under 1% of its historic range by brown and brook trout introductions. Phenotypic work reported 13 populations of pure Apache trout and 19 "mostly pure" populations. Hybrids with rainbow trout do not show intermediacy, and hatchery stock from the East Fork White River was used for recovery. ASU studies found low-frequency hybrids, attributed to maternal control by Apache trout, suggesting extirpation by piscicide would destroy unique genetic diversity.
- [[Gila Topminnow]]: Recorded in the 1930s, later persisting in only one spring in Santa Cruz County, Arizona. Reintroduction attempts were hampered by competition from mosquitofish. Monkey Springs stock was genetically invariant and of low fecundity, so the authors recommended using more heterozygous populations from thermally fluctuating springs. This protocol has been adopted for recovery.
- The Virgin River project in Utah, which aimed to remove introduced red shiner and restore native minnows and suckers, illustrates that genetic diversity of both target and non-target species has likely been lost.

**Ecosystem and landscape conservation**
- Conservation at the ecosystem level is being pursued through special management emphasis on unique aquatic ecosystems, such as [[Aravaipa Creek]], which contains 7 of the original 17 native fish species of the Gila River fauna, three of them TES species.
- Land acquisition by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (San Bernardino Ranch) and The Nature Conservancy (Ash Meadows, Nevada; Muleshoe Ranch, Arizona) protects habitats for multiple species.
- Landscape-level biodiversity is unlikely to be fully achieved because river basins are irreparably disjunct, and the water demand of rapidly growing states such as Arizona and Nevada precludes total restoration of major ecosystems.

**Conclusions**
- Conservation should progress from single to multiple species and from small to large habitats where feasible.
- Acquisition of aquatic habitats should proceed on an ecosystem basis and be complemented by a vigorous research and development program, with short-term species preservation and long-term maintenance of biodiversity over the greatest possible landscape extent.
- Research efforts should evaluate the effects of stream renovation on all aquatic genetic diversity, including macroinvertebrates, and should avoid a "tunnel vision" approach to treatment with piscicides.
