# Recovery from Acid Pollution in Shallow Strip-Mine Lakes in Missouri

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This 1965 reprint of a 1964 conference paper by [[Robert S. Campbell]] and colleagues at the University of Missouri describes four shallow [[Strip-Mine Lakes]] in [[Calloway County]], central [[Missouri]]. The lakes were formed by [[Coal Mining]] in the [[Bevier Formation]], and their water chemistry was shaped by [[Acid Mine Drainage]]. Data were mainly collected monthly from January 1962 to March 1964, and the paper is explicitly a report on research in progress. The authors describe a predictable sequence of recovery from acid to alkaline conditions, marked by water colour changes (red, blue-green, grey) and by increasing biotic diversity. They also report that recovery rates differ strongly even among adjacent lakes of the same age, and that alkaline drainage from farmland appears to accelerate recovery.

## Background & Description

Strip-mine lakes form in pits left after coal is removed by stripping. The authors note that such lakes occur across some 65,000 acres of stripped land in Arkansas, Oklahoma, Kansas, Iowa and Missouri. Newly formed lakes are often acidic because of [[Sulfuric Acid]] generated from sulfur-bearing wastes. As the water ages, pH rises and the lake may pass through distinctive colour stages. Eventually the water becomes alkaline and can support fish.

The study had two aims:
1. To describe the physical, chemical and biological features of four central Missouri strip-mine lakes.
2. To characterise recovery from acid pollution in ecological terms.

Three of the lakes had been studied since 1940 by [[Crawford]] (1940–41) and [[Heaton]] (1949–50). The coal of the Bevier Formation is a highly volatile bituminous type with a sulfur content of 3 to 5 per cent. It may contain small amounts of [[Pyrite]] and marcasite.

The lakes are:
- **Lake A1**: formed by mining in 1935 near Millersburg, about five miles from the others. Observations began in July 1963. Area 0.3 acres; watershed 1.5 acres.
- **Lake A2**: formed in 1919–1921 near Carrington. Area 0.5 acres. The text gives a five-acre watershed, and this value should be checked against Table I.
- **Lake B**: formed in 1919–1921 near Carrington. Area 1.5 acres; watershed 12 acres in the text.
- **Lake D**: formed in 1919–1921 near Carrington. Watershed 95 acres, including some farmland.

The lakes lie in a line, with no more than 50 ft between neighbours, but each has a separate watershed. Routine physical, chemical and biological measurements were taken monthly. More complete seasonal water analyses were made by the University Spectrographic Laboratory, using [[Standard Methods for Water Examination]], flame photometry and spectroscopy for heavy metals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake A1 (red, most acidic).** Water is deep red-amber and very acidic (pH 2.0–2.9). It contains very high iron (104–6,200 ppm), sulfate (777–7,600 ppm), calcium (63–308 ppm) and magnesium (31–232 ppm). Specific conductance ranges from 1,450 to 12,000 µmhos/cm, and total solids from 1,000 to 11,000 ppm. The lake is usually thermally stratified. Biota are very sparse: only two aquatic insect groups ([[Corixidae]] and [[Ceratopogonidae]]) and the flagellate Euglena have been collected. Winter ice cover led to strong chemical and thermal stratification that persisted into early March 1964. The authors attribute this to the large density difference between layers. Iron rose steadily from surface to bottom.

**Lake A2 (blue-green, intermediate).** Water is exceptionally clear and blue-green; leaves on the bottom are visible at 13 ft. The pH is 3.4–4.6. Iron stayed at or below 10 ppm in 1962–1964, compared with 6,200 ppm in Lake A1. In 1940, when the lake was more acidic (pH 2.8), Crawford recorded iron up to 168 ppm. Waste coal piles in the watershed maintain the acidity. The lake supports no fish and no rooted plants. In July–October 1963, the desmid [[Closterium]] sp. caused yellow-green water and reduced visibility. The rotifer [[Brachionus urceolaris]], associated with acid water, was present. Thirteen plankton taxa and twelve groups of bottom organisms were recorded, with Tendipedidae larvae the most abundant. The lake was thermally unstratified in 15 of 22 observations from 1962–1964.

**Lake B (alkaline, black-water).** pH ranges from 6.8 to 7.6. Specific conductance is 235–520 µmhos/cm, and total iron does not exceed 2 ppm. Methyl orange alkalinity ranges from 3 to 102 ppm. Rooted aquatic plants covered the entire bottom in 1962. Thirty-five plankton species were identified. The bottom fauna was dominated by [[Chaoborus]], Caenis and Tendipedidae. Seven fish species were collected in 1963. [[American Beaver]] used the lake for a bank den in 1963.

**Lake D (grey, late-stage).** The water is frequently grey from clay turbidity, and the lake is more turbid than Lakes A2 or B. Silt and clay have nearly divided the lake into two basins. The pH is 6.9–7.9, and specific conductance (141–360 µmhos/cm, based on Table I) and total solids are lower than in the other lakes. Methyl orange alkalinity ranges from 61 to 143 ppm. One rooted plant species is present. The bottom fauna includes Chaoborus, Hexagenia and Sphaeriidae. Twenty-three plankton species were identified, with [[Keratella cochlearis]] and [[Daphnia parvula]] among the most numerous. Seven fish species were collected in 1963. Beavers used Lake D, and established dens there.

**Chemical comparison.** Lake A1's water resembles typical mine discharges (Braley, 1954), though it has higher total iron and sulfate.

## Key Findings

**1. A predictable recovery sequence.** Recovery moves from acid to alkaline stages, identifiable by colour and chemistry:
- *Early (red)*: pH below 3; iron in thousands of ppm as colloidal iron hydroxides; specific conductance 1,400–12,000 µmhos/cm.
- *Intermediate (blue)*: pH usually 3–4; most iron precipitated as ferric hydroxide; specific conductance 500–600 µmhos/cm; total solids up to 600 ppm. Clay particles flocculate at this pH, so loss of transparency is due to algal blooms.
- *Late (grey)*: pH 7 or higher; silt and clay turbidity; specific conductance below 400 µmhos/cm; total solids up to 400 ppm.

**2. Biotic enrichment with recovery.** Taxon counts in 1940 and 1963 rose in each lake (for example, Lake B from 21 to 50 and Lake D from 31 to 52). Recovery brings an increase in biotic quality, measured by the number of genera and species.

**3. Variable recovery rates.** Lakes A2, B and D were excavated in 1919–1921, lie within 50 ft of each other, and have similar spoil-bank drainage areas. Yet their water acidity ranges from pH 3.4 (A2) to 6.8–7.6 (B) and 6.9–7.9 (D). Rate of recovery depends on the ratio of spoil-bank drainage area to water area, the amount of exposed pyrite, and leaching rate.

**4. Waste coal delays recovery.** Waste coal piles in the watershed of Lake A2 have delayed recovery for about 45 years.

**5. Farmland runoff accelerates recovery.** The 95-acre watershed of Lake D is largely grass or cultivated farmland with little acid-forming soil. Crawford (1942) suggested that excess alkaline drainage and silt from farmland accelerated recovery, and the authors' observations confirm this. Recovery was already under way in 1940, and the pH has changed little in 24 years.

**6. Ice-driven stratification.** Ice cover produced strong chemical stratification in Lake A1. Freezing removes iron and sulfate from surface water, which concentrates minerals beneath the ice (following Mortimer, 1941–42). Only Lake A1 kept this stratification after the ice melted, which the authors attribute to density differences.

**7. Fish distribution.** Fish were absent from the acidic lakes A1 and A2, and seven species were collected from each of the alkaline lakes B and D in 1963. The paper gives no species names.

**Overall conclusion.** Strip-mine lakes follow a definite sequence of recovery from acid pollution, but the rate varies even among neighbouring lakes of the same age. Farmland runoff can speed recovery. This was a progress report, and the authors recommend continued study.
