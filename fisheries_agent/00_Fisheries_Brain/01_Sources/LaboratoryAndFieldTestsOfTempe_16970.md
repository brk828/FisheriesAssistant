# Laboratory and Field Tests of Temperature Tolerance on Gambusia a. affinis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #thermal-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary
This study by Gerking, Ratcliff, and Otto (1975) investigated thermal tolerance in the [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia a. affinis*) using both field (stream-side) and laboratory approaches at [[Indian Hot Springs]], Graham County, Arizona. The researchers exploited a natural thermal gradient — a spring-fed channel cooling from ~48.5°C at the source to ambient temperatures downstream — to test [[Critical Thermal Maximum]] (CTM) and upper lethal temperature limits under field-acclimated (fluctuating temperature) conditions, comparing results against controlled laboratory data previously published by Otto (1973, 1974). The study found close agreement between field and laboratory thermal tolerance metrics, with field-acclimated fish showing slightly elevated CTM values, attributed to the effects of fluctuating (cyclic) acclimation temperatures rather than constant laboratory regimes. Life-stage and sex-based differences in heat tolerance were also documented, with immature fish and males consistently outperforming adult females.

## Background & Description
- **Life stages tested**: Fry (<1 cm), immatures (~1.5 cm), adult males (~2.4 cm), adult females (~2.3 cm).
- Size classes were used as a proxy for developmental stage in thermal tolerance comparisons.
- No detailed external morphological descriptions provided; the paper is physiological/ecological in focus, concentrating on thermal stress responses rather than anatomy.
- Sexual dimorphism in body size is implicit (males smaller than females), consistent with typical [[Poeciliidae]] life history patterns.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: Thermal spring-fed channel and associated irrigation ditch at Indian Hot Springs, Eden, Arizona. Spring water emerges at 48.5°C and cools along a ~100 m channel, creating a natural thermal gradient ranging from ~41°C near the source to ~36°C at 50 m downstream.
- **Sole fish species**: *Gambusia a. affinis* was the only fish species present in the tested stream channel, indicating a specialized or exclusive niche in this extreme thermal environment, likely due to competitive exclusion of less heat-tolerant species.
- **Thermal exposure**: Fish in the lower reaches of the ditch are continuously exposed to elevated, fluctuating temperatures dependent on weather, time of day, and water diversion volume (controlled by a junction box regulating flow between the ditch and a swimming pool).
- **Acclimation regimes**: Fish were acclimated in wire enclosures at pre-selected sites along the thermal gradient for periods of 3–7 days prior to testing, mimicking natural fluctuating acclimation rather than artificial constant-temperature laboratory conditions.
- This system serves as a natural analog for studying [[Thermal Pollution]] effects and extreme habitat adaptation in freshwater fish.

## Key Findings

### Critical Thermal Maximum (CTM)
- CTM measured via 0.3°C/min heating rate until loss of equilibrium.
- Subpopulation acclimated to 36.9°C: CTM = 43.8°C.
- Subpopulation acclimated to 35.9°C: CTM = 43.6°C.
- These values closely match Otto's (1973) laboratory CTM of 43.8°C for adult females acclimated at equivalent temperatures.
- CTM by life stage (acclimated to 29.0°C): fry = 42.2°C; immatures = 41.8°C; adult males = 42.4°C; adult females = 42.3°C.
- Field-acclimated (fluctuating temperature) fish showed CTM values ~0.5°C **higher** than laboratory constant-temperature-acclimated fish, suggesting a tolerance benefit from cyclic thermal exposure.
- Immature survivors from a high-mortality event (35 m site, ~38°C) showed CTM = 44.0°C, the highest recorded, supporting a **survival advantage in immatures** over adults.
- Findings align with [[Feldmeth Stone Brown 1974]] work on pupfish (*Cyprinodon nevadensis amargosae*), though that study found cyclic acclimation extended the **lower** tolerance limit rather than the upper one. In contrast, Otto (1974) found fluctuating high-temperature exposure could extend CTM by 1–3°C above constant-acclimation values.

### Upper Lethal Temperature / Median Survival Time
- "In-stream" tests (Table 1) conducted across subpopulations at 10, 20, and 30 m from the junction box on two dates (July 17 & 18, 1971).
- Median survival time was inversely correlated with test temperature: as temperature decreased from 41.3°C to 37.9°C, median survival increased from ~275 to ~446 minutes.
- Below 36.9°C, virtually no mortality occurred (only one adult female died after >12 hours at subpopulation 40).
- Estimated **Upper Incipient Lethal Temperature** ≈ 37.4°C (midpoint between 36.9°C survival and 37.9°C at 446 min median survival) — within 0.5°C of Otto's laboratory value for fish acclimated at 35.0°C.
- **Sex/age differences**: Adult males slightly outperformed adult females at all tested temperatures (up to 30 min difference in median survival). Immatures outperformed both adult sexes (up to 32 min difference vs. adult females).

### Lab-Type Tests (Fiberglass Tank Trials)
- Adult females acclimated to 35.8°C showed greater heat resistance than those acclimated to 29.0°C (e.g., at 42.0°C: 25 min vs. 4 min median survival).
- Results were comparable to Otto's laboratory data for females acclimated at 35.0°C and 30.0°C (Table 2).
- Adult males and fry showed substantially greater survival times than adult females under identical acclimation and test conditions:
  - Males (29°C acclimation) at 40.0°C: 1,180 min vs. females: 30 min.
  - Fry (35.9°C acclimation): 2,230 min at 41.0°C and 1,775 min at 41.5°C, vs. ~70 and ~50 min for adult females at the same temperatures.

### Overall Conclusions
- Field (fluctuating-temperature) acclimation and laboratory (constant-temperature) acclimation produce **similar** median survival times, upper incipient lethal temperatures, and CTM values, validating laboratory-based thermal tolerance research for ecological extrapolation.
- Minor differences are attributed to natural fluctuating temperatures and other uncontrolled environmental variables in the field.
- Across nearly all metrics, **males, fry, and immature fish exhibited greater heat resistance than adult females**, an ontogenetic/sex-based pattern with potential implications for population thermal ecology and survival during heat stress events.
- Field distribution of *Gambusia* in the thermal gradient closely tracks laboratory-derived thermal tolerance limits, confirming that lab-based indices have strong real-world predictive value (per discussion with Hyatt and Gerking).
