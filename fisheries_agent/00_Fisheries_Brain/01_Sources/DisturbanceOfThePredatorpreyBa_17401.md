# Disturbance of the Predator-Prey Balance as a Management Technique

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 paper by [[Kenneth D. Carlander]] (Iowa State College, reprinted in the *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*) argues that deliberately disturbing the [[Predator-Prey Balance]] in freshwater game fish populations can improve angler success and can serve as a practical management tool. The argument rests on two case studies from [[Clear Lake]], Iowa: an exceptional [[Walleye]] harvest in 1951, and a strong [[Northern Pike]] catch after a stocking in 1953–1954. The author presents the technique as a "shot-in-the-arm" that raises the rate of harvest rather than the underlying fish production, and that cannot sustain good fishing on its own.

## Background & Description

The paper begins from the observation that fish abundance alone does not guarantee good fishing. Fish must also be feeding and catchable. Seasonal shifts in behavior or food availability can make fish hard to catch, and management responses such as extended seasons and year-round fishing have been used to offset these shifts.

The principal game fishes are predators. The author draws on earlier work that suggested the balance between predators and their prey influences fishing success:

- Johnson (1949) reported that removing 24 pounds of herring per acre from an 1,100-acre Minnesota lake improved [[Lake Trout]] angling, and that removing 100 yellow perch per acre from a 124,000-acre lake improved walleye angling.
- Bennett (1954a) recommended late-summer [[Reservoir Drawdown]] partly to alter predator-prey relationships and boost [[Largemouth Bass]] reproduction the following year.
- Bennett (1954b) observed that spring draining of [[Ridge Lake]], which reduced small bluegill, crayfish and other bass foods, was followed by higher than normal percentages of bass being caught.

Carlander defines predator-prey balance not as a fixed numerical equilibrium but as a "see-saw" relationship. Prey increases tend to drive predator increases, and the reverse. Other environmental factors can break this tendency, so prey may remain high, rise, or fall independently. When the predator-to-prey ratio is high, predators have more difficulty finding enough food. Lowering that ratio, by adding predators or removing prey, can therefore raise fishing success. The author stresses that the relationship is complex and that this lever will not always work.

Clear Lake is described as a 3,643-acre eutrophic lake with a maximum depth of 20 feet. It supports a varied warmwater fish community and, in each year, provides over 150 angling hours per acre and an annual harvest of over 60 pounds of fish per acre (Di Costanzo and Ridenhour, 1957). The lake has been studied annually since 1946.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Walleye and yellow perch in 1950–1951.** Walleyes were the most sought-after game fish. Most walleyes caught in 1951 were 4 to 7 years old, so they had been in the lake during the less successful fishing of the previous years. Experimental gill net catches (per 125-foot net per 24 hours) were 13.9 in 1949, 18.0 in 1950, and 14.8 in 1952. In 1951, the net catch fell to 5.1, even though angler success was high. Extensive seining in 1950 found no young yellow perch in areas where they had previously been abundant. The author attributes this to poor reproduction, possibly caused by scarce aquatic vegetation in 1949 and 1950.

Without young perch, walleyes probably relied more on young yellow bass. The 1950 yellow bass year class was fairly abundant but disappeared over the winter of 1950–1951, and no yearling yellow bass were found in 1951. The author suspects that heavier-than-normal walleye predation removed this year class before spring. Walleyes were likely hungrier when the season opened on May 15, 1951, and became more catchable. Prey loss, through reduced perch and bass, therefore disrupted the balance in the walleyes' favor.

**Northern pike stocking, 1951–1954.** Northern pike were a minor part of the Clear Lake population and catch until 1953–1954. A few adults were stocked in 1951 in [[Ventura Marsh]], a 630-acre marshy bay separated from the lake by a road grade and a permanent fish trap. Young pike raised in the marsh were released into the lake through the trap. More than 5,000 fingerlings were counted moving into the lake in 1952 (Moen and Lindquist, 1954). In fall 1953, about 17,000 young pike, 10 to 16 inches long, were transferred from the marsh to the lake (Carlander and Ridenhour, 1955; Ridenhour, 1957).

**Quantitative reasoning.** The author estimates that Clear Lake has a standing crop of about 300 to 400 pounds of fish per acre, based on comparisons with other rich lakes (Carlander, 1955). Work on farm ponds and food conversion suggests about 5 pounds of forage fish per pound of predator. This implies about 50 to 70 pounds of predatory fish per acre (walleyes, bass and pike). In fall 1953 about 5 pike per acre were stocked. By spring 1954 these fish averaged about 1.5 pounds, adding about 7 pounds per acre, or at least a 10 percent increase in predator biomass. The population showed no stunting and no crowding of pan fishes, which the author took as evidence that the system was reasonably balanced before stocking.

## Key Findings

- **Walleye success in 1951.** Exceptional early-season walleye angling in 1951 is "perhaps" explained by two linked events: reproductive failure of yellow perch in 1950 and elimination of the 1950 yellow bass year class through above-normal predation. The author presents this as a plausible hypothesis rather than a proven cause.
- **Northern pike harvest in 1954.** The pike catch opened unusually well in mid-May 1954. Over 650 tagged pike were turned in by anglers before July 1. More than 20 percent of the fish tagged when stocked the previous fall were caught in the first six weeks of the season. Some anglers feared the fishery would be depleted.
- **Stocking effect on predators.** Stocking about 5 pike per acre in fall 1953 raised predator biomass by at least 10 percent. Anglers' harvest did not fully restore balance, and by late summer many thin pike were found dead along shore and some caught pike were in poor condition.
- **No detected effect on walleyes.** The author expected the added pike to increase walleye catch, since walleyes are the other principal predator. No such increase was detected. Hazzard and Shetter (1939) had found that stocking one trout species did not change the catch of another, though it did increase catch of stocked and native fish of the same species.
- **Limits of the technique.** Carlander concludes that predator-prey disturbance strongly affects catch, but that it is largely a method of increasing the rate of harvest, not fish production. Successful angling lowers the predator population, which moves the system back toward balance, so the benefit is temporary. The technique is most useful when fish are abundant but not biting.
- **Sources of disturbance.** Balance may be disturbed by drawdown, by failure or destruction of fish food organisms, or by abrupt increases in predators. These disturbances can arise naturally or be engineered by managers.
