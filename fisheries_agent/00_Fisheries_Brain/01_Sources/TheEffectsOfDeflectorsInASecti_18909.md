# The Effects of Deflectors in a Section of a Michigan Trout Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This study evaluates whether current deflectors (wing-type stream-improvement structures) increase the number, depth, and yield of pools for [[Brook Trout]] in a 1,605-foot section (Section B) of [[Hunt Creek]], Michigan. Data were gathered over eight years: three before and five after installation of 24 pool-forming deflectors, with habitat, food, and fish-population data covering one year before and three years after. The authors conclude that the increase in angling success resulted from more, larger, and deeper pools created by the structures, mainly through better survival of young trout within the section rather than immigration from neighboring waters.

## Background & Description

The work was carried out by the Michigan Department of Conservation at the [[Hunt Creek Fisheries Experiment Station]], with the [[Michigan Institute for Fisheries Research]] contributing. Earlier work cited includes [[Hubbs]]-era advocacy of stream improvement (1932), [[Tarzwell]]'s Michigan and Arizona studies (1937, 1938), and studies in Connecticut and the intermountain West. Few of these had measured actual outcomes, so this study was designed to quantify effects on pools, food, fish populations, and angler yield.

Hunt Creek is about 12 miles long, with relatively rapid flow, springs at intervals, and a watershed of sand, gravel, and peat with aspen, pine, hardwood, and swamp-conifer cover. Section B flows through swamp with tamarack, balsam, spruce, and poplar. Before improvement, the flow was about 1.5 feet per second in mid-channel, the stream was shallow with few pools, and shelter was scant. The bottom was mostly moderate-to-fine gravel and sand, with muck at the margins. Sparse [[Chara]] and [[Veronica]] grew near the banks.

Principal fish species were brook trout and the [[Muddler]] (*Cottus cognatus*). Occasional species included the mudminnow (*Umbra limi*), brook stickleback (*Culaea inconstans*, transcribed in the source as "Eucalia"), bluntnose minnow (*Pimephales notatus*, transcribed as "Hyborhynchus"), fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*), northern redbelly dace (*Chrosomus eos*), and blacknose shiner (*Notropis heterolepis*, transcribed as "Votropis").

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical habitat.** Installation of 24 deflectors (22 single-wing, 1 double-wing, 1 diversion) placed one structure roughly every 70 feet. Each was a log crib (about 30 × 30 inches × 25 feet) filled with gravel, sand, sod, and earth, with wings set at 35° to 45° to the stream's center thread. Deflector-accelerated current scoured pool floors and deposited sand and silt in bars and behind the wings. Gravel was exposed in faster-flowing areas. Overall section area and mean depth changed little, so the authors describe the scour and deposition as local and compensatory.

**Bottom fauna.** Square-foot (Surber-type) samples taken at 20 stations were compared before and after improvement. Total number and volume of organisms decreased, but taxa that trout eat most often (certain Trichoptera, Chironomidae, and *Hyalella*) increased. Predaceous forms such as the stonefly *Isogenus* and snipe-fly *Atherix* did not invade the area. These food-supply findings are preliminary; the full analysis was reserved for a separate publication.

**Fish populations.** Five representative sample areas (60 to 83 feet long) were blocked and sampled each August. Sampling used seines in 1941 and an electric shocker in 1942 to 1944. Brook trout numbers rose and small trout increased in number, while average size of fingerlings and illegal trout declined slightly. Muddlers and minnows increased, and crayfish decreased.

**Spawning.** Natural spawning occurred every fall throughout the study. In fall 1944, 32 redds were observed, several along the gravelly upstream faces and ends of deflectors. The authors attribute the population increase mainly to better survival of young trout, linked to habitat changes.

## Key Findings

**Pool and habitat results**
- Pools judged capable of holding legal-sized trout rose from **9 to 29**.
- Average pool depth increased by about **6 inches** (average maximum depth 1.52 ft in 1939 vs. 2.07 ft in 1943 and 2.08 ft in 1944).
- Total pool area in 1944 was **5,720 sq ft**, a pool-to-stream area ratio of **1:5.2**, with an average of **238 sq ft** per pool.
- Average depth over the whole section was 1.29 ft in 1939 and 1.30 ft in 1944, too small a change to be significant.

**Fish population results**
- The brook trout count in the sample areas rose from **453 (1941)** to **558 (1944)**. Corrected counts were 529 and 683 for the same years.
- Illegal trout (4 to 6.9 inches) increased from **99 to 141** fish.
- The shocker was estimated to be **95.7%** efficient overall across sizes.
- Average size of fingerling trout fell from 2.97 inches (1941) to 2.68 inches (1944), and illegal trout fell from 4.9 to 4.71 inches.

**Angling results (creel census, 3 years before vs. 5 years after)**

| Measure | Change |
|---|---|
| Total legal catch (average 29.3 → 64.6 fish) | +120.4% |
| Pounds of legal trout removed (4.34 → 10.48 lb) | +141.5% |
| Catch per hour (0.45 → 0.61 legal fish) | +35.5% |
| Pounds per hour (0.067 → 0.098) | +46.2% |
| Angling pressure (64.91 → 106.65 hours) | +64.3% |
| Unsuccessful angling days | −20.3% (64% → 51% average) |
| Average legal fish length / weight | −1.2% / −3.4% |

**Origin of the increase**
- Fin-clipping and tagging showed that only a small share of the increase came from migration. Roughly **96%** of the Section B anglers' catch originated within Section B.
- Of 548 trout marked moving downstream through the Tributary 3 weir, 79 were recovered, 15 of them in Section B.
- Section A catch per hour rose 41.1% and Section C fell 14.3%, so the authors conclude that the neighboring sections did not produce comparable gains.

**Economic findings**
- Construction plus five years of maintenance totaled **$512.16**, with construction at $382.16 (about $15.50 per structure).
- The cost per additional trout kept was **$2.82** for the five-year period. A projected 20-year estimate, assuming $26 per year in maintenance and 36.3 extra trout per year, gives **$1.24** per trout.
- The authors suggest that fewer, better-placed deflectors could achieve similar results.

**Conclusions**
1. Current deflectors significantly increased pool number and depth. The increase in legal brook trout and angler yield was attributed mainly to improved survival of young trout produced within the section, and to the habitat changes that made this possible.
2. Bottom-food results were mixed. Total fauna declined, but the forms trout consume most increased.
3. The authors describe stream improvement as expensive but, when compared with planting legal-sized hatchery fish, a feasible way to produce angler yield without the drawbacks of stocking.
