# Endangered and Threatened Fishes of the Upper Colorado River and Bureau of Reclamation Projects (1977)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cypriniformes

## Executive Summary

This 1977 final report by [[Northwest Fisheries Research]] (N.F.R.I.), prepared for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], assessed the present status of four endangered or threatened fishes in the Gunnison and upper Colorado rivers: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Field work ran from February to November 1976. Squawfish and razorback suckers were still present in the Colorado River, mainly in [[Backwater Habitat]], but were nearly absent from the Gunnison. The report attributes the decline to a mix of flow alteration, dams and irrigation diversions, hybridization with introduced suckers, and competition from exotic fishes. It recommends a ten-year moratorium on development in the study area and a dedicated hatchery program.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin has a high level of fish endemism (reported as 74%). Its native fishes evolved in a harsh river system with steep canyon walls, swift turbid flows, high dissolved salts, heavy sediment loads and extreme temperature swings. Development since the late 1800s, including [[Irrigation Diversion]] systems, dams, hydropower, waste loads and exotic species, has confined many endemic fishes to the Upper Colorado River Basin, including the [[Green River]] system.

All four target species were classed as endangered or threatened by Colorado and protected in Utah. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] listed the Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub as endangered. Listing proposals for the Bonytail Chub and Razorback Sucker were pending at the time.

Study area:
- **Colorado River:** about 130 miles, from the mouth of [[Debeque Canyon]] to Moab, Utah. The mean flow of record at the head of DeBeque Canyon is 3,775 cfs (range 1,050 to 22,500 cfs). The river falls 448 feet between Cameo, Colorado and the Utah line, with about 158,000 acres of upstream irrigation.
- **Gunnison River:** about 50 miles, from Delta, Colorado to its confluence at Grand Junction. Mean flow at Whitewater is 2,562 cfs. The reach drops about 380 feet.

The [[Grand Valley]] is arid, with a mean annual precipitation of eight inches and a mean annual temperature of 52°F. Water temperatures reach 80°F (27°C), near the upper tolerance of coldwater game fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Barriers and diversions.** The [[Redlands Power Diversion]] (built 1907) on the Gunnison is considered an effective fish migration barrier. On the Colorado, the [[Government Highline Canal]] roller dam is an effective barrier most of the year, and the Palisade Irrigation District diversion is an effective barrier at all times. The Grand Valley Canal deflector dam blocks migration only at very low flow.

**Species assemblage.** Before European settlement, the Gunnison was dominated by endemic species: [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] in the middle reaches, with [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Roundtail Chub]] further downstream. Exotic introductions after about 1940, including trout, catfish, minnows and suckers, have substantially altered the community. The report notes uncertainty about [[Common Names Confusion]] in early literature, where "Bonytail" and "Roundtail" were used interchangeably.

**Water chemistry.** Irrigation return flows add turbidity and salts. Squawfish and razorbacks successfully spawned at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]], where TDS is about 820 ppm. Waters in the study area were below this value during the late spring to early summer spawning period.

**Agricultural drains.** Grand Valley drains showed extreme variation in flow and chemistry. Only two native species were collected there (the rest were introduced and tolerant of high TDS). The drains provide substantial food from irrigation runoff, mainly earthworms, and serve as nursery habitat. The report judged these return flows to contribute significantly to the local fishery.

**Macroinvertebrates.** Riffle samples used a [[Surber Sampler]] (about 9 sq ft total). Dominant taxa included Diptera (midges and damselflies), caddisflies, stoneflies, planarians, earthworms and freshwater shrimp. Crustaceans and mayflies were present but not abundant.

## Key Findings

**Sampling effort and methods**
- Colorado: 30 sampling trips totaling 80 man-days. Utah: 10 trips totaling 25 man-days.
- Gear included graduated and straight trammel nets, hand seines, and electrofishing (2,500 W generator). Electrofishing was judged among the least effective methods for large-river endangered species.
- Scientific collecting permits were issued by Colorado, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Utah. Colorado's permit (No. 76-95) allowed up to 250 Colorado Squawfish, 100 Humpback Chub, 100 Bonytail Chub, 250 Razorback Sucker and 500 Channel Catfish. The federal permit allowed up to 250 squawfish and 100 Humpback Chub by trammel net, with fish to be returned alive.

**Gunnison River**
- No federally listed endangered fish were captured.
- No squawfish were taken despite intensive, directed effort. Residents reported squawfish were fairly common near Delta into the late 1950s.
- The most common large natives were Flannelmouth Sucker, Bluehead Sucker and Roundtail Chub.
- The Gunnison is not considered historic habitat for Bonytail or Humpback Chub.
- Trout were found only in the uppermost section; catfish were common only in the lowest section.
- A 1976 backwater salvage on the Colorado (connected 1970, isolated five years) yielded five largemouth bass, fifty bluegill, 75 carp and 20 bullheads, with no endemic fish left.

**Colorado River**
- Squawfish and razorbacks were found mostly in backwaters. Backwaters with significant numbers of either species were relatively large and well connected to the river.
- [[Walker Wildlife Area]] consistently held a viable population of both razorbacks and squawfish from 1973 onward and is the only known site where both persist in comparatively significant numbers.
- Four squawfish were taken in Utah in 1976 with limited sampling. This is too few for any statement on relative abundance.
- Humpback Chub was documented only at Black Rocks, about 1.5 miles upstream of the Colorado–Utah state line, in a canyon with deep holes.
- Squawfish and razorbacks at [[Palisade Labor Camp]], [[Connected Lakes]] and the Roller Dam spillway were recorded in 1974–1976 collections (for example, 75 squawfish at Walker Wildlife Area in 1975).

**Flow conditions**
- 1975 was selected as an average year for flows and fishery conditions.
- Gunnison peak flow in 1976 was about 1,850 cfs, below what N.F.R.I. considers optimal for backwater reproduction.
- Colorado peak flows in 1976 nearly matched 1975 but occurred in early June, about one month before spawning.
- Low 1976 water levels kept fish out of many backwaters that held them in wetter years.

**Species-specific conclusions**
- *Colorado Squawfish:* a small but apparently self-maintaining population persists in the Colorado. The Redlands Dam is considered an effective barrier, yet squawfish persisted above it for at least 70 years. Flow alteration on the Gunnison is considered a likely significant factor in decline.
- *Razorback Sucker:* more common than squawfish in the study area. Migration barriers are not considered a major threat. Competition and hybridization with introduced [[Western White Sucker]] and [[Western Longnose Sucker]] are considered significant, as they reduce spawning success.
- *Humpback Chub:* among the rarest fishes in Colorado, known from one location. The report calls for work to start soon.
- *Bonytail Chub:* not captured by the authors. The most recent study (Holden 1975) found them very rare in the Green River–Yampa confluence area.

**Recommendations**
1. Study habitat preferences and spawning requirements of squawfish and razorbacks to support a restoration program.
2. Designate a spawning-period flow regime for the Gunnison.
3. Impose a ten-year moratorium on development in the study area.
4. Continue the Utah tagging study, which has provided movement data and showed fish tolerate tagging and netting well.
5. Build a hatchery dedicated to these species as part of the proposed Dominguez Reservoir project, which would inundate a section of the Gunnison. Hatchery production should be an emergency measure, not a substitute for population protection.
