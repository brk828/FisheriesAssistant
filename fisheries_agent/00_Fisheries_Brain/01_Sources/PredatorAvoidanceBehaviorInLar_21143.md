# Predator Avoidance Behavior in Larval Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This [[Arizona State University]] Master of Science thesis (Diary Marie Smith, July 1992) tests the "[[Naivete Hypothesis]]": that larvae of native southwestern fishes, which evolved with few piscine predators, are less capable of [[Predator Avoidance Behavior]] than larvae of species native to the [[Mississippi River Basin]]. Larvae of three southwestern species were compared with two Mississippi-region species in laboratory trials measuring schooling, survival and distance kept from a predator. The results did not support the hypothesis. Southwestern larvae showed equal or stronger avoidance in most comparisons, with the clearest differences in schooling. The author concludes that predation may not be the main cause of native fish decline, while noting that the study's design limits firm conclusions.

## Background & Description

Native fishes of the American Southwest are in decline. Four big-river endemics of the [[Colorado River Basin]] are federally listed as endangered: [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and humpback chub (*Gila cypha*). All are long-lived with high adult survival, but recruitment is largely absent. Viable eggs and larvae are produced, yet juveniles are rare or missing. The most likely explanations for larval mortality are [[Starvation]] and predation.

Non-native species from the Mississippi basin have been introduced into the Southwest, and most of them are piscivorous. The author argues that these introduced fishes survive through egg and larval stages to recruit into adult populations. Native prey species, by contrast, may have evolved under lower predation pressure and reduced selection for anti-predator tactics, partly because of heavy natural [[Silt Turbidity]] before damming and channelization.

Test species were:
- **Southwestern natives:** Razorback sucker, Bonytail and Colorado River squawfish.
- **Mississippi-region natives (prey):** [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersonii*) and [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*).
- **Predators:** [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and red shiner. All are native to the Mississippi drainage and established in the Colorado system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Predation pressure:** Only 4 of 37 native Colorado basin species eat other fishes, and only the squawfish is clearly specialized. Its feeding is sporadic, and it often has an empty stomach.
- **Visibility:** Pre-dam Colorado River waters were heavily silted, which probably lowered predation rates. The author suggests this selected less elaborate avoidance behavior in native prey.
- **Introduced species:** Of 37 introduced species (Meffe, 1985), 86% are piscivorous, which the author treats as a contrast with the native fauna.
- **Cover use:** Native southwestern larvae show less use of cover. Razorback sucker larvae at 2–3 days post swim-up use the upper third of the water column, while white sucker larvae remain in gravel until the 12–14 mm mesolarval stage. Mississippi-region species may therefore make more effective use of cover.
- **Lab conditions:** Water was held at 20–23 °C. Dechlorinated water was conditioned for at least 12 hours. Light regimes changed partway through the study (24 h light, then 12 h light/12 h dark).
- **Other threats:** Habitat alteration and dewatering, competition with introduced fishes, and egg predation are also listed as major threats.

## Key Findings

**Study design**
- Prey: 10 larvae per trial. Each Run had 12 trials (3 per predator type and 3 control). Trials were 15 minutes long, with one reading per minute.
- Prey total length ranged from 3.3 to 19.1 mm. Predators were about 40 mm total length.
- The test tank was divided into a 3 × 3 grid (9 areas). Distance and schooling were scored from these readings.
- Predators were not used more than once, with a few exceptions.

**Distance from predator**
- Southwestern and Mississippi-region catostomid larvae did not differ significantly in distance maintained from predators.
- Cyprinid larvae kept greater distances from *G. affinis* (P = 0.0004) and *L. cyanellus* (P = 0.0001) than in control trials.
- A two-way interaction with *C. lutrensis* was significant (P = 0.0302). Southwestern *G. elegans* kept greater distances than Mississippi-region *C. lutrensis* in control trials.

**Schooling**
- Southwestern catostomid larvae (*X. texanus*) schooled significantly more than *C. commersonii* with *L. cyanellus* and *C. lutrensis* predators (P = 0.0001 each).
- With *G. affinis*, a three-way interaction was significant (P = 0.0049). *X. texanus* schooled more than *C. commersonii* in both predator and control trials.
- Metalarval stages schooled more than mesolarvae with *L. cyanellus* (P = 0.0139).
- Southwestern cyprinid mesolarvae (*P. lucius* and *G. elegans*) schooled more than *C. lutrensis* with *G. affinis* predators.
- Metalarval *X. texanus* showed a large drop in schooling when a predator was present, which the author links to *G. affinis* staying near the surface.

**Survival**
- Survival of catostomid larvae did not differ significantly between regions. Metalarvae survived better than mesolarvae with *G. affinis* (P = 0.0846).
- With *G. affinis*, *G. elegans* survived better than *C. lutrensis* (P = 0.0001), and *P. lucius* survived better than *C. lutrensis* (P = 0.0163).
- With *L. cyanellus*, *C. lutrensis* survived better than *P. lucius* (P = 0.0077). This is the only result favoring the Mississippi species.
- Metalarvae survived better than mesolarvae overall (P = 0.0001).

**Magnitude of change**
- Difference scores (predator minus control) showed no significant regional differences for distance, with one exception.
- For schooling, a metalarval *X. texanus* drop was significant with *G. affinis*, and a two-way interaction (P = 0.0003) was found for catostomids.

**Overall conclusions**
- Southwestern larvae showed a trend toward stronger avoidance. This was mild for survival and distance but strong for schooling.
- The results do not support the naivete hypothesis. The author states that predation in the larval stage "may not be the major cause" of native decline, and that interspecific differences in avoidance behavior "may be the most important factor" in some respects.
- The author notes that the "naivete" case is "strong" enough to warrant further investigation.

**Limitations**
- Only two Mississippi-region species were tested, and they differed in rearing source, stock quality and size.
- *C. commersonii* were farmed fish, and *X. texanus* and *C. lutrensis* had different die-off histories.
- *C. lutrensis* were much smaller than other prey at similar stages, which may have lowered their scores on size alone.
- Key avoidance behaviors (cover use, burst speed, startle response timing) were not tested. Comparative data for Mississippi and southwestern larvae are not available.
- Stage and rearing differences may have affected the results. The author recommends wild-caught adults, more Mississippi-region species, and better size matching.
