# Post-Impoundment Fisheries Survey of Fall River Reservoir, Kansas

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Clupeidae #Centrarchidae #Ictaluridae #Catostomidae #Sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This 1954 study, published in the *Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science*, assesses the early fish community of [[Fall River Reservoir]], a newly impounded reservoir in Greenwood County, Kansas. Authors Roy Schoonover (Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Commission) and William H. Thompson (U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Tulsa District) sampled about three surface acres of cove habitat with rotenone in late August 1952. Gizzard shad dominated the catch by number and weight, and age-and-growth data were compiled for several sport and forage species. The authors concluded that largemouth bass, crappie and channel catfish had not yet reached their peak, and that the reservoir's fisheries were underutilized.

## Background & Description

The reservoir lies on Fall River about four miles northwest of the town of Fall River and seventeen miles southeast of Eureka. At conservation pool elevation of 948.5 feet mean sea level, it holds 27,000 acre-feet over 2,600 acres.

Gates were closed on 12 August 1948 for interim storage, and final storage began 20 April 1949. The pool rose from 919.30 to 938.98 feet in April 1949, reached 946.60 feet in May and 949.15 feet by June 1949, then fluctuated within a few feet of that level. Higher pool stages occurred in July–August 1950 and May–July 1951.

**Methods:** Two cove areas totaling roughly three surface acres were treated with rotenone. Fish were counted or weighed in the field, with groups estimated when full enumeration was impractical. Scale and spine samples were taken for [[Age and Growth Analysis]] of representative individuals of key species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Community structure:** The reservoir was dominated by [[Gizzard Shad]], which made up about 97% of individuals and 83% of biomass in the collection. Sampling was limited to shallow coves, so the authors noted that crappie and young-of-the-year sport fish were likely underrepresented.
- **Comparison with Grand Lake, Oklahoma:** A 1949 survey of [[Grand Lake]] also found shad dominant (92.36% by number), with drum second at 1.83%.
- **Stocking:** [[White Bass]] were introduced by the Kansas Forestry, Fish and Game Commission on 27 April 1950, but none were recovered in 1952. Approximately 695,000 [[Walleye]] fry were planted in May 1952.
- **Growth context:** Growth rates were compared with Oklahoma and other Kansas impoundments, where channel catfish overabundance can reduce growth.

## Key Findings

**Overall catch (approx. three acres, 25–27 August 1952):**
- Total of 244,463 fish weighing 3,988.4 pounds, or about 1,329 pounds per surface acre.
- Species ranked by number after shad: [[Freshwater Drum]] (~5,000), [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (~1,000), [[Common Carp]] (~500), green sunfish, crappie, channel catfish, bluegill, bullhead, [[Largemouth Bass]] (129), carpsucker, [[Flathead Catfish]], gar, walleye, largemouth buffalo, and redhorse sucker.
- No white bass were collected.

**Species-specific results:**
- **[[Largemouth Bass]]:** 126 aged fish; 106 belonged to the 1952 year-class. Average growth was slow, requiring about two years to reach 10 inches and slightly over half a pound. The authors suggested the population had not yet peaked and that better bass fishing was indicated.
- **[[White Crappie]]:** 60 aged fish. First-year growth increased regularly from 1949 to 1952. No 1950 year-class fish were found, possibly indicating a weak year-class. Average length of 1951 fish at end of second year was 8.7 inches.
- **[[Black Crappie]]:** Seven aged fish; four belonged to the 1950 year-class, possibly indicating a more successful hatch than for white crappie that year.
- **Freshwater Drum:** 32 aged fish, 25 from the 1951 year-class. Growth was under three inches per year, so the authors doubted drum would become important to sport or commercial fisheries.
- **Common Carp:** 42 aged fish, 41 from the 1950 year-class, suggesting reduced recent reproduction. Third-year fish averaged 12.2 inches and 12.7 ounces.
- **Smallmouth Buffalo:** 67 aged fish, 62 from the 1949 year-class. Fourth-year fish averaged 9.4 inches and 6.5 ounces.
- **[[Flathead Catfish]]:** 16 aged fish. Fourth-year fish (1949 class) averaged 16.4 inches and 1.5 pounds, well above the 8.9 inches and 0.2 pounds reported for the same class at Grand Lake.
- **[[Channel Catfish]]:** 21 aged fish. Third-year fish averaged about 10 inches. Growth was similar to Oklahoma and other Kansas impoundments.
- **[[Bullhead Catfish]]:** 13 aged fish. Third-year fish averaged 8.6 inches, near the Oklahoma average.
- **[[Walleye]]:** 12 stocked fish measured 6 to 7⅜ inches, indicating growth of over six inches in about four months. Thirty-eight walleye collected below the dam on 3 February 1953 averaged 10.5 inches.

**Conclusions and recommendations:**
- Year-class strength varied: smallmouth buffalo peaked in 1949, carp in 1950, and white crappie and drum in 1951.
- Carp and buffalo require continued monitoring before they can be ruled out as nuisance species.
- Crappie, channel catfish and largemouth bass had not reached peak development. The 1952 bass year-class was expected to improve angling by 1954.
- The authors concluded that the reservoir's fisheries could be used far more than they were at the time.
