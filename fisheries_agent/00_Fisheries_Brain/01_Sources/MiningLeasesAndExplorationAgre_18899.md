## Executive Summary

Don H. Sherwood, a Denver mining attorney, argues in a 1987 seminar paper that the mining industry has changed fundamentally since the late 1970s. Oil-company acquisitions, foreign ownership, hostile takeovers, spin-offs and bankruptcies have produced a "new model" mining company that he describes as a "monster." Landowners who lease mineral rights, including large timber companies, railroads and [[Alaska Native Regional Corporations]], face risks that traditional boilerplate contracts do not address. The paper's central advice is that landowners should require a full, severable guarantee from the parent corporation, and should treat leases and exploration agreements with suspicion.

## Key Findings

1. **Changed industry structure.** Oil-company ownership of metal mining, foreign subsidiaries and the spin-off of assets have created companies with "huge debt, but no geologists" and short-lived corporate identities.
2. **Subsidiary "chains" shield parents.** Mining companies often take title to exploration prospects through wholly owned subsidiaries. The author regards the stated reasons (avoiding board approval and SEC contingent-liability footnotes) as unconvincing. The main reason is to limit parent liability.
3. **Exploration-subsidiary bankruptcy.** Since 1981, some solvent parent companies have let exploration subsidiaries enter bankruptcy, leaving lessors to deal with trustees and creditors' committees.
4. **Landowner liability.** Under current liability-without-fault trends, the landowner can become the "sole target" of clean-up claims once the operator and intervening operators cannot pay.
5. **Spin-offs.** A subsidiary can be spun off to shareholders with its obligations, so the landowner loses recourse unless the parent guarantee survives.
6. **Guarantee as the key protection.** The author advises that a full and severable guarantee of all obligations, including indemnification, by the parent corporation is essential. The guarantee must not be assignable and must survive spin-offs and assignments. He calls it malpractice not to insist on one, and says a refusal should be treated as evidence of intent to escape liability.
7. **Exploration agreements versus leases.** Exploration agreements generally terminate within two to five years if commitments are unmet. Leases are harder to terminate, since forfeiture is difficult to prove and "commercial production" can extend them indefinitely.
8. **Bankruptcy effects.** Creditors may keep a lease alive if its rental obligations are nominal, even when no production occurs and no royalties flow. Exploration agreements are harder for creditors to keep alive, because the required work is costly.
9. **Advance minimum royalties.** These are recoupable, and the author notes they are often too low or eroded by inflation unless escalator clauses are included.
10. **Assignment and change of control.** Customary "consent not unreasonably denied" language is inadequate. Assignees may be shells or foreign entities, and a hostile takeover of the lessee may warrant a right to terminate.
11. **Core recommendation.** "No guarantee, no contract." The author holds that the mining company, not the landowner, should bear the risk.
