# FY-1989 Draft Annual Report: Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fishes, Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This January 1990 draft annual report, circulated by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program), reviews fiscal year 1989 work on endangered fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. It compiles accomplishment highlights, a summary of changes and shortcomings, and individual project reports from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the Service, the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], Utah, and the Colorado Water Conservation Board. Work covered [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. Several planned projects slipped, and a number were cancelled by mid-year reprogramming. The report is a draft: Utah's reports and several project reports were not yet received, and comments were requested by January 31, 1990, before finalization under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]].

## Background & Description

The [[Recovery Implementation Program]] operates under direction from the Colorado River Recovery Implementation Committee, which requires every funded project to submit an annual report covering accomplishments against objectives, a preliminary data analysis, recommendations, and an accounting of funds. The Technical Group reviews these reports and advises the Committee.

The FY-89 Approved Work Plan (Table 1.1) totaled $2,303.2 thousand. The Revised Work Plan (Table 1.2, revised 1/12/90) totaled $2,324.7 thousand. Funding came from the Service, Reclamation, Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming. Spending by category in the revised plan was approximately:

- Habitat Management: $991.3K
- Research, Monitoring, and Data Management: $267.0K
- Program Management: $290.7K
- Stocking: $255.5K
- Other: $388.0K
- Nonnative Fish/Sportfish: $120.2K
- Habitat Development: $12.0K

The target species are the native fishes of the big rivers of the upper basin. The report describes several as rare or endangered, with wild populations surviving only in parts of the system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Work addressed flow needs, habitat, and interactions with nonnative species:

- **Flow and habitat.** Flow recommendations were developed for the [[Yampa River]], the [[15-Mile Reach]] (July to September, a period of critically low flows), and the upper Colorado River, along with the [[Flaming Gorge Biological Opinion]] supporting analyses. The [[Ruedi Reservoir]] water lease (10,000 acre-feet per year over 40 years) supplements 15-Mile Reach flows. Instream flow work relied on the [[PHABSIM]] model, which the report notes is problematic in large, turbid rivers.
- **Nonnative interactions.** Predation by and competition from nonnative gamefish, including [[Northern Pike]] and channel catfish, were studied in the Yampa and Colorado rivers, as were the habitat preferences of nonnative and native young fishes.
- **Tributaries and canyons.** The [[Little Snake River]], a major tributary to the Yampa and Green rivers, was found to provide habitat for endangered fishes during spring runoff. [[Cataract Canyon]] was studied for native fish spawning and humpback chub.
- **Backwaters and nursery habitat.** The [[Green River]] backwater studies examined how flow affects nursery habitat for Colorado squawfish.
- **Taxonomy.** Confusion over the taxonomy of the [[Gila Taxonomy]] genus prompted a review of existing information on species, distribution, and collections.

## Key Findings

**Accomplishments**

- Supporting hydrology and biology reports for the Flaming Gorge Biological Opinion were largely completed.
- Yampa River Phase I (biological) and Phase II (interim flow recommendations) reports were produced. The PHABSIM results were not used for Yampa flow recommendations.
- The 15-Mile Reach flow recommendations for July–September were finalized. Summer 1989 Ruedi releases were reported as successful.
- The [[Propagation Genetics Management Plan]] was implemented: an ad hoc Propagation Work Group was formed, a full-time propagation coordinator was hired, and the plan was updated.
- Information and education outputs included the "Swimming Upstream" brochure, the first Recovery Program newsletter, and five permanent river-access signs in Colorado.
- The standardized monitoring program for adult and young-of-the-year Colorado squawfish was completed (see [[Standardized Monitoring Program]]).

**Hypotheses developed**

- Adult humpback chub numbers may be limited by availability of their preferred unique habitat.
- Colorado squawfish and sympatric fish reproductive success is related to peak river discharge, though the relation may differ among species. Enhancing non-native fish reproduction may be an unavoidable consequence of flows recommended for squawfish.
- Age-0 squawfish habitat use resembles that of several nonnative fishes but differs from some native species.

**Kenney Reservoir stocking**

- About 32,000 Colorado squawfish averaging 2.3 in. were stocked on 26 April 1989, versus a planned 50,000 averaging 4 in.
- In 2 years of sampling, 246 squawfish were collected in the reservoir and 213 in the White River below Taylor Draw Dam, including wild adults. Stocked fish were found up to 28.5 river miles downstream.
- Stocked fish appeared to grow to 5–6 in. in year one and 9–10 in. in year two.
- Recommendation: stock fish at least 4 in. in 1990, preferably in late May. See [[Kenney Reservoir]].

**PIT tag study ([[PIT Tagging]])**

- Tag retention and detection were high in juvenile razorback sucker, juvenile Colorado squawfish, and adult roundtail chub.
- Survival in riverside gravel-pit ponds was low, attributed to capture and handling stress and uncontrollable water levels. The effect of tags on growth and survival could not be evaluated.
- The draft report recommends continued evaluation on fish around 100 mm.

**Overwinter survival (laboratory study)**

- Hatchery-reared age-0 Colorado squawfish (30, 36, and 44 mm) were held at 3–4 °C for 210 days, with dry-pellet, starved, and brine-shrimp feeding regimes.
- Fish grew little, condition declined with winter exposure, and lipid content was inversely related to winter duration.
- Survival was significantly lower in starved small and medium fish than in fed fish of those sizes.

**Yampa River northern pike study**

- Eight field trips yielded 171 northern pike, 37 channel catfish, 23 Colorado squawfish, and 3 smallmouth bass.
- Totals to date: 252 northern pike captured (188 tagged, 29 recaptured) and 93 channel catfish captured (58 tagged, 1 recaptured).
- Radio-tagged pike moved long distances during runoff, and two overwintered in a deep pool reach. No squawfish were identified in pike stomachs.

**Little Snake River**

- Two radio-tagged Colorado squawfish used the river in spring, one moving on to the Yampa Canyon spawning area.
- In 308 adult and large juvenile fish collected, there were seven suspected humpback chub, two hybrids, and one Colorado squawfish. Colorado roundtail chub made up 53% of the catch.
- Juvenile sampling (1,260 fish) was dominated by flannelmouth sucker (82%).
- The authors recommend maintaining natural flow regimes and protecting the canyon section.

**Budget and program issues**

- Reclamation gave an extra $30,000 to the Larval Fish Laboratory for Flaming Gorge larval sampling. The Service saved $41,000 by deferring a hire, with $32,000 going to a Reclamation flow alternatives study and $9,000 to Gila taxonomy.
- Cancelled due to mid-year reprogramming: Bonytail Retrieval, Issue Paper on Exotic Fish Management, and Establishment of Recovery Goals.
- The Gila taxonomy project manager (Smithsonian) began in July 1989. A draft work plan was circulated for review in mid-December, and the initial timeframe was judged "very optimistic." Spending was $62,000 against a $53,000 estimate.
- The Upper Colorado River Basin database held 158 data files from 4 organizations. Many researchers missed the March 1 submission deadline.
- Delays pushed the San Juan River Management Plan to April–May 1990 and the Razorback Sucker Management Plan to October 1990.

**Recommendations (selected)**

- Establish base or core funding for the two field stations and a contingency fund for urgent situations.
- Create a full-time fish culturist position at the Ouray Fish Culture Facility.
- Conduct a genetic census of all target species before further habitat loss or hybridization.
- Maintain the Little Snake River's natural flow regime and protect its canyon.
- Incorporate the San Juan River fully into the upper basin program.
