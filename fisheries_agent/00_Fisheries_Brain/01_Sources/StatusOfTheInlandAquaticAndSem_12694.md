# Status of the Inland Aquatic and Semi-Aquatic Mollusks of the American Southwest

#malacology #hydrobiidae #assimineidae #physidae #thiaridae #conservation

## Executive Summary

This 1973 report, submitted by James J. Landye of the [[Lower Colorado River Basin Research Laboratory]] (Arizona State University) to the U.S. Department of Interior, Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, Office of Rare and Endangered Species, assesses the status of threatened, rare, and endangered aquatic and semi-aquatic mollusks in inland deserts of the American Southwest. The report documents numerous populations of described and undescribed species, the majority of which belong to the snail family [[Hydrobiidae]]. It identifies groundwater pumpage, direct spring modification, and biological pollution by introduced species as the main threats. It proposes species-by-species protective measures and calls for urgent further survey work, because many springs are disappearing before their snail faunas, particularly soft anatomy needed for taxonomy, can be studied.

## Background & Description

**Scope and geography.** Investigations covered portions of west Texas, southern New Mexico, Arizona, southeastern California, southern Nevada, and southern Utah. More than 90% of the animals listed in the appendices are hydrobiid gastropods.

**Why hydrobiids dominate.** Hydrobiids are relicts of at least Pliocene age. Their dispersal ability is very limited and their habitat requirements are narrow. When [[Desertification of the American Southwest]] isolated springs, many populations became restricted, and many spring complexes now hold snails that have evolved into distinct taxa. Hydrobiids persist in seepages too small or harsh for most other organisms. This makes them useful indicators for [[Biogeography]] and [[Paleohydrology]].

**Survey completeness.** Coverage was relatively good in Arizona, New Mexico, and west Texas. Southern Nevada and eastern California require more field work because of their numerous small spring systems, which probably support endemic species and higher taxa.

**Taxonomic difficulties.** Systematics of invertebrates in the Southwest, especially Hydrobiidae, are poorly resolved. Many taxa cannot be defined without soft-anatomy data, and the author notes that taxonomy of many hydrobiids must await study by highly trained specialists.

**Appendices (summary).**
- **Appendix I:** Species accounts for threatened, rare, or endangered taxa, arranged by state (Arizona, California, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Utah) using a standardized format.
- **Appendix II:** Evaluation of the endemic molluscan fauna of the [[Ash Meadows]] system, Nye County, Nevada.
- **Appendix III:** Summary of biological data on the Oriental snail [[Melanoides Tuberculatus]] introduced into North America.
- **Appendix IV:** Review of human parasites associated with the Oriental snails [[Thiara Granifera]] and *Thiara (Melanoides) tuberculata*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Reasons for decline

1. **Groundwater pumpage (greatest present hazard).** Pumping lowers spring levels, sometimes many kilometers from the spring. Major springs in the White River Valley, Ash Meadows (both Nevada), and Death Valley, California, depend on the same basic aquifer ([[Winograd 1971]]). Documented extirpations include:
   - Comanche Spring, Ft. Stockton, Texas: dried by pumpage from its direct aquifer; its largely endemic fauna, including fishes, mollusks, and likely crustaceans, was destroyed.
   - Springs and spring runs near Roswell, New Mexico: dried by water mining.
   - Lower Mimbres basin near Deming, New Mexico: pumpage within the past 5 to 7 years caused essential drying of springs in northern Chihuahua, Mexico, and destruction of much of the endemic mollusk and fish fauna at Ojo de Palomas Vieja.

2. **Direct spring manipulation.** Spring heads are capped, piped, or channelized into canals. Living mollusks were rarely found in modified springs, except widespread, tolerant species. Damming also harms endemic mollusks. At the [[Owens Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon radiosus*) refuge in eastern California, an endemic mollusk was seemingly extirpated when its head-spring habitat was flooded.

3. **Biological pollution by introduced species (likely major future factor).** Introductions of non-native vertebrates and invertebrates are best prevented before they occur, through bans or permits based on impact evaluation. Specific threats include:
   - *Melanoides tuberculatus*: common in the aquarium trade; has decimated populations of the native hydrobiid genus *Tryonia* in springs of the Muddy (Moapa) River valley, Pahranagat Valley, and Ash Meadows, Nevada.
   - *Corbicula manilensis*: an Oriental clam becoming increasingly widespread.
   - *Thiara granifera*: an Oriental gastropod recently collected at Resting Springs near Tecopa, California, and implicated as a host for human parasites.

### Recommended agency action
Federal, state, and local agencies should be contacted immediately regarding the possible harmful effects of introduced mollusks on native animals, plants, ecosystems, and people.

### Associated biota
Several endemic springs also harbor fishes, crustaceans, and other invertebrates, including the [[Ash Meadows Pupfish]] system, the Quitobaquito pupfish (*Cyprinodon maculatus*, n. subsp.), the Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), an endemic isopod (Sphaeromatidae, the only inland U.S. occurrence of the family, probably an undescribed genus), and endemic amphipods.

## Key Findings

### Threats and conservation priorities
- **Groundwater pumpage** is the greatest present hazard. Spring discharge is linked through shared aquifers across basins, so pumping in one valley can extirpate springs elsewhere.
- **Direct spring modification** (capping, piping, damming, channelization) is the second-largest threat.
- **Biological pollution by introduced species** is likely to be a major future threat. Prevention is far more effective than remediation.
- **Urgent further survey** is required. Many springs are disappearing, and without soft-anatomy data many hydrobiid taxa may never be defined. The report describes this as a biological "salvage" operation.

### Taxonomic and biogeographic findings
- More than 90% of listed taxa are Hydrobiidae.
- A "remarkable number" of undescribed taxa were found in a brief survey.
- Many spring snails are relicts of an estuarine, salt-marsh ancestry of great antiquity (reported for *Assiminea* species).
- *Fluminicola* from Nevada is used in its broadest sense and will likely be placed in a new genus.
- *Tryonia* of Ash Meadows may comprise several species or subspecies based on male soft anatomy; female anatomy remains to be studied.
- *Fontelicella micrococcus* at Roger's Spring (Ash Meadows) demonstrates past links between the Death Valley and Ash Meadows systems.
- The hydrobiid *Travertine Springs* and *Littlefield Springs* taxa, and the White River Valley taxon, are described as new genera and species.

### Key population and status data
- **Ash Meadows *Fluminicola erythropoma*:** locally common (≈2/cm²) in suitable habitat totaling about 25 m² in 1973 across the surveyed Ash Meadows system. Habitat at Point of Rocks Spring complex was halved in four years.
- **Overton *Assiminea* n. sp.:** found rarely (≤1/cm²) along the first 10–15 m of the spring run at Blue Point Spring.
- **Roswell *Fontelicella* n. sp.:** habitat about 4 m × 1 m; snail abundant, averaging 7/cm²; population rose substantially between 1967 and 1973 sampling.
- **Roswell *Tryonia* n. sp.:** habitat about 4 m × 1 m; relatively rare (about 1/cm²); none found in 1967 and 1971, but relatively easy to collect in 1973.
- **Red Rock *Fontelicella* n. sp.:** found rarely (<1/cm²) along about 20 m of spring run; spring runoff from Hidden Lakes lowered water temperature to at least 9.0 °C, possibly the lowest recorded habitat temperature for southwestern hydrobiids.
- **Blue Spring (NM) *Fontelicella* n. sp.:** average density ~2/cm² along the upper natural run; Blue Spring populations stable in 1968, 1971, and 1973; Castle Spring populations fluctuated from absent (1967) to abundant (1971) to low (1973).
- **Burn's Spring *Tryonia* n. sp.:** about 30 individuals taken in two visits; habitat restricted to 3 m of spring run; classed as exceedingly endangered.
- **Littlefield Springs hydrobiid:** abundant populations in 1972 were reduced to fewer than 30 individuals in 1973 after bulldozer operations near a highway; a transplant of 30 individuals was made from the southernmost spring.
- **Three Forks hydrobiid:** restricted to about 5 m of spring head and run about 1 m wide; densities up to 2/cm².

### Melanoides tuberculatus (Appendix III)
- **Identification:** light to dark brown, with vertically oriented darker strips; apex sharp and needle-like; shell up to 35 mm long, typically about 20 mm; operculum thick, blackened, and markedly ovate.
- **Survival tests (laboratory):**
  - Eight hours in 10 mg/L sodium cyanide: no deaths at onset of an eradication attempt.
  - Five days of desiccation at room temperature (relative humidity 5–15%): all 50 individuals of various sizes survived.
  - About 48 hours in a commercial chlorox and water mixture (active chlorine concentration unknown): about 95% mortality, with survivors severely damaged; some larger animals reproduced after transfer to tap water.
- **Reproductive effects on egg-laying fishes:** 123 situations were reviewed; 84 met criteria. Of these, 30 tanks had snails present and none produced young fish. Of 54 tanks without snails, 49 (96.3%) produced at least one to many tens of young.
- **Predation:** at Blue Point and Rogers springs (Overton arm of Lake Mead), the convict cichlid (*Cichlasoma nigrofasciatum*) guards nests, but nocturnal *Melanoides* were observed ringing the nest and feeding on eggs (1969).
- **Dispersal pathways (speculative):** fish and aquatic plant transfers (e.g., *Ceratophyllum*, water hyacinth), bird digestive tracts, dried mud on birds or equipment, and children moving animals between springs or home aquaria.
- **Impact on native *Tryonia* (Ash Meadows):** introduced about 1971; essentially extirpated *Tryonia* by 1972–73. Introduction into Big Spring (c. 1970) reduced native *Tryonia* almost to zero.

### Parasitology (Appendix IV)
- *Thiara granifera* and *Melanoides tuberculatus* are known intermediate hosts for parasitic trematode flukes.
- **Paragonimus westermani** (lung fluke): second intermediate host is a crayfish or crab; occurs in North American mammals; in the Southwest, crayfish harvested for food may serve as hosts. The report suggests this parasite may have repeatedly arrived with military personnel returning from Southeast Asia.
- **Metagonimus yokogawai** (heterophyid fluke): secondary hosts are cyprinid or siluriform fishes, including *Leuciscus*, a genus related to Western American *Gila*.
- Appendix IV lists at least 15 heterophyid species with known snail, fish, and mammal hosts, noting that fish-eating birds may act as final hosts, which could allow rapid dispersal if introduced.

### Management recommendations (species-specific)
- Protect and fence endangered springs (e.g., Point of Rocks Spring complex, Badwater seepage, Hot Creek, Flag Spring, Monkey Spring, Tavasci Spring).
- Include Blue and Rogers springs in the [[Lake Mead National Recreation Area]] master plan as natural areas; ban further distribution of *Melanoides*.
- Eradicate *Corbicula manilensis* from Montezuma Castle National Monument and warn visitors against transporting it into Montezuma Well.
- Maintain a small trickle of water from Sedillo Spring to sustain the endemic isopod at Socorro.
- Request official notification and cooperation from landowners, including the Nature Conservancy (Canelo Cienega), Deep Springs College, Los Angeles Water and Power (Wilkerson Springs), and the Roswell Country Club.
- Monitor populations annually or semi-annually at many sites and maintain baseline data.
- Provide increased protection from oil and brine spills at Diamond-Y (Wilbank) Spring; oil companies (Exxon, Northern Natural Gas, Phillips Oil Co.) had already built retaining dikes. An ecological study by Steve Kennedy was underway in 1973–74.
- Consider designating the Ash Meadows area as part of [[Death Valley National Monument]] or as a separate protected entity, given its endemic fauna and its hydrologic link to Death Valley aquifers.
