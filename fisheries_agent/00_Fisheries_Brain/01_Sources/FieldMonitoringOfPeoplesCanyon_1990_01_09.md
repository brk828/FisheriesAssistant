# Field Monitoring of Peoples Canyon (1989–1990)

## Executive Summary
This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum (9 January 1990) documents a site visit to [[Peoples Canyon]], Arizona, conducted with the [[Bureau of Land Management]] on 19 December 1989. The visit assessed the status of two federally endangered fish species — [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]] — previously introduced into the canyon as part of a native fish reintroduction/refugium effort. A major flood event of unknown timing caused severe geomorphic disturbance to the stream channel, resulting in the **complete extirpation of both fish species** from Peoples Canyon. Amphibian populations (lowland leopard frogs) persisted and showed evidence of successful recruitment despite the disturbance.

## Background & Description
The source memo does not provide direct morphological descriptions of the fish species themselves; it is a management/monitoring report rather than a taxonomic account. Relevant taxonomic identifiers include:
- **[[Gila Topminnow]]** (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) — family [[Poeciliidae]], a small live-bearing desert fish native to the Gila River basin.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius macularius*) — family [[Cyprinodontidae]], a small, hardy desert cyprinodontid adapted to extreme thermal and salinity fluctuations.

Both are federally listed endangered species and are commonly used in Southwestern U.S. native fish refugium/reintroduction programs due to their tolerance of harsh, variable desert stream conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat type**: Intermittent desert canyon stream subject to periodic, high-magnitude flash flooding; substrate ranges from fine sediment/herbaceous banks to scoured parent rock and coarse allochthonous debris post-flood.
- **Disturbance regime**: The watershed experiences recurrent flood events capable of restructuring channel morphology — this event caused channel downcutting of nearly one meter, formation of large pools, uprooting/shredding of riparian trees, and loss of herbaceous ground cover.
- **Species-specific resilience**:
  - [[Gila Topminnow]] persisted through periodic flooding for over 5 years following its 1984 introduction, suggesting relatively higher flood resilience within this system — until the terminal flood event documented here.
  - [[Desert Pupfish]] showed a documented history of repeated local elimination by flood events despite multiple reintroduction attempts, indicating lower population resilience or refugial habitat stability compared to topminnow in this specific watershed.
- **Co-occurring fauna**: [[Lowland Leopard Frog]] populations remained common post-flood, with tadpoles present, indicating continued amphibian breeding habitat suitability despite fish extirpation — highlighting differential vulnerability to flood disturbance across taxa.
- **Regional context**: Peoples Canyon is linked hydrologically to the broader Santa Maria and Big Sandy River watershed; USGS hydrograph stations in this vicinity were proposed as a means to establish the timing of the flood event.

## Key Findings
- **Introduction history**:
  - [[Gila Topminnow]]: single introduction of n=800 individuals on 13 July 1984.
  - [[Desert Pupfish]]: four separate introductions — 18 August 1982 (n=81), 22 December 1983 (n=112), 14 May 1985 (n=325), and 9 September 1988 (n=1,000).
- **Survival outcomes**: Topminnow persisted >5 years despite periodic flooding; pupfish were repeatedly extirpated by flood events across multiple introduction attempts, reflecting poor long-term establishment success in this watershed.
- **Terminal event**: A large-magnitude flood (exact timing undetermined) caused severe physical alteration of the stream channel — tree uprooting/shredding, ~1 m channel downcutting, large pool formation, and loss of herbaceous cover/streambank scouring to bedrock.
- **Outcome**: Both [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]] were confirmed **extirpated** from Peoples Canyon as of the December 1989 survey.
- **Amphibian resilience**: Lowland leopard frogs remained common with active tadpole recruitment, suggesting differential resistance/recovery capacity to flood disturbance compared to the introduced fish populations.
- **Management implications**: Findings were circulated to multiple stakeholders including [[Arizona State University]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], indicating this site's role in a broader interagency native fish conservation/refugium network for endangered Sonoran Desert fishes.
