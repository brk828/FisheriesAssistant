## Executive Summary

This 1950 ichthyological survey by Robert Rush Miller and Howard Filliott documents the first fish collections from the Rio San Pedro drainage in Sonora, Mexico, and from San Bernardino Creek near Douglas, Arizona. The study represents a significant contribution to understanding the freshwater [[Fish Fauna of Mexico]], establishing new distributional records for several species previously unknown from Mexican waters. Notably, the survey confirms three species and one subspecies as new records for Mexico, including the [[Gila Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), and both a native and introduced catfish/sunfish pairing. The work also highlights concerning environmental conditions — including extreme regional aridity (annual rainfall ~12 inches) and drastically reduced stream flow — that likely threaten native fish populations such as the [[Gila Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*) and [[Scaleless Spinedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), neither of which were observed during the survey despite historical presence in the region.

## Background & Description

- **Catostomus insignis** (Gila sucker): Specimens ranged from half-grown (68–112 mm SL) to large adults (264–290 mm SL). One large female extruded ripe eggs under slight pressure, indicating imminent or active spawning.

- **Tiaroga cobitis** (Loach minnow): Small adults measuring 37–48 mm in length; collected from rocky riffle habitats.

- **Ameiurus melas melas** (Northern black bullhead): Distinguished by shorter spines, a heavier body, and fewer anal rays (18–21, usually 19–20) compared to the southwestern subspecies. Largest specimen collected weighed 16 ounces.

- **Ameiurus melas catulus** (Southwestern black bullhead): Anal ray counts of 20 (n=3), 21 (n=3), and 22 (n=2). Specimens with 20 rays exhibited elongate dorsal and pectoral spines, a diagnostic character for this subspecies. Length range 57–209 mm.

- **Lepomis macrochirus purpurescens** (Southeastern bluegill): Adults measured 102–105 mm. Distinguished by broad, comparatively few vertical bars and 12 anal rays. In life, soft posterior fin tissue (especially anal fin) appeared reddish in half-grown individuals. Contrasts with *L. m. speciosus*, which has a modal anal ray count of 10 and narrower, more numerous bars.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat conditions**: The Rio San Pedro region is characterized by extreme aridity; average annual rainfall at San Pedro Ranch (1935–1949) was only 12 inches. The surrounding landscape is open, dry, and increasingly water-stressed.

- **Catostomus insignis**: Found in the Rio San Rafael tributary of Rio San Pedro, near a large rock dam. Collected both above and below this structure, indicating habitat connectivity was still partially intact at the time.

- **Tiaroga cobitis**: Restricted to rocky riffle microhabitats within the Rio San Pedro/Rio San Rafael confluence. Riffles were shallow (2–4 inches deep), narrow (1–3 feet wide), covered in dense green algae, and fully sun-exposed. Separated by long stretches of shallow sandy substrate unsuitable for the species. Collected using derris root (a piscicide/rotenone-like substance).

- **Ameiurus melas melas**: Abundant in Rio Rafael and an associated reservoir. Found in isolated pools maintained by spring seepage, cut off from the main channel by gravel bars. Heavily exploited by local fishers in the reservoir. Historical testimony suggests introduction as early as 1906.

- **Ameiurus melas catulus**: Collected from San Bernardino Creek and an adjacent pond near the U.S.–Mexico border, within the [[Rio Yaqui]] drainage — notable because this subspecies is not native to Mexican waters west of the Continental Divide.

- **Lepomis macrochirus purpurescens**: Collected from San Bernardino Creek in shallow water (up to 4 feet deep). This subspecies is native to the southeastern U.S. (Florida to North Carolina) and its presence in Sonora is attributed to hatchery introduction, likely originating from stock transplanted via the Federal hatchery at San Marcos, Texas (originally sourced from Lake Park, Georgia — hence the local name "Georgia bluegill"). It was preferentially stocked over the native *L. m. speciosus* due to faster growth and better acceptance of artificial food. Not detected during the 1950 survey despite prior 1943 records, likely due to extreme low water/near-complete desiccation of the creek in the U.S. portion.

- **Pantosteus clarki** and **Meda fulgida**: Both historically inhabited Rio San Pedro in Mexico and persist in the Arizona portion of the river, but were not recorded during this survey — possibly due to unfavorable (reduced) water conditions, with potential for recolonization if conditions improve.

## Key Findings

- First documented fish collections from the Mexican portion of the [[Rio San Pedro]], a tributary of the [[Gila River]] system, establishing new Mexican distributional records for *Catostomus insignis*, *Tiaroga cobitis*, and two subspecies-level records involving *Ameiurus melas* and *Lepomis macrochirus*.
- *Ameiurus melas catulus* and *Lepomis macrochirus purpurescens* records from San Bernardino Creek represent introductions/range extensions not native to Mexico west of the Continental Divide, underscoring anthropogenic influence on regional fish faunas.
- Evidence of active hatchery-mediated translocation of *Lepomis macrochirus purpurescens* from Georgia to Texas to Mexico highlights early 20th-century fish stocking practices favoring non-native subspecies for aquaculture traits (growth rate, feed acceptance).
- Severe regional aridity and reduced stream flow are implicated in the likely decline or local extirpation of native species such as *Tiaroga cobitis*, *Pantosteus clarki*, and *Meda fulgida*, suggesting these findings may serve as an early historical baseline for tracking [[Freshwater Fish Decline in Arid Southwestern Drainages]].
- Reproductive data (ripe eggs in *Catostomus insignis*) indicate the species was spawning in the Rio San Rafael in late April, providing phenological data relevant to conservation timing.
- The paper documents anthropogenic exploitation pressure (heavy local fishing of introduced *Ameiurus melas melas* in a reservoir), illustrating early interactions between introduced species and subsistence/recreational fisheries in the region.
