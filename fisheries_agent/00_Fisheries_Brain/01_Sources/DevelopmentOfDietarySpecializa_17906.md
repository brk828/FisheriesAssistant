# Development of Dietary Specializations in a Summer Community of Juvenile Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 study by [[Allen Keast]] (Queen's University) examines how a species-rich community of juvenile lake fishes in a [[Macrophyte Weedbed]] in [[Lake Opinicon]], Ontario, partitions food while growing rapidly. Food was not limiting: prey abundance peaked alongside plant growth in mid-July. Diet overlap among co-occurring juveniles was low, and [[Mouth Width]] helped separate species by prey size. Juvenile diets differed from both preceding larval diets and adult diets, so early transition to adult-type feeding was only a partial explanation for diet separation.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a conflict in summer lake fish communities. Many species produce young at the same time, and these young are confined to weedbeds to avoid predation ([[Predator Avoidance]]). Larvae and early juveniles are limited to small prey such as copepods and small cladocerans by their small mouths and bodies, yet growth is fastest in the first months of exogenous feeding. The study tested four questions: (1) what juveniles eat, (2) how much diet overlap exists, (3) whether the resource base limits feeding, and (4) whether co-occurrence depends on an early shift to adult-type diets.

Juvenile period was defined by ossification, caudal flexion, and fin development (Balon 1975). The study covered mid-June to late August 1980, with fish sampled by a weighted seine at roughly two-week intervals. Diet was measured both as prey numbers and as mean percent volume, with prey volumes estimated from body dimensions using geometric approximations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Weedbed development:** In early May the site held only compacted remains of the previous summer's vegetation. Plants reached 0.4-0.6 m by mid-June and full development was reached in mid-July, at 0.8-1.2 m average height with mean dry-weight biomass of 480 g m⁻². Dominant plants included [[Potamogeton Robbinsii]], [[Vallisneria Americana]], and [[Myriophyllum]] species.
- **Resource base:** Sampling covered zooplankton (Schindler trap), zoobenthos (Ekman grab), and invertebrates on macrophyte foliage. The two major prey groups were [[Cladocera]] (38 species recorded in the lake, 28 of them chydorids) and [[Chironomid Larvae]] (about 120 species).
- **Seasonal coupling:** Fish abundance and species diversity peaked in mid-July, alongside peaks in major prey. Fish dispersed in August as prey numbers dropped and the weedbeds declined.
- **Study site:** Lake Opinicon (7.7 x 0.6 km), part of the Rideau Canal system, with sampling at the head of Cow Island Bay.

## Key Findings

- **Community assembly:** The community grew from two species in mid-June to six in early July, peaked at nine species and 260 individuals per 100 m² in mid-July, and fell to four species by late August.
- **Diet overlap:** In mid-July, Schoener overlap exceeded 0.5 for only five of 36 species pairs and 0.25 for eleven. Overlap was consistently low across sampling dates, in contrast to greater overlap in the preceding May-June larval community (Keast 1980).
- **Diet breadth:** Broadest in [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Rock Bass]] and in *Fundulus diaphanus*; narrowest in *Notropis heterodon* and *Notemigonus crysoleucas*.
- **Food limitation:** Prey abundance and fish consumption corresponded closely for small chironomid larvae, newly hatched Ephemeroptera nymphs, and the cladocerans *Bosmina longirostris* and *Chydorus sphaericus*. The authors concluded food was not limiting.
- **Mouth width:** Mouth width was 0.2-0.4 mm at 9-12 mm body length (0.2 mm in *P. notatus*) and increased four to five times by late summer. Largemouth Bass and Rock Bass had the largest mouths and the steepest increase relative to body length, and they consumed a wider size range of prey.
- **Planktivore selection:** Planktivores kept consuming prey of 0.2-0.4 mm throughout the six-week period, despite growing mouths, and focused on the two most abundant small cladocerans.
- **Chironomid feeding:** *F. diaphanus*, *Lepomis* spp., and *P. notatus* fed mainly on chironomid larvae under 3 mm, while Largemouth Bass and Rock Bass took increasingly larger larvae as they grew.
- **Adult diet timing:** Adult-type diets were reached at different times. *L. macrochirus* had its adult diet by fall, *L. gibbosus* began molluscivory by its first spring, and Largemouth Bass became predominantly piscivorous the following spring. Decapods, a major adult food of Rock Bass, did not enter its diet until age 3. The authors concluded that early transition to adult diets was only a partial mechanism for diet separation.
- **Predator protection and school formation:** Weedbeds protect predator-prone small fish, and juveniles of several species join adult schools in August to forage in open water.
- **Conclusion:** The weedbed supported a varied juvenile fish community with abundant, diverse prey and some predator protection, enabling rapid growth in the first summer of life.
