# Procedures for Stocking Nonnative Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #nonnative-fish-management

## Executive Summary

This document is a draft policy framework, dated December 10, 1993 and revised for a January 5, 1994 meeting, that governs the stocking of nonnative fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin. It was developed by consensus among the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the fish and wildlife agencies of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming. Its purpose is to ensure that all future stocking of nonnative fishes is consistent with the recovery of four endangered fishes under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]]: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. The procedures sort stocking into three categories: routine (acceptable), unacceptable, and case-by-case evaluation. The document also includes a cover memorandum, a draft meeting agenda, a summary of public comments, and a schedule for finalization.

## Background & Description

The procedures were built on several principles. The Recovery Program aims to recover the four endangered fishes by establishing self-sustaining populations and protecting their habitat. The procedures also allow nonnative sport fishing and private aquaculture to continue where they do not conflict with recovery. The Recovery Program directs that stocking of nonnative species be confined to areas where the absence of potential conflict with rare or endangered species has been demonstrated. The procedures are not intended to abolish nonnative stocking, but to guide it so that it is consistent with recovery.

The document states that the [[Endangered Species Act]] responsibilities of the Service and the statutory fish and wildlife management responsibilities of the states cannot be abrogated. The stated goal is consensus, so that neither agency must independently assert its authority.

The procedures are organized into the following parts:
- **Part II.A (Table 1):** Nonnative species and locations/situations where routine stocking is acceptable. Examples include [[Triploid Grass Carp]] (in ponds and reservoirs outside the 40-year floodplain, screened to prevent escapement), [[Redside Shiner]], [[Threadfin Shad]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]] and [[Utah Chub]].
- **Part II.B:** Species evaluated case by case, including [[Walleye]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Tiger Muskie]] and [[Wiper]] (a striped bass × white bass hybrid).
- **Part II.C:** A case-by-case proposal must address five minimum items: purpose and location; species, numbers and rationale; escapement potential and control measures; potential impacts on threatened and endangered species and remedial measures; and the expected effect on the endangered fishes.
- **Part II.D (Table 2):** A list of about 25 nonnative species already present in the Upper Basin but not routinely managed by stocking, such as [[Common Carp]], [[Northern Pike]] and several minnows, suckers, bullheads and darters. Any stocking proposal for these would need case-by-case evaluation.
- **Part III:** Introduction of new species, which is expected to be rare and requires ecological justification.
- **Part IV:** No stocking of nonsalmonid nonnative species into riverine habitat occupied by the endangered fishes.
- **Part V:** A multi-step review process (see Key Findings).
- **Part VI:** Procedures for requesting modification.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document states that habitat and biological communities in the Upper Basin have been severely altered. It notes that, although it is difficult to fully assess the relative contribution of habitat alteration and nonnative species to the decline of the endangered fishes, it is not possible to definitively identify the contribution of each factor. In many cases, the spread of nonnatives was made possible by habitat alteration and by the introduction of, and demand for, fish stocking for recreational purposes. These factors are to be considered in recovery efforts.

Nonnative stocking is described as an important component of sport fishery management and commercial aquaculture in the Upper Basin. Under the procedures, the states will incorporate the procedures into their aquaculture permitting processes.

Several reservoirs and lakes are described as exempt from further review under the procedures because they have state management plans approved by the Service: Rio Blanco, Hollenbeck, Mark Mesa, Chipeta, Crawford, Namaquinnep, Puett, Summit and Totten reservoirs (Colorado). Changes to these plans, such as additional stocking, would trigger review.

Escapement from ponds and reservoirs into rivers is a central concern. Several routine stocking entries are conditioned on screening, separation by waters containing established reproducing predator or competitor populations, or isolation from surface connection with rivers.

## Key Findings

- **Routine stocking (Table 1):** Stocking is acceptable only in specified waters, such as upper-basin ponds and reservoirs outside the 40-year floodplain, lakes and ponds above [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], and [[Lake Powell]]. Several species are restricted to standing waters separated from endangered-fish habitat by waters containing established reproducing populations of predators or competitors. Utah chub may continue only in the Utah sub-basins where they currently exist.
- **Case-by-case species (Part II.B):** Walleye, yellow perch, tiger muskie and wiper require individual evaluation.
- **Public comment:** Comments came from about 20 parties. About 10 supported the procedures, and several opposed them. Opponents argued that the procedures do not provide for resolution of conflicts, that stocking would still harm endangered fish, and that the states had not adequately acknowledged the impacts of stocking. Commenters included the National Parks Service, the United Sportsmen Council, the Colorado Wildlife Commission and several conservation groups (NWF, EDF, CWF, HWF, Audubon).
- **Review process:** (1) The proponent submits a formal stocking proposal. (2) A 60-day public and agency review period follows. (3) Informal ESA consultation is initiated to determine whether listed species may be affected. (4A) For proposals not subject to Section 7, the states and Service review comments and attempt to resolve objections within 30 days. Unresolved disputes go to the state wildlife commission, which must give at least 30 days' notice before deciding. (4B) For proposals subject to Section 7, formal consultation with the lead federal agency is required before stocking proceeds. The Service has 90 days to complete consultation and 45 days to issue a biological opinion. Reasonable and prudent alternatives are identified where a jeopardy or adverse modification finding is made.
- **Scheduled milestones:** January 5, 1994 meeting with interest groups; January 18, 1994 Recovery Program Management Committee review; January 27, 1994 Implementation Committee review; March 10, 1994 possible state commission endorsement; May 1, 1994 formal acceptance by the states and Service through a Memorandum of Understanding.
- **Pending deliverable:** Appendix A, which provides the scientific basis for the routine-stocking conclusions, was not included and was to be circulated after the first of the year.
