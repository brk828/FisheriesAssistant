# Gape limited piscivores, planktivore size refuges and the trophic cascade hypothesis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This paper tests the idea that the effectiveness of a [[Trophic Cascade Hypothesis]] depends on how strongly piscivores can reduce planktivorous fish. The authors argue that not all planktivore populations are equally vulnerable to [[Gape-Limited Piscivory]]. Large, deep-bodied planktivores can escape predation in a [[Planktivore Size Refuge]], which limits the extent to which piscivore manipulations cascade down to [[Zooplankton]] and [[Phytoplankton]]. The work combines a simple morphological model, an illustrative comparison of four planktivore and two piscivore species, and a census of fish from [[Patten Lake]], Texas. The authors conclude that the cascade should be most pronounced in lakes dominated by small, shallow-bodied planktivores.

## Background & Description

The study was published in the journal of the Section of Ecology and Systematics, Cornell University, and the authors are affiliated with Cornell, Texas Christian University and Blue Water Science. The submission date was 6 December 1990 and acceptance was 23 February 1991.

The trophic cascade hypothesis predicts that increasing piscivorous fish biomass will reduce planktivorous fish, increase herbivorous zooplankton and reduce phytoplankton. The authors note that this framework assumes all planktivore populations are equally susceptible to reduction, which they argue is incorrect.

The model rests on two morphological variables:

- **[[Piscivore Mouth Width]]** (w): the constraint on ingestible prey size. Mouth width is used as the operational measure of the gape.
- **[[Planktivore Body Depth]]** (d): the dorsoventral dimension of prey. The authors argue this is a more appropriate measure than total body length, because prey are usually swallowed head or tail first.

The relative vulnerability of a prey body depth is:

V_d = 1 − Σ W_w (for w ≤ d, where W is the frequency of mouth widths in the piscivore population)

The probability of ingestion is:

P(I)_d = V_d^r × D

where D is the frequency of body depth in the planktivore population and r is a proportionality constant set to 0.80, following [[Peters 1983]] and the scaling of consumption with piscivore body mass. The model is an inverse of the filter-feeding copepod model of Boyd (1976), with prey sizes of interest being those small enough to pass through the "mesh" of the piscivore's mouth.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Model predictions**

- Prey with body depths at or below the smallest piscivore mouth width are vulnerable to all piscivores (V = 1).
- Prey deeper than the largest mouth width are not vulnerable to any piscivore (V = 0).
- Intermediate prey are vulnerable to a fraction of the population (0 < V < 1).
- In a hypothetical population, roughly one third of planktivores have body depths larger than the largest piscivore mouth width and are in an absolute size refuge.
- The mode of ingested prey is shifted toward smaller sizes than the mode of available prey, because piscivore mouth widths are smaller than prey body depths.

**Species example**

The authors modelled four planktivores ([[Fathead Minnow]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Bluegill]], [[Gizzard Shad]]) and two piscivores ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Northern Pike]]). Size-frequency data were pooled from Carlander (1969, 1977) and other sources. Mouth widths and body depths were derived from regressions in Lawrence (1957) and Gilliland et al. (1981). The model is explicitly simplified: it does not include encounter behaviour, prey choice, or temporal and spatial changes in populations. The authors state that these factors would not remove the existence of a size refuge.

**Patten Lake field census**

[[Patten Lake]] is a 7.7 ha impoundment 10 km west of West, Texas, USA. It was built in the 1920s, drained and restocked in 1940, and used by a fishing club until 1978. In spring 1979, the lake was drained through a trench into a catch basin fitted with 1.3 cm mesh screen. Of 11,458 individuals of the species counted, 8% were subsampled for length and weight. Small species such as [[Golden Shiner]], [[Mosquitofish]] and [[Red Shiner]] largely escaped the mesh, and shoreline seining had found few of them before draining.

Standing biomass highlights from the census table:

| Species | Biomass (kg/ha) |
|---|---|
| Gizzard shad | 128.7 |
| Bluegill | 70.4 |
| Channel catfish | 39.1 |
| White crappie | 45.2 |
| Largemouth bass | 18.4 |
| Redear sunfish | 14.9 |

The full table lists 17 taxa, including black and yellow bullheads, longear sunfish, warmouth, green sunfish and carp.

The bass size structure was skewed toward smaller individuals. The authors attribute this partly to selective removal of large bass by fishing. The near absence of bass and planktivores under 10 cm likely reflects draining before spawning, when small fish were at their annual minimum.

**Biomanipulation context**

The authors link their model to [[Biomanipulation]] programmes, in which managers attempt to reduce phytoplankton by increasing piscivore pressure. They also address harvest management, including minimum length limits and [[Slot Limits]], which protect large piscivores and so may increase predation on large planktivores.

## Key Findings

1. **Planktivore vulnerability depends on morphology.** Small, shallow-bodied planktivores are highly vulnerable to gape-limited piscivores and are unlikely to maintain large populations in their presence. Large, deep-bodied planktivores have adults with very low vulnerability and can coexist with piscivores.

2. **Predicted outcomes by species.** For the modelled species, all sizes of [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Golden Shiner]] were predicted to be ingested by both piscivores, roughly in proportion to their population frequencies. About 5% of [[Bluegill]] and 30% of [[Gizzard Shad]] had relative vulnerabilities of 0. Ingestion of bluegills and shad was predicted to fall mainly on smaller, younger fish. Larger bluegills and shad should be ingested by [[Northern Pike]] because its mouth widths are larger than those of [[Largemouth Bass]].

3. **Patten Lake supports the model.** In Patten Lake, 85% of the dominant forage species had body depths greater than the mouth width of 75% of largemouth bass. Fewer than 5% of forage fish had relative vulnerabilities of 0.50 or greater.

4. **Experimental evidence is consistent with the model.**
   - Where planktivores were small, shallow-bodied minnows, piscivory decimated them and produced detectable effects on zooplankton and phytoplankton (Bennndorf et al. 1984; Spencer & King 1984; Carpenter et al. 1987).
   - In a pond experiment with [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]], bluegills remained abundant and there was little evidence of cascading effects (Hambright et al. 1986).

5. **Large planktivores constrain cascades in practice.** Large planktivores typically dominate fish biomass in many lakes (Cooper et al. 1971; Noble 1981). Their continued consumption of zooplankton and their production of young-of-year fish may limit the cascade.

6. **Restocking case studies show limited durability.**
   - In [[Round Lake]], Minnesota, the lake was rotenone-treated and restocked with one piscivore for every 2.2 bluegills. Phytoplankton abundance was reduced, but the success lasted only about two years before planktivores recovered.
   - In [[Bautzen Reservoir]], GDR, piscivore stocking with [[Walleye]] and pike, plus harvest limits, reduced age 0+ [[European Perch]]. The effects diminished over five years as perch grew from 8.0 to 80.0 g and the density of deep-bodied roach increased.

7. **Management implications.** Minimum length limits can reduce the average size of piscivores and may increase large planktivores. Protecting large piscivores with slot limits should increase predation on large planktivores, so these approaches are compatible with biomanipulation. The authors also warn that stocking exotic piscivores is risky, citing the impacts of [[Nile Perch]] in Lake Victoria and [[Peacock Bass]] in Gatun Lake.

8. **Scope and limitations.** The authors acknowledge that the model is simplified and that behaviour, temporal change and spatial factors are not included. Whether the mouth-width and body-depth constraints apply to other assemblages, such as salmonids, is uncertain. The authors call for more study of the piscivore–planktivore interaction in lake management.

9. **Disciplinary conclusion.** The authors argue that limnology and fisheries biology have been artificially separated and that this separation hinders understanding of ecosystem dynamics. They suggest that piscivores, as both game fish and top predators, could help bring the two fields together.
