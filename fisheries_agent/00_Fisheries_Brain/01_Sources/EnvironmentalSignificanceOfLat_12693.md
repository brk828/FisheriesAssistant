# Environmental Significance of Late Quaternary Nonmarine Mollusks from Former Lake Bretz, Lower Grand Coulee, Washington

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-mollusks

## Executive Summary

This 1973 Washington State University M.A. thesis in anthropology by James Jerry Landye uses a Late Quaternary nonmarine mollusk assemblage from the former [[Lake Bretz]] (Lower Grand Coulee, Washington) to reconstruct past aquatic environments and to assess the resources available to [[Early Man]] in eastern Washington. The fauna, from more than 85,000 individual shells, is dated to about 12,000 years before present by [[Glacier Peak Ash]] within the deposits. The author concludes that Lake Bretz was a cool, spring-fed freshwater lake that existed for a short geologic interval (at least 35 years, possibly a few hundred), and that its shoreline offered abundant food and material resources to people who may have camped there.

## Background & Description

The thesis addresses a gap: detailed analyses of Pleistocene nonmarine molluscan assemblages in the Pacific Northwest had not previously been conducted. Its stated goals were to (1) review published work on Pleistocene and Recent nonmarine mollusks in the Pacific Northwest, (2) build a reference molluscan collection for the [[Laboratory of Anthropology]] at Washington State University, (3) assess paleoenvironmental, hydrological and terrestrial resource implications, and (4) demonstrate both the value and the interpretive problems of molluscan assemblages from a well-dated, undisturbed deposit.

The thesis rests on the premise, drawn from [[Taylor]] (1965) and [[Scott]] (1963), that most Late Pleistocene species lived in environments similar to those they occupy today, so that aquatic mollusks can indicate water size, velocity, salinity, alkalinity, purity and temperature. The author notes cautions: differential preservation, death rates, redeposition and the [[Lyell Uniformitarian Principle]] all affect reconstructions. Historical debate between [[Frank C. Baker]] and [[Bohumil Shimek]] over variation versus extinction is also reviewed; the author found no evidence that any Lake Bretz species is extinct.

The review of early work covers collections from the 1835 [[Thomas Nuttall]] expedition, the [[Wilkes Expedition]], the British-American Boundary Commission, Junius Henderson's 1920s Soap Lake collections, Walter Eyerdam, and modern limnology by [[W. T. Edmondson]] at [[Soap Lake]] and [[Lenore Lake]]. The appendices contain a U.S. Geological Survey checklist of Quaternary localities (about 22 entries), an annotated checklist of localities sampled in this study (about 14 entries), and a systematic account of taxa.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and sampling.** The primary locality, L65-1, is a barrow-pit exposure about three miles north of Soap Lake at the base of the east coulee wall. A section of about 3 m was divided into nine stratigraphic units (A to I), each sampled at approximately 0.06 m³. Units A and B showed evidence of reworking and were processed by dry screening; units C to I were cemented with carbonate, soaked for 24 to 48 hours, and washed through nylon screen. Glacier Peak ash floats for 24 to 48 hours before waterlogging, a property the author exploited for separation.

**Lake characteristics.** Lake Bretz was about 20 miles long and 1 mile wide, with a highest stable level of 1,159 ± 1 ft (about 353.3 m) and a shoreline of about 72 km. It was fed by springs and aquifers from the Upper Grand Coulee and the Columbia River. Its environments included perennial slackwater littoral zones, open water with some current near Park Lake, and cool perennial water at the outflow south of Soap Lake.

**Modern context.** The remnant lakes (Park, Blue, Alkali, Lenore, Soap) form a salinity gradient from north to south, with sodium bicarbonate dominating in the north and sodium sulfate in Soap Lake. Soap and Lenore lakes contained no living mollusks in 1966 to 1968 sampling. Only a relict population of *Vorticifex effusus* survived at Delany Spring.

**Climate and geology.** Regional climate was inferred to be cooler than present. Lake Bretz formed when the glacially dammed Columbia River was diverted through the coulee, with the last catastrophic flood estimated at 18,000 to 20,000 years ago. Lake levels fell as the Columbia resumed its course and aquifers were cut off, making the basin internally drained and concentrating salts.

**Early Man context.** Sites cited include [[Lind Coulee]] (45GR97, about 8,700 years old in the original report, possibly older), [[Marmes Rockshelter]] (45FR50, early components about 10,000 years B.P.), [[Windust Caves]] (45FR46), [[Granite Point]] (45WT41) and sites in adjacent regions. Early Man evidence from the Lower Grand Coulee itself was not found.

## Key Findings

**Fauna (L65-1 assemblage)**
- At least 19 species representing 12 genera were recorded from the 3 m section. Two pelecypod genera (*Anodonta*, *Pisidium*) and seven aquatic gastropod genera (*Valvata*, *Lymnaea*, *Gyraulus*, *Armiger*, *Vorticifex*, *Planorbella*, *Physa*) were present. *Musculium lacustre* was added from two other Lake Bretz samples.
- Over 85,000 shells were recovered. *Pisidium*, *Valvata* and *Gyraulus* together made up over 90% of each unit. *Pisidium* exceeded 62,000 valves and *Valvata* exceeded 38,000 individuals.
- Lowest unit I contained 3,486 mollusks. Abundance increased upward through the sequence to unit D, then decreased in unit C.
- Three terrestrial snail species (*Discus cronkhitei*, *Zonitoides arboreus*, cf. *Succinea*) totaled only 11 individuals, probably washed in from adjacent land, suggesting sedges and perhaps a few trees near the margin.

**Environmental inferences**
- *Pisidium idahoense*, a cool-water large-lake indicator, and *Valvata humeralis* point to cool, clear, perennial water.
- *Anodonta californiensis*, *Lymnaea* cf. *L. binneyi* and *V. effusus* were larger, thicker-shelled and more abundant at L68-8 and L68-9 near Park Lake, implying more current and oxygenation than at L65-1.
- Fossil ostracods identified by [[Denis Delorme]] included *Candona acuminata* and *Cypria ophthalmica* (cold-water littoral) and *Ilyocypris bradyi* and *I. gibba* (fluvial), suggesting greater current early in the lake's history.
- Fossil fish bones were assigned to Cottidae and Cyprinidae, and a duck bone and anuran bone were also found. Fish identification was incomplete.
- Water chemistry was unlike the modern Soap and Lenore lakes; the inferred waters were low in sodium or calcium bicarbonate, and temperatures were a few degrees cooler than today.

**Age and duration**
- Glacier Peak ash places deposits at about 12,000 years old.
- Individual *Anodonta californiensis* were 3 to 5 years old in units C through I. With seven units, the minimum lake age was estimated at 35 years; extrapolating about 3 m of lower sediment gave about 100 years. Overall the author concluded the lake lasted at least 35 years and possibly a few hundred years.

**Cultural implications**
- Lake Bretz offered sedges for mats and baskets, trees for resources, freshwater clams (*A. californiensis*, a possible fish-host dependency on *Gila* spp.) and waterfowl, and fish. The author notes that no identifiable *Gila* remains were found.
- Camps would be above the highest stable level; the author recommends concentrating searches along and above the 72 km shoreline, though many shoreline segments lie on abrupt coulee walls.

**Methodological points**
- The author argues stratigraphic position, representative sample size, and bulk washing through fine screen are essential; hand-picking biases results.
- Shell radiocarbon dating can be affected by old carbon from terrestrial sources.

**Conclusions (summary of chapter 6)**
1. Lake Bretz existed about 12,000 years ago at a shoreline elevation of about 353.3 m.
2. It supported abundant mollusks and fish, with aquatic vegetation, birds and anurans.
3. The fauna likely derived from the Columbia River drainage during late glacial time and was introduced when the Columbia was diverted.
4. Early Man, if present, could have used lake-related resources; search should target rockshelters and open sites along and above the shoreline.
5. Declining aquifer supply and rising salinity extirpated most of the lake macrofauna, producing the modern relict lake chain.
6. Lake Bretz was a short-lived phenomenon in the geologic history of the Columbia Basin.
