# Ecology of the Mississippi Silverside (Menidia audens) in Lake Texoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #atherinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 Master of Science thesis, published as Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory Bulletin No. 6, examines the life history, distribution, and forage value of the [[Mississippi Silverside]] (*[[Menidia Audens]]*) in [[Lake Texoma]]. Fieldwork ran from June 1965 through August 1966, centered on the [[Buncombe Creek Arm]]. The study found that the species lives mostly in the [[Littoral Zone]] within the upper five feet of water, shows no detectable day-night migration, lives about 16 months, and spawns over an extended period from late March to mid-July. Its main ecological importance is as prey: it made up 78% of identifiable fish in the stomachs of young [[Largemouth Bass]].

## Background & Description

The Mississippi silverside is a freshwater atherinid closely resembling the [[Brook Silverside]] (*[[Labidesthes Sicculus]]*). Adults of the two species can be told apart by the brook silverside's smaller scales and more pronounced beak.

- **Oklahoma records:** First collected in Oklahoma in 1949 from an overflow area of the Red River in Bryan County. The first Lake Texoma specimen was taken in 1953. By 1959 it was among the most abundant species in the lake and had apparently replaced the brook silverside.
- **Residual brook silversides:** Two adult brook silversides were still collected in Buncombe Creek in October 1965, so a few persist.
- **Range extensions:** The species was introduced to [[Boomer Lake]] near Stillwater, where several hundred specimens were collected in February 1966. An introduction to Lake Lawtonka was reported, but its status was undetermined.
- **Unresolved question:** The biological basis for the near-replacement of the brook silverside is unknown. Earlier work by Saunders (1959) found little difference in food habits between the two species.

The study had two aims: to learn as much as possible about the life history of the Mississippi silverside, and to assess its contribution as forage for piscivorous fishes in Lake Texoma.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and sampling.** Collections were made monthly in the Buncombe Creek Arm using three gears:

- **Seine:** A 20-ft, 1/8-inch mesh straight seine, with one night and one day haul at each of six stations sampled at least monthly. Stations differed in bottom type, vegetation, and wind exposure. Some stations had hard clay, others sandy-silty bottoms with [[Chara]] growth, and Station 5 had no vegetation.
- **Electric shocker:** A 220 V boom-type [[Electric Shocker]], used January through May 1966 for 30-minute open-water and shoreline samples.
- **Vertical gill net:** A small-mesh net, 10 ft long and 20 ft deep, used August, September, and November 1965. Depth markers at 4-ft intervals recorded depth of capture.

Fish were preserved in 10% formalin. Turbidity, temperature, and wind were recorded at each station.

**Vertical distribution.** Of 39 Mississippi silversides caught by vertical gill net, 38 came from the upper four feet, and the one exception was about six inches below the four-foot marker. Shad (*[[Dorosoma Spp]]*) were distributed fairly uniformly to 16 ft. Catches were contagious, indicating schooling or aggregation. The species was regularly seen at the surface in open water.

**Lateral distribution.** Shoreline catches were markedly higher than open-water catches in both day and night samples. The species was concentrated in the littoral zone for most of the year, although some individuals were collected offshore in every month sampled.

**Habitat within the littoral zone.** Analysis of variance showed significant station differences. Fish were most abundant at stations sheltered from the prevailing southerly winds and with relatively sandy bottoms and submergent vegetation such as Chara.

**Diel behavior.** No significant differences were detected between night and day catches (F = 3.71, not significant). Saunders (1959) had proposed a nocturnal shoreward migration, but this study found no evidence for it.

## Key Findings

**Life history**

- **Longevity:** Adults were never taken after July 31, suggesting a maximum lifespan of about 16 months.
- **Size:** The largest Texoma specimen was 119 mm total length. A 130 mm specimen weighing 9.65 g came from Boomer Lake in February 1966.
- **Growth:** Growth stopped in October and resumed in March. The 1964 and 1965 year-classes disappeared in late July of the year following hatching.
- **Aging:** Scale reading was unsatisfactory. No definite annuli were detected in scales from fish collected in every month.
- **Sex differences in growth:** Females grew faster than males. Differences were very highly significant for the 1964 year-class in June 1965 and became significant for the 1965 year-class from October onward.

**Sex ratios**

- Females predominated in August, September, and October samples.
- Males predominated in April and May. This is attributed to unisexual male aggregations in the littoral zone.
- A female predominance returned in June, possibly from dispersal of male aggregations.
- Sex ratios did not differ significantly from 1:1 in winter and early spring.

**Reproduction and fecundity**

- Spawning was never observed. Indirect evidence indicates spawning began in late March or early April and extended through mid-July, with the population spawning more smoothly than Saunders suspected.
- Gonads developed in early spring and regressed in fall. Ripe females always contained immature eggs, and no completely spent female was found.
- Mean fecundity was **984 mature eggs per female** (SD 358.6, range 384-1699), based on 31 females from the summers of 1965 and 1966.
- Total weight and body weight correlated most strongly with egg number (r = 0.913 and 0.912). Total length correlated at r = 0.872, and ovary weight at r = 0.775.

**Length-weight relationships**

- Slopes did not differ significantly between sexes in any season.
- Elevations differed significantly for young-of-year fish in summer (males heavier at a given length) and for adults in spring (females heavier, likely from egg development).

**Forage value**

- **Largemouth bass:** Of 290 identifiable fish from the stomachs of 536 young largemouth bass, 78% were Mississippi silversides. They occurred in 72% of stomachs containing identifiable fish. Shad were next, at 15% of identifiable fish and 20% of stomachs.
- **Smallest prey:** Silversides were eaten by bass as small as 24 mm total length.
- **Feeding pattern:** Multiple silversides were often found in one stomach, with one 30 mm bass containing 49 fry. This may reflect schooling behavior.
- **White bass:** Mississippi silversides were the predominant food of white bass 38-148 mm total length and an important food for all sizes.
- **White crappie:** Only occasionally eaten. Crappie occur mainly deeper than four feet, while silversides rarely exceed five feet.
- **Gar:** Silversides were the predominant fish in 386 young gars, and 54% of fish in adult [[Longnose Gar]] stomachs.
- **Conclusion:** The value of the species as forage lies in its abundance in the littoral zone rather than in any selective preference by predators.

**Overall conclusion:** The author concludes that the Mississippi silverside is an important forage species for piscivorous fishes feeding at the surface or in littoral areas of Lake Texoma. Its small adult size, prolonged spawning, and relatively high fecundity make large numbers available to both young-of-year and adult predators throughout the year.
