# Ware River Intensive Watershed Study

#marine-biology #estuarine-ecology #water-quality

## Executive Summary

The [[Ware River Intensive Watershed Study]] was an [[Environmental Protection Agency]] (EPA) project, conducted through the [[Chesapeake Bay Program]] and carried out by researchers at the [[Virginia Institute of Marine Science]]. It measured how runoff from four small catchments (forested, residential, and two row-crop agricultural sites) contributes [[Nonpoint Source Pollution]] to the [[Ware River]] estuary and [[Chesapeake Bay]]. Runoff quantity and quality were monitored from October 1979 to July 1981. The study found that stormflow carried most of the phosphorus, nitrogen, BOD5, and suspended solids loads, while the estuary showed only slight, short-lived impacts from nonpoint pollution.

This document is an EPA Project Summary. It does not address fish or other aquatic fauna. The subject is water chemistry, nutrient loading, and estuarine water quality.

## Background & Description

**Objective:** Characterize the contribution of major land uses to nonpoint source loadings into the Ware River and Chesapeake Bay, and measure how storm water affects estuarine water quality.

**Study sites:** The four catchments represent land uses that cover about 87 percent of the Ware basin:
- **[[Forested Catchment]]:** An undisturbed mixed forest with moderate slopes and poorly drained soils. It has continuous baseflow in winter.
- **[[Residential Catchment]]:** A low-density subdivision on the estuary shoreline. Homes use [[Septic Tanks]], and stormwater is controlled by roadside ditches. It has continuous baseflow in winter.
- **[[Row-Crop Agriculture]] (lowland):** A corn and soybean rotation with poorly drained soils. It has continuous baseflow in winter.
- **[[Row-Crop Agriculture]] (upland):** A corn and soybean rotation on light, erosive, well-drained soils. It showed no baseflow during the study.

**Methods:**
- Flow was measured with H-flumes and flowmeters.
- Automatic composite samplers were paced by flow.
- Recording rain gauges (sensitive to 0.01 inch) were installed at each site.
- Runoff and baseflow samples were analyzed for total and dissolved phosphorus, total and dissolved Kjeldahl nitrogen, BOD5, suspended solids, ammonia, nitrate-nitrite nitrogen, and dissolved silica.
- Estuarine stations were sampled semi-monthly by runabout, with submersible pumps, and several intensive surveys tracked diel and tidal responses.

**Study duration:** The document gives the monitoring period as October 1979 to July 1981 ("two-year period"). It also states both "27-month study" (Methods) and "22-month period" (Results). These figures conflict in the source text.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Estuarine setting:** The Ware River has moderate nutrient levels. In summer, some nutrients, particularly inorganic phosphorus and organic nitrogen, reach levels associated with moderate enrichment. The river is typical of small Chesapeake Bay tributaries. Nutrient levels are higher at low tide, the estuary is more laterally homogeneous than longitudinally, and vertical gradients exist for dissolved oxygen, total phosphorus, and suspended solids.
- **Nutrient limitation:** [[Phytoplankton]] are generally [[Phosphorus Limitation|phosphorus limited]]. During the annual spring blooms (April 1979 and March 1980), plankton uptake of inorganic nitrogen makes the system [[Nitrogen Limitation|nitrogen limited]].
- **Dilution and sedimentation:** Impacts of nonpoint pollution are slight and short-lived. Dilution by Bay waters and sedimentation in upstream marshes limit them, so impacts are mainly seen in the shallow upstream estuary.
- **Hydrology:** Baseflow was 35 to 60 percent of total flow at the forested and residential sites. Nearly half of total flow came from groundwater over the study period. Water table conditions drove high winter baseflow.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** No anoxic conditions were found at any season or station. Dissolved oxygen saturation was significantly higher at the mouth. The study reports 90 percent saturation at the mouth, and lower values upstream.

## Key Findings

### Runoff Concentrations and Loads
- Concentrations increased during stormflow for all constituents except dissolved silica. The average increase was an order of magnitude for particulate materials and a factor of two for dissolved constituents.
- Total phosphorus, total nitrogen, and dissolved ammonia were substantially higher in runoff from the two agricultural sites than from the residential and forested catchments.
- The residential catchment had high dissolved nutrient and BOD5 concentrations in both baseflow and storm runoff.
- The upland agricultural site had the lowest runoff volume. Its nutrient concentrations were high, but its total loads were lower than the forest or residential catchments. Much of its rainfall percolated into rapidly permeable soils.
- Storm runoff produced **83%** of total phosphorus, **70%** of total nitrogen, **62%** of BOD5, and **91%** of suspended solids loads.
- Only **13 of 114** site-events had rainfall greater than 5 cm, yet these events accounted for more than 50 percent of measured storm runoff.
- Analysis of individual storms showed no clear relationship between rainfall amount and runoff or loading.
- The residential site produced the most storm runoff and the highest loading rates for all constituents except phosphorus and suspended solids. Its mean and median loading rates were the highest, while the agricultural sites produced occasional very high individual rates.
- The authors attribute the residential site's high dissolved nutrient baseflow, particularly orthophosphorus, to possible leaching from nearby septic tank drainfields.

### Estuarine Water Quality
- The Ware River contains a moderate amount of nutrients. Concentrations were generally low near the mouth and higher upstream, where low TN:TP ratios were attributed to point source discharge.
- Nutrient concentrations fluctuated with the tides. Maximum ammonia, total phosphorus, and total Kjeldahl nitrogen values occurred at low water slack, while the highest nitrate-nitrite values were present at high water slack.
- The estuary is essentially tidally dominated, particularly in the upper reaches. Freshwater flow may create a two-layer circulation system, with significant responses to salinity gradients.
- Impacts from nonpoint source runoff on the estuary were found to be slight to occasionally moderate, and relatively short-lived.

### Recommendations and Implications
- Nutrient loading rates vary by land use and fluctuate seasonally. The rates are a function of runoff quantity rather than concentration.
- Loading rates per unit rainfall can be used to extrapolate to other years and rainfall records, and to compare with other watersheds.
- The authors recommend that the loading data be used to calibrate mathematical models of watershed runoff for the Bay. They also suggest that future watershed studies monitor flow to adequately characterize coastal watersheds.
