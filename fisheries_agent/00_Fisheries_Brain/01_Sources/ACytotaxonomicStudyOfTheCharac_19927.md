# NSF Research Grant G-19449: A Cytotaxonomic Study of the Characeae - Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #characeae #freshwater-algae #cytotaxonomy

## Executive Summary

This progress report covers research conducted under NSF Research Grant G-19449 (continuation of NSF-G13261) from January 1, 1962, documenting cytotaxonomic investigations of the [[Characeae]] (stoneworts) at the [[University of Louisville]]. Principal Investigator [[Arland Hotchkiss]] and team conducted extensive field collections across the United States and Mexico, performed chromosome counts on numerous species, maintained living cultures, and investigated ecological relationships. The project expanded to include two graduate students ([[Donald Tindall]] and [[Takashi Sawa]]) and established permanent herbarium collections with exsiccatae distribution. Major findings include documentation of chromosome number patterns correlating with tribal divisions (multiples of 3 in [[Nitelleae]], multiples of 7 in [[Chareae]]), extensive ploidy variation in the [[Chara zeylanica]] complex (14-70 chromosomes), and completion of a detailed life history study of [[Nitella flexilis]] in [[Doe Run]], Kentucky.

## Background & Description

The [[Characeae]] (stoneworts or charophytes) represent an ancient group of freshwater macroalgae divided into two tribes: [[Nitelleae]] and [[Chareae]]. This research program employed cytological methods, particularly chromosome counting from antheridial filaments, combined with traditional morphological taxonomy to resolve systematic relationships within the family. The classical classification system, largely shaped by A. Braun in the 19th century, divides the genus [[Chara]] into sections based on stipulode arrangement (single vs. double whorls) and cortication patterns. However, this system has faced challenges in accommodating morphological variation and determining natural relationships.

The project assembled extensive collections from field expeditions spanning multiple states and Mexican provinces. Specimens were preserved in both dried/mounted form and fluid preservation, with cytological preparations mounted on permanent slides accompanied by voucher specimens. Living cultures were maintained to provide material for chromosome analysis, experimental work, and morphological comparisons under standardized conditions. The culture collection proved invaluable for bringing immature field specimens to reproductive maturity suitable for cytological examination.

Collaborations with numerous botanists provided materials from [[Australasia]] (Dr. [[Richard Wood]]), [[Indiana]] (Mrs. [[F. K. Daily]]), and various other locations. International exchange of cytological information occurred with researchers in Belgium, Japan, and across North America.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Characeae]] occupy diverse freshwater habitats including permanent lakes, temporary ponds, spring streams, ditches, and bogs across a wide geographic range. Environmental data collected by [[D. R. Tindall]] documented habitat conditions including substrate type, water temperature (12.5-35.6°C), total alkalinity (22-310 mg CaCO3/l), chloride content, and pH (7.0-9.8). Species showed varying degrees of habitat specificity:

[[Chara brittonii]] demonstrated extreme habitat restriction, occurring only in raised bogs with marl substrates, high alkalinity (290 mg/l), cool temperatures (12.5°C), and pH 7.42. In contrast, [[Chara contraria]] exhibited wide ecological tolerance, occurring across temperature ranges of 16.7-30.5°C and diverse pH conditions, adapting morphologically to flowing versus still water habitats.

[[Nitella flexilis]] in [[Doe Run]], Kentucky, occupied a perennial position in a limestone spring stream community on fine sand and silt substrates. The species demonstrated adaptation to unstable silty bottoms through vegetative reproduction from entrenched plant parts, withstanding increased flow velocities. Water conditions remained relatively constant: temperature ca. 14°C, pH 7.0-7.2, alkalinity 200-250 mg/l, dissolved oxygen 7.0-8.5 mg/l.

Community structure along stream longitudinal profiles reflected velocity-substrate relationships, with distinct populations forming superficially monospecific stands. Associated epiphytic diatom flora included [[Cocconeis placentula]], [[Meridion circulare]], and [[Gomphonema acuminatum]], showing seasonal and positional variation within [[Nitella]] beds.

## Key Findings

**Chromosome Number Patterns and Tribal Divisions:**

The research established fundamental cytological distinctions between the two tribes of [[Characeae]]. In Tribe [[Nitelleae]], chromosome numbers occur in multiples of 3, with no living species having fewer than 6 chromosomes. At least two distinct series exist: a 6, 12, 18, 36 series (illustrated by [[Nitella opaca]] n=6, [[Nitella flexilis]] n=12, [[Nitella acuminata]] n=18, [[Nitella megacarpa]] n=36) and a 9, 18 series (illustrated by [[Nitella asagrayana]] n=9, [[Nitella tenuissima]] n=9). All heterothalamous species examined showed n=18 chromosomes.

In Tribe [[Chareae]], chromosomes occur in multiples of 7, with no living species having fewer than 14 chromosomes. This pattern held consistently across diverse morphological groups within the tribe.

**Extensive Field Collections (1960-1961):**

Three major expeditions documented in detail:
- Kansas to Mexico (August 1960, March-April 1961): 64 collections with chromosome counts ranging from n=9 to n=56
- Texas to Mexico (September 1961): 40 collections including [[Chara hydropitys]] n=14, [[Chara hornemanii]] n=28, multiple [[Chara zeylanica]] variants
- Regional collections from Kentucky, Indiana, Tennessee, North Carolina, New York: 29 collections documenting local species diversity

Total of 97 numbered culture lines established, maintaining living material for ongoing cytological and experimental work.

**Systematic Implications for Ecorticate Species:**

All ecorticate [[Chareae]] examined (including [[Chara braunii]], [[Chara brittonii]], [[Nitellopsis]], [[Protochara]] species) showed complete absence of ploidy beyond the n=14 chromosome level in both monoecious and dioecious forms. This cytological uniformity suggests ecorticate species form a natural group more closely related to each other than to corticate [[Chara]] species. The findings support removing ecorticate species from genus [[Chara]] to separate genera, potentially creating a restricted family or subfamily of Chara-like genera distinct from corticate forms.

**Ploidy Variation in the Chara zeylanica Complex:**

The [[Chara zeylanica]] complex (Series [[Gymnobasalia]]) demonstrated unprecedented ploidy variation:
- n=14: Dioecious and disjunct forms (e.g., [[Chara sejuncta]])
- n=28: Majority of collections, representing normal diploid level
- n=42: Approximately 12 counts from various forms
- n=56: Ten counts, including [[Chara haitensis]]
- n=70: Single collection of [[Chara foliolosa]] from Florida, representing the highest chromosome number recorded for any charophyte (10x base of 7)

This extreme ploidy range (2x to 10x) within a morphologically similar complex suggests the "[[Chara zeylanica]] complex" encompasses multiple cryptic species distinguishable primarily by chromosome number. The cytological evidence supports earlier taxonomic treatments recognizing multiple species (Robinson 1906) rather than lumping all forms under a single binomial.

**Correlation of Chromosome Number with Sexual Condition:**

A consistent pattern emerged linking ploidy level to sex organ distribution. Low chromosome numbers (n=6 in [[Nitella opaca]], n=14 in [[Chara aspera]], [[Chara braunii]], [[Chara sejuncta]]) correlated with dioecious or disjunct (sex organs at different nodes) conditions. Monoecious species consistently showed higher chromosome numbers, suggesting polyploidy may facilitate the evolution of monoecy in charophytes.

**Life History of Nitella flexilis in Doe Run:**

[[Donald Tindall]]'s thesis research documented a perennial population showing unlimited growth and greatly exaggerated secondary branching, unusual for the species. Chromosome counts confirmed n=12 in both vegetative tissues and pre-gametic antheridial filament nuclei, establishing the gametophytic nature of the plant body. Absence of meiosis before gametogenesis provided indirect evidence that reduction division occurs at oospore germination.

Gametangial production occurred at relatively consistent rates during winter and spring under constant spring water conditions (14°C, pH 7.0-7.2). Increased light in March-April showed no particular effect on sex organ production, though turbidity from high discharge may have reduced light significance. In October, production decreased but maturation was enhanced, suggesting relatively low light and temperature favor production while increased light reduces production but enhances maturation. No sexual reproduction was observed; population maintenance occurred entirely through vegetative propagation from rhizoidal production at branchlet nodes.

**Taxonomic Recommendations:**

The research questioned the classical division of genus [[Chara]] into two primary sections ([[Haplostephanae]] vs. [[Diplostephanae]]) based on stipulode arrangement. The existence of parallel morphological groups in both sections, combined with cytological evidence, suggests this dichotomy may be artificial. The findings support recognizing a larger number of variables reflecting greater complexity of interrelationships, viewing the genus as representing branching end-points of several distinct evolutionary lines rather than a few closely connected linear sequences.

The proposal to elevate tribal status of [[Chareae]] and [[Nitelleae]] to ordinal or family level would provide taxonomic flexibility for accommodating variation within each group and properly recognizing the fundamental cytological distinction (base numbers 7 vs. 3) between them.

**Ecological Patterns:**

Species showed varying degrees of ecological specialization. [[Chara brittonii]] demonstrated extreme habitat restriction (raised bogs, marl substrates, specific temperature/pH conditions), while [[Chara contraria]] showed wide tolerance across temperature (16.7-30.5°C), pH, and alkalinity ranges with corresponding morphological plasticity. [[Chara vulgaris]] preferred constant low temperatures, high alkalinity, minimum chlorides, and buffered pH around 8.0, making it particularly suited to limestone spring streams.

The [[Chara zeylanica]] complex showed "extremely diverse nature with respect to morphology, cytology, and ecological relationships," further supporting the interpretation of this complex as comprising multiple cryptic species rather than a single variable taxon.
