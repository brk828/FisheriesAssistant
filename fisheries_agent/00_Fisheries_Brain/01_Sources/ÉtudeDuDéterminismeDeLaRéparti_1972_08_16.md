## Executive Summary
This study applies **factorial correspondence analysis (FCA)** — a multivariate statistical technique for simplifying large contingency tables — to investigate the environmental determinants of habitat selection in four sympatric leaf-warbler species of the genus [[Phylloscopus]]: *P. trochilus*, *P. collybita*, *P. sibilatrix*, and *P. bonelli*. Using 111 point-count samples (IPA method) from the [[Forêt de Fontainebleau]], each characterized by 68 habitat descriptor variables and 12 bird-frequency variables, the analysis (run on an IBM 360/50 computer, program P0525) extracted three principal factors accounting for 10%, 8%, and 7% of total variance, respectively. Each factor was interpreted ecologically: **Factor 1** as an index of overall woody-plant stand density, **Factor 2** as a deciduous–conifer gradient (analogous to elevational gradients in mountain forests), and **Factor 3** as density of medium-sized trees ("perchis," or pole-stage stands). All four species showed distinct but overlapping associations with understory structure and ground substrate, while responses to canopy and mid-story tree density were more species-specific.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
Each species exhibited a distinguishable **"synthetic biotope"** based on associations with ground cover, shrub density, and canopy/sub-canopy structure:

- **[[Phylloscopus trochilus]] (Willow Warbler):** Associated with dense shrub stratum, grassy ground cover interspersed with small shrubs and patches of bare litter, and open/sparse pole-stage stands (perchis clair). Frequency increases as canopy ("futaie") density approaches zero — favors open, low-canopy habitats.

- **[[Phylloscopus collybita]] (Common Chiffchaff):** Associated with dense shrub layer and bramble-dominated grassy ground cover lacking visible leaf litter; largely indifferent to pole-stage and canopy tree density. Frequency level "2" could not be statistically assessed due to insufficient data.

- **[[Phylloscopus sibilatrix]] (Wood Warbler):** Associated with a substrate of dead leaves or needles covering the ground uniformly, and scattered/sparse shrubs; indifferent to canopy density. Frequency increases with denser pole-stage stands and greater bracken fern (*fougère aigle*) cover.

- **[[Phylloscopus bonelli]] (Western Bonelli's Warbler):** Associated with patchy fern or shrub cover (notably absent at the lowest frequency level), relatively indifferent to grassy ground cover and canopy density. Frequency increases with more scattered (open) shrub and pole-stage strata, and with increasing presence of conifers in both strata. Notably, this is the only species reaching peak frequency under strong positive Factor 2 values (deciduous→conifer gradient), consistent with it being the only one of the four species remaining common in subalpine forests of the Alps — linking the deciduous-conifer gradient to elevational/altitudinal gradients.

## Key Findings
- **Factor 1** (10% of variance): Represents overall density of the woody plant community (tree/shrub stand density); strongly structures species distributions.
- **Factor 2** (8% of variance): Represents a deciduous-to-conifer vegetation gradient, ecologically analogous to montane elevational gradients; only *P. bonelli* shows strong positive association, mirroring its persistence in subalpine Alpine forest.
- **Factor 3** (7% of variance): Represents density of medium-height trees (pole-stage/"perchis"); influences species distributions more selectively (e.g., *P. sibilatrix* and *P. bonelli*) than globally across all four species.
- All four Phylloscopus species show **precise associations with low vegetation strata and ground substrate type** (litter, fern, bramble, grass), but differ in their sensitivity to canopy ("futaie") vs. mid-story ("perchis") density — only one species (*P. bonelli*) responds strongly to large-tree density, and two species (*P. sibilatrix*, *P. bonelli*) respond to medium-tree density.
- The study demonstrates that **factorial correspondence analysis** is a powerful tool for ecological classification problems, enabling researchers to infer probable determinants of breeding population distribution from multivariate habitat survey data — a methodological approach with potential cross-disciplinary application (including aquatic/fish community-habitat studies, though none are addressed in this particular paper).
