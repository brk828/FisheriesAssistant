# Geohydrology and Ground-Water Quality of San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge, Arizona

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #poeciliopsis

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1989 [[U.S. Geological Survey]] (USGS) Water-Resources Division package, consisting of a cover letter, a manuscript routing sheet, a colleague review form, and a preliminary draft report by Steve A. Longsworth. The draft describes the geohydrology, ground-water occurrence and movement, and chemical quality of ground water in the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]]. The study was prepared in cooperation with the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS). The refuge was established in 1982 to protect wetland habitat and the endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]], a small freshwater fish. The report concludes that ground water provides adequate quantity and quality for wetland restoration under current conditions, but that its long-term response to pumping, particularly from Mexico, cannot yet be predicted.

## Background & Description

- **Cover letter and routing:** Dated August 17, 1989, from the USGS Tucson District Chief to the USFWS Regional Director, Region 2 (Albuquerque). Review comments were requested by September 21, 1989. The routing sheet classifies the document as an interpretive report and lists the report as a USGS Water-Resources Investigations Report (project AZ088). It has 52 pages, 7 illustrations, and 4 tables.
- **Review form:** A blank 16-item colleague review checklist covering organization, clarity, jargon, data pertinence, and illustrations. No completed reviews are included.
- **Study purpose:** Begun in 1984 in cooperation with USFWS, the study aimed to define the geohydrologic system "on the basis of limited data available for the valley."
- **Refuge:** About 2,300 acres in the southern part of [[San Bernardino Valley]], established in 1982 to protect a wetland ecosystem and the endangered Yaqui topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*). Marshes, ponds, springs, and flowing wells sustain the habitat.
- **Habitat history:** Land clearing and cattle grazing before USFWS acquisition caused a decline in native fish and wildlife. Previously drained wetlands have since been restored using water from flowing wells and springs. A refugium for the Yaqui topminnow has been established, and it requires a dependable long-term water supply.
- **Physiography:** The valley lies partly in the US and partly in Mexico. Altitudes range from about 8,000 ft in the [[Chiricahua Mountains]] to about 3,400 ft on the refuge valley floor, with an average north-to-south slope of about 40 ft/mi. Land use is primarily cattle grazing, with no extensive farming in 1989.
- **Climate:** Annual precipitation is about 13 in. on the valley floor near the refuge (4,000 ft), almost 18 in. at Rucker Canyon (5,340 ft), and more than 25 in. at about 7,000 ft in the Chiricahua Mountains.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geologic framework**

- The basin formed in middle to late Tertiary time through normal faulting during [[Basin and Range Province]] disturbance.
- Basin fill consists of Cenozoic alluvium interlayered with late Tertiary and Quaternary [[Basalt Flows]] and tuff. Basalt volcanism occurred roughly 3 to 0.27 million years ago, and driller's logs indicate at least eight basalt flows.
- Lower basin fill is finer grained and may be several hundred feet thick. Upper basin fill is coarser and interfingers with basalt.
- Stream alluvium is less than 100 ft thick, thickest along [[Black Draw]], the main surface-water drainage. The deepest part of the basin, in the northwest near the Chiricahua Mountains, exceeds 800 ft to bedrock.

**Aquifer system**

- Permeable basin-fill sediments and stream deposits are the primary water-bearing units. Basalt layers and fine sediments create confined or semiconfined conditions.
- Within the refuge, the lower basin fill is confined by low-permeability clays or dense basalt. Springs occur where basalt flows end and expose permeable upper basin-fill sediments.
- Ground water moves from mountain-front recharge areas toward the basin center and then south, approximately paralleling surface-water flow. Some water moves along shallow basalt layers and discharges through springs and seeps on the refuge's intermediate terrace.

**Water use and withdrawals**

- Data from 57 wells outside the refuge show 46 used for stock supply, 8 for domestic supply, and 3 unused. There are no irrigation withdrawals in the US part of the valley.
- Ground-water pumping for agriculture in Mexico is expected to increase. The effect on refuge wells is not known.

**Surface water and precipitation**

- Surface-water supplies are described as limited and unreliable, which is why ground water is used for restoration.
- Ephemeral streams drain the surrounding mountains into Black Draw, which is perennial only in a short reach inside the refuge near the international boundary.

**Springs and wells**

- The refuge contains 10 to 11 wells (the abstract and introduction give different counts) and at least three springs. Nine of the ten wells flow at land surface, and well 9 is at or near land surface.
- [[House Spring]] supplies the restored ranch house and feeds House Pond, North Pond, and Tule Pond. Astin Spring discharges into Black Draw north of the refuge.

## Key Findings

**Ground-water levels**

- Ground-water levels have remained stable since initial measurements in the mid-1950s. Between 1956 and 1989, 56 water-level measurements were made at 21 wells.
- Well (D-24-31)05acc (500 ft deep) was 96 ft below land surface in May 1974 and 93 ft in July 1985.
- Well (D-24-30)16ccc (100 ft deep) was 30 ft below land surface in both January 1977 and January 1989.
- Well (D-23-31)29dcal (300 ft deep) was 165 ft in December 1956 and 166 ft in March 1987.
- Deep wells can have higher hydraulic head than shallow wells. In June 1974, the 900 ft well north of the refuge had 24 ft to water, and the 130 ft well had 26 ft.

**Well discharge**

- Flow measurements began in December 1983 by USFWS staff. Average discharge ranged from 6.2 to 108 gal/min across refuge wells (1983–88).
- Total average daily flow available is 350,640 gallons. Total average annual flow is 127,983,600 gallons, or about 400 acre-ft. The report states this would cover the refuge with about 2 in. of water.
- Well 10 (583 ft deep) draws most of its flow from solution-enlarged zones. More than half of its flow occurs near 488 ft.
- Discharge from flowing wells has been essentially unchanged since monitoring began in 1983. Pumping from well 10 for Twin Pond between 1982 and 1987 may have affected other wells, but this effect was masked by seasonal variation.

**Chemical quality**

- Samples from 18 wells and 2 springs (1957–1987) were below Federal and State maximum contaminant levels for drinking water. Spring samples were below State limits for aquatic and wildlife protected use. The report notes that no State surface-water standards exist for the valley.
- Two water types were identified:
  - **Calcium-carbonate type** (four wells, including (D-24-31)03bab and (D-24-31)10ccc): calcium 50–150 mg/L, alkalinity 334–927 mg/L as CaCO₃, specific conductance 730–1,700 µS/cm, and dissolved solids 602–1,070 mg/L.
  - **Sodium-bicarbonate type** (14 other wells): calcium 5.4–98 mg/L, alkalinity 144–243 mg/L, specific conductance 304–702 µS/cm, and dissolved solids 209–477 mg/L.
- House Spring is a very hard magnesium-bicarbonate water with 23 mg/L calcium, 225 mg/L alkalinity, 553 µS/cm specific conductance, 331 mg/L dissolved solids, and 4.1 mg/L total nitrogen. The nitrogen may come from caliche or livestock operations.
- Arsenic, chromium, lead, and selenium in refuge wells and House Spring were below State limits. Mercury and cadmium were also below limits.
- Radionuclides (eight samples from four refuge wells) showed dissolved gross beta of 3.3–31 pCi/L and dissolved gross alpha of 3.7–81 µg/L. Compliance with gross-alpha limits cannot be determined without radon, uranium, and radium-226 data. Arizona State University analyses found no man-made radionuclides.

**Long-term water-supply outlook**

- Under present conditions, stock and domestic withdrawals in the US and irrigation withdrawals in Mexico do not severely impact flow at refuge wells.
- Wells near the international boundary would have the greatest impact on refuge flow. Detailed Mexican pumpage records are not available.
- The report concludes that current information is insufficient to predict aquifer response to increased pumping. It recommends measuring aquifer characteristics (transmissivity, hydraulic conductivity, storage coefficient) and implementing a monitoring program for discharge, water quality, and nearby pumpage.

**Summary conclusion**

- The hydrologic system provides adequate ground-water conditions for restoring and maintaining the wetland fish and wildlife habitat. Small artesian flows are available from nine wells, and small pumps could supplement short-term demand. The total effects of environmental stresses cannot be evaluated with current data.
