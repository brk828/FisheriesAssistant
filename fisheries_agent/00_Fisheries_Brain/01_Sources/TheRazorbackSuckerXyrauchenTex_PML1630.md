# The Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the Upper Colorado River Basin, 1974–76

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Technical Paper (No. 99, 1980) by Charles W. McAda and Richard S. Wydoski reports a 1974–76 field study of the [[Razorback Sucker]], a native [[Colorado River Basin]] fish recommended for listing as "threatened." The study documented small but concentrated populations at two sites, the mouth of the [[Yampa River]] (Echo Park) and a flooded gravel pit near Grand Junction, Colorado, and examined age, growth, fecundity, spawning, and movement. The authors conclude that the Yampa River is critical to the species' survival, and that reproductive failure is the likely main driver of decline.

The source is a scientific technical report, not a proposal or budget. Its front matter contains a long list of related U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service technical papers and bibliographic entries, which are summarized rather than reproduced here.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large catostomid. A mature male measured 505 mm total length and weighed 1,500 g (collected at Echo Park, November 1975).
- **Sexing:** The last anal-fin ray is curved in mature males and straight in mature females.
- **Status:** Declining mainly because of human alteration of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It was recommended for listing as "threatened" (the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Humpback Chub]] were already designated "endangered").
- **Endemism:** About 74% of native [[Colorado River Basin]] fish species are endemic to the basin (Miller 1958).
- **Study objectives:** (1) distribution and relative abundance; (2) movements, especially spawning migrations from the upper Green River into the Yampa River; (3) spawning areas and ecological requirements; (4) summary of life history.
- **Study period and methods:** Fish were sampled from August 1974 to November 1976, using trammel nets, seines, electrofishing and [[Ultrasonic Telemetry]] tracking. Endangered and threatened fish were released, and razorback suckers were tagged with Floy anchor tags.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Basin alteration:** Dams for hydropower and irrigation changed the [[Colorado River Basin]] in three main ways: seasonal flows declined while daily fluctuations increased, summer water temperatures fell and winter temperatures rose, and turbidity was reduced.
- **Exotic species:** The authors report 10 native and 19 introduced fish species in the mainstem upper Colorado River (Holden and Stalnaker, 1975a). Introduced predators such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Channel Catfish]] are considered a likely factor in the failure of native reproduction.
- **Study sites:**
  - Yampa River stations 1–2 (Lily Park, Castle Park): steep canyons, deep fast water, cobble and boulder substrate.
  - Station 3 (Echo Park, Yampa–Green confluence): main collection site.
  - Station 8 ([[Walker Wildlife Area]]): a flooded gravel pit of 25.6 ha, 0.5–2.0 m deep, connected to the Colorado River by a 6 m canal, near Grand Junction.
  - Green River stations 4–7 (Island Park, Horseshoe Bend, Ouray, Sand Wash): open valleys with low velocity and sand or silt substrate.
- **Climate and water:** Average annual precipitation is 25 cm. Air temperatures range from 40 °C in summer to −25 °C in winter; water temperatures range from 0 °C to 30 °C.
- **Riparian vegetation:** Box elder, cottonwood, willow, tamarisk, rabbitbrush, big sagebrush and Utah juniper.
- **Water quality measures:** Temperature (Ryan thermograph and pocket thermometer), turbidity (Jackson turbidimeter) and velocity (Gurley current meter).

## Key Findings

**Distribution and abundance**
- Range has shrunk substantially. Early reports described large numbers in the lower basin (Salton Sea, San Pedro River), but the species has disappeared from much of the Gila River arm.
- Razorback suckers were most common at two sites: the Yampa mouth (station 3, early spring) and the Colorado River gravel pit (station 8, year-round). They were never numerous.
- Only a few individuals were found elsewhere: one in December 1974 at station 7, and two in April and one in October 1976 at station 4. None were collected at stations 1, 2, 5 or 6.
- In Lake Mead and Lake Mohave, reservoirs held adults but no evidence of young was found. Lake Havasu, Mead and Mojave contained only mature fish.

**Age and growth**
- Ages were estimated from scales, with 4 to 9 growing seasons completed. All sampled fish were mature.
- Fish from the Colorado River were significantly larger than same-age fish from the Yampa and Green rivers (P < 0.10 for males, P = 0.01 for females). The authors attribute this mainly to warmer Colorado River water.
- Age estimates are uncertain: the annuli were hard to read, the method was not validated, and tagging may have slowed growth in some fish.

**Length–weight relation**
- The log-weight/log-length regression was not significantly different between sexes or between river systems, though Colorado River fish tended to be longer and heavier.

**Reproduction and fecundity**
- Estimated fecundity ranged from 24,490 eggs (fish 529 mm TL) to 76,876 eggs (fish 484 mm TL). Ten specimens were dissected, and two ovary counts were used to check the estimates.
- Fecundity showed no significant relation to total length.
- Spawning occurred in April and May at water temperatures of 6–18 °C. Three probable spawning sites were identified, most often a cobble gravel bar in the Yampa River 400 m upstream from the mouth (about 1 m depth, velocity about 1 m/s). Fourteen spawning fish (2 females, 12 males) were taken there in May 1975.
- Reproductive failure is considered the main factor in decline. Young were rarely found, and juveniles may be confused with young [[Flannelmouth Sucker]].

**Movement**
- Radio tracking used five males and one female, tagged with ultrasonic transmitters. Range was 150 m in quiet water and only 10 m in turbulent water.
- Tagged fish did not move more than 1 km up the Yampa River before returning to the mouth.
- A female tagged in the Green River was recaptured two weeks later at the Yampa mouth, 21 km upstream, while ripe. This suggests a spawning migration.
- Razorback suckers apparently left the Yampa mouth in late spring or early summer.

**Community composition**
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] was the most common species at most sites (roughly seven times more frequent than razorback suckers in trammel net catches at station 3).
- In the gravel pit (station 8), native fish formed a small share of the catch. Introduced [[Common Carp]], green sunfish and black bullheads together made up nearly a third of the total.
- Razorback suckers made up 0.8–4.2% of the catch at station 8.

**Hybridization**
- Hybrids with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]] were documented, including a razorback × white sucker hybrid. The authors note that young hybrids may be misidentified.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- The Yampa River is critical to the survival of the razorback sucker and other native fish. It warms the Green River and helps restore historical temperature conditions.
- About one-fourth of the basin's river length is inundated by reservoirs. Changes in temperature or streamflow upstream could significantly reduce the remaining habitat of the four endangered or threatened fishes.
- Why reproduction has failed is unclear, because the closely related [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (with similar spawning needs) is still doing well.
