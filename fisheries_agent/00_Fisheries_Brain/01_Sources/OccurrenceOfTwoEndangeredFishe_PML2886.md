# Occurrence of Two Endangered Fishes in the Little Snake River, Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This manuscript, prepared by [[Edmund J. Wick]], [[John A. Hawkins]] and T. P. Nesler, reports the first documented occurrence of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and suspected [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the [[Little Snake River]], a tributary of the [[Yampa River]] in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. The Little Snake River had been largely overlooked as habitat for endangered fishes. Sampling in 1988 was prompted by observations of suitable canyon habitat and by the collection of humpback-like chub. The authors conclude that the river's canyon reach is important native fish habitat that should be protected, and that natural flow regimes should be maintained.

The accompanying correspondence is an acceptance letter from *The Southwestern Naturalist* (19 July 1990), with page-charge and proof instructions.

## Background & Description

The Little Snake River delivers significant water and sediment to the Yampa River. Earlier surveys (Holden 1973) had not recorded endangered fishes there, although local ranchers and anglers reported Colorado Squawfish and Humpback Chub using the river periodically (Seethaler 1978). Information on squawfish use was gathered alongside radiotelemetry studies of adult spring migration in the Yampa River. Field work was funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]] through the Endangered Fishes Investigations study.

Taxonomic uncertainty remains a problem for the *Gila* complex of the Colorado River Basin, which is the focus of ongoing work by the [[Recovery Implementation Program]]. Photographs of the suspected humpback chub were reviewed by researchers working on the taxonomy of the complex. They concluded that the fish fell within the range of variability seen in populations from the Colorado River, lower Green River and Yampa Canyon. Other researchers suggested the fish might be hybrids within the *Gila* complex.

The Little Snake River study area extended from the Yampa confluence upstream to river kilometer (RK) 56.0. The reach below RK 56.0 meandered through pasture and cropland over sandy substrate. A steep-walled sandstone canyon about 3.2 km long lies between RK 12.2 and the lower reaches. Its upper 1.5 km is constricted, steep and boulder-cobble bottomed. Deep, turbulent eddies form along canyon walls during spring runoff and become pools at baseflow. In July, the deepest pool had a maximum depth of 3 m. Downstream of the canyon, the river runs over sand through irrigated cropland and [[Cottonwood]] bottomlands, where pools are few, scattered and shallow.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** Flows were high in late spring and dropped quickly in June, falling to about 5 m³/s by 6 July. August and September flows were at base levels of roughly 0.2 to 0.3 m³/s, rising to about 0.85 m³/s in October. The authors describe the Little Snake River as an example of the discharge variation under which native fishes evolved in the [[Colorado River System]].

**Radiotagged squawfish.** Two adult Colorado Squawfish were implanted with radio transmitters in September 1987 on the Yampa River, 3 km above the Little Snake confluence. One 824 mm TL fish was located in the Little Snake River on 24 and 25 May in a large eddy below an island at RK 2.2. After flows declined it moved to the Yampa River, then migrated to the Yampa Canyon spawning area by early July. It later returned to an overwintering area above the confluence, where it was found on 2 October. The second fish was seen moving upstream in main-channel run habitat on 5 June, and its radio contact was lost after 30 June. Water temperature was about 19°C in the Little Snake and cooler in the Yampa.

**Community composition.** Sampling for adult fish used trammel nets, seines, electrofishing and angling during spring runoff, July and October. Of the adult and large juvenile fish collected, 308 fish are reported in the study, with suspected humpback chub taken from eddies 1.1 to 1.3 m deep over sand and boulder substrate. Young-of-year collections on 25 June used fine-mesh seines and dipnets. A total of 1,250 fishes from 10 to 30 mm TL were collected, and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] made up 82% of them, with [[Bluehead Sucker]] at about 17%. Other species included [[Sand Shiner]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[White Sucker]], [[Black Bullhead]] and [[Common Carp]].

**Suspected humpback chub.** Seven suspected humpback chub were collected, along with suspected hybrids of humpback chub and [[Roundtail Chub]] and one Colorado Squawfish. Two suspected humpback chub (219 mm and 310 mm TL), collected 9 June, had breeding tubercles. Roundtail chub were the most numerous collected fish (538 reported). Suspected humpback chub from the Little Snake were similar in appearance to fish from [[Cross Mountain Canyon]] on the Yampa and [[Debeque Canyon]] on the Colorado River. These three canyons represent the known upstream distribution limits of humpback-like chub in these rivers. Unlike large typical humpback chub from [[Black Rock Canyon]] and [[Westwater Canyon]], the Little Snake fish lacked an abrupt nuchal hump and fleshy snout. The authors attribute this to phenotypic variation, smaller size and younger age, or hybridization.

**Spawning.** Because a radiotagged squawfish was near the canyon, spawning in the canyon was considered. Further evidence suggested the adults had left before spawning. Supporting observations included the lack of tuberculation or expressible gametes in the one large adult collected, the absence of other squawfish near radiotagged fish, the later detection of one radiotagged fish at a known Yampa Canyon spawning area, and the collection of larval squawfish. The authors state that these results do not completely rule out spawning in the Little Snake River.

**Habitat value.** Backwaters and flooded mouths of smaller tributaries on the Yampa and Colorado rivers provide warmer water and more abundant forage during spring runoff. The authors suggest that adult squawfish use these habitats to prepare physiologically for spawning. Juvenile roundtail chub and suckers in the Little Snake may serve as prey for adult squawfish. Warmer water and feeding opportunities are considered important for conditioning fish for migration and spawning (McKeown 1984).

## Key Findings

- **First documented occurrence.** Colorado Squawfish and suspected Humpback Chub were recorded in the Little Snake River in 1988, extending known endangered fish habitat to this tributary.
- **Canyon as critical habitat.** The canyon reach holds deep eddies and pools that concentrate fishes, especially during spring runoff. The authors recommend its protection.
- **Seasonal use.** Suspected humpback chub were restricted to the spring runoff period, probably moving into deep, warm eddies and then leaving as discharge dropped and eddies became pools. Squawfish moved in and out with spring flows.
- **Taxonomic status unresolved.** Suspected humpback chub resemble fish from other canyon populations but differ in key morphological traits. Their status as pure humpback chub, phenotypic variants or hybrids remains open, pending [[Recovery Implementation Program]] studies.
- **Spawning not confirmed.** Squawfish probably left the Little Snake before spawning, but spawning there cannot be ruled out.
- **Flow management.** Natural flow regimes should be maintained to preserve this tributary habitat for native fishes.
- **Further research recommended.** The authors call for more study of the Little Snake River and other upper Colorado tributaries to clarify distribution, taxonomy and behavior of endangered fishes.
