# Genetic Marker Identification in Largemouth Bass with Electrophoresis of Low-Risk Tissues

#ichthyology #marine-biology #micropterus-salmoides

## Executive Summary

This study tests whether [[Electrophoretic Genotyping]] can be carried out on [[Largemouth Bass]] (*[[Micropterus Salmoides]]*) using low-risk tissues, so that fish can be genotyped without the loss of health or reproductive performance that invasive [[Fish Biopsy]] or necropsy causes. The authors sampled erythrocytes, blood plasma, caudal and pelvic fin, and scale epithelium from live hatchery fish and resolved enzyme products by [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]]. Their main result is that low-risk tissues, particularly [[Caudal Fin Clip]] samples, separate the [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] subspecies from the [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] subspecies with greater than 99% certainty. The approach is presented as a practical tool for [[Brood Fish Selection]], stock identification, and research on [[Endangered Species]].

## Background & Description

Largemouth bass is the study model. The authors describe two subspecies, the northern largemouth bass (*M. s. salmoides*) and the Florida largemouth bass (*M. s. floridanus*), together with intergrade hatchery stocks. The work was published in the Transactions of the American Fisheries Society (1986). Its authors were from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] [[National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] in San Marcos, Texas, and from the University of Texas System Cancer Center in Smithville, Texas. The paper notes that it also forms part of a dissertation by J. H. Williamson at Texas A&M University.

The paper explains why genetic markers are valuable. Unlike artificial marks or tags, they are permanent, heritable, and do not alter fish behavior or general health. They are useful for monitoring gene frequency changes and [[Stock Contamination]]. Existing electrophoretic methods relied on tissue sources that endanger animals, such as dermal muscle punches (Crawford et al. 1977), adipose fins (Vuorinen and Piironen 1984), and liver biopsy (Harvey et al. 1984). Biopsied fish often cannot be reliably used as brood stock or re-identified in the wild, and small fish or fish lacking adipose fins are poor candidates for punch sampling.

The authors list several applications that would benefit from non-lethal genotyping:
- Identifying brood fish for selective breeding.
- Screening brood fish for marker protein loci.
- Maintaining strain purity and identifying offspring of known genotype for stocking.
- Identifying rare, endangered, or threatened species or individuals.
- Verifying fish taken during fishing tournaments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper focuses on hatchery and managed populations rather than on habitat or ecosystem conditions. Its ecological relevance lies in the management problem it addresses. Most recent introductions have involved releasing Florida largemouth bass into northern populations, so the ability to recognize Florida-specific markers in living fish is important for fishery managers. The authors also state that population genetic studies on endangered species could proceed without the loss of individual fish. They do not report field habitat data, water chemistry, or community data.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Four stocks were sampled: *M. s. floridanus* (Florida stock); *M. s. salmoides* (West Lake, Texas stock); and two intergrade hatchery stocks (Inks Dam, Texas, and Marion, Alabama).
- Fish were anesthetized in 200 mg/l [[Tricaine Anesthesia]]. Blood was drawn from caudal vessels in heparinized syringes and separated into plasma and red cells. One pelvic fin and a section of caudal fin were removed, and scales and epidermis were scraped from the caudal peduncle.
- Tissues were stored at -80°C before electrophoresis.
- Vertical starch gel electrophoresis followed Brewer (1970) and Siciliano and Shaw (1976), with histochemical stain recipes from Shaw and Prasad (1970), Siciliano and Shaw (1976), and Harris and Hopkinson (1977). Three buffer systems were used: Tris-EDTA-borate (pH 8.0), Tris-citrate (pH 7.0), and Tris-maleate (pH 7.4). Glyoxalase resolved best on the Tris-maleate buffer.
- Enzyme nomenclature followed Philipp et al. (1983) and the Commission on Biochemical Nomenclature (1978).

**Enzyme resolution**
- The authors identified at least 64 enzyme locus products in Florida, northern, and intergrade largemouth bass using invasive methods.
- Thirty-six protein and enzyme systems were adequately resolved from noninvasively sampled tissues (Table 1). Based on the Xiphophorus enzyme systems used as a reference, the authors estimate at least 44 coding loci in this sample.
- Locus products resolved per tissue type were:
  - Caudal fin: 40
  - Pelvic fin: 39
  - Scale epithelium: 38
  - Hemolysates (red blood cells): 34
  - Blood plasma: 3
- Plasma proteins and esterases were excluded because the number of coding loci was uncertain.
- Beyond caudal fin, plasma, pelvic fin, and scale epithelium gave no extra information, except that α-glucosidase was resolved from plasma and pelvic fin.
- The abstract reports that low-risk tissues resolved a large majority of the 64 gene products examined. The exact count and percentage are partly illegible in the scan, so they are not reported here.

**Polymorphism and subspecies discrimination**
- Of 70 structural loci examined across the wider body of work, 23 (33%) were polymorphic.
- Of these 23 polymorphic loci, at least 16 (about 70%) can be resolved from blood or fin tissue.
- Fixed allelic differences at two loci separate Florida from northern subspecies: [[Isocitrate Dehydrogenase-B]] (IDHB) and [[Glutamate-Oxaloacetate Transaminase-B]] (GOTB, synonym aspartate aminotransferase-B). IDHB can be resolved from blood or fin tissue.
- The authors report that the subspecies can be distinguished with greater than 99% certainty using only tissues that do not significantly debilitate fish.
- No fewer than 40 enzyme locus products can be resolved from caudal fin clips alone. This may allow genetic studies of populations where sacrificing individuals is infeasible or undesirable.
- Figure 1 shows polymorphic phenotypes for adenosine deaminase, IDHB, [[Mannosephosphate Isomerase]], peptidase-3, superoxide dismutase-2, and triosephosphate isomerase-2. Some phenotypes, including extra SOD bands and TPI2 heterodimers, reflect additional loci.

**Conclusions and implications**
- Electrophoretic genotyping with low-risk tissues can shorten breeding programs by one or more generations. Rather than genotyping offspring to infer parental genotype, managers can identify brood fish directly and keep them in use, with little or no loss of individual or reproductive performance.
- Population genetic studies using fin samples alone can match many published studies based on killed individuals in the number of loci analyzed.
- With suitable background genetic data, the method can estimate the proportions of strains or stocks in wild or managed populations.
- The authors call for more allele frequency data from additional Florida and northern largemouth bass populations.
