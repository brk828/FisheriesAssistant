# Native Fish Stocking and Stock Tank Management at Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge

#ichthyology #marine-biology #native-fish-stocking

## Executive Summary

This progress report, prepared by the [[Center for Environmental Studies]] at [[Arizona State University]] for interested parties, documents native fish stocking and monitoring at small impoundments ("tanks") in the [[Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge]], Arizona, from about 1987 to 1989. The work tracked fish growth and survival in several tanks, recorded precipitation and evaporation, and assessed whether the tanks could hold water and fish through drought. The main conclusions were that survival of stocked native fish was not yet quantified, that drought and temperature extremes strongly shaped tank habitat, and that some tanks could support native fish if managed carefully. Several recommendations followed, including removing nonnative fish and limiting harvest timing.

## Background & Description

The study was a cooperative effort involving Arizona State University personnel and refuge staff. It was carried out between September 1987 and September 1989, with sampling at multiple tanks. Fish were measured and weighed when captured. The report notes that the tanks are typical of semiarid stock ponds, many of which are fed by rainfall runoff and lose water to evaporation and infiltration.

The source text is heavily affected by scanning errors. Several tank names and the native fish species are not reliably legible. The report's species stocking table (Table 1, a summary of native fish stocking, with fish counts, total lengths and dates through October 1989) is largely unreadable, so no species checklist can be reliably reproduced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tank hydrology and drought.** Tank water levels fluctuated widely, depending on rainfall and temperature. Several tanks dried completely during the drought. Tanks on the western margin of the refuge held water more consistently than tanks in the central area. The report attributes this to the nearby mountains, which may receive more precipitation, and to runoff inflow.

**Precipitation.** Monthly and summer precipitation data (in inches) were compiled from [[National Weather Service]] stations near the refuge (Table 3). Summer values were given for 1988–89, covering three months (July–September).

**Predation and nonnative fish.** The report notes that nonnative fish in some tanks reached large numbers and recommends their removal from tanks used for native fish. It also suggests that some native fish survived in tanks that had been dried and refilled.

**Habitat condition.** Habitat degradation had reduced the wetland cover and vegetation in the refuge, and several tanks were overgrown with aquatic plants. Management of vegetation was recommended as part of tank maintenance.

## Key Findings

- **Tank Survival and Growth.** At the tank recorded as "Tequila Tank," fish were stocked on 31 March 1987 and averaged about 17.9 mm in December 1988 (n = 110). Earlier sampling in May 1988 gave n = 44. A second tank showed mean fish size of 4.3 cm in May 1986 (n = 14), and larger fish in December 1986.
- **Genetic Sampling.** Thirteen specimens were collected in November 1987 and preserved for genetic studies. Length and weight data were to be collected later.
- **Drought Effects.** Fish losses during dry periods were substantial, and at one tank nearly all native fish were lost during drying. Tanks that retained water through drought supported fish better.
- **Temperature and Water Loss.** The report links high summer temperatures and high evaporation to rapid water loss. Summer evaporation rates were high enough to dry shallow tanks in a single season.
- **Harvest Timing.** Harvest of stocked fish should be completed after two summers. Differential mortality of larger fish in the 1987–89 period was linked to precipitation and temperature extremes, and the report advises against extending harvest beyond two summers unless climatic conditions are favorable.
- **Survival Estimates.** Native fish survival rates had not yet been determined. The authors state that fish stocks and habitat are suitable for short-term mark-recapture estimates of population size, which can be readily done in tanks with known stocking densities.
- **Stocking Density Experiments.** If survival is high enough to support harvest, the authors recommend experiments to determine stocking densities that maximize fish size and number of harvested fish, to be conducted with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].
- **Recommended Stocking.** Stocking should use young fish available from regional hatchery facilities. The report notes that native fish can be raised in stock tanks under cover, but that some tanks become unsuitable because they dry or become hostile to fish.
- **Nonnative Fish Removal.** Tanks holding large numbers of nonnative fish should be cleared before native stocking.
- **Contact.** The report invites readers with further questions to contact the authors at the Center for Environmental Studies.
