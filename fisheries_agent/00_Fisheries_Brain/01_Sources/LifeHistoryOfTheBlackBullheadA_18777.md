# Life History of the Black Bullhead (Ameiurus melas) in Clear Lake, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 study by [[John L. Forney]] (Iowa State College, Department of Zoology and Entomology) examines the life history of the [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus melas*) in [[Clear Lake]], a shallow eutrophic glacial lake of 3,643 acres in north-central Iowa. Data came mainly from a 1951 field effort, supplemented by [[Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit]] records from 1943 and 1947–1954. The paper covers sampling gear effects, age determination from vertebrae, opercles and spines, growth, length-weight relationships, diet, parasites, reproduction, survival and angling trends. Growth, condition and parasite burden differed among three lake areas, and year-class strength, linked to aquatic vegetation, drove population abundance.

## Background & Description

- **Angling importance:** Bullheads were described as the most widely popular sport fish in Iowa. Abundance and average size in Clear Lake had varied in recent years, with angling success varying accordingly.
- **Study species:** Nearly all bullheads examined from Clear Lake were *Ameiurus melas*. A few [[Yellow Bullhead]] (*Ameiurus natalis*) had been recorded.
- **Study area:** Clear Lake is shallow and eutrophic, with a surface area of 3,643 acres and a drainage area barely larger than its basin. Three areas were recognized in 1951 with separate, distinguishable bullhead populations during summer:
  - **East end:** oval, 2.1 × 3.8 miles, maximum depth 20 ft, sandy shallows grading to muck below about 7 ft.
  - **West end:** maximum width 0.7 mile, length 1.0 mile, maximum depth 7 ft, vegetated shoreline.
  - **[[Ventura Marsh]]:** formerly connected to the west end, now isolated by a road fill and a state carp trap; filled with vegetation by mid-July 1951.
- **Measurements:** Standard length was measured to the tail crease and total length to the caudal fin tip. The conversion was Standard length = 0.833 × Total length, based on 654 fish of 8–12 in total length. The ratio shifted slightly with size (0.826 at 6.5–7.4 in SL to 0.843 at 9.5–10.2 in SL).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling and gear.** Most adults were taken with experimental [[Gillnet]]s, supplemented by wing nets, wire traps and hoop nets in 1951, 1953 and 1954. Mean lengths showed no appreciable gear selectivity within the 8–12 in size range. Condition (plumpness) did differ by gear, with a mean ponderal index of 3.30 for 364 gillnet fish versus 2.82 for 101 wire-trap fish and 2.90 for 91 hoop-net fish (ANOVA F = 32.66, significant at the 1% level). Gillnets were lifted every two hours, whereas traps and wing nets were lifted after 12–24 hours. Gillnet fish were fuller and had swallowed water, which explained only part of the difference.

**Age determination.** Alternating dark and light bands on [[Vertebral Centra]] were treated as annual marks. Opercles and dorsal spines were also tested. Spine sections matched vertebral marks in number and position but were harder to prepare. Opercular marks for the first three years were obscured by thickening of the anterior bone. A well-defined ring that appeared in July 1951 was judged a false annulus.

**Temperature and growth.** Summer 1951 was cool: mean June, July and August temperatures were 62.8°, 70.0° and 67.0°F, 3.0–4.1°F below normal at Mason City. Young-of-the-year grew more slowly that year than in other years.

**Vegetation and reproduction.** Young-of-the-year were very abundant in 1945–1947, when vegetation was at a maximum. Vegetation was sparse in 1948–1950, when few young were seen. Reproduction succeeded in 1951 as vegetation recovered, and it stayed successful in 1952–1954 under moderate vegetation.

**Parasites and disease.** [[Clinostomum complanatum]] (yellow grub) was the most consequential parasite. It was nearly 100% prevalent in Ventura Marsh bullheads in August, versus under 1% elsewhere, with infestations above 500 cysts per fish.

## Key Findings

- **Population structure:** In 1950, 84 adults averaged 7.4 in total length with four year marks. These and all adults netted in 1951–1952 were survivors of the 1946 year class. The 1946 class was the only large group, and it dominated catches until about 1950.
- **Year-class strength:** Young-of-the-year were abundant in 1943, 1945–1947, 1951–1954 and scarce or absent in 1948–1949 and 1950. The strong 1951 class was thinned by winter mortality (dead 2–3 in fish along the shore in spring 1952).
- **Growth:** The 1946 year class grew slowly from 1948 through 1950 (0.04 in/week in 1950, 0.24 in/week in 1951) and showed little growth in 1952–1954. Ventura Marsh fish grew at 0.09 in/week in 1951, versus 0.25 in (west) and 0.24 in (east).
- **Condition:** Ventura Marsh fish declined in condition through the summer. Males averaged 12.7 g heavier than females at equal length. East-end fish averaged 35.1 g heavier than west-end fish at 209.2 mm mean length.
- **Length-weight relationships:** Regression slopes differed among 1950, 1951 and 1952 (F = 35.33), and 1952 fish were lighter for their length than 1951 fish.
- **Diet:** Adults ate mainly chironomid larvae (almost entirely *Tendipes tentans*) in 1951. Young-of-the-year under 0.9 in fed only on entomostraca, and larger young added chironomids and *Hyalella*.
- **Parasites:** Of 399 adults examined in 1951, five internal parasites were recorded. Two tapeworms (*Corallobothrium fimbriatum* and *C. giganteum*) had no detectable effect on condition. Only *Clinostomum complanatum* appeared to significantly affect growth or survival.
- **Reproduction:** Spawning ran from late June (first spent female June 26) to early August (last ripe female August 6), peaking in early July. Fecundity was about 3,283 eggs for 8–8.9 in females and 3,845 for 9–9.9 in females. Schools concentrated along vegetated shoreline.
- **Behavior:** Adults tended young schools for a time, typically in water over two feet deep, then deserted them when the young reached about 1 in.
- **Predation:** No bullhead remains were found in walleye, yellow perch, northern pike or yellow bass stomachs in 1951, so predation seemed to have little effect that summer.
- **Angling:** Catch per 24 hours in experimental gillnets rose from 0.4 (1949) to 13.4 (1950), 9.5 (1951) and 16.1 (1952). The 1951 and 1952 year classes supported angling from 1953 onward.
- **Conclusion:** Aquatic vegetation, first-winter survival and parasitism (in Ventura Marsh) were identified as the main controls on bullhead abundance and growth.
