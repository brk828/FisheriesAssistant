# Preliminary Draft Management Plan for RPA 3 Native Fish Habitats

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #habitat-restoration

## Executive Summary

This memorandum and preliminary draft management plan, dated August 11, 1998, addresses implementation of Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) 3 from the April 30, 1997 Biological Opinion on [[Bureau of Reclamation]] operations on the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The RPA requires establishment of at least 300-600 acres of protected habitats for endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The plan outlines management principles for isolated backwater habitats designed to support self-sustaining populations of these native fish species, free from non-native fish predation. Implementation involves partnerships with federal agencies ([[Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Bureau of Land Management]]), state wildlife agencies (Arizona, California, Nevada), and academic institutions ([[Arizona State University]]). The document represents a preliminary draft circulated for review, with full draft anticipated by end of August 1998.

## Background & Description

The [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) is a highly streamlined cyprinid reaching 600 mm in length (300-350 mm more common), characterized by a thin, pencil-like caudal peduncle and large falcate fins. This long-lived species may reach 49 years of age. Young prey on aquatic invertebrates; adults utilize aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, plankton, and occasionally small fish. Though characterized as mainstem-dwelling, bonytail chubs successfully utilize backwater habitats.

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a robust catostomid easily recognized by the large keel behind the head and anterior to the dorsal fin. These large fish reach almost one meter in length, with most Lake Mohave individuals between 500-750 mm. Also long-lived (45+ years), young razorback suckers eat mostly plankton while adults utilize benthic food sources. Historically, razorback suckers extensively used backwater habitats, especially during spring and summer.

Both species' distributions and population sizes have been significantly reduced from pre-1850s levels. The pre-development [[Colorado River]] was characterized by extremely variable flows, water quality, and a mosaic of aquatic, marsh and riparian habitats in all stages of seral succession. Backwaters would form, age, become isolated, and eventually be eliminated by river flow events—these were important habitats for native fish species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The lower Colorado River's historic floodplain contained diverse backwater habitats crucial for native fish life histories. Current backwater types include: (1) completely isolated ponds connected to river only subsurface; (2) ponds separated by permeable dikes, channelized banks or similar structures; and (3) connected embayments. Connection degree often varies with river level.

Climate along the lower Colorado River features very hot summers. In isolated ponds without sufficient circulation, evaporation rates, increasing temperature, and low dissolved oxygen levels increase fish kill risks. Productivity may become an issue in some habitats. Successful recruitment of young requires adequate amounts of appropriately-sized plankton. Because natural dispersal from ponds cannot occur, populations may increase beyond levels the productivity can support, creating stunted populations.

The project aims to mimic historical backwater function while preventing non-native fish contamination. Physical barriers (screens, sand filters, permeable barriers) will separate habitats from the river system, though complete prevention of non-native fish entry cannot be guaranteed. More publicly accessible habitats face higher reintroduction risk.

## Key Findings

**RPA Requirements and Timeline:**
- Establish at least 300-600 acres of protected native fish habitats by April 2002 (five years from April 30, 1997 Biological Opinion)
- Minimum habitat size reduced from 100 acres to 3-20 acres to provide more effective population management
- [[Bureau of Reclamation]] has identified over 250 acres of existing open waters for potential use
- Implementation must occur within five-year BO period; long-term maintenance will continue beyond

**Habitat Management Approach:**
- Habitats will support "wild" populations, not hatchery populations, with minimum human oversight
- Intensive management activities will be rare events responding to specific needs
- Ongoing management will consist primarily of periodic sampling of fish populations and habitat conditions
- Each habitat will have site-specific management plan developed as approved for project inclusion
- Adaptive management approach using initial evaluation criteria

**Renovation and Stocking:**
- All habitats with extant fish populations will be renovated prior to stocking native fish
- Renovation will use toxicants (rotenone) or complete drying to eliminate non-native fish
- Native fish and valuable game fish will be removed before renovation when possible
- Stocking will use fingerling-size fish rather than subadults to reduce demand on limited grow-out space

**Species Stocking Strategy:**
- Questions remain about behavioral interactions between bonytail chub and razorback sucker in confined habitats
- Until more information obtained: one-half of habitats stocked with both species, one-quarter each with single species
- For bonytail chub: only source is [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] broodstock from [[Lake Mohave]]
- For razorback sucker: wild-caught larvae from Lake Mohave are preferred source to maximize genetic variance
- Stocking estimates must be provided to hatchery/collection organizers before December 31 of preceding year

**Population Management:**
- Monitoring will include water quality parameters (temperature, conductivity, dissolved oxygen) 2-4 times yearly initially
- Fish populations assessed at least annually or on habitat-specific schedule
- Monitoring will detect non-native fish contamination, size structure of native fish community, and population ratios
- Lack of recruitment or overpopulation (stunting) may require management intervention
- Management options for overpopulation include: complete elimination and restocking, partial removal by netting/electroshocking, habitat fertilization, or spawning area manipulation

**Partnerships and Governance:**
- Cooperative Agreements with Arizona Game and Fish Department, California Department of Fish and Game, and Nevada Department of Wildlife
- Technical review group will provide advice on site identification, monitoring needs, problem identification, and research proposals
- Group will include representatives from federal agencies, state agencies, researchers, and interested parties
- Annual meetings to review monitoring results and determine next year's monitoring needs

**Genetic Management:**
- Overall genetic management plan needed to ensure individual populations maintain genetic variance
- Wild razorback sucker larvae preferred over hatchery-spawned fish to provide broader genetic representation
- [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] developing management plan for bonytail chub broodstock at Dexter
- Site-specific plans must integrate new habitats into existing habitat mix for genetic diversity maintenance

**Long-term Considerations:**
- Habitats may require replacement as they age and value diminishes
- Replacement options: utilize another existing open water area, or create new ponds in floodplain
- Criteria for habitat abandonment/rehabilitation: habitat quality, water quality, productivity, fish population health, economics, availability of replacements, past management problems
- Potential for creating series of ponds mimicking natural meander succession across floodplain

**Compliance and Permits:**
- NEPA compliance on project as whole to be accomplished in 1998
- Federal Endangered Species Act permits required for hands-on participants
- State scientific collecting permits likely required
- Compliance with Clean Water Act, regulations on toxicant application
- California actions must comply with state environmental laws

**Research Opportunities:**
- Habitats will provide research opportunities on life history, behavior, interactions with non-native fish, habitat needs, and small impoundment management
- Research funding not directly provided under RPA 3 but may be available under RPAs 4 and 14
- Technical review group will provide recommendations on research proposals

**Meeting Summary (June 9, 1998):**
- Overall habitat design should minimize exotic species introduction, sustain healthy distributed populations, use many small ponds with few large ones
- Adopt adaptive management approach with initial evaluation criteria
- Develop emergency response plan for acceptable changes in habitat quality and fish population composition
- Create public information materials addressing stakeholder concerns (rifle clubs, anglers, Indian Tribes)
- Construct ponds with maximum depth of 5 feet
