# Genetic Characterization of Sonoran Topminnow Populations

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Philip W. Hedrick]] and [[Karen M. Parker]] (Arizona State University) uses molecular markers to characterize the genetic structure of endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Yaqui Topminnow]] populations in Arizona. The authors sampled nine additional Gila topminnow populations and two Yaqui topminnow populations and compared them with earlier data from four Gila topminnow watersheds. Analyses used five polymorphic [[Microsatellite Loci]] and a [[Major Histocompatibility Complex]] (MHC) gene. The results identify five natural groups of Gila topminnow, trace the ancestry of several restocked and recolonized sites, and show that the Gila and Yaqui topminnows are highly divergent. The authors recommend that the Gila topminnow groups be managed as [[Evolutionarily Significant Unit]]s (ESUs) and [[Management Unit]]s (MUs), and that the two taxa be considered for ranking as separate species.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis* subsp. *occidentalis*) was once one of the most abundant fishes in the [[Gila River Drainage]]. It now survives naturally in the United States in only four isolated Arizona watersheds, comprising eight populations. Other populations exist because of extensive restocking. At least one site on the [[Santa Cruz River]] appears to have been recolonized naturally.

The Yaqui topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis* subsp. *sonoriensis*) was never widespread in the United States, because the Yaqui River drainage covers only a small part of southeastern Arizona. It now occurs in the United States only on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]], in the upper Yaqui watershed.

Conservation of endangered species depends on identifying evolutionarily significant units. The authors define ESUs as populations evolving independently of others, following [[Waples]] (1995). They define MUs as populations that have diverged in allele frequency and are connected by low gene flow, following [[Moritz]] (1994). Molecular variants can reveal differences between groups that were previously indistinguishable.

**Sampled populations and sources**

- **Gila topminnow (13 populations in total):** The four earlier-studied sites were [[Bylas Spring]] 2, [[Cienega Creek]], [[Monkey Spring]] and [[Sharp Spring]]. The nine new sites were Bylas Spring 1, [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]], Watson Wash (Safford), Cottonwood Spring, Red Rock at Cott Tank, Red Rock Falls, Coalmine Creek, [[Sonoita Creek]] (below Patagonia Lake), and the Santa Cruz River (downstream of the Nogales wastewater treatment plant outflow). Samples were mainly collected by David Weedman (AGF).
- **Yaqui topminnow (2 populations):** Tule Spring (n = 20) and North Pond (n = 19), both on the San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge. Samples were collected by Kevin Cobble (USFS).

Site histories matter for interpreting the genetics. Boyce Thompson was used extensively for restocking and was thought to derive from Monkey Spring fish, possibly with input from Bylas Spring and/or the extinct [[Coscio Wash]] population. Watson Wash is a warm spring stocked from an unknown source. The Santa Cruz site appears to have been recolonized from unknown sources.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study addresses genetic isolation and connectivity among fragmented spring and stream habitats in the Sonoran Desert. Gila topminnow populations occur in isolated watersheds, and the genetic patterns largely reflect that isolation. Human-mediated restocking has moved fish among sites, and the authors use genetic data to reconstruct those movements. The Santa Cruz River site illustrates natural recolonization in a river system affected by urban wastewater discharge.

The comparison with [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) restriction-site data from [[Quattro]] et al. (1996) and with morphological and behavioural differences (Minckley, personal communication) supports the view that the two taxa have been isolated for a long time.

## Key Findings

**1. Five natural Gila topminnow groups**
Microsatellite analyses separate natural populations into five groups:
1. **Bylas Spring:** Bylas Spring 1 and 2 are very similar to each other and distinct from other sites. They show low genetic variation, measured as heterozygosity and number of alleles.
2. **Monkey Spring–Cottonwood Spring:** These samples are similar to each other and distinct from all other natural populations, with intermediate genetic variation.
3. **Red Rock–Coalmine–Sonoita:** Sonoita Creek, Coalmine Canyon, Red Rock at Cott Tank and Red Rock Falls cluster together, with Coalmine and Sonoita being very similar. Red Rock at Cott Tank is variable at only one locus. These populations have intermediate variation.
4. **Cienega Creek:** Intermediate genetic variation, with nearly non-overlapping alleles at loci Pooc-C15 and Pooc-LL53 compared with Sharp Spring.
5. **Sharp Spring:** Higher than average genetic variation. It shares no alleles with other groups at the MHC locus.

**2. Ancestry of restocked and recolonized sites**
- **Boyce Thompson** appears to derive primarily from Monkey Spring, consistent with its documented origin. It shows no detectable ancestry from Bylas Spring, because it has no allele overlap with Bylas Spring samples at locus Pooc-G10 or at the MHC gene. It carries an MHC allele (Pooc-I2) at low frequency, not found elsewhere, and a relatively high frequency of allele Pooc-9, which has been found only at Sharp Spring. These alleles may reflect ancestry from Coscio Wash, but the authors state that this cannot be tested because no Coscio Wash samples exist.
- **Watson Wash (Safford)** is very similar to Boyce Thompson, indicating that Boyce Thompson was the source.
- **Santa Cruz River** clusters with the nearby Sonoita Creek samples. It is the most variable sample analysed, with variation at all five microsatellite loci and the highest heterozygosity and number of alleles. It shares several alleles with Sharp Spring, but the extent of ancestry from upper Santa Cruz stocks is unclear.

**3. Yaqui topminnow genetics**
- Three loci (Pooc-4-44, Pooc-Acc and Pooc-G53) that were invariant in Gila topminnow are variable in the Yaqui topminnow. Locus Pooc-G10 did not amplify reliably and could not be used.
- Eight MHC alleles were found in the two Yaqui samples. None occurred in any Gila topminnow population.
- The two Yaqui samples are very similar in allele frequencies and in the amount of genetic variation, which is substantial.

**4. Extreme divergence between the two taxa**
- For six loci, including MHC, no alleles are shared between the Gila and Yaqui topminnows.
- For three loci, allele sizes differ greatly between taxa. At locus Pooc-O056, Gila topminnow alleles range from 143 to 153 base pairs, while the Yaqui topminnow is fixed for allele 256, more than 100 base pairs larger.
- The authors note that mutations at dinucleotide microsatellites usually change allele size by only two base pairs. The large difference therefore implies very long and complete isolation.
- This is consistent with mtDNA restriction-site differences reported by Quattro et al. (1996).

**5. Conclusions and management recommendations**
- **Five natural Gila topminnow groups** were identified: Bylas, Monkey Spring–Cottonwood Spring, Red Rock–Coalmine–Sonoita, Cienega Creek and Sharp Spring.
- The earlier recommendation that Monkey Spring be treated as an ESU is supported. Monkey and Cottonwood Springs should tentatively be treated as a single ESU.
- Bylas Spring 2, Cienega Creek and Sharp Spring should be treated as separate MUs, as previously suggested.
- The Sonoita Creek group (Red Rock–Coalmine–Sonoita) should tentatively be treated as another MU.
- Boyce Thompson descends mainly from Monkey Spring, with possible Coscio Wash ancestry but none from Bylas Spring.
- Watson Wash descends from Boyce Thompson.
- The recolonized Santa Cruz population descends mainly from lower Sonoita Creek, possibly with some upper Santa Cruz (Sharp Spring) ancestry.
- The Yaqui topminnow samples from Tule Spring and North Pond are similar in allele frequencies and genetic variation.
- The Yaqui and Gila topminnows are highly divergent. Together with the mtDNA data and morphological and behavioural differences, this indicates they should be considered for ranking as **separate species**.

**6. Caveats**
The authors describe these results as preliminary. Conclusions may change with further analysis and with MHC data from the remaining samples.
