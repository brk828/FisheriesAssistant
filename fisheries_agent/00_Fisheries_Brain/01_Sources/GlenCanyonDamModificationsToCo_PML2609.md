# Glen Canyon Dam Modifications to Control Downstream Temperatures: Plan and Draft Environmental Assessment

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This January 1999 draft Environmental Assessment (EA) by the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]], Upper Colorado Region, evaluates modifying the intakes of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] so that warmer surface water can be released into the [[Colorado River]] below the dam. Cold, deep-intake releases (about 8–10 °C) are believed to limit reproduction of native, warmwater and endangered fishes, particularly the [[Humpback Chub]]. The [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] (FWS) recommended in its [[Biological Opinion]] that Reclamation evaluate and, if feasible, implement temperature controls.

The preferred "Action Alternative" uses a low-cost design that removes the top of the existing trashrack structure and installs a surface gate, at an estimated feasibility cost of $15 million. It would allow release temperatures of up to about 15 °C during summer, creating a tiered cold/warmwater fishery, under an [[Adaptive Management]] program monitored by the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring And Research Center]]. The [[No-Action Alternative]] continues existing cold releases. If warmwater releases prove harmful, cold releases remain available.

## Background & Description

Before [[Glen Canyon Dam]] was built (closed in the mid-1960s), the Colorado River through Grand Canyon warmed from near freezing in winter to about 26–30 °C in late summer. The pre-dam fish community was dominated by warmwater non-native species, with [[Channel Catfish]] comprising 90% of the fish community in Glen Canyon (Woodbury 1959, as cited). Reservoir filling and deep penstock intakes produced year-round releases of roughly 8–10 °C.

The native fish assemblage is considered highly unusual. Of eight native species, three (Colorado Pikeminnow, Bonytail, Roundtail Chub) have been extirpated from Glen and Grand Canyons; two (Humpback Chub, Razorback Sucker) are listed as endangered; Flannelmouth Sucker is a candidate species; Bluehead Sucker is common only in tributary inflows; and Speckled Dace is relatively common. Non-native species, including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Common Carp]], and [[Channel Catfish]], now dominate.

The temperature control design was developed by an interdisciplinary team through value planning. Eight design proposals were screened (Proposals 1, 2A, 2B, 3, 4, 5A, 5B). Proposal 2A was selected: it uses two fixed openings (cold and warm), with each of the eight penstocks individually set to select cold or warm water, and blending used to tune outflow temperature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Primary productivity.** The post-dam food base is built on [[Cladophora Glomerata]], a filamentous green alga made possible by clear water, with diatoms and invertebrates such as [[Gammarus Lacustris]] and midges (Chironomidae) forming the food chain. Pre-dam productivity was limited by turbidity. Haden et al. (1997) found that Gammarus survival was similar at 10 °C and 20 °C over 30 days, with slightly lower growth at 20 °C.

**Nutrients.** Lake Powell retains nearly 98% of total phosphorus entering it. Surface withdrawals during summer are modeled to raise detritus concentrations below the dam by about 300%, potentially increasing river productivity.

**Competition and predation.** Warmwater releases would likely increase carp and catfish populations. The GCMRC modeling suggests physical controls (harvesting) may be needed. [[Asian Tapeworm]] (Bothriocephalus acheilognathi) and [[Anchor Worm]] (Lernaea cyprinacea) are parasites of concern; warmer releases could increase their spread.

**Whirling disease.** Not yet observed below the dam, but severe infections in young-of-year rainbow trout begin near 9 °C, peak near 14 °C, and decline to near zero at 18.5 °C. Warm releases could increase severity if the disease becomes established.

**Other species.** Threadfin shad (Lake Powell forage fish) experience occasional winterkill when surface temperatures fall below about 10 °C. The [[Peregrine Falcon]], [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Bald Eagle]], [[Kanab Ambersnail]], [[Southwestern River Otter]], [[Osprey]], [[Belted Kingfisher]], and [[Northern Leopard Frog]] were also evaluated; most impacts were judged negligible or indirectly beneficial.

**Recreation and economics.** About 125,000 angler trips in 1991 and roughly 33,000 day rafting trips were recorded. Regional recreation spending was estimated at about $25.7 million (1995 dollars), supporting about 585 jobs. Hydropower impacts were estimated at an average monthly loss of about $37,000 (less than 1%), though physical model results suggest headloss up to 4 feet could increase this.

**Hydropower and air quality.** Glen Canyon Dam generates about 75% of total CRSP power. Some lost generation could be replaced by fossil-fuel plants, slightly increasing SO₂ and NOₓ emissions, though this is judged negligible compared with annual variation.

## Key Findings

- **Cold releases are the constraint.** Release temperatures of 8–10 °C are below the 16–22 °C range needed by native species for spawning, incubation and larval survival (Valdez). Reproduction of native fish in the mainstem is essentially absent; all documented spawning occurs in warm tributaries and springs.
- **Thermal shock and swimming impairment.** Valdez and Carothers (1998) concluded that most larval Flannelmouth Sucker, Bluehead Sucker, and Humpback Chub descending into the cold mainstem die from thermal shock or predation, and swimming ability may be reduced by as much as 98% by cold temperatures.
- **Proposed operation.** Release temperatures would ramp up to about 15 °C, be sustained through summer, and be blended with cold water to hold the cap. Warm releases could be as short as one month (to address thermal shock) or run May through September.
- **Selected design cost.** Proposal 2A is estimated at $15 million capital (Reclamation's Denver cost estimate), with annual operation and maintenance below $100,000. Alternatives such as the Navajo Powerplant option, minimum-pool operation, summer release minimization, dam removal, and reservoir destratification were eliminated.
- **Cost comparisons.** Proposal 4 (external frame) was estimated at $148.5 million, and Proposal 2B at $35 million. Proposal 1 (spillway release) was estimated at about $13.5 million per year in lost power revenues.
- **Operational limits.** Warm releases would only be possible when the reservoir is between 3,670 and 3,700 feet, which Reclamation projected about 85 of 100 years during May–October.
- **Expected benefits.** Warm releases should improve Rainbow Trout growth (the 8 °C releases sit at the bottom of the optimal range, and growth is limited by 8.25% per °C below 15 °C) and humpback chub spawning in the mainstem.
- **Risks.** Carp and catfish could increase in the mainstem; physical controls may be required; Lake Powell surface temperatures could cool by up to 1 °C with 140 days of warm releases.
- **Adaptive management.** Because of ecological complexity, no fixed operating plan can optimize goals. The plan relies on iterative experiments, annual revision, and reversibility to cold releases if warm releases fail to help endangered fish.
- **Schedule.** Reclamation planned a Finding of No Significant Impact, modification of intakes beginning fiscal year 2000, and temperature control possibly available by summer 2002. Comments were due March 26, 1999.
- **Historical analogues.** Flaming Gorge Dam (about 13.5 °C summer release) increased trout growth from about 45 mm to 150 mm per year; Hungry Horse Dam warm releases were expected to increase trout growth rates two to five times.
