# A Review of the [[Lingcod]] (*Ophiodon elongatus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ophiodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 review by J. B. Phillips of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] (Marine Resources Operations) assembles what was known about the life history of the [[Lingcod]] and the status of its fishery. The lingcod is prized as a food and sport fish, and local concern had developed that conservation measures for the fishery might be needed. The review covers catch records for California and the wider Pacific coast, sport (party-boat) catch, fishing gear, products, habitat, migration (tagging), diet, maturity, [[Spawning]], [[Fecundity]], [[Age and Growth]], and current [[Fishery Regulations]].

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** The lingcod is the only member of the family [[Ophiodontidae]]. It is closely related to the [[Scorpaenidae]] (rockfishes), [[Cottidae]] (sculpins), and [[Anoplopomatidae]] (sablefish), which are grouped in the order Scleroparei, or mail-cheeked fishes.
- **Range:** From northern Baja California to northwest Alaska. Moderately abundant in California ocean waters, but the area of greatest abundance lies north of California.
- **Appearance:** Color varies from nearly black or brown to green, with darker mottling and spotting on the back and sides. The upper sides and back are often spotted with orange or yellow.
- **Name origin:** Early British settlers in the Pacific Northwest saw a resemblance to the European ling (*Molva*) and added "cod," producing "lingcod." Other names include cultus cod, Pacific cultus, green cod, blue cod, white cod, ling, greenling codfish, leopard cod, buffalo cod, and bocalao.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and depth**
- Lives near the bottom, generally in and around rocky areas, reefs, and kelp beds, especially where there is strong tidal movement.
- Most abundant at depths of 60 fathoms or less. Occasionally caught in 100 fathoms off California and reported as deep as 200 fathoms off the [[Queen Charlotte Islands]], [[British Columbia]] (Wilby, 1937).

**Movement (tagging)**
- Canadian tagging from 1939 to 1954: 3,145 lingcod tagged and released on banks in the [[Strait of Georgia]] and off the west coast of [[Vancouver Island]], British Columbia.
- 419 tagged fish were recovered. About 9 percent had moved more than five miles from release areas. One movement of 80 miles was recorded. The significant movements were considered random.
- Immature fish (less than 22 inches total length) moved more widely, with some recovered as far as 25 miles from release areas.
- Large mature fish had more restricted movements, suggesting they settle into a "niche."
- A tagged male recovered after 12 years and 2 months at liberty was near the tagging locality, supporting the conclusion that lingcod lead a rather sedentary existence.

**Diet and predator-prey relationships**
- Very young lingcod in British Columbia feed on crustaceans such as shrimp until large enough to eat small fishes such as small herring.
- Adults eat nearly any live prey, including sand lances, herring, young lingcod, dogfish shark, gray cod, pollack, rockfish, crabs, shrimp, and squid.
- A 54-pound lingcod caught off Monterey, California, on January 27, 1938, contained a 12-inch starry rockfish (about 1¼ pounds) and an 18½-inch canary rockfish (about 3 pounds).
- Eelgrass, hydroids, and gravel have occasionally been found in stomachs, likely ingested with bottom prey.
- Lingcod are cannibalistic. Chatwin (1956b) observed reefs in British Columbia occupied entirely by large fish and suggested large fish may suppress recruitment of young fish to those reefs.

**Behavior**
- Spend considerable time resting on the bottom, watching for prey.

## Key Findings

### Catch and Fishery Status

- **California commercial catch:** Varied between 400,000 and 2,000,000 pounds annually since 1916, averaging just under one million pounds.
- In 1957, landings of 1.5 million pounds were the sixth highest in the 42-year period with available records.
- Landings are greatest in the northern part of the State and decline at southern ports. South of Point Conception (Los Angeles and San Diego regions), commercial landings are unimportant.
- **Pacific coast total:** Oregon, Washington, British Columbia, and Alaska landings varied between 6 and 18 million pounds since 1935, averaging about 9 million pounds. Annual totals by region are tabulated in Table 1 of the source.
- **California party-boat (sport) catch:**
  - Nearly 1 percent of the California party-boat catch by number from 1947 to 1957.
  - Decreased from about 26,000 in 1949 to 13,000 in 1953, then rose to an all-time high of 38,000 in 1957 (Table 2).
  - The highest sport catch occurs in the [[Monterey-Santa Cruz Region]]. Adjacent regions (San Francisco–Half Moon Bay to the north and San Simeon–Avila to the south) are next in importance.
  - Over the past 10 years, 60 percent of party-boat lingcod were taken from northern ports.
  - San Diego accounts for about 15 percent of the party-boat catch.
  - In northern California, the sport catch is small because anglers focus on salmon; about two-thirds of fish caught from northern California party boats are salmon.

### Gear and Products

- **Gear:** Handlines, trolling lines, otter trawls, and drag nets. Live bait on a handline is more effective than dead bait, though dead bait is accepted when jigged just above the bottom. Shallow-water trolling with bait is usually most effective, especially over rocky areas. Trolled salmon spoons and nickel-plated jigged lures are also used.
- Commercial handliners in deeper water use a 2- to 3-foot metal "spreader" bar with large hooks at each end, referred to as a "jig."
- **Products:** Highly esteemed as fresh fish, and the bulk of the commercial catch is marketed fresh; a small portion is frozen. Flesh may be greenish, which is harmless and disappears on cooking.
- **Insulin:** Lingcod are among the richest known fish sources of insulin, but a more dependable supply exists elsewhere, so insulin has not been commercially exploited.
- **Liver oil:** Extremely rich in vitamins A and D. Livers brought fishermen $2 per pound during 1943–1946. In 1947, the market deteriorated due to growing production of synthetic vitamin A.

### Life History

- **Stock relationships:** Preliminary comparisons of spawning season, fecundity, weight-length relationships, and size at maturity show close correspondence between California stocks and those to the north.
- **Maturity** (October–November 1957, Fort Bragg, California; 119 fish: 64 males, 55 females):
  - Males and females begin maturing at about 23 inches total length.
  - Practically all specimens were mature at about 25¾ inches.
  - Females are about three years old at 23 inches and four years old at about 25¾ inches.
- **Size limit context:** British Columbia's minimum commercial size (3 pounds dressed, head off) corresponds to about 23 inches total length, near the break between immature and mature fish.

### Spawning and Early Development

- **Season:** Mid-December to mid-March in Pacific Northwest waters, generally matching the California season. In some areas, spawners shift from deeper offshore waters to inshore subtidal reefs.
- **Reproduction:** Eggs are fertilized externally and are adhesive, sticking in large masses to rocky crevices. The male guards the eggs, repelling intruders until hatching.
- **Eggs:** Freshly laid eggs are 2.8 mm (about ⅛ inch) in diameter and pinkish. On contact with water, the outer coat swells to 3.5 mm. Eggs turn white, then dull yellow or greenish brown due to diatom accumulation. About six weeks are required for hatching.
- **Larvae:** About one-half inch long at hatching, with a yolk sac that disappears around day 10. Larvae are elongate, with large blue eyes, a large mouth, a bright green gall bladder, and a large yellow oil globule near the liver.
- **Postlarval stages:** Little is known. Young fingerlings of 3 to 5 inches are occasionally taken by seining in eelgrass areas during summer.

### Fecundity

- The largest females apparently lay at least 500,000 eggs per season.
- A British Columbia female measuring 41 inches and weighing 32 pounds had ovaries weighing 10 pounds 14 ounces (about one-third of body weight), containing a calculated 518,000 eggs.
- A Monterey, California, female measuring 45 inches had 436,000 eggs weighing 8.3 pounds, or 20 percent of total body weight. (The source OCR reads the body weight as "413 pounds," which appears to be a scan error for roughly 41.5 pounds, consistent with the 20 percent figure.)

### Age and Growth

- Scales and otoliths (earstones) did not yield reliable age readings.
- **Vertebrae** (cleaned by boiling and dried) showed dark growth marks interpreted as annual checks under bright light (Chatwin, 1954, 1956a).
- Validation: Vertebral readings were compared with monthly length-frequency samples of young fish from shallow water (beach seines, night dip nets, small-mesh trawls), with good agreement for O-ring and I-ring fish. A group of closely spaced rings in the vertebra center was identified as false checks.
- Larger fish were checked against growth increments of 234 tagged lingcod recovered over several years.
- **Growth curves** (Chatwin, 1954, 1956a): Males up to 14 years and females up to 16 years. Sexes differ in growth rate from about age three. Males grow more slowly and have a shorter lifespan than females.
- Table 4 gives average lengths at each age for male and female lingcod in British Columbia waters.
- **Maximum size:**
  - Males: about 36 inches total length and 22 pounds.
  - Females: Wilby (1937) cited a maximum of five feet and 70 pounds. Wilby also recorded a 52-inch, 54-pound specimen.

### Regulations

- No commercial regulations specific to lingcod exist in California, Oregon, Washington, or Alaska.
- **British Columbia:**
  - Minimum weight of 3 pounds for cleaned fish with head off, corresponding to a 3.6-year-old fish about 23 inches total length.
  - A three-month winter closure during the spawning season has been in effect in recent years.
  - Both measures originated as recommendations from the fishing industry, not from biological need.
- At the current rate of fishing, the minimum size limit appears appropriately placed relative to maximum yield. The effect of the spawning closure has not been measured because no reliable method exists.
- **California sport limit:** Anglers are limited to a bag of 10 lingcod of any size per day.
