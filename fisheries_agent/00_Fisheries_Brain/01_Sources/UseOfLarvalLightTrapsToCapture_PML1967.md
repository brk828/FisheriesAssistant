# Larval Light Traps for Capturing Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 methods paper, reprinted from *The Southwestern Naturalist* (vol. 38, no. 4), describes the design, testing and field use of a larval light trap built to monitor spawning success of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada. The species is federally endangered. Biologists from several agencies were rearing larvae in isolated backwaters with the goal of growing them to about 30 cm before reintroduction, a size thought to reduce predation by game fish. The trap was modified from a [[Killgore Light Trap]] design. Over 1992 field trials, 226 trap sets totaling 1,327 trap hours were completed at two spawning sites. Early trials with chemical light sticks and bait caught no larvae. Later trials with halogen bulbs and shorter sets were the final protocol. The authors state that the paper documents the first concerted attempt to collect larval catostomids with traps.

## Background & Description

**Species status.** The razorback sucker was once common throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Habitat degradation and predation by non-native fish have made it uncommon to rare. The only large relic population cited is in Lake Mohave.

**Rationale for trap development.** Razorback sucker larvae show a positive phototactic response. They had been collected with hand-held spotlights and dipnets (Marsh and Langhorst, 1988; Minckley et al., 1991; Bozek et al., 1991). Earlier light traps had proven ineffective for this species. Low catches were attributed to light intensity and to short battery life. Light traps had successfully captured other phototactic fishes, but catostomids had not been collected in appreciable numbers.

**Trap design.** The trap was based on [[Killgore Light Trap]] descriptions (Killgore, 1991) and built from 6 mm thick [[PVC]] material:
- Four 20 cm lengths of 10 cm diameter pipe were spaced 1 cm apart in a shamrock pattern and fixed to two flat disks 25 cm in diameter.
- Tops and bottoms were painted black to restrict illumination to the trap sides and entrances.
- Flotation was removed so traps could rest directly on the substrate.
- Entrances were enlarged to 10 mm.
- Power was supplied by extending electrical cable to 12-volt DC gel-cell batteries on shore, which solved the battery-life problem.

**Attractants tested.** Four attractants were compared:
- Dog food
- Cyalume chemical light sticks (11 lux)
- 12-watt halogen bulb (2,100 lux)
- 55-watt halogen bulb (4,500 lux)

Light intensity was measured 20 cm from the source with a Sperry Model SLM-110 light meter. Water clarity during the test was measured at NTU 1.7.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study sites:** Yuma Cove, described as isolated, and Arizona Bay. Both are known razorback sucker spawning sites in Lake Mohave.
- **Water transparency:** Excellent at both sites, with Secchi depths normally exceeding 4–5 m.
- **Sampling season:** 19 February to 30 April 1992.
- **Larval presence** at each site was confirmed by spotlight and dipnet collections before trap work.
- **Sample handling:** Traps were lifted carefully off the bottom and enclosed in a dipnet of 505 µm mesh to prevent escape. Traps were flushed with filtered water. Samples were examined on site with a portable light table. Larvae were counted and released unharmed, except reference specimens retained for identification.
- **Management context:** The work supports a multi-agency effort to rear suckers in isolated backwaters for later reintroduction into the reservoir.

## Key Findings

1. **Initial protocol failed.** During the first two weeks, only cyalume light sticks and dog food were used, at depths of 1, 2 and 3 m. Across 60 overnight trap sets, these attractants caught no razorback sucker larvae, even though larvae were being collected by spotlight and dipnet.
2. **Protocol revised.** On 4 March, 12-watt and 55-watt white lights were added. Sampling effort was also changed from overnight sets to shorter sets of 1 to 2 hours, and trap depth was limited to 1 m.
3. **Final protocol.** Two sets of four traps each were deployed, one in Yuma Cove and one in Arizona Bay. The traps tested dog food, cyalume sticks, and the 12-watt and 55-watt bulbs.
4. **Overall effort.** 226 trap sets were completed, totaling 1,327 trap hours:
   - Yuma Cove: 135 sets, 758 trap hours
   - Arizona Bay: 91 sets, 569 trap hours
   - The early cyalume and dog-food sets are included in these totals.
5. **Methodological conclusions.** The authors state that the paper documents the first concerted attempt to collect larval catostomids with traps. They recommend design modifications that may improve light-trap effectiveness for other species.
