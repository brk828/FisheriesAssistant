# Death Valley Pupfishes: Literature Review and Annotated Bibliography

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This literature review, prepared by Robert W. Clarkson and edited by Paul C. Marsh for [[Death Valley National Park]], supports development of a management plan for four endemic pupfish subspecies of the Death Valley system. The review screened 229 references, of which 66 were judged directly relevant to management and 163 ancillary. Each subspecies is treated for discovery and naming, distribution, habitat, research, and management actions. The review concludes that these fishes have low within-population genetic diversity and high among-population divergence, so they need management different from typical stream-based models. The main threats are groundwater pumping, nonnative species, and habitat drying, and the report recommends monitoring, protection of habitat, and contingency refuge planning.

## Background & Description

Death Valley pupfishes (*Cyprinodon* spp.) are endemic to the Death Valley system and occupy some of the most extreme aquatic habitats known for fishes. The four recognized subspecies are:

1. **Saratoga Springs pupfish**, *C. nevadensis nevadensis*
2. **Amargosa River pupfish**, *C. nevadensis amargosae*
3. **Salt Creek pupfish**, *C. salinus salinus*
4. **Cottonball Marsh pupfish**, *C. salinus milleri*

Most authors trace these taxa to an ancestral *Cyprinodon* in [[Lake Manly]], a Pleistocene lake. The review reports that isolation of the Amargosa and Salt Creek drainages occurred about 4,000 to 8,000 years ago, citing Hubbs and Miller (1948). Cottonball Marsh was disconnected from the Salt Creek drainage about 2,000 years ago, according to LaBounty and Deacon (1972) and Courtois (1980).

Key taxonomic and historical references include:
- Eigenmann and Eigenmann (1889), the original description of *C. nevadensis* from Saratoga Springs.
- Miller (1943), the original description of *C. salinus* from Salt Creek.
- LaBounty and Deacon (1972), the original description of *C. milleri* from Cottonball Marsh.
- Miller (1948), a comparative treatise on Death Valley pupfish morphology and habitats.
- Soltz and Naiman (1978), a comprehensive natural history monograph.
- Moyle (2002), a revised summary of inland California fishes.

The report also notes the [[Death Valley Zoogeographic Model]] (Meffe and Vrijenhoek 1988). Under this model, existing populations are remnants of larger interbreeding populations, and gene flow among them is limited.

**Search methods:** Searches used Google Scholar advanced search with terms for each subspecies and general Death Valley aquatic habitat. The team also reviewed Desert Fishes Council proceedings, the Minckley Database managed by Marsh & Associates, and citation indices.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Saratoga Springs (*C. n. nevadensis*)**
- Main spring pond is about 10 m in diameter and 1 to 2 m deep, with a soft bottom. Water is nearly constant at 28 to 29 °C.
- Overflow forms marshy ponds with a combined area of 10 to 15 acres, where temperatures are more variable.
- Fish avoid waters above 35 to 38 °C in the lower marshes. Deacon (1968) reported fish in 42 °C water in summer.
- Primary threats are groundwater pumping and nonnative species (Moyle et al. 1995).

**Amargosa River (*C. n. amargosae*)**
- Distribution covers the middle and lower Amargosa River, Amargosa Canyon, and spring outflows near Tecopa. A single specimen was collected at Badwater (Alley et al. 1971).
- Canyon water temperatures range from 10 to 38 °C seasonally. Salinity ranges from 14 to 39 ppt.
- Sympatric nonnative species include [[Western Mosquitofish]] and [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] in the canyon reach.
- Threats include habitat dewatering, nonnative species including [[Salt Cedar]], and unauthorized off-road use.

**Salt Creek (*C. s. salinus*)**
- Known only from Salt Creek and associated marshes in Inyo County. Perennial flow begins at McLean Springs.
- Discharge from McLean Springs is 2.8 × 10⁻³ to 5.5 × 10⁻³ cms (0.1 to 0.2 cfs).
- Water temperatures range from near freezing to above 40 °C, and salinities reach 24 ppt. The review calls it one of the harshest pupfish habitats in the system.
- The [[Furnace Creek]] spring system is the most likely water source (Jahren and Sanford 2002).

**Cottonball Marsh (*C. s. milleri*)**
- Restricted to Cottonball Marsh, the largest marsh on the Death Valley floor. Fish occur along roughly 3.3 km of habitat (Threloff 1997).
- Salinity ranges from 14 to 160 ppt, but fish are rarely found above 70 ppt (Sada and Deacon 1995).
- Water temperatures range from near freezing in winter to nearly 40 °C in summer.
- The marsh is remote, which limits physical disturbance and illegal introductions.

## Key Findings

**Life history and reproduction**
- All four taxa share a similar spawning pattern in spring-fed and riverine habitats. Males defend territories, and spawning produces one or two eggs per sequence, with no parental care.
- In riverine systems (Amargosa and Salt Creek), pupfish form schools and loose aggregations with little courtship. The review cites Soltz and Naiman (1978) in calling territorial breeding maladaptive in these ephemeral streams.
- Life spans vary. Saratoga Springs fish live about 8 to 9 months after reaching 15 mm, with a maximum of 15 to 18 months. Salt Creek fish usually live one year or less, though up to 2 to 3 years has been reported.

**Population data**
- Saratoga Springs main pond: 800 to 3,000 fish, smallest in March and largest in September (Deacon 1967, 1968; LaBounty 2003). Sada (2003) found populations and habitats changed little over 40 years.
- Amargosa headwater springs: 20 to 150 individuals (Soltz and Naiman 1978).
- Salt Creek: 0.4 fish/m² in winter to 527 fish/m² in summer (1993 data, Sada and Deacon 1994, 1995). Populations may reach millions in favorable high-water conditions (Miller 1943).
- Cottonball Marsh: 185 fish/m² in spring and 0.3 fish/m² in autumn (1993 data).

**Thermal tolerance**
- Upper thermal limits are approximately 21 °C above the acclimation temperature, but the limits depend on acclimation (Brown 1971; Brown and Feldmeth 1971).
- Amargosa egg production occurs only between 22 and 34 °C (Shrode and Gerking 1976, 1977).

**Genetics**
- Duvernell (1998) and Duvernell and Turner (1999) found no substantial subdivision among Amargosa populations, with one exception: a small spring population north of Tecopa.
- The Salt Creek subspecies completely lacks mtDNA variation, which suggests past bottleneck events. It nonetheless maintains multiple [[Major Histocompatibility Complex]] alleles at one locus (Fisher 2000).

**Threats**
- Groundwater pumping (Furnace Creek and Saratoga Springs).
- Nonnative fishes and crayfish. Paulson and Stockwell (2019) found that red swamp crayfish significantly reduced adult survival and reproduction of Amargosa pupfish in the lab. Hereford (2016) reported declining pupfish catch rates and rising crayfish catch rates in the canyon between 2010 and 2014.
- Flooding. Williams and Bolster (1989) documented Salt Creek mortality during a flash flood.
- Saltcedar encroachment, which Scoppettone et al. (2011) associate with more nonnative fish and crayfish.

**Management and protection**
- All four taxa are protected within Death Valley National Park. Most of the Amargosa canyon reach is managed by The Nature Conservancy and the Bureau of Land Management.
- Moyle et al. (1995) recommended annual monitoring, protection against dewatering, identification of temporary holding facilities, and federal listing of Salt Creek pupfish.
- Courtois (1980) and a National Park Service–California Fish and Game Memorandum of Understanding set management priorities for Cottonball Marsh, including water flow, habitat integrity, and nonnative fish prevention.
- Sada and Deacon (1994, 1995) recommended baseline studies and removal of nonnative fishes from Death Valley waters.

**Literature summary**
- Of 229 references, 66 were directly relevant and 163 ancillary.
- The annotated bibliography and "Additional References" list cover physiology, taxonomy, and genetics. Many entries were judged to have no direct management relevance.
- Several key sources could not be obtained electronically and were flagged for the National Park Service to acquire.
