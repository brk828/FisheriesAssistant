# Position Paper: Conserving Federally Threatened, Endangered, and Proposed Species while Enhancing Recreational Fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1994 interagency position paper (jointly drafted by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and [[National Marine Fisheries Service]]) addresses the tension between conserving federally threatened, endangered, and proposed ([[T/E/P Species]]) aquatic species and maintaining/enhancing recreational fisheries programs in the United States. Rather than presenting primary morphological or ecological field data on a single species, the document synthesizes national-scale conservation statistics and establishes policy principles for balancing [[Recreational Fisheries Management]] with [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) obligations. It emphasizes an ecosystem-based approach to fisheries management, recognizing that healthy [[Aquatic Ecosystems]] are prerequisite to both species recovery and sustainable recreational fishing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper frames aquatic species within a landscape-level ecosystem context rather than describing niche parameters for an individual taxon:

- **Habitat degradation** is identified as the primary driver of declines among indigenous fish species, extirpating [[Federal T/E/P Species]] from historically occupied ranges.
- **Nonindigenous fish species** have become naturalized in most U.S. aquatic ecosystems, sometimes displacing indigenous species and creating management conflicts between [[Indigenous Fish Species]] and [[Nonindigenous Fish Species]].
- Aquatic ecosystem health is described as directly reflective of surrounding landscape condition; ecosystem quality is stated to limit the capacity of habitats to "support or produce fish."
- The document links **drainage-basin-scale management** to compatibility between recreational fisheries and [[Federal T/E/P Species]] conservation, recommending ecological justification for introductions of new species or hybrids per the [[American Fisheries Society]] Position Statement on the Introduction of Aquatic Species.
- Recommends differentiated management emphasis: conservation/recovery of indigenous species in relatively unaltered habitats, versus continued reliance on nonindigenous species for recreational opportunities in significantly altered habitats.

## Key Findings

- **Economic scale of recreational fisheries**: In 1991, over 50 million U.S. anglers spent $24 billion on tackle, equipment, food, lodging, and related expenditures, generating over $2.1 billion in federal income tax revenue and supporting 1.3 million jobs.
- **Species decline statistics**:
  - In 1989, 364 species and subspecies of North American fish were identified as needing special management consideration (Williams et al., *Fisheries* 14(6): 2-20) — an increase of 139 taxa over the prior decade.
  - As of August 1994, 103 fish species and 56 clam/mussel species were listed as threatened or endangered under 50 CFR 17.11 & 17.12.
  - Approximately 20% of North American fishes, 36% of crayfishes, and 55% of freshwater mussels were ranked as extinct or imperiled (Sport Fishing and Boating Partnership Council, 1994).
- **Primary cause of decline**: Habitat loss and degradation are cited as the generally recognized primary causes of fish population declines, often precipitating shifts toward nonindigenous species management.
- **Legal/policy framework**: Identifies key statutes governing the balance between conservation and recreational use, including the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), the [[Fish and Wildlife Act of 1956]], the [[Federal Aid in Sport Fish Restoration Act of 1950]] (Dingell-Johnson Act), the [[Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act]], the [[Federal Water Project Recreation Act]], and the [[Reclamation Act]].
- **Governance structure**: States retain primary trustee authority over fish and wildlife resources unless preempted by federal authority; FWS and NMFS roles concentrate on federally permitted/licensed/funded water projects and ESA-listed species recovery.
- **Action framework**: Proposes national-level actions across six domains — laws/policy, partnerships, ecology and recreational fisheries, public outreach/education, funding, and mitigation — including establishment of an annual recreational fisheries summit and a national recreational fishing mitigation policy.
- **Funding concern**: Advocates for dedicated federal/state funding streams for T/E/P species conservation to prevent diversion of [[Sport Fish Restoration]] revenues away from their designated recreational fisheries purposes.
