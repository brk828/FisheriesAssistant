# Predator Removal Proposal for the Little Colorado River Confluence, Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae #salmonidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1997 proposal from the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] describes a field study to test whether removing large nonnative predatory and competitive fishes from the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), within [[Grand Canyon]] National Park, can reduce predator pressure on native fishes. Large nonnative fishes, notably [[Channel Catfish]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]], are common near the confluence with the [[Colorado River]] and may threaten endangered [[Humpback Chub]]. The project will combine snorkel transect counts before and after removal, five days of multi-gear removal in June 1997 (gill nets, setlines, spear gunning and angling), and stomach and parasite analyses of captured fish. A follow-up survey is planned for September 1997, with a final report with management recommendations due January 25, 1998.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River system through Grand Canyon historically held eight endemic fish species. Four of these have been extirpated from the region, including [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Habitat loss, blockage of migration routes, cold water releases from dam operations, and discharge fluctuations are cited as causes of decline. [[Humpback Chub]] is listed as endangered. [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] have declined in abundance but persist, largely by spawning in tributaries.

The proposal reviews evidence that nonnative fishes contribute to native fish declines. Predation wounds have been reported on Humpback Chub, but the impact of large nonnative fishes is not fully understood (Marsh and Douglas 1997). The authors note that managers appear to recognize the problem, yet no predator removal had been attempted in the LCR at the time.

Stated objectives for 1997 are to:

1. Evaluate gill nets, setlines, spear guns and angling for removing potential predators of native fishes.
2. Determine stomach contents of large nonnative predators.
3. Determine the incidence and infestation rates of parasites in large nonnative fishes.

The project team includes William R. Persons, Timothy L. Hoffnagle, Mark J. Brouder, David W. Speas (Arizona Game and Fish Department), Robert W. Clarkson ([[Bureau Of Reclamation]]) and Paul C. Marsh (Arizona State University). The document also includes resumes and publication lists for team members, which are not summarized here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Drainage and flow:** The LCR drains 141,155 km² of eastern and northern Arizona and western New Mexico, according to the proposal. The lower 21 km is perennial, fed by springs. The largest, [[Blue Spring]], supplies a nearly constant base flow of 6.3 m³/s.
- **Temperature:** Water at Blue Spring is about 20 °C. Water near the LCR mouth averages about 9 °C warmer than the regulated Colorado River (Kaeding and Zimmerman 1983).
- **Clarity:** The LCR is one of the few relatively unaltered tributaries in Grand Canyon with natural hydrographic dynamics favorable to native fishes. Sampling timing was chosen because the river is usually at base flow by early June, when water is clear and fish are easier to capture.
- **Study area:** The lower 1.2 km (1,200 m) of the LCR is divided into three areas:
  - Area 1: confluence to first riffle (~200 m upstream)
  - Area 2: first riffle to "Railroad Tie Rock" (~500 m upstream)
  - Area 3: "Railroad Tie Rock" to "Travertine Dam" (~1,100 m upstream)
- **Nonnative fishes present:** Channel Catfish, Common Carp, Rainbow Trout, Brown Trout, Black Bullhead and Yellow Bullhead.
- **Logistics:** Workers will camp at the "Boulder camp" about 2.2 km upstream. Helicopter access and carcass removal are provided. Spear gunning and angling are restricted to early morning or late afternoon to avoid visitors. The proposal estimates that several thousand pounds of fish may need helicopter removal.

## Key Findings

**Methods and sampling design**

- **Snorkel transect surveys:** Five snorkelers will float downstream in a line, counting fish, with counts before removal, immediately after removal, and in autumn. Two or three replicate counts per area are planned, with shoreline and main-channel zones counted separately. Underwater visibility will be measured as the maximum distance at which an adult-catfish-sized silhouette can be recognized. Lights will be tested in undercut areas.
- **Gill netting:** Six to eight multifilament nets (15 m × 1.8 m, 102 mm bar mesh) will be set on the bottom overnight. Nets will be checked every 2 hours on the first night to ensure native fish are not being captured. Catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) is measured as fish per 15 m of net per hour.
- **Setlines:** Three to five night sets, each with a 30 m mainline and 10 hooks spaced 3 m apart. Hooks (tuna circle hooks, 8/0 to 10/0) are sized to exclude Humpback Chub. CPUE is expressed as fish per twelve-hour set.
- **Spear gunning and angling:** Teams of two will spear Channel Catfish. Effort is recorded as hours spent hunting. Anglers will target trout at the confluence, with CPUE recorded as fish per hour.
- **Water quality:** Temperature will be logged near the confluence. Turbidity (NTU) will be measured three times daily. Discharge above base flow will be estimated from a nearby USGS gauging station (near Cameron, AZ).

**Fish data and laboratory analysis**

- All nonnative predatory and competitive fishes collected will be measured (total length, mm) and weighed (g).
- Channel Catfish, Rainbow Trout, Brown Trout, and both bullhead species taken by spear or angling will be sacrificed. Their digestive tracts will be preserved for stomach content analysis, with diet quantified by frequency of occurrence, percent composition by number, and weight.
- Gastrointestinal parasites will be identified and counted.

**Statistical analysis**

- The null hypothesis is that predator counts after removal equal counts before removal.
- Short-term effectiveness will be tested by comparing pre- and post-removal counts. Long-term recovery will be tested by comparing immediate post-removal counts with autumn counts.
- A two-way ANOVA without replication will be used. If variance among replicates differs significantly, a Friedman's nonparametric two-way ANOVA will be applied.

**Schedule and deliverables**

- **June 2–3, 1997:** Staff transported to camp; first snorkel surveys.
- **June 3–9, 1997:** Removal effort (the proposal describes a five-day removal period).
- **June 10, 1997:** Post-removal snorkel survey; camp broken down.
- **September 1, 1997:** Follow-up snorkel survey.
- **Interim trip report:** Due after field activities.
- **Final report with management recommendations:** Due January 25, 1998.

**Permits and funding**

- National Park Service and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service permits were in process at the time of writing.
- The Bureau of Reclamation will provide helicopter transport. Arizona Game and Fish Department (Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Studies) will cover labor, travel and supplies.
