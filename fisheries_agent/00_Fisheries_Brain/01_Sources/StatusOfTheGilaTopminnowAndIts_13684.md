# Status of the Gila Topminnow and its Co-occurrence with Mosquitofish

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #poeciliidae #endangered-species #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

This 1977 USDA Forest Service research paper examines the decline of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*) and its complex ecological relationship with the invasive [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*). Contrary to prior assumptions of rapid (within one year) competitive exclusion, the authors document two natural populations in southeastern Arizona (upper [[Santa Cruz River]] and [[Sonoita Creek]]) where the two poeciliids co-occurred for extended periods (7–13+ years), particularly in structurally complex habitats and near spring sources. Ultimately, mosquitofish displaces the native species — likely via direct predation — but Gila Topminnow persists longest in constant-temperature springhead habitats that mosquitofish rarely colonize. These spring refugia are identified as critical sources for potential future repopulation and conservation management.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy**: [[Gila Topminnow]] is a small livebearing fish in family [[Poeciliidae]], scientific name *Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*.
- **Historic distribution**: Formerly widespread throughout the [[Gila River]] system of Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora, Mexico; prior to 1940 considered the most abundant "low desert" fish in the region.
- **Habitat preference**: Shallow water (<20 cm), warm (generally >20°C), including constantly flowing springs, cienegas (marshes), and permanent/intermittent streams below 1,800 m elevation.
- **Ecological guild**: Along with pupfishes and juvenile native minnows/suckers, historically occupied habitats largely free of highly predaceous fishes.
- **Decline drivers**:
  - Habitat reduction/modification (arroyo cutting, vegetation change) over the past 70–80 years (Hastings 1959; Hastings and Turner 1966).
  - Introduction of non-native predatory fishes since ~1930, principally [[Mosquitofish]], and secondarily juvenile [[Sunfish]] and [[Largemouth Bass]].
- **Legal status**: Listed as endangered by the U.S. Department of Interior (1976).
- **Co-occurring native fauna** (historic upper Santa Cruz/Sonoita basins): [[Gila Chub]], [[Longfin Dace]], [[Gila Mountain-Sucker]], [[Gila Coarse-Scaled Sucker]], speckled dace, and a Monkey Spring [[Pupfish]] species.
- **Introduced fauna recorded in the system**: [[Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]], black and yellow bullhead catfishes, channel catfish, Yaqui catfish, carp, goldfish, and various salmonids.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study area**: Semi-desert oak-grassland streams in Pima and Santa Cruz Counties, Arizona — primarily the upper [[Santa Cruz River]] and [[Sonoita Creek]] drainages — representing essentially the entire present range of Gila Topminnow (plus two isolated populations at Bylas Springs and Cocio Wash).
- **Upper Santa Cruz River**:
  - Drainage area ~213 km², originates in Canelo Hills (~1,800 m elevation), flows south into Mexico.
  - Discharge (since 1949): ranges from 0 to ~140 m³/s, mean ~0.1 m³/s.
  - Channel incised (2–6 m deep) in silt-sand/organic deposits; remnant cienega habitats persist in wider channel sections and near spring seepages.
  - Aquatic vegetation: sago pondweed, water milfoil, naiad, stonewort, watercress, sedges/grasses.
- **Sonoita Creek**:
  - Drainage area ~541 km²; average discharge ~0.2 m³/s (range 0–~400 m³/s).
  - Erosive stream with shifting sand bottom, sparse aquatic vegetation (seasonal watercress), extensive cottonwood galleries.
  - [[Lake Patagonia]] impoundment (filled early 1970s) stabilized downstream flow, creating habitat favorable to still-water introduced fishes.
- **Mosquitofish invasion history**:
  - Rare/absent in Sonoita Creek drainage until ~1969, then spread rapidly.
  - First recorded co-occurring with Gila Topminnow in Arizona in 1926 (Gila River at Dome; Salt River near Tempe) and 1941 (Tonto Creek).
  - Between 1950–1977: resurveys of known Gila Topminnow localities found replacement by mosquitofish at 15 sites, desiccation/elimination of aquatic life at 7 sites, and persistence of the native species at only 7 isolated springs/stream segments.
- **Springhead refugia**: Gila Topminnow, *Gila* chubs, and occasionally speckled dace are the only native Arizona fishes that penetrate immediate spring outflows. Mosquitofish rarely occupies springhead habitats within its introduced Arizona range (unlike in native range, e.g., Texas/northern Mexico, where other *Gambusia* species occupy headsprings).
- **Segregation behavior**: In both the Santa Cruz River and Sonoita Creek, mosquitofish tended to occupy quiet water and dense vegetation, while Gila Topminnow favored open water, areas of moderate current, and structure (logs, debris).

## Key Findings

- **Rediscovery of co-occurrence**: Gila Topminnow was found coexisting with mosquitofish and [[Green Sunfish]] in April 1976 in the upper Santa Cruz River near Locheil — a site sampled five times from 1964–1974 without any topminnow records, highlighting the difficulty of confirming true local extinction.
- **Relative abundance shifts over time** (upper Santa Cruz River, site A):
  - April 1976: mosquitofish outnumbered Gila Topminnow ~100:1 (likely underestimated due to unseen female mosquitofish shoals).
  - September 1976: ratio improved to ~6 topminnow per 150 mosquitofish (~25:1), with all life stages of topminnow present.
  - March 1977: mosquitofish outnumbered topminnow 23:1 at site A, but only 3:1 at site D (further upstream).
- **Quantitative data (March 1977)** documents co-occurrence at multiple stations in the Santa Cruz River and Sonoita Creek, including [[Sheehy Spring]], where native fishes (Gila Chub and Gila Topminnow) dominated **above** a deep natural pool, while mosquitofish and far fewer chubs occurred **downstream** of it.
- **Springhead exclusion of mosquitofish**: At [[Cottonwood Spring]] (Sonoita Creek drainage), mosquitofish had direct water access for at least 2 years but did not penetrate the spring source population of Gila Topminnow — supporting the refugium hypothesis.
- **Timeframes of co-occurrence**:
  - Sonoita Creek: potential interaction ~7 years (since mosquitofish introduction/spread).
  - Upper Santa Cruz River: mosquitofish present for at least 13 years; Gila Topminnow presumed eliminated until rediscovered in 1976.
- **Mechanism of decline**: Direct predation by mosquitofish on adults and young of Gila Topminnow is the apparent ultimate cause of displacement (per Schoenherr 1974), consistent with reports of mosquitofish suppressing/eliminating other fish species elsewhere (Myers 1965).
- **Historical co-occurrence records**: A 1943 museum collection series documented Gila Topminnow at 11 localities, 5 of which also yielded mosquitofish, including artificial ponds/canals and larger streams (Gila River, San Pedro River, Sabino Creek, Potrero Creek) — indicating that co-occurrence in complex habitats is not a solely modern phenomenon.
- **Management implication**: Surviving topminnow populations concentrated in large springs largely avoided by mosquitofish serve as natural reservoirs for potential repopulation of adjacent habitats if conditions become favorable (e.g., after mosquitofish removal or habitat renovation).
- **Conservation recommendations**:
  1. Discover additional natural habitats suitable for establishing new populations.
  2. Renovate formerly occupied waters and reintroduce Gila Topminnow.
  3. Create quasi-natural refuge habitats.
  4. Maintain continuous surveillance, since replacement by non-native fishes can occur within a single season in some cases.
  5. Support educational and legal/enforcement measures to prevent unauthorized fish introductions.
