# Stream Channelization, Agricultural Drainage, and Natural Stream Oases

#freshwater-ecology #stream-ecology #aquatic-communities

## Executive Summary

This State of the Art article reviews how [[Stream Channelization]] and [[Agricultural Drainage]] alter the biotic communities of flowing waters in agricultural regions of the United States. The authors argue that channelization changes in-channel productivity and shifts faunal composition toward tolerant species, and that the effects extend downstream. They also propose that undisturbed reaches, termed [[Natural Stream Oases]] or [[Downstream Refugia]], can persist within degraded watersheds and may buffer adjacent areas. The article concludes that testing this idea requires baseline studies on small watersheds, followed by a large-scale pilot project on a major river, and that cooperation among resource disciplines is essential.

## Background & Description

Drainage development and channelization have been widespread. The article cites a figure of nearly 1.7 million acres drained in 1974 alone (U.S. Bureau of the Census 1974). Activity was greatest in the north central states (Minnesota, Illinois, Indiana, and Ohio) and the [[Lower Mississippi Valley]]. No formal census of channelization exists, so figures are incomplete. The [[Soil Conservation Service]] reported completion of about 196,934 miles of drainage field ditch and 388,810 miles of drainage main or lateral line. Reported channelized mileage in Minnesota, about 21,700 miles (Funk and Ruiz 1971), is considered questionable because the source of the state total is not reliable. The authors note that reliability of these figures is limited and their usefulness for impact assessment is doubtful.

The unit of management is the individual watershed, and the authors stress that each one functions as an independent unit. Channelization affects both small headwater streams and larger intermediate rivers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**In-channel effects.** Channelization reduces habitat complexity and shifts the biota. Channelized reaches show increased relative abundance of herbivores and grazers, reflecting a shift from allochthonous (terrestrial) inputs to in-channel primary productivity. Communities move from sensitive species to tolerant species that can survive the imposed environmental extremes.

**Downstream effects.** Literature on downstream impacts is mixed. Agricultural runoff can deliver [[Sedimentation and Turbidity]], [[Nutrient Loading]], pesticides, and other contaminants, and these are often assumed to degrade natural reaches. Some studies, however, found that downstream effects were minimal in at least two cases, and some natural reaches showed little impact. Natural streams appear to absorb moderate disturbance without losing structure or function, but channelized streams lack this capacity and can cross a "threshold level" beyond which recovery fails. Erosion control on the channel and land surface is therefore critical.

**Key parameters.** The authors identify four parameters that play primary roles in determining the biological integrity of lotic systems (the scan garbles some names). [[Habitat Structure]] is considered the most important, because it mitigates the effects of upstream channelization and drainage.

**Riparian and wetland effects.** [[Floodplain Wetlands]] and other riparian habitats are affected by drainage. A study cited as Barclay (1980) found that reducing large riparian forests to small forest islands had minimal impact on the life-form composition of the [[Avian Community]].

## Key Findings

- **Paradox of downstream effects:** Although much literature predicts severe impacts on natural reaches below intensively farmed watersheds, the authors' experience shows that healthy aquatic communities can persist in natural oases or refugia downstream from degraded habitats.
- **Buffering hypothesis:** Undisturbed areas downstream may exist as natural ecosystems and buffer farther-downstream areas. This hypothesis requires examination.
- **Habitat structure as key mitigator:** Natural reaches within agricultural drainage systems provide habitat structure that offsets the effects of upstream channelization.
- **Need for baseline research:** Further work on small watersheds is needed across varied geographic, topographic, and climatic regions where agricultural development is expanding. A larger sample size is required to identify the mitigating factors.
- **Large-scale pilot project proposed:** A major river that has not been altered but drains an agricultural region should be studied to test whether natural areas mitigate upstream drainage effects. The authors suggest expanding the oasis or refugia concept to all tributary systems to evaluate potential for [[Watershed Management]] and rehabilitation.
- **Cooperation among disciplines:** Success depends on collaboration among aquatic ecology, wildlife biology, hydrology, forestry, soil science, agricultural engineering, the [[Soil Conservation Service]], academic researchers, and local interests with shared understanding of needs and goals.
- **Policy shift:** The authors note that pollution control from the late 1960s through the 1970s focused on [[Point-Source Abatement]]. They call for similar effort in the 1980s toward [[Nonpoint Source Pollution]] control, including establishing natural stream reaches as oases within agricultural watersheds.
- **Multiple benefits:** Preserved natural stream reaches provide research baselines, aesthetic and recreational value for local populations, philosophical or non-monetary value, watershed stabilization for farmers, and improved biotic integrity for both small streams and larger receiving waters.
- **Compatibility of goals:** The authors argue that agricultural economic goals and food supply needs are not inherently incompatible with maintaining high-quality natural stream ecosystems.
