# Threatened Native Wildlife in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #threatened-species #arizona-wildlife

## Executive Summary

This 1982 document is the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]]'s list of threatened native wildlife, approved by the Arizona Game and Fish Commission on 10 December 1982. It was first developed in 1975 and revised in 1978. The list covers 93 species and subspecies, grouped by the degree of threat and the probability of [[Extirpation]] from Arizona. Its main message is that habitat destruction is the most pervasive cause of decline, and that recovery depends on protecting and restoring the natural habitats these animals occupy. Although the document is a status list rather than a research study, it contains the group definitions, the species accounts, and a summary count table.

## Background & Description

The list identifies animals that are generally characterized by two features:

1. Small populations in Arizona, often substantially reduced from historic levels.
2. Occurrence in threatened habitats.

Specimen collecting and legal bag limits are handled separately through hunting and fishing regulations. The document uses four groups:

- **Group 1:** Species or subspecies extirpated from Arizona since 1900 but not extinct elsewhere. Some are extinct throughout their range, such as the Monkey Springs Pupfish (*Cyprinodon* sp.).
- **Group 2:** Species whose continued presence in Arizona is in jeopardy because of substantial population declines. Extirpation from the state is highly probable without recovery efforts.
- **Group 3:** Species whose continued presence could be in jeopardy in the foreseeable future. Serious threats exist to their habitats, and populations have either declined or are limited to few individuals in few locations. The document notes that most native fish fall here.
- **Group 4:** Species facing a moderate threat to their habitats. With greater threat or documented declines, they would become candidates for Group 3.

Some Group 1 animals may already be extirpated from Arizona while still existing elsewhere. Species marked with an asterisk are on the [[Federal Endangered Species List]] or the federal threatened list.

The list is updated as new information arrives. Records and information on status should be sent to the Endangered Species Coordinator at the Phoenix office of the Department.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary threat:** Habitat destruction and alteration, including stream modification, river channelization, dams, groundwater depletion, grazing, woodcutting, and urban and agricultural development. The document calls it the most pervasive cause of decline.
- **Biological threats:** Competition with, predation by, and hybridization with [[Nonnative Fish Introduction]] (for example, introduced sunfish, mosquitofish, and nonnative trout), and introduced bullfrogs, which threaten some amphibians.
- **Other threats:** Collecting for pets (Desert Tortoise), prairie dog control programs (Black-footed Ferret), pesticide effects on birds of prey, and conflicts with livestock or humans that caused the loss of large carnivores.
- **Recovery:** Captive breeding and reintroduction are underway or planned for several species, often using stock from Mexico or hatcheries. As habitats recover, populations are expected to recover and species may be removed from the list.
- **Key localities named:** The [[Colorado River]] system, [[Little Colorado River]], [[Virgin River]], [[Gila River]], the San Bernardino Valley, and [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]].

## Key Findings

### Fish

- **Group 1 (3 entries):** [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (extirpated from the Colorado River system, with captive-bred populations for reintroduction); Yaqui Catfish; Desert Pupfish. The Yaqui Catfish and Desert Pupfish entries describe reintroduction from Mexico or captive stock once habitat is restored.
- **Group 2 (about 11 entries):** Includes [[Gila Trout]] (occurs in only one Arizona locality), [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Yaqui Chub]] (only one native population, a few individuals in a spring on San Bernardino NWR), [[Woundfin]] (restricted to the Virgin River), and the Yaqui Shiner, Yaqui Sucker, Mexican Stoneroller, and Topminnow. The Yaqui Shiner was last seen in 1970.
- **Group 3 (about 8 entries):** Includes Apache Trout (threatened mainly by hybridization with nonnative trout), Colorado River Roundtail Chub (two subspecies), Gila Chub, Sonoran Chub, Little Colorado River Spinedace, Quitobaquito Pupfish, and [[Gila Topminnow]]. The Gila Topminnow was reduced to fewer than 10 sites and was reintroduced to more than 70 sites.
- **Group 4 (3 entries):** [[Spikedace]], [[Loach Minnow]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Spikedace and Loach Minnow are now known from very few localities, with a large population of Loach Minnow known from Aravaipa Creek. Razorback Sucker survives in small populations in Lake Mohave and Lake Mead, and has been reintroduced to the Gila, Verde, and Salt rivers.

### Amphibians and Reptiles

- **Group 1:** Huachuca Tiger Salamander, possibly extirpated after genetic swamping by introduced bait-stock salamanders; Tarahumara Frog, nearly extirpated with the last known individuals south of the Santa Rita Mountains.
- **Group 3:** Relict Leopard Frog (threatened by river modification), Yellow Mud Turtle (threatened by proposed introduction of game fish), [[Desert Tortoise]] (population believed to be declining; threatened by collecting and habitat loss), Flat-tailed Horned Lizard, Narrow-headed Gartersnake, Mexican Gartersnake, Mountain Skink, Arizona Gilbert's Skink, Sonoran Green Toad, Burrowing Toad, Lowland Leopard Frog, Narrow-mouthed Toad, Western Tree Frog, Arizona Ridge-nosed Rattlesnake, and others.
- **Group 4:** The document lists species such as the [[Desert Massasauga]] (known from only four areas in the southeastern corner of the state, with agricultural development as the major threat) as moderate-threat candidates.

### Birds

- **Group 1:** [[Aplomado Falcon]] (last recorded in the 1890s; recent visits are transient and infrequent).
- **Group 2 (examples):** [[Southern Bald Eagle]] (fewer than 15 nests known in Arizona), Gray Hawk, and Masked Bobwhite (extirpated in the early 1900s and reintroduced to Altar Valley in the 1970s, with a small, tenuous population).
- **Group 3 (about 15 entries):** Includes [[Peregrine Falcon]] (fewer than 100 pairs; threatened by eggshell thinning, now decreasing), Osprey, Mississippi Kite, Common Black-Hawk, Yuma Clapper Rail, Tropical Kingbird (fewer than 15 pairs breed in Arizona), Thick-billed Kingbird, Five-striped Sparrow, and Bairds Sparrow.
- **Group 4:** Great Egret, Snowy Egret, Black-crowned Night-Heron, Spotted Owl, Violet-crowned Hummingbird, Elegant Trogon, Black-capped Gnatcatcher, Sprague's Pipit, and others.

### Mammals

- **Group 1:** [[Grizzly Bear]] (last known record in the early 20th century), [[Jaguar]] (a few scattered recent records, never common in Arizona), and Gray Wolf (considered extinct in the state). [[Black-footed Ferret]] is also listed here, with scattered recent but unverified reports.
- **Group 2:** Otter (Colorado and Gila River tributaries; unconfirmed sightings persisted into the 1970s).
- **Group 3:** Western Jumping Mouse, [[Sonoran Pronghorn]] (a small population persists in the Sonoran Desert; historic habitat loss is the main threat), and [[Desert Bighorn Sheep]] (two subspecies, threatened by disease, competition with livestock, and urban encroachment).
- **Group 4:** [[Mount Graham Red Squirrel]] (known from few specimens; threatened by habitat destruction from recreation development, timber harvest, and woodcutting).

### Overall Count

The document's summary table lists totals by group and taxon, but OCR damage makes the individual cell values unreliable. The document's own stated total is 93 species and subspecies.
