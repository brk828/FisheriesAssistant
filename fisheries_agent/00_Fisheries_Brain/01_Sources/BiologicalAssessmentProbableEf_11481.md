# Biological Assessment: Hoover Dam Powerplant Modification Project

#ichthyology #endangered-species #native-fish #Colorado-River #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #hydroelectric-impacts

## Executive Summary

This biological assessment evaluates probable effects of the proposed [[Hoover Dam]] powerplant modification project on five federally listed or candidate species under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The Bureau of Reclamation proposes adding 500 MW of generating capacity through construction of a subsurface powerhouse, increasing total plant capacity from 1,800 MW to 2,300 MW and maximum releases to 62,000 cubic feet per second. Species assessed include the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*, proposed for listing), [[Bald Eagle]] (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*), [[Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus*), and [[Desert Tortoise]] (*Gopherus agassizii*). The assessment concludes that project implementation would have no significant adverse effects on these species, primarily because limiting factors (especially predation by exotic species for native fish) are independent of flow regime modifications.

## Background & Description

### Project Context

[[Hoover Dam]] was constructed 1931-1935 pursuant to the Boulder Canyon Project Act of 1928 to provide flood control, water storage, and hydroelectric power generation. The dam created [[Lake Mead]], the nation's largest manmade lake, and operates one of the world's largest hydroelectric powerplants with 17 generating units. A routine uprating program is replacing worn components, increasing capacity from 1,340 MW to approximately 1,800 MW by the early 1990s due to improved technology and materials.

### Proposed Modification

The project involves constructing a subsurface powerhouse approximately 50 feet downstream from the existing Arizona powerhouse, containing two 250-MW generators. This would add 500 MW to the uprated capacity for a total of 2,300 MW at rated head of 490 feet. Maximum releases during peak generation would reach 62,000 cubic feet per second.

### Species Status

**Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*): Once abundant throughout the mainstream [[Colorado River]] and principal tributaries, bonytails were still common in [[Lake Mead]] after Hoover Dam closure (Moffet 1943) but considered rare by 1950 (Wallis 1951). Presently believed extirpated from Glen Canyon Dam to Hoover Dam. Small populations persist in the Upper Colorado River Basin and [[Lake Mohave]] in the lower basin, with the Lake Mohave population near extinction. Hatchery stocking attempts have had mixed results.

**Razorback Sucker** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Historically abundant throughout 3,500 miles of Colorado River basin, supporting a significant commercial fishery in southern Arizona in the early 1900s. Distribution now reduced to approximately 750 miles in upper basin and 200 miles in lower basin. An estimated 65,000 adult razorback suckers occur in [[Lake Mohave]] (Minckley 1983), with 758-1,138 in the upper Green River (Lanigan and Tyus 1989). No significant recruitment documented in recent years despite spawning activity.

**Bald Eagle** (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*): Winter resident associated with unfrozen aquatic habitats. The 1990 National Wildlife Federation wintering count documented minimum of 14 bald eagles for [[Lake Mead]] area, with 14-24 eagles sighted annually since 1979. No known roost sites within project area and no breeding records for Lake Mead/Lake Mohave area. No sightings between May and late September. Current breeding population concentrated in central Arizona with potential for expansion to Lake Mead and Lake Mohave.

**Peregrine Falcon** (*Falco peregrinus*): Historically common in Arizona but declined during 1950s to only three active nests by 1975 (Ellis 1976). Recent increase in sightings and active nest locations in southwest. Ongoing study from Fortification Hill to 4 miles below Willow Beach identified five active nest sites, with nearest approximately 1.5 miles north of proposed project site.

**Desert Tortoise** (*Gopherus agassizii*): Mohave population listed as endangered; Sonoran population (south and east of Colorado River) remains candidate species. Extremely long-lived reptile with nonuniform distribution throughout Mohave and Sonoran Deserts. Found mainly in mountainous, rocky desert terrain in southern and central Arizona; elsewhere in flatlands, bajadas, and washes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Native Fish Ecology

**Bonytail Chub**: Morphological characteristics represent adaptations for survival in turbid, swift flows of pre-dam Colorado River (Vanicek 1967). Prefer eddy and pool habitats in canyons with shifting sand substrate, water depths 3-4 feet, and relatively constant, moderately swift current (Behnke and Benson 1980). Feed mainly on surface for terrestrial insects, plant debris, and algae. In [[Lake Mohave]], bonytails found primarily between mid-lake and Davis Dam, with frequent angler captures near Katherine's Landing and Davis Dam during summer. Recent May netting surveys located adults using shallow rock points and shoals along Cottonwood Basin.

**Razorback Sucker**: Adult razorback suckers migrate considerable distances to spawn (Tyus 1987). Lower basin spawning occurs late January through April (Ulmer 1980, Langhorst and Marsh 1986, Mueller 1989) at water temperatures 52.7°-64.4°F. Spawning accomplished with several males accompanying single female over gravel bars swept free of silt by currents. In [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Senator Wash Reservoir]], spawning occurs on gravel bars cleaned by wave action. Eggs settle into gravel interstices; larvae remain in gravel until swim-up.

Multiple investigations collected viable fertilized eggs and larvae in spawning areas, but few collected larvae larger than 14 mm in wild, indicating little or no successful recruitment (Tyus 1987). Marsh and Langhorst (1988) recovered larvae up to 20 mm total length in isolated [[Lake Mohave]] backwater where predators had been eradicated, but fish disappeared within one month following predator reinvasion.

Food habits: Larvae from reservoirs selected *Bosmina* spp. (Cladocera) and avoided Copepoda; backwater larvae selected *Bosmina* and avoided Rotifera (Marsh and Langhorst 1988). Adults consume benthic fauna and flora and inorganic materials (Marsh 1987). In [[Lake Mead]], algae most common food item, followed by plankton, insects, and decaying organic matter (Jonez and Sumner 1954). Chironomids most prominent in Lake Mohave specimens.

### Predation as Limiting Factor

Concentrations of exotic species reported in razorback sucker spawning areas include [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*) (Jonez and Sumner 1954, Marsh and Langhorst 1988, Ulmer 1980, Bozek et al. 1984). Larvae and larger razorback suckers found in stomachs of green sunfish, warmouth (*Lepomis gulosus*), channel catfish, flathead catfish (*Pylodictis olivaris*), and threadfin shad (*Dorosoma petenense*).

Primary cause for lack of young razorback suckers throughout basin for last 30 years attributed to predation from introduced species. Evidence indicates remaining razorback population composed primarily of old individuals slowly dying off.

### Reintroduction Efforts

FWS and Arizona Game and Fish Department conducted 10-year cooperative effort to reintroduce young razorback suckers into historic habitats. Approximately 9.5 million small fish introduced in Verde, Salt, and Colorado Rivers. Despite these efforts, very few razorback suckers believed to have reached adulthood. Monitoring showed predation by ictalurid catfishes primary deterrent to reestablishment (Marsh and Langhorst 1988).

**1985-1986 Yuma Cove Experiment**: Biologists placed sexually active razorback suckers in naturally isolated portion of [[Yuma Cove]] after renovating backwater to remove all fish. Experiment proved successful—adults spawned and swim-up fry grew to 22 mm (more than 50% greater than maximum sizes in unprotected lake). However, rising reservoir levels in May 1986 reclaimed backwater and young razorback suckers disappeared within one month following incursion of non-native fishes. This demonstrated that existing habitat conditions in Lake Mohave support razorback sucker spawning and growth, with predation as primary limiting factor.

### Raptor Ecology

**Bald Eagle**: Wintering eagles associated with aquatic habitats. Distribution dependent on prey density, suitable perch and roost sites, weather conditions, and freedom from human disturbance (Ohmart and Sell 1980). Opportunistic feeders consuming fish (especially [[Carp]] and [[Channel Catfish]]), waterfowl, carrion, and small mammals. Fish primary prey during breeding season (Rubrink and Podborny 1976). Perch sites usually in trees or cliffs near open water with wide flight clearances. Night roosts located near food sources and protected from adverse weather.

**Peregrine Falcon**: Potential habitat composed of cliffs adjacent to productive hunting habitat associated with wetlands, rivers, and lakes. Eyries typically located within cliffs overlooking preferred habitat (Call 1978). Diet consists mainly of birds including waterfowl, shorebirds, songbirds, and gallinaceous birds. Nesting peregrines can be very sensitive to human disturbance, generally locating nests far from human populations (Porter and White 1973), though birds have nested on skyscrapers and bridges in large cities.

### Desert Tortoise Ecology

Soil composition and texture key habitat features for cover and egg-laying requirements. Burrows constructed in soils ranging from sandy-loam to light gravel-clay. Food items include annual forbs, grasses, cacti, and shrubs; diet highly variable and determined by plant availability. Sensitive to livestock grazing and off-road vehicle use, which have contributed to decline throughout habitat range.

## Key Findings

### Impact Assessment by Species

**Bald Eagle**: Project implementation would have no foreseeable impact. Would not result in direct mortality or displacement; possible perch sites, nest sites, or foraging areas would neither be created nor destroyed. Only conceivable impact to prey would be fish in first 12 miles below Hoover. Impacts to fish in river, especially carp and catfish, expected to be minor to nonexistent. Therefore, no impacts expected to wintering bald eagles.

**Peregrine Falcon**: Implementation not expected to adversely affect availability of nest or perch sites, nor reduce food source. Increased human activity during construction could disturb individual falcons using specific site. Potential disturbance not thought significant due to small area involved and low density of peregrine falcons.

**Desert Tortoise**: No change in present status expected from implementing proposed action. Habitat immediately adjacent to river's edge from Hoover to downstream of Willow Beach very marginal at best. Terrain mostly consists of steep canyon walls, especially near Hoover where greatest water fluctuation (approximately 16 feet) will occur. Potential requirement of 5 surface-acres of desert wash habitat for materials borrow site. Specific site not yet identified; environmental clearances including desert tortoise surveys will be required once site identified.

**Bonytail Chub**: Fish surveys suggest bonytails rare if not absent in cold water (54°F) areas of [[Black Canyon]]. If present, cold water coupled with unsuitable habitat would make reproduction and viable population impossible. Changes from implementing proposed project would not adversely impact further existence of bonytail chub.

**Razorback Sucker**: Although razorback suckers spawn in [[Lake Mohave]] producing swim-up fry, no documented natural recruitment to adult population. Primary cause for lack of young razorback suckers throughout basin for last 30 years attributed to predation from introduced species (carp, green sunfish, channel catfish, largemouth bass). Considerable evidence that remaining razorback population composed primarily of old individuals slowly dying off.

Natural recruitment primarily hampered by predation. Increased reservoir elevation fluctuations may possibly reduce number of razorback suckers hatched; however, since no evidence of successful recruitment to adult population exists, only impact would be to predators feeding on razorback sucker larvae. Project could only be viewed as detrimental to sucker recruitment if Lake Mohave's fishery could be reverted to all native species and exotic predation factor eliminated. Removal of reservoir's current sportfishery would not be desirable nor technically feasible.

Increased fluctuation of releases to Lake Mohave would in no way change or impair current status of razorback sucker. Razorback suckers successfully spawning, eggs hatch producing swim-up larvae, but larvae apparently never mature due to tremendous predation pressure from exotic fishes. Similar conditions reported for [[Senator Wash Reservoir]], California, where adult razorback suckers spawn in wave wash zone producing larvae despite daily elevation fluctuations of nearly 8 feet—significantly greater than conditions expected from project.

### Conservation Initiatives

Bureau of Reclamation recently organized effort to examine if backwater facilities could be established for propagation and rearing of razorback suckers and other native fish in [[Lake Mohave]]. This action appears most feasible way of securing and maintaining continued existence of these species in lower Colorado River. Currently, Reclamation working with National Park Service, Arizona Game and Fish Department, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and Nevada Department of Wildlife to initiate this recovery concept.

### Consultation History

Coordination with U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service under Endangered Species Act originally completed for proposed Hoover Dam Modification Project in April 1981, subsequently updated April 1, 1982. Although no action taken since, renewed interest surfaced and FWS reconsulted January 1990. FWS responded with updated endangered species list February 1, 1990, identifying five species either listed as endangered or candidate for listing that may occur within proposed project area.

### Study Methods

Information obtained from literature and field survey information. Information from 1983 Draft Environmental Impact Statement and 1983 Endangered Species Assessment also used. Local agencies, organizations, and individuals contacted to obtain information on present status and recent sightings of listed species in project area.
