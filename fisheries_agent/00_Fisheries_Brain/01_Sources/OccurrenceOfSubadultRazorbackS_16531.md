# Occurrence of Subadult Razorback Sucker in the Green and Yampa Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This manuscript (Ms. no. 95-130 in peer review at the [[Great Basin Naturalist]]) by [[Timothy Modde]] of the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) reports the occurrence of subadult [[Razorback Sucker]] in the [[Green River]] and [[Yampa River]] and examines whether wild fish are surviving beyond their first growing season. Nine subadult fish were collected in 1994 from ponds in [[Leota Bottoms]] on the [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] (ONWR), with an additional fish taken by angling in a Yampa River backwater in 1993. Scale-annulus analysis indicated that the Leota fish were second-year fish spawned in 1993, and the Yampa fish was entering its third growing season. The author concludes that some limited recruitment is probably occurring, while noting that the population remains in decline under the current hydrologic regime.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) once ranged throughout the major tributaries of the [[Colorado River Basin]]. By the early 1990s it was commonly found only in [[Lake Mohave]] and the middle Green River, and both populations were described as remnants with little or no demonstrated recruitment (Minckley et al. 1991). The middle Green River population is the largest occupying native riverine habitat (Lanigan and Tyus 1989). Decline has been linked to construction of mainstem reservoirs, and in the Upper Colorado River Basin it is associated with the absence of survival in early life stages (McAda and Wydoski 1980; Tyus 1987; Tyus and Karp 1990).

The author notes that although spawning occurs successfully in the Upper Basin, recruitment had not been documented. Survival of young fish is thought to depend most on suitable [[Nursery Habitat]] (such as [[Floodplain Wetlands]]) and on limiting [[Nonnative Fishes]] that prey on or compete with young razorback sucker (Meffe 1985; Marsh and Langhorst 1988; Papoulias and Minckley 1990). Larvae drift downstream from riverine spawning sites, but the riverine habitats used by larvae and juveniles were poorly documented.

Prior juvenile records include:
- Tyus et al. (1965): eight juveniles (90 to 115 mm total length) from backwaters of the Colorado River near Moab, Utah, in the early 1960s.
- Gutermuth et al. (1995): two juveniles (37 and 29 mm) from a lower Green River backwater in 1991.
- Utah Division of Wildlife Resources staff: two juveniles (59 mm and 29 mm) from Green River backwaters in 1993 (Robert Muth, Colorado State University, personal communication).

Valdez and Wick (1983) argued that backwaters and embayments are important nursery habitats because they have low water velocity, warmer temperatures, and more food organisms than the adjacent river. The present paper is presented as the first report of wild razorback sucker surviving beyond their first growing season in the Upper Colorado River Basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** The study area comprised the Green River from the mouth of the Duchesne River (river kilometer, RK, 399.4) to its confluence with the Yampa River (RK 555), plus the lower 74 km of the Yampa River. Flows reflect a relatively natural Yampa hydrograph combined with regulated Green River flows from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]], which has reduced both the variation and magnitude of the annual hydrograph and narrowed the historic channel (Andrews 1986).

**Leota Bottoms.** Located about 130 river kilometers below the Yampa confluence, Leota Bottoms is the largest of six wetlands on ONWR, covering approximately 650 hectares. It is divided into 10 ponds managed for waterfowl since ONWR was established in 1962. River water reaches the wetland only through gravity canals during high spring flows or by pumping at lower flows, and it does not directly access the ponds during extreme floods (greater than 710 cms, as in 1983 and 1984). Water moves sequentially from lower- to higher-numbered ponds, and a raised culvert prevents exchange between Ponds 2 and 4, so aquatic organisms cannot pass between them. During sampling, water in Ponds 3 and 4 was confined to borrow areas with maximum depths under 1.2 m.

**Hydrology.** Green River discharge in 1993 at the Jensen U.S.G.S. gauge exceeded 595 cms (40% exceedance flow). High flows kept all ONWR wetlands full through the fall of 1993. Winter 1992–93 snowfall was 111% of normal in the Uintah Basin. Field notes from February 14, 1993 recorded 0.6 m of ice over Leota Pond 4 and only 0.25 m of water, with about 0.23 m of snow on top, suggesting possible [[Winterkill]] conditions.

**Historical sampling.** The area was sampled extensively between 1979 and 1994 by the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] using electrofishing boats, trammel nets, angling, and seining. Efforts targeted the four endangered fishes of the upper basin: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), razorback sucker, [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*).

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Leota Pond 3 was sampled about monthly from June 9 to September 14, 1994, with two overnight gillnets per effort.
- Leota Pond 4 was sampled October 14, 1994 by electrofishing and November 2, 1994 with trammel nets during draining, to recover yearling razorback suckers stocked in 1991.
- Scales were removed from nine of ten fish. Age was determined from annulus formation, and back-calculated growth was estimated by direct proportion (Weatherley and Gill 1987).

**Collections in 1994**
- Nine subadult razorback suckers were collected from Leota Bottoms, ranging from 235 to 366 mm total length.
- One 240 mm fish was taken from Leota 3 on June 9, 1994 (no scales taken).
- Two fish were captured and scaled from Leota 3 on June 28, 1994.
- No razorback suckers were collected from Leota 3 from July through September.
- Four fish came from the borrow area of Leota 4 on October 14, 1994, and one more was taken there by trammel net on November 2, 1994.
- None of the fish stocked in 1991 were collected; the author determined this from size and estimated age.
- In October 1992, 18 fish stocked in 1991 were captured in Leota 4, ranging from 336 to 411 mm.

**Age and growth**
- The Leota fish were in their second growing season and were therefore spawned in 1993.
- Age II fish averaged 324 mm at capture, with first-year growth averaging 172 mm and second-year growth averaging 153 mm.
- Growth was greatest in spring, based on back-calculation.
- Fish collected June 28 and October 14, 1994 showed little growth difference, though they came from different ponds.
- Leota 4 yearlings grew similarly to fish in Leota 3, which had no physical connection to Leota 4.

**Yampa River record**
- A 389 mm razorback sucker was angled from a Yampa River backwater at RK on June 9, 1993.
- Scale analysis indicated it was just entering its third growing season, which places its spawning in 1992.

**Recruitment interpretation**
- The author argues that the appearance of subadults in the Yampa and middle Green rivers indicates some limited recruitment is probably occurring.
- Hundreds of razorback sucker larvae were collected in the middle Green River in 1993 and 1994 by Colorado State University, the U.S. Park Service, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Robert Muth, personal communication). Tyus (1987) also reported larvae below a known spawning site at RK 499.
- Despite these larval and juvenile records, no reports of wild razorback sucker recruiting beyond age 0 had been published before this study.
- Razorback suckers spawn on the ascending limb of the hydrograph, so drifting larvae are dispersed during peak runoff and can reach wetlands (Tyus and Karp 1990). Documented use of wetlands by immature razorback suckers in the basin had not been previously observed.

**Stocking failure**
- Despite 1991 stocking, growth and age analysis showed none of these fish remained in the pond in 1994. The author links this to the 1992–93 winter conditions, which may have caused winterkill.

**Growth comparisons**
- [[Humphrey Pond]], a riverside gravel pit near Grand Junction, Colorado: fish averaging 55 mm stocked in June 1987 reached 307 mm and 333 g by mid-November, and 404 mm and 920 g by September 1988 (Osmundson and Kaeding 1989).
- Lake Mohave: growth exceeding 400 mm in the first growing season (Mueller et al. 1993).
- Middle Green River growth appears slower than in the upper and lower Colorado, probably because of the shorter growing season at higher latitudes.

**Conclusions**
- Some limited recruitment is probably occurring, most likely in high-flow years when razorback suckers can access floodplain wetlands.
- The population has declined from its historic abundance in the Green River and is probably continuing to decline under the current hydrologic regime (Lanigan and Tyus 1989).
