# Results of the 1992-1994 Aravaipa Creek Fish Monitoring Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department technical report presents results of monthly fish monitoring in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a spring-fed tributary of the [[San Pedro River]] in southeastern Arizona, from August 1992 through April 1994. The project was designed to track native fish populations and to watch for the invasion of [[Red Shiner]], a nonnative cyprinid implicated in native fish declines. No red shiner was collected during the monitoring, and a single red shiner was found during an October 1993 multi-agency survey. The authors conclude that the threat from red shiner appears to have abated, but that a renewed invasion remains likely. They recommend continued monitoring, a yearly full-creek survey, reduced habitat sampling, and construction of a fish barrier in the lower creek as soon as possible.

## Background & Description

Aravaipa Creek flows nearly 75 km from near Klondyke (Graham County) to the San Pedro River (Pinal County) through the Galiuro Mountains. It is one of the last Upper Sonoran Desert streams sustaining a diverse native fish assemblage. Seven native species occur there:

- **Federally threatened:** [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*).
- **Candidate species:** [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Desert Sucker]] (*Catostomus clarki*, also *Pantosteus clarki*), [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*), [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*).

Nonnative fishes recorded in the 1980s included fathead minnow, green sunfish, yellow bullhead, common carp, black bullhead, mosquitofish and largemouth bass. Red shiner first appeared in September 1990.

Since 1988, an inter-agency group (USFWS, USBR, BLM, AGFD, The Nature Conservancy and ASU) has evaluated a fish barrier to stop nonnative spread from the San Pedro River. A late-winter flood in 1991 appeared to expel red shiner, and barrier plans were put on hold while monitoring was intensified. Monthly monitoring began in August 1991 under BLM, TNC and ASU. This report covers phase two, which was led by AGFD and ran from August 1992 to April 1994. Methods followed phase one to maintain continuity. A 25 to 27 year fish record compiled by Dr. W.L. Minckley at ASU supplements these data.

**Objectives:**
- Primary: document native and nonnative populations over two years, with emphasis on red shiner invasion, and build a close-order dataset to interpret the long-term record.
- Secondary: monitor early life-stage periodicity, gross habitat characteristics, and habitat change over time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design.** Nine stations, each about 200 m long, were sampled. They were grouped into four reaches sharing geomorphic features:
- East End (Guest House, Chimney Rock)
- Canyon (Turkey Creek, Wagner Ranch)
- [[Wilderness Area]] (Hell Hole, Horse Camp, Painted Cave), reached by walk-through trips
- West End (White's Ranch, Sycamore Tree)

Seining covered all habitats at each station, using [[Seining]] with 15' and 6' seines. [[Electrofishing]] with backpack shockers was used on quarterly walk-through trips over 100 m at each site. Subhabitats were classed as run, riffle or pool. Habitat variables included depth, width, flow, substrate, canopy and riffle embeddedness.

**Hydrology.** The creek has a bimodal flood regime. Flash floods, including a February-March 1993 flood, were considered important in shaping fish populations and sampling efficiency.

**Threats.** The authors note two main threats: nonnative fish invasion (possibly through biological interactions with red shiner) and habitat degradation. Other concerns included:
- Tailings from mine sites near Klondyke, which the Arizona Department of Environmental Quality (ADEQ) found were entering the watercourse in March 1993. ADEQ reported a pond pH of 4.85 on the upper tailings.
- Recreational pressure, as the Wilderness Area permits 50 visitors per day and visitors must walk the stream channel.
- A pending road repair Environmental Assessment, whose draft lacked runoff and sedimentation estimates.

## Key Findings

**Sampling totals**
- Native fishes totaled 71,041 individuals (99.8%). Nonnative fishes totaled 128 (0.2%).
- Nonnatives were 73 yellow bullheads, 38 mosquitofish and 17 green sunfish.
- Nonnatives were most common in the West End and Wilderness Area reaches, and none were caught at the East End stations.
- Nonnative counts were highest in August (44 in 1992 and 43 in 1993), dropping about 75% by the following November (14 and 11).

**Red shiner**
- No red shiner was collected during the 1992-1994 monitoring.
- The October 1993 Red Shiner Survey (about 387 person-hours of seining along the creek, from the Turkey Creek confluence to the San Pedro River) found one red shiner, below the Sycamore Tree station.

**Species-level results**
- **[[Loach Minnow]]:** Most frequent at East End stations. Seine relative abundance averaged 2.0%, and electrofishing averaged 14.8% (peak 37% at the East End in March 1993). Juveniles appeared in May 1993, and 20+ young-of-year fish were collected in December 1992, suggesting fall spawning that year.
- **[[Spikedace]]:** Nearly absent from West End stations. Highest absolute numbers were at the East End. Juveniles were captured more often by seine than by electrofisher. Spawning may occur in late summer, with fall recruitment.
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]:** Consistently under 5% of the catch and typically under 3%. Highest at Wilderness Area stations and absent from the West End. Adults were caught more often than juveniles.
- **[[Longfin Dace]]:** The most common native species in the creek, comprising 41% of the total catch. Relative abundance was 65% at the West End, 30% in the Canyon, 26% at the East End and 17% in the Wilderness Area. Electrofishing was more effective than seining.
- **[[Desert Sucker]]:** Second most common species, at 23.1% of the total catch. Juveniles outnumbered adults on the West End by more than 15 to 1. Electrofishing was far more efficient than seining (CPUE 68.1 vs. 10.0 per 10 m²).
- **[[Sonora Sucker]]:** Adults were rare (0.8% of the total catch). Juveniles outnumbered adults by more than 11 to 1 at the West End.
- **[[Speckled Dace]]:** The least common native fish, with 380 individuals (0.5%). Absent from the West End and highest at the East End.

**Habitat**
- Total sampled habitat: runs 98.367 m² (as printed), riffles 22,890 m², and pools 3,310 m². Pools deeper than 1.5 m could not be sampled.
- Canyon and Wilderness Area stations held about two-thirds of the pool habitat.
- Chi-square tests showed observed habitat use differed significantly from expected for most native species (p < .001). Nonnative samples were too small to test.

**Multivariate reanalysis (Appendix 6, Kubly)**
- Two multivariate methods were used on the nine sites: [[Discriminant Function Analysis]] and [[Nonmetric Multidimensional Scaling]].
- The first two discriminant functions explained 75.3% and 22.2% of the variance.
- Two NMS dimensions explained 91.1% of the variance.
- Fish assemblages at the upper and lower stations were the most distinct and least variable. Canyon assemblages were more dynamic and had lower classification success.
- The author concluded that any reduction in sites would lose less information if taken from the exterior groups. The Turkey Creek and Wagner Ranch grouping was judged unwarranted.

**Management recommendations**
1. Continue monitoring while red shiner remains a threat, with frequency tied to nonnative trends.
2. Conduct an extensive yearly survey of the entire creek during the period when red shiner is most likely to be found, using the October 1993 Red Shiner Survey protocol. Representative reaches may be sampled if time or personnel are limited.
3. Focus future work on fish data rather than habitat parameters, keeping only thorough photo point records, because habitat data are often equivocal.
4. Initiate a systematic watershed fish survey, including cattle tanks, and an eradication project if warranted.
5. Survey the San Pedro River 5 km either side of the Aravaipa confluence.
6. Build a fish barrier in the lower reach of Aravaipa Creek as soon as possible. Red shiner were found in lower Aravaipa in October 1993 and can enter all reaches.
7. Monitor native fishes before and after any road repair dewatering.
8. Study the effects of recreational use on fishes and expand public education and interpretation.

**Conclusion:** Native fish did not decline during the study period, so the authors do not recommend changes to BLM or TNC management. Continued research is needed to identify threats.
