# Influence of Pump-Storage Generation on Central Arizona Reservoirs, 1973–1975

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department-sponsored final report (an interim report, per its authors) by W. L. Hinckley and Randy H. McNatt, dated 30 October 1975, examines how pumped-storage generation at [[Mormon Flat Dam]] and [[Horse Mesa Dam]] has altered the two lowermost on-river reservoirs of the [[Salt River Reservoirs]] chain, [[Canyon Lake]] and [[Saguaro Lake]]. The study focuses on two parameters, water temperature (thermal stratification) and [[Dissolved Oxygen]], and uses dye tracing, isohet mapping and operational records. The authors conclude that pump-generation operation causes substantial thermal loading and disrupts the stratification of downstream reservoirs, that water movement is now bidirectional, and that riparian and littoral habitats have changed. Effects on fish were only partly quantified: fish kills occur at pumping onset, and some large fish survive being pumped upstream.

## Background & Description

The report compiles selected, previously unpublished information on the two lowermost reservoirs of the Salt River chain, which are low-elevation, hot-desert systems subject to large and unusual modifications over roughly 12 years. Earlier work by [[Rinne Studies]] (1973, 1975) described the sequence of events shown in Figure 2, and that author may have overstated generation capacities by using penstock passage volumes alone. Key structures include:

- **Mormon Flat Dam**: pump-generation unit completed in May 1971; back-pumping at the dam returns water upstream into Canyon Lake.
- **Horse Mesa Dam**: pump-generation system tested in 1972 and reversed flow in Canyon Lake; generation capacity increased by 71.2% by June 1972.
- **Stewart Mountain Dam**: unmodified apart from outlet repair in 1954; outlet capacity is about 50% of the back-pump capacity of Mormon Flat and Horse Mesa dams.
- **Roosevelt Lake**: upstream storage reservoir acting as a sediment and nutrient trap.

The report treats thermal data as reliable to within 0.5 °C, and dissolved oxygen as reliable to within 0.2 mg/l.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Theoretical framework.** Water of similar total dissolved solids but differing temperatures resists mixing because of density. Dye studies in Canyon Lake (1970) showed that the lateral basins of the drowned river channel remain largely independent of the upstream-to-downstream flow of cooler hypolimnetic generator water. Counter-clockwise surface circulation occurred in the lowermost basin, with no evidence of surface water entering the canyon leading to Mormon Flat Dam. Before 1971, the lowermost reservoirs showed well-defined density currents fed by penstock generation from upstream reservoirs.

**Stratification and oxygen.** Roosevelt Lake showed dissolved oxygen deficits below 10–15 m from June to September or early October. Before 1971, downstream reservoirs usually held more than 2.0 mg/l dissolved oxygen, reflecting less decomposer activity in bottom waters and mixing from upstream sources. Stratification patterns of oxygen therefore followed the same density-current-driven passage of water through the system.

**Operations.** Pumping at Mormon Flat occurred sporadically in 1971 (43 days) and was similarly intermittent in 1972–1973 (100 and 55 days). In 1974 it became heavy, with 250 pumping days moving 481,232 acre-feet, equal to 8.3 times the storage capacity of Canyon Lake (57,905 acre-feet at full pool). Horse Mesa pumped 613,499 acre-feet upriver in 258 days into [[Apache Lake]] in 1974, equal to 10.6 times Canyon Lake's capacity.

**Riparian and littoral change.** Pumped storage stabilized reservoir levels and produced a riparian community absent in earlier surveys (O'Degard, 1973). Cattail, giant reed, diverse sedges, willow and cottonwood, and submergent aquatic vegetation now occupy hundreds of meters of shoreline at Apache Lake.

## Key Findings

- **Thermal stratification in Canyon Lake** changed sharply over 1970–1975. The metalimnion centre was about 5 m deep in 1970–1971 and 1973, about 2.5–3.5 m in 1972, and nearly 1 m in 1974, when surface temperatures reached 26 °C and bottom temperatures exceeded 10 °C for most of the year. The lake stratified erratically in 1974, and destratification occurred later and at temperatures 2–4 °C higher than in other study years. Conditions in 1975 resembled 1971–1973 more than 1974.
- **Pumpage disrupts stratification over a wide area.** During a 1971 test pumping at Mormon Flat, stratification (thermal, oxygen and other parameters) was broken across a considerable distance above the dam. Surface isohets moved upstream and were forced into an up-lake pattern. Recovery to stratification was relatively rapid after a single pumping event but persisted at detectable levels with heavy and frequent pumping.
- **Bidirectional flow.** Dye studies and isohet analysis showed that the entire water mass of Canyon Lake moved upstream during joint Mormon Flat and Horse Mesa pumping. Saguaro Lake water entering the forebay in 1971 was at 0.83–0.94 m/s. A pumping test on 7 June 1971 showed upslope movement of surface isohets and temperature changes at 300 m downstream of the dam.
- **Dissolved oxygen patterns** tracked thermal changes. In 1974, oxygen declines in Canyon and Saguaro lakes were much greater than in other years and were judged too large to be purely physical. The authors suggest a change in biotic activity.
- **Thermal loading** of downstream reservoirs by pump-generation was inferred from hypolimnetic temperatures that were higher in 1974 (below 16 °C in Canyon Lake and below 18 °C in Saguaro Lake in 1975 and 1974 respectively, versus lower values in earlier years). No significant correlations with climate were found, so the authors attribute the heat gain to inflow–outflow and homogenization of water from Canyon and Apache lakes.
- **Fish effects (partial).** A substantial fish kill occurs at the onset of pumping, and the number of moribund or dead fish declines as pumping continues. Survival of pumped fish was not yet quantified. Trout tagged and stocked in Canyon Lake were caught by anglers in Apache Lake, showing that some relatively large fish survive being pumped upstream.
- **Plankton** responses were inconclusive. A major shift in zooplankton species composition occurred over the study period, and phytoplankton blooms were sporadic and highly variable.
- **Habitat change** includes a new riparian community and submerged vegetation at Apache Lake, with littoral zones now the focus of future study.
- **Status.** The authors describe the report as an interim final report of an ongoing study. Earlier biological interpretations were tentative and under re-evaluation, and dye studies and experimental designs for estimating pumped-fish survival were in progress.
