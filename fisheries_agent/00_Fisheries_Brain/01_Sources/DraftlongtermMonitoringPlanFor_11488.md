# Long-Term Monitoring Plan for Fish Populations in Selected Waters in the Gila River Basin, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This DRAFT plan, dated June 7, 1996, was prepared by the [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Region, Phoenix Area Office) for the [[U S Fish And Wildlife Service]] and the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]]. It implements a Reasonable and Prudent Alternative (RPA) of the [[Biological Opinion]] on the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) water deliveries to the [[Gila River Basin]]. The RPA requires a baseline study and long-term monitoring of the presence and distribution of non-native fishes in the CAP aqueduct and selected river and canal reaches.

The plan defines the parameters to monitor (species richness, species distribution, and assemblage structure), repeatable sampling methods, statistical approaches with effect-size criteria, a standardized database, and a management action plan for detections of new non-native species or exceeded criteria. The monitoring is intended to run for the 100-year life of the CAP, or until the status of the listed fishes changes under the Endangered Species Act.

## Background & Description

**Central Arizona Project and listed species.** The CAP transports Colorado River water from [[Lake Havasu]] to central and southern Arizona through open aqueducts and pipelines. Congress authorized it in the Colorado River Basin Project Act of 1968, and construction was largely completed in 1993. The aqueduct system is a conduit for potential transfers of non-indigenous fishes and other aquatic organisms from the Lower Colorado River into Gila River Basin drainages.

The FWS Biological Opinion (1994) determined that the project would jeopardize four threatened or endangered fishes native to the Basin: [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). It also determined that the project would adversely modify designated critical habitat for the latter three species.

**Monitoring categories.** The plan follows MacDonald et al. (1991) in distinguishing three monitoring types: baseline monitoring, project monitoring, and trend monitoring.

**Target reaches.** Six areas are to be monitored:
1. CAP aqueduct
2. [[Salt River Project]] (SRP) canals
3. Florence-Casa Grande (FCG) Canal
4. [[Salt River]] between Stewart Mountain Dam and Granite Reef Dam
5. Perennial reach of the [[Gila River]] below Coolidge Dam
6. Perennial reaches of the [[San Pedro River]] downstream from the U.S.-Mexico border

**Purpose and scope.** The plan is meant as a guide for contracted and Reclamation investigators to standardize methods and ensure comparability of results. The document notes that a plan without threshold criteria and management actions would be "surveillance" (Cairns 1980). The field methods handbook referenced in the plan had not yet been developed at the time of the review letter.

**Comparison reference.** [[Aravaipa Creek]] is described as a relatively pristine system with an important native fish community. It is proposed as a regional reference site for estimating attainable conditions and natural variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Variability and disturbance.** The plan states that in disturbed systems such as the target reaches, determining a "normal" assemblage structure is problematic. Many factors contribute to variability, and distinguishing natural from human-induced causes is difficult. Introduced species may add to this variability in altered habitats.

**Geomorphology.** Native and non-native fishes respond differently to disturbances such as flooding across geomorphic reach types, from highly confined canyons to poorly confined valleys. Reaches are therefore stratified by stream gradient and channel confinement, an approach analogous to the landtype stratum of Lotspeich and Platts (1982).

**Macrohabitats.** Sampling stations are stratified into pool, riffle, and run units (after Bisson et al. 1982 and Platts et al. 1983). Macrohabitat distribution reflects channel geomorphology and influences the aquatic community.

**Flow regime.** Hydrology of all target reaches except the San Pedro River is artificially controlled by dam releases and canal dry-ups. Discharge abatement at Coolidge Dam beginning in October also influences sampling timing on the Gila River. Species present in the Gila River Basin streams include small-bodied native fishes, which the plan notes are typical of smaller Gila River Basin streams.

**Non-native species.** Table 4 lists recent occurrences of fishes in the target canals and streams, including non-native species such as [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Chaenobryttus cyanellus*), and several tilapia species. Native species in the table include Gila robusta (roundtail chub), Agosia chrysogaster (longfin dace), Catostomus insignis (Sonora sucker), and Xyrauchen texanus (razorback sucker). The table is a source-based occurrence list and is not a population survey.

**Canal conditions.** Canal habitats are deep, with high current velocity, especially on the CAP aqueduct. SRP and FCG canals are dewatered at roughly the same time each year. Canal habitats have not been typed in the literature, so canal stations are not stratified by macrohabitat.

## Key Findings

**Objectives of the monitoring plan**
- Five objectives are defined: (1) determine parameters to monitor; (2) identify statistical techniques and effect-size criteria; (3) determine sampling methods; (4) develop a standardized statistical database; (5) develop a management action plan.
- The plan's goal, from the Biological Opinion, is "to establish baseline data on the presence and distribution of non-native fishes in the target reaches and to detect changes in the species composition or distribution."
- Collection of a single specimen of a new species should be considered adequate to initiate immediate intensive monitoring and possibly more extreme management actions.

**Statistical design (Objective 2 and Appendix I)**
- The null hypothesis is that there is no change in a monitored parameter. Statistical power should be at least 0.8, with α = 0.05 as the Type I error level.
- Per the Wilson power analysis (Appendix I, prepared by [[Jeffrey R Wilson]] of Arizona State University): a 25% change in a species' relative abundance is detectable at power >80% if at least 30 individuals of that species are collected per sample. If 60 individuals are collected, a 10% change is detectable at power >80%.
- A 30% or smaller change in relatively rare species (<25 individuals collected per sample) would not be probable to detect.
- The recommended analysis is a [[Repeated Measures Anova]] (nested, after Maceina et al. 1994). Stratification by short-lived (<3 years) and long-lived (>3 years) native and non-native fishes will be evaluated.
- In the Appendix, the Aravaipa Creek model found a species difference p-value of .0001. The Gila and San Pedro data found a stream effect p-value of .0025 in the percent-abundance model and .0006 in the square-root model.

**Sampling reaches and stations**
- Three systematic sampling stations are to be permanently established within each geomorphic stream reach, where possible.
- Canal stations are primarily forebays upstream of pumping plants, such as the Salt-Gila pumping plant on the Fannin-McFarland portion of the CAP.
- Because annual SRP dry-ups vary, fixed stations on SRP canals were removed except between the electrical fish barriers and Granite Reef Dam on the Arizona and South canals. Sampling below the barriers is opportunistic.

**Stream sampling effort**
- Intensive quantitative sampling covers a 200 m reach, with extensive qualitative sampling of uncommon habitats in contiguous reaches.
- The plan cites a minimum sampling length of 35 times mean stream width (Lyons 1992). Sampling a 200 m stream with mean width of 5.7 m or less satisfies the length:width ratio of 35.
- The reduction from 400 m to 200 m is a major change from the earlier draft, along with the addition of qualitative sampling for rare species.
- Upstream station boundaries are 200 stream meters above the downstream boundary, measured along the thalweg.
- Block nets are not required, based on Simonson and Lyons (1995) and Vadas and Orth (1993).

**Sampling gears**
- Electrofishing is the primary descriptor of assemblage structure at each station. It is effective in shallow habitats (generally <2 m deep) and is best for large-bodied species.
- Electrofishing is ineffective in both very low and very high conductivity waters.
- Seining is most effective for small-bodied species in shallow (<1.5 m deep), turbid, smooth-bottomed habitats.
- Pulse rates should be kept below 30 pulses per second if fish injury is an issue for management agencies. The plan cites Reynolds (1983) that 40-120 pulses per second are more effective on small fish and minnows typical of smaller Gila River Basin streams.

**Sampling periodicity**
- Fixed stations are to be sampled once annually, during autumn (September 1 to November 30 when possible), to avoid larval fishes and near-modal discharge.
- Random stations on the San Pedro River are sampled on a temporally random schedule, with one additional event per reach annually (Table 2 gives a 10-year schedule).

**Minimum data and field records**
- At least 50 lengths are recorded for each species from each sampling station for small-bodied fishes. Larger, longer-lived species should also have at least 50, preferably more.
- Water quality (temperature, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, pH) is recorded at each station.
- Discharge is measured at 10 minimum (preferably 25) equidistant points across the stream, following Rantz et al. (1982).

**Vouchers, permits, and quality control**
- Several specimens of each permitted species are preserved as museum vouchers in 10% formalin, housed at the [[Arizona State University Collection Of Fishes]].
- The incidental take statement in the Gila River Basin Biological Opinion serves as Reclamation's permit for take of federally protected species during monitoring.
- A one-day training session is held annually with all field crews before autumn sampling.
- The first season or two is treated as a pilot project, with a post-implementation meeting to revise methods.

**Database and reporting**
- Investigators must submit original field forms and electronic data files within 90 days of completing field sampling.
- Reclamation provides a dBASE V data entry and verification program.
- By May 1 each year, Reclamation will provide FWS, AGFD, NMGF, and interested parties a written annual report.
- A comprehensive report of accumulated annual data will be submitted every five years.

**Management action plan (Objective 5)**
- If a new non-native species is detected, the first response is immediate intensive investigation of its distribution and relative abundance, with reach-wide surveys extending upstream and downstream from the point of detection.
- Possible actions include renovation of the affected reach with or without fish barriers, selective removal of species, regulations to enhance harvest, reconsultation with FWS, controlled flooding if the reach is downstream of a storage reservoir, and habitat modification.
- NEPA and other compliance must be completed in advance for urgent action. A list of potential vendors for ichthyocides should be prepared.
- Management actions are to be funded by the FWS Nonnative Management Fund under the Gila River Basin Biological Opinion (RPA #4).

**Data discrepancies noted in the source**
- The Aravaipa Creek data are described as covering 1965-1990 in the main text, but as 1943-1990 in Appendix I.
- The Appendix refers to "at least 25" of each species for detecting a 30% change, while the main text states 30 individuals for a 25% change.
