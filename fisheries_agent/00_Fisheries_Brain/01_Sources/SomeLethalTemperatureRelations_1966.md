#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 study by Albert V. Tyler (University of Toronto, Great Lakes Institute) examines the upper lethal temperature relations of two sympatric minnow species, **[[Chrosomus Eos]]** (northern redbelly dace) and **[[Chrosomus Neogaeus]]** (finescale dace), which are known to hybridize in the wild (New 1962). Using standardized acclimation and lethal-bath methodology (following Fry et al. 1946; Brett 1952), the author identified **three distinct "lethal effects"** underlying mortality distributions at high temperatures — the first attributable at least partly to thermal shock, the second and third of undetermined mechanism. Both species showed similar heat tolerance when acclimated to ≥15 °C, but at 9 °C acclimation, *C. eos* was less heat-tolerant than *C. neogaeus*. *C. eos* also displayed significantly greater heat resistance in summer than winter at equivalent acclimation temperatures, demonstrating a seasonal (not just acclimation-driven) component to thermal tolerance. The paper introduces the concept of the **"partial resistance zone"** as a quantitative link between Fry's (1947) "resistance zone" and "tolerance zone" concepts.

## Background & Description

- **Taxa studied:** *Chrosomus eos* Cope and *Chrosomus neogaeus* (Cope), both Cyprinidae ("minnows").
- **Size range of test specimens:** 4–7 cm total length.
- **Hybridization:** Known to occur between the two species in sympatric populations ([[New 1962]]), though no hybrid individuals were detected in the study's stocks.
- **Physiological death criterion:** Cessation of opercular movement and touch response were unreliable indicators of death (fish could recover), so **cessation of heartbeat** (observed via oscillating light reflection near the pectoral girdle) was used as the definitive "resistance time" endpoint — heartbeat persisted after opercular/swimming movement ceased.
- No detailed external morphological description is given; the paper is physiological/ecological in focus.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Distribution:** Collected from Algonquin Park and York County, Ontario; the two species are frequently **sympatric** in the same shallow lake habitats.
- **Habitat:** Small lakes/streams in the [[Great Lakes Region]] with seasonal temperature variation; shallow water zones used for co-occurrence sampling.
- **Thermal ecology:** Fish were seasonally acclimated in the lab to reflect natural photoperiod and ambient lake temperatures (9, 15, 20, 25 °C acclimation regimes), simulating winter and summer thermal regimes.
- **Water quality:** Aerated, filtered well water used for all trials (mineral analysis previously reported in [[Tyler 1964]]).
- **Comparative context:** Findings are compared with other freshwater and marine ectotherms' lethal thermal responses, including [[Girella Nigricans]] (greenfish, chill-coma phenomena; Doudoroff 1942), *Oncorhynchus* salmonids (Brett 1952; Brett and Alderdice 1958), the guppy *Lebistes reticulatus* (Gibson 1954), and freshwater amphipods/isopods (Sprague 1959) — situating *Chrosomus* within a broader framework of ectotherm thermal tolerance research.

## Key Findings

### Three Lethal Effects
- **First effect:** Shallow-slope probit segment; associated at least partly with **thermal shock** from abrupt transfer to lethal temperature. Never accounted for >50% mortality in a sample. Could be reduced/delayed by gradual tempering (gradual staged transfer or slow warming in a sealed bag) prior to lethal exposure — demonstrating this effect is independent of the second effect.
- **Second effect:** Steep-slope probit segment, occurring at **all acclimation levels in both species and seasons**; most closely tied to standard test-temperature-dependent thermal death, showing a clear log-time vs. temperature relationship. Used to define the **incipient lethal temperature** boundary of the "partial resistance zone."
- **Third effect:** Occurred sporadically after long exposure (>3000 min), less dependent on test temperature; possibly linked to secondary causes (e.g., starvation) rather than direct heat effects. Found in both summer and winter tests.
- Mortality distributions were log-normal (per Fry et al. 1946) but sometimes showed **complex, two-segment probit lines**, reflecting conjoined log-normal distributions — a pattern echoed in the literature for [[Doudoroff 1942]] (primary/secondary chill-coma), [[Brett 1952]] (Pacific salmon), and [[Gibson 1954]] (guppy "first/second phases").

### Species Comparison
- At acclimation ≥15 °C: *C. eos* and *C. neogaeus* show **nearly identical** heat resistance/tolerance ("partial resistance zones" overlap).
- At 9 °C acclimation: **[[Chrosomus Neogaeus]] is more heat-tolerant than [[Chrosomus Eos]]**, i.e., *C. eos* has a narrower partial resistance zone at low acclimation temperatures.

### Seasonal Effect (C. eos only)
- *C. eos* is **significantly more heat-resistant in summer than winter** at equivalent acclimation temperatures — demonstrated via 12-hour lethal temperature comparisons (cross-validated against Brett 1944 field data).
- The **upper boundary** of the partial resistance zone (severe lethal temperatures, 30-min median resistance time) shifted only slightly from winter to summer.
- The **lower boundary** (incipient lethal temperature of the second effect) shifted much more from winter to summer.
- Net result: the **winter partial resistance zone is larger** than the summer zone — summer gains in tolerance are concentrated at the "incipient" (less severe) end rather than uniformly across the zone.

### Conceptual Contribution
- Introduces the **"partial resistance zone"** — a quantitative construct bridging Fry's (1947) **resistance zone** (3 variables: acclimation temp, test temp, median resistance time) and **tolerance zone** (2 variables: acclimation temp, incipient lethal temp), enabling direct comparison of resistance and tolerance concepts on a single graph.
- Defined operationally using the 30-minute median resistance time as the upper boundary and the incipient lethal temperature of the second lethal effect as the lower boundary.

### Methodological Notes
- Acclimation protocol: +1 °C/day (warming) or −0.5 °C/day (cooling); minimum 1–2 weeks stable at target acclimation temperature; ≥3 weeks total lab conditioning.
- Lethal bath temperature control: ±0.05 °C; acclimation tank control: ±0.5 °C.
- Observation schedule followed a logarithmic time series (continuous for first 14 min, then intervals increasing by factor of 1.26).
- Statistical treatment followed [[Fry Et Al 1946]] probit/log-time graphical method for determining median resistance time and incipient lethal temperature.
