# Use of Ecoregions in Biological Monitoring

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[Robert M. Hughes]], [[Steven A. Heiskary]], [[William J. Matthews]] and [[Chris O. Yoder]] evaluates whether [[Ecoregions]] provide a useful geographic framework for biological monitoring of lakes and streams. The authors compare ecoregions with [[Fish Faunal Regions]], [[River Basins]] and physiographic regions across several U.S. states and New Zealand, then describe how Minnesota and Ohio use ecoregions for lake management and biocriteria. They conclude that ecoregions are the single most suitable regional framework, but that they are insufficient alone and should be combined with drainage-based units and hierarchical subregionalization.

## Background & Description

Aquatic classification aims to stratify variance: the more variance a classification explains, the more useful it is. Traditional frameworks were river basins, catchments and physiography, but [[Aquatic Ecoregions]] were proposed as landscape-based units grouping water bodies that would be naturally similar absent permanent human settlement. Ecoregions are defined by land surface form, soil, potential natural vegetation and general land use (Omernik 1987).

The chapter states three purposes: (1) compare fish faunal regions and ecoregions, (2) summarize the experience of two states using ecoregions as management units, and (3) discuss concerns about their use.

Prior studies reviewed include [[Ross 1963]] (terrestrial biomes and caddisflies), [[Legendre and Legendre 1984]] (climate, geomorphology and vegetation over basins for Quebec fish), [[Jackson and Harvey 1989]] (fish faunas in 286 Ontario lakes, correlated with geographic proximity), and [[Corkum]] studies of benthic invertebrates (1989, 1990).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish faunal regions versus ecoregions**
- **Missouri:** [[Pflieger 1971]] and Pflieger et al. (1981), using data from over 1,600 localities, described three fish faunal regions plus a large river fauna. Except in northwest Missouri, these correspond well with Omernik's ecoregions, probably because both reflect physiography.
- **Kansas:** [[Hawkes et al. 1986]] used 410 stream sites to identify six fish regions, mostly drainage-delineated, which bore no resemblance to Omernik's six ecoregions.
- **Nebraska:** [[Bazata 1991]] used discriminant analysis on 350 sites and found five fish faunal regions more appropriate than Omernik's seven ecoregions.
- **Oregon:** [[Hughes et al. 1987]] analyzed 9,100 collections from 85 catchments. Of 10 physiographic provinces, 2 of 18 river basins, and 8 ecoregions tested, ecoregions were the most suitable statewide, but insufficient alone.
- **Arkansas:** [[Rohm et al. 1987]] (22 streams, six ecoregions) and [[Matthews and Robison 1988]] (over 2,000 collections, 101 drainages) both distinguished two lowland regions and an Ozark region. Table 8.1 shows ecoregions misclassified fish assemblages less often than basins in every faunal region (e.g., 34% vs 64% for region 1; 46% vs 83% for region 4).
- **Tennessee Valley reservoirs:** [[McDonough and Barr 1977]] clustered 21 reservoirs by position, size, basin and elevation. Reanalysis revealed a clearer relationship with ecoregions, with only two misclassifications.

**Scale and heterogeneity**
- Basins can miss large river faunas, and ecoregions alone cannot capture within-drainage heterogeneity (e.g., Willamette River reaches, Calapooia River dissolved oxygen).
- Agencies cannot develop separate regions for every assemblage; ordination of multiple assemblages with physical and chemical variables has not been demonstrated.

## Key Findings

**Management applications**

- **Minnesota:** The Minnesota Pollution Control Agency used a 1,400-lake database, existing fish data and 90 minimally disturbed reference lakes to evaluate four ecoregions (NLF, CHF, WCP, NGP). Total phosphorus and fish assemblages differed markedly among ecoregions. Residents in clearer-lake regions required lakes twice as clear for swimming and nuisance-bloom judgments. Two to three limnologists manage about 12,000 lakes using this framework.
- **Ohio:** The Ohio EPA used ecoregions and data from over 300 minimally disturbed reference sites to set biological criteria for 45,000 miles of streams and rivers. Warmwater criteria were set at the 25th percentile of reference values for headwater, wading and boat size classes in each region. In a study of over 400 sites, 61% attained narrative criteria, while numerical macroinvertebrate criteria gave 34% attainment and 44% non-attainment (Yoder 1991).

**Reference site selection (Table 8.3)**
- Six-step procedure: define areas, delineate ecoregions and basins, define water body types, delineate candidate watersheds, eliminate disturbed areas, field-verify, and determine the number of reference sites.
- Reference sites should be monitored for at least two assemblages.

**Concerns and answers**
- Ecosystem health is defined as a balanced, integrated, adaptive community comparable to natural regional habitat (Karr and Dudley 1981). Several natural "healthy" communities exist, so pristine-only references are unrealistic.
- Ecoregions can be aggregated to a biome-level classification, and the Omernik and Gallant (1990) map is at biome resolution.
- Basins often cross drastically different ecoregions, so both basins and ecoregions should be evaluated, especially for fishes.
- Ecoregions are too detailed for some users and not detailed enough for others. A hierarchical system is needed; the EPA Science Advisory Board recommended subregionalization in 1991, but this had not occurred.

**Conclusions**
- Museum fish data are valuable for estimating species pools and calibrating indices such as the [[Index of Biotic Integrity]], but must be interpreted with caution.
- The authors call for stronger federal leadership, academic involvement through funding mechanisms, and integration with the [[Environmental Monitoring and Assessment Program]] (EMAP). They note that monitoring receives a very small share of state and federal budgets and that its information is little used in management decisions.
