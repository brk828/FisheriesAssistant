# Impacts of Electrofishing on Fish: Survey Request and Draft Recommendations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a December 1991 package from the Biology Committee of the [[Upper Basin Endangered Fish Recovery Implementation Program]]. It asks Colorado River Basin researchers to share their experience with [[Electrofishing]], including observed injuries, and to recommend safer practices. The request supports a literature review by [[Darrel E. Snyder]] of the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at [[Colorado State University]], conducted under [[Robert T. Muth]]. The attached draft introduction argues that electrofishing can cause substantial spinal injury, particularly to certain species, and that the problem may be more widespread than assumed. It proposes a set of policy recommendations covering moratoria, equipment and waveform choices, documentation, training, and research. The package also advertises a two-day course on the [[Fish Health Condition Profile]], held in March 1993 (the scan reads "1992" but the course date is garbled).

## Background & Description

**Survey request.** The memo asks recipients to return responses by 10 January 1992 or as early as possible. It invites them to forward the questionnaire to other electrofishing researchers. Final results will be distributed by the Upper Basin program and the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] office. Responses are attributed to individuals only with the respondent's specific permission.

**Survey questions.** The questionnaire covers five areas:
1. Nature and extent of the respondent's electrofishing experience (supervisor, crew leader, or crew member).
2. Environments sampled and conditions, including habitat type, season, time of day, water temperature, and conductivity.
3. Species and size groups sampled or monitored, with emphasis on endangered species.
4. Equipment and techniques, including gear type, waveform, and settings.
5. Observed adverse or injurious effects, including physical injury, abnormal behavior, and mortality, along with the electrical conditions under which they occurred.
6. Recommendations for optimal efficiency and minimal injury.

**Distribution.** The mailing list is long and includes staff from the [[US Fish and Wildlife Service]], the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], state wildlife agencies in Utah, Colorado, New Mexico, and Arizona, Bio/West Inc., Arizona State University, and Navajo Nation fish and wildlife staff.

**Draft introduction.** The attached draft describes electrofishing as a valuable sampling method used for about four decades. It states that recent studies by [[Sharber and Carothers]] (1988, 1989), [[Holmes et al.]] (1990), and [[Meyer and Miller]] (1991, in press) documented substantial injury to the spine of certain fish species. These injuries occurred with relatively modern equipment using [[Pulsed Direct Current]]. The author notes that most existing data concern salmonids, especially [[Rainbow Trout]], but concern for other species is serious. The [[Alaska Department of Fish and Game]] imposed a moratorium on electrofishing in waters containing large rainbow trout.

**Why the issue is emerging now.** The draft suggests that injury has long been an unrecognized by-product of fish collection. Fish that appear to recover and swim away may still have spinal damage, which can show up later as deformities. Many such injuries are not obvious at capture. Other biologists have argued that years of electrofishing have not visibly affected the populations studied. The author responds that injury may still matter where it occurs in large numbers, and that the question concerns resource reduction and data quality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Injury types.** Most injuries reported are spinal compression fractures, dislocations, and related internal hemorrhage and tissue damage. Broader questions include effects on swimming ability, behavior, growth, reproduction, and the mechanisms of injury.
- **Electric field zones.** The draft emphasizes the "zone of tetany" around the anode, where fish are immobilized and may be exposed to higher risk of electrocution, respiratory failure, and other injury. Reducing this zone is a central engineering goal.
- **Information gaps.** Published symposia and books on electrofishing (including FAO and EIFAC volumes) gave little attention to injury and mortality, apart from advice to minimize the zone of tetany. Research is often limited in scope and difficult to compare because gear, techniques, environmental conditions, fish species, and terminology vary.
- **Influencing variables.** Water conductivity, temperature, and field strength all affect fish response. Experiments must test these across the ranges expected in the field.
- **Proposed sponsors.** The author suggests that a coordinated, multi-agency effort (Bureau of Reclamation, US Fish and Wildlife Service, consortiums, and professional societies such as the American Fisheries Society) is needed.

## Key Findings

**Draft recommendations for electrofishing policy and research**

1. **Limit use near endangered species.** Minimize electrofishing of, or in the vicinity of, endangered species until response thresholds and injury susceptibility are determined in controlled experiments with hatchery stock. Institute a moratorium on new sampling programs using electrofishing unless the work is critical and no less damaging alternative exists.
2. **Substitute alternatives.** Where less harmful sampling methods are effective and do not compromise comparisons with past data, replace electrofishing with those methods.
3. **Update equipment if electrofishing remains necessary.**
   - Abandon [[Alternating Current]] (AC) gear except when all captured fish will be killed or preserved.
   - Consider [[Direct Current]] (DC), which is generally less injurious than PDC, where its power and effectiveness are adequate.
   - If using PDC, use waveforms, frequencies, pulse durations, and power levels that cause the least damage while maintaining capture efficiency. Square waves with frequencies of about 30 Hz or less, such as the [[Complex Pulse Waveform]] (CPS), are currently recommended but need more research.
   - Operate with the largest practical anode and the lowest effective power to minimize the zone of tetany.
4. **Standardize documentation.** Record output parameters, conductivity, water temperature, and field strength (voltage gradient at specified distances) for each operation, along with any injuries or abnormal behavior for all species.
5. **Preserve and examine injured fish.** Provide for preservation (icing, freezing, or formalin) and documentation of injuries in mortalities and severely injured fish. Documentation should include photographs, autopsies, and X-rays where possible, using standardized injury descriptors such as those agreed on at the 1991 Western Division American Fisheries Society electrofishing session.
6. **Ensure safe operation and training.**
   - Adopt national or state electrofishing guidelines where adequate (the US Fish and Wildlife Service guidelines may need updating).
   - Require that team leaders be trained and certified, and that other members be trained for their specific program.
   - Require current first aid and CPR certification, with periodic recertification in electrofishing, first aid, and CPR.
   - Use courses such as the one offered through the Fisheries Academy of the US Fish and Wildlife Service, which is normally offered at least once a year.
7. **Sponsor research.**
   - Determine field strength thresholds for immobilization (narcosis and tetany) and for spinal injury, using DC and selected PDC currents across a range of conductivities and temperatures. Include X-ray checks on test fish.
   - Compare existing and potential PDC waveform improvements, including pulse form, frequency, width, and pattern. Check whether leading-edge voltage spikes in square waves cause problems.
   - Develop electrode systems that reduce the zone of tetany and adjust to conductivity, such as floating spherical or hemispherical electrodes, adjustable insulated electrodes, protective non-conductive grids, and underwater lights for night sampling.
   - Build a "black box" controller that uses in-situ sensors for conductivity, temperature, voltage gradient, and possibly depth to maintain constant effective fields and automatically compensate for changes. The system would shut down when conditions become unsafe or when settings would harm non-target species or sizes.
8. **Build facilities and field-test results.** Establish a portable or non-exclusive field laboratory for research and testing, developed with commercial electrofishing manufacturers and experts, with X-ray and film facilities. Field-test new equipment and investigate portable on-site X-ray equipment so that captured fish can be examined for spinal injury without being killed.

**Fish health course announcement**

- The Larval Fish Laboratory at Colorado State University in Fort Collins offers a two-day short course on 10–11 March (year garbled in scan) on autopsy-based fish health and condition assessment.
- The course introduces the Fish Health Condition Profile (HCP), developed over about 20 years by Ron Goede of the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources. The HCP uses standardized quantitative and semi-quantitative measurements to assess relative health and condition of a fish population. It does not require specialized laboratory facilities or extensive analytical equipment.
- Instruction combines presentations on background and methodology with hands-on laboratory work on live fish. Participants will calculate, summarize, and interpret data.
- The course fee is $250.00 per person. One hour of academic credit is available for an additional fee (the amount is garbled in the scan) and depends on program approval. Registration closes 27 January 1992 and is limited to 30 participants.
- Contacts are Robert T. Muth and the Larval Fish Laboratory (phone numbers listed in the announcement).
