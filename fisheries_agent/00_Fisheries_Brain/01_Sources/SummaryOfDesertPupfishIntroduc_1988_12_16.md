# Summary of Desert Pupfish Introduction Records for Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinodontidae #DesertPupfish #ConservationBiology

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1988 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) memorandum from William G. Kepner to Dean Hendrickson of the Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD), compiling historical introduction (reintroduction/translocation) records for the [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) in Arizona. Records are divided by subspecific form: the [[Gila Desert Pupfish]] (*C. m. macularius*) and the [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (*C. m. eremus*). The data were compiled to support the Desert Fishes Recovery Team's efforts, referenced in the context of "Battle Against Extinction: Native Fish Management in the American West," and copied to Dr. W. L. Minckley, Chairman of the [[Desert Fishes Recovery Team]]. The note catalogs specific stocking events, including numbers of individuals released, dates, precise legal locations (Township/Range/Section), elevations, and the responsible agency or institution for each introduction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The recorded introduction sites reflect the species' tolerance for small, isolated, often spring-fed desert aquatic habitats, consistent with the ecology of [[Desert Pupfish]] as a habitat generalist adapted to extreme, fluctuating desert water bodies. Introduction/refugia sites spanned a range of elevations (1,360–3,420 ft) and habitat types, including:

- **Natural spring and canyon habitats**: Tres Alamos Falls, Peoples Canyon, Peoples Canyon Spring, Mesquite Spring, Howard Well, Grapevine Spring (Yerba Mansa), Bog Hole (Coronado National Forest), Tinney Tank (Audubon Research Ranch)
- **Constructed/institutional refugia**: Deer Valley High School Refugium, Boyce Thompson Southwestern Arboretum, Desert Botanical Garden, Arizona State University, Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum

This dual reliance on both wild spring habitats and captive/refugial institutional ponds illustrates a core management strategy for the species: maintaining redundant, geographically dispersed populations (in situ and ex situ) to buffer against extirpation — a hallmark of [[Endangered Species Conservation]] strategy for desert fishes. The species' presence in artificial refugia (zoos, arboreta, university ponds) underscores its conservation-dependent status and the limited extent of remaining native habitat within its historic range in the [[Gila River Basin]] and Sonoran Desert drainages.

### Additional Notes

- Introductions were carried out through multi-agency collaboration, primarily involving the **Bureau of Land Management (BLM)**, **Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD)**, **U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS)**, the **University of Arizona**, **Arizona State University**, and the **Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum**.
- Peoples Canyon (T12N R10W SW¼ Sec. 14) received *C. m. macularius* stockings on at least three separate occasions (1982, 1983, and 1988), suggesting either repeated reinforcement of a persistent population or repeated failure/recolonization needs at this site.
- The document illustrates an early, systematic effort by federal and state agencies to establish redundant populations of both pupfish forms as an [[Ex Situ Conservation]] and reintroduction strategy prior to broader Endangered Species Act recovery planning efforts.
- No population survival, genetic, or monitoring outcome data are included in this document; it is strictly a historical stocking-record summary.
