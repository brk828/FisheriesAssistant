# Physical Habitat Use by Loach Minnow in Southwestern Desert Streams

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study by John N. Rinne quantifies the physical habitat used by the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), a small, obligate riffle-dwelling cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico. Habitat data (velocity, depth, substrate) were collected in [[Aravaipa Creek]] (Arizona) over 1982–1985 and in several larger streams of the [[Upper Gila River Basin]] (New Mexico) in June 1983. Habitat use varied with stream size and habitat availability and was related to fish size and the fish's response to current. The author concludes that the species is broadly adapted to physical habitat, and that habitat loss across its range, rather than a narrow habitat requirement, is the main cause of its decline.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The loach minnow is a small (<80 mm total length, TL), short-lived cyprinid. Adults reach up to about 70 mm TL, and modal size in the studied streams was approximately 40–50 mm TL.
- **Life stages (TL):** Larvae <28 mm; juveniles 29–37 mm; adults >38 mm.
- **Morphology:** The fusiform, dense, compressed body and large pectoral fins are presumed adaptations to swift water. The fish nonetheless avoids extreme currents, moving in short bursts between sheltered "pockets" behind substrate.
- **Conservation status:** Threatened in Arizona, endangered in New Mexico, and listed as threatened by the [[United States Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1985). Range reduction results from habitat loss to dams, water diversion, and introduced non-native fishes (see [[Minckley]], 1973).
- **Study rationale:** Little specific information on habitat requirements existed, and most prior work came from the upland [[Sonoran Desert]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study areas**

- **Aravaipa Creek (primary site):** A low-gradient, narrow (3–5 m), often braided, hydrologically stable stream with gravel-pebble substrate. Modal flows peak in winter, but late-summer convectional storms can produce sudden, violent flash floods. Data came from the upper reaches, where the species is most abundant. The reach lies within the Upland [[Sonoran Desert]] biome.
- **Upper Gila River basin (comparative sites):** Sampled in the Gila River mainstem, the Forks of the Gila, the [[San Francisco River]], and the [[Tularosa River]] in New Mexico. Streams were 5–15 m wide, with discharge of 1.0 to 5 m³/s. Surrounding communities are semi-desert grassland and Great Basin conifer woodland, on the northern fringe of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]].

**Physical habitat variables**

- **Velocity:** Measured 5 cm above the substrate with a direct-reading flow meter.
- **Depth:** Recorded with a modified metal meter rule.
- **Substrate:** Visually classed as sand (<2 mm), gravel (3–16 mm), pebble (17–64 mm), cobble (65–256 mm), or boulder (>256 mm). Two intermediate classes (sand-gravel and gravel-pebble) were added, giving seven classes in total (see [[Substrate Classification]]).

## Key Findings

**Methods**

- Sites were chosen from prior collection experience and qualitative habitat descriptions. Transects were placed where the species was first captured, plus at least two more 5 m upstream and downstream. A maximum of 10 transects were sampled per reach.
- Fish were immobilized by DC [[Electrofishing]] and captured with a 1 m block seine. Each sampled area was approximately 1–2 m².
- Habitat was measured with a meter-transect method, taking 21 measurements per transect. These were averaged for analysis (see [[Transect Sampling]]).
- Aravaipa Creek was sampled seven times across winter, spring, and summer. The upper Gila was sampled once (June 1983) at six sites, five of which contained the species.
- Statistics included unpaired t-tests (depth, velocity), a G-statistic test of independence (substrate), Bartlett's test for homogeneity of variance, [[Analysis of Variance]] (ANOVA), and Tukey's multiple range test.

**Aravaipa Creek results**

- Occupied waters were significantly shallower than unoccupied waters in spring and summer, but not in winter.
- Velocity did not differ between occupied and unoccupied waters except in summer, when fish occupied only slower currents.
- Fish occupied gravel-pebble substrate (mean indices approximately 2.5). Except in summer, they used larger substrate sizes than were available.
- The G-statistic for all seasons combined was 25.2, indicating that substrate use depended on substrate size.
- Depth and velocity subgroupings among the seven sample dates showed no evident seasonal pattern.
- Adults occupied slightly faster and deeper water than juveniles.
- In summer 1983, larvae occupied significantly (P < 0.01) shallower (<10 cm) and slower (<20 cm/s) water than adults, normally in stream margins.
- Juveniles used smaller substrate than adults in winter and summer, but not in spring.

**Upper Gila River basin results**

- Occupied sites were significantly shallower than unoccupied sites in three of five streams.
- Occupied sites had significantly greater velocity than unoccupied sites in four of five streams, which contrasts with Aravaipa Creek.
- Substrate indices of 3.0–3.4 (pebble-cobble) exceeded availability. Modal values and the G-statistic (54.51) nonetheless indicated that substrate use mostly tracked availability.
- Among streams, available depths and occupied depths did not differ significantly. Available velocities differed highly significantly (P < 0.01), with four velocity subgroupings and no clear pattern by stream size or location.
- Occupied waters in Aravaipa Creek were shallower than in the upper Gila streams, except in the San Francisco River. Substrate indices and modal substrate values were also lower in Aravaipa.
- The San Francisco River, the stream most similar in size and hydrology to Aravaipa, had the lowest substrate index recorded for the species in the upper Gila (2.8), and its habitat use did not differ significantly from Aravaipa.

**Interpretation and discussion**

- Habitat use reflects both stream size and habitat availability. Larger, faster streams have greater modal and flood flows, which favor deeper water and larger substrate.
- The species avoids swift current (>40 cm/s), occupying shallower, slower water or the lee side of pebble-cobble substrate.
- Estimated velocities varied widely across streams, possibly because the fish spend time near zero velocity on the bottom.
- The author notes that [[Minckley]] (1973) was partly correct in describing the species as restricted to "gravelly" riffles, but larger substrate was used in larger streams.
- Differences from another study ([[Turner and Tafanelli]], unpublished) were attributed to velocity measurement height: they measured at 0.6 depth, whereas this study measured 5 cm off the bottom, which yields lower velocities. Their mean velocities were about double those reported here.
- Other factors that may influence habitat use include food supply, temperature, interspecific interactions, and flow regime (see [[Moyle and Baltz]], 1985; [[Schlosser]], 1985).

**Conclusions and management recommendations**

- The loach minnow is widely adaptable to physical habitat across its range, but extensive habitat loss from dams, water diversion, and non-native fishes has caused range contraction and its threatened status.
- Conservation of remaining habitats, and inclusion of additional habitats, is needed. The author recommends a river-basin or species-group approach, following [[Johnson and Rinne]] (1982) and [[Rinne]] (1984).
- Sustained underwater observations are needed to determine how much time the fish spend in near-zero-velocity water compared with faster water.
