# Cibola High Levee Pond Draft Annual Report for FY-2002

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft annual report from the [[U.S. Geological Survey]] Fort Collins Science Center and the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (AZFRO-PA) documents fish sampling, limnology, spawning and recruitment at [[Cibola High Levee Pond]] (CHLP), a roughly 2-ha isolated former channel of the [[Colorado River]]. The pond was stocked as a grow-out facility for [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] between 1993 and 1996, and stocking and removal were suspended in December 1998 after both species were found reproducing. The authors describe this as unprecedented for these river-adapted fish in an isolated, non-flowing pond. In 2002 the community was estimated at roughly 6,000 bonytail (>15 cm) and 1,100 razorback sucker, and the report outlines plans for telemetry and predator-prey studies.

## Background & Description

CHLP was chemically renovated in 1993 and stocked by the Fish and Wildlife Service with 58,300 juvenile bonytail and 14,000 razorback suckers from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and Technology Center. Stocked fish were small, averaging 66 mm (bonytail) and 98 mm (razorback sucker) in total length. Fish reaching 30 cm were [[PIT Tagging|PIT tagged]] and relocated to [[Lake Havasu]] or the Colorado River; more than 225 bonytail and 760 razorback suckers were relocated between 1993 and 1998.

The pond is about 350 m long, up to 100 m wide, with a volume of roughly 25,000 m³ and a maximum depth slightly above 3 m. It lies between two parallel levees, and the river is generally about 0.5 m higher than the pond. Dense submergent vegetation includes [[Spiny Naiad]] (*Najas marina*) and [[Sago Pondweed]] (*Potamogeton pectinatus*). Riprap covers about 40% of the shoreline, and the northwestern shore is vegetated with cattail, saltcedar and mesquite. Sampling in FY-2002 used electrofishing, [[Trammel Nets]], [[Fyke Nets]], [[Minnow Traps]], [[Hoop Nets]] and [[Light Traps]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Water quality was described as normal. Temperatures ranged from 10.7 °C to 24.9 °C, with a rapid rise from 7 to 23 °C in two weeks in spring. Dissolved oxygen never fell below 7 mg/L, conductance averaged 1,182 µmhos/cm (range 1,167–1,199), and pH averaged 7.9 (7.6–8.1). Plankton tows were collected at 2 m but had not yet been analysed.

Invertebrates were abundant in light-trap samples and include [[Crayfish]], dragonfly, damselfly and mayfly nymphs, scuds and water boatmen. Crayfish were the most prevalent mortality factor for captured fish and were observed stalking fish schools. Beaver dens and underwater cover were used by small bonytail, which may reduce crayfish predation. Nonnative fish are scarce: a single 10-kg [[Channel Catfish]] was the only nonnative fish captured.

## Key Findings

- **Reproduction and recruitment:** Both species spawned in 2002. Razorback suckers spawned over gravel and bonytail over riprap. Eggs were found at both sites, and bonytail larvae were the only larvae collected. Bonytail produced young in each of 2000–2002, and thousands of year-class I bonytail were observed.
- **Razorback sucker recruitment failure:** No young-of-year razorback suckers were captured in 2002. The 15–17 cm cohort seen in 1999 was absent in 2001 and 2002, suggesting little or no recruitment in those years.
- **Population estimates (2002 season):** The pond held an estimated 7,842 bonytail (95% CI 5,951–11,635) and 987 razorback suckers (95% CI 844–1,190). Estimated density was about 4,350 fish/ha, with a standing crop of 635.6 kg/ha for fish >10 cm. Bonytail made up 88.5% of numbers, while razorback suckers made up 67% of biomass.
- **Catch effort:** Over 4,000 net hours captured nearly 1,300 bonytail and over 180 razorback suckers, with electrofishing adding 250 fish. Mortality was generally below 1%.
- **Gear performance:** Electrofishing was most effective (nearly 100 fish/hour) but biased toward larger fish. Trammel nets averaged 11 fish/net set/hour. Minnow traps had the lowest catch rate (0.001 fish/hr), and baiting increased only crayfish and tadpole numbers.
- **Light trap concerns:** Overnight light traps contained large numbers of aquatic insects, and odonates may prey on larvae. A set of 8 sets near spawning sites yielded no larvae, but two-hour sets on one night caught 5 larvae.
- **Spawning behaviour:** Ripe bonytail were captured during the first three weeks of April, and ripe razorback suckers were collected from November through May, with females from February to late April. Bonytail spawning appeared to be short and possibly nocturnal.
- **Ecological significance:** The authors argue that a non-flowing oxbow-like setting can support spawning and recruitment, suggesting oxbow communities may have been essential to these species' survival strategy.
- **Work plan for FY-2003:** Map bottom type and cover, establish four bench-marked shore sites, and conduct [[Telemetry]] studies with [[Arizona State University]] (razorback sucker in 2003, bonytail in FY-2004). A predator-prey study with [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] will test 16 nonnative fish species on razorback sucker larvae.
