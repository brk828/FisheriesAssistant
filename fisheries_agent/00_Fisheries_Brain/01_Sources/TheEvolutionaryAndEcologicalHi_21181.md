# Fossil Fish Fauna of the Rio Lerma Basin, Mexico: Evolutionary and Ecological History

#ichthyology #marine-biology #goodeidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 University of Michigan doctoral dissertation by Michael Leonard Smith examines the evolutionary and ecological history of the freshwater fish fauna of the [[Rio Lerma Basin]] in the western [[Mesa Central]] of Mexico. The work combines fossil fish collections from Late Miocene to Late Pleistocene localities in Jalisco with osteological comparisons to living fishes, and interprets the fauna's zoogeographic tracks and the drainage history of the region. The author argues that paleontological data can illuminate the ecology of living fishes, and that uniform ecological response is an untenable assumption in paleoecology. The dissertation describes several new fossil species, reconstructs former drainage connections between [[Lake Chapala]] and the Atotonilco-Zacoalco basin, and links the fauna's endemism to long isolation on the Mesa Central.

## Background & Description

The dissertation has a methodological preface and theoretical chapter on [[Uniform Ecological Response]] and [[Evolutionary Paleoecology]], arguing that fossil ecology cannot be assumed identical to modern ecology across evolutionary time. It notes that ecologists often under-use paleontological data, and that the fish fauna was studied in this work "not so much to learn what fish indicate about the past as to learn what the past can tell about fish."

The fauna is described as distinctive and highly endemic. [[Regan]] (1906-08) treated the Lerma fauna as one of several zoogeographic sub-regions of the Nearctic. The Goodeidae are described as viviparous, comprising roughly 35-40 species confined largely to the Mesa Central.

Fossil localities (seven listed in Figure 2) include:
- **Santa Rosa** (Miocene or Pliocene): laminated lacustrine shales with goodeids.
- **Atotonilco El Bajo, El Tecolote, Lake Chapala/Zacoalco exposures** (Late Pleistocene).
- **Jocotepec Quarry** (Pleistocene): the richest site, with nineteen fish species reported.
- **Ajijic** (Late Pleistocene): a large salmonid.
- **Chapala Formation type section** (Plio-Pleistocene).

Approximately half of the roughly fourteen known fossil freshwater fish localities in Mexico lie in the western Mesa Central.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Mesa Central is a plateau bounded by the Sierra Madre Oriental and Sierra Madre Occidental, shaped by Oligocene-Miocene volcanism and by strike-slip faulting associated with the Chapala-Acambay fault. The region developed as a series of endorheic basins that were later integrated into the Lerma and Rio Grande de Santiago systems. The [[Rio Grande de Santiago]] is eroding headward into the plateau and is inferred to have captured the Lerma system, lowering [[Lake Chapala]] levels and cutting off the outlet through the Jocotepec pass.

Depositional settings range from fine-grained, possibly stratified lake-bottom sediments (Santa Rosa, Chapala section, Jocotepec lacustrine beds) to cross-bedded sands and pumice conglomerates interpreted as fluvial or nearshore deposits. The author infers that some goodeids (e.g., [[Tapatia occidentalis]]) lived near lake margins where sand was ingested, based on gut-content grains.

The dissertation discusses regional climate: the Mesa Central may not have experienced pluvial episodes comparable to northern Mexico, though some climatic fluctuation is inferred from the former presence of salmonids and a centrarchid. The salmonid at Ajijic is interpreted as likely derived from a sea-run stock of [[Oncorhynchus]], implying past depression of sea-surface temperatures and a southward displacement of salmonid range.

## Key Findings

**Systematics and new taxa**
- Several new fossil species are described, including *Rhabdofario rugosus*, *Moxostoma ammophilum*, *Yuriria elliana*, *Algansea milleri*, and *Ictalurus spodius*.
- *Rhabdofario rugosus* is a lake-dwelling salmonine from late Pleistocene deposits near El Tecolote, associated with *Yuriria elliana* and *Ictalurus*.
- *Moxostoma ammophilum* is distinguished from the *M. robustum* species group by maxillary and opercular characters; the name refers to the sandy sediments where it occurs.
- *Ictalurus spodius* is a large extinct catfish, nearly one meter standard length by extrapolation, and is distinguished from the living *I. dugesi* mainly by pectoral-spine dentations.
- Principal components analysis of maxillae could not separate the living *Moxostoma* taxa, while fossil maxillae were partly distinct.

**Goodeid osteology**
- Osteological characters suggest generic groupings that do not match the Hubbs and Turner (1939) groupings based on ovarian and trophotaenial characters.
- Jaw suspension divides goodeids into a *Characodon* group (non-protrusile premaxillae) and a *Goodea* group (protrusile premaxillae).
- [[Tapatia occidentalis]] from Santa Rosa has 25-31 total vertebrae, the lowest count in the family, and is a maximum 20 mm standard length.

**Zoogeographic tracks**
- Three biogeographic tracks are recognized: a Pacific coastal track (salmonids, lampreys), a western mountain track (*Yuriria*, *Algansea*, related to *Gila*), and a Lerma-Rio Grande track (*Moxostoma*, *Micropterus*, *Ictalurus*).
- The fossil *Moxostoma ammophilum* provides independent support for the Lerma-Rio Grande track.
- The Lerma fauna has very limited affinities with the neotropical fauna; Cichlidae and Characidae are absent from the Mesa Central.

**Drainage history**
- The Mesa Central was probably drained westward through the Chapala basin and captured by the Rio Grande de Santiago.
- A late Pleistocene connection between Chapala and the Atotonilco-Zacoalco basin is inferred from geology and from shared species.
- Former outlets through the Rio Ameca and Rio Coahuayana are hypothesized.

**Extinction and climate**
- Eight of the fossil species are extinct; four are still represented in the area by living relatives.
- Extinct top carnivores (*Rhabdofario rugosus*, *Micropterus relictus*, the Ajijic salmonid) were associated with lacustrine sediments. Their loss may reflect shrinking aquatic habitat, with changing drainage patterns and possibly climate.

**Stated limitations**
- The author notes that inferences about the biology of fossil species are admittedly weak.
- Some Alvarez (1966, 1974) material from the Solórzano collection mixed two drainages and could not be assigned to one lake with confidence.
