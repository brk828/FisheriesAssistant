# Annotated Bibliography for the Woundfin (Plagopterus argentissimus)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #desert-ecology

## Executive Summary

This annotated bibliography, compiled by Terry J. Hickman in September 1987, provides a comprehensive review of 90 references concerning the [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), an endangered cyprinid minnow endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The woundfin is a streamlined, silvery minnow with a distinctive sharp dorsal spine, belonging to the unique tribe [[Plagopterini]] (which includes genera *Meda*, *Lepidomeda*, and *Plagopterus*). Historically distributed from the Salt and Verde Rivers junction near Tempe, Arizona, to the mouth of the Gila River at Yuma, and throughout the [[Virgin River]] basin, the species is now restricted to the Virgin River from above Lake Mead, Nevada, upstream to the Ash and La Verkin Creek confluence in Utah. Listed as endangered on October 13, 1970, the woundfin faces threats primarily from habitat modification, water diversion, and competition with exotic species, particularly the [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*).

## Background & Description

The woundfin (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) was originally described by E.D. Cope in 1874, with the scientific name derived from Greek: *argentissimus* meaning "most silvery," *plagos* meaning "to wound," and *pterus* meaning "fin." The species is characterized by its streamlined body, flat head, conspicuous sharp dorsal spine, and complete lack of scales. Adults typically reach 55-60mm total length during their first growing season, with mean growth rates of approximately 3mm/week during the first four months.

The [[Virgin River]], the species' last stronghold, originates from two major forks in Utah (North and East Forks) at elevations between 8,000-10,000 feet, flowing through southwestern Utah, northwestern Arizona, and southern Nevada before emptying into Lake Mead at approximately 1,200 feet elevation. The woundfin's upper distributional limit occurs at approximately 3,000 feet elevation below the confluence of the North and East Forks near Springdale, Utah.

Taxonomically, the woundfin belongs to the tribe Plagopterini within the subfamily Leuciscinae of family Cyprinidae. The tribe is notable as one of the few North American cyprinid groups not known to hybridize with other genera. All Plagopterine species examined possess a diploid chromosome number of 50, with the woundfin's karyotype comprising nine pairs of metacentric, 13 pairs of submetacentric-subtelocentric, and three pairs of acrocentric chromosomes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The woundfin exhibits remarkable adaptations to the extreme fluctuating conditions characteristic of desert river systems. The species demonstrates high tolerance for elevated temperatures (collected in ranges from 7°C to 35.5°C, with optimum temperatures between 25-28°C) and salinities (up to 12,000 µmhos/cm). Critical thermal maxima reach 39.5°C at 25°C acclimation, indicating exceptional thermal tolerance among Virgin River fishes.

Habitat preferences show strong association with runs (75% of collections) over shifting sand substrates, with lesser occurrence in riffles (20%) and pools (5%). Adults prefer swift water (average velocity 0.42 m/sec) of medium depth (average 43 cm), while young-of-year occupy shallow, moderately flowing water adjacent to stream banks or in eddy currents below rocks and brush. The species commonly inhabits areas with large amounts of organic debris mixed with sandy bottom substrates.

The woundfin's diet reflects omnivorous feeding habits, consuming benthic and drift invertebrates (particularly simuliid larvae during day, *Hydropsyche* spp. at night, and ephemeropteran larvae throughout 24-hour periods) and plant matter. Daily ration estimates reach approximately 8% body weight at temperatures varying between 15-25°C. The relatively continuous feeding pattern minimizes competition with other species during periods of minimum food abundance.

Reproduction occurs primarily in spring when water temperatures reach 25-26°C, with spawning aggregations forming in pools up to 1m depth. Groups of 15-20 individuals spawn over 5-10 cm rock substrate in currents of 0.06-0.09 m/sec at depths of 10 cm. Eggs are adhesive, dropping between rock interstices where they adhere to undersides. Development period spans 10-11 days, with hatchlings measuring 8-11mm. Fecundity data indicates females produce approximately 1,500 eggs. Evidence suggests females do not spawn until their third year while males become capable after their first year. A smaller secondary spawn by one-year-old fish may occur in July or August, with major adult mortality following reproduction.

The species faces significant threats from habitat modification, including water diversions for irrigation, municipal and industrial uses. [[La Verkin Springs]], a system of over 100 thermal springs, profoundly affects the Virgin River by increasing conductivity and temperature while decreasing pH and dissolved oxygen, historically preventing upstream woundfin dispersal. Introduction and establishment of exotic species, particularly the red shiner, poses serious competitive threats. The [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), likely introduced through red shiners, represents an additional parasitic threat to endemic Virgin River fishes.

## Key Findings

**Distribution and Status**: The woundfin has experienced dramatic range contraction from its historical distribution throughout the lower Colorado River basin (Salt River, Gila River, Colorado River, Virgin River) to current restriction within the Virgin River system from La Verkin Springs downstream to Lake Mead. The species has been extirpated from all areas except the Virgin River basin due to drought, agricultural operations, water shortage and diversion, and exotic species introductions. Transplant attempts to four localities (Hassayampa River, Salt River, Sycamore Creek, Paria River) between 1969-1972 proved unsuccessful.

**Flow Requirements and Reproductive Success**: Multiple studies (1977-1986) demonstrate critical relationships between streamflow and woundfin reproductive success. Poor recruitment occurs with spring flows below approximately 130 cfs, while best recruitment associates with spring flows of 200-800 cfs. Flows exceeding 1,000 cfs also correlate with relatively poor recruitment. The 1977 record low flow year resulted in poor to non-existent reproduction throughout most of the species' range, with population effects extending beyond 1980. Recommended minimum flows range from 80-100 cfs during April through mid-July and 60 cfs for remainder of summer above the Virgin Gorge, with 80-100 cfs recommended for winter. Periodic floods throughout the year are considered essential for maintaining suitable woundfin populations.

**Population Structure and Monitoring**: Long-term monitoring (1977-1986) reveals significant temporal and spatial variation in woundfin abundance and recruitment. The species comprised 30-100% of fish collections in lower mainstream areas (averaging less than 50%), 50% in middle reaches, and less than 33% in upper mainstream. La Verkin Creek populations generally comprised less than 50% of fish fauna. Population structure analysis indicates two- and three-year-old fish serve as primary spawners in spring. Length-frequency analysis identifies fish less than 60mm as young-of-year, though some 50-60mm individuals may represent previous year's spawn.

**Water Development Impacts**: The [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] project, completed in 1985 as substitute for the abandoned [[Warner Valley Project]], specified flow requirements judged essential for woundfin survival. However, these requirements were apparently violated for at least 112 days during June-October 1985 and 108 days during June-September 1986. During 1985-1986, the red shiner became dominant throughout the Arizona/Nevada reach for the first time, with concurrent native species declines. Woundfin population size declined dramatically in more than 50% of remaining range following Quail Creek Reservoir construction.

**Exotic Species Interactions**: The red shiner, first documented in Virgin River at Bunkerville Bridge, Nevada in 1964, has expanded throughout the system, likely migrating upstream from Lake Mead impoundment. Below Mesquite Diversion, Nevada, red shiners frequently dominate fish assemblages where woundfin occur but remain less abundant. Food partitioning studies reveal biologically significant overlap between red shiner and woundfin on four of sixteen possible occasions, with competition potentially more severe below Mesquite diversion during periods of reduced food abundance. Thermal preference studies suggest red shiners may have competitive advantage at higher temperatures (above 25°C), while woundfin may have advantage at lower temperatures.

**Habitat Quality Indicators**: The best woundfin habitat occurs in relatively unmodified river segments where native fishes comprise the overwhelming majority of fauna. Stations above Quail Creek Reservoir, below Hurricane Bridge, and above Washington Fields Diversion consistently provide optimal habitat for all size classes. The 10-mile stretch between Ash/La Verkin Creek confluence and Washington Fields Diversion represents the least modified and most productive section in Utah. Areas below major irrigation diversions (Washington Fields, Mesquite, Riverside) show consistently poor recruitment and reduced populations, reflecting cumulative impacts of flow reduction and habitat degradation.

**Parasites and Disease**: Seven parasite species were documented in woundfin: yellow grub (*Posthodiplostomum minimum*), Asian fish tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), *Diplostomum spathaceum* metacercariae, *Gyrodactylus* sp., anchor worm (*Lernaea cyprinacea*), *Trichodina* sp., and ICH ciliate (*Ichthyophthirius multifilis*). Parasite loads correlate with water quality and habitat disturbance, with highest numbers and frequencies in "disturbed" sites. Low flows and increased total dissolved solids associate with higher parasite frequency and mean numbers. The Asian tapeworm poses potentially severe threats to endemic Virgin River fishes, particularly the [[Virgin River Chub]].

**Conservation Implications**: The coexistence of water development and woundfin persistence requires careful planning and comprehensive understanding of species requirements. Critical factors include: (1) maintenance of adequate flows throughout the year with periodic floods; (2) preservation of relatively unmodified river segments; (3) control of exotic species introductions and establishment; (4) monitoring and management of parasite loads; and (5) protection of water quality parameters. The species' adaptation to highly variable, turbid, sandy-bottom rivers with high temperatures and salinities represents specialized requirements that become increasingly difficult to maintain under anthropogenic modification.
