# Distribution and Evolution of the North American Catostomid Fishes of the Subgenus *Pantosteus* (Genus *Catostomus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 monograph by Gerald Ray Smith (Miscellaneous Publications, Museum of Zoology, University of Michigan, No. 129) revises the western North American suckers of the subgenus [[Pantosteus]], long considered a genus in its own right. Using morphometric and meristic data from 25 population groups, osteology, and hybridization evidence, the author recognizes six species within the subgenus and concludes that it should be ranked as a subgenus of [[Catostomus]] rather than a separate genus. The distribution of these fishes is closely tied to mountain-stream habitats and to the history of drainage basins in western North America. The study also documents extensive interspecific hybridization, particularly between Pantosteus and Catostomus species in the Columbia and Colorado basins.

## Background & Description

*Pantosteus* suckers are generally small freshwater fishes with a flattened head, a sharp-edged hard keratinized scraping edge on the jaws, large lips with papillae, and a long intestine (at least four times body length in adults) with a black peritoneum. Adults range from about 100 mm to approximately 300 mm in total length. Lifespans are reported as roughly two to four years.

The taxonomic history is complicated. Earlier authors placed these fishes in *Pantosteus* on the basis of the reduced or closed frontoparietal [[Fontanelle]], a feature Smith found to be variable and only partly diagnostic. The author notes that the fontanelle has been the key character in early classifications, but other meristic and osteological traits show considerable overlap with *Catostomus*. Eight nominal species had been recognized by earlier workers; Smith reduces the group to six valid species, with *Catostomus* (*Pantosteus*) *plebeius*, *C. platyrhynchus*, *C. discobolus*, *C. clarkii*, *C. santaanae* and *C. columbianus* forming the subgenus.

Key diagnostic traits include a sharp central deflection of the gnathic ramus of the dentary, enlarged and robust jaw bones, a dorsal part of the pterotic that forms a vertical ridge, and a deep, short caudal peduncle in many forms, although peduncle depth varies strongly with habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Pantosteus fishes are closely associated with cool, clear waters with moderate to swift current and rocky substrates. Mountains and drainage divides act as the primary barriers isolating populations, and stream capture is comparatively uncommon. Intermittent streams, waterfalls, and warm, sluggish lower reaches create additional barriers.

Smith documents a strong relation between caudal peduncle depth and current velocity. Populations from torrential canyon reaches of the [[Colorado River]], Green River and Virgin River show slender peduncles and large fins, an adaptation to swift water, whereas fish from slower mountain streams and headwater tributaries have deeper peduncles. Diet is largely algae, diatoms and detritus, with small invertebrates taken secondarily; sand and silt are ingested and pass through the long gut.

Spawning occurs in spring and early summer, with timing varying by latitude and elevation. Males develop breeding tubercles on the anal fin, caudal peduncle and paired fins.

## Key Findings

- **Species recognized:** six valid species within *Pantosteus*: *C. plebeius* (Rio Grande and Mexican plateau drainages), *C. platyrhynchus* (Great Basin, upper Colorado, Columbia, Missouri, Saskatchewan and Fraser drainages), *C. discobolus* (Colorado above Grand Canyon, upper Snake, northern Bonneville basin), *C. clarkii* (Gila, Virgin, Bill Williams and White rivers), *C. santaanae* (Santa Ana and Los Angeles-area streams) and *C. columbianus* (Columbia River system below the Snake River falls, Fraser River).
- **Generic status:** Smith concludes that *Pantosteus* is phylogenetically nested within *Catostomus* and should be treated as a subgenus, because of widespread hybridization and intermediate morphology, particularly in *C. columbianus*.
- **Vertebral counts:** post-Weberian vertebrae range from about 38 to 46 across the subgenus, with *C. santaanae* and *C. plebeius* at the low end and *C. discobolus* and some *C. platyrhynchus* at the high end.
- **Predorsal scales** vary widely, from about 13 to 75 across the subgenus; the largest scales occur in the Gila River system and smallest counts in the southern populations of *C. plebeius*.
- **Gill rakers:** *C. discobolus* and *C. clarkii* have more numerous, finer gill rakers than *C. plebeius*, *C. santaanae*, *C. platyrhynchus* and *C. columbianus*; gill raker number increases with body size.
- **Caudal peduncle depth** ranges from about 6 to 12 percent of standard length and tracks current speed; slender forms characterize swift canyon populations of the Green, Colorado and Virgin rivers.
- **Hybridization:** Smith documents natural hybrids between *Pantosteus* and *Catostomus* species in at least 15 localities, including the Columbia, Colorado and Snake drainages. Patterns of character variation are interpreted as evidence for limited introgression rather than complete hybrid swarms.
- **Evolutionary history:** the subgenus is inferred to have arisen from a *Catostomus*-like ancestor during Tertiary and Pleistocene drainage changes in the Colorado Plateau and Great Basin. Smith proposes that *C. columbianus* and *C. platyrhynchus* were early offshoots and that *C. discobolus* and *C. clarkii* diverged along the Colorado River system.
- **Introgression case study:** populations of *C. discobolus* in the Little Colorado River show *C. plebeius*-like characters (lip and jaw development, vertebral counts) that the author attributes to historical stream capture and introgressive hybridization.
- **Conservation/taxonomic note:** several populations, including those in the Nevada Great Basin and the Harney Basin, are described as taxonomically unclear and warrant further study.
