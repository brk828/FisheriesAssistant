# Effects of Fish Toxicants on the Limnology of Three Cold-Water Ponds, Montana

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1962 study by Robert E. Wollitz, published in the *Proceedings of the Montana Academy of Sciences*, examines how commercial fish toxicants alter the physical, chemical and biological character of three adjacent [[Three Forks Ponds]] (East, Middle and West) east of Three Forks, Montana. Sampling ran from April 1956 to April 1958 in three phases: pre-treatment baseline, toxicant application, and post-treatment recovery. Four toxicants were used: [[Toxaphene]] (East and Middle ponds), [[Chem-Fish Special]] and [[Pro-Noxfish]] (both [[Rotenone]]-based), with the Middle Pond requiring three sequential treatments to achieve a kill. The fish-kill work was aimed at removing rough and panfish species, while the study tracked effects on [[Phytoplankton]], [[Zooplankton]] and bottom and plant-inhabiting invertebrates.

The document also contains a second, shorter paper (pp. 82–92) by Donald J. Forrester on land snails as possible intermediate hosts of *Protostrongylus stilesi*, a lungworm of [[Bighorn Sheep]] in western Montana. It is a literature review and collection-design introduction, with no results reported.

## Background & Description

Fish toxicants were increasingly used in fisheries management by the late 1950s, but their effects on fish-food organisms were poorly documented. Wollitz's stated aim was to identify important changes in the physical, chemical and biological characteristics of ponds resulting from toxicant use.

The three study ponds were dredged gravel pits, with water supplied mainly by seepage and springs and no surface inlets or outlets:

| Pond | Surface area | Volume | Max depth | Mean depth | Bottom |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| East | 12.9 acres | ~100 acre-feet | 12 ft | 7.9 ft | Coarse gravel and muck; not dredged since 1930s |
| Middle | 20.3 acres | ~183 acre-feet | 18 ft | 9 ft | Mostly muck, some sand and gravel |
| West | 12.8 acres | ~132 acre-feet | 19 ft | 10 ft | Fibrous peat; periodically dredged since 1936 |

Fish stocking history was recorded in part. About 8,000 four-inch [[Largemouth Bass]] were planted in the East Pond in 1938, with another stocking in 1944. The Middle Pond received 2,440 five-inch [[Rainbow Trout]] in 1941, but no trout were found during the study. Fish presumed introduced by the public (possibly via bait fishermen) included [[Carp]], [[White Sucker]] and [[Black Bullhead]] in the Middle Pond.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical setting.** Frequent winds and shallow depths prevented marked thermal stratification. Surface temperatures ranged from 60 to 78 °F in summer 1956. Ice formed in early to mid-November and broke up in late March. Pre-treatment, visibility was usually under five feet and turbidity averaged 8.2 ppm in the East Pond. Oxygen saturation was usually below 100 percent and lowest in winter. Phenolphthalein alkalinity ranged from 0 to 18 ppm (East Pond), methyl orange alkalinity from 142 to 330 ppm, and pH from about 7.0 to 9.0 across ponds. Nitrates and phosphates were low (NO₂ roughly 0.07–0.21 ppm; PO₄ 0.00–1.00 ppm).

**Vegetation and biota.** The East Pond had emergent [[Great Bullrush]] and cattail along about 40 percent of its shoreline. Submerged growths of muskgrass (*Chara*) and water milfoil were dense. The Middle Pond had similar emergent vegetation with muskgrass, sago pondweed and milfoil over much of the bottom. The West Pond had emergent vegetation only on the east shore and was dominated by muskgrass.

**Fish community before treatment.** The East Pond held [[Bluegill]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Carp]], longnose and white suckers, and [[Golden Shiner]]. The Middle Pond held largemouth bass, black crappie, yellow perch, white sucker, carp and black bullhead. The West Pond held bluegill, largemouth bass and yellow perch, presumed to have been transplanted from the Middle and East ponds.

**Toxicant mechanisms.** The authors attribute the post-treatment turbidity decline mainly to removal of "rough fish" that stir up bottom sediment. Dense vegetation in the Middle Pond may have absorbed toxaphene and released it later, prolonging toxicity. Literature cited by the author notes that aquatic plants accumulate toxaphene (Kallman et al., 1962), and that light and warm temperatures help detoxify water (Hooper and Grzenda, 1957).

## Key Findings

**Methods overview.** Plankton were sampled with a 1.2-liter Kemmerer bottle every two weeks in open water and monthly under ice. Bottom organisms were collected with an Ekman dredge (1.5 sq ft samples at 10–15 ft). Plant-inhabiting organisms were sampled with a metal cylinder (21 × 6 inches) at 12–24 inches depth. Toxicant penetration was checked using healthy fish held in wire cages at various depths.

**East Pond (toxaphene, 16 July 1957).**
- 60% emulsifiable toxaphene applied at 0.13 ppm, from 6:15 to 9:00 a.m.
- Fish distress began 45 minutes after application; the first deaths were small largemouth bass and bluegill.
- Caged fish died between 5 and 14 hours. All caged fish were dead by the next morning.
- Six 48-hour gill-net sets one month later found no living fish, and the kill was assumed complete.
- Recovery of dead fish (68 man-hours) yielded 325.0 fish/acre (78.2 lb/acre). Bluegill, yellow perch, largemouth bass and black crappie made up 94% of the number and 37% of the weight. Carp, longnose and white suckers made up about 5% of the number and 63% of the weight.
- The water was considered nontoxic by November 1957.

**Middle Pond (three treatments).**
- *Chem-Fish Special* (5.5% rotenone, 11% other cube extractives, 83.5% inert ingredients) at about 0.7 ppm, 17 July 1957. Live fish remained, mainly among vegetation.
- *Pro-Noxfish* (2.5% rotenone, 2.5% sulfoxide) at about 0.95 ppm, 17 September 1957. Centrarchids were eliminated, but black bullheads and white suckers survived.
- *Toxaphene* at 0.17 ppm, 17 October 1957. Three weeks later, four 48-hour and two 72-hour gill-net sets found no living fish.
- Dead-fish recovery (60 man-hours) yielded 1,706.4 fish/acre (53.6 lb/acre), dominated by bluegill (59.9%) and yellow perch (36.7%).
- The pond was still toxic to trout on 31 May 1958, about 7.5 months after treatment.

**West Pond (Pro-Noxfish, 18 July 1957).**
- Applied at about 0.7 ppm, with distress noticed after 10 minutes and near-total mortality within 23 hours.
- Water was nontoxic 17 days later, and six 24-hour and three 48-hour gill-net sets found no live fish.
- Recovery (23 man-hours) yielded 463.2 fish/acre (55.1 lb/acre), with yellow perch at 83.2% of the number.

**Water quality after treatment.**
- Turbidity fell from pre-treatment averages to below 3.0 ppm almost immediately in the East Pond, and below 1.0 ppm by winter 1957–58.
- Oxygen exceeded 100 percent saturation at all times in the East Pond. Under ice, values of 121 and 145 percent were recorded on 10 January and 12 February 1958.
- Middle Pond visibility increased from 8.8 ft to 14.3 ft after Chem-Fish Special.
- Nitrate and phosphate levels showed no important change after treatment.

**Phytoplankton.**
- The East Pond was dominated by *Dinobryon* (Chrysophyta), with diatoms in nearly all samples. Ceratium (Pyrrophyta) was the most common dinoflagellate.
- Toxaphene sharply reduced *Dinobryon* and eliminated *Ceratium* completely. *Ceratium* first reappeared on 24 March 1958.
- Rotenone-based treatments had little effect on most phytoplankton, consistent with earlier derris studies.

**Zooplankton.**
- All Cladocera and Copepoda disappeared after toxaphene in the East Pond and did not return until 20 September 1957. At that time the water was still toxic to trout.
- Nauplii did not appear until 4 November, suggesting that advanced copepodids may have emerged from cysts.
- In the Middle Pond, Pro-Noxfish eliminated Cladocera, and toxaphene removed all zooplankton. The first recovery (a single *Polyarthra*) came 85 days later, and *Diaphanosoma* was last noted at 175 days.

**Bottom and plant-inhabiting organisms.**
- Tendipedidae (midge larvae) and Tubificidae (oligochaete worms) were the dominant benthos in all ponds.
- In the East Pond, neither family declined after toxaphene. The author suggests inflowing springs as a possible explanation, and notes this disagrees with other studies.
- In the Middle Pond, Tendipedidae fell 80% after 29 days and 95% after 162 days following toxaphene. Tubificidae were about twice as abundant after treatment.
- Gastropods (*Gyraulus*, *Physa*, *Lymnaea*) were dominant among plant-inhabiting organisms in the East Pond and became more abundant after treatment.

**Principal conclusions.** Toxaphene and rotenone-based toxicants effectively eliminated the target fish, but their effects on fish-food organisms varied widely. Toxaphene produced the most lasting zooplankton and benthos disruption, and the prolonged toxicity in the Middle Pond illustrates how vegetation and ice cover can extend the period in which fish cannot be safely stocked.

**Forrester (1962) on snail hosts.** The study planned to collect land snails and slugs across ten bighorn sheep ranges in western Montana to determine which mollusk genera serve as intermediate hosts for *Protostrongylus stilesi*. The review cites earlier work naming *Helicella ericetorum*, *H. obvia* and *H. bollii* as hosts of a related sheep lungworm, and additional candidate snails including *Succinea altaica*, *Pupilla muscorum* and *Vallonia costata*. The paper reports no field results.
