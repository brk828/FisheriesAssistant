# Backwater Nursery Habitat Use by Young-of-the-Year Chubs (Gila spp.) and Colorado Pikeminnow (Ptychocheilus lucius) in Desolation and Gray Canyons of the Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 2000 final report from the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] assesses backwater nursery habitat use by young-of-the-year (YOY) [[Chubs]] (*Gila* spp.) and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] in [[Desolation Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]] of the Green River, Utah, during 1994–1996. The work was funded under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (RIP) for Endangered Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin (Project FG-39) and contributes to a new Biological Opinion for [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] operations. A total of 729 backwater and low-velocity habitats were sampled, and fish were collected by seining. Chubs and pikeminnow showed only weak or inconsistent habitat preferences, while flow and temperature conditions strongly influenced recruitment and growth.

## Background & Description

Four endangered fishes of the Colorado River system, [[Bonytail Chub]], Colorado Pikeminnow, [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], were listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1974. Poor survival of young fish and low recruitment are considered primary reasons for their decline. Dam construction has altered flow patterns and magnitudes, with negative effects on early survival.

The *Gila* spp. complex includes bonytail, humpback and [[Roundtail Chub]], plus hybrids. Humpback chub are an obligate riverine species requiring temperatures above 16°C to spawn. Colorado pikeminnow larvae drift long distances to reach nursery habitats; two primary spawning areas are the Yampa River above its confluence with the Green River and [[Three Fords Rapid]] in Desolation Canyon.

The study objectives were to determine reproductive success, growth and backwater habitat use of early life stages; continue PIT tagging of juvenile and adult *Gila* spp.; and monitor trends in the non-target fish community. Study reaches were the canyon segments of the middle Green River, Desolation Canyon (RK 345.9–249.4) and Gray Canyon (RK 249.4–212.4). Desolation Canyon is deep and narrow below RK 321.8, with sand/silt and cobble/boulder substrates, while Gray Canyon is restricted by perched floodplains and sandstone cliffs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Backwaters were sampled four times yearly (post swim-up, fall, post-winter) with a 3 mm mesh seine, and habitat variables (length, width, depth, temperature, turbidity, aspect, cover) were measured. Each backwater was classified by formation type: [[Secondary Channel]], [[Migrating Sand Wave]], [[Horseshoe Vortex]], [[Shoreline Eddy]], [[Constricted Reach Eddy]], [[Low Velocity Flow]] and [[Isolated Pool]]. Analyses included linear regression, chi-square and contingency tests, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and [[Discriminant Function Analysis]]. Sampling was limited by high water in 1995–1996 and by the July 1994 change in protocol.

**Flow and habitat.** Shoreline eddies were the most common backwater type (36.2%), followed by secondary channel (26.5%) and constricted reach eddy (25.9%) habitats. Eddy-formed backwaters appeared stable across flow events, whereas migrating sand waves and secondary channels were sensitive to flood flows.

**Fish community.** Fourteen species were captured (six native, eight nonnative). Redshiner and fathead minnow were the most numerous species in both canyons. Four larval razorback suckers were found in Desolation Canyon in June and July 1994.

## Key Findings

**Chubs**
- Chubs showed no preference for backwater formation type (chi-square non-significant for all sampling periods).
- Backwaters used by chubs were significantly longer, wider, deeper and more turbid than unused ones (pooled data), but preferences shifted between months.
- Discriminant models could not separate chub-used from unused backwaters (pooled classification accuracy no better than 4%; error rates 30–42% for monthly samples).
- Chub recruitment was highest in 1994 (mean CPUE 10.31 for the 1994 cohort), dropped to 0.31 in 1995 and rose to 3.28 in 1996.
- Species composition of the 1993 cohort (n=47): 57.4% roundtail, 36.2% humpback, 6.4% possible bonytail. Overall fin ray counts (n=246): 65.4% roundtail, 32.9% humpback, 1.6% bonytail.
- Mean YOY chub length reached 60.8 mm by September 1994, versus 33.8 mm in 1995.

**Colorado Pikeminnow**
- Pikeminnow used scour-formed and migrating sand wave backwaters and avoided constricted reach eddies and shoreline eddies.
- Used backwaters were larger, with greater volume and less cover; discriminant models were more successful than for chubs but still weak (explaining at most 28% of variance for monthly data).
- The 1996 cohort was the most abundant (n=1,729); catches were the highest recorded in Middle Green River nursery studies, although the Ouray reach had the second-lowest CPUE in 1996, contradicting the trend.
- A strong negative correlation (r² = 0.49) was found between pikeminnow CPUE and the duration of flows above 75% of the peak spring flow.
- Mean YOY length was 57.7 mm in 1994, 24.8 mm in 1995 and 38.4 mm in 1996.

**Flow, Temperature and Community**
- Peak-flow duration and extended high flows were linked to poor recruitment in 1995; warmer water and shorter peaks in 1996 favored spawning and growth.
- Chub CPUE correlated weakly and positively with five nonnative species (e.g., red shiner, fathead minnow), suggesting that chub gains may be offset by competition or predation.
- The report concludes that flow manipulation alone is insufficient to control nonnative fishes.

**Recommendations**
- Avoid prolonged high flows and cold temperatures during peak runoff; achieve summer base flows by mid-July.
- Maintain late-season flows between roughly 2,000 and 4,000 cfs to stabilize backwater and shoreline habitats.
- Continue monitoring spawning sites in Desolation Canyon and the [[Three Fords Spawning Site]], and survey YOY fish twice yearly (early August and late September).
- Future chub studies should target low-velocity shoreline habitats, and nonnative fish recruitment and interactions with native species should be investigated.
