# Hybridization of Four Species of Sunfishes (Centrarchidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin by William F. Childers examines hybridization among four sympatric sunfishes of the tribe [[Lepomini]]: [[Red-ear Sunfish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Warmouth]]. Using laboratory stripping experiments, pond isolation experiments, and observations of reproduction over 1958–1964, the study measured gamete longevity, embryonic viability, hybrid sex ratios, fecundity, growth, and vulnerability to angling. Several F1 hybrids proved fertile and capable of forming large F2 populations, challenging the prevailing belief that male hybrid sunfish are sterile.

## Background & Description

Hybridization is common in plants and considered an important speciation mechanism there, but in fishes natural hybrids are rare: Slastenenko (1957) listed only 167 known natural interspecific fish hybrids worldwide, about 90 percent from freshwater and about two-thirds of those from North America. Hubbs (1955) suggested introgressive hybridization has been significant in speciation within the Lepomini.

The [[Centrarchidae]] (sunfishes) are freshwater fishes of temperate North America, centered on the Mississippi River basin. The family comprises 27 species; the Lepomini tribe includes 11 species of *Lepomis* and related genera. Reported natural Lepomini hybrids number at least 21 of 55 morphologically distinguishable F1 combinations (21 of 110 theoretical crosses, given that reciprocal crosses cannot be told apart morphologically).

The four study species were chosen for local availability, sport-fishing importance, taxonomic relationships, and contrasting morphology, habitat use, and reproductive behavior. Bluegill, green sunfish, and warmouth are native to east-central Illinois; red-ear sunfish, a more southern species, was introduced there in 1946. All four overlap geographically and are sympatric in the region.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preferences:** Warmouth are mainly pond and lake fish with a preference for soft, mucky, vegetated bottoms, and are less tolerant of turbidity. Green sunfish are tolerant of turbidity and siltation, colonize small temporary streams, and often produce stunted populations when they reach new waters first, though their largest populations occur in clear, low-competition habitats. Bluegill and red-ear sunfish reach their largest populations in clear lakes and ponds; red-ear congregate around brush, stumps, and logs more than bluegill.
- **Lepomini evolution:** The paper proposes that the Centrarchidae date from the early Cenozoic, with the Mississippi basin as a probable center of origin. Rocky Mountain uplift from the Miocene or early Pliocene isolated western centrarchids, and fossil warmouth and black crappie occur in middle Pliocene deposits in Kansas.
- **Spawning and nesting:** Spawning ran from mid-May to August or September. Males built saucer-shaped nests in water usually less than 3 feet deep. Red-ear, bluegill, and green sunfish nested in colonies, while warmouth were solitary. Males fanned eggs and fry and defended them vigorously.
- **Water source for experiments:** Well water from Parkhill's Lake Park Subdivision was aerated and charcoal-filtered; total dissolved minerals were 239 ppm, hardness 148 ppm as CaCO3, and pH 8.8 (analysis by the [[Illinois Water Survey]]).

## Key Findings

- **Gamete longevity limits sperm drift:** Average functional life was about 94, 60, and 47 minutes for warmouth, bluegill, and green sunfish eggs, respectively, and approximately 1 minute for sperm of all three species (warmouth 1.1, green sunfish 1.0, bluegill 1.0 minutes). The author concludes that most hybrids arise from interspecific pair formation rather than sperm drift.
- **Stripping experiments:** Eggs and sperm from the four species were paired in 16 combinations (11 experiments, 132 samples) yielding the four pure species and 12 hybrid types. Hatching success was 39% (red-ear), 58% (warmouth), 78% (green sunfish), and 87% (bluegill). Warmouth × bluegill and warmouth × red-ear crosses were 100% lethal in the sense that over 90% of larvae were abnormal; warmouth × green sunfish was partially lethal (only 55% of hybrid fry normal versus 75% of pure green sunfish).
- **Hybrid viability:** Apart from the warmouth-female crosses, no hybrid type differed significantly from its maternal parent in hatching; BR and GR hybrid fry were significantly longer-bodied than pure red-ear fry.
- **Developmental thresholds:** The mean alpha temperature threshold of development for 12 kinds of zygotes was 18.3 °C (64.9 °F), and about 280 centigrade developmental units (500 Fahrenheit units) were needed for 50 percent hatching.
- **Isolation experiments:** Of 32 pond isolation experiments (1957–1963), 27 were reported in detail. R×G, G×B, and W×G crosses hybridized every time they were tested. Five ponds were excluded for contamination or drying. Female bluegills spawned with red-ear males whose scarlet opercular tabs had been removed, but not with intact males (a single experiment in 1964 supporting the role of tab color in isolation).
- **Fertility of hybrids:** Contrary to Hubbs & Hubbs (1933), the author found that many F1 hybrids are fertile. Most F1 types produced abundant F2 generations in ponds without other fish, and backcrosses and outcrosses were successful.
- **Sex ratios:** RB, BR, and BG F1 hybrids were 97% male; WG 84% male; RG, GB, and BW about 70% male; GR and RW about 50%; GW only 16% male. The author notes that Haldane's rule suggests the female is heterogametic in bluegill, red-ear, and green sunfish, and the male is heterogametic in warmouth.
- **Population control with bass:** RG, GB, and BW F1 hybrids produced abundant F2 generations without other fish or with hybrid crappies, but only scarce F2 hybrids when stocked with [[Largemouth Bass]]. Preliminary trials suggested certain hybrid–bass combinations provide better fishing than bass with parent species.
- **Growth:** Because stocking densities and pond conditions varied, no valid growth comparisons were possible between hybrids and parents. In two controlled trials, average length increases of hybrids did not differ significantly from those of parental species. The largest hybrid captured (May 1965) was a 6-year-old GB F1 at 310 mm and 965 g.
- **Hemoglobin:** BW, GW, and WG F1 hybrids showed 25–40% of hemoglobin with electrophoretic properties differing from the parents, plus greater heme-heme interaction and better gas transport, which the author interprets as hybrid vigor.
- **Angling vulnerability:** Hybrids were easily caught. At Lincoln Trail State Park, anglers removed about 10,800 naturally produced BG or GB F1 hybrids in 1958, with 82% taken in the first 3 days. At Ridge Lake, 1,209 hybrids were removed in 1962, with 88% taken in 15 days.
- **Management implication:** Overpopulation of sunfish is the greatest management problem in Illinois lakes with largemouth bass. The author proposes hybrids stocked with bass as a possible remedy.
