# Growth and Survival of Walleye in Rearing Ponds near Lake Winnipegosis, Manitoba

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This Canadian Technical Report from the [[Department Of Fisheries And Oceans]] (Western Region, Winnipeg) evaluates small, shallow [[Rearing Ponds]] on the shores of [[Lake Winnipegosis]] as nursery areas for young-of-the-year [[Walleye]]. The study, conducted in 1977, examined growth, survival, food habits and escapement of walleye stocked in two ponds, [[Coleman Pond]] and [[Freshwater Pond]]. Stocking was at 1,700 and 1,900 fry/ha. Estimated survival in Coleman pond from May to August was 56.5%. Fish reached 95 mm (Coleman) and 114 mm (Freshwater) fork length by autumn release. The authors conclude that growth matched or exceeded that in comparable rearing ponds and natural lakes, and they propose methods (culvert currents, electric seine) to improve fingerling escapement before freeze-up.

## Background & Description

Commercial walleye harvests from Lake Winnipegosis averaged about 145,000 kg annually in 1971–1978, only 23% of the 1946–1955 production. To rehabilitate the fishery, ponds and back bays have been used as nursery areas since 1971. Of 10 ponds developed by 1977, five were stocked that year: [[Chartrand Pond]], [[Pacheta Pond]], [[Kelly Pond]], Coleman and Freshwater.

The ponds are shallow (maximum depth 2.5 m). Coleman pond covers 117 ha with a maximum depth of 2.0 m; Freshwater covers 105 ha with a maximum depth of about 1.5 m. Both were formed by boulder/rubble dikes across bay mouths, fitted with 0.6 m culverts and adjustable gates. Severe late-winter oxygen depletion ("[[Winterkill]]") removes most fish, including predators such as [[Northern Pike]] and [[Burbot]], before walleye are stocked.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions**
- Ponds warm quickly after ice breakup and remain thermally unstratified through summer. Maximum summer temperature was 26.0 °C in Coleman and 26.5 °C in Freshwater (25 June). By 19 October temperatures were 10.0 °C and 8.5 °C.
- Coleman was markedly more saline than Freshwater (specific conductance, chloride and TDS). Both are "saline eutrophic" by the [[Rawson and Moore Classification]]; Coleman is a true Na-Cl type, and Freshwater an intermediate Na-Cl type.
- Water levels fluctuated 2–3 cm daily through the permeable dikes and fell 13 cm from May to September. Autumn culvert flows ranged 40–90 cm/s and were wind-influenced.

**Food web**
- Zooplankton dominated early diets: [[Diaptomus]] in Coleman and [[Daphnia]] in Freshwater.
- [[Gammarus]] (amphipods) became important from late June, and insect larvae (Corixidae, Chironomidae, Dytiscidae) in July–August.
- Fish prey (fathead minnows and brook sticklebacks) appeared in Freshwater stomachs when walleye reached about 68 mm.

**Egg incubation and fry**
- Eggs were collected from walleye in trap nets at the mouths of the Duck, Drake and Sclater rivers and reared at the [[Duck Bay Hatchery]].

## Key Findings

- **Stocking:** 200,000 eggs were placed in each of the Coleman and Freshwater floating cages in May 1977. Total stocking was 1,450,000 fry in the five ponds, with an overall rate of 2,894/ha (Coleman 1,709/ha; Freshwater 1,905/ha; Pacheta 5,476/ha). Overall 1977 release, including rivers, was 2,950,000.
- **Early mortality:** Egg mortality in Coleman was 0.91%. Freshwater lost essentially all hatched fry, probably from hypoxia (<4 ppm in cages) and trace hydrogen sulfide. About 200,000 unfed fry were released to replace them.
- **Growth:** Fry averaged 6 mm at hatch and about 71 mm by mid-July in Coleman (60 days) and 68 mm in Freshwater. Final mean lengths in October were 95 mm (Coleman, range 78–148 mm) and 114 mm (Freshwater, range 89–136 mm). Lake Winnipegosis young-of-the-year reach about 120 mm by the end of summer.
- **Survival:** Petersen mark-recapture (1,326 fin-clipped fry, 13 August) estimated about 129,572 walleye at marking (95% CI 90,249–192,980). Weighted catch-based estimate was 112,973 (CI 105,109–120,837), giving 56.5% survival (CI 52.5–60.4%).
- **Escapement:** Exit traps recorded 16,261 walleye (Freshwater) and 15,017 (Coleman) up to mid-October. Only about 13% of Coleman's estimated fish were known to leave, and roughly 98,000 fate-unknown fish remained. Exit rates in October dropped to 13/day in Coleman versus 325/day in Freshwater.
- **Hydraulics:** Daily catches peaked during high southerly winds, with outflows up to 1 m/s, linked to water "piling up" in the northern basin. Walleye tended to move toward outflowing currents.
- **Recommendations:** Create an outflow current in culverts by pumping, or herd fish with an electric seine, which is being developed at the [[Freshwater Institute]].
- **Stocking density comparison:** Coleman was stocked at 5,000 fry/ha in 1978, raising estimated production from 7.1 kg/ha to 16.8 kg/ha. Growth in 1978 was somewhat better than in 1977, suggesting higher stocking rates could be used without substantially reducing growth.
- **Benchmarking:** Reported survival rates from other systems were lower (Oneida Lake under 0.5% for natural fry; Wisconsin ponds mean 7.8%; Minnesota ponds mean 19.2%; Michigan 0–24.4%). Minnesota constructed ponds averaged 44.8 kg/ha walleye production.
- **Cannibalism:** Walleye reached 180 mm by late October 1978 and became cannibalistic. Cannibalism rose as stocking approached 20,000 fry/ha in New York studies.
