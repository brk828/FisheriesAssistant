# Indigenous Fishes of Western North America and the Hypothesis of Competitive Displacement: *Meda fulgida* (Cyprinidae) as a Case Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1994 *Copeia* study tests whether the invasive [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) is actively displacing the threatened [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) in the [[Gila River Basin]]. The authors compared habitat use by each species when allopatric, allotopic, and syntopic across six stream reaches in [[Arizona]] and [[New Mexico]]. Red shiner used similar microhabitat regardless of co-occurrence, whereas spikedace living with red shiner shifted into significantly swifter currents. The authors interpret this as evidence of interference competition, while noting that the mechanism remains undetermined.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** The spikedace is a small stream cyprinid, the sole member of a monotypic genus, endemic to the Gila River basin. It was federally listed as threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]] in 1986 (USFWS, 1986). Adults aggregate in shear zones upstream of gravel-sand bars and in eddies below riffles.
- **Invader:** The red shiner, native to midwestern North America, was introduced into the mainstream [[Colorado River]] in the 1950s. It became invasive, reached south-central Arizona by 1963, and spread into New Mexico by the late 1970s. It tolerates turbidity, high temperature, low dissolved oxygen, and intermittency, and is considered a habitat generalist.
- **Historical context:** Western fish evolution was shaped by geologic and climatic fragmentation of aquatic habitats, producing a depauperate, endemic fauna likely lacking competitive abilities and predator defenses relative to faunas from more speciose regions.
- **Competing hypotheses:** The *replacement hypothesis* attributes native declines to environmental degradation, while the *displacement hypothesis* attributes them to competition or predation by nonnatives. Competition had seldom been rigorously demonstrated for western fishes before this study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Spikedace disappeared progressively upstream in the Gila basin, in the same direction the red shiner spread, and remnant populations now occur in only four small areas.
- Water development is also cited as a cause of decline, so the study sought to separate displacement from replacement.
- The authors note that [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) has affected native fishes largely indirectly, by increasing turbidity through eliminating aquatic vegetation.
- Ecological framework: the study focuses on interference competition for space (microhabitat use). It draws on [[Niche Shift]] theory, [[Syntopy]] versus [[Allopatry]] terminology, and [[Habitat Selection]] studies in other fish communities (e.g., [[Rainbow Trout]] and [[Brown Trout]]).

## Key Findings

**Study design**
- Six stream reaches (each 500–1500 m) were sampled in March–June 1987 and grouped as disjunct allopatric (spikedace alone, e.g., East and West Aravaipa Creek, Gila-Gila; red shiner alone, Sycamore Creek), allotopic (Verde River), or syntopic (Gila-Redrock).
- One to three seine sites (100–200 m) per reach; 60–100 standard seine hauls at each site (2.0 × 1.2 m, 3.2 mm mesh).
- Measured variables: current velocity, depth, and weighted substrate particle size. Analyses used weighted ANOVA with Sidak multiple comparisons.

**Results**
- **Red shiner:** Occupied similar microhabitats whether allopatric, allotopic, or syntopic. Where alone, it occupied deeper and slower water than available in two of three reaches; across all reaches, it used significantly slower currents and smaller substrates.
- **Spikedace (isolated):** Used places significantly shallower and slower than available, over smaller substrates.
- **Verde River comparison:** Where captured alone, red shiner used significantly slower currents than spikedace (Table 7).
- **Spikedace (syntopic):** Shifted into significantly swifter currents than when isolated. Where the two were caught together, spikedace habitat was statistically indistinguishable from red shiner habitat.

**Conclusions**
- The authors conclude that spikedace are displaced into swifter currents by the red shiner, and that this supports interspecific competition for space (inferred from niche overlap and syntopic niche shift).
- The mechanism is unresolved. The authors favor invader aggressiveness and native submissiveness, with novel-stimulus avoidance as an alternative.
- Recommended follow-up includes manipulative experiments to observe direct interactions and to test whether spikedace fitness declines when subordinated, possibly through metabolic measurements.
- Limitations: data were collected in one season and the niche shift may vary seasonally or with habitat availability.
