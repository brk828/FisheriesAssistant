# Fishes of Lake Mead and Lake Mohave: Base Fisheries Data, Boulder Canyon to Davis Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries #centrarchidae #salmonidae #clupeidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Nevada Department of Wildlife biological bulletin (Biological Bulletin No. 7, 1978), by Robert C. Allan and Dennis L. Roden, compiles base fisheries data for the [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]] reservoirs on the [[Colorado River]] between Boulder Canyon and Davis Dam. Funded by the Water and Power Resources Service, the report reviews the species list, stocking history, angler harvest, and life histories of the principal game and forage fishes, and documents the decline of native Colorado River fishes.

The central management story is the collapse of the [[Largemouth Bass]] fishery in Lake Mead after the 1963 filling of [[Lake Powell]] and the repeated spring drawdowns, which was partly offset by the 1954-55 introduction of [[Threadfin Shad]] as forage and by later stocking of [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Striped Bass]].

## Background & Description

**Lake Mead.** Created by [[Hoover Dam]] (completed 1935; reservoir filled 1941), Lake Mead is about 110 miles (177 km) long at full pool, up to 590 feet (179.8 m) deep, with about 550 miles (885 km) of shoreline. It comprises the Boulder, Virgin and Overton basins, linked by narrow canyons.

**Lake Mohave.** Formed by [[Davis Dam]] (construction begun 1942, major completion by 1951), Lake Mohave is a narrow reservoir about 67 miles (107.8 km) long, with roughly 200 miles (321.8 km) of shoreline and a maximum depth of just over 100 feet (30.5 m) at the dam. Before impoundment, the river below Hoover Dam was a cold, clear, swift stream (54-61 F; 12.2-16.1 C), which supported a large rainbow trout fishery.

**Fish occurrence.** The source's Table 1 lists about 35 fish species recorded from the two reservoirs, including native, introduced, incidental and bait species. Native Colorado River fishes include the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Humpback Sucker]] and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]]. Several Colorado River species are listed as extinct, rare or endangered in the reservoirs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Forage base.** Threadfin shad became established in 1954-1956 and became the principal forage for game fishes. Shad are a planktivore that occupies the upper 50 feet (15.2 m) of water in summer and descends to about 160 feet (47.5 m) in winter in the Boulder Basin.
- **Water-level fluctuation.** Rising spring levels supported bass spawning and production (for example, 1973 in Lake Mead), while declining spring levels reduced cover, plankton and year-class strength.
- **Thermal and oxygen structure.** Summer stratification created lethal temperatures in the epilimnion and a low-oxygen thermocline that limited trout habitat in Lake Mead's Boulder Basin.
- **Cold-water tailwater.** The Lake Mohave cold-water section below Hoover Dam favoured trout and humpback suckers, while the warm upper strata favoured bass, crappie and channel catfish.
- **Invertebrates and plants.** Asiatic clam (Corbicula) became abundant after about 1962-1963. Zooplankton (Cladocera and Copepoda) was a key food for young bass. Aquatic vegetation provided cover, with its availability tied to water level.
- **Disease and parasites.** [[Infectious Pancreatic Necrosis]] was confirmed at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] in 1974 and was reported eradicated from the hatchery by 1976. Parasites were largely incidental in the reservoirs, though more common in crowded hatcheries.

## Key Findings

- **Largemouth bass decline.** Largemouth bass comprised over 50 percent of the Lake Mead harvest from 1953 to 1969, with more than 400,000 taken annually. From 1970 the share fell below 50 percent, and in 1977 bass ranked below channel catfish and black crappie in total catch.
- **Harvest trends.** Lake Mead catch per angler was between one and two fish per angler day after 1966. The 1977 channel catfish harvest was the largest game-species harvest in Lake Mead (over 170,000 fish).
- **Rainbow trout.** Rainbow trout were the most important game fish in Lake Mohave, typically above 50 percent of the harvest. Lake Mead trout fishing was good from 1970 to 1975 and then declined sharply in 1976, with the trout share falling to about 1 percent.
- **Striped bass.** Introduced into Lake Mead in 1969, striped bass reproduced naturally from 1973 onward and became increasingly important (over 13,000 harvested in 1977, nearly double the prior year). The authors suggest they may have contributed to trout losses.
- **Threadfin shad.** Introduced in 1954 (274 fish into Lake Mead) and 1955 (6,500 into Lake Mohave, plus 11,000 more into Lake Mead), shad became the dominant forage for bass, trout, striped bass and catfish. Reproduction is protracted (spring through fall), with eggs taking about 2-3 days to hatch.
- **Rainbow trout life history.** Spawning is in gravel redds in spring, with spawning in Lake Mohave limited to under 2 miles (3.2 km) of habitat after impoundment, down from about 40 miles (64.4 km) before.
- **Stocking history.** Over 3.7 million rainbow trout were stocked into the Colorado River and Lake Mohave between 1935 and 1951, and kokanee salmon introductions (over 500,000 in 1962-1965) failed to establish.
- **Colorado River natives.** The Colorado River chubs (bonytail, humpback, roundtail) and Colorado squawfish are much reduced, and the authors attribute the decline to dam-related habitat changes. Captures of bonytail have not been verified from Lake Mead since 1967.
- **Tournaments.** Tagging showed poor returns from tournament-released bass (6.2 percent in a spring 1977 tournament, against 48 percent for electrofished bass over the same 8-month period), suggesting high post-release mortality.
- **Management recommendations.** The authors recommend tournament rule changes, including penalties for small or dead fish, keeping large and stressed fish, scheduling events to avoid temperature extremes, and reducing catch limits.
- **Regulation and disease control.** The [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]] adopted a fish disease certification policy (1973, amended 1974), and walking catfish were placed on the prohibited species list.
