# Public Hearing Transcript: Proposed Threatened Listing of the Spikedace and Loach Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #minnows

## Executive Summary

This document is the verbatim transcript of a [[Public Hearing]] held by the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (U.S. Department of the Interior) on October 7, 1985, at Western New Mexico University in Silver City, New Mexico. The hearing addressed proposals to list the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and the [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) as threatened species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] and to designate [[Critical Habitat]] for each. Presenters from the Service explained the biology, threats and listing process, while municipal officials, water-development groups, a state agency representative, a geohydrologist, business leaders and members of the public gave oral testimony. The central tension was between species protection and the water supply needed for regional development, particularly the proposed [[Conner Dam]] on the [[Gila River]].

## Background & Description

**Species profiles (as presented by Service biologist [[Gerald Burton]]):**

- **Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*):** Roughly three inches long, olive to brown with mottling. Breeding adults show red fins and lips. Diagnostic features include two light spots and a dark band near the tail base. Females mature and spawn in their second year, typically in late winter or early spring, laying about 250 to 600 eggs. Eggs are deposited under rocks in riffle areas; excess silt smothers them. Young and adults feed on mayfly and black fly larvae and favor riffles with appropriately sized cobble.
- **Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*):** A silvery minnow rarely exceeding three inches, with a distinctive spine on the dorsal fin and black flecks along the body. Males turn brassy during spawning. Spawning runs from early May through June; two-year-old fish may spawn twice a year. Females deposit about 100 to 300 eggs over sandy areas off riffles. Young appear April through May and reach about 1.5 inches by late fall. Spikedace prefer moderate to large streams with cobble, gravel and sandy patches, and runs and riffles.

Dr. [[James E. Johnson]], Chief of Endangered Species for the Albuquerque Regional Office, explained the [[Endangered Species Act]], including the definitions of "endangered," "threatened," "critical habitat" and "take," and the Section 7 consultation requirement for federal agencies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic and current range:**

- Both species were historically found across the [[Gila River Basin]] (including the Verde, Salt, San Pedro, San Francisco and Blue rivers) and were reported in the late 1800s for the loach minnow.
- **Spikedace:** Eliminated from about 94% of its historic range (remaining area roughly 6%). Since 1951 it has disappeared from the Gila between the Arizona-New Mexico border and the Middle Box, and has declined severely in the East Fork of the Gila. Recent finds include [[Eagle Creek]] and parts of the [[Verde River]].
- **Loach Minnow:** Eliminated from about 85% of its historic range (remaining area roughly 15%). Surveys in 1983-84 found populations in only two small stretches of the [[San Francisco River]], with sparse populations elsewhere. Remaining populations occur in [[Aravaipa Creek]], the Blue River, the San Francisco and the Gila, with small isolated groups on the White River near Fort Apache.
- The Service stated that about 70% of native Gila Basin fish are now rare, threatened or endangered, and that the species' loss could mean losing an entire natural system found nowhere else.

**Threat factors:** The Service identified Factor 1 (habitat destruction or modification) and Factor 3 (disease or predation) as the most important for these species. Specific threats include:

- **Dams and reservoirs:** Neither species can live in reservoirs. Major proposals include the [[Central Arizona Project]] and its Upper Gila Water Supply Study, plus CAP plans affecting spikedace habitat on the upper Verde.
- **Water pumping, diversion, channelization and siltation:** These reduce water tables, stream flow and habitat quality.
- **Watershed uses:** Cattle grazing, timber harvest, mining and road building cause erosion.
- **Exotic fish:** Bass and catfish are predatory, and the red shiner competes for food and space. Native fish are adapted to natural flooding patterns, which non-native fish are less able to withstand.

The Service noted that if all proposed projects were built, only the Forks area of the Gila and the upper Blue River would remain relatively unaffected, and that extinction of both species would then be likely.

**Regional water context:** Speakers from the [[Silver City]] area said that local aquifers are nearly fully developed and that surface water is needed for future growth. The [[New Mexico Interstate Stream Commission]] stated that the state's remaining Gila water entitlement depends on the New Mexico Unit of the Central Arizona Project, which authorizes about 18,000 acre-feet per year (per [[Public Law 90-537]]).

## Key Findings

**Listing process and timeline (Service presentation):**

- Two separate proposals (one per species) were published in the Federal Register on June 18, 1985. Each has a listing decision (biology-only, by law) and a critical habitat decision (subject to economic considerations).
- Options for listing are threatened, endangered or not listed. The process from proposal to final decision takes roughly two to five years.
- **Comment deadline:** November 8, 1985. Written comments were to go to the Regional Director, Fish and Wildlife Service, Albuquerque.
- **Final decision deadline:** June 18, 1986, as required by the Endangered Species Act.

**Proposed critical habitat:**

- **Spikedace:** Aravaipa Creek; the Gila River from the mouth of the Middle Box upstream to Mogollon Creek; lower West and Middle Forks of the Gila; and the upper Verde River from Sullivan Dam to Sycamore Creek.
- **Loach minnow:** Overlapping areas plus the San Francisco River below the Blue River mouth, the Highway 180 to Kelly Flat reach, the Tularosa River (Negrito Creek to Cruzville), and the Blue River including lower Campbell and Dry Blue creeks.

**Key testimony and positions:**

- **[[Steve May]] (Mayor of Silver City):** Asked whether the decision would function as a roughly 50-year management plan and argued that water rights should remain available for future use if economically feasible.
- **[[Richard C. Johnson]] (Hooker Dam Association):** Opposed the listing. Questioned inconsistencies between Service statements on flooding, cited the agency's own 1985 letter, and argued that about 380 km of loach minnow habitat and 154 km of spikedace habitat make threatened status unwarranted. Recommended repopulation from the Dexter hatchery and proposed that the snail darter case serve as a cautionary example.
- **[[Joan Hardesty]]:** Supported listing and critical habitat, citing the importance of genetic variability to long-term survival.
- **[[Fred Trauger]] (geohydrologist):** Argued that the 18,000 acre-feet of water is needed, that drought and climate, not man, drove the decline, and that extinction of these fish is inevitable.
- **[[Steve Reynolds]] (New Mexico Interstate Stream Commission):** Cited a letter from the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]] (Director Harold Olson) stating that Conner Dam would almost certainly make a significant Gila reach ecologically unsuitable for the spikedace and especially the loach minnow. He recommended deferring critical habitat designation for the Middle Box to Mogollon Creek reach until the New Mexico Unit site is settled, and noted that flood-cleanup coordination could be affected by critical habitat.
- **[[Carl Couret]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service):** Stated that Conner Dam alone would not cause extinction of the spikedace, that Gila habitat supports over 60% of bird species recorded in New Mexico, and that the Cliff-Gila Valley provides the best habitat for both species.
- **[[Joseph E. Ward]] (graduate student):** Argued that watershed mismanagement, including overgrazing, is the main threat and that flood-control dams would worsen it.
- **[[Steve Marlatt]] (fishery biologist):** Reported a 30 to 45% reduction of native New Mexico fish over 100 to 200 years and argued that long-term ecological value outweighs short-term gains.
- **[[Propst]] (Game and Fish Department):** Stated there are no plans to eliminate existing game fish, and explained that flooding can both harm and benefit fish habitat, depending on degree.

**Outcome:** The hearing was a public information and comment session. No decision was made at the meeting. Final determinations were left to the Service, after comments and an economic analysis of critical habitat.
