# Virgin Spinedace Proposed Listing as a Threatened Species

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1994 package from the [[United States Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) concerning the [[Virgin Spinedace]], a small endemic minnow of the [[Virgin River Basin]]. It contains a cover letter, the Federal Register proposed rule (*Federal Register*, Vol. 59, No. 95, May 18, 1994), a supporting letter from an Arizona State University researcher, a July 1994 hearing and comment-extension notice, and several unrelated Federal Register notices (an FCC radio channel change and a GSA fire-protection rule).

The Service proposes to list the Virgin spinedace as a **threatened** species under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. The fish has lost about 40 percent of its historic habitat, and water development, nonnative species, habitat fragmentation, drought, pollution, and inadequate state protection are cited as ongoing threats. Critical habitat was not proposed in this rule and was deferred to a later date.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The species was first included with the [[Little Colorado Spinedace]] and was later recognized as an unnamed *Lepidomeda* species. [[Miller and Hubbs]] (1960) described the subspecies *Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*. The name derives from Latin *mollis* (soft) and *spina* (spine), referring to the soft-tipped second dorsal spine. The fish belongs to the endemic western tribe [[Plagopterini]], which contains three genera (*Lepidomeda*, *Meda*, and *Plagopterus*). *Lepidomeda* includes four species, among them the [[White River Spinedace]], the [[Pahranagat Spinedace]], and the [[Big Spring Spinedace]] (a second subspecies).

**Morphology and life history.**
- Adults are 80–120 mm (3–5 in) in total length.
- The body is broad and silvery with dusky or olive-green saddles along the sides.
- Adults have eight dorsal rays and typically nine anal rays.
- Breeding adults show reddish-orange fin bases during the spawning season.
- The fish matures in about 1 year, lives about 3 years, and spawns from April to June at mean daily water temperatures of 13–17 °C (55–63 °F).
- Spawning occurs over gravel and sand at the shallow downstream end of deep pools. One female was observed depositing eggs, which were fertilized by several males.

**Habitat preferences.** Virgin spinedace prefer clear, cool, flowing streams with pools, runs, and riffles. [[Rinne]] (1971) found them in pools, often with undercut banks, debris, or boulders. Other studies reported them using deep pools in the North Fork and shallow runs with emergent vegetation in Beaver Dam Wash. Overall they favor the zone between high and low velocities with cover such as boulders, undercut banks, or vegetation. They feed in midwater and at the surface on drifting plant material, aquatic and terrestrial invertebrates, and debris.

**Petitions and status.** The Service received a petition from the [[Bonneville Chapter of the American Fisheries Society]] on June 15, 1992, and another from the [[Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance]] on August 17, 1992. Both requested endangered status. The [[American Fisheries Society]] has listed the fish as threatened in its 1979 and 1989 lists of endangered, threatened, or special concern fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic and current distribution.**
- Historically, the fish was common in clear tributaries and some mainstem reaches above [[Pah Tempe Springs]] near Hurricane, Utah, and occurred less often below the springs.
- Early collections came from the Virgin River near the Utah–Arizona border, Bunkerville (Nevada), Veyo, Ash Creek, the North Fork of the Virgin River in Zion National Park, and Beaver Dam Wash.
- [[Valdez et al. 1991]] estimated a 40 percent loss of historic habitat, about 140 km (87 mi).
- The fish no longer occurs in Nevada and is rare in Arizona. About 60 percent of the historic habitat remains, all in Utah.
- Currently occupied areas include the mainstem Virgin River above the Quail Creek Diversion, three reaches of [[Beaver Dam Wash]], the [[Santa Clara River]] between Veyo Springs and Gunlock Reservoir, isolated reaches of Moody Wash and Mogatsu Creek, and a 6.3 km (3.9 mi) reach of the lower Santa Clara River.
- Recent records also come from the lower reaches of La Verkin, Ash, North, and Shunes creeks and both forks of the Virgin River.
- Declines have been reported for about 50 years. [[Cross]] (1975) reported that the species had not been found below Littlefield, Arizona, since 1942.

**Major threats (Section A–E of the rule).**
- **A. Habitat destruction and modification:** Water development is the primary threat. Diversions built in the early 1900s on Leeds, Ash, and North creeks dewatered habitat. Rapid human population growth has increased demand for water. Proposed reservoirs on North Creek, Ash Creek, the North and East forks, and Beaver Dam Wash would further harm the species. Livestock grazing, feedlots, mining, residential development, and [[Urbanization]] also degrade habitat, including fragmentation.
- **B. Overutilization:** Not identified as a factor. The fish was once used as bait in the lower Colorado River but no longer is.
- **C. Disease and predation:** Nonnative species are a major concern. [[Largemouth Bass]] selectively prey on the fish, and [[Brown Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]] stocked in reservoirs can migrate into spinedace habitat. Rainbow trout below Schroeder Reservoir probably block upstream recolonization. [[Crayfish]] and the [[Red Shiner]] may prey on larvae and young-of-year fish. The red shiner also is linked to declines of the [[Woundfin]]. The [[Redside Shiner]] declined significantly after earlier concern. The [[Asian Tapeworm]] arrived with the red shiner in 1986 and probably harms spinedace under stressful conditions such as low water.
- **D. Inadequacy of regulatory mechanisms:** About 54 percent of land along the Virgin River is privately owned. Utah lists the species as protected and prohibits direct take but cannot adequately protect habitat. Nevada has similar take prohibitions. Arizona offers no protection. The Service judged current protection inadequate.
- **E. Other natural or human factors:** Drought dewaters reaches and isolates populations. Artificial barriers now prevent fish from retreating to refugia, and natural recolonization is unlikely. Irrigation and municipal diversions can dry reaches that otherwise hold minimum flows. Pollution from municipal and agricultural return flows, pesticides and herbicides, cattle waste, and mineral springs also threatens the species. Cyanide contamination from a gold mine on the East Fork of Beaver Dam Wash is under study.

**Status determination.** The Service found the species is not in danger of extinction throughout all or a significant portion of its range, so it does not meet the criteria for endangered status. It is, however, likely to become endangered in the foreseeable future if present threats continue.

**Related regulatory context.** The Service's proposal references Section 4 (listing and critical habitat), Section 7 (federal consultation), and Section 9 (prohibitions on take) of the Act. Permits for scientific or enhancement purposes are handled by the Office of Management Authority in Arlington, Virginia. Other listed relatives include the woundfin and Virgin River chub, which are endangered, and the spikedace, Little Colorado spinedace, and Big Spring spinedace, which are threatened.

## Key Findings

- **Proposed action:** List the Virgin spinedace as a threatened species (proposed rule published May 18, 1994). The Service is not proposing to list it as endangered.
- **Habitat loss:** About 40 percent (140 km; 87 mi) of historic habitat has been lost. About 60 percent remains, all in Utah.
- **Critical habitat deferred:** Critical habitat was not proposed in this rule. The Service planned to propose it at the same time as the woundfin and Virgin River chub, no later than April 1, 1995.
- **Primary threat:** Water development and associated dewatering, reservoir construction, and fragmentation.
- **Nonnative species:** Largemouth bass, trout, crayfish, red shiner, and the Asian tapeworm are documented threats.
- **Protection gaps:** Utah and Nevada prohibit take but cannot protect habitat, and Arizona provides no protection.
- **Public process:**
  - Original comment deadline: July 18, 1994.
  - Public hearing requests were due July 5, 1994.
  - A public hearing was held in St. George, Utah, on July 13, 1994.
  - The comment period was extended 30 days, to August 17, 1994.
- **Supporting comment:** An Arizona State University Center for Environmental Studies professor supported the listing (July 1, 1994). The letter called for reconstituting the Virgin River fishes recovery team and for securing adequate stream flows.
- **Other contents (unrelated to the fish):** The package includes an FCC proposal to change Channel 239A to Channel 239A... at Bamberg, South Carolina (Radio Broadcasting Services), and a GSA proposed rule on fire-protection engineering equivalency (comment extension to July 25, 1994). These are not relevant to the Virgin spinedace.
