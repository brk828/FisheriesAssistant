# Commercial Fisheries Potential of Central Arizona Reservoirs: Catch Techniques and Assessment (Quarterly Progress Report, 1968)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #commercial-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This quarterly progress report, prepared by W. L. Minckley and dated April 15, 1968, is a [[Commercial Fisheries Research And Development Act]] document issued under the [[Bureau Of Commercial Fisheries]], [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]], for the state of Arizona (Sub-Project No. 6-1-R-2, "Investigation of Catch Techniques"). The reporting period is garbled in the scan but ends March 31, 1968. The study evaluates catch gear and catch per unit effort on [[Roosevelt Lake]] and [[Apache Lake]] on the Salt River, assesses the commercial fishery potential of buffalofishes, carp and [[Threadfin Shad]], and discusses reservoir morphometry and human-use problems. Basic life-history studies were continuing and were to be reported later.

## Background & Description

The project examined the commercial potential of "rough" fishes in four Salt River impoundments in central Arizona: Roosevelt, Apache, Canyon and Saguaro lakes. Roosevelt Lake is the largest and has extensive shallow, littoral water. The other lakes impound deep-cut canyons with vertical rocky banks that drop off to more than 100 feet.

The target and associated species are:

- **Buffalofishes:** [[Smallmouth Buffalofish]], [[Black Buffalofish]] and [[Bigmouth Buffalofish]] (reported together as "Buff." where not separated). Black buffalofish occur in Apache Lake only.
- **Carp:** [[Common Carp]].
- **Threadfin shad:** [[Threadfin Shad]].
- **Game and other species:** [[Channel Catfish]], [[Yellow Bullhead]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Redear Sunfish]], [[Black Crappie]], and minor cyprinids and suckers.

Gear used, listed in order of decreasing intensity of use:

1. Flag-type gill nets (meshes 1/2 to 5 inches, 8 ft deep)
2. Night electrofishing from a boat with overhead lights (220 V, 2600 W generator)
3. Trammel nets (inner-wall meshes of 2 and 2-1/2 inches)
4. Experimental gill nets with leadline and corkline (1/2 to 2 inch meshes, 6 ft deep)
5. [[Hoop Nets]] (with or without wings and leads)
6. Miscellaneous [[Seines]] (15 to 60 ft)
7. [[Ichthyotoxins]] (low-concentration [[Rotenone]] in selected bays)
8. Shrimp trawl with otter boards (mid-water tows at 3 to 5 mph)

Catch data from gill and trammel nets are standardized as fish per 100 feet of net per 12-hour net night, one unit of effort.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal patterns.** Catches were generally highest in spring and summer and lowest in autumn and winter, a pattern consistent across lakes and across both the test-netting and commercial-report data.

**Habitat and depth.** The best catches of buffalofishes and large carp came from 12 to 20 ft of water, with nets set at about 45° from shore. In April through June these fish move into water shallower than 6 ft, but pleasure boats made such sets impractical. Roosevelt Lake's broad shallow littoral zone allowed fish to escape electrofishing. Apache Lake's rocky, steep margins and small bays allowed fish to be "cornered."

**Threadfin shad behavior.** Shad are strongly attracted to lights, concentrate in large night schools, move into shallow water, and are most abundant near the surface in summer and autumn. They are rapidly immobilized by electricity and then sink.

**Disturbance.** Pleasure boats repeatedly ran over and entangled both test nets and commercial nets, and their effect on results was not estimated. Fluctuating water levels, drawdowns for power or irrigation, debris from high runoff, and currents from releases between dams all increased maintenance. Uncleared vegetation left standing at impoundment created obstructions, and shrubs and small trees invaded banks during drawdowns.

**Human factors.** Conflicts with the sport fishery, and public destruction of commercial nets, were described as a major constraint.

## Key Findings

### 1. Catch Techniques

- **Flag-type gill nets:** Meshes under 1-1/4 inch captured mostly small fish and gave little information beyond seasonal presence of threadfin shad. Meshes of 1-1/4 to 1-1/2 inch selected for larger carp, some buffalofishes and especially [[Largemouth Bass]]. The 2-inch mesh gave dramatic increases in buffalofish and carp catches and was especially efficient for game fish. At 3 inches, buffalofish catches rose sharply, and that mesh was used by commercial fishermen. Buffalofish do not gill; they become entangled by fins and opercles, which makes them easy to remove. Meshes larger than 3 inches were inefficient.
- **Trammel nets:** These gave substantially larger catches per unit effort than any other comparable gear. The 2-inch inner-wall net was about twice as efficient as the 2-1/2-inch net. The authors could not fully explain the difference. They suggested net age (the 2-1/2-inch net was new and white, the 2-inch net brown and nearly invisible) as a partial factor. Game fish rarely gilled and could be released largely unharmed. Trammel nets worked best set parallel to shore just outside probable spawning areas, where catches were mostly ripe or ripening fish, with many males caught around few females.
- **Electrofishing:** Used as a sampling tool, not as a harvest test. The numbers in the tables are therefore minimum estimates. Largemouth bass and sunfish were highly susceptible, with bass per hour well above buffalofish per hour in almost all periods. Channel catfish rarely float when stunned, so their data were considered unreliable. Carp catches reached more than 100 fish in a few minutes when fish concentrated in small bays.
- **Weighted gill nets:** Catches were nearly identical to flag-type nets of the same mesh, but they were repeatedly disturbed by boaters and were judged impractical.
- **Hoop nets:** Forty net nights in 4 to 12 ft of water took no buffalofish. The carp caught were all under one pound, but large numbers of sunfish and bass were taken. The authors judged this gear seasonal and difficult to set and recover in these lakes, though possibly useful during spawning movements.
- **Seines:** Usable only in a few limited sandy or gravelly areas. Only one commercial-sized carp was taken in a 60-ft bag seine. Small-mesh seining was valuable for early life-history studies of threadfin shad.
- **Ichthyotoxins:** [[Rotenone]] was highly efficient for threadfin shad at low concentrations, but the fish sink rapidly, so toxins were judged unlikely to be a practical harvest method.
- **Trawling:** A mid-water shrimp trawl was tried on one day with no success because of high wind and bottom snags. A surface trawl combined with powerful night lights was suggested as a possible summer threadfin harvest method, but it was not tested.

### 2. Commercial Fishery Potential

- **Direct estimates:** No direct population estimates were made.
- **Buffalofishes:** Commercial catch fluctuations across years mainly reflected effort. Average weights of commercially caught buffalofish did not change significantly from 1964 to the present, and there was no evidence of population decline or expansion. Recruitment into the population was not detected in 1966–68, but the authors noted that a previous study (Beers, 1965) had the same problem for 1963–64, while fish hatched during Beers' study years were now being caught.
- **Indicative density:** Trammel-net averages suggested a minimum of about 12 adult buffalofish per acre. This corresponds to roughly 60 lb per acre in Roosevelt Lake and 72 lb per acre in Apache Lake. The authors estimated standing crops of about 600,000 lb in Roosevelt Lake (about 10,000 habitable acres) and about 150,000 lb in Apache Lake (about 1,800 acres). Reported commercial harvests of 8,000–18,000 lb per year (1965–67) from Roosevelt Lake and 10,000–15,000 lb per year (1966–67) from Apache Lake were judged unlikely to exceed sustainable levels.
- **Average weights:** Commercial records give mean buffalofish weights of 4.0–5.0 lb in Roosevelt Lake (1963–68) and about 6.0–6.4 lb in Apache Lake.
- **Carp:** Abundant in all lakes, but rarely large enough for commercial use. Carp made up less than 2% of the fish reported by commercial fishermen. Serrated spines in the fins tore nets and slowed removal. The authors judged the population able to sustain exploitation if a market for small and medium carp existed. They noted that North American information on this species was surprisingly scarce given its abundance.
- **Threadfin shad:** Probably the largest vertebrate biomass in these impoundments. Harvest methods had not been developed, but lights as attractants combined with an efficient entrapment device looked promising. Given the lakes' size, irregular shorelines, cover and high reproductive potential, the authors thought over-exploitation unlikely without great effort. They considered threadfin shad an ideal species for a specialized product fishery (for example, pet food or a sardine-like product) if processing, preservation and marketing problems could be solved.

### 3. Morphometry, Uses and Recommendations

- Only Roosevelt Lake has extensive shallow water that is easily fished. In Apache Lake, bays and inlets were worked intensively, while open waters were untouched.
- Commercial fishermen worked mainly near their camps and did not actively pursue fish aggregations, as is common in other freshwater commercial fisheries.
- Public education through news media for several months before expansion was recommended to reduce conflicts with the sport fishery and to prevent net destruction.
- Additional study of carp relative to other fauna was recommended, but time limitations prevented it.
