# Floodplain Wetland Suitability, Access, and Potential Use by Juvenile Razorback Suckers in the Middle Green River, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines whether a manipulated floodplain wetland in the [[Middle Green River]] provides suitable nursery habitat for juvenile [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a federally endangered catostomid of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. During the high-water years of 1995 and 1996, environmental conditions in [[Old Charley Wash]] (a 147-ha floodplain depression, of which 80 ha was studied) were favorable for age-0 razorback suckers and other fishes, whereas main-channel habitats were not suitable as rearing sites. The authors conclude that larval survival and growth are higher in floodplain depressions than in the main channel, but that flood connectivity was too brief for all larvae to reach these sites. Restoring floodplain connectivity through [[Levee Removal]] and adequate flood duration is recommended. The effect of nonnative fishes on razorback suckers in floodplain wetlands remains undefined.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was once common throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]] but has declined substantially and was listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1991. Decline is attributed to lack of recruitment, linked to habitat alteration after dam construction on main-stem rivers and tributaries, and to predation by nonnative fishes. Adults in the upper basin are mainly captured in low-gradient or floodplain reaches, with the largest population in the middle Green River. Juveniles were historically associated with floodplain inundation, and the species spawns on the ascending limb of spring hydrographs, which maximizes access of newly emerged larvae to inundated floodplains.

The construction of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in 1963 markedly reduced the magnitude and duration of floodplain inundation on the Green River. Because little was known about early life-history requirements, links between those needs and floodplain habitat had not been established. The study had three objectives: (1) measure environmental variables (dissolved oxygen, temperature, depth, zooplankton density) in the wetland through summer; (2) determine larval access to floodplains by comparing larval drift timing with bank-full flows; and (3) census fishes in the wetland to determine whether razorback suckers used the site.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Larval collections occurred in the middle Green River between river kilometers 482 and 395 above the Colorado confluence. Water entered Old Charley Wash by gravity flow through an inlet canal when discharge reached 240 m³/s, and by overbank flow over the natural levee at about 396 m³/s. The inlet was closed during this study, so all water entered by overbank flooding. Dominant vegetation was smartweed (*Polygonum* sp.) and sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), with bulrushes (*Scirpus* sp.) and cattails (*Typha* sp.) at the margins. The wetland drained before winter and refilled each spring.

**Water quality and prey.** Wetland temperatures exceeded main-channel temperatures through spring and summer. Maximum surface temperatures were 26.2 °C (15 July 1995) and 26.6 °C (10 July 1996). Dissolved oxygen did not fall below 4.0 mg/L in 1995 and remained above 2.0 mg/L into July 1996. Maximum depth exceeded 2.0 m during river connection and declined to about 1 m by midsummer. Zooplankton consisted mainly of cladocerans and copepods, peaking at 54.3 and 42.8 individuals per liter in 1995 and 1996.

**Flow regime.** Discharge inundated the wetland above 396 m³/s for 48 days in 1995 (peak 527 m³/s on 8 June) and 38 days in 1996 (peak 623 m³/s on 20 May). Larval access to most floodplains (above 575 m³/s, the average bank-full flow at the Ouray floodplain transects) was blocked under regulated flows from 1993 to 1996. Under unregulated flows, access would have occurred in 3 of 4 years.

**Fish community.** Nonnative species dominated the wetland. Common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) and fathead minnow (*Pimephales promelas*) together accounted for 91.2% of the weight and 60.6% of the fish collected in 1995 and 1996 respectively. Other common taxa included [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], [[Channel Catfish]], and red shiner. Biomass totals were 126.7 kg/ha (1995) and 71.4 kg/ha (1996). Adult fish entering from the river made up 80% and 77% of biomass in each year.

## Key Findings

- **Razorback sucker use:** Age-0 razorback suckers were the most abundant native fish in the wetland. In 1995, 28 fish averaged 94 mm total length (range 74–125 mm) and 9.5 g. In 1996, 45 fish averaged 65.9 mm (range 44–83 mm) and 3.8 g. Two adults were collected in 1995 and none in 1996. Juvenile [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], flannelmouth sucker, and roundtail chub were also present.
- **Recruitment in the wetland:** Age-0 fish were a small fraction of total biomass but the numerical majority of the catch, indicating successful recruitment within a nonnative-dominated community.
- **Physical suitability:** Warm, shallow, low-velocity conditions were near the temperature preference reported for juvenile razorback suckers (22.9–24.8 °C), and dissolved oxygen was largely above the 5.0 mg/L optimum suggested for warmwater ponds.
- **Prey availability:** Wetland zooplankton densities far exceeded those in riverine backwaters reported by Mabey (1993), which ranged from 1.3 to 7.1 per liter in July and August. Density comparisons used different mesh sizes, so direct comparison should be interpreted with caution.
- **Main-channel limitations:** Main-channel habitats offered little refuge from high velocity and had lower temperatures and less cover and food. Larval razorback suckers were present in the river after floodplains were isolated, meaning some larvae were stranded in the channel.
- **Timing of access:** Larval fish first appear in the main channel during peak flows and persist up to 6 weeks, but flow connectivity to floodplains lasted only 38–48 days. The timing and duration of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] releases therefore strongly determine larval access.
- **Predation and competition:** Nonnative fishes such as black bullhead and green sunfish may reduce razorback sucker survival, but juveniles survived to the end of the growing season. Because the wetland dried before spring flows, residual nonnative effects on recruitment could not be assessed.
- **Management implications:** The authors recommend levee removal and adequate flood duration to restore floodplain connectivity. They suggest that if historical habitat is restored, predation by nonnative fishes may be secondary rather than primary as a recruitment limit. They also note that stocking-related research and dispersal studies are relevant to the species' recovery.
