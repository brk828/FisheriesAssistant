# Effect of Physical Activity on Rainbow Trout Resistance to Ammonium Chloride and Zinc Sulphate

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1963 preliminary study by D. W. M. Herbert and D. S. Shurben tested whether swimming activity makes [[Rainbow Trout]] more susceptible to two common pollutants, [[Ammonium Chloride]] and [[Zinc Sulphate]]. Hatchery-reared yearling trout were held against known current velocities in stirred aquaria and compared with fish in still water exposed to the same poison solutions at the same time. Susceptibility was measured as the [[Median Tolerance Limit]] (TLm), the concentration killing 50% of fish.

Current speeds up to 55% of the maximum sustainable velocity produced no detectable change in toxicity. At 85% of that maximum (37.7 cm/s), the 24-hour TLm for ammonium chloride was 0.81 times the still-water value, and the 48-hour TLm for zinc sulphate was 0.70 times the still-water value. The authors concluded that the difference between laboratory and river susceptibility is small enough to be neglected in most pollution control work. They also reported that oxygen consumption data were broadly consistent with [[Lloyd Hypothesis]] on gill irrigation.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The study used [[Salmo Gairdnerii]] (rainbow trout). The fish were hatchery-reared yearlings averaging 12.3 cm from snout to hindmost caudal fin edge. Batches of ten fish were selected at random for each test.

**Rationale.** Standard [[Toxicity Testing]] keeps fish in still water with food supplied. Rivers, however, require fish to swim against currents, hunt, and avoid predators, which increases [[Oxygen Consumption]] and gill ventilation. The authors reasoned that greater activity might increase susceptibility, since earlier work (Weiss & Botts, 1957; Lloyd, 1961) linked falling [[Dissolved Oxygen]] with higher toxicity.

**Poisons.** Ammonium chloride and zinc sulphate were chosen because they are common pollutants believed to act through different mechanisms. Ammonium chloride was expressed as mg nitrogen/l, and zinc sulphate as mg Zn/l.

**Apparatus.** Experimental aquaria were 17 in. square with glass walls 19 in. high. A plastic-coated steel mesh divided each tank into an upper chamber, where fish were held, and a lower chamber containing horizontally rotating paddle blades. Current velocities were measured with a miniature current meter ([[Department of Scientific and Industrial Research]], 1959). Velocities varied across the tank (Table 1), so the swimming speed of a station-holding fish depended on its position. The authors identified this as a limitation of the apparatus.

**Test conditions.** Dilution water had a hardness of 320 mg/l CaCO3 and an alkalinity of 240 mg/l CaCO3, with pH held at 7.7–7.8 by aeration with air and carbon dioxide. Temperature stayed between 10 and 14 °C. Control tests with no fish deaths were run in each experiment. Experiments were conducted from November 1961 to March 1962.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study frames its results in terms of river conditions. Rainbow trout in rivers must maintain position against flow, and the authors judged that in nature they are "nearly always much less active" than when swimming at 70–85% of their maximum sustainable speed for one or two days. The main exception noted was migration up long series of rapids to spawning grounds, where fish may approach maximum activity for long periods and susceptibility could rise to the level seen at the highest test velocity.

The mechanism proposed for the activity–toxicity link is gill irrigation. [[Lloyd Hypothesis]] states that toxicity rises as the poisonous solution passes faster over the respiratory surface of the gills. Under this model, the ratio of toxicity in current to still water should follow the square root of the ratio of metabolic rates. [[Saunders Gill Irrigation Research]] indicated that the oxygen extraction fraction is likely below unity in active fish, which would modify the prediction.

## Key Findings

**1. Maximum sustained swimming speed**
- Nineteen of twenty trout (mean length 11.3 cm) held station for 48 hours against 43.4 cm/s.
- A second batch (mean length 12.2 cm) could not hold against 45.2 cm/s.
- Maximum sustainable speed was therefore about 44 cm/s, or 3.7 body lengths per second.

**2. Test velocities**
- Toxicity tests used average current velocities of 17.1, 24.2, and 37.7 cm/s.
- The fastest of these was 85% of maximum sustainable speed.

**3. Ammonium chloride (24-hour TLm)**
- Still-water TLm values across the three experiments were 31.1, 33.5, and 36.5 mg N/l.
- Differences at currents up to 24.2 cm/s were attributed to sampling error.
- At 37.7 cm/s the TLm in current was 0.81 times the still-water value. Probit analysis showed this was statistically significant (P < 0.01), though the difference was small.

**4. Zinc sulphate (48-hour TLm)**
- Still-water values ranged from 1.5 to 3.8 mg Zn/l, showing more variability than ammonium chloride.
- At currents up to 24.2 cm/s, the difference from still water was under 6%.
- At 37.7 cm/s the TLm was 0.70 times the still-water value, significant at P = 0.001.

**5. Oxygen consumption**
- Metabolic rate rose only slightly up to 24.2 cm/s, then increased rapidly at higher speeds (Fig. 2).
- The authors noted that the onset of increased susceptibility coincided with this rapid rise.

**6. Predictions versus observations**
- Lloyd's model predicted TLm reductions of about 17% at 24.2 cm/s and about 25% at 32 cm/s.
- Observed reductions were no detectable change at 24.2 cm/s and about 12–16% at the speed tested near 32 cm/s.
- The authors judged the discrepancy too small to invalidate the hypothesis, given the limitations of the apparatus.

**7. Conclusions**
- Forcing trout to swim for one or two days at 85% of maximum speed reduced TLm by only about 20–30%.
- At 70% of maximum speed, the reduction was estimated at no more than about 12%.
- Activity-related susceptibility is therefore unlikely to alter the application of laboratory toxicity results to river pollution control where trout fisheries are protected, except under exceptional sustained exertion.

**8. Limitations stated by the authors**
- True swimming speeds of individual fish could not be measured or controlled accurately.
- Swimming speed, oxygen consumption, and toxicity were not measured simultaneously for each batch.
- The authors recommended more refined studies to define the relationships between activity, metabolic rate, and susceptibility.
