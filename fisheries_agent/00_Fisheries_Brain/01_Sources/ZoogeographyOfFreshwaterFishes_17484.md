# Zoogeography of Freshwater Fishes of the Western Gulf Slope of North America

#ichthyology #marine-biology #zoogeography #cyprinidae #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by John V. Conner and Royal D. Suttkus presents a faunistic and historical zoogeographic analysis of freshwater fishes in seven Gulf of Mexico drainages lying between the [[Mississippi Basin]] and the [[Rio Grande Basin]]: the Calcasieu, Sabine Lake, Galveston Bay, Brazos, Colorado, San Antonio Bay and Nueces. Of 195 species recorded above tidal influence, 102 are considered strictly freshwater native fishes and form the basis of the analyses. The ichthyofauna is depauperate relative to central and eastern North America but transitional in composition, with eastern lowland, Great Plains and southwestern elements interdigitating. The authors attribute the fauna's assembly to stream captures, cross-grading, estuarine flooding and interconnecting drainages during Pleistocene sea-level fluctuations.

## Background & Description

The "western Gulf Slope" comprises drainages that flow generally northwest-to-southeast into the Gulf. The chapter draws on the senior author's doctoral dissertation (Conner 1977), personal collecting, museum records and literature. Records were accepted as "confirmed" only when verified by the authors or a recognized taxonomic authority; additional records were accepted under less rigid criteria once a species' presence in a drainage was documented.

Key terms defined in the text:
- **Range**: the total geographic area occupied by a taxon.
- **Distribution**: occurrence in a particular region.
- **Pattern**: repetition of distributional characteristics among species.
- **Drainage**, **system** and **basin**: hierarchical hydrographic units after Jenkins et al. (1972).

Habitats were categorized by gradient (pool-riffle ratio above or below 20:1 for lowland vs. upland) and width (rivers >70 m, streams ~10–70 m, creeks <10 m), plus special types: coastal marshes, swamps and springs. The Coastal Plain/interior physiographic break is the lowland/upland boundary.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physiography.** The western Gulf Slope spans three physiographic provinces: the [[Gulf Coastal Plain]], the [[Great Plains]] (High Plains, Edwards Plateau, Central Texas sections) and the [[Central Lowlands]] (Osage section). The Calcasieu and Sabine Lake drainages lie wholly within the Coastal Plain.

**Provenance and climate.** Eastern rivers (Calcasieu, Sabine, Neches, San Jacinto) drain only Coastal Plain deposits and lie in a humid subtropical zone. Western rivers (Brazos, Colorado, Nueces) rise on Pliocene High Plains deposits and cross semiarid steppe. Normal annual precipitation falls from about 160 cm over the Calcasieu watershed to less than 38 cm near the Brazos and Colorado headwaters.

**Water chemistry (Water Year 1966).** Ionic concentrations rise westward. Eastern streams are soft to moderately hard and slightly acidic (pH minimum 5.6–5.7 in Sabine and Neches). Trinity and westward waters are hard to very hard and basic (maximum pH 8.0–8.2). Chloride and sodium are unusually high in western rivers; the Nueces had bicarbonate 207.0 mg/l and chloride 33.0 mg/l, while the Guadalupe's and San Antonio's sodium values were 25.0 and 76.0 mg/l respectively. Specific conductance ranged from 178 µmhos/cm (Neches) to 904 µmhos/cm (San Antonio).

**Biogeographic provinces** (after Dice 1943 and Blair 1950): Austroriparian (Calcasieu, most of Sabine Lake and Galveston Bay), Texan (upper Sabine, upper Galveston Bay, middle/lower Brazos, Colorado and San Antonio Bay), Tamaulipan (middle/lower Nueces), Balconian (Edwards Plateau and Central Texas) and Kansan (southern High Plains, upper Brazos and Colorado).

**Glacial context.** The Pleistocene glaciations never reached the study area, but glacial sea-level drops exposed the continental shelf, linking drainages. The authors note that about 60% of the living North American fauna is of Late Miocene or younger age (R. R. Miller 1965).

## Key Findings

**Fauna composition and diversity**
- 195 species in 47 families occur in the major drainages between the Mississippi and Rio Grande basins (Table 12.1).
- 76 species are euryhaline or diadromous; 2 catfishes are subterranean; 15 were introduced by humans. The remaining 102 native strictly freshwater species form the analytical base (about one-fifth of the North American freshwater fauna).
- Eight of these are western Gulf Slope endemics, six of which are drainage endemics.
- Five families dominate: Cyprinidae, Catostomidae, Ictaluridae, Centrarchidae and Percidae.
- Cyprinodontiform contribution is three to four times that of areas to the east (except peninsular Florida).

**Drainage distinctiveness and endemism**
- Only three drainages hold endemics: San Antonio Bay (three spring isolates: [[Gambusia geiseri]], [[Gambusia georgei]], [[Etheostoma fonticola]]), Colorado (one: [[Gambusia heterochir]]) and Brazos (two riverine shiners: [[Notropis buccula]] and [[Notropis oxyrhynchus]]).
- The Brazos and Colorado show the most distinctive faunas, with a marked break at the Brazos/Colorado divide.
- Richness by drainage: Sabine Lake 77 species (richest), Calcasieu 75, Galveston Bay 70, Brazos 68, Colorado 59, San Antonio Bay 49, Nueces 35.
- 73 range limits occur within the study area (67 widely distributed species); 61 (91%) are southwestern limits.

**Faunal affinities (average faunal resemblance)**
- "Close" relationships (60–79%) occur in 18 of 21 drainage pairs. Calcasieu–Sabine Lake share 97%; Nueces–San Antonio Bay share 84%.
- Rank correlation analysis confirms a major ichthyofaunal break at the Brazos/Colorado divide, separating Calcasieu–Brazos from Colorado–Nueces.

**Historical zoogeography**
- Fauna is partitioned into three elements:
  - **Autochthonous** (17–19 species): endemic or semiendemic, mostly upland or riverine, derived largely from northern (Great Plains) stocks.
  - **Allochthonous** (39–50 species): mainly eastern lowland invaders via coastwise dispersal, plus one Neotropical invader ([[Astyanax mexicanus]]) and a few Great Plains forms.
  - **Residual** (32–41 species): ubiquitous or fluviatile forms that are relatively old and conservative.
- Dispersal mechanisms reviewed: stream captures (e.g., Pecos River piracy; a Brazos tributary diversion to the Red River about 600,000 years ago), local inland flooding, estuarine flooding and interconnecting drainages during lowered sea levels. The authors propose that interconnecting drainages account for most faunal exchange except strictly upland forms.
- Interconnection scenario: about 20,000–18,000 years ago the Colorado, Brazos and "Galveston River" flowed in parallel valleys, while Sabine Lake and Calcasieu joined on the exposed shelf. Four master streams likely existed during low sea stands, versus seven present-day drainages.
- The Nueces appears to mark the southwestward limit of most eastern lowland/Mississippi Valley elements. The authors argue the 230-km Laguna Madre was an insurmountable barrier for Astyanax, favoring an upland dispersal route.

**Taxonomic and records issues**
- Lee et al. (1980) showed 38 disagreements (27 species) with this chapter's drainage occurrences; only four were genuine post-1977 range extensions.
- Nine records by Lee et al. could not be verified, probably due to mislocated map points.
- The authors recommend closer taxonomic scrutiny of starhead-type topminnows from the Pearl, Pascagoula and Mobile Bay drainages.

**Authors' conclusion**
- The western Gulf Slope ichthyofauna is "largely transitional," with maximum interdigitation of eastern lowland, northern and Rio Grande elements between the Galveston Bay and Nueces drainages. Its history reflects Late Tertiary and Quaternary drainage rearrangement and sea-level-driven connections rather than simple centers of origin.
