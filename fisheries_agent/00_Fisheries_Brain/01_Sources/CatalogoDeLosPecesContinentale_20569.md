# Catálogo de los Peces Continentales de Quintana Roo

#ichthyology #marine-biology #continental-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1996 catalogue by Juan Jacobo Schmitter-Soto (El Colegio de la Frontera Sur, Unidad Chetumal) inventories the fishes of the continental waters of [[Quintana Roo]]: [[Cenotes]], lagoons, ponds, and the [[Río Hondo]] with its tributaries. It lists 125 species in 43 families, of which 86 species (31 families) are considered strictly continental. The work provides a systematic list, family and species keys, detailed species synopses (taxonomy, morphology, distribution, ecology, and local status), a zoogeographic discussion, and an assessment of threats from tourism, aquaculture, and introduced species. It is designed to serve both specialist ichthyologists and a general audience.

## Background & Description

**Scope and limits.** The catalogue covers fishes of inland waters, including high-salinity sites such as the [[Nichupté Lagoon System]]. Marine fishes appear only if they enter coastal lagoons or rivers during their life cycle or accidentally, and are excluded above 32‰ salinity. Introduced species such as [[Tilapia]] are excluded from the synopses. Taxonomy follows the most recent specialist revisions for each group; no new taxonomic proposals are made. [[Chetumal Bay]] and Yalahau bay are excluded as habitats, while small brackish lagoons between Punta Herrero and Xcalak are included.

**Study area.** Quintana Roo covers 58,443 km² between 17°48'–21°10'N and 86°46'–89°08'W. The climate is tropical with summer rains; annual precipitation ranges from 700 to 2000 mm and mean temperature is 26°C (range 21–28°C). Two main hydrological basins are recognised: the arheic Cobá district in the north, with almost entirely subterranean drainage, and the Río Hondo basin in the southeast.

**Sampling methods.** The state was divided into ten hydrological or physiographic zones, with an average of 24 water bodies visited per zone. Permanent water bodies were sampled at least twice, in dry and rainy seasons. Gear included hand nets, chinchorros, cast nets, double-cone traps, hooks, suction pistols with free diving, and visual records. Specimens were fixed in 10% formalin, then preserved in 70% ethanol. Habitat variables recorded included water colour, transparency (Secchi disk), temperature, salinity, conductivity, dissolved oxygen, pH, depth, and georeferenced position. The reference collection is held at ECO-CH (ECOSUR Chetumal).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitats.** The peninsula offers varied aquatic habitats: cenotes at different stages of formation, Pliocene fault lagoons in the west, the Río Hondo and Bacalar lagoons in the south, and coastal lagoons on both coasts. The world's longest flooded cave system lies near the [[Sian Ka'an Biosphere Reserve]]. Water is hard due to calcareous bedrock, and permanent lagoons can expand up to 150% in area between dry and rainy seasons.

**Threats.** The author stresses that tourism is not impact-free. Road construction blocks mangrove water flow, hotel discharge pollutes coastal waters, and the Cancún–Tulum corridor is under particular pressure. Even "ecotourism" harms cenotes when riparian vegetation is cleared or artificial light is introduced into caves, which promotes algal growth and deters bats that supply organic matter.

**Aquaculture and introductions.** At [[Lake Chichancanab]], accidental release of [[Tilapia]] coincided with a rise in tilapia abundance and a decline in at least two of the five endemic cyprinodonts. The author cites Contreras-Balderas that 136 of nearly 500 Mexican freshwater species are threatened, with exotic introductions a main cause. Native mojarras could provide inexpensive protein to rural populations without ecosystem damage.

## Key Findings

- **Inventory:** 125 species (43 families) recorded in inland waters; 86 species (31 families) are strictly continental and form the core of the synopses.
- **Sampling effort:** Ten zones, about 24 water bodies per zone, 240 localities listed in Appendix 2.
- **Dominant freshwater species:** [[Astyanax Aeneus]] is present in one of every two water bodies visited; [[Gambusia Yucatana]] occurs at nearly 75% of sites; [[Poecilia Mexicana]] at nearly 50%.
- **Zoogeography:** [[Astyanax Aeneus]] and [[Rhamdia Guatemalensis]] are the only primary freshwater fishes of the peninsula. Unlike the catfish, the characin did not use cenote tunnels to disperse, so its distribution reflects geological history.
- **Endemism:** [[Lake Chichancanab]] harbours five endemic *Cyprinodon* species, including [[Cyprinodon Beltrani]], which made up 68–85% of the local bolines. [[Poecilia Velifera]] is endemic to the Yucatán Peninsula, restricted to small coastal cenotes in northern Quintana Roo.
- **Cave fishes:** [[Ogilbia Pearsei]] (Bythitidae) and [[Ophisternon Infernale]] (Synbranchidae) are blind, depigmented, and considered endangered due to low density, habitat fragility, and genetic isolation.
- **Declines:** *Cyprinodon simus* has not been detected in recent years and is considered endangered. *C. verecundus* is extremely rare and restricted to hard-substrate habitats.
- **Eel status:** [[Anguilla Rostrata]] cenote populations may be threatened; they are thought to have lost catadromy and complete their life cycle in cenotes, where they act as apex predators.
- **Mortality event:** In June and August 1996, mass mortality of [[Arius Assimilis]] occurred in Chetumal Bay. A preliminary report attributes it to organochlorine pesticides in sediments, which weakened immune defences and allowed opportunistic parasites and bacteria to infect the catfish, aggravated by organic pollution.
- **Local use:** Several species are commercially or locally important, including [[Caranx Latus]] (caught with transmallo nets on the southern coast), [[Megalops Atlanticus]] (prized by sport anglers in the Río Hondo), and mojarras such as [[Petenia Splendida]] and "*Cichlasoma*" *synspilum*.
- **Conservation message:** The author argues that inventories must include distribution, morphological and genetic variation, and relative abundance, not just species lists, to guide protected-area planning and sustainable use.
