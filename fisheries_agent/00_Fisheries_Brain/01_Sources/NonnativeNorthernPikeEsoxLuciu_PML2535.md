# Peer Review of Manuscript on Non-Native Northern Pike and Walleye in the Green River Basin, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This document is an editorial review file for a manuscript by Harold M. Tyus and James M. Beard, submitted to [[Great Basin Naturalist]] (Brigham Young University). The manuscript reports the abundance, distribution, diet, and reproductive status of two non-native piscivores, [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) and [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*), in the [[Green River Basin]], Utah. The file includes the editor's request letter (September 1989), an external review letter by Paul Marsh (October 1989), and a completed review form. The reviewer judged the work publishable in principle but recommended **major revision** before acceptance. The review's central concern is that the threat these predators pose to endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]] is only partly demonstrated.

## Background & Description

- **Northern Pike** was introduced into an impoundment of the Yampa River drainage near Craig, Colorado, in 1977. It was first collected in the mainstream Yampa in 1979 and was reported as increasing there by 1983–1984.
- **Walleye** is presumed to have been introduced to the Green River drainage in the 1950s. It is widely distributed but low in abundance.
- The basin is a recovery area for four rare and endangered Colorado River fishes. Of 27 non-native fishes introduced into the basin, the two large piscivores are of particular concern.
- Control of non-native fishes is a recovery measure under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for the upper Colorado River basin.

The manuscript (titled in the review as a study of abundance, distribution, and diet of non-native fishes in the Green River basin) draws on electrofishing and multi-gear sampling from 1979 to 1989.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling framework (as described in the manuscript):**
- Mainstream Green River sampled over 517 km from the Yampa confluence in Dinosaur National Monument to the lower Green and Colorado rivers, plus the lower 73 km of the Yampa River.
- Six relatively homogeneous strata (A–F) were sampled with electrofishing, seines, trammel nets, and wire traps, across prerunoff, runoff, and postrunoff periods.
- Catch per hour (c/h) was the standardized effort measure. Opportunistic sampling was excluded from c/h calculations.
- Stomach contents were identified to the lowest possible taxon with a dissecting scope. Additional stomachs came from the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources and BIO/WEST, Inc.

**Habitat use:**
- Northern pike occupied shallow, low-velocity shoreline habitats with aquatic and bank vegetation, a pattern similar to that reported elsewhere.
- Walleye were usually captured in slow shoreline runs associated with emergent or bank vegetation.
- Both species overlapped in habitat with [[Colorado Squawfish]] in shoreline runs and backwaters, as shown by collections and radiotelemetry studies. This overlap underpins the concern about competition and predation.

## Key Findings

### Manuscript content (as summarized in the abstract and text)

- **Sample size and size structure:** 84 northern pike and 79 walleye were collected from 1979 to 1989. Pike averaged 619 mm total length (range 321–1,045 mm) and walleye averaged 511 mm (range 395–686 mm). All fish were adults or large juveniles.
- **Abundance trends:** Mean catch rate of pike rose from 0.05 to 0.14 fish/h, while walleye fell from 0.09 to 0.02 fish/h. The pike:walleye ratio was 0.5 in 1981 and increased thereafter.
- **Distribution:** 78% of pike (n = 59) and 90% of walleye (n = 50) were taken in Stratum F during 1984–1988. Forty-three percent of pike in that stratum came from near Ashley Creek.
- **Range expansion:** Pike were confined to upper strata (F and G) from 1981 to 1986. By 1987 they had moved downstream into Stratum D, and by 1988 they reached Stratum A.
- **Movement:** Two tagged pike moved about 110 km (April 1982–May 1983) and 78 km (June 1988–May 1989). Pike moved more than 75 km per year in large western rivers (n = 2).
- **Diet of northern pike (123 stomachs):** Most stomachs contained fish, and the diet was dominated by non-native prey, notably [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]]. Three native species were also eaten, including flannelmouth sucker, speckled dace, and bluehead sucker. Roughly 54.5% of stomachs were empty. Soft-rayed fishes predominated, and channel catfish was the only spiny-rayed fish eaten.
- **Diet of walleye (61 stomachs):** 24 stomachs (39.3%) were empty and 10 (16.4%) held unidentifiable remains. Six fish species were eaten (five non-native, one native). Channel catfish and fathead minnow were most frequent. Walleye also ate catfish and cannibalized conspecifics in other systems.
- **Reproduction:** No age-0 pike or walleye were captured, so recruitment is undocumented. However, 33 female pike were in breeding condition at water temperatures of about 10–19 °C. Of ripe females, 76% had empty stomachs, consistent with reduced feeding before spawning. One female walleye in breeding condition was taken in May at about 13 °C, which is warmer than reported native-range spawning temperatures.
- **Predation on endangered fishes:** Predation on rare or endangered fishes was not detected. The authors note that the rarity of these fishes would make such predation hard to document.

### Reviewer's assessment and recommendations

- **Overall verdict:** The information is important and should be published in [[Great Basin Naturalist]], but the manuscript needs substantial work before it is ready. The recommendation is major revision.
- **Scope:** The reviewer recommended splitting the material into two papers (for example, one on distribution and abundance and one on food habits and reproduction). The current text covers too much, and the balance between text, literature, tables, and figure is poor.
- **Introduction:** Should be rewritten to focus on the paper's purpose. The opening discussion of impaired native Colorado River fishes should be shortened or removed, since the main subject is pike and walleye.
- **Methods:** Should be shortened. Details on sampling strata may remain relevant to the [[Colorado River Fishery Project]] but could be condensed into qualitative descriptions of reach locations, using river-mile designations.
- **Results and discussion:** Results and discussion should be separated. Distribution, abundance, and food-habit data should be presented separately and combined only in the discussion.
- **Figures and tables:** The map (Figure 1) and Tables 1 and 4 should be omitted, with their key content summarized in the text. Table 2 should be abbreviated.
- **Editing:** The whole manuscript needs careful editing to remove redundancy, streamline wording, and tighten sentences.
- **Scope of results:** The reviewer noted that content on pike and walleye data was scarcely mentioned in some sections and needed more attention.

### Implications for management

- The authors concluded that predation risk to rare fishes may be reduced, and possibly removed, if pike and walleye stocking stops.
- They recommended that further introductions into the Green River and its tributaries be terminated before self-sustaining populations establish.
- The reviewer agreed that the manuscript should be made available to interested readers once revised and asked to see any future version.
