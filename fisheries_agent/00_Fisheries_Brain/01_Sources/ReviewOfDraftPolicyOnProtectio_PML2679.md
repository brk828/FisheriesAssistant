# Endangered Species Act Hybrid Policy: Protection, Taxonomy, and Genetic Evidence

#ichthyology #marine-biology #hybridization-policy

## Executive Summary

This document is a compilation of internal U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) materials, Solicitor's Office opinions, a hybrid review group report, a Federal Register draft, and supporting scientific papers and case studies. Together they address whether hybrids of species, subspecies, or populations listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) of 1973 should receive legal protection. The Service's long-standing position, grounded in 1977–1984 Solicitor's opinions, was that hybrids of listed species were not protected. A November 1990 review group and a draft National Policy Issuance proposed a reversal in part: protecting naturally occurring hybrids through the "similarity of appearance" provisions and generally treating listings as inclusive of introgressed individuals. The material is pre-decisional and marked draft, with no final policy adopted in the text.

## Background & Description

The ESA protects species, subspecies, and, for vertebrates only, distinct population segments. The Act's legislative history and text offer no explicit guidance on hybrids. The Solicitor's Office issued a series of opinions:

- **May 18, 1977:** Hybrids of listed species were covered, based on the ESA's definition of "fish or wildlife" including offspring.
- **August 2, 1977:** Reversed the earlier view; hybrids were not protected because protection would disrupt listed gene pools and conflict with the Act's conservation purpose.
- **Subsequent opinions (1980–1984):** Reaffirmed non-protection; a 1981 opinion stated that producing hybrids of two subspecies would not serve the Act. An August 24, 1984 opinion held that offspring of two populations of the same subspecies (e.g., Southern Selkirk caribou augmented with another woodland caribou herd) are covered.

The review group defined a working biological definition: hybrids result from breeding between full species, whereas breeding among subspecies or populations is "crossing." Hybridity is treated as one end of a continuum of introgression.

Related concepts include [[Hybridization]], [[Introgression]], [[Biological Species Concept]], [[Evolutionary Species Concept]], [[Similarity of Appearance]], [[Mitochondrial DNA]], [[CITES]], and [[Listing and Delisting]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Several taxa illustrate the policy problem:

- **[[Florida Panther]] (*Felis concolor coryi*):** Population of roughly 30 to 50 animals in Big Cypress and Everglades. O'Brien et al. (1990) reported two genetic stocks: a Big Cypress lineage with *F. c. coryi* mtDNA type A, and an Everglades lineage with type B mtDNA and an APRT-B allele shared with a South American subspecies (*F. c. puma*). The Everglades lineage likely derives from captive Piper stock released between 1957 and 1967. Big Cypress panthers showed high rates of spermatozoal abnormalities (>90%) and cryptorchidism (~20%) attributed to inbreeding.
- **[[Gray Wolf]] (*Canis lupus*) and [[Coyote]] (*Canis latrans*):** Lehman et al. (in press, cited in the petition findings) reported coyote mtDNA in Minnesota wolves. A FWS Information Bulletin reported that more than 50% of 72 Minnesota gray wolves sampled had coyote mtDNA, with mtDNA transfer appearing unidirectional from coyotes to wolves. No nuclear DNA evidence supported introgression in U.S. wolves, and no coyote phenotypic traits were recorded.
- **[[Red Wolf]] (*Canis rufus*):** Hybridization with coyotes followed the decline of the last wild population; captive breeding and reintroduction at Alligator River NWR and a proposed reintroduction at the Great Smoky Mountains National Park were discussed. Coyotes occupy about 90% of the red wolf's historic range.
- **[[Spotted Owl]] (*Strix occidentalis*):** Barrowclough and Gutiérrez (1990) found no genetic variation at 22 of 23 loci across Oregon and California populations; the single variable locus (EST-D) showed a fixed difference (F_ST = 0.55) between New Mexico (*S. o. lucida*) and Pacific Coast populations. Authors suggested the two allopatric groups may represent separate species.
- **[[Koloa]] and [[Mallard]] (Hawaiian duck complex):** Griffin and Browne (1990) concluded koloa are genetically similar to mallards, with introgressive hybridization on Kauai and possibly no intact koloa population on Oahu. Recommendations included ending koloa introductions and controlling feral mallards.
- **[[Pacific Walrus]] (*Odobenus rosmarus divergens*):** Preliminary mtDNA analysis (Information Bulletin 90-3) identified four haplotypes among six walruses from the Chukchi Sea, with low overall variability.
- **[[Dusky Seaside Sparrow]] (*Ammodramus maritimus nigrescens*):** Extinct in 1987 after captive breeding and crossing with a morphologically similar subspecies (*A. m. peninsulae*) was considered.
- **[[Peregrine Falcon]] (*Falco peregrinus*):** Eastern *anatum* stock was used for reintroduction, raising questions about protection of birds nesting under bridges.
- **[[Southern Selkirk Mountain Caribou]]:** A 1984 opinion addressed augmentation with an unlisted herd of the same subspecies.
- **[[Southern Sea Otter]]:** Augmentation with an unlisted subspecies was judged not to change the biological status of the listed subspecies.
- **[[Ozark Cavefish]] and [[Striped Bass]]:** Striped bass hybrids have largely replaced the native race in the wild in the southeastern United States.

## Key Findings

- **Policy options (January 1991 draft):** Option 1 (no protection, status quo), Option 2 (protect hybrids), and Option 3 (case-by-case protection). The review group recommended a hybrid option: a conservative policy that treats listings as inclusive of individuals sharing parental morphological and genetic traits, presumes "evident hybrids" at the species level covered under similarity of appearance, and allows generic rule coverage with exceptions.
- **Exclusion of captive and feral exotics:** The proposed policy would not apply to captive-held or feral hybrids of exotic species in other countries.
- **Introgression among subspecies:** Limited introgression would not be classed as hybridization; crosses are treated as members of the subspecies they most closely resemble.
- **Recommendations (Refsnider, November 14, 1990):** Redefine species to tolerate some gene flow; develop guidelines for "species" under the ESA; formulate factors guiding hybrid treatment; adopt a generic rule; coordinate with outside experts.
- **Critique of molecular genetics (O'Brien and Mech; Shepard):** Few loci (e.g., a few dozen) may not capture the full genomic difference; preliminary results should undergo peer review; mtDNA is maternally inherited and does not measure nuclear introgression.
- **Florida panther genetic stocks:** The Everglades group, with its introduced mtDNA and APRT-B allele, raised policy questions about the value of protecting the entire population.
- **Proposed Federal Register listing and delisting:** The gray wolf delisting petition (1990) was found not to present substantial information warranting delisting, citing old, qualitative, and non-U.S. data and noting that the single supporting study (Lehman et al. in press) addressed past, infrequent hybridization.
- **Reintroduction and augmentation:** Experimental populations under ESA Section 10(j) were suggested as a safer approach for red wolves and caribou transplants.
- **Permit guidance (1980 Solicitor's memo):** Permits for hybrids should be issued only with appropriate conditions, and the FWS was advised to develop a "look alike" policy.
- **Finding of coyote mtDNA in gray wolves (Lehman et al.):** Characterized as evidence of past but not current hybridization in the United States.
- **Hybrid policy as recurring issue:** Debate persists about species definition, the "unit of recovery," and the role of taxonomy in listing decisions, with the ESA's tenets of preserving genetic diversity and maintaining ecosystems central to the debate.
