# Ecological Review and Management Recommendations for Recovery of the Endangered Gila Topminnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This review by W.L. Minckley synthesizes the ecology of the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and proposes recovery actions. The author argues that the species historically persisted through cycles of expansion during wet periods and retreat into [[Population Refugia]] during droughts and cold events. Human alteration of watersheds, the spread of the [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), and the loss of connectivity now prevent this natural pattern. The review concludes that recovery requires protecting remnant populations, translocating fish directly by managers, controlling predators, and considering [[Winter Cold Events]] in site selection. It also recommends managing the species as a single [[Evolutionarily Significant Unit]] rather than as separate [[Management Units]].

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow is a small, livebearing fish that was once widely distributed below about 1600 m elevation in the [[Gila River Basin]] of Arizona and New Mexico and in northwestern Sonora, Mexico. It has been extirpated from much of its U.S. range and now persists naturally in only a few isolated springs and stream segments. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service lists it as endangered. In Mexico, the species and the closely related *P. sonoriensis* are listed as threatened. The author treats *P. sonoriensis* as a full species, based on data from Quattro et al. (1996).

Taxonomic names of the mosquitofish group were revised by Rauchenberger (1989) and Mayden et al. (1992). The author retains "western mosquitofish" for *Gambusia affinis*, a species confirmed by morphological examination of 423 Arizona specimens.

Human activities drove the species' decline. Livestock grazing and timber harvest began altering vegetation before the 1800s. Irrigation development in the 1800s changed flow patterns. From the 1940s and 1950s, introduced nonnative species restructured aquatic communities. The decline accelerated after the western mosquitofish, a [[Predation|predatory]] livebearer, became established and spread. Mosquitofish were first recorded in Arizona in 1926. Topminnows often disappeared within months of mosquitofish detection.

### Life History and Habitat

- **Lifespan and reproduction:** The species is short-lived, with an average natural lifespan of less than one year. A female bears 5-20 young at 24-28 day intervals. The reproductive season extends 8-9 months, and may be year-round in warm, constant-temperature springs.
- **[[Superfetation]]:** Females can carry two or more cohorts at different developmental stages. Females can also produce multiple broods from a single insemination.
- **Maturation:** Young reach sexual maturity in 60 or fewer days in warm water.
- **Habitat use:** Fish occupy water a few centimeters deep in slow currents, concentrating in protected inlets, debris, and vegetation.
- **Diet:** The species is omnivorous, eating mostly detritus and also live plants and invertebrates.
- **Tolerance:** It endures intermittency and moderate floods, but severe flooding eliminated some translocated populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Natural community.** Under natural conditions, topminnows shared their microhabitat with few native fishes. Desert pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*) was the only species likely to co-occur year-round. Pupfish were far rarer than topminnows: 12 samples from 8 localities before 1930, compared with about 50 collections from about 20 sites for topminnows. Interactions between the two species were limited in deeper water, because pupfish stay near the bottom.

**Original habitats.** Before human alteration, precipitation infiltrated more slowly, floods were less frequent, and groundwater supported reliable baseflows. Stream channels were more complex, with abundant pools formed around tree roots, logs, boulders, and beaver dams. Riparian vegetation included gallery forests and marshlands. Groundwater exchange and shading moderated water temperatures in both summer and winter.

**Thermal ecology.** Topminnows tolerate high summer temperatures and most winter cold at low elevations. However, their northern and upper altitudinal range limits appear to follow winter water temperatures. The Gila River basin is the northernmost extent of this tropical genus.

- A freeze in December 1978 brought nighttime air temperatures to or below -6.0°C. Freezes lasting more than 12-20 consecutive hours are catastrophic for many warm-adapted Sonoran Desert plants.
- On 11 December 1978, the author found a few hundred dead topminnows in ice-covered backwaters of Cienega Creek. Active fish were concentrated where a spring inflow kept the water 3.0-5.0°C warmer.
- Of 15 pre-1930 localities, 10 (67%) were at or within a few kilometers of groundwater-fed marshes or spring inflows.

**Anthropogenic pressures.** Dams, diversions, incised channels, and groundwater pumping fragment habitat and dry springs. Springs have often been "improved" by capping, impoundment, or diversion. Even where surface water persists, reduced baseflows lead to rapid temperature swings and degraded riparian vegetation. Floods are now more intense because of watershed degradation, and mosquitofish persist in artificial habitats such as stock ponds, from which they can recolonize streams.

## Key Findings

1. **Historical dynamics.** Topminnows expanded during wet periods of high habitat connectivity and contracted during drought or cold events. Remnant refugia, especially permanent oases fed by groundwater, allowed recolonization.

2. **Predation as a driver of decline.** Mosquitofish are an effective topminnow predator. Some topminnow populations coexisted with mosquitofish for long periods, and a few still do. Eight of the remaining natural sites are either shared with mosquitofish or directly accessible to them. Only two springs and one creek lacked mosquitofish.

3. **Stocking success.** Of 175 sites stocked, 18 (10.3%) had topminnows that survived 3-20 years, and 157 (89.7%) failed (Weedman and Young 1997). Recorded causes at 96 failed sites were desiccation (57), flooding (27), and winter cold (6). Mosquitofish, water quality, dredging, and livestock overuse were each noted at only 1-2 sites, and 61 failures were attributed to unknown factors.

4. **Temperature association.** Topminnow localities were far more likely to occur near warm water than expected. Sixteen of 18 persistent translocated populations (88.9%) were in springs or spring-fed habitats. Only 48 of 157 failed translocations (30.6%) were at or near springs or spring-associated creeks.

5. **Winter cold as a hypothesized limiting factor.** The author hypothesizes that severe winter cold has historically limited topminnow populations, and that this factor now interacts with other human-caused pressures. This hypothesis is supported by the spatial patterns and freeze observations above, though the author notes that the causes of some declines remain unclear.

6. **Genetic structure.** The Monkey Spring population is genetically distinct. It averages 0.62 private alleles per locus across 5 microsatellite loci, compared with 0.14, 0.15, and 0.25 in three less isolated stocks (Bylas and Sharp springs, Cienega Creek). Shared loci among populations ranged from 0.75 to 0.86. The author argues that genetic differentiation reflects isolation and repeated immigration cycles rather than permanent divergence.

7. **Management recommendations.** The review identifies four disruptions that must be addressed to prevent extinction:
   - (1) Restore connectivity, or translocate fish directly by managers, since fish can no longer move between sites.
   - (2) Protect existing natural refugia, maintain stocks at appropriate size and genetic quality, and establish artificial refugia.
   - (3) Re-create the historical dispersal pattern by periodically moving fish from unstable sites back to reliable refugia.
   - (4) Eliminate or control mosquitofish and other predators in refugia and in nearby populations.

8. **Management units critique.** The author disagrees with Simons et al. (1989), who proposed separate management units for four Arizona areas. The author argues that the evolutionarily significant unit is the whole metapopulation, except possibly the differentiated Monkey Spring population. Monkey Spring fish should be maintained without gene flow from other populations, but allowed to contribute genes to other stocks.

9. **Translocation sites.** Thermal conditions should be considered when selecting translocation sites, since this may increase success.
