# Population Genetics of Endangered Catostomid Fishes of Northern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This draft final report (April 1, 1991) from the [[University Of California]] Department of Wildlife & Fisheries Biology, prepared under California Department of Fish and Game contract FG-8143, uses [[Allozyme Electrophoresis]] to assess the genetic status of three federally and state-listed endangered catostomid suckers: the [[Modoc Sucker]], [[Shortnose Sucker]], and [[Lost River Sucker]]. The main concern is that [[Hybridization]] with other sucker species, along with [[Habitat Alteration]], may erode species-specific characters. The study sampled nine localities and found moderate genetic diversity and little evidence of hybridization in the shortnose and Lost River suckers. The Modoc sucker could not be sampled because of drought, so that objective was deferred.

## Background & Description

The three focal species are native to northern California and southern Oregon and have all suffered census declines, mainly from habitat loss and hybridization (Williams et al. 1989).

- **[[Modoc Sucker]]** (*Catostomus microps*): a small, fine-scaled sucker known from only two disjunct areas, the Turner-Hulbert-Washington Creeks drainage and the Rush-Johnson Creeks system. The most recent census cited is 1,399 individuals (Mills 1980). Threats include grazing-induced erosion, drought, and presumed hybridization with the [[Sacramento Sucker]], which gained access to spawning sites through eroded stream barriers.
- **[[Shortnose Sucker]]** (*Chasmistes brevirostris*): reported hybridizing with [[Klamath Smallscale Sucker]] in [[Copco Reservoir]] and with [[Klamath Largescale Sucker]] in [[Clear Lake Reservoir]]. Recent spawning runs show severe declines with little or no recruitment.
- **[[Lost River Sucker]]** (*Deltistes luxatus*): once widespread across the Lost River system, now found mainly in Copco Reservoir, Clear Lake Reservoir, and [[Upper Klamath Lake]]. Draining of Sheepy Lake, Lower Klamath Lake, and Tule Lake in the early 1920s sharply reduced its census (Coots 1965).

Taxonomy is unsettled. The Lost River sucker has been placed in *Chasmistes*, in *Catostomus*, and as the sole extant member of *Deltistes* (Miller and Smith 1967, 1981). Competing hypotheses place *Chasmistes* as either primitive (Ferris and Whitt 1978) or derived (Miller and Smith 1961).

The study had six objectives: (1) establish an electrophoretic laboratory; (2) develop an internal catostomid genetic standard using the [[Sacramento Sucker]] as reference; (3) characterize presumed pure populations; (4) determine the genetic status of presumptive hybrids; (5) assess systematic relationships among *Chasmistes*, *Deltistes*, and *Catostomus*; and (6) correlate meristic and genetic data for Modoc sucker populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sampling:** Suckers were collected at nine localities in California and Oregon. Sites included [[Upper Klamath Lake]], [[Clear Lake Reservoir]], [[Copco Reservoir]], Eagle Lake (Tahoe sucker), and [[Cedar Creek Moon Reservoir]]. Tissues (muscle, heart, eye, liver) were homogenized and analyzed for 14 protein systems (Table 2).
- **Drought:** Continuing California drought prevented collection of any Modoc suckers. Reassessment was planned for the following spring, with a possible option to raise young-of-the-year at the UC Davis aquaculture facility for reestablishment and later genetic study.
- **Habitat pressure:** The authors identify human modification and destruction of habitat as the principal cause of decline, which they describe as the overwhelmingly common factor in both historical and recent extinctions. Hybridization is a secondary threat.
- **Life-history context:** The [[Warner Sucker]] shows facultative potamodromy, with lake-dwelling adults and resident stream forms. This example illustrates how a single gene pool can produce markedly different morphs.

## Key Findings

**Methods and genetic markers**
- Hybridization was assessed in three levels: Level I (complete allelic differences), Level II (low-frequency allelic overlap), and Level III (moderate-to-high overlap, assessed by the genotype index of Campton 1987).
- Maximum undetected hybridization (*q*) was calculated with an acceptable error rate (α) of 0.05.
- Genetic distance used Rogers' index. Trees were built with a modified Wagner method and a frequency-restricted presence/absence procedure.

**Genetic diversity**
- Electrophoresis of 9 populations detected 53 alleles at 19 polymorphic loci. Ten loci were monomorphic.
- Thirteen private alleles were found.
- Roughly one-fifth of loci were polymorphic within any population. Percent polymorphic loci ranged from 17.2 (CCMR) to 34.5 (Sacramento sucker).
- Mean heterozygosity (H) ranged from 0.03 in Lost River sucker to 0.11 in Eagle Lake Tahoe sucker. The authors describe these values as well within the teleost range (Nevo et al. 1984), indicating populations have not reached levels where genetic extinction is imminent.

**Shortnose sucker population structure**
- Relative gene diversity among the three shortnose populations (Gst) was 0.069, which implies historical gene flow above 3 individuals per generation.
- Each population contributed roughly 93% of the species' total genetic diversity, so each is considered an equal representative of the species' genetic characteristics.

**Hybridization results**
- No clear evidence of hybridization was found between shortnose suckers and either Klamath smallscale or largescale suckers.
- Two cases of possible Level II hybridization were found. No Level III hybridization was detected.
- Maximum estimates of undetected hybridization ranged from 1% to 15% (Table 5), largely reflecting small sample sizes. Shortnose–Klamath smallscale estimates ranged from 2% to 5%.
- The Ldh-B2 (100) allele, fixed in Klamath largescale suckers, occurred at a frequency of 0.03 in Clear Lake shortnose suckers. The authors treat this as a weak indicator of introgression.
- The authors concluded that the three shortnose populations tested are "pure."

**Lost River sucker**
- The sample was small (n = 9). Introgression from shortnose into Lost River sucker could not be excluded at a 15% level.
- An apparent Level II signal from Ck-2 frequency (87%) was judged unlikely to be valid, since Ck-2 (100) is probably the ancestral state retained as a polymorphism.

**Modoc sucker and Cedar Creek population**
- The Cedar Creek, Moon Reservoir (CCMR) population was initially thought to be close to Modoc suckers. Genetically it was most similar to the Tahoe sucker from Eagle Lake (EAG).
- CCMR showed four fixed allele differences from Sacramento sucker, with a maximum hybridization estimate of 1%. The CCMR–EAG hybridization estimate was 5%.
- A Pgm-1,2 rediploidization event was found only in CCMR and EAG and was interpreted as diagnostic of that lineage.

**Systematics**
- Both cladistic analyses clearly separated CCMR and EAG from the other species. Genetic differences may justify CCMR and EAG as distinct species.
- The genetic distance cladogram had a patristic/phenetic deviation of 0.62 in the text, so confidence in it is low. The frequency-restricted presence/absence tree (Figure 3) was considered more reliable.
- About half of characters showed homoplasy.
- Shortnose and Lost River suckers were sister taxa, supported by the Ldh-B2 (95) allele.
- If Klamath smallscale sucker belongs in the Lost River/shortnose lineage, *Chasmistes* is monophyletic and *Catostomus* is paraphyletic, making *Catostomus* invalid as currently circumscribed.
- The authors judged the Lost River sucker's monotypic genus status uncertain and called for additional samples.

**Prior hybridization claims reviewed**
- Miller and Smith (1981) reported 55% hybrids (7 of 7) in Copco Reservoir and 30% (11 of 27) at Lake of the Woods, Oregon.
- Andreasen (1975) found no evidence of mass hybridization or introgression.
- Buth et al. (1987) reported a maximum of 3% introgression between razorback and flannelmouth suckers.
- Bartley et al. (in prep.) found that presumed hybrid cui-ui suckers from Pyramid Lake were genotypically identical to pure cui-ui, with a maximum hybridization probability below 0.5%.

**Interpretation**
- The authors argue that population genetics theory shows very low introgression rates can homogenize populations. A 55% hybridization rate would likely eliminate species-specific characters, a pattern not seen here.
- They suggest that novel morphologies in suckers may arise from [[Phenotypic Plasticity]] and polygenic variation within a single gene pool, rather than from hybridization.

**Management implications**
- Recovery efforts should focus on the populations most likely to represent pure genetic makeup, which requires genetic diagnostics for the shortnose and Lost River suckers.
- Increasing sample sizes would reduce hybridization estimates. Additional Lost River and Modoc sucker samples are needed.
