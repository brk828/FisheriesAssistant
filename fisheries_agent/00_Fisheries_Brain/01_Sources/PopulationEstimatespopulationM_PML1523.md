# Population Estimates and Movements of Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study quantifies the abundance and movements of adult [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), an endangered cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River]] system, within the lower [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) in northern [[Grand Canyon]]. Adults were captured, PIT-tagged, and released during 19 sampling trips between July 1991 and December 1992 at three reaches. The authors tested whether adult residency and movement were restricted to the reproductive period. The data show that movements combine a spring upriver migration with extensive, protracted summer-to-winter residency, indicating that adults remain in the LCR for much of the year. The authors suggest this may be a life-history shift linked to the thermal regime created by [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin supports the most distinctive fish fauna in North America, with species-level endemism of about 75% (93% when undescribed forms and subspecies are included). This biodiversity competes with heavy water development, a conflict the authors describe as classic and ongoing. Many native fishes are endangered or candidates for listing because of habitat modification. Their morphological adaptations, including reduced body-surface impacts and specialized morphology, suit boulder-strewn, high-velocity rapids.

*Gila cypha* is considered the most specialized minnow in western North America. It is restricted to the Colorado River and major swift tributaries, and it occurs sporadically and is seldom locally abundant. The type specimen was taken in 1932 at Bright Angel Creek (141.3 river km below Glen Canyon Dam), and the species was described by [[Robert Rush Miller]] in 1946. The Grand Canyon population was not recognized as reproducing until 1975. The largest known population was once thought to be near Grand Junction, Colorado, based on 32 specimens.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study river.** The LCR drains approximately 141,000 km² of eastern and northern Arizona and western New Mexico. It flows 412 km to the Colorado River. Most of its upper 390 km is seasonally dry because of land use and water impoundments dating to the turn of the century. Flow in the lower 21 km is perennial, sustained by groundwater springs, the largest being [[Blue Springs]] (river kilometer [RKM] 21). At base flow the river is saline (conductivity above 5000 µmhos/cm) and travertine-forming, which gives it a turquoise color. Travertine deposits create pools, runs, rapids, and waterfalls. [[Atomizer Falls]] at RKM 14.9 marks the upstream limit of *G. cypha* distribution.

**Riparian vegetation.** Native species include catclaw acacia, honey mesquite, coyote willow, and arrowweed. Nonnative species include tamarisk and camelthorn. Giant reed and cattail occur patchily.

**Dam effects.** Dams and diversions have sharply reduced river habitat. Suitable habitat for large-river fishes fell from 2400 km to 965 km (Miller, 1982). Mainstem fishes in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]] were extirpated, except for relict populations of [[Longfin Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]. Closure of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] in 1963 created [[Lake Powell]] and changed temperature, sediment, and salinity regimes. Downstream, the fish community shifted toward coldwater nonnative species such as rainbow trout and brown trout. Five of eight indigenous fishes persist within Grand Canyon National Park, usually in warm tributaries and backwaters. The LCR's temperature and flow resemble the pre-dam Colorado mainstem, which may explain why *G. cypha* persisted there.

**Methods.**
- *Sampling:* Three base camps were set up at the Confluence (RKM 0.55), Powell Canyon (RKM 3.1), and Salt Canyon (RKM 10.8), covering reaches of 1.2, 1.3–7.0, and 8.0–14.9 km respectively. Hoop nets and trammel nets were used.
- *Marking:* Adults (>150 mm TL) received [[PIT Tags]] and were released at capture sites.
- *Population analysis:* Closed-population models were run in the program CAPTURE. Nine models were evaluated, and the best-fitting model was retained for each capture-history matrix. Fifty-seven matrices were produced by reach and trip, and 19 were produced by month for the whole LCR. Relative population estimates were compared using ANOVA and ANCOVA, with fishing effort as a covariate.

## Key Findings

**1. Abundance estimates.**
- The best-fitting estimate for the entire 19-month study was 4,509 adults, derived from the [[Pollock And Otto Estimator]] (the scanned value is partly garbled).
- In May 1992 the Confluence held an estimated 1,320 adults. The whole LCR held an estimated 4,346 adults in May 1992 (4,602 when summed by reach).
- Earlier estimates for the Confluence were 1,800 (1987) and 2,900 (1988). An estimate of 25,000 was reported for the whole LCR in 1989.
- Population estimates differed significantly among reaches (F = 4.19; P < 0.01), with the highest values at the Confluence. Fishing effort also varied (F = 6.40; P < 0.0035).

**2. Population closure.** The authors judged closure to be supported within each reach and month because samples were brief and between-reach movement during sampling was negligible. Closure was less secure for the 19-month pooled analysis, which the authors regard as heuristic rather than practical.

**3. Spring upriver movement.** Some adults moved upriver in early spring (March to May), consistent with reproduction. Greater numbers were found at the Confluence in spring 1992, which supports the idea that the Confluence is a staging area before upstream movement.

**4. Postreproductive downstream movement.** Downstream movement after spawning was slow and spread out over a long period. In 1991, 13 recaptures moved upstream and 28 moved downstream, out of a total of 3,272 recaptures.

**5. Strong residency.** Most movement was static within reaches. In 1992, about 70% of recorded movements were within reach, and only one of 4,030 recaptured fish moved upstream, with none moving downstream. Tag returns showed long-term residency, especially from summer through winter:
- At the Confluence, at least 76% (n = 54) of fish tagged in summer were recaptured there summer through winter.
- At Powell Canyon, 78% (n = 80) of fish tagged in summer were recaptured there summer through winter.
- At Salt Canyon, 93% of fish tagged in summer were recaptured there summer through winter.

**6. Habitat use.** Adults are primarily nocturnal. In the LCR they use deep cut banks with overhanging reeds, sheer rock outcrops, and deep pools by day, and they are active at dawn, dusk, and night. Greater numbers were recorded at Salt Canyon than at Powell Canyon. The authors suggest this reflects greater habitat complexity (travertine dams, eddy/run interfaces, deep pools) or a physical or chemical barrier at RKM 14.9 that causes fish to accumulate.

**7. Life-history implications.** The authors conclude that the LCR functions as a warm-water refugium for overwintering adults. They offer two hypotheses:
- Residency is an ancestral, pre-dam trait.
- Residency is a post-dam shift in which adults avoid cold mainstem water.

The second hypothesis is supported anecdotally by three observations: upriver movers are mainly large (presumably older) fish; juveniles are washed into the mainstem by floods, but adults under 200 mm TL are seldom found there; and the LCR's hydrology and temperature resemble the pre-dam river. The authors note that *G. cypha* can live more than 20 years. They also note that flow management could help restore a more natural hydrograph and support long-term survival.

**8. Conservation outlook.** The authors argue that the LCR is central to the long-term survival of *G. cypha* in Grand Canyon. Political and economic forces shape the outcome of conservation, and these forces can outweigh cost-efficiency considerations.
