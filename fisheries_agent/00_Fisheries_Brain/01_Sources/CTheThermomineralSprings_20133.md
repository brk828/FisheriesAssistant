# Lake Kinneret: Water Chemistry, Thermo-Mineral Springs and Water Balance

#limnology #hydrology #water-chemistry

## Executive Summary

This excerpt examines the water chemistry and water budget of [[Lake Kinneret]]. The lake's ionic composition differs sharply from its main inlet, the [[Jordan River]], because of [[Thermo-Mineral Springs]] on the shore and lake bottom. Chloride in the lake is about 250 ppm, compared with 20 ppm or less in the Jordan. The document estimates that these springs add roughly 150,000 tons of chloride per year, with about half coming from [[Sublacustrine Springs]] and seepages. It also presents the lake's [[Water Balance]], including precipitation, runoff composition and inflow from the drainage basin. No fish, invertebrate or other biological taxa are discussed.

## Background & Description

Lake Kinneret is a large inland freshwater body. Its main water source is the Jordan River. Most inland freshwater bodies show a "normal" ionic sequence (Ca > Mg > Na > K; HCO3 > SO4 > Cl). The lake departs from this pattern, with a sequence of Na > Mg > Ca > K and Cl > HCO3 > SO4 (Fig. 34, from [[Serruya and Pollingher 1971]]). The text attributes this difference to saline groundwater entering the lake.

Thermo-mineral springs in the lake vicinity were mapped and characterized (Fig. 35, Table 13). They fall into three groups:

- **Western coast springs:** [[Ein Shneva Group]], [[Ein Nur Group]], [[Ein Fuliya Group]] and [[Hamei Tiberias Group]].
- **Sublacustrine springs:** [[Barbutim Group]] and [[Ma'agan Group]], located under the lake. Their exact positions were determined during bathymetric surveys in the 1960s by [[TAHAL]].
- **Saline seepages** along the eastern and western shores.

The table gives an estimated total chloride input of about 76,550 units from known western-coast springs, and an overall estimate of about 71,000 (units as printed) for all sources. The Table 13 figures include mean values and ranges that appear in the source with OCR-degraded units, so they should be checked against the original.

Two sites are described in more detail:

- **Barbutim group:** A series of "craters" in a NE–SW depression in the northwestern sector of the lake. The largest crater, at the southern end of the depression, has the strongest discharge.
- **Ma'agan group:** Six circular funnels about 800 m offshore from the Tiberias fisheries port. The largest is about 40 m wide and 20 m deep.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The springs are a major concern for water quality. The Fuliya springs alone are estimated to deliver about 150,000 tons of chloride per year (±20%). Although their discharge is modest, they add a large salt load. About half of the saline influx comes from springs on or near the shore, and the other half from sublacustrine springs and seepages. The document says this salt threatens lake water quality and motivated detailed studies of the source of the saline water and the hydraulic routes that bring it to the surface. These studies are reported in Chapter V, which is not included here.

The water balance places the lake within its catchment:

- **Precipitation:** Ranges from about 400 mm/yr over the lake to about 1,200 mm/yr in the [[Mount Hermon]] area. The watershed receives a total average of 2,160 (units garbled in the source; likely million cubic metres per year). Actual totals vary widely: the text gives a deviation of "160 to 30%" for very wet and very dry years. This is probably a scan error and should be verified.
- **Runoff:** About 75% of surface runoff is groundwater-fed [[Baseflow]], which is fairly uniform. The remaining 25% is storm and surface runoff. Large interannual variation mainly reflects the surface response to rainfall amount and timing.
- **Inflows:** The drainage area supplies 66% of total lake inflow. The average net Jordan inflow is 558 MCM. The text states that 295 MCM lost each year through [[Evaporation]] must be replaced by an equal additional inflow, which comes mainly from the immediate catchment.
- **Aquifers:** Four areas were distinguished: basalt-covered northern and western sectors (125 km²), an 85 km² area dominated by an aquifer unit, and [[Turonian–Cenomanian Karstic Formations]] extending over 180 km². Annual superficial and underground influx was estimated for 1959–1960 and 1973–1974.
- **Golan Heights:** Contributes about 60% of the total inflow (the remainder of the sentence is cut off in the source).

## Key Findings

- The lake's ionic composition (Na > Mg > Ca > K; Cl > HCO3 > SO4) is the reverse of the typical inland freshwater pattern and results from saline inputs.
- Lake chloride is about 250 ppm, whereas the Jordan River carries 20 ppm or less. Thermo-mineral springs account for the difference.
- Estimated chloride loading from springs is about 150,000 tons per year (±20%), roughly half from sublacustrine springs and seepages.
- The Hamei Tiberias group has the most concentrated water (about 16,500–18,200 ppm chloride, 56–61 °C), but only a small discharge.
- The Barbutim and Ma'agan sublacustrine groups are notable features on the lake floor, with the Ma'agan funnels the largest at about 40 m wide and 20 m deep.
- Annual precipitation ranges from 400 to 1,200 mm across the basin, and wet and dry years cause large deviations from the average.
- About 75% of surface runoff is baseflow, and 66% of lake inflow comes from the drainage basin.
- Average total aquifer influx (217 MCM) closely matches the Kinneret Basin contribution in the overall water balance (212 MCM), and the Golan Heights contributes about 60%.
