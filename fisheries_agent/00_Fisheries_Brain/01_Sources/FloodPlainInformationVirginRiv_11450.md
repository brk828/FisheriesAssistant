# Flood Plain Information: Virgin River and Fort Pierce Wash, Vicinity of St. George, Utah

#flood-hydrology #flood-plain-management #river-engineering

## Executive Summary

This 1973 report, prepared by the [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] Los Angeles District for the [[Washington County]] Commission, maps flood hazards along the [[Virgin River]] and its major tributary, [[Fort Pierce Wash]], near [[St. George]], Utah. It documents historical floods, describes channel and bridge obstructions, and models two future design floods: the [[Intermediate Regional Flood]] and the [[Standard Project Flood]]. Its purpose is land-use planning and [[Flood Plain Management]], not flood control design. The document contains no fish, invertebrate, or other biological survey data; its aquatic content is limited to channel and flood hydraulics.

## Background & Description

**Settlement history.** Prehistoric Indigenous peoples irrigated the basin. Mormon pioneers settled it systematically from 1857 onward, and [[St. George]] was founded in 1861. Settlers placed agriculture on the flood plain because the land was flat and water was available, despite early diary warnings about river sweeps. The region is known as "Dixie" from its early cotton experiments.

**Population (1970).** St. George had about 7,100 residents and Washington about 750. [[Bloomington]] is unincorporated, with several hundred inhabitants by 1972 after new developments.

**Virgin River.** An interstate stream draining about 6,000 square miles, with headwaters in Kane County, Utah. It flows southwesterly into [[Lake Mead]] on the Colorado River in Arizona. The study-reach drainage is 3,880 square miles, and the gradient averages about 13 feet per mile.

**Fort Pierce Wash.** A major tributary with a 1,660 square-mile drainage area, headwaters near the north rim of the Grand Canyon, and a gradient of about 20 feet per mile in the study reach. Its terrain is hilly with rock outcroppings.

**Climate.** St. George lies in an arid zone with long hot summers and infrequent rainfall. Recorded extremes are -11°F and 116°F, with a mean annual temperature of about 61°F. Mean annual precipitation ranges from about 8 inches at low elevations to about 40 inches in the high mountains, averaging about 13 inches across the drainage.

**Flood plain development.** Land is mainly agricultural and open lowland. Four roads cross it: [[Interstate 15]], Utah State Route 64, County Road 212, and the Man of War Road. Development pressure is rising with population and tourism.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Storm types.** Three storm types produce flooding:
- General winter storms (November–April), which cause the major Virgin River floods, sometimes with rain on snow.
- General summer storms (May–October), often tropical-derived.
- Summer thunderstorms (July–October), which are short, cover areas of a few hundred square miles, and mainly affect small tributaries.

**Flood behavior.** Streamflow rises quickly from a dry wash or small perennial flow into a "raging torrent." Small tributaries may reach flood stage in under an hour, while the Virgin River may peak more than a day later. Sediment loads are extremely high, and floods are strongly erosive.

**Vegetation and obstructions.** Brush, large trees, and [[Salt Cedar]] along banks impede flow, raise backwater, and create debris jams that can break loose as surges. Debris collecting on the six bridges can cause structural failure or washout of approaches. Figures document obstructions along the [[Santa Clara River]] and Fort Pierce Wash.

**Channel dynamics.** Erosion and deposition shift channel alignment between floods, raising streambeds and changing flood paths. Bank-protection efforts have had limited success. Tire matting near Virgin River mile 112.5 was washed away or undercut in the 1971–1972 flows, and car bodies were wired to a roadway embankment to slow erosion.

**Existing flood control.** No major flood control projects exist or are under construction on the study reaches. [[Gunlock Dam]] on the Santa Clara River is a water supply reservoir expected to reduce intermediate regional flood peaks by only about 8 percent. An irrigation diversion dam just upstream of the study limit does not affect major flood flows.

**Warning and response.** The [[National Weather Service]] (NOAA) provides forecasts and radar/satellite monitoring. Local media and law enforcement disseminate warnings. No specific flood-fighting or evacuation plans exist for the Virgin River area.

## Key Findings

**Historical floods**
- Damaging floods occurred near St. George as early as 1858. Twenty-nine major floods have struck the Virgin River since then.
- Highest recorded Virgin River peak: **22,800 cfs** at Virgin, Utah, on **December 6, 1966**. Peak at Littlefield, Arizona was **35,200 cfs**, versus a previous 22,000 cfs (March 3, 1938). Hurricane peak was estimated at 20,100 cfs.
- Highest recorded Fort Pierce Wash peak: about **15,000 cfs** in **August 1971** (U.S. Soil Conservation Service estimate), versus a gaged 8,760 cfs in 1964.
- Recurrence intervals: about **65 years** for the 1966 Virgin River flood and about **45 years** for the 1971 Fort Pierce Wash flood.
- The 1969 flood peaked at 13,660 cfs (Virgin) and 12,800 cfs (Hurricane), partly destroying the Washington Bridge.

**Design floods (Table 4)**

|---|---|---|---|
| Virgin River, upstream from Shinob Kibe (RM 115.5) | 1,586 | 36,000 | 82,000 |
| Virgin River, upstream from Fort Pierce Wash (RM 108.7) | 1,663 | 35,000 | 79,000 |
| Virgin River at Bloomington (RM 106.5) | 3,880 | 46,000 | 104,000 |
| Fort Pierce Wash at confluence with Virgin River | 1,660 | 24,000 | 75,000 |

- The intermediate regional flood has an average recurrence of once in 100 years, though it may occur more than once in a year.
- The standard project flood represents a reasonable upper limit, typically about 40–60 percent of the probable maximum flood.

**Hazard thresholds**
- Floodwater 3 or more feet deep moving at 3 or more feet per second can sweep people off their feet.
- Intermediate regional flood velocities: main channel 5–15 fps; overbank 1–5 fps.
- Standard project flood velocities exceed these by 1–5 fps.
- Virgin River peak stages for both design floods could occur within 32 hours of the start of net rainfall.

**Bridge vulnerability (Table 5)**
- Six bridges cross the study reaches. All except the Interstate 15 bridge are expected to suffer damage in the design floods.
- Example: the Washington Bridge (County Road 212, RM 114.71) has a low chord of 2,623 ft and a standard project flood elevation of 2,626 ft, so the design flood exceeds its low chord.

**Recommendations and purpose**
- The report does not propose engineering solutions. It supports land-use controls, flood proofing, and other [[Flood Plain Management]] measures to prevent intensified losses.
- Future flood-height maps (figures 18–22) and flooded-area plates (3–14) are intended for planners.
- Channel alignment shown on plates may shift, and mapped overflow limits may differ from ground conditions.
