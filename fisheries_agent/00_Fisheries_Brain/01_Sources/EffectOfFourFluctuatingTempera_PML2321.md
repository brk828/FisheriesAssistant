# Effect of Fluctuating Temperature Regimes on Cui-ui Survival and Larval Size

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study by Scoppettone, Buettner and Kisler (1993) tested how four diel fluctuating water temperature regimes affected [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*) survival from egg fertilization to swim-up, the timing of hatching and swim-up, and the length and dry weight of larvae at swim-up. The work was motivated by concern that elevated springtime water temperatures in the lower [[Truckee River]], caused largely by reduced flows from water diversion, may reduce cui-ui reproductive success. Swim-up success was highest in the coolest regime (8.9–15.0 °C) and lowest in the warmest (17.8–23.9 °C), and the authors concluded that cui-ui appeared best adapted to the coolest of the four regimes.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Cui-ui]] is an obligate lake dweller found only in [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada, and belongs to the family Catostomidae (suckers). It is listed as endangered under the U.S. Department of the Interior (1973).
- **Spawning:** Adults migrate each spring to the south end of Pyramid Lake and enter the Truckee River to spawn when flows and temperatures allow. Females lay roughly 60,000 to 195,000 eggs.
- **Longevity and history:** Cui-ui can live up to 43 years, which allowed the population to survive a 19-year period with virtually no adult recruitment. Physical modifications permitted passage around the delta at the river mouth, and annual spawning frequency has increased since 1976.
- **Management context:** Upstream storage and diversions have often left insufficient Truckee River inflow to reach the delta. Water stored in [[Stampede Reservoir]] is used to augment stream flows for reproduction.
- **Objective:** Evaluate recruitment from fertilization to swim-up under four laboratory temperature regimes to help establish temperature criteria favorable to cui-ui in the lower Truckee River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Temperature hypothesis:** Elevated springtime water temperature in the lower Truckee River is hypothesized to reduce reproductive success, and reduced springtime flow from agricultural and municipal diversion is the primary cause.
- **Fluctuating versus constant temperature:** Most temperature studies use constant temperatures, but in temperate streams water temperature fluctuates daily. The authors note that temperatures considered lethal under chronic exposure may not be if fish experience them only for several hours a day (Hokanson et al. 1977).
- **Spawning timing:** Cui-ui spawning is timed to the [[Sierra Nevada]] snowmelt peak, when lower Truckee River flow is high and water is cool.
- **Experimental water:** Water came from the Truckee River, pumped from the [[Marble Bluff Fishway Canal]], and was held at 10.6–12.7 °C before testing.
- **Regime design:** Each regime had a 6.1 °C daily fluctuation, the largest in the five preceding spawning seasons. Upper and lower limits closely bracketed temperatures that spawners experienced in those seasons.
- **Methods (brief):** Eggs (about 100 per jar) were placed in PVC egg jars and exposed to programmed diel temperature cycles. Survival was compared with a two-factor split-plot ANOVA, with temperature regime as the main factor, four experimental runs as the second factor, and five jars per treatment as subplots. Larval length and weight were compared with one-way ANOVA.

- **Survival trend:** Swim-up survival generally rose as water temperature fell. The three coolest regimes did not differ significantly from one another (p > 0.291). Survival in the warmest regime was significantly lower than in the others (p = 0.008).
- **Hatching timing:** Mean hatching peaked at 5 days after fertilization at 17.8–23.9 °C and at 14 days at 8.9–15.0 °C.
- **Swim-up timing:** Swim-up peaked at 9 days in the warmest regime and 23 days in the coolest. Duration of hatching and swim-up was 3 days at the warmest regime, versus 7 and 10 days at the coolest.
- **Inverse relation:** Hatching and swim-up time varied inversely with incubation temperature.
- **Larval length (1988):** Total length increased significantly as temperature decreased. Differences were smallest between 8.9–15.0 and 12.2–18.3 °C (p = 0.005), slightly larger between 12.2–18.3 and 15.0–21.1 °C (p < 0.001), and greatest between 15.0–21.1 and 17.8–23.9 °C (p < 0.001).
- **Larval dry weight (1988):** The heaviest larvae came from the coolest regime (p = 0.007) and the lightest from the warmest (p = 0.011). No significant difference was found between the 12.2–18.3 and 15.0–21.1 °C treatments (p = 0.880).
- **Conclusion:** The authors concluded that the coolest regime (8.9–15.0 °C) offered the greatest chance of survival from egg fertilization to swim-up and beyond, and that cui-ui appeared best adapted to the coolest regime.
- **Interpretation:** Larger, more robust larvae are likely better able to feed and escape predation, and may cope better with the energy demands of the post-swim-up period. Warmer temperatures may increase energy expenditure on maintenance, leaving less yolk energy for growth.
- **Upper thermal limit:** Cui-ui tolerated 23.9 °C when temperature fluctuated, while earlier unpublished work found no larvae swam up at a constant 23.3 °C.
- **Confounding factor:** The authors suspect that daily removal of dead and fungused eggs may have triggered premature hatching of neighboring embryos, which could partly explain the shortened hatching period.
- **Future work:** The authors recommend studying whether spawning-season timing affects gamete temperature tolerance and whether fluctuating temperatures during the spawning season influence survival.
