# Status of the Shorthead Sculpin (Cottus confusus) in the Flathead River, British Columbia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 status report by [[A. E. Peden]] and [[G. W. Hughes]] of the [[British Columbia Provincial Museum]] evaluates the [[Shorthead Sculpin]] (*Cottus confusus*) in the Flathead River system of British Columbia, the only known Canadian population of the species. As of 1981 a large population was present, but the authors consider it threatened because of its restricted distribution, lack of long-term population data, difficulty distinguishing it from the [[Slimy Sculpin]] (*C. cognatus*), and the risk of habitat degradation from the proposed [[Sage Creek Coal Project]]. The species was assigned threatened status by [[COSEWIC]] on 10 November 1983.

## Background & Description

The Shorthead Sculpin is a small, little-known cottid seldom exceeding 105 mm in length. Its body is light brown with dark mottling, and some individuals show three dark bars beneath the second dorsal fin. It was only recently recognized as a distinct species (Bailey and Bond 1963) and is very difficult to separate from the sympatric Slimy Sculpin. The authors note that some differences, such as fewer individuals with a post-maxillary pore and a somewhat higher fin-ray count, may distinguish Flathead drainage populations from others within the species.

The species was originally described from the Columbia and Snake River systems in Idaho, Montana and Oregon and from Puget Sound drainages in Washington. In Canada, it was known from only 26 specimens before 1981. The [[British Columbia Provincial Museum]] survey collected approximately 400 individuals in the lower 24 km of the Flathead River and tributaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution:** Substantial numbers occurred in the main Flathead River at about 1372 m (4500 ft) and in the lower reaches of Howell, Cabin, Burnham, Couldrey, Commerce and Sage creeks. Kishinena Creek also held some fish, but samples were too few to judge population size. Upstream areas of the Flathead, Commerce, Sage and Kishinena creeks held more Shorthead than Slimy Sculpins, contrary to earlier inferences. Distribution overlap zones were unchanged between 1980 and 1981.

**Habitat:** The species was most abundant over stony bottoms with little sediment and slow current, not necessarily in riffles. Riffle bottoms were composed of polished rocks or pebbles, while pools held more sediment. Shorthead Sculpins generally occurred further upstream than Slimy Sculpins, suggesting a preference for cooler water (not confirmed). Water in segments with known populations had alkalinity of 88–143 mg/L, pH 7.9–8.6, and turbidity of 0.3–68 JTU (mean 10.6 or lower) (BC Water Investigations Report, 1978).

**Threats:** The [[Sage Creek Coal Project]] proposes a new townsite, roads, transmission lines, diversion of [[Howell Creek]] upstream on the Flathead, groundwater use, and impoundments that could drain into the river. Removal of rubble during mining would greatly alter terrain. Logging, forest fires and siltation have occurred in the basin, but their effects on habitat are unquantified.

**Protection:** No species-specific measures were in force; general federal and provincial fish, wildlife and water quality laws probably offer minimal protection.

## Key Findings

- **Population:** About 400 specimens were captured in August 1981 by [[Electrofishing]], indicating a substantial lower-basin population, though population size and trends could not be estimated reliably.
- **Catch rates:** Shorthead Sculpins were caught at 2–8 fish per 100 seconds, versus 14 Slimy Sculpins per 100 seconds upstream, suggesting lower density per unit area.
- **Juveniles:** Only 14 first-year-class specimens were obtained; the authors state that if juveniles are as scarce as collections suggest, the population is in trouble.
- **Reproduction:** Maturity is reached at two to three years; females breed each April. Eggs per female ranged from 128 (55 mm standard length) to 690 (99 mm). Males may mature earlier (two years versus three for females), though this is unconfirmed.
- **Growth:** Canadian populations appear to grow faster than [[Big Lost River]] fish in Idaho, with third-year-class fish reaching 60–70 mm.
- **Diet:** Stomach analysis showed a predominance of aquatic insects, with other invertebrates and small fish possibly important.
- **Threat assessment:** The authors judge the population threatened on four grounds: restricted range, lack of long-term data, identification difficulty, and risk from coal development.
- **Conservation recommendations:** Monitor populations over several years, collect samples throughout the year to determine residency versus downstream migration, study juveniles, and assess habitat competition with the Slimy Sculpin.
- **Survey gaps:** Earlier provincial inventories (Sheehan et al. 1980) likely included mixed sculpin samples and did not separate the two species, so the Shorthead Sculpin has been overlooked in official surveys.
