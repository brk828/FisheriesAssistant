# 2003 Status of the Gila Topminnow and Desert Pupfish in Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) technical report, authored by Jeremy B. Voeltz and Robert H. Bettaso (December 2003), summarizes the status of two endangered fishes, the [[Gila Topminnow]] and the [[Desert Pupfish]], based on monitoring from 1998 through 2003. Both species declined sharply after European-American settlement of the [[Gila River Basin]], and the desert pupfish was eliminated from Arizona's natural waters apart from [[Quitobaquito Spring]]. Since 1936, reestablishment efforts have reached 178 wild localities, but only 19 sites supported Gila topminnow in 2003 and only 2 sites supported desert pupfish. The report reviews the recovery criteria, catalogs extant, failed, and extirpated sites, and recommends an aggressive program of 15–20 new stockings per year starting in 2004, possibly leading to downlisting by about 2020.

## Background & Description

**Gila Topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*)** is a small live-bearing minnow in the family [[Poeciliidae]]. In Arizona the genus includes a second species, the Yaqui topminnow (*P. sonoriensis*), native to the Rio Yaqui basin. The Gila topminnow historically occupied shallow margins of main river channels, backwaters, larger tributaries, and natural springs. It preferred quiet, warm water with slow current and abundant aquatic vegetation.

**Desert Pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*)** is a member of the family [[Cyprinodontidae]]. Its Arizona populations were mostly descended from fish originally taken from [[Santa Clara Slough]], Sonora, Mexico. The Santa Cruz pupfish population at [[Monkey Spring]] became extinct in the late 1960s, apparently after [[Largemouth Bass]] were introduced to its confined habitat.

**Quitobaquito Pupfish (*Cyprinodon eremus*)** is the only natural pupfish population remaining in Arizona, at [[Quitobaquito Spring]] within Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument. Introductions of this species elsewhere in the monument are believed to have failed, and mixed desert and Quitobaquito populations have complicated the species' reestablishment history.

**Regulatory history.** The Gila topminnow was listed as endangered in 1967 (32 FR 4001), together with the Yaqui topminnow. The first [[Recovery Plan]] was approved on 15 March 1984. A 1981 [[Memorandum of Understanding]] among the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS), U.S. Forest Service, and Arizona Game and Fish Commission guided reestablishment work during 1982–1985. The desert pupfish was also federally listed, and the recovery strategy is set out in a draft revised plan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Natural drought historically caused local extirpations, and populations recolonized rewetted habitats from permanent springs and small refugia. Human-caused declines followed several pathways:

- Dams that dewatered channels and blocked dispersal
- Introduced nonnative predatory and competitive fishes
- Wetland drainage for public health and agriculture
- Spring desiccation caused by groundwater pumping and arroyo cutting from land-use practices

Threats documented during the 1998–2003 monitoring include:

- **Nonnative fishes**, including [[Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Red Shiner]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]
- **Habitat loss** from drying, drought, and flooding (for example, Cave Creek was flooded in January 1993, the largest flood on record for that waterway)
- **Vegetation overgrowth**, especially cattails (Typha) and duckweed, which reduced open water
- **Livestock and recreational use**, including cattle trampling and heavy grazing near springs
- **Illegal stocking** of nonnative fishes into artificial ponds

Reestablished populations exist mainly in springs, seeps, stock tanks, and stream reaches within the species' historic range, as well as in some sites outside it (for example, [[Yerba Mansa]]).

## Key Findings

**Recovery criteria and population strategy**

- The original 1984 plan set a downlisting target of 20 wild populations persisting for 3 years. These criteria were met from 1985 to 1990, but downlisting was not pursued because many populations appeared fragile.
- The draft revised strategy recognizes **8 natural [[Metapopulation]]s at 14 localities** in the Gila basin (Level-1), which hold the only genomes available for recovery. Examples include the Upper Santa Cruz River (including [[Sharp Spring]]), Redrock Canyon, Monkey Spring, Lower Sonoita Creek, [[Cienega Creek]], and the Bylas Spring Complex (including [[Bylas Spring]]).
- **[[Level-2 Populations]]** are reestablished populations in the most natural available habitat that need minimal human help. They must persist at least 10 years to qualify.
- **[[Level-3 Populations]]** are reestablished in remaining natural or semi-natural habitats and may need more intervention. They may disappear if replicates are established elsewhere.
- Downlisting requires at least 4 pure Level-2 sites per metapopulation (32 total) and at least 20 mixed-origin Level-2 populations. Between zero and 60 Level-3 populations are needed, depending on how many Level-2 populations exceed the minimums. Level-3 populations count half as much as Level-2 populations.

**Gila topminnow status**

- Since 1936, 178 wild localities have been stocked or colonized by natural dispersal. More than 200 reestablishment efforts or dispersals occurred in total. Five stocking records could not be verified.
- Of the 19 sites known to support Gila topminnow in 2003, one (Yerba Mansa) lies outside historic range. The O'Donnell Creek population was stocked in 1974 but not collected again until 2003. Its origin is uncertain, so it is excluded from age calculations.
- Among the extant reestablished populations, the average age is 14+ years. [[Hidden Water Spring]] is the longest-surviving population at 27 years.
- Three of the 18 extant populations within historic range also contain nonnative fish. At [[Salt Creek]], mosquitofish were removed and native vegetation replanted in 1997–1998, and topminnows from Arizona State University were restocked.
- Table 4 shows that the number of successful introductions declined over time, from 182 introductions considered in total to 19 successful as of 2003.

**Desert pupfish status**

- Desert pupfish were stocked into 17 wild Arizona sites, 3 of them outside historic range. Only **2 reestablished populations remain extant**: Cold Springs (Site #85) and Lousy Canyon (Site #306).
- Four populations are of questionable genetic integrity because of possible hybridization with the Quitobaquito pupfish. Two of these (Kino Spring and Buffalo Corral Pond/Spring) were renovated, restocked with pure *C. macularius*, and later failed. One (Bog Hole) failed, and one (Finley Tank) still exists.
- Cold Springs had red shiners removed by seining and electrofishing in autumn 1998. Lousy Canyon was stocked in 2000–2001 with Gila topminnows from Coal Mine Spring and pupfish from the Imperial National Wildlife Refuge.

**Failed and extirpated sites**

- The report lists about 40 extirpated Gila topminnow populations that have been formally declared extirpated by AGFD and USFWS.
- Many failed sites were reviewed for restocking. Common causes include desiccation, flooding, cattail overgrowth, nonnative fish, and illegal introductions. Notable examples include [[Alambre Tank]], Mud Spring Tank, and Cave Creek, which was flooded out in 1993.
- All water sources at [[Fort Huachuca]] that once held topminnows or pupfish had been lost by 1989, and none remained in 2003.

**Recommendations**

- Begin an aggressive reestablishment program in 2004, stocking **15–20 sites each year** where suitable, with more sites than required to allow for some losses among Level-3 populations.
- Develop an implementation plan that prioritizes sites, identifies the managing entity, the source population, and the target level, and defines management needs.
- Use **Safe Harbor Agreements** on non-federal lands. Under section 10 of the [[Endangered Species Act]], landowners would receive assurances that no additional restrictions will be imposed due to conservation actions. Benefits include new habitats, mosquito control with native fish instead of mosquitofish, and new partnerships.
- If the plan's targets are met, downlisting could be pursued about 10 years after the required Level-2 and Level-3 populations are established, possibly by **2020**.
- Continue to identify new sites where reestablishment fails to maintain the required numbers.
