# Interactions Between Loach Minnow and Native Arizona Fishes: A Laboratory Aquarium Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1996 study by Tim Hurst (Department of Zoology, [[Arizona State University]]) examined behavioral interactions between the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and three native Arizona fishes: [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*), [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), and [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*). Six laboratory aquaria were used to tabulate "hits" (one fish attacking another) and analyze them by [[Analysis of Variance]] (ANOVA) and t-tests. Interactions occurred with all three species, but the greatest number involved [[Spikedace]], which was described as an active fish that aggressively taunts others. Male loach minnows actively defended their territory and undercut shelter. The author concluded that the species probably interact in the wild, and that interactions may intensify where loach minnow ranges overlap with spikedace.

## Background & Description

The study was motivated by the decline of native southwestern freshwater fishes, driven by [[Habitat Destruction]] (damming, channel alteration, riparian destruction, water diversion, groundwater pumping) and by [[Nonnative Species Introduction]], including the [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*). The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed the loach minnow and spikedace as Threatened throughout their native ranges. According to the text, no similar study of these four species had been conducted before.

**Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)**
- Small stream fish of swift riffles in water less than 6 inches deep.
- Small swim bladder; it sinks unless swimming vigorously.
- Typical lifespan about 24 months.
- Opportunistic omnivore feeding mostly on aquatic insect larvae.
- Stores adipose tissue in summer to survive low-food winters.
- Spawns March to June; eggs attach to the undersides of rocks on the leeward side of riffles.
- Both sexes guard nests, described as rare among cyprinids.
- Currently occupies about 15% of its historical range (Divine, 1986).

**Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*)**
- Small stream fish of pools and moderately swift creeks, generally less than 1 meter deep.
- Aggregates in shear zones, eddies, and the downstream edge of riffles.
- Breeding runs from April through June (spawning reported March through May).
- Eats primarily mayfly adults and nymphs, plus drift material.
- Eggs are demersal, developing on gravel and cobble riffles.
- Lifespan approximately 13 months, rarely exceeding 24 months.
- Remaining populations: Aravaipa Creek, Eagle Creek, and the upper Verde River in Arizona, plus the mainstem Gila River and its East, Middle, and West forks in New Mexico.

**Longfin Dace (*Agosia chrysogaster*)**
- Still occupies most of its native range, including most sandy-bottomed Arizona streams below 1,500 (elevation as given in the source).
- Tolerant of temperature extremes and low oxygen.
- Opportunistic omnivore eating mainly insects when abundant, and algae.
- Spawns December to July; males are territorial about sand nests.
- Midwater swimmer.

**Sonoran Sucker (*Pantosteus clarki*)**
- Medium-sized catostomid, 100 to 280 mm in length.
- Found in turbulent water, moving between riffles and rapids and protected spots near boulders; adults use deeper undercut areas.
- Diet is mostly filamentous algae and diatoms scraped from stones, with seasonal insect larvae.
- Spawns in late winter and early spring; rarely eaten by predators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

All four species share shallow, flowing habitats and must compete for food and space, which the author predicted would produce interactions. Water flow, food availability, and elevation were identified as limiting factors for these fishes. Loach minnows and longfin dace overlap widely in diet in Aravaipa Creek. The species co-occurred for millennia (Marsh, 1986), but habitat use varies with stream morphology (Rinne, 1989). Spawning seasons of loach minnow and spikedace overlap substantially, and spawning often correlates with flooding and the biannual rain pattern in Aravaipa Creek.

The experiment was designed around the hypothesis that interactions would not be detrimental to survival but would likely reduce spawning activity.

## Key Findings

**Experimental design**
- Six 10-gallon aquaria, each with a [[Powerhead Current]] to simulate riffle flow, gravel substrate, and cobble undercut areas.
- Lighting approximated natural sunlight cycles; fish were fed daily with live brine shrimp and bloodworms.
- Phase one: tanks 1 and 2 held one Sonoran sucker with a breeding pair of loach minnows; tank 3 held one spikedace with a breeding pair of loach minnows; tank 4 held a breeding pair of each of loach minnow and spikedace; tanks 5 and 6 held a breeding pair of loach minnows with a breeding pair of longfin dace.
- Phase two: tanks 1 to 3 had one spikedace with a loach minnow pair; tanks 4 to 6 had a spikedace pair with a loach minnow pair.

**Statistical approach**
- Hits were tabulated per fish per tank per observation period.
- Phase one: ANOVA on hits by date and by tank; a p-value above 0.05 was used to justify pooling. Tanks 1 with 2 and 5 with 6 were pooled; tanks 3 and 4 could not be pooled because of unequal spikedace sample sizes.
- Phase two: t-tests on tanks 2 vs. 3 and 4 vs. 5; no significant difference was found between tanks 3 and 4 and tanks 5 and 6, so data were pooled. Tanks 1 and 6 were excluded because no interactions occurred.

**Results**
- Phase one spanned eleven one-hour trial periods. The author judged the interaction "significant," although the reported ANOVA tests did not show significant differences across dates or tanks (probability values above 0.05).
- Most interactions occurred in tank 3 (one spikedace, two loach minnows). The other tanks, especially 1, 2, 5, and 6, had far fewer.
- Loach minnow with longfin dace produced more interactions than loach minnow with suckers, but both were at low levels.
- Phase two included four observations, recorded as one-half hour each in the Results; the Methods state intervals of one and one-half hours. Hits tended to rise over time, and tank 4 had the most hits.

**Discussion and conclusions**
- Interaction ranking: single spikedace with loach minnows (greatest), then two spikedace with loach minnows, then sucker and longfin dace pairings (fewest).
- Spikedace are highly active and taunt loach minnows. Male loach minnows defend the undercut area and attack spikedace that approach it.
- Both loach minnow and spikedace prefer fast water, so they competed for Powerhead flow. This competition also occurred with suckers, but less often.
- Breeding pairs kept each other occupied, which reduced interactions.
- Larger male loach minnows (tanks 3, 4, and 5) were associated with more interactions.
- Courtship behavior changed little, contrary to the original hypothesis.
- Laboratory artifacts were considered, but the author judged they did not drive the results.
- Recommendations: observe fish by snorkel in natural streams, use longer observation periods, build long trough-style streams, and study spikedace interactions with longfin dace, which may be more severe than with loach minnow.
