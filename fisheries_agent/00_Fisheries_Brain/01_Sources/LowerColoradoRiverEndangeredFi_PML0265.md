# Razorback Sucker Habitat Use in the Imperial Division: Phase II Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This preliminary progress report, prepared by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] under a [[Cooperative Agreement]], tracks [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) released into the [[Imperial Division]] of the [[Lower Colorado River]]. The study used [[Ultra-Sonic Telemetry]] to test whether fish used general habitat types (main channel, side channels, backwaters, impoundment, and [[Squaw Lake]]) in proportion to their availability. Using 154 relocations of the first fish group during December 1995 to December 1996, the authors found significant [[Habitat Selection]]: fish used the impoundment and backwaters more than available and the main channel less than available. The report is a preliminary, not-to-be-cited draft, and a second fish group was released in December 1996 to extend the analysis.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker was historically abundant in the mainstem and major tributaries of the lower Colorado River. Dam construction, water diversion, power production, and introduced non-native fish have nearly eliminated it from the river. Remaining important populations are mostly in [[Lake Mohave]], with lesser numbers in Lake Mead and Senator Wash Reservoir.

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed the species as endangered in November 1991 under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. In April 1994 it designated much of the historic range as [[Critical Habitat]], including the 100-year floodplain of the Imperial Division. Under Section 7 of the Act, federal agencies must ensure their actions do not jeopardize the species or adversely modify designated critical habitat.

Habitat use is well documented in the upper basin and some lower-basin reservoirs, but little is known for the Imperial Division. Its habitat is a long, uninterrupted stretch of mainstem river with numerous side channels and backwaters, bordered by wetland vegetation, and it differs from upper-basin and reservoir habitats. The project was designed in two phases under a 1995 joint agreement. Phase I was a literature review that produced [[Habitat Assessment Index]] values for the species (Bradford and Vlach 1995). Phase II, the subject of this report, is an in situ study of habitat use in the Imperial Division.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Logistics limited the work to the lower 12 km of the Imperial Division, bounded to the south by [[Imperial Dam]]. Fish could leave the area through the Colorado River main channel and, less often, through the Gila Gravity Canal and All-American Canal. Those canals have debris racks that normally block fish of this size, but the racks were periodically lifted for cleaning, which gave brief exit opportunities. Aquatic habitat was digitized into an AutoCAD database from aerial photographs. The mainstem was divided into twelve 0.8 km reaches, and each backwater, side channel, mainstem reach, Squaw Lake, and the impoundment was treated as a separate sampling site.

**Microhabitat and site variables.** At numerous stations in each site, the team measured depth, velocity, substrate type, temperature, conductivity, turbidity, dissolved oxygen, and percent submerged aquatics, at both bottom and mid-depths. Site-level descriptors covered accessibility to razorbacks, shoreline type (including percent emergent vegetation), and general shape.

**Study fish.** Two groups were implanted with [[Ultra-Sonic Telemetry]] transmitters and [[PIT Tagging]] tags:
- **Group 1:** 26 fish implanted on November 14, 1995 (Sonotronics CT-82-2 transmitters, 73-80 kHz; AVID PIT tags). The fish came from a pond at Floyd Lamb State Park, Nevada, and were from a 1984 year class produced at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] from Lake Mohave brood stock. Twenty-six survived surgery. They were transported on December 5, 1995, acclimated, and released on the following day. No transport or release mortality was seen.
- **Group 2:** 38 fish implanted in November 1996 using the same surgical methods. These fish had been held at Bubbling Ponds Fish Hatchery for about one year. Five died at the hatchery, probably because they were smaller and had less body cavity room for the transmitter. The 33 surviving fish were released in the Imperial Division on December 19, 1996, with no apparent release mortality.

**Monitoring methods.** Fish reconnaissance surveys were run by boat about every three weeks using a manual receiver and directional hydrophone. Surveyors stopped the motor and listened in a 360° arc at several frequencies in the 70-80 kHz range. Points, dense vegetation, bends, and noise from currents or boat traffic reduced detection. Locations were plotted on maps. Three [[Remote Sensing Stations]] were placed at the river and canal exits to detect emigrants. They were hard to maintain, suffered vandalism and unreliable power, and transmitter frequencies drifted with water temperature, which made individual identification unreliable.

## Key Findings

**Fish disposition (Group 1, December 1995 to December 1996)**
- Study fish were located 154 times within the study area during the period.
- The last three reconnaissance surveys relocated 10 of the original fish. Five of these had returned after an extended absence.
- One mortality was confirmed visually. Five more were suspected because the transmitter stayed in the same place for three consecutive surveys.
- The remaining fish were unaccounted for. Some may have left the study area, and others may have exceeded transmitter life. Transmitter life was expected to be about 14 months, ending around January 1997.
- An August 5-9, 1996 search of the river upstream of the study area, covering backwaters and side channels to Palo Verde Diversion Dam, found no study fish. The authors could not tell whether the fish were absent or undetectable because of background noise. (The search distance is garbled in the scan.)
- Two reconnaissance surveys after Group 2's release located 27 new fish and seven original fish. All Group 2 fish were either near their release sites or had moved to different sites.

**Habitat selection test**
- A [[Chi-Square Test]] showed use differed significantly from availability (χ² = 180.625, df = 4, P = 0.0000).
- [[Bonferroni Confidence Intervals]] on observed use (Table 2): backwaters 0.33-0.51, impoundment 0.16-0.32, main channel 0.04-0.15, side channels 0.02-0.12, Squaw Lake 0.10-0.24.
- The authors interpreted backwaters as used slightly more than available, the impoundment as used markedly more, the main channel as used substantially less, and side channels and Squaw Lake as used about equal to availability.

**Conclusions and management implications**
- Razorback suckers, often seen as habitat generalists, showed significant habitat selection in this first year of data.
- The authors expect the second year of data to change the degree of selection only slightly. Early movement patterns of Group 2 were similar to Group 1.
- Analysis of specific habitat features driving the non-random use has not yet been done. The authors think more "availability" data are needed to detect subtle differences between used and unused habitats, and they expect Group 2 data to supply this.
- The results should help management agencies conserve and restore habitat for the species.
- Two lessons for future telemetry work: fish may leave the study area without dying, and ultra-sonic tracking is probably ineffective in the faster, noisier upper sections of the Imperial Division mainstem.
- Final analyses of the Group 2 data were planned for late 1997.
