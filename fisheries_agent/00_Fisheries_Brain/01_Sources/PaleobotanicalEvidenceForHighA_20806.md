# Paleobotanical Evidence for High Altitudes in Nevada During the Miocene

#paleobotany #paleoclimatology #paleoaltimetry #woody-dicotyledons

## Executive Summary

This paper uses [[Leaf Physiognomy]] of fossil floras to reconstruct the elevation of western Nevada during the [[Miocene]]. Analyses of 12 mid-Miocene floras indicate that the [[Basin and Range Province]] stood about 3 km above sea level at 15 to 16 million years ago (Ma), roughly 1 to 1.5 km higher than its present altitude. Most of the subsequent descent to present-day elevations appears to have occurred by about 13 Ma. The authors argue that the thinning crust of the region requires subsidence rather than the late Cenozoic uplift proposed by earlier paleobotanical work, and they suggest that the widely used uplift-driven model of [[Ice Age Onset]] should be reevaluated.

## Background & Description

Fossil plants are considered sensitive indicators of past environments, and the paper contrasts two paleobotanical approaches:

- **[[Nearest Living Relative Method]]:** assumes a fossil species had the same environmental tolerances as its nearest living relative. The authors note that this assumption (that plants do not evolve new environmental adaptations) makes conclusions from this method doubtful to varying degrees. Earlier work using this method placed most of the Basin and Range of Nevada below 1 km until less than 5 Ma, when uplift was inferred to have begun.
- **Leaf physiognomy:** relates gross leaf features (outline, shape, and size) to climate. These features change predictably along present-day environmental gradients, and the authors use them as the basis for quantitative reconstruction.

To calibrate the method, leaves from at least 20 species of woody dicotyledons were collected near meteorological stations, mainly in North America and the Caribbean region, between 18°N and 62°N. The sampled vegetation ranged from desert to wet tropical forest to boreal forest. The data were analyzed with the [[Climate-Leaf Analysis Multivariate Program]] (CLAMP) using [[Canonical Correspondence Analysis]], which the authors prefer over linear methods such as multiple regression because leaf physiognomic traits respond nonlinearly to environmental variables.

The paper focuses on three inferred parameters: mean annual temperature (T), specific humidity (q), and [[Enthalpy]] (H). Enthalpy is central because it is a thermodynamic property of the atmosphere that varies with altitude. The authors rely on the principle of [[Moist Static Energy]], which is approximately zonally invariant, so that differences in mean annual enthalpy between sites at similar paleolatitudes give estimates of differences in potential energy, and therefore of altitude.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Ordination results (Fig. 1):** Axis 1, explaining about 50% of physiognomic variation, represents temperature factors. Axis 2, explaining about 20%, represents water stress. Enthalpy has a vector length almost as long as mean annual temperature, indicating that it is nearly as significant as temperature in explaining variation. Other tested variables (cold- and warm-month temperatures, growing season length, seasonal precipitation, and relative humidity) had shorter vectors than T and H. The modern calibration set excluded subalpine samples.

**Error budget:** The authors estimate an error of 4.5 kJ/kg from longitudinal variation in moist static energy, and a standard error of 4.2 kJ/kg for predicted enthalpy. Combined for two coeval sites, these produce a standard error of about 760 m in the estimated altitude difference.

**Geological setting:** The crust of much of the region has been extended and thinned over the past 40 to 50 million years. The authors argue that isostasy in a thinning crust requires subsidence. Paleobotanical evidence also supports Mesozoic thrust faulting and crustal thickening that built high terrain in the area before 16 Ma. By analogy with the [[Andes]] and the [[Tibetan Plateau]], elevations could have been higher than about 3 km before 16 Ma. Declining elevations coincide roughly with the onset of [[Basin and Range Faulting]] in the [[Great Basin]].

**Climatic comparison:** The [[Oxygen Isotope Record]] from marine sediments shows global cooling between 15 and 10 Ma, whereas the Nevada floras show a trend toward warmer temperatures. The floras also show moderate reversals in cooling during 13 to 15 Ma, which the authors use to justify alternative elevation estimates for some sites.

**Lapse rates:** The reconstructed mid-Miocene [[Terrestrial Lapse Rate]] was 2.0 to 3.3°C/km. This is about half the average modern worldwide value of 5.5°C/km, but comparable to the present-day empirical value for much of mid-latitude western North America.

## Key Findings

### Paleoaltitude of Western Nevada

- The authors estimate western Nevada at about 3 km elevation at 15 to 16 Ma, 1 to 1.5 km above present.
- Assemblages from rocks deposited 15 to 16 Ma imply paleoaltitudes of 2.9 to 3.2 km (Table 1 sites such as [[Eastgate and Middlegate]], [[Pyramid]], [[Buffalo Canyon]], and [[Fingerrock and Goldyke]]).
- The [[Fallon]] and [[Chloropagus]] assemblages imply altitudes close to present values, suggesting collapse may have occurred between 14 and 15 Ma. The authors caution that these assemblages are small, so the interpretation is uncertain.
- The [[Chalk Hills]] and [[Aldrich Station]] assemblages indicate that by about 12.5 to 13 Ma, western Nevada stood near its present altitude.
- Alternative estimates using the low-altitude [[Neroly Flora]] (10.5 to 11.0 Ma) or the [[Molalla Flora]] (13.1 Ma, Oregon) place the Chalk Hills and Aldrich Station altitudes no more than 400 m above or 600 m below present values.

### Sierra Nevada and Adjacent Basins

- The [[Weaverville Flora]] (about 14 to 18 Ma) comes from three low-altitude basins assumed to have been part of one depositional basin. CLAMP results indicate little or no change in basin altitude since deposition.
- The Mohawk, Webber Lake (about 14 to 18 Ma), and Table Mountain (10.5 Ma) assemblages from the [[Sierra Nevada]] all lie within 700 m of present altitudes.
- Since 14 to 18 Ma, the Sierra Nevada may have been no more than the western flank of the high plateau that formed western Nevada. The authors state this is consistent with the uplift model of Small and Anderson.

### Comparison With Earlier Estimates

- Earlier estimates of 3 to 4 km for mid-Miocene altitude were based on CLAMP temperatures combined with modern lapse rates. The present study's 2.9 to 3.2 km estimates are somewhat lower.
- The results contradict the claim that late Cenozoic uplift of Nevada occurred. The authors say the elevation drop is consistent with paleobotanical evidence of high late Eocene elevations in the Rocky Mountains of Colorado (about 35 Ma).
- The authors note that earlier estimates of early and middle Tertiary paleoaltitudes for western North America were probably too low.
- The contention that late Cenozoic uplift triggered the Ice Age should be reevaluated.

### Data and Quality Notes

- Table 1 summarizes environmental estimates for about 20 distinct Miocene leaf assemblages from California, Oregon, and Nevada, including estimated altitude, T, H, paleolatitude, and TLR. Some assemblages appear in more than one band, and two alternative reconstructions are given for the late middle Miocene.
- The CLAMP database grew from 106 samples to 97 after removing 25 outliers and inliers. Some samples from southern Sonora and Baja California were added later.
- Estimated altitudes derive from enthalpy differences between coastal and inland assemblages divided by 9.8 and assume a poleward enthalpy decline of 0.6 kJ/kg per degree of latitude.
