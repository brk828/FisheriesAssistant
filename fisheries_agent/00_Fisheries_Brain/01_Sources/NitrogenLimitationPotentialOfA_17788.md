# Nitrogen Limitation Potential of Arizona Streams and Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #limnology

## Executive Summary

This survey by [[Grimm and Fisher]] (Arizona State University) characterizes ambient nitrogen (N) and phosphorus (P) in natural running waters of Arizona and southern Utah. Samples from 196 sites on 157 streams in 13 drainage basins were analyzed to assess potential nutrient limitation using the atomic N:P ratio, with a threshold of 16:1 ([[Redfield Ratio]]). The study concludes that N is most often the critical element for algal production in the region, that [[Spring-Source Sites]] and [[Flood Water]] carry high nitrate concentrations, and that nitrate declines downstream of springs, probably through [[Autotrophic Uptake]]. The authors argue that phosphorus standards are largely irrelevant in Arizona and that nutrient management should be nitrogen-focused and basin-specific.

## Background & Description

The study distinguishes **potential** nutrient limitation, inferred from elemental ratios, from **actual** limitation, defined by [[Liebig's Law of the Minimum]] as reduction of a concentration to limiting levels by uptake. The paper focuses on the former. Earlier work in the [[Gila River Basin]] had found N:P below 6 in most systems during summer, suggesting potential N limitation.

Sites were classified by stream type (summer low flow, winter high flow, flood, and spring/spring-source) and by vegetation formation (desertscrub, grassland, chaparral, woodland, forest, after [[Lowe 1964]]). Summer low-flow sites (n = 92) were clear and often supported well-developed algae; winter high-flow sites (n = 14) were less clear and had less algae; flood sites were highly turbid. Spring and spring-source sites (n = 56) included off-channel springs and surface sources in intermittent channels. Desertscrub sites (n = 87) were most numerous, followed by grassland (n = 40), forest (n = 25), chaparral (n = 23), and woodland (n = 21).

Methods included filtration through Whatman GF/F filters, nitrate reduction by cadmium-copper columns, ammonium by the phenol-hypochlorite method, and phosphorus as [[Soluble Reactive Phosphate]] (SRP) and total dissolved P after UV oxidation. Nitrate-N was used in the N:P numerator because ammonium is unstable beyond about 4 hours, so N:P was calculated from SRP (or TDP where SRP was zero).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sycamore Creek** (Maricopa County) was sampled roughly monthly for four years and is the most intensively studied system, with mainstem and tributary sites (Pine, Camp, Mesquite, Picadilla, Rock Creeks). Its mean N:P for algal taxa was 17.1 ± 2.4 (95% CI), while [[Cladophora Glomerata]] ranged from 8.65 to 17.5.
- Nearly all perennial Arizona flow was represented, with sites concentrated in central watersheds. Large mainstems were underrepresented, and impoundment and flow regulation were noted as altering natural chemistry.
- Regional patterns: the [[Salt River Basin]] and Tonto, Sycamore, and Verde watersheds in central Arizona were largely N-limited in summer; the [[Little Colorado River]] basin showed low N:P; the Santa Cruz, San Pedro, and Gila basins showed low nitrate at most summer sites, with exceptions. Springs at the San Bernardino Ranch (artesian, probably from a common aquifer) had high nitrate.
- Volcanic-geology watersheds of the north were cited as relatively P-rich, which may favor potential N limitation in western North American drainages generally.
- Contrast: eastern North American aquatic systems are often P-limited, as cited from [[Likens 1972]] and [[Schindler 1977]].

## Key Findings

- **Ranges and means:** Nitrate-N averaged 252 µg/L (range 0 to 5,640 µg/L; SD 558). SRP averaged 41 µg/L (range 0 to 407 µg/L; SD 54). Total dissolved N ranged up to 2,450 µg/L in northern sites, and Total dissolved P ranged from 5 to 180 µg/L in the north.
- **Highest nitrate:** Leslie Creek source (site 194) at 5,640 µg/L, the highest recorded. Virgin River (site 1) and Colorado River (site 6) were high in northern Arizona; many Salt River flood and spring sites exceeded 1,000 µg/L.
- **N:P by site type:** Summer low-flow sites had significantly lower nitrate-N and N:P than spring-sources and floods (ANOVA among stream types: nitrate-N F = 8.96, p < 0.0001; N:SRP F = 7.4, p = 0.0001).
- **Potential N limitation:** 72% of all sites and 89% of low-flow stream sites had N:P < 16. Only 11% of low-flow sites had N:P > 16 (potential P limitation).
- **Actual limitation:** At 50% of stream sites and 35% of all sites, nitrate-N was below 10 µg/L, suggesting actual N limitation may occur. SRP was below 10 µg/L at 89% of sites, so actual P limitation was judged rare.
- **Downstream change:** Below 20 spring-sources, nitrate-N declined in all but one stream (Sonoita Creek, source concentration 0), with reductions up to 100%. SRP declined in only 12 streams, with a maximum reduction of 79%. N:P declined in all but two streams, often shifting from P-limiting to N-limiting values.
- **Vegetation type:** No significant differences among vegetation classes for any nutrient, likely because stream type dominated the variation.
- **Other ratios:** Ammonium was low at most sites. Dissolved organic N was often the largest fraction of total dissolved N in central and northern sites.
- **Management recommendations:** Given high between-basin variance, the authors recommend regional or basin-specific standards rather than statewide ones. They state phosphorus standards are "largely irrelevant," and that nitrogen "is clearly most often the critical element" and should be the research and management focus.
- **Supporting evidence:** Nitrogen was found to be the actual limiting nutrient to summer primary production in Sycamore Creek (Grimm and Fisher 1986).
