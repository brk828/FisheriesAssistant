# Use of Larval Light Traps to Capture Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 study, published in *The Southwestern Naturalist* (Vol. 38, No. 4), tests a light trap for capturing larval [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]]. The razorback sucker is federally listed as endangered, and its wild population there is threatened by habitat degradation and non-native fishes. Biologists from six agencies were spawning and rearing the species in backwaters to fish of about 30 cm before release. The authors modified an existing [[Light Trap]] design and tested four attractants over the spring 1992 spawning season. White halogen light attracted far more larvae than dog food or chemical light sticks, and capture rates rose with light intensity up to a point. The authors recommend caution with high-intensity light and further research to find optimal intensities.

## Background & Description

- **Target species:** The razorback sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*) was once common throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It now survives mainly in a few populations, including Lake Mohave. Larvae are phototactic, and earlier collections used spotlights, dipnets, and light traps, with poor results for light traps.
- **Trap design:** The authors based their design on [[Killgore Trap Design]] (Killgore, 1991). Traps were made of 6 mm thick PVC. Four 20 cm long, 10 cm diameter pipes were fused in a shamrock pattern to two flat disks 25 cm in diameter. The tops and bottoms were painted black to limit illumination to the trap's sides. Floats were removed so traps rested on the substrate, and entrances were enlarged to 19 mm.
- **Light and power:** Light intensity was measured 20 cm from the source with a Sperry light meter. Power problems from short battery life were solved by running cable from the traps to 12-volt DC gel-cell batteries on shore.
- **Sampling sites:** [[Yuma Cove]] and [[Arizona Bay]], two razorback sucker spawning sites in Lake Mohave. Water was clear, with Secchi depths of 4–5 m and turbidity of 1.7 NTU.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Phototactic species:** Several fish species respond to light. Reported thresholds include minnows (*Phoxinus phoxinus*), which respond only above a certain intensity (0.2–0.002 lux). Sunfish (*Lepomis* sp.) showed eye flickering at about 1,000 lux. [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*) showed phototactic responses up to 2,400 lux.
- **Avoidance of high light:** Several authors have reported fish avoiding high light intensity. Odonate nymphs and possibly razorback sucker larvae may avoid intensities above 4,500 lux.
- **Site differences:** Yuma Cove was isolated and highly productive. Dense zooplankton and macroinvertebrate swarms were attracted to the lights and obscured light readings. Arizona Bay was less productive, and its clearer water gave better trap performance for the 12-watt lights.
- **Turbidity:** The authors suggest larvae may be drawn into traps more effectively in turbid water.

## Key Findings

1. **Sampling effort:** 226 trap sets were completed between February and April 1992, with sampling twice a week. The text gives set counts by treatment and site, but the scan makes some total trap-hour figures unclear.
2. **Attractant performance:**
   - Dog food traps (n = 82) never exceeded 0.1 larvae per hour.
   - [[Cyalume Light Sticks]] (n = 92) produced at most 0.2 larvae per hour.
   - Neither bait nor chemical light attracted any razorback sucker larvae in the first 60 overnight sets.
3. **White light performance:**
   - 12-watt white lights (about 2,100 lux): 2.8 larvae/h (n = 15) in Yuma Cove and 29.8 larvae/h (n = 11) in Arizona Bay.
   - 55-watt halogen lights (about 4,500 lux): 6.0 larvae/h (n = 13) in Yuma Cove and 14.5 larvae/h (n = 13) in Arizona Bay.
4. **Odonate nymphs:** In Arizona Bay, 12-watt lights captured 3.03 nymphs/h, compared with 0.11 nymphs/h for 55-watt lights. This suggests the brighter light deterred nymphs.
5. **Larval size:** In a subsample of 48 larvae, mean total length was 11.3 mm (range 10.5–12.7 mm, SD = 0.5).
6. **Trap efficiency:** The authors estimate that fewer than 20% of larvae observed near the traps entered them.
7. **Method advantages:** Light traps give an index of relative abundance, not absolute density. Unlike netting, trapping is not biased by collector skill, and it can expand sampling effort.
8. **Recommendations:** Use white light rather than colored light or bait. Use high-intensity light with caution. Field-test optimal intensities for less light-sensitive species and turbid waters. The [[Gel-Cell Battery]] power supply allows more flexibility in intensity and duration than earlier designs.
