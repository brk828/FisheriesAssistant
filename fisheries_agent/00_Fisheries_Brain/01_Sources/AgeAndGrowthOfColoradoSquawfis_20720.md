# Age and Growth of Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Colorado River Basin, 1978–1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1991 draft completion report on the [[Age and Growth]] of endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. It was prepared by John A. Hawkins (Larval Fish Laboratory, Colorado State University) for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]]. A cover letter from the Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program asked external experts, including W.L. Minckley, to comment by July 15, 1991. The report combines scale-based ageing of 326 fish, back-calculated growth, weight-length and condition analyses, and a review of tagging records. An appendix also documents problems in the consolidated [[Tag Database]].

## Background & Description

Colorado squawfish is widely cited as North America's largest minnow. Historical accounts describe a maximum length of about 6 ft (1.8 m) and 100 lb (45 kg) in the lower Colorado River (Miller 1961). Early anglers reported fish 30 to 36 in (762 to 914 mm) long. Few fish over 31 in (787 mm) have been collected in the last 25 years. The species is one of several endangered fishes of the basin, alongside [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]].

Three earlier age and growth studies were reviewed:

- **[[Vanicek and Kramer 1969]]:** 167 fish aged from the Yampa and Green rivers in Dinosaur National Monument, 1964–1966. Only 38 adults were aged, and validation did not extend to older fish.
- **[[Seethaler 1978]]:** 68 fish from the Colorado River and 63 from the Green and Yampa rivers, collected 1974–1976. Maturity was assessed macroscopically in 147 fish.
- **[[Musker 1981]]:** 152 fish (Colorado 14, Green 103, Yampa 26, White 9), 1979–1981. Small samples led to inaccurate scale-radius regressions, and the first annulus was missed, so fish were underaged by one year.

The study set five objectives: evaluate scales as an ageing method, validate scales against other bony structures and mark-recapture data, compare growth among populations, determine age composition, and compare growth and longevity by sex. Objectives 2 and 5 were not met. Otoliths, vertebrae and opercula were not used for validation because the author's expertise was insufficient, and too few fish were sexed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Residency and stocks:** Adults tend to remain in specific river reaches. Fish were therefore grouped by river (Colorado, Green, White and Yampa). Smaller tributary samples were pooled with the nearest mainstem: Price and Duchesne with the Green, Little Snake with the Yampa, and Gunnison with the Colorado.
- **Seasonality:** Growth is linked to temperature and food, so fish were also grouped by month. Maximum relative condition occurred in June before spawning. Minimum condition in July or August was probably linked to migration or spawning. Condition recovered in late summer or early fall, which suggests feeding and growth are important then.
- **Growth season:** April through September were treated as potential growth months.
- **Annulus timing:** Annulus formation was observed in late May by the author and in early June by Vanicek and Kramer (1969). Growth resumption depends on food and temperature, so fish in warmer habitats such as shallow backwaters may start growing sooner than fish in colder main channels.
- **Recruitment:** Fish from the Yampa River were absent below about 350 mm TL. Most were over 450 mm, which suggests recruitment into the Yampa occurs after sexual maturity (> 428 mm TL), possibly after migration to spawning areas on the lower Yampa.

## Key Findings

**Sample and length data**

- Tagging and recapture records from 1978–1989 totalled 2,176. Most came from the Green (1,086), Yampa (465), Colorado (422) and White (146) rivers. Of the tagged fish, 279 were recaptured once, 42 twice and three three times.
- Of 165 fish sexed during spawning, 79% (130) were male.
- Tagged fish averaged 536 mm TL (range 81–896 mm). Mean lengths were 541 mm (Colorado), 519 mm (Green), 498 mm (White) and 577 mm (Yampa). The modal group was 500–550 mm.

**Weight-length relationships (mature fish, > 428 mm TL)**

- Slopes did not differ significantly among months within a river, but intercepts did (P < 0.05). Coefficients were therefore pooled by river:
  - Colorado: log₁₀W = −6.384 + 3.463 log₁₀L
  - Green: log₁₀W = −5.692 + 3.206 log₁₀L
  - White: log₁₀W = −5.555 + 3.156 log₁₀L
  - Yampa: log₁₀W = −6.026 + 3.339 log₁₀L
- Colorado and Yampa fish were heavier at a given length than Green and White fish.
- The author judged the Vanicek and Kramer (1969) and Seethaler (1978) equations not directly comparable, because sample sizes and maturity filtering differed.

**Ageing and validation**

- Scales were taken from 272 tagged fish (1979–1989) and 64 untagged Green River fish (1990). Regenerated scales made up about 25% of samples, and a minimum of six scales was usually needed for one usable scale.
- The fish-length to scale-radius regression, based on 336 fish, was L = 185.6356 SR + 45.1507 (r² = 91%). Ten fish with indistinct annuli were dropped, leaving 326 aged.
- **The first annulus was not observed on any scale.** Expected first-annulus lengths from [[Valdez 1990]] were 33.2–42.7 mm, which suggests most fish did not form a discernible annulus.
- Two hatchery-reared fish (born June 1974, stocked April 1980, recaptured May 1984) were correctly aged at 10 years after adding the undetected first annulus.
- Of 278 recaptured fish, only 18 had readable scales at both captures. The recapture interval was correctly predicted for seven fish (ages 7–13 at first capture). Eleven were wrong, ten by ±2 years and one by 6 years.
- The author concluded the scale method is suitable for at least the first 10 years, and that ages over 10 years are subject to unknown error.

**Age composition and maximum age**

- Most mature fish were age VII in the Green River, VIII in the White and IX in the Yampa. The Yampa had the broadest age range.
- The oldest fish were two Yampa River fish aged 18 years, over 800 mm long. Oldest fish from other rivers were 16 years (Green and White) and 12 years (Colorado). Musker's oldest fish was 13 years (879 mm, Yampa). Vanicek and Kramer's and Seethaler's oldest fish were 11 years.

**Back-calculated growth**

- Back-calculated lengths for all rivers combined (n = 326) averaged 94 mm at age 1, 150 mm at age 2, 220 mm at age 3, 286 mm at age 4 and 345 mm at age 5.
- Growth rates were similar among rivers up to about age VII or VIII, after which they diverged.
- Previous studies' lengths were 1 year younger at a given size than these, so this study's fish appear about one year older at length.
- Annual increments exceeded 60 mm between ages 3–4 or 4–5. They fell to about 30 mm after age XII and increased again around age XVI.

- The Colorado River estimate (L∞ 2837.7 mm, K 0.02304, n = 14) is biologically implausible given the tiny sample.
- Apart from Seethaler's data, growth was apparently similar among the studies.

**Relative growth and tagging effects**

- Relative growth from back-calculated lengths declined with age, to about 5–10% per year, and fish over 12 years grew about 6% of body length per year.
- Recaptured fish grew only about 1–2% of body length per year above 500 mm. The author suggested this may reflect a negative effect of tagging.

**Data management and recommendations**

- The consolidated database contained 3,740 records. Colorado squawfish made up 2,184 of the tagged fish. Other species included humpback chub (654), razorback sucker (362), roundtail chub (163) and bonytail chub (16).
- Errors documented in appendices included transposed or truncated tag numbers, duplicate records with conflicting data, colour-code mistakes and averaged lengths. Duplicates and erroneous records were corrected where verifiable.
- Recommendations: continue tagging adults and extend it to juveniles in lower reaches; phase out Carlin tags, which may harm growth and behaviour, in favour of [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag]] (PIT) tags; consolidate tagging records annually into a basin-wide database; and adopt standard data-collection procedures across agencies.

**Preliminary conclusions**

- Scales can be used to age Colorado squawfish, with the first annulus accounted for, at least through about age 10.
- Growth diverged among rivers after ages VII or VIII.
- The author planned to expand this work in a master's thesis due in late fall 1991.
