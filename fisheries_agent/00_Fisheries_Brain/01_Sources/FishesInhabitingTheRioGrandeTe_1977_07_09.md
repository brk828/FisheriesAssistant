# Fishes Inhabiting the Rio Grande, Texas and Mexico, Between El Paso and the Pecos Confluence

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #riverine-ecology #Cyprinidae #Rio-Grande

## Executive Summary

This study (Hubbs et al., 1977) surveys the fish fauna of the middle Rio Grande, spanning approximately 500 km between El Paso, Texas, and the confluence with the Pecos River, including reaches near [[Presidio]], the [[Rio Conchos]] confluence, and the [[Lower Canyons Of The Rio Grande]] within [[Big Bend National Park]]. Based on collections from 1954 and two intensive 1977 sampling trips (March and April), the authors identify **three distinct faunal assemblages** shaped primarily by salinity, streamflow permanence, and connectivity to tributary creeks: the **Saline Rio Grande fauna** (upstream of the Conchos, low diversity, salt-tolerant), the **Rio Conchos–Rio Grande fauna** (species-rich, riverine, downstream of the Conchos), and the **Tributary Creek fauna** (Chihuahuan Desert-derived species dependent on clear perennial tributaries). Two historically documented species, the [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus platyrynchus*) and the [[Rio Grande Bluntnose Shiner]] (*Notropis simus*), appear to have been extirpated from the study area, while several endangered/restricted species (e.g., *Gambusia gaigei*, *Cyprinodon eximius*) persist in vulnerable tributary refugia. Human impacts—channelization, irrigation diversion, reservoir regulation, and introduced species—are identified as major structuring forces on this fauna.

## Background & Description

- **Study region:** The Rio Grande between El Paso, Texas and the Pecos River confluence, a ~500 km reach historically understudied compared to Colorado, New Mexico, and downstream Texas segments.
- **Hydrology:** The river is highly modified near El Paso, where most flow is diverted for irrigation, leaving the channel frequently dry between El Paso and Ft. Hancock. Downstream, groundwater upwelling creates a saline stream that remains small until augmented dramatically by the [[Rio Conchos]] (historically and at time of study ~2 orders of magnitude greater flow than the Rio Grande itself, regulated by Luis L. León Reservoir).
- **Channel modification:** ~150 km northwest of the study area and a 16 km segment near the Conchos confluence have been leveed/channelized; an intervening 300 km segment was, at the time, proposed for "channel rectification," while ~200 km was proposed as a protected "wild river" (the Lower Canyons).
- **Minimal human impact zone:** Between Presidio and Amistad Reservoir, impacts are largely indirect (stream gauging weirs, river fords, minor irrigation diversion, overgrazing, exotic species introductions, pesticide runoff, mine tailings leaching).
- **Sampling methodology:** Collections were made via seining at 30+ stations mapped in two figures (Figure 1: channelization-affected reach near El Paso/Presidio; Figure 2: [[Lower Canyons Of The Rio Grande]]). Comparative use was made of long-term Big Bend National Park collections (1954–1976) previously reported by Hubbs (1958) and Hubbs & Wauer (1973).
- **Historical context:** Cited baseline salinity/flow issues date back to Emory (1859) and Thomas (1963), indicating persistent long-term aridity and intermittency in this reach.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Three Faunal Assemblages

**1. Saline Rio Grande Fauna** (upstream of Rio Conchos confluence)
- Dominated by four widespread, salt- and flow-variability-tolerant species: *Dorosoma cepedianum*, *Cyprinus carpio* (exotic), *Notropis lutrensis*, and *Lepomis cyanellus*.
- Low diversity (Shannon H' generally <2), attributed to harsh abiotic conditions: high salinity and intermittent flow.
- Fish were concentrated in pools rather than riffles; riffle sampling was largely unproductive.
- Impacted by exotics: *Cyprinus carpio* (likely suppressing native *Carpiodes* abundance), *Morone chrysops* (stocked near Del Rio, sparse due to trophic position), and *Pimephales vigilax* (probable bait-bucket introduction, first record east of Val Verde Co.).
- [[Indian Hot Springs]] (warm, salt-laden) were found to be entirely fishless, though *Gambusia affinis* congregated at the warm/cold water interface where springs entered the Rio Grande.

**2. Rio Conchos–Rio Grande Fauna** (between Conchos and Pecos confluences)
- Higher diversity: 20 species recorded near Presidio; 23 species in the Lower Canyons.
- Composed of riverine species not dependent on tributary creeks, e.g., *Notropis jemezanus*, *N. lutrensis*, *N. braytoni*, *Rhinichthys cataractae*, *Hybopsis aestivalis*, *Ictalurus punctatus*, *I. furcatus*, *Pylodictis olivaris*, *Dorosoma cepedianum*, *Cycleptus elongatus*, *Carpiodes carpio*.
- Only one introduced species in this assemblage: *Menidia beryllina* (inland silverside), a recent (post-1960) euryhaline invader likely spreading from Amistad Reservoir bait releases; predicted to eventually colonize the saline upstream Rio Grande.
- *Rhinichthys cataractae* considered a genetically/ecologically isolated population adapted to deep, warm canyon habitat, cut off from other populations by the saline/intermittent upstream reach — proposed as a diagnostic indicator taxon (with *Cycleptus elongatus*) for this river segment.
- Seasonal/ontogenetic sampling artifacts noted: absence of adult *Cycleptus* and *Rhinichthys* upstream likely reflects young-of-year timing and gear limitations (seines ineffective for adults in high water) rather than true absence.

**3. Tributary Creek Fauna** (Chihuahuan Desert-derived assemblage)
- Depends on tributary creeks (e.g., [[Capote Creek]], [[Alamito Creek]], [[Tornillo Creek]], [[Terlingua Creek]]) for all or part of life history.
- Three species may occur in creek or river (*Notropis lutrensis*, *Pimephales promelas*, *Notropis braytoni*).
- Three species use creeks only as juveniles (*Moxostoma congestum*, *Carpiodes carpio*, *Cycleptus elongatus*), with adults distributed elsewhere in the river.
- Eleven species most common in creeks but occasionally found in the river: *Cyprinodon eximius*, *Campostoma ornatum*, *Notropis chihuahua*, *Fundulus kansae*, *Astyanax mexicanus*, *Gambusia affinis*, and sunfishes (*Lepomis gulosus*, *L. cyanellus*, *L. microlophus*, *L. macrochirus*, *L. megalotis*).
- Two species restricted entirely to tributary waters: *Gambusia gaigei* (federally endangered, endemic to Big Bend NP) and *Dionda episcopa*.
- Seasonal migration between creeks and the Rio Grande documented (following Hubbs & Wauer 1973 for Terlingua Creek); 1977 sampling provided first direct evidence of fish movement into the river from creeks for two species.
- Notable faunal contrast between Capote Creek and Alamito Creek despite similar physicochemical conditions — attributed to differential exclusion by saline Rio Grande water at creek mouths blocking tributary-fauna colonization.

### Human & Exotic Species Impacts
- Exotic species of concern: *Cyprinus carpio*, *Morone chrysops*, *Pimephales vigilax*, *Menidia beryllina*, *Fundulus kansae* (the latter's spread flagged as a "primary concern," with recommendation against further bait-minnow introductions).
- *Micropterus salmoides* (largemouth bass) interpreted as likely naturalized/indigenous-adjacent recreational resource rather than a recent harmful introduction, expected range-wide east of the Conchos confluence.
- *Lepomis cyanellus* newly confirmed in Big Bend National Park, corroborating Hubbs' (1958) earlier prediction.

## Key Findings

- **Extirpated/possibly extinct species:**
  - *[[Scaphirhynchus platyrynchus]]* (shovelnose sturgeon) — historically reported near Albuquerque (Cope & Yarrow, 1875); only hearsay reports remain from Dryden Crossing and Coahuila tributaries; presumed eliminated from study area.
  - *[[Notropis simus]]* (bluntnose shiner) — historical New Mexico and downstream-of-Del Rio records predate or coincide with earliest study-area collections; absent from Trevino-Robinson's (1959) downstream survey; may be extinct in U.S. waters; recommend surveying the lower Rio Conchos for possible remnant populations.

- **Species counts by reach:**
  - 15 collections west of Conchos influence: 11 species, dominated by 7 ubiquitous species found in ≥9 collections (*Dorosoma cepedianum*, *Cyprinus carpio*, *Notropis lutrensis*, *Carpiodes carpio*, *Ictalurus punctatus*, *Gambusia affinis*, *Lepomis cyanellus*).
  - 6 collections near Presidio: 20 species (11 new relative to upstream reach; 2 absent that were present upstream).
  - 13 collections in the Lower Canyons: 23 species, 13 shared with Presidio-area/Big Bend records.

- **Endangered / range-restricted taxa highlighted:**
  - *[[Gambusia gaigei]]* — Big Bend endemic, endangered, restricted to tributary waters only.
  - *[[Cyprinodon eximius]]*, *[[Campostoma ornatum]]*, *[[Notropis chihuahua]]* — commonly listed as endangered due to U.S. range restricted to creek-mouth habitats; recommended for careful monitoring to prevent extirpation.
  - *Dionda episcopa*, *Lepomis gulosus*, *Lepomis microlophus* — recorded by Hubbs (1958) as inhabiting small clear tributaries, rare/absent from the river proper.

- **Notable range extensions / new records:**
  - First record of *Pimephales vigilax* east of Val Verde County (likely bait-bucket origin).
  - First report of *Fundulus kansae* in McKinney Spring, Big Bend National Park, reflecting ongoing introduced-species spread documented since Hubbs & Wauer (1973).
  - *Cyprinodon eximius* subsequently confirmed from Terlingua Creek (Miller, 1977), resolving one discrepancy with Hubbs (1958).

- **Species reported historically but not recaptured in 1977 samples:** *Anguilla rostrata* (catadromous; blocked by Falcon/Amistad Dams), *Hybognathus placitus* (absence "inexplicable"), *Aplodinotus grunniens* (likely just overlooked).

- **Diversity metric:** Shannon diversity index (H') values in the Saline Rio Grande assemblage generally fall well below 2.0, reflecting the constraining effects of salinity and flow intermittency on species richness/evenness.

- **Indicator taxa for river segment identification:** Presence of *Rhinichthys cataractae* and/or *Cycleptus elongatus* in a Texas collection is diagnostic of origin from the Rio Grande between Presidio and Amistad Reservoir, given their apparent absence/rarity elsewhere in the region.

- **Conservation recommendations:**
  - Continued monitoring of creek-mouth habitats supporting endangered Chihuahuan Desert fishes.
  - Cessation of further bait-minnow introductions to curb spread of exotics such as *Fundulus kansae*.
  - Targeted survey of the lower [[Rio Conchos]] to determine possible persistence of *Notropis simus*.
