# Dysommina rugosa, An Apodal Fish from the North Atlantic, Representing a Distinct Family

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Dysomminidae #eel #deep-sea-fish #taxonomy

## Executive Summary

This 1951 publication by Béhlke and Hubbs describes [[Dysommina rugosa]], a deep-sea eel from the [[North Atlantic]] that represents a new family, [[Dysomminidae]]. Two specimens were collected by the U.S. Fish Commission Steamer "Albatross" in 1886 from 352 fathoms depth in the [[Gulf Stream]] off southern Georgia. The species was independently described by Ginsburg (1951), but Béhlke and Hubbs provide detailed morphological and osteological analysis demonstrating that this genus cannot be placed in any existing eel family. The unique combination of characters—including paired frontals, enlarged otic bulla, ankylosed vomer-ethmoid, reduced palatopterygoid, and distinctive tooth pattern—necessitates recognition of a new family.

## Background & Description

[[Dysommina rugosa]] is an elongate, compressed, slender apodal fish with the anus positioned far anterior to mid-length. The body lacks scales and has smooth skin. Eyes are covered by a skin layer and positioned anterior to the mouth angle. The snout is long, rounded, and projects beyond the lower jaw, covered with small papillae along with the lips and lower jaw.

**Morphological measurements** (USNM 44324, 289 mm total length):
- Head length to anterior gill opening: 41.5 mm (0.14 of standard length)
- Snout to dorsal origin: 56 mm (0.19 of standard length)
- Snout to anal origin: 89 mm (0.31 of standard length)
- Greatest body depth: 21 mm
- Snout length: 15 mm
- Eye diameter: 4 mm
- Gape: 22 mm
- Pectoral fin length: 7 mm

The mouth cleft is wide, horizontal, and lateral. Anterior nostrils are tubular near the snout tip; posterior nostrils have flared rims with enlarged anterior flaps, positioned laterally immediately before the eye. Teeth are small and sharp, arranged in bands on maxillaries and mandibles. The vomer bears 4-5 large, conical, widely spaced teeth set in individual pockets on the vomerine shaft.

**Dentition details**: Maxillary teeth arranged in 4-5 wavy, irregular rows; mandibular teeth similar, in bands widest anteriorly. The vomerine canines are much enlarged, widely separated, with bases enclosed in skin folds lining the mouth roof, similar to some dysommids but lacking the anterior pair of vomerine teeth present in [[Dysomma anguillare]].

Branchial apertures are crescentic, oblique to nearly horizontal, positioned below the pectoral base and well separated ventrally. The dorsal fin commences behind the head, anal fin before mid-length immediately behind the anus. The caudal fin is well developed, pointed, and confluent with dorsal and anal fins. Vertical fin membranes are thick. Approximately 16 branchiostegals are present.

**Osteological features**: Frontals and parietals are paired. The mesethmoid is a thin V-shaped element, tear-drop shaped in cross-section, fused with the vomer. Pterygoid bones are reduced to thin splints throughout. The prootic is thin, little flattened anteriorly, expanding into a bulb posterodorsally. Maxillaries and mandibles are long and thin, bearing wide tooth bands. The hyomandibular is one of the largest bones, composed of a strong ridge and supporting shelves equal in width to the wide head of the bone. The opercle is elongate and low, originating on the lower half of the hyomandibular. The basioccipital-vomerine axis is virtually straight. Approximately 13-16 rudimentary gill rakers occur on the lower limb of the first arch. The slit behind the fourth gill is reduced to about one-half the length of the lower first gill arch.

**Coloration**: In alcohol, uniformly light tan with small groups of melanophores more or less evenly spaced along the body mid-line. Peritoneum black.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The two specimens (USNM 44324 and SU 24284, 215 mm total length) were collected at [[Albatross]] Station 2669, coordinates 31°09'00" N, 79°33'30" W, off [[Cumberland Island]], Georgia, on May 5, 1886. Collection was made using a large beam trawl in the [[Gulf Stream]].

**Environmental parameters**:
- Depth: 352 fathoms (approximately 644 meters)
- Bottom temperature: 43.7°F (6.5°C)
- Bottom substrate: Gray sand and dead coral

This deep-sea benthic habitat in the [[North Atlantic]] represents typical conditions for deep-water eel species. The relatively cold bottom temperature and substantial depth indicate an abyssal or bathyal zone environment.

## Key Findings

**Taxonomic placement and family establishment**: [[Dysommina]] cannot be properly placed in any described eel family as currently defined. While bearing external resemblance to several dysommids, particularly in tooth pattern, the genus exhibits a unique combination of characters:

- **Similarities to [[Moringuidae]]**: Palatopterygoid reduced to a splint; vomer ankylosed with ethmoid
- **Differences from Moringuidae**: Tail much longer than trunk; vertical fins not confined to tail; bands of teeth on maxillaries and mandibles (versus uniserial or absent)

- **Similarities to [[Heterenchelidae]]**: Tail much longer than trunk
- **Differences from Heterenchelidae**: Vomer ankylosed with ethmoid rather than separated by suture; palatopterygoid reduced to splint rather than well-developed and laminar

- **Similarities to [[Kaupichthys]]**: Trunk shorter than tail; lacking suture between ethmoid and vomer
- **Differences from Kaupichthys**: Posterior nostrils lateral rather than labial and tubular; palatopterygoid reduced to thin splint rather than well-developed and laminar; different character and shape of branchial openings

**Family diagnosis**: Based on the distinctive combination of characters—enlarged otic bulla, paired frontals, reduced palatopterygoid, ankylosed vomer-ethmoid, unique tooth arrangement, and external morphology—[[Dysommina]] is established as the type genus of a distinct family, [[Dysomminidae]].

**Historical context**: The species was first described in manuscript by Hubbs in 1916 as part of a collaborative report with Dr. C. H. Gilbert. Re-examination by Béhlke prompted combination of two independently written manuscripts. Ginsburg (1951) published a description of what appears to be the same form, but the authors' conclusions differ notably, leading to publication of this independent analysis using Ginsburg's names.

**Specimen data**: Two specimens examined represent the entire known material of this species at the time of publication. The permanent retention of one example at [[Stanford University]] was facilitated through the cooperation of the [[National Museum]].
