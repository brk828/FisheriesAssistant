# Endangered Species Act Reauthorization Under Economic and Political Pressure (1992)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1992 *CQ* news article by Phillip A. Davis examines the pending reauthorization of the [[Endangered Species Act]] (PL 93-205). The author argues that reauthorization will depend heavily on election-year politics and the economic effects of the recession. Business coalitions seek greater weight for economic factors and a narrower definition of "species," while environmentalists defend the law as the "crown jewel" of US environmental legislation. The article describes the reauthorization bill introduced by Rep. Gerry E. Studds (HR 4045), the congressional and agency studies underway, and several case studies in which listing decisions created economic conflicts. The article notes that the outcome was unresolved as of January 1992.

## Background & Description

The [[Endangered Species Act]] was enacted in 1973 to keep "politics and money" from determining the fate of threatened plants and animals. Under Section 4, listing decisions must rest "solely on the basis of the best scientific and commercial data available," so economic impacts are considered later by the Cabinet-level [[Endangered Species Committee]], nicknamed the "God Squad." The act is described as among the most popular laws Congress has passed. Its successes include the recovery of the [[Bald Eagle]] and the stabilization of the [[Grizzly Bear]] population.

Key actors include:
- **Legislators:** Rep. Gerry E. Studds (D-Mass.), chairman of the Merchant Marine subcommittee; House Speaker Thomas S. Foley (D-Wash.); Sen. Mark O. Hatfield (R-Ore.); Sen. Max Baucus (D-Mont.), chair of the Senate Environmental Protection Subcommittee; Rep. John D. Dingell (D-Mich.), original sponsor of the 1973 act.
- **Business groups:** Robert Szabo's proposed [[National Endangered Species Act Reform Coalition]]; Laer Pearce of the Southern California Building Industry Association.
- **Environmental groups:** Michael Bean of the [[Environmental Defense Fund]]; George T. Frampton Jr. of [[The Wilderness Society]].
- **Federal agencies:** the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[National Marine Fisheries Service]], and Interior Secretary Manuel Lujan Jr., who in May 1990 called the act "just too tough." Interior's deputy assistant secretary, Ed Cassidy, said the department was "taking a hard look" at its administration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article describes species conflicts across several regions:

- **[[Northern Spotted Owl]] (Oregon):** Protection halted 44 planned timber sales on federal land. Timber lobbyists say 5,000 jobs are at stake.
- **[[California Gnatcatcher]] (Southern California):** A small bird that is rare in Southern California but common in Baja California. Its preferred habitat, coastal sage scrub, overlaps with prime developable land in Orange, Riverside and San Diego counties. Builders said up to 200,000 jobs could be affected.
- **[[Snake River Chinook Salmon]] (Washington/Pacific Northwest):** Efforts to protect endangered salmon could raise the state's electric rates by $200 million. Business interests argue some protected salmon are subgroups of larger, non-threatened populations.
- **[[Tennessee Snail Darter]] (Tennessee):** Construction of the Tellico Dam was determined in 1975 to threaten the fish's habitat. The dispute led to the creation of the God Squad. The panel supported the fish, but Congress passed an appropriations rider allowing the dam to be completed. The Fish and Wildlife Service has conducted no recovery activities since.
- **[[Gray Wolf]] (Montana/Wyoming):** Listed June 4, 1973, and reported as "improving." Ranchers oppose its slow reintroduction to Yellowstone National Park from Canadian populations.
- **[[Mount Graham Red Squirrel]] (Arizona):** Listed June 3, 1987, and reported as "declining." Its habitat conflicted with an international observatory. Congress limited the environmental review, and construction continued after the Forest Service concluded it would not harm the squirrel.
- **[[Kemp's Ridley Sea Turtle]] (Gulf of Mexico):** Listed December 2, 1970, and reported as "declining." Shrimp nets killed many turtles. Shrimpers were required in 1989 to use "turtle excluder devices."
- **[[Louisiana Black Bear]]:** Added to the threatened list on December 30, 1991.

## Key Findings

**Scale of the problem and costs**
- The Interior Department's [[Endangered and Threatened Species Recovery Program]] report found that 76 percent of the 615 listed species are less than a quarter of the way toward full recovery.
- Full recovery for an estimated 3,600 threatened plant and animal species is estimated at $4.6 billion.
- A September 1990 inspector general report estimated that listing a single species costs $60,000.
- The Fish and Wildlife Service estimates that fewer than 25 percent of listings trigger economic conflicts.
- An inspector general audit found that at least 34 plant and animal species went extinct in the past 10 years without official protection. Some 3,600 "candidate" species await investigation.

**Funding**
- Interior will spend about $35 million in fiscal 1992 on endangered species programs, plus $16 million for research and law enforcement.
- Studds' bill would authorize $517 million over five years for Interior and Commerce, more than twice the amount authorized for 1988 through 1992.

**Studds' reauthorization bill (HR 4045)**
- Introduced November 26, 1991, and quickly gained 30 cosponsors. It is six pages long.
- Recovery plans would be due by December 31, 1996, for all 601 species awaiting protection. A two-year deadline would apply to species listed after 1993.
- It would favor "integrated multispecies recovery plans" that protect discrete habitats and allow development nearby.
- Federal agencies would get 60 days to respond to violations without lawsuits. Citizen suits would be permitted during that period for emergencies posing "a significant risk" to listed species.
- A new $20 million revolving Habitat Conservation Fund would support state, local and advocacy group conservation studies.

**Studies and oversight**
- On November 27, 1991, Foley, Hatfield and Studds asked the National Academy of Sciences for an in-depth study. The study could take up to two years.
- The General Accounting Office is conducting three related studies: a review of federal activity under the act over 17 years, a report on declining Pacific Northwest wild salmon, and an examination of act-related contracting.

**Political outlook**
- The Bush administration had not taken a clear position but showed some sympathy for business concerns. Officials said the removal of White House Chief of Staff John H. Sununu would make the process less divisive.
- Environmentalists doubt a Democratic Congress would pass the bill only for Bush to sign it to bolster his environmental credentials.
- Congress has several options: delay reauthorization until after the 1992 elections (the law stays in force with funding through annual appropriations), or pass a one-year reauthorization with a larger rewrite in 1993 or later.
- In the Senate, Baucus planned a straightforward reauthorization with hearings in 1992. The subcommittee's workload from clean water and solid waste reauthorizations works against the act.
- The 1988 reauthorization stalled in the Senate. Western senators opposed hunting penalties, and Southern senators opposed shrimper device requirements for sea turtles.
