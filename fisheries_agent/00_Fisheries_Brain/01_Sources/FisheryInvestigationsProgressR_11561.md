# Central Arizona Project Canal System Fishery Investigations: 1987 Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #cyprinidae #moronidae #dorosomatidae

## Executive Summary

This 1987 progress report from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] presents preliminary findings of fishery investigations in the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) canal system, which conveys Colorado River water from [[Lake Havasu]] toward central Arizona. Sampling in 1987 used standardized gill netting, electrofishing (aided by blocking gill nets), angling, stomach content analysis, and a 12-week egg and larval study (April to June 1987) at the Havasu and Bouse Hills pumping plants. Ten fish species have now been recorded in the canal through 1986-87. The canal fishery is dominated in Reach 1 by [[Striped Bass]] and [[Largemouth Bass]], with [[Threadfin Shad]] largely depleted by late 1987. The report concludes that the analysis is premature, that [[Striped Bass]] spawning within the canal is a key unresolved concern, and that management options such as periodic removal should be explored with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]].

## Background & Description

**Project context.** The CAP was authorized in 1968 and was nearing completion at the time of the report. Under the Final Environmental Impact Statement for the [[Havasu Pumping Plant]], Reclamation committed to monitor fish losses from Lake Havasu, and this commitment was expanded to investigate the importation of Colorado River fishes into central Arizona by the canal. The investigation was initiated in 1986 with Reclamation as lead agency, with participation from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), [[Arizona State University]] (ASU), the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS), and the [[Central Arizona Water Conservation District]] (CAWCD). Two companion efforts exist at [[Lake Havasu]] (preoperational water quality by Roline and Lieberman 1985; FWS fishery work in preparation) and at [[Lake Pleasant]] (a 2-year AGFD study).

**Canal description.** Water is lifted 824 feet from Lake Havasu by the Havasu Pumping Plant, through 7 miles of tunnel under the Buckskin Mountains. The Granite Reef Aqueduct is 190 miles long and divided into 12 reaches. Reach 1 is the first 17-mile section, with a concrete-lined bottom width of 24 feet for the first 5,550 feet, widening to an 80-foot bottom for storage. The [[Bouse Hills Pumping Plant]] (BHPP) sits at the end of Reach 1. Other reaches share a 24-foot bottom width, approximately 18-foot depth, and 1 1/2:1 side slopes. Under full operation (3,000 cfs) velocity would exceed 3 feet per second. Downstream facilities include the [[Little Harquahala Pumping Plant]] (Mile 57), [[Hassayampa Pumping Plant]] (Mile 120), and [[Salt-Gila Pumping Plant]] (Mile 190), followed by the Salt-Gila and Tucson Aqueducts.

**Canal operations.** In 1987 the CAP diverted 388,000 acre-feet (af), approximately 35 percent of Arizona's allocated portion (1.1 million af average) of the Colorado River. Monthly diversions peaked in summer, with September (72,590 af) and July (70,764 af) the highest and January (2,980 af) the lowest. No major dewaterings occurred in 1987; in late November and early December water in Reach 1 was drawn down to about 6 feet at the lower end.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Species and entrainment.** Fish are recruited into and distributed through the canal by entrainment from Lake Havasu and by natural reproduction in Reach 1; stocking by locals is also reported. Threadfin shad, largemouth bass, and bluegill are distributed from Lake Havasu to Brady Pumping Plant (Mile 250). Green sunfish, black crappie, and channel catfish reach the Hassayampa Pumping Plant; carp range to Salt-Gila Pumping Plant; razorback sucker and striped bass were collected only as far as Little Harquahala.

**Predator-prey dynamics.** Species composition varies between reaches and years, which the report attributes partly to dewatering, maintenance, and increasing predator size and numbers. In Reach 1, threadfin shad (85 percent of spring 1986 gill netting; 77 percent in spring 1987) were heavily exploited by largemouth and striped bass, and none were observed or collected by December 1987. Year classes 0 and I were absent for all species. In Reach 3, a late-1986 dewatering led to a fish die-off and a community dominated (91 percent) by shad, followed by a predominantly sunfish community (92 percent) at the Hassayampa forebay.

**Habitat and cover.** Protective cover is extremely limited. Small fish used cracks or joints in the lining, safety ladders, and submerged and floating tumbleweeds. Hundreds of sunfish used intake penstocks as cover when not in use. Large schools of largemouth bass used tumbleweed mats as overhead cover, and threadfin shad were observed spawning on this material.

**Water quality.** Fall diel measurements at five pumping plants recorded 12.0 to 14.8 °C temperature, pH 7.6 to 8.8, dissolved oxygen 9.2 to 11.5 mg/L, and conductivity 820 to 868 micro-ohms/cm. Little variance was noted. Sediments with high organic content, and gas generation suggestive of hydrogen sulfide or methane, were observed at forebay entrances and bends. Heavy periphyton (pennate diatoms, Lyngbya) occurred in Reach 3, and submergent vegetation including [[Sago Pondweed]] (Potamogeton pectinatus), [[Spiny Naiad]] (Najas spp.), and Chara spp. was reported, with Sago Pondweed causing maintenance problems in the first five reaches of the Granite Reef Aqueduct.

**Other biota.** Naucoridae (water bugs) and the snail Helisoma companulata were common. Diet analyses also identified Corbicula, algae, gastropods, and a snake's skeletal remains.

## Key Findings

**Species and distribution**
- Ten fish species were recorded in 1986-87; no additional species were collected in 1987. Neither adult razorback suckers nor redear sunfish were collected in 1987.
- Over 2,000 fish were collected during spring and fall 1987 sampling.
- Electrofishing in December 1987 produced 1,705 fish from 28,051 seconds of effort (3.65 fish/min); 175 fish were tagged.
- Angling in Reach 1 over 112.5 hours yielded 365 fish (3.24 fish/hour), with [[Black Crappie]] the most common catch (205), followed by [[Largemouth Bass]] (104) and [[Striped Bass]] (49). A majority were caught on artificial lures. Black crappies appeared much more susceptible to angling than to netting or electrofishing.

**Population and mark-recapture**
- A Reach 1 electrofishing effort for a density/biomass estimate failed because only one of four observed tagged largemouth bass was recaptured; both recovered tagged striped bass were heavily infected with fungus, one dead and one dying.

**Condition and growth**
- Condition (K) factor for largemouth bass fell from 1.39 (spring) to 1.21 (fall); striped bass fell from 1.63 to 1.09. The author states that growth and K typically increase over this period, supporting depletion of the prey base.

**Diet**
- Spring striped bass (n=15 stomachs): all contained chironomid pupae; by fall (n=16), 68.7 percent were empty. Fall diet was mainly filamentous algae, snails, ostracod shells, and scales, suggesting bottom scavenging.
- Largemouth bass (n=57; 35 spring, 22 fall): fish under 300 mm shifted from zooplankton (63.6% to 0%) and insects (63.6% to 50%) to a rise in empty stomachs (27.3% to 58.3%). Bass over 300 mm shifted from fish (50% to 8.3%) to insects (33.3%) in fall; empty stomachs rose from 29.2% to 41.7%.
- Black crappie (n=16; 15 spring, 1 fall): 93.3 percent of spring fish fed on zooplankton and 80 percent on insects.

**Egg and larval entrainment (April 7 to June 18, 1987)**
- 64 duplicate samples were collected downstream of Bouse Hills (4,213 m³ sieved) and 50 at the Buckskin Mountain Tunnel Outfall (4,439 m³ sieved), totaling 228 individual samples.
- Samples yielded 19,867 eggs and 99,744 larvae. Eggs: 18,691 (4.20 eggs/m³) at Havasu and 1,176 (0.28 eggs/m³) at Bouse Hills.
- Larval densities at Bouse Hills were about 20 times greater than at Havasu.
- Threadfin shad larvae: 0.8058 fish/m³ entering Reach 1 vs. 20.9751 fish/m³ exiting at Bouse Hills (approximately 25-fold increase), with shad observed spawning on drift tumbleweed piles.
- Embryo density rose from 5.33 to 22.76 embryos/m³ (ratio 1:4.3) through Reach 1.
- Channel catfish and razorback sucker larval densities declined, suggesting little or no successful reproduction in the canal.
- Water temperature during sampling ranged from 15 to 26 °C, bracketing the 16 to 21 °C optimum spawning range for striped bass (Moyle 1976).

**Striped bass reproduction**
- Only five pelagic eggs (averaging 1.8 mm, with oil globules), all from one June 3 sample at 10 p.m., were identified as striped bass, representing 0.02 percent of the Buckskin outfall sample at 0.0011 fish/m³. No striped bass larvae were found at either site.
- Over 100 adult striped bass from Reach 1 were examined; ripe males were common, but no mature females were found. Calculations (Grabowski et al. 1984) indicate striped bass up to 30.5 cm could pass the pumps.
- Striped bass males mature at the end of their first year; females mature at 4 to 6 years (Moyle 1976). The author expects female maturity within the next year or two.

**Management and recommendations**
- Striped bass are concentrated in Reach 1 (several thousand fish) and appear less susceptible to downstream drift under present operations.
- Potential striped bass spawning could drastically change downstream fisheries and threaten [[Lake Pleasant]] once connected to the system.
- Proposed options include removal of striped bass by mechanical, drainage, or approved fish toxins on a routine 3-year cycle; the author notes this may also optimize threadfin shad production and drift.
- AGFD and FWS expressed concern about importation of striped bass and tilapia into central Arizona.

**Future work**
- Egg and larval studies will continue through 1988 with expanded sampling through Little Harquahala and Hassayampa, concluding by end of 1988.
- Large framed nets will be placed downstream of pumping plants to measure rate and timing of juvenile and adult passage, expected to run 8 months.
- Fish survey work will move from biannual (spring and fall) to quarterly sampling in 1988-89. A concluding report is anticipated for spring 1990, with work completed by end of 1989.
