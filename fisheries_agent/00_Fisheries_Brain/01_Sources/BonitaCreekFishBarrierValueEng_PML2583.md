# Bonita Creek Fish Barrier Value Study Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-barrier

## Executive Summary

This draft Value Study Report, prepared by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Lower Colorado Region, Phoenix Area Office) and dated May 18, 2006, reviews the design of a proposed [[Fish Barrier]] on Bonita Creek, Arizona. The barrier is intended to stop the upstream spread of non-native fishes into habitat for native fishes, as required under a [[Biological Opinion]] tied to [[Central Arizona Project]] water deliveries to the Gila River Basin. The Value Study Team met on May 15, 2006 for a four-day study.

The estimated cost of the baseline concept is $1,399,195 (excluding unlisted items and contingencies). The team developed four proposals. If all savings proposals were accepted, their maximum savings potential is $500,427 after deducting the study cost ($34,000) once. Proposals No. 1, 2 and 3 are interdependent (accepting one precludes the others); Proposal No. 4 is independent. Two additional ideas were referred to the design team.

## Background & Description

**Regulatory driver.** In the late 1980s, concern arose over introduction and spread of non-native fishes into the Gila River Basin through CAP water deliveries. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] issued a Biological Opinion requiring Reclamation to protect native fishes, and fish barriers are specifically required.

**Site.** Bonita Creek is a tributary of the Gila River in Graham County, Arizona. The proposed site lies about 1.5 miles upstream of the confluence. At the site the creek is perennial with a flow of 2–4 cfs. The City of Safford, Arizona, about 18.5 miles away, has adequate size to supply local construction needs. The canyon is roughly 163 feet wide abutment to abutment. Abutment rock is volcanics and conglomerates, believed to continue below alluvium; rock was not encountered at reasonable depths (<60 feet) 15 feet or more from the abutments. Groundwater is about 4 feet deep across the channel, requiring extensive dewatering.

**Design.** The baseline structure is entirely reinforced concrete with four components:
- Upstream scour wall, extending to the depth of natural channel scour
- Crest, providing a minimum four-foot vertical drop to prevent fish movement
- Apron, a sloping grade for high-velocity flow exiting the structure
- Downstream scour wall, with riprap to prevent a scour hole (scour holes would serve as staging and refuge areas for non-native fishes)

The upstream end is arched to transmit loads to the abutments, eliminating piles.

**Access and design flow.** Reclamation must maintain roadway access for the City of Safford pipeline road, which is attached to the canyon walls near the barrier and serves an infiltration gallery about three miles upstream. A vehicle ramp at one abutment must still preserve the four-foot drop. The barrier is designed to withstand a 100-year instantaneous peak flow of 32,700 cfs (USGS gauging).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Target concern:** Non-native fish passage into native fish habitat in the Gila River Basin.
- **Stakeholders:** U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service and Reclamation (preventing non-native passage), [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (terrestrial impacts), [[Bureau of Land Management]] (landowner, opposed rerouting the road), City of Safford (access, water supply and quality), a cattle rancher and the general public (access), and Central Arizona Water Conservation District (maintenance).
- **Code and permit requirements:** Biological Opinion; Reclamation safety standards; Arizona Department of Environmental Quality turbidity limits; Section 404 and 402 permits from the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers; NEPA compliance; Storm Water Pollution Prevention; Arizona Department of Water Resources (dewatering wells).
- **Restrictions:** Site clearing must occur outside the breeding season of the [[Willow Flycatcher]]; construction is to occur in periods of low flood probability; continuous access must be maintained for the City of Safford and cattle rancher.
- **Function analysis:** The FAST diagram identified the barrier's core functions as preventing non-native fish passage, sediment collection, habitat destruction, and maintaining flows, access and project longevity, with riprap, excavation and dewatering supporting them.

## Key Findings

**Baseline cost breakdown (highlights):** Structural concrete and mass concrete, excavation, dewatering and riprap dominate the estimate; the total baseline is $1,399,195.52.

**Proposals and savings (after implementation costs, before study cost):**

| No. | Proposal | Net savings | Relationship |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Construct barrier as a straight crest, using reinforced earth | $29,402 | Interdependent (with 2 and 3) |
| 2 | Use [[Roller Compacted Concrete]] (RCC) for crest, apron and scour wall; eliminate downstream scour wall and riprap | $502,890 | Interdependent (with 1 and 3) |
| 3 | Place grouted riprap for portions of the downstream apron | $31,587 | Independent (but interdependent with 1 and 2 per report) |
| 4 | Reduce apron and cutoff wall thickness by 3 inches | $10,062 | Independent |

Notes on each proposal:
- **Proposal 1:** Simpler construction, reduced apron length, less flow concentration; structure stability no longer depends on abutment rock quality. Structure life may be reduced; longevity of reinforced earth was not evaluated. Nonrecurring cost falls from $1,399,195 to $1,294,794.
- **Proposal 2:** Shorter construction and simpler submittals. Disadvantages include sensitivity of RCC placement to high ambient temperature and possible aggregate treatment. Foundation substrate is unconsolidated and may need treatment. Value concept cost $821,305.
- **Proposal 3:** Replaces most of the structural apron with bedding and grouted riprap sourced from excavation; the low-flow notch concrete stays. Value concept cost $1,342,608.
- **Proposal 4:** Reduces apron and cutoff wall by 3 inches (apron reduction beyond 9 ft downstream), saving about 28 CY structural and 22.75 CY mass concrete. Value concept cost $1,379,133. Must confirm overturning and shear resistance at the crest.

**Ideas referred to design team:** Sheet pile wall instead of concrete barrier, and slurry backfill about a sheet pile wall.

**Ideas not developed:** About 60 ideas were screened out, mainly for feasibility and cost. Examples include electrical barriers, boulders, piled old cars or tires, ice dams, sterilizing the river, impounding and flushing the creek, and changing construction methods to avoid dewatering.

**Concerns raised by the team:**
1. Vent the air cavity beneath the barrier wall to prevent fish being drawn over the wall by negative pressure.
2. Dewatering cost may be low and should be re-evaluated.
3. Riprap cost may be overestimated if sourced from excavation; unclear whether blasting is included.
4. A cantilevered cap may be needed to prevent fish passage at flows above about 800 cfs, because low flow fills the tailwater "tree-nappe."
5. Numerous warped, two-way sloped sections could complicate construction.
6. Mass and structural concrete unit costs may be low; local suppliers could help verify.

The report notes that the cost model is conceptual, savings are at a similar level of development to baseline estimates, and final values may change as designs proceed.
