# An Enzyme Method Of Clearing and Staining Small Vertebrates

## Executive Summary
This 1967 paper by William Ralph Taylor (Smithsonian Institution, Division of Fishes) introduces an **enzyme-based clearing and staining method** for preparing transparent, bone-stained vertebrate specimens, as an alternative to the traditional [[Schultze Method]] (alkaline maceration with KOH/NaOH). The method uses **pancreatic trypsin** buffered in a **sodium borate (borax) solution** to digest muscle and connective tissue while preserving skeletal integrity, followed by staining with [[Alizarin Red S]] and long-term storage in glycerin. Taylor successfully applied the technique to several hundred specimens spanning **more than 100 fish species across 40 families**, as well as amphibians, reptiles, and mammals. The method is particularly valuable for restoring old, opaque, or poorly preserved museum specimens — including those previously damaged by KOH-based clearing — that are otherwise difficult to process using classical alkaline techniques.

## Background & Description
- **Purpose**: To develop a gentler, more reliable alternative to alkaline (KOH/NaOH) tissue maceration for producing cleared-and-stained osteological specimens, especially from long-preserved museum material that had become opaque, dark-stained, swollen, or ruptured under traditional methods.
- **Core innovation**: Substitution of **enzymatic digestion** (pancreatic trypsin/pancreatin) for strong alkali maceration, buffered in a **borax (sodium tetraborate) solution** that maintains a stable, favorable pH (~7.5+) over extended periods and suppresses bacterial odor/growth.
- **Historical context**: Builds on and critiques prior clearing methods, including:
  - [[Schultze Method]] (1897) — original alkaline clearing technique.
  - Modifications by Davis and Gore (1947), Evans (1948), Hollister (1934), Jensh and Brent (1966).
  - Early enzyme attempts by Moser (1906) using potassium carbonate + trypsin (found by Taylor to be slow, acid-drifting, and damaging to bone minerals).
  - Piechocki (1961) summary of papain, pepsin, and sodium sulphide–trypsin defleshing methods (found inferior to borax-buffered trypsin).
  - True (1948) — acidified pepsin method for fat/protein removal prior to staining.
- **Materials required**: Alizarin Red S (Biological Stain Commission-certified), potassium hydroxide, distilled water, 3% hydrogen peroxide, borax (sodium tetraborate), U.S.P. white glycerin, purified trypsin powder (pancreatic protease/pancreatin, free of collagenase and largely free of elastase), thymol (antimicrobial), 40% formaldehyde solution, ethyl alcohol, xylene.
- **Enzyme sourcing**: Fisher Scientific "Purified Trypsin Powder" (1:80 N.F.) and Difco "Trypsin, Difco Certified" (1:250 N.F.) both yielded good results. Approximately 100 g of trypsin is sufficient for approximately 100 small specimens.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
While this is fundamentally a laboratory/museum preparation methodology rather than a field ecology study, several biologically relevant parameters are documented:

- **Temperature sensitivity**: Digestion is temperature-dependent; a maximum of **30°C** is recommended for fishes to avoid connective tissue denaturation, whereas warm-blooded vertebrates (birds, mammals) and reptiles from hot environments can tolerate slightly higher temperatures without damage.
- **pH dependency**: Trypsin and chymotrypsin activity is optimal between **pH 7.5–9.0**; activity declines sharply below pH 7.5 or with excessive alkalinity. Borax buffer maintains pH stability better than alternative buffers tested (e.g., trisodium phosphate, hexamine, sodium hydroxide, potassium carbonate, sodium sulphide), all of which caused rapid pH drop and loss of alizarin-stained bone minerals.
- **Enzyme inhibitors relevant to specimen chemistry**: formaldehyde, alcohol, thymol, glycerol, fats, sugars, heavy metals, charcoal, unsaturated fatty acids, and bacterial contamination all inhibit trypsin activity — directly affecting how museum specimens (often stored in formalin/alcohol) must be pre-treated (washed) before enzymatic digestion.
- **Taxonomic breadth**: The method was validated across a wide range of vertebrate habitats and lineages — over 100 fish species (40 families), plus amphibians, reptiles, and mammals — indicating broad applicability regardless of original ecological habitat, though pigment-heavy taxa (e.g., [[Atherinidae|Atherinid Fishes]]) required longer bleaching due to thick pigment layers.
- **Specimen origin caveat**: Effectiveness depends heavily on prior preservation history (fresh vs. long-term alcohol/formalin storage), not on the organism's native aquatic/terrestrial habitat.

## Key Findings
- **Borax buffer superiority**: A **30% saturated sodium borate solution** (approximately 0.9–1.0 g sodium borate per 100 mL at 25°C) provides the best combination of enzyme activity support, pH stability (≥7.5), and bacterial/odor suppression compared to other tested buffers (Gomori-type buffers, sodium sulphide, potassium carbonate, sodium hydroxide).
- **Advantage over alkaline maceration**: Enzymatic digestion removes muscle tissue and stains via digestion rather than swelling, resulting in **slight collapse rather than rupture** of membranes — producing more anatomically faithful cleared specimens, especially from old or poorly preserved material.
- **Restoration capability**: The method can **rescue previously KOH-cleared glycerin specimens** that had become brown, opaque, split, or fragmented, by rehydrating and re-digesting them — often restoring near-normal anatomical position as swollen tissue collapses during digestion.
- **Optimal treatment protocol** (12-step method):
  1. Fix specimens in buffered 10% formalin (~4% formaldehyde) for 7–10 days.
  2. Wash out formaldehyde thoroughly (residual formalin inhibits/blocks enzyme activity — even 0.1% formalin can halt digestion entirely).
  3. Bleach in H₂O₂/KOH stock solution (enhanced by light exposure).
  4. Immerse in borax buffer solution (10–40× specimen volume, minimum ~200 mL).
  5. Add trypsin powder (~0.25 tsp / 0.45 g per small specimen).
  6. Digest until 10–25% of muscle tissue remains; change solution weekly.
  7. Stain in Alizarin Red S dissolved in KOH stock solution.
  8. Rinse, eviscerate, remove scales/unwanted tissue.
  9. Resume digestion to clear remaining muscle/stain residue.
  10. (Optional) Alcohol/xylene series to remove residual fats/oils.
  11. (Optional) Dilute KOH soak to remove guanine deposits.
  12. Transfer through graded glycerin series (40% → 70% → 100%) with thymol for long-term storage in glass containers (avoiding metal, which discolors glycerin).
- **Formaldehyde as critical inhibitor**: Even trace residual formaldehyde (0.033%) in buffer prolonged clearing time several-fold; concentrations ≥0.1% prevented digestion entirely — establishing thorough formalin washout as essential pre-treatment.
- **Solution longevity**: Digestion solutions retain peak activity for 7–10 days; should be discarded within 2 weeks to prevent bacterial putrefaction and excessive pH drop.
- **Failure modes identified**: Poorly preserved or previously alcohol-stored specimens (with prior connective tissue breakdown) may disintegrate during digestion — a risk shared with, but generally less severe than, caustic alkali treatment, since enzymatic digestion avoids the bulky swollen tissue mass that causes physical damage during handling.
- **Elastase/collagenase purity requirement**: Effective trypsin preparations must be free of collagenase and largely free of elastase to prevent unwanted degradation of collagen and elastin in connective tissue.
- **Comparative pH-damage data**: Specimens held in solutions with pH dropping below ~7.0 exhibited measurable **loss of alizarin-stained bone minerals** (e.g., dilute trisodium phosphate: pH 10.3→4.8; dilute sodium hydroxide: pH →5.0–6.0), whereas solutions maintained above pH 7.0 did not show this mineral loss — reinforcing the importance of the borax buffering system.
