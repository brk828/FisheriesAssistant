# Longitudinal Trends in Channel Characteristics of the Colorado River and Implications for Food-Web Dynamics

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared by [[John Pitlick]] and [[Robert Gress]] (Department of Geography, University of Colorado, March 2000) for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (USFWS), examines how lengthwise changes in the physical character of the [[Colorado River]] influence habitats and food resources for native fishes, especially the endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]]. The study covers about 300 km from Rulison, CO, to Potash, UT, combining cross-section surveys, gauge-based sediment load analysis, aerial photograph change analysis of the Rifle-DeBeque reach, and flow modeling of coarse-sediment transport thresholds.

Key conclusions: channel width changes little downstream while depth roughly doubles; suspended sediment loads rise almost fourfold through the study area; reservoir operations have reduced peak flows and annual sediment loads since the late 1950s; and framework gravels begin moving at roughly 35–55% of bankfull discharge.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin supports several federally listed endangered fishes. Demographic work by [[Osmundson et al. 1998]] found juvenile and subadult pikeminnow (under about 7 years and 550 mm) in canyon-bound reaches below [[Westwater Canyon]], while adults (over 550 mm) occur in alluvial reaches near Grand Junction. Historically the Rifle-DeBeque reach supported pikeminnow and [[Razorback Sucker]], but diversion dams in DeBeque Canyon block upstream migration.

The study is the second of a three-part project. It follows [[Pitlick et al. 1999]] on the Grand Junction reaches and runs alongside work by [[Lamarra 1999]] (trophic structure) and [[Osmundson 1999]] (fish community and water temperature).

The river was divided into 11 subreaches (strata), classed as fully alluvial (A) or quasi-alluvial (QA). Reach-average values include bankfull width ranging from 77 m (DeBeque Canyon) to 203 m (Professor Valley), bankfull depth from 2.45 m (Rulison-DeBeque) to 6.43 m (Big Bend), and median surface grain size D50 from 57 mm (Rulison-DeBeque) to 0.025 mm (Potash, sand bed).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology:** Runoff comes mainly from spring snowmelt in the Rocky Mountains. The river receives little additional water but much sediment from shale and sandstone surface erosion on the Colorado Plateau. About 24 reservoirs (>6.2 × 10^6 m³ capacity) lie upstream of the Colorado-Utah state line and store roughly half the annual flow. Diversions remove 10–20% of natural flow.
- **Regulation effects:** Mean instantaneous peak discharges at Cameo and Cisco fell by 29% and 33% after 1950 (Cress, 1997). Base flows in fall and winter rose.
- **Monsoon influence:** Late-summer storms produce high-concentration sediment pulses, becoming more frequent toward the southwest (downstream).
- **Riparian zone:** Native cottonwoods persist, while non-native tamarisk and Russian olive dominate many lower reaches.
- **Biological link:** Light penetration limits primary production in turbid, deep water. Downstream deepening and sediment increase reduce the sunlit bed area available to periphyton and benthic invertebrates.

## Key Findings

**Historical channel change (Rifle-DeBeque, 1937 vs. 1995 aerial photographs)**
- Average main-channel width showed no significant change over about 60 years.
- Island area decreased by about 20%; side-channel and backwater area decreased by about 31%.
- Proportioned over the 58 km reach, side-channel/backwater loss equals about a 6 m reduction in average width.
- Total remaining side-channel/backwater area in Rifle-DeBeque is roughly equal to that lost in the 15- and 18-mile reaches (about 60–70 ha, per [[Pitlick et al. 1999]]).
- Habitat suitability here is uncertain: [[Osmundson 1999]] reports water temperatures less favorable for pikeminnow growth. Adults may still benefit, especially in low-water years.

**Suspended sediment loads (USGS gauges)**

| Site | Long-term mean annual load (×10^6 tons/yr) |
|---|---|
| Glenwood Springs | 0.4 |
| Cameo | 1.5 |
| Colorado-Utah State Line | 3.4 |
| Cisco, UT | 5.4 |

- Loads rise almost fourfold downstream. Concentration, not discharge, drives the Cameo-Cisco increase.
- Sediment-concentration data show no appreciable change in concentration or grain size over time.
- Annual loads from 1934–1958 to 1959–1997 were 25–35% lower, mainly from reduced high-flow frequency. Mann-Whitney tests gave significant differences (p = 0.002–0.02 for sediment load; no significant discharge change).
- Annual sediment load varies more than discharge (coefficient of variation 0.66–0.82 vs. 0.29–0.40).

**Longitudinal channel trends**
- Substrate size decreases slowly relative to gradient, contrary to the common assumption of strong correlation.
- Bankfull depth roughly doubles (about +100%) over 300 km while bankfull width increases only about 30%. This departs from typical alluvial rivers, where width-depth ratio usually increases downstream.
- Bankfull dimensionless shear stress (τ*) is roughly constant through the gravel-bed reaches (Rulison to Professor Valley), implying a similar transport threshold from reach to reach.

**Coarse-sediment transport thresholds**
- Initial motion of framework gravels occurs at roughly 35–55% of bankfull discharge. Bankfull flow occurs on about 30 days per year.
- Estimated discharges, by tributary grouping (m³/s):
  - Above the Gunnison River (strata 9–11): initial motion 211–278; bankfull 580–623.
  - Gunnison to Dolores (strata 6–8): initial motion 497–548; bankfull 979–1,320.
  - Below the Dolores (strata 2–5): initial motion 561–659; bankfull 1,500–2,000.
- Estimates vary by a factor of 3–4 within a reach, reflecting local width and depth variation.
- Recommended flows: [[Bankfull Discharge]] flows from 1/2 bankfull upward carry about 50% of the annual load and should recur (at least 30 days per year).

**Biological implications**
- Mobile gravel substrates occur throughout the study area, as do backwaters.
- Turbidity is higher in lower reaches. The combined effect of depth and turbidity makes the lower river more box-like and less favorable for primary production.
- Periphyton and macroinvertebrate biomass decline from upper to lower reaches (Lamarra, 1999). Forage fish are much less abundant below Westwater Canyon (Osmundson, 1999).

**Recommendations**
1. Continue geomorphic studies above Palisade as DeBeque Canyon dams are modified for fish passage.
2. Monitor scour and fill in side channels and backwaters in the Rifle-DeBeque reach.
3. Continue USGS suspended sediment sampling.
4. Limit agricultural sediment and irrigation-return inputs near Grand Junction.
5. Maintain flows in the 1/2 to full bankfull range to deliver sediment and maintain habitat, and consider land-use measures on the Roan Plateau.
