# Generalized Geology and Structure of the Winkelman 15-Minute Quadrangle and Vicinity, Arizona

#geology #structural-geology #arizona-geology

> **Scope note:** This document is a geological paper (Krieger, *Journal of Research, U.S. Geological Survey*, 1974). It does not address fish, invertebrates, plankton, water chemistry, or aquatic habitat. The species, family, habitat, and ocean-basin fields are therefore marked N/A.

## Executive Summary

M. H. Krieger describes the geologic structure and stratigraphy of the [[Winkelman 15-Minute Quadrangle]] in Pinal and Gila Counties, southeastern Arizona. A northwest-trending belt of steeply dipping Precambrian and Paleozoic sedimentary rocks separates two contrasting structural domains. To the southwest, [[Precambrian Basement]] rocks, largely [[Ruin Granite]], are exposed for 60 mi. To the northeast, Precambrian and Paleozoic sedimentary rocks are gently tilted and intricately faulted.

The author interprets the steep belt as a single monocline later segmented by strike-slip and high-angle normal faults. Most tilting occurred after deposition of the early(?) Miocene [[San Manuel Formation]]. Faults that now appear high-angle are interpreted as former low-angle thrusts that were tilted to near-vertical during monoclinal folding. The youngest structures include low-angle gravity slides and [[High-Angle Normal Fault]]s that formed the [[Basin And Range Fault System]].

## Background & Description

The paper accompanies four 7½-minute quadrangle maps at 1:24,000 scale (Krieger, 1974a–d). These maps, together with the author's stratigraphic descriptions, are the primary references for detailed local data. Figure 1 indexes the study area and about twenty neighboring quadrangles in southeastern Arizona. The acknowledgments credit colleagues including Max D. Crittenden, Jr., and Norman G. Banks for discussion and review.

**Stratigraphy (oldest to youngest):**

- **Precambrian basement:** [[Pinal Schist]] and intrusive [[Ruin Granite]], dated at about 1,430 m.y. (Silver, 1968; Damon and others, 1962). The schist and granite were deformed, then overlain with angular unconformity by the unmetamorphosed [[Apache Group]] and the disconformable [[Troy Quartzite]].
- **Diabase:** About 1,200 m.y. old, forming dikes and sills in the Apache Group and Troy Quartzite and sill-like masses in the schist and granite. Sills in granite and schist lie parallel to the pre-Apache erosion surface.
- **Paleozoic:** Cambrian formations were deposited after a long erosional interval. Devonian, Mississippian, and Pennsylvanian rocks overlie the Cambrian strata.
- **Late Cretaceous volcanic rocks:** The [[Williamson Canyon Volcanics]] (andesitic), the [[Cloudburst Formation]] (latitic volcanics, fanglomerate, and breccias), and a rhyodacitic to quartz latitic pyroclastic unit. Late Cretaceous diorite and granodiorite, and Late Cretaceous or early Tertiary (Laramide) porphyry, intrude the older rocks.
- **Tertiary sedimentary rocks** (formerly Gila Conglomerate), now divided into:
  - [[San Manuel Formation]] (oldest): alluvial and playa deposits with interbedded andesite and megabreccias; probably early Miocene.
  - [[Big Dome Formation]]: alluvial, overlying the San Manuel unconformably.
  - [[Quiburis Formation]] (youngest): alluvial and lakebed facies, containing Hemphillian vertebrate fossils indicating middle Pliocene age.
- **Quaternary:** Pleistocene and Holocene alluvium.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper contains no biological data. Its environmental content concerns paleoenvironments and landscape history:

- **Playa conditions:** Claystone and mudstone with abundant mud cracks and curled mud chips indicate that part of the San Manuel Formation was deposited in a flat playa environment. The thrusts of the older structural phase are also described as forming in a flat environment.
- **Basin formation:** Basin-and-range faulting produced the basin filled by the Pliocene Quiburis Formation, on which the [[San Pedro River]] now flows.
- **Paleo-exposure:** Clasts of Ruin Granite in the basal San Manuel of the Putnam Wash quadrangle show that Precambrian basement was exposed west and southwest of the study area before San Manuel time.
- **Regional boundary:** A major northwest-trending fault, approximately beneath the [[Gila River]] and San Pedro River, separates the structurally complex southwest block from gently tilted terrain to the northeast.

## Key Findings

**1. Monocline as the dominant structure.** The steeply dipping belts are east-facing monoclines, not eastern limbs of anticlines. The author argues the almost complete absence of west-facing beds over more than 60 mi to the southwest rules out an anticline. Isolated west-facing beds are attributed to drag or later deformation.

**2. Timing of folding.** Monoclinal folding began before, and continued during and after, deposition of the early(?) Miocene San Manuel Formation. Evidence includes:
- The San Manuel dips steeply adjacent to the monoclinal ridges, though generally 20°–30° less steeply than older rocks.
- Radiometric dates on a rhyodacite tuff in the upper San Manuel (Crozier Peak quadrangle) are 18 and 24 m.y. (discordant biotite and sanidine).

**3. Thrusts older than the San Manuel.** Faults now steeply dipping are interpreted as tilted low-angle thrusts:
- [[Ripsey Wash Fault]]: an imbricate zone of east-facing slices, with slices spaced 50–100 ft apart north of point B. Cretaceous porphyries in some strands prove faulting preceded tilting.
- [[Romero Wash Fault]]: exposed in two segments; the southern segment is occupied by a tabular Cretaceous porphyry body.
- A thrust extending from the Crozier Peak quadrangle into the Black Mountain quadrangle, and possibly a thrust in the northwest Lookout Mountain quadrangle.

**4. Tear faults.** Northeast-, east-, and southeast-trending steeply dipping cross faults terminate at thrusts or transfer slip between thrust surfaces. Two east-southeast faults near point D, dipping about 60° south, show apparent reverse uplift on the south. The author notes this may reflect rotated strike-slip tears.

**5. Strike-slip faults segmenting the monocline.** Two major left-lateral strike-slip faults divide the monocline into en echelon segments:
- The northern fault moved the ridge between points B and C.
- The southern fault moved it from point G to east of point H.
- Evidence includes abrupt strike changes in the Crozier Peak quadrangle (suggesting drag) and offset of the post-San Manuel Camp Grant fault.

**6. Gravity slides.** Low-angle décollement surfaces separate younger sedimentary or volcanic rocks from older basement. Key examples:
- [[Camp Grant Fault]] in the Putnam Wash quadrangle: a surface dipping about 20° west (Figure 7), separating Ruin Granite from the San Manuel Formation, with conglomerate above it dipping about 35° northeast.
- Probable slides beneath the [[Cloudburst Formation]] in the Black Mountain and Mammoth quadrangles. In the Mammoth quadrangle, Creasey (1965, 1967) interpreted the structure as a gently east-dipping thrust, with overlying Cloudburst dipping 35°–65° east.
- The Camp Grant fault is offset by younger strike-slip faults and cut by north-trending normal faults.

**7. High-angle normal faults (youngest).** North-northwest-trending faults cut all older structures and are part of the [[Basin And Range Fault System]]:
- The Cowhead Well fault extends at least 12 mi from north of Putnam Wash into the western Mammoth quadrangle and is younger than both the San Manuel and the Camp Grant fault.
- The [[Galiuro Volcanics]] (early Miocene, dated 22–26 m.y.) east of the San Pedro River has been downdropped at least 5,000 ft.
- The change from low-angle gravity sliding to high-angle normal faulting is placed in the late Tertiary.

**8. Regional correlation.** Other monoclines lie 15–20 mi northwest of the Kearny quadrangle and in the Kearny quadrangle itself. The Kearny monocline extends over 6 mi and is interpreted as a separate but probably contemporaneous feature. Its diabase sill marks the area where the monocline flattened westward.

**9. Dating constraints.**
- Apache Leap Tuff: 20 m.y., older than the Big Dome and probably younger than the San Manuel.
- Rhyolite tuff in the Whitetail Conglomerate: 32 m.y. (Oligocene).
- Nonwelded ash-flow tuff in the Big Dome Formation (Kearny quadrangle): 14 m.y. (biotite) and 17 m.y. (hornblende), late Miocene K-Ar ages.

**10. Conclusions and development sequence.** The author proposes this sequence:
1. Imbricate thrusts and tears formed in a flat environment, later tilted as an east-facing monocline developed.
2. Folding began before and continued after San Manuel deposition, also forming monoclines to the north and northwest.
3. East-trending strike-slip faults segmented the monocline into three en echelon segments.
4. Parts of the San Manuel detached and slid over basement on a low-angle gravity surface.
5. North-trending high-angle normal faults cut all earlier structures.

The paper also notes that structural complexity in the study area contrasts sharply with the gently tilted, intricately faulted terrain northeast of the Gila and San Pedro Rivers (Ransome, 1919; Wilson and others, 1969).
