# Biogeography of Freshwater Fishes of Northwestern Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This 1996 Arizona State University Master of Science thesis by Heidi Blue Blasius analyzes the freshwater ichthyofauna of six river basins in extreme northwestern Mexico, chiefly in the State of Sonora, and asks whether within-region fish distributions reflect [[Ecologic History]] or [[Geologic History]]. The native fauna comprises 8 families, 13 genera and 20 species, or 25 entities when informally recognized and hybrid taxa are counted. The fauna is predominantly Nearctic in affinity. The author concludes that present-day ecology, not geologic history, best explains within-region distributions, and recommends prompt conservation planning because development and [[Nonnative Fishes]] are expected to erode native diversity.

## Background & Description

The study addresses the transition between Nearctic and Neotropical biotas. Mexico as a whole holds more than 500 freshwater species in nearly 50 families (Miller 1986). The study region drains the Sierra Madre Occidental and the [[Mesa del Norte]] westward across the [[Sonoran Desert]] to the Gulf of California. Four desert basins (Sonoyta, Concepción, Sonora, Mátape) lose their water to evapotranspiration and alluvial percolation, while the [[Rio Yaqui]] (about 70,000 km² in Mexico) and [[Rio Mayo]] (about 12,000 km²) reach the sea.

Fish records were sorted into three evidence levels: Level I (museum specimens), Level II (peer-reviewed literature) and Level III (agency or "gray" literature). Records were mapped in ARC/INFO using [[Geographic Information Systems]] and USGS and Mexican topographic quadrangles. Museum specimens from ASU and UMMZ were checked for questionable localities, and other museum records were excluded when doubtful.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geology.** The region's hydrography reflects [[Laramide Orogeny]] mountain building, Oligocene drainage formation, Miocene [[Basin and Range]] disruption, and Late Miocene to Pliocene uplift that reconnected the upper Gila River to the lower Colorado.

**Climate and hydrology.** Desert basins have experienced long-term desiccation. Northern rivers show bimodal discharge (summer monsoon floods, winter snowmelt floods). The Rio Mayo has a single late-winter high-discharge period. Average annual discharge figures include Rio Sonoyta about 1.9 × 10⁶ m³, Rio de la Concepción 1.51 × 10⁶ m³, Rio Sonora 1.71 × 10⁶ m³, and Rio Yaqui 27.9 × 10⁸ m³ at its mouth, which is more than 75% of the yield of all other study streams combined.

**Habitat.** Montane creeks are clear, cool and perennial. Ciénegas, which are riparian marshes, are increasingly lost. Low-elevation reaches are often intermittent, persisting only where groundwater surfaces.

**Vegetation.** Vegetation grades from [[Sonoran Desertshrub]] in the north through Sinaloan Thornshrub to Sinaloan Deciduous Forest in the south, with elevational zonation up to conifer forest.

**Sampling bias.** Sampling clusters along the US border and transportation routes. Gaps exist in the barrancas of the Mayo and Yaqui and on the agricultural deltas, where the fauna was already heavily altered before collecting began.

## Key Findings

**Fauna composition**
- Eight native families, 13 genera, 20 species; 25 entities including informal and hybrid taxa.
- Cyprinidae is the most speciose and widespread family, occupying sea level to the highest collected elevations.
- Four minnows (*Agosia chrysogaster*, *Campostoma ornatum*, *Codoma ornata*, *Cyprinella formosa*) make up a large share of locality records.
- The genus *Gila* contains several restricted endemics, including *G. purpurea* (Yaqui chub), *G. ditaenia* (Sonora chub) and *G. eremica* (desert chub).
- Five catostomids occur; *Catostomus bernardini* has the widest altitudinal range (90–2100 m).
- Ictalurus pricei (Yaqui catfish) is essentially confined to the Rio Yaqui basin.
- Poeciliids (three *Poeciliopsis* taxa plus a hybrid all-female form) and one cichlid (*Cichlasoma beani*) represent Neotropical affinities.

**Biogeographic affinity**
- 20 species (83%) are Nearctic in relationships; four (17%) have Neotropical affinities.
- Dorosoma smithi and Ictalurus pricei are considered ultimately Rio Grande–Mississippi derived, although a southern transcontinental origin is speculated for them.
- Thirteen of 24 species (54%) are regional endemics; single-basin endemics are *G. purpurea*, *G. ditaenia*, *Gila* sp. (Concepción) and *C. wigginsi*, together 21% of the fauna.

**Species richness**
- Across 11 basins (the six study basins plus adjacent Gila, Mimbres, del Carmen, Santa María and Casas Grandes systems), native richness correlates strongly with basin size (r = 0.60, df = 9, p = .005).
- After removing the Yaqui and Gila rivers, the relationship is not significant (r = 0.02, df = 7, p = 0.756), which the author attributes to habitat heterogeneity limits in small desert basins.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- Within-region distributions are better explained by present-day ecology than by geologic history.
- Regional mixing is enhanced by long-term habitat availability, headwater interdigitation in the mountains, and low latitudes that allow temperate fishes to reach far upstream.
- [[Nonnative Fishes]] (8 families, 19 species recorded; many introduced for sport, bait or food) are expected to spread and displace natives as reservoirs and regulation increase.
- The author strongly recommends a prompt conservation program that uses the distribution maps to identify concentrations of native species.
