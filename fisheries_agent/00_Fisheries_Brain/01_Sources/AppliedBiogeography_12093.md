# Applied Biogeography

#marine-biology #biogeography #ornithology

## Executive Summary

This chapter argues that [[Applied Biogeography]] should be guided by an "ethic of organic diversity": humans should add as little as possible to the global rate of species extinction, and lower it where possible. The authors stress how poorly known the living world is, and they warn against uncontrolled biotic mixing, using the proposed sea-level [[Panama Canal]] as the main example. They then apply [[Island Biogeography Theory]] to nature reserve design, using the forest-bird losses on [[Barro Colorado Island]] as a case study, and close with ideas for biotic enrichment and the creation of new communities.

## Background & Description

The authors open by noting that biologists do not know the number of species to the nearest order of magnitude. Estimates for animals rose from about three million (C. B. Williams) to about ten million. The upward revision reflects two findings: whole faunas, such as marine annelids, [[Abyssal Benthos]], and many insect taxa, remain poorly explored, and many "sibling species" complexes are common even in well-studied groups.

The chapter's central example is the [[Panama Isthmus]]. Its emergence three to five million years ago separated Pacific and Caribbean marine populations. The two sides differ in ways that matter ecologically:

- **Atlantic side:** moderate tides, sandy beaches, [[Mangrove Swamps]], and rich [[Coral Reefs]].
- **Pacific side:** strong tides, silty water, periodic [[Upwelling]] of cold nutrient-rich water, rocky shores from lava flows, and limited, depauperate coral reefs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Marine faunal divergence.** Of roughly 20,000 marine animal and plant species on both sides of the isthmus, perhaps no more than 10 percent are shared. For fishes and mollusks, fewer than 1 percent are shared.

**Competing predictions for a sea-level canal.** Four positions were debated:

1. Limited exchange, mainly Pacific to Atlantic, with no serious ecosystem disturbance.
2. The richer Atlantic biota would outcompete Pacific species, with combined extinctions possibly reaching 5,000 species ([[Briggs Argument]]).
3. Pacific species may be more opportunistic and better at invading disturbed areas. Under this model, flow would be mainly Pacific to Atlantic, so the total impact cannot be predicted.
4. Exchange would be unpredictable and dangerous, with extinction possible through competition and hybridization with geminate forms.

The authors conclude that biogeography lacks the theory and experience to predict the outcome. They cite the [[Yellow-Bellied Sea Snake]] as an example of how one wrong introduction could cause serious damage. Earlier mixing of aquatic biotas through the [[Great Lakes]] via the Erie and Welland canals is cited as a warning.

**Island biogeography and reserves.** Species number *S* scales with area *A* as log *S* = *a* + *z* log *A*, with *z* usually between 0.2 and 0.4. A rough rule is that a tenfold increase in area roughly doubles equilibrium species number. Newly isolated islands lose species over decades on the smallest islands and over centuries on islands comparable to large national parks.

**Barro Colorado Island (BCI).** The island is a 15.7 km² hilltop of lowland tropical forest, isolated about 1914 when [[Gatun Lake]] formed. It has been a protected reserve since 1923. Terborgh's extinction model predicted a decline of nearly 10 percent in resident bird species, which closely matched observations.

**Ecological truncation.** Large or specialized species disappear first. Hawaiian birds, such as the Kioea and the long-beaked Mamos, were lost early, and disease-carrying mosquitoes and lowland cultivation were implicated. On the New Guinea land-bridge islands, the largest island retained only 45 of 134 land-bridge bird species.

## Key Findings

**1. Ethic and knowledge gap.** Wilson and Willis propose an ethic that humans should not increase, and should lower where possible, the global species extinction rate. They emphasize that the number of species is uncertain to within an order of magnitude.

**2. Caution and biological barriers.** The authors recommend a "holding operation" to preserve diversity until systematics and ecology mature. Two independent groups, the [[University of Miami Team]] and the [[Committee of Ecological Research for the Interoceanic Canal]] (CERIC), recommended a biological barrier before any canal opens. Proposed barriers include bubble curtains, ultrasonic screens, and heated or fresh water.

**3. Barro Colorado forest-bird losses.** Of 208 bird species breeding on BCI in Chapman's time (1920s-1930s), 45 had disappeared by 1970. About 32 lost species were second-growth or edge birds, which are "weed" species that are not at high extinction risk on the mainland. About 13 were tall-forest species, which the authors expect to disappear as surrounding forests are cut. Loss rates have stayed at about ten species per decade, roughly three of them forest inhabitants.

**4. Mechanisms of loss.** Causes are idiosyncratic and not fully identified. Ground-nesting and ground-foraging birds were especially affected. Army-ant followers, a guild of seven species, declined sharply; the largest, *Neomorphus geoffroyi*, had already disappeared before 1960. Possible factors include reduced leaf litter, sparse ground cover, and high predation on nests, possibly linked to the loss of large predatory mammals.

**5. Density compensation.** Some small generalist birds are more abundant on the island than on nearby mainland forests. This "density compensation" does not offset the loss of large species, so diversity and biomass remain lower.

**6. Nature reserve design rules.**
- Make individual reserves as large as possible, and stop acquiring land at the point of diminishing returns for the most extinction-prone taxa.
- Place multiple reserves for unique habitats close together, so that populations can recolonize each other.
- Make reserves round and continuous, because of the peninsula effect.
- Extend extinction models to all taxa, including invertebrates and microorganisms, with priority for endemic and vulnerable groups.

**7. Small parks become invader-dominated.** Small parks tend to be dominated by rats, cockroaches, and sparrows. In Tappan Square, Oberlin, Ohio, only pigeons and starlings wintered.

**8. Planned biotic enrichment.** Species packing and "assembly rules" could enable planned communities that exceed natural assortative equilibria. The authors cite the Kaingaroa Forest of New Zealand (about 250,000 acres of exotic conifers) as a case where native birds and invertebrates thrived in a mixed community. They argue this is "what works in part by accident can be brought closer to perfection through design."

**9. Creation of new communities.** The authors discuss trace-element transformation of Australian deserts, and artificial reefs made from concrete rubble and tires off Florida and California, as early examples of deliberate community creation.
