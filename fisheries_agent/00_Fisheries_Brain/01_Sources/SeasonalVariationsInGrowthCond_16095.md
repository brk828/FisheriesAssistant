# Seasonal Variations in Growth, Condition and Gonads of Dormitator Latifrons in the Chone River Basin, Ecuador

#ichthyology #marine-biology #eleotridae

## Executive Summary

This 1984 *Journal of Fish Biology* study by B. D. Chang and W. Navas examines the natural life cycle of [[Dormitator Latifrons]], a food fish locally called "chame," in the [[Chone River Basin]] of [[Manabi Province]], Ecuador. Fieldwork was conducted in 1981 across upstream freshwater floodplains, the river above a seasonal dam, and downstream [[Mangrove Estuaries]] affected by [[Shrimp Farming]]. The authors found that growth, condition (Fulton's K) and gonad development track the flood cycle and salinity. Reproduction peaks during floods but continues at low levels in the dry season, which points to a loosely defined spawning period rather than a strict one. The estimated floodplain yield was c. 115 kg/ha in 1981.

## Background & Description

*Dormitator latifrons* is a popular food fish on the Pacific coast of Ecuador, where it is known as "chame." The fishery is centred on the Chone River system, where it is the most common fish caught. Fish are taken with [[Cast Nets]] in the river and with bamboo-fence traps in floodplains. It is also a common by-catch in shrimp farms in the estuarine zone. Small fish (5-15 cm) from estuaries and floodplains are used in simple pond culture. Earlier work cited in the paper reports that the species feeds mainly on phytodetritus.

The study set out to document seasonal changes in growth, condition and gonads across habitats. The species belongs to the family [[Eleotridae]].

**Study area**
- The Chone River, with its main tributary the Carrizal River, is c. 160 km long and drains c. 230,000 ha.
- A small earth dam c. 30 km upstream of the coastal port of Bahía de Caráquez was closed during the dry season to hold fresh water for irrigation. This dam is a key factor in the system's ecology.
- Upstream (freshwater zone): the river is 10-30 m wide and bordered by c. 6,000 ha of floodplains with abundant [[Water Hyacinth]] and *Ceratophyllum* sp.
- Downstream (estuarine zone): the river widens and is bordered by c. 4,000 ha of mangrove swamps and shrimp farms. Tidal range is 1-3 m.

**Sampling sites:** Upstream river (Site R), floodplains (F1-F4), and estuarine mangrove canals (D1-D4). Site D2 was lost to shrimp-farm construction in September 1981.

**Methods (summary)**
- Cast-nets, bamboo-fence traps and dipnets were used. Fish under c. 8 cm were under-represented, so 1-3 cm juveniles were sampled separately.
- Growth in length was estimated from cohort mean lengths (Petersen method), and instantaneous growth rate (G) was derived from weight changes.
- Length-weight relationships, [[Fulton's Condition Factor]] and the [[Gonadosomatic Index]] were calculated for 50-400 g fish. Sample sizes were usually 50-150 fish per site-period.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and seasonality**
- Climate has a wet season (January-April) and a dry season (May-December). Air temperature varied little, so temperature and photoperiod were judged unlikely drivers of reproduction.
- During floods (the dam was open 13 February to 13 May 1981), water and fish moved freely between upstream and downstream zones. In the dry season, the dam cut this connection, except for a small link at Site F2 until July.
- By the end of the dry season, most floodplains had dried. Surviving fish concentrated in deep upstream river pools and in estuarine areas.

**Salinity and pH**
- Upstream salinity was 0‰, except in residual floodplain pools (2-6‰) at the end of the dry season.
- Downstream surface salinity fell to 0-1‰ during floods and rose to c. 50‰ by the end of the 1981 dry season. The rise was later and more abrupt at Site F1.
- pH at Site R fell during floods and rose during the dry season.

**Fish migration**
- Flood-time migration moved fish from estuarine areas toward upstream floodplains, as shown by large catches at Site R.
- Bamboo-fence traps and the dam prevented fish from returning downstream as water levels fell.

**Abundance and yield**
- Site R catches reached up to 1,000 kg/day at flow peaks and were c. 10-20 kg/day in the dry season. Rough fisher estimates gave a total of c. 10-15 t at Site R while the dam was open.
- Estuarine cast-net estimates at Site D4 were c. 2 fish/m² during floods and 11 fish/m² in the dry season. The authors caution that these sites were chosen for high abundance.

## Key Findings

**1. Growth tracks water level and salinity**
- *Upstream (Sites R, F3, F4):* growth was 0.055-0.068 cm/day (G = 0.007-0.011/day) from February to August (high water). It fell to 0.034 cm/day (G = 0-0.006/day) from August to December. At Site F2, where water was lower, growth dropped to 0.003 cm/day (G = 0.004/day) in May-September.
- *Downstream (Sites D1-D4):* growth was 0.055-0.066 cm/day (G = 0.014-0.017/day) during floods and 0.026-0.040 cm/day (G = 0.004-0.009/day) from June to December. Downstream growth was negatively correlated with salinity (Spearman, P<0.05).
- *Site F1:* growth was 0.078-0.083 cm/day (G = 0.019-0.023/day) in April-May and August-October, and 0.009 cm/day (G = 0.002/day) in October-December.

**2. Length-weight relationship**
- Pooled slope b = 3.09, not significantly different from 3, indicating isometric growth.
- Intercepts (a) differed significantly between sites (P<0.05), indicating condition differences.

**3. Condition factor (K)**
- K was high during floods, fell in May-June, peaked mid dry season upstream, and declined late in the dry season when water was low. The decline came earlier at Site F2.
- Downstream, K was negatively correlated with salinity (P<0.05). It rose from January to March-April, fell from June to September, and stayed low until the end of the dry season.

**4. Reproduction and maturity**
- Smallest ripe fish: a 3.5 g (6.4 cm) male and a 6.0 g (7.5 cm) female.
- Smallest fish ready to spawn: a 60 g (15.5 cm) male and a 105 g (20.5 cm) female, both caught in the river while the dam was open.
- Estimated egg count in ripe ovaries: c. 1-3 × 10⁵ eggs/g.
- Gonadosomatic index peaked during floods (up to 0.083 in males and 0.176 in females) and was low in the dry season.
- During floods, 100% of fish over 50 g had ripe gonads. Dry-season ripe gonads were significantly smaller and often flaccid, suggesting partial spawning or degeneration.
- Ready-to-spawn fish made up 70% of fish over 100 g at Site R and 25% at floodplain sites while the dam was open. None were found in the dry season.

**5. Spawning pattern**
- Main reproduction occurred during the flood season, consistent with earlier Central American reports. Some reproduction also occurred in the dry season, when juveniles were present in both upstream and downstream zones.
- The authors conclude the species is a "total spawner" in principle, but the spawning period is less distinct than in classic floodplain species.
- Gonad development appeared to respond to a combination of rising water level and currents, falling salinity and pH. High water alone was insufficient, as shown by small gonads in June after the dam closed.
- Spawning sites were not directly observed. Juvenile distribution suggests estuarine spawning, while ripe fish in upstream sites during floods suggest upstream spawning.

**6. Age and recruitment**
- Fish hatched in the 1981 flood season would likely reach reproductive size by the next flood season or within two years. The largest fish sampled (41 cm male, 39 cm female) were probably 2-3 years old.

**7. Yield estimate**
- In 1981, floodplain owners reported a total catch of c. 40,000 kg from the sampled floodplain area (c. 350 ha when flooded), a yield of c. 115 kg/ha. Written records were lacking, so this is a rough estimate.
- The authors compare this with the 40-60 kg/ha/year sustained yield that [[Welcomme]] (1979) gave for normally exploited floodplains.
- Yields were reported to be higher in years with more intense floods.

**8. Effect of the dam**
- The dam altered natural seasonal water-level patterns and blocked estuarine and freshwater exchange during the dry season. Floodplains dried almost completely, leaving the estuarine zone and deep upstream pools as the main refuges.
- Intense floods can keep floodplains wet, allowing more fish to survive to the next flood.

**Conclusions**
Growth, condition and reproduction in *Dormitator latifrons* follow the flood-dry cycle and salinity gradients in the Chone system. The dam changes these natural patterns, and the species' persistence depends on refuge availability at the end of the dry season.
