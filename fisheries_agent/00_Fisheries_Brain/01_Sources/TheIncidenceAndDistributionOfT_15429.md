# The Incidence and Distribution of the Bass Tapeworm (Proteocephalus ambloplitis) in Southern New Hampshire Waters

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Centrarchidae #parasitology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1957 technical report documents a comprehensive survey of bass tapeworm ([[Proteocephalus ambloplitis]]) infections across 124 lakes and ponds in southern [[New Hampshire]]. Conducted during 1955-1956 under the Dingell-Johnson Project F-8-R, the study examined 1,960 fish representing 12 species to assess the parasite's role in declining [[Smallmouth Bass]] populations. The survey revealed that 80.2% of smallmouth bass were infected with plerocercoid larvae, with infections ranging from light to heavy. Of the 124 water bodies surveyed, 89 showed evidence of infection while 35 were tentatively classified as negative. The study concluded that indiscriminate fish stocking was largely responsible for widespread parasite distribution and recommended complete reclamation with rotenone as the only viable control method.

## Background & Description

The [[Bass Tapeworm]] ([[Proteocephalus ambloplitis]]) is a cestode parasite with a complex life cycle involving multiple hosts. Adult tapeworms inhabit the digestive tracts of definitive hosts including [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rock Bass]] (Ambloplites rupestris), Eastern burbot (Lota lota), bowfin (Amia calva), and [[Yellow Perch]]. The parasite produces characteristic dumbbell-shaped eggs that are consumed by copepod first intermediate hosts (including Hyalella knickerbockeri, Cyclops prasinus, C. albidus, and C. leuckarti). Within the copepod, the oncosphere develops into a procercoid larva. When infected copepods are consumed by suitable second intermediate hosts (various fish species), the procercoid penetrates the intestinal wall and becomes a plerocercoid larva, migrating to the liver, spleen, kidneys, gut walls, or gonads. The cycle completes when a definitive host consumes an infected fish, with the plerocercoid developing into an adult tapeworm in the intestine.

The survey employed multiple capture methods including 100-foot nylon gill nets placed above shoal areas, angling with rod and reel, seining, and spot reclamation using emulsifiable rotenone (treating 0.5 to 1 acre areas at 6 feet maximum depth). Field examinations involved exposing viscera and counting individual larvae, with infections classified as light (1-9 larvae), moderate (10-49 larvae), or heavy (50+ larvae). The parasite frequency index was calculated as the percentage of infected fish of a particular species compared to total examined.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The study area encompassed most freshwater ponds in [[Merrimack County]], [[Belknap County]], [[Hillsboro County]], and [[Cheshire County]], plus one lake each in [[Carroll County]] and [[Sullivan County]]. These water bodies were selected based on suspected historical or current [[Smallmouth Bass]] populations. The widespread distribution of [[Proteocephalus ambloplitis]] in [[New Hampshire]] waters was attributed to the introduction of infected bass from outside sources, followed by subsequent intrastate transfers that disseminated the parasite throughout the region.

Different fish species exhibited varying susceptibility to infection. [[Smallmouth Bass]] showed the highest infection rates and severity, with heavily infected specimens displaying abnormal quantities of connective tissue in the coelom that obscured visceral organs. Most larvae were found on mesenteries or embedded in proliferated connective tissue associated with parasite migration. [[Yellow Perch]] typically harbored larvae in the liver within whitish, thick-walled cysts that appeared to retard larval growth. [[White Perch]] demonstrated a severe host reaction, with larvae enclosed in tough whitish cysts; most larvae appeared dead or degenerating with brownish or purple coloration, suggesting this species is not a satisfactory host.

[[Pleasant Lake]] in [[New London]] provided significant evidence linking low infection rates to healthy bass populations. This water body yielded 44 smallmouth bass (the highest number from any single lake), ranging 2 to 12.8 inches in length, with most carrying fewer than 10 larvae or being completely parasite-free. The presence of numerous bass with low infection rates suggested normal reproductive capacity and sufficient annual recruitment to maintain population levels.

## Key Findings

**Survey Scope and Sample Sizes:**
- 124 lakes and ponds surveyed across six counties
- 1,960 total fish examined representing 12 species
- 192 [[Smallmouth Bass]] from 54 water bodies (2.0 to 19.7 inches length)
- 472 [[Yellow Perch]] from 92 ponds (2.3 to 17 inches length)
- 47 [[Largemouth Bass]] from 20 lakes (2.5 to 13.7 inches length)
- 154 [[Eastern Chain Pickerel]] from 68 lakes (5.0 to 23.5 inches length)
- 303 sunfish from 68 ponds
- 173 [[Brown Bullhead]]s captured
- 118 [[White Perch]] from 20 lakes

**Infection Rates by Species (Parasite Frequency Index):**
- [[Smallmouth Bass]]: 80.2% infected (154 of 192 fish)
- [[Yellow Perch]]: 46.2% infected (218 of 472 fish)
- [[Eastern Chain Pickerel]]: 39.6% infected (61 of 154 fish)
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: 34.0% infected (16 of 47 fish)
- Sunfish: 25.7% infected (78 of 303 fish)
- [[White Perch]]: 22.8% infected (27 of 118 fish)
- [[Brown Bullhead]]: 22.5% infected (39 of 173 fish)

**Infection Patterns and Severity:**
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] measuring 10-13 inches were most heavily infected
- Younger bass specimens showed fewer parasites or were completely negative
- Infections in [[Smallmouth Bass]] were predominantly moderate to heavy
- [[Largemouth Bass]] infections ranged from light to moderate, with one exception from [[Jackman Reservoir]] showing very heavy infection
- [[Eastern Chain Pickerel]] typically carried 1-15 larvae in liver, fat, and mesenteries; one specimen from [[Upper Suncook Lake]] contained 106 larvae
- Only 7 sunfish were moderately infected; remainder were lightly infected or negative
- Only 7 [[Brown Bullhead]]s were moderately infected
- Only 4 [[White Perch]] were moderately infected; most larvae in this species were dead or degenerating

**Water Body Classification:**
- 35 ponds tentatively classified as negative for bass tapeworm
- Only [[Hubbard Pond]] (Rindge) had an existing [[Smallmouth Bass]] population and tested negative
- 39 ponds (31.45%) showed heavy infection
- 37 ponds (29.84%) showed moderate infection
- 13 ponds showed light infection
- Many "negative" samples considered inadequate for definitive determination

**Comparative Regional Data:**
The study compared infection rates with neighboring states. [[Connecticut]] data showed smallmouth bass with 30-69% infection rates, while [[Central Massachusetts]] reported 95% infection in 20 examined bass. [[New Hampshire]]'s 80.2% infection rate (192 fish examined) fell between these values.

**Pathological Effects:**
- Heavily infected bass contained abnormal connective tissue proliferation obscuring visceral organs
- Larvae commonly found on mesenteries and embedded in connective tissue networks
- Liver infections showed larvae in thin membranous cysts near organ surface
- Gonadal infections occasionally observed, though often gonads were unidentifiable due to connective tissue masses
- Previous research cited indicated larvae in ovaries caused egg degeneration and prevented spawning
- Absence of well-defined bass fry schools in suitable habitats attributed to heavy infections

**Management Implications:**
- Indiscriminate fish stocking identified as primary cause of widespread parasite distribution
- [[Todd Lake]] (Bradford) and [[Skatutakee Lake]] (Harrisville) showed low infection rates (10% and 10.9% respectively) in [[Yellow Perch]], suggesting light infections in unmeasured bass populations
- Control under natural conditions deemed extremely difficult
- Complete reclamation with rotenone identified as only viable control method
- Non-parasitized bass from swift-running rivers and streams recommended for restocking
- Bass fry captured immediately after yolk sac absorption (before feeding) recommended for stocking to minimize contamination risk

**Recommendations Issued:**
1. Prohibit transfer of fish from infected ponds to any other water bodies
2. Consider managing heavily infected ponds for alternative species
3. Implement complete reclamation and restocking with non-parasitized fish when possible
4. Prohibit use of [[Yellow Perch]], [[White Perch]], and sunfishes as bait fish
5. Enact stringent regulations preventing indiscriminate fish transfers by private individuals

The report emphasized that while heavy plerocercoid infections likely prevent bass reproduction, other factors affecting bass survival must be considered, including absence of suitable spawning facilities and the species' exacting habitat requirements. The study was funded through Federal Aid to Fisheries Project F-8-R under the Dingell-Johnson Act.
