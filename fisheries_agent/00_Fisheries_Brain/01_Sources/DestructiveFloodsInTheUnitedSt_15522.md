# Destructive Floods in the United States in 1905: Colorado and Gila River Basins

#hydrology #flood-ecology #colorado-river-basin

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Geological Survey Water-Supply and Irrigation Paper, compiled under [[Edward Charles Murphy]] and others and published in 1906, documents the destructive floods of 1905 in the United States. Its main focus is the [[Colorado River Basin]] and the [[Gila River Basin]] of the arid Southwest. A prolonged series of spring floods (January to April 1905) was followed by a short but very large flood in late November 1905. The paper reports river discharge and stage records, precipitation totals, damage to infrastructure and irrigated land, and a national analysis of flood-flow characteristics and frequency. The document contains no fish or species-level data; its aquatic content concerns river hydrology and flood behaviour.

## Background & Description

The paper pairs hydrological records with a regional description of each drainage. Key background points include:

- **Colorado River:** Formed by the junction of the [[Green River]] and [[Grand River]] in southern Utah. It flows nearly 1,000 miles southwesterly to the Gulf of California. Its principal tributaries are the Gila, [[Little Colorado River]], [[San Juan River]], Virgin, and Williams rivers.
- **Basin extent:** The Colorado drainage (including the Green and Grand) covers about 225,000 square miles, spanning 43.5° to 31° N and 115.5° to 106° W. Canyon depths reach 3,000 to 6,000 feet below the surrounding plateaus.
- **Gila River:** Rises in southwestern New Mexico, flows through Arizona, and joins the Colorado about 1 mile above Yuma. Its total drainage area is 71,140 square miles. Principal tributaries include the [[Salt River]], [[Verde River]], San Francisco, Agua Fria, Hassayampa, San Pedro, and Santa Cruz rivers.
- **Hydrological character:** Slopes are steep and nearly impervious, so run-off is rapid and flow fluctuations are very large. About 40 per cent of the Gila basin lies below 3,000 feet and is largely agricultural land dependent on irrigation. About 27 per cent lies above 5,000 feet and supplies most of the water.
- **Precipitation:** Mean annual precipitation ranges from under 5 inches in the southwestern basin to over 15 inches on high plateaus and mountains. Headwaters of the Duchesne River receive more than 20 inches, consistent with a measured run-off of 14.5 inches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper's environmental content concerns how floods reshaped river channels, floodplains and human-managed water systems:

- **Channel change:** Sandy banks were easily eroded. The Gila bed in the Solomonsville–San Carlos reach was doubled or trebled in width. Uprooted cottonwood, mesquite and willow areas became river bed or bare mud flats. Deposits of 6 inches to 4 feet buried land in some protected areas.
- **Lowland inundation:** On the lower Colorado, about 200,000 to 225,000 acres of lowlands flood when the Yuma gage reaches about 24.5 feet. In 1905 the lowlands were flooded for about forty-five days.
- **Salton Sink:** The sink lies about 200 feet below sea level. An unregulated diversion of Colorado River flow through the Alamo canal, begun for irrigation of the Imperial Valley (about 1,000,000 acres below sea level), eroded rapidly. By 30 June 1905 about 22 per cent of the river flow was entering the canal, by 8 July 67 per cent, and by October the whole flow.
- **Regional flood regimes:** The paper groups streams by flood behaviour. Northern snowmelt streams (Nos. 1–6) have small maximum run-off rates of 15 to 25 second-feet per square mile. Arid and semiarid streams (Nos. 23–25) have very small flood rates, with large floods driven by heavy rainfall.

## Key Findings

**Spring floods, January to April 1905**

- The total run-off of the Colorado at [[Yuma Gaging Station]] from January to July 1905 was 1.6 times that of the corresponding period in 1903.
- The Gila at Dome had a total run-off of 2,957,800 acre-feet for January to May 1905, versus 31,000 acre-feet for the same period of 1903. The summary section gives the five-month total as 2,957,400 acre-feet, so the two figures should be checked against the original.
- Precipitation from January to April 1905 was several times the normal at nearly all reporting stations. Some places normally receiving about half an inch per month recorded 5 to 7 inches per month. In parts of the Salt and Verde basins, precipitation over the four months exceeded 20 inches.
- The Gila at Dome peaked at about 95,000 second-feet on 20 March 1905. Its flow at the Arizona dam was about 1.36 times its volume at Dome for the same period.
- At Hardyville on the Colorado, discharge rose from 64,310 second-feet on 5 June to 95,620 second-feet on 10 June.
- The San Francisco River at Alma (drainage area 1,800 square miles) was tabulated daily for January to April 1905.

**Colorado tributaries, June 1905**

- The [[Green River]] at Greenriver, Utah (drainage 35,200 square miles) rose from 29,260 to 37,700 second-feet between 5 and 30 June. Its peak daily rate was about 1 second-foot per square mile. Earlier records include 65,800 second-feet in May 1897 and 58,350 second-feet in June 1899.
- The [[Grand River]] at Palisade, Colorado, carried 29,000 to 41,300 second-feet between 2 and 16 June. Its peak was 4.83 second-feet per square mile. The prior four-year maximum was 24,800 second-feet in May 1904.
- The [[Gunnison River]] at Whitewater, Colorado, carried 17,780 to 28,080 second-feet between 1 and 16 June, with a peak of 3.67 second-feet per square mile.
- The [[San Juan River]] near Farmington, New Mexico, carried 15,600 to 24,800 second-feet between 1 and 21 June.

**Little Colorado River**

- Excessive rainfall produced floods that destroyed several large dams and deprived many thousand acres of irrigated land of water.
- The maximum stage, caused by failure of the St. Johns dam, was 14.5 feet, about 3 feet higher than the November 20 stage (discharge then estimated at 211,000 second-feet). The OCR text for the March–April discharge range is unclear and should be verified against the scan.

**Gila River flood of 1905 (spring)**

- Floods along the Gila near Solomonsville caused great damage. Several hundred acres of land washed away, orchards and alfalfa fields were destroyed, the San Carlos railway bridge was lost in January, and rail traffic was interrupted from 10 January to 15 April.
- The Gila at Dome recorded a rate of 92,000 second-feet on 8 February 1905 at a stage of 16.95 feet. The bed is sandy and changes shape during floods, so rate estimates may be largely in error.

**November–December 1905 flood (Gila basin)**

- A short but very large flood occurred from 27 November to 2 December. Precipitation in Arizona for November was nearly 4 inches above average at 56 stations.
- The Gila at Dome reached about 95,000 second-feet on 26 November, the same as the spring peak.
- The Salt at Roosevelt reached 97,710 second-feet on 27 November, more than twice the spring maximum. The estimated morning maximum was 148,000 second-feet, with the stage 35.8 feet.
- The Verde at McDowell peaked at 61,460 second-feet on 27 November, nearly twice the greatest spring rate.
- The Colorado at Yuma peaked at 102,700 second-feet in this flood, about 8,000 second-feet less than its 20 March 1905 peak.
- Damage was severe. The Arizona dam and all other dams on the Salt were swept away, and several railway bridges over the Gila and Salt were destroyed or damaged. The Roosevelt dam's flume and cofferdam were completely removed.

**Little Colorado, November 1905**

- The stage at Woodruff rose from 7 feet on 26 November to 20.9 feet on 27 November. Flow rose from about 3,000 to a much higher rate (the OCR value is unreadable).
- At Holbrook, flow rose from 160 to 20,180 second-feet in twenty-four hours.

**Flood frequency (national analysis)**

- The paper tabulates flood-flow data for many streams, giving drainage area, record length, largest daily rate, stage range and the frequency of floods relative to the largest recorded flood.
- Northern group (Streams 1–6): spring snowmelt floods dominate. Floods of the first or second magnitude are expected about once every twelve to fifteen years.
- Second group (Streams 7–11): floods occur about once every twenty to forty years.
- Arid and semiarid streams (Streams 23–25): very large floods are rare and usually caused by heavy rain.
- The Illinois and upper Ohio rivers have a small rate of flood flow, with spring as the main flood season.

**Management implications**

- The paper's findings are intended to inform water-supply planning and flood protection. Key lessons include the need for reliable gaging (the Gila bed's shifting channel made estimates unreliable), the vulnerability of irrigation diversions in unstable channels, and the difference between frequent snowmelt floods and rare, extreme rainfall floods.
