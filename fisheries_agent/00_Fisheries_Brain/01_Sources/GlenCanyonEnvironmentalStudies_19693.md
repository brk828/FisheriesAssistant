# Glen Canyon Environmental Studies Aquatic Coordination Team Records (1990–1991)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This compilation gathers management records from the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) [[Aquatic Coordination Team]] (ACT), 1990–1991, plus supporting technical material. It covers meeting minutes, follow-up memoranda, a [[Population Viability Analysis]] (PVA) workshop summary, a hydrogeologic study of springs in the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR) gorge, USFWS sampling protocols, an Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) trip report, an annual summary of tailwater research, and correspondence on propagation and permits. The dominant focus is the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) and its habitat, with secondary attention to [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and tailwater [[Rainbow Trout]]. The material is mainly administrative and programmatic. It documents coordination, permitting, propagation planning, and sampling design, and it includes some quantitative data.

## Background & Description

The ACT was formed to coordinate aquatic research funded under GCES, which was designed to assess effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on fish and their habitat in Glen and Grand Canyons. Participating entities included the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS, Pinetop Fishery Assistance Office), [[Arizona State University]] and the Navajo Nation, [[Bio/West]], and the Bureau of Reclamation (GCES office). Meetings were held in Pinetop, Flagstaff, Phoenix, and Tempe, and recurring themes included permits, data sharing, radio communication, helicopter logistics, and tribal consultation with the Hopi and Navajo.

The humpback chub is a federally listed endangered fish whose LCR population resides in a limited reach of roughly 14 km. Research goals included distribution, reproduction, early life stages, diet, age and growth, and habitat use. The FWS study plan emphasized microhabitat use in 500 m study areas at the Confluence, Lower Base Camp (Powell Canyon), Upper Base Camp (Salt Trail), Atomizer Falls, and Blue Springs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The LCR is a turbid, low-diversity stream dominated by native fishes. The humpback chub is the dominant species, with speckled dace, flannelmouth sucker, and bluehead sucker as subdominant natives. Common carp and channel catfish are present, and only adult carp appeared to avoid hoopnets. Electrofishing was judged ineffective in LCR waters because of high conductivity, and seining was limited by travertine and boulders.

Groundwater from the [[Little Colorado River Springs]] sustains baseflow. Loughlin (1983) reported 56 springs between river miles 3.7 and 20.9, with discharges from under 1 gpm to 43,500 gpm and a total of 211 cfs. Spring water mostly ranged from 66 to 77 °F, and the [[Sipapu Spring]] reached 80 °F. Springs concentrate where canyon floor intersects structural features, and none occur in the reach where the canyon parallels faults. Water at [[Blue Springs]] is about 75% from the San Francisco volcanic field and 25% from the Black Mesa basin. Total dissolved solids at Blue Springs are 2,270 mg/l west of the fault and 4,100 mg/l east of it.

Tailwater conditions below Glen Canyon Dam are shaped by flow fluctuation. Ten major trout stranding areas were identified between river miles 0.5 and 14.6 at Lee's Ferry. Summer 5,000 cfs flows killed trout three times in 1990, probably from elevated water temperature. Dam operations also limit benthic and algal food resources above 5,000 cfs.

## Key Findings

**Propagation and holding facilities**
- The ACT considered [[Humpback Chub Propagation]] at the Bubbling Ponds/Page Springs facility. Setup cost was estimated at $10,000, with 1.5–2 months needed before the facility was ready.
- Bubbling Ponds water ranged from 66 to 68 °F. Problems included effluent entering Oak Creek, inadequate security, inability to separate sexes, and unavoidable ICH risk.
- Genetic guidance recommended at least 100 males and 100 females, giving n=200, to maintain paired matings and population genetic characteristics. Twenty fish would suffice for thermal-shock experiments, but more would be needed for sex ratio and survivability.
- By August 1990 the group recommended that [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] hold experimental fish, with Bubbling Ponds as a potential future refugium.
- A protocol for the chub removal was required, covering number removed, genetic heterozygosity, conservation rationale, and ultimate fate.

**Permits and handling**
- The [[Biowest Permit]] was approved, with a possible increase from 60 to 75 fish reviewed in summer 1991. The Biological Opinion was pending.
- A PIT tag minimum size reduction from 175 mm to 150 mm was requested. The group agreed to tag all native fish over 150 mm, provided this did not interfere with endangered fish handling.
- Fish-handling stress reduction included salt treatment, anesthetic (TMS-222), prophylactic antibiotics, avoidance of metal containers, and maintaining temperature and dissolved oxygen during transport.

**Sampling methods**
- The FWS mini-hoopnet (20 in × 4 ft, 1/4 in mesh) was the principal device. Nets were set and run every 12 hours, not more than one day per month per location, and proposed low-impact rules limited sampling to no more than 3 consecutive days every four weeks.
- Hoopnets are time-dependent samplers, so soak times should not exceed one day. Escape rates rise with soak time, and Patriarche (1968) found complete escape of some species within 3 days.
- Radiotelemetry achieved 44 implanted tags with 75–80% recontact. Tag life was 50 days for 9 g tags and 93 days for 11 g tags. Internal tags were being evaluated to eliminate external antennas.
- Trammel nets were discontinued in the LCR by AGFD in July 1991 because of chub mortality risk.

**Field data (AGFD trip report, March 26–April 14, 1991)**
- About 50 humpback chub were captured, including 14 recaptures. Ten adults (5 males, 5 females) were flown to Page Springs Hatchery.
- Total lengths ranged from 69 to 446 mm.
- Discharge at Cameron was 228 cfs base, peaking at 474 cfs. Conductivity fell from 3,630 to 2,180 µS/cm, and alkalinity was 484 mg/l at 12.5 km.
- Only one female yielded extrudable eggs in the field, and only a small proportion of males were ripe.

**Population viability (PVA workshop, February 6–7, 1991)**
- Forty-two nonnative fish species have been introduced into the Colorado system, 10 of them abundant.
- Bonytail are virtually extirpated in the wild except in Lake Mojave, where fish are nearly 50 years old. About 1,000 old razorbacks remained in the Green River, with no evidence of successful recruitment.
- Gene flow between Lower and Upper Basin humpback chub has been interrupted.
- Workshop summary: a formal PVA cannot yet be done for razorback sucker or bonytail because populations are declining. Formal PVA is appropriate for Colorado squawfish and LCR humpback chub. A qualitative PVA is recommended for all four species.
- Estimated Colorado squawfish adults in the Yampa/Green were 3,000, with 500 in the Colorado subbasin, 50 in the San Juan, and 2 in the Lower Colorado River Basin.

**Tailwater trout (AGFD annual summary, June 28, 1991)**
- Earlier Phase I work indicated 27% of adult rainbow trout at Lee's Ferry (1984–1986) were naturally produced.
- Spawning bars at 4.0 and 8.9 river miles had 430 and 178 redds located, respectively. Very little spawning occurred at 6.1 and 14.0 miles.
- Fluorescent pigment marks were retained by 77% of 1989 stocked trout and 58% of 1990 stocked trout after 2–3 years. Binary coded wire tags were under evaluation.

**Management priorities**
- Data requests must be specific, and broad requests will not be honored. Permits must be secured before any trip.
- Helicopter training is mandatory for field personnel, and LCR landing zones should be wetted to reduce blowing sand.
- For Colorado squawfish, the stated research priorities included genetic stock structure, sex ratio of spawners, proportion of mortality from predation and sampling, and selenium tolerance.
