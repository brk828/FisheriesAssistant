# Crustacea from the Bolsón of Cuatro Ciénegas, Coahuila, Mexico

#carcinology #aquatic-biology #crustacea

## Executive Summary

This paper reviews the crustacean fauna of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] in Coahuila, Mexico. Twelve species are recorded. Among them are three cirolanid isopods, one stenasellid isopod, two hadziid amphipods and several non-endemic taxa such as [[Artemia]] and [[Hyalella azteca]]. Roughly 75% of the crustaceans show Mexican affinities and 50% are endemic to the basin. The author argues that crustacean diversity is genuinely low in the basin's thermal and hypogean waters, and attributes this to habitat stability. He also argues that undiscovered species are likely to be found, because microhabitats and cryptic taxa remain under-sampled. The paper is partly a historical account of how the fauna was discovered and partly a biogeographic discussion.

## Background & Description

The study of [[Cuatro Ciénegas Crustacea]] is described as "largely a saga of serendipity." Much of the work was done by a small group of collectors, including [[W. L. Minckley]], and was guided by [[Thomas E. Bowman]] at the Smithsonian Institution. The author frames the basin's reputation for exciting diversity as a product of striking endemism, abundant new taxa and the presence of several cirolanid isopods. These features are unusual in non-marine settings. The paper is written in an anecdotal style and is explicitly reflective about the history of Mexican carcinology.

Table 1 of the source lists the known taxa by class, order and family. The main groups are:

- **Branchiopoda:** [[Artemia]] (brine shrimp) from Laguna Salada, and [[Streptocephalus]] (fairy shrimp), which are probably undescribed species.
- **Copepoda:** a harpacticoid close to [[Cletocamptus albuquerquensis]].
- **Ostracoda:** [[Chlamydotheca]], which is abundant and serves as food for the endemic pupfish *Cyprinodon* (given as "Tenapene coahuila" in the source).
- **Peracarida, Isopoda:** [[Speocirolana thermydronis]], [[Sphaerolana interstitialis]], [[Sphaerolana affinis]] and [[Mexistenasellus coahuila]].
- **Peracarida, Amphipoda:** [[Hyalella azteca]], [[Mexiweckelia colei]] and [[Paramexiweckelia particeps]].
- **Decapoda:** [[Palaemonetes suttkusi]], a shrimp found in some lagunas.

The author says that only 12 species have been recorded, and that the amphipods and isopods are the abundant groups. Mysid peracarids are absent from the basin, although at least four troglobitic mysid species are known from Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat stability and diversity.** The author draws on [[Ward and Stanford]] (1983), who proposed an intermediate-disturbance model of diversity in lotic systems. Diversity peaks at moderate disturbance levels. It is lowest in constant environments such as thermal springs, and also low in heavily disturbed habitats such as acid mine discharges. The author suggests that the thermal, interstitial and subterranean waters of the basin are stable habitats, and that this stability explains the low crustacean diversity.

**Colonization and invasion.** Cirolanid and stenasellid isopods, along with hadziid amphipods, have not colonized common epigean (surface) environments. The author links this to competition from aquatic insects. Asellid isopods and gammarid amphipods, by contrast, have invaded lakes, streams and caves far from the sea. The Cuatro Ciénegas basin has benthic mayfly nymphs in one laguna, but no flabelliferan isopods, stenasellids or amphipods.

**Predation and biogeography.** [[Stock]] (1982) found that hadziid amphipods and cyclopoid copepods are mutually exclusive in West Indies habitats. The author suggests testing this in the basin, since the predatory nature of the amphipods seems central. At present only one thermosbaenacean is known from mainland North America, [[Monodella texana]] from the Edwards Plateau of Texas. Hypogean habitats lacking weckeliid amphipods could therefore be good places to find copepods or thermosbaenaceans related to *Monodella*.

**Relict and continental patterns.** The [[Speocirolana]] species show a north-south distribution. This follows the Upper Jurassic coastline and the old Paleocene–Eocene shorelines, as described by [[Axelrod]] (1979). The author concludes that the genus probably has a pre-Tertiary origin and that its members moved directly from retreating seas into interstitial and hypogean habitats. The thermophilic isopod [[Thermosphaeroma]] occurs in hot springs of western Texas and New Mexico, and a sphaeromatid population might yet be found in the basin.

## Key Findings

- **Species count and affinities.** Twelve species are recorded. About 75% have Mexican affinities and 50% are endemic to the basin. Two endemic genera, [[Sphaerolana]] and [[Paramexiweckelia]], are restricted to the basin. Four genera were originally described from Cuatro Ciénegas material.
- **Isopod discoveries.** [[Speocirolana thermydronis]] was described by [[Cole and Minckley]] (1966) from a single 15 mm female collected in April 1964. This was followed by 29 more animals collected in August 1967. The genus *Sphaerolana* was erected in 1970 for two species, *S. interstitialis* and *S. affinis*. The type locality of *S. affinis* was a small funnel-shaped pit, Pozo Embudo, near Ejido Santa Tecla.
- **Sphaerolana differences.** The two *Sphaerolana* species differ in antenna 2, pleopod 1, pleotelson and uropod. *S. affinis* is larger, with specimens 1.6–2.4 times longer than *S. interstitialis* in some populations. Where the two species co-occur in Taylor's Spring, the author suggests character displacement and possible reproductive isolation.
- **Mexican stenasellids.** The stenasellid [[Mexistenasellus coahuila]] was known from only 10 specimens when first described. The author notes that eight stenasellid species are known from the New World, all from Mexico. He adds that *M. coahuila* is probably the most thermally tolerant species, having been found in waters above 30 °C.
- **Amphipod discovery.** [[Mexiweckelia colei]] was first found in Taylor's Spring. *Paramexiweckelia particeps* (formerly *Mexiweckelia particeps*) was reassigned to a new monotypic genus, pending publication by Holsinger (in press).
- **Hyalella variation.** The author argues that the "one species, *Hyalella azteca*" concept is no longer accurate. He cites two other American species, *H. texana* and *H. montezuma*, and notes that the Coahuila populations may include several undescribed species. One spring holds snow-white individuals with black eyes.
- **Artemia taxonomy.** The name *Artemia salina* should not be applied to the basin's brine shrimp, following [[Clark and Bowen]] (1976). The Laguna Salada population has not yet been given a specific name.
- **Correction of earlier data.** The author corrects two earlier reports that the copepod *Cletocamptus* lived in extremely hypersaline water. One report gave 909.4 g/l total dissolved solids. A second gave about 400 g/l. The summation of major ions and silica from [[Minckley and Cole]] (1968b) gives 99.85 g/l, which the author considers the credible value.
- **Future prospects.** The author predicts further discoveries, including:
  - Cladocerans, copepods and ostracods in shallow lagunas and wetland beds.
  - Additional stenasellids in the basin and the wider Mexican region.
  - Sphaeromatid isopods in thermal waters.
  - *Streptocephalus* species, such as *S. linderi* and *S. machini*, already known from Coahuila.
- **Research recommendations.** The author proposes testing whether weckeliid amphipods and cyclopoid copepods exclude each other in the basin. He also suggests that hypogean crustaceans may have greater genetic variability than their epigean relatives. This could make isolation a major driver of speciation.
