# The Introduction of Kokanee Red Salmon (Oncorhynchus nerka kennerlyi) into Lake Tahoe, California and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Salmonidae #fisheries-management #species-introduction

## Executive Summary

This 1951 report documents the introduction program of [[Kokanee]] salmon (*Oncorhynchus nerka kennerlyi*), a landlocked form of red salmon, into [[Lake Tahoe]] to restore game fish populations following the decline of native [[Cutthroat Trout]]. The program, conducted jointly by California and Nevada fish and game agencies, involved large-scale fingerling plantings beginning in 1949, with 613,475 fish stocked in 1950 alone. The kokanee was selected for its open-water feeding habits, non-predatory nature, excellent spawning capabilities in shore areas and tributary streams, and suitability to Lake Tahoe's cool water temperatures.

## Background & Description

[[Lake Tahoe]] historically supported a "fabulous population" of [[Cutthroat Trout]] that had ceased to produce satisfactorily by the mid-20th century. Introduced [[Lake Trout]] (mackinaw) and [[Rainbow Trout]] populations failed to meet expectations for angling production relative to the lake's size and recreational importance. Rainbow trout production was limited by reduced spawning areas due to summer water use from tributary streams, while lake trout production was constrained by shortage of forage fish in open and deeper waters.

The [[Kokanee]], a landlocked form of red salmon native to Pacific northwest waters, was first introduced into California waters experimentally in 1941. An accidental escapement from [[Tahoe Hatchery]] in 1944 produced returning spawners in 1946 averaging 14.5 inches in length, demonstrating the species' viability in Lake Tahoe.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The kokanee was selected for Lake Tahoe introduction based on several ecological factors:

1. **Feeding ecology**: Open-water lake fish feeding on minute plants and animals (plankton), avoiding serious competition with shoal or bottom-feeding game fish
2. **Non-predatory nature**: Not carnivorous in relation to other fish species
3. **Reproductive strategy**: Utilizes both gravelly shore areas and tributary streams for spawning; Lake Tahoe's abundant shore spawning areas considered favorable
4. **Temperature requirements**: Prefers cool water; Lake Tahoe temperatures very suitable
5. **Forage potential**: May provide food source for other game fish if sufficiently abundant

The species does not compete directly with existing game fish for food resources and appears well-suited to Lake Tahoe's environmental conditions.

## Key Findings

**Initial Introduction and Natural Reproduction**
- First kokanee introduction: 1944 (accidental escapement from [[Tahoe Hatchery]])
- First returning spawners: 1946, averaging 14.5 inches in length
- Demonstrated successful reproduction and survival in Lake Tahoe environment

**Stocking Program Implementation**
- 1949: 90,000 fingerlings planted in three tributary streams (California side)
- 1950: 613,475 fingerling kokanee planted in lake and tributary streams
- Eggs obtained from [[Idaho Department of Fish and Game]]
- Approximately 338,900 fish raised at [[Idlewild Hatchery]] (Washoe County, Reno, Nevada)
- Remainder raised at [[Basin Creek]] and [[Tahoe State Fish Hatcheries]] (California)

**Distribution Methods**
- Lake plantings: 150-gallon fish planting tank mounted on barge
- Shore spawning areas selected along California and Nevada shorelines
- Planting locations predetermined through surveys by Nevada and California personnel
- Barge method necessary for accessing snowbound areas during April-June planting period
- Tributary stream stocking accomplished by truck

**Management Strategy**
- Program received full sanction and support from [[Nevada Fish and Game Commission]]
- Interstate cooperation between California and Nevada agencies
- Artificial stocking planned to continue through 1953
- Goal: establish initial population for natural reproduction buildup
- Shore planting strategy designed to utilize kokanee homing instinct for maximum spawning area utilization

**Habitat Enhancement**
- Stream improvement work underway: barrier removals, stream flow maintenance, spawning area improvement
- Follow-up studies planned for spawning runs and spawning success monitoring

**Performance Cautions**
- Success not uniformly good in other California waters where kokanee introduced
- Limited growth observed in [[Echo Lake]]
- Low survival to maturity in some years in other waters
- Authors caution against considering kokanee "a panacea for all of the lake's problems"
- Long-term success dependent on spawning success in coming years
