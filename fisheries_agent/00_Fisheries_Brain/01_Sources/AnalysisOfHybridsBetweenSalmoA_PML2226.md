# Hybrids Between Apache Trout and Rainbow Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper reports the first known F1 hybrids between the [[Apache Trout]] (*Salmo apache*) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*). Two hybrids were reared at the [[Page Springs Fish Hatchery]] in Arizona and compared morphologically with two rainbow trout and four native Apache trout. Across 29 characters, the hybrids were intermediate in 15, leaning toward rainbow trout in 4 and toward Apache trout in 5, with no clear trend in the rest. The authors use these results to argue that intermediate phenotypes cannot by themselves be taken as proof of [[Introgression]] in wild trout populations, and that [[Habitat Loss]] and degradation deserve at least equal weight with genetic factors in recovery planning.

## Background & Description

- **Authors and affiliations:** John N. Rinne (USDA Forest Service, Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station, Forestry Sciences Laboratory, Tempe, AZ); Roger Sorenson (Arizona Game and Fish Department, Page Springs Fish Hatchery); Scott Belfit (USDA Forest Service, Tempe, AZ).
- **Study species:** Apache trout is a native Arizona salmonid, described by Miller (1972). Rainbow trout, widely stocked across western North America, is the hybridization partner.
- **Background problem:** Widespread, indiscriminate stocking of rainbow trout has led to introgression with native western trout, a process that has also affected the native trout of this region. Patterns of contamination are linked in time and space to stocking history. Because field patterns of variation are hard to interpret, a cooperative effort began in 1981 to artificially hybridize rainbow trout with native Apache trout, and a similar effort began in Dade (spring 1982).
- **Materials and methods:** Eggs were taken from two or three-year-old native Apache trout brood stock at the Soldier Creek facility and fertilized with milt from two fifteen-month-old male rainbow trout, using the dry spawn technique. Hybrid and parental specimens were photographed, preserved in 10% formalin, and later stored in 60% ethanol. Morphometric and meristic characters followed Hubbs and Lagler (1970), with characters defined by Miller (1972). Four native fish were selected from a larger preserved sample for comparison.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hybridization and reproductive isolation:** Hybrids between closely related trout are generally considered to be intermediate in phenotype, which made the identification of wild hybrids possible. Miller (1972) found no reproductive barrier in western trout, and Gold et al. (1979) reported reduced hybrid fertility at the backcross level. The lack of obvious zones of hybridization in White Mountains streams (Rinne 1983) may reflect extreme domestication of rainbow trout and reduced reproductive capacity in the wild, but the authors caution that massive swamping of the wild genome may also be a form of reproductive isolation.
- **Habitat and management pressures:** Habitat loss and degradation are considered as important as hybridization in the decline of native western trout (Behnke and Zarn 1976). Stocking of rainbow trout has reduced the number of remaining pure native populations.
- **Related hybridization studies:** Clarkson (1982) reported intermediate phenotypes in hybrids between native Arizona suckers (Catostomidae). Gold et al. (1979) reported on hybrids between rainbow and golden trout (*Oncorhynchus* stock). Rinne (1983) documented phenotypic variation in White Mountains trout populations and demonstrated that apparent introgression was only partly attributable to hybridization.

## Key Findings

1. **Morphological intermediacy is case-by-case:** Of 29 measured characters, 15 were intermediate between the parental means. Four were closer to rainbow trout, five were nearer the native Apache trout, and six showed no definite trend toward either parent.
2. **Mean values can exceed the parents:** For 10 of the 29 characters, the mean values of the hybrids were higher than either parent.
3. **Meristic counts:** Mean counts for 12 characters were intermediate, and the hybrids tended more toward rainbow trout than toward native trout for several meristic traits. Specific counts appear in the source tables (Tables 1 and 2) and are not reproduced here.
4. **Coloration:** Color patterns of the hybrids were reported as intermediate in several cases, but this was not generalized across all specimens.
5. **Congenital deformity:** One specimen developed a congenital deformity, ventral curvature of the vertebral column, and did not survive to the sacrifice stage.
6. **Intermediacy is not guaranteed in nature:** The authors argue that the assumption that hybrids in nature are always identifiable by intermediate phenotype is an erroneous basis for management decisions. Management based on this assumption could lead to the destruction of native populations through stream renovation or stocking.
7. **Management recommendations:**
   - Additional F1 hybrids and backcrosses should be produced to verify the lack of intermediacy and better define the range of phenotypic variation in native trout.
   - Agencies must give extrinsic factors (habitat loss and degradation) at least equal consideration to intrinsic (genetic) factors in recovery efforts for these native trout.
