# Desert Fishes: Aquatic Environments, Physiology and Ecology of Arid-Region Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[James E. Deacon]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] reviews the inland, indigenous fishes of climatic deserts, concentrating on the hot deserts of the southwestern United States and northern Mexico. It describes desert aquatic habitats (rivers, springs, marshes and lakes), the physical and chemical conditions that shape them, and the biological responses of fishes to those conditions, including dispersion, reproduction, feeding, and tolerance of flood, drought, temperature, salinity, oxygen and other gases. The authors argue that desert fishes are shaped by high environmental variability and by the widespread disruption of habitats through impoundment, groundwater pumping and introduced species, which has caused extirpations and declines across the region.

## Background & Description

The chapter situates desert fishes within the broader fish diversity of arid lands. Fishes in deserts are heterogeneous: some species are unique and isolated, while others range widely across drainages shaped by past and present connections. Most successful desert fishes display generalized body forms, with only minor adjustments in shape and function to cope with harsh conditions. The authors note that the western North American fauna is relatively depauperate, with about 200 native fishes, and that only one, the [[Speckled Dace]], is considered widespread.

The authors follow Cole (1968) in using a broad definition of desert, since many waters discussed transcend several biomes. Aquatic habitats of arid lands, apart from springs fed by subterranean water, depend on surface flow or subsurface infiltration from adjacent, more mesic zones. Discussion of introduced fishes is limited to their effects on the systems they entered.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Desert rivers and streams.** Desert streams are open-ended, unidirectional systems with great spatial and temporal variability. Large rivers such as the [[Colorado River]] and the Nile are more predictable than small desert creeks, which are strongly influenced by cyclonic storms. The Colorado at Yuma ranged from a peak discharge of about 4.25 × 10⁸ m³/min in 1916 to 20.5 m³/min in 1934. Water temperatures in large desert rivers rarely drop below 10–12°C in winter and rarely exceed 30°C in summer, while backwaters and lentic areas can exceed 35°C at the surface. Small Arizona creeks have recorded maxima from 24°C to 40.3°C, with backwaters reaching 33–42.7°C.

**Springs and marshes.** Desert springs range from swift-flowing rheocrenes to quiet limnocrenes and seepages. Many emit thermal or meteoric water heated by geothermal gradients. Springs are chemically and thermally more constant than other desert waters, though some show seasonal fluctuation. Dissolved solids increase toward the termini of spring systems, and travertine deposition is common. Desert marshes are densely vegetated by sedges, are highly fluctuant in temperature and salinity, and resemble estuarine environments. The [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]] in Coahuila, Mexico, is a major example, with limnocrenes once supporting marshes and streams over more than 200 hectares.

**Lakes.** Closed-basin (endorheic) lakes depend on fine balances of inflow, evaporation and seepage. Pleistocene lakes such as [[Lake Lahontan]] and the pluvial lakes of the Great Basin have left playas and remnant lakes such as [[Pyramid Lake]]. Few natural desert lakes support fishes, since many are too saline or ephemeral. The [[Salton Sea]] arose inadvertently in 1905 when the Colorado River broke through irrigation works, forming a lake about 25 m deep.

**Historical change.** Human activity over recent decades has accelerated desiccation of streams, and large rivers have been beheaded by impoundment. Overgrazing in southern Arizona (1870–1890) preceded a drought in 1891–1894 that killed about 75% of the herd, and arroyo cutting from the 1880s altered channels. Channelization and impoundment have essentially eliminated the indigenous fauna of the largest streams.

## Key Findings

**Physical and chemical constraints**

- **Flooding:** Flash floods seldom annihilate fishes, which avoid floods by moving behind boulders or into backwaters. Narrow, high-gradient streams offer no refuge. A 1970 flood in Sycamore Creek destroyed gauging devices and killed human lives, yet longfin dace recolonized within weeks, with young hatching within 1 week and filling the creek within a month.
- **Turbidity and sedimentation:** Extremely high suspended solids can suffocate fishes. Some fishes, such as the lungfish and an eel, have mucus that precipitates clays. Turbidity shapes eye reduction, barbel development and brain morphology.
- **Drought:** Drying pools produce mass mortality, but longfin dace survived beneath algal mats, swimming in a few millimeters of water at night. Introduced species such as [[Mosquitofish]], red shiner and fathead minnow spread rapidly into temporary waters.
- **Temperature:** Desert fishes tolerate a wide range. Field maxima generally fall below 45°C, and reproducing populations rarely exceed 35°C. Recorded Cyprinodon atrorus at 47.2°C in August 1968, and a pool at 48.9°C was documented for Lucania browni (now Cyprinodon macularius) in Baja California. Pupfish move to cooler microhabitats, and acclimation to warm water is faster than to cold.
- **Salinity:** Fishes persist in salinities up to 2–3 times seawater. Cyprinodon atrorus tolerates up to 95 g/L, and some cottonball marsh pupfish survive 78 g/L. Recently dead fish were found at 240 g/L, possibly preserved in brine.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Oxygen is probably the most frequent limiting factor. Desert pupfish tolerate the lowest known minimum, about 0.13 mg/L, and dive into anoxic mud. Springfishes ([[Crenichthys Baileyi]] and C. nevadae) reduce activity below about 2 mg/L.
- **Carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulfide:** Montezuma Well in Arizona contained free CO₂ up to 600 mg/L, and fishes introduced there died quickly. Cyprinodon dispar survived sulfide concentrations of 12–200 mg/L in a Cyrenaican sulfur lake.
- **Light:** Ultraviolet radiation is lethal to killifish embryos at early stages. Many desert fish eggs are transparent or protected by pigmentation.

**Biological responses**

- **Dispersion:** Distributions are limited by geographic barriers or environmental tolerance. Stream fish distributions follow gradient, with current-adapted forms such as [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Woundfin]] showing streamlined or flattened bodies, and smaller fishes in quieter habitats.
- **Springs:** Springheads are species-poor, while large limnocrenes such as Cuatro Ciénegas support more diverse faunas (10–14 species in larger springheads).
- **Reproduction:** Many desert fishes show spring-summer reproductive surges. Cyprinodon atrorus spawns from late January to November in natural habitats. Day length appears important in timing reproduction, but temperature, food and population density also contribute. Some fishes, such as the Arkansas River shiner, spawn during floods.
- **Feeding:** Desert fishes are opportunistic. Diets shift with size and season. Pupfish often feed on bottom sediments and detritus, and high temperatures increase food intake but reduce conversion efficiency.
- **Competition and predation:** Introduced species have caused declines in native fishes. Largemouth bass caused extinction of at least two desert fish populations. Mosquitofish is implicated in declines of many fishes, including the [[Gila Topminnow]] and desert pupfish. Native fishes often persist by moving into physiologically harsh zones unsuitable for introduced species.

**Conservation implications**

- Many native desert fishes have been extirpated or greatly reduced. Authors argue that intensive impoundment, groundwater pumping and introduced species have produced declines that could be irreversible within a few years.
- Populations can recover after favorable flow years. Between 1963 and 1970, populations of longfin dace, speckled dace and Gila mountain-sucker in Aravaipa and Sonoita creeks fluctuated markedly with discharge.
- Authors urge attention to ecology of eggs and larvae, which remain poorly studied for desert fishes.
