# First Canadian Record of Grass Carp (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*) in Lake Erie

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This note reports the first Canadian record of the exotic cyprinid [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idella*). A male specimen, 758 mm total length (TL), was captured in a trap net in [[Lake Erie]] about 1 km off the west shore of [[Point Pelee National Park]] on 27 September 1985. The authors describe the fish's morphology, distinguish it from a possible look-alike, summarize its biology and invasion history, and assess the risk of an established Canadian population. They conclude that the fish probably reached Lake Erie by moving eastward around the end of the lake from Michigan and Ohio, and that its testis suggests it may not have been sterile, unlike the triploid stock used in U.S. stocking programs.

## Background & Description

The authors frame the discovery as part of a broader pattern. Canada's freshwater fish fauna grows through the northward spread of North American species, which normally causes little concern. The northward movement of exotic species introduced in the United States is a different matter. The discovery is compared to the escape of captive [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) into public waters years earlier.

**Capture details**
- Date: 27 September 1985
- Gear: trap net set about 1 km off the west shore of Point Pelee National Park, at the level of Lake Pond
- Captured by: P. Krause, who did not recognize the fish
- Sex: male
- Measurements when fresh: 700 mm fork length (FL), 758 mm TL, 5.3 kg
- Voucher: Royal Ontario Museum, ROM 49248

**Morphological features (as described)**
- Large, cylindrical cyprinid with a small, broad, blunt head, subterminal mouth, unspecialized lips and no barbels
- Anal fin set far back on the body. The distance from anal fin origin to caudal base is three or more times the distance from anal fin origin to snout tip (after Pflieger 1975)
- Short-based dorsal fin with 7 or 8 rays, originating over or slightly behind the pelvic fin origin
- Lateral line scales 40–45, large compared with native cyprinids, with prominent dark pigment on the back and sides
- Pharyngeal teeth: North American literature gives 2,4–4,2. Berg (1965) reported a range of combinations, including one tooth in the inner row and five in the outer row. The Lake Erie specimen's right pharyngeal arch had 1, 5 teeth (as reported). The teeth are large, with strongly compressed crowns and deep parallel grooves ([[Pharyngeal Teeth]])
- Name meaning: *Ctenopharyngodon* means "comb-like throat teeth"

**Size**
- Reported to reach 1 m in length (Smith 1979)
- Weight reported as 32 kg (Berg 1965) or 45 kg (Fedorenko and Fraser 1978)

**Look-alike species**
The authors consider the only probable identification confusion in Ontario to be with large [[Semotilus Corporalis]] (fallfish). Differences include a larger head relative to body length in *S. corporalis*, an anal fin farther forward (ratio of 2+ rather than 3+), teeth with uncompressed crowns, and probable visible flap-like maxillary barbels in large individuals. *S. corporalis* does not occur in Lake Erie, so confusion is unlikely there unless it crosses eastern Lake Ontario.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native and introduced range**
- Native range: from the Amur River in the eastern U.S.S.R. south to the West River in southwest China and Thailand (Guillory 1980)
- Introduced first in Alabama and Arkansas from Taiwan and Malaysia; now reported in at least 40 U.S. states
- Originally imported into the U.S. as a possible control of nuisance aquatic vegetation

**Tolerances and biology (as reported)**
- Water temperature tolerance: 0–35°C
- Oxygen: tolerates concentrations as low as 0.0005 ppt (Guillory 1980, as printed)
- Largely vegetarian; reportedly eats more than its body weight per day
- In Illinois, reached 6.5 kg by its third summer (Smith 1979)
- Fecundity: 816 000 eggs reported by Berg (1965) for a Soviet individual 76 cm long and 7.4 kg. The authors state this is double that of [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy*), one of the more fecund local species, but lower than that of Common Carp
- Testis of the Lake Erie specimen was not highly developed
- Spawns (possibly only) in running water at 27–29°C (Wheeler 1975)

**Habitat impact concern**
The main concern, following Pflieger (1975), is that the species may destroy fish and wildlife habitat by eliminating all vegetation. Potential damage to desirable [[Aquatic Vegetation]] is its major drawback in Canada (Fedorenko and Fraser 1978). An anonymous 1987 report states that Grass Carp in ponds have eaten roots from under shoreline plants and dragged vegetation into the water. The species also eats animal matter, may scavenge, and may prey on young fishes (Fedorenko and Fraser 1978).

**Establishment risk in Canada**
- The risk of an established population is reduced because the species grows well only in warm water, and it is not known to be established at most introduction sites (Guillory 1980)
- The northern limit of native distribution (about 50°N) runs through Ontario at the level of [[Lake Nipigon]]

**Distribution and dispersal**
- Michigan: many records from streams flowing into western [[Lake Erie]] and [[Lake St. Clair]]
- Ohio: an older record from an [[Ohio River]] tributary (Olentangy River); more recent Lake Erie records from the mouth of the Toussaint River (16 March 1985) and Trane Creek near Bono (30 April 1986)
- Nearest record to Canadian waters: south shore of [[Lake Ontario]], near Rochester, New York
- The authors judge it probable that the fish moved eastward around the end of Lake Erie from Michigan, rather than across the west end of the lake. They note this route has been proposed for other recent immigrants (Crossman and Simpson 1984)
- Michigan and Ohio records are unusual because both states prohibit diploid and triploid Grass Carp (Allen and Wattendorf 1987)

**Triploid stock**
Grass Carp supplied for U.S. stocking from Stuttgart, Arkansas, are certified by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service as triploid and therefore sterile (Anonymous 1985). See [[Triploid Sterility]]. The testis of the Lake Erie specimen suggests it may not have been sterile.

## Key Findings

- **First Canadian record:** one male *Ctenopharyngodon idella* (758 mm TL, 700 mm FL, 5.3 kg) was captured in Lake Erie on 27 September 1985.
- **Likely dispersal route:** the authors suggest the fish came from Michigan and Ohio populations by moving eastward around the western end of Lake Erie.
- **Identification:** the species is most likely to be confused with large *Semotilus corporalis* in Ontario. Diagnostic features are the anal fin position, pharyngeal tooth crown shape, and head size.
- **Reproductive potential:** an egg count of 816 000 was reported for a 76 cm, 7.4 kg fish, about twice that of Muskellunge.
- **Main ecological threat:** elimination of aquatic vegetation and resulting habitat loss, which is the principal concern for Canada.
- **Establishment risk:** judged reduced because the species requires warm water and is not established at most introduction sites. The native northern limit (about 50°N) passes through Ontario near Lake Nipigon.
- **Sterility question:** the Lake Erie specimen's testis suggests it was probably fertile, which conflicts with the sterile triploid stock used in U.S. programs.
- **Research gap:** the authors state the spawning requirement (running water at 27–29°C) is given with a question mark, so spawning behavior needs confirmation.
