# Nitrogen and Phosphorus Dynamics in Hot Desert Streams of Southwestern USA

#ichthyology #marine-biology #desert-streams

Note: the source is a limnological study of nutrients and periphyton, not a fish study. The tag line above keeps the required format; the taxon focus is aquatic primary producers (periphyton).

## Executive Summary

This 1981 study by Grimm, Fisher and Minckley, published in [[Hydrobiologia]], examines whether nitrogen or phosphorus limits primary production in small, intermittent and continuously flowing streams of the [[Gila River]] basin in Arizona. Thirty-eight low-elevation streams and stream segments were sampled, mainly in summer 1979, and 52 sites were analyzed for nutrient chemistry. The authors found that free-flowing streams had low nitrogen-to-phosphorus (N:P) ratios, that nitrate was depleted downstream faster than [[Soluble Reactive Phosphate]], and that nitrate uptake tracked periphyton abundance while phosphorus uptake did not. They concluded that nitrogen is the likely limiting nutrient in free-flowing streams, with phosphorus controlled by factors other than biological uptake.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Small, spatially and temporally intermittent streams of the desert Southwest are largely shaded only in part and are highly productive, with high standing crops of algae and autotrophic activity ([[Busch & Fisher 1981]]).
- **Prior understanding:** Earlier data suggested that nitrogen rather than phosphorus limited aquatic production in Southwestern streams, but this was based on few sites.
- **Hypotheses tested:**
  1. Low N:P ratios are characteristic of streams in the region.
  2. Net autotroph production is responsible for observed variation in nitrogen and phosphorus concentrations.
  3. Nutrient depletion at high biomass levels can make nutrients limit primary production.
- **Hydrology:** Winter and summer precipitation with spring and autumn drought characterizes the lowland Gila basin. Flow is often restricted to isolated segments where bedrock forces groundwater to the surface. Morphology is largely shaped by catastrophic floods, and channels are wide and mostly unshaded.
- **Floods and nutrients:** Summer storms transport nutrients and organic matter accumulated in desert soils. Earlier work reported elevated dissolved nutrients, especially nitrogen, after a Sonoran Desert summer flood ([[Fisher & Minckley 1978]]).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling design**
- 52 water-sampling sites on 38 streams, grouped as 27 surface-flow sources in isolated segments and 25 continuously flowing streams. Flood water was collected at three times.
- Most sites lay in the [[Sonoran Desert]]. Sites 26 to 36 were in a transition zone between the Sonoran and [[Chihuahuan Desert]], and others were in chaparral.
- Nutrient uptake was measured at 10 sites on 21 dates, using change in nutrient concentration corrected for discharge and segment area.
- Intensive diel sampling was done on three summer dates at [[Sycamore Creek]] (site 5), with samples every 4 hours over 24 hours.

**Water chemistry**
- Waters were carbonate dominated, with alkalinities of 2 to 5 meq·l⁻¹, sulfate of 0.1 to 2 meq·l⁻¹ and chloride of 0.2 to 1 meq·l⁻¹.
- Sulfate and conductivity peaked at three nearly dry streams (sites 7, 8 and 9).
- Mean conductivity at the remaining sites was 420 µS·cm⁻¹ (range 240 to 700). Silica ranged from 4 to 12 mg·l⁻¹.

**Methods**
- Nitrate was the main inorganic nitrogen form. Ammonium was measured by the phenol-hypochlorite method, and nitrite was never detected.
- SRP was measured colorimetrically; total dissolved phosphorus was estimated after persulfate digestion.
- Periphyton was measured as chlorophyll a and ash-free biomass.
- Gross primary production and respiration were modeled from chlorophyll, light and temperature ([[Busch & Fisher 1981]] model).

## Key Findings

1. **Nitrate was the dominant inorganic nitrogen form.** Concentrations ranged from 0.07 to 105.00 µM (mean 21.86 ± 8.05, n = 52). Nitrate was significantly higher in segment sources and flood waters than elsewhere (p < 0.001).
2. **Ammonium was minor.** It ranged from 0 to 3.64 µM (mean 0.81 ± 0.30 µM, n = 42), about 6% of dissolved inorganic nitrogen.
3. **SRP was not elevated in sources or floods.** SRP ranged from 0.10 to 6.85 µM (mean 1.95 ± 0.45, n = 52) and made up about 71% of total phosphorus. SRP did not differ significantly between sources and continuous streams (p > 0.1).
4. **N:P ratios separated source and free-flowing waters.** All 25 continuously flowing streams had N:P of 15 or less, and at 20 of them N:P was below 6. Sources and floods had higher, more variable ratios.
5. **Nitrate declined downstream faster than SRP.** In 23 of 25 segments, source nitrate exceeded 3.6 µM and declined downstream. SRP declined at 20 sites and stayed constant or rose slightly at the rest.
6. **Nitrate uptake tracked periphyton.** Uptake ranged from −0.1 to 2.7 µmol·m⁻²·h⁻¹ and was significantly correlated with chlorophyll a (r = 0.733, p < 0.01, n = 20) and biomass (r = 0.760, p < 0.01, n = 20).
7. **SRP uptake did not track periphyton.** Uptake ranged from −0.14 to 0.58 µmol·m⁻²·h⁻¹, with no significant correlation to chlorophyll a or biomass.
8. **Uptake was not diel-dependent.** At site 5, nitrate and SRP uptake occurred in both light and dark. Mean daily uptake was 14.9 mmol·m⁻²·d⁻¹ for nitrate and 1.63 mmol·m⁻²·d⁻¹ for SRP.
9. **Predicted and observed nitrate uptake agreed.** Observed nitrate uptake correlated with predicted uptake (r = 0.827, p < 0.01, n = 12). SRP showed no significant agreement (r = 0.517, p > 0.1, n = 12).
10. **Denitrification is probably minor.** Non-algal organic matter in sandy sediments was below 0.1%, and nitrate concentrations were far lower than in the comparison systems.
11. **Nitrogen is the likely limiting nutrient.** Free-flowing streams had low N:P and mostly nitrate below 3.6 µM, which the authors describe as incipient nitrogen limitation. Sediments likely supply phosphorus, through sorption and calcium phosphate solubility control.
12. **Management implication:** High stream productivity in deserts is likely restricted near points where subsurface water surfaces. Sustained downstream productivity probably needs exogenous nitrogen inputs, such as nitrogen fixation.
