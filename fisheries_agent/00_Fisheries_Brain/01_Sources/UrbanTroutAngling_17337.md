# Urban Trout Angling

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trout

## Executive Summary

This Department of Fish and Game administrative report (Inland Fisheries Administrative Report No. 63-3, submitted February 15, 1963, by Alex Calhoun) argues that stocking trout in urban waters could provide 1,000,000 or more angler days of fishing in California each year, possibly as many as 3,000,000. A state-wide program would cost at least $500,000 a year, probably about $1,000,000, and could eventually approach $3,000,000 annually.

Urban trout fishing currently operates on a small scale. The Department stocks about 10 percent of its catchable-sized trout in 13 urban lakes and streams, and private stocking also occurs, particularly in Southern California. Demand is strong, but a lack of funds blocks expansion. The report recommends [[Pay-As-You-Fish Financing]], in which daily angler fees cover all costs, as the most viable path. Its success at [[Murray Lake]] and other sites suggests it can make large urban programs financially feasible for the first time in California. The report also calls for enabling legislation, since existing law does not explicitly authorize fee-fishing for trout in public waters.

## Background & Description

**Scope of demand.** Table 1 estimates the recreational potential of urban trout angling by metropolitan area, using one angler day per licensed angler, with rough 1960 population figures. Estimated totals were about 1,200,000 angler days, based on a conservative assumption that urban anglers average one trip a year. The areas considered included [[Los Angeles]], [[San Diego]], [[San Francisco-Oakland]], and [[San Jose]].

**Existing program.** In 1962, the Department's urban trout program (Table 2) stocked 18 urban waters with a total of 733,500 eight-inch trout, roughly 10 percent of all trout stocked by the Department. Stocked sites included the San Joaquin River below Friant (56,000), [[Morena Lake]] (25,000), [[Merced Lake]] (150,000), Nimbus Lake (30,000), and the Santa Clara and San Mateo county waters (75,000 and 67,000).

**Financing dilemma.** The Department must refuse urban demand for lack of funds, which causes public relations problems. The report notes that angling license revenues were established as use fees to control harvest of wild fish and to fund natural fisheries. Urban trout fishing is "pure recreation," closer to golf or swimming, so the report concludes that license revenues are an inappropriate funding source.

**Pay-as-you-fish model.** Participants pay daily fees that cover all costs. Operations described include:

- [[Murray Lake]], San Diego (Helix Irrigation District)
- [[Wohlford Lake]], San Diego County (Escondido Mutual Water Company)
- [[Irvine Lake]], Orange County (Stevenson Lake Corporation)
- [[Missouri Trout Parks]] (Missouri State Conservation Commission)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water conditions.** Many urban reservoirs were warmwater fisheries before the all-year trout season in Southern California made winter trout stocking feasible. Murray Lake originally produced about 40 pounds per acre per year of warmwater fish. The report describes Wohlford as historically an outstanding warmwater producer, chemically treated in fall 1958 and restocked. Trout stop biting when water warms, so the Murray season was scheduled to end on July 5 and begin in December.

**Mixed fisheries.** Naturally produced warmwater fish (black bass and sunfish) were a state responsibility and contributed to the catch. At Irvine Lake, warmwater fish supplied a relatively larger share of the catch, so its stocking rate was lower.

**Spring-fed streams.** The Missouri parks use spring-fed streams flowing roughly 12 to 60 million gallons per day, which support high-density trout fishing.

**Management responsibilities.** The Commission sets angling regulations, and the Department enforces them and assists with fishery management. All stocked fish are planted under permit from the State and become State property once released in public waters. State angling licenses and stamps are required on all daily-fee waters.

## Key Findings

**Potential and cost**
- Urban trout fishing could yield 1,000,000 to 3,000,000 angler days annually in California.
- Estimated state-wide costs: at least $500,000; more probably $1,000,000; ultimately up to about $3,000,000 per year.

**Financing success**
- [[Murray Lake]]: attendance rose from about 7,000 angler days (warmwater-only, 1951–52 estimate) to 53,062 permits in 1960–61 and 50,378 in 1961–62. The catch rose from about 7,600 pounds to roughly 53,000 pounds. Fees were $1.00 in 1960–61, raised to $1.25 in 1961–62. The Helix Irrigation District earned a profit of several thousand dollars, and the program was fully self-supporting.
- Murray stocking totaled 52,300 pounds in the 1960–61 season (valued at $41,840 at $0.80 per pound). Percentage recapture was 90 (1959), 84 (1960), 87 (1961), and 88 (1962) percent.
- [[Wohlford Lake]]: 22,577 permits sold and 53,383 fish (21,900 lbs) planted in 1960. The company reported 90.57 percent of trout caught. Its manager said trout-only stocking was "not very profitable," but profit improved after the bag limit was cut from ten to five trout in 1962.
- [[Irvine Lake]]: 56,124 permits sold over a 127-day 1961 season; 34,600 pounds of trout planted, including fish up to three pounds; $1.50 daily fee. No catch records were kept.
- [[Missouri Trout Parks]]: 180,023 angler days in 1961 across four stream sections, with a $1.00 fee that keeps the program self-supporting. Stocking is at least 10-inch trout, roughly 24 fish per tag sold.

**Design principles for successful programs**
- Start programs as financially self-supporting. Continuous heavy attendance spreads costs and keeps the per-angler charge low.
- Maintain consistently good fishing; stocking about one pound of trout per angler day satisfied anglers and allowed reasonable fees.
- Include large trout (often a pound or more) and some size variation to create trophy appeal.
- Charge children a fee; the managers considered this essential. The report notes that young anglers could take trout worth nearly $5.00 in market value.
- Keep bag limits small (about five fish) to support financial success.
- Choose waters that can accommodate enough anglers for reasonable fees but are small enough for good angling. Murray and Wohlford (about 100 acres open) were considered satisfactory; Missouri experience shows streams can be quite small.

**Policy recommendations**
- New legislation is needed to explicitly authorize fee-fishing for trout in public waters. Existing water district fee practices stretch legal theory and do not cover freely open public waters.
- Suggested legislative actions include a State policy recognizing non-profit daily-fee financing and authorization for State, local, and quasi-public agencies to operate programs.
- New laws should not weaken State control over fish, private stocking, angling regulations, fishery management, or state licenses.
- The Department might operate programs in suitable state parks or as a service to local jurisdictions; test waters could build planning experience.
- Permit vending machines used by the U.S. Forest Service at campgrounds were noted as a possible tool, and Missouri planned to install one to reduce labor and traffic.

**Overall conclusion:** The report presents a choice between two paths. The pay-as-you-fish path promises new recreation with few financial problems or controversy. The traditional license-revenue path continues the controversy and missed opportunities of the existing catchable-trout program. The authors favor the former, and the report concludes that its timing is right.
