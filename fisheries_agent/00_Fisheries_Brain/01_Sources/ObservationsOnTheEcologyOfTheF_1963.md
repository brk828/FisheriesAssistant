# Observations on the Ecology of the Freshwater Jellyfish in Lake Mead, Nevada

## Executive Summary
This study by Deacon & Haskell documents the ecology, behavior, and population dynamics of the [[Freshwater Jellyfish]] (*Craspedacusta sowerbyi*) in [[Lake Mead]], Nevada, based on eleven field collections and targeted diel activity studies conducted from October 1962 to January 1963. Key findings include a pronounced diel vertical migration pattern (concentration near the bottom in early morning, near-uniform distribution by mid-afternoon), a strong positive phototaxis distinct from light-induced activity, a heavily female-skewed sex ratio throughout the study period, and a population bloom correlated with above-average water temperatures and an unusually rapid rise in reservoir water level following a period of unusual stability.

## Background & Description
*Craspedacusta sowerbyi* Lankester is a freshwater hydrozoan medusa with a cosmopolitan but sporadic global distribution. Prior ecological work (Pennak 1956, Lytle 1960/1962, Rice 1958, Woodhead 1943) established baseline patterns of seasonal occurrence and morphology in other North American water bodies. This paper follows up on Deacon and Haskell's (1963) initial report of the species' appearance in Lake Mead in 1962–63, the first substantiated record for the reservoir (an earlier, unconfirmed sighting was reported by NPS Ranger Captain Gene Gatzke).

**Study Site:**
- **Lake Mead** — reservoir formed by [[Hoover Dam]] (completed 1935; lake filled by 1941).
- Water chemistry: hard water, total dissolved solids ~800 ppm.
- Water level regime: characteristic slow drawdown from midsummer through following spring, followed by a rapid spring rise (multiple-use reservoir).
- Primary study location: **Swallow Cove**, Boulder Basin (northern boundary), immediately upstream of Hoover Dam; supplementary collections from **Vegas Wash**.
- Swallow Cove study grid: ~250 x 400 ft area, 26 stations spaced 50 ft apart; depth range 15–70 ft; substrate primarily sand/silt with rocky margins along a NE cliff face; green algal scum on silt bottom; no rooted aquatic vegetation (attributed to extreme water-level fluctuation).
- Medusae reported widely in sheltered coves throughout Boulder Basin and in Boulder Canyon, but never confirmed in open water away from shore.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat preference:** Medusae favored sheltered, shallow coves over open lake water; no reliable sightings in open water were reported by any observer.
- **Water level correlation:** The 1962 "jellyfish bloom" followed an unusually stable water level period (Jan 1959–Apr 1962) followed by a rapid rise to near-record high lake elevation — a sequence unique in the reservoir's history up to that point (see historical water-level record, 1940–1963).
- **Temperature correlation:** Above-average June–December water and air temperatures (Boulder City Weather Station and Bureau of Reclamation records) coincided with population bloom; population decline coincided with maximum surface water temperatures falling below 60°F after 2 January 1963.
- **Substrate/vegetation:** Absence of rooted aquatic vegetation in the study area, likely due to extreme water level fluctuations typical of Lake Mead's operational regime.
- **Comparative ecology:** Similar to findings in Virginia (Rice 1958) and Colorado (Pennak 1956), sex ratios in Lake Mead were skewed and bloom periods were short-lived, suggesting these are general life-history traits of the species rather than site-specific anomalies.
- **Trophic behavior analogy:** The feeding-associated vertical migration pattern (rising via pulsation, sinking with tentacles extended) parallels behavior documented in the marine hydrozoan *[[Gonionemus]]* (Hyman 1940).

## Key Findings

### Population Timeline
- First appeared in Lake Mead: **June 1962** (per Harry Wham, Whamco Divers).
- Became abundant: **late September 1962**.
- First formal collections: **25 October 1962**; eleven collections at ~weekly intervals through **25 January 1963**.
- Population remained high in Swallow Cove through at least **11 January 1963**; sharply reduced by **25 January** (only 12 medusae collected); **completely absent by 8 February 1963**.
- Vegas Wash population: abundant 16 Nov 1962, greatly reduced by 14 Dec 1962, absent by 18 Jan 1963.

### Diel Vertical Migration & Activity Patterns
- **Morning (1000 hrs):** Medusae strongly concentrated near the bottom (e.g., 30–45 ft depth averaged 19.3 medusae/4 bounce dives vs. much lower counts near surface).
- **Midday (1200–1400 hrs):** Vertical distribution became nearly uniform, interpreted as a sign of maximal population-wide activity (pulsation-driven rise/sink feeding behavior).
- **Depth-based habitat preference:** Deeper stations (45 ft) consistently held larger populations than shallower (30 ft) stations at all sampled times (Table 2).
- **Surface abundance:** Lowest at night (2400–0430 hrs), increasing rapidly after sunrise, reaching a maximum by 1000–1400 hrs. Shaded banks (beneath high NE cliff) showed delayed surface appearance relative to sun-exposed banks, directly implicating light as a proximate trigger for activity.
- **Nighttime behavior:** Very few medusae observed on the bottom despite inferred presence there; hypothesized burial in soft silt or extreme difficulty of visual detection due to transparency. Floating individuals at night were motionless/slowly sinking, resuming pulsation only when touched or illuminated.

### Light Response (Laboratory Experiment)
- A divided-aquarium light-choice experiment (60-W incandescent lamp, 13-day trial) demonstrated a clear **positive phototaxis** in *C. sowerbyi*, distinct from a generalized light-induced increase in activity.
- Authors propose a compound behavioral model: phototaxis draws medusae toward the light (surface) during the day; upon reaching maximum illumination (the surface), a feeding-related behavioral response predominates, causing the animal to sink — explaining the observed uniform vertical distribution at midday rather than pure surface aggregation.

### Sex Ratio
- Females consistently outnumbered males across all eleven collections (Table 4).
- Males occurred sporadically and always in low numbers, with the exception of **2 December 1962**, when male abundance was at least 5x higher than any other sampling date (20 males per 100 females).
- **No males were collected after 21 December 1962**, coinciding closely with maximum daily water temperatures dropping below 60°F.
- This pattern (sporadic, low male occurrence) parallels observations by Rice (1958) in a Virginia quarry pond.

### Environmental Correlates of Bloom/Decline
- **Bloom onset:** correlated with above-average water/air temperatures and a rapid, unusually high rise in lake water level after an atypically stable low-water period.
- **Decline:** correlated with water temperatures dropping below 60°F; authors caution correlation may not indicate causation, since jellyfish bloom periods are generally short-lived across studied populations (citing Lytle 1963, Pennak 1956, Rice 1958, Woodhead 1943), suggesting a possible innate seasonal termination of the medusoid stage independent of temperature.

### Methodological Notes
- Vertical distribution data collected via 19 SCUBA divers (Las Vegas Silver Flippers Diving Club) performing standardized "bounce dives" with hoop-sampling technique across 26 stations.
- Surface abundance data collected via boat transects illuminated by Coleman lantern, with dual independent observer counts; observer discrepancies attributed to differences in visual acuity/conditions rather than true abundance differences.
- Sex determination via gonad squash microscopy, sampling until 100 females identified per collection (or entire collection if fewer available).
