# Fish Movement Through Intermittent Stream Channels: A Case History Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #intermittent-streams

## Executive Summary

This report, prepared by Sally E. Stefferud and Jerome A. Stefferud in cooperation with Paul C. Marsh of the [[Native Fish Lab]] at Arizona State University for the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (dated August 13, 2007), examines whether fish, both native and nonnative, can move upstream through "dry" intermittent and ephemeral stream reaches in Arizona. The authors argue that this movement is real but poorly documented, and that it has direct consequences for barrier placement in invasive-species management. Eighteen case studies from Arizona streams are presented, drawn mainly from the authors' knowledge rather than an exhaustive literature or agency search. The report concludes that such movements are moderately likely in a given year and high over multi-decade periods, and that physical barriers, not dry channels, are the reliable control.

## Background & Description

The report frames the debate over [[Nonnative Fish Invasion]] of southwestern waters. Few field biologists in the Southwest doubt that most fish can traverse dry reaches during flow events, yet the process has been poorly documented and often met with skepticism.

**Flow terminology** (from USGS descriptors):
- **Perennial**: flows year-round, though it may cease under severe drought.
- **Ephemeral**: flows only during and immediately after rain; channel lies above the water table.
- **Intermittent**: flows at certain times of year, may cease in dry years; includes perennial-interrupted, spatially intermittent, and subterranean types.

Arizona supports all of these flow patterns. Over the past 150 years, groundwater pumping, diversion and watershed alteration have reduced perennial flow and increased perennial-interrupted and intermittent channels.

**Life-history context:** Native species such as [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Longfin Dace]] colonize temporarily wetted habitats. Nonnative species such as [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Red Shiner]] tend to succeed in these systems because they are adapted to variable hydrology.

**Key framing questions:** how much flow, for how long, and with what combination of variables a fish can transit a dry reach. Answers vary by reach, species, age class and flow event.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area:** Eighteen streams in three basins tributary to the Gulf of Mexico, including six subbasins of the [[Gila River]]. Case studies were selected because they are small, naturally intermittent or ephemeral, and have little sport-fishery potential, reducing the chance of stocking.

**Confounding factors:** Arizona's larger intermittent streams are often linked to reservoirs, ponds and stocking, which makes the source of fish hard to establish. The evidence is anecdotal, so alternative sources (upstream populations, stocking) cannot always be excluded.

**Flow thresholds from the 2001 [[Central Arizona Project]] biological opinion:**
- [[Santa Cruz River]] near its mouth: flow about 20% of the time, with 30 to 150 days of continuous flow documented.
- [[Hassayampa River]] near Morristown: flow about 50% of the time, with 30-day flows reaching the mouth roughly once every 10 years.
- Reach of [[Scottsdale]] (Indian Bend Wash): surface water about 1% of the time.

**Movement criteria:** A fish may successfully move through a normally dry reach if the reach is long (for example, >10 miles), surface flow is very infrequent and brief (as in flash flooding), and the gradient is high (as in constricted canyons or steep reaches). A physical barrier such as a waterfall, dam or concrete structure reduces the likelihood to near zero.

**Methods:** Direct observation during high-flow events is not generally possible. The authors used case studies of changes in known distributions in Arizona's perennial and intermittent channels, selected to minimize confounding.

## Key Findings

**Conclusions and summary of cases:**
- Native and nonnative fish, including [[Longfin Dace]], [[Sonora Chub]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Red Shiner]] and [[Green Sunfish]], have moved upstream through dry reaches in multiple Arizona streams.
- Upstream movement through dry channels is the main mechanism of invasion considered; its likelihood is moderate in a given year and high over multi-decade periods.
- Physical barriers are the most technically effective control. The report argues that dry reaches should not be assumed to block invasion, citing the [[Aravaipa Creek]] red shiner case, where a 5-6 mile intermittent reach did not stop colonization.

**Summary of case studies (18 streams):**
- **[[Aguajita Spring]]** (Pima County): nonnative [[Western Mosquitofish]] and native [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] and longfin dace found in 1990; fish were assumed to have moved 1.5 miles of dry channel.
- **[[Aravaipa Creek]]** (Pinal County): in 1990 red shiner moved through the intermittent reach; colonization succeeded in 1997. Concrete barriers were completed in 2001 after delays.
- **[[California Gulch]]** (Santa Cruz County): [[Sonora Chub]] expanded from two core areas over 2.2 miles of intermittent channel.
- **[[Cave Canyon]]** (Cochise County): longfin dace present; green sunfish from Presa La Casa Grande (Mexico) restricted by bedrock barriers.
- **[[Connor Wash]]** (Gila County): longfin dace of unknown source found in 1994; no surface water in 2001 survey.
- **[[Dry Creek]]** (Yavapai County): longfin dace moved upstream at least twice over a 3.3-mile dry reach.
- **[[Joaquin Creek]] and [[Sycamore Canyon]]** (Cochise County): green sunfish spread upstream from Presa La Casa Grande.
- **[[Lime Creek]]** (Yavapai County): green sunfish periodically enter but do not persist; a barrier is recommended.
- **[[Los Fresnos Arroyo]]** (Sonora, Mexico): nonnative fishes invaded the [[Los Fresnos Cienega]] through a 5-mile perennial-interrupted reach.
- **[[O'Donnell Canyon]]** (Santa Cruz County): green sunfish removed by piscicide; reinvasion prevented by concrete grade-control structures.
- **[[Oak Grove Canyon]]** (Santa Cruz County): longfin dace and western mosquitofish recorded.
- **[[Redrock Canyon]]** (Santa Cruz County): western mosquitofish and longfin dace from two sources, with a natural falls acting as a barrier.
- **[[Sharp Spring]]** (Santa Cruz County): western mosquitofish colonized from the Santa Cruz River.
- **[[Temporal Gulch]]** (Santa Cruz County): desert sucker and longfin dace present; the lower 2 miles appear inhospitable to movement.

**Interpretation:** Fish were recorded across 18 Arizona streams; the report stresses that these cases are a non-exhaustive sample and that many more likely exist. The authors conclude that the stated source-uncertainty is outweighed by the likelihood of natural movement, particularly in winter when conditions favor it.
