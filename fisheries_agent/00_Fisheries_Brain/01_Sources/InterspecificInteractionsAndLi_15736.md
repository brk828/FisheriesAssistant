# Interspecific Interactions and Limiting Factors of Abundance and Distribution in the Red River Pupfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This field study examines why the [[Red River Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon rubrofluviatilis*) is restricted mainly to saline waters within its range in the [[Red River]] and [[Brazos River]] drainages of western Oklahoma and Texas. Based on 59 seining localities sampled in 1969 and 67 museum collections from 1963, the authors found no intrinsic physiological barrier to the species. Instead, they argue that competition and behavioral interference with fresh-water fishes, especially the [[Plains Killifish]] (*Fundulus zebrinus*), limit its distribution. A west-to-east "filter bridge" of declining saline streams is identified as the main constraint on eastward range expansion.

## Background & Description

The study addresses how interspecific interactions shape the abundance and distribution of a [[Cyprinodontidae]] species. Most cyprinodontids occupy restricted, often isolated waters with few other fishes, and introduced exotics have contributed to the decline of several members of the family. Prior work on cyprinodontid ecology was sparse, and the authors focus on the pupfish's relationship with *F. zebrinus*, its closest sympatric ecological and phylogenetic associate.

**Methods (summary):**
- **Field collections:** 59 localities in the Red River drainage (Oklahoma and Texas), sampled during seining trips of 8–14 June and 4–8 July 1969, using a 10-ft seine.
- **Measured variables:** turbidity, stream width and depth, vegetation, bottom type, current speed, and salinity (estimated as total dissolved solids with a meter calibrated to [[Sodium Chloride]] standards).
- **Standardization:** Abundance was corrected to a standard of five seine hauls, based on the assumption that hauls containing a species represent its preferred habitat.
- **Supplementary data:** 67 collections from the [[University Of Oklahoma Museum Of Zoology]] (1963, Lindsey and Bates), restricted to the pupfish's range, plus behavioral observations from 1967 to 1970.
- **Statistics:** Product-moment correlations, with angular transformation of proportions, and contingency (chi-square) analysis of species co-occurrence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geographic range and abundance:** The pupfish occurs commonly in western parts of its range and sporadically in eastern parts. Its western boundary follows the [[High Plains Escarpment]]. Its easternmost Red River record is Oscar Creek near Oscar, Jefferson County, Oklahoma. Its easternmost Brazos record is near Seymour, Baylor County, Texas. It is abundant in the Salt Fork of the Brazos but rare in the Double Mountain Fork, where a single 18 mm specimen was taken in 10 collections at seven localities. It was not recorded from the Clear Fork of the Brazos or the Washita River.

**Physical setting:** Larger streams are shallow and low-gradient, with sandy bottoms and little emergent vegetation. Flow fluctuates strongly, with floods after heavy rain and intermittency during droughts. Much of the western region is underlain by chloride salt beds and gypsum. Dissolution of these minerals, together with oil-field pollution, low precipitation (ca. 50 cm/year), and high evaporation, produces saline streams. Salinity declines from west to east as fresh-water tributaries increase. Western streams often exceed 10 ppt, while eastern streams, especially small ones, are typically 1 ppt or less.

**Climate:** Air temperatures range from near −15 °C to about 43 °C. Shallow flowing water tracks air temperature closely, usually within 2 °C. Shallow pools can develop thermal gradients of 3–4 °C, providing partial refuge.

**Fauna:** The local fauna is depauperate compared with eastern Oklahoma and Texas, which the authors link to greater habitat instability and structural homogeneity in the west. Collections with fewer than five species occurred at salinities of 10–20 ppt or more and in transient fresh-water streams.

**Species associations:** Contingency analysis of the 12 most frequent species identified three positively associated groups:
1. **Rubro-zebrinus association:** *C. rubrofluviatilis* and *F. zebrinus*.
2. **Placitus-bairdi association:** *Hybognathus placitus*, *Notropis bairdi*, *N. percobromus*, and *Hybopsis aestivalis*.
3. **Lutrensis-affinis association:** *Notropis lutrensis*, *Gambusia affinis*, *Lepomis cyanellus*, *L. megalotis*, *Pimephales promelas*, and *P. vigilax*.

The placitus-bairdi and lutrensis-affinis groups showed negative associations. Among the 1969 collections, the pupfish was found at salinities of 0.4–51.7 ppt, and *F. zebrinus* at −3 to 43.2 ppt (the table's range values are reported as recorded).

## Key Findings

- **Salinity restriction:** The pupfish was found across a wide salinity range, from 0.4 ppt to near 150 ppt, so low dissolved solids alone do not limit it. Its occurrence was, however, strongly skewed toward saline waters. In collections above 10 ppt, it occurred in 100% of them, compared with 26% of more dilute collections.
- **Association with killifish:** *F. zebrinus* occurred in 83% of collections above 10 ppt and 74% of more dilute collections. The probability that a collection with pupfish also contained *F. zebrinus* was near 0.90.
- **Competitive exclusion hypothesis:** Pupfish numbers were negatively correlated with species number (r = −0.66; 0.01 < p < 0.05), and the relationship appeared closer to an exponential decline than a linear one. The authors conclude that the pupfish is effectively excluded from fresh water by extrinsic competition or predation, not by intrinsic physiological limits.
- **Filter-bridge model:** Reinforcement from saline source populations declines with distance eastward, creating a west-to-east "filter bridge" that limits range expansion. The pupfish's "fundamental niche" includes dilute waters, but its "realized niche" is restricted to saline waters.
- **Suppression of killifish at high salinity:** *F. zebrinus* abundance was depressed in saline waters where the pupfish was the main cohabitant, despite suitable physical habitat. The authors attribute this to pupfish competition and interference.
- **Extreme-salinity case:** On 14 July 1971, both species occurred in an intermittent pool of the Elm Fork (chloride 110 ppt; TDS 140–150 ppt). Roughly 1000 dead killifish but only one dead pupfish were found, suggesting a difference in salinity tolerance. Two miles downstream (86 ppt), the pupfish outnumbered killifish 100:1.
- **Feeding competition:** Both species feed mainly by nipping and digging on the bottom. Pupfish fed at 60–70 acts/min at peak activity, compared with 30–40 for killifish. Gut contents of the pupfish and killifish were dominated by bottom sediments (78% and 76%). The pupfish appears less selective and more active, which makes it less susceptible to feeding disruption.
- **Territorial and reproductive interference:** Pupfish males defend rigid territories from which they exclude all intruders except receptive females, and they often disrupt killifish courtship and spawning. Pupfish spawn from February to November, against March to October for killifish, and at temperatures of 13–34 °C versus about 20–34 °C. Pupfish males begin territorial defense earlier in the day.
- **Broad adaptations:** The pupfish is omnivorous, feeds at all water levels, has a long spawning season, tolerates a wide thermal range, and breeds in varied sites. The authors argue these traits give it a numbers advantage in simple faunas but make it overextended in species-rich prairie streams.
- **Temperature limits:** The highest successful incubation temperature recorded for fishes is 36.5 °C in the pupfish. Feeding occurs from about 4 °C to 40 °C or above.
- **Moderate-salinity success:** At 10–20 ppt, where at least 12 potentially competing species occur, the pupfish's competitive traits may still allow it to persist.
- **Overall conclusion:** Interspecific interaction is the primary factor shaping the pupfish's distribution and abundance. No single competitor appears responsible. Instead, the combined effects of several species, with *F. zebrinus* as the main competitor, likely exclude the pupfish from fresh water.
