# Application of Stoeltzner's Method to Determine Growth of Fish Scales

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This short paper from the [[Transactions of the American Fisheries Society]] (1965) evaluates whether [[Stoeltzner's Method]], a cobalt-sulfide staining technique originally developed for bone and later adapted to fish scales by [[Wallin Method|Wallin (1957)]], can reveal recent scale growth in fishes. The authors tested the method at [[Iowa State University]] between 1959 and 1960 with the aim of providing a rapid way to determine whether a fish was growing at the time of capture. The method produced visible growth zones, but these persisted in fish that were presumed to be non-growing, so the authors abandoned the adaptation.

## Background & Description

- **Principle:** Scales are a specialized bone that form naturally as thin sections. Treating a scale with [[Cobalt Nitrate]] and then [[Ammonium Sulfide]] forms brown or black cobalt sulfide where bone salts (cobalt bound to phosphate groups) are present.
- **Expected staining pattern (Wallin 1957):** Older calcified portions stain brown, recently calcified portions stain black, and the peripheral growth zone remains clear. Scales of fish in warm water with abundant food showed broad growth zones; scales of starved fish in cold water showed narrow or no zones.
- **Protocol developed by the authors:** Because Wallin gave no concentrations or staining times, the authors used 5% solutions: scales were soaked in cobalt nitrate for 2 minutes, rinsed in distilled water for ½ minute, placed in ammonium sulfide for 1 minute, rinsed briefly, and dried between microscope slides.
- **Species examined:** Carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), carpsuckers (*Carpiodes* spp.), redhorses (*Moxostoma* spp.), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), sunfishes (*Lepomis* spp.), [[Walleye]] (*Sander vitreus*, given as *Stizostedion vitreum*), [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), and small minnows (Cyprinidae), in both dried and fresh scales.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Study sites:** Des Moines River and Clear Lake, Iowa, plus the Mississippi River near Burlington, Iowa.
- **Growth drivers noted:** Water levels, temperature and food availability can change rapidly, which motivated the search for a method that reveals current growth status.
- **Controlled starvation test:** Fish were held in an aquarium at [[Iowa State University]] at 69 °F with no food.
- **Winter condition:** The Mississippi River was frozen over when fish were collected, where growth was presumed unlikely.

## Key Findings

1. **Aquarium test (starved fish):** Scales from four carpsuckers, retained after capture on 22 October 1959 (from a sample of 11 carpsuckers, 1 carp and 1 redhorse, all with definite growth zones), all still showed growth zones after 2 weeks. After 5 months with no food, the one remaining fish still showed growth zones. The method therefore did not detect the expected cessation of growth.
2. **Mississippi River test (March 1960):** Of 15 carp and 4 buffalo (*Ictiobus* sp.) collected from a frozen river, 2 carp and 2 buffalo had poor or very small growth zones, while 13 carp and 2 buffalo had zones "somewhat larger but still small" relative to scale size. Growth zones in fish that were probably not growing indicated a problem with the method.
3. **Authors' conclusion:** Either fish growth is slowly recorded in scales, or the staining method lacked sufficient sensitivity. Because the goal was to detect growth over the shortest possible interval, the adaptation of Stoeltzner's method was abandoned.
4. **Methodological note:** The paper highlights that Wallin's original report omitted reagent concentrations and staining times, so the authors had to establish their own protocol through experimentation.
