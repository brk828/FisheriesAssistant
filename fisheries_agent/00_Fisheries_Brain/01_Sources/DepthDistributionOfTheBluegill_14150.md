# Depth Distribution of the Bluegill (*Lepomis macrochirus*) in Farm Ponds During Summer Stratification

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 study by I. B. Byrd (Alabama Department of Conservation, Montgomery) examined how far [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*) use deep water in [[Farm Fish Ponds]] during [[Summer Stratification]]. Fish were sampled with wire traps set at multiple depths, paired with [[Dissolved Oxygen]] and [[Carbon Dioxide]] measurements and temperature profiles. Bluegills concentrated in shallow water, and the study identified a combined critical condition (dissolved oxygen of 0.3 p.p.m. or less with carbon dioxide of 4.4 p.p.m. or more) that defined a "critical depth." The author concluded that low oxygen and high carbon dioxide, rather than temperature, limited bluegill use of deeper water. The work was conducted from June 24 through September 12, 1949.

## Background & Description

The bluegill is widely stocked in farm fish ponds following recommendations by [[Swingle and Smith]] (1942), making its depth distribution a practical management concern. Anglers commonly assumed fish always retreat to the deepest, coldest water during summer heat; this study tested that assumption.

Earlier work framed the problem as follows:
- [[Shelford and Allee]] (1913, 1913a) showed that fishes avoid water high in carbon dioxide and low in dissolved oxygen, and that fish entering such water often died.
- [[Shelford]] (1914) proposed carbon dioxide as an index of water suitability, noting that 7.1 p.p.m. or more was usually accompanied by oxygen depletion.
- [[Pearse and Achtenberg]] (1920) found [[Yellow Perch]] in Wisconsin lakes concentrated just above the [[Thermocline]], entering stagnant water below it only occasionally.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study ponds.** The main work was done in a 2-acre pond considered typical of Alabama farm ponds. Four additional ponds (3, 6, 22, and 26 acres) were sampled briefly. All ponds were fertilized and managed following Swingle and Smith (1942).

**Sampling methods.**
- Bluegills were captured in 1-inch or ¼-inch mesh wire traps with two funnels, with interwoven white oak strips to prevent escape. Traps were baited with cottonseed cake or left unbaited.
- Traps were set on the bottom at 2–9 feet in the 2-acre pond, 4–8 feet in the 22-acre pond, and 4.5–8 feet in the 3-, 6-, and 26-acre ponds. Traps were checked every 6 to 45 hours.
- Water was collected with a [[Bottle Train Sampler]]. Dissolved oxygen was measured by the [[Rideal-Stewart Modification of the Winkler Method]]; carbon dioxide by the Association of Official Agricultural Chemists (1945) tentative method for free carbon dioxide.
- Temperatures were read with a [[Foxboro Portable Resistance Thermometer]] (°F), at 3 inches below the surface and at 1-foot intervals below.

**Thermal and oxygen conditions.**
- Surface-to-bottom temperature differences usually ranged from 12 to 18 °F.
- The 2-acre pond had a greater temperature gradient than the larger, wind-exposed ponds.
- Wave action mixed the 22-acre pond to about 8 feet, whereas in the sheltered 2-acre pond circulation rarely extended deeper than about 4 feet.

**Temporary drivers.** Heavy rain, cool spells, and extended cloudiness (1–3 days, often with abundant phytoplankton) shifted the critical depth. Flood inflows and convection from cooling distributed oxygen to stagnant water, while cloudiness and phytoplankton-driven oxygen depletion pushed the critical depth upward, about 18 inches nearer the surface.

## Key Findings

- **Shallow preference:** Bluegills were most abundant in shallow water during summer stratification.
- **Critical condition:** Survival for 6–45 hours was not possible at dissolved oxygen of 0.3 p.p.m. or less with carbon dioxide of 4.4 p.p.m. or more.
- **Critical depth:** Normally about 5 feet in the 2-acre pond and about 7 feet in the 22-acre pond. Critical depth increased with pond surface area, attributed to deeper wind-driven mixing.
- **Variability:** Critical depth rose after strong winds, heavy rain, or cool periods, and fell during prolonged cloudiness or reduced deep-water photosynthesis.
- **Mortality by condition (Table 1, average mortality over 6–45 h):**
  - Dissolved oxygen 0.0–0.3 p.p.m. with CO₂ 4.4–39.6 p.p.m.: 90.4%
  - 0.4–0.6 p.p.m. with CO₂ 4.4–12.1 p.p.m.: 26.2%
  - 0.7–0.9 p.p.m. with CO₂ 3.3–6.6 p.p.m.: 12.4%
  - 1.0–1.9 p.p.m. with CO₂ 1.1–7.7 p.p.m.: 9.9%
  - 2.0 p.p.m. and above with CO₂ 0.0–5.5 p.p.m.: 0.2%
- **Mortality threshold:** Mortality at 0.3 p.p.m. oxygen or less was reported as 64.2% greater than at 0.4–0.6 p.p.m.
- **Seasonal turnover:** In late August and early September, cooling deepened the critical depth until fall overturn made the whole pond usable; bluegills then dispersed to all depths.
- **Conclusion:** Ponds were clearly stratified in summer, and bluegills could not efficiently use cold, deep water because of oxygen and carbon dioxide limits, not temperature alone.
