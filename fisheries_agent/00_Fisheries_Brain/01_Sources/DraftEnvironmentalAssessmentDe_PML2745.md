# Designation of Critical Habitat for the Rio Grande Silvery Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft Environmental Assessment, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in Albuquerque, New Mexico, evaluates the designation of critical habitat for the endangered [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (*Hybognathus amarus*) under Section 4 of the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The designation was required by a February 1999 federal court order, which set a final deadline of June 23, 1999. The preferred alternative designates the mainstem of the Rio Grande in Socorro, Valencia, Bernalillo and Sandoval counties, New Mexico, about 262 km (163 mi) from the downstream side of the State Highway 22 bridge below [[Cochiti Dam]] to the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe Railroad crossing near San Marcial. The document concludes that the designation would add essentially no protection beyond that already provided by the species' 1994 listing and existing [[Section 7 Consultation]] requirements.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy:** One of seven species in the genus *Hybognathus*; first described from 19th-century railway surveys (Girard 1856).
- **Morphology:** A small minnow; adults may reach 90 mm total length, silver with emerald reflections.
- **Reproduction:** Spawning lasts about one month in late spring to early summer, coinciding with spring runoff, at water temperatures of 20–24 °C. Most spawners are Age 1 fish. Mean clutch size is about 270 eggs, and females may produce 3 to 18 clutches in a 12-hour period. Eggs are semi-buoyant and non-adhesive, drifting for up to 50 hours.
- **Growth and life span:** Larvae are about 1.7 mm at hatching and grow rapidly in low-velocity backwaters, reaching 39–41 mm by late autumn. Maximum longevity is about 25 months, and few survive beyond 13 months. The population is composed mainly of two age classes.
- **Diet:** The elongated gut indicates herbivory. Sand and silt in gut contents suggest that epipsammic algae is an important food source.
- **Habitat selection:** Fish favor low-velocity areas such as eddies, pools and backwaters, and avoid main-channel runs. Most were collected at velocities below 10 cm/s, rarely above 40 cm/s, and at depths under 20 cm or 31–40 cm. Silt was the substrate for more than 90% of individuals.
- **Historical range:** Once among the most abundant and widespread fishes in the basin, from Española, New Mexico, to the Gulf of Mexico, and in the [[Pecos River]]. It has been extirpated from the Pecos through habitat degradation and hybridization with the introduced [[Plains Minnow]].
- **Listing rationale:** Listed as endangered because of significant habitat loss. The sole extant population is threatened by annual dewatering, largely from agricultural diversion in the Middle Rio Grande Valley.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats and habitat modification**

- **Dewatering:** During a below-average water year, the channel may be dry from Isleta Diversion Dam downstream for 111 mi (about 179 km) for two months or more. Two consecutive dry years can eliminate the species from desiccated reaches.
- **Diversion dams:** These entrain fish into irrigation canals and block upstream recolonization, leaving downstream populations vulnerable.
- **Mainstem dams:** [[Cochiti Dam]], [[Abiquiu Dam]], [[El Vado Dam]], [[Heron Reservoir]] and others regulate flow, trap sediment and nutrients, favor nonnative fishes, and prevent dispersal.
- **Channelization and levees:** These straighten and shorten reaches, increase velocity, reduce habitat complexity and sever the river from its floodplain. Meanders, oxbows and backwaters have largely disappeared.
- **Water quality:** Low-flow periods are dominated by municipal and agricultural discharge.

**Reach-by-reach overview (four reaches of the Middle Valley, plus the Rio Grande ecosystem context)**

- **Cochiti Reach:** Clear, cool water below the dam; the silvery minnow is rare here.
- **Angostura Reach:** Includes the Albuquerque metropolitan area; receives major point discharges from the North and South Diversion Channels and the Albuquerque Wastewater Treatment Plant (about 80 cfs). Abundance increases downstream but never reaches the levels of the San Acacia Reach.
- **Isleta Reach:** Among the most adversely impacted by diversions (up to 1,070 cfs diverted at Isleta Diversion Dam); typically supports few fish.
- **San Acacia Reach:** The longest uninterrupted segment and the largest population. Historically had no return points for diverted water until 1996; the [[Low-Flow Conveyance Channel]] carried diverted water directly to Elephant Butte Reservoir.

**Other fauna and flora**

- **Fish:** Historic native fauna included 17–27 species. Several have been extirpated, and nonnatives now make up 38–76% of species. Red shiner was the dominant native, comprising 53% of one catch (Platania 1993).
- **Riparian vegetation:** Cottonwood-dominated bosque, with invasive Russian olive (north) and salt cedar (south).
- **Birds:** 277 bird species recorded in the Velarde to San Acacia corridor (Hink and Ohmart 1984); 241 species (74% of New Mexico's 325) recorded in the Middle Rio Grande riparian zone and adjacent agricultural land. The corridor is a key migratory stopover.
- **Reptiles, amphibians and mammals:** 57 reptile species and 13 amphibian species may occur; 16 small mammal species and 18 large mammal species were documented.
- **Listed species:** [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (endangered), [[Bald Eagle]] (threatened), [[Interior Least Tern]] (endangered), whooping crane (experimental non-essential population), [[American Peregrine Falcon]] (endangered), and mountain plover (proposed threatened).

**Socioeconomic and tribal context**

- Irrigated agriculture dominates land use (about 90% of river water use in the impact area). In 1993, 51,202 acres were cropped, with alfalfa at 60%.
- Six Pueblos (Cochiti, Santo Domingo, San Felipe, Santa Ana, Sandia and Isleta) are within the Middle Valley and are sovereign entities. Appendix 1 addresses the five conservation standards of Secretarial Order 3206.

## Key Findings

1. **Court-ordered designation:** The final designation was required by a District of New Mexico court order and had to be published by June 23, 1999.
2. **Preferred alternative:** Designate the continuous mainstem of the Rio Grande (about 163 miles) as critical habitat, the only area where the species has been collected in the recent past (50 years) and where it is currently known to exist.
3. **Primary constituent elements:** Stream morphology that supplies flowing water for food and cover; water quality that prevents stagnation; and water quantity that prevents isolated pools.
4. **Sampling decline:** Forty-six collections from 1990 to spring 1992 yielded 23 species and 38,501 specimens, but only 10 silvery minnows. Sites that had previously been dominated by the species produced none on resampling.
5. **Limited added benefit:** The EA concludes that designation would provide effectively no additional protection, because Section 7 consultation with the Corps and Reclamation has applied since the 1994 listing and designation duplicates existing protection.
6. **Alternatives rejected:** A no-action alternative was retained as the baseline. Adding new areas was rejected because no additional qualifying habitat was identified. Excluding portions of the river was rejected because the habitat is continuous and subject to interrelated impacts.
7. **Impact assessment:** Under both alternatives, the EA finds no significant impacts on fish, wildlife, plants, recreation, water management, agriculture or socioeconomic conditions beyond existing impacts from the 1994 listing.
8. **Economic data:** Agriculture employed 14,078 people (about 2% of total employment) in 1993, and about 1% of total income in the project area.
