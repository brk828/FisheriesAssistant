# Toxicity to Fish of Poisonous Substances Found in Industrial Waste Water

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1946 paper by J. Grindley of the [[Water Pollution Research Laboratory]] (Watford, England) reports controlled laboratory bioassays of nine toxic substances that occur in industrial waste water. Tests used [[Eurasian Minnow]] (*Phoxinus phoxinus*) and yearling [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri* var. *shasta*) to estimate the threshold concentration at which fish lose equilibrium during short exposures of a few days. The results are intended as a guide to the relative toxicity of substances that treatment processes must remove before effluent reaches rivers. The study found that the substances differed widely in potency. Dinitrophenol was far more toxic than picric acid, arsenite was more toxic than arsenate, and hard water increased the toxicity of ammonium salts.

## Background & Description

Most earlier toxicity work on industrial effluents had been done in America on fish species not found in the British Isles. Data on British fish were sparse, and test conditions were often unreported. The research formed part of wider investigations into treating and disposing of industrial waste water, with the aim of lowering toxic concentrations to levels that would not damage fisheries in a river of known size.

Grindley noted that short-term threshold tests are not a direct measure of long-term river safety. Lower concentrations acting over long periods could damage fisheries directly or through effects on food organisms. Short-term thresholds were still considered useful for ranking substances.

Several factors affect toxicity: the water type, poison concentration, temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, and the species, size, and condition of the fish. Individual variation in susceptibility was considered the largest source of error.

**Methods**

- **Test water:** [[Watford Tap Water]] had a temporary hardness of 26.5 and total hardness of 27.8 parts CaCO₃ per 100,000, with pH about 7.4. Some tests used distilled water aerated for several hours. Later tests were held at 18 °C.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** Measured by the [[Winkler Method]] at the start and end of tests. Because chromates interfere with this method, a separate calibration was used to set aeration rates.
- **Test animals:** Minnows (age unknown) and trout (about 1 year old, 3–4 in. long). Fish were held in large glass tanks with a slow flow of dechlorinated tap water and fed minced bullock's heart.
- **Endpoint:** Fish were removed to fresh water when they lost equilibrium. Recovery or death was recorded.
- **Toxicity index:** Individual toxicity was 100 divided by the immersion time in minutes. The average toxicity of each solution was the mean across fish. Standard deviations were reported to show spread. Test solutions were renewed every 2 days in longer tests.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper treats fish as sensitive indicators of effluent quality in rivers, and it also raises the effect of toxicants on invertebrates that form the fish food supply. Two cladocerans, [[Daphnia Magna]] and other invertebrate groups, appear in comparison studies. Chironomid larvae, mayfly nymphs, and freshwater shrimps are mentioned in the context of arsenite. Several North American fish species were referenced for comparison.

Water chemistry played a central role in the results. pH values below 5.0 in potassium dichromate solutions were associated with acidity effects, whereas higher pH solutions showed other toxic factors. Ammonium toxicity rose with pH and with water hardness.

## Key Findings

**Substances tested:** Sodium arsenite, sodium arsenate, sodium picrate, sodium dinitrophenate, zinc sulphate, potassium chromate, potassium dichromate, ammonium chloride, and ammonium sulphate.

**Arsenic compounds ([[Sodium Arsenite]] and [[Sodium Arsenate]])**
- Neutralized arsenite was more toxic to minnows than arsenate at the same arsenic concentration.
- At about 20 p.p.m. As, minnows in arsenite survived a mean of about 36 hr before overturning. At about 250 p.p.m. As, minnows in arsenate survived about 16 hr.
- No fish recovered after removal to fresh water.
- Earlier studies cited include Wiebe (1930), who found 5 p.p.m. As as arsenite non-deleterious to several North American species. Ellis (1937) reported 1 p.p.m. As killed *Daphnia magna* in distilled water. Surber & Meehan (1931) reported fatal effects on invertebrates at 1.9–3 p.p.m. As.

**Phenolic compounds ([[Sodium Picrate]] and [[Sodium Dinitrophenate]])**
- At a given concentration, dinitrophenol was much more toxic than picric acid.
- At 200 p.p.m., mean immersion before overturning was about 26 hr for picric acid and about 22 min for dinitrophenol.
- The limiting concentration for dinitrophenol was estimated at about 30 p.p.m.; picric acid's limit was judged to be of the same order.
- Overturning was clearly defined, and no fish recovered.

**Zinc sulphate ([[Zinc Sulphate]])**
- Tested only at 25 p.p.m. Zn in distilled water with 10 trout.
- Mean immersion before losing equilibrium was 133 min.
- The endpoint was sharply defined, and all fish died after transfer to fresh water.

**Chromium compounds ([[Potassium Chromate]] and [[Potassium Dichromate]])**
- At equivalent chromium concentrations, dichromate was more toxic than chromate.
- The lowest tested concentrations (20 p.p.m. Cr) gave mean immersion times of about 60 hr for chromate and about 72 hr for dichromate.
- Dichromate solutions fell below pH 5.0 at higher concentrations, and the toxicity was attributed partly to acidity.
- The limiting concentration for both salts was just under 20 p.p.m. Cr.
- Ellis (1937) found 100 p.p.m. CrO₃ (52 p.p.m. Cr) did not kill goldfish in 100 hr in hard water.
- Pentelow (private communication) reported 5.2 p.p.m. Cr from potassium dichromate slightly toxic to brown trout (*Salmo trutta*) in Nottingham tap water.
- Anderson (1944) found the *Daphnia magna* threshold for chromic acid in Lake Erie water was below 0.3 p.p.m. Cr.

**Ammonium salts ([[Ammonium Chloride]] and [[Ammonium Sulphate]])**
- Equal amounts of the two salts in the same water type gave no appreciable difference in toxicity.
- Solutions were much more toxic in hard tap water than in distilled water.
- In distilled water the limiting concentration was just under 100 p.p.m. NH₃. Some fish were unaffected after 4 days at that level.
- In tap water, ammonium chloride was slightly toxic at 50 p.p.m. NH₃.
- Wells (1915) found 170 p.p.m. NH₃ as ammonium chloride killed bluegills in 4 hr 45 min in hard water, but the mean survival time in distilled water was 18 days.
- Ellis (1937) concluded that toxicity increases by at least 200% as pH rises from 7.4 to 8.0.
- Clark & Adams (1913) found 57 p.p.m. NH₃ had no effect on shiners and carp.
- Anderson (1944) reported *Daphnia magna* thresholds of less than 43 p.p.m. NH₃ (as NH₄Cl) and less than 28 p.p.m. NH₃ (as (NH₄)₂SO₄).

**Methodological conclusions**
- Trout overturning was well defined, and few fish recovered when returned to fresh water.
- Results support the idea that temperature, pH, hardness, and aeration must be controlled for reproducible threshold values.
- The author notes the largest source of error is individual variation among fish.
