# Observations on Spawning and Migration of Chinook Salmon in the Columbia River near Hanford Works

#ichthyology #marine-biology #oncorhynchus

## Executive Summary

This report by D. G. Watson presents field observations on [[Chinook Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*) spawning and juvenile migration in the [[Columbia River]] near the [[Hanford Works]]. Studies began in fall 1947 to determine whether [[Pile Effluent]] discharged from the Hanford Works adversely affects a local population of an economically important food fish. The main indicators were annual [[Aerial Redd Surveys]], carcass examinations, [[Fry Sampling]], and radioactivity measurements in fish tissue. The author concludes that no harm has been detected so far, while noting that observations covered only one four-year cycle, so a definitive conclusion is premature.

## Background & Description

Chinook salmon are an important food fish in the Columbia River. Adult returns depend on several factors: survival of young in fresh water, natural ocean mortality, and artificial mortality from commercial and sport fisheries. The author considers young salmon the most vulnerable life stage to pile effluent because of:

- lower tolerance to pollution;
- longer exposure time to the effluent;
- higher [[Metabolic Rate]];
- resulting higher [[Radioactivity Density]].

The study assumed that the local spawning population is a distinct race, descended from ancestors that also spawned in this vicinity. It further assumed that this race faces the same survival hazards as the overall [[Fall Run]] of chinook in the Columbia River. Under that assumption, the number of spawners in the local area should be proportionally as large as the overall fall-run count. Unusual juvenile mortality peculiar to this reach would therefore show up as a deviation from that proportion.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and methods.** Aerial surveys were flown each fall from 1947 over the river section from North Richland to Priest Rapids. More than one survey was usually flown each year to capture the spawning peak and the maximum number of redds (spawning nests). Results are summarized in Table 1 of the source, which is not reproduced here.

**Spawning timing and location.** Spawning begins around mid-October and ends by late November. Most spawning occurs in the same areas year after year. Fry still carrying more than half their yolk sac were taken, which suggests that some eggs were spawned in the immediate vicinity, though many young passing the site probably originated upstream of the Hanford Works.

**Carcass surveys.** Spawned-out carcasses appear on the shore from late October. During routine sampling, sex and gonad condition were recorded, and length was measured when carcasses were not too decomposed. Nearly all examined fish appeared to have spawned completely. The author cautions that shoreline carcass counts depend on river currents, which vary between years, so they cannot be used as a measure of the number of spawning fish.

**Life history uncertainty.** Opinion was divided on whether most Columbia River chinook complete their life cycle in four or five years. The author assumed four-year fish for the local population in order to link 1947 parents with 1951 spawners.

**Juvenile observations.** Observations on young chinook began in spring 1949. Young fish first appear in the river in mid-spring (the exact month is garbled in the scan), are most abundant in April and May, and are no longer evident by the end of June. The river's large size prevented any direct estimate of the number of young fish passing the site.

## Key Findings

- **Spawning trend:** Redd counts in 1951 for the local area exceeded the 1947 brood-year count, while the overall fall run in 1951 was substantially lower than in 1947. The author interprets this as evidence that the young from the 1947 spawning were not harmed by Hanford Works effluent.
- **Carcass radioactivity:** Tissues from dead and nearly dead salmon recovered along the river showed very low activity, all below 10 (units garbled in the scan). The author notes that chinook rarely feed after entering fresh water, which may partly explain these low values.
- **Fry abundance:** Fry appeared more abundant along the shore in 1951 than in 1949 or 1950, the two previous years.
- **Fry size:** No significant differences in length or weight were found among sampling locations, which the author takes to indicate either a single race or a homogeneous mix of several races.
- **Mean fry size:**
  - 1950: 39 mm and 0.65 g on average.
  - 1951: 38 mm and 0.61 g on average.
- **Fry radioactivity:** The average radioactivity density of chinook fry is comparable to that of young of other native fish species.
- **Laboratory tolerance:** Controlled incubation and rearing experiments, reported elsewhere, showed that early-life chinook can tolerate pollutant concentrations several times higher than those normally found in the river.
- **Overall conclusion:** Because only one four-year cycle has been observed, the author states it is premature to say definitively that the fish are not harmed by Hanford Project pollutants. Current investigations indicate that no harm has been done.
