# Native Fish Populations in the Virgin River, 1976–1993

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft report by [[BIO/WEST, Inc.]] (prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], co-funded by the [[Southern Nevada Water Authority]]) re-examines a 1976–1993 monitoring database for native fishes of the Virgin River. Its central aim is to determine what caused the decline of [[Woundfin]] and other native fishes, and whether the long-term database is reliable enough to support that inference. The authors first tested the statistical reliability of the data, then used log-transformed woundfin abundance to describe year-to-year changes at four primary stations. They found that first-pass and seine-unit counts are nearly interchangeable, that season, habitat, water temperature and discharge explain little year-to-year variation, and that woundfin declined at Ash Creek, Beaver Dam Wash and Twin Bridges while Hurricane Bridge remained comparatively stable. Red shiner invasion is linked to losses at Beaver Dam Wash; drought and Quail Creek Reservoir effects are considered but their roles remain unresolved.

## Background & Description

**Study focus.** The Virgin River database, assembled by [[Thom Hardy]] at [[Utah State University]], contains sampling records from the [[Virgin River Fishes Recovery Team]] (originally the Woundfin Recovery Team) beginning in 1976, usually in spring (April–May) and fall (September). Few researchers had used it because of its size and complexity.

**Species of concern.**
- [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*): listed as Endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973; it fell from abundant over 100 river miles to common in only about 30 miles.
- [[Virgin River Chub]] (*Gila seminuda*): listed as Endangered because of perceived population reductions.
- [[Virgin Spinedace]] (*Lepidomeda mollispinis*): proposed for listing as Threatened due to habitat loss and fragmentation.
- [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*): a non-native species whose increase is a central question.

**Stakeholder questions.** [[Jim Heinrich]] of the Nevada Department of Fish and Game posed six questions, covering the woundfin decline, drought effects, the [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] (completed early 1985) and its 1989 dam break, the cause of red shiner increase, and the effect of the 1988 chemical treatment in Utah.

**Prior analyses.** The report reviews earlier work:
- [[Deacon and Hardy (1984)]] linked spring flow to woundfin reproductive success, concluding flows below 130 cfs or above 1000 cfs reduced it.
- [[Deacon (1988)]] attributed woundfin declines in Utah to Quail Creek flow changes and sinkhole-related water-quality changes, and linked red shiner at Beaver Dam Wash to low-flow years.
- [[Gregory and Deacon (1994)]] blamed Quail Creek operations, red shiner invasion and the 1988 poisoning for native fish losses.
- [[Lentsch and Bradwich]] (undated) linked red shiner expansion to a lack of flash floods above 2,000 cfs.
- [[Hardy et al. (1989)]] analyzed Hickman collections (July 1984–August 1987), recommended at least 25 seine units to detect density changes, and developed suitability curves for IFIM analyses.
- [[Hardy et al. (1995)]] found woundfin populations tracked flow-year strength and concluded Quail Creek had little negative effect.

**Workshop.** A researchers' workshop at UNLV (July 10–12, 1995), organized by Dr. Deacon, did not reach its goal of explaining the decline. It instead exposed doubts about database consistency and prompted the reliability analyses reported here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Standard seines measured 15 × 4 ft with 1/8-inch mesh, hauled for 30 ft in sand runs. Early monitoring used non-standard protocols, and seine sizes were not standardized until about 1980. Data were grouped by season: fall (8/15–11/14), winter (11/15–2/14), spring (2/15–5/14) and summer (5/15–8/14).

**Dataset.** Of 3,068 samples, 69 were excluded for single passes or erroneous pass information. The final four primary stations were:
- Ash Creek (river mile 133–135)
- Hurricane Bridge (RM 123–129)
- Twin Bridges (RM 111.6)
- Beaver Dam Wash (RM 75.25)

**Flow and temperature.** Discharge at the day of sampling differed significantly between seasons (p < 0.001), in the order summer < fall < winter < spring. Most samples (2,219 of 2,983) lacked water temperature, so it was estimated by regression (R² = 0.782). Temperature explained essentially none of the woundfin variation (R² ≤ 0.052).

**Habitat.** Runs, riffles and pools dominated the sampling (2,761 of 2,983 samples). Woundfin were rarely found in riffles and pools. Runs held 2,183 samples and had the lowest CV (91.2), compared with pools (118.3) and riffles (122.8).

**Drought and flow.** The authors used a K-means cluster analysis of flow-duration curves (Littlefield, Arizona gage) to define drought years as 1970–1972, 1974–1977 and 1989–1992. The 1989 Quail Creek dam breach flow was excluded as unnatural.

## Key Findings

**Data reliability**
- First-pass counts and seine-unit counts were highly correlated in all 16 station-season cases (mean R² = 0.915; slopes 1.021–1.174). The authors concluded they are interchangeable for this dataset.
- Seine-unit data had slightly lower variability (CV 217.1 vs. 235.3 overall), a difference of about 7.5%.
- Excluding suspicious records did not significantly change the ANOVA results (p = 0.819).

**Season, habitat and temperature**
- Season affected woundfin catch at three of four stations. Fall exceeded spring only at Hurricane Bridge (p = 0.006).
- Habitat differences were significant at Ash Creek, Hurricane Bridge and Twin Bridges but not Beaver Dam Wash. Restricting data to runs reduced CV by only about 2.5–5%.
- Discharge explained a modest share of variation (R² 0.038–0.267 in spring and summer). Water temperature explained essentially none.

**Woundfin trends**
- Abundance differed significantly among years at all stations in both spring and fall (ANOVA p ≤ 0.0001 in most cases).
- Ash Creek and Beaver Dam Wash showed highly significant declines (Beaver Dam Wash fall R² = 0.799, p < 0.0001; spring R² = 0.854).
- Hurricane Bridge showed no fall trend (p = 0.700) but a significant spring decline (R² = 0.438, p = 0.010).
- Twin Bridges showed no significant linear fall trend (p = 0.160), but spring declined (R² = 0.455, p = 0.012).
- Woundfin were locally extirpated from Beaver Dam Wash by fall 1988.

**Causal assessment**
- **Red shiner:** Beaver Dam Wash showed a significant negative woundfin–shiner relationship in fall (R² = 0.458, p < 0.0001) and spring (R² = 0.234, p < 0.0001). Shiner abundance rose sharply in fall 1985, and woundfin collapsed about a year later.
- **Drought:** Drought years had fewer woundfin at Hurricane Bridge (p < 0.001), but drought did not explain declines at Ash Creek, Twin Bridges or Beaver Dam Wash. Early-drought abundance at those stations was about 2.5 (log scale), a "High" level.
- **High peak flows:** Years with high peaks (1978, 1980, 1993) had more woundfin at Ash Creek, Twin Bridges and Beaver Dam Wash.
- **Quail Creek Reservoir:** Considered a likely cause of the Ash Creek decline, but not proven; no minimum flows apply to that reach.

**Other species**
- Shiners rose significantly after 1985 at Beaver Dam Wash (spring p = 0.0002; fall p = 0.0001) and at Twin Bridges (spring p = 0.0057).
- Dace showed no consistent post-1985 change, and dace abundance at Beaver Dam Wash and Twin Bridges was unaffected by red shiner.

**Conclusions**
- Database trends are real and not explained by random sampling variation, but attribution is incomplete.
- Woundfin decline is most strongly linked to red shiner invasion at Beaver Dam Wash, while Ash Creek and Twin Bridges remain unexplained.
- The authors stress that the analyses support hypotheses rather than definitive causes, and that the data are limited for species that prefer habitats outside sand runs.
