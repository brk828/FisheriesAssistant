# A New Species of Cycleptus (Cypriniformes: Catostomidae) from Gulf Slope Drainages, with a Review of Distribution, Biology, and Conservation Status

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

Burr and Mayden (1999), in the [[Bulletin of the Alabama Museum of Natural History]], describe a new sucker species, *Cycleptus meridionalis* (Southeastern Blue Sucker), and review the genus *Cycleptus*, an endemic North American catostomid. The authors examined over 390 museum specimens and found geographically concordant variation in dorsal-fin ray number, scale counts, body shape and lip morphology. The new species is restricted to Gulf Slope drainages from the [[Mobile Basin]], Alabama, to the [[Pearl River]], Louisiana, and is distinguished from [[Blue Sucker]] (*C. elongatus*) mainly by fewer fin rays and scales. Samples from the Rio Grande were divergent in some traits but were allocated to *C. elongatus* pending further study. The paper also reviews life history and conservation status, concluding that the genus is reproducing and recruiting at many sites, although it is listed as threatened or of special concern in several states, and recommends monitoring rather than formal protection.

## Background & Description

*Cycleptus* was described by [[Lesueur]] in 1817 as *Catostomus elongatus* from a dried specimen collected by Thomas Say in the Ohio River. Rafinesque's genus *Cycleptus* was later resurrected by Agassiz (1854), and the name has been stable since. A fossil-tooth species from Asia (*C. robustus*) was considered misidentified. The genus is regarded as the sister taxon of the Chinese sucker *Myxocyprinus*, a pattern shared with the [[Paddlefish]] and [[Giant Salamander]] lineages.

Diagnostic features of the genus include a large body (to about 825 mm SL), an elongate body, a small head, a long-based falcate dorsal fin with 25 to 34 rays, a large forked caudal fin, lips covered in papillae, and males that bear white breeding tubercles in spring. Chromosome number is 2n = 96-100, indicating tetraploidy.

Summary of the species comparison (Burr and Mayden 1999):

| Character | *C. meridionalis* | *C. elongatus* |
|---|---|---|
| Dorsal-fin rays (usual) | 25-29 | 28-34 |
| Caudal-peduncle scale rows | 16 | 19-20 |
| Body-circumferential scale rows | 37-40 | 41-49 |
| Lateral-line scales | 49-53 | 53-58 |
| Snout and dorsal fin base | Shorter | Longer |

Morphometric analysis of 82 *C. elongatus* and 28 *C. meridionalis* used sheared principal components analysis to remove size effects, with separate analyses for each sex. Lip papillae are short and blunt in *C. meridionalis*, whereas [[Rio Grande Blue Sucker]] populations show elongate, barbel-like papillae in adults.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Both species occupy strong currents in the main channels of medium to large rivers, over sand, cobble, gravel or bedrock. Juveniles use shallower riffles and gravel bars. The species increasingly uses human-made structures such as wing dykes, dam bases, reservoir deep zones and revetted banks. It tolerates turbidity where currents are swift enough to prevent silt deposition.

**Life history.** Both species are long-lived, with ages reported at 9-10 years in several studies, at least 12 years in others, and estimates of 30+ years based on opercle annuli. Adults reach large sizes, and spawning occurs in spring (late April to May in the upper Mississippi, February to March in Mississippi). Spawning involves massive upstream migrations to fast riffles. Adults feed mainly on aquatic insect larvae, with caddisflies, midges and mayflies prominent. Known predators are unclear, and a trematode, *Myzotrema cyclepti*, infects the gills.

**Threats.** Dams block migration, and fish are stranded below them (556 adults stranded below McAlpine Dam on the Ohio River, 1984-1985). Other threats include water-quality decline, excessive spawning-run catches, channel changes, and surface-water depletion in New Mexico.

**Range.** *C. elongatus* occupies the Mississippi, Missouri, Ohio, Arkansas, Red, Sabine and Rio Grande basins. *C. meridionalis* is known from the Mobile basin and the Pearl and Pascagoula rivers. A gap in vouchered records exists in central Texas.

## Key Findings

- **Two species recognized:** *C. meridionalis* is described as new; *C. elongatus* is retained for the Mississippi and Rio Grande systems and some Texas drainages.
- **Diagnostic counts:** Fin-ray, scale and snout differences are geographically concordant across specimens.
- **Rio Grande populations:** Divergent lip papillae, faint striping and golden body colour were judged insufficiently consistent, so these were allocated to *C. elongatus* with recommendations for genetic and tubercle studies. Preliminary allozyme work shows fixed differences in Rio Grande samples.
- **Abundance in the Rio Grande:** Recent electrofishing and seining near Big Bend National Park found *C. elongatus* to be the most abundant large fish, with larvae at every station.
- **Reproduction and recruitment:** Recruitment is documented at many sites across the range, including post-1993 flood spawning in the upper Mississippi.
- **Abundance of *C. meridionalis*:** 559 individuals were observed or captured in the Pearl and Pascagoula rivers (1996-1997), and 135 adults were captured below Millers Ferry Lock and Dam in 1995. Earlier reports of rarity are considered misleading.
- **Conservation status:** Listed as threatened in several states and jeopardized across much of its range, and a candidate for federal listing. The authors argue that the species is not jeopardized across all or a significant portion of its range and recommend monitoring spawning sites, population numbers and recruitment rather than formal protection.
- **Management needs:** Maintaining water quality, minimising dam construction and habitat modification, and securing perennial flows in the Pecos River are identified as priorities.
- **Biogeographic significance:** The disjunct Cycleptus + Myxocyprinus pattern parallels that of paddlefishes, giant salamanders and *Alligator*, suggesting an ancient pattern.
