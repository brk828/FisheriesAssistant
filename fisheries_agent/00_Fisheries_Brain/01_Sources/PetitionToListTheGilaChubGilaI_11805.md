# Gila Chub (Gila intermedia)

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #cyprinidae #endangered-species #gila-river-basin

## Executive Summary

This 1995 petition, submitted by the Southwest Center for Biological Diversity, requests federal listing of the **[[Gila Chub]]** (*Gila intermedia*) as an endangered species under the Endangered Species Act. The Gila chub is a cyprinid minnow endemic to the **[[Gila River Basin]]** that has been extirpated from New Mexico and survives at fewer than 10–15 locations in Arizona. Population declines since the 1950s stem from the combined effects of **habitat degradation/loss** (cienega destruction, grazing, water diversion, groundwater pumping, timber harvest) and **introduction of predatory/competitive exotic fishes** (e.g., largemouth bass, green sunfish, red shiner). The petition documents taxonomy, natural history, associated species, historical/current distribution, and recommends designation of critical habitat.

## Background & Description

### Taxonomic History
- Originally described by **Baird and Girard (1854)** as *Gila gibbosa* from a Santa Cruz River specimen; renamed *Tigoma intermedia* in 1856. The name *gibbosa* is invalid due to homonymy.
- Currently classified by the American Fisheries Society as a subspecies of **[[Gila Robusta]]**, but most authors (notably **Rinne 1976** and **DeMarais 1986**) support full species status based on:
  - Morphological differences (scale counts, fin ray counts, caudal peduncle ratios)
  - Maintained reproductive independence in contiguous populations with *G. robusta* over evolutionary time
  - Presence of unresolved phenotypic intermediates complicating clean separation
- No current zones of sympatry with *G. robusta* are known, though older records suggest local sympatry in complex habitats.

### Physical Description
- Dark steel-grey, chunky-bodied fish; occasionally lighter belly, diffuse lateral bands, **no basicaudal spot**.
- Breeding males: bright red/orange coloring on lower cheek, posterior lips, paired fin bases, and ventro-lateral surfaces including caudal peduncle.
- **Size**: Mature adults reach 8–10 in (20–25 cm); average 5–8 in. Adult females up to 25 cm.
- **Meristics**:
  - Dorsal fin rays: ≤8 (rarely 9)
  - Anal fin rays: ≤8
  - Pelvic fin rays: 8–9
  - Total vertebrae: 38–45 (usually <42)
  - Lateral line scales: fewer than 80 (key distinguishing trait vs. *G. robusta*)
  - Head-length/caudal-peduncle-depth ratio: ≤3.0
  - Pharyngeal teeth formula: 2, 5-4, 2 (similar to *G. robusta*)
- Large, thick, broadly imbricated scales, usually with basal radii.
- Large females may develop an abrupt, fatty nuchal hump.

### Phylogeny
- Genus *Gila* traceable from **Mid-Miocene** to present; relatives of *G. robusta* appear in late Pliocene records.
- **Rinne (1976)** hypothesizes *G. intermedia* precursors dispersed into north-flowing tributaries of the pre-Gila River Basin during the Pliocene/Pleistocene, likely originating from southern relatives:
  - *G. ditaenia* (Rio de la Concepcion, Arizona/Sonora)
  - *G. nigrescens* (Guzman Basin, NM/Chihuahua)
  - *G. pandora* (Rio Grande/Pecos basins)
- Populations likely derived from stream captures by north-flowing Gila River tributaries.

### Ontogeny & Life History
- Breeding season: late spring through summer (**[[Minckley 1983]]**), sometimes into autumn.
- Spawning behavior poorly documented; likely occurs on submerged aquatic vegetation. Large females attended by multiple smaller males.
- Maturity reached in second or third year.
- **Griffith and Tiersch (1989)** data (n=113 fish, Redfield Canyon):
  - Size range: 45–222 mm total length (TL)
  - Age-length estimates: Age 1 = 90 mm, Age 2 = 135 mm, Age 3 = 160 mm, Age 4 = 183 mm
  - Evidence of 4 age classes suggests lifespan of 3–4 years.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Requirements
- **[[Gila Chub]]** is highly secretive, favoring deep waters of small creeks, **[[Cienegas]]**, and some impoundments.
- Habitat use shifts by life stage:
  - Juveniles: shallowest waters, among plants/woody cover
  - Sub-adults: swifter currents (riffles), active throughout day
  - Adults: deep pools with undercut banks, overhanging vegetation/woody debris; feed morning and evening
- **Diet**: Omnivorous — small fish, aquatic/terrestrial invertebrates, filamentous algae.

### Cienega Ecosystem
- Found in the **Basin and Range Geomorphic Province** (San Simon, Sulphur Springs, San Pedro, Santa Cruz valleys).
- Formed where mountain-front faults release groundwater as springs; alluvial deposits provide aquifer storage.
- Characterized by warm, stable flows; rarely subject to winter harshness or scouring floods.
- Depositional zones with meters of organic substrate; pools have vertical organic-sediment walls with undercut banks.
- Characteristic vegetation: sedges (Cyperaceae), rushes (Juncaceae), grasses (Gramineae), water purslane (*Ludwigia natans*); aquatic species include watercress (*Rorippa nasturtium-aquaticum*) and water pennywort (*Hydrocotyle verticillata*).

## Key Findings

### Distribution — Historical
- Endemic to the **[[Gila River Basin]]** (Mexico, New Mexico, Arizona); once widespread in southeastern Arizona.
- Northern range limit: upper Verde River below the Mogollon Rim (Big Chino Wash, Oak Creek).
- Historically common in Santa Cruz River system, upper San Simon River tributaries (San Simon Cienega, Hidalgo Co.), Salt and Gila Rivers.
- Historic range also included Agua Fria, San Pedro, and Verde River basins.
- Documented in Tularosa River (Catron Co.) and Duck Creek (Grant Co.), New Mexico.

### Distribution — Current (as of petition, 1995)
- **Extirpated** from New Mexico entirely.
- Extirpated from Fish Creek, Cave Creek, Babocomari River, and Monkey Springs (Arizona).
- Limited to **fewer than 15 streams** in the Gila River basin (central/southeastern Arizona); abundant in **no more than 10** locations.
- Documented recent populations include: Silver Creek, Cienega Creek, Bonita Creek, Rye Creek, Rio San Pedro, Redfield Creek, Bass Creek, Hot Springs Creek, O'Donnell Creek, Blue River, Sheehy Spring.
- Multiple historically-occupied sites surveyed with **zero Gila chub found**, including Indian Creek, Lousy Canyon, Larry Creek, Ash Creek, Little Ash Creek, Dry Creek, Brady Canyon, and numerous Tonto Creek tributaries.

### Threats to the Species

**1. Habitat Degradation and Loss** (identified as leading cause since the 1960s)
- **Grazing**: Cattle introduced by Spanish missionaries (17th–18th century); intensified under Anglo-American settlement post-Civil War. Major mortality overgrazing event in Arizona, 1893. Effects include:
  - Soil compaction via cattle trails
  - Destruction of riparian vegetation (destabilizing soils, reducing water retention)
  - Upland vegetation loss → increased runoff and "flashy" flooding
  - Result: siltation/filling of pools, destruction of spawning substrate, loss of undercut bank cover
- **Water diversion & groundwater pumping**: Direct water removal from habitat; fish stranded in irrigation ditches; dams causing genetic isolation between populations; water table decline causing arroyo cutting (e.g., Santa Cruz River desiccation).
- **Timber harvest**: Increased soil erosion, sedimentation, flood intensity, and water table lowering.
- **Additional factors**: Mining, reservoir creation, drainage channelization, off-road vehicle use, historic beaver trapping (reducing low-velocity/high-cover habitat since 1830s), over-fishing.

**2. Predatory and Competitive Exotic Species** (co-identified by TNC as principal cause of decline)
- Documented population collapses:
  - Monkey Spring, NM population decimated by **largemouth bass** predation.
  - San Carlos River decline correlated with increasing **green sunfish** abundance.
- Lower Bonita Creek population (below "the Narrows") shows poor body condition, ectoparasite infestation, and disease — linked to exotic fish presence and habitat alteration; upstream population (same creek) remains healthy despite presence of some same exotic species.
- TNC identifies key competitive/predatory exotics: *Cyprinella lutrensis*, *Ictalurus punctatus*, *Pylodictis olivaris*, *Gambusia affinis*, *Lepomis cyanellus*, *Micropterus salmoides*, *M. dolomieui*.

### Regulatory Status (at time of petition)
- Arizona: listed as **threatened**.
- New Mexico: listed as **endangered, Group 3** (but species already extirpated there).
- No formal habitat protections or management/restoration plans existed in either state.
- BLM/Arizona Game and Fish proposed "seed fish" translocation from Silver Creek to Agua Fria basin streams — petitioners questioned wisdom of this without prior habitat restoration or hatchery program.

### Expert Opinions
- **NMDGF (1988)**: Confirms range-wide decline; cites spring habitat modification, arroyo cutting/dewatering, and predation by introduced bass/catfish as major threats, especially in habitat-limited areas.
- **Minckley (1985)**: Recommends direct habitat acquisition/easements on cienegas/spring-fed streams; notes extirpations following introduction of largemouth bass, bluegill, and goldfish; supports "threatened" listing.
- **USFWS Desert Fishes Recovery Team (Feb 1992)**: Recommended listing Gila chub as threatened or endangered based on collective expert knowledge.
- **TNC**: Warned continued Arizona presence "could be in jeopardy in the near future."

### Petition Recommendation
- Formal recommendation for **federal endangered species listing**.
- **Critical habitat designation** recommended for all currently occupied sites plus key unoccupied areas suitable for restoration-based recovery.
