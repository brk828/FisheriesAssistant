# An Aquacultural Evaluation of Florida, Northern, and Hybrid Largemouth Bass (*Micropterus salmoides*)

## Executive Summary
This study (Williamson & Carmichael, 1988) presents a comprehensive aquacultural strain comparison of two largemouth bass subspecies — the [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides salmoides*, N) and the [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides floridanus*, F) — along with their reciprocal F1 hybrids (NXF and FXN), conducted at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's San Marcos National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center. Using electrophoretically verified broodstocks, the researchers evaluated food trainability, growth, survival, feed efficiency, stress response to net confinement, cold tolerance, and low dissolved oxygen (DO) tolerance under intensive culture conditions. The Northern strain consistently outperformed the Florida strain and hybrids across nearly all aquacultural performance metrics, with **no evidence of heterosis** detected in any trait examined. These findings support selection of the Northern subspecies as the preferred candidate strain for intensive largemouth bass aquaculture as a food fish.

## Background & Description
- **Species/Taxon**: [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), family [[Centrarchidae]].
- **Strains studied**:
  - **Northern (N)**: *M. s. salmoides*
  - **Florida (F)**: *M. s. floridanus*
  - **Hybrids**: Reciprocal F1 crosses, NXF (Northern female × Florida male) and FXN (Florida female × Northern male)
- **Taxonomic reference**: Subspecies designations follow [[Bailey And Hubbs 1949]].
- **Genetic verification**: All broodstock and experimental fish were identified via electrophoretic analysis of fin tissue to confirm subspecific identity (Carmichael et al. 1986; Williamson et al. 1986), addressing prior concerns about misidentification in strain-based breeding studies (Harvey et al. 1980; Philipp et al. 1983; Williamson 1983).
- **Origin of broodstock**: Wild fish of both subspecies were collected from natural populations in 1980, spawned in 1981 to generate broodstock, and re-spawned in 1984 to generate the four experimental crosses (N, F, NXF, FXN) used in trials.
- **Context**: Largemouth bass is a major North American sportfish with a growing interest in production as a food fish, particularly in Asian and West Coast North American markets, motivating this strain-performance research for [[Intensive Aquaculture]] applications.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Rearing system**: Fish were reared in 0.04-hectare earthen ponds and fiberglass tanks supplied with well water, reflecting controlled hatchery/aquaculture conditions rather than natural habitat.
- **Stocking strategies**: Two pond experimental designs were used — **separate** (single-strain ponds) and **communal** (mixed-strain ponds), following the communal testing method of [[Wohlfarth And Moav 1985]] to control for pond-to-pond environmental variance.
- **Water quality stressors tested**: Low dissolved oxygen, low water temperature, ammonia, MS-222 (anesthetic), and osmoregulatory salts — simulating real-world pond water quality perturbations encountered in intensive aquaculture.
- **Environmental tolerance implications**: Because Florida bass are native to warmer subtropical waters and Northern bass to more temperate/cooler regions, their differential tolerance to cold and hypoxia reflects underlying biogeographic adaptation between subspecies.

## Key Findings

### Food Training (19-day trial)
- Training success ranked: **N (96%) > NXF ≈ FXN (89–92%) > F (80%)** (chi-square, P < 0.01).
- No evidence of [[Heterosis]] in food trainability.

### Growth — Separate Ponds (111 days)
- Mean harvest weight ranking: **N (117.3 g) > NXF (90.9 g) > F (78.3 g) ≈ FXN (74.5 g)**.
- N strain significantly larger than all others; no heterosis observed.

### Growth — Communal Ponds (127 days)
- Mean harvest weight ranking: **N (147.9 g) > NXF (108.5 g) > FXN (90.0 g) ≈ F (84.9 g)**.
- Significant strain × pond interaction detected, but strain effect dominated growth variation.
- No heterosis observed even under shared-environment (communal) testing.

### Survival — Separate Ponds
- Total survival ranged 90–98%; ranking: **F (97.6%) ≈ FXN (97.1%) > NXF > N**.
- F strain was the only strain without significant among-pond variability.
- No heterosis for survival.

### Feed Efficiency — Separate Ponds
- N strain: 80.3% feed efficiency (feed conversion 1.24), significantly better than the other three strains (57–64% efficiency; conversions 1.55–1.75).
- Ranking: **N > NXF ≈ FXN ≈ F**.
- Communal pond feed efficiencies ranged 70.6–74.5% (not broken out by strain).

### Stress Response — Net Confinement (12–24 h)
- **F strain fish consistently died first** and had highest mortality in all trials (reared separately or communally); F fish required reduced confinement duration/severity due to loss of equilibrium.
- N strain showed the least severe stress response (lowest mortality).
- No heterosis observed for stress tolerance.

### Low Temperature Tolerance
- Fish slowly cooled to ~2°C and held for 5 days: **F strain mortality = 48.3%**, FXN = 3.0%, **N and NXF = 0% mortality**.
- All strains survived rapid cooling to 1°C followed by rapid rewarming to 21°C within 90 minutes — indicating brief cold shock is tolerated by all strains, but **sustained cold exposure is poorly tolerated by Florida-strain fish**.

### Low Dissolved Oxygen (DO) Tolerance
- Residual DO at death (indicator of hypoxia tolerance) was strain- and temperature-dependent:
  - At 22°C: **N (0.56 mg/l) and F (0.62 mg/l) tolerated lower DO** (died at lower residual DO) than hybrids FXN (0.71 mg/l) and NXF (0.82 mg/l).
  - At 14°C: both N and F showed higher residual DO at death compared to 22°C, indicating reduced hypoxia tolerance at cooler temperatures for the parental strains.
- **Metabolic rates** were significantly lower at 14°C than 22°C across all strains; NXF had the highest mass-specific metabolic rate at both temperatures. When corrected for body size (standardized to 10-g fish), strain differences in metabolic rate disappeared.

### Chemical Stressor Effects (Ammonia, MS-222, Salts)
- **Ammonia (0.70 mg/l un-ionized)**: Residual DO at death was highest in F (1.95 mg/l), intermediate in hybrids, and lowest in N (1.11 mg/l) — indicating **N fish tolerated combined ammonia + hypoxia stress better** than F fish.
- **MS-222 and salts**: No significant strain differences in residual DO at death.
- **Metabolic rate effects**: Ammonia had no detectable effect on metabolic rate; salts reduced metabolic rate similarly across strains (~48–52% reduction); MS-222 reduced metabolic rate most strongly in N fish (36% reduction) vs. 21–29% in other strains.

## Overall Conclusions
- The **Northern largemouth bass strain outperformed Florida and hybrid strains** across virtually all aquacultural performance traits: faster growth, better feed conversion, higher food-training success, greater resistance to net-confinement stress, better cold tolerance, and better tolerance of low DO combined with ammonia stress.
- **No heterosis** was detected in hybrids for any trait measured — hybrid performance was generally intermediate between parental strains rather than exceeding the better-performing parent.
- Findings support selection of the [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] strain as the preferred candidate for **intensive food-fish aquaculture**, given its superior handling tolerance, growth rate, and water-quality resilience compared to [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] and their [[F1 Hybrid]] crosses.
- Growth to ~150 g within 127 days demonstrates that intensive largemouth bass culture is feasible and comparable to other established aquaculture species.
