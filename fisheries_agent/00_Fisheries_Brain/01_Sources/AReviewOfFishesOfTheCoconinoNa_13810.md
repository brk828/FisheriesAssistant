# Review of Fishes of the Coconino National Forest Region, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report by [[W. L. Minckley]] (Arizona State University) reviews the native and introduced fishes of the [[Coconino National Forest]] (CNF) region of Arizona. It was prepared as a final report to the CNF in April 1993. It compiles voucher specimen records from the [[University Of Michigan Museum Of Zoology]] and Arizona State University, stocking records from the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]] (AZGF), and published and unpublished literature.

Fifteen native fish taxa are recorded from within or adjacent to the forest. Thirteen persist as natural or reintroduced stocks, and nine are federally listed or candidates for listing. At least 36 non-native species have been introduced, and 19 appear self-sustaining. The overall pattern is decline of native species and expansion of introduced ones, consistent with the rest of the American West.

## Background & Description

The CNF lies across two major external watersheds and a small internal closed basin. The northern portion drains to the [[Little Colorado River]] and then the Colorado River within Grand Canyon. The southern portion drains via the [[Verde River]] to the Salt-Gila system and the Colorado River near Yuma. Precipitation is high at elevation, but fragmented, porous bedrock absorbs surface runoff, which re-emerges as springs and seeps. Many fish habitats are therefore spring-fed or intermittent.

The report defines its scope as voucher-supported records plus lesser reliance on published sources. It notes several shortfalls:

- The Tulane University collection was not included because it was being moved and computerized.
- AZGF Native Fishes and Heritage databases were not used. Data were unconfirmed, input lagged, and some information on protected species was sensitive.

Taxonomy is a recurring theme. Native trouts were moved from *Salmo* to *Oncorhynchus* following [[Smith And Stearley 1989]]. The chubs of the genus [[Gila Genus]] represent a complex whose taxonomy is unresolved, and the report keys out morphological forms in Appendix III.

The report also includes a 1991 letter from the CNF requesting a historical review of fish records for five drainages: West Clear, Wet Beaver, East Clear, Oak, and Sycamore creeks. It asks for a cost and timeline estimate. The reply estimates about $400 for travel to the Tulane collection, plus 40 to 60 hours of student wages ($240 to $360). It also notes overhead of 5.0% on purchase orders and about 1.0% for workers' compensation on student wages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native species decline.** Most native fishes have lost range and abundance. The report does not settle the cause of decline. It contrasts two hypotheses:

- The **replacement** hypothesis holds that habitat change is the primary cause, with non-natives filling altered habitats.
- The **displacement** hypothesis holds that direct negative interactions with invading non-natives reduce native taxa.

Speckled dace are still widespread in highland habitats not yet invaded by minnows, which the report sees as relevant to this debate. Sampling is too sparse to resolve it.

**Non-native impacts.** Several introduced species are implicated in native declines:

- [[Red Shiner]] are aggressive, warm-water tolerant competitors and are linked to declines of spikedace and longfin dace.
- [[Mosquitofish]] prey on larval stages of small natives.
- [[Flathead Catfish]] are major piscivores that deter razorback sucker and Colorado squawfish reintroductions.
- [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Brown Trout]] are predators that appear linked to native declines where they co-occur.

**Habitat requirements.** Several natives depend on specific conditions. The loach minnow needs clean gravel/cobble riffles, so siltation from grazing and timbering is a likely driver of its loss. The longfin dace persists in sand-bottomed runs and pools, but its populations are fragmented by diversions that prevent recolonization after drought.

**Sampling bias.** The discussion stresses that museum and survey data are poor quantitative measures. Gear selectivity, habitat coverage, and collector preference all bias results. Museum collections over-represent rare species, so abundance inferences from them are unreliable. Absence from a collection is not proof of absence. Common carp, for example, appear in only seven cataloged samples despite being widespread.

## Key Findings

**Native taxa**

- **[[Gila Trout]]** (*Oncorhynchus gilae*): The original Verde River populations were extirpated through hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]] and other introduced trouts by the 1890s. In 1974, 65 wild-caught juveniles from Main Diamond Creek, New Mexico, were released into Gap Creek. The population was self-sustaining through 1987 but seemed near failure when last sampled in 1990.
- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*): Once common in the Verde mainstem, it was formerly collected at hundreds per hour, where tens are now taken. It has suffered significant reductions in range and abundance.
- **[[Gila Chub]]** (*G. intermedia*), **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*G. robusta*), and the "problematic form" (*Gila* sp.): The three taxa remain locally common to rare. The report supports Category 2 candidate status for all three, and notes that *G. robusta* was rediscovered in Chevelon and East Clear creeks in 1991 after being thought extinct in the Little Colorado basin. The bonytail (*G. elegans*) is not recorded from the CNF but was formerly present nearby.
- **[[Little Colorado Spinedace]]** (*Lepidomeda vittata*): Its range contracted sharply after 1983. The mainstem of East Clear Creek lost it, and upper Chevelon and Silver creeks no longer hold it. White Mountain and lower Chevelon populations persist. Between 1963 and 1966, it was found at 17 of 21 localities in the East Clear Creek drainage. A draft recovery plan was scheduled for 1993.
- **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*): Listed as threatened in 1988. In a 1938 UMMZ collection from the Verde mainstem it made up 48% of 587 fishes. In 1950 it was 51 of 638 fishes (8.0%). It was absent in later years, which the report treats as likely local extirpation. It was not caught in the 1960s anywhere in the Verde system, but persisted at Perkinsville and lower Sycamore Creek into the 1980s.
- **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*): Listed as threatened in 1986. It disappeared early from the Verde system, with the last specimens from the late 1930s. The author rejects its synonymy with *Rhinichthys*.
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): Known from archaeological remains near Perkinsville, and re-introduced since 1985 downstream of Perkinsville.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): Listed as endangered. It is being reintroduced through a 10+ year AZGF-USFWS recovery program.
- **[[Sonora Sucker]]** (*Catostomus insignis*): Persists in deep pools near gravelly riffles. It is secretive by day, so collecting at night would sample it better.
- **Desert and bluehead suckers** (*Pantosteus clarki*, *P. discobolus*): Desert suckers remain relatively abundant, though large adults have declined in the mainstem Verde. Bluehead suckers are comparably abundant where natural waters persist.
- **Speckled dace** (*Rhinichthys osculus*): Still among the most widespread species, but less common in larger downstream habitats than 30 to 50 years ago.

**Non-native taxa**

- 19 non-native species appear self-sustaining, and at least 17 more persist only through sporadic stocking or are missed in surveys.
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) are widespread in larger waters, though only seven cataloged samples include them.
- The [[Grass Carp]] (*Ctenopharyngodon idellus*) is authorized for vegetation control. AZGF has issued at least 300 permits, and triploidy is not a guarantee of sterility.
- About 25,000 [[Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy*) were planted in Mormon Lake in 1973. None has been recorded since.
- Several cold-intolerant species, including [[Threadfin Shad]] (tolerance below about 12°C), tilapia, and [[Striped Bass]], are unlikely to persist.

**Recommendations**

- The author strongly supports Category 2 candidate status for all three *Gila* taxa.
- Museum and agency data should be checked, computerized, and made available before being used in management decisions.
- Field notes and photographs held by museums should be consulted, since they may contain information on discarded specimens and sampling conditions.
