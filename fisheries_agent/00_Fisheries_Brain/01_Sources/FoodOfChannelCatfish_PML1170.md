# Food of channel catfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictalurus

## Executive Summary

This study by Jearld and Brown (1971) describes the diet of [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) in [[Lake Carl Blackwell]], a turbid reservoir in Oklahoma. The authors examined stomachs from 196 fish collected between October 1967 and August 1969, 128 of which contained food. Catfish under about 300 mm total length fed mainly on invertebrates, notably [[Chironomid Larvae]] and [[Mayfly Nymphs]]. Larger fish became piscivorous, and fish made up 87% of total food volume across the sample. The work supports the view that the species is basically omnivorous, with a shift toward fish as body size increases.

## Background & Description

Assigning fish species to feeding categories is needed to describe predator-prey relationships in population dynamics, especially when applying [[Swingle Ratios]] (Swingle 1950; Swingle and Swingle 1967). Previous food studies of channel catfish, including work on the Des Moines River (Bailey and Harrison 1948), the Missouri River (Russell 1965), and Tenkiller and Fort Gibson Reservoirs (Clemens 1954), characterized the species as omnivorous.

The study area, [[Lake Carl Blackwell]], lies 9 miles NW of Stillwater, Oklahoma. The earth and rockfill dam was completed in 1937, and the lake reached spillway level in 1945. At spillway level it holds approximately 80 million m³ (65,000 acre-ft) of water over a maximum surface area of 3,700 acres. The maximum depth is 10 m, but most of the lake is less than 6 m deep. The drainage area is about 14 times the reservoir surface area, and the shoreline is roughly 100 miles long. The shallow basin and low surrounding terrain allow prevailing winds to keep the water nearly continuously circulated, producing turbid water and nearly uniform physical and chemical conditions from top to bottom in most seasons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Fish were caught mainly by [[Gill Netting]], with some taken in wire and barrel traps. Total length was recorded, and stomachs were preserved in 10% formalin. Volumes were measured in graduated cylinders (large stomachs) or graduated centrifuge tubes (small stomachs). Item counts, per cent volume, and per cent occurrence were calculated from the pooled contents, following standard [[Stomach Content Analysis]] procedures.

**Prey categories.** The authors grouped the food into invertebrates (including [[Crayfish]], isopods, and mollusks), insects, fish, vegetation, detritus, and one terrestrial vertebrate. "Detritus" included sand, mud, zooplankton, phytoplankton, and unidentifiable partly digested material.

**Size-related diet shift.** Diet changed with predator size. Length classes below about 300 mm were numerically dominated by insects. In the 29-32 cm class, fish comprised a larger share of food volume than insects, and this class marked the transition toward piscivory. Fish remains were found in all seasons and all size groups, but whole fish occurred mostly in the larger classes, while smaller classes contained mainly scales and bone fragments.

**Comparison with other systems.** The size at which catfish became predominantly piscivorous differed among waters. Russell (1965) reported this transition in the [[Missouri River]] (Ponca section, Nebraska), and Bailey and Harrison (1948) reported it in the [[Des Moines River]]. Busbee (1968) found piscivory began at about 380 mm in a Florida lake and fish hatchery.

## Key Findings

**Sample and occurrence**
- 196 stomachs examined; 128 contained food.
- Fish occurred in 44% of stomachs with food, detritus in 32%, and insects in 20%.
- By per cent frequency of organisms, insects were most common at 79.1%, but fish contributed a larger share of total volume.

**Invertebrate prey**
- [[Chironomid Larvae]] were the most numerous prey and occurred in 17 stomachs, with a mean of 35 per stomach where present. They made up 66.7% of organisms by frequency.
- [[Mayfly Nymphs]] (Ephemeroptera) were the other principal insect prey, found in 14 stomachs.
- Other insects were less common: Coleoptera (in 3.7% of stomachs), unidentified insects (5.9%), Odonata, Culicidae, and terrestrial insects.
- Crayfish were the most frequent crustacean, occurring in 10 stomachs and making up 2.3% of total food volume. One fish contained 17 undigested crayfish.

**Fish prey (87.0% of total volume)**
- [[Gizzard Shad]] (*Dorosoma cepedianum*) comprised 33.6% of total food volume and appeared in 23 stomachs. The largest shad was 154 mm, found in a 489 mm catfish that held seven shad.
- [[White Crappie]] (*Pomoxis annularis*) made up 17.5% of volume.
- [[White Bass]] (*Roccus chrysops*) was identified from a single specimen (1.3% of volume).
- Unidentified centrarchids accounted for 22.5% of fish volume, and unidentifiable fish remains for 12.1%.

**Terrestrial vertebrate**
- One [[Woodrat]] (*Neotoma floridana*), with a volume of 170 ml, made up 5.7% of total food volume. Heard (1959) had earlier reported cotton rats in 33% of 21 catfish from the same lake.

**Size relationships**
- Catfish under 300 mm fed primarily on invertebrates.
- Fish dominated stomach contents as length exceeded 300 mm.
- The sharp drop in fish contribution in the 65-74 cm class was attributed to one catfish that contained a woodrat and numerous mayfly nymphs.

**Conclusions**
- Channel catfish in this reservoir are omnivorous as juveniles and piscivorous as adults, with the change occurring at roughly 300 mm total length.
- Turbid, well-mixed conditions in [[Lake Carl Blackwell]] may shape the invertebrate and fish prey available to the population, though the authors did not test this directly.
