# Nonnative Fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin: An Issue Paper

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #ictaluridae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This draft issue paper, prepared by [[John A Hawkins]] (Larval Fish Laboratory, Colorado State University) and [[Thomas P. Nesler]] (Colorado Division of Wildlife) for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] Biology Committee (August 1991), provides guidance on the impacts of nonnative fishes on the four endangered fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. It combines a literature review of more than 100 papers and reports with a questionnaire completed by 26 biologists and managers (from 40 contacted).

Nonnative fish numbers have risen in upper-basin rivers and their abundance is linked to habitat alteration and flow events. Predation is the most documented interaction, mainly where natives were reared in large numbers, held in confined areas, or stocked. Food competition and spatial competition are suggested but rarely quantified against food availability. Disease introduction and hybridization were not documented as problems, and habitat alteration by introduced fishes had not been reported in the upper basin. Respondents identified six species as most problematic: [[Channel Catfish]], [[Red Shiner]], [[Northern Pike]], [[Common Carp]], [[Green Sunfish]] and [[Fathead Minnow]]. Most respondents viewed flow-related habitat change as at least as important as nonnative fishes. Habitat manipulation combined with flow management was the most cited feasible option.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River Basin's native fish fauna was described as depauperate by early ichthyologists. Of 52 listed fishes in the upper basin (Table 1, from [[Tyus et al. 1982]]), the document notes that a large share (about 42%) are nonnative to the basin, compared with fewer than 10% in the eastern United States. Native taxa named include [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*).

Introduced species were stocked for sport and forage, and some arrived through escape from aquaria, bait buckets and aquaculture. The authors define the impact of an introduced species as any measurable change in the native community attributable to the exotic, and note that demonstrating causation is difficult because habitat alteration often masks direct effects.

The study was funded by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service under Section 6 of the Endangered Species Act. The questionnaire was a modified [[Delphi Technique]] sent in April 1990 to 40 people, with a reminder in September 1990.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors adopted the [[Taylor Impact Framework]] (Taylor et al. 1984), which groups effects into:

- **Trophic alterations:** predation, food competition, and forage supplementation.
- **Spatial alterations:** competition for habitat, spawning space, or feeding stations.
- **Habitat alteration:** vegetation removal and water quality degradation.
- **Other effects:** hybridization, disease or parasite introduction, combined effects, no effect, positive effects, and incidental take of endangered species by anglers.

Flow regime was repeatedly identified as a key control. Documented examples include:

- Abundance of [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Sand Shiner]] and [[Red Shiner]] decreased with higher peak flow in the 15-mile reach of the Colorado River (Osmundson and Kaeding 1991).
- In Arizona, [[Mosquitofish]] suffered greater losses than native [[Sonoran Topminnow]] during floods, and was often eliminated within months where the two were sympatric (Meffe 1984).
- Floods shifted nonnatives from narrow canyon reaches to floodplain reaches where they persisted (Minckley and Meffe 1987).
- Lower spring flows favored nonnatives in Utah's White River (Lanigan and Berry).

Lower-basin and other drainage studies, including the Pit River and the Virgin River, were used for context.

## Key Findings

**Literature review**

- **Predation:** Piscivorous nonnatives were among the most cited negative effects. Documented cases include channel catfish preying on juvenile Colorado squawfish and humpback chub (Coon 1965; Kaeding and Zimmerman 1983; Karp and Tyus 1990b), and [[Osmundson 1987]] confirming predation by largemouth bass, green sunfish, black crappie and black bullhead on stocked squawfish in riverside ponds. In [[Lake Mohave]], [[Marsh and Langhorst 1988]] attributed loss of young razorback sucker to predation; green sunfish collected over 24 hours contained razorback sucker in nearly 40% of samples.
- **Predation negatives:** Many predation studies found no evidence. Stomach analysis of 123 northern pike and 61 walleye found no squawfish (Tyus and Beard 1990); none of 321 striped bass contained endangered fishes (Persons and Bulkley 1982). Authors caution that inability to detect predation does not mean it is absent, since prey may be identifiable for only about 4 hours after ingestion.
- **Competition for food:** Dietary overlap between young squawfish and nonnatives was documented (Jacobi and Jacobi 1981; McAda and Tyus 1984; Grabowski and Hiebert 1989), but no study measured food availability concurrently, so competition was not confirmed.
- **Spatial competition:** Microhabitat overlap was reported for young squawfish, red shiner and fathead minnow, and in aquaria green sunfish and fathead minnow showed territorial aggression (Karp and Tyus 1990a).
- **Forage and anti-predator defences:** Red shiner was the main food item of young squawfish. Channel catfish lodged in squawfish throats in rare cases (Pimentel et al. 1985), though they were not a preferred prey.
- **Disease, hybridization, habitat:** Flagg (1981) noted differing pathogen groups in exotics. Osmundson (1987) reported the first Asian tapeworm record in the upper basin, probably introduced with hatchery-reared squawfish. Hybridization with the four endangered fishes was not documented. No habitat alteration by exotics was reported in the upper basin.
- **Incidental take:** Up to 70% of tagged squawfish in the Green River have been caught by anglers (Tyus and Karp 1989).

**Questionnaire results (n = 26 respondents)**

- 81% said impacts from introduced fishes were a problem, usually qualified by "needs proof."
- 77% identified nonnative species as presenting problems; six species were the most cited: channel catfish, red shiner, northern pike, common carp, green sunfish and fathead minnow (Table 3).
- 62% said predation was documented (answered yes by 60%); 50% said competition was documented; 40% said a beneficial effect was documented.
- Northern pike was judged to be increasing in abundance and impact; most other species were constant or inversely related to flow.
- Only about one-third of respondents viewed angling mortality as a significant problem, mostly federal staff. Colorado squawfish, a late-maturing species, was considered vulnerable.
- Recommended management options included reducing or regulating stocking (cited by almost half), habitat management combined with flow manipulation (most cited feasible option), and sport fishery regulation and angler education.
- Eradication was viewed as feasible only in small headwater systems, such as the greenback cutthroat trout and Gila trout restorations.
- Most respondents rated habitat change and flow alteration as equal to or greater than nonnatives as threats. Seven noted cumulative or synergistic effects.

**Research recommendations (selected)**

- Rate nonnative-native interactions as high priority and target the most abundant or potentially harmful species.
- Confirm suspected interactions in laboratory or controlled field settings.
- Measure the effects of habitat enhancement on natives and exotics, and the effects of predation on stocked endangered fish.
- Avoid enhancing sport fisheries for nonnative species until negative interactions are confirmed.

**Management recommendations (selected)**

- Restrict baitfish and nonnative stocking to off-channel water bodies isolated from the river.
- Monitor angler harvest in high-use reaches; prioritize public education on catch and release.
- Consider high spring flows and flow manipulation to favor natives.
- Stock endangered fish at 200-250 mm (squawfish) or over 300 mm (razorback sucker) to reduce predation.
- Improve communication between game and nongame management sections.

**Implementation strategy**

The authors state that field management should proceed in parallel with research. Some actions, such as baitfish restrictions, angler education and flow studies, can begin now, while reintroduction and control of nonnatives await research results.
