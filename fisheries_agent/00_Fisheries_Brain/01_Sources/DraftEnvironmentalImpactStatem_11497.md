# Draft Environmental Impact Statement: Proposed Mt. Graham Astrophysical Area

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Forest Service Draft Environmental Impact Statement (DEIS, October 1986) evaluates land-allocation options for a 3,500-acre high-elevation area at the summit of [[Mt. Graham]] in the [[Pinaleno Mountains]], Coronado National Forest. [[Steward Observatory]] (University of Arizona) proposed astronomical telescope development on the site. The Forest Service Preferred Alternative (PA) and six other alternatives (A through F) are compared. Alternatives A, B and C allow no astrophysical development; D, E, F and PA allow 7 to 60 acres of exclusive-use development.

The central ecological finding is that development increases extinction risk for the [[Mt. Graham Red Squirrel]], a small, isolated population in old-growth spruce-fir forest. The estimated 30-year extinction risk ranges from 20 percent under non-development alternatives to 35 percent under PA and 45 to 50 percent under D, E and F. Impacts also extend to [[Apache Trout]] habitat, [[Black Bear]] habitat, [[Cienega]] wetlands and the area's [[Endemic Species]].

## Background & Description

The Pinalenos are the highest mountains south of the [[Mogollon Rim]], rising to 10,720 ft at Mt. Graham. They are part of the Basin and Range province and are the only mountain range in southeastern Arizona with a paved road above 7,000 ft, apart from [[Mt. Lemmon]]. Testing for astronomical site quality began in 1981 under the [[Smithsonian Institution]] and the University of Arizona, and a 1984 Steward Observatory proposal triggered the separate EIS process.

Steward Observatory proposes 13 telescopes: 7 large (over 7.5 m) and 5 small (under 4.0 m), plus an interferometer. The 30-year build-out is divided into three phases (1986–1990, 1990–1996, 1996–2016). Phase 1 has assured funding; Phases 2 and 3 do not. The Texas 5-meter telescope already operates, the 10-meter submillimeter telescope (SMT) is under construction in Germany, and the VATT 1.8 m primary mirror has been cast. The [[National New Technology Telescope]] (NNTT) is a major component of the later phases.

The planning environment is described as having:
- Water quality rated high, with fecal coliform levels within standards except for direct domestic use.
- Forest Service management prescriptions and [[Executive Order 11990]] wetland protections for cienegas.
- Significant [[Zuni Tribe]] concerns about religious use and archaeological sites.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation.** Two life zones occur: mixed conifer and spruce-fir. The spruce-fir zone covers about 680 acres above 10,200 ft and is the only location in the Pinalenos with pure spruce-fir stands. The area also includes Douglas-fir, aspen and cienega vegetation. The [[Mt. Graham Spruce]] is a Mexican variety with longer lifespan and broader crown than northern relatives. Table 16 lists plants of special interest, including federally listed *Erigeron pringlei* (USFWS C-2) and several endemics such as *Potentilla albiflora*.

**Wildlife.** Above 9,000 ft, historic records list 21 mammal species, 8 reptiles and 50 birds. The Mt. Graham red squirrel is proposed for federal endangered listing. Its midden-based population is 114 in the 3,500-acre area: 92 in spruce-fir and 22 in mixed conifer. The black bear population, the largest south of the Mogollon Rim (grown to about 150 animals), depends on high-elevation spring food sources. The spotted owl was heard within 1,000 ft of the project area, though no roosting, breeding or feeding sites were found.

**Fish and aquatic life.** Of 17 fish species that have existed in the Pinalenos and surrounding valleys, only three native forms are known, and two are probably extinct on Mt. Graham. Apache trout (Salt River form, federally threatened) have been introduced to several creeks, including Deadman, Marijilda and Grant Creeks, with some reproduction. They have hybridized with rainbow trout in some streams. The speckled dace, last recorded in Grant Creek in 1965, is considered extinct. Apache trout need small, high-gradient brooks where summer temperatures rarely exceed 60°F, under a closed forest canopy.

**Cienegas.** Three cienegas are unique to southern Arizona. They occur between 10,200 and 10,400 ft and produce perennial surface water from snowmelt. Together they cover roughly 40 acres (the soil survey cites under 20 acres of cienega soil). They support bog-like vegetation and possibly a Pleistocene pollen record.

**Hydrology.** Of an average 35 inches of annual precipitation, about 60 percent falls as snow. Most surface water is already appropriated. The [[Frye Canyon Watershed]] has been closed to commercial recreation since 1930 to protect the municipal supply of Safford and Thatcher.

**Geology.** Pleistocene periglacial features lie a few hundred feet below the High Peak summit. They are considered scientifically valuable evidence of permanent ice at a southern latitude. The bedrock is metamorphic gneiss, and landslide potential is described as low.

## Key Findings

**Alternatives compared** (acreage and telescopes):
- **A** (No Action): no development; existing Forest Plan management.
- **B**: no development; emphasis on wildlife and dispersed recreation.
- **C**: no development; 1,000 acres of wilderness and a 680-acre ZBA; roads closed.
- **D**: up to 15 acres, 5 telescopes, 284-acre restricted buffer.
- **E**: up to 31 acres, 11 telescopes plus NNTT or interferometer, 738-acre buffer, 25-person dormitory.
- **F** (Steward Observatory's preferred alternative): up to 60 acres, all 13 telescopes, 1,240-acre buffer, 40-person dormitory.
- **PA** (Forest Service Preferred): up to 7 acres, 5 telescopes, 123-acre buffer, 1,000 acres of wilderness, 569-acre ZBA; no NNTT or dormitory.

**Red squirrel risk** (Table 5, 30-year extinction probability):

| Alternative | A | B | C | D | E | F | PA |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Risk | 20% | 20% | 20% | 45% | 50% | 50% | 35% |
| Total squirrels | 114 | 114 | 114 | 99 | 100 | 88 | 111 |

The text gives the F value as 85 while Table 5 gives 88. Estimated midden losses are 15 (D), 14 (E), 24 (F) and 4 (PA), with 4 to 10 further indirectly affected.

**Habitat losses.** Direct loss: 39 acres (D), 71 (E), 105 (F), 23 (PA) including road widening. The [[Black Bear]] habitat capacity reduction is at least 2% (PA), 5% (D), 7% (E) and more than 10% (F).

**Water.** Projected runoff increases range from 0 to 10 percent by watershed, with increases most noticeable in dry years. A diversion from Deadman Creek would take up to 7 percent of low-flow yield at Safford's intake, 35 percent at the boundary and 22 percent of High Peak Cienega flow. The EIS recommends no diversion when flows fall below aquatic-life maintenance levels and no withdrawals from sources other than Deadman Creek.

**Recreation and visitation.** Under development alternatives, recreation visitor days (RVDs) grow with telescopes. The NNTT is projected to add up to 50,000 visitors per year. Carrying capacity of 15,000 observatory visitors is reached by 2019 under PA and D, 2015 under E and 2019 under F. Non-development alternatives A and B reach 470,000 RVDs by 2022 at 2% annual growth.

**Visual and cultural.** Visual quality is significantly affected in all development alternatives, with most change under E and F. Archaeological site AR-03-05-04-102 lies in the exclusive-use area under all development alternatives and is considered unavoidable. Site AR-03-05-04-103 (rock cairns) may be avoidable.

**Socio-economic.** Graham County has a population of 23,200 and Safford 7,700, with unemployment at 15 percent. Arizona astronomy facilities spent almost $34 million in 1982–83 and employed 860 people, with about 90 percent of funding from federal sources. Development alternatives add about 330 to 400 tourism-related jobs (averaged over 50 years) and about 90 to 175 operations jobs.

**Costs.** Forest Service costs total $114,910 (A) rising to $266,410 (F) in period 1, with PA equal to D at $175,290 in the same period.

**Ranking of visual impact** (low to high): C, B, A, PA, D, E, F.

**Management commitments in the PA.** A red squirrel recovery plan; consultation with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service on critical habitat; siting to avoid middens; no development in cienega watersheds; water hauled from off-forest; restricted night public access; and Steward Observatory notified of all prescribed burns.

**Bottom line.** The document presents a trade-off between astronomical research value and irreversible loss of an isolated high-elevation ecosystem. It states that losses of "special ecosystem" information are irretrievable and irreversible. Comments on the DEIS were due January 20, 1987.
