# Starvation-Induced Mortality of Sea-Caught Jack Mackerel

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trachurus

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Theilacker]] tests whether starvation is a major natural cause of mortality for young [[Jack Mackerel]] (*Trachurus symmetricus*) at sea. Using [[Histological Analysis]] and [[Morphometric Analysis]] calibrated in the laboratory, the author examined larvae from a 4-day survey grid 350 km off California and from nearshore stations near the [[California Channel Islands]] and [[Tanner Bank]]. Starvation was common in offshore first-feeding larvae (about 70% by the abstract's figure) and much less common near islands and banks (about 12%). Mortality from starvation fell to zero by about 2 weeks of age. The paper supports the [[Critical Period Hypothesis]] for recruitment and presents histological and morphometric methods for monitoring larval condition in field samples.

## Background & Description

Jack mackerel hatch with yolk reserves lasting about 5 days at 15.0–15.5°C. After the yolk is absorbed, larvae must feed within about 8 days or die of starvation. In the laboratory, starvation for only 1 day slows growth, and normal growth resumes only 2–3 days after feeding begins ([[Theilacker 1978]], [[Theilacker 1981]]). The size at first feeding is about 3.2 mm standard length (SL).

Before this study, the field importance of starvation was unknown. The [[Critical Period Hypothesis]] (reviewed by [[May 1974]]) proposed that year-class strength is set early in life by food availability at first feeding. Earlier field evidence was mostly circumstantial. [[O'Connell 1980]] had documented starving ocean-caught larvae in [[Northern Anchovy]].

**Methods, briefly:**
- **Sampling:** In May 1980, a 400 mi² grid with 41 stations spaced 4 mi apart was sampled over 4 days. Samples were taken with oblique [[Bongo Net]] tows and a 1 m net (505 µm mesh) for the upper 50 m. Samples for histology were preserved immediately in [[Bouin's Solution]] to prevent autolysis.
- **Sample sizes:** More than 2,000 jack mackerel were collected offshore. Of these, 445 larvae were counted and measured, and 969 were prepared for histology. Nearshore, 41 fish from 14 hauls were analyzed.
- **Histology:** Eleven tissue characteristics (brain, digestive tract, liver, musculature) were graded and summed. Larvae were placed in four categories: healthy, recovering, starving and dying.
- **Morphometrics:** A [[Stepwise Discriminant Analysis]] (SWDA) using 11 body-part measurements and ratios classified fed versus starved fish. It correctly classified about 85% of laboratory-reared fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Larval distribution and habitat.** A strong larval density gradient was present across the offshore grid. Densities were 100–300 larvae per sample in central stations and near the western boundary, 20–50 to the north and east, and close to zero at the southern stations. The author found no consistent difference in starvation incidence between high- and low-density areas.

**Water temperature.** Satellite imagery showed the grid matched the 15–16°C range associated with jack mackerel spawning ([[Farris 1961]]). Surface temperature rose from 15.2°C in the north to 16.8°C in the south. Most fish were found at 16.1–16.6°C, which is warmer than the laboratory culture temperature. Inshore water was about 14°C. A warm, thin lens of open-ocean water intruded shoreward over deeper coastal water.

**Shrinkage correction.** Net capture and preservation shrink larvae, and shrinkage varies among body parts. Laboratory experiments estimated shrinkage for a standard 8-minute field tow followed by [[Bouin's Solution]] preservation. Standard length, head length and eye diameter shrank more in the field than in lab-preserved fish. Body depth at the anus was an exception, shrinking less under field conditions (20–23%) than in the lab (25%). Formalin preservation caused a slight increase in eye size.

**Prey availability.** [[Arthur 1976]] examined 750 specimens from 65 offshore samples and found that about 60% of first-feeding jack mackerel and about 10% of older larvae had empty stomachs. This is consistent with the histological findings.

## Key Findings

1. **Starvation is common at first feeding offshore.** Histology classified 59% of the smallest larvae (<3.5 mm SL) as dying of starvation, 23% as recovering and only 19% as healthy. Starvation fell to 16% in the 3.5–4.0 mm class and to about 3% in larger larvae (>4.0 mm).
2. **Nearshore larvae were in better condition.** Around the islands and banks, the proportion of starving larvae was about 12%. Island-habitat fish had more liver glycogen reserves than offshore fish, suggesting better feeding conditions there.
3. **Mortality declines with age.** Starvation-induced mortality dropped to zero by about 2 weeks of age in both habitats.
4. **Morphometrics confirmed histology.** The SWDA morphometric method gave results essentially the same as the histological method. Removing pectoral body depth from the analysis reduced predictive accuracy from 85% to 78%.
5. **Starvation-specific tissue signs were identified.** Brain lesions appeared in about 1% of fish. Wavy muscle fibers, luminal vacuoles, midgut sloughing and basal separations between mucosal cells were noted. Hindgut eosin-staining inclusions indicated past feeding. Pale inclusions in starving fish suggested feeding at some earlier time.
6. **Mortality estimates for [[Northern Anchovy]].** Recalculated starvation-induced mortality for northern anchovy first-feeding larvae was 35–46% per day, compared with total mortality of about 39% per day. This suggests starvation is the main source of mortality at first feeding for anchovy too.
7. **Estimates are likely conservative.** The author argues that the starvation estimate for jack mackerel is more likely to be too low than too high. Warmer field temperatures alter starvation and growth durations, robust fish may be retained better in nets, and predators may preferentially take unhealthy larvae.
8. **Methods for monitoring.** Histological and morphometric indices can be used to monitor larval condition in field samples. Morphometrics suits large sample sizes, while histology gives more biological detail. Both require careful net tows, preservation and calibration for shrinkage.

**Limitations noted by the author:** Only one survey year was sampled, and it is unknown whether the offshore starvation level is typical. The pancreas could not be graded because of staining variability. Some fish had illegible or unreliable counts in the source scan. The author notes that more research is needed to link larval condition to recruitment, as recruitment strength varies widely among year classes ([[Year-Class Strength]]).
