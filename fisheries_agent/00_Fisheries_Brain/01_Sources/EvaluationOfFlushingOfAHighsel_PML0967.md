# Evaluation of Flushing of a High-Selenium Backwater Channel in the Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study tested whether a water control structure, opened on 5 December 1996, that routed Colorado River flow through a former gravel-pit backwater channel at [[Walter Walker State Wildlife Area]] (WWSWA) would lower selenium and other inorganic elements in water, sediment, aquatic invertebrates and forage fish. Selenium fell sharply in every abiotic and lower-trophic compartment after the structure opened. Muscle plug selenium in [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], an endangered species, did not show a matching decline. The authors judged that forage fish selenium in 1998 still posed a level of concern for piscivorous pikeminnow.

## Background & Description

- **Study system:** A backwater channel receives river water, groundwater from the underlying [[Cobble Aquifer]], and seepage from [[North Pond]] and a marsh. Groundwater and seeps carried elevated selenium, linked to irrigation drainage.
- **Motivation:** Selenium contamination has been documented across the upper and lower Colorado River basins. Several endangered fishes, including the Colorado pikeminnow, [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]] and [[Humpback Chub]], use the upper basin, and the backwater channel was managed as a nursery and habitat area under the [[Recovery Implementation Program]].
- **Sampling:** Fish were collected by the Colorado River Fishery Project using fyke, trammel and minnow-trap nets, seining and electrofishing. Aquatic invertebrates came from modified light traps and sediment grabs. Sediment was sampled by grab, core and trap. Selenium was measured by atomic absorption with hydride generation, and other elements by ICP.
- **Sample size:** Only one razorback sucker was collected in 1995–1999, so it contributed no data. Colorado pikeminnow were collected each year, with muscle plugs taken for selenium analysis.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Selenium pathways:** Elevated selenium in filtered water correlated with nitrate (r = 0.90), calcium (0.85), hardness (0.83), magnesium (0.81), sulfate (0.81) and conductivity (0.79). This points to irrigation-influenced groundwater as the source.
- **Flow dependence:** Conductivity was lowest during runoff and highest during low flow. Years 1995 and 1997 were high-flow years, while 1996 and 1998 were average-flow years.
- **Sediment:** Selenium in sediment correlated positively with total carbon (r = 0.61) and volatile solids (r = 0.45), and negatively with total solids (r = −0.42).
- **Bioaccumulation:** Selenium in invertebrates correlated with filtered water (r = 0.83) and sediment (Spearman r_s = 0.81). Selenium in forage fish correlated with filtered water (r = 0.58) and sediment (r = 0.75).
- **Recovery and depuration:** Cited studies report whole-body selenium half-lives of roughly 19–70 days in young fish, and more than 100 days for adult razorback sucker muscle plugs. Lake Belews showed selenium residues only 25% lower after five years of reduced inputs.
- **Forage fish community:** Native fish made up 38% of trammel-net catches in 1995, 28% in 1996 and 64% in 1997. The authors note that sampling effort varied greatly between years, so these shifts are hard to interpret.

## Key Findings

- **Water:** Filtered selenium at the channel stations (excluding WW1 and WW4b) fell from 21.0 µg/L (1995, n = 47) and 23.5 µg/L (1996, n = 35) to 2.1 µg/L in both 1997 (n = 47) and 1998 (n = 31). Station WW4b, at the marsh, stayed elevated, reaching 31 µg/L in January 1998.
- **Sediment:** Top-layer selenium fell from 8.5 µg/g (1995, n = 12) and 8.2 µg/g (1996, n = 17) to 4.8 µg/g (1997, n = 4) and 1.1 µg/g (1998, n = 8).
- **Aquatic invertebrates:** Selenium fell from 27.4 µg/g (1996, n = 13) to 15.5 µg/g (1997, n = 9) and 4.9 µg/g (1998, n = 6). Only WW7 showed no decline.
- **Forage fish:** Selenium fell from 27.2 µg/g (1996, n = 24) and 20.2 µg/g (1997, n = 23) to 8.6 µg/g (1998, n = 21), a fourfold drop from 1996 to 1998. Fathead minnow and green sunfish dominated the samples.
- **Colorado pikeminnow muscle plugs:** Means were 9.8 µg/g (1995, n = 49), 9.5 µg/g (1996, n = 40), 9.0 µg/g (1997, n = 54) and 10.3 µg/g (1998, n = 3). No significant change occurred across years, and the 1998 sample was very small. Muscle selenium decreased with increasing fish length (r = −0.37, n = 162) and weight (r = −0.34, n = 157).
- **Flow link:** Muscle plug selenium was most strongly correlated with mean May river flow (r = −0.87, p = 0.05). It was not correlated with selenium in water, sediment, invertebrates or forage fish (all p > 0.25).
- **Toxicity thresholds:** The 1998 mean forage fish selenium of 8.6 µg/g exceeded the whole-body thresholds of 4 µg/g (Lemly, 1996) and >4 µg/g (Stephens et al., 1997). The authors judged it a level of concern for piscivorous Colorado pikeminnow.
- **Conclusion:** Flushing lowered selenium in water, sediment, invertebrates and forage fish. Because pikeminnow tissue did not respond in the same way, the authors recommended that continued flushing would likely have further reduced ecosystem selenium. The study period was too short to show a pikeminnow response.
