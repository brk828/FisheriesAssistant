# Endangered and Threatened Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado State University Cooperative Extension bulletin (issued September 1980, revised February 1983, with 1982 updates) describes the biology, distribution, status and causes of decline of the endangered and threatened fishes of the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]]. It covers five focal species: the [[Colorado River Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]]. It also explains how the [[Endangered Species Act]] may create conflicts with water and energy development. The authors conclude that the native fishes declined mainly because of [[Dams and Reservoirs]], land and water use, and the resulting advantage given to [[Non-Native Fishes]]. Recovery depends on hatchery propagation as a stopgap, habitat management, and control of non-native predators and competitors.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River basin is arid to semiarid and has enormous flow, temperature and sediment extremes. Historically, sediment loads exceeded 100,000 acre-feet deposited in the Gulf of California each year. The basin has been isolated from neighboring basins for millions of years, so its fauna is highly [[Endemism|endemic]]. The Colorado basin has the highest percentage of endemic species of any river basin in North America.

Of the 13 species native to the upper basin, six are endemic: the squawfish, three chubs, the razorback sucker and the flannelmouth sucker. The other seven are non-endemic headwater or widespread species. All 13 persist in the upper basin, but all have been depleted. Five are listed as endangered or threatened in Colorado. The squawfish, bonytail and humpback chub are also federally endangered. The [[Kendall Warm Springs Dace]], a speckled dace subspecies, is federally endangered because its entire habitat is less than 1,000 feet of stream.

Key species traits:
- **Colorado River Squawfish:** the largest minnow native to North America, a predatory, pike-shaped fish. Specimens 18 inches or longer are easily identified. Largest recent specimen: about 3 feet and 15 pounds. Historic maximum was estimated at 5 to 6 feet and 60 to 80 pounds.
- **Humpback Chub:** a rounded body hump, streamlined fins and a small eye. Maximum size is 16 to 18 inches. It was first described from Grand Canyon in 1946.
- **Bonytail Chub:** very streamlined, with a thin caudal peduncle and typically 10 dorsal and 10 anal fin rays.
- **Razorback Sucker:** a sharp-edged, bony hump, 14 or 15 dorsal fin rays, and 45 or more first-arch gillrakers. Reaches more than 10 pounds and possibly more than 20 years of age.
- **Colorado River Cutthroat Trout:** distinguished by red or orange slash marks under the lower jaw and large rounded spots. It is one of about 15 cutthroat subspecies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Dams and reservoirs** are the most obvious cause of decline. Construction began with [[Hoover Dam]] in 1930, and the large dams and reservoirs were built over the following 36 years. Reservoirs such as [[Lake Mead]], [[Lake Powell]], [[Lake Mohave]], [[Lake Havasu]] and [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] converted a turbulent river into cold, clear, stable-flow ponds. Native fishes cannot reproduce successfully in reservoirs. Adults persist, grow old and die out.

**Cold-water releases** from dams (about 40° to 50°F from great depths) keep the river too cold for spawning. Releases from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] eliminated squawfish from 65 miles of the Green River. Only after the [[Yampa River]] warms the Green River do temperatures reach 70°F or more.

**Land and water use** (logging, grazing, agriculture, irrigation) removed vegetation, increased erosion and flood peaks, and reduced quiet backwater nursery habitat. Squawfish disappeared from the [[Gila River]] this way.

**Hydrology and water quality:** the annual average virgin flow at Lee's Ferry was 14,906,000 acre-feet. By 1975, 3,823,900 acre-feet (26%) had been lost to consumptive use, transmountain diversion and evaporation. Peak spring flushing flows are now at all-time lows. Irrigated lands in Grand Valley return 8 tons of salt per acre. Overgrazed rangeland can contribute up to 30 tons per acre. Salinity has doubled or tripled in some reaches.

**Non-native fishes** dominate most basin communities, with more than 30 species introduced. The [[Redside Shiner]] was first recorded in the Yampa River in 1961. It spawns earlier than squawfish and saturates the nursery habitat. [[Red Shiner]] appears to coexist with young squawfish. Largemouth bass and sunfishes preyed heavily on hatchery-reared squawfish in the Colorado River.

**Hybridization** threatens some species. Razorback suckers hybridize with flannelmouth suckers. Almost half of razorbacks captured in the Green River from 1967 to 1973 were hybrids. Humpback, bonytail and roundtail chubs also hybridize, and the bonytail is being absorbed into the other populations.

**Water development** and the [[Endangered Species Act]] interact directly. Section 7 requires federal agencies to ensure their actions do not jeopardize listed species. Private projects become subject to Section 7 only when federal permits or funding are involved.

## Key Findings

**Colorado River Squawfish**
- Only one squawfish found in the entire lower basin since 1968.
- The Green River from the Yampa confluence to the Colorado, about 200 miles long, is the greatest stronghold, with successful reproduction consistently detected.
- Radio tracking showed long-distance movements. One fish tagged in upper Lake Powell moved about 200 miles up the Colorado to a probable spawning site near Clifton, Colorado.
- The lower Green River below the Yampa is the principal nursery area. Young-of-year abundance rose sharply after [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] outlet modifications in 1978 restored summer temperatures.
- In 1982, the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] stocked 30,000 squawfish of 2 to 3 inches into the Colorado River below Grand Junction.

**Humpback Chub**
- The largest known concentration is in the lower Little Colorado River, Arizona. The Ruby and Westwater Canyon populations are treated as one population.
- Most tagged fish moved less than half a mile from their capture point. A tagged fish moved 13 miles from Westwater to Ruby Canyon.
- Laboratory tests showed it tolerates total dissolved solids up to 11,600 ppm, about one-third the salinity of the ocean, and is more resistant to toxic compounds than channel catfish, fathead minnow and bluegill.
- Hybridization is probably less of a threat than once believed. Many specimens merely showed normal hump variation.

**Bonytail Chub**
- Described by the authors as the rarest native fish and the species nearest extinction.
- The last Gila River record was in 1926. The last verified Colorado records are many years old.
- Intensive 1981 and 1982 sampling failed to find a viable population. Specimens from Coal Creek Rapids were hybrids.
- Lake Mohave still holds bonytails. In November 1981, 42,000 hatchery-reared bonytails were stocked there.

**Razorback Sucker**
- Spawning was observed at 34°F to 68°F, in 1 to 20 feet of water. In river environments, spawning on gravel bars occurred at about 62°F.
- In Senator Wash Reservoir, predation by non-native fishes completely suppressed reproduction. In Lake Mohave, some eggs escaped predation and a few larvae were found.
- Around 250 razorbacks were stranded in 1977 when a San Juan River irrigation reservoir was drawn down.
- Its status is more hopeful than other species because of wider distribution and use of artificial ponds. However, it is not federally listed, so it has received less attention.

**Colorado River Cutthroat Trout**
- Spawns at about 45°F. The subspecies is generally ecologically similar to other trout.
- Once occupied all cold waters of the upper basin. Now only a few pure populations remain in isolated headwaters.
- Trappers Lake, Colorado, has a native population, though it has had some past hybridization.
- In 1980 to 1982, the Colorado Division of Wildlife found 12 new populations of pure or nearly pure Colorado River cutthroat, limited to small headwater streams.
- Restoration is easier than for other species. Chemical removal of non-natives followed by transplanting pure stock has worked for greenback cutthroat.

**Endangered Species Act**
- The first federal endangered list was issued in 1964. The Act of 1973 (P.L. 93-205) added Section 7 protections.
- The 1978 amendments required an economic analysis before critical habitat designation and created an exemption process through a Review Board and seven-member Endangered Species Committee. Five of seven votes are needed for an exemption.
- The 1982 Amendments, signed October 13, 1982, removed the requirement for critical habitat and economic evaluation before listing, shortened deadlines, and created the "experimental population" category, which can facilitate reintroduction.
- The authors stress that realistic administration means helping developers locate, design and operate projects to minimize harm to species.
