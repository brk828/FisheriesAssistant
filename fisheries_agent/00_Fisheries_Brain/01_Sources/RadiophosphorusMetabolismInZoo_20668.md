# Radiophosphorus Metabolism in Zooplankton and Microorganisms

#ichthyology #marine-biology #gammaridae #artemiidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 laboratory study by [[Eugene Harris]] (Dalhousie University, published in the [[Canadian Journal of Zoology]]) measured the exchange of radioactive phosphorus (³²P) between water and three test systems: the brine shrimp [[Artemia]], the marine amphipod [[Gammarus Locusta]] (beach flea), and natural microorganisms. Results were expressed as [[Turnover Time]] values. The central conclusion is that marine invertebrates do not absorb appreciable phosphorus directly from water; instead, they acquire it mainly as particulate matter, chiefly bacteria. The work is not a fish study. It addresses zooplankton and microbial phosphorus cycling in lake and marine water.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a gap in limnology: the quantitative role of bacteria in lake metabolism was poorly documented. Earlier work by [[Coffin et al.]] on the Punchbowl acid bog lake had shown that zooplankton took up ³²P surprisingly quickly, which raised the question of whether zooplankton absorb phosphorus directly through the body wall, gills and intestine or obtain it in organic form from food.

The lake is described as an equilibrated system of water and solids rather than a simple nutrient reservoir (after [[Hayes et al.]]). Turnover times were derived from the uptake equation Ac = Ac∞(1 − e^(−t/T)), which is linearised by plotting log(Ac∞ − Ac) against time.

Test animals:
- **Artemia**: reared from eggs in Halifax sea water, fed yeast with occasional Ulva or Chlamydomonas. About 12 mm long, with continuous filter-feeding.
- **Marine Gammarus locusta**: collected under stones and seaweed in North West Arm, Halifax, and kept at about 4 °C. They survived 2–3 months with minimal care.
- **Freshwater Hyalella**: abandoned because cannibalism made results unreliable.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Microbial conversion**: In distilled water with carrier-free ³²P, only 40% of the isotope precipitated as inorganic phosphate. About 99% was recovered after ashing, which implies that microorganisms had converted roughly three-fifths of the phosphorus to organic form.
- **Bottle effects**: Sessile bacteria on glass walls removed 16.7% of ³²P after 155 hours and 45% after 10 days. Surface-to-volume effects make bottle turnover faster than in open lakes ([[Zobell]] and Anderson).
- **Feeding**: Artemia feed on protozoa, unicellular algae, bacteria and detritus, capturing particles of about 0.25 µm and probably smaller.
- **Literature context**: [[Rigler]] reported much faster inorganic phosphate turnover (under 5 min to 30 min) in Ontario lakes, using a different equilibrium definition (organisms versus molecular-filtrate solution).

## Key Findings

- **Artemia**: T = 14 hours. At equilibrium each gram contained 8.2% of the initial water ³²P count.
- **Marine Gammarus**: T = 42.5 and 43.5 hours in two experiments (about 43 hours overall). Uptake shows a lag of a few hours.
- **Microbial equilibrium**: Organic–inorganic phosphorus equilibrium in Chocolate Lake water gave T = 19 hours for organic P and 27 hours for inorganic P (water). The calculation used a slope of 0.03876 and a final activity of 41.5.
- **Sterile animals**: Gammarus treated with terramycin, which removed most bacteria, took up hardly any radiophosphorus. Evidence therefore points to bacteria as the vehicle for uptake.
- **Antibiotic series**: With minimal, medium and maximal terramycin pretreatment, uptake was considerably less than the control in all three cases. The control had about 1.25 × 10⁵ bacteria per ml.
- **Temperature**: At room temperature (22–25 °C), Gammarus took up about four times as much ³²P as animals at 2–5 °C by 95 hours. Organic ³²P peaked at 85% within minutes and reached about 60% in animals at room temperature after only 4 hours.
- **Control baseline**: Untreated Gammarus organic phosphorus was 63.7 ± 4.9%.
- **Mechanism**: The authors conclude that Gammarus does not absorb phosphorus through the body wall, gills or gut in significant amounts, which agrees with [[Bond]] and [[Krogh]]. Uptake is best explained by ingestion of particulate bacteria, as shown by [[Stuart et al.]] for Moina.
- **Limitations**: The author notes that bottle experiments exaggerate bacterial growth relative to open lakes, and that the Gammarus turnover data came from only two runs.
