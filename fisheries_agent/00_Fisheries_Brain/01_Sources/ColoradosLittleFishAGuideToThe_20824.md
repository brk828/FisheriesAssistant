# Colorado's Little Fish: A Guide to the Minnows and Other Lesser-Known Fishes of Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1985 field guide, written by John Woodling for the [[Colorado Division of Wildlife]], describes about 50 of the roughly 80 fish species found in Colorado. These are the minnows, suckers, darters and other species seldom sought by anglers, often labelled "trash" or "forage" fish. The guide covers species descriptions, identification keys, county distribution maps, life history notes, and the freshwater habitat types of the state's river basins. It also documents how dewatering, pollution, reservoirs, channelization and stocking have reduced native fish communities. The book is aimed at bait dealers, the general public, high school students and first-year college biology students.

## Background & Description

The guide was published in June 1985 by the Colorado Division of Wildlife and was partly funded under the [[Commercial Fisheries Research and Development Act]] (Project No. 1-161-R-1, National Marine Fisheries Service). Acknowledgements credit numerous biologists and volunteers for records, specimen collection and manuscript typing.

Key background points:

- **Scope:** About 80 fish species occur in Colorado, out of more than 40,000 described fish species worldwide. About 30 are commonly sought by anglers; the remaining 50 are the subject of this book.
- **Naming:** The title "Little Fish" was chosen over terms such as "trash" or "forage" fish, which carry negative connotations. The preface notes that some included species exceed one foot in length, and a [[Colorado Squawfish]] specimen has been recorded at 65 pounds.
- **Sources:** Where Colorado data were lacking, species accounts draw on studies from other states. Historic work by [[David Starr Jordan]] (1891) and [[Max Ellis]] (1914) is cited as the baseline for Colorado's fish fauna.
- **Identification:** The guide uses taxonomic keys to family, genus and species, along with photographs, descriptions and distribution maps. Keys are arranged as couplets. The authors caution that distribution maps are not final and that unexpected identifications should be rechecked.
- **Fish structure:** Only bony fishes (soft-rayed and spiny-rayed) occur in Colorado. Lampreys, sharks and rays are absent. The section explains gills, skin, scales (cycloid and ctenoid), fins, digestive tract and age determination from scale rings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**River basins.** Each of Colorado's basins is physically isolated and holds a distinct fauna. Stream capture has occasionally moved species between basins. Of the more than 40 species that may occur in a drainage, fewer than a dozen are native to any one system.

| Basin | Notes from the guide |
|---|---|
| Colorado River | Western slope; nine native species per Ellis (1914), 13 or 14 per Behnke and Benson (1980) |
| Rio Grande | Some streams disappear into the San Luis Valley; four species collected by Jordan (1891), five described by Ellis (1914) |
| Arkansas River | Seventeen native species listed by Ellis (1914); flows dry on the eastern plains |
| Cimarron River | No permanent flow in Colorado; three species collected |
| Smoky Hill River | No permanent standing water recorded in Colorado; no fish records |
| Republican River | Fed by [[Ogallala Aquifer]] springs; fourteen native species (Cancalosi 1980) |
| South Platte River | 38 species reported native, possibly 29 (Propst 1982) |
| North Platte River | Nine species recorded (Propst 1982) |

**Habitat types.** The guide describes:

- **Coldwater mountain trout streams:** high dissolved oxygen, low dissolved solids, gravel-to-boulder substrate, shaded.
- **"Big River" habitat:** the western slope Colorado, Yampa, White, Dolores and Animas rivers, with fast spring runoff, deep pools and boulder rapids.
- **Transition zones:** where mountain streams reach the plains. These support the highest species counts because coldwater and warmwater fishes overlap.
- **Eastern plains rivers:** braided, sandy channels, very warm summers, and salinity above 4,000 ppm in the lower Arkansas.
- **Intermittent plains tributaries:** persistent only as potholes.
- **Spring-fed streams:** stable, cool refuges, notably in the Republican Basin.

**Human impacts.** The guide identifies several drivers of decline:

- **Mining:** acidic, metal-laden effluent from the 1800s mining boom, still leaching from inactive portals and tailings.
- **Municipal sewage:** chlorine and ammonia toxicity, and nutrient enrichment producing nightly oxygen crashes. The South Platte receives effluent from Denver, Greeley and Fort Collins.
- **Agriculture:** fertilizer runoff, cattle trampling of banks, riparian clearing, silt and irrigation return flows.
- **Dewatering and diversion:** reservoirs, transmountain diversions (including the Fryingpan-Arkansas project), and irrigation diversions that dry streams.
- **Channelization and thermal releases:** straightened channels lose pools and cover, and cold reservoir releases eliminate warmwater species downstream.
- **Stocking:** since the mid-1880s, fish stocking, including accidental introductions through bait buckets and fish culture, has introduced exotic and non-native species such as [[Grass Carp]], [[Tench]] and [[Goldfish]].

## Key Findings

**Species status and rarity**

- Four minnow species have been extirpated from the state's Arkansas and South Platte drainages: hornyhead chub, blacknose shiner, lake chub and Arkansas River speckled chub. Four more are reported but lack existing specimens.
- Of the 50 species in the book, 12 are rare (marked with an asterisk). Four are extremely rare, with only one or two specimens collected since 1980: northern redbelly dace, southern redbelly dace, plains minnow and river shiner.
- The [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Bonytail]], [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] are federally and state listed as endangered. The squawfish, bonytail and humpback chub are restricted to West Slope mainstems.
- The [[Southern Redbelly Dace]] persists in a single Arkansas River tributary at Pueblo. The guide describes its survival as "extremely tenuous."

**Bait and commercial use**

- Ten species are abundant enough to harvest as bait (Table 3), including [[White Sucker]], [[Creek Chub]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Golden Shiner]] and [[Sand Shiner]].
- Fathead minnows and golden shiners are suited to pond culture. The guide describes fathead minnows as "perhaps the best bait fish found in Colorado."
- Using live minnows is prohibited in many areas. Harvest and sale require a commercial fishing license from the Colorado Division of Wildlife.
- Rare species, including the plains minnow, brassy minnow, southern redbelly dace, river shiner, Arkansas darter and common shiner, should not be used as bait.

**Introductions and ecological concerns**

- The [[Redside Shiner]] is expanding its range on the West Slope. It may prey on squawfish eggs and compete with young squawfish, though its effects are not yet fully measured.
- [[Grass Carp]] are not a reliable solution to aquatic weeds. They eat poorly below 55–60°F, and a large number of small fish (60 per acre) is required. Releases require a permit.
- [[Carp]] have become established in many warmwater waters and have caused turbidity. Barr Lake carp were commercially fished in 1980.
- Hybridization among Gila chubs and between suckers is common. In one example, 30% of specimens at a site were hybrids.

**Taxonomic notes**

- Gila chubs (roundtail, humpback and bonytail) were once considered variants of one species. Smith et al. (1979) identified them as separate species based on 34 characters.
- Mottled and Paiute sculpins cannot be reliably separated in Colorado. The guide describes only the mottled sculpin (see [[Mottled Sculpin]]).
- The longnose and speckled dace are separated by the presence or absence of a frenum. Jordan (1891) noted that some western Colorado specimens had both traits.

**Reproduction and life history examples**

- A 15-inch carp female carried 36,000 eggs and a 33-inch female carried 2,208,000 (Swee and McCrimmon 1966).
- Humpback chub eggs hatched at 79–84% in 66–68°F water, but only 2% at 54–56°F (Hamman 1982). Bonytail eggs incubated at 54–56°F showed 96% failure to hatch.
- Gizzard shad reach maximum egg production at two years, with an average of 379,000 ova.

**Conservation recommendations**

The guide concludes that native species require cool, clear, permanently flowing habitat. Protecting refuges such as the Republican Basin, Plum Creek, parts of the St. Vrain and Poudre, and other transition-zone reaches is essential. Pollution and dewatering in these areas could eliminate remaining populations. Restocking with native species is possible where donor populations survive. Any new introduction should be avoided, since exotic species have caused much of the damage.
