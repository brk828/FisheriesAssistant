# Breeding Habits of Lake Trout in New York

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Fishery Bulletin, by William F. Royce of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Fishery Bulletin 59, Volume 52, 1951), reports a study of the breeding habits and early life history of [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*) made in 1939, 1940 and 1941 on several [[New York Finger Lakes]] and Adirondack lakes. The work covers sexual dimorphism, age and size at maturity, timing and duration of spawning, spawning habitat, the spawning act, egg survival, temperature requirements, predation, and the early development and juvenile ecology of the species. It is a revision of a 1943 Cornell University doctoral thesis.

## Background & Description

Lake trout are a major food fish in the [[Great Lakes]] and a prized game fish in smaller northeastern U.S. and southern Canadian lakes, where commercial fishing is usually prohibited. Heavy fishing has caused population declines in some lakes, so knowledge of eggs, larvae and sub-catchable juveniles is needed for conservation and for stocking new waters. Before this study, the young of the species were almost unknown, since very few wild fish under 8 inches had been seen.

**Sexual dimorphism:** Lake trout lack the hooked jaw (kype) typical of mature males of other salmonids, and sex cannot usually be told from the head alone. Their normal coloration varies among lakes, from silvery in the clear Finger Lakes to very dark in brown-water Adirondack lakes. During courtship, males develop intense color (light backs and very dark, lustrous sides), but this fades once fish are netted or speared. The author contrasts lake trout with [[Pacific Salmon]], which develop kypes and fast before spawning. Lake trout feed through the spawning period, so their gonads may be fuelled by ingested food.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Maturity and growth:** Fish mature mostly around the sixth year. In Seneca Lake, mature fish were 26 to 30 inches; in Keuka Lake, 18 to 24 inches; in Skaneateles Lake, some matured at 15 to 16 inches. Algonquin Park lakes ranged from 14 to 18 inches. The author argues that the uniform 15-inch New York minimum legal size may be too restrictive in some lakes and too lax in others, so growth rate and fishing pressure should guide size limits.

**Spawning timing and light/temperature:** Lake trout spawn once a year in autumn. Timing is linked to light, temperature, lake depth and the lake's turnover. Shallow lakes spawn earlier; deep lakes later. [[Seneca Lake]], the deepest New York lake (625 ft), is an exception, with spawning from late September through October in 100 to 200 ft of water, well before turnover. The author infers two New York races: an early, deep-water Seneca race and a shallow-water race in the Finger and Adirondack lakes.

**Spawning habitat:** Spawning areas are clean gravel or rubble with crevices where eggs can roll. Currents and waves keep these areas free of silt, and deltas in the Finger Lakes create them. Spring water was not associated with spawning sites.

**Comparative studies:** The author reviews Great Lakes races (siscowet, common and Rush Lake trout) and Lake Nipigon's three races, which spawn at different times and depths.

## Key Findings

- **Sexual dimorphism:** Sex is almost impossible to determine by head examination. Male color changes during courtship are the only notable difference.
- **Age at maturity:** Of 33 mature fish from Seneca Lake, 13 had 5 annuli and 20 had 6. At Lake Simcoe, 13 of 20 five-year-olds and 16 of 17 six-year-olds were mature. At Keuka Lake, 15 of 18 five-year-olds and 5 of 6 six-year-olds were mature.
- **Spawning time and weather (Raquette Lake, 1933–41):** Multiple regression of spawning peak date on September air temperature and cloudy days was significant (R = 0.8643, DF = 5). Neither factor alone was significant. At Upper Saranac Lake (6 years of data), the relation was not significant (R = 0.6990).
- **Spawning season duration:** Lasted 10 to 20 days in smaller New York lakes, and 7 to 10 days in Lake George. In Seneca Lake, the earliest and latest egg dates were September 23 and November 3. Overall, spawning periods ran about 20 days, varying from 10 to 40.
- **Spawning act:** Observed at Otsego Lake, mainly in the evening (peak 5 to 9 p.m.). No nest or redd was built, and males cleaned several hundred square feet of bottom. Spawning involved 2 to 10 fish in a group, with each act lasting only a few seconds. The trout were not monogamous.
- **Egg survival:** A sample of 309 eggs collected on December 28, 1941, from Otsego Lake contained 5.8% unfertilized and 15.2% dead; 79% were alive. The author regards this as a near minimum survival estimate.
- **Predation:** No lake-trout alevins were found in the stomachs of 61 fish of 11 species captured during the vulnerable alevin period. In Seneca Lake, lake-trout eggs were found in all 31 small lake trout examined (up to 147 eggs in one fish).
- **Temperature:** Lake-trout eggs tolerate lower temperatures than other trout and can develop under winter ice if they do not freeze. The 140-day incubation at Otsego Lake suggests 36 to 37°F. Alevins probably avoid temperatures above 60°F.
- **Hatching and emergence:** In Otsego Lake in 1941, eggs hatched about April 15 and fry left the spawning area around May 20 to 25. A single advanced fry from Seneca Lake (April 2, 1940) suggested hatching in late January.
- **Juvenile ecology:** Repeated gear trials (gill nets, trawls, set lines, minnow traps) failed to capture early fingerlings. Keuka Lake juveniles (6 to 15 inches) fed mainly on [[Mysis Relicta]] or on alewives, and were captured mostly at depths of 40 to 120 feet.
- **Overall conclusion:** Natural lake-trout reproduction is efficient. Young fish live on rocky bottoms in deep water in summer, and spawning-site selection parallels that of stream-dwelling trout.
