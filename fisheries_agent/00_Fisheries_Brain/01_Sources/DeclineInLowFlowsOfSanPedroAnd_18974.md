# San Pedro River Beaver Reintroduction Proposal and Hydrological Concerns

#ichthyology #riparian-ecology #conservation-management #Castoridae #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document consists of correspondence from May 1995 regarding a proposed beaver (*Castor canadensis frondator*) reintroduction to the [[San Pedro River]] [[Riparian National Conservation Area]] in Arizona. Dr. Robin D. Silver raises concerns about declining river flows (67% reduction in low flows from 1943-1992) and potential impacts of beaver dams on native fish recovery and exotic species proliferation. The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and [[Bureau of Land Management]] proposed reestablishing beaver between Hereford bridge and Highway 90 bridge, citing potential benefits for water recharge and habitat enhancement. Dr. R.D. MacNish from the [[University of Arizona]] provides critical hydrological analysis challenging assumptions about groundwater recharge benefits.

## Background & Description

The [[San Pedro River]] historically supported large beaver populations in southeast Arizona. Past transplant efforts failed due to inadequate habitat placement. The current proposal targets the upper San Pedro River section between Hereford bridge and Highway 90 bridge, identified as having necessary habitat components and abundant food resources for a self-sustaining population.

Source beaver would come from the greater [[Colorado River]] drainage, specifically from Cluff Ranch (north Pinaleno Mountains), Yuma area populations, or other Arizona locations. Initial transplants were planned for September 1995, with subsequent releases as needed to establish the population.

The correspondence also mentions planned fieldwork to [[Lake Mohave]] (May 8-12, 1995) to observe and photograph [[Bonytail Chub]], indicating concurrent native fish conservation efforts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrological Conditions**: Hydrological data from University of Arizona researchers documented a 67% decline in low flows of the San Pedro River from 1943 to 1992, representing significant long-term water availability reduction.

**Stream Hydrology**: From below Palominas to Charleston, the San Pedro functions as a "gaining stream" where water moves from mountain front areas through the aquifer into the stream channel. This hydrological characteristic fundamentally affects how beaver dams would interact with groundwater systems.

**Water Budget Concerns**: Dr. MacNish identified critical water consumption differences:
- Phreatophytic vegetation: 2-3 acre-feet per acre annually
- Open water (ponds): over 6 acre-feet per acre annually
- Beaver ponds would approximately double consumptive water use per acre compared to riparian vegetation

**Riparian Habitat**: The target reach possessed adequate willow and cottonwood resources to support beaver populations, representing recovered riparian conditions suitable for recolonization.

## Key Findings

**Proposed Benefits** (from AGFD/BLM):
- Increase on-site water recharge and sediment deposition
- Improve water quality and nutrient cycling
- Enhance riparian habitat
- Increase wildlife viewing and recreational opportunities
- Restore historically present species to ecosystem

**Hydrological Concerns** (Dr. MacNish):
- Beaver dams would NOT increase groundwater recharge in gaining stream reaches
- Damming would raise floodplain aquifer levels by backing up water moving toward the river, not through infiltration
- Elevated water tables would reduce depth to water, likely increasing phreatophyte water consumption
- Replacement of vegetation with open water would roughly double consumptive water use per acre
- Net effect would be significant increases in total water consumption in the riparian corridor

**Conservation Concerns** (Dr. Silver):
- Potential negative impacts on native fish recovery efforts in context of severely declining flows
- Risk of creating habitat favorable to exotic species including [[Bullfrogs]]
- Need for expert evaluation of ecosystem interactions

**Administrative Process**: Public comment period established through May 25, 1995, with potential for public scoping meetings if significant interest warranted. Comments directed to Bureau of Land Management, Tucson office.
