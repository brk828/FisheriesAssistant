# Behavioral interactions between desert pupfish and mosquito fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This doctoral dissertation (Mark Russell Jennings, [[University Of Arizona]], 1986; School of Renewable Natural Resources, Wildlife and Fisheries Science) tests whether [[Desert Pupfish]] and [[Mosquitofish]] regulate their numbers through emigration in experimental habitats, and whether the two species can coexist. Using replicated outdoor and laboratory tanks, the author found that both species regulate population size via emigration in response to resources, and that adult pupfish and mosquitofish coexisted and reproduced together for 5 days without a significant change in population size compared with single-species controls. The study suggests that native fish replacement by mosquitofish in the American Southwest is likely a product of long-term interactions in low-complexity, closed systems, not short-term competition in open ones.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:**
  - Desert pupfish (*Cyprinodon macularius*): descendants of stock collected in April 1982 from Bates Well, Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument, Arizona.
  - Mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis affinis*): descendants of stock collected in March 1984 from a naturalized population in Chalk Reservoir on Rail X Ranch, about 3.8 km west of Catalina, Arizona; origin probably a post-1950 introduction by the Arizona State Public Health Department. Fish matched the western mosquitofish as described by [[Hubbs]] (1961) and Rosen and Bailey (1963).
- **Husbandry:** Stocks were held in large 5.5-m diameter outdoor pools at the Arizona Cooperative Fishery Unit research facility, Tucson. Adults were captured with unbaited minnow traps (40 cm long, 19 cm diameter) and sorted by sex and size.
- **Size classes used:** Pupfish 25–30 mm total length (TL); mosquitofish males 30–35 mm TL, females 40–45 mm TL. Mixed-sex groups were used, with each sex at least one third of stocked individuals.
- **Experimental tanks:** Galvanized sheet-metal shells, 1.8 m diameter by 1.1 m deep, lined with blue 10-mil polyvinyl sheeting. Each had a wooden outlet box with notched [[Plexiglas]] panels acting as a one-way exit, following the design of [[McMahon]] (1984). Tanks were arranged in two groups of four in a partly shaded area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Theoretical framework:** The study tests self-regulation through behavioral spacing and emigration (Taylor and Taylor 1977; Zumnicki 1978), previously applied to desert pupfish by McMahon (1984). Comparable data for mosquitofish were lacking.
- **Life-history traits of concern:** High reproductive capacity, year-round breeding, and omnivorous feeding (Hildebrand 1917; Krumholtz 1948; Cox 1966, 1972) suggest both species could rapidly overpopulate a habitat that cannot regulate numbers.
- **Diet:** Pupfish eat a wider range of plants and animals than mosquitofish; competition for food is probably negligible in the wild (Moyle 1976a; Walters and Legner 1980). Laboratory work shows pupfish are more effective predators of mosquito larvae in dense vegetation (Legner and Medved 1974; Walters and Legner 1980).
- **Habitat modification:** Extensive alteration of desert aquatic systems, such as the [[Gila River]] basin, has produced closed systems that prevent emigration and may create "fence effects" detrimental to native fishes (Minckley and Deacon 1968, 1974).
- **Replacement under closed conditions:** Mosquitofish replaced native fishes such as the Gila topminnow within roughly 4 months to 3 years in closed systems (Miller 1961; Minckley and Deacon 1968; Meffe et al. 1983).
- **Evolutionary context:** Pupfish coevolved with diverse predatory fishes in Plio-Pleistocene interconnected lakes and marshes; most piscivores disappeared during Pleistocene stresses, while pupfish retained emigration-based regulation.

## Key Findings

- **Emigration-based regulation:** Both species have the innate ability to regulate numbers to available resources via emigration when habitats are open to egress. Habitats lacking food, cover and substrate triggered emigration; those with these resources induced residency.
- **Carrying capacity response to cover:** Resident fish numbers varied directly with experimental cover reductions. Halving cover caused 35–50% more individuals to emigrate than when cover stayed at its fixed level (Figures 10–11, as cited).
- **Mixed-species stocking:** Adding adult mosquitofish to established adult pupfish populations at constant resources produced no significant change in population size for either species compared with single-species controls over 5 days (P>0.05).
- **Differential resource use:** Pupfish stayed near the substrate and cover, feeding on micro- and macrofauna on cover, while mosquitofish occupied surface waters between cover patches and used cover mainly to flee predators.
- **Reproduction:** Adult pupfish and mosquitofish reproduced successfully when mixed for 5 days in open habitats. Male pupfish defended nest sites from mosquitofish, and young pupfish appeared in mixed and control pools.
- **No aggression observed:** In open systems, no chasing, harassment or fin fraying of pupfish by mosquitofish was recorded, in contrast to reports from closed settings. The author attributes those reports to fence effects.
- **Mortality event:** A large pupfish die-off in one tank was attributed to nitrogen supersaturation ("gas-bubble disease") based on specimen examination; that tank was excluded from further experiments.
- **Conclusions:** Adult pupfish can coexist with adult mosquitofish for at least short periods, provided the habitat has an outlet for emigration and features allow differential resource use. The author could not determine whether coexistence is indefinite, and recommended year-round, long-term experiments in habitats of varying complexity, plus research on the exact mechanisms of species replacement.
