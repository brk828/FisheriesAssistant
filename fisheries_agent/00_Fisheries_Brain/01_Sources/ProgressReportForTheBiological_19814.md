# 1980 Progress Report: Vegetation and Flora Survey of the George Whittell Wildlife Preserve

#ichthyology #marine-biology #botany

*Note on scope: the source concerns terrestrial and riparian plant communities, not fish. The fish-oriented tags above are the mandated template; the content is botanical.*

## Executive Summary

This final 1980 report covers the vegetation and flora component of a long-term biological survey of the [[George Whittell Wildlife Preserve]] in Graham and Pinal counties, Arizona. The survey was funded by the Defenders of Wildlife Trust and began on 15 May 1979. Its objectives were to map vegetation at 1:24,000, compile an annotated flora, establish permanent study plots, photograph those plots, document non-native species (especially [[Salt-cedar]]), and recommend management actions. The survey identified 23 natural vegetation associations plus one successional association, about 320 vascular plant species in 87 to 88 families, and 15 permanent study plots. The authors report heavy livestock grazing in parts of the preserve and a threat from salt-cedar along [[Aravaipa Creek]]. They recommend yearly range monitoring, improved grazing distribution, closure of a livestock exclosure, and mechanical salt-cedar control.

## Background & Description

The preserve lies at the northeastern edge of the [[Sonoran Desert]], in a transition zone between arid desertscrub and wetter vegetation to the north and east. Its diversity is attributed to this transitional position, a 3,600-foot elevational range, varied terrain, and permanent water from streams and seeps. The study design evaluated botanical resources at three levels: species identity and distribution, vegetation association structure, and future change.

The survey team comprised:
- Part I, Vegetation and flora: [[Peter L. Warren]] and L. Susan Anderson (this report)
- Part II, Amphibians and reptiles: Terry B. Johnson
- Part III, Birds: G. Scott Mills
- Part IV, Mammals: Robert B. Spicer

Terry B. Johnson edited the volume as Survey Coordinator. This is the second of three consecutive annual reports. Intensive fieldwork ran from 1979 to 1981, with monitoring every third year from 1984. The report is a preliminary progress document for members of the Trust and is not a publication.

**Methods (summary):**
- **Vegetation mapping** used standardized field forms recording species prominence (1 to 5 scale), cover, height, and terrain. Associations were defined by characteristic species, with a frequency threshold of about 0.7. The map uses a 1:24,000 base built from [[U.S. Geological Survey]] quadrangles. The minimum mapping unit is about 10 acres for contrasting types and 40 acres for less contrasting types.
- **Permanent study plots** are 10 m × 30 m (0.03 ha). Cover was measured along seven 10 m line transects (70 m total) using the line-intercept method ([[Canfield Line-Intercept Method]]). Plots were photographed from eight points each.
- **Flora** was compiled from herbarium specimens (deposited at the University of Arizona), personal collections, and visits roughly once or twice a month over 1.5 to 2 years. Nomenclature follows Lehr (1978) and Benson (1969) for cacti.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Biotic communities and associations.** The survey recognized five communities: Sonoran Desertscrub, Desert Grassland/Semi-desert Scrubland, Chaparral, Evergreen Woodland, and Deciduous Riparian Forest. The 24 mapping units run from the driest to the wettest types. Examples include the [[Paloverde-Brittlebush-Saguaro Association]] on lower south-facing slopes (2500 to 2900 ft), the [[Scrub Oak Chaparral]] on north-facing slopes (3400 to 4700 ft), and the [[Cottonwood-Seep Willow-Willow Association]] along perennial streams. Limestone Scrub was the most restricted type, represented by only two small limestone outcrops. The grassland/scrubland community had the best representation in area and variety.

**Diversity drivers.** The authors attribute high local vegetation diversity to the wide elevational range, geologic variety (limestone, igneous, and metamorphic rock), and the range of landforms from mesas to slickrock terraces and alluvial floodplains. Their comparison is that the preserve supports roughly as many associations as the [[Mazatzal Mountains]] and [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], though those areas are three and fifteen times larger.

**Grazing impact.** Heaviest livestock damage was found in the [[Yucca Shrub-grassland]] on level mesas north of Table Mountain. This association had the lowest species diversity of all sampled associations, and grasses were far more abundant on adjacent hillsides than on the mesas. Grasses declined as grazing increased. The authors note the best native bunchgrass stands are in the [[Blue Grama Shrub-grassland]], which is relatively inaccessible to livestock.

**Exotic species.** Eighteen naturalized introduced species in eleven families were listed in Table 17. Most are common weeds across Arizona. Two of the most abundant are [[Storksbill]] and [[Puncture Vine]]. The authors credit Fred Wood with scattering grass seed across the preserve, which explains the high share of exotic grasses. Salt-cedar was found at widely scattered localities, including seedlings in Aravaipa Creek and near springs in Buzan Canyon. [[Tree-of-heaven]] was spreading along old-field margins on the east end.

## Key Findings

**Vegetation and study plots**
- 23 natural vegetation associations were identified, plus one successional association on cleared floodplain fields (the Old Field Second Growth type). The sampling intensity ranged from 1 site (Limestone Scrub) to 32 sites (Scrub Oak Shrub-grassland). A total of 250 documentation sites were sampled.
- 15 permanent study plots were established: 9 at the west end and 6 at the east end. They span about 1,800 ft of elevation. The Yucca Shrub-grassland plot had the lowest diversity. Cover ranged from 32.7% (Semi-desert Scrub with Juniper) to 126.5% (Mesquite Bosque). Riparian plots showed multi-layer canopy cover above 100%.
- The Mesquite Bosque was the densest woody type, but it had the lowest local species diversity of the sampled associations.

**Flora**
- About 320 vascular plant species in 87 to 88 families were recorded (the summary and the introduction differ slightly on family count). The authors estimate this includes more than half of the species eventually expected, and possibly "more than 1/2" in the methods section.
- Range extensions were documented for [[Yewleaf Willow]] (Salix taxifolia), [[Lowell Ash]] (Fraxinus lowellii), and [[Bird-beak]] (Cordylanthus laxiflorus). Bird-beak was a new record for Graham County.
- 25 species are introduced exotics according to the summary, while the flora section cites 18 naturalized species in Table 17. The discrepancy is in the source.

**Discussion and recommendations**
- Livestock grazing has substantially altered the vegetation, mainly by reducing perennial bunchgrasses and lowering species diversity. The authors state that the extent cannot be fully evaluated without livestock-free comparison areas.
- Salt-cedar ([[Tamarix pentandra]]) is the exotic of greatest concern because it displaces native riparian species. Chemical control reduces above-ground growth but does not eliminate populations. Mechanical removal below ground level is the recommended option, and it is feasible on [[Aravaipa Creek]] only while the species is not well established. The authors stress that control must be watershed-wide to prevent re-establishment from upstream.
- The authors recommend: (1) yearly range condition monitoring by a qualified range scientist with recommended stocking rates; (2) better livestock distribution, such as placing salt blocks away from water; (3) repair and closure of a 50-acre livestock exclosure in upper Virgus Canyon; and (4) mechanical salt-cedar removal along streams, with annual surveys and a cooperative watershed program.
- The authors note that they do not take a position on whether the semi-desert scrubland is a natural climax or a grazing-induced disclimax. They describe associations by present structure only.

**Plans for 1981 (Appendix IV)**
- Few or no changes to the vegetation map are expected. A few sites will be resampled.
- No new study plots are planned.
- Flora collection will emphasize spring (March to May) and late summer (September and October) blooms.
- No major new exotic species are expected. Salt-cedar locations will continue to be documented.
