# Endangered Fish Management and Trout Life History in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Desert Fishes Council proceedings entry (1997 symposium, published January 30, 1999) contains three bilingual papers (English with Spanish summaries) on fishes of the [[Colorado River]] system. The first paper, by Marsh, sets out a method for deriving quantitative interim recovery goals for four federally listed endangered fishes of the upper basin using [[Minimum Viable Population]] analysis. The second, also by Marsh (Arizona State University), reports on the survival of stocked [[Bonytail]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]]. The third, from Brigham Young University, compares the life history of [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] across a metapopulation in the Sheep Creek drainage of the Uinta Mountains, Utah. The text is an OCR scan with garbled passages, so some figures below are read from partly legible text.

## Background & Description

**Minimum viable population paper.** Four endemic fishes of the upper [[Colorado River Basin]] are federally listed as endangered: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*). The abstract notes that the 1978 draft recovery plan for the Colorado pikeminnow called for quantifiable recovery goals. The paper's stated aim is to propose interim quantifiable management objectives for all four species and to explain how they could be set. Such goals must be scientifically defensible and also acceptable to resource managers who weigh other human-use values.

**Lake Mohave repatriation paper.** Bonytail and razorback suckers of varying size and age have been stocked into [[Lake Mohave]] since the early 1980s. About a decade after the first stockings, the multi-agency [[Native Fish Work Group]] decided to replace the aging adult razorback sucker population with 50,000 young fish. The plan had three parts: (1) use wild-caught razorback sucker larvae reared on site, (2) stock fish large enough to be beyond the size range of most predators, and (3) run a follow-up program to measure success in terms of recruitment to the adult stock and genetic diversity.

**Cutthroat trout paper.** The study examines life history variation in the [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] metapopulation of the Sheep Creek drainage in the Uinta Mountains of northeastern Utah. It was carried out alongside concurrent studies of mitochondrial DNA haplotype and protein variation.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Lake Mohave paper describes a reservoir system in which stocked native fishes are recaptured through many efforts by a range of agencies and individuals. The cutthroat trout paper describes an alpine stream environment, where the authors state that the harsh alpine climate limits fish growth and life-history traits. Stocked [[Rainbow Trout]] are also present in that study system and are compared with the native trout.

The minimum viable population paper rests on the premise that population size must exceed the minimum effective population size. Maintaining that number depends on temporal variance in reproductive success for each species and on environmental variability within the river system.

## Key Findings

**1. Quantitative recovery goals (Marsh, minimum viable population paper)**
- The author proposes interim, quantifiable management objectives for all four endangered Colorado River fishes.
- The approach uses life-cycle data and demographic parameters built into life-cycle models to set population size targets based on species-specific effective population sizes.
- Key drivers identified are the temporal variance in reproductive success and the variability of the environment.
- The author presents the work as a template for setting goals for endangered fishes in the upper basin. The full numerical targets are not present in the legible text.

**2. Survival of stocked razorback suckers in Lake Mohave (Marsh, repatriation paper)**
- Preliminary results, based on data through 1996, indicate about 56 percent survivorship of 4,580 stocked razorback suckers.
- This gives a repatriated population of 2,575 individuals.
- Numbers through autumn 1997 were to be added to a final abstract.
- The program aims to replace aging adults with about 50,000 young fish, using wild-caught larvae reared on site.

**3. Status of bonytail (Marsh, repatriation paper)**
- Recaptures of stocked bonytail are too few to support a population estimate.
- Some repatriated fish are encountered, but the author states that the species is in "extreme jeopardy."
- Acquiring and maintaining wild broodfish is identified as the top priority for bonytail recovery.

**4. Life history of Colorado River cutthroat trout (Brigham Young University paper)**
- Fish were collected in spring 1996 from seven sites in the Sheep Creek drainage, Uinta Mountains. The site count is partly legible in the scan.
- Age was determined by counting annuli. Annulus formation was validated using fish from one site.
- Growth rates and fecundity (the number of ova per gram of ovary) were compared across sites.
- Few native fish were older than a few years, and growth is limited by the harsh alpine climate.
- Stocked rainbow trout, aged 16 to 18 months, were compared with the native cutthroat trout in the same system.

**Overall conclusions**
- Quantitative goals for endangered fishes should draw on population-level theory (effective population size, variance in reproduction), not on fixed numbers alone.
- Stocking can supply large numbers of adults, but recruitment and genetic diversity must be monitored to judge success.
- Species with very few recaptures, such as bonytail, depend first on securing wild broodstock.
