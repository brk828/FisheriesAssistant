## Executive Summary

This 1956 study by Jack S. Dendy compares the abundance and vertical distribution of bottom-dwelling invertebrate fauna in two fertilized experimental fish ponds in Auburn, Alabama, stocked under contrasting predator-prey regimes: one with piscivorous [[Largemouth Bass]] only (Bass-only pond), and one with a combination of [[Largemouth Bass]] plus insectivorous [[Bluegill]] and [[Shellcracker]] (Bass-Bluegill pond). Despite nearly identical fertilization regimes producing similar basic productivity, the two ponds diverged sharply in benthic invertebrate standing crop and, critically, in harvestable fish yield. The Bass-only pond accumulated massive standing stocks of chironomid larvae (particularly [[Chironomus plumosus]]) and phantom midge larvae ([[Chaoborus]] spp.) that were poorly utilized, yielding low angler harvests (20–42 lbs/acre). The Bass-Bluegill pond, in contrast, showed heavy predation pressure that suppressed benthic standing crop but produced substantially higher hook-and-line yields (132–169 lbs/acre). The study demonstrates that bottom fauna abundance alone is an unreliable proxy for pond productivity in terms of harvestable fish biomass, and that trophic structure (presence/absence of insectivorous fish) is a critical mediating factor.

## Background & Description

While this paper is primarily ecological rather than morphometric, it records several size/volume-based metrics relevant to invertebrate prey items and fish specimens examined:

- **Fish sampled:**
  - [[Bluegill]] specimens examined for stomach contents ranged 1–7 inches total length (43 of many contained sufficient food for analysis).
  - [[Largemouth Bass]] specimens examined ranged 3–11 inches total length (29 contained food).

- **Invertebrate prey volume (average volume per individual, cc):**
  - *Chaoborus* spp. larvae: ranged from ~0.0006 to ~0.00225 cc per individual depending on season and pond; generally larger in the Bass-only pond.
  - *Chironomus plumosus* larvae: ranged from ~0.0006 to ~0.0311 cc per individual, with consistently larger average individual size in the Bass-only pond compared to the Bass-Bluegill pond (Table III).

- **Extreme density observations:**
  - Bass-only pond, 15 ft depth (Dec 1, 1947): up to 1,014 *Chaoborus* individuals per 36 sq. in. sample (≈4,056 individuals/ft², 7.64 cc/ft²).
  - Bass-only pond, 6 ft depth (July 22, 1949): 89 *Chironomus plumosus* larvae/pupae per sample, 2.25 cc (≈356 individuals/ft², 9.00 cc/ft²).
  - During stratification, average *C. plumosus* volume at 6 ft was 7.5 cc/ft² in the Bass-only pond vs. <0.1 cc/ft² in the Bass-Bluegill pond.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat & Study Sites:**
- Two ponds at the Alabama Polytechnic Institute Agricultural Experiment Station, Auburn, AL: "S-15" (Bass-only, 5.7 acres) and "S-6" (Bass-Bluegill, 26 acres), both with maximum depth ~17 ft. A third pond, Lake Auburn (12 acres), was included for comparative context.
- Both ponds underwent seasonal thermal and chemical (oxygen) stratification in summer, creating an oxygen-poor profundal zone that fluctuated in depth and severity through the season.
- Surface-to-bottom temperature gradients ranged from 80–90°F (surface) to 60–65°F (bottom) in summer.
- Oxygen concentration in the upper 5 ft rarely fell below 1 ppm; deeper waters frequently became hypoxic (<1 ppm), as tracked via Foxboro resistance thermometry and Swingle-Johnson water analysis methods.

**Trophic Structure & Refuge Dynamics:**
- The profundal (deep, oxygen-poor) zone functions as a partial refuge for benthic invertebrates from fish predation, consistent with prior findings by [[Eggleton 1931]] and [[Ball And Hayne 1952]].
- However, this refuge is incomplete: [[Bluegill]] have been shown (Byrd 1952) to tolerate low-oxygen water for extended periods (up to 24+ hours at ~6 ft depth), allowing them to forage into marginally hypoxic zones that would otherwise protect invertebrates.
- In the Bass-only pond (lacking insectivorous fish), *Chironomus plumosus* became heavily concentrated at 5–7 ft depth (peak at 6 ft) during stratification — a pattern absent in the Bass-Bluegill pond, directly attributable to the foraging pressure of bluegill in that depth zone.
- Both *C. plumosus* and *Chaoborus* were generally more abundant in deep water than shallow, with *C. plumosus* peaking sharply at 11 ft and declining abruptly at 12 ft in both ponds (mechanism unexplained by the data).
- *Chaoborus* larvae exhibit diel vertical migration, moving from deep water by day to surface strata at night — making them accessible to bass in oxygenated surface water despite otherwise inhabiting the hypoxic refuge zone by day.

**Diet Analysis:**
- [[Bluegill]] stomach contents were dominated by chironomid larvae/pupae by volume; smaller individuals fed heavily on small *Tanytarsus* larvae; ~25% of bluegill examined (regardless of size) had consumed substantial *Chaoborus* larvae/pupae — contrasting with earlier reports (Howell 1942; Ball 1948) that bluegill do not eat *Chaoborus*.
- [[Largemouth Bass]] stomachs contained mostly large chironomid pupae (presumably *C. plumosus*) and *Chaoborus* larvae/pupae, with relatively few chironomid larvae — suggesting bass captured pupae at the surface during emergence rather than foraging directly on the bottom, and may have scavenged dead/dying larvae during severe stagnation events.

**Other Benthic Taxa (minor components):**
- Chironomidae: *Procladius* spp. (2–5 ft), *Tanytarsus* spp. (2–4 ft, peak at 2 ft), *Clinotanypus* spp. (3–4 ft), *Cryptochironomus* spp. (2–3 ft), miscellaneous Chironomidae (1–16 ft).
- Ceratopogonidae larvae common at ≤6 ft but low volume.
- Trichoptera (*Oecetis*), Ephemeroptera, Odonata, Coleoptera, Hydracarina, Ostracoda, Gastropoda: rare, mostly shallow water.
- Turbellaria (*Dugesia* sp.) and *Hydra* sp.: seasonally abundant in shallow water but low volumetric contribution.

## Key Findings

1. **Fertilization parity, ecological divergence:** Both ponds received approximately equal fertilizer inputs (Table I: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium in varying combinations across 1947–1949), establishing comparable basic (primary) productivity, yet fish yield and invertebrate standing crop diverged dramatically based on fish community structure alone.

2. **Stratification effect on benthos:** During summer stratification, bottom fauna in the Bass-only pond concentrated strikingly at 5–7 ft depth (peak at 6 ft), while no such concentration occurred in the Bass-Bluegill pond — attributed to active bluegill predation at that depth despite marginal hypoxia.

3. **Non-stratified periods:** When ponds were not stratified, benthic fauna became more widely dispersed, and Bass-Bluegill pond invertebrate populations increased substantially compared to stratified periods.

4. **Fish yield outcomes:**
   - Bass-Bluegill pond: 169 lbs/acre (1949), 132 lbs/acre (1950) hook-and-line harvest.
   - Bass-only pond: only 42 lbs/acre (1949), 20 lbs/acre (1950); draining in late 1950 yielded only 33 lbs/acre total.
   - This demonstrates that presence of an efficient insectivore (bluegill) linking invertebrate production to harvestable predator (bass) biomass is essential for maximizing yield.

5. **Bottom fauna abundance ≠ productivity:** High standing crops of benthic invertebrates in the Bass-only pond did NOT translate to high fish yield — the bass alone were inefficient converters of the invertebrate food base. Conversely, in the overcrowded, under-fertilized [[Lake Auburn]] comparison pond, benthic organisms were nearly absent (over-consumed by an overabundant bluegill population) and fish yield to anglers was also poor — showing that *both* extremes (too little or too much insectivore pressure) reduce productivity outcomes.

6. **Optimal trophic balance:** Maximum harvestable yield occurred when piscivore (bass) population size was sufficient to control insectivore (bluegill) abundance, maintaining a balanced supply of invertebrate food for both trophic levels — supporting a "balanced pond" management model for fisheries productivity.

7. **Methodological implication:** The authors conclude that benthic invertebrate surveys alone are insufficient and potentially misleading indicators of pond fish productivity; interpretation requires concurrent knowledge of fish population structure, feeding guild composition, and other ecological/limnological factors (e.g., oxygen stratification dynamics).

8. **Novel dietary observation:** This study's finding that bluegill actively consume *Chaoborus* larvae/pupae in significant quantities (~25% of specimens examined) contradicts earlier studies ([[Howell 1942]], [[Ball 1948]]) reporting no bluegill predation on this taxon, suggesting geographic or pond-specific variability in bluegill diet plasticity.
