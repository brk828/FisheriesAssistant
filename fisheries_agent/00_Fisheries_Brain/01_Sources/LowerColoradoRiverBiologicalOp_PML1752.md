# Lower Colorado River Biological Opinion Review: Comments on Bonytail Chub and Razorback Sucker Provisions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #xyrauchen-texanus

## Executive Summary

This document is a set of critical comments by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University, Department of Biology) on the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's 1997 [[Biological Opinion]] for [[Lower Colorado River]] operations and maintenance, from [[Lake Mead]] to the Southerly International Boundary. The comments focus mainly on the [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], and touch on the [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]], [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] and [[Flat-tailed Horned Lizard]]. A companion memorandum addresses Provision (RPA) 10, the razorback sucker recruitment requirement in Lake Mead.

Minckley's central argument is that the Biological Opinion is fragmented and single-species oriented, lacking the ecosystem integration the Service itself advocates. He warns that this will lead [[Bureau of Reclamation]] to launch separate, short-term projects that do not complement each other before the [[Multi-species Conservation Plan]] is complete. He argues that [[Non-native Fish]] are the chief barrier to recovery, that small habitats are better investments than a few large ones, and that recruitment and incidental-take requirements are often unmeasurable or unrealistic.

## Background & Description

- **Document type:** Formal review letter with attached comments on the Biological Opinion (dated 1997), plus a memorandum to Mike Walker (Bureau of Reclamation) on RPA 10.
- **Principal species:** Bonytail chub and razorback sucker, both listed as endangered. The razorback sucker is the species with a scientific name given in the source (*Xyrauchen texanus*).
- **Other species addressed:** Southwestern willow flycatcher, Yuma clapper rail and flat-tailed horned lizard, discussed briefly.
- **Governing framework:** The Endangered Species Act, Reasonable and Prudent Alternatives (RPAs) and Reasonable and Prudent Measures (RPMs), a consultation period of about 5 years, and a planned 50-year [[Multi-species Conservation Plan]] (MSCP).
- **Cited sources:** Dowling et al. (1996a, b) on mitochondrial DNA; Minckley et al. (1991) on razorback sucker management; Horn (1997) on nutritional limits to recruitment; Marsh (1994); Modde et al. (1996); Allan & Roden (1978); Jones & Sumner (1954).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and water management.** The Lower Colorado River is a sequence of reservoirs, dams, pumps and diversions. Its "old floodplain" habitats and backwaters once supported these fishes. Minckley argues the historical floodplain was dominated by many small aquatic habitats at different successional stages, to which the biota was adapted. Water bodies of 100 acres or more are hard to manage, and he suggests series of small habitats of about 8 to 10 acres are less complex and cheaper to build and permit.

**Non-native fishes.** Minckley identifies non-native predators and competitors as the dominant threat. He quotes the Biological Opinion (p. 134): habitat improvements for non-native fish make efforts to recruit native fish "largely unproductive," and the habitat value of backwaters is "negated to a large degree" by invasion. Non-native fish can enter through unauthorized stocking, live bait use, or accidental transfer of eggs and larvae by birds such as pelicans.

**Recruitment.** Both species have recruited in the absence of non-native predators and competitors, including in mud-bottomed ponds. Where such predators are present, recruitment has not occurred. Adult razorback suckers persist in the mainstem, reservoirs and some canals, and small "recruits" have been found in Lake Mohave and the lower Little Colorado River.

**Successional change.** Minckley notes that nesting habitat for the flycatcher is restricted to certain stages of ecological succession, which makes permanent protection of specific sites questionable. He proposes that succession itself should be assessed as a long-term "threat."

## Key Findings

### 1. Structural critique of the Biological Opinion
- The document lacks synthesis. Its "ecosystem management" approach reduces to single-species provisions, which Minckley expects will lead to separate working groups and projects with little coordination.
- He calls for a single synthesis of existing knowledge on native and non-native fishes, reviewed by a group of specialists to identify real information gaps, before any research proposals are funded.
- He questions whether Reclamation's Biological Opinion duties and the MSCP's goals are separate, and asks for clarification of how the two timelines (MSCP completion in about 3 years; long-term implementation by May 15, 2001) can work together.

### 2. Short-term provisions for the bonytail chub and razorback sucker
- **Augmentation (Provision 1):** Caution is advised on the genetic variability of stocks from sources other than Lake Mohave, citing Dowling et al. (1996a, b). Minckley notes an ambiguity between "stocking 50,000 fish" and "establishing a population of 50,000 fish," which are different targets.
- **Review and evaluation (Provision 2):** Minckley agrees with this provision.
- **Impoundments (Provision 3):** He strongly supports dedicated water bodies for native fish in the old floodplain and considers 600 acres a reasonable initial figure. He recommends that numerous small water bodies be favored over a few large ones, and that exclusion of non-native fish be the priority.
- **Research funding (Provision 4):** He argues that enough scientific knowledge and technology for recovery already exist, citing Minckley et al. (1991). He calls the provision's wording on habitat preference and competitor/predator management "far too vague" and recommends withdrawing it.

### 3. Habitat and recovery targets
- The Biological Opinion sets a minimum impoundment area of 300 acres, with a 600-acre total as the target, and restricts development to the old floodplain. Minckley notes this excludes much of each species' original range, including some formally designated Critical Habitat.
- He notes that "build at least three impoundments" is general, and that the requirements are negotiable.
- For the bonytail chub, negotiations must occur before unoccupied habitat below Lake Havasu can be stocked.

### 4. Provisions on the Southwestern Willow Flycatcher
- Minckley finds an inequity: Reclamation must pay additive compensation for lost flycatcher habitat, but there is no equivalent requirement to compensate for effects on the bonytail chub and razorback sucker from the same operations.
- He raises questions about protecting specific sites or habitat types for the flycatcher, given the flycatcher's reliance on successional stages.

### 5. Ecosystem-level restoration (Provision 14)
- This provision is the only "ecosystem-level" requirement in the Biological Opinion. Minckley recommends an independent, objective review by a group external to federal and state agencies, assessing biotic communities and life histories before and after modification.
- He judges it improbable that self-sustaining populations of the bonytail chub and razorback sucker can be re-established in the channel while large numbers of non-native predators and competitors remain, since these cannot reasonably be eradicated or sufficiently controlled with present technology.
- He suggests naturalized reaches could still hold breeding adults of high genetic variability.

### 6. Provisions judged impossible or unnecessary
- **Recruitment (Provision 10):** Minckley considers this provision impossible to satisfy and recommends it not be considered further except within continued study of razorback sucker biology.
- **Reintroduction (Provision 9):** He agrees with it.
- **Stranding (RPM 1):** Quantifying spawning and nursery habitat exposed by water-level fluctuations would require "lots of guesswork," given how rare the fish are. He recommends the RPM be amended or suppressed.
- **Dams (RPM 2):** Fish passage and mortality through dams and pumps cannot practically be avoided. He argues that resources would be better spent elsewhere.
- **Entrainment (RPM 3):** He has little objection to evaluating diversions, but considers large canal and pipeline modifications premature.

### 7. Incidental take
- Minckley notes the Biological Opinion's distinction between take that precludes recruitment and take causing individual mortality. He argues that any introduction or stocking of non-native fish in a listed species' range could be defined as take, which he says "casts a dark light" on federal and federally supported sport fisheries.
- He notes an inequity: flycatcher eggs, young and nests are protected as individuals, while bonytail and razorback larvae and juveniles are not.
- He argues that assessing the amount of take for these fishes is "essentially impossible," which he thinks makes the RPMs moot.

### 8. Lake Mead razorback recruitment (RPA 10 memorandum)
- The RPA requires Reclamation to determine the conditions that allowed razorback recruitment in Lake Mead, present a plan, and begin implementation by May 15, 2001.
- Minckley says RPA 10 seems based on verbal reports and hearsay rather than published sources. He doubts that the Lake Mead population came from reproduction in the 1930s, but does not rule it out.
- Possible sources of the present fish include recruitment at one or more times in the 60+ years since impoundment, entry from Grand Canyon before the post-1962–63 cold water from Lake Powell, or progeny of the original stock reported as plentiful from the late 1930s to the early 1970s. He notes a reported small razorback sucker that was released, with no photo or other documentation located.
- He reports that razorback sucker lifespan may be 40–50+ years, and eggs number about 1,800 per cm of standard length. Minckley illustrates that with an assumed survival change from 0.1% to 1.0%, a theoretical population could more than double from 1,000 to over 2,000 individuals in one generation.
- He identifies two prerequisites that he considers unlikely to be met: determining individual ages without sacrificing endangered fish (otoliths), and obtaining very detailed river and reservoir operations data. He notes that recreating the conditions of the 1950s would depend on the historic filling of Lake Mohave (partial filling, drainage and final filling in 1954).
