# Riparian Habitat and Instream Flow Studies: Lower Verde River, Fort McDowell Reservation, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Centrarchidae #Salmonidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 report by the [[Riparian Habitat Analysis Group]] of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 2, Albuquerque) evaluates the riparian and aquatic resources of the lower [[Verde River]] along the [[Fort McDowell Reservation]], Arizona. It argues that riparian vegetation is economically and ecologically valuable, and that the fishery and riparian forest depend on sustained instream flow. Its central recommendation is that a minimum flow of 200 cubic feet per second (cfs) be maintained at all times, with no instance below 100 cfs during drought, to protect fish habitat, aquifer recharge for the [[City of Phoenix Well Field]], and remaining [[Cottonwood]] stands.

## Background & Description

The report is a joint study by McNatt, Hallock and Anderson, transmitted by a cover letter from the Albuquerque office. It is organized in three parts: riparian values, aquatic resources and instream flows, and riparian vegetation with groundwater.

- **Riparian value:** The authors state that about 15 percent of original riparian habitat remains in Arizona, and less when only Sonoran Desert riparian areas are considered. The Fort McDowell reach is described as a rare mix of cottonwood, mesquite and other vegetation.
- **Economic values cited:** Wildlife observation (over 46,000 visits in 1978 to three southern Arizona riparian areas, about $5,143,000 in tourist expenditure), dove hunting (over $23,000,000 in Arizona in 1978), honey production, grazing, fuelwood, flood control and water-quality maintenance.
- **Methodology:** The [[Incremental Analysis Method]] of the [[Instream Flow Group]] is used. It combines hydraulic simulation of the reach, composite probability-of-use curves, and [[Weighted Usable Area]] (WUA) calculations for each species and life stage.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Riparian-aquatic linkage.** The report states that much of the energy in aquatic food webs originates in riparian systems. Detritus from riparian vegetation supports insects and fish, and the [[Bald Eagle]], an endangered species, feeds on Verde River fishes including channel catfish, carp and suckers. Fish remains around eagle nests comprised 26 percent carp, and channel catfish plus native suckers collectively almost 70 percent.

**Bird diversity.** The report cites 166 bird species known to frequent riparian habitats in the region (Johnson et al. 1979). Ohmart (1976) recorded 102 species using riparian habitat within the [[Orme Dam]] reservoir site, with as many as 89 species affected if vegetation is eliminated. The Verde River is described as having among the highest bird population densities in North America.

**Hydrology.** Before 1939 the Verde was free-flowing, with a mean monthly flow of 791 cfs; the lowest recorded mean daily flow was 29 cfs. Since [[Bartlett Dam]] was completed in 1939–40, mean monthly flow has been 181 cfs, but flows are highly erratic. In 1977 the river was essentially dewatered below the dam; the USGS gauge at Highway 87 recorded zero flow for 26 consecutive days in May and June.

**Temperature.** At 17 cfs, water released at 58°F had warmed to 83°F ten miles downstream. Summer temperatures regularly exceeded 80°F at flows under 200 cfs, and mean temperatures near the dam were at or above 70°F in late summer and early fall.

**Groundwater.** The USGS (McDonald et al. 1945) found the Phoenix well field is recharged mainly by the Verde. Silt-free recharge is about 2 feet per day over the wetted area, and recharge from river flow of 200 cfs, possibly as low as 100 cfs, sustains the full pumping rate of 7–8 million gallons per day without lowering the water table.

**Vegetation decline.** Cottonwood mortality since 1977 averaged 45 percent along the reservation reach, reaching 60–84 percent in the four-mile reach below Sycamore Creek. Mortality was much lower above Sycamore Creek (17 percent) and nearly absent along lower Sycamore Creek, where the water table stayed high.

## Key Findings

- **Riparian value:** The Fort McDowell reach is considered extremely valuable for wildlife, recreation, and as a renewable resource for grazing, fuelwood and honey.
- **Fish community:** Sixteen species were recorded in the 1975–76 survey. The most abundant was the introduced [[Red Shiner]]; the sixth most abundant was [[Channel Catfish]]. Largemouth bass, bluegill, green sunfish and smallmouth bass are poorly suited to flows of 200 cfs or more, with WUA declining rapidly above about 200 cfs.
- **Native fishes:** Sonora and desert suckers were abundant but no curves exist for them, so white sucker curves were used as a surrogate. [[Roundtail Chub]] is rare, with no young observed, and benefits from flows of 200–300 cfs.
- **Trout:** Rainbow trout (stocked, roughly 2,000 per month November–March) and brown trout show maximum WUA at about 200–300 cfs. Rainbows lost 77 percent of WUA at 50 cfs versus 28 percent for browns.
- **Carp and suckers:** Carp WUA peaks at 50–100 cfs; sustained flows of 200–300 cfs would likely reduce carp abundance.
- **Invertebrates:** Riffle cobble areas above Sycamore Creek contain the greatest diversity, while sand and gravel below it contain few organisms.
- **Groundwater:** Maintaining at least 100 cfs (200 cfs preferred) sustains aquifer recharge and the riparian community.
- **Cottonwood regeneration:** Natural regeneration has largely failed because reservoir operations eliminated the spring floods required for seed germination.
- **Recommendation:** The USFWS recommends a minimum flow of 200 cfs at all times, with no instance below 100 cfs under drought, plus possible transplanting to reestablish stands.
