## Executive Summary

This 1988 summary report documents a cooperative field effort on [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona-Nevada border) targeting the endangered [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), with secondary data collection on [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) populations. Using electrofishing and trammel netting across five sampling camps, the team captured only a single bonytail (which subsequently died en route to Dexter National Fish Hatchery from capture-stress-related causes) but successfully tagged at least 660 razorback suckers. Comparative catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) analysis spanning 1975–1988 indicates no statistically significant change in razorback sucker abundance over 14 years, and no significant increase in striped bass abundance since their introduction into the system was first detected in 1981 samples. The report underscores the establishment of a robust long-term baseline dataset for future trend detection in this reservoir ecosystem.

## Background & Description

Limited direct morphological data are reported; the paper is primarily a population-monitoring and tagging summary. Notable size-related observations include:

- **[[Striped Bass]]**: Individuals sampled since 1981 have consistently spanned a substantial size range, from approximately **20 cm total length** to **more than 75 cm total length**, indicating a persistent multi-cohort population structure rather than a single recruitment pulse.
- **[[Bonytail]]**: No morphometric data recorded for the single captured specimen; it died from presumed capture-stress-related causes prior to detailed examination, though scale samples and vital statistics were collected for striped bass and forwarded to Arizona Game and Fish Department (Kingman office).
- No length/weight data provided for [[Razorback Sucker]], though the largest historical dataset (1975–present) exists for this species via consistent trammel-netting methodology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Lake Mohave]] functions as a critical refugial habitat within the lower Colorado River system for two federally significant catostomid/cyprinid taxa:

- **[[Bonytail]]** (*Gila elegans*): Extremely rare in current sampling (n=1 across the entire effort), reflecting its critically endangered status in this reservoir; historically a Colorado River big-river cyprinid, now largely dependent on hatchery supplementation (Dexter National Fish Hatchery) for population recovery.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*): The dominant target species, captured across all five sampling camps in both electrofishing and trammel-net efforts, indicating a broadly distributed but numerically stable population within the lake. CPUE stability since 1975 suggests the population, while endangered, is not currently declining under existing lake conditions.
- **[[Striped Bass]]** (*Morone saxatilis*): A non-native piscivore established in Lake Mohave since at least 1981, co-occurring with native and other introduced species (carp, rainbow trout, channel catfish, largemouth bass, bluegill, green sunfish, black crappie). Its stable, wide size-range population suggests ongoing reproduction/recruitment within the reservoir, with potential predatory interactions with native catostomids.
- The broader trammel-net community data reveal a habitat dominated numerically by [[Carp]] (55.8% of total catch) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (11.2%), with [[Razorback Sucker]] comprising 18.5% of total trammel-net catch — highlighting significant niche overlap and competition/predation pressure from introduced species in this altered reservoir ecosystem.

## Key Findings

- **Bonytail capture**: Only 1 individual captured during the entire 1988 effort; transported to Dexter National Fish Hatchery but died from capture-stress-related causes — underscoring the extreme rarity of this species in Lake Mohave.
- **Razorback sucker tagging**: At least 660 individuals tagged, representing a substantial mark-recapture effort for long-term population monitoring.
- **Electrofishing results** (razorback sucker only, Table 1): Total of 327 fish captured across 4 camps (Camp 2 excluded due to no data) over 496.8 minutes of effort; overall catch rate of 0.66 fish/minute, with high camp-to-camp variability (0.24–1.42 fish/minute).
- **Trammel netting results** (Table 2): Total of 1,615 fish captured across 4 camps (139.94 net units), with Carp (55.8%) and Razorback Sucker (18.5%) as the two most numerically dominant taxa; Bonytail represented by a single individual (0.06% of catch).
- **Long-term CPUE trends**: Razorback sucker abundance in trammel-net samples has remained statistically unchanged since 1975 — a 14-year stable baseline.
- **Striped bass trend**: No statistically significant increase in abundance detected since first appearing in sampling in 1981; population spans a wide size range (20–75+ cm TL), suggesting ongoing recruitment.
- **Data quality note**: Camp 4 trammel-net data (15 razorback suckers, CU=1.6, 9.41 units) were excluded from compiled totals due to incomplete total-catch recording.
- **Methodological value**: Consistent use of standardized trammel-netting methodology since 1975 has produced a robust baseline dataset, positioning researchers to detect future population changes in both [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Striped Bass]] with statistical confidence.
- **Logistical support**: Bureau of Land Management communication infrastructure was highlighted as critical to the operational success of this multi-camp cooperative sampling effort.
