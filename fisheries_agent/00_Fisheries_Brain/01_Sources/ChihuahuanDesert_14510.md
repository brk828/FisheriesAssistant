# 90 Years and 535 Miles: Vegetation Change Along the Mexican-American Border

#botany #vegetation-dynamics #chihuahuan-desert

## Executive Summary

This excerpt from Robert R. Humphrey's book compares historic photographs of International Boundary monuments (erected in the 1890s) with vegetation observed by the author in summer 1983. The survey covers Monuments 73 through 81 in the [[Chihuahuan Desert]], from the riparian bottom of [[Guadalupe Canyon]] to the ridges of the [[Perilla Mountains]]. The main pattern is a shift from grass-dominated or mixed grass-scrub cover toward shrub dominance, especially on upland sites, with the strongest contrasts seen where a boundary fence has produced different grazing pressures on either side of the line. The author also argues that an 1890s government report understated shrub cover and overstated grass cover.

## Background & Description

The text is a photographic and ecological revisit to a series of boundary monuments along the US–Mexico line. Each entry pairs an early photograph with the author's 1983 field observations, and most entries list the dominant plants present at the time. The method is essentially [[Repeat Photography]] combined with species inventories and visual estimates of grass versus shrub cover. No quantitative cover values, plot counts or sample sizes are reported. Scientific names are largely not given; the only genus named is *Sphaeralcea* (a mallow).

The document also cites [[Senate Document 247]] (Part II, p. 187), which described the vegetation near several monuments, and a 1958 [[U.S. Geological Survey]] quadrangle map that the author used to reconcile place names.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Grazing history:** Long-continued, heavy grazing occurred near Monument 73 in both Mexico and the United States, linked to a nearby ranch home and reliable canyon water. At Monument 76, boundary fencing created marked contrasts in [[Grazing Pressure]] between the two sides.
- **Riparian zone:** Trees and shrubs along the Guadalupe Canyon stream probably changed little and may have become denser.
- **Upland and bajada sites:** These show the most consistent change, with grasses declining and shrubs increasing.
- **Wetland past:** The San Bernardino valley near Monument 77 was described historically as flat, marshy, grassy, spring-fed and pooled. By 1983 the monument stood in a dense brush stand.
- **Palatability:** The author notes [[Bush Muhly]] as highly palatable and mentions it occurring alongside more heavily grazed [[Tobosagrass]] on the US side at Monument 76.

## Key Findings

**Monument 73 (riparian, Guadalupe Canyon)**
- Riparian woody cover included velvet mesquite, algerita, snakeweed, desert broom, western hackberry, and sycamore and velvet ash along the watercourse.
- Hillside scrub included ocotillo, squawbush, beebrush, banana yucca and Palmer agave, with only a few perennial grasses (sideoats grama and tanglehead).
- The hillside appears to have shifted from nearly pure grass to scrub-dominated cover.

**Monuments 74–76 (bajada and hilltop sites)**
- No significant vegetation change was seen since erection, but amole at Monument 74 has increased and is now dominant. A single palmilla plant appears in the 1893 picture and none was found in 1983; the author considers this a minor individual difference.
- At Monument 75, the Mexican side was dominated by snakeweed, velvet mesquite and huajilla, while the US side had lighter grazing and two grasses: tobosagrass (widespread) and black grama (local).
- At Monument 76, grazing contrasts across the fence produced plant differences: the heavier-grazed Mexican side was dominated by snakeweed, creosotebush, tarbush and other shrubs, while the US side added gray coldenia and desert zinnia and held more tobosagrass mixed with bush muhly.
- The author states that the early photographs contradict the 1890s report's claim of "a moderate growth of grass beside the usual varieties of cacti": the pictures show no visible grass or cacti, and scrub is clearly dominant.

**Monument 77 (San Bernardino valley)**
- The 1890s report describes a flat, grassy, marshy valley with springs and pools. The author reconciled the river name with a mapped drainage called Black Draw.
- In 1983 the area had an almost pure overstory of velvet mesquite, a scattering of four-wing saltbush, and an understory of snakeweed, with no perennial grasses.

**Monuments 78–79 (upland bajada toward the Perilla Mountains)**
- Vegetation appeared essentially unchanged over the roughly 92 years since erection.
- Cover was exclusively scrub, dominated by creosotebush, tarbush, spring whitethorn, mariola, Englemann prickly pear, snakeweed, desert holly and gray coldenia. No perennial grasses were recorded.

**Monument 80 (north-facing Perilla Mountains ridge)**
- Compared with the earlier photograph, grass cover has been almost completely lost and shrubs have increased.
- Whitethorn acacia and snakeweed are most abundant, with prickly pear, cane cholla, squawbush, Palmer agave, velvet mesquite, banana yucca, odora and huajilla also present.
- Only two grasses were recorded, in very minor amounts: curly mesquite and threeawn.

**Monument 81 (highest point of the boundary, Perilla Mountains)**
- Located 0.83 mile west of Monument 80; reaching it required about 35 minutes of hiking over steep terrain.
- The 1893 photograph is too limited to confirm grass presence, but the author infers a marked decline in grasses and an increase in shrubs and woody plant size.
- Present cover is dominated by whitethorn acacia and Englemann prickly pear, with ocotillo, banana yucca, Palmer agave, a mallow (*Sphaeralcea*) and a perennial buckwheat as additional species.
- Slightly more grass occurred on the US side of the fence. Only two grass species were recorded on either side: sideoats grama and bush muhly.

**Overall pattern**
- Shrub and scrub dominance has increased across most upland and bajada sites, while grasses have declined or disappeared, with the clearest losses on the Perilla Mountain ridges.
- Riparian vegetation appears more stable.
- Grazing pressure, as shown by the fence contrasts at Monument 76, is a likely driver, although the document does not test this statistically.
- The 1890s government description of the vegetation is judged unreliable, with the photographs used as the stronger evidence.
