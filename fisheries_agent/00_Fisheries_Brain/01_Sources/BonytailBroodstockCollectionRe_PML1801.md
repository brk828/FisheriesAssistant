# Bonytail Broodstock Collection and Stocking Survival in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada (2004)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service interim report, prepared for the [[National Fish and Wildlife Foundation]] and the Multi-Species Conservation Plan, documents 2004 spring sampling in [[Lake Mohave]] for wild [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), an endangered cyprinid largely extinct in the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Despite roughly 615 fish of nine species captured in trammel nets and 71 fish in hoop nets, no bonytail were caught in 2004. The authors attribute this to sampling variability, poor recruitment, and very low survival of stocked hatchery fish, and they argue that stocking larger fish (>300 mm TL) should be seriously considered.

## Background & Description

Wild bonytail populations are virtually extinct in the Colorado River basin. Historical records came from Grand Canyon, Lake Mead, Lake Mohave, the Colorado River from Parker Dam to Yuma, and the Colorado River delta in Sonora, Mexico. Bonytail collected from Lake Mohave in 1980 were used by researchers to establish [[Hatchery Broodstock]] for stocking.

Bonytail were listed as endangered in 1980, a recovery plan was completed in 1991, and critical habitat was designated in 1994. Hatchery stock traces to a very small founding group of fewer than 10 wild fish collected in 1981 at the [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Those fish were later transferred to the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]. Current broodstock is therefore genetically narrow, and the hatchery has been stocking this material throughout the Colorado River system.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area.** Lake Mohave was sampled on five occasions between 15 March and 28 May 2004. Trammel nets were set near Yuma Cove, from Cottonwood Cove to Davis Dam (RM 30), and in the Arrowhead Cove area, about 5 km north of Katherine Landing. These two areas were the historical capture locations for bonytail.

**Gear and methods.** Trammel nets (100 m × 2 m, 25 cm outer and 5 cm inner mesh) were fished for one to four nights, set perpendicular to shore and run in the morning. Hoop nets (1 m × 2.5 cm mesh) were set without bait in less than 2 m of water among tamarisk. Captured native fish were measured, weighed, scanned for coded-wire and PIT tags, and sampled for fin clips for genetic analysis at [[Arizona State University]]. Non-native fish were counted and released.

**Community composition.** Trammel nets caught nine species, dominated by [[Common Carp]] (29%) and [[Striped Bass]] (25%). The only native fish caught was [[Razorback Sucker]]. Hoop nets caught green sunfish, bluegill, and largemouth bass, with a rainbow trout and a striped bass also recorded.

**Genetic context.** Broodstock diversity is low, so recovering wild-caught individuals could meaningfully improve the genetic representation of hatchery progeny. The authors estimate that adding even one wild fish could increase the genetic effective size by about 20% (Hedrick et al. 2000).

## Key Findings

- **Trammel netting (15 March–28 May 2004):** 105 net sets (12-hour), totaling 1,050 m of net and 1,200 net-hours. Total catch was 615 fish, averaging 5.9 fish/net (range 3.7–10.2 across weeks) and 2.0 fish/30 m of net.
- **Trammel-net species composition:** Nine species, in decreasing order: carp (29%), striped bass (25%), [[Threadfin Shad]], razorback sucker (the only native), largemouth bass, green sunfish, [[Yellow Bullhead]], channel catfish, and [[Red Shiner]]. Only one native fish (razorback sucker) was captured.
- **Hoop-netting (29 March–28 May 2004):** 49 sets (12-hour; 576 net-hours), totaling 71 fish, averaging 1.5 fish/net. Species were green sunfish, bluegill, and largemouth bass.
- **Bonytail capture:** No bonytail were captured in 2004. Five recaptured bonytail were recorded from 2001–2004 (three in 2001, one each in 2002 and 2003; none in 2004). One of these was a fish with a haplotype matching a Lake Mohave stocked fish that later died; a second originated from the Arrowhead Cove area.
- **Stocking history:** 180,129 bonytail have been stocked in Lake Mohave since 1981. Between 1981 and 1995, 155,005 fish (86%) were 72–102 mm TL. The remaining 25,124 (14%), stocked 1996–2002, averaged 250 mm TL.
- **Historical catch:** Between 1980 and 1996, 91 bonytail were captured from Lake Mohave (about 5.7 per year).
- **Razorback sucker comparison:** In March 2001, about 3,954 wild adult razorback suckers were estimated in Lake Mohave. Survival of juveniles strongly depended on stocking size: fish >350 mm were recaptured nearly ten times more often than those <300 mm.
- **Key inference:** The authors estimate roughly 2,600 post-1999 bonytail could have reached a survivable size, but few were stocked above 300 mm, so the number may be lower. The authors argue the failure to capture bonytail likely reflects poor survival of small releases.

**Recommendations**

1. Continue monitoring for bonytail in May in the Cottonwood East basin and near Arrowhead Cove, and extend sampling to March and April where possible.
2. Increase bonytail stocking length to >300 mm TL throughout the lower Colorado River basin.
3. Add any Lake Mohave bonytail >400 mm TL to broodstock.
4. Create an interagency genetics team to track bonytail genetics in the lower basin.
5. Explore other capture methods for Lake Mohave bonytail.
6. Restart telemetry studies to focus sampling and clarify movement and habitat needs.
