# Feud Flares Up Over Plan to Shut Down Washington Dam System to Save Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1999 *Wall Street Journal* news article examines a proposal to breach (remove) four dams on the lower [[Snake River]] in Washington and Idaho to restore endangered [[Chinook Salmon]] and [[Sockeye Salmon]] runs. The article frames the dispute as a conflict between potential winners (environmentalists, trucking and highway construction firms) and potential losers (farmers, barge operators, electric utilities). Federal economic studies are pending, and the article reports conflicting impact estimates from both sides. Congress is expected to decide on funding the breaching the following year, after the [[Army Corps Of Engineers]] releases its economic study in December 1999.

## Background & Description

The [[Lower Granite Dam]] is one of eight dams spanning the 465-mile [[Snake River]] and [[Columbia River]] system in the [[Pacific Northwest]]. Built by the [[Army Corps Of Engineers]] in the 1960s and 1970s for hydroelectric power, the dams support a substantial economy:

- Lewiston, Idaho functions as an inland port, with about 50 million bushels of grain loaded onto barges annually, mostly bound for Asia.
- The hydroelectric plants produce about 11% of the power used in the region, from Washington to Montana.

The four dams targeted for removal are Lower Granite, [[Little Goose Dam]], [[Lower Monumental Dam]], and [[Ice Harbor Dam]]. Their removal would free about 130 miles of the currently dammed river. The Clinton administration has decided that salmon restoration now outweighs the need for these dams. The Corps is also studying the larger [[Columbia River]] dams, but no one has yet proposed breaching them.

Salmon are described as endangered in the region. Federal biologists estimate that removing the four dams would allow at least 30,000 Chinook and Sockeye salmon to return to spawn, compared with predam runs of 50,000 or more and recent runs far lower. Idaho officials publicized the return of a single sockeye salmon to the [[Stanley Basin]] in August 1999; only four male salmon completed the trip to the spawning area that year.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article is primarily economic, but it describes several ecological and regulatory pressures:

- **Salmon decline:** Dams block migration routes, and very few fish now reach upstream spawning grounds.
- **Endangered Species Act:** Federal officials must produce a plan by year end to comply with the [[Endangered Species Act]] and save Snake River salmon. Without dam removal, the article warns this could mean sharp limits on timber cutting, irrigation, grazing, and ocean fishing across the Northwest.
- **Water quality:** Compliance with the [[Clean Water Act]] with the dams in place could cost at least an additional $460 million, according to ECONorthwest, a firm working for environmental groups.
- **Disputed salmon returns:** The [[Columbia River Alliance]] estimates only about 2,000 fish would return if the dams were breached, a figure it argues is too small to justify the costs.

## Key Findings

**Economic costs of breaching (opponent and utility estimates):**

- **Power:** The [[Bonneville Power Administration]] (BPA), which distributes power from 30 dams, would lose 1,231 megawatts, or 11% of its total supply. Replacing that power would cost about $250 million annually. BPA must also keep paying $864 million it owes for dam construction. Electric bills would rise by $3.20 to $6.50 per month. BPA has taken no position on breaching, stating that the cost estimate is solid but "the benefits are pretty squishy."
- **Agriculture:** About 35,000 acres of irrigated farmland would lose water, with alternative delivery systems estimated at about $10 million. A 5,000-acre wheat and barley farm in Hooper, Washington, would face about $35,000 in added annual costs, mostly in shipping. Shifting grain from barges to trucks and trains would cost farmers about $35 million annually.
- **Transportation:** Upgrading regional road and rail systems to handle the added load could cost as much as $406 million.
- **Lewiston:** Lowering the Snake from 14 feet to 7 feet at Lewiston would make barge traffic virtually impossible. The city estimates a loss of $117 million annually in river-related business and about 4,500 jobs.

**Economic benefits of breaching (proponent estimates):**

- Increased sport fishing and commercial fishing could add about $390 million annually to the Northwest economy, according to [[American Rivers]].
- Dam removal would create about 10,000 construction jobs, plus additional mitigation-related jobs.
- Environmental advocates concede short-term financial stress but argue for long-term regional benefits.

**Economic cost of not breaching:** Proponents argue that retaining the dams carries large costs too, including Clean Water Act compliance and Endangered Species Act restrictions. The article notes that a separate proposal to spend $7.8 billion on [[Everglades Restoration]] makes breaching the dams look "not too expensive" by comparison.

**Unresolved issues:** The article highlights major uncertainties, including whether salmon will return in meaningful numbers at all, and whether the benefits can be quantified as clearly as the costs. The decisive economic study from the Corps is due in December 1999, and Congress is expected to make the final funding decision in 2000.
