# Distribution and Angler Use of the Roundtail Chub (Gila robusta) in the Upper Verde River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1989 report by Charles D. Ziebell and Richard R. Roy of the [[Arizona Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit]] examines the distribution, seasonal movements and angler use of the [[Roundtail Chub]] in the Upper [[Verde River]], Arizona, from 1987 to 1988. The study was run cooperatively with the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] and the [[Arizona Flycasters Club]]. Roundtail chubs were scarce: 118 were captured, 60 were large enough to tag, and only one tagged fish was recaptured. Most fish were concentrated in a short reach near Perkinsville and the P-6 Ranch. The creel census indicated very low angler success. The authors also documented heavy mortality of fish stranded in irrigation diversion canals during drawdowns, and identified water diversion and habitat alteration as the main threats.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), a native cyprinid of Arizona. It is not protected by any federal or state regulation, but it is taken as a sport fish, and angler use had not been documented.
- **Diet:** Opportunistic omnivore feeding on aquatic insects, amphipods, algae and diatoms; crayfish and fish are sometimes eaten.
- **Habitat:** Usually found in pools and eddies, rarely in swift water.
- **Reproduction:** Spawning begins when water temperature reaches 55 °F (18.3 °C), in pool-riffle areas with sandy-rocky substrates. Females probably become reproductive at about 100–150 mm at roughly two to three years of age. Eggs hatched in 126 hours at 19 to 20 °C, and larval periods lasted up to 53 days.
- **Movement:** Movement depends on stream size. In [[Aravaipa Creek]], individuals moved a maximum distance of about 4.8 km, and more typical movements were shorter.
- **Thermal limit:** Reported lethal temperature limit is 34.4 °C.
- **Local name:** Residents call it the "Verde trout," and it is the only remaining native warmwater fish in Arizona used by fishermen in the area.

The study had two objectives:
1. Determine distribution and movements of the roundtail chub, with emphasis on season, direction, distance and water diversions.
2. Determine fishermen use of the roundtail chub, cooperatively with the Game and Fish Department and the Flycasters Club.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and methods.** The Verde River was sampled at eight stations from Perkinsville to Beasley Flat, covering 85 river kilometers. Fieldwork ran from July 1987 to October 1988, with 24 field trips. Fish were collected with trammel nets, beach seines and a backpack shocker. Two irrigation diversions were also sampled: the O.K. diversion on the Verde River and the Ranger Station diversion on [[Wet Beaver Creek]]. Fish were measured, weighed, scale-sampled and tagged with Floy anchor tags.

**Water diversion effects.** Diversion canals can trap fish as water levels fall. The O.K. diversion was partially dewatered after heavy rains in November 1987 and fully drained in February 1988 for maintenance. The Ranger Station pool dried in February 1988 after winter storms and floods breached the dam. Fish concentrated in shrinking pools, where they were exposed to predation by [[Great Blue Heron]], raccoons and people, and die-offs were frequent. Stranded fish were also reported in irrigated fields, but the reported frequency was limited.

**Habitat.** Most roundtail chubs were associated with riffle-pool habitat. Adults were found in pools about two meters deep, below riffles, with undercut banks and logs or tree roots for cover. Juveniles used riffle, pool and submerged vegetation habitats less than one meter deep. Cross (1978) reported that roundtail chubs in the [[Virgin River]] were endangered by low water levels from irrigation withdrawals.

**Associated fauna.** The Verde River sample included 431 individuals of other species, dominated by red shiners, Sonora suckers, desert suckers and smallmouth bass. Channel catfish and flathead catfish were frequent but less numerous. The O.K. diversion contained 12 species, mostly red shiners and [[Smallmouth Bass]]. Rainbow trout stocked in Wet Beaver Creek occasionally entered the Ranger Station diversion.

## Key Findings

**Roundtail chub abundance and distribution**
- 118 roundtail chubs were captured; 60 were large enough to tag. The smallest tagged fish measured 152 mm TL and the largest 450 mm TL. Most were under 390 mm and weighed under 650 g.
- Distribution was highly concentrated: 85% of fish were found in the Perkinsville and P-6 Ranch area (about 5 km of the 80 km surveyed). P-6 Ranch alone accounted for 64%.
- Scale aging was unsuccessful because no annuli were visible, even under scanning electron microscopy.
- Only one tagged roundtail was recaptured (about 100 m from its tagging site, after about three months). Movement could not be evaluated.
- No roundtail chubs were collected at the Ranger Station diversion. Eleven were found in the O.K. diversion, and nine [[Colorado Squawfish]] were captured there in February 1988.

**Tagging and other species**
- 268 fish were tagged and released overall, 202 of them non-roundtail species.
- Four non-roundtail recaptures occurred, all at the original capture site.

**Creel census (July 1987 to October 1988)**
- Of 300 scheduled weekend counts, 93 were conducted; of 450 scheduled weekday counts, 91 were conducted.
- Mean angler count was 1.52 on weekends and 0.46 on weekdays. Only 23 anglers were interviewed, although 190 anglers were counted fishing.
- Catfish were the most sought species (47%). Any species was acceptable to 30% of anglers.
- Of anglers, 65% had never heard of roundtail chub; 34% said they specifically fished for it.
- Catch per hour was 0.25 fish overall, 0.069 in area A, 0.207 in area C and 0.372 in area E. No fish were caught in areas B or D.
- Estimated catch for the whole study was 1,147 smallmouth bass, 630 roundtail chubs, 611 channel catfish, 250 suckers and 210 carp. About 60% of fish caught were released.
- Fishing effort totaled 191,603 hours over 15 months on 6.7 miles (10.8 km) of river.

**Diversions and management**
- Seventy-six percent of fish in the Ranger Station diversion were smallmouth bass; 69% of fish in the O.K. diversion were red shiners.
- The authors concluded that the diversions provide poor fish habitat and that the habitat differs markedly from the river's riffle-pool habitat.
- Suggested mitigation: rotating screens within the first 100 m of diversions could exclude fish, but cost and maintenance may prohibit this measure.
- The authors identified strong water laws that account for fish needs as the greatest management need for Arizona stream fisheries, because current control over the amount of water diverted for irrigation is minimal.

**Limitations**
- Sample sizes were small. The authors stated that the creel sample was too small to be statistically valid, and that night fishing was not included.
- Only 242 of the scheduled sample periods were conducted (about 7% of the possible 2,550 data sets), although the survey was designed to sample 30%.
