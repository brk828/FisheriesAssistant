# Razorback Sucker and Colorado Squawfish Reintroduction and Monitoring, Salt and Verde Rivers, Arizona (1991–1992)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft Endangered Species Act Section 6 special report, prepared by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) and dated July 15, 1992, documents the stocking, radiotelemetry monitoring, habitat use and water quality work for two endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fishes, the [[Razorback Sucker]] and the [[Colorado Squawfish]], in the Verde and Salt rivers of central Arizona. Hatchery-reared fish were released in 1991–1992, mainly into the Verde River, because telemetry and electrofishing were impeded by high conductivity in the Salt River. The report concludes that reintroduction using larger, older fish has been successful, with long-term survival of tagged fish, restricted movement ranges (generally within about 9 km), and distinct habitat preferences that differ from available habitat.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) are endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Both were historically present in the Gila and Colorado drainages of Arizona.
- **Historical decline:** By 1960 both species were extirpated from the Gila Basin and the lower Colorado River. Razorbacks were last collected in the Verde basin in 1954 and the Salt drainage about 1949; squawfish were last collected in the Salt River system in 1958. Both persisted in the Upper Colorado Basin (Green, Yampa, Colorado rivers) and San Juan River. Relict razorback populations in Lake Mead, Lake Mohave, Lake Havasu and below Glen Canyon Dam were thought to consist mainly of older adults, suggesting greatly reduced reproduction.
- **Listing:** The squawfish was listed as endangered in 1967 and the razorback in 1991.
- **Reintroduction history:** A razorback program began in 1981 under a 10-year Memorandum of Understanding between AGFD and USFWS, initially using larval and juvenile fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Survival was low, largely due to introduced predators such as [[Ictalurid Catfish]]. Squawfish reintroduction began in 1985 under an "experimental, non-essential" designation of the 1984 ESA amendment. The report states that over 600,000 squawfish and 11,800,000 razorbacks had been stocked into the Verde, Salt and Gila drainages by the end of 1990, with few surviving more than one year.
- **Study objectives:** (1) stock hatchery-reared razorbacks and squawfish of varying sizes into suitable Verde and Salt river waters from 1991 to July 1992; (2) monitor post-stocking dispersal and survivorship; (3) determine temporal and spatial movements and habitat use of radio-tagged fish.
- **Study areas:** The Verde River study reach ran from [[Sullivan Lake]] (RKM 145) to Horseshoe Reservoir (RKM 0). The Verde River is 286 km long and drains about 16,896 km². The Salt River reach ran from the Highway 60 bridge (RKM 90) to Gleason Flat (RKM 60). Work focused on the Verde because of Salt River telemetry and electrofishing constraints.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Verde River community:** Native fishes in natural populations include longfin dace, desert sucker, Sonora sucker, [[Gila Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], speckled dace and the federally threatened [[Spikedace]]. At least ten introduced species are established, including red shiner, fathead minnow, [[Common Carp]], channel catfish, smallmouth bass and brown trout. Native species dominate headwaters but decline below Perkinsville.
- **Salt River community:** Only five native fishes persist in natural populations. About nineteen exotic species occur, including northern pike, several buffalo species, flathead catfish and largemouth bass.
- **Vegetation:** Verde headwaters are dominated by seep willow, velvet ash and Goodding willow, with Utah juniper giving way to Fremont cottonwood and Arizona sycamore downstream. The Salt River canyon is shaped by floods that limit riparian vegetation, with Baccharis and salt cedar at the water's edge.
- **Hydrology:** Verde flows are fairly constant in upper reaches and more seasonally variable below the Verde Valley. Salt River discharge is highly variable.
- **Water quality (Verde River, 24-hour measurements weekly from September 1991):**
  - Dissolved oxygen fluctuated most strongly from September to November and April to May, attributed to photosynthesis by river vegetation.
  - pH varied little from September through May; lowest in December (7.48) and highest in April (8.74).
  - Water temperature followed daily and seasonal air temperature.
  - Conductivity was higher in warm months and lower in winter, with a March dip attributed to dilution from February to early March flooding.

## Key Findings

**Stocking and survival**
- Between May 1991 and March 1992, seven release events occurred on the Verde River, mostly at RKM 121.6–120.2 (see Table 1 in source).
- A total of 501 razorbacks (355–660 mm total length) and 10 squawfish (562–669 mm) were released into the Verde River. All were PIT-tagged.
- Eighteen adult razorbacks and ten squawfish received radio transmitters before release.
- Twenty-five of the 501 stocked razorbacks had been recaptured at the time of writing. The authors judged that razorbacks of the size released are surviving fairly well.

**Radiotelemetry and movement**
- Radio-tagged razorbacks travelled a mean cumulative distance of 16.22 km per fish (May 1991–May 1992). Mean downstream movement was 1.11 km/week and mean upstream movement 0.76 km/week.
- Radio-tagged squawfish travelled a mean cumulative distance of 13.24 km per fish (February–May 1992). Mean downstream movement was 1.01 km/week and upstream 0.42 km/week.
- Flood events caused two razorbacks to move 38.5 km and 66.8 km downstream. One squawfish moved 30.1 km in one week.
- Excluding flood effects, both species appear to stay within about 9 km upstream or downstream of their release point.
- Diel patterns: razorbacks moved more at night (16.8 m/h) than by day (8.6 m/h), a significant difference (p < 0.001). Squawfish showed no significant diel difference (14.6 m/h by day, 13.7 m/h at night).

**Telemetry accuracy**
- Single-blind triangulation tests (44 runs, three observers) showed no significant difference among observers (p > 0.05). The 95% confidence interval for longitudinal error was 2.73–4.27 m and for cross-channel error 2.69–4.17 m.

**Habitat**
- Available habitat in the Verde reach was 27.3% pools, 15.0% glides, 7.5% riffles and 40.2% runs.
- Razorbacks occupied pools in 57.5% of observations, glides in 34.7%, runs in 3.7%, backwaters in 2.1% and eddies in 1.5%. Primary substrates were sand or pebble. Mean depth was 0.74 m and mean velocity 0.05 m/s.
- Squawfish occupied glides in 50%, pools in 32.2% and runs in 17.7% of observations. Mean depth was 0.97 m and mean velocity 0.25 m/s, with mainly silt and pebble substrates.
- Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests showed both species used depth, velocity and substrate differently from availability (all p < 0.01).

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The authors conclude that reintroduction with older, larger fish has been successful and that telemetry data on habitat use can help guide future reintroduction and predict fate in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]].
- Further research is needed on the diel movement difference, which may relate to feeding ecology (razorbacks as bottom-feeders, squawfish as piscivores).
- Limited sampling and the unexplained flood-driven movements are noted as constraints on conclusions.
