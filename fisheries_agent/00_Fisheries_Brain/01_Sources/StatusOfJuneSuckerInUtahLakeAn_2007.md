# Status of June Sucker in Utah Lake and Refuges

#ichthyology #Catostomidae #lakesucker #endangered_species

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary:** [[June Sucker]] (*Chasmistes liorus*) is one of four extant lakesucker species in North America (family [[Catostomidae]]), endemic to [[Utah Lake]] and its tributaries. First described by Jordan (1878) from specimens collected in the 1870s, the species was federally listed as endangered in 1986 with critical habitat designated as the lower 7.8 km of the [[Provo River]]. Wild June sucker mature in approximately their fifth year of life based on opercle aging. Evidence from a small number of individuals suggests potential life span exceeding 40 years. The species was historically abundant, supporting large harvests by aboriginal humans and European settlers. Named for spawning runs that historically occurred in June, spawning now occurs mid-April to early May, possibly in response to altered hydrologic conditions from water development. The species exhibits morphological and ecological differences from sympatric [[Utah Sucker]] (*Catostomus ardens*), though molecular analysis suggests recent divergence from a common progenitor. Hybridization between June and Utah suckers has been documented, with timing of hybridization initiation uncertain but possibly intensified during 1930s drought conditions.

**Habitat Associations:** June sucker historically occupied [[Utah Lake]], a shallow (average depth 2.9 m, maximum 4.2 m at compromise elevation) 38,400-ha lake with no thermal stratification. Historic lake conditions included relatively stable water levels, lower turbidity, deeper littoral zone, productive wetland perimeter habitats, and macrophyte beds providing nursery habitat. Current lake conditions are homogeneous and turbid, devoid of aquatic vegetation due to increased nutrient loading, lake level fluctuations, and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) impacts. Spawning habitat was historically more extensive, with several large tributaries (Provo River, Spanish Fork River, Hobble Creek) providing diverse braided, slow, meandering channels with warm, slow water pools and marsh habitats. Currently, only the lower approximately 4.8 km of Provo River up to an irrigation diversion barrier is used for spawning, with an additional 3 km accessible in very high water years. River channelization, dredging, irrigation depletions, and upstream storage (Deer Creek Reservoir constructed 1941, Jordanelle Reservoir first filled 1996) have severely reduced habitat complexity. June sucker have successfully spawned and recruited in lentic refuge habitats including [[Red Butte Reservoir]], [[Camp Creek Reservoir]], and Arrowhead Pond, suggesting potential for in-lake spawning historically and currently.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration:** Annual spawning runs occur in the [[Provo River]], with timing shifted from historic June spawning to current mid-April to early May runs, likely due to altered flow regimes from upstream reservoir operations. Between 1997 and 2003, June sucker returned to spawn between 20 April and 1 July, with estimated onset in late May (1997–1998), first week of June (1999), and late April (2000–2004). The natural spring runoff peak has been altered due to upstream reservoir storage, with lowest flows observed in early 1980s. Cooperative efforts are underway to restore timing and duration of more natural hydrograph by acquiring and releasing water to mimic historic flows. Spawning in lentic habitats (reservoirs, ponds) has been documented in refuge populations, suggesting potential for lake spawning similar to other lakesuckers (e.g., Upper Klamath Lake native lakesuckers spawn in association with subsurface groundwater discharge areas). Drifting larvae require suitable nursery habitat with cover from predators.

**Trophic & Community Interactions:** June sucker coexist with [[Utah Sucker]] in Utah Lake, with environmental influences appearing important for evolution of feeding habits and observed morphologies of the two species. The historic ichthyofauna of Utah Lake included 12 native fish species. Currently, a large suite of nonnative fish species is present, most thought to directly or indirectly impact June sucker. In 1978–1979 inventory, common carp represented 66.2% of catch by number and 90.9% by weight, followed by white bass (26.7% by number, 4.2% by weight). Native species (June and Utah suckers combined) represented only 0.4% of total number and 0.5% of weight. [[White Bass]] (*Morone chrysops*) and common carp have greatest negative impacts through direct and indirect effects. Predation by nonnative fish on June sucker, especially drifting larvae, has been extensively documented. Common carp habitat modification reduces emergent macrophytes serving as larval cover, increasing predation exposure. June sucker successfully spawned and recruited in Arrowhead Pond in presence of [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*), providing evidence of importance of cover for larvae. Vegetative cover permits increased larval survival.

**Environmental Drivers:** Water development history profoundly impacted June sucker habitat. First water diversion (Bean Ditch) constructed 1849; by 1850 several larger diversions existed. In 1872, low dam across lake outflow to Jordan River changed Utah Lake function to storage reservoir. Pumping plant built 1902 allows lowering lake below outlet elevation. Lake surface elevation fluctuates on average 1.2–1.5 m annually, approximately 3.4 m over several years. Average annual inflow (1951–1990) is 898 × 10⁶ m³, with 428 × 10⁶ m³ discharged to Jordan River and 470 × 10⁶ m³ lost to evaporation. Extended drought in 1930s decreased lake area and volume. Altered flow regimes, channelization, and diversion dams blocking migration increase hybridization potential with Utah sucker. Temperature and flow appear to be important spawning cues; traditional June spawning timing on descending hydrograph limb before water temperatures warmed may have maintained spawning run distinct from April-spawning Utah sucker. Current early spawning (March onwards) may reflect altered environmental cues. Warmer water (18.5°C vs. 15.6°C) and specifically formulated feeds improve June sucker growth and survival in hatchery settings.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory:** June sucker declined precipitously in the 20th century from historically abundant populations supporting large harvests. Wild population size at time of 1986 endangered listing was estimated at less than 1,000 adults and may be smaller at present. Less than 300 adult June sucker have been captured annually during spawning runs in early 21st century. Recruitment failure in Utah Lake is not attributable to poor reproductive success but rather to low survival of early life stages. The proportion of spawning run fish consisting of captive-reared individuals has been increasing, reaching 75% of captured fish in 2003 spawning run. Dwindling numbers of wild fish combined with increasing proportions of stocked fish returning to spawn indicates barriers to recruitment being addressed by recovery actions. More than 46,000 June sucker of varying ages currently held outside Utah Lake as of October 1, 2005. Of approximately 6,000 PIT-tagged June sucker stocked from various facilities, 253 have been recaptured during spawning runs (though this may underestimate survival as not all spawning fish are captured and some stocked fish may not yet be sexually mature). Largest numbers of returning stocked fish came from Red Butte Reservoir, with individuals from Fisheries Experiment Station and Camp Creek also recorded.

**Threat & Risk Assessment:** Primary threats include: (1) **Nonnative fish species** – 17 nonnative species documented, with common carp (most abundant at 66.2% by number, 90.9% by weight in 1978–1979) and white bass having greatest negative impacts through predation on larvae and habitat modification. Current carp population estimate is 7 million (c.i. 6–10 million). (2) **Habitat loss and alteration** – Loss of extensive wetland at river-lake confluence, river channelization, bank armoring, reduced spawning habitat complexity, loss of aquatic macrophytes providing larval cover. (3) **Altered flow regimes** – Upstream reservoir storage (Deer Creek 1941, Jordanelle 1996) altered natural spring runoff peak and reduced flows, especially severe in early 1980s. (4) **Hybridization** – Altered flows and decreased spawning habitat increase potential for hybridization with Utah sucker. Fish in recent spawning runs exhibit morphologies consistent with June sucker, Utah sucker, and hybrids. (5) **Historic overharvest** – Contributed to initial population declines. (6) **Water level fluctuations** – Increased fluctuations from downstream demands and upstream storage. (7) **Increased turbidity and nutrient loading** – From urbanization and carp activity. Extinction threat was considered eminent at time of listing, with Scoppettone and Vinyard (1991) stating "the June sucker population may have declined so low that extraordinary efforts will be required to avert extinction."

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations:** The multi-partner [[June Sucker Recovery Implementation Program]] (formalized 2002) pursues recovery while allowing continued water use, addressing six recovery elements: (a) nonnative and sportfish management, (b) habitat development and maintenance, (c) water management and protection, (d) genetic integrity and augmentation, (e) research, monitoring, and data management, (f) information and education. **Specific actions:** (1) **Continue captive propagation and stocking** – Captive propagation has been most visible and active recovery action, primary source of fish released into wild. Network of hatcheries and refuges will be maintained for foreseeable future. Produce fish for release when they reach 150 mm length thought to help avoid predation. (2) **Implement nonnative fish control** – Develop and implement strategies to reduce and control nonnative species, especially white bass and common carp. Carp control feasibility study completed two seasons; assess how continuous removal project can be made effective and affordable. Consider sportfishing interests through creel survey. (3) **Restore and enhance habitat** – Target areas important to early life stages, especially historic tributary flood plains and deltaic interface with lake. Program acquired lands at Hobble Creek mouth; pursue easements on lands near Provo River mouth for potential grow-out ponds. Increase habitat complexity at river mouth, restore conditions allowing aquatic macrophyte restoration in key areas. Develop selective passage gates and structures to help young fish survive to recruitment. (4) **Manage water for June sucker** – Continue cooperative efforts to provide minimum flows in Provo River, effective even in drought years. Investigate pulsing flows to distribute larvae from river where limited cover exists. Acquire water to mimic natural hydrograph during spawning period. Reestablish more natural hydrograph so species experience traditional spawning cues of flow and temperature, potentially maintaining distinct spawning runs from Utah sucker. (5) **Genetic management** – Use emerging genetic data to maximize available genetic diversity in managed habitats. Conduct crosses using individuals from different year-classes. Investigate genetic structure and diversity of captive and wild spawning fish to direct production. Consider that advancing age of oldest brood lot fish may require collection of additional wild fish before senescence. Revise captive management plan (completed 2004) with newly available molecular data. (6) **Refine propagation protocols** – Improve captive reproduction through collaboration with U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Bozeman Fish Health and Technology Center. Use warmer water (18.5°C) and specifically formulated sucker feeds (Razorback Diet). Evaluate extensive culture (standing water bodies) vs. intensive culture (indoor tanks); extensive culture appears to produce superior quality fish. Consider protocol of streamside spawning, hatching in protected locations, holding in protected locations or nearshore areas until fish can avoid predation. Improve artificial spawning success through hormone studies (Ovaprim, human chorionic gonadotropin) and temperature manipulation (13°C for 4 months improves ovulation). (7) **Maintain refuge populations** – Continue holding June sucker at multiple sites (Fisheries Experiment Station, Camp Creek Reservoir, Ensign Ponds, Arrowhead Pond, USU Millville; reintroduce to Red Butte Reservoir after dam repairs complete in 2006). Refuges important for species conservation and source of fish for stocking. Thin populations every 1–2 years to maintain health and transfer to Utah Lake. (8) **Monitor stocking success** – Continue PIT-tagging and monitoring of stocked fish returns to spawning runs. Refine stocking protocols to introduce most robust and genetically diverse individuals. (9) **Conduct research** – Continue investigations of genetic diversity, morphological variation, heritability of traits, ecological needs of young fish, responses to habitat variables, ecological and morphologic differences between sucker species. (10) **Ecosystem approach** – Recognize that both June and Utah suckers benefit from habitat improvement, nonnative control, and instream flows. Maintain diverse environment where both species can survive, as environmental influences appear important for maintaining evolutionary trajectory of two sucker forms.

**Biological Reference Points:** Not reported in traditional fisheries management sense.
