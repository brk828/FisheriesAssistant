# Aphanius Dispar: The Arabian Killifish as a Hardy Aquarium Fish and Mosquito Predator

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This hobbyist article, written by an aquarist, profiles [[Aphanius Dispar]], a small [[Cyprinodontidae|cyprinodont]] killifish known as the [[Arabian Killifish]]. The author argues that the name "Middle East killifish" would better describe a species distributed from northern India around the Arabian Peninsula, along both sides of the [[Red Sea]], and into the Mediterranean through the [[Suez Canal]]. The fish tolerates a wide range of salinities and temperatures, preys on malaria-carrying [[Anopheline Mosquito]] larvae, and is easy to keep and breed. The article also describes colour variants, a parasitic [[Trematode Fluke]] infestation that mimics a genetic trait, and a two-pair breeding method for beginners.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy and nomenclature.** The species was first described as *Lebias dispar* by [[Rüppell]] in 1828. It has since undergone no fewer than six name changes, and it was assigned its current name, *A. dispar*, by Klauzewitz in 1967. The author notes that the name may change again. The subspecies *A. dispar richardsoni* (Boulenger 1907) is recorded from [[Wadi Al Hassa]] near Safi, Jordan.

**Morphology.** The fish is minnow-shaped and grows to about 80 mm (3 inches). Key features include:
- **Males:** brownish gray with many iridescent bluish-white spots on the flanks. The caudal fin has 5 to 6 transverse bars, the terminal bar yellow and the others alternating dark and light blue. Males have longer fins and more colour than females.
- **Females:** brownish gray with 8 to 20 narrow transverse bars on the flanks, with gray or transparent fins.
- **Young:** may show faint brown transverse bars on the caudal peduncle. Young males may have blue tails, and yellow bars develop with maturity.

Sexing adults is straightforward because of [[Sexual Dimorphism]] in fin length and colour.

**Colour variants.** Male marine specimens tend to be bluish gray. An occasional [[Gold Colour Morph]] has a rich overall gold colour, iridescent blue flank spots, orange-gold gill-cover spots and yellowish fins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salinity and temperature tolerance.** The author describes the species as "extremely tolerant" with respect to salinity and temperature. It occurs in cooler seawater, brackish estuarine water, and freshwater springs in the interior of Saudi Arabia. Spring water temperatures are in the lower 90s °F.

**Artesian springs and water quality.** The springs are artesian, arising from aquifers about 600 feet underground. The water has high mineral content and is very hard. The author notes that tap water in his district comes from wells drawing on the same aquifers, which is relevant to his aquarium husbandry.

**Habitat examples.** The author observed wild fish at the [[Qatif Oasis]] (in a drainage ditch and a well) and at [[Al-Azizia Oasis]] in eastern Saudi Arabia. Collections were made across Saudi Arabia's [[Eastern Province]].

**Biological control.** Wherever it occurs, the species is an efficient predator of Anopheline mosquito larvae, the vectors of malaria. Dr. R. H. Daggy of the Arab-American Oil Company noted this predation. In 1948 he introduced the fish to the al-Kharg area of Saudi Arabia as part of a [[Malaria Control]] scheme.

**Parasitism.** Some wild fish carry irregular greenish-black specks on the body, fins and tail. The author initially suspected a new species or a genetic factor. [[Jim Chambers]] of the British Museum (Natural History), London, identified the specks as [[Metacercaria]] larvae of a trematode fluke. The adult worm lives in aquatic birds and sheds eggs in their droppings. The eggs hatch in water, larvae enter snails, and later bore into fish skin and encyst. The cycle completes when a bird eats the fish. The encysted fluke is dormant and does not appear to harm the fish, which the author reports thrive, breed readily and produce healthy young in captivity.

## Key Findings

- **Distribution:** *A. dispar* is found from northern India, around the Arabian Peninsula, on both sides of the Red Sea, and into the Mediterranean via the Suez Canal. The author proposes "Middle East killifish" as a more accurate common name.
- **Environmental tolerance:** The species tolerates a wide range of salinities and temperatures, from marine and estuarine water to hard, mineral-rich artesian springs.
- **Biocontrol value:** It is an efficient predator of malaria-vector mosquito larvae, a trait that led to its 1948 introduction to al-Kharg for malaria control.
- **Colour and sexing:** Males are more colourful and have longer fins than females, so adults are easily sexed. Gold-coloured and blue-tailed variants occur.
- **Parasite misidentification:** Black specks previously thought to be a genetic trait were identified as encysted trematode metacercariae. The fish are not adversely affected.
- **Spawning behaviour:** Males drive females onto coarse gravel, where they release eggs and sperm simultaneously. Eggs are nonadherent and fall between large gravel grains, which protects them from the parents. The author regards male displays toward rivals as aggressive rather than courtship.
- **Development:** Eggs hatch in about 10 days. Fry accept [[Artemia]] (brine shrimp) and prepared fry food and grow rapidly. Fish can be sexed at about 3 months and spawned at about 5½ months.

**Breeding protocols described:**
1. **Two-pair method:** Two pairs were placed in an aquarium with tap water at 80–82 °F, [[Hornwort]] (*Ceratophyllum demersum*) and large-grained gravel. Over several months the population grew from fry to adults.
2. **Selected-pair method:** Sexes were separated for 10 days on a varied diet. A 40 × 25 × 25 cm (about 16 × 10 × 10 in) tank was prepared two days before spawning, with large-grain gravel, tap water at 80 °F and weighted hornwort. The pair stayed 10–12 days, and the first fry appeared about 48 hours after the parents were removed, with numbers rising over the next 9–10 days.
3. **Multi-male method:** A larger tank with 3–4 males and roughly double the number of females produced numerous fry and allowed the same observations. The author considers it the most interesting method for photographing spawning.

**Recommendation.** The author recommends *A. dispar* as a beginner's egg-laying fish. Its simplicity and readiness to spawn make it a strong alternative to the [[Zebra Danio]] (*Brachydanio rerio*), a longstanding beginner favourite.
