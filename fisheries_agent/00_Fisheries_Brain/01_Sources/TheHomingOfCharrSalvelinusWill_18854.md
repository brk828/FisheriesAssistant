# Homing Behaviour of Windermere Charr (Salvelinus willughbii)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Winifred E. Frost]] of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] examines the breeding behaviour of the [[Windermere Charr]] (*Salvelinus willughbii*, Günther), a charr of the English [[Lake District]]. The study identifies two reproductively isolated populations in [[Windermere]], an autumn-spawning and a spring-spawning group, separated by the timing and location of breeding. Using tag-recapture and displacement experiments, the author shows that sexually mature charr return to the same spawning ground year after year and, when displaced, home back within hours. The author argues that this homing is essential to maintaining morphological differentiation between the two populations. Homing to the natal (birth) site remained untested at the time of publication.

## Background & Description

- **Taxon:** *Salvelinus willughbii* (Günther), recorded by Regan (1911) as *Salvelinus alpinus willughbii*. It occurs in several English Lake District lakes, including Windermere.
- **Definition of homing:** After [[Gerking]] (1959), homing is the choice a fish makes to return to a formerly occupied place rather than to other equally probable places. This paper restricts the term to breeding-site fidelity, not return to the birthplace (as in [[Atlantic Salmon]]).
- **Study populations:**
  - *Autumn spawners*: spawn October to December, peak mid-November, in shallow water (1–3 m) on the lake shore and in the [[River Brathay]] (about 1.3 km upstream of the river mouth).
  - *Spring spawners*: spawn January to April, peak mid-February, in deep water (12–15 m) just off the shore, in the lake only.
- **Study sites:** Detailed work was confined to three north-basin sites: Low Wray Bay and Red Nab (west shore, autumn lake spawners), Holbeck Point (east shore, spring spawners), and Purdom's Dub in the River Brathay (autumn river spawners).
- **Historical note:** [[Thomas Pennant]] suspected in the 18th century that the autumn and spring charr were distinct kinds.
- **Marking methods:** Subcutaneous tags ([[LeCren]], 1954) combined with fin clips, used from 1950 onward, gave the most satisfactory results.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Autumn and spring spawners mix when not breeding and are widely dispersed across the northern part of the lake, as shown by angler recaptures made April–September with plumb lines.
- Windermere charr breed successfully in both still and running water. The eggs are lightly covered by gravel or hidden under stones, which allows aeration even in still water.
- Comparative evidence from other lake-dwelling salmonids (see Key Findings) places the topic within a broader pattern of homing, including [[Brown Trout]] in Dunalastair Reservoir, [[Cutthroat Trout]] in [[Yellowstone Lake]], and [[Lake Trout]] in several North American lakes.
- Abnormal spawning conditions were linked to deviation from homing. Lake Storsjoute (Sweden) and Shirley Lake (Ontario) showed over-crowded or altered spawning grounds, and Ullswater charr disappeared, probably because of lead-mine washings reaching their spawning beck.

## Key Findings

1. **Two reproductively isolated populations.** Marking showed that autumn and spring spawners are distinct populations separated by time and place of breeding. No autumn spawner was recaptured breeding in spring at Holbeck Point, and no spring spawner was found breeding in autumn at Low Wray or Red Nab.

2. **Between-season fidelity.** Of 584 marked charr recaptured one or more years after tagging, only six were taken on a different spawning ground. All six were autumn fish recaught in the lake the following autumn (three Low Wray Bay fish at Red Nab, two Red Nab fish at Low Wray Bay, and one Brathay fish at Red Nab; the paper's listing gives one Brathay and three Low Wray Bay fish, see source). The author notes these may be true strays or fish intercepted en route. Individual charr returned to the same ground for two, three and even four consecutive years.

3. **Within-season displacement.** Breeding charr were removed from their spawning ground and released elsewhere in the same season. Displaced fish returned to their own ground, sometimes within 24 hours. The shortest recorded homing time was 17 hours, for a Brathay spawner released at Low Wood. Table II summarises 17 river and 9 lake experiments plus a 4-experiment spring control series; the totals are not reproduced here.

4. **Repeated displacement.** In November 1956, 144 Brathay charr were released in Windermere. Of these, 38 were recaptured in the Brathay and displaced a second time. Four of those were recovered and displaced a third time, and two males were recaptured again in the Brathay, showing that the urge to return persisted through the breeding season.

5. **River versus lake release.** Fish released in rivers homed less often than those released in the lake. The author attributes this to random movement: roughly half of river-released fish would head in the wrong direction. Some Brathay fish had to travel downstream, which is unusual for their normal upstream homing.

6. **Limitations.** Netting efficiency varied among sites, and males stay longer on spawning grounds (mean 12.6 days versus 3.8 days for females in the Brathay), so true return proportions cannot be compared between sites or sexes.

7. **Morphological differentiation.** Mean gill raker counts were 13.3 for lake autumn spawners and 15.1 for spring spawners, a statistically significant difference. Brathay autumn spawners averaged 13.6. Spring spawners grow faster than autumn spawners after their second year. Homing is considered essential for maintaining this divergence. Lake and river autumn spawners home to separate grounds but do not differ morphologically, possibly due to cross-breeding through strays.

8. **Natal homing untested.** A plant of 290 hatchery-reared 1+ charr, adipose-clipped and released at Red Nab in April 1957, produced no recaptures in 1959 or later. The author suggests few survived to maturity. Further experiments with eggs and fry were in progress.

9. **Deviation and survival.** Deviation from strict homing may benefit a species under abnormal conditions, for example by spreading spawners when grounds are overcrowded. Strict homing can, however, be harmful, as in the Ullswater lead-pollution case.
