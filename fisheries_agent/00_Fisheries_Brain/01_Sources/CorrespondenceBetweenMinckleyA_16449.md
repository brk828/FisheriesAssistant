# Virgin River Roundtail Chub Federal Listing Proposal and Correspondence

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #conservation

## Executive Summary

This document comprises correspondence and a Federal Register proposal (June 24, 1986) to list the [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta seminuda*) as an endangered species with designated critical habitat under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The subspecies is endemic to the [[Virgin River]] system in southwestern Utah, northwestern Arizona, and southeastern Nevada. Dr. W.L. Minckley (Arizona State University, Chairman of the [[Desert Fishes Council]]) provided supporting comments emphasizing the unique genetic lineage and precarious status of this fish. The proposal documents extensive habitat loss since the mid-1800s due to water diversions, agricultural development, and urban growth, with current distribution reduced to approximately 50 miles of mainstream habitat from near Mesquite, Nevada upstream to La Verkin, Utah (excluding the Virgin River Narrows).

## Background & Description

*Gila robusta seminuda* was first collected and described by members of the Wheeler Survey near Washington, Utah (Cope and Yarrow 1875). It was characterized as intermediate between *Gila robusta* and *Gila elegans*. Holden and Stalnaker (1970) determined that the name *seminuda* referred exclusively to the Virgin River population, confirmed as a valid subspecies by Smith et al. (1977) through extensive taxonomic analyses.

The [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] is a silvery medium-sized minnow, generally less than 15 centimeters (6 inches) total length. The back, breast, and part of the belly have small, deeply embedded scales that may be absent in some individuals—the source of the subspecific name "seminuda" (half-naked). A closely related undescribed form of *Gila robusta* occurs in the [[Moapa River]], Nevada, originally a Virgin River tributary but now both tributary to [[Lake Mead]].

Historically abundant throughout the Virgin River from near Riverside, Nevada upstream to La Verkin Springs near Hurricane, Utah, the species has experienced dramatic range contraction. Present distribution (as of 1986) includes mainstream Virgin River from Mesquite, Nevada upstream to La Verkin Springs, excluding the Virgin River Narrows of Arizona. It is the rarest native fish in the Virgin River and occurs only in mainstream habitat, with only one tributary record (Cross 1975).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] inhabits deeper areas with swift but not turbulent water, generally associated with boulders or other cover (Minckley 1973). It occurs over sand and gravel substrates in water less than 90°F (32°C) and demonstrates high tolerance to salinity and turbidity (Deacon and Holden 1977). The species is omnivorous, consuming algae, aquatic and terrestrial insects, organic detritus, and crustaceans (Cross 1975).

The Virgin River presents extreme environmental conditions that historically served as barriers to exotic fish invasion: naturally high salinity, temperature (100-109°F from La Verkin Springs), turbidity, and highly fluctuating flows. La Verkin Springs discharges approximately 11 cubic feet per second with salinity of 9,650 milligrams per liter. [[Littlefield Spring]] below the Virgin Narrows contributes additional highly saline water. These conditions favor native fauna adapted to extremes while limiting exotic species establishment.

Cross (1975) found the chub occurred 80% of the time in unmodified habitat, 20% in slightly modified habitat, and rarely in extensively modified habitat. Cross also documented very few young-of-the-year fish and few adults over seven inches standard length, indicating poor recruitment. The [[Woundfin Recovery Team]] reported good chub reproduction in 1983 due to high water but no evidence of successful reproduction in 1984. Hickman (1985) reported successful 1984 reproduction with young-of-the-year comprising 14% of total catch.

Lands along occupied river sections are owned by the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM), States of Utah and Arizona, and private landowners. In Arizona, approximately 80-90% of riverine lands are BLM-administered, with private lands concentrated near Littlefield. In Utah, about 13 miles are BLM lands, with the remainder privately owned or state parcels.

## Key Findings

**Listing Status and Rationale:**
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed listing *Gila robusta seminuda* as endangered (not threatened) due to reduced range, extensive past habitat loss and alteration, and high demand for future Virgin River water use
- The [[American Fisheries Society]] lists the species as endangered due to habitat destruction (Deacon et al. 1979)
- Arizona lists it as endangered Group 2; Nevada as sensitive; Utah as threatened
- State listings protect against unregulated taking but provide no habitat protection

**Threats to Survival (Section 4(a)(1) Factors):**

*Factor A - Habitat Destruction/Modification (Primary Threat):*
- Five major water diversions remove all flow from long river stretches during peak irrigation season; three diversions within current chub range
- Summer flow often consists only of groundwater accretions and spring inputs below diversions
- Washington County, Utah population increased from 13,669 (1970) to 26,065 (1980), with projections for continued rapid growth driven by retirement and recreational communities
- Water availability identified as limiting factor for future growth
- Existing projects (Gunlock Reservoir, Kolob Reservoir, Ash Creek Reservoir) cause cumulative impacts through water withdrawal, discharge pattern changes, pollution, sedimentation, and channel modification
- [[Quail Creek Water Reclamation Project]] (under construction) will divert flood flows for storage, ensuring year-round minimum releases of 86 cubic feet per second
- Potential future projects include Warner Valley Energy System (decided against in 1982), Dixie Project (two dams and extensive canals), La Verkin Springs Unit desalinization (economically unviable as of 1984), and Lower Virgin River Unit salinity reduction
- USDA Soil Conservation Service has proposed flood control and irrigation projects with unknown effects on chub habitat

*Factor B - Overutilization:*
- No evidence of overutilization for commercial, recreational, scientific, or educational purposes

*Factor C - Disease or Predation:*
- Virgin River has relatively few exotic fish species compared to other Colorado River basin waters due to extreme environmental conditions
- Few exotic predators (green sunfish, black bullhead, largemouth bass) have invaded with limited success over past 70 years
- [[Red Shiner]] recently found upstream into Utah, moving progressively from Lake Mead; implicated in decline of other native species and considered threat to federally endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*)
- Red shiner may threaten early life stages of chub
- Any actions altering extreme environmental conditions (impoundment, desalinization) would allow exotic species incursion, severely impacting native fauna evolved with limited predation and competition
- Parasites (black grub and learnea) found in minor infestations (Radant and Coffeen 1983); parasites introduced by exotic fish from Lake Mead are known problem in Moapa River *Gila robusta* form (Wilson et al. 1968)

*Factor D - Inadequacy of Regulatory Mechanisms:*
- No provisions in Utah or Nevada water law for acquisition and protection of instream water rights for fish and wildlife habitat preservation
- This deficiency identified as major factor in decline of many native fishes

*Factor E - Other Factors:*
- Naturally restricted range makes subspecies extremely vulnerable to threats
- Any activity affecting water quantity or quality in Virgin River affects all individuals
- Possible extinction from single action
- Extensive habitat alteration may have tilted competitive edge toward exotic species
- Habitat degradation and loss over past 130 years compounds vulnerability

**Population Status:**
- Large decrease in range and numbers occurred primarily 1860-1900 when present water diversions were constructed and valley/riverbanks highly modified by agricultural development
- Unhealthy population structure observed: most fish same size and age rather than ideal distribution of few older fish, larger number of sexually mature fish, and large number of young-of-the-year
- Inconsistent recruitment threatens population persistence
- Long-term consistent sampling data needed to assess population stability; such data currently lacking

**Critical Habitat Designation:**
Proposed critical habitat encompasses approximately 50 miles of Virgin River mainstream:
- **Arizona (Mohave County):** Main channel from Nevada-Arizona state line upstream to west boundary of Section 31, T41N, R14W
- **Nevada (Clark County):** Main channel from Mesquite diversion dam (NE¼ of NW¼ of Sec. 21, T13S, R71E) upstream to Nevada-Arizona line
- **Utah (Washington County):** Main channel from Arizona-Utah state line upstream to Utah State Highway 9 (formerly 15) crossing north of Hurricane (SW¼ of Sec. 25, T41S, R13W)
- Excludes approximately 14-mile section of Virgin River Narrows

Primary constituent elements include: deeper pools and runs with cover in mainstream channel; warm, saline, turbid water; rock-sand-gravel substrates; few or no exotic fish species; periodic flooding to maintain habitat quality.

Not all sections provide year-round habitat, but all contain habitat used during some portion of the year. No smaller or alternative area would allow long-term survival and recovery.

**Activities Potentially Affected:**
- Water diversion, excessive groundwater pumping, impoundment
- Channelization, excessive sedimentation from agriculture and watershed disturbances, riparian destruction
- Release of chemical or biological pollutants, removal of natural chemical components (desalinization)
- Introduction of exotic fish species and associated parasites
- Federal actions by [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Army Corps of Engineers]] (Section 404 permits under Clean Water Act)
- Federally funded flood control, agricultural, channelization, highway and bridge construction projects

**Conservation Value:**
Dr. Minckley emphasized the Virgin River roundtail chub represents "a group of fishes with a complex and fascinating evolutionary history, only remnants of which remain." Protection under the Endangered Species Act would allow "maintenance of an unique genetic lineage, values of which are unestimatable." He noted that high environmental quality attracting recreationists and retirees is equally protected by listed species and critical habitat designation, "a fact that is seldom considered."

**Regulatory Framework:**
Section 7(a)(4) of the Act requires Federal agencies to confer informally on actions likely to jeopardize proposed species or adversely modify proposed critical habitat. Upon listing, Section 7(a)(2) requires Federal agencies to ensure activities they authorize, fund, or carry out are not likely to jeopardize the species or destroy/adversely modify critical habitat. The Act prohibits take, import, export, interstate commerce, and possession of illegally taken specimens, with permits available for scientific purposes, species propagation/survival enhancement, and incidental take associated with otherwise lawful activities.
