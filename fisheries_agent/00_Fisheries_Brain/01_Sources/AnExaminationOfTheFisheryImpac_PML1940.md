# Concrete Lining Impacts on the Fishery of the Coachella Canal, Southeastern California

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation study, authored by Gordon Mueller, Gary Bryant, and Tom Burke, examines how [[Concrete Lining]] of a 3.4 km section of the [[Coachella Canal]] changed its fish community. Fish were sampled during a [[Dewatering Survey]] in November 1984 and compared with earlier earthen-canal data from [[Beaty Surveys]] and [[Minckley Surveys]]. Fish density, biomass and species diversity all fell after lining. The fishery became overwhelmingly dominated by [[Channel Catfish]]. Lentic, cover-oriented species such as [[Largemouth Bass]] and sunfishes were nearly eliminated. The authors describe the results as indicative trends rather than conclusive evidence, because the lined reach is short and the baseline data are limited.

## Background & Description

Water scarcity in the arid southwest has encouraged accelerated canal lining programs in the [[Imperial Valley]] and [[Coachella Valley]] to reduce seepage losses. The authors note that earthen delivery canals, though often regarded as monotonous habitats, support substantial aquatic diversity. Lining could therefore have significant ecological consequences.

The study reach was part of the [[Colorado River]] Basin salinity control project and lies between check drops 7.4 and 9.5, about 8 km from the [[All-American Canal]] headworks. The concrete section is 3.4 km long, with a 4.9 m bottom width and about 16.3 m top width. Maximum water depth is 3.2 m, and the design operational velocity is 1.4 m/s. Earthen sections were typically about 20 m wide with depths of 2 to 5 m.

Sampling used methods similar to those of [[Minckley Surveys]] (Minckley et al. 1983). Two 4 m x 4 m steel-framed nets were placed downstream to block fish escape during dewatering. Fish were collected with 6 mm mesh bag seines, dip nets, and by bucket. The canal was divided into four sections (A–D), ranging from 244 m to 1,610 m long. Fish counts were multiplied by average weights to estimate total biomass.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Concrete lining substantially altered habitat. Lined canals are narrower, deeper and faster-flowing. They usually lack aquatic vegetation and protective cover, and the earthen canal's bank and submergent vegetation and irregularities were the main sources of spawning and juvenile habitat. The authors argue that the loss of low-velocity areas and cover is the mechanism behind the fishery decline.

The authors also note that the lined reach is probably sustained by downstream migration from earthen sections of the [[All-American Canal]] and the [[Colorado River]]. They state that secondary downstream effects from lining may have influenced earlier data.

Grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) were introduced for aquatic weed control in the region ([[Triploid Grass Carp]]). Five experimental grass carp were included in one earlier sample. The control section in the study was not stocked, and its fish community was similar to the lined section.

## Key Findings

**Lined canal fishery (November 1984)**
- Mean density was 0.037 fish/m² and mean biomass was 4.171 g/m².
- A total of 3,439 fish were collected or observed. Only six species were recorded.
- [[Channel Catfish]] made up 96.6% of the fishery on average. Section A had the highest density (0.136 fish/m²), and Section D the lowest (0.022 fish/m²).
- Channel catfish accounted for about 72.9% of total biomass, and [[Carp]] for about 25.9% in the entire reach.
- The other five species were rare, together making up about 3.4% of individuals. [[Threadfin Shad]] and [[Red Shiner]] were each about 1% of the population. Only a single [[Largemouth Bass]] and a single [[Tilapia]] were recorded.
- Channel catfish lengths were mostly 10–25 cm, with some individuals over 57 cm. Strong modes between about 9 and 26 cm likely represent year classes 0–3. The fish appeared healthy, with no abnormalities.

**Earthen canal comparison**
- Pre-lining density ranged from 0.034 to 0.229 fish/m², and biomass from 6.245 to 49.369 g/m².
- Earthen sections supported 16 species (Minckley et al. 1983). Six to eight of these made up more than 1% of the population each.
- [[Channel Catfish]] made up 78.8% to 86.1% of the earthen fishery.
- [[Largemouth Bass]], sunfishes, and [[Flathead Catfish]] together made up 7.9% to 15.6% of the earthen fishery. These species were absent or nearly absent after lining.

**Change after lining**
- Species diversity declined by about 62.5%, with only six of 16 previously recorded species present.
- Density declined by 59.7% to 83.8%, and biomass by 48.1% to 91.6%. Scenario-based estimates varied with assumptions, and the authors caution that these ranges are not firm.
- The control scenario, which used [[Beaty Surveys]] data as a baseline, showed a density change of -76.9% and a biomass change of -43.5%, so the lining effect is not separable from other factors.
- Regardless of scenario, the authors conclude that lining reduced both fishery density and biomass.

**Ecological interpretation**
- Swift-water species such as channel catfish and threadfin shad persisted. Cover-dependent and lentic species such as largemouth bass, sunfishes, and flathead catfish declined or disappeared, matching predictions by Minckley et al. (1983).
- The authors predict that as lining expands, the loss of protective cover will progressively reduce reproductive habitat for the remaining species.

**Caveats**
- The lined section represents only a short portion of the total canal system.
- Sampling bias is assumed to be minimal, but annual variation in reproduction, biomass, stocking, and maintenance could not be controlled.
- An older catfish mode in the 1980 Minckley sample (larger than 30 cm) could not be explained by stocking records, but aging studies (McCarthy and Marsh 1982) attributed it to strong 3rd and 4th year classes.
