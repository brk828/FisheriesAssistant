# Aspects of Melanism in Acanthopterygian Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #acanthopterygii

## Executive Summary

This paper by C. M. Breder, Jr. (Zoologica, Vol. 54, No. 4, 1969) examines a rare form of background-contrasting melanism in young acanthopterygian teleosts. Individuals become nearly completely black while living over light sandy bottoms, and in this state they are solitary, quiescent, agonistic, and freeze when approached. The phenomenon is documented chiefly in [[Chaetodipterus Faber]] (Atlantic Spadefish), [[Trachinotus Falcatus]] (Permit-like Pompano), [[Lagodon Rhomboides]] (Pinfish), and [[Menticirrhus]] species, with more than fifteen additional cases of uncertain status. The author combines a literature review, field records, aquarium experiments in plastic basins, and a variable-light aquarium to test the conditions that produce and sustain the black phase.

## Background & Description

The black phase is a pigment state in which melanophores are dispersed and then multiplied, producing a full-body melanism. Breder describes two timescales of darkening: rapid physiological dispersion of melanin granules (minutes to seconds) and slower morphological development of new melanophores (weeks). Reversion to a light phase follows the same two timescales, which explains why some fishes take up to about three weeks to lighten noticeably.

Black-phase fishes are typically young, roughly 9 to 35 mm standard length, and lack countershading. Their social and locomotor behaviour contrasts sharply with conspecifics in the light phase, which school, are active, and flee.

Key taxa examined include:
- **Trachinotus falcatus**: small black individuals, about 16 to 35 mm s.l.; iris may be ruby red while black.
- **Chaetodipterus faber**: black phase at about 9 mm, persisting to about 30 to 35 mm in Florida Gulf waters but reaching 300 mm in the Bahamas.
- **Lagodon rhomboides**: one 15 mm black individual, which bleached within minutes of capture.
- **Menticirrhus** spp.: black individuals in schools of otherwise pale fishes, usually 20 to 30 mm.

Breder notes that the phenomenon is recorded in roughly eight genera confirmed by observation and about nine more by inference, mainly in Percoidei and Antennarioidei.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The black phase appears to depend on several environmental conditions, which the author lists as:
1. Water clear and shallow enough for good light penetration.
2. Sufficient illumination.
3. Light bottoms or the water surface, not intermediate depths.
4. Presence of inert, dark, typically black objects (charred wood, dead leaves, wrack) resting on the bottom or floating.

Turbidity strongly limits the phenomenon. In Florida's turbid Gulf waters, [[Chaetodipterus Faber]] black-phase fish were not seen above about 35 mm, while in clearer Bahamian waters they reached 300 mm. Seasonally, the black phase occurs mainly from late spring through summer, peaking in July and August, and disappears in autumn. Water temperatures below about 70 °F appeared to prevent effective melanin concentration in the aquarium study.

Breder proposes that fish hatch from pelagic eggs, spend their larval life as plankton among floating blackened debris, and then settle onto dark bay bottoms. This suggests that exposure to dark backgrounds during early life initiates the melanism. Black-phase fish are often found among patches of trash, and in one case a *Trachinotus* was reported to resemble a spray of sargassum.

## Key Findings

- **Behavioural contrast:** Black-phase fish are solitary, quiescent, and agonistic. They freeze on approach, often in a coiled pose, whereas light-phase fish school and flee.
- **Taxonomic distribution:** Confirmed in Carangidae, Sparidae, Sciaenidae and Ephippidae, with suggestions in other teleost families and in one holostean, [[Lepisosteus Osseus]] (Longnose Gar).
- **Experimental result (variable-light aquarium):** A 50 mm *Chaetodipterus faber* was black at incident light values of about 375 f.c. (albedo 0.40), but banded at all readings below 300 f.c. with the transition unstable. No bars appeared above 150 f.c. in the transition analysis reported in the summary table, and the fish was banded at low albedo values (0.06 to 0.20).
- **Plastic basin trials:** Two *Trachinotus falcatus* in black phase remained inert and did not respond to a net or hand, while light-phase conspecifics fled. Six light-phase *Lagodon* ignored dark spots and reacted to disturbance rather than background.
- **Turbidity effect:** The black phase disappeared in turbid flowing water and returned in clear water, with a solid black pattern appearing after about five days of clearing.
- **Hormonal and nervous control:** Breder concludes that the black phase is primarily driven by the nervous system under environmental stimulation rather than by endocrine peculiarity. Vision is essential, since blinded fish darken, but intact fish in the black phase responded to purely optical cues.
- **Survival interpretation:** The author suggests that black-phase fish match or resemble drifting dark debris and that freezing on a contrasting background buys time for slower morphological lightening.
- **Ontogenetic vs phylogenetic:** The phenomenon is mainly ontogenetic, though possibly phylogenetic in *Chaetodipterus faber*.
- **Summary of limits:** The phenomenon is limited to warm water in the warm half of the year, is mostly seen in young fish, and is confined to a narrow set of background conditions.
