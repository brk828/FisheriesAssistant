# Comparative Survival of Repatriated Razorback Suckers in Lower Colorado River Reach 3, 2014–2016

#ichthyology #catostomidae #endangered_species #stocking_evaluation

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary:** [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) is a long-lived catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Once abundant throughout its range, populations have steadily declined due to habitat alterations from dam construction and interactions with non-native species. The species is federally listed as endangered. Adult razorback suckers exhibit relatively high survival (>75% annually). Evidence of natural recruitment is limited to a single population in [[Lake Mead]]; all other populations depend entirely on continued stocking. Age, maturity, fecundity, and growth parameters are not reported in this document.

**Habitat Associations:** [[Razorback Sucker]] populations in [[Reach 3]] (the 84-mile section between [[Davis Dam]] and [[Parker Dam]], including [[Lake Havasu]]) occupy riverine sections immediately downstream from Davis Dam, rocky canyon-like shoreline with backwater habitat (Zone 3-2), open water reservoir sections (Zone 3-3), and areas near [[Parker Dam]] including the [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]] (Zone 3-4). Major spawning aggregations occur from Laughlin, Nevada, downstream to Needles, California, particularly in [[Laughlin Lagoon]], [[Park Moabi]], and [[Topock Marsh]]. Fish aggregate in these areas during the spawning season (January to April).

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration:** Telemetry studies (Wydoski and Lantow 2012) demonstrate that razorback suckers stocked in lower Lake Havasu (Zone 3-4) can move upstream to spawning areas in Zones 3-1 and 3-2, given sufficient time. However, the greater distance from lower stocking sites to spawning areas may increase exposure to piscivores. Fish show fidelity to spawning aggregation sites during the reproductive season. Emigration through [[Parker Dam]] or deletion through water intake structures is negligible; razorback suckers occupy only regions upstream of these structures.

**Trophic & Community Interactions:** Not reported in detail. The document notes negative interactions with non-native sport fishes contributed to population declines. Predation is a significant mortality factor: [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) in [[Lake Mohave]] prey on razorback suckers <15 inches (380 mm) TL, with survival <10% for fish released at these sizes. [[Lake Havasu]] contains an additional predator, [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), which has a larger gape than striped bass and can ingest razorback suckers exceeding 27.6 inches (700 mm) TL. Non-native species captured during monitoring included common carp, gizzard shad, and various sport fishes.

**Environmental Drivers:** Habitat complexity, presence and abundance of large predaceous fishes, and other environmental factors contribute to differences in survival between reservoirs. [[Lake Havasu]] was impounded in 1938; recruitment events became rare due to negative interactions with non-native fishes. The last documented capture of wild adult razorback suckers was in [[Laughlin Lagoon]] in 1986. Reservoir operations (releases at Davis Dam, Parker Dam, and from Alamo Dam into the Bill Williams River) regulate water levels and flow.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory:** The razorback sucker population in [[Reach 3]] has remained stable from 2011–2015. Population estimates: 2,454 (95% CI: 1,910–3,150) in 2011; 4,508 (4,015–5,061) in 2012; 4,456 (4,089–4,856) in 2013; 4,935 (4,629–5,262) in 2014; and 4,923 (4,652–5,209) in 2015. Estimates after 2011 are approximately twice the 2011 estimate with non-overlapping confidence intervals. The population is wholly dependent on the stocking program. More than 75,000 razorback suckers (>11.8 inches or 300 mm) have been stocked into Reach 3 since 2006. Between January 1, 2011, and April 30, 2015, 38,464 razorback suckers with 134-kHz PIT tags and recorded TL at release were stocked. Annual collective sampling efforts contacted 1,972 (2014), 4,142 (2015), and 3,027 (2016) individual razorback suckers.

**Threat & Risk Assessment:** Primary threats include habitat alteration from dams, predation by non-native fishes (striped bass, flathead catfish), and lack of natural recruitment. The species remains federally endangered. Post-stocking survival is size-dependent: contact rates as high as 10% for fish released at 13.8 inches (350 mm) in Reach 3, compared to 1.8% in Lake Mohave for similar-sized fish. First-year survival is significantly impacted by size at release and time prior to scanning; season of release had minimal detectable impact once time-at-large was accounted for. Second-year survival estimates (model-averaged): 0.901 (2012–13), 0.793 (2013–14), 0.743 (2014–15), 0.505 (2015–16). Maintenance of the population is entirely contingent on continued stocking.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations:** (1) Continue coordinated stocking and remote PIT scanning efforts in high-contact areas ([[Laughlin Lagoon]], [[Park Moabi]]) to maintain adequate recontact rates for population estimates. (2) If additional spawning sites are identified, employ similar coordinated stocking and scanning efforts to avoid bias. (3) Stock larger fish (>15 inches or 400 mm TL preferred) to maximize post-stocking survival, though smaller fish (≥13.8 inches or 350 mm) show higher survival in Reach 3 than in Lake Mohave. (4) Coordinate stocking regime with sampling regime to test specific hypotheses about factors affecting post-stocking survival. (5) Continue monitoring with both remote PIT scanning (for population estimates and survival) and biannual netting/electrofishing (for health, growth, genetics data). (6) Implement more structured stocking protocols with replicate cohorts of similar sizes within the same year to improve mark-recapture model resolution. (7) Focus stocking efforts in Zone 3-2 (Park Moabi area), which shows highest contact proportions and contains major spawning aggregations.

**Biological Reference Points:** Size at release is the most important factor impacting post-release survival. Mean contact rate for fish at 13.8 inches (350 mm) TL is near 10% in Reach 3. In Lake Mohave, razorback suckers released at <15 inches (380 mm) TL experience low (<10%) survival attributed to striped bass predation; fish released at >19.7 inches (500 mm) are less vulnerable and show approximately 80% survival. Adult razorback sucker survival is relatively high (>75% annually). The minimum time at large between release and contact for inclusion in mark-recapture models was 120 days (4 months). Population estimates are valid for the marking period (January 1 to April 30 of each year). Best management practices may differ among populations of the same species in different reservoirs due to habitat complexity and predator assemblages.
