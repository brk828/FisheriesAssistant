# Fisheries Research in New York Farm Ponds and Warm-Water Fish Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #salmonidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This reprint from the [[New York State Conservationist]] (August-September 1952) presents a long-term [[Fisheries Management]] research program on New York farm ponds, along with a companion essay on diagnosing and correcting overpopulation and stunting in warm-water lakes. Farm pond construction in New York rose from 1,900 ponds in 1950 to 3,254 completed as of January 1, 1952, with [[Soil Conservation Service]] assistance. The research is run by the [[Farm Pond Research Unit]] at [[Cornell University]] under contract to the [[New York State Conservation Department]], funded through the [[Dingell-Johnson Act]]. Investigations cover trout ponds, warm-water game fish ponds, and [[Bait Minnow]] ponds, with about 120 ponds under study. The key findings are that trout perform well in New York ponds when stocking and pond conditions are right, that the southeastern warm-water pond model may not transfer directly to New York, and that overabundant, stunted fish populations are a common problem that management must address.

## Background & Description

- **Program origin:** Investigations began in 1949 as graduate studies by two students at Cornell University. Since April 1952 the work has been handled under contract, with an overall aim of determining management methods for maximum economically produced crops of fish on a [[Sustained Yield]] basis.
- **Research categories:** (1) [[Trout Stocking]] ponds; (2) warm-water ponds stocked with [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill Sunfish]]; (3) [[Bait Minnow]] production ponds.
- **Scale:** About 120 ponds across New York are under investigation, mostly privately owned and studied under cooperative agreements with owners. Funding and staff limit work to ponds needed directly for research.
- **Methods:** Research relies on trial and error, with groups of ponds stocked at different rates and revisited at intervals. Success is measured by fish number and total weight, growth rate, and numerical balance among species. [[Seining]] is the main sampling method; two successive seine hauls can give reliable population estimates. Population weight is estimated by multiplying the estimated population by average individual weight. Drained ponds allow complete enumeration.
- **Mark-recapture procedure:** Fish are concentrated in the seine bag, weighed, and temporarily marked by clipping a corner of the tail fin. They are released, and the pond is seined again. The trout population is estimated from the total number of trout and the percentage of marked fish recovered.
- **Authors:** Alfred W. Eipper, Research Associate (farm pond article); A. C. Perry, Sr. Aquatic Biologist (warm-water fish populations article).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pond water supply:** Ponds with a permanent spring water supply are ideal for trout. Some runoff-fed ponds nonetheless support good year-round trout populations. Survival is highest in runoff ponds at higher altitudes and in those with a maximum depth of at least eight feet, with depths of six feet or more over at least one-third of the pond area.
- **Temperature limits:** Many New York farm ponds have summer water temperatures too high for trout and are instead stocked with warm-water species.
- **Shorter growing season:** New York's growing season is shorter than in the Southeast. Bluegills spawn in the first year after stocking, but bass do not reproduce until the second summer after planting, giving bluegills an early advantage.
- **Trout reproduction:** With a few notable exceptions, [[Brook Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Brown Trout]] do not reproduce in New York farm ponds.
- **Stunting and overabundance:** Warm-water lakes and ponds commonly contain too many fish rather than too few, and these are often undesirable in size or species. Fish production is best measured by poundage, which depends on the water's [[Carrying Capacity]]. Overabundant populations of [[Bullhead]] and [[Pumpkinseed]] often stunt, sometimes below harvestable size.
- **Competition:** Sportsmen who selectively keep large predatory fish and return small panfish to the water can unknowingly increase competition for food among the remaining fish.
- **Typical Lake X profile:** A representative small warm-water lake is about 20 acres, with an average depth of 10 to 12 feet and a maximum depth of about 25 feet. It has submerged weed beds and lily pads and supports [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Chain Pickerel]], [[Yellow Perch]], [[Pumpkinseed]], [[Rock Bass]], and bullheads.

## Key Findings

### Farm pond expansion
- Farm pond construction in New York grew from 1,900 ponds in 1950 to 3,254 ponds completed as of January 1, 1952. These totals exclude ponds built without S.C.S. assistance.
- Fish rearing ranks high among owners' desired pond uses.

### Trout ponds
- Across 30 farm ponds stocked with fingerling trout over the past four years, average annual survival was about 65 per cent during the first two years after stocking.
- Mortality increases after age two, possibly linked to sexual maturity. Experiments are testing [[Hybrid Trout]] crosses of brook trout with rainbow or brown trout, which may mature late or not at all.
- Stocking rates: 400 spring fingerlings or 300 fall fingerlings per surface acre. Trout ordinarily reach seven inches or larger by spring of the following year, and 13 to 15 inches in their third year.
- A typical one-acre New York trout pond should contain 90 to 120 pounds of fish by the end of the first summer after stocking. The pond supplies all necessary food, so supplementary feeding is unnecessary.
- A newly built pond should have a four- to six-month grace period after filling before first stocking, so nutrients and aquatic food organisms can establish.
- Because trout generally do not reproduce, ponds must be restocked every two or three years. Fall fingerlings (about six inches long) are preferred for restocking because they are less likely to be eaten by large fish from the previous planting.
- Trout ponds should be stocked with trout only. Introducing sunfish or bullheads is a common cause of trout pond failure.

### Warm-water ponds
- Most New York warm-water ponds have been stocked with largemouth bass and bluegill sunfish at 100 bass to 1,000 bluegills per surface acre, the standard southeastern recommendation.
- Investigated New York ponds show a pronounced tendency for bluegills to become overabundant in the third or fourth year after stocking, producing a large, stunted bluegill population and much reduced bass reproduction.
- Trials are testing bass-to-bluegill ratios of 1 to 1 and 1 to 5. Other approaches under investigation include:
  - Keeping the conventional 1 to 10 ratio but planting bass one year before bluegills so both spawn in the same season.
  - Substituting a less productive prey species for bluegills.
  - Adding [[Chain Pickerel]] as a predator to control bluegills.
- Ponds with a single species that can succeed alone are also being considered. Several ponds have recently been stocked with [[Channel Catfish]] fingerlings, and further trials are planned.
- It will take several years before reliable information on these techniques is available.

### Bait minnow ponds
- Strong demand, high prices, and increasing restrictions on seining minnows from natural waters make minnow propagation in warm-water ponds promising.
- A survey of bait minnow dealers across the State is underway.
- Experiments have started in about 25 ponds to evaluate six native bait minnow species for pond culture, testing stocking rates, fertilization, and supplementary feeding.
- More cooperating private ponds are needed, and no management recommendations for minnow ponds in New York are yet available.

### Warm-water lake management principles
- Typical problem lakes have too many fish of undesirable size or species, not too few fish.
- Fish production is measured by poundage, not numbers, so a water body's carrying capacity can support many small fish or fewer large ones.
- Overabundant small species can stunt, as seen in bullhead ponds producing only "runts" three or four inches long and sunfish ponds where individuals do not exceed three inches.
- Recommended corrective measures, used singly or in combination:
  - **Education:** Teach fishermen the relationships among game, forage, and panfish species, and encourage them to harvest panfish. [[Sportsmen Clubs]] can help.
  - **Netting:** Large-scale removal of stunted panfish by [[Haul Seining]] and trap-netting has improved growth and condition of preferred species in many waters. Success depends on the lake's physical characteristics and the species' susceptibility to netting.
  - **Selective stocking:** Stocking larger predatory species can help if the lake suits the species. Simply adding more problem fish is not effective.
  - **Renovation:** As a last resort, poisoning the population and restocking, which requires careful weighing of gains against costs and risks.
