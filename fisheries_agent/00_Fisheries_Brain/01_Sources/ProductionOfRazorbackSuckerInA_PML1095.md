# Razorback Sucker On-Site Propagation and Grow-Out in Yuma Cove Backwater, Lake Mohave (1992)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final technical report, prepared by Michael J. Horn and Paul C. Marsh of [[Arizona State University]] for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], documents the 1992 attempt to produce [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), an endangered catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]], in an isolated backwater at Yuma Cove on [[Lake Mohave]]. The work followed earlier, partly failed attempts (1984–1987) and a 1990 effort by the [[Native Fish Work Group]]. The 1992 cycle was the first successful one: larvae were produced, grown to 30 cm+ sub-adults, and partly returned to the lake. Production was still far below what is needed to replace the adult population.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Razorback Sucker]] is endangered and endemic to the Colorado River basin. Lake Mohave holds the largest remaining population, estimated at about 60,000 adults (Marsh 1992). Adults are mostly old individuals, and the population has shown no detectable recruitment in nearly four decades (Minckley 1983; Minckley et al. 1991).
- **Recruitment failure:** Larvae appear each year but disappear before reaching juvenile size. Predation by non-native species on early life stages is considered a major factor (Marsh and Langhorst 1988). Genetic diversity in Lake Mohave fish is considered extraordinary (Dowling and Minckley, in press).
- **Augmentation rationale:** Stocking young fish elsewhere proved impractical because of predation losses. Introductions of nearly 20 million fish into the Colorado and [[Gila River]] basins produced disappointing survival (Hendrickson 1992). Growing fish to a relatively predator-immune size of 30 cm total length (TL) before stocking was proposed as a remedy. Hatchery space and long-distance transport limit this approach. The primary hatchery is the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico, more than 1200 km from the river.
- **On-site advantages:** On-site production uses local brood fish, has negligible transport distances, offers flexible harvest timing, and allows in situ study.
- **Study site:** Yuma Cove is a naturally isolated backwater adjacent to Arizona Bay on Lake Mohave. It was connected to the lake by a berm of coarse gravel. Its water level and quality tracked the lake's fluctuations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Connectivity and hydrology:** The backwater was hydrologically connected to Lake Mohave through the berm. High lake elevations combined with strong westerly winds eroded that berm in 1985 and 1987, allowing non-native fish to invade and young and adult razorbacks to escape.
- **Water quality stress:** In deeper areas, dissolved oxygen fell below 1 mg/L, and temperatures in places approached 30 °C. These conditions, together with falling lake level, are thought to have driven mid-summer growth stagnation and mortality.
- **Vegetation:** Dense growths of sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*) and spiny naiad (*Najas marina*) hampered sampling and recovery.
- **Predators:** Light trapping revealed an unexpectedly high density of predatory [[Odonate Naiads]] (dragonfly and damselfly larvae) in the backwater.
- **Nutrient input:** A flash flood in August carried large amounts of organic material, including feral burro (*Equus asinus*) feces, into the backwater. This likely boosted primary and secondary production.
- **Management history:** The backwater was treated with [[Rotenone Treatment]] (ichthyocide) in 1984, October 1985, winter 1990–91, and 25 November 1992 to remove non-native fishes and reset the system.

## Key Findings

### Methods
- Stocking: 27–28 January 1992, with 28 ripe females and 60 males from the adjacent Lake Mohave shoreline. Of the males, 53 (88%) were ripe.
- Larval sampling: spotlights were initially used, then [[Light Trapping]] was adopted as the standard method because it was more convenient and efficient.
- Juvenile sampling: seining, entanglement netting, traps, and other gear, with [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tagging]] (PIT tags) applied at the end of the season.
- Marking: right pectoral fin clips from 16 June to 22 July 1992 for [[Mark-Recapture Estimation]].
- Removal: from 29 July, fish were moved to [[Davis Cove]], a netted, non-native-depleted cove.

### Growth
- Earliest post-larvae (25 February) averaged 11.0 mm TL, about 12–15 days post-fertilization.
- Growth was exponential through late spring. It slowed to near nil for about two weeks in mid-July, likely due to lake-level decline, rising temperatures, and low dissolved oxygen.
- Growth resumed in early August and was approximately linear through late November.
- Average TL at nine months was 35.4 cm (about 4 cm/month). The largest individual was 39.1 cm.
- The report gives the daily rate as 0.028 cm/day, which conflicts with the monthly figure. The monthly figure is consistent with the 35.4 cm average.
- Growth exceeded hatchery rates at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and was about twice that in central Arizona urban lakes (Marsh 1990).
- At least five ripe males were present by November, at nine months of age. The previously reported earliest maturity for males was two years.

### Abundance and Harvest
- Three multiple-census estimates of juveniles on 29 July (from mark-recapture data, 16 June–29 July):
  - Schnabel: 306
  - Modified Schnabel: 276
  - Schumacher-Eschmeyer: 329 (95% CI 257–422)
- Recovery was 296 individuals moved to Davis Cove after 22 July, and 298 total including dead fish.
- Only 64% (34 of 53) of marked fish were recovered after 22 July. The authors attribute the loss to mortality during poor water quality in June and early July, when fish were emaciated and infested with *Lernaea*.
- Using the Schumacher-Eschmeyer estimate as a baseline with 64% survival predicted a final harvest of about 211 fish (the Schumacher-Eschmeyer range implied 161–302). The authors judged the census methods reliable for future use.

### Adult Retrieval
- A total of 87 adult razorbacks (or their parts) were recovered from the backwater.
- 83 (94%) were captured alive and returned to Lake Mohave.
- Four (5%) natural mortalities were recorded.
- Four fish had no detectable tags and were re-tagged.
- One adult (1%) remained unaccounted for at reclamation.
- Table 3 caption gives the stocking date as January 1991, which conflicts with the text (1992).
- Stocked adults were augmented in the backwater by offspring at a factor of 2.5 (females) to 5 (males).

### Production Shortfall and Constraints
- The authors state that current production is inadequate. Replacing the adult population within 10 years would require 6,000–12,000 sub-adults annually, which is 20 to 40 times the 1992 output.
- Space constraints: the backwater may be too small to produce target numbers. Fish should be harvested at smaller sizes, and additional habitats should be added.
- Juvenile mortality between hatching and juvenile size probably causes the greatest losses. Poor water quality reduced numbers by about a third. Capture and handling stress probably contributed, so sampling should be curtailed under such conditions.
- Vegetation removal or control could substantially increase harvest efficiency.
- Hatchery-produced fish stocked in Davis Cove grew more slowly than on-site fish in Yuma Cove. The cause (habitat, initial size, predation, or other factors) is unknown.
- Lake Mohave's monthly level fluctuations may affect backwater fish. Efforts to stabilize or avoid such variation were recommended.
- Conclusion: The program has the potential to contribute substantially to the recovery of the species, but sustained effort will be needed to solve several specific problems.
