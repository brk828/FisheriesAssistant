# Public Perceptions of and Support for Arizona's Native Fish

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #social-science #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This bachelor's thesis by Lydia A. Breunig (Arizona State University, 1995) presents results from a telephone survey of 448 Arizona residents examining public awareness, knowledge, and support for conserving [[Arizona Native Fish]]. The study employed a pre-test/post-test design to assess whether providing factual information about native fish affects support for conservation efforts. Respondents were divided into non-anglers (urban and rural residents) and anglers. Results demonstrate that the general public has limited awareness and several misconceptions about Arizona's endemic fish species. While anglers and males generally possess greater knowledge about native fish, women show significantly stronger support for conservation measures. The study reveals that 24 of Arizona's 30 native fish species are listed as endangered at state and/or federal levels, with habitat alterations from cattle grazing, dams, irrigation, declining water tables, and mining identified as primary threats since the late 1800s.

## Background & Description

Arizona is home to over 30 endemic fish species that evolved specialized adaptations to survive in isolated streams, rivers, and small pools under severe environmental conditions since the [[Pleistocene]]. These native fish populations have faced dramatic declines since the late 19th century due to multiple anthropogenic factors: cattle grazing in [[Riparian Areas]], introduction of [[Non-Native Fish]], dam construction, irrigation practices, declining water tables, and mining operations. All of Arizona's native fish are currently listed as endangered or threatened at the state level.

The study was motivated by W.L. Minckley's observation that legislative, conservation, academic, and management attempts have largely failed to reverse the endangerment of Arizona's native fish, and that public opinion recognizing native fishes as valuable resources is essential for successful conservation. The research employed a telephone survey administered in February 1995 through the Survey Research Center at [[Arizona State University]], achieving an overall response rate of 52% (50.0% urban, 66.0% rural, 51.5% anglers). The survey consisted of approximately 40 closed-ended questions covering outdoor activities, environmental attitudes, knowledge of native fish, and willingness to support various conservation measures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Arizona's aquatic habitats have been severely altered over the past century. Native fish species that once served as important food sources for pioneers and Native Americans have been pushed to the brink of extinction. The primary threats identified include:

- **Habitat modification**: Cattle grazing in riparian zones, dam construction, and irrigation canals have fundamentally altered stream and river ecosystems
- **Water availability**: Declining water tables and water diversion for agriculture and urban use have reduced available habitat
- **Non-native species**: Introduction of non-native fish species for recreational fishing has created competition and predation pressures
- **Mining impacts**: Mining operations have degraded water quality and habitat structure

The study reveals significant public misconceptions about threats to native fish. Nearly 50% of non-anglers incorrectly believed intensive commercial fishing contributed to native fish decline, while the majority did not recognize cattle grazing as a contributing factor. Many respondents incorrectly identified acid rain, increasing global temperatures, and motor boating as major threats.

## Key Findings

**Public Awareness and Knowledge:**
- Only 33.8% of non-anglers were aware that Arizona has native fish species, compared to 67.6% of anglers
- 68.8% of anglers could recognize the name of one or more native fish species, versus only 35.8% of non-anglers
- Over 40% of non-anglers did not know whether native fish populations were declining
- Anglers demonstrated significantly higher mean knowledge scores (1.755) compared to both urban and rural non-angler groups

**Support for Conservation:**
- Non-anglers showed strong support for conservation across all measures, with majority support for each conservation action
- Anglers demonstrated lower overall support scores (mean: 5.842) compared to urban residents (7.234) and rural residents (6.322)
- Urban residents showed significantly higher support than anglers for most conservation measures
- The only conservation measure lacking majority support among anglers was higher taxes

**Gender Differences:**
- Males in both angler and non-angler groups demonstrated greater knowledge about native fish
- Females showed significantly higher support for conservation efforts despite lower knowledge scores
- Female anglers had mean support scores of 6843 compared to male anglers at 5595
- 76.5% of anglers were male, while 63.9% of non-anglers were female

**Effect of Information Provision:**
- Overall support scores showed mixed results after information was provided
- Support increased for most individual conservation measures after information was given
- Notable exception: willingness to support water consumption regulations declined after information provision across all groups
- Support for cattle grazing restrictions increased substantially after respondents learned of its impact on native fish habitat
- Anglers became less willing to support reduction of non-native fish stocking after receiving information

**Demographic Patterns:**
- Age showed no significant relationship with knowledge or support in non-anglers
- Education level positively correlated with knowledge (respondents with college or graduate degrees scored significantly higher)
- Education level showed no significant correlation with support for conservation
- Mean age across groups was approximately 45 years with normal distribution

**Knowledge-Support Relationship:**
- No significant positive correlation between knowledge and support was found
- Results suggest a negative correlation, particularly evident in anglers and males who had highest knowledge but lowest support scores
- This finding challenges assumptions that education alone promotes conservation support

**Specific Conservation Measures:**
- 83.15% of anglers and 86.23% of urban residents supported general efforts to protect native fish from extinction
- Support for regulating water consumption: 66.48% (anglers) to 78.26% (urban residents)
- Support for higher taxes: lowest across all groups (38.55% anglers, 50.72% urban residents)
- Support for limiting dam construction: 60.34% (anglers) to 73.91% (urban residents)
- Support for cattle grazing restrictions: 56.42% (anglers) to 69.57% (urban residents)
- Support for reducing non-native fish stocking: 62.01% (anglers) to 76.81% (urban residents)

The study concludes that while the Arizona public demonstrates strong general support for native fish conservation, there exists limited awareness and substantial misconceptions about the actual threats facing these species. The findings suggest that targeted education efforts, particularly regarding the impacts of cattle grazing and water management, could enhance conservation support, though the relationship between knowledge and support is more complex than simple information provision.
