# Fish Collections from the Rio San Pedro and San Bernardino Creek, Sonora-Arizona Border

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

This scanned journal article (OCR-degraded, from the *Journal of the Washington Academy of Sciences*) reports freshwater fish collections from the [[Rio San Pedro]] and [[San Bernardino Creek]] on the [[Sonora-Arizona Border]]. It also contains a taxonomic note on an invertebrate with leg and genital structures, whose identity is unclear from the scan. Collections span 1930 to 1944. The paper documents the southwestern subspecies of the [[Black Bullhead]], notes several sucker and minnow species of the drainage, and records a [[Black Bullhead]] introduction at an early date.

## Background & Description

- **Study region:** Upper [[Rio San Pedro]], a river that flows from northern Sonora, Mexico, into Arizona and is part of the drainage of the Gila River system. The article describes it as a permanent or near-permanent stream in its upper reaches, with a steep, uneven flow.
- **Collectors and dates (as legible in OCR):**
  - Collections near San Pedro Ranch, April 21–22, 1930, by the authors and Francis H. Miller.
  - Collection on April 2, 1930 (OCR-garbled), at the junction of the Rio San Pedro and Rio San Bernardino, about 4 miles south of San Pedro Ranch.
  - Collection by James H. Simon on September 10, 1943, at San Bernardino Creek, about 1 mile south of the U.S.–Mexico border.
  - Collection by Marrin Frost and John Hendrickson on April 8, 1944, in a pond about 1 mile upstream.
- **Taxonomic context:** The paper compares the local bullhead with the northern subspecies, [[Ameiurus melas]] (Rafinesque), and the southwestern subspecies [[Ameiurus melas calidus]] (Girard). It also notes that the local bullhead has longer dorsal and pectoral spines and fewer anal rays.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat structure:** The collecting site on the Rio San Pedro had a riffle roughly 25 feet long, split into rivulets about 1–2 feet wide and 2.5 inches deep. Another riffle was about 1 foot long and 3 feet wide, and a long, shallow stretch was about 80 feet across. Rocks were covered with algal growth, and the river bank was lined with cottonwoods and similar riparian vegetation.
- **Pool habitat:** A pool about 25 feet long, 3–12 feet wide and about 4 feet deep was cut off from the main stream by a gravel bar about 5 feet high. Spring seepage maintained its water level.
- **Water conditions (April 21–22, 1930):** Water temperature about 73°F; air temperature about 85°F.
- **Rainfall:** The average annual rainfall for 1915 to 1949 (inclusive) at Sonoita-area stations was only about 11 inches (OCR ambiguous), according to the report.
- **Elevation:** The San Pedro Ranch sits at roughly 4,000 feet (OCR-garbled).
- **Human use:** Local people fished the pool heavily. The authors recorded a 16-ounce bullhead as the largest specimen taken there.
- **Invasive introduction:** The authors note that the bullhead was introduced at an early date, probably before 1906, according to local testimony.

## Key Findings

- **Fish collection, Rio San Pedro (1930):** The authors collected 3 large bullheads and 1 half-grown bullhead from the Rio San Pedro and its tributaries. A single half-grown bullhead was caught in a seine at night. One large female bullhead was carrying eggs under slight pressure, suggesting spawning was imminent or in progress.
- **Pool catch:** A single pool yielded about 123 catfish, mostly young, which were crushed and discarded. This indicates high local harvest.
- **Specimen size range (Shallow riffles, 1930):** Fish ranged from roughly 37 to 118 mm total length (OCR-uncertain).
- **Southwestern bullhead records:**
  - 3 young-adult specimens (about 117–151 mm) seined by J. H. Simon, San Bernardino Creek, September 10, 1943.
  - 5 adult specimens (119–209 mm) caught by Frost and Hendrickson, April 8, 1944.
  - Anal ray counts were 20 (n=3), 21 (n=3) and 22 (n=2).
- **Subspecies conclusion:** The paper places the Rio San Pedro bullhead with the southwestern subspecies [[Ameiurus melas calidus]] because of its longer spines and fewer anal rays than the northern form.
- **Species possibly extirpated or absent:** Two fishes known from the drainage, the [[Gila Loach]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and the [[Gila Mountain Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarkii*), were not seen during the survey, though both still occur north of the border in Arizona. The [[Scaleless Spinedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) was noted alongside them as a species of concern.
- **Recorded first Mexican records:** The paper says several of the species (listed as first records from Mexico) were deposited in the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology.
- **Non-fish taxon:** A taxonomic description of a small arthropod-like invertebrate with a bifurcated ninth leg and an unusual gonopod structure is included. Its exact identity is obscured by OCR errors, so it should be verified against the original publication.
- **Overall pattern:** Shallow riffles, pools and spring-fed sections form the core habitat, and water is limited by low rainfall. Early introduction and heavy local harvest are the main human pressures recorded.
