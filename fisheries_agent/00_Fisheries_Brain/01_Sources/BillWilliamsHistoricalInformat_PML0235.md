# Bill Williams River Basin: Historical Records, Ranch Histories and Refuge Flow Logs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is an archival compilation sent by Chuck Minckley to Paul Marsh on June 5, 2007, for preservation. It gathers historical travel accounts, oral histories, ranch and mining records, flood and flow logs, and refuge annual notes for the [[Bill Williams River]] and its delta. It covers roughly 1775 to 2005. Fish appear only incidentally: an oral interview records that large [[Razorback Sucker]] and, less often, [[Bonytail]] were caught in flood pools near the historic Esquerra ranch. The bulk of the material concerns river hydrology, riparian vegetation loss and recovery, land use, and wildlife on the [[Lake Havasu National Wildlife Refuge]].

## Background & Description

- **Archive provenance:** Minckley notes the refuge staff supplied the "History of Refuge" document, which includes a 2004 interview by Kathleen Blair. He is passing it on because it had "no where to go in my files."
- **Historical accounts:**
  - [[Francisco Garces]] (August 2, 1775) named the Bill Williams the Rio de Santa Maria and described the [[Colorado River]]'s seasonal rise (February to June) and fall, with riparian groves of willow, cottonwood and mesquite.
  - [[Amiel Whipple]] (February 1853) described the Colorado near the Bill Williams junction: the fork was about 25 feet wide and 2 feet deep, and the Colorado about 250 yards wide.
- **Families and land use:**
  - The [[Esquerra Family]]: Ramon Esquerra (born 1844 in Rayon, Sonora) homesteaded near the [[Kohen Ranch]] around 1870, dug an irrigation ditch with two dynamited tunnels, and grew beans, corn, wheat, alfalfa, melons, figs, pomegranates, peaches and grapes. He raised 18 children (not all survived).
  - Joe (Leon) Padilla, a grandson, was born on the ranch in March 1919 and was interviewed in January 2004.
- **Mining:** [[Swansea Mining Town]] operated from 1862. Silver-lead was struck in 1866, copper later; a town of about 500 existed by 1909, and production ran until 1944.
- **Dam:** [[Alamo Dam]] was begun in 1968, washed out, and was completed in 1970 as a [[U.S. Army Corps of Engineers]] flood-control structure.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Basin character.** The Bill Williams basin covers about 5,000 square miles. The Big Sandy and Santa Maria rivers join to form the Bill Williams just above Alamo Lake, about 40 miles upstream of its junction with the Colorado at [[Lake Havasu]].

**Flow regime before and after the dam:**
- Pre-dam peaks exceeded 100,000 cfs, perhaps 200,000 cfs rarely, with summer base flows at zero for long stretches.
- Post-dam maximum flood is about 7,000 cfs. Base flow is more stable, but the 1970s included long periods (most of the decade) with no flow at all, unique to the post-dam record.
- The log lists post-dam floods from 1969 to 1995, typically 1,000 to 4,000 cfs, with the 1993 flood at 7,000 cfs and the 1995 flood at 6,800 cfs.

**Riparian and vegetation effects:**
- The 1980 flood (about 4,000 cfs over winter and spring) killed gallery cottonwoods (800 acres of gallery cottonwood; 1,500 acres of riparian habitat damaged in total). In a 40-acre plot near Mosquito Flats, over 95% of cottonwoods and 85% of willows died.
- In 1985, 85% of cottonwoods under four years old died from drought and low groundwater.
- Salt cedar (tamarisk) invaded aggressively after 1980, and willows recovered better than cottonwoods.
- Dewatering for alfalfa irrigation by Planet Ranch and inadequate Alamo releases were identified by a Rivers West hydrologist as major causes of riparian loss in 1978 to 1980.
- Fires destroyed riparian cover in 1981, 1989, 1990, 1994 and 1995.

**Wildlife notes:**
- Bighorn sheep: 15 in the Buckskins (1985), about 30 (1989), about 50 (1990). Radio-collared animals crossed the river that marks the boundary between *O. c. mexicana* and *O. c. nelsoni*.
- Mule deer, javelina, beaver (described as "very active" in one year, increasing by 1963), otter ("occasional"), antelope, porcupine and a mountain lion with cubs.
- Birds: 78 heron and 85 cormorant nests on Heron Island in an undated refuge report; Western Grebe breeding (1963); Pelicans; a Yellow-billed Cuckoo population estimated at 75 birds in 1990.

**Fish.** Padilla recalled that during floods large fish moved up the river into pools, were caught with pitchforks, and were dried in the sun like beef jerky. He identified the mounts in the refuge office as Razorbacks (most common catch) and Bonytails (less often).

**Human pressures.** Trespass cattle (up to 300 head plus 35 trespass animals in 1963), grazing leases in the delta (125 AUMs in 1962, 600 AUMs in 1964), and sheep grazing by Planet Ranch (1986) raised predator-control and disease concerns for bighorns.

## Key Findings

- **Hydrologic transformation:** Alamo Dam converted a highly variable flood-and-drought river into one with lower peaks (about 7,000 cfs maximum) and more stable base flow, but with multi-year periods of no flow in the 1970s.
- **Riparian loss and recovery:** Floods, drought, dewatering, fire and salt cedar invasion repeatedly reduced cottonwood and willow stands, and recruitment depended strongly on flood timing.
- **Land protection:** The Nature Conservancy bought the Kohen and Esquerra ranches in 1975 to 1977 (1,748 acres) and transferred them to the refuge, which farmed them for wildlife until the 1981 floods; fields were then left to natural succession.
- **Refuge boundary changes:** In 1941, 748 acres of the Bill Williams delta were included in Lake Havasu Refuge. In 1965, the refuge lost 23,760.6 acres (more than half its area) when Arizona and California took over the lake shoreline.
- **Ownership changes:** Arizona Ranch and Minerals bought the three ranches in the 1950s; Scottsdale bought them in July 1984 for their water rights.
- **Fish record:** Oral history indicates Razorbacks and Bonytails were once taken in flood pools near the Esquerra ranch, a record of native fish presence that predates the dam.
- **Data caveats:** Source notes contain internal inconsistencies (for example, the dam start date is given as 1968 and 1969, and the 1980 flood as 4,000 cfs and the 1969 flood as 2,500 cfs in different sections). Scientific names of the fish are not given; identifications rest on refuge mounts and a family member's recollection.
