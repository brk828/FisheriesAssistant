# Recreational Use Patterns at Flaming Gorge Reservoir, 1963-1965

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife publication (Resource Publication 70, January 1969) by John G. Hewston and the late Donald R. Franklin, produced through the [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit]] at Utah State University, documents how recreational use developed on [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] during its first three years of filling (1963-1965). Data came from interviews, mailed questionnaires, creel censuses, postal-card surveys, traffic counts and agency records. Visits rose from an estimated 231,065 in 1963 to 786,103 in 1965. Sight-seeing dominated in 1963, while fishing for [[Rainbow Trout]] became the leading primary purpose in 1964 and 1965. The study concludes that access points, facility development and fish condition shaped angler distribution, and that the reservoir substantially boosted the local economy.

## Background & Description

The study addresses a gap in planning knowledge for new reservoir-based [[National Recreation Area]]s. Reservoirs were said to make up about one-third of U.S. inland fishing waters, excluding the Great Lakes, and to support 20 percent of sport fishing trips (Jenkins, 1961). The reservoir was built by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] on the Green River, a tributary of the Colorado River, as part of the [[Colorado River Storage Project]]. The dam is 450 feet high. At full pool the reservoir was expected to cover about 42,000 acres, with 375 miles of shoreline and 3,789,000 acre-feet of storage. It began filling November 1, 1962.

The reservoir straddles two topographic zones: a high desert upper portion (about 60 miles long, 6,100-6,500 ft elevation, sagebrush and shadscale vegetation) and a forested, steep-walled canyon lower portion on the eastern Uinta Mountains edge. Annual precipitation was 7-10 inches on the desert and about 12 inches in the canyons.

Pre-impoundment use was limited: trout fishing in mountain tributaries, [[Channel Catfish]] angling in the warm, turbid Green River, big-game hunting for pronghorn, elk and mule deer, and occasional camping. Administration was split among the [[U.S. Forest Service]] (Ashley National Forest), the [[Bureau of Land Management]], and state fish and game agencies. After impoundment, the [[National Park Service]] took over the non-forest lands, and the Bureau of Reclamation managed the dam and water levels.

Before impoundment, the Colorado, Utah and Wyoming agencies rotenone-treated about 450 miles of the Green River and tributaries in 1962 to remove undesirable species. Stocking began in spring 1963 with 500,000 [[Kokanee Salmon]] and 4,000,000 [[Rainbow Trout]] fingerlings.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water and temperature:** The lower canyons were cool, shaded and protected from high waves. Turbidity from the Green River and tributaries in April and May affected spring angling distribution. Summer heat had no discernible effect on angler distribution.
- **Fish condition:** Condition factors (K) of rainbow trout in age groups II-IV were 1.11-1.48 (1963), 1.01-1.19 (1964) and 0.99-1.98 (1965), as reported by Eiserman et al. Trout in the canyon were in noticeably poorer condition through 1965, and some anglers moved to the desert to catch "plumper" fish.
- **Ice and seasons:** Ice formation dictated winter fishing locations. Fishing was restricted on ice in Units 1 and 2 in early 1964. Open-water angling ran May through October.
- **Fish community:** Pre-impoundment fish were removed, so only stocked game fish drew anglers. Cutthroat trout, carp, brook trout and brown trout were occasionally caught. Kokanee stocked in 1963-1964 were not found in creel checks.
- **Habitat and wildlife:** Visitors often did not recognise small wildlife such as chipmunks, ground squirrels and songbirds as wildlife, and many anglers could not tell one fish species from another.

## Key Findings

**Visitation and origin**
- Estimated visits: 231,065 (1963), 521,843 (1964), 786,103 (1965). Forest Service area had about twice the Park Service visits in 1964-1965.
- Utah residents were present in 68% of parties in 1963 and 82% in 1964 and 1965. Wyoming, Colorado and California were the other main contributors.
- Salt Lake City was the most common large-city origin (32%, 42%, 41% of parties), followed by Ogden.

**Purpose and activities**
- Primary purpose: sight-seeing 56% of parties in 1963; fishing led in 1964 (42%) and 1965 (37%), with sight-seeing second (37%, 36%).
- Sight-seeing was the most common activity (74%, 76%, 91% of parties), followed by fishing in 1964 (71%) and 1965 (75%).
- Fishing involvement rose from 16% of parties in 1963 to 71% and 75% in 1964-1965.

**Sport fishery**
- Catch rate: 1.24 fish/angler-hour (1964) and 1.33 (1965). Boat anglers outperformed shore anglers (1.46 vs 0.79 in 1964; 1.64 vs 1.17 in 1965). Including released fish, the 1965 rate was 1.47.
- Rainbow trout made up over 99% of the catch in 1964 and 1965.
- Mean length of creeled trout was 11.2 inches in both years; Unit 1 averaged 12.2 inches and Unit 4 averaged 9.8 inches (1964) and 10.5 inches (1965).
- Angler success (parties with fish): 84% (1964) and 93% (1965).
- Bait was used by 71% of anglers, with angleworms (42%) the most popular. Metal lures (48%) were preferred over plugs (26%).

**Angler distribution**
- Access points, especially boat ramps, were the main determinant. Most boat anglers fished within 5 miles of their launch site.
- Facility development on the desert (temporary sites in 1964, permanent Lucerne Valley site in 1965) strongly influenced distribution.
- Reciprocal fishing stamps offset state-line licensing effects; boat registration laws had no effect.

**Facilities and visitor satisfaction**
- Visitors rated scenery highest (mean 4.87-4.96 of 5), followed by parking, weather and camping. Fish-cleaning stations (2.64-3.00) and swimming areas (2.99-3.38) rated lowest.
- Campgrounds were designed mainly for tent campers and were often inadequate for parties with about 1.3-1.5 vehicles and trailers.
- Many boat ramps were out of the water until late summer.
- Improved facilities were requested by more than two-thirds of parties each year.

**Economic impact**
- Visitor expenditures within 50 miles were $611,339 (1964) and $1,443,049 (1965). Mean daily spending per visitor was $2.13 (1964) and $2.11 (1965).
- New businesses included marinas, grocery stores, a riding stable and a gas station, and the local economy was judged to have improved.

**Local residents**
- Local use was present in about one-fourth of interviewed parties. Over 95% said recreation sites were easy to find.
- Nearly all local respondents judged the fishery a definite improvement over pre-impoundment conditions. Big-game hunting quality was seen as declining by 30-63% of respondents.

**Conclusions**
- Fishery management appeared successful in creating a quality rainbow trout fishery, though canyon trout condition began declining by late 1965.
- Future use patterns could not be predicted from this study alone. The authors recommend that facilities be built before a reservoir is filled, and that interpretive programs be developed early.
