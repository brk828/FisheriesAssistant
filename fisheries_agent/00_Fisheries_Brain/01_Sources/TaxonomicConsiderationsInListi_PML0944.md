# Taxonomic Considerations in Listing Subspecies Under the U.S. Endangered Species Act

#ichthyology #marine-biology #taxonomy

## Executive Summary

This perspective paper by [[Haig et al. 2006]] in *Conservation Biology* examines how taxonomic uncertainty, particularly around subspecies, complicates listings under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA). Although roughly one-quarter of ESA-listed taxa are subspecies, the authors find no universally accepted subspecies definition across taxonomic groups and no published standardized criteria. They propose two minimum biological criteria, discreteness and significance (borrowed from the [[Distinct Population Segment]] policy), as a starting point for defining subspecies, and urge professional taxonomic societies to publish and periodically update peer-reviewed species and subspecies lists. The paper includes a review of subspecies use in birds, mammals, fishes, herpetofauna, invertebrates, plants and fungi.

## Background & Description

- **Authors and affiliations:** Susan M. Haig and co-authors from the [[USGS Forest and Rangeland Ecosystem Science Center]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and Oregon State University.
- **Publication:** *Conservation Biology* 20(6): 1584–1594, December 2006. Submitted November 22, 2005; accepted May 3, 2006.
- **Scope:** A Spanish-language abstract is included. Keywords: distinct population segment, evolutionarily significant unit, IUCN Red List, nomenclature, taxonomy.
- **Legal background:** The original 1973 ESA definition of "species" included any subspecies of fish, wildlife or plants. The 1978 amendment substituted "any distinct population segment (DPS) of any species of vertebrate fish or wildlife, which interbreeds when mature." Plants are not covered by DPS listings, and "take" prohibitions do not apply to them under the ESA.
- **Other frameworks recognizing infraspecific taxa:** [[IUCN Red List]], [[CITES]] appendices, TRAFFIC, Brazil's national endangered species list, Canada's Species at Risk Act, Australia's Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act, and South Africa's Biodiversity Act.
- **Concepts discussed:**
  - [[Biological Species Concept]] (Mayr 1963): based on reproductive isolation, which the authors note is rarely applied to subspecies.
  - [[Phylogenetic Species Concept]]: species are the smallest monophyletic units, so subspecies are not recognized.
  - [[75 Percent Rule]] (Amadon 1949): a subspecies is valid if 75% or more of a population is separable from all (or >99% of) members of the overlapping population. The authors note disagreement over the threshold.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Conservation rationale:** Infraspecific taxa represent evolutionary potential within a species. Listing them allows managers to target the most critical unit in need of protection, focusing efforts on vulnerable areas rather than whole species.
- **Taxonomic patterns across groups:**
  - Vertebrates and plants have more described subspecies than invertebrates and fungi, which remain mostly studied at the species level.
  - Birds are better dispersers, which the authors suggest may yield fewer or less distinct subspecies.
  - Fish subspecies are often based on allopatry, and [[Mayden 1999]] noted that strict criteria could assign a unique subspecies to every isolated creek or pond.
- **Historic examples of failed subspecies:** The leopard (*Panthera onca*), Seaside Sparrow (*Ammodramus maritimus nigrescens*) and Red-winged Blackbird (*Agelaius phoeniceus*) subspecies did not hold up under scrutiny.
- **Application of subspecies in listings:** Some subspecies are well accepted, including the Florida panther (*Felis concolor coryi*), Northern Spotted Owl (*Strix occidentalis caurina*) and Marbled Murrelet (*Brachyramphus marmoratus marmoratus*).

## Key Findings

**Prevalence of subspecies in ESA listings (as of October 2005, from Table 1):**

| Taxon | Subspecies share of ESA listings (as stated) |
|---|---|
| Birds | 43% of ESA-listed birds |
| Mammals | Over two-thirds |
| Fish | Only 1.5% of U.S. fish listings are at subspecific rank; 31% (44/142) of domestic fish listings are at DPS level |
| Herpetofauna | 29% |
| Plants | 123 of 745 listed plants (subspecies including varieties) |
| Lichens/fungi | Only two lichen species listed (*Cladonia perforata*, *Gymnoderma lineare*) |

Overall, about one-quarter of ESA-listed taxa are subspecies.

**Taxonomic statistics (Table 3):**
- Birds: 9,588 species worldwide; 650 infraspecific taxa described.
- Mammals: 5,416 species; 432 infraspecific taxa.
- Fish: approximately 24,600 species; 2,424 infraspecific taxa.
- Amphibians: 5,743 species; 263 infraspecific taxa.
- Reptiles: 8,240 species; 287 infraspecific taxa.
- Invertebrates: about 1,288,518 described species (roughly 96% of described animals); infraspecific counts are approximate.
- Fungi: 80,000–150,000 described species of an estimated 1.5 million; about 800 new names are catalogued each year.

**Key points by taxonomic group:**
- **Birds:** Following the BSC, 315 of 607 North American species were reclassified as subspecies. The AOU stopped listing subspecies after 1957 due to committee time constraints. A global assessment found 36% of avian subspecies to be phylogenetically distinct, with the lowest distinctness in North American and European taxa.
- **Mammals:** Editors of *Mammal Species of the World* update the list every 10 years. Some traditional subspecies may not reflect genetic differences, and cryptic taxa have been overlooked.
- **Fish:** The Name of Fishes Committee does not list subspecies. Some taxonomists argue against fish subspecies, favoring the DPS and ESU approach for Pacific salmon.
- **Herpetofauna:** The subspecies proportion within genera ranges from 0% (crocodiles) to 56% (snakes), with up to 12 subspecies per species. The subspecies concept has fallen from favor in modern herpetological systematics.
- **Invertebrates:** Subspecific ESA listings are mostly limited to well-described groups such as butterflies and freshwater mollusks. DPSs are not available for invertebrates, though subspecies are.
- **Plants:** Of 104 contemporary monographs reviewed by McDade (1995), 56% included infraspecific taxa, with subspecies and variety used roughly equally. This supports the ESA's inclusion of "variety."
- **Fungi:** Subspecies, variety and form are used interchangeably in practice, and no world or North American checklist exists. Ranks are often converted in later treatments, so standardized criteria are unlikely in the near future.

**Recommendations:**
1. **Minimum biological criteria:** Propose discreteness and biological significance as starting criteria for subspecies. Satisfying them would not automatically warrant Linnaean trinomial recognition.
2. **Unidirectional influence:** Conservation priorities should influence taxonomy only through legal requirements, and taxonomy should not be shaped by conservation goals.
3. **Documentation:** New subspecies descriptions should explicitly state their concepts, criteria and geographic distributions.
4. **Multiple lines of evidence:** Combine morphological, molecular, ecological and behavioral data. Significant phenotypic differences may indicate local adaptation when direct evidence is impractical.
5. **Standardized lists:** Professional societies should publish and update peer-reviewed lists, potentially hosted on [[Global Biodiversity Information Facility]] or the [[National Biological Information Infrastructure]].
6. **Agency support:** Professional societies should maintain rosters of qualified taxonomists and help identify outdated listings for revision.
7. **Adaptive management:** Assessments should be reevaluated periodically as techniques evolve.

**Case example:** The final rule for the Southwestern Willow Flycatcher (*Empidonax traillii extimus*) relied on a "majority opinion" that it was a valid subspecies, illustrating how agencies must interpret contested taxonomy.

**Conclusion:** The authors argue that the ESA's inclusion of subspecies and DPSs provides taxonomists with a distinctive opportunity. Clarifying subspecies status would benefit listing decisions and taxonomic understanding. The authors acknowledge that their recommendations are U.S.-centric and call for a global effort, including through CITES and the IUCN Red List.
