# The Control of Population-Density through Social Behaviour: A Hypothesis

#population-ecology #ornithology #behavioural-ecology #marine-biology

## Executive Summary

This paper, by V. C. Wynne-Edwards (published in *Isis*, 1959), proposes that animal populations are normally held at a safe density by **social conventions** rather than by direct competition for food. Food is the ultimate limiting resource, but animals compete for conventional substitutes such as [[Territoriality|territory]], nest-sites, roost positions and [[Social Hierarchy|social rank]]. These conventions cap density at an optimum level, which protects the food supply from depletion, a process the author calls "overfishing." Social competition is density-dependent and gives animals an index of their own numbers, which acts as a feedback signal for density regulation through emigration and changes in birth and survival rates. The paper is a hypothesis built mainly on bird examples, with a marine case study on pelagic birds and plankton. The author notes that a fuller treatment was in preparation.

## Background & Description

The paper opens by noting that animals are dispersed in many patterns, from tightly gregarious flocks and colonies to evenly spaced individuals. Many birds switch between dispersal modes by season or time of day, for example roosting or breeding together and feeding over a wide area. Population density is still measurable as individuals per unit area, and the chosen area unit should match the animals' mobility.

The author argues that animals play a large part in setting their own densities. Food is almost always the critical limiting resource. Humans show the same pattern when they exploit fisheries and whaling under free enterprise: they risk depleting the stock and entering a spiral of diminishing returns. The paper then asks whether natural selection could have produced a "protective convention" in animals comparable to the human agreements used to limit catches.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Marine evidence.** During the [[Dana Expedition]] to the North Atlantic (1920-22), [[Jespersen (1930)]] recorded pelagic bird numbers and took routine plankton hauls. Both varied more than a hundredfold between the richest and poorest areas. When averaged on a 10-degree latitude-longitude grid, crude bird density and crude plankton density correlated at 85% (P < 0.001). The author also cites [[Hardy & Gunther (1935)]], who found a similar density correlation around South Georgia between [[Whalebone Whales]] and their main food, the [[Antarctic Krill]] (*Euphausia superba*).

**Terrestrial evidence.** [[Kluyver & Tinbergen (1953)]] studied [[Great Tit]], [[Blue Tit]] and [[Coal Tit]] populations in Holland over many years. These species bred at consistently higher densities in attractive mixed woods than in pinewoods, and their minimum acceptable territory size was smaller in the richer woods. Birds preferred mixed woods, but once those were full, surplus birds settled in pinewoods. The author reads this as attractiveness being balanced by the repelling effect of the resident population.

**Human parallels.** The author notes that the [[International Whaling Convention]] used catch quotas expressed in "blue-whale units," closing the fishery once an agreed catch was reached. He also mentions limiting fishermen or dividing fishing grounds as alternatives. These human measures serve as analogues for the animal conventions he proposes.

## Key Findings

- **Food is the ultimate limit.** Animals regulate their own densities to match food supply, and food sets the ceiling density a habitat can support.
- **Overfishing is inherent.** Unrestricted competition for food tends toward over-exploitation in every hunter-prey relationship, not only in humans.
- **Territory as convention.** Breeding territories are conventional parcels that remove direct competition for food. They cap density when their size tracks habitat productivity, as with the tits.
- **Communal conventions.** In colonial birds such as [[Rook]]s, nest-sites act as the token objective. Their number is limited by the colony perimeter, and access to communal food depends on holding one.
- **Social hierarchy.** [[Peck Order]]s in higher vertebrates allocate scarce resources to high-ranking individuals and push surplus individuals out, which reduces pressure on the habitat.
- **Year-round regulation.** Robins hold feeding territories outside the breeding season. Roosting [[Starling]]s defend customary perches and may move on when a roost becomes congested.
- **Epideictic displays.** Collective displays such as the "crows' parliament" of Rooks and Jackdaws may have evolved to demonstrate population density and provide feedback for regulation.
- **Social basis.** These conventions require an underlying social organisation. The author argues that society may have originated as a vehicle for conventional competition, with rivalry always present beneath cooperation.
- **Feedback mechanism.** Social competition is density-dependent and gives an index of numbers. This feeds back into regulation via emigration and via changes in birth and survival rates, with the latter considered the most common route.
- **Scope.** The author states that the hypothesis is developed mainly with birds but may apply to other animals. He calls for further testing, and the paper is presented as a hypothesis, not a proven theory.
