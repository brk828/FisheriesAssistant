# Dexter National Fish Hatchery Annual Narrative Report, FY 1982

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This annual narrative, submitted November 10, 1982 by Hatchery Manager [[Buddy L. Jensen]], reviews the work of the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (NFH) in New Mexico during fiscal year 1982. The station's fish culture program consists of holding, spawning, rearing and distributing imperiled desert fishes. The year's main work was research into holding, spawning and culturing endangered species, especially [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]]. Both species had poor survival from fry to fingerling stages. Disease outbreaks ([[Costia]], [[Lernaea]]) and a parasitic fungus ([[Ichthyophonus]]) also affected production. The report also covers hybridization work on [[Gila Hybrids]], [[Humpback Chub]] broodstock problems, successful spawning of [[Comanche Springs Pupfish]], and cooperation with other agencies.

## Background & Description

**Staff.** The station had five permanent employees and several seasonal or temporary workers. Key roles included:
- [[Buddy L. Jensen]], Hatchery Manager (GS-11)
- Theop Inslee, Fish Culturist (GS-11)
- Troy D. Linham, Assistant Manager and Maintenance Worker (GS-7, promoted to GS-7 in early October)
- Sharon Coats (promoted to GS-5 Office Assistant in September)
- Phyllis Inslee, who transferred to the U.S. Postal Service in October

Other staff included a coop student, a YCC enrollee, three fish taggers, and a student volunteer. Roger Hamman (Willow Beach NFH) was detailed to the station, and Ron Rivard (Mescalero NFH) spent two weeks there assisting with tagging operations.

**Mission.** For FY 1982 the station was asked to:
- Provide refuge for 12 species
- Produce 100,000 fry and 100,000 fingerling Razorback Suckers, and 100,000 fingerling Colorado Squawfish
- Continue captive culture work on the Humpback Chub and Comanche Springs Pupfish
- Support the Rio Grande Fishes Recovery Team
- Distribute fish to Arizona and Colorado, among other states

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report describes the station as a captive-propagation center for fishes that are rare in the wild. Hatchery work depends on controlling water conditions, plankton blooms and food supply. Several failures were linked to these factors:
- **Zooplankton:** Poor production of zooplankton blooms in February reduced the food available for fry.
- **Holding time:** Fry were kept in holding houses too long after swim-up.
- **Predation:** Tiger salamander larvae preyed on fry in ponds.
- **Source material:** Wild broodstock came from [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. Fish were also taken from the [[Pecos River]] for bluntnose shiner sampling.

Stocking locations included the [[Salt River]] (Arizona), the [[Colorado River]], and hatchery ponds.

## Key Findings

**Razorback Sucker**
- 147 additional adults were collected from Lake Mohave. These were added to 75 adults held over from FY 1981.
- 71 females were spawned, producing about 7,500,000 eggs.
- 1,660,652 fry hatched. The percent hatch figure is illegible in the scan.
- 570,000 fry were hauled to Arizona and stocked in the Salt River and its tributaries. About 110,000 more were stocked in four rearing ponds. Several hundred thousand fry died in the holding house from a Costia outbreak.
- Pond return was poor: 48,800 one- to two-inch fingerlings were recovered, a return the report calls poor. The report attributes this to poor zooplankton blooms, holding fry too long, unavailable food organisms, and salamander predation.
- 42,000 three- to six-inch fingerlings were nose-tagged and distributed.

**Colorado Squawfish**
- 113 adults were received from Willow Beach NFH and 73 from the Region office. These were added to 173 adults held over from FY 1981.
- About 3.06 million eggs were produced. The number of females spawned is partly illegible in the scan.
- 400,000 fry were stocked in three ponds for rearing.
- Return was extremely poor: about 33,100 one-inch fingerlings, roughly an 8.3% return. A further 23,460 fingerlings were recovered from Pond 1B.
- Starvation was the probable limiting factor, because food was scarce while staff were occupied tagging 42,000 razorback suckers. The report says earlier spawning would likely have improved returns. Lack of knowledge about fertilization and timing also contributed.
- Several thousand two- to three-inch nose-tagged fingerlings were delivered to Region 2 for distribution in the Colorado River.

**Gila Hybrids**
- Two Gila hybrid F2 self-crosses and four hybrid back-crosses were completed.
- Several hundred specimens, from swim-up fry to six-inch fingerlings, were preserved and sent to Dr. W. L. Minckley at Arizona State University for genetic and taxonomic study.

**Humpback Chub**
- 30 brood fish from Willow Beach NFH arrived in October 1981. They did not feed well, had lesions, and were in poor condition at spawning time.
- Males produced good milt, but no viable eggs were obtained from the females. Males were used in back-crosses with parental species.
- Several Little Colorado River fish were lost or sacrificed. Suspected hybrids were euthanized. The remaining fish were donated to the Arizona-Sonora Desert Museum in Tucson.

**Comanche Springs Pupfish**
- 300 newly hatched fry were moved to a new pond on June 25.
- The fish matured and spawned by August 31, a period of 67 days. The report notes that a 70-day maturation period is likely, given earlier observation.
- By mid-September the pond held several thousand young.

**Other Species**
- Bluntnose Shiner: Four adults from FY 1981 were moved to stream habitat. No reproduction was observed.
- Woundfin: Eleven adults overwintered and produced a few fry. 25 fish were moved to a new pond in fall.

**Specimen Distribution**
- Demand for preserved and live specimens remained high. Recipients included the Smithsonian Institution, the American Museum of Natural History, Colorado Division of Wildlife, UCLA, Arizona State University, Brigham Young University, Oklahoma State University, New Mexico State University, and Eastern New Mexico University. One institution name is illegible in the scan.

**Disease and Fish Health**
- Costia outbreaks in razorback fry held in the holding house caused severe losses. Formalin prophylaxis reduced but did not eliminate the problem. In future, fry will be stocked as soon as they swim up.
- Lernaea (anchor worm) infestations affected razorback brood, squawfish brood, and a population of Chihuahua chub. These were controlled with Masoten treatments.
- Ichthyophonus was diagnosed in Pecos Gambusia (since removed from the station), mosquitofish, Razorback Sucker, Comanche Springs Pupfish, and Gila Topminnow. The fungus appears to limit adult survival of both pupfish and topminnow. Theop Inslee was preparing a proposal to study it.

**Cooperation with Other Agencies**
- Worked with Arizona on razorback sampling and extensive Gila topminnow transplants.
- Assisted New Mexico with sampling of the Pecos River for bluntnose shiners.
- Dr. Paul Marsh (Arizona State University) spent several weeks at the station studying temperature and hatching requirements for Colorado River fishes.
- Daniel Theisen, a student volunteer from Eastern New Mexico University, spent two months at the station.
- The hatchery manager serves on the Rio Grande Fishes Recovery Team and taught the endangered species section of the Fish Hatchery Managers Course at the Fisheries Academy, Leetown, West Virginia.
- Cancerous growths from razorback suckers were provided to the Smithsonian Institution.

**Operations and Visitors**
- No cyclical maintenance funds were available.
- The station received 657 visitors during the fiscal year.
