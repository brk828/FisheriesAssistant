# Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program: Committee Minutes, Work Plans and Priority Reaches for Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a compiled record from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (Service), Region 6, concerning the [[Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]] for four endangered fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin (UCRB). It includes the minutes of the February 23, 1988 Implementation Committee meeting, the committee's organizational charter, funding guidelines for in-kind services, a short-term flow work plan for the Yampa River and 15-mile reach, a flow-hydrology consultation flow chart, a list of priority work items, a March 1, 1988 report ranking river reaches for [[Water Rights Acquisition]], a table of water depletions, and appendix tables ranking river reaches by life stage.

The species concerned are the [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The Yampa River was ranked Priority 1, while the White River and the 15-mile reach of the Colorado River were ranked Priority 2.

## Background & Description

The four fishes are rare Colorado River species listed as endangered. The Recovery Program, finalized September 29, 1987, sets out the framework for their recovery. The Implementation Committee was formed to oversee the program. Its initial membership includes the Regional Directors of the Service and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], one representative each appointed by the governors of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, an area manager of the [[Western Area Power Administration]], one water development representative, one conservation representative, and two nonvoting members (a Program Director and a Secretarial observer). Decisions are made by consensus, and the committee is explicitly not an advisory committee under the Federal Advisory Committee Act.

Meeting minutes record the appointment of Tom Pitts (water development) and Carse Pustmueller (conservation, provisional pending a supporting resolution). Galen Buterbaugh was selected chairman and John Hamill was selected Program Director. Two work groups were formed: a Management Group (eight members) and a Technical Group (chaired by Jim Bennett of the Colorado Division of Wildlife).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document stresses that natural flows of the Yampa River are essential for the perpetuation of native fishes. The Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team identified the Yampa River, the Colorado River from Lake Powell to Palisade (including the 15-mile reach), and the White River as essential habitat that must be protected before Colorado squawfish can be considered for delisting.

Key ecological points:

- **Yampa River:** Supports a reproducing population of humpback chub between river miles (RM) 18-37, one of two known Colorado squawfish spawning areas in the basin, and a major producer of fish for the Green River basin. The Little Snake River, a tributary, supplies about 80% of the sediment and about 27% of the flow reaching the Yampa at Deerlodge Park (5.5 miles downstream of the confluence), mostly as spring runoff. Peak spring flows may maintain channel equilibrium, flush substrates for squawfish spawning, and form flooded backwaters used by squawfish and razorback sucker.
- **White River:** Serves as a major holding area for adult squawfish outside the spawning season. Taylor Draw Reservoir has reduced the adult range to RM 109. No squawfish spawning has been documented here.
- **15-mile reach (Colorado River, RM 170-185):** Used year-round by adult squawfish and razorback sucker. One of two confirmed razorback spawning areas in the Upper Basin occurs at RM 179 near Clifton, Colorado, though no recruitment has been documented. It is subject to low flows during late-summer irrigation, especially in dry years.

Water development pressures include the Stagecoach Project (Upper Yampa), the Sandstone Project (Little Snake), the White River Dam (State of Utah, with a 1982 no-jeopardy opinion), and numerous upstream diversions on the Colorado that could reduce flows, mainly during spring runoff. Table 1 lists about 15 projects that had completed Section 7 consultation but were not online by 1988; the cumulative depletion above the Cisco gage totals 382.90 thousand acre-feet (KAF), with 250.31 KAF above Palisade.

## Key Findings

**Committee organization and governance (February 23, 1988 meeting)**
- Tom Pitts accepted as water development representative; Carse Pustmueller accepted provisionally for conservation interests.
- The Implementation Committee will meet in August and January to align with federal budget and field-work cycles. The next meeting was set for August 30, 1988.
- Galen Buterbaugh will request a Secretarial observer at the level of Assistant Secretary. CREDA's request for nonvoting status was addressed by placing its representative on the mailing list instead.

**Funding**
- Annual state contributions in the final plan: Colorado $104,000; Utah $73,000; Wyoming $23,000.
- Annual program budget need referenced as $2.3 million; priority projects above that cost were to be listed for the Program Director.
- The Fish and Wildlife Foundation is prepared to administer water rights acquisition funds at an overhead of about 1-2%, pending resolution of contracting issues.
- Region 6 requested $10 million from the Land and Water Conservation Fund for FY90 water rights acquisition.

**In-kind services guidelines (February 24, 1988)**
- In-kind contributions must first appear in the annual work plan.
- Unanticipated expenditures require Management Group approval and an offsetting reduction in the work plan.
- Excess contributions may be credited to future payments.
- Staff time for administrative (implementation and management) committees does not qualify, except pre-approved travel. Technical committee staff time and travel do qualify, up to the amount allocated in the work plan.

**Yampa River and 15-mile reach flow work plans**
- The Yampa work plan lists 19 tasks (YR 1-19) covering squawfish adults, spawning, larvae, humpback chub and razorback sucker. It runs from early 1988 to a final report due April 1, 1988.
- The 15-mile reach short-term plan targets biologically defensible flow recommendations for July, August and September. It calls for a PHABSIM physical habitat model, a temperature, pesticide, turbidity and introduced-fish assessment, and flow recommendations for dry, average and wet years.

**Priority reaches for water rights acquisition (March 1, 1988)**
- **Priority 1: Yampa River (RM 0-140).** Highest biological significance. Contains spawning areas, a reproducing humpback chub population (RM 18-37), and bonytail records (RM 0-28). Water threats include the Stagecoach Project (under construction, funding incomplete) and the Sandstone Project (EIS in preparation).
- **Priority 2(a): White River (RM 0-156).** Large numbers of year-round adult and juvenile squawfish, but no documented spawning. Humpback chub and bonytail are absent, and razorback records are rare.
- **Priority 2(b): Colorado River 15-mile reach (RM 170-185).** Important adult habitat for squawfish and razorback. Threatened by low flows and upstream depletions.
- Ranking assumes that funds will be adequate and that Green and Colorado mainstem needs will be handled through Section 7 consultations on Flaming Gorge and Blue Mesa reservoirs. These priorities will be re-examined after those consultations.

**Unresolved issues**
- Whether sufficient program progress exists to accept the $10/acre-foot developer contribution to offset depletions (Section 7 question tied to the Sandstone project) was not agreed.
- Carse Pustmueller stressed that research must show hatchery-reared fish can form a self-sustaining population before a hatchery is built. Colorado was considering a hatchery.
- Wyoming requested information on the Service's and Reclamation's work plan processes and on conservation group contributions.

**Priority work items (Attachment 7)**
- Review the existing recovery effort, develop the 1988-89 work plan, and continue water rights acquisition, including seeking additional Congressional funding and a legal/policy review of instream flow protection.
- Review the Service's plan for meeting hatchery-rearing needs for endangered fishes, for research.
