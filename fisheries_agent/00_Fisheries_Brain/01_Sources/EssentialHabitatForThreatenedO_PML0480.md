# Essential Habitat for Threatened or Endangered Wildlife in Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #cyprinidae #percidae #catostomidae #ornithology #mammalogy

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Division of Wildlife document (January 1978) compiles species accounts defining "essential habitat" for threatened, endangered, and rare vertebrates in the state. It covers 10 fish species (three cutthroat trout subspecies, two chubs, two darters, a sucker, and the Colorado squawfish), 4 bird species, and 9 mammal species, plus a regional appendix. Each account gives status, habitat requirements, limiting factors, occupied range, and a recommended essential habitat description. The document states that essential habitat is a biological concept with no legal status, distinct from "critical habitat" designated under the federal Endangered Species Act. The aquatic accounts are prepared largely by David Langlois, Nongame Aquatic Specialist.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Provide biological guidance for identifying habitat needed to maintain or recover listed species, recognizing that knowledge of distribution and requirements is incomplete.
- **Scope of essential habitat:** Space for growth, movement, and territorial behavior; nutritional requirements; and sites for reproduction, all of which may be defined by biological, physical, or behavioral needs.
- **Preface:** Frames wildlife as a valuable state resource, with the Division of Wildlife responsible for all wild animals in Colorado and for recognizing species that have disappeared or are declining.
- **Structure:** Each species account lists status, habitat requirements, limiting factors, original and occupied range, essential habitat description, and references.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Cutthroat trout (three subspecies).** All three are harmed chiefly by introduced non-native trout. Rainbow and Yellowstone cutthroat introductions caused hybridization, brook trout and brown trout caused competitive displacement, and habitat degradation from mining, logging, road building, irrigation diversion, and overgrazing raised water temperatures and silted, turbid, or dry streambeds. Greenback trout do not persist in water consistently above 70°F. Suitable habitat is moderate gradient, abundant riparian vegetation, and rocky-to-gravelly substrates with cover from undercut banks, overhanging vegetation, and boulder eddies.

**Fishes of the Arkansas and South Platte systems.** The Arkansas River speckled chub requires warm, low-gradient river channels with sand or gravel and a noticeable current; large cold-tailwater reservoirs and impoundments prevent successful spawning. The Arkansas darter depends on small springs and seeps with coarse gravel and watercress, which dry rapidly when the water table drops. The johnny darter needs clear, cool, slow-flowing water and is sensitive to silt, pollution, and flow reduction. The plains orangethroat darter needs sand or sand-gravel substrate with strong continuous flow, and is harmed by silt and well-pumping that lowers water tables.

**Big-river fishes of the Colorado system.** The razorback sucker, bonytail chub, humpback chub, and Colorado squawfish all depend on large, warm, turbid mainstream rivers. Flaming Gorge Dam, its cold releases, and the Navajo Reservoir altered temperatures and blocked spawning. Water diversions for irrigation and municipal use, sewage and industrial effluent, rotenone treatment in 1962, and competition from introduced species (such as largemouth bass, green sunfish, and red shiner) contributed to decline. Hybridization with the flannelmouth sucker threatens the razorback.

**Birds.** The White Pelican needs isolated nesting islands with gentle shorelines and protection from mammalian predators and human disturbance, with nearby feeding reservoirs. The American Peregrine Falcon nests on cliffs 200 to 400 feet tall with southerly exposure, and hunts within about ten miles of the nest. The Southern Bald Eagle nests near large rivers and reservoirs, with the few known Colorado sites lying at the species' peripheral range. Prairie grouse and cranes depend on native tallgrass and midgrass communities, with the Greater Prairie Chicken and Lesser Prairie Chicken affected by grazing, drought, and land conversion, and the sharp-tailed grouse on mixed-grass–shrub ecotones.

**Mammals.** The Gray Wolf, Grizzly Bear, Black-footed Ferret, Wolverine, River Otter, and Lynx all face low or uncertain presence in Colorado. The ferret depends on prairie dog towns, and the otter depends on year-round flowing water with beaver populations.

## Key Findings

- The document covers **10 fish species**, **4 bird species**, and **9 mammal species**, with essential habitat defined where data allow.
- **Rio Grande cutthroat trout:** Essential habitat requires about 300 acres in at least 25 headwater streams occupied, plus two 20-acre lakes for brood stock.
- **Greenback cutthroat trout:** About 600 acres in at least 50 headwater streams, plus two 20-acre lakes; the species does not persist above 70°F.
- **Colorado River cutthroat trout:** About 50 headwater streams, with about five acres of habitat occupied and two 20-acre lakes for brood stock.
- **Johnny darter:** About 200 acres in at least 15 streams and lakes.
- **Plains orangethroat darter:** Essential habitat includes at least five separate streams.
- **Razorback sucker:** About 50 miles of river and at least 10 backwaters, with channelization near Grand Junction to be stopped.
- **Humpback chub and Colorado squawfish:** Essential habitat is limited by poor knowledge, with the Yampa, Colorado, and Gunnison rivers considered essential to the squawfish in Colorado.
- **Peregrine falcon:** Key hunting areas lie within ten miles of nesting cliffs, and the 1972–1977 data report 15 to 31 eyries visited and 8 to 11 occupied sites, with productivity of 0.2 to 1.6 young per pair; a minimum of 1.4 young per pair is considered necessary to sustain the wild population.
- **Greater Prairie Chicken:** Essential habitat supports about 600 birds; nesting blocks should be at least eight square miles, at least 50% grassland, with at least 25% meeting structural criteria.
- **Greater Sandhill Crane:** The Routt County, Jackson County, and Moffat County populations total 76 breeding pairs. The goal is 115 pairs, and Morgan Bottom on the Yampa River is the largest staging area in Colorado.
- **Whooping Crane:** No part of Colorado can be listed as essential habitat, because migrants are sporadic and the experimental flock has no established pattern.
- **Gray Wolf, Grizzly Bear, and Wolverine:** No essential habitat can be identified under present knowledge; the wolf would require a contiguous area of about 2,000 square miles.
- **Black-footed Ferret:** No essential habitat is defined until confirmation, though prairie dog towns in several named areas deserve special consideration.
- **Lynx:** Essential habitat is based on recent records in the Gore Range and Clear Creek County areas, with any suitable habitat with an adequate prey base considered potential habitat.
- **Overall recommendation:** Essential habitat should be reviewed for mainstream water development projects, and areas must be inventoried further before final designations.
