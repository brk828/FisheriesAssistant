# Genetic Variation in Desert Pupfish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (Echelle, Oklahoma State University, 30 August 1998, to C. O. Minckley, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service) uses mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variation to assess the genetic structure of the [[Desert Pupfish]] across its extant natural range. Samples came from 11 wild populations (226 specimens) and a captive stock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH; 33 specimens). The study concludes that the Rio Sonoyta/Quitobaquito populations represent a separate species, *C. eremus*, distinct from the wider-ranging *C. macularius*, and provides ten conservation genetics recommendations. The letter cover page and the draft preliminary note from the author are also included, with questions about map distances, but these are administrative.

## Background & Description

The desert pupfish (Cyprinodontidae) historically ranged from headwater tributaries in southeastern Arizona and northern Sonora across southern Arizona, west to the Salton Sea area of southern California and south into the [[Colorado River Delta]]. Populations were once abundant in springs, seeps, sloughs and backwaters, but have since declined and fragmented because of introduced species, habitat alteration, reservoir construction and surface-water diversions.

Two subspecies were previously recognized: *C. eremus* (Quitobaquito Springs and the ponded outflow in [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]], Arizona) and the wide-ranging *C. macularius*, which includes Rio Sonoyta in northern Sonora. The species is federally endangered, and a recovery action plan (Marsh and Sada, 1991) exists. Mexican populations were largely absent from earlier genetic surveys.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and history:** Before upstream reservoir and diversion projects, Colorado River flooding of the delta would have connected numerous periodically interconnected pupfish populations. The last flow into the Salton Sink occurred 1905–1907, when the river breached an irrigation canal and created the present Salton Sea. Laguna Salada last received Colorado River flow in 1983–1984, a period associated with dispersal and increased pupfish abundance.
- **Population dynamics:** Populations show "boom and bust" cycles, with expansion and dispersal alternating with declines, isolation and local extinction. The authors link this to fishes of arid western North America.
- **Isolation timing:** Volcanic lava flows from the [[Pinacate Volcanic Field]] diverted the Rio Sonoyta southward, probably within the last 100,000 years, which is consistent with recent divergence of the Sonoyta/Quitobaquito lineage.
- **Morphology and colour:** Breeding males of the Salton Sea and lower Delta populations show bright yellow-orange caudal coloration absent in Quitobaquito and Rio Sonoyta males. Meristic and morphometric differences exist, but Rio Sonoyta is intermediate, so the two forms are not diagnosably different in these traits.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**
- 259 specimens were screened by single-stranded conformational polymorphism (SSCP) of two mtDNA ND2 gene fragments (333 bp and 325 bp). This produced 13 composite haplotypes.
- Representative haplotypes were sequenced for the full ND2 gene (1047 bases) and a 337-bp D-loop segment. Phylogenetic analysis used [[Paup Software]] with *C. variegatus* as outgroup.
- Population genetic statistics were computed with [[Arlequin Software]], including an analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) across three regions: Salton Sea, lower Colorado River Delta (LCRD) and Rio Sonoyta/Quitobaquito.

**Genetic structure**
- Two reciprocally monophyletic mtDNA groups were found: Rio Sonoyta/Quitobaquito (haplotypes L and M) and the Salton Sea/LCRD clade (11 haplotypes).
- Every wild sample had two to five SSCP variants. The DNFH stock, founded from about 280 individuals in 1983, was monomorphic for haplotype A.
- Haplotype A made up 70–95% of samples in both the Salton Sea and LCRD regions.
- No significant haplotype frequency difference existed between Quitobaquito and Rio Sonoyta samples, or within either the Salton Sea or LCRD.
- AMOVA on haplotype frequencies (F-statistics) attributed 50.6% of genetic variance to among-region differences, 48.9% to within-sample variation and 0.5% to among-sample differences within regions. Sequence-based statistics (Φ-statistics) attributed 99.2% to among-region variance.
- Among-region divergence between the Salton Sea and LCRD was only marginally significant once Rio Sonoyta/Quitobaquito samples were excluded (P = 0.061 and 0.045). It was linked to secondary haplotypes B, C and F.

**Taxonomic conclusion**
- The authors conclude that *C. eremus* (Rio Sonoyta/Quitobaquito) and *C. macularius* (Salton Sea/LCRD) are separate species. Support includes reciprocal mtDNA monophyly, breeding colour differences and historic isolation.

**Conservation genetics recommendations (summary of ten points)**
1. Perform a nuclear DNA survey, such as microsatellites, since current knowledge relies on mtDNA and partial allozyme data.
2. Maintain at least one, preferably more, captive stocks of each species at state or federal facilities. Re-introductions should use 200–300 minimum specimens in roughly a 1:1 sex ratio, supplemented by about 10% wild samples.
3. Minimum captive stocks: *C. eremus* from Rio Sonoyta and Quitobaquito Springs/Pond; *C. macularius* from Salton Sea and LCRD.
4. Replace the DNFH stock with one founded from a wild population, since it lost variability in 15 years of captivity without supplementation.
5. Establish a control system for managers of captive and semi-natural stocks, following the three-tier approach in the recovery plan.
6. Found captive stocks from large, genetically diverse natural populations of several hundred individuals, without recent bottlenecks.
7. Infuse captive stocks with wild fish, ideally annually, to counter genetic drift.
8. Genotype founding stocks or early descendants, using both mtDNA and nuclear markers.
9. Monitor population size, age/size structure and the number of territorial males, keeping effective size as large as possible.
10. Monitor genetic structure of captive stocks, about every two or three generations (roughly every other year), using about 30 fish per sample.

**Genetic exchange programme (proposed)**
- Manage four units separately: *C. eremus* (Quitobaquito, Rio Sonoyta) and *C. macularius* (Salton Sea, LCRD). Gene transfers should occur only within these units.
- Proposed guidelines: transfers should be small (for example, 1% or less of the recipient population), biased toward neighbouring populations, and infrequent (once every few years).
- The Cerro Prieto population in the LCRD may be a zone of transition, owing to its shared secondary haplotype with Salton Sea samples. Further study with larger samples and nuclear markers is advised.
- The author states that current data are insufficient to decide on exchange protocols.
