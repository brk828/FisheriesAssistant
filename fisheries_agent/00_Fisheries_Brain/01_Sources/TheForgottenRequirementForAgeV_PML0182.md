# The Forgotten Requirement for Age Validation in Fisheries Biology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #sebastidae

## Executive Summary

Beamish and McFarlane (1983) argue that fish age estimates are routinely used in fisheries science without being validated. A review of 500 studies (1907–1980) found that only 6% mentioned age validation or attempted to validate the ageing technique, and fewer than 1% validated all age classes. Among 75 later stock-dynamics papers (1965–1980), 40% mentioned or attempted validation and none validated all age groups used. The authors contend that ages greater than the maximum validated age must not be considered accurate, and that inaccurate ages have caused serious errors in management. They recommend [[Mark-Recapture Studies]] or use of [[Known-Age Fish]] to validate all age classes, and, where this is impossible, ageing by several methods with explicit consideration of error.

## Background & Description

The paper distinguishes **validation** (proving a technique is accurate) from **precision** (reproducibility among readers), which is not a measure of accuracy. Early pioneers such as [[Van Oosten]] (1923, 1929, 1941) and Hile (1936) stressed validation; Van Oosten noted that age determinations for at least 60 freshwater species by more than 80 authors in over 150 papers had only rarely evaluated the [[Scale Method]] critically. The authors compare validation to standardising solutions or calibrating instruments in other sciences.

Two case species illustrate the consequences of unvalidated ageing:

- **[[White Sucker]]** (*Catostomus commersoni*): Early scale-based studies (e.g., [[Stewart 1926]], Spoor 1939, Eddy and Carlander 1942) suggested fish died by about age 9 or 10. Studies using validated [[Fin-Ray Ageing]] revealed that many fish survive after growth nearly ceases, with an unvalidated maximum age of 23 years, more than double earlier life-span estimates. Scale annuli are difficult to identify after about age 5, whereas fin-ray annuli are distinct.
- **[[Pacific Ocean Perch]]** (*Sebastes alutus*): Scale-based ageing by foreign fleets portrayed fish as fast-growing and short-lived. [[Otolith]] surface and section studies showed older fish, and subsequent work greatly reduced estimated natural mortality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The authors argue that age errors are not random but biased toward younger ages, clustering estimates near the age where a technique breaks down. This inflates mortality estimates, particularly for slower-growing males, and can mask strong year classes, differences in growth among stocks, and the role of longevity in population persistence. Reduced growth after maturity, driven by sex, maturation, food limitation, behaviour, or environment, can make annuli hard to read and may be important for maintaining populations. Life-history traits such as age at maturity, growth rate, reproductive frequency and fecundity are shaped by long-term adaptation, so accurate ages are described as "the key" to understanding them under fishing stress.

## Key Findings

- Only about 6% of 500 surveyed studies (1907–1980) mentioned or attempted age validation; fewer than 1% validated all age classes.
- Of 75 stock-dynamics papers (1965–1980) using fish ages, 40% mentioned or attempted validation; none validated all age groups.
- Estimated ages greater than the maximum validated age should not be considered accurate.
- White sucker lifespan was substantially underestimated; an unvalidated age of 23 years was reported, more than double earlier estimates.
- Pacific ocean perch age revisions reduced estimated natural mortality and led to a more conservative management strategy. The authors estimate lost wholesale value to Canadian industry of about 4 million 1981 Canadian dollars annually; this is presented as an approximation, and the authors acknowledge that better management of the early fishery is questionable.
- Recommended validation methods are mark-recapture studies, use of known-age fish, and, where needed, fluorochrome marks such as [[Oxytetracycline Marking]] visible under ultraviolet light; scales or fin rays can be removed at tagging for later comparison.
- Where validation is impossible, fish should be aged by several methods and errors explicitly considered.
- Errors in ageing are non-random and biased toward younger ages, leading to overestimated mortality and misread population structure.
