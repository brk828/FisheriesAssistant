# Correspondence on Apache Trout Genetic Analysis and Management Strategy

#ichthyology #salmonidae #conservation-genetics

## Executive Summary

This January 1990 letter from Dr. John N. Rinne (USFS Rocky Mountain Forestry Sciences Lab) to Jim Hanson (USFWS Pinetop) discusses electrophoretic genetic studies of [[Apache Trout]] populations in the [[White Mountains]] and their implications for conservation management. Rinne compares recent genetic analyses with his earlier phenotypic assessments, finding general agreement on "pure" populations but disagreement on "mostly pure" versus "hybrid" designations. The letter advocates caution in stream renovation decisions, arguing that some hybrid populations may naturally trend toward pure [[Apache Trout]] in the absence of [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking, and that broad-scale chemical renovation could eliminate valuable genetic heterozygosity and natural experiments.

## Background & Description

The correspondence addresses ongoing conservation efforts for [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*), a native salmonid of Arizona mountain streams. Dr. Rinne had previously conducted phenotypic analyses classifying stream populations as "pure," "mostly pure," or "hybridized." A recent electrophoretic genetic study re-evaluated these populations, prompting this comparative analysis and management discussion.

Key streams mentioned include [[Boggy Creek]], [[Conklin Creek]], [[Centerfire Creek]], [[North Fork Big Diamond Creek]], [[Paddy Creek]], [[Hughey Creek]], [[Grant Creek]], [[East Fork White River]], and [[Ord Creek]]. The letter references 18 streams classified as "mostly pure" in Rinne's 1985 paper.

Non-target species potentially affected by management include *Rhinichthys* (dace) and *Catostomus* (suckers). [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) and [[Brook Trout]] ("Mr. Brookie") are mentioned as non-native species co-occurring with [[Apache Trout]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[White Mountains]] region has experienced extensive fisheries management over 15+ years, including stream renovation using fish toxicants. [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking has been common practice in many streams, contributing to hybridization with native [[Apache Trout]]. Management priorities have focused on native trout conservation on both Forest Service and Reservation lands.

The letter discusses ecological interactions between [[Apache Trout]] and non-native salmonids. [[Brown Trout]] do not hybridize genetically with [[Apache Trout]] but their competitive effects remain undemonstrated. In [[Ord Creek]], both [[Brown Trout]] and [[Brook Trout]] co-occurred with [[Apache Trout]], potentially contributing to native trout decline.

Stream renovation with toxicants has been used historically as a management tool throughout the West and Southwest, with documented impacts on native fish fauna and unknown genetic effects on non-target species.

## Key Findings

**Genetic vs. Phenotypic Analysis Comparison:**
- Most streams evaluated as "pure" by both phenotypic and genetic methods showed agreement
- Greatest disagreement occurred with Rinne's "mostly pure" designation, which genetic analysis sometimes classified as "hybrid"
- Streams Rinne classified as hybridized ([[North Fork Big Diamond Creek]], [[Paddy Creek]], [[Hughey Creek]]) were confirmed as hybrids by genetic study
- [[Boggy Creek]] (Rinne: "pure"), [[Conklin Creek]] (Rinne: "mostly pure"), and [[Centerfire Creek]] (Rinne: "mostly pure") were designated "hybrid" by genetic analysis

**Genetic Heterogeneity Within Populations:**
- Dr. Tom Dowling's (ASU) genetic analyses indicate "pure" individuals exist alongside "hybrids" within the same stream samples
- [[Grant Creek]] phenotypic observations: in 30-fish sample, some individuals resembled [[East Fork White River]] fish while others were obvious hybrids
- Dowling's interpretation: populations with mixed pure/hybrid individuals will not "swamp out" genetically
- In absence of [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking, such populations likely trend toward pure [[Apache Trout]] over time

**Management Implications:**
- If all 18 "mostly pure" streams were genetically evaluated and designated hybrids, this would create numerous renovation candidates and undermine Recovery Team goals
- Stream renovation could destroy "natural experiments" valuable for study
- Renovation could permanently remove [[Apache Trout]] heterozygosity inherent in these streams
- [[Ord Creek]] case study: despite extensive, well-intended management effort over 15 years, stream still lacks "pure" [[Apache Trout]]

**Recommended Management Approach:**
- Caution against "broad brush stream renovation" approach
- Need for comprehensive management strategy incorporating all available information and expertise
- Streams with [[Brown Trout]] present opportunities for natural competition studies
- Recovery Team meeting should address contemporary strategy development
- Integration of genetic studies (electrophoretic and Dowling's ASU work) with phenotypic data essential
