# The Origin and Fate of the Red Wolf

#ichthyology #marine-biology #canidae

*Note: This document concerns a terrestrial mammal (Canidae). The hashtags above follow the required template; the taxonomic tag for this document is #canidae.*

## Executive Summary

This position paper, prepared for the 2nd North American Symposium on Wolves, reviews the long-running taxonomic debate over the endangered [[Red Wolf]] (*Canis rufus*). Authors from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) and the Point Defiance Zoo and Aquarium conclude that the red wolf is a valid species or, at minimum, a subspecies of the [[Gray Wolf]] (*Canis lupus*). They argue that mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) evidence of coyote or gray wolf lineages does not demonstrate that the red wolf arose entirely through hybridization. The authors recommend that the USFWS continue to aggressively pursue recovery under the [[Endangered Species Act]].

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic dispute:** Most authorities since the late 1970s regard *C. rufus* as a full species. [[Lawrence and Bossert]] (1967, 1975) argued for subspecific status within *C. lupus*. [[Mech]] (1970) proposed that the red wolf was a fertile hybrid population of gray wolf and [[Coyote]] (*C. latrans*).
- **Legal status:** Listed as endangered in 1967 and given priority protection under the ESA of 1973. A 1986 reintroduction at [[Alligator River National Wildlife Refuge]] (ARNWR), northeastern North Carolina, was described as successful to date.
- **Petition:** The American Sheep Industry Association petitioned the Secretary of the Interior to delist the red wolf. USFWS (Henry 1991) found the petition did not present substantial information that delisting was warranted.
- **Physical traits:** The red wolf is intermediate between coyote and gray wolf in most features. Disproportionately long legs and large ears are the most distinctive external characters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Diet:** Analysis of 1,300 scats from reintroduced wolves showed [[White-Tailed Deer]] (*Odocoileus virginianus*) and [[Raccoon]] (*Procyon lotor*) as the primary year-round food items. Adult deer were documented as healthy prey, not only carrion. Gray wolves rely more heavily on ungulates. Coyotes use deer seasonally, mainly during fawning and hunting seasons.
- **Social behavior:** Reintroduced red wolves are highly social, forming territorial packs. Yearlings and two-year-olds often remain with parents to help rear pups. Intraspecific aggression caused the deaths of 7 wolves. Social structure resembles that of gray wolves, whereas coyotes are more asocial.
- **Hybridization history:** Hybridization with coyotes is inferred to have begun in the last ~100 years, most substantially in central Texas. Specimens from about 1890 to 1930 show that the species was still largely unmodified in much of its range.
- **Genetic context:** All North American *Canis* share a diploid chromosome number of 78. Hybridization is common among vertebrates, with hundreds of hybrid zones documented (Barton and Hewitt 1985, 1989).

## Key Findings

1. **Morphometric analysis:** A new canonical discriminant analysis of skulls (SAS 1987) used four reference groups: 50 coyotes (pre-1925, Idaho), 38 gray wolves (Alaska), 28 gray wolves (Minnesota and Isle Royale, 1960s–1970s), and 22 red wolves (pre-1940, southeastern US). Nine captive-population founders and five reintroduced descendants were also analyzed. The three species formed distinct groups. The red wolf fell between coyote and gray wolf but did not merge with either.
2. **Fossil evidence:** Irvingtonian (middle Pleistocene) specimens from Florida are approximately the same size and proportion as modern red wolves. The authors interpret the red wolf as a surviving intermediate stage in wolf evolution from a small coyote-like ancestor to the modern gray wolf.
3. **Founding stock:** Over 400 wild canids from southeastern Texas and southwestern Louisiana were examined from 1973 to 1980. Only 43 were admitted to the breeding program as probable red wolves, and 14 animals became the founding stock.
4. **Vocalizations and behavior:** [[McCarley]] (1977) found consistent vocal differences between red wolves and coyotes. Earlier studies by Riley and McBride (1972) and Shaw (1975) documented behavioral and ecological differences in Texas and Louisiana populations.
5. **mtDNA limitations:** [[Wayne and Jenks]] (1991) found that red wolves carried either gray wolf or coyote mtDNA. The authors argue this does not show significant coyote or gray wolf nuclear DNA. Only about 4% of the canid mtDNA genome had been assessed. mtDNA is a single locus and may not reflect species phylogeny.
6. **Great Lakes comparison:** [[Lehman et al.]] (1991) reported coyote mtDNA in 62% of gray wolves in Minnesota and 100% on Isle Royale. Field observations and preliminary nuclear DNA analysis showed no substantial morphological, behavioral, or nuclear differences, questioning the hybrid-zone interpretation.
7. **Species concept:** Under the [[Biological Species Concept]], hybridization must be assessed in context. Many species concepts allow limited genetic interchange unless it causes species disintegration.
8. **Media and citations:** The authors criticize [[Gittleman and Pimm]] (1991) for errors about red wolf taxonomy and for mischaracterizing its history. They note that the red wolf was named in 1851, but earlier names existed, and that most authors have accepted species status since at least Goldman (1937, 1944).
9. **Conclusions:** Current evidence best supports *C. rufus* as a valid taxon, probably a full species, and at minimum a subspecies of *C. lupus*. The authors support the Recovery Program's proposed nuclear DNA analysis and further skull and behavioral studies.
10. **Policy:** The USFWS is developing an ESA policy on protecting introgressed species, which will rescind previous Solicitor's opinions and require case-by-case management. The Red Wolf Recovery Team proposed a symposium on the species concept and the ESA.
