# Fishes of Moses Lake, Washington

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Centrarchidae #Percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 survey by Kenneth Ellison Groves, published by the Walla Walla College Department of Biological Sciences, documents the fish fauna of [[Moses Lake]] in the Columbia Basin of eastern Washington. Field work ran from March to August 1950. The study traces the history of [[Common Carp]] in the lake, tabulates the species present, and assesses how carp interact with [[Game Fish]] populations. It also addresses the lake's physical setting, hydrology, and the planned raising of its water level under the [[Columbia Basin Project]]. The author concluded that carp are a nuisance to recreation and aesthetics but do not appear to be ruining the game fishery.

## Background & Description

The lake is about 16 miles long, averages half a mile wide, covers roughly 8,000 acres, and has nearly 120 miles of shoreline. It lies at 1,046 feet elevation and is the third largest lake in Washington. Its northern and southern "horns" give it a pipe-like form. Carp entered during the 1904 flood that carried lake water to the [[Columbia River]]. Early settlers and the State Game Department attributed the original fauna to squawfish, redhorse suckers, and small bullheads.

A 1925 flood-related breach and the 1929 dam construction are central to the lake's history. The dam's six unscreened gates release surplus water, and fish losses occur when gates close after high water. The author also records a long record of stocking and commercial carp fishing, from the 1916–1920 Schneirla operation to the Ninnewash Fish Company (1918–1928).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and inflows.** The lake's main inflows are [[Crab Creek]], which sinks into porous ground and reappears south of the lake, and [[Rocky Ford Creek]], fed by springs. Rocky Ford Springs (22 springs, maximum discharge of 30,000 gallons per minute) hold a constant temperature of 50°F year-round and supply the [[Troutlodge Hatchery]]. The only outlet is Lower Crab Creek, which flows 49.4 miles to the Columbia River when the dam gates are open.

**Water quality.** Surface pH read 7.0 in July 1950. The bottom is mucky in most areas, with sandy substrate on the south and southwest shores. Carp spawning and feeding stir up sediment from March through August. Algal blooms and irrigation return flows add to turbidity.

**Water level.** Maximum recorded level was 1,047.83 feet (April 18, 1941) and minimum was 1,038.17 feet (August 27, 1910), a range of almost ten feet. The Columbia Basin plan proposed raising the level to 1,052 feet, which the author considered harmful to shore life and aquatic plants. A footnote states that the present level was later announced to remain unchanged.

**Vegetation.** Tules (*Scirpus validus*), cattails (*Typha latifolia*), rushes, bur-reed, pondweed, and willows are present. Vegetation was increasing according to early residents, which the author linked to reduced carp grazing. Plants serve as spawning substrate for carp and as cover for larval bass.

**Climate.** The area is semi-arid. Summer surface temperatures rose from 42°F (March) to 72.5°F (July 1950).

## Key Findings

- **Species count:** The survey documents at least 12 species present, with one more probable. Table 2 lists about 30 taxa in total, including endemic, introduced, unsuccessfully introduced, hatchery-escape, and reported-but-not-found categories.
- **Dominant fish:** Carp are the most numerous and largest fish. Seining at the carp-feeding ponds on May 12, 1950 collected 67 breeding carp. A later seine across from the Marina yielded 111 carp, of which two had spinal curvature.
- **Game fishery:** Yellow perch, bluegill, largemouth and smallmouth bass, and black crappie are flourishing. The author reports that these game fish feed on carp eggs and young carp.
- **Carp control:** A 1950 rotenone test over two acres killed 5,000 game fish but only 100 carp, so poisoning plans were canceled. The author judged poisoning impractical and expensive.
- **Creel survey:** Game protector J. H. Laney's day checks (April–August 1950) recorded 5,716 fish. Bluegill made up over one-third of game fish taken. Yellow perch dominated April checks at 93.58%.
- **Fish losses:** On March 31, 1950 the author found several hundred dead or dying black crappies, yellow perch, and carp at the dam.
- **Commercial history:** The Schneirla brothers grossed $29,000 in one season. The Ninnewash Fish Company shipped 80 carloads of carp (1918–1928). Average carp weight dropped from about 12 pounds to roughly half that over time.
- **Stocking:** Recorded plantings from 1908 to 1940 include bass, trout, catfish, bluegills, crappies, sunfish, and yellow perch. Fish and Wildlife Service records list a 1929 blackspotted trout planting of 28,000.
- **Trout:** Lake conditions were judged unsuitable for trout because of warmth and sediment. Rainbow trout found in the lake are believed to be hatchery escapes.
- **Recommendations:** The author advised restocking game fish to control carp and noted that fishing pressure was rising. He recommended against rotenone because of cost and ineffectiveness.
- **Carp myth:** Stomach analyses did not confirm that carp prey on game fish eggs or young. Illinois River data cited in the text suggest carp may benefit game fish populations.
