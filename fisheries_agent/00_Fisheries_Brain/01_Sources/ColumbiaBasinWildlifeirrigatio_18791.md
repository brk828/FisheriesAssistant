# Columbia Basin Wildlife/Irrigation Development Study

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Salmonidae #Percidae #waterfowl #irrigation

## Executive Summary

This December 1984 Bureau of Reclamation report (REC-ERC-83-6), prepared jointly by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] and the [[Washington Department of Game]], evaluates how irrigated farming and irrigation project operation affect fish and wildlife in the [[Columbia Basin]] of central Washington. Field work (mostly 1975–1980) focused on ring-necked pheasants, Canada geese, ducks (especially mallard, teal and redhead), rainbow trout and yellow perch. The study asks how the Columbia Basin Irrigation Project, which unintentionally created much of the region's wetland and fishery habitat, can be managed so that wildlife benefits persist for the life of future irrigation projects. Its recommendations are framed as transferable to irrigation developments elsewhere in the western United States.

## Background & Description

The report is organized into an Executive Summary and five technical chapters, plus a Chapter I–V bibliography structure and appendices:

- **Chapter I:** Waterfowl use of irrigation canals, laterals and drains.
- **Chapter II:** Waterfowl use of lakes, ponds and marshes.
- **Chapter III:** [[Ring-necked Pheasant]] populations and irrigation development.
- **Chapter IV:** Wildlife damage to crops and livestock.
- **Chapter V:** [[Aquatic Studies]] of water chemistry, invertebrates, fish production and water-level fluctuations.

The [[Columbia Basin Irrigation Project]] supplies more than 543,000 acres of cropland. Water is pumped from the [[Columbia River]] at Grand Coulee Dam, and about 3,200–3,529 miles of canals, laterals and open drains (figures vary between chapters) distribute it. Irrigation return flows and seepage have raised groundwater and formed many potholes, marshes and perennial streams, and water now covers about 53,000 acres. The wildlife and fishery gains were an unplanned by-product of development, so no legal mandate protected them, and the report notes that declines in pheasants and ducks from the mid-1960s onward prompted the study.

Key sampling details reported:
- Chapter I sampled 229 miles (about 6%) of irrigation watercourses, 1977–1979, over 212 survey trips.
- Chapter II intensively studied 74 lakes and potholes (22 scabrock lakes, 52 flatland lakes).
- Chapter V examined 30 static lakes, 20 flowing waters and drains, and seven seep lakes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat structure.** Two geologic settings shaped wetlands: channeled basalt scablands ([[Scabrock Lakes]], steep-sided, 8–130 acres, 15–80 ft deep) and glacial outwash "flatlands" (shallow, alkaline, abundant littoral area). Irrigation drains flow year-round because of supercharged groundwater, whereas canals and laterals are dry from late fall to early spring.

**Water chemistry and invertebrates.** Fourteen of eighteen water chemistry variables differed significantly between groups of waters. Lakes without fish exceeded thresholds for alkalinity (700 ppm), nitrate (4.0 ppm), phosphate (2.0 ppm), sodium (400 ppm), total dissolved solids (1,400 ppm) and pH (9.1). Sixty-two species of Cladocera and Copepoda were identified from 30 lakes; zooplankton are the most important food of the game fish studied. Benthic invertebrate abundance fell as irrigation influence increased, which the authors attribute to foraging by carp and suckers entering through canals.

**Carp and vegetation.** [[Common Carp]] were the most common fish in flatland waters and, by dispersing through canals and drains, suppressed submergent vegetation. Lack of submergents was directly related to carp abundance.

**Predation and disturbance.** Predators destroyed at least 30% of duck nests; birds (ravens, magpies, gulls) were the main culprits. Human recreation, especially the spring fishing season, lowered duck densities, and cattle grazing reduced nesting and brood use.

## Key Findings

**Waterfowl on irrigation watercourses**
- Seventeen duck species were recorded; mallard, blue-winged and cinnamon teal made up 77% of observations, and redhead was the most common diver.
- Breeding-season use averaged 4.2 ducks/mile on drains, 2.0 on laterals and 1.2 on canals.
- Of 400 brood sightings (1978–1979), 55% were on canals, 22.5% on laterals and 22.5% on drains; estimated brood densities were 1.6/mile on drains, 1.0 on canals and 0.5 on laterals.
- Concrete-lined channels were avoided (under 1% of observations); broods used only earthen banks with vegetation at the waterline.
- An estimated minimum of 1,624 duck broods use open, unlined drains, canals and laterals each summer. Watercourses function mainly as travel lanes between nest sites and wetlands.
- Only 11 nests were found in roughly 120 miles of canal bank (one per 10.6 miles).

**Waterfowl on lakes, ponds and marshes**
- Spring/summer duck density was about twice as high on State-managed wetlands as on Federal or private ones; ducks per acre in summer averaged 0.8 (all ownership), versus 12.9 in fall.
- Waters without carp held three to four times as many ducks per acre as waters with carp.
- Duck broods were densest on ponds under 1 acre, and broods used wetlands under 5 acres heavily.
- Waters open to public fishing from April to July had the lowest spring/summer duck use; closed waters had the highest.
- Duck nests were about 17 times more numerous on wetlands than on roadsides; 68% of measured dabbler nests lay within 100 ft of water.
- Of 169 duck nests with recorded status, 31% hatched; 54% failed, with predators responsible for 57% of failures.
- Grazed wetlands and uplands held roughly half as many nesting attempts as ungrazed land (χ² = 3.8, P < 0.05).
- Canada geese nested only on islands, with successful nesting recorded on islands in ponds as small as 10 acres.
- Estimated 365 Canada goose nesting pairs in the Columbia Basin plus 267 on the adjacent Columbia River.

**Pheasants**
- Pheasant harvest peaked at about 260,000 birds in 1966 and fell to 143,000 by 1976.
- Only four factors correlated with spring and fall pheasant density; spring densities were 86% correlated with the four variables, mainly the amount of unused land.
- Right-of-way strips make up over 70% of unfarmed land on intensively farmed sections; a minimum of 15 acres per section is recommended for winter cover.
- Alfalfa nesting led to losses of up to 60% of hens and nests during the first cutting.

**Fish and aquatic systems**
- Instream cover was the most important feature for fish abundance in flowing waters; concrete-lined canals with no drop structures had the lowest cover and fish abundance.
- Yellow perch and rainbow trout production were ranked by length-weight slope and condition factor (K), and by weight gains of planted trout.
- Water chemistry differed significantly between medium and poor perch lakes and between good and medium trout lakes.
- Aquatic herbicide use should be minimized; cleanout every two or more years may be more cost-effective.

**Management recommendations (summary)**
- Use open channels rather than buried pipelines; avoid concrete lining; install parallel drains where seepage is excessive.
- Create one cove or pond per mile of channel; buffer strips at least 30–50 ft wide.
- Keep spring creeks and seep waters isolated from irrigation waterways to exclude carp.
- Eliminate grazing within 100 ft of wetlands; control public access on waterfowl wetlands; retain new seep wetlands in public ownership.
- Guarantee water rights for habitat development areas for the life of the project.
- Establish large reserves, with at least a portion of new wetlands closed to fishing, boating and hunting.

**Conclusions**
The report concludes that the wildlife and fishery values of the Columbia Basin were a fortuitous by-product of irrigation, and that protecting them requires designing wildlife benefits in from the start, including water rights, public ownership of new wetlands, open channels, carp exclusion and reduced disturbance.
