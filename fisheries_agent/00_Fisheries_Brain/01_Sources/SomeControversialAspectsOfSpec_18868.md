# Controversial Aspects of Speciation in African Cichlid Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1960 paper by [[Geoffrey Fryer]] of the East African Fisheries Research Organization critiques conclusions drawn by [[Lowe McConnell]] on the evolution of [[Tilapia]] and other African cichlids. Fryer argues that two phenomena must be kept separate: [[Intralacustrine Speciation]] (the radiation of species flocks within a lake, seen in genera such as [[Haplochromis]]) and simple specific proliferation (seen in [[Tilapia]] and many non-cichlid African genera). He contends that inshore habitats and an algal diet have not restricted speciation, that [[Predation]] has accelerated rather than retarded it, and that closely related species can coexist and breed in proximity when suitable conditions are met. The paper does not report new field sampling. It rebuts claims using published catch data, fossil records, growth studies and comparative observations from the [[East African Great Lakes]].

## Background & Description

The paper addresses the cichlid assemblages of African lakes, which have attracted much speculation about their evolution. Fryer accepts Lowe McConnell's factual data but disputes several of her conclusions. Two main groups are compared:

- **Species flock genera** (e.g., [[Haplochromis]], [[Lethrinops]], [[Lamprologus]]) that have undergone extensive [[Intralacustrine Speciation]].
- **The genus [[Tilapia]]**, which has shown little intralacustrine speciation. Its species are large, morphologically similar and ecologically generalized. Trophic specialization is limited to two types, weed chopping and fine-particle feeding.

Several Tilapia species are mouth brooders, a reproductive strategy Fryer considers important for intralacustrine speciation, though his reasons differ from Lowe McConnell's. The paper also draws comparisons with [[Cyclopoid Copepods]], which show a similar shift from carnivory to algal feeding.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and diet.** Lowe McConnell proposed that inshore dwelling and an algal diet restricted speciation. Fryer disputes this. A Lake Nyasa rock-frequenting group of eleven closely related, co-existing species, plus two [[Haplochromis]] species, feeds largely or entirely on [[Algal Diet|algae]] growing on rocks. Fryer also notes that [[Phytoplankton]] concentrates in inshore waters of most African lakes, so phytoplankton-feeding fishes should favour inshore areas. Several so-called open-water species, such as *T. esculenta* and *T. variabilis* in Lake Victoria, are in fact confined to inshore belts.

**Body size.** Most Tilapia are larger than members of species flocks in other cichlid genera. Citing [[Hutchinson Size Diversity Principle|Hutchinson's]] work on size and diversity, Fryer argues that small size allows specialization to small environmental elements and so supports diversity. Growth data for *T. esculenta* (Garrod 1959) show individuals reaching about 15 cm after one year and about 19–21 cm after two years, which puts them beyond the size of many predators.

**Predators.** The key predators discussed are [[Lates]] and [[Hydrocyon]] (absent from Lakes Nyasa, Victoria and Edward in some cases), together with [[Bagrus]], [[Clarias]], [[Rhamphochromis]] and [[Protopterus]]. Fryer argues that the [[Worthington Hypothesis]], which holds that these predators restrict speciation, is refuted by evidence from Lake Edward and elsewhere.

**Lake morphology.** Differences in Tilapia distribution reflect lake morphology rather than predator presence. In southern Lake Nyasa, *T. saka-squamipinnis* group species frequent open water, while in the steeper north they occur inshore.

## Key Findings

- **Two distinct speciation types.** [[Intralacustrine Speciation]] is shown by several cichlid genera. Simple specific proliferation, shown by [[Tilapia]], is equally marked in mormyrids (*Gnathonemus*, *Marcusenius*), characins (*Alestes*, *Distichodus*), the catfish *Clarias*, and cyprinids (*Labeo*, *Barilius*, *Barbus*). Most of these are not mouth brooders.
- **Flock sizes.** [[Haplochromis]] has about 70 species in [[Lake Victoria]] and more than 100 in [[Lake Nyasa]]. [[Lethrinops]] has 24 Nyasan species and [[Lamprologus]] has 34 in [[Lake Tanganyika]]. The Nyasan Tilapia flock has only four species, plus an unrelated endemic, *T. shirana*.
- **Inshore speciation.** Speciation has been most active among inshore species of other cichlid genera. Inshore habitat is therefore not a brake on speciation.
- **Algal feeding.** Eleven closely related rock-frequenting Nyasan species subsist largely on rock-surface algae. Poll (1956) reports similar findings for a Tanganyikan group with an independent history.
- **Alternative evolutionary routes.** Lake flock genera became specialized and small. The genus Tilapia stayed large and unspecialized, which allowed wide geographic ranges (*T. galilaea* from Senegal and the Gambia through the Niger, Volta and Nile systems to the Jordan Valley; *T. nilotica* further south into the Congo system) and success in fish ponds.
- **Fossil evidence against predator restriction.** [[Lates]] fossils found in Holocene deposits of [[Lake Edward]] show that Lates and the speciating [[Haplochromis]] flock co-existed until very recent times. This undermines Worthington's view (Greenwood 1959b).
- **Predator abundance in Lake Victoria.** Corbet (1959) found that more than 78% of 1,000+ *Bagrus docmac* had fed on cichlids, and over 80% of nearly 400 *Clarias mossambicus* had done so. Fisheries figures for 1957 record about half a million Bagrus through recording stations, and Bagrus plus Clarias made up roughly 12% of the gill-net catch in Kenyan and Ugandan waters and as much as 85% in Tanganyikan waters (figures approximate, biased by sampling locations).
- **Predation favours speciation.** Bagrus and Clarias prey far more heavily on [[Haplochromis]] than on Tilapia, yet only Haplochromis shows intralacustrine speciation. Fryer argues that predation removes unfit individuals and keeps prey density low enough to limit competition, so it promotes speciation.
- **"Lurking" predators.** Fryer argues that ambush predators such as Bagrus and clariids are important cichlid predators. Iles (in press) analysed food data from about 3,000 *Bagrus meridionalis*. In an aquarium observation, *Clarias* attacked juvenile Tilapia and Lebistes at night when they were resting.
- **Spine and body-size differences.** Lowe McConnell linked larger fish and stouter Tilapia spines in Lakes Albert and Rudolf to predators. Fryer rejects this. He notes that *T. melanopleura* is much larger than *T. sparrmani* for genetic reasons, and that the strongest spines he has seen are in a Lake Kitangiri species, where the only predators are Clarias and Protopterus.
- **Inshore distribution of *T. esculenta* and *T. variabilis*.** *T. esculenta* is seldom found more than five miles offshore and rarely more than twenty miles from land in Lake Victoria. *T. variabilis* is also concentrated in a narrow inshore belt. Fryer argues that species restricted to inshore waters have undergone the most speciation, so predator-driven inshore restriction would not support Worthington's theory.
- **Breeding and ecological isolation.** In Lake Nyasa, three closely related Tilapia species feed together but breed in different places and at different times (Lowe McConnell 1959). Fryer argues that ecological isolation is not essential. Closely related species can coexist and breed in close proximity when food is abundant, population density is controlled (for example by predators), and courtship patterns act as sterility barriers. Small size may allow cichlids to partition a single habitat for breeding.
- **Conclusion.** Fryer concludes that the idea of predators restricting speciation is unfounded. Predators, mainly fish other than Lates and Hydrocyon, probably accelerate speciation. Speciation in inshore-dwelling cichlid species is the most active, so inshore habits and algal diets are not restrictions.
