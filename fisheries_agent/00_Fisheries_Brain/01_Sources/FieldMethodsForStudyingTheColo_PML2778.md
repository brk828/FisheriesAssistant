# Field Methods for Studying the Colorado River Fishes in Grand Canyon National Park

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This methods paper evaluates field equipment and sampling techniques for studying the fish assemblage of the [[Colorado River]] in [[Grand Canyon National Park]], with emphasis on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Work ran from October 1990 to November 1991 as part of the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES), which assessed the effects of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations on native fishes. The authors tested small maneuverable research boats, modified sampling gears, electrofishing, and [[Radiotelemetry]] in whitewater reaches. They conclude these methods are effective for improving understanding of the ichthyofauna of these remote, little-known regions.

## Background & Description

Whitewater canyon reaches of the Colorado River basin have historically been among the least intensively sampled areas in the basin. Access is limited, and conventional gears are often ineffective. Surveys within National Park Service jurisdiction indicated these reaches hold some of the last populations of several federally listed endemic fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), Humpback Chub, [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The Grand Canyon contains the largest known Humpback Chub population.

The study area comprised 275 km of river from Kwagunt Rapid (RK 91) to Diamond Creek (RK 366). It was divided into three reaches (Kwagunt Rapid to Red Canyon; Red Canyon to Havasu Creek; Havasu Creek to Diamond Creek) and subdivided into strata based on the geomorphic classification of [[Grand Canyon Geomorphology]] (Schmidt and Graf). Sampling sites were randomly selected from these strata to achieve an approximately even distribution across habitat types.

**Research boats.** Two boat models were used: the SU-16 (4.9 m), used for electrofishing, and the heavy-duty SH-170 (5.2 m), used for netting and radiotracking. Both were inflatable, Hypalon-hulled craft with marine plywood floors and a 40-hp Yamaha outboard. The SU-16 had a square bow giving more room for netters; the SH-170 had a tapered bow, though no performance difference was noted. Boats were operated by skilled handlers, and large rapids were sometimes ascended upstream or negotiated by backing down into eddies.

**Electrofishing system.** Two SU-16 boats carried a 5,000-W generator and a Mark XX Complex Pulse System (CPS) to produce pulsed direct current and reduce spinal injuries. Two 40-cm spherical stainless steel electrodes were used. Typical output was 200-250 V and 8-15 A, with water conductivity of 832 to 1,103 µmhos/cm. Anode and cathode were swapped hourly to clean electrode surfaces. The live well was built into the internal frame, eliminating external drag.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Colorado River basin is difficult for ichthyofaunal work. Access points are few and sometimes hundreds of kilometers apart. Snowmelt flows can increase volume 10- to 20-fold (the river rose from 200 to 2,000 m³/s in 1986). Daily hydropower fluctuations below dams such as [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and Glen Canyon Dam make travel hazardous, alter habitat, and can strand or inundate gears. Turbidity varies widely and affects fish behavior, electrofishing visibility, and conductivity, which in turn influences electrofishing efficiency. Rapids further limit travel and sampling opportunities.

Filamentous algae (*Cladophora glomerata*) accumulated rapidly on nets, especially at higher flows, making them visible to fish and ineffective. Bonytail had recently been captured in Black Rocks, Desolation and Gray canyons, and Cataract Canyon, indicating additional persistent populations.

## Key Findings

**Electrofishing (highest catch rate for chub).** Electrofishing produced the highest catch rate for Humpback Chub at 12.6 fish/10 h. It yielded 241 of 281 (86%) young-of-year, 60 of 77 (78%) juveniles, and 39 of 606 (6%) adults, accounting for 380 of 964 chub captured. Of 9,063 fish captured in Grand Canyon, 5,613 (62%) were captured by electrofishing. Bruises or spinal deformities were observed on 1.3% of fish, mostly non-target species. Among rainbow trout, 62 of 3,013 (2.1%) showed bruises and 9 (0.3%) showed spinal deformity; among brown trout, 2 of 601 (0.3%) showed bruises. Three rainbow trout died from electrofishing. One adult Humpback Chub died after extended narcosis.

**Trammel nets (most efficient gear for chub).** Catch rates were 12.1 fish/30 m/100 h for 3.8-cm mesh and 6.6 fish/30 m/100 h for 2.5-cm mesh. The 3.8-cm mesh captured 271 chub (28%), all adults (45% of adults in the discussion). The 2.5-cm mesh captured 134 chub (14%), including 128 adults and 6 juveniles. Only one Humpback Chub died from a trammel net.

**Gill nets.** Gill nets were used at 3.8 cm and 5.1 cm mesh, plus experimental panels of 1.3, 2.5, 3.8, and 5.1 cm. Gill nets held fish by the head and gill area, making injury more likely to be fatal. No Humpback Chub were lost among 168 caught. The 5.1-cm mesh contributed only about 2% of chub captured and was judged ineffective.

**Hoop nets and minnow traps.** Hoop nets (0.6, 0.9, and 1.2 m) caught two chub each in the 0.6-m and 1.2-m nets (0.4 fish/100 h) and none in the 0.9-m nets. Hoop nets were not effective in the mainstem. Unbaited minnow traps caught 40 young-of-year and 3 juvenile chub (0.7 fish/100 h), with no injuries, and were the only gear besides electrofishing to catch young-of-year.

**Seines.** Seines (3 m × 0.9 m, 0.3-cm mesh) captured no Humpback Chub but caught many other native and nonnative species.

**Radiotelemetry.** Fifty-three adult Humpback Chub were radio-tagged from October 1990 to November 1991. Six fish received 9-g transmitters and 47 received 11-g transmitters. Four of the six 9-g fish were tracked 30-59 days (mean 50.8 days); 43 of the 47 11-g fish were tracked 56-187 days (mean 99 days). Only one of the 53 fish died following surgery and initial tracking. Transmitters were implanted in the peritoneal cavity, with weight limited to 2% of body weight in air. Fifteen frequencies and three pulse rates (40, 60, or 80 pulses/min) yielded 45 unique signatures. Signal range was 57 m at a maximum depth of 2-4 m in the mainstem (860 µmhos/cm) but only 3 m in the Little Colorado River (1,630 µmhos/cm). Tests showed 11-g external-antenna transmitters were received from 50 m at 1.5 m depth and from 100 m at 1 m depth. Radiotelemetry revealed spawning ascents into the [[Little Colorado River]] confirmed by declining mainstem catches.

**Conclusions.** The authors concluded that the small research boats, modified gears, and radiotelemetry were effective in whitewater regions. They emphasized that continued refinement is needed and that trained, experienced biologists and boat handlers are essential to sampling validity and safety.
