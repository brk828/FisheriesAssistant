# Studies in the Genus Ictiobus (Buffalofishes)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1958 Oklahoma State University master's thesis by William Ray Heard examines the taxonomic confusion among the small-mouthed [[Buffalofish]] forms, [[Smallmouth Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus bubalus*) and [[Black Buffalo]] (*Ictiobus niger*), in Oklahoma reservoirs. Using controlled breeding attempts, larval rearing, and morphometric analysis of adults from the Red River and Illinois River, the study found that the two forms could not be reliably separated. The author concludes that the small-mouthed buffalofish may represent one highly variable species, or two species that have hybridized to the point of introgression. Larval development was documented and found broadly consistent with other [[Catostomidae]].

## Background & Description

- **Subfamily:** Ictiobinae of the sucker family Catostomidae. Three U.S. species are recognized: *I. bubalus*, *I. niger*, and the easily separated [[Bigmouth Buffalo]] (*I. cyprinellus*).
- **Nomenclatural history:** [[Rafinesque]] described *Catostomus bubalus* and *C. niger* in 1820 without preserved types. Agassiz (1854, 1855) and later authors shuffled the names, and Hubbs (1930) resurrected *niger* for the black buffalofish. Taxonomic keys (Bailey 1956, Trautman 1957, Hubbs and Lagler 1949) rely on relative characters: projected body depth, head width, eye size, and maxilla length.
- **Study aims:** Observe the development and behavior of young *I. bubalus*, *I. niger*, and a putative hybrid; produce known hybrids; and evaluate whether morphological keys can separate the forms.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Life history:** Buffalofishes typically spawn in March and April at about 60 °F, communally, on aquatic and flooded terrestrial vegetation, usually after spring floods or water-level rises (Canfield 1922; Yeager 1936; Walker and Frank 1952). No reports on reservoir reproductive ecology were found.
- **Reservoir effects:** Reservoirs increase fishery productivity, but the buffalofishes in Oklahoma reservoirs appear to exceed their stream potentials, unlike many stream fishes that decline.
- **Habitat divergence (hypothesis):** Trautman (1957) associates *bubalus* with swift water and *niger* with eddies and backwaters; the author proposes that reservoirs offer less habitat diversity, which may blur these forms.
- **Hybridization in the wild:** Trautman (1957) reported an *I. bubalus* × *I. cyprinellus* hybrid from western Lake Erie, linked to introduction of a stream species into a lake habitat.
- **Sampling locations:** Red River below [[Denison Dam]] (turbine raceways), Illinois River at the upper end of [[Tenkiller Reservoir]], and [[Fort Gibson Reservoir]].

## Key Findings

**Materials and methods**
- Adults were collected from the Red River (Bryan County) and the Illinois River/Tenkiller area (Cherokee County). Forty *I. niger*-identified fish were taken from the Denison Dam turbine raceway on January 24, 1957.
- Of about 150 small-mouthed buffalofish caught at Tenkiller, 26 were identified as *bubalus*; the rest were difficult to assign.
- Four one-acre or fractional-acre ponds were used for natural propagation. Artificial spawning used acetone-dried pituitary from a flathead catfish, with a second spawning on April 18, 1957, producing an estimated 80% hatch from 5,000–6,000 eggs.

**Egg and larval development**
- Newly hatched prolarvae measured 5.5–6.5 mm with about 27 myotomes; pigment was restricted to the eyes.
- Eggs were tannish-yellow, adhesive, and opaque, with a mean diameter of 2.1 mm (range 1.9–2.5 mm) after water hardening.
- Incubation at 80 °F: hatching began within 83 hours; most eggs in the aquaria hatched between 130 and 140 hours at 71.6 °F.
- Yolk absorption was complete by about 144 hours (about 7.5 mm). The 144-hour larva corresponds most closely to stage 33 of [[Winn and Miller]]'s table.
- Postlarvae (8–13 mm) developed a stomach, fin rays, and forked caudal fin. Mouth movement from terminal to ventral and the division of external nares were tied to juvenile metamorphosis, with scale formation near 23–27 mm.

**Behavior**
- Prolarvae swam vertically to the surface in repeated caudal thrusts, apparently instinctive rather than a physiological necessity. Eight prolarvae confined under a dish continued this behavior for 48 hours.
- Feeding began on day 7 on "Micrograin"; plankton (Copepoda, Cladocera) was added on day 10.
- Juveniles (16–20 mm) fed vigorously on larger plankton in a manner resembling feeding minnows.

**Taxonomic results**
- Projected body depth/standard length: 69% of all fish were identifiable as either *bubalus* or *niger*. Only 50% of Illinois River fish and 81% of Red River fish fell within the *niger* range.
- Head width/standard length: 49% of fish could be either species.
- Eye size in head length: 62% could be either species.
- Eye size in snout length: 58% could be *bubalus*, 47% *niger*, 13% either.
- Body depth over curvature and maxilla length provided the closest grouping of specimens. Overall, the author estimated no more than 20% of 3,178 fish from Fort Gibson, Tenkiller, and Texhoma could have been confidently designated.

**Propagation outcomes**
- Natural propagation in ponds produced no detectable spawning or young in any pond.
- Artificially spawned young from known parents (four fish, 178.5 mm mean standard length at 6½ months) appeared more *bubalus*-like, while the parents appeared more *niger*-like. The parents were not reliably identifiable, so the young could not be called hybrids.
- Heavy rain (7.5 inches) on May 20, 1957, and predation by golden shiner, green sunfish, and bluegill caused heavy losses.

**Conclusions**
- Identification of the small-mouthed buffalofishes in Oklahoma is doubtful; the forms may be one highly variable species whose extremes correspond to the *bubalus* and *niger* types.
- If two species exist, hybrid introgression has "all but eliminated species separation in many Oklahoma waters."
- Larval development agrees with that of other suckers (Stewart 1926; Winn and Miller 1954).
- Limitations: samples were non-random and small, so the study does not prove the two species are invalid.
