# Key to the Fishes of Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This Utah state fish key, compiled by [[Arthur E. Dammann]] and [[Arden R. Gaufin]] at the University of Utah, provides a family-level and genus/species-level [[Dichotomous Key]] for the fishes of Utah. It lists **53 species**, of which **31 are native** and **22 are introduced**. The text also explains how the state's fish fauna arose through the geological isolation of drainage basins, and it describes the morphology and diagnostic features used to identify each group. Nearly all species are food or game fishes; the exception is a minnow introduced for [[Mosquito Control]] and the ornamental [[Goldfish]].

## Background & Description

**General fish biology.** The introduction defines fishes as aquatic, bilaterally symmetrical vertebrates with a cartilaginous or bony skeleton, paired fins, gill respiration throughout life, a two-chambered heart, and egg-laying reproduction with development usually outside the body. All Utah fishes belong to the subclass [[Teleostei]] (bony fishes).

**Family key.** The key separates families primarily by fin position (pelvic fins abdominal versus thoracic), presence or absence of an [[Adipose Fin]], barbels, scales, and dorsal fin structure. Major families covered:

- [[Catfish Family]] (Ameiuridae): barbels present, naked skin; channel catfish and common bullhead.
- [[Grayling Family]] (Thymallidae): tall sail-like dorsal fin; Montana grayling.
- [[Salmon and Trout Family]] (Salmonidae): large or medium scales, adipose fin present; Pacific salmon, trouts, chars.
- [[Whitefish Family]] (Coregonidae): small terminal mouth, deeply forked tail; whitefishes and cisco.
- [[Sucker Family]] (Catostomidae): inferior sucking mouth, pharyngeal comb-like teeth; mountain, fine-scaled, and western suckers.
- [[Minnow Family]] (Cyprinidae): short dorsal fin, pharyngeal teeth; carp, goldfish, chubs, daces, roundtails.
- Desert Minnow family (printed as "Medidae"): single genus *Lepidomeda*, represented in Utah by *Lepidomeda vittata*, native to the Virgin River drainage.
- [[Livebearer Family]] (Poeciliidae): anal fin anterior to dorsal fin; top minnow.
- [[Sunfish Family]] (Centrarchidae): compressed body, dorsal fin in two parts, 3 to 10 anal spines depending on genus; black crappie, black bass, bluegill, green sunfish.
- [[Perch Family]] (Percidae): divided dorsal fin; yellow perch.
- [[Sculpin Family]] (Cottidae): scaleless, fan-like fins; mountain and Rocky Mountain bullheads.

**Morphological terminology.** The text illustrates standard external parts (caudal fin, dorsal, pectoral, pelvic, anal fins, lateral line, snout) used throughout the key.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical isolation and speciation.** During the late Pleistocene, the [[Lake Bonneville]] basin formed part of the [[Snake River]]–[[Columbia River]] drainage, allowing fish to migrate widely. As Lake Bonneville declined, western Utah became a closed basin and its fish were isolated. The [[Sevier Lake Basin]] later separated from the [[Great Salt Lake Basin]]. As Great Salt Lake grew saltier, it formed a barrier between the Bear, Weber, and Utah Lake–Jordan River systems. The text argues this progressive isolation led to inbreeding and the formation of well-defined species. Examples include native whitefishes restricted to [[Bear Lake]], and distinct minnows, chubs, and suckers in the Great Salt Lake and Sevier basins.

**Introductions and hybridization.** Since European settlement, desirable food and game fishes have been introduced into Utah lakes and streams. Closely related native and introduced species have interbred, producing hybrids that are difficult to identify, particularly among trouts (see [[Hybridization]]).

**Basin distribution.** Species accounts place fishes in several regions:

- Great Salt Lake Basin: Northern Utah mountain sucker, Utah Lake mullet, Northern Utah dace, Utah Lake June sucker.
- Bonneville Basin: Bonneville minnow, Bonneville chub, Utah chub, Bonneville cutthroat.
- Colorado and Green River basins: Flannel-mouthed sucker, hump-backed sucker, roundtail chub, white salmon, Colorado River cutthroat.
- Sevier River basin: Dusky dace, central Utah mountain sucker.
- Bear Lake: Bonneville whitefish, Bear Lake whitefish, Bonneville cisco.
- Introduced lakes: [[Fish Lake]], [[Strawberry Reservoir]], [[Echo Reservoir]], and the lakes of the Uintah Mountains.

**Ecological notes.** Several species are valued for controlling pests. The introduced top minnow feeds heavily on mosquito larvae, and the text urges owners of ornamental ponds to stock it from local mosquito abatement commissions. Utah Lake supports such abundant mullets and June suckers that it was reputed to be the greatest sucker pond in the world. The Pacific salmon species introduced to Fish Lake and Strawberry Reservoir may no longer reproduce, possibly because their sea-run life history was disrupted in freshwater.

## Key Findings

- **Fauna size:** 53 fish species are listed for Utah: 31 native and 22 introduced.
- **Speciation via isolation:** The authors attribute the origin of many endemic species to the progressive isolation of basins following Lake Bonneville's decline and the rise of Great Salt Lake.
- **Pacific salmon:** Silver salmon, chinook salmon, and sockeye (little redfish) were introduced to Fish Lake and Strawberry Reservoir. Their successful reproduction is doubtful.
- **Trout identification:** Eight trout species and forms are keyed, including the Yellowstone, Pyramid Lake, Bonneville, and Colorado River cutthroats, steelhead (Rainbow), German brown trout, and McCloud River rainbow trout. Many are difficult to separate because of hybridization.
- **Large introduced predators:** [[Mackinaw Trout]] (lake trout) reach about 30 lb locally, and German brown trout occasionally reach 25 lb. Channel catfish can reach 2 ft and 25 lb.
- **Largest and smallest fishes:** The "white salmon" (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) of the Colorado and Green rivers is the largest minnow, 1 to 3 ft, up to 5 ft and 80 lb. The Bonneville minnow, about 1.5 inches long, is the smallest native fish.
- **Restricted endemics:** The Bonneville whitefish, Bear Lake whitefish, and Bonneville cisco are known only from Bear Lake; the June sucker is abundant in Utah Lake; the dusky dace is restricted to the Sevier River system; the Virgin River desert minnow (*Lepidomeda vittata*) is native only to the Virgin River drainage.
- **Pest control:** The introduced top minnow (*Gambusia affinis affinis*, 1.5–2 in) is valued for consuming mosquito larvae.
- **Identification challenges:** Hybrids among native and introduced salmonids, and among some sucker and dace forms, complicate identification.
- **Gaps:** The text does not provide population abundance data, conservation status, or quantitative habitat measurements. It is a taxonomic and descriptive identification guide.
