# Age Estimation for Razorback Sucker from Lake Mohave, Arizona and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

McCarthy and Minckley (1987) evaluated seven bony structures for age estimation in the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) from [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona and Nevada. Only [[Sectioned Otoliths]] gave reliable ages, especially for large, old fish. Seventy wild-caught fish were estimated at 24 to 44 years old at capture (1981–83). The authors found no detectable recruitment since 1958 and little since 1954, and they concluded that the Lake Mohave stock is likely to be extirpated before the year 2000 if the pattern seen in other [[Colorado River Basin]] reservoirs continues.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid fish endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Its range once extended from the Green River in Wyoming to the Colorado River delta in northwestern Mexico. It was historically abundant and an important food fish for Native Americans and early settlers. Today, large adults persist in the upper Colorado and Green rivers, and small fish are rare and geographically scattered.

Lower basin reservoirs were first colonized by substantial numbers of razorback suckers. The fish disappeared from [[Gila River]] system reservoirs in the 1950s, about 40 years after dam closure. Remaining populations in [[Lake Powell]] and [[Lake Havasu]] are sparse. A large population persists only in Lake Mohave, where [[Davis Dam]] was closed in 1942–51 and the reservoir filled in 1953–54.

Lake Mohave fish are consistently large (females averaged 50 cm and males 45 cm standard length in 1981). They often show blindness and tumors, which suggest considerable age. Annual spawning and larval production occur, but larvae disappear before reaching 12 mm total length, so recruitment is absent.

The study's objectives were to:
- evaluate bony structures for age estimation;
- test the hypothesis that Lake Mohave fish are long-lived;
- describe individual growth patterns.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study material and methods.** The authors collected and examined 88 wild-caught fish from Lake Mohave, using trammel nets and beach seines in 1980–84. Sources included the Dexter National Fish Hatchery (9 broodfish), Arizona Game and Fish Department (4), and California Department of Fish and Game (16). Forty-five hatchery-reared, pond-reared fish of known age were used as validation controls, drawn from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]. These included year classes 1974, 1979, 1980, and 1984 (30 young-of-the-year).

**Structures examined.** The seven structures were:
- opercular bones
- cleithral bones
- branchiostegal rays
- vertebral centra
- scales
- pectoral fin rays
- sagittal otoliths (intact and sectioned)

Annuli were counted only when a mark crossed the whole structure. Age reliability declined as more marks were excluded.

**Growth and back-calculation.** Standard length at each annulus was back-calculated from otolith radius using the [[Fraser-Lee Back-Calculation]] approach, with a developmental origin of about 10 mm. Otolith growth was assumed to parallel somatic growth. Otolith annulus formation is linked to seasonal temperature variation, which the authors consider likely in warm-monomictic reservoirs such as Lake Mohave.

**Reservoir effects.** Growth to the fifth annulus was faster in fish hatched after [[Davis Dam]] closure. This may reflect enhanced [[Zooplankton Feeding]] in a filling reservoir. The species has a protrusible mouth and closely spaced gill rakers, which indicate facultative planktivory. Adults in Lake Mohave feed mainly on zooplankton. The authors suggest the species may be better adapted to backwater and oxbow habitats of the unmodified river.

**Related species.** The authors cite [[Cui-ui]] (*Chasmistes cujus*) in Pyramid Lake, Nevada, which stops growing at about 20 years of age, as a comparison for long-lived catostomids.

## Key Findings

**1. Structure reliability**
- Only sectioned otoliths gave readable, consistent annuli in large, old fish.
- Opercula, cleithra, branchiostegal rays, and vertebrae were unreliable because articular thickening and crowding obscured inner annuli. This led to underestimation.
- Scales were questionable to useless. Estimates from scales of 14 Lake Mohave fish ranged from 12 to 29 years, and different scales from the same fish gave very different results.
- Fin rays also gave inconsistent results.
- Otolith sections were readable in 74 of 88 fish (84.1%). Unreadable sections were due to:
  - breakage during preparation (2.3%)
  - sections not passing through the focus (3.4%)
  - irregular margins (10.2%)

**2. Validation**
- In 5 hatchery fish older than a year, complete annuli matched known ages on all seven structures.
- None of the 15 hatchery young-of-the-year showed annuli.
- Annular marks in wild fish near the focus resembled those in hatchery fish, which supports using structures for early-life ages.

**3. Age range**
- Wild fish from Lake Mohave were estimated at 24 to 44 years old at capture (1981–83).
- Sectioned otoliths from all 70 wild fish showed 23 or more annuli. The oldest was 44 years, with the margin counted as a final annulus.
- Sixty-two fish (88.6%) hatched before or during the construction and filling of Lake Mohave (1942–54).

**4. Growth**
- Females reached an average standard length of 140 ± 4 mm at the first annulus and 276 ± 7 mm at the fifth.
- Males were significantly smaller: 103 ± 4 mm at the first annulus and 214 ± 8 mm at the fifth (Student's t-test, p < 0.05).
- Mean first-year growth was 26.7 ± 0.8% of standard length at capture in females. About 50% of total standard length was reached by year five.
- Growth slowed to a few millimeters per year after the fifth or sixth year.
- Fish hatched after closure of Lake Mohave in 1951 grew faster to the fifth annulus:
  - males: 180 ± 7 mm (n = 5) before vs. 234 ± 11 mm (n = 7) after
  - females: 256 ± 19 mm (n = 8) before vs. 298 ± 10 mm (n = 16) after
- First-annulus size did not differ significantly.

**5. Recruitment and extirpation**
- No detectable recruitment has occurred in Lake Mohave since 1958, and little since 1954.
- Based on the pattern in other basin reservoirs (disappearance 40–50 years after impoundment), the authors predict extirpation of the Lake Mohave stock before the year 2000 unless the trend is reversed.

**6. Maximum size**
- The largest Lake Mohave specimens were an unweighed female of 57.6 cm SL (73.6 cm TL) and a 4.99 kg female.
- Historical records of larger fish, such as those from Saguaro Lake, are considered suspect because no specimens were seen.

**7. Comparative growth**
- Tagged razorback suckers in the Green River, Utah, grew an average of 2.2 mm per year (n = 39, 1–8 years).
- Growth rates in upper basin adults are also very slow, consistent with the Lake Mohave findings.
