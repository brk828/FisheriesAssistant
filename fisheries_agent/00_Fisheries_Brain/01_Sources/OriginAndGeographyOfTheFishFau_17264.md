# Origin and Geography of the Great Lakes Fish Fauna

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

Bailey and Smith (1981) review the origin, geography and postglacial history of the fish fauna of the [[Laurentian Great Lakes]] basin. The native fauna comprises 153 species in 64 genera and 25 families. The authors argue that the fauna is recent, since the basin was glaciated during the [[Wisconsinan Glaciation]] and most species colonized within the past 14,000 years. Colonization occurred mainly through open-water connections with ice-free refugia, with additional access from stream capture and canals. The paper also addresses within-species differentiation and the taxonomically difficult [[Coregonus]] complex.

## Background & Description

The Great Lakes are the largest freshwater reservoir on Earth. Each of the five principal lakes ranks among the nine largest freshwater lakes in the world, and the basin also contains thousands of smaller lakes and extensive stream systems. Ice exposure dates from about 14,000 years ago at the southern edge of the basin and about 9,000 years ago in the north.

The fauna is relatively large for a region of recent colonization and northern latitude (chiefly 40°–50° N). The authors attribute this partly to multiple northward postglacial dispersal routes through major waterways that drained into the Mississippi River basin, a centre of temperate freshwater fish diversity.

Table 1 gives the composition of the fauna. Including introduced forms, it totals 174 species, 71 genera and 28 families, with 180 taxa when subspecies are counted. Of these, 21 alien forms are included. Families with the most species include [[Cyprinidae]] (53 species, 55 taxa), [[Percidae]] (21 species) and [[Salmonidae]] (20 species), with [[Centrarchidae]] also well represented (12 species).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geological chronology.** The authors reconstruct lake history in four stages, drawing on Prest (1970), Zoltai and others:

1. Periglacial and [[Lake Algonquin]] stage, 14,000–10,600 years ago, with diverse outlets, initially to the south.
2. Maximum isolation of lakes, 10,600–8,100 years ago, with major drainage to the St. Lawrence.
3. Broadly connected [[Nipissing Stage]], 8,100–6,000 years ago, ending in a three-outlet phase.
4. Modern configuration, from 6,000 years ago to the present.

Key connections included the [[Chicago Outlet]] to the Mississippi, the Maumee–Wabash route, and the North Bay outlet to the St. Lawrence. Lakes [[Lake Agassiz]] and [[Lake Duluth]] provided northwestern dispersal routes to the Arctic drainage. Modern isolation of the Great Lakes from the Mississippi followed closure of the Chicago and North Bay outlets about 6,000 years ago. Lake Ontario was linked to the upper lakes again in 1825 by the [[Welland Canal]].

**Paleoecology.** Fossil fish records from periglacial deposits are scarce. Fossil marine mammals in Michigan beaches (a finback whale rib, a sperm whale vertebra and rib, a bowhead whale rib, and two walrus records) suggest large volumes of water and extensive aquatic habitat in periglacial lakes. The authors state that fish faunas during glacial advances were more restricted, since southward drainage slopes limited lake habitat south of the basin.

**Refugia and colonization routes.**
- [[Mississippi Refugium]]: the source of most species. Species distribution patterns indicate colonization of the Great Lakes by 122 kinds solely from Mississippi basin refugia.
- [[Atlantic Refugia]]: 36 species colonized from Atlantic drainage refugia. Of these, 14 species (17 kinds) entered only from the Atlantic, and 22 species came from both Atlantic and Mississippi refugia.
- Dual refugia: at least 18 kinds show dual origins.

The authors estimate that it is unlikely any species colonized from an Alaskan refuge in the past 14,000 years.

**Stream capture.** The [[Genesee River]] case study illustrates a non-glacial route. The Genesee population of [[Copper Redhorse]]'s relative [[Exoglossum laurae]] is disjunct from its Ohio basin range and probably entered through the [[Stream Capture]] of Black Creek and Oil Creek in Allegany County, New York. An Oil Creek collection of July 28, 1937 included Exoglossum laurae and 19 other species.

## Key Findings

- **Recent fauna:** The fish fauna of the Great Lakes is of recent origin. All species now present colonized within the past 14,000 years, and the paper notes that interglacial faunas were erased by each ice advance.
- **Three modes of access:** Immigration through open-water channels from refugia, intra-basin evolution in new habitat, and human-mediated introduction. Introduced fishes were exceptionally successful because the Great Lakes were ecologically unsaturated.
- **Within-species differentiation:** Six species are treated as having two subspecies each. Complementary pairs include [[Rhinichthys]] (*R. atratulus atratulus* and *R. a. meleagris*), [[Fundulus diaphanus]] (*F. d. diaphanus* and *F. d. menona*) and [[Fantail Darter]] (*Etheostoma flabellare flabellare* and *E. f. lineolatum*).
- **Walleye and blue pike:** [[Blue Pike]] (*Stizostedion vitreum glaucum*) was endemic to Lakes Erie and Ontario and is now extinct. The authors consider it a stock of the widespread [[Walleye]] rather than a valid subspecies, because subspecies are defined as allopatric.
- **Coregonus problem:** The authors recognize great genetic differentiation within the ciscoes and chubs (*Coregonus*). At least four and possibly up to eight forms survived or differentiated during the last glaciation in proglacial waters. Morphological and biochemical data support only one to four current biological species apart from [[Lake Whitefish]] (*C. clupeaformis*). Stocks are isolated by homing behaviour specific to time and place. The lack of intrinsic reproductive isolation makes rare forms vulnerable to extinction through hybridization.
- **Taxonomic conservatism:** The authors list 10 coregonine species without additional subspecies. Hubbs and Lagler (1958) had provisionally accepted 14 species and 45 total taxa for coregonines, and had also recognized subspecies in several other species that the present authors consider unsupported.
- **Management-relevant species:** Species of management interest with Atlantic refugium populations include [[Lake Trout]] (*Salvelinus namaycush*), sea lamprey (*Petromyzon marinus*) and Atlantic salmon (*Salmo salar*). Species of management interest from Mississippi refugia include lake sturgeon (*Acipenser fulvescens*), gars, [[Cisco]] stocks and walleye.
