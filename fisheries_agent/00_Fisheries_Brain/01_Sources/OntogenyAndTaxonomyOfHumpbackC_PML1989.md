# Ontogeny and Taxonomy of Humpback Chub, Bonytail, and Roundtail Chub Larvae and Early Juveniles

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This doctoral dissertation by [[Robert T. Muth]] (Colorado State University, 1990) describes the early life stages and develops diagnostic keys for three endemic [[Gila]] chubs of the [[Gila robusta Superspecies Complex]]: [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*G. elegans*), and [[Colorado Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta robusta*). Over 750 known-identity cultured and wild larvae and young-of-the-year (YOY) juveniles were analyzed for developmental, morphometric, and meristic features. The work addresses a long-standing inability to identify young of these federally listed or sensitive fishes, which has hampered research, management, and recovery efforts.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomic context:** The three taxa form a closely related complex endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Adults often exceed 40 cm total length, and their morphology reflects adaptation to swift, turbulent, sediment-laden flows, ranging from the highly specialized Humpback Chub to the more generalized Colorado Roundtail Chub.
- **Conservation status:** Humpback Chub and Bonytail are federally listed as endangered. Bonytail is considered the rarest native Colorado Basin fish, with no confirmed natural recruitment documented since the 1950s. Humpback Chub reproduces naturally, with its highest concentrations in the lower [[Little Colorado River]] and in Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon reaches of the Colorado River. Colorado Roundtail Chub is widely distributed but listed as protected or of special concern by several basin states.
- **Nomenclature confusion:** Common names such as "bonytail" and "roundtail" were used interchangeably in older literature, and *G. robusta* was also called squawfish, Virgin trout, and Gila trout.
- **Rationale:** Identification of young Gila was "usually left at the genus level" in field collections, limiting use of early-life data for monitoring and life-history studies.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Threats:** Decline of these fishes is attributed to habitat modification and loss, introduction of non-native species, and water pollution.
- **Hatchery and stocking:** Hatchery culture of Humpback Chub and Bonytail began in the early 1980s (at [[Hilltop Beach National Fish Hatchery]] and Dexter); stocked fish were tracked by radio telemetry, with only four of 11 (1988 stocking) and two of 47 (1989 stocking) surviving to transmitter end-of-life.
- **Hybridization:** Hybridization among *Gila* may occur in disturbed river sections, and Rosenfeld (1986) and Douglas et al. (1989) documented taxonomic uncertainty. Some field-collected young from Cataract Canyon were classified as possible hybrids.
- **Environmental modifiers of meristics:** The author notes that meristic counts are labile and respond negatively to water temperature (colder water generally yields higher counts), with experiments in other cyprinids and percids supporting this pattern.
- **Culture conditions:** Brood stock were collected from the Little Colorado River, Black Rocks, Lake Mohave, the Yampa and White rivers, and the Colorado River near Horse Gulch. Cultured young were reared at 16–25 °C in raceways and troughs, fed zooplankton and commercial trout starter diets.

## Key Findings

**Developmental timing**
- Most developmental events occurred at a smaller size in Bonytail than in Humpback or Colorado Roundtail Chub.
- Colorado Roundtail Chub transformed to the juvenile period, formed the gut loop, and absorbed the finfold at smaller sizes than the other two.
- Humpback Chub hatched and acquired pectoral and pelvic fin rays at smaller sizes than Colorado Roundtail Chub.
- Egg diameter after fertilization and water hardening averaged 2.2 mm (range 2.0–2.4) for Bonytail, 2.7 mm (2.3–3.3) for Humpback Chub, and 2.8 mm (2.7–3.1) for Colorado Roundtail Chub, according to the author's reported values.
- Within-taxon variability in melanophore pigmentation was high, and no taxon-specific differences in melanophore distribution were found.

**Meristic characters (diagnostic)**
- Bonytail was readily distinguished by postvent and total myomeres or vertebrae, gill rakers, and modal dorsal fin principal ray or pterygiophore number. Bonytail typically had 10 dorsal principal rays, 133 or more total gill rakers, and 20 or more postvent and 49 or more total myomeres/vertebrae.
- Humpback and Colorado Roundtail Chub had 110 or fewer total gill rakers, typically 19 or fewer postvent and 47 or fewer total myomeres/vertebrae, and typically 9 dorsal principal rays.
- Humpback Chub and Bonytail typically had 10 anal fin principal rays, versus 9 in Colorado Roundtail Chub; this was the only meristic series, besides dorsal rays and myomere-related counts, that separated the two non-Bonytail taxa.

**Morphometric characters (diagnostic)**
- Six morphometrics separated metalarvae and juveniles. Bonytail juveniles had narrower caudal peduncles. Humpback Chub and Bonytail had a longer caudal peduncle relative to trunk length (CPLR) than Colorado Roundtail Chub, with 80–100% of Humpback and Bonytail specimens exceeding the Colorado Roundtail mean.
- All fins were longest in Humpback Chub and shortest in Colorado Roundtail Chub; combined fin length (ΣF as % SL) gave the best separation between the latter two.
- Eye diameter, once considered diagnostic, did not differ notably among the three fishes.

**Mouth and snout**
- Larger Humpback Chub metalarvae (over 15 mm SL) showed a somewhat subterminal mouth overhung by the snout. Clear differences from the other taxa appeared only after juveniles reached about 30 mm SL, and within-taxon variability limited their diagnostic value for YOY.

**Keys and methods**
- Artificial dichotomous keys were developed for protolarvae, flexion and postflexion mesolarvae, metalarvae, and YOY juveniles (under 50 mm SL), using DELTA-based programs. Keys often conclude with "*Gila* sp." when identity is ambiguous.
- Measurements were analyzed via log-log allometry plots (Fuiman and Corazza 1979 method). Most growth coefficients were below 1.0, indicating negative allometry.
- Exponential growth regressions for cultured young (SL against days after hatching) were presented for each taxon, and age-back-calculation equations for spawning-date estimation were reviewed and refined. The author noted that a re-evaluation of [[Haynes et al. (1985)]] age equations for Colorado Squawfish produced a simpler equation for fish under 22 mm TL.

**Author's conclusions and recommendations**
- Taxonomic characters from known-identity cultured fish should be complemented with field-collected wild young, since culture can produce morphological and pigment artifacts.
- The author states that field identification of wild young *Gila* using these criteria is "not realistic" and that keys "can be a misleading illusion" if used too literally.
- Dissertation data should be tested across broader populations and the entire *G. robusta* superspecies complex, including hybrid studies and selected habitat parameters.
- A Smithsonian Institution taxonomy project for *Gila* was discussed, with preliminary electrophoretic results (Buth, Minckley, Mayden) suggesting subspecies of *G. robusta* are biochemically similar while Humpback Chub and Bonytail are distinct.
- The dissertation questions whether listing the entire complex under the Endangered Species Act would resolve taxonomic issues, noting the species definition in the 1973 Act and the expected opposition from water developers.
