# Report from the Entomology Lab on the Caddisfly Survey

#aquatic-entomology #trichoptera #caddisfly

## Executive Summary

This internal report from the [[Entomology Lab]] documents a survey of caddisfly (Trichoptera) swarms along the [[Colorado River]] near Parker and Ehrenberg, Arizona, and across the river in California. Residents reported nuisance swarms, and the survey team inspected trailer parks, a fish-farm-style complex, and standing-water areas. The team found that the caddisfly problem appears to be linked to river water flow regulation rather than to any single cause. Insecticide applications for mosquito and pest control were reported in the area, but residents and managers said caddisfly numbers declined in some locations after spraying. The authors conclude that no single control measure is a panacea and that long-term population effects are likely limited.

## Background & Description

The survey was prompted by reports of caddisfly swarms affecting residents along the lower river. Caddisflies are aquatic insects in the order [[Trichoptera]]. Their larvae live in water, often in silk-lined cases or nets, while the winged adults are the stage that swarms and causes nuisance problems. The report notes that the species were not identified in the text, and no species names are given.

Survey activities included:

- **Parker, Arizona:** Visit to a complex whose manager reported that caddisfly populations had dropped after an insecticide application. Larvae were found in loose nets covered with small rocks.
- **Rust River Glass:** Interview with Mr. Rust, the coordinator of a local [[Mosquito Control]] program. He described the spraying schedule (see Key Findings).
- **Trailer park below [[Headgate Dam]]:** Inspection of an area where caddisflies were present on palm trees and wires. Residents said the area had also been sprayed.
- **California side:** Visit to two trailer parks. Residents reported caddisfly problems that eased at the same time as on the Arizona side. No chemicals were applied on the California side.
- **Standing-water areas:** Several pools along the California side were surveyed and found to contain caddisflies.
- **Ehrenberg, Arizona:** Inspection of a trailer court along the river on Wednesday, June 4. The same caddisfly species was present, and residents said the insects are a recurring problem.

Specimens were collected from all locations. The report notes that the insects were found on algae in some cases and in other habitats in others, which may explain differences in larval occurrence. The authors cite [[Lehmkuhl 1979]] on caddisflies' preference for riffle areas and an additional source on the varied habitat use of caddisfly larvae.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Residents reported that caddisflies have been present for many years, generally limited to emergence periods in the spring and fall. Many said the problem began after a "flood" event, and that it is worse during periods of low water and lessens when water levels are high. The authors connect this pattern to known caddisfly ecology, in which riffle habitats and water flow strongly influence populations.

The authors could not determine whether the spring and fall emergences represent two generations of the same species or two different species. They noted that all caddisflies observed appeared to be the same type.

The report describes the broader context as one of altered river conditions. The caddisfly problem is attributed to unnatural river flow regimes and water management. The authors also note that the insects in the area are a seasonal nuisance rather than a lasting ecological threat.

## Key Findings

- **Caddisfly swarms are a recurring nuisance** in Parker, Ehrenberg, and nearby California trailer parks, with residents describing them as a problem since a past flood event.
- **Insecticide use:** [[Mr Rust]], coordinator of the local [[Mosquito Control]] program, reported that [[Malathion]] was applied as a mosquito control measure in a fogger-type application. The schedule was one application per week after 9:00 p.m. for several weeks, with a follow-up application after the dam gates reopened, and a single pest control application shortly before Memorial Day. The treated area was described as a limited stretch of river, and the report notes some of these details are unclear due to OCR errors.
- **Reported effects of spraying:** The complex manager and residents on the Arizona side reported that caddisfly numbers dropped after spraying. On the California side, where no chemicals were applied, residents reported that caddisfly problems eased at about the same time.
- **Timing and seasonality:** Caddisfly activity appears concentrated in spring and fall and is greater at low water levels.
- **Likely cause:** The problem appears to be tied to river water flow regulation. The authors conclude that the caddisfly outbreak is the result of altered river conditions rather than a single cause.
- **Long-term impact:** The authors assess the long-term effects on the caddisfly population as likely minimal, since only adult caddisflies are affected and populations appear to decline equally on both sides of the river.
- **Management recommendations:** The report recommends:
  - Regulating water flow where practicable.
  - Using insecticides only as little as necessary.
  - Monitoring caddisfly populations.
  - Cooperation between agencies and residents, including reporting of swarm events so that a response team can be dispatched.
- **No single solution:** The authors conclude that there is no single panacea for controlling these insects.
