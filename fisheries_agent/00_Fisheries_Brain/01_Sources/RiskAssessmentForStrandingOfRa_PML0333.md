# Risk Assessment for Stranding of Razorback Sucker and Bonytail Chub in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Reclamation report (Tom Burke, Fishery Biologist, Boulder City, NV, dated 28 May 1999) assesses the risk that daily water-level fluctuations below Hoover, Davis and Parker dams cause stranding of [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]]. It was prompted by a 1997 [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] Biological Opinion that found Reclamation's operations resulted in incidental "Take" of both species. The assessment combined a literature review, a review of published spawning observations, and direct field observations in February and March 1998. The author concludes that the risk is low, provided daily flow fluctuations remain within present patterns, and recommends further observation of spawning and egg incubation and a reevaluation of risk in 2003.

## Background & Description

- **Regulatory trigger:** Reclamation's 1996 Biological Assessment of operation and maintenance on the Colorado River (Lake Mead to Mexico) made a "May Affect" call, because eggs of razorback sucker might be exposed to air in fluctuation zones. The 1997 Biological Opinion found incidental take, but judged it low because extant populations were small.
- **Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*):** Adults spawn in reservoirs and riverine reaches. Eggs are adhesive, and incubation lasts 5-10 days.
- **Bonytail Chub (*Gila elegans*):** Extremely rare in the wild for at least three (possibly five) decades. Spawning behavior is poorly documented.
- **Literature search method:** Reclamation's in-house review of 16 volumes of [[Current References In Fish Research]] (1979-1994) found no papers on fish stranding. A contracted search by Arizona State University (Pacey and Marsh, 1998) used several major databases and returned 125 citations of potential relevance, none of which addressed stranding of the target species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flow regime:** Daily fluctuations are greatest immediately below dams and diminish downstream. Magnitude is highest in summer and lowest in winter. Normal weekday high releases from Hoover Dam are 20,000 cfs (winter), 32,000 cfs (spring) and 38,000 cfs (summer). Corresponding releases at Parker Dam are 10,000, 14,000 and 19,000 cfs.
- **Razorback Sucker concern period:** February and March, when fluctuations are smallest. Daily ranges are 5,000-30,000 cfs at Hoover, 5,000-20,000 cfs at Davis and 5,000-10,000 cfs at Parker.
- **Bonytail Chub concern period:** May, with daily ranges of 10,000-38,000 cfs at Hoover, 10,000-23,000 cfs at Davis and 6,000-? cfs at Parker (the figure is illegible in the source).
- **Spawning habitat:** Wash fans and gravel shelves that remain wetted. Spawning occurs at water temperatures around 18 °C in the Green River. The reach between Hoover Dam and Lake Mohave is considered too cold for bonytail spawning, per the 1990 [[Bonytail Chub Recovery Plan]].
- **Spawning depth and velocity:** At the [[Mueller Spawning Site]], nests lay at 4.5-6.5 feet deep, with mean velocities of 0.12-0.36 m/s.
- **Competition and predation:** Adult carp were abundant at the 1954 bonytail spawning site in Lake Mohave, and carp were presumed to eat most eggs.

## Key Findings

**Literature review**
- No published reports of stranding of razorback sucker or bonytail chub, or of any other native Colorado River fish, were found.
- Stranding was largely associated with salmonids, and with fish in tidepools.
- The only bonytail spawning observation is Jonez and Sumner (1954), in Lake Mohave in May 1954, with about 500 bonytail on a quarter-mile of gravel and no young observed.
- Mueller (1989) documented the only direct observation of razorback spawning in a riverine habitat, with up to 31 adults and 18 spawning depressions in a 300 m² site below Hoover Dam. Eggs and larvae were collected, and the nests were not dewatered during the January-March 1984 study.

**Direct observations (February-March 1998)**
- Three spawning aggregations were found on wash fans between Hoover Dam and Willow Beach. Site 2 was dewatered overnight at its canyon mouth, but none of the occupied areas with two or more fish were dewatered.
- At Site 3, fish were in water deeper than 5 feet, with river flows of 25,000-30,000 cfs.
- Spawning was not confirmed at Site 1, and disturbance by canoeists ended that observation.
- Fish left the spawning areas in late March, possibly after electrofishing and netting in the week of 9 March 1998 for hatchery broodstock.

**Conclusions**
- Risk of stranding to both species is judged **low** under current flow patterns.
- Fish reside at spawning areas for 4-10 weeks and appear to acclimate to diurnal flow changes.
- Abrupt changes from steady flow to fluctuating flow may be harmful, so either constant steady flow or consistent fluctuating flow is considered acceptable.

**Recommendations**
- Observe spawning and egg incubation in riverine reaches, including by SCUBA as Mueller did below Hoover Dam, if spawning is found below Davis and Parker dams.
- Add work tasks to the five-year [[Four-Party Agreement]] studies (USGS-BRD, FWS, Reclamation and ASU) to assess the relationship between fluctuating flows, spawning and egg incubation.
- Reevaluate the risk of incidental take in 2003.

**Augmentation status (as of 1999)**
- More than 240,000 razorback suckers have been repatriated to Lake Mohave, and more than 12,000 to Lake Havasu.
- About 2,000 razorback suckers have been released below Parker Dam, with smaller bonytail releases.
- Radio and sonic telemetry studies continue below Davis and Parker dams.
