# Predicted and Observed Toxicities of Several Sewage Effluents to Rainbow Trout

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This two-part study by R. [[Lloyd]] and D. H. M. [[Herbert]] (Jordan in the source) tests whether the acute toxicity of [[Sewage Effluent]] to [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerii*) can be predicted from chemical analysis of a few common poisons. The authors summed the individual toxicities of [[Ammonia Toxicity]], [[Phenol Toxicity]], [[Cyanide Toxicity]], [[Zinc]] and [[Copper]] and compared the predicted [[Toxicity Index]] with bio-assay results. The 1961-62 survey covered 11 works (A-K); the 1962-63 survey covered 12 samples (G2, G3, M, N-V). Prediction agreed with observed toxicity within ±30% for about two-thirds of effluents. Under-prediction occurred at works with high industrial inputs, and over-prediction was linked to metal complexation and cyanide-metal interference in the analytical method.

## Background & Description

The authors' earlier field and laboratory work showed that fish in an effluent-receiving river were killed either by low [[Dissolved Oxygen]] alone or by low, sub-lethal oxygen combined with dissolved toxicants, with ammonia considered among the most important. Recent work showed that dissolved free [[Carbon Dioxide]] and oxygen increase ammonia toxicity, and that mixtures of poisons may act additively. The study therefore asked two questions:

1. How far can effluent toxicity be explained by [[Ammonia Toxicity]], [[Phenol Toxicity]], [[Heavy Metals]] and [[Cyanide Toxicity]]?
2. Do dissolved toxic substances in effluents pose a potential hazard to fisheries?

### Methods (1961-62 Survey)

- **Effluents:** Spot samples (100 gal) from 11 works (A-K), covering a range of treatment processes. Works A, B and F treated mainly domestic sewage; the others received mixed domestic and trade wastes. Samples were settled, filtered through glass wool and stored at 9°C or lower.
- **Test conditions:** Hard borehole water (320 p.p.m. as CaCO3), 10°C, dissolved oxygen at 50% of air saturation, pH 7.9 (with CO2 enrichment).
- **Fish:** Rainbow trout 3-5 in long, batches of 10 per dilution, tested for 3 days with daily solution changes. Controls showed no deaths.
- **Special case:** Effluent J was tested twice, as Ja (standard hard water) and Jb (soft water, 45 p.p.m. CaCO3, pH 7.2, fish acclimatised for 5 days).
- **Analysis:** Standard Ministry of Housing and Local Government methods for ammonia, [[Biochemical Oxygen Demand]], permanganate value, heavy metals and [[Synthetic Detergents]]; nitrite, monohydric phenols and free cyanide by specified colorimetric methods.

### Methods (1962-63 Survey)

- Twelve effluents collected November 1962 to March 1963. Several works were affected by abnormally cold weather, so samples may not represent typical quality.
- Effluent G was sampled three times (G, G2, G3) as gas liquor discharge increased. Effluent M came from a pilot-scale filter. Samples N-V came from works serving industrial areas.
- Test conditions matched the earlier survey, with average dissolved oxygen of 43-47% of saturation and mean pH of 7.73-7.95. Sodium bicarbonate was added to low-alkalinity Effluents U and V.
- Total phenols were measured, and Effluent O's free cyanide was assessed by two methods.

### Key Definitions

- **[[Lethal Threshold Concentration]]:** the concentration killing only 50% of test fish after prolonged exposure.
- **[[Median Tolerance Limit]] (TLm):** the concentration killing 50% of fish within a specified time.
- **[[Toxicity Index]]:** the number of dilutions needed to kill 50% of fish within a specified time. The 3-day Toxicity Index was the main metric.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper treats the river as a receiving environment. Dissolved oxygen below an outfall is lower than in unpolluted water, so the test held oxygen at 50% of saturation: well above the lethal level for trout, but low enough to affect the toxicity of effluent. Chalk-stream conditions (pH 7.5-8.0) were represented in the test water.

The authors state that the lowest-oxygen effects and effluent toxicants interact. This means effluent hazard depends on river chemistry as well as the discharge itself. Temperature was held at 10°C to slow biological breakdown during tests, but the toxicity of the poisons had been measured at 10-20°C, so corrections were made using a Q10 of 2.35 and oxygen-correction factors from earlier work.

## Key Findings

### Predictive method

- Predicted toxicity assumes the toxicity of a mixture equals the sum of the fractional toxicities of its components. The lethal threshold of a mixture is reached when the summed ratios of concentration to threshold equal unity.
- Ammonia thresholds were calculated for each test's average conditions. Phenol, cyanide, copper and zinc values came from published 3-day TLm data at air-saturated oxygen, corrected to 10°C.

### Results: first survey (11 effluents, A-K)

- The effluents covered a wide range of quality. Curves for Effluent H suggested a true lethal threshold, since its toxicity did not increase with longer exposure. Effluents D, J and K would likely have shown greater toxicity with longer exposure at lower concentrations.
- Observed 3-day Toxicity Indices included A 1.6, E 3.0, H 1.7, I 1.7, D 2.4, K 4.9, Jb 4.5 and Ja 15.6, with predicted values broadly similar in most cases. Predictions tended to underestimate toxicity slightly, suggesting unmeasured substances may have contributed.
- Predicted contributions varied greatly between effluents. For example, ammonia contributed about 86% of Effluent A's toxicity, and 68% in another works. Some effluents had large unexplained fractions.
- Effluent A had the highest detergent residue (9.7 p.p.m.), yet its toxicity was fully explained by ammonia, copper and zinc. The authors concluded that detergent residues contribute little, if any, to toxicity.
- Lead, nickel and chromium concentrations were too small to contribute significantly.
- Dilutions required to reduce effluents to a 3-day Toxicity Index of 0.5 were compared with B.O.D.-based dilutions. For two of 11 effluents (C, Ja) the B.O.D. estimate was lower, and for three others (A, F, K) the two estimates were similar. For the remaining six, B.O.D. implied much greater dilution than the bio-assay.

### Results: second survey (12 samples, G2-V)

- The most toxic effluent of the first survey required a 46-fold dilution to produce only 50% mortality in 3 days.
- Of 24 samples across both surveys, 6 were correctly predicted to be non-toxic, and 13 of the remaining 18 were predicted within ±30% of observed values.
- Four effluents (O, P, S, V) were poorly predicted. Two under-predictions exceeded 30% and came from works with high industrial inputs.
- Over-prediction was most marked in Effluent O, which had the highest copper content. Only about half the soluble copper in such effluents may be toxic, because part of it is complexed (following McDermott et al.).
- Effluent O's free cyanide was 0.08 p.p.m. HCN by the modified Epstein method, but 0.67 p.p.m. by neutral distillation. The distillation value was implausibly high and was likely caused by breakdown of copper and nickel cyanide complexes, which are relatively non-toxic to fish.

### Conclusions

- Chemical prediction gives a reasonable estimate of effluent toxicity for about two-thirds of effluents.
- Bio-assays are still necessary to determine with certainty whether an effluent is toxic. Paired chemical analysis of the bio-assay sample allows the relative importance of each poison, and the effects of changing effluent composition or river conditions, to be assessed.
- There was no correlation between B.O.D. and toxicity caused by dissolved poisons in either survey.
