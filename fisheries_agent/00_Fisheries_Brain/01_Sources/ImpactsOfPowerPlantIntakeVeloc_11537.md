# Impacts of Power Plant Intake Velocities on Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-entrainment

## Executive Summary

This Fish and Wildlife Service topical brief (FWS/OBS-76/204, March 1977), written by John Boreman of the [[Biological Services Program]]'s National Power Plant Team, describes how the velocity of water drawn into power plant cooling intakes drives the death of fish and other aquatic organisms through [[Entrainment]] and [[Impingement]]. The brief states that water withdrawal can sometimes cause greater fishery losses than thermal pollution. It reviews documented kills, explains why intake velocity measurements and predictions are unreliable, and sets out a strategy for environmental reports and [[316(b) Demonstration]] studies. It also summarizes current federal velocity guidance, notably a maximum approach velocity of about 0.5 fps. The brief is intended for fish and wildlife biologists who review electric power projects.

## Background & Description

The brief belongs to the Topical Briefs series published by the National Power Plant Team of the Power Plant Project, [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], U.S. Department of the Interior. The series gives concise background, discussion, bibliographies, and in some cases strategies for power-plant issues. The [[Biological Services Program]] was created to supply scientific information and methods on environmental issues affecting fish, wildlife, and their ecosystems, including power plants, and it supports decision makers through the [[U.S. Environmental Protection Agency]] and [[U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission]] review processes.

The brief's central concern is the intake structure, which withdraws large volumes of water. Its abstract identifies the biological problems associated with intake velocities and the information needed to assess impacts on fishery resources.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Scale of losses.** The brief compiles EPA-reported entrainment and impingement events. Entrainment records (Table 1) include:
- [[Brayton Point Plant]], Mount Hope Bay, Massachusetts: 7.0 to 165.5 million menhaden (and some river herring) killed per day in summer 1971, with a kill of 165.5 million on July 2 and 50 million fish killed over 11 days in August 1971.
- [[Millstone Plant]], Niantic Bay, Connecticut: 36 million fish killed over 16 days in November 1971 (probably menhaden and blueback herring), and 2.5 million flounder entrained in April-June 1972.
- Connecticut Yankee, Connecticut River: an estimated 179 million fish larvae killed per year (1969-1970).
- [[Indian Point]], Hudson Estuary, New York: a predicted 7.3 million striped bass larvae and juveniles killed per year.
- [[Seabrook Plant]], Hampton-Seabrook Estuary, New Hampshire: a predicted 74 million clam larvae killed per day at startup, with a lower rate after equilibrium.

Impingement records (Table 2) cover roughly 20 sites, including Galveston Bay, Texas (7,191,785 fish impinged in one year, 1969-70), Indian Point on the Hudson (kills of 1.0 to 1.5 million fish per year, with a predicted total of 6.5 million per year), [[Crystal River Plant]], Florida (a predicted 400,000 fish and 100,000 shellfish per year), Oyster Creek in Barnegat Bay, New Jersey (10,000 fish and 5,000 crabs destroyed per month in spring and summer 1971), and Surry Power Station on the James River, Virginia (6 million river herring destroyed in 2-3 months).

**Hydrodynamic setting.** The brief identifies three hydrodynamic conditions that control entrainment and impingement:
- Stagnant pool systems (lakes or ponds without prevailing currents)
- Unidirectional systems (streams, rivers, or coastal systems with one prevailing current)
- Multidirectional flow systems (estuaries)

**Intake design.** The location of the structure relative to shoreline, bottom, and surface affects how many organisms are withdrawn. Physical modifications such as [[Skimmer Walls]], submerged weirs, and [[Velocity Cap]]s can limit the zone of withdrawal, and screens set at an angle to the inflow may reduce impingement of small fish.

**Biological factors.** Vulnerability depends on:
1. Motility
2. Physiological and behavioral responses to temperature, salinity, oxygen, and currents
3. Vertical and horizontal distribution near the intake
4. Growth rate, which sets the period of vulnerability in each life stage

## Key Findings

**Problems in predicting and evaluating impacts**

- **Zone of water withdrawal:** No universally accepted prediction method exists. Three-dimensional velocity profile models linked to species movement are still in formulation, simple models omit key hydrodynamics, and dye or drogue studies do not reflect organism reactions near intakes.
- **Swimming speed is not enough:** Impingement and entrainment depend on more than the ability to hold position against a current. Temperature and salinity strongly influence impingement at estuarine intakes, and rheotaxis, phototaxis, and tactile responses also matter. Fish that hold in front of an intake can become entrained or impinged once they tire.
- **Burst-speed arguments:** Claims that a species can escape by a sudden burst of speed are seldom supportable, because orientation, distance, direction, and stimuli cannot be sufficiently documented.
- **Velocity measurements:** Reports often give an average intake velocity without the range across the structure. Some zones may be near zero while others are much higher. Low velocities are hard to measure accurately.
- **Stagnant pools:** Reservoirs or dredged areas built to keep pumps from running dry can create stagnant zones where sediment settles, organisms concentrate, and vulnerability increases.
- **Discharge proximity:** Fish and shellfish are often attracted to the discharge, so an intake placed too close to it can increase losses.

**Recommended strategy for entrainment**

Eggs and early larvae are the most vulnerable stages, and intake volume often matters more than intake velocity. Project reports should include:
- Species-specific vertical and horizontal distribution of eggs and larvae within the withdrawal zone
- Duration of egg and larval stages, spawning season length, and temperature-dependent development
- Tidal, current, and behavioral factors that raise vulnerability
- Water withdrawal relative to the water body's net flow
- Egg and larval population estimates
- Significance of losses to recreational and commercial fisheries

Recommended reduction methods are skimmer walls, velocity caps, submerged weirs, relocating the intake to less critical habitat, closed-cycle cooling, and seasonal operation of once-through cooling during low-impact periods. The brief stresses that the best way to minimize impact is to site plants where important resources are scarce.

**Recommended strategy for impingement**

Juvenile (post-larval) fish are much more vulnerable to impingement than other stages, though substantial adult impingement can also occur. Reports should include:
- Movement patterns of critical species through the withdrawal zone
- Physical and biological factors raising vulnerability (temperature, salinity, currents, behavior)
- Evidence that fish returned by [[Fish Bypass Systems]] survive, grow, and reproduce
- Numbers and sizes of impinged fish relative to water volume, velocity, season, temperature, tide, and illumination
- Impingeable stock estimates and fishery impact evaluation

Reduction methods include [[Travelling Screens]], fish bypass systems, louvers, and intake relocation. Air bubbler screens and sound waves have not been shown to be effective.

**Current policy**

- The EPA (1973) recommends intake velocities below 0.5 fps at the trash rack so fish can escape the screenwell.
- The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (1975, Regulatory Guide 4.7) recommends sites where important species are scarce and low approach velocities are attainable, with maximum acceptable [[Approach Velocity]] of about 0.5 fps. Acceptable velocities vary with species, site, and season.
- The Fish and Wildlife Service's Instructional Memorandum RB-44 (February 5, 1973) states that 0.5 fps protects most small fish, but lower velocities are needed to avoid entraining larvae and plankton unless an effective escape bypass is provided.

**Conclusions**

Closed-cycle cooling is not the best available technology in every case. Minor changes to existing open-cycle systems should be recommended whenever possible, and each system should be judged case by case. The brief directs readers to the [[Atomic Industrial Forum]] source book on cooling water intakes (Battelle 1975), the EPA cooling water intake development document (1976a), and the EPA 316(b) technical guidance manual (1976b) for detailed analyses.
