# White River Fishes Study: Fish Survey of the White River, Colorado and Utah (1981)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) study surveyed the fishes of the [[White River]] in Colorado and Utah during 1981, as part of a larger effort on the upper [[Colorado River Basin]]. The work was funded through a memorandum of understanding with the [[Bureau of Land Management]] (BLM). Its primary objectives were to evaluate the White River as habitat for endangered Colorado River fishes and to provide baseline data. The study used a standardized sampling program, a radiotelemetry study of [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], and a survey for young-of-the-year (YOY) Colorado squawfish. The survey found seven native species and several exotic species, and it confirmed that Colorado squawfish use the river throughout the year.

## Background & Description

- **Program origin:** The FWS began studying the mainstem fishes of the upper Colorado River Basin in July 1979. The program covered the [[Colorado River]] from Lake Powell to Palisade, Colorado, and the [[Green River]] from its confluence with the Colorado River up to Split Mountain Canyon in Dinosaur National Monument. In fall 1980 it expanded to include the [[Yampa River]] and White River.
- **Objectives:** The study had two main objectives: (1) evaluate the White River as habitat for endangered Colorado River fishes, and (2) provide baseline data.
- **Programs designed to meet these objectives:**
  1. A standardized sampling program for all fishes.
  2. A radiotelemetry program for Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*).
  3. A program to determine the presence, abundance, and distribution of YOY Colorado squawfish.
- **Study area:** The White River rises in the Flattop Mountains of western Colorado and flows uninterrupted for 402 km before joining the Green River near Ouray, Utah. The surveyed reach was 211 km long, from the White–Green confluence near Ouray to [[Rio Blanco Lake]], an off-stream reservoir in Colorado. Elevation ranges from 1,521 m above sea level at the mouth to 1,723 m at Rio Blanco Lake. Land use in the upper study area is agricultural (alfalfa, pasture, grain and corn). The lower reach is largely undeveloped, but some impacts come from energy development, and several water development projects were planned.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stratification and sampling design**

- The river was divided into five homogeneous sections (V, W, X, Y, Z), each 28–53 km long. These were identified by evaluating fishery habitat and included canyons, low-gradient braided sections, and one high-gradient, relatively clean-water section. Treating them as discrete strata made the samples representative of all habitats and reduced overall sampling variability.
- Sampling procedures followed Archer et al. (1980). Sample stations were randomly selected within each stratum using a table of random digits.
- At each 0.8 km station, all habitat types were sampled for 24 hours using:
  - [[Hoop Nets]] (1.6 × 1.8 m, 2.5 cm mesh)
  - Seines (1.0 × 1.8 m, 5.3 mm mesh)
  - [[Electrofishing]] gear (DC variable-voltage pulsator with a 240 V generator)
  - Wire traps (0.45 m diameter, 0.77 m long, 2.5 mm mesh)
- After the first station, electrofishing continued 7.2 km downstream, where a second 0.8 km station was sampled for another 24 hours.
- Stratum V was only 29 km long, so electrofishing effort there was halved, and the second station was sampled only 3.2 km downstream.

**Data management**

- Data were stored in the [[Manage Database]] system, developed by the Western Energy and Land Use Team (WELUT).
- Analysis capabilities were added to MANAGE through [[Statistical Analysis Software]] (SAS) routines, which allowed ANOVA and regression analysis.

**Habitat and distribution notes**

- Adult Colorado squawfish were mainly collected from eddies and shoreline habitat. Their substrate at collection points was dominated by sand (55%) and silt-clay.
- Juveniles were found in slower-moving waters, mainly backwaters and eddies.
- Relative abundance (catch per unit effort) varied with life stage and stratum, reflecting habitat preference and availability.
- Strata V, W, and X had higher percentages of native species than the other strata. The reason is unknown. Habitat differences may favor native species over exotics.

## Key Findings

**Overall catch**

- Across 1980 and 1981, the team collected a reported 1,560 fishes during standardized sampling (the total is partially illegible in the scanned text, so verify against the source). An additional 10,000 fishes were collected during 1980 supplemental surveys. Larval sampling in 1981 yielded 65 fish.
- Table 1 lists total sampling effort by gear type and stratum. Gear types were seines, electrofishing, trammel nets, and wire traps.

**Species present**

- Seven native species were present in the White River. The scanned species names are partly garbled, but the legible native taxa include:
  - Bluehead sucker (*Catostomus discobolus*)
  - Flannelmouth sucker (*Catostomus latipinnis*)
  - Colorado pikeminnow (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
  - A speckled dace-type cyprinid (*Rhinichthys* sp., partly illegible)
- One species was uncommon but found throughout the study area.
- Exotic species included *Cyprinus carpio* (carp), *Ictalurus punctatus* (channel catfish), and *Notropis lutrensis* (red shiner). Carp, channel catfish, and red shiner were the most abundant and widely distributed exotics.
- Among native species, a single cyprinid was the most common fish in the collections. Figure 2 shows total fish captures by stratum and age group.

**Native versus exotic composition**

- Strata V, W, and X had higher percentages of native species than the other strata. Values in the scanned text are partially garbled, but they appear to be roughly 90% or more.

**Colorado squawfish**

- A total of 58 Colorado squawfish were collected in all samples during 1980–81. The scanned counts indicate 39 adults and 19 juveniles, but these figures are partly illegible.
- Colorado squawfish were collected from all five strata. Stratum counts were V = 1, W = 5, X = 8, and the remaining strata were partly illegible.
- Colorado squawfish made up less than 1% of all fish collected during this study. Colorado squawfish over 500 mm TL also made up less than 1% of fish collected during standardized sampling in both the Green River and Yampa River.
- Larval sampling was conducted in each stratum from 22 to 29 July 1981. No Colorado squawfish larvae were collected. The authors attribute this to low flow conditions in 1981 and a possible drift mechanism; the results are not fully analyzed.

**Radiotelemetry**

- In 1980, researchers reported the movement of a radio-tagged Colorado squawfish between the Green and White rivers.
- In 1981, nine adult Colorado squawfish were captured and radio-tagged in the White River.
- Two fish were tracked for 4 and 5 months. One was relatively sedentary, and the other was highly mobile. The mobile fish moved from the White River into an adjacent river and later returned. Contact was lost in early October.

**Conclusions and recommendations**

- The data indicate that Colorado squawfish use a major portion of the White River for considerable periods of time. They can move both upstream and downstream over relatively long distances.
- This paper summarizes only part of the findings. The authors recommend consulting the full White River report (Hus and Harper 1982) for more detail.
- The authors recommend combining data from the Green, Yampa, and White River studies before drawing meaningful conclusions about endangered species in any sub-basin.
