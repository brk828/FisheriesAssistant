# Farm Fishpond Construction and Management in Alabama

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Alabama Polytechnic Institute Extension circular, written by [[Earl Franklin Kennamer]] (Fish and Wildlife Specialist), is a practical guide for farmers who want to build, stock, fertilize, and manage a farm pond for food, recreation, and income. It covers site selection, watershed and water supply design, earthen dam engineering, drainage and spillways, and the biological management of a [[Balanced Fish Population]] of [[Bluegill]], [[Shellcracker]], and [[Largemouth Bass]]. It also explains control of muddy water, weeds, algae, and predators, and how to run a fee-fishing ("pay pond") operation or sell fish legally.

## Background & Description

The circular frames the farm pond as a multi-purpose asset. Farmer records cited in the text suggest that each acre of a well-managed pond can yield:

- 100 to 150 pounds of good food
- 300 hours of healthful sport and relaxation
- $50 to $300 in fishing-rights income
- $60 to $80 if fish are marketed
- an increase in farm value

Construction guidance is detailed and quantitative. Key design rules include:

- **Watershed:** about 15 acres of pasture or cover-crop watershed, or 30 acres of timbered watershed, per acre of pond water. Heavy clay soils may need half that; very sandy soils may need double.
- **Pond size:** a 1-acre pond can support family fishing, but a 3- to 5-acre or larger pond is considered most satisfactory.
- **Depth:** most water should exceed 3 feet, with the deepest point (at the dam base) 7 to 12 feet in a small pond.
- **Dam:** an earthen dam with a central clay core wall and two [[Brace Slope]] sides built at a 2-to-1 slope, with a core trench of at least 10 feet in heavy clay.
- **Freeboard:** 2 feet for ponds of 3 acres or less, 3 feet for larger ponds.
- **Drain pipe:** 4 inches for small ponds, 6 to 12 inches for large ponds. Recommended materials are asbestos-cement, galvanized steel, and cast iron. Cement and terra cotta are not recommended.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The circular is built on the principle that plankton drives fish production. [[Plankton]] feeds water insects and other organisms, which in turn feed bluegills and shellcrackers, which feed bass. Several limiting factors are discussed:

- **Site contamination:** eroded or row-cropped slopes muddy the water. Chemicals applied to nearby crops can wash in and kill fish. Shale, limestone, and deep sandy soils hold water poorly. Flood-prone areas can admit "trash" fish.
- **Water chemistry:** groundwater from springs is often saturated with [[Carbon Dioxide]] and iron and has little oxygen. Aerating it over gravel or step falls drives off carbon dioxide and adds oxygen.
- **Oxygen stratification:** in summer, oxygen concentrates in the upper water layer. Bluegills may suffocate deeper down, so the best summer fishing is in the top 4 feet. In fall, surface water cools, sinks, and mixes oxygen throughout the pond.
- **Turbidity:** silt and colloidal clay block sunlight needed by plankton and can cover eggs.
- **Weeds and algae:** shallow water and clear water both favor weed growth. Above-water weeds include cattails and water lilies. Underwater weeds include [[Chara]] (muskgrass). Floating algae come in "winter" and "summer" types.
- **Predators and nuisances:** [[Muskrat]] and [[Beaver]] burrowing can damage dams. [[Crayfish]] build burrows. Water snakes and turtles are pests but cause little damage to fish stocks. Herons and kingfishers are protected by law.

## Key Findings

**1. Site and watershed design**
- Choose a site with the greatest water area and the shortest dam, away from eroded slopes, row crops, shale, limestone, and flood zones.
- A check pond above a fishpond can trap silt on steep watersheds.
- A float valve on a feed pipe through a check-pond dam can regulate inflow automatically.
- Two small ponds on separate watersheds are often better than one large pond, and low dams are cheaper than high ones.

**2. Dam construction**
- Clear and burn trees and shrubs in the basin. Leave shade trees whose trunks are at least 5 feet from the shoreline. Slope the shore so the water is 2 to 3 feet deep around the edge, which reduces weed growth.
- Build the dam in 6-inch layers and pack each well to reduce settling.
- Dig the core trench 3 feet or more deep, down to hardpan, and fill it with sticky, tough clay.
- Put topsoil only on the back brace slope. Do not plant trees on the dam.

**3. Spillways**
- An overflow pipe at normal water level, with a concrete trough on the rear slope, handles constant outflow.
- The emergency spillway floor should sit 4 inches above the overflow pipe. Its minimum width is 10 feet, plus 0.5 foot for each watershed acre. Examples given: 100 acres = 82 feet; 200 acres = 146 feet; 300 acres = 172 feet; 250 acres = 159 feet by interpolation.
- Overflow depth should stay under 6 inches. Deeper flow calls for widening.

**4. Seepage and drainage**
- Seepage is treated with [[Bentonite]] at 1 to 2 pounds per square foot of dam surface, or with soda ash at 5 to 25 pounds per 100 square feet where clay content is 25% or more.
- A pond can be drained in a few weeks. Doubling pipe diameter more than quadruples flow.
- Gate valves placed below the dam are convenient but stay under pressure. A 90° elbow with a screw-in brass plug is an inexpensive alternative.

**5. Stocking**
- Stock only bluegills, shellcrackers, and largemouth bass.
- Fertilized ponds: about 1000 bream and 100 bass per acre.
- Unfertilized ponds: no more than 500 bream and 50 bass per acre. Overstocking causes failure from the start.
- Close the drain and poison the water a month before stocking, using [[Rotenone]] from derris or cube root. Poison is effective only in warm weather. It breaks down within 10 days to 2 weeks, and in cold weather the water may stay unsuitable for weeks.
- Gambusia minnows are harmless and serve as bass food.

**6. Fertilization**
- Fertilized ponds can yield 100 to 150 or more pounds of table-size fish per acre per year.
- Typical rates per surface acre: 100 lb of 8-8-2, or 40 lb of 20-20-5, or equivalent combinations of 0-16-8 with ammonium nitrate or nitrate of soda.
- Begin in early February or March and fertilize through October, about 8 to 10 or more times per year.
- Stop fertilizing when the hand is no longer visible at elbow depth, or every 3 to 4 weeks if mineral coloration obscures the view.
- Do not fertilize muddy, weed-infested, lightly fished, or overcrowded bluegill ponds.

**7. Water quality management**
- For turbid water from colloidal clay, apply gypsum at 15 to 35 pounds per 1000 cubic feet of water. It does not appreciably change pH.
- For "milky" water, apply cottonseed meal and superphosphate in place of regular fertilizer until the water clears.

**8. Weed and algae control**
- Remove young above-water weeds by hand. Established weeds can be cut below the surface or controlled with [[2,4-D]] ester spray (1 to 2 teacups per 5 gallons of water, with detergent) on sunny, still days.
- Winter fertilization (starting January 1 to 15, then weekly) can smother underwater weeds with algae.
- [[Sodium Arsenite]] treats underwater weeds: 1 gallon of 4-pound strength per 16,000 cubic feet of water for whole-pond treatment, or 1 gallon per 6,500 to 8,500 cubic feet for shoreline patches. Treat only one-third to one-half of the pond at a time. It is extremely hazardous, and strict precautions apply.
- Floating winter algae are treated with bluestone (copper sulfate) at 2 to 3 pounds per acre-foot, applied to one-fifth to one-fourth of the pond at weekly intervals.
- Summer algae are treated with sodium arsenite or with rosin compounds.

**9. Pond balance and corrective action**
- A balanced pond has bass able to keep bluegill reproduction in check, with few bluegills the size of bait minnows.
- Overcrowded bluegills are reduced by seining the shoreline every 4 to 5 days or by partial poisoning. Too few bass calls for stocking 50 to 100 bass fry or fingerlings per acre.
- Bass that are thin with an overcrowded bluegill population indicate too many bass in a new pond. Remove bass by fishing.
- Ponds infested with shad, catfish, or other undesirables should be drained and repoisoned at 3 to 5 pounds of 5% rotenone per acre-foot.

**10. Fishing and marketing**
- Bass should not be fished until after spawning, to avoid overpopulating bluegills.
- Bass weighing 1 pound or less should be released. Large bass should be removed when possible.
- You cannot catch more than about 60% of the fish in a pond, no matter how much you fish it.
- Pay ponds of 3 acres or more can earn fees. Set a low bass limit and a more generous bream limit, and provide life preservers and safe boats.
- Fish can be marketed only after registering the pond with the State Conservation Department.

**11. Biological and seasonal behavior**
- Bluegills spawn heavily from late spring to early summer, at water temperatures of 80° to 85°F, and stop when the water falls below 80°F in the fall.
- Bass become active when water reaches 50° to 55°F. Spring is the best season to catch them.
- A rising barometer indicates good fishing weather, but does not guarantee results.

**12. Ongoing practices and emerging ideas**
- Lowering the water level a few feet in fall may reduce overpopulated bluegills. Lowering it in spring or hot summer risks oxygen loss and fish kills.
- Black plastic strips on the shallow bottom may shade out underwater weeds.
- Fatty alcohols placed on the water can reduce evaporation by 50 to 70%, where evaporation loses up to 10 acre-feet per year.
- Plastic sheeting can stop seepage in porous soils.
