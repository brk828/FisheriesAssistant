# Differential Responses of Native and Non-Native Fishes to Flooding in the Arid Southwest

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Poeciliidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, by Minckley and colleagues (Arizona State University and the Savannah River Ecology Laboratory), examines how flash flooding structures fish communities in the arid mountainous streams of Arizona and western New Mexico. Non-native fishes introduced into unregulated streams are often unable to withstand floods and are reduced or destroyed by them, whereas native fishes show little response and their assemblages remain similar before and after major floods. The authors attribute this differential response to evolutionary history: native fauna have long been exposed to flash floods in constrained channels, while introduced species that evolved in mesic lowlands generally have not. Regulation of rivers by dams removes this flood disturbance, allowing introduced species to dominate, and the authors recommend that managers of endangered native fauna preserve natural flood regimes rather than control discharge.

## Background & Description

- **Regional fauna:** The native fish fauna of western North America is depauperate and unique. The arid Southwest contains only about 37 recognized native species and subspecies in Arizona and New Mexico west of the Continental Divide, and 14 of these are endemic. Many are relicts of ancient genera.
- **Persistence traits:** Surviving native species are described as (1) generalists with traits that promote persistence, (2) specialists of long-existing habitats such as thermal springs or highly erosive streams, or (3) species preadapted to extreme temperature or salinity.
- **Extinction pressure:** At least four species and six subspecies in six genera have become extinct in the last 40 years through human disturbance. Of 67 U.S. fishes listed as threatened or endangered, 45 (67%) live in arid zones.
- **Introduced species:** More than 140 fish species have been established outside their native ranges in North America, through sport stocking, forage, biological control, aquaculture, the aquarium trade, and accident. Some southwestern species originated from the Mississippi basin or other mesic lowland drainages, while others are exotics from Eurasia, Central America, and Africa. Introduced species are less restricted by natural barriers because of human dispersal.
- **Community transformation:** Southwestern stream fish communities are being transformed from native faunas to mixed native–non-native assemblages, or in some cases to entirely introduced assemblages.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flood hydrology of arid lands.** Southwestern watersheds are geologically active, mountainous, sparsely vegetated, and steep-walled. Summer monsoon storms can increase discharge by three or more orders of magnitude within seconds to minutes. Flash floods are followed by high, variable flows that subside within hours, and channels are transformed almost instantly from dry or intermittent streams to torrents. Winter floods are rarer and typically require unusual magnitude, such as rain on snowpack.

**Contrast with mesic lowlands.** Mesic watersheds have low relief, deep soils, dense vegetation, meandering channels, and broad floodplains. Floods there are cumulative and slow, with floodplain river lakes serving as refugia for fishes.

**Stream gradients and relief.** Southwestern stream gradients are one to two orders of magnitude greater than those of mesic lowlands (Table 1), with almost no floodplain in canyon-bound reaches. Discharge in Arizona streams (for example Santa Cruz River and Gila River) was highly concentrated in brief periods: the upper 5% of discharge days in arid systems yielded a much larger share of annual water yield than in the Suwannee and Santa Fe rivers, which had more even discharge distributions (Figs. 2–3).

**Physical and chemical effects.** Floods remove terraces, transport boulders and large organic debris, cause sudden temperature drops of 15 °C or more, and produce rapid fluctuations in other physical and chemical parameters.

**Selective pressures.** Mesic fishes can typically escape high flows by moving into floodplains or to slower downstream habitats. In arid canyon-bound systems, fishes cannot escape by moving onto floodplains, and downstream displacement often strands them in desiccating channels or terminal alluvial deposits. Native fishes also concentrate in canyons in summer, presumably to avoid elevated water temperatures in shallow unshaded reaches. Morphological adaptations (depressed heads, streamlined bodies, reduced scales, and narrow caudal peduncles) in native cyprinids and catostomids are interpreted as aids to swimming in fast, turbulent flows.

**Species replacement patterns.** Environmental extremes often exclude introduced fishes. For example, temperatures above 36 °C and dissolved oxygen below 1.0 mg/l in some Nevada springs exclude non-native fishes but are tolerated by endemic [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Spring Fish]] (*Crenichthys baileyi*). [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*) survived extreme salinity and temperature until introduced [[Western Mosquitofish]] and exotic [[Cichlids]] arrived, and the species has nearly disappeared and is proposed for endangered listing.

**Dams and impoundments.** Regulation of the Colorado River and other western rivers reduces flood habitats and favors introduced groups such as clupeids, salmonids, cyprinids, ictalurids, poeciliids, centrarchids, and cichlids. Native species such as [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] persisted for up to 30 years in lower Colorado reservoirs without successful recruitment before declining to local extinction.

## Key Findings

1. **Native fishes persist through floods; non-native fishes do not.** In canyon-bound reaches of unregulated streams, fish faunas shifted from a mixture of native and non-native species to predominantly native assemblages after large floods. Non-native species were reduced or removed.

2. **Flood magnitude determines the effect.** Floods approaching or exceeding two orders of magnitude above mean discharge depleted or removed non-natives. Intermediate floods (about one order of magnitude) depleted but rarely destroyed non-native populations. Floods less than one order of magnitude above mean discharge had no discernible effect. Declines in non-natives with increasing flood magnitude were highly significant (Fig. 5); native species showed no comparable relationship.

3. **Species-specific responses:**
   - [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) were substantially reduced by high-intensity floods.
   - [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) was depleted but rapidly recolonized from protected habitats.
   - [[Western Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) was decimated but recovered rapidly, especially where survivors were present or reintroduced.
   - [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], and [[Bluegill]] appeared highly susceptible, though smallmouth bass and green sunfish are partly stream-adapted and usually reappeared within a few weeks.
   - [[Exotic Cichlids]] introduced to unregulated streams invariably disappeared.
   - [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*) was depleted by large floods, but as a stream-adapted species it is expanding its range.

4. **Native losses were uncommon but occurred.** Depletions of native fishes were most frequent in the most constrained streams. In Sycamore Creek a large flood destroyed young and reduced adults of [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*). A flood in Eagle Creek in 1984–85 numerically reduced the entire native fauna of six species. A very large flood in Aravaipa Creek in 1993 (about 2,000 times mean discharge) had no discernible effect on seven native species.

5. **Dams change the outcome.** In regulated systems with controlled releases, non-native faunas showed little change, while emergency releases that approached flash-flood magnitude removed most non-natives from canyon-bound reaches of the Salt and Verde rivers, leaving native [[Catostomids]] and a few [[Longfin Dace]].

6. **Coexistence case study (Sharp Spring, Arizona).** Native Sonoran topminnow (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*) and mosquitofish coexisted. After floods, mosquitofish declined by more than 90% while topminnow suffered only about a 15% decline. Thousands of topminnows were found stranded in pools downstream, and most mosquitofish collected downstream were transported from habitat. In a laboratory flume with surging flows, topminnow maintained position by swimming against currents, a behavior that appeared genetic, while mosquitofish, especially young fish, failed.

7. **Distributional patterns.** Native fishes persist in substantial numbers in streams that are distant from population centers, protected by Indian reservations or other reserves, or located in rugged terrain.

8. **Flood-driven dynamic equilibrium.** The authors propose that regular, strong floods hold non-native fishes at low levels, allowing native species to recover between events, creating a dynamic equilibrium (Fig. 7). If floods are curtailed by damming, non-native fishes typically increase toward nearly 100% of the fauna.

9. **Management implication.** Maintaining native fish faunas requires natural flood regimes. Instream flow techniques developed mainly for salmonids may not apply well to warmwater arid-land fishes. The authors recommend that managers of endangered native faunas define and perpetuate naturalness in southwestern stream habitats rather than attempt to improve on nature through discharge control.

**Conclusion:** Declines in native western fish faunas are linked to flow regulation and to introduced species, but the evolutionary history of native fishes in flood-prone systems explains why they resist floods that eliminate lowland introduced species, and why flood removal favors invasion.
