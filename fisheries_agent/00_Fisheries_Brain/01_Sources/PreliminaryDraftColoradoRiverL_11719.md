# Lower Colorado River Basin Indigenous Big-River Fishes Management Prospectus

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #recovery-planning #colorado-river

## Executive Summary

This preliminary draft management prospectus, submitted by the Arizona Fishery Resources Office to the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service Southwest Regional Director in February 1995, outlines a comprehensive fisheries management plan for four federally endangered "big-river" fish species in the Lower Colorado River Basin: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). The document addresses the dramatic alteration of the Colorado River ecosystem through dam construction, water diversion, and introduction of non-native species, which have driven these large, long-lived native fishes to the brink of extinction. The plan proposes establishment of refugia populations, genetic management protocols, captive propagation facilities, habitat restoration, and coordinated management actions to prevent extinction and eventually achieve recovery and delisting of these species.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] historically was a river of extremes, flowing from Rocky Mountain headwaters at 4000 meters elevation through the hottest, driest North American deserts to the Gulf of California. The basin encompasses 650,000 square kilometers, draining portions of seven U.S. states plus northwestern Mexico. Historically characterized by spring snowmelt floods followed by warm, reduced summer flows, the river alternated between slow-moving turbid valley reaches and turbulent canyon rapids with some of North America's largest whitewater.

Under these extreme conditions evolved four of the world's largest and most unusual freshwater fishes, referred to as "big-river" species. All four attain large sizes and great ages. The [[Colorado Squawfish]] developed as an apex predator with large head, raptorial pharyngeal teeth, and adaptations for near-shore ambush predation. All four species are now federally listed as endangered, with three having designated critical habitat downstream from [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Colorado squawfish was extirpated from the lower basin in the 1960s.

The Colorado River now comprises one of North America's most altered aquatic ecosystems. Hydroelectric power generation, irrigation, and livestock grazing have fundamentally changed watershed ecology. For management purposes, the watershed is divided into upper and lower basins separated near Glen Canyon Dam, Arizona. The lower basin includes the Colorado mainstem and the Paria, Little Colorado, Virgin, Bill Williams, and Gila River sub-basins and tributaries across Arizona, California, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, Sonora, and Baja California Norte.

Species declines are attributed to habitat loss and modification combined with negative interactions with introduced non-indigenous species. Dams have blocked historic movement corridors, released water too cold for spawning, created long dry reaches, and removed innate hydrologic variability from the system. Predation on eggs, fry, and juveniles by non-indigenous fishes has been identified as a major depletion factor.

**Current Status by Species:**

- **Colorado Squawfish**: No designated critical habitat in lower basin; represented only by captive broodstock at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and "experimental, nonessential" populations in Salt and Verde Rivers that cannot directly contribute to recovery under the Endangered Species Act
- **Bonytail**: Occurs infrequently in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]; extreme rarity requires emphasis on germplasm and broodfish acquisition; captive broodstock maintained at Dexter and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]
- **Humpback Chub**: Occurs in Colorado River and lower Little Colorado River; far more common than bonytail; management partially addressed by Grand Canyon Protection Act environmental studies and Glen Canyon Dam biological opinion
- **Razorback Sucker**: Largest population in Lake Mohave; undetermined population in [[Lake Mead]]; individuals captured occasionally in Grand Canyon area and downstream from Davis Dam; rapidly becoming rare

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The four big-river fishes evolved specialized adaptations to the Colorado River's extreme hydrologic regime. Prey species developed high reproductive potentials, rapid growth, hydrodynamic body shapes, falcate fins, and powerful musculature to avoid predation. The Colorado squawfish evolved slow growth to large size, initially exploiting slower-growing individuals and later consuming large fish.

Torrential floods alternating with prolonged drought represented hydrologic conditions to which indigenous taxa were adapted. Modern dam operations have fundamentally altered this regime. Dams create long dry reaches when closed for reservoir filling, block movement corridors, release water too cold for spawning, and most significantly, remove innate system variability. These subtle changes may represent the most significant impact on native fish populations.

The lower basin differs substantially from the upper basin in having fewer and less extensive free-flowing river reaches. However, the lower basin contains larger population sizes of bonytail, humpback chub, and razorback sucker than elsewhere. Development is relatively stabilized with most dam sites already utilized and few new projects anticipated, providing a stable planning foundation.

Non-indigenous species interactions represent a critical threat. Predation on young life stages by introduced fishes has been repeatedly identified as a major depletion factor across multiple studies. The management plan must address recruitment failure as a central challenge.

## Key Findings

**Management Goals and Objectives:**

The prospectus establishes a hierarchical framework of management levels:
1. **Prevent Extinction**: Secure reproductive adults and confirm survival of young to maturity
2. **Perpetuate Genetic Variability**: Plan and implement genetic and broodstock management
3. **Stabilize Populations**: Develop habitats of sufficient physical, chemical, and biological quality
4. **Expand Populations**: Restore natural habitats and dispersal corridors
5. **Self-Sustaining Populations**: Promote natural recruitment and dispersal to expand range
6. **Recovery**: Down-list or de-list big-river fishes

**Genetic Management Plan:**

Genetic management must maximize and conserve variability in founder stocks and offspring. Key approaches include:
- Maximize numbers of individuals contributing to recruitment; for razorback suckers, collect wild-caught larvae using phototactic response at different times and places during extended breeding season in Lake Mohave
- Base artificial propagation on strict pedigree with measured gamete contributions from individuals with known genetic attributes, or statistical breeding regimes ensuring all broodstock alleles are represented in progeny
- Experimental isolation of embayments with removal of non-indigenous fishes and stocking of wild breeding fish for natural reproduction
- Broodstock management emphasizing maximal use of wild individuals, infusion of wild fish with F1 "domestic" broodfish to minimize inbreeding, and strict pedigree application when necessary
- Future actions include cryopreservation germplasm banking, characterization of gamete donors to verify non-hybrid status, and genomic evaluation to verify lack of hybridization contamination

**Refugia Development:**

**Riverine Cove Refugia** (building on methods developed for Lake Mohave razorback suckers):
- Manage selected river coves as protected habitats on [[Lower Colorado River National Wildlife Refuges]] including Bill Williams River, Cibola, Havasu, and Imperial NWRs
- Create fish exclusion barriers and remove resident fishes from designated critical habitat coves
- Identify habitats that can be isolated with gravel berms or fine mesh barriers, are amenable to piscicide treatment, and suitable for secured growth (typically less than 5.0 ha surface area)
- Remove all resident fishes and stock one or more of the four species within 3 years, beginning on NWRs
- When stocked fish reach approximately 300 mm total length, use them to augment existing populations, re-introduce elsewhere, and maintain as gene pool reserves
- Periodic renovations needed to control competing fishes; repeated stockings likely necessary

**Large Isolated Habitat Refugia** (80 ha or larger):
- Plan and implement creation and stocking of large isolated habitats on NWRs within 5 years, compatible with refuge management
- Includes renovation of existing water bodies and construction of new habitats using ecosystem concepts including waterfowl, riparian, and neotropical bird habitat enhancement
- Modify habitats to encourage reproduction and population development as sources for re-introduction
- Expand within 10 years to other federal lands and recovery areas as agreements develop, especially for humpback chubs and Gila River basin habitats
- Exploit uncommon opportunities when major reservoirs drain for maintenance or drought, stocking coincidentally with refilling to take advantage of predator absence, rapid growth potential, and expanded gene pool for natural selection

**Propagation Facilities:**

- **[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]**: Currently holds broodstock of bonytail, razorback sucker, and Colorado squawfish; should develop captive humpback chub population; expand production capabilities for all four species; continue genetic and biological research support
- **[[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]**: Currently rearing razorback suckers with bonytail refugia population; maintain broodstock, serve as refugia, develop and increase production for all four species
- **[[Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery]]**: Potential facility to assist Dexter; could maintain broodstock, serve as refugia, and develop production capabilities

**Fish Health and Monitoring:**

Natural and semi-natural habitats must be examined for health-related problems by examining resident fishes prior to renovation and stocking. Health of indigenous fish in hatcheries and stocked populations should be monitored annually and prior to site-to-site transfers to minimize disease transmission.

**Re-introductions and Range Expansion:**

- Modify designation of Colorado squawfish populations in Salt and Verde Rivers from "experimental, nonessential" to "experimental, essential" so they may be included in recovery planning along with associated habitat
- Create additional populations in lower Colorado River basin
- Expand to other designated critical habitats, public lands, and elsewhere contingent on cooperative agreements
- Restoration of free-flowing river areas in Salt, Gila, Verde, Bill Williams, and Colorado Rivers have potential for development

**Research Needs:**

- Survey habitats to determine refugia population status and river populations
- Conduct life history evaluations related to competitors and predators to develop methodology ensuring survival of stocked fishes
- Study controlled water release effects (hydrographs) on target and non-target fishes
- Locate suitable habitats for additional population refuges
- Develop standardized, centralized database for stocking and capture information, compatible with Upper Colorado River databases and Arizona Heritage Data Management System

**Management Team and Coordination:**

Establish management team and coordinator to:
- Make fisheries management recommendations furthering recovery
- Consolidate best available information on population stabilization and perpetuation
- Develop cooperative ventures among stakeholders; coordinate with upper basin representatives
- Develop, revise, and modify plan objectives and actions during implementation
- Work closely with USFWS ecosystem representatives across species range
- Include scientists and managers from Federal, State, and private organizations chosen for species knowledge, ecosystem expertise, interrelated knowledge (hydrology), and understanding of user needs

**Partnerships identified** include Federal agencies (Bureau of Reclamation, Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Indian Affairs, Fish & Wildlife Service divisions, Forest Service, National Park Service), State agencies (Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada, California game and fish departments), Indian tribes (White Mountain Apache, San Carlos Apache, Hualapai, Hopi, Navajo, Colorado River Tribes, others), academia (Arizona State University, Northern Arizona University, University of Arizona, others), and non-governmental organizations (American Rivers, Desert Fishes Council, The Nature Conservancy, Sierra Club, others).

**Evaluation:**

All management actions will be closely tracked, monitored, and evaluated as integral components of all study plans and activities. The Arizona Fisheries Resources Office (lead station) will approve all endangered big-river fishes study protocols prior to implementation as required for permitting.

The plan differs from existing upper basin recovery plans due to larger lower basin population sizes, stabilization in development with few new projects anticipated, and potential inclusion of independent basins draining into Mexico in regional ecosystem planning. The prospectus demonstrates flexibility and adaptability, advocating ecosystem-level habitat improvement, development, and circumvention of recruitment failure as principal recovery means.
