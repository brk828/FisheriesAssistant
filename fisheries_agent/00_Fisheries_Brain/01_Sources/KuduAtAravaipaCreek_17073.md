# Niches for Alien Animals: Pleistocene Extinction and Exotic Large Herbivores in the American Southwest

#mammalogy #paleoecology #megafauna #ichthyology

## Executive Summary

This document combines a 1970 [[Paul S. Martin]] letter to [[Huey D. Johnson]] of [[The Nature Conservancy]] with a reprint of Martin's paper "Niches for Alien Animals" (*BioScience*, vol. 20, no. 4, February 15, 1970). Both argue that large herbivores lost in the [[Pleistocene Megafauna Extinction]] left unfilled ecological niches in the arid American Southwest and northern Mexico. Martin proposes controlled introduction of African or Asian large mammals, such as [[Greater Kudu]] and [[Giant Eland]], as a range-animal alternative to [[Bos]] cattle, framed as "conservation through introduction." The letter also addresses a fish-related concern: any introduction of exotic fish, such as [[Mosquito Fish]] or bait fish, must be prevented in [[Arivaipa Creek]].

## Background & Description

Martin, a paleoecologist at the University of Arizona's Department of Geosciences, writes from the perspective of the fossil record. His central claim is that many "native" American mammals are themselves Old World immigrants. Deer, buffalo, moose and mountain sheep arrived over the [[Bering Land Bridge]] within roughly the last 100,000 years, while horses and camels had much older American lineages.

Key points from the paper:

- **Extinction scope:** Late Pleistocene extinction eliminated mainly large terrestrial mammals. Martin states that the phenomenon did not leave a mark on the marine or terrestrial plant fossil record.
- **Timing:** Extinction began in Africa and Southeast Asia roughly 50,000 years ago, reached the Americas later, and swept the larger oceanic islands (New Zealand and Madagascar) within the last 1,000 years.
- **Niche vacancy:** Large herbivores were not replaced by new species or by extensive immigration, leaving "empty niches."
- **Survivorship:** Survivors (deer, antelope, wapiti, musk oxen, caribou, moose, pronghorn, mountain goats, mountain sheep, bison) represent only about 30% of the earlier big-game fauna.

The letter's core argument is that mosquito fish and kudu are not equivalent threats. Martin notes that the historical damage done by exotic fish does not automatically justify rejecting large mammal introductions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Pleistocene Abundance and Range Capacity

Martin argues that the Pleistocene fauna was abundant. He cites African biomass figures (Table 1) to infer the former richness of North American megafauna. He estimates that the Pleistocene game-carrying capacity of western North America "must have equaled, and very likely exceeded" the roughly 40 million livestock units it supports today (Stoddart and Smith, 1950).

Table 1 summarizes biomass in animal units per square kilometer or square mile across 12 localities. Examples include:

- Lake Manyara, Tanzania (elephant-buffalo): 125 animal units per sq mi
- Ngorongoro Crater, Tanzania (zebra-wildebeest): 35 per sq mi
- Serengeti Plains, Tanzania (zebra-wildebeest): 29 per sq mi
- Wyoming Bison Range: 20 per sq mi
- Cochise County, Arizona, cattle on mesquite-creosote-bush: 7 per sq mi

### Competition and Immigrant Impacts

Martin reviews the record of immigrant mammals. [[Rancholabrean]] invaders from Eurasia replaced few native species. The grizzly bear group (*Ursus*) is cited as replacing the short-faced bears (*Arctodus*). Massive late extinction struck Eurasian newcomers such as *Symbos* and *Cervalces*, as well as Pliocene relicts such as peccaries and mastodonts.

[[Bighorn Sheep]] (*Ovis*) are described as a late Eurasian immigrant that coexisted with native horses, camels, ground sloths, mammoths and predators. Feral burros (*Equus hemionus*), numbering from 500 to 15,000 according to McKnight (1958), compete with sheep in Death Valley and along the Colorado River. Martin questions why the mountain sheep should count as more "native" than the burro.

Camels offer another example. Martin notes that the camel's American lineage is far older than that of the bison. Army-introduced camels in the 1850s fed on mesquite, greasewood, cactus and sagebrush.

### Vegetation and Browse Plants

Martin argues that southwestern desert vegetation was shaped by Pleistocene browsers:

- **Creosote bush** (*Larrea tridentata*) dominates shrub communities and is largely untouched by cattle. Chew and Chew (1965) found about 1,200 pounds of dry matter per acre per year in a desert shrub community in Cochise County. Martin cites roughly 1,400 kg/ha, the caloric equivalent of 6,100 kg/yr of forage. About 900 kg/yr of this was inedible to cattle. Desert shrub communities support only two to six head per square mile. Adjacent grassland supports five times as many cattle under similar precipitation.
- **Chemical defenses:** Repellent oils occur at concentrations of up to 5% in many desert shrubs, including creosote bush and sagebrush (*Artemisia*). Sagebrush oils retard cellulose digestion in mule deer and cattle rumen microbes (Nagy et al., 1964). Shrubs without oils are typically thorny or deciduous. Few desert shrubs are both thorny and aromatic.
- **Browse habitat:** Martin estimates about one million square miles of browse habitat across the Southwest and Mexico, and suggests this rangeland could support 10 to 20 million new animal units (one animal unit equals 1,000 lb, or a cow and calf). The 1959 *Natural History* version gives 15 to 30 million animals.

### Freshwater Fish and Stream Protection

The letter treats Arivaipa Creek as the central freshwater concern. Martin states that the stream "must be protected against any introductions of fish." He notes that the Bureau of Land Management closed the road below Salagar's partly because traffic could damage the stream, with numerous fords involved. He considers vehicle traffic a lesser threat than introduced [[Mosquito Fish]] or non-native bait fish. Both Martin and BLM agree on this priority.

## Key Findings

- **Pleistocene niche vacancy:** Martin argues that megafaunal extinction left unfilled browsing niches in the American Southwest. Cattle (*Bos*) are a grass-preferring species poorly matched to a land dominated by shrubs.
- **Historical immigrants:** Many "native" American large mammals arrived from Eurasia within the last 100,000 years. Martin argues that this weakens purist objections to exotic large mammal introductions.
- **Recommended candidates:** Martin cites ecological studies favoring [[Greater Kudu]], [[Giant Eland]], giraffe, roan antelope, springbok and elephant as potential range animals. Eland are already being ranched in South Africa.
- **Economic comparison:** Martin reports that Arizona ranches of 450 head earned a 2.0–2.4% return on fixed investment ($9,000–$15,000 per year). Dasmann's study, cited by Martin, estimates that game cropping could yield three to five times the profit of cattle ranching.
- **Regulatory safeguards:** Martin notes that USDA quarantine laws bar direct imports. Only animals born in approved zoos or game farms may be stocked, which he judges to reduce disease risk to "negligible."
- **Management, not introduction, is the risk:** Martin acknowledges that overstocking could damage vegetation, but argues this is a management problem that applies equally to cattle, goats and sheep.
- **Proposed Wood Ranch trial:** The letter identifies the [[Wood Ranch]] as a possible site, but Martin says it is "by no means certain" to be the best starting place. He stresses that no rush should disrupt fund raising. The [[Wood Brothers]] are receptive and bring practical cattle experience.
- **Program ownership:** Martin argues that a program of this scale should ultimately be undertaken by the federal government and state land-grant colleges, but that The Nature Conservancy is the first organization to attempt it. He estimates that government action is unlikely for another 10–20 years.
- **Funding request:** Martin asks for at least $500 in student support for a vegetation survey already started with Jim King and Kent Boyer.
- **Field observations:** The survey found saguaro growing within 100 yards of pinyon, and pinyons of the one-needle subspecies of *Pinus edulis*. Vegetation above 3,500 ft shows central-west Arizona affinities rather than encinal character. Martin describes the lava canyons tributary to Arivaipa Creek as unusual.
- **Central conservation argument:** Martin argues that demonstrating the value of African large mammals as range animals would make protection in Africa more likely. He concludes that "to tolerate further decimation" of African and Asian ungulates, the "gene bank" for future domestication, would be "unthinkable."
