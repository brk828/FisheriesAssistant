# Surface Pits in the Family Esocidae II: Epidermal-Dermal Interaction and Evidence for Aplasia of the Lateral Line Sensory System

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines two types of surface pits in [[Esox Americanus]] and [[Esox Niger]] of the [[Esocidae]]: [[Sensory Pit|sensory]] pits containing [[Neuromast|neuromasts]], and [[Nonsensory Pit|nonsensory]] pits containing [[Central Cells]]. Both pit types sit beside a notched [[Cardioid Scale]]. The authors test whether these pit cells control pit and scale-notch formation, and whether the nonsensory pit represents an [[Aplasia|aplasic]] state of the [[Lateral Line System]]. Surgical removal, cauterization and induced [[Neuromast Regeneration]] experiments support both hypotheses. The authors also argue that epidermal cells control the form of dermal structures, not only their composition, which extends the [[Epidermal Co-Participation Hypothesis]]. The evidence is described as circumstantial, because the target cell types could not be removed in isolation.

## Background & Description

Esox americanus and Esox niger each have two surface pit types that are morphologically very similar. Both are associated with a modified, notched (cardioid) scale. The pits differ only in their specialized epidermal cells. The sensory pit contains functional neuromasts. The nonsensory pit contains a group of distinct epidermal cells called central cells, which extend the full length of the pit. Ultrastructural work by Merrilees and Crossman (1973) found no function for the central cells. Their location and light-microscope structure, however, resemble the [[Primordial Cells]] that form the first stage of [[Neuromast]] development or regeneration.

The authors hypothesized that:

1. The central cells of the nonsensory pit, and the neuromasts of the sensory pit, form and maintain the pit and the scale notch.
2. Central cells are structurally and functionally equivalent to primordial cells. They never develop past this stage and stay arrested for the animal's life, producing aplasia (incomplete or faulty development) of the lateral line system.

Experiments used the subspecies E. a. americanus (North Carolina) and E. a. vermiculatus (Ontario). Animals were anesthetized in 0.01% tricaine methanesulphonate (MS 222), and surgery was performed under a stereomicroscope.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is a developmental and cellular study, so it contains little ecological information. The authors note that variation in lateral line structure (canals, pits, free neuromasts) correlates with modes of life (Dijkgraaf, 1963). They suggest that neuromasts may control this variation. The authors also place the findings in a comparative frame. Faulty lateral line development has been reported in the loach [[Misgurnus Anguillicaudatus]] and the cyprinid [[Cirrhina Mrigala]]. Aplasic conditions occur in the mammalian inner ear, and the authors consider the sensory epithelia of the two systems to be at least superficially comparable. They also suggest that the [[Mesenchymal Cord]] described in [[Fundulus Heteroclitus]] may help form canals.

## Key Findings

**Pit cells control scale morphology**

- **Scale removal, pit cells intact:** 19/19 regenerated scales from sensory pits (7 fish) and 22/22 from nonsensory pits (10 fish) were notched (cardioid).
- **Scale and all pit cells removed:** 8/8 sensory-pit scales (7 fish) and 10/10 nonsensory-pit scales (6 fish) regenerated without a notch, and no pit reappeared.

**Removal of all pit cells with scale in place**

- **Dissection:** Only 1/10 sensory-pit scales (7 fish) and 2/10 nonsensory-pit scales (3 fish) lost the pit and filled the notch. In one dissected sensory pit a neuromast later appeared at the anterior end, so dissection had left some pit tissue behind.
- **Cauterization:** All pits filled in. Sensory: 16/16 (2 fish). Nonsensory: 15/15 (4 fish). Scale-material deposition (% of notch length) was 94.9 ± 2.1 SE (range 68–100) for sensory pits and 91.2 ± 1.6 SE (range 82–100) for nonsensory pits. Pits filled with epidermis, and melanocytes appeared in the former pit area.

**Selective removal of central cells or neuromasts**

- **Nonsensory central strip removed:** 34/41 pits (6 fish) lost the pit and filled the notch, with deposition of 84.6 ± 1.6 SE % (range 66–100). The remaining 7 pits kept their normal features.
- **Control (side strip, no central cells):** Only 2/34 pits (3 fish) filled in, and 32 persisted. The difference was significant (χ² = 15.795, P < 0.01, 1 df).
- **Sensory strip including neuromasts:** 12/12 pits (2 fish) filled in, with deposition of 90.9 ± 2.3 SE % (range 80–100). These pits behaved like nonsensory pits after central-cell removal.

**Induction of neuromast regeneration**

- Posterior neuromasts of 41 sensory pits (two sets, 3 and 4 fish) were removed. Primordial cells were found at 14 days but not at 4 or 7 days.
- By 21 days, the anterior cells had partly regenerated into neuromasts while the posterior cells stayed primordial. Development was graded along the pit.
- Neuromasts were structurally mature by 35 days.
- Of 26 pits examined by electron microscopy at nine stages (4 and 7 days, then every 7 days to 56 days), the induced primordial cells at stage 1 closely resembled central cells.

**Arrested primordial cells maintain the pit**

- Thirty sensory pits (set 1: 10 pits from two fish; set 2: 20 pits from two fish) had all neuromasts except the anterior one removed. That remaining neuromast was then cauterized and the lateral line nerve sectioned. Denervation was repeated every four weeks.
- After 90 days, 18 of 30 operations (set 1: 4 and 0 pits; set 2: 7 and 7 pits) kept a pit and cardioid scale. These pits had no neuromasts and no nerve supply.
- In these 18 pits, new scale material averaged 36% of notch length (SE 3.0, range 18–63%). In the 12 pits that filled in, it averaged 88% (SE 2.7, range 69–100%).
- All six sectioned persistent pits contained primordial cells still at stage 1. The two sectioned filled-in pits had none.
- The arrested cells showed graded development, with more cells at the anterior end, but no organization of infranuclear mitochondria or supranuclear endoplasmic reticulum. The authors describe this as essentially an experimental conversion of a sensory pit into a nonsensory pit, maintained for three months.

**Conclusions**

- The central cells and neuromasts control the formation and maintenance of pits and scale notches. This extends the epidermal co-participation hypothesis from composition to form.
- The nonsensory pit represents an aplasic condition of the lateral line system. Direct proof would require converting central cells into functional neuromasts, which the authors propose as future work, possibly by restoring innervation.
