# Report of the National Fish Hatchery Review Panel (November 11, 1994)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonids

## Executive Summary

This report from the [[National Fish Hatchery Review Panel]], convened at the request of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) Director and conducted by [[The Conservation Fund]], evaluates the future role of the [[National Fish Hatcheries]] (NFH) program. The Panel concludes that many hatchery practices have contributed to fish declines rather than reversing them. Its central recommendation is that the hatchery program should become a support function for [[Ecosystem Management]], with habitat protection and restoration as the priority.

The Panel states that nearly 400 taxa "either have or need special protection in some part of their range" (Moyle and Leidy 1992), and that 27 species and 13 subspecies of North American fish have become extinct since 1900. Moyle and Leidy (1992) grouped the causes of fish decline into five categories: competition for water, habitat alteration, pollution, introduction of exotic species, and commercial exploitation. Hatcheries, once seen as compensation for development impacts, are now recognized as "part of the problem." Some hatcheries have released exotic species and hybrids that compete with and prey on natives, and others have saturated natural waters with hatchery fish, leading to overharvest of wild stocks. The Panel also notes that domestication in hatcheries reduces fitness under natural conditions, and that interbreeding with wild fish reduces their fitness (Reisenbichler 1994).

The main recommendations are:
- Form an [[Ecosystem Management Team]] (EMT) in each region to assess the ecosystem impacts of each hatchery.
- Close or divest facilities that are unalterably disruptive, economically unfeasible, or no longer needed, and do not transfer them in ways that perpetuate the problem.
- Prioritize hatchery work on threatened and endangered species, mandated mitigation, and sport fish restoration, in that order.
- Stop stocking private waters and phase out production for enhanced recreational fishing in coordination with state agencies.
- Use the savings from divestiture for ecosystem assessment, operations, and retrofitting remaining facilities.

The Panel did not reach consensus on the federal role in recreational fishing. The majority view is that providing hatchery fish for recreational enhancement is not a federal responsibility. A [[Minority Report on Recreational Fishing]] by Val S. Nash argues that federal hatcheries should continue supplying fish for federal waters and for states that depend on them.

## Background & Description

**Historical context.** Federal fish propagation began at Bucksport, ME in 1871 and at [[McCloud River National Fish Hatchery]], California, in 1872. Early fish were moved by horse-drawn carts, trucks, and railroad cars for wide distribution, and hatcheries were regarded as "the mainstay of recreational fishing." Three hatcheries founded between 1879 and 1889 still operate: [[Neosho National Fish Hatchery]] (MO), [[Leadville National Fish Hatchery]] (CO), and [[Craig Brook National Fish Hatchery]] (ME). The number of hatcheries once reached 96. Construction and upgrading expanded between 1934 and 1960 under the White Act (1930), Coordination Act (1934), and Mitchell Act (1934). Since the 1980s, budget reductions and changing management philosophies led to the closure or divestiture to states of 17 federal hatcheries.

**Current program.** The Fishery Resources (FR) program includes 78 hatcheries, 9 [[Fish Disease Control Centers]], and 6 [[Fish Technology Centers]]. These hatcheries produce 220 million fish of 69 species for mitigation, restoration, threatened or endangered species preservation, and recreational fishing. Of the 78 NFHs, 52 produce coldwater species, 13 warmwater species, and 13 a combination of coldwater and warmwater fishes. The 1993 budget was $54.1 million, including $3.5 million in reimbursable funds. NFHs account for about 80% of FR dollar and personnel resources. Table 1 of the source lists all facilities and their production emphasis.

**Panel and process.** The Panel had 17 listed members, including university faculty, state agency representatives, federal agency representatives (retired and active), a private aquaculture representative, and a conservation group representative. Two former Service employees chaired the review. The Panel held meetings in Bloomington, MN on April 9, June 8, and July 13, 1994. Draft recommendations were submitted on September 14, 1994, a preliminary draft final report on September 28, and the final report by November 11, 1994.

**Scope.** The report covers sport fish restoration, recreational fishing, impacts on native fishes, threatened and endangered species, mitigation, tribal and treaty obligations, private aquaculture, public education, and future roles of federal hatcheries under ecosystem management.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Anadromous salmon decline (Pacific Northwest).** Runs of [[Pacific Salmon]] continue to decline despite record releases of hatchery smolts. The source describes several contributing factors:
  - Hydropower dams and turbines, with "80% of migrating smolts" killed in turbines according to regional biologists. The report also states that "80% or more" of downstream migrants released from mitigation hatcheries are killed in turbines.
  - Conversion of riverine systems to near-slackwater reservoirs, which reduces current, warms water, and favors predators such as squawfish and walleye.
  - Clear-cut logging, excessive road building, and livestock grazing, which warm spawning streams and cause siltation that suffocates fry and incubating eggs.
  - Blockage of traditional upriver spawning areas by dams.
  - Naivete, or genetic and behavioral traits, of hatchery fish that may reduce their survival in the wild.

  The consensus of the review group is that "alteration and destruction of habitat are the major causes of the decline."

- **Native fish and genetic diversity.** The Panel agrees that stocking hatchery fish has had "a negative impact on many native stocks nationwide." Hatchery stocks can alter gene pools, usurp spawning habitat, compete with wild progeny, and introduce predators. The source notes a lack of field data on the magnitude and distribution of genetic variability in wild populations.

- **Reservoirs and tailwaters.** Impoundments can create habitat that is unsuitable for native species. At [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]], a mitigation-based sport fishery for salmonids requires annual stocking because there is no suitable spawning habitat. The recent stocking of [[Striped Bass]] has led to predation on stocked salmonids. Cold tailwaters below warmwater impoundments, such as the [[Green River]] case, support "put, grow, and take" trout fisheries with little or no spawning habitat.

- **Evolutionarily significant units.** The National Marine Fisheries Service designated [[Evolutionarily Significant Units]] (ESU) for Pacific salmon. The Snake River [[Sockeye Salmon]] population in Redfish Lake was designated as the remnant of an ESU, as were Snake River fall and spring/summer [[Chinook Salmon]]. In 1993, a single male Sockeye returned to the traditional Snake River spawning area.

## Key Findings

**Program performance and conditions**
- Nearly 400 taxa need special protection in some part of their range; 27 species and 13 subspecies of fish have been extinct in North American waters since 1900 (Moyle and Leidy 1992).
- Hatcheries "often do not meet expectations" and "can be part of the problem" through exotics, hybrids, saturation of natural waters, and overharvest of wild fish.
- Restoring all 78 hatcheries to full capability would cost approximately $270 million, which the Panel considers "not realistic."
- Most federal funding (about 80%) goes to hatchery operation, which the Panel considers excessive given the need for habitat work.
- Maintenance has been neglected to the point that major funding is needed to restore hatcheries.
- The Panel reached consensus that not all 78 hatcheries will be needed to meet ecosystem managers' needs.

**Recommendations for structure and policy**
- Form regional EMTs immediately; complete their review and deliver recommendations to the Director before the end of FY1995.
- Hatcheries should be a support function to ecosystem managers. Priority areas are threatened and endangered species, mandated mitigation, sport fish restoration, and recreational fishing if consistent with national goals.
- Do not propagate any species without a distinct ecosystem management plan with step-down strategies.
- Do not stock private waters with federally produced fish, and phase out federal production for enhanced recreational fishing in coordination with states.
- Use hatchery production only where habitat is protected or restored. Artificial propagation should "not be substituted for an aggressive program of habitat restoration."
- Establish a national fisheries management plan covering at least 5 years, with annual review and budgets focused on ecosystem management projects.

**Threatened and endangered species**
- Hatchery use requires a rigorous [[Population Viability Analysis]] (PVA) before captive propagation.
- If a species is brought into captivity, habitat must be restored within 3 to 5 generations, or the species should be transferred to aquaria or zoos.
- Maintain heterozygosity in captive populations and consult population geneticists from the beginning of propagation.
- Avoid manipulating strain characteristics to improve hatchery survival, because this fosters domestication.

**Mitigation**
- Of the 78 hatcheries, 38 are involved in producing fish for mitigation. Some are statutory mitigation hatcheries and others are de facto mitigation hatcheries.
- The Panel "strongly recommends" re-opening the mitigation issue with Congress and project sponsors to negotiate expedited fish passage research, scaled-down production, and reimbursement of operating costs.
- A 1991 Service audit of two Pacific Northwest water development projects (four mitigation hatcheries) indicated the Service should be compensated, involving approximately $1.5 million.
- The Panel opposes introducing non-endemic salmon stocks into the Snake River or other depleted areas.

**Recreational fishing and sport fish restoration**
- Waters created with federal funds may warrant stocking until a self-sustaining population is established, typically "three to five years of annual stocking." Federal obligation ends once a reproducing population is in place.
- If a federally funded project creates a trout tailwater with no spawning habitat, the beneficiaries should pay for production in perpetuity.
- Recreational fishing can be managed through fly-fishing-only waters, hook-and-release, and restricted limits.
- The majority view holds that enhanced recreational fishing with federal hatchery fish is not a federal responsibility unless required by Congressional mandate, mitigation, or tribal and treaty obligations.

**Tribal, treaty, and private aquaculture matters**
- Propagated fish for tribal waters should be the same stock or strain as endemic fish; if endemic stocks are gone, use the most closely related stock.
- The Service should not stop fish health services to non-federal clients until state, private, or [[U.S. Department of Agriculture]] (USDA) services are in place. Beginning October 1, 1994, the Service planned to stop servicing state and private producers.
- The Panel recommends transferring the generic responsibility for assisting private aquaculture to USDA, with an interagency group to address impacts of imports and escapees.
- The Panel recommends evaluating leasing of federal hatcheries to private operators with federal fish health support.

**Communication and partnerships**
- Visitors and politicians often view hatcheries as existing "to produce fish, the more the better." The Panel recommends a new public education approach that presents hatcheries as support for resource managers.
- The Panel recommends that the Service re-establish a national fisheries program with clear goals, uniform guidance, and phased transition with cost-sharing when federal support ends.
- States report that the Service "often acts unilaterally and arbitrarily." The Panel recommends consultation before policy changes and gradual phase-out with state takeover.

**Minority Report (Val S. Nash)**
- Recreational fishing is primarily a state responsibility, but states argue that federal stocking of federal waters should continue.
- Some southeastern sport fisheries (Georgia, South Carolina, North Carolina, Tennessee, and West Virginia) were developed as joint state-federal efforts.
- State takeover of a federal hatchery could require "up to 10 years" in states that rely entirely on federal hatcheries. Without a timed phase-out, the impact on angling "will be immediate and catastrophic."

**Specific recommendations (33 total, in priority order)**
- Primary mission: support ecosystem management.
- Artificial propagation should not substitute for habitat restoration.
- Federal obligation for stocking federally funded waters ends once self-sustaining populations are achieved.
- Hatchery stocking should occur only where suitable habitat, food, and spawning areas are documented.
- Facility review criteria include mitigation or treaty obligations, species capability, cost-effectiveness, flexibility for [[Threatened and Endangered Species]] work, and physical condition.
- Hatcheries should not be retained as refugia for species with no hope of wild re-establishment; that role belongs to zoos and aquariums.
- Increase the sharing of hatchery expertise through publications, newsletters, and workshops.
- Staff morale among NFH employees is low, and managers and employees need better information about their roles.
