# Responses of Northern, Florida, and Hybrid Largemouth Bass to Low Temperature and Low Dissolved Oxygen

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This experimental study compares two subspecies of [[Largemouth Bass]], the [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] (*M. s. salmoides*) and the [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] (*M. s. floridanus*), with their reciprocal F₁ [[Hybrid Largemouth Bass]] strains. The fish were tested for survival and feeding under low temperature and for physiological responses to low [[Dissolved Oxygen]] (DO) in [[Sealed-Jar Hypoxia Bioassay]] tests. Strain identity was verified by [[Protein Electrophoresis]]. The authors found that Florida fish suffered high mortality during prolonged cold, that the hybrids were intermediate and resembled northern fish, and that DO tolerance and oxygen-use patterns differed by strain and acclimation temperature. Salts, the anesthetic [[Tricaine]] (MS-222), low temperature and ammonia all modified the response to hypoxia. The authors suggest that strain contamination through hybridization may explain inconsistent results in earlier studies, and that strain choice matters for stocking and transport.

## Background & Description

*Micropterus salmoides* is cultured in hatcheries for stocking freshwater fisheries and for [[Aquaculture]]. Its two subspecies are morphologically similar but can be distinguished by protein electrophoresis. The northern subspecies is named for northern latitudes and the Florida subspecies for Florida, reflecting their historical distributions. An original intergrade zone north and east of the Gulf of Mexico has been enlarged and blurred by [[Stocking]] (Bailey and Hubbs 1949; Philipp et al. 1983).

Many transplants have succeeded in establishing sport fisheries, but others have failed. The authors attribute some failures to differing subspecies biology, particularly temperature tolerance inferred from natural latitudinal ranges. Earlier comparisons of survival and growth had given variable results, which the authors suspect reflects inadequate verification of subspecies identity.

The study had three aims:
- Measure survival and feeding of the four strains under low temperature.
- Test hypoxia responses in sealed jars at two acclimation temperatures (14°C and 22°C), with salts, MS-222 and ammonia.
- Use [[Average Metabolic Rate]] (AMR) and residual DO as physiological indicators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hatchery conditions.** Fish were reared in open ponds at the [[San Marcos National Fish Hatchery]] and moved at about 40 days old to 740-L holding tanks. Water was supplied at 22°C, pH 7.3, DO 5–6 mg/L and hardness 300 mg/L as CaCO₃. Food was withheld for 24 h before experiments. Fish were fed pelleted feed (Biodiet), supplemented with live [[Goldfish]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] during cold trials.

**Simulated cold events.** Temperature was lowered by about 1°C/d over 20 days to 1°C and then raised to 21°C within 90 min. This was meant to mimic sudden weather changes or [[Power Plant Impoundments]]. A separate trial held fish at 2.0 ± 0.4°C for 5 days to mimic severe winter conditions.

**Hypoxia conditions.** Individual fish were held in 510- or 960-mL sealed jars with initial DO near saturation (8.1–9.4 mg/L). Death was defined as cessation of opercular movement and convulsive seizure. Residual DO at death was the key tolerance index. Salts were a mixture of sodium chloride, potassium chloride, potassium phosphate and magnesium sulfate. These salts are used to limit osmotic dysfunction during [[Fish Transport]]. The authors note that the furnace failure in the wet laboratory lowered test temperatures to near 16.5°C in the salts trial.

**Chemical stressors.** Ammonia was tested at 0.70 mg/L un-ionized, close to the 24-h median lethal concentration for this species. MS-222 was tested at 25 mg/L. Both are relevant to culture and handling. The discussion links these chemicals to the principles described by [[Sealed-Jar Hypoxia Bioassay]] studies of [[Pulp Mill Effluent]] toxicity.

## Key Findings

**1. Low-temperature survival differed by strain.** Fish cooled slowly and held at about 2°C for 5 days showed the following mortality:
- Florida (F × F): 48%, significantly higher than the other strains.
- Hybrid F × N: 4–5%.
- Northern (N × N) and hybrid N × F: nil.

All fish survived a brief drop to 1°C followed by rapid warming to 21°C and fed normally within 24 h. Feeding was normal down to 13°C, slow between 12 and 6°C, and absent below 6°C.

**2. Residual DO depended on strain and acclimation temperature.**
- At 22°C, residual DO was lower for N × N (0.56 mg/L) and F × F (0.62 mg/L) than for F × N (0.71 mg/L) and N × F (0.82 mg/L).
- At 14°C, all strains were similar (0.73–0.84 mg/L).
- The oxygen-use pattern therefore varied with temperature, not just genetics.

**3. Additives changed hypoxia tolerance.** At 22°C, residual DO was raised by ammonia, MS-222 and salts. Ammonia gave the highest values:
- Ammonia (0.70 mg/L un-ionized): F × F 1.95, N × N 1.11, F × N 1.37, N × F 1.58 mg/L.
- Among ammonia-treated fish, F × F had higher residual DO than N × N.
- No strain differences in residual DO appeared after MS-222 or salts treatment.

**4. Metabolic rates varied with conditions.**
- AMR was lower at 14°C than at 22°C for all strains.
- At 22°C without additives, AMR was about 97 mL O₂/kg/h (N × N) to 129 mL O₂/kg/h (N × F); F × F was 108 and F × N was 116.
- Salts lowered AMR by 21% (F × F), 36% (N × N), 29% (F × N) and 25% (N × F).
- MS-222 and low temperature also lowered AMR. Ammonia did not change AMR, so its toxicity appears unrelated to metabolic rate.

**5. Oxygen-uptake dynamics differed.** In the first 30 min of sealed-jar tests, F × F fish used about 17% more oxygen than N × N fish. Both strains then fell to similar rates. The authors interpret this as an initial agitation phase followed by fatigue, which may give oxygen-independent and oxygen-dependent phases of metabolism.

**6. Hybrids were intermediate and closer to northern fish.** The authors state that both hybrid strains resembled N × N more than F × F in cold responses. They suggest that hybridization may have confounded earlier studies and explain their variable results.

**7. Management implications.** The authors conclude that:
- Subspecies and hybrid strains differ in temperature and low-DO responses, and these differences should guide choice of fish for stocking.
- Low temperature, salts and anesthetics markedly reduce AMR and may benefit handling and transport.
- Cold-exposure differences may extend to responses to secondary stressors such as [[Net-Confinement Stress]] and disease, which were not tested here.

**Limitations noted in the text.**
- Oxygen uptake curves used only three fish per strain, and statistical analysis of corrected AMR was not performed.
- The salts-treated tests had lower temperatures than planned.
- The text lists magnesium sulfate as "118 g/L", but the results table gives 118 mg/L; the table value is used here.
