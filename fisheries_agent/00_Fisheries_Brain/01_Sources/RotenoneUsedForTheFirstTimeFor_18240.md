# Rotenone Used for Scientific Fishing in Puerto Rico (1952)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #pomacentridae

## Executive Summary

This 1952 article from the *Turtox News* (General Biological Supply House, Chicago) describes the first use of [[Rotenone]] for scientific fish collection in [[Puerto Rico]]. The author, Virgilio Biaggi Jr. of the [[University of Puerto Rico]] (Mayagüez), reports that the method was applied during an ichthyology course taught by visiting professor [[Luis René Rivas]]. The technique gave access to small, cryptic reef fishes that were hard to take with nets or hook, and it added "several hundreds" of specimens to the department collection. The article stresses that the method requires trained personnel, shallow water, and careful timing with the tide. The issue also carries Turtox catalog advertisements, which are not research content.

## Background & Description

- **Publication:** *Turtox News*, January 1952, Volume 30, Number 1. Copyright held by General Biological Supply House, Inc., Chicago, Illinois.
- **Author:** [[Virgilio Biaggi Jr.]], Department of Biology, College of Agriculture and Mechanic Arts, University of Puerto Rico, Mayagüez.
- **Context:** In summer 1951, Dr. Luis René Rivas visited under a joint agreement between the [[University of Miami]] (Coral Gables, Florida) and the University of Puerto Rico. He taught a course in General Ichthyology, which included field methods for scientific fishing.
- **Poison:** Rotenone is an agricultural insecticide and fish poison. It occurs in plants of the family Leguminosae (bean and pea family). The most common source plant is [[Derris]], native to Australia, Southern Asia and Oceania, with another form found in South America. South American Indians have long used it as a fish poison.
- **Preparation used:** Commercial rotenone at 5% strength, in powder form.
- **Other content:** The issue lists the Turtox Biology Catalog No. 52 (620 pages) and an offer of named moth cocoons (set of 12, $4.00). It also advertises whale eyes and whale barnacles (*Coronula diadema*) preserved in formalin, priced per specimen and per dozen. These items are commercial and carry no scientific data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study setting.** Fieldwork took place on close-to-shore [[Coral Reef]]s. The first trial was on a reef about fifty yards from shore, where the deepest part was less than four feet. The article repeatedly states that rotenone works best in shallow water, where most or all fish can be collected.

**Tidal control.** The author says the poison should be applied only on an incoming tide. This carries killed fish toward shore, not out to open ocean.

**Fish response timeline (as reported):**
- About 10 to 15 minutes after application: first signs of poisoning.
- Affected fish rise to the surface and swim on their sides over long distances, which is the easiest time to catch them.
- About 1 hour after application: bottom searches with diving hoods or goggles recovered many fish that had escaped surface collection.
- 2 to 3 hours after application: large numbers of dead fish were found washed up on shore.
- About 30 minutes after application: eels (Ophichthyidae, Muraenidae, Leptocephalidae) began to appear. The author describes them as the most poison-resistant group. They usually died on the bottom, where divers had to collect them, though an occasional one surfaced to breathe air.

**Human safety.** The author states that, if used correctly with precautions, rotenone "will do humans no harm." He also warns that untrained users waste poison and harm fish populations without producing useful scientific results.

**Field procedure (summary):**
- Rotenone was mixed in buckets, with powder filling about one quarter of each bucket and sea water added while a student stirred until dissolved.
- Four to six buckets were prepared per session.
- Students spread along the shore over about 25 to 30 feet and sprinkled the solution into the water. It could be applied from shore outward or from the reef inward.
- The solution formed a brownish cloud that sank toward the bottom.
- Dip-net crews worked the surface, and divers searched the bottom.

## Key Findings

1. **Method validation:** Rotenone was used for scientific fishing in Puerto Rico for the first time during this course. It gave access to small reef fishes that were hard to collect otherwise, and it added several hundred specimens to the Biology Department collection.

2. **Conditions for success:** The author lists four requirements: shallow water, correct time of day, an incoming tide, and trained personnel. Results depended on careful technique before and during application.

3. **Sensitivity differs among taxa:** The first fishes affected were small reef species of Pomacentridae, including the Beaugregory (*Pomacentrus leucostictus*) and the Sergeant Major (*Abudefduf saxatilis*). Eels were the most resistant and appeared later.

4. **Collection breadth:** The author reports collections from 26 named families. Of these, 23 are fish families, including Pomacentridae, Sphyraenidae (barracudas), Apogonidae (cardinal fishes), Clupeidae, Carangidae, Serranidae, Lutjanidae, Haemulonidae, Labridae, Scaridae, Chaetodontidae, Acanthuridae, Balistidae, Tetraodontidae, Scorpaenidae, and Pleuronectidae. The remaining three are eel families. Several localities in western and southwestern Puerto Rico produced "most satisfactory" results.

5. **Named species:** The text names only four species: the Beaugregory (*Pomacentrus leucostictus*), the Sergeant Major (*Abudefduf saxatilis*), the Spot-finned Cardinal Fish (*Apogonichthys stellatus*), and the Spotted Cardinal Fish (*Apogon maculatus*). No quantitative sample sizes are given.

6. **Recommended references:** The paper cites Beebe & Tee-Van (1933) on Bermuda shore fishes, Breder (1948) on Atlantic coast marine fishes, Nichols (1929, 1930) on the fishes of Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands, and U.S. Fisheries Leaflet 350 (1949) on rotenone as a fish poison.

7. **Conservation caution:** The author warns that rotenone used carelessly harms "fish colonies" without benefit to science. Its use should therefore be limited to trained investigators and shallow sites.
