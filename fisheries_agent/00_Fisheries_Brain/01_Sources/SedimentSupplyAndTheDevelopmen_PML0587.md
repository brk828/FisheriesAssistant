# Sediment Supply and the Development of the Coarse Surface Layer in Gravel-Bedded Rivers

#fluvial-geomorphology #sediment-transport #gravel-bed-rivers

## Executive Summary

This 1989 *Nature* paper by Dietrich, Kirchner, Ikeda and Iseya proposes that the coarse surface layer (armour or pavement) of gravel-bedded rivers forms mainly where local [[Bedload Transport]] exceeds the local [[Sediment Supply]], not because of an inherent property of poorly sorted sediment. Laboratory flume experiments with progressively reduced sediment feed support this view. The authors suggest that the degree of bed surface coarsening could therefore be used to assess how river beds respond to sediment changes caused by [[Land Use]].

## Background & Description

- **Phenomenon:** The bed surface of most gravel rivers is considerably coarser than the sub-surface and the gravel load being transported. This coarse layer, often called an armour or pavement, affects river dynamics, morphology and ecology.
- **Existing explanations:** Prior work attributed [[Armouring]] to (1) the tendency of small grains to settle between larger ones during active transport of all sizes, and (2) [[Selective Erosion]] or trapping of finer particles when coarse grains are relatively immobile. Where bedload supply is cut off below dams, selective erosion causes coarsening.
- **Proposed model:** Surface coarsening develops when local bedload supply from upstream is less than the flow's transport capacity. This is tested in laboratory experiments.
- **Transport theory:** Above the [[Critical Boundary Shear Stress]], bedload transport rate rises rapidly with small increases in shear stress. The simple transport equation (equation 1) uses empirically determined coefficients, with the exponent usually close to 1.5. On a mixed-grain bed, the critical shear stress is roughly proportional to the median grain size of the bed surface.
- **Dimensionless transport ratio:** The authors define q\*, the transport rate of the coarse surface normalised by the rate for a surface as fine as the sub-surface or load (equations 2 and 3). The hypothesis predicts q\* ≈ 1 when supply matches transport capacity, and q\* → 0 as the surface coarsens under reduced supply. See [[Dimensionless Transport Ratio]].
- **Scope note:** The document concerns sediment and channel processes. It does not address fish or other aquatic organisms directly, although it mentions fish production as a river use that may be affected by land-use-driven sediment changes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sediment supply controls:** In most rivers, limited supply of gravel from hillslopes, chemical and mechanical breakdown of bed material, net loss to floodplains, and distance downstream from upland sources restrict bedload supply and promote coarsening.
- **Local effects:** Flow divergence and cross-channel rolling of particles can reduce supply and coarsen individual bed patches.
- **Continuum of responses:** The authors describe a range from well-armoured beds below dams (where all bedload supply is eliminated) to armour-free surfaces on bars below landslides or other local high-supply sources.
- **Land use conflicts:** Forest cutting and related land use can alter sediment supply, creating conflicts with river uses such as fish production.
- **Predicted responses to increased erosion:** In rivers with predominantly low q\* values, extra bedload may be absorbed by raising q\* locally and widening high-q\* zones. In streams with high initial q\*, increased load should instead cause net deposition, pool infilling and channel migration. Increased fine supply may cause bed fining, which can then mobilise coarser fractions.

## Key Findings

1. **Supply controls coarsening.** Reducing sediment supply to a flume, while holding water discharge and load grain-size distribution constant, progressively coarsened the bed surface.
2. **Median surface grain size by supply rate:**
   - At 17.4 g min⁻¹ per cm width (high supply), the surface D50 equalled the load D50 (3.7 mm) within measurement error. The surface became finer and more poorly sorted.
   - At 6.1 g min⁻¹ per cm width, D50 = 4.3 mm, with a marked reduction in bed-surface sand.
   - At 1.7 g min⁻¹ per cm width, D50 = 4.9 mm.
3. **Spatial zonation:** At high supply, mobile bedload sheets 1–2 grain diameters thick migrated downstream. Each sheet had coarse (congested), fine (smooth) and intermediate (transitional) zones, with median diameters of 4.7 mm, 2.7 mm and 3.7 mm respectively. Reduced supply produced fewer, less distinct sheets, expanding coarse inactive zones and confining transport to a narrower fine-textured active zone.
4. **Mechanism:** Fine grains stop in pockets between coarse particles. Partial filling reduces grain friction and wake momentum loss, which increases local transport and flow velocity and produces a narrow active band bounded by less active coarse surfaces.
5. **Model agreement:** Predicted q\* values from equation (3) agreed well with experimental results. The Yalin bedload equation predicted observed transport rates more accurately, so it was used for the authors' data. Critical shear stress was estimated using τ\*c = τc × [(ρs − ρ) g D50]⁻¹ = 0.045, and a ≈ 1.06.
6. **Flume conditions:** The flume was 7.5 m long and 0.3 m wide, with a constant slope of 0.0046 and a water discharge of 0.6 l s⁻¹ per cm width. Mean water-surface slope fell from 0.0052 to 0.0046 and then to 0.0035 as loading decreased, while mean depth rose from 10 to 11 cm. Runs lasted six to eight hours. Boundary shear stress fell from 52 to 47 and then to 39 dyne cm⁻², which is within the stated uncertainty of the slope measurements.
7. **Reinterpretation of prior work:** The authors argue that the [[Parker-Klingeman Model]] experiments (coarse layer from vertical winnowing, giving equal mobility) were run at very low sediment feed relative to shear stress, so surface coarsening reduced the gap between imposed and critical shear stress. [[Kuhnle and Southard Experiments]] used large initial excess shear stresses (τ/τc > 4), and a coarse layer again developed consistently with the authors' theory. Kuhnle and Southard themselves suggested their initial surface was too fine for equal mobility, which implicitly recognises the role of supply.
8. **Field application:** The authors propose that surveys of q\* could quantify river sensitivity to land-use-driven supply changes. Field data would come from bankfull channel geometry, bed-surface grain analysis and sub-surface sampling for different grain-size zones. Preliminary [[California Rivers]] surveys show q\* variation with sediment supply similar to the theory's predictions, but these results were in preparation at publication.
9. **Caveat:** Uncertainty in the shear stress estimate from bed-slope variability was close to the differences between runs. The Parker and Klingeman comparison relied on a supercritical-flow experiment, so its quantitative interpretation is uncertain.
