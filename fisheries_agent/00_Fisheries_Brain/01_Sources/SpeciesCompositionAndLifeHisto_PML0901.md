# Species Composition and Life Histories of Aquatic Insects in a Lowland Sonoran Desert Stream

#ichthyology #marine-biology #aquatic-insects #entomology

## Executive Summary

This study by Paul C. Tarshis of the Department of Zoology and Entomology, Colorado State University, documents the species composition and life-history traits of aquatic insects in Sycamore Creek, a lowland Sonoran Desert stream in central Arizona. Sampling from July 1977 to November 1979 recorded 99 aquatic insect taxa and 104 macroinvertebrate taxa in total. Life histories of 17 taxa were examined through laboratory and field rearing, with emphasis on developmental time, reproductive periods, and dormancy. The study finds that desert stream insects differ sharply from those of temperate mesic streams: most lack dormancy, reproduce continuously, and develop rapidly, with a few exceptions (stonefly egg diapause, tabanid diapause). Floods emerged as the major selective pressure shaping these life histories.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a gap in knowledge: the only prior published life-history work on desert lotic insects covered [[Tabanidae]] (Smith 1975) and [[Belostomatidae]] (Belostomatidae work by Smith and others), and few studies had examined the aquatic insect fauna of arid American Southwest streams in depth. Objectives included compiling the species present, describing their geographic origins and distributions, documenting life-history characteristics (developmental times, reproductive periods, dormancy), and assessing how these traits respond to environmental cues.

The study site is Sycamore Creek (approximately 33°45′N, 111°55′W), which flows about 65 km through the Mazatzal Mountains before entering the [[Verde River]] at 430 m elevation. Mean annual precipitation is 51 cm at midbasin and 23 cm near the mouth. Most of the channel is unshaded, and the watershed has a history of [[Flash Flood Events]], including a flood described as scouring the channel.

Methods included Surber and dip net sampling (270 µm mesh), a light trap for adult data, laboratory rearings in 19-liter aquaria at 22 ± 2 °C under natural photoperiods, and field rearings in plywood boxes (60 µm nylon mesh) placed in the stream for 6–9 days. Developmental times were estimated from larval size-class data and tray data, with the authors noting that asynchronous oviposition limited the reliability of size-based estimates.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Thermal regime:** Stream temperatures rarely exceeded the 10–30 °C range. Diel extremes in the main channel were about 6–12 °C in some months (e.g., June 17–25 °C). Substrate temperatures can reach very high levels, and surface soil temperatures near 65 °C were noted for desert areas.
- **Hydrology:** Floods exceeding 10 m³/s occur roughly one to two times a year, with counts ranging from 1 to 16 per year (Thomsen and Schumann 1968). Winter floods can sustain high flows for days to weeks.
- **Precipitation:** Most rain falls in the summer monsoon (July through September), with little precipitation in spring.
- **Fauna origin:** The fauna is primarily composed of Neotropical and Nearctic elements, with a substantial number of southwestern endemics. *Mesocapnia arizonensis* is endemic to Arizona.
- **Comparative richness:** Sycamore Creek's 99 taxa exceed those of other desert streams: 67 taxa in Pinto Creek (listed as 48 in the text, with Aravaipa Creek at 56 and Piceance Creek at 67 cited for comparison).

## Key Findings

**Species composition**
- 99 aquatic insect taxa and 104 total macroinvertebrate taxa collected. The count is acknowledged as an underestimate because many taxa could not be identified to species.
- Insect orders represented: Ephemeroptera (9 taxa), Plecoptera (2), Trichoptera (multiple), Lepidoptera (1), Coleoptera (about 31, mostly Dytiscidae and Hydrophilidae), Hemiptera (10), Diptera (28), Odonata (5), Megaloptera (1), plus five non-insect groups.
- Several species are endemic to Arizona or the arid Southwest; the Arizona endemic *Mesocapnia arizonensis* (Plecoptera) is the most notable.

**Life histories of 17 taxa**
- **Ephemeroptera:** Four mayfly species (including *Baetis quilleri* and *Callibaetis montanus*) developed in 8–14 days from egg to adult, reproduced continuously, and lacked dormancy.
- ***Mesocapnia arizonensis*:** Adults present only in winter; eggs exhibited diapause, with hatching ceasing when maximum stream temperature exceeded 20 °C.
- ***Graptocorixa serrulata* (Corixidae):** Development took about 21 days (460 degree-days) and the species may produce up to 17 generations per year.
- **Beetles:** Ten dytiscid and hydrophilid species showed pronounced seasonal reproduction. Six peaked in spring and the others in summer. Tropisternus ellipticus developed in roughly 53 days from egg to adult.
- **Chironomids:** Four chironomid species and one ceratopogonid developed rapidly (9–16 days), reproduced continuously, and lacked dormancy.
- ***Tabanus dorsifer*:** Diapause-capable; adults emerged in spring and reproduced in July and August.

**Dormancy and drought**
- Dormancy was restricted to stonefly egg diapause and tabanid diapause. Many Sycamore Creek insects lacked dormancy altogether, which the authors contrast with temperate streams.
- Drought avoidance occurred through rapid development and timing of oviposition by adults, for example, beetles ovipositing in pools that retain water.

**Flood effects**
- Flood losses averaged 86% for some taxa; for example, reported losses included 96% for *Baetis quilleri*, 80% for *Leptohyphes packeri*, and 96% for *Tricorythodes dimorphus* (see flood-loss table).
- Using 19 years of discharge records (1960–1979), the authors calculated flood-free probabilities for development times of 10, 21, 35, and 50 days. Recolonization after floods depends on nearby adults, and frequent floods can prevent long-development taxa, such as main-channel caddisflies, from persisting.

**Conclusions**
- Life-history adaptations in desert stream insects are shaped by the predictability of climatic events and the nature of channel processes.
- Desert streams lack the widespread dormancy found in temperate lotic systems; flood frequency is the strongest selective force.
- Life histories have consequences for community and ecosystem dynamics, and studies of desert insects are important for understanding how aquatic communities respond to environmental change.
