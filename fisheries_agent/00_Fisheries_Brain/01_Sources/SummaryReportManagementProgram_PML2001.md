# Summary Report: Sport Fishery Management on the San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation, Arizona (1966)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 summary report, prepared by [[Richard J Navarre]], Fishery Management Biologist for the [[Bureau Of Sport Fisheries And Wildlife]] (Division of Fishery Services, Phoenix, Arizona), reviews sport fishery management on the [[San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation]] during 1966. Management was carried out under a 1958 agreement between the San Carlos Apache Tribe, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, and the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife, under which the Bureau provides technical assistance and fish for stocking.

The fishery is dominated by [[Rainbow Trout]] stocked in stock tanks, with warmwater species established in ponds and [[Smallmouth Bass]] established in the [[Black River]]. Angler use rose 54 percent over the previous year. The report concludes that the Reservation's recreational potential is high but will require an ambitious development program, including new trout lakes, road access, campgrounds, and business sites, to be realized.

## Background & Description

The report covers fishery activities administered under the 1958 agreement among the Tribe, the Bureau of Indian Affairs, and the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife. Fisheries are managed in three categories:

- **Stock tanks (trout management):** 56 tanks.
- **Stock ponds (warmwater management):** 44 tanks and ponds.
- **Streams:** the Black, Salt and Black River tributaries, which are difficult for anglers to reach.

[[Seneca Lake]] is the only water body on the Reservation built specifically for fishing. The report gives its surface area as about 227.5 acres in the text (OCR reading; the Table 1 entry lists 27.5 acres, so the figure should be verified).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and seasonality:** Heavy snow and rainfall early in 1966 produced runoff that filled most tanks by the start of the fishing season, assuring adequate water for a year-round fishery.
- **Seneca Lake limitations:** Summer fishing was poor because of high [[Water Temperature]] and [[Dissolved Oxygen]] depletion in the deep, cool-water layer. Public-use facilities such as tables and fireplaces were also lacking.
- **Access constraints:** The large, easily accessible reservoirs off the Reservation draw most anglers, so trout fishing in the tanks is relatively unpopular. Stream fishing on the Black and Salt Rivers is severely curtailed by the lack of access roads into the canyons.
- **Warmwater suitability:** Three tanks were stocked with [[White Bass]] as a trial, to determine whether the species suits pond fishery management.

## Key Findings

**1. Angler use and stocking**
- Fishing use increased 54 percent from 1965 to 1966. The 1965 figure is given as 13,153 man-days; the 1966 figure as printed in the OCR text ("201534") is unclear and should be verified against the original.
- 56 stock tanks were under trout management and were stocked with [[Rainbow Trout]] weighing 4,477 pounds (as printed). The tanks offering trout fishing received the heaviest use. The most popular were Ginnas (Upper Highway), Dry Lake, and the tanks along U.S. Highway 666.
- Table 1 totals 41,354 rainbow trout (9,584 pounds) across stock tanks, including Seneca Lake.
- 44 ponds were managed for warmwater fish. Ponds with established [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Channel Catfish]] reproduce successfully, so no stocking was needed there.
- Table 2 lists 135 [[White Bass]] (weight illegible in the scan) stocked in three tanks to test their suitability.

**2. Seneca Lake**
- Seneca Lake received 14,500 catchable-size rainbow trout weighing 3,722 pounds in 1966.
- Spring fishing was good, but summer fishing was poor because of thermal and oxygen stress.

**3. Black River smallmouth bass**
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] were first planted in the Black River in 1964 and successfully spawned there in 1965, establishing a self-sustaining population.
- In 1966, about 3,000 three-inch fingerlings (OCR-garbled count) and 4,000 seven-inch fingerlings were planted in the Black River.
- The report states no further fishery development is needed on the Black River until access roads into the [[Black River Canyon]] are built.

**4. Recreational potential and recommendations**
- The report judges the Reservation's recreational potential as high and estimates gross returns to the Tribe could exceed $1,000,000 annually if fully developed.
- Recommended steps include:
  1. Constructing twenty trout lakes totaling at least 600 surface acres, each with complete public-use facilities.
  2. Improving roads, including the road under construction from U.S. Highway 70 through Point of Pines, across the Black River, to Haverick and Highway 666, which will become a primary access route.
  3. Making the Black River Canyon bottom accessible, with campgrounds built along the river.
  4. Developing summer home sites in the Ponderosa Pine region, with leasable lots near fishing lakes. The pine-covered flats at Point of Pines are identified as a good site.
  5. Establishing Tribal business locations to serve tourists and recreational users, with Point of Pines becoming an important summer business area once the new road is completed.
- Development would create construction and maintenance jobs for tribal members and business opportunities.
- The report recommends that the Tribe keep game and fish permit-sale revenue in a separate account to fund lake and campground construction, making future development self-generating.

**5. Point of Pines Creek lake**
- The [[Bureau Of Indian Affairs]] funded a new fishing lake on [[Point Of Pines Creek]]. Construction began in 1966 but has been slow, and campground development and public opening are expected to take even longer.
- Continued growth in fishing pressure depends on establishing new fishing waters.

**6. Overall conclusion**
- The 1966 program was active, but fishing opportunity on the Reservation is limited mainly by access. The report concludes that a substantial development program is needed for the Tribe to exploit its recreational potential.
