# Plankton Community Impacts of Two Omnivorous Filter-Feeding Cichlids (*Tilapia galilaea* and *Tilapia aurea*) in Lake Kinneret

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cichlidae

## Executive Summary

This 1988 study in the *Canadian Journal of Fisheries and Aquatic Sciences* (Vinyard, Drenner, Gophen, Pollingher, Winkelman and Hambright) tests how two planktivorous cichlids, [[Tilapia Galilaea]] and [[Tilapia Aurea]], affect plankton in [[Lake Kinneret]], Israel. The work combined [[Laboratory Selective Grazing Experiments]] with replicated [[Mesocosm Experiments]] run in spring and summer. Both species suppressed crustaceans and rotifers. *T. galilaea* suppressed *Peridinium* more consistently and enhanced fewer nanoplankton taxa. The authors suggest that the two species compete for plankton, which bears on the decline of the native *T. galilaea* catch after [[Blue Tilapia]] stocking.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** Two cichlids were studied. *T. galilaea* (Saint Peter's fish, also *Sarotherodon galilaeus*) is a native fish of Lake Kinneret. *T. aurea* (blue tilapia) was previously rare in the lake and became common after stocking.
- **Diet:** Both species are primarily planktivorous, feeding on zooplankton and phytoplankton (Spataru 1976; Spataru and Zorn 1978; Gophen 1980).
- **Feeding mode:** Fish larger than 62 mm SL (*T. galilaea*) and 76 mm SL (*T. aurea*) feed as pump filter-feeders, using rapid non-directed suctions. Smaller fish may filter-feed but also visually locate and attack individual prey.
- **Hypothesis:** Based on diet studies and laboratory experiments, the authors proposed that competition between the two species could have contributed to the reduced catch of *T. galilaea*. Earlier work (Gophen et al. 1983) had found similar zooplankton selectivity in the two species, but phytoplankton selectivity had not been compared.
- **Study design:** Two sets of experiments assessed selective grazing and community structure effects on plankton.
- **Context:** The lake supplies about 35% of Israel's freshwater. [[Peridinium]] accounts for over 80% of annual algal biomass there.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Seasonal phytoplankton cycle.** The Lake Kinneret phytoplankton community alternates between *Peridinium* dominance in winter and spring and nanoplankton dominance in summer and fall (Pollingher and Berman 1978). The experiments were timed to capture both phases.

**Management relevance.** Serruya et al. (1980) suggested that increasing *T. galilaea* density could suppress *Peridinium* abundance and reduce suspended and dissolved organic material. The authors' concern is that displacement of *T. galilaea* by *T. aurea* could increase algal abundance and reduce water quality.

**Experimental setting.** Fish were collected from the lake or obtained from kibbutz Mevo Hama fish-rearing ponds. Water and plankton were pumped from near the lake shore and offshore. Tank temperatures were 17–22 °C in spring and 28–32 °C in summer. Continuous inflow of about 1 L lake water/min was added to the tanks after the first experiment, reducing diel dissolved-oxygen fluctuations.

**Related work.** Earlier studies by Drenner and colleagues (1982, 1984a, 1984b, 1986, 1987) reported direct suppression of zooplankton and phytoplankton by omnivorous filter feeders. They also proposed indirect enhancement of phytoplankton through nutrient effects and zooplankton predation. Small nanoplankton that these fish graze inefficiently may be especially vulnerable to such enhancement.

## Key Findings

### 1. Selective grazing (laboratory)

- Feeding rate constants (k) were measured in 60-L tubs holding five to eight fish (121–142 mm SL) at 28.8 ± 0.1 °C. Initial zooplankton densities ranged from 278 to 3780 organisms/L.
- Feeding rates of both species increased with particle size, leveling off above about 500 µm³ (×1000 µm³ axis in Fig. 1).
- Maximum feeding rates for both species were on zooplankton and [[Peridinium Cinctum]].
- *T. galilaea* fed more heavily than *T. aurea* on [[Peridinium Elpatiewsky]] and on intermediate-sized nanoplankton.
- Neither species efficiently grazed the smallest nanoplankton.

### 2. Spring mesocosm experiment (24 March 1985, 3 weeks)

- **Zooplankton:** Both species reduced crustaceans and rotifers. Taxa reduced by *T. galilaea* included *Ceriodaphnia* spp., adult *Mesocyclops* sp., copepodids, and copepod nauplii. Those reduced by *T. aurea* included *Ceriodaphnia* spp., *Bosmina* spp., adult *Mesocyclops* sp., copepodids, copepod nauplii, and *Keratella* spp.
- **Phytoplankton:** Both species reduced *Peridinium* spp. and chlorophyll concentration. Fish presence was also associated with increased densities of several nanoplankton taxa, including *Tetraedron* spp., *Chrysochromulina* spp., and *Acronema augustum*. Because nanoplankton contributed little to total algal biomass, these increases did not offset the chlorophyll loss from *Peridinium* suppression.
- **Nutrients:** Fish increased dissolved inorganic nitrogen (mainly ammonia and nitrate) but not total nitrogen. Total phosphorus fell in tanks with fish (no fish: 0.063 mg/L; *T. galilaea*: 0.053; *T. aurea*: 0.048; both: 0.042; *T. galilaea* main effect P = 0.022; *T. aurea* P = 0.005). The authors attribute the phosphorus loss mainly to rapid sedimentation of fecal pellets.

### 3. Summer mesocosm experiment (5 August 1985, 3 weeks)

- Nanoplankton dominated the phytoplankton. The smaller *P. elpatiewsky* was the most abundant dinoflagellate.
- Only *T. galilaea* reduced *Peridinium* spp. *T. aurea* was associated with increased chlorophyll concentration.
- *T. galilaea* suppressed more and enhanced fewer nanoplankton taxa than *T. aurea* did overall.
- Both species reduced zooplankton taxa (including *Ceriodaphnia* spp., copepodids, *Hexarthra* sp., and *Polyarthra* spp.).
- Total chlorophyll was much lower in summer than in spring.

### 4. Experimental design and statistics

- The experiment used a 2 × 2 factorial design: no fish, *T. galilaea*, *T. aurea*, and both species. Each treatment had two replicates in a randomized block design across eight tanks.
- Spring stocking: 6 fish of each species per single-species tank and 12 per species in the combined tank. Mean added biomass was 620.5 g per species group (mean SL = 140 mm).
- Summer stocking: 5 fish of each species per single-species tank and 10 per species in the combined tank. Mean added biomass was 610.1 g per species group (mean SL = 151 mm).
- Doubling biomass in the combined tanks held intraspecific competition constant while allowing interspecific competition to vary.
- Data were analyzed with multivariate profile analysis of repeated measures. Significance was set at P < 0.1.
- Significant interaction effects were rare, so the two species' effects were largely independent and additive. The exceptions were a few taxa (e.g., nauplii, copepodids, *C. turgidus*, *P. cinctum*) in spring and a few taxa (e.g., *C. citriformis*, *R. minuta*) in summer.

### 5. Fish growth and biomass

- **Spring:** Fish biomass increased in all tanks. *T. galilaea* alone gained 9.9% and *T. aurea* alone gained 4.0% (t = 3.497, P = 0.002). With both species present, *T. galilaea* gained 3.3% and *T. aurea* gained 0.7% (t = 3.174, P = 0.005). Both species grew significantly less when the other species was present (P = 0.001 and P = 0.002).
- **Summer:** Fish biomass declined in all treatments. *T. galilaea* alone lost 5.6% and *T. aurea* alone lost 9.2% (P = 0.054). With both present, *T. galilaea* lost 14.3% and *T. aurea* lost 5.3% (t = 4.317, P = 0.001). Both species lost significantly more weight when the other was present.
- **Interpretation:** The authors suggest *T. galilaea* may have a higher food conversion efficiency (Spataru 1976).

### 6. Competition and resource overlap

- Production of each species was lowest when both were together, which the authors interpret as evidence of potential competition for plankton resources.
- The two species overlapped strongly in resource use, especially for zooplankton and *P. cinctum*.
- The authors note that limited behavioral flexibility in the experiments may have inflated apparent niche overlap.
- The data support Gophen et al. (1983): stocking *T. aurea* could divert resources from *T. galilaea* in Lake Kinneret.

### 7. Conclusions

- Both species suppress crustaceans and rotifers equally.
- *T. galilaea* is the more effective suppressor of *Peridinium* and of nanoplankton overall. This matters most in the summer nanoplankton period, when *T. aurea* may enhance algal biomass.
- The experiments used high fish biomass to obtain short-term community responses. The authors judge the responses qualitatively similar to lake impacts, though possibly different in magnitude.
