# Changes in a River's Physical Characteristics Under Reduced Flow Due to Hydroelectric Diversion

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1959 paper by Brian Curtis, a fishery consultant and former Supervising Fisheries Biologist, reports how a river's wetted perimeter, depth, cross-sectional area, and mean water velocity change as flow is reduced below a [[Hydroelectric Diversion]] dam. The work was part of a program by the [[Pacific Gas and Electric Company]], with consultative input from the [[California Department of Fish and Game]]. Surveys on the [[Pit River]] (40 cross sections) and the [[North Fork Feather River]] (25 cross sections) were used to inform how much water should be released to maintain a [[Rainbow Trout]] sport fishery and the associated fish community. The central finding is that wetted perimeter (a proxy for bottom habitat) declines far less than volume of flow, while mean water velocity declines much more sharply.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Between a [[Diversion Dam]] and its [[Powerhouse]], water is diverted through a conduit, leaving the natural channel greatly reduced in flow. The question of how much water to maintain for aquatic life had long concerned both the company and the state.
- **Origin of study:** In late 1952 the company initiated a program to gather factual data. Curtis states that data of this kind had not been obtained before and that such work requires large resources and the ability to control stream flow.
- **Rivers studied:** The [[Pit River]] and the [[North Fork Feather River]], both in Northern California, where the company planned new power plants. Both supported a sport fishery for [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdnerti*, as written).
- **Methods:** Stream-bottom area was treated as a primary control on trout food production. Cross sections were surveyed at evenly spaced stations, fixed in advance, with survey crews instructed not to shift stations to easier locations, to approximate a random sample.
- **Pit River method:** Flows were released at 50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 c.f.s., controlled by automatically operated gates and measured by a recording gauge below the dam. Stations were placed at 1,000-foot intervals along the channel centerline. Each cross section was profiled with a surveyor's level, and maximum depth, wetted perimeter, and cross-sectional area were scaled from plotted drawings. Mean velocity was calculated as release volume divided by area.
- **Feather River method:** Twenty-five stations at 500-foot intervals, measured at controlled flows of 140, 200, 300, and 800 c.f.s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pit River hydrology:** The river originates in lava formations that absorb winter rainfall and release it gradually. Mean annual flow was 2,636 c.f.s. over a 42-year period, with a maximum flood of 30,200 c.f.s. in 1937. Minimum natural summer flow in normal years was probably on the order of 2,000 c.f.s., and summer flow most of the time ranged from 3,000 to 3,500 c.f.s.
- **Pit 4 reach:** The diversion dam sits at 2,400 ft elevation and the [[Pit 4 Powerhouse]] at 2,080 ft. Most water is diverted into a 4-mile tunnel. Gradient is 8 feet per 1,000. The source gives the channel distance as "about 74 miles," which appears to be an OCR or typographical issue, since 40 stations at 1,000-foot intervals span 40,000 feet (about 7.6 miles).
- **Fish community:** Beyond [[Rainbow Trout]], the mixed population includes [[Sacramento Sucker]] (*Catostomus occidentalis*), [[Hardhead]] (*Mylopharodon conocephalus*, a large cyprinid), and [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) and other rough fish. Curtis notes that changes in velocity could benefit some species and harm others, redistributing species by location and proportional numbers.
- **Bottom fauna:** Velocity changes were expected to alter bottom-dwelling organisms quantitatively and qualitatively, indirectly affecting fish. Curtis cites [[Needham And Usinger 1956]] on macrofauna variability in a riffle as relevant background, but notes that much remains unknown.
- **Temperature and sun exposure:** At 3,500 c.f.s., water crossed the dam-to-powerhouse distance in about three hours. At 250 c.f.s. with mean velocity near 1 ft/s, transit took about 11 hours, exposing water to the full daily high-temperature period. Peak summer air temperatures reach 100°F in the shade.
- **Accretion:** Groundwater, springs, and tributary inflow added about 50 c.f.s. between dam and powerhouse, constant across release volumes. The largest single input was [[Deep Creek]] near Station Y-29 at 6 c.f.s. Maximum dam water temperature was close to 68°F, while Deep Creek reached 55°F. Cool [[Accretion]] water added 25 percent to volume at 200 c.f.s. and 100 percent at 50 c.f.s., producing a cooling effect that increased as flow decreased.

## Key Findings

**Pit River (40 stations, 1,000-foot intervals, Pit 4 Dam to Pit 4 Powerhouse)**

- **Wetted perimeter declines more slowly than flow.** Volume at 50 c.f.s. is 20 percent of volume at 250 c.f.s., but wetted perimeter at 50 c.f.s. is 79 percent of its 250 c.f.s. value.
- **Reducing 200 c.f.s. to 100 c.f.s.** (a 50 percent cut) lowers wetted perimeter by only 9 percentage points. Submerged area at 100 c.f.s. remains over 90 percent of its 200 c.f.s. value, and the total bottom population is estimated to remain over 90 percent under equal conditions.
- **Mean wetted perimeter (Table 1 and 2):** 120.2 ft at 250 c.f.s., 114.9 ft at 200, 109.7 ft at 150, 104.6 ft at 100, and 95.4 ft at 50 c.f.s.
- **Total submerged area (illustrative):** 120.2 × 40,000 ft = 4,808,000 sq ft of bottom covered at 250 c.f.s.
- **Depth and cross-sectional area** closely parallel wetted perimeter in percentage reduction. Mean maximum depth was 6.16 ft at 250 c.f.s. and 5.10 ft at 50 c.f.s. Mean cross-sectional area was 340.6 sq ft at 250 c.f.s.
- **Mean velocity shows the largest reduction.** Mean velocity was 0.982 ft/s at 250 c.f.s. and 0.346 ft/s at 50 c.f.s. (about 35 percent of the 250 c.f.s. value). Velocity percentages in Table 1 were 88, 75, 58, and 35 at 200, 150, 100, and 50 c.f.s., respectively.
- **Velocity effects** are described as "really striking and significant," with likely effects on bottom organisms and on fish species differently.
- **Reference case at 3,500 c.f.s.:** Width 160.1 ft, maximum depth 10.2 ft, area 76.0 sq ft (as reported in the source; the velocity value was not legible in the OCR).
- **Temperature:** Maximum water temperature between dam and powerhouse was 71°F at 200 c.f.s., 70°F at 100 c.f.s., and 70.4°F at 50 c.f.s. (OCR-corrected from "704"). Cool accretion water eliminated a significant temperature difference among these flows.

**North Fork Feather River (25 stations, 500-foot intervals, Gansner Bar to Queen Lily Camp Ground)**

- **Gradient:** 12 feet per 1,000 in the survey section, a steeper and faster stream than the Pit.
- **Flows surveyed:** 140, 200, 300, and 800 c.f.s. The 800 c.f.s. flow approximates the estimated normal uncontrolled summer flow but was excluded from percentage calculations because it lies outside the Pit River range. Percentages were calculated for 300, 200, and 140 c.f.s.
- **Results:** Percentage patterns closely resembled those of the Pit River, and Curtis suggests this may hold for many fast-flowing mountain streams. The pattern would differ in flat, slow-moving rivers.

**Overall conclusions**

- The study supplies data for determining the minimum release below hydroelectric diversion dams for aquatic life. It shows that bottom habitat (wetted perimeter) is relatively resilient to flow reduction, whereas velocity and temperature responses are more pronounced and potentially more consequential for fish.
