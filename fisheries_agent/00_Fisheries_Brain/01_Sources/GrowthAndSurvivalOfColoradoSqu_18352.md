# Growth and Survival of Colorado Squawfish in the Upper Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study estimates growth and adult survival of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) in the upper [[Colorado River]] using fish captured from 1990 through 1995. It combines scale-based ageing for young fish with growth increments from recaptured, [[Passive Integrated Transponder Tag]]-marked adults. It then applies a modified [[Chapman-Robson Survival Method]] to estimate survival of fish ≥550 mm TL, and uses simulations to estimate age at length. The authors set these estimates as a baseline for future monitoring. They also argue that the historical absence of very large fish reflects a lower-basin origin of large individuals rather than a simple decline in growth or survival alone.

## Background & Description

Colorado squawfish is a long-lived cyprinid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]] and listed as endangered. Earlier growth and age estimates relied on [[Scale Analysis]], which the authors consider unreliable for old fish. Scales underestimate ages of long-lived individuals, and the first annulus often does not form in year one. Compression and loss of outer annuli further complicate ageing. Validation with known-age fish is scarce for non-game species. Earlier tag-based studies also raised the concern that external [[Carlin Dangler Tag]]s reduce growth, which would bias scale-versus-recapture comparisons.

The study area covered about 300 km of occupied habitat in the upper Colorado River, upstream of the [[Green River]] confluence. River kilometres (RK) are measured from the Green River confluence. Diversion dams at RK 298.1 and RK 303.0 block upstream movement, and the [[Gunnison River]] is blocked 3.5 km upstream of its mouth. [[Westwater Canyon]] (RK 181–200) divides the upper and lower reaches. Adults concentrate in the upper reach, while early life stages are mainly found in the lower reach. Westwater Canyon was not sampled.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** During spring runoff (late April to mid-June), adults occupy low-velocity, off-channel backwaters. Sampling targeted these sites with [[Trammel Netting]], using boat-driven "active" capture. Shorelines were also [[Electrofishing]]ed where backwaters were rare.
- **Temperature and growth:** A 13 °C threshold for growth is generally reached in the study area in March or April. The authors note this gives 1–3 months of extra growth before spring measurements compared with winter-based ageing.
- **Predators and mortality agents:** Great blue herons (*Ardea herodias*) and bald eagles (*Haliaeetus leucocephalus*) are likely long-standing sources of adult mortality. Introduced [[Channel Catfish]] are a newer hazard: catfish spines have lodged in squawfish pharynxes or oesophagi, and several large fish showed gular perforations or slits.
- **Human-related factors:** Gravel-pit ponds on the floodplain can trap fish when flows recede, causing stranding. Settler and resident angling and netting from the 1890s to 1950s probably raised mortality. Angling-related mortality is thought to have declined over the past 20 years.
- **Historical size:** Anecdotal accounts report fish of 1200–1800 mm TL, far larger than any captured in the past 20 years.

## Key Findings

**Growth from scales (ages 3–6):** Mean annual growth ranged from 82.0 mm/year (3-year-olds) to 32.2 mm/year (6-year-olds), with a coefficient of variation near 1.0.

**Growth from recaptures (adults):**
- Fish 400–449 mm TL grew 42.7 mm/year.
- Fish 500–549 mm TL grew 19.8 mm/year.
- Fish ≥550 mm TL grew 9.5 mm/year on average, varying from 4.2 to 12.8 mm/year by size group. These differences were not significant.
- Growth in one year did not predict growth in the next for most fish ≥400 mm TL.

**Age at length (simulation of 10,000 fish):**
- Length range at age was ±100 mm at age 10 and 200 mm at age 30.
- Ages for a given length differed by up to 24 years for 850 mm fish.
- Estimated ages: about 15 years for 600 mm (range 10–22), 25 years for 700 mm (16–30), and 32 years for 800 mm (20–40).
- A 900 mm fish has a minimum age of about 34 years, with an average of 47–55 years.

**Survival:** Survival of fish ≥550 mm TL was 0.83–0.87 (P < 0.05), with a best fit of 0.85. Pooling all years gave 0.82–0.87. Survival estimates were stable across years, and catch rates from 1991–1994 showed no significant change (Kruskal-Wallis P = 0.590).

**Rarity of large fish:** About 1,080 subadult and adult fish were captured during 1979–1995. Only three exceeded 900 mm TL (0.28%). The largest, 960 mm TL, was captured in 1990 by Utah Division of Wildlife Resources biologists.

**Comparison with earlier studies:**
- Mean annual growth of recaptured fish ≥450 mm TL was 14.4 mm (SE = 1.2, N = 121). For fish ≥500 mm TL it was 11.9 mm (SE = 1.0, N = 104).
- These values match the 10–15 mm/year reported for Carlin-tagged fish, suggesting dangler tags had little effect on growth.
- The 30 mm/year scale-based increment reported by [[Hawkins 1992]] appears too high for fish above 500 mm TL.
- Survival of 0.83–0.87 exceeds the 0.81 estimated by [[Gilpin 1993]] for the Green River population, though the ranges may overlap.

**Explanations for lost large fish:** The authors reject two earlier hypotheses, slower growth (linked to loss of [[Bonytail]]) and lower survival (linked to angling). They propose that very large fish grew in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]], where longer seasons gave 1.5–2.3 times the thermal units of the upper basin, then moved upstream. Dams and extirpation of lower-basin populations would then explain their recent absence.

**Method notes:** The survival method is not a substitute for [[Jolly-Seber Model]] analysis. It suits situations with long-term population structure data but not consistent capture histories. Electrofishing over-represented large fish, so only trammel-net data were used for survival estimates.

**Management implications:** The authors present these values as a baseline for monitoring. They recommend future studies use PIT tags to avoid dangler-tag bias, and they flag channel catfish predation as a likely significant mortality source needing further study.
