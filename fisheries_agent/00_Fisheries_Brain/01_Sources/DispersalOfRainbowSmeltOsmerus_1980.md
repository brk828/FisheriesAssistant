# Dispersal of Rainbow Smelt (*Osmerus mordax*) into the Upper Mississippi River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #osmeridae

## Executive Summary
This note documents the first confirmed occurrence of [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) in the [[Mississippi River]] outside of the [[Great Lakes]] watershed, based on three winter collections (December 1978) from Illinois localities (Grand Tower, Chester, and Columbia). The authors argue that the smelt did not originate from a direct introduction, but instead dispersed naturally from [[Lake Michigan]] via the [[Illinois River]], using the [[Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal]] as a dispersal corridor during periods of high water and canal dilution. Meristic analysis of 10 specimens confirmed that the Mississippi River population is referable to the nominal subspecies *Osmerus mordax mordax*, the same form historically introduced into the Great Lakes in the early 1900s. The study raises concerns about continued range expansion of this cold-water species into the Mississippi drainage, contingent on winter temperatures and canal discharge patterns.

## Background & Description
Meristic counts from 10 specimens collected from the Mississippi River were used to determine subspecific identity per [[McAllister 1963]] criteria:

| Character | Range | Mean (X̄) |
|---|---|---|
| Pored lateral line scales | 14–17 | 15.6 |
| Midlateral scales | 47–50 | 48.0 |
| Dorsal rays | 9–10 | 9.6 |
| Anal rays | 14–16 | 14.9 |
| Gill rakers | 32–35 | 33.5 |
| Total vertebrae (incl. urostyle; n=20) | 62–65 | 62.9 |

- **Standard length range (104 specimens, December collections):** 41–86 mm, mean = 58.7 mm.
- **Age structure (via scale annuli):** individuals aged between 0+ and 1+ years, indicating a young population dominated by juveniles/sub-adults.
- These meristic values allocate all Mississippi River specimens to the nominal subspecies **[[Osmerus mordax mordax]]** (North American Atlantic smelt), matching the form historically introduced into the [[Great Lakes]] in the early 1900s, and consistent with stock from the Illinois portion of Lake Michigan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Typical habitat:** *O. mordax* normally inhabits midwaters of lakes and inshore coastal waters, moving into flowing streams/rivers only during spawning ([[Scott and Crossman 1973]]).
- **Observed habitat in Mississippi River:** Found exclusively over hard-packed sand bottom, at depths of 15 cm–1.5 m; captured in both strong current (Grand Tower) and quieter water (Chester).
- **Water temperature at capture:** 3–6°C (4°C at Grand Tower, 6°C near Chester on 21 Dec; 3°C near Columbia on 24 Dec) — consistent with the species' strong preference for cold water.
- **Light sensitivity / diel behavior:** *O. mordax* is photosensitive ([[Ferguson 1965]]), concentrating near the bottom in deep water during daylight; this may explain higher near-shore catch rates at dusk (Chester, 1630–1730 hr) versus midday (Grand Tower, 1300–1600 hr).
- **Co-occurring species:** *Dorosoma cepedianum*, three species of *Hybopsis*, three species of *Notropis*, *Pimephales vigilax*, and *Ictalurus punctatus*, among a total of >35 fish species known from the sampled reach. Despite this diversity, smelt were the **most common species** captured on the dates sampled.
- **Diet (stomach content analysis, n=10):** 29% Ephemeroptera (mayfly larvae/nymphs), 71% Copepoda — indicating an opportunistic invertivorous diet dominated by planktonic crustaceans.
- **Thermal tolerance limits ecological persistence:** Authors predict *O. mordax* is unlikely to survive summer conditions in the Mississippi River due to preference for cold water and avoidance of free-flowing water outside spawning season, suggesting the population may currently be restricted to seasonal (winter) residency.

## Key Findings
- **First record** of *Osmerus mordax* naturally occurring in the Mississippi River outside the Great Lakes watershed, based on collections from three localities in Illinois in December 1978 (57 specimens at Grand Tower, 55 near Chester, 2 near Columbia), plus a subsequent 1979 collection of 91 more specimens (Grand Tower, Fayville, and near Wickliffe, Kentucky).
- **Dispersal route hypothesis:** The [[Chicago Sanitary and Ship Canal]] connects Lake Michigan to the Illinois River, and during high-water events, pollution dilution allows Great Lakes fish species (including smelt) to disperse into the Illinois River and subsequently the Mississippi River.
- **Supporting precedent:** A single *O. mordax* specimen (60 mm SL) was previously collected from the Illinois River at Pekin in 1966, corroborating the Illinois River as the dispersal corridor. Additionally, [[Pungitius pungitius]] (ninespine stickleback), formerly thought restricted to Lake Michigan in Illinois, has similarly been found at two Illinois River localities — reinforcing the pattern of Lake Michigan taxa using the canal system to invade the Illinois-Mississippi system.
- **Direct introduction ruled out**: The number of specimens and their occurrence across three widely separated localities makes an unauthorized/direct introduction into the Mississippi River highly improbable.
- **Rapid dispersal timeline:** No smelt were captured during a September 1978 sampling of the same Mississippi River reach, implying the species dispersed from Lake Michigan (or an intermediate point on the Illinois River) to the Mississippi River within approximately 3 months.
- **Possible climatic driver:** The authors speculate that severe winter conditions over the preceding two years may have facilitated this recent range expansion.
- **Future monitoring concern:** Anticipated increases in Chicago Sanitary Canal water diversion in 1979 could further facilitate smelt escape from Lake Michigan, potentially establishing at least a **winter-resident population** in the upper Mississippi River. A monitoring program was established to track persistence and further spread.
- **Taxonomic conclusion:** Mississippi River smelt are meristically consistent with *[[Osmerus mordax mordax]]*, the same subspecies originally introduced into the Great Lakes, confirming the population's Great Lakes origin rather than a distinct or native Atlantic Coast lineage.
