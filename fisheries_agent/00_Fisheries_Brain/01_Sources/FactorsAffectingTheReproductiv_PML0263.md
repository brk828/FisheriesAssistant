# Factors Affecting the Reproductive Success of Razorback Suckers and Bonytail Chubs in Lake Mohave, Arizona-Nevada, 1982-1983

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This Master of Science thesis by Michael Anthony Bozek (University of Nevada, Las Vegas, 1984) examines reproduction of the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) in [[Lake Mohave]] during the 1982-1983 spawning seasons. Lake Mohave holds the largest known Razorback Sucker population and the last known concentration of Bonytail Chub in the Colorado River System. The Bonytail population is very low and probably nearing extinction, while Razorback Suckers spawn abundantly over extensive, unique gravel shelves. Successful spawning and hatching occur across ambient reservoir temperatures, but desiccation from water-level fluctuations and wave-driven siltation appear to be the major sources of egg and larval mortality. Predation is believed to be the principal limit on recruitment, though the study found little direct evidence of predation on eggs or larvae.

## Background & Description

The document is a thesis (title page, approval page, abstract, table of contents, acknowledgments, introduction, methods, results, discussion, literature cited, and three appendices). It does not describe a field survey of the whole ecosystem; its focus is on two native catostomid and cyprinid fishes and the physical and biological factors governing their spawning.

- **Status of the species:** Both species were once widespread in the Colorado River and have declined over the past 50 years. Razorback Suckers remain rare in most of the upper basin, and Bonytail Chubs are thought to be extirpated from most of the lower river.
- **Historical context:** [[Hoover Dam]] (completed 1935) stabilized flows, reduced sediment loads, and converted the river to cold, clear water. [[Davis Dam]] (completed 1951) formed Lake Mohave, flooding roughly 73 km² of desert and creating about 322 km of new shoreline.
- **Study objectives:** Determine the distributions and abundances of both species, test whether successful spawning occurs, and identify the primary factors affecting spawning success.
- **Study area:** The reservoir extends about 109 km south from Hoover Dam to Davis Dam. Water levels fluctuate about 5 m annually. Mean annual temperature is about 19 °C, and annual precipitation is about 8 cm.
- **Methods:** Shoreline cove surveys (February 1982), trammel netting at twelve locations, nighttime dip-net larval collections, laboratory temperature-development experiments (using [[Thermal Units]]), depth-distribution analysis of [[Redd]]s and water-level data, and non-parametric statistics (Kruskal-Wallis, Mann-Whitney U, Chi-square) run in SPSS.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Hydrology and temperature:** Discharge from Hoover Dam is hypolimnetic, keeping Black Canyon water at 12-13 °C. Reservoir temperatures during spawning ranged from 10-22 °C. Water levels rise in winter and spring, flooding terrestrial vegetation.
- **Geomorphology and substrate:** Gravel from desert washes became the dominant shoreline substrate. Summer drawdowns and wave action create flat, shelf-like gravel terraces. The Six Mile Coves area (Cottonwood Basin) and Arizona Bay (Little Basin) contain the largest spawning gravel flats.
- **Wind and wave exposure:** The reservoir's long north-south fetch and strong prevailing winds generate severe seasonal wave action, especially in the open basins.
- **Vegetation and cover:** The shoreline is Mojave Desert scrub with tamarisk, while submerged beds of *Myriophyllum*, *Chara* and *Potamogeton* occur offshore.
- **Invertebrates and food web:** Aquatic insects declined after the river was converted to a reservoir. Oligochaetes, midges and amphipods now dominate the invertebrate fauna of Black Canyon.
- **Other fishes:** Common Carp, Channel Catfish, Largemouth Bass, Rainbow Trout, Striped Bass and Threadfin Shad are present. Razorback Sucker × Flannelmouth Sucker hybrids also occur.

## Key Findings

**Population status and distribution**
- Lake Mohave holds the largest known Razorback Sucker population. Thousands of spawning adults were observed in the Six Mile Coves and Arizona Bay areas, with other spawning sites at Yuma Cove, Great West Cove, Tequila Cove, Nine Mile Coves and Three Mile Flats.
- Trammel netting produced 525 males and 307 females, all tagged or fin-clipped. Only seven were recaptured (one a recapture from a previous study), a rate that the author interprets as indicating a large population.
- Only eight Bonytail Chubs were captured in the whole study (all in the Six Mile Coves area, March-April 1982). Minckley (1983) had recorded 18 in eight years. The population is considered very low.

**Spawning timing and conditions**
- Spawning ran from November through May. Peak spawning occurred from January through March at 10.7-17.5 °C. Males arrived earlier than females.
- Over 50% of males and females examined were ripe during peak spawning. Condition factors declined in March, marking the start of decline in the peak period.

**Egg and larval development**
- Hatching success was high across 12-20 °C, reaching about 60% in artificially spawned eggs. Hatching at 8 °C did not occur successfully, and 10 °C produced reduced success. The thermal minimum for the population was estimated at about 8 °C.
- Hatching times were 17.5-22.1 days at 10 °C, 10.7-11.4 days at 15 °C, and 6.6-7.0 days at 20 °C.
- Hundreds of protolarvae were collected at night in the Six Mile Coves and Arizona Bay areas in January-March 1982-1983 (mean total length about 11 mm). Larvae were never observed during the day, which suggests nocturnal behavior and a benthic habit until they migrate.

**Mortality factors**
- Water-level fluctuations: Up to 70% of the spawn may have been decimated by drawdowns in February and March of 1982 and 1983. Eggs were exposed to possible stranding for up to 20 days during the colder months.
- Wind and waves: A severe March 1983 storm caused silt to cover redds and led to the complete abandonment of the Six Mile Coves area. Experimental enclosures with silt cleared from the substrate showed about 100% egg mortality after siltation.
- Predation: Egg remains were found in only four of 63 catfish sampled. No eggs were found in carp stomachs, though carp were observed feeding in redds. The author concludes predation is probably not the major source of egg mortality, but cannot rule out larval predation.

**Health and population structure**
- Eye afflictions were common. The author found 3% blind in one eye and 1% blind in both eyes, compared with Minckley's (1983) reported 29.9% and 6.9%.
- Bacterial infections peaked during spawning, and infection rates were significantly higher in females than males (p < .001).
- Males averaged 554.4 mm total length (1982) and females 628.2 mm (1982). Females were significantly larger than males (Mann-Whitney U, p = .001).
- Of 17 otolith-aged fish, ages were reported at 30-52 years. The author questions this, arguing that the size and health of fish point to recruitment.

**Recruitment and management implications**
- The author argues that recruitment is occurring. Juvenile razorbacks were not captured, but sampling methods were biased toward adults. The author recommends summer sampling, diving surveys, and an accurate aging technique.
- Gravel spawning substrate in Lake Mohave is regenerated by desert-wash inputs and drawdown wave action, making it unique among Colorado River reservoirs.
- Bonytail Chub restocking may be the only way to prevent extirpation from Lake Mohave.
- An appended hatchery letter (1983) reports 318 two-year-old Razorback Suckers from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (190 females, 128 males), with males averaging 392 mm and 706 g, and females averaging 404 mm and 859 g.
