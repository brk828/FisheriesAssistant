## Executive Summary

This document is a formal comment letter from American Rivers (a river conservation NGO) addressing the Washington County Water Conservancy District's Draft Virgin River Habitat Conservation and Management Plan (HCMP). While not a primary research paper, it synthesizes and critiques the scientific basis used to justify water development actions affecting three federally significant native fishes of the [[Virgin River]]: the [[Virgin Chub]], [[Woundfin]], and [[Virgin Spinedace]]. The letter raises substantive concerns about methodology (particularly reliance on depth/velocity habitat modeling), the proposed removal of high-TDS discharge from [[Pah Tempe Springs]], and the adequacy of monitoring and adaptive management frameworks intended to support species recovery amid continued water development in the [[Washington County]] region.

## Background & Description

The source text does not provide direct morphological descriptions (e.g., meristic counts, body shape, coloration) of the three native fish species. However, it does note **species-specific ecological/physiological tolerances** relevant to habitat characteristics:

- **[[Virgin Spinedace]]**: Documented preference for **clear water** (low total dissolved solids, TDS), implying sensitivity to turbidity/mineral loading.
- **[[Woundfin]]** and **[[Virgin Chub]]**: Adapted to, and apparently reliant upon, conditions of elevated TDS historically contributed by [[Pah Tempe Springs]] discharge into the river. This suggests physiological tolerance to (or ecological dependence on) higher mineral/saline conditions relative to spinedace.

No quantitative morphometric data (length, weight, fin ray counts) are included in this document.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **System**: Virgin River, a desert riverine system in the Colorado River Basin (Washington County, Utah / Arizona border region), including the reach from [[Hurricane Bridge]] to the [[Washington Fields Diversion]], and further downstream past [[Quail Creek]] and [[Quail Creek Reservoir]].
- **Habitat drivers under debate**:
  - **Depth and velocity** — used by Dr. Thom Hardy's studies as primary determinants of "habitat availability," but the letter argues these are only two of many abiotic factors and are **not proven determinants of population abundance**.
  - **Total Dissolved Solids (TDS)** — a key water chemistry variable historically elevated by natural discharge from [[Pah Tempe Springs]]. The native fish community evolved under these high-TDS conditions.
  - **Flow volume** — Minimum flow requirements (e.g., 86 cfs, with a proposed reduction to 50 cfs in winter) are central to non-jeopardy determinations for endangered species, with a dispute over the correct **measurement location** (Hurricane Bridge vs. Washington Fields Diversion) to ensure flow integrity is maintained along the full reach.
- **Interspecies contrast in water quality niche**:
  - [[Virgin Spinedace]] is associated with **clearer, lower-TDS water** and might benefit from removal of Pah Tempe Springs discharge.
  - [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin Chub]] populations may **decline** if high-TDS water from the springs is diverted, suggesting a niche partitioning where these species are adapted to the naturally mineralized conditions of the historic river reach.
- **Historical/anthropogenic context**: Native fishes have persisted alongside diversions and dams for over 100 years, though this longevity is explicitly challenged in the letter as insufficient evidence that current or future water development is compatible with species recovery.
- **Referenced empirical study**: Gregory and Deacon (1994) analyzed fish abundance from Pah Tempe Springs to Quail Creek and found that current reach operations have contributed to a **decline in native fish populations**, contradicting habitat-model-based conclusions of "no take."

## Key Findings

1. **Habitat modeling critique**: The Plan's near-exclusive reliance on Dr. Thom Hardy's depth/velocity flow studies is scientifically insufficient and methodologically unverified within the broader desert fish research community; depth and velocity are not shown to correlate directly with population abundance.
2. **Pah Tempe Springs removal risk**: Proposed piping of high-TDS discharge from [[Pah Tempe Springs]] to below the [[Washington Fields Diversion]] may **increase [[Virgin Spinedace]] abundance** downstream but could **decrease populations of [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin Chub]]**, producing an uncertain net effect on overall habitat quality rather than a clear improvement.
3. **Flow reduction concerns**: A proposed winter flow reduction from **86 cfs to 50 cfs** (based on Hardy's feasibility conclusion) was used to support a "no take" determination, which the letter argues is not adequately substantiated, especially in light of the Gregory and Deacon (1994) findings of ongoing population decline under current conditions.
4. **Flow measurement location discrepancy**: The Plan measures the required 86 cfs flow at the Washington Fields Diversion, while the governing non-jeopardy opinion for [[Quail Creek]] specifies measurement at **Hurricane Bridge** — a discrepancy that could allow insufficient flow to persist through the full reach critical to fish survival.
5. **Underutilized data resources**: Substantial existing data — including the **Virgin River Fishes Database** and pre/post-1985 surge flow data collected by **Terry Hickman** and the Virgin River Fishes Recovery Team — have not been fully analyzed to assess TDS impacts, flow-volume relationships, and pre/post-[[Quail Creek Reservoir]] construction effects on fish abundance and population structure.
6. **Recommendation for independent scientific review**: The letter calls for a workshop of independent native fish experts to design analyses, an independent modeler to execute them, and an ongoing adaptive management framework linking monitoring data directly to operational and management changes — none of which the draft Plan currently provides for.
7. **Governance gaps**: The Plan concentrates administrative and review authority within the Washington County Water Conservancy District, lacks meaningful public involvement mechanisms, and its Technical Advisory Committee includes no independent academic or environmental technical expertise.
