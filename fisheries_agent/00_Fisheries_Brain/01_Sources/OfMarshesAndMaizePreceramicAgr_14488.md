# Of Marshes and Maize: Preceramic Agricultural Settlements in the Cienega Valley, Southeastern Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #archaeology #paleoethnobotany #early-agricultural-period

## Executive Summary

This monograph (Anthropological Papers of the University of Arizona No. 59, 1995) by [[Bruce B. Huckell]] reports 1983 excavations at two buried late preceramic sites in Matty Canyon, a tributary of [[Cienega Creek]] in southeastern Arizona: the [[Donaldson Site]] (AZ EE:2:30 ASM) and [[Los Ojitos]] (AZ EE:2:137 ASM). The work argues that these settlements show sedentary, agriculture-supported village life well before pottery, and proposes replacing the term "Late Archaic" with the [[Early Agricultural Period]], subdivided into the [[San Pedro Phase]] and [[Cienega Phase]]. The volume is a field and laboratory report, not a fisheries study; no fish or aquatic vertebrate taxa are reported. Its aquatic-related content concerns the riparian and cienega environment, including marsh plants, freshwater mollusks and amphibians.

## Background & Description

The monograph was written by Bruce B. Huckell with contributors Lisa W. Huckell, Penny Dufoe Minturn and Lorrie Lincoln-Babb, and published by the University of Arizona Press in 1995. A foreword by William J. Robinson notes that [[Frank W. Eddy]] first excavated AZ EE:2:30 in 1955 to 1957 as a master's thesis, and that his findings, including a midden about 140 m long and roughly 0.40 m deep, conflicted with the mobile hunter-gatherer model of the [[San Pedro Stage]] of the [[Cochise Culture]].

The sites were rediscovered after 1980 flooding eroded the arroyo banks. The Donaldson Site was named for lessee John Donaldson, who reported a burial to [[Emil W. Haury]] in 1982. Excavation occurred in May and June 1983, with a further flood-exposed salvage in October 1983. Work covered 48 m² at the Donaldson Site and under 9 m² at Los Ojitos.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Cienega Creek Basin is a north-south trending Basin-and-Range valley, largely semidesert grassland, bordered by the Santa Rita, Empire, Whetstone and Canelo ranges. Cienega Creek is ephemeral in its upper reaches and perennial over the lower two-thirds to three-quarters of its length. The name derives from Spanish *cienega*, groundwater-supported marsh.

Key biotic communities include:
- Riparian cottonwood-willow gallery forest and cienega marsh (cattail, rushes, sedges)
- Semidesert grassland (sacaton, grama)
- Madrean oak woodland (Emory oak, junipers) at higher elevations
- Montane conifer forest above about 2,061 m

Annual precipitation at Elgin is about 35.5 cm (1941 to 1970 mean), with 62.5 percent falling in the summer rainy season, which coincides with the growing season of New World cultigens. Eddy's work and pollen studies by [[Paul Schultz Martin]] indicate that cienegas persisted through the sequence of alluvial units. Arroyo cutting after about 1890, linked to overgrazing and drought, transformed shallow cienega channels into deep, steep-walled arroyos. Matty Canyon sites were preserved by rapid burial and a high water table.

Radiocarbon dating places the cultural deposits in roughly the 800 B.C. to A.D. 200 range. The alluvial sequence shows floodplain aggradation over about 4,000 years.

## Key Findings

**Site characteristics**
- The Donaldson Site midden is about 140 m long, averages 0.40 m thick (range 0.25 to 1.00 m), and is dark gray, clayey, and organically rich.
- Twenty-six cultural features were uncovered at the Donaldson Site, including two pit structures (Features 11 and 17), six human burials, seven rock clusters, nine pits and one roasting pit.
- Los Ojitos deposit was 0.30 to 0.90 m thick, with six single inhumations, one multiple secondary burial (at least four individuals) and four rock clusters.

**Dating**
- Donaldson Site calibrated dates (1 sigma) range from about 750 B.C. to 390 B.C. (wood charcoal) and 790 B.C. to 410 B.C. (maize, AMS).
- Los Ojitos dates (2 sigma) span about 810 B.C. to A.D. 130, but stratigraphy places Los Ojitos older, by about 200 years, than the Donaldson Site.
- Peat marker beds M1 and M2 help correlate the two sites.

**Subsistence and plant remains**
- Flotation of 14 samples yielded more than 10,000 macrofossils (estimated 10,145 at the Donaldson Site; about 8,854 estimated at Los Ojitos). Initial results identified 29 genera from 23 families.
- Maize is the only positively identified cultigen; squash (*Cucurbita*) may be present.
- Maize was found in every Donaldson feature and in all three Los Ojitos levels. Estimated abundance at the Donaldson Site averaged about 124 macrofossils per liter (279 estimated).
- Wild plants include grasses (*Sporobolus*), sedges, acorns (*Quercus emoryi*), walnut, mesquite, juniper, cheno-ams, purslane, agave, yucca, saguaro, prickly pear and sumac.
- Agave tissue and teeth represent the earliest documented mescal use in a southeastern Arizona preceramic context.
- Cupule analysis suggests small, early maize with 12- and 14-row cobs predominating at Los Ojitos.

**Artifacts**
- Donaldson Site: 299 flaked stone implements (bifacial 42.1%, unifacial 39.1%); 46 projectile points, of which 27 are [[Cienega Point]] style (77.1% of classified points).
- Cienega points show narrower stems than San Pedro points (mean maximum stem width 11.59 mm vs. 22.17 mm at Milagro).
- Unusual items include 25 wedges, 69 ground stone specimens including perforated stone rings, trays, and discoidals, and five shell items.
- Debitage averaged 197 artifacts per square meter in the midden. Tool production was significant at both sites.

**Burials**
- Donaldson Site: five preceramic burials (four flexed, one extended), mostly adult males and females.
- Los Ojitos: ten individuals across seven burials, with a cemetery-like clustering along about 10 m of the bank.
- Flexed interment was the predominant mode of treatment.

**Fauna**
- Donaldson Site sample: 108 identified specimens in 1983 (about 575 bones total, 81% unidentifiable scrap); 153 in Eddy's 1955-1957 sample.
- Mule deer dominated large mammals; jackrabbits (NISP 30) and cottontails (NISP 18) represented leporids. Other taxa include bighorn sheep, pronghorn, gray fox, coyote, cotton rat, pocket gopher, Woodhouse's toad, turtle and heron.
- Artiodactyl index 0.66; lagomorph index 0.27.

**Pathology and diet**
- Porotic hyperostosis occurred in four Los Ojitos females, consistent with maize-dependent diets.
- Caries frequency among five Los Ojitos primary inhumations was 9.9% (14 of 142 teeth).
- Dental wear angles suggested a mixed or agricultural economy.

**Conclusions**
- The author argues that maize and squash were present and integral to subsistence, and that these settlements were sedentary and village-like well before ceramics.
- The author proposes the [[Early Agricultural Period]] (about 1500 or 1200 B.C. to A.D. 200), with the [[San Pedro Phase]] (about 1500 to 500 B.C.) and [[Cienega Phase]] (500 B.C. to A.D. 200), replacing "Late Archaic" for agriculturally based preceramic sites.
- The volume describes the Cienega phase and its assemblages as a distinct cultural unit in southeastern Arizona.
