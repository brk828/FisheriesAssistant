# Letter from Temple Reynolds to Clifford Barrett Regarding Glen Canyon Dam Conservation Measures

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #ConservationBiology #EndangeredSpecies

## Executive Summary
This 1988 letter from the Arizona Game & Fish Department (AGFD) to the U.S. Bureau of Reclamation addresses a dispute over the scope of Conservation Measures being drafted as part of the Biological Opinion for [[Glen Canyon Dam]] operations. While the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Consultation Team had narrowed draft measures to address only the [[Humpback Chub]], AGFD argued this contradicted earlier agreements (dated October 16, 1987) to protect four species: [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], Humpback Chub, and the peregrine falcon — later expanded to include the [[Razorback Sucker]]. The letter emphasizes that recovery data generated from Lower Basin (Gila River Basin) stocking programs has direct scientific utility for Upper Basin conservation efforts, given the impracticality of studying sparse wild Upper Basin populations. The document also raises non-fish conservation concerns (peregrine falcon and bald eagle habitat use in the Grand Canyon) tied indirectly to river/riparian insect prey-base health, which is itself influenced by dam-regulated flows.

## Background & Description
*No direct morphological data is provided in this correspondence.* The letter is administrative/policy-focused rather than a primary morphological study. However, it implicitly references life-history stage variation:
- Native large-river fishes discussed ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Humpback Chub]]) are members of two distinct families: minnows (Cyprinidae) and suckers (Catostomidae), both endemic to the Colorado River Basin and adapted to historically turbid, warm, fluctuating riverine conditions altered by dam regulation.
- Note is made that **not all life history stages** (e.g., larval, juvenile, adult) are currently detectable in remaining natural Upper Basin populations, but are present and observable in Lower Basin stocked populations — implying morphological/ontogenetic staging data gaps in the wild Upper Basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat context**: The species of concern inhabit the mainstem [[Colorado River]] and its tributaries, spanning the Upper Basin (Utah/Colorado region) and Lower Basin (Arizona, including the [[Gila River Basin]] and [[Grand Canyon]] reaches below Glen Canyon Dam).
- **Population status disparity**: Upper Basin natural populations of these fishes exist at **low densities**, resulting in high variance and limited statistical power for habitat and life-history studies. In contrast, AGFD's large-scale stocking program in the Lower Basin (Gila River Basin) is producing recoverable individuals of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in sufficient numbers to build robust habitat preference datasets.
- **Homing/imprinting hypothesis**: AGFD planned studies to test whether Colorado squawfish **imprint on chemical cues at incubation sites** and exhibit **natal homing behavior** (analogous to Pacific salmon) for reproduction — a hypothesis with direct implications for spawning habitat protection and species recovery planning in both Upper and Lower Basins. This research question was flagged as a priority in the (then-current) draft Colorado squawfish recovery plan.
- **Indirect trophic linkages**: The letter draws an ecological connection between river/riparian insect populations (supported by aquatic and riparian habitats shaped by dam flow regimes) and the terrestrial/avian food web — specifically, insects support the [[White-throated Swift]], which is the primary prey of the [[Peregrine Falcon]] in the Grand Canyon region. This illustrates a cross-taxon dependency chain: **Dam flow regime → aquatic/riparian insects → swifts → peregrine falcons**.
- **Avian habitat association**: [[Bald Eagle]] use of the Grand Canyon (notably a newly observed concentration near Nankoweap in late 1987) is hypothesized to be linked to fledgling survival and recruitment from the broader southwestern breeding population (31 known nests in Arizona/Sonora, Mexico), suggesting the canyon may function as important post-fledging or wintering habitat contingent on dam-regulated river conditions.

## Key Findings
- **Scope dispute**: Draft Biological Opinion Conservation Measures (as of April 1988) addressed only the Humpback Chub, despite a prior (Oct. 1987) interagency agreement to cover four species: Bonytail Chub, Colorado Squawfish, Humpback Chub, and Peregrine Falcon (later Razorback Sucker and Bald Eagle were added per a March 7, 1988 letter listing 10 candidate Conservation Measures).
- **Lower Basin data utility**: AGFD's Gila River Basin stocking program for Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker is beginning to yield recoverable wild individuals, enabling large-scale habitat preference datasets that are impossible to obtain from sparse Upper Basin natural populations.
- **Novel research hypothesis**: A proposed AGFD study aims to test **chemical imprinting and natal homing behavior** in Colorado Squawfish, a question deemed critical to species recovery basin-wide and already emphasized in the latest draft recovery plan.
- **Four proposed Conservation Measures** put forward by AGFD:
  1. Assist recovery efforts for Bonytail Chub, Colorado Squawfish, and Razorback Sucker in the Lower Basin.
  2. Survey the inner gorge of the Grand Canyon for nesting Peregrine Falcons (diet and productivity data).
  3. Investigate diet/habitat requirements of White-throated Swifts and other peregrine prey species, emphasizing riparian/aquatic habitats.
  4. Document origin and habitat use of Bald Eagles currently residing in the Grand Canyon.
- **Cross-basin conservation argument**: The letter is a clear historical example of using experimental/stocked population data from one river basin (Gila) to inform recovery planning for genetically/ecologically related wild populations in another basin (Upper Colorado), a strategy justified by the impracticality of large-scale experimentation on endangered wild populations.
- **Emerging ecological uncertainty**: The letter documents (as of 1987–88) newly discovered concentrations of Bald Eagles near Nankoweap in the Grand Canyon, representing an unresolved conservation question regarding population origin and habitat dependency at the time of the Biological Opinion's drafting.
