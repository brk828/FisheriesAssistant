# Life History and Status of the Shorthead Sculpin (*Cottus confusus*) in Canada and Its Sympatric Relationship with the Slimy Sculpin (*Cottus cognatus*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cottidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Grant W. Hughes and Alex E. Peden of the [[British Columbia Provincial Museum]] (published in *Canadian Journal of Zoology*, 1984) examines the [[Shorthead Sculpin]] in its only known Canadian range, the [[Flathead River]] drainage of southeastern British Columbia. The population sits at the northeastern edge of the species' range and is considered threatened, mainly because proposed [[Coal Mining]] development could destroy the downstream habitat it occupies. The authors also address a practical problem: the shorthead sculpin is easily confused with the sympatric [[Slimy Sculpin]] in the field. They therefore built a diagnostic character index and discriminant analyses that reliably separate the two species, and they describe their habitat segregation, distribution, age structure, and diet.

## Background & Description

- **Taxonomy and identity:** Both species belong to the family Cottidae (sculpins). *Cottus confusus* is distinguished chiefly by the presence of palatine teeth, papillate head, a single median occipital pore, and a higher number of dorsal soft rays. Palatine teeth are considered the most reliable character, but they are often obscured by overlying tissue, so the authors recommend external characters that are easier to see.
- **Range:** Outside Canada, the species occurs in the Puget Sound, Columbia River, and Snake River drainages of Washington, Oregon, and Idaho. Canadian populations are therefore at the northeastern periphery of its geographic range.
- **Prior knowledge:** Only 26 Canadian specimens of *C. confusus* were known before 1981. Information on the Montana portion of the Flathead drainage was limited and largely unpublished.
- **Field identification problem:** Earlier British Columbia field parties may have recorded *C. confusus* as *C. cognatus*. Morphometric characters such as anal ray count and pelvic ray length overlap between the two species, which motivated the study.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and water conditions**
- The Canadian Flathead River lies above 1200 m elevation.
- Water temperatures range from 0 °C (ice conditions, December–January) to 20 °C in August.
- Discharge ranges from 2.2 to 233 m³·s⁻¹ (May–December), based on 1976 monitoring at the international border.
- Shoreline and bottom type in August 1981 were mostly cobble and smooth stones, with little cover except interspaces between stones.
- Surface water temperature and flow rate did not differ significantly between sites dominated by either species (t-test, α = 0.05).

**Distribution and habitat segregation**
- *C. confusus* was generally found farther downstream, below about 1372 m elevation.
- *C. cognatus* predominated in the main river upstream of the confluence with [[Middlepass Creek]], and this pattern was confirmed by an October 1979 census.
- Capture rates were much higher at upstream *C. cognatus* sites than at downstream *C. confusus* sites.
- Sites were classified by species predominance, with 81% or more of catches belonging to one species.

**Associated fauna**
- Only five young [[Bull Trout]] (*Salvelinus confluentus*) and one small [[Westslope Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarkii lewisi*) were captured alongside the sculpins.
- Mountain whitefish (*Prosopium williamsoni*) and largescale sucker (*Catostomus macrocheilus*) are also known from the Canadian Flathead drainage.

**Diet**
- Stomachs contained mainly aquatic insects, all larvae except one pupa, plus two nematodes. One specimen contained fish remains.
- Common prey included Siphlonuridae (mayflies), Chironomidae (midges), Chloroperlidae (stoneflies), and Dytiscidae (diving beetles).
- Siphlonuridae occurred in 75% of *C. confusus* stomachs from allopatric sites (N = 8).
- Ephemeroptera were absent from *C. cognatus* stomachs, but sample sizes were considered too small to attribute ecological importance to this.

**Threats**
- The [[Sage Creek Coal Project]] proposed for the Flathead Valley could seriously damage the few known Canadian streams with shorthead sculpins.
- *C. confusus* is more at risk than *C. cognatus* if coal mining begins along [[Cabin Creek]], especially if [[Howell Creek]] is diverted into the Flathead River upstream.
- Fish in the upper river could move downstream in winter, when flows are minimal, and be exposed to pollution from the mine.
- Sage Creek may provide a refuge.

## Key Findings

**1. Conservation status**
- The [[COSEWIC]] subcommittee for Fish and Marine Mammals recommended threatened status for the Canadian population, based on the authors' 1982 status report.
- Over 400 specimens were collected in August 1981, a large increase over the 26 previously known Canadian specimens.
- The sampled sites represent probably less than 1% of the apparently suitable habitat in the lower Flathead River. The authors estimate the sampled size range population may have exceeded 40 000 fish.

**2. Diagnostic character index**
- Six characters were combined into a [[Character Index]] (palatine teeth, anal ray number, head papillae, occipital pores, pelvic ratio, and dorsal soft ray number).
- The index shows a clear separation between species: *C. confusus* (N = 79) and *C. cognatus* (N = 40).
- [[Discriminant Analysis]] was run on 38 *C. confusus* and 41 *C. cognatus*. The number of median occipital pores alone gave 87.3% correct classification.
- Combining median occipital pores with dorsal soft ray number raised correct classification to over 96%.
- A five-variable model (occipital pores, dorsal soft rays, pelvic ratio, snout ratio, and head ratio) gave 97.5% correct classification, with 0% of *C. confusus* misclassified.
- Discriminant analysis is considered robust despite departures from multivariate normality and equal covariance assumptions.

**3. Field identification**
- Palatine teeth are the key character, but they are hard to see without magnification. The occipital pore count, which also requires some magnification, is almost as reliable and easier to see, especially in small fish.
- The papillate head is a useful preliminary field marker for large specimens.
- The utility of the occipital pore character may be local, since the proportion of *C. cognatus* with two pores is higher in the Flathead River than in some other British Columbia drainages.

**4. Life history**
- Length frequencies of the two species were similar.
- Age was determined from otoliths in 15 specimens:
  - Young of the year: 37.0 ± 1.4 mm SL (N = 5)
  - One-year-old females: 48.6 ± 2.6 mm SL (N = 6)
  - One-year-old males: 64.4 ± 4.9 mm SL (N = 3)
  - One two-year-old female: 71.4 mm SL
- Males grew faster than females in the Flathead River, although a larger sample is needed to confirm this.
- Males outnumbered females 74 to 49 in 123 sexed specimens, a sex ratio that changed with size. This contrasts with findings from the [[Big Lost River]], Idaho.
- A few very small *C. confusus* may indicate unusually late spawning.

**5. Recommendations**
- Longer-term monitoring is needed to assess the species' distribution and ecology and possible changes in status.
- Researchers should use the diagnostic characters to assess the impact of development in the Flathead Valley.
- Voucher collections should be deposited with the British Columbia Provincial Museum or another recognized fish collection.

**Catalogue:** Specimens are held at the British Columbia Provincial Museum under catalogue numbers BCPM981-158 to BCPM981-183.
