# Fish resources of Dhofar khawrs (coastal lagoons) in the Sultanate of Oman

#ichthyology #marine-biology #estuarine-ecology #coastal-lagoons #Mugilidae #Chanidae

## Executive Summary

A 1992–1993 fish resource monitoring programme surveyed nine [[Dhofar Khawrs]] (coastal lagoons) in southern Oman, recording **47 fish species across 25 families**. The community was dominated numerically and by biomass by **[[Milkfish]]** (*Chanos chanos*, 60.7% of catch) and **[[Flathead Mullet]]** (*Mugil cephalus*, 18.4% of catch), together comprising ~80% of total catch. **Khawr Sawli** supported the highest species diversity (26 species), while the isolated **Khawr Baleed** had the lowest (2 species), directly reflecting degree of connectivity to the sea and water quality. Water temperature, salinity, and dissolved oxygen followed strong seasonal cycles tied to monsoon flooding and sandbar dynamics, with **massive fish mortality events** documented following heavy rains (e.g., >10,000 dead fish in Khawr Salalah, April 1992) and during monsoon-related sewage pollution (Khawr Jnawf, September 1992). The study identifies significant aquaculture potential and proposes management interventions addressing pollution, overgrazing, and uncoordinated development pressures across fragmented governmental jurisdictions.

## Background & Description

- **Study area**: The Sultanate of Oman has a 1700 km coastline; the Dhofar Governorate contains 44 khawrs along ~100 km of sandy coast, mostly east of Salalah. Nine khawrs were selected for fish monitoring: **Mughsayl, Qurm Al-Kabir, Salalah, Baleed, Dahareez, Jnawf, Sawli, Taqah,** and **Rawri**.
- **Khawr typology**: Sizes range from 3.2 ha (Baleed) to 58 ha (Rawri); habitats include open water, mangrove (*[[Avicennia Marina]]*), and reed-dominated systems (*Typha angustata*, *Phragmites australis*). Outer zones are fringed by grasses (*Paspalum vaginatum*, *Sporobolus arabicus*); floating/submerged plants (*Potamogeton pectinatus*, *Najas marina*) occur in less saline systems.
- **Hydrology**: Most khawrs are separated from the sea by narrow, fragile sand bars for most of the year, opening seasonally during the **monsoon (June–September)** and after **heavy spring rains** (cyclonic storms recur ~every 5–7 years). **Khawr Taqah** is unique in maintaining a permanent freshwater spring-fed connection to the sea (5.6 m deep, 140 l/s discharge). **Khawr Baleed** has lost its natural drainage connection due to upstream development.
- **Methods**: Eight field trips (Jan 1992–Feb 1993); water column profiling via Hydrolab Surveyor-3 (temperature, pH, salinity, dissolved oxygen); fish sampled via gillnets (2.0–11.5 cm mesh) and opportunistic collection of flood-killed fish; species identified per Fischer & Bianchi (1984) and Smith & Heemstra (1986), classified per Nelson (1984).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Water Quality Dynamics
- **Temperature**: Followed strong seasonal cycles; surface water up to 5°C warmer than deep water in large khawrs. Winter surface temps ranged 17–22°C (Jan 1992), rising to 26–32°C by spring flooding (April) and 29–32°C during peak pre-monsoon (May). Thermal stratification was most pronounced in deep khawrs (e.g., Rawri: 29°C surface vs 26°C bottom at 6.3 m).
- **Salinity**: Highly variable, driven by freshwater seepage vs. seawater intrusion. Ranged from 3‰ (Rawri, winter) to 42.5‰ (Jnawf, hypersaline, Dec 1992). Flood events in April 1992 caused divergent responses: minimal change in **Rawri** and **Mughsayl**, sharp salinity **increases** in **Taqah, Sawli, and Jnawf** (seawater intrusion), and a >50% salinity **decrease** in **Qurm Al-Kabir** (rainwater dilution, 31‰→16‰).
- **Dissolved Oxygen**: Complex patterns driven by temperature, wind, depth, salinity, sediment load, and submerged plant photosynthesis. Winter stratification produced oxygen decline with depth in Rawri and Mughsayl. Conversely, Taqah and Sawli showed **higher DO in bottom waters** than surface, attributed to submerged macrophyte photosynthesis. Post-flood **sedimentation and organic decomposition** drove DO to near **0.0–1.8 mg/l** (hypoxic/anoxic) in Sawli, Rawri, and Dahareez in April 1992. Anoxic bottom water conditions persisted through the monsoon in most khawrs except Mughsayl.

### Habitat–Diversity Relationship
- Species richness ranking: **Sawli (26) > Jnawf (25) > Taqah (20) > Dahareez (15) > Rawri (12) > Salalah (11) > Qurm Al-Kabir (9) > Mughsayl (8) > Baleed (2)**.
- Low diversity in **Baleed** attributed to lack of sea connectivity limiting recruitment; high diversity in **Sawli, Jnawf, Taqah** linked to habitat heterogeneity and favorable water quality.
- Separation from the sea for most of the year is hypothesized to **reduce predation pressure**, allowing khawrs to function as effective nursery/shelter habitat.

### Mortality Events (Environmental Stressors)
- **April 1992 flood mortality**: >10,000 dead fish (7 species) in Khawr Salalah on 10 April 1992, mostly harvestable [[Milkfish]] (modal length 40–45 cm TL) — linked to blocked sandbar preventing escape and oxygenation.
- **Stranding mortality**: Extensive fish losses in Sawli, Rawri, Taqah, and Dahareez when water levels dropped rapidly after khawr-sea connection, leaving fish stranded.
- **September 1992 pollution mortality (Jnawf)**: Several hundred dead mullet, gizzard shad, jack, tigerfish, barracuda, and stingray washed ashore; coincided with elevated *Escherichia coli* levels (<180/100 ml), discoloured water, foul odor, filamentous algal blooms, and dead mangrove trees — attributed to illegal sewage dumping.

### Human Impacts
- **Fishing**: Small-scale gillnet, cast net, and handline fishing, concentrated during rough-sea monsoon periods.
- **Overgrazing**: Cattle, goats, and camels graze/water in khawrs year-round, intensifying pre-monsoon; vegetation removal (including mangrove leaf/seed harvesting) degrades nursery habitat.
- **Urbanization/development**: Road construction (Mughsayl), embankment (Dahareez), infilling, upstream damming, and sandbar blockage (Salalah) disrupt natural flood dynamics and drainage.
- **Recreation & littering**: Vehicle damage to vegetation and shoreline littering (cans, bottles, plastics) most severe in Taqah, Rawri, Dahareez, Jnawf, Sawli, Qurm Al-Kabir; Khawr Salalah remains relatively clean due to fencing as a bird sanctuary.

## Key Findings

- **47 fish species / 25 families** recorded across nine khawrs during 1992–1993; **[[Milkfish]]** (312 kg, 60.7%) and **[[Flathead Mullet]]** (94.7 kg, 18.4%) dominated total catch.
- **Reproductive timing**: [[Flathead Mullet]] mature in **winter**; [[Milkfish]] mature in **early spring**; most other species mature in **spring–summer**; the **[[Sleeper]]** (*Ophiocara porocephala*) is exceptional in breeding **year-round** and is independent of sea access for reproduction (along with the toothcarp *Aphanius dispar*), tolerating conditions from near-fresh (Baleed) to hypersaline (Jnawf).
- **Commercial invertebrates**: Three species with commercial value collected — **Indian white shrimp** (*Penaeus indicus*), **mud crab** (*Scylla serrata*), and **swimming crab** (*Portunus pelagicus*), primarily from Khawr Sawli and Qurm Al-Kabir.
- **Size structure**: [[Milkfish]] showed marked spatial/seasonal size variation — large adults (65–80 cm TL, 2–4 kg) in winter in Rawri/Sawli; young-of-year (~10 cm) dominant in summer as large fish emigrate to sea to spawn. [[Giant Trevally]] (*Caranx ignobilis*) first recorded at 15 cm TL (spring 1992), reaching 30–40 cm by Feb 1993; also an obligate sea-spawner.
- **Catch by khawr**: Highest total catch in **Rawri** (largest khawr, 58 ha, deepest at 8.6 m) and **Taqah** (permanent sea connection and freshness).
- **Diversity–connectivity link**: Species richness strongly correlates with degree and stability of sea connection and water quality maintenance; isolated khawrs (Baleed) show severely depressed diversity.
- **Management recommendations**:
  1. Controlled/managed connection of khawrs to the sea (e.g., Khawr Salalah) to allow fish escape during flood/anoxia events and enable breeding migration.
  2. Mangrove afforestation programmes (modeled on Qatar's 1981 program) to counter overgrazing-driven mangrove loss in Jnawf and Qurm Al-Kabir.
  3. Public awareness programmes given the khawrs' ethnic, archaeological, ecological, and economic significance.
  4. Improved inter-agency coordination (multiple overlapping Omani ministries/authorities) with the **Technical Secretariat of the Planning Committee for Development and Environment of the Dhofar Governorate (TS-PCDEDG)** recommended as lead coordinating body.
- **Aquaculture potential**: Presence of culturable species ([[Milkfish]], [[Flathead Mullet]], shrimp, mud crab) combined with variable salinity regimes positions Dhofar khawrs as candidate sites for pilot aquaculture, pending feasibility studies given system dynamism.
