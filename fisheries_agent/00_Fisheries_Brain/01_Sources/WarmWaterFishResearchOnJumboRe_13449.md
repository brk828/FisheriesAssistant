# Warm Water Fish Research on Jumbo Reservoir, 1951-1953

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Percidae #Centrarchidae #Salmonidae #Ictaluridae #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Colorado Game and Fish Department report, written by Fish Technician Rex I. Taliaferro and published in 1955, synthesizes warm-water fishery research on [[Jumbo Reservoir]] from 1949 through 1953. The study covered food habits and growth of major game and rough fish, a physical, chemical and biological survey, experimental fishing, experimental [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking, marked perch and crappie studies, and fish salvage from the outlet ditch. The author concluded that declining [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Crappie]] populations, combined with overfishing and failed crappie spawns, reduced angler success, and that management should focus on rough fish control, catch regulation, and selective stocking.

## Background & Description

Jumbo Reservoir (also called Julesburg Reservoir) was first filled in 1907 and serves primarily to store irrigation water. At full pool it is about 27 feet deep and covers 1,578 surface acres with a 7.9-mile irregular shoreline; it can be drawn down to 2 feet and 116 acres. The surrounding terrain is flat with some rolling hills, and adjacent land is marsh or native pasture.

The project began in 1949 when Fish Technician Robert Evans investigated why perch and crappie grew larger there than in other Colorado warm-water lakes (Evans, 1952). Declining game fish numbers and seasonal variation in angler success then broadened the work. Sampling used gill nets, fyke nets, seines, plankton samplers, Ekman bottom dredges, creel census and warden reports. Age was determined from scales (or pectoral spines for catfish and bullheads), and stomach contents were analyzed by volume and frequency of occurrence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical and chemical conditions**
- Surface temperatures ranged from 32 °F (under ice) to 82 °F (August 1952). No thermocline was found; surface and bottom temperatures were usually similar.
- Secchi transparency ranged from 5 to 102 inches. Lowest values followed filamentous algae blooms; highest values occurred under ice cover.
- pH stayed between 8.0 and 8.5. Dissolved oxygen ranged from 3.7 to 9.8 ppm. Total alkalinity ranged from 131 to 325 ppm, with incoming water more alkaline than lake water.
- Water level fluctuated about 18 feet (maximum about 25 feet, minimum 7 feet). The author noted that this instability hindered establishment of aquatic vegetation, reducing food organisms and spawning habitat.

**Plankton and bottom fauna**
- Zooplankton important to fish were [[Daphnia]], [[Bosmina]], [[Cyclops]], [[Diaptomus]] and Cerodaphnia. Phytoplankton included [[Aphanizomenon]] (a dominant filamentous alga forming blooms that decayed with offensive odor) and Asterionella.
- Bottom fauna was dominated (over 96 percent) by [[Chironomus]] larvae, with 0 to 164 per square foot and annual averages of 48.8 (1951), 20.3 (1952) and 22.5 (1953).

**Fish community**
- The reservoir supported perch, crappie, western white sucker, plains carpsucker, carp, bullhead, channel catfish, rainbow trout and sunfish. Unscreened inlets admitted fathead minnows and gizzard shad from the South Platte River system.

## Key Findings

**Catch and fishing success**
- Gill netting yielded 1,010 fish in 1949-1950, 3,915 in 1951, 2,530 in 1952 and 4,630 in 1953 (the report summary cites 12,085 gill-netted fish in total). Catch per 100 foot-hours rose from 0.53 (1951) to 3.4 (1953).
- Perch and western white suckers each made up about 73 percent of gill net catches from 1951 to 1953, but perch share increased as sucker share fell.
- Fyke net catch rose from 0.18 fish per hour (1951) to 0.36 (1952) and 2.3 (1953). Perch provided 43 to 62 percent of catches in those years.
- Seining caught 58,255 fish in 1952 (78.2 percent fathead minnows) and 10,955 in 1953. Overall seine total was 69,210.
- Creel catch per man-hour (cpmh) was 0.73 in 1950, 0.51 in 1951, 0.90 in 1952 and 1.40 in 1953; 2.27 was recorded in 1949. Yellow perch made up 89.2 percent (1952) and 93.3 percent (1953) of creel catches.

**Spawning**
- Yellow perch spawned about late April; crappie spawned late June to late July; bullheads late June through July; western white suckers in May; plains carpsuckers and carp from May to July.
- Crappie nests in shallow water were vulnerable to drawdown and wave action, a likely cause of poor spawns.

**Food habits** (stomachs examined)
- Yellow perch (805): diet shifted with size and season, from Chironomus and cladocerans (1951) to fish as the major food later (July to December 1952 and throughout 1953). Perch preferred Cladocera over Copepoda.
- Crappie (326): fish were the most important food; Chironomus, cladocerans and copepods were important in 1951.
- Rainbow trout (207): fish were the main food; minnows exceeded game fish in 1953.
- Channel catfish (38), bullheads (154): fish dominated; terrestrial insects and crayfish were locally important.
- Carp (126), plains carpsucker (185), western white sucker (234): Cladocera, Copepoda and Chironomus larvae were the main foods, so these rough fish were judged to compete with game fish.

**Age and growth**
- Yellow perch (1,017 fish, ages I to VII): 1951 age-group I averaged 6.3 inches; 1952 and 1953 fish grew faster than in other studies. 88.1 percent of 1951 perch were under three years old. Females outgrew males.
- Black and white crappie (344 fish, ages I to IX): 1951 age-group III averaged 11.4 inches for black crappie; the largest specimen was a 17.1-inch ripe female white crappie weighing 3 lb 3 oz.
- Channel catfish (38 fish): the largest was 33.8 inches, 20 pounds, 8 years old. Bullheads (106 fish, ages I to V) and channel catfish outgrew those in Two Buttes Reservoir.
- Carp (118 fish, ages 0 to IX) showed an unusually large 1946 year class.

**Trout experiment**
- In 1952, 35,376 creel-size rainbow trout (5 to 11 inches) and 284,900 fingerlings were stocked. Anglers caught 632 fish (0.2 percent). Total return from all sources was 1,854 fish (about 0.6 percent).
- Summer 1952 high water temperatures, drawdown, bird predation, outlet losses and [[Lernaea]] infestation caused heavy trout mortality.
- Creel share of trout fell from 16.2 percent (1951) to 6.5 percent (1952) and 1.0 percent (1953). The program was abandoned.

**Fish salvage and stocking**
- Outlet ditch salvage in 1952 recovered 6,956 trout, 11,288 perch and 8,472 crappie; 972 trout were lost on June 28 and August 6.
- Salvage in 1953 yielded 12,680 perch, 240 crappie and 120 bullheads, among others.
- Stocking between 1951 and 1953 included 36,462 yellow perch, 33,024 black and white crappie, 17,813 channel catfish and about 336,000 trout.

**Marked fish study**
- Perch and crappie from North Sterling Reservoir were tagged and stocked (3,200 perch and 791 crappie, 1949-1951). Returns were too few to compare growth.

**Causes of declining angling success** (as the author interpreted them)
1. Overfishing of perch during ice fishing.
2. Heavy angling of crappie spawners at a spawning bed in 1947.
3. Failure of the 1950 crappie spawn due to high winds and drawdown.

**Management actions and recommendations**
- Ice fishing was closed in winters 1951-52 and 1952-53. A 10-fish bag limit applied to perch 8 inches or longer. Crappie spawning areas were to be posted. Ice fishing was recommended to reopen for 1953-54.
- Recommendations: (1) control rough fish through annual removal; (2) make no future trout plants; (3) keep current perch and crappie catch regulations; (4) stock channel catfish and crappie, especially when natural crappie reproduction fails; (5) test exotic predatory game fish if angling does not improve; (6) prohibit fishing in crappie spawning areas; (7) conduct limited annual investigations.
- Experimental angling found live minnows the best bait for crappie and large perch; perch were best taken 1 to 2 feet off bottom, and crappie at 3 to 6 feet depth.
