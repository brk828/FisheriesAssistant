# The Ecology of Sport Fish in Two Dredged Backwaters of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Master of Science thesis in fishery biology, by Mark Allen Singer (University of Arizona, 1973), assesses the sport fishery of two recently dredged backwaters, [[Backwater A-7]] and [[Backwater A-10]], on the [[Lower Colorado River]]. Sampling ran monthly from November 1971 to October 1972. The study focused on four game species: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Redear Sunfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]]. The author concludes that the backwaters provide a favorable environment for these fish, with adequate food, no stunting, a long growing season, and mostly resident populations. Channel Catfish did not spawn, and steep shorelines limit suitable habitat. Overall, the backwaters are judged to have good, though not exceptional, fishery potential.

## Background & Description

The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] has altered the Lower Colorado River for decades through dams, hydroelectric power, irrigation and channelization. As part of a later multipurpose management program, the Bureau dredged backwaters to enhance fish and wildlife and provide recreational fishing. Dredging of A-7 ran from mid-1969 to November 1969, and dredging of A-10 from April to May 1970.

The study addressed five questions:

1. Do the backwaters provide suitable spawning areas and protective cover?
2. What are the primary and secondary forage organisms, and is the food supply adequate?
3. Do the fish grow well?
4. Are the fish residents or transients?
5. What effect do water fluctuations have on the fishery?

Both backwaters have a main channel with numerous "fingers" and are connected to the river by culverts. A-7 is the larger of the two (30.35 ha maximum surface area, versus 12.34 ha for A-10). Their bottoms are generally steep, with some gradual shoals. Substrates range from silt and detritus in deep water to sand, gravel and small rocks at the shore. Dominant submergent plants are [[Spiny Naiad]], [[Sago Pondweed]] and [[Watermilfoil]]. [[Broad-leaf Cattail]] lines 40 to 50% of the shoreline in both backwaters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water regime.** Backwater levels follow releases from the [[Palo Verde Diversion Weir]], 24.2 km upriver. Daytime releases during spring and summer cause fluctuations of up to 20 cm within 24 hours. Weir discharges peak in July and are lowest in October (Table 2 in the source gives monthly means and ranges).

**Water quality.** Temperatures ranged from 9.1°C in December to 31.5°C in July, with daily summer thermal stratification that broke down at night. Dissolved oxygen ranged from 6.0 to 13.6 mg/L. A localized area in A-7 (capture area 4) dropped to 0.3 mg/L in May and June. pH ranged from 6.7 to 9.4, and total alkalinity from 67 to 223 mg/L.

**Forage base.** The food web is largely autochthonous. [[Tendipedid Larvae]] (chironomids) were the most abundant invertebrates in benthic and weed samples. [[Threadfin Shad]] served as the main prey of adult bass.

**Cover and habitat.** Submergent vegetation was the main protective cover for adult and fingerling bluegill and redear sunfish and for adult bass. Juvenile bass used cattails. Steep, sandy, eroding shorelines had little cover and few or no fish.

## Key Findings

### Sampling and Community
- A-7 held 16 species from six families. A-10 held 13 species from four families.
- The three most abundant species in A-7 were threadfin shad, largemouth bass and bluegill. In A-10 they were largemouth bass and bluegill, with fewer shad.
- Other common species included channel catfish, carp and redear sunfish.

### Spawning
- Largemouth bass spawned from March through May. No bass nests were seen. Bass fry of 0.9 cm SL were first captured on March 24 at about 20°C.
- Bluegill and redear sunfish spawned from May to August, starting at about 25°C. Eight bluegill nesting colonies were recorded in A-7 and eight in A-10, averaging eight nests per colony. All five redear colonies were in A-10, averaging 19 nests per colony.
- Redear nests were found at depths of 50 to 80 cm. Both species preferred gravel covered with sand on gradually sloping or flat bottoms.
- No channel catfish spawning was detected. The smallest catfish captured was 12.0 cm SL. The author attributes this to a lack of suitable spawning sites, which points to the riprapped shoreline of A-7 as the only plausible site.
- Diurnal water fluctuations may expose shallow nests to air and desiccate eggs, which may reduce spawning success.

### Food Habits
- Tendipedid larvae were the primary forage for bluegill, redear sunfish and channel catfish in both backwaters. They were eaten year-round and most heavily in winter and spring.
- Bluegills under 3.0 cm SL ate mainly zooplankton. Larger bluegills ate insects (from 43 families and 14 orders) and aquatic plants. Spiny naiad was the dominant plant material.
- Largemouth bass under 4.0 cm SL ate zooplankton. Fish first appeared in bass diets at 6.0 cm SL. Bass fed on threadfin shad, bluegill and bass fry, and on insects from 17 families and 10 orders.
- Threadfin shad were the main food of adult bass. In A-7 bass ate shad and immature Diptera in spring, and bluegill in fall. In A-10 bass ate mainly shad in winter and summer.
- Channel catfish were omnivorous. Tendipedid larvae occurred in 49.5% of stomachs from both backwaters. Plant material was frequent, and its frequency increased with fish length. In A-10, cicadas were the main summer food.
- Redear sunfish from A-10 were too few for analysis.

### Condition and Growth
- Mean condition (K) for bass and bluegill fell within the average range by published standards, indicating an adequate food supply and no stunting.
- Bluegill condition was significantly higher in A-7 than in A-10 (P = 0.05).
- Bass calculated weights at 30, 35, 40 and 45 cm were higher in A-7 (642.5 to 2396.3 g) than in A-10 (641.4 to 2196.8 g). The author links this to more shad in A-7.
- Growth occurred year-round. Winter rates were lowest and April to July rates highest. The growing season was estimated at 11 months, with 4 months of optimum growth.

### Tagging and Movement
- A total of 757 fish were tagged (437 in A-7, 320 in A-10). Tagging covered fish over 12.0 cm SL, from November to April.
- Overall, 73 fish (9.6%) were recaptured: 39 by research sampling and 34 by anglers.
- Recapture rates by species (A-7 + A-10): largemouth bass 14.5% (39 of 269), bluegill 8.4% (18 of 214), channel catfish 6.7% (12 of 180), redear sunfish 4.3% (4 of 94).
- All recaptured fish were found in the backwater where they were tagged. None crossed the internal culvert in A-10.
- Average recorded movement was greater in A-7. Bass moved 0.92 km in A-7 and 0.19 km in A-10. The longest movement was 2.26 km by a bass over 188 days.
- Fish are "predominantly residents." Some exchange with the river occurs but appears to have little effect on the fishery.

### Management Implications and Conclusions
- Dredged backwaters can support sport fish with cover from submergent vegetation and cattails.
- Steep sandy shorelines limit spawning and cover, reducing potential recruitment.
- Channel catfish recruitment depends on the river or stocking.
- Water-level fluctuation changes habitat and can expose nests. Its effects on the fishery are considered significant.
- The author concludes that A-7 and A-10 "do not provide exceptional fisheries," but "do have a good potential."
