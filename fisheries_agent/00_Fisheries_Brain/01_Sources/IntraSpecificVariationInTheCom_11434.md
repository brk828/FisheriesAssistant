## Executive Summary

This study by James C. Underhill (University of Minnesota, 1961) examines intra-specific meristic variation in **1,351 specimens** of the [[Common Shiner]] (*Notropis cornutus frontalis*) collected between 1952–1960 from multiple drainage basins in Minnesota and South Dakota (Mississippi, Minnesota, Missouri, St. Croix, and Red River systems, plus Lake Superior). The research systematically evaluated eight meristic characters for evidence of sexual dimorphism, seasonal variation, annual variation, intra-basin variation, and inter-basin variation. Findings indicate that while three characters (lateral line scales, caudal peduncle scales, and pectoral fin rays) show considerable and biologically meaningful variation across streams and basins, four other characters (dorsal, anal, and pelvic fin rays) remain remarkably stable. The paper argues that inter-basin variation is largely a statistical artifact of pooling non-homogeneous intra-basin samples, and that variability patterns may relate to the species' relatively broad habitat tolerance compared to the more habitat-specific [[Bigmouth Shiner]] (*Notropis dorsalis*).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Preferred habitat**: Small streams, small rivers, and lakes; the [[Common Shiner]] shows a broad and relatively **continuous distribution** across available microhabitats within a stream system.
- **Rarely found** in large-river habitats (represented by only 1–2 individuals when present).
- Contrasts with the [[Bigmouth Shiner]], which shows habitat specificity for shifting sand and sand-gravel substrates, resulting in patchier, more discontinuous distribution within streams.
- The paper hypothesizes a link between **habitat specificity and meristic variability**: species with narrower habitat preferences (bigmouth shiner) show greater intra-stream and annual variation, while habitat generalists (common shiner) show more stable, homogeneous populations within a single stream.
- Biogeographic note: The [[Red River]] and [[Minnesota River]] populations are historically connected via glacial meltwater drainage through the **River Warren** (late-glacial precursor of the Minnesota River), explaining genetic/meristic similarity despite current geographic isolation (e.g., Jim Creek vs. Little Minnesota River, only 2 miles apart at one point in Roberts County, SD).

## Key Findings

1. **No sexual dimorphism** detected in any of the eight meristic characters (Credit River comparison, Table 1).

2. **Seasonal variation**: Comparing May vs. October 1954 samples (Credit River), only **scales below the lateral line** showed significant seasonal difference (May significantly higher, p=0.01); all other characters were stable.

3. **Annual variation**: Zumbro River samples (1952–1954) showed significant annual differences only in **scales above the lateral line**; pectoral fin rays showed an "intermediate" variation pattern (1952 vs 1953/1954 not significant, but 1953 vs 1954 significant).

4. **Intra-stream (station-to-station) homogeneity**: Samples from different stations on the same stream (Rock Creek: 2, 4, 6 mi apart; Zumbro River: 30, 60, 70 mi apart) showed **no significant differences**, indicating homogeneous populations within a single stream.

5. **Intra-basin heterogeneity**: Comparing different streams within the *same* drainage basin (Minnesota River tributaries — Table 4) revealed **significant variation** in lateral line scales, caudal peduncle scales, and pectoral fin rays, but not in dorsal, anal, or pelvic rays. E.g., [[Pomme de Terre River]] shiners had significantly more pectoral rays than [[Little Minnesota River]] and [[Yellow Bank River]] populations (p<0.001).

6. **Inter-basin variation is not greater than intra-basin variation**: Comparison of geographically isolated but historically connected populations ([[Jim Creek]], SD vs. [[Little Minnesota River]]) showed **no significant difference**, despite belonging to different modern drainage basins (Table 5) — supporting the River Warren connectivity hypothesis.

7. **Basin-pooled averages can be misleading**: When pooled by major drainage basin (Table 6), the [[Lake Superior]] population showed significantly **more** lateral line scales but significantly **fewer** pectoral fin rays than all other basins — a pattern that superficially supports **Jordan's Rule** (northern populations have more parts) for one character but contradicts it for another, undermining any simple latitudinal cline interpretation.

8. **Methodological caution**: The author argues that pooling sub-samples from non-homogeneous populations (i.e., inter-basin averaging) merely reflects and can amplify **intra-basin variation**, and may create a **statistically erroneous impression** of range-wide species variation — a caution relevant to broader [[Fish Systematics]] practice.

9. **Comparison with congener**: *N. cornutus* is **less variable** overall than the [[Bigmouth Shiner]] (*Notropis dorsalis*), which displayed greater annual and intra-stream variation in a parallel study (Underhill & Merrell, 1959) — attributed to differing habitat specificity between the two species.

10. Developmental timing (per [[Tanning 1952]]) may explain why different meristic characters show different variability: characters determined during different developmental stages may be exposed to differing degrees of environmental stability, explaining why fin ray counts (dorsal/anal/pelvic) are stable while scale counts and pectoral rays vary considerably.
