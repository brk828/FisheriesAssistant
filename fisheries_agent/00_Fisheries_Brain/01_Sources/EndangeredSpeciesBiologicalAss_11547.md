## Executive Summary

This 1984 Bureau of Reclamation Biological Assessment evaluates potential impacts of the **Dirty Devil River Unit** — a salinity-control project designed to remove ~20,600 tons/year of salt from the Colorado River system via saline water collection (alluvial wells in Hanksville Salt Wash and Emery South Salt Wash) and deep-well injection into the Coconino Sandstone formation — on federally protected species in the unit area. Of primary ichthyological relevance are two endangered [[Colorado River Basin]] fish: the **[[Colorado Squawfish]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and the **[[Razorback Sucker]]** (*Xyrauchen texanus*), both surveyed in the Dirty Devil arm of **[[Lake Powell]]** during 1983–1984. Field sampling documented seasonal occupation of the river/reservoir confluence by both species but found no evidence of riverine spawning, recruitment, or long-term dependence on Dirty Devil River inflow. The Bureau of Reclamation concluded the project would **not jeopardize** either species. Non-fish species assessed (black-footed ferret, Wright's fishhook cactus) were determined to be absent from or unaffected by the project footprint.

## Background & Description

### Colorado Squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)
- Largest North American cyprinid; native piscivore of the Colorado River drainage.
- Sampled individuals (n=19, adults only) ranged from **521–705 mm** in length and **0.62–2.78 kg** in weight (see [[Table I - Colorado Squawfish Data]]).
- Several fish showed direct evidence of feeding on [[Threadfin Shad]] (partially digested prey observed).
- Individually marked with Fish and Wildlife Service Carlin tags; some fitted with surgically implanted radio tags.
- Notable long-distance movement: one recaptured fish had traveled ~55 miles from the Colorado River arm of Lake Powell to the Dirty Devil arm within a 2-month period, indicating high mobility within the reservoir system.

### Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- Considered the rarest native fish in the Upper [[Colorado River Basin]].
- Distinctive keeled nape (razorback) morphology (implied by common name; not directly described in text).
- Sampled individuals (n=7) ranged from **507–643 mm** in length and **1.70–3.55 kg** in weight (see [[Table II - Razorback Sucker Data]]).
- One adult male collected in April was documented as sexually ripe.
- One recaptured individual exhibited scoliosis.
- Four fish were surgically implanted with radio tags; all subsequent relocation attempts failed, likely due to high water conductivity interfering with weak radio signal transmission.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat context:** The Dirty Devil arm of Lake Powell extends ~14 miles up the old river channel from a point ~1 mile northwest of Hite Marina. The river itself exhibits extreme hydrologic variability (0–35,000 cfs), high silt load, and wide thermal range (32°F–80°F+), resulting in poor water quality that supports only highly tolerant species and virtually no aquatic macroinvertebrates in lower reaches.
- **Transition zone:** Where river water mixes with reservoir water, conditions improve, creating a mixed riverine–lacustrine fish assemblage: **[[Flannelmouth Sucker]]** and **[[Channel Catfish]]** (riverine) intermixed with reservoir species — largemouth bass, sunfish, striped bass, black crappie, carp, and walleye.
- **Prey base:** [[Threadfin Shad]] were abundant near driftwood/submerged tree cover (favorable shad spawning habitat), and appear to be a key seasonal food resource attracting Colorado squawfish to the confluence area. Razorback suckers were inferred to exploit detritus concentrations in the same zone.
- **Seasonality:** Squawfish were captured March–June (peak in April); razorback suckers were captured January–May. Both species appear to use the Dirty Devil confluence **seasonally** rather than as permanent habitat.
- **Reproductive evidence:** Larval fish sampling in the uppermost reservoir arm identified four larval fish species common to Lake Powell/Dirty Devil River, but **no threatened or endangered larval fish** (squawfish or razorback) were detected in either survey period, suggesting no local spawning or recruitment.
- **Historical context:** Populations of both species declined basin-wide following water resource development and introduction of exotic fish species; closure of **[[Glen Canyon Dam]]** in 1963 blocked upstream migration and inundated 187 miles of the Colorado River, fundamentally altering habitat availability.

## Key Findings

1. **No riverine occurrence documented:** Despite past sampling of the Dirty Devil River and tributaries, no Colorado squawfish or razorback suckers have ever been collected from the river itself — only from the Lake Powell reservoir arm.
2. **Seasonal attractant, not critical habitat:** The Dirty Devil River/Lake Powell confluence functions as a seasonal food-resource attractant (threadfin shad for squawfish, detritus for razorback sucker) rather than essential spawning or rearing habitat.
3. **No evidence of spawning use:** Larval fish surveys found no endangered species larvae, supporting the conclusion that neither species uses the Dirty Devil River for reproduction.
4. **High mobility confirmed:** Radio/Carlin tag recapture data demonstrated squawfish movement of at least 55 miles within Lake Powell over ~2 months, underscoring the reservoir-wide (not localized) ecology of the species.
5. **Minimal hydrologic impact:** The project's maximum surface/groundwater depletion of 2.75 cfs (3 cfs cumulative) was judged negligible relative to the river's natural flow extremes (0–35,000 cfs) and would not measurably affect fish populations in Lake Powell.
6. **No-jeopardy determination:** The Bureau of Reclamation concluded the Dirty Devil River Unit would **not jeopardize** the continued existence of the [[Colorado Squawfish]] or [[Razorback Sucker]].
7. **Non-fish species screened out:** The [[Black-footed Ferret]] was not confirmed present in the immediate project area (only 2 sightings basin-wide, 1981–1985, both removed from the project site), and no white-tailed prairie dog colonies (prey base) were found on-site. The [[Wright's Fishhook Cactus]] survey was inconclusive pending flowering-stage identification (planned for spring 1985), but impacts would be avoided via alignment modifications if the species is confirmed present.
