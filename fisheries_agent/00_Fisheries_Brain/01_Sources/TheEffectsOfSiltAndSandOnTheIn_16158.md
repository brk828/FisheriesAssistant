# The Effects of Silt and Sand on the Invertebrate Fauna of Streams and Rivers

#limnology #freshwater-invertebrates #benthic-macroinvertebrates

## Executive Summary

This journal article by F. M. Chutter, from the [[Limnological Research Group]] at the National Institute for Water Research (Grahamstown, South Africa), examines how [[Silt and Sand]] alter the invertebrate fauna of streams and rivers. Most earlier work dealt with complete smothering of habitats. Chutter's field study of the [[Vaal Dam]] catchment and the [[Vaal River]] system instead shows that fauna change with the amount of silt and sand even where habitats are not smothered. Fauna density was not reduced overall, but the variety of animals and the densities of many individual groups declined. The article concludes that increased silt and sand can change stone-dwelling fauna without smothering and makes sediment fauna more unstable, which reduces it.

## Background & Description

The article is a research paper published in *Hydrobiologia* (Vol. 34, Fasc. 1, 1969). The surrounding pages are the journal's author guidelines, which set out language, formatting, and citation rules but contain no ecological data.

Silt and sand are inert solids. The article identifies two main ways they affect stream fauna, following [[Hynes 1960]]:

- **Suspended solids** reduce light penetration, which prevents plant and algal growth. Herbivores then lack food, and detritivores depend on allochthonous detritus.
- **Settled solids** smother algae, kill rooted plants and mosses, and clog interstitial spaces between stones, eliminating habitat.

South African rivers carry considerable sediment. [[Schwartz and Pullen 1966]] estimated that 0.14% to 4.25% of annual flow consists of sediment. Specific values were 0.31% for the Vaal River before [[Vaal Dam]] and 0.24% for the Wilge River before it enters Vaal Dam.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Individual species responses (literature review):**

- [[Ellis 1936]] found that freshwater mussels could not tolerate a silt layer 4 to 1 inch thick over suitable sediments. Mussels caged above the sediment were unaffected, so accumulated fine particles on the bottom, not the water itself, were the problem. Silty water also interfered with feeding and respiration.
- [[Harrison and Farina 1965]] studied three African planorbid snails in naturally turbid water with 360 ppm and 190 ppm suspended solids. *Lymnaea natalensis* developed normally in both. *Biomphalaria pfeifferi* did not lay eggs at 360 ppm but laid them on vegetation and glass at 190 ppm, where they developed normally. Egg capsules of *Bulinus globosus* on glass swelled at 360 ppm and fell off at 190 ppm, killing the embryos. Centrifuged controls developed normally.
- [[Wu 1931]] observed that [[Simulium]] larvae moved away from silt-covered surfaces even where current was suitable. [[Harrison and Elsworth 1958]] found the mayfly nymph *Pseudocloeon vinosum* only on "clean" vegetation.

**Community-level studies (literature review):**

- [[Cordone and Kelly 1961]] reviewed North American studies, which were all of stony bottoms altered by clearly defined sources such as gravel washing, placer mining, and logging. Faunal density usually fell where sedimentation occurred, but the studies were not detailed enough to show which groups were most affected.
- [[Herbert et al. 1961]] studied Cornish trout streams affected by china-clay wastes. Standing crop in control streams was 19 times greater than in the most affected streams. Gastropods occurred at all control sites but at none of the affected sites.
- [[Hamilton 1961]] studied a shallow, fast-flowing stream receiving sand-pit washings. He concluded that high turbidity alone did not harm the bottom fauna, and that only a thick layer of settled material eliminated normal fauna.

**Study area zonation:** The [[Vaal Dam]] catchment was divided into a Source Zone, an [[Eroding Zone]], and a Depositing Zone. The Depositing Zone was split into a [[Stable Depositing Zone]], with aquatic plants such as *Potamogeton* and *Lagarosiphon* and fringing *Cyperus* and *Phragmites*, and an [[Unstable Depositing Zone]], with little vegetation, eroded banks, and extensive sand and mud banks. "High-lying" Unstable Depositing Zones occurred where stable or eroding zones were expected. Three impoundments act as silt traps on the Vaal River: [[Vaal Dam]], the [[Vaal Barrage]], and the [[Vaal Hartz Diversion Weir]].

**Other factors:** Temperature did not differ enough between zones to explain faunal differences. In winter, mean temperatures rose from 10°C in the Eroding Zone to 11.4°C in the normal Unstable Depositing Zone, and the lowest recorded temperature in the Unstable Depositing Zone was 4.5°C. Current speed did not correlate with animal abundance.

## Key Findings

**1. Variety fell where silt and sand were high (Table I).** The mean number of animal kinds per sample on stones in current was highest in the Stable Depositing Zone in summer (42, n=10). The Unstable Depositing Zone dropped to 22 (n=18) in summer, and the high-lying Unstable Depositing Zone was also 22 (n=6). Summer variety in the Unstable Depositing Zones was the lowest recorded.

**2. Total density was not reduced (Table II).** Density per 0.1 m² was highest in the Stable Depositing Zone in every season. In summer, the Unstable Depositing Zone (666, n=7) was similar to the Stable Depositing Zone (757, n=7), so silt and sand had no detectable effect on overall density.

**3. Individual groups responded differently (Table III).**
- Nais spp., Ostracoda, and *Burnupia* spp. were common only in the Stable Depositing Zone in summer.
- Tricladida and Chironomidae had very low summer densities in the Unstable Depositing Zones but not in the Eroding or Stable Depositing Zones. Chironomidae were abundant in the Unstable Depositing Zones in winter and dry early summer.
- Hydrachnellae, Caenidae, and Elmidae were abundant in all seasons in the Eroding and Stable Depositing Zones but scarce in the Unstable Depositing Zones.
- *Neurocaenis* spp. was the only group whose density increased with silt and sand. Its hairy mouthparts may allow it to strain food from silty water.
- Density changes in Baetidae, *Choroterpes (Euthraulus)*, Hydropsychidae, and Simuliidae were not clearly linked to silt and sand at the family level.

**4. Species-level Baetidae and Hydropsychidae responses.** *Baetis harrisoni* and *Centroptilum sudafricanum* occurred in the Eroding Zone in all seasons, and were replaced in the Unstable Depositing Zone by *Baetis glaucus* and *Centroptilum excisum*. In summer, the high-lying zone's Baetidae shifted to the Unstable Depositing Zone species. *Cheumatopsyche thomasseti* was more abundant than *C. afra* in the Unstable Depositing Zone and in the high-lying zone, suggesting that silt and sand influence the relative abundance of these two species.

**5. Downstream responses.** Below [[Vaal Barrage]] and at Lindeques' Drift (about 8 km below the Barrage), fauna were more stable and resembled the Stable Depositing Zone. Below the [[Vaal Hartz Diversion Weir]] (samples from about 10 km to 120 km downstream), *Neurocaenis* was most common at the station with the most silt and sand. Chutter concludes that these shifts support silt and sand, rather than unrecognised factors, as the cause of zonal differences.

**6. Sediment fauna (Table IV).** Summer density fell in both fine and coarse sediments, most sharply in the Eroding and Unstable Depositing Zones. Surface-dwelling forms (e.g., Ilyocryptus, Paracyclops, Ostracoda, Tanytarsini, and Orthocladiinae) made up a much larger share of the fauna in unstable zones. Their summer decline was steeper in fine sediments, which are more easily swept away. Burrowing forms were denser in fine than in coarse sediments in unstable zones, which suggests fine sediments are more stable at depth.

**7. Methodological caveats.** Chutter notes that the conclusions rest on field observations only. Temperature records were not detailed enough, and diurnal temperature range may differ in shallow high-lying zones. He also notes that the Vaal River below the Barrage and below the Weir was not sampled for sediment fauna.

**8. Conclusions.** Silt and sand can change the composition of stone-dwelling fauna without smothering the habitat. Increased silt and sand in river beds increase sediment instability, which harms sediment fauna. Chutter also observes that natural erosion in South Africa may be higher than in regions with more evenly distributed rainfall, citing [[Barrow 1801]] on a muddy flood on the [[Orange River]].
