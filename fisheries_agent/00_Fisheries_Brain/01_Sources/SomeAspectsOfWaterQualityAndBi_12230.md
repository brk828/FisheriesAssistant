# Some Aspects of Water Quality and Biology of the Missouri River

## Executive Summary
This paper (Whitley & Campbell, 1974) synthesizes ~50 years of water-quality and biological change in the [[Missouri River]] resulting from three major anthropogenic drivers: **main-stem impoundment**, **channelization**, and **industrial/municipal pollution**. Six main-stem dams inundated 897 miles of river but created 1,875 mi² of clearwater reservoir habitat with improved water quality and enhanced tailwater fish growth. Conversely, channelization of 731 miles of river below Sioux City eliminated roughly 50–64% of river/wetland area, collapsing plankton and benthic invertebrate standing crops and severely degrading fish habitat and spawning/nursery grounds. Pollution, historically severe near urban centers (e.g., a 1964 fish kill near Kansas City), has partially abated for gross organic loading but persistent toxicants (chlorinated hydrocarbons, PCBs) and emerging thermal pollution from power plants remain concerns. The work is a synthesis/review rather than a primary morphological study, drawing on decades of USFWS North Central Reservoir Investigations and Missouri Dept. of Conservation data.

## Background & Description
*This is a systems-ecology/water-quality review rather than a morphological study; direct anatomical descriptions are minimal. Species-level physiological/morphological notes reported by cited sources include:*

- **Gill thickening**: Suspended solids at 90–810 ppm produced gill thickening in some trout species (but not others), potentially increasing susceptibility to environmental stress (Herbert & Merkens 1961).
- **Growth rate plasticity**: [[Channel Catfish]] and other species show measurably different growth rates between channelized and unchannelized river reaches and between reservoir and tailwater habitats, indicating strong phenotypic plasticity in growth as a response to food/habitat conditions rather than fixed morphology.
- **Species-specific turbidity tolerance**: Juvenile and adult fishes tested with montmorillonite clay turbidity showed no acute lethal effects at natural concentrations (Wallen, 1951), suggesting behavioral/physiological tolerance rather than structural vulnerability at the tested doses.
- Declining species noted: [[Pallid Sturgeon]] (*Scaphirhynchus albus*) and [[Lake Sturgeon]] (*Acipenser fulvescens*) — both large-bodied, benthic, long-lived species particularly sensitive to habitat alteration.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Historical river character**: A large, turbid, meandering, alluvial river (2,464 miles pre-alteration) with side channels, sloughs, and backwaters serving as critical [[Nursery Habitat]] and spawning sites for many fish species and as primary plankton production zones.
- **Main-stem reservoirs** (Fort Peck, Garrison/Sakakawea, Oahe, Big Bend/Sharpe, Fort Randall/Francis Case, Gavins Point/Lewis and Clark Lake): function as clearwater, plankton-rich standing-water ecosystems distinct from the historic riverine niche; support cyprinids, catfishes, and centrarchids, though several taxa (cyprinids, black bullhead, [[Pallid Sturgeon]], longnose gar, quillback, green sunfish, bluegill, pumpkinseed) declined post-impoundment due to reduced habitat heterogeneity and lower turbidity favoring predation.
- **Tailwater zone** (~6,000 ft below dams): a uniquely enriched niche receiving discharged zooplankton, insect nymphs, algae, and fish from the reservoir, producing superior fish growth rates relative to both the reservoir and the free-flowing channelized river (except channel catfish).
- **Channelized river reach** (731 mi below Sioux City): a simplified, high-current, low-turbidity-variance habitat lacking quiet-water refugia, brush/log substrate, and backwaters — loss of spawning/nursery niche and food-web base (plankton, benthos).
- **Energy base**: Aquatic food web in the unchannelized/channelized river is driven largely by allochthonous terrestrial organic material (drift), with ~99% of drift of terrestrial origin (Langemeier, 1965), supporting Berner's (1951) hypothesis that higher trophic levels (fish) depend on terrestrially derived energy inputs in this turbid, low-primary-production system.
- **Pollution gradient niche stress**: Increasing industrial/municipal pollution and thermal loading downstream near population centers (e.g., Kansas City) creates additional physiological stress zones, compounding habitat degradation from channelization.

## Key Findings
- **Impoundment footprint**: 6 main-stem dams inundated 897 river miles but created 1,875 mi² (1,261,000 acres) of standing water; combined reservoir volume ≈ average 3-year river flow at Sioux City (Benson, 1968).
- **Reservoir water quality**: Clearwater, plankton-rich, no dissolved oxygen deficiency; photosynthetic production in [[Lewis and Clark Lake]] increased 2–3× from headwater to tailwater.
- **Fish decline in reservoirs**: Adult and young fish stocks declined in Lewis and Clark Lake, Francis Case, Sharpe, and Oahe since 1958 due to shoreline habitat loss; growth rates of most fishes also declined within reservoirs proper.
- **Species diversity loss**: Benson (1968) recorded only 59 fish species in main-stem reservoirs vs. 80 species listed by Pflieger (1971) for the Missouri River reach — supporting a post-impoundment reduction in species diversity.
- **Turbidity reduction**: Kansas City average annual turbidity fell from 1,300–3,200 ppm (1918–1952) to 470–800 ppm post-impoundment (~65% decrease); Kansas City sediment load down 25–30% (Jordan, 1968); St. Louis turbidity down >50%.
- **Alkalinity buffering**: Reservoirs act as mixing basins that delay seasonal alkalinity trends by 1–6 months downstream and reduce alkalinity via algal photosynthesis/CaCO₃ sedimentation.
- **Plankton export**: Lewis and Clark Lake discharges ~12,619 metric tons (wet weight) of zooplankton annually into the tailwater, driving superior fish growth there (Cowell, 1967; Walburg et al., 1971).
- **Temperature regulation**: Impoundments delay spring warming and fall cooling downstream due to thermal inertia of large water masses.
- **Channelization losses**:
  - 731 river miles below Sioux City fully channelized; river shortened by 75 miles (Vance, 1971).
  - Comparison of 1879 vs. 1967 Corps maps (91 mi sampled) showed a 64% loss of water/wetland area (37,463 → 18,315 acres; 265 acres/mile lost) (Robinson, 1973).
  - Commercial fishery harvest fell from 1.5 million lbs (1894) to ~270,000 lbs (1963); MDC records show decline from 503,117 lbs (1945) to 77,317 lbs (1966), with licensed fishermen dropping from 771 to 391.
  - Catch composition shifted from catfish/buffalo dominance (pre-1900) to carp dominance (50–80% of catch) post-1900.
- **Plankton in channelized river**: Net plankton density averaged only 67 individuals/liter (Berner, 1951) — orders of magnitude lower than the unchannelized [[Illinois River]], where backwaters/sloughs were the primary plankton source (Kofoid, 1908).
- **Benthos standing crop** (lb/acre wet weight), unchannelized (Ponca, NE) vs. channelized (Plattsmouth, NE):

| Substrate | Unchannelized | Channelized |
|---|---|---|
| Main Stream | 0.52 | 0.02 |
| Mud Bank | 0.47 | 0.37 |
| Chute | 0.04 | N/A |
| Pile Dike | N/A | 0.04 |
| **Total** | **1.03** | **0.43** |

  Contrast with unchannelized [[Illinois River]] benthos: 261 lb/acre (Berner, 1951) — Missouri River channelized reaches averaged <0.4 lb/acre.
- **Energy source**: ~99% of invertebrate drift material was of terrestrial origin (Langemeier, 1965), reinforcing reliance of higher trophic levels on allochthonous inputs.
- **Pollution events**: Major fish kill in 1964 near Kansas City (Whitley, 1964) linked to industrial pollution; persistent organochlorine pesticide and PCB residues detected in Missouri River fish (Henderson et al., 1969).
- **Thermal pollution**: Natural summer water temperatures already exceed 88°F in unaltered lower river stretches; new 2,500 MW plant and two nuclear plants (Nebraska) add localized thermal stress via heated cooling effluent.
- **Overall system verdict**: Impoundments produced net water-quality *benefits* to the downstream flowing river (turbidity, alkalinity, temperature moderation, tailwater fish growth), while channelization produced net *detriments* (habitat, plankton, benthos, and fishery losses), and pollution/thermal stress remain unresolved emerging threats.
