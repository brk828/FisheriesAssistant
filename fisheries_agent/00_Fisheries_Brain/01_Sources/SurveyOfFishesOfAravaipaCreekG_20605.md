# 1974 Survey of Fishes of Aravaipa Creek, Graham and Pinal Counties, Arizona

#ichthyology #desert-ecology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #stream-ecology #invasive-species

## Executive Summary

A fish survey of [[Aravaipa Creek]] in [[Arizona]] was conducted November 9-11, 1974, by Arizona State University's Ichthyology class under supervision of Dr. W. L. Minckley. The survey documented seven native fish species across 27 sampling stations using seining and electrofishing. Key findings include dramatic increases in [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) populations (>60% of total fish in mainstream), corresponding declines in [[Gila Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), stable [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta grahami*) populations, and concerning declines in [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*). The invasive [[Green Sunfish]] (*Chaenobryttus cyanellus*) was discovered as far upstream as [[Virgus Canyon]], representing a significant threat to native species. Population shifts are attributed to ongoing "sanding in" processes and sustained low discharge conditions.

## Background & Description

[[Aravaipa Creek]] in [[Pinal County]] and [[Graham County]], [[Arizona]], represents one of the few remaining relatively unaltered desert stream systems in Arizona. The creek supports seven native fish species, each from different genera, making it valuable for studying desert stream ecology. Continuous monitoring has been conducted since 1963 (Barber and Minckley, 1966; Minckley, 1972, 1973b).

**Native Species Present:**

- **[[Longfin Dace]]** (*Agosia chrysogaster*, [[Cyprinidae]]): Prefers shallow streams with constantly shifting sandy beds
- **[[Gila Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*, [[Cyprinidae]]): Found above gravel beds in deeper, fast-flowing water and shallow pools at riffle ends
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta grahami*, [[Cyprinidae]]): Inhabits pools and deeper, slower-moving waters
- **[[Gila Mountain-sucker]]** (*Pantosteus clarki*, [[Catostomidae]]): Feeds almost exclusively on [[periphyton]] (aufwuchs) on permanent stream bed structures
- **[[Gila Sucker]]** (*Catostomus insignis*, [[Catostomidae]]): Feeds mostly on aquatic invertebrates including insect larvae; prefers pools
- **[[Speckled Dace]]** (*Rhinichthys osculus*, [[Cyprinidae]]): Limited range in upper stream segments
- **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*, [[Cyprinidae]]): Restricted distribution

**Introduced Species:**

- **[[Green Sunfish]]** (*Chaenobryttus cyanellus*, [[Centrarchidae]]): Introduced by ranchers into artificial ponds; carnivorous centrarchid

## Ecological & Environmental Context

[[Aravaipa Creek]] is experiencing a prolonged period of "sanding in"—gradual sand accumulation on the stream bed—coupled with sustained low discharge levels. These conditions are driving major ecological shifts in fish community composition through habitat alteration and competitive interactions.

**Habitat Processes:**

The sanding process fills natural pools, decreases surface water (more subsurface flow through sand), and shifts the stream from a permanent gravel-based system toward a more temporary sandy-bed system. This process typically originates at upper stream reaches and progresses downstream. Sand accumulation reduces [[periphyton]] establishment on permanent substrates, directly affecting species dependent on this food source.

**Competitive Relationships:**

Multiple apparent competitive exclusion relationships exist:

1. **[[Longfin Dace]] vs. [[Gila Spikedace]]**: Where one dominates, the other is nearly absent. Longfin dace benefits from sandy, shallow conditions; Gila spikedace requires more permanent gravel beds and deeper water.

2. **[[Gila Mountain-sucker]] vs. [[Gila Sucker]]**: Strong inverse relationship; where one is abundant, the other is reduced. Gila mountain-sucker depends on periphyton on permanent substrates; Gila sucker feeds on invertebrates less dependent on substrate permanence.

3. **[[Loach Minnow]] vs. [[Speckled Dace]]**: Presence of one tends to necessitate near-absence of the other, though 1974 data were too sparse for strong conclusions.

**Reproductive Ecology:**

Reproductive success of smaller species (speckled dace, loach minnow) appears closely linked to substantial runoff events in desert streams. Continued low discharge may suppress recruitment.

## Key Findings

**Sampling Methods:**

- 27 stations sampled (Stations 1-13 used seining and electrofishing; Stations 14-27 used 10-foot seine with 1/8-inch mesh exclusively)
- Standard protocol: 2 minutes seining per station covering approximately 1,000 ft² area
- Results reported as percent occurrence of each species relative to total fish captured

**Stations 1-13 (Lower Stream, Pooled Results):**

- [[Longfin Dace]]: 61% (dramatic increase from previous years)
- [[Gila Spikedace]]: 9% (dramatic decline)
- [[Roundtail Chub]]: 24% (stable)
- [[Gila Mountain-sucker]]: 3% (stable, approximately same as 1973)
- [[Gila Sucker]]: 52% (declining trend continues)
- [[Speckled Dace]]: 0%
- [[Loach Minnow]]: 0%
- [[Green Sunfish]]: 6% (found in artificial ponds and nearby deep pools)

**Critical Findings:**

1. **[[Longfin Dace]] Expansion**: Increased to >60% in mainstream at most stations; benefits from sanding and shallow-water habitat expansion

2. **[[Gila Spikedace]] Decline**: Sharp population drops; losing habitat as gravel beds are covered by sand

3. **[[Gila Mountain-sucker]] Decline**: Decreasing upstream (Stations 14-26) due to periphyton loss from sanding; maintaining populations downstream and in isolated pools (Stations 21, 24 with 62% and 57% respectively)

4. **[[Gila Sucker]] Increase**: Substantial increases upstream as competition from Gila mountain-sucker decreases; less affected by sanding (invertebrate prey less substrate-dependent)

5. **[[Speckled Dace]] Rarity**: Found at only two stations; average decreased from 5% (1973) to 2% (1974)

6. **[[Loach Minnow]] Decline**: Found at only two stations; drastically reduced from 1972 levels; dangerously low numbers

7. **[[Green Sunfish]] Range Expansion**: Found at [[Virgus Canyon]] (Station 25, 6% of population)—first record this far upstream; likely planted by humans rather than natural migration; poses significant threat to native species

**Recommendations:**

1. Continue annual monitoring of fish populations
2. Implement broader ecological study of entire stream system (physical, chemical, biological factors)
3. Develop immediate action plan to control green sunfish spread, particularly removal from Virgus Canyon
4. Carefully plan any management actions to avoid disturbing other stream components
5. Maintain preservation of Aravaipa Creek in natural state

**Conservation Concerns:**

The [[Green Sunfish]] has demonstrated ability to eliminate native fishes in other Arizona streams (e.g., [[Black River]], [[San Carlos River]]). Its presence at Virgus Canyon represents an alarming development requiring immediate attention. The loach minnow and speckled dace populations are at dangerously low levels and require close monitoring.
