# Postflood Recolonization Pathways of Macroinvertebrates in a Lowland Sonoran Desert Stream

#macroinvertebrates #aquatic-insects #stream-ecology

## Executive Summary

This study examines how stream macroinvertebrates recolonize [[Sycamore Creek]], a lowland [[Sonoran Desert]] stream, after floods. The authors tested the hypothesis that most taxa recolonize through aerial pathways. Using colonization trays, mark-recapture of adult beetles, diel drift and upstream-movement nets, and sediment cores, they found that aerial colonization dominated the taxon-level picture in both seasons. Drift and upstream movements, by contrast, supplied most individuals but came from relatively few taxa. Seasonal flood timing shaped which aerial colonists dominated.

## Background & Description

Stream macroinvertebrates are periodically reduced by disturbances such as ice scour, floods and drought. Recovery depends on [[Recolonization Pathways]], which shape community composition and reflect selective pressures on species' life histories. [[Williams and Hynes]] identified four principal pathways:

- [[Aerial Colonization]] by flying adults
- [[Drift]] (downstream movement in the water column)
- [[Upstream Movement]] by crawling or swimming organisms
- [[Vertical Movement]] from deeper substrates

In permanent streams downstream drift usually dominates. In intermittent streams, aerial and vertical sources are the main routes. Prior studies in African and Canadian streams supported this pattern.

Floods in the Sonoran Desert occur in two seasons:

- **Winter (November to April):** large frontal systems produce floods and extended high flows lasting days to weeks.
- **Summer (July to October):** locally intense thunderstorms produce [[Flash Floods]] lasting only a few hours.

Floods in both seasons eliminate 80-100% of the benthic fauna (Gray, 1980).

Of the 104 taxa present at Sycamore Creek, 40 have long-lived, flight-capable aquatic adults (Coleoptera and Hemiptera). Most remaining taxa (mayflies, caddisflies, small dipterans) reproduce year-round and lack dormant substrate stages. Few taxa are dormant, such as [[Mesocapnia Arizonensis]] and [[Tabanus Dorsifer]]. The authors therefore expected vertical movements to contribute few colonists.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site and sampling.** Work was done at Sycamore Creek, Arizona (33°45'N, 111°30'W), at sites 620-650 m elevation on the main stem. Flood conditions included:

- March 1979 flood: maximum discharge 20 m³/s, preceded by December 1978 (159 m³/s) and January 1979 (74 m³/s) floods.
- August 1979: a series of flash floods, with no floods in the 4 months before.

**Methods.**
- *Beetle marking:* adults were marked by scratching an elytron, which marks permanently without injury. Experiments covered side pools (October 1979), a 10-km reach (August 1979) and one site (March 1979).
- *Diel sampling:* drift and upstream nets were set in June 1979, 9 weeks after the last flood, at a stable-flowing segment.
- *Colonization trays:* plywood trays with mesh allowed colonization through a single pathway. Aerial trays (1600 cm² each) and vertical trays (680 cm² each) were left 9 days. Drift and upstream nets ran 24 hours and were scaled to 1 m².
- *Sediment cores:* taken in July 1979 at one site in main-channel, channel-edge and bank habitats, to 50-60 cm depth.

**Key constraints.** Trays underestimated aerial and vertical contributions. Mayflies and small dipterans can develop from egg to adult in 6-14 days, emerging insects in vertical trays died before being counted, and predation was extreme. In one aerial tray, 117 adult *Deronectes nebulosus* eliminated all mayflies and chironomids.

## Key Findings

**1. Aerial colonization dominated by taxon count.**
- In summer, 17 of 26 principal-pathway taxa (65%) colonized aerially (Table 3).
- In winter, 14 of 22 taxa (64%) did so (Table 4).
- The hypothesis was supported in both seasons.

**2. Drift and upstream movement supplied most individuals.**
- Summer: drift contributed 73% of individuals and upstream movement 16%.
- Winter: drift contributed 65% and upstream movement 23%.
- Individuals came from relatively few taxa, mainly Baetis quilleri and Cricotopus sp. in summer, and Probezzia sp. and chironomids in winter.

**3. Seasonal differences in aerial colonists.**
- Winter aerial colonists were mainly non-reproductive aquatic beetle and hemipteran adults, which avoid floods.
- Summer colonists were mainly ovipositing adults (Ephemeroptera and Diptera).
- Frequent winter floods reduced or eliminated short-lived adult populations such as caddisflies.
- Isolated summer floods had little effect on populations, because adults still in the stream before flooding quickly recolonized.

**4. Low dispersal in most beetles.**
- Of 50 dytiscids and hydrophilids marked in side pools, only 4 were recaptured.
- None of 59 *Tropisternus ellipticus* marked in August was recaptured.
- All 12 *Helichus immsi* marked in March stayed within a few meters over 2 weeks.

**5. Drift is limited to few taxa.**
- In June, 21 of 40 taxa were collected in drift, but five taxa made up 96% of drifting individuals (Baetis quilleri 61%, Cricotopus sp. 11%, Brillia sp. 11%, Simulium sp. 8%, Dicrotendipes sp. 5%).
- Only 7 taxa showed behavioral drift, peaking near midnight. The other taxa drifted rarely, mainly with disintegrating algal mats.
- Only 10 taxa moved upstream, and upstream numbers were about 4% of drift numbers.

**6. Upstream movement increases after floods.**
- The drift-to-upstream ratio was about 25:1 in nonflood periods and 1.5-4.6:1 after floods, indicating stronger upstream movement following high discharge.

**7. Vertical distribution is shallow.**
- Most taxa and individuals were in the upper 10 cm of substrate.
- Deeper records were few: *Helicopsyche mexicana* at 30 cm, *Tabanus dorsifer* at 40 cm, and chironomids and ceratopogonids below 10 cm.
- No taxa were found along the bank, so habitat boundaries follow saturated substrate.

**8. Vertical movement mattered more after winter floods.**
- Vertical movements contributed few individuals in summer but were more important after winter floods (Table 4: 10% of individuals).

**9. Colonization tray timing.**
- In a test of tray deployment, 9 days yielded 1706 individuals from 18 taxa, compared with 1238 individuals from 21 taxa at 3 days and 1607 individuals from 18 taxa at 6 days.

**10. Conclusion.** Postflood recolonization occurs mainly from aerial sources, because benthic organisms avoid severe substrate scour by leaving as aerial adults. The numerical dominance of drift likely reflects secondary dispersal of propagules from limited oviposition sites, mainly along stable channel edges. Floods are an important selective pressure in lowland desert streams.
