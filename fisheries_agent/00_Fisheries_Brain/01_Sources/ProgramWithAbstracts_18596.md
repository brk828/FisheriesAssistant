# Desert Fishes Council 24th Annual Symposium (1992): Program, Agency Reports and Abstracts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #Cyprinodontidae #Salmonidae #Poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the program and table of contents for the 24th Annual Symposium of the [[Desert Fishes Council]], held November 18–21, 1992. It combines a session schedule with abstracts for roughly 70 agency reports, contributed papers, workshop talks, business-meeting resolutions and posters. The meeting covered the conservation, recovery and management of endangered and endemic fishes of arid western North America and northern Mexico. Agency reports from federal, state and conservation organizations dominate the early sessions. Contributed research spans genetics, life history, habitat use, parasitology, hydrology, archaeology and non-native species impacts.

## Background & Description

The Desert Fishes Council (DFC) was founded in 1969. The program states that the organization will celebrate its 25th anniversary the following year and reports membership growth of more than 500. The symposium was organized into four to five sessions per day, using concurrent tracks (3A and 3B) in La Posada venues. Presenters are marked by asterisks as students, and Hubbs Student Paper competitors are identified separately.

Several recurring threads organize the material:

- **Agency reports:** Conservation activities by the [[USDA Forest Service]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], [[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Nature Conservancy]], and state agencies in Arizona, California, Nevada, Texas, Utah and Kansas.
- **Recovery programs:** Upper Colorado River fishes, [[Apache Trout]] (a Trout Unlimited and DFC workshop), and the Virgin River and Ash Meadows systems.
- **Taxonomy and genetics:** Pupfish, cutthroat trout, sucker and chub phylogeography, using allozymes, mtDNA, RFLP and PCR.
- **Ecology and life history:** Larval drift, young-of-year feeding, predator avoidance, and food webs of endangered fishes.
- **Policy:** The [[Aquatic Nuisance Prevention and Control Act]] (PL 101-646) and three DFC resolutions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most of the work concerns fishes of isolated desert waters, where spring-fed habitats, drought, groundwater pumping and exotic species are the dominant threats. Key concerns in the abstracts include:

- **Groundwater and spring declines:** Water-level decline at [[Devil's Hole]] and a 6–8 inch drop after the June 28, 1992 earthquake. Texas spring systems and Chihuahuan Desert springs are described as declining.
- **Exotic species:** Non-native [[Largemouth Bass]], cichlids, mollies, goldfish and gizzard shad threaten native fish. The [[Asian Fish Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) appeared suddenly in 1990 humpback chub in the Little Colorado River.
- **Hybridization:** [[Pecos Pupfish]] are being genetically replaced by introduced sheepshead minnow; [[Gila]] species show introgression in the Colorado basin.
- **Habitat disturbance:** Livestock damage in Nevada and Utah, dewatering in the Virgin and Snake rivers, a 1990 wildfire (the Dude Fire) in Arizona, and hydrologic change from dams.
- **Ecosystem interactions:** Zooplankton, benthic microcrustaceans, macroinvertebrates and mollusks are documented as food resources and habitat indicators.

## Key Findings

### Agency Reports and Program Status

- **The Nature Conservancy:** Has been involved in protecting or managing more than 30 desert fish species or subspecies, 19 of them federally listed. It owns or manages at least 10 preserves. Future work is expected to focus on water rights and cooperative projects with public land agencies.
- **USDA Forest Service:** Eighty-eight listed or candidate fish species and 26 federally listed invertebrates occur on National Forest System lands. About 41 federally listed fish occur in the DFC region. Management of nearly 25% of those 41 species is supported by specific programs.
- **Bring Back The Natives:** A USFS, BLM and National Fish and Wildlife Foundation program launched in September 1991. The first year funded 19 projects for 14 native species. Phase two includes 34 projects in 13 states.
- **Bureau of Land Management:** In 1992 acquired 5,027 acres of desert dace habitat at Soldier Meadows, Nevada, plus a conservation easement on 5,000 adjacent acres. Acquired 1,260 acres and water rights on New Mexico's Black River, and 1,200 acres at Dos Palmas, California.
- **Upper Colorado Recovery Program:** A $3.0M work plan was developed for FY 1993. Razorback sucker status is described as critical, with few adults and no recruitment. Bonytail chub are very rare, with none seen in the Upper Basin for several years. Critical habitat designation was expected to reach draft rule stage in September 1993.
- **Nevada Department of Wildlife:** Responsible for 75 endemic non-game fish taxa. Nevada has 17 federally endangered, 5 threatened and 33 candidate species or subspecies.
- **Lower Colorado (Bureau of Reclamation):** Reared razorback suckers in Lake Mohave backwaters, where about 10,000 fingerlings (average TL 68 mm) were transferred from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. About 6,000 bonytail chub were also transferred in October 1992. Collected 30 adult razorback suckers from Lake Mead, the largest such collection in over 20 years.

### Key Population Data

- **Devil's Hole pupfish:** Combined spring counts averaged 272 in April 1992 (239 in 1988, 151 in 1987). Fall counts averaged 472 in October 1991. Numbers have stabilized at an annual peak of around 500 since 1988. The Hoover Dam refugium held fewer than 5 fish, and no reproduction was recorded after 10 fish were added in December 1991.
- **Pahrump killifish:** The combined population at three sites is approximately 24,000 fish.
- **Hiko and Blue Link springfish:** Mark-recapture estimates were 7,450 and 6,000 fish respectively. Crystal Spring fish were estimated at only 20–30 due to exotics.
- **Razorback sucker (Green River):** Total population declined 50% since 1988. Recapture data show limited recruitment.
- **Humpback chub:** 1,324 of 1,392 fish (95%) captured in the mainstem Colorado River from October 1990 to June 1992 were in a 13-km reach. Adult chub showed high fidelity to specific locales: 83.3% of 48 radiotagged adults had net displacement under 2 km.
- **Rio Grande silvery minnow:** Has undergone a 95% reduction in historic range. It was taken at 46 of 81 (57%) mainstem collections in 2002 study reaches along 186 km of river.
- **Warner sucker:** Drift behavior was studied from May to July 1992 using drift nets and manual flow experiments.

### Genetics and Taxonomy

- **Humboldt cutthroat trout:** 62 of 64 fish (96.9%) from seven populations shared a distinct mtDNA haplotype. Morphological discriminant analysis classified them correctly at 95.8% and 93.2%. The authors argue for formal subspecies recognition.
- **Cutthroat trout PCR-RFLP:** 40 unique restriction fragment polymorphisms separated all cutthroat subspecies and some populations.
- **Razorback sucker mtDNA:** Lake Mohave was the most diverse population. Diversity declined from south to north, consistent with reinvasion after Pleistocene glaciation or a historic cline in population size.
- **Pecos pupfish:** Allozyme and mtDNA frequencies did not differ significantly at any sample location. This supports genetic replacement or augmentation rather than displacement.
- **Death Valley pupfishes:** Allozymes from 32 loci support monophyly of the *C. nevadensis* complex, but a diphyletic origin was nearly as parsimonious. The authors suggest introgressive hybridization.
- **Desert pupfish refugia:** Divergence among refugia was attributed mainly to genetic drift and founder effects. One significant association suggested variation was lost through serial bottlenecks.

### Ecology and Management Findings

- **Non-lethal sampling:** A stomach pumping technique evacuated 100% of gut contents in 20 roundtail chub with no pharyngeal tooth damage. Fin tissue alone is a limited source of gene products for razorback sucker genetics.
- **Electrofishing injury:** Spinal injury rates were 8% (n=45) for a VVP-15 single pass, 13% (n=110) for a CPS single pass, and 30% (n=65) for unmarked fish on the fourth pass.
- **Hurricane and wildfire:** The Dude Fire (1990) burned over 25,000 acres, destroyed over 50 homes and killed 6 people. Immediate post-fire fish and macroinvertebrate sampling showed no marked change, but later flooding strongly affected populations.
- **Bonytail reintroduction:** Of 152 hatchery bonytail released in the Green River in 1988–1989, nearly all died within two weeks.
- **Razorback larvae light traps:** Among 226 trap sets (1,327.6 trap hours), larvae responded only to white light, not to bait or cyalume sticks.
- **Asian fish tapeworm:** Infestation was 91.7% in May–June 1990 chub larvae and 100% in 1990 young-of-year. Only 44% of 18 adults from 1990 were infested. The effects on hosts are not well studied.
- **Gambusia predation:** Experiments indicate that size-selective predation by mosquitofish on small Heterandria, rather than competition, is the dominant interaction.
- **Microcrustaceans (Green River):** 25 species collected. Floodplain wetlands held the most species and densities of about 205,922 per cubic meter in plankton.
- **June sucker:** White bass predation was limited only by gape size, and white bass selectively fed on gizzard shad (Chesson's alpha 0.677) over June sucker (0.322).
- **Historic fish use:** Archaeofaunal remains from the Pueblo Grande Hohokam site (A.D. 1100–1450) include nearly 30% fish among 10,000 bones. Most fish were under about 250 mm, suggesting size decline.

### Policy and Resolutions

- The Aquatic Nuisance Prevention and Control Act of 1990 required agency reports to Congress on non-indigenous species, with progress reported as delayed.
- **Resolution 1992-1** encourages the Aquatic Conservation Network and non-professional aquarists to participate in professionally guided conservation.
- **Resolution 1992-2** urges protection of the Los Angeles Basin's remaining inland fishes. Four of seven native species were extirpated by the late 1940s, and the remaining three cyprinoids are in danger.
- **Resolution 1992-3** thanks the Centro Ecológico de Sonora for establishing a translations subcommittee so Proceedings abstracts can be published in Spanish.
- A proposal by J. E. Johnson would create the first DFC standing committee to monitor selected southwestern fishes and publish annual species accounts.
