## Executive Summary
This document is a 1988 interagency correspondence from the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (Lake Powell Fisheries Investigations) to Arizona State University, relaying historical capture records of two endemic, rare Colorado River Basin fishes — the [[Razorback Sucker]] and the [[Humpback Chub]] — collected in Lake Powell and its San Juan River arm during the early-to-mid 1970s and 1980s. The records document sporadic but confirmed reproductive-condition captures of Razorback Sucker at tributary confluences (Piute Farms Wash, Neskahi Canyon) between 1982–1984, and two isolated angler-caught Humpback Chub records from Antelope Canyon in 1977. The letter notes that no Razorback Sucker had been captured since 1984, underscoring concerns about population decline and prompting referral to ongoing nongame fish surveys at the mouth of the San Juan River.

## Background & Description
### Razorback Sucker (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- **Total Length (TL):** 545–560 mm across all recorded specimens
- **Weight:** 1800–2000 g (measured in the 1982 sample)
- **Sex/Reproductive state:** All captured individuals were ripe (spawning-condition) males
- **Secondary sexual characteristics:**
  - Pronounced tubercules on the posterior half of the body, especially concentrated on the anal fin
  - Dark red spawning coloration mottling most of the body
  - Vent notably swollen and distended, described as resembling a "pouch for sperm storage"
  - Fish would emit sperm when stroked (manual expression), even post-peak spawning condition

### Humpback Chub (*Gila cypha*)
- No morphometric data recorded; specimens were angler-caught
- One specimen preserved as a photographic record only (fish harvested)
- A second specimen was seized by conservation officers (COs) but could not be kept alive; reportedly sent for further examination/records

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Razorback Sucker**
  - Captured specifically at tributary confluence/backwater habitats: [[Piute Farms Wash]] and [[Neskahi Canyon]] along the San Juan River arm of [[Lake Powell]]
  - Capture timing (April and November) spans both spring and fall, with April captures showing fish "past the spawning peak," suggesting these confluence habitats serve as **spawning or staging areas**
  - Captured using experimental gill nets (100–200 ft long), fished over multi-day/multi-night efforts, indicating low catch-per-unit-effort consistent with a rare/endangered population
- **Humpback Chub**
  - Recorded from [[Antelope Canyon]], another canyon tributary system within Lake Powell
  - Captures were incidental (angler-caught) rather than from targeted scientific sampling, suggesting very low encounter rates in standard surveys

## Key Findings
- Only a handful of confirmed [[Razorback Sucker]] captures exist across a decade (1982, 1983, 1984), all at San Juan River tributary mouths, all documented as ripe males in spawning condition
- No Razorback Sucker captures were recorded after 1984, a nearly four-year gap by the time of the 1988 letter — a strong indicator of population decline or extreme rarity in Lake Powell
- Both confirmed [[Humpback Chub]] records (1977) came from **incidental angler catches** rather than systematic surveys, with one fish lost before it could be preserved for study, highlighting the difficulty of monitoring this species in reservoir habitat
- The correspondence explicitly frames these captures as the **total known records of Threatened & Endangered (T&E) fish** in Lake Powell over roughly a decade, emphasizing the extreme rarity of both taxa in this reservoir system
- The letter recommends redirecting inquiries to the [[Utah Division of Wildlife Resources]] nongame section's ongoing endemic fish studies at the mouth of the [[San Juan River]] as the best current source of status information
- Findings support the broader conservation narrative that [[Lake Powell]], despite being a reservoir, retains connectivity value for endemic Colorado River Basin fishes via its inundated tributary canyon systems
