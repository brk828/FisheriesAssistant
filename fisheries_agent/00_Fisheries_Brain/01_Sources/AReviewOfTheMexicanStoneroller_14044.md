# Review of the Mexican Stoneroller, *Campostoma ornatum* Girard (Pisces: Cyprinidae)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1976 taxonomic review by Brooks M. Burr, published in the *Transactions of the San Diego Society of Natural History*, examines the [[Mexican Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma ornatum*) across its range. Based on morphological study of specimens from throughout its distribution, the author concludes that the species is distinctive within the genus *Campostoma* and is highly variable, so the nominal subspecies *C. ornatum pricei* should be discontinued and the species treated as one variable entity. The paper also addresses the species' geographic variation, dispersal history, ecology, relationships, and conservation status.

## Background & Description

The genus *Campostoma* comprised three morphologically similar species: the widespread [[Central Stoneroller]] (*C. anomalum*), the [[Largescale Stoneroller]] (*C. oligolepis*), and *C. ornatum*, which occurs mainly in west-central Mexico and is allopatric to *C. anomalum*.

Taxonomic history is extensive, with the species listed in dozens of faunal reports and checklists from 1856 onward. The original description was by Girard (1856). Rutter (1896) reported that intestinal loops did not encircle the air bladder in specimens from Rucker Canyon, Arizona, and the Rio Conchos, Chihuahua, a finding significant because the genus diagnosis had largely relied on this trait. Jordan and Thoburn had described *C. pricei* from Rucker Canyon, and its status as a synonym was debated.

Key morphological features:
- **Scales:** Very small scales. Lateral-line scales 54–84 (usually 58–77); body circumferential scales 44–64 (usually 47–60); predorsal scales 25–40 (usually 27–36); caudal peduncle scales 23–32 (usually 24–31).
- **Gill rakers:** 14–20 on the first arch.
- **Fin rays:** Dorsal and pelvic rays 8; caudal usually 19; anal usually 7; pectoral 16–18.
- **Body:** Stout, with a somewhat acute, rounded snout; ventral mouth; small eye set high on the head.
- **Size:** Moderate; largest specimen 114 mm SL (male). Largest female 80 mm SL.
- **Tubercles:** Breeding males lack nape and anterolateral tubercles but bear head tubercles; females also bear head tubercles, a unique trait in the genus.
- **Intestine:** Loops encircled the posterior air bladder in only 12 of 60 (20%) specimens checked.
- **Coloration:** Usually mottled; juveniles often have a dark lateral stripe. Breeding males show a velvet-black medial fin band; breeding females show a black dorsal fin band.

Type material: Girard (1859) cited a syntypic series (USNM 77). The author designated one specimen (about 105.5 mm SL, with pharyngeal arches removed) as lectotype, and the other three became paralectotypes (USNM 214999). The holotype of *C. pricei* (SU 1177) is a 76.7 mm SL nuptial male from Rucker Canyon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat:** Mexican stonerollers occur mostly in riffles, chutes, and pools of creeks and rivers. Water is warm and clear (sometimes slightly turbid). The largest collections come from gravel runs or gravel-bottom pools. Vegetation may be abundant or absent. The species favors headwater situations, which may explain its absence from mainstream collections.

**Reproduction:** Nuptial males and gravid females were collected March–June in Chihuahua and Sonora, in February in Durango (Rio Nazas), and tuberculate males in October in Texas. Nuptial males ranged 55–105 mm SL. In Tornillo Creek, Texas, the breeding season is probably winter and spring. In Rucker Canyon, cleared gravel areas suggesting spawning were seen in late May.

**Diet:** Intestinal contents suggest a diet similar to *C. anomalum*, consisting mainly of diatoms, bacteria, and algae.

**Parasites and predation:** Specimens were relatively free of external parasites, except a heavily diseased collection from Rio Trujillo (a monogenetic fluke). Large nematodes were found in intestines of Rio Trujillo adults. One predation record exists: specimens in the stomachs of *Salmo gairdneri* (rainbow trout) reported by McNatt (1974).

**Biogeography:** The species is widespread in Mexico, with its center of distribution in Chihuahua, Sonora, and Durango. Its dispersal likely involved stream capture (e.g., the Rio Conchos capture by the Rio Papigochic) and former pluvial lake connections between the Rio Nazas, Rio Trujillo, and the Rio Grande basin. Competition with [[Plains Killifish]] (*Fundulus kansae*) may have contributed to its decline in Texas.

**Conservation status:** Miller (1972) regarded it as threatened in Texas and Arizona because of drying streams. It is recognized as a peripherally threatened species in the United States. Minckley (1973) considered it extinct in Arizona, but it was found still common in Rucker Canyon and Leslie Creek, with specimens collected as late as 1974.

## Key Findings

- **Taxonomy:** *C. ornatum* is extremely variable in meristic and morphometric traits, but its variation is irregular and discordant from drainage to drainage. Subspecific name *pricei* is discontinued, and the species is treated as one entity pending further evidence.
- **Diagnostic distinctions:** Its very small scales, female head tubercles, and infrequent intestinal coiling around the air bladder separate it from *C. anomalum* and *C. oligolepis* (summarized in a comparison table).
- **Geographic variation:** Mean meristic values generally increase from south to north. Gill raker number and mottling increase from east to west, while body depth decreases. Populations in the Rio Nazas and Rio Trujillo have the deepest bodies; Rio Sonora populations are the most slender with the highest mean gill raker counts.
- **Anomalous populations:** The Rio Santa Isabel population has consistently high scale counts, and the Rio Moctezuma population has low scale counts, with no evident explanation for either.
- **Relationships:** *C. ornatum* is most closely related to *C. anomalum*. It shows primitive traits (small scales, intestinal coiling) and derived traits (loss of nape tubercles, female head tubercles, reduced body size). The author considers it close to the ancestral stock of the genus.
- **Status:** Abundant at many north-central Mexican localities, with more than 300 specimens collected at several sites. Rare in collections from western coastal drainages (Sonora, Urique, Piaxtla), possibly reflecting recent arrival.
- **Recommendations:** Additional breeding-season collections and comparative life history studies may justify a future reappraisal of the Rio Nazas, Rio Trujillo, Rio Moctezuma, and Rio Santa Isabel populations.
