# Arizona Proposed Revision of the List of Threatened Native Wildlife (1987)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #herpetology #ornithology #mammalogy #threatened-species

## Executive Summary

This document is a review packet issued by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (Nongame Branch) in August 1987. It invites expert comment on a proposed revision of the state's List of Threatened Native Wildlife, last revised in 1982. The packet contains a cover letter, ground rules for structured review, a summary table of proposed status changes, and species accounts for fish, amphibians and reptiles, birds, and mammals. Species are sorted into four groups under [[Threatened Species Listing Criteria]] that emphasize historic population declines, identifiable threats, and small populations or ranges within Arizona. Comments were due in September 1987, for presentation to the Arizona Game and Fish Commission in October or December 1987.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Revise the 1982 list for presentation to the Commission. The cover letter notes that a broad professional and public review is the foundation of the list.
- **Review rules:** Comments must be structured and specific. Suggested changes to listing criteria or group definitions are to be submitted separately from comments on list composition, and the department stated it did not anticipate changing group criteria or definitions.
- **Group definitions:**
  - **Group 1:** Extirpated from Arizona in recent history.
  - **Group 2:** Continued presence in jeopardy because of substantial population declines; extirpation highly probable without conservation efforts.
  - **Group 3:** Could be in jeopardy in the foreseeable future. Serious threats have been identified and populations have declined or are limited to few individuals in few localities.
  - **Group 4:** Moderate threat to occupied habitat, but substantial population declines have not been documented.
- **Change codes:** The summary table marks each species as added, deleted, or changed in status, with its 1982 and proposed group.
- **Source quality:** Several page headers and some text are OCR-degraded. The fish section header reads "1992," and the mammal header reads "1997." Context indicates these are the 1987 proposal. The prior list was 1982.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Most listed taxa are tied to riparian and aquatic systems. Dominant threats recur across groups:

- **Habitat alteration and destruction:** Channelization, dams and diversions, groundwater drawdown, riparian clearing, woodcutting, grazing, agriculture, urban and recreational development, and mining.
- **Nonnative species:** Introduced sportfishes and predatory fish, [[Bullfrog]] introductions, and [[Largemouth Bass]] are repeatedly implicated in declines.
- **Other causes:** Pesticides, collecting for the pet trade, persecution, road building, and disturbance at nest and roost sites.

Many fish accounts involve the [[Colorado River]], [[Gila River]], [[Virgin River]], and [[Little Colorado River]] systems, with [[Lake Mohave]] holding important remnant populations. Several taxa are restricted to small springs or isolated ranges, which makes them especially vulnerable.

## Key Findings

### Fish

**Group 1 (extirpated, 6 species):** [[Colorado River Squawfish]], Yaqui shiner, Yaqui sucker, Yaqui catfish, [[Desert Pupfish]], and Monkey Springs pupfish. The Monkey Springs pupfish became extinct around 1971 after habitat alteration and introduction of [[Largemouth Bass]]. Squawfish reintroduction is underway using captive stock in the Salt and Verde drainages. Yaqui fishes are planned for reintroduction on the San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge.

**Group 2 (jeopardy, 13 species):** Examples include [[Gila Trout]] (one locality, Prescott National Forest), [[Bonytail Chub]] (only natural Arizona population in Lake Mohave, with no evidence of recruitment), [[Humpback Chub]] (Grand Canyon and Little Colorado River), [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Gila Topminnow]] (reduced to five natural populations), [[Yaqui Topminnow]], and [[Quitobaquito Pupfish]] (sole natural population at Quitobaquito Springs, [[Organ Pipe Cactus National Monument]]).

**Group 3 (8 species):** Includes Arizona trout, Colorado River and Gila River roundtail chubs, Gila chub, Little Colorado River spinedace, [[Spikedace]], Rio Yaqui longfin dace, and [[Loach Minnow]]. Spikedace is known from only three localities in Arizona.

**Group 4:** Speckled dace only, with population losses linked to trout introductions.

### Amphibians and Reptiles

About 22 taxa are covered.

- **Group 1:** Tarahumara frog, reduced to few individuals; decline possibly linked to heavy metal toxicity.
- **Group 2:** Huachuca tiger salamander (known only from the San Rafael Valley), [[Chiricahua Leopard Frog]], Plains and Relict leopard frogs, [[Desert Tortoise]], flat-tailed horned lizard, narrowheaded and Mexican garter snakes, and Desert-grassland massasauga (four areas plus two historic sites).
- **Group 4:** Sonoran green toad, barking frog, lowland and northern leopard frogs, Plains narrow-mouthed toad, northern casque-headed frog, two fringe-toed lizards, mountain skink, Arizona ridge-nosed rattlesnake, yellow mud turtle, and others.

Desert tortoise declines are attributed mainly to collection for pets and habitat destruction.

### Birds

About 55 taxa are covered.

- **Group 1:** [[California Condor]] (last seen near Williams in 1924), Aplomado falcon, Masked bobwhite (reintroduction underway at Buenos Aires National Wildlife Refuge), and Thick-billed parrot (reintroduction begun in 1986).
- **Group 2:** [[Southern Bald Eagle]] (about 23 nesting pairs as of 1982, with numbers increasing), [[Gray Hawk]] (about 55 nesting pairs), Ferruginous pygmy-owl, and California black rail.
- **Group 3:** Clark's grebe, American bittern, great and snowy egrets, osprey, Mississippi kite (about 20 pairs), northern goshawk, ferruginous hawk, [[Yuma Clapper Rail]], buff-breasted flycatcher, willow flycatcher, and veery.
- **Group 4:** Roughly 40 species, including brown pelican, black-crowned night-heron, wood stork, Botteri's sparrow, and southern spotted owl.

### Mammals

About 25 taxa are covered.

- **Group 1:** Black-tailed prairie dog, New Mexican banner-tailed kangaroo rat, Camp Verde Arizona cotton rat, [[Mexican Gray Wolf]] (only slightly over two dozen survive in zoos), Intermountain gray wolf, grizzly bear (last recorded individual killed in 1935), black-footed ferret, jaguar, and ocelot.
- **Group 2:** Water shrew (three specimens known), [[Sanborn's Long-Nosed Bat]] (drastic recent declines in roost sites and colony sizes), [[Mount Graham Red Squirrel]] (about 100 years of mining-related and other disturbance), Hualapai Mexican vole, river otter, Yuma puma, and [[Sonoran Pronghorn]] (small population in southwestern Arizona).
- **Group 3:** [[Mexican Long-Tongued Bat]] (declining, poorly known) and Navajo Mountain vole.
- **Group 4:** Arizona shrew, red bat, southern yellow bat, spotted bat, meadow jumping mouse, and bat roosts generally. Desert bighorn sheep are also listed.

### Notable Conclusions

- The packet relies on structured, specific public and expert comment before the Commission decision.
- Declines are driven mostly by habitat modification and nonnative species, which affect fish most strongly.
- Reintroduction and captive propagation programs are active for several fish and birds, but the success of many efforts is still unknown.
