# Genetic Marker Identification in Largemouth Bass with Electrophoresis of Low-Risk Tissues

## Executive Summary
This 1986 study (Carmichael, Williamson, Schmidt & Morizot) presents a methodological advance in fisheries genetics: the use of **low-risk, non-lethal tissue samples** (erythrocytes, blood plasma, caudal and pelvic fin clips, and scale-epithelium) for **electrophoretic genotyping** of [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), rather than invasive liver biopsy or lethal necropsy. The researchers screened hatchery-reared stocks representing the [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] subspecies (*M. s. floridanus*), the [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] subspecies (*M. s. salmoides*), and two intergrade hatchery stocks (Marion, Alabama; Inks Dam, Texas). They identified 64 enzyme locus products via necropsy, of which 44 (69%) could also be resolved from low-risk tissues. Combined with prior published loci, a total of 70 structural loci have now been characterized in largemouth bass, 23 of which (33%) are polymorphic. Critically, diagnostic loci distinguishing the Florida and northern subspecies — especially isocitrate dehydrogenase-B (IDHB) — can be resolved from blood or fin tissue alone, enabling >99% certainty subspecies discrimination **without sacrificing or debilitating the fish**. This has significant implications for [[Selective Breeding Programs]], brood-stock management, stock-purity monitoring, and genetic studies of endangered fish populations.

## Background & Description
This paper is a biochemical/genetic study rather than a morphological one; "morphological" data pertain to tissue sampling sites and isozyme phenotypes rather than gross anatomy:

- **Sampled tissues:** red blood cells (erythrocytes), blood plasma, caudal fin, pelvic fin, scale-epithelium, liver (necropsy reference), skeletal muscle (necropsy reference).
- **Sampling method:** Fish anesthetized in 200 mg/L tricaine; blood drawn from caudal vessels via heparinized syringe and centrifuged into plasma/red-cell fractions; a pelvic fin and section of caudal fin excised; scales/epidermis scraped from the caudal peduncle. Tissues stored at −80°C prior to analysis.
- **Electrophoretic technique:** Vertical starch gel electrophoresis (per Brewer 1970; Siciliano and Shaw 1976), using two principal buffer systems — Tris-EDTA-borate (pH 8.0) and Tris-citrate (pH 7.0) — plus Tris-maleate (pH 7.4) for glyoxalase resolution.
- **Enzyme/protein systems assessed:** 44+ presumptive coding loci examined; 36 protein/enzyme systems adequately resolved from non-invasive tissues (Table 1), including ACP, ACON1/2, ADA, AK, ADH, CKA–D, ENO, FDP1/2, FUM, GALT, GAL isoforms, G6PD, GPI1/2, GOT (A/B/M), GAPD1/2, αGPDA/B, GLO, GUK1/2, Hb1/2, ITP, IDHA/B, LDHA/B/C, MDH1–3, ME, MPI, NP1/2, PEP1–3, PGM, 6PGD, PGK, PK1/2, SOD1/2, TPI1/2.
- **Resolution by tissue type (locus products resolvable):**
  - Caudal fin: 40 loci
  - Pelvic fin: 39 loci
  - Scale-epithelium: 38 loci
  - Hemolysates (red blood cells): 34 loci
  - Blood plasma: only 3 loci (excluding plasma proteins/esterases with uncertain locus number)
- Caudal fin sampling alone captured essentially all information obtainable from plasma, pelvic fin, or scale-epithelium, with one exception: α-glucosidase (αGLU), resolved only from plasma and pelvic fin.
- Representative diagnostic phenotypes illustrated (Figure 1): ADA (caudal fin), IDHB (caudal fin), MPI (caudal fin), PEP3 (skeletal muscle), SOD2 (liver), and TPI2 (caudal fin, showing homodimer/heterodimer banding with TPI1).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
While this is primarily a laboratory/genetics methods paper, the ecological context concerns **stock structure and management** of largemouth bass populations:

- **Subspecies distinction:** [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] and [[Northern Largemouth Bass]] are managed as genetically distinct stocks with documented **intergrade hatchery populations** (e.g., Marion, Alabama; Inks Dam, Texas) resulting from historical stocking and introgression.
- **Stocking impacts:** Most recent introductions have involved release of Florida largemouth bass into northern populations, making detection of Florida-specific alleles (e.g., at IDHB) valuable for tracking [[Gene Flow]] and stock contamination in the wild.
- **Habitat/rearing context:** Fish used were hatchery-reared, sourced from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center (San Marcos, Texas), reflecting the aquacultural and fisheries-management framing of the study rather than a wild ecological survey.
- **Applications noted:** Genetic marker identification supports selective breeding, monitoring of brood fish, verification of stocked vs. naturally produced fish, maintenance of strain purity, production of known-genotype offspring, identification of rare/endangered species or individuals, and verification of fish in [[Fishing Tournaments]].

## Key Findings
- A total of **70 structural loci** have now been electrophoretically characterized in largemouth bass (combining this study with prior work), of which **23 loci (33%) are polymorphic**.
- Of the 64 enzyme locus products examined via necropsy in this study, **44 (69%) could be adequately resolved from low-risk (non-lethal) tissues**.
- At least **16 of 23 (70%) known polymorphic loci** can be resolved using blood and/or fin tissue alone — without killing or seriously debilitating the animal.
- **Caudal fin clips alone** yielded resolution of **40 enzyme locus products**, making fin sampling a highly efficient single-tissue source for genetic screening.
- **IDHB (isocitrate dehydrogenase-B)** and **GOT-B (glutamate-oxaloacetate transaminase-B)** are key diagnostic loci separating Florida and northern largemouth bass subspecies (per Philipp et al. 1983); IDHB specifically can be resolved from blood or fin tissue, enabling subspecies identification with **greater than 99% certainty** using only low-risk samples.
- Low-risk tissue sampling permits genotyping of **brood fish** without loss of reproductive value, potentially shortening breeding studies by one or more generations (avoiding the need to genotype offspring to infer parental genotype).
- The technique is particularly valuable for **endangered or threatened species/populations**, where lethal sampling is undesirable or infeasible, since fin-clip-based population genetic studies can rival the loci coverage of many previously published lethal-sampling studies.
- Establishes a transferable methodological framework (echoing enzyme nomenclature and homology work in [[Xiphophorus]] poeciliids per Morizot and Siciliano 1984) for applying **non-lethal electrophoretic genotyping** broadly across fisheries management and aquaculture contexts.
