# Predicting Frost Heaving Susceptibility of Arizona Soils

#forest-soils #frost-heaving #soil-science #pinus-ponderosa

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station research note (RM-295, August 1975) by L. J. Heidmann reports a 1971 study of the [[Frost Heaving]] susceptibility of six northern Arizona forest soils. Fifteen soil variables were tested in a [[Stepwise Regression]]. The final model used [[Bulk Density]], [[Sand Content]], and [[Calcium]] and accounted for 83 percent of the total variation (R²) in heaving. Bulk density and sand content alone gave a two-variable equation that explained 71 percent of the variation and was judged the more practical option. The author notes that the equation still needs further testing before it can be considered reliable.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** Frost heaving is a major cause of first-year mortality of [[Ponderosa Pine]] (*Pinus ponderosa* Laws.) seedlings in northern Arizona (Larson 1961).
- **Mechanism:** Heaving results from segregation of soil water into ice layers (lenses, needle ice, stalactite ice, comb ice). Water in small pores and adsorbed on particles freezes at a lower temperature than pure water. This depressed freezing point creates the free energy that draws water to the freezing zone and lifts the soil (Martin 1958).
- **Pore size and texture:** Water segregates in soils that are permeable and develop negative pressure (tension). Both properties depend on pore size, which depends on particle size or texture. Silty soils are considered ideally suited to heaving (Penner 1958).
- **Scope:** The 1971 comprehensive Arizona study aimed to identify frost-susceptible soils and possible control methods. This note covers only the identification of susceptible soils.
- **Ecosystem context:** The source concerns terrestrial forest soils and tree regeneration. It contains no fish, aquatic invertebrate, plankton, or water chemistry data.

**Soil texture and properties:**
- Soils were sampled at 0 to 2.5, 2.5 to 7.6, and 7.6 to 15.2 cm depths.
- [[Tie Park]] had essentially no sand (0 percent in the surface layer). [[Beaverhead Flat]] and [[Kelly Tank]] were roughly one-half to two-thirds sand.
- All soils had a fairly high silt content.
- Organic matter ranged from less than 1 percent at [[Beaverhead Flat]] to 7 percent at [[Beaver Creek Watershed 14]].
- Bulk density ranged from less than 1 at [[S-3 West]] to 1.79 at [[Beaverhead Flat]].
- Low bulk density is generally associated with finer particles (clays and silts). High bulk density is associated with coarser particles (sands and gravels).

**Clay mineralogy** (X-ray diffraction, 0 to 5 scale):
- Montmorillonite was highest at [[Beaverhead Flat]] and [[Tie Park]], which showed the greatest total heaving in laboratory tests.
- No samples contained chlorite.
- Relative amounts of montmorillonite, mica, vermiculite, and kaolinite varied among sites.

## Key Findings

1. **Predictive model:** Bulk density and sand content (two variables) explained 71 percent of the variation in heaving. Adding calcium raised R² to 83 percent.
   - Two-variable equation: **Y = -2.52 + 3.67 X(BD) - 0.026 X(SAND)**, where Y is heaving in mm/day.
   - Three-variable equation (as printed; calcium coefficient garbled by OCR, verify against original): **Y = -2.67 + 4.10 X(BD) - .90 X(Ca) - .02 X(SAND)**.
   - Heavier soils (higher bulk density) and lower sand content produce more heaving.
2. **Single-variable correlations:**
   - Bulk density: r = 0.61 (strongest with heaving)
   - Montmorillonite: r = 0.53
   - Potassium: r = -0.44
   - Montmorillonite and bulk density: r = 0.67
   - Montmorillonite did not enter the equation even though it was positively correlated with heaving.
3. **Stepwise model fit:** Bulk density entered first and explained 37 percent of variation (R²). Sand content in step two raised this to 71 percent.
4. **Model performance:** The regression performed reasonably well overall. Two exceptions were a negative predicted value for [[Kelly Tank]] at 0 to 2.5 cm, and an unusually large prediction for [[Beaverhead Flat]] at the same depth.
5. **Laboratory test method:**
   - Cylinders were 3.3 by 7.6 cm, made of PVC, and filled with soil sieved to less than 2 mm, packed to field bulk density.
   - Samples were frozen from the surface downward in a [[Freezing Chest]] at a constant ambient temperature of -3°C.
   - Each test ran for 10 days. Surfaces were checked every 8 hours for onset of freezing. Heave height (mm) and total freezing depth were recorded.
   - Analysis of variance showed significant differences among soil samples (P = 0.01).
   - [[Beaverhead Flat]], [[Tie Park]], and [[Beaver Creek Watershed 14]] at 2.5 to 7.6 and 7.6 to 15.2 cm heaved more than the other samples.
6. **Field observations at S-3:**
   - Larson (1961) found that several hundred first-year ponderosa pine seedlings, over half the study population, heaved in one night in October 1957. The laboratory heaving of the S-3 samples was intermediate.
   - Soil at S-3 heaved almost 5 mm in one night in December 1973, at a moisture content of about 50 percent.
7. **Interpretive thresholds:**
   - Author's guideline: a predicted value below 0.5 mm/day suggests heaving is not a problem. A value of 2 to 3 mm/day could indicate a serious problem.
   - Haley (1953) scale: 0 to 0.5 mm/day negligible, 2.0 to 4.0 mm intermediate, over 8 mm/day high.
   - No soil in this study approached Haley's high rate in the laboratory. The author suggests a laboratory rate of 3 to 4 mm/day would indicate high susceptibility.
8. **Mechanistic findings:**
   - Silty soils are most susceptible because their pores are small enough for tension and large enough for water movement.
   - Heavy clay soils have limited permeability. Sandy soils are permeable but develop little tension.
   - Heaving increased with bulk density in a related study (Heidmann and Thorud 1975). Water reached the surface of the least dense samples within minutes, and took several hours in the densest. Total equilibrium water absorption was about the same across density levels.
   - Compaction could increase capillary flow in these high-silt, low-clay soils, but the soils likely cannot be compacted enough to restrict flow.
9. **Clay mineral type matters:**
   - In exploratory tests, [[Montmorillonite]] did not heave during a 7-day freeze, while [[Kaolinite]] heaved as much as 200 percent (Heidmann 1974b).
   - Sodium-saturated montmorillonite holds water between particles without segregating. Calcium-, magnesium-, or hydrogen-saturated montmorillonite holds little water between particles (Grim 1952).
   - Soils here had higher exchangeable calcium and magnesium than sodium, which may leave more water available for segregation and explain the montmorillonite correlation.
10. **Core conclusion:** Frost heaving is directly related to the flow of water to the freezing front. Soil texture and permeability, which control flow rate, are of primary importance. Other variables studied were not strongly correlated with heaving.
