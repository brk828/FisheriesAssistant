# Freshwater Triclads of North America II: New or Little-Known Species of *Phagocata*

#invertebrate-zoology #turbellaria #planariidae #phagocata

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper, part of a revision of the North American freshwater triclad (Turbellaria) fauna, describes one new species and redescribes or expands on three little-known species of the genus [[Phagocata]]. The new species, [[Phagocata Bulbosa]], is described from North Carolina and is morphologically very similar to several other pigmented American planarians, so it can be separated only by serial sections of the reproductive anatomy. The paper also adds data on [[Phagocata Crenophila]] (Utah and California), [[Phagocata Tahoena]] (endemic to [[Lake Tahoe]] so far), and [[Phagocata Oregonensis]] (Portland, Oregon). The author argues that *P. tahoena* deserves full species rank rather than subspecies status under *P. nivea*.

## Background & Description

**Genus concept.** [[Phagocata]] was established by Leidy (1847) as a subgenus of *Planaria* for *Planaria gracilis* Haldeman (1840). Its original distinguishing feature, multiple pharynges, was later found in other planarian genera (*Crenobia*, *Sphalloplana*) and so has no generic significance. The genus is defined by its copulatory apparatus: oviducts that unite into a common oviduct opening into the genital atrium, a male atrium without radial muscle plates, no adenodactyls, and usually two eyes. In the broad sense, including *Fonticola*, *Atrioplanaria* and *Penecurva*, it comprises close to 50 species and subspecies in the Northern Hemisphere (Eurasia and North America).

**Study material.** The author examined specimens collected in the field or sent by collaborators, largely from the [[Carolina Biological Supply Company]] and the Powell Laboratories. Identification relied on [[Copulatory Apparatus]] anatomy in sagittal sections, because external features are unreliable in life.

**Species treated:**

- **[[Phagocata Bulbosa]]** (new species): holotype is a series of sagittal sections (U.S. National Museum No. 40967). Mature animals reach 12 mm long and 1.7 mm wide; the pharynx is about one-fifth of body length.
- **[[Phagocata Crenophila]]** Carpenter, 1969: additional morphology and distribution. Carpenter gave mature lengths of 12–22 mm and widths of 1.5–2 mm; the author's mature specimens averaged about 17 mm by 2 mm.
- **[[Phagocata Tahoena]]** Kawakatsu, 1968: a plump species, living animals about 6 mm by 1 mm, with a rounded head and a weakly developed submarginal adhesive zone.
- **[[Phagocata Oregonensis]]** Hyman, 1963: an unpigmented, white species up to 8 mm by 1.5 mm, with supernumerary eyes common in older cultured individuals.

**Comparative remarks.** *P. bulbosa* is indistinguishable in life from *Phagocata velata*, *P. vernalis*, *P. crenophila*, *Planaria dactyligera*, *Hymanella retenuova* and possibly *P. monopharyngea*. Its anatomy is most similar to several exotic species, including Japanese forms (*P. kawakatsui*, *P. iwamai*, *P. teshirogii*), *P. uenoi* from Manchuria and *P. altaica* from the Altai Mountains.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and collecting sites.**

- *P. bulbosa* was collected on 13 June 1968 at the Warren Laboratories grounds (Warren County, North Carolina), at two side-road localities. The type locality was a seepage area with highly colored water, with *Hymanella retenuova* present. The second locality yielded *P. bulbosa* with *Planaria dactyligera musculosa*.
- *P. oregonensis* was found in Woodbury Spring, a small limnocrene with almost stagnant, clear water in the Eastmoreland District of Portland, Oregon (visited 12 September 1967). Recorded conditions were water temperature 11.2 °C and pH 6.3. It occurred with *Dendrocoelopsis vaginata* and *Polycelis* sp.
- *P. tahoena* and *P. crenophila* were collected from [[Lake Tahoe]] and Utah sites, including deep-water localities. Kawakatsu's material came from 40 Ekman dredge bottom samples at depths of 15 to 1632 feet in Lake Tahoe.

**Collecting method.** Liver baits were placed in perforated jars weighted with a canvas bag of stones and suspended from a float, exposing baits at 40, 69 and 89 m for 24 hours. The perforations exclude crayfish and other scavengers. This method, recommended by Sell (1916), is effective for many chemically responsive species, but not for some such as *Procotyla fluviatilis*, which require searching or debris collection.

**Laboratory conditions.** Cultures were held in spring water at about 14 °C and fed beef liver and [[Tubifex]] worms.

**Parasites and symbionts.**

- Most *P. bulbosa* specimens carried the holotrichous ciliate [[Sieboldiellina Planariarum]] in the intestine and pharyngeal pouch. Some were also infested with the peritrichous epizoite *Urceolaria mitra*.
- Some *P. tahoena* were infested with [[Gregarine Parasites]], encysted in the mesenchyme or free in the gut. The cysts contained paired gametocytes, similar to those described from *Dendrocoelopsis piriformis*.

## Key Findings

**1. Description of *Phagocata bulbosa*.** The species is characterized by its pigmentation, a single pharynx, a large spherical penis bulb containing a seminal vesicle, a conical penis papilla with a narrow ejaculatory duct opening at its tip, a far-caudal opening of the common oviduct, and no sphincter on the bursal duct. Its ventral testes extend from behind the head to the mouth level, and its copulatory bursa is highly lobate.

**2. Reproduction and development (*P. bulbosa*).**

- Cocoons were unstalked, ellipsoidal and attached to the substrate by a jelly-like substance.
- Two cocoons from a mixed culture measured 0.7 × 0.9 mm and 0.8 × 1.2 mm and hatched after 36 and 50 days.
- One cocoon from another culture produced three young after 38 days. These matured, or developed reproductive parts, in about four months.
- No asexual reproduction (fissioning or fragmentation) was observed in over one year of culture.

**3. Taxonomic separation of *P. tahoena* from *P. nivea*.** Kawakatsu treated *P. tahoena* as a subspecies of *P. nivea* Kenk (1953). The author maintains species rank based on:

- plump body form and a rounded anterior end (truncate in *P. nivea*)
- eye position and the presence of body pigment
- the penis lumen opening at the tip of the papilla (ventral in *P. nivea*)

The author also notes the evolutionary oddity of a pigmented deep-lake form closely related to an unpigmented species.

**4. Anatomy of *P. oregonensis*, corrected.** The author's slides show that each sperm duct enters the penis bulb and opens separately into the penis lumen. This corrects Hyman's (1963) interpretation that the ducts unite into a common sperm duct. The penis lumen combines the functions of a seminal vesicle and an ejaculatory duct without histological differentiation. Testes extend from the second intestinal side branch to the posterior end, contrary to Hyman's report.

**5. Accessory eyes.** Supernumerary eyes in *P. oregonensis* are common in old individuals kept in culture for several months. Their frequency rises with age, as previously documented in *Dugesia lugubris* (Balazs, 1962).

**6. Range and distribution.**

- *P. tahoena*: so far known only from Lake Tahoe. Two specimens were trawled at about 40 m off Al Tahoe on 23 September 1967. About half a dozen were collected in Emerald Bay at about 40 m on 12 July 1968. Over 100 specimens were taken at 89 m off Pope Beach on 12 July 1968. The author suggests the species may occur elsewhere in the poorly explored Sierra Nevada lakes.
- *P. oregonensis*: known from the type area, Woodbury Spring, Portland.

**7. Culture behavior of *P. oregonensis*.** Maintained for 13 months at 14 °C on alternating beef liver and live Tubifex, specimens became sexually mature but produced no egg capsules, and no fission occurred.

**8. Methodological note.** Sectioned anatomy was essential to identify the species. Mixed collections and cultures could not be sorted by external features alone, so the author stresses serial sections as the reliable identification method for this group.
