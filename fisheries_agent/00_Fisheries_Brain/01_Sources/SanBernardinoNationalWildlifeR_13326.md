# Master Plan for San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Ictaluridae #Catostomidae #Poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) master plan sets a 10-year framework for the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] (NWR), a 2,308.9-acre refuge in the extreme southeastern corner of Arizona. The refuge was purchased in 1982 mainly to protect the Rio Yaqui fishes, which had lost most of their U.S. habitat through groundwater depletion, erosion, grazing and introduced fish. The overriding goal is to establish self-sustaining populations of six rare Rio Yaqui fishes so that they can be downlisted or delisted within the plan period (Phase I 1985–1990; Phase II 1990–1995).

The plan pairs fish recovery with restoration of the wider desert ecosystem: cienegas, springs, ponds, streams, grasslands and riparian woodland. It treats most of its objectives and strategies as experimental and tentative, pending a study of the refuge aquifer. Public use is limited and compatible with wildlife programs, centered on birding, interpretation, hunting of doves, quail and rabbits, and photography.

## Background & Description

**Setting and history.** The refuge lies in the San Bernardino Valley, part of the upper Rio Yaqui system, 16 miles east of Douglas, Arizona, on the U.S.–Mexico border. Before about 1800 a perennial stream created marshy [[Cienega Habitat]] across the valley. Heavy grazing since the early 1800s, drought and poor range practices removed most native grasses, promoted mesquite bosque, and caused severe erosion that formed [[Black Draw]] and Hay Hollow Draw. The area's history spans Paleo-Indian, Desert Culture, Opata, Chiricahua Apache and ranching periods. The Nature Conservancy bought the land in 1979, and cattle were later removed.

**Acquisition and purpose.** The [[Sonoran Topminnow Recovery Plan]] recommended the refuge to protect the [[Yaqui Topminnow]] and related fishes. Springs and artesian wells offered a stable, low-cost water source able to sustain fish through drought. The refuge was established April 1, 1982, with a stated mandate to preserve one of the last two U.S. sites of the endangered Yaqui topminnow.

**Target fishes.** The refuge was purchased to protect six Rio Yaqui fishes, listed in phylogenetic order:

| Species | Status at purchase |
|---|---|
| [[Yaqui Chub]] (*Gila purpurea*) | Endangered |
| [[Mexican Stoneroller]] (*Campostoma ornatum*) | Candidate |
| [[Beautiful Shiner]] (*Notropis formosus*) | Threatened |
| [[Yaqui Catfish]] (*Ictalurus pricei*) | Threatened |
| [[Yaqui Sucker]] (*Catostomus bernardini*) | Candidate |
| [[Yaqui Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis*) | Endangered |

Two additional common Rio Yaqui fishes, [[Longfin Dace]] (*Agosia chrysogaster*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), are managed as Species of Special Emphasis. At purchase, only two native fish species survived on the refuge.

**Physical setting.** Elevation runs from about 3,720 ft to 3,920 ft. Soils are deep, calcareous, well-drained Karro Association soils on the valley floor, with Bonita-Sontag Association soils on the uplands. The climate is biseasonal, with mean annual precipitation of 12.4 inches, summer maximum temperatures above 95 °F and winter mean minimums near 40 °F. Air quality is generally good, but the refuge is designated Class II under the Clean Air Act and is affected by smelters in Douglas and across the border.

**Vegetation and wildlife.** Chihuahuan desertscrub covers about 1,875 acres, with irrigated pasture (250 acres), mesquite bosque (250 acres) and riparian/aquatic habitat (125 acres) in the fact sheet's summary. The refuge supports mule deer, javelina, coyotes and rodents. At least 124 bird species have been recorded, including waterfowl, shorebirds and rare visitors from Mexico. The [[San Bernardino Snail]] is found on the refuge.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Aquatic features.** The refuge has 17 aquatic features, most of them artesian springs or wells: Border Springs (East and West), Bathouse, Upland, Bunting, North Pond, Cottonwood, Middle, Cienega, Twin Pond, [[House Pond]] and its channel, Tule Pond, Black Draw, Snail Spring, the NE Windmill and Hay Hollow. Artesian flow is thought to derive from an 1887 earthquake-created fault barrier. Total spring outflow averages about 175 gallons per minute. Water chemistry is similar across the artesian wells, which suggests a shared aquifer. The Black Draw waters come from a different source, separated by an impervious layer.

**Threats.**
- **Exotic fishes.** [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) occupy House Pond, Black Draw and Border Springs and can eliminate native topminnows. They were introduced for mosquito control. Illegal bait and game-fish introductions are a constant risk.
- **Erosion and aquifer decline.** Downcutting in Black Draw (14 feet deep at the south end) and Hay Hollow may have lowered the water table and reduced perennial flows.
- **Degraded habitat.** Woody invasion and invasive forbs reduce infiltration and cover.
- **Trespass cattle** from Rancho San Bernardino in Sonora.
- **Geothermal exploration**, which could affect the artesian aquifer. Section 7 consultation is required.
- **Upstream land use.** Recharge depends on landowners up the valley, so FWS plans to influence range management beyond the boundary.

**Public and local context.** Douglas (population about 13,000) is the nearest town. Local residents resented closure to hunting and grazing and the removal of game fish. Visitation is expected to be low, mostly birders and visitors to the adjacent [[Johnson Historical Museum]].

## Key Findings

**1. Fish population objectives (tentative).** Objectives are expressed as numbers of viable populations, with approximate totals:
- Yaqui topminnow: 13 self-sustaining populations. Totals reached about 20,000 in the warm season and 12,000 in winter.
- Yaqui chub: six populations, about 37,000 fish at seasonal peak.
- Beautiful shiner: five populations, about 60,000 fish.
- Yaqui sucker: five populations, about 4,800 fish.
- Yaqui catfish: four populations, about 2,500 fish.
- Mexican stoneroller: three populations, about 1,500 fish.
- Longfin dace and roundtail chub: no numeric objective, managed incidentally.

**2. Management strategy.** Fish management aims to create many isolated, self-sustaining aquatic systems. Each can be drained to remove exotics, and each can be restocked from other systems or from Dexter National Fish Hatchery. Recreating natural sympatry (coexistence) among fishes is a central goal. Developments are phased and experimental: phase I trials at the Border Springs, Bathouse and Black Draw, followed by phase II projects at Upland, Cottonwood/Middle/Cienega, Twin Pond and Tule Pond.

**3. Habitat restoration targets.** Approximate restored acreages are about 25 acres of cienega, about 49 acres of riparian habitat, and about 1,480 acres of grassland. Black Draw is to be stabilized with check dams and gabions, aiming to restore up to 1.5 miles of perennial stream. Hay Hollow is to follow if Black Draw succeeds.

**4. Key uncertainties.** A lack of aquifer and fish data is the greatest hindrance to management. A three-year aquifer study is needed. The plan states that objectives must be considered tentative and strategies experimental until accurate data support firm decisions. Pumping is deferred until the study is complete.

**5. Public use.** The entire refuge is open except two closed aquatic complexes, North Pond/Bunting Spring and Cottonwood/Middle/Cienega Springs. Two interpreted loop trails (about 2 miles total) start from an overlook with a parking area for about 10 cars. Hunting of doves, quail and rabbits is allowed east of Black Draw only. Estimated annual visitation is 3,650 for 1984–1989 and 4,790 for 1990–1995. The expected summer peak is 20 to 25 visits per day.

**6. Costs and staffing.** Staffing needs are a refuge manager, a biological technician, a full-time maintenance worker and a half-time clerk, at a salary total of $77,600. Total annual costs are $150,000. Most habitat work is low-cost, hand-labor and volunteer-based.

**7. Other wildlife objectives.** Aplomado falcon reintroduction is considered possible. Peregrine falcon habitat is to be protected, and waterfowl, shorebird and marshbird habitat is to be improved incidentally to fish work.

**8. Cultural resources.** A 1984–1985 survey by the Arizona State Museum covered 2,000 acres and recorded 33 prehistoric and historic sites plus 99 isolated features. The plan cites 35 known sites and sets a preservation-in-place objective under the National Historic Preservation Act.

**9. Policy basis.** The plan rests on the Endangered Species Act, the National Wildlife Refuge System Administration Act (compatibility test), the Refuge Recreation Act, the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, the Fish and Wildlife Act and the National Historic Preservation Act.
