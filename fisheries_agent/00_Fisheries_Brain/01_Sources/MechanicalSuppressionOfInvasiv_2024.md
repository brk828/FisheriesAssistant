# Mechanical Suppression of Invasive Northern Pike in Box Canyon Reservoir, Washington

#ichthyology #Esocidae #invasive_species_management

## Species Profile & Ecological Context

**Taxonomic & Life-History Summary**: [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*), family [[Esocidae]], is a highly fecund opportunistic piscivore native to portions of Alaska, Montana, Missouri, Nebraska, and the Great Lakes region but absent from Pacific drainages south of the Alaska Range prior to European settlement. The species was illegally introduced to Box Canyon Reservoir, Washington, first documented in 2004, expanding from an estimated 394 individuals (>350 mm TL) in 2006 to 5802 individuals in 2010. Northern Pike exhibit sexual dimorphism; males mature at younger ages and smaller sizes than females. In this population, most females matured at 500–600 mm TL. Growth was rapid, with fish recruiting to 350-mm gill nets by age 2. The species is capable of consuming prey fish exceeding 50% of their own length. Spawning occurs in April at 8–12°C in shallow, vegetated habitats. Sex ratios remained approximately 1:1 throughout the study, indicating suppression targeted both sexes equally across the spawning season.

**Habitat Associations**: Northern Pike in Box Canyon Reservoir concentrated in shallow (<2 m depth), flooded habitats with aquatic macrophytes and seasonally inundated terrestrial vegetation during spawning (March–June). The southern reservoir section (RKM 96–143) contained extensive sloughs, bays, and backwater areas providing optimal spawning and rearing habitat. Five productive slough sites (4.7–71.0 ha surface area) accounted for 61% of total catch. The northern reservoir section (RKM 54–95) was more riverine with less slough habitat and lower Northern Pike abundance. Slough sites warmed up to 5°C above main-stem temperatures in April, facilitating early spawning. Main-stem reservoir sites produced only 96 fish in 154 nets (3% of total catch), confirming sloughs as critical habitat.

## Behavioral & Ecological Findings

**Movement Patterns & Migration**: Northern Pike exhibited predictable spawning migrations to shallow slough habitats beginning in March (ice-out) and peaking in April. Males reportedly enter spawning sites earlier than females. Large, ripe females were occasionally observed arriving late (June) after most spawning had ceased, contrary to some literature suggesting larger females ripen earlier. Spawning ceased by early May. The species demonstrated strong site fidelity to productive slough locations, with repeated annual targeting of the same sites yielding consistently high catches during initial suppression years (2012–2014). Telemetry data (referenced but not detailed) informed initial site selection for suppression efforts.

**Trophic & Community Interactions**: Northern Pike predation caused precipitous declines in the Box Canyon Reservoir fish community between 2004 and 2011. Electrofishing CPUE declined by 75% for [[Largemouth Bass]], 80% for [[Pumpkinseed]], 35% for [[Yellow Perch]] in sloughs, and 85% for native [[Peamouth]] and 52% for [[Northern Pikeminnow]] in river habitats. Bioenergetics modeling indicated Northern Pike consumed substantial numbers of prey fish. Although native salmonids ([[Bull Trout]], [[Westslope Cutthroat Trout]], [[Mountain Whitefish]]) rarely appeared in Northern Pike diets, the invasion threatened ongoing ESA recovery efforts and native fish conservation programs. The pre-invasion fish community (1988–2004) was stable, prey-crowded, and dominated by nonnative [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Pumpkinseed]]. Northern Pike replaced these species as the dominant predator, fundamentally altering community structure.

**Environmental Drivers**: Water temperature was a critical driver of spawning timing and habitat selection. Sloughs warmed to optimal spawning temperatures (8–12°C) in April, earlier than main-stem habitats. Reservoir elevation and flow influenced access to spawning sites and suppression logistics. Water depth <2 m was strongly associated with Northern Pike presence; nets set at maximum depths <2 m captured twice as many fish as nets >4 m depth. Bycatch survival rates exceeded 90% but were inversely related to water temperature increases later in the season (May–June), influencing the decision to discontinue suppression after April 30 following 2013.

## Conservation Status & Population Dynamics

**Population Trajectory**: The Northern Pike population expanded sixfold from 2004 to 2011, reaching a presuppression (2011) CPUE of 13.2 fish/net in the core area. Intensive mechanical suppression (2012–2018) removed 17,464 Northern Pike in 4868 net sets, with 92.9% captured in the first 3 years. Mean SPIN (spring pike index netting) CPUE in the core area declined 82.6% to 2.3 ± 0.8 fish/net by 2012, further declining to 0.1 fish/net (95% CI ±0.0 to ±0.2) by 2017. Using catch-effort maximum likelihood, Hayes (2013) estimated the 2012 presuppression population at 6570 fish (95% CI 6109–7031) >350 mm TL; first-year suppression removed 83–95% of this population. The target abundance (<1.7 fish/net, 87% reduction) was achieved annually from 2013–2018 in the core area. Northern reservoir CPUE declined >99% from presuppression levels, achieving the <0.5 fish/net target by 2014. A significant rebound occurred 2017–2018 when effort was reduced to <267 net sets, indicating this level may be inadequate for long-term control. The 2018 increase (0.6 ± 0.5 fish/net) occurred primarily at one slough (70% of 2018 catch), likely representing high juvenile survival from a few remaining adults due to reduced intraspecific competition and cannibalism.

**Threat & Risk Assessment**: Northern Pike pose substantial ecological risks to native salmonids ([[Bull Trout]], [[Westslope Cutthroat Trout]]), ongoing ESA recovery efforts, and all fisheries in the Columbia River basin. The species threatens significant investments in upstream fish passage at Box Canyon and Albeni Falls dams, tributary passage improvements, habitat restoration, and native salmonid conservation hatchery programs. Left unchecked, Northern Pike could undermine four decades of planned conservation work. The invasion originated via emigration from upstream Montana/Idaho sources or illegal introductions. Continued illegal stocking and natural range expansion remain ongoing threats. The species' high fecundity, rapid growth, opportunistic piscivory, and ability to extirpate prey bases create high invasion risk. Compensatory responses (increased survival/growth of juveniles, earlier maturation, shifts in migration timing) could hamper suppression, but these were not observed during this study. The 2018 rebound demonstrates rapid population recovery potential if suppression effort is insufficient.

## Management Guidance & Policy Takeaways

**Actionable Recommendations**: (1) **Immediate decisive action**: Implement intensive mechanical suppression immediately upon detection, targeting spawning aggregations in shallow (<2 m), vegetated sloughs during March–April peak spawning. (2) **Equipment specifications**: Use 46-m × 1.8-m sinking gill nets with five ascending mesh panels (2.5, 3.2, 3.8, 4.4, 5.1 cm bar measure), maximized buoyancy, set perpendicular to shore, fished overnight (~23 h soak time). All mesh sizes captured wide size ranges; small mesh toward shore increased catch slightly. (3) **Effort requirements**: Initial suppression requires substantial commitment (two 3-person crews, 7 days/week, March–June) for 3 years to disrupt recruitment. At five most productive sites, 6.2–9.6 nets/ha/season removed 52.9 fish/ha/season initially, declining 98% to <1.0 fish/ha/season by 2016–2018. Over 267 net sets annually may be required for long-term control in systems of this size (~3500 ha). (4) **Spatial strategy**: Repeatedly target productive slough locations with high net density. In Box Canyon Reservoir, 51 discrete sloughs accounted for 97% of catch; five sites produced 61% of total catch with 46% of effort. Discontinue targeting consistently unproductive sites. (5) **Temporal strategy**: Focus effort March–April (prespawn/spawn aggregation period); discontinue after April 30 to reduce bycatch mortality and increase efficiency. Spawning ceases early May. (6) **Adaptive management**: Reduce effort by up to 50% annually once CPUE targets are met, but maintain annual monitoring to detect recruitment rebounds. Transition from suppression (2012–2014) to control phase (2015–2018) demonstrated feasibility of reduced effort, but 2017–2018 rebound indicated minimum thresholds exist. (7) **Public engagement**: Conduct lengthy, comprehensive outreach (presentations, media, signage, open houses) before implementation to garner support. Angler exploitation alone (20% estimated in 2010) is insufficient for population control. Harvest derbies had limited success and were discontinued after 3 years due to low catch rates. (8) **Regulatory framework**: Reclassify as prohibited species (Washington upgraded Northern Pike from game fish to level I prohibited species in 2011); encourage angler harvest and reporting. (9) **Rapid response planning**: Develop interagency rapid response plans (e.g., Washington's 2023 plan) containing detailed guidance, coordination strategies, and implementation protocols. (10) **Bycatch considerations**: Expect significant nontarget catch (63,420 fish, 22 species captured 2012–2018); survival rates >90% but decline with temperature. Native salmonids accounted for <0.1% of bycatch in this system but may be higher elsewhere, requiring modified approaches in anadromous zones.

**Biological Reference Points**: Target abundance: <1.7 Northern Pike/net (87% reduction from presuppression baseline of 13.2 fish/net) in core spawning areas; <0.5 fish/net in riverine sections. Minimum control effort: >267 net sets annually in ~3500-ha reservoir to prevent population rebound. Critical habitat: shallow (<2 m) sloughs with vegetation. Spawning temperature: 8–12°C (April). Recruitment to gear: age 2, ~350 mm TL. Female maturity: 500–600 mm TL initially, shifted to smaller sizes after suppression. Proportional size distribution (PSD) varied from 85 (fast growth, stable recruitment) presuppression to 36 (balanced) in 2013, rebounding to 85 during control phase. Relative weight (Wr) generally increased with size category, exceeding national standard (Wr = 100) for quality-length and larger fish; stock-length and smaller fish at or below standard. Sex ratio remained ~1:1 throughout, indicating effective targeting of entire spawning population.
