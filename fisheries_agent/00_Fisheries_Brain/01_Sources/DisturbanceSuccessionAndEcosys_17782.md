# Disturbance, Succession and Ecosystem Processes in Desert Streams: A Sycamore Creek Case Study

#ecology #aquatic-ecology #stream-ecosystems #periphyton #desert-stream #hyporheic-zone

## Executive Summary

This book chapter by [[Nancy B. Grimm]] (Arizona State University), part of the 34th [[British Ecological Society]] Symposium *Aquatic Ecology: Scale, Pattern and Process* (University College Cork, 1992), uses a long-term case study of [[Sycamore Creek]], an intermittent northern [[Sonoran Desert]] stream studied since 1978, to examine how flash floods, drying and nutrient availability structure succession and ecosystem processes. Succession is examined at three spatial scales (particle, subreach, reach) and three temporal scales (interflood periods, years, decades). The central conclusion is that [[Nitrogen Limitation]] constrains succession at every scale, with autogenic (algal-generated) reductions in nitrogen supply increasing through time, while hydrological disturbance dominates at annual scales.

## Background & Description

- **Definitions.** Disturbance is a discrete event causing significant mortality and altering organism-to-resource ratios. Stability comprises [[Resistance and Resilience]]; succession is defined simply as the sequence of changes at a site after disturbance, without requiring community-structure change.
- **Disturbance types.** Disturbances are classed as autogenic (biotic, small-scale, e.g. invertebrate grazing and sloughing of algal mats) or allogenic (abiotic, large-scale, e.g. drying, [[Spates and Flash Floods]], debris torrents, fire ash inputs and catchment logging).
- **Hierarchy of scales.** Following [[Frissell et al. (1986)]], the chapter uses particle (~0.1 m), subreach (1–10 m) and reach (10–100 m) scales, with assemblages as patches within a reach.
- **Study site.** Sycamore Creek has a catchment of 500 km². Modal discharge is 0.01–0.05 m³/s in summer and 0.1–0.5 m³/s in winter. Substrata range from coarse sand to boulders, arranged as riffles and runs with few pools. Surface flow near the Verde River confluence occurs only above >10 m³/s.
- **Biota.** Primary producers include more than 100 diatom species, filamentous cyanobacteria (*Calothrix*), *Cladophora glomerata*, Zygnemataceae, *Anabaena* mats and macrophytes (*Potamogeton*, *Chara*, *Zannichellia*). Macroinvertebrates are dominated by collector-gatherers such as *Fallceon quilleri* and chironomids, with the caddisfly *Helicopsyche mexicana* and snail *Physella virgata* as scrapers.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Flash floods** are among the most severe in North America (Baker 1977). Because intense rainfall runs off as overland flow, hydrographs are flashy in ephemeral channels. Precipitation and floodwaters are nitrogen-rich, making storms an important nitrogen source ([[Grimm 1992]]).
- **Drying** is a protracted disturbance beginning with boundary shrinkage and ending in complete surface-water loss. Subsurface flow in the [[Hyporheic Zone]] may persist, and rewetting initiates post-drying succession ([[Stanley and Fisher 1992]]).
- **Hyporheic exchange.** Upwelling zones (positive vertical hydraulic gradient, VHG) supply nitrogen to surface biota; downwelling zones are nitrogen-depleted by algal uptake. Interstitial water flows up to 0.15 cm/s, and oxygen is rarely depleted.
- **Productivity.** Late-succession Sycamore Creek is highly productive, and floodwaters leave bare gravels rapidly recolonized by diatoms. High algal standing crops develop within one to two months given adequate interflood periods.
- **Other systems.** Comparable hydrological control has been documented in New Zealand foothill rivers ([[Biggs 1988]]), Californian regulated and unregulated rivers ([[Power 1992]]) and gravel-bedded rivers in Montana and New Mexico.

## Key Findings

**1. Particle scale (periphyton on cobbles).** A 3-month enrichment and grazing experiment (Peterson & Grimm 1992), using clay flower-pot saucers with nitrate and malathion agar treatments, found:
- Strong enrichment effects after 12 days but no grazer effect.
- Differences largely disappeared by day 40; only chlorophyll *a* stayed higher in enriched treatments.
- Nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria dominated unenriched substrata (*Calothrix*, 95% of biovolume by day 90). On enriched substrata, nitrogen fixers reached 65% of biovolume by day 90.
- Diversity was higher under nitrogen-replete conditions.
- Malathion excluded only one grazer taxon, the moth larva *Petrophila jaliscalis*.
- Autogenic controls (mat thickness blocking nutrient diffusion) intensify with succession, and flash floods reset allogenic constraints by scouring periphyton.

**2. Subreach scale (upwelling and downwelling zones).**
- Streamwater nitrate correlates positively with VHG, and high late-succession algal standing crops occur at upwelling zones (Valett et al., in press).
- Cladophora dominates upwelling zones; cyanobacteria dominate downwelling zones.
- Thick algal crusts can block infiltration, producing perched surface water over a dry hyporheic zone, though this is probably patchy at large scales.
- Drying weakens or reverses upwelling, cutting off nitrogen supply.

**3. Reach scale (runs and riffles).**
- Fisher et al. (1982) documented an algal succession from bare sand to diatoms, filamentous chlorophytes and a mixed chlorophyte–cyanobacterial mat over 63 days.
- Across 18 successional sequences (1984–1987), five sequence types were identified (Table 4.1). Cyanobacteria-dominated sequences (types D and E) had low nitrogen flux, and types B and D showed precipitous macroinvertebrate declines (hypothesised, untested, to result from detrital food-quality deterioration).
- Preliminary nitrogen-fixation estimates were of the same order as whole-system daily nitrogen retention, making fixation an increasingly important autogenic nitrogen source through successional time.
- Lateral stranding of algal assemblages accounted for 6% of organic matter export over a 63-day interflood period (Fisher et al. 1982).

**4. Annual scale.** Algal biomass is driven more by time since flood, discharge and flood magnitude than by nitrogen availability. Together these variables gave a multiple r² of 0.68 (Fisher & Grimm 1988). Peak flows may be four to five orders of magnitude above base flow.

**5. Decadal scale.**
- Floodwater dissolved inorganic nitrogen reached up to 3–5 mg/l after dry periods of about three years (1978 and late 1980s). It was low in the mid-1980s after wet years (1978–1980).
- Over the decade, floodwater nitrogen concentration was inversely correlated with the number of floods in the prior three years (Grimm & Fisher 1992).
- Desert soils act as nitrogen stores, linking climatic variability to aquatic productivity.

**6. Conclusions.** Nutrient constraints, disturbance and biological interactions jointly control stream structure and processes. Although the case study is specific to arid streams, the mechanisms are considered broadly applicable to lotic systems. Nitrogen governs succession rate, productivity and assemblage structure, but its importance varies by timescale: it is counterbalanced by fixation during interflood periods, minor relative to floods annually, and modulated by climate over decades.
