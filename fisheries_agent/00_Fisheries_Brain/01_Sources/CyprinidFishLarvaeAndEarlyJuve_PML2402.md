# Cyprinid Fish Larvae and Early Juveniles of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Technical Publication No. 47 (Colorado Parks and Wildlife, May 2016) provides a comprehensive guide to the morphology, meristics, morphometrics and pigmentation of the larvae and early juveniles of 15 cyprinid species in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] (UCRB). It was prepared by the [[Larval Fish Laboratory]] at Colorado State University for the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]]. The guide completes a series begun over 35 years earlier and is designed to support the identification of endangered and non-native cyprinids in early life-stage collections. Its core products are detailed species accounts, comparative summary tables, and a [[Computer-Interactive Key]] built on the DELTA/Intkey system, supplemented by a family-level larval key and pictorial guide. Data were drawn from more than 1,700 specimens and 120 three-view drawings.

## Background & Description

The guide covers early life history identification, which matters because larval collections can document spawning sites, larval production, nursery habitat and year-class strength, and can detect species that are hard to sample as adults. Identification is difficult because cypriniform larvae change dramatically in form between hatching and juvenile stages.

The UCRB supports 68 fish species in 14 families, of which only 13 are native. Cyprinids make up 24 species (35%) of the basin fauna, with 5 native cyprinids. Three native cyprinids are federally endangered: [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*). The remaining natives are [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*). The guide treats 10 non-native cyprinids, including [[Red Shiner]], [[Common Carp]], [[Utah Chub]], [[Brassy Minnow]], [[Golden Shiner]], [[Sand Shiner]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Longnose Dace]], [[Redside Shiner]] and [[Creek Chub]]. Nine other non-native cyprinids are described only briefly, as they are rare in the basin.

Developmental stages follow a combined terminology integrating [[Hardy Terminology]], [[Ahlstrom Terminology]] and [[Snyder Terminology]]: protolarva, mesolarva (preflexion, flexion, postflexion subphases) and metalarva, followed by juvenile. Gut form is scored in five phases.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The guide reports that UCRB fish assemblages have been heavily altered by dams, water diversions and non-native species. Recovery of the endangered natives involves flow protection, habitat work, control of non-native fishes, and hatchery rearing and stocking. Recent distributions for all basin fishes are summarized by sub-basin (HUC 1401 to 1408) for the period 2004 to 2014.

Reproductive guilds and early habitats differ among species. Many cyprinids are non-guarding lithophils or phytolithophils, while [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Creek Chub]] are nest-guarding or brood-hiding speleophils. Larvae of most natives occupy near-shore, low-velocity habitats. Colorado Pikeminnow larvae are transported downstream in considerable numbers after emerging from spawning substrate. Hybridization among native Gila, and among other cyprinids, is documented and can complicate identification.

Common Carp are the most widely distributed non-native cyprinid in the Colorado River Basin. Grass carp in the UCRB are assumed to be sterile triploids. Speckled dace include the federally endangered subspecies *R. o. thermalis*, restricted to Kendall Warm Springs in Wyoming.

## Key Findings

- **Scope:** More than 1,700 specimens were analyzed, and 66 new three-view drawings were prepared, for a total of 120 illustrations (8 per species) covering the protolarval, mesolarval, metalarval and early juvenile phases.
- **Species accounts:** Each of the 15 covered species has an account with adult description, reproduction, early life history, illustrated development, and tables of size at developmental events, meristics and morphometrics.
- **Key tools:** The cyprinid [[Computer-Interactive Key]] (Intkey, DELTA format) allows identification by species, sub-basin or custom subset. A family-level key covers all larvae in the basin, and a pictorial guide to families is appended.
- **Identification approach:** The authors recommend identification based on multiple characters. Taxa that cannot be resolved should be marked tentatively with "?", or left at genus or family level.
- **Myomere counts:** Total myomere counts for UCRB cyprinids are reported as a diagnostic character. Siefert's (1969) method is recommended for preanal and postanal counts.
- **Morphometrics:** Measurements are expressed as percent standard length (%SL), because standard length avoids the allometric effect of caudal fin growth. Head length is redefined for larvae as the distance to the origin of the pectoral fin.
- **Pigmentation:** Melanophore characters (49 defined characters with 2 to 8 states) are the most useful pigment features, but they can be affected by preservation, fading and culture conditions.
- **Gut development:** Gut form is classified in five phases, from a straight gut to a full fold. Most cyprinids reach only a short, S-shaped gut (phase 3), whereas Catostomids and some cyprinids develop long, coiled guts.
- **Native species status:** Of the 13 native UCRB fishes, 8 are present in all sub-basins. Roundtail chub is endangered in New Mexico and a species of special concern in other states. Bonytail is functionally extirpated as a wild, self-sustaining population.
- **Gaps:** The larvae of Paiute sculpin remain undescribed, and the taxonomy of several sculpin and dace groups is still being revised. The guide notes that larval descriptions for most non-native species already existed elsewhere, but these were not comparable with this study's methods.
