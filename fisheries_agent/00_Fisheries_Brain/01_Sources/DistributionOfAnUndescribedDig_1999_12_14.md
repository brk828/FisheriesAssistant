## Executive Summary

This 1999 research proposal/report by K.S. McDermott and T.M. Brandt (National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center, San Marcos, TX) investigates the distribution of an **undescribed digenetic trematode** (family Heterophyidae) that uses the invasive snail **[[Melanoides Tuberculata]]** as an intermediate host and infects the gills of several **federally endangered and threatened fishes** in West Texas spring systems. The parasite, first documented in the San Antonio Zoo in 1990, was subsequently found infecting the endangered **[[Fountain Darter]]** (*Etheostoma fonticola*) in the Comal River as early as 1994. The study's two objectives were: (1) to map co-occurrence of *M. tuberculata* and the parasite across West Texas spring/stream sites, and (2) to test for **differential susceptibility** to infection among related fish species/genera (particularly *Gambusia* and *Cyprinodon* spp.) via controlled laboratory infection trials. Field surveys found high infection prevalence and heavy gill cyst loads in several sites (notably San Felipe Creek and Balmorhea Cienega), with substantial variation in cyst burden between fish species and even within congeneric species.

## Background & Description

- **Parasite identity:** An unnamed digenetic trematode (Digenea: Heterophyidae), morphologically and ecologically similar to the Asian fluke **Centrocestus formosanus**, but distinguishable from it. Identification via North American keys failed, suggesting either an introduced species or a previously uncollected native fluke.
- **Discovery history:**
  - First found in 1990 in *Melanoides tuberculata* in a tributary of the San Antonio River (San Antonio Zoo) by Knott and Murray (1991).
  - Possibly first reported in the U.S. in Florida (Blazer and Gratzek 1985).
  - Documented in the **[[Comal River]]** fountain darter population by 1994, and in San Marcos River fountain darters as a comparison population.
- **Life cycle** (Kothari and Murray 1992):
  1. Adult fluke resides in the intestine of a piscivorous bird or mammal (definitive host).
  2. Eggs pass out in host feces into water.
  3. Eggs are ingested by the exotic snail *M. tuberculata*; miracidia hatch inside the snail.
  4. Redia and cercariae develop within the snail's digestive gland.
  5. Cercariae emerge (positively phototactic) and infect fish gills, forming metacercarial cysts.
  6. A definitive host (bird/mammal) consumes infected fish, and the cycle continues.
- **Known/possible definitive hosts:** Yellow-crowned Night-Heron (*Nyctanassa violacea*), four other bird species, and the federally endangered **Whooping Crane** (*Grus americana*) (Santos and Murray 1997); experimentally infected chickens and laboratory rats.
- **Known/possible intermediate (fish) hosts:** Cyprinidae, Characidae, Cichlidae, Poeciliidae, and multiple West Texas endangered species (see Key Findings).
- **Snail hosts:** *Melanoides tuberculata* (exotic thiarid snail, established in south Texas since 1964) is the primary host; the related exotic snail *Thiara (Tarebia) granifera* was not found infected in preliminary sampling and does not host *C. formosanus* (Martin 1958).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat description:** The parasite and snail co-occur in spring-fed systems associated with the **Edwards Aquifer** (Comal and San Marcos springs, ~22–23.3°C) and analogous West Texas artesian spring systems with warm, stable temperatures and often low-flow, ponded conditions — conditions favorable for trematode transmission (Ginetsinskaya 1968: higher infection in small water bodies, low current, warm water).
- **Three study regions:**
  1. **Del Rio area (Val Verde Co.)** – Devils River minnow, Rio Grande darter, proserpine shiner, Conchos pupfish.
  2. **Balmorhea/Fort Stockton area (Reeves & Pecos Co.)** – Comanche Springs pupfish, Pecos gambusia, Leon Springs pupfish, Pecos pupfish, Big Bend gambusia.
  3. **Menard County** – Clear Creek gambusia (Clear Creek).
- **Threats to endangered fishes:** habitat loss from downstream dam construction/flooding, reduced spring flow from groundwater over-pumping, and introduced species impacts (multiple USFWS recovery plans cited: 1980a, 1980b, 1982, 1984, 1985).
- **Vector concerns:** Yellow-crowned Night-Herons feed/nest along ponds and rocky-shored streams; possible introduction pathways for the parasite include birds carrying it from Central America or Florida populations into West Texas spring systems.
- **Snail infection dynamics:** Comal River *M. tuberculata* population heavily infected (1008/3600 snails infected) vs. San Marcos River population very lightly infected (1/3600), suggesting localized parasite establishment tied to definitive host presence (e.g., heron nesting/roosting trees present over Comal/San Antonio Rivers but not upper San Marcos River).
- **Minimum infective snail size:** 17 mm total length (smallest *M. tuberculata* found infected in Comal River).

### Additional Notable Findings
- **Differential susceptibility observed:** Preliminary data suggest congeneric fish species (e.g., within *Gambusia* and *Cyprinodon*) differ substantially in both total cyst burden and proportion of developing (mature-track) cysts, motivating the planned controlled laboratory infection experiment.
- **Host suitability:** The fountain darter appears to be a poor host — most metacercariae in its gills are dead/undeveloped (Mitchell et al. 1998) — yet heavy cyst loads may still impair survival via gill damage/respiratory stress, as documented for the analogous parasite *[[Centrocestus Formosanus]]* (Lo and Lee 1996a).
- **Diagnostic criteria used:** Presence of eyespots only = recent infection/no development; visible "X"-shaped excretory vesicle with eyespots absent = successful development toward maturity.
- **Experimental design (lab infection, planned):** Six fish species/hybrids (*Cyprinodon elegans*, *C. bovinus*, *C. variegatus × C. pecosensis* hybrids, *Gambusia gaigei*, *G. nobilis*, *G. geiseri*) exposed to standardized cercarial doses (~5,000 cercariae/tank) from Comal River *M. tuberculata*, with 20 fish/species in five replicate groups of four, housed 3 weeks post-exposure before sacrifice and gill examination.
