# Genetic Monitoring of Wild and Repatriated Razorback Sucker in Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study monitors the [[Genetic Diversity]] of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada, which holds the largest remaining population of the species. The population has collapsed from more than 60,000 to fewer than 1,000 fish over the past decade, mainly because of recruitment failure caused by predation on larvae. A [[Repatriation Program]] captures wild larvae, rears them in protected custody until they can escape predation, and releases them back into the lake. The authors used [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (mtDNA) [[Cytochrome B]] variation, screened by [[Single-Stranded Conformational Polymorphism]] (SSCP) and sequencing, to test whether the program transmits the genetic variation of wild adults into larvae and then into repatriated fish. Their central finding is that adults, larvae and repatriates do not differ meaningfully, so the program appears to be preserving genetic diversity. The authors caution that the reproductive success of repatriates is still untested.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large catostomid fish endemic to the larger streams of the [[Colorado River]] system. Its habitat has been heavily modified and controlled. Small, nonsustaining populations exist in [[Lake Mead]] and the [[Green River]], and Lake Mohave is the largest remaining population. Natural recruitment into the Lake Mohave population had not been documented since the 1950s. The species was listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], and the authors describe it as at immediate risk of functional extinction.

Genetic diversity in the razorback sucker is unusually high for a vertebrate, which suggests that the current populations are remnants of a very large historical population. The authors note that reduced genetic variation and viability have already been documented for the species, which makes genetic concerns especially pressing for recovery. The [[Native Fish Work Group]] (NFWG), formed in 1997 from a multi-agency cooperative, initiated the repatriation program in 1991.

Under the program, wild larvae are captured annually, reared in a hatchery to juvenile size, moved to protected seminatural grow-out sites, and released back into the lake once they reach subadult size. Through 2003, nearly 500,000 larvae had been harvested and more than 80,000 young fish stocked. Captive survival improved to typically above 90% as methods were refined. Repatriated fish, including sexually mature individuals, are regularly encountered during monitoring, which suggests demographic progress.

The study is framed against [[Captive Breeding]], the conventional approach to enhancing endangered fishes. The authors argue that captive breeding can reduce genetic variation and that a repatriation approach that uses natural reproduction may maximize representation of wild individuals without the cost of maintaining large broodstocks.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Recruitment bottleneck:** Predation on early life stages is the main barrier to recruitment. The repatriation program circumvents it by holding larvae until they are large enough to survive.
- **Genetic risk:** High fecundity makes large numbers of repatriates possible through hatchery methods. However, if these fish descend from a small number of parents, the population could enter a downward spiral of reduced variation and [[Inbreeding Depression]].
- **Spatial and temporal structure:** Larvae were sampled across four areas, Nine Mile, Tequila, Yuma and Wrong Cove, over the January to April spawning period. The authors interpret the observed patterns in light of demographic studies showing that fish move among spawning areas.
- **Marker choice:** Because mtDNA is maternally inherited without recombination, the study reflects a female-biased perspective. The authors cite radiotelemetry work showing no significant difference in movement between males and females, which supports using mtDNA as an approximation of neutral marker movement.

## Key Findings

**Sampling and methods**

- Wild larvae were collected by dip net at night, from 1997 to 2003. The target was 25 larvae per area, four collections per year, for a target of 400 larvae per year.
- Repatriates were sampled from six spawning seasons (1997–1998 and 2000–2003) and identified by a unique passive integrated transponder tag. Wild adults were captured by trammel net. Historical wild-caught samples from the 1980s and early 1990s were combined with the 1997–2003 material.
- Sequencing of a 311 bp fragment of cytochrome b across the samples identified **28 haplotypes** among **296 individuals**. Sequence variation included 23 variable positions and eight amino acid substitutions. The authors describe this as atypical for cytb, with an unusually high number of first-position changes.
- Phylogenetic analysis recovered 27 trees of 29 steps. There was some homoplasy (CI = 0.68, RI = 0.82), but most haplotypes differed by single mutations.

**Wild adults**

- Analysis of 272 wild adults from three collections (late 1980s, early 1990s and late 1990s) identified 19 haplotypes.
- Gene diversity values were 0.69 (1980s), 0.59 (early 1990s) and 0.66 (late 1990s). The differences were not statistically significant.
- Variation among the three adult collections was not significant (F_ST = −0.003, P = 0.55), so the years were pooled as a baseline.

**Larvae**

- Analysis of 2,432 larvae in 99 temporal collections found significant differences among temporal collections within years. The authors attribute these to sampling error, because only a finite number of females spawn at a given time and place.
- Among-year F_ST values ranged from 0.02 to 0.10 and were significant. Differences among areas were not significant, so there is no evidence that groups of females consistently use particular areas.
- Of 93 larval collections, 25 showed reduced levels of diversity or haplotype number relative to the adult baseline. After Bonferroni correction (P < 0.0005), only a few comparisons remained significant. The Results section reports eight of 186 comparisons, and the Discussion reports four. The authors argue that the Bonferroni correction is conservative, so the uncorrected significant deviations may still be biologically relevant.
- The 1998 example from Tequila Cove shows that these deviations are temporally random. Diversity was 0.00 in one collection and 0.72 in the next.

**Repatriates**

- Samples of 255 repatriates from 11 cohorts (1992–2002) contained 14 haplotypes. Small cohorts were pooled into 1992–1994 and 2000–2002 groups.
- Repatriate cohorts did not differ from one another (F_ST = 0.008, P = 0.134). The authors note the value approaches significance and that some sample sizes are small.
- Repatriate diversity fell within the range expected from random draws of the adult population, so repatriates are considered representative of the adult population.

**Transmission from adults to repatriates**

- An AMOVA of adults, larvae and repatriates found significant differences among samples within the three groups (F_SC = 0.006, P < 0.001). The authors describe this level as biologically negligible.
- No significant difference was detected among the three life-history stages (F_ST = −0.001, P = 0.761). The authors conclude that the stratified sampling design transmits genetic variation from adults through larvae into repatriates.

**Conclusions and management implications**

- The repatriation program appears to be effective in preserving the genetic variation of wild adults, at least to this point.
- Repatriates' reproductive capacity has not yet been assessed. The authors recommend periodic monitoring of genetic variation in larval samples, because reduced variation in repatriates would also show up in larvae.
- If variation declines, the authors suggest that intervention may be needed to ensure that certain individuals are not over-represented in the repatriate population.
