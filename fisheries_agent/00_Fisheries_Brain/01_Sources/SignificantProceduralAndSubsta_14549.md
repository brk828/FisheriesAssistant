# Lower Colorado River Endangered Species Conservation Program: Stakeholder Critique of Procedural and Substantive Defects

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-species-policy

## Executive Summary

This document is a letter dated October 16, 1995, from [[American Rivers]] (Pamela Hyde, Director, Southwest Programs) to John G. Rogers, Deputy Director of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]]. It raises formal concerns about the early development of the [[Lower Colorado River Endangered Species Conservation Program]], a [[Habitat Conservation Plan]] (HCP) being prepared by the [[Lower Division States Tribes]] and the Department of the Interior. American Rivers supports a proactive approach to endangered species but argues that the process has serious procedural and substantive flaws. The letter asks the Service to reassert its role as an accountable regulator. It does not present new biological data on any species.

## Background & Description

The letter responds to the early stages of a Lower Colorado River HCP. American Rivers states that significant decisions were already being made by the [[Lower Division States Tribes Endangered Species Steering Committee]] and its Working Group without stakeholder or public input. It recommends that a Lower Colorado River HCP Steering Committee, including conservation organizations, be formed and that a public outreach and education program start immediately.

The program's governing document is a [[Memorandum of Agreement]] (MOA). The letter quotes its stated goal: to "accommodate current water diversions and power production and optimize opportunities for future water and power development, while working toward the conservation of habitat and toward the recovery of included species, and reducing the likelihood of additional species listings." A draft [[Sufficient Progress Agreement]] (SPA) accompanies the MOA.

The letter is copied to state and federal officials, including representatives of the Arizona Department of Water Resources, Arizona Game and Fish Commission, Colorado River Board of California, California Department of Fish and Game, Colorado River Commission of Nevada, Nevada Division of Wildlife, [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the Solicitor of the Department of the Interior, and the [[Colorado River Indian Tribes]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The letter addresses the "lower Colorado River ecosystem" and "endangered species in and along the lower river," but it does not name any specific species, populations, or habitat measurements. Its environmental concern centers on the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]'s [[Biological Assessment]] of current dam operations. That assessment is an informal first step in [[Section 7 Consultation]] on the effects of dam operations on listed species and [[Critical Habitat]]. American Rivers argues that dam operations may need to be modified to protect listed species, and that the HCP process as drafted would prevent such changes.

The regulatory framework discussed includes:
- [[Endangered Species Act]], Section 7 (federal agency actions) and Section 10 (incidental take permits for non-federal actors)
- [[Reasonable and Prudent Measures]] (RPAs) and conservation recommendations in biological opinions
- [[Interim Conservation Measures]] (ICMs), intended as good-faith assurances during program development

## Key Findings

The letter identifies five main problems and several corrective actions.

1. **Primary goal favors the status quo.** The MOA places water diversions and power production on equal or higher footing than species conservation. American Rivers argues this conflicts with the Service's duty under the Endangered Species Act to recover listed species. It also questions whether closing off alternatives for minimizing take is consistent with the Section 10 requirement to minimize and mitigate impacts "to the maximum extent practicable." Proposed remedy: a two-pronged goal giving conservation and recovery equal standing with accommodation of current water and power uses, each pursued to the maximum extent practicable.

2. **Three years of regulatory assurances for program development.** The MOA states that the agreement will serve as the RPAs and conservation recommendations for biological opinions on included species during the three-year HCP development period, as long as sufficient progress is made. The draft SPA says that implementation or success of ICMs, and funding levels, are not the basis for judging progress. American Rivers concludes that assurances are granted in exchange for "paper tasks" with no guarantee of success, which it considers an unreasonable exchange.

3. **"Wrapping in" Section 7 consultations.** The MOA would make the agreement the RPAs for all Section 7 consultations. American Rivers argues this improperly merges Section 10 (private and non-federal parties) with Section 7 (federal agencies). It warns that this would preclude all regulatory actions to protect endangered species and raises legal and procedural concerns. The Bureau of Reclamation's Biological Assessment is already being folded into the HCP. The letter argues that modifying dam operations is unlikely to appear among the RPAs, is unlikely in the three-year window, and may never occur. If current dam operations jeopardize species, the Service should tailor RPAs to the Bureau's actions.

4. **Self-defined "sufficient progress."** The MOA's enforcement mechanism depends on sufficient progress, but the signatories themselves set annual mileposts, judge whether progress is adequate, and can adjust mileposts that are missed. American Rivers compares this to a student writing, grading, and revising his own test. It argues the Service must keep unilateral authority to define and evaluate mileposts. Otherwise, it has effectively granted regulatory assurances without conditions, which the letter says would likely abrogate the Service's duties under the Endangered Species Act and invite legal challenge.

5. **Ambiguous role of the Fish and Wildlife Service.** American Rivers says the Service has not clearly defined its role, has accepted an MOA focused on water and power, has deferred to other signatories on funding, timetable, and scope, and has delayed public involvement. It argues these actions depart from the Act's requirements. It calls on the Service to act as "an accountable regulator and director" even if this is unpopular with other participants.

**Overall conclusion:** American Rivers supports a proactive, cooperative HCP and offers assistance. It requests that the Service correct these defects promptly, open the process to stakeholders and the public, and restore the Service's independent regulatory authority. The letter warns that failure to do so would likely lead to an ineffective conservation program and a loss of stakeholder support.
