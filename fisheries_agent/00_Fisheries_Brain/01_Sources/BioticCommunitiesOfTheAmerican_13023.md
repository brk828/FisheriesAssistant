# Biotic Communities of the American Southwest: United States and Mexico

#ichthyology #marine-biology #biogeography

## Executive Summary

This is the front matter, introduction and chapter sequence of *Biotic Communities of the American Southwest – United States and Mexico*, edited by [[David E. Brown]] and published as a companion to the 1:1,000,000 color map *Biotic Communities of the Southwest* ([[Brown and Lowe Map]], 1978/1980). It describes the Southwest, a region of roughly equal parts United States and Mexico, as a meeting place of North American biomes, and organizes its natural communities into a hierarchical, ecosystem-based classification with 27 biotic communities across six parts: tundras, forests and woodlands, scrublands, grasslands, desertlands and wetlands. The work was initiated in 1974 by the USDA Forest Service Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station to tie wildlife to recognized biomes for management, and the text is explanatory rather than exhaustive. Aquatic and wetland content is included as Part 6, which the foreword describes as the first holistic treatment of these neglected Southwestern wetlands.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The publication was produced for land managers and naturalists who needed a physical and ecological description of the mapped communities. It accompanies the map, with the table of contents matching the map legend. Foreword author [[David E. Brown]] (January 1982) recounts that the region's biomes, from [[Chaparral]] to [[Mohave Desertscrub]], lie close together, so that several biomes can be sampled in one day's fieldwork.

**Management framework.** Patton (Management Applications chapter) argues that the 1970s environmental laws, including the [[National Environmental Policy Act]], [[National Forest Management Act]] and [[Endangered Species Act]], required systematic, hierarchical resource inventories. Ecological units (e.g., Montane Conifer Forest, Sonoran Desert) are favored over purely geographic or descriptive labels because they share evolutionary history.

**Classification system.** The system follows the [[Life-Zone Concept]] of [[C. Hart Merriam]] (1890, 1898) and is open-ended, with digit levels from formation down to biome. Biomes are vegetation-based communities determined mainly by climate (minimum seasonal temperature and precipitation); biotic provinces are faunal and floral units. Boundaries are often tenuous and shaped by elevation, slope exposure and inversions.

**Study area.** The Southwest spans about 26°N to 38°N. Parts or all of Arizona, Baja California Norte and Sur, Chihuahua, Coahuila, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Sonora, Texas and Utah are included. Eleven biotic provinces occur within it.

**Historical background.** Chapter authors [[David E. Brown]], [[W.L. Minckley]] and [[James P. Collins]] divide Southwestern ecological study into three overlapping periods: Exploration (1807–1900, trappers, military surveys and physician-naturalists), Inventory (1850–1940, hunter-naturalists and collectors) and Synthesis (1890 to present, professional ecologists). Key figures include [[Zebulon Pike]], [[John C. Fremont]], [[William Emory]], [[Elliot Coues]], [[Edgar Mearns]], [[Forrest Shreve]] and [[Carl L. Hubbs]]. Historical fish work is noted, for example the description of the [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] in the 1850s, and [[Hubbs]] and [[Robert Rush Miller]] assembling one of the largest freshwater fish collections in the world at the University of Michigan.

**Chapter inventory (summary).** The volume contains about 27 biome chapters grouped as follows:
- **Tundras:** [[Alpine Tundra]] (one chapter).
- **Forests and woodlands:** boreal and subalpine conifer forests, cold-temperate montane forests, [[Great Basin Conifer Woodland]], [[Madrean Evergreen Woodland]], [[Californian Evergreen Forest]], relict conifer forests and [[Sinaloan Deciduous Forest]].
- **Scrublands:** [[Subalpine Scrub]], [[Great Basin Montane Scrubland]], [[Californian Coastalscrub]], [[Californian Chaparral]], [[Interior Chaparral]] and [[Sinaloan Thornscrub]].
- **Grasslands:** [[Alpine and Subalpine Grassland]], [[Montane Meadow Grassland]], [[Plains and Great Basin Grasslands]], [[Semidesert Grassland]], [[Californian Valley Grassland]] and [[Sonoran Savanna Grassland]].
- **Desertlands:** [[Great Basin Desertscrub]], [[Mohave Desertscrub]], [[Chihuahuan Desertscrub]] and [[Sonoran Desertscrub]].
- **Wetlands:** a single holistic Part 6 chapter by Minckley and Brown.

Appendices give a digitized classification system for North American biomes and a list of scientific and common names of the plants and animals used as examples, arranged by biome. The list of references (Literature) is extensive and is not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate and evolutionary history.** Southwestern landscapes are presented as the product of three Tertiary geofloras: the Neotropical-Tertiary (mesophytic broadleaf evergreen) to the south, the Arcto-Tertiary (temperate conifer and deciduous) to the north, and the Madro-Tertiary (sclerophyll and microphyll, dry-adapted) that spread over the region as aridity increased during the second half of the Tertiary. Modern desertscrubs, chaparral and evergreen woodlands largely derive from the Madro-Tertiary flora, while relict conifer and riparian forests are remnants of the Arcto-Tertiary flora.

**Quaternary changes.** Alpine tundra and subalpine forests were most extensive in the recent Pleistocene. Glaciers captured high mountains, and wetter, cooler conditions allowed range shifts in elevation and latitude. Post-Wisconsinan migrations established present subalpine and montane forests within about the last 10,000 years. Warm-season monsoon rainfall, reduced in the middle to late Holocene, produced the modern desert assemblages.

**Vegetation and fauna patterns.**
- Desertland and grassland are the two base formations, together covering more than 70% of the Southwest.
- Mountain ranges create vertical zonation, so communities of several biomes can be seen on one slope.
- Riparian deciduous forests (cottonwood, sycamore, willow) are water-controlled relicts that persist throughout the region.
- Overgrazing and fire suppression have produced widespread shrub invasion (mesquite, juniper, snakeweed) into grasslands, and introduced annual grasses now dominate much of Californian Valley grassland.

**Conservation concerns noted in the text.** Several vertebrates and communities are described as declining or lost, including the Masked Bobwhite (faced with extinction), the Mexican Grizzly, the Merriam Elk and the Gray Wolf. Sonoran savanna grassland and Californian Valley grassland have been largely converted, and tall-grass prairie remnants are considered endangered habitat.

**Wetlands.** The text states that Southwestern wetlands were long neglected. Most are small and were omitted from the map except where important. Aquatic environments have their own evolutionary history and endemic fish faunas, which the foreword identifies as a key reason for treating them as natural rather than cultural features.

## Key Findings

- **Biome framework established.** The Southwest was mapped into 27 biotic communities, classified hierarchically and tied to climate and vegetation, providing a common basis for wildlife habitat assessment.
- **Evolutionary origin.** Modern Southwestern communities largely assembled from Tertiary geoflora migrations and Pleistocene–Holocene climate shifts, so their composition is historically contingent.
- **Alpine tundra is small and species-poor.** In New Mexico, about 142 vascular plant taxa were reported from the alpine zone, and Arizona's San Francisco Peaks support roughly 82 to 88 species. The Wheeler Peak and Blanca Peak comparison gave a Sorensen similarity of 0.70 (63 species shared).
- **Forest biomes are climatically defined.** Rocky Mountain subalpine forests occur above about 2,450–2,900 m, with Engelmann Spruce and Corkbark Fir. Montane forests are dominated by Ponderosa Pine, with Douglas-fir and White Fir mixed conifer at higher, cooler elevations. Madrean oak-pine and encinal woodlands extend into Mexico.
- **Great Basin conifer woodland** is one of the most extensive vegetation types in the Southwest, centred on juniper and pinyon at about 1,500–2,300 m with 250–500 mm precipitation.
- **Chaparral and scrub.** Californian chaparral holds nearly 900 vascular plant species. Interior chaparral covers about 1.4 million ha in Arizona. Sinaloan thornscrub is reclassified to include parts of Shreve's Sonoran Desert subdivisions, with 1,600–2,000 perennial plants per ha in typical stands.
- **Sinaloan deciduous forest** enters the Southwest only in southeastern Sonora and extreme southwestern Chihuahua and is described as rich in tropical elements, with a canopy 10–15 m high.
- **Grassland losses.** Semidesert grassland has been widely replaced by mesquite, juniper, snakeweed and creosotebush, and more than 400 alien species now account for much of Californian Valley grassland cover.
- **Desert fauna and flora.** Mohave Desert ephemerals number about 250 taxa, of which perhaps 80–90 are endemic. Great Basin Desert occupies roughly 59,570 km², and its shadscale and sagebrush series reflect cold-temperate affinities.
- **Wetlands are a priority gap.** The authors argue that Southwestern wetlands were under-studied until recently, and that Part 6 is the first holistic treatment of them.
- **Management implication.** Managers are encouraged to plan at the ecological-unit scale, using biotic community data stored in local and regional wildlife databases for environmental impact statements and habitat projects.
