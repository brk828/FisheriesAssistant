# Drainage Evolution and Fish Dispersal in the Central Appalachians

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This monograph reconstructs how freshwater fishes dispersed across the central Appalachians by linking geological evidence of drainage rearrangement with present-day fish distributions. The author argues that [[Stream Piracy]] and divide migration, combined with Pleistocene glacial ponding in the [[Teays River]] system, created the main routes by which [[Mississippi Basin]] fishes reached the [[Atlantic Slope]]. A central claim is that the [[Gauley River]] and [[Greenbrier River]] formed an alternative dispersal corridor that bypassed the [[Kanawha Falls]] barrier. The paper is a hypothesis-driven synthesis supported by a 1976 ichthyofaunal survey and a review of prior geological and zoogeographic literature.

## Background & Description

Fish zoogeographers often must rely on geomorphological literature to infer past drainage connections, and much of that literature is dated and contested. The author stresses that zoogeographic conclusions are most reliable when based on combined geological and biological evidence.

Stream capture (piracy) can transfer biota between drainages. Geological indicators include elbows of capture, barbed tributaries, abandoned channels, wind gaps, and large valleys occupied by small streams. The pirate stream is generally expected to receive more species from the captor than it delivers, unless the captured fauna is depauperate.

The Mississippi Basin is described as the primary center of origin and dispersal of freshwater fishes east of the Rocky Mountains. The Pliocene-Pleistocene Teays River, precursor to the present New-Kanawha system, is considered the main eastward dispersal route to the Atlantic Slope and northward route to the upper Ohio system.

The fish-family context is summarized from the paper's own totals. Cyprinidae (230 native species), Catostomidae (61), Ictaluridae (40) and Percidae (148) together account for 479 of 728 North American primary freshwater species (about 65%). These families are intolerant of salt water and are therefore unlikely to have dispersed by coastal routes. The darter tribe Etheostomatini is described as endemic to North America and probably no older than Pliocene.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Drainage divide migration.** The Mississippi-Atlantic divide has migrated westward since the Tertiary. North of Roanoke, Virginia, the divide between the New River and the Roanoke and James Rivers has shifted 64 to 120 km. Between the Monongahela and Potomac Rivers the shift is 130 to 160 km. The author reports that the divide migration rate is debated: Thompson (1939) estimated about 1.12 km per million years, whereas the figures in this paper imply about 56.3 km per million years. [[Hack]]-era geomorphologists are cited as favoring a much older, Mesozoic or Cretaceous, divide position, which the biological evidence is said to contradict.

**Teays River system.** The Pliocene Teays was the major drainage from the east-central United States to the ancestral Mississippi. Its route west of West Virginia is disputed, with proposed paths through the [[Mahomet Valley]] in Illinois or the Wabash Valley. The Teays later shifted from a westward course to Huntington, West Virginia, toward Point Pleasant.

**Gorge and falls barriers.** [[Kanawha Falls]] (7.3 m high) lies less than 2.0 km below the New-Gauley confluence at Gauley Bridge and has long been considered a barrier. Upstream falls include Wylie, Bull and Sandstone, and the [[New River]] gorge is incised as much as 457 m over about 90 km. Rapids and low falls in the gorge may have hindered movement in both directions.

**Pleistocene ponding.** Glacial ice impounded the Teays Valley to form [[Lake Tight]]. Estimates of its extent and duration vary. Neff and others (1970) suggested it formed during the Kansan stage and lasted 25 to 30 thousand years. Janssen and McCoy (1953) estimated about 6,500 years of Pleistocene ponding. Maximum ponding is reported near 262 m and possibly above 305 m, covering 15,540 to 18,130 km². Kanawha Falls today sits at about 198 m elevation.

**Karst and captures.** The [[Greenbrier Valley]] is underlain by soluble limestone, up to 90 m thick in the north and 366 m in the south. About 900 caves occur in the karst near Lewisburg, West Virginia. The author argues that rapid cave formation after the Greenbrier's capture of Gauley headwaters facilitated subterranean transfer of groundwater and continued encroachment on neighboring drainages.

**Montane stresses.** The New River system today is depauperate. Factors cited include bedrock-boulder substrate, high discharge, steep gradient, scouring, few plants, prevalent falls and rapids, poorly developed floodplains, and scarce soft-bottom habitat. High sulfate concentrations and environmental degradation are also mentioned.

## Key Findings

1. **Gauley River as a dispersal corridor.** A 1976 survey (Hocutt and others, 1979) recorded six species not previously reported above Kanawha Falls: *Lampetra aepyptera*, *Moxostoma erythrurum*, *Ictalurus natalis*, *Ictalurus nebulosus*, *Noturus flavus* and *Percina caprodes*. The author argues these species reached the upper Kanawha by the Gauley route, which was a more favorable path than the evolving Teays gorge.

2. **Three working hypotheses.** (1) The Gauley was a viable alternative for fishes that negotiated the Kanawha Falls area. (2) The Pliocene Appalachian Plateau extended farther east than today and was drained mainly by the Gauley. (3) The Greenbrier, a minor Pliocene tributary, captured its present drainage from the Gauley in the Pleistocene, displacing the Plateau's eastern edge about 35 km west.

3. **Evidence for the eastern Plateau.** Geological indicators include Droop Mountain (above 914 m, capped with sandstone), the Allegheny Front as a retreating escarpment, hanging valleys on Gauley tributaries, and Hills Creek, which heads on the Plateau and flows east into the Greenbrier.

4. **Lake Tight's faunal filtering role.** The author concludes that Lake Tight, together with the gorge and falls, acted as a faunal filter. None of these barriers was fully effective alone, but collectively they helped shape the unique New River fauna. Glacial ponds may have been warmer than previously assumed, allowing dispersal of temperate forms.

5. **Origin of *Percina oxyrhyncha*.** The sharpnose darter is interpreted as Teays-derived. Its northern range was obliterated by Pleistocene glaciation, and southern Teays tributaries served as refugia. Its sibling, *Percina phoxocephala*, is associated with streams north and west of the Ohio River.

6. **Routes to the Atlantic Slope.** Teays fishes reached the Atlantic Slope through captures with the Gauley-Greenbrier, Fincastle-Old Roanoke, and Monongahela-Potomac systems. The James River received part of its fauna directly from the Teays or indirectly through the Old Roanoke River. Multiple invasions are proposed, and an early Pliocene wave is distinguished from a later wave of Mississippi-affinity Atlantic endemics.

7. **Recent captures.** The author stresses that captures recognizable today are relatively recent events. Thompson's (1939) concept of recent capture is cited, and the faunal sequence across the Appalachian divide is considered too complex to reconstruct precisely.

8. **Caution on specific records.** The distribution of *Moxostoma rhothoecum* in the Greenbrier may reflect remnants of a Pleistocene Teays population or reintroduction via a Greenbrier-James connection. Roughly 75 collections (1971-1978) in the upper Greenbrier failed to yield *Etheostoma nigrum* or *M. rhothoecum*, so both may now be extirpated there.

9. **Overall conclusion.** The author concludes that the Mississippi-Atlantic divide has been a dynamic feature shifting north and west. Faunal similarity between drainages implies divide migration and faunal exchange in relatively recent Pliocene-Pleistocene times. The author describes the reconstructed Gauley-Greenbrier sequence as hypothesis-level and says more study is required before firm conclusions can be drawn.
