# Autumn Spawning and Reproductive Notes on Loach Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This note reports the first documented autumn spawning of the threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), a small, darter-like cyprinid endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]]. Observations were made in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, in September 1988 and March 1979. The authors found that loach minnows nest in cavities beneath rocks on riffles, that males guard eggs and defend the nest, and that a female may also guard the nest. These findings place the species among the rare North American minnows that use hole-nesting with male parental care, and they suggest that its spawning season is more flexible than previously assumed.

## Background & Description

The loach minnow is a small (<70 mm SL) cyprinid listed as threatened by the [[United States Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1985) and as endangered by Arizona and New Mexico. Habitat alteration and the establishment of non-native fishes are cited as the main causes of its decline. Because management depends on biological data, the authors note that information on habitat use, diet, age, growth and reproduction is important (see [[Minckley]] 1965, 1973; [[Britt]] 1982; [[Rinne]] 1985, 1989).

Earlier work considered the species a spring spawner. The authors report autumn spawning, which had not been recorded before, and add further notes on its reproductive habits.

### Study Setting

- **Aravaipa Creek**, a tributary of the [[San Pedro River]] in Arizona, in the [[Sonoran Desert]].
- **25 September 1988:** A short reach was temporarily diverted into an irrigation canal, drying a riffle with two loach minnow nest cavities. Before drying, depths ranged from 5 to 30 cm, currents were turbulent and moderate in velocity, and the substrate was cobble, gravel and coarse sand. Water temperature was 23°C between 0900 and 1030 h.
- **March 1979:** 13 egg-containing cavities were studied in the same area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Nest Structure

All 13 cavities studied in 1979 were beneath rocks. The authors reported:

- **Location:** 11 (84.6%) were in shear zones at riffle edges. The text reports two (75.4%) within riffles, but the counts imply about 15.4%, so the figure may contain a typographic error.
- **Flow position:** 10 cavities (76.2%) opened into eddying currents on the downstream side. Three (23.8%) had entrances on the shoreward side, in turbulent water between "shooting" flow and downstream eddies.
- **Shape:** Cavities were longer than wide. Entrances were apparently narrower than cavity widths and typically collapsed when rocks were lifted.
- **Water depth:** Depth over nest rocks averaged 10.8 cm.

The 1988 cavity was about 2.5 cm wide, 1.2 cm high and 8.0 cm long, with walls and floor of coarse sand and pea-sized gravel. Nest cavities are not natural depressions, so the authors infer that the fish either excavate them or enlarge existing spaces. Aquarium observations show loach minnows pushing aside gravel with their snouts while feeding and enlarging cavities.

### Egg Masses

- Eggs were attached to the rock roof and to the back of the cavity in the 15 nests examined.
- Masses were either adherent, single-layered (n = 9) or pendulous, multilayered (n = 6), hanging toward the floor.
- Each mass was uniform in colour and developmental stage, suggesting a single spawning event.
- The two masses collected in 1988 contained about 90 and 125 eggs. Counts were made after transport, and some eggs had already hatched.
- Previously reported averages per rock were 63 eggs (range 9–260, n = 25; [[Britt]] 1982) and 52 eggs (range 4–250, n = 80; [[Propst]] et al., in litt.).

### Parental Behaviour

- In 1988, a male and female (50–60 mm SL, both in breeding colours) shared one nest. A large male (about 65 mm SL) occupied another.
- In 1979, males darted in and out of nine of 13 nests, and one nest held a pair.
- Males rested on cavity floors facing the entrance, fanned the egg mass with their pectoral fins (possibly aerating or cleaning it), and left the cavity several times per minute.
- When rivals approached, a male flared its opercula, showed its red branchiostegal region, made threatening side-to-side head movements, and lunged from the entrance up to three or seven times before retreating.
- A female was seen returning repeatedly to a nest with eggs close to hatching, which raises the possibility of female guarding.

### Seasonal Timing and Hydrology

Earlier studies described a spring-dominated season:

- [[Minckley]] (1973) reported spawning in late winter and early spring, with young-of-the-year in February and March. Later observations placed the main breeding period from March to June, with some reproduction in December–February.
- [[Britt]] (1982) found a gonosomatic index peak in April 1979, minimum values in August 1979, and eggs in April to early June in the Gila River, New Mexico.
- [[Propst]] et al. (in litt.) recorded a peak gonosomatic index and spawning in April 1984, over a 2- to 3-week period.

The region has a bimodal flow pattern of winter rains, spring drought, [[Summer Monsoon]] floods and autumn drought. Floods occur in January–February and August–September, with base flows in March–June and October–December. Water temperatures in spring and autumn are similar, usually exceeding 20°C in part of the habitat, so autumn conditions may be as favourable for spawning as spring conditions. A spate occurred 12 days before the 1988 nests were found (USGS discharge data). The authors suggest that flooding may cue reproduction, as proposed for several southwestern fishes, and that some individuals may stay reproductively capable through summer.

## Key Findings

1. **Autumn spawning is documented.** Two nests with eggs and breeding males were found on 25 September 1988, the first record of autumn spawning in this species.
2. **Hole-nesting with male guarding.** Loach minnows nest in rock-roofed cavities on riffles, and males guard eggs. This places the species in the hole-nesting guild with male care (see [[Reproductive Guilds]]; [[Balon]] 1975), which is rare among North American minnows.
3. **Possible female guarding.** A female was observed guarding a nest with eggs near hatching. If confirmed, the species would be unusual among cyprinids, where biparental nest guarding has not been documented.
4. **Flexible reproductive season.** The authors ask whether autumn spawning is restricted to special conditions, occurs regularly in part of the population after summer floods, or happens throughout summer and autumn. Further study is needed to identify the cues and assess how common the behaviour is.
5. **Relevance to conservation.** Because loach minnows are threatened, knowing their timing, nesting habitat and parental care is important for protecting riffle habitat and planning flow management, including the effects of diversion and drying.
6. **Evolutionary interest.** The species is thought to be related to a "*Rhinichthys*-like" stock of western cyprinids, and no *Rhinichthys* species is a demonstrated hole-nester. The authors consider *T. cobitis* important for studying the evolution of nesting and parental care.
