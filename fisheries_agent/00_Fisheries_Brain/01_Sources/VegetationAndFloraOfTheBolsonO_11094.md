# Vegetation and Flora of the Bolsón of Cuatro Ciénegas Region, Coahuila, Mexico

#botany #plant-ecology #vascular-flora

## Executive Summary

This 1979 botanical paper by [[Donald J. Pinkava]] (Arizona State University) describes the vegetation and vascular flora of the [[Cuatro Ciénegas Basin]], an intermontane valley at the eastern edge of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] in central Coahuila, Mexico. The study area covers about 2000 km², including the surrounding limestone sierras. The author reports a known flora of 824 taxa, organizes the vegetation into eight zones, and presents an annotated catalogue of the vascular plants with habitat notes and specimen citations. The paper stresses the region's endemism, its unusual aquatic and gypsum habitats, and the need to preserve part of the ecosystem.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** The bolsón is about 40 km east-west and 25 km north-south. It is bisected by the Sierra de San Marcos. Elevation ranges from about 740 m on the basin floor (the Executive-level summary gives about 700 m) to more than 3,000 m atop [[Sierra de la Madera]]. Mean annual precipitation is under 200 mm (Shreve, 1944). The town of [[Cuatro Ciénegas de Carranza]] sits near the mouth of the [[Rio Cañón]].

**Hydrology.** The basin has abundant water, much of it subterranean. The paper notes seven major epigeal drainage systems, the largest being Rio San Marcos (locally Rio Mesquites). Rivers can reach over 20 m wide and 2.5 m deep, with flow from nearly 0 to 75 cm/s. Spring water can be thermal, up to 31 °C (Minckley, 1969). Water emerges from travertine-lined tubes or from [[Posos]] (pits), which vary from under 1 m to over 10 m deep and from a few centimeters to over 200 m in diameter.

**Geomorphic processes.** The Rio Churince flows into [[Laguna Grande]], where evaporation produces nearly pure gypsum. Wind moves the gypsum into dunes. Plugged outflows create travertine deposits, and blocked inflows lead to eutrophication and marsh formation. Evaporating lagunas become playas such as Laguna Grande and Laguna Salada.

**Authorship and prior work.** Ivan M. Johnston first collected in the basin in 1938. Research resumed about 25 years later through teams from Arizona State University (including [[W. L. Minckley]]), the University of Texas at Austin, and the Desert Botanical Garden. Minckley's 1967 invitation brought the author to the basin.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Vegetation zones.** The paper divides the vegetation into eight groupings:

1. **Basin grasslands:** saline zacatón grassland dominated by [[Sporobolus Airoides]], *Distichlis stricta*, and *Monanthochloe littoralis*.
2. **Aquatic and semiaquatic habitats:** sedges, grasses, and aquatic plants around posos and lagunas.
3. **Gypsum dunes:** active dunes up to six meters high, stabilized by [[Prosopis Glandulosa]] and *Yucca treculeana*.
4. **Transition zone:** scattered shrub and tree "islands" (mogotes) ringing the basin and lagunas.
5. **Desert scrub:** Chihuahuan Desert scrub led by [[Larrea Tridentata]].
6. **Chaparral:** oak, heath, and shrub communities on northerly and easterly exposures.
7. **Oak-pine and oak woodlands:** on moister slopes and canyon bases.
8. **Montane forests:** mesic forests of pine, fir, and Douglas-fir on upper north-facing canyons.

**Succession in posos.** Newly formed posos have little more flora than the surrounding grassland. Sedges and grasses colonize the slumping banks, and species accumulate as the poso matures. The author reports that early invaders persist in mature stages, so diversity is additive. Mature posos such as Posos de los Fresnos hold aquatics including [[Nymphaea Ampla]], *Utricularia obtusa*, and *Chara* spp.

**Gypsophily.** [[Gypsophily]] (restriction to calcium sulfate substrates) is a key theme. [[Ivan M. Johnston]] (1941) noted that the Cuatro Ciénegas locality was the only one where he found possible gypsophiles associated with pronounced halophytes. Dune endemics include several composites, such as *Machaeranthera gypsophila*, *Gaillardia gypsophila*, and *Dyssodia gypsophila*.

**Paleoecology.** Meyer (1973) compared modern pollen rain with late Quaternary deposits and found that basin-floor vegetation has been essentially equivalent, if not identical, for the last 30,000–40,000 years.

**Conservation.** The author cites the region's endemic animals (Minckley 1969, 1976), its unique aquatic habitats, and its scenic value as reasons for urgent preservation.

## Key Findings

- **Flora size:** The known vascular flora comprises 824 taxa. The Table 1 and catalogue text gives 786 species in 429 genera from 111 families, plus cultivars at a distance from habitation (6 species, 6 genera, 3 families). The Summary, however, gives 796 species. The two totals do not agree and the paper does not explain the difference.
- **Taxonomic composition:** Table 1 breaks the flora into divisions (Lycopodinae, Filicinae, Gymnospermae, Angiospermae, with Monocotyledonae and Dicotyledonae). The OCR of the table is partly garbled, so its numbers are not reliable here.
- **Type localities:** 26 taxa have their type locality in the region. Of these, 8 taxa are endemic, marked with "!" in the text. The new taxa noted include *Abutilon*, *Clematis*, *Sabatia*, *Phacelia*, *Salvia*, and *Cirsium*, with at least six undescribed taxa from these genera.
- **Endemic and notable species:** Examples include the endemic [[Abies Coahuilensis]] (type locality in the Cañón de la Hacienda area), *Tiquilia turneri*, *Penstemon henricksonii*, and *Euphorbia pinkavana*. Notable records include *Selaginella lepidophylla* (resurrection plant), *Lophophora williamsii*, and *Brahea berlandieri*.
- **Disjunct record:** *Fimbristylis thermalis* is described as disjunct from its main distribution in the southwestern US and Baja California.
- **Catalogue scope:** The catalogue covers ferns and allies, gymnosperms, aquatic plants (for example, *Najas*, *Ruppia maritima*, *Zannichellia palustris*, *Potamogeton nodosus*), grasses, sedges, and orchids. Each entry gives habitat, frequency by zone, and specimen citations. Collector codes identify the herbaria (for example, ASU, TEX-LL, GH).
- **Nomenclature:** Names follow the *Manual of the Vascular Plants of Texas* (Correll & Johnston, 1970).
- **Acknowledgments and funding:** The study was carried out under the auspices of W. L. Minckley's NSF grants GB-2461 and GB 64774. Many specialists contributed identifications for particular families and genera.
