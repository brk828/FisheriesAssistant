# Fish and Other Aquatic Resource Trends in the United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #percidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service technical document (Gen. Tech. Rep. RMRS-GTR-53, 2000) by [[Andrew J. Loftus]] and [[Curtis H. Flather]] supports the 2000 [[RPA Assessment]] required under the Renewable Resources Planning Act of 1974. It reviews national trends in water quality, habitat, fish and aquatic species populations, and resource use, and examines how land use shapes aquatic ecosystems. Data came mainly from existing state and federal agency sources. The report concludes that no national survey accurately describes the condition of U.S. waterways, that aquatic fauna are disproportionately imperiled, and that effective recovery depends on integrating land, water and fish management, as shown in nine case studies.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and scope.** The report is not a comprehensive status account of every aquatic resource. It focuses on general habitat condition, species status and use trends from select surveys, and nine [[Case Studies]] covering lands beyond National Forests, including some nearshore marine species.

**Legal framework.** Federal management rests on overlapping authorities, including the [[Magnuson-Stevens Act]] (1976, amended 1996, with essential fish habitat provisions), the [[Coastal Zone Management Act]] (1972), the [[Clean Water Act]], the [[Lacey Act]] (1900; fish added by the 1926 [[Black Bass Act]]), the [[Interjurisdictional Fisheries Act]] (1986), the [[Atlantic Striped Bass Conservation Act]] (1984), the [[Atlantic Coast Cooperative Fishery Management Act]] (1993) and the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Under 16 U.S.C. 694, Forest Service fish sanctuaries require state legislative approval.

**Ecological role.** Aquatic species link ecosystems. [[Pacific Salmon]] move nutrients and energy between sea, freshwater and land, affecting terrestrial wildlife. The authors argue the indirect ecological benefits of aquatic fauna may exceed their direct consumptive value (Dailey 1997).

**Scale of resources.** The United States has about 3.5 million miles of streams (roughly two-thirds perennial), 41 million acres of lakes and reservoirs, 100,000 square miles of estuaries (excluding Alaska) and about 300 million acres of wetlands (EPA 1996). National Forest System lands hold 128,000 miles of fishable streams and rivers and 2.2 million acres of lakes, ponds and reservoirs. Fish supply protein and recreation for about one-fifth of U.S. residents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** According to EPA (1996), 70% of assessed river miles, lake acres and estuarine area supported the Clean Water Act "aquatic life use." Between 1980 and 1989, a greater share of sites improved than degraded for five of six indicators: dissolved oxygen, dissolved solids, fecal coliform, suspended sediment, and phosphorus and nitrate.

**Habitat alteration.** Only 2% of the more than three million miles of lower-48 streams remain in a condition that qualifies them for wild and scenic designation. The other 98% are affected by dams, diversions or significant development (Wilcove and Bean 1994). [[Sedimentation]] is among the most serious pollution threats. Agriculture is the largest nonpoint source, with an estimated 675 million to one billion tons of eroded soil reaching waterways each year. Riparian filter strips can cut nutrients in agricultural runoff by up to 80%.

**Contaminants.** [[Contaminant Advisories]] are a poor proxy for water quality because criteria vary across agencies. More than two-thirds of 1,363 surveyed watersheds had fish consumption advisories (EPA 1998), and 7% were "areas of probable concern." Advisories covered 15% of the nation's total lake acres and 5% of river miles, and 96% of 1996 advisories were for mercury.

**Wetlands.** Over half of the roughly 221 million acres of wetlands present before European colonization have been lost. Freshwater (palustrine) wetlands made up 85% of losses. About 75% of estuarine fish species of recreational or commercial value depend on marshes. One Florida study valued each additional acre of wetland at $79.60 for the recreational fishery.

**Sediment and species.** Freshwater mussels are especially sensitive to siltation, dams and invasive species such as [[Zebra Mussel]] and [[Asian Clam]].

## Key Findings

**Aquatic species status**
- About 800 freshwater fish species occur in the United States. Population trends for most are unknown, and species counts alone do not indicate ecosystem integrity.
- Fish have been listed as threatened or endangered at about twice the rate of other vertebrates over the last two decades. Mussels are imperiled at even higher proportions.
- 111 fish taxa are officially listed under the ESA. The American Fisheries Society (1989) listed 364 fish taxa warranting protection.
- The Nature Conservancy classed 55% of North American mussels as extinct or imperiled, compared with 7% of birds or mammals. Of 297 native mussel species, 213 are endangered, threatened or of special concern, and 21 taxa are extinct.
- Four main causes of extinction in 40 North American fish taxa (Miller et al. 1989): habitat loss (73%), introduced species (68%), chemical pollution (38%), and overharvest (15%).

**Marine and anadromous species**
- Of U.S. marine fish stocks assessed in 1993, 40% were overutilized and 43% fully utilized.
- Of 15 distinct Pacific Northwest salmon and trout population segments, all are listed under the ESA.
- In southeast Alaska, fewer than 1% of identifiable stocks were at high or moderate extinction risk, versus 15% in Washington.

**Economic and social context**
- Recreational fishing participation was stable between 1991 and 1996, but the percentage of the U.S. population that fishes continues to decline.
- Forest Service lands support nearly 27 million angler use days, with angler spending of about $3 billion and economic output of $8.5 billion (Maharaj and Carpenter 1999).
- The main reasons for not fishing more were time and competing interests; resource conditions were cited by 1–2% of respondents or fewer.

**Case study outcomes**
- **[[Midwest Crappie Production]] (Rathbun Lake, Iowa):** Turbidity explained 49% of variation in crappie abundance. Watershed erosion (321,000 tons of sediment yearly) was a key driver.
- **[[Canyon Creek Restoration]] (Arizona):** Riparian and in-stream work cut average water temperature by 9.4 °C (21.7 °C to 12.3 °C, 1985–1994). Natural brown trout reproduction resumed after over 40 years without it.
- **[[Michigan Arctic Grayling]] restoration:** 145,000 fingerlings were stocked in 13 lakes and river systems. Survival was poor, and no natural reproduction was recorded. The original strain is extirpated.
- **[[Lake Superior Lake Trout]] recovery:** Over 90% of Michigan-waters lake trout caught in 1994 were of wild origin. Sea lamprey control, harvest closures and spawning habitat restoration were central.
- **[[Chesapeake Bay Restoration]]:** Phosphorus fell 16% between 1985 and 1992 (target 40% by 2000). Striped bass populations along the Atlantic coast rose nearly 800% over 15 years. The Susquehanna River shad run grew from under 6,000 to over 200,000 fish.
- **[[Columbia River Salmonids]]:** Total return ranged from 3.2 million (1986) to 856,500 (1994). Only 3 of 44 Snake River streams had redds in 1994, the lowest on record. Recreational salmon fishing expenditures fell 37–51% between 1985 and 1991.
- **[[Southern Appalachian Aquatic Resources]]:** 190 aquatic species are endangered, threatened or of special concern, including 57 mussels and 62 fish. Seventy percent of sampled locations showed fish community degradation.
- **[[Atlantic Salmon]] (New England):** Historic runs of about 500,000 fish have declined to historic lows. Low marine survival is a major factor, and returns could potentially double without West Greenland and Canadian intercept fisheries.
- **[[Multi-State Aquatic Resources Information System]] (MARIS):** Crappie catch-per-unit-effort data from Illinois, Ohio and Wisconsin were analyzed. Three of 15 Illinois lakes had significant negative trends for white crappie. Only four of six member states had data available.

**Management conclusions**
- No single data source adequately measures public use or resource status. Harvest data can be misleading under regulatory caps.
- Under 33% of Forest Service line officers thought monitoring adequately documented aquatic changes on their forests.
- Road-building significantly increases sediment yield. Conventionally harvested watersheds produced 100 times more sediment than undisturbed controls.
- The report recommends a holistic ecosystem approach, broad-scale status and trend information, and standardized data sharing to meet RPA reporting mandates.
