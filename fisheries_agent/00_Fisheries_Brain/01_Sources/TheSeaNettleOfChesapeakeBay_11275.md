# Summer Sea Nettle in Chesapeake Bay: Biology, Stinging Hazard, and Control Research

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Pelagiidae

## Executive Summary

This report, by Leonard P. Schultz and David G. Carge of the [[Chesapeake Biological Laboratory]], describes the summer sea nettle (*Chrysaora quinquecirrha*, formerly *Dactylometra quinquecirrha*), a recurring jellyfish nuisance in [[Chesapeake Bay]]. The medusae appear by late June, peak in July and August, and cause declines in swimming, economic losses to beach operators and fishermen, and painful stings. The document covers the species' two-stage life cycle, its feeding and stinging biology, population fluctuations since 1960, and control efforts, including physical barriers, sediment application, and biological control by the [[Shell-Less Nudibranch]] (*Cratena pilata*). No effective control method has yet been identified.

## Background & Description

The summer sea nettle is a cnidarian in the phylum Coelenterata, related to corals, sea anemones, and hydras. The medusa may reach 9 inches in diameter, has a translucent gelatinous bell, 8 nerve centers in the bell margin, numerous fishing tentacles (over 4 feet long in large individuals), and four oral arms. Upper- and middle-bay medusae are milky white, while lower-bay individuals show 16 short red bars on the bell. Chemical analysis reports about 97% water and about 3% protein and salts (the source gives "39% protein and salts and 97% water," which is internally inconsistent and is reported here as stated).

Medusae have separate sexes. Gonads become mature from July onward. Fertilized eggs develop into swimming planulae, which settle on hard surfaces and form polyps. The document also describes related species found in the bay:

- [[Moon Jellyfish]] (*Aurelia aurita*): abundant in the lower bay in summer, but not a problem because its sting is scarcely felt.
- *Rhopilema verrilli*: too rare to be of concern; dried individuals are eaten in Japan, Hong Kong, and Taiwan.
- [[Red Winter Jellyfish]] (*Cyanea capillata*): appears in December, disappears by the end of May, and has a weak sting.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life cycle and alternation of generations.** Polyps (scyphistomae) reproduce asexually by cysts, stolons, and strobilation, while medusae reproduce sexually. A polyp can produce about 5 ephyrae per episode (some over 10), up to 4 times per season. The authors estimate that one polyp could yield about 1,000 medusae in a year under the stated assumptions. Polyp densities vary: one shell had 1,200 polyps, but usually fewer than 25 polyps and cysts occur, and many shells have none. Strobilation begins when water reaches about 17 °C (63 °F). Polyps can encyst and survive winter, and may persist for several years.

**Salinity tolerance.** The sea nettle tolerates salinities from 7 to 35 ‰ and dies below 5 ‰.

**Hydrodynamics.** Wave action and currents cause daily variation in surface abundance. Medusae sink under turbulence and are returned to beaches by surface currents.

**Feeding.** Medusae drift passively and paralyze prey with nematocysts. Common food includes small fishes, worms, shellfish larvae, sea walnuts, and small invertebrates and protozoans. Digestion takes 8 to 12 hours at room temperature.

**Population fluctuation.** Staff of the Chesapeake Biological Laboratory have recorded relative abundance since 1960. The species was scarce in 1960 and 1961 and abundant thereafter. In 1963, 1966, and 1969, medusae disappeared suddenly by early September. Proposed causes include heavy rain, sedimentation, a jellyfish disease, or predators; none has been confirmed.

**Predators and competitors.** The [[Shell-Less Nudibranch]] is the main polyp predator; one individual ate 500 polyps in 3 days. Juvenile [[Harvestfish]] (*Peprilus alepidotus*) and juvenile orange [[Filefish]] (*Alutera schoepfi*) feed on medusae, but none is effective at population reduction. Four small fishes compete for shell space and destroy some polyps: the clingfish, striped blenny, feather blenny, and naked goby. None measurably reduces the population.

## Key Findings

- **Nuisance and economic impact:** The sea nettle hinders swimming, causes economic loss to beach operators and fishermen, and is the bay's most troublesome pest.
- **Stinging hazard:** Nematocyst venom is a complex protein, not formic acid. Most stings cause inflamed skin lasting a few hours; a few people have allergic reactions requiring hospitalization. Papain (a meat-tenderizer enzyme) gave almost immediate relief when applied quickly, though a second application is often needed. The thin skin of the arms, legs, and body is vulnerable, while palm skin is usually too thick to be penetrated.
- **Barrier results:** Galvanized wire hardware cloth on wooden frames, spaced on posts 10 feet apart, has been successful. Fish netting has had only partial success, as it clogs, tears, and fragments medusae whose detached tentacles still sting. All barriers require daily servicing. A combination of water jets and netting is considered the most promising, but remains experimental.
- **Sediment control:** Sediment 1/8 inch deep stops asexual reproduction in 3 to 4 weeks, but also kills small oysters and other sessile organisms, so it is impractical.
- **Chemical control:** Chemicals that kill polyps also kill oysters, crabs, and other marine organisms. Large-scale chemical or netting control is considered impractical given the bay's size.
- **Biological control:** The shell-less nudibranch is the only predator found that voraciously eats polyps. Laboratory culture is underway to assess its use.
- **Research priorities:** The authors call for continued research on biology, control methods, hormones that might retard reproduction, barrier design, and substances that neutralize venom.
