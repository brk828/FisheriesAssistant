# Evaluation of Personnel, Facilities, and Programs at Dexter National Fish Hatchery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #endangered-fishes

## Executive Summary

This document is an evaluation by [[Wendell L. Minckley]] (Professor of Zoology, Arizona State University) of the staffing, facilities, and programs at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH), an endangered-species conservation facility. The author argues that DNFH's goals (perpetuating endangered fishes in their original genetic state and re-establishing viable wild populations) require stable expert staff, better laboratory, office, and library space, and a backup holding facility for critically endangered stocks. The text also covers production, refuge, and research priorities, fisheries assistance, and security. Appended field records (1988 to 1989) document high mortality in [[Bonytail]] transported to and held in river pens, and a 1992 memorandum transmits a related stress study for technical review.

## Background & Description

DNFH is described as an advanced-concept conservation center whose mission has three components: production, refuge, and research. "Maintenance" means perpetuating species in their original genetic state, and "recovery" means re-establishing viable populations in natural habitats. The author frames personnel policy around three goals:

1. Retaining highly qualified, dedicated staff.
2. Training skilled people in hatchery management, with the aim of permanent employment.
3. Discouraging transfer of personnel who are not firmly committed to Endangered Species Programs.

The author contrasts academic promotion ladders (Instructor, Assistant and Associate Professor, Full Professor) with federal and state systems, in which advancement usually requires moving to a new position. He proposes a tiered system that keeps expertise at the station.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Many critically endangered species at DNFH survive in very small populations, and some hatchery broodstocks are larger genetic pools than any wild population. Other species are restricted to single localities in nature and could be lost to one catastrophe. The author stresses that loss of a broodstock would be a final, major failure if the species is extirpated in the wild.

Documented losses at the hatchery show that stocks can be compromised despite good care:

- [[Sonoran Topminnow]] stocks were contaminated by [[Pecos Gambusia]].
- Gulls preyed on [[Woundfin]] (called "squawfish" in the source text).
- Yaqui Chub failed to reproduce successfully.
- Other die-offs occurred from unknown or undocumented causes.

Security concerns included unauthorized fish movement between ponds, a Leon Springs Pupfish found in a razorback sucker pond (spring 1995), a Pecos Gambusia in a Sonoran Topminnow broodstock, largemouth bass in the sump, and predation by gulls, terns, and osprey.

Genetic concerns focus on bottlenecks and hybridization. Razorback sucker broodstock management should maintain roughly 500 to 1,500 wild-caught fish (based on the Lake Mohave population). The author notes that sex ratio, mixing of sperm from multiple males, and culling of aberrant individuals have only been addressed superficially.

## Key Findings

### Personnel and Staffing
- Proposed structure: one Hatchery Manager, two Assistant Managers (physical facilities; production/broodstock or refuge/research), an Office Manager, a technical assistant, a typist, two non-technical staff for the physical plant, and two trainees.
- Trainees and assistants should have training in basic ecology and/or systematics, with emphasis on fishes. Postdoctoral scientists could fill the Assistant Manager role.
- Technical staff should be exempt from maximum-time limitations and encouraged through recognition of scholarship, publication, and society membership.

### Facilities
- **Laboratory:** Current facilities are inadequate. Microscopes need service, and space is at a premium. The author suggests large flexible rooms (about 15 x 20 feet or larger) with movable tables, stainless steel sinks, hot, cold, and distilled water, exhaust hoods, and electrical outlets (115 and 220 V) with line stabilization.
- **Offices:** About 100 to 150 square feet per permanent employee, with access to the laboratory.
- **Library:** Severely lacking and needed because of DNFH's distance from a major library. Recommended journals include *Copeia*, *Southwestern Naturalist*, *Transactions of the American Fisheries Society*, *Progressive Fish-Culturist*, and the *Journal of North American Fisheries Management*. Museum mailing lists and reprint exchanges should be established.
- **Housing:** On-station housing attracts trainees and postdoctoral staff and improves security.

### Holding Facility
- The author recommends an enclosed, climate-controlled aquarium facility holding 20 to 50 species in large tanks, each with individual in- and outflow of well water.
- Summer water temperature should be near 22 °C (heated from well water), with air maintained at 15–18 °C in winter and below 25 °C in summer.
- The facility should be restricted to authorized personnel, isolated from pesticide application, and monitored daily.
- The present room with eight aquaria is inadequate.
- Species needing backup include Bonytail, Razorback Sucker, Big Bend Gambusia, and Leon Springs Pupfish. Species with only one population or with unavailable broodstock sources (e.g., Humpback Chub) should also be protected.

### Production and Broodstock
- Production of many fishes has been spectacularly successful, but the author worries that success with easier species (e.g., Colorado Squawfish, Razorback Sucker, most poeciliids) has reduced effort on difficult species such as Woundfin and Yaqui Chub.
- Heterozygous Sonoran Topminnow stocks were transferred to DNFH after a published criticism of the reintroduction program (in *Science*) argued that broodstocks were homozygous and of low fecundity. The author disputes the low-fecundity claim.
- Bonytail reproduction in hatchery ponds indicates that the species may establish reproducing populations.

### Refuge and Alternatives
- Species categorized as needing refuge include Bonytail, Razorback Sucker (absent from the wild or at risk), Big Bend Gambusia, Leon Springs Pupfish, Desert Pupfish, and Pahranagat and Humpback chubs.
- Options include Fish and Wildlife Service refuges for large-river fishes and contracts with universities or state agencies for smaller species. Estimated initial costs for holding facilities are partly illegible in the source but are described as modest relative to DNFH's total program.
- Refuge waters should be closed to fishing to protect migratory birds and endangered fishes.

### Research and Fisheries Assistance
- Research priorities: propagation and rearing of diverse fishes; field experiments on growth, feeding, and reproduction; and genetic studies on broodstock.
- Recommended Research Advisory Group of no more than eight technical staff, including Recovery Team leaders.
- The Fisheries Assistance position should split duties among field monitoring, on-site studies, and liaison with state and academic personnel. The author warns against letting liaison work dominate.

### Recommendations Summary
- Establish the backup holding facility and maintain multiple secure stocks.
- Retain samples (at least 50 specimens, twice yearly) in a permanent museum.
- Maintain an observer who visits ponds at least two hours daily and logs events.
- Begin the proposed refuge-based approach immediately.

### Field Records (1988–1989)
- **Bonytail, 1988 (Green River pen study):** Of 13 fish in a 90-day trial, five died. Fish were lethargic at the study's end.
- **Bonytail, 1989 transports:** Fish from DNFH were transported to the Green River (fifteen implanted May 22, thirteen released May 30). Ten of 21 implanted fish died after a river-elevation drop, and all pen fish had died by June 12.
- **Bonytail, June–July 1989:** Of 22 transported fish, 15 were implanted June 27. Seven of the remaining ten (four controls, three implanted) died. A second group of 22 was treated with antibiotics, vitamin C, and fungicide. Seven of ten pen fish died (diagnosed as columnaris infestation) between July 24 and 28.
- **1992 memorandum:** Lee Lentsch (Utah Native Wildlife Program Coordinator) circulated a report on pre-introduction stress and survival of Bonytail stocked in the Green River, for technical review.

### Public Display and Security
- Aquarium displays educate the public but are poorly maintained; feeding should use living foods to reduce cleaning problems.
- Security measures include disinfecting boots and equipment between ponds and monitoring transfers between ponds. Unauthorized visitors were rare, and staff occupancy of on-site housing was a positive security factor.
