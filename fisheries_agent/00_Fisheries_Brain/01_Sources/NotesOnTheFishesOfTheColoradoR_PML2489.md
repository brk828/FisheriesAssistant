# Fish Fauna of the Colorado River near Moab, Utah

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #ictaluridae #cyprinodontidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

A field survey of the [[Colorado River]] near [[Moab Utah]] (from Moab about 25 miles downstream) was conducted by Taft, Murphy, and Frost of Brigham Young University between May 1962 and October 1963 (dates partly garbled in the scan). The 2,295 specimens collected represent 5 families, 13 genera, and 16 fish species. Native catostomids and cyprinids were found alongside several introduced species. [[Red Shiner]] made up about 45 percent of the catch and was reported for Utah for the first time. A crayfish, *Orconectes virilis*, was also recorded for the first time in this section of the river and formed a large part of the diet of larger fishes. The study was funded by the Texas Gulf Sulphur Company, Potash Division.

## Background & Description

The paper is a short faunal and ecological note on the fishes of the Colorado River near Moab. The authors used the collections to document species occurrence, size ranges, habitat use, and diet. Most species were taken in quiet backwaters and tributary washes, with some in the main channel.

The fish community described includes:

- **Catostomidae (suckers), four species:** [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Mountain Sucker]], and [[Humpback Sucker]]. Suckers are described as native to the upper and middle or lower Colorado River drainages.
- **Cyprinidae (minnows and carps), six species:** [[Common Carp]], [[Bonytail]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Speckled Dace]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Red Shiner]].
- **Ictaluridae (catfishes), two species:** [[Black Bullhead]] and [[Channel Catfish]].
- **Cyprinodontidae (killifishes), one species:** [[Rio Grande Killifish]].
- **Centrarchidae (sunfishes), three species:** [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Bluegill]].

Several taxa are documented as introduced to Utah, including the common carp (introduced in the late nineteenth century; year garbled in scan), black bullhead (introduced 1914), largemouth bass (introduced into Utah; date garbled), and the fathead minnow (first reported for Utah in a 1959 survey of Glen Canyon).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use.** Quiet backwaters and tributary washes were the main collection sites for most species. Several species were associated with specific substrates or conditions:

- Killifish were commonly found near the mouths of tributary washes and over gravel-bottomed backwaters.
- Fathead minnows were associated with the highly turbid water of the river.
- Green sunfish were common in all parts of the study area, usually near shore.
- The bluegill was considered apparently adapted to turbid habitat in the lower river.

**Diet.** Food habits ranged from herbivory and detritivory to carnivory:

- Suckers fed largely on algae, organic matter, and ooze. The humpback sucker's food was mainly detrital ooze from muddy backwaters.
- Common carp fed mainly on amorphous organic matter.
- Speckled dace had stomachs full of green algae (*Cladophora*) and detritus.
- Fathead minnows ate mainly plant and organic detritus.
- Black bullhead, a carnivorous species, ate small fishes, aquatic insects, detritus, and seeds.
- Channel catfish stomach analysis of 37 individuals showed a diet of minnows, 45 percent organic detritus, and 41 percent crayfish.
- The Rio Grande killifish fed strongly on aquatic insects.
- Largemouth bass fed on aquatic insects; sunfish fed strongly on minnows.

**Crayfish.** The crayfish *Orconectes virilis* was new to this section of the river. It was verified by H. H. Hobbs, Jr., of the U.S. National Museum. The species is native to the Great Lakes and Mississippi drainages and prefers rivers over ponds and other lentic waters. It was commonly collected in spring and summer and was a major food item for the larger fishes near Moab.

## Key Findings

1. **Species richness.** Sixteen fish species from five families and 13 genera were recorded from 2,295 specimens.

2. **Dominance of Red Shiner.** The red shiner made up about 45 percent of the total collection. It was taken throughout the survey period in all parts of the river. This was the first published report of the species in Utah. Spawning was observed from late May to July in quiet backwaters and tributary washes. The species was maintained in laboratory aquaria for 18 months.

3. **Native suckers.** Four sucker species were collected:
   - *Flannelmouth sucker* (122 specimens, approximately 82 to 155 mm, upper limit partly illegible in the scan). Most were taken in quiet backwaters, with some in the main channel and at tributary mouths. Diet was mainly algae, organic matter, and ooze.
   - *Bluehead sucker* (sample size partly garbled; length 41 to 89 mm). All were small individuals; diet was mainly algae.
   - *Mountain sucker* (17 specimens, 45 to 73 mm). Feeds on algae.
   - *Humpback sucker* (count partly illegible; 90 to 115 mm). Native to the lower Colorado River. Diet was detrital ooze in muddy backwaters.

4. **Common carp.** Twenty-two fish were collected. Specimens ranged from about 118 to 280 mm, and carp were not common in this part of the river. One gravid female was found.

5. **Bonytail and Colorado squawfish.**
   - *Bonytail:* Several specimens were collected, but the authors state that their sampling techniques were not fully adequate for swift-water species. Their notes on this species are tentative.
   - *Colorado squawfish:* Described as the largest North American minnow. Most specimens were collected in early summer and were young fish, taken in quiet backwaters. Stomachs of these young fish were empty or contained detritus.

6. **Speckled dace.** Only nine fish were collected, measuring 6 to 42 mm. Subspecific identity was uncertain.

7. **Fathead minnow.** This minnow was first reported for Utah in 1959. Several hundred specimens were collected (partly illegible in the scan). Spawning fish, large males, and gravid females were observed in early summer. The species was successfully reared in aquaria for at least 18 months under laboratory conditions.

8. **Black bullhead.** This introduced catfish (introduced to Utah in 1914) was collected in backwaters, ranging from 118 to 149 mm. It preferred still water areas and was considered carnivorous.

9. **Channel catfish.** Specimens ranged from about 98 to 146 mm (partly garbled in the scan). The species is native to the Mississippi drainage and is now widespread in the Colorado River. Its omnivorous feeding habits have helped it establish itself in the river.

10. **Rio Grande killifish.** Thirty-one fish were collected, but the length range is partly illegible in the scan. Killifish were kept in laboratory aquaria for a year.

11. **Largemouth bass.** Five small specimens (35 to 45 mm) were collected. The authors suggest that inappropriate sampling methods may have limited the catch.

12. **Green sunfish.** Specimens ranged from 24 to 130 mm and were found in all parts of the study area, most often near shore.

13. **Bluegill.** One specimen (48 mm) was collected from a backwater. Its stomach was empty.

14. **Non-native species.** Several introduced species were found, including carp, black bullhead, largemouth bass, and fathead minnow. The authors note that the native fauna is accompanied by these introductions in the study reach.

15. **Acknowledgments.** The research was supported by a research grant from the Texas Gulf Sulphur Company, Potash Division.
