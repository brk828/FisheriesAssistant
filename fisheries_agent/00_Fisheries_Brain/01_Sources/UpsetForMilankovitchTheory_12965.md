# Nature Vol. 359 (29 October 1992): Devils Hole Chronology, Milankovitch Doubts and Science Notes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #palaeoclimatology

*Note: This source contains no fish or aquatic-organism data. The aquatic link is limited to the Devils Hole aquifer and marine foraminifera used as climate proxies. Tags reflect the actual content.*

## Executive Summary

This issue excerpt from *Nature* (vol. 359, 29 October 1992) contains a News & Views essay by [[Wallace S. Broecker]] on the [[Devils Hole Calcite Record]], a palaeoclimate dataset that challenges the timing of the ice ages predicted by [[Milankovitch Theory]]. The essay weighs new isotope and uranium-series evidence against the accepted marine chronology. Broecker concludes that the Devils Hole chronology is the best available isotopic age in its range, yet the record is still irreconcilable with the marine record and Milankovitch cyclicity. The excerpt also includes shorter notes on supramolecular chemistry, animal chemical defence, boron fibre growth, rhinoceros skin mechanics and gecko-inspired adhesives.

## Background & Description

**Milankovitch theory** proposes that ice ages are driven by periodic changes in Earth's orbit: the tilt of the rotation axis, the point of closest approach to the Sun (precession), and orbital eccentricity. These changes alter summer insolation in the Northern Hemisphere mid-to-high latitudes. Peaks and troughs in the radiometrically dated ice-volume record match the expected insolation variations closely. The oxygen isotope composition of marine foraminifera tracks precession, and the magnitude of climate change scales with eccentricity.

**The Devils Hole record** comes from vein calcite coating the hanging wall of an extensional fault in an aquifer in southern Nevada. An isotope shift at Termination II, the end of the penultimate ice age, was first dated in 1988 to 145,000 years, against the previously accepted 128,000 years. The vein's oxygen isotope ratios are interpreted as recording local precipitation changes.

Termination II is the transition from the penultimate glacial period to the last interglacial. The shift in the ¹⁸O/¹⁶O ratio is the key marker in the record.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Transit-time problem:** Water recharges the aquifer far from the recorder site, so the isotopic signal is delayed. Ages are therefore minimum values unless transit time is corrected for.
- **Regional drying:** Radiocarbon work in the Great Basin shows pronounced drying 12,000–13,000 years ago. [[Lake Lahontan]] shrank to isolated remnants and [[Searles Lake]] dried completely.
- **Global synchrony:** Radiocarbon evidence indicates the last termination began about 14,000 years ago and was largely complete by about 9,000 years ago. The ocean record shows an abrupt change at Termination II.
- **Mixing hypothesis:** Dispersion through a fracture network was considered as an explanation for the slow Devils Hole termination and the long warm interval after it. Broecker argues this would need a transit time of 15,000 years or more, which the data do not support.

## Key Findings

**1. The Devils Hole record is extended and strengthened.**
- The ¹⁸O record now extends back two more full climatic cycles, to about 550,000 years.
- It matches the marine ¹⁸O record closely, indicating the vein calcite changed in harmony with deep-sea foraminifera.
- Uranium-series measurements by mass spectrometry verify and refine the earlier chronology.
- ²³⁰Th/²³⁴U measurements on water currently upwelling in Devils Hole confirm the 60,000-year age for the outermost vein surface.
- The independent timescale matches the ²³⁰Th-based dating.

**2. The Termination II discrepancy persists.**
- The Devils Hole termination is longer and more gradual than the ocean record.
- The Devils Hole warm interval lasts twice as long as in the ocean record.
- Coral ages from the last high sea stand range from 118,000 to 135,000 years, a range Winograd's group cites in support of their Termination II age.
- A Caribbean sediment date of 128,000 years fits the accepted marine chronology.

**3. Broecker's assessment.**
- The Devils Hole chronology is considered the most robust isotopic age in this range, owing to concordant uranium-series ages, good preservation and many stratigraphically ordered radiometric dates.
- Broecker remains uncertain. He concludes that either the Devils Hole or the marine chronology must give way, and climate modellers should prepare for "a world without Milankovitch."

**4. Other items in the issue.**
- **Chemical defence:** [[Loggerhead Shrike]] birds in Florida can eat [[Lubber Grasshopper]] prey, which are unpalatable to 21 species of bird and lizard tested, after the toxins decline in aged corpses. Shrikes often impale prey on thorns or barbed wire.
- **Boron fibres:** Wallenberger and Nordine grew boron fibres 6 µm in diameter and up to 2 m long by laser-dissociating high-pressure diborane gas in hydrogen. Growth ran at about 0.5 mm per second. Only single-crystal silicon carbide whiskers have higher tensile strength.
- **Rhinoceros skin:** Shadwick and colleagues studied skin from a white rhinoceros (Duncan) at Calgary zoo. Rhino skin has properties intermediate between skin and tendon, giving high strength, stiffness and work of fracture. It tears easily, which may turn fights into gashes rather than lethal punctures.
- **Supramolecular cages:** [[Leonard F. Lindoy]] reports a hydrocarbon cage synthesis. Attempts at full dehydrogenation with palladium-charcoal at 500 °C failed. Platinum-catalysed hydrogenation fully reduced the aromatic rings. The work contributes to receptors with hydrophobic cavities, with potential uses including pollution control.
- **Fractal sticker:** A "Daedalus" column describes a gecko-inspired adhesive built from graded fibres that adhere through van der Waals forces, with proposed applications in products such as "Fracfix."
