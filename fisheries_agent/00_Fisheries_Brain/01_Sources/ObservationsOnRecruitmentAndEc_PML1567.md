# Razorback Sucker Reintroduction and Recruitment in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Paul C. Marsh]] and [[W. L. Minckley]] (*Great Basin Naturalist*, Vol. 49, No. 1, January 1989) reviews the status of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) in the lower [[Colorado River]] basin. The species was once abundant and endemic to the basin but had become rare, with natural populations largely restricted to a few sites. The authors describe reintroductions made between 1980 and 1988 across Arizona, California, and Nevada, and report evidence of wild-produced juveniles. They conclude that recruitment is occurring but remains uncertain, and that canal systems may function as sinks for drifting larvae and juveniles.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Xyrauchen texanus*, a large catostomid sucker endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]].
- **Status:** Historically abundant; by the study period it persisted in only a few locations, which prompted the reintroduction program.
- **Study scope:** The paper addresses the lower Colorado River, from the Parker and Headgate Rock area downstream through the [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]] reaches, plus the associated canals and drains of the [[Imperial Valley]] and [[Yuma Area]].
- **Sources of fish:** Most stocked fish were hatchery-reared, many from [[Lake Mohave]] broodstock or [[Senator Wash Reservoir]] progeny. Most captured juveniles came from canal sites. Figure 1 is a sketch map of the lower river showing capture and stocking localities.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Canal and drain habitats.** Irrigation canals divert water by gravity at points such as [[Headgate Rock Dam]], [[Palo Verde Diversion Dam]], and [[Imperial Dam]]. Flows reach up to about 400 m³/s. Canals are dewatered periodically for cleaning and repair, usually in the off-season, which can kill stranded fish. Drains carry return flows, are more permanent than canals, and are often vegetated with macrophytes. The authors found no known razorback suckers from drains, although sampling there was limited.

**Predators and competitors.** Nonnative predators, including [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), centrarchids, and ictalurid catfishes such as [[Flathead Catfish]] (*Pylodictis olivaris*), are abundant in drains and reservoirs and prey on young razorback suckers.

**Larval dispersal.** Larvae are most likely passively entrained into canals. Drifting larvae and juveniles are carried downstream, and the authors note that reintroduced juveniles also show a strong tendency to move downstream. Related catostomids in the [[Gila River]] were concentrated near banks, which the authors cite as a possible analogue.

**Reproduction.** A small number of adults spawn in [[Lake Havasu]], and some offspring may pass through penstocks at [[Parker Dam]] and enter downstream intakes. Spawning sites elsewhere are unknown but may exist.

## Key Findings

**1. Reintroduction history (1980–1988)**
- The first lower-river reintroduction occurred in 1980, with hatchery-produced adults released into the isolated [[West Pond]], Imperial County, California.
- In 1983, 457 hatchery-reared razorback suckers (average 95 mm TL) were stocked. Later fish from the same broodstock were held in a rearing enclosure at West Pond. They were not monitored again until 1988, when no razorback suckers were encountered.
- In February 1986, 57 survivors (average 28.5 cm, 39 measured) were stocked into an isolated pond. The authors state these fish probably did not contribute to downstream populations.
- The first major mainstream reintroduction (1986) released nearly 70,000 juveniles at several localities, including [[Devil's Elbow]], [[Topock Gorge]], Lake Havasu, and the [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]. More than a million additional larvae were stocked downstream.

**2. Wild recruitment evidence**
- In 1987, 38 juveniles attributable to the 1986 reintroductions were recovered near Parker, Arizona. Three more fish were recorded in 1988. Some of these fish were considered wild because they lacked hatchery marks.
- Otolith analysis of wild-caught fish showed ages consistent with spring spawning in several years, indicating successful natural spawning, but the sample sizes were small.
- Downstream recruitment from Lake Mohave remains weak. Adults are present, but individuals of reproductive size are scarce and widely dispersed. The authors state that Lake Mohave and Lake Havasu provide convincing evidence of recruitment potential, but recruits are not yet sufficient to maintain a self-sustaining population.

**3. Sources of recruits and loss mechanisms**
- Canals appear to trap drifting larvae and juveniles. Fish captured in canals may be dewatered, entrained, or killed during maintenance, so potential recruits may be lost.
- Some stocked fish may have dispersed from the reintroduction sites toward the [[Coachella Canal]] and [[East Highline Canal]], producing eight post-reintroduction occurrences.

**4. Distinguishing reintroduced from wild fish**
- The authors recommend marking hatchery fish with oxytetracycline (OTC) so that stocked and naturally produced fish can be discriminated. Without such marks, the relative contributions of reintroduction and natural recruitment cannot be assessed.

**5. Management recommendations and conclusions**
- Canal maintenance schedules should account for the risk to fish, and dewatering should be timed to limit mortality.
- Predator populations in drains and reservoirs should be considered when planning stocking, since nonnative predators can strongly reduce survival of young fish.
- Continued monitoring and marking of stocked fish are needed to evaluate whether self-sustaining populations are developing.
