# Arizona Nongame Strategic Plans 1987-1991

#ichthyology #marine-biology #nongame-wildlife-planning

## Executive Summary

This March 1988 document is the [[Arizona Nongame Strategic Plan]] (1987-1991), prepared under Federal Aid Project FW-11-R by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD). It sets policy and five-year goals for the Department's [[Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program]], which is run through the [[Nongame Branch]]. Although the plan is broad, its coverage includes a sub-section on [[Native Fish]] that treats the 35 native fish taxa of Arizona, along with sections on amphibians and reptiles, raptors, nongame birds, and nongame mammals.

The plan states that Arizona's native wildlife is threatened mainly by habitat destruction and alteration, and secondarily by exotic species, overuse, and a growing human population. It also documents rapid growth in nonconsumptive wildlife use, a funding structure that depends largely on hunting and fishing license sales, and a Department whose staff and data resources cannot keep pace with rising demand for environmental review and planning.

## Background & Description

**Mission and authority.** AGFD protects Arizona's wildlife and provides fish- and wildlife-oriented public benefits. It is also responsible for watercraft safety and regulation. Authority comes from [[Arizona Revised Statutes]] Title 17, including ARS-17-102 (wildlife is State property), ARS-17-201 (the Commission controls the Department), and ARS-17-231 (the Commission establishes broad policies and long-range programs).

**Planning process.** Planning began with a questionnaire to Wildlife Managers, each responsible for one of 46 Management Units. Sub-teams drafted goals and objectives for each taxon group. The draft then went through Department-wide review, agency and public review, Director's Staff review, Commission review at a public meeting, and publication. Strategic plans are reviewed every 5 years and are described as "working guides."

**Definitions.** "Nongame" is a legal and practical term, not a biological one. Under ARS 17-101, nongame fish are all fish except game fish, and aquatic wildlife includes fish, amphibians, mollusks, crustaceans, and soft-shelled turtles. The plan notes that many legally nongame species are hunted, trapped, or fished, and that some endangered species are legally game. The plan also distinguishes consumptive from nonconsumptive use, and it discusses the [[Watchable Wildlife]] program and the [[Nongame Checkoff]] as sources of public confusion about how nongame funds are spent.

**Program history.**
- 1967: AGFD created the first state agency nongame position in the nation, a Nongame Biologist in the Game Branch.
- 1974: A second nongame position was created in the Fisheries Branch for nongame fish, reptiles, and amphibians.
- 1978-1979: The State contracted [[The Nature Conservancy]] to develop a pilot program, which became the [[Arizona Natural Heritage Program]] (initiated 1979, run for 4 years).
- 1982: A voluntary wildlife contribution checkoff on State income tax forms was enacted and implemented for the 1982 tax year.
- July 1983: The Heritage methodology was assimilated into AGFD, forming the Nongame Branch within the Wildlife Management Division.

**Wildlife diversity.** The plan states that more than 800 vertebrate species occur, or occurred within the past 100 years, in Arizona. The document also gives an alternate breakdown of native vertebrates: 31 species (35 forms) of fishes, 24 amphibians, 96 reptiles, 510 birds, and 138 mammals. It notes more than 4,000 native plant kinds and approximately 300 major plant communities or habitat types.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native fishes.** The plan covers 35 native freshwater fish taxa. Historically, indigenous peoples used several species. Overgrazing in the 1890s and arroyo incision began to alter aquatic habitats. Key points:
- Only one extinction is recorded in modern times: the [[Monkey Springs Pupfish]] (1971), lost to habitat destruction and the stocking of exotic sportfish.
- Five other species were extirpated from Arizona waters in the decade after 1967, but all five still exist elsewhere in the wild or in captivity.
- At least 70 exotic fish species have been introduced to the State, and more than 50 are now established. Many compete with or prey on native species.
- Arizona's native fish resource is "greatly depleted below historic levels."
- Seventeen of the 35 taxa are federally listed as endangered or threatened under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The State's [[Threatened Native Wildlife List]] (1982) includes 26 native fish taxa.
- Streams with wholly native fish communities are rare. Only one stream still supports 7 native species without exotic fishes, which is [[Aravaipa Creek]].
- Reintroduction and refugia work involves [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] in New Mexico and AGFD hatcheries.

**Amphibians and reptiles.** The herpetofauna comprises 120 native species: 1 salamander, 23 toads and frogs, 4 turtles (including 1 tortoise), 49 snakes, and 43 lizards. The plan identifies these threats:
- Habitat loss and degradation from urbanization, agriculture, mining, grazing, and recreation are described as having "far greater impact" than collection or consumptive use.
- Illegal commercial trade in reptiles is widespread. Some species sell for up to several hundred dollars per animal on the black market.
- Rattlesnake hunting has increased through the 1970s and 1980s, driven by demand for articles made from rattlesnake parts.
- Two amphibian species are thought to have been lost from Arizona: the [[Tarahumara Frog]] (extirpated in the early 1980s) and the relict leopard frog, which may now be extinct.
- The [[Leopard Frog Complex]] is of particular concern. Scientists recently determined there are at least four distinct species in the State, most of which appear to be in some jeopardy.
- The [[Desert Tortoise]] was petitioned for endangered listing in September 1985. AGFD found the Arizona population did not warrant listing, and the Commission ratified that position in April 1986. The Fish and Wildlife Service later found the petition warranted but precluded by a listing backlog.

**Raptors.** Thirty-four raptor species are year-round residents or breed in Arizona. Of 42 species known to have occurred, 3 are federally listed and 9 are on the State's Threatened Native Wildlife list. Four species received the most management attention: [[Bald Eagle]], [[Northern Goshawk]], [[Peregrine Falcon]], and [[Spotted Owl]]. Key points:
- The plan says only the bald eagle and the gray hawk have well-known status.
- Two species are extirpated: the [[Aplomado Falcon]] and the [[California Condor]].
- Adult bald eagles were shot in November 1986 and June 1987.
- The [[Adobe Mountain Wildlife Center]] receives more than 150 shot raptors each year.
- Falconry is permitted for only 8 of the 41 regularly occurring raptors. About 70 licensed falconers are in the State.
- The Navajo Nation requested permits for ceremonial use of up to 18 golden eagles in 1986.

**Nongame birds.** Arizona has about 450 nongame bird species, and the plan notes that little baseline information exists for most of them.
- About one-third of breeding bird species also occur widely in the western United States and Mexico. More than a quarter have close affinities with Mexican and Central American avifauna. About 8% are "southwest endemics."
- Lowland riparian forest and woodland species make up more than 30% of the 26 non-raptorial birds on the Threatened Native Wildlife list. Cienega-dependent species comprise an additional 12%.
- The [[Yuma Clapper Rail]] will probably be extirpated outside the Lower Colorado River by the turn of the century.
- Human-associated species (e.g., rock dove, house sparrow, European starling) are among the few expected to increase.

**Nongame mammals.** Arizona has 134 native mammal species in the plan's management history, and 138 in the vertebrate count. Key points:
- Eight species or subspecies are federally listed: grizzly bear, black-footed ferret, Mexican wolf, Sonoran pronghorn, jaguar, ocelot, Mount Graham red squirrel, and Hualapai Mexican vole.
- Five of these (grizzly bear, black-footed ferret, Mexican wolf, ocelot, and jaguar) are extirpated from Arizona but not yet believed extinct.
- The last confirmed grizzly bear was killed on September 13, 1935, in Strayhorse Canyon.
- The black-footed ferret was last documented in the wild in Arizona in 1929.
- Thirty-one species or subspecies are Category 2 candidates in the 1985 Federal Notice of Review.
- At least 58 nongame mammals provide recreational opportunities or are considered pests.

**Human use and economics.**
- The 1980 National Survey estimated 253,740 nonconsumptive users (age 16+) in Arizona. Primary nonresidential users spent an average of $139.33 per year, implying a total of slightly more than $35 million.
- About 1,286,700 secondary nonresidential nonconsumptive users were estimated, and 58% of nonconsumptive users said wildlife was an important reason for choosing a recreation location.
- National Audubon Society membership in Arizona increased 307% from 1972 to 1983. Sierra Club membership increased 21% from 1982 to 1985. The Arizona Nature Conservancy had more than 7,000 members in 1987.
- Some popular native fish areas, such as [[Grand Canyon National Park]] and Aravaipa Creek, are restricted to foot entry by permit only.

**Habitat protection.** About 250,000 acres from the Heritage habitat protection agenda have been set aside for conservation. The [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] was the first federal refuge established to protect native fish. Critical habitat has been designated for the desert pupfish, Little Colorado spinedace, and Sonora chub, and proposed for the loach minnow, spikedace, Virgin River roundtail chub, and woundfin.

## Key Findings

**Program structure and capacity**
- The [[Nongame Data Management System]] (NGDMS) tracks approximately 200 vertebrate species and more than 400 plant species, with more than 6,000 occurrence records. Every project proposal is evaluated against it.
- The Nongame Branch included a supervisor, secretary, and six biologists at the time of writing. More than 60 field surveys, monitoring efforts, reintroductions, and studies were underway.
- More than 500 significant project proposals are reviewed each year for impacts on nongame and endangered wildlife.
- Annual increases of as much as 110% in land-use proposals received have occurred since 1979.
- The plan identifies a central funding dilemma: staff time devoted to user-oriented activities comes at the expense of resource-oriented work, and the program depends on users who may not be paying for it.

**Native fish**
- Historically, [[Monkey Springs Pupfish]] extinction occurred in 1971.
- Surveys remain incomplete. 37 of the 63 perennial Little Colorado River streams have never been sampled, and 32 of 58 perennial Gila River basin streams above the Salt River confluence are unsurveyed.
- The plan's native fish goals include completing a statewide stream inventory, establishing a long-term monitoring plan for all 35 taxa, and establishing native fish refugia at the Hassayampa River (Palm Lake), Sonoita Grasslands (Bog Hole), and three other sites.
- Future prospects are judged "bleak" for any native fish except possibly the Apache trout, Gila trout, desert pupfish, and Gila topminnow.

**Amphibians and reptiles**
- Objectives through 1992 include determining the status of all native ranid frogs, the Sonoran green toad, desert tortoise, snapping turtle, and several garter snakes; determining the causes of Tarahumara frog declines; evaluating the impact of [[Bullfrogs]] on garter snakes and native ranid frogs; and evaluating commercialization.
- The plan's regulatory history records changes in bullfrog seasons and reptile bag limits from 1942 through 1988. Bullfrog seasons were changed to unlimited year-round take in 1988, except in La Paz, Mohave, and Yuma counties.

**Raptors**
- Five planning objectives through 1992 include a statewide conservation plan, published population information for State-listed raptors, habitat recommendations, statewide monitoring for eight priority species, and post-release tracking of rehabilitated birds from the Adobe Mountain Wildlife Center.
- The plan identifies persecution, pesticide contamination, access, and mortality from powerlines and highways as key future threats.
- The plan calls for recovery of the aplomado falcon and California condor, noting captive breeding programs may provide releasable birds.

**Nongame birds**
- Objectives include status investigations for all non-raptorial Threatened Native Wildlife birds, habitat criteria for those species, monitoring of significant colonial waterbird rookeries, evaluation of a statewide breeding bird atlas, and a public awareness program.
- The plan identifies birdwatching and nature photography as potentially disruptive to sensitive breeding birds, especially ardeids and raptors.

**Nongame mammals**
- Goals and objectives through 1992 include refining legal definitions of mammals, completing recovery or conservation plans for federally listed and Group 1 or 2 mammals, evaluating reintroduction of extirpated mammals, publishing status reports for Group 3 and 4 species, and identifying regional patterns of endangerment.
- The plan concludes that the future supply of most nongame mammals is expected to decrease whether or not demand increases.

**Problems common to all nongame wildlife (ranked by 1985 survey)**
1. Habitat manipulation
2. Habitat ownership and retention
3. Management data quality
4. Law enforcement
5. Other agency interests
6. Population management
7. Information and education
8. Access
9. Public interest
10. Legal problems
11. Competition and predation from domestic and exotic animals

**Document notes.** The plan's vertebrate counts are internally inconsistent. The 120 amphibian and reptile species total matches the component breakdown, but the overall figures (31 fishes, 24 amphibians, 96 reptiles, 510 birds, 138 mammals) differ from other sections. Raptor totals also vary between 41, 42, and 43 species across different tables and sections. These discrepancies appear in the source and are reported as given.
