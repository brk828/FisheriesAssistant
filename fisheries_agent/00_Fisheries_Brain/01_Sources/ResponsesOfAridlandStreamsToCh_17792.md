# Responses of Arid-Land Streams to Changing Climate

#freshwater-ecology #limnology #arid-land-streams

## Executive Summary

Grimm and Fisher argue that streams of arid lands are sensitive indicators of climate change and a preview of how wetter North American streams may respond as aridity increases. Using long-term work on [[Sycamore Creek]], an intermittent stream in the northern [[Sonoran Desert]] of central Arizona, they show that small changes in precipitation amount, variability, and seasonality produce large changes in runoff. Flash floods and drying are the dominant disturbances, and they control productivity, nitrogen cycling, and the exchange of organic matter between streams and surrounding desert. The chapter closes with research priorities: higher-resolution climate models, long-term studies that account for spatial scale, and comparative studies that use spatial gradients to stand in for future temporal change.

## Background & Description

**Climate context.** The authors note that a global temperature rise of 1-2°C by 2050 is described as a near certainty, and that general circulation models (GCMs) project northern hemisphere warming with less midlatitude rainfall. GCMs fit regional scales poorly, so the authors argue that regional predictions should combine empirical studies, local models, and comparative ecosystem studies.

**Arid lands.** Arid and semiarid lands cover about one-third of Earth's land surface and house about one-fifth of the world's population. Drylands make up nearly half the land area of the continental United States. Desertification expands arid areas and can be intensified by [[Global Warming]].

**Three reasons to study desert streams.** The authors argue that desert streams:
1. Preview how wetter North American streams may change as dry conditions increase.
2. Respond strongly to slight changes in precipitation, so their response is amplified relative to terrestrial ecosystems.
3. Have communities strongly shaped by severe disturbance (flash floods and drying), so understanding current disturbance responses may help predict responses to new extremes.

**Hydrology.** Desert spates are "flashy," with abrupt rises and falls in discharge driven by intense rainfall running off as overland flow. Channels are wide relative to the wetted stream, and riparian vegetation is confined to flood margins, so it shades the stream only minimally. Drying proceeds through three stages: boundary shrinkage, loss of surface water (the dry phase), and rewetting. During the dry phase, flow may persist as [[Hyporheic Zone]] flow, but surface-restricted organisms such as fish experience high mortality.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Recovery from disturbance.** Summer cloudburst spates last an hour or two but cause substantial mortality. Recolonization by algae and macroinvertebrates begins within hours of recession, and predisturbance biomass and productivity return within a few weeks to 1-2 months. Recovery is attributed to high temperatures (10-20°C in winter, 20-30°C in summer), high solar insolation (1000-2000 µE/cm² at midday), and rapidly recolonizing species. Time since the last flash flood was the best predictor of algal biomass (multiple r² = 0.70, n = 352).

**Nitrogen limitation.** Nitrogen limits primary productivity and postflood algal growth, and indirectly limits invertebrate biomass through microbial conditioning of detritus. Nitrogen effects are overridden at annual scales by hydrologic disturbance. Between floods, low dissolved nitrogen results from biotic uptake, so nitrogen-limited dynamics are largely under biotic control.

**Shifting controls.** The authors propose a conceptual model in which control moves over time among disturbance, biotic interactions, and morphometric constraints (system shrinkage). Frequent flooding keeps a stream under disturbance control, while more stable flow lets biotic control develop. Streams subject to drying experience disturbance and biotic controls in sequence.

**Desert matrix linkages.** Sycamore Creek is a "green island" in a "barren sea." Its primary productivity is near 8 g m⁻² d⁻¹, about 50 times the surrounding Sonoran Desert (0.15 g m⁻² d⁻¹). Because of this contrast, organic matter moves from stream to land, the reverse of the forest-to-stream exchange typical of mesic landscapes. Between floods, nitrogen moves outward through riparian uptake, insect emergence, algal stranding, and denitrification. The only significant interflood nitrogen input is fixation by cyanobacteria and symbiotic diatoms.

**Scale.** The authors use a hierarchical framework, from single particles and organisms (scale 1) to whole drainage networks (scale 7), and note that conditions within a stream can vary more than between streams. Stream-to-stream comparisons must therefore be made at matching spatial scales.

## Key Findings

- **Temperature trend:** A rise of 1-2°C by 2050 is treated as near certain, and regional GCM predictions remain uncertain.
- **Hydrologic amplification:** At Sycamore Creek, from 1961 to 1988 precipitation varied by a factor of 6 while runoff varied by a factor of 225. The annual runoff ratio ranged from 0 to 29% (mean 8%, n = 28).
- **Runoff extremes:** Thirteen years had runoff below 5% of precipitation, and runoff exceeded 20% in only 4 years. Wet years produced major floods that reshaped channels; dry years produced earlier intermittency and riparian invasion of the channel.
- **Seasonal control:** Nearly all runoff occurs in winter. Winter precipitation is highly correlated with annual runoff (r = 0.84, p < 0.0001). Summer, by contrast, hosts the highest biological activity and the most severe monsoon flash floods.
- **Productivity contrast:** The terrestrial-to-aquatic primary productivity ratio is about 600 for Bear Brook, New Hampshire (mesic forest), 3.3 for Fort River, Massachusetts, and 0.02 for Sycamore Creek. Sycamore's aquatic productivity is 8 g m⁻² d⁻¹.
- **Geomorphic change:** Severe summer floods of 1984-1985 cut sand volume in a 12-km reach from 60,900 to 42,100 m³. Smaller floods from 1985 to 1987 restored it to about 55,000 m³. The large losses had a recurrence interval of about 10 years.
- **Floodwater nitrogen:** Mean floodwater dissolved inorganic nitrogen across desert streams was 0.44 mg/L (n = 34). Sycamore Creek ranged from 0.20 to 3.22 mg/L (n = 16). Floodwater nitrogen was inversely correlated with the number of floods in the previous 3 years (r = 0.62, n = 16).
- **Mechanism proposed:** Nitrate accumulates on desert soils during drought and is depleted by repeated rainfall-runoff events. This depletion may explain the decline in floodwater nitrogen from 1978 to 1983.
- **Historical precedent:** Arroyo cutting in southern Arizona after about 1880 converted cienegas into incised channels. Slight changes in the frequency of large rains are cited as a likely trigger, alongside cattle grazing.
- **Sensitivity model:** Runoff changes are strongly amplified in arid basins where less than 10% of rainfall becomes runoff. Changes in seasonality (for example, all 58 cm of annual rain falling in summer) would likely increase intermittency and spate severity.
- **Space-for-time substitution:** Gradients of flash flood magnitude (the [[Flash Flood Magnitude Index]], with hot spots above 0.7 in the Southwest and Texas) and of precipitation seasonality across the North American deserts can serve as proxies for future conditions. The authors suggest Arizona streams may resemble New Mexico streams today in about 30 years.
- **Research priorities:**
  1. Higher-resolution climate models for regional predictions.
  2. Long-term, spatially explicit studies that separate climate effects from other stressors.
  3. Long-term sites isolated from anthropogenic disturbance such as grazing, water diversion, mining, and exotic species.
  4. Comparative ecosystem studies, which the authors describe as still in their infancy.
- **Ecosystem value:** Desert streams support endangered fish, riparian wildlife, recreation, and water supply. They are also used as waste receivers and provide the main link between surface water and groundwater, making them diagnostic of environmental health.
