## Executive Summary

This document is a 1980 U.S. Bureau of Reclamation (Water and Power Resources Service) memorandum and associated contract Scope of Work requesting Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) participation and partial funding for a 2.5-year (30-month) study of the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] and the (soon-to-be-listed) [[Razorback Sucker]] in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Black Canyon]]. The study, budgeted at approximately $240,000, was commissioned to determine whether proposed hydroelectric operational changes and construction projects at [[Hoover Dam]] and vicinity would jeopardize the continued existence of these two fish species, as mandated under the Endangered Species Act. The study also includes a substantial component (15% of effort) dedicated to characterizing the [[Periphyton]] community (particularly *Cladophora*) in Black Canyon, which supports the aquatic food web relevant to these fish.

## Background & Description

The source document is administrative/programmatic in nature and does not provide direct morphological descriptions of the species. However, it references key biological data collection methods intended to characterize the populations, including:

- **External tagging**: Use of "easily recognizable color coded external fish tags" for population tracking — implies external tagging is feasible on both species without significant morphological obstruction.
- **Telemetry compatibility**: Both species are noted as suitable candidates for internal/external radio or ultrasonic telemetry tag attachment, implying sufficient body size to carry tracking devices.
- **Species distinction**: Bonytail Chub and Razorback Sucker are treated as morphologically and taxonomically distinct target species, with the [[Razorback Sucker]] noted as "common" in Black Canyon below Hoover Dam relative to the far rarer Bonytail.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Both species inhabit the [[Lake Mohave]] reservoir system and the riverine [[Black Canyon]] reach immediately below [[Hoover Dam]]. The Bonytail Chub is noted as existing **only** in Lake Mohave among extant wild populations at the time (1980).
- **Trophic base**: The study explicitly links fish population health to the underlying [[Periphyton]] community, especially the filamentous green alga *Cladophora*, which supports associated invertebrate species forming part of the forage base.
- **Spawning ecology**: The scope of work targets investigation of:
  - Spawning site locations and habitat criteria (redds)
  - Spawning behavior and seasonal timing
  - Egg viability and incubation conditions
  - Post-larval survival conditions
  - Egg/fry predation pressure from other fish species
- **Movement ecology**: Planned telemetry studies (6 Bonytail, 14 Razorback Sucker minimum) aimed to assess:
  - Effects of Hoover Dam discharge operations on fish movement in Black Canyon/Lake Mohave
  - Preferred habitat areas
  - Intermixing between canyon and lake subpopulations
  - Seasonal or daily migration patterns
- **Competition**: Study explicitly seeks to establish habitat and forage competition between the target species and other fish species in the system (e.g., black bass, under a concurrent Nevada Department of Wildlife study).
- **Anthropogenic pressures**: Species distribution and reproduction are threatened by proposed hydrological modifications:
  1. Uprating of Hoover Powerplant generators (discharge increase from 40,000 ft³/s to 49,000 ft³/s)
  2. Addition of new generating units (discharge increase to 62,000 ft³/s)
  3. Rifle Range Pumped-Storage Project (causing reverse flows up to 5,000 ft³/s in Black Canyon)
  4. Reregulation of Lake Mohave (potential lake level reduction from 630–647 ft down to as low as 570–600 ft elevation)

## Key Findings

- The [[Bonytail Chub]] was federally listed as **endangered** on May 13, 1980, with Lake Mohave representing its sole known extant population at the time.
- The [[Razorback Sucker]], while "common" in Black Canyon below Hoover Dam, was anticipated to be listed as endangered within a few years of this document (1980).
- An ongoing FWS captive breeding effort was underway at the **Willow Beach Fish Hatchery**, Arizona, using wild-captured Bonytail Chub as broodstock.
- The study design allocates effort as: Razorback Sucker study (35%), Bonytail Chub study (35%), Periphyton study (15%), and Analysis of Operational Impacts (15%).
- Minimum sampling targets specified: at least **100 Razorback Suckers** and "all available" Bonytail Chubs to receive external color-coded tags; a subset (6 Bonytail, 14 Razorback) to receive telemetry devices tracked monthly over 24-hour periods across two 13-month sessions.
- Key unresolved ecological questions framed by the study include whether **natural recruitment** is occurring in either species' population, whether viable spawning is occurring for both sexes of both species, and whether current dam operations expose spawning redds to desiccation during incubation.
- Proposed mitigation questions raised include feasibility of habitat improvement for reproduction, modified dam operation schedules to improve spawning success, periodic stocking to maintain adult population levels, and predation control measures.
- The study explicitly connects reservoir/river hydrological engineering decisions (Hoover Powerplant uprating, new generating units, Rifle Range pumped-storage, and Lake Mohave reregulation) to direct and indirect risks for both the target fish species and the supporting [[Periphyton]] community structure in [[Black Canyon]].
- Funding was structured as a joint Bureau of Reclamation/FWS partnership ($210,000 / $30,000 respectively) across FY1981–FY1983, contingent on a formal FWS letter of commitment.
