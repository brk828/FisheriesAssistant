# Spawning Runs from Pyramid Lake in 1986 and Studies of Cui-ui Life History

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service report (Great Basin Complex, Reno, Nevada; September 1987) documents the 1986 spawning runs of [[Cui-ui]], [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Tahoe Sucker]] through the [[Marble Bluff Fish Facility]] on the Truckee River, and summarizes multi-year studies of cui-ui fecundity, age structure, sex ratio, adult movement, egg and larval temperature tolerance, and larval emigration. The 1986 runs were the largest recorded at the facility, attributed to improved access across the Truckee River delta and high spring inflows after Pyramid Lake rose to its highest elevation in about 40 years. Findings bear directly on the endangered cui-ui recovery strategy, particularly the management of river flows and temperatures during spawning and incubation.

## Background & Description

The report covers three fishes of Pyramid Lake:

- **Cui-ui** (*Chasmistes cujus*): an endangered catostomid. Adults in the 1986 run ranged from 334 to 663 mm FL.
- **Lahontan cutthroat trout** (*Salmo clarki henshawi*): mature fish in the 1986 run ranged from 285 to 795 mm FL, with ages estimated at 2 to 8 years.
- **Tahoe sucker** (*Catostomus tahoensis*): run size was roughly estimated rather than counted.

The study site was the [[Marble Bluff Fish Facility]], a river trap and fishway at Marble Bluff Dam that has operated since 1976. Companion work includes the [[Pyramid Lake Fishway]], the [[Captain David Numana Fish Hatchery]] (built 1981 by the Pyramid Lake Paiute Tribe) and the [[David Koch Cui-ui Hatchery]]. Earlier work is cited from Coleman (1986), Scoppettone et al. (1983, 1986) and Koch (1981, 1982).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Run timing and magnitude were tied to lake elevation, river access and inflow. Pyramid Lake had risen about 28 feet since 1981, inundating the shallow braided delta and moving the lake–river interface closer to Marble Bluff Dam. Spring inflows reached 5,000 cfs in March and about 2,000 cfs in May 1986. Cui-ui movement tracked river temperature: catches rose during warming trends and fell during cold, stormy weather, with cui-ui most active in late afternoon and early evening.

Temperature controls reproduction. Water released from [[Stampede Reservoir]] can regulate lower Truckee River temperatures, so knowing egg and larval thermal limits helps set releases. River temperatures can fluctuate up to 6°C within a day in spring and early summer. Larval emigration occurred mainly at night, and drift was not significantly influenced by moon phase or turbidity.

## Key Findings

**Spawning runs (1986)**
- Cui-ui: 35,080 adults passed upstream, the largest run in the facility's history (next largest 13,804 in 1982). Peak run was about April 10–11.
- Lahontan cutthroat trout: 1,397 adjusted (2,084 counted, with 33 percent re-entering after dropping over the dam). This was nearly twice the 1985 run and the largest in 10 years.
- Tahoe suckers: about 55,000 estimated, the largest since the facility opened. Peaks occurred April 11 and May 12–17.
- Runs began 2 to 4 weeks earlier than in previous years, at water temperatures 2–4°C cooler.

**Cutthroat trout**
- Male to female ratio was 1.0:1.9; mean fork length was 575 mm for males and 532 mm for females.
- Stocking history: 49 percent of fish were tagged, with the Captain David Numana Fish Hatchery group making up 63 percent of recaptures.
- The share of ripe females fell from 55 to 14 percent over four years, so fewer eggs were collected (416,000, 475,000 and 201,000 in 1984–1986).

**Cui-ui age, sex and fecundity**
- Of 456 adults aged from 1986 mortalities, 12 year classes (1950–1980) were found. The 1969 class made up 73.3 percent, followed by the 1976 class (11.1 percent) and the 1974 class (4.4 percent).
- Sex ratio shifted to 1.0:1.3 in 1986 from 1.0:1.9 in 1985, attributed to recruitment of young fish with a near-equal sex ratio.
- Fecundity was 21,561 eggs for a 431 mm (7-year-old) female and 192,548 eggs for a 657 mm (32-year-old) female, from 113 females sampled 1982–1986. Mean yearly fecundity for the 1969 class ranged from 81,000 to 94,000 eggs.

**Adult movement and spawning frequency**
- Of 122 radio-tagged adults (65 females, 57 males), most migrated only about 5 km upstream of Marble Bluff Dam. Males stayed in the river an average of 10.2 days and females 8.1 days; the 1986 average was 13.0 days.
- Tagged fish released after the run peak dropped back to the lake more often (55 percent) than those tagged earlier (11 percent).
- About 4,000 adults were marked with external anchor tags. Recapture rates for males rose from 22.3 percent (1983 release) to 42.3 percent (1986), and females from 12.2 to 23.9 percent. The authors could not fully explain this trend.
- Most cui-ui entered the run only occasionally. Males entered more often than females, and the authors judge that river access and inflow strongly influence spawning frequency.
- Prespawning aggregate estimates ranged from 90,000 to 200,000 adults; the authors describe these as "ballpark" figures.

**Egg and larval temperature tolerance**
- Egg-to-swim-up survival was highest (about 73.4 percent) at 9.4–15.5°C and lowest (13.7 percent) at 17.8–23.9°C.
- Median hatch took 4 days at the warmest treatment and 14 days at the coolest.
- 24-hour TL50 was 29.7°C for newly hatched larvae and 30.0°C for swim-up larvae.
- Survival declined sharply above about 14.2°C mean daily temperature, consistent with Koch (1981).

**Larval production and survival**
- Estimated cui-ui larval production was about 43 million in 1985 and 225 million in 1986. Tahoe sucker production was about 16 million in 1985 and 167 million in 1986.
- Egg-to-drift survival was about 10 percent (1985) and 16 percent (1986) for cui-ui, and 11 and 35 percent for Tahoe suckers.
- Larval drift lasted 33 to 39 days. Most larvae left the gravel near dusk.
- Estimates carry substantial uncertainty from net clogging, sorting losses (estimated 5–10 percent) and flow-meter bias.

**Conclusions and management recommendations**
- Early runs at cooler temperatures improved embryo survival and reduced water needed from Stampede Reservoir.
- Cui-ui can spawn yearly, but most adults migrate infrequently. Low year-class diversity is a serious concern: if new year classes (1971–1980 were seen in small numbers in 1986) do not replace the 1969 class, the population will decline.
- Recommended actions include maximizing attraction and instream flows for cui-ui, keeping river flow during incubation at or below about 17°C, using Stampede storage sparingly, and dredging a passage channel across the delta.
