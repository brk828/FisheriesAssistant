# Wildlife status in Arizona (fishes)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae #conservation-status #arizona-fish-fauna

## Executive Summary

This 1973 status report by W. L. Minckley catalogs the native freshwater [[Ichthyofauna of Arizona]], summarizing historic range and conservation status for **32 species/subspecies** across **9 families**. The overarching finding is a state-wide pattern of severe range contraction: "All fish species in the state have suffered vast reductions in their former ranges." Status codes (E, EE, ER, U, P, Un) reveal that the majority of taxa are already **Endangered and Rare (ER)** or **Endangered and possibly Extinct (EE)**, with many classified as **Unique (Un)** — reflecting high endemism tied to isolated desert drainage systems including the [[Colorado River]], [[Little Colorado River]], [[Gila River]], and the Mexican [[Rio Yaqui]] and [[Rio Sonoyta]] drainages.

## Background & Description

The report is a status survey rather than a morphological treatise, but it implicitly documents notable **intraspecific diversification** driven by geographic isolation:

- **[[Gila Robusta]] (roundtail chub)** — three recognized subspecies (*grahami*, *seminuda*, and nominal *robusta*) differentiated by drainage: Verde/Salt/Gila tributaries, Colorado River mainstem, and mixed populations, respectively.
- **[[Catostomus]] sucker complex** — multiple undescribed *Catostomus* species morphotypes noted, including at least **two distinct morphotypes** in the Little Colorado River (Morphotype A) versus Grand Canyon populations (Morphotype B), suggesting incipient speciation within the Colorado River drainage.
- **[[Pantosteus]] mountain-suckers** — similarly show unresolved species-level diversity, with distinct undescribed forms in the Bill Williams and Virgin River drainages (the latter possibly represented by two forms).
- **[[Cyprinodon macularius]] (desert pupfish)** — at least two subspecies-level lineages noted: a widespread Gila/Colorado form and the highly restricted **Quitobaquito pupfish**, isolated in a single spring system.
- **[[Lepidomeda]] spinedaces** — two species-level taxa (*vittata*, *mollispinis*) tied respectively to the Little Colorado and Virgin River systems, reflecting parallel adaptive radiation of spiny-finned cyprinids in isolated basins.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Species are organized by their historic drainage-based ranges, reflecting strong habitat-elevation and drainage-basin specificity:

- **[[Colorado River]] mainstem and larger tributaries** — historically hosted large-bodied pelagic and benthic specialists such as the [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) — all now severely restricted following mainstem impoundment (e.g., Lake Mohave).
- **[[Little Colorado River]] system** — supports endemic forms including the [[Little Colorado River Spinedace]] and a distinct undescribed *Catostomus* sucker morphotype.
- **[[Gila River]] basin** (below ~6,500 ft) — the center of diversity for the *Gila* chub complex, [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis occidentalis*), [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), [[Gila Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*), and multiple sucker and catfish taxa; elevation zonation is explicit (e.g., *Meda fulgida* restricted to 1,000–6,000 ft).
- **[[Virgin River]] system** — harbors unique, isolated lineages: *Gila robusta seminuda*, *Lepidomeda mollispinis mollispinis*, [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*), and an undescribed Virgin River mountain-sucker (*Pantosteus* sp.).
- **[[Rio Yaqui]] and [[Rio Sonoyta]] drainages (Mexico border, SE Cochise Co.)** — a biogeographically distinct assemblage including *Gila purpurea* (Yaqui chub), *Notropis formosus mearnsi* (Yaqui shiner), *Campostoma ornatum pricei* (Mexican stoneroller), *Catostomus bernardini* (Yaqui sucker), *Ictalurus pricei* (Yaqui channel catfish), and *Poeciliopsis occidentalis sonoriensis* (Yaqui topminnow) — largely peripheral (P) populations at the edge of their Mexican range.
- **Isolated spring systems** — extreme endemism exemplified by the **Quitobaquito pupfish** (single spring, Rio Sonoyta drainage) and the **Monkey Spring pupfish** (*Cyprinodon* sp., Santa Cruz Co.), both classified Endangered/Unique.
- **Estuarine/marine-influenced peripherals** — *Elops affinis* (machete), *Mugil cephalus* (striped mullet), and *Eleotris picta* (spotted sleeper) occur in the lower Colorado River mainstem as peripheral (P) species originating from the Gulf of California, representing the only taxa in the list with marine connectivity.

## Key Findings

- **Widespread range contraction**: Every native fish species in Arizona had experienced substantial reduction from historic ranges by 1973, regardless of status category.
- **High endemism ("Un") status**: The majority of native cyprinids, catostomids, and cyprinodontids are flagged as biologically unique, reflecting Arizona's role as a center of desert fish diversification.
- **Extinction-level threats already realized**: The [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*) and *Cyprinodon* Monkey Spring pupfish were listed EE/E, indicating near or complete extirpation from Arizona even at time of writing.
- **Taxonomic uncertainty as a conservation red flag**: Multiple "species" designations remain informal (e.g., undescribed *Catostomus* and *Pantosteus* morphotypes), underscoring that cryptic diversity may have gone extinct before formal description.
- **Peripheral marine strays**: A small set of euryhaline/marine species (*Elops affinis*, *Mugil cephalus*, *Eleotris picta*) use the lower Colorado River as a peripheral extension of Gulf of California habitat, distinguishing them ecologically from the endemic desert fish fauna.
- **Drainage isolation drives subspeciation**: The *Gila robusta* and *Pantosteus* complexes exemplify how fragmentation among the Colorado, Gila, Virgin, and Bill Williams systems produced multiple distinct, geographically restricted lineages — most already critically imperiled by the time of this 1973 assessment.
- **Elevation zonation**: Several species show clear elevational range limits (e.g., *Meda fulgida* and *Tiaroga cobitis* restricted to 1,000–6,000 ft in the Gila basin), highlighting elevation-mediated habitat partitioning within single drainages.
