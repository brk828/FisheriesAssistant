# Investigation of Crayfish Control Technology

#ichthyology #marine-biology #crayfish

## Executive Summary

This final report, prepared for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] under a cooperative agreement funded by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], reviews methods for controlling or eradicating nonindigenous crayfish in freshwater systems. North America hosts 390 native crayfish species (75% of the world total), yet Arizona and the Colorado River basin have no native crayfish. Introduced species such as [[Signal Crayfish]], [[Red Swamp Crayfish]], [[Northern Crayfish]] and [[Rusty Crayfish]] have spread widely and harm native fishes, amphibians and invertebrates. The estimated economic cost of a small subset of introduced freshwater species in the United States is $4.1 billion annually.

The review classifies control methods into five categories (legislative, mechanical, biological, physical, chemical) and evaluates each for effectiveness, cost and environmental risk. The authors conclude that no single method is reliably sufficient, that manual removal and trapping cannot eradicate populations, and that biocides, particularly [[Pyrethrum]]-based treatments combined with deoxygenation or high-pH ammonia, offer the most practical eradication option for small, isolated waterbodies.

## Background & Description

Crayfish are often central components of freshwater food webs. Changes in their populations therefore cause broad ecosystem effects, including altered algae, macrophyte, macroinvertebrate, amphibian and fish communities. Introductions occur through several vectors. In North America, the live bait trade is considered the most important vector, followed by aquaculture, aquarium and pond trade, and biological supply trade.

In Arizona, crayfish were first stocked by the Arizona Game and Fish Department and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service about 30 years before the report, for aquatic weed control and as forage for sport fish. Two non-native species now occur in the state: [[Northern Crayfish]] (*Orconectes virilis*) and [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] (*Procambarus clarkii*).

Peay and Hiley (2001) identify three questions that determine whether eradication is feasible: whether a control method can confirm effectiveness, whether it can remove all or enough aliens to prevent spread, and whether it is specific to alien crayfish. The central concept is [[Minimum Viable Population Density]] (MVPD), the density below which a population is expected to go extinct. Holdich et al. (1999) list criteria for any program: environmental safety, good chance of success, low cost, low labor, no human hazard, and public acceptability.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native fauna impacts:** Nonindigenous crayfish have extirpated native crayfish populations and contributed to the global extinction of at least one native species. Studies document declines in native fishes, amphibians and macroinvertebrates.
- **Predator–prey relationships:** Fish predators (including [[Eel]], [[Perch]], [[Pike]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Brook Trout]]) can reduce crayfish populations, but cover is important for juvenile survival, and predation can sometimes increase production.
- **Disease:** [[Crayfish Plague]] (*Aphanomyces astaci*) is usually 100% lethal to susceptible European species, while North American crayfish carry it chronically and are relatively immune.
- **Burrowing and desiccation tolerance:** Crayfish survive long periods out of water when moisture is available. Signal crayfish survived more than 12 weeks in a dried streambed and burrowed extensively into banks in Britain.
- **Overland movement:** In Arizona, [[Northern Crayfish]] were documented moving overland up to 6,060 m between ponds.
- **Non-target effects:** No biocide yet tested is fully specific to crayfish. Most affect other arthropods, and many affect fish.

## Key Findings

**Legislative control**
- Current U.S. federal and state regulations are judged inadequate. Federal law focuses on terrestrial pests and ballast water, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service uses a "black list" approach.
- [[Lodge et al. 2000]] recommended a white list approach, aquaculture and aquarium restrictions, and banning live crayfish as bait.
- Arizona enacted regulations in 2001 prohibiting live crayfish transport within most of the state, but the authors judge this to have come too late for eradication.

**Mechanical control**
- Manual removal cannot achieve eradication at any effort level. In the River Gwash (UK), catches fell from 2,227 to 1,009 crayfish across five efforts, but the decrease could not be attributed to population reduction.
- Trapping catches less than 10% of the active population in intensive studies. In Sweden, intensive trapping reduced *A. astacus* by 50% over three years. In France, regular trapping of *P. clarkii* reduced but did not eliminate the population.
- Small-mesh traps caught a broader size range (19–72 mm CL) than [[Swedish Trappy]] traps (35–76 mm). Refuge traps are still under trial.
- Modified minnow traps (Morgan et al. 2001) reduced fish capture significantly (P<0.0001) without reducing crayfish capture (P=0.34).
- Electrofishing is effective only on a limited portion of a population and is not a viable control method.

**Biological control**
- Fish predators have no realistic chance of eradicating an established population and little chance of reducing it, according to Peay and Hiley (2001).
- [[Crayfish Plague]] could theoretically eliminate susceptible species, but spread risk to native populations is high. No crayfish-specific [[Bacillus Thuringiensis]] strain exists.

**Physical control**
- Draining ponds fails to eliminate crayfish. Kozak and Policar (2003) found signal crayfish survived draining and winter exposure to −20 °C.
- A catchpit barrier at West Tanfield Fishery was installed in 2000, but no results were available at the time of the report.

**Chemical control**
- [[Pyblast]] (3.0% natural pyrethrins) killed signal crayfish at 0.05 mg/L within 4 hours in pond water, and gave 100% mortality within 10 minutes when sprayed directly on crayfish.
- Deoxygenation with sodium sulphite, or ammonia (20–30 mg/L at pH 9.5 or above), gave 100% mortality within 12–24 hours in trials.
- Chlorine at 10–100 mg/L killed signal crayfish within 24 hours, but organic sediment inactivates it.
- [[Baythroid]] was highly selective for crayfish over bluegill (selectivity index 0.02 for crayfish vs. 0.00015 for...), but is not registered for any aquatic use in the United States.
- Ivermectin, diflubenzuron, surfactants and pheromone baited traps showed mixed or limited evidence. Pheromone traps caught 10.25 males per trap versus 0.167 females in sex-pheromone traps.
- [[Rotenone]] is widely used on fish, but crayfish appear more tolerant. Killing *Orconectes rusticus* required 10 mg/L Noxfish (96 h). Rotenone is toxic to fish and amphibians at lower levels.

**Conclusions**
- Each nuisance population must be assessed separately, and multiple approaches are likely needed.
- Trapping is unlikely to eradicate populations, though it may suppress them with sustained effort.
- In Arizona, most waters are connected, so eradication is feasible mainly in small, geographically isolated ponds. The report recommends re-examining the state's current control strategy.
