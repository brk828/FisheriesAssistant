# Trichodina renicola (Mueller, 1931): A Urinary Ciliate Parasite of the Chain Pickerel

#ichthyology #marine-biology #urceolariidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Justus F. Mueller]], a field naturalist at the [[Roosevelt Wild Life Station]], formally describes a peritrichous ciliate that lives inside the urinary system of the [[Chain Pickerel]] (*Esox niger*) in [[Oneida Lake]], New York. The organism was first published as *Cyclochaeta renicola* in 1931. Mueller then reassigned it to the genus [[Trichodina]] on anatomical grounds, specifically the absence of cirri. The species is notable as the first *Trichodina* reported from the urinary tract of a fish. It is also highly host-specific: infection was found only in *Esox niger*, not in the closely related [[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) sharing the same habitat. The paper covers systematics, anatomy, binary fission, host relationships, pathology and transmission. The mode of transmission remains unknown.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic history.** The chain pickerel in Oneida Lake was repeatedly found to carry a peritrichous ciliate in its urinary tract. It was initially placed in *Cyclochaeta* and announced in a 1931 abstract for the Seventh Annual Meeting of the American Society of Parasitologists. The full paper was not presented at that meeting because the author could not attend.

**Systematic position.** *Trichodina*, *Cyclochaeta* and *Urceolaria* are closely related. Mueller followed [[Fulton, 1923]] in placing them in the family Urceolariidae Stein, the third family of the order Peritrichida. He criticized [[Calkins, 1926]] for reducing the group to a subfamily of Vorticellidae and for suppressing *Urceolaria*. He also noted that [[Kudo, 1931]] omitted *Urceolaria* and placed the other two genera in Trichodinidae. Mueller retained *Urceolaria* because of its distinctive skeletal features.

**Morphology.** Key diagnostic features are summarized below:
- **Body size:** normally about 0.080 mm in diameter. The range recorded is 0.070 mm (small specimens) to 0.096 mm (large specimens).
- **Shape:** a compressed, hemispherical body. It is less arched than most *Trichodina* species.
- **Skeletal ring:** a striated ring-band plus a denticulate ring (corona).
- **Corona:** usually 56 denticles, a count the author regards as higher than any other known member of the Urceolariidae.
- **Posterior ciliary girdle:** membranelles of ten to twelve cilia each. Cirri are completely absent, which justified the transfer to *Trichodina*.
- **Macronucleus:** horseshoe-shaped. The micronucleus was not found in many specimens.
- **Velum:** the least specialized part of the sucking disc, but thicker than in most species of the genus.

**Reproduction.** The species reproduces by transverse binary fission. Newly divided individuals have only half the normal number of denticles and a shorter macronucleus. A second denticle ring is expected to form de novo, and the inherited ring is then resorbed. Mueller did not observe these later stages directly in *T. renicola*, but he inferred the process from related species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Host relationships.** Twelve pickerel were examined after the initial discovery on December 26, 1930. Only two lacked the parasite. Infected fish looked externally normal. Infection intensity ranged from sparse populations at the neck of the bladder to heavy infections in which the protozoa were "literally swarming" and the ureters and kidneys were also involved. Heavy infections produced a creamy bladder content.

**Host specificity.** Mueller examined hundreds of fish of about thirty species from Oneida Lake for parasites. *Trichodina* was found only in *Esox niger*. The closely related *Esox lucius*, collected from the same habitat and in the same net as infected pickerel, was not infected. By contrast, *Trichodina urinicola* infects a range of amphibian hosts.

**Distribution within the lake.** Infected pickerel were found at Big Bay and Fish Creek (about twenty miles apart) and in Cleveland Harbor on the north shore. This suggests broad distribution across the lake. No other *Trichodina* species were found in Oneida Lake, and gill examinations for ectoparasites revealed neither *Trichodina* nor *Cyclochaeta*.

**Pathology.** Little is known about *Trichodina* pathology in general. Mueller documented three potential mechanisms of harm:
1. Physical interference with gas exchange, which could cause suffocation in heavy infections.
2. Possible toxic substances released by the parasites.
3. Mechanical damage from the sucking disc. Parasites attached to the bladder wall can form a deep suction cup, producing laceration and desquamation of epithelial cells. Clumps of dislodged cells were found in the ureter lumen.

The glandular kidney tissue appeared normal, and the parasites did not enter the tubules. Mueller concluded that the mechanical injury did not appear severe enough to cause serious consequences, although toxic effects could not be ruled out.

**Transmission.** The mode of transmission is unknown. Mueller proposed a hypothesis in which the parasite briefly lives ectoparasitically and reenters through the cloaca, but he considered the real process probably more complex. Free-living parasites removed from the bladder into lake water swam briefly, swelled, and died within two to three minutes. No ectoparasitic *T. renicola* was found on infected pickerel. Mueller also cited [[Rosseter, 1886]] and Fulton's work on *Trichodina urinicola*, which showed that endoparasitic forms often cannot survive outside the host.

**Setting.** The study was conducted under the [[Roosevelt Wild Life Forest Experiment Station]] of the [[New York State College of Forestry]] at Syracuse. The station was founded under a 1919 state law and authorized to cooperate beyond state boundaries. Its aim was forest wild-life research.

## Key Findings

- ***Cyclochaeta renicola* is reclassified as *Trichodina renicola*.** The reassignment rests on the absence of cirri on the posterior ciliary girdle.
- **Diagnostic features:** a hemispherical body of about 0.080 mm, and a corona with 56 denticles, a higher count than that of any other Urceolariidae known to Mueller.
- **Comparison with congeners:** *T. pediculus* has 24–26 denticles. *T. urinicola* has 28–36 hooks. Diller's *Trichodina* sp. from tadpoles had 15–24 denticles (mode 21) and was only about 0.035 mm in diameter.
- **Host specificity:** the parasite is found only in *Esox niger*. Among hundreds of fish of about thirty species examined, no other host was infected, and *Esox lucius* was not infected.
- **Prevalence:** the parasite infected 10 of the 12 pickerel examined after discovery.
- **Distribution:** it occurs at Big Bay, Fish Creek and Cleveland Harbor in Oneida Lake.
- **Significance:** it is the first *Trichodina* reported from the urinary tract of a fish, extending this habit beyond amphibians.
- **Pathology:** mechanical damage to the urinary epithelium occurs, but Mueller judged serious consequences unlikely. Toxic effects remain uncertain.
- **Transmission:** unknown. Free-living forms die within minutes, and no ectoparasitic stage was found on infected pickerel.
- **Reproduction:** binary fission is confirmed by young specimens with half-normal denticle numbers, but later developmental stages were not observed.
- **Deposit:** a slide of stained specimens was deposited in the U.S. National Museum as cotype material.
