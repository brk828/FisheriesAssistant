# Swim Bladder Filling in Salmonids: First Inflation Without Access to an Air Surface

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1960 study by J. S. Tait, published in the *Canadian Journal of Zoology*, examines how young [[Salmonidae]] fish inflate their [[Swim Bladder]] for the first time. Four species were tested: [[Brown Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Lake Trout]], and [[Lake Whitefish]]. Fish reared without access to an air surface failed to fill their swim bladders for periods of 22 to 84 weeks, yet survived and filled them within hours to days once given access to air. The pneumatic duct remained open throughout, and lake trout fry with unfilled bladders swam vertically up to 900 ft, suggesting that deep-water hatched fish can reach the surface with relative ease.

## Background & Description

The swim bladder of newly hatched fish is typically gas-free. Its initial filling generally coincides with the disappearance of the yolk sac and the onset of free-swimming, food-seeking behavior. Physostomous fish, which have a pneumatic duct connecting the bladder to the gut, must swallow air to fill it. The study addresses four questions:

1. Are these salmonids dependent on swallowing air for initial filling, as shown previously for brown trout by [[Von Ledebur]] (1928)?
2. Can they survive without filling their swim bladders?
3. Does the pneumatic duct close through disuse, as in [[Physoclist]] fish such as [[Three-Spined Stickleback]] and [[Sea Horse]]?
4. Can young fish swim vertically to the surface without bladder buoyancy?

The questions matter most for salmonids that hatch in deep water or in shallow lakes where [[Ice Cover]] persists, because filling may require a long ascent or be delayed beyond ice-out.

Spawning data (Table I) list spawning times and depths for [[Lake Trout]], [[Lake Whitefish]], [[Siscowet]] (*Cristivomer siscowet*), and [[Lake Herring]] relatives across lakes including [[Lake Superior]], [[Lake Michigan]], [[Lake Nipigon]], [[Lake Ontario]], [[Lake Constance]], and [[Seneca Lake]]. Lake trout spawning depths ranged from 1–4 ft in Lake Louisa to 300–480 ft for siscowets in Lake Superior. Brown and rainbow trout were used because they were readily available and spawn in streams.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Rearing methods.** Eggs came from Ontario Provincial Hatcheries, local netted whitefish, and laboratory stock. Fry were reared in three types of screened container that prevented surface access: battery jars (1955), aluminum-screened cages (1955), and 3.5-gallon bottles (1956 onward), the last being the most effective. Dissolved gas was reduced slightly below saturation to prevent bubble formation, with water aerated at 9–14 °C and cooled to 4–9 °C.

**Diet.** Trout received ground beef liver, liver mixed with turkey feed, and enchytraeid worms. Whitefish received [[Artemia]] and later enchytraeids.

**Detection methods.** Filling was assessed by buoyant swimming behavior, dissection, pipette transfer of live fish to a light source (a filled bladder appears as a silvery blue sac), and dental X-rays (DF45 film, 70 kV, 10 mA).

**Temperature.** Mean rearing temperatures in the experiments ranged from about 3 °C to 10 °C. Higher temperatures advance the filling stage.

## Key Findings

**Dependence on air swallowing and survival**

- Control fish filled their swim bladders at the start of feeding: brown trout (3 weeks at 3 °C, according to Table II), rainbow trout (about 5–6 weeks), and lake trout (about 4–5 weeks at 10 °C).
- Whitefish controls filled their bladders at 2–3 months after hatching, at 20–22 mm length, at a mean temperature of 4.5 °C.
- Fish reared without surface access did not fill their bladders for these periods (Table II):
  - Lake trout (1956): 22 weeks
  - Rainbow trout (1955): 64 weeks, with 241 fish examined
  - Brown trout: 84 weeks (133 examined)
  - Whitefish: 56 weeks (37 examined)
- Fish survived without gas in their bladders. High mortality was attributed mainly to difficulties with feeding and cleaning in overcrowded containers, not to the bladder condition itself.

**Pneumatic duct patency**

- The pneumatic ducts remained open in all four species, even after 22 weeks in lake trout and up to 84 weeks in brown trout. Fish released into open tanks then filled their bladders within hours to days.
- X-rays confirmed empty bladders before release and filled bladders afterward. One whitefish had a small, J-shaped bladder with a single bubble.

**Behavior**

- Whitefish swam continuously with their heads up and rarely rested on the bottom.
- Lake trout alternated between swimming and resting and circled the bottle periphery at about 45°.
- Rainbow trout kept themselves suspended with rapid tail thrusts.
- Brown trout remained on the bottom with outstretched pectoral fins and were in the best condition, suggesting that energy spent maintaining position contributed to poor condition in the other species.

**Vertical swimming (lake trout fry, about 6 weeks past normal filling)**

- Six runs covered 105–900 ft, taking 17–129 minutes at average rates of 3.5–7.0 ft/min.
- The 900 ft run lasted 129 minutes at 7.0 ft/min, with a mean temperature of 13 °C.
- The author estimated that fry in the deepest part of Lake Superior (about 1300 ft) could reach the surface in roughly 3 hours at the observed rate.

**Conclusions**

- Initial filling in these salmonids depends on swallowing air.
- Delayed filling does not close the pneumatic duct or eliminate the ability to fill later.
- Ice cover is unlikely to prevent whitefish from filling, because their filling occurs 2–3 months after hatching.
- Lake trout hatched early in the fall may fill their bladders before ice-out, and any delay is unlikely to be serious.
- Deep-water hatched fry can likely reach the surface with relative ease.
