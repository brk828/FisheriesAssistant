## Executive Summary

This document is a 1995 advocacy letter and bibliography from Southwest conservation organizations (Southwest Center for Biological Diversity, Southwest Forest Alliance, Forest Guardians, Sierra Club chapters, and others) addressed to the USDA Forest Service Regional Forester Charles Cartwright. ## Background & Description

Not applicable — no organismal (fish or otherwise) morphological data is present in this source. The text instead discusses stand structure metrics for **[[Ponderosa Pine]]** forests:

- **Rummell (1951) study (central Washington):**
  - Ungrazed (40+ years, no fire for 125 years): 85 trees/acre <4" dbh; described as having an "almost unbelievably lush mat" of pine grass.
  - Grazed forest (same fire history): 3,291 trees/acre <4" dbh (2,033 pine, 1,016 Douglas fir, 242 Western larch).
  - Total trees/acre (all diameter classes, ungrazed "open and parklike" plot): ~134.4/acre — contrasted against the accepted Forest Service presettlement estimate of 15–50 trees/acre.

- **Zion National Park study (1983):** Comparison of Horse Pasture Plateau (grazed until ~1960) vs. Greatheart and Church Mesas (never grazed, isolated by cliffs/slickrock). Both dominated by Ponderosa pine and Gambel oak with similar large-ungulate browsing history; grazed plateau showed dense thickets of Ponderosa, Gambel oak, and Rocky Mountain Juniper saplings absent on ungrazed mesas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The source addresses terrestrial forest/rangeland ecology rather than an aquatic niche:

- **[[Fire Suppression]]** vs. **[[Livestock Grazing]]** as competing/compounding causal factors in forest overstocking.
- Grazing reduces herbaceous understory ([[Pine Grass]]) competition, which the studies argue is the "prime factor" enabling dense tree seedling establishment — more significant than fire exclusion alone.
- The Zion study frames livestock grazing as the critical factor converting **[[Savanna]]** ecosystems to dense forest thickets, independent of fire frequency changes.
- Historical context: Aldo Leopold (1923) noted grass competition and fire were "always paired, never isolated" as inhibitors of pine reproduction, complicating earlier causal attribution.
