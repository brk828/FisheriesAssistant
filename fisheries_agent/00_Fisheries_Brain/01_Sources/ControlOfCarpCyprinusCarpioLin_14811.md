# Control of carp, Cyprinus carpio Linnaeus, a review of the literature with special reference to bait poisoning

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #invasive-species #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This 1957 graduate literature review by J.L. Stegman synthesizes contemporary research on controlling invasive populations of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) in North American freshwater systems. Native to Asia and introduced to Europe circa 1500 A.D. and to the United States in 1872, the carp failed to meet expectations as either a food or sport fish and instead became an ecologically disruptive invasive species. The report reviews physical (netting, seining, electrical), chemical (general and selective piscicides), and bait-poisoning control strategies, concluding that no single method achieved practical, large-scale, cost-effective control as of the mid-1950s. Central to the review is an evaluation of selective bait poisoning using [[Calcium Carbide]] and other toxicants as a promising but still experimental approach.

## Background & Description

The source is a management-focused literature review rather than a morphological study; direct anatomical descriptions of *Cyprinus carpio* are minimal. However, relevant physiological data affecting toxicant susceptibility are noted:

- **Respiration rate** was identified by Belding (1929) as a major determinant of piscicide toxicity in fish, influenced by water temperature, activity level, acclimatization, and body size.
- **Metabolic rate** was identified (Basset, 1956) as the principal factor governing differential [[Rotenone]] toxicity between carp and [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*).
- Carp exhibit **feeding behavior** (bottom-oriented, vegetation-disruptive) that increases water turbidity — an indirect ecological/behavioral trait exploited in habitat-damage assessments rather than a morphological trait per se.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat plasticity**: Carp show unusually broad tolerance across pond, lake, river, and stream systems, and across both warm- and cold-water thermal regimes (Simon, 1946; Beckman, 1952; Ward, 1954), a key trait enabling competitive success against native [[North American Freshwater Fish]] species.
- **Trophic role**: Not a predatory species, but carp degrade habitat quality through destruction of aquatic vegetation and resuspension of bottom sediments, increasing turbidity (Threinen & Helm, 1954; Tryon, 1954; Marz & Cooper, 1957).
- **Invasive impact**: These habitat modifications are cited as detrimental to native fish assemblages, framing carp as an ecosystem-altering invasive species requiring active management — a case study relevant to [[Invasive Fish Species Management]].
- **Co-occurring species affected by control efforts**: Non-target mortality was documented in [[Bonytail]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Bluegill]] (*Lepomis macrochirus*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*), [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), and [[Crappie]] (*Pomoxis* spp.) during broad-spectrum piscicide applications, highlighting the ecological trade-offs of non-selective chemical control.

## Key Findings

### Physical Control Methods
- **Seining**: Effective in select Indiana lakes (Ricker & Gootschalk, 1941) but inconsistent elsewhere due to poor seining/landing conditions; labor- and cost-intensive.
- **Netting** (gill, trammel, fyke, hoop, haul seines): Deemed ineffective for large-scale carp control in New York waters (Loeb, 1950a).
- **Electrical control**: Impractical at scale in Texas (Burr, 1931) and New York (Loeb, 1955, 1957b); useful only for small-scale collection.

### Chemical Control — General Toxicants
- **[[Rotenone]]**: 5% emulsifiable rotenone at 0.5 ppm (52°F) produced ~100% carp kill (Weiter & Starr, 1956) but was non-selective. Basset (1956) found 0.1+ ppm of 5% liquid rotenone lethal to carp vs. only ~50% lethality in black bullheads — indicating species-specific metabolic sensitivity.
- **Tephrosia vogelli** (African hoary pea, powdered seed): Lethal to bluegill and largemouth bass at 2 ppm (70°F) (Blommaert, 1950); untested directly on carp but proposed as a candidate.
- **Metal toxicity** (Podubsky & Stedronsky, 1949): Copper, brass, nickel, bronze, silver, zinc, and mercury toxic to carp fry in laboratory trials; tin, manganese, and iron largely non-toxic. No field validation conducted.
- **Sodium sulfate (Na₂SO₄)**: Lethal at 0.55–5.0 mg/l depending on pH (Tomiyama & Yamagawa, 1950).
- **Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)**: Lethal to carp within hours; Chamberlin (1947) reported 6 ppm killing carp in Florida waters; Belding found 5.0 ppm lethal after ~1.6 hours exposure.
- **[[Toxaphene]]**: Hemphill (1954) achieved total carp kill at 0.1 ppm in an Arizona lake field trial. Application recovered ~220 tons of carp plus substantial non-target mortality (bonytail, bass, bluegill, bullheads). Rain-induced dilution halted the kill after 48 hours. Treatment cost: ~8 cents/acre-foot of water. Alkaline water caused rapid chemical breakdown, permitting restocking within four weeks.

### Selective Poisoning
- Inspired by [[Sea Lamprey]] (*Petromyzon marinus*) control research in Michigan (~4,500 chemicals screened), suggesting the feasibility of developing carp-selective toxicants (Loeb, 1957a) — a conceptual bridge from single-species eradication programs to broader invasive fish management.

### Bait Poisoning Experiments
- **Design criteria** for effective carp bait: water-insoluble, low toxicity to non-target animals/humans, and capable of dissipating within ~48 hours (Loeb, 1957c).
- **Calcium carbide (CaC₂) trials** (Huston, 1955, 1956):
  - Two forms tested: "pea-sized lump carbide" and "Number 14, No Dust" granulated carbide, each requiring waterproof coatings (paraffin, liquid plastic, beef tallow, gelatin capsules) to delay water reactivity (see waterproofing comparison table below).
  - Laboratory force-feeding: "Number 14" carbide/beef tallow mixture achieved 14/16 carp mortality at 96 hours vs. 1/16 for strychnine-tallow controls — suggesting superior efficacy of carbide-based bait.
  - **Waterproofing performance summary**:
    | Method | Effective Duration | Notes |
    |---|---|---|
    | Paraffin coating (lump carbide) | 72 hrs | Economical; digestibility uncertain |
    | Liquid plastic | <48 hrs | Expensive; poor waterproofing |
    | Beef tallow (lump carbide) | 1–24 hrs | Very low waterproofing |
    | Gelatin capsules (No. 14 carbide) | Indefinite | Difficult to prepare; tends to float |
    | Beef tallow, pre-reacted 96 hrs (No. 14 carbide) | Indefinite | Continuous reactivity with water |
  - **Field trial (Colorado, 30-acre lake)**: 10,000–12,000 pellets broadcast; only one crappie and small numbers of carp/bullhead recovered dead after 3–7 days, with inconsistent carbide detection in carcasses — indicating low field efficacy despite promising lab results.
- **Multi-toxicant bait trials** (Haskell, 1956): Calcium carbide, toxaphene, DDT, malathion, chlordane, Pival, and red squill combined with doughballs, grain, and cottonseed meal baits tested in the Colorado River. Results were largely negative — bait was consumed but caused no mortality, likely due to insufficient dosage or gastric neutralization. Anise oil was used to mask taste/odor deterrents with partial success before carp again refused bait at increased dosages.
- **Barium oxalate crystal bait** (Williams, pers. comm., Michigan DOC): Corn impregnated with barium chloride and oxalic acid formed barium oxalate crystals; bait was ignored, requiring force-feeding. No immediate mortality, but delayed winter mortality of 74 carp was observed with no non-target mortality — suggesting possible indirect or delayed toxic effects.

### Overall Conclusion
No single physical, chemical, or bait-based method reviewed achieved a practical, economically feasible, large-scale solution for carp control by 1957. Most approaches were either too costly, labor-intensive, non-selective, or logistically constrained for widespread application, underscoring the ongoing need for [[Selective Piscicide]] development in [[Freshwater Fisheries Management]].
