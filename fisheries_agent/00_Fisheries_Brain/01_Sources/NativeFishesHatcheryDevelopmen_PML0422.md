# Native Fishes Hatchery Development Protocol

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #conservation-hatchery

## Executive Summary

This 2005 final report from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], funded by the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] and administered by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, sets out a protocol for collecting, transporting, quarantining, maintaining, propagating and repatriating listed native fishes of the [[Gila River Basin]]. The protocol is designed to reduce the risk of catastrophic loss of rare populations in the wild through a captive "refuge" population, with eventual repatriation to protected habitat. It acknowledges that the planned facility at BPH cannot support full broodstock management, so genetic and husbandry compromises must be accepted and managed through careful collection, mating and augmentation practices.

## Background & Description

The report covers native fishes of the Gila River basin, most of which are in decline. Species are grouped into two categories:

- **Long-lived, typically large-bodied species** (for example, [[Roundtail Chub]]), which are more amenable to refuge maintenance. Broodstock planning has time to develop because adults persist in captivity, and the species' recognition as a sportfish should help secure funding.
- **Short-lived, typically small-bodied species** (for example, [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]), which must reproduce within their first year in captivity, produce far fewer eggs per fish than large-bodied species, and spawn multiple clutches. Their broodstock plans should be developed before the need arises.

The report also references the [[Gila Trout]], an endangered salmonid for which a multi-million-dollar broodstock facility exists at the New Mexico National Fish Hatchery. The BPH facility, by contrast, will consist of rearing tanks supplied with flow-through artesian well water. The report notes that a comprehensive broodstock program is beyond its current capacity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats.** The report identifies the presence of [[Non-Native Predators]] as the foremost limiting factor for native species. Rare populations are now so low that they are virtually impossible to find in the wild. Any wild fish found are expected to be few in number.

**Flooding.** Extensive flooding in spring 2005 in the southwestern United States raised hopes for a rebound of rare natives, since these species generally survive floods better than non-natives ([[Minckley and Meffe]] 1987). Floods also clean and expose spawning substrates. Based on this, wild loach minnow or spikedace found after flooding should be collected for captive propagation rather than left in the wild.

**Habitat needs.** Loach minnow and [[Speckled Dace]] are bottom-dwelling and secretive, so captive tanks should include cover and substrate. Most other species tolerate provided cover well.

**Parasites.** Fish may carry the internal parasite [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*), which must be treated before release into the refuge.

## Key Findings

### 1. Genetic Risks of Small Captive Populations
- Effective population size is correlated with fitness and persistence (Reed 2005).
- Small founding populations cause genetic drift, [[Inbreeding Depression]] and loss of genetic diversity. These effects are immediate and worsen as founding size decreases.
- Hatchery fish are exposed to artificial selection, including culling and survival of unfit fish.
- Mixing stocks from disparate locations can cause [[Outbreeding Depression]] by disrupting co-adapted gene complexes.
- Broodstock should be established only as a last resort, and only while wild causes of decline are being addressed.

### 2. Refuge Population Guidelines (Locally Abundant Populations)
- Collect at least **100 adults** (50:50 sex ratio) from the wild.
- Spawn randomly and hold at least **2,000 F1 progeny** produced over the following years, to represent the captive adult population's genetics.
- Add wild fish to the captive adult population annually to increase genetic diversity and replace dead or senescent adults.
- Use this population as a safeguard, not as a source for ongoing production or translocation.

### 3. Rare Population Guidelines
- Collect all available wild fish. If immediate loss is likely, rear all F1 progeny to adulthood for reintroduction, ideally with a batch mark for identification.
- Hold adults and F1 progeny for **at least two years** to allow more wild fish to be added.
- Use [[Paired Mating]] (controlled outcrossing) if fewer than **20 adults** are captured or if genetic screening shows severe loss of diversity in F1 progeny. Random mating is otherwise preferred, because arbitrary pairing introduces artificial selection and may produce matings that would not occur in the wild.

### 4. Collection
- Seine size bias can skew sex ratios and select for larger adults. Loach minnow show strong sexual dimorphism, with males sometimes twice the size of females.
- Use the smallest practicable mesh size.
- Collect the first 50 males and 50 females showing no visible deformities, parasitic disease or infection.

### 5. Transportation
- Haul fish in tanks with clean, smooth-sided interiors, filled with source water, containing about 0.6% salt solution (the OCR is ambiguous here), and carrying a backup oxygen supply.
- Acclimate temperature by floating the sealed bag in the tank, and check fish health and oxygen at least every two hours.

### 6. Quarantine and Treatment
- Isolate quarantined fish physically from other tanks, raceways and ponds, and divert outflow water away from the refuge.
- Begin prophylactic treatment for external pathogens immediately: 24-hour treatments for 3 consecutive days at 18–20 °C, using malachite green, nitrofurazone or acriflavine (IchGuard, if available).
- Treat for Asian tapeworm before fish enter the refuge.

### 7. Maintenance
- Loach minnow and spikedace do well on a combination of Tetramin flake and freeze-dried bloodworms. Large-bodied species such as chubs and suckers do well on standard trout chow.
- Feed ad libitum once or twice daily in flow-through tanks, watching for food washout. Automatic feeders are expensive and risk overfeeding.
- Give each tank its own cleaning equipment and dipnet to limit disease transfer. Sterilize buckets and nets with a standard hatchery cleaning agent, and pressure-wash tanks once per month.
- Record every mortality (species, source, date) in a daily logbook and spreadsheet to track mortality rates.

### 8. Propagation
- Propagation techniques for loach minnow and spikedace are already established (Childs 2004; Parmenter and Platania 2004).
- Captive fish will be encouraged to spawn. Loach minnow eggs are incubated in the adjacent wetlab, and juveniles are moved back to the refuge.
- Spikedace tanks are lined with pea gravel so eggs settle out of reach of adults. Larvae are dip-netted to an adjacent 1,900 L tank.
- Hold all F1 progeny in separate tanks to avoid backcrossing and to build redundancy.

### 9. Repatriation
- Decide on a case-by-case basis.
- Before any introduction, obtain concurrence from resource managers (conservation biologists and sportfish managers), landowners (state, federal and private) and grazing permittees.
- Complete environmental assessments by the Arizona Game and Fish Department and any involved federal agency, showing no negative consequences for the species or environment.
- Replicating a wild population directly requires at least 100 adults (50:50). If the source population is too small, F1 progeny from the captive population can be used instead.

### 10. Review Comments
Reviewer comments appended to the draft raise concerns that the proposed approach may be unrealistic for short-lived fish. They suggest stocking F1 progeny immediately while continuing to augment the captive population with new wild stock, and augmenting repatriated populations with F1 fish from the captive population.
