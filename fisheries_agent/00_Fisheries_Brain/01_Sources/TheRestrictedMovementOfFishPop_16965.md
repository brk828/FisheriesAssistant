# The Restricted Movement of Fish Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-behaviour #home-range #homing

## Executive Summary

This review, by [[Shelby D. Gerking]] of the Department of Zoology, Indiana University, argues that many fish species remain in a restricted area for long periods, and that this habit is far more widespread than previously recognised. The argument draws on Gerking's own mark-recapture studies of Indiana stream populations, on a literature survey of stream, lake and ocean fishes, and on the behavioural mechanisms that could explain site fidelity. The review covers [[Home Range]], [[Territoriality]], [[Reflex Behaviour]], [[Non-Reproductive Social Behaviour]] and [[Homing]], and ends with practical advice for fisheries biologists. The author's central claim is that restricted movement is common, that it is not limited to the spawning season, and that it has direct consequences for population estimates, stocking and growth.

## Background & Description

The review began with two stream populations in Indiana. In a quarter-mile bedrock stream divided into four sections, fish were marked by clipping fins and followed through summer surveys for four years. On average about 80% of recaptured fish were found in the same pool from one year to the next, and some were found in the same place for four consecutive years. A sand-and-gravel stream studied for one summer gave essentially the same results.

Gerking uses the term [[Home Range]], as used by mammalogists and ornithologists, for the area an animal normally travels. He reserves [[Territoriality]] for areas actively defended against intruders, because observing defence in natural populations is difficult. The review's Table 1 lists 34 species with restricted movement not linked to spawning. These include salmonids, catostomids, ictalurids, percids, centrarchids, and several marine reef families such as Holocentridae, Chaetodontidae, Acanthuridae, Pomacentridae, Gobiidae and Cottidae.

Gerking also surveys [[Homing]], which he defines as choosing to return to a formerly occupied place over other equally probable places. Table 2 lists 21 species that home, including six stream fishes, nine lake or pond fishes and six ocean forms. Salmon, eel and shad are excluded because they are well known.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Floods, droughts and persistence.** The review asks why stream populations are not disrupted for long periods by floods or drought. Floods seem to have little effect unless the environment is greatly changed. Dry parts of intermittent streams are repopulated quickly with little change in species composition. Homing may explain this recolonisation, and the review proposes that returning fish may be the same individuals that were driven out.

**Stray fish.** Some individuals always wander. The proportion of strays ranges from about one-third of a population to none. [[Stefanich]] (1952) sampled 150 ft sections of Prickley Pear Creek, Montana, for two years. About 71% of tagged brown trout were recovered in the sections where they were tagged in the same year, and only six of 24 random-section recaptures had moved. Brook trout, mountain whitefish and western white suckers showed similar fidelity. [[Funk]] (1957) argued for a distinct mobile group, but his data came from angler recaptures, which Gerking considers biased toward strays.

**Hatchery trout.** Stocked trout also tend to stay near release points. [[Cooper]] (1953) found that trout planted in water below 10 °C moved rapidly downstream, while those planted in warmer water stayed near the planting site.

**Tagging and design bias.** Gerking and [[Williams]] (1957) both warn that marking can affect behaviour. [[Miller]] (1957) concluded that Petersen tags weakened cutthroat trout and caused downstream movement. Arbitrary study boundaries also bias results, because the home range of boundary fish extends outside the study area. Gerking concludes that precise quantitative home-range measurements are not possible with current aquatic techniques.

**Reflex and sensory orientation.** Fishes form lasting associations with their surroundings. [[Goldsmith]] (1905–1914) showed that the common goby kept an association between food and forceps colour for 7–12 days. [[Miller]] (1954) found that cutthroat trout kept their association with a home pool for about 30 days. [[Hasler]] (1956) showed that minnows orientated to the water's marks and irregular oxidation patterns rather than to a light source. Earlier work by [[Lyon]] (1904) and [[Gray]] (1937) showed that vision and the semicircular canals guide orientation in currents, and that touch is important for bottom-dwelling fishes. Lateral-line and olfactory cues are also discussed. The review notes that the relative importance of the senses is still unresolved.

**Social behaviour.** Territoriality and social hierarchy occur in many species, both in and out of the breeding season. [[Braddock]] (1945, 1949) described a straight-line "nip-right" hierarchy in the swordtail. [[Greenberg]] (1947) showed territory-holding and dominance in immature green sunfish, with larger fish holding larger territories and males ranking above females. [[Newman]] (1956) found aggression between rainbow and brook trout in aquaria and in Sagehen Creek, California. Brook trout were dominant in nine of twelve interspecific experiments. [[Winn]] (1958) found that darters mostly attacked their own species when defending territories. Aggression also acts as a spacing mechanism that influences population density.

**Competition and growth.** [[Allee]] et al. (1948) found that green sunfish in groups of four grew faster than single fish in a quarter of the water volume. High-ranking fish ate more and grew faster regardless of sex. [[Miller]] (1958) found that hatchery cutthroat trout placed in a resident stream lost weight, died at higher rates, and had higher blood lactate, suggesting active competition for space.

## Key Findings

- **Restricted movement is widespread.** At least 34 fish species show restricted movement outside the breeding season or when immature. Some defend areas (territories), while others only show limited movement (home ranges).
- **Site fidelity persists over time.** Gerking's own stream work found about 80% of recaptures in the same pool year to year.
- **Homing is not confined to spawning.** Experimentally displaced longear sunfish largely returned. Of 35 displaced fish, 26 returned to a home pool within two weeks. The review lists 21 homing species.
- **Brown trout homing (Stuart 1957).** Of 3000 fin-clipped fish, only one errant fish was found. Of 22 mature fish transplanted across a reservoir, all 34 recaptured fish had crossed back to their native streams.
- **Stream spawners return to natal sites.** [[Schuck]] (1943) found most wild brown trout returned to their original sections after spawning migrations.
- **Lake fishes home.** [[Shoemaker]] (1952) reported that 86% of recaptured pumpkinseeds and 95% of recaptured yellow bullheads returned to their home netting area in Lake Myosotis, New York.
- **Estuarine and coastal homing.** [[Hasler]] & Wisby (1958) reported that 91% of 310 recaptured green sunfish returned to the corner of a half-acre pond where they had been caught, and 68% of 256 largemouth bass returned to the home side of two lakes.
- **Tidepool and reef homing.** Gobies jump directionally between pools at low tide (Aronson 1951). Wooly sculpin and opaleye migrate with the tides and find their way home. Bermuda reef red hinds and Nassau groupers homed after transfer (11 and 12 recaptures, respectively).
- **Walleye and dwarf sucker stream fidelity.** [[Stoudt]] (1939) reported that 90% of 300 recaptured walleye returned to their chosen stream. [[Dence]] (1948) found only 3% of 1943 recaptures had changed spawning stream.
- **Smallmouth bass.** [[Larimore]] (1952) reported that 17 of 32 transfers returned to their home pool within 1–2 weeks.
- **Petrale sole.** Tagging suggested a return to specific spawning "deeps," and fish may adopt the migration pattern of the stock to which they are recruited.
- **Definition of homing.** Gerking proposes that homing is the return to a formerly occupied place instead of going to other equally probable places. He argues that restricting homing to the return of spawning adults to natal streams is too narrow.
- **Practical applications.** (1) Population estimates for territorial or restricted-range species should use randomised capture, since arbitrary stations sample only nearby fish. (2) Stocking should scatter fish over a wide area, because hatchery fish may stay where released and increase competition with wild stock. (3) Aggression can limit movement and population density. (4) Social rank may explain differences in individual growth.
- **Conclusion.** Gerking's summary holds that restricted movement and homing are more common in fishes than generally appreciated, that no single mechanism is likely to explain homing, and that the behaviour has practical value for managing density, growth and stocking.
