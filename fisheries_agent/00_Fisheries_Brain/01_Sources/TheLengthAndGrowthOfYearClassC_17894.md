# Length and Growth of 0-Year Class Creek Chubs in Relation to Domestic Pollution

#ichthyology #marine-biology #creek-chub

## Executive Summary

This study examines how partially treated domestic sewage from Wilmington, Ohio, affects the size and growth of [[Creek Chub]] (*Semotilus atromaculatus*) in [[Lytle Creek]]. The authors, working at the [[Robert A. Taft Sanitary Engineering Center]] of the U.S. Public Health Service, compared 0-year-class fish across five stations representing clean-water and recovery zones. Mean lengths of the same year class differed significantly among zones. Fish were largest in the enriched lower recovery zone and smallest in the upper clean-water zone. Growth patterns and age structure also varied by zone. The authors conclude that creek chubs in this stream form several non-migratory local populations, and they attribute the differences to food supply, stream flow, [[Dissolved Oxygen]] levels, and the fertilizing effect of the sewage effluent.

## Background & Description

**Study species.** The creek chub was chosen because it is the predominant fish in Lytle Creek and occurs at every station where fish were found. The authors describe it as a tolerant species. Its diet, as summarized from [[Dobie, Meehan, and Washburn 1948]], is omnivorous and includes vegetable matter, aquatic and terrestrial insects, crayfish, small fish, snails, and other available organisms.

**Study site.** Lytle Creek receives the partially treated domestic sewage of Wilmington, Ohio. The sewage treatment plant outlet lies 7.2 miles above Todd's Fork. During summer, septic conditions make the stretch extending almost 3 miles downstream of the outlet fishless. Five stations were sampled, numbered by river miles above the confluence:
- Upper clean-water zone: Stations 9.5 and 8.7
- Recovery zone: Stations 4.2 (upper portion) and 2.8 (lower, enriched portion)
- Lower clean-water zone: Station 1.0

**Background research.** The authors build on earlier work by [[Katz and Gaufin 1953]], which showed that fish species number and population size increased as sewage was purified downstream. They also reference [[Gaufin and Tarzwell 1952]], which correlated bottom macroinvertebrate populations with pollution level, season, and physical and chemical conditions. Earlier reports had described pollution-associated abnormalities and dwarfing in fish (notably [[Hubbs 1933]]). Other reports described beneficial effects of sewage enrichment, such as increased fish production in Lake Erie and the Illinois River.

**Methods.**
- Fish were collected with 14-inch square-mesh seines at all stations on the same day during monthly collections from February through September 1952.
- Specimens were preserved in 8-percent formalin and measured for total length to the nearest millimeter.
- Age was determined by [[Scale Aging]].
- Only 0-year-class fish were used for growth comparisons, because older fish were few in number.
- Differences in mean length were tested with the t-test. Where variances differed significantly, [[Welch's Modified T-Test]] was used. The critical t value for 50 degrees of freedom at the 99% level was 2.67.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Food supply.** Bottom macroinvertebrates were abundant throughout the creek. The volume of potential food organisms was about three times greater at Station 4.2 than at Stations 2.8 and 1.0. Despite this, fish at Station 4.2 remained few in number and species, and their growth nearly stopped in late summer. The authors suggest that differences in invertebrate composition may matter more than total volume, but they lacked data on creek chub diet or stomach contents.

**Stream flow.** The very dry summer of 1952 left no detectable flow at the upper clean-water stations (9.5 and 8.7). Fish there were confined to shallow, isolated pools, and intense competition for food probably limited growth. Below the sewage outlet, flow was adequate at about 1 to 2 cubic feet per second, with normal riffle and pool habitat. The authors also note that fish from small streams are generally smaller than conspecifics from larger streams.

**Dissolved oxygen.** Hourly oxygen readings from August 22 and 23, 1951 (from Gaufin and Tarzwell, 1952) showed:

| Station | Maximum (ppm) | Minimum (ppm) |
|---|---|---|
| 4.2 | 5.5 | 2.0 |
| 2.8 | 14.7 | 3.2 |
| 1.0 | 9.6 | 5.3 |

At Station 4.2, all fish were restricted to pool areas just below riffles, where mechanical aeration raised oxygen levels. Long June and July days, along with phytoplankton photosynthesis, allowed adequate foraging. As days shortened, the hours of adequate oxygen decreased, which limited feeding and growth.

**Sewage effluent.** The authors suggest that the rapid growth, large size, and abundance of chubs at Station 2.8 may reflect a fertilizing effect of the sewage effluent on aquatic life. This effect was no longer evident at Station 1.0, where fish were significantly smaller.

## Key Findings

1. **Size differences by zone (August 1952 abstract means, total length):**
   - Upper clean-water zone (Station 8.7): 42.3 mm
   - Upper recovery zone (Station 4.2): 53.3 mm
   - Lower recovery zone (Station 2.8): 63.8 mm (largest)
   - Lower clean-water zone (Station 1.0): 52.0 mm

2. **Consistent differences across years.** Early spring 1952 collections showed the 1951 year class at Station 2.8 was larger on average than at Station 1.0. The same pattern appeared in the 1950 year class sampled in October 1950.

3. **Growth patterns differed.** Fish at Station 4.2 grew rapidly from June 17 to July 23 but slowed afterward, with almost no growth between August 25 and September 24. Growth at other stations, including Station 1.0, remained rapid into early fall.

4. **Age structure.** On August 25, 1952:
   - Station 4.2: 40 fish, 100.0% 0-year class, 0.0% older
   - Station 2.8: 310 fish, 84.8% 0-year class, 15.2% one year or older
   - Station 1.0: 267 fish, 83.5% 0-year class, 16.5% one year or older

   The authors infer that the upper recovery zone population does not survive winter and is replenished each year by spring migrants from downstream. Older fish were present at the other stations despite considerable predation by minnow fishermen.

5. **Non-migratory populations.** Persistent size differences among stations indicate that creek chubs form several independent local populations, at least during their first year. The authors compare this with brown trout and with home-range studies by [[Gerking 1953]] and [[Stefanich 1952]].

6. **Comparison with other localities.** Station 2.8 fish were larger than [[Ohio Hatchery Creek Chubs]] early in the season but grew more slowly by the fourth month. They were also larger than older wild fish from Tennessee and Ontario reported by [[Hart 1952]]. Lytle Creek fish did not show the continued post-first-summer growth seen in [[Michigan Hatchery Creek Chubs]].

7. **Factors influencing growth.** Food supply, stream flow, dissolved oxygen, and sewage effluent effects were all considered. The authors conclude that the variation in growth is probably due to a combination of these factors, and that the observed pattern of growth does not correlate with the volume of bottom organisms alone.

8. **Overall conclusion.** Domestic sewage pollution clearly affects the growth of creek chubs in Lytle Creek. In this case, enrichment near the outfall appears to benefit growth, while the upper recovery zone's low oxygen and reduced foraging hours limit it.
