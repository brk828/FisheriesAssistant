# Fish Growth Rate Studies: Techniques and Role in Surveys and Management

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fish-growth #age-and-growth

## Executive Summary

This 1956 review by Kenneth D. Carlander of Iowa State College, reprinted from the Transactions of the Twenty-first North American Wildlife Conference, appraises the methods, data treatment, and purpose of [[Fish Growth Rate Studies]]. Growth rates are highly responsive to environmental conditions, making growth data valuable indicators of population welfare, yet the author argues that growth studies are often conducted "because we know how to make them" rather than to answer specific questions. The paper covers age determination from [[Scale Reading]] and other hard structures, [[Body-Scale Relationship]] corrections, statistical treatment of growth data, [[Sampling Bias]] in collections, and the role of growth data in routine surveys and [[Fisheries Management]]. It closes with a call for more planning, critical analysis, and reporting of results.

## Background & Description

Growth studies rest on three methods: direct observation (hatcheries, stocked populations, tagged fish, with the caveat that tagging may alter growth), length-frequency analysis (useful only to about the second or third year of life because age-group size distributions overlap), and interpretation of growth rings on scales, otoliths, vertebrae, opercular bones, spines, and fin rays. The paper focuses on the ring-interpretation method.

Key technical notes:
- **Scales** are the most easily collected, stored, and examined structure. Scales can be mounted between glass slides and viewed under low power or projected with a 35 mm slide projector.
- **Scale impressions** (plastic impressions of the sculptured surface) avoid erosion pits on the underside but require periodic checks for edge recording and "plastic flow" distortion (Smith, 1954).
- **Microprojectors** and **nomographs** speed measurement and back-calculation of lengths. Nomographs were described as early as 1918 but became common in the US only in the last 15 years.
- **Bones, otoliths, spines and fin rays** are usually sectioned, using razor blades or small power saws, and examined with transmitted and reflected light.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author notes that formation of one annulus per year is accepted as the rule, at least in temperate regions. The real difficulty is correct interpretation: false annuli can form during a fish's first summer, crowded rings on old fish are hard to read, and false rings are mainly caused by retarded growth, the same factor that produces true annuli. False rings may also appear on bones and fin rays under conditions that cause them on scales.

Body growth is generally not directly proportional to scale growth. The true relationship is curvilinear, but the paper recommends assuming a rectilinear relationship except in special studies, since the portion of growth used for back-calculation is nearly rectilinear in most populations.

Growth is regionally influenced, so unusually slow growth in one water compared with neighbouring waters is usually attributed to overpopulation. Conversely, rapid growth in an overfished water and slow growth in an overpopulated one can both produce abundant small fish (Bennett, 1945).

## Key Findings

- **Growth as indicator:** Growth rates respond readily to environmental factors, so they are valuable indicators of population welfare, but studies are often made from habit rather than defined need.
- **Scale reading is an art:** Accurate reading needs training and judgment. Reading scales blind to probable age, duplicate readings separated by days, and independent readings by two biologists reduce bias. Fish with disagreeing readings should be assigned to the most likely age group and reported, not discarded, because excluding them underrepresents older age groups.
- **Bones as verification:** Bones and fin rays are underused and could verify scale readings, though scales remain easier to handle.
- **Back-calculation:** Back-calculated lengths are simple but time-consuming. Assuming rectilinear body-scale regression is generally safer than fitting curvilinear models from inadequate samples. Mottley's direct scale comparison saves effort but needs larger samples and a stable body-scale relationship.
- **Annual increments:** Increments are more informative than calculated lengths for detecting change. They must be computed per individual fish or per age group. Averaging across the whole population has produced negative increments even when every fish grew.
- **Terminology:** "Growth rate" should refer only to rate of change in size with time. Average lengths at age are an absolute growth curve.
- **Statistical comparison:** Growth patterns vary, so simple comparisons such as time to reach catchable size or mean length at age 2–4 are practical. Growth models (Bertalanffy, Hile, Walford) are seldom applied. Most workers do not test whether observed differences could be due to chance.
- **Sampling is the hardest problem:** Random samples are nearly impossible, since gear is size-selective and fish occupy different habitats as they grow. Several semi-independent populations may be sampled without recognition.
- **Sample size (Table 1):** The table gives required sample sizes at 95% confidence (19 of 20 cases) across about 25 species and sources. Combined median for estimating mean length within 1% is 546 specimens (range 90–3384), for 2% is 152 (range 27–854), and for 5% is 28 (range 8–145). The text states that over 550 specimens are needed for 1% precision and that 30 fish give roughly 5% precision. Increasing sample size does not fix biased sampling.
- **Survey value:** Routine surveys are usually small and seasonally inconsistent, so conclusions should be tentative and only large deviations treated as significant. Back-calculation rarely justifies its cost in surveys, since large growth differences appear in lengths at capture. Surveys still provide regional "standard" growth data and can reveal dominant year classes and population shifts.
- **Management implications:** Growth data help diagnose overfishing or underfishing, guide changes in fishing methods, and evaluate management practices. For commercial fisheries, growth and year-class studies underpin optimum yield.
- **Call for purpose:** The author recommends defining the question before sampling, sizing samples for the required confidence, critically analysing results (including unexpected ones), and publishing concise summaries. Diminishing returns from general growth studies of freshwater fishes should be considered.
