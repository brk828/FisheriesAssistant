# Unusual Drift of Physa gyrina in a Small Minnesota Stream

#malacology #freshwater-biology #physa-gyrina

## Executive Summary

This short research note reports an unusually large daily drift of adult [[Physa Gyrina]] (a freshwater pond snail) in [[Fort Ridgely Creek]], a small warmwater stream in southwestern [[Minnesota]]. Adult drift reached 5.33 x 10^5 individuals per m³/s, far above previously reported gastropod drift rates. Drift usually rose sharply around [[Sunrise]], but no consistently repeated [[Diel Drift Periodicity]] was found. The authors attribute the high drift to increased population density and/or reproduction, and suggest a physiological mechanism: metabolic lowering of [[Specific Gravity]] that makes the snails [[Buoyancy|buoyant]] enough to be carried in the water column. Gastropods are generally considered a minor component of stream drift, which makes this occurrence unusual.

## Background & Description

Gastropods are generally regarded as a minor component of stream [[Drift Ecology|drift]], which is the downstream transport of invertebrates in flowing water (Waters 1969; Hynes 1972). Earlier studies reported gastropod drift at lower densities. [[Logan 1963]] recorded snail drift in a Montana stream, and [[Clifford 1971]] reported mean daily drift of *Physa* and *Lymnaea* in a small Alberta stream. The mean value in the Clifford summary is illegible in the source scan, so it is not reproduced here. By contrast, adult *Physa gyrina* drift of up to 5.33 x 10^5 per m³/s was recorded at Fort Ridgely Creek.

The study examined adult *P. gyrina*, defined as snails with shell length greater than 2.5 mm. The stream was sampled from September 1975 through May 1977. Its headwaters are intensively ditched for agricultural drainage, while the study reaches downstream were undisturbed and natural. The work was supported by funds from the Office of Water Research and Technology, administered by the University of Minnesota's Water Resources Research Center, and was published as a scientific journal series paper of the Minnesota Agricultural Experiment Station.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study site.** Fort Ridgely Creek is a small, warmwater stream. The study reach had large, smooth-surfaced mineral substrates, macroalgae, and abundant food resources (epilithic and epiphytic microalgae). These conditions were considered suitable for dense snail populations. Snails were also observed in reaches upstream of the study site, though these were not quantitatively sampled.

**Benthic standing stock.** Ten Surber benthos samples (0.7 m² each) were taken on fifteen dates from nearby riffle areas. The mean density of adult *P. gyrina* in the benthos is illegible in the source scan. The snails made up 16.6 percent of the macrobenthic fauna, which the authors describe as a relatively high standing stock.

**Sampling methods.** Duplicate 24-hour drift samples were collected on ten dates using nets of 15 x 30 x 100 cm with 471-micron mesh.

**Possible causes considered.** The authors evaluated several explanations for the drift of large, heavy adult snails, which would not normally be expected to drift easily:
- *Toxicant input.* An insecticide or herbicide may have been accidentally applied upstream in the agricultural area, forcing snails downstream to find better water quality.
- *Food loss.* A toxicant may have degraded the algal food supply.
- *Hydrological and thermal change.* Lowered water levels and elevated temperatures in upstream unshaded reaches may have occurred before a late summer and autumn 1976 drought.

The authors argue these factors are unlikely to explain the pattern. Such events should also have affected other macrobenthos, but drift of other organisms in May 1976 was similar to that in earlier and later months. Snail drift is therefore interpreted as a behavioral response linked to population density and/or reproduction. Juvenile gastropods and gelatinous egg sacs of *Physa* were also abundant in the May 1976 drift samples.

## Key Findings

- **Daily drift range.** Daily drift of adult *P. gyrina* ranged from zero (January 1975) to 1398 individuals per net (May 1976).
- **Peak drift rate.** The May 1976 daily drift equated to 5.33 x 10^5 adult *P. gyrina* per m³/s. This is far above previously reported gastropod drift rates. The figure includes only adults; juveniles were recorded at numbers several orders of magnitude higher.
- **Hourly peak.** The maximum hourly rate observed was in May 1976. The hourly count is illegible in the source scan.
- **April 1976 peak.** A relatively high drift peak in April 1976 resulted from catastrophic drift during high discharge. It is not strictly comparable with samples collected under normal flow conditions.
- **Diel pattern.** Hourly samples from May 29 to 30, 1976 showed mean drift of 60.9 individuals per net per hour during daylight and 52.1 during darkness. Drift rose sharply around sunrise and then dropped over the next four hours. A similar pulse appeared on most other sampling dates. No clear diel pattern was established, although drift showed at least a weak relationship with [[Photoperiod]].
- **Proposed mechanism.** Some *Physa* species can reduce their specific gravity (Pennak 1978). Many live floating snails were observed in drift samples in May 1976. The authors propose that metabolic lowering of specific gravity makes the snails buoyant. This would facilitate movement within the water column and could allow redistribution of individuals throughout the stream.
- **Conclusion.** The authors conclude that the unusually high drift of *P. gyrina* in May 1976 was most likely a behavioral phenomenon related to increased population density and/or reproduction, not a response to toxicants or environmental stress.
