# Yampa River Flows and Colorado Squawfish Reproductive Success in the Green River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1980 report by [[Bio/West Inc]], prepared for the [[National Park Service]] to support a reserved water right claim for [[Dinosaur National Monument]], tests whether flow in the [[Yampa River]] controls the reproductive success of endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the upper [[Green River]]. The authors compare fish age-class data from two long-term studies (1962–1966 and 1977–1979) with [[USGS Gauging Stations]] hydrographs. They conclude that natural spring flows from the Yampa are strongly correlated with successful squawfish reproduction, and that regulated flow and cold water from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] are the main threats. The report also argues that [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] depend on large-river habitat maintained by natural flows.

## Background & Description

- **Target species:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) is described as America's largest minnow, reportedly reaching about 6 feet and 80–100 pounds. It is long-lived and matures sexually at about 7 years. It spawns in summer (July to early August) in the upper Green River at water temperatures near 70°F.
- **Life history:** Young fish first eat small crustaceans. As they grow they shift to larger insects and, by about 8 inches, feed almost entirely on fish. Adults change habitat seasonally.
- **Listed and proposed species:** The document states that Colorado Squawfish and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) are endangered. [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) are proposed for listing.
- **Hypotheses tested:**
  1. Reduced flows from river regulation have degraded instream habitat for rare fishes.
  2. Successful squawfish reproduction depends on natural or near-natural flows.
- **Objective:** Determine the relationship between Yampa River flow and squawfish reproductive success in the Green River below the Yampa confluence.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling and aging approach**
- Age classes were assigned from [[Length-Frequency Analysis]] (following [[Vanicek 1967 Study]]). Age 0 fish are found August–December, age I the following year, and so on.
- Young-of-the-year are readily caught by seine in August–October. Yearlings are most common in May and June.
- Success was judged qualitatively as **good** (young fairly common for the effort expended, or a cohort commonly found at ages I–III) or **poor** (few young or juveniles despite adequate effort). Raw catch numbers were considered unreliable because of habitat and effort differences.

**Study areas and datasets**
- **[[Bio/West Studies]] (1977–1979):** Intensive seining of the Jensen–Ouray reach (about 20 river miles studied in 1979) in multiple projects, including [[Moon Lake Project]] work and a [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] inlet modification study.
- **[[Vanicek 1967 Study]] (1964–1966):** Dinosaur National Monument sites including [[Echo Park]], [[Island Park]] and [[Split Mountain]]. Seining effort was high.

**Flow and temperature**
- The [[Jensen Gauge]] represents flow from the Yampa mouth to Ouray. The [[Greendale Gauge]] represents Green River flow from Flaming Gorge Dam to the Yampa mouth. The difference between the two was treated as Yampa input.
- Normal years show a high spring peak in May or June, followed by recession in late June and July.
- After 1967, regulated releases from Flaming Gorge Dam dominated flow in the Green River above the Yampa, and cold releases lowered water temperatures.

## Key Findings

**1. Reproductive success by year (1975–1979, Jensen–Ouray)**
- Good success in 1975, 1976, 1978 and 1979.
- Poor success in 1977. No 1977 year class was found in 1978, and only one 5-inch specimen from that year class was collected in May 1979.
- The report concludes that 1977 was in fact a very poor reproductive year, not an artifact of low sampling effort.

**2. The 1977 anomaly was flow-driven**
- Most years had natural flows. 1977 lacked the usual high spring peak because of very little Yampa runoff.
- In 1977 the Green River supplied most flow at Jensen, except during May and June, when about half the flow was Yampa water.
- The authors conclude that the Yampa is critical to squawfish success and that major reductions in its spring flow would be very harmful.

**3. Historical reproduction in Dinosaur National Monument (1962–1966)**
- Squawfish spawned in the Monument in all years from 1962 to 1966, but success was lowest in 1965.
- Revised analysis suggests 1962 was a good year. Some age I fish in 1964 may have been age II, and the 1962 class appeared as age III fish in 1965 (4 individuals) and the 1963 class in 1966 (5 individuals).
- No evidence was found that 1965 was a good year. Vanicek's effort in 1965 was similar to other years.

**4. Cold water, not just flow, explains 1965**
- April, May and June temperatures at Jensen were colder in 1965 than in 1963, 1964 or 1966.
- Cold ambient conditions delayed runoff, and large April releases from Flaming Gorge Dam added to the cold.
- Days above 60°F by June 30 were 17 in 1965, fewer than any other year from 1962 to 1966.

**5. Post-1967 collapse of reproduction**
- Young squawfish have rarely been found in Dinosaur National Monument since 1966. Vanicek's 1967 Echo Park collection of 69 young was mostly yearlings. Holden found juveniles in Echo Park in 1968 but very few in 1969 and none in 1970 or 1971.
- Reproductive success apparently collapsed around 1967–1968, coinciding with a collapse of the trophy trout fishery below Flaming Gorge Dam.
- Holden and Crist (1978) linked these declines to increased regulated flows from Flaming Gorge since 1967, which produced colder water that warmed more slowly downstream.
- Water release data show that post-1966 monthly releases were higher than pre-dam flows in all months except February, March and April. Released water was also 1–2°C colder in June–August.

**6. Squawfish reproduction elsewhere**
- Young-of-the-year were common in the lower Green River in Canyonlands National Park in 1970 and 1971 under normal flows, and yearlings were common between Ouray and Gray Canyon.
- In 1977 no young-of-the-year were found between Ouray and Gray Canyon. They were found about 25 miles upstream from Green River, which supports poor 1977 success in the reach above Gray Canyon.

**7. Other rare fishes**
- Bonytail chub appear to have disappeared from the Green River system from the early 1960s, corresponding with Flaming Gorge Dam closure. Loss of reproduction and flow are suspected causes. Only 36 adults were collected in Holden's four-year study, and only three possible juvenile bonytails were found in Vanicek's 1964–1966 collections.
- Humpback chub and razorback sucker depend on large rivers. Few are found in tributaries such as the Yampa compared with the mainstem Green and Colorado.
- No young-of-the-year squawfish have ever been found in the Yampa above its mouth, even though they reproduced in the Green in 1963, when the Green was almost 100% Yampa water.

**8. Temperature versus flow**
- Temperature was not the cause of poor success in 1977. Temperatures in 1977 were intermediate, and 1977 had about as many days above 15°C as other years.
- Temperature is still a factor. It can limit spawning even when flows are adequate.

**9. Conclusions and management implications**
- Natural spring flows from the Yampa are highly correlated with squawfish reproductive success in the upper Green River.
- Regulating the Yampa would likely have serious consequences for squawfish reproduction. Regulated flows may be lower than squawfish need, and water temperatures at lower flows are more variable.
- Combined regulation of the Green and Yampa could be disastrous, and coordinating dams on both rivers to produce normal flow and temperature patterns would likely undermine hydropower potential.
- Recent inlet modifications at Flaming Gorge Dam are expected to improve squawfish reproduction in Dinosaur National Monument. Regulating the Yampa would likely negate this benefit.
