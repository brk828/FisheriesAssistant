# Elopid Fish from the Codell Sandstone of Kansas and Evolutionary Lines in Reptilian Tooth Replacement

#ichthyology #marine-biology #elopidae #paleontology #herpetology

## Executive Summary

This Transactions of the Kansas Academy of Science note (Vol. 61, No. 2, 1958) combines two separate studies. The first, by [[Halsey W. Miller]] Jr., describes an incomplete, undetermined elopid fish from the [[Codell Sandstone]] (Upper Cretaceous) of Ellis County, Kansas, and interprets the sandstone as a near-shore littoral deposit of the regressing [[Carlile Shale]] sea. The second, by [[Hobart M. Smith]], is a theoretical review of tooth attachment and replacement in reptiles and its possible bearing on the origin of mammalian dentition. The excerpt ends mid-argument in the second paper.

## Background & Description

**Fossil specimen.** [[Edward E. Brookless]] of Hays, Kansas, found the fish in 1954 at the NE 1/4, Sec. 29, T. 11 S., R. 18 W. It was donated to the [[Fort Hays Kansas State College Museum]] and catalogued as specimen 6993-2. The fossil is preserved as an internal cast and an external cast. Scales and opercular plates are attached to the external cast. The caudal third of the body is missing, so the genus cannot be determined.

**Diagnostic traits of Elopidae.** Elopids are large, herring-like fishes with a fusiform trunk and a relatively short caudal region. Their parietals are large and meet along the midline of the skull. The supraoccipital is not prominent. Both maxilla and premaxilla are present. Small teeth occur on the jaws, parasphenoid, glossohyal and pharyngeal bones. Some species have enlarged axillary scales.

**Living relatives.** [[Megalops]] species occur in the East Indies and South Seas. The [[Tarpon]] inhabits near-shore Atlantic waters and enters rivers and bays, and anglers have caught it in inland fresh water. The [[Ten Pounder]] lives in the open Atlantic and also in coastal rivers and inlets.

**Reptilian tooth replacement.** Smith contrasts primitive vertebrates (pleurodont, polyphyodont, polyodont, with polymodal replacement) with mammals (thecodont, diphyodont, oligodont, with unimodal replacement). He argues that several living and extinct reptile groups show the intermediate conditions that may have produced these mammalian features.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment character.** Moss (1932) describes the Codell Sandstone as uniformly fine (average 0.4 mm) angular quartz grains with fragments of altered feldspar and small fish teeth and bones. Most grains are slightly frosted and pitted. Heavy minerals are zircon (most abundant), tourmaline, garnet, rutile and staurolite, with traces of anatase, chlorite, muscovite, corundum and topaz.

**Stratigraphy.** The Codell Sandstone lies at the top of the Carlile Shale (Colorado Group) and ranges from a feather-edge to about 25 feet thick. Locally it is represented by silty shale and thin sand laminae. It is disconformably overlain by the Fort Hays member of the [[Niobrara Formation]]. It grades laterally into the Carlile Shale and is a time equivalent of part of the upper Carlile.

**Depositional interpretation.** Miller weighs littoral against eolian origin. Fine grain size, rounding, pitting of resistant grains, and marine fish remains favor a littoral deposit of the regressing [[Western Interior Seaway]]. Some grains may have come from nearby sand dunes. The faunal and depositional break between the Niobrara and Carlile indicates a regression or relative fall in sea level in the Western Interior during that interval.

**Palaeoenvironment of the fish.** Because living elopids inhabit near-shore marine and fresh water, Miller infers that the Codell elopid lived in a similar setting. No other identifiable fossils have been recovered from the Codell Sandstone in Kansas to date.

## Key Findings

- **Specimen status:** Elopid fish FHSC 6993-2 is well preserved but incomplete, lacking the caudal third. It is assigned only to an undetermined genus within [[Elopidae]].
- **Age and setting:** The Codell Sandstone is Upper Cretaceous, lies atop the Carlile Shale, and reaches about 25 feet in thickness.
- **Environment:** Grain size (average 0.4 mm), rounding and pitting, plus the marine fish remains, support a littoral origin. Eolian input is considered possible but secondary.
- **Sea-level change:** The Niobrara–Carlile break reflects a regression of the Western Interior sea, with a probable hiatus between Codell and Fort Hays deposition.
- **Ecological inference:** The Codell elopid probably tolerated near-shore marine and brackish-to-fresh conditions, like its living relatives.
- **Reptilian replacement model (Smith):** Vertebrates ancestrally replace teeth in multiple waves. In each wave, every second to sixth tooth is replaced nearly simultaneously along the jaw, and the cycle continues posteriorly. This avoids large toothless gaps.
- **Mammalian contrast (Smith):** Mammals show thecodont attachment, diphyodonty, oligodonty, and a single replacement wave along the full jaw length. Smith proposes that reptilian intermediate conditions helped produce these features, a link he says had not been stated explicitly before, especially for tooth anchorage.
- **Limitations:** The fish study rests on a single incomplete specimen with no other Codell fossils reported. The reptile paper is a conceptual review, and the excerpt ends before its full argument.
