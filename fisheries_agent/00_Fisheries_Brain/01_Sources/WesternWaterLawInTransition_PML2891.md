# Western Water Law in Transition

#freshwater-ecology #water-law #hydrology

## Executive Summary

This essay by [[Charles F. Wilkinson]] traces how western United States water law developed from the [[Gold Rush]] mining camps to the present and argues that the system is in transition. It describes how [[Prior Appropriation Doctrine]] and [[Riparian Doctrine]] shaped allocation, how [[Winters Doctrine]] rights for Indian tribes create senior claims, and how a "[[Iron Triangle]]" of interests long controlled federal subsidies and state water distribution. The author identifies new pressures (budget limits, population growth, groundwater depletion, pollution, and environmental values) that are pushing policy toward federal involvement, conservation, and public-interest controls. The essay is a commentary rather than a field study. It contains no fish or aquatic-species data, and its quantitative content concerns water budgets, withdrawals, and consumption.

## Background & Description

- **Origins:** Mining camps during the [[California Gold Rush]] needed assured water supplies and had no legal framework. Miners developed their own customs, which Congress endorsed in the [[Mining Act of 1866]]. Prior appropriation ("first in time, first in right") was adopted because water was scarce and had to be diverted to be used.
- **Riparian contrast:** Eastern and English [[Riparian Doctrine]] gave landowners adjoining a stream shared rights and protected the watercourse as an integrated unit. Western settlers largely rejected this rule because it did not fit arid conditions.
- **Federal role:** The [[Reclamation Act of 1902]] authorized federal irrigation projects. The [[Bureau of Reclamation]] supplies a large share of western water, and special water districts supply about half of irrigation water. These districts often use acreage-based voting (one acre, one vote) and tax-exempt bonds.
- **Winters Doctrine:** In the 1908 [[Winters v. United States]] decision, the Supreme Court held that Indian water rights were implicitly reserved when reservations were created, regardless of state-law diversion requirements. Indian rights are therefore senior in most western basins. A report for the [[Western Governors Association]] estimated these rights could total more than 45 million acre-feet per year (about 55 billion m³), over three times the annual flow of the [[Colorado River]]. One acre-foot equals 325,000 gallons.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Groundwater and surface water.** Post-World War II high-lift pumping allowed large groundwater reserves to be exploited. The author notes that many [[Groundwater Aquifers]] are not renewable and can be "mined out." Because groundwater is hydrologically linked to surface water, the author argues that separate management fails and that [[Conjunctive Management]] is required. The [[Ogallala Aquifer]] is cited as an interstate example where shortages have forced valuable farmland out of production.

**Water budget (1973 U.S. Water Resources Council data).** The source summarizes the [[National Water Budget]] for the conterminous United States. Key points:
- About one-third of precipitation accumulates as ground or surface water storage, the only portion developable for beneficial use (1,450 billion gallons per day, bgd, as stated).
- Two-thirds evaporates quickly and is not available for use.
- Accounting for seasonal and drought variability, less than half of the potential supply would be available in most years (675 bgd).
- In 1975, withdrawals totaled less than 60 percent of potential supply (about 85 percent fresh and 15 percent saline sources).
- Irrigation is the largest user, with about 54 percent of withdrawals consumed. Agriculture accounts for over 90 percent of total consumption.
- Municipal consumption was about 24 percent of withdrawals; commercial and manufacturing consumption about 13 percent.

Several figures in this budget table are garbled by OCR, so specific per-category totals are not reported here.

**Irrigation waste.** The [[Soil Conservation Service]] estimated annual irrigation waste at 24 million acre-feet (about 30 billion m³). The author states that this loss is nearly double the Colorado River's annual flow and exceeds the total consumption of all municipalities and industries nationally. Some "waste" returns to streams as downstream return flow, which complicates its identification.

**Consumption and withdrawal (Census data, 1980 vs. 1985).** Per-capita withdrawal is given as 1,953 gallons per day (1980) and 1,650 gallons per day (1985). Total consumption figures are partly illegible but appear to be about 100 bgd (1980) and 95 bgd (1985).

**Instream flows and public trust.** Most western legislatures recognize recreation and wildlife as beneficial uses, and many states have established [[Instream Flows]] that keep water in streams for fish, wildlife, recreation, and aesthetics. The author notes these programs are still weak and need improvement. The federal [[Endangered Species Act]] requires sufficient flows to protect listed species, and [[Salmon and Steelhead Management]] in the Northwest has affected water allocation.

## Key Findings

**1. Federal presence is unavoidable and growing.** The author argues federal involvement is driven by interstate conflicts (state borders rarely match river basins), treaties with tribes, Canada, and Mexico, and not primarily by subsidies, which are expected to decline. Future federal projects will likely involve more local cost-sharing, smaller subsidies, and greater attention to [[Indian Water Rights]]. Few new projects will proceed without an Indian component.

**2. The "Iron Triangle" shaped past policy.** The author describes an alliance of private western development interests and state agencies, federal water agencies, and western-controlled congressional subcommittees. This arrangement produced heavily subsidized federal projects with water distributed under state law, favoring consumptive commodity users. Federal funding became "almost ritualistic."

**3. Conservation is the central policy tool.** The author expects conservation to be the most important development in state and federal policy. Water should be obtained mainly by reducing waste in existing uses rather than building new projects, and interbasin transfers should be limited to cost-effective schemes. Incentives such as tax breaks, low-interest loans, and subsidies should be combined with compulsion, especially [[Water Pricing Reform]] to end artificially low prices.

**4. Environmental values are reshaping water law.** Recreation and wildlife are increasingly accepted as beneficial uses. The essay highlights several cases:
- **[[Mono Lake Case]] (National Audubon Society v. Superior Court):** The California Supreme Court held that Los Angeles diversions were subject to the [[Public Trust Doctrine]], which had always protected Mono Lake and must be weighed against consumptive rights. The court said environmental factors must be considered alongside appropriative rights.
- **[[Imperial Irrigation District]] decision (1994):** The State Water Control Board found the district had violated California's constitutional and statutory bans on waste. The district was found to have allowed more than 1 million acre-feet to flow to the [[Salton Sea]] as return flow. It diverts about 2.9 million acre-feet annually, about 10 percent of the river's average yield. The board ordered a plan to control losses. A possible agreement with the [[Metropolitan Water District]] would fund conservation in exchange for salvaged water.
- **[[Sporhase v. Nebraska]] (1982):** The Supreme Court held that interstate commerce can override a state's ban on water exports, stating that states regulate water but do not own it, calling state ownership "a legal fiction."

**5. Montana and Arizona show emerging models.**
- **[[Montana Public Interest Legislation]] (1985):** Added public-interest criteria to the state water code, with especially rigorous criteria for diversions over 4,000 acre-feet. It created a limited water-leasing program and broke from laissez-faire prior appropriation.
- **[[Fort Peck-Montana Compact]] (1985):** A state-tribal water rights settlement with the Assiniboine and Sioux tribes, supported by the [[Montana Reserved Water Rights Compact Commission]], the only state effort to institutionalize such negotiations. Some tribes oppose quantifying their future Winters rights, while others prefer negotiated settlement over litigation.
- **[[Arizona Groundwater Management Act]] (1980):** Described as the most far-reaching state water conservation statute. It requires management plans to bring groundwater withdrawal down to the rate of recharge by 2025, creates a state regulatory agency, sets mandatory water duties for farms, funds land retirement, bars new irrigated acres in designated areas, and imposes "groundwater withdrawal fees."

**6. Prior appropriation has failed in key respects.** The author concludes that the doctrine encourages waste, that its "guarantee to use whatever amount of water was diverted" reflects an era of plenty, and that its first-in-time priorities produce a rush among states and users. Unchecked, it can drain streams, and the market has not protected fish, wildlife, or the environment. Public-interest limits on private water rights are increasingly accepted, much as land-use regulation has limited absolute property rights.

**7. Transition and outlook.** The essay concludes that the central issue for coming years is achieving a fair accommodation between traditional uses and modern values. It notes that reconceptualizing water as "conservation" and "water use" now includes rafters, trout fishermen, and artists as legitimate water users alongside a coal-fired plant. The author expects these shifts to grow into a legal structure that better reflects contemporary values.
