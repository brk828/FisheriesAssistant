# Aquatic Nematocerous Diptera of Tom Wallace Lake, Kentucky

#limnology #entomology #diptera #chironomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 taxonomic and ecological study, the second in a series on a Kentucky knobs lake, documents the immature stages (and some adults) of aquatic nematocerous Diptera from [[Tom Wallace Lake]], a small impoundment near [[Louisville Kentucky]]. Fifteen species were recorded from bottom sediments over 35 months of sampling, plus adults reared or taken at light. The paper aims to provide identification keys and baseline data so that future faunal changes as the lake ages can be detected. The author, Stuart E. Neff of the University of Louisville, treats the three families as separate taxonomic groups and notes that many generic names were in flux at the time.

## Background & Description

Benthic fauna are central to estimates of lacustrine productivity, and the study argues that precise identification of all life stages is needed for quantitative benthic work and for autecological studies of aquatic insects. Nematocerous fly larvae dominate the macrobenthos of the lake. Other taxa noted in the littoral zone include megalopterans (*Sialis* sp.), gastropods (*Physa*), naiads of Odonata and Ephemeroptera, and oligochaetes of the genus *Dero*, which contribute little to total benthic biomass.

Three families were represented:
- **[[Culicidae]]** (subfamily Chaoborinae), with one species, [[Chaoborus Punctipennis]].
- **[[Chironomidae]]** (listed as Tendipedidae in the paper), the most diverse group, with 13 species across several subfamilies and tribes.
- **[[Ceratopogonidae]]** (listed as Heleidae), with one species, *Palpomyia* sp.

The paper gives larval, pupal and adult diagnostic characters for each species. Key taxonomic features include mandible and paralabial plate structure, antennal ring organs, anal gills, pupal respiratory organs and spurs, and wing venation (for example, the length of the Cu fork petiole).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** An [[Ekman Dredge]] covering 36 square inches collected bottom samples, which were screened through a 40-mesh sieve. Collections were irregular, from September 1951 to July 1954. No sampling occurred in August or January. In summer 1954 a light was used to collect winged adults along the shore. Several adults were also reared from larvae and pupae in the laboratory.

**Depth zonation.** Species distributions were tied to the littoral, [[Sublittoral Zone]] and [[Profundal Zone]] and to the basin slope and isobaths:
- *Chaoborus punctipennis* was found throughout the year in profundal sediments. It was rarely found above the 4 m isobath.
- *Procladius culiciformis* was most numerous at sublittoral depths of about 4 m, but also occurred in profundal samples.
- *Tendipes decorus* and *T. plumosus* were common in the upper profundal and sublittoral zones, rarely deeper than 6.5 m or higher than the 3.5 m isobath.
- *Palpomyia* sp. was most abundant at about 3 m, with only two July specimens from 6.5 m and none deeper than 5 m otherwise.

**Behavior and physiology.** Chaoborid larvae show a [[Diel Vertical Migration]], rising from bottom ooze at night to feed on zooplankton. Some were found in subsurface samples at 5 to 7 m during the day. Many chironomid larvae contain erythrocruorin, a hemoglobin-like pigment that gives them their red color and the name "blood-worms." Lindeman (1942) reported that *T. decorus* and *T. plumosus* tolerate [[Anaerobic Conditions]] well.

**Life history notes.** Many *Procladius culiciformis* larvae (2nd and 3rd instars) were found in ooze on November 22, 1952. The author suggests that half-grown larvae overwinter in the sediments and emerge in spring. Published work cited for this species reports two generations per year in a bog lake in Minnesota. The larvae of *Cricotopus trifasciatus* mine spongy plant tissue, which is consistent with their absence from dredge samples.

## Key Findings

1. **Species richness:** Fifteen species of aquatic nematocerous Diptera were dredged from Tom Wallace Lake sediments over 35 months. Three families and five subfamilies were represented. Thirteen species were tendipedids, with one culicid and one heleid.

2. **Dominant species:** *[[Chaoborus Punctipennis]]*, *[[Procladius Culiciformis]]* and *[[Tendipes Decorus]]* were the numerically predominant species. *Chaoborus* was the most abundant dipteran larva. Profundal regions held more than 55,000 individuals per square meter at times.

3. **Chaoborus distribution:** The larvae are not strictly benthic. Their daily migration into the water column means that bottom samples underestimate their true role in the lake.

4. **Procladius ecology:** *P. culiciformis* was the most abundant tanypod in littoral and sublittoral areas and occurred in more deep profundal samples than either *Tendipes* species, which are usually considered typical of profundal communities.

5. **Tendipes separation:** *[[Tendipes Decorus]]* and *[[Tendipes Plumosus]]* are very similar as larvae and adults. The paper proposes separating larvae by the color of the basal mandibular tooth (pale in *T. decorus*, dark in *T. plumosus*), but flags this character as needing further verification. Johannsen's length-based criterion (18 mm) was judged unreliable.

6. **Procladius pusillus:** One specimen was reared from a 1954 profundal larva, and smaller adults collected at light were tentatively assigned to this species. The author notes that the identifications are uncertain.

7. **Taxonomic revision:** The paper notes that Townes (1945) raised many subgenera to generic rank, which complicates names and identifications. Valid names and familiar synonyms are given throughout.

8. **Monitoring baseline:** The stated purpose is to document the fauna so that later workers can detect changes as the lake ages. The paper therefore serves as a faunal baseline for [[Tom Wallace Lake]] in 1951–1954.
