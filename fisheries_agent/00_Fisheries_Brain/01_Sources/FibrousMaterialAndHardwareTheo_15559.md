# Theory and Estimation of Fishing Gear

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-science #gear-selectivity #gill-nets

## Executive Summary

This document presents Professor F.I. Baranov's foundational theoretical work on fishing gear design and operation, originally published in Moscow (1948) and translated from Russian. The work comprises two main sections: Chapter 1 (Section 2) on fibrous materials and thread specifications, and Chapter 7 (Sections 42-48) on the theory of gill-net fishing. Baranov develops mathematical models for optimal mesh size selection based on fish morphology, introduces the concept of relative efficiency curves for gill nets, and establishes standardized methods for thread numeration and strength testing. The work provides empirical data from [[Caspian Sea]] expeditions and [[Lake Krugloe]] Fisheries Station experiments, establishing fundamental principles still relevant to modern fisheries gear technology.

## Background & Description

### Thread and Twine Specifications

The document establishes the metric system for thread numeration, where the thread number indicates length in metres per gram. For example, if 50 m of thread weighs 2 g, the thread is designated No. 25 (50/2 = 25). This replaced the earlier English system which used different standards for linen, hemp, and cotton materials.

Twine is characterized by a fraction indicating the thread number and strand count. A twine designated No. 24/2 consists of 2 strands of No. 24 thread. The "standard number" is obtained by reducing this fraction (No. 24/2 = No. 12 standard). The "aggregate number" (actual number) accounts for shortening during twisting: 2-5% for single-twist linen/hemp, and 20-25% for thick double-twist twine.

Moisture content affects measurements: cotton materials normally contain 8.5% moisture, while linen and hemp contain 12% compared to absolutely dry material. Experiments at [[Lake Krugloe]] showed weight variations of 3% (linen) and 3.4% (hemp) between dry winter air and moist summer conditions.

The metrical number (N) is calculated as: N = un(l/p), where u is the correction coefficient for twisting (ranging from 1.03 for single-twist hemp to 1.20 for thick double-twist cotton), l is length in metres, p is weight in grams, and n is the number of strands.

Theoretical thread diameter is calculated as: d = 1.0(N)^-0.5 mm (assuming specific gravity γ = 1.5 for linen, hemp, and cotton). For twine: single-twist d = 1.1(n/N)^0.5; double-twist d = 1.3(n/N)^0.5.

Standard strength is defined as the product r(N/n) = C, where r is breaking strength. This equals the "tear length" (R), the length of twine whose weight numerically equals its breaking strength.

### Gill-Net Theory

Gill nets operate by setting a net wall in the path of moving fish. Fish are captured through three mechanisms: (1) becoming wedged in meshes at the widest body diameter or gill covers, (2) entanglement in loosely-set netting, and (3) wrapping in net pockets formed during struggle.

The mechanical analysis considers forces at equilibrium when a fish attempts to pass through a mesh. At point O on the mesh perimeter, three forces act: (1) longitudinal force OA (fish effort), (2) pressure OB in the mesh plane (thread tension), and (3) resistance OT perpendicular to the body surface. The resultant OC must be perpendicular to the body surface for equilibrium.

Maximum pressure before the thread slides backward is: OC = OA(sin α), where α is the angle between the thread and body surface tangent. The degree of body tapering is quantified as: B = (S₂ - S₁)/c, where S₁ and S₂ are perimeters at cross-sections separated by distance c.

Fish are held most securely when angle α equals an optimal value α₀. At extreme values α₁ (too small, excessive compression allows passage) and α₂ (too large, insufficient grip allows escape), capture fails. The range α₁ < α < α₂ defines successful capture conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The work draws on extensive field data from multiple environments:

**[[Caspian Sea]] Expeditions**: Studies of [[Clupeonella brashnicovi]] (Caspian herring) fishing provided data on mesh selectivity in marine conditions. The April 3, 1913 catch data demonstrated complex selectivity patterns requiring probabilistic analysis.

**[[Lake Krugloe]] Fisheries Station**: Systematic experiments (1923-1924) on [[Rutilus rutilus]] (roach), [[Perca fluviatilis]] (perch), and [[Esox lucius]] (pike) established fundamental mesh-size relationships. Spring 1923 experiments with 24, 28, and 32 mm mesh nets yielded optimal length determinations.

**Environmental Factors**: The document acknowledges that fish condition varies with feeding conditions, sex, season (affecting gonad development and body condition), and water body characteristics. These factors influence the mesh-size/fish-length relationship.

**Species Associations**: Combined nets are designed for associated species like [[Sander]] (pickerel) and [[Acipenser stellatus]] (stellate sturgeon), reflecting ecological co-occurrence patterns.

## Key Findings

### Optimal Mesh-Size Relationships

**Thread Diameter to Mesh Bar Ratio (d:a)**:
- Highly efficient gill nets: d:a = 0.005 (used in winter ice fishing when fish are less mobile)
- Standard efficient and strong nets: d:a = 0.01 (typical commercial gill nets)
- Inefficient nets: d:a = 0.02 (fish still entangle, problematic for drag nets)
- Entanglement-preventing nets: d:a = 0.05 or greater (used in drag net central sections)

Increasing thread diameter from 0.50 to 0.75 mm (at ~45 mm bar mesh) resulted in a 3-5 fold decline in catch, demonstrating the critical importance of thread thickness.

**Mesh Bar to Fish Length Formulas**:

Three fish body-type categories were established:

1. **High-bodied fish** ([[Cyprinus carpio]], [[Abramis brama]], goldfish): a = 0.20L or a = 7.0(P)^0.333
2. **Medium-bodied fish** ([[Rutilus rutilus]], whitefish): a = 0.15L or a = 6.0(P)^0.333  
3. **Elongate fish** (pickerel, mackerel, mullet): a = 0.10L or a = 5.0(P)^0.333

Where: a = optimal bar measure (mm), L = fish length (mm), P = fish weight (g)

**Species-Specific Coefficients**:
- [[Rutilus rutilus]] (roach): a = 0.15L (confirmed by experiments showing optimal lengths of 16 cm for 24 mm mesh, 19 cm for 28 mm mesh, 21 cm for 32 mm mesh)
- [[Clupeonella brashnicovi]] (Caspian herring): a = 0.125L, K₁ = 0.12, K₂ = 5.8
- Shad (Alosa sp.): K₁ = 0.13, K₂ = 5.5

### Mechanical Capture Analysis

Student A.N. Ivanov's 1924 measurements at [[Lake Krugloe]] established that fish were captured in meshes whose perimeter was approximately 60% of body length (range: 57-64%). The relationship between greatest body perimeter and optimal mesh perimeter averaged 1.25 for both [[Rutilus rutilus]] and [[Clupeonella brashnicovi]].

Detailed observations showed captured fish typically pulled 5-10 or more meshes onto their bodies in succession. The "first mesh" that initiated capture could be isolated for laboratory analysis.

### Relative Efficiency Curves

The relative efficiency of gill nets follows a Gaussian (normal) distribution curve with equation: Y = (n/√π)(e^(-n²X²)), where X is deviation from optimal fish length and Y is capture probability.

**Key efficiency parameters**:
- Standard deviation (r) = approximately 7.5% of optimal fish length (average of 6% for roach and 8.5% for Caspian herring)
- 10% deviation from optimal length (or 35% deviation in weight) represents the practical efficiency limit for commercial fishing
- 20% length deviation reduces relative efficiency to only 2-3%, effectively eliminating capture

**Intersection point method**: When two nets of different mesh sizes are fished simultaneously, the fish length at which catch curves intersect (X₀) allows calculation of optimal lengths:
- X₁ = X₀(a₁+a₂)/2a₂
- X₂ = X₀(a₁+a₂)/2a₁

Where a₁ and a₂ are the bar measures of the two nets.

### Gear Strength Requirements

Three incompatible strength requirements were mathematically defined:

1. **Equal general strength**: d₂ = d₁(a₂/a₁)^0.5 (number of knots per unit length inversely proportional to bar size)
2. **Equal efficiency**: d₂ = d₁(a₂/a₁) (maintains constant d:a ratio)
3. **Equal local strength**: d₂ = d₁(a₂/a₁)^1.5 (resists fish effort proportional to weight/length³)

For example, comparing 30 mm and 60 mm bar nets with d = 0.3 mm baseline:
- Same general strength: d = 0.42 mm
- Same efficiency: d = 0.6 mm  
- Same local strength: d = 0.85 mm

The choice depends on fishing conditions: efficiency is prioritized for gill nets, while general strength (aN ≈ constant) is critical for trap nets where mesh entanglement is undesirable.

### Advanced Gill-Net Designs

**Frame Nets**: Incorporate "bridles" connecting head- and foot-lines at intervals, with hanging coefficient U = 0.5 or less. For 1 m height nets, 50 cm intervals use 40-50 cm bridles. The net is divided into square "windows" with loosely hanging netting, creating shallow pockets that capture fish through entanglement rather than wedging alone.

**Trammel (Three-Mesh) Nets**: Consist of small-mesh inner wall between two large-mesh outer walls, all on common head- and foot-lines. Inner wall twine is 3-4 times thinner than outer walls. Outer wall height is 20-50% less than inner wall, creating loose hanging. Fish pass through the first outer wall, wedge in the inner wall, and drag it through the second outer wall, forming an escape-proof pocket.

Comparative experiments at [[Lake Krugloe]] showed trammel nets and standard gill nets (both 25 mm mesh, No. 200/6 twine) caught equal numbers and sizes of [[Rutilus rutilus]], but trammel nets required twice the time for fish removal, proving unprofitable under those conditions. However, trammel nets are advantageous for fast-moving species like [[Esox lucius]] (pike) in autumn, allowing use of 2-3 times thicker twine while maintaining efficiency.

**Combined Nets**: Two or three gill nets of different mesh sizes on common side-lines, designed for associated species (e.g., pickerel and [[Acipenser stellatus]]). Fish of appropriate size wedge in the first net encountered; smaller fish pass through to be captured in finer mesh. Single-wall combined nets use small mesh in upper section and large mesh below (or vice versa for bottom nets), exploiting vertical distribution patterns.

### Experimental Design Requirements

For reliable mesh selectivity studies:
- Nets must have uniform bar measure, accurately measured
- Twine material and thickness should correspond to bar size (d:a ratio)
- Bar differences between compared nets should be approximately 15% (not too large or too small)
- Nets should be set alternately for reliability
- Minimum 500 fish per species per net type required for statistical validity
- Nets should be identical in length, height, and setting depth
- Faulty catches (entangled nets, incorrect setting) must be rejected
- Pooling catches from different sets is undesirable as it devalues reliable data

**Temporal heterogeneity problems**: Sequential passage of different-sized schools can bias results. Small fish clog small-mesh nets first, reducing efficiency for later-arriving large fish, while large-mesh nets remain effective. Prolonged fishing allows progressive clogging of all mesh sizes, distorting catch ratios while maintaining normal length distributions within catches.
