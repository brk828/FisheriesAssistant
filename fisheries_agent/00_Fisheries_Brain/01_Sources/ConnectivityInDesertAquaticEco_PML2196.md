# Connectivity in Desert Aquatic Ecosystems: The Devils Hole Story

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This conference paper by Alan C. Riggs (U.S. Geological Survey) and James E. Deacon (University of Nevada, Las Vegas) uses [[Devils Hole]], a water-filled fissure in the Mojave Desert, as a case study of how desert groundwater, geology, climate, cultural values and law intersect. The authors reconstruct the geologic origin of the Hole, its hydrologic connection to the [[Ash Meadows Flow System]], and the history of the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*), which lives in the smallest habitat known to harbor an entire vertebrate species. The paper argues that the Hole opened to the surface about 60,000 years ago, that the pupfish's colonization pathway remains unexplained, and that pupfish survival depends on a small area of shallow shelf habitat. Groundwater pumping in the late 1960s and early 1970s triggered a landmark [[Water Rights]] case decided by the [[U.S. Supreme Court]] in 1976, and the authors note the population has since declined for reasons not fully understood.

## Background & Description

- **Setting:** Devils Hole is a skylight to the water table in one of the harshest North American deserts. It is a tectonic fissure in Paleozoic carbonate rock, part of a corridor of contiguous carbonate section extending south from east-central Nevada through the [[Spring Mountains]].
- **Water chemistry:** Water is supersaturated with calcium carbonate, which precipitates on the walls as mammillary calcite (underwater), folia (at the water table), and flowstone (above water). These morphologies record past water-table levels.
- **Pupfish biology:** The Devils Hole pupfish has small body size, no pelvic fins, a rounded caudal fin, reduced sexual dimorphism and no male territorial behavior. Its diverged lineage is dated to roughly 0.2 to 0.6 Ma by [[Smith et al. 2002]] (a range the authors consider likely overestimated).
- **Taxonomic context:** Four pupfish species occur in the Death Valley/Ash Meadows region, plus the Owens pupfish nearby. Genetic work separates two lineages, one linked to the [[Lower Colorado River]] and Owens Valley pupfishes and the other to Death Valley/Ash Meadows forms.
- **Cultural context:** The Nevada Springs culture occupied the area more than 9,000 years ago. The Timbisha Shoshone hold traditional stories of "waterbabies" at the pool. The 1849 Death Valley 49ers recorded the site.
- **Legal and policy context:** Devils Hole was reserved as a 16-hectare (40-acre) portion of [[Death Valley National Monument]] by Presidential Proclamation No. 2961 (1952). The [[National Park Service Organic Act]] (1916) frames management goals.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical conditions**
- Water temperature is nearly constant at about 33.5 to 34 °C throughout most of the cavern system; dissolved oxygen is about 2.3 to 2.6 mg/l.
- Over the inner shallow shelf, temperature and DO fluctuate widely. The authors report DO rising from about 2.5 mg/l in the morning to as much as 9.5 mg/l in the afternoon, and diel temperature swings of 31.5 to 35 °C.
- Narrow fissure geometry limits sunlight. Direct sunlight reaches the water from mid-February to late November. Primary productivity from direct light ranged from about 35 mg O₂/m²/hr in April to 5 mg O₂/m²/hr in October (Wilson et al. 2001).

**Energy inputs**
- Annual energy fixation from direct light is about three times that from indirect light.
- Windblown organic input (about 39 g C/m²/yr) exceeds energy from sunlight (about 24 g C/m²/yr), and is especially important in winter.

**Shallow shelf**
- The shallow shelf, a breakdown block, is the key pupfish habitat. Its shallow water supports most spawning and primary productivity.

**Water-table history**
- Water level in Devils Hole has declined over time, with a net downward trend. Browns Room records show the water has never risen high enough to overflow the lip in the last 116,000 years, but the authors caution that this is not proof.

**Human impacts**
- Irrigation pumping in the late 1960s lowered the water level to -1.19 m (September 20, 1972). Only 13% of the shelf substrate was then submerged, and water volume over the shelf fell to 5% of the 1967 level.

## Key Findings

1. **Opening date:** Devils Hole most likely opened to the surface about 60,000 years ago, when mammillary calcite precipitation abruptly stopped after roughly 500,000 years of continuous deposition.
2. **Colonization gap:** No surface-water connection between Devils Hole and nearby springs has been found since opening, so the route by which pupfish entered remains unexplained. The authors note that pupfish are not known to tolerate subterranean conditions.
3. **Water-table record:** Using Szabo et al. (1994), the water table has never exceeded about 9 m of the 17 m needed to overflow in the last 116,000 years.
4. **Legal outcome:** The 1974 Ninth Circuit ruling and the 1976 U.S. Supreme Court decision in *Cappaert v. United States* upheld a federal reserved water right sufficient to preserve the pool's scientific value. The District Court (December 22, 1977) set the minimum level at -2.7 m below the copper washer, and the Supreme Court's framework permits a level up to -0.37 m.
5. **Population trends:** Population counts from 1972 to 2003 (Table 2) show a 1977 to 1996 mean population from the compiled counts. In 2002, pupfish showed no summer increase for the first time in 30 years. Since the mid-1990s, the annual maximum has dropped sharply.
6. **Reproductive timing:** Peak spawning is February to May. Gustafson and Deacon (1998) found more than 50% of larval production on the inner one-third of the shallow shelf in 1996 and 1997, and only about 13% on the outer third.
7. **Temperature thresholds:** Saratoga Springs pupfish (*Cyprinodon nevadensis*) eggs hatched at only about 10% under 36 °C. Devils Hole's thermal environment is near or above the physiological limits for oogenesis.
8. **Diet:** Stomach contents are dominated by calcium carbonate crystals, which were ingested to consume diatoms. Macroinvertebrates per stomach rose from about 2 in summer to about 6 in winter.
9. **Algal shift:** Dominance on the shallow shelf shifted from *Spirogyra* to a yellow-green *Oscillatoria* mat in 2002. The authors suspect it reduced spawning success. A 2003 flood disturbance broke up the mat, and the population rose from July to September 2003.
10. **Shelf occupancy:** The share of the population on the shallow shelf during February to April fell from 49 to 68% (1974 to 1993) to 26 to 34% (1993 to 2003).
11. **Management implications:** Possible causes include the observation platform's shading, upgradient groundwater pumping in the Ash Meadows flow system, and climate-driven water-table decline. The authors state that the District Court of Nevada can order corrections.
