# Fish Without a River: Captive Breeding and Reintroduction of Endangered Fishes in Western North America

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This analytic essay examines captive breeding and reintroduction programs for endangered native fishes in [[Western North America]], focusing on two contrasting groups: long-lived, large-bodied riverine species ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail]], [[Razorback Sucker]]) and short-lived marshland species ([[Sonoran Topminnow]], [[Desert Pupfish]]). The authors argue that while captive fish programs are biologically successful due to high fecundities and established culture techniques, practical and political obstacles threaten the long-term survival of these species. The paper reviews the unique aquatic habitats of the American West, describes the native fish faunas that evolved within them, and discusses the biological, methodological, and sociopolitical challenges facing conservation efforts in the region.

## Background & Description

### Riverine Species

The [[Colorado River]] historically flowed 2700 km through alternating canyons and basins, dropping 4.2 km in elevation from headwaters to the [[Sea of Cortez]]. Natural discharge fluctuations ranged from near zero to almost 7000 m³/second at Yuma, Arizona. Water temperatures ranged from 0°C to >32°C, and the river averaged >0.62% silt content, carrying an annual average of 1.64×10⁸ metric tons of sediment to the sea.

Native big-river fishes evolved unique morphological adaptations to turbulent, sediment-rich conditions:

- **[[Humpback Chub]] (Gila cypha)** and **[[Razorback Sucker]] (Xyrauchen texanus)**: Developed humped backs, presumably acting as hydrofoils for positioning in currents
- **[[Bonytail]] (Gila elegans)**: Evolved thin caudal peduncles and large falcate fins for explosive propulsion through rapids
- **[[Colorado Squawfish]] (Ptychocheilus lucius)**: Largest native North American minnow, reaching 1.8 m length and 45 kg weight; top carnivore in riverine habitat

All species attain large size (≥40 cm total length) and are known or suspected to live to great age. [[Razorback Sucker]] lives at least 44 years, [[Bonytail]] to 49 years, and [[Colorado Squawfish]] approximately 30 years.

### Marshland Species

Three basic types of marshy wetlands exist in the arid west:
1. High-elevation montane wetlands/meadowlands
2. Low-elevation riverine marshes with cattail, giant reeds, and tall sedges
3. True cienegas at intermediate elevations with low-statured sedges

**[[Sonoran Topminnow]] (Poeciliopsis occidentalis)**: Small live-bearing fish endemic to southwestern United States and northwestern Mexico below ~1400 m elevation. Two subspecies (P. o. occidentalis in north, P. o. sonoriensis in south) are morphologically and genetically distinct. Northern subspecies now restricted to isolated Arizona localities; southern subspecies remains relatively widespread in Mexico.

**[[Desert Pupfish]] (Cyprinodon macularius)**: Originally distributed throughout marshes, cienegas, and stream margins, also achieving large populations in highly saline, seasonally hot-water habitats (salt content >2× seawater, temperatures >38°C). Populations in [[Gila River]] basin essentially gone by 1950s.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Degradation

Since 1900, the [[Colorado River]]'s natural variability has been systematically controlled. Ten major dams and water manipulation structures were constructed since 1913 on the mainstream and major tributaries. Key impacts include:

- Regular periodicity replacing wide natural variation
- Nutrient capture by phyto- and zooplankton in reservoirs
- Sediment deposition in reservoirs (only 65% of sediments passing through [[Grand Canyon]] 1925-1935 reached Yuma)
- Channel incision and backwater drainage below dams
- Elimination of natural flood-drought cycles
- Altered water temperatures and flow patterns
- Curtailment of upstream migrations and downstream larval drift

### Life History Strategies

**Long-lived riverine species** display K-selected characteristics:
- Sexual maturity: 2-5+ years
- Fecundity: 1000-2000 ova per cm body length ([[Razorback Sucker]])
- Reproductive lifespan: decades
- Strategy allows missing multiple years between successful reproduction

**Short-lived marshland species** display r-selected characteristics:
- Life expectancy: <1 year average
- Seasonal population oscillations (lowest late winter/early spring, peak autumn)
- Relatively few young per female, but extended reproductive period
- Young mature in weeks and enter reproductive population
- Vulnerable to rapid extinction spirals during second winter under stress

### Threats to Native Fishes

1. **Habitat modification**: Drastic changes in discharge, temperature regimes, loss of natural habitats
2. **Introduced species**: 
   - [[Mosquitofish]] (Gambusia affinis): Predation on [[Sonoran Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]]
   - African cichlids (Tilapia spp.) and [[Sailfin Molly]] (Poecilia latipinna): Competition in saline refugia
   - Increased predation, competition for food/space, disease/parasitism
3. **Flow depletion and groundwater pumping**: Loss of marshlands and cienegas
4. **Stream incision**: Drainage of water tables supporting wetlands

## Key Findings

### Terminology and Rationale for Reintroduction

The authors distinguish three approaches:
- **Introduction**: Establishment where species never previously occurred (high ecological risk but may be necessary when historical habitat no longer exists)
- **Restocking/Supplemental stocking**: Artificial population density increase within natural habitat
- **Reintroduction**: Establishment in areas of historical extirpation

The [[IUCN]] (1984) states reintroduction is appropriate only where original extinction causes have been removed and habitat requirements satisfied. However, this standard is "frequently and necessarily abrogated" for Western fishes, as rectifying habitat deterioration would require removing major dams ([[Hoover Dam]], [[Glen Canyon Dam]], [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]), which is "socially abhorrent and unacceptable."

### Rationale for Reintroduction Programs

1. **Philosophical/cultural**: Species associated with humankind since prehistoric times are part of native peoples' culture and history
2. **Ecological**: Restores ecosystem components in fragile desert environments
3. **Educational**: Reestablishment of natural diversity enhances area values, teaching potential, and research opportunities
4. **Public relations**: Attracts lay public, demonstrates environmental fund usage
5. **Economic**: Native game species can improve habitat productivity and stimulate public interest

### Captive Breeding Challenges

**Long-lived species** require:
- Substantial facilities for large fish
- Secure hatchery with adequate water supply (e.g., [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico)
- Long-term maintenance of broodstocks (decades)
- Patience for environmental cues that may never recur in altered systems

**Short-lived species** require:
- Constant surveillance to avoid catastrophic population crashes
- Rapid response capability
- Understanding that populations can "rapidly disappear during the second winter, in a rapid spiral toward extinction"

### Current Status of Target Species

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: Restricted to upper basin (Yampa, Green, Colorado rivers); not collected downstream from [[Lake Powell]] since early 1960s
- **[[Humpback Chub]]**: Limited to few tens of kilometers in deep, canyon-bound segments; presumably abundant in canyons now drowned by impoundments
- **[[Bonytail]]**: One of most endangered species in North America; fewer than 10 wild individuals seen since 1978
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Occurs sporadically in small numbers in upper basin; persists as large adults in lower basin reservoirs like [[Lake Mohave]]
- **[[Sonoran Topminnow]]**: Northern subspecies restricted to isolated Arizona localities; southern subspecies relatively widespread in Mexico
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]**: [[Gila River]] basin populations essentially extinct by 1950s; severe habitat refugia invaded by tolerant non-natives

### Critical Conclusions

The authors argue that "protection of native fishes in the American west is no longer constrained by lack of accumulated scientific knowledge." Ichthyologists and fishery managers understand the biological problem, and knowledge gaps can be filled quickly. **What is lacking is "simply the will to support the endangered species mandate."**

The fundamental question is "whether endangered fish species, located on state and federal lands in the American west, will be fully protected, as decreed by law, through exercise of authority by responsible federal agencies." If responsibilities are abrogated, "extinction of the native will be assured."

### Success Factors for Fish Programs

Captive fish programs are frequently successful because:
- Fishes display high fecundities
- Produce abundant, precocious progeny over short periods
- Have been successfully cultured for hundreds of years
- Are focus of population genetic studies evaluating production potential, genetic variability, and stock-environment matching

However, reintroduction success requires:
- Extensive planning and facility development
- Production and transport equipment
- Identification of suitable reintroduction habitats
- Legislative agreements (complex and time-consuming)
- Years of post-release monitoring to confirm reproductive population establishment
- Long-term resource guarantees

The authors emphasize that "it is a mistake to equate the mere release of animals with their reintroduction. The release of an organism signals only the beginning of its testing time."
