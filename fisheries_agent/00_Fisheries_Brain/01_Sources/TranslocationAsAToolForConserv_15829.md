# Translocation as a Tool for Conserving Imperiled Fishes: Experiences in Western United States

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae #poeciliidae #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This review by W. L. Minckley (Arizona State University) examines translocation, the movement of imperiled fishes among natural localities or from nature to propagation facilities and back, as a conservation tool in western North America. The region's native freshwater fauna is declining rapidly: at least 20 species have gone extinct in the past century, and about 120 of the remaining 200 taxa are of special concern. The paper addresses legal and political obstacles, biological criteria for site selection and stock sourcing, captive propagation, monitoring, documentation, and a set of case histories. It concludes that translocation success is hard to define and predict, that the technique should be a stopgap rather than a substitute for habitat protection, and that results must be published in the peer-reviewed literature.

## Background & Description

The paper frames translocation within the decline of western fishes. Most threatened taxa belong to three groups: cyprinoids (minnows and suckers), salmonoids (trouts and salmons), and cyprinodontoids (livebearers, killifishes and allies). Key drivers include aridity, dam construction (about 50 federal dams by 1930 and roughly 1000 more from 1930 to 1980), groundwater pumping, and invasion by [[Non-Native Fishes]].

Key terms defined in the paper:
- **Translocation and transplantation**: synonyms for any movement of a taxon between places.
- **Stocking**: physically placing an organism in a new location. Reintroductions occur inside the native range; introductions occur outside it (after [[Hendrickson and Brooks 1991]]).
- **Preservation**: preventing extinction, the first step only.
- **Conservation**: maintaining evolutionary potential, which requires viable populations sustaining themselves over ecological time of tens to thousands of years.

Legal context: the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) evolved from the Endangered Species Preservation Act of 1966 (ESA itself dates to 1973). A 1982 amendment created "experimental, essential" and "experimental, non-essential" categories. Essential populations keep full ESA protection, while non-essential populations waive most protection and leave stocked habitats unprotected.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Site suitability.** [[Moyle and Sato 1991]] proposed preserve-design criteria. The paper gives five: (1) necessary habitat resources; (2) enough area and population size for self-sustainability; (3) within-site replication; (4) protection from edge and external effects; (5) replication at distant, similarly protected sites. Criteria 1 to 3 are most often applied. Small, short-lived species (under 3 years) can often meet these locally, while large, long-lived species (over 5 years) need major watersheds.

**Natural refugia and dispersal.** Permanent springs served as stable refugia, while less-than-permanent marshes and streams provided connectivity and gene flow. Dams, diversions and water-table declines have cut off these routes, so translocation must now replace natural dispersal. Less-than-permanent sites remain valuable as transplant sites.

**Hybridization and invasion.** Non-native species have caused serious harm. Examples include the spread of [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), which replaces native topminnows (*Poeciliopsis* spp.), and hybridization between the non-native [[Sheepshead Minnow]] (*Cyprinodon variegatus*) and the Pecos pupfish (*C. pecosensis*). Most western fishes evolved in allopatry and may be especially prone to hybridization.

**Habitat disturbance.** Preparing sites by chemical or physical means has caused collateral damage, including loss of endemic hydrobiid snails in California springs (Landye 1983) and the genetic changes documented in endangered chubs (*Gila seminuda*) after poisoning an exotic invader (DeMarais et al. 1993).

## Key Findings

- **Translocation planning:** Consensus on the need for and location of translocations is the most complex step. Governmental units hold conflicting mandates, and approval often depends on whether a project impinges on resource use or proprietary rights.
- **Legal instruments:** The 1982 "experimental" categories were meant to speed research but allowed unprotected "non-essential" populations. Authorization can take two years or more, and one federal stocking of desert pupfish and Gila topminnow stalled for more than 5 years.
- **Stock sourcing:** Stocks should be chosen by genetic variability, geographic proximity and ecological fit. Numbers should reflect effective population size rather than census numbers. Allendorf and Ryman (1987) set 25 reproducing individuals per sex as an "absolute minimum" for salmonids. Hatchery practice at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] typically used 30 to 200 individuals to establish populations of short-lived taxa.
- **Hybrid thresholds:** Allendorf and Leary (1988) suggested 1.0% foreign genes as a practical threshold. Even highly introgressed stocks should be treated as irreplaceable if they are the last remnant of a taxon.
- **Captive propagation:** Dexter NFH (New Mexico) was dedicated in the 1970s with three goals: refuge, research and propagation. In 1989 it had 48 earthen ponds and 8 permanent staff. The 1992-93 budget was about $350,000, projected to rise to $570,000 by 1995-96. Twenty-four taxa were housed there between 1974 and 1989. Short-lived fishes breed readily in ponds, while long-lived fishes such as [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] require artificial spawning, costly habitat and supplemental feeding. About 100,000 ova were produced per 55-cm razorback sucker.
- **Taxonomy and documentation:** Unresolved taxonomy has delayed recovery of *Gila* chubs for almost 30 years. Voucher specimens or photographs are needed, and the paper calls for quarantine before any introduction outside native range.
- **Monitoring:** Monitoring should establish survival, establishment, growth quality, and research baselines. Failure can only be diagnosed with monitoring frequent enough to bracket the disappearance. One topminnow population persisted 10 years, then vanished after a flood of expected once-per-century magnitude, and was successfully restocked.
- **Success rates:** Across 407 sites for about 40 short-lived taxa (Hendrickson and Brooks 1991), the overall success rate was 26.3%. Excluding a single large [[Gila Topminnow]] program (208 sites, 18.3%), the rate was 34.7% across 199 sites. Of the taxa, 14 succeeded at all sites, 17 partly succeeded, and 9 failed at all sites. The authors could not predict success reliably.
- **Case histories:** Of eight critical cases, six survived. Two (Monkey Spring pupfish and Amistad gambusia) went extinct despite efforts. The [[Yaqui Chub]] was moved to an isolated creek in 1969. It recovered to widespread distribution after 1980 translocations from Dexter NFH, with allozyme variability maintained. The [[Big Bend Gambusia]] survived through repeated restocking, expulsion of mosquitofish, and protection in a National Park.
- **Extinction risk and long-lived species:** For razorback suckers, stocking of about 15 million fish (1981-1989) would leave roughly one fish per surface hectare across 20,550 ha, which is difficult to monitor. Success for such species may take decades to assess.
- **Documentation gap:** Much translocation data remains in "gray literature." About half of the Hendrickson and Brooks data came from personal communications, files and unpublished reports. Of about 350 citations on razorback suckers, roughly 50% were gray literature. The author urges formal publication from the outset.
- **Central conclusion:** The author, in principle, opposes translocation and regards it as an emergency, stopgap measure. Habitat conservation should be the long-term goal, with translocation kept as an essential interim tool.
