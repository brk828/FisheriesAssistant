# The Ecosystem Approach: Healthy Ecosystems and Sustainable Economies (Volume I Overview, Interagency Ecosystem Management Task Force, 1995)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ecosystem-management

## Executive Summary

This note summarizes the Volume I Overview of the June 1995 report of the [[Interagency Ecosystem Management Task Force]], circulated as a series of six email parts. The report defines the [[Ecosystem Approach]] as a goal-driven method for sustaining or restoring natural systems, built on a collaboratively developed vision of desired future conditions that integrates ecological, economic, and social factors within ecologically defined geographic boundaries. It identifies eight recurring implementation barriers, including federal agency coordination, partnerships with nonfederal stakeholders, and the [[Federal Advisory Committee Act]], and offers recommendations for each. The document is not a fisheries study; fish appear only as examples of resource decline and economic value.

## Background & Description

The Task Force was established in August 1993 following the [[National Performance Review]] call for a proactive, ecosystem-based federal approach to sustainability. Its report has three volumes: Volume I (this overview), Volume II (implementation issues), and Volume III (case studies). Only Volume I is included in the source; Volumes II and III were noted as not yet released.

The working group examined seven case studies: [[Anacostia River Watershed]], [[Coastal Louisiana]], [[Great Lakes Basin]], [[Pacific Northwest Forests]], [[Prince William Sound]], [[South Florida Ecosystem]], and [[Southern Appalachians]]. Issues were grouped into five areas: budget, institutional issues, public participation, science and information, and legal authorities.

The principles are framed as federal guidance and include shared visions, coordinated federal approaches, ecological approaches that maintain biological diversity, respect for private property rights, adaptive management, use of the best science, and establishment of baseline conditions for monitoring.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report cites several examples of resource stress:

- Declining salmon populations in the Pacific Northwest and oyster stocks in the Chesapeake Bay.
- Declining migratory bird populations and degraded coral reefs.
- Fishing: the fishing industry contributes more than $10[0] billion annually and about one and a half million jobs, yet nearly 80 percent of commercial species are overfished or harvested unsustainably (figure as printed in the OCR text; the exact value is partly garbled).
- Gulf of Mexico: the one-billion-dollar-a-year fishery depends on coastal habitat, since 90 percent of Gulf commercial fish species require estuarine wetland habitat during some life stage.
- Louisiana has lost over 1,000 square miles of coastal wetlands since the 1950s and continues to lose about 30 square miles per year.
- Waquoit Bay, Cape Cod: residential land use in the watershed changed from 2 percent in 1950 to 20 percent in 1992; population rose 15-fold in 50 years.
- The Everglades is described as a pillar of southern Florida's economy, linked to Florida Bay tourism, fishing, and water supply.
- Greater Yellowstone: from 1969 to 1989 jobs grew by almost 66,000 (68 percent) and personal income grew by nearly $2.2 billion (99 percent).

## Key Findings

**Core definitions and goals**
- An ecosystem is an interconnected community of living things, including humans, and its physical environment.
- The goal is to restore and sustain ecosystem health, productivity, and biological diversity, together with quality of life, in a manner integrated with social and economic goals.

**Benefits identified**
- Consensus-building and earlier public involvement, reducing costly [[Litigation]].
- Prompt action on problems before they become critical.
- Greater certainty for businesses, including "one-stop shopping" for permits.
- Managerial and budgetary efficiency through reduced duplication.
- Reduced burden on small landowners and reduced "boom-and-bust" disruption.

**Eight implementation issues and recommendations (summary)**

1. **Federal agency coordination:** Missions and planning are fragmented, often along jurisdictional rather than ecological lines. Examples include regional task forces, the [[Regional Ecosystem Office]] in the Pacific Northwest, and the [[Office of Forestry and Economic Development]].
2. **Shared vision:** Most case-study visions were general. The Pacific Northwest [[Forest Plan]] was a top-down federal vision, while the [[Anacostia River Watershed]] plan was a local vision.
3. **Partnerships with nonfederal stakeholders:** FACA procedures limit contact. Recommendations include flexibility, chartered advisory committees only where cost-effective, and technical assistance to private landowners such as the [[Malpai Borderlands Group]].
4. **Communication with the public:** Public outreach is seen as secondary. Recommendations include communication plans, community relations specialists, and interagency "circuit rider" teams.
5. **Resource allocation and management:** Budgets are program-specific and tied to outputs. None of the seven case studies had integrated budgets. Reported examples include a 30 percent efficiency gain in Idaho Bureau of Land Management operations and a $4 million annual saving projected for the new BLM budget structure.
6. **Knowledge base and science:** Science is narrowly focused, disciplinary, and poorly translated for managers. Recommendations include regional science planning bodies, "scientific translators," and standards for ecosystem studies.
7. **Information and data management:** Recommendations include collaborative regional data management and common standards under [[Executive Order 12906]], with the [[National Spatial Data Infrastructure]] as a national effort.
8. **Adaptive management:** Few precedents exist. Barriers include underfunded long-term monitoring, the lack of standards for when change requires plan revision, and the lack of national monitoring standards.

**Framework steps**

The report outlines a typical sequence: define the area of concern; involve stakeholders; develop a shared vision; characterize the historical and present ecosystem; establish goals; develop an action plan; monitor conditions and evaluate results; and adapt management in light of new information.

**Monitoring purposes**

Implementation monitoring (standards followed), effectiveness monitoring (results achieved), and validation monitoring (assumptions sound).

**Selected case-study outcomes**

- The Malpai Borderlands Group, a ranching community of fewer than 100 families along about 60 miles of the U.S.–Mexico border, is described as working toward grassland restoration on a million-acre project area.
- The Georgia-Pacific and Nature Conservancy agreement covers 32 square miles in North Carolina.
- The Applegate Partnership in western Oregon is cited as a successful voluntary partnership, while the Little River Adaptive Management Plan is cited as a cautionary example.

**Concluding points**
- The ecosystem approach is presented as a process, not a mapping convention, and as providing no additional federal authority.
- It is not a "cure-all"; conflicts may still require courts.
