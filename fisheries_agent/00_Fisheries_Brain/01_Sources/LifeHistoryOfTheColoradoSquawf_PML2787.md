# Life History of the Colorado Squawfish and Colorado Chub in the Green River, Dinosaur National Monument, 1964-1966

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the life history, growth, diet, reproduction and distribution of the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and the [[Colorado Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) in the [[Green River]] within [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Fieldwork ran from May 1964 to October 1966, after the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in November 1962. The authors collected 1,469 squawfish and 2,393 chubs using gill nets, seines and an electric shocker. Operation of [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]] reduced the range of both species, altered seasonal flow and temperature, and slowed growth. Both species still spawned successfully below the [[Yampa River]] confluence in all four post-impoundment years. The study also documents two chub forms, the [[Roundtail Chub]] and the [[Bonytail Chub]], and argues they may warrant species rather than subspecies status.

## Background & Description

- **Colorado Squawfish:** The largest native minnow in North America, with reported sizes of 36 kg (80 lb) and larger. It is endemic to the Colorado River drainage and has been listed by the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife as a "Rare and Endangered Fish and Wildlife Species of the United States." Little was known of its life history at the time of the study.
- **Colorado Chub:** Two morphological variants occur in the study area. The "roundtail" was classified as *Gila robusta robusta* and the "bonytail" as *G. r. elegans* by Miller (1946). Taxonomy was poorly understood, and no criteria existed for separating young fish under 200 mm total length, so specimens below that size were combined as Colorado chub.
- **Study Context:** In September 1962, before dam closure, about 90 miles of the Green River and its tributaries were treated with [[Rotenone Treatment]] to eradicate non-game fish and prepare for a sport fishery in the new reservoir. The study was a follow-up to assess the effects of this program and of the dam on fish populations and habitat in the Monument.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow and Temperature Regime.** Before impoundment, the Green River had high spring run-off peaking in June and low, stable flows in winter and fall. After dam closure, the characteristic spring high and winter low were replaced by a relatively stable seasonal discharge. Below the Yampa River, the seasonal flow pattern was closer to pre-impoundment conditions because the Yampa water moderated the dam's effects.

Downstream temperatures varied with summer discharge:
- In low-discharge years (1963 and 1965), summer temperatures from Bridgeport to the Yampa River approximated pre-impoundment values.
- In high-discharge years (1964 and 1966), temperatures were well below pre-impoundment levels as far downstream as Island Park and Jensen.
- Winter temperatures were higher than before impoundment.

**Water Quality.** Dissolved oxygen ranged from 6.4 to 8.5 ppm. Total alkalinity, mainly from bicarbonates, ranged from 65 ppm at spring run-off to 160 ppm in late summer. pH ranged from 7.8 to 8.6. Water from the dam was clear and nearly sediment-free, but tributaries carried heavy silt. Turbidity peaked at up to 5,000 Jackson Turbidity Units during spring run-off and fell below 100 units by late summer.

**Associated Fauna.** Other common species in the Monument included [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Common Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Redside Shiner]] and [[Channel Catfish]].

## Key Findings

### Distribution and Range Reduction
- Colorado squawfish were found from the Yampa River mouth downstream to Ouray. None was found above [[Echo Park]], 65 miles below the dam, and none in fast water.
- Colorado chubs were common from the Yampa mouth downstream through the Monument and occasionally found at [[Lodore]], 47 miles below the dam. Chubs had been present between the dam site and Lodore before impoundment but were taken above Lodore only in 1963.
- Squawfish showed no distinct habitat preference. Adults used pools, eddies and runs over various bottoms. Chubs preferred pools and eddies over silt or silt-boulder bottoms. Young of both species occurred in quiet water or shallow pools.

### Growth and Age
- Scales from 182 squawfish and 333 chubs (including 49 roundtails and 67 bonytails) were aged using the [[Scale Analysis]] method, validated by Hile (1941). Scales were not readable on fish under 35 mm.
- The oldest, largest squawfish was an 11-year-old female, 610 mm long and weighing 2,268 g. The largest squawfish weighed 2.3 kg (5 lb).
- The oldest roundtail was a 7-year-old female, 366 mm long and weighing 393 g. The oldest bonytail was 7 years old, 388 mm long and weighing 422 g.
- Squawfish annual increments were greatest in year five and then declined. No sex-related growth difference was found.
- [[Growth Rate]] of both species declined after impoundment. Goodness-of-growth tests showed squawfish growth was +19.0% above the 8-year average in 1958 and -27.1% below it in 1965. Roundtail and bonytail growth was slower in 1963-1965 than in pre-impoundment years. The largest deviations were +9.4% and +7.0% in 1962 and -13.3% and -7.6% in 1965.
- The authors suggest slower growth resulted from altered [[Thermal Regime]] (too cool or too warm for growth) and possibly from biased sampling of older fish.
- Days with mean water temperature above 60 F at Jensen averaged about 115 pre-impoundment. The count was 147 in 1963, 103 in 1964 and 83 in 1965.
- Bonytails grew faster than roundtails after the third year of life. The roundtail became heavier than the bonytail as length increased.

### Length-Weight Relationships
- Squawfish: log W = -5.4177 + 3.126 log L. The slope was significantly higher than 3.0 (P < 0.01), so weight increased slightly faster than the cube of length.
- Roundtail: log W = -5.2442 + 3.086 log L.
- Bonytail: log W = -4.7899 + 2.860 log L.
- An analysis of covariance found significantly different length-weight relationships between the two chub forms (P < 0.01). See [[Length-Weight Relationship]].

### Diet and Feeding
- Young squawfish up to 50 mm fed mainly on cladocerans, copepods and chironomid larvae. Insect use peaked at about 100 mm, after which fish became the main food.
- Squawfish over 200 mm ate only fish, and 39% of their stomachs were empty. Tapeworms (*Proteocephalus* sp.) were found in 65% of squawfish stomachs over 200 mm.
- The study found no seasonal or geographic dietary shifts in squawfish.
- Roundtails were opportunistic, eating fish, aquatic insects and terrestrial insects. 35% of their stomachs were empty, and 23% of those over 200 mm contained tapeworms.
- Bonytails fed mainly on terrestrial insects, plant debris and filamentous algae. Only 11% of their stomachs were empty, and no tapeworms were found.
- Young chubs ate mostly aquatic insects, then a broader mix of aquatic and terrestrial insects with age. Chubs over 200 mm mainly ate terrestrial insects, often taken from the surface drift.

### Reproduction and Spawning
- Spawning was inferred from ripe or spent adults and from young-of-the-year collected 3 to 6 weeks later. No eggs were located for either species in the Green River.
- **Chub:** Spawning occurred when water temperature reached about 65 F. It was late June to early July in 1964 and 1965, and about mid-June in 1966, when the temperature rise and water-level recession came earlier. Roundtails and bonytails spawned at the same time of year but were never caught together in the same gill-net set.
- **Squawfish:** Spawning occurred roughly one month after water temperature reached 65 F, between late June and early August. In 1966 it was about a month earlier than in 1964 and 1965.
- Squawfish spawned successfully in all four post-impoundment years, but only below the Yampa confluence.
- Chubs spawned successfully below the Yampa confluence in all four years. No spawning success was found above it in 1964 and 1966, when discharge-driven temperatures stayed well below 65 F. Chubs reproduced at Lodore only in 1965.
- In Lake Mohave, about 500 bonytails were observed spawning over a gravel shelf in May 1954. Each female had three to five male "escorts," and a female was estimated to contain about 10,000 ripe eggs.

### Year-Class Strength
- Squawfish year-classes were poor in 1965 (only 42 young-of-the-year collected) and weak in 1962. An abundant year-class was produced in 1963, the first year after closure, when flow was extremely low.
- An abundant chub year-class was produced in 1964, a year of relatively high discharge.
- No consistent relationship between mean annual discharge and year-class strength was apparent. Estimates relied on seine captures of shallow-water young fish, which may bias the results.

### Possible Causes of Decline in Large Squawfish
- The very large squawfish reported in earlier decades were absent in recent years. Local fishermen blamed competition with introduced channel catfish, reporting dead squawfish with catfish spines lodged in the pharynx or esophagus.
- Other possibilities included heavy exploitation by anglers, and reduced growth caused by parasite loads acquired from predation on introduced fish.

### Taxonomic Implications
- The authors argue that the roundtail and bonytail may be sibling species rather than subspecies. The two forms occur sympatrically in the Green River and in the Flaming Gorge Reservoir basin, and differ in growth, length-weight relationship and diet.
- Jordan (1891) also reported both forms from the Gunnison River near Delta, Colorado. Roundtails were thought to occupy smaller tributaries and upper river reaches, and bonytails the main channels of larger rivers.

### Conclusions
- Operation of Flaming Gorge Dam reduced the range of both species and altered their seasonal thermal environment. Growth declined after impoundment.
- The environmental requirements of both species appear to be met below the Yampa confluence, where both species continue to reproduce.
- Between Flaming Gorge Dam and Echo Park, the absence of squawfish, the reduced chub range and the lack of chub reproduction in 2 of the last 3 years indicate that critical requirements, particularly water temperature, are not being met.
