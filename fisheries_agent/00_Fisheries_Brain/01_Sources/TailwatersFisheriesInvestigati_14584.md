# Tailwaters Fisheries Investigations: Colorado River Below Glen Canyon Dam (1964-1966)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #tailwater-ecology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This report documents two consecutive creel census studies conducted at [[Lee's Ferry]] on the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] from July 1964 through June 1966. The first segment (July 1964-June 1965) recorded 2,216 angler trips over 176 census days, yielding 8,047 [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) and 523 [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) in 8,572 hours, with success at 0.99 fish per hour and 76.5% angler success. The second segment (July 1965-June 1966) documented 776 angler trips over 93 census days, producing 2,122 rainbow trout and 38 channel catfish in 3,394 hours, with reduced success at 0.64 fish per hour and 65% angler success. Stocking programs included both catchable-sized and fingerling rainbow trout, with marked fish returns estimated at 32.2-47%. Limnological studies revealed satisfactory water quality for trout (dissolved oxygen 8-10 ppm, pH 8, temperatures 46-68°F), with plankton and algae serving as primary food sources. The fishery was sustained primarily through stocking rather than natural reproduction due to fluctuating water levels.

## Background & Description

[[Lee's Ferry]] is located approximately 15 miles below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and provides the only public vehicular access to the [[Colorado River]] between the dam and [[Lake Mead]]. The fishable area extends approximately 16 miles: 3 miles accessible from shore and up to 14 miles accessible by boat depending on water levels. This investigation represents segments two and three of a six-year study financed primarily by Section 8 of the Colorado River Storage Project (Public Law 485) to evaluate the tailwater fishery created by [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

The creel census employed road blocks and river bank checks, utilizing Arizona Game and Fish Department standard forms to record party size, sex, hours fished, success rates, catch composition, and methods. Pressure estimates were calculated by direct expansion of known daily pressure, accounting for weekday/weekend/holiday variations. Fish were measured (all to nearest inch, subsample to 0.1 inch) and weighed (subsample to 0.01 pound). Return rates were determined using fin-clipped marked fish from stocking programs.

**Stocking Programs:**
- **1964-1965:** 23,500 catchable rainbow trout (4,000 marked), 10,000 fingerlings (5,000 marked)
- **1965-1966:** 7,000 catchable rainbow trout (2,500 marked), 19,000 fingerlings (4,500 marked)

The reduction in catchable stocking during the second segment reflected an attempt to transition from put-and-take to fingerling-based management, though this resulted in decreased angler pressure (35% decline).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water Quality (1965-1966):**
- Dissolved oxygen: 8-10 ppm
- pH: 8
- Alkalinity: 137-171 ppm
- Hardness: 274-359 ppm
- Temperature: 46-68°F
- Flow: Average ~11,000 cfs (relatively stable compared to previous period)

**Biological Productivity:**

*Plankton:* Bi-monthly 15-minute drift samples yielded trace (<0.1 ml) to 2.5 ml volumes. Zooplankton and phytoplankton served as primary food sources for trout throughout the study period.

*Benthos:* Limited but increasing populations detected. Three organism types collected:
- Bloodworms (Chironomidae): 0-8.73 per Eckman sample
- Small black snails: 0-4 per square foot
- Diptera larvae: 0-10.2 per Surber sample

Benthic organisms were improving due to more stable water flows and increasingly appeared in stomach contents, suggesting potential future importance as food items.

*Food Habits:* Analysis of 845 trout stomachs (1965-1966) revealed:
- Zooplankton: 390 occurrences (46%)
- Phytoplankton/algae: 404 occurrences (48%)
- Debris: 128 occurrences
- Adult insects: 60 occurrences
- Bloodworms: 7 occurrences
- Aquatic plants: 4 occurrences
- Bait: 106 occurrences

Fish subsisted primarily on plankton and attached algae, with seasonal variation in food availability.

**Species Composition:**

Abundant: [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*), [[Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), [[Green Sunfish]] (*Lepomis cyanellus*)

Common: [[Channel Catfish]], [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*), [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*), [[Largemouth Bass]] (*Micropterus salmoides*)

Rare: [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Colorado River Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)

**Habitat Characteristics:**

Water releases from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] created drastic water level fluctuations that hampered natural reproduction. The 1964-1965 period experienced highly variable flows (5,000-45,000 cfs), while 1965-1966 saw more stable conditions averaging 11,000 cfs. High turbidity during spring runoff months reduced fishing success. In September 1965, dam releases varied 250 feet vertically, drawing plankton-rich water from the phototrophic zone (~30 feet below surface), which improved trout condition.

## Key Findings

**Angler Success and Pressure (1964-1965):**
- Total effort: 176 census days
- Angler trips checked: 2,216
- Estimated total trips: 4,161
- Harvest: 8,047 rainbow trout, 523 channel catfish (checked); estimated 13,512 rainbow trout, 985 channel catfish (total)
- Success rate: 0.99 fish/hour, 3.87 fish/trip
- Angler success: 76.5%
- Total hours: 8,572 (checked); 15,723 (estimated)

**Angler Success and Pressure (1965-1966):**
- Total effort: 93 census days
- Angler trips checked: 776
- Estimated total trips: 2,681
- Harvest: 2,122 rainbow trout, 38 channel catfish (checked); estimated 7,895 total fish
- Success rate: 0.64 fish/hour, 2.8 fish/trip
- Angler success: 65%
- Total hours: 3,394 (checked); 11,435 (estimated)

**Boat vs. Shore Angling:**

*1964-1965:* Boat anglers (10% of pressure) achieved 1.02 fish/hour vs. 0.98 fish/hour for shore anglers. Boat anglers maintained more consistent success between stocking events.

*1965-1966:* Boat anglers (51% of pressure) achieved 0.79 fish/hour vs. 0.42 fish/hour for shore anglers. The dramatic increase in boat angling reflected availability of rental boats and superior success rates. Boat anglers accessed concentrated fish populations near the dam and avoided high turbidity areas.

**Stocking Returns:**

*1964-1965:* Marked catchable trout return estimated at 32.2% during study period. However, total harvest (13,512 trout) equaled 57.5% of catchables planted (23,500), indicating carry-over from previous stocking and possible recruitment from [[Lake Powell]].

*1965-1966:* Marked catchable return estimated at 47%. Notably, smaller plants (under 2,000 fish) yielded higher return percentages:
- March 11, 1965 plant (2,000 fish): 56.3% return
- June 22, 1965 plant (2,000 fish): 42.1% return
- Larger plants (5,000 fish): 10.6-22.1% return

Total harvest (8,345 rainbow trout) exceeded catchable plants (7,000), suggesting recruitment from fingerling plants, carry-over, or immigration from [[Lake Powell]]. Three marked trout from March 1964 plant were recovered 16-17 months later at 16-17 inches, demonstrating long-term survival. Two trout marked in [[Lake Powell]] were collected at dam base, confirming downstream recruitment.

**Fish Condition and Growth:**

*1964-1965:*
- Average weight: 0.72 pounds
- Average length: 11.3 inches
- Condition factor (C): 50
- Marked improvement from first study segment (C=34)

*1965-1966:*
- Average weight: 0.54 pounds
- Average length: 10.9 inches
- Condition factor (C): 41
- Modal size class: 10-11 inches

The improved condition in 1964-1965 resulted from plankton influx when dam releases drew from the phototrophic zone. The decline in 1965-1966 reflected reduced catchable stocking and younger age structure. Despite adequate condition factors, fewer large fish (>15 inches) were creeled than expected for a three-year-old fishery, suggesting either: (1) large fish present but not caught, (2) emigration from fishable area, or (3) dietary deficiency limiting growth.

**Angler Demographics:**

*1964-1965:* Primary origins were Page (37%), Flagstaff (21%), and Phoenix (10%), with 9% from out-of-state.

*1965-1966:* Primary origins were Flagstaff (24%), other Arizona cities (27%), and Phoenix (20%), with Page declining to 11% and out-of-state 9%. Page residents likely utilized [[Lake Powell]] fishery instead.

**Seasonal Patterns:**

Success varied substantially by month, ranging from 0.07 fish/hour (April) to 2.27 fish/hour (July 1964). Lowest success occurred during spring runoff (February-May) when high, turbid flows reduced fishing quality. Success peaked immediately following catchable trout plants. November 1964 showed exceptional success (1.13 fish/hour, 88% angler success) following stocking.

**Channel Catfish:**

Channel catfish abundance declined dramatically between study periods (523 checked in 1964-1965 vs. 38 in 1965-1966). Average size was 10 inches. The decline suggests limited recruitment and possible emigration downstream.

**Management Implications:**

Natural reproduction was "greatly hampered" by drastic water level changes characteristic of dam releases. The fishery required sustained stocking to maintain catch rates. Individual catchable plants should be restricted to under 2,000 fish to maximize return percentages. Marked fingerling plants were recommended to evaluate growth and contribution. The extent of fishery below [[Lee's Ferry]] (downstream 16+ miles) required evaluation, as evidenced by marked fish recovery at Cathedral Rapid (5 miles below Lee's Ferry).

**Limnological Conclusions:**

All physical and chemical parameters supported a coldwater trout fishery. Plankton populations were satisfactory and heavily utilized. Benthic organisms were present in limited but increasing numbers, suggesting potential for enhanced productivity as the tailwater matured. The lack of large trout despite adequate food and condition factors warranted intensified study of food organism availability and potential dietary deficiencies. Introduction of additional benthic organisms should be evaluated to diversify the food base.
