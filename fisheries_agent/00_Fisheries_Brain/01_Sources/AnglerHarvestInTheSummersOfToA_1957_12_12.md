# Angler Harvest in the Summers of 1953 to 1956 at Clear Lake, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fisheries #Centrarchidae #Moronidae #creel-census

## Executive Summary

This study, conducted by the Iowa Cooperative Fisheries Research Unit, presents a four-year (1953–1956) creel census of angler harvest at [[Clear Lake (Iowa)]], a large (3,643 acre), shallow (max depth 20 ft), eutrophic natural lake. The research quantified total fishing effort (man-hours), catch composition by species, and catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) across multiple angler types (boat, dock, shore, wader). The dominant fishery was driven by [[Yellow Bass]] and [[Black Bullhead]] populations. A significant population crash of yellow bass following a mortality event in November 1955 caused a dramatic ~50% collapse in overall fishing success and total harvest in 1956, illustrating the tight coupling between a single dominant species' population dynamics and the entire recreational fishery's economic/recreational output.

## Background & Description

*Note: This paper is a fisheries management/creel census study and does not provide detailed morphological descriptions of individual species. Species were identified taxonomically for harvest accounting purposes only.*

Species recorded in the creel census (minimum 13 species):
- **[[Yellow Bass]]** (*Roccus mississippiensis*) — dominant catch species; population skewed toward older fish prior to 1955 mortality event.
- **[[Black Bullhead]]** (*Ictalurus melas*, referred to as "bullheads") — numerically stable contributor regardless of percentage share shifts.
- **[[Bluegill]]** (*Lepomis macrochirus*) — increases attributed to strong 1951 and 1952 year classes (see [[Di Costanzo 1957 Growth Study]]).
- **[[Yellow Perch]]** (*Perca flavescens*) — steadily declining population/catch across the study period.
- **[[Crappie|Black and White Crappie]]** (*Pomoxis nigromaculatus* / *P. annularis*) — treated as combined taxon; stable catch, popular with dock fishermen, especially in spring.
- **[[Walleye]]** (*Stizostedion vitreum*) — low overall percentage but historically significant; primarily caught in May–June.
- **[[Northern Pike]]** (*Esox lucius*) — catch fluctuations driven by a 1953 stocking event and two subsequent mortality events (Aug 1954, May 1955).
- **[[Channel Catfish]]** (*Ictalurus punctatus*), **[[Largemouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus salmoides*), **[[Smallmouth Bass]]** (*Micropterus dolomieu* — rare), **[[White Bass]]** (*Roccus chrysops* — sporadic, signs of spawning recovery), **[[Pumpkinseed]]** (*Lepomis gibbosus* — likely undercounted due to angler misidentification as bluegill), and **[[Common Carp]]** (*Cyprinus carpio* — incidental catch).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Clear Lake functions as a **shallow, eutrophic, warm-water lake ecosystem** typical of the Midwestern United States, supporting a diverse warm-water game fish assemblage (per [[Bailey and Harrison 1945]]).

- **Habitat structuring**: Bulrush beds served as critical structural habitat, concentrating both [[Yellow Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] populations, particularly during fall 1954 and summer 1955, leading to incidental bluegill catches by anglers targeting yellow bass.
- **Angler-habitat interactions**: Distinct fishing zones (boat, dock, shore, wader) corresponded to different species assemblages and catch efficiencies — wader anglers, fishing in vegetated shallow/littoral zones, consistently had the highest CPUE (~2 fish/hour), implicating littoral zone productivity.
- **Population disturbance events**: Two documented mass mortality events shaped community dynamics:
  - November 1955: Yellow bass mortality (age-selective, removed older cohorts).
  - August 1954 & May 1955: Northern pike mortalities.
- **Seasonal habitat use**: Spring (ice-out to mid-June) fishery showed different species availability (bullhead- and walleye-dominated) compared to summer (yellow bass- and bluegill-dominated), suggesting seasonal shifts in species accessibility/behavior.
- **Trophic/recruitment link**: Strong 1951–1952 bluegill year classes point to recruitment variability as a major driver of multi-year population and fishery trends.

## Key Findings

- **Catch composition shifts**: Yellow bass comprised 38–53% of summer catch in 1953–1955 but collapsed to 14% in 1956 following the Nov. 1955 mortality event — catch dropped to roughly one-tenth of 1955 levels.
- **Bullhead stability**: Despite fluctuating percentage share (25.9–60.2%), bullhead numerical catch remained relatively constant across years, indicating a numerically resilient population.
- **Bluegill increase linked to year-class strength**: Strong recruitment in 1951–1952 drove increased bluegill catches; incidental bycatch by yellow-bass anglers in rush habitat also inflated apparent bluegill catch in certain years.
- **Yellow perch decline**: Dropped from 14.2% (3rd most caught species) of the 1953 catch to <1% by 1956, cause undetermined.
- **Walleye/Northern Pike**: Combined contributed ≤1% of total summer catch annually; northern pike catch spiked in 1954–55 due to a 1953 stocking event, then declined by 1956 due to fishing mortality and natural mortality events.
- **Fishing success (CPUE) collapse in 1956**: Overall catch/fisherman-hour fell ~50% (1.21 in 1955 to 0.62 in 1956), driven almost entirely by the yellow bass crash — demonstrating a strong causal link between single-species abundance and overall angler satisfaction/success.
- **Wader vs. shore efficiency**: Waders averaged ~2 fish/hour vs. ~0.5 fish/hour for shore anglers, likely driving an observed shift in angler behavior toward wader fishing over the study period.
- **Fishing pressure trends**: Pressure was 60 man-hours/acre (1953), rising to ~80 man-hours/acre (1954–1955), then dropping to 55.1 man-hours/acre (1956) — attributed to the yellow bass fishery failure. Even this reduced pressure was considered high relative to other large natural lakes.
- **Total harvest**: Summer harvest ranged from 22.6–35.3 lbs/acre (1953–1955) but fell to 12.3 lbs/acre in 1956 (one-half to one-third of prior years).
- **Spring fishery importance**: The spring fishery (ice-out to mid-June) contributed substantially to annual harvest — in 1956, nearly twice as many fish were caught in the (shorter) spring census period as in the summer period, with bullhead and walleye more prominent in spring catch.
- **Unsampled harvest components**: Fall/winter fishing, night fishing, and non-daylight effort were not systematically sampled but were considered non-trivial; total annual harvest was estimated to be **over twice** the summer daylight estimate alone, with average annual harvest estimated at **≥60 lbs/acre** and **~150 man-hours/acre** of recreational fishing effort.
- **Management implication**: Authors suggest improved catches of desirable species (walleye, northern pike) could rapidly increase fishing pressure, and that abundant young yellow bass observed in 1956 suggested a possible fishery rebound in 1957–1958.
