# The Role of Groundwater in Climate Warming Impacts on Stream Salmonines

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Meisner]], [[Rosenfeld]], and [[Regier]] (*Fisheries*, Vol. 13, No. 3, May–June 1988) examines how [[Groundwater Temperature]] responds to [[Climate Warming]] and how that response will affect stream [[Salmonines]]. The authors argue that groundwater will mediate much of the biological impact of warming on freshwater fishes. Groundwater temperatures are expected to follow projected increases in mean annual air temperature, modified by local microclimate and topography. Warmer groundwater would raise [[Redd]] temperatures and may depress [[Interstitial Oxygen]], while shrinking summer thermal habitat at low altitudes and latitudes and expanding it at high altitudes and latitudes.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses stream salmonines of the Northern Hemisphere, dominated by the genera *Salmo*, *Salvelinus*, and *Oncorhynchus*. The species most discussed are:

- [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*)
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*)
- [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*)

Groundwater affects these fishes in several ways. It maintains coldwater headwater habitats, provides [[Baseflow]], and moderates seasonal air-temperature swings. It supplies [[Coldwater Refugia]] in summer and warmwater refugia in winter. Salmonine abundance and biomass have been linked to groundwater presence, and trout spawning areas have long been associated with [[Groundwater Discharge]] sites.

Groundwater discharge also provides stable thermal conditions for developing eggs and fry. It helps prevent [[Anchor Ice]] formation, which can cause high mortality in post-emergent trout.

The authors state that the troposphere is warming in response to rising atmospheric CO2 and other greenhouse gases. General circulation model scenarios are described as "fairly crude." They project increases in mean annual air temperature of up to 5.5°C in North America within 40–50 years. For the [[Great Lakes Basin]], projected increases are 3.2–4.8°C. Winter warming is expected to be greater because of reduced albedo from less snow and ice. Precipitation projections are less reliable than temperature projections.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Heat sources and the neutral zone.** Solar radiation is the main heat source for the ground. The annual geothermal flux is about 0.004% of annual solar energy received, a value the authors describe as enough to melt a 5 mm ice sheet spread over the Earth's surface. Mean surface soil temperature generally exceeds mean annual air temperature by about 1°C, as a rule of thumb. Seasonal soil temperature fluctuations decrease with depth until they reach the [[Neutral Zone]], where temperature stays constant. The neutral zone is deepest at high latitudes (18.6 m at 60–70°N in the summarized table) and shallowest near the equator (7.3 m at 0–10°N). Southern Hemisphere neutral zones are generally shallower than Northern Hemisphere equivalents at the same latitude. Whether a water table lies above or below the neutral zone determines whether aquifer temperatures fluctuate seasonally. Shallow aquifer discharge varies more than deep discharge.

**Air–ground coupling.** Surface temperatures are controlled by the [[Energy Balance Equation]], which links evaporation, condensation, convection, conduction, and longwave radiation. Warmer air holds more water vapour, which reduces evaporative heat loss from the ground and increases sensible heat conduction. Greater atmospheric moisture also increases longwave re-radiation, a positive feedback in warming.

**Latitude and altitude.** Regional groundwater temperatures track mean annual air temperature isotherms more closely than latitude or solar radiation. Collins (1925) analysed about 3,000 measurements and found 10–20 m groundwater in the United States was 1.1–1.7°C warmer than mean annual air temperature. Heath (1964) found groundwater at 2–5 m in rural New York averaged 1.6°C above mean annual air temperature. This relationship breaks down in Canada, Alaska, and the northeastern United States because of [[Snow Cover]] insulation. Where discontinuous [[Permafrost]] occurs, neutral-zone temperatures are 3.0–3.5°C above mean air temperature. Groundwater temperature also declines with altitude. A regression from 65 Swiss springs (800–2,500 m) gives:

T = 11.28 − 0.0041 H + 1.27 A (R² = 0.77)

where T is spring temperature (°C), H is altitude (m), and A is aspect (south = 1.0, east and west = 0.5, north = 0.0). This implies a decline of about 4.1°C per 1,000 m, similar to the [[Lapse Rate]] of air. North-facing springs are 1.0–2.0°C cooler than south-facing ones.

**Surface cover, infiltration, and buffering.** Vegetation and organic litter reduce temperature range. In one study, groundwater beneath a field was 1.0–2.0°C warmer at maximum than beneath a mature forest, and mean annual temperature was 0.8°C cooler under the forest. Warm summer rain can raise groundwater temperature, while cold spring-thaw recharge can depress it. Snowmelt usually brings small streams close to 0.0°C. Kobayashi (1981) found, however, that a cold stream stayed near 3.5°C during early-spring snowmelt, which indicated melt water percolated through the ground. Permeable soils therefore buffer stream temperatures and sustain baseflow.

## Key Findings

1. **Warming projections.** Mean annual air temperature in North America may rise up to 5.5°C within 40–50 years. Greater winter warming is expected, and precipitation projections are less certain.

2. **Groundwater as a mediator.** If groundwater temperature is strongly linked to air temperature, climate warming will affect stream salmonines mainly through groundwater. Baseflow-driven increases of 4–5°C in low-elevation, low-latitude streams would reduce preferred summer habitat.

3. **Summer contraction and winter gain.** Trout zones would retract upstream and coldwater refugia below discharge sites would shrink. In winter, warmer groundwater would expand warmwater refugia and reduce anchor ice.

4. **Range expansion at high elevations and latitudes.** Warming could widen trout distributions where year-round low temperatures now limit them. At high latitudes, thawing permafrost could allow year-round groundwater flow suitable for reproduction and overwintering.

5. **Groundwater and trout abundance.** Several studies link trout population size to groundwater. Benson (1953) found trout numbers and redd counts varied directly with discharge. Latta (1965) found a strong correlation between age-0 brook trout and groundwater. Bowlby and Roff (1986) found groundwater discharge explained the most variation in brook trout biomass among southern Ontario streams.

6. **Thermal habitat requirements.** Brook, rainbow, and brown trout prefer 12–18°C. Population size depends on the amount of near-optimal thermal habitat during critical summer periods. The authors propose adapting the lake-based "thermal habitat space" concept, developed by [[Christie and Regier]], to streams.

7. **Redd selection.** Brook trout, and to a lesser degree rainbow and brown trout, choose groundwater discharge areas for redds. Their eggs and larvae depend on stable temperatures, and interstitial oxygen in redds is a key concern under warming.

8. **Regional uncertainty.** Groundwater temperature maps derived from isotherms are accurate only to about 1.0–2.0°C. Local data and local microclimate corrections are needed for precise assessments. Temperature-depth patterns, snow cover, and permeability all modify the regional signal.
