# Colorado Squawfish Reintroduction and Monitoring in Arizona, 1985

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This annual report by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD), prepared for the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS), summarizes the 1985 reintroduction and monitoring of [[Colorado Squawfish]] in the [[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]], Arizona. More than 96,000 fingerlings and 442 larger fish (355-405 mm TL) were stocked at six locations between August and October 1985. Post-stocking monitoring recovered 7 stocked squawfish in September, all dead or dying, with empty stomachs. A January 1986 survey collected five large adults, all alive, which showed visceral fat and therefore evidence of feeding. The author concludes that re-establishment in the lower Colorado River basin appears attainable under proper protocols, and recommends evaluating short-term dispersal, long-term survival and homing behavior.

## Background & Description

The Colorado squawfish (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) was widespread across the [[Colorado River Basin]] until the early 1900s. By 1985, populations persisted only in the upper basin (Green and Colorado rivers and their larger tributaries). In the lower basin, the species had not been collected since the early 1960s and was presumed eliminated by habitat development and modification.

The species was listed as endangered under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973), and a recovery plan was approved in 1978. Upper-basin habitats continued to be fragmented by large dams and diversions. Before 1985, recovery efforts were limited. Managers believed lower-basin recovery potential existed, and a 1978 plan to reintroduce squawfish into the upper Salt River was never carried out because of water-use and political conflicts.

Related recovery work provided precedents:
- The unlisted but endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] was reintroduced to the [[Gila River]] basin in 1981 under a 10-year agreement between USFWS and AGFD.
- A recovery program for the [[Gila Topminnow]] began in 1982 and served as a test case for introducing endangered species without disrupting existing water uses.

The 1985 squawfish stockings were classified as "nonessential experimental" under Section 10(j) of the ESA. These populations are not essential to species survival and are not protected under Section 7.

**Study area:** The upper Salt River runs through a canyon with considerable relief, with deep pools, shallow riffles and long deep runs. The Verde River flows through lower-relief terrain with braided channels, large backwaters and finer substrates. Both systems have tributaries that drain narrow canyons and are subject to flash flooding. The uppermost barriers to riverine conditions are [[Roosevelt Lake]] on the Salt River and [[Horseshoe Lake]] on the Verde River.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stocking protocol:** Fish were produced at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH), with a portion reared by AGFD from fry produced at Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery in 1981. Fish were transported in untreated hatchery well-water, and temperatures were tempered by adding river water to the tanks. Small fish (15-125 mm TL) were placed in quiet backwaters; larger fish were released into medium-velocity runs and pools.

**Predation and native fish communities:** The report notes that natural flow regimes benefit native fishes and disadvantage introduced species (Minckley and Meffe 1986). Periodic removal or reduction of introduced fishes allows natives to reinvade formerly unsuitable areas. Razorback sucker work in the Gila basin found that downstream dispersal of stocked fish led to predation by [[Flathead Catfish]] and [[Channel Catfish]], so stocking was shifted upstream into tributaries, which have fewer introduced predators. Larger stocked fish may also reduce predation losses. Similar predation effects were expected for squawfish.

**Dispersal and movement:** Adult squawfish are known to move long distances (up to about 200 km), presumably to spawn. Razorback suckers, by contrast, remained in localized areas near the bottom in hatchery raceways and ponds, whereas squawfish moved throughout accessible areas.

**Homing:** Radiotelemetry studies show mixed results. Tyus (1985) reported extensive late spring-summer movements, but other studies found limited movement. Insufficient data exist to confirm homing. Concern was raised that non-homing hatchery fish might compete with residents for food and space without contributing to reproduction.

## Key Findings

- **Stocking:** More than 96,000 fingerlings and 442 larger squawfish (355-405 mm TL) were stocked at six sites (Salt and Verde drainages, including Cherry Creek, Fossil Creek, West Clear Creek and the Verde River) from August through October 1985. Fingerlings came from DNFH and AGFD rearing.
- **Initial monitoring (3-4 September 1985, Verde River at Perkinsville):** Overnight trammel netting collected 7 squawfish from the 26 August 1985 stocking, along with 3 native and 3 introduced species. All seven were dead or dying when the nets were checked, even though the trammel nets were designed to keep fish alive.
- **Diet:** All stomachs examined from both the September and January collections were empty. The author suggests fish either regurgitated contents at capture or were held long enough for complete digestion.
- **Further surveys:** Seining in September and a November ASU trip failed to catch additional stocked squawfish. A January 1986 ASU survey collected five large squawfish (TL 333-412 mm); all were alive and showed visceral fat, which indicates feeding. An unverified angler report of a squawfish caught at Perkinsville in November 1985 was also received.
- **Dispersal (prior studies):** Of six fish radiotelemetered by Tyus et al. (1981), three did not move, one moved 25 km, and two moved 356 and 455 km. Tyus et al. (1985) recorded 261.5 km movement for six fish. Miller et al. (1984) found little movement in 1982 (one fish moved 320 km; the other 21 moved less than 65 km) and in 1983 (ten fish moved less than 50 km).
- **Conclusion on homing:** Evidence is insufficient to support homing; further investigation is recommended because of its implications for recovery protocol.
- **Recommendations:** Evaluate short-term dispersal of fingerlings in the Verde River (fall 1986 studies planned), long-term survival, homing, and predation by introduced fishes, which may be reduced by stocking fish of larger size and at favourable times of year.
- **Overall assessment:** Re-establishment of squawfish in the lower Colorado basin is uncertain but appears achievable if riverine conditions are maintained and proper recovery protocol is followed.
