# Systematics and the Conservation of Biological Diversity

#systematics #biodiversity #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This essay argues that [[Systematics]] is central to the conservation of [[Biological Diversity]]. The author describes biodiversity as an interdisciplinary science sitting between pattern and process, and as a socio-political activity shaped by the anthropocentric goals of the [[Convention On Biological Diversity]]. Because resources are limited and knowledge of the biota is fragmentary, the author contends that a predictive natural classification is the best tool for extrapolating knowledge and setting conservation priorities. The essay concludes that systematists have a responsibility to act proactively, not only to respond to societal needs.

## Background & Description

The document is a discussion paper, not a field study. It has no sample sizes or site data of its own. It draws on policy documents, including the [[Global Biodiversity Strategy]] and *Caring for the Earth*, and on the Convention's three objectives: conservation of biodiversity, sustainable use of its components, and fair sharing of benefits from genetic resources.

The author frames the debate with the following points:

- **Two-sided discipline:** Biodiversity "exists at the interface of pattern and pro­cess." The [[Twin Biological Hierarchy]] (genealogical versus ecological) is cited as one example, following [[Eldredge And Salthe]] (1984).
- **Complementarity of approaches:** Citing [[Olivier Rieppel]] (1988), pattern and process analyses are described as complementary "ways of seeing." Pattern analysis gives the only guide to common ancestry, while process analysis gives pattern a causal explanation.
- **Evolving concepts:** The essay traces [[Alpha Diversity]], beta and gamma diversity, and the time dimension, and explains how the [[Root-Weight Index]] became an early formal measure of [[Taxic Diversity]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The essay's ecological content centres on how little is known about the living world:

- **Number of species:** Estimates of extant species range from 3 to 100 million. [[Hammond]] (1992) gave a considered estimate of 12.5 million. About 1.5 to 1.8 million species have been formally described, though synonymy makes these limits hard to assess.
- **Biocontrol case:** A [[Cassava Mealybug]] was found attacking crops in Africa in 1973. Pest losses were massive by 1989. The pest turned out to belong to an undescribed species complex, and it occurs naturally only in the southern Neotropics. A matching encyrtid wasp was introduced, and it now covers more than 12 million km² of the African range. The estimated annual benefit is $200,000,000 (Herren & Neuenschwander, 1991). The author uses this case to show that a predictive classification makes rapid [[Biological Control]] possible.
- **Chemical diversity:** Schulz et al. (1993) examined male pheromone-gland secretions of 10 African [[Milkweed Butterflies]]. They separated 214 substances in 14 chemical classes. Individual species carried 12 to 59 compounds, many restricted to one or a few closely related species.
- **Extratropical plants:** A table lists essentially extratropical plant families, most with only one or a few species, grouped as Southern Hemisphere, Northern Hemisphere, or bipolar.

## Key Findings

1. **Systematics is essential to biodiversity science.** Classifications are predictive and allow "interpolations through data-gaps, and extrapolations to be made to new situations." Classification is therefore an intelligence system, not passive pigeon-holing.

2. **Biochemical prospecting benefits from classification.** A cladistic classification (Ackery & Vane-Wright, 1984) points to closely related species, such as *Amauris dannfelti* and *A. inferna* for a ketone, and *Danaus gilippus* for a terpene. The author adds that any diverse group, such as the Hymenoptera, could be a source of new chemicals.

3. **Option value underpins taxic measures.** Following Humphries et al. (1995), the current goal is to assess option value. The author argues for focusing on heritable characters rather than individual species, since characters are the "fundamental currency units of option value."

4. **Character richness versus species richness.** In the Figure 1 example of eight land plants, choosing three species gives 56 possible combinations. Under character richness, only 10 combinations maximise value. Under character combination richness, only two are selected. The author notes that species richness usually approximates both measures when many taxa are involved.

5. **Complementarity guides area selection.** The author favours complementarity over absolute richness. In Table 2, area 2 plus area 3 covers all eight species, whereas adding areas stepwise starting from the richest is less efficient. Both step-wise and set-wise procedures have a place, depending on circumstances.

6. **All areas matter.** Unique value in one area does not make adjacent areas unimportant. Tropical forests and coral reefs are rich, but ocean bottoms and species-poor high latitudes also make unique contributions.

7. **Systematists should respond to society's needs.** Listed responsibilities include:
   - Demystifying taxonomy and making products such as identification keys more accessible.
   - Improving distributional data, specimen curation, and [[Electronic Databases]].
   - Applying advanced technology such as interactive keys and sequence data.
   - Training new systematists and building national biodiversity institutes.
   - Engaging in public education.

8. **Systematists must also be proactive.** The author calls for leadership in policy, including a proposed [[Systematics Agenda 2000]] to create species-level and higher-category databases. He notes that separate priority analyses for different taxa often reach conflicting conclusions, and cites taxon summation and higher taxa as two proposed solutions.

9. **Biodiversity is a social issue.** Human demand drives extinctions, synanthropic spread, and homogenisation. The author argues that the [[Global Environment Facility]], described as the largest single funding source for biodiversity work, is under-exploited by systematists.

10. **Closing warning.** Drawing on Borges's story "Death and the Compass," the author urges that systematic work be interesting, sound, timely, and outward-facing, not self-seeking or "old wine in new bottles."
