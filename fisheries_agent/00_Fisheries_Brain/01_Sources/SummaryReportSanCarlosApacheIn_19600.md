# Fishery Management Program, San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation (1966 Summary Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 summary report, prepared by Richard J. Navarre, Fishery Management Biologist, reviews sport fishery management on the [[San Carlos Apache Indian Reservation]] in Arizona. Management was carried out under a 1958 agreement among the [[San Carlos Apache Tribe]], the [[Bureau of Indian Affairs]], and the [[Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife]], which provided technical assistance and fish for stocking. Fishing use rose 64 percent, from 13,153 man-days in 1965 to 20,534 man-days in 1966. Most use occurred in [[Stock Tank Fishery|stock tanks]] stocked with [[Rainbow Trout]]. The report concludes that the reservation's recreational potential is high but largely unrealized, because access roads, public facilities and new fishing waters are lacking.

## Background & Description

- **Management framework:** Activities followed a 1958 agreement in which the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife agreed to provide technical assistance in managing and developing reservation fishery resources and to supply fish for stocking.
- **Waters managed:**
  - 56 stock tanks under trout management.
  - 44 stock ponds managed as warm-water fisheries.
  - Streams confined to the Black, Salt and San Carlos Rivers, which are not easily accessible to anglers.
  - [[Seneca Lake]], a 27.5 surface acre reservoir in the northwest corner of the reservation, the only water body built specifically for fishing.
- **Fish species referenced:** [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[White Bass]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Black Crappie]], [[Green Sunfish]].
- **Report status:** Dated February 15, 1967, distributed to the San Carlos Tribal Council, BIA offices, Washington and Regional offices, and Fishery Services Field Offices. It was approved by the Division of Fishery Services, Phoenix, Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water supply:** Heavy snow and rainfall early in 1966 produced heavy runoff that filled most tanks by the start of the fishing season, assuring adequate water for a good fishery all year.
- **Warm-water tanks:** [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] reproduced successfully in the tanks, so no stocking was needed there.
- **Seneca Lake limitations:** Fishing was poor through the summer of 1966 because of high water temperatures and dissolved oxygen depletions in the deeper, cooler waters.
- **Black River habitat:** [[Smallmouth Bass]] introduced in 1964 reached maturity and spawned successfully in the [[Black River]] in 1966. Development of this fishery is deferred until access roads into the [[Black River Canyon]] are built.
- **Access constraints:** The lack of public use facilities (picnic tables, fireplace units) at Seneca Lake suppressed camping and fishing trips. Fishing on the Black and Salt Rivers is severely curtailed by the lack of access roads into the canyon.
- **Competition from reservoirs:** Warm-water fishing in tanks was unpopular because nearby large, easily accessible reservoirs were preferred by anglers.

## Key Findings

**Fishing use and stocking (1966)**

- Man-days of fishing rose 64 percent, from 13,153 (1965) to 20,534 (1966) (Figure 1).
- 56 stock tanks under trout management were stocked with 27,886 rainbow trout weighing 4,427 pounds (text figures).
- Most popular trout waters: Gimme, Upper Highway, B.S. and Dry Lake.
- 44 stock ponds were managed as warm-water fisheries; no fish stocking was needed because species reproduced successfully.
- 135 yearling [[White Bass]] weighing 27 pounds were stocked in three tanks to test their suitability for pond management (Table 2).
- Most warm-water fishing occurred at Big Boni, Big Bonito and Bobcat.

**Stream fishery**

- [[Smallmouth Bass]] planting schedule on the Black River was completed in 1966 with 3,300 three-inch fingerlings weighing 55 pounds, the third and last scheduled planting (earlier plantings in 1964 and 1965).
- 4,000 seven-inch [[Channel Catfish]] weighing 222 pounds were planted in the Black River.

**Seneca Lake**

- Trout fishing was good in spring 1966 but declined sharply in June, and was poor through the summer due to high water temperatures and low dissolved oxygen in deeper waters.
- 14,500 catchable-size rainbow trout weighing 3,722 pounds were stocked in 1966.

**Data discrepancies in the source**

- Table 1 totals list 41,354 RbT and 9,584 pounds for all trout tanks, which differ from the 27,886 fish and 4,427 pounds given in the text for stock tanks.
- Table 1 lists Seneca Lake with 13,468 RbT weighing 5,157 pounds, which differ from the 14,500 fish and 3,722 pounds given in the text.

**Recreational development recommendations**

- Recreational potential is high, and gross returns to the Tribe would exceed $1,000,000 annually if it were fully developed.
- Long-range goals proposed:
  1. Construct twenty trout lakes totaling at least 600 surface acres, each with complete public use facilities.
  2. Improve reservation roads, notably the route from Highway 70 through Point of Pines, across the Black River, to Maverick and Highway 666.
  3. Make the [[Black River Canyon]] bottom accessible from the Black River Pumping Station upstream to the eastern boundary, with campgrounds along the river.
  4. Develop summer home sites in the Ponderosa Pine regions, near fishing lakes (the pine flats at Point of Pines suggested).
  5. Establish tribal businesses to serve tourists and summer recreationists, with Point of Pines as a key business area.
- Keep game and fish permit sales funds in a separate account for lake and campground construction so developments become self-generating.
- The Bureau of Indian Affairs funded a new fishing lake on [[Point of Pines Creek]]; construction began in 1966 but was slow, and opening it to public fishing with campgrounds was expected to be slower.
- Continued growth in fishing pressure depends on establishing new fishing waters.
