# Biochemistry and Morphology of Upper Klamath Lake Suckers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1991 final report, submitted by Phillip M. Harris (Oregon State University) to the [[Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife]], tests whether [[Shortnose Sucker]] (*Chasmistes brevirostris*), [[Lost River Sucker]] (*Deltistes luxatus*) and [[Klamath Largescale Sucker]] (*Catostomus snyderi*) hybridize in the Upper Klamath Lake basin. Both the shortnose and Lost River suckers had declined sharply over the previous 50 years and were listed as Endangered Species, so identifying pure and hybrid individuals was considered essential for propagation and conservation.

Allozyme electrophoresis found **no evidence of hybridization**. A single fixed difference at the LDH-B2* locus separated shortnose suckers from Klamath largescale suckers, with a maximum estimated probability of undetected hybridization of 6%. Morphological work on juveniles was preliminary but showed good discriminant classification. Propagation of crosses was only partly successful because of larval die-offs.

## Background & Description

**Species and decline.** Populations of the shortnose and Lost River suckers declined over roughly 50 years. The stated causes were extensive habitat alteration, overfishing of spawning stocks, possible hybridization, and little or no recruitment (Bienz and Ziller, 1986). The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service listed both species as Endangered (1988).

**Hybridization hypothesis.** Hybridization between shortnose and Klamath largescale suckers had been proposed on the basis of reduced gillraker counts and substantial changes in mouth morphology (Andreasen, 1975; Miller and Smith, 1981; C. Bond, pers. comm.). Miller and Smith (1981) gave hybridization estimates from morphology as high as 55%.

**Objectives.**
1. Test the hybridization hypothesis by identifying pure and hybrid individuals from their biochemistry.
2. Use external morphology as independent corroboration.
3. Examine biochemistry and morphology of juveniles from known reciprocal crosses among the three species.

**Sampling.** Muscle and plasma were the main tissues. Eye and liver were also collected from some groups. Tissues were stored at -80 °C. Fish came from five locations in the basin:
- Klamath largescale suckers from the [[Williamson River]] (upper reach, 4 May 1990) and the [[Chiloquin Dam]] fish ladder on the [[Sprague River]] (19 March to 1 May 1990), plus mortalities at [[Braymill Fish Hatchery]].
- Shortnose suckers from the Sprague River (19 March to 8 May 1990) and the Lower Williamson River, plus juvenile losses at Braymill Hatchery (27 April 1990).
- Lost River suckers from the Sprague River (18 April to 7 May 1990), the [[Link River Dam]] (2 October 1990, collected by D. Lassuy), and Sucker Springs (March and April 1991).

**Methods.** Horizontal starch gel electrophoresis examined 17 enzymes (Table 1), giving 24 loci in the interspecific analysis. Analyses used BIOSYS-1 (Swofford and Selander, 1981). Each species at each site was treated as a separate population. Key tests were [[Heterozygosity]] (Nei, 1978), [[Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium]] exact tests, [[Nei's Genetic Distance]], [[Wright's F-Statistics]], and the "Level 1" maximum-probability method of Moyle and Berg (1991) for undetected hybridization.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Spawning timing.** Migration windows reported by Andreasen (1975) were mid-March to early April for largescale suckers, late March to mid-April for Lost River suckers, and from mid-April for shortnose suckers. Dunsmoor's 1985–1991 field observations shifted these later: mid-March to early April (largescale), mid-April to early May (Lost River), and mid-May to early June (shortnose). Historical mass spawning of all three species in the Lower Williamson and Sprague rivers was reported for the 1940s and 1950s. Separate spawning times are cited as evidence against hybridization.
- **Lake conditions.** The authors cite "declining environmental conditions in the lake" as one possible factor in the absence of the species *stomias* (see Morphological Change below).
- **Hatchery effects.** Hatchery-reared fish and artificial selection could shift allele frequencies, but the author judged this of little concern for managers.
- **Population structure.** Sampling suggests multiple local populations, and the spawning populations within Upper Klamath Lake and Agency Lake are not fully described.

## Key Findings

**Hybridization test (biochemistry)**
- No evidence of hybridization was found. No fixed allelic differences occurred at the species level.
- The exception was LDH-B2* in eye tissue: shortnose sucker (Sprague River) had LDH-B2*95 and Klamath largescale sucker (Williamson) had LDH-B2*100. Based on LDH-B2*95, the maximum probability of undetected hybridization was **6%**.
- LDH-B2*95 occurred in 36% of Williamson largescale suckers and in 93% of Lost River suckers at Link River Dam. The authors interpret it as a possible primitive character shared by all three species, noting that California populations of shortnose and Lost River suckers showed it at 100%.

**Interspecific diversity**
- Of 24 loci, 12 varied in at least one species.
- Klamath largescale sucker: mean H = 0.10, %P = 41.7.
- Shortnose sucker: H = 0.08, %P = 25.0.
- Lost River sucker: H = 0.07, %P = 33.3.
- Nei's D: 0.077 (largescale vs Lost River), 0.064 (largescale vs shortnose), 0.059 (shortnose vs Lost River). The authors note that D depends on locus number, so 24 loci probably underestimate true divergence.

**Shortnose sucker populations**
- Four of 18 loci varied in the two populations. Both had %P = 22.2.
- H was 0.09 in the Lower Williamson population (SNS-W) and 0.05 in the Sprague River population (SNS-S).
- Mean Fst = 0.264, described as "very great" differentiation. This was driven by sME-A*125 (frequency 0.95 in SNS-S), which the author flags as possibly an artifact of small sample sizes (n = 6 vs 19).
- Mean F-IS = 0.177 and F-IT = 0.395 indicate heterozygote deficiency.

**Klamath largescale sucker populations**
- Sprague River: 9 of 24 loci varied, H = 0.11, %P = 37.5. Four loci deviated from HW (heterozygote deficiency). Three unique alleles were found, none in other populations.
- Williamson River: 3 of 18 loci varied, H = 0.05, %P = 16.7. One locus (mAAT-2*) deviated from HW.

**Lost River sucker populations**
- Williamson River: 2 of 16 loci varied, H = 0.05, %P = 12.5. No locus deviated from HW and no diagnostic alleles were found.
- Sucker Springs: H = 0.04, %P = 26.7. Three of four polymorphic loci deviated from HW, and three rare alleles were present.
- Link River Dam: all six polymorphic loci deviated from HW. Two unique alleles (sME-B*90, TPI*80) were found relative to Sucker Springs, which may indicate an unidentified population.

**Morphology (preliminary)**
- A discriminant function on juveniles correctly classified 100% of shortnose suckers, 85% of largescale suckers, and 90% of Lost River suckers.
- Principal component analysis separated smaller shortnose suckers (20.9–49.2 mm SL) by head width at the pectoral fins.
- Caudal peduncle depth may help identify Lost River juveniles in Upper Klamath Lake (Markle, pers. obs.).

**Reciprocal crosses (Objective 3)**
- Propagation was only partly successful. Larvae suffered massive die-offs in both 1990 and 1991, producing incomplete developmental series.
- No female largescale suckers in spawning condition were collected during the other species' spawning periods.
- Larvae are being held for future [[Mitochondrial DNA]] and PCR work.

**Morphological change in shortnose suckers**
- Gilbert (1898) described *C. brevirostris* with a mouth angle of about 15° and *C. stomias* with an angle above 45°. The current *brevirostris*-type fish resemble his *brevirostris* description, while 1940s–1950s fish resemble *stomias*. If two species once existed, the synonymy of *stomias* with *brevirostris* is wrong.
- Explanations offered include misidentification, age-related mouth change (possibly maturing after age 12–16), overharvest in the 1920s–1930s, and phenotypic plasticity. A declining mean gillraker count is not explained by the age hypothesis. Plasticity in gillrakers is supported by work on ciscoes (Todd et al., 1981) and bloater (Crowder, 1984).

**Recommendations**
1. Move to [[Mitochondrial DNA]] analysis (restriction endonucleases), since allozymes gave incomplete resolution in adults. Future collections should focus on eggs and hatchery gonadal and liver tissue, and muscle if mtDNA work proceeds.
2. Survey other spawning populations of shortnose and Lost River suckers in Upper Klamath Lake and Agency Lake. Conduct demographic and genetic studies before adding populations to propagation efforts.
3. Use allozymes on larvae to find diagnostic alleles that are masked in juveniles and adults.
4. Recover DNA from museum specimens to test changes in morphology and the possibility of hybridization over time.
