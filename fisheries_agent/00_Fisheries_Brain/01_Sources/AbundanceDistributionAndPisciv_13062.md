## Executive Summary

This study documents the invasion, spread, abundance, and feeding ecology of two non-native piscivorous fishes—[[Northern Pike]] (*Esox lucius*) and [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*)—in the Green River basin of Utah and Colorado. Northern pike, introduced into the Yampa River in 1977, expanded downstream through the Green River system, with documented movements of 78–110 km in about one year. Walleye, introduced decades earlier (1950s) via the Duchesne River, were widespread but declining in abundance relative to pike between 1981 and 1988. Diet analysis revealed both species consume a mix of native and non-native fishes, with a preference for soft-rayed prey. No direct predation on rare or endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]] was documented, but habitat overlap and dietary breadth raise concern for recovery efforts targeting species such as [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], and [[Razorback Sucker]]. No evidence of successful recruitment (age-0 fish) was found for either introduced predator, despite documented breeding condition in female pike.

## Background & Description

- **Northern Pike (n=84 collected, 1979–1989):**
  - Mean total length (TL): 619 mm
  - Range: 321–1,045 mm
  - All specimens considered adults or large juveniles.

- **Walleye (n=79 collected, 1979–1989):**
  - Mean total length (TL): 511 mm
  - Range: 395–686 mm
  - All specimens considered adults or large juveniles.

- **Reproductive Status:**
  - 33 female northern pike captured in breeding condition (mature ovaries with ripe eggs), April–June, water temps 10–19°C.
  - Native spawning temperature range for *E. lucius* is 4.4–11.1°C (Scott and Crossman 1973), suggesting Green River spawning (if occurring) happens at elevated temperatures relative to native range.
  - One female walleye in breeding condition captured in May at 13°C water temperature (native spawning range: 5.6–11.1°C).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat Use:**
  - Northern pike: shallow, low-velocity shoreline habitats with prominent aquatic and bank vegetation — consistent with habitat preferences documented in other river systems ([[Diana et al. 1977]], [[Chapman and Mackay 1984]]).
  - Walleye: slow shoreline runs associated with emergent or bank vegetation; native habitat preference includes deep, turbid large rivers with weed beds, woody debris, boulders, and shoals.
  - 78% of pike (n=59) and 90% of walleye (n=50) were captured in Stratum F of the Green River (1984–1988).
  - 43% of pike within Stratum F were captured near [[Ashley Creek]]; walleye were more dispersed.

- **Spatial Expansion:**
  - Pike confined to upper river strata (F, G) from 1981–1986.
  - By 1987, pike invaded mid-section Stratum D.
  - First captured in lower Green River (Stratum A) in 1988.
  - Documented long-distance movement: one pike moved ~110 km (Apr 1982–May 1983); another moved 78 km (Jun 1988–May 1989) — demonstrating high mobility (>75 km/year) in large western rivers.

- **Population Trends:**
  - Pike abundance increased 1984–1988 (catch rate 0.05 → 0.14 fish/hr).
  - Walleye abundance decreased over same period (0.09 → 0.02 fish/hr).
  - Pike/walleye ratio rose from 0.5 (1981) to 12.0 (1988), suggesting possible competitive displacement — northern pike are known to prey on and compete with walleye ([[Scott and Crossman 1973]]).
  - Walleye may also be self-limiting via cannibalism ([[Mathias and Li 1982]]).

- **Recruitment:**
  - No age-0 (young-of-year) northern pike or walleye captured despite evidence of breeding condition, suggesting low or non-existent recruitment in the Green River system.

- **Sympatry with Endangered Species:**
  - Northern pike and [[Colorado Squawfish]] documented using similar habitats (shoreline runs, backwaters) via direct capture and radiotelemetry ([[Valdez and Masslich 1989]], [[Wick and Hawkins 1989]]).
  - Raises concern for potential competition for food resources between pike, walleye, and native piscivore Colorado squawfish.

## Key Findings

- **Northern Pike Diet (n=123 stomachs):**
  - 54.5% empty stomachs.
  - Of stomachs with food, 97.6% contained fish; 2.4% non-fish items.
  - 12 fish species identified as prey (9 non-native, 3 native).
  - Dominant prey: [[Red Shiner]] (*Notropis lutrensis*) and [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*).
  - Native prey included [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Bluehead Sucker]].
  - Non-fish items: leopard frog (*Rana pipiens*), king snake (*Lampropeltis* sp.), detritus.
  - Only spiny-rayed fish consumed was channel catfish (found in 2 stomachs) — supports literature consensus that pike prefer soft-rayed prey.
  - 13 stomachs (10.6%) contained unidentifiable fish remains.
  - 76% of breeding-condition female pike had empty stomachs, suggesting a "spawning fast" phenomenon consistent with [[Frost 1954]].

- **Walleye Diet (n=61 stomachs):**
  - 39.3% empty stomachs.
  - 16.4% contained unidentifiable fish remains.
  - 6 fish species identified as prey (5 non-native, 1 native).
  - Dominant prey: [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) and fathead minnow.
  - Only native prey item: flannelmouth sucker.
  - Notably, 10 channel catfish and 1 black bullhead (spiny-rayed) were consumed, contrasting with general literature suggesting walleye prefer soft-rayed fish.
  - One stomach contained vascular plant material.

- **Conservation Implications:**
  - No direct evidence of predation on endangered Colorado River fishes ([[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]]) — all soft-rayed species that fit the demonstrated prey preference profile of both predators.
  - Rarity of these endangered species may make predation events difficult to document, especially at vulnerable young-of-year/juvenile stages.
  - Absence of documented recruitment suggests the current predation threat may be limited to standing introduced populations, and could diminish if stocking programs are discontinued.
  - Authors recommend termination of continued stocking of northern pike and walleye in the Green River and tributaries to prevent establishment of self-sustaining populations, in line with the interagency [[Recovery Implementation Program]] for upper Colorado River basin endangered fishes.
