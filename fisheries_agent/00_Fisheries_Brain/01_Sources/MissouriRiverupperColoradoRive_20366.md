# Floodplain Habitat Restoration and Native Fish Recovery in the Missouri and Upper Colorado Rivers

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document, dated April 1995, is a set of technical reviews and correspondence on restoring large regulated rivers for endangered native fishes. Its core is a concept paper by [[Larry W. Hesse]] (River Ecosystems, Inc.) arguing that the [[Missouri River]] and the [[Upper Colorado River]] share physical histories of dam regulation, channelization and loss of flood pulses. Hesse argues that site-specific projects will fail unless they sit inside a system-level plan that restores natural hydrology, sediment transport and [[Dynamic Equilibrium]] in the channel and floodplain.

The remaining sections are reviews of the [[Floodplain Habitat Restoration Program]] FY95 work plan. Reviewers (a Nature Conservancy staffer, a fisheries biologist, a former Colorado River researcher, a Bureau of Reclamation-area hydrologist and an academic biologist) largely agree that the program is dominated by [[Population Augmentation]] for [[Razorback Sucker]]. They call for an ecosystem approach, a conceptual framework with testable hypotheses, and clearer funding and monitoring.

## Background & Description

**Missouri River.** Hesse describes the river as controlled by seven large mainstem earthen dams. The lower reach was channelized and leveed, causing downcutting below the dams, floodplain disconnection, and a top width that either expanded into reservoirs or shrank to a few hundred meters in channelized reaches. Hesse reports very dramatic population losses of [[Flathead Chub]], [[Plains Minnow]], [[Western Silvery Minnow]] and [[Sauger]] within his career, and says at least seventeen native fish species have declined sharply over the past 20 years (Hesse et al. 1993).

Pre-channelization reference data come from a 1923 War Department survey of Nebraska bends including Hamburg, Tobacco and Kansas Bend:

- Active erosion zone width (including the thalweg): about 230 m to more than 1,100 m.
- Active erosion zone of the channelized reach: about 147,310 ha (364,000 acres).
- Floodplain along the channelized reach: more than 770,000 ha (1.9 million acres).
- Mean natural channel width reduced from 720 m (2,363 ft) to 240 m (789 ft) by channelization (Morris et al. 1968).
- Thalweg maximum width 60 m (197 ft) and maximum depth 6.0 m (19.7 ft) at Tobacco Bend.

Hesse classifies the river as "pulse stable": a recurring flood cycle that native species depend on to complete their life cycles.

**Upper Colorado River.** Citing Minckley (1979), Bishop and Porcella (1980), Stanford and Ward (1986) and Graf (1985), Hesse describes the pre-control basin as having "spectacular variations in discharge," high flood sediment transport, rapids between sand-bottom pools, arroyo cutting, braided and meandering channels, and oxbow lakes and transitory marshes in lower reaches. Hesse sees similarities with the Missouri because both are arid, have spectacular runoff events, and drain the Rocky Mountains to the coast.

**Key species and taxa named.** Endangered or declining taxa include [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]], [[Squawfish]], [[Sturgeon Chub]], [[Sicklefin Chub]], [[Paddlefish]] and sturgeon species. Non-natives of concern are [[Red Shiner]] and sunfish. The documents do not give scientific names or family assignments.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical habitat and the flood pulse.** Hesse argues that protecting physical habitat is a prerequisite for biotic integrity. Pre-control rivers had "topoinstability" (lateral erosion, sedimentation, vertical scour and fill) that was in dynamic equilibrium, where sediment inflow equaled outflow. This created a "full mosaic" of habitats (main-channel banklines, side channels, extra-channel areas) for feeding, spawning, nursery and over-wintering. Hesse suggests this mosaic and the unique hydrologic, sediment, photoperiod and temperature regime helped exclude non-native species.

Channelization removed the mosaic, cut off the flood pulse that triggered reproduction, reduced overbank flow and floodplain productivity, eliminated large woody debris inputs, and trapped sediment behind dams. Reduced flooding also slowed bank erosion, allowing top width to stabilize.

**Habitat selection.** Hesse notes that spawning habitat for razorback sucker is coarse cobble in natural backwaters or on coarse tributary material (Minckley et al. 1991). Adult razorbacks use complex cover in eddies and backwaters, and radio-tracking (Tyus 1987) found them in current along main-channel margins and mid-channel sandbar pools. Hesse reports that sandbar pools were essential for most native river fishes and that most species prefer some current. Hesse believes optimal habitat no longer exists on the Missouri, so research on least-altered systems may not guide restoration (Tyus 1992).

**Turbidity and non-natives.** Hesse suggests that red shiners should decline with increased turbidity, which would also harm sunfish (Heimstra et al. 1969). He recommends allowing some turbidity from seasonal flow increases and possibly adding physical measures to raise it.

**Water chemistry and contaminants.** Selenium is a major concern. Reviewers note the need for precise sediment selenium data. Hesse recommends a broad sampling program to measure floodplain selenium at high flows, limited to how selenium levels relate to discharge. [[Selenium]] impacts on razorback survival and growth are discussed but considered lower priority unless a plan exists to eliminate selenium if it limits populations.

**Navigation and regulation.** On the Missouri, navigation is the main barrier to restoring channel width, since the Corps of Engineers must keep a navigation channel. Hesse notes there is no navigation on the Colorado, which removes one hurdle. The revision of the Master Water Control Manual took about five years and satisfied almost no one.

## Key Findings

**1. Hesse's central thesis (1995 concept paper):**
- Site-specific projects must be part of a larger, implementable system plan. Re-connecting [[Boyer Chute]] (Nebraska, 1992) and [[Grand Pass]] (Missouri, pumped water) showed limited benefits so far.
- Recovery needs dynamic interaction among floodplain, sediment load and runoff. Projects with water control structures cannot be called "natural" or "dynamic."
- The Corps of Engineers accepts in principle that reconnecting cut-off bends and a semblance of the natural hydrograph can help native species. Restoration would require re-engineering the cross-section of affected bends.

**2. Hesse's specific recommendations:**
- Prioritize the floodplain between thalweg and bankfull elevations. An inventory (per Appendix II of the Flooded Bottomland package) had not covered this zone.
- Focus first on a reach of the [[Green River]] from river mile 264 to 305, near known spawning habitat.
- Acquire a continuous riparian corridor, for example 50 m on each bank along the 64 km reach, which is cheaper because it is typically low-value, flood-prone land.
- Alter water management to mimic historical volume and timing as far as possible.
- Allow bankfull discharge across restored sites, using main-channel diversion structures and reduced chute closures.
- Use the 1923 cross-section and simulated discharge to calculate a site-specific bankfull discharge.
- Develop six protocols: flooded bottomland projects for razorback production; a contiguous corridor along the Green, Colorado and Gunnison; annual overbank flooding of the new floodplain; reopened cut-off channels without control structures; main-channel top-width increase for sandbars; and alternative sediment and organic matter sources.

**3. Baumgartner review (Nature Conservancy, 14 April 1995):**
- Supports the Floodplain Habitat Restoration Program and its Program Goal of restoring natural floodplain functions, but disputes its approach on three grounds: over-focus on [[Population Augmentation]], a [[Site-by-Site Approach]] rather than an ecosystem approach, and no conceptual framework.
- Recommends removing Objective 1 (short-term razorback stabilization) and moving it to the propagation element. Both the Biology and Management Committees had recommended this in February 1995.
- Notes that the program's funding from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] Capital Improvement Program restricts research and monitoring, while suiting site-specific propagation.
- Recommends a conceptual framework with clear objectives and testable hypotheses, citing the [[Flaming Gorge Research Program]] and [[Aspinall Research Program]] as models. Recommends a hypothesis-driven experimental design based on [[Stanford 1994]], revised site selection, and coordination with other recovery elements.
- Site-by-site assessments: [[Old Charlie Wash]] and [[Leota L-7]] target augmentation; L-7 should be put on hold. Walter Walker is a useful selenium research site. Adobe Creek Phase I is a complementary repeat of the Walter Walker study. Adobe Creek Phase II and Johnson, Wyasket and Sheppard should be put on hold. The 29-5/8 Road Gravel Pit comparison is useful but needs study of natural fluvial processes.

**4. Mueller letter (National Biological Service, 26 March 1995):**
- Questions the weak justification for flooding bottomlands. Before spending millions, the hypothesis that lost bottomland limits razorback recruitment should be tested, and other factors such as predator-prey interactions and food production should be examined.
- Points out that the program ignores low flows, trickles and droughts. Drought-period canyon refugia may have concentrated fish and predators, and spring floods dispersed larvae away from predators.
- Recommends small rearing ponds to augment river populations and test predator-prey and food-base questions.

**5. O'Brien memo (3 April 1995):**
- Recommends evaluating bottomland restoration system-wide. Flood frequency and duration losses result from reduced peaks under [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] releases and cumulative upstream depletions and diversions.
- Argues that the river is still responding to declining sediment load, and that channel incision at Ouray has not finished. Tributary flooding is not making up the sediment loss.
- Warns that a large flood (larger than the 1984 event) could degrade the channel several feet or deposit sand near the Leota 7 inlet weir, potentially making the design non-functional.
- Proposes evaluating gages at Jensen, Ouray and Lily (Little Snake) for long-term trends, especially in the ratio of sand to fine sediment. Also lists broad-scale options such as flow augmentation timed to sediment loads and artificial sediment sources.

**6. Bayley comments (20 April 1995):**
- Generally satisfied with the umbrella work plan but raises many specific points:
  - Experiments should be long-term because of year-to-year hydrologic variation.
  - Sites should be abandoned reluctantly in the short term.
  - Monitoring should include environmental factors, not just fish population trends.
  - Experiments need proper non-native controls in which survival is hypothesized to be inferior.
  - Objective 2 should refer to "adaptive management of floodplains and their natural functions."
  - Concerns that the Genetics Panel's definition of "wild population" is imperfect, and that the extant population has survived the changed environment.
  - Questions whether stocking early matches natural spawning timing.
  - Asks why Horsethief Canyon is a reference site, and why Colorado River sites only were chosen for selenium work.
  - Raises long-term O&M cost of highly controlled sites and whether they use up better sites needed for natural restoration.

**7. Arizona State University letter (7 April 1995, signature illegible):**
- Argues that recovery efforts should be primarily biological: prevent extinction, stabilize populations, maintain genetic variability, then expand populations. Habitat construction should follow from this.
- Recommends opportunistic use of any available floodplain habitat, with direct suppression of non-indigenous species, and notes that screening does not work and drainage is best if habitat dries thoroughly.
- Criticizes the "insider" character of the program, and recommends opening the project to outside expertise through public RFPs and formal proposal review.

**8. Overall consensus across reviews:**
- Floodplain restoration is essential to endangered fish recovery.
- Current work is too focused on augmentation and site-by-site management.
- Program success should be judged at the river-system level.
- Long-term sediment and hydrologic trends need to be understood before major investment.
- Funding, monitoring and experimental design need to be overhauled.
