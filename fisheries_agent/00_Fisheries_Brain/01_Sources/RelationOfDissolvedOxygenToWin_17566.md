# Relation of Dissolved Oxygen to Winter Mortality of Fish in Michigan Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how degrees of [[Dissolved Oxygen]] depletion under ice and snow relate to fish mortality and survival in shallow southern Michigan lakes during the [[Winterkill]] of 1944–45. Eleven lakes were sampled. Heavy winterkill occurred only where oxygen fell to approximately 0.6 p.p.m. or less. [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] suffered the greatest losses, while [[Golden Shiner]] and bullheads survived best. The authors estimated at least 2 million fish died across 51 lakes, about three-fourths of them game fish. The work is a field-based comparison of oxygen thresholds and species-specific tolerance, rather than a controlled experiment on causes of death.

## Background & Description

Winterkills of fish on an important scale occurred in many Michigan lakes during 1935–36, 1939–40, 1940–41, and 1944–45. The affected lakes were very shallow, with muck or peat bottoms and abundant vegetation. Most occurred in the southern half of the Lower Peninsula, and the reasons for this distribution were unclear. A questionnaire to conservation officers yielded 51 lakes with heavy kills in 1944–45, and the authors believed the true number exceeded 75. Earlier counts from [[Greenbank]] (1945) recorded 34, 12, and 11 "most serious" lakes for the three earlier winters.

Winterkill is linked to prolonged cloudy ice and heavy snow, which block sunlight needed for [[Photosynthesis]]. Organic decomposition then consumes oxygen faster than it is produced. Oxygen is typically lower at depth, and under extreme depletion it may reach only traces near the surface. Fish death has been attributed to low oxygen, to toxic gases such as [[Hydrogen Sulfide]], methane, or carbon dioxide, or to both. The authors noted this was not settled by controlled experiments, but considered the question secondary to their main contribution: how well fish survive at very low oxygen levels.

The study drew on four data sources: dissolved oxygen profiles from January to March 1945; direct observations of fish behavior and mortality; counts of dead fish stranded on shore when ice broke up; and netting surveys in 1945 and 1946. Oxygen was measured by the unmodified [[Winkler Method]] using a can-type sampler.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lake characteristics.** The eleven lakes ranged from 4 to about 400 acres. Maximum depths were mostly under 20 feet, and average depths ranged from roughly 3 to 7 feet. Most had very small inlets and outlets or none, so little water was exchanged during winter. Rooted plants were abundant in most. The six lakes without heavy winterkill were, in most features, very similar to those that suffered it.

**Snow and ice.** The lakes froze by early December 1944 and were covered with several inches of snow from December 11 to February 15. Snow depth averaged about 3 inches (range 2–5). Ice averaged about 6 inches on January 12, 10 inches on January 29, and 12 inches on February 12. Snow and ice stayed dry, with little meltwater.

**Thaw.** A 24-hour thaw with light rain on February 14–15 cleared most snow and admitted surface water through ice fractures. This marked the end of the downward oxygen trend, and oxygen began to recover. Further thaws in late February and early March added more water. The lakes were clear of ice by March 16.

**Oxygen stratification.** Surface samples (6 inches to 1 foot deep) almost always contained the most oxygen. Under severe depletion, oxygen survived only in a thin layer just beneath the ice.

**Fish communities.** Species examined included largemouth bass, bluegill, black crappie, pumpkinseed, warmouth, mud pickerel, northern pike, three bullhead species, tadpole madtom, lake chubsucker, yellow perch, Iowa darter, golden shiner, three shiner species (blackchin, blacknose, and others), central mudminnow, blackstripe topminnow, brook silverside, and bowfin, 21 species in all.

**Study lakes.** Green Lake (about 90 acres) had a single inlet of roughly 150 yards with a flow near 50 gallons per minute, which served as a refuge for many fish. [[First Sister Lake]] had no inlet or outlet. A wind-driven air pump there kept an opening in the ice, but it did not raise oxygen levels. [[Batteese Lake]] was among the lakes with the widest range of oxygen readings.

## Key Findings

**Oxygen threshold for heavy kill.** Heavy winterkills occurred only where dissolved oxygen fell to about 0.6 p.p.m. or below. Oxygen in the study lakes reached maximum concentrations of 0.2 to above 5.0 p.p.m. near the time of greatest depletion.

**Species-specific mortality.** Largemouth bass and bluegills suffered the greatest mortality, reaching apparently 100 percent in some lakes. Pumpkinseed, mud pickerel, northern pike, lake chubsucker, bullheads, and golden shiner showed high survival, even where oxygen fell to 0.3 or 0.2 p.p.m. No lake lost all its fish.

**Mortality estimate.** Counts of dead fish from seven lakes indicated at least 2 million dead fish across 51 lakes, about three-fourths of them game fish. The authors called these figures conservative, because some dead fish sink and small ones disintegrate.

**Windrowed kill.** In Batteese, Goose, Green, and Merkle lakes, about 330,000 dead fish were windrowed on shore, averaging several hundred per acre. Most were half-grown or adult, and bluegill predominated. Pumpkinseed, bullheads, largemouth bass, chubsucker, yellow perch, and northern pike were also represented.

**Survival after the kill.** Netting in 1945 and 1946 showed that a fair number of bass and bluegills survived in Batteese Lake, a few in Goose Lake, and apparently none in Green or Merkle lakes. Yellow perch, chubsuckers, and northern pike had considerable mortality but higher survival than bass and bluegills. Bullheads and golden shiners survived best.

**Selective winterkill.** The kill was selective against the species most valued by anglers. The desirable game fish suffered the heaviest losses, while the least wanted species survived best. The authors noted a similar pattern reported by Eddy and Surber (1943) in Minnesota.

**Behavior under low oxygen.** Fish gathered in inlets and near air holes. At Green Lake, an estimated 2,000 to 4,000 fish were in the inlet on January 29, rising to about 15,000 by January 31. Oxygen at the head of the inlet was 7.1 p.p.m., but only 0.05 p.p.m. at its mouth. At First Sister Lake, an estimated 10,000 to 20,000 fish, mostly golden shiners and bullheads, were milling at a hole with surface oxygen of about 0.2–0.3 p.p.m. on February 14, and many lived for at least a week below 0.5 p.p.m.

**Site-level outcomes.**
- *Cavanaugh, Four Mile, Mill, Sugarloaf, Cassidy, and Grass lakes:* minimal or no kill, with winter minima at or above about 0.7 p.p.m.
- *Green Lake:* oxygen below 1 p.p.m. for at least a week; very heavy kill of bass, bluegill, pumpkinseed, perch, and crappie, with good survival of perch, bullheads, and golden shiners.
- *Merkle Lake:* oxygen 0.2 to 0.0 p.p.m.; very heavy kill, with some survival of bullheads and golden shiners.
- *Goose Lake:* oxygen 0.5 to 0.05 p.p.m.; heavy kill of bluegill and bass, with good survival of perch and bullheads.

**Conclusion.** The authors concluded that the most important point is the extent to which fish survive very low oxygen. Survival depended strongly on species, so lake-level kill severity could not be predicted from oxygen alone without knowing which fish were present.
