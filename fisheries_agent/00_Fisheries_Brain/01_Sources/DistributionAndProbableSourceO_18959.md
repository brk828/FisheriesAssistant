## Executive Summary

This 1980 paper by Silver and Fielden documents a field investigation (1974, 1977) of nitrate concentrations in groundwater across [[Paradise Valley (Arizona)]], a structural basin near Phoenix, Arizona. Analysis of 138 wells revealed nitrate concentrations ranging up to 132 mg/l, well above the U.S. EPA's maximum contaminant level of 10 mg/l NO₃-N for public drinking water established under the [[National Interim Primary Drinking Water Regulations]]. The authors propose **two competing hypotheses** for the nitrate source: (1) a conventional anthropogenic explanation involving nitrogenous fertilizers and treated wastewater effluent, and (2) an alternative natural-geologic explanation involving ancient volcanic ammonium salts (NH₄Cl) leached from tuffs in the [[Superstition Mountains]], oxidized to nitrate, and deposited within a buried braided-stream sand and gravel unit ~152 m (500 ft) below the surface. The paper does not definitively resolve which mechanism is responsible, instead advocating for a "multiple working hypotheses" approach to groundwater contamination investigations.

## Sedimentological and Stratigraphic Characteristics

**Aquifer system stratigraphy and composition:**
- **Lower alluvium**: sand and gravel near basin margins, grading to silt/clay centrally; contains basaltic/andesitic interbeds.
- **Middle alluvium**: clay/silt-dominated with interbedded coarse sand bodies up to 75 m (245 ft) thick; interpreted as a [[Braided-Stream Deposit]] and playa lake complex.
- **Upper alluvium**: unconsolidated sand, silt, gravel, with boulder-sized fragments near the Salt River.
- **Papago Buttes Conglomerate**: basal Tertiary strata — indurated siltstone, sandstone, conglomerate.
- The nitrate-bearing sand body is linear, trending east-west, 1–2 km (0.6–1.2 mi) wide, consistent with braided-stream channel geometry (per [[Ore 1965]] criteria).

## Hydrogeological and Environmental Setting

- **Basin setting**: [[Paradise Valley Basin]] is a northwest-trending structural trough between the Phoenix and McDowell Mountains, filled with late Tertiary–Quaternary alluvium, covering ~640 km² (247 mi²).
- **Aquifers**: Principal aquifers are the lower and middle alluvium units; the upper alluvium is a strong aquifer but largely above the water table except in the southern valley. Conglomeratic units serve as local aquifers in the south.
- **Hydraulic properties**: Transmissivities of 770–2,234 kl/day/m; horizontal groundwater flow rates of 1–304 m/yr; unsaturated zone vertical percolation estimated at ~3 m/yr under irrigation.
- **Anthropogenic pressures**: Rapid population growth increasing reliance on groundwater; irrigation on the [[Salt River Indian Reservation]]; individual septic tank systems; seepage from the [[Arizona Canal]]; upgradient wastewater discharge from the Mesa treatment plant.
- **Paleoenvironmental context**: Depositional environment during the Quaternary included alluvial fan, braided-stream, and playa lake settings similar to present-day conditions — comparable to nitrate-evaporite associations described in the [[Chilean Nitrate Deposits]] (Atacama-type systems).

## Key Findings

- **Nitrate distribution**: Average nitrate across 138 wells = 14 mg/l (8.7 mg/l excluding 10 high-value wells >45 mg/l). Northern two-thirds of the valley: <8.0 mg/l NO₃. Southern third: highly variable, with 5 wells at 20–44 mg/l and 10 wells at 45–132 mg/l.
- **Spatial pattern**: Two narrow, east-west trending linear belts of high nitrate, connecting near [[Camelback Mountain]], correlate with a buried sand body at the base of the middle alluvium — interpreted as a braided-stream channel deposit.
- **Health context**: Nitrate >10 mg/l NO₃-N linked to infant methemoglobinemia (blue baby syndrome); potential nitrosamine formation in the human GI tract raises carcinogenicity concerns; nitrate also harmful to certain crops at critical growth stages.
- **Anthropogenic contamination hypothesis**: Fertilizer use (especially on the Salt River Indian Reservation) and treated wastewater effluent identified as plausible nitrate sources; septic tanks contribute background nitrate but are not spatially correlated with high-nitrate zones; feedlots and canal seepage considered minor/inconclusive contributors.
- **Natural-source hypothesis**: No correlation observed between well depth and nitrate concentration, arguing against simple surface infiltration. A specific sand/gravel horizon (interpreted braided-stream channel) is consistently associated with high-nitrate wells. Authors propose NH₄Cl derived from Superstition Mountain tuffs was oxidized to nitrate and concentrated via evaporation in abandoned stream channels, later buried by clay.
- **Attempted fertilizer isotopic tracing**: Application of the Kreitler and Jones (1976) technique to distinguish fertilizer-derived nitrogen was inconclusive and nonreproducible.
- **Regional correlation**: The nitrate-rich linear belt aligns with a regional trend of poor-quality groundwater extending northwest across the Salt River Valley and with similar linear belts identified further west, suggesting a laterally extensive paleochannel system.
- **Conclusion**: The authors could not definitively distinguish between anthropogenic (fertilizer/wastewater) and natural (volcanogenic/braided-stream) origins for the nitrate anomalies and recommend a multiple-working-hypotheses approach for future groundwater quality investigations.
