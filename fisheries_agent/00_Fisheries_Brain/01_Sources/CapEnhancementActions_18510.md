# Lower Colorado River CAP Enhancement Suggestions for Native and Sport Fishes (1993)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 letter from [[C.O. Minckley]], Project Coordinator at the [[Parker Fishery Resource Office]] (FRO), responds to a request from [[Bureau Of Reclamation]] staff for fishery-related enhancement actions that could be funded or supplied through the [[Central Arizona Project]] (CAP) on the lower [[Colorado River]]. The letter covers refuge management, inland fisheries and related factors from Davis Dam downstream. It states that sport fish will not be actively managed or supplemented, but that native fish, particularly [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], will receive active management through growout facilities on refuges. It also proposes CAP water for riparian restoration on the [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]] and for flows in the [[Gila River]] below [[San Carlos Dam]]. The author stresses that these measures should be integrated across the ecosystem and supported by long-term, biologically sound monitoring.

## Background & Description

The letter is a formal, single-author correspondence dated 10 August 1993 and addressed to Mr. Bruce Ellis of the Arizona Projects Office. It was sent in reply to a request for suggestions for CAP enhancement actions. It is not a research report, and it contains no new field data, sample sizes or statistics.

The Parker FRO has three roles described in the letter:

- It provides fishery assistance and addresses management needs for the [[Lower Colorado River National Wildlife Refuges]] and nearby Indian Tribes.
- It is the lead station for the "big river fishes": [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]].
- It represents the Arizona Fishery Resource Office (AZFRO) on other listed and nonlisted native fishes. These include [[Little Colorado Spinedace]], [[Spikedace]], [[Woundfin]], [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Loach Minnow]], longfin dace and Rio Yaqui fishes.

Unless stated otherwise, the letter addresses three refuges: [[Lake Havasu National Wildlife Refuge]], [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]] and [[Imperial National Wildlife Refuge]]. The author notes that the refuge water needs document provided at a 30 July meeting covers water needs well but does not discuss fishery issues in detail. The author is writing a refuge fishery management plan, which is the reason for addressing this topic.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sport fish.** Sport fish on the refuges are not being actively managed, and no supplemental stockings are planned. Populations are to be maintained by natural reproduction. A monitoring schedule will assess their status and species composition, at a frequency of twice yearly or more often depending on the refuge. Any extra water delivered for revegetation or other purposes could benefit these populations in selected areas. The author cannot foresee requesting CAP water specifically for sport fish.

**Native fish.** Active management is planned for bonytail chub and razorback sucker. The plan involves growout facilities on all lower Colorado River refuges to produce larger individuals (greater than 22 inches) for release into the wild. The work includes developing currently isolated backwaters and building small dredged ponds. Enhancement in this context could take the form of equipment and personnel. Additional water could allow growout facilities in areas that are currently marginal, and it would increase habitat for these species. Newly developed areas are to be managed for all wildlife to help maintain and improve biodiversity. The author notes this type of development is also feasible on the Chemehuevi and Colorado River Indian tribal lands.

**Riparian corridor.** On the [[Bill Williams River National Wildlife Refuge]], CAP water could support maintenance and revegetation of the [[Riparian Corridor]] and help re-establish native fishes. The letter proposes supplying water to the City of Scottsdale in place of pumping from the Planet Ranch aquifer, which would make the water available for the refuge.

**Gila River system.** The letter suggests CAP water be delivered to the [[Gila River Indian Tribe]] under its allocation. Water the Tribe would normally use could be stored in San Carlos Reservoir and released on a scheduled basis to maintain the riparian corridor and native fishes below the dam. The author notes that the [[Gila Topminnow]], [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]] recovery plans are complete but have not been implemented, and describes this as an opportunity to act.

**Integration.** The author stresses that these proposals should not be standalone actions. They should integrate the wider biological community and support the "Area of Ecological Concern" in the draft [[Lower Colorado River National Wildlife Refuge Comprehensive Management Plan]]. In the Gila River Basin, complete river sections with associated springs and cienega habitats should be considered rather than isolated fragments.

## Key Findings

1. **No active sport fish management.** Sport fish on refuges will be left to natural reproduction. Supplemental stocking is not planned, and CAP water is not requested for sport fish. Monitoring will occur twice yearly or more often, depending on the refuge.

2. **Active native fish management.** Bonytail chub and razorback sucker will be managed through growout facilities on all lower Colorado River refuges. The aim is to produce individuals larger than 22 inches for release. Refuge development will be designed to benefit all wildlife and biodiversity.

3. **Water for habitat and growout.** Additional water could expand habitat, enable growout facilities in marginal areas and support riparian revegetation.

4. **Bill Williams River reach.** CAP water, supplied to Scottsdale in place of Planet Ranch aquifer pumping, could support riparian maintenance and native fish re-establishment on the Bill Williams River refuge.

5. **Gila River below San Carlos Dam.** Allocating the Gila River Indian Tribe's CAP water and releasing stored San Carlos Reservoir water over time could maintain riparian and native fish habitat. The author highlights that completed recovery plans for the Gila topminnow, spikedace and loach minnow remain unimplemented.

6. **Integrated, long-term approach.** Proposals should be linked to one another and to the refuge comprehensive plan. Studies below [[Parker Dam]] should track re-introduced fish and their interactions with sport fish. Long-term, biologically sound programs are needed to inform future native fish management.
