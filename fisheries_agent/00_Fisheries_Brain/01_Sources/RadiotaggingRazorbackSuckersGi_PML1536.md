# Radio-Tagging Razorback Suckers in the Gila River, Arizona, 1988–1989

#ichthyology #marine-biology #razorback-sucker

## Executive Summary

This abstract, by Paul C. Marsh and W. L. Minckley of the [[Center for Environmental Studies]] and the Department of Zoology (13 June 1989), reports radio-tracking of hatchery-propagated and wild [[Razorback Sucker]] stocked into the [[Gila River]] near Safford, Arizona, in 1988. Two release groups were tracked: 14 fish in May 1988 and nine fish in October 1988. Both groups showed short- and longer-term downstream movement, with few fish moving upstream. Most fish were lost from contact by 1989. The authors consider the likely fate of the lost fish to be either death with transmitters undetected or movement into [[San Carlos Reservoir]], where radio signals attenuated sharply with depth.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Razorback Sucker]], a large catostomid of the Colorado River system that is the focus of reintroduction efforts.
- **Fish sources:**
  - Four wild adults trammel-netted 20–23 May 1988 from [[Lake Mohave]], Arizona-Nevada.
  - Ten hatchery-propagated F1 fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], New Mexico (ages given in the OCR as 4, 6, or 7 years).
  - A second group of nine 4-year-old hatchery fish from Dexter, surgically tagged under controlled conditions.
- **Tagging method:** Internally implanted radio transmitters ([[Radio Telemetry]]) were placed by [[Surgical Implantation]]. Fish were held in an outdoor pond for recovery after the second surgery. One fish died the day after surgery and another about a month later.
- **Release site:** The old Safford Bridge on the Gila River, Greenlee County, Arizona.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Stream conditions:** Occupied channels were typically shallow, with depths rarely exceeding 0.5–0.8 m. Habitats were mostly wide, sandy, mid-channel runs. Other fish used eddies, quiet backwaters, and pools next to the main flow.
- **Dispersal pattern:** Downstream movement dominated. Few fish moved upstream.
- **Regional comparison:** Similar habitat use was reported for studies on the [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]] in Colorado and Utah. The authors note that these upper basin streams differ substantially in size and other features from the Gila River.
- **Reservoir conditions:** San Carlos Reservoir has shallow shorelines and deltaic areas. Radio transmission there was strongly limited by water depth, which the authors attribute possibly to water conductivity, to transmitter or receiver condition, or to unknown factors.
- **Mortality and loss:** Carcasses and transmitters were recovered with evidence of scavenging or predation (for example, a transmitter with bite marks and a body with talon-related injuries). Earlier high short-term mortality was noted for the May 1988 group.

## Key Findings

### May 1988 Release (14 fish, released 24 May 1988)

- **Status by mid-July 1989:**
  - 2 fish with working transmitters remained alive.
  - 8 fish died, and their transmitters were recovered.
  - Contact was lost with 2 fish after 12 hours.
  - 2 fish died and/or released their transmitters, but these were not retrieved.
- **Upstream movement:** 3 fish moved an average of 7.2 km upstream (range 0.1–20.7 km), with an average of 10 days of contact (range 1–32).
- **Downstream movement:** 11 fish moved an average of 11.9 km downstream (range 1.6–32.0 km), with contact maintained for an average of 19 days (range 4–32).
- **Habitat at occupied locations (n = 30):** Channel width 10 ± 5 m (SD), depth 0.4 ± 0.3 m, current velocity 0.21 ± 0.17 m/s.
- **Habitat difference:** Habitats used by upstream and downstream movers differed significantly.

### October 1988 Release (9 fish, released 12 October 1988)

- **Downstream movement:** 7 fish moved an average of 8.1 km downstream (range 0.1–37.2 km). They remained viable for an average of 39 days (range 1–126).
- **Lost contact:** 2 fish were lost shortly after stocking.
- **Habitat at contact locations (n = 11):** Channel width 10 ± 3 m, depth 0.5 ± 0.4 m, velocity 0.30 ± 0.20 m/s.
- **Velocity comparison:** Contact-location velocity was higher than for the May fish (0.21 ± 0.12 m/s), but the difference was not statistically significant.
- **Transmitter battery life:** Short contact periods for the October fish were attributed to transmitter battery failure, since most batteries had been running for periods approaching their rated lifespans.

### Search Efforts

- **Stream surveys:** Trucks and all-terrain vehicles searched 128 km of major canals and drains in the Safford Valley and the Gila River reach from the San Jose Diversion Dam to the San Carlos Reservoir delta. No fish were located.
- **Reservoir testing (April 1989):**
  - Transmitters suspended 3 m below the surface could not be detected.
  - Reception range at 1 m depth was about 5 m.
  - In air, straight-line detection ranged from about 50 to more than 150 m.
- **Reservoir search:** Shorelines and deltaic areas of both the Gila and San Carlos arms, plus cover near Coolidge Dam, were searched for 3 days without detection.

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- Radio-implanted razorback suckers stocked into the Gila River showed short- and long-term downstream movement, with few upstream movers.
- The ultimate fate of nine fish remains unknown. The authors judge it unlikely that their transmitters were buried too deeply to detect, since transmitters were recovered at depths approaching 0.3 m elsewhere in the study.
- The authors suggest the fish may have entered San Carlos Reservoir, where they became effectively lost to further contact.
- Recommended follow-up includes tracking fish in more confined habitats and intensive sampling of fishes in San Carlos Reservoir.
