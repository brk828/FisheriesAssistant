# Fisheries Survey of the Gila and Mimbres Rivers Drainages (1952–1955)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This New Mexico Department of Game and Fish Project Completion Report (Federal Aid Project F-1-R, July 1955) summarizes a 1952–1955 survey of the [[Gila River]] and [[Mimbres River]] drainages. The survey inventoried fish resources, assessed relative abundance and size structure, and developed management recommendations aimed at sustained yield of game fishes. The report is a synthesis of stream-by-stream surveys that are filed separately with the state agency.

The survey found that trout, particularly [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]] and the native [[Gila Trout]], dominated the cold, high-elevation streams, while warm lower-elevation reaches supported suckers, [[Gila Chub]] and warm-water game fishes. Hybridization between Gila and rainbow trout was widespread, and recommendations covered stocking, regulations, habitat repair, vegetation control and minnow harvest.

## Background & Description

**Study area and purpose.** The waters lie in Catron, Grant, Hidalgo, Luna and Sierra counties. The Gila River is part of the [[Colorado River Basin]], while the Mimbres River drains to the [[Rio Grande]]. Both rise in the Mogollon and Black Mountain ranges, with elevations from 3,700 to 10,775 feet. Life zones range from Lower Sonoran (mesquite and creosote) to Canadian (spruce, fir and aspen). Much of the area lies within the [[Gila National Forest]].

**Scope.** Table 1 lists about 50 streams and lakes, with maximum and minimum elevations and estimated miles of permanent water. Examples include the Gila River (90 miles of permanent water), San Francisco River (90 miles), Iron Creek (70 miles) and Wall Lake (18 acres). Bear Canyon Reservoir (23 acres) and San Simon Lake (1.7 acres) were the three lakes studied with Wall Lake.

**Climate and hydrology.**
- Average annual precipitation at Fort Bayard, 1949–1954: 12.6 inches. At Hedrick Ranch on the East Fork of the Gila: 15.0 inches.
- The Gila River above the gauge near Gila (about 1,870 square miles) showed peak flows in 1932, 1937, 1941 and 1949. The report notes a downward trend over the 21-year record.
- The Mimbres River drainage covers 183 square miles. The 29-year average flow at Bear Canyon was 11.6 cubic feet per second.
- The 1954 mean temperature at Silver City was 57.4 °F, about 3.9 °F above the long-term average.

**Fish introduction history.** Plantings since 1901 included several trout species, catfish, black bass, bullhead and bream. A trout hatchery built in 1923 on the West Fork of the Gila was abandoned in 1935 because of inaccessibility. Local residents reported that trout once extended to near Red Rock, but warming waters have shifted the river toward suckers and warm-water game fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.**
- Stream fish were sampled by electrofishing with a light-weight, 110-volt AC shocker rated at 400 watts. Equipment was packed on horses or mules for remote drainages.
- Sampling used measured reaches with stream blocks (seines or nets) to prevent escape. Estimates were corrected only as stated in the Results section below.
- Lake fish were sampled with experimental gill nets and seines for relative abundance only.
- Trout stomachs were preserved and examined under a dissecting scope.
- Bottom fauna were collected with a square-foot sampler.
- Water chemistry used field tests and laboratory analyses by the [[Soil Conservation Service]].

**Water chemistry.**
- Lakes: pH 8.0–10.5; dissolved oxygen 4.7–12.0 ppm; total hardness 80–240 ppm. All three lakes were classed as hard water.
- Streams varied with elevation. High-elevation streams were medium-hardness waters and lower-elevation streams were hard. For example, Willow Creek at 8,000 ft had pH 7.5 and hardness 110 ppm (CO₂ and other values as recorded); the Gila River at 5,000 ft had pH 8.0 and hardness 110 ppm.

**Habitat.** Barriers included waterfalls (one about seven feet high), extensive dry stream-bed sections that isolate fish, and beaver dams. Log dams installed in the early 1930s provided cover and holding pools for trout.

**Bottom fauna.** Analyses of 24 randomly selected bottom samples showed that [[Trichoptera]] (caddisflies) were the most abundant group, averaging 40.9 per square foot and occurring in 100% of samples. Ephemeroptera (mayflies) averaged 27.5 per square foot (96% occurrence), followed by Diptera (11.1 per square foot). The Mimbres samples (Table 4) closely matched the Gila averages.

**Vegetation.** Dense [[Typha]] growth at San Simon Lake hampered shore angling. Wall Lake had heavy aquatic vegetation.

## Key Findings

**1. Species inventory.** Table 2 lists about 21 fish species (salmonids, centrarchids, ictalurids, catostomids and cyprinids). The most important were:
- Trout: Gila Trout (*Salmo gilae*), Rainbow Trout (*Salmo gairdneri*), Brown Trout (*Salmo trutta*), Brook Trout (*Salvelinus fontinalis*).
- Warm-water game fish: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Smallmouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Channel Catfish]], and bullheads.
- Native non-game fish: Gila Sucker, Mountain Sucker, Colorado River Chub (*Gila robusta*), and minnows such as Longfin Dace and Speckled Dace.

**2. Abundance.**
- Four streams had game-fish populations above 1,000 trout per mile: upper Mimbres River, South Fork of Whitewater Creek, Dry Creek and Iron Creek.
- The Summary cites populations "over 1600 per mile" in four streams, which conflicts with the Results section's 1,000-per-mile figure. Both figures are reported as published.
- Recovery was assumed to be no greater than 73% per section, but Figure 8 values were not corrected for recovery.
- Rough fish were most numerous in Taylor Creek, the Mimbres, Gila, San Francisco and Beaver creeks. Several high-elevation streams had no rough fish.

**3. Distribution by elevation.** The greatest variety and numbers of fish occurred in warm, low-elevation streams. Above 7,000 feet, few fish other than trout and dace were found. At 5,000 feet, suckers, chubs, dace, bass, catfish and others occurred.

**4. Species distributions.**
- *Salmo gairdneri* was found in 30 streams and sometimes made up nearly 100% of the population. It provided most angling of any game species because it lived in more accessible waters.
- *Salmo gilae* was found in 16 streams, usually above 7,000 feet. It is the only native trout. Its range had shrunk sharply and it was in danger of further decline due to fishing and stocking pressure.
- *Salmo trutta* occurred along the entire middle fork of the Gila River and in most tributaries, maintained mainly by natural reproduction.
- *Salvelinus fontinalis* was found only in the South Fork of Whitewater Creek, despite widespread planting.
- Smallmouth bass were found in small numbers in the Gila and East Fork. Largemouth bass were abundant in San Simon Lake, which had excellent natural reproduction.
- Bear Canyon Reservoir held a large [[Bluegill]] population, which was increasing despite heavy fishing pressure.

**5. Hybridization.** Extensive hybridization between *S. gilae* and *S. gairdneri* was observed in many trout streams, with evidence of influence from [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarkii*) in some streams.

**6. Food habits.**
- Aquatic insects formed the bulk of trout diets. Terrestrial insects were important in some streams.
- *S. gairdneri* (Gila, 15 stomachs): Coleoptera and Trichoptera were most important; debris occurred in 100%.
- *S. gairdneri* (Mimbres, 20 stomachs): Similar to the Gila fish.
- *S. gilae* (15 stomachs): Coleoptera and Trichoptera dominated; debris in 80%.
- *S. trutta* (Gila): Diptera most frequent; Hymenoptera highest by average number; debris in 73%.
- *S. fontinalis* (South Fork Whitewater): Diptera dominated; debris in 100%.

**7. Growth.**
- *S. trutta* generally had the best growth rate.
- *S. gilae* was the slowest-growing trout after its first year.
- *S. gairdneri* (Gila, 37 scales): averaged 3.75, 6.00 and 7.62 inches in years 1–3.
- *S. gilae* (57 scales): averaged 4.12, 5.50, 7.37 and 9.37 inches in years 1–4.
- *S. trutta* (24 scales): averaged 5.87, 8.62, 9.62 and 11.75 inches in years 1–4.
- *S. fontinalis* (13 scales): averaged 4.00, 6.62 and 7.75 inches in years 1–3.
- Mimbres rainbow trout (9 scales) grew faster than Gila rainbow trout in corresponding years.
- The age method for *S. gilae* was flagged as not validated.

**8. Size composition.** Most trout populations were under 10 inches, with a normal decline in numbers from small to large sizes. Rough fish were mostly under 12 inches.

**9. Management recommendations.** The report made 12 recommendations, including:
1. Continue supplementary stocking of catchable rainbow trout in accessible trout waters.
2. Liberalize regulations and increase the trout possession limit in remote areas.
3. Repair or install channel-improvement structures on Willow Creek, Gila Creek and Black Canyon Creek.
4. Keep Bear Canyon Reservoir as a bass-bluegill lake. Test channel catfish there in 1955.
5. Control aquatic vegetation in Wall Lake by drawing the water down 4–5 feet annually in June or July. This succeeded in 1954.
6. Control submerged and emergent vegetation in San Simon Lake, possibly by periodic herbicide spraying.
7. Experimentally plant smallmouth bass in seven streams, including the San Francisco and Gila rivers.
8. Plant more harvestable species in San Simon Lake, such as redear sunfish and channel catfish.
9. Permit minnow taking by traps only in designated waters.
10. Experiment with controlling the minnow population in Wall Lake.
11. Conduct a more detailed fishing-pressure study.
12. Continue periodic checks on important fishing waters.

**10. Accessibility and pressure.** Many of the best trout streams are reachable only by pack trip of several miles. From Silver City, reaching many streams requires a 60–90 mile drive plus a 10–15 mile pack trip. This limits fishing pressure there. Bear Canyon Reservoir and Wall Lake are easily reached by car and receive heavy pressure.

**11. Overall conclusions.**
- Precipitation and runoff of the Gila River showed a gradual decline in recent years.
- The range of trout appears to have decreased within recent history.
- Aquatic insect supply was adequate to support good trout populations.
- Fair trout growth was observed overall.
