# An Instream Flow Philosophy for Recovering Endangered Colorado River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by Harold M. Tyus of the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] argues that instream flow decisions for four federally listed [[Endangered Species Act]] fishes of the Colorado River should aim at recovery rather than mere survival. The four species are [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], and [[Bonytail]]. The author contends that flows based on current habitat use may only slow decline, because the fish are occupying marginal, altered habitats. He contrasts a "[[Survival Flows]]" philosophy with a "[[Recovery Flows]]" philosophy, reviews the flow-assessment tools in use, and recommends combining empirical studies, models, simulations, and structured judgment to determine flow needs.

## Background & Description

The paper's abstract describes the four species as remnant stocks persisting in a basin where the fauna is unique and insular. Tyus frames the problem within a broader North American decline. Over the past 100 years, 3 genera, 27 species, and 13 subspecies of fishes went extinct, mainly from physical habitat alteration and introduced fishes. A 1989 review of the [[Endangered Species Act]] list found 139 new taxa warranting protection, and no species had been removed through successful recovery. Stream fish losses are attributed largely to altered streamflows.

The [[Colorado River Basin]] is in an extremely arid region, and its water has been diverted for agriculture since before Euro-American settlement. Major mainstream dam construction began in the early 1900s, following the [[Colorado River Compact]] and the Boulder Canyon Project Act (1928–1929). By the 1960s much of the mainstream had become a system of dams and diversions, changing flow timing, duration, magnitude, temperature, and channel morphology, and fragmenting habitat.

The fauna is divided into three groups: high- or intermediate-elevation fishes, small-stream endemics, and "big-river" fishes (the Colorado River fishes, mostly cyprinids and catostomids). The four endangered species belong to the big-river group. All four are federally listed as endangered. The razorback sucker was first proposed for threatened listing in 1980 and was later listed as endangered, with only relict, non-recruiting stocks remaining.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Lower basin.** Conversion to dams and diversions replaced native fishes with a new fauna of about 44 forms, 20 of them abundant locally or regionally. Colorado Squawfish has been extirpated from the lower basin. Bonytail and Razorback Sucker persist only in some impoundments and are not presumed self-sustaining. Humpback Chub reproduction is restricted to the [[Little Colorado River]] in the Grand Canyon.

**Upper basin.** Recovery prospects are considered best here, because about 2,000 km of occupied habitat remains. The native fauna includes six endemic large-river species and six headwater forms. Of 42 introduced fishes reported, fewer than 10 are abundant. Colorado Squawfish persists in the [[Yampa River]], the [[Green River]], the upper Colorado mainstream, and the lower San Juan River. Humpback Chub reproduces successfully in the Yampa and upper Colorado rivers. Razorback Sucker persists in several reaches but shows no recent recruitment. Bonytail is extremely rare.

**Flow alteration history.** Early operation of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in the 1960s eliminated most native fishes in 128 km of the upper Green River above [[Dinosaur National Monument]]. Downstream flows and temperatures remained unsuitable for many kilometers. Standing crops in the Green River below the Yampa confluence are maintained by that tributary's more natural regime.

**Biotic interactions.** Habitat use by the endangered fishes is affected by introduced species. [[Northern Pike]] and [[Channel Catfish]] occupy adult Colorado Squawfish habitat. Introduced fishes dominate juvenile squawfish habitat and prey on young fish. Channel Catfish may adversely affect Humpback Chub populations. Predation by [[Common Carp]] and other nonnative fishes is associated with the lack of Razorback Sucker recruitment.

**Floodplain habitat.** Seasonally flooded bottomlands in the upper Green River, lost through reduced spring flows and impoundment, may matter for fish production, as documented in floodplain rivers worldwide. The author says these areas need further study.

## Key Findings

**1. Recovery versus survival.** Most flow determinations for endangered Colorado River fishes have aimed at population survival, based on recent habitat use. The author argues that present habitats are presumably suboptimal, so preserving current conditions may further decline. He calls for a recovery philosophy that seeks increased standing crops, more reproducing populations, and eventual delisting.

**2. Limits of standard flow tools.** The [[Instream Flow Incremental Methodology]] (IFIM) and its [[PHABSIM]] component are widely applied. The author notes that PHABSIM does not establish whether a valid link exists between physical habitat and fish standing crops, and its utility has been widely debated. Its use for endangered Colorado River fishes has been judged inappropriate: some habitats were not easily simulated, microhabitat parameters were not the only factors required, and conflicting flows were obtained for different species and life stages at the same location.

**3. Community complexity.** Physical habitat–abundance relationships are reported for small coldwater streams with 1 to 10 fish species. Little relationship has been found in large warmwater streams with 30 or more species. The relationships among flow, habitat, and fish production are poorly understood.

**4. Minimum flows are inadequate.** Citing Stalnaker (1990), the author argues that "minimum flow is a myth." Once water is appropriated, the minimum becomes the management target and effectively the average flow. Only regimes that provide wet, average, and dry years are likely to meet the life requirements of most fishes. Colorado water law allows protection only of the minimum amount needed, which limits recovery.

**5. Need for integrated management.** Flow alone cannot recover these species. The author calls for evaluating limiting factors, propagation and genetics options, and field testing of alternative management practices, including different flows. Monitoring is needed to confirm that agreed flows are actually delivered and to evaluate their effects.

**6. Proposed approach.** The author recommends combining empirical studies, analytical models, computer simulations, and subjective assessments. He also proposes controlled experiments, such as laboratory studies, reclaimed stream sections, or newly built channels, to test whether flows limit recovery of each life stage and to isolate "nonflow" impacts.

**7. Institutional context.** More than 100 Biological Opinions have been issued under Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act in the upper basin. The [[Recovery Implementation Program]] was set up to recover listed fishes while working within existing state and federal water agreements. Cooperators disagree on how to determine the magnitude, timing, and duration of the annual flow regimes needed.

**8. Broader conservation concern.** The "fundamentally insular" nature of the fauna suggests other species may become endangered. As each species disappears, the author expects recovery of the remaining forms to become harder as native ecosystem perturbations increase.

**9. Conclusion.** The author concludes that instream flow requirements for endangered fishes should be set with respect to recovery, not survival. Survival is too often synonymous with "status quo," which may ultimately lead to extinction in declining populations. Successful recovery will require many stakeholders with differing water-use interests to base decisions on a recovery flow philosophy grounded in sound ecological principles and biological information.
