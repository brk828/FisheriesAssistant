# Buffalo Springs Lake Job Completion Report (1964 Limnological and Game Fish Investigation)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae #ictaluridae #limnology

## Executive Summary

This Texas Parks and Wildlife Department job completion report (Federal Aid Project F-7-R-12, Job D-3, dated August 9, 1965) examines why [[Buffalo Springs Lake]], a freshwater reservoir near Lubbock, supports a poor [[Game Fish]] fishery. The study covers 1964 and combines creel census, gill netting, seining, water chemistry and fish autopsies. The central finding is that a seasonal, spreading [[Toxic Layer]] of [[Hydrogen Sulfide]] and [[Ammonia Nitrogen]], combined with low dissolved oxygen and dense [[Sago Pondweed]] growth, makes the lower water column lethal to fish. Game fish did not reproduce in 1964, and [[Black Bullhead]] and [[Bluegill]] dominate the population. The report recommends watershed cleanup, breaking up [[Thermal Stratification]], controlling sago pondweed earlier, and reducing bullhead and bluegill numbers.

## Background & Description

The study was a [[Job Completion Report]] under the [[Federal Aid in Fisheries Restoration Act]], prepared by George G. Henderson, Jr., and approved by Leo D. Lewis, Regional Supervisor. Stated objectives were to:

- Correlate game fish productivity with physical, chemical and environmental factors.
- Map contours, vegetation and bottom type.
- Estimate benthos, plankton and forage production.
- Measure water quality, dissolved gas, pH and temperature seasonally.
- Chart fish location and density, and count bacteria.

Several objectives were not met. Bottom type was not mapped, benthos and plankton were not sampled, and the bacterial study was abandoned on advice from a [[Texas Technological College]] bacteriologist. Fish traps were tried and abandoned because they caught too few fish.

The lake covers 225 surface acres and is fed by an upstream chain of [[V-8 Ranch Lakes]] and the [[Double Mountain Fork]] of the [[Brazos River]]. Its bottom is covered by a thick layer of black muck.

**Species recorded:** The 1964 checklist includes 20 species. Game species were [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[White Bass]] and [[White Crappie]]. Dominant non-game species were [[Black Bullhead]], [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Orangespotted Sunfish]] and [[Longear Sunfish]], plus carp and minnows. Key plants were sago pondweed (*Potamogeton pectinatus*), bulrush, cattails, and sedges.

Inflow averaged 7.52 cubic feet per second from August 9 to November 25, 1964, though the authors considered this estimate high.

**Thermal and chemical stratification:** The lake stratified from mid-April to September. The toxic layer typically sat near 18 feet on average, shifted rapidly, and often followed a temperature break of 3°F or more. It occurred at pH 7.8 to 9.0 and temperatures of 62°F to 80°F. Fall overturn ran from October 15 to October 28, 1964.

**Sago pondweed:** This plant was the major vegetation affecting fish. It grew where water was 5 feet deep or less, choked the upper lake by August, and regressed from mid-November, leaving the lake without submerged plants by mid-December. Decay consumed oxygen, released organic wastes, and pushed fall pH to 8.8 to 9.0. City mowing from mid-July by the Lubbock County Water Control and Improvement District No. 1 gave limited control.

**Contaminant inputs:** Effluent from Lubbock storm drains, a sewage disposal plant, a dump and industrial plants entered the lake. Runoff from [[Leuter's Feed Lots]] on the V-8 Ranch lakes was also reported.

## Key Findings

**Fish mortality and toxic conditions**

- The toxic layer was the main cause of mortality. Dissolved gases, ammonia nitrogen and hydrogen sulfide were always present in the layer, and ammonia reached 6.5 ppm and hydrogen sulfide exceeded 5 ppm in summer.
- In July, 114 of 126 fish (90.48%) caught in net No. 8 were dead. Dissolved oxygen at 8 feet was 1.8 ppm, and the water there was 80°F.
- On April 9, a shoreline circuit counted 107 dead or dying largemouth bass, 22 black bullheads and 322 sunfish.
- Caged bio-assays confirmed toxicity. Bullheads exposed for 15 minutes at 22 feet on August 6 died within 24 hours, and bullheads in the 15-foot test on July 8 died within 40 minutes to an hour. In a July 17 test at 17 feet, where no hydrogen sulfide was present, no effects were seen.
- State Chemist bio-assays showed 100% mortality within 12 to 24 hours at ammonia concentrations of 4, 6 and 10 ppm.
- The report concluded that effects beyond low oxygen, including synergism among ammonia, hydrogen sulfide and oxygen deficit, were killing the fish.

**Pathology and contaminants**

- Autopsied bullheads showed hemorrhage, seeping blood vessels, reddened fins, anchor worms (*Lernaea* sp.) and *Saprolegnia* on dead fish. The authors attributed the deaths to stress and reduced disease resistance.
- Dead fish contained DDT and metabolites, with one largemouth bass at 6.09 ppm and 24 sunfish averaging 1.49 ppm. The report judged pesticides were not the major cause of death.

**Population and fishery**

- Game fish did not reproduce in 1964. No young-of-year largemouth bass, channel catfish, white bass or crappie were found, and bullheads and sunfish had large numbers of young.
- Creel census (1,001 of 39,085 paid fishermen interviewed; 714 season passes at $20 each) estimated 176,642 fish weighing 52,357 pounds removed, about 232.74 pounds per acre. Black bullhead made up 67.95% of the catch by weight.
- Netting produced 9,096 fish, of which bluegill was 63.89% and black bullhead 32.85%. Game fish were 1.84% of netted fish. Netting and creel data closely matched.
- Seining found forage species at 31.88% of the seinable population. Sunfish and bullheads were overabundant and reduced insect and plankton food for game fish.
- A 1964 tagging program marked 7,320 fish, of which 345 were tagged. Recapture data were too sparse for a population estimate.

**Management recommendations**

1. General cleanup of the watershed to improve water quality.
2. Break up thermal stratification, for example by draining the bottom outflow, piercing the old dam, pumping or bubbling water, or feeding inflow to the lake bottom through canals.
3. Apply accelerated control to sago pondweed, starting earlier and removing decaying weeds faster.
4. Reduce bullhead and bluegill numbers to lessen competition with game fish, which the authors expected to greatly improve the game fishery.
