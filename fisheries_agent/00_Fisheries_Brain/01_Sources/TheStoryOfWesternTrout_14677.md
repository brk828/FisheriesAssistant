# The Story of Western Trout

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1955 magazine reprint, from the April 1955 issue of *Sunset* (Lane Publishing Co.), was issued for the California Department of Fish and Game. It profiles the trout of the American West, covering five native trout and three introduced species, and gives a state-by-state report on trout fishing. It combines popular natural history, a behavioral account of [[Steelhead]] migration, and a geological history of the region's trout, with a closing note offering a framed reproduction of the trout painting.

## Background & Description

- **Native Western trout (five):** [[Dolly Varden]] (a char), [[Golden Trout]], [[Cutthroat Trout]], [[Rainbow Trout]], and [[Steelhead]] (sea-run rainbow).
- **Imported trout (three):** [[Eastern Brook Trout]] (a char related to Dolly Varden, from the Atlantic coast), [[Brown Trout]] (from Europe), and [[Lake Trout]] (from the Middle West).
- **Taxonomy:** The text places the trout in the order Salmonidae, which also includes salmon, ciscoes and Rocky Mountain whitefish. Chars (*Salvelinus* and *Cristivomer*) and "true" trouts (*Salmo*) are not separable by external structure, so species recognition is the only reliable identification.
- **Evolutionary age:** The author states that salmonoids were contemporary with the dinosaurs in Cretaceous time. They lack many specialized organs, appendages and senses compared with more advanced fishes.
- **Two principal races:** Western *Salmo* split into the rainbow series and the cutthroat series. Before ichthyologists grouped native Western trout (except Dolly Varden) under these two headings, more than 20 "distinct species" had been named.
  - *Rainbow series:* no teeth on the hyoid; usually a lateral red or pink stripe; often heavier and stockier.
  - *Cutthroat series:* teeth on the hyoid bone at the base of the tongue; red throat markings, from a bright slash to a faint discoloration.
- **Steelhead transformation:** A rainbow that migrates to sea loses its rainbow traits. Its back turns steely blue, its spots and carmine stripe fade, and its body lengthens. After about two years at sea it returns to spawn, regaining rainbow colors.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat preferences:** Rainbows tend toward seaward and southward habitats and need clean, cold, aerated shallow water over sand-gravel beds to reproduce. Cutthroats are inland and northern, cold-water fish that tolerate slower water.
- **Range overlap:** The Columbia River serves as a seaway for inland cutthroats and as an entry to the Rockies for sea-run rainbows. Cutthroats range from Cape Mendocino to Alaska; south of Cape Mendocino they are practically absent as a native. The cutthroat series is not represented in Central Valley tributaries.
- **Dispersal mechanisms:** Transfers between watersheds are best explained by "stream capture," in which glaciers, alluvial fans, crustal movement or aggressive headward erosion redirect drainage.
- **Geological history:**
  - The Sierra Nevada fault block rose, and its rivers once flowed directly into a gulf of the ocean where the Sacramento and San Joaquin valleys now lie.
  - [[Pleistocene Glaciation]] forced trout out of high habitats; isolated fish persisted in ice-free "islands," producing the golden trout (west Sierra) and Piute trout (east Sierra).
  - [[Lake Bonneville]] covered 19,000 square miles and, once isolated from the Pacific, concentrated salts and forced trout up streams.
  - [[Lake Lahontan]] survives as Pyramid, Winnemucca and Walker lakes.
  - In the [[Kern River]], isolation produced the Gilbert trout.
- **Behavior and environment:** Trout feed heavily at dusk, rely on current and cover (undercut banks, logs, eddies), and have limited visual detail. Color varies with pigment spots and surroundings, and dark coloring offers protection from predators.
- **Human pressure:** In California, the main threats are dams, water diversion, new mountain roads, grazing, logging and fires, alongside increasing fishing pressure. Many fish are stocked under [[Put-and-Take Stocking]] programs.

## Key Findings

**Natural history and identification**
- Rainbow and cutthroat series are distinguished by hyoid teeth, throat markings and the lateral stripe. Borderline or hybrid types are assigned to one group "in the absence of evidence to the contrary."
- The rainbow is the most popular trout among anglers and hatcherymen: the easiest to propagate, rear, transport and transplant, but hardest to bring to net, ounce for ounce.
- Rainbows will spawn only in clean, cold, aerated, swiftly moving shallow water over sand-gravel beds.
- The female digs the nest and drops eggs; the male releases milt and guards the nest. The female then refills the nest with gravel.
- In a [[Mount Shasta Hatchery]] observation, rainbow trout with red dorsal tags were attacked and killed by their fellows, and the attacks targeted the tag itself. Trout with other tag colors were not attacked. The author concludes this undermines the belief that trout lack color perception.
- Brown trout are described as hard to catch and not especially antagonistic toward other trout, although they are sometimes blamed for other species' declines.

**State-by-state fishing data (as reported in 1955)**
- **California:** Most fish and most fishermen of any Western state. The state leads in planting "catchable" fish. Creel checks of marked fish showed more than 50 per cent caught in the same season; the text also says not more than 2 per cent of planted fry and fingerlings are taken. Season April 30 (May 28 in some areas) to October 31; resident license $3. Major steelhead rivers include the [[Klamath River]], [[Trinity River]] and [[Eel River]].
- **Oregon:** Season April 30 to October 9; residents $4, nonresidents $15 (or 7 days, $5). About 2½ million "catchable" trout planted, mostly rainbow. Golden trout are being experimented with in the Wallowa Mountains.
- **Nevada:** About 6 million fingerlings and reared trout planted annually. Season May 15 to October 31. [[Pyramid Lake]] rainbows are very large and nearly indistinguishable from ocean steelhead.
- **Idaho:** 600,000 pounds of trout, mostly rainbow, planted in 1953–54. Kamloops (rainbow) record 87 pounds (Lake Pend Oreille); mackinaw record 51 pounds (Priest Lake). Undesirable species were killed with [[Rotenone]]. Summer season June 4 to October 31; nonresident license $19.
- **Washington:** Winter steelhead catch rose from 23,000 (1947–48) to more than 100,000 (1951–52) after a postwar stocking program. Fish raised to seaward-migrant size, rather than released as fry, are credited with the improvement. About 40 million game fish planted annually, about half "silver trout" (freshwater sockeye salmon). Season April 17 to October 31.
- **Utah:** Record fish include brown trout 37 pounds 4 ounces (Logan River), lake trout 42 pounds (Fish Lake) and cutthroat 28 pounds (Strawberry Reservoir). Of about 2,000 fishing lakes, roughly half are in the Uinta Range, which supplies 42 per cent of the state's water. General season May 28 to October 9; nonresident license $10.
- **Arizona:** Cutthroats up to 13 pounds in White Mountain lakes. About 3¼ million trout planted yearly, nearly a million of them catchables. Open all year with local exceptions; resident $2.50, nonresident $10.

**Conclusion of the report:** The author concludes that on the right day, stream and hole, "the fishing's fine," while noting increasing fishing pressure on shrinking trout habitat across the West.
