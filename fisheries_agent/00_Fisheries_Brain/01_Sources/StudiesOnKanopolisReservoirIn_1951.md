# Studies on Kanopolis Reservoir in 1950

## Executive Summary
This paper by Otto W. Tiemeier (Kansas State College, 1951) documents an early-succession ecological survey of the newly impounded [[Kanopolis Reservoir]] (Ellsworth County, Kansas), constructed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers primarily for flood control on the Smoky Hill and Kansas Rivers. The study, conducted over a single field season (June 15–August 31, 1950), addressed four inter-related phases: (I) factors causing turbidity, (II) mapping of vegetational types, (III) aquatic vegetation dynamics, and (IV) plankton and related limnological studies (pH, temperature, transparency). A major biological driver identified was the feeding/rooting activity of [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*), which destroys aquatic vegetation and increases turbidity, compounding the effects of extreme water-level fluctuation (up to 32 feet in nine weeks) typical of flood-control impoundments. The authors recommend commercial carp removal, targeted revegetation with water-tolerant plant species, and land-use changes on agricultural margins to reduce siltation and improve the reservoir as a fish and waterfowl habitat.

## Background & Description
Fish-specific morphological data are limited to *Cyprinus carpio* (carp):
- Sample of 11 carp examined after a 24-foot reservoir rise.
- Observed schools ranged from 12–50 individuals.
- Most individuals measured **13–14 inches in length**, averaging approximately **1.5 pounds**.
- Growth rate was noted as rapid, with high palatability of flesh at time of capture, suggesting a young, fast-growing population in a newly formed reservoir.
- No morphological data were reported for other fish species; the study is otherwise ecosystem- and plankton-focused rather than fish-morphology-focused.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
**Habitat context:** [[Kanopolis Reservoir]] is a flood-control impoundment with an extremely fluctuating water level (conservation pool 1459 ft; observed rise of 32 ft above pool within the 1950 study period), creating a highly unstable littoral and mud-flat environment unsuitable for long-term establishment of either fully aquatic or fully terrestrial vegetation.

**Carp niche and impact:**
- *Cyprinus carpio* fed extensively on vegetation and seeds (95% of gut contents were plant material, primarily *Polygonum* and *Setaria* seeds/sprouts), with the remainder composed of coleopterans and crustacean plankters.
- Rooting behavior suspends bottom sediments, elevating turbidity and preventing reestablishment of submergent and emergent [[Aquatic Macrophyte Succession]], directly linking fish behavior to reduced light penetration and photosynthetic rates (per Low & Bellrose '44; McAtee '39).
- Comparative case study: Middle Harbor Lake (Lake Erie), where carp removal via rotenone (Anderson 1950; Weier & Starr 1950) led to dramatic transparency improvement (Secchi depth rising from 10 in. to 50 in.) and recovery of *Chara*, *Potamogeton*, and *Vallisneria* beds, supporting the paper's causal argument regarding [[Carp-Induced Turbidity]].

**Plankton community structure (Table 4 summary):**
- Ten stations sampled across the reservoir for pH, temperature, Secchi transparency, phytoplankton, and zooplankton counts.
- pH range: 7.4–7.7 (surface and bottom), indicating mild alkalinity tolerable for most regional flora/fauna.
- No thermocline detected; wind-driven mixing in the shallow reservoir prevented stratification. Water temperatures ranged 22–29.5°C, informing recommendations against introducing cold-water-intolerant [[Fish Transplantation]] candidates.
- Phytoplankton dominated by blue-green algae (*Anabaena*, *Spirulina*, *Aphanizomenon*, *Phormidium*) and green algae (*Microspora*, *Spirogyra*, *Ophiocytium*); counts peaked at 2,080,254/L (Station 5, Red Rock Cove) coincident with rising water levels in mid-August.
- Zooplankton composition: ~56% micro-crustaceans (*Daphnia*, *Bosmina*, *Diaptomus*, *Canthocamptus*), ~42% protozoans (*Ceratium* most common), ~2% rotifers (*Anuraea* most common). Zooplankton peaks lagged behind phytoplankton peaks, a classic predator-prey/grazing succession pattern in [[Freshwater Plankton Dynamics]].

**Vegetation and turbidity feedback loop:** Mud flats (up to 1,000 acres) lacking vegetative cover were identified as the primary turbidity source, compounding wave action and carp rooting. Dominant colonizing genera included *Polygonum*, *Helianthus*, *Erigeron*, and *Chenopodium*, consistent with prior Kansas lakebed succession studies (McGregor '48; McGregor & Volle '50).

## Key Findings
- **Turbidity drivers**: Primary causes identified were (1) absence of stabilizing vegetation on mud flats, (2) carp rooting/feeding activity, (3) extreme water-level fluctuation (up to 32 ft in 9 weeks) destroying established vegetation, and (4) upland feeder-stream siltation (not directly studied).
- **Transparency data**: Secchi disc readings ranged from 5 inches (near plowed agricultural land at Westerman Hill) to 33 inches (open lake near dam); average transparency improved from 21.3 in. to 25.2 in. as water levels fell between August 11–28.
- **Carp gut analysis**: 95% plant material by volume, implicating carp as active disruptors of shoreline revegetation and contributors to sediment resuspension.
- **Population management recommendation**: Commercial carp removal recommended, citing Grand Lake, Oklahoma precedent (~50 lbs carp/acre) and Kanopolis's estimated 700+ productive acres.
- **Revegetation recommendations**: Prioritize *Echinochloa crusgalli* (wild millet, self-reseeding, strong root system), *Polygonum lapathifolium* and *P. pennsylvanicum* (smartweeds, high waterfowl food value), and *Leersia oryzoides* (rice cutgrass, rhizomatous soil binder) — all native/regionally adapted species with demonstrated duck-food value (per Bellrose '41; Low & Bellrose '44).
- **Land management recommendation**: Convert agricultural land between contour 1470 and the purchase line to permanent grass cover to reduce plowed-soil turbidity contributions; restrict cattle access to shoreline vegetation.
- **No thermocline** observed in any sampling period, attributed to shallow depth and consistent wind mixing — relevant for future [[Fish Stocking Suitability]] assessments (species intolerant of 24–28°C should not be introduced).
- **Absence of submergent macrophytes**: Virtually no submergent vegetation (*Potamogeton*, *Najas*, *Ceratophyllum*) was established in the main reservoir body, with trial plantings of *Potamogeton* unsuccessful, likely due to fluctuating water levels and turbidity — a key unresolved question for long-term [[Reservoir Plant Succession]].
- Study explicitly framed as a **baseline/first-year dataset**, with authors cautioning that ecological succession trends in large, fluctuating reservoirs require multi-year monitoring beyond the scope of this single season.
