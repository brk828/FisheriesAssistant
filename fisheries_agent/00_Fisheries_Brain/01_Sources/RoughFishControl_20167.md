# Rough Fish Control Methods and Rotenone Application in California Fisheries Management

#fisheries-management #aquatic-ecology #chemical-treatment #rough-fish-control

## Executive Summary

This 1963 administrative report from the California Department of Fish and Game provides comprehensive guidance on rough fish control as a fisheries management tool. The document reviews theory, methods, and practical considerations for chemical treatment of waters to eliminate undesirable fish species and improve game fish populations. [[Rotenone]] emerges as the primary piscicide, with detailed coverage of its toxicity, application methods, costs, and safety considerations. The report addresses treatment planning, species-specific susceptibility, selective control techniques, and post-treatment evaluation protocols. Alternative chemicals including [[Toxaphene]], [[Endrin]], and [[Acrolein]] are discussed but generally rejected for California use due to persistence or safety concerns.

## Background & Description

Rough fish control represents an important fisheries management tool designed to improve sport fishing by eliminating competition, predation, disease, and water turbidity caused by non-game species. Complete fish kills followed by restocking can temporarily restore fishing quality to levels found in new water bodies. The report emphasizes that treatment decisions must be carefully evaluated, as some "rough fish" provide valuable forage (e.g., [[Tui Chub]] in [[Eagle Lake]]) and native species with restricted distributions require protection from extermination.

[[Rotenone]], derived from roots of [[Derris]] and [[Lonchocarpus]] plants, acts by blocking chemical reactions in gill filaments, causing suffocation. Available as 5-20% powder or ~5% emulsion, rotenone shows variable toxicity depending on species, fish size, and water temperature (greatest effectiveness between 50-75°F). Species susceptibility ranges from highly sensitive trout and native cyprinids (0.025-0.050 ppm) to resistant [[Carp]], [[Goldfish]], and [[Bullhead]] (0.1-0.2 ppm or higher).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Chemical treatments dramatically affect aquatic ecosystems beyond target fish species. Recent research demonstrates that rotenone drastically reduces open-water zooplankton populations (primarily [[Cladocera]] and [[Copepoda]]), with recovery requiring several months after water becomes non-toxic to fish. This finding suggests delayed restocking may enhance survival and growth of planted fish by allowing zooplankton recovery. Zooplankton in weedy areas show better survival, raising concerns that fish larvae might similarly survive in vegetation, potentially reestablishing unwanted populations.

Natural detoxification depends on initial concentration, water temperature, sunlight, and time. Post (1958) provided formulas for estimating toxicity duration: d = 93 - 3/4 T (for 45-60°F) or d = 38 - 3/4 T (for 60-80°F), where d equals days of toxicity and T equals average daily temperature. Waters under ice and snow may remain toxic for five months or more. The hypolimnion can retain toxicity after surface layers detoxify, requiring careful testing before fall or spring overturn to avoid planted fish mortality.

Stream treatments in California (17 streams treated) typically show rapid rough fish population recovery with questionable and temporary benefits, highlighting the need for rigorous evaluation of such projects. Pre-impoundment treatments of drainages above new reservoirs have become common practice, though their actual benefits remain inadequately documented. Untreated waters like [[Folsom Reservoir]] sometimes produce outstanding [[Largemouth Bass]] fishing in early years, questioning the necessity of pre-treatment.

## Key Findings

**Species-Specific Treatment Requirements:**
- [[Trout]], native cyprinids, [[Suckers]]: 0.025-0.050 ppm
- [[Sunfish]]: 0.050-0.075 ppm
- [[Bullhead]] (Ictaluridae): 0.1-0.2 ppm
- [[Carp]]: 0.113-0.2 ppm
- [[Goldfish]]: 0.2 ppm (some individuals extremely resistant)

**Cost Comparison (1963, per acre-foot at 0.5 ppm):**
- 5% powder in burlap bags: $0.60
- 20% powder in burlap bags: $1.40
- 5% emulsion: $1.33
- 2.5% rotenone + 2.5% synergist emulsion: $4.07

**Safety and Toxicity:**
Rotenone shows low mammalian toxicity (lethal oral dose ~0.2 grams pure rotenone for humans) but causes serious eye inflammation and skin irritation with continuous handling. Adverse effects on swine documented. Continuing sublethal dietary doses may cause fatty liver and kidney changes, allergies, and pterygiums. Workers require protective equipment when handling powder formulations.

**Alternative Chemicals:**
- [[Toxaphene]]: Used at [[Grassy Lake]] (0.1 ppm) and [[Big Bear Lake]] (0.2 ppm) but rejected for future California use due to long-lasting residual effects and bioaccumulation through food chains
- [[Acrolein]]: Requires 3.0 ppm for complete carp/goldfish/bullhead eradication; more expensive than rotenone; water toxic only a few days; concentrated form highly toxic to humans
- [[Copper Sulfate]]: Experimental selective control at 2-3 ppm ($1.44 per acre-foot) for carp, [[Golden Shiner]], [[Hitch]], and bullhead while sparing [[Green Sunfish]] and largemouth bass

**Selective Control Methods:**
- Low-dose rotenone (0.0065-0.015 ppm): Selectively kills [[Gizzard Shad]] and [[Drum]] in southeastern states
- Malathion-dibrom combination (2:3 ratio at 0.1 ppm): Selectively kills sunfish with minimal largemouth bass loss; cost $1.12 per acre-foot
- Epilimnion treatment: Special rotenone formulations treat warm surface waters (0.025-0.01 ppm) without harming trout in cold hypolimnion

**Partial Control Limitations:**
Partial removal programs generally show questionable results. Stream treatments produce rapid rough fish recovery. Partial carp and bluegill removal in Oklahoma lakes gave variable results, with one lake experiencing subsequent carp explosion. Partial black bullhead and bluegill removal showed negligible benefits. The report emphasizes that few rough fish can rapidly repopulate waters, making partial control economically unjustifiable except in unusual circumstances.

**Carp-Specific Control:**
- Water level drawdown: [[Fort Randall Reservoir]] (South Dakota) controlled carp by lowering water 2+ feet within one week of egg deposition to expose spawning shallows
- Poisoned baits: Limited success with calcium carbide pellets coated in beef tallow; carp detect poison
- Aerial rotenone application: 0.1 ppm successfully killed spawning adults and eyed eggs in Idaho shallows; 0.05 ppm failed due to avoidance behavior
- Baited concentration: Sour corn at inlet currents concentrates carp and catfish for targeted treatment with weighted tarpaulin enclosure and 0.05 ppm rotenone

**Application Methods:**
Powdered products create irritating dust requiring masks; packaged in burlap bags for boat-towing application. Emulsive products applied by: (1) premixing and pouring, (2) spraying or pumping into deep water using centrifugal pump with venturi system, (3) Venturi pump (Amundson boat bailer) on outboard motor for metered flow from drum. Aircraft application used for isolated areas (helicopter costs: $130/hour, $300/day minimum in 1963).

**Detoxification:**
Chemical detoxification generally disappointing in California; attempted only under unusual circumstances. Methods include chlorine compounds (difficult, expensive, requires continuous application) or potassium permanganate (1 ppm per 0.025 ppm rotenone; caution: 3 ppm toxic to bluegill at 68°F, 4 ppm toxic to trout). Water supply reservoirs successfully treated using activated carbon (30 mg/L per 0.05 ppm active ingredient) to remove taste and odor.

**Storage and Potency:**
Rotenone powder deteriorates with time; heat accelerates detoxification. Storage in cool locations recommended. Use within one year if possible; assay for rotenone content if older than one year. Large lots should undergo routine analysis as manufacturer concentrations may be inaccurate.

**Mandatory Requirements:**
Post-treatment evaluation mandatory for all projects using Form FG 741. Documentation must include costs, effort, water conditions, fish kill estimates, restocking data, and treatment details. Pre-treatment planning requires Form FG 740 with accurate water volume measurements, inlet/outlet locations, species present, shallow weedy areas, access problems, water uses, and operational plans for major treatments.

**Native Species Protection:**
The report emphasizes absolute obligation to protect native species with restricted distributions as unique natural resources. No excuse exists for risking extermination of any native species through chemical treatment programs.
