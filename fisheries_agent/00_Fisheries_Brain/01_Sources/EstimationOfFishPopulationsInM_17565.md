# Estimation of Fish Populations in Michigan Lakes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This 1951 study by Gerald P. Cooper of the Michigan Department of Conservation estimates fish populations in two inland Michigan lakes, [[Sugarloaf Lake]] and [[Fife Lake]], using trap-net [[Petersen Mark-Recapture Method]] sampling. Fish were fin-clipped, released, and recaptured during 1948 to 1950, and population totals were computed with the [[Schumacher and Eschmeyer Formula]], with [[Schnabel Formula]] estimates used as a comparison. The study tests the critical assumptions of mark-recapture, especially whether marked fish show [[Homing Behavior]], and compares trap-net totals with [[Creel Census]] estimates. Bluegill dominated both populations.

## Background & Description

The paper argues that inland sport-fish management is shifting from descriptive to quantitative approaches. Evaluating [[Fishing Regulations]], [[Hatchery Stocking]], and habitat improvement requires either a direct count of fish in a water body or a quantitative creel census. Electric shocking works for streams, but no equivalent tool existed for lakes, so lake studies rely on sampling plus an assessment of bias. Only small ponds that could be drained or poisoned allowed direct checks of accuracy.

The study addressed three behavioural questions: whether fish should be released at the capture site or elsewhere, whether individual fish keep a "home niche," and whether displaced fish become more vulnerable to recapture. It also aimed to measure survival of hatchery [[Smallmouth Bass]] fingerlings marked by fin clipping.

Netting used two trap-net sizes described by [[Crowe Trap Net Design]]:
- **3-foot net:** single pot, 3 x 3 x 6 ft, 12-ft wings, 100-ft lead, 2.5-in and 3-in stretched mesh.
- **6-foot net:** double pot, 6 x 4 x 16 ft, 20-ft wings, 150-ft lead, mesh of 2¼, 3, and 4 in.

Fish under 6 inches were excluded, and pike under 14 inches were excluded from the census.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sugarloaf Lake** (Washtenaw County, in glacial moraines) covers 180 acres. Most of the lake lies between 2 and 5 feet deep, with a maximum depth of 20 feet. Aquatic vegetation is abundant except inside the 10-foot contour. The fish community is typical of southern Michigan lakes. The lake has about 80 cottages, public access, and heavy fishing pressure.

**Fife Lake** (Grand Traverse and Kalkaska counties) covers 575 acres and reaches a maximum depth of 55 feet. About 67 percent of its area is less than 20 feet deep, and submerged plants are generally abundant to 15 feet. It has about 80 cottages and moderate fishing intensity. Its fish community, ranked by angler importance, includes yellow perch, bluegill, pumpkinseed, northern pike, largemouth and smallmouth bass, rock bass, black crappie, walleye, and bullheads.

The study found that the lakes lack large non-predaceous fish, and that yellow perch are likely undercounted by trap nets.

## Key Findings

**Sampling effort and marking**
- Netting ran at 30 stations in fall 1948, 20 in spring 1949, and 40 in spring 1950 on Sugarloaf Lake. Fife Lake was netted at 60 stations from June 16 to July 19, 1950.
- In four netting periods, 17,887 fish were marked and released, and 2,991 were recaptured (17 percent). Bluegill made up about half of the marked fish.
- Fish were marked with a different fin clip for each lake half and each year.

**Population estimates (Schumacher and Eschmeyer formula)**
- Sugarloaf Lake legal-size totals were 17,648 (fall 1948), 15,531 (spring 1949), and 22,178 (spring 1950). The three-year average was 18,452 fish, or 102 fish and 43.1 lb per acre.
- Fife Lake's estimated total was 99,056 fish, or 172 fish and 62.3 lb per acre.
- Bluegill exceeded half of the total in both lakes. On Sugarloaf the three-year average was about 12,715 bluegill (18.6 lb/acre). On Fife the 1950 estimate was 56,511 bluegill (23.6 lb/acre).
- Standard errors on Sugarloaf were about 5 to 8 percent for bluegill (12,000 to 14,000 fish) and 10 to over 50 percent for species under 1,000 fish.
- The Schnabel formula gave somewhat higher totals than the Schumacher and Eschmeyer formula.

**Homing and redistribution**
- Marked fish released at a central station tended to be recaptured in the half of the lake where they were marked. Of 2,682 Sugarloaf recoveries, 1,590 were in the home half and 1,092 in the opposite half. Chi-square values were 72.1 (west) and 25.4 (east), with P < 0.001.
- The homing effect was at most about 18 percent of recaptures. Analysis of distance-weighted "migration indexes" indicated that the home-half bias was probably due largely to distance from the nets rather than to homing instinct (t = 1.08, probability of difference about 70 percent).
- Recaptures at odd- versus even-numbered stations showed no homing to individual net sites.

**Sugarloaf population trend**
- Estimates rose with the number of trap-net stations (20, 30, and 40). Extrapolation suggested the true population may be closer to 30,000 fish. The authors considered this conjectural and did not resolve the variation.

**Creel census comparison**
- The creel census covered about 14 percent of angling effort on Sugarloaf Lake.
- Creel-based estimates were about double the trap-net estimates. Totals were roughly 35,000 to 45,000 (creel) versus 15,000 to 20,000 (trap net) for 1949 and 1950. The cause was not determined.

**Conclusions**
- Trap-net mark-recapture gave usable population estimates for these lakes, with errors judged inconsequential for general management purposes.
- The authors suggested that combined netting and angling is theoretically the better approach, since it avoids some sources of bias in each method alone.
