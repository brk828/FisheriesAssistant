# White Sucker Population of South Bay, Lake Huron, and Effects of the Sea Lamprey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study analyzes the ecology and population dynamics of the [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*) in South Bay, a 22.5 km arm of [[Lake Huron]] on the southeast corner of [[Manitoulin Island]]. Data came from an experimental fishery (1947–1964) and a mark-recapture program in 1955 and 1956, in which 1,667 suckers were tagged and 8% were recovered. Suckers moved extensively but were treated as separate fishing areas for population estimates. The 1956 adult population was estimated at about 160,000 fish (range 100,000–380,000), with exploitation below 2% and annual survival of roughly 70–75%. Although population size may not have changed, the size structure shifted after the early 1950s through loss of large fish, which the author attributes to [[Sea Lamprey]] predation following the collapse of [[Lake Trout]] in South Bay.

## Background & Description

The [[White Sucker]] is a benthic catostomid that inhabits both basins of South Bay and is common in all gear types. Adults are slow-growing: the length–weight relationship in June was W = 5.883 × 10⁻⁴ L^2.92262, with W in pounds and L as fork length in inches, based on 811 fish of 9–18 inches. Mean annual length increment was about 0.3 inches (7.6 mm). Scales did not form an annulus every year, so they were unreliable for ageing, consistent with earlier work on [[Dwarf White Sucker]].

The study used [[Pound Net]] catches as the main data source, supplemented by hoop, trap, and gill nets. Fish were tagged with oval yellow celluloid tags attached at the base of the dorsal fin. Tagging studies were checked for [[Tag Loss]], handling mortality, and emigration, with little evidence that these biases were important.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

South Bay (about 45°35′N, 82°W) has an area of 85.5 km² and is split by a 1.2 km constriction into an outer basin (maximum depth about 12 m, 16% of area) and an inner basin (maximum depth about 55 m, 84% of area). In the ice-free season, suckers occupy water less than about 36 m deep, about 70 km² of the bay. They ascend tributaries to spawn in spring.

Marked changes in the fauna followed the late 1940s:
- The [[Sea Lamprey]] invaded and attacked large fish by rasping holes in their bodies.
- The [[Lake Trout]] population, estimated at more than 20,000 adults in 1948, was virtually extinct by 1952 and probably gone completely by 1953.
- The [[Cisco]] population declined drastically, reaching a low in 1960.
- The [[Alewife]] first appeared in 1951 and became extremely abundant from 1954.

The author does not speculate on how these changes affected suckers, but notes they were large enough to have influenced the ecosystem.

## Key Findings

- **Tagging and movement:** Of 1,667 tagged suckers (76 in 1955, 1,591 in 1956), 8% were recovered. Mean minimal distance travelled was 3.8 km during a summer (range 0.6–12.9 km for group means). Distance showed no relationship to time at large and did not differ between June and July. One sucker was recaptured about 56 km away after 5 years.
- **Restricted movement:** Recapture ratios showed the population was not homogeneous. The bay was divided into area 1 (outer basin), area 2 (middle inner basin), and area 3 (south-east inner basin), each treated separately.
- **Population size, 1956:** Estimates for the three areas were combined to about 160,000 adults (100,000–380,000 by confidence intervals), or about 23 fish per hectare and 17.5 kg/hectare for the area shallower than 36 m. The author advises viewing these estimates with reservation.
- **Exploitation:** Rates were 0.7% (area 1), 1.4% (area 2), and 1.8% (area 3). Annual exploitation was below 2%.
- **Survival:** Tagged-fish and catch-curve estimates gave a mean annual survival of 74.3%. Survival decreased with size, from about 88.5% at 15.5–16.5 inches to 51.7% at 19.5–20.5 inches. Olson (1963) reported 86.9% for a Minnesota lake.
- **Growth:** Adult growth averaged 7.6 mm per year, with no difference between large and small tagged fish.
- **Size structure:** The proportion of suckers 15 inches or longer dropped from about 60% in the early 1950s to about 10% in 1964.
- **Lamprey effect:** About 10% of suckers 15 inches or longer bore lamprey scars during 1947–1953, rising afterward. The author concludes that the sea lamprey removed large suckers, coinciding with the lake trout collapse. Natural mortality, not fishing, was the main loss source.
- **Population trend:** Pound net catches recovered by 1958 to levels similar to before 1952, suggesting the total population may not have declined, though the pound net catch is not a validated index.
- **Conclusion:** The sea lamprey changed the size composition of the sucker population, but whether it reduced the population's size remains uncertain.
