# Effects of Water Quality and Habitat on Composition of Fish Communities in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fish-communities

## Executive Summary

This USGS fact sheet reports on an August 1996 fish community survey at 15 stream sites in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] (UCOL) study unit, conducted under the [[National Water-Quality Assessment Program]] (NAWQA). It examines how natural and human factors, including elevation, water temperature, dissolved solids, suspended sediment, substrate, and land use, relate to the composition of fish communities. The authors conclude that fish community differences track the two major physiographic provinces, with cold-water, invertivore-dominated communities in the [[Southern Rocky Mountains Province]] and warm-water, omnivore-dominated communities in the [[Colorado Plateau Province]].

## Background & Description

**Purpose and rationale.** NAWQA emphasizes multidisciplinary assessment, combining physical, chemical, and biological information to describe water quality. Fish communities are one assessed component. The authors note that fish communities show high natural variability but are useful indicators of ecosystem health. They are also valued by the public as ecologically relevant and are directly tied to legislative mandates because of human-use and endangered-species concerns (Meador et al., 1993; Bertrand et al., 1986).

**Study area.** The UCOL study unit covers about 17,800 square miles, varying greatly in climate, geology, topography, and hydrology. The primary river, the [[Colorado River]], originates in the mountains of central Colorado and flows about 230 miles southwest into Utah, dropping about 10,000 feet in elevation. Major tributaries include the Blue, Eagle, Roaring Fork, and [[Gunnison River]]s. The study unit is divided roughly equally between:

- The **Southern Rocky Mountains** province: high-elevation, forested headwaters with crystalline bedrock, high gradients, and coarse substrates.
- The **Colorado Plateau** province: low-elevation, sparsely vegetated, low-gradient streams with fine substrates and high salinity.

**Field methods.** Fish were collected at 15 sites by electroshocking. Wadeable sites were sampled over reaches 450 to 650 feet long, and nonwadeable sites over 1,000 to 3,000 feet, using boat-based electrofishing equipment. Fish were identified, measured, weighed, and released. Most sites were sampled at a single reach; three sites (Baker, site 1; Gore, site 4; Dry Creek, site 12) used multiple reaches to assess variability within a stream segment of about 1 to 2 miles. U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service personnel assisted with collection. Site types were grouped as:

- **Seven** sites draining specific land uses (agriculture, mining, urban/recreation).
- **Six** sites with multiple land uses.
- **Two** background sites, generally unaffected by human activity.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fish community structure.** A fish community is a group of fish inhabiting a stream reach that interact with each other. Structure is determined by species present, relative abundances, life stages, size distributions, and spatial and temporal distribution (Meador et al., 1993). Natural variability is linked to elevation, stream temperature, physical habitat, and water quality.

**Trophic classification.** Fish were grouped by feeding guild using a generalized classification (Schrader, 1989):

- **Invertivores** consume mostly invertebrates.
- **Omnivores** consume invertebrates, fish, and plants.
- **Herbivores** consume mostly plants.
- **Piscivores** consume mostly fish.

**Water temperature and elevation.** Water temperature is inversely related to elevation: as elevation decreases, stream temperature increases. Temperature is a major controlling factor in fish community composition. Cold water in the Southern Rocky Mountains favors [[Cutthroat Trout]] and sculpins, while warm water on the Colorado Plateau favors suckers, minnows, and sunfish (Coutant, 1977).

**Dissolved solids and suspended sediment.** Dissolved solids (a measure of salinity, the sum of major cations and anions) and suspended sediment are generally lower in the Southern Rocky Mountains than on the Colorado Plateau. Sucker species tolerate higher concentrations and were more prevalent where dissolved solids were high. Changes in geology, from crystalline bedrock in the mountains to sedimentary deposits on the plateau, help explain these differences (Apodaca et al., 1996). Suspended sediment carries metals, trace elements, and pesticides downstream, and plateau species tolerate high concentrations.

**Stream habitat and substrate.** Invertivore species are more abundant in streams with cobble and gravel substrates, which provide invertebrate habitat. Most cold-water species need gravel and cobble for spawning. Headwater substrates are mostly coarse material, while plateau streams are dominated by fine sand, silt, clay, and muck. Those fine materials reduce habitat for aquatic plants and invertebrates and fill the spaces between rocks, reducing shelter and spawning areas for fish. Warm-water omnivores tolerate these conditions better.

**Human influences.** Mining, agriculture, dams, and recreation influence water quality and habitat, and these effects appear in the fish data (see Key Findings).

## Key Findings

**Overall community patterns**

- Fish community composition reflects the water quality and habitat that characterize the two physiographic provinces.
- Of the total number of fish in the UCOL basin, **94 percent** were classified as invertivores or omnivores. The remaining **6 percent** were herbivores or piscivores.
- Invertivores predominated at Southern Rocky Mountains sites. Omnivores predominated at Colorado Plateau sites. Herbivores and piscivores occurred only at Colorado Plateau sites.
- Sites in a **transition zone** between provinces (the Gunnison River site 7, the Uncompahgre site 8, and the Dolores site 9) showed overlapping trophic classes. Site 7 is downstream of a large dam and is hydrologically a Southern Rocky Mountains site, with cold-water releases and suitable habitat supporting high-elevation species.

**Site-level results**

- **No fish** were found at French Gulch (site 2, a mining site) or Dry Fork (site 10, a background site). At French Gulch, cutthroat trout are present upstream of the mined area but absent downstream. At Dry Fork, the absence was attributed more to low streamflow and associated habitat than to water quality.
- The Blue River site (site 3, a mining site) had low fish abundance and few species compared with other Southern Rocky Mountains sites.
- Red Wash (site 14, agricultural) had fewer individuals and species than other Colorado Plateau sites.
- Of the **13 sites** with fish, **eleven** contained both native and nonnative species.
- Only **nonnative** species were present at two sites: site 3 (Blue River, downstream of a mined area) and site 7 (Gunnison River below Gunnison Tunnel, downstream of a series of dams).
- Site 6 (Gunnison River at Country Road 32) supported a high-elevation, cold-water sucker species and may be affected by fishing pressure on trout and by increased pooled area below a reservoir, which favors suckers.
- Site 9 supported primarily low-elevation, warm-water sucker species.
- At site 12 (Dry Creek), invertivore and omnivore guilds were more evenly represented. **90 percent** of invertivore species collected were minnows, which are characteristic of Colorado Plateau habitat.

**Multiple-reach variability**

- At Baker (site 1), species counts ranged from **6 to 9** among sampled reaches, the greatest among-reach difference in species number.
- At Gore (site 4), total fish abundance ranged from **115 to 371** among reaches, the greatest among-reach difference in abundance.

**Water quality and habitat relationships**

- Water temperature and dissolved solids are generally lower in the Southern Rocky Mountains than on the Colorado Plateau.
- Suspended sediment concentrations increase downstream on the Colorado Plateau, with species diversity of omnivores also increasing downstream.
- Substrate composition and trophic classes together indicate a relationship between fish communities and their habitat.

**Summary conclusions**

- Southern Rocky Mountains communities are dominated by invertivores such as trout, in forested, high-gradient, cold-water streams with boulder, cobble, and gravel substrates.
- Colorado Plateau communities are dominated by omnivores, herbivores, and piscivores such as suckers, minnows, and sunfish, in slow, warm, low-gradient streams with high dissolved solids, high suspended sediment, and fine substrates.
