# Desert Fishes Council Abstract: Population Estimates of Big-River Fishes in the Little Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Desert Fishes Council abstract (1995 symposium, published 1996) reports on the [[Desert Fishes Council]] research on four indigenous big-river fishes of western North America, all tied to habitat disturbance from human-modified river systems. The study estimates seasonal and annual population sizes of these species in the [[Little Colorado River]] near the [[Grand Canyon]], using [[Mark-Recapture Analysis]] data. The abstract also summarizes a companion genetics progress report on [[Gila Mitochondrial DNA]] in the same genus. The OCR scan is heavily degraded, so the Spanish summary was used to check several names and values.

## Background & Description

The abstract addresses four large-river fishes native to the Colorado River system:

- [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), listed as federally endangered.
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), listed as federally endangered.
- [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), listed as federally endangered.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), listed as federally endangered.

The [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) is described as a candidate for listing and as under review, though the OCR text is unclear on its exact status. A seventh species, a catostomid sucker, is also mentioned. Its name is garbled in the scan, and it is most likely *Pantosteus discobolus* (inferred, not confirmed).

The authors describe the life histories of these species as "enigmatic." Population densities are relatively unknown in most tributaries.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat disturbance:** The abstract attributes the decline of these native fishes to habitat alteration caused by human activity and modern water management.
- **Study site:** The work focuses on the Little Colorado River, a major tributary of the Colorado River in the [[Grand Canyon]] region.
- **Life history link:** The authors argue that differences in population estimates between species reflect differences in longevity and life history traits.
- **Temporal variation:** Population estimates were compared across seasons and years, showing both within-season and between-season variation.

## Key Findings

1. **Population estimation method:** Population sizes were estimated from [[Mark-Recapture Analysis]] data using a model that accounts for immigration, emigration, and ontogeny (growth-related changes). This allowed seasonal and annual estimates.
2. **Effort normalization:** Estimates were standardized by fishing effort and compared within and between species, by year and by season.
3. **Species differences:** Differences in population estimates among species are attributed to longevity and life history traits, rather than to sampling artifacts alone.
4. **Conservation status:** Three of the four core species (Colorado Pikeminnow, Humpback Chub, Bonytail, and Razorback Sucker, all four named as endangered) face federal protection; the Roundtail Chub is a candidate for listing.
5. **Genetics progress report:** A companion report, "Evolutionary Genetics of *Gila*," examines [[Mitochondrial DNA]] (cytochrome b region, restriction site data, and sequence data) to study evolutionary relationships within the genus. The abstract is a progress report, so no final conclusions are given.

**Limitations:** The OCR is badly degraded. Exact sample sizes, numerical population estimates, and confidence intervals are not legible in the provided text, so no specific figures are reported here.
