# Impact of Hybrid Amur in Two California Irrigation Systems: 1982 Progress Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This progress report describes the third year (1982) of a study on the effects of [[Hybrid Amur]] (female [[Grass Carp]] x male [[Bighead Carp]]) on the aquatic ecosystem of two southern California irrigation systems: the [[Coachella Canal]] and [[Wormwood Lateral 3]]. It covers aquatic plants, resident fishes, invertebrates and water quality. The study was conducted by the Coachella Valley Water District, the Bureau of Reclamation and cooperating agencies.

Key results: at peak growth (August), all hybrid-stocked areas held significantly less plant biomass than unstocked controls. Hydrilla regrew in Wormwood Section 2, the lowest-density stocking, after water temperatures fell. Condition factors of resident fishes were higher in stocked areas than in controls in both canals. No negative effects on invertebrates or water quality were detected. In a separate trial, surgically sterilized white amur eliminated all vegetation in six days at 650 kg/ha, whereas hybrids did not noticeably reduce vegetation, though muskrat burrowing invalidated quantitative consumption comparisons.

## Background & Description

Dense aquatic vegetation restricts flow in irrigation canals. Mechanical and chemical control are costly, temporary and environmentally damaging, so [[Biological Control]] with herbivorous fish has attracted attention. [[White Amur]] (grass carp) is used for canal weed control in several countries, but its introduction is banned in 40 US states because of concerns over reproduction. A Hungarian team produced the [[Hybrid Amur]] in 1975 by crossing female grass carp with male bighead carp. The F1 hybrid is believed to be sterile and feeds similarly to grass carp, which would prevent overpopulation and reduce risks to waterfowl and other components of the ecosystem.

The study has two objectives: (1) evaluate hybrid amur's capacity to control aquatic vegetation, and (2) determine its impact on resident fishes, invertebrates and water quality. It was co-funded by the Coachella Valley Water District, Imperial Irrigation District, California Department of Fish and Game, California Department of Water Resources and the [[Bureau of Reclamation]].

**Study sites**
- **Coachella Canal:** a 196 km open concrete-lined conveyance system (approx. 34.0 hm³ capacity, 1.2 m/s velocity; flow 2.8 to 25.5 m³/s). The study covered a 5 km portion divided into reaches (0.73 to 2.27 km) by siphons and screens. Dominant submerged plants were Eurasian watermilfoil, sago pondweed and curlyleaf pondweed.
- **Wormwood Lateral 3:** a small delivery canal (design capacity 3 m³/s, average depth 1 m, width 3.2 m) about 18 km southwest of El Centro. Hydrilla, first found in the Imperial Irrigation District in 1977 and infesting about 644 km of canals, was the principal hydrophyte.

**Study design and history.** The original plan (one year of baseline data, two years of treatment) was revised after first-year results showed extreme plant variability. Exclosures (approx. 2 m wide and 6.5 m long) were built in each experimental area in spring 1981 to serve as within-area controls. Hybrids were purchased from commercial hatcheries in Arkansas. A ploidy check found 100% triploids in the first shipment, but a second shipment in November 1980 contained a high proportion of diploids. Stocked 1980 fish were therefore over 250 mm long. Mortality was high: 81% of 1979 stock died May to October 1980, and 55% of 1980 stock died November 1980 to July 1981. Stocking was delayed to June 1981, and only one Coachella reach and three Wormwood sections could be stocked. In May 1982 Wormwood Section 3 was made an unstocked control after overwinter losses.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Resident fishes.** The Coachella Canal supports game fish including channel catfish, flathead catfish, common carp, largemouth bass, bluegill and tilapia, plus threadfin shad, mosquitofish and red shiner. Potential negative effects of hybrid amur include loss of spawning, rearing or protective habitat, reduced food (plant-associated invertebrates), and competition for food and space. Electrofishing was used in Reach 25 throughout, and in Reach 27 until April 1981, after which drawdown and seining were used because hybrids can be lured by electroshocking.

**Invertebrates.** The benthic community of the Coachella Canal is dominated by the bivalve [[Corbicula]] and includes chironomids, oligochaetes and nematodes. Wormwood Lateral 3 invertebrate densities were much higher, though its gross taxonomic composition was similar.

**Plant–invertebrate links.** Plant-associated invertebrates include trichopterans, ephemeropterans, chironomids, oligochaetes, zygopterans, ostracods and the snail *Physa*.

**Water quality.** Monthly physicochemical sampling covered temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen, conductivity, alkalinity, hardness, turbidity, TDS, phosphorus, nitrogen forms, COD and total organic carbon.

**Context.** The authors note that the Coachella Canal is an extreme lotic environment. It is completely channelized, with a smooth sandy bottom except at siphon entrances and on the sloped berms, which limits habitat for fish. Earlier studies suggest plant cover above about 30% harms centrarchid condition and populations, which may be more critical in canals without riparian cover.

## Key Findings

**Vegetation control (Part I)**
- In August, all hybrid-stocked areas had significantly lower plant biomass than unstocked controls.
- Coachella Reach 27 downstream exclosure had significantly higher vegetation than the adjacent treatment line on all 1982 sampling dates. The upstream exclosure was also higher in April and October. Reach 26 exclosure also showed enhanced growth, which the authors attribute to sediment deposition on the inside of a bend in Reach 27.
- Wormwood Section 1: exclosure plant density was significantly greater in August, October and December; vegetation was mainly sago pondweed with increasing hydrilla.
- Wormwood Section 2: hydrilla density in the exclosure quickly exceeded the canal and reached a self-limiting level by August. Autumn growth was dominated by new stems (8.9 stems/m² in August rising to 1420.4 in October), with stem length increasing only about 25%. Section 2 is considered understocked.
- Section 4 was essentially barren until October, when hydrilla appeared and was significantly denser in the exclosure in October and December.
- Eurasian watermilfoil control was questionable; authors suggest hybrids may avoid it because of distasteful compounds.

**White vs. hybrid amur (Part II)**
- Fish were similar in size (white TL 251 mm, W 176 g; hybrid TL 250 mm, W 169 g). Stocking was 72 white and 74 hybrid amur at 650 kg/ha.
- All vegetation in white-amur segments was eliminated by day six. Hybrid segments showed no noticeable reduction.
- Muskrat burrowing at partitions allowed fish mixing, so relative consumption rates could not be quantified.
- Authors attribute the 1981 failure to acclimation time, larger fish in 1982, and stocking before peak growth.

**Resident fishes (Part I)**
- Condition factors (K) were higher in Reach 27 (stocked) than Reach 25 (control) in December 1982 for all four game species, except largemouth bass over 350 mm. Four of nine comparisons were statistically significant.
- Wormwood: nine of ten channel catfish size-class comparisons in January 1983 showed higher condition in treatment sections. Five were significant, three of these against the control with the highest hybrid density.
- Authors conclude that hybrids, or removal of plants by hybrids, had a positive effect on resident fish condition.

**Invertebrates (Part IIa and IIb)**
- Coachella: no consistent treatment effect. Corbicula was denser in Reach 25 on all post-introduction dates, with two significant differences. Pre-introduction data were inadequate for comparison.
- Wormwood: few significant canal–exclosure differences and no consistent pattern. Of 134 correlations between benthos and plant density, nine (6.7%) were significant at the 5% level, close to chance.
- Plant-associated invertebrate abundance appears to scale directly with plant biomass. Only two of twelve slope comparisons were significant, so there is no evidence that hybrids alter invertebrate density per unit plant or prefer particular plant species.

**Water quality (Part III)**
- Coachella: only pH and conductivity showed significant slope changes after introduction (of 13 parameters tested in total, two were significant); no clear impact.
- Wormwood: only chemical oxygen demand of ten parameters changed significantly (p < 0.05).

**Overall conclusions**
- Positive impact on resident fish condition; no detectable negative impact on invertebrates or water quality.
- Limitations: experimental scale is small relative to potential use, sampling was infrequent, and impact assessment was secondary to efficacy testing.
- Mechanical and chemical control are episodic and more disruptive, whereas herbivorous fish remove plants gradually. The authors conclude that hybrid amur use "is not necessarily calamitous" to the ecosystem, in contrast to alternative control methods.
- Future work will focus on optimizing stocking to control vegetation effectively while leaving enough cover for fish.
