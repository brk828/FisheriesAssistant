# Interagency Proposal for Native Fish Rearing Facility on Lake Mohave

#ichthyology #conservation-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This February 1990 correspondence from the Bureau of Reclamation Lower Colorado Regional Office documents an interagency initiative to develop native fish rearing facilities on [[Lake Mohave]] for [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*). The proposal responds to critical population declines, with existing populations consisting solely of individuals over 30 years old showing little to no natural recruitment. The Bureau of Reclamation, [[National Park Service]], and [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] coordinated to develop a three-phase plan (FY 1990-1992) to construct backwater rearing facilities with barriers to prevent predator access, based on successful 1984 experimental work at [[Arizona Bay]].

## Background & Description

The [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) populations in [[Lake Mohave]] have been subjects of multi-agency research and monitoring for several years prior to 1990. Age structure analysis revealed populations composed entirely of old fish (>30 years), indicating recruitment failure and imminent population collapse within years.

Previous experimental work by Dr. Paul Marsh, Dr. W. L. Minckley, and Jim Brooks demonstrated the feasibility of spawning and rearing suckers in isolated backwaters. They renovated a naturally isolated backwater and stocked it with spawning [[Razorback Sucker|Razorback Suckers]]. The backwater "teemed with young Suckers" following successful spawning. However, rising lake elevations reconnected the backwater to the main reservoir, allowing exotic predator access and resulting in rapid disappearance of small juveniles as exotic fish numbers increased.

The proposed solution involves constructing permanent or temporary barriers to create cost-effective, low-maintenance rearing facilities where fish could be raised to sizes less susceptible to predation before strategic release into the lake to replenish existing populations.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Lower Colorado River]] basin native fish populations face critical threats from exotic predator species and altered habitat conditions. The complete absence of natural recruitment in both [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] populations indicates fundamental ecological disruption preventing successful reproduction or juvenile survival in the main reservoir environment.

The backwater rearing facility concept addresses predation as the primary limiting factor for juvenile survival. By isolating spawning adults and developing juveniles from predators until they reach less vulnerable sizes, the approach aims to bypass the recruitment bottleneck while maintaining genetic diversity from wild [[Lake Mohave]] populations.

The initiative operates under the Bureau of Reclamation's newly adopted Fishery Policy emphasizing commitment to maintain or enhance fishery resources on Reclamation projects, and Memorandums of Understanding with [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and [[National Park Service]] for cooperative management.

## Key Findings

**Population Status and Urgency:**
- [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] populations in [[Lake Mohave]] consist solely of individuals >30 years old
- Little to no natural recruitment documented
- Populations expected to die off within the next few years
- Loss of these genetic pools characterized as "disastrous"
- "Time is running out for these populations"

**Proof of Concept:**
- 1984 [[Arizona Bay]] renovation demonstrated successful spawning and rearing in isolated backwaters
- Spawning [[Razorback Sucker|Razorback Suckers]] successfully reproduced in renovated backwater
- Backwater "teemed with young Suckers" under isolated conditions
- Rising lake elevations reconnecting backwater to main reservoir allowed predator access
- Small juveniles quickly disappeared as exotic fish numbers increased following reconnection

**Proposed Three-Phase Implementation:**

*Phase 1 (FY 1990):*
- Create Federal team ([[Bureau of Reclamation]], [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[National Park Service]])
- Identify and resolve Federal barriers
- Identify backwater sites
- Develop preliminary designs and costs
- Identify funding sources and agency commitments
- Write draft proposal

*Phase 2 (FY 1991):*
- Invite state agencies ([[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Nevada Department of Wildlife]]) and public participation
- Request input from other agencies
- Reexamine agency resources and contributions
- Refine designs, costs, and management objectives
- Finalize proposal
- Enter formal interagency agreement designating cost and management responsibilities

*Phase 3 (FY 1992):*
- Construct and initiate backwater facilities
- Develop design specifications
- Award construction contracts
- Construct facilities
- Initiate operations

**Meeting Coordination:**
- Executive staff meeting held January 16, 1990
- All three Federal agencies expressed need and support for the concept
- Planning meeting scheduled March 2, 1990 at [[Arizona State University]] Krause Hall
- Field surveys scheduled March 19-23, 1990
- Objectives include developing proposal outline, work schedule, and identifying realistic objectives for FY 1990-1992

**Technical Considerations:**
- Permanent or temporary barrier construction required
- Permits needed: endangered species, 404, 402, park authorizations
- [[National Environmental Policy Act]] compliance required
- Management goals and methods to be determined
- Replication of 1984 [[Arizona Bay]] renovation work recommended
