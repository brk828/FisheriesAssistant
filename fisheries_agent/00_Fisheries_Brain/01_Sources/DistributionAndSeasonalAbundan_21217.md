# Distribution and Seasonal Abundance of Entomostraca in Moorland Ponds near Windermere

#limnology #freshwater-biology #entomostraca

## Executive Summary

This study by W. J. P. Smyly, of the [[Freshwater Biological Association]], examines the distribution and seasonal abundance of [[Entomostraca]] (small freshwater crustaceans in the orders [[Copepoda]], [[Cladocera]] and [[Ostracoda]]) in three small moorland ponds on Claife Heights near [[Windermere]]. Sampling ran from April 1951 to December 1953. The work contrasts open water with marginal weed-beds and compares the sampling efficiency of several gear types. The main results are that more species occur among water plants than in open water, that local distribution is often linked to particular plants or bottom type, that species show distinct seasonal periodicities, and that the drainings of [[Scale Tarn]] strongly reduced weed-associated Cladocera for the rest of the study.

## Background & Description

**Study ponds.** The ponds were formed about 50 years earlier by damming and lie on boulder clay or thin peat over Bannisdale slate. Their chemistry is similar (pH 6.8, Ca 5.0 p.p.m.). Mean depth is about 1 m and maximum depth at the dams about 15 m.

| Pond | Area (m²) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| [[Scale Tarn]] | 6,100 | Relatively open; drained and refilled July and October 1951 |
| [[Hodson Tarn]] | 4,700 | Sheltered on three sides by wooded slopes; [[Myriophyllum]] in deep water |
| [[Wise Een Tarn]] | 44,500 | Relatively open; studied earlier (Smyly 1952) |

Water-level variation was small. Ice covered the ponds for at least a few weeks each winter and persisted longest on Hodson Tarn.

**Vegetation.** [[Carex Rostrata]] was the common shallow-water plant. In Hodson Tarn it formed one bed choked with rotting stems, while in Scale Tarn it formed an almost continuous fringe with little detritus. [[Potamogeton Natans]] occurred in small amounts beyond the Carex zone. Deep-water plants were [[Myriophyllum]] sp. in Hodson Tarn and [[Nitella Translucens]] in Scale Tarn. Nitella almost filled the central region of Scale Tarn before the drainings destroyed it, leaving the middle virtually weed-free for most of the study.

**Sampling methods.**
- **Open water (mid-pond):** a [[Tow-Net Sampling]] net (60 meshes/in.). In Wise Een Tarn a boat towed it for equal times on each visit. In the other two ponds it was cast from the dam on a 14 m line, five casts per visit.
- **Marginal weeds:** a [[Tube Sampler]] (metal tube 52 cm long, 8.5 cm diameter) that isolates a vertical water column; a conventional pond-net; and trays holding nine open-mouthed bottles left on the bottom for 24 hours.
- **Counting:** total counts for tube and tray samples and for tow-net samples under about 300 animals. Larger tow-net samples were sub-sampled in 5 ml fractions. Identification used keys by Scourfield and Harding (1941), Gurney (1931–33) and Klie (1928).
- **Replication:** three tube samples per station. Replicate counts fitted a [[Poisson Distribution]] in most species.

The tube was compared with the [[Macan Grab]] (1949). It caused less disturbance and damage, and it caught more scarce bottom-dwelling species than either the grab or the pond-net. Its limits are that it works only at wading depth, needs a firm clay bottom for a plug, and may miss surface-living animals.

Sampling frequency was fortnightly in 1951 and 1952 and monthly in 1953, with gaps caused by the drainings and by Hodson Tarn not being visited from April to July 1951. Collecting usually took place between 10 a.m. and noon. One comparison at dusk and after dark in Wise Een Tarn showed no difference in relative abundance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Weed association.** More species were found among weeds than in open water. Five species were abundant in open water: [[Diaptomus Gracilis]], [[Daphnia Longispina]], [[Ceriodaphnia Pulchella]], [[Bosmina longirostris]] and [[Polyphemus pediculus]]. Of these, only Daphnia longispina was rare in weed-beds. Scarcity of Daphnia longispina in weeds matches earlier literature and was consistent across all three ponds where weeds were sampled.

**Unexplained patterns.** The distributions of Ceriodaphnia pulchella and [[Diaphanosoma Brachyurum]] were hard to explain. Ceriodaphnia pulchella was abundant in both weeds and open water in Scale Tarn but scarce in Hodson Tarn. Diaphanosoma brachyurum, usually pelagic, was rare in both settings in these ponds but was found in the weedy middle of Hodson Tarn and in [[Nitella Translucens]] in Wise Een Tarn. The author suggests the two species may compete, with weedy middle regions favouring Diaphanosoma and open water favouring Ceriodaphnia. Records from Three Dubs Tarn (Macan 1949) support the idea: Diaphanosoma formed under 10% of open-water Crustacea when weed was sparse, but was the commonest plankton species when Nitella formed a dense sward.

**Plant and bottom associations.** Local distribution varied from random to bunched in every species. Some species were tied to particular habitats:
- [[Sida Crystallina]] was common beneath floating [[Potamogeton Natans]] leaves, sometimes up to 20 individuals per leaf, and was scarce in other weeds.
- [[Eurycercus Lamellatus]], [[Chydorus Sphaericus]] and Peracantha truncata were especially abundant in Nitella. Simocephalus exspinosus was often numerous in Carex.
- [[Ilyocryptus Sordidus]] and [[Candona Candida]] were more abundant on soft mud than on gravel or clay.
- [[Drepanothrix dentata]] and [[Cypria exsculpta]] were abundant where detritus covered the bottom.

**Vertical distribution.** Tray and tube catches showed that most swimming species lay within about 10 cm of the bottom in daylight. Surface-living Sida crystallina and bottom-living Ilyocryptus sordidus were scarce in the open-mouthed bottles. Cyclops agilis, Cyclops macruroides and Simocephalus exspinosus clung to vessel walls and were caught more in the bottles than in the tube. Cyclops fuscus, which hangs from the surface film, was taken more in the pond-net than in the tube.

**Seasonality.** Some species were mainly cold-season forms (Canthocamptus staphylinus, Cyclops agilis, Cyclops macruroides). Others were warm-season forms (Diaptomus gracilis, Ceriodaphnia pulchella, Cyclops viridis, Cyclops albidus, Eurycercus lamellatus, Peracantha truncata, Simocephalus exspinosus, Sida crystallina). Chydorus sphaericus was abundant in spring and numerous in winter. Most Cladocera overwintered as resting eggs, although some parthenogenetic females survived, and adult Chydorus sphaericus were found under thick ice. Canthocamptus staphylinus encysted in bottom mud in summer, and Cyclops bicolor encysted as copepodid stages III–IV in winter. Ice-melt timing also affected Diaptomus gracilis: in early March it was abundant (100–200 per five hauls) in Wise Een and Scale Tarns but scarce (under 10 per five hauls) in Hodson Tarn until mid-May. The author attributes this to the sheltered position of Hodson Tarn, which receives less sun and wind.

**Effects of draining.** Scale Tarn's drainings in July and October 1951 removed weed-dwelling Cladocera that had been abundant in 1951. The pond had only small puddles when empty, and these held mainly copepods. Scarcity of weed Cladocera in 1952 and 1953 is attributed to the drainings. Open-water populations of Diaptomus, Daphnia and Ceriodaphnia recovered quickly, so the drainings appear to have had a negligible effect on open-water Entomostraca.

## Key Findings

1. **Species richness:** Forty-seven species were recorded in marginal weeds of Scale Tarn. Of these, 16 were abundant (over 30/litre), 22 numerous (10–30/litre) and nine rare (never over 10/litre). Scale Tarn had 14 abundant weed species against five in Hodson Tarn.
2. **Abundance comparison:** Scale Tarn had more abundant species and larger population numbers than Hodson Tarn for most species, except detritus-dwellers.
3. **Weeds vs open water:** More species occurred among weeds. Only Daphnia longispina was abundant in open water but rare in weed-beds.
4. **Maximum Eurycercus lamellatus:** The author reports a maximum of 19,800 (units given as m² in the source) in July 1951 in Scale Tarn. Dr W. E. Frost identified this as the only cladoceran extensively eaten by fish in these ponds. It was not found in Hodson Tarn during the three study years.
5. **Gear efficiency:** The tube caught more scarce and bottom-living species than the pond-net or the Macan grab. Tow-nets and pond-nets caught free-swimming forms about equally, but bottom-crawlers were taken two to three times as often by the tube.
6. **Eucyclops ecology:** Cyclops macruroides and Cyclops macruroides denticulatus were relatively abundant in Hodson Tarn but scarce in Scale Tarn, except in June and August 1951. Cyclops agilis was abundant in Scale Tarn on all occasions but scarce in Hodson Tarn, apart from a spring 1952 rise. The author suggests the two macruroides forms share more ecology with each other than with agilis.
7. **Competition hypothesis:** Diaphanosoma brachyurum and Ceriodaphnia pulchella may compete, with weedy middle regions favouring the former and open water the latter.
8. **Seasonal change in Hodson Tarn:** Weed species rose and planktonic species fell in the middle of Hodson Tarn, mainly in 1953, as Myriophyllum grew. The author links this to Myriophyllum development.
9. **Distribution pattern:** Local distribution was random or bunched depending on species. Cyclops viridis and Cyclops albidus were most often random, and non-random distribution was more common in Cladocera than in Copepoda.
10. **Puzzling fluctuations:** Chydorus sphaericus was scarce in spring 1953 despite being abundant in 1951 and 1952. Ilyocryptus sordidus was abundant until January 1953, then scarce. Candona candida was numerous from May to September 1952, then scarce in winter. The author suggests winter migration to deeper water, following Berg (1938) in Esrom Lake.
11. **Conclusion:** Open-water Entomostraca were largely unaffected by the drainings. Weed-associated Cladocera were strongly reduced, and their recovery was limited within the study period.
