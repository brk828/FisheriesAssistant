# Quantitative Genetics in Conservation

#conservation-genetics #quantitative-genetics #population-genetics #drosophila

## Executive Summary

This document is a book chapter by Michael Lynch on how [[Quantitative Genetics]] should inform the conservation of endangered species. Its central argument is that molecular markers are weak proxies for the adaptive genetic variation that determines whether a population can survive environmental change. The chapter sets out population-size thresholds for maintaining that variation, a model of the maximum rate of environmental change a population can track, and an analysis of how [[Deleterious Mutations]] accumulate and drive [[Mutational Meltdown]] in small populations. It closes with recommendations on captive breeding, [[Population Augmentation]], and the risks of mixing stocks. The scanned pages also include a reference list from an unrelated chapter on tiger beetles, musk turtles and mosquitofish phylogeography. Fish content is limited to a few examples.

## Background & Description

The chapter opens with the observation that US Endangered Species Act listing policy has waited until populations shrink to roughly 1,000 individuals for animals. The author argues that such thresholds fall within the range where short-term extinction risk is a serious concern. The text describes [[Quantitative Genetics]] as the study of traits controlled by many loci and modified by environment. Its success in plant and animal breeding is cited as evidence of its practical utility.

Key concepts introduced:

- **[[Heritability]] (h²):** the fraction of phenotypic variance with an additive genetic basis, equivalent to the parent-offspring regression slope.
- **[[Breeders' Equation]]:** response to selection equals the selection differential multiplied by heritability. The author notes it separates evolution into selection and inheritance components.
- **[[Effective Population Size]] (Ne):** the size of an idealized population that would show the same genetic drift as the actual population. For many species it is about one-tenth to one-third of the number of breeding adults.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The chapter places conservation genetics within pressures such as habitat degradation, pollution, global climate change, species introductions and harvesting. Most of these impose conditions a population has not experienced before. The author argues that the rate of environmental change matters as much as its magnitude, and that small populations are least able to track it.

Several aquatic examples appear in the text:

- A [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia*) experiment in which replicate populations passed through small bottlenecks. Average allozyme heterozygosity fell, but some replicates increased.
- Freshwater sculpins (*Cottus*), where a strong positive correlation was reported between allozyme heterozygosity and phenotypic variation within populations.
- *Daphnia* populations, where no association was found between allozyme heterozygosity and total genetic variance for fitness characters.
- Atlantic salmon and hatchery and wild salmonid interactions, cited in the context of domestication and fitness loss.

Most quantitative results come from *Drosophila melanogaster* and are applied generally. The [[Maximum Rate Of Sustainable Evolution]] model, and the mutation parameters used to estimate mutation load, derive from fly data.

## Key Findings

**1. Molecular diversity is a weak surrogate for adaptive variation.** The author gives four reasons:
- Molecular heterozygosity is introduced at about 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁵ per year (OCR-garbled exponents, as read), while quantitative variation enters at about 10⁻³ to 10⁻² per generation.
- Non-additive gene action can make additive variance rise after bottlenecks. The cotton-top tamarin, with very low molecular heterozygosity, still shows high heritability for body weight.
- Sampling error makes the link between the two kinds of variation statistically unreliable.
- Experimental and comparative data (mosquitofish, *Drosophila*, sculpins, humans, gophers) show inconsistent correlations.

**2. Population size thresholds.**
- Ne above roughly 1,000 is the level beyond which further increases add little adaptive variance.
- For many species this corresponds to 5,000 to 10,000 breeding adults per generation.
- Populations of more than 10,000 reproductive adults are described as likely to be limited for adaptive variation, and the author says genetic integrity is at risk at about two to three orders of magnitude below that. He concludes that much higher protection standards are needed.

**3. Loss of variation.** In a small population, about 1/(2Ne) of genetic variance is lost each generation, balanced by mutational input. Half of founder additive variance is lost after about 1.4 Ne generations and 90% after about 4.6 Ne.

**4. Maximum sustainable rate of evolution.**
- Populations with Ne below 1/(4r_max) have no long-term viability even in a constant environment.
- For large populations the critical rate of environmental change is bounded by a function of heritability, mutation rate and the population growth rate.
- The author concludes that even large populations are unlikely to track environmental change faster than a few percent of a phenotypic standard deviation per generation.
- Climate change could threaten isolated populations as strongly as habitat loss threatens short-term survival.

**5. Deleterious mutations.** Three sources of load are identified: segregating ancestral mutations, new mutations in the isolated population, and recurrent fixation of mutations.
- Using *Drosophila* parameters (U ≈ 1.5 genomic deleterious mutation rate, s ≈ 0.01, h ≈ 0.36, with lethal mutations about 0.02 per genome per generation), each individual carries roughly 140 mildly deleterious segregating genes.
- Purging of ancestral load requires Ne greater than about 1/(2s) for partial and 10/(2s) for near-complete removal. Under random drift at Ne below about 33 for these parameters, load is maximal and fixations accumulate.
- Fitness declines by about 2.2% per generation in very small populations under the *Drosophila* parameters.
- With Ne of several dozen, mean time to extinction from mutation accumulation is a few tens to a few hundred generations in the absence of stochasticity.

**6. Mutational meltdown.** Once fitness falls below the replacement level, drift and mutation accumulation reinforce each other and the population declines rapidly. Stochastic fluctuations can shorten meltdown time by an order of magnitude or more. The text notes captive environments may render harmful mutations nearly neutral, and that harsh environments expose recessive load, which increases inbreeding depression.

**7. Captive breeding and purging.** Purging of inbreeding depression through intentional inbreeding has been recorded in short-term studies of flies, plants and mice, but usually at the cost of extreme selection. Self-fertilized plant lines (*Eichhornia paniculata*) showed no further inbreeding depression, yet crosses between lines showed fitness gains, so the absence of local depression does not rule out a substantial mutation load.

**8. Population augmentation and domestication.** Augmenting wild populations with captive stock carries risks:
- Local adaptations can be disrupted, and domestication selection in captivity can favour traits that are deleterious in nature.
- Hybridization can break up coadapted gene complexes and cause outbreeding depression, as seen in fish crosses within a drainage basin and *Daphnia* crosses within a pond.
- Negative effects may appear only in later generations, so early success can encourage further augmentation that later proves harmful.

**9. Management recommendations.** The author argues that protection standards must be considerably higher than current listing thresholds, and that captive breeding programs should be designed to limit both domestication and relaxation of natural selection.
