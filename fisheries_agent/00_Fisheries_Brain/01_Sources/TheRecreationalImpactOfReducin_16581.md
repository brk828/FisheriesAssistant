# The Recreational Impact of Reducing the "Laughing Waters" of Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #desert-streams #instream-flow #wilderness-management

## Executive Summary

This paper is primarily a sociological and water-policy study examining how reduced streamflow in [[Aravaipa Creek]], Arizona, affects recreational visitor perceptions and experience within the [[Aravaipa Canyon Wilderness]]. It is not a fish biology paper per se, but it references the native fish assemblage of Aravaipa Creek as a key rationale for the [[Bureau of Land Management]]'s (BLM) 1981 instream flow permit application. The creek supports seven native fish species, including two federally threatened species, and prior instream flow studies (Turner and Tafanelli 1983; Minckley 1981) had recommended minimum and average monthly flow regimes to sustain native fish habitat. The current study found that visitors ranked water as the single most important recreational attribute of the wilderness (33.4% ranked it #1), and that visitor perceptions of water quantity (and indirectly, perceived water quality) were statistically related to actual streamflow via logistic regression modeling. The paper argues that flow regimes needed for fish and wildlife purposes (peaking in spring runoff and summer monsoon) do not align with peak hiking recreation seasons (April–May, September–October), highlighting a tension between fisheries-based instream flow standards and recreational needs.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat structure**: Native fish seek food and shelter among **boulders and mats of watercress** within the stream channel, indicative of a benthic/structure-associated lotic habitat use pattern typical of small desert perennial streams.
- **Riparian context**: The stream corridor is lined with cottonwood, Arizona walnut, alder, willow, mesquite, and box elder, forming a riparian oasis that supports a broader vertebrate community (200+ bird species, 46 mammal species, 46 reptile species, 8 amphibian species), reflecting the ecological centrality of the aquatic-riparian interface to the wilderness ecosystem as a whole.
- **Flow regime dependency**: Native Southwestern desert fishes are described as having evolved under natural flow regimes with peak flows during **spring runoff (January–March)** and **summer monsoons (June–August)**. This seasonal flow dependency is central to the fish habitat conservation rationale for the instream flow permit.
- **Recommended instream flow requirements for fish/wildlife** (from cited prior studies, not original data in this paper):
  - BLM's initial (1981) estimate: average monthly flow of 15 cfs total (10 cfs for fish and wildlife, 5 cfs for recreation).
  - [[Minckley 1981]] (expert report on Southwestern fishes): monthly flows recommended to vary between 10–25 cfs while maintaining an average of ~18 cfs.
  - [[Turner and Tafanelli 1983]] (instream flow incremental methodology study for USFWS): detailed monthly flow needs specifically to sustain aquatic habitat of the two federally threatened fish species.
  - Reported average annual flow of Aravaipa Creek: 18–25 cfs (U.S. Bureau of Land Management 1988a); measured mean annual flow during the visitor survey period was 27.5 cfs (95% C.I. = ±2.2 cfs), median flow 22.7 cfs, modal flow 24.2 cfs.
- **Habitat degradation pathway under reduced flow**: As streamflow drops, swimming holes become shallower, algal/aquatic plant mats become exposed and desiccated, and pools disconnect from the main channel, becoming stagnant — a process with direct implications for fish habitat quality as well as visitor perception.
- **Non-fishery recreational use**: Despite the presence of native fish, the creek supports no recreational fishery; nature-study observation of fish is a passive recreational activity, with 70% of surveyed visitors expressing interest in observing/identifying fish on future visits (lower than for mammals, reptiles/amphibians, and birds — see Table 2 in source).

## Key Findings

- **Legal/policy driver**: The BLM's 1981 application for an instream flow permit (Application No. 33-87114) was motivated substantially by the need to protect native fish habitat, particularly for the two threatened species, in accordance with Arizona's water code recognizing fish/wildlife and recreation as beneficial uses of surface water.
- **Flow-habitat mismatch for management**: A key finding of this study (not primarily fish-focused but fish-relevant) is that flow regimes optimized for native fish and wildlife (spring/summer peak flows) do not coincide with peak human recreational hiking seasons (spring and fall shoulder months: April, May, September, October). This suggests that an instream flow permit based solely on fisheries/wildlife flow models may be insufficient to meet legally mandated recreational beneficial-use requirements.
- **Streamflow perception linked to habitat cues**: Visitors could detect small reductions in streamflow (logistic regression showed a 45% increase in odds of perceiving "less than preferred" water per 1 cfs drop below 23 cfs), and this perception was likely driven by visible habitat degradation cues (stagnant pools, dead/drying algal mats) that are also indicative of degraded aquatic habitat conditions relevant to fish.
- **No direct water-quality–flow relationship found** via visitor rating (P < 0.05 not significant), but a proxy behavioral measure (likelihood of purifying drinking water) was significantly related to flow, again implicating perceived habitat/water quality changes at lower flows.
- **Outcome**: The sociological/recreational data generated by this study helped the BLM substantiate recreation as a legally defensible beneficial use in Arizona's instream flow permitting process (approved 1989), complementing the fish-habitat-based justification developed in [[Turner and Tafanelli 1983]] and [[Minckley 1981]].
- **Broader ecological significance**: Aravaipa Creek is characterized as an unusually intact, perennial desert stream system relative to most Sonoran Desert streams, which have been degraded by grazing, irrigation, watershed management, urbanization, flood control, and groundwater overdraft — making it a regionally significant refuge for native desert fish fauna and other wildlife.
