# Habitat Use by Striped Bass in a Thermally Stratified Reservoir

#ichthyology #marine-biology #moronidae

## Executive Summary

This study by [[Matthews]] and colleagues examines seasonal habitat use by [[Striped Bass]] (*Morone saxatilis*) in the uplake area of [[Lake Texoma]], a 36,000-hectare reservoir on the Oklahoma-Texas border formed by the [[Red River]] impoundment. Gill-net sampling from 1981 to 1996 and water quality measurements were used to relate catch to temperature and dissolved oxygen. The authors found strong seasonality tied to a threshold near 22°C for large fish, and a temperature-related decline in small fish catch at and above 27–29°C. The findings support the [[Thermal Squeeze Hypothesis]] and the [[Temperature-Oxygen Hypothesis]], and they carry direct implications for fishery managers estimating available habitat.

## Background & Description

Striped bass are anadromous in their native range and are widely stocked in impoundments. Earlier reservoir studies, cited in the paper, described habitat use in only limited detail, and the authors note that lake studies had documented temperatures occupied by tagged fish (for example, [[Coutant and Carroll]], 1980) without linking those patterns to whole-lake water quality. Previous work on [[Cox and Coutant]] (1981) established growth of juvenile striped bass across constant temperatures, with maximum growth near 26°C when fish were fed to satiation.

Field methods included gill netting and profiling of temperature and dissolved oxygen at each collection site, with net placement recorded at depths along the water column. Data came from the uplake area of Lake Texoma, with fish grouped into large, medium and small size classes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Lake Texoma's downlake waters overlie an anoxic hypolimnion during summer stratification, which constrains where fish can occupy the water column. The uplake area was warmer, with surface temperatures often exceeding 22°C in summer, while cooler, oxygenated water persisted closer to 25°C in the downlake region at depth. Coutant's [[Thermal Refugia Hypothesis]] predicts that large striped bass use cool, oxygenated water at depth or downlake, and the authors' data are consistent with this pattern. The population was also shown to tolerate temperatures as high as 29°C, which the authors interpret as a sign that temperature alone does not drive adult distribution; intolerably high temperature combined with low oxygen throughout the water column appears to be the factor.

## Key Findings

- **Seasonal absence of large fish:** Large striped bass were completely absent from collections June through September, and they were collected only when uplake surface temperatures exceeded 22°C during the warmer period. Large fish remained in the study area despite temperatures well above 22°C.
- **Medium-sized fish:** Catch per net of medium fish was significantly lower during June–September, showing a significant decline in abundance under warm conditions.
- **Small fish and oxygen:** Small striped bass were collected in only 3 of 27 nets set where dissolved oxygen was below 6.0 mg/L, compared with 37 of 50 nets where oxygen equaled or exceeded 6.0 mg/L.
- **Temperature threshold for small fish:** Catch per unit effort fell from 11.9 small fish per net at 22–30°C (approximate) to 3.0 at 29°C and 1.9 at 30°C. Catch was significantly higher at temperatures below 27°C (mean = 6.5) than at 27°C or higher (F = 3.84, p < 0.05).
- **Four temperature ranges:** Mean catch per unit effort for small fish across the ranges 22.0–24.9°C, 25.0–26.9°C, 27.0–29.9°C and above 29°C was 7.0, 6.1, 6.5 and 1.9, respectively. Differences were significant at p = 0.05.
- **Ecological interpretation:** Small fish appear to respond to temperature in the field in a way consistent with the laboratory growth results of Cox and Coutant (1981), suggesting an underlying physiological constraint above about 29°C.
- **Management implication:** The authors recommend that fishery managers account for reservoir-wide profiles of physicochemical conditions and the size-class-specific responses of striped bass when estimating total available habitat. Seasonal habitat shifts may also influence interactions with other species.
