# Lampetra Richardsoni: The Western Brook Lamprey

#ichthyology #marine-biology #petromyzonidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic paper describes **[[Lampetra Richardsoni]]**, a new nonparasitic lamprey from freshwater streams of the Pacific drainage of North America. For about a century, western North American brook lampreys had been reported as the European [[Lampetra Planeri]], largely on the assumption that the two were identical. The authors show that the western form is distinct in dentition, body proportions, pigmentation of the head and tail, and geographic range. The description rests on 275 specimens: 69 transformed adults and 206 ammocoetes (larval stages). The paper also reviews the authors' broader conclusion that a distinct endemic parasitic species occurs in western North America (see [[Lampetra Ayresii]]).

## Background & Description

**Taxonomic context.** The family is [[Petromyzonidae]]. The authors note that the name must be spelled Petromyzonidae, not Petromyzontidae, because the stem of the type genus is *Petromyzon-*. Earlier authors (more than 20 listed in the introduction) had recorded *L. planeri* from western North America. The absence of this species from Asia, together with the authors' earlier finding that the parasitic [[Lampetra Fluviatilis]] has a distinct western counterpart, prompted the re-examination.

**Etymology.** The species is named for Sir John Richardson, a author of several important works on North American fishes.

**Diagnosis.** *L. richardsoni* differs from *L. planeri* in four characters:
- The middle circumoral tooth is usually bicuspid rather than tricuspid.
- The branchial region is longer.
- Ammocoetes and transformed individuals have pronounced dark pigmentation on the head and caudal fin.
- It is restricted to western North America rather than Europe.

**Holotype.** A male, 154 mm total length, prespawning (maturity stage 4), collected 30 May 1942 at Smith Creek, [[Cultus Lake]], British Columbia (tag 4554R). It has 64 trunk myomeres. Its disc length is 5.9% of total length, and its branchial length is 10.4%.

**Material examined.** The 275 specimens were:
- 69 transformed individuals: 52 from British Columbia, 15 from Washington, 2 from Oregon.
- 206 ammocoetes: 48 from British Columbia, 158 from Washington.

Collections were taken from many localities, including [[Holmes Creek]] (Vancouver Island), [[Smith Creek]] (Cultus Lake), Millstone River (Nanaimo), North Creek (near Seattle), Steel Lake, Evans Creek, Ames Lake, and the Chehalis and Yakima rivers. Material came from several museums, including the British Columbia Provincial Museum, the University of Washington, and the University of Michigan Museum of Zoology.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Life history.** Early in transformation (maturity stages 1–2), nonparasitic lampreys burrow into stream bottoms or hide under stones, which is why they are rarely collected. After transformation they move toward spawning areas. They do not feed as adults and live only about 4–6 months. They die after spawning, and carcasses decompose quickly or are consumed by aquatic insects and fishes. The authors therefore define three practical maturity stages for nonparasitic forms:
- Stage 3: early maturity, dorsal fins separated.
- Stage 4: prespawning, dorsal fins adjoining.
- Stage 5: spawning, gonads filling the body cavity, dorsal fins contiguous, and a well-developed "anal fin" in females.

**Measurement constraints.** Lampreys have only a notochord as their axial skeleton. Fresh specimens shrink by 2–5 mm (in specimens about 150 mm long) after placement in 4–5% formalin, and lengths are recorded only to the nearest millimetre. These factors, along with sexual dimorphism, explain some irregularities in the proportional data.

**Geographic distribution.** *L. planeri* is confined to freshwater streams of the Atlantic drainage of Europe. *L. richardsoni* occurs in streams of the Pacific drainage of North America, specifically British Columbia, Washington, and Oregon. The authors also identified two ammocoetes (106 and 175 mm) from Lake McDonald, Alaska (collected 1907), as this species, so its presence in Alaska is possible but unconfirmed. The authors say this distribution pattern parallels that of the parasitic pair [[Lampetra Ayresii]] and [[Lampetra Fluviatilis]].

## Key Findings

**1. Dentition distinguishes the two species.**
- Middle circumoral tooth bicuspid in 21 of 23 specimens (91%) in *richardsoni*. In *planeri*, Zanandrea (1958) found it tricuspid in 40 of 47 specimens (85%).
- Infraoral cusps average 7.7 in *richardsoni* (7.8 in 43 British Columbia specimens, 7.4 in 11 Washington specimens) versus 7.1 in *planeri*. The authors note that further study is needed to judge significance.
- Transverse lingual cusps are smaller in *richardsoni*. Maxillary labials are better developed in *planeri*.
- Mandibular labials are absent in both species, though Zanandrea (1958) reported minute ones in *[[Lampetra Zanandreai]]*. The authors say their nature needs further study.

**2. Body proportions differ in the branchial region.**
- Mean branchial length (% of total length): 9.6 (females) and 9.8 (males) in *richardsoni*, versus 8.7 and 8.6 in *planeri*.
- Disc length as a percentage of branchial length: 58.1 (females) and 66.7 (males) in *richardsoni*, versus 64.5 and 78.9 in *planeri*.
- Disc length as a percentage of total length is nearly equal in the two species.
- Ammocoete branchial length averages 12.5% in *richardsoni*, compared with 11.1% and 10.6% in two *planeri* samples.

**3. Size and myomere counts.**
- Transformed females range from 101 to 149 mm (mean 133 mm, n = 16). Males range from 121 to 154 mm (mean 137 mm, n = 29). The smallest transformed specimens are a male of 80 mm and a female of 93 mm.
- Trunk myomeres: 60–67 (mean 63.2, n = 58 transformed) in *richardsoni*, versus 60–65 (mean 62.2) in *planeri*. Ammocoetes have 57–65 (mean 60.7, n = 121), the same as *planeri* ammocoetes.

**4. Pigmentation is the most important ammocoete character.**
- Dark caudal fin pigmentation is present in both transformed and larval *richardsoni*. Among 64 *planeri* ammocoetes, only 1 (1.6%) had pigmentation on the tail around the notochord. Adult *planeri* show weak "peppered" pigment only when spawning.
- Subocular pigmentation: present in 87.3% of *richardsoni* ammocoetes, absent in *planeri*.
- Prebranchial blotch: present in all *richardsoni* ammocoetes, moderate or strong in 91.5% of larger individuals, versus 28% in *planeri*.
- Upper branchial region: well pigmented in 91.5% of larger *richardsoni* ammocoetes, versus fewer than 5% of *planeri*.
- Lower surface of the lower lip is pigmented in every *richardsoni* ammocoete but none of *planeri*.

**5. Sexual dimorphism.** Males have greater disc length, prebranchial length, tail length, and second dorsal fin height. Females have greater trunk length.

**6. Relationship to other species.** The dark caudal pigment makes *richardsoni* resemble *[[Lampetra Zanandreai]]* more than *planeri*. The authors say this similarity is superficial, because *zanandreai* has far fewer trunk myomeres and differs in other respects.

**7. Conclusion.** The authors conclude that the western North American nonparasitic brook lamprey is a distinct species, *Lampetra richardsoni*, and that earlier records of *L. planeri* from western North America refer to it. They also acknowledge that some characters need further study, including the infraoral cusp difference and the status of mandibular labials.
