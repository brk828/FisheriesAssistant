# Cenozoic Volcanic Geology of the Basin and Range Province in Hidalgo County, Southwestern New Mexico

#geology #volcanology #ash-flow-tuff

## Executive Summary

This preliminary field-guide paper (New Mexico Geological Society Guidebook, 29th Field Conference, 1978) describes the mid-Tertiary volcanic stratigraphy and structure of roughly 5,000 km² in [[Hidalgo County]]. The document contains no fish, invertebrate, plankton, water-chemistry or aquatic habitat data, so the aquatic-specific fields are recorded as N/A. Its content is entirely geological: a sequence of felsic [[Ash-Flow Tuff]] sheets erupted from multiple source calderas during the Oligocene and Miocene, with [[K-Ar Dating]] used to constrain ages. The authors note that stratigraphic correlations between ranges remain provisional.

## Background & Description

- **Study scope:** About 5,000 km² of southwestern New Mexico, in the Mexican Highlands section of the [[Basin and Range Province]].
- **Research history:** Project began in 1973 and was still in progress at publication. Funding came from NASA, the New Mexico Energy Resources Board, the U.S. Geological Survey, and NSF, among others.
- **Prior work:** Mapping by Zeller, Gillerman, Wrucke and Bromfield, Flege, and Strongin, who predated the recognition of ash-flow sheets as products of large central caldera complexes.
- **Limitations:** Information comes mostly from the ranges, with little from intervening basins. Only fragments of the proposed caldera complexes are exposed, and the Mexican border area is poorly known.
- **Basement:** Precambrian crystalline rocks and Paleozoic and Cretaceous sedimentary rocks. Late Cretaceous rocks lie directly on Precambrian rocks in the northern part of the county.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper is not ecological. Its environmental content is limited to geological settings that bear on the landscape history:

- **Laramide-age andesitic volcanism** in the [[Lordsburg]] area and [[Peloncillo Mountains]], and [[Timberlake Fanglomerate]] conglomerates with red siltstone and sandstone.
- **Shallow sedimentary basins** that formed near the end of mid-Tertiary volcanism, with basin-fill conglomerate interbedded with undated tuffs.
- **Valley-fill sediments:** Late Pleistocene to Holocene lake beds, stream deposits and alluvial fans in the Animas, San Luis, Playas, Lordsburg and Hachita valleys.
- **Basin and Range faulting:** Continues into the present, with Holocene scarps in valley fill in Animas Valley and San Simon Valley.
- **Mineralization:** Small mineral deposits are associated with source calderas. Copper skarn occurs at the [[Apache Hills]] stock contact, and the paper references the [[Lightning Dock Geothermal Area]] and fluorspar, uranium, manganese and travertine occurrences.

## Key Findings

### Regional volcanic framework
- Mid-Tertiary volcanism began with poorly dated andesite flows and breccias. These were followed, between about 37 and later than 24 m.y., by quartz latite to rhyolite ash-flow tuffs erupted from **at least nine** known or suspected central cauldrons.
- The general progression in rock type is crystal-poor quartz latite, then crystal-rich quartz latite, then crystal-poor quartz-sanidine rhyolite. Dark andesite, latite and basalt flows are interlayered locally.
- Cauldron sizes, internal structures and erosion depths vary. Some formed from single or closely spaced eruptions, others from many eruptions with sedimentary intervals. Several resurged.

### Cauldrons described
- **[[Muir Cauldron]] (Pyramid Mountains):** Shallow but complicated. Collapse occurred in two stages. The first was driven by eruption of about 425 m of remaining [[Tuff of Woodhaul Canyon]], which produced megabreccia with blocks over 50 m wide. Later collapse was linked to the [[Tuff of Graham Well]].
- **[[Tullous Cauldron]] (Animas Mountains):** Fed the [[Bluff Creek Formation]], with up to about 750 m of crystal-poor ash-flow tuff on the west flank and about 350 m on the east flank. It did not resurge.
- **[[Juniper Cauldron]] (Animas Mountains):** Resurgent. Filled by at least hundreds of meters of crystal-rich [[Oak Creek Tuff]]. The Animas stock and Walnut Wells monzonite porphyry intruded it.
- **Animas Peak Cauldron:** Only a fragment is exposed, filled by the crystal-rich, mafic tuff of Black Bill Canyon (about 350 m exposed).
- **[[Cowboy Rim Cauldron]] (Animas Mountains):** Over 800 m of [[Gillespie Tuff]] fills the basin, with the margin partly controlled by a major fault.
- **San Luis Cauldron (Sierra San Luis, Sonora/Chihuahua):** Tentative. Ring-fracture domes of the rhyolite of Packer's Trail are dated at 23.0 ± 0.5 m.y.
- **[[Geronimo Trail Cauldron]] (Peloncillo and Guadalupe mountains):** Filled by about 420 m of the tuff of Guadalupe Canyon, dated at 24.2 ± 0.5 m.y. It was gently domed during resurgence.
- **[[Rodeo Cauldron]] (South-Central Peloncillo Mountains):** Filled by about 1,000 m of the tuff of Black Mountain. Its minimum age is about 26 m.y., based on sanidine dates of 26.4 ± 0.7 m.y. (Marjaniemi, 1969, recalculated).
- **Apache Hills cauldron:** Resurgent, dated through the intrusive stock.

### Key stratigraphic units
- **[[Chapo Formation]] (Apache Hills):** About 1,600 m in four members (lower quartz latite 400 m, andesite 230 m, upper quartz latite 230 m, basalt and andesite 700 m). The basal lower quartz latite is dated at 30.6 ± 1.1 m.y.
- **[[Rimrock Mountain Group]] (Pyramid Mountains):** Eight ash-flow cooling units, three intercalated basaltic andesite units and sedimentary beds. Tuff 6 (34.2 ± 0.6 m.y.) forms prominent cliffs and is compared with the Gillespie Tuff (32.9 ± 0.7 m.y.).
- **Coyote Hills stratigraphy:** About 2,000 m of outflow sheets and intermediate lava flows, cut by faults trending mostly northwest to west-northwest.

### Geochronology (Table 1, K-Ar)
- Table 1 lists **22 K-Ar samples** analyzed by Damon and Shafiqullah at the University of Arizona. Ages range from about 0.5 Ma to 56.5 Ma.
- Key dates: 56.6 ± 1.2 m.y. (mineralized granodiorite porphyry, Lordsburg); 36.8 ± 0.8 m.y. (tuff near the Muir cauldron edge, the most reliable biotite date there); 35.2 ± 0.8 m.y. (Oak Creek Tuff); 34.8 ± 0.8 m.y. (Animas stock); 29.6 ± 0.6 m.y. (Park Tuff); 24.2 ± 0.5 m.y. (tuff of Guadalupe Canyon).
- Youngest dates: basalt at San Luis Pass, 6.8 ± 0.2 m.y.; Pleistocene basalt in Animas Valley, 0.5 ± 0.03 m.y. Basaltic andesite in Gila River valley fill is dated at 20.9 ± 1.5 m.y., the closest constraint on the onset of Basin and Range faulting.
- Analytical constants used include λβ = 4.963 × 10⁻¹⁰ yr⁻¹, λε = 0.581 × 10⁻¹⁰ yr⁻¹, λ = 5.544 × 10⁻¹⁰ yr⁻¹, and ⁴⁰K/K = 1.167 × 10⁻⁴ atom/atom.
- Several dates are flagged as unreliable. The 33 m.y. date for the andesite of Holtkamp Canyon seems spurious, and the 30.7 ± 0.7 m.y. date for Double Adobe Latite appears out of stratigraphic order.

### Structural conclusions
- Basin and Range border faults truncate all earlier structures, including the cauldrons. Trends are northerly in the western part of the county and northwesterly in the eastern and northern parts.
- Some Oligocene cauldron-collapse faults and Laramide faults were reactivated during Basin and Range activity.
- The arch between Granite Gap and Steins Pass may be the eroded resurgent dome of a cauldron. This is unconfirmed, because the Weatherby Canyon and Steins Mountain formations have not been correlated and no southern margin is known.

### Stated limitations
- Correlations between ranges are tentative and require more petrographic and chemical work.
- Several units are undated or have uncertain correlations, including the tuff of Dutchman Canyon and the moonstone-bearing tuff of Skeleton Canyon.
