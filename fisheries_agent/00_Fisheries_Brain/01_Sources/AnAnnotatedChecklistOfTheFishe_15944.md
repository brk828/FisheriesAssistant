# Annotated Check-List of the Fishes of the Iowa-Cedar River Drainage Basin, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This annotated checklist, compiled by [[Robert E Cleary]] and published in the *Iowa Academy of Science* (Vol. 60, 1953), documents the fish fauna of the [[Iowa River]] and [[Cedar River]] drainage basin in Iowa. Collections were made from 1948 to 1952 at 189 stations, supplemented by 22 lake collections from the [[Iowa Conservation Commission]]'s Lake Survey. The checklist includes 22 families and 104 species and subspecies in the main table, rising to 109 species and subspecies when five species found only in [[Clear Lake]] are included. Relative abundance is recorded for each river reach, and the note highlights taxonomic revisions, species introductions, and lake-specific records.

## Background & Description

The Iowa-Cedar River basin drains 11,615 square miles, roughly one-fifth of Iowa, and is the second largest inland drainage basin in the state. The [[Iowa River]] rises in Crystal Lake, Hancock County, and flows 329 miles southeast with a 685-foot total fall. Its gradient is gentle (1.5 feet per mile) at the headwaters, steepens to 7.5 feet per mile in Franklin and Hardin Counties, and then levels off below Iowa Falls. Its only important tributary is the English River.

The [[Cedar River]] rises near Hayfield, Minnesota, and flows about 300 miles to its junction with the Iowa, 28 miles above the [[Mississippi River]]. Its steepest section, from the Iowa-Minnesota border to Floyd, exceeds 5 feet per mile. Its main tributary is the [[Shellrock River]], which in turn has two major tributaries, the West Fork and the [[Winnebago River]].

About 70 percent of the basin lies in the eastern Iowa lobe of the [[Wisconsin Glaciation]]. The remainder lies mostly in the Kansan glacial area, with the lower Iowa River crossing the Illinoisan glacial area. Soils include Wisconsin drift in the upper Iowa basin, Mississippi loess in the middle and lower reaches, and Iowan drift along much of the Cedar.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling methods.** Most collections came from flowing waters, using short 14-inch drag seines. Larger seines of 4¼ to 1¼ inch mesh (up to 300 feet long), trap nets, and hoop nets were also used. Standard lake survey gear consisted of a 500-foot, ¾-inch mesh seine plus trap nets. Marginal ponds and small lakes were included to broaden habitat coverage.

**Lake populations.** Natural lakes sampled included [[Clear Lake]], Rice Lake, East and West Twin Lakes, and Crystal Lake. Except for Clear Lake, these are small, shallow, and prone to periodic winter kills. The most persistent populations there were black bullheads, fathead minnows, and common suckers. Recent stockings of [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Northern Pike]] had been made, and [[Carp]] were abundant in Crystal Lake.

Artificial impoundments (Beeds, Pine, Union Grove, Cherry, and Lake MacBride) held established populations of black and white crappies, bluegills, black bullheads, and largemouth bass. Common suckers and fathead minnows were the main forage fish. Species from the impounded streams also appeared in samples, but all were also found in the flowing waters of the basin.

**Historical and institutional sources.** The checklist incorporates earlier work by [[David S Jordan]] and [[Seth Eugene Meek]] (1885, 1892, 1893), [[Carl Hubbs]], J. Clark Salyer, and [[Reeve M Bailey]]. Collections are housed at the University of Michigan, [[Iowa State College]]'s Museum of Zoology, and several other colleges. Because some of Meek's early records were confused or could not be verified, certain species were listed only as literature citations.

## Key Findings

**Scope and effort**
- 254 collections were made at 189 stations between 1948 and 1952, plus 22 lake collections from the Lake Survey crew.
- The checklist comprises 22 families and 104 species and subspecies in the main table. Including the five Clear Lake-only species brings the total to 109 species and subspecies.
- Nomenclature and systematic order follow [[Reeve M Bailey]]'s 1951 Iowa checklist.

**Abundance categories** (the author's relative opinion, used only as an index for each collection):
- A = Abundant; C = Common; Oc = Occasional; R = Rare; VR = Very rare.
- Superscript notations mark literature-only records (Meek 1892 or 1893) and species verified from, but not listed in, Meek's collections.

**Clear Lake**
- [[Bailey and Harrison 1945]] listed 43 species from Clear Lake. Two more were added later, bringing the total to 45.
- The two additions were the northern hog sucker (*Hypentelium nigricans*) and the brook stickleback (*Culaea inconstans*, rendered "Eucalia" in the source). Both normally inhabit small creeks and are thought to have been introduced in an angler's minnow bucket.
- Five species were found only in Clear Lake in recent collections: [[Northern Muskellunge]] (*Esox masquinongy immaculatus*), spottail shiner (*Notropis hudsonius*), yellow bullhead (*Ameiurus natalis*), pumpkinseed (*Lepomis gibbosus*), and scaly Johnny darter (*Etheostoma nigrum eulepis*).
- The single muskellunge specimen was only the second known record of this subspecies in the state's inland waters.

**Taxonomic revisions**
- Some of Meek's records, especially in *Carpiodes*, *Moxostoma*, and black-striped *Notropis*, were confused. The author therefore omitted some doubtful species from the main table.
- Re-examination of Meek's specimens at the Chicago Natural History Museum by Dr. Carl Hubbs corrected several identifications. Specimens previously called *Notropis heterodon* were reassigned to *N. roseus richardsoni*, *N. chalybaeus*, and *N. heterolepis* from West Liberty, Dumont, and Cedar Rapids. These black-striped *Notropis* are included in the checklist.

**Notable taxa listed in the table**
- Lampreys, sturgeons, and paddlefish are present but rare or occasional, including the [[American Brook Lamprey]], [[Paddlefish]], and [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]].
- Salmonids (brown, rainbow, and [[Brook Trout]]) are listed as rare or occasional, consistent with the warm, low-gradient habitat of most of the basin.
- Centrarchids and percids are well represented, with [[Walleye]] and crappies among the more common game fish.
- Cyprinids, the most diverse family, include numerous *Notropis* and *Hybopsis* species, along with the Iowa darter and several other darters.
