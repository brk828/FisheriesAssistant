## Executive Summary

This FY93 annual project report documents ongoing efforts under the [[Colorado River Recovery Program]] (Project No. 17) to clarify the taxonomic status, distribution, and genetic/morphological variation of chubs within the **[[Gila Robusta Complex]]** across the Colorado River basin, including comparative sampling from five Mexican drainages. The project integrates non-lethal allozyme electrophoresis, morphometric (truss-based) image analysis, and DNA sequencing (mtDNA and RAPD) to resolve whether populations traditionally identified as **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta*) and **[[Humpback Chub]]** (*Gila cypha*) represent distinct evolutionary taxa, and whether introgressive hybridization is occurring in canyon-reach populations. Approximately 1,200 live chubs were sampled non-lethally across the basin, supplemented by ~460 museum specimens representing extant and extinct populations. While allozyme and morphological data collection are essentially complete and preliminary analyses are underway, the DNA component suffered a major setback following the death of the Brigham Young University principal investigator and subsequent contamination issues, delaying non-recombinant DNA analyses by an estimated two years. The project's overarching goal is to provide a scientific foundation for recovery planning, habitat management, and propagation decisions for endangered [[Gila (Genus)|Gila]] forms.

## Background & Description

- **Data acquisition**: Video-cam imagery of chub specimens digitized into a computer database at Arizona State University (ASU), covering ~1,200 newly sampled specimens plus ~460 supplementary museum specimens (extant and extinct populations); an additional ~50 museum specimens anticipated.
- **Method**: Truss network analysis using anatomical landmarks, supplemented by computer-calculated "helping landmarks" specifically designed to assess **nuchal hump development** — a key diagnostic trait distinguishing [[Humpback Chub]] from [[Roundtail Chub]].
- **Analytical approach**: Canonical discriminant and cluster analyses conducted at three levels of biological organization — population, "species," and genus.
- **Preliminary sample**: 363 specimens from several upper basin canyon habitats and the [[Grand Canyon]].
- **Key morphological findings**:
  - Significant intraspecific (population-level) shape variation exists among populations, but this variation is **not correlated with geographic proximity**, suggesting populations behave as largely independent evolutionary units.
  - In most sampled localities, roundtail-type and humpback-type chubs remain morphologically **distinguishable from one another** in both sympatry and allopatry.
  - **Exception**: At **[[Cataract Canyon]]** and **[[Desolation Canyon]]**, heterospecific (mixed-taxon) populations were morphologically more similar to *each other* than to allopatric "conspecifics" elsewhere — a "locality effect" potentially indicating introgressive hybridization or convergent local adaptation to canyon habitat.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: Mainstem Colorado River canyon reaches (e.g., Grand Canyon, Cataract Canyon, Desolation Canyon), tributary streams (Gila River system tributaries in Arizona), and both confluent and isolated tributary populations across the Colorado River drainage.
- **Distribution studied**: Nine populations across six drainages sampled in Mexico (Yaqui, Mayo, Fuerte, Sinaloa, Culiacan) plus lower Colorado Basin tributaries (Arizona, excluding White Mountain Apache lands, which were not accessible for permitting reasons).
- **Comparative taxa**: Rio Grande chub and Chihuahua chub obtained from New Mexico colleagues as outgroup/comparative genetic material for the broader *Gila* complex.
- **Population structure implications**: The lack of correlation between morphological variation and geographic proximity suggests fine-scale local evolutionary divergence rather than clinal variation, with canyon-reach environments potentially acting as selective pressures driving convergent morphology (nuchal hump) independent of taxonomic identity.
- **Conservation relevance**: Findings on hybridization/convergence at Cataract and Desolation canyons bear directly on habitat management and propagation decisions for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and are central to defining "discrete taxa" for Endangered Species Act recovery purposes.

## Key Findings

1. **Sampling completed**: All five known Mexican drainages inhabited by the *Gila robusta* complex sampled (~200 chubs, nine populations, six drainages); Arizona Gila River tributary sampling completed except Apache lands.
2. **Allozyme electrophoresis**: ~55 presumptive loci screened across ~1,200 sampled chubs (Genetics Analyses, Inc.), yielding ~66,000 individual runs. Preliminary results show **no fixed allelic differences** among upper basin *Gila* populations, though analysis is incomplete.
3. **Morphological database**: Essentially complete acquisition of video-imagery data for ~1,200 extant specimens plus museum specimens representing extinct populations.
4. **Taxonomic ambiguity in canyon reaches**: Roundtail and humpback chub morphotypes are generally distinguishable, but converge morphologically at Cataract and Desolation canyons — raising the possibility of ongoing introgressive hybridization or environmentally-driven convergence (nuchal hump development).
5. **DNA setback**: The mtDNA sequencing project at Brigham Young University collapsed following the PI's death (October 1992) and postdoc departure (February 1993); sequencing of 55 samples revealed **mammalian DNA contamination**, rendering that data largely unusable. RAPD-based total DNA analysis also showed insufficient repeatability for large-scale population extrapolation.
6. **Project transfer**: DNA work reassigned to Dr. Thomas Dowling at Arizona State University (beginning January 1994) due to his prior expertise with *Gila* mtDNA; DNA studies now treated as a separate scope-of-work component, delayed ~2 years.
7. **Personnel changes**: ASU morphological work led by Dr. Michael E. Douglas and postdoc Dr. Douglas McElroy (departed August 1993); succeeded by incoming postdoc Dr. John M. Lynch (January 1994).
8. **Reporting timeline**: Full electrophoretic/morphological report due October–November 1994; integrated report incorporating DNA studies due 1996.
9. **Budget**: FY93 funds provided and expended were balanced (~$272K expended, no reported surplus/deficit).
