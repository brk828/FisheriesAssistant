# Conservation of Fishes of the Death Valley System in California and Nevada

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This 1967 field survey, published in the [[California-Nevada Section Transactions]] (1969), assessed the current status of the native fishes of the [[Death Valley System]], a formerly interconnected Pleistocene drainage in eastern California and southwestern Nevada. Of nine species in three families treated, the [[Killifishes]] (Cyprinodontidae) receive the most attention, because five forms are threatened with extinction or have very restricted ranges. The author, [[Robert Rush Miller]], surveyed sites in June and July 1967, gave population counts for the [[Devils Hole Pupfish]] and the [[Pahrump Valley Poolfish]], and proposed a network of refugia for the most endangered forms. One refuge, the [[Owens Valley Native Fish Sanctuary]] at [[Fish Slough]], was scheduled for completion in summer 1969.

## Background & Description

The Death Valley System once united the isolated waters of [[Owens Valley]], Death Valley, the [[Amargosa River]] and the [[Mohave River]] basins. Many of its native fishes have declined or disappeared over the past two decades. Several taxa appear on the [[Redbook]] (U.S. Department of the Interior, 1967) and the IUCN Redbook (1969). Field work ran from June 26 to July 10, 1967. It was supported by the [[National Park Service]], the [[National Science Foundation]] (grant GW-4854), and conservation funds from the Zoological Society of San Diego via the American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists.

The study covered:

- **Cyprinodontidae (killifishes):** [[Cyprinodon Diabolis]], [[Cyprinodon Nevadensis]], [[Cyprinodon Salinus]], [[Cyprinodon Radiosus]], and [[Empetrichthys Latos]]. [[Empetrichthys Merriami]] was also discussed, but it had not been taken since 1948 and may be extinct.
- **Cyprinidae (minnows):** [[Gila Mohavensis]], an undescribed [[Gila]] from Owens Valley, and [[Rhinichthys Osculus]].
- **Catostomidae (suckers):** one undescribed sucker, [[Catostomus]] sp. nov., from the Owens River basin.

An undescribed *Cyprinodon* population from Cottonwood Marsh, described by LaBounty and Deacon, is noted but not named.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Threats.** The main drivers of decline were:

- Competition with and predation by introduced species, including [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*), [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Bullfrog]], [[Crayfish]], and [[Black Molly]].
- Hybridization with the introduced [[Coastal Californian Minnow]] (*Gila orcutti*), which contributed to the decline of the Mohave chub.
- Diversion of water, ditching of spring outflows, and marsh drainage.
- Physical risk to small, isolated habitats, including deliberate vandalism and flash floods.

**Habitat conditions.** The species occupy very specialised environments:

- *C. diabolis* lives in a single spring pool at [[Devils Hole]], Ash Meadows, Nevada.
- *C. salinus* occupies pools in Salt Creek, Death Valley, where air reached 105°F and water 92°F on June 29, 1967. Adults use cooler water in deeper channels.
- *C. radiosus* survives in a small marsh at Fish Slough, north of Bishop, California, and tolerates temperatures from near freezing to at least 95°F.
- Many Ash Meadows springs and the [[Lake Tuendae]] system at [[Zzyzx Resort]] provide refuges for the remaining populations.

**Refugium candidates** (from a review of [[Thompson, 1929]] and field checks) included:

- [[Lower Warm Spring]], [[Saline Valley]], which was too hot (about 110°F) for fish but could be cooled through ponds and channels.
- [[Resting Springs]], [[Inyo County]], with a steep descent to the ranch house that would allow barriers against upstream invaders.
- [[Bitter Spring]], San Bernardino County, which was destroyed by a flash flood on the survey date.
- [[Cushenbury Springs]] and [[Cottonwood Spring]], both in San Bernardino County. Cushenbury might suit a cold-water fish if its owner agreed. Cottonwood's aquifer and ownership were uncertain.
- [[Deep Springs Valley]], where no Salt Creek pupfish survived, carp were abundant, and cold-tolerant species such as *Rhinichthys* were thought more suitable.
- [[Sheep Creek Spring]], rejected as unsuitable because it lies in a narrow canyon prone to flash floods.

## Key Findings

**Population counts and status**

- ***Cyprinodon diabolis* (Devils Hole):** A minimum count of 464 individuals was made in 1967, including 31 specimens that were preserved and many young. This is higher than any previous estimate, which ranged from about 100 to 400. The population was judged healthy and may have reached 800. The 1966 estimate was 180 to 200 individuals. The main threat is deliberate or careless destruction, so the author recommended establishing the species elsewhere.
- ***Empetrichthys latos* (Pahrump Valley, Manse Ranch):** About 1,300 individuals were removed from the main spring pool on July 2 to 4, 1967, including roughly 300 young. Goldfish were mostly eliminated from the spring and ditches, and the native fish were returned. This is the last known population of the genus.
- ***Empetrichthys merriami*:** Only 31 specimens were collected from 1891 to 1942, and one was taken in 1948. Big Spring yielded 15 of these 31 specimens. Searches in 1967 found none. The species is probably extinct.
- ***Cyprinodon radiosus* (Owens Valley):** Thought extinct between 1939 and 1942, it was rediscovered in July 1964 at Fish Slough. The population is at most about 200 individuals.
- ***Cyprinodon salinus* (Salt Creek):** Pools were "teeming," with populations "existing by the millions," in the same order of magnitude as observed 29 years earlier. The population is in excellent condition.
- ***Cyprinodon nevadensis* (Amargosa):** Six subspecies are recognised. Two are extinct or nearly so (*calidae* at Tecopa and *shoshone* at Shoshone). A remnant of *shoshone* was found in April 1969 (one male, two females, one juvenile). *C. n. pectoralis* at Schoolhouse Spring needs protection. Each of ten Ash Meadows springs supports roughly 50 to 2,000 individuals.
- ***Gila mohavensis*:** The species persists in the Mohave River but is scarce there. It is abundant only at [[Zzyzx Resort]] spring-fed lake, which is in excellent condition but has an uncertain future.
- ***Rhinichthys osculus*:** No dace were found at Little Lake, but local differentiation within and between the Owens and Amargosa drainages was noted in preserved material.
- **Owens Valley chub and sucker:** Current status was not determined.

**Management recommendations**

The report made nine main recommendations:

1. Establish refugia as soon as possible for *C. radiosus*, *C. diabolis*, *C. n. shoshone*, *E. latos*, and *G. mohavensis*.
2. Transplant *C. diabolis* and possibly *E. latos* to warm springs at Saline Valley after suitable habitats are built. If both *Cyprinodon* species are moved, keep them in separate springs.
3. Rehabilitate the original habitat at Fish Slough for *C. radiosus*, remove exotics, and install barriers.
4. Reestablish the other three Owens Valley fishes in the rehabilitated Fish Slough habitat.
5. Examine Bitter Spring as a site for *G. mohavensis*, including whether pumped water could maintain a pond above flood level.
6. Give formal protection to Lake Tuendae at Zzyzx.
7. Withdraw Big Spring, Jack Rabbit Spring, and (if ownership permits) Schoolhouse Spring from public use, fence them, remove exotics, and install barriers.
8. Search further for remnant stocks of the Shoshone and Tecopa pupfish and act quickly to save them.
9. Determine ownership of Cottonwood Spring with a view to developing it as a refugium.

**Research and sampling guidelines**

- The author judged that the removal of 15 adult *C. diabolis* per month for one year would be unwise. Removing 15 adults and as many juveniles two or three times a year would not, in his view, be dangerous.
- Full population counts by removal should be made no more than once a year, and less often if the population appears impaired.
- Walking over the shallow shelf can damage eggs and young, so this should be minimised.
- *C. diabolis* appears unsuitable for artificial propagation, which makes wild refugia essential.

**Other points**

- The author suggested that a sign reading "TAKE NO FISH" be added at the end of the Salt Creek road, and that *C. salinus* be placed in the Visitor Center courtyard pool as a public display.
- Legal disputes over Zzyzx Resort, including a Bureau of Land Management eviction effort and a claim for $34,187 in damages and back rent, put the Mohave chub and Amargosa pupfish at risk. Formal protection was urged regardless of the outcome.
