# Spawning Ecology of Flannelmouth Sucker (*Catostomus lattipinnis*) in Two Small Tributaries of the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study reports the first published accounts of spawning behavior and spawning site selection of the [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] in two small tributaries of the lower [[Colorado River]] within [[Grand Canyon]], Arizona: the [[Paria River]] and [[Bright Angel Creek]]. Spawning was observed in the Paria River in 1992 and 1993 and in Bright Angel Creek in March 1993. The species showed promiscuous mating, selected substrates of 16 to 32 mm, and spawned in shallow, moderately flowing water. Spawning fish in the Paria River were markedly larger than in 1981, suggesting a shift in stock size structure. The authors argue that these tributary spawning runs are valuable baseline indicators for monitoring native fish responses to altered [[Glen Canyon Dam]] flow management, while raising concern that recruitment from the Paria River may be very low.

## Background & Description

- **Study species:** [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], an endemic catostomid of the Colorado River basin, still locally abundant in Grand Canyon but with a reportedly reduced historic range.
- **Regional context:** Native fishes of the Colorado River have declined substantially. Four of the original eight native species are considered extirpated from Grand Canyon, including [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Roundtail Chub]]. The [[Humpback Chub]] is federally listed as endangered. [[Bluehead Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]] remain widely distributed.
- **Drivers of decline:** Construction of Glen Canyon Dam (completed 1963) stabilized seasonal temperature, discharge and sediment load, and non-native fish introductions (at least a dozen species) further affected the native ichthyofauna.
- **Knowledge gap:** Prior to this work there were no detailed accounts of flannelmouth sucker using small Grand Canyon tributaries, and no published spawning behavior or habitat selection data from any location in the Grand Canyon.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Paria River:** Drains about 3650 km² in the Escalante Mountains and Paria Plateau of southern Utah and enters the Colorado River 25 km below Glen Canyon Dam. Speckled dace is the only perennial resident fish; flannelmouth and bluehead suckers coexist with introduced rainbow trout at the confluence. The river has broad daily temperature swings, frequent summer flash floods and very high summer temperatures (up to 34 °C), making it an unlikely rearing area.

**Bright Angel Creek:** Drains 262 km² on the north rim of Grand Canyon and enters the Colorado River 165 km below the dam. Low summer temperatures (< 23 °C) support resident introduced rainbow and brown trout. Its spring hydrograph rises during April and peaks about a month later than other Grand Canyon tributaries, a key contrast with the Paria River.

**Management relevance:** Agencies were weighing alternative dam flow-release schedules that could alter temperature and hydrologic regimes, with the goal of ameliorating post-dam habitat change and enhancing native fish. Baseline spawning data were therefore considered essential.

## Key Findings

**Spawning behavior**
- Spawning was observed in the Paria River on 20 March 1992 and from 28 March to 10 April 1993, and in Bright Angel Creek from 16 to 19 March 1993 (detailed observations on 18 March 1993).
- Mating was promiscuous: females were typically paired with two or more males, and partners changed between events. Multiple egg deposits from different females sometimes occurred at one site.
- In Bright Angel Creek, 34 spawning acts were observed; eggs were deposited at 12 sites averaging 0.51 m² each (total 6.16 m²). One site sustained 60% of the witnessed spawning events.
- Two males usually flanked one female in 59% of observed mating acts. Mating lasted 2 to 8 seconds. Nest excavation was not observed, and eggs settled into interstitial spaces in loosely compacted gravel.
- Site-guarding and territorial behavior were not observed, although males showed some site fidelity.
- Brown trout were observed preying on recently deposited eggs (over 50 eggs recovered from one trout's stomach).

**Spawning habitat**
- Substrate class 16–32 mm (pebble) was selected disproportionately in both streams, despite contrasting stream substrate matrices.
- Paria River: spawning depths 5–25 cm (mean 15.4 ± 4.8 cm, n = 50); mean column velocities 0.16–1.0 m/s; substrates 2–97 mm. Spawning depths were shallower than available stream-wide habitat (K-S p-values reported for depth, substrate and bottom velocity).
- Bright Angel Creek: spawning depths 19–41 cm (mean 28.2 ± 5.3 cm, n = 65); mean velocities 0.23–0.89 m/s; substrates 6–289 mm. Fish selected shallow depths and small-to-moderate substrates, but spawning areas did not differ significantly from stream-wide availability.
- Water temperature during spawning: 9–18 °C in the Paria River and 13–15 °C in Bright Angel Creek.
- Egg diameters: 3.4–3.8 mm (Paria River, mean 3.5 ± 0.11 mm, n = 50); 2.5–3.8 mm (Bright Angel Creek, mean 3.3 ± 0.28 mm, n = 25).

**Spawning distribution and timing**
- Spawning fish ascended at least 9.8 km up the Paria River and 1.25 km up Bright Angel Creek. In Bright Angel Creek, fish did not move beyond RK 1.2 despite no barrier below RK 9.5, and no habitat difference explained this.
- In the Paria River, no spawning occurred between the confluence and RK 2.7. Spawning was recorded at RK 2.8, 4.0, 6.0, 9.2 and 9.8.
- In 1993, males entered the Paria River after a flash flood on 13 February, females first appeared on 1 March, and spawning was first observed on 28 March at 9 °C. Ripe male proportions stayed between 80 and 100%, while ripe females rose from 12% (1 March) to 72% (8–10 April).
- A high-volume flash flood on 31 March 1992 was followed by no adult catches upstream of the confluence for the rest of that year.

**Size structure**
- Spawning fish in upstream Paria River areas ranged from 385 to 580 mm total length (mean 476 ± 37.5 mm, n = 275). These were significantly smaller than fish captured at the river mouth (mean 502 ± 41.1 mm, n = 785).
- Females (mean 511 ± 34.8 mm, n = 95) were significantly larger than males (mean 460 ± 24.4 mm, n = 172) in spawning areas.
- Spawning fish in 1993 averaged about 50 mm (reported as 53 mm in the abstract) larger than fish from the same reach in 1981 (mean 426 ± 33.4 mm, n = 286), indicating a shift in stock size structure.
- Twenty-one large fish (580–620 mm), mostly female and unripe, were taken at the confluence; several showed morphology suggesting flannelmouth × razorback sucker hybridization.
- In Bright Angel Creek, 11 ripe fish (370–569 mm) were captured from one spawning area (mean 499 ± 14.5 mm).

**Sex ratio**
- Spawning-site visual estimates in Bright Angel Creek were about 3 males : 1 female (range 1–4), confirmed by stripping gametes from captured fish.
- Paria River catches gave an overall spawning-season ratio of 2 : 1 (males : females), while year-round sampling at the confluence yielded a near-even ratio of 0.96 : 1 (n = 1063).

**Recruitment concerns and conservation implications**
- Larvae and embryos were scarce in the Paria River: 8 caught in 1992 and none in 1993 despite intensive sampling, versus 10–15 larvae collected by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] in 1991.
- Recruitment in the mainstream Colorado River and [[Little Colorado River]] appears successful, and larvae first appear at a backwater near RK 47.3.
- Juvenile catches in the mainstream have declined sharply since the pre- and immediate post-dam eras of the 1960s and 1970s.
- The authors suggest the Paria River stock may be aging, with low or no recent spawning success, and that tributary flow conditions and proximate cues (possibly combined with photoperiod) may drive spawning. Synchronous spawning across tributaries with dissimilar temperature and flow regimes suggests other cues also operate.
- The authors recommend that tributary spawning aggregations serve as sensitive, cost-efficient indicators and baseline data for monitoring the effects of altered dam operations.
