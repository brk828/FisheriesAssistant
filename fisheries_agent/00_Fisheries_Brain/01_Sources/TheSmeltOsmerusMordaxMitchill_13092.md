# THE SMELT, *Osmerus mordax* (Mitchill)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Osmeridae

## Executive Summary

This 1953 Michigan Department of Conservation pamphlet by Dr. John Van Oosten summarizes the biology, introduction history, ecology, and fishery management of the [[Rainbow Smelt]] (*Osmerus mordax*) in the [[Great Lakes]] and Michigan's inland lakes. The [[Rainbow Smelt]] is a non-native species first introduced into the St. Mary's River in 1906, with the successful establishment traced to a 1912 planting in [[Crystal Lake (Michigan)]] using eggs from [[Green Lake, Maine]]. The species subsequently spread throughout all Great Lakes except Lake Ontario (where it is native) and became both an ecologically significant forage fish and an economically important sport and commercial species. The document also details a catastrophic 1923 mass mortality event, spawning run phenology data from 1951–1952, and practical fishing regulations and food preparation methods.

## Background & Description

- **Body form:** Slender, silvery fish with a translucent greenish back; body shape resembles the [[Lake Herring]]
- **Scales:** Thin, moderately large, easily dislodged ("rubbed off")
- **Fins:** Deeply-forked caudal (tail) fin; small fatty [[Adipose Fin]] located behind the dorsal fin
- **Head/Mouth:** Relatively large mouth; head and jaws resemble those of [[Trout]]
- **Dentition:** Numerous sharp, strong canine teeth present on the jaws, tongue, and roof of the mouth
- **Taxonomic relationships:** Distant relative of [[Trout]], [[Lake Chub]], [[Lake Herring]], and [[Whitefish]]
- **Size at maturity:** First spawns at approximately 7 inches length, age 2
- **Dominant size class in spawning runs:** 7–9 inches, ~2 ounces, ages 2–3

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat & Depth Distribution
- Great Lakes proper: adults most abundant at depths of **60–120 feet**
- [[Green Bay]] (highest concentration in the species' range): most common at **15–50 feet** under ice
- Inland lakes (e.g., [[Crystal Lake (Michigan)]], [[Lake Charlevoix]]): concentrate near bottom at ~0 feet depth in winter; deeper in summer; commonly found between 15–60 feet
- Strong preference for **cold, clear water (59°F or less)**, which restricts summer distribution

### Spawning Ecology
- Spawning migration triggered by water temperature reaching approximately **40°F or higher**
- Pre-spawn behavior: inshore movement and formation of large, dense schools as ice breaks up
- Stream entry occurs heavily at **night**
- Run duration: typically ~2 weeks (range: 1 week to 1 month)
- 1951–1952 peak timing: April 18–May 6 (Upper Peninsula); April 2–May 8 (Lower Peninsula)
- Best dip-netting locations: streams draining into the northern third of [[Lake Michigan]] (including [[Green Bay]]), followed by the [[Saginaw Bay]] watershed
- Feeding largely ceases during spawning, especially in females

### Diet
- **Juveniles:** microscopic plants/animals (plankton), crustaceans, worms, insects
- **Adults (~5–6 inches+):** may prey on fish, but predominantly other [[Rainbow Smelt]], [[Lake Shiner|shiners]], or other non-game/non-commercial species
- Notably, investigators found **no significant predation** on young game or commercial fish species in smelt stomach contents, countering public perception

### Trophic Role
- Serves as a critical forage species for major Great Lakes predators:
  - [[Lake Trout]]
  - [[Walleye]]
  - [[Yellow Perch]]
  - [[Burbot]]
  - Other predaceous species

## Key Findings

1. **Introduction history:** Smelt are not native to the Great Lakes drainage; introduced in 1906 (St. Mary's River), with the only successful establishments at [[Crystal Lake (Michigan)]], [[Howe Lake]], and [[Trout Lake (Michigan)]]. The 1912 [[Crystal Lake (Michigan)]] planting (eggs from [[Green Lake, Maine]]) is believed to be the source of all subsequent Great Lakes populations (excluding native Lake Ontario stock).

2. **1923 Mass Mortality Event:** A major unexplained infectious disease outbreak caused massive smelt die-offs beginning in Lake Huron in fall 1922, spreading through Lakes Huron and Michigan and connected inland lakes, peaking in [[Green Bay]] in February–March 1923. Estimated losses: ~13 million lbs (1923) and ~50 million lbs cumulative (1923–1926).

3. **Population recovery:** Michigan commercial smelt production rebounded from ~200 lbs (1924) to 2,660,545 lbs (1951, second-highest on record) and an estimated 2,360,000+ lbs in 1952 (all-time record, per preliminary reporting). Minor post-spawning mortality events recurred in 1950 and 1952 due to physical bruising on crowded spawning grounds, but were much smaller in scale than 1923.

4. **Fishery methods:** Commercial harvest occurs primarily January–March under ice in [[Green Bay]] using small-mesh (1-1/4 to 1-3/4 inch) gill nets, fyke nets, and pound nets. Recreational harvest includes ice-fishing via shanties (hook and line) and spring dip-netting with hand nets during spawning runs (primarily April).

5. **Regulatory framework (Michigan, as of 1953):**
   - Hand nets (≤5 ft circumference) permitted March 1–May 31, in designated waters
   - Unlimited hooks per line permitted for smelt fishing
   - No size limit or creel limit
   - Open season year-round in waters open to fishing generally

6. **Culinary value:** Winter-caught and spawning-run smelt are considered prime table fish; summer-caught fish are typically too soft-bodied. Preparation methods documented include frying, broiling, baking (two methods), smoking, and pickling (herring-style brine/vinegar method).
