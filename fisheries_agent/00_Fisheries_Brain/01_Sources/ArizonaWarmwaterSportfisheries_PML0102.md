# Arizona Warmwater Sportfisheries Strategic Plan 1990–1995

#ichthyology #marine-biology #warmwater-sportfish

## Executive Summary

This strategic plan from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] sets out goals, objectives, and management strategies for warmwater sportfisheries for 1990–1995. It assesses current supply and demand, projects future trends, and proposes solutions to thirteen management problems. The central finding is that demand for warmwater angling (4.6 million user days in 1986) is projected to rise to 4.9 million by 1995, which is well below the estimated supply potential of about 27 million user days. Growth in demand is expected to outpace opportunity mainly because of limits on new water, competing uses, and declining natural reproduction on some major waters.

## Background & Description

The plan is prepared under Arizona Revised Statutes Title 17, which vests wildlife management in the Game and Fish Commission. Its stated mission is to conserve, enhance, and restore Arizona's wildlife resources and habitats, and to provide wildlife-associated and safe watercraft recreation. Funding comes from angling and hunting license revenue and from the federal [[Dingell-Johnson Act]] (Federal Aid in Sport Fish Restoration), which is supported by an excise tax on fishing equipment.

The plan was developed by a warmwater strategic planning team of fisheries managers, specialists, statisticians, and planners. Input came from public meetings in several Arizona communities, questionnaires, and the 1986 statewide angler survey.

Historically, Arizona's warmwater fishes were largely introduced from elsewhere. Early anglers and miners relied on native species such as the Colorado River squawfish, flannelmouth sucker, roundtail chub, and Yaqui catfish. Regulations developed over time:

- **1887:** Use of explosives and other methods to take fish was made unlawful.
- **1893:** Laws limited take to hook and line or spear.
- **1903:** The spear was removed as a legal method, and the first minimum length limits were set (six inches for black bass, strawberry bass, and crappie).
- **1905:** The first closed season (September 1 to December 1) and first possession limits (20 pounds or 40 fish) were introduced.

According to the plan, all later regulations are variations of these Territorial laws.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Arizona's warmwater fish populations depend on natural reproduction. Stocking is useful mainly for establishing new populations, not for sustaining existing fisheries. Warmwater fishing opportunity therefore depends on available habitat and angler access.

Warmwater fish occur in a community. Predator and prey relationships and competition for space and food mean that management of one sport species affects others. This diversity of species and interactions is what makes warmwater fishing so varied.

Water storage in Arizona is usually driven by irrigation, hydroelectric power, flood control, and municipal needs. Warmwater sportfishing is typically secondary to these uses. Key environmental pressures identified include:

- Declining natural productivity in [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Mohave]], which is reducing fish populations.
- Anticipated changes in operation of [[Lake Pleasant]] under the [[Central Arizona Project]].
- Water level fluctuations in rivers and reservoirs that disrupt fishing and fish population stability.
- Low fertility in some lakes that limits sportfish populations.
- Pollutants and toxic substances that pose risks to people who eat fish.

## Key Findings

**Supply**
- About 354,800 acres of impounded water (lakes, reservoirs, ponds, and tanks) and 35,840 acres of flowing water (about 1,400 linear miles) are managed for warmwater species.
- Twelve water types are defined by habitat, management goals, and techniques (see [[Large Inland Reservoirs]] and [[Colorado River Reservoirs]] as examples).
- Potential supply is estimated at a capacity of 0.4 to 100 angler days per acre, based on 2 to 6 times 1986 use.
- Only a few managed waters are owned or controlled by the Department or the State.

**Demand**
- In 1986, more than 346,000 resident and 43,000 nonresident anglers were licensed.
- Licensed residents spent more than 6.6 million user days on all waters, including 4.6 million on warmwater fisheries. Nonresidents (mainly from California, Nevada, and Utah) spent an additional 461,000 user days.
- About 130,000 unlicensed juveniles are not included in these figures.
- Nearly half of resident warmwater days were spent on large inland reservoirs close to urban centers. About three quarters of nonresident warmwater days were spent on Colorado River reservoirs.
- Trout was the most preferred fish (41.4% of residents and 44.3% of nonresidents). [[Largemouth Bass]] ranked second (35.9% of residents and 29.0% of nonresidents).
- About 23 warmwater species are sought by anglers.

**Projections**
- Warmwater demand is projected to reach 4.9 million user days by 1995, with no growth anticipated for nonresidents.
- The current rate is about 12 angler days per acre per year statewide.
- Boating capacity is being exceeded on some inland reservoirs, and access to Colorado River reservoirs is becoming a serious problem.
- Future use is expected to be constrained because new recreational lakes may never be built, given water demand and Arizona water law. Improved access to existing waters is seen as more feasible, funded partly by the [[Wallop-Breaux Act]].

**Species Management**
- Overutilized species: [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Channel Catfish]], and [[Crappie]]. Numbers may decline with increasing pressure and lower natural production.
- Underutilized species: [[Bluegill]], [[Walleye]], [[Yellow Perch]], yellow bass, [[Carp]], and [[Tilapia]]. Efforts should direct new pressure toward these species.

**Goal and Objectives**
- Goal: maintain or enhance the availability and diversity of warmwater sportfishes and their habitat, including distribution and abundance.
- Objective 1: provide fishing opportunity for 4.9 million angler use days by 1995.
- Objective 2: achieve a mean statewide angler success rate of 2.0 warmwater fish per day.

**Management Problems and Strategies (13 total)**
1. Overutilized species: review size and bag limits and closed seasons; encourage anglers to "limit their catch, not catch their limit."
2. Underutilized species: evaluate two-pole regulations and publish educational material.
3. Limited access and facilities: improve roads, trails, piers, and boat ramps.
4. Lack of habitat structure: develop habitat improvement plans and compare natural and artificial habitat.
5. Intense user competition: work with agencies on water-based recreation management and evaluate the effect of tournament angling.
6. Low fertility: conduct and evaluate [[Fertilization]] projects.
7. Water level fluctuation: work with regulating agencies and the Legislature on instream flow rights, and develop populations less affected by fluctuations.
8. Fishing law violations: expand enforcement and education.
9. Limited species diversity: evaluate introducing or reintroducing species, including two-story fisheries.
10. Low public awareness: increase information and education about programs and opportunities.
11. Inadequate data on periodically [[Stocking]]-supported species (such as channel catfish and walleye): examine stocking rates, creel returns, and growth.
12. Inadequate harvest data: develop statewide surveys and databases.
13. Toxic substances: assess water quality, determine toxin levels in fish, and work to reduce toxic discharges.

**Historical and National Context**
- The 1986 statewide angler survey and national data show Arizona anglers and days of participation increased between 1980 and 1985. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service national survey reported 501,600 anglers in 1980 and 638,000 in 1985 (total, residents and nonresidents).
