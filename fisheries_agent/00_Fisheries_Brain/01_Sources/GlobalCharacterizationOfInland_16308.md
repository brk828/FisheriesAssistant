# Global Characterization of Inland Fishery Enhancements and Associated Environmental Impacts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #inland-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This FAO Fisheries Circular (No. 945, 1999, 89 pp.) characterizes inland fishery enhancements worldwide. It is based on an automated search of the [[Aquatic Sciences and Fisheries Abstracts]] (ASFA) for 1978–1997, cross-checked against the [[FAO Database on Introductions of Aquatic Species]] (DIAS) and the [[FAO Hatchery Production Database]]. Results were stored in an Access-based [[Inland Fishery Enhancements Database]] (IFED). The study finds that [[Stocking]] and [[Introductions]] are the most common enhancement techniques. Environmental engineering and fertilization are rarely documented. Pre-intervention environmental assessments are very scarce, while post-intervention studies are numerous, particularly on predation by introduced species. Section 5, written by an environmental consultant, reviews impacts, prevention and mitigation measures, and the status of environmental studies.

## Background & Description

Inland capture fisheries that depend on natural production are often fished at or above sustainable levels, and their yields have plateaued. [[Fishery Enhancement]] techniques are used to raise yields where conventional management (mesh-size rules, closed areas and seasons) is hard to enforce or insufficient. [[Welcomme and Bartley Classification]] (1998) lists nine enhancement types: introductions, stocking, fertilization, environmental engineering, elimination of unwanted species, artificial fauna, modification of water bodies, cage culture with intensified capture fishing, and aquaculture as intensive ponds. A tenth, genetic modification, is covered in Section 5.

Key contextual points from the introduction:
- Chinese reservoir yields rose from 180 kg/ha (1957) to 650 kg/ha (1996).
- Finnish lake fisheries depend largely on regular stocking of [[Brown Trout]].
- The 1995 [[Kyoto Declaration]] and the 1997 [[FAO/DFID Expert Consultation on Inland Fishery Enhancement]] (Dhaka) emphasized know-how transfer, community management and integrating technical, socio-economic and cultural factors.

Methods: the ASFA search used WINSPIRS software with search profiles for Africa, Asia, Latin America, Oceania (first) and North America and Europe (later). The search periods were 1978–1996 for stocking, 1978–1996 for introductions, and 1978–1997 for engineering and fertilization. About 3,523 references were downloaded and 496 (14.1%) were judged relevant. For North America and Europe, 1,864 and 1,089 references were retrieved, respectively, using key-term selection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Section 5 reviews impacts of each enhancement type, with prevention (P), mitigation (M) and rehabilitation (R) measures:

- **[[Predation]] and [[Competition]]:** Predators have direct effects (consuming adults, fry and eggs) and indirect effects (altered distribution and behavior). In several cases emigration of prey fish reduced their numbers as much as direct predation. Competition arises from diet overlap, since introduced fishes are generalists. Introductions into niches already filled by native species should be avoided.
- **[[Disease Transmission]]:** Introduced species may carry new parasites or act as reservoirs for endemic diseases. Mitigation includes health certification, quarantine and diagnostics.
- **[[Habitat Alteration]]:** Examples include feeding on submerged macrophytes, salmonid redd digging, and carp-driven turbidity. Pikeperch introduction in Lake Gjersjøen reduced pelagic refuge for juvenile roach.
- **[[Genetic Changes]]:** Hybridization, introgression and outbreeding depression are possible. Translocations of native-like stocks can be more damaging than introductions of alien genera. Stocks adapted to local conditions and using sterile or non-breeding stock are recommended.
- **[[Overstocking]]:** Surpassing carrying capacity reduces growth and increases mortality. A study in Thai village fisheries found about 50% of village fisheries overstocked.
- **Fertilization:** Nutrient loading beyond carrying capacity causes eutrophication, and unknown waste sources add health and disease risks.
- **Cage culture and aquaculture:** Feed-related eutrophication, disease, and escapes are the main concerns.
- **Genetic modification:** Impacts resemble those of introductions and stocking, possibly intensified by altered phenotype. Containment is the key control.

Environmental studies status: most literature comes from the USA, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and Northern and Western Europe. Developing-country information comes mainly from development-aid projects. Pre-intervention assessments are few (examples in Table 23 include the Mozambique Chidenguele lakes, New Zealand's channel catfish proposal, Papua New Guinea's Sepik-Ramu project, and northeast Thailand). Tools include pilot studies, theoretical models (e.g. [[Ecopath II]]), and checklists/protocols such as [[Kohler and Stanley Protocol]] (1984) and [[Bain Checklist]] (1993).

Constraints: lack of pre- and post-intervention data, difficulty separating enhancement effects from external drivers (e.g. Nile perch versus eutrophication and overfishing in [[Lake Victoria]]), and uneven detectability of impacts (predation is easy to see, genetic effects are not). Spatial replication across many small water bodies is proposed as a practical design. Institutional feedback effects, such as community management following tilapia stocking in Laos, also complicate assessment.

## Key Findings

**Overall characterization**
- The ASFA-based method was effective for classifying enhancement types, water body types and countries but less accurate for enhancement purposes and for success/failure.
- Stocking and introductions dominate. Environmental engineering (fish attractors, sanctuaries, spawning habitats, fencing, floodplain-river reconnection, fishways) is used with considerable success but is poorly evaluated globally, mostly in Asia. Fertilization is very scarce, especially for large water bodies.
- Introductions and stocking are mainly for food and income. Recreation is secondary.

**Regional patterns**
- **Asia and Oceania:** Introductions were listed as 185 (Asia/USSR) and 160 (Oceania) by Welcomme (1988). About 46% established. Mozambique tilapia, common carp, rainbow trout and Chinese carps are the key introduced species. China has introduced about 50 species and produced 10,000 t of icefish in 1997 (8,000 t from transplants). Stocking is most abundant in India, Sri Lanka, China and Australia. Silver and bighead carp make up 60–80% of stocked species in China. Bangladesh oxbow stocking raised yields from 121 to 520 kg/ha (1991–92 to 1995–96).
- **Africa:** Introductions outweigh stocking. Nile perch (Lake Victoria, 1954) created a major commercial fishery and is linked to the decline of haplochromines. The Tanganyika sardine (Limnothrissa miodon) succeeded in Lake Kariba and Lake Kivu. Of 41 species introduced to South Africa, 21 established. Stocking is least common in Africa (11 ASFA references).
- **Latin America:** Tilapias and salmonids created significant fisheries. Of 42 species introduced to Colombia, 31 established. In northeast Brazil reservoirs, tilapia yields rose from 45 to 120 kg/ha. Cuba produced 180 million tilapia and 79 million carp seeds in 1994.
- **North America:** The largest ASFA set (697 introduction references). Major concerns are invasions by zebra mussel, ruffe and sea lamprey in the Great Lakes. Stocking efforts include lake trout restoration (157.8 million stocked, 1950–1987, Great Lakes), walleye rehabilitation in Saginaw Bay and Lake Michigan, and a tenfold increase in angler effort in Lake Michigan (1963–1985).
- **Europe:** Stocking (400 references) and introductions (280 references) were the main searches. The former USSR had 72 introduced species, of which 29 established. In Finland, brown trout stocking underpins lake yields. Sweden's crayfish invasion displaced native noble crayfish.

**Validation and method**
- About 15% of downloaded references were relevant (Africa, Asia, Latin America, Oceania).
- In a 70-reference North American sub-sample, country was identified correctly in 100% of cases, water body in 85%, enhancement type in 84%, and purpose in only 30%.

**Environmental impacts**
- Pre-intervention environmental assessments are scarce, but over 200 ASFA references address introductions, at least 90 of them on predation.
- Recommended measures include spatial replication, genetic stock identification before introduction, watershed studies, screening of inlets and outlets, scatter or trickle stocking, and environmentally sound hatchery and cage management.

**Conclusions and recommendations (FAO)**
- Extend the IFED to Europe and North America through abstract-level classification.
- Search additional sources (grey literature, project documents, consultancies, national agencies).
- Expand analysis to environmental engineering and other enhancement types, including cage culture.
- Improve FAO hatchery statistics for reliable production trends.
- Conduct detailed benefit and success/failure studies, since abstracts alone are insufficient.
- Review environmental impacts of enhancement types beyond introductions, stocking and genetic modification.
