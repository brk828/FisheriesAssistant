# Removal of Radio-Isotopes During Purification of Domestic Water Supplies: Radio-Iodine (I-131)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #water-treatment

## Executive Summary

This laboratory-scale study examines whether [[Radio-Iodine]] (I-131), a fission product and medical isotope, is removed from hard water by conventional drinking-water treatment. Normal coagulation with alum or ferric salts, rapid sand filtration and softening removed very little activity. Adding about 0.085 p.p.m. silver nitrate before alum coagulation removed more than 90 per cent of the activity when the floc was settled. Slow sand filtration did not retain the isotope: effluent activity rose to about half the influent level and kept leaching for days or weeks after contaminated feed stopped. The authors conclude that radio-iodine can pass through standard treatment and that silver-assisted coagulation is a possible remedy, though it was not tested at full scale.

## Background & Description

- **Origin of the contaminant:** Nuclear reactors ("piles") produce I-131 by irradiating natural tellurium (Te-130 to Te-131, then decay to I-131). The isotope has a half-life of 8.0 days and decays by beta and gamma emission to stable xenon. It is produced at Harwell and supplied as an aqueous iodide solution.
- **Scale of use:** The authors cite about 60,000 millicuries despatched to six large US cities between 1 January and 30 June 1951, equivalent to over 60 g of pure radium in disintegration rate.
- **Sources to water:** Fission products from atomic bomb explosions include radio-iodine (radioactive iodide was reported in New England snow). Medical use, with doses of many millicuries for thyroid treatment, leads to excretion and discharge to sewers, where a [[Sewage Treatment]] study (Belcher, 1951) found only about 1 per cent removal in primary sedimentation, about 5 per cent in percolating filters and 11 to 14 per cent in activated sludge.
- **Guideline:** The provisional [[International Commission On Radiological Protection]] (1951) drinking-water limit for I-131 is 3 x 10⁻⁵ µc per cm³, stated as 136 millicuries per million gallons.
- **Iodine in natural waters:** Total iodine in 81 English and Welsh drinking-water samples ranged from 1.0 to 52.2 µg/l, and in 61 Scottish samples from 0.7 to 5.2 µg/l (Murray et al., 1948). Iodine is assumed to occur mainly as iodide.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Chemical behaviour:** Radio-iodine acts as a tracer for stable iodide, so its fate follows the stable form. Exchange with elementary iodine, adsorbed iodide and suspended organisms can alter this. Iodide-iodate exchange in alkaline solution is very slow.
- **Biological role in filters:** Slow sand filters depend on a [[Schmutzdecke]] layer of micro-organisms and algae that oxidise organic matter. Algal photosynthesis supplies oxygen, so filter performance may depend on light.
- **Test water:** Experiments used a "synthetic tap water" of calcium bicarbonate (initial alkalinity 2 to 200 p.p.m. CaCO₃, adjusted to pH 8) and unfiltered Surbiton reservoir water.
- **Concentration used:** About 2 µc/l of I-131, equal to roughly 2 x 10⁻⁵ µg I/l, with a carrier of about 10⁻² µg from Harwell tellurium.

## Key Findings

**1. Coagulation alone is ineffective.** No significant I-131 removal occurred with alum, ferric sulphate or alum plus activated silica across alkalinities of 2 to 200 p.p.m. CaCO₃. Hydrogen peroxide and nitrite had no effect. Hypochlorite produced a slight, unexplained loss.

**2. Silver greatly improves removal.** With 10 µg/l iodide present, 90 to 95.5 per cent of the initial activity was removed when 85 µg/l Ag was added before coagulation (Table II). Additions above 0.085 p.p.m. Ag gave little further gain. Theory predicted 99 per cent removal of iodide as silver iodide, and the shortfall is attributed to incomplete floc removal, fine precipitate solubility and mixed silver halide crystals.

**3. Continuous pilot plant results.** In the coagulation–sedimentation–rapid sand filtration rig (alum 20 p.p.m. Al, filtration 120–131 gal/ft²/h), the silver-dosed line removed 96.6 per cent of activity on average, against 10.3 per cent in the control. Beaker tests gave 93.7 per cent removal with silver after 4 hours, against 8.2 per cent without.

**4. Backwashing clears the filter.** Activity was removed from the filter by backwashing, and later clean water showed little contamination. Control-filter removal may have come from iodine exchange with algae, which are removed during treatment.

**5. Softening does not remove I-131.** Neither lime-soda softening nor base exchange with siliceous or carbonaceous zeolites removed significant activity.

**6. Slow sand filters retain and then release I-131.** With 6 hours of application, effluent activity reached only about 20 per cent of the influent level, yet discharge continued for at least 14 days after clean water resumed. With 48-hour application, similar slow leaching was seen (Fig. 10). Over prolonged exposure, the proportion removed was never below 25 per cent (Fig. 11). The authors suggest that this may reflect equilibrium with stable iodine in the water, but that hypothesis was not tested.

**7. Operational conclusions.** Under the conditions tested, conventional treatment and slow sand filtration are unlikely to remove radio-iodine. Silver addition before alum coagulation is promising for large-scale use but needs confirmation. A contaminated slow sand filter could keep discharging activity for weeks.

**8. Limitations.** The work was laboratory- and pilot-scale, and the study did not examine reservoir storage. Silver's cost, toxicity and effect on water quality were not evaluated. Chemical analysis of effluent throughout the experiments showed no radiation effect on filter performance.
