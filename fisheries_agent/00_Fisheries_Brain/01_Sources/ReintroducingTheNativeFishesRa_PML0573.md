# Razorback Sucker Reintroduction and Upper Colorado Basin Fish Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This compiled document contains four items on endangered fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]:

1. A 1981 U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service paper by James E. Johnson on the reintroduction of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) into historic habitat in Arizona's [[Gila River Basin]], undertaken "in lieu of listing" under the [[Endangered Species Act]].
2. A 1981 fishery survey of the Yampa and Green rivers in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] by McNatt and Skates.
3. A review by William E. Loudermilk on razorback sucker life history and causes of decline.
4. A short proceedings and news update covering razorback research at Senator Wash Reservoir and a channel catfish introduction issue.

Together they show that razorback sucker is in serious decline, that its natural reproduction is restricted to a few sites, and that hatchery-based reintroduction was adopted as a recovery strategy despite incomplete knowledge of its habitat and early life history.

## Background & Description

**Razorback sucker** is a large catostomid endemic to the Colorado River system. Adults reach 5–6 kg (Loudermilk). In 1981 the Lake Mohave broodstock averaged 57.7 cm and 2.6 kg (Johnson). Maturity begins at about 5–7 years of age, and spawning occurs at 12–18 °C from January to April, at depths of 0.5–3.0 m (Loudermilk). Adults feed on algae, dipteran larvae, and zooplankton, and have long digestive tracts, no pyloric caeca, and poorly differentiated stomachs.

**Fishery survey species:** McNatt and Skates report native species including [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and razorback sucker, along with flannelmouth and bluehead suckers, speckled dace (*Rhinichthys osculus*), and mottled sculpin (*Cottus bairdi*). Introduced species, including common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) and channel catfish (*Ictalurus punctatus*), were widespread.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic range and decline.** Razorback suckers historically occupied the Colorado, Gila, Salt, Verde, and San Pedro rivers and Tonto Creek (Minckley, cited by Johnson). In the Gila basin they were generally found below about 4,000 ft elevation and disappeared by the mid-1950s. Loudermilk attributes decline to habitat alteration, including seven dams built on the lower Colorado from 1909–1938, channelization, reduced peak flows, silted and compacted reservoir substrates, and blocked migration routes to tributaries. He emphasizes that predation matters now, but that egg and larval mortality may be of equal or greater importance.

**Remaining populations.** Loudermilk lists only four groups known to reproduce naturally: the Yampa River, the upper Colorado River near Clifton and Grand Junction, Lake Mohave (Arizona/Nevada), and Senator Wash Reservoir (California). Johnson notes that upper-basin status information was lacking, though more than two years of field work by the Colorado River Fish Project produced fewer than 40 specimens, all old fish.

**Habitat potential.** Johnson estimates about 60 miles of stream above Horseshoe Reservoir (Verde River), 40 miles above Lake Roosevelt (Salt River), and at least 20 miles above San Carlos Reservoir (Gila River) could support razorback suckers, though the reasons for their disappearance there remain unknown.

**Hybridization.** Razorback suckers hybridize with flannelmouth, white, and bluehead suckers. Almost half of razorbacks sampled below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] from 1957–1973 were hybrids (Vanicek; Holden and Stalnaker, cited by Loudermilk). Loudermilk suggests hybrid introgression may contribute to the gradual decline.

**Yampa and Green rivers.** The Yampa is the only major Green River tributary whose historic flows remain unaltered by dams. Proposed projects, including the Juniper-Cross Mountain reservoirs and the Cheyenne Water Project, threaten its wild state. The Yampa drains a steep-walled canyon in the lower 72 km of its course and joins the Green at river km 552.

## Key Findings

### Reintroduction program (Johnson)
- Over **15,000** young razorback suckers were released in the Gila basin in 1981.
- Under a [[Memorandum Of Understanding]] with the [[Arizona Game And Fish Department]], the Service agreed to produce up to **100,000** razorbacks per year for a 10-year period. In exchange, the state agreed to stock historic habitats and monitor results. The MOU prevents listing while the program appears successful.
- Hatchery spawning was chosen over stocking adults. On January 15, 1981, **136** adults were collected from Lake Mohave and moved to [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]. Many were in poor health, and **about 20,000** young were produced for the 1981 year class.
- The **"Johnny Appleseed" approach** (stocking and letting fish find suitable habitat) was chosen as the cheapest test of whether razorbacks can survive present conditions in historic streams.
- Stocking sites were tributaries, chosen to mimic natural nursery habitat and avoid large predatory fish. Sites included [[Eagle Creek]], [[Cherry Creek]], [[West Clear Creek]], and [[Oak Creek]], plus Gila River, Salt River, and Verde River reaches.
- **Stocking totals in 1981:**

| Stocking event | Fish stocked |
|---|---|
| June 30–July 1 (5 sites) | 7,000 |
| September 9–10 (5 sites) | 8,100 |
| **Total** | **15,100** |

- The June fish averaged about 9.1 cm and 6 g. Predation by green sunfish (*Lepomis cyanellus*) on young was observed almost immediately.
- The planned early-1982 stocking into Lake Mohave (OCR reads "41000," probably about 4,000) is intended to maintain a genetic stock for future recovery, because the lake population consists mainly of large, old, and sometimes diseased fish.
- **Monitoring:** The program will be evaluated annually for 15 years. Fish were marked with tetracycline; Lake Mohave fish had a pelvic fin clipped and cold-branded. Future fish will receive coded wire tags. As of the report, only **one** of the 15,100 fish had been recaptured: a 17.1 cm individual in an irrigation ditch near Salome, Arizona, on November 11, 1981.
- Johnson describes the program as a model: reintroduction "need not bring an end to sport fishing."
- Related efforts include an agreement to reintroduce the [[Gila Topminnow]] and the stocking of about **41,500** young bonytail into Lake Mohave on October 19 and November 3, 1981. A New Mexico MOU (October 20, 1981) covers stocking in the San Juan River. Utah and Colorado declined similar agreements.

### Yampa and Green rivers survey (McNatt and Skates)
- **20** fish species were recorded in Dinosaur National Monument, 17 in both rivers. Twelve introduced species were collected.
- Eight native species were present in both rivers, including razorback sucker, Colorado squawfish, and humpback chub.
- **17** Colorado squawfish were captured in standardized sampling. Only five came from the lower Yampa canyon. An additional **32** were tagged during spawning studies in early July 1981.
- Razorback suckers were rare: **four** were taken in the Yampa and **one** in the Green.
- Five humpback chubs were collected.
- Only **five** *Gila cypha* were found, all in deep pools or eddies near boulders.
- *Gila elegans* was not found in 1981 and appears extirpated from this reach. It was reported as abundant in 1964–66.
- Results are preliminary. The final report was due January 1982.

### Life history and recovery (Loudermilk)
- Fecundity was estimated at **27,400–744,000 eggs** per female, based on a small sample with a poor relationship between egg count and body length (the OCR text is partly garbled here).
- Eggs are 1.8–3.2 mm in diameter and adhesive. Survival is poor at about 12 °C; at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], 14–16 °C incubation gave low survival.
- In an aquarium trial at Senator Wash Reservoir, **100** eggs incubated at 20–23.5 °C produced **467** survivors (as printed).
- Larvae spent 2–3 days in the upper third of the water column after swim-up, then moved to the bottom.
- Sub-adult thermal preference was **25.4 °C** (Bulkley et al.).
- Loudermilk argues recovery must define and provide egg and larval requirements. Reintroduction without these will not restore natural recruitment. He recommends an electrophoretic comparison of Yampa, Lake Mohave, and Senator Wash stocks before choosing broodstock, because of hybridization.
- He also warns that MOUs assuming reintroduction gives adequate protection may weaken habitat safeguards under related federal laws. The lower Colorado River is suggested as the most feasible mainstream recovery site, because it is warm enough for egg incubation.

### Senator Wash Reservoir update
- Radio-tagged razorbacks were tracked for over 14 months across two spawning seasons; three spawning areas were located.
- Channel catfish and bluegill were confirmed to prey on recently spawned eggs.
- Periodic water-level fluctuation exposed spawning gravels during the spawning season.
- A USFWS-funded study is mapping gravel and rock bars in the lower river. The recovery plan is to be updated and the species re-proposed for federal listing.
- A channel catfish introduction into the Amargosa River drainage via a rearing pond in Shoshone, California, was flagged for evaluation, since high summer temperatures could be lethal to the catfish.
