# Gila Trout Recovery and the Cattle Grazing Conflict in the Gila Wilderness

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This magazine feature examines the recovery of the [[Gila Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus gilae*), a federally endangered salmonid of the high, dry [[Gila River]] drainage in southern New Mexico and Arizona. The article argues that the species' survival is undermined by livestock grazing on National Forest and Wilderness lands, and that political pressure and weak enforcement by the [[U.S. Forest Service]] have slowed recovery. It profiles the key actors: the [[Gila Trout Recovery Program]] leader [[David Propst]], the environmental group [[Gila Watch]] and its director [[Susan Shock]], and the rancher [[Kit Laney]]. It also describes the [[Wise-Use Movement]] opposition in [[Catron County]].

## Background & Description

The Gila trout is described as a striking native salmonid, with iridescent gold flanks, spotted head and back, and parallel yellow slashes under the lower jaw. Its coloration is distinct from both cutthroat and rainbow trout. The author calls it a "desert beast" able to withstand water temperatures of about 80°F, and says it grows faster and larger than the introduced salmonids that replaced it in native streams.

The species was first described in the scientific literature about 45 years before the article, when it was already in decline. It was listed as endangered in 1966 in the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] "Red Book," and it remains on the endangered list. The article notes that the species has few champions because no one can legally fish for it.

The [[Gila Trout Recovery Program]] is a cooperative effort among the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, the U.S. Forest Service and the [[New Mexico Department of Game and Fish]]. The article states that the program is in its twenty-fifth year. The [[Recovery Team]] is a three-person group that can only make recommendations.

Historically, Gila trout were called "speckled trout." An ancient Game and Fish document states that at [[Willow Creek]] they were so abundant that anglers could catch them at about one per minute, with a typical weight of one-half to one pound.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Grazing impacts on streams.** The article's central thesis is that livestock grazing is the main threat to Gila trout habitat. Cattle trample stream banks, compact soils and churn up flood plains. Where vegetation that once slowed runoff has been grazed away, flash floods erode channels and carry silt into streams. The author describes dry, blown-out stream beds, cracked and compacted stock tank banks, and algae-covered pools fouled by cow manure.

**Fire and flood events.** In August 1988, heavy runoff loaded with silt from a cow-trampled flood plain wiped out 90 percent of the Gila trout population in McKnight Creek. A year later, the perennial section of Main Diamond Creek was damaged by fire and flood. The article states that the most stable population, perhaps as many as 9,000 fish, was lost in these events. The recovery team evacuated 556 fish to two federal hatcheries, but about 300 of them were killed by herons and by a snake that became stuck in an intake pipe.

**Hybridization and non-native fish.** Non-native salmonids have contaminated the native range of the Gila trout. Rainbow-Gila hybrids dominate some streams, such as Black Canyon Creek, where pure rainbows are also present. Managers use [[Antimycin]], a toxicant that is safer than rotenone, to remove alien trout and restore native genetics.

**Riparian and wilderness protection.** The [[1964 Wilderness Act]] forbids logging and development in federal wilderness, but the article argues that the Forest Service has allowed grazing to expand. Trespass cattle and new stock tanks have been placed in wilderness springs, seeps and wet meadows that feed Gila trout habitat. The article also notes that the critical habitat of two other threatened fish that share the Gila trout's range, the [[Spikedace]] and the [[Loach Minnow]], was designated in this period.

**Other species.** The article mentions desert suckers, speckled dace, western bluebirds and Steller's jays in the Black Canyon area, along with wildlife such as herons and snakes.

## Key Findings

**Grazing on the Diamond Bar Allotment**

- A four-year range study completed in 1983 found that the Diamond Bar Allotment should drop from 1,188 head of cattle to 833.
- The Federal Intermediate Credit Bank of Austin, Texas, which controlled the permit after foreclosing on the previous owner, claimed a cut would reduce the property's value by $500,000.
- The Forest Service reversed its decision and allowed 1,188 head, provided 15 stock tanks were built in wilderness springs, seeps and meadows. The author says this violated the 1964 Wilderness Act.
- In 1985, after [[Kit Laney]] bought the ranch and permit from the bank, he asked to build more than 15 stock tanks. The Forest Service then approved a plan for 33 tanks.
- Following a review process forced by Gila Watch, the Forest Service's draft preferred alternative scaled the permit back to 600–800 head and 20 wilderness stock tanks. The Forest Service admits grazing on the allotment exceeds sustainable levels.
- Gila Watch cites the Forest Service's own studies, which support about 300 cows, against the 1,000 head allowed on the allotment.

**Recovery Status and Setbacks**

- In 1987, the recovery team was about to propose downlisting the species from "endangered" to "threatened." Downlisting would have allowed legal angling and public participation.
- After the 1988 and 1989 disasters, Propst wrote in the June 1992 *Southwestern Naturalist* that the situation had shifted from proposed downlisting to a "distinct possibility of extinction."
- Shock states that there are about one-tenth as many Gila trout alive today as there were in 1986.
- In 1994, restoration of Mineral Creek and Willow Creek was delayed by the Forest Service and Game and Fish after opposition from Catron County groups. Four of the five original Gila trout strains have been replicated, and Mineral Creek was to provide a safe haven for the fifth.
- The Gila Fish and Gun Club argues that "fish hatcheries are a better alternative" to restoring wild, self-sustaining populations.

**Restoration Timeline**

- Propst estimates that if cattle were removed from Black Canyon Creek, modest habitat recovery would take about five years. After that point, the team would consider poisoning the hybrids and reintroducing pure Gila trout.

**Legal Actions**

- Gila Watch sued the Forest Service twice in the past year: once for allowing grazing expansion in the wilderness in violation of the 1964 Wilderness Act, and once for allowing trespass cattle to graze six miles of Main Diamond Creek. The creek was acquired in June, and it is legally off-limits to grazing.
- Gila Watch plans to sue again if the species is downlisted to "threatened."

**Opposition and Politics**

- Catron County's [[Wise-Use Movement]] groups have opposed restoration and the Endangered Species Act. They have also challenged federal ordinances, including claims that the federal government controls more land than county residents.
- The article describes the Wise-Use opposition as having "loud, obnoxious" tactics, but argues that its threats are exaggerated and that its members depend on federal grazing subsidies.

**Conservation Actions Recommended by the Article**

- Write to District Ranger Sue Kozacek (Wilderness District, Gila National Forest) to stop trespass grazing, remove trespass cattle and reduce stock numbers on the Diamond Bar Allotment.
- Write to Forest Service Chief Jack Ward Thomas to adjust cattle numbers on public land to meet management goals.
