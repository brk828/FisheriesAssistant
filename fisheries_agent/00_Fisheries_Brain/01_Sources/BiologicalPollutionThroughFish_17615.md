# Biological Pollution Through Fish Introductions

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This note covers the front matter of *Biological Pollution: The Control and Impact of Invasive Exotic Species*, a 1993 proceedings volume edited by Bill N. McKnight and published by the [[Indiana Academy of Science]]. The symposium was held in Indianapolis on October 25–26, 1991, and drew about 300 people from 35 states and abroad. The volume's central chapter, by Walter R. Courtenay Jr., reviews how fishes were moved beyond their native ranges, why they were moved, and what the consequences have been, with a focus on North America. Courtenay argues that every introduction is a potential "biological time bomb," that most introductions were made for food, sport, bait, aquaculture, biological control or the aquarium trade, and that the outcome of introductions has been poorly studied and poorly regulated.

## Background & Description

**Volume context.** The proceedings grew out of a symposium that marked the 100th anniversary of the Biological Survey Committee of the [[Indiana Academy of Science]], which was originally named the "Committee for the Restriction of Weeds." The conference also coincided with the approaching quincentennial of Columbus's voyage. The editor framed the meeting around four goals: improving communication among state, federal and international agencies; establishing a federal framework for regulation; emphasizing education; and securing funds to address invasives. The volume lists 15 featured papers, covering plants, insects, earthworms, birds, mammals, aquatic invertebrates and fishes, plus legal and policy topics. The table of contents includes the [[Zebra Mussel]] and [[Spiny Water Flea]] in the Great Lakes, the [[Fire Ant]], the [[Asian Tiger Mosquito]], [[Garlic Mustard]], [[Purple Loosestrife]] and [[Dogwood Anthracnose]].

**Chapter scope.** Courtenay's chapter is organized by historical period of introduction, then by the pathway of introduction: food, sport, biological control, the aquarium trade, aquaculture, species preservation, canals, and ship ballast. It closes with a discussion of impacts and management.

**Key terminology.** The chapter uses "invasive" and "exotic" for species established outside their native range, and "biological pollution" for introductions viewed as potentially permanent environmental contamination. It also distinguishes:
- **Intercontinental** introductions (across continents)
- **Intracontinental** or **translocations** (within a continent or nation)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historical periods.** Courtenay describes three major waves of international fish transfers:
1. **Medieval period:** Common carp were moved into central and western Europe for pond culture. Goldfish were introduced to Europe as ornamentals. Escapes from culture were common.
2. **Colonial and trade period:** This began mid-19th century (possibly late 17th century) and ended with World War II. "Acclimatization societies" promoted introductions. The motives were largely sentimental and sporting, and native biota was often considered "expendable."
3. **Post-war period:** Beginning in the 1950s, jet cargo transport and the aquarium and hobby industry expanded rapidly. Aquaculture interest grew again, and intercontinental transfers have continued.

**Scale of transfers.**
- Welcomme (1981) listed about 170 species transferred internationally.
- Welcomme (1988) cited 237 inland species introduced beyond historical ranges into 140 countries, based on 1,354 known introductions.
- Courtenay and Taylor (1984) counted 168 fish species transferred beyond their native ranges within the contiguous United States. Courtenay (1991) reported 158 species moved and another 70 or more introduced.
- Most introductions internationally were for aquaculture, then sport, then improvement of wild stocks, accidental introduction and biological control. Common carp, Mozambique tilapia and rainbow trout were the most widely introduced.

**Pathways of introduction.** Courtenay groups introductions by pathway:
- **Food and aquaculture:** Common carp and brown trout were early examples. The first common carp introduction into North America was made in New York in 1831. The U.S. Fish Commission imported 338 carp from Germany in 1873. Blue tilapia were promoted in Mexico.
- **Sport and bait:** 53 species were moved as game fishes and 58 as live bait out of an estimated 168 intracontinental fishes established in the contiguous 48 states. Canada had 48 introduction records, of which 12 were intercontinental.
- **Biological control:** Mosquitofishes ([[Gambusia Affinis]]) were introduced to many countries to control mosquitoes, with little success and serious ecological harm. [[Grass Carp]] were introduced to control aquatic vegetation.
- **Aquarium trade and hobbyists:** About 65% of roughly 70 foreign fishes established in U.S. waters (including Hawaii) were known or believed to have escaped from aquarium culture facilities or been released by hobbyists.
- **Species preservation:** Introductions for conservation were the only category where Courtenay saw consistent concern about spread beyond historic ranges.
- **Canals and aqueducts:** The [[Welland Canal]] (1829) allowed the [[Sea Lamprey]] to reach the upper Great Lakes, where it was first recorded in Lake Erie in 1922. Ashworth (1986) suggested that the third construction phase, which opened direct upstream flow, enabled the invasion.
- **Ship ballast:** The yellowfin goby and chameleon goby reached San Francisco Bay this way. The [[Round Goby]] and ruffe were also linked to ballast water. About one third of ballast-mediated transfers in the Baltic Sea originated in North America.

**Notable cases.**
- **Grass carp:** Introduced to at least 47 nations. In the U.S., it has reproduced in the lower Mississippi and Atchafalaya systems since about 1975. Young grass carp made up as much as 20% of total fish biomass on intake screens of some Louisiana power plants. Triploid techniques were developed later to reduce reproduction risk.
- **Pacific salmonids and Great Lakes:** Introduced salmon controlled alewives but accumulated PCBs and displaced the forage base, and [[Lake Trout]] were later found to carry higher organic toxin levels. In 1988, about 2.5 million lake trout were destroyed in hatcheries to control epizootic epitheliotropic virus disease (EEVD).
- **Tilapias:** Widely spread in Mexico by government agencies, sometimes into waters with endemic, endangered species. Blackchin tilapia reached a Florida commercial fishery, possibly through deliberate release.
- **Asian tapeworm chain:** A parasite from grass carp cultured in Arkansas reached endangered [[Woundfin]] in the Virgin River through red shiner baitfish releases.

## Key Findings

- **Introductions are rarely evaluated.** Courtenay argues that the claim of "relatively little environmental damage" is invalid because many introductions are discovered long after they occur, in waters that were poorly surveyed beforehand. Impacts are often inferred rather than measured.
- **Impacts are documented.** The chapter cites numerous studies showing negative effects of introduced fishes on native species, including hybridization, predation and competition.
- **Biological control with fishes is unreliable.** Insects are often oligophagous or monophagous, but fishes are generalists that shift diets over their life histories. Mosquitofish and grass carp show that fish control of pests can backfire.
- **Aquaculture is a major future risk.** Courtenay considers aquaculture the greatest potential source of future invasive fishes. He recommends diking facilities, sand and gravel filtration of effluents, and sterilization or triploidy. He notes that triploidy does not guarantee sterility but makes successful reproduction very unlikely.
- **Sport fishery management drives many introductions.** Managers often stock reservoirs and tailwaters with nonnative species to address problems caused by dams and altered flows, usually aiming for short-term goals.
- **Funding is skewed.** Courtenay reports that "untold billions" are spent on introductions while few funds examine impacts. Federal funds exceeding 100 million dollars go to states each year on a 3:1 matching basis for sport fishing, while only a small fraction supports endangered fish.
- **Management needs ecological training.** Courtenay argues that fishery managers need better training in ecology before they influence introductions, and that agencies need policy frameworks at international levels such as FAO and USAID.
- **Recommendations.** He calls for testing expected impacts before introductions, stricter regulation, education for aquaculturists, aquarium owners and hobbyists, and ballast water controls.
- **Conclusion.** He asserts that "not all fish introductions are inherently bad," but that every introduction will affect native biota, and that each should be treated as a potential "biological time bomb."
