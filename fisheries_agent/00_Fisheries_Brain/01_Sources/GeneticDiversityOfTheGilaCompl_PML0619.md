# Genetic Diversity of the Gila Complex as Determined by Mitochondrial DNA Analysis

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final report to the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (Contract # 4-FC-40-15700, Arizona State University) examines mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variation in the [[Gila Complex]] of North American minnows (genus *Gila*, family Cyprinidae). The three mainstem Colorado River chubs, [[Humpback Chub]] (*G. cypha*), [[Bonytail]] (*G. elegans*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*G. robusta*), are endangered or candidates for listing, and morphological evidence of hybridization has raised concern that their distinctiveness is being lost. The study combined [[Restriction Endonuclease Analysis]] and direct sequencing of cytochrome b (cytb) and ND4L genes to build a phylogeny of the genus, and used RE and single-stranded conformational polymorphism (SSCP) surveys to quantify introgression in the upper Colorado River basin. Results show that the three taxa have diagnostically distinct mtDNA, that introgression has been extensive and long-standing, and that management should treat the complex as an interacting whole.

## Background & Description

The genus *Gila* was estimated to contain roughly 30 species across western North America, several of which are extinct, endangered or threatened. Its taxonomy has been repeatedly revised, and earlier morphological and allozyme work has suggested that many derived characters are shared with other western genera, making the genus possibly polyphyletic. Mitochondrial markers were chosen because they can be assayed relatively cheaply across large numbers of individuals.

The project had three subprojects (allozymes, morphology and mtDNA) with three objectives: (1) identify mtDNA characters diagnostic for each taxon; (2) quantify the extent of mtDNA hybridization and introgression among members of the complex; and (3) assess the evolutionary (historical) significance of hybridization.

Methods included:
- Density-equilibrium ultracentrifugation or CTAB/phenol-chloroform DNA extraction.
- Digestion with 14 restriction endonucleases, with cleavage maps built from double digests.
- PCR amplification and dideoxy sequencing of cytb (about 1140 bp) and ND4L (about 338 bp), using *Siphateles bicolor* as outgroup.
- Parsimony, neighbor-joining and bootstrap analyses in PAUP* 4.0.
- Survey of upper basin populations by RE digestion (AccI, HinfI, ScrFI) and SSCP of cytb and ND2 fragments, with sequencing of variants, and population-genetic analysis (haplotype and nucleotide diversity, AMOVA) in ARLEQUIN.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Colorado River chubs are large river forms adapted to swift canyon reaches (*G. cypha*, 200–300 mm, with a dorsal hump and concave head) and backwater and eddy habitats (*G. elegans*, up to 520 mm). *G. elegans* was once common in the Green, Colorado and Gila rivers but is now rare in nature, with scattered captures in Lake Mohave and the upper basin. Most *G. elegans* stock descends from a very small number of hatchery founders at Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center (DNFH).

The region's geological and climatic history is considered in the discussion, including isolation of populations such as Pahranagat Valley (about 14,000 years) and upper Little Colorado River (about 250,000 years). Relevant past and present drivers discussed include dams, hatchery stocking and the decline of native fish, with human impacts described as recent in comparison with the long history of hybridization.

## Key Findings

**Chapter I: Phylogenetic analysis**
- Restriction site analysis (14 enzymes) recognized 100 sites. Within-sample variation was limited (e.g., 1–3 polymorphic sites). Sequence divergence within taxa was typically under 0.4%.
- *G. robusta* showed three distinct haplotype groups: the Yaqui River form (later recognized as *G. minacae*, >6% divergence), lower Colorado River populations (sharing haplotypes with *G. intermedia*), and upper basin populations (identical to *G. cypha*, with >4% divergence from lower basin fish).
- *G. intermedia* was indistinguishable from *G. robusta* from the same drainage, which may reflect hybridization.
- Combined cytb and ND4L analysis gave 580-step trees (CI = 0.51, RI = 0.77). *G. atraria* was paraphyletic, and *G. cypha* was nested within the *G. atraria* clade, consistent with introgression from *G. atraria* into *G. cypha* early in the Pleistocene.
- Pairings with bootstrap support of 100% include *G. elegans*–*G. seminuda* and *G. robusta*–*G. intermedia*.
- The findings conflict with allozyme and morphological topologies, which is interpreted as evidence of mtDNA introgression among lineages.

**Chapter II: Introgression in the upper Colorado River basin**
- Lower basin "allopatric" samples showed limited admixture. Of 21 *G. cypha* from the Little Colorado River confluence, 18 had typical *G. cypha* RE patterns and three had *G. elegans* patterns.
- Of 231 individuals from middle and upper basin populations analysed by RE, 34 had *G. elegans* patterns (mostly *G. seminuda* from the Virgin River, all 32 specimens) and 197 had *G. cypha* haplotypes. No *G. robusta* mtDNA was found.
- Including SSCP, 312 specimens were examined in total, and 51 had *G. elegans* mtDNA.
- Across 327 individuals from 18 middle and upper basin localities, only *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* haplotypes were found. Local *G. elegans* frequency was about 10% (Westwater Canyon, 10.8%).
- Gene flow among upper basin samples appears substantial, with most variation within populations.

**Chapter III: Population structure of *G. cypha* and *G. elegans***
- *G. cypha* (n = 177, 12 localities) yielded 17 composite ND2 haplotypes. Nine were private alleles, all of which were tip haplotypes.
- AMOVA for *G. cypha* gave F_ST = 0.29 among samples, with 71% of variation within populations. Among-group differences were significant, with F_CT = 0.18.
- Green and upper Colorado groups showed F_ST = 0.09, with 91% of variation within populations.
- *G. elegans* (n = 46) yielded 11 haplotypes, with nucleotide diversity up to 0.005. A distinctive lineage was restricted to the Virgin River and Lake Mohave.
- Pahranagat Valley and Chevelon Creek populations were monomorphic.

**Management and evolutionary conclusions**
- The three taxa are diagnosable by mtDNA and deserve specific status.
- Introgression has been extensive and is not explained by recent human activity alone. Complete replacement of *G. robusta* mtDNA in the upper basin would require far longer than humans have occupied the region.
- Hybridization may increase evolutionary flexibility by creating new genotypes for local selection.
- Management recommendations include:
  - Listing the entire complex as endangered, with protection extended to hybrids.
  - Avoiding large-scale stocking of hatchery *G. elegans*, given that only three or four founders contributed to the DNFH stock, which is monomorphic for haplotype AA.
  - Incorporating wild *G. elegans* from Lake Mohave and the upper basin into the broodstock to restore genetic diversity.
  - Prioritizing in situ management of riverine populations.
