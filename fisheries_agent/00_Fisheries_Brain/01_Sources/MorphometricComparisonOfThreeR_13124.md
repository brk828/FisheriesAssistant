# Morphometric Comparison of Three Races of Kokanee (Oncorhynchus nerka) Within a Large British Columbia Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1957 study by E. H. Vernon, published in the *Journal of the Fisheries Research Board of Canada*, examines whether non-anadromous kokanee spawning in [[Kootenay Lake]] form a single homogeneous population or discrete races. Mature fish were sampled from seven tributary streams in 1951 and from selected streams through 1954. Kokanee grouped into three areas (South End, North End, West Arm) that differed consistently in size, age at maturity, vertebral count and other traits, and these differences remained stable across four years. Straying between home areas was low (2.8% of 1,131 fish). Rearing experiments showed that parental differences in vertebral count were transmitted to offspring. The author concludes that natal-stream homing is the principal isolating mechanism producing three essentially discrete races with some genotypic divergence.

## Background & Description

Kokanee are non-anadromous freshwater populations of [[Sockeye Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*). They are much smaller than sockeye and have restricted migration, and they occur in isolated lakes from Oregon to northwestern Alaska, eastern Siberia and the Kamchatka Peninsula. Prior work by [[Ricker]] (1938, 1940) on [[Cultus Lake]] residual sockeye suggested kokanee arose separately in many lakes from residual off-shoots of sea-going sockeye. [[Foerster]] (1947) showed that lake-locked stock released as smolts could migrate to sea and return as mature sockeye.

Kootenay Lake was chosen because it supports large numbers of kokanee spawning in several widely separated streams and because natural falls on the [[Kootenay River]] isolate it from anadromous stocks. The lake is a deep glacial trench, 65 miles (104 km) long and 154 square miles (399 sq. km) in area, and is considered oligotrophic. For analysis it was divided into three arms radiating from Balfour: the North End (influenced by the [[Lardeau River]] inflow), the South End (influenced by the Kootenay River inflow) and the West Arm.

Methods included dip-net collection of mature fish, photographic records and caliper measurements, counts of vertebrae, gill rakers, pyloric caeca and scale rows, scale-based age determination, and regression analyses of head length, peduncle length and eye diameter on fork length. Progeny from parents of the three races were reared in a uniform hatchery environment to six months after hatching.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The author describes physical features that likely produce different environments among areas:

- **North End:** Cold Lardeau River inflow sinks beneath the surface and flows south along the western margin, adding heat to deeper strata.
- **South End:** Warmer, more mineral-rich Kootenay River inflow spreads over the surface, with higher production of bottom organisms reported (Larkin, 1950).
- **West Arm:** Narrow, with a perceptible current draining the whole lake inflow and a shallow sill at its entrance that draws surface water and plankton from the main lake.

Kokanee are largely plankton feeders. Growth differences in the first year are attributed to local limnological conditions, while mixing of older fish across the lake likely reduces racial growth differences. Schooling behaviour of young fish is suggested as a further mechanism maintaining separation, though kokanee schooling had not been studied directly. Environmental effects on meristic counts are discussed with reference to temperature studies in herring, salmonids and other fishes ([[Tester]], [[Taning]], [[Lindsey]]).

## Key Findings

**Racial groups within the lake**
- Kokanee formed three groups, summarized as:
  - South End: about 18.5 cm and 55 g, maturing at 3 years.
  - North End: about 21.5 cm and 105 g, maturing at 4 years.
  - West Arm: about 24.5 cm and 155 g, maturing at 3 years.
- Within each area, fish were similar in growth rate, vertebral count and secondary sex characters.

**Differences between areas (1951, representative streams)**
- Mean fork length differed significantly between areas (P<.01): South End 18.78 cm, North End 21.73 cm, West Arm 24.66 cm (Cultus, Lardeau and Kokanee Creeks; 26 of each sex).
- Mean vertebral count: North End 65.50, West Arm 64.90, South End 64.15 (chi-square = 36.013, 4 d.f., P<.01).
- Gill raker count was significantly lower in the South End (mean 33.85) than in the North End (35.10) and West Arm (35.50).
- West Arm scales had a first-annulus radius about 82% larger than North End and 88% larger than South End scales (P<.01).
- Eye diameter was largest in the West Arm, and West Arm males had the longest heads.
- South End females produced significantly more ova per gram (3.44) than North End (2.33) or West Arm (2.06) females.
- Spawning began 10 to 15 days earlier in the South End than in the North End, with the West Arm intermediate.

**Stability over time (1951–1954)**
- Differences between areas in size and vertebral count persisted in every year sampled, with the order West Arm > North End > South End for size and North End > West Arm > South End for vertebrae.
- Age-class dominance was stable: West Arm and South End fish were mainly 3-year-olds (87–97%), and North End fish were almost entirely 4-year-olds.

**Straying**
- Of 1,131 kokanee sampled over three years, 32 (2.8%; 21 males, 11 females) were identified as strays.
- Fifteen were South End fish spawning in the West Arm, and one was a West Arm fish spawning in the South End. No strays were found among 391 North End fish.
- The largest group, 16 fish (9%) in Goat Creek in 1951, was identified as North End strays into the South End.
- Morphological variation between streams within an area was too slight to evaluate straying within areas.

**Rearing experiment**
- Parents from three races were artificially spawned and reared in identical hatchery conditions.
- Mean vertebral count in both parents and progeny followed North End > West Arm > South End, so the parental difference was transmitted. The gap was reduced in progeny (maximum 1.19 vertebrae versus 2.10 in parents).
- South End progeny were significantly smaller at two weeks (2.14 cm vs 2.24 and 2.26 cm) and at six months (3.66 cm vs 4.11 and 4.13 cm).
- The strong first-year growth difference seen in wild West Arm fish was not reproduced in hatchery progeny, which the author attributes to a possible confounding response to the hatchery environment.

**Conclusions**
- Natal-stream homing is the principal isolating mechanism producing three essentially discrete kokanee races in Kootenay Lake, leading to some genotypic divergence despite an average interbreeding rate of about 3%.
- Many racial traits are probably phenotypic, but vertebral count differences appear partly heritable.
- Similar genetic divergence is considered likely among sockeye races, and management should proceed on that assumption.
