# Lake Lawtonka Fishery Survey by Population Estimation Techniques

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Sciaenidae

## Executive Summary

This Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory report (Alfred Houser, 1959 survey; supporting agencies the Oklahoma Wildlife Conservation Department and the University of Oklahoma Biological Survey) assesses the [[Lake Lawtonka]] fishery. A mark-and-recapture survey in June-July 1959 estimated a standing crop of 639 fish and 218 pounds per surface acre. Age and growth histories compiled from 1951-1959 collections show that overabundant [[Freshwater Drum]] are the principal cause of poor angling returns, with the drum standing crop at 159 pounds per acre. The report recommends a rotenone treatment, discontinuation of game fish stocking, a creel census, and experimental stocking of [[Threadfin Shad]].

Note: the document is a scanned technical report; OCR errors were corrected silently. Its Abstract refers to a fish-standing-crop figure of 159 pounds per acre for drum, which matches the drum estimate in the results.

## Background & Description

Lake Lawtonka is a municipal reservoir impounded in 1905 as a water supply for the City of Lawton and [[Fort Sill Military Reservation]]. The dam has been raised four times; the maximum size is 2,398 surface acres. The lake was drained in 1937 and reimpounded in 1939, then stocked with game and panfish. Angling returns reached their lowest point around 1950.

Management history includes:
- Commercial removal of rough fish after a 1950 survey.
- Two [[Rotenone]] treatments (September 1953 and October 1954) targeting [[Gizzard Shad]] and freshwater drum. The biologist estimated a 70 percent reduction in gizzard shad.
- Recommendations after 1954 (more brush shelters, commercial rough-fish removal, a creel census, facility improvements, and ending annual stocking). Most were followed, but active management eventually ended.

Survey methods (1952-1959) included rotenone, seines, gill nets, wire traps, trap nets and a trawl. Ages and growth were read from scales and spine sections using the direct proportion method. Population estimates used 15 small trap nets set around the shoreline, with marked fish released at a central point. Estimators were [[Schnabel Method]], [[Schumacher and Eschmeyer Method]] and [[Latta Method]] (selectivity correction).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Species richness:** Collections since 1951 yielded 37 species and one hybrid (carp × goldfish). Most are common to all Oklahoma reservoirs. Minnow diversity is probably underestimated because intensive complete collections were never made.
- **Water level:** Low lake levels in 1953-1954 reduced the reservoir to about 1,000 acres. Drought persisted for several years. Rainfall in 1957 raised lake levels considerably.
- **Community imbalance:** The report attributes the drum overpopulation to drought-era conditions in which drum reproduction and survival were better than in other species. Drum filled the niche vacated by the rotenone removals.
- **Habitat constraints:** Submerged small trees and weeds around the shoreline prevented seining, and suitable cleared seining areas could not be found.
- **Forage:** Gizzard shad were scarce in 1959. Predatory fish were in poor condition until a June spawn of young-of-the-year shad restored forage.

## Key Findings

**Standing crop (June-July 1959)**
- Total estimate: 1,277,173 fish weighing 436,060 pounds (639 fish, 218 pounds per acre, exclusive of young-of-the-year).
- 18,315 fish (1.43 percent of the population) were marked; 617 were recaptured.
- [[White Crappie]]: 92 fish and 17.6 pounds per acre (based on 7,624 marked fish and 144 recaptures). This is high compared with 14 pounds per acre from 19 rotenone samples in other State reservoirs. The 1957 year class was the most numerous, with fish averaging 7.2 inches.
- [[Channel Catfish]]: 19.4 fish and 10.9 pounds per acre (3,965 marked, 405 recaptured). This is low compared with 28 pounds per acre from 16 reservoirs.
- [[White Bass]]: 1.6 fish and 2.9 pounds per acre. This is low compared with 4 pounds per acre elsewhere.
- [[Flathead Catfish]]: under 1 fish and about 3 pounds per acre (none of 29 marked fish recaptured). Other Oklahoma reservoirs average 6 pounds per acre.
- **Freshwater drum:** 477.7 fish and 159.1 pounds per acre (955,400 fish; 3,717 marked, 9 recaptured). Rotenone estimates elsewhere ranged up to 95 pounds per acre (average 32). This is identified as the most significant factor in the poor fishery.
- [[River Carpsucker]]: 5.2 fish and 12 pounds per acre. [[Carp]]: 16.5 fish and 10.4 pounds per acre. Both are secondary concerns.
- [[Largemouth Bass]]: only 3 captured. The report presumes an adequate population because bass are known to evade traps.

**Growth (1948-1958 histories)**
- Largemouth bass (45 fish, 8 year classes): faster than the State average early on; growth declined in the early 1950s and is now approximately average.
- White crappie (143 fish, 8 year classes): very close to the State average.
- Black crappie (23 fish): below average.
- Channel catfish (65 fish, 13 year classes): similar to the State average; reach 12 inches by year 4 and up to 30 inches by year 9.
- White bass (155 fish, 9 year classes): growth high after 1951 stocking, then declined to below average.
- Bluegill (64 fish, 9 age groups): low compared with the State average.
- River carpsucker (49 fish, 9 year classes): unusually large sizes, up to 6 pounds and 22.5 inches. Maximum observed length 21.2 inches.
- Freshwater drum (418 fish, 11 year classes): "alarmingly low" growth, described as "indicative of a most unhealthy condition". Brief improvement followed the 1953-1954 rotenone treatments, but growth declined again.

**Rough-fish removal test**
- Fifteen trap nets baited with cottonseed pellets over 8 days removed: drum 132 lb (16.5 lb/day), river carpsucker 1,479 lb (185 lb/day), carp 1,534 lb (192 lb/day). The average catch per net lift was 26.2 lb. Trap netting was judged impractical in July.

**Management conclusions and recommendations**
1. **Rotenone treatment** is the most expedient fix for the drum overpopulation, but only if a continuous management plan with post-treatment evaluation is adopted. Costs are much higher now that the lake is full.
2. **Commercial fishing** is not a solution. Carp and river carpsucker stocks are too small to sustain removal without subsidy. Cleared seining areas should be maintained.
3. **Discontinue game fish stocking** unless an analysis of the population shows a definite need.
4. **Creel census** should be kept in operation at all times, since no measure of fishing quality exists.
5. **Experimental stocking of threadfin shad** is suggested, as they have high reproductive potential and may suppress gizzard shad.

The Abstract summarizes the broader conclusion: drum standing crop was the most significant factor in poor angling returns, and population reduction plus other management steps are needed.
