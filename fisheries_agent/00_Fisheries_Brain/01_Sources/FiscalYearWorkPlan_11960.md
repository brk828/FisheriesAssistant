# Fiscal Year 2000 Work Plan: Recovery Implementation Program for Endangered Fish Species in the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) document, dated August 30, 1999, is the work plan and budget for the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] (Recovery Program) for FY 2000, which runs from October 1, 1999. The Colorado River Implementation Committee approved it on August 30, 1999. The plan compiles more than 90 scopes of work from state and federal agencies, universities, private contractors, and Program staff. Its projects implement priority actions in the Program's Recovery Action Plan ([[RIPRAP]], dated April 1, 1999), which aims to recover four endangered fishes: [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]].

The total FY 2000 Recovery Program budget is $11.45 million. This includes a $3.3 million established annual budget, about $4.2 million in total annual funds once other contributions and Section 7 funds are added, and $7.228 million in Reclamation capital funds.

## Background & Description

The Recovery Program is a basin-wide, multi-stakeholder effort. Its Implementation Committee includes representatives from the FWS, the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming state governments, the Western Area Power Administration, environmental groups, and Upper Basin water users. The Colorado River Energy Distributors Association and the Program Director serve as nonvoting members.

The annual budget is $3.3 million. This is a 1.6 percent increase, based on the 1998 Consumer Price Index, over the $3.2 million FY 99 budget. Contributions by the FWS ($860,400 established, $1,122,700 expected annual funds), Reclamation ($2,152,900 established, $2,163,300 expected annual funds), and the states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming bring the total to $4.2 million. Reclamation's capital projects add $7.228 million, and the Water Users Section 7 Funds add $64,700.

The planning cycle runs from November to December, when the Program Director issues early guidance and annual progress reports are due. It moves through technical and management committee reviews of the RIPRAP and draft guidance, then scope development, and ends with Implementation Committee approval of the Annual Work Plan. The goal is to have contracts issued and funds allocated by December 1999.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document centers on habitat needs of the endangered fishes in a river system altered by dams, diversions, and reservoir operations. Its main threats and management responses are:

- **Flow alteration:** Depletions, timing of releases, and fluctuating flows from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] affect spawning, rearing, and overwintering habitat. Winter flows in the middle Green River nursery area are dominated by Flaming Gorge releases.
- **Barriers and entrainment:** Dams and diversions block upstream movement and draw fish into canals. Examples include the Price-Stubb Diversion Dam, which has blocked passage since near the turn of the century; the Tusher Wash and Grand Valley Irrigation Company intakes, which can entrain native and endangered fishes; and the Grand Valley Project Diversion Dam.
- **Floodplain degradation:** Levees and altered hydrology have reduced natural floodplain function, which the Program aims to restore.
- **Nonnative fishes:** Established species such as cyprinids, centrarchids, and [[Northern Pike]] prey on or compete with young endangered fish. The Program treats full eradication as unlikely and focuses on prevention and active control.
- **Contaminants:** Selenium occurs naturally in Colorado River habitats, but human activity has raised concentrations. Adverse effects include reduced growth and survival.

## Key Findings

### 1. Budget allocation by recovery element

The FY 2000 budget is distributed roughly as follows:

| Recovery element | Approx. share of budget |
|---|---|
| Instream flow identification and protection | ~38% |
| Habitat restoration (fish passage and floodplains) | ~23% |
| Propagation and genetics management | ~12.4% |
| Program management | ~14% |
| Research, monitoring, and data management | ~6% |
| Nonnative fish and sportfish impacts | ~5.8% |
| Information, education, and public involvement | just under 1% |

### 2. Instream flow protection

- **[[Grand Valley Water Management]]** is the largest single project, at about $3 million (listed as $3,156,200). It involves canal improvements on the Government Highline system and could yield up to 30,000 acre-feet for endangered fish via storage in Green Mountain Reservoir. Progress is behind schedule, and the Management Committee will review spending at mid-year.
- Other flow measures include coordinated reservoir operations for spring peak flows, a coordinated facilities study, and a lease of 21,650 acre-feet from [[Ruedi Reservoir]].
- On the [[Yampa River]], the Program plans a programmatic biological opinion, with a final version targeted for the end of FY 2000. A lease of 2,000 acre-feet from Steamboat Lake will augment late-summer flows.
- Instream flow identification studies include a project on the effects of fluctuating flows on young overwintering Colorado pikeminnow (total budget $97,800; outyear cost $189,600), a Duchesne River flow-needs study (final report due 2001), and channel monitoring of geomorphic change in the 15-Mile Reach.

### 3. Habitat restoration

- **[[Fish Passage]]:** The [[Redlands Fish Passage]] structure on the Gunnison River restored access to 57 miles of habitat. It has been used by more than 40 Colorado pikeminnow and more than 20,000 other native fishes. Passage at the Grand Valley Irrigation Company diversion (completed FY 98) added 3 miles. Passage at Price-Stubb (5.4 miles) and the Grand Valley Project (Roller Dam, 47 miles) continues. Canal intake screening at the Tusher Wash and Grand Valley Irrigation Company diversions is also planned.
- **[[Floodplain Habitat Restoration]]:** Ten floodplain sites (8 on the Green River, 2 on the Colorado) have been restored through levee removal. Landowner interest in easements totals 14 owners of more than 3,000 acres on the Green, 13 owners of about 250 acres on the Colorado, and 5 owners of about 125 acres on the Gunnison.

### 4. Nonnative fish and sportfish control

Activities include removing small cyprinids from the lower Colorado and Green rivers (Utah, $91,800; Colorado, $54,000); electrofishing centrarchids from backwaters ($52,000); translocating [[Northern Pike]] from the Yampa River ($95,700); and reclaiming floodplain ponds, with up to 20 ponds per year ($292,000). The Program is also evaluating a net screen at Highline Reservoir.

### 5. Propagation and genetics

Hatchery and refugia facilities at [[Ouray National Fish Hatchery]], Wahweap, and Grand Valley produce fish for stocking. Planned work includes experimental bonytail stocking, razorback sucker stocking evaluation, moving wild adult Colorado pikeminnow from Palisade to Rifle, and acquiring additional growout ponds. The Facilities Operation and Maintenance line totals $641,700, and Propagation Facility Expansion totals $665,300.

### 6. Research, monitoring, and data management

- Recovery goals for all four endangered fishes, in both the upper and lower basins, will be completed and published in the Federal Register for comment in FY 2000.
- The [[Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program]] (ISMP) is being revised to emphasize population estimates over catch-per-unit-effort estimates. FY 2000 monitoring covers humpback chub at Yampa Canyon, Black Rocks, Westwater, Desolation/Gray Canyon, and Cataract Canyon (population estimates at the first three), Colorado pikeminnow in the Green and upper Colorado reaches, and razorback suckers basinwide.

### 7. Water user funding

- Project proponents entering [[Endangered Species Act]] Section 7 consultation pay a one-time charge of $10 per acre-foot of average annual depletion, adjusted for inflation. The FY 2000 charge is $14.36 per acre-foot.
- These [[Section 7 Funds]] are held by the [[National Fish and Wildlife Foundation]]. About $230,000 was available as of October 1, 1999. FY 2000 expenditures of $64,700 leave an estimated $165,300, plus new deposits and interest.
- Three projects will use these funds: flow and temperature gage operation ($34,700), a water rights consultant (up to $10,000), and peer review of geomorphology studies (up to $20,000).

### 8. Contingency list and open needs

If more money becomes available, the Implementation Committee has approved contingency projects. These include larval and adult razorback sucker monitoring ($42,500 and $134,000), Lodore channel monitoring ($29,000), additional Ouray hatchery operations, a water treatment retrofit at Ouray (up to $100,000), and Hartland fish passage (cost to be determined).
