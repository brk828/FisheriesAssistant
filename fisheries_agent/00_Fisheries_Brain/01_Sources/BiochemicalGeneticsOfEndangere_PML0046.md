# Biochemical Genetics of Endangered Colorado Squawfish Populations

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the genetic makeup of two [[Hatchery Stocks]] of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) with two wild populations from the [[Green River]] and [[Colorado River]]. Using [[Starch Gel Electrophoresis]] on 44 presumptive loci, the authors found the hatchery and wild fish to be very similar (unbiased [[Genetic Identity]] > 0.99). The fish show surprisingly high [[Heterozygosity]] for an endangered species, and the hatchery stocks show no clear signs of [[Genetic Drift]] or [[Bottleneck Effect]]. The authors conclude that the hatchery fish are genetically representative of the wild populations sampled. A secondary aim showed that five polymorphic loci can be scored from non-lethal [[Fin Tissue Sampling]], which can help managers monitor breeding and restocking programs.

## Background & Description

The Colorado squawfish is the largest North American cyprinid. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] listed it as endangered in 1967. Historically it was a top predator throughout the Colorado River ecosystem and most larger tributaries. It is now restricted to the Green, Yampa, and upper mainstream Colorado rivers. Some individuals make spawning migrations of up to 200 km to preferred whitewater canyons. Historically, fish over 30 kg were not uncommon, but wild-caught individuals in the last two decades rarely exceeded 7 kg. Fish take 5–6 years to reach sexual maturity.

Two captive populations are maintained at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico):

- **1981 year class:** derived from 29 fish collected from the Colorado and Green rivers. Three females and ten males were bred in 1981. The 1981 stock was sampled as N = 7.
- **1987 year class:** derived from eight Yampa River fish (sex ratio unknown), bred in 1974. One female and five males produced this year class. The 1987 stock was sampled as N = 31.

Rearing techniques have been successful. More than 176,000 young have been reintroduced into the Salt and Verde rivers of Arizona, and further releases into the lower Colorado River were planned. The authors note that no genetic analyses of wild or hatchery stocks had been conducted before this study. A central concern is whether hatchery fish genetically represent wild populations, since this determines the success of restocking.

The study had two objectives: (1) compare hatchery and wild populations using [[Allozymes]]; and (2) test whether enzymes can be assayed from biopsied tissue such as fin epithelium, which does not endanger the fish.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document places the fish within the river systems that structure its population. Wild fish were sampled at three localities on the Colorado River and five on the Green River (river kilometres measured from the confluence of the two rivers). The Colorado squawfish's restricted range and long migrations mean that wild populations are spatially separated. The authors note that departures from Hardy–Weinberg proportions may indicate reduced [[Gene Flow]] between localities, which challenges the assumption that a whole river system is a single population.

The comparison species, the [[Sacramento Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus grandis*), serves as a benchmark for natural variability. Its heterozygosity (1.1%) and polymorphic loci (4.2%) were lower than those of the Colorado squawfish in this study.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- Starch gel electrophoresis was performed on muscle, liver, fin, and brain plus eye tissues from frozen specimens, following Siciliano and Shaw (1976).
- Of 44 presumptive loci scored, Table 1 lists the enzymes, buffer systems, and tissues used. Gels (12% w/v) ran at about 200 V for 18 h.
- Analyses used the BIOSYS-1 program. Heterozygosity was calculated by direct count. Hardy–Weinberg tests used the Levene (1949) small-sample correction and exact probabilities. Clustering used the unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic averaging (UPGMA) and [[Nei Genetic Distance]] / Nei's unbiased identity (Nei 1978).
- Twenty-six fish from each wild population were sampled.

**Genetic variation**
- At least 9 of 44 loci were polymorphic.
- Mean heterozygosity ranged from 2.6% (Dexter 1981) to 5.3% (Colorado River). The values were 2.6% (Dexter 1981), 4.7% (Dexter 1987), 5.3% (Colorado River), and 3.1% (Green River). These are similar to the average of nine other cyprinid genera (3.78 ± 0.80%; Avise and Ayala 1976).
- The proportion of polymorphic loci ranged from 9.1% to 13.6%. This is higher than the 4.2% reported for Sacramento squawfish.
- The high heterozygosity in the Dexter 1987 stock likely reflects the high heterozygosity of the Yampa River parents. Overall, the species appears to maintain high heterozygosity across its range.

**Allele patterns**
- EST-1\*100 frequency was 0.929 (Dexter 1981) and 0.950 (Dexter 1987), compared with about 0.577 in both wild populations. This suggests a trend toward fixation of the most common allele in hatchery stocks.
- The Green River population was the only one carrying a third EST-1 allele.
- Variation at TPI-1\* appeared only on Ridgway buffer, not on tris-EDTA–borate or tris-citrate buffers. The authors show that single-buffer systems can miss alleles.
- The PEPS\*62 variant appeared only in the Dexter 1981 stock. The authors suggest it is probably present at low frequency in natural populations.

**Hardy–Weinberg deviations**
- In the Colorado River samples, GPI-2\* and EST-1\* deviated significantly (P < 0.01), both due to heterozygote deficiency.
- In the Green River, EST-1\* deviated at P = 0.05.

**Genetic identity and relationships**
- Nei's unbiased genetic identity was greater than 0.99 for all pairwise comparisons.
- The cophenetic correlation coefficient was 0.896, indicating a good fit between data and phenogram.
- Similarity was comparable to that among geographic populations of other cyprinids. The authors note that the hatchery stocks are at most two generations removed from their wild founders, so isolation and inbreeding effects would not yet be expected. They caution that greater divergence may appear after more generations unless breeding programs preserve variability.

**Fin tissue sampling**
- Five polymorphic loci (GPI-1\*, EST-1\*, TPI-1\*, TPI-2\*, PEPS\*) could be resolved from fin tissue. The authors describe this as a nonlethal method suitable for monitoring allele frequency changes in breeding stock.

**Conclusions**
- Significant drift and bottleneck effects, as shown by allozyme frequencies and heterozygosity, had not occurred in captive populations. The authors see no reason to expect major allele frequency changes at fitness-relevant loci.
- The authors conclude that the hatchery populations appear representative of the two wild populations sampled.
- Management recommendations include monitoring hatchery allele frequencies with fin-tissue markers and designing breeding programs that preserve variability.
