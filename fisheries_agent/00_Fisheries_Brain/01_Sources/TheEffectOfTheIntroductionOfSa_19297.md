# Freshwater Fish and Invertebrate Population Demonstrations, English Lake District (1965)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This section of the 1965 *Mitteilungen der Internationalen Vereinigung für Theoretische und Angewandte Limnologie* symposium report records three exhibits from the [[Freshwater Biological Association]]. Each illustrates how fish predation, food supply, and territorial behaviour shape freshwater populations.

- **[[Macan]]** studied the effects of introducing [[Salmo Trutta]] into [[Hodson's Tarn]], a small moorland fishpond, over five years. Fish removed many species, reduced others, and left some unchanged. The author proposed that population sizes are regulated intrinsically, through territories and food-limited growth, rather than by predators.
- **[[Charlotte Kipling]]** reported on the population dynamics of [[Esox Lucius]] in [[Windermere]], based on about 7000 aged fish. Year-class strength was unrelated to adult stock but strongly related to summer temperature.
- **[[Jean McCormack]]** compared [[Perca Fluviatilis]] populations in Windermere and [[Ullswater]], and described trout survival in a stony stream.

## Background & Description

**Hodson's Tarn (Macan).** The tarn is just under half a hectare in extent and about 3 m deep at its deepest point. Its margins near the inflow are fringed with *Carex inflata*, and the centre is overgrown with *Potamogeton natans*. Shallow-water plants include *Littorella uniflora*, and deeper water supports *Myriophyllum alterniflorum*. Fish were removed at the start of the experiment, and the population was then observed for five years. In autumn 1960, 500 small trout were introduced, followed by 500 more a year later. Results were published in Macan (1964, 1965).

**Pike in Windermere (Kipling).** Since 1944 the Freshwater Biological Association has caught pike each winter in gill nets. Age and growth were determined from opercular bones for about 7000 fish. Preliminary estimates were made of year-class strength (at age 8), numbers, and biomass. Gill nets catch pike from age 3 upwards.

**Lake District fish populations (McCormack).** This exhibit included a simplified life-history diagram for trout in a typical small stony stream, and a comparison of perch populations in two similar lakes. The full perch study is reported in McCormack (1965).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Fishpond food web.** Before fish were introduced, Hodson's Tarn had abundant surface predators and insect larvae. These included tadpoles, *[[Notonecta]]* (which hunts at the surface), and dytiscid beetle larvae. Once trout arrived, these species became rare, mainly because their behaviour makes them easy prey. Other taxa, such as *[[Leptophlebia Marginata]]* and *[[Enallagma Cyathigerum]]*, stayed as abundant as before, even though trout stomach contents showed heavy predation, especially in winter.

**Cover and territory.** Macan argued that the availability of vegetation or *Sphagnum* cover determined how many *[[Corixa Castanea]]* could survive. Individuals without a sheltered territory were likely to be eaten.

**Dragonfly nymphs.** *[[Pyrrhosoma Nymphula]]* and *Enallagma* are ambush predators. They wait at vantage points for entomostracans and chironomid larvae. The number of full-grown nymphs stayed similar each year, even though the initial population varied, which suggests that the number of usable vantage points set the limit.

**Windermere pike and temperature.** Pike year-class strength varied greatly. The largest year-class was at least five times the smallest. Netting in the first few years cut numbers and biomass roughly in half. Numbers later recovered to the pre-netting level, but biomass reached only about 70% of it. Pike older than 8 years became rare.

**Lake District perch and trout.** In Windermere, perch were heavily trapped after 1941, and their average weight rose from 80 g to 170 g. In Ullswater, where there was no fishery, catch per trap and average weight stayed unchanged.

## Key Findings

### 1. Trout introduction in Hodson's Tarn (Macan)

- Some species nearly disappeared after the introduction of trout, including tadpoles, *Notonecta*, and dytiscid larvae.
- Some species declined but persisted, including *Corixa castanea* (confined to areas with cover) and various Trichoptera (less abundant but with unchanged distribution).
- Some abundant species were unchanged, including *Leptophlebia marginata*, *L. vespertina*, *Cloeon simile* (*C. dipterum* disappeared), *Enallagma cyathigerum*, *Pyrrhosoma nymphula*, and *Limnaea pereger*.
- The author found no evidence of cannibalism in the Odonata. Growth in dragonfly nymphs was related to food supply: when the population was small, most nymphs completed growth in one season, but when it was large, many needed two.
- Macan proposed that "starvelings" (undersized nymphs) fill vacancies left by predation. Predation therefore increases total production through a generation but leaves the final number unchanged.
- A possible "Rose effect" (inhibition by larger individuals) was considered and judged less likely than food-and-territory limitation. Macan said a definite conclusion needed experimental work.
- *Limnaea pereger* counts across ten stations rose from 9 in 1956 to 157 in 1963. The author suggested a compensatory mechanism means the breeding population does not depend on the level of predation.
- In discussion, Macan acknowledged that proving the absence of a species is difficult. He suggested that nothing may have disappeared, but that the reductions were striking.

### 2. Pike population dynamics in Windermere (Kipling)

- About 7000 pike were aged from opercular bones, with year-class strength varying by at least a factor of five.
- Early gill netting halved numbers and biomass. Numbers recovered to pre-netting levels, but biomass only reached about 70%.
- Year-class strength showed no correlation with the number or biomass of adult pike at hatching, which suggests that density did not limit recruitment at the levels studied.
- Year-class strength was strongly correlated with a cumulative temperature index for the first summer after hatching. Warmer summers produced more fish in the year-class.
- A full account was being prepared for the *Journal du Conseil*.

### 3. Lake District fish populations (McCormack)

- **Trout, stony stream:** Egg-to-fry survival was about 90%. Only about 3% of fry survived to become yearlings, and the author attributed the 97% loss to failure to secure a territory.
- **Perch, Windermere vs. Ullswater:** Both lakes are basically similar. Windermere has pike, while Ullswater has no large predator. Windermere's catch was reported as "3.000" fish per trap (as printed) at an average weight of 80 g. Ullswater's catch was 150 fish per trap at 86 g.
- Intensive trapping reduced the Windermere perch population, and average weight rose to 170 g. Ullswater's population remained steady and low, and its cause is unknown. Predation was ruled out, though cannibalism was possible.

### Overall conclusion

Across these exhibits, population size in freshwater systems appears to be regulated more by access to food, cover, and territory than by predator density. Temperature during early life stages also strongly influenced year-class success in pike. Several conclusions remained provisional and awaited experimental confirmation.
