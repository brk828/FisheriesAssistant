# Differential Use of Stream Habitat by Spawning Catostomids

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines how spawning suckers (family [[Catostomidae]]) use [[Deer Creek]], a third-order tributary of the middle [[Wabash River]] in Carroll County, Indiana. Spring sampling showed clear partitioning of the stream by species and by age-size group. [[White Sucker]] dominated the headwaters, while redhorse (*Moxostoma* spp.) were more abundant downstream. [[Shorthead Redhorse]] appeared only in the main stem during spawning. Spawning began only after [[Stream Temperature]] rose above or near 10 °C and water levels had dropped to suitable depths. The authors argue that this segregation by species, size, age, time and habitat reduces competition for spawning sites and larval rearing habitat, and that tributary use by river populations is important for management.

## Background & Description

Catostomids are common river fishes that are widely thought to ascend small streams to spawn in spring. Earlier work reported that the extent of upstream movement by adults, and the degree to which resident and migrant suckers contribute to stream spawning, remained unclear. The authors noted that this gap limited management and understanding of how stream alteration might affect catostomid populations.

Earlier studies had shown interspecific differences. [[Black Redhorse]] and [[Golden Redhorse]] in Missouri streams appeared to move only short distances to spawn, while golden, shorthead and silver redhorse in an Iowa tributary showed no clear spawning migration. Silver redhorse and [[Quillback]] in an Ohio tributary did increase in abundance during spring.

Deer Creek drains 709.7 km² of agricultural land. Its mean annual discharge is 6.71 m³/s, and daily discharge ranges from 0.18 to 407.6 m³/s. Six stream sections of 0.4–0.9 km were sampled across four zones (I–IV) on Deer Creek and two first-order tributaries, [[Bachelor Run]] and [[Paint Creek]]. Paint Creek was narrower and slower-flowing than the other two streams. Twelve catostomid species occur in Deer Creek. This study focused on the most abundant species to determine how the creek is used for spawning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Adults were collected with [[Hoop Net]] and [[Blocknet Sampling]] during spring and fall, with blocknets fished for 4-hour periods around the clock. Depth, velocity and substrate size were measured at sites where suckers were observed spawning. Sex and spawning readiness were determined from external characteristics, and age was estimated from [[Age Determination]] using pectoral fin rays. Flow was measured with a Pygmy-type flow meter.

**Spawning habitat.** Spawning site characteristics differed among species:

| Species | Substrate | Depth | Velocity |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Golden Redhorse]], [[Shorthead Redhorse]], [[Northern Hog Sucker]] | Medium gravel, riffles | 30–60 cm | 0.4–0.9 m/s |
| [[White Sucker]] | Medium gravel, upper reaches | 20–25 cm | 0.50–0.59 m/s |
| [[Creek Chubsucker]] | Fine gravel | 50–75 cm | 0.10–0.24 m/s |

Comparative values from other studies include black redhorse on rubble and gravel riffles at 15–60 cm (Missouri), shorthead redhorse on riffles at 15–21 cm (Illinois), golden redhorse on gravel and rubble at 0.9–1.2 m/s (Ohio), and creek chubsuckers at 50–80 cm (North Carolina). Northern hog suckers, white suckers and creek chubsuckers appear to have similar requirements, so they may compete for sites.

**Hydrology and temperature.** Water temperatures in the sampled reaches ranged from 10 to 22.5 °C during spawning. Upstream and downstream temperatures differed by no more than 1 °C. Spawning followed sharp drops in discharge and temperature rises above 10 °C.

## Key Findings

- **Spatial segregation by species.** White suckers were concentrated in the first-order tributary and upper zones. Redhorse were more abundant downstream. Shorthead redhorse were found only in the main stem during spawning, and white suckers spawned only in zones I and II.
- **Size and age segregation.** Spawning golden and black redhorse from zone IV were significantly older than those upstream at zone II (P<0.01 and P<0.05). Spawning golden and black redhorse at zone IV were also significantly larger than those in the tributary (P<0.01). In autumn, golden redhorse at zone III were significantly smaller and younger than spring fish from zones II or IV (P<0.01). The authors suggest that smaller golden redhorse reside in the Deer Creek system year-round, while larger adults enter the lower main stem only to spawn.
- **Hog sucker size segregation.** Spawning northern hog suckers in the first-order tributary were significantly smaller than those from downstream zones, which suggests that headwaters do not attract larger adults.
- **Temperature threshold.** Spawning did not begin until water temperatures remained at or above about 10 °C for some time. In 1977, spawning occurred about a month earlier than in 1978 or 1979 because of an early warming trend.
- **Spawning sequence.** Across three years, the species order was: white sucker, silver redhorse, northern hog sucker, creek chubsucker, shorthead redhorse, quillback, black redhorse, golden redhorse and river carpsucker. Segregation by time, size and habitat is likely to reduce competition for spawning habitat and larval development areas.
- **Spawning depth and velocity.** Golden redhorse, shorthead redhorse and northern hog suckers all spawned on similar riffles and sometimes on the same shoals at the same time. Golden redhorse spawned over a greater area of stream, which may allow larger adults to avoid competing with younger adults.
- **Age estimation.** Scales were unreliable for estimating age in fish older than about age 5 in Deer Creek catostomids. Fin rays were used instead. A high percentage of older fish did not form an annulus each year after age 7, so mean ages of spawning groups may be underestimated.
- **Management implications.** Tributaries provide important spawning areas for river suckers. Changes in flow regime, substrate type or fish movement could alter these populations, and the authors recommend further study of tributary use before stream alteration.
