# Occurrence of Two Endangered Fishes in the Little Snake River, Colorado

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #freshwater-ecology #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This study documents the first confirmed occurrence of the endangered [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and suspected [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) in the [[Little Snake River]], a major tributary of the [[Yampa River]] in the Upper Colorado River Basin. Prior surveys (Holden, 1973) failed to document either species in this river, despite anecdotal reports from local ranchers and anglers. Using radiotelemetry and multi-gear fish sampling (trammel nets, seines, dipnets, angling) during spring runoff and baseflow periods in 1988, researchers confirmed seasonal, discharge-dependent use of the river's canyon reach by both endangered species, along with a broader native and non-native fish assemblage. The findings implicate the lower 12 km canyon section of the Little Snake River as significant habitat warranting protection, and raise unresolved taxonomic questions regarding the identity of the "suspected" humpback chub, which may represent phenotypic variants or hybrids within the [[Gila Complex]].

## Background & Description

- **Colorado Squawfish** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*):
  - Two radiotagged adults tracked; one measured **824 mm total length (TL)**.
  - The captured individual was **neither tuberculate nor in ripe (spawning) condition**, suggesting it had not yet reached reproductive readiness or had already spawned elsewhere.

- **Suspected Humpback Chub** (*Gila cypha*):
  - Size range of captured specimens: **216–310 mm TL** (seven individuals collected).
  - Two specimens (219 mm and 310 mm TL), collected 8 June, exhibited **breeding tubercles**, indicating reproductive maturity/spawning condition.
  - Notably, specimens from the Little Snake River **lacked the abrupt nuchal hump and fleshy snout** characteristic of larger, "typical" humpback chub from Blackrocks or Westwater Canyon on the [[Colorado River]].
  - This morphological divergence is attributed to one or more of: **phenotypic variation**, **smaller size/younger age** of specimens, or **hybridization** with other members of the [[Gila Complex]] (e.g., with [[Colorado Roundtail Chub]], *Gila robusta robusta*).
  - Two suspected **humpback chub × Colorado roundtail chub hybrids** were also collected, underscoring ongoing taxonomic ambiguity.
  - Photographic comparisons showed similarity to humpback-like chub from [[Cross Mountain Canyon]] (Yampa River) and [[Debeque Canyon]] (Colorado River) — both representing known upstream distributional limits for humpback-like chub.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study Area & Habitat Structure:**
- The Little Snake River study reach spanned river km (RK) 0.0 (confluence with Yampa River) to RK 56.0.
- **RK 56.0–14.0**: Meandering channel through pasture/cropland; predominantly sand substrate.
- **RK 12.9–9.7 (canyon section, 3.2 km)**: Steep-walled sandstone canyon.
  - Upper 1.6 km: constricted, steep gradient, boulder/rubble/cobble substrate; deep turbulent eddies during runoff becoming deep pools (up to 3 m) at baseflow — critical fish habitat.
  - Lower 1.6 km: less constricted, braided, cobble/gravel substrate.
- **RK 9.7–0.0**: Sand substrate through irrigated cropland and cottonwood bottomlands; few suitable fish habitats at baseflow.

**Hydrology:**
- Discharge highly variable: peak of **127 m³/s (20 May)**, declining to **0.03 m³/s (September)**.
- Rapid June decline: 71 m³/s (7 June) → 11 m³/s (27 June) → 5 m³/s (6 July).
- Base flows of 0.2–0.3 m³/s in Aug–Sept; slight rise to 0.85 m³/s in October.

**Species Use Patterns:**
- Colorado squawfish appear to use the Little Snake River seasonally (spring) for **warmer water temperatures and enhanced feeding opportunities**, potentially aiding physiological conditioning for migration/spawning — consistent with patterns described for backwaters and tributary mouths on the Yampa/Colorado rivers ([[Holden and Wick 1982]]).
- One radiotagged squawfish moved from the Yampa River into the Little Snake River (24–25 May, RK 9.2 eddy; RK 10.8 by 8 June), then returned to the Yampa River as flows declined (26 June), migrating to the **Yampa Canyon spawning area** by 6–9 July, before returning to its Yampa River overwintering area by 2 October.
- A second radiotagged squawfish was observed moving upstream on 8 June in main-channel run habitat (water temp 19°C in Little Snake vs. 16°C in Yampa), later detected moving downstream in the Yampa River before contact was lost.
- Suspected humpback chub occurrence appears **restricted to the spring runoff period**, associated with deep turbulent eddies — a habitat type consistent with known [[Humpback Chub]] ecology ([[Valdez and Clemmer 1982]]). As eddies transitioned to pools during declining flow, suspected humpback chub apparently left the tributary.
- The canyon's deep pools (vs. scattered/shallow pools elsewhere in the river) provided critical concentrated fish habitat, especially at baseflow (July–October).

**Community Composition (Adult/Large Juvenile Sampling, n=308):**
- [[Colorado Roundtail Chub]] — 53% (numerically dominant)
- [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) — 20%
- [[Common Carp]] (*Cyprinus carpio*) — 11%
- [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*) — 9%
- Minor species (<3% each): [[Bluehead Sucker]] (*Catostomus discobolus*), [[Black Bullhead]] (*Ictalurus melas*), [[White Sucker]] (*Catostomus commersoni*)
- 7 suspected humpback chub, 2 suspected hybrid chub, 1 Colorado squawfish.

**Larval/Young-of-Year Sampling (28 June 1988, n=1,260; 10–30 mm TL):**
- Flannelmouth sucker — 82%
- Bluehead sucker — 17%
- Minor: [[Sand Shiner]] (*Notropis stramineus*), [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*), Colorado roundtail chub.

## Key Findings

- **First documented occurrence** of Colorado squawfish and suspected humpback chub in the Little Snake River, confirming previously unverified angler/rancher reports (Seethaler, 1978).
- The lower 12 km **canyon reach** is identified as important, potentially critical habitat for native and endangered fishes and is recommended for protection.
- Evidence suggests Colorado squawfish **use the Little Snake River pre-spawning** for warm-water conditioning but **likely leave prior to spawning** in the river itself — supported by: (1) lack of tuberculation/ripe gametes in the captured adult, (2) absence of additional squawfish near radiotagged fish locations, (3) subsequent tracking of a radiotagged fish to a known Yampa Canyon spawning site, and (4) no larval squawfish collected. However, spawning within the Little Snake River cannot be fully ruled out.
- Suspected humpback chub in the canyon may represent chub **seeking new habitat for living or spawning**, given the presence of tuberculate individuals.
- Significant **taxonomic uncertainty** persists regarding the identity of Little Snake River "humpback-like" chub:
  - Some researchers (R. Valdez, H. Tyus, R. Muth, personal communication) consider the specimens within the range of natural variability observed in humpback chub from the Little Colorado River, Lower Green River, and Yampa Canyon.
  - Others suggest they may be **hybrids within the Gila complex** (e.g., with Colorado roundtail chub).
  - This uncertainty is tied to broader ongoing taxonomic studies under the **Recovery Implementation Program** for endangered Upper Colorado River Basin fishes.
- The Little Snake River exemplifies **natural flow variability** historically shaping native fish evolution in the Colorado River system; maintaining natural flow regimes is recommended to preserve this tributary as important habitat.
- Findings support continued study of Little Snake River and similar tributaries to clarify **distribution, taxonomy, and behavior** of endangered Colorado River Basin fishes.
