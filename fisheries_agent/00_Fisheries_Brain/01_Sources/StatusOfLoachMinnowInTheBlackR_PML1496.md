# Status of Loach Minnow in the Black River Drainage, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1996 field survey, reported in March 1997 by Paul C. Marsh of [[Arizona State University]] for the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], sampled the [[Black River]] drainage on land administered by the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]]. The study began as a general fisheries survey but was refocused after the discovery of a new population of federally threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*). Fish were collected at 47 locations on 24 streams, yielding 12 species: six native and six non-native. The loach minnow population is a significant range record, and the report identifies trout and [[Smallmouth Bass]] as threats to native fishes, particularly the threatened [[Apache Trout]] and loach minnow.

## Background & Description

The [[Black River]] forms where its East and West Forks, with headwaters at roughly 9,000 and 11,000 feet (2,743 and 3,353 m), join, and it later merges with the [[White River]] to form the [[Salt River]]. The drainage crosses the [[Apache-Sitgreaves National Forests]], the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]], and private land. It is managed mainly as a trout fishery and has a long history of stocking non-native species.

**Native species (6):**
- [[Apache Trout]] (*Oncorhynchus apache*), federally threatened; historically present and recently reintroduced
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*)
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*), federally threatened; first recorded from the system in 1996
- [[Sonora Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*)
- [[Desert Sucker]] (*Pantosteus clarki*)

**Non-native species (6):** [[Rainbow Trout]], an Apache x rainbow trout hybrid, [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Fathead Minnow]], and [[Smallmouth Bass]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Methods.** Four collecting trips were made on 10–14 June, 4–6 July, 22–26 July, and 5–8 August 1996. Fish were captured by gill net, trammel net, dip net, seine, battery or gas-powered backpack [[Electrofishing]], and angling, with methods matched to habitat. Sampling was concentrated on riffles, the habitat preferred by loach minnow. Fish were identified, counted, and released. Voucher specimens were preserved and deposited in the [[Arizona State University Museum]], Tempe. Twenty-five loach minnow were frozen on dry ice for [[Genetic Analysis]], and one more was preserved in formalin, for a total of 26 specimens. The work was done under state and federal scientific collecting permits.

**Threats.** The report cites several pressures on native fishes:
- Introduced [[Smallmouth Bass]] and trout are predators. Trout may be the major threat to loach minnow, and they also appear to have displaced [[Roundtail Chub]] from upper reaches.
- Hybridization with [[Rainbow Trout]] threatens pure [[Apache Trout]]. Rainbow trout have direct access to streams that hold pure Apache trout.
- Historical [[Rotenone]] treatment in 1958 killed fish along the North Fork of the East Fork from Crosby Crossing to Diamond Rock Lodge, aiming to remove "rough fish" for trout fishing. Ironically, this reach later became the site of the new loach minnow population.

## Key Findings

**Loach minnow discovery.** Loach minnow were common along more than 2.25 miles (3.6 km) of the North Fork of the East Fork of the Black River, from 0.25 mile (0.4 km) above [[Three Forks]] downstream to at least 0.5 mile (0.8 km) above [[Open Draw]]. The report states that there are no stocking records, so the population is considered native rather than introduced. The nearest known population in the Black River watershed lies more than 130 miles (208 km) downstream in the [[White River]]. The authors judged natural upstream migration unlikely because of that distance and because of predatory non-native fishes.

**Genetics.** Analysis of 25 individuals showed the Black River population is distinct from all other populations examined. The White River population has not been analyzed genetically.

**Distribution of other native fishes.**
- Roundtail chub was locally abundant near Forest Service Road (FSR) 25, where a reproducing population was found in an area with fewer trout and no established smallmouth bass. It was not found near Three Forks in 1996, although an earlier report recorded it there.
- [[Speckled Dace]], [[Sonora Sucker]], and [[Desert Sucker]] were common throughout much of the study area.
- Apache trout and Apache x rainbow hybrids occurred at several sites.

**Non-native fishes.**
- [[Brown Trout]] was the most frequently encountered non-native fish, found in the upper headwaters, lower reaches, and between.
- [[Rainbow Trout]] occurred in six streams but was never abundant.
- [[Brook Trout]] was found only in Boneyard Creek, where it was locally abundant.
- [[Fathead Minnow]] occurred in high-elevation headwaters of the North Fork of the East Fork and at Open Draw.
- [[Smallmouth Bass]] was found only in the lowest 2 miles (3.2 km) of the Black River, although spot samples above that reach may have missed it. The species may extend upstream to Buffalo Crossing.

**Sampling scope.** Sites were recorded by stream, date, electrofishing time (seconds), and catch by species. Examples include the North Fork of the East Fork at [[Three Forks]], where 1,648 fish were recorded on 12 June 1996 (the total includes 329 loach minnow records from a related sampling effort). Many other sites were dry or yielded no fish. Full site tables are in Appendix A and are not reproduced here.

**Recommendations.**
1. Determine the full extent of the Black River loach minnow population. Gaps remain: about 3 miles (4.8 km) above Three Forks were not sampled, and about 1.5 miles (2.4 km) near Open Draw were not examined.
2. Treat identification of all loach minnow populations as a priority 1 task in the [[Loach Minnow Recovery Plan]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service 1988).
3. Avoid future trout stockings where they could jeopardize loach minnow, and curtail introductions of non-native fishes into loach minnow habitat, also a priority 1 task in the recovery plan.
4. Define the upstream limits of [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]] in the lower study area.
5. Obtain White River specimens for genetic comparison, to complete the picture of loach minnow populations.
6. Coordinate with the [[Fort Apache Indian Reservation]] tribe, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the U.S. Forest Service to recover threatened fishes.
