# Bonytail Chub Recovery Plan

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #Colorado-River-Basin

## Executive Summary

This 1987 recovery plan addresses the critically endangered [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), a large-river cyprinid native to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. Listed as endangered on April 23, 1980, the species appears close to extinction with no evidence of successful reproduction in recent years. Only isolated adult specimens have been captured in widely separated locations of the Upper Colorado River Basin, while old adults persist in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]] without juvenile recruitment. The plan was prepared by the Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Region 6. The immediate goal is preventing extinction; downlisting requires establishing four self-sustaining populations, while delisting requires six populations with legally protected habitats.

## Background & Description

The [[Bonytail Chub]] is a streamlined cyprinid adapted to swift waters of the Colorado River system, closely related to [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Originally described by Baird and Girard (1853), adults commonly reach 30-35 cm total length. The species exhibits an elongated, laterally compressed body with a long, thin caudal peduncle, small head with terminal mouth, and smooth predorsal hump in adults. Dorsal and anal fin rays typically number 10-10 (distinguishing it from roundtail chub at 9-9 and humpback chub at 9-10). Gill rakers on the second arch number 18-21 versus 12-15 in roundtail chub.

Adult coloration includes gray or olivaceous back with white, silvery sides and belly. Breeding males develop bright red-orange lateral slashes between paired fins and fine breeding tubercles on head and anterior body. Pharyngeal teeth follow the 2,5-4,2 pattern typical of large-river chubs. Positive identification remains problematic, particularly for juveniles, due to overlapping morphometric characteristics among the three *Gila* species. Hybridization between species has been suggested by multiple investigators.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Historic Distribution**: The bonytail chub originally occurred throughout the Colorado River system in main channels and larger tributaries. Historical records document presence in the [[Green River]] from [[Flaming Gorge]] basin (Wyoming) through [[Dinosaur National Monument]] (DNM), [[Desolation Canyon]], [[Gray Canyon]], and lower reaches. In the [[Colorado River]] mainstem, the species ranged from near the [[San Juan River]] mouth to the Gulf of California. Major tributaries included the [[Gila River]] to Fort Thomas, Arizona, the [[Salt River]], the [[Gunnison River]] near Delta, Colorado, and lower [[Yampa River]]. The species was apparently abundant in the late 1800s, with Jordan (1891) seining five specimens in the Green River at Green River, Utah, and Kirsch (1888) reporting they "took the hook freely" in the Gila River.

**Present Status**: The species is now extremely rare. In the lower basin, large old adults (primarily females) persist in Lakes Mohave and Havasu without reproductive success. Since 1974, only 19 individuals have been collected by biologists from Lake Mohave, with several more reported by anglers. Stocking efforts from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] included approximately 4,500 tetracycline-marked fingerlings (1982), 45,000 swim-up fry (1983), 68,211 swim-up fry (1984), and 12,618 4-inch fingerlings (1985) into Lake Mohave, with unknown success.

In the upper basin, Holden and Stalnaker (1975) found only 36 adults during 1968-1971 studies, with 34 from upper Green River in DNM and no young. Vanicek (1967) could regularly catch adult bonytail chub in DNM during 1964-1966, but numbers declined dramatically after [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure in November 1962. Strong year classes existed in 1959, 1960, and 1961, but reproduction appears to have dropped dramatically in 1963-1964. A single bonytail chub was collected in 1984 from Black Rocks area of the Colorado River. Two suspected specimens (adult 386 mm TL, juvenile 46 mm TL) were sampled in [[Cataract Canyon]] in 1985.

**Habitat Requirements**: Bonytail chubs are considered big-river or main stem species, rarely collected from smaller tributaries. In DNM, Vanicek (1967) noted adults used pools and eddies rather than areas with stronger current, showing no habitat preference difference from adult roundtail chub. Young chubs (not separated by species) consumed primarily chironomid larvae and mayfly nymphs, transitioning to floating food items as they grew. Adult bonytail chubs fed on terrestrial insects, plant debris, and filamentous algae, indicating surface feeding behavior. No fish remains were found in stomach contents.

**Reproductive Biology**: Spawning has not been observed in rivers. Extrapolation from ripe fish collections suggested spawning occurred in DNM during late June and early July at water temperatures around 18°C (Vanicek and Kramer 1969). Ripe bonytail and roundtail chubs were temporally synchronous but spatially separated. In [[Lake Mohave]], Jones and Sumner (1954) observed spawning behavior with approximately 500 individuals congregating over gravel bars in water up to 9 m deep. Males outnumbered females approximately 2:1, with females typically escorted by 3-5 males. Fertilized eggs were deposited randomly without parental guarding.

Artificial spawning at [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (1981) using six females and five males from Lake Mohave showed spawning began at 20°C water temperature. Hatching success was 90% at 20°C (99-174 hours), 55% at 16-17°C (170-269 hours), and only 4% at 12-13°C (334-498 hours). The 96% failure rate at coldest temperatures implicates cold tailwaters below main stem reservoirs as a factor in species decline.

**Growth and Age**: Vanicek and Kramer (1969) determined growth through scale back-calculation in DNM. Young reached 55 mm their first growing season, 100 mm their second season, and 158 mm their third season. Bonytail chubs grew faster than roundtails after reaching 150 mm length. The largest specimen was 388 mm and 7 years old. Growth rates slowed after Flaming Gorge Dam closure in November 1962.

## Key Findings

**Reasons for Decline**: Multiple factors contributed to population collapse:

1. **Dam Construction and Operation**: Main stem dams created the primary impact in both upper and lower basins. [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (closed November 1962) and [[Glen Canyon Dam]] (closed 1963) altered flow regimes, reduced summer water temperatures in tailwaters, eliminated riverine habitat in reservoirs, and changed natural flow patterns. Hamman (1982b) demonstrated that cold tailwater temperatures (12-13°C) resulted in 96% egg mortality, directly linking reservoir operations to reproductive failure.

2. **Habitat Loss**: Direct loss of riverine habitat occurred through reservoir inundation. Flow depletions from irrigation and consumptive water uses further degraded remaining habitat. In the lower basin (Gila River system), Miller (1961) documented flow depletions from vegetation loss due to overgrazing, groundwater depletion, dams, irrigation, and mining. The lower Gila River nearly dried up in early 1900s with greatly reduced flows, increased flash flooding, and channel cutting.

3. **Nonnative Species**: Introduction of competing and predatory nonnative fish species throughout the Upper Colorado River Basin (Tyus et al. 1982) likely contributed to decline. Kaeding et al. (1986) suggested synergistic effects of increased nonnative fishes combined with altered flow and temperature regimes adversely impacted the species.

4. **Hybridization**: Potential hybridization with [[Roundtail Chub]] and [[Humpback Chub]] may threaten genetic integrity. Stalnaker and Holden (1973) and others suggested hybridization occurs. Valdez and Clemmer (1982) hypothesized that altered water regimes (reduced flows, increased temperatures) resulted in overlapping spawning periods among *Gila* species, breaking down reproductive isolation mechanisms. Small remaining population size increases hybridization likelihood.

5. **Temporal Pattern**: In upper basin, decline accelerated in early 1960s coinciding with major dam closures. Vanicek and Kramer (1969) documented strong year classes in 1959, 1960, and 1961, but reproduction dropped dramatically in 1963-1964 after Flaming Gorge Dam became operational. In lower basin, decline occurred earlier in 20th century, with species becoming rare in Colorado and Gila Rivers near Yuma by 1940-42 and absent by 1950 (Miller 1961).

**Recovery Strategy**: The plan establishes a three-tiered objective structure:

- **Interim Objective**: Prevent immediate extinction
- **Downlisting Criteria**: Establish and maintain four viable, self-sustaining populations
- **Delisting Criteria**: Secure six naturally self-sustaining populations with legally protected habitats

**Priority Actions** (from stepdown outline):

1. **Status Determination**: Compile historical population data, develop improved taxonomic identification criteria, conduct intensive sampling in [[Gray Canyon]], [[Desolation Canyon]], DNM (Green and Yampa Rivers), and other potential habitats including Lakes Havasu and Mohave.

2. **Population and Habitat Monitoring**: Develop and implement monitoring procedures for all age classes and critical habitats. Monitor seasonal sampling to determine population trends. Emphasize factors necessary for successful reproduction including spawning sites, water temperature, flow, and larval rearing areas. Utah Division of Wildlife Resources and Colorado Division of Wildlife designated as primary monitoring agencies for their jurisdictions.

3. **Threat Assessment and Protection**: Assess impacts of development projects on flow, temperature, and water quality. Identify and assess impacts of nonnative species competition and predation. Study parasitism extent. Determine hybridization impacts and significance. Discontinue nonnative fish introductions with negative impacts. Enforce existing laws through Section 7 consultation compliance and agency coordination.

4. **Life History Research**: Describe spawning requirements in riverine and reservoir environments. Document movement patterns. Identify specific habitat requirements for all life stages (spawning, nursery, over-winter, adult). Describe food habits, age distribution, growth rates, and population dynamics. Define what constitutes a self-sustaining population. Determine reasons for hybridization.

5. **Population Restoration**: Determine specific habitat requirements and establish stocking site selection criteria. Recommended reintroduction sites include Green River below [[Jones Hole Creek]], [[Island Park]], [[Split Mountain Canyon]] (all in DNM), and Green River in Desolation and Gray Canyons. Upper Gila River identified as additional potential site. Develop appropriate stocking procedures, propagation techniques, and maintain diversified gene pool. Secure rearing facilities including [[Page Springs Hatchery]] (Arizona) in addition to Dexter NFH. Consider upper basin hatchery location.

6. **Genetic Management**: Maintain captive broodstock at Dexter NFH established from 1981 Lake Mohave collections. Supplement gene pool with additional wild individuals, milt, or fertilized eggs to secure genetic heterozygosity for at least 20 generations.

7. **Habitat Enhancement**: Improve or enhance potential riverine areas to create and protect spawning, nursery, adult holding, and winter habitats based on research results. Develop basin-wide management plan once populations are identified or restored.

8. **Public Engagement**: Conduct information and education programs. Produce and disseminate materials describing the species, habitat needs, limiting factors, and recovery efforts. Provide workshops for public agencies on endangered species responsibilities and identification techniques.

**Implementation Priorities**: The plan defines three priority levels: Priority 1 (actions absolutely essential to prevent extinction), Priority 2 (actions to maintain current population status), and Priority 3 (all other actions for full recovery). Implementation schedule includes estimated costs (1987 values), cooperating agencies, and proposed completion dates. General categories include Information Gathering/Research, Management, Acquisition, and Other (information/education, law enforcement, regulations, administration).

**Critical Uncertainties**: Positive identification of *Gila* species remains problematic due to overlapping morphometric characteristics, particularly in juveniles. The plan calls for expert team review of *Gila* taxonomy and recommendations for resolving identification issues. Actual numbers of remaining bonytail chubs in Lakes Mohave and Havasu are unknown. Success of reintroduction attempts (1982-1985) in Lake Mohave remains undetermined due to limited monitoring results.
