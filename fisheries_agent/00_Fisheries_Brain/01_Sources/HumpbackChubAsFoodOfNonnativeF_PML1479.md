# Native Fish Distribution, Predation, and Shoreline Habitat in the Grand Canyon Region

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #salmonidae #cyprinidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This note summarizes three bilingual (English/Spanish) papers from the [[Desert Fishes Council]] 1994 Symposium Volume, published in 1995. All three address native fishes of the [[Grand Canyon]] region of Arizona, with a focus on the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and related natives such as [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] and [[Speckled Dace]].

The studies cover three themes:
1. What controls native fish distribution in small tributary streams.
2. How non-native fishes prey on young humpback chub in the [[Little Colorado River]].
3. How shoreline geomorphology predicts the distribution of subadult humpback chub in the [[Colorado River]].

The authors identify [[Water Temperature]], [[Non-Native Species]] (especially trout), [[Waterfalls]] and [[Spawning Migration]] as major controls on tributary fish distribution. They also conclude that even modest predation by non-native fishes could significantly reduce humpback chub recruitment.

## Background & Description

**Paper 1: Biotic and abiotic factors affecting distribution of native fishes in streams tributary to the Colorado River in Grand Canyon.**
Six small streams in or near Grand Canyon were surveyed seasonally from January 1992 to August 1993. Sampling covered the first 5 km upstream of each confluence with the Colorado River, and up to 14 km on some streams. The study streams include [[Bright Angel Creek]], [[Tapeats Creek]], [[Deer Creek]], [[Paria River]], Shinumo Creek and Kanab Creek.

**Paper 2: Humpback chub as food of non-native fishes in the Little Colorado River.**
Stomach contents were examined in monthly samples from June 1991 to July 1994. Predators included non-native rainbow and brown trout, [[Channel Catfish]], and yellow and black bullheads. A total of 315 guts were analyzed.

**Paper 3: Use of geomorphology to predict subadult humpback chub distribution in the Colorado River of Grand Canyon.**
The study examined subadult humpback chub (less than 200 mm TL) along about 16 miles of the Colorado River below the Little Colorado River confluence. It used electrofishing and measured shoreline geomorphology, substrate, depth, velocity and cover. It also considered the effects of interim flow operations at [[Glen Canyon Dam]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Tributary streams.** Native fishes persisted in some streams but not others. Shinumo and Kanab Creeks held perennial populations of bluehead sucker and speckled dace. The Paria River always contained speckled dace, but flannelmouth sucker occurred there only in spring. Bright Angel Creek was dominated by introduced trout, with native suckers and dace present seasonally. Tapeats Creek held only rainbow trout, and no fish were observed in Deer Creek above its mouth.

**Predation.** Stomach contents were dominated by detritus, algae and aquatic insects. Diversity of food items was low. Native fishes, including humpback chub, were a minor food component, occurring in 5% of the 315 guts. However, non-native fish were abundant. The authors' scenario (5% of predators each eating two fish per week) shows that 1,000 predators could consume about 5,200 fish per year.

**Shoreline habitat.** Shorelines with vegetation, debris and sloped banks held higher densities of subadult chub than shorelines dominated by gravel, cobble, sand or bedrock. Narrow shoreline segments with greater total area generally supported higher densities than wider segments with more rocky substrate. Hydraulic and geologic criteria were used to classify shoreline extents.

## Key Findings

- **Six streams surveyed:** Fish populations were sampled seasonally from January 1992 to August 1993 in six small streams near Grand Canyon.
- **Perennial native populations:** Shinumo and Kanab Creeks supported perennial bluehead sucker and speckled dace.
- **Seasonal occurrence:** Flannelmouth sucker occurred in the Paria River only in spring, while speckled dace were present year-round.
- **Trout dominance:** Bright Angel Creek was dominated by introduced brown and rainbow trout. Tapeats Creek held only rainbow trout.
- **Barren reach:** No fish were found in Deer Creek upstream of its mouth.
- **Main controls:** Physical habitat (especially water temperature), introduced trout, waterfalls that block upstream movement, and spawning migrations were identified as the most significant factors.
- **Low native prey share:** Humpback chub and other native fishes made up 5% of predator stomach contents (315 guts), and their food was dominated by detritus, algae and insects.
- **Predation risk:** Hypothetically, 1,000 predators eating two fish weekly could consume about 5,200 fish per year.
- **Size of consumed chub:** Measured humpback chub consumed by predators (n = 11) averaged 121 mm TL and were mostly yearlings.
- **Mortality rates:** Age- or size-specific mortality was not quantified. Recaptures of PIT-tagged chub over 150 mm TL suggested low mortality rates.
- **Potential population impact:** The authors conclude that annual losses of several thousand young chub to non-native predators could significantly affect the population by reducing recruitment.
- **Shoreline preference:** Subadult humpback chub were most abundant along vegetated, debris-laden and sloped shorelines.
- **Stability under flows:** The stability of these shoreline habitats under interim flow operations at Glen Canyon Dam was examined.

Figures reported here are those legible in the text. The predation percentage was read as 5%, which is consistent with the 5,200-fish calculation.
