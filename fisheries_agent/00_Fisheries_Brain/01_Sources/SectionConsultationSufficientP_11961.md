# Recovery Implementation Program Recovery Action Plan (RIPRAP) for the Endangered Fishes of the Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a 1996 Fish and Wildlife Service memorandum distributing a revised [[Section 7 Consultation]] agreement and the [[Recovery Implementation Program]] Recovery Action Plan ([[RIPRAP]]). Together they govern recovery of four endangered [[Colorado River Fishes]]: [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail]]. The Recovery Program aims to recover these fishes while allowing existing and new water development to proceed in the Upper Basin under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The RIPRAP is the operational plan: it lists actions, schedules, responsible agencies and budgets, and it serves as the reasonable and prudent alternative (RPA) to jeopardy and to adverse modification of critical habitat for projects in Section 7 consultation. The estimated Recovery Program budget for FY 1996 to FY 2003 is about $104 million.

## Background & Description

**Document structure.** The document has two parts. Part One is the Section 7 Agreement, finalized October 15, 1993, which sets out how depletion impacts of new projects, and impacts of historic projects, are handled in Section 7 consultations. Part Two is the RIPRAP, which is reviewed and revised annually. The annual revision process begins each January.

**Target species and recovery goals.** The overall goal is naturally self-sustaining populations and protected habitat, leading to recovery and delisting of the four species. Recovery goals differ by species:
- Colorado squawfish and humpback chub: establish and maintain natural self-sustaining populations and habitat.
- Bonytail: prevent extinction, given its critical population status in the Upper Basin.
- Razorback sucker: prevent extinction in the wild, because successful recruitment of young fish has been limited.

**Recovery elements.** Actions are grouped into recovery elements:
1. Protect instream flows (habitat management)
2. Restore habitat
3. Reduce negative impacts of nonnative fishes and sportfish management
4. Conserve genetic integrity and augment or restore populations
5. Monitor populations and habitat and conduct research
6. Increase public awareness and support (General Support Plan only)
7. Provide program planning and support (General Support Plan only)

**Action plans.** The RIPRAP has a General Recovery Program Support Action Plan and subbasin plans for the Green River (mainstem, Yampa and Little Snake, Duchesne, White), and the Colorado River (mainstem, Gunnison, Dolores). Tasks are marked with ">" if they produce a measurable population or habitat response, legal flow protection, or reduced extinction threat. Tasks marked "*" support the RPA for critical habitat.

**Governance.** The participants include the [[Bureau of Reclamation]], the [[Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[Western Area Power Administration]], the states of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming, and several water, environmental and power groups. The Management and Implementation Committees oversee the program.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Range loss and habitat change.** Passage barriers have confined the endangered fishes to about 20 percent of their former range. Upper Basin floodplains were once inundated by spring runoff, but much of the river is now channelized by levees, dikes, rip-rap and tamarisk. Peak spring flows have also declined because of upstream impoundments. Flooded bottomlands support large numbers of zooplankton and benthic organisms. Razorback suckers use them for feeding before and after spawning, and Colorado squawfish use them for feeding before migrating to spawning areas.

**Flow and temperature.** [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] has changed the reach directly downstream. Its cold releases are presumed to exclude the endangered fishes from the reach between the dam and the Yampa confluence. Flow needs are determined mainly by the Fish and Wildlife Service and address reproduction, food supply, nonnative fishes, channel structure, sediment, substrate, vegetation and temperature.

**Contaminants.** Selenium, petroleum derivatives, heavy metals and uranium are present in the upper basin, and several contaminant "hot spots" have been identified. Selenium contamination at Stewart Lake and Ashley Creek near Jensen, Utah, is a source of impact to razorback suckers. Uranium processing in the Dolores drainage in the late 1940s to 1960s is thought to have contributed to squawfish decline. Remediation is handled outside the Recovery Program.

**Nonnative fishes.** The upper basin has 52 fish species, of which only 13 are native. [[Northern Pike]] is a major concern. Researchers found that young squawfish made up 5% of northern pike diet in the Green River, and one pike could consume 100 or more squawfish per year. Pike also prey on roundtail chub and may feed on humpback chub in the Yampa.

**Subbasin significance.**
- **Green River:** the highest-priority area for Colorado squawfish, important for humpback chub and bonytail, and supports the largest known natural-river population of razorback suckers.
- **Yampa River:** essential to Green River recovery. It has one of two confirmed squawfish spawning areas in the Upper Basin and contains a small self-sustaining humpback chub population. The [[Little Snake River]] supplies about 28% of the Yampa's flow and 60% of its sediment.
- **Duchesne River:** squawfish and razorback suckers use its lower 15 miles, especially during spring runoff.
- **White River:** [[Taylor Draw Dam]] (completed 1984) blocked squawfish migration to the upper river.
- **Colorado River (Rifle to Lake Powell):** supports several important populations. Humpback chub occur at Black Rocks and Westwater Canyon, with a smaller population in Cataract Canyon. Upstream habitat between Palisade and Rifle has been unoccupied since diversion dams blocked migration in the early 1900s.
- **Gunnison River:** occupied by squawfish and historic habitat for razorback suckers. The 10-foot [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] limits migration.
- **Dolores River:** historical squawfish habitat, now used only in the lower mile.

## Key Findings

**Section 7 framework**
- The Recovery Program is intended to serve as the RPA to jeopardy and to adverse modification of critical habitat for new projects (depletion impacts) and historic projects (all impacts except discharge of contaminants such as trace elements, heavy metals and pesticides).
- The Fish and Wildlife Service decides whether progress is sufficient, using measurable population response, measurable habitat improvement, legal flow protection, or reduced extinction threat. Progress is evaluated separately for the Colorado and Green River subbasins.
- If progress is insufficient, biological opinions specify RIPRAP actions that must be completed to avoid jeopardy. The Recovery Program is responsible collectively, not individual project proponents.
- Agreement is effective upon RIPRAP adoption. Historic projects before January 22, 1988 (the Cooperative Agreement date) are covered. Increases in depletion after that date are subject to the depletion charge.

**Funding**
- Total budget FY 1996 to FY 2003: about $104 million. Approved work plans total about $61 million.
- Annual operating budget of about $3 million, contributed by Reclamation, the Service and the three states. Over FY 1996 to FY 2003 this totals about $30 million.
- A one-time depletion fee on new water projects was $13.04 per acre-foot as of October 1, 1995. Fees could provide about $1 million through 2003.
- Full recovery scope would need about $64 million: about $29 million for water acquisition and about $35 million for capital construction (fishways, hatcheries and bottomland restoration).
- Annual operation and maintenance for refugia, hatchery and fish passage facilities is about $9.6 million through FY 2003.

**Flow protection**
- Colorado: the Colorado Water Conservation Board adopted its instream flow policy on March 9, 1994. Its two-level acceptance (legal protection with or without modifiable qualifications) addresses scientific and water-availability uncertainty.
- Utah: the State Engineer would condition new and pending applications on predetermined streamflow levels, which is the most common planned strategy.
- The Colorado Water Conservation Board filed for a junior instream flow right in the Colorado 15-Mile Reach (581 cfs for July to September) in 1992. It has been litigated since then.

**Water and fish passage**
- Ruedi Reservoir has provided 20,000 acre-feet of water for the 15-Mile Reach since 1990. An interim agreement covers remaining uncommitted water, and one-year contracts cover up to an additional 21,650 acre-feet for 1996 and 1997.
- Fish passage is planned at Grand Valley Irrigation Company Diversion Dam, Price-Stubb Dam and Government Highline (Roller Dam). Access to roughly 50 miles of historic habitat would be restored. A fish ladder at Redlands was scheduled for completion in 1996.

**Habitat restoration**
- A 1994 inventory covered 870 miles of the Colorado, Green, Gunnison, Yampa and White rivers. Site restoration began in 1994 and continues until at least 1999.
- Restoration to date includes Old Charlie Wash (Ouray NWR), and three Colorado River floodplain sites near Grand Junction.
- A floodplain issue paper will address legal, institutional and political strategies for floodplain restoration and protection.

**Genetics and propagation**
- Stocking is an interim tool. Recovery depends on self-sustaining wild populations, and success depends on flow, habitat and nonnative fish actions being in place.
- Four planning documents guide the work: Genetics Management Guidelines, Genetics Management Plan, Annual Propagation Operations Plan and Coordinated Hatchery Facility Plan.
- Experimental razorback sucker stocking began in 1994 in the Colorado River near Rifle and Grand Junction, and in the Gunnison River near Delta.

**Critical habitat**
- The critical habitat final rule was published March 21, 1994 and became effective April 20, 1994. The Service reviewed the RIPRAP and recommended modifications to cover the primary constituent elements: water quantity and quality, physical habitat, and biological environment.
- Key modifications include adjudication of instream flow filings (up to three years), entrainment structures at Government Highline and Redlands, and selective removal of northern pike from the Yampa and Gunnison rivers.

**Monitoring and uncertainty**
- Recovery success will be judged ultimately by species response, which may take many years. In the interim, progress is gauged by RIPRAP accomplishments and Interim Management Objectives.
- Participants disagree over the efficacy of the interim nonnative fish stocking procedures. A NEPA evaluation of stocking control options was scheduled for 1996, with a decision in summer 1996.
