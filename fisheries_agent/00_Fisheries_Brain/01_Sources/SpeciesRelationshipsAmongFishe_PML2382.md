# Species Relationships among Fishes of the Gila robusta Complex in the Upper Colorado River Drainage

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This taxonomic study addresses the [[Gila robusta Complex]], a group of chubs (family Cyprinidae) in the Colorado River system that had long been difficult to classify because of subjective and discontinuous character coding. The authors used multivariate analysis of 34 meristic and morphometric characters from 140 specimens, paired with a new, direct measurement of the [[Nuchal Hump]], to show that three reproductively isolated species occupy the main channels of the Colorado and Green rivers: [[Gila robusta]], [[Gila cypha]], and [[Gila elegans]]. The paper also treats peripheral populations as a [[Gila Robusta Superspecies]] and offers diagnostic keys, a corrected type locality, and a critique of earlier erroneous statements about these fishes. The authors stress that the species are endangered or threatened and that dams and reservoirs, including [[Lake Powell]], are altering the habitat that sustains them.

## Background & Description

The complex includes the most extreme members of the genus in their specialization to big-river habitat. Earlier workers disagreed about the status of these forms because the nuchal hump, the key visual trait, had been scored subjectively. The study had three objectives:

1. Determine the validity and relationships of the roundtail, bonytail, and humpback forms in the big-river habitat.
2. Determine their relationships to other forms of *Gila* in the middle and upper Colorado River basins.
3. Establish characters useful for identifying young and adult fish in the field and laboratory.

The authors treat the [[Gila Robusta Complex]] members in the [[Gila River Basin]] as a separate evolutionary line, with *Gila intermedia* considered related to *G. robusta*.

Morphology was quantified from 34 characters, including fin-ray counts, gill rakers, precaudal vertebrae, pharyngeal arches, and body proportions. Specimens were drawn from museum collections, including the [[University of Michigan Museum of Zoology]], [[Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History]], and [[Arizona State University]]. Two analyses were run independently, so that the population analysis and the character analysis could test each other without circular reasoning.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat:** The three big-river species are restricted to unmodified, swift-water main-channel habitat. Smaller tributaries such as the Virgin River hold a single population with intermediate morphology, which the authors interpret as an adaptation to local conditions rather than an intergrade or hybrid.
- **Diet and feeding:** The species appear to be omnivorous to carnivorous, with specializations linked to habitat. Jaw and tooth characters were similar across species, while gill raker counts differed.
- **Threats:** Dams destroy swift-water habitat above the barrier and drastically reduce temperature and increase turbidity below it. Introduced exotic competitors and predators, particularly on young fish, threaten populations, especially in modified reaches.
- **Reservoir effects:** Glen Canyon Dam and the resulting [[Lake Powell]] created a natural experiment. Early indications are that the two more specialized species may not persist in the reservoir environment, and that the three species are hybridizing there.
- **Historical distribution:** [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) formerly moved up the [[Little Colorado River]] to the base of Grand Falls.

## Key Findings

### Species Identification
- Principal components analysis of 34 characters from 140 specimens separated *G. robusta*, *G. cypha*, and *G. elegans* into three distinct clusters with almost no overlap when the first three axes were combined.
- The authors conclude the three forms are genetically distinct, reproductively isolated populations in big-river habitat. Some individuals near cluster borders may be hybrids.
- *G. cypha* is intermediate between *G. robusta* and *G. elegans* in general but extreme in several respects. The authors suggest *G. cypha* and *G. elegans* each arose from *G. robusta* through separate speciation events. *G. robusta* retains the most primitive characters of the three.
- The [[Nuchal Hump]] was quantified as the ratio of body length to the depth of the frontal depression. Ratio ranges by taxon were *G. cypha* 5–13, *G. elegans* 15–29, *G. robusta* 28–207, and *G. robusta seminuda* 31–121. The ranges overlap in part, so the ratio is used with other characters.
- One *G. cypha* specimen (UMMZ 181281) fell in the *G. robusta* cluster because of its high fin-ray count and gill-raker characters. Its nuchal hump ratio (9.2) and other traits identified it as *G. cypha*.

### Diagnostic Characters
- Dorsal and anal fin rays: *G. robusta* usually has 9 dorsal and 9 anal rays; *G. cypha* usually has 9 dorsal and 10 anal; *G. elegans* usually has 10 dorsal and 10 or 11 anal; *G. robusta seminuda* is intermediate.
- Gill rakers and precaudal vertebrae distinguish *G. robusta seminuda* from typical *G. robusta*. Total vertebrae (excluding the Weberian apparatus) were 40–45 (modal 42) in *seminuda* and 39–44 (modal 42) in *robusta*. Precaudal counts gave better separation.
- The authors provide a dichotomous key to species in main-channel habitat, based on nuchal hump presence, frontal profile, fin-ray counts, and caudal peduncle depth.

### Virgin River and Peripheral Populations
- Virgin River chubs form a single variable population that shares some variation with all three big-river forms. None have distinct humps.
- The authors conclude that tributary environments select for a variable average of the three morphotypes, but the heterogeneity of medium-sized rivers is insufficient to support three separate species.
- Gill raker counts separate Virgin River fish well from *G. robusta* in the main river.

### Taxonomic Recommendations
- The authors recognize a *Gila robusta* superspecies including *robusta*, *cypha*, and *elegans*, plus subspecies such as *G. robusta seminuda* of the Virgin River, a population in the San Juan River, and isolated populations in northern and western Mexico.
- *G. intermedia* is placed in the *robusta* superspecies on the basis of shared characters and possible gene exchange through *G. robusta* "*gilae*"-type populations, though it may be a separate phyletic line.

### Type Locality Revision
- Earlier sources placed the types of *G. elegans* and *G. robusta* at the "Zuni River, New Mexico." The authors reject this because the Zuni River in 1852 was a rivulet unsuited to these fish, and *G. elegans* is unknown from the Zuni basin.
- The authors assume the correct type locality is at the base of Grand Falls on the Little Colorado River (station 13 in Sitgreaves' 1854 report).

### Correction of Earlier Statements
- Stroud (1963) claimed that Flaming Gorge Reservoir released large numbers of humpback chubs, all male. Later surveys (Vanicek 1967; Vanicek et al. 1970; Holden and Stalnaker 1975) found humpback chub absent from the area and recorded only three specimens of *G. cypha* below the mouth of the Yampa River, all taken during 1964–1966.
- Juveniles were identified to species at small sizes: *G. robusta* to 20 mm, *G. cypha* to 54 mm, and *G. elegans* to 22 mm total length.

### Conservation and Research Needs
- The authors call for research on habitat, food, reproduction, recruitment, hydrodynamics, and energetics of these forms, which they regard as essential for sound management.
- Maintaining large sections of unspoiled, natural main-channel habitat is identified as the key requirement for persistence of these species.
