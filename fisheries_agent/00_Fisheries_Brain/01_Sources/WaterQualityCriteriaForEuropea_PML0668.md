# Water Quality Criteria for European Freshwater Fish: Finely Divided Solids and Inland Fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This document is EIFAC Technical Paper No. 1 (1964) from the [[European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission]] (EIFAC), prepared by a Working Party on Water Quality Criteria for European Freshwater Fish and published by the [[Food and Agriculture Organization]] (FAO) of the United Nations, Rome. It reviews the literature on the effects of [[Suspended Solids]] (finely divided solids in suspension) on freshwater fisheries and proposes **tentative water quality criteria** based on four categories of suspended solid concentration. The Working Party concluded that no sharply defined concentration separates harmful from harmless levels, and that the evidence was too thin to distinguish between different kinds of solids. The paper is the first in a series of [[Water Quality Criteria]] reports for European freshwater fish.

A short 2002 fax appended to the document asks a reviewer to assess the use of an Arizona statewide surface water standard of 80 mg/L suspended sediment concentration as a substitute for base-flow conditions.

## Background & Description

The Working Party was set up at the Commission's request to develop water quality criteria for finely divided solids and inland fisheries. Its first draft was presented at the Third Session of EIFAC, held at Schärfling am Mondsee, Austria, in April 1964, and was approved by the Commission. The report was later reissued in French and English and submitted to the FAO Director-General for wider distribution, including reprinting in scientific and trade journals.

The report is organized around these topics:

- Literature survey of direct effects on fish death or survival, growth, and disease resistance
- Effects on spawning and egg development
- Effects on behaviour and migration
- Effects on food supply
- Effects on the efficiency of fishing methods

The authors state that they could not verify all the original literature, and that many studies were unpublished or lacked measured suspended-solid concentrations. Results were reported as quoted by the original authors, using different measurement methods, so direct comparison is limited.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Working Party identified five ways in which suspended solids can harm a fishery:

1. Directly affecting fish, by killing them or reducing growth and resistance to disease
2. Preventing successful development of eggs and larvae
3. Modifying natural movements and migrations
4. Reducing the abundance of food available to fish
5. Affecting the efficiency of fishing methods

These factors can act together to harm a fishery. Sources of suspended solids in surface waters include:

- Erosion and construction works
- Forestry operations and road building
- Mining and quarrying (including coal washery and china-clay wastes)
- Sewage and industrial effluents, including pulp and paper wastes

Organic solids can lower dissolved oxygen as they decompose, and some solids may carry toxic substances. The report did not treat these chemical effects in depth, nor the interaction of solids with temperature, pH or dissolved oxygen.

The authors noted that suspended solids reduce the bottom fauna of invertebrates by settling and smothering, which reduces food supply for [[Trout]] and other fish. Data from the [[Rogue River]] in Oregon and California streams showed much lower invertebrate densities where mining had introduced silt.

## Key Findings

### Tentative Water Quality Categories

The Working Party proposed four categories for chemically inert suspended solids in waters otherwise suitable for fisheries:

- **(a) Under 25 p.p.m.:** No evidence that concentrations below 25 p.p.m. have harmful effects on fisheries.
- **(b) About 25 to 80 p.p.m.:** Good to moderate fisheries are usually possible. Fish yield may be somewhat lower than in category (a).
- **(c) About 80 to 400 p.p.m.:** Waters normally in this range are unlikely to support good fisheries, although good fisheries may sometimes occur at the lower end.
- **(d) Above 400 p.p.m.:** Fisheries are likely to be poor. Several thousand p.p.m. may not kill fish in short exposures, but high concentrations can damage fish over longer periods.

The Working Party stressed that these are tentative and that the evidence did not define a precise concentration threshold for risk.

### Direct Effects on Fish Survival

- Short-term exposure to high concentrations often caused little mortality. In one study, [[Rainbow Trout]] survived exposure to 50,000 p.p.m. of gravel-washing silt for one day, but concentrations had to be raised to about 150,000 p.p.m. to kill them within that period.
- Goldfish and [[Common Carp]] endured maximum turbidities of 100,000 p.p.m. for short periods, and some individuals survived 220,000 p.p.m. for one to three weeks.
- Survival of [[Chinook Salmon]] and rainbow trout was reported at 20,000 p.p.m. of wood fibre for short periods.
- Long-term exposure to lower concentrations caused gill damage in rainbow trout, including thickened and proliferated gill epithelium after several weeks at a few hundred p.p.m.
- Kaolinite and montmorillonite were reported to be lethal to some fish at 192 p.p.m.; charcoal was lethal at 82 p.p.m. Pond sediment was not lethal up to 1,458 p.p.m.

### Growth and Disease

- Wood fibre and coal washery solids at concentrations in the range of 50 p.p.m. reduced growth rate of trout in laboratory experiments. Fish still grew reasonably well in the presence of abundant food, and yearling fish more than trebled their weight in six months at 200 p.p.m. coal washery waste.
- [[Brown Trout]] held at 200 p.p.m. of wood fibre or similar material developed [[Fin Rot]] more often than controls.

### Effects on Spawning and Eggs

- Settled solids that blocked gravel reduced survival of trout and salmon eggs. Egg mortality of up to 50% in some streams was reported at high silt loads.
- [[Atlantic Salmon]] and brown trout eggs develop successfully only where water flows through the gravel. Survival of [[Chum Salmon]] eggs rose with the velocity of water through the gravel.
- Chum salmon eggs needed at least 1 p.p.m. dissolved oxygen at early stages. Oxygen demand by eggs increased with high carbon dioxide concentrations.

### Behaviour and Migration

- Salmon could pass through turbid water in rivers, but high suspended solid concentrations could delay upstream migration and prevent spawning. Spawning fish in some rivers turned back when water became turbid.
- The report notes that some salmon species avoided turbid tributaries and used clear water for spawning.

### Food Supply

- Suspended solids reduced bottom invertebrate populations, sometimes sharply. Cladocerans and copepods were affected at 300 to 400 p.p.m., with clay the most harmful.
- The [[Daphnia]] reproduction rate increased at lower concentrations (around 73 p.p.m. pond sediment).
- In one Cornish stream, bottom fauna at 60 p.p.m. was roughly equal to that in a clear stream, but trout diets were mostly terrestrial insects.

### Fishing Efficiency

- Turbidity reduced the efficiency of some fishing methods. In one Wisconsin study, catch rates fell as turbidity increased.

### Final Conclusions and Recommendations

- The authors concluded that at present no precise criterion distinguishes among the many kinds of finely divided solids or the types of inland fisheries they affect.
- Waters with concentrations below 25 p.p.m. are generally suitable for good fisheries.
- The most important fact for any proposed standard is that solids concentration, not only the kind of solid, drives harm to fish.
- The report recommends further research on the effects of suspended solids on invertebrates, fish eggs, and fish behaviour, and on methods of measurement.

### Arizona Standard Review (2002 Correspondence)

A 2002 fax from a state correspondent asks Dr. Marsh, a recognized expert on Arizona native fish, to review the EIFAC article within the context of using an Arizona statewide surface water standard of **80 mg/L** for suspended sediment concentration as a substitute for base-flow conditions. The correspondent specified that no single standard would apply during precipitation events. The fax requested a response within a short time frame.
