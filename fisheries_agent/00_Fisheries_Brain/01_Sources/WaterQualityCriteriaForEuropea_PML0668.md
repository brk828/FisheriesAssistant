# Water quality criteria for European freshwater fish. Report on finely divided solids and inland fisheries

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1964 technical paper from the [[European Inland Fisheries Advisory Commission]] (EIFAC) Working Party on Water Quality Criteria is the first in a series defining water quality requirements for European freshwater fish. It reviews the literature on how suspended (finely divided) solids affect fisheries and proposes tentative, provisional criteria based on suspended solids concentration (SSC) in parts per million (p.p.m.). The working party concluded that no sharp threshold exists; rather, fishery risk rises with concentration, and the authors defined four risk categories for chemically inert solids in otherwise satisfactory waters.

The document also includes a short cover-page fax note (2002) from an Arizona water-quality official asking an expert reviewer to consider an 80 mg/L SSC standard as a substitute for turbidity standards. That note is administrative and does not add scientific data.

## Background & Description

- **Origin:** Prepared by the EIFAC Working Party, chaired in part by J.S. Alabaster (UK), with members from Sweden, UK, Germany, France, and the FAO Secretariat. Mr. D.W.M. Herbert largely drafted the report.
- **Publication:** Presented at the Third Session of EIFAC, Scharfling-am-Mondsee, Austria, 20–25 April 1964; issued as EIFAC Technical Paper No. 1, FAO, Rome, July 1964 (21 pp.).
- **Rationale:** Water quality standards existed for drinking, agricultural and industrial uses (via [[World Health Organization]]), but fish-specific criteria had received little attention. Water was often judged adequate merely in the absence of obvious kills.
- **Scope:** Five harm pathways were identified: (a) direct effects on fish (death, growth, disease resistance); (b) impaired egg and larval development; (c) altered movements and migration; (d) reduced food supply; (e) reduced efficiency of fish capture. Toxic solids, dissolved oxygen depletion, temperature and pH effects were explicitly excluded.
- **Units:** Studies reported mass per volume or [[Secchi Disc]] transparency readings; these could not be interconverted, so original units were retained.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sources of suspended solids** include soil erosion, engineering works, forestry, sewage, mining (china-clay, coal, gold dredging, quarry and granite wastes), pulp and paper mill effluents, and sand washing.

**Species sensitivity varies.** Notable examples cited:
- [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*): survived one day at 80,000 p.p.m. silt; sensitive to chronic exposure, with gill epithelial thickening and fin-rot at lower concentrations.
- [[Walleye]] (*Stizostedion vitreum*): very sensitive; killed within 72 h by 100 p.p.m. wood pulp in one study.
- [[Fathead Minnow]] (*Pimephales promelas*): unaffected by 20,000 p.p.m. wood pulp over 96 h.
- [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*) and [[Pacific Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus* spp.): sensitive during egg development and spawning, but migrate through turbid estuaries.
- [[Whitefish]] (*Coregonus*): severely affected in several lakes, as they depend on clear, cold water and plankton.
- [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], and [[Channel Catfish]]: reduced yields and growth in turbid farm ponds and reservoirs.

**Food web effects:** Turbidity reduced bottom fauna (for example, in Californian and North Carolina streams, and Cornish streams), zooplankton such as [[Daphnia]], and benthic invertebrates in lakes such as the Chiemsee and Mondsee. Inert clay and mineral solids were generally more harmful to invertebrates than organic deposits, which sometimes supported dense populations of chironomids and *Asellus*.

**Behaviour:** Some species avoided turbid water (king salmon, minnows, cutthroat trout), while European eel migrated more during turbidity. Atlantic salmon passed through turbid estuaries.

## Key Findings

**Tentative water quality criteria** (chemically inert solids, otherwise satisfactory waters):

| Category | Suspended solids (p.p.m.) | Expected fishery status |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | < 25 | No evidence of harm |
| (b) | 25–80 | Good or moderate fisheries usually possible; yield may be somewhat lower than in (a) |
| (c) | 80–400 | Unlikely to support good fisheries; fisheries sometimes found at lower end |
| (d) | > 400 | At best poor fisheries |

**Additional recommendations:**
- Temporary high concentrations (several thousand p.p.m.) may not kill fish over hours or days but should be prevented in rivers where good fisheries are to be maintained.
- Salmon and trout spawning grounds require special protection and should be kept as free as possible from finely divided solids.

**Key supporting evidence:**
- Laboratory survival: 40,000 p.p.m. bentonite killed harlequin fish within a day; 225,000 p.p.m. was survived for 1–3 weeks by some goldfish and carp (Wallen, 1951).
- Chronic effects: 200 p.p.m. solids produced substantial mortality in rainbow trout over weeks to months, but 200 p.p.m. coal washery solids caused no deaths over 40 weeks in one study. Spruce fibre at 200 p.p.m. caused 50% mortality after 15 weeks.
- Growth: 50 p.p.m. wood fibre or coal washery solids reduced trout growth; yearling trout still more than tripled weight over 8 months at 200 p.p.m.
- Disease: trout in 270 p.p.m. diatomaceous earth suffered more fin-rot; the lowest concentration reported to increase disease susceptibility was 100 p.p.m.
- Reproduction: Silt blocked gravel and suffocated eggs (rainbow trout, silver salmon, chum salmon). Chum salmon eggs need at least 1 p.p.m. dissolved oxygen early and 7 p.p.m. later. Brown trout did not dig redds in silted gravel. Campbell (1954) found all eggs died within six days at 1,000–2,500 p.p.m. in the Powder River, Oregon.
- Pond yields (Buck, 1956; 39 farm ponds): clear ponds (<25 p.p.m.) 164.5 lb/acre; intermediate (25–100 p.p.m.) 94.0 lb/acre; muddy (>100 p.p.m.) 25.3 lb/acre. Critical concentration for reproduction was about 75–100 p.p.m.
- Daphnia: harmful levels were 392 p.p.m. kaolinite, 102 p.p.m. montmorillonite and 82 p.p.m. charcoal; reproduction increased at lower concentrations.
- Field evidence: in a Cornish china-clay system, the bottom fauna was about one-ninth of clean-stream density at 1,000 and 6,000 p.p.m.; trout populations were normal at 60 p.p.m.
- Lake Mondsee (Austria): clay turbidity cut annual Daphnia production from about 400,000 kg to 80,000 kg fresh weight, followed by very low whitefish catches.
- Survey (Herbert and Richards, 1963): the critical concentration for fisheries lay approximately between 100 and 300 p.p.m., with overlap between categories.

**Overall conclusion:** No sharp threshold exists. Risk increases steadily with concentration, and the authors stated that criteria are provisional and subject to revision as data accumulate.

**Context of the 2002 cover note:** An Arizona official requested expert review of a proposed statewide surface water standard of 80 mg/L SSC, measured only at base flow, as a substitute for turbidity standards. The note is administrative and does not itself report data.
