# Observations on Eels from the Windermere Catchment, England

#ichthyology #marine-biology #anguillidae

## Executive Summary

This paper by [[Winifred E. Frost]], translated from English into German for the *Zeitschrift für Fischerei und deren Hilfswissenschaften* (Band X N.F., 1961, Heft 8–10, with a 1962–1963 supplement), reports field observations on the [[European Eel]] (*[[Anguilla Anguilla]]*) from the Windermere catchment in the English [[Lake District]]. The work was carried out by the [[Freshwater Biological Association]] between 1940 and 1950, with additional study on the River Bann in Northern Ireland. The lecture covers five topics: the size–age relationship of female [[Silver Eel]]s, the growth of large females, deflection of migrating eels by light, stopping of migrating eels by light, and the diet of eels in waters holding [[Salmonid Fishes]]. Frost concludes that body length, rather than age, most likely triggers seaward migration, and that underwater lighting can both divert and delay eel migration.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** *Anguilla anguilla* (European eel). Silver eels (Blankaale) are the migrating adult stage; [[Yellow Eel]]s (Gelbaale) are the feeding, resident stage.
- **Fisheries context:** British and Irish eel fisheries are mainly for silver eels caught as they leave inland natural waters. Yellow-eel fishing is rare, and eels are not cultured in ponds. The most important fisheries are in Ireland.
- **Research base:** The [[Freshwater Biological Association]] studied eels mainly in the [[Windermere Catchment]], with [[Rosemary H. Lowe]] and Frost working between 1940 and 1950. Field sites are shown in the paper's Abbildung 1 (Lake District, 1940–1945 and 1941–1944 versions in the text).
- **Ageing method:** Ages refer only to the years spent in freshwater.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Migration and body size**
- Female silver eels in Windermere most commonly migrate at 54–60 cm. Most of these fish had spent 10, 11 or 12 years in fresh water, but some spent 9, 13, 14 or 15 years.
- Frost argues that length, not age, triggers migration. She suggests that length is an index of a physiological change involving gonad development, body weight-to-length ratio and fat content.
- Comparable data from [[Bellini's Comacchio Experiments]] (cultured eels) show females reaching a migratory length of 55–66 cm after about 4.5 years and 79–90 cm after 6.5–7 years. Data from [[Esrum Lake]] (Rasmussen) are cited as supporting the same view.

**Growth of large females**
- Most female silver eels are 50–60 cm long, but about 15% of Windermere migrants were longer than 65 cm.
- Yellow-eel age–length relationships are best described by two regression lines (19–58 cm and 61–95 cm) rather than one. The lines intersect at about 50 cm and 9 years, suggesting that larger eels grew faster either throughout life or from about age 9.

**Diet and interaction with salmonids**
- Yellow eels (30–60 cm) from Windermere and other Lake District lakes ate mainly benthic invertebrates. Fish were rarely eaten, and no salmonids were found.
- In Windermere, [[Arctic Char]] (*Salvelinus alpinus willughbii*) spawn in shallow water in November and deeper water in February. Eels caught on these spawning grounds had stomachs full of char eggs.
- Eels of 17–30 cm from rivers near Windermere also fed mainly on benthic invertebrates. Salmonid eggs, fry or fingerlings were taken only very rarely.
- Frost concludes that the eel is a much more important food competitor of salmonids than a predator on them.

**Light and migration**
- Experiments at [[Cunsey Beck]], the outflow of [[Esthwaite Water]], were run between 1941 and 1944 at four Lake District sites. Lowe's work was the basis for this lecture.
- Underwater lights were mounted in metal tubes hung from wires across the river. Lights were switched on alternately on each bank for quarter-, half- or full-hour periods, and catches in traps were counted.

## Key Findings

1. **Length at migration:** Female silver eels in Windermere most commonly migrate at 54–60 cm, after 10–12 years in fresh water. Overall, silver eels measured 47–97 cm, with a mean of 67 cm given in the text. [[Table 1]] gives a mean of 60.8 cm across 240 females, so the two figures should be read with care.
2. **Age range:** Females spent 9–19 years in fresh water, with a mean migration age of 12–13 years.
3. **Size over age:** Eels of the same length migrated at very different ages, supporting the idea that length or a physiological state linked to it triggers migration. Frost describes length as an index, not a direct cause.
4. **Sample base:** 1122 silver eels (males and females) were measured at the Cunsey Beck trap between 1939 and 1944 (Abbildung 2).
5. **Growth split:** Two regression lines describe yellow-eel growth, meeting at 50 cm and 9 years. Frost proposes that this point marks a critical physiological transition, after which eels either become silver within 2–3 years or grow more slowly to a larger size before maturing.
6. **Light deflection:** In a 12-night trial at Cunsey Beck, 72% of 476 eels were caught on the unlit side of the river, compared with about 50% expected if the light had no effect. Lights shining upstream were effective at low intensities (the text gives "5–0,7" candle power, probably 0.5–0.7). Frost suggests light could raise trap catches and keep eels away from turbines.
7. **Light stops migration:** Underwater lights slowed the downstream run of silver eels. A trial net placed to guide eels into it failed, but the lights did hold migration back long enough to lift other gear. The paper notes this approach was already in use.
8. **Diet:** Yellow eels in Lake District waters feed mainly on benthic invertebrates. They take salmonid spawn when it is available, most strikingly char spawn in Windermere, which makes them a food competitor of salmonids.
9. **Practical implications:** Frost recommends that eel culture aim to bring eels to the critical length quickly to boost productivity. She also sees value in using light to divert eels into traps or away from turbines.
10. **Research gaps:** Frost notes that the eel–salmonid relationship deserves much more attention, and the Bann fishery problems were to be raised in discussion.
