# Arizona Cold Water Fisheries Strategic Plan 1985-1990

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae #fisheries-management #strategic-planning

## Executive Summary

This document is the Arizona Game and Fish Department's Strategic Plan for Cold Water Fisheries covering 1985-1990, prepared under Federal Aid Project FW-11-R. The plan addresses the management of Arizona's trout fisheries, which support over 220,000 licensed anglers annually who harvest over 3 million trout and contribute $64 million to the state economy. Arizona's cold water fisheries consist of 170 streams (1,552 miles, 1,471 surface acres) and 168 lakes (3,915 surface acres) managed primarily for trout species including [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Arizona Trout]], and [[Gila Trout]]. The plan identifies critical supply-demand imbalances: while habitat may be adequate short-term, hatchery production must increase 55.7% by 1990 to meet projected demand of 2.3 million angler-days. Six management concepts are defined ([[Intensive Use]], [[Basic Yield]], [[Blue Ribbon]], [[Featured Species]], [[Wildfish]], and [[Urban]]) to provide diverse angling experiences. Major challenges include antiquated hatchery facilities, habitat degradation from livestock grazing and timber harvest, inadequate biological data, and law enforcement issues.

## Background & Description

Arizona's native trout fauna historically consisted of only [[Arizona Trout]] (*Salmo apache*) and [[Gila Trout]] (*Salmo gilae*) in White Mountains, Blue River, Eagle Creek, and upper Verde River tributaries. Beginning around 1900, the U.S. Bureau of Fisheries introduced exotic species: [[Rainbow Trout]] (*Salmo gairdneri*) arrived before 1900, [[Cutthroat Trout]] (*Salmo clarki*) followed, and [[Brook Trout]] (*Salvelinus fontinalis*) arrived in 1917. [[Brown Trout]] (*Salmo trutta*) was introduced in 1933, grayling in 1940, and sockeye and coho salmon in the 1950s-1960s. The first state hatchery was constructed in 1922 on the South Fork of Little Colorado River near Greer. Today's system comprises six hatchery stations evolved from fifteen different facilities built over 80 years.

The Department operates under authority of Arizona Revised Statute Title 17, which vests control in the Game and Fish Commission and establishes that wildlife (including fish) are property of the state. Over 54.7% of stream management reaches (53.4% by acreage) occur on U.S. Forest Service lands, 21.6% on Indian Reservations, and 13.3% on private lands. Approximately 27% of trout lakes are on Forest Service lands, 35% on Indian Reservations, and 21% privately owned (though most private lakes are small, comprising less than 4% of total surface acreage).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Arizona's cold water fisheries face multiple ecological challenges. Water quality degradation results from excessive nutrient input causing algal blooms and aquatic vegetation overgrowth, leading to fish die-offs. Concentrated livestock use causes bank destruction, siltation, nutrient mobilization, and reduction of streamside vegetation. Silvicultural practices adjacent to streams reduce shading (increasing water temperatures), increase erosion and siltation, and add litter and nutrients. Off-road vehicle use results in channelization and vegetation destruction. Mining operations (both active and abandoned) discharge pollutants degrading stream quality. Regulated flows subject streams and reservoirs to periodic dewatering.

Competition with and predation by non-game species results in poor survival of stocked trout. Water quality at stocking time sometimes causes immediate fish mortality. The natural productivity varies greatly among waters, with some capable of supporting fisheries through natural reproduction while others require intensive catchable stocking. Approximately 1,488,000 licensed angler-days were expended in 1981, with 64.7% on U.S. Forest Service lands, 19.1% on National Park Service lands, and 11.7% on Indian Reservation waters.

The supply of trout is almost entirely dependent on hatchery production. In 1981, the Arizona Game and Fish Department stocked only about one-third of all trout stocked in Arizona waters; the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and private entities provided the remainder. From 1955-1981, while Arizona's population increased 183%, catchable trout stocking increased only 22% (0.8% annually) and fingerling stocking increased only 12% (0.5% annually). The relative supply of trout per Arizona resident in 1981 was only half what it was in the late 1960s.

## Key Findings

**Supply-Demand Analysis:**
- Current supply: 1,552 miles of streams (1,471 acres) and 3,915 lake acres managed primarily for trout
- 1981 demand: 1,760 million angler-days (33% greater than observed use of 1,326 million days)
- 1990 projected demand: 2,300 million angler-days (45% greater than projected supply-limited use)
- Hatchery production must increase 55.7% over 1981 levels to meet 1990 demand

**Management Objectives (1985-1990):**
- Increase quantity of trout available for harvest 30% by 1987 and 55% by 1990
- Provide additional 20,000 [[Blue Ribbon]] angler-days by 1987, 50,000 by 1990
- Provide additional 10,000 [[Featured Species]] angler-days by 1987, 20,000 by 1990
- Accommodate additional 10,000 [[Wildfish]] angler-days by 1990
- Provide combined [[Intensive Use]] and [[Basic Yield]] additional 300,000 angler-days by 1987, 600,000 by 1990
- Provide additional 30,000 artificial lure only angler-days by 1987, 65,000 by 1990
- Initiate two research projects by 1987, five by 1990
- Resolve four management problems by 1987, eight by 1990

**Management Standards by Concept:**
- **Intensive Use:** 30 angler-days/acre/week (lakes), 40 (streams); harvest 0.5 fish/hour; primarily [[Rainbow Trout]]
- **Basic Yield:** 9 angler-days/acre/week (both); harvest 0.3 fish/hour; multiple species
- **Blue Ribbon:** 2.5 angler-days/acre/week (lakes), 3-4 (streams); harvest 0.1-0.2 fish/hour; fish size 12-16 inches
- **Featured Species:** 2 angler-days/acre/week (lakes), 4 (streams); harvest 0.2-0.5 fish/hour; [[Arizona Trout]], [[Arctic Grayling]]
- **Wildfish:** 4 angler-days/acre/week; harvest 0.1 fish/hour; naturally reproduced populations
- **Urban:** 50 angler-days/acre/week; harvest 0.2 fish/hour; within 5 miles of cities >20,000 population

**Critical Problems Identified:**
1. Inadequate biological data and census programs for sound management decisions
2. Antiquated, inefficient hatchery facilities limiting production capacity
3. High cost of hatchery-reared trout production
4. Poor survival rates of stocked fish due to competition, predation, and water quality
5. Uneven distribution of stocked fish among anglers and over time
6. Habitat degradation from livestock grazing, timber harvest, mining, and ORV use
7. Excessive nutrient input causing algal blooms and vegetation overgrowth
8. Non-compliance with fishing regulations (license, bag limits, gear restrictions)
9. Unauthorized stocking of fish and prohibited species by private individuals
10. Insufficient research on survival rates, harvest rates, and cost-effectiveness of management practices

**Species Distribution:**
- [[Rainbow Trout]]: primary species in 60% of streams (by acreage) and 85% of lakes
- [[Brown Trout]]: primary in 34% of streams and 7% of lakes
- [[Brook Trout]], [[Arizona Trout]], [[Gila Trout]]: smaller percentages
- [[Cutthroat Trout]]: present but not primary management species in any water

**Habitat Ownership:**
- U.S. Forest Service: 57.2% of stream miles, 49.8% of lake acres
- Indian Reservations: 21.6% of stream miles, 33.0% of lake acres
- Private lands: 13.3% of stream miles, 3.9% of lake acres (though 22.8% by number)
- National Park Service: 4.3% of stream miles, significant Colorado River acreage

**Research Needs:**
- Determine survival and harvest rates of stocked trout
- Develop standardized angler use and harvest census system
- Identify optimal species, strain, and size for individual waters
- Evaluate stocking equipment and techniques to minimize stress
- Assess cost-effectiveness of chemical/mechanical weed control and biological renovation
- Identify use conflicts between trout fishing and other recreation
- Determine nutrient budgets and sources for problem lakes
- Develop law enforcement effectiveness evaluation system
- Profile fishing law violators and identify violation motivations

**Economic Impact:**
- Over 220,000 licensed trout anglers annually
- Nearly 1 million trout fishing trips per year
- Over 3 million trout harvested annually
- $64 million annual contribution to Arizona economy

The plan emphasizes that it is a "dynamic document" requiring comprehensive review every three years with new strategic plans prepared at five-year intervals to respond to changing conditions, improved information, and evolving angler preferences.
