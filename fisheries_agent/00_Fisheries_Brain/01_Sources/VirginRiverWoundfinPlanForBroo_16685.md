# Virgin River Woundfin Plan for Brood Stock, Culture, and Salvaged Fish

## Executive Summary
This 1999 draft management plan from the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (UDWR) establishes a comprehensive strategy for the conservation of the endangered [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) in the [[Virgin River]] drainage (Utah, Arizona, Nevada). The plan responds to severe population declines driven by habitat alteration and competition from nonnative [[Red Shiner]] (*Cyprinella lutrensis*). Central to the plan is the establishment of a genetically robust brood stock and culture program at the [[Wahweap Hatchery]] (Big Water, Utah), using salvaged wild fish from a buffer zone between the Washington Fields and Johnson diversions. The document details culture techniques, disease management protocols, rigorous genetic guidelines (effective population size targets), and a tiered priority system for salvage and stocking of woundfin to support the broader [[Virgin River Fishes Recovery Plan]] (VRRP) and [[Virgin River Resource Management and Recovery Program]] (VRRMRP).

## Background & Description
- **Species**: [[Woundfin]] (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) — a small minnow (family Cyprinidae, subfamily Plagopterinae) indigenous to the Virgin River drainage.
- **Conservation status**: Listed as endangered under the U.S. Endangered Species Act in 1973.
- **Regulatory history**:
  - Original recovery plan developed in 1979, updated in 1984.
  - Superseded by the [[Virgin River Fishes Recovery Plan]] (VRRP) in 1995.
  - Implementation guided by the [[Virgin River Resource Management and Recovery Program]] (VRRMRP), a cooperative state/local/federal management framework (UDNR 1999).
- **Primary threats**: Habitat alteration (dewatering, diversions) and introduction of nonnative fish, particularly the [[Red Shiner]], causing competition and displacement (Deacon 1988; Addley and Hardy 1993; USFWS 1994).
- **Core management tool**: Rotenone chemical treatments to eradicate red shiner from discrete river segments (Comella and Fridell 1998a, 1998b), maintaining a "buffer zone" between the Washington Fields Diversion and the Johnson Diversion that separates native woundfin populations (upstream) from red shiner populations (downstream).
- **Culture facility**: [[Wahweap Hatchery]], operating since 1971 (originally for sportfish stocking of Lake Powell), began endangered-species culture in 1992 to support the [[Colorado River Recovery Program]]. Endangered fish culture comprises ~72% of hatchery resources.
  - Located ~5 miles from Big Water, Utah, adjacent to Wahweap Creek (ephemeral tributary to Lake Powell).
  - Water source: well water, capacity 1,600 gpm, servicing 35 ponds.
  - Well water temperature: 16.5°C; pond temperatures range 16.3–25°C, exceeding 20°C for at least 7 months/year.
  - Indoor facilities currently limited to garage/office/food storage; expansion planned.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Range & abundance**: Woundfin remain most abundant in Utah between Pah Tempe Hot Springs and the Washington Fields Diversion — the last major naturally reproducing wild population in the system.
- **Buffer zone dynamics**: The 6.4-mile reach between Washington Fields and Johnson diversions is chemically treated intermittently (4 treatments 1988–1991; 3 treatments in 1996) and is prone to dewatering during low-flow/high-irrigation-demand periods. Woundfin recolonize this buffer via downstream drift within months of treatment/dewatering, making it a renewable and representative salvage source directly derived from the upstream wild population.
- **Nonnative species pressure**: The [[Red Shiner]] is the principal competitor; a five-phase, step-wise downstream chemical eradication program targets: (1) Washington–Johnson diversions reach, (2) Fort Pearce Wash, (3) Washington Fields drains, (4) Johnson Diversion to Webb Hill barrier, and (5) Webb Hill barrier to the state line barrier.
- **Reintroduction protocol**: Woundfin are only reintroduced to treated reaches once the reach directly downstream is confirmed free of red shiner, preserving eradication gains.
- **Fish handling protocol for upstream transplants**: (1) measure/identify all fish, (2) triple-sort/verify ID by three observers, (3) hold/observe in aquariums, (4) exclude fish <40 mm total length, (5) use transport water sourced upstream of Washington Fields Diversion.
- **Disease context**: Virgin River fishes carry Asian tapeworm and Ich, both significant causes of hatchery mortality, necessitating quarantine protocols.

## Key Findings

### Culture & Production Parameters
- Minimum initial salvage target: **500 woundfin** for brood stock establishment.
- Culture ponds: two 0.4-acre ponds covered with 2-inch bird netting; ~20 spawning baskets (18×18×2 in) per pond, filled with 2–3 inch cobble, placed in April.
- Brood stock split equally between the two production ponds; fry grown out in same ponds until stocking size.
- **Annual production estimate**: 7,500–10,000 fish per 500 breeding adults.
- **Growth rate**: 2–2.5 inches per month; fish can be stocked the same year spawned.
- **Reproductive biology**: Woundfin spawn at age 1; best production from age 2–3; high mortality by age 5, requiring brood replacement before then.
- Techniques patterned after the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico) model.

### Disease Management
- All salvaged fish undergo quarantine in two isolated 4-foot round tanks with a separate drain line.
- Treatment for Asian tapeworm and Ich per UDWR Fish Health Specialist protocols (Dr. Chris Wilson, Fisheries Experiment Station, Logan, Utah).
- Up to 120 fish/year sacrificed for disease inspection/health certification (drawn from brood stock offspring, not breeding population).

### Genetic Management Guidelines
- **Effective population size (Ne)** benchmarks discussed:
  - Ne = 50: threshold to avoid observable inbreeding depression (Franklin 1980).
  - Ne = 100: recommended minimum for hatchery populations supporting wild stock enhancement (Kincaid 1983).
  - Ne = 500: originally proposed for long-term genetic diversity maintenance (Franklin 1980; Soulé 1980).
  - Ne = 1,000: sufficient under quasi-equilibrium model (Lynch 1996); beyond this, genetic variance becomes largely independent of population size.
  - Ne = 5,000: theoretical "safe" figure accounting for deleterious/quasi-neutral mutation dynamics (Lande 1995, building on Lopez and Lopez-Fanjul 1993a/b).
- **Practical target**: Minimum salvage of 500 fish, calibrated to exceed Ne = 100 despite anticipated reductions from skewed sex ratio and non-random mating.
- **Sex ratio caveat**: Woundfin sex ratio may be skewed up to 5 males:1 female (Hammon, Dexter NFH, pers. comm.), which alone would reduce Ne of 500 census fish to ~275; under a monogamous mating system, Ne could fall to ~165, or lower still with mortality/immature-fish collection effects (potentially approaching the Ne = 100 floor).
- **Drift rate**: Loss of heterozygosity/genetic variance occurs at ~1/(2Ne) per generation (Wright 1931; Lande and Barrowclough 1987).
- **Sampling for genetic monitoring**: At least 30 individuals from initial salvage preserved (frozen, stored at UDWR Salt Lake City) for future genetic/morphological analysis, sex-ratio determination, and comparison of heterozygosity, allelic diversity, fecundity, and bilateral asymmetry across generations.
- **Supplementation strategy**: Periodic infusion of wild fish recommended when available, preferentially sourced from Virgin River areas least influenced by prior stocking, to counteract genetic drift and avoid overlapping generations (F1 fish separated from parent stock and stocked promptly to minimize artificial selection exposure).
- **Outbreeding depression risk**: Considered low relevance for this program because the target stocking area's woundfin distribution is largely limited to the same reach as the salvage source, meaning salvaged fish likely represent a high proportion of the species' extant genetic diversity (per Templeton 1994 framework).

### Salvage & Production Priorities (Ranked)
**Salvage priorities:**
1. Wahweap Hatchery brood stock establishment/maintenance (annual, 2000–2010).
2. Re-establishment in [[LaVerkin Creek]] (annual, 1999–2005).
3. Re-establishment in red-shiner-treated river segments/tributaries (annual, 2000–2010).
4. Release upstream between [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] and Washington Fields Diversion (2000–2010).
5. Transplant between Pah Tempe Hot Springs and Quail Creek Reservoir / Quail Creek diversion (2000–2010).
6. Support lower Virgin River basin recovery (Arizona/Nevada).
7. Scientific research/experimental studies (including outside historic range).
8. Release downstream of Bloomington prior to red shiner eradication (2000–2010).

**Production (stocking) priorities:**
1. Re-establishment post-red-shiner-eradication by river segment (annual, 2001–2010).
2. Reintroduction/supplementation in upper Virgin River system, including LaVerkin Creek (annual, 2001–2010).
3. Support lower basin recovery (Arizona/Nevada).
4. Establish additional populations within historic range.
5. Scientific research/experimental populations (including outside historic range).
6. Release downstream of Bloomington prior to eradication projects (annual, 2001–2010).

### Project Costs & Implementation Timeline
- Total additional program cost (beyond existing funding): **$5,500/year** (2 seasonal staff × 30 days ≈ $5,000; disease inspection staff 1 person × 2 days ≈ $500).
- Existing infrastructure (two 0.4-acre ponds) already in place at Wahweap; no major capital funding needed initially.
- **Timeline**:
  - Salvage for stream transplants: as early as 1999–2000, tied to treatment/dewatering schedules.
  - Initial brood stock salvage: summer/fall 2000.
  - First hatchery-propagated cohort: spring 2001.
  - Initial stocking from Wahweap Hatchery: fall 2001.

### Plan Objectives Alignment
- Establish viable [[Woundfin]] brood stock at Wahweap Hatchery.
- Establish a second refugia/hatchery population (per VRRP Objective 3.1, requiring ≥2 facilities alongside [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]).
- Secure state/federal approval for transplants under Utah Wildlife Code 23-14-21.
- Implement VRRP Objectives 1.2 (nonnative eradication/native reestablishment), 1.31 (propagation technique refinement), and 3.1 (genetic brood stock maintenance), with re-evaluation triggered once wild populations are stable and self-sustaining for 10 years.
