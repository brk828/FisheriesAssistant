# Grass Carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella): FAO Species Synopsis of Biological Data

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the FAO Fisheries Synopsis No. 135 (1983) on the [[Grass Carp]], *Ctenopharyngodon idella* (Cuvier and Valenciennes, 1844), prepared by Jerome V. Shireman and Charles R. Smith of the Center for Aquatic Weeds, University of Florida, with funding from the US Fish and Wildlife Service. It reviews identity, distribution, bionomics, life history, population structure and dynamics, exploitation, aquaculture and weed control potential. The species is valued for aquaculture and for aquatic weed control, and its widespread introduction has produced both benefits and ecosystem disruption. The text is a large review with extensive literature citations; this note summarises its main content.

The document header lists other FAO synopses in the same series (e.g., on tunas, Nile perch, pigfish and Mediterranean species), but these are cover-page listings only.

## Background & Description

**Taxonomy.** The species belongs to Phylum Chordata, Class Osteichthyes, Order Cypriniformes, Family Cyprinidae, Subfamily Leuciscinae. It is the sole member of its genus; no subspecies are recognised. Synonyms include *Leuciscus idella* and *Ctenopharyngodon aethiops*.

**Morphology.** The body is oblong with moderate to large cycloid scales (lateral line scales 40–45 reported), a rounded belly and broad head. Diagnostic counts given are D. 3/6–8, A. 3/7–8, V. 10, L.L. 38–42. The pharyngeal teeth are biserial (formula 2.5–4.2 and variants). The grinding surface of the pharyngeal teeth is a key feature. The intestine is long and relative intestinal length increases from 0.5 in larvae to 2.5 in adults. Gonadal differentiation occurs at a mean total length of 58 mm (50–60 days old) in USA-reared fish.

**Common names.** Grass carp, white amur, and numerous vernacular names across Asia and Europe are tabulated by country (summarised here, not reproduced).

**Hybridisation.** Natural hybridisation is not documented, but artificial crosses have been made with [[Silver Carp]], [[Bighead Carp]], [[Black Carp]], [[Common Carp]], [[Goldfish]], [[Black Bream]] and others. Hybrid progeny show intermediate morphology, and gynogenetic and androgenetic progeny have been produced.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Native range and introduction.** The natural range is eastern China and the USSR (Amur River), in low-gradient rivers, lakes and ponds. The species has been introduced to more than 50 countries (Table IV). Natural reproduction is established in only a few regions, including the Amur and Tone River (Japan), and the Lower Mississippi and Atchafalaya Rivers (USA); reproduction elsewhere is largely unverified.

**Spawning.** Spawning requires a river of sufficient length, temperatures around 18°C and current velocities of 0.6–1.8 m/s. Eggs are semi-buoyant and may drift 50–180 km before hatching. Pelagic larvae migrate downstream, then move to floodplain nursery areas. Juveniles can move up to 1,000 km from spawning grounds.

**Habitat selection.** Adults in Lake Conway (Florida) preferred densely vegetated inshore areas 1–3 m deep. Adults show seasonal movements; in Red Haw Lake (Iowa), the inshore to midwater contact ratio was 51/49 in June, rising to 100/0 by September.

**Tolerances.** Eggs need 21–25°C for normal development; a drop to 18°C or below causes very low survival. Fry tolerated 16–40°C in Indian studies. Fry tolerate salinities up to about 12‰; the species could not complete its life cycle at higher salinity. Mean lethal minimum dissolved oxygen for acclimated fry and yearlings in Poland was 0.41 and 0.2 mg/L respectively.

**Feeding and food.** Young grass carp initially eat zooplankton, then shift to macrophytes at about 2 cm. Adults can consume a large proportion of body weight daily: 26–28% in a US lake eating hydrilla; 50–100% in some juvenile feeding trials. Food conversion ratios of 14–54 have been reported for Soviet ponds, and 1.1–1.5 for fingerlings on a formulated diet.

**Ecosystem effects.** Heavy stocking (45–69 kg/ha) can eliminate macrophytes, reduce invertebrate diversity, increase phytoplankton blooms (often blue-green algae) and degrade water quality. Overstocking has caused algal blooms and reduced fish diversity in some American lakes. In other cases, controlled stocking has benefited game fish and cyprinids through macrophyte removal.

## Key Findings

- **Growth and maturity:** Maturity occurs at 1–11 years and 58–67 cm standard length in females; males mature about one year earlier at 51–60 cm. Mature fish can exceed 1 m; the largest Amur fish examined by Berg (1964) was 110 cm and 15 kg.
- **Longevity:** Scale annuli indicate fish of 15+ years are known.
- **Fecundity:** Absolute fecundity ranges from tens of thousands to about 2 million eggs, averaging around 500,000 for 5–7 kg broodstock. Relative fecundity in one Amur study averaged 110 eggs per gram of body weight without viscera. Fecundity correlates strongly with weight and length, and is influenced by fat content and feeding conditions.
- **Spawning season:** Temperate populations spawn over a limited season (roughly May–August, peaking June–July). Tropical populations may spawn year-round.
- **Artificial propagation:** Induced spawning by pituitary injection was first achieved in China in 1960 and the USSR in 1961. Females typically receive two injections; hatching occurs 19–23 hours after fertilisation at 21–25°C.
- **Population structure:** In the Amur basin catches, females outnumbered males roughly three to one in pooled data (Gorbach 1961). Shelton and Jensen (1979) found a 1:1.03 male-to-female ratio in 770 cultured juveniles.
- **Population decline and control:** Catches in the Amur basin declined substantially, and in the Tone River natural populations declined after dam construction and reduced macrophyte cover. Chemical control, electrofishing, and rotenone are used to remove introduced populations, though rotenone can show selectivity toward grass carp over largemouth bass.
- **Predation:** Largemouth bass predate fingerlings; in one experiment fingerlings suffered almost total mortality. Birds, water snakes, otters and fish predators also prey on the species.
- **Disease and parasites:** Over forty protozoan species, numerous trematodes, cestodes, nematodes and crustaceans have been recorded. Spring viremia (*Rhabdovirus carpio*) is a major viral disease; *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi* causes losses in common carp culture.
- **Aquaculture:** Grass carp are used in polyculture and as weed control agents in ponds, canals and reservoirs. They tolerate stocking densities of 400–1,000 fish/ha, and yields in Far East polyculture have been reported as high as several tonnes per hectare annually.
- **Regulation:** Russia permits introduction for weed control; Austria allows free purchase and stocking; in the USA, 35 states had banned importation by 1976, and Arkansas was among the more liberal states.
- **Research priorities identified:** The authors highlight sterile or monosex stocks (via gynogenesis or sex reversal with methyltestosterone), interspecific hybridisation for sterile weed-control fish, and controlled stocking rates to limit ecosystem disruption.
