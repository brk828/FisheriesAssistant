# Humpback Chub: Diminished Flows Impede Recovery in National Parks

#ichthyology #marine-biology #humpback-chub

## Executive Summary

The [[Humpback Chub]] is a rare, endangered freshwater fish of the [[Colorado River]] Basin. Diminished water flows, altered temperatures, and competition from nonnative fish threaten its recovery. Of its seven remaining populations, three occur within the [[National Park System]]. The largest is near [[Grand Canyon National Park]] in Arizona, where the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) estimates there may be as many as 10,000 fish. Conservation groups argue that current recovery plans are inadequate, and that dam operations and power-industry influence limit the flow and habitat changes needed to protect the species and related endangered fishes.

## Background & Description

- **Size and lifespan:** Humpback chub grow up to 20 inches long and can live up to 30 years.
- **Taxonomic confusion:** Scarce records and a resemblance to the historically more prevalent [[Roundtail Chub]] make it hard for scientists to know how many humpback chub once inhabited the basin. The species once ranged through the 1,700-mile river basin from Wyoming to the Pacific.
- **Evolutionary history:** Scientists believe the fish evolved roughly 10 million years ago (OCR reads "10,Om years ago") in the swift, turbulent, canyon-bound rivers that once carved deep canyons through the region. Its hump is considered an adaptation to fast water, pushing the fish down to calmer water near the riverbed. Its large fins let it hold position in slow-moving areas, and it feeds on insects that become trapped in backwaters.
- **Appearance:** The fish is olive green and silver in color.
- **Listing history:** The species was federally listed as endangered in 1967.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Population status**
- Three of the seven remaining populations live in National Park System waters.
- The largest population is in and around [[Grand Canyon National Park]], Arizona, with USFWS estimating as many as 10,000 fish.
- Two smaller populations persist in [[Dinosaur National Monument]], Colorado, and [[Canyonlands National Park]], Utah.
- USFWS has no estimate for the species' total population.

**Threats**
- Altered river habitat caused by dams throughout the basin.
- Changes in stream temperature.
- Reduced food availability due to altered flows.
- Competition and predation from introduced nonnative fish.
- Water releases from deep reservoirs, such as cold water that favors nonnative predators like [[Brown Trout]] and reduces the surface area that collects solar energy, which otherwise warms the water.

**Water management**
- Chubs need relatively warm water, but cold releases from reservoirs such as [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] on the [[Green River]] in Wyoming work against that need.
- Diminished flows are being addressed in areas including Flaming Gorge Dam and several Colorado River tributaries.
- National park protection alone does not ensure the species' survival, because the river segments that flow through parks are regulated by dams, diversions, and water projects that supply water across seven states.

**Broader context**
- The article describes the humpback chub's decline as part of a wider pattern of degradation in western rivers.
- The Upper Colorado River's native fish once relied on the river's abundant supply of native fish for food during hard times. Today many of these species are endangered.
- Of the Upper Colorado River's 14 native fishes, four are listed as endangered: the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], humpback chub, and roundtail chub. The article says other species are under consideration for federal listing.

## Key Findings

1. **Population size is uncertain.** Scarce records and similarity to roundtail chub limit knowledge of historic and current numbers.
2. **The largest population is in Grand Canyon.** USFWS estimates as many as 10,000 fish there.
3. **Park protection is insufficient.** Dams, diversions, and water projects that supply seven states regulate river flows through parks, so park boundaries do not guarantee habitat protection.
4. **Temperature and flow are key stressors.** Cold reservoir releases and reduced flows affect the species' need for warm water and food.
5. **Nonnative fish compound the threat.** Introduced predators and competitors reduce survival.
6. **Recovery plans are contested.** [[National Parks Conservation Association]] Rocky Mountains regional director [[Mark Knudson]] says the draft plan does not outline the necessary steps to protect endangered Colorado River fishes. The association calls recent recommendations seriously inadequate to restore riparian habitat, citing influence from the power industry, as stated by [[Peterson]].
7. **USFWS is preparing a Biological Opinion.** The agency is expected to release one recommending water flows and temperatures to protect endangered fish.
8. **The species is an ecosystem indicator.** [[Bob Muth]], a USFWS fish biologist for the [[Upper Colorado River Recovery Implementation Program]], describes endangered fish as a "canary in the coal mine," an official indicator of the environmental health of the ecosystem.
