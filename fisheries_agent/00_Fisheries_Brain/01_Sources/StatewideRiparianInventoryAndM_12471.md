# Statewide Riparian Inventory and Mapping Project — Executive Summary

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riparian-ecology #freshwater-ecology #remote-sensing

## Executive Summary

This report is **not a fish-specific study** but a habitat-scale inventory and mapping assessment of riparian ecosystems across Arizona, conducted by the Arizona Game and Fish Department's Nongame and Endangered Wildlife Program under the "Waters – Riparian Protection Program" (A.R.S. 45-101). Because riparian zones constitute the critical aquatic-terrestrial ecotone supporting fish and other aquatic/semi-aquatic wildlife, this project's findings are foundational to understanding freshwater fish habitat availability, connectivity, and degradation risk in the arid Southwest. The study used Landsat Thematic Mapper (TM) satellite imagery, aerial videography, and extensive field groundtruthing (1992–1996) to delineate and classify riparian vegetation along both **perennial** and **intermittent** streams, building on and revising the earlier work of [[Valencia Et Al 1993]]. Key outputs include an accuracy assessment of remote-sensing-derived riparian maps, a revised vegetation classification, and a newly created GIS database of ~620 miles of intermittent streams in Arizona — a dataset directly relevant to modeling [[Riparian Habitat]] connectivity for native desert fish populations.

## Background & Description

Not applicable — this source addresses landscape-scale riparian vegetation classification rather than organismal morphology. Vegetation classes documented (structural analogs to habitat "morphology") include:

- **Low-elevation riparian communities**: tamarisk (*Tamarix* spp.), cottonwood-willow, mesquite
- **Mid-elevation communities**: mixed broadleaf deciduous stands
- **High-elevation communities**: conifer-oak, mountain shrub, wet meadow
- Understory riparian obligates poorly resolved by aerial sensors: Arizona madrone, broadleaf deciduous trees, mountain willows

More than 270 plant species were identified from perennial-stream riparian polygons during groundtruthing, later consolidated from 17 to 10 vegetation classes to improve classification accuracy (35% → 45%).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Riparian corridors were defined per [[Lowe 1985]] as vegetation associations occurring in or adjacent to drainages/floodplains, structurally and compositionally distinct from surrounding upland climax vegetation — broader than the strict statutory definition requiring deep-rooted, water-table-dependent species (A.R.S. 45-101.6).

Key niche/habitat findings:
- **Perennial stream riparian area (final SRIM estimate):** 165,263 acres (66,908 ha) across 10 vegetation classes — a 38% reduction from the earlier [[Valencia Et Al 1993]] estimate, largely due to corrected riparian/upland boundary delineation.
- **Elevational zonation:** Low-elevation sites (tamarisk, cottonwood-willow, mesquite) transition to mixed broadleaf communities at mid-elevations, and to conifer-oak, mountain shrub, and wet meadow communities at high elevations — a gradient with direct implications for [[Coldwater Fish Habitat]] vs. [[Warmwater Fish Habitat]] partitioning.
- **Narrow riparian corridors:** 86% of montane riparian areas were narrower than the 197-ft (60 m) minimum mapping unit and could not be resolved by remote sensing — indicating that narrow, high-gradient streams (often supporting native trout and other coldwater fish) are systematically underrepresented in landscape-scale habitat inventories.
- **Intermittent streams:** A newly compiled GIS database identified ~620 miles (1,000 km) of intermittent streams in Arizona — 25% more than the mapped perennial stream network — with 54% in the high-elevation "low contrast zone" and 46% in the lower-elevation "high contrast zone." Intermittent streams are ecologically significant as seasonal connectivity corridors and refugia for native desert fishes.
- **Streamside vegetation density:** In the low contrast (high-elevation) intermittent zone, only 34% of stream miles (2,134 of 3,243 mi surveyed) exhibited streamside vegetation denser/greener than adjacent uplands, suggesting riparian buffering (shade, bank stability, allochthonous input relevant to aquatic food webs) is patchy and fragmented along many intermittent reaches.

## Key Findings

1. **Perennial stream riparian/upland classification accuracy**: 87% (1,461 of 1,671 sampled polygons correctly classified as riparian vs. upland) using combined TM satellite imagery + aerial videography.
2. **Detailed vegetation series classification accuracy**: Only 35% correct under the original 17-class system ([[Valencia Et Al 1993]]); improved to 45% after consolidating to 10 classes. The SRIM maps should be used cautiously for detailed vegetation-class distribution.
3. **Area correction**: ~930 acres (376 ha) removed from prior perennial riparian estimates due to narrow (<197 ft) montane riparian zones unresolvable by remote sensing; overall statewide perennial riparian area revised down by ~101,500 acres (41,093 ha, 38%).
4. **Intermittent stream ("high contrast area") TM satellite classification** performed poorly: only 58.8% overall accuracy, with user accuracy of just 42.3% under the strict "TM Satellite" method (Table 2).
5. **Relaxed spatial-matching approaches** improved apparent accuracy: a "non-site-specific" pixel-adjacency method raised user accuracy to >80%, but independent verification against USGS topographic maps justified only 26% of these reversals, dropping true accuracy to 62.8% — below the 80% target threshold.
6. **Aerial videography in the "low contrast" (high-elevation intermittent) zone** performed well for coarse cover discrimination:
   - Distinguishing <25% vs. ≥25% vegetative cover: 95% accuracy.
   - Distinguishing riparian-obligate/facultative vegetation from upland vegetation of similar canopy density: only 55.6% accuracy.
   - Distinguishing combined low-cover classes from combined denser-vegetation classes: 100% accuracy, leading to a simplified two-class scheme (denser/greener streamside vegetation vs. not).
7. **Technology limitations**: TM satellite pixel resolution (98 ft/30 m at time of study) was too coarse relative to the narrow, linear geometry of riparian corridors, causing "hybrid reflectance" misclassification. Newer imagery (16 ft/5 m resolution) is identified as a major improvement opportunity for future riparian and, by extension, aquatic habitat mapping.
8. **Field crew consistency**: Ground crews agreed on riparian vs. upland plant designation ≥85% of the time overall, and 100% for nine dominant plant species, establishing a reliable groundtruthing baseline.
9. **Management recommendations** included developing a [[Riparian Vulnerability Index]] (analogous to an Aquifer Vulnerability Index), integrating [[Heritage Data Management System]] records with hydrologic/riparian GIS layers, and using buffer algorithms to constrain intermittent-stream riparian designations — all relevant infrastructure for future fish and aquatic habitat conservation modeling in Arizona.
