# Razorback Sucker Status in the Lower Colorado River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

A 1981 trammel and hoop net survey by the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] assessed the status of the [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*) along roughly 170 miles of the [[Lower Colorado River]], from Parker Dam south to Morelos Dam. Despite sampling timed to the January–May spawning period and placed near probable spawning substrates, no razorback suckers were captured. The fish community was dominated by exotic species, with only [[Striped Mullet]] and [[Tenpounder]] representing native or migrant marine taxa. The authors conclude the population persists at severely diminished levels, undetectable by reasonable sampling effort, and call for coordinated management action to prevent further decline.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a native Colorado River catostomid. It was listed as endangered by California and Nevada and was a candidate for federal listing at the time of the report. The [[Colorado River Fishes Recovery Team]] recommended threatened status in the upper basin and evaluation for listing in the lower basin. A 1981 memorandum of understanding between the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 2) and the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] launched a ten-year stocking program in the Gila, Salt and Verde rivers. Federal listing was deferred until that reintroduction could be evaluated in 1991.

Decline has been documented since the early 1950s. Principal causes cited are dams and other riverine modifications that altered the historical physical, chemical and biological character of the river, and predation by introduced exotic fishes on early life stages. Channelization associated with floodplain development is also implicated.

Survey methods included:
- **Gear:** Trammel nets (inner wall 5.1 cm square monofilament mesh; outer wall 30.5 cm mesh) and baited hoop nets (5.1 cm mesh, 4.9 m long, 1.2 m throat diameter).
- **Effort:** Overnight sets at approximately 1.5 km intervals, with 2–4 trammel nets and 4–6 hoop nets per set.
- **Mapping:** A [[Lowrance Sonar]] depth recorder (accurate to 0.3 m) was used to graph about 125 miles of main channel, with cross-sectional transects every river mile and longitudinal transects every 3,800 m.
- **Analysis:** Gear efficiency was compared using the [[Mann-Whitney U Test]] at P = 0.05.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The lower river is divided by the [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]] into 10 administrative divisions; seven were surveyed (Havasu, Parker, Palo Verde, Cibola, Imperial, Laguna, Yuma). Downstream of the Imperial Division, most flow is diverted through the [[All-American Canal]] and the [[Gila Main Canal]], and the river averages about 49 m wide and 1.5 m deep, resembling a canal. Flows above about 51,000 cfs reduced trammel net efficiency.

**Water quality and flow.** Organic drift (detritus and aquatic plants such as *Typha*, *Myriophyllum* and *Potamogeton*) increased in spring and reduced trammel efficiency. Upstream reservoirs release cold hypolimnetic water, which delays summer warming, and flow regulation has removed historical spring flood peaks.

**Spawning habitat.** Suitable spawning substrate is extremely limited and occurs only in the Parker and Havasu Divisions. Senator Wash Reservoir has coarse, angular pyroclastic gravels in the shoreline zone, and razorbacks there spawned along the same short shoreline sections in consecutive years. Temperatures of 12–20 °C were similar at spawning areas in Senator Wash Reservoir and Lake Mohave.

**Predators and competitors.** Introduced [[Channel Catfish]], [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Bluegill]] are widespread and established. [[Cichlids]] and [[Flathead Catfish]] are expanding upstream, and predation on eggs and larvae is believed to be extensive.

## Key Findings

- **No razorback suckers were captured** after 1,176 hours of trammel net effort, 1,324 hours of hoop net effort and graphing of about 125 river miles.
- **Fish community:** Composition was similar across divisions and dominated by exotic species. The four most abundant species were carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), bluegill, largemouth bass and channel catfish.
- **Native and migrant taxa:** Striped mullet (*Mugil cephalus*; 25 specimens, mean TL 41.3 cm, mean weight 0.78 kg) and tenpounder (*Elops affinis*) occurred only in the Yuma Division. A 39-specimen juvenile tenpounder group was recorded from Rockwood Heading in November 1979.
- **Sporadic records:** Juvenile and adult razorback suckers have been documented from Parker Dam to the Coachella Canal terminus, suggesting reproduction still occurs within or upstream of the study area. In the 3 to 5 years before this survey, routine sampling found razorbacks only in Senator Wash Reservoir, where adult numbers were estimated at fewer than 200 in 1981.
- **Gear complementarity:** Trammel nets sampled open-water species well, while hoop nets were more effective for redear sunfish, bluegill, black crappie and bullheads. Gear efficiency did not differ significantly within most divisions (the Imperial Division was the exception).
- **Habitat change:** Bank stabilization has confined the channel and prevented floodplain inundation, reducing backwater and gravel-bar spawning habitat. Remaining backwaters are silting and succeeding to marsh.
- **Temperature:** Razorback egg development is impaired at 10 °C, 15 °C and 30 °C but normal at 20 °C. Adults prefer 22.9–28.8 °C.
- **Recruitment bottleneck:** Successful larval-stage reproduction has been documented, but recruitment beyond the larval stage is limited by slow growth, predation and restricted spawning habitat.
- **Causal summary:** Slow growth to sexual maturity, low adult abundance, multiple spawning on restricted habitats and predation on early life stages together yield very limited reproductive potential.
- **Recommendations:** The authors urge a moratorium on exotic fish introductions, habitat enhancement and mitigation for proposed projects, adjusted size and bag limits on exotic sportfish, and a major reintroduction and monitoring program in the lower river.
