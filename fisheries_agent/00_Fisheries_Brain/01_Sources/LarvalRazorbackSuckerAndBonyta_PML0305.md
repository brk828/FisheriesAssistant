# Larval Razorback Sucker and Bonytail Survival and Growth with Nonnative Fish in the Baeser Floodplain Wetland

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae #cyprinidae #endangered-fish

## Executive Summary

This Utah Division of Wildlife Resources final report, prepared by Ronald E. Brunson and Kevin D. Christopherson (October 2005) for the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]], tests how many [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail]] larvae must enter a reset [[Floodplain Wetland]] for measurable survival to occur in the presence of abundant [[Nonnative Fish]] predators. The study used twelve 1/8-acre [[Enclosure Experiment|enclosures]] at the [[Baeser Floodplain Wetland]] on the [[Green River]]. Larval survival was detected at densities as low as 400 larvae/acre, and survival was documented in both 2003 and 2004. The authors recommend managing floodplains under the [[Reset Theory]] and working toward entrainment of at least 400 larvae/acre.

## Background & Description

**Context.** The Recovery Program began [[Floodplain Restoration]] on the Green River in 1996 by mechanically lowering levees (see [[Levee Removal]]) to increase river–floodplain connection. Floodplain wetlands are considered critical rearing habitat for endangered razorback sucker. Nonnative fish such as fathead minnow, black bullhead, green sunfish and common carp flourish in these wetlands and may suppress survival of the rarer native species. Drought can eliminate fish populations in a wetland ("reset"), so initial fish densities at the next inundation are low.

**Study objectives.**
1. Evaluate survival and growth of larval razorback sucker across a range of stocking densities in enclosures with nonnative predators.
2. Evaluate survival and growth of larval razorback sucker and bonytail stocked together at equal densities.

**Study area.** The Baeser Bend floodplain, administered by the [[Bureau of Land Management]], lies near river mile 273 on the east bank of the Green River. A lateral breach allows the floodplain to inundate about 38 acres at 13,000 cfs. The twelve enclosures were built on the east shore and extended to the wetland center.

**Enclosure design.** Each enclosure used field fence and steel t-posts framed with 7-mm hardware cloth, a layer of 1-mm mesh screen, and an outer poly-tarp. The bottom of the screen was buried 30 cm, and the tarp was anchored with rebar.

**Larval sources.** Bonytail and razorback sucker larvae came from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (and a second hatchery for razorback sucker, named in the source as "ayy National Fish Hatchery," likely a scan error).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water quality.** Water depth was held at about 0.6 m. Recorded temperatures ranged from 18 to 32 °C, which the authors judged suitable for growth of young-of-the-year warm-water fish. Dissolved oxygen rarely fell below 5.0 mg/L during sampled periods. Zooplankton were very abundant both inside and outside the enclosures. Fresh water was pumped in from the river when quality or depth threatened fish.

**Hydrology and disturbance.** In 2003, unpredicted high flows (about 19,000 cfs) overtopped and inundated the enclosures. This let nonnative fish enter the controls, so the planned control enclosures without nonnative fish could not be maintained. Water levels then stayed high enough that additional pumping was needed.

**Nonnative community.** In 2003, the natural invaders were dominated by young-of-the-year [[Common Carp]], followed by [[Red Shiner]] and [[Fathead Minnow]]. [[Green Sunfish]], [[Black Bullhead]], juvenile [[White Sucker]] and [[Sand Shiner]] together made up less than 1% of the initial catch. In 2004, nonnative fish were stocked at fixed numbers per enclosure (38 fathead minnows, 21 red shiners, 8 black bullheads, 12 green sunfish and 2 carp) based on earlier [[Stirrup Floodplain Wetland]] work (Christopherson et al. 2004).

## Key Findings

### Methods Summary

- **Sampling:** Enclosures were netted repeatedly with three (2003) or multiple [[Fyke Net]]s and abundance was estimated by [[Depletion Estimation]] with linear regression, with 95% confidence intervals. The 2003 sampling ran 5–29 August; the 2004 sampling ran 7–23 July.
- **Growth:** Calculated as the difference between mean length at capture and at stocking, divided by days in the enclosure.
- **Escapement checks:** Fyke nets placed outside the enclosures captured only nonnative fish and one bonytail in 2003. In 2004 eight nets captured only nonnative fish, but 31 bonytail escaped into an adjacent enclosure.

### Razorback Sucker Survival and Growth

- **2003:** Survival was highly variable, from zero in several enclosures (18,000, 1,200 and 800 larvae/acre) to about 11% in an enclosure stocked at 36,000 larvae/acre. The authors describe these estimates as conservative because of escapement.
- **2003 growth:** Larvae averaged 49 mm after 64 days, with growth averaging 0.6 mm/day (range 0.40 at 18,000/acre to 0.92 at 4,000/acre).
- **2004:** Razorback sucker survival was confirmed in all twelve enclosures. Survival ranged from about 5% to 58% (the text gives an OCR-garbled range), and the authors note that one 800 larvae/acre enclosure had a survival estimate significantly higher than the others (Student's t, P < 0.01).
- **2004 growth:** Larvae averaged 54 mm (range 25–92 mm) at removal on 13 July, with growth averaging 0.6 mm/day (range 0.5 at 40,000/acre to 0.7 at 400/acre).
- **Recovered totals:** 616 surviving razorback sucker were collected in 2003 and 1,095 in 2004. About 1,000 young-of-the-year were moved to the Stirrup wetland in 2004.

### Nonnative Fish Dynamics

- **2003:** Nonnative fish abundance ranged from 3,993 to 15,216 across enclosures. Higher nonnative densities did not clearly reduce razorback sucker survival.
- **2003 final biomass:** About 75% common carp, 20% fathead minnow, 3% black bullhead and 2% green sunfish.
- **2004:** Final nonnative densities reached 48,705–209,819 fish/acre. Carp numbers exploded, which the authors attribute to age-0 carp entering through seams and adults jumping the enclosure walls when water stood at the top of the walls. The 2004 biomass was about 71% carp, 11% fathead minnow and 12% green sunfish.

### Bonytail Survival and Growth

- **2003:** Two enclosures received 2,000 bonytail larvae before high flows. Only 10 bonytail were captured in eight enclosures, measuring 57–83 mm (mean 70 mm) with growth of 0.6 mm/day. Survival was confirmed despite escapement.
- **2004:** At 8,000 larvae/acre, sympatric bonytail survival was 15.6% and 16.5% (about 16–17%). Bonytail measured 21–76 mm (mean 32.4 mm) with growth of 0.4 mm/day. Roughly 200 surviving bonytail were moved to the Stirrup wetland.

### Principal Conclusions

- Larval razorback sucker survival was detected 88 days after introduction at a density as low as 400 larvae/acre.
- Bonytail survival was detected 106 days after introduction in 2003 and 66 days after introduction in 2004 (sympatric with razorback sucker at 8,000 larvae/acre).
- Survival estimates indicate fall survival of larval razorback sucker is largely independent of nonnative fish densities, which the authors read as supporting the Reset Theory.
- Escapement from the enclosures makes the survival estimates conservative, though the authors judged that it did not change their conclusions.

### Recommendations

1. Identify, monitor and manage the variables needed to entrain at least 400 larvae/acre into floodplain wetlands. These include adequate spawning adults, wetland proximity to spawning areas, survival of drifting larvae, and river flow dynamics that bring larvae into wetlands.
2. Continue managing floodplains under the Reset Theory, since these habitats remain important for endangered fish even with abundant nonnative predators.
3. In an average or high-flow year, stock at least 400 larvae/acre into reset sites to test the theory under more natural conditions.
4. Evaluate connection configuration, location and river discharge to improve larval entrainment, and assess the conditions needed for razorback sucker and bonytail to recruit back to the river.

### Limitations

The authors acknowledge that the enclosures were not fully secure. Overtopping in 2003 and carp entry in 2004 mean the nonnative community was not controlled as planned, and the 2003 controls failed. Several survival and growth figures in the OCR text are garbled, and the report contains internal inconsistencies between the Executive Summary, Results and Conclusions. Readers should check the original PDF for exact values.
