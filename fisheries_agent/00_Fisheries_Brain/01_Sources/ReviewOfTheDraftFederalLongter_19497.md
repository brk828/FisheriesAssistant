# Review of the Draft Federal Long-Term Monitoring Plan for the Colorado River Below Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #riverine-ecosystem

## Executive Summary

This National Research Council (NRC) report reviews the Bureau of Reclamation's Draft Federal Long-Term Monitoring Plan for the Colorado River between [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and [[Lake Mead]], a reach of roughly 255 miles through Grand Canyon. The review was prepared by the Committee to Review the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES), sponsored by the Bureau of Reclamation under Cooperative Agreement 6-FC-40-04240, and published in 1994. The committee judges that the draft plan will be important as a model for regulated river monitoring, but that it "does not yet meet" criteria of being well designed, clearly explained and efficient. Its main shortcomings are the absence of cost estimates, firm research recommendations, administrative strategy, and sufficiently specific sampling frequencies, sites and methods.

The appended draft plan (Appendix B, Duncan T. Patten, May 1993, advance draft) defines the monitoring program in response to the [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] of 1992.

## Background & Description

Glen Canyon Dam was completed in 1963 under authority of the [[Colorado River Storage Project Act]] of 1956. Its operating rules, based on water delivery and peaking hydropower, were broadened by the [[Colorado River Basin Project Act]] of 1968 and the Grand Canyon Protection Act of 1992, which directs protection and mitigation of natural and cultural resources in [[Grand Canyon National Park]] and [[Glen Canyon National Recreation Area]].

The GCES program, begun in 1982, expanded from the immediate dam area to the full 255-mile reach by 1986. Annual spending peaked at $11–12 million in 1990–1993. Work was carried out by the Bureau of Reclamation, National Park Service, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Western Area Power Administration, Arizona Game and Fish Department, U.S. Geological Survey, Indian tribes and consultants.

The NRC committee (chaired by William M. Lewis, Jr.) was asked in 1986 to review GCES, and in October 1992 convened a workshop of 50 invited participants to inform the GCES senior scientist drafting the plan.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The draft plan identifies ten critical attributes for monitoring:

1. Quantity and quality of water from Lake Powell and in the canyon
2. Sediment dynamics and sediment budget
3. Fish (aquatic food base, native and non-native fishes including trout)
4. Vegetation (woody riparian plants, emergent marsh plants)
5. Wildlife and wildlife habitat
6. Endangered and special status species, including [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]]
7. Cultural resources
8. Recreation
9. Powerplant supply of hydropower
10. Non-use valuation

Key ecological context noted in the plan:

- The Colorado River is described as "the most heavily regulated large river in North America" since closure of the dam.
- [[Tributaries]] such as the Paria and [[Little Colorado River]] supply most sediment; the plan notes Moenkopi Wash is unmeasured.
- Tributaries serve as relatively pristine refugia for native fishes and riparian systems and as "control" sites.
- Riparian vegetation falls into three communities: old high water zone (OHWZ), new high water zone (NHWZ) and near-shoreline marshes; only NHWZ and marshes are directly affected by dam operations.
- Beach-building flows would exceed 30,000 cfs, possibly considerably higher, and could substantially reconfigure sediment deposits.
- Lake Powell measurements showed high concentrations of mercury and selenium.
- The 1989 [[Church Rock Uranium Mill Spill]] released about 100 million gallons of mixed solids and liquids, including thorium-230 and radium-222, into the Puerco and lower Colorado river system.
- The inner canyon is managed as wilderness under the 1964 Wilderness Act, which the committee cites in calling for minimal visibility of monitoring equipment.

## Key Findings

**Committee conclusions on the draft plan**

- The plan is "confusing" and needs extensive revision for clarity, logical cohesion and specificity. Writing is described as convoluted, with vague terms such as "attribute inputs," "visitations," "statistical reliability" and "value evaluation."
- The plan lacks cost estimates and should tabulate costs for each data type; the committee notes that an arbitrary budget could render the program ineffective.
- Research is weakly specified. The committee recommends a continuously funded research program complementary to monitoring, with its approximate size stated.
- Administration is undefined. The committee opposes control by a federal cooperators' committee and recommends an administratively independent program that issues contracts, archives data and coordinates review, with open competitive contracting.
- The plan ties objectives too narrowly to the preferred alternative of the draft Environmental Impact Statement; the committee recommends an objective of assessing change under any operating regime.

**Specific technical recommendations**

- **Lake Powell:** Quarterly forebay sampling is insufficient; sampling should be at least monthly. Nitrogen and phosphorus fractions need specification, and taxon-specific zooplankton and phytoplankton enumeration should not be replaced by chlorophyll a.
- **Water chemistry:** Quarterly measurements are inadequate; sampling should be at least bimonthly and probably monthly. Nitrogen fractions (total N, nitrate N, dissolved organic N) and phosphorus fractions (particulate P, total soluble P, soluble reactive P) should be measured, along with chlorophyll a and algal counts.
- **Gaging:** The committee sees no need for a new gage, and recommends retaining all existing gages. The National Canyon gage is the least important but should stay given its established record, though the draft's discussion of removing it should be deleted.
- **Sediment quality:** Suspended and bed sediments should be sampled annually at a few locations for chemical analysis; fish tissue should be assessed annually for contaminants.
- **Sediment transport from tributaries:** The plan should not bind the program to a specific agency such as the [[U.S. Geological Survey]].
- **Non-invasive methods:** The committee supports reducing permanent physical installations, particularly cableways, and suggests alternatives such as eye bolts, remote sensing, time-lapse photography and automated data loggers, with a specific transition date.
- **Fishes:** Monitoring of trout should go beyond creel census and guide surveys to include growth rates, feeding habits, distribution, age structure and spawning success. The committee calls for stronger attention to endangered fish welfare, given U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service reservations about daily fluctuating flows.
- **Vegetation:** The plan appears to confuse "quadrants" with "quadrats," and "statistically significant number of sampling sites" needs a definite sample size.
- **Cultural resources:** Monitoring should be selective, historic sites should receive substantial weight alongside prehistoric sites, and "visitations" should be replaced by standard methodology.
- **Recreation and power:** Commitments to specific data on recreation, hydropower and economics are needed rather than deferral to other agencies. Nonuse values deserve a specific subheading and more extensive discussion.

**Points from the draft plan itself (Appendix B)**

- Monitoring is defined as the "repetition of measurements over time for the purpose of detecting change," distinct from inventories and research.
- The geographic scope runs from the [[Lake Powell]] forebay to Separation Canyon (River Mile 240), the generally accepted head of Lake Mead; the lateral extent is at least the 100,000 cfs elevation.
- Thirteen GIS reaches of 2 to 12 miles were established by GCES, with topographic data at 1:2400.
- Sediment budgets would be computed for three reaches: Lees Ferry to the Little Colorado River, Little Colorado River to Bright Angel Creek, and Bright Angel Creek to Diamond Creek.
- Fish sampling: adults of long-lived taxa annually, moving to a four-year cycle as information improves; short-lived species and young-of-year twice annually.
- Cultural sites in the most impacted category would be monitored quarterly, with about 20% of other sites visited annually; sites with rapid degradation weekly by time-lapse camera.
- Recreation data would be compiled annually, with recreationist values surveyed every five years or after unusual events.

**Summary of the committee's final recommendations (seven in total)**

1. Include a comprehensive table listing information types, frequency, and number and location of sites.
2. Broaden objectives to cover any operating regime, not only the preferred alternative.
3. Increase frequency of seasonal or quarterly measurements.
4. Specify a complementary research program.
5. Assign the long-term program direct, perpetual responsibility for specific data, without delegation to agencies.
6. Emphasize non-invasive methods and remove permanent cableways.
7. Propose administration, review, contracting mechanisms and cost estimates.
