## Executive Summary

This document is a **peer review/commentary** (by Robert G. Howells) on a March 1990 management proposal by Gustaveson, Maddux, and Bonebrake evaluating the introduction of a forage fish species — primarily **[[Rainbow Smelt]]** (or an alternative **[[Pygmy Smelt]]** dwarf form) — into **[[Lake Powell]]** to support an unstable **[[Striped Bass]]** fishery whose natural reproduction has outpaced the existing forage base (dominated by **[[Threadfin Shad]]**). The review does not present new empirical morphological or physiological data but critically evaluates the proposal's ecological assumptions, alternative forage options, and risk factors, especially concerning potential impacts on endemic **threatened and endangered fishes** in Lake Powell's tributary streams. The reviewer remains cautiously skeptical that a single-species smelt introduction will durably resolve the forage deficit problem, and stresses the need for baseline temperature-tolerance data and long-term monitoring.

## Background & Description

No direct morphological data (meristics, body proportions, coloration) are provided in this source, as it is a management review rather than a taxonomic or anatomical study. Relevant size/behavioral distinctions discussed include:

- **[[Rainbow Smelt]]**: Standard-sized forage smelt being considered for introduction; concern raised that predation by very large **[[Striped Bass]]** on standard rainbow smelt could mirror the severe depletion previously observed in **[[Threadfin Shad]]** populations.
- **[[Pygmy Smelt]]**: A dwarf morphotype/option proposed as an alternative or co-introduced species. Smaller body size is noted to increase vulnerability to predation by small predators but may paradoxically offer *some protection* against selective predation by very large striped bass, which may preferentially target larger, energetically efficient prey items rather than expend excess energy foraging for smaller pygmy smelt.
- **[[Gizzard Shad]]**: Noted to grow quickly to a large body size, which was a stated reason for its rejection as a forage candidate; the reviewer challenges this reasoning, arguing striped bass in large reservoirs should be capable of consuming even large gizzard shad.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Reservoir setting**: Lake Powell functions as a large inland reservoir supporting an artificial predator-prey forage system centered on introduced **[[Striped Bass]]** as the apex sport predator.
- **Forage base instability**: Existing forage (**[[Threadfin Shad]]**) has been severely suppressed by striped bass predation, creating a single-predator/single-prey system described as inherently unstable — compounded by potential additional predation from **[[Walleye]]** on any introduced smelt.
- **Endangered species interactions**: The proposal argues limited competition between smelt and endemic **threatened and endangered fishes** within the main reservoir body; however, the reviewer raises concern that smelt could range into **tributary streams**, where interaction risk with endangered fishes (and potential to block species recovery) may be substantially higher than reservoir-proper observations suggest.
- **Multi-species forage complex**: A mixed clupeid–smelt forage assemblage is discussed as potentially more ecologically stable over the long term than a single-species introduction, though it may favor adult striped bass over juveniles — a tradeoff the reviewer considers potentially beneficial given overproduction of striped bass relative to forage supply.
- **Species interactions**:
  - **[[Smallmouth Bass]]** introduction is suggested as ecologically better suited to Lake Powell than **[[Largemouth Bass]]**, potentially developing into a viable long-term fishery.
  - **[[Gizzard Shad]]** are reconsidered as a forage candidate; unlike threadfin shad (fully consumable across size classes), gizzard shad could maintain a persistent adult spawning stock producing continuous smaller forage recruits, but pose greater risk of negative interaction with endemic endangered fishes.
  - Potential antagonistic interaction between introduced smelt and threadfin shad is flagged as unaddressed — smelt could either relieve predation pressure on shad (enabling shad recovery) or act as an additional competitor/predator further suppressing shad populations.

## Key Findings

- **Smallmouth bass** introduction viewed favorably as a longer-term angling opportunity substitute for largemouth bass/crappie fisheries.
- **Endangered species risk** is understated for tributary streams; reservoir-proper assumptions of low smelt-endangered fish competition may not extrapolate to stream habitats.
- **Multi-species forage complex** option is more thoroughly and favorably addressed in this revised proposal than in the original draft; may offer greater long-term forage stability despite added complexity in impact prediction.
- **Pygmy smelt** could serve a risk-mitigation role if co-introduced with rainbow smelt, buffering against a collapse scenario similar to that experienced by threadfin shad under striped bass predation.
- **Rejected management options** (increasing reservoir productivity, stocking additional threadfin shad, sterile/triploid striped bass, striped bass removal via gill netting, shad nursery areas) are generally agreed upon as impractical or ecologically pointless, consistent with the original proposal's conclusions.
- **Striped bass population control** via selective gill netting during spawning or removal of spawning-area protections is recommended to reduce natural striped bass reproduction, which is argued to exceed the reservoir's forage-carrying capacity.
- **Gizzard shad rejection reconsidered**: The reviewer argues against premature rejection, noting interaction risk with endangered fishes — not rapid growth to large size — is the more valid rejection criterion.
- **Critical data gaps identified**:
  - Absence of laboratory-derived **temperature tolerance data** for rainbow smelt, with reliance on field-based inference of upper thermal limits considered insufficient and potentially underestimating true tolerance.
  - No long-term empirical data exist on co-occurrence/sustainability of landlocked **striped bass and rainbow smelt** in inland reservoir systems, raising uncertainty about whether smelt could sustain striper populations without following the threadfin shad collapse trajectory.
- **Illegal translocation risk**: High likelihood that anglers will informally transport smelt to other water bodies; recommends restricting live smelt possession to the reservoir proper and extending existing public relations campaigns discouraging unauthorized forage fish relocation.
- **Overall conclusion**: The reviewer remains skeptical that a rainbow smelt introduction alone will resolve Lake Powell's striped bass forage deficit or that smelt will remain ecologically confined to the reservoir as intended. Comparisons to precedent systems (Santee Cooper, Lake Texoma) indicate striped bass can impose major forage stress even where they do not naturally reproduce. Recommends close post-introduction monitoring to inform future forage-fish introduction decisions elsewhere.
