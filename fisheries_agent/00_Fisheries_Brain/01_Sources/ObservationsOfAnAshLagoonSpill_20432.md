# Observations of an Ash Lagoon Spill on the New River, Virginia

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology

## Executive Summary

On 23 July 1973 a collapse of the retaining wall of a [[Flyash Lagoon]] at a synthetic fibers plant near Pearisburg, Virginia, released an estimated 7 million liters of slightly alkaline water (pH = 7.7) and ash into the [[New River]]. The spill offered a rare chance to collect fishes and macroinvertebrates immediately before and after the impact. Species diversity and relative abundance indicated that fishes and macroinvertebrates were little affected, and water chemistry was projected to remain within known tolerance ranges of the local biota.

## Background & Description

- **Source:** Ash (flyash and bottom ash) from burning about 1,400 tons of coal per day at a synthetic fibers plant, transported to the storage lagoon by an open, once-through system using New River water. Because water was not recycled, high concentrations of [[Calcium Hydroxide]] were avoided, unlike the highly alkaline (pH 12–12.7) closed-loop lagoon studied at the [[Clinch River]] flyash spill.
- **Discharge:** Normal lagoon discharge exceeded 3.8 million liters per day; the released volume included reserve water and ash when the retaining wall ruptured. Input was diverted to a standby lagoon immediately.
- **Study river:** The New River in the study area has a mean summer discharge of about 70 m³/s and an annual mean of 130 m³/s. Width averages 100 m and depth about 2 m. Daily discharge can fluctuate by as much as 40 m³/s due to hydroelectric operation of [[Claytor Lake Dam]], 80 km upstream.
- **Dispersion theory:** Pollutant dispersal depends on the pollutant's nature, environmental conditions (e.g., wind), longitudinal dispersion physics, discharge manner, and decay of peak concentration. Eddy zones behind bed irregularities trap and slowly release pollutant, producing "stretch-out."
- **Recovery theory:** Stream recovery depends on stress severity and duration, associated stresses, recolonization by biota, and residual effects on non-biological units such as substrate.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Suspended ash effects** (after Cairns, 1968) include abrasion, sediment blanketing, reduced light penetration, substrate for bacterial growth, adsorption of chemicals, and dampened temperature fluctuations.
- **Water chemistry:** pH is a poor indicator in this river because it often oscillates above 8.0 naturally; [[Total Alkalinity]] (as mg/L CaCO₃) was used to track the spill.
- **Benchmark tolerance:** Flyash discharge parameters, projected from 1971–1972 lagoon effluent data, fell within tolerance limits of fishes and macroinvertebrates common to the New River.
- **Study stations:** Five stations were sampled, from a reference site 0.8 km above the lagoon to a site 14 km downstream at Glen Lyn, Virginia. The spill site was the "Turnhole," 0.2 km below the mouth of Stillhouse Branch.

## Key Findings

- **Dispersal rate:** The wash load traveled at about **1.4 km/hr** from the spill area to the most downstream station, usually ahead of changes in soluble calcium carbonate measured as total alkalinity.
- **Timeline:** The rupture occurred at 0945 on 23 July 1973. Ash reached Station 3 by early afternoon, Station 4 by 1625 and Station 5 by 2000. Stations 2 and 3 showed substantial visible recovery by 0630 on 24 July, while Stations 4 and 5 remained affected until suspended material tapered off in the following hours.
- **Chemistry normalization:** pH and total alkalinity returned to normal within 36 hours at all sampling stations.
- **Fish community:** A total of **29 fish species** was collected at Stations 4 and 5 before the slug of spilled water passed. Collections from 24 July through September 1973 yielded the same species with similar relative abundance. No dead fish were observed.
- **Historical comparison:** The authors cite [[Cairns (1968)]], who recorded 28 fish species in a Kansas River reach in 1958 at turbidities exceeding 72,000 mg/L without gill clogging or other damage.
- **Laboratory controls:** Control fish at a [[Temperature Preference]] wet lab at Glen Lyn, Virginia, were held in aquaria that received settled ash to 2.5 cm depth. No mortality occurred, supporting the field results.
- **Macroinvertebrates:** Qualitative collections 1–3 weeks after the spill showed diversity, measured by the [[Sequential Comparison Index]], was little affected. Biomass could not be assessed, and effects appeared minor even immediately below the spill site.
- **Residual ash:** Settled ash persisted in zones of separation for about two weeks, but was flushed quickly from riffle habitats.
- **Conclusion:** Any effects on biota were judged negligible. The calculated dispersion rates are presented as useful inputs for modeling waste dispersion in the New River and similar streams.
