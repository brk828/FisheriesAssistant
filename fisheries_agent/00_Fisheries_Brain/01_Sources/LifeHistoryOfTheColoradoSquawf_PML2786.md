# Colorado Squawfish and Colorado Chub Life History in the Green River, Dinosaur National Monument, 1964–1966

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the life history and ecology of the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) and the [[Colorado Chub]] (*Gila robusta*) in the Green River of [[Dinosaur National Monument]], Colorado–Utah, during 1964–1966. Both are large, endemic cyprinid minnows whose ranges have shrunk as rivers have been modified. The work followed the closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] in November 1962, which changed the river's flow and temperature regime downstream. The authors used scale-based age and growth analysis, length-weight regressions, stomach analysis, and observations of spawning. They report reduced growth after impoundment, a link between dam-altered water temperatures and reproduction, and distinct diets and growth patterns in the two chub forms.

## Background & Description

The Colorado squawfish is the largest native minnow in North America, with reported sizes of 36 kg and larger. The Colorado chub is also large. Little was known about the life history of either species before this study. The squawfish was listed by the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife among "Rare and Endangered Fish and Wildlife Species of the United States."

The Colorado chub includes two morphological variants, the "roundtail" (*G. r. robusta*) and the "bonytail" (*G. r. elegans*). Taxonomy of these forms was described as poorly understood. Young fish under about 200 mm could not be reliably assigned to either form, so they were grouped as Colorado chub in several analyses.

Background to the study includes the September 1962 rotenone treatment of the Green River upstream of the dam. This aimed to remove non-game fish before a sport fishery was established in the new reservoir. After dam closure, about 90 miles of river were inundated.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study area and methods.** Sampling was concentrated at Lodore, Echo Park, Island Park, and Split Mountain within the Monument, with supplemental stations at Little Hole, Bridgeport, and Ouray. Gear included 75- and 150-ft gill nets, seines, an electric shocker powered by a 130-volt generator, tow nets, food strainers, and a modified scoop shovel. Fish were preserved in formalin, then transferred to isopropyl alcohol. Total length and weight were recorded, and about 10 scales were taken per fish. Water temperature was monitored with [[Ryan Model D Recording Thermometers]], and U.S. Geological Survey gauges at Greendale and Jensen supplied additional data.

**Hydrology.** Before impoundment, flow peaked in June with high spring run-off. After closure, the characteristic spring high and winter low were replaced by a stabilized seasonal pattern. Below the mouth of the [[Yampa River]], flow changes were muted, and the Yampa's waters modified the dam's effect.

**Temperature.** Before impoundment, water temperatures at Greendale and Jensen rose from about 33 °F in March to a mean of about 72 °F in July. After closure, temperatures at Greendale stayed largely between 35 and 50 °F. Influence of the dam varied with summer discharge. In low-discharge years (1963, 1965), summer temperatures approximated pre-impoundment conditions. In high-discharge years (1964, 1966), temperatures stayed well below pre-impoundment levels as far downstream as Island Park and Jensen.

**Water chemistry.** Dissolved oxygen ranged from 6.4 to 8.5 ppm. Total alkalinity, mainly bicarbonates, ranged from about 65 ppm at spring run-off to 160 ppm in late summer (the lower value is OCR-ambiguous in the source). pH ranged from 7.8 to 8.6. Dam discharge was clear, but tributaries carried heavy silt. Turbidity reached up to 5,000 Jackson Turbidity Units during run-off and declined to under 100 units later in summer.

**Habitat use.** Adult squawfish were found in pools, eddies, and runs over varied bottoms, but never in fast water. Adult chubs favored pools and eddies over silt or silt-boulder bottoms and were absent from swift water. Young of both species occurred in quiet water or shallow pools over silt, sand, and sometimes gravel. Other common species included [[Speckled Dace]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Common Carp]], [[Fathead Minnow]], [[Redside Shiner]], and [[Channel Catfish]].

## Key Findings

**Distribution**
- Colorado squawfish occurred from the Yampa River mouth downstream to Ouray. Before impoundment it had also been collected between the Yampa mouth and the dam site.
- Colorado chub occurred from the Yampa mouth downstream through the Monument and was occasionally found at Lodore. Before impoundment it was present between the dam site and Lodore, but above Lodore only in 1963.

**Age and growth**
- Scales from 182 squawfish were analyzed. The oldest and largest was an 11-year-old female, 610 mm long and 2,258 g.
- Mean total length at first annulus was reported as a single value in the source (OCR-garbled). Average annual increment peaked at 73 mm in year five.
- Growth rates declined steadily across 1958–1965. In 1958, annual increment was 19% above the 8-year average. In 1965 it was 27% below average.
- No growth difference between sexes was observed.
- Scales from 333 chubs were analyzed, including 49 identified as roundtails and 67 as bonytails. The oldest roundtail was a 7-year-old female, 366 mm and 393 g. The oldest bonytail was 7 years old, 388 mm, and 422 g.
- Bonytails were longer than roundtails at successive ages above age III. Roundtails grew faster in the first year. Slower growth in post-impoundment years (1963–1965) was documented for both forms. The poorest growth was in 1965 (−13.3% roundtail, −7.6% bonytail).
- The growth decline was attributed to altered seasonal temperature patterns caused by dam operation. Two alternative explanations were offered: differential survival of fast-growing fish, and misinterpreted annuli on older fish.

**Length–weight relationships**
- The squawfish length–weight slope (3.126) was significantly higher than 3.0 (*t*-test, *P* < 0.01), indicating that weight increased slightly faster than length cubed.
- Roundtail and bonytail regressions differed significantly (analysis of covariance, all *F* values significant at *P* < 0.01). Roundtails became heavier than bonytails as body length increased.

**Diet**
- Stomachs from 198 squawfish (15–579 mm) were analyzed. Cladocerans, copepods, and chironomid larvae were important up to 50 mm. Insects dominated at 50–100 mm, after which fish became the main food.
- Fish were the only food in squawfish over 200 mm. Thirty-nine percent of stomachs from fish over 200 mm were empty. Bass tapeworm was found in 65% of those stomachs.
- Stomachs from 307 chubs (15–290 mm) were analyzed. Chironomid and mayfly nymphs dominated small fish. Larger chubs consumed terrestrial insects, including beetles, grasshoppers, and ants. Plant debris was common.
- Thirty-five percent of roundtail stomachs were empty, versus 11% of bonytail stomachs. Roundtails were opportunistic, eating fish, aquatic insects, and terrestrial insects. Bonytails fed mainly on terrestrial insects, plant debris, and filamentous algae.

**Reproduction**
- Squawfish spawning was not directly observed, but ripe and spent adults were collected in June–August. Ripe fish appeared about one month after water temperature reached 65 °F. Post-larvae of 12–20 mm were collected 3–4 weeks later.
- Squawfish reproduced successfully in all four post-closure years, but only below the Yampa confluence. Year-class strength was poor in 1965 (42 young-of-year collected) and abundant in 1963.
- Chubs spawned successfully in all four post-closure years. They did not reproduce above the Yampa in 1964 and 1966, when water temperatures stayed below the approximate 65 °F spawning threshold. An abundant chub year class was produced in 1964.
- Chub spawning in 1966 began about a month earlier than in 1964–1965, apparently because of earlier warming.

**Sex ratio and maturity**
- Sex ratio was nearly 1:1 for ages V and VI. Females were scarce after age VI, and all squawfish aged VII and older collected were male, although the source does not state how many fish this comprised.

**Impoundment effects**
- The authors concluded that reduced growth and reproduction above the Yampa were tied to altered temperature regimes. Large squawfish have not been reported in recent years. Local fishermen attributed this partly to competition with introduced channel catfish, and reported dead squawfish with catfish spines lodged in their throats.
- The environmental requirements of both species appeared to be met in the Green River below the Yampa confluence. Between Flaming Gorge Dam and Echo Park, requirements (particularly water temperature) were not being met.

**Taxonomy**
- The authors recommended treating the roundtail and bonytail as subspecies only if they interbreed in sympatry, which they did not resolve in this study. Bonytails were thought to inhabit more restricted main-channel habitat.

**Comparative data**
- The [[Sacramento Squawfish]] reached about 9 years old in the Sacramento River (Tsai and Murphy, 1950). The [[Northern Squawfish]] reached 13 years in northern Idaho (Jeppson and Platts, 1959), with rapid growth in years two and three. Northern squawfish in [[Cascade Reservoir]] reached 11 years.
- Roughly 500 bonytails were observed spawning over a gravel shelf in [[Lake Mohave]] in May 1954. A 605 mm female contained an estimated 10,000 ripe eggs.
