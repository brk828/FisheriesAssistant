# Aroostook River Salmon Restoration and Fisheries Management (1956)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 report by Regional Fishery Biologist Kendall Warner, prepared for the [[Maine Department of Inland Fisheries and Game]] and the [[Atlantic Salmon Commission]], condenses a multi-year biological and obstruction study of the Aroostook River drainage. The river is described as potentially one of Maine's best [[Atlantic Salmon]] rivers, although its salmon run had declined almost to extinction. The [[Brook Trout]] sport fishery remains large and valuable but is constrained by four obstacles: obstructions to fish migration, detrimental logging practices, fluctuating stream flow, and pollution. The report evaluates salmon and trout potential, catalogs dams and their fishway status, and recommends restoration measures, several of which concern the [[Aroostook Falls]] obstruction.

## Background & Description

**Drainage.** The [[Aroostook River]] is a major sub-drainage of the St. John River system. It drains approximately 2,440 square miles and runs about 105 miles from the forks of Munsungan and Millinocket Streams to its mouth at Fort Fairfield, Maine, entering Canada there. About 1,632 miles of tributary stream feed it, and it falls about 250 feet above Fort Fairfield. The drainage contains 156 lakes and ponds. The largest is Squapan Lake (5,120 acres), followed by Millinocket Lake (2,701 acres), Munsungan Lake (1,415 acres) and Millimagassett Lake (1,410 acres). Annual rainfall at Presque Isle averages about 34 inches. The bedrock is mainly folded and faulted Ordovician, Silurian and Devonian slates, shales, sandstones, argillites and limestones.

**Fish community.** The report lists 26 resident and anadromous species. The main ones are:

- **Coldwater game fish:** [[Brook Trout]], [[Lake Trout]], [[Landlocked Salmon]] and [[Rainbow Trout]], which was introduced several years before the survey.
- **Competitors and predators:** [[Fallfish]], [[Yellow Perch]], suckers and the [[American Eel]], which the report considers the most serious predator.
- **Anadromous species:** [[Atlantic Salmon]] (historically the main anadromous run), [[American Shad]], [[Alewife]] and [[Striped Bass]]. The last three are found only below Aroostook Falls.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat threats.** The report identifies several interacting pressures:

- **Dams and obstructions.** Log-driving and pulp-driving dams have been built since the early 1800s. Most lack fish passage, and the few fishways built were poorly designed (see [[Log-Driving Dams]]).
- **Bulldozing.** Bulldozing of streams for pulp driving destroys bank cover, pools, riffles and aquatic insect habitat. A 1952 Maine law limited bulldozing to 1,000 feet per mile in unorganized townships; this was reduced to 500 feet per mile in 1954 (see [[Bulldozing of Streams]]).
- **Cutting practices.** Clearcutting, haul-road bulldozing and removal of ground cover increase runoff, lower water levels, and cause siltation and turbidity.
- **Flow fluctuation.** Dam owners, mainly industrial interests, often close gates completely. This strands fish, raises summer temperatures, reduces insect production and crowds fish into shrinking pools (see [[Fluctuation of Stream Flow]]).
- **Lake manipulation.** Some lakes were flowed without clearing timber, and some outlets were bulldozed, dropping levels by two to three feet.
- **Pollution.** Industrial effluent, mainly from potato starch plants, is the most serious source. Domestic sewage, sawdust, pea-vinery wastes and insecticide spills also contribute (see [[Pollution]]).

**Spring and summer trout ecology.** Trout disperse and feed actively in cool spring water, then concentrate in spring holes, brook mouths and cold tributaries as main-stem temperatures rise. The report notes that the main-stem trout population may depend on the carrying capacity of these spring-influenced areas during hot weather.

## Key Findings

### Historical and recent salmon runs
- Early accounts (Atkins 1874, Anonymous 1895) describe substantial runs, with 125 salmon caught near Caribou and 200 speared near Salmon Brook and Machias in 1873, a year considered the best in a long time.
- The [[Aroostook Falls]] dam was built in 1906 and was not given a fishway until 1936. The run was almost completely blocked during those 30 years.
- Since the [[Tinker Fishway]] was built in 1936, only limited numbers have passed, and the run has never regained its former abundance.
- Recent catches include a 9 lb salmon near Caribou (1951), a 14 lb salmon from the Little Madawaska River (1955), and a fish reported over 15 lb from Umcolcus Stream (1955).

### Obstruction survey (1950–1955)
- 70 man-made obstructions and 2 natural falls were catalogued.
- Only 8 of the 70 had fishways, and only 3 of these worked satisfactorily at most water levels. The other 5 need alteration or rebuilding.
- The 62 dams without fishways received these recommendations: fishway recommended (11), removal (11), removal at end of use (6), now passable (15), minimum flow only (5), no recommendation (12), further investigation (2).

### Aroostook Falls fish passage
- The [[Tinker Dam]] at Aroostook Falls (New Brunswick) is the major barrier. It operates on an 85-foot head, and its diversion dam sits at the head of a 3,000-foot gorge.
- The Tinker fishway has 13 pools, with flows of 5 to 12 cubic feet per second. It has serious obstacles at the entrance and at a three-foot jump between pools 3 and 4.
- Flow analysis showed the tailrace is the dominant attraction at flows of 1,750 cfs or less. The gorge is superior at 3,500 cfs and above. Between these values, fish may use either route.
- Mason (1954) predicted salmon arrival at Aroostook Falls in May, with a peak in the last ten days of June and a run ending in November.
- The 1953 fishway design by H. A. Lynch was estimated at $125,000. It has 84 pools over 840 feet, compared with 75 pools over 900 feet at the Tobique fishway, and its pools are about 18% smaller in surface area and volume. The report judged it likely to be more turbulent and harder for fish to pass.
- The report concludes that a dual solution is required: rebuild the gorge fishway for the early run (May to mid-June) and build a pool-type tailrace fishway for the main run. A "blind fishway" trap, following Mason's proposal, is recommended as an interim measure.
- Summer 1955 weir trials at the tailrace were ineffective because of washouts and debris. Four salmon (9 to 18 lb) were trapped and released above the dam.

### Other dams on the main river
- [[Caribou Dam]] (about 12 ft high) has a new pool-type concrete fishway, built in 1955. The report judges the passage problem there "largely solved," provided the fishway is maintained.
- [[Sheridan Dam]] is a log crib and timber structure with a head of about 6½ feet. It blocks most of the run after mid-June, so it needs a fishway to reach spawning grounds above.
- [[Beechwood Dam]], under construction on the St. John River since June 11, 1955, is scheduled for completion in late 1957. It is about 1,600 feet long and 100 feet high, with a 60-foot head. Its upstream passage uses an elevator rather than a pool-type fishway, and the future of both the Aroostook and Tobique runs depends on it.

### Habitat suitability
- The main river has abundant spawning and nursery area. Nearly half its length is suitable nursery area and about one-third is adequate spawning area.
- Its lower 31 miles below Presque Isle have very little spawning or nursery area.
- Tributary habitat varies. [[Little Madawaska River]] is one of the most promising systems, with excellent water quality, more than 13 miles of spawning area, and negligible pollution.
- [[Presque Isle Stream]] has about 7 miles of adequate spawning area, but starch pollution and the dam must be addressed first.
- [[Salmon Brook]] was historically abundant but is now blocked by two dams and polluted by a food-processing plant.

- In 1955, overwinter survival was judged good in the Little Madawaska River, and the parr had dispersed widely.
- No fish from the 1951 plant, expected chiefly in 1955, were reported.

### Logging and water-level effects
- Bulldozing was extensive from about 1950, when virtually all of two Mooseleuk watershed brooks were bulldozed. Big Hudson Brook was bulldozed for about seven miles, and Mule Brook for about three miles.
- Bulldozing destroys shade, cover, pools and riffles, raises summer temperatures, and silts spawning gravels.
- Manual clearing of The Fork (Twenty-mile Brook) caused less bank and bottom damage and preserved some pools.
- Reduced flow is a common result of gate manipulation. Minimum-flow provisions are recommended for Millinocket, Munsungan, Mooseleuk, Squapan and Big Machias streams, among others.

### Proposed power projects
- **[[Castle Hill Project]]:** a 72-foot dam with 60-foot gross head that would flood about 5,000 acres and about 10 miles of salmon habitat, reducing main-river spawning and nursery area by about one-third.
- **[[Masardis Project]]:** a 110-foot storage dam impounding a 17,800-acre reservoir, flooding about 24 miles of the Aroostook and two miles of Mooseleuk Stream, including 14 miles of salmon spawning and nursery area.
- **[[Twelve-Mile Project]]:** a 100-foot dam on the Big Machias River that would flood about 5,800 acres and destroy most valuable salmon habitat above the dam.

### Summary and recommendations
- The report calls for rebuilding the [[Tinker Fishway]] and building a tailrace fishway at Aroostook Falls.
- It recommends fishways for Sheridan, and for rebuilt fishways at Mooseleuk, Twelve-Mile, Millinocket and Big Machias dams.
- It calls for new fishways at Limestone, Presque Isle, Mapleton, Upper McNally, Lower Hudson and Dudley Brook dams, and removal of 11 unused dams.
- It recommends minimum-flow provisions for the main tributaries, limiting bulldozing, restoring bulldozed streams, leaving uncut bank strips, and reducing starch-plant pollution.
- It recommends opposing the Castle Hill, Masardis and Twelve-Mile dams because of their effects on salmon spawning and nursery area and brook trout habitat.
