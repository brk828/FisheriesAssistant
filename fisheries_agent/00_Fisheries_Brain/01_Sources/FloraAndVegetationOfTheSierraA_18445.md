# Flora and Vegetation of the Sierra Ancha Experimental Forest, Arizona

#botany #plant-ecology #vascular-flora

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Forest Service Research Paper (RM-41, 1968) by Charles P. Pase and R. Roy Johnson describes the vegetation and vascular flora of the [[Sierra Ancha Experimental Forest]], a 12,820-acre watershed research unit in Gila County, central Arizona. The forest, part of the [[Tonto National Forest]] and set aside in 1932, spans 3,550 to 7,724 feet. The authors describe the physical setting (climate, geology, soils), seven upland vegetation types plus riparian zones, and a checklist of 726 species and 9 varieties of vascular plants. About 70 percent of the flora is southwestern or western in origin, and only 13 species (1.8 percent) are endemic to Arizona.

*Note on the source:* This is a terrestrial and riparian plant study. It contains no fish, invertebrate, plankton, or water-chemistry data. Aquatic material is limited to stream and riparian vegetation and general hydrologic remarks.

## Background & Description

The Sierra Ancha Experimental Forest is a field unit of the [[Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station]], devoted mainly to watershed research. It lies midway between Globe and Young. The paper's stated aims are to summarize the physical environment, describe each major plant association, and catalog all known vascular plants collected since the forest was established.

Plant collections date from the forest's establishment. Most specimens are deposited in the Forest Service Herbarium (Washington, D.C.) and in herbaria at the Forest Hydrology Laboratory (Tempe), Arizona State University, and the University of Arizona. Earlier work covered ferns and fern allies (Little 1938) and liverworts (Little 1939). Two introduced grasses, *Agropyron intermedium* (collected at Workman Creek) and *Poa bulbosa* (collected at Parker Creek), are reported as new to the Arizona flora.

Nomenclature follows Hitchcock (1950) for grasses, Little (1953) for trees, and Kearney and Peebles (1960) for other plants. Common names follow Kelsey and Dayton (1942).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Climate.** Three elevation zones show distinct temperature and precipitation patterns:

- **Upper zone:** Annual precipitation averages 33.4 inches, with 11.0 inches (33 percent) falling June through September. Range is 18.6 to 49.9 inches. Snow is common above 6,000 feet, and the upper Workman Creek snowpack at 7,000 feet often exceeds 4 feet.
- **Intermediate zone (4,800–6,000 ft):** Headquarters station (5,100 ft) averages 24.7 inches, with 7.5 inches (30 percent) in June–September. Range is 13.1 to 42.0 inches.
- **Lower zone (south end):** Mean rainfall is 16.2 inches (range 10.2 to 22.6 inches), with 5.7 inches (35 percent) in June–September. A spring drought usually runs from mid-April to mid-July.

The authors use median rather than mean precipitation to explain plant distribution. Their reasoning is that in arid settings a single large storm can distort the mean without much ecological effect.

**Geology and soils.** The range is a domed uplift of sedimentary, metamorphic, and igneous rock, mainly Precambrian Apache Group strata. The [[Dripping Springs Quartzite]] is 550 to 700 feet thick and produces shallow, fine-textured soils. This restricts moisture penetration and helps explain low retention storage and high water yields in the Parker and Pocket Creek watersheds. Intermediate-zone soils derive from deeply weathered diabase and support deep shrub roots. One study recorded 13 grams of chaparral roots per cubic foot at 12 feet, the maximum depth sampled. Californian chaparral shrubs were rooted to 28 feet (Hellmers et al. 1955).

**Riparian systems.** One perennial stream (Workman Creek) and two intermittent streams (Parker and Pocket Creeks) originate in the forest. Their combined length is about 8.5 miles. Parker and Pocket Creeks flow intermittently in most summers, but scattered pools and subsurface water persist along the channels. Riparian vegetation runs through all vegetation types, with [[Arizona Alder]], [[Bigtooth Maple]], and Arizona walnut dominating the upper reaches and [[Arizona Sycamore]] and Arizona walnut dominating the lower reaches.

## Key Findings

### Vegetation Types

Eight types are recognized, from high to low elevation: mixed conifer, mountain park, ponderosa pine, chaparral, oak-woodland, desert grassland, and desert shrub. The riparian type cuts across all of them. The paper's acreage table gives:

| Type | Acres | Percent |
|---|---|---|
| Conifer (pine-fir and ponderosa pine) | 3,776 | 29.5 |
| Mountain park | 30 | 0.2 |
| Oak-woodland | 131 | 1.0 |
| Chaparral | 7,302 | 57.0 |
| Desert grassland | 1,351 | 10.5 |
| Desert shrub | 162 | 1.3 |
| Riparian (est. 66 ft average width) | 68 | 0.5 |
| **Total** | **12,820** | **100.0** |

- **[[Mixed Conifer]]** (below 6,000 ft to more than 7,500 ft) is dominated by [[Douglas-Fir]], [[White Fir]], and ponderosa pine. Understory includes Gambel oak and New-Mexican locust.
- **[[Mountain Park]]** on the Middle Fork of Workman Creek has deep, fine soils and high rainfall. Grasses include Kentucky bluegrass and orchardgrass.
- **[[Ponderosa Pine]]** (about 5,500 to 7,000 ft) is the dominant tree on drier sites, with alligator juniper and Emory oak in the understory.
- **[[Oak-Woodland]]** occurs in Parker and Pocket Creeks at 4,800 to 5,300 ft, dominated by Arizona white oak and Emory oak.
- **[[Chaparral]]** develops best on diabase-derived soils at 4,500 to 6,000 ft. [[Shrub Live Oak]] often makes up 60 percent or more of woody cover. Few ring counts exceed 78 years, with no large fires in many years.
- **[[Desert Grassland]]** lies mostly on Dripping Springs Quartzite at 4,000 to 4,800 ft. Dominant perennial grasses are summer-growing gramas. Broom snakeweed is the most abundant half-shrub.
- **[[Desert Shrub]]** occupies canyon breaks at about 3,550 to 4,500 ft. Yellow paloverde, jojoba, and saguaro are characteristic.

### Flora and Distribution

The checklist lists 726 species and 9 varieties (varieties counted as separate taxa). The authors assigned each to a geographic group:

| Group | Species | Percent of flora |
|---|---|---|
| Arizonan (endemic) | 13 | 1.8 |
| Californian | 8 | 1.1 |
| Southwestern | 292 | 39.7 |
| Western United States | 220 | 29.9 |
| North American | 152 | 20.7 |
| Tropical | 3 | 0.4 |
| Introduced | 47 | 6.4 |

Southwestern and western groups together make up about 70 percent of the flora. [[Endemic Species]] include *Cimicifuga arizonica*, which is known from only two other central Arizona sites, and *Rumex orthoneurus*, which was otherwise known from only two Chiricahua Mountain collections and was found on rich, moist soil in Workman Creek.

The introduced group consists mainly of plants of disturbed sites, such as red brome, Kentucky bluegrass, and shepherd's purse.

### Checklist Summary

The checklist assigns each species to vegetation types (SDS, G, C, OW, PP, PF, MP), sites, and abundance classes (rare, occasional, frequent, abundant, very abundant). The paper acknowledges that much of this information is subjective, because collection sheets often lacked it. The list spans about 90 plant families, indexed by page. Dominant families include Compositae, Leguminosae, Gramineae, and Cactaceae. Full species lists appear in the original paper and are not reproduced here.

### Conclusions and Significance

- The forest's flora is primarily southwestern and western, reflecting its position at the edge of Mexican and Rocky Mountain distributions.
- Vegetation tracks sharp changes in temperature and precipitation across elevations.
- Shallow quartzite soils control water retention and yield, which shapes the watershed research purpose of the forest.
- Chaparral and riparian communities are important for wildlife cover (see Reynolds and Johnson 1964 for vertebrate habitat relations).
