# Rio Grande Silvery Minnow Critical Habitat: Potential Economic Consequences

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This document is the cover letter and draft report of the [[Economic Consequences of Critical Habitat Designation]] for the [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (RGSM), an endangered fish listed on July 20, 1994. The U.S. [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Service) proposed critical habitat along the main stream of the [[Middle Rio Grande]], from just below [[Cochiti Dam]] to the Atchison Topeka and Santa Fe Railroad crossing near San Marcial. The draft economic analysis, prepared by [[ECONorthwest]] in February 1996, concludes that the designation would have both negative and positive effects whose net effect is probably close to zero. The only federal agency expected to change its activities is the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BuRec), through its river-maintenance program. The cover letter (dated April 6, 1999, with comments open until May 7, 1999) states the Service's position that most economic effects attributed to the designation actually stem from the listing itself, so incremental effects of designation are expected to be minimal.

## Background & Description

**Species.** The RGSM is a small cyprinid minnow. The report notes it is the sole survivor of a guild of five species with similar habitat requirements. It once was abundant throughout the Rio Grande but now occupies roughly a 170-mile stretch of the Middle Rio Grande, about 5 percent of its historic range.

**Regulatory framework.** Under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA), listing prohibits "taking" of the species by anyone. Critical habitat designation, by contrast, affects only federal agencies, which may not fund, authorize, or carry out actions that destroy or adversely modify critical habitat. The Service states that, for most species, the "jeopardy" and "adverse modification" prohibitions are in general redundant. Economic impacts may be considered for designation but not for listing.

**Report structure.** The draft has eight chapters: a reader's guide, analytical structure, economic setting, Rio Grande and the economy, federal agency impacts, a static estimate, long-run adjustment, and overall consequences. Appendices list questionnaire recipients.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology.** The Rio Grande delivers about 1,060,000 acre-feet (af) per year to the Middle Rio Grande on average (standard deviation 525,000 af). Roughly 352,000 af per year is consumed by irrigation, municipal-industrial use, seepage, and evaporation. Years with flows below 536,000 af are classed as low-flow years. Natural flow is insufficient for all consumptive demands in about two of every ten years.

**Infrastructure.** Key structures include [[Cochiti Dam]] (Corps of Engineers, 886,000 af capacity), [[Elephant Butte Dam]] and Reservoir (BuRec, about 2 million af), [[Abiquiu Dam]], [[El Vado Dam]], the [[San Juan-Chama Project]], diversion dams at Angostura, Isleta, and San Acacia, and the [[Low-Flow Conveyance Channel]], which has been inoperable since 1985.

**Riparian ecosystem.** The [[Bosque]] is a cottonwood riparian forest described as one of the largest in the world. The report cites Naiman et al. (1993) on riparian corridors as among the most diverse habitats on Earth.

**Water law and governance.** New Mexico does not recognize instream water rights. Allocation follows [[Prior Appropriation]], with tribal, Rio Grande Compact (1938), and U.S.-Mexico Treaty (1906) obligations at the top of the hierarchy. The [[Middle Rio Grande Conservancy District]] (MRGCD) claims rights to irrigate 123,267 acres and irrigates about 64,000 acres per year.

**Subsidies.** The report asserts that federal subsidies strongly distort water use. Citing the Secretary of the Interior (1994), BuRec irrigation subsidies averaged about $30 per af in 1982 (over $50 per af in today's dollars).

## Key Findings

**1. Economic effect confined to BuRec river maintenance.** BuRec's river-maintenance program costs about $3.5 million per year (historical budget), and the designation could raise costs by up to 40 percent. If budgets fall 31 percent, the impact is estimated at $0.8–1.4 million.

**2. Water effects are small.** The designation could reduce water available for consumptive use by a maximum of 4,000 af, under 0.5 percent of inflow and about 1 percent of consumptive use. Water salvaged through river maintenance was historically up to 14,400 af per year, but the current program salvages less, and the BuRec could not quantify the current amount.

**3. Low value of affected water.** Net earnings from irrigated pasture are about $19 per af per acre, and the marginal value of federally subsidized irrigation water is estimated at zero. The estimated loss from 4,000 af in pasture is $0–76,000. If the reduction hit urban use (marginal value over $300 per af), the loss would be about $1 million.

**4. Employment effects.** The designation could cost approximately 8 agricultural jobs (about $10,000 earnings each), with the net employment effect "near zero."

**5. Recreation and amenity value.** A 1995 survey of New Mexico households (Berrens et al.) found willingness to pay of $30 per household per year for five years for instream flows. Across roughly 580,000 households, this implies about $17.4 million per year (about $8.5 million over five years for the Middle Rio Grande flows that benefit the RGSM, per the report's text). Estimated recreational fishing value of water is about $100 per af.

**6. Municipal water supply not expected to be threatened.** Municipalities can buy water rights, fund river maintenance, or use pricing and conservation, so a shortage for municipal users is judged unlikely.

**7. Overall effect near zero.** The report concludes the evidence is insufficient to determine net benefits or costs, though it "probably would yield net benefits for the nation as a whole," with the overall net effect "probably close to zero."

**8. Property rights.** The designation is not expected to affect private property rights. The MRGCD and water users have no demonstrated right to federal funds for river maintenance.

**9. Stated Service position.** The Service's April 1999 letter argues that the BuRec's concerns are attributable to listing rather than designation, so it "do[es] not expect to find any incremental economic effects associated with the designation of critical habitat above and beyond the effects of listing." Comments remained open until May 7, 1999, and a public hearing was scheduled for April 29, 1999.

**10. Regional economic context.** Agriculture accounts for about 2 percent of total employment and 1 percent of income in the study area, which is dominated by the [[Albuquerque Metropolitan Area]], [[El Paso Metropolitan Area]], [[Las Cruces Metropolitan Area]], and [[Santa Fe Metropolitan Area]]. Roughly 98 percent of the study-area population lives in these metropolitan counties.

**Limitations.** The report stresses that the quantity of available data does not indicate economic importance, and that estimates are imprecise, especially for specific sectors and locations.
