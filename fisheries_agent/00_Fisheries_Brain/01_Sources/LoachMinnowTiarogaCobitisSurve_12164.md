# Loach Minnow Survey, Fort Apache Reservation (February and June 1988)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #tiaroga-cobitis

## Executive Summary

This 1988 report from [[Western Technology And Engineering Inc]] (WESTECH) documents a search for the federally threatened [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) in the [[White River]] and two tributaries, the [[East Fork White River]] and [[North Fork White River]], on the [[Fort Apache Reservation]]. Surveys ran February 17–20 and June 6–9, 1988, for the [[White Mountain Apache Tribe]] as part of environmental studies for the proposed [[Miner Flat Dam]] and [[Canyon Day Irrigation Project]]. Sampling used backpack [[Electrofishing]] at 25 stations in total. Only 12 loach minnows were captured (1 in February, 11 in June), and 10 of the June captures came from one East Fork reach that appeared to hold a breeding population. No loach minnows were found in the North Fork, and the mainstem population appeared not to be significant.

## Background & Description

**Purpose and sponsorship.** The study was sponsored by the White Mountain Apache Tribe through a contract with [[Morrison-Maierle Inc]]. The tribe's Game and Fish Department issued the sampling permit. The report describes sampling sites, methods and results.

**Study species.** The loach minnow is a Federally listed threatened species. [[Minckley 1973]] describes it as "restricted to gravelly riffles in smaller to moderately-larger creeks and rivers." Specimens from New Mexico were reported in six inches of water or less (Bob David, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, personal communication). Most sampling therefore targeted shallow riffles, with some deeper runs and pool edges also searched.

**Personnel.** The field crew was Patrick Farmer and Carlene Farmer of WESTECH. Joe Elliott supervised the study and assisted for the first two days of the February trip. Qualifications appear in Appendix A. Patrick Farmer has experience in trout electrofishing, sedimentation studies and benthic macroinvertebrate surveys. Carlene Farmer has limnological and macroinvertebrate experience and runs a laboratory for chemical and biological investigations.

**Methods.**
- Survey routes covered the North Fork from the proposed Miner Flat Dam site to the East Fork confluence, the East Fork from a bridge in section 29 to the North Fork, and the mainstem from the forks' junction to below Canyon Day.
- A [[Coffelt BP-6 Backpack Electroshocker]] was used, which allowed thorough sampling of shallow riffles. [[Propst Et Al. 1985]] also used electroshocking in these streams.
- Each station's length, width, depth and air and water temperature were recorded (Tables 2–3). Substrate and turbidity were described qualitatively (Tables 4–5).
- Fish were dip-netted, counted and released. Representative specimens were preserved and identified using [[Minckley 1973]] and other references.
- Canyons and altered habitat segments were mapped but not sampled, except that canyons were walked or viewed from edges.

**Station changes.** Of the 25 stations, two had been sampled earlier by [[Propst Et Al. 1985]]. The report notes that one earlier East Fork reach was misidentified (it was not in section 25), and an East Fork station was too turbid to sample in February and was later replaced by a slightly upstream station after the original was found altered.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat and substrate.** Most stations had cobble or rubble substrate, the habitat preferred by loach minnows, but most also had moderate to high fine sediment. Sediment was greatest on the mainstem and least on the upper North and East Forks. The authors suggest sediment may not be permanent, since the February survey followed a December flood.

**Periphyton and algae.** [[Minckley 1973]] associates loach minnows with algae beds over cobble or gravel with little sediment. No algae beds were observed at any study-area station. The authors propose that sediment inhibits algae growth, or that productivity differs from the streams Minckley studied.

**Flow and turbidity.** In February, runoff and turbidity were rising with warm weather and snowmelt. Shallow riffles had few aquatic insects and little periphyton, and they produced very few fish. In June, turbidity was lower, so more area could be sampled and fish were easier to see. North Fork and mainstem water depths stayed about constant from February to June, while the East Fork became shallower. This was attributed to prolonged runoff on the North Fork.

**Disturbance.** The East Fork occupied reach had been partially altered by bridge construction and flood control, and had a drainage ditch inflow. A North Fork reach was disturbed by a gravel retrieval operation and was not sampled. The steep canyon from the Miner Flat Dam site to [[Alchesay Spring]] lacked suitable habitat. Several mainstem stations were affected by an irrigation dam and channelization.

**Non-native species.** [[Smallmouth Bass]] (two individuals in each month) and [[Rainbow Trout]] (stocked; two in June, none in February) were the only introduced fish recorded. The authors suggest sampling methods and recent flooding limited non-native captures.

## Key Findings

**Loach minnow captures**
- February 1988: one loach minnow, from East Fork station 5 on February 18, in about 50–60 cm of water in a deep riffle/run next to a bridge.
- June 1988: 10 loach minnows on the East Fork (8 at station 4, 2 at station 5), and one at mainstem station 16. The station 16 fish was about 31 mm long.
- No loach minnows were captured in the North Fork in either month.
- Overall, 12 loach minnows were captured out of 1,158 fish (1.0%).

**Breeding population.** The June East Fork captures included several breeding males and a gravid female, along with younger size classes. Only about 20 percent of the habitat at stations 4 and 5 was sampled to limit disturbance, so the population was likely larger than the catch suggests.

**Species composition (February and June combined, Table 12)**
- [[Sonoran Sucker]] (*Catostomus insignis*): 869 (75.0%)
- [[Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus*): 251 (21.7%)
- [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*): 12 (1.0%)
- [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*): 19 *G. r. robusta* and 1 *G. r. grahami*
- Smallmouth Bass: 4; Rainbow Trout: 2

Sonoran suckers outnumbered speckled dace at nearly all stations. February counts of suckers were probably overestimated and dace underestimated because of a field identification error that was resolved by June.

**Sampling efficiency.** June sampling was more productive at stations sampled in both months. Stations 1, 4 and 5 produced almost twice as many fish in June as in February. On the mainstem, most stations sampled in both months yielded the same or fewer fish in June, and the reason was not evident.

**Habitat associations.** Loach minnows were found with speckled dace, but dace occurred at sites without loach minnows. Some habitat that looked typical for loach minnows was unoccupied, and occupancy within a reach was uneven. Stations 19 and 23 on the North Fork appeared to be the best habitat seen in the study area, yet no loach minnows were found there.

**Mainstem conclusion.** The authors concluded that the mainstem reach does not support a significant loach minnow population. Reasons included: no population was found despite sampling essentially all suitable habitat; the single fish was a young age class; and the capture may represent downstream drift from the East Fork or upstream movement, which the authors describe as conjecture.

**Overall conclusions.**
- Loach minnows persist in the East Fork, which has supported the species in the past (Propst et al., 1985).
- The East Fork population occupies a reach where the channel was partially disturbed, which suggests the species may tolerate some bank or channel disturbance.
- Sedimentation was greatest on the mainstem and least on the upper North and East Forks.
- The East Fork was considered the most likely stream in the study area to contain loach minnows, and the survey was meant to inform the Miner Flat Dam and Canyon Day Irrigation Project.
