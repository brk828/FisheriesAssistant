# Values of Hunting and Fishing in Arizona, 1960

#ichthyology #marine-biology #recreational-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1962 socioeconomic survey, prepared by [[William C. Davis]] for the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] under Federal Aid project FW-11-R-2, estimates the participation, economic contribution, motivations and public recommendations associated with hunting and fishing in Arizona during 1960. Personal interviews with 1,029 license holders (drawn from roughly 237,000 licenses) indicate that about 183,000 people took part, generating an estimated $40.15 million in direct expenditures plus $1.6 million in license fees. The study frames these sports as producing recreational, health, aesthetic, social, economic, intellectual, character and religious values, and calls for expanded facilities and better public information.

## Background & Description

The survey was authorized on February 21, 1961, and carried out through the [[Bureau of Business and Public Research]], University of Arizona. Its three aims were to measure the direct economic contribution of participants, observe the qualitative values people derive from hunting and fishing, and record their recommendations for improving state facilities.

Sampling used a two-stage cluster design: 34 of about 340 licensed dealers were chosen with probability proportional to sales, and 1,600 names were drawn, yielding 1,029 completed interviews. The stated accuracy is ±10 percent of the mean at a 95 percent confidence level. Direct expenditures were defined narrowly, counting only outlays induced exclusively by hunting and fishing; shared items were prorated and depreciation was applied to capital goods. Comparisons with the 1956 survey (see [[Wildlife Bulletin No. 4]]) show exclusive-use expenditures up 35 percent and shared-use items down, with a total slightly below 1956.

Target categories included [[Cold Water Fishing]] (trout), [[Warm Water Fishing]] (bass, catfish), [[Big Game Hunting]], [[Small Game Hunting]], [[Waterfowl Hunting]], [[General Game Hunting]] (predators and jackrabbits) and [[Salt Water Fishing]] conducted outside the state.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document addresses wildlife as a managed resource rather than ecosystem processes. Harvest totals were 3.5 million cold water fish and 3.8 million warm water fish, roughly 35,000 deer, and about 1.2 million quail and 1.3 million doves. The authors note that game is "growing more scarce in the opinion of many," and that sustaining the harvest will require cumulative investment in wildlife habitat, water and shelter. Respondents proposed habitat work such as new lakes in southern Arizona, small-stream dams, water-circulation and weed control, carp and "trash fish" management, and reduced harvest of does, quail and doves. Harvest estimates for bighorn sheep (fewer than 50) and bear (under 100) were explicitly uncertain.

## Key Findings

**Participation**
- 237,000 licenses were sold to 183,300 people; about 15,000 (≈8%) were true nonresidents.
- Resident participants numbered 168,000, roughly one in five Arizonans aged 12 and over.
- The most active age group was 35–44 (26.6% of participants versus 19% of the population).
- Skilled and semi-skilled workers made up the largest occupational share (about 41%).
- Families above $5,000 income participated at roughly twice the rate of the general population. Visitors' median income was $7,550 versus $6,350 for residents.

**Motivations** (about 4,244 reasons from 1,016 persons; average of four per person; see [[Motivational Values of Hunting and Fishing]])
- Recreation 41%, bodily health 23%, aesthetic 13%, association 11%, economic 5%, intellectual 3%, character 2%, religious and other 2%.
- Ninety-five percent of respondents planned to hunt or fish again in 1961.

**Harvest** (1960)
- Fish: 7.3 million (cold water 3.5M; warm water 3.8M).
- Big game: about 42,000, including 35,000 deer, 833 antelope, 756 elk, 1,420 turkeys and 4,000 javelinas.
- Small game: 1.17M quail, 1.3M doves, 464,000 cottontails, 5,200 squirrels.
- Waterfowl: about 50,000 (ducks); general game: about 105,000.

**Expenditures** (see [[Total Expenditures for Hunting and Fishing]])
- Total: $40,151,000 (residents $37.9M, 95%; nonresidents $2.26M, 5%).
- Fishing ≈ 60% (cold water $12.2M; warm water $12.2M). Big game $8.27M (≈20%). Small game $4.05M (≈10%). Salt water fishing by residents out of state $3.06M.
- Car transportation was the largest item at about 37%; sporting equipment about 38%; lodging about 13%; additional food about 10%.
- Expenditure of about $25 per $1 of state wildlife investment was cited as roughly 25:1 (about $1.3M state spending in 1960).

**Recommendations** (about 2,143 suggestions; see [[Recommendations of Hunters and Fishermen]])
- Information services: about 40% of participants sought better information on hunting and fishing locations; maps, bulletins and regulation clarity were the top requests.
- Convenience facilities (27%), habitat improvement (21%), regulation modification (18%), safety regulation (15%), stocking increases (12%), access roads (5%), and wildland preservation (about 1.3%).

**Conclusions**
- Many requested services already exist, pointing to a need for better public relations.
- Some requests (e.g., certain access roads) fall outside departmental authority or budget, implying a need for interagency coordination.
- The authors recommend that facilities for continued hunting and fishing be expanded to meet population growth.
