# Competition for Food Between an Introduced Crayfish and Two Native Fishes of the Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #cambaridae

## Executive Summary

This laboratory study by Jeanette Carpenter (U.S. Geological Survey, Fort Collins Science Center; published in [[Environmental Biology of Fishes]], 2005) tests whether the introduced [[Virile Crayfish]] (*Orconectes virilis*) reduces the growth of two native Colorado River basin fishes through competition for food. Density-manipulation experiments compared the growth of [[Gila Chub]] (*Gila intermedia*) and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) with and without crayfish, at low and high densities. Crayfish reduced fish growth, but the effect differed sharply between species: flannelmouth suckers were strongly suppressed by crayfish, whereas Gila chub were more affected by their own conspecifics than by crayfish. Crayfish growth was not significantly altered by either fish.

## Background & Description

- **Study organisms:**
  - *Gila intermedia* (Gila chub): endemic to the [[Gila River Basin]], a sub-basin of the Colorado River. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service proposed listing it as endangered (USFWS 2002).
  - *Catostomus latipinnis* (flannelmouth sucker): endemic to the entire Colorado River basin, with state heritage ranks ranging from vulnerable to critically imperiled.
  - *Orconectes virilis* (virile crayfish): non-native, aggressive, omnivorous crayfish now established throughout the basin alongside *Procambarus clarkii* (red swamp crayfish).
- **Diet overlap:** Wild individuals of all three species share an omnivorous diet in which aquatic invertebrates are common prey.
- **Context:** The Colorado River basin has no native crayfish, so native fishes there may be more vulnerable than fishes that co-evolved with crayfish. The study ties into regional concern over the [[Decline of Native Fishes]] and the need for basic ecological data on these species.
- **Hypothesis:** Crayfish reduce native fish growth by competing for food.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Broader crayfish impacts:** Introduced crayfish are known to reduce macrophytes, native crayfish, macroinvertebrates and amphibians. Few prior studies had shown effects on fishes, mainly through predation or competition for shelter. The author knew of no published study on food competition between introduced crayfish and native fishes.
- **Field setting:** Sabino Creek (Pima County, Arizona). Natural densities were 5–10 m⁻² for Gila chub and 3–11 m⁻² for crayfish >25 mm carapace length. The dominant invertebrates were chironomid larvae.
- **Experimental conditions:** Tanks were 200 L aquaculture units in a greenhouse at the University of Arizona's Environmental Research Laboratory, filled with 143 L of aged city water. Food was chironomid larvae (0.75 g per tank per day, about 80 chironomids), which the authors estimated as eight times higher than natural densities in Sabino Creek. Temperature was held at 20–23 °C in the flannelmouth sucker trial.
- **Interference vs. exploitative competition:** The author suggests crayfish compete mainly by interference, since they handle chironomids more slowly and *O. virilis* is very aggressive. Activity levels and agonistic behavior may also raise fish energy expenditure, which complicates growth-based interpretation.

## Key Findings

**Methods overview**
- Four treatments per species: crayfish alone (3 per tank), fish alone (3 per tank), fish alone (6 per tank, intraspecific test), and crayfish plus fish (6 per tank, interspecific test).
- Each treatment began with five replicates. Analyses used one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni-adjusted α = 0.017; a randomized complete block design was tested and rejected by AIC.
- Gila chub trial: 28 October – 22 November 1999 (26 days). Flannelmouth sucker trial: 17 January – 10 February 2000.

**Gila chub results**
- Growth differed among treatments (F₂,₁₂ = 21.33; p = 0.0001).
- Low-density controls grew 0.25% body weight d⁻¹. Gila chub with crayfish grew 0.02% d⁻¹. High-density single-species tanks lost weight (−0.22% d⁻¹).
- Interspecific competition (crayfish) was not significant (p = 0.534 in Table 1); intraspecific competition was significant (p = 0.129 for the 3-fish vs. 6-fish comparison, with the reported post hoc results showing the stronger effect).
- Crayfish growth did not differ among treatments (F₁,₈ = 0.93, p = 0.36).

**Flannelmouth sucker results**
- Two low-density tanks were removed after the parasite *Ichthyophthirius multifiliis* developed. In one mixed tank, crayfish consumed 2 of 3 fish, and that tank was also excluded.
- Growth differed among treatments (F₂,₉ = 19.68; p = 0.0005).
- Low-density controls grew 0.71% body weight d⁻¹. Crayfish-treatment suckers grew 0.01% d⁻¹. High-density single-species tanks grew 0.32% d⁻¹.
- Crayfish growth did not differ between control and mixed tanks (0.04% vs. 0.03% d⁻¹; F₁,₇ = 0.21, p = 0.66).

**Conclusions**
- Crayfish reduced growth of both fish species, but suckers were much more strongly affected than Gila chub.
- For Gila chub, intraspecific crowding had a larger effect than crayfish presence.
- A field experiment in Sabino Creek (1996) found no food competition between crayfish and Gila chub. The author attributes this to the difficulty of detecting effects in field settings and considers the laboratory result to corroborate the field result.
- The author recommends future work separating interference from exploitative competition, observing fish–crayfish interactions and activity, and varying food quantity.
- The results may apply to naive wild fish populations in areas newly invaded by crayfish. Competitive effects are likely to vary among native species.
