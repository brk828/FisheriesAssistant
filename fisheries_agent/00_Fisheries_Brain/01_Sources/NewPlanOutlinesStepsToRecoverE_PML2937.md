# Four Endangered Fishes Recovery Plan: Upper Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

A news article in the *Endangered Species Technical Bulletin* (Vol. XIX, No. 1, 1994) by Connie Young describes a new five-year [[Recovery Plan]] for four endangered fishes endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. The plan was completed in 1993 by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS). It calls for protecting and restoring river flows, building fish passage structures, restoring habitat, controlling non-native fish, reducing contaminants, and stocking hatchery-raised fish. It is a multi-agency effort involving federal and state agencies, environmental groups, and water user organizations. Federal permitting under [[Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act]] will apply to new and existing water development projects in the upper basin.

## Background & Description

The four species covered by the plan are:

- [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*, called Colorado squawfish in the source)
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)

All four are endemic to the Colorado River system and were listed under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. The plan is the product of a multi-agency recovery effort. [[John Hamill]], a biologist who directs the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]], is quoted in the article. He states that the program will use the recovery plan as a guide for the actions needed to recover the species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The article describes the main threats and habitat problems the plan addresses:

- **Altered flow regimes:** Dams have changed the river's natural hydrology. The plan seeks to restore more natural flows.
- **Blocked migration:** Dams and diversions prevent the fish from reaching parts of their historic range.
- **Loss of nursery habitat:** Dikes, levees, and other barriers have cut off critical backwaters and floodplain wetlands. Young native fish use these nutrient-rich areas to grow before entering the river.
- **Non-native fish:** Introduced species compete with, prey on, and hybridize with the endangered fishes.
- **Contaminants:** Selenium, pesticides, petroleum derivatives, heavy metals, and uranium threaten the fish.

## Key Findings

**1. Flow restoration**
- Flows in the Colorado, Gunnison, Dolores, Green, Yampa, White, Little Snake, and Duchesne rivers will be managed. The most significant changes will occur in the Green River downstream of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and in the Gunnison River below [[Blue Mesa Dam]].
- Water from these two dams is being released to mimic historical high spring flows and lower, more stable flows during the rest of the year.
- [[Fish and Wildlife Service]] and state biologists are monitoring the effects on the endangered fishes.

**2. Habitat restoration**
- Selected dikes, levees, and other barriers to critical wetlands and floodplain backwaters will be removed or altered. This makes habitat available again to the native fish.
- Wetlands targeted for enhancement include [[Ouray National Wildlife Refuge]] on the Green River in northern Utah, a site on the Colorado River near Moab, Utah, and the [[Escalante State Wildlife Area]] on the Gunnison River downstream from Delta, Colorado.

**3. Fish passage**
- Fish ladders and other passageways will be built to allow the endangered fishes to reach more of their historical range.
- Construction of a fish ladder at the [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] on the lower Gunnison River was scheduled to begin in 1995.

**4. Non-native fish management**
- The FWS and the states of Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming are developing guidelines for stocking non-native fish in upper basin lakes and reservoirs.
- The guidelines aim to minimize predation, competition, and hybridization with the endangered species. They also aim to continue providing sport fishing opportunities.

**5. Contaminant reduction**
- Recovery Program participants will help agencies outside the program evaluate and reduce contaminants that harm the endangered fishes. These contaminants include selenium, petroleum derivatives, heavy metals, and uranium.

**6. Hatchery stocking**
- Hatchery-raised individuals of the endangered species will be stocked in the wild when needed to supplement limited wild populations.
- In 1994, razorback suckers were scheduled to be stocked in parts of their historical habitat in the Gunnison River.

**7. Regulatory and policy framework**
- The plan was agreed to by state and federal agencies, environmental groups, and water user organizations.
- Section 7 of the Endangered Species Act will apply to new and existing water development projects in the upper Colorado River Basin. Projects that require a federal permit will be reviewed by the FWS, and the actions identified in the plan will be considered in that review.
