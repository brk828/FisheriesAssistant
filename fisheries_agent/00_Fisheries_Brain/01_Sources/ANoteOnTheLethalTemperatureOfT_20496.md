# Lethal Temperature of the Aurora Trout (*Salvelinus timagamiensis*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salvelinus

## Executive Summary

This 1962 research note by Peter F. Sate, published in the *Canadian Journal of Zoology* (Contribution No. 1, Great Lakes Institute, University of Toronto), tests whether the [[Aurora Trout]] is more closely related to the [[Brook Trout]] or to the [[Alpine Char]]. The author compares upper lethal temperatures (log mean resistance times) of aurora trout with published [[Lethal Temperature]] data for brook trout and alpine char, all acclimated to 10 °C. Aurora trout thermal behaviour closely matched that of brook trout and diverged from alpine char, supporting earlier morphological and taxonomic evidence for a close aurora trout–brook trout relationship.

## Background & Description

- **Known range:** The aurora trout occurs, as far as known, only in three lakes: White Pine, Whirligig, and Wilderness, in Gamble Township of the Timagami Provincial Forest, Ontario.
- **Diagnostic traits:** It is distinguished from the brook trout by its lack of vermiculations and a more or less complete lack of red spots. The two species occur together in the same lakes.
- **Taxonomic history:** The species was described by [[Henn and Rinkenbach]] (cited in the text as 1925). In their original description, they placed it in the *alpinus* group but noted its resemblance to the brook trout in maxillary size and shape and in the truncate caudal fin.
  - Martin measured four aurora trout, found them consistently closer to *fontinalis* than to *alpinus* in proportions, and concluded it was a subspecies of the brook trout.
  - Vladykov, in a study of eastern North American chars, treated it as simply a colour phase of the brook trout.
- **Study aim:** To clarify the relationship of the aurora trout to the brook trout and alpine char by comparing upper lethal temperatures.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Experimental material and husbandry**
- 150 fingerling aurora trout were supplied by the Ontario Department of Lands and Forests from the Hills Lake Hatchery.
- Stock fish were held in running, aerated water at 9–11 °C.
- Two groups of 50 fish were acclimated in running water at 10.0 °C and 20.0 °C from June 9 to July 4, 1961. The 20.0 °C group developed an apparent sporozoan kidney infection and was discarded.
- A single test at 27.0 °C gave a log mean resistance time comparable to that of a similarly acclimated brook trout.

**Lethal temperature method**
- Following the approach of [[Brett]] (1952) and [[Fry]] (1957), fish were acclimated as one group to a set temperature for an extended period. Subsamples were then moved directly into constant-temperature baths and the time of each death recorded.
- Six fish were tested at each of four lethal temperatures: 24.5, 25.0, 25.5, and 26.0 °C.
- Baths were 50-gallon tanks of well-aerated water. Cooling used a closed aluminium coil with circulating cold water; heating used a glass-covered immersion heater with a thermostat held to within ±0.1 °C. Detectable deviation from the test temperature was seldom found.
- Fish were watched until all had died.

**Comparison data**
- Brook trout and alpine char data came from [[McCauley]]'s work (1956 thesis; 1958 paper), all acclimated to 10 °C.

## Key Findings

1. **Thermal tolerance resembles the brook trout.** The mean log resistance time of *S. timagamiensis* consistently followed that of *S. fontinalis* and diverged from *S. alpinus*.
   - The difference between aurora trout and brook trout log mean resistance times (averaged across Ontario and Pennsylvania summer groups) ranged from 0.017 to 0.171, with a mean of 0.079.
   - This is within the same order of variation seen among the brook trout samples themselves.

3. **Conclusion:** The authors state that the thermal behaviour of the aurora trout is similar to that of the brook trout. This supports the other evidence for a closer relationship between the aurora trout and the brook trout than between the aurora trout and the alpine char.

4. **Limitations:**
   - Only six aurora trout were tested per temperature, and the 20 °C acclimation group was lost to infection.
   - The 27.0 °C test was a single trial.
   - The study was based on one stock population of hatchery-reared fingerlings.
