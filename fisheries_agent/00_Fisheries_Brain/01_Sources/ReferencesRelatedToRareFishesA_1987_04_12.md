## Executive Summary

This document is an annotated bibliography — not a primary research report — compiled by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's Division of Endangered Species (Denver, 1987) to catalog literature from **1980 to 1987** related to rare fish conservation and natural resource management in the **Upper Colorado River Basin**. The corpus spans over 80 citations covering endangered [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (historically called "Colorado squawfish"), [[Humpback Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]], and native cutthroat trout subspecies, alongside broader ecosystem concerns: dam and reservoir impacts, introduced species interactions, habitat alteration from energy development, larval fish sampling methodology, and recovery/hatchery program planning. The bibliography reflects the foundational scientific and regulatory literature underpinning what became the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]].

## Background & Description

Direct morphological data are not presented in this bibliography (it is a citation index), but referenced works address species-level biological traits indirectly:

- **[[Colorado Pikeminnow]]** (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): North America's largest native cyprinid; life history and spawning behavior discussed via comparison to the congeneric [[Northern Squawfish]] (Beamesderfer & Congleton 1982), and movement/feeding studies in reservoir inlet habitats (Persons et al. 1982).
- **[[Roundtail Chub]]** (*Gila robusta robusta*, Cyprinidae): Larval-period culture techniques documented by Muth, Haynes & Carlson (1985), implying detailed developmental/larval morphology data exist in the primary source.
- **[[Redside Shiner]]** (*Richardsonius balteatus*): Range extension into the Upper Colorado River drainage noted by Haynes, Muth & Wycoff (1982), indicating morphological/taxonomic identification was used to confirm new distributional records.
- **Cutthroat trout subspecies** ([[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]], [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]]): Distinguished at the subspecies level in headwater stream studies (Scarnecchia & Bergersen 1986), reflecting recognized morphological/genetic divergence within *Oncorhynchus clarkii*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The bibliography documents several recurring ecological themes for Upper Colorado River Basin fishes:

- **Backwater habitats**: Natural vs. artificial backwaters serve as critical native fish nursery/rearing habitat (Valdez & Wick 1983; Kennedy 1979), particularly important for larval and juvenile [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] and [[Humpback Chub]].
- **Mainstem large-river habitat**: Species such as the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] rely on high-velocity mainstem reaches, cobble-bar morphology (O'Brien 1984), and reservoir inlet zones (Persons et al. 1982) for feeding and movement.
- **Headwater streams**: [[Greenback Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] occupy threatened, fragmented headwater habitats in the Rocky Mountains (Scarnecchia & Bergersen 1986).
- **Reservoir/regulated-river systems**: Flaming Gorge Dam altered downstream flow and temperature regimes on the Green River (Andrews 1986); Taylor Draw Reservoir and Redlands Diversion Dam projects raised concerns over fish passage and thermal regimes (Grenney & Caupp 1981; Tipton and Kalmbach 1984; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers 1986).
- **Introduced species interactions**: Non-native [[Striped Bass]] and other introduced fishes compete with and prey upon native/endangered species, with documented negative effects on endangered Colorado River fishes (Kaeding 1986; Minckley 1982).
- **Desert aquatic ecosystems**: Broader southwestern desert fish community ecology — habitat size vs. species richness, predator-prey coexistence under disturbance, and adaptations to warm desert conditions — is treated in comparative works by Smith (1981), Meffe (1983, 1984, 1985), and Minckley & Meffe (1987).

## Key Findings

- **Habitat alteration from energy development** (dams, diversions, water withdrawals) is repeatedly identified as a primary driver of decline for endangered Upper Colorado River fishes (Behnke 1980; Molles 1980; Hickman 1983; Jacobsen 1982).
- **Introduced/non-native fishes** are implicated as a significant negative factor for native and endangered species populations via predation, competition, and trophic disruption (Minckley 1982; Kaeding 1986).
- **Backwater and cobble-bar habitats** are essential nursery grounds for larval and juvenile endangered fishes, and manmade backwaters can partially substitute for natural ones (Valdez & Wick 1983; O'Brien 1984).
- **Hatchery and stocking programs** were being actively evaluated as recovery tools for [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Humpback Chub]], and related endangered taxa, including feasibility studies for a dedicated Colorado River endangered fish hatchery (Valentine 1983; Valdez 1985; Rinne et al. 1986).
- **Reservoir operations** (e.g., Flaming Gorge, Taylor Draw, Lake Powell, Glen Canyon) measurably alter downstream temperature, flow, and morphology, with cascading effects on native fish spawning and recruitment (Andrews 1986; Maddux et al. 1987).
- **Fish passage engineering** (e.g., Redlands Diversion Dam fishway) emerged as a management strategy to reconnect fragmented habitat for migratory native fishes (Tipton and Kalmbach 1984; U.S. Army Corps of Engineers 1986).
- **Larval fish sampling methodology** was actively developed during this period, including novel light-trap techniques for low-velocity riverine habitats (Muth & Haynes 1984) and standardized ichthyoplankton survey strategies (Muth, Haynes & Miller 1983).
- **Legal and institutional frameworks**, particularly the Endangered Species Act and Section 7 consultation processes, shaped water management and mitigation strategies across the basin (Johnson & Rinne 1982; Lambertson 1982; Prewitt & Stalnaker 1982).
- **Comparative desert fish ecology** research (Meffe 1983–1985; Minckley & Meffe 1987) established broader ecological principles — such as flood-mediated selection favoring native over non-native fishes — relevant to Upper Colorado River management strategy.
- This bibliography itself represents a precursor synthesis effort, building on Wydoski et al.'s (1980) earlier annotated bibliography for the same ecosystem, and anticipates the consolidated recovery planning that followed under the [[Upper Colorado River Endangered Fish Recovery Program]].
