# Soil Survey of the Upper Gila Valley Area, Arizona (1933)

#soil-science #pedology #irrigated-agriculture

## Executive Summary

This Bureau of Chemistry and Soils survey, by E. N. Poulson and F. O. Youngs, maps and classifies the soils of the irrigated [[Upper Gila Valley]] in [[Graham County]], Arizona, covering 106 square miles (67,840 acres). The survey concentrates on the valley trough along the [[Gila River]], where gravity-fed irrigation is feasible. Soils are grouped by subsoil lime accumulation into three classes, and productivity is rated for cotton, alfalfa, small grains, corn, and sorghum. The survey's central findings are that deep silting from turbid irrigation water has made most farmland fertile and largely uniform, that salinity is the main constraint on the heavy, low-lying soils, and that cotton is the dominant crop.

## Background & Description

**Location and extent.** The area lies along the Gila River in Graham County, about 184 miles east of Phoenix and 245 miles west of El Paso. It is about 45 miles long and includes the area of an earlier 1903 soil survey of the [[Solomonsville Area]]. It lies within the [[Basin and Range Province]], where mountain ranges surround alluvial valleys. [[Mount Graham]] rises to 10,500 feet.

**Physiography.** The Gila River enters the valley at about 3,050 feet and leaves at about 2,600 feet, with a gradient of roughly 10 feet per mile. The flood plain averages about one-half mile wide and floods only in exceptional years. Terrace fronts rise several hundred feet in places. The valley is well drained, and only a few areas are waterlogged.

**Vegetation.** Native vegetation is sparse desert scrub. Creosotebush covers the upland mesas. Mesquite, squawbush, and saltbush occur on fans and terraces. Saltgrass and seepweed grow on alkali flats. Cottonwood, willow, tamarisk, and arrowweed line the river, and saltgrass and tules occupy permanently wet depressions.

**Settlement and population.** Spanish explorers passed through early, but Apache resistance delayed settlement until the early 1880s. Mexican settlers came first, and Mormon colonizers from Utah arrived in the late 1880s. Graham County was organized in 1881. The 1930 census recorded 10,373 people, with Safford (2,033), Pima (980), and Thatcher (889) the largest towns.

**Climate.** The climate is arid, with intense sunlight and high summer heat. At Thatcher (elevation 2,800 ft), mean annual precipitation is 9.04 inches, ranging from 2.98 to 17.38 inches. Most rain falls as torrential summer storms, and snow seldom falls. The mean annual temperature is 62.6 °F. The frost-free season averages 205 days, between an average last killing frost of April 11 and first frost of November 2. Recorded extremes are 115 °F and 9 °F.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and irrigation.** Irrigation water is diverted from the Gila River for an irrigable area of 35,000 to 40,000 acres. There are no storage reservoirs, so supply depends on natural flow. Eleven diversion canals are mapped. Priority rights govern distribution under a water commissioner. Diversion headworks are often torn out by floods and rebuilt. Spillways return excess water to the river.

**Silting.** Turbid irrigation water deposits fine, organic-rich silt on fields. In places the silt exceeds 8 feet. This builds granular, fertile topsoils that resemble prairie soils, and it progressively masks the original soil profiles. Deposition is thickest on lower field ends and gentle slopes.

**Salinity and alkali.** Salts come from two sources: ground water with poor drainage, and salt-laden parent material, mainly old lake clays. Salts are chiefly sodium chloride and sodium sulphate ("white alkali"). Sodium carbonate ("black alkali") is only slight in most soils. The Imperial and Land soils are most affected. Reclamation by leaching and silting works where underdrainage is adequate. The largest poorly drained, salt-affected area lies north of Central, where a central drainage system is judged insufficient.

**Soil formation.** The area is in the southern Gray Desert soil province. Parent material and age matter more than moisture or vegetation. Soil age follows physiographic position: old high terraces and mesas carry the most developed soils, and young flood-plain soils are least altered. Granitic materials dominate south and west of the Gila River, while basalt, andesite, and rhyolite dominate to the north and east.

## Key Findings

- **Survey scope:** 106 square miles (67,840 acres) mapped in Graham County, Arizona. Soils were classified into series, types, and phases, with miscellaneous land classes such as riverwash and rough broken land.
- **Lime-based soil groups:**
  1. Soils without definite subsoil lime accumulation: Gila, Pima, Cajon, Imperial, and Land. These are the most important agricultural soils.
  2. Soils with moderate subsoil lime: Anthony and Mohave.
  3. Soils with high subsoil lime: Laveen and Cave. These are of little agricultural value. The Cave soils lie above the irrigation supply and sit on an indurated caliche hardpan.
- **Pima silty clay loam** is one of the most extensive and productive soils. It is deeply silted, friable, and well drained. Cotton yields run 1¼ to 2¼ bales per acre, with wheat 30 to 45 bushels and barley 30 to 50 bushels. Its cotton productivity index is 200.
- **Cajon sandy loam** (silted and deep-silted phases) is equally productive and is the main soil around Safford. Cotton yields run 1 to 2 bales per acre in the silted phase and 1¼ to 2¼ bales in the deep-silted phase.
- **Imperial and Land soils** are heavy, compact, and salt-rich. Unsilted Imperial silty clay is agriculturally unimportant. Silted and reclaimed phases produce cotton and small grains. Silted Land silty clay yields 3 to 5 tons of alfalfa per acre.
- **Cotton dominance:** At least 11,000 acres (about one-third of the irrigated area) were planted to cotton in 1933. By 1930, cotton acreage was twice that of alfalfa. Cotton is grown on silted soils because fertility is high and fiber is uniform. Cotton is planted in April and harvested in September and October.
- **Crop rotation:** Alfalfa is grown for 3 to 4 years in rotation with cotton to counter compaction and silt-induced crown burial. Wheat and barley are seeded in fall and harvested in May and June, followed by corn or hegari in the same year.
- **Salinity thresholds:** The survey estimates that 0.4 to 0.5 percent salts in the top 12 inches is the tolerance limit for most plants. At 0.3 percent, many crops grow seriously retarded.
- **Reclamation evidence:** The virgin Imperial soil near San Simon Creek contained 0.2 percent salts in the top 12 inches and an average of 0.44 percent to 6 feet. After leaching and silting, the severely affected Imperial soil averaged 0.23 percent to 6 feet, and it now produces excellent cotton. Near Bryce, a silted Land soil field was leached to an average below 0.2 percent to 6 feet.
- **Productivity ratings:** Table 3 gives indexes (base 100 = best inherent US soils) for each soil type. Some irrigated soils reach 200 for cotton, meaning twice the yield of the best Cotton Belt soils without irrigation or fertilizer. Ratings reflect long-term productivity, and the authors caution that they are not direct land values.
- **Drainage conclusion:** The authors judge that successful drainage of the Central area probably requires intercepting drains set at right angles to the slope, along the contact between permeable upland soils and heavy Imperial soils.
- **Survey context:** The survey updates the 1903 Solomonsville survey. It found that the Gila and Pima soils had been confused in the earlier work, partly because silting had changed the soils themselves.
