# Speciation, Endemism, and Human-Driven Decline of Chihuahuan Desert Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Salvador Contreras-Balderas]] examines how the fish fauna of the [[Chihuahuan Desert]] arose and why it is disappearing. The region holds 107 native species, mostly of northern origin, distributed across interior drainages that once belonged to the former Rio Bravo system. The author argues that isolated desert basins are species-poor but highly endemic, that infraspecific divergence in widespread cyprinids records the history of river connections and desert expansion, and that documented community collapses at seven Chihuahuan localities are driven by water extraction, pollution, siltation, and exotic species. The paper also reviews captive propagation at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] as a conservation response.

## Background & Description

The Chihuahuan Desert fish fauna comprises 107 native species, according to [[Robert R. Miller]] (this volume). Most fishes are of northern origin, shown by the presence of identical or closely related species. The fauna includes fishes from interior drainages that once formed part of the Rio Bravo, namely the Mimbres, Casas Grandes, Santa Maria, Carmen, Nazas, Aguanaval, Palomas, Parras, Illescas-Moctezuma, and Hediondilla rivers, plus the Encinillas, Bustillos, and Santiaguillo lagoons.

The author describes the group's status through several lenses:

- **Basin size and richness:** Isolated Chihuahuan basins hold roughly one-half to one-fourth as many fish species as comparable exterior rivers that reach the sea (see [[Species-Area Relationship]]).
- **Endemic genera:** Several genera are restricted to very small springs or drainages, including [[Megupsilon Aporus]], [[Xenoophorus]], [[Ataeniobius Toweri]], and [[Cualac Tessellatus]], probably through [[Genetic Drift]] in small, old habitats.
- **Undescribed diversity:** After Miller (1977) and the author's estimates, there are 17 undescribed species and at least 20 undescribed subspecies.
- **Knowledge gap:** Only one published zoogeographic study exists (Smith 1966, on *Pantosteus plebeius*), and only species described after 1957 are known from good variational data.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Speciation and basin size.** Fish richness, as well as genus and family counts, correlates with basin area. The author attributes this to habitat diversity, larger rivers traversing more varied regions. Endemism shows a U-shaped relationship with basin area: it is negative in the smallest basins and turns positive as streams enlarge. Table 1 supports this, with an endemic point scheme of 3 for genera, 2 for species, and 1 for subspecies. Exterior drainages instead show a positive size-endemism correlation.

**Intraspecific divergence.** Widespread [[Notropis Lutrensis]] (sensu lato) shows strong geographic variation in meristic characters. Percent divergence from the San Juan reference stock, based on 13 characters across 13 populations, was:

| Race or complex | Divergence (%) |
|---|---|
| Samalayuca complex (Casas Grandes, Carmen, North and South Yaqui, Satiz) | 42.4 to 83.8 |
| Santa Maria (*N. l. santamariae*) | 51.0 |
| Nazas-Aguanaval (*N. l. garmani*) | 46.2 |
| Conchos (undescribed subspecies) | 26.4 |
| Upper Rio Grande and Pecos | 13.0 and 10.2 |

Assuming divergence reflects time since separation from the main river, the Upper Rio Grande and Pecos races are the most recent, followed by the Conchos, then the Nazas-Aguanaval, with the Samalayuca complex the oldest. The author infers that desert advance proceeded from north and west toward south and east.

**Stream capture.** [[Codoma Ornata]] shows a Y-shaped cline across six basins, with the Lower Conchos race as the presumed ancestral form. Divergence from it was 24.8% (Yaqui), 51.6% (Nazas), 81.3% (Upper Conchos), and 86.9% (Mezquital). The author proposes stream capture, specifically the loss of the Guadiana Valley from the Nazas to the Mezquital, as the mechanism, distinguishing this from relict distribution patterns.

**Size and complexity of communities.** The author concludes that small, old basins and simple communities favor stronger divergence, while large, young basins and complex communities favor smaller divergence. He labels these inductive statements needing further support.

**Habitat preferences.** Most declining species are bottom-dwelling forms that prefer well-oxygenated, clear, moving water over sand or gravel. Several are herbivorous algae feeders, a food source that disappears in turbid water.

## Key Findings

**Documented community losses at seven localities** (see [[Fish Community Decline]]):

- **[[Rio Chihuahua]], Chihuahua City:** 12 native species known until 1901; 3 collected in 1964 and 3 in 1968, one of them rare. The introduced [[Lepomis Macrochirus]] appeared after 1964. Causes include channelization, damming, and siltation.
- **[[Rio Conchos]] at Camargo (Santa Rosalia):** 16 native species in 1901, reduced to 8 in 1964 plus one exotic ([[Cyprinus Carpio]]). Both endemic *Etheostoma* species were gone. Agricultural withdrawal lowered flow.
- **Rio Conchos at Jiménez:** 15 known species plus 3 probable in 1901; 12 in both 1964 and 1968. *Catostomus conchos* has been seen only three times since 1903 and may be extinct.
- **[[Parras Basin]], Coahuila:** Native totals fell from 8 (1880) to 3 (1973). Four exotics were recorded by 1968, and *Gambusia affinis speciosa* was collected in 1973, possibly through mosquito control. Three native fishes remain, rare and endangered.
- **[[Hediondilla Basin]] (Potosi Spring), Nuevo León:** Enlarging the spring in the 1950s may have helped natives. Black bass (*Micropterus salmoides*) were introduced in early 1974, and the natives have almost disappeared.
- **[[Rio Santa Catarina]], Monterrey, Nuevo León:** Native species fell from 10 (Girard, 1856–1859) to 8 (1903) and 6 (1967–68). By 1972 massive hybridization between *Xiphophorus couchianus* and the exotic *X. variatus* was evident, and by 1973 only hybrids remained in the spring.
- **[[La Media Luna]], Rioverde, San Luis Potosí:** Five species were present in 1903 and five rare in 1968. Exotics [[Tilapia Aurea]] and [[Gambusia Panuco]] were found in 1972. At least seven species are endemic to the spring or upper Rio Verde.

**Reasons for decline** (author's summary):

1. Agriculture lowers the water table, increases aridity and siltation, and dams raise water temperature and reduce dissolved oxygen.
2. Untreated sewage and industrial effluent add pollution and salinity to already saline, highly evaporative waters, and rivers become intermittent muddy pools.
3. Indiscriminate stocking of exotic fishes by culturists disregards biological principles.
4. Simple desert communities lack adaptation to predators and competitors. *Megupsilon aporus* and *Cyprinodon* sp. coexisted with the exotic *Carassius auratus* but succumbed to the predatory black bass.

**Captive propagation and recovery:**

- Since 1974, [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico) has served as refuge and production center for imperiled southwestern fishes, underwriting survival through a protected gene pool and freeing biologists to focus on recovery.
- Dexter staff assisted programs for [[Razorback Sucker]] (Ouray NWR, Utah), [[Cui-ui]] (Pyramid Lake Paiute, Nevada), and Klamath Lost River and shortnose suckers (Oregon), and advised Nuevo León on refugia.
- For [[Gila Topminnow]], the Fish and Wildlife Service replaced a genetically uniform Monkey Springs stock in 1985 with more heterozygous fish from Sharp Spring, Arizona, to test the role of genetic diversity in population persistence. A Bureau of Land Management pilot program aims to expand reintroductions to more than twenty sites within two years.
- The author cautions that recovery failures are expected and should not discourage efforts.

**Concluding argument:** Insufficient biological knowledge underlies unplanned development in the Chihuahuan Desert. The author calls for integrated ecological planning, protection of the fish fauna as a scientific, cultural, aesthetic, and practical resource, and an end to aquatic habitat destruction, which he describes as thoughtless and fruitless.
