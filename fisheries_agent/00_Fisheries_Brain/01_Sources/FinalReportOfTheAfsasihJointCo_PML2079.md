# Taxonomic Status of Gila Chubs in the Lower Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This report from the [[AFS/ASIH Joint Committee on the Names of Fishes]] reviews morphological and genetic evidence on the chubs of the genus [[Gila]] in the lower [[Colorado River]] basin of Arizona and New Mexico. The committee concludes that the available data support recognition of only one species, the [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta* Baird & Girard 1853). It does not support the three-species arrangement (*G. robusta*, [[Gila Chub]] *G. intermedia*, and [[Headwater Chub]] *G. cypha*) used in the two most recent editions of the *Common and Scientific Names of Fishes*.

## Background & Description

The committee (chaired by L. M. Page, with members including C. C. Baldwin, H. Espinosa-Pérez, C. R. Gilbert, K. E. Harrel, R. N. Lea, N. E. Mandrak, J. J. Schmitter-Soto and H. J. Walker) was asked to assess the taxonomy of *Gila* across the Little Colorado, Bill Williams, Gila, Verde and Salt River drainages. The review drew on published studies and on unpublished data presented at a meeting in Phoenix, Arizona, in April 2016.

Morphological studies have varied in their conclusions, recognizing one to three species (and sometimes subspecies) of *Gila* in the basin. The three-species view rests largely on characters presented by [[Minckley and DeMarais 2000]], who recognized three species in the [[Gila River Basin]] even though considerable overlap existed in all characters analyzed.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report describes a complex [[Western North America Geological History]]. Tectonic activity in the western United States over the past 15 million years, described by [[Flint 1971]], produced periods of geographic isolation of basins, followed by lineage mixing and hybridization. This history has produced substantial morphological variation within and among populations and has confounded the taxonomy of chubs and several other western fish groups, as shown by [[Smith et al. 2010]] and [[Unmack et al. 2014]] for suckers.

Phenotypic plasticity, linked to stream size, flow and substrate, is an alternative explanation for observed differences among populations. The committee notes that the morphological variation in *G. robusta* in the lower Colorado basin exceeds that usually found within a single species in a North American basin.

## Key Findings

- **One species recognized:** Morphological and genetic data do not define populations of *Gila* in the lower Colorado basin as members of more than one species. The committee recommends recognizing only *G. robusta*.
- **Morphological evidence is inconsistent:** The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] (AGFD) could not verify the species-level observations of Minckley and DeMarais (2000). [[Carter et al. 2016]] found the diagnostic characters too variable and mutually contradictory to assign individuals reliably.
- **Conflicting discriminant analysis:** [[Brandenburg et al. 2015]] reported that individuals from New Mexico could be assigned to one of the three species. However, they found the three species sympatric at almost all localities sampled, and noted that the species-specific characters overlapped, with non-overlapping characters separated by very small margins. They also reported little intraspecific variation within or between systems, and strong evidence of hybridization.
- **Genetic data support one species:** Prior studies using mitochondrial DNA ([[Gerber et al. 2001]]; [[Schwemm 2006]]; [[Schönhuth et al. 2013]]), nuclear DNA (Schwemm 2006; Schönhuth et al. 2013) and microsatellites ([[Dowling et al. 2015]]) found no genetic markers that distinguish species.
- **Next-generation sequencing analysis:** [[Copus et al. 2016]] (University of Hawaii, commissioned by AGFD, Appendix A) found no morphological support for more than one species. Their phylogenetic analyses of mitochondrial and nuclear genomes revealed extremely shallow coalescence of putative species and no monophyly for currently recognized taxonomic units.
- **Rarity of such patterns:** The committee knows of no North American basin with a monophyletic group of species that cannot be distinguished morphologically. If *Gila* in the lower Colorado basin were such an exception, this should be evident in genetic analyses, and it is not.
- **Explanation of differences:** Observed differences that led to the three-species hypothesis are, in the committee's view, most likely the result of past isolation and differentiation followed by recent mixing and hybridization, or of phenotypic plasticity.
- **Recommendation:** Recognize *Gila robusta* as the only species of *Gila* in the lower Colorado River basin, and revise the common and scientific names lists accordingly.
