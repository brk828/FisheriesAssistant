# Ash Meadows Species Recovery Plan (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, 1990)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinodontidae

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service recovery plan, prepared by Donald W. Sada for the Eastern Mohave Desert Fishes Recovery Team, addresses 12 federally listed species of [[Ash Meadows]] in Nevada and California, as well as the candidate species that share their habitat. Ash Meadows is described as the sole habitat for roughly 25 to 33 unique plants and animals (the figure differs between the summary and the introduction), most of them endemic. The plan takes an ecosystem-level approach: recovery tasks are to be implemented only where they enhance the whole system, not single species. Principal threats are introduced non-native aquatic animals, exotic plants, groundwater pumping, and habitat alteration. The recovery objective is delisting for all species except the [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]], which can only be downlisted to threatened. The estimated total recovery cost is $7,345,000 over 1990 to 2005, with delisting to begin in 2005 if criteria are met.

## Background & Description

**Setting.** Ash Meadows lies at about 2,200 feet elevation in the Mojave Desert, roughly 40 miles east of Death Valley National Monument headquarters and 90 miles northwest of Las Vegas. It covers about 50,000 acres of desert uplands and spring-fed oases. Rainfall averages less than 2.75 inches per year and annual evaporation exceeds 98.50 inches. The area is an isolated "watered island" whose springs have persisted since the late Pliocene/early Pleistocene, allowing relict taxa to differentiate.

**Biogeography.** Ash Meadows fishes and mollusks are most closely related to species to the south and east, while the [[Speckled Dace]] is linked to northern latitudes and is believed to have entered from the lower [[Colorado River]] basin. Pluvial climates provided colonization opportunities, and xeric conditions later isolated populations.

**Listed species.** The plan covers 12 federally listed taxa, including four fishes, the Ash Meadows naucorid, and seven plants. The Ash Meadows killifish (*Empetrichthys merriami*) is extinct, and the Ash Meadows vole has not been seen since 1933. Table 1 lists federal status, with listing dates, for each species:
- Endangered: Devil's Hole pupfish (1967), Warm Springs pupfish (1970), Ash Meadows Amargosa pupfish (1982), Ash Meadows speckled dace (1982), Amargosa niterwort (1985).
- Threatened: Ash Meadows naucorid, blazing star, milk-vetch, sunray, spring-loving centaury, gumplant, and ivesia (1985).

**Species accounts (selected):**
- [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon diabolis*): restricted to a limestone cave; described by Wales in 1930. Population fluctuates annually, from a maximum of 553 in summer to a minimum of 127 in winter (monthly and quarterly counts, 1972 to 1984). Maximum length rarely exceeds 0.98 inches.
- [[Warm Springs Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis pectoralis*): occupies six small springs covering less than 0.77 square mile. No spring discharges more than 1.17 gallons per second. School Spring counts in November 1972 to 1974 ranged from 136 to 231 individuals.
- [[Ash Meadows Amargosa Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis mionectes*): occupies 10 spring areas. Crystal Pool discharges 62.93 gal/sec. Counts at Jackrabbit Spring were 568 (1982) and 1,189 (1983); at Big Spring, 1,189 (1982) and 1,822 (1983).
- [[Ash Meadows Speckled Dace]] (*Rhinichthys osculus nevadensis*): a cyprinid that feeds on drifting insects and reaches about 3.9 inches. The total population is estimated at 500, with critical habitat of about 36 acres.
- [[Ash Meadows Naucorid]] (*Ambrysus amargosus*): known only from Point of Rocks Springs, where flowing water passes over rock and pebble substrate. Roughly 10 acres are designated critical habitat.
- [[Devil's Hole Warm Spring Riffle Beetle]] (*Stenelmis calida calida*): a candidate aquatic insect associated with spring habitats in southern Nevada.
- Plants: [[Spring-Loving Centaury]] (the only listed plant not endemic to Ash Meadows), [[Ash Meadows Gumplant]], [[Ash Meadows Ivesia]], [[Ash Meadows Blazing Star]], [[Ash Meadows Milk-Vetch]], [[Ash Meadows Sunray]], and [[Amargosa Niterwort]].
- Candidates include 13 snails, the Ash Meadows montane vole, the white-faced ibis, and several plants.

**Area of Management Concern.** A boundary of 23,094 acres was proposed, including BLM lands (9,243 acres), Fish and Wildlife Service lands (12,438 acres), National Park Service land (40 acres), and private land (1,371 acres). The [[Ash Meadows National Wildlife Refuge]] was established on June 13, 1984, after [[The Nature Conservancy]] purchased 12,654 acres of private land.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat types.** Listed and candidate species occupy aquatic/riparian and terrestrial habitats. Aquatic species depend on spring pools and outflow channels with thermal or cool, well-watered conditions. Terrestrial plants depend on alkaline soils, which are either moist (ivesia, niterwort, gumplant) or dry (blazing star, sunray, milk-vetch).

**Decline and causes.** Ash Meadows has been disturbed in several phases:
1. Early Native American and settler use, with limited but locally significant habitat change. The killifish was extirpated by introductions combined with habitat alteration.
2. The early 1960s peat mining of about 2,000 acres of Carson Slough, which removed about six feet of substrate and eliminated some of the largest marshes in southern Nevada.
3. Spring Meadows Ranch operations from the late 1960s to the late 1970s, which pumped groundwater, channelized springs, and cleared riparian vegetation. This drove the Devil's Hole pupfish population below 150 individuals.
4. Municipal development from 1979 onward, planned for about 55,000 inhabitants.

Non-native species are the greatest current threat. The plan's species list includes sailfin mollies, mosquitofish, largemouth bass, black bullheads, bullfrogs, crayfish, turban snails, wild horses, salt cedar, and Russian olive. Roads also facilitated introductions. About 65 miles of gravel and unimproved roads exist within essential habitat.

**Essential habitat.** The essential habitat boundary covers the known range of all endemic species and the area where groundwater removal most affects spring discharge. Devil's Hole essential habitat is 21,760 acres, and Warm Springs pupfish essential habitat is 2,240 acres.

**Ecosystem concept.** The plan argues that the species are intertwined: disturbance to one habitat type is likely to affect the other, and the terrestrial and aquatic critical habitats overlap substantially.

## Key Findings

- **Recovery objective:** Delist all species except Devil's Hole pupfish, which can be downlisted to threatened. Delisting is considered after five years of sustained achievement of the downlisting criteria.
- **Downlisting criteria (eight conditions for five years):** eradication of non-native species from essential habitat; groundwater protection so spring flows return to historic rates; reestablishment of historic channels; secure essential habitat; historic presence of listed fishes in all springs; niterwort present at all historic localities; refugia established at Hoover Dam and the Amargosa Pupfish Station; and at least 300 Devil's Hole pupfish in winter and 700 in late summer or early autumn.
- **Devil's Hole water level:** The court-enforced minimum is 2.7 feet below the datum. Recovery requires a return to 1.4 feet below the copper washer. The plan sets an interim minimum of 2.7 feet.
- **Costs and schedule:** Total estimated cost is $7,345,000 (in thousands: 7,345). Annual costs peak near $737,000 in 1992, drop to $230,000 by 2005, and are divided into four need categories. Need 1 (securing habitat and water) totals $1,062,000; Need 2 (preliminary research), $907,000; Need 3 (management), $3,531,000; Need 4 (monitoring and reintroduction), $1,747,000; Need 5 (determine objectives), $98,000.
- **Priority tasks:** Secure and protect land and water; remove non-native species; restore spring flows to historic channels; reestablish native plant and aquatic communities; maintain refugia genetic diversity by adding 10 fish annually from the parent population.
- **Monitoring:** Ongoing monitoring of Devil's Hole (U.S. Geological Survey water levels since 1962; National Park Service pupfish counts since 1976). Warm Springs, Amargosa pupfish, and speckled dace are to be monitored every two years for representative populations. The naucorid requires semi-annual monitoring after initial recovery.
- **Plan limitations:** The plan states that goals and objectives depend on appropriations and budget constraints, and that it does not necessarily represent official agency positions.
