# Glen Canyon Dam Adaptive Management Program: 1999 Symposium Abstracts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This is a volume of abstracts from a symposium held February 16-17, 1999, at Grand Canyon National Park and organized by the [[Grand Canyon Monitoring and Research Center]] (GCMRC). It covers the [[Glen Canyon Dam]] Adaptive Management Program, which oversees the Colorado River ecosystem from the dam to Lake Mead. The volume contains about 30 papers in four sessions plus two posters. Most are labeled "Preliminary Results—Study in Progress." Topics include:

- sediment and sand-bar dynamics
- water quality and the aquatic food base
- native and non-native fish monitoring
- riparian and wetland vegetation
- endangered birds and snails
- cultural and recreational resources

The most prominent management themes are the conservation of the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], the trade-offs of experimental flows (especially [[Steady Flow Experiment]] proposals), and the need for baseline data before any risky action.

## Background & Description

The Colorado River corridor from the forebay of [[Glen Canyon Dam]] to upper [[Lake Mead]] is described as the longest undeveloped riparian segment in the United States, though it differs greatly from its natural state. The dam was completed in 1963.

The [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] of 1992 (Public Law 102-575), the 1995 Glen Canyon Dam Environmental Impact Statement (GCDEIS), and the 1996 Record of Decision (ROD) created the Glen Canyon Dam Adaptive Management Program. It has four parts:

- the [[Adaptive Management Work Group]] (AMWG), a Federal Advisory Committee of 27 stakeholders that sets objectives and information needs
- a Technical Work Group
- the GCMRC, formally established in October 1996, which runs monitoring and research
- an Independent Review Panel

The GCMRC's monitoring strategy rests on four principles. The ecosystem is bottom-up, driven by geomorphology, climate, and dam operations. It is strongly human-dominated after more than a century of non-native species introductions and dam construction. Stakeholder mandates conflict. Independent, peer-reviewed science is the most credible basis. The program uses a protocol evaluation program (PEP) for each resource area, followed by pilot monitoring.

The volume is organized into four sessions:

1. The aquatic food base and its controls (water quality, sediment, hydrology)
2. Synthesis of historic physical and fisheries data, and conceptual modeling
3. Endangered terrestrial species and physical-habitat linkages
4. Cultural and recreational resources linked to physical processes

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sediment and sand bars.** Dam operations have changed sediment supply. Tributary floods, especially the [[Paria River]] and the [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR), deliver sediment that travels downstream as "sediment waves." Post-dam sand storage is controlled mainly by flows that build or erode sand bars, eddies, and channel beds.

**Water quality.** Above-average inflows to [[Lake Powell]] since 1993 have lowered salinity and conductivity throughout the reservoir and tailwater. Hypolimnetic temperatures have risen, and tailwater mean temperatures went from 8°C to over 9°C.

**Aquatic food base.** Food availability below the dam is generally low. Autochthonous (in-river) production is consumed quickly, and tributary sediment limits benthic invertebrate communities. Daily fluctuating flows also affect carbon production.

**Backwaters and vegetation.** Backwaters (return-current channels) provide warm, low-velocity habitat for native fish. Their area is reduced by sediment deposition and by terrestrialization. Flow management is intended to rejuvenate them.

**Terrestrial and cultural resources.** The dampened hydrograph has allowed marsh and riparian vegetation to expand. Archaeological sites on pre-dam terraces are eroding, and the cause (dam operations or climate) is still being tested.

## Key Findings

### Sediment and Geomorphology
- **Sediment waves (Topping et al.):** After a large tributary flood, the sediment-wave front passes Diamond Creek within a few days. The finest half of the new sand passes after about a month. All of it may leave Grand Canyon within 80 days to about a year. Managers must therefore act quickly after tributary floods. Options include short high flows to store sand, or reduced dam releases until a high flow is feasible.
- **Pre- vs. post-dam storage (Rubin and Topping):** Pre-dam multi-year storage was less than 0.1%. The dam has caused net depletion of fine sediment. Seasonal storage was greatest when tributary input was high and mainstem discharge was low.
- **Ungaged tributaries (Webb and Griffiths):** More than 700 ungaged tributaries drain almost 13,000 km². They yield an average of 200 tonnes of sediment per km² of drainage area. That totals about 26 million tonnes every 10 years, with nearly 8 million tonnes per decade added in Marble Canyon (about 2 million tonnes of it sand). Debris flows contribute only 1% of sediment yield.
- **Sand bars (Parnell et al.):** Thirty-five eddy sand bars were monitored from April 1996 to April 1998. The 1996 Beach/Habitat-Building Flow (BHBF) temporarily reversed erosion of high-elevation bars. By April 1998, high-elevation bar volume was about 96% above pre-BHBF levels in Marble Canyon and 45% above in Grand Canyon. However, upper Marble Canyon bar volume was at its lowest monitored level, 27% below 1991 baseline.
- **Campsites:** Most campable sand-bar area occurs between 708 and 1,699 m³/s. Future controlled floods need not exceed 1,699 m³/s to affect campsites.
- **Long-term sand bars (Grams and Schmidt):** No long-term bar degradation was found at sites more than 95 km below the dam. Progressive degradation has occurred further upstream.

### Water Quality and Food Base
- **Water quality (Hueftle and Vernieu):** The hypolimnion diluted from a maximum of 1,050 µS/cm in 1991 to 600 µS/cm. Among nutrients, only nitrate-nitrite tracks dilution. Phosphorus and ammonia rose lake-wide after 1991, with a substantial increase in 1996.
- **Macroinvertebrates (Blinn et al.):** In 1997, the first year of full use of EIS-mandated flows (minimum 142 m³/s, maximum 708 m³/s), benthic standing mass fell at all sites. Macroinvertebrate biomass dropped to the lowest level recorded, 1991's. The filamentous alga *Oscillatoria* returned to the varial zone, which will impede recovery.
- **Thermal modification:** The authors say the environmental analysis of dam thermal modification is inadequate. They ask GCMRC to start a full environmental impact statement and gather baseline data.

### Native and Non-Native Fishes
- **Historic synthesis (Valdez and Carothers):** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's 1995 Biological Opinion found that dam operations jeopardize the razorback sucker and the humpback chub. The razorback sucker is virtually extinct in Grand Canyon. The humpback chub, though reproducing in the LCR, has declined over a 35-year record.
- **Steady flow experiment:** The Reasonable and Prudent Alternative proposed sustained releases in April–May, then low steady flows in June–October. This would be done only in low-release years (below 8.23 million acre-feet). Workshop biologists agreed that steady flows would likely help native fish but would also benefit non-native predators and competitors. The authors recommend a non-native fish control strategy before any steady flow experiment.
- **Native fish monitoring 1998 (Gorman et al.):** About 120 field days between March 1998 and January 1999 captured 4,771 fish. In the mainstem, non-natives were 1,368 of 2,335 fish (59%). Rainbow trout (n=868, 37%) were the most common non-native. Among 967 native mainstem fish, speckled dace (n=403, 17%) and humpback chub (n=391, 17%) were most abundant. In the LCR, native species made up 1,362 of 1,600 fish (85%), with humpback chub at n=914 (57%). Mini-hoopnets revealed humpback chub in the 80–200 mm size class, a population feature not previously observed.
- **Parasites and condition (Gorman et al.):** Mainstem fish had higher condition factor and body fat and lower parasite infection rates than LCR and tributary fish. Anchorworm (*Lernaea cyprinacea*) occurred in 0–5% of fish, and Asian tapeworm (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) in 12–32%. Both were lower than in previous years, possibly due to prolonged spring tributary flooding.
- **Survival rates (Walters and Korman):** For humpback chub, the best-fit apparent survival was 84%, versus CAPTURE-based estimates of 75–85%. That implies a stable population at 85% or a decline of about 9% per year at 75%. The authors judge the lower end unlikely. For flannelmouth sucker, apparent survival was 75%, versus a CAPTURE estimate of 90%, which suggests the population is not declining. Results are preliminary.
- **Rainbow trout (McKinney et al.):** Dam operations are the main driver of rainbow trout standing stocks in the Lees Ferry reach (1991–1997). Higher, more stable minimum releases support greater stocks. Small trout (<305 mm) respond to physical factors. Fish of 305–405 mm appear food-limited due to competition, and their densities are negatively associated with *Gammarus* and gastropod densities.
- **Recruitment model (Walters and Korman):** A model for Lees Ferry rainbow trout predicted a significant rise in juvenile recruitment starting in 1990, coinciding with reduced diurnal fluctuations under interim flows.
- **Conceptual risks:** A temperature control device (TCD) could benefit native fish but might stimulate carp, brown trout, and channel catfish. The authors argue that risky options such as a TCD should wait until reliable baseline abundance indices exist.

### Endangered Species and Riparian Systems
- **Kanab ambersnail (Jonas et al.; Sorensen et al.):** Population estimates at Vasey's Paradise in 1998 were 9,500 (April), 7,000 (May), 8,000 (July), and 16,000 (September). More than 100 springs and seeps were surveyed without finding wild snails. In 1998, 450 snails were translocated to three new sites (150 each) in Grand Canyon National Park. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service requires a second population before another BHBF, and three years of monitoring may be needed to confirm self-sustainability.
- **Southwestern willow flycatcher (Spence and Tibbitts):** In 1998, the species reached its lowest abundance since surveys began in 1982, with a single pair at river mile 50.5L. Their three hatched young disappeared and were presumed predated. No cowbird parasitism occurred.
- **Birds overall:** Winter surveys documented 82 terrestrial species in the riparian zone. Three species were new to the region. Up to 14 bald eagles were located. The 1998 breeding season recorded 126 species in total.
- **Vegetation (Kearsley and Ayers):** Inundated near-shore vegetation is an order of magnitude more abundant than reattachment-bar backwater habitats. Sampling effort in floristic and structural work exceeds the minimum needed.
- **Backwater habitats (Parnell et al.):** Deposition in the 1996 experimental flow reduced backwater area at several sites. No scour was observed in return-current channels, so the experiment did not achieve its rejuvenation goal.

### Cultural and Recreational Resources
- **Archaeological sites (Neal and Gilpin):** Of 338 sites, about 249 are actively monitored. In Grand Canyon, 94 are stable and 66 are "most disturbed." In Glen Canyon, 15 are stable and 12 need immediate remedial action. Seventy-three sites have received preservation treatment, and 14 have undergone data recovery.
- **Terrace erosion (Thompson and Potochnik):** Preliminary climate research shows no difference in intense monsoon frequency between the pre- and post-dam 31-year periods. About 70% of studied catchments show deposition from 1983 and 1996 high flows, which may help mitigate gully erosion.
- **Hopi ethnobotany (Yeatts and Ferguson):** The fall 1998 river trip documented 52 plant species, including 17 new ones. The final report is due by the end of 1999.
- **Recreation (Stewart et al.):** The study surveys anglers, rafters, commercial patrons, and guides on flow-related preferences, including beach size and number. A mailback survey was scheduled for Spring 1999, with a draft report in August 1999.

### Conclusions and Recommendations
- Experimental actions need clearly stated management objectives, event-based monitoring, and baseline data.
- Non-native control should be in place before any steady flow experiment.
- The Lees Ferry tailwater, LCR, and key tributary mouths are priority monitoring sites for humpback chub.
- Future research should quantify tributary carbon transfer using stable isotopes, and study the effects of diurnal fluctuations on carbon production.
