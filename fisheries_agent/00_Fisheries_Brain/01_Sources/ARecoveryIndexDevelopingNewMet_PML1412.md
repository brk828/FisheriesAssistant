# Recovery Indices for Endangered Species: Tracking Progress Under the U.S. Endangered Species Act

#ichthyology #marine-biology #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This article by [[Timothy Male]], [[Michael J. Bean]], and [[Scott Walsh]] (Environmental Defense) argues that the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) lacks quantitative metrics for judging whether it is achieving species recovery. The authors propose a family of **[[Recovery Index]]** measures, built from annual wild population counts for 30 well-funded listed species, to track progress objectively. Three weighting methods are tested: [[Abundance-Weighted Recovery Index]], [[Standardized Recovery Index]], and [[Growth-Weighted Recovery Index]]. The authors conclude that the growth-weighted version is the most useful metric, while cautioning that the 30-species set is illustrative rather than ideal.

## Background & Description

- **Problem:** The ESA (1973) aims to prevent extinctions and promote recovery, but the debate over its success persists because few metrics exist. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (USFWS) reports progress under the [[Government Performance and Results Act]] using counts of species categorized as "improving" or "stable." The authors argue these categories are imprecise, subjective, and amount to the agency "grading its own performance."
- **Recovery criteria:** Recovery plans commonly rely on three overlapping criteria: (1) number of individuals in the wild, (2) number of viable wild populations, and (3) progress in removing or controlling threats.
- **Analogy:** The authors base the index on the [[Dow Jones Industrial Average]], which tracks 30 large U.S. companies that represent about one quarter of U.S. stock market value. A few "blue chip" companies dominate market value in the same way a few species dominate endangered species funding.
- **Funding concentration:** About 85 percent of federal and state endangered species expenditures reported by USFWS went to just 75 species. The authors ranked these 75 species by total recovery and land acquisition funding from 1989 to 2004.
- **Data sources:** Population counts were drawn from technical reports and peer-reviewed publications. Totals included wild individuals, mostly adults or breeding adults. Multi-year range-wide census data from 1985 to 2005 were found for 30 species. These 30 were selected because data were available, not because they are ideal. Funding and population data are available from the first author on request.
- **Gap filling:** Most species lacked annual data, so the authors used the compound annual growth rate equation to estimate missing years and project populations for species lacking estimates in the last two years of the sampling period.

### Species Set

The 30 species include birds, mammals, reptiles, and fishes, spanning marine, aquatic, and terrestrial life histories. Representative species include:

- [[California Condor]] (*Gymnogyps californianus*): highest growth rate, +16.5% per year
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): lowest growth rate, -25.1% per year; an endangered Colorado River basin fish
- [[Atlantic Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*): -10.9% per year
- [[Hawaiian Monk Seal]] (*Monachus schauinslandi*): +2.2% per year, 1,409 individuals cited in 2001
- [[Wood Stork]] (*Mycteria americana*): 19,142 individuals estimated in 2001
- [[Indiana Bat]] (*Myotis sodalis*): -1.07% per year
- [[Steller Sea Lion]] (*Eumetopias jubatus*): -4.07% per year

Full growth-rate data for all 30 species are in the source's Table 1 and are not reproduced here.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Diversity of life histories:** The authors emphasize that a future index should include marine, aquatic, and terrestrial species from varied regions of the country.
- **Taxonomic bias:** Birds, mammals, and fishes are disproportionately represented among the best-funded species and include a lower percentage of declining species than other groups (Male and Bean 2005). A more taxonomically balanced set is recommended.
- **Annual plants:** Counts of individuals may be inappropriate for species with large year-to-year fluctuations. For these, the number of populations or element occurrences may be more robust (Wilcove et al. 2006).
- **Data quality:** Changes in census methods, discovery of new populations, and chance variation in the share of individuals counted all influenced year-to-year estimates. The authors nonetheless argue that the trends reflect real changes in the number of wild individuals.
- **Historical context:** The Dow grew at an annual compound rate of 8.3 percent from December 1973 (when the ESA was signed) to April 2006, illustrating the kind of simple, comprehensible index the authors envision.

## Key Findings

### Index Performance (1985–2005)

| Index | Key result |
|---|---|
| [[Abundance-Weighted Recovery Index]] | Total abundance declined 1.71% per year from 1985 to 2001, then rose 4.77% per year after 2001. Driven by large populations and declines in the Indiana bat, Steller sea lion, and razorback sucker. |
| [[Standardized Recovery Index]] | Compound annual growth of 22.2%. Increased in every year except a 55% decrease (1989–1990) and a 4.1% decrease (2001–2002). Hypothetical starting value of 10,000 grew more than 5,500% by 2005 (set at 1.0 in 1985 in the text). |
| [[Growth-Weighted Recovery Index]] | Average annual growth of 5.0%. Negative only in 2001–2002. A hypothetical 10,000 individuals in 1985 reached 31,897 by 2005 (about 3.2 times the starting value; the text describes this as "almost 320 percent"). |

### Overall Results

- Across all species and years, the average growth rate was +4.26% per year, although several species declined across the 20-year period.
- The two species removed from the endangered list during the period, the [[Aleutian Canada Goose]] and [[Eastern Brown Pelican]], had large positive effects on the abundance index after 1994.
- The 30 species received about 25 percent of reported funding from 1989 to 2004, yet represent less than one percent of listed species.

### Methodological Comparisons

- In the abundance-weighted index, five species accounted for 78 percent of variation. For example, the 120,000 Steller sea lions in 1985 outweighed the 39 Puerto Rican parrots in 1994.
- In the standardized index, no single species accounted for more than 4.1 percent of variation. However, it must be recalculated every year because species means and standard deviations change with new data.
- Under the growth-weighted index, individual species growth varied widely. In 1990, growth ranged from -46 percent for the [[Puerto Rican Parrot]] (*Amazona vittata*) to +38 percent for the [[California Least Tern]], with a mean across species that the source gives as 3.1 percent (OCR-garbled in the original).

### Conclusions and Recommendations

- The authors conclude that the **growth-weighted Recovery Index** is likely the most useful measure of recovery progress.
- The abundance-weighted index is less robust because it is dominated by a few species.
- Future index development should use a taxonomically balanced, well-funded species set with frequent range-wide surveys, diverse life histories, and geographic representation.
- Adopting such an index would give agencies a strong incentive to collect increasingly accurate population data.
