# Coded Wire Snout Tag for Inland Fishery Studies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #trout

## Executive Summary

This short technical note by [[Charles Berry]] of the [[Utah Cooperative Fishery Unit]] at [[Utah State University]] describes the use of a [[Coded Wire Tag]] injected into the snout of fingerling fish as a method for individual or group identification in inland fisheries. Coded wire tagging had been used extensively in [[Pacific Salmon Fisheries]] but, before this work, had seen limited inland use in two studies: [[Pyramid Lake]], Nevada (cutthroat trout) and [[Porcupine Reservoir]], Utah ([[Rainbow Trout]]). The note reports tagging mortality, tag retention, field detection, and the practical advantages and costs of the [[Snout Tagging]] method.

## Background & Description

The tagging system uses a small steel wire tag injected into the snout of an anesthetized fingerling. Each tag is approximately 0.25 mm in diameter and 1 mm long, and is permanently etched on three sides with a binary code. A [[Magnetic Detector]] allows tagged fish to be located in the field. Tagged fish are identified by removing the snout or head, returning it to the laboratory, extracting the tag, and reading it under a dissecting microscope.

The source cites the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (1978) special report on the coded wire tagging program as the basis for the wider marine use of the technique.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The note focuses on fish handling and marking rather than on ecosystem conditions. Its relevance to aquatic ecology lies in the trade-offs of marking: the tag must not alter growth or behavior, must persist over the fish's life, and must be recoverable without harming the study. Snout tagging is also compatible with studies that require head removal, such as surveys for [[Eye Fluke Disease]]. Habitat information is limited to the two reservoir and lake sites where the method was applied.

## Key Findings

**Tagging mortality and handling effects**
- Tagging mortality in [[Rainbow Trout]] was reported as less than 0.005% (the value is partly illegible in the scan). Most mortality was attributed to stress from handling and anesthesia, citing [[Hudy]] (1980).

**Tag retention**
- Tag loss after 2 weeks averaged 3.5%.
- No tag loss occurred between 2 weeks and 3 months, as the tag becomes encapsulated by wound-healing tissue.
- One group showed an unexplained high tag loss of 20%.

**Field detection and recovery**
- Tagged fish could be readily detected after one year in the wild.
- Tag removal and reading in the laboratory took about 1 to 2 minutes per fish.

**Advantages of snout tagging**
1. No detectable effect on fish growth or behavior.
2. Tag retention over the life of the fish.
3. Rapid tagging, at 20 to 60 fish per minute depending on the number of operators.
4. Ability to distinctly mark a large number of fish groups.
5. Compatibility with specialized studies requiring head removal.

**Disadvantages**
1. Equipment cost of about $10,000 for tagging and about $3,000 for the detector.
2. Time required for tag removal and reading (1 to 2 minutes per fish).

**Overall assessment**
The author concludes that advantages and disadvantages are relative to the alternative marking methods being compared. The reference list includes [[Hudy]] (1980) on six [[Rainbow Trout]] strains stocked as fingerlings in Porcupine Reservoir, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1978) special report.
