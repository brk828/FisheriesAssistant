# Food Habits of the Southern Channel Catfish in the Des Moines River, Iowa

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This study examines the diet of the [[Southern Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus lacustris punctatus*) in a short reach of the [[Des Moines River]] in Iowa. Stomachs from 912 fish (769 containing food) were collected from September 1940 to October 1941 and analyzed by volume, frequency of occurrence, and item count. The catfish proved omnivorous, with [[Aquatic Insects]] and fish as staple foods, seasonal use of [[American Elm]] seeds, and rare use of other organisms. Diet shifted with body size, season, water clarity, temperature, and breeding condition. The authors link these patterns to food availability and shelter, and they recommend habitat diversity for management of prairie river catfish.

## Background & Description

The authors frame food habits as a central driver of fish ecology. Growth depends on nutrition, competition for food can cause stunting, and predation on eggs and fry can limit populations. Bottom feeders such as carp and suckers can also increase turbidity and harm game species. The study was designed as an intensive investigation at one locality, so its results apply to that ecological setting. Its objectives were to:

1. Determine the qualitative and quantitative composition of the diet across young-of-year, juvenile, and adult fish.
2. Determine daily and seasonal feeding trends.
3. Test whether prey selection reflects preference or simply relative abundance, size, and behavior.
4. Assess the effects of temperature, water-level fluctuation, and turbidity on feeding.

The authors note that field work was shortened, and heavy spring and early summer 1941 rains limited the specimens available for some size and season classes.

Fish were collected by three methods:

- Angler-provided stomachs from 22 legal-sized fish.
- Seining with a 25- by 6-foot net, which worked well for young fish but poorly for adults in deep, swift water.
- [[Bank Pole Fishing]] at night, using baited hooks on willow poles, which was the main method for adults.

Stomach contents were sorted into insects, fish, plants, and miscellaneous items. Volumes were estimated as percentages, and items were counted under a dissecting microscope. Fish were grouped into four size classes:

- Under 4 inches
- 4.0 to 7.9 inches
- 8.0 to 11.9 inches
- 12 inches or more (legal size in Iowa)

Growth was roughly 4 inches per year over the first 3 years, so the first three classes correspond approximately to the first, second, and third years of life.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Physical setting.** The study reach had a stream gradient of 2.53 feet per mile. Average summer width was about 200 feet, and surface area was about 18 acres. The channel alternated between long pools (about 8 feet deep), rubble riffles, and shifting sand bars. Outside meander banks were steep and eroded, periodically felling trees that formed [[Drift Jams]]. Mean discharge at Boone (1921–1940) was 1,240 second-feet, ranging from 17 to 24,300.

**Biotic setting.** Aquatic vegetation and most invertebrates were scarce, while the fish fauna was rich. The benthos was sparse: 461 organisms (11.45 cubic centimeters) were collected in 81 square feet with a [[Surber Sampler]], an average of 14.9 organisms per square foot. Nearly all were insects. In contrast, drift jams, submerged brush, and logs held a rich insect fauna and abundant bryozoans (*Plumatella fungosa*), although they covered less than 1% of the stream area. The authors suggest these structures are central to fish food production.

**Fish community.** About fifty fish species occur in or near the study area. Most are small forage species, mainly minnows and darters. The [[Spotfin Shiner]] (*Notropis spilopterus*) and [[Sand Shiner]] (*Notropis deliciosus*) together made up about 60% of small fish. Other game and pan species included walleye, smallmouth bass, and several crappies and bullheads, plus rare northern pike and yellow perch. Non-game species, especially carp and at least ten suckers, were likely much heavier in total biomass than the game fish. The authors recommend further study of their interactions with channel catfish.

**Earlier diet studies.** The paper reviews prior work from Forbes (1888) on Illinois and Mississippi river fish (insects 44% of volume, with mollusks also important), and studies by McAtee and Weed, Shira, Moore, Sibley, Ewers and Boesel, Boesel, McCormick, Rice, Dill, Menzel, and Dendy. These reported omnivory, with variable importance of mollusks, crustaceans, and fish across waters. The authors attribute the Des Moines River's low use of crustaceans, mollusks, and algae to their rarity there.

## Key Findings

**Overall diet**
- The channel catfish is omnivorous. Some 50 families of insects were recorded, and an [[Insect Identification List]] of many genera is provided in the source.
- Insects and fish are staples. Plant seeds are taken in season, and other items appear in small numbers.
- Four insect orders contribute most of the diet: [[Diptera]], [[Ephemeroptera]], [[Trichoptera]], and [[Coleoptera]].
- Within Diptera, [[Chironomidae]] (tendipedids, or midges) dominate the diet of young catfish, with [[Simuliidae]] (blackflies) also common.
- Mayflies were mainly *Potamanthus*, *Pentagenia*, and *Heptagenia* nymphs. Caddis flies were dominated by larval [[Hydropsyche]].
- Minnows (Cyprinidae) were the dominant fish food, with at least 14 species recorded.

**Size-related shifts**
- Fish under 4 inches ate insects in 99% of stomachs, which made up 98% of food volume. Insects were 81% of aquatic insects eaten by this group, and Diptera were the bulk.
- Fish 4–12 inches still relied mainly on insects, but ate larger mayflies and caddis flies more often. Small fish and plant seeds became more important.
- Fish 12 inches or more ate insects in 80% of stomachs, but insects made up only 28% of volume. Fish were more important volumetrically, and the diet was more varied, with more terrestrial insects.
- Trichoptera made up 8% of aquatic insects eaten by young catfish but 72% of those eaten by fish 12 inches or longer.
- Forage fish averaged about 1 per 2–3 stomachs in half-grown fish, and nearly 2 per stomach in legal-sized fish.
- Fish 8 inches or longer averaged over 20 elm seeds per stomach. One 12.5-inch fish held 1,129 seeds.
- Fish eggs appeared in five catfish (4–8 inches, taken in May), with no stomach containing more than three. The authors judged channel catfish to be minor egg predators here.
- Cannibalism matched the abundance of young catfish: they made up 2% of small fish in random seine samples and 2% of fish found in stomachs.

**Seasonal and availability patterns**
- The most marked seasonal signal was in plant food, almost all terrestrial. [[American Elm]] seeds were eaten heavily in May and June, and wild grape seeds appeared only in fall. Corn appeared in late summer and fall.
- Terrestrial insects peaked during the May beetle flight, and most other aerial insects were eaten in late summer and fall.
- Benthic samples showed Diptera falling from 68% of insects in spring to 16% by September. Trichoptera rose from 10% to 81%, and Ephemeroptera fell from 11% to 2%.
- Diptera remained important in the diet of fish under 12 inches in spring. Trichoptera became dominant in summer and fall, outnumbering Diptera in fish over 8 inches.
- Ephemeroptera showed no drop in stomach frequency and were eaten most often in summer.
- The authors suggest that floating debris masses harbored many mayflies not captured by bottom sampling, which may explain the mismatch with diet.

**Prey selection**
- Forage fish proportions in stomachs closely matched their proportions in seine samples. The spotfin shiner was 49% of river fish and 43% of fish eaten. The sand shiner was 18% and 17%. The bullhead minnow was 5% and 10%. These three species made up 72% of forage fish and 70% of fish eaten.
- The authors conclude catfish show no marked selection among fish prey, though habitat overlap may slightly increase vulnerability of some species.
- Predation on aquatic insects roughly tracked their abundance, but the authors caution that abundance does not equal availability, since some prey (such as caddis larvae in cases) are harder to catch.

**Feeding behavior and environment**
- Low, clear water in September 1941 increased forage fish predation. Legal fish averaged 3.5 forage fish in September, compared with 0.7 in August and 0.5 in July, when water was higher and more turbid.
- Feeding peaked from sundown to about midnight. Catches from dusk to midnight were about twice as heavy as those before dawn, and fish taken at dusk often had empty stomachs.
- Bright moonlight during clear-water periods appeared to inhibit nocturnal feeding, according to a 1946 observation by the junior author. Turbid conditions did not seem to change night feeding.
- Feeding continued between 50°F and 94°F with no clear temperature threshold. At over 90°F, 64% of fish had stomachs at least one-quarter full. Between 50°F and 60°F, 54% did.
- Winter feeding was rare. In February 1940, only 2 of 17 large catfish taken from a frozen river at 32°F had any food.
- Adults appear to stop feeding during the breeding season. A male guarding a nest had an empty stomach, and baited traps catch few fish during spawning, which the authors read as evidence of a temporary halt in feeding.

**Management implications**
- In normal water years, reproduction and early survival of channel catfish in prairie rivers are usually high. Severe early or mid-summer floods appeared to reduce reproduction or survival.
- Large numbers of legal-sized catfish depend mainly on shelter and food supply. Long, straight, uniform reaches with shifting sand bottoms provide neither.
- Recommended habitat measures include deep pools, lateral lagoons, backwaters (valuable as flood refuges), snags, submerged logs, boulders, rock riffles, and overhanging trees.
- Drift jams along eroding banks were considered among the richest food and shelter sites. Anchoring felled trees with cables was proposed as a cheap method that also protects shorelines.
- Bank protection through streamside planting and removal of livestock grazing was also recommended. The authors point to soil conservation and pollution abatement in Iowa as complementary efforts.
