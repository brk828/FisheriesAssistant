# Dexter National Fish Hatchery & Technology Center: FY-2003 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-fish

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service annual report for fiscal year 2003 documents the activities of the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]] in Dexter, New Mexico. The Center propagates and maintains imperiled native fishes of the American Southwest, conducts applied research in [[Molecular Ecology]], marking techniques and water reuse, and supports recovery programs under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. In FY-2003 the station held 16 listed species and 1 species of special concern. Major outputs included bonytail, Colorado pikeminnow, razorback sucker, woundfin and Rio Grande silvery minnow production, the Phase III facilities improvement project, and a broad outreach program. The report also includes water quality data, production tables, ADA accessibility review findings, and monitoring reports for bonytail and Rio Grande silvery minnow rearing.

## Background & Description

The hatchery was authorized under the White Act of 1930 and acquired in 1931 for $3,265.90. It covers 640 acres in Chaves County at an elevation of 3,500 feet. Operations began in 1932 as a warmwater game fish hatchery producing [[Largemouth Bass]], smallmouth bass, bluegill and [[Channel Catfish]] for southwestern reservoirs. After the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973, the station was dedicated to imperiled fishes beginning in 1978, with three goals: establish a refuge for critically imperiled fishes, develop spawning and culture techniques, and produce fish for reintroduction. It was designated a Technology Center in FY-1991, one of seven in the Fish & Wildlife Service.

Fish culture water comes from five shallow aquifer wells totaling more than 2,000 gallons per minute. Reported well water characteristics are 64 °F, pH 7.2–8.5, total hardness 2,100 ppm and total dissolved solids 3,500 ppm. Facilities include 46 earthen or lined ponds (0.1–1.0 acre), fiberglass raceways, circular and rectangular tanks, aquaria, and three water reuse systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The station sits on the northern fringe of the Chihuahua Desert, with 12 inches average rainfall and a 180–200 day growing season. Hatchery effluent collects in two sumps and supports wetlands within a Wildlife Management Area co-managed with the New Mexico Department of Game and Fish.

Water quality was sampled in 2003 by the New Mexico State University Soil, Water, Air Testing Lab, mainly to establish a baseline because of nearby dairy operations and the risk of fecal leaching into the shallow aquifer. Sampled sources included Pond 5-B, the Reuse System and East Bay Well No. 1. Dissolved oxygen was 9.68 mg/L at the pond and 7.47 mg/L at the reuse system. Retesting is proposed for 2004.

Regional drought affected several species. In June 2002, New Mexico Department of Game and Fish rescued fish from the Pecos River, including [[Pecos Bluntnose Shiner]], speckled chub, red shiner, sand shiner and Rio Grande shiner. Dexter kept bluntnose shiners to build a refugium population, and about 50% of these fish were gravid females.

## Key Findings

**Species holdings and status**
- 16 listed species and 1 species of special concern held in FY-2003. All but one, the Pahranagat roundtail chub, come from New Mexico or neighboring states and Mexico.
- Listed species include [[Bonytail]] (endangered, "most endangered fish in North America"), [[Colorado Pikeminnow]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Woundfin]], [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]], [[Chihuahua Chub]], [[Virgin River Chub]], [[Pahranagat Roundtail Chub]] and [[Yaqui Sucker]] (listed as species of concern in the source).
- Other captive stocks without active augmentation include Leon Springs pupfish, Gila topminnow, Big Bend gambusia, desert pupfish and Guzman beautiful shiner. Each stock is about 500–1,000 individuals.

**Bonytail program**
- The broodstock contained five year classes and more than 2,500 individuals. In 2003, 2,118 fish were PIT tagged and stocked at Lake Havasu (Partners Point).
- Table 2 distribution totaled 248,640 fish across destinations, including 202,500 fry to the Colorado River Fishery Project at Vernal, Utah.
- A 2003 pond rearing completion report recorded 3,400 fish harvested, 97.7% survival over the grow-out, and 12.1% reaching the 250 mm target. An additional 2,453 fish were PIT tagged for Lake Havasu.
- Fish health inspection found Asian tapeworm, treated with Droncit.

**Razorback sucker**
- Broodstock development began in 1994 and was scheduled for completion in 2004. The 1981 year class was spawned on March 20.
- 18,000 larvae from Yuma and Tequila coves in Lake Mohave were received for rearing before stocking below Parker Dam.
- In a 2003 augmentation run, 92.5% survival was reported, and 44.8% reached 305 mm.

**Colorado pikeminnow**
- Spawning totaled 2,996,506 eggs across four events. Broodstock came from 1974 Yampa River stock and 1981 Colorado/Green River stock.
- Distribution totaled 238,482 fish, including 210,418 fingerlings to the San Juan River Basin Recovery Implementation Program.

**Rio Grande silvery minnow**
- 20 wild-origin pairs yielded about 30,000 eggs. Approximately 170,000 wild-spawned and 30,000 captive-spawned fish were stocked into five ponds.
- 113,850 fish from the 2002 year class were marked with VIE tags and released in 2003. 102,454 minnows were marked for field identification.
- VIE tag retention was 98% over one month and over 90% over eight months. A dip-based calcein batch marking method was tested, with up to 1,000 fish per minute reported.

**Woundfin and Virgin River chub**
- Woundfin was cultured since 1979. In fall 2003, 2,800 fish were shipped to Utah. In 2003, 2,270 VIE-marked and 2,199 calcein-marked fish were stocked in the Virgin River.
- Adult survival in one ponding season ranged from 77.8% to 91.4%.

**Chihuahua chub, Pahranagat roundtail chub and Yaqui sucker**
- 14,752 Chihuahua chub progeny were wire tagged and released in the Mimbres River with The Nature Conservancy.
- The Pahranagat roundtail chub population was increased from 68 to 500 fish. Field surveys found only 14 fish, so the species is considered effectively extinct in the wild.
- Only 7 Yaqui suckers remained in 2003. A spawning attempt failed to produce viable eggs.

**Genetics and molecular ecology**
- Microsatellite markers were developed for bonytail, with 11 informative markers used to genotype all adults. Statistical analysis placed them into five founding families with 92% accuracy.
- Work also covered Colorado pikeminnow, razorback sucker, humpback chub, woundfin and Rio Grande silvery minnow markers.

**Partnerships and outreach**
- Cooperative projects included the Lake Mohave Razorback Sucker Roundup, the Virgin River Recovery Program, the San Juan River program and the Upper-Middle Rio Grande Ecosystem team.
- Public outreach reached more than 5,700 visitors at Mescalero and state fair booths and about 2,000 at the Dragonfly Festival. The station also served as the run site for the Milkman Triathlon.
- A dragonfly and damselfly checklist identified 71 Odonata species, including 31 damselflies and 40 dragonflies.

**Infrastructure and accessibility**
- Phase III construction, completed June 5, 2003, added 23 tenth-acre ponds, two quarter-acre ponds, four replaced concrete raceways and two HDPE-lined ponds.
- An ADA review (March 21, 2003) found the 1995 administration, culture and shop buildings largely compliant. The 1981 visitor center had the most deficiencies. Estimated costs were $5,410 for doors and $3,500 for restrooms. Display renovation requires at least $150,000.

**Budget and workforce**
- Operational costs rose from $436,856 (FY-1999) to $661,549 (FY-2003).
- Full-time equivalents rose from 8 to 11.38. Eight volunteers contributed 631 hours in 2003.
