# A Guideline for Portable Direct Current Electrofishing Systems

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources Technical Bulletin (No. 31, 1971) develops a field guideline for portable, hand-held direct current (DC) [[Electrofishing]] systems used to capture fish in Wisconsin streams. The authors argue that anode current is a good electrical measure of electrofishing effect, and that less current is needed as [[Water Conductivity]] falls. Using 50 operator survey forms from 15 people, five field trips, and conductivities of 50 to 450 micromhos/cm, the authors derived a minimum required current curve, a recommended current band, and a practical lookup table (Table 4) linking water conductivity, generator voltage and suggested [[Anode Size]]. The bulletin also covers [[Electrode Resistance]], [[Generator Characteristics]], water depth, waveform ripple, and generator power sizing.

## Background & Description

The bulletin notes that many factors affect DC electrofishing, including electrical parameters, water conductivity, stream width and depth, water clarity, temperature, current, target species and operator skill. At the time, Wisconsin equipment was described as inflexible, offering little opportunity to adjust for these factors. The stated objectives were to document equipment variation and techniques in use, clarify the purely electrical parameters that govern performance, and provide a basis for proper electrical use of the equipment.

A basic system is described as having five components:

1. **Power source**: usually an engine-driven DC generator, with rating (kilowatts) matched to stream size (number of anodes) and conductivity range.
2. **[[Cathode]]**: as large as practical; often the boat bottom.
3. **Anodes**: loop-shaped (or spherical) electrodes on insulated handles, not long and slender.
4. **Metering**: voltmeter and ammeter.
5. **Safety switches**: low-voltage relay system for emergency power disconnection.

The theoretical basis is drawn from human electric-shock research (Dalziel and Lee, 1968) and Cuinat (1967): current through the fish, rather than voltage, is the variable most closely tied to the electrotaxis response.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Water conductivity** is the key environmental control. Low-conductivity water makes it hard to force enough current into the water, but less anode current is needed there because the fish becomes a relatively better current path. Conductivities of 50 to 450 micromhos/cm were sampled; no field data existed below 50.
- **Bottom and depth**: sandy or rocky bottoms give the best results because low bottom conductivity keeps current in the water. Deep water requires roughly twice the current to maintain the same effective range.
- **Water temperature** in trials ranged from about 48°F to 70°F. Insufficient data were available to draw conclusions, though its effect on conductivity and fish physiology was acknowledged.
- **Fish body conductivity** is of the order of 500 micromhos/cm or more (Monan and Engstrom, 1963), so the low end of the curve falls where water is far less conductive than fish.
- **Species scope**: most data came from brook and brown trout in shallow streams; warmwater fishes are expected to differ in susceptibility and were not tested.

## Key Findings

**1. Survey basis and effectiveness trend**
- Fifty completed survey forms were collected from 15 persons; five field trips provided firsthand electrical data.
- Operators reported effective or very effective fishing when total current approached about 50% of the current reported by Cuinat (1967). Ineffective fishing was reported only well below this level.
- One high-voltage test (470 V, 16-inch anode, 60 micromhos/cm) was judged very effective, with a catching range of at least 6 feet and no fish injury observed.

**2. Guideline curves (Figures 1 and 2, two anodes)**
- The minimum required current curve (dashed line) was set at about twice the highest "ineffective" data level.
- An upper bound was set at twice the single-electrode current from Cuinat's data, marking the potentially harmful zone. No Wisconsin fish mortality was reported.
- The region between these curves is the expected effective range with portable equipment. Below 50 micromhos/cm the curve is speculative.

**3. Water depth**
- Deep water requires approximately twice the current for the same effective range. Lower resistance in deep immersion can reduce generator voltage and range even as anode current rises.

**4. Generator waveform**
- High-frequency ripple from electronically rectified AC-to-DC generators sluggishly inhibits fish, causing stunning and poor capture. The remedy is an electrical filter.

**5. Electrode resistance (Table 1, Appendix B)**
- Larger electrodes have lower resistance. Resistance varies inversely and linearly with conductivity.
- Measured anode resistance in use averaged about 160% of deep-water measured values, used for shallow-water estimates.
- At 400 micromhos/cm with a 7 ft × 2.5 ft cathode (15 ohms), the 16-inch anode is the lowest-resistance option.
- For large two-anode systems, a 15-ohm cathode is nearly half the total resistance, so enlarging the cathode is recommended.

**6. Anode size, voltage and conductivity (Figures 3 and 4)**
- Using a 250 V supply, the 8-inch anode line exceeds the guideline to a conductivity of 260 in shallow water.
- At 200 V, 8-inch anodes are effective down to a conductivity of 450. Sixteen-inch anodes at 400 V reach down to 25.
- Increasing loop diameter reduces resistance far more than thicker loop material. For an 8-inch loop, going from 0.25 to 0.50-inch material cut resistance only 12%.

**7. Cathode size (Figure 5)**
- The largest possible cathode should be used. Extra flexible cathodes can be dragged behind the boat, but more than two are not advisable. A cathode placed in the stream can be worked upstream to about 100 feet.

**8. Generator characteristics (Figure 6)**
- Voltage regulation is the percentage drop under load, and it bends the operating line as conductivity rises.
- **Homelite DC** (Model 24D230; 230 V, 10.9 A, 2500 W; no-load 264 V; regulation 14.8%): a conventional DC shunt generator, nearly constant-voltage; effective fishing with 12-inch anodes to about conductivity 160; can supply two 12-inch anodes beyond 600.
- **Powermaker AC-DC** (Model EAD-1250; 115 V, 10 A, 1150 W; no-load 260 V; regulation 126%): ripple-heavy and too low in output voltage (down to about 130 V under load) to be effective. A transformer on the AC side could improve performance.
- **T & J** (Model 1736 DCV; 125/250 V, 7 A, 1750 W; no-load 320 V; regulation 28%): the best of the three; effective with 12-inch anodes to about conductivity 100, but reaches its 7 A rating near 400 micromhos/cm, where 8-inch anodes or one 12-inch anode are needed.

**9. One-anode and three-anode operation (Figures 7 and 8)**
- Current per anode falls as more anodes share one generator. One 8-inch anode is about as effective as one of three 16-inch anodes run together, and the single small anode may outperform them because of generator voltage drop.
- Maximum current per anode, and hence range, comes from one large anode with the largest possible cathode.
- One 16-inch anode at 350 V fishes effectively down to conductivity 30, compared with 60 for two 16-inch anodes at 350 V.

**10. Power requirements (Table 3)**
- Two-anode minimum power for shallow streams is roughly 690 to 1000 W, depending on conductivity and anode size.
- Midline power for larger or deeper streams runs roughly 1500 to 4000 W.
- A 1000 W generator is sufficient for shallow streams up to 500 micromhos/cm. About 4000 W is needed in larger streams at that conductivity.
- Single-anode power is about 40% of the two-anode table value. Three-anode power is about 180% (single-anode and three-anode factors are described as 40% and 180% respectively).

**11. Guideline Table (Table 4)**
- Lists minimum and maximum current by conductivity (20 to 500 micromhos/cm) and the suitable anode sizes (8, 12, 16 inches) for voltages 200 to 450 V.
- Operators should use the nearest listed conductivity, then the largest recommended anode. Blank cells mean poor expected results or generator overload.
- Current per anode can be raised by increasing voltage, using larger anodes, using fewer anodes, or enlarging the cathode.

**12. Summary recommendations**
- Anode current is the governing electrical measure. Deep water and large streams need roughly twice the minimum current.
- Operator skill and experience strongly affect results. Cooperative use of two or more anodes to direct fish and use natural barriers improves effectiveness.
- The guideline is a starting point only and needs more field testing, especially at low conductivity, for other species, deep water, and extreme temperature or current.
