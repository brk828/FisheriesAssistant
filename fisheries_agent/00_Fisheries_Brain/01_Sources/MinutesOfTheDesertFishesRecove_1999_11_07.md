## Executive Summary

This document records the proceedings of the **Desert Fishes Recovery Team** meeting held in Phoenix, Arizona (November 7–8, 1990), chaired by [[W.L. Minckley]]. The meeting addressed conservation status and management strategy for federally protected native fishes—primarily [[Spikedace]] and [[Loach Minnow]]—inhabiting [[Aravaipa Creek]], a critical perennial desert stream in the Gila River drainage. Central concerns included the potential invasion of nonnative [[Red Shiner]] into the upper reaches of the creek, design of fish barriers to exclude nonnatives, establishment of long-term monitoring protocols, and initiation of captive propagation programs. A secondary agenda item introduced new genetic modeling work related to the [[Desert Pupfish Recovery Plan]].

## Background & Description

No direct morphological descriptions of the species are provided in this administrative document; the meeting is management- and policy-focused rather than taxonomic. Species referenced include:

- **[[Spikedace]]** (*Meda fulgida*) — small-bodied native cyprinid, subject of draft federal recovery plan.
- **[[Loach Minnow]]** (*Tiaroga cobitis*) — small-bodied benthic native cyprinid, co-occurs with spikedace; recovery plan drafted jointly.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]** (*Cyprinodon macularius*) — small cyprinodontid; genetics and reintroduction population dynamics discussed via [[Jason Dunham]]'s proposed Master's research.
- **[[Red Shiner]]** (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) — nonnative invasive cyprinid identified as a primary ecological threat to native Aravaipa Creek fish assemblage.
- Reference is made to "the other 5 native fishes" of Aravaipa Creek, implying a broader native fish community (unnamed in text) targeted for captive propagation efforts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat**: [[Aravaipa Creek]] is characterized as having a "perennial reach," supporting a diverse native fish assemblage including spikedace, loach minnow, and at least five other native species. The creek includes both wilderness and non-wilderness segments, with the upper reach near [[Turkey Creek]] identified as ecologically distinct and in need of protection.
- **Reproductive Season**: Native fish reproduction in Aravaipa Creek is noted to occur March–October, informing the proposed monthly monitoring schedule during this period.
- **Invasive Threat Dynamics**: The nonnative [[Red Shiner]] poses a displacement risk to native fishes; its distributional status in the upper creek was unresolved at the time of the meeting, prompting a scheduled survey (December 8–9, 1990).
- **Barrier Ecology**: A proposed three-barrier system (lower barrier, Turkey Creek barrier, and a middle buffer barrier) reflects a staged, spatially explicit approach to excluding nonnative fish invasion while preserving native fish refugia — an applied conservation strategy analogous to habitat fragmentation management.
- **Genetic Population Structure**: Discussion of [[Desert Pupfish]] genetics highlights concern over stochastic genetic processes (drift, rare allele loss) in small reintroduced populations, an ecological/genetic niche concern for fragmented desert fish populations.

## Key Findings

1. **Barrier Strategy**: The Recovery Team recommended construction of **three fish barriers** on Aravaipa Creek — a lower barrier (already in planning with Bureau of Reclamation design support), a Turkey Creek barrier to protect the upper creek, and a middle buffer barrier — enabling staged nonnative eradication and long-term management ([[Fish Barrier Management]]).

2. **Red Shiner Assessment**: Consensus could not be reached on the current severity of red shiner presence in upper Aravaipa Creek; a dedicated field survey was scheduled for December 8–9, 1990 to resolve this uncertainty.

3. **Monitoring Protocol**: A continuous, system-wide monitoring program was recommended — monthly during the March–October reproductive season, less frequent in winter — covering all life stages (adults, juveniles, larvae) of the fish community. Estimated effort: 2 field days per survey with a 3-person crew, plus lab analysis time. Cost-sharing was proposed among [[The Nature Conservancy]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], [[Bureau of Land Management]], and Minckley's group.

4. **Population Genetics Priority**: The genetic significance of Aravaipa Creek populations of spikedace and loach minnow (and possibly other natives) relative to the broader species range was flagged as requiring urgent study, to be funded (~$33,000) from existing barrier-related budgets.

5. **Captive Propagation**: Immediate development of captive propagation techniques for spikedace, loach minnow, and five other native fishes was recommended, both for conservation research and as an insurance population against habitat loss or chemical treatment (piscicide) contingencies.

6. **Governance Gap**: The Team identified the need for a formal **oversight body** integrating all agencies, organizations, and academic/private biologists involved in Aravaipa Creek management — indicating a governance gap in coordinated desert fish conservation.

7. **Budget Reallocation**: A previously planned larval drift study was deprioritized, with its allocated funding redirected toward higher-priority monitoring and barrier-related biological studies.

8. **Desert Pupfish Genetics Initiative**: [[Jason Dunham]] proposed a Master's-level modeling study on stochastic genetic effects (e.g., genetic drift, rare allele loss) in reintroduced [[Desert Pupfish]] populations, informed by electrophoretic data from the Dexter population and Arizona reintroduction sites — intended to guide future genetic management (e.g., translocation of genetic material) in real-world reintroductions.

9. **Regulatory Status**: Draft recovery plans for spikedace and loach minnow were published in the Federal Register for public comment (closing November 26, 1990), marking formal progress toward federal recovery plan finalization.
