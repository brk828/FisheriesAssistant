# Management Assessment of the Impacts of Illegally Introduced Northern Pike (*Esox lucius*) at Parker Canyon Lake

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) draft management paper, authored by Don Mitchell (Fisheries Specialist, Region V), assesses the threat posed by illegally introduced [[Northern Pike]] in [[Parker Canyon Lake]], a man-made reservoir in southeastern Arizona. Pike were likely introduced around late 1996 to early 1997. Sampling confirmed reproduction and a population of up to four year classes. The paper concludes that pike threaten the lake's warm-water and [[Rainbow Trout]] fisheries, and could escape through flood spill or illegal transfer to other regional waters, including native fish habitat in the [[Santa Cruz River]] drainage. It evaluates four management options and recommends aggressive removal, preferably by drawdown, vegetation control, and repeated [[Rotenone]] treatment.

## Background & Description

- **Lake setting:** Built in 1962 with Federal Sport Fish Restoration funds and opened in 1964. It sits at about 5,000 ft in oak woodland, with about 82 surface acres and just under 1,600 acre-feet at the time of writing, versus about 124 acres and roughly 3,000 acre-feet when full. The watershed is nine square miles and there are no downstream water rights.
- **Stocking history:** Originally a cold-water trout fishery (25,000 rainbow trout initially). Illegal introductions of [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill Sunfish]], redear, green sunfish and black bullhead in the late 1960s shifted management to a self-sustaining warm-water fishery in the early 1970s. Channel catfish, coho salmon, red shiner, fathead minnow, threadfin shad and freshwater shrimp were also stocked. Nearly one million rainbow trout have been stocked, with over 20,000 per year currently.
- **Angler use:** A 1993 survey estimated 13,937 angler days, with 4,692 in the October to March trout season. Of anglers, 42.1% targeted rainbow trout. The paper estimates trout anglers alone generate $328,252 in direct and $592,740 in indirect annual economic benefit.
- **Management shift:** Region V began moving toward a cold-water, put-grow-and-take emphasis, supported by a zooplankton food study begun in 1998, with a final report expected by the end of 2000.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Pike biology and sampling:** Pike are described as hardy and adaptable. They tolerate low oxygen (as low as 0.1 mg/L for several days in winter) and high temperatures (die-off not reported until 32 °C in one study). The cited habitat criteria for pike in Ohio impoundments were temperatures below 26 °C and dissolved oxygen above 3.0 mg/L.
- **Thermal and oxygen suitability:** Temperature and oxygen profiles from [[Pena Blanca Lake]] and [[Patagonia Lake]] (May to November 1999) were reported to contain zones meeting these criteria, suggesting possible pike survival there.
- **Dispersal pathways:** Escape is considered possible through flood spill, which the lake is expected to experience roughly three years in ten. Downstream creek temperatures are cool enough for survival even in hot months. Downstream habitat is described as small and intermittent, with limited reproduction and long-term survival potential. The Gila topminnow occurs in the upper Santa Cruz River in Mexico.
- **Regional threats:** Other regional high-elevation cold-water lakes, [[Riggs Flat Lake]] and [[Rose Canyon Lake]], are considered classic pike habitat and at high risk from illegal introduction.
- **Native fish concern:** Native fishes in the Santa Cruz and San Pedro drainages are already in a precarious state from other non-native species.

## Key Findings

**Population status**
- Pike were first caught by anglers in summer 1997. Sampling in 1998 (154 net hours) caught one adult, and a further survey (174 net hours) caught two more.
- By late 1999, over 25 pike had been reported caught by anglers, and 45 specimens had been collected overall. Fish were aged at 3 to 4 years, though scale ageing was noted as potentially unreliable.
- September 1999 electrofishing produced three juveniles (283–340 mm) and gill netting (64 net hours) produced three adults (664–770 mm). These confirmed reproduction. A gravid female was collected in December 1999.
- Authors describe the population as likely expanding and healthy, with up to four year classes.

**Impacts on the lake fishery**
- Predation on centrarchids is occurring. Four small pike had largemouth bass fingerlings and two had sunfish. Larger pike shift to a diet of rainbow trout once they reach a size that allows them to take stocked trout.
- A 200-mm rainbow trout was found in one adult's stomach in September 1999.
- Using a stocking rate of about 24,000 catchable trout per year at $2.25 each ($54,000 total), the authors estimate pike losses of $5,625 to $9,450 per year, based on [[Rich (1992)]] loss rates. They expect actual losses could be higher because of the milder climate and longer growing season.
- Literature cited includes Horseshoe Lake, Minnesota (sport fish growth declines over more than 10 years), and Escanaba Lake, Wisconsin (smallmouth bass and bluegill declines). Other studies report mixed results.

**Management options (Figure 8 matrix)**
1. **Adapt management to pike:** No upfront cost, but could cost millions over the long term in lost revenue. It does nothing to prevent escape or expansion.
2. **Selective removal (electrofishing, netting, liberalized bag limits):** Considered cost-prohibitive and poorly successful. Cited work found no abundance change after six years of trapnetting. Recent AGFD effort caught 9 pike in over 300 net hours (about 0.03 fish per hour), so catching 100 fish would take over 3,000 net hours.
3. **Reduce spawning habitat (vegetation removal or drawdown):** Chemical control costs $50–$100 per gallon. Mechanical harvest costs about $3,400 for 10 days of weed harvester work. Habitat replacement for centrarchids could add up to $50,000. Drawdown is the cheapest option but carries refill risk. This is considered suppression only.
4. **Total renovation (piscicide use or complete drying):** Rated the most effective option for full removal and for ending expansion, but the most publicly controversial. Rotenone costs about $75 per gallon (Noxfish 5%), with roughly 2.5 ppm needed. Antimycin A is more costly, and a single-treatment estimate for the lake was $140,000 (400 units). Refilling after complete drying could take two to four years. Restocking could take up to five years to fully restore warm-water fish. Trout could be restocked sooner.

**Recommendations**
- AGFD should aggressively remove pike from Parker Canyon Lake now, as removal will be cheaper than after range expansion.
- The preferred strategy combines a drawdown to a manageable level, mechanical removal of as much vegetation as possible, and at least three rotenone treatments over six to eight months.
- Public involvement is to be built through a stakeholder list and public meetings, including the storeowner, campground concessionaire, U.S. Forest Service, and local homeowners association.
- Outlet screening and timed releases are advised if the lake is drained, to protect downstream native larval fish.
