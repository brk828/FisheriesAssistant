# Fishes in Arid Lands: A Portfolio of Fish Species and Their Habitats in the Sonoran Desert Region

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service General Technical Report draft, by [[John N. Rinne]] (Rocky Mountain Forest and Range Experiment Station) and [[W. L. Minckley]] (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Arizona State University), presents an illustrated portfolio of the native fishes of the [[Sonoran Desert]] Region of the United States and Mexico. It covers the regional geologic and climatic setting, the natural waters that support fish, and the fish communities of rivers, deltas, headwaters, springs, cienegas and lakes. The authors argue that native fishes are losing the competition for water with humans, that at least 10 western fish taxa have disappeared since about 1960, and that no fewer than 50 others are threatened. They conclude that biological interactions with nonnative fishes are the ultimate cause of native fish declines in the United States. ## Background & Description

The Region's arid lands were shaped by tectonic activity, including uplift of the [[Sierra Nevada]] and movement along the San Andreas fault system, and by changes in atmospheric circulation. Permanent high-pressure cells keep the lowland deserts dry. Evaporation from exposed water in the Sonoran Desert may exceed 2.5 m per year. Lakes once common in the basins have largely dried, leaving springs, streams and a few playas as aquatic refuges.

The authors group the fauna into two ecological subdivisions: riverine species and a smaller group restricted to springs and their outflows. Lake-adapted fishes are almost absent. The account moves through:

- **Riverine communities:** the [[Colorado River Delta]], lower rivers above tidal influence, and intermediate-elevation streams.
- **Headwaters:** cold-water zones dominated by trouts.
- **Springs and cienegas:** isolated habitats that have produced highly distinctive populations.
- **Lakes:** including the [[Salton Sea]] and Laguna Salada, both filled by diverted river water.

Within the Region, the [[Colorado River]] and [[Rio Yaqui]] are the major systems, with the [[Gila River]] as the main tributary of the Colorado in Arizona and New Mexico.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Flow regime and habitat.** Desert rivers carried extreme flows. Before human modification, the Colorado near Yuma ranged from less than 0.35 to more than 7,000 cubic meters per second. Floods were rapid and violent, moved large volumes of sediment, and reshaped channels. Low flows left clear, warm water and could approach or exceed the critical thermal maxima of fishes. Water tables in valleys were historically within a few meters of the surface and fed oxbows, marshes and permanent pools. Canyon bedrock forced groundwater to the surface, creating reliable reaches.

**Deltaic and estuarine links.** The Colorado Delta was a tidal system, with tides in the Sea of Cortez exceeding 9.2 m at the river mouth. Marine species such as [[Totoaba]], [[Striped Mullet]], machete (*Elops affinis*) and snooks penetrated the lower river, and dissolved and particulate organic matter from the river fertilized the Gulf. Reservoir entrapment of these nutrients has not been assessed but was presumed substantial.

**Springs and cienegas.** Springs are uncommon but provide a disproportionate share of aquatic habitat because they are permanent. Cienegas, organic marshland deposits in Arizona, buffer drought flows. Many were lost after the late 1800s, when floods incised them into arroyos.

**Threats.** The authors identify dams, channelization, diversion, cold-water releases below dams, overgrazing, logging, mining effluent, sewage and nonnative species. Introduced predators and competitors, especially [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Mosquitofish]] and African cichlids, are presented as the main driver of replacement.

## Key Findings

**Scale of loss and status**

- Since about 1960, at least 10 species and subspecies of western fishes have disappeared, and no fewer than 50 others are considered threatened.
- Of 47 freshwater species treated, 12 occur only in Mexico, 13 only in the United States, and 22 are shared.
- Seventeen species are officially listed or proposed for listing as threatened or endangered by the U.S. Department of Interior. One ([[Razorback Sucker]]) is under intensive management in lieu of listing. Three are extinct: Pahranagat spinedace, Las Vegas speckled dace and [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]].
- Of the 18 listed, soon-to-be-listed or managed species, six persist in substantial numbers in Mexico, six never occurred there, and six are extirpated there.
- Four widespread species remain common in both countries: [[Roundtail Chub]], [[Longfin Dace]], Mexican stoneroller and Rio Grande mountain-sucker. Even these have lost range.

**Species accounts (selected)**

- **[[Colorado Squawfish]]:** the largest North American minnow, reaching almost 2.0 m and perhaps 45 kg. Adults are piscivorous.
- **[[Razorback Sucker]]:** adults exceed 80 cm and 5.0 kg. A remnant Lake Mohave population may exceed 500,000 individuals, yet there has been no evidence of recruitment for at least 20 years.
- **[[Bonytail Chub]]:** fewer than 25 individuals were caught in intensive effort since 1975. Hybridization with other chubs appears to occur, possibly because mates are scarce. Propagation has been initiated.
- **[[Humpback Chub]]:** a canyon specialist with a humped back and a soft, overhanging snout. It persists in Grand Canyon and the Little Colorado River.
- **[[Woundfin]]:** the last known refuge is the Virgin River mainstream.
- **[[Sonoran Topminnow]]:** the northern form (*P. o. occidentalis*) now occupies fewer than 10 natural localities in the United States. Predation by mosquitofish can eliminate local populations within a year.
- **[[Desert Pupfish]]:** tolerates high temperatures and salinities. It has largely been replaced by introduced species and is nearly gone from the Salton Sea.
- **Yaqui topminnows:** include an all-female, hybrid-origin system in which males are absent. Offspring carry the mother's genes combined with those of a new male.
- **[[Apache Trout]] and [[Gila Trout]]:** hybridization with nonnative rainbow trout reduced the Apache trout to less than 15% of its former range by the 1970s. The Gila trout survives naturally in only five headwater streams, with fewer than 7,000 estimated in the late 1970s.
- **[[Springfish]] and [[Moapa Dace]]:** thermal-spring endemics. The Moapa dace is listed as endangered, largely due to introduced aquarium fishes and [[Mosquitofish]].

**Salton Sea and deltaic history**

- The Salton Sea, created 1905–1907 when the Colorado River flowed uncontrolled for almost 3 years, covered about 550 km².
- Striped mullet were commercially fished there until the 1950s but did not persist; machete never reproduced.
- Prehistoric stone weirs on the Salton basin shorelines indicate a substantial past fishery for razorback suckers.
- The Salton Sea fauna was progressively displaced. African cichlids spread by the late 1970s and invaded the sea itself.

**Causes and hypothesis**

- The authors note that physical and chemical alterations explain much of the recent extinction rate, but these factors are similar in both countries. They therefore look for other explanations for the differing patterns.
- Mexico's northwestern watersheds were relatively isolated from introduced fishes. The "Gran Desierto" limited movement of nonnatives.
- The central hypothesis: biological interactions with introduced predators and competitors are the ultimate cause of native fish declines in the United States, even where physical conditions appear natural.
- Native fishes evolved with few efficient predators, since the region lacked gars, pikes, most catfishes, sunfishes and basses for a long time. Introduced predatory species are mostly quiet-water fishes, which made impoundments easy to colonize.

**Recommendations and gaps**

- Research should test the hypothesis through experimental approaches, with stocking of food and game fishes in Mexico as a near-term priority.
- The authors urge perpetuation of native fishes and natural aquatic systems through hatchery propagation and habitat protection.
- Ecology is poorly known for many Mexican species, including roundtail chub populations, Yaqui suckers and Cahita, Leopold's and Opata suckers.
