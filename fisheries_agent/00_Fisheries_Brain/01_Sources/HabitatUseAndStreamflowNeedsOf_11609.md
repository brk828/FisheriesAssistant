# Habitat Use and Streamflow Needs of Rare and Endangered Fishes, Yampa River, Colorado

#ichthyology #endangered-species #habitat-use #streamflow #Colorado-River-basin #cyprinidae #catostomidae #conservation

## Executive Summary

This report evaluates habitat use and streamflow requirements of four federally listed or candidate fish species in the [[Yampa River]], [[Colorado]]: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). The [[Yampa River]] is the only large river in the [[Upper Colorado River Basin]] where flow patterns remain substantially unaltered by water development projects. The river supports all its native fish fauna, including self-sustaining populations of rare species. From 1980-1988, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service biologists conducted extensive field studies using radiotelemetry, electrofishing, trammel netting, and other techniques to document distribution, abundance, habitat use, and reproductive behavior. The report identifies critical river reaches, spawning grounds, nursery areas, and migration corridors. Natural flow events—particularly spring peak flows, gradual summer flow decline, and stable low flows in fall and winter—are essential to the survival and recovery of these species. The [[Yampa River]] is assigned highest priority for water rights acquisition under the Recovery Implementation Program.

## Background & Description

The [[Yampa River]] originates on the western slope of the [[Rocky Mountains]] at approximately 3,780 m elevation and flows about 320 km westward to its confluence with the [[Green River]] at [[Echo Park]], [[Dinosaur National Monument]], at approximately 1,524 m elevation. The river drops 2,256 m over its course. The mainstream comprises high-gradient canyon reaches dominated by boulder, cobble, and gravel substrates (including [[Juniper Mountain Canyon]] at RK 145.6-141.9, [[Cross Mountain Canyon]] at RK 94.1-88.9, and [[Yampa Canyon]] at RK 75.2-0) and low-gradient meandering reaches with finer substrates in agricultural valleys.

Average annual discharge is approximately 60.5 m³/s, with about 28% contributed by the [[Little Snake River]]. Flows begin rising in late March due to spring runoff and remain high through July, with mean spring runoff flow of about 153.18 m³/s. Peak flows can be extreme; a maximum discharge of 939.56 m³/s was recorded on 18 May 1984 at [[Deerlodge Park]]. Following spring runoff, flows decline toward a monthly base flow of about 14.15 m³/s for August through March. This recurring cycle of high spring flows followed by lower flows represents the natural flow regime.

The [[Yampa River]] is the only large river in the [[Colorado River Basin]] not substantially altered by water development. In contrast, construction of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] and [[Fontenelle Dam]] on the [[Green River]] in the 1960s eliminated spring peak flows and increased baseline discharge. The spring and early summer peak in the existing [[Green River]] hydrograph below the [[Yampa River]] confluence is maintained by [[Yampa River]] spring runoff.

Native fishes include cyprinids ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Roundtail Chub]] [*Gila robusta*], [[Speckled Dace]] [*Rhinichthys osculus*]); catostomids ([[Razorback Sucker]], [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] [*Catostomus latipinnis*], [[Bluehead Sucker]] [*C. discobolus*], [[Mountain Sucker]] [*C. platyrhynchus*]); salmonids ([[Colorado River Cutthroat Trout]] [*Salmo clarki pleuriticus*], [[Rocky Mountain Whitefish]] [*Prosopium williamsoni*]); and sculpins (*Cottus bairdi* sp.). Native fishes persist despite introduction of at least 18 nonnative species and dominate the fish community (>70% of catch). Persistence of native fishes is associated with maintenance of usable habitat due to fluctuating seasonal and annual flows.

Historically, native cyprinids and catostomids were dominant in mainstream habitats throughout the [[Colorado River Basin]]. All four focal species were widely distributed and common-to-abundant but are now threatened with extinction due to habitat loss, flow regulation, temperature and sediment regime alteration, proliferation of introduced competitors and predators, and other anthropogenic disturbances. The [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]] are federally listed as endangered. The [[Razorback Sucker]] is a candidate species protected by state statutes in Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, and Utah.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]] constitute the most important riverine system for maintenance and recovery of rare [[Colorado River]] fishes. The [[Yampa River]] provides a natural shape to the [[Green River]] hydrograph below their confluence, mitigating adverse effects of flow regulation from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. Spring runoff from the [[Yampa River]] maintains the spring and early summer peak in the [[Green River]] hydrograph.

The [[Green River Basin]] supports the largest numbers of [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] in native riverine habitats. The [[Humpback Chub]] is self-sustaining in the [[Yampa River]], representing one of few remaining extant populations. In the lower [[Colorado River Basin]] (below [[Lee Ferry]], Arizona), [[Colorado Squawfish]] have been extirpated; relict populations of [[Bonytail Chub]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] remain in some impoundments; and [[Humpback Chub]] persist only in the [[Little Colorado River]].

The [[Yampa River]] was divided into eight relatively homogeneous river sections for study purposes, extending from [[Echo Park]] (RK 0) to near [[Williams Fork]] (RK 198.4), later extended to RK 224 near [[Craig]], Colorado. Studies employed standardized methods including radiotelemetry, electrofishing, trammel netting, angling, and seining. From 1980-1988, U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and Colorado Division of Wildlife biologists compiled habitat use data for all fish species captured.

**[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: Adult distribution extends from the mouth (RK 0) upstream to [[Craig]] (RK 224). The upper [[Yampa River]] (RK 81.6-198.4) is a concentration area for overwintering adults. During winter, adults use backwaters, runs, and eddies, most commonly in shallow, ice-covered shoreline areas. In spring and early summer, adults occupy backwater habitats or flooded bottomlands. Radio-tracking data showed 66% use of shoreline backwater habitat in 1981 (low-flow year) and 40% use of flooded bottomlands in 1983 (high-flow year). In mid-to-late summer, adults occupy eddies, pools, runs, and shoreline backwaters over sand and silt substrates, most often in deeper shoreline habitats.

Two major spawning migrations have been identified. In May and early June, [[Colorado Squawfish]] migrate to spawn in riffle and pool habitat of the lower 51.2 km of [[Yampa Canyon]] (Fig. 4). From 1981-1988, 43 fish were radio-tracked to the spawning reach, including 28 from upper [[Yampa River]], 13 from [[Green River]], and 2 from [[White River]]. Average one-way movement was about 124.8 km; one [[White River]] fish migrated about 372.8 km. Spawning migrations were initiated from 27 May to 13 June, earlier in low-water years (1981) and later in high-water years (1983). Migrations were associated with highest spring flows and river temperatures generally exceeding 14°C.

Spawning occurs in the lower 51.2 km of [[Yampa River]], particularly from [[Warm Springs Rapid]] (RK 6.56) to vicinity of [[Harding Hole]] (RK 32), with concentration near [[Mathers Hole]] (RK 28.3). Spawning behavior includes: (1) resting-staging phase in pools or large shoreline eddies (average depth 2 m, velocity 0.2 m/s); and (2) deposition-fertilization phase on cobble bars (average depth 1 m, velocity 0.5 m/s). From 1981-1988, total spawning period lasted 4-5 weeks; optimum spawning period averaged about 26 days. Spawning occurred with declining flows following spring runoff and increasing temperatures (14.5-27.5°C, average minimum 19°C, average maximum 24°C). Mean discharge during optimum spawning period ranged from 25.27 m³/s (1981) to 108.25 m³/s (1982).

From 1981-1988, 308 [[Colorado Squawfish]] (including 208 ripe adults) were collected at two confirmed spawning sites. Ripe males (N=194) were bronze colored and heavily tuberculated. Only 14 ripe or spent females were positively identified, with 42 additional suspected females, indicating a paucity of adult females and high male-to-female ratio.

Larval [[Colorado Squawfish]] emerge as sac fry from cobble bars in [[Yampa Canyon]] and drift downstream to concentrate in shallow backwater habitats in the [[Green River]]. About 16 days are required for transport from midpoint of spawning grounds (RK 26.4-29.1) to mouth of [[Yampa River]]. From 1979-1981, peaks of abundance occurred about 160 km downstream of spawning reach. Postlarval (young-of-the-year) [[Colorado Squawfish]] are rare in [[Yampa River]]; farthest upstream record is RK 24.

Juvenile [[Colorado Squawfish]] (60-450 mm total length) are poorly understood due to rarity. From 1980-1988, only 3% (N=198) of all [[Colorado Squawfish]] >60 mm collected were juveniles. Downstream drift of larvae suggests long-distance upstream movement by juveniles is needed to repopulate upstream areas, probably occurring during late juvenile or early adult stage.

**[[Humpback Chub]]**: Adult [[Humpback Chub]] (>230 mm) were captured in canyon-bound habitat in lower 89.6 km of [[Yampa River]] and lower 16 km of [[Little Snake River]]. From 1986-1988, 88 captures (9 recaptures) occurred between RK 6.4 and 73.6. Adults were most often collected in eddy habitat (average depth 2 m), particularly shoreline eddies created by large boulders and rapids, commonly with [[Roundtail Chub]] and introduced [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*).

Spawning in [[Yampa Canyon]] was documented in 1986 with capture of two spent females and two ripe males, confirmed in 1987 (2 ripe females, 7 ripe males, 11 tuberculated fish) and 1988 (1 ripe female, 5 ripe males, 2 tuberculated fish). Total of 32 mature [[Humpback Chub]] (3 recaptures) were captured in spawning condition in [[Yampa Canyon]] (RK 19.2-64) in shoreline eddy and run habitat. Spawning occurred shortly after peak spring flows. Thirteen juvenile (168-227 mm) [[Humpback Chub]] have been collected between RK 0.16 and 64, most in shoreline eddies and runs. Young-of-the-year have been tentatively identified in lower 64 km of [[Yampa Canyon]].

**[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Habitat requirements are largely unknown. Fish collections before and after closure of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] indicated fair numbers at confluence of [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]]. Recent investigations yielded few captures. Holden and Stalnaker (1975) reported 36 captures in [[Yampa River]] (lower 16 km) and upper [[Green River]] between 1968-1970. One captured in lower [[Yampa River]] in 1979, one suspected juvenile in 1987.

**[[Razorback Sucker]]**: More than 500 adult [[Razorback Sucker]] have been captured in flat-water sections of upper [[Green River]] (RK 282-552) and lower 21 km of [[Yampa River]]. During nonbreeding season, adults were most common in shoreline runs and mid-channel sand bars in mainstream [[Green River]], with average depth <2 m and velocity <0.5 m/s. Adults overwinter in [[Echo Park]] area.

Spawning activity documented in lower [[Yampa River]] near confluence with [[Green River]] and in upper [[Green River]]. Thirty-two ripe [[Razorback Sucker]] (6 females, 26 males, including 2 recaptures) were captured on cobble and gravel bars in lower [[Yampa River]] in 1975, 1981, 1988, and 1989 at average depth 0.61 m and velocity 0.64 m/s. Water temperatures averaged 15°C. Recapture of ripe male at RK 0.16 in 1988 (originally captured same locality 1981, also ripe) indicates fidelity to spawning site; this fish moved at least 52.8 km from upper [[Green River]] to reach lower [[Yampa River]] to spawn. Spawning occurred on ascending limb of spring hydrograph. Capture of ripe fish and tentative identification of larvae indicates successful reproduction, but little indication of widespread recruitment to juvenile stage throughout [[Colorado River Basin]].

## Key Findings

**Critical River Reaches and Habitat Requirements:**

1. **[[Colorado Squawfish]]**: Adult distribution RK 0-224; overwintering concentration RK 82-198; migration RK 0-224 (May-August); spawning RK 6-51 (June-August), particularly RK 6.56-32 with concentration near [[Mathers Hole]] (RK 28.3); larval drift RK 0-50 (July-August); juvenile distribution RK 0-224.

2. **[[Humpback Chub]]**: Adult distribution and concentration RK 0-90, particularly RK 6-64; spawning RK 19-64 (May-July); larval and juvenile occurrence RK 0-90.

3. **[[Bonytail Chub]]**: Adult distribution RK 0-16; factors limiting distribution and abundance unknown.

4. **[[Razorback Sucker]]**: Adult distribution RK 0-21; spawning RK 0-6 (April-June); larval occurrence RK 0-6.

**Limiting Factors:**

*[[Colorado Squawfish]]:*
- Spring peak flows necessary for initiation of spawning migration
- Overbank flooding and flooded bottomlands important for physiological readiness
- Localized water input (seeps, springs) at spawning grounds may provide orientation cues
- Low number of spawning adults, particularly ripe females (male:female ratio highly skewed)
- Availability of cleaned cobble and boulder surfaces for egg adhesion; timing and duration of flushing flows critical
- Susceptibility to angling and incidental capture
- Competition and predation by nonnative fishes ([[Northern Pike]] [*Esox lucius*], [[Channel Catfish]])
- Stability of winter flows; fluctuations disturb preferred winter habitats
- Stream blockage eliminates migration routes and access to overwintering areas
- Low flows in late summer and fall critical for nursery habitat availability; unusually high flows reduce backwater habitat and survivorship of young
- Predation on juveniles by nonnative fishes ([[Yellow Bullhead]] [*Ictalurus natalis*], [[Largemouth Bass]] [*Micropterus salmoides*], [[Green Sunfish]] [*Lepomis cyanellus*], [[Black Crappie]] [*Pomoxis nigromaculatus*], [[Black Bullhead]] [*Ictalurus melas*], [[Channel Catfish]])
- Competition with young nonnative fishes ([[Green Sunfish]], [[Red Shiner]] [*Notropis lutrensis*], [[Fathead Minnow]] [*Pimephales promelas*]) for backwater habitat and food

*[[Humpback Chub]]:*
- Spring peak flows necessary for spawning; spawning occurs shortly after highest spring discharge
- Availability of shoreline eddy habitat for spawning
- Competition and predation by [[Channel Catfish]] (30% of catch in spawning habitat 1987-1988)
- Availability of canyon-bound habitat
- Habitat alteration may promote hybridization with other *Gila* species

*[[Bonytail Chub]]:*
- Factors unknown; species never reported as abundant in [[Yampa River]]
- Decline in [[Echo Park]] area possibly due to flow and temperature changes from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] closure
