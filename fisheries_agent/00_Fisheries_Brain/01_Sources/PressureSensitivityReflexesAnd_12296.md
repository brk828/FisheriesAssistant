# Pressure Sensitivity, Reflexes, and Buoyancy Responses in Teleosts

#ichthyology #marine-biology #teleostei

## Executive Summary

This 1966 study by F. H. McCutcheon, published in *Animal Behaviour* (vol. 14), examines how free-swimming teleost fishes sense and correct small changes in ambient pressure to hold neutral buoyancy. Using a glass pressure-gradient tube and cinematography, the author studied twelve species, mainly marine [[Physoclist Swimbladder]] fishes, with the [[Pinfish]] and [[Black Sea Bass]] analysed in most detail. The work shows that these fishes detect pressure changes below 0.5 cm H₂O, establish a reference "fix" position, and compensate through behaviour that combines yawning, fin movements and locomotion.

The pages also include two unrelated fragments: a page on locomotory behaviour in the grain weevil *Sitophilus granarius* (Surtees), and a page on mouse behaviour and genotype (van Abeelen). Neither concerns fishes, so they are not summarised further here.

## Background & Description

The swimbladder is the only gas-filled organ in vertebrates that is not connected to the exterior in physoclist fishes. Its volume controls buoyancy, which the author compares to the postural role of gravity in land animals. Earlier work had focused on gas secretion, hydrostatic properties and time-magnitude correlates, using units such as min/hr and cm Hg/atm. These units were too coarse for the sensitivities the author sought to measure.

Previous studies had identified several control mechanisms:

- [[Pressure Sensitivity]] in the bladder, with receptor and reflex roles reported by Koshtoyants and colleagues.
- Conditioned responses to pressure of about 0.5–1.0 cm H₂O in Dijkgraff's work.
- Vagal control of secretion and resorption, and the role of exteroceptors, described by Fange.
- Muscular compression, a finer adjustment than secretion, reported in carp, minnow and pinfish.
- A [[Yawn Reflex]] in pinfish, first described by McCutcheon (1958, 1962).

The study used twelve species, all marine physoclists except the goldfish. These were the pinfish, black sea bass, jumping mullet, spadefish, Holbrook's porgy, common and striped killifishes, gag, gray triggerfish, spiny boxfish, sand perch and goldfish. The main experimental fishes were pinfish, sea bass and jumping mullet.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Field data on low-pressure effects were scarce. The author proposed that the laboratory behaviours are relevant to natural habitats for several reasons:

- All marine species studied occur in estuarine and sound waters for much of the year. Fixed objects and substrata are available there, unlike in the open ocean.
- Sea bass are found especially near the bottom, beside pilings and sunken objects.
- Most species, except the jumping mullet, stay close to the bottom and resist being lured upward more than a few metres.
- Territorial, nest-protection and hiding behaviours, and camouflage, all depend on holding a position, which requires buoyancy control.

The author also linked buoyancy behaviour to tidal, barometric and reproductive behaviour. He suggested that fishes respond to ordinary barometric changes, consistent with fishermen's observations relating catches to weather. Nikolsky (1963) reported that fishes are used in Japan to forecast weather from their behaviour. A possible tidal rhythm in buoyancy compensation was also proposed, but not tested.

## Key Findings

**Methods**
- The apparatus was a cylindrical glass tube with an adjustable angle. The sine of the tube angle set the amplification of depth changes, from ×1.6 at 1° to ×50 at 38°.
- Pressures tested ranged from 0.02 to 400 cm H₂O.
- Pinfish, sea bass and jumping mullet were filmed (3200 ft of 16 mm film, up to 64 frames/sec).
- Fishes adapted for 4–10 days before controls were established, and remained in the tube for up to 46 days. Total analysis and recording time was about 325 days.

**Fix position (fix₀)**
- Each fish established a reference "fix" position, comparable to a set point, within about 1–3 days.
- Pinfish preferred the lower third of the depth range at tube angles above about sine 0.02. Sea bass set fix in the lower 10% of the range.
- Once established, the fix was held within a few centimetres, and held in three dimensions to less than 2 mm during terminal adjustment.
- Vision was not essential: sea bass and pinfish compensated continuously in darkness, except during brief flashes.

**Yawn reflex**
- Yawns occurred in all species when pressure dropped for a minute or more. A change of only 0.2 cm H₂O was enough.
- Yawns marked the end of volume compensation. At larger displacements, yawn position and frequency depended on the direction and size of the change.
- Yawns also occurred at low frequency in resting fish. Their frequency increased with light, sound or vibration.
- In Table I, pinfish mean yawn intervals ranged from about 5 to 31 min across conditions, with the lowest yawn frequency during early response to strong negative buoyancy (30–100 cm H₂O).

**Pressure sensitivity and reaction time**
- Fishes were sensitive to less than 0.5 cm H₂O. Reaction time for visual, ventral tactile and pectoral responses was about 0.1 sec.
- At slow pressure change (0.1 cm/sec), passive reactions occurred first: a passive head rise of about 15° for a −1.0 cm change, and a fall of about 40° for +1.0 cm.
- A faster change (2.0 cm/sec) triggered immediate reflex correction.

**Local and locomotor compensation**
- For changes of 0.1–1.5 cm H₂O, local compensation occurred near the fix, mainly by pectoral movements and eye scanning.
- Above about 1.0 cm, cyclic locomotor compensation began. Positive buoyancy led to more extensive ranging; negative buoyancy led to higher-frequency cycling.
- Fishes sought minimum displacements of about 0.5–1.0 cm for testing and about 3–6 cm for adjustment excursions.
- Adjustment for negative buoyancy went directly to neutrality. Adjustment for positive buoyancy passed through an absorption overshoot before secretion-compression.

**Reactions to anomalous pressure**
- In the final stage of compensation, a very slight (0.5–1.0 cm), momentary (1–3 sec) change caused fish to leave the fix and test randomly. Repeated disturbance could push fish into a catatonic-like immobility lasting hours.
- Pressure gradients reversed against normal swimming caused strong excitement and escape behaviour, and disorientation. Fish then hid behind the bottom flange, anchored by a fin, and needed six or more hours to recover.
- Strong, rapid negative pressure (about 20 cm Hg in 0.5 sec) was noxious. Pinfish could be conditioned to avoid levels in the tube. Negative pressure above about 2 cm sometimes caused scooping of the bottom, which would form a nest-like base in nature.

**Dorsal fin reactions**
- Strong positive buoyancy caused prolonged dorsal fin erection. Equal negative buoyancy did not.
- The dorsal fin was also erect during alarm reactions. Flicking of the dorsal fin reliably signalled an imminent terminal yawn, usually within a minute or two.

**Buoyancy state and interpretation**
- Equilibrated swimbladder pressure was normally about 3–10 cm H₂O above ambient, which gives negative buoyancy at rest. Immobile fish therefore sink, and remain as passive objects near the bottom.
- The author concluded that negative buoyancy is the normal control state and that positive buoyancy is aversive.
- Adrenergic fibres control gas absorption, and cholinergic fibres control secretion (Fange, 1953).

**Conclusions**
- Physoclist teleosts show buoyancy behaviour with a sensitivity and precision that the author considered comparable to postural regulation in terrestrial mammals.
- Behavioural involvement is complex enough to account for many field observations of barometric, tidal and reproductive behaviour.
