## Executive Summary
This 1989 letter from the Desert Fishes Council (Executive Secretary E. P. Pister) to the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service addresses a fisheries management crisis on the [[Virgin River]]. A chemical treatment (piscicide/rotenone-type poisoning) had been conducted below the Washington Diversion to eradicate the invasive [[Red Shiner]], which threatened the endangered [[Woundfin]] and candidate species [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]]. Shortly after this action, a flash flood caused by the failure of the [[Quail Creek Dam]] on January 1, 1989, further disrupted the system. The letter critiques the lack of rigorous contingency planning in the eradication effort, urges rapid follow-up action (including installation of fish barriers), and calls for emergency listing of the roundtail chub along with a population genetics study of the native fish community.

## Background & Description
The letter is policy/management-focused and does not provide direct morphological, meristic, or anatomical data. However, it references key taxonomic and status distinctions relevant to identification and conservation:
- **[[Woundfin]]** (*Plagopterus argentissimus*) — federally endangered cyprinid native to the Virgin River, distinguished from other native minnows by its distinctive evolutionary lineage within the Virgin River basin.
- **[[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]]** (referred to as "Virgin Roundtail") — a candidate species at the time, considered part of the *Gila robusta* complex, with suspected "distinctive divergence" suggesting a unique evolutionary lineage or subspecies within the Virgin River system.
- **[[Red Shiner]]** — an invasive, non-native cyprinid (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) whose upstream range expansion via the Virgin River Gorge triggered the eradication effort.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **System:** [[Virgin River]] mainstream and the **Virgin River Gorge**, a tributary system connected to the Colorado River basin, spanning Utah, Arizona, and Nevada.
- **Hydrological Alteration:** Historically, flows through the Virgin River Gorge were intermittent during summer due to irrigation diversions established near St. George, Utah, in the late 1800s. This intermittency likely served as a natural barrier limiting the upstream spread of [[Red Shiner]].
- **Anthropogenic Flow Change:** Unprecedented rainfall in summer 1983 and subsequent flow manipulation enabled by the [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] created continuous summer flows through the gorge, allowing red shiner to invade upstream native fish habitat.
- **Invasive Species Pressure:** [[Red Shiner]] competition/predation pressure was severe enough to necessitate a targeted piscicide treatment below the Washington Diversion to protect the native fish community, particularly [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]].
- **Disturbance Event:** The catastrophic failure of [[Quail Creek Dam]] on January 1, 1989, produced a flash flood that scoured affected river reaches immediately following the poisoning event, compounding stress on native and non-native fish populations alike.
- **Barrier Ecology:** A planned fish barrier (agreed upon by the Washington County Water Conservancy District) was intended to functionally replace the historic intermittent-flow barrier, preventing red shiner reinvasion from downstream reaches.

## Key Findings
- **Population Range Collapse:** The Virgin River Roundtail Chub has reportedly declined across ~70% of its native range in Arizona and Nevada, and at the time of writing had been "decimated throughout 94% of its native range," indicating severe historical range contraction.
- **Compounding Threats:** The combination of the piscicide treatment (targeting red shiner but affecting the broader fish community) and the subsequent flash flood is assessed as having pushed the roundtail chub population "to an extremely precarious level," providing biological justification for emergency listing under the Endangered Species Act.
- **Invasion Pathway Identified:** Red shiner's upstream colonization of the Virgin River Gorge is directly linked to anthropogenic changes in summer flow regimes enabled by [[Quail Creek Reservoir]] operations — a clear case of hydrological modification facilitating invasive species range expansion.
- **Management Recommendations:** The Council formally requested six specific actions: (1) readiness to re-treat with piscicide if red shiner reappears; (2) installation of new fish barriers at the lower gorge and near the Nevada state line; (3) transparency on the roundtail chub's listing status; (4) sustained population monitoring; (5) a formal population genetics/evolutionary study of pre- and post-project fish populations; and (6) frequent integrity checks of the upper gorge fish barrier.
- **Opportunistic Restoration Potential:** The unintended combination of poisoning and flooding was framed as a rare opportunity to extend native fish rehabilitation an additional 20 miles downstream by adding a new barrier at the lower end of the gorge, potentially restoring the [[Woundfin]] and [[Virgin River Roundtail Chub]] to their range down to the Nevada/Arizona state line.
- **Evolutionary Significance Concern:** The Council raised concern that unique, locally-adapted genomes within the native fish community could have been lost due to the disturbance event, and alternatively noted the post-disturbance period could allow observation of new "adaptive peaks" forming — an argument for urgent genetic study.
- **Procedural Critique:** The letter explicitly criticizes the absence of standard best-practices protocols (e.g., dye-trial rehearsals, water chemistry checks, inter-agency consultation) during the piscicide operation, citing this as reflective of inadequate planning rather than necessarily a lack of concern for the affected fauna.
