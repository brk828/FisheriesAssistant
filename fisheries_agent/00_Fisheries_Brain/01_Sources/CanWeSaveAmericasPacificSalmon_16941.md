# Pacific Salmon Recovery: Nine Expert Perspectives on Saving the Northwest's Anadromous Stocks

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This Summer 1993 [[Trout Unlimited]] feature compiles nine essays by fisheries scientists, managers, tribal leaders, policy advisors and conservationists answering one question: what can the [[Clinton Administration]] and Congress do to recover native Pacific salmon? It follows the earlier [[Pacific Salmon at the Crossroads]] report, which documented widespread stock extinction and depletion. The essays agree that the know-how for recovery largely exists; the obstacles are political, economic and institutional. Recurring recommendations include protecting and restoring watersheds, reforming hatchery practice, reining in hydropower and water diversion impacts, coordinating federal and state agencies, and treating ecosystem health rather than commodity output as the management goal. Several contributors also stress that compartmentalized, incremental fixes will fail.

The compilation also contains reader correspondence, an opening editorial, and advertisements. These are omitted here except where they bear on the salmon issue.

## Background & Description

The document concerns Pacific salmon (*Oncorhynchus* spp.), steelhead and sea-run cutthroat trout, all anadromous salmonids. The "Crossroads" report identified 214 genetically distinct, reproductively isolated stocks as "at risk of extinction" across California, Oregon, Washington and Idaho. Contributors note that these stocks span virtually every river draining to the Pacific from San Diego to Puget Sound. Of more than 400 stocks evaluated by the American Fisheries Society, 100 were already extinct, 214 were rated at moderate or high risk or of special concern, and about 120 were considered secure.

Key species and stocks discussed include:

- **Spring-run [[Chinook Salmon]]** of the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers, now reduced to roughly 500 spawners per year in Ishi's canyon creeks (Deer Creek), and the Snake River fall and spring/summer runs.
- **[[Coho Salmon]]**, reduced statewide in California to fewer than 5,000 wild spawners from an estimated 200,000 about 50 years earlier.
- **[[Sockeye Salmon]]** of the Snake River, listed as endangered in 1991.
- **[[Steelhead]]** and sea-run cutthroat trout, with the Snake River supplying about 55% of the Columbia Basin's steelhead.
- Other sea-run fish cited in California include green sturgeon, eulachon, smelts and lampreys.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The essays identify several interacting causes of decline, grouped by contributors as the "Four H's" (hydropower, habitat, hatcheries, harvest) plus water diversion and ocean interception:

- **[[Hydropower]] and dams:** Columbia Basin adult returns fell from an estimated 10 to 16 million at the time of the 1855 treaties to about 2.5 million today, with roughly 75% returning to hatcheries built to mitigate dam impacts. The [[Northwest Power Planning Council]] estimated hydropower accounts for 5 to 11 million adult fish lost, between 70% and 80% of total decline from all causes. The mainstem dams converted free-flowing migration corridors into slackwater reservoirs where smolts lose their migratory drive and become prey.
- **Habitat degradation:** Forestry, road building, grazing, mining, and historic splash dams (56 in the western Columbia Basin and 55 in the Willamette and Deschutes watersheds, built 1880–1910) simplified streams. Deep pools declined sharply; [[Jack Williams]] cites Forest Service resurveys showing pools per mile down 67% in the Grande Ronde, 75% in Catherine Creek and 85% in Beaver Creek.
- **Hatcheries:** Contributors argue hatchery fish compete with wild fish, interbreed with local populations, reduce genetic diversity, and sustain mixed-stock fisheries that overharvest wild runs.
- **Water diversion:** Under the [[Prior Appropriation]] doctrine, fish have no inherent water rights in most western states, and irrigation systems divert large volumes with heavy losses.
- **Harvest and interception:** Mixed-stock ocean and river fisheries, Canadian interceptions, and high-seas driftnets all remove wild fish.

## Key Findings

**Scale of decline**
- Columbia River run: an estimated average of 16 million adults before white settlement (range 10–40 million); about 4–5 million around 1980; 2–2.5 million in the mid-1980s, of which only 300,000–500,000 were wild; about 1 million entered the river mouth in 1991.
- Oregon coastal coho: of 55 stocks evaluated by ODFW, 41 were depressed, 2 of special concern, 6 of unknown status, and only 6 healthy.
- Northern California: 35 Chinook and coho stocks face varying extinction threats.
- Washington coast and Puget Sound: 41 anadromous salmonid stocks are at risk or of special concern.
- A study by [[Peter Moyle]] and Larry Brown found nearly half of California streams that once held coho runs no longer do.
- Lichatowich reports the Columbia has lost 95% of its natural productivity and some Oregon coastal streams produce under 10% of historic wild coho levels.

**Habitat and watersheds**
- The BLM reports about 109 of the 214 at-risk stocks occur on its 270 million acres; the Forest Service estimates about 135 stocks on 191 million acres of National Forest System land.
- A [[Frissell and Nawa]] (1992) study found instream structure failure averaged 48% over five to ten years in eight southwest Oregon coastal streams.
- A BLM Salem District report found more than 40% of riparian habitats and stream channels in poor condition. The Forest Service reports 80% of fish habitat in the Upper Grande Ronde Basin fails existing Forest Plan standards.
- Best Management Practices do not ensure watershed integrity; cumulative effects must be assessed at the watershed scale.

**Hatcheries and genetics**
- Scientific evaluation cited by Bakke indicates hatchery fish are at least nine times less productive in nature than wild fish and replace rather than add to natural production.
- Columbia River natural productivity is about 3% of historic levels, and coastal coho productivity about 10% of 1900 levels.
- Bakke's essay cites over 100 populations extinct in the western United States since the turn of the century.
- Contributors propose a three-part hatchery system: [[Production Hatcheries]], [[Experimental Hatcheries]] and [[Temporary Streamside Hatcheries]] (cited success: Lagunitas Creek, Marin County, a Trout Unlimited-sponsored program).
- Eleven reform steps are listed by Bakke, including defining production objectives, collecting baseline genetic data, setting effective population sizes and controlling introgression.

**Hydropower and the Snake River**
- Four mainstem dams were added between 1967 and 1975; runs collapsed. Mortality of smolts passing Ice Harbor Dam rose from an average of about 10% in the mid-1960s to 50–90% or more in drought years after upper Snake dams were built.
- Federal listings: Snake River sockeye endangered (1991); Snake River spring/summer Chinook and fall Chinook threatened (1992).
- A NMFS biological opinion for 1993 reportedly admitted up to 77% of spring and summer smolts may be killed in migration, yet was reversed from "jeopardy" to "no jeopardy" after lobbying.
- Options debated include barging smolts around the dams and drawing down lower Snake reservoirs; the Corps' Lower Granite drawdown test was announced for spring 1994.
- The Miller-Bradley Bill (1992) allocates 800,000 acre-feet per year for fish and wildlife in California.

**Water use**
- Deschutes River: about 78% of diverted water (663,000 acre-feet) is lost before use, with 44% lost before farm weirs.
- Savage Rapids Dam (Rogue Basin) is described as destroying $5 million worth of salmon annually (1980 calculation), with only 17% of diverted water reaching crops.
- Over 3,000 Oregon irrigation diversions lack screens to protect fish.

**Policy and governance**
- Federal agencies lack a comprehensive approach; [[Charles Gauvin]] argues compartmentalized solutions are doomed and incremental decisions beget comprehensive failure.
- Essays urge adoption of [[Ecosystem Management]], with ecosystem health as the primary goal replacing commodity targets, and performance measures on natural-economy health alongside harvest data (Lichatowich).
- [[Co-management]] in Washington between tribes and state (SASSI inventory, Timber-Fish-Wildlife Agreement, U.S.-Canada Salmon Interception Treaty of 1985) is presented as a working model by Bill Frank, Jr.
- Oregon's coastal restoration planning (December 1992 consensus, 90-day "fast start" items) and the Umpqua coalition are offered as state-led models (Jim Martin).
- Congress appropriated about $100 million in 1993 across seven federal agencies for salmon survival; four lawsuits challenge the Northwest Power Planning Council's Salmon Strategy, two claiming it does too little and two claiming it does too much.
- Authors disagree on [[Endangered Species Act]] listing: Moyle favors using the ESA as a prod while avoiding unnecessary listing; Martin argues coastal listings would create gridlock and recommends a coast-wide plan as an alternative.

**Recommendations (selected)**
- Moyle: operate water projects "as if fish mattered," end double agricultural subsidies, manage national forests for fish, begin large-scale whole-stream restoration (citing [[Rosgen-Style Restoration]]), impose a temporary ban on wild harvest with marked-hatchery-only fishing, keep the ESA strong, and integrate environmental education into schools.
- Williams: adopt a common BLM–Forest Service watershed strategy, prioritize headwater protection, avoid commodity targets, and support a National Biological Survey.
- Brunelle and Curtis (WaterWatch): use [[Bureau of Reclamation]] conservation projects and enforce "beneficial use without waste" to keep saved water instream, and reform hatchery and habitat planning through explicit protection-first priorities.
- Hallock: sustain the federal commitment expressed through the Northwest Power Act of 1980, which calls for protecting all fish and wildlife in the Columbia Basin, not just listed species.
- Frank: support cooperative management and sustained federal funding of tribal participation, which he argues is cheaper and more effective than litigation.
- Lichatowich: balance the "industrial economy" against the "natural economy"; salmon fisheries alone generate about 60,000 jobs and $1 billion in regional wealth.
- Bakke: conserve populations and their habitats as the unit of conservation, since population diversity is the evolutionary potential of each species.

**Overall conclusion**
The contributors agree that a sound recovery program is technically feasible but politically difficult. Success depends on national attention, federal leadership, state and tribal partnerships, and long-term commitment; restoration will take decades, and no "silver bullet" exists.
