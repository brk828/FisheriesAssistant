# The Mesquite Problem on Southern Arizona Ranges

#botany #range-ecology #prosopis-juliflora

## Executive Summary

This 1952 USDA Forest Service circular by [[Kenneth W. Parker]] and S. Clark Martin examines the invasion of grasslands by [[Velvet Mesquite]] (*Prosopis juliflora* var. *velutina*) across southern Arizona and the wider Southwest. Mesquite now covers about 9 million acres in southeastern Arizona and more than 70 million acres of range in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. Roughly half of that area is estimated to have been invaded since livestock were introduced. The authors show that mesquite suppresses [[Perennial Grasses]], depletes [[Soil Moisture]], and accelerates [[Soil Erosion]]. Their 1940–48 experiments at the [[Santa Rita Experimental Range]] found that killing mesquite roughly doubled grass cover within three years. The circular evaluates chemical and manual control methods, estimates costs and returns, and recommends an early, persistent control program.

## Background & Description

The range supports upwards of a quarter million cattle, valued at roughly 25 to 50 million dollars, in the eastern half of southern Arizona's roughly 15 million acres of grazing land. The climate is mild enough for yearlong grazing with little supplemental feeding. The authors state that reduced forage from competing woody vegetation is a major constraint on livestock production.

Mesquites (*Prosopis* spp.) are legume shrubs or trees. Trees can reach 40 to 50 feet, with dark, deeply furrowed bark and straight spines on the twigs. Flowers are small and greenish-yellow, borne in clusters from late April to late July. Pods (“beans”) of 4 to 8 inches contain 10 to 20 seeds each. The authors follow Benson and Kearney and Peebles in treating most Southwestern mesquites as one species with three varieties: honey mesquite (*var. glandulosa*) in Texas, western honey mesquite (*var. torreyana*) in New Mexico and southeastern Arizona, and velvet mesquite (*var. velutina*) in Arizona. Tornillo or screwbean (*P. odorata*) is largely confined to valley bottomlands.

Growth form depends on site. Velvet mesquite is a large tree in river bottoms such as the San Pedro and Santa Cruz, a many-stemmed shrub on sand dunes, and a scraggy woodland form on compact upland soils. Root systems are extensive. Lateral roots of velvet mesquite spread 50 feet or more, and western honey mesquite laterals reached 75 feet on the Jornada Experimental Range. Taproots on floodplains may reach 25 feet or more.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution and climate.** Mesquites are largely limited to altitudes below 5,500 feet and perform best below 4,500 feet, where the frost-free season is 200 days or more. Temperature is described as the single most important factor limiting their geographic range; in the Great Plains they reach only the southern parts of Kansas and Colorado. Annual rainfall ranges from about 3 inches (Yuma, Arizona) to 30 inches. The authors note that mesquite is an efficient water user, but it demands far more water than associated grasses in the same type.

**Values.** Historically, mesquite supplied fuel and posts. Posts from heartwood lasted 50 to 75 years, but posts from young trees decayed within 8 to 10 years, and demand for fuel wood has declined. Mesquite provides cover and food for quail, doves, and other wildlife, and its flowers supply nectar for honeybees. Catlin found velvet mesquite leaves equal in feeding value to good alfalfa hay, and mesquite pods superior to alfalfa in digestibility. However, the authors measured only about 40 pounds of leafage per acre in a moderate stand and an average of 13.7 pounds of beans per acre per year over five years. The bean crop failed completely in 1942. They conclude that forage values are usually overestimated.

**Causes of invasion.** The circular attributes encroachment to several interacting factors rather than a single cause:
- Cessation of recurrent [[Prairie Fire]] events, which earlier controlled seedlings.
- Seed dispersal by [[Livestock Grazing]] through cattle dung, with up to 1,000 sound seeds per cow chip (13% immediately viable), and by [[Rodent Seed Dispersal]] through caching by [[Merriam Kangaroo Rat]].
- Drought, which kills grass and opens space for seedlings, and heavy grazing, which reduces grass vigor and soil structure.
- Seed longevity: seeds collected in 1903 remained viable after 44 years in herbarium storage.
- Partial natural checks, including bean weevils that kill up to 90% of embryos and jackrabbit and rodent browsing of seedlings.

**Invasion history.** Griffiths documented mesquite spreading on the Santa Rita after fencing in 1903, and Thornber recorded young plants “coming in quite thickly” over 35,000 acres. Repeat photography from 1903 to 1941 shows grassland converting to a mesquite aspect. Upson, Cribbs, and Stanley's survey in southeastern Arizona found about 61% of the area with a light stand, 11% medium, and 4% heavy (over 80 trees per acre). Heavy stands were confined mainly to bottomlands. The authors estimate that about three-fourths of the velvet mesquite in the state represents invasion within the past half century.

## Key Findings

**1. Scale of the problem.** The circular reports about 9 million acres of mesquite in southeastern Arizona, with more than 70 million acres in the Southwest: about 55 million in Texas, about 10 million in New Mexico, and more than 9 million in Arizona. A conservative estimate puts roughly 35 million acres as invasion within the past century.

**2. Suppression of grass.** Grass cover declines nearly linearly as [[Mesquite Crown Cover]] increases. The critical threshold is about 7% mesquite crown cover, at which grass cover is about 20%. Above that, mesquite prevents perennial grasses from reaching satisfactory forage cover. Between 1932 and 1949 on the Santa Rita, perennial grass cover on heavily protected plots fell to less than one-tenth of its 1932 level.

**3. Elimination experiment (1940–48).** Sixteen one-acre plots were treated with four combinations: none, mesquite killed, [[Burroweed]] grubbed, or both. Key results:
- Within three years, perennial grass stand on mesquite-killed plots was double that of untreated plots.
- Average perennial herbage yield was 301.7 pounds per acre on mesquite-killed plots versus 143.0 pounds on untreated plots.
- Grass death loss in the 1942 drought was 42.8% on mesquite-killed plots versus 88.6% on untreated plots. In the 1947–48 droughts it was 73% versus 85%.
- Annual grass yield on mesquite-killed plots averaged five times that of untreated plots.
- Burroweed elimination alone produced only temporary grass gains, attributed largely to soil loosening from grubbing.

**4. Effects on individual species.** Mesquite removal benefited [[Rothrock Grama]] (short-lived and drought-sensitive), [[Black Grama]] (long-lived and drought-enduring), and perennial threeawns, which roughly doubled during the favorable 1946 season on mesquite-controlled areas. Arizona cottongrass showed a strong response on other parts of the Santa Rita.

**5. Soil moisture.** In the 1946 season, velvet mesquite depleted soil moisture to at least 30 feet from the stem in all sampled layers. Examples from the 6–12 inch layer at 10 feet: 81 days of unavailable moisture with live mesquite versus 10 days where mesquite was killed. The authors conclude that stands as light as 15 plants per acre, with roots reaching 30 feet, will reduce forage production.

**6. Erosion.** Accelerated sheet and rill erosion occurred across the study area before treatment. Killing mesquite and the resulting grass cover appeared to halt it on treated areas, while erosion continued on adjacent mesquite-infested land. Gully erosion on bottomlands can lower the water table so much that restoring grass requires mesquite removal plus check dams and water-spreading structures.

**7. Thinning study.** At four sites (A–D, 2-acre plots established in 1945), mesquite was thinned to 25, 16, 9, or zero plants per acre. Herbage yields rose with each degree of thinning, beginning at the lightest level at most sites. Site B's increases were three to four times the unthinned yield. The authors conclude that stands above about 9 to 16 plants per acre eventually reduce herbage yield.

**8. Reseeding.** [[Lehmann Lovegrass]] establishment was poor in unthinned stands. Seedlings numbered 23 per 100 square feet at site A and 3 at site B, versus 528 and 21 where mesquite was completely killed. Mesquite elimination is therefore necessary for successful artificial reseeding in moderate to heavy stands.

**9. Control methods.** Sprouting is the central obstacle. Dormant buds sit on the stem base within about 6 inches of the soil surface, not on roots. Effective control must kill these buds or remove them by grubbing. The authors recommend:
- [[Sodium Arsenite]]: the most effective chemical, applied by frilling or basin methods. Basin application killed 95% of mesquites versus 40% without basins. Frilling at a 1:2 concentration gave over 90% kill. Best timing is December to March, with January and February preferred. The chemical is extremely toxic to people and animals and must be handled with strict protective measures.
- [[Diesel Oil]] and light petroleum oils: kills of 85% or more were achieved. Three gallons of Diesel oil treated 20 plants adequately in sandy loams. Kerosene and stove oil were more toxic than Diesel at 1 pint per plant, but Diesel was about 25% cheaper in southern Arizona.
- [[2,4-D and 2,4,5-T Herbicides]]: results were erratic, mostly causing defoliation rather than kill, and the authors do not recommend them for arid Arizona ranges.
- Hand grubbing is recommended for seedlings, and machinery for dense stands.

**10. Economics.** Costs in 1947 were about 4 cents per plant for Diesel oil spraying. Labor accounted for 91% of frilling costs and 56% of basin costs. Diesel control on a section (640 acres) of range averaging 101 mesquites per acre would cost $2,585.60 (at $4.04 per acre). The estimated increase is 2,150 pounds of beef per year, worth $365.07 at the 1941–50 average calf price. At 5% interest, the outlay would be repaid in about 9 years, with benefits lasting a further 16 years over an assumed 25-year effect.

**11. Recommendations.**
- Begin control early, when invasion is light and costs are low.
- Start near ranch headquarters or in holding pastures, where the work is most visible and livestock handling improves.
- Use grubbing for seedlings, manual arsenite or Diesel oil for stands under roughly 300 plants per acre, and power machinery for denser upland stands or bottomlands, followed by manual follow-up.
- Control is practical only on former good grassland with 12 to 20 inches of rainfall and a substantial grass remnant. Where grass cover is below 10%, pair control with reseeding.
- Work in strips, mark boundaries, and schedule work for cool winter months to reduce worker hazards.
- Grazing systems that build perennial grass vigor (such as rest from summer grazing) discourage seedling establishment, though they are unlikely to replace direct control once mesquite is established.
