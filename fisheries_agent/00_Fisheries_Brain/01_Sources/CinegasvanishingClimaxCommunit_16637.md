# Ciénegas of the American Southwest: Vanishing Climax Communities

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

Hendrickson and Minckley review the historical distribution, cultural history, hydrology and ecology of [[Ciénega]] habitats in southeastern Arizona. The authors define the ciénega as a mid-elevation (1,000–2,000 m) wetland of permanently saturated, highly organic, reducing soils, dominated by low sedges, and fed by perennial springs and headwater streams. They hypothesize that the ciénega is an aquatic climax community, supported by paleoecological evidence of long-persisting conditions (over 4,000 years in places). Ciénegas and other marshland habitats declined sharply in the past century, with [[Arroyo Cutting]] and [[Overgrazing]] by cattle identified as the main proximate drivers, while the role of climate remains debated. The authors argue that ciénega conditions could be restored at historic sites by providing a constant water supply and reducing catastrophic flooding.

## Background & Description

The paper is a historical and ecological review rather than a field survey. It draws on early explorer journals, mission records, 19th- and 20th-century surveys, palynological studies and hydrological reports. The geographic scope is Arizona south of the Gila River and east of the Avra-Altar Valley, with case studies of the [[San Simon Valley]], [[Sulphur Spring Valley]], [[San Pedro Valley]], [[Santa Cruz Valley]] and the middle [[Gila River]].

Three marshland types are distinguished in Arizona (Table 2 summarizes attributes):

- **Alpine meadowlands** (>2,000 m): cold-adapted rushes, sedges and grasses within conifer forest, frost-affected.
- **Ciénegas** (1,000–2,000 m): perennial spring and headwater wetlands with low sedges (e.g., *Eleocharis* spp.), rushes, and rare cattails; scarcely fluctuating water and few scouring floods.
- **Riverine marshes** (<1,000 m): transitory, tall-emergent marshes (*Phragmites australis*, *Typha domingensis*, *Scirpus* spp.) in oxbows and along large, high-order channels, prone to removal by floods.

The authors' plant checklist (Table 1, preliminary) lists macrophytic taxa across aquatic, semiaquatic and riparian categories, including Characeae, Cyperaceae, Poaceae, Salicaceae and Onagraceae, and records the wetland flora of the region's ciénegas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Hydrology and geomorphology.** Ciénegas form where groundwater intersects the surface, typically above impervious dikes, faults, bedrock or convex-concave channel profiles. The authors apply Chorley's dynamic equilibrium concept and Bull's [[Critical Power Threshold]] (C.P.T.) to explain when a channel aggrades (favoring ciénega) or incises (causing drying). Permanent groundwater, stable discharge and headwater position are key prerequisites.

**Succession.** The proposed successional sequence runs from [[Cattail]] (*Typha*) and tall bulrushes to willows (*Salix gooddingii*, *S. lasiolepis*) and finally to a low-sedge (*Eleocharis*) climax. Organic accumulation lowers redox potential (Eh), excluding deep-rooted colonists. Incision can create vertical-walled pools and allow Goodding Willow and Fremont Cottonwood to establish.

**Biota.** Ciénegas support detritus-based communities and narrow-niche specialists. Notable taxa include hydrobiid snails (*Tryonia*, *Fontelicella*), thick-bodied chubs (*Gila intermedia*; *Gila purpurea* in the Yaqui system), and the endangered [[Sonoran Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), now largely restricted to ciénegas and springs in the United States. Large river fishes such as [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]] were associated with riverine marshes when discharges were greater and more stable.

**Climate.** The region has a bimodal rainfall pattern (summer monsoon and winter), annual precipitation of 13–48 cm, and high spatial variability. Pollen records show past Pluvial conditions and altithermal periods. Some evidence suggests decreasing winter rainfall since 1895, but no statistically significant change was found in annual totals.

**Human history.** Four cultural phases are described: prehistoric Hohokam and other agricultural peoples; Spanish missions and ranching (1600s–1700s); Mexican ranching (after 1821); and Angloamerican settlement with major cattle expansion after railroad access in the 1880s. Epidemics, Apache raids, mining, irrigation and groundwater pumping all shaped the landscape.

## Key Findings

- **Definition and status:** Ciénegas are restricted to mid-elevation headwater wetlands with permanently saturated organic soils. Their area declined greatly over the past century, and many historic sites are now incised arroyos.
- **Documented losses:** The San Simon Valley changed rapidly after about 1885, and San Simon Ciénega (486 ha, per Schwennesen, 1917) survives only as an artificially maintained, lentic reservoir behind a 1953 concrete dam. Hooker Ciénega had 46 aquatic and semiaquatic taxa recorded by Yatskievych and Jenkins (1981).
- **Climax hypothesis:** Paleoecological data (Martin, 1963a) indicate ciénega conditions persisting over more than 4,000 years, supporting the idea that ciénegas are a persistent, long-lived aquatic climax.
- **Causal debate:** Arroyo cutting is associated with overgrazing combined with drought (1891–93 livestock mortality of 50–75%), channelization, and road and railroad embankments. The authors argue that grazing concentrated cattle near water and damaged ciénegas, and that stock tanks may reduce pressure on natural waters. Climate is considered a possible co-factor, but the evidence is contested.
- **Cattle estimates:** The authors estimate about 377,474 cattle grazing four counties in the five years before the 1893 drought (after a 50% correction to tax returns), versus 180,200 for 1977–1981.
- **Stock tank hypothesis:** Only 27 critically placed stock tanks would add grazing area equivalent to a perennial Rio San Pedro along its full length.
- **Fish records:** Historic accounts of "salmon trout" along the San Pedro are attributed to [[Colorado Squawfish]], which the authors describe as formerly abundant and nearing extinction.
- **Riverine marsh distinction:** Riverine marshes are transitory and dominated by lentic water and tall emergents; the authors place the transition to riverine marsh at a point of increasing watershed size and reduced structural control.
- **Restoration recommendations:** Maintain permanent groundwater, avoid pumping-driven fluctuation, use sealed alluvial cross-sections (check dams that intersect groundwater barriers), and prioritize ciénegas for conservation as unique remnants of natural heritage. Open-water fish habitat may require dredging or limited grazing.
- **Pumping and urban threats:** Groundwater pumping (especially near Eloy and Tucson) and projected development at Empire Ranch are identified as threats to base flows in Ciénega Creek, Sonoita Creek and Babocomari River.
- **Ecological framework:** The authors argue that the [[River Continuum Concept]] is largely inappropriate for southwestern streams because unpredictable flow precludes biotic communities dependent on upstream inputs.
