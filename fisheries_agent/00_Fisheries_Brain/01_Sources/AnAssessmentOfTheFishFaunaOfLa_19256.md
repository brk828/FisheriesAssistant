# Fish Fauna of Lake Calumet and Adjacent Wetlands, Chicago, Illinois: Past, Present, and Future

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This 1984 study in the *Transactions of the Illinois Academy of Sciences* (Greenfield and Rogner, Northern Illinois University) surveys the fishes of [[Lake Calumet]] and its adjacent wetlands in Chicago. Sampling ran from September 1981 through August 1982 using seines and electrofishing. The survey recorded 27 species from 10 families. Twenty-six species occurred in Lake Calumet and eight in the adjacent wetlands, with one species, the [[Central Mudminnow]], found only in the wetlands. Comparison with historical records suggests several species have been extirpated from the lake. Using [[Karr Index]] scoring, the authors rated the whole-lake fish community "Good" (score 48) and judged it relatively diverse, though not unique. The authors recommend against further filling and slip construction in the northern lake, which they expect would degrade the existing fauna.

## Background & Description

Lake Calumet is the largest body of water in Cook County west of Lake Michigan. The lake and its wetlands were relatively undisturbed until about 1930, when beach-sand mining and filling of the northern marsh began. Later dredging and filling created several large slips on the east shore, and two dikes now cross the lake. Published fish data for the area are sparse. The earliest record is [[Nelson 1876]], who reported nine species from the [[Calumet River]]. Other sources include [[Meek and Hildebrand 1910]], [[Forbes and Richardson 1920]], unpublished Field Museum collections from 1941, [[Vidal and Wight 1975]] (samples from 1952 and 1970), [[Dennison 1978]], and [[Polls et al 1980]], which sampled [[Turning Basin Number 5]].

The authors undertook the study because the lake is unusual within Chicago city limits, pre-disturbance fish data exist, and future development could alter the fauna. The lake was divided into nine sampling sites, and the adjacent wetlands were treated as a single unit because several sites were modified during the study.

**Methods (summary):**
- Collections were made on 11 dates between September 1981 and August 1982.
- Gear included 15 m and 7.6 m bag seines, a 4.6 m minnow seine, and a Coffelt electroshocker (AC, 125–250 V).
- Specimens were preserved in 10% formalin, transferred to 40% isopropyl alcohol, or released if field-identifiable.
- Identification followed [[Smith 1979]]. Vouchers were deposited at the Field Museum, the Illinois Natural History Survey, and the NIU fish collection.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Habitat conditions varied across the lake:
- **Shorelines:** Mostly cement rubble, with soil and vegetation in some areas and steep wood or metal walls along the dredged slips.
- **Depth:** Mostly shallow (around 2 m or less), with dredged areas reaching about 10 m. The bottom was mostly soft mud.
- **Connectivity:** The lake links to Lake Michigan through the Calumet River and to the DesPlaines River through the Little Calumet River and Calumet-Sag Channel. The authors caution that species collected in the lake may not reproduce there.

Site descriptions highlight these differences. Site 1 (north) is deep, with about 8 acres (3.2 ha) of emergent vegetation. Site 3 has a spit, a beach, and an inlet fed by wetland discharge. Site 5 is a dredged east-shore slip with cement rubble. Site 9 at [[Turning Basin Number 5]] is shallow, with mud and gravel. The site 4 canal runs alongside the Calumet Expressway Frontage Road.

Historical comparison indicates changes in the fauna:
- Becker (1976) listed 46 species for the general Lake Calumet area, so the 26 lake species recorded here represent 56.5% of that total.
- Of the 15 species Smith (1979) listed for Lake Calumet, eight (53.3%) were pre-1908 records.
- The tadpole madtom, starhead topminnow, northern pike, grass pickerel, and Iowa darter were not found in this survey, though some of these may be older records. The orangespotted sunfish was recorded here but not by Becker.

## Key Findings

**Species richness and distribution**
- 27 species from 10 families were collected. Twenty-six occurred in Lake Calumet and eight in the adjacent wetlands.
- The maximum at any wetland site was five species. The maximum at any lake site was 16 species.
- The [[Chinook Salmon]] was present in the fall. No endangered or threatened species were collected or observed.
- Sites 1, 3, and 9 each held 16 species, the highest in the lake.

**Reproduction**
- Young-of-the-year of all eight wetland species were captured, indicating reproduction in the wetlands.
- Young-of-the-year of six species were captured in the lake: Johnny darter, channel catfish, black bullhead, [[Largemouth Bass]], orangespotted sunfish, and [[Gizzard Shad]]. Gravid female and ripe male [[Common Carp]] and [[Fathead Minnow]] were also taken.

**Wetland versus lake comparison**
- Apart from two ponds south of 122nd Street, where the central mudminnow was abundant, the wetlands did not differ notably from the lake. The authors suggest the mudminnow's abundance there may relate to the absence of predatory fishes, dense aquatic vegetation, and tolerance of winter low oxygen.

**Karr Index assessment**
- Whole lake: score 48, "Good." The authors note Karr developed the index for streams, so the boundaries do not transfer directly to lakes. If the index had been designed for lacustrine systems, the rating would likely have been higher.
- Site scores: site 9 = 44; site 1 = 42; site 3 = 42; site 4 = 26 (poor); site 5 = 32 (poor); site 6 = 31 (poor); sites 7 and 8 = 36 (fair-poor); site 2 = 40 (fair).
- Site 1 is judged the highest-quality community. It has the largest game-fish populations, including [[Black Crappie]], white crappie, largemouth bass, pumpkinseed, and bluegill. It also has abundant [[Sand Shiner]] and [[Emerald Shiner]], and a single spotfin shiner.

**Indicator species**
- Black crappie and yellow perch indicate clear water, and black crappie are less tolerant of turbidity than white crappie.
- Bluntnose minnows dominated in the lake and fathead minnows in the wetlands. Bluntnose minnows favor less turbid water.
- The continued presence of the sand shiner, which is sensitive to siltation, suggests the lake has not been degraded enough to eliminate it.
- The spottail shiner was most abundant at site 9, suggesting acceptable water quality there.

**Freshwater drum**
- A single specimen was taken at site 3. Literature cited in the paper suggested the species had been extirpated from the region, so this is a notable record.

**Habitat modification**
- Sites with the most extensive modification, especially the dredged east-shore slips at site 5, have fewer species and less habitat variety.
- Soil over cement rubble encourages vegetation, which supports fishes and their invertebrate prey. The authors suggest many sites could improve by covering rubble with soil.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- If Lake Calumet avoids severe pollution from adjacent industry or landfills, it should continue to support fish reproduction.
- Additional filling of the northern lake would detrimentally affect the good fauna at site 1. Further slip construction would also reduce habitat suitability, as shown by comparing developed and undeveloped areas.
