# Biology and Vital Statistics of Rainbow Trout in the Finger Lakes Region, New York

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This paper, a 1959 study published in the *New York Fish and Game Journal* (Vol. 6, No. 2) and derived from a doctoral thesis by [[Wilbur L. Hartman]], reports a joint study of [[Rainbow Trout]] in the [[Finger Lakes]] (Cayuga, Seneca, Skaneateles, Keuka and Canandaigua Lakes and their spawning tributaries). The work was conducted from 1953 to 1957 by the [[New York State Conservation Department]] and [[Cornell University]]. Populations are sustained largely by natural reproduction. Of 2,803 adult fish sampled and tagged, scale readings were obtained for 2,513. The author concludes that stream habitat restoration is fundamental to the fishery, and that extending the lake-fishing season into fall is the most practical current way to increase harvest, since natural mortality after spawning is roughly double angling loss.

## Background & Description

Rainbow trout (*Salmo gairdneri*) were introduced to the Finger Lakes in the 1880s, with later plantings in the 1920s. Populations are now maintained largely by natural propagation. Skaneateles, Seneca, Keuka and Canandaigua Lakes support the largest populations, while introductions were essentially unsuccessful in Cayuga Lake; Owasco Lake once held a large population. Earlier work by [[Rayner]] (1941), [[Webster]] and [[Hatch]] (1957) addressed management and natural spawning success.

Study streams were [[Grout Brook]], [[Catherine Creek]] and [[Cold Brook]], with [[Naples Creek]] and [[Canandaigua Lake]] included in a separate Conservation Department study. Spawning runs were sampled by direct-current electrofishing in spring, with fish tagged using serially numbered mandible tags. Ages were determined from scales read by back-projection at 41 diameters, using annuli and spawning marks as criteria. Creel censuses were conducted by state game protectors during opening week (1955–1957).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lakes:** The Finger Lakes lie in deep, north–south glaciated valleys on the Appalachian Plateau. The six trout-supporting lakes are oligotrophic, with abundant oxygen at all depths, low summer bottom temperatures and pH usually between 7 and 8. Otisco Lake is comparatively shallow and falls outside this category.
- **Fish community:** Table 2 lists numerous "cold water" and "warm water" fishes across the lakes, including [[Lake Trout]], [[Lake Cisco]], [[Common Whitefish]], [[Alewife]], [[Smelt]], [[Yellow Perch]] and [[Largemouth Bass]].
- **Lake lamprey:** [[Sea Lamprey]] (*Petromyzon marinus*) has been present in Cayuga Lake since 1875 and Seneca Lake since 1894. Its role in rainbow trout life history is unknown.
- **Streams:** Grout Brook, Catherine Creek and Cold Brook drain limited watersheds and flow into the southern ends of the lakes. Bottoms are mainly rubble. Water chemistry was saturated with oxygen with pH about 8. Calcium carbonate hardness was 110 ppm (Grout Brook), 170 ppm (Cold Brook) and 185 ppm (Catherine Creek).
- **Thermal regime:** Summer maxima were 65°F in Grout and Cold Brooks (sustained by springs) but 85°F in Catherine Creek, where summer flow is reduced and riffles are unshaded.

## Key Findings

**Population structure and life history**
- Large numbers of fry hatch annually in the tributaries; Grout and Cold Brooks serve as parr nurseries, with some parr remaining up to 3 years.
- Time spent as parr influenced the age at which fish first returned to spawn. Fish with one parr year appeared at younger ages than those with two.
- On average, males spawned for the first time at a younger age than females. Few fish of either sex spawned more than once; the record was five spawnings for several Catherine Creek fish.
- Repeat spawners made up 18–33 per cent of spawning populations, with maiden fish dominating in all instances.
- Sex ratios varied between samples; females slightly predominated overall.

**Growth**
- Females were significantly larger than males through age group IV in Grout and Cold Brooks, and through age group V in Catherine Creek.
- Catherine Creek fish grew fastest. Grout Brook growth leveled off in older fish, which the author attributes to limited forage in Skaneateles Lake, whereas alewives in Seneca and Keuka Lakes supported better growth.
- Compared with 1939 data for Grout Brook, mean lengths were generally greater in 1939, though maximum length was greater in 1953–1956 (24.3 inches versus 22.5 inches).

**Mortality and exploitation**
- Stream fishing removed much more of the spawning populations than lake fishing: averages of 17 per cent (Grout Brook), 13 per cent (Catherine Creek) and 15 per cent (Cold Brook) by stream, versus about 5 per cent by lake after return.
- Total utilization was about 29 per cent for Grout Brook, 25 per cent for Catherine Creek and 32 per cent for Naples Creek.
- Natural mortality was estimated to be about double angling loss; male survival was substantially lower than female survival after spawning.
- Lamprey attacks marked 28 per cent of fish scarred and about 13 per cent with open wounds in Seneca Lake.

**Management implications**
- The bag limit of five for streams does not restrict catch, since most successful anglers caught one fish.
- The 7-inch stream size limit protects 1- and 2-year parr but leaves all spawning adults vulnerable.
- Lake fishing exploitation is low and spread across the season, so the 15-inch lake size limit and bag limit of three appear non-restrictive.
- A trial extension of the lake season to December 1 at Skaneateles Lake (1956–1957) produced little additional harvest, though the 1957 extension added 3 per cent of recoveries.
- Stream maintenance is critical: present conditions in all three streams were judged poor and deteriorating. Recommended actions include removing barriers, adding pools and cover in second-class spawning areas, and protecting first-class spawning areas (about 18 per cent of stream length).
- Extension of the lake season into fall was identified as the most practical near-term way to increase harvest.
