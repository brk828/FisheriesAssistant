# Sediment Accumulation and Original Basin Morphology of Winona Lake, Indiana

#limnology #aquatic-ecology #sedimentation

## Executive Summary

This study maps the sediment fill of [[Winona Lake]], a kettle-hole glacial lake in Kosciusko County, Indiana. Using 75 borings through the organic sediment down to gravel or sand, the author reconstructed the original lake bottom, six cross-section profiles, and a contour map. The lake's original basin held about 33.9 million cubic meters of water space, and about 14.8 million cubic meters (43.6%) of it is now filled with sediment. Most sediment lies in deep water rather than near shore, which contradicts common assumptions about lake filling. The study also offers an explanation for the unusually thick deposits on the [[Marl Islands]]. The work is a contribution from the Biology Department of Heidelberg College and the Zoology Department of Indiana University.

## Background & Description

**Study purpose.** The paper describes how sedimentary deposits are distributed across the main body of the lake, excluding the portion labeled Little Eagle. The author aims to make more accurate generalizations about how lakes fill and become extinct, and plans similar studies on other lake types.

**Setting.** Winona Lake lies on the western border of the [[Packerton Moraine]], an interlobate kettle moraine between the Huron-Erie and Saginaw lobes of the [[Wisconsin Ice Sheet]]. It is a kettle-hole glacial lake. Its greatest length is 1.7 miles, its average width is slightly more than 0.5 mile, and its maximum depth is 80 feet. Two streams, Cherry Creek and Sugar Creek, enter the lake, and one outlet drains it. A wave-cut terrace along the eastern shore lies 5 to 8 feet above the present lake level, which suggests an earlier lowering of the lake.

**Boring method.** Sediment cores were collected by a technique adapted from sulphur-well recovery. An iron casing (1¼ inch diameter) was driven into the sediment, with a half-inch tubing inside it. Water forced through the tubing washed sediment out of the casing, and a sediment plug was then drawn up for each sample. Two quarter-inch holes drilled 4 feet above the tubing tip kept soft samples from being expelled. Water-based drilling used a float anchored in place, with a derrick, block and tackle, and windlass. Holes were drilled in 80 feet of water through more than 50 feet of sediment. Samples were taken at roughly 10-foot intervals and stored in labeled pint fruit jars.

**Mapping.** Borings were laid out along lines labeled A-A, B-B, and so on, and the map was built from their depths relative to present water level. Scattered borings 66 to 75 helped refine the original bottom between the main sections. The contour interval was two meters, and volumes were calculated by treating the basins as stacked frustra of inverted cones, with areas measured by planimeter. The present basin is 24 m deep (12 frustra), and the original basin is 38.8 m deep (20 frustra).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Original bottom.** The original basin was irregular, with knobs, swales, and ridges resembling the surrounding morainic terrain. Lake currents had sorted the original bottom materials before lake sediments began to dominate. Ridges were covered in coarse gravel, and depressions held fine sand.

**Sediment composition.** Sediment in the middle of the lake averages almost 50% silicon dioxide, compared with about 10% near shore. The remainder is mostly carbonates. The [[Marl Islands]] are about 90% carbonate.

**Wave and current processes.** Shore zone wave action abrades and grinds sediment into fine particles that remain in suspension and spread across the lake before settling in deep water. Unabraded material settles along the shore. Currents remove sediment from ridges and carry it to depressions, which fill first.

**Vegetation role.** Where the marl islands' original peaks lay close enough to the surface, vegetation could take root. This vegetation trapped sediment rapidly and shielded it from currents. Once accumulations reached the wave zone, erosion removed them about as fast as they formed.

## Key Findings

1. **Total sediment volume:** 14,819,625 cubic meters, or 43.6% of the original basin volume (33,934,857 m³). The present basin volume is 19,115,232 m³.
2. **Deposition is concentrated in deep water.** The 50% sediment contour lies near the 8–10 m level. Area inside the 8-meter contour is 917,376 m², and the original basin area outside it is 2,338,496 m² (2.5 times greater). The author concludes that settling was about 2.5 times faster, on average, inside the 50% contour than outside it.
3. **Original basin was far more irregular than the present bottom.** The original bottom had five deep holes (A–E), compared with one in the present basin, plus ridges and shelves.
4. **Sediment depth in deep holes is strikingly uniform.** Original depth versus sediment depth:
   - Hole A: 26.6 m original, 13.1 m sediment
   - Hole B: 38.8 m original, 14.8 m sediment
   - Hole C: 21.5 m original, 15.5 m sediment
   - Hole D: 20.3 m original, 9.8 m sediment
   - Hole E: 16.6 m original, 13.6 m sediment

   The author concludes that deposition is largely independent of hole depth and distance from shore, as long as the hole lies below the wave zone.
5. **Sediment depth follows bottom configuration.** Deepest sediment occurs in holes, the shallowest on steep slopes and ridges, and intermediate amounts on gentle slopes. For example, along section B-B, sediment was 43.5 ft and 51.2 ft in two depressions but only 9.8 ft on a ridge.
6. **Open-water area lost.** The original lake area (3,255,872 m²) minus the present area (2,037,128 m²) gives 1,218,744 m² (37.4%) of the original area now emergent.
7. **Marl islands.** Both islands sit on original knobs (peaks about 8 m and 10 m below the original surface), rising 4 to 10 m above surrounding bottom. Their sediment depth equals or exceeds that of the surrounding depressions, which is the reverse of the usual pattern on ridges. The islands now lie about 1.5 m below the water surface (the source text is OCR-garbled here).
8. **Conclusion on filling pattern.** The lake is filling faster in the middle than along the shore, which the author says is contrary to what has often been assumed. Fill is controlled largely by bottom configuration until depressions reach the level of adjacent ridges.
