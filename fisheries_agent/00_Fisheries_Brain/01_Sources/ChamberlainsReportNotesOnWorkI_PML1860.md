# Chamberlain's 1904 Arizona Field Notes: Freshwater Fish Survey and Habitat Decline

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

These field notes, from a 1904 survey by Chamberlain during a tour of Arizona, record fish collections and stream conditions across the [[Colorado River]], [[Santa Cruz River]], [[Sonoita Creek]], [[San Pedro River]], the [[Gila River]] basin, and the [[Salt River]] basin. Chamberlain used seine hauls, hook-and-line and [[Powder Fishing]] at numbered stations. He recorded a native fauna of small cyprinids, topminnows, suckers and a few larger species, and he described most of the streams as in decline. The main causes he identified were irrigation diversion, livestock trampling, mining wastes and sediment, dynamite use, and the loss of riparian timber. The notes mix field observations with local reports, and some identifications are flagged as uncertain.

## Background & Description

The document is a set of field notes from an itinerary covering January to April 1904. It begins at Yuma, moves through Tucson, the Santa Cruz and San Pedro valleys, the upper Gila, and the Tonto Basin, and returns to Tucson before departing for Alaska on 28 April. The survey was organized around numbered collecting stations, with entries for water temperature, flow, channel form, and substrate. Species were identified mostly by common local names, with scientific names added in parentheses. Several names carry question marks or "spelling uncertain" notes, so the taxonomy should be treated as provisional.

Stations are grouped by drainage:

- **Colorado River (Station I):** Seined at the mouth of the [[Gila River]] channel near Yuma, 27 February 1904. Three hauls were made, using a 45-ft net.
- **Santa Cruz River (San Xavier and Nogales):** Examined at San Xavier, where a dam diverted the entire flow to irrigation.
- **Sonoita Creek and [[Monkey Spring]]:** Examined near Patagonia, where a spring-fed pond held several native species.
- **San Pedro River, Babacomari Creek:** Sampled near Fairbank and Charleston.
- **Gila River basin:** Covers Safford, the [[Cienega Spring]], San Francisco River, Blue Creek, K.P. Creek, and the Gila above Duncan.
- **Salt River basin:** Covers Roosevelt, Tonto Creek, Pine Creek, East Verde River, and [[Fossil Creek]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water conditions.** Temperatures varied widely with elevation and flow. Recorded values include 62°F for the Colorado River and 65°F for its backwater on 27 February, 78°F at San Xavier on 29 March, 80°F at Monkey Spring, and 82°F on the Gila at Safford at 3 p.m. Cooler high-elevation sites included 60°F on K.P. Creek and East Verde River, 53°F on Pine Creek, and about 70°F at Fossil Creek springs. Flows were modest: Sonoita Creek carried 50–70 inches (1.25–1.75 cfs), Monkey Spring about 50 inches (1.25 cfs), Pine Creek about 15 inches (0.38 cfs), and the Gila diversion about 80 inches (2.0 cfs). Water quality was often alkaline or saline, with lime encrustations at Monkey Spring and Fossil Creek and salt-rich water in the Salt River system.

**Habitat types.** The stations covered a range of habitats: sandy desert channels, backwater sloughs, cienegas, lime-deposit spring pools, riffles in mountain streams, and rocky canyon pools.

**Aquatic and riparian vegetation.** Water cress, Chara, duckweed, pondweeds, sedges, willow, and cottonwood were recorded. Vegetation was sparse in some reaches and dense in others. Cattle trampling and timber cutting are described as reducing it.

**Human pressures.** Chamberlain identified several:
- Irrigation dams and ditches that diverted nearly all flow, left channels dry, and blocked fish migration.
- Livestock grazing that trampled banks and destroyed plants.
- Mining operations at Clifton and Charleston, whose sediment and possible cyanide contamination were suspected in fish losses.
- Sediment from floods and mining that filled pools and suffocated fish.
- Dynamite fishing, which Chamberlain said killed far more fish than could be recovered.

## Key Findings

**Fish assemblages by drainage:**

- **Colorado River backwater (Yuma):** Common carp and black bullhead were abundant, with about 100 carp (5–7 in) per haul. Bonytail chub and razorback sucker were present, and a dozen large razorback suckers came from one deeper haul. No Colorado squawfish were taken.
- **Santa Cruz River (San Xavier):** [[Agosia]], [[Poecilia]] (Sonora topminnow), and the [[Gila Chub]] were the dominant fishes. The Gila chub was the most abundant species here. Two suckers were present, [[Desert Sucker]] and [[Sonora Sucker]]. Common carp had been introduced from Silver Lake near Tucson.
- **Sonoita Creek:** Agosia and Poecilia were very abundant. Desert and Sonora suckers were found in a small pool. Chamberlain linked the less advanced sexual condition of the fish to the higher elevation and cooler season.
- **Monkey Spring:** The upper pond held Sonora topminnow, [[Monkey Spring Pupfish]], and Gila chub, all considered native and reaching the spring by natural means. A Yaqui catfish had been introduced. The longest Gila chub seen was about 8 inches. Chamberlain noted three suckers with dark lateral blotches, which he could not find in fish smaller than 4 in or larger than 6 in.
- **Babacomari Creek:** Only Agosia were found, crowded in pools as if trying to ascend the canyon. No other species were seen.
- **Cienega Spring (Safford):** Gila chub were abundant, and the young appeared to be two year-classes. Agosia were present in a nearby stream. Catfish introduced to a private pond in 1902 spawned, and the young later largely disappeared.
- **Blue Creek and K.P. Creek:** Agosia were found in Blue Creek. K.P. Creek held only trout, with no other fish known. The largest trout measured about 9 in, and 12 in was the reported maximum.
- **Salt River basin:** Headwater chub, Sonora sucker, and [[Speckled Dace]] (identified by Chamberlain as loach minnow in one place) were found in East Verde River. Only desert sucker was recorded at Pine Creek. Fossil Creek held desert sucker, headwater chub, and speckled dace, and collections there were made with powder. Only Agosia were seen in the Fossil Creek ditches.

**Declines reported by local residents:**

- Once-abundant species, including [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Colorado Squawfish]], were said to have declined sharply. Razorback suckers were once sold in Tombstone as "buffalo."
- On the upper Gila, fish were abundant several years earlier, and no Colorado squawfish, suckers or other large fish had been taken in the two preceding years.
- Chamberlain thought mine wastes were a probable cause but not the sole one. He considered that other causes, including irrigation, drought and habitat loss, were sufficient to explain the decline.

**Main threats and conservation concerns identified:**

1. Irrigation diversions and temporary dams. Most dams blocked upstream and downstream fish movement, and unscreened ditches carried fish into fields.
2. Sediment and flood damage from deforestation, grazing, and mining waste, which filled pools and reduced the refuges that larger fish depend on.
3. Explosive fishing, which Chamberlain considered wasteful and destructive.
4. Drought and climate change, which the local residents reported were drying out the region, with abandoned farms in the Tonto Creek Valley cited as evidence.

**Recommendations implied by the notes:** Chamberlain recommended caution with explosives, suggesting that collecting be done in pools with strong bottom currents rather than in shallow water. He also noted the need to protect deep pools and riparian vegetation.
