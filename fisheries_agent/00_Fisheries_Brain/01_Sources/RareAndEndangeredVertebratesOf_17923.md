# Rare and Endangered Vertebrates of Alabama (1972 Symposium Report)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-fishes

## Executive Summary

This June 1972 publication from the Alabama Department of Conservation and Natural Resources, Division of Game and Fish, reports the results of the first Symposium on Rare and Endangered Vertebrates of Alabama. Expert panels in four vertebrate classes (mammals, birds, amphibians and reptiles, and fishes) classified species into [[Rare Species|Rare-1 and Rare-2]], [[Endangered Species]] and [[Status Undetermined]] categories. The report was compiled to answer growing public, governmental, educational and industrial requests for a state list of threatened vertebrates and to guide protection efforts and legislation. The fish section is the most detailed and reports that 49 of Alabama's 250 freshwater fish species, about 20 percent, are endangered, rare or declining faster than they can sustain themselves.

## Background & Description

The symposium grew out of a meeting on rare and endangered birds, which found that no concise status definitions existed and that classifications depended heavily on the opinion of one or two observers. The panel approach was adopted so that recognized experts could assess each vertebrate class.

Definitions used in the report:

- **Rare-1:** adapted from the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife. The species is not presently threatened with extinction but occurs in such small numbers that it may become endangered if its environment worsens. This includes species rare throughout their range.
- **Rare-2:** the species may be abundant where it occurs but is known from few localities or a restricted habitat within Alabama. This category was added because Alabama is only a small part of the continent.
- **Endangered:** the species is threatened with extinction through habitat destruction or modification, over-utilization for commercial or sporting purposes, disease or predation, or other natural or man-made factors.
- **Status Undetermined:** the species may be rare or endangered, but there is not enough information to decide. The category flags species for research.

Each species entry gives its known Alabama range, the probable cause of decline and a range map. Coding by colour: mammals green, birds blue, reptiles and amphibians red, fishes yellow. The report was edited by James E. Keeler, Chief, Game Research, and produced with help from panel members at Birmingham-Southern College, which hosted the symposium.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report consistently identifies habitat change as the main driver of decline:

- **Impoundment and dams:** reservoirs inundate spawning habitat, and dams block migrations. The report cites [[Tennessee-Tombigbee Waterway]] and Alabama River development as threats to large-river fishes.
- **Channelization and siltation:** ditching streams raises turbidity and temperature and makes diverse habitats uniform.
- **Pollution and pesticides:** chlorinated hydrocarbons such as DDT are linked to declines in pelicans, raptors and other predators near the top of the food chain. Strip-mine acid runoff threatens rivers such as the [[Cahaba River]].
- **Spring and groundwater loss:** many cave and spring fishes depend on stable water tables. Municipal water demand, well pumping and spring impoundment for sport fishing are threats.
- **Forest clearing and development:** clear-cutting pine and hardwood ravines and coastal development reduce habitat for salamanders, snakes, birds and tortoises.
- **Persecution and collecting:** illegal shooting, over-collecting, and "gassing" of [[Gopher Tortoise]] burrows at "Rattlesnake Rodeos" threaten snakes and other burrow-dwellers.
- **Natural events:** the report notes that hurricanes, such as Hurricane Camille in 1969, destroyed coastal salt marsh habitat.

## Key Findings

### Overview by class

- **Mammals:** the large predators (black bear, [[Red Wolf]], cougar) have been extirpated from most of their range. Bats that use caves are heavily affected by disturbance. Several beach mice are endemic and threatened by land development.
- **Birds:** about 380 species have been recorded in Alabama. The report excludes migrants and accidental visitors from the list. Specialists are the most vulnerable, while adaptable species such as the House Sparrow and Starling thrive.
- **Amphibians and reptiles:** 142 species occur in the state, 6 of them venomous (all snakes). The state's 23 turtle species rival only Mississippi's. The report's list of possible threats has seven categories, including urbanization, channelization, forest clearing, water pollution, strip mining, coastal dredging and deliberate persecution.
- **Fishes:** 250 freshwater species are known, of which 49 are rare, endangered or declining. Ten have disappeared where they were once captured, and three are totally extinct.

### Selected species highlights

- **Mammals:** the [[Indiana Myotis]] is federally listed as endangered. The [[Red Wolf]] may survive only as a possible hybrid near Mobile.
- **Birds:** the [[Brown Pelican]] declined from about 1,800 birds in 1956 to about 60 in 1971. The [[Red-cockaded Woodpecker]] is endangered due to loss of mature pine habitat. The [[Ivory-billed Woodpecker]] was last reported in Alabama in 1907. [[Bachman's Warbler]] was last reported in the state in 1959. A [[Bald Eagle]] and [[American Peregrine Falcon]] are federally listed. A ruffed grouse remnant population in Jackson County descends from 60 birds from Ohio released in 1958.
- **Reptiles and amphibians:** the [[Red Hills Salamander]] (undiscovered until 1959) is endemic to Alabama and is the only living member of its genus. The [[Flattened Musk Turtle]] is endemic to the Warrior River system. The [[Florida Pine Snake]] and [[Eastern Indigo Snake]] rely on gopher tortoise burrows. The [[Mississippi Alligator]] is improving but absent from much of its former range.
- **Fishes:** six Alabama endemics survive: Cahaba shiner, skygazer shiner, unnamed cavefish, lowland banded sculpin, pygmy sculpin and watercress darter. Two endemics (whiteline topminnow and unnamed pygmy sunfish) appear extinct. The [[Watercress Darter]] is known from a single small spring in Bessemer, with probably fewer than 800 individuals. The [[Pygmy Sculpin]] is restricted to [[Coldwater Spring]], which supplies Anniston's water. The [[Lake Sturgeon]] was last captured in Alabama in the early 1950s. The [[Shovelnose Sturgeon]] and [[Crystal Darter]] depend on undisturbed gravel bars in the Cahaba and Tombigbee rivers. The [[Atlantic Sturgeon]] needs fresh water to spawn, and river modification is a serious limit.

### Status categories for fishes

- **Rare-1** (examples): blue shiner, bluestripe shiner, skygazer shiner, lowland banded sculpin, Tuscumbia darter, freckled darter.
- **Rare-2** (examples): Atlantic sturgeon, popeye shiner, southern redbelly dace, brindled madtom, least killifish, mottled sculpin.
- **Endangered** (examples): shovelnose sturgeon, frecklebelly madtom, Cahaba shiner, pygmy sculpin, crystal darter, coldwater darter, watercress darter, goldline darter.
- **Status Undetermined** (examples): American brook lamprey, lake sturgeon, Alabama shad, flame chub, blue sucker, harelip sucker, whiteline topminnow.

### Conclusions and recommendations

- The lists are meant to guide research, protection and legislation. Species in Status Undetermined need study.
- Protection of spring habitats, undisturbed gravel bars, headwater streams and salt marshes is central.
- Land and water planning (dams, channelization, mining, water supply) should consider the vulnerability of endemic and restricted-range species.
- The authors note that classifications may change as new information emerges, and that extinction is partly natural but has been greatly accelerated by human activity.
