# Genetic Diversity of Razorback Sucker as Determined by Restriction Endonuclease Analysis of Mitochondrial DNA

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This 1993 final report to the US Bureau of Reclamation, by Thomas E. Dowling and W. L. Minckley of Arizona State University, uses restriction endonuclease analysis of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) to measure genetic diversity in the endangered [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a large catostomid endemic to the [[Colorado River Basin]]. It examines wild populations, then hatchery year classes produced at the [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (DNFH), and ends with management recommendations for [[Lake Mohave]]. The authors conclude that wild populations are genetically diverse but not strongly differentiated, that hatchery propagation has reduced diversity in at least one year class, and that wild larval collection is the preferred conservation option.

## Background & Description

The razorback sucker is a large "big-river" catostomid historically widespread across the Colorado River basin, including the Gila River drainage. Human impacts (dams, habitat modification, non-native species competition, predation and hybridization) have extirpated it from much of its range. It was federally listed as endangered by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in 1991. Remaining populations are almost entirely adults, thought to be 25 to more than 40 years old, and recruitment is undetected.

Captive breeding began in 1981, when adults from Lake Mohave were transported to DNFH to form a broodstock "as a hedge against extinction." The report treats this hatchery stock as a test case for whether production-oriented hatchery methods can preserve the genetic variation that conservation requires.

The study uses mtDNA, which is maternally inherited and evolves rapidly in many vertebrates, making it useful for tracing maternal lineages. Eight restriction enzymes were used to generate composite haplotypes for each individual. Allozyme analyses, reported separately by investigators at the University of California, Los Angeles, are referenced but not included.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Historical distribution and range contraction:** Razorbacks were most likely discontinuously distributed before development, concentrated in wide, non-canyon mainstem and large-tributary reaches. Dams now disrupt movement corridors.
- **Lake Mohave:** Formed in 1954 by closure of [[Davis Dam]]; about 115 km² at full pool, with a 60 km riverine upper reach and a wider lower basin. Tag-recapture data estimate fewer than 55,000 adult razorbacks.
- **Remaining wild populations:** The upper Green River has fewer than 1,000 adults; Lake Mohave has roughly 55,000.
- **Recruitment failure:** Strong initial year classes in new reservoirs failed to reproduce, and populations disappeared about 40 years after impoundment. Hypotheses tested included larval transport through dam operations (considered unlikely), food limitation (supported in lab and pond studies), and predation by non-native fishes (considered well supported). Twenty-three species have been introduced into Lake Mohave, at least three-quarters of which are known or predicted to eat eggs or larvae.
- **Climate history:** Full glacial conditions about 18 thousand years ago (ka) were roughly 6.3 °C cooler on the Colorado Plateau. The authors argue this made upper-basin rivers thermally unsuitable, leaving the lower basin as a thermal refugium.
- **Hybridization:** Introgression with [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (*Catostomus latipinnis*) and other introduced suckers was assessed.

## Key Findings

**1. Wild population mtDNA variation**
- Restriction analysis of 146 specimens with eight enzymes produced about 160 fragments per individual and 38 composite haplotypes. An additional 15 individuals were partially analyzed, and eight were found to carry flannelmouth sucker mtDNA.
- Haplotype diversity was highest in Lake Mohave (h = 0.92) and lowest in the upper Colorado River (h = 0.00). Mid-basin samples had h values of 0.73 to 0.82.
- Haplotypes per individual (corrected for sample size) were 0.67 at Lake Mohave and 0.04 in the upper Colorado River.

**2. Population structure**
- Monte Carlo tests showed significant haplotype frequency heterogeneity among populations, both with all samples (χ² = 239.5 for 4+6 bp data) and without the upper Colorado River sample.
- Only about 20 to 25% of variation lay among populations (θ = 0.22 and 0.24). Jackknife tests did not detect significant differentiation.
- Nucleotide divergence was small (total 0.0047; among-population 0.00097, about 2%).
- Phylogenetic and phenetic analyses did not partition haplotypes geographically. The most divergent haplotypes differed by only 0.7%.

**3. Hybridization**
- Only 1 of 119 morphologically identified razorbacks from Lakes Mohave, Mead, and Powell and the Green/Yampa rivers carried flannelmouth mtDNA.
- Introgression was higher in the Little Colorado River (5 of 11 individuals, all morphologically intermediate) and in upper Colorado River ponds (2 of 29).
- Flannelmouth suckers in the upper Colorado River showed considerable introgression with introduced [[White Sucker]] (*C. commersoni*).

**4. Interpretation**
- The authors reject a "sink" model in which Lake Mohave accumulated upstream variation. They favor a recolonization model: the lower basin served as a source, and upstream populations are younger and carry subsets of its haplotypes.
- High diversity at Lake Mohave implies descent from a large number of females in a formerly panmictic basin population.

**5. Hatchery year classes (DNFH)**
- Source sample: 25 Lake Mohave individuals, h = 0.91 (95% CI 0.80–0.98).
- The 1987 and 1989 year classes retained diversity comparable to the source (h = 0.89 and 0.91; 2 and 4 unique haplotypes, respectively).
- The 1990 year class was significantly less diverse (h = 0.71; 0.40 haplotypes per individual), with 17 females and only 11.1% survival to swim-up.
- Simulations showed that h is insensitive to broodstock size above roughly 15 to 20 females, so it misrepresents the true loss of variation.
- Likely causes include unequal parental contributions, pooling of fertilized ova, and possible inbreeding within a broodstock that was increasingly composed of progeny of a few early females.

**6. Management recommendations**
- Three options were compared: (1) large-scale hatchery culture, which yields the most fish but the least variation; (2) predator-free cutoff bays used as semi-natural spawning sites, which yield intermediate numbers and variation (five pilot bays used 145 females from 1984 to 1992); and (3) collection of wild-spawned larvae from Lake Mohave, which captures variation from the many adults that reproduce each year. Two collectors can harvest hundreds to thousands of larvae in a few favorable nights.
- The authors strongly recommend option 3 and that it begin immediately, while keeping the hatchery and bay programs as backups.
- They recommend replacing the inbred DNFH broodstock with wild-caught fish, not releasing current hatchery progeny into the wild, and rearing larvae as near Lake Mohave as possible.
- They do not advocate maximizing mtDNA variability as a selection goal.
