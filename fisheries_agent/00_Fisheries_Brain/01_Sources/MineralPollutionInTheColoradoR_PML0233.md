# Mineral Pollution in the Colorado River Basin

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-salinity

## Executive Summary

This 1973 paper, by William C. Blackman, Jr., Jim V. Rouse, George R. Schillinger and William H. Shafer, Jr., summarizes the methodology and findings of the [[Colorado River Basin Water Quality Control Project]], a federal-state effort begun after a 1960 interstate conference on pollution of the Colorado River. The project examined mineral quality (salinity), measured as total dissolved solids (TDS), through statistical analysis of historical records and field studies in 1964–66. It distinguishes two mechanisms of salinity increase, salt loading (adding salts from discharges and natural sources) and salt concentrating (losing water through consumptive use), and quantifies the major sources. The document does not address fish or other aquatic organisms; its focus is water chemistry and salt budgets.

## Background & Description

The project was established after recommendations from the first session of a joint federal-state conference, held in January 1960 under Section 8 of the [[Federal Water Pollution Control Act]]. The conference was called by the governors of Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico and Utah. The project had three objectives:

1. Determine the location, magnitude and causes of interstate pollution.
2. Determine and evaluate damages to water users caused by pollution.
3. Develop and recommend control measures.

Detailed salinity studies began in 1963. Findings were reported in *The Mineral Quality Problem in the Colorado River Basin*, with methods in three appendixes, and the material was entered into the record of the 7th Session of the Colorado Conference at Las Vegas, Nevada, on 14 February 1972. Salinity is described as a basinwide problem that has become critically important to users of lower Colorado River water.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Salt loading** sources include:
- **Municipal and industrial wastes**, where wastewater from groundwater-supplied communities always adds salt.
- **Mining**, through seepage from tailings and waste ponds and mine discharge.
- **Oil and gas operations**, where brines and brackish produced water are discharged to streams. The authors state that existing water quality regulations are inadequate for this.
- **Irrigation return flows**, where salts are dissolved from soils during prolonged contact with water.
- **Natural sources**, including [[Saline Mineral Springs]] (point sources) and diffuse runoff, groundwater and bank storage (diffuse sources). Diffuse sources are generally of much greater magnitude than point sources in the basin.

**Salt-concentrating effects** arise from consumptive water use, which removes water without removing salt. Key examples are thermal power plant evaporation, evaporation from large impoundments such as [[Lake Mead]] and [[Lake Powell]], irrigation consumption, and out-of-basin diversions.

**Geology** strongly controls quality. Crystalline upland rocks yield low-TDS runoff, while lowland formations such as the [[Paradox Formation]], [[Mancos Shale]] and saline Tertiary lakebeds yield highly mineralized water.

## Key Findings

**Time trends (1940s–1963 records, 27 stations, 353 station-years):**
- During base-flow months (August–March), significant changes were detected at 11 upstream locations. Increases occurred at 4 stations, decreases at 4, and both at 2 (the study's summary of changes).
- During runoff months (May–June), five upstream stations showed significant increases and none showed decreases.
- All changes upstream of Hoover Dam were associated with streamflow changes. Downstream of Hoover Dam, all stations showed significant increases, linked to the mid-1950s drought, irrigation drainage, dam closures and increased consumptive use.

**Source quantification:**
- In the upper basin, mean flow was 19,263 cfs and the salt load entering [[Lake Powell]] averaged 26,160 tons/day (June 1965–May 1966). Runoff contributed 52% of that load.
- Irrigation contributed 37% of upper basin salt load. Point sources contributed 10%, and municipal and industrial effluents about 1%.
- Evaporation from Lakes Mead and Powell increased TDS by roughly 63 and 37 mg/l in water year 1970.
- Water losses from Lakes Mead and Powell totaled approximately 1,250,000 acre-ft in water year 1970.
- In the lower basin, consumptive use greatly exceeded inflow. Salt sources added an estimated 5,484 tons/day, and about three-fourths of the salt load came from the upper basin.
- The Blue Springs near the Little Colorado River mouth contributed about 1,500 tons/day, the largest single point source.
- Out-of-basin diversions were planned to increase, with further exports expected to account for about half of the Colorado River's average flow.

**Subbasin findings:**
- The Dolores River system had a mean salt load of 456 tons/day at Bedrock, with about 688 tons/day added by solution in [[Paradox Valley]].
- Paradise Hot Springs on the West Fork of the Dolores had TDS of 5,500 mg/l and contributed 1.2 tons/day.
- Irrigation salt yields in the Wellton-Mohawk and Yuma areas and in the [[Palo Verde Irrigation District]] were summarized, with PVID averaging 2.1 tons/yr/acre.

**Conclusions:** Changes in mineral quality upstream of Hoover Dam are tied to streamflow patterns, while downstream changes reflect both flow and irrigation drainage. Headwater streams are generally calcium bicarbonate waters, while downstream reaches become sodium calcium sulfate or sodium chloride types where saline formations and drainage enter. Existing regulations were judged inadequate for oil-field discharges, and the authors recommend that out-of-basin exports be offset by good-quality augmentation to prevent further degradation.
