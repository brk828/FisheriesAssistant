# Sources of Springs Supplying Base Flow to the Verde River Headwaters, Yavapai County, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This USGS Open-File Report (99-0378, 2000) by Laurie Wirt and H.W. Hjalmarson identifies the aquifers and flow paths that supply the perennial base flow of the uppermost [[Verde River]] in Yavapai County, Arizona. Using multiple independent lines of evidence (geology, groundwater levels, precipitation and streamflow records, downstream base-flow gains, water-budget analysis, and stable isotopes), the authors conclude that interconnected aquifers in [[Big Chino Valley]] are the primary source of [[Big Chino Springs]], which supply at least 80 percent of the upper Verde River's base flow. The reach is a habitat for native fish, including the threatened [[Spikedace Minnow]] and the extirpated [[Loach Minnow]], and is part of a designated Wild and Scenic River corridor.

## Background & Description

- **Study reach:** About 24 miles of perennial river from the mouth of [[Granite Creek]] (river mile 2.0) to Perkinsville (river mile 26). Virtually all base-flow gains upstream of Perkinsville occur between river miles 2.1 and 4.0.
- **Base flow:** The mean base flow from 1963 to present is 24.9 ft³/s at the USGS gage near Paulden (09503700, river mile 10), with mean daily values ranging from 15 to 33 ft³/s.
- **Regulatory context:** The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (1999) proposed critical habitat designation for the spikedace minnow and loach minnow below Sullivan Dam. Congress designated the Verde River from Camp Verde to Sycamore Creek a Wild and Scenic River in 1984.
- **Human context:** [[Yavapai County]] is the fastest-growing non-metropolitan county in Arizona (3.4 percent annual growth, about four times the national average). Population rose from 37,000 (1970) to 140,000 (1996) and is projected to reach 313,000 by 2020. Much of this growth is in [[Little Chino Valley]], where the City of Prescott obtains most of its water.
- **Methods used:** Geologic information, groundwater levels, precipitation and streamflow records, downstream base-flow measurements, water-budget components, and stable-isotope geochemistry (δ18O, δ2H, δ13C).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Geologic controls.** The upper Verde headwaters area is structurally complex. Key features include:

- A thick clay (playa) unit in the center of Big Chino Valley that impedes groundwater movement from the upper to lower basin.
- Basalt flows (Late Tertiary to about 5 million years ago) that filled drainages and formed an obstacle near [[Sullivan Lake]]. Ground water exits the basin through fractures and solution cavities in the [[Martin Limestone]].
- The [[Big Chino Fault]], whose coarse, limestone-rich alluvial fan deposits form a permeable conduit.
- Precambrian granodiorite and Laramide monoclines east of Paulden, which act as barriers to downgradient flow toward Hell Canyon.
- Solution features and springs at river miles 2.3 to 4.0 (e.g., an artesian pond about 3.5 ft above river level at river mile 2.5).

**Water levels and pumping.**

- Little Chino Valley groundwater levels have declined by more than 75 ft since 1940 in the north end of the basin. At least 20 wells declined 1 to 11 ft per year.
- Del Rio Springs averaged 1,460 ± 60 acre-ft per year (1997–1999), about 50 percent of the 1940–1945 mean of roughly 3,000 acre-ft per year.
- Little Chino artesian pumping in 1993 was 12,800 acre-ft.
- Big Chino irrigation pumping decreased by about 350 acre-ft/yr over three decades, coinciding with rising water levels in the southern aquifer.

**Stable isotopes.** Values are presented as δ18O and δ2H (per mil, relative to standard).

- Big Chino Springs (δ18O ≈ −10.2) and Lower Granite Spring (δ18O ≈ −9.7) differ, and Verde base flow shifts between them downstream of river mile 2.3.
- Little Chino wells and Del Rio Springs (δ18O ≈ −9.9) closely match Lower Granite Spring.
- Sullivan Lake and Stillman Lake water is evaporatively enriched and is not a significant base-flow source.
- Carbon-13 enrichment in Big Chino Springs and Verde base flow indicates extensive travel through limestone. Little Chino groundwater is comparatively depleted in δ13C (non-carbonate).

## Key Findings

1. **Primary source:** Interconnected aquifers in Big Chino Valley supply at least 80 percent of upper Verde base flow via Big Chino Springs, which discharge through the Martin Limestone between river miles 2.3 and 4.0.
2. **Lower Granite Spring and Del Rio Springs:** Groundwater discharge at these springs is most likely derived from the Little Chino Valley aquifer, based on matching isotopic signatures and water-level contours. Little Chino may contribute a minor fraction of Verde base flow via lower Granite Creek. Little Chino outflow contributes less than 20 percent of base flow.
3. **Water-budget relations:** Base flow in the Verde River was approximately 110 acre-ft/yr higher following a decrease of 350 acre-ft/yr in Big Chino pumping. A 1-ft decline in the index well correlates with a 1.3 ft³/s decrease in base flow. Only about 31 percent of the pumping change is reflected in base-flow gains; storage increase and reduced Little Chino inflow may account for the remainder.
4. **Pumping impacts:** The 1964 filling of recreation lakes (Holiday Lake Estates) reduced Verde base flow by about 5 ft³/s, to a mean daily low of 15 ft³/s (May 13–23, 1964). Flow recovered to 22–23 ft³/s after pumping ceased.
5. **Storage estimates:** Groundwater in storage (to 1,000 ft depth) is estimated at 6 million (Little Chino), 11 million (Big Chino), and 3 million (Williamson Valley) acre-ft (Hjalmarson, 1967). The authors estimate 10 to 20 million acre-ft across the three basins.
6. **Non-contributing regions:** Ash Fork, Big Black Mesa, and Williamson Valley/Walnut Creek were judged unlikely to supply significant base flow directly, though their isotopic signatures are similar to Big Chino limestone wells. The Glidden well water level (~4,218 ft) lies below the Big Chino Springs outlet altitude (4,220–4,240 ft), arguing against Big Black Mesa contribution.
7. **Climate:** No statistically significant trend in precipitation or recharge was detected. Increased base flow is therefore attributed to anthropogenic causes, mainly reduced irrigation pumping.
8. **Outlook:** Future groundwater withdrawals near the basin outlet would likely reduce spring discharge. Additional modeling and monitoring are recommended, and the authors state that hydrologic data are sufficient to qualitatively but not quantitatively assess pumping effects.
