# Colorado River: Water Law, Allocation and Future Management

#hydrology #water-law #colorado-river

## Executive Summary

This *Waterline* issue examines the [[Colorado River]], the dominant river system of the southwestern United States and a key water source for seven states and northwestern Mexico. The authors (from Arizona, California, New Mexico, Utah and Nevada) cover the legal history that governs water rights, the interstate and international compacts that apportion the river, the role of federal reclamation projects, and the outlook for future management. The central argument is that demand for Colorado River water is approaching or exceeding supply, shifting policy from developing new water toward managing, reallocating and conserving existing supplies.

## Background & Description

- **River system:** The Colorado begins a roughly 1,400-mile run to the sea from two main headwaters: the [[Green River]], rising in southeastern Wyoming, and the Colorado River, rising in the Rocky Mountains of Colorado. The two join in southeastern Utah, above [[Lake Powell]].
- **Users:** More than 20 million people depend on the river for drinking water, irrigation, industry and recreation.
- **Legal foundations:** The authors describe the "Law of the River" as a combination of federal legislation, state legislation, the [[Colorado River Compact]], an interstate compact, a treaty, contracts, a U.S. Supreme Court decree and administrative decisions.
- **Historical doctrines:**
  - The [[Riparian Rights Doctrine]] from England and the eastern United States was ill-suited to the arid West, where water had to be diverted to distant lands.
  - Because most western land was federally owned until about 1862, no private riparian owners existed. Settlers therefore developed the [[Prior Appropriation Doctrine]] ("first in time, first in right"), with beneficial use as the measure of a right.
  - Conflicts arose when riparian claims derived from federal land grants, and courts increasingly rejected the riparian doctrine for the arid West.
- **Federal role:** The authors stress that the federal government holds dual interests: sovereign powers under the U.S. Constitution, which Congress cannot delegate to the states (such as the power to regulate interstate commerce), and proprietary ownership of public lands acquired through the Louisiana Purchase, the Oregon Compromise, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo and the Gadsden Purchase.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The authors note that the river is one of the most heavily engineered in the world, so changes at one point reverberate through the whole system. New upper-basin development or water exports may increase downstream salinity and threaten fish and wildlife habitat.
- Western streams have irregular flow, with wet and dry cycles and damaging spring floods followed by dry riverbeds in August. Storage is therefore indispensable to a dependable supply.
- Early development largely ignored ecological effects, including those on Mexican users downstream. The authors argue that instream uses supporting fish, recreation and aesthetic values are now recognized as legitimate beneficial uses.
- Forest and watershed management, cloud seeding and salinity control are described as ways to alter the river's yield. Salinity control links the United States and Mexico through the [[Colorado River Basin Salinity Control Project]].
- Upper-basin energy development (oil shale and related resources) is expected to add a large potential water demand.

## Key Findings

### Major Compacts and Apportionments

- **[[Colorado River Compact]] (1922):** Signed November 24, 1922, and ratified by six of seven states; Arizona held out until 1944, mainly over the inclusion of the Gila River. The compact divides the system between the Upper Basin (Colorado, New Mexico, Utah, Wyoming and part of Arizona) and the Lower Basin (Arizona, California, Nevada, New Mexico and Utah).
  - Each basin received 7.5 million acre-feet per year (maf/yr) of consumptive use, with the Lower Basin given the right to increase use by 1 maf/yr.
  - Mexico's delivery is to come from surplus waters beyond the 16.0 maf/yr total; any deficiency is to be borne equally by the two basins.
  - The Upper Basin may not deplete flow at [[Lee Ferry]] below 75 maf over any ten consecutive years.
- **[[Boulder Canyon Project Act]] (1928/1929):** Approved the compact and authorized [[Hoover Dam]], power plants and the [[All-American Canal]]. The act required California to limit its use to 4.4 maf/yr plus one-half of any surplus, enacted through the California Limitation Act in March 1929. Construction of Hoover Dam began in 1931.
- **[[U.S.-Mexico Water Treaty]] (1944):** Divided the Colorado, Rio Grande and Tijuana rivers and guaranteed Mexico 1.5 maf/yr from the Colorado. [[Davis Dam]] was completed in 1950 to re-regulate releases from Hoover Dam.
- **[[Upper Colorado River Basin Compact]] (1948):** Apportioned Upper Basin water as 50,000 acre-feet per year to Arizona, with the remainder divided: Colorado 51.75%, New Mexico 11.25%, Utah 23%, Wyoming 14%.
- **[[Colorado River Storage Project Act]] (1956):** Authorized major Upper Basin storage units, including Glen Canyon Dam, Flaming Gorge Dam, the Curecanti Unit (Blue Mesa, Morrow Point and Crystal dams), and Navajo Dam, with about 33.6 maf of storage capacity. Participating projects are repaid partly from hydroelectric revenues.
- **[[Arizona v. California]] (March 1964 decree):** Apportioned Lower Basin water as 2.8 maf/yr for Arizona, 4.4 maf/yr for California and 0.3 maf/yr for Nevada when at least 7.5 maf is available. Surplus beyond 7.5 maf is divided 50% to California and 50% to Arizona, with Nevada receiving a 4% share.
- **[[Colorado River Basin Project Act]] (1968):** Authorized the [[Central Arizona Project]] and five Upper Basin projects. The United States assumed responsibility for the Mexican Treaty obligation when the river is augmented by 2.5 maf/yr. The 1970 operating criteria aim to maintain a minimum of 8.25 maf/yr at Lee Ferry.

### Supply, Demand and Storage

- **Reservoir capacity:** The system holds over 60.3 maf of usable storage, including 4.1 maf of flood-control space, leaving 56.2 maf available for conservation each January.
- **Yield:** Estimates of the river's yield vary (roughly 13 to 15 maf/yr at Lee Ferry, according to different analyses), reflecting different flow records, including tree-ring reconstructions that extend about 450 years. One author concludes that the long-term dependable yield at Lee Ferry is about 14 maf/yr.
- **Projected balance (in thousands of acre-feet):**

| Scenario | Total Requirements | Dependable Yield | Surplus (+) / Deficiency (-) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Present | 12,300 | 14,000 | +1,700 |
| 1990 | 13,300 | 14,000 | +700 |
| 2000 | 14,200 | 14,000 | -200 |

- Water requirements are projected to equal the dependable yield in the early 1990s and exceed it thereafter. Shortages would first fall on Lower Basin users, with the Central Arizona Project subject to limits to assure California's 4.4 maf/yr.
- **Current use:** California used about 4.8 maf/yr (down from a peak of 5.3 maf/yr). Arizona's depletions were about 1.2 maf/yr, expected to reach 2.8 maf/yr. Upper Basin depletions were about 4 maf/yr, projected at 4.2 maf/yr by 1990 and about 4.7 maf/yr by 2000.

### Federal Reclamation

- The [[Reclamation Act of 1902]] created what became the [[Bureau of Reclamation]]. [[Roosevelt Dam]] on the Salt River, built 1905–1911, was the agency's first large storage facility, and it remains the highest rock masonry dam in the world.
- Hoover Dam, completed in 1935, supplies hydroelectric power to over 12 million people and irrigates about 1.5 million acres. It draws roughly 700,000 visitors a year, and its 50th anniversary was celebrated in 1985.
- The All-American Canal irrigates 600,000 acres in the Imperial and Coachella valleys.
- The Central Arizona Project, authorized in 1968, is designed to deliver 1.2 maf annually to Phoenix and Tucson, with deliveries to Phoenix beginning in December 1985.
- The planned Yuma Desalting Plant, a reverse osmosis facility designed to treat irrigation return flows, is expected to be completed in the late 1980s.

### Emerging Trends and Challenges

- **Water markets:** Municipal water sells for $200 or more per acre-foot, compared with about $15 for agricultural water, pushing transfers toward higher-value uses. The San Diego County Water Authority sought rights to upwards of 300,000 acre-feet per year from a proposed Yampa River reservoir in Colorado.
- **Federal courts and state rights:** In the [[Sporhase Decision]], the U.S. Supreme Court held that water is an article of commerce, so state laws prohibiting exports can violate the commerce clause. In contrast, [[Colorado v. New Mexico]] (1982) reinstated the importance of state lines.
- **Conservation:** Beneficial use standards are tightening, and conservation is being imposed as a prerequisite for further development. The authors expect higher prices to retire marginal users.
- **Federal-state relations:** New federal starts are becoming rare, and cost-sharing by local beneficiaries is increasingly required.
- **Indian water claims:** Reserved tribal water rights, established under the [[Winters Doctrine]], far exceed current use. Claims such as the Navajo Nation's could seriously affect existing users. Leasing reserved water to non-Indian users is being explored, but off-reservation transfers may be politically restricted.
- **U.S.-Mexico relations:** Mexicali Valley demand is growing, and the authors foresee possible pressure to reopen negotiations over the division and quality of flows.

### Augmentation Options

- **Importation:** Diverting water from the Columbia or Missouri basins is hampered by federal restrictions through 1988, high costs, energy needs and environmental and political obstacles.
- **Desalination of sea water:** Judged infeasible because of construction and energy costs.
- **Vegetation management:** A 1979 Forest Service report estimated that managing about 16% of the basin could add up to 6 maf/yr, but a realistic goal is about 1 maf/yr, available only 20 to 30 years after a program starts.
- **[[Cloud Seeding]]:** The most promising option. The eight-year Colorado River Augmentation Demonstration Program, estimated at $68 million (first year $12.5 million), aims to show that seeding winter storm clouds over six major watersheds can add about 1.6 maf/yr. Results would not be confirmed until about 1993, and cost-sharing between the states and the federal government is unresolved.

### Policy Conclusions

- The authors describe a shift from "development of new water" to "better management of water already developed," emphasizing reuse, reallocation and conservation.
- Water management must be system-wide, because conservation upstream matters to lower-basin cities and to Mexico.
- Coalitions must broaden beyond past beneficiaries, and costs must be fairly distributed.
- Leaders will need training, tolerance and decisiveness. One author argues that water is finite but renewable, and that the central challenge of the coming decade is designing a legal system that both protects and permits use.
