# Critical Habitat Designation for Four Colorado River Endangered Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Final Rule, published by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] in the *Federal Register* on March 21, 1994 (59 FR 13374), designates critical habitat for four endangered fishes of the [[Colorado River Basin]]: the [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Humpback Chub]], and [[Bonytail Chub]]. The designation followed a 1993 court order under the [[Endangered Species Act]] (ESA) requiring the Service to act by March 15, 1994. The rule covers river reaches across seven states, totaling roughly 1,700 river miles for the razorback sucker alone, and incorporates an economic analysis and an exclusion process that allowed areas to be removed from designation based on economic or other relevant impacts.

## Background & Description

- **Native range and status:** The four fishes evolved in a basin altered by dams, water diversions, and nonnative species. The document states that no completely unaltered habitat remains in the Basin for these species.
- **Razorback Sucker:** Once abundant and widely distributed. The largest remaining stock is in [[Lake Mohave]] (estimated 20,000 individuals). Adults use varied habitats, including eddies, backwaters, flooded bottomlands, and sandbars, and spawn over gravel bars in spring as water levels rise.
- **Colorado Squawfish:** The only living representative of the genus *Ptychocheilus* in the Basin, with fossil ancestry from roughly 5 million years ago. Native populations are restricted to the upper Basin (Wyoming, Colorado, Utah, New Mexico) and have been extirpated from the Lower Basin. Spawning is linked to declining flows in June, July, or August at water temperatures of 22 to 25 °C.
- **Humpback Chub:** Life history and habitat use in the Grand Canyon were studied through the Grand Canyon Environmental Studies (GCES) program. The species was among the initial species listed under the ESA in 1967.
- **Bonytail Chub:** Included among the four species; the document notes that information on its distribution and habitat was limited at the time of earlier listing.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat degradation:** Altered flow regimes, reduced spring peak flows, warmer river temperatures, and loss of inundated floodplains have all contributed to decline. Nonnative fishes thrive in altered conditions and compete with or prey on native species.
- **Larval ecology:** Razorback sucker larvae prefer shallow, quiet zones after hatching and drift downstream in lab studies. Colorado squawfish larvae drift for about 6 days over roughly 160 km to lower-gradient nursery areas that are nutrient-rich.
- **Floodplains:** The 100-year floodplain was used as a surrogate to delineate habitat. Inundated floodplains, backwaters, and side channels provide nutrients, food, and spawning/rearing habitat. The document states that developed floodplain land is unlikely to provide constituent elements.
- **Reservoirs:** Razorback suckers and bonytail chubs use reservoirs such as [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Powell]]. Reservoir habitat is protected regardless of pool elevation.
- **Flow management:** The [[San Juan River Recovery Implementation Program]] (SJRIP) seeks to use [[Navajo Dam]] releases to restore habitat-forming flows and water temperatures for the Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker.

## Key Findings

- **Designated area:** Critical habitat covers river reaches in Arizona, California, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Utah, and Wyoming, including the Colorado, Green, Yampa, White, Gunnison, Dolores, San Juan, Gila, Salt, and Verde Rivers. The razorback sucker designation alone totals about 1,700 river miles (approx. 2,700 km) and covers about 49 percent of the historical habitat for the species.
- **Land ownership:** Designated reaches cross public and private lands. Land ownership tables list shoreline and river mileage by owner.
- **Legal basis:** The Service had no alternative but to designate critical habitat after the court found it had violated the ESA by failing to designate for the razorback sucker at listing. The Service decided to designate for all four species because they coexist and share habitat.
- **Economic analysis:** Conducted by [[Brookshire et al. (1994)]] using a [[Computable General Equilibrium Model]] (CGE) and regional input-output models. Analysis covered 1995 through 2020 and found no significant national economic impacts.
- **Exclusion process:** Areas can be excluded if economic or other benefits of exclusion outweigh the benefits of inclusion, provided exclusion does not result in extinction of a species. The Service determined that no exclusions were made.
- **Recovery plans:** Recovery plans exist for the Colorado Squawfish and Razorback Sucker (approved 1979, revised 1984 and 1991), the Humpback Chub (1979 and revised 1990), and the Bonytail Chub (1990).
- **Public comment:** The Service responded to 60+ issues raised in public comments. Key responses covered flood plain designation, takings implications (none found under Executive Order 12630), tribal trust responsibilities (designation applies to tribal lands where federal actions are involved), and water rights (not affected).
- **Regulatory effect:** Designation does not create a new permit or requirement for private activities. Section 7 consultation applies only to federal actions. Federal activities that may affect critical habitat must avoid destruction or adverse modification.
- **Effective date:** The rule took effect April 20, 1994, with questions directed to the Utah Field Office.
