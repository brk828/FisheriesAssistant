# Clearcutting and Burning Slash Alter Quality of Stream Water in Northern Idaho

#freshwater-ecology #watershed-hydrology #water-quality

## Executive Summary

This USDA Forest Service research paper (INT-168, June 1975) by Gordon G. Snyder, Harold F. Haupt, and George H. Belt, Jr. evaluates how [[Clearcutting]] and [[Slash Burning]] change stream water chemistry in the [[Cedar-Hemlock-Grand Fir Ecosystem]] of northern Idaho. Three clearcut-burned units of different size, soil, and drainage pattern were monitored on the [[Priest River Experimental Forest]] for 21 months (fall 1970 to summer 1972). Stations were placed upstream (control), on-site, and downstream (off-site) of each unit. Most units retained [[Buffer Strips]] of unlogged timber along channels.

The study found that on-site stream water changed clearly: pH, electrical conductivity, turbidity, filterable solids, [[Bicarbonate]], [[Sulfate]], potassium, calcium, and magnesium all increased. Off-site changes were smaller and depended on local drainage pattern. Chloride did not change with treatment. The paper contains no fish or other aquatic fauna data; its ecological focus is on water chemistry and nutrient export.

## Background & Description

**Motivation.** Congressional hearings in 1971 raised two concerns about public land management: long-term nutrient depletion after harvest and resulting changes in stream and lake water quality. Earlier studies, such as those at [[Hubbard Brook Experimental Forest]], offered few generalizable baselines, so the authors sought data for a new ecosystem type.

**Study area.** The [[Priest River Experimental Forest]] covers 2,577 ha in the Idaho Panhandle National Forests. Elevations range from 671 to 1,798 m, and 85 percent of the area is mountainous. The climate is maritime, with 89 cm of precipitation (mostly snow) from October to June. Soils include silt loams over Precambrian metamorphic rock, with a volcanic ash cap on mountain slopes.

**Study units.**

- [[Benton Creek]]: 44 ha clearcut in 1969 and broadcast burned in 1970. The clearcut boundary lies about 61 m from the creek. A south-facing section drains directly to the on-site station without a buffer strip, but only during rapid snowmelt or heavy rain.
- [[Ida Creek]]: about 2.6 ha at the headwaters on level ground (745 m). Logging was completed by August 1970 and slash was burned in September 1970. A 61 m buffer lies between the clearcut and the creek. A spring crosses the clearcut without a buffer, and the flow goes subsurface before reemerging to form the main channel.
- [[Canyon Creek]]: about 26 ha (23 ha clearcut, 3 ha partial cut), clearcut and burned in fall 1970. A buffer of about 30 m separates the unit from the creek. A spring crosses the clearcut in a defined channel without a buffer.

**Objectives.** The study aimed to (1) evaluate changes in physical and chemical stream water quality on and off the units, (2) characterize nutrient losses under different drainage patterns, and (3) test whether buffer strips control sediment and nutrient losses.

**Sampling and analysis.** Samples were collected weekly and after storms in polyethylene bottles, frozen unpreserved, and analyzed at the University of Idaho. pH and bicarbonate were tested first because they change quickly after thawing. Analyses covered pH, electrical conductivity, turbidity, filterable solids (0.45-micron filtration), chloride, nitrate (ion-electrode, detection limit about 0.6 mg/L), sulfate (turbidimetric), and sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium (atomic absorption). Dissolved oxygen was saturated in all samples. Tannins and lignins were below detection and were dropped from routine analysis.

**Statistics.** Means, variances, and standard errors were computed for each station. Changes between stations were tested with paired t tests (α = 0.05). Because the upstream and downstream stations were close together, the authors assumed equal pre-treatment quality there, so any significant downstream increase was attributed to the clearcut-burned area.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Nutrient export background.** Natural nutrient losses from forests were considered low. Earlier work at Hubbard Brook found clearcutting with herbicide raised streamflow ion export 2 to 53 times in the first year, mainly from disruption of the nitrogen cycle. Roadbuilding, clearcutting, and burning in other regions raised losses temporarily but generally without degrading water quality. This study was the first to examine stream chemistry in the [[Cedar-Hemlock-Grand Fir Ecosystem]].

**Precipitation inputs.** Incoming particulate matter was high. Filterable solids in precipitation exceeded natural stream losses from undisturbed land by two to eight times. Precipitation pH (6.8) became alkaline on contact with watershed soils. Only trace chloride was detected in precipitation.

**Algal nutrients.** The paper notes that [[Eutrophication]] depends on available nutrients for algae. Nutrient concentrations at the upstream stations already exceeded minimum algal requirements (Greeson 1969). The impact of dissolved nutrients on downstream aquatic ecology was described as generally unknown.

**Human health.** Concentration increases did not exceed U.S. Public Health Service (1962) drinking water standards.

## Key Findings

**1. Precipitation and particulate input**
- Nitrate and potassium in precipitation matched upstream stream concentrations. All other nutrients were lower, and only trace chloride was found.

**2. On-site changes (clearcut-burned stations versus upstream controls)**
- Significant increases were found in pH, electrical conductivity, turbidity, suspended solids, bicarbonate, sulfate, potassium, calcium, and magnesium. Chloride and sodium showed no treatment effect.
- Benton ON electrical conductivity averaged three times the Benton ABOVE value, but its variance increased 40 times.
- Benton ON turbidity was almost nine times the Benton ABOVE value. Turbidity at Ida Creek returned to prior levels within 6 months.
- Filterable solids at on-site stations increased 14 times (Benton), 4 times (Ida), and 6 times (Canyon) relative to upstream stations.
- Mean bicarbonate at Benton ON was about 51 mg/L, compared with about 13 mg/L upstream, an increase of almost fourfold. Variance rose 68 times at Benton ON and 116 times at Canyon ON.
- Nitrate at Benton ON averaged 8 mg/L, about eight times the upstream value. Other sites showed nitrate below 1 mg/L.
- Potassium rose significantly at all three on-site stations, but at Ida Creek it fell back to normal within about 1 year.
- Sodium changed little, with a significant change only at Canyon Creek.
- Sulfate increased at on-site stations, though the Ida Creek increase was small.

**3. Off-site changes (downstream versus upstream)**
- Parameters that increased most consistently were bicarbonate, sulfate, calcium, magnesium, and electrical conductivity.
- At Benton and Canyon Creeks, off-site increases were smaller than on-site increases because dilution by the main channel offset the enriched side tributary.
- At [[Ida Creek]], the off-site station showed the highest concentrations (bicarbonate about 22 mg/L, roughly twice the upstream value). Because the main channel's flow was small, the process was concentration rather than dilution. Lateral leachate from enriched surface layers was the likely source.

**4. Seasonal patterns**
- Bicarbonate, sulfate, magnesium, and electrical conductivity were lowest during high spring flows and highest at low flow, consistent with dilution.
- Calcium rose during snowmelt and high flow, so calcium losses from the clearcut-burned area occurred mainly during high runoff.
- Sodium was the only off-site ion that tracked seasonal runoff.

**5. Comparison with other studies**
- Cation increases were lower than those following wildfire in central Washington ([[Tiedemann]] 1974) except for sodium, and similar to logging-and-burning results in western Oregon ([[Fredriksen]] 1971).
- Unlike the Hubbard Brook herbicide study, sulfate and bicarbonate increased at all on-site stations, and bicarbonate was the largest anion increase.

**6. Buffer strip effectiveness**
- Filterable solids rose significantly at on-site stations but not at off-site stations, suggesting [[Buffer Strips]] and the forest floor trap sediment effectively.
- Buffer strips did not clearly filter dissolved nutrients. Their main benefit appears to be a physical barrier against direct contact with nutrient sources.
- The authors recommended continued buffer use for stream temperature moderation and bank protection, and advised caution in cutting near springs, seeps, and watercourses. They advised disposing of slash by means other than burning.

**7. Conclusions**
- Clearcutting and burning produced on-site stream water changes. Off-site changes were less intense in two of three units, and the third (Ida Creek) differed because of its drainage pattern.
- Increases stayed within drinking-water standards and nutrient concentrations already exceeded algal minimums upstream.
