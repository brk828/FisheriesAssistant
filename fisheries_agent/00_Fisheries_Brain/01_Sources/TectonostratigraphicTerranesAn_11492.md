# Tectonostratigraphic Terranes and Tectonic Evolution of Mexico

> Note on scope: this document is a geology monograph (GSA Special Paper 278, 1993), not a fish or aquatic-ecology study. No fish species, families, or aquatic habitats are treated. ## Executive Summary

Special Paper 278 by [[Richard L. Sedlock]], [[Fernando Ortega-Gutiérrez]] and [[Robert C. Speed]] is a two-part synthesis of the geology of [[Mexico]] and northern [[Central America]]. Part 1 divides the region into 17 tectonostratigraphic terranes, 16 of which are named after indigenous cultures (North America is the exception), and gives a morphotectonic and plate-tectonic framework, terrane descriptions, radiometric data tables, and boundary summaries. Part 2 offers a speculative kinematic model of the Late Precambrian to Cenozoic evolution of these terranes. The authors describe the reconstruction as one internally consistent, testable solution rather than a unique one.

## Background & Description

- **Authorship and publication:** Sedlock (San Jose State University), Ortega-Gutiérrez (Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México, Instituto de Geología) and Speed (Northwestern University). Published by the Geological Society of America in 1993.
- **Scope:** Mexico and northern Central America, with discussion extending to the southwestern United States, the Caribbean and northwestern South America.
- **Data base:** The terrane division draws on a larger body of published and unpublished geophysical and geologic data than earlier divisions, notably [[Campa-Uranga and Coney Terrane Division]] (1983, 1987) and the DNAG Continent-Ocean Transects.
- **Data limitations acknowledged by the authors:** Dense tropical vegetation hides bedrock in southeastern Mexico and Central America. Much of northern and central Mexico is covered by late Mesozoic marine strata (Gulf of Mexico sequence) and Cenozoic volcanics ([[Sierra Madre Occidental]] and [[Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt]]). Few pre-Cretaceous U-Pb or Ar-Ar dates appear in refereed journals, and much of the Spanish-language literature has limited circulation.
- **Terminology:** Terrane is defined after Howell and others (1985) as "a fault-bounded package of rocks of regional extent characterized by a geologic history which differs from that of neighboring terranes."

## Ecological & Environmental Context

This volume is a tectonic and geologic synthesis, so its "environmental context" concerns tectonic settings and paleoenvironments rather than ecosystems.

- **Morphotectonic provinces:** The [[Gulf of California]] sits in an extensional province that includes the eastern margin of Baja California and coastal Sonora, Sinaloa and Nayarit. East of the [[Sierra Madre Occidental]] lie the western and eastern Mexican [[Basin and Range Province]]. The [[Sierra Madre Oriental]] is a Laramide fold-and-thrust belt. The [[Mesa Central]] is an elevated plateau. The [[Sierra Madre del Sur]] contains basement as old as Precambrian. The [[Yucatan Platform]] is a low plateau of subhorizontal Mesozoic strata.
- **Active plate boundaries:** Mexico lies on the North American plate. Baja California is attached to the Pacific plate. The Caribbean boundary is a sinistral fault system (Motagua, Polochic, Jocotán-Chamelecón faults). Subduction of the Cocos plate beneath North America occurs at the Acapulco and Middle America trenches. The Rivera plate is kinematically distinct.
- **Heat flow and crust:** Background heat flow in the Gulf of California is about 200 mW/m²; the Guaymas basin reaches 6,250 mW/m² near hydrothermal vents. Crustal thickness of northern Mexico is about 40 km (Gomberg and others, 1988), with a lithospheric thickness of 70 to 80 km.
- **Economic setting:** Mexico ranks seventh in world crude oil reserves and third in production, and is the world's leading silver and bismuth producer.

## Key Findings

**Headline reconstruction points (Part 2).** The authors list eleven salient features of their model:

1. Grenville basement in eastern and southern Mexico is far-traveled relative to the southern end of the Grenville belt in North America.
2. The late Paleozoic Ouachitan suture did not extend into central Mexico.
3. The Permo-Triassic continental arc affected only the far eastern and far northwestern margins of Mexico; most of the country was oceanic terranes west and south of the Pangean arc.
4. Continental Mexico grew most markedly during the Late Triassic and Jurassic through episodic terrane accretion.
5. Mesozoic southward and westward growth shifted arc magmatism in the same direction.
6. Terranes translated along margin-parallel faults. Convergence and translation were sinistral from the Triassic(?) to the Early Cretaceous, and dextral in the mid-Cretaceous and Paleogene.
7. Jurassic Gulf of Mexico rifting was not kinematically tied to sinistral slip on the [[Mojave-Sonora Megashear]].
8. About 15° of northward displacement of Baja California in the Late Cretaceous and Paleogene requires an earlier southward episode.
9. The Cretaceous reconstruction is consistent with terranes in the western US and Canada having originated at Mexican latitudes.
10. The Caribbean plate, including the [[Chortis Terrane]], has moved 1,000 to 2,000 km eastward since about 45 Ma.
11. Basin and Range extension affects most of Mexico north of about 20°N, an area larger than the US province.

**Plate-motion values (Part 1).**
- Caribbean-North America relative motion near Guatemala: 12 ± 3 mm/yr, S75–80E (NUVEL-1 model).
- Pacific-North America relative motion: 50 ± 1 mm/yr at 23°N and 46.5 ± 1 mm/yr at 31°N.
- Cocos-North America convergence: about 55 mm/yr near Colima, 60 near Acapulco, 75 near the Chiapas-Guatemala border.
- Rivera plate motion increases from about 6 mm/yr at the Tamayo transform to 20–30 mm/yr at the Acapulco Trench.

**Seismicity and historical events.** The 1976 Motagua fault earthquake had M_s 7.5. Large subduction-related events include 1985 Michoacán (8.1), 1907 and 1957 Acapulco (7.8, 7.5), 1978 Oaxaca (7.7), and 1932 Jalisco (8.1).

**Terrane count and naming.** The 17 terranes are Chatino, Chortis, Coahuiltecano, Cochimi, Cuicateco, Guachichil, Maya, Mixteco, Nahuatl, North America, Pericú, Seri, Tahué, Tarahumara, Tepehuano, Yuma Composite and Zapoteco. Each terrane description includes a geologic history, rock units, a tectonostratigraphic column, and a radiometric table.

**Terrane-specific highlights.**
- [[Chatino Terrane]]: the Xolapa Complex (Jurassic–mid-Cretaceous metamorphic ages); Tertiary plutons decrease in age from about 45 Ma (Acapulco) to about 12 Ma (Puerto Ángel).
- [[Chortis Terrane]]: Paleozoic–Precambrian basement; paleomagnetic data indicate more than 100° clockwise rotation in the Jurassic–Early Cretaceous and more than 100° counterclockwise rotation later.
- [[Cochimi Terrane]]: Mesozoic ophiolite, arc and blueschist units; the authors reinterpret the boundary faults as normal faults. Blueschist peak conditions are 5–8 kbar and 170–300°C, dated to about 115–105 Ma.
- [[Guachichil Terrane]]: Grenville gneiss (Novillo and Huizachal units) with Sm-Nd dates of about 900 Ma.
- [[Maya Terrane]]: Gondwanan-type basement; a Permo-Triassic arc; the El Tambor ophiolite subterrane accreted in the Maastrichtian.
- [[Nahuatl Terrane]]: Tierra Caliente Complex and Upper Mesozoic Assemblage; granitoid ages decrease west to east.
- [[Perici Terrane]]: Late Cretaceous granitoids; mid-Cretaceous deformation with left-oblique displacement.
- [[Seri Terrane]]: Proterozoic basement (1,750–1,710 Ma granitoids, about 1,450 Ma anorogenic granitoids) overlain by Proterozoic–Paleozoic shelf and basin sequences; Permo-Triassic thrusting of basinal rocks over shelf rocks.

**Key terrane boundaries.** The [[Mojave-Sonora Megashear]] (Late Jurassic sinistral slip), the Tosco-Abreojos fault zone (Miocene Pacific–North America boundary), the Polochic and Motagua faults, the La Paz fault, the Juárez suture, and the Oaxaca fault.

**Gaps and caveats.** The authors state that Part 2 is "a first attempt" and that many terrane boundaries, ages and kinematic histories will change with new data. Several paleomagnetic and geochronologic interpretations are described as conjectural in the text, for example the Coahuiltecano basement.
