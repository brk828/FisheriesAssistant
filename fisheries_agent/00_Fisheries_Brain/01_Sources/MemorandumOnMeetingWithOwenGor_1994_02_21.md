## Executive Summary

This document is not a primary research paper but an internal Arizona State University memorandum from W. L. Minckley to Duncan T. Patten regarding a meeting with Owen Gorman (USFWS) about ongoing [[Humpback Chub]] habitat research under GCES-II (Glen Canyon Environmental Studies) in the [[Grand Canyon]] and [[Little Colorado River]] (LCR). While primarily administrative/evaluative in nature, the memo contains embedded ichthyological data on humpback chub ontogenetic habitat shifts, tributary habitat comparisons, and methodological critique of habitat-use quantification techniques. Key management context is the [[Conservation Measure]] requirement to establish a "second breeding population" of humpback chubs, and the scientific urgency to study the large 1993 year class to fill data gaps for intermediate size classes (75–150 mm TL).

## Background & Description

No direct morphometric data on the species is presented; however, the memo references **size-based ontogenetic classes** used operationally as proxies for life-history stage in habitat-use studies:

- **Small fish**: unspecified lower bound to ~50–75 mm TL
- **Intermediate fish**: ~75–150 mm TL (identified as a critical **data gap** due to project timing, 1992 flooding, and sampling method delays)
- **Large fish**: >125–150 mm TL

These size thresholds correspond to distinct behavioral/ecological shifts (see below), leading Gorman to treat each stage functionally as an "ecological species" for analytical purposes.

Indices of reproductive condition recorded per individual fish (not true morphometric characters but condition indices):
- Tuberculation
- Body coloration
- Body distension in females (gravidity proxy)
- Milt expression in males

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Ontogenetic habitat shift pattern in [[Humpback Chub]]** (as described by Gorman, corroborated by Minckley's independent observations):

| Size Class | Diel Activity | Habitat Use |
|---|---|---|
| Small (<50–75 mm) | Diurnal | Shorelines/stream edges |
| Intermediate (75–150 mm) | Largely diurnal | Deeper water, often with adults |
| Large (>125–150+ mm) | Markedly nocturnal | Deep water, association with vertical cover |

- Habitat use "changes abruptly" across these size thresholds, supporting treatment of life stages as distinct ecological units in study design.
- Key habitat covariates measured: **current speed, substrate composition, depth**, and derived indices of "cover" (combining slope, stone size, distance metrics).
- Habitat data collection was concentrated in low-variance, relatively stable systems near headwaters or mesic zones — raising concern about **transferability of methods** to high-variance mainstem/tributary habitats actually occupied by humpback chub.

**Tributary comparisons discussed**:
- [[Little Colorado River]] — primary chub habitat, subject of most detailed habitat dataset (via University of Arizona M.S. thesis).
- [[Paria River]] — habitat data collected but framed around [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] spawning, not chub-specific habitat characterization.
- [[Havasu Creek]] — explicitly excluded from this round of discussion/comparison.
- [[Bright Angel Creek]] — proposed as a candidate site for an artificially managed "second breeding population" (e.g., constructed pools).
- Mainstem Colorado River with **multilevel intake structures** — alternative concept discussed for establishing a second breeding population without requiring a separate tributary.

**Data quality caveat**: Tributary datasets (excluding LCR) were judged insufficient in quantity and quality to support more than assessment of "gross similarities or dissimilarities" with the LCR — a critical limitation for evaluating whether any tributary could support a full humpback chub life cycle (spawning, rearing, adult habitat all required for population establishment).

**Flood/disturbance ecology**: LCR habitat and fish-use data collected pre-1993 reflect a "stable" hydrologic period; 1993 flooding altered habitat structure, creating an opportunity (via 1994 follow-up sampling) to document **post-flood habitat recovery** alongside continued fish-habitat association data.

## Key Findings

- **Data gap identified**: Insufficient habitat-use data exist for humpback chub in the critical 75–150 mm TL intermediate size range, driven by 1991 sampling-method delays and 1992 flood disruption — this gap justifies extending 1993 year-class monitoring into 1994.
- **Tributary vs. LCR comparability**: Existing non-LCR tributary data (excluding Havasu Creek) are of lower quality/quantity than LCR data; statistically defensible comparisons are possible only at a coarse ("gross similarity/dissimilarity") level, complicating evaluation of the [[Conservation Measure]] goal of a second breeding population.
- **Definitional ambiguity**: Whether "second breeding population" must occur in a distinct natural tributary, or could be satisfied via a managed tributary (e.g., [[Bright Angel Creek]]) or engineered mainstem habitat (multilevel intakes), remains unresolved and is flagged as a policy-relevant scientific question.
- **Methodological validation**: Field techniques criticized informally as "grossly qualitative" (e.g., the "Roubidoux stick" cover-measurement technique) were found upon review to be well-quantified and supported by extensive data in a documented D-Base system; criticism was attributed to inadequate presentation/documentation in annual reports rather than flawed methodology.
- **Analytical approach**: Gorman's framework treats current speed, substrate, and depth as covarying parameters for testing habitat-use hypotheses (vs. null/random-use models), incorporating a spatial (3D) plus temporal (4th dimension) structure across life-history stages.
- **Programmatic risk factor**: The overarching concern threading through the memo is investigator over-extension (Gorman managing field work, tributary studies, LCR studies, and report writing simultaneously under soft-money/limited FY1994 funding), which the author flags as the primary threat to final data/report quality — a non-biological but critical caveat for interpreting forthcoming GCES-II humpback chub products.
- **Recommendation**: Extension of the 1993 year-class follow-up study (reduced from 5 to 2–3 LCR trips) is supported; additional standalone tributary habitat characterization work is not recommended due to opportunity costs against higher-priority LCR/chub analysis and reporting tasks.
