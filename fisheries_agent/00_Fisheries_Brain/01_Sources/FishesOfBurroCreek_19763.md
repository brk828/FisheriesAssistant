# The Fishes of Burro Creek, Mohave and Yavapai Counties, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This 1966 survey by James Johnson and Murray Itzkowits, with input from W. L. Minckley, documents the fish fauna of [[Burro Creek]], a tributary of the [[Bill Williams River]] in western Arizona. The Bill Williams drainage was poorly studied compared with the [[Gila River]] and [[Little Colorado River]] systems, largely because of its isolation and poor access. The survey found that most of Burro Creek is intermittent. Fish persist in isolated pools above [[Arizona Highway 93]], where seven species were recorded. Native [[Gila Robusta]] dominate the upper reaches, while introduced species such as [[Lepomis Cyanellus]] and [[Notropis Lutrensis]] are abundant in some pools. The authors also review the introduction histories of these species and note that the stream's water chemistry may limit fish occupancy in some sections.

## Background & Description

**Regional setting.** Arizona has three major drainages (Gila-Salt, Little Colorado and Bill Williams), all of which reach the [[Colorado River]]. The Bill Williams system is isolated. Its only towns, Wikieup and Bagdad, have a combined population of under 2,000. Major highways U.S. 66, 89 and 60 bypass it, and the only access is [[Arizona Highway 93]]. Burro Creek is one of the tributaries of the Bill Williams River, alongside the Santa Maria and Big Sandy rivers.

**Methods and constraints.** Obtaining adequate maps was a major problem, and many access roads were hard to find. Much of the creek is reachable only by jeep road, on horseback or on foot. Sampling used seines (including larger seines in deep pools) and a gill net set for two nights. Scale growth rings were examined to estimate hatching year.

**Stream morphology.** Below Highway 93, the creek was very muddy, about 0.5 m wide and 10 cm deep at its deepest point. It appeared to be cutting a new channel within the old bed and was assumed to be non-permanent. Above the highway, the creek consisted of isolated pools linked by shallow (about 10 cm) riffles, with pools ranging from shallow runs to pools more than 1 m deep and up to roughly 600 × 50 m in size.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Pool-to-pool movement.** Fish, mainly Gila robusta, were seen fleeing across shallow riffles when chased by a seine. The authors suggest that fish move between pools, which may prevent local populations from stabilizing and may help them avoid being stranded as pools dry.

**Barriers and connectivity.** A roughly 10 m waterfall below the second large pool appears to block upstream movement of Notropis lutrensis, although individuals were found in pools halfway up the falls. Densities of Notropis and Lepomis below the falls were similar to those near the Highway 93 bridge, which the authors take as evidence that these lower pools are connected more often than those above the falls.

**Groundwater and chemistry.** Above the Burro Creek Ford beaver pond, the stream reached a "headwaters" area with a marl deposit extending about 50 m downstream. The authors attribute this to groundwater picking up carbon dioxide from soil respiration, forming carbonic acid. When the water surfaced, CO2 diffused out, pH rose, and calcium bicarbonate converted to insoluble [[Calcium Carbonate]] that precipitated as [[Marl Deposit]]. Fish and plants were absent in this area, possibly because of the altered water chemistry. Further upstream, three dry stream beds were found that stayed dry for at least three to five miles.

**Stream intermittency.** Above the pool series, the stream disappeared underground for an unknown distance. Reports from Bagdad Copper Corporation staff stated that one creek (recorded as "Bolder Creek," probably a transcription error) had no permanent water, while another ("Wilder Creek") had some permanent pools. The authors could not verify these statements because of weather and road conditions.

## Key Findings

**Fish records by site**
- **Below Highway 93:** Two of three crossings were dry, and the third had water but no fish.
- **Above Highway 93 (small pools):** [[Notropis Lutrensis]], [[Agosia Chrysogaster]] and [[Gila Robusta]].
- **Larger pools near the bridge:** The species above, plus [[Cyprinus Carpio]], [[Ictalurus Melas]] and [[Lepomis Cyanellus]]. One [[Catostomus Insignis]] was captured below the bridge.
- **Large pool about 3/4 mile above the bridge:** A large school of suckers avoided capture. Nineteen Gila robusta (6 to 10 inches) were collected.
- **[[Burro Creek Ford]] pool:** Lepomis cyanellus was extremely abundant but all were small (2 to 7 cm). Gila robusta occurred in schools of 100 or more.
- **Below the beaver pond:** Lepomis were still abundant, and small Gila robusta were the only other fish found for about a quarter mile.
- **Two deep pools below the Ford (gill net, two nights):** Gila robusta, Ictalurus melas, Cyprinus carpio and Catostomus insignis. Lepomis and Agosia were seined.
- **Pools below the 10 m falls:** Lepomis, Notropis, Gila robusta, Ictalurus sp., Cyprinus carpio and an unidentified sucker.

**Population notes**
- Except for Gila, the fish in the large pool were similar in size, and no young-of-the-year were taken.
- Scale growth rings indicated that the non-Gila fish hatched in the same year.

**Taxonomic comparison (Table 1)**
- A table compared the Burro Creek Gila robusta with subspecies *G. r. intermedia*, *G. r. robusta* and *G. r. jordani*, using meristic characters from [[La Rivers 1962]].
- Burro Creek fish have pelvic fin rays of 8/8 and lack a nuchal hump, which is absent in older fish of some other subspecies.

**Diet**
- Nine Gila robusta stomachs were examined. Most were empty. One contained plant-like material, but the OCR text on the diet results is largely unreadable.

**Introduced species history**
- [[Lepomis Cyanellus]]: described by Rafinesque in 1819. Introduced to Utah in 1890 and recorded in the Salt River (1926) and Lake Mead (1942).
- [[Ictalurus Melas]]: described by Rafinesque in 1820. Probably introduced to Nevada around the mid-1800s. First collected in Arizona in the Colorado River at Yuma in 1904.
- [[Notropis Lutrensis]]: native to the central United States. Miller (1952) judged it unsuited to the Colorado River and "not harmful" to its fauna. The authors argue that this overlooks effects on tributaries where the species does establish, and its recent establishment in Burro Creek supports that concern.

**Historical record**
- A U.S. Geological Survey interview recorded an "old Indian" who remembered catching [[Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*) with pitchforks at the mouth of Burro Creek during the spring run.
