# Yaqui Fishes Recovery Plan (Technical Review Draft)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #ictaluridae

## Executive Summary

This technical review draft, prepared by the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (Region 2, Albuquerque, New Mexico), is a recovery plan for three freshwater fishes of the [[Rio Yaqui Drainage]]: the [[Yaqui Chub]], the [[Yaqui Catfish]], and the [[Beautiful Shiner]]. The Yaqui chub is federally listed as endangered; the other two are threatened. All three have been extirpated from the United States, except the Yaqui chub, which survives at a few sites in Arizona. Declines are attributed mainly to stream channelization, water diversion, aquifer pumping, grazing-related erosion, pollution, and introduced predatory or competing fishes. The plan centers recovery on the [[San Bernardino National Wildlife Refuge]] and calls for habitat protection, nonnative fish control, captive culture at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], reintroductions, research, and public education. The document is explicitly a draft, not an approved government document, and its goals depend on appropriations and cooperation with Mexico.

## Background & Description

The plan covers three species:

- **Yaqui chub (*Gila purpurea*)**: A chubby-bodied fish rarely exceeding 150 mm. Males turn metallic blue during breeding; females remain straw-colored. It is federally endangered and listed as Group II by the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]].
- **Yaqui catfish (*Ictalurus pricei*)**: Found from highland streams (about 2100 m elevation) to the delta. It rarely exceeds 500 mm and closely resembles the [[Channel Catfish]]. It is federally threatened and listed as Group I in Arizona, meaning it is extirpated from the state but may be reestablished there.
- **Beautiful shiner (*Notropis formosus*)**: Similar in size and appearance to the [[Red Shiner]]. Breeding males turn bright metallic blue with red-orange fins. It is federally threatened and listed as Group I in Arizona.

Historically, the species had restricted ranges. The Yaqui chub occurred in San Bernardino Creek, Turkey Creek (Morse Canyon), and the Rio Yaqui system. The last abundant U.S. locality was [[Astin Spring]], which dried in 1969. The Yaqui catfish was recorded in San Bernardino Creek and was introduced to the upper Santa Cruz River in 1899, where it persisted until the late 1950s. The beautiful shiner occurred in San Bernardino Creek, its associated cienegas, and the [[Mimbres River]] in New Mexico.

Taxonomy of the beautiful shiner is disputed. Earlier workers described several names (including *N. f. mearnsi* and *N. f. formosus*), and Chernoff and Miller (1982) placed all nominal forms in synonymy with *N. formosus*, recognizing no subspecies. The plan nonetheless treats two stocks as distinct: the eastern *N. f. formosus* (Casas Grandes, Santa María, and Carmen basins) and the western *N. f. mearnsi* (Rio Yaqui). The plan keeps the two stocks separate for reintroduction.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat use.** The Yaqui chub is secretive and highly localized. In the wild it occupies the most permanent habitats, including deep pools, areas behind boulders, and undercut banks, often fed by spring underflow. On the refuge it is found in springs, artesian wells, and ponds such as [[House Pond]] and [[North Pond]]. The beautiful shiner has been recorded in small muddy pools, riffles, intermittent pools with high riffle percentages, rapids exceeding 1 m/s over rocky substrate, and stock ponds.

**Feeding and reproduction.** Spawning occurs in spring and early summer. Small adult Yaqui chub mainly eat algae and some terrestrial insects and spiders, and take small fishes and aquatic insects when available.

**Associated fauna.** Hendrickson et al. (1980) most often found the beautiful shiner and Yaqui catfish with longfin dace, Mexican stoneroller, Yaqui sucker, Sonoran topminnow, a suspected hybrid topminnow, river carpsucker, roundtail chub, and carp.

**Knowledge gaps.** The plan repeatedly states that little is known about these species. No research has been published on the Yaqui catfish, and its biology is assumed to resemble that of channel catfish. Collection data are described as scant.

**Threats.** The main drivers of decline are:

- Stream channelization and diversion of large water volumes.
- Overgrazing, which increases erosion and sediment transport and reduces flow permanence.
- Industrial and domestic pollution, including mine and smelter effluents and sewage.
- Excessive aquifer pumping, which reduces the spring flow that supplied much of the habitat.
- Introduced predators and competitors, including [[Largemouth Bass]], bluegill, black bullhead, channel catfish, mosquitofish, and green sunfish.
- Hybridization and replacement by the red shiner and channel catfish.

## Key Findings

**Current status and distribution**

- **Yaqui chub:** Present at three U.S. localities: House Pond, North Pond, and San Bernardino Creek on the refuge, plus Leslie Creek in the Swisshelm Mountains. House and North Ponds have established self-sustaining populations. The San Bernardino Creek population is low and fluctuates with stream flow. In Mexico, the species has declined severely. A 1978 survey of the Rio Yaqui system produced only one specimen.
- **Yaqui catfish:** Absent from the United States since the late 1950s. It persists in larger and moderate Rio Yaqui streams and in the Casas Grandes basin. No quantitative abundance data exist for Mexico.
- **Beautiful shiner:** Extirpated from the United States. Still found in the Rios Yaqui, Casas Grandes, Santa María, and Carmen drainages. The 1978 survey found it relatively scarce in streams but abundant in a large earthen tank near Arroyo Moctezuma, Chihuahua.

**Survey results (Hendrickson et al. 1980)**

- Of 90 separate site collections in the Rio Yaqui drainage, the Yaqui catfish was collected at 11 sites and the beautiful shiner at 23.
- The Yaqui chub was collected at only one site, with one specimen.

**Past conservation actions**

- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service purchased the San Bernardino Ranch to create a refugium for these fishes. A draft master plan has been prepared.
- In 1969, about 200 Yaqui chub (and 200 Yaqui topminnow) were moved from Astin Spring to a permanent 400 m reach of [[Leslie Creek]]. The population established and persists.
- House Pond was drained and treated with a toxicant to remove exotic fish, then restocked with Yaqui chub and topminnow from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], where chub culture has succeeded since 1976.

**Recovery criteria**

- **Yaqui chub:** Downlist when six stable populations exist at the refuge and the Leslie Creek population is secure. Delist when a secure, viable population exists within its historic range in Mexico.
- **Yaqui catfish:** Delist when four stable populations exist at the refuge.
- **Beautiful shiner:** Delist when five stable populations exist at the refuge and the eastern subspecies (*N. f. formosus*) is reestablished in the Mimbres River, New Mexico.
- The master plan goal is self-sustaining populations of six Rio Yaqui fish species within 10 years.

**Key management actions (step-down outline)**

1. **Protect and monitor existing populations.** Survey the chub once a year in mid to late summer, keep a photographic habitat record, preserve no more than 20 percent of captured fish, and compile an annual FWS report. Regulate land and water uses; build barriers against nonnative fish at the Black Draw border; and remove nonnative fish from ponds and streams in the drainage above and adjacent to the refuge.
2. **Obtain Mexican stocks.** Request permits from Mexican officials. Collect a minimum of 200 individuals each of the catfish and both shiner stocks, with 400 preferred for population genetics, and return offspring to Mexico.
3. **Culture and maintain stocks at Dexter NFH.** Develop culture methods (catfish spawning and rearing techniques are expected to transfer from channel catfish). Test F-1 genetic composition with starch-gel electrophoresis. Hold refugium populations until recovery. The hatchery's existing stock is infected with an exotic Asian tapeworm, so fresh stock from Mexico is desirable.
4. **Reintroduce fish on the refuge.** Stock four Yaqui catfish populations (House Pond 1000, Black Draw 500, and two border springs at 500 each, as suggested carrying capacities) and five beautiful shiner populations (Tule Pond 1000, Twin Pond 6000, House Pond 50,000, North Pond 2000, and Upland Spring 1000). The Mimbres River reintroduction of the eastern stock is the second priority.
5. **Research.** Study basic biology, spawning and growth, habitat preferences, and competition and predation, especially as the refuge will also host reintroduced roundtail chub, Mexican stoneroller, longfin dace, and Yaqui sucker alongside the endangered [[Yaqui Topminnow]].
6. **Enforce laws and engage the public.** Apply federal and Arizona protections until delisting. Develop an interpretive program at the refuge, outreach to private landowners, an information pamphlet, a video (including PBS distribution), and news releases.

**Private lands and Section 7 review**

- The Leslie Creek population is the only Yaqui chub population on private land. The plan calls for a habitat protection agreement with the landowner, with access and management rights to remove nonnative fish, followed by a habitat management plan.
- Federally funded or authorized projects affecting these fishes must be reviewed and consulted with the Fish and Wildlife Service under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]].

**Stated plan limitations**

- The document is a draft, not approved by the Fish and Wildlife Service or any agency.
- Goals and funding are contingent on appropriations, priorities, and budget constraints.
- The plan's emphasis is on U.S. recovery, but it commits to working cooperatively with the Mexican Government.
