## Executive Summary

This 1948 paper by John D. Hem (USGS) documents rapid and large-magnitude fluctuations in **dissolved solids concentration** in several Southwestern United States rivers — the [[Rio Grande]], [[Pecos River]], and [[Gila River]] — as a function of changing stream discharge. Although this is not a fish-specific ichthyological study, it is highly relevant to [[Freshwater Fish Habitat]] and [[Aquatic Ecosystem Water Chemistry]], since dissolved solids concentration, salinity, and chloride levels are primary abiotic drivers of habitat suitability for native Southwestern fish fauna (e.g., [[Gila Chub]], [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]], and other desert stream endemics). The paper demonstrates that dissolved solids concentration is inversely related to discharge during spring snowmelt runoff but can behave unpredictably during flash-flood events, with conductance and chloride levels changing by over 100% within hours. This has direct implications for the physiological stress tolerances and distribution patterns of fish inhabiting these variable desert lotic systems.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper characterizes the physicochemical environment experienced by aquatic organisms in Southwestern desert streams, which constitutes critical context for the **abiotic niche** of native fish assemblages:

- **Seasonal patterns**: During sustained high-flow spring snowmelt runoff (Rio Grande at San Acacia, April–June 1945), specific conductance dropped to a minimum of 30.4 (K×10⁵ mhos at 25°C) during peak flow, versus ~70 before/after — indicating dilution of dissolved solids during high discharge. This creates a seasonally predictable low-salinity window potentially important for spawning or larval fish stages of species adapted to the [[Rio Grande]] system.
- **Flash-flood dynamics**: In summer months, flash floods from local convective storms cause rapid, large swings in dissolved solids. On the [[Gila River]] near Bylas, AZ (August 1943), conductance ranged from a minimum of 73.0 to a maximum of 404 over the month, with changes of 100% or more within a few hours during flood events — a potentially significant osmotic stressor for resident fish.
- **Groundwater-surface water interaction**: Low-flow baseflow in these systems is dominated by highly mineralized groundwater seepage, while flash-flood water is comparatively dilute. This creates a dynamic mosaic of water quality conditions that fish must tolerate, relevant to the concept of [[Desert Stream Fish Adaptations]].
- **Pecos River basin geology**: Due to extensive soluble rock (evaporite) formations in the Pecos River basin, flood waters near Artesia, NM carry higher dissolved solids than comparable Gila River floodwaters (conductance ranged from 115 to 677 across August 1939) — indicating basin-specific baseline salinity differences relevant to comparative [[Fish Osmoregulation]] studies across Southwestern river systems.
- **Diurnal chloride cycling linked to riparian vegetation**: In extremely low-flow reaches of the Gila River near Fort Thomas, AZ, dense stands of transpiring salt cedar (*Tamarix gallica*) along the channel margins were found to drive a diurnal discharge and chloride concentration cycle. Chloride peaked (~2690 ppm) at maximum discharge and dropped to a minimum (~2610 ppm) at minimum discharge, apparently due to evaporative salt accumulation on wetted channel sands during low flow, followed by redissolution as water levels rose. This mechanism illustrates a direct riparian vegetation–water chemistry–fish habitat linkage relevant to [[Riparian Vegetation Effects on Stream Salinity]].
- **Extreme salinity levels**: Chloride concentrations recorded near Malaga, NM on the Pecos River reached over 1200 ppm, and reach as high as ~2600+ ppm in the low-flow Gila River channel near Fort Thomas — levels that would be physiologically challenging or limiting for many freshwater fish taxa, underscoring the highly saline-tolerant nature of native Southwestern desert fish fauna.

## Key Findings

- Dissolved solids concentration in Southwestern streams is tightly coupled to discharge, but the relationship is basin- and season-specific rather than universal.
- During sustained spring snowmelt high flow, conductance remains low and relatively stable (Rio Grande, San Acacia, 1945).
- During summer flash floods, conductance/discharge relationships are highly unstable, with dissolved solids concentration dropping sharply during flood pulses and rebounding as flow recedes (Gila River at Bylas; Pecos River at Artesia).
- Daily single-sample monitoring is inadequate to characterize true water chemistry variability in these systems; sub-daily (3+ samples/day) sampling revealed conductance swings from 97.7 to 261 (and up to 232 within 4 hours) at San Acacia in August 1946.
- Hourly sampling of the Pecos River near Malaga (July 1937) showed chloride fluctuating between ~1060–1200+ ppm within a single day even under "normal" flow conditions, driven by dilution/concentration dynamics with saline groundwater inflow and salt dissolution from the channel.
- A previously undocumented diurnal chloride/discharge cycle was identified in the Gila River near Fort Thomas (June 1944), attributed to riparian phreatophyte (salt cedar) transpiration drawing down the water table during daylight hours and evaporative salt concentration in the channel bed.
- Discussant C.S. Scofield noted that reservoir regulation (e.g., via impoundments) tends to dampen these natural fluctuations in both discharge and dissolved solids concentration, an important consideration for interpreting historical vs. regulated-river fish habitat conditions in the [[Southwestern United States]].
- Overall, the paper underscores that native Southwestern fish fauna evolved under conditions of extreme, rapid, and unpredictable fluctuation in salinity and dissolved solids — a foundational consideration for [[Desert Fish Conservation]] and habitat management under altered flow regimes (e.g., dam regulation, water diversion).
