# Competition in Tropical Stream Fishes: Support for the Competitive Exclusion Principle

#ichthyology #marine-biology #characidae

## Executive Summary

This study by Thomas M. Zaret and A. Stanley Rand tests the [[Competitive Exclusion Principle]] in a natural community of nine to twelve sympatric fish species from a lowland stream in Panama. Using [[Stomach Content Analysis]] from a dry-season and a wet-season collection, the authors measured [[Food Overlap]] between species and estimated food abundance independently of overlap. Food overlap was lowest in the dry season, when food abundance was also lowest. The authors interpret this coincidence as support for the principle's predicted consequences, while acknowledging that the link between food abundance and competition is not fully resolved.

## Background & Description

The competitive exclusion principle holds that two sympatric populations occupying the same ecological niche cannot coexist indefinitely, so one excludes the other. Because the principle is difficult to prove or disprove directly, earlier support was largely indirect and relied on inferences about past competition. The authors aimed for a less circular test: measure [[Niche Overlap]] and resource abundance independently of competition, then check whether overlap changes with observable seasonal conditions.

The study area was a stretch of the Rio Pedro Miguel, a stream within the [[Madden Road Forest Preserve]] in the [[Panama Canal Zone]], lined on both sides by tall forest. The wet season (May to January) brings fast, swollen water. The dry season (January to May) reduces stream volume until it leaves isolated pools.

Niche was characterized by two parameters: habitat (four categories per pool: surface, edge, middle, bottom) and food type. Morphological measures were rejected because fish mouths are soft and extensible, so feeding behavior had to be known before the relevant dimensions could be measured.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Sampling.** Dry-season fish were netted on 13 April 1969 and again on 21 April 1969, when one pool was resampled and a third pool was cleared with [[Rotenone Sampling]]. The wet-season sample was taken on 22 September 1969 from a 60-m seined and rotenoned section of fast water that included two dry-season stations. The authors note that oxygenation may have rendered rotenone ineffective in moving water, so the wet-season sample probably missed some fish.

**Specimen counts.** Dry-season samples yielded 10 species, with a maximum of 8 in any one pool; the third pool alone contained 153 specimens. Wet-season samples yielded 11 species, 9 of which also occurred in the dry season, with 338 specimens in total.

**Habitat partitioning.** Dry-season species fell into five habitat groups:
- Surface: [[Gephyrocharax Atricaudata]]
- Edge: [[Poecilia Sphenops]] and [[Neoheterandria Tridentiger]]
- Middle: [[Roeboides Guatemalensis]] and [[Astyanax Ruberrimus]]
- Bottom (diurnal): [[Piabucina Panamensis]], [[Aequidens Coeruleopunctatus]], and [[Eleotris Picta]]
- Nocturnal bottom feeders: [[Rhamdia Wagneri]] and [[Synbranchus Marmoratus]]

Diel separation was treated as an additional niche axis. [[Gobiomorus Dormitor]] was a wet-season replacement for Eleotris, occupying a similar niche. [[Plecostomus Plecostomus]] was also included in the feeding-method table.

**Feeding.** Gephyrocharax, a surface feeder, took mainly terrestrial arthropods. Poecilia ate only algae, and Neoheterandria ate small insects. Astyanax in dry-season pools fed in the middle area on filamentous algae, while Roeboides ate insects. Piabucina took fruit and prawns from the bottom. Aequidens and Eleotris ate mostly aquatic arthropods, with Eleotris taking larger items such as odonates and large ephemeropterans. Synbranchus ate vertebrates including small fish and frogs.

## Key Findings

- **Dry-season overlap was low.** Species with similar habitats also had the most distinct diets. Five dry-season pairs exceeded the authors' threshold of 0.60 for significant overlap, but none of these overlaps occurred between species in the same habitat.
- **Wet-season overlap rose.** Several species that were tightly partitioned in the dry season overlapped broadly in the wet season. Astyanax now shared the surface with Gephyrocharax and overlapped strongly with it and with Roeboides. Seven of nine species showed increased overlap values in the wet season.
- **Astyanax shifted its diet.** In the dry season, Astyanax fed in the middle habitat almost exclusively on filamentous algae. The authors attribute this to competitive displacement from the surface by Gephyrocharax and from the middle by Roeboides. In the wet season, it returned to surface feeding and ate more terrestrial insects.
- **Food was more abundant in the wet season.** Four lines of direct evidence support this:
  1. Gut contents were larger in Gephyrocharax, Roeboides, Astyanax, and Aequidens.
  2. Fat bodies were present in the wet season but not in late dry season fish.
  3. Trichoptera, a major food source, appeared only in the wet season.
  4. Prawn number and biomass were roughly ten times higher in the wet season.
- **Indirect evidence agrees.** Flying insect abundance is reported as lower in the dry season, based on studies of a bat and a spider in Panama. Work on the Rupununi savanna fishes in British Guiana found a large wet-season food increase.
- **Overall conclusion.** Food overlap was lowest when food abundance was lowest, which matches the predictions of the competitive exclusion principle. The authors argue this provides strong support for the principle in a natural community.
- **Caveats.** The authors state that the relationship between food abundance and competition is not fully clear. Wet-season streams have more surface and substrate area, which may make food harder to find even when it is more abundant. A measure of food availability, rather than general abundance, would be needed to settle the question. The authors also note that the observed low dry-season overlap contrasts with Lowe-McConnell's (1964) report of dry-season overlap, which she attributed to critical food shortages. The present collections were made while food was still available.
- **Unresolved cases.** The reason Piabucina was absent in the wet season is unknown. Rhamdia and Eleotris had the greatest potential overlap in the dry season, but they are active at different times of day, so the degree of overlap is unknown.
