# Springtime Foods of Bonytail (Gila elegans) in a Lower Colorado River Backwater

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This study documents the springtime diet of endangered [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*) in [[Cibola High Levee Pond]], an isolated backwater of the [[Colorado River]] on [[Cibola National Wildlife Refuge]]. Stomach contents were nonlethally sampled from 72 fish in May 2003 and May 2004 during evening and night collections. Bonytail were omnivorous, and diet composition shifted with body size: plant material declined while invertebrates and fish increased in larger individuals. Feeding was more intense at night, consistent with telemetry showing adults are nocturnal. The study also reports the first record of the Asian tapeworm, *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*, from open waters of the lower Colorado River system, and the first detection of nonnative [[Bullfrog]] and [[Red Swamp Crayfish]] in bonytail diet.

## Background & Description

Bonytail is a large cyprinid native to the Colorado River basin and is critically imperiled. Permit limits and its conservation status restricted the sample size. Adults are active at night and dormant under cover during the day, based on [[Radio Telemetry]] work at the same site (Marsh et al., 2013).

[[Cibola High Levee Pond]] is a small (ca. 2 ha) remnant of the lower Colorado River channel, lying between the river and inland high levees. It was reclaimed in 1993 to remove nonnative fishes and then stocked with bonytail and [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*). At the time of the study, only these two species were present, with stocked and naturally produced individuals across multiple generations. Other species, including [[Western Mosquitofish]], [[Bluegill]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Threadfin Shad]], and catfishes, had been removed in earlier years and were not known to be present during the study.

The study had three aims: document bonytail foods in spring, examine how diet varied with fish size, and assess temporal patterns in feeding and food use.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Cibola High Levee Pond is part of the management strategy for endangered Colorado River fishes. Backwaters like it are considered critical to the life-history needs of native species. The authors argue that such habitats can be designed to foster the development of important food resources.

Previous diet records for bonytail, from natural streams, [[Green River]] (Utah), [[Lake Mead]], and [[Lake Mohave]], show a generalist pattern. Diets included benthic and drifting invertebrates, terrestrial insects, plankton, algae, and occasional fish. The authors note that their results add substantial detail to feeding ecology but little new qualitative information.

Among the parasites found, the Asian tapeworm was previously reported from [[Humpback Chub]] in the Little Colorado River (Clarkson et al., 1997). The authors suggest the parasite may spread to other species and places. Its introduction to the pond is unclear, but it may have arrived with hatchery stock or with unauthorized stocking.

## Key Findings

**Sample and design**
- Total sample: 72 bonytail, collected with [[Trammel Net Sampling]] in May 2003 (n = 28) and May 2004 (n = 44).
- Two feeding periods were sampled: evening (1800–2400 h) and night (0100–0545 h).
- Stomach contents were removed nonlethally using [[Gastric Flushing]] through a squeeze bulb and tubing matched to fish gape size.
- Gastrointestinal contents were sieved (mesh stated as 500 µm), and six categories were visually estimated: amorphous organic matter, inorganic matter, plant, fish, invertebrate, and other.

**Fish size**
- 2003 fish: 376–510 mm total length (mean given as garbled in source); 305–1,136 g (mean 565 g).
- 2004 fish were smaller: 271–509 mm total length (mean 325 mm).

**Diet composition**
- Bonytail were omnivorous. Amorphous organic matter was predominant in many samples.
- Inorganic material included pebbles, sand, and insect larval cases, mainly trichopterans such as Hydroptilidae.
- Plants included *Najas*, *Potamogeton*, and *Chara*.
- Invertebrates included copepods, ostracods, *Daphnia*, red swamp crayfish (*Procambarus clarkii*), the Asian clam (Corbiculidae, *Corbicula fluminea*), dipteran larvae and adults, Notonectidae, and odonate nymphs and adults.
- Other matter included remains of [[Bullfrog]] (*Lithobates catesbeianus*).
- Fish remains occurred in 6 of 72 samples (8%), and only in fish longer than 425 mm. Bonytail and razorback sucker could not be distinguished in gut samples.
- Plant proportion decreased, and invertebrate and fish proportions increased, with fish size.
- Mean wet weight of stomach samples was 1.54 g (nonzero range 0.06–13.97 g; SE 0.35). Sample weight showed little linear relationship with body weight or length.

**Empty stomachs and timing**
- 13 of 72 fish (18%) had empty tracts.
- Empty tracts were more than four times more frequent in evening samples (33%) than at night (7%) (Yates-corrected chi-squared, χ² = 6.44, df = 1, P < 0.05).
- Gross gut composition was similar between feeding periods, but volume and feeding intensity were highest at night.
- Analysed contents (excluding empty tracts) were 20 evening and 39 night samples.

**Parasites**
- Asian tapeworm, *Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*, was positively identified in one specimen.
- Proglottids, presumed to be this species, were found in 8 of 72 fish (11%) across all size classes, but scolexes were absent, so identification is presumptive for these fish.
- This is the first record of the Asian tapeworm in bonytail from natural habitat on the lower Colorado River.

**Management implications**
- Detection of nonnative bullfrog and crayfish in bonytail diets suggests that bonytail may help suppress these animals in off-channel habitats, supporting native fish.
- Bonytail may also consume nonspiny-rayed nonnative fishes such as western mosquitofish, which could benefit native fishes in managed ponds.
- The authors recommend that researchers and managers be alert to the tapeworm and report incidents as they occur.

**Limitations**
- Gastric flushing was not tested against surgical extraction. Flushing may favor smaller, more digestion-resistant items, so larger food, including fish, may have been underestimated.
- Sample size was limited by permits and the species' status, and the study was restricted to one site over two years.
