# Life History of the Grass Pickerel (Esox americanus vermiculatus) in Oklahoma

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This Oklahoma Fishery Research Laboratory bulletin, derived from a [[Master of Science Thesis]] at the University of Oklahoma (1968), documents the first detailed study of the [[Grass Pickerel]] in Oklahoma. Fieldwork ran from 1963 to 1966 across six counties of southeastern Oklahoma. The study covers distribution, habitat, morphology, associated fishes, food habits, reproduction, age and growth, and parasites. The grass pickerel is at the western edge of its range and appears to be declining, with the strongest populations in the [[Little River System]]. It is a vegetation-associated predator that shifts from crustaceans and insects to fish as it grows.

## Background & Description

The grass pickerel (*Esox americanus vermiculatus*) is a freshwater member of the [[Pike Family (Esocidae)]]. In Oklahoma it is confined to six southeastern counties, which mark the western edge of its North American distribution, apart from a relict population in Nebraska. Prior work on the species focused on the northern range (Canada, Michigan, Wisconsin, Ohio), so the southern populations were poorly known.

Morphological characters were compared with the [[Redfin Pickerel]] (*E. a. americanus*) and the intergrades described by [[Crossman]] (1966). Oklahoma fish mostly matched the grass pickerel subspecies. Study area details are summarized below.

- **Study area:** Oak-pine forest, floodplains with [[Cutoff Lakes]] (oxbow lakes), and steep Ouachita Mountain drainages with elevations of 750 to 1,250 feet.
- **Climate:** Humid and mesothermal; annual rainfall 44 to 57 inches; growing season about 235 days.
- **Water chemistry:** Streams are acidic (mean pH 6.8), reservoirs alkaline (mean pH 8.1), cutoff lakes near neutral (pH 6.9 to 7.2); conductivity 55 to 760 µmhos at 25 °C.
- **Sampling:** 59 collections at 49 sites by the author, totaling 546 pickerel. Museum records added 456 pickerel from 97 sites.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Distribution.** Grass pickerel were most abundant in the [[Little River System]] and [[Mountain Fork River]] but rare in the [[Kiamichi River]] drainage, except at [[Lake Raymond Gary]]. The Arkansas and Red rivers were judged unsuitable habitat. The author concluded that the species' range was once larger and is now shrinking, with losses in the Poteau system (Lake Wister and Crooked Slough yielded no pickerel). An 1858 record from Coal Creek near McAlester supports the earlier, wider range. Possible causes include land use, soil erosion, forest burning, stream pollution, and low-water dams.

**Habitat.** Pickerel favored dense submerged vegetation over mud bottoms in shallow, weedy creek heads, isolated pools, and braided channels. They were rare in open water, in cutoff lakes, in farm ponds, and in the main current of rivers. Key plants included *Potamogeton pectinatus*, *Najas guadalupensis*, *Ceratophyllum demersum*, and *Chara* sp. Pickerel were never found around cattails.

**Associated species.** A total of 76 species, excluding pickerel, was collected; 44 were closely associated with pickerel. [[Sunfishes]] (Centrarchidae) were the most frequent associates, with *Lepomis cyanellus* most widespread. Darters (*Etheostoma*) followed, then minnows (Cyprinidae). The most common associates included [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) and the [[Brook Silverside]] (*Labidesthes sicculus*).

## Key Findings

**Morphology.** Of 257 specimens measured, 137 were used for meristic counts. Lateral line scales ranged from 96 to 124 (mode 114). Pickerel from Oklahoma most closely matched *E. a. vermiculatus* in the four Crossman characters. Right-side branchiostegal dominance was seen in 9 of 137 fish (7%).

**Food habits.** Of 525 stomachs examined, 365 (69.5%) contained food.
- Pickerel 12 to 49 mm fed mainly on copepods, cladocerans, chironomids, and ephemeropterans.
- Fish first appeared at 30 mm and became the main food above 80 mm.
- In pickerel 200 to 340 mm, fish occurred in 81% of stomachs and made up 91% of volume.
- Lake pickerel ate almost only fish (98% of volume); stream pickerel had a more varied diet.
- Cannibalism was recorded in only 4 of 525 fish.

**Feeding selection.** Young pickerel (30 to 199 mm) positively selected darters, while larger fish preyed in proportion to relative abundance (Ivlev's electivity index).

**Reproduction.** Oklahoma pickerel likely spawn in late February or early March, with some winter and fall spawning reported. The smallest mature female was 118 mm and the smallest mature male 109 mm, both during March. Some individuals mature in their first year. The sex ratio was 1:1 (total sample chi-square non-significant).

**Age and growth.** A total of 715 fish were aged by scales; 54% of the 514 fish from the author's collections were age-group 0 (279 of 514). Maximum age was age-group IV (4 years), with females older and larger than males. Calculated mean total lengths at the end of years 1 to 4 were 117, 197, 251, and 304 mm. Oklahoma growth was below that in Wisconsin and Ohio but above that in Canada. The scale-based annulus method was difficult, and the authors note irregular growth, possibly due to winter spawning and mild winters.

**Length-weight.** The combined relationship was log W = −5.3953 + 3.0606L. Males over 210 mm from lakes were heavier than females of the same length. Stream pickerel were heavier at a given length than lake pickerel.

**Parasites.** Two trematodes, *Azygia longa* and *A. angusticauda*, were found in stomachs only in fish from the Little River system (13 localities). Infection did not visibly affect growth.

**Conservation.** The author recommends further study because the range appears to be contracting and optimal growing conditions may be limited in southeastern Oklahoma.
