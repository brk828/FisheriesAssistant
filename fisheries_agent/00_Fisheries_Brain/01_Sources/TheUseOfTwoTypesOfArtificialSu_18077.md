# Artificial Substrate Samplers for Macroinvertebrates in the Rat River, 1970

#benthic-macroinvertebrates #freshwater-ecology #trichoptera

## Executive Summary

This manuscript report (Research and Development Branch, Manitoba Department of Mines and Natural Resources; prepared for the 19th Annual Meeting of the Midwest Benthological Society, 1971) compares two artificial substrate samplers for collecting [[Benthic Macroinvertebrates]] in the [[Rat River]], southeastern [[Manitoba]]. The [[Chicken Barbecue Basket Sampler]] (filled with rock rubble) and the [[Steel Tray Sampler]] were deployed at four stations between April 24 and September 15, 1970. The study aimed to assess relative sampling efficiency, derive quantitative data, and determine the time needed for stable colonization. The document is marked "Not for Publication" and its data are interim, so citation requires permission.

## Background & Description

Artificial substrates are used to sample stream and lake fauna where [[Current Velocity]], water depth, or bottom sediments prevent use of dredges and conventional samplers. The author notes three known problems: quantitative data are scarce, few studies have compared sampler efficiency, and disturbed samplers lose organisms, which biases quantitative results. The time to reach stable populations is also variable.

The [[Rat River]] flows about 70 miles (approx. 113 km) from its source to the [[Red River]], passing through several geologic and vegetation zones. The study reach lay about 40 miles (64.3 km) south of [[Winnipeg]] and was described as relatively unpolluted, with no large population centres upstream.

Sampler design:
- **Baskets:** chicken barbecue baskets, 10 in. (25 cm) long and 6.5 in. (16.25 cm) in diameter, filled with stones 2–4 in. in diameter. Each weighed about 10 lb (22 kg).
- **Trays:** steel trays, 16 in. (400 cm, reported as printed) square and 1¼ in. (3.5 cm) deep, with two layers of expanded metal screen. Each weighed 22 lb (48.4 kg).
- Six of each sampler type were set per station. Trays were placed upstream of baskets to limit current impedance.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Stations and sampling design:** Four stations comprised two riffle sites (Stations 1 and 2) and two pool sites (Stations 3 and 4). Set periods were five one-week, five two-week, four three-week and four four-week intervals. Retrieval used scuba in April and May, then hand retrieval in June after temperatures rose and currents slackened. A hand winch on the boat aided retrieval.

**Physical conditions:**
- Water temperature rose from 32 °F (0 °C) to 45 °F (7.2 °C) between April 24 and May 22, reached the high 60s °F by June 23, and peaked at 80 °F (26.6 °C) by August 15.
- Current velocities fell continuously from May 15 to August 15, when no flow was detectable. Rainfall after August 25 restored flow comparable to August 18.

**Fauna and seasonal shifts:** Early-season samples were dominated by lotic taxa such as Psychomyiidae, Philopotamidae and Helicopsychidae caddisflies, which were most abundant when currents were strongest. Quiet-water families including Dytiscidae, Gomphidae and Aeschnidae were found only on August 11 and 18. Chironomid genera shifted from lentic to lotic assemblages over the season. [[Hydropsyche]] (Trichoptera) showed two emergences, late May and mid-to-late July. The genus [[Cheumatopsyche]] was more common later in the season, possibly because it tolerates warmer water and lower velocities. Adult [[Calopteryx]] (damselflies) were common along the bank in July, and nymphs were collected on August 11 and 18.

**Incidental fish:** Fish were not targeted. Several individuals were captured in the samplers, including *Noturus gyrinus* (stonecat), *Ambloplites rupestris* (rock bass), *Etheosoma nigrum* (johnny darter), *Percina maculata* (blackside darter), *Lota lota* (burbot), unidentified fry, and lamprey ammocoetes.

## Key Findings

**1. Sampler efficiency.** Basket samplers collected more organisms than tray samplers in every period. Ratios of basket to tray catch were:

| Period | Basket:Tray ratio |
|---|---|
| One week | 1.45:1 |
| Two weeks | 1.15:1 |
| Three weeks | 2.33:1 |
| Four weeks | 1.28:1 |
| All periods | 1.46:1 |

Paired "t" tests indicated that baskets were significantly more effective over all periods (Table 3). The three-week F-test was marginally significant, so the t-test was still applied, following Steel and Torrie (1960).

**2. Taxonomic coverage.** Both samplers collected the same groups in each period, and no taxon was collected exclusively by one sampler type. In total, 15 orders, 32 families and 58 genera and species were recorded. Basket samplers consistently yielded higher percentages of major faunal groups than trays. The author attributes this to rock rubble being a better substrate, limited access to trays (the 1/4 in. gap between lid and sides), sediment accumulation in trays, and lower food supply in trays.

**3. Stable colonization.** Basket samplers reached stable populations within about 21 days (three weeks). Tray populations were still increasing at 28 days. The author cites a literature range of 25 to 135 days for stable colonization.

**4. Sampler practicality.**
- Basket recovery: 86 of 108 (80%).
- Tray recovery: 102 of 108 (94%).
- Trays were harder to set and retrieve because of their weight, but organisms could be removed from trays in about half the time needed for baskets.
- Trays were painted red and may have been less visible, although size and weight may also have limited removal.

**5. Composition agreement.** In 11 of 18 collections (61%), both samplers yielded the same faunal composition. Composition was most stable after the two- and three-week periods.

**6. Water temperature and emergence.** Temperature was considered a major driver of insect development rates. Chironomid (midge) emergence was detectable between July 23 and July 28. Ephemeroptera early instars were abundant by July 18, suggesting prior emergence.

**7. Conclusions and recommendations.** The author concludes that:
- Baskets are more effective than trays for sampling macroinvertebrates.
- Trays provide quantitative data but are difficult to set and retrieve.
- Baskets can be used in streams where dredges fail because of depth, current or substrate, and can compare sites within a stream, between streams, and at the same site over time.
- The main application proposed is monitoring water quality changes reflected in altered aquatic fauna.
- Where quantitative data are essential and cost is not limiting, trays should not be overlooked.
