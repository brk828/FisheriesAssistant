# Palo Verde Valley Agricultural Drain Pesticide and Heavy Metal Study (1975)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #ictaluridae #centrarchidae #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

In January 1975 the California Department of Fish and Game's [[Water Quality Monitoring]] staff conducted a brief pilot study of agricultural drainage in the [[Palo Verde Valley]], adjacent to the [[Colorado River]]. The study was prompted by proposed discharge requirements for the [[Coachella Valley Water District]] and the [[Palo Verde Irrigation District]]. Agency reviewers judged that the proposed monitoring of total dissolved solids, settleable solids and flow could not provide the toxicity-relevant data needed to protect fish and wildlife, and that the proposed number and frequency of sampling points were inadequate.

The team sampled four stations, collected sediment, [[Asian Clam]] (Corbicula), and several fish species across the food chain, and measured basic water chemistry. Organophosphate and thiophosphate pesticides were absent, and [[PP'-DDE]] appeared at trace levels in clams and fish. Copper, zinc and mercury increased downstream in some organisms, and [[Largemouth Bass]] carried higher residues than their prey. The authors described the work as inconclusive and recommended more extensive sampling.

## Background & Description

- **Purpose:** Establish whether agricultural return flows discharged into the Colorado River system carried pesticides and heavy metals that could harm aquatic life, and trace how these pollutants moved through the food chain.
- **Legal basis:** The document cites [[Fish and Game Code Section 5650]], which prohibits placing substances deleterious to fish, plant life, mammals or birds into waters of the state.
- **Study area:** Irrigation water is purchased from the Colorado River and distributed by canals. Return flows drain into the Palo Verde Valley drain network, which had developed established fish and wildlife populations.
- **Biota:** Local fauna includes Channel Catfish, Largemouth Bass, Redear Sunfish, Common Carp and Bluegill. Muskrats, raccoons, waterfowl and shorebirds also use the drains, and birds prey on clams and crayfish. Many non-game species occur, and beaver are present.
- **Personnel:** Report prepared by Perry L. Bergesell (Associate Water Quality Biologist) and Brian (Assistant Water Quality Biologist). Electroshocking was assisted by Department fisheries biologists from the Blythe office.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Sampling Stations

Four stations were established along the drainage system, with equal spacing between endpoints:

| Station | Location | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Palo Verde Diversion Dam | Small cove on the Colorado River, slow-moving water, mostly coarse gravel |
| 2 | East Side Drain (south of old Highway 60) | Dense vegetation, much silt, about 20 ft wide, 3 ft mean depth |
| 3 | Main Drain (west side, north of Palo Verde Avenue bridge) | About 25 ft wide, gravel and silt bottom, abundant bulrushes |
| 4 | Palo Verde Outfall Drain (near the town of Palo Verde) | About 100 ft wide, sand and silt, shallow areas, dense vegetation |

### Food Chain Organisms

- **Threadfin Shad** (*Dorosoma petenense*): planktivore, forage fish.
- **Common Carp** (*Cyprinus carpio*): benthic feeder on invertebrates, aquatic plants and insects.
- **Sunfish** (scientific name garbled in the OCR): carnivore feeding on insects and small organisms.
- **Largemouth Bass** (*Micropterus salmoides*): top predator.
- **Asian Clam** (*Corbicula* sp.): filter feeder chosen to represent a distinct trophic level and to track the fate of metals and pesticides in water.

Fish were collected by gill net and electroshocking boat. Clams were collected with a dip net, and sediment was sieved.

### Water Chemistry

Field measurements included salinity, conductivity, total dissolved solids (TDS), pH, dissolved oxygen (D.O.), turbidity, ammonia and temperature. Temperature ranged between 11.5°C and 19.5°C. Key trends from the sampling run:

- **Conductivity:** rose from about 825 µmhos at Station 1 to a peak of about 2,200 µmhos at Station 3, remaining high at Station 4 (about 2,150 µmhos).
- **TDS:** increased steadily downstream to 1,175 ppm at Station 4. The starting value is partly illegible in the OCR.
- **Turbidity:** variable, from 1 JTU at Station 1 to 15 JTU at Station 3 and 10 JTU at Station 4.
- **Salinity:** 1.0 o/oo at Stations 1 and 2, and 2.0 o/oo at Stations 3 and 4.
- **Dissolved oxygen:** declined downstream to 9.6 mg/L at Station 4.
- **pH:** dropped slightly from about 8.1 at Station 1 to 7.8 at Station 4.
- **Ammonia:** varied considerably, from a low of 0.02 mg/L at Station 3 to a higher value at Station 4 (value partly illegible).

Sulfide and turbid water may have interfered with some analytical results.

## Key Findings

### Pesticides
- Organophosphate and thiophosphate pesticides were not detected in sediment, clams or fish.
- No generalized carbamate scan was possible, so that class remains unassessed.
- Trace levels of p,p'-DDE were found in clams and fish but not in sediment. Fish showed a downstream increase in p,p'-DDE, while clams showed no detectable residues.
- Lindane may accumulate in sediment.
- Pesticide levels were low, probably because sampling occurred in January, a period of low pesticide application.
- The laboratory concluded that no unusually high pesticide residues were found, and that fish showed a slight buildup of p,p'-DDE in the drain.

### Heavy Metals
- **Copper:** a slight increase occurred in clams, and copper appeared to be reconcentrated in the Palo Verde outfall. Copper levels in sediment and organisms increased downstream in some samples.
- **Zinc:** concentrations in clams fluctuated greatly from station to station. Where a trend was present, levels appeared to decrease downstream.
- **Mercury:** substantial increases were noted in Largemouth Bass, especially at the Palo Verde outfall. Mercury was not detected in all clams and sediments.
- **Iron:** traces found in all sediment samples but not reaching the level at Station 1 in both wet and dry samples.

### Bioaccumulation and Pathways
- Pesticides were more concentrated in agricultural return flow than in supply water.
- Largemouth Bass had higher concentrations than either prey species (Threadfin Shad or Redear Sunfish), suggesting [[Bioaccumulation]] up the food chain. The authors called this possibility real but unproven.
- Metal and pesticide levels increased downstream in most parts of the system, consistent with leaching of materials into the water as it moves downstream.

### Limitations and Recommendations
- The study was brief and sampling was limited to one season (January 1975). The authors described their results as inconclusive.
- Several analyses were not completed, and some samples were insufficient for comparison, including some copper and mercury analyses.
- The team concluded that the monitoring required under the proposed discharge permits was insufficient, and that more sampling sites, more frequent sampling and toxicity testing were needed to protect aquatic life.
