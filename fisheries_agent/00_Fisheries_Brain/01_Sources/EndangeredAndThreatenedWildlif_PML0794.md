# Critical Habitat Designation for the Rio Grande Silvery Minnow

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service's final rule (Federal Register, 19 February 2003) designates [[Critical Habitat]] for the [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] (*Hybognathus amarus*), a federally listed endangered cyprinid. The designation covers approximately 157 river miles of the middle Rio Grande, from Cochiti Dam in Sandoval County downstream to the utility line crossing in Socorro County, New Mexico, plus the tributary Jemez River from Jemez Canyon Dam to the upstream boundary of Santa Ana Pueblo. Critical habitat is defined as the river channel within the existing levees or, where no levees exist, 300 feet (91.4 m) of riparian zone on each side of the bankfull stage. Lands of the Pueblos of Santo Domingo, Santa Ana, Sandia and Isleta were excluded under [[Section 4(b)(2) Exclusions]] because their special management plans were judged to provide equal or greater conservation benefit. The Pecos River and lower Rio Grande (Big Bend reach) were also excluded. The cover letter transmits the rule with the final Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) and final Economic Analysis.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** The [[Rio Grande Silvery Minnow]] is a small, fast-maturing, hardy fish that historically inhabited wide, shallow, sandy-bottomed reaches of the Rio Grande and Pecos River. Fish live roughly 1 to 2 years, and the document notes that fish born in 2000 would be age-2 by 2002 and could be the only survivors to spawn in a given year.
- **Listing history:** Proposed for listing in 1993 and listed as endangered in 1994 under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. A court-ordered deadline led to a proposed critical habitat rule on 6 June 2002 (67 FR 39205), with a comment period that closed 2 October 2002.
- **Range reduction:** The species now occupies less than 5 percent (the text notes "a percent" in OCR; the fraction is stated as less than 5 percent in the text) of its historic range. Dams such as [[Elephant Butte Reservoir]] and diversions such as the [[Isleta Diversion Dam]] and [[San Acacia Diversion Dam]] fragmented habitat.
- **Recovery Plan:** The 1999 Recovery Plan (Service 1999) sets two primary goals: (1) stabilize and enhance populations in the middle Rio Grande; and (2) reestablish the species in at least three other areas of its historic range.
- **Captive propagation:** Silvery minnows are propagated at several facilities in New Mexico, and about 225,500 individuals had been released into the wild since May 1996.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Primary constituent elements (PCEs):** The rule identifies physical and biological features essential to conservation, including flowing water sufficient to reduce formation of isolated pools, shallow sandy or silty substrate, and conditions that do not increase prolonged periods of low or no flow. Isolated pools persisting longer than about 48 hours typically lose the species, and drying of pools can increase temperature and reduce dissolved oxygen.
- **Flow dynamics:** Spring runoff coincides with and triggers spawning. Releases such as the 1,850 cfs release from Elephant Butte on 7 May 2002 were made to provide spawning flow. Prolonged low or no flow, channel degradation, and bed aggradation at the lower end of the Low Flow Conveyance Channel (LFCC) are documented threats.
- **Hybridization and non-native fish:** Non-native species and hybridization with congeners, including the [[Plains Minnow]] (*Hybognathus placitus*), have been documented; the rule describes the role of non-native fish in the species' decline as uncertain.
- **Water management:** Supplemental water from the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BOR) has been leased since 1996. The economic analysis estimates an average annual deficit of 40,427 acre-feet in the middle Rio Grande, and an approximately 22,000 af release occurred in 2001. In 2000, about 168,000 af was provided for the species and irrigation.
- **Threats:** Irrigation diversions, unscreened diversions, stranding in drying channels, predation, and water quality issues (including algal blooms) were identified as concerns.
- **Development exclusions:** Developed flood control facilities, paved roads, bridges, parking lots, dikes, levees, diversion structures, railroad tracks, water diversion canals outside natural channels, active gravel pits, cultivated agricultural land, and residential, commercial and industrial developments are not critical habitat.

## Key Findings

- **Designated area:** About 157 river miles of the middle Rio Grande (Cochiti Dam to the utility line crossing) and the Jemez River from Jemez Canyon Dam to the Santa Ana Pueblo boundary. The Pecos River (Summer Dam to Brantley Dam) and lower Rio Grande (Big Bend National Park downstream to the Terrell/Val Verde County line) were analyzed and excluded.
- **Excluded Pueblo lands:** Santo Domingo, Santa Ana, Sandia and Isleta, based on special management plans and the benefit of voluntary cooperation. The Service found that exclusion would not cause extinction of the species.
- **Recovery approach:** The Service adopted a [[Section 10(j) Experimental Population]] strategy for reestablishment in the Pecos River and lower Rio Grande, arguing that critical habitat designation would undermine the flexibility of 10(j) status.
- **Economic impact:** Estimated potential economic effects range from $1.9 to $16.2 million annually. The rule was certified as not having a significant effect on a substantial number of small entities, since the projected share of affected small businesses is well below the 20 percent threshold.
- **Regulatory context:** Critical habitat does not create a refuge, does not impose section 9 take prohibitions beyond those already in place, and applies only to federal actions. Consultation under section 7 is required for federally funded or authorized projects.
- **Public comment:** Over 40 comment letters were received on the proposed rule, and the Service addressed comments on water supply, flood control, Tribal sovereignty, economic methods and the mapping precision of constituent elements.
- **Specific Pueblo-related outcomes:** The Service concluded that excluding the Pueblo lands would not result in extinction, and noted that Pueblo cooperation is essential for surveys, water quality sampling and augmentation protocols.
