# Lower Colorado River Basin Fisheries Management Plan for Indigenous Big-River Fishes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae #endangered-species #recovery-planning #habitat-management

## Executive Summary

This preliminary draft management plan (December 1994) addresses the conservation and recovery of four federally endangered "big-river" fish species in the [[Lower Colorado River Basin]]: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), [[Bonytail]] (*Gila elegans*), and [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*). Prepared by the Arizona Fishery Resource Office for the U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service Southwest Region, the plan proposes integrated management actions to stabilize and maintain populations until historic habitats can be recovered. The approach differs from upper basin recovery plans by emphasizing refugia development, captive propagation, genetic management, and ecosystem-level habitat improvement to address recruitment failure caused by habitat alteration and non-indigenous species predation.

## Background & Description

The [[Colorado River]] historically flowed 650,000 square kilometers from Rocky Mountain headwaters (4000 m elevation) through extreme desert environments to the Gulf of California, experiencing torrential spring floods from snowmelt followed by reduced warm summer flows. The river comprised slow-moving turbid valleys alternating with canyon rapids—conditions to which native fishes adapted. Under these extreme conditions evolved four large, unique freshwater fishes: [[Colorado Squawfish]] (the largest North American cyprinid, a raptorial predator with specialized pharyngeal teeth), [[Razorback Sucker]] (distinctive with pronounced dorsal keel), [[Bonytail]] (streamlined cyprinid), and [[Humpback Chub]] (with characteristic nuchal hump).

All four species are federally listed as endangered. Three have designated critical habitat downstream from [[Glen Canyon Dam]]; [[Colorado Squawfish]] has no designated critical habitat in the lower basin, having been extirpated in the 1960s. The lower basin encompasses the Colorado mainstem and Paria, Little Colorado, Virgin, Bill Williams, and Gila River sub-basins and tributaries across Arizona, California, Nevada, New Mexico, Sonora, and Baja California Norte.

Current status by species:
- **Colorado Squawfish**: Represented only by captive population at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] and "experimental, nonessential" populations in Salt and Verde Rivers (Gila River sub-basin). The plan recommends changing designation to "experimental, essential" to allow inclusion in recovery planning.
- **Bonytail**: Occur infrequently in [[Lake Mohave]] and [[Lake Havasu]]. Captive populations maintained at Dexter and [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]].
- **Humpback Chub**: Occur in [[Little Colorado River]] at confluence with Colorado River. Management partially addressed by [[Grand Canyon Protection Act]] of 1993 and biological opinion on Glen Canyon Dam operations.
- **Razorback Sucker**: Largest population in Lake Mohave; population of undetermined size in [[Lake Mead]]; individuals captured occasionally in Grand Canyon area and downstream from [[Davis Dam]]. Becoming rare rapidly. Captive population at Dexter.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The Colorado River now flows through some of the most altered ecosystems in North America. Human development (hydroelectric power generation, irrigation, livestock grazing) has fundamentally altered watershed ecology. Declines in big-river fishes are attributed to:

1. **Habitat Loss and Modification**: Dams block historic movement corridors, create long dry reaches, release water too cold for spawning requirements, and remove natural hydrologic variance from the system. Torrential floods alternating with drought—conditions to which indigenous taxa were adapted—no longer occur.

2. **Non-Indigenous Species**: Predation on eggs, fry, and juveniles by introduced fishes identified as major depletion factor. Competition from non-native species further stresses populations.

3. **Recruitment Failure**: Current populations show little to no natural recruitment, with aging cohorts not being replaced.

The lower basin differs from upper basin in having larger population sizes for bonytail, humpback chub, and razorback sucker, and relative stabilization in development (most dam sites already used, few new projects anticipated), providing a more stable planning base. However, free-flowing river reaches are fewer and less extensive than in upper basin.

Genetic information exists for all species but requires expansion. Studies document: biochemical genetics of Colorado squawfish (Ammerman and Morizot 1989), bonytail broodstock allozyme variation (Minckley et al. 1989), humpback chub genetics (DeMarais et al. 1992; Dowling and DeMarais 1993), and razorback sucker population differentiation (Buth et al. 1987; Dowling and Minckley 1993).

## Key Findings

**Management Goals and Objectives:**
The plan establishes hierarchical objectives: (1) Prevent extinction by securing reproductive adults and confirming survival of young to maturity; (2) Perpetuate existing genetic variability through genetic and broodstock management; (3) Stabilize populations by developing habitats of sufficient quality; (4) Expand populations by restoring natural habitats and dispersal corridors; (5) Achieve self-sustaining populations with natural recruitment; (6) Down-list or de-list species.

**Primary Management Actions:**

1. **Riverine Cove Refugia**: Develop and manage selected river coves as protected habitats on [[Lower Colorado River National Wildlife Refuges]] (Bill Williams River, Cibola, Havasu, Imperial NWRs). Create fish exclusion barriers in designated critical habitat (less than 5.0 ha surface area), remove resident fishes with piscicides, stock one or more target species. When fish reach approximately 300 mm total length, use to augment existing populations, re-introduce elsewhere, or maintain as gene pool reserve. Plan calls for implementation within 3 years beginning on NWRs.

2. **Large Isolated Habitats**: Plan and implement creation of large refugia (80 ha or larger) on NWRs within 5 years, including renovation of existing waters and construction of new habitats managed using ecosystem concept. Expand within 10 years to other federal lands and targeted recovery areas. Modify habitats to encourage reproduction and serve as sources for re-introduction.

3. **Captive Propagation Facilities**: 
   - [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery and Technology Center]]: Currently holds broodfish of bonytails, razorback suckers, and Colorado squawfish. Should develop captive humpback chub population, maintain broodfish for genetically representative progeny, serve as refugia, and increase production capabilities for all four species.
   - [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]]: Currently rearing razorback suckers with bonytail refugia population. Should maintain broodfish, serve as refugia, and increase production for all four species.
   - [[Bubbling Ponds State Fish Hatchery]]: Has potential to assist Dexter as culture facility.

4. **Genetic Management Plan**:
   - Establish gene bank with cryopreserved gametes from at least 100 individuals of each species. Some methodology exists for razorback sucker and bonytail sperm cryopreservation (Tiersch et al. 1994).
   - Characterize all gamete donors through genomic genetic analyses to ensure no hybridization.
   - Conduct genomic studies on all populations to verify lack of contamination, develop protocols to minimize inbreeding and genetic drift, set acceptable variability levels for broodfish.
   - Secure broodfish and germplasm representative of genome as determined by management team.
   - Use non-invasive tissue sampling when possible after initial characterizations.
   - Integrate closely with upper basin genetic efforts.

5. **Alternative Collection Methods**: Test collection of river-spawned razorback sucker larvae using positive phototactic response. Collect at different times during extended breeding season at various Lake Mohave locations to obtain genetic diversity. Acknowledge experimental weaknesses: unknown number and genetic makeup of contributing parents, potential environmental impacts on physiological development, behavioral alterations from relocation, limited fish health information from small specimens. Sample all survivors to 300 mm for genetic composition, health diagnoses, and performance characteristics before release.

6. **Reservoir Stocking Strategy**: Take advantage of rare reservoir drainage events (maintenance, drought) to stock adult and larval fish coincidentally with refilling, exploiting: absence of predators allowing recruitment, rapid growth potential and longevity creating long-lived populations even if later recruitment suppressed, expanded population size allowing natural selection on abundant gene pool. Provides protection against delays in other management actions.

7. **Fish Health Monitoring**: Examine resident fishes in natural and semi-natural habitats for health problems prior to renovation and stocking. Monitor health of indigenous fish in hatcheries and stocked populations annually and prior to site-to-site transfers to minimize disease transfer.

8. **Supporting Studies**:
   - Survey habitats to determine refugia population status and production needs
   - Conduct life history evaluations related to competitors and predators
   - Study hydrographs and controlled water releases for potential benefits
   - Locate suitable habitats for additional populations
   - Determine relationships among fishery community members

9. **Management Team and Coordination**: Establish management team and coordinator to advocate, coordinate, and implement plan. Team will make fisheries management recommendations, consolidate best available information, develop cooperative ventures among stakeholders, coordinate with upper basin representatives, develop and modify objectives as implementation proceeds, work with USFWS ecosystem representatives, and consist of scientists and managers from Federal, State, and private organizations chosen for species knowledge, ecosystem expertise, and understanding of user needs.

**Partnerships**: Plan emphasizes developing diverse partnerships for success, including Federal agencies (Bureau of Reclamation, Bureau of Land Management, Bureau of Indian Affairs, Fish & Wildlife Service divisions, Forest Service, National Park Service), State agencies (Arizona, New Mexico, Nevada, California game and fish departments), Indian tribes (White Mountain Apache, San Carlos Apache, Hualapai, Hopi, Navajo, Colorado River Tribes, others), academia (Arizona State University, Northern Arizona University, University of Arizona, others), and non-governmental organizations (American Rivers, Desert Fishes Council, The Nature Conservancy, Sierra Club).

**Rationale Differences from Upper Basin**: This plan differs from upper basin recovery plans by emphasizing ecosystem-level habitat improvement, development of refugia, and circumvention of recruitment failure as principal recovery means, justified by larger lower-basin population sizes and relative stabilization in development providing stable planning base.

**Evaluation**: All management actions will be closely tracked, monitored, and evaluated as integral component of all endangered aquatic species study plans.

**Additional Taxa**: Appendix A lists additional indigenous fishes in five bio-regions of lower Colorado River basin requiring future conservation attention, including Apache trout, Gila trout, Virgin chub, various spinedace species, multiple sucker species, desert pupfish, Gila topminnow, and others with varying listing status (Endangered, Threatened, Candidate 2, Unlisted).
