# Case Study: Colorado River Squawfish Recovery and Reintroduction Efforts

#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae #endangered-species #conservation-biology

## Executive Summary

This case study examines the conservation status, recovery efforts, and reintroduction programs for the [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*), the largest cyprinid in North America. Once a dominant predator throughout the [[Colorado River]] system, the species declined dramatically from the 1930s to 1970, becoming extirpated from the lower basin and severely diminished in the upper basin. Listed as endangered in 1967, the species now numbers approximately 9,000 adults (circa 1999), with ~8,000 inhabiting the [[Green River]]. The primary causes of decline were dam construction (1956-1963) and introduction of over 44 non-native fish species. Recovery efforts involve a complex institutional framework including the [[Recovery Program for the Endangered Fish of the Upper Colorado River Basin]] (established 1987), with a $60 million investment over 15 years focused on flow management, habitat restoration, captive breeding at [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]], and strategic reintroductions. The document emphasizes that without major habitat restoration, reintroduction efforts serve primarily as stopgap measures, and that habitat improvement may be more effective than reintroduction alone.

## Background & Description

### Species Characteristics
The [[Colorado Squawfish]] is a long-lived cyprinid (7-30 years lifespan) that historically dominated the mainstem [[Colorado River]] and its tributaries as the top predator. The species exhibits potamodromy, migrating upstream or downstream to spawning grounds that can be over 100 km distant. Tyus (1990) suspects fidelity to specific spawning areas, suggesting the existence of different genetic strains in different rivers.

### Historical Status and Decline
In the early 1930s, the species was still abundant throughout the Colorado River system. By 1970, it was extirpated from the lower [[Colorado River]] drainage and severely diminished in the upper Colorado. The chief agents of decline were:

1. **Dam Construction**: Several large dams constructed 1956-1963 for power generation, recreation, and irrigation
2. **Non-native Species**: Introduction of over 44 species of non-native fish

### Legal Protection
- Listed in 1967 as endangered under the 1966 Endangered Species Preservation Act
- Received additional protection with passage of the Endangered Species Act in 1973
- Initial Colorado Squawfish Recovery Plan completed in 1978
- Recovery planning coordinated with three other endangered Colorado fishes: [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), and [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)

### Current Distribution (as of late 1990s)
- **Extirpated**: Lower Colorado River
- **Most abundant**: [[Green River]] below [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] (~8,000 adults)
- **Persistent populations**: Upper Colorado River mainstem, [[Yampa River]], [[Gunnison River]], [[White River]], [[San Juan River]], [[Price River]]
- All populations have reproduced in the past 30 years

## Ecological & Environmental Context

### Habitat Requirements and Dam Impacts

Dams affected the [[Colorado Squawfish]] through multiple mechanisms:

1. **Habitat Inundation**: Spawning areas and other important habitats were flooded by reservoirs (Tyus 1992)
2. **Migration Barriers**: Prevented migration to spawning grounds (Wydoski and Hamill 1991; Tyus 1992)
3. **Thermal Regime Alteration**: Periodic discharge of cold water reduced hatching and survivorship of larvae (Marsh 1985; Childs and Clarkson 1996; Lupher and Clarkson 1995)
4. **Flow Regime Changes**: Reduced severity and duration of flooding that maintained optimal habitat for all life stages (Milhous 1998)
5. **Spawning Flow Reduction**: Reduced flow during critical spawning period (mid-June to early August) (Milhous 1998)

### Non-native Species Impacts

The second major factor in decline was introduced non-native fish, especially cyprinids that:
- Enter critical backwater nursery sites
- Prey on young squawfish
- Prevent recruitment of larvae and juveniles into adult age classes (Thiede et al. 1996; Marsh 1985; Tyus 1991)

The altered hydrological regime may favor non-native species by creating flow regimes and water temperatures resembling their native environmental conditions (Clarkson et al. 1995).

### Chemical Contamination

Chemical contamination primarily from returning irrigation water to tributaries may adversely affect younger life stages (Hamilton and Buhl 1997; Buhl and Hamilton 1996).

### Critical Habitat Designation

The Recovery Program identified 159 km of the Colorado River system as "extremely important" to the survival of the squawfish and other endangered fish.

## Key Findings

### Institutional Framework and Funding

**Major Institutions:**

1. **[[Desert Fishes Council]]** (formed 1969): Advocacy and research group following symposium by American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists; convenes annual symposium and publishes proceedings online

2. **[[Colorado River Fish Project]]** (established 1979): Research consortium funded partly by U.S. Bureau of Reclamation to gather information for recovery planning; 1981 symposium summarized 1975-1981 research results

3. **Windy Gap Process** (1981): USFWS initiative extracting $1.3 million from private water development projects for endangered fish research, including captive rearing and reintroduction; abandoned due to controversy in favor of Interagency Coordinating Committee

4. **[[Recovery Program for the Endangered Fish of the Upper Colorado River Basin]]** (formed 1987): Implemented under Memorandum of Understanding signed by Secretary of Interior, governors of Colorado, Wyoming, and Utah, and Western Area Power Administration administrator

**Recovery Program Budget and Activities:**
- $60 million investment over 15 years
- FY94 budget: $7.7 million ($4.4 million for floodplain restoration, fish passage construction, hatchery facility planning, water management)
- Congressional appropriations: $1 million (1988) for in-stream flow rights; over $10 million (1993) for recovery plan implementation

**Five-Point Recovery Strategy:**
1. Modify dam management procedures and purchase water rights for in-stream flow
2. Develop and manage endangered fish habitats
3. Produce hatchery-reared fish for research and reintroductions
4. Restrict stocking of problematic non-native fish and eliminate angling pressure
5. Conduct research and monitoring to improve and assess recovery activities

**Recovery Program Members:** USFWS, USBR, Western Area Power Administration, states of Colorado, Wyoming and Utah, environmental groups, water users, Colorado River Energy Distributors Association

### Flow Management Recommendations

**Conservative Fisheries Biologist Recommendations:**
- Pre-dam peak flows with minimum daily flow fluctuations <5% in volume for Colorado and most tributaries (Stanford 1994)
- [[Gunnison River]]: minimum flow of 300 cubic feet per second (8.5 cubic meters per second) establishing 1-foot depth in main channel (Burdick 1997)

**Milhous (1998) Sediment-Based Recommendations for Gunnison River:**
- **Maintenance flows**: 210 cubic meters per second during spawning period
- **Flushing flows**: 210-484 cubic meters per second for:
  - Scouring nursery areas in side channels (210 cms)
  - Removing sand and fine particles from spawning riffles (355 cms)
  - Removing gravel from spawning male resting pools (484 cms)

These sediment-based recommendations vastly exceed the more conservative fisheries biology recommendations.

### Experimental Flow Releases

**[[Glen Canyon Dam]] Experiment (1996):**
- Early spring experimental release averaging 8,000 cubic feet per second (227 cubic meters per second) before and after flood peak
- Preliminary results: native species not affected, densities of some exotic species below dam reduced (Hoffnagle and Persons 1997)

### Habitat Restoration Accomplishments

1. **Fish Passage**: $1 million fish ladder completed around [[Redlands Diversion Dam]] on lower [[Gunnison River]]; within one year, squawfish migrated into 50-mile section where considered extirpated (Anonymous 1996; Pfeifer 1997)

2. **Non-native Species Control**: 
   - Mechanical removal tested in three rivers (Pfeifer 1997)
   - Spring flooding in [[San Juan River]] temporarily depressed non-native fish populations (Pfeifer 1996)

3. **Policy Agreements**: "Interim procedures for stocking nonnative fish species in the Upper Colorado River Basin" (Hamill 1995; Pfeifer 1997)

4. **Critical Habitat**: Designation completed for squawfish and three other endangered Colorado fishes (Hamill 1995)

5. **Floodplain Restoration**: Pilot research and restoration projects initiated (Pfeifer 1996; Pfeifer 1997)

6. **In-stream Flow Rights**: Petitioning underway (Hamill 1995)

7. **Dam Relicensing Research**: Research initiated to guide USFWS Biological Opinions for relicensing three large dams (Pfeifer 1996; Pfeifer 1997)

### Captive Breeding Program

**[[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]** (primary facility):
- Established in 1930s originally for warm-water non-native sport fish
- Techniques for squawfish breeding developed by modifying existing non-native fish techniques
- 75 adult fish removed from wild 1973-1988 for broodstock (Johnson and Jensen 1991)
- Fewer than 50 adults used as broodstock; electrophoretic analysis confirmed juveniles adequately represented Green and Upper Colorado River populations
- Wild fish used as broodstock when possible; F₁ fish used when more offspring needed

**Production Numbers:**
- By 1977: 600,000 larvae per year produced via hormonal induction and hand stripping (Philippart 1996)
- 1987: 380,000 fish produced; over 275,000 sent to other hatcheries for testing, growth, and wild stocking (Johnson and Jensen 1991)

**Breeding Goals:**
1. Preserve genetic variety of wild fish
2. Grow fish for research
3. Reintroduce squawfish to extirpated streams (Pfeifer 1996)

### Reintroduction Efforts

**Legal Framework:**
- Reintroductions generally limited to reaches where squawfish absent
- Fish designated as "nonessential experimental" under section 10(j) of Endangered Species Act, therefore not provided full protection (Brooks 1986)
- Some reintroductions also conducted in reaches where already present (Tyus 1991)

**Major Reintroduction Events:**

1. **[[Salt River]] and [[Verde River]]** (1985, 1987):
   - First large-scale reintroduction near Mexican border in lower Colorado drainage
   - 1985: 96,000 fry and 442 small fish (35-40 cm) released; surveys through January 1986 found surviving fish healthy (Brooks 1986)
   - 1987: 100,000 75mm fry released (Philippart 1996; Johnson and Jensen 1991)
   - Concern: high numbers of predatory non-native fish posed threat to long-term survival (Johnson and Jensen 1991)

2. **Upper Colorado River Mainstem** (1980-1984):
   - Both juvenile fish (6-year-old) and small fry released
   - Some larger fish recaptured 1980-1988 (Tyus 1991)

3. **[[Kenny Reservoir]], [[White River]]** (1988-1990):
   - Over 96,000 fingerlings released
   - Squawfish rapidly disappeared from reservoir
   - Technique not recommended for mitigating impoundment impacts (Trammel et al. 1993)

4. **[[San Juan River]]** (1996-1997):
   - Experimental reintroduction and recapture of 200,000 hatchery-reared young-of-the-year
   - Purpose: determine limiting stages in life history to develop flow recommendations for [[Navajo Dam]] (Converse et al. 1999)

5. **[[Dolores River]]**: Reintroduction proposed by Masslich and Valdez (1992)

**Success Criteria:**
Research scientists and fisheries biologists believe reintroduction will be proven successful when released fishes spawn and produce young. Ultimate evidence of success depends on finding that numbers of larval and juvenile squawfish increase over time and that recruitment to adult stage occurs (Stanford 1994). USFWS has sampling regime sensitive enough to detect these increases (Stanford 1994).

### Major Obstacles to Success

**Water Rights and Development Pressure:**
- The [[Colorado River]] described as "the most heavily used, controlled, and fought-over river in the world" (Wydoski and Hamill 1991)
- Existing laws protect only minimal flow, insufficient for recovery and delisting (Tyus 1992)
- Powerful development interests compete for water to expand residential, commercial, and agricultural areas
- Approximately 30 million people depend on power and water supplied by Colorado River basin dams

**Dam Management Opposition:**
- Large dams (1956-1963) have highly influential, well-organized supporters
- Opposition to necessary changes in dam management greatly slows progress
- Lack of adequate flow models to predict timing, duration, and volume of water releases needed (Stanford 1994)

**Recreational Fishing Conflicts:**
- Dam impoundments spurred economic boom in recreational sport-fishing
- Restoring natural flow regime would likely cause significant decline in "angler welfare" related to reservoir fishing, not immediately compensated by better river fishing (Schmidt and Steward 1998)
- Cold dam tailwaters created trout fishery with many supporters

### Critical Assessment and Conclusions

**Habitat Restoration as Primary Need:**
- "Captive propagation is not an end in itself, but rather a means of perpetuating species until suitable habitats can be found, rebuilt, or created" (Johnson and Jensen 1991)
- "Habitat loss was presumably the prime factor associated with extirpation of squawfish in the lower basin, and if habitats have not improved, reintroductions are at best a stopgap measure" (Tyus 1991)
- Without major habitat restoration, reintroduction efforts may not result in recruitment of adults, successful spawning, and perpetuation of new generations
- If conditions returned to pre-dam normalcy, Colorado squawfish might recover on its own (Osmundson and Burnham 1997)

**Alternative Strategies:**
- Incremental improvements in stream condition within reaches where squawfish already survives may be more effective than reintroduction in expanding total population
- May be necessary to create habitats isolated from mainstem Colorado, remove non-native fish, and reintroduce squawfish and other endangered fishes into these reaches (Minckley, personal communication)

**Optimal Role for Captive Breeding:**
- Small space allocated to Colorado squawfish at hatcheries (which also breed many other endangered desert fishes) is not financial burden
- Much of recovery budget consumed by river management projects, not captive breeding
- Best focus: preservation of endangered genetic strains in different rivers and spawning areas until river habitat can be restored
- If dam relicensing creates favorable flow conditions in streams where squawfish numbers quite low (e.g., [[San Juan River]]), reintroduction may be prudent to rapidly build up numbers
- Reintroduction provides invaluable research opportunities into life history requirements and causes of extinction, assisting conservation of wild populations (Minckley 1995)

**Information Dissemination:**
- [[Desert Fishes Council]] annual symposium with proceedings published online
- Annual research meeting in upper basin since 1983 involving federal, state, academic, and private-sector scientists
