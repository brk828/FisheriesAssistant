# Glen Canyon Dam Discharge Temperature Control Draft Appraisal Report

#ichthyology #native-fish #endangered-species #dam-operations #temperature-control #humpback-chub

## Executive Summary

This draft appraisal report, dated June 14, 1994, evaluates methods to study and implement temperature controls at [[Glen Canyon Dam]] for the recovery of native and endangered fishes below the dam. Cold water discharges (8-10°C year-round) from the dam's deep hypolimnetic releases severely limit main channel spawning of native fishes, particularly the endangered [[Humpback Chub]], which currently spawns primarily in the warm [[Little Colorado River]] tributary. The report proposes retrofitting the dam with a selective level withdrawal structure to allow warmer water releases (up to 21°C in summer), scaled from the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] retrofit at approximately $60 million construction cost, with an additional $15 million for planning studies, post-construction testing, and NEPA compliance. Authority exists under Section 8 of the [[Colorado River Storage Project Act]] (CRSP Act), which authorizes facilities "to mitigate losses of, and improved conditions for, the propagation of fish and wildlife." The report recommends a phased approach with accelerated planning, potentially using an Environmental Assessment/Finding of No Significant Impact (EA/FONSI) for construction of a "test facility," followed by post-construction monitoring to measure actual impacts and optimize temperature release patterns through adaptive management.

## Background & Description

[[Glen Canyon Dam]] stores and releases water from [[Lake Powell]], which has an active capacity of almost 25 million acre-feet. The powerplant has a release capacity of 33,200 cfs. Deep penstocks draw water from approximately 200 feet below the surface, creating year-round cold releases of 8-10°C (46-48°F). Pre-dam river temperatures ranged from just above freezing to about 30°C. Current releases warm longitudinally downstream to about 16°C at 240 miles below the dam, but these temperatures remain insufficient for native fish spawning, which requires 16-23°C.

The proposed selective level withdrawal structure would install control gates over the power penstock intakes on the dam face, allowing operators to draw water from warmer surface layers during summer months. Computer modeling by Ferrari (1987) predicted that multi-level intakes could increase discharge temperatures by up to 10°C (18°F) depending on season. The structure design is scaled from the successful [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] retrofit completed under the same CRSP Act Section 8 authority.

Alternative approaches were evaluated but found less feasible: non-structural re-operation alternatives would eliminate power production and water storage; using waste heat from [[Navajo Powerplant]] would be extremely expensive and inflexible; and maintaining reservoir levels to discharge warm water from existing outlets would have severe operational impacts.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The affected area encompasses the [[Colorado River]] corridor in northern Arizona from [[Lake Powell]] through Glen and Grand Canyons to [[Lake Mead]], nearly 300 miles. The cold water releases have created an excellent blue ribbon trout fishery below the dam but prevent native fish from thriving and spawning in the mainstem.

The endangered [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*) currently spawns almost exclusively in the [[Little Colorado River]], a warm unregulated tributary located 77 miles below the dam. This extreme habitat limitation makes the population vulnerable to catastrophic events. Other native species affected include [[Flannelmouth Sucker]], [[Bluehead Sucker]], [[Speckled Dace]], and [[Razorback Sucker]], all requiring 16-23°C for successful spawning, egg incubation, and larval survival.

The existing post-impoundment aquatic system has developed around near year-round releases of 8-10°C water with low nutrient and sediment levels. [[Lake Powell]] is very efficient at retaining nutrients through biological processes and settling, resulting in extremely low nitrogen and phosphorus levels in deep water near the dam, and even lower levels near the surface where withdrawals would occur.

Water quality considerations include: dissolved oxygen levels currently mildly undersaturated (about 5 mg/L) in summer from deep releases; surface withdrawal would increase summer DO but potentially decrease it to critically low levels (2-4 mg/L) during later cold water releases unless operations are carefully managed. Salinity levels in the surface layer are lowest in the lake; summer surface releases would reduce salinity somewhat but not approach pre-dam levels or impact fish and wildlife.

## Key Findings

**Authority and Funding**: Section 8 of the [[Colorado River Storage Project Act]] provides clear authority to investigate, plan, construct, operate, and maintain facilities to mitigate losses and improve conditions for fish and wildlife propagation. This authority was successfully used for the [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] selective withdrawal retrofit. Section 8 funds are non-reimbursable under the CRSP Act. However, Section 12 appropriation ceiling increases would be needed before major construction, as most of the authorized ceiling for [[Glen Canyon Dam]] has been consumed.

**Cost Estimates**: Preliminary total costs estimated at $75 million, including approximately $60 million for selective level withdrawal structure construction (scaled and indexed from [[Flaming Gorge]] costs), and $15 million or more for planning studies, post-construction testing, and NEPA compliance.

**Native and Endangered Fish Benefits**: Temperature modification is identified as the only way to alleviate the known restriction by cold water temperatures to successful mainstem reproduction of native fishes. Releases of 15°C in June and July should warm to 19-20°C (optimum for spawning, incubation, and rearing) in the mainstem reach beginning approximately 200 km below the dam. Valdez concluded that warming would provide favorable thermal conditions and that other environmental factors (life history requirements, habitat limitations) would likely prevent problematic invasion by non-native warm water competitors from [[Lake Mead]].

**Trout Fishery Impacts**: The existing cold water trout fishery below the dam operates at slightly below optimal temperatures. Moderate warming (5-6°C) would likely benefit the [[Lee's Ferry]] tailwater fishery by providing more optimal temperatures for rainbow trout, cutthroat trout, brook trout, and brown trout growth rates. Extremely warm releases could damage this resource, but proposed release temperatures are only 5-6°C warmer than current conditions.

**Competition and Non-Native Species**: Major concerns include potential invasion by warm water competitors from [[Lake Mead]], particularly [[Channel Catfish]] which prey upon and compete with native species and have been observed feeding on chub at the mouth of the [[Little Colorado River]]. However, the report notes that [[Lake Mead]] and its warm water fishery existed before [[Glen Canyon Dam]] cooled river temperatures, and chub successfully competed with warm water predators then. Other non-native species ([[Carp]], [[Fathead Minnows]], [[Red Shiners]], [[Striped Bass]]) are expected to have limited impacts due to habitat requirements, current velocity intolerance, or existing distribution patterns. Cold water releases could be used strategically (1-in-5 year warm releases for chub spawning) if competition becomes problematic.

**Lake Powell Fishery**: [[Threadfin Shad]], an important forage fish for the lake game fishery, already experience occasional winterkill from low reservoir temperatures. Warm water releases would decrease winter lake temperatures, potentially aggravating this problem. This could be mitigated by stocking more cold-tolerant species, though this would violate the current Fish and Wildlife Service moratorium on introducing new exotic species.

**Primary Productivity Concerns**: Surface withdrawals may decrease nutrients discharged to the downstream environment, potentially reducing productivity in the river system and [[Lake Mead]]. However, surface discharge may also increase export of algae and small organisms from [[Lake Powell]]'s surface layer, somewhat offsetting nutrient loss by supplementing the food base. The proposed small temperature change (5-6°C) should not impact the aquatic system to any great extent.

**Parasites and Disease**: Two parasites of concern are Asian tapeworm (found in [[Humpback Chub]] in Grand Canyon) and anchor worm (common in native and non-native fishes in the Upper Colorado River Basin). Valdez concluded that egg maturation of Asian tapeworm would probably not occur in the mainstem even under temperature augmentation, though temperatures would be suitable for tapeworm survival. Effects of anchor worms on fishes appear not to lead to significant mortalities.

**Lake Mead Impacts**: Warmer summer inflows would lighten the water entering [[Lake Mead]], increasing nutrient loading in the inflow zone and potentially increasing primary productivity in Boulder Bay, a relatively poor productivity area. Summer surface temperatures at [[Lake Mead]] are usually above 20°C while current inflows are about 16°C; existing inflows flow under the reservoir's surface layer, making nutrients less available to algae. Any small evaporation savings at [[Lake Powell]] would likely be offset by increased evaporation at [[Lake Mead]].

**Planning Approach Recommendations**: The report strongly recommends a phased approach recognizing that "it is unreasonable to expect scientists to be able to predict in great detail what could be measured with a test facility." The Glen Canyon Selective Level Withdrawal Work Group observed that "in almost every case, additional effects occurred (from temperature controls) that were either unpredicted or resulted in changes greater or lesser than anticipated." 

Three planning options are presented:
- **Option 1**: Traditional sequential planning and construction (slowest, 8+ years longer)
- **Option 2**: Phased with EA/FONSI on test facility construction (fastest, recognizes that construction itself causes no impacts)
- **Option 3**: Phased with PR/EIS on test facility (intermediate approach)

The report recommends Option 2 or 3, noting that the critical NEPA issue is not construction of the facility but its operation. An EA/FONSI could evaluate the potential range of impacts from operation while allowing rapid construction. Post-construction testing would directly measure impacts of various operational scenarios, and a full planning report/EIS would then select the recommended operation based on known, measured impacts rather than predictions.

**Adaptive Management**: The report proposes an adaptive management strategy to optimize release temperatures after construction, similar to recovery programs elsewhere in the Basin. Phase 2 post-construction studies would carefully monitor impacts to native fishes, non-native competition, primary productivity, trout fishery, lake fisheries, water quality, and other resources to determine optimum release patterns.

**Schedule Acceleration**: Using "parallel" rather than "sequential" scheduling could potentially reduce the study/construction schedule by 8 years. This approach assumes legislation, construction funding, and design work can be completed concurrent with preparation of the planning report/EIS. The critical path goes through PR/EIS, Record of Decision, construction, and into testing and further NEPA compliance.

**Precedents**: The [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] retrofit provides a strong precedent. An EA/Determination of No Significant Impact was completed in 1976 for outflow temperature controls, with few negative impacts expected. The operation continues to evolve through post-construction monitoring. Similarly, [[Shasta Dam]] Outflow Temperature Control (1991) and [[Hungry Horse Dam]] Selective Withdrawal System (1994 draft EA) provide recent examples of similar projects.

**Expert Consensus**: Clarkson (Arizona Game and Fish), Gorman (Fish and Wildlife Service), Kubly (Arizona Game and Fish), Marsh (Arizona State University), and Valdez (Bio/West Inc.) concluded in their 1994 report that "Water temperature modification and sediment augmentation are additional elements likely required to complete the restoration process" and that temperature modification "is the only way to alleviate the known restriction by cold water temperatures to successful mainstem reproduction of native fishes."
