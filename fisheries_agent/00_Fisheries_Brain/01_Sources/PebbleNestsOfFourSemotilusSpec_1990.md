#ichthyology #freshwater-biology #Cyprinidae

## Executive Summary
This study by Maurakis, Woolcott, and Magee (1990) provides the first quantitative comparison of pebble-nest construction across four species of the genus *Semotilus* (Cyprinidae): the pit/mound-building [[Semotilus Corporalis]] (fallfish), and three closely related pit/ridge-building taxa — [[Semotilus Atromaculatus]] (creek chub), [[Semotilus Lumbee]] (Sandhills chub), and an undescribed diminutive form, [[Semotilus Sp.]] (tentatively linked to *Semotilus thoreauianus*). Nests were collected from streams in Virginia, Maryland, North Carolina, and Georgia (1982–1988) and analyzed for pebble size composition, nest dimensions, stream characteristics, and near-bed water current dynamics. *S. corporalis* built significantly larger pit/mound nests in wider, deeper streams, while the three smaller species built structurally similar pit/ridge nests in comparable, smaller stream habitats. Electivity index analysis showed no significant difference among species in selection of the three largest pebble size classes (6.0, 11.3, 23.0 mm), and ridge/mound morphology functioned as a hydraulic breakwater, substantially reducing current velocity in the downstream spawning pit — a mechanism thought to facilitate egg and milt deposition and enhance interstitial aeration.

## Background & Description
- **Body size differences among species** (correlated with nest size):
  - *S. corporalis*: largest species, SL 128.9–265.7 mm
  - *S. lumbee*: intermediate, SL 129.1–185 mm
  - *S. atromaculatus*: SL 130–145.2 mm
  - *Semotilus sp.*: smallest, SL 67.1–67.5 mm (~half the length of adult male *S. atromaculatus*)
- **Taxonomic comparison** (Table 1): *Semotilus sp.* vs. *Semotilus thoreauianus* (Jordan 1877) — both robust, short-bodied, short round head, 8 anterior-bilobed tubercles, dorsal fin rays = 8; differences noted in anal fin rays (8 vs. 7), pelvic fin length (reaches vent vs. short of vent), and lateral line scale counts (LL 49.3 [48–51] vs. 48; above LL 9.2 [9–10] vs. 9; below LL 6 vs. 5).
- **Eggs**: Nonadhesive, demersal eggs found in nests of all four species.
- Reproductive males exhibit nest-building behavior with genus-specific structural features: a **pit** excavated at the downstream portion of the nest, restricted to members of the genus *Semotilus*.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Habitat**: All species nest at the heads of riffles in clear montane, Piedmont, or upper Coastal Plain streams.
- **Stream dimensions** (significant interspecific differences):
  - Depth: *Semotilus sp.* (12.5 cm) < *S. atromaculatus* (15.5 cm) < *S. lumbee* (20.7 cm) < *S. corporalis* (52.7 cm)
  - Width: *Semotilus sp.* (1.7 m) ≈ *S. lumbee* (1.9 m) < *S. atromaculatus* (2.7 m) < *S. corporalis* (5.7 m)
  - *S. corporalis* occupies significantly larger, deeper streams, consistent with [[Hynes 1979]]'s principle that larger fish occupy larger streams.
- **Water temperature at active nests**: not significantly different among species (mean range 13.2–15.0°C; overall range 10–17°C).
- **Spawning timing**: *Semotilus* spp. are among the earliest spring spawners compared to other pebble-nesting cyprinid genera (e.g., [[Exoglossum]], [[Nocomis]]).
- **Flow microhabitat**: Mean stream flows were subcritical and turbulent (per [[Davis and Barmuta 1989]] classification). Nest ridges/mounds create "isolated roughness flow" (low k/L ratio), generating horseshoe vortices that produce a low-velocity refuge in the spawning pit — critical for facilitating vertical descent of gametes.
- Larger, mature *S. corporalis* mounds create a "chaotic flow" regime analogous to a shallow riffle due to increased mound height.

## Key Findings
- **Nest size**: *S. corporalis* pit/mound nests were significantly larger in mound width (60.9 cm), mound height (38.1 cm), pit depth (14.5 cm), and pit width (39.0 cm) than pit/ridge nests of the other three species. *S. lumbee* had the longest pits (36.3 cm) and longest ridges (96.2 cm, though not always significantly).
- **Pebble composition**:
  - ~83% of *S. corporalis* nest material was in the 23.0 mm (49.3%) and 11.3 mm (33.6%) size classes.
  - The three smaller species showed highest percentages in 11.3 mm and 6.0 mm classes (e.g., *S. lumbee*: 48.7% and 33.5%, respectively).
  - Substrate at *S. corporalis* nest sites was 61.1% composed of 23.0 mm pebbles — the highest substrate proportion of any species for that size class.
- **Electivity index (Ivlev 1961)**:
  - No significant interspecific difference in electivity for the three largest pebble classes (6.0, 11.3, 23.0 mm).
  - Despite *S. corporalis* nests being dominated by 23.0 mm pebbles, this size class did NOT show high electivity because it was also highly abundant in the ambient substrate — indicating non-selective incorporation rather than active preference.
  - *S. atromaculatus* and *S. lumbee* showed high electivity for 6.0–23.0 mm pebbles; *Semotilus sp.* was the only species to select pebbles <6.0 mm, and showed no significant electivity differences across the 0.8–23.0 mm range.
- **Hydraulics**: Average current velocity in the spawning pit was significantly lower than at all other nest locations (before ridge, over ridge, after pit) across all species (Table 10). Backward stepwise regression identified **stream depth, ridge length, and ridge height** as the three significant physical predictors of reduced pit current velocity.
- **Froude number classification**: Pit flows (x̄ = 0.015–0.035) were consistently lower than stream flows (x̄ = 0.074–0.111) or over-ridge/mound flows (x̄ = 0.120–0.394), confirming the pit as a hydraulically sheltered spawning microhabitat.
- **Near-bed flow ratios**: k/L and D/j ratios never exceeded 1.0 for any species; D/k ratio for *S. corporalis* (0.610) was markedly lower than for the other three species (4.334–5.595), reflecting proportionally taller mound structures relative to water depth.
- **Behavioral/evolutionary inference**: Structural similarity in pit/ridge nest architecture among the three creek-chub-type species ([[Semotilus Atromaculatus]], [[Semotilus Lumbee]], [[Semotilus Sp.]]) supports their proposed monophyly, distinct from the pit/mound strategy of [[Semotilus Corporalis]].
- Contrary to reports of communal mound-spawning in more northern *S. corporalis* populations ([[Ross and Reed 1978]]), Virginia populations showed eggs concentrated only in discrete areas at the downstream base of mature mounds, suggesting continued nest construction after spawning.
