# Colorado River Fish Monitoring Project, 1982-1985

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (April 1987) from the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS) Vernal Field Station summarizes field work on the endangered fishes of the upper [[Colorado River]] Basin, mainly in the [[Green River]] and its tributaries. The work was a continuation of the 1979-1981 Colorado River Fishes Project and was done for the [[Bureau of Reclamation]] (BR) under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Its main focus is the [[Colorado Squawfish]], with supporting studies of the [[Razorback Sucker]] and the [[Gila Complex]] (humpback chub, bonytail and roundtail chub).

The report identifies two confirmed squawfish spawning sites, in [[Yampa Canyon]] and [[Gray Canyon]], and describes long-distance spawning migrations by radiotelemetry. It links recruitment failure in 1983 and 1984 to high late-summer flows from [[Flaming Gorge Dam]]. The authors conclude that the absolute flow and temperature needs of the squawfish are still unknown, but that there is enough water to support the species if it is managed properly.

## Background & Description

**Study history.** The work grew out of the [[Colorado River Fishes Project]] (1979-1981). Its goals were to expand the information on endangered fish populations, to determine instream flow needs, and to identify spawning and nursery habitat. Related studies on the [[Yampa River]] and [[White River]] were run concurrently. In 1984 the FWS, BR and the states of Colorado, Utah and Wyoming formed the [[Upper Colorado River Basin Coordinating Committee]] (UCRBCC), which produced a set of [[Sensitive Areas]] for management. Objective 3 of the monitoring program (effects of [[Flaming Gorge Dam]] peaking flows on young squawfish) was dropped because flood conditions prevented the required experimental releases.

**Species focus.**
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*): the widest-ranging endangered fish in the upper basin and the main target species. The report notes that the rationale that squawfish needs would also cover other species has been abandoned.
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*), [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*) and [[Roundtail Chub]] (*Gila robusta*): the report says the bonytail was rarely studied because so little data existed.
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*): studied for life history and habitat, with a total of 323 fish captured in the upper Green River.

**Study area.** The Green River sub-basin covers about 116,000 km² (45,000 mi²) of the 284,000 km² upper basin. The primary study area runs from Echo Park (RM 345) in [[Dinosaur National Monument]] to the Green-Colorado confluence in [[Canyonlands National Park]] (RM 0). The Yampa River enters the Green at Echo Park and dominates spring-summer runoff. Its flow was above 10,000 cfs in June, and runoff in 1983 and 1984 exceeded 20,000 cfs. Water below Flaming Gorge Dam, built in 1963, dominates the fall-winter low flows.

**Habitat classes.** Field sampling used a standard classification of [[Main Channel]], [[Backwater]], [[Side Channel]], [[Eddy]], [[Pool]], [[Riffle]], [[Run]], [[Shoreline]] and [[Flooded Bottom]] habitats, plus sediment classes from silt to bedrock.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The report describes the basin as historically harsh: large seasonal flow swings, high turbidity, wide temperature ranges and high dissolved solids. The native fish fauna evolved under these conditions. Reservoirs, flow diversions, consumptive uses and non-native species have since changed the system. Fossil records of *Gila* and *Ptychocheilus* go back about 8 million years, and the system has been isolated for millions of years, which the report presents as a reason these fish are specially adapted to the basin.

**Sampling methods.** Key methods included:
- [[Spring Survey Program]] electrofishing, using a standardized gear and a boom-mounted boat rig, in Green, Yampa and White river strata (April 15 to June 1).
- [[Radiotelemetry]] of squawfish and razorback suckers, with transmitters of about 11 to 33 g, operating at 40.660-40.700 MHz, with a stated battery life of 6 to 18 months.
- [[Larval Drift]] sampling with 1 m² ichthyoplankton nets and seines, sampling larvae and young-of-the-year (YOY) fish.
- [[Incremental Stream Flow Methodology]] and [[PHABSIM]] modeling of spawning habitat.
- Tagging with Carlin dangler tags (tags numbered and color-coded for each river).

**Habitat and water conditions.** Adult squawfish use eddies and shoreline runs over sand and silt for most of the year. In 1983 and 1984, flooded shoreline areas held adults and juveniles and were warmer during daylight than the channel. Water temperatures during spawning were 25-27 °C in both the Yampa and Green rivers.

## Key Findings

**Population and abundance**
- 419 Colorado squawfish were captured in the Green and Duchesne rivers during the Spring Survey Program, 1980-1985: 329 adults (79%) and 90 juveniles (21%).
- The mean adult catch rate was 0.545 fish per hour. Juvenile catch rate averaged 0.14 fish per hour. Juveniles were most abundant in the lower Green River (Strata A and B).
- Adults were distributed almost equally across the upper river (less than 0.1 fish per hour difference between strata), so the UCRBCC high-concentration criteria were hard to apply there.

**Movement and migration**
- A total of 112 squawfish were radio-implanted and contacted in April and May, 1980-1985. Table 3 totals 123 fish, a discrepancy in the source.
- Movement classes: 40 migratory, 12 non-migratory, 37 partial migrants, 25 partial non-migrants and 9 lost. The authors state that 63% of adults tracked all season were highly mobile.
- Yampa spawning migrations covered 40-176 km (average 138 km). Some fish from the [[White River]] also used the lower Yampa Canyon.
- Fish showed upstream and downstream movement and fidelity to spawning sites. Two ripe fish tagged at the Yampa site in 1981 and one in 1982 returned to the same location.

**Recapture evidence**
- 101 previously tagged squawfish were recaptured. Of 37 fish captured both inside and outside spawning dates, 30 (81%) moved more than 32 km.
- Of 56 fish caught and recaptured only outside spawning periods, 47 (84%) were recaptured within 32 km of their original capture, which suggests home ranges.

**Spawning**
- Two major spawning areas were confirmed: lower [[Yampa Canyon]] (RM 0-20) and [[Gray Canyon]] (RM 145-160). Seven other areas were suspected.
- 1981-1985 collections yielded 208 adult squawfish, of which 160 (77%) had breeding tubercles. Of these, 120 ripe males averaged 559 mm TL and 7 ripe females averaged 710 mm TL. The authors attribute the low female count to difficulty in capture or lower abundance.
- Spawning took place on riffles adjacent to deeper runs and pools, in water about 1.8 m deep (Yampa pools averaged 2.1 m; deposition riffles averaged about 0.9 m).
- Spawning activity occurred at mean water temperatures of 22-24 °C, with declining discharge. The spawning period was about 3-4 weeks.
- The authors propose a [[Groundwater Seepage Hypothesis]] for homing, but this is not confirmed.

**Early life history and recruitment**
- Larvae drift downstream from spawning sites to nursery habitats. This is supported by peaks in abundance downstream of the Yampa and Gray Canyon spawning sites.
- In 1983 and 1984 the Green River had poor survival of YOY squawfish, although larval abundance was high.
- In August 1983 the temperature difference between the Yampa and Green rivers was 10 °C, versus 0-1.5 °C in 1979-1981. Laboratory work at Utah State University found larvae subjected to a 10 °C drop showed stress and became moribund.
- Larval catch per effort in backwaters was negatively correlated with mean August-September discharge at Jensen, Utah (r = -0.89, p = 0.05). Mean total length of young in October was also negatively correlated with that discharge (r = -0.87, p = 0.05). Mean length in 1983-1984 was reduced 27% compared with 1979-1980.
- High-density nursery areas were identified at RM 60-110 and 200-280.

**Flow recommendations**
- Model outputs for the Yampa at Cleopatra site suggested average target flows of 7,350 cfs in June, 1,600 cfs in July and about 440 cfs in August for spawning habitat (the source text reads 400 cfs in August).
- The authors stress these are preliminary and not FWS-endorsed flow recommendations.

**Razorback sucker**
- 323 razorback suckers were captured in the upper Green River (mouth of Duchesne to mouth of Yampa). Lengths ranged from 426 to 608 mm.
- Ripe males averaged 503 mm (N = 37) and ripe females 544 mm (N = 23). Females were significantly longer and heavier (P < 0.01).
- The razorback-to-flannelmouth sucker ratio was 1:355 (0.28%) in all gear, and 1:32 (3%) in spring electrofishing.
- Growth of recaptured adults averaged 2.2 mm per year. One 505 mm specimen had more than 12 annual rings.
- Spawning was confirmed in 1984 by larval capture (10.6-13.6 mm). Recruitment appears to fail, possibly because of predation by introduced species and habitat change after the 1962 Flaming Gorge impoundment.

**Gila complex**
- 523 Gila specimens were identified: 50 G. robusta, 63 G. cypha and 1 G. elegans from the Green; 135 G. robusta and 24 G. cypha from the Yampa; 21 G. robusta from the White; and 115, 37 and 80 unidentified *Gila* spp. respectively.
- Humpback chub made up about 28% of Gila captured in the Green and 12% in the Yampa. No humpbacks were found in the White River.
- Three ripe humpbacks were caught in 1985-1986. Only Yampa Canyon was confirmed as a humpback spawning area under UCRBCC criteria.
- The authors conclude the *Gila robusta* complex is highly polymorphic and that its genetic diversity should be protected without delay.
- Few bonytail have been reported in 20 years. The report recommends protecting Gray Canyon and treating Dinosaur National Monument as a possible site for population augmentation.

**Conclusions and recommendations**
- The authors say flow and temperature "windows" may be too simple, given different needs at each life stage.
- Abnormally high fall discharges can destroy a year class by inundating nursery areas. Elevated late-summer flows should be avoided.
- Discharge should be controlled each year to match larval growth and survival needs, with FWS input into release planning.
- The authors conclude there is adequate water in the Green River to support the Colorado squawfish and razorback sucker if it is managed properly, but more habitat-requirement research is needed.
