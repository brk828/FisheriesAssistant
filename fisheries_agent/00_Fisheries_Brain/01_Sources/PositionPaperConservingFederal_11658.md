# Conserving Federally Threatened, Endangered, and Proposed Species while Providing and Enhancing Recreational Fisheries Programs

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management

## Executive Summary

This working draft (dated 10/25/94) is a position paper setting out a national commitment to conserve [[Federally Threatened, Endangered, and Proposed Species]] while providing for and enhancing [[Recreational Fisheries]]. It frames aquatic ecosystems as essential to both species recovery and angling, argues that fisheries management must be multi-purpose and ecosystem-based, and lists action items grouped under legal principles, partnerships, ecology, outreach, funding and mitigation. The paper is a policy statement rather than a research study and presents no original field data.

## Background & Description

The paper stresses the economic and cultural importance of fisheries. In 1991, over 50 million anglers spent $24 billion on tackle, equipment, food, lodging and other fishing-related items. These expenditures generated over $2.1 billion in Federal income tax revenue and provided employment for 1.3 million people.

Conservation status is presented as a serious concern:

- In 1989, 364 species and subspecies of North American fish were judged to need special management consideration, an increase of 139 taxa over roughly ten years earlier (Williams et al., *Fisheries* 14(6)).
- As of August 1994, 103 fish species and 56 clam/mussel species were on the threatened or endangered list (50 CFR 17.11 & 17.12).
- About 20 percent of fishes, 36 percent of crayfishes and 55 percent of freshwater mussels are ranked as extinct or imperiled (Sport Fishing and Boating Partnership Council, 1994).

The document attributes most fish declines primarily to [[Habitat Loss and Degradation]], and notes that past management, including introductions of [[Nonindigenous Species]], contributed to losses of indigenous fishes.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper treats fish health as a direct reflection of landscape health and argues that the productive capacity of aquatic ecosystems is limited by their quality. Key ecological positions include:

- Management must rest on understanding the habitat requirements of all aquatic species, game and nongame, indigenous and nonindigenous.
- Nonindigenous species are now naturalized in most U.S. aquatic ecosystems and some provide significant social and economic benefits, but their risks were often poorly understood when introduced.
- In heavily altered habitats, nonindigenous species remain an important option for angling; in relatively unaltered habitats, the emphasis should be on [[Indigenous Fish Conservation]].
- Habitat degradation has extirpated Federal T/E/P species from parts of their former range.
- Humans are treated as integral ecosystem components whose needs and impacts must be considered.

The legal framework cited includes the [[Endangered Species Act]] (1973, as amended), the [[Fish and Wildlife Act]] of 1956, the [[Federal Aid in Sport Fish Restoration Act]] of 1950, the [[Fish and Wildlife Coordination Act]], the Federal Water Project Recreation Act, the Reclamation Act and the [[Dingell-Johnson Act]]. The paper states it is not an exhaustive legal review.

## Key Findings

**1. Position and principles**
- National commitment to conserve Federal T/E/P species and to provide for and enhance recreational fisheries.
- Fisheries management should be multi-purpose and ecosystem-based, with game and nongame fishes treated as complementary or at least compatible where possible.
- Partnerships among Federal agencies, States, Tribes, private organizations, the recreational fisheries community and the public are essential, and an annual recreational fisheries summit is proposed to evaluate actions.

**2. Legal and policy action items**
- [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] (FWS) and [[National Marine Fisheries Service]] (NMFS) should ensure consistent ESA administration, implement at the lowest feasible organizational level, and resolve conflicts early with partners.
- Entities undertaking recovery actions must fund and implement effectiveness evaluations and adjust actions accordingly.
- Federal agencies should maintain and increase public fishing opportunities as the 1956 Act requires, and maintain FWS hatchery production for recreation and mitigation unless coordinated reductions are agreed.
- States retain primary authority over fish and wildlife within their borders unless preempted by Federal law; Sport Fish Restoration revenues must be spent only on their defined purposes.

**3. Ecology and stocking**
- New species or hybrid introductions must be ecologically justified and assessed for impacts on Federal T/E/P species, referencing the American Fisheries Society position statement on aquatic species introductions.
- Stocking of cultured fish into waters with Federal T/E/P species must follow plans addressing genetics, disease control and conservation.
- Areas important to species recovery should be re-evaluated, and areas that do not conflict with conservation needs identified.

**4. Outreach, funding and mitigation**
- Public understanding of how T/E/P conservation relates to recreational fishing is limited and must be improved through outreach and public involvement.
- Stable, additional funding is sought, including increased Federal support to States and targeted pre-listing conservation funds, so conservation does not divert angler dollars.
- Where angling opportunities are curtailed for conservation, compatible replacement opportunities should be developed, and a national recreational fishing mitigation policy is to be sought.

**Status:** This is a working draft. It sets out goals and proposed actions but reports no new empirical results, sample sizes or measured outcomes beyond the cited statistics.
