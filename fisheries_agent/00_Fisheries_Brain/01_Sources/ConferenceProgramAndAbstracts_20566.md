# The Ecology and Conservation of the Willow Flycatcher: 2000 Conference Program and Abstracts

#ornithology #tyrannidae #willow-flycatcher

## Executive Summary

This document is the program and abstract volume for *The Ecology and Conservation of the Willow Flycatcher*, held 24–26 October 2000 at the [[Arizona State University]] Memorial Union in Tempe, Arizona. The conference was sponsored by the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], [[U.S. Bureau of Reclamation]], [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]], [[U.S. Forest Service]], [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], and ASU's Plant Biology Department. The program comprised eight paper sessions, a poster session, a special session on the [[Survey Protocol]], and a field trip to [[Roosevelt Lake]].

The abstracts cover the endangered [[Southwestern Willow Flycatcher]] (*Empidonax traillii extimus*) and the other willow flycatcher subspecies. Major themes include nest success and predation, [[Brown-Headed Cowbird]] parasitism and control, population status and distribution across the Southwest and California, winter ecology in Central America, habitat modeling, riparian restoration, and the adequacy of the federal survey protocol and consultation process.

## Background & Description

The willow flycatcher is a small insectivorous passerine in the family Tyrannidae. It was historically treated as a polytypic species with four recognized subspecies, though the morphological separation is subtle. Abstracts in this volume discuss *E. t. extimus*, *E. t. brewsteri*, *E. t. adastus*, *E. t. traillii* and *E. t. campestris*. Migratory birds travel distances "as far as 14,400 km (9000 mi)" during migration, and spend roughly half the year on Central and South American wintering grounds.

The Southwestern Willow Flycatcher was federally listed as endangered in 1995. The conference abstracts describe it as breeding only in dense riparian habitats in Arizona, New Mexico, southern California, extreme southern Nevada, southern Utah, southwestern Colorado and possibly southwestern Texas. Rangewide surveys since 1993 have documented "approximately 900 flycatcher breeding territories," roughly twice the number known when survey efforts began.

Key sites discussed include the [[Gila River]] in New Mexico (the largest known nesting concentration), the [[Middle Rio Grande]], the [[Lower Colorado River]], the [[Santa Ynez River]], the [[Upper San Luis Rey River]], [[Warner Valley]] and the [[San Pedro River]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Flycatchers occupy patches of riparian vegetation, often in local clumps. Abstracts describe preferences for dense multi-storied stands, thick willow or alder understories, calm backwaters, and sites with saturated soils or overbank flooding. Saltcedar ([[Tamarisk]]) is widely used for nesting. Its value to the species is contested: one abstract states that it "is nesting habitat for a large proportion of the Southwestern Willow Flycatcher population," while the conference also addresses [[Saltcedar Biological Control]] research.

**Threats.** The abstracts identify several pressures:
- Water management, channelization, dams, groundwater pumping and livestock grazing as the major stressors to riparian habitat (Ohmart).
- Nest predation as a primary source of nest loss.
- Cowbird parasitism, which "usually results in complete nest loss."
- Habitat degradation from sand mining, agriculture, grazing, urbanization, altered hydrology and exotic plants.
- Possible contaminants, though these were largely ruled out as a cause of deformities.
- Band-induced leg injuries.

**Management.** Responses include cowbird trapping and shooting, riparian restoration (including beaver relocation), habitat modeling, captive propagation as a theoretical option, and review of agency consultation on projects that affect the species.

## Key Findings

### Reproduction and Nest Success
- **Willamette Basin, Oregon** (Altman): Mayfield nest success was 41% (n=147) over two years. Valley (39%, n=75) and forest (42%, n=72) sites did not differ significantly, nor did riparian (44%, n=31) and upland (40%, n=116) habitats. Cowbird parasitism was 4.1% (n=6), with 5 of 6 parasitized nests failing. The author raises concerns about viability in northwestern Oregon.
- **Central Arizona** (Rourke et al.): Of 439 nests with known outcomes, 238 fledged at least one young. Of 201 failed attempts, 71.1% were depredated, 17.4% abandoned or deserted, 6.0% had infertile clutches, 2.0% failed due to cowbirds, 1.0% to weather and 2.5% for other reasons.
- **Upper San Luis Rey River** (Quon and Haas): Data from more than 150 nests (1995–2000) were analyzed by nest order, height, placement and host species.
- **Central Sierra Nevada** (Bombay et al.): Nest success ranged from roughly 25% to 50%, with predation the main cause of failure. Over 200 nests were followed from 1997, and 238 nestlings were banded. Juvenile return rates were 5–15% annually, and dispersal of up to 5.6 km was documented.
- **Warner Valley, California** (King and King): Ten nests were found, and 7 (70%) fledged at least one young; no cowbird parasitism was known. All nests were in thin-leafed alder, and mean shrub cover within 5 m of nests was 56%.
- **Upper Gila River** (Stoleson and Finch): Nesting success was positively correlated with nest height, and flycatchers disproportionately nested in boxelder while avoiding willows.

### Predation
- Time-lapse video (Mccarthey and Bolen) at 31 flycatcher nests and 12 nests of other riparian species recorded 22 nest visits by predators, including 20 predation events. Diurnal snake predation accounted for 45% of visits, with common kingsnakes responsible for 7 of 8 snake depredations. Cooper's hawks caused 7 predation events. The study reports that 67% of nest failures in central Arizona were due to predation.

### Cowbird Parasitism and Control
- **Middle Rio Grande** (Best et al.): Nest parasitism fell from 66% (n=6 nests) in 1995 to 11% (n=19 nests) in 2000. Trapping since 1996 did not clearly reduce localized cowbird populations across years, and the small sample size makes effectiveness hard to assess.
- **Fort Hood, Texas** (Eckrich et al.): Vireo nest parasitism was 90.9% in 1987 and remained above 63% under early control. After a comprehensive strategy began in 1991, parasitism dropped below 6% in the two most recent years. The installation covers 88,500 ha.
- **Anderson Mountain** (Stake et al.): Parasitism exceeding 90% fell sharply after weekly one-hour shooting in 2000, suggesting a low-cost option for small patches.
- **Cowbird synthesis** (Rothstein et al.): Control "typically boosts flycatcher productivity" but is "not consistently correlated with increases in flycatcher population sizes." The authors recommend restricting control to heavily impacted populations.
- **Movements** (Sechrist and Ahlers): Mean daily cowbird movement was 1.9 km (1998, n=15) and 1.4 km (1999, n=16).

### Population Status and Distribution
- **California** (Kus et al.): The 1999 statewide population was 160 territories at 33 sites in nine counties, about 20% of the U.S. population. Of the populations, 88% had fewer than 10 territories, and 82% had fewer than five.
- **Sierra Nevada** (Stefani): The Sierran population was estimated at 300–400 individuals, with over 100 known sites.
- **New Mexico** (Williams): Some 300 territories occur in about 35 locales, with most birds in the Gila drainage.
- **South Fork Kern River** (Whitfield): Breeding pairs fell from 44 in 1989 to 12 in 2000. Egg hatchability dropped from an average of 90.2% (1989–1996) to 74.5% (1997–2000), compared with 97% in other populations (McCabe 1991).
- **Santa Ynez River** (Farmer et al.): The total population was estimated at 10–20 pairs and may be stable or slowly declining.
- **Lower Colorado River** (McKernan and Braden): Breeding numbers included more than 20 pairs at the Virgin River and Topock Marsh, and 1–5 pairs at several other sites.
- **Wasatch Plateau, Utah** (White and Ahlers): A minimum of 72–76 territories was estimated along a 30 km reach. DNA analysis of three birds suggests closer affinity to *E. t. adastus*.
- **Middle Rio Grande** (White et al.): A minimum of 72 territories was estimated from 2000 results, with about 50% detected through Reclamation surveys.
- **Alberta** (Kulba and McGillivray): 32 habitat patches with territorial males were found, restricted to the Foothills and Rocky Mountains.

### Demography and Movement
- **Central Arizona** (Paxton and Sogge): 473 adults and nestlings were banded from 1996 to 1999. Adult survivorship averaged 55%, site fidelity was 36%, and about 15% of adults moved to a new site each year.
- **Postfledging** (Whitfield et al.): Of 39 fledglings from 16 nests, survival was 89.7% over the first 11 days after fledging, with broods staying together and females feeding fledglings longer than males.
- **Stopover habitat** (Johnson et al.): Over 380 sites were surveyed along the Colorado, San Juan and Green Rivers (1992–2000). Most detected birds were migrants, with large numbers in the first sample period (15–30 May).

### Genetics, Vocalizations and Identification
- **Genetics** (Paxton et al.): Using mitochondrial cytochrome-b and AFLP markers on more than 180 birds, significant differences were found between all but one pairwise subspecies comparison. The endangered subspecies shows slight signs of a genetic bottleneck.
- **Song** (Sedgwick): *extimus* songs are slightly lower in pitch, longer in duration and given with less energy. The subspecies occurs north to 37° N at low elevation.
- **Vocal behavior** (Yard and Brown): Females produced 20.3% of songs (probability 0.203). Song rates varied by nesting phase and time of day.
- **Identification** (Unitt and Messer): A discriminant function correctly identified 146 of 153 fresh-plumaged adult Willow and Alder flycatchers.

### Winter Ecology
- **Costa Rica** (Koronkiewicz et al.): About 200 flycatchers were found at 12 of 20 sites, in low-lying Pacific coast areas with dense vegetation and slow freshwater.
- **Territoriality** (Koronkiewicz et al.): Two seasons of data showed site fidelity, use of the same site across the winter, and exclusive territories maintained by song and agonistic behavior.
- **Panama and El Salvador** (Lynn and Whitfield): In Panama, 66 birds were found at 9 of 23 sites. In El Salvador, 274 birds were found at 24 of 28 sites.

### Survey Protocol and Management Process
- The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service convened a special session to review the protocol, including survey objectives, timing, number of surveys, confidence levels and identification criteria.
- **Field evaluation** (Shook et al.): Experienced and inexperienced surveyors both detected the species, but estimates of territories differed markedly. The authors argue the protocol does not allow enough time within occupied areas to reveal actual territory numbers.
- **Consultation review** (Greenwald and Suckling): 28 biological opinions from 1994–97 covered livestock grazing (11), water management (8), construction (5), mining (3) and oil and gas leasing (2). Take was authorized for 16 projects, amounting to at least 120 territories, or about 20–25% of the estimated population. Six projects were judged to jeopardize the species, and the authors found no clear jeopardy threshold.

### Habitat Modeling and Restoration
- **Nest-site model** (Hatten et al.): Using NDVI from TM satellite imagery, 322 of 644 nest sites were used to build the model. It was 91% accurate for suitable sites and 72.5% for unsuitable sites, rising to 98% accuracy for suitable sites when extrapolated to Alamo Lake.
- **Gila River restoration** (Boucher et al.): Flycatchers colonized a bank-stabilization site in 1997 and an upstream site on private land in 1999. The authors suggest proximity to source populations should prioritize restoration.
- **Leg injuries** (Haas and Hargrove): Bands that were too large caused injuries. Of 10 flycatchers fitted with metal bands, 3 showed abrasive injury within two weeks.
- **Captive propagation** (Kuehler and Lieberman): The Hawaiian Endangered Bird Conservation Program has hatched over 220 chicks of 12 native forest species, and the authors suggest a "rear and release" program may hold promise for the willow flycatcher.
- **Environmental contaminants** (Mora et al.): DDE, PCBs and most metals were not implicated in observed deformities on the Lower San Pedro River. Unusually high strontium in eggs warrants further study.
