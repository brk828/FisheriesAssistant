# Great Lakes Gill Net

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-gear #great-lakes-fisheries

## Executive Summary

This 1968 Bureau of Commercial Fisheries leaflet documents the construction, materials, and operational methods of gill nets used in the commercial fishery of the [[Great Lakes]]. At the time of publication, gill nets accounted for nearly half of the total annual American catch of roughly 60 million pounds of fish, harvested by approximately 3,800 fishermen operating over 1,600 boats. The report is a technical gear synopsis rather than a species biology study, detailing net construction (twine, cordage, mesh sizing), vessel types ("Great Lakes tugs"), and the full operational cycle of setting, lifting, and maintaining gill nets.

## Background & Description

- **Historical context**: Gill nets were among the earliest fishing gears introduced to the Great Lakes, predating European settlement, with Indigenous peoples relying on Great Lakes fish as a primary food source. Most gear types (shallow trap net, gill net, pound net, set hooks, seine) were established by the late 1800s; the deep trap net was added in the 1920s.
- **Scale of fishery**: ~95,000 square miles of combined Canadian-U.S. Great Lakes waters; ~60 million pounds of fish produced annually by American fishermen; fishing season generally 9 months, extending into winter under ice-free conditions.
- **Vessels ("Great Lakes tugs")**: Evolved from small open boats and sailing schooners to steel or wood-hulled tugs (30–75 feet, 2–5 person crews) with high closed-in superstructures for net handling. Vessels have limited range (~30 miles from home port) and return nightly; larger vessels double as icebreakers for smaller craft, extending the fishing season. See [[Colvin 1960]] for deck design details.
- **Twine and cordage**:
  - Nylon (patented by [[DuPont Chemical Company]] in 1934) has largely displaced natural fibers (cotton, linen, manila).
  - Multifilament nylon twine classified by **denier** (weight in grams per 9,000 meters of yarn); common Great Lakes sizes: Nos. 34, 40, 42.
  - Monofilament nylon netting is coded by diameter or breaking strength; per [[Molin 1959]], monofilament nylon nets caught 7× more fish than cotton nets and 4× more than multifilament nylon nets in Swedish lake trials, attributed to the transparency of single-filament netting. Drawbacks include stiffness, higher cost, and difficulty of repair (knots set chemically or via double knots).
  - Cordage (float lines, lead lines, breast lines, bridles, anchor/buoy lines) made of cotton, manila, Dacron, Nylon, Polyethylene, or Polypropylene; anchor/buoy lines range ¼–½ inch diameter.
- **Mesh size measurement**: Defined by **bar (square) measure** (one side of square mesh including one knot) or **stretched (extension) measure** (distance between two opposite knots under tension). State statutes often specify "inside measure" excluding knots. Material treatment (wetting, dyeing) can shrink/stretch twine by ⅛ inch or more.
- **Net construction**: Basic strip of netting 20–100 meshes deep with double-twine selvage top and bottom; hung to maitre cord at ratios from half to third basis (e.g., 24 in. mesh : 12 in. maitre, or 18 in. mesh : 12 in. maitre). Nylon's tendency to entangle small fish has led fishermen toward tighter/flatter hanging ratios (<18:12). Seaming twine (≤ No. 9 or 12 cotton-equivalent) attaches webbing to maitre cord via clove-hitch-like knots every 3–6 meshes. Floats (wood, aluminum, plastic) spaced every 6–8 feet along the float line; lead weights (pipe or split leads, 1–4 oz, or iron rings ~4 in. diameter in Lakes Erie/Ontario) counterbalance floats.
- **Net units**: Individual nets 100–600 feet long, stored in boxes (3–4 ft × 2 ft × 12 in.). A "gang" = 8–12 boxes of nets (~8,000 feet), bridled at each end with ¼-inch+ rope to anchor and buoy systems, marked with pole/flag buoys.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Depth range and habitat**: Gill nets fished across a broad depth range of 1 to 120 fathoms throughout the Great Lakes.
- **Regional targeting**:
  - **Lakes Superior, Huron, and Michigan**: primarily chubs, lake herring, common whitefish, and yellow perch.
  - **Lake Erie**: yellow pike, yellow perch, common whitefish, and white bass.
  - Anchoring practices differ regionally: chain anchors common in upper Great Lakes; double or four-fluked anchors used in Lakes Erie and Ontario.
- **Mesh size vs. target species** (stretched measure):
  - 1½–1¾ inch: bait fish, smelt, other small species.
  - 2½–3 inch: lake herring, chubs, yellow perch.
  - 4½–6 inch: lake trout, common whitefish, yellow pike.
  - >8 inch (rare): carp and lake sturgeon.
- **Water temperature and catch quality**: In summer waters of 65–78°F, nets must be tended daily to prevent spoilage from suffocated fish. In colder waters, up to 20 miles of gill net can be fished per vessel, lifting 8–10 miles per day. Daily catches range 100–3,000 lbs, with larger catches during spring and fall spawning seasons due to fish concentration.
- **Midwater fishing**: Some nets are "canned" or floated at controlled depths using drop lines, allowing targeting of fish at specific water column strata rather than only bottom-set configurations.

## Key Findings

1. **Gill nets remain a dominant gear**, accounting for nearly half of total Great Lakes commercial catch as of the report's publication, valued for low equipment investment and adaptability to small vessels.
2. **Monofilament nylon significantly outperforms** multifilament nylon and cotton in catch efficiency (per [[Molin 1959]] Swedish comparative trials: ~7× cotton, ~4× multifilament nylon), attributed to reduced visibility to fish.
3. **Net construction is highly customized** to local conditions (depth, bottom type, target species behavior), though a common structural pattern (maitre cord frame, hung mesh netting, float line, lead line) is shared across the fishery.
4. **Fishing operations are structured around "gangs"** of nets (8–12 boxes, ~8,000 ft), with vessels typically maintaining multiple gangs in rotation (some in water, some ashore for repair/drying).
5. **Species-specific mesh sizing is a key management/selectivity variable**: mesh size correlates directly with target species size class, from small baitfish/smelt (1.5–1.75 in.) up to lake sturgeon and carp (>8 in.).
6. **Regional gear variation** exists in anchor types (chains vs. fluked anchors) and net-lifting techniques (bow-hauling in shallow water vs. curved/bight trailing in open water), reflecting adaptation to local bathymetry and species behavior (see [[Lead (Fish Behavior)]] — tendency of fish to follow underwater obstacles rather than cross them).
7. **Net maintenance practices**: Nets are cleaned every 2–14 days via soap or hot water treatment to remove algae/bacteria buildup, then dried and repaired on reels — critical for maintaining gear efficiency and longevity despite nylon's reduced need for chemical preservation compared to natural fiber nets.
