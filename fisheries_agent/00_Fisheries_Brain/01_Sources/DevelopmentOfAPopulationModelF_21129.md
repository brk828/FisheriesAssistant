# Development of a Population Model for Humpback Chub (Gila cypha) in Grand Canyon

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This BIO/WEST, Inc. interim report (June 23, 1995), prepared by [[Ronald J. Ryel]] and [[Richard A. Valdez]] for the Bureau of Reclamation and the [[Glen Canyon Environmental Studies]] (GCES) program, describes the development of a quantitative population model for the endangered [[Humpback Chub]] in Grand Canyon. The report outlines a five-element modelling program, presents a conceptual compartmental model divided into five subpopulations, reviews model types, and summarizes adult population estimates and survival from mark-recapture data (1990–1993). Its central conclusions are that a modelling program is feasible, that an age-structured projection-matrix approach is preferred, and that the model should serve primarily as an organizing and integrating tool for research and monitoring rather than as a precise predictor.

## Background & Description

**Purpose.** The modelling program aims to provide an integrated quantitative characterization of the humpback chub population, a tool for understanding population dynamics and environmental interrelationships, identification of missing life-history information, and evaluation of long-term monitoring and population viability.

**Study species.** Of the four endangered mainstem Colorado River fishes ([[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail]] and humpback chub), humpback chub in Grand Canyon were judged best suited to modelling. Reasons given include accessible sampling methods for all life stages, a sizable existing database, high fidelity to a small geographic region (permitting closed-system analyses), and life history similar to many freshwater forms.

**Program elements.** The work is organized into five elements: (I) feasibility evaluation, (II) conceptual population model, (III) identification and assimilation of state and rate variables, (IV) mathematical and logical relationships, and (V) model development and implementation. Elements I–II are reported as complete; elements II–V are described as progressing toward an age-structured model.

**Modelling approaches.** Three approaches are recognized: natural history description, conceptual compartmental models, and formal mathematical models. The authors recommend integrating all three. Model types considered include [[Simple Birth-Death Models]] (judged of limited use because they cannot separate life stages) and [[Age- and Stage-Structured Models]] (judged most useful; implemented via [[Leslie Matrix]] projection matrices).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Subpopulation structure.** The Grand Canyon population is treated as one population, isolated from upper Colorado River basin populations, and divided into five subpopulations (Table 1):

- **Colorado River Upstream (CRU):** About 5% of chub captured 1990–1993 occurred outside the 30-km area around the LCR inflow; small aggregations at RM 27, RM 30 (largest, ~30 fish by mark-recapture) and RM 57–58.
- **Colorado River/LCR Inflow (CRI):** About 95% of mainstem chub occur within roughly 30 km around the [[Little Colorado River]] confluence (RM 58–77). Most adults ascend the LCR to spawn February–May and descend June–July.
- **Colorado River Downstream (CRD):** Small aggregations at multiple river miles, with the largest at RM 126–129 (about 100 fish). Reproduction outside the LCR is rare, attributed to cold water released from [[Glen Canyon Dam]] preventing egg maturation and larval survival.
- **Little Colorado River (LCR):** Holds a large proportion of the population year-round; reproduction from age 3+ fish.
- **Tributaries (TRI):** Small numbers captured in Bright Angel, Shinumo, Kanab and Havasu creeks; local reproduction is uncertain and recent evidence of spawning (gravid fish, eggs, larvae) was not found.

**Threats and uncertainty.** The report lists environmental uncertainty (food supply, competing or predatory fishes, parasites, dam-controlled flow and temperature), demographic uncertainty, catastrophes (toxic releases, disease, major storms) and genetic uncertainty as factors in viability. Demographic and environmental uncertainties are judged most likely to affect persistence. Genetic uncertainty is considered unlikely to be significant, partly because no congeneric species (roundtail chub, bonytail) co-occur with the population.

**Associated management context.** Work addresses two of seven conservation measures from the 1978 Biological Opinion on Glen Canyon Dam operations, and is linked to the Glen Canyon Dam Environmental Impact Statement (GCDEIS) and long-term monitoring under adaptive management.

## Key Findings

- **Feasibility:** The modelling program was judged feasible and recommended, because population size, distribution and movement are reasonably understood and demographic data for humpback chub exceed those of any other native mainstem Colorado River species.
- **Adult population (closed models, LCR aggregation):** Estimates for 1991–1993 averaged approximately 3,000–3,500 adults (TL ≥ 200 mm), with 95% confidence intervals about ±20%. Closed-model estimators M<sub>t</sub>, Darroch M<sub>t</sub>, Schnabel M<sub>t</sub>, and Chao estimators were similar; the Schnabel mean was 2,994.
- **Pooled 1990–1993 estimates** were roughly 1,000 higher, attributed to violation of closure from mortality and recruitment.
- **Open-model estimates** (Jolly-Seber models A, B, D) gave mean N of 3,080–3,192, consistent with closed-model results.
- **Adult survival:** Model B, the simplest model fitting the data, gave seasonal survival of 0.932 (95% CI 0.890–0.973), equivalent to annual survival of 0.755 (95% CI 0.627–0.896). Roughly 735–857 adults could be lost and replaced annually.
- **Size structure anomaly:** Adults of 200–300 mm were markedly under-represented relative to a stable size distribution, relative to chub >300 mm (2,172–2,764 by closed estimators). Two hypotheses (gear selectivity versus lower survival of smaller chub) were tested; survival of chub >300 mm was similar to all-adult survival, suggesting gear or habitat bias rather than low survival is most likely, though the authors could not fully resolve this.
- **Other aggregations** (Pumpkin Spring, Havasu, Shinumo, 30-mile) were estimated at about 4 to 60 adults each, each below 2% of the LCR aggregation. Mainstem MGG aggregation (1993) was estimated at 89–103 adults.
- **Model structure:** An age-structured projection model is recommended, with submodels used for specific objectives rather than precise prediction. Sensitivity analysis is proposed to focus monitoring on key parameters.
- **Recommendations:** Circulate the interim report to Grand Canyon researchers; refine the conceptual model; solicit data; use an age- or stage-based structure; define GCES integration objectives; integrate modelling with monitoring; and conduct a viability analysis after Elements I–V are complete, including effects of a possible second spawning population.
