## Executive Summary

This 1961 report documents the pioneering effort to culture [[Channel Catfish]] (*Ictalurus punctatus*), a nonnative game fish, at the [[Central Valleys Fish Hatchery]] in Elk Grove, California — the only state-operated warm-water hatchery on the Pacific coast at the time. Beginning with wild broodstock trapped from the [[Sacramento River]] and [[Sutter Bypass]] in 1957, the hatchery scaled production from 130,000 fingerlings (1958) to 973,668 fingerlings produced in 1960 (686,078 planted). The paper details trough/tank design, water supply management, spawning behavior in artificial containers, egg incubation using a mechanical paddle system, disease control (notably [[Ichthyophthirius]] outbreaks), and fingerling transport logistics. The work established replicable protocols for warm-water hatchery operations and stocking of channel catfish into California reservoirs and rivers.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Ictalurus punctatus* (Channel Catfish), family Ictaluridae.
- **Broodstock size classes (1960 spawning ponds)**:
  - Pond 11: 50 females (avg. 9.1 lb), 45 males (avg. 9.2 lb); largest females 15 lb, largest male 14 lb.
  - Pond 10: 50 females (avg. 6.1 lb), 41 males (avg. 6.7 lb); largest females 7 lb, largest males 8 lb.
  - Pond 9: 50 females (avg. 5.2 lb), 41 males (avg. 5.8 lb); largest female 7 lb, largest males 8 lb.
- **Egg counts**: Calculated at 500 eggs per ounce of spawn weight. Individual spawns ranged from 7,500 to 57,500 eggs.
- **Fecundity by broodstock size**: Larger broodstock (Pond 11) produced disproportionately more spawns (37 spawns, 1,040,500 eggs) than medium (Pond 10: 23 spawns, 525,000 eggs) or small broodstock (Pond 9: 2 spawns, 52,500 eggs) — indicating a strong positive relationship between adult body size and spawning success.
- **Fingerling size grading**: Sizes at planting ranged widely, from 350 fish/oz (very small fry-stage fingerlings) up to 13 fish/oz (larger pond-reared fingerlings). Fish reared in ponds for extended periods (Pond 8) reached the largest documented size (13/oz).
- **Fry development**: Eggs hatched in 6–7 days; fry were transferred to rearing troughs ~3 days post-hatch when they began "turning grey," indicating pigmentation as a developmental marker for handling readiness.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Native/introduced status**: Channel catfish is nonnative to California, having become established in southern California reservoirs, the [[Colorado River]], and the lower Sacramento River drainage — motivating active management propagation for further introduction into warm-water reservoirs statewide.
- **Thermal preferences**:
  - Hatchery operation water temperatures ranged 66–84°F, optimal 74–80°F for egg/fry handling.
  - Well water supply was a constant 68°F but required warming (via a 1.87-acre reservoir pond) for optimal spawning/rearing conditions.
  - Ideal transport temperature: 65–75°F.
- **Light sensitivity**: Fingerlings performed best when shielded from bright light; hatchery troughs/tanks were painted black (asphaltum) and fitted with covers/shade to mimic dark, sheltered conditions consistent with natural benthic/nocturnal behavior of catfish.
- **Spawning habitat preference**: Adults strongly preferred enclosed, dark cavity-type structures for nesting — of 62 spawns, 43 were in painted milk cans versus only 19 in terra-cotta pipe "kegs," suggesting cavity size/shape or darkness influenced spawning site selection. Wild spawning was also observed opportunistically in muskrat holes and open pond-bottom nests.
- **Reproductive behavior**: Guarding behavior by adults (likely males) was noted — nest checks required a gloved hand due to bite risk from the guarding parent, consistent with known paternal care in *Ictalurus* species.
- **Disease ecology**: [[Ichthyophthirius]] (Ich), *Trichodina*, and *Scyphidia* were recurring ectoparasitic/protozoan disease agents in crowded hatchery conditions. Pond-to-pond seepage and contact with wild-caught forage fish (carp and goldfish from ricefield drainage canals) were identified as probable infection vectors, highlighting the disease-transmission risk of shared water sources and mixed-species holding in aquaculture settings.
- **Gas-bubble disease**: An early (1958) issue in which fry developed distended abdomens and swam belly-up was linked to supersaturated gases in the water supply; resolved via perforated splash plates at trough inflows to promote degassing.

## Key Findings

- **Hatching mechanism breakthrough**: Successful egg hatching was only achieved after installing a mechanical paddle apparatus that mimicked the natural fanning behavior of guarding male catfish; paddles cycled at 72 passes/minute (36 rpm shaft, 2 sweeps/revolution).
- **Chemical treatments**:
  - Malachite green (2 g/gallon stock solution, 10 cc dosed morning and evening) controlled fungal growth on eggs.
  - Pyridylmercuric acetate (PMA) at 2 ppm for 1 hour, applied weekly, effectively controlled *Ichthyophthirius*, *Trichodina*, and *Scyphidia* outbreaks in troughs/tanks.
  - A commercial herbicide (1–2 ppm) controlled nuisance plant growth in the water-supply reservoir pond.
- **Pituitary extract found unnecessary**: Unlike some other hatchery protocols, injected pituitary extract produced no noticeable spawning improvement; natural pond spawning (broodstock released loose into ponds with nesting containers) was fully adequate.
- **Pen spawning failure**: Paired spawning pens (one male/one female per pen) were unsatisfactory compared to open-pond spawning with multiple nesting containers (15–20 per pond) and unrestricted broodstock mixing.
- **Production scale-up (1958–1960)**:
  | Year | Troughs | Fingerlings Produced | Fingerlings Planted |
  |------|---------|----------------------|---------------------|
  | 1958 | 8 | 130,000 (121,060 planted per summary) | 121,060 |
  | 1959 | 20 | 627,440 | 620,000 (approx.) |
  | 1960 | 20 + 3 redwood tanks + 2 concrete tanks | 973,668 | 686,078 |
- **Pond rearing trial mixed results**: Attempting to grow surplus fingerlings to larger size in earthen ponds succeeded in two of four ponds (Ponds 7 & 8, ~63% survival to planting size, growing from 60–100/oz to 13–30/oz) but resulted in total loss in the other two ponds (Ponds 4 & 17) due to uncontrolled *Ichthyophthirius* outbreaks traced to water seepage and forage-fish contamination.
- **Transport robustness**: Channel catfish fingerlings proved exceptionally hardy for transport — an interstate case study (Oregon State workers) moved 36,000 fingerlings (30/oz) 17 hours with zero mortality, including 11-day delayed-mortality checks showing no losses. Standard hatchery loading rate: 8 oz of fingerlings per gallon of water.
- **Diet regimen**: Fry/fingerlings thrived on raw ground beef liver supplemented with prepared trout pellets (fine crumble); ocean fish was rejected by broodstock as a food source. Hourly feeding (8 a.m.–5 p.m.) was standard; night feeding was tested but found unnecessary.
- **Relevance to broader stocking program**: This hatchery effort directly supported introduction of [[Channel Catfish]] into additional California warm-water reservoirs, complementing existing wild populations in the [[Sacramento River]] drainage, [[Colorado River]], and southern California reservoirs, and expanding the species' range as a sport fishery resource, as seen in stocking events like the [[Owens River]] planting of 57,410 fingerlings in 1960.
