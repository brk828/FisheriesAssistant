# Norfield Copy-Strip: A Method for Preparing Checklists and Bibliographies

#ichthyology #marine-biology #scholarly-publishing

## Executive Summary

This short methods article by E.J. Crossman and Cheryl D. Goodchild describes a modular, strip-based system for building and maintaining long literature-cited lists, faunal checklists and annotated bibliographies. The approach, the [[Norfield Copy-Strip]] system, was used in the [[Department Of Ichthyology And Herpetology]] at the [[Royal Ontario Museum]] in Toronto. It was applied to a literature-cited list of about 1,400 references, an annotated bibliography of about 600 references with a geographic subject index, and a major text on the [[Freshwater Fishes Of Canada]]. The authors' central claim is that separating each entry onto its own strip avoids retyping and re-proofreading, which is the main source of new errors in revised lists. The document is not a fish-biology study. It contains no species, specimen or ecological data. Two advertisements for Canadian publishing reference works are appended.

## Background & Description

**Problem addressed.** Scientists and scholars often need long, frequently changing lists. Revising these lists usually means retyping whole pages, which introduces new errors and forces every page to be checked again.

**The system.** The [[Norfield Copy-Strip]] system consists of:
- Connected, individual strips sold in several depths (for example six-line and three-line strips) that fit into a typewriter.
- Plastic holder pages into which the separated strips are mounted by their tabs.
- Accessories described in the figure caption: a strip separator, a strip sorter (organizer), a strip shifter for inserting entries, and a divider.

The system was devised originally for address lists. It is described as moderately expensive in materials but time-saving for both typist and author. It originated in Great Britain (Kalamazoo Ltd, Birmingham) and was available in North America through Norfield Hart Ltd, Toronto.

**Workflow for the freshwater fish text.** Each reference was typed on a strip as it entered the manuscript, without regard to alphabetical order. Most citations fitted on one double-spaced six-line strip, with occasional second strips for long citations. The strips were then sorted alphabetically, mounted, and photocopied as the general literature-cited section. Strips for the per-family "Suggested Reading" lists were then pulled and remounted on separate holder pages, while the general list was kept intact.

**Workflow for the annotated bibliography.** Source material was a haphazard card file, an unverified typed list and two computer printouts. Each item was transferred to a 3 x 5 inch verified card, with the annotation typed on the back. Each bibliographic entry was built from three strips:
1. A narrow three-line strip with author name(s), date of publication and geographic area treated.
2. A six-line strip with the rest of the citation.
3. A six-line strip with a brief annotation.

Strips were marked on their mounting tabs so that the three parts could be correlated, and were proofread once before mounting. The body was mounted in author–date order and photocopied as typesetter's copy in twenty-page sections, to limit the cost of holder pages. The author strips were then reorganized by a subject classification written on their backs, to build the subject index.

## Key Findings

- **Retyping is the main hazard.** Revising long lists by retyping whole pages introduces new errors, so all retyped material must be checked again. The strip system removes this step, since only new material is typed and proofread.
- **Scale of the test case.** The [[Royal Ontario Museum]] team compiled a literature-cited list of approximately 1,400 references and an annotated bibliography of approximately 600 references with a geographic subject index.
- **Modular editing.** Entries can be deleted, corrected, replaced or inserted between existing strips without retyping the rest of the list. Photocopies of the mounted pages serve as working copy, circulation copy or printer's manuscript.
- **Dual-purpose card file.** The 3 x 5 inch verified card file served as a required first stage and a continuing check on the strips, and it was kept as a separate, ongoing reference index.
- **Revision control.** Cards for references found after publication are filed alphabetically and marked on one edge with red felt marker, so they can be found and converted to strips for the next revision.
- **Layout decisions up front.** Layout, format and style were fixed in consultation with the editor before typing, and strips were not reworked after mounting.
- **Holder-page capacity.** Holder pages are thirteen inches long but were filled only to eleven inches, so the photocopies could stand upright in a typesetter's copy-holder. Blank strips were left in position for references still awaiting verification, so the manuscript could go forward before all citations were confirmed.
- **Practical trade-offs.** The authors describe the system as costly in materials, and holder pages were expensive, which led to the twenty-page sectioning. They also note that the strips had to be removed anyway to prepare the subject index, so this was no hardship.
