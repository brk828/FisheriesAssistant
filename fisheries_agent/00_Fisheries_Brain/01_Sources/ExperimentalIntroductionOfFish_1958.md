## Executive Summary

This 1957–1958 Texas Fish and Game segment completion report documents an experimental fisheries program introducing marine and freshwater game species into inland Texas reservoirs. Two Gulf of Mexico marine species — [[Redfish]] (*Sciaenops ocellatus*) and [[Speckled Weakfish]] (*Cynoscion nebulosus*, also called speckled trout) — were transplanted into the saline waters of [[Lake Kemp]] and the upper Big Wichita River to establish new game fisheries. Separately, [[White Bass]] (*Roccus chrysops*) were introduced into [[Buffalo Lake]] to create a sport fishery and provide a predator to control [[Gizzard Shad]] and other rough fish. As of this reporting period, no reproduction had been confirmed for any of the three introduced species, and growth rates for the marine transplants remained undetermined. No further marine stockings were planned pending cost-benefit justification, while white bass restocking continued to support biological control of forage fish.

## Background & Description

**Program objective:** Experimentally introduce non-native fish species into Texas waters either to (1) create new sport fishing opportunities or (2) introduce predators to control rough and forage fish populations.

**Species and rationale:**
- **Redfish** and **Speckled Weakfish**: Captured live from Gulf of Mexico waters along the Texas Coast (notably near Rockport) and transported to the highly saline waters of [[Lake Kemp]] and the upper [[Big Wichita River]], including the tributary [[Salt Creek]]. The elevated salinity of these inland waters was believed to make them suitable habitat for these normally estuarine/marine species.
- **White Bass**: Sourced from [[Lake Diversion]] and introduced into [[Buffalo Lake]] to establish a sport species and serve as an open-water predator against gizzard shad and other rough fish.

**Stocking history:**

*Redfish:*
- 1954: 58 young-of-year stocked near Cara Blanca Camp, Lake Kemp (pre-dates formal project approval)
- June 1955: 12 adults (1–3 lbs each)
- August 1955: 2 individuals (~0.5 lb each)
- October 1955: 2 yearlings
- March 1956: 1,603 fingerlings to Lake Kemp and Salt Creek (~300 died in transit/shortly after; ~1,300 survived)
- November 1956: 4 young-of-year survived transport
- May 1957: 4 yearlings
- **Cumulative total prior to this segment: 1,382 redfish**

*Speckled Weakfish (Trout):*
- August 1955: 25 specimens (7–12 inches)
- October 1955: 9 yearlings, 9 fingerlings
- March 1956: 89 fingerlings
- July 1956: 15 adults
- September 1956: 28 variable-size trout (all believed under 1 year)
- May 1957: 9 trout (final stocking)
- **Cumulative total prior to this segment: 184 speckled weakfish**

*White Bass:*
- Summer 1954: 28 adults from Lake Diversion
- July 1955: 78 additional individuals
- July–October 1957: 1,420 fingerlings/yearlings (major restocking after chemical treatments)

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Lake Kemp**: A large (22,800-acre) reservoir with unusually saline water chemistry, hypothesized to support marine/estuarine species such as redfish and speckled weakfish. Habitat includes stumps, rocks, and deep-water zones that complicated seining efforts.
- **Salt Creek**: A saline watershed tributary stream used as a secondary release site for redfish fingerlings.
- **Buffalo Lake**: Subject to chemical rotenone treatments (May 1956, June 1957) for selective removal of [[Gizzard Shad]] and [[Carp]]; white bass proved highly susceptible to rotenone, causing incidental mortality and prompting a pause in restocking until treatments ceased.
- **Spawning habitat limitations**: White bass spawning in Buffalo Lake was constrained by drought conditions — the watershed failed to receive rains common elsewhere in Texas, leaving the lake at low levels and depriving fish of flowing tributary water and wave-swept sandy beaches needed for spawning.
- **Predator-prey dynamics**: Gizzard shad populations rebounded rapidly following chemical treatments, motivating renewed white bass stocking as a biological control measure rather than continued chemical control.

## Key Findings

- **Total introductions (cumulative to report date):**
  - 1,382 redfish and 184 speckled weakfish stocked in Lake Kemp/Big Wichita system
  - 1,420 white bass stocked in Buffalo Lake during this segment (July–October 1957)

- **No confirmed reproduction** was detected for redfish, speckled weakfish, or white bass as of the report date.

- **Growth rates undetermined** for redfish and speckled weakfish due to:
  - Reluctance to use gill nets (risk of injuring/killing valuable experimental fish)
  - Unreliable angler-reported catches (many were false, mistaken IDs, or unverifiable due to consumed/destroyed specimens)

- **May 26–27, 1958 survey**: ~2,400 feet of gill nets set across Lake Kemp (headwaters to dam) plus check-seining; **zero redfish or speckled weakfish captured**. Researchers noted this was inconclusive given the lake's large size (22,800 acres) relative to sampling effort.

- **White bass condition factor** recovery data from June 1955–May 1956: values up to 3.5 recorded among 16 fish caught in gill nets, suggesting good physical condition despite low net-capture numbers; angler-reported catches were more frequent than net catches.

- **Rotenone sensitivity**: White bass showed high susceptibility to chemical treatments intended for gizzard shad/carp control, observed as incidental mortality during both 1956 and 1957 treatments.

- **Management decisions:**
  - No additional marine species stockings planned for Lake Kemp until cost-benefit justification established
  - White bass restocking resumed (1957) after halting chemical treatments, prioritizing biological control of shad
  - Enhanced natural history/netting surveys planned for the next segment to more thoroughly assess redfish/speckled weakfish status in Lake Kemp
  - Buffalo Lake project work largely discontinued except periodic re-surveys to monitor white bass status
