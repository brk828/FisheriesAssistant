# Canyon Lake Fish Habitat Development Project

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona Game and Fish Department (AGFD) Region VI report, written by Jim Warnecke, Region VI Fisheries Specialist, documents a volunteer-supported project to install artificial fish habitat in [[Canyon Lake]], Maricopa County, Arizona, between February 1995 and March 1996. The project placed [[Berkley FISH HAB]] modules (zinc-weighted polypropylene strands) in two coves to provide escape cover, increase periphytic food production, and attract predatory game fish. Evaluation used diving, gill netting, sonar, electrofishing, plankton trawling, angler surveys and creel census.

Physical installation succeeded and the modules remained intact, with periphytic algal growth on the strands. Biological results were limited. Structure-seeking fish, especially [[Largemouth Bass]] and [[Walleye]], were more common in developed zones in gill-net catches, but evaluation was constrained by poor visibility, sampling-gear failures and unmarked sites. The author concludes that localized use by structure-seeking fish was indicated, and recommends denser placement, site marking and comparative habitat trials.

## Background & Description

Canyon Lake lies about 42 miles east of Phoenix. It formed in 1925 after the construction of [[Mormon Flat Dam]] on the [[Salt River]]. The reservoir operates at an average elevation of 1,655 feet above mean sea level and covers an average of 550 surface acres. It is the third of four reservoirs in a chain on the Salt River, and annual water-level fluctuations rarely exceed an average of 3 vertical feet.

Previous habitat surveys found the lake nearly devoid of submergent vegetation in its littoral (shallow) areas. Escape cover use by game fish such as Largemouth Bass and forage fish such as [[Threadfin Shad]] was therefore severely limited. To address this, the project aimed to stock artificial cover in two selected areas and to run a one-year evaluation of fish and angler use.

**Objectives**
- Increase available nutrients for small game and forage fish through periphytic growth on the artificial habitat.
- Provide escape cover for game fish.
- Increase catchability of game species by attracting predators.
- Monitor the effectiveness of [[Artificial Habitat]] (FISH HAB), document public relations benefits, and provide quality fishing opportunities.

**Partners and Permits**
- Volunteers and equipment came from [[Anglers United]] (a nonprofit conservation group), U.S. BASS, the Arizona Bass Federation, and the Arizona State University Wildlife Society.
- The [[Tonto National Forest]], the primary recreational-use agency for the reservoir, granted a categorical exclusion from detrimental aquatic-ecology impacts.

**Implementation**
- Each cover site measured 25 × 75 feet. Two areas were selected: the mouth of Tortilla Cove and upper Tortilla Cove, and Boulder Cove (LaBarge) and upper Boulder Cove, with associated control zones.
- Modules were bundled in groups of 20 to simulate a "bush" and sunk to depths of 15 to 19 feet. Roughly 50 bushes (about 1,000 modules) were placed on April 11, 1995, although the project report states that 1,800 modules were purchased.
- Modules were placed so as not to impede boats or swimmers. Sites were deliberately left unmarked to limit public attention, and two AGFD divers checked spacing and vertical integrity.
- Pre-installation publicity included a newspaper article in the *Arizona Republic and Gazette* (April 3, 1995 as printed) and coverage by three TV stations, a cable network and a local outdoor magazine on installation day.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The reservoir's littoral zone lacked natural cover, so structure was the main limiting factor for shelter and foraging by game and forage fish. The modules were placed at 15 to 19 feet, deeper than the shallow shoreline where most anglers fish. Water visibility was poor during the summer and fall diving sessions, which limited direct observation. Depth and bottom obstructions also reduced the effectiveness of several sampling methods.

Placement was designed to avoid translocation by wave action or inter-reservoir currents. Divers found the modules had sunk deep enough into the sediment to stay in place throughout the evaluation period. Their random, scattered arrangement, produced by dropping bushes from boats, did not resemble dense natural vegetation.

## Key Findings

**Monitoring Methods**
- **SCUBA Survey:** Performed in August and October 1995 and March 1996. Two divers swam two perpendicular transects at each site.
- **[[Gill Netting]]:** Set in August and November 1995 and March 1996. A 150-foot experimental net (six 25-foot panels, mesh 1 to 3 inches) was fished for 12 to 18 hours overnight in both developed and control areas.
- **[[Sonar Chart Recording]]:** A Lowrance chart recorder was used with gill netting to detect fish by echolocation.
- **[[Electrofishing]]:** A 240-volt electroshocker boat surveyed the two developed sites in May 1995.
- **Location Angling:** Volunteer professional anglers fished the habitat zones one day per quarter.
- **Plankton Trawl:** A Clarke-Bumpus zooplankton trawl sampled two 75-foot transects.
- **[[Creel Census]]:** Angler surveys were conducted four half-days per month for the entire year.

**Results**
- **Physical integrity:** SCUBA surveys confirmed that modules were intact, with no slumping or drooping. Periphytic algal growth was visible on the strands in March 1996.
- **Diving observations:** Visibility ranged from 0 to 2.5 feet, so direct fish observations during summer and fall were ineffective.
- **Gill netting:** Carp, Green Sunfish, Yellow Bass and Yellow Bullhead were caught at comparable rates in developed and control areas. Largemouth Bass were caught in greater numbers in developed zones, as were [[Walleye]], which the author attributes possibly to feeding activity given their open-water habits.
- **Sonar:** Charts indicated module placement, integrity and some fish association with the sites. Species were not confirmed, but fish appeared small and may have been juvenile or forage fish.
- **Electrofishing:** Ineffective. The unit's roughly 10-foot effective field was too short for the 15 to 20 feet of water over the habitat.
- **Plankton trawl:** Ineffective because of shallow depths and substrate obstructions.
- **Angler use:** Because the sites were unmarked, most anglers fished around them without targeting them. No specific association between the artificial structure and catch rates was documented. Three volunteer professional anglers fished the zones at least once per quarter with no notable success.

**Cost and Effort**
- The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] spent an estimated $6,570 in total, with 42 man-days of effort, 1,620 vehicle miles and 25 boat hours.
- Volunteers donated 12 man-days, drove 340 miles and logged 2 boat hours. At Game and Fish rates, this equates to $4,974 (rendered as "$41974" in the scan).

**Recommendations**
1. Make the sites denser by adding modules or reducing the site size, since the bushes were too widely spaced to attract structure-seeking fish effectively. The department intends to obtain more FISH HAB modules.
2. Mark the sites with informational buoys so anglers can target them. This would improve the assessment of attractor effectiveness and inform the public about the structures.
3. Carry out comparative stocking of other habitat types, such as trees and stake beds, in other reservoir areas to evaluate use by [[Structure-Seeking Fish]] such as Largemouth Bass and [[Black Crappie]].
4. Intensify echo-sounding and confirm sonar targets with gill-net catches. These methods may provide the best depiction of structure use.

**Conclusion**
Localized use of the FISH HAB modules by structure-seeking fish was indicated. The author notes that future projects should account for the limits of placing and evaluating artificial habitat in large reservoir systems.
