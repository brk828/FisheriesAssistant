# Fish Introductions: Good Management, Mismanagement, or No Management?

#ichthyology #marine-biology #fisheries-management #exotic-species #conservation-policy

## Executive Summary

This critical review examines fish introduction practices in North American waters, evaluating whether such introductions represent sound management, mismanagement, or absence of management. The authors, Walter R. Courtenay, Jr. and C. Richard Robins, analyze the eight-step procedure proposed in 1973 for evaluating fish introductions and assess its adoption and implementation by fisheries agencies. Through multiple case studies—including the [[Salton Sea]], [[Colorado River]] reservoirs, [[Lake Powell]], and various state programs—the document demonstrates that most agencies have failed to follow established protocols, particularly the critical step of independent peer review. The analysis reveals a pattern of "copycat management" where successful introductions in one location are replicated elsewhere without adequate consideration of ecological differences, often resulting in environmental damage and genetic contamination of native stocks.

## Background & Description

The document addresses the growing problem of fish introductions into non-native waters, reviewing literature through the early 1980s. The authors outline an eight-step procedure originally proposed by Courtenay and Robins in 1973, rooted in suggestions by Lachner et al.: (1) rationale, (2) search and selection, (3) preliminary assessment of impact, (4) publicity and review, (5) experimental research, (6) publicity and independent review, (7) implementation, and (8) monitoring. This procedure was adopted by the [[American Fisheries Society]] in 1972 as a position statement but was subsequently weakened in 1986 revisions that removed the requirement for independent agency review—a change the authors consider "fatal to the policy."

The rationale step requires demonstrating why native species are inappropriate and assessing the validity of the introduction purpose. Search and selection involves matching biological specifications against all candidate species, avoiding reliance on single expert opinions. Preliminary impact assessment includes studying species in their native biotopes. The publicity and review process solicits expert advice broadly, recognizing that valuable information exists in "gray literature." Experimental research must be conducted in confined waters to prevent escape. Most critically, independent review prevents agencies from approving their own studies and recommendations—a safeguard that protects jurisdictions that may receive introduced animals without knowledge or consent.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The document examines introductions across diverse aquatic ecosystems, from hypersaline inland seas to freshwater reservoirs and marine environments. The [[Salton Sea]] case represents a unique situation where a newly formed, essentially sterile saline body received systematic introductions of marine species from the [[Gulf of California]]. Between 1948 and 1956, 33 marine fish species were introduced; only three became established: [[Sargo]] ([[Anisotremus davidsoni]]), [[Bairdiella]] ([[Bairdiella icistia]]), and [[Orangemouth Corvina]] ([[Cynoscion xanthulus]]). These created a viable sport fishery in otherwise unproductive waters. However, later unauthorized introductions of [[Mozambique Tilapia]] ([[Tilapia mossambica]]) complicated this success, causing collapse of native [[Desert Pupfish]] ([[Cyprinodon macularius]]) populations through predation and competition.

The [[Colorado River]] system illustrates cascading ecological impacts from dam construction and fish introductions. Closure of [[Hoover Dam]] in 1935 began transforming flowing riverine habitats into lacustrine reservoirs. Native cyprinids and catostomids declined as managers introduced centrarchids ([[Largemouth Bass]], [[Bluegill]], [[Black Crappie]]) and [[Channel Catfish]]. [[Striped Bass]] ([[Morone saxatilis]]) introductions into [[Lake Mead]] in 1969 initially succeeded but depleted the [[Threadfin Shad]] ([[Dorosoma petenense]]) forage base, causing collapse of a concurrent [[Rainbow Trout]] stocking program. Investigations revealed that upstream reservoirs ([[Lake Powell]], [[Flaming Gorge Reservoir]]) acted as nutrient traps, depriving Lake Mead of plankton production necessary to support shad populations—a factor that should have been considered before introducing additional predators.

Genetic concerns emerge prominently in discussions of [[Zander]] ([[Stizostedion lucioperca]]) proposed for [[Lake Sakakawea]], North Dakota, and [[Amur Pike]] ([[Esox reicherti]]) introduced to Pennsylvania. Both species are closely related to native congeners ([[Walleye]] and [[Northern Pike]], respectively), raising risks of hybridization and genetic swamping. The document notes that [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] ([[Micropterus salmoides floridanus]]) introductions into Texas waters containing northern subspecies represent "genetic experimentation without any knowledge of the consequences." Intergeneric hybrids, such as [[Striped Bass]] × [[White Bass]] and the "blue bass" ([[Largemouth Bass]] × [[Bluegill]]) from Hawaii, further illustrate uncontrolled genetic manipulation.

## Key Findings

**Procedural Failures**: The 1972 [[American Fisheries Society]] position statement on exotic fish introductions received little recognition and was rarely followed. The 1986 revision deleted the critical requirement that introducing agencies submit results for independent evaluation, removing the cornerstone of the original procedure. State agencies demonstrate particular reluctance to adopt procedures they perceive as restricting freedom of choice, often claiming "state's rights" to introduce organisms that will clearly range beyond state boundaries without consulting neighboring jurisdictions.

**Case Study Outcomes**: The [[Salton Sea]] represents successful "shotgun" management in a virgin environment with no native fauna to lose, creating a viable sport fishery from marine species introductions. However, this success was compromised by later unauthorized [[Tilapia]] releases. Texas attempted numerous "copycat" introductions based on Salton Sea success, mostly failing due to inadequate chemical and physical water analyses. Texas introduced [[Orangemouth Corvina]], hybridized it with [[Spotted Seatrout]] ([[Cynoscion nebulosus]]), and released hybrids and parent stocks into reservoirs—raising concerns about genetic dilution of an important native food fish throughout the Gulf of Mexico.

**Colorado River Management Problems**: [[Lake Powell]] managers proposed introducing [[Rainbow Smelt]] ([[Osmerus mordax]]) to supplement declining [[Threadfin Shad]] populations supporting [[Striped Bass]] and [[Walleye]], despite evidence that upstream reservoirs function as nutrient traps. Rainbow smelt feed on eggs and larvae of other fishes and will likely pass through [[Glen Canyon Dam]] into downstream reservoirs and the [[Grand Canyon]], potentially impacting remaining native fishes. Arizona, California, and Nevada will inherit Utah's management problems. The proposal notably lacked data on factors other than predation causing fish declines or assessment of whether nutrient supplies could support plankton production for another plankton-feeding forage fish.

**Genetic Contamination Risks**: Introduction of closely related or sister species poses serious genetic risks. [[Zander]] introduction to Lake Sakakawea could hybridize with native [[Walleye]], potentially modifying gene pools. [[Amur Pike]] in Pennsylvania has already produced hybrids with [[Northern Pike]]. Texas introductions of intergeneric hybrids ([[Red Drum]] × [[Black Drum]]) into reservoirs demonstrate primitive gene splicing without understanding adaptive consequences. The extinct [[Blue Pike]] ([[Stizostedion vitreum glaucum]]) suffered genetic swamping as one cause of extinction. Subspecific mixing, such as [[Florida Largemouth Bass]] into Texas waters with northern subspecies, represents genetic experimentation with unknown consequences.

**Tiered Introduction Failures**: Managers repeatedly attempt to solve problems created by previous introductions through additional introductions, compounding difficulties. [[Mozambique Tilapia]] established in [[Salton Sea]] canals, initially for weed control, later invaded the sea itself. Utah introduced crayfish ([[Orconectes virilis]]) that altered food webs and reduced [[Rainbow Trout]] growth, then suggested introducing additional species to prey on crayfish—reminiscent of the children's story "The King, the Mice and the Cheese." This tiering of introductions is neither new nor procedurally sound.

**Marine Introduction Failures**: Hawaii represents the only area where marine fish introductions were attempted systematically. Of 21 species deliberately introduced, 7 became established, plus 6 unplanned introductions. Of these 13, 4 are rated as mistakes. [[Marquesan Sardine]] ([[Sardinella marquesensis]]) may cause clupeid poisoning (one death recorded), and [[Bluespotted Grouper]] ([[Epinephelus argus]]) causes ciguatera poisoning. The Marquesan sardine was misidentified when introduced. Only [[Bluestriped Snapper]] ([[Lutjanus kasmira]]) may have value. Hawaii now requires an Advisory Committee on Aquatic Biota to screen all applications to import live exotic fishes.

**Cichla Introductions**: Florida introduced [[Peacock Cichlid]] ([[Cichla ocellaris]]) and [[Speckled Pavon]] ([[Cichla temensis]]) into Dade County canals beginning in 1984, marketed as "peacock bass" and "butterfly cichlid" to attract anglers. The proposal assumed these predators would control previously introduced cichlids, but they may instead impact native centrarchids including [[Largemouth Bass]]. In [[Gatun Lake]], Panama, peacock cichlid "effectively eliminated six of the eight common fish species and drastically reduced a seventh," potentially increasing mosquito populations. Taxonomy of genus Cichla remains poorly studied, with probable undescribed species and at least one known misidentification, raising questions about actual species introduced.

**Jurisdictional Conflicts**: Introductions frequently create interjurisdictional problems. [[Striped Bass]] introduced to [[Lake Mead]] were claimed unable to survive passage through [[Hoover Dam]] turbines, yet appeared in [[Lake Mohave]] by 1981. [[Cichla]] releases in southeastern Florida occurred in areas connected to [[Everglades National Park]], where [[National Park Service]] managers are charged with maintaining environments without nonnative species. North Dakota's [[Zander]] proposal affects South Dakota, Nebraska, Iowa, Kansas, and Missouri downstream. Animals respect no state or national boundaries, yet agencies introduce species without consulting affected jurisdictions.

**Aquaculture Escape Risks**: Mariculture expansion using floating pens in open coastal waters creates escape risks, especially during storms. [[Atlantic Salmon]] ([[Salmo salar]]) culture in Puget Sound, Washington, outside native range, exemplifies this concern. Warmwater inland aquaculture, though seemingly "safe," also experiences escapes. The authors emphasize that "for whatever purpose an exotic fish is used, escape is virtually inevitable; thus, this eventuality should be considered." The marine aquarium trade, while unlikely to establish populations due to complex life histories and small numbers, should still prohibit releases as they are not part of approved management schemes.

**Policy Recommendations**: The authors advocate for restoration of the 1973 procedure with mandatory independent peer review, which the 1986 [[American Fisheries Society]] revision fatally weakened. They recommend establishing interjurisdictional and interagency councils like the [[Colorado River Wildlife Council]], involving all appropriate agencies and interest groups. The [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] model of publishing proposals for endangered species reintroductions and inviting public commentary represents positive management. Federal agencies considering adoption of the 1986 position statement, despite its shortcomings, represents encouraging progress. Withholding federal funds (such as [[Dingell-Johnson Federal Aid in Fish Restoration Program]]) to noncooperating states may be the only enforcement mechanism.

**Fundamental Management Failures**: The document concludes that most introductions represent attempts to correct perceived imbalances in man-made or man-altered habitats, or problems created by previous introductions, without following established procedures. "Copycat" introductions replicate successes elsewhere without considering ecological differences. Success in one situation does not predict similar performance elsewhere, even when proponents select the "perfect fish." Using introductions to correct past mistakes frequently compounds problems, indicating mismanagement or absence of management. The practice represents "at best a lottery in which an occasional lucky or even well-thought out success is replayed, only to result in losses in the form of noncorrectable environmental mistakes of varying severity."
