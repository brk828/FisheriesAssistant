# Age and Growth of the Fluke in Great South Bay and Their Significance to the Sport Fishery

#ichthyology #marine-biology #paralichthyidae

## Executive Summary

This 1961 study by [[John C Poole]] of the [[New York State Conservation Department]] determined the age and growth of [[Summer Flounder]] (*Paralichthys dentatus*, "fluke") in [[Great South Bay]] using annular marks on the [[Otolith]] (sagitta). Growth was back-calculated for 357 fish taken in 1958 and 1959. The sport fishery depended mainly on 1- and 2-year-old fluke (77% of the 1958 catch, 90% in 1959). Because 1-year-olds made up 45% (1958) and 66% (1959) of hook-and-line landings, the author concluded that annual abundance swings are driven mainly by differences in the previous year's spawning success.

## Background & Description

- **Species:** [[Paralichthys Dentatus]], a large-bodied flatfish of the Atlantic coast.
- **Research context:** Interest in fluke migration and the [[Winter Trawl Fishery]] off Virginia and North Carolina (began about 1920) prompted earlier work. [[Pearson (1932)]] described that fishery; [[Neville et al. (1939)]] noted a marked decline in New York catches; [[Westman (1946)]] used tagging in the South Shore region of Long Island and recommended a uniform size limit and careful harvest.
- **Earlier aging:** [[Hildebrand and Shroeder (1928)]] estimated age from length frequencies in Chesapeake Bay, but did not separate sexes and their aging was approximate.
- **Motivation:** Fishermen reported declining catches and reduced late-summer landings in Great South Bay, prompting the broader investigation described by [[Alperin and Poole (1956)]].

### Methods

- **Scales failed:** Scale samples from 50 fish (1956) showed no discernible annuli.
- **Otoliths used:** The sagitta, the largest of three inner-ear bones, was adopted for aging. A 1957 sample of 100 otoliths showed opaque rings formed in late winter and early spring.
- **Sampling:** [[Otter Trawl]] sampling at four locations in Great South Bay (1958–1959), plus [[Seine Netting]] of young fish in 1958 with a 300-foot small-mesh seine.
- **Storage and preparation:** Otoliths stored in 3% tri-sodium phosphate solution (after Clemens, 1950), cleared 3 days in glycerin, and measured at 9X magnification in units of 0.048 mm.
- **Young-of-the-year check:** In 1958, 890 young fish were measured in July and September. Unimodal, step-like length distributions showed a single year class, so these fish were classed as Age Group 0.
- **Validation:** Marks were tested for timing. Early-summer increments were small and late-summer increments were large in both 1958 and 1959, consistent with one annulus forming yearly at about the same time.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Fluke spend summers in nearshore New York waters and become available to the offshore winter trawl fishery off the Virginia capes, where fishing intensity was high (Westman, 1946).
- Length-based growth is fast in the first year and slows afterward, reflecting the age-structured dependence of the sport fishery on young cohorts.
- The data cover mainly fish under 4 years old. Larger fish were scarce in the Great South Bay population during 1956–1959, so the study does not represent most of the species' lifespan.

## Key Findings

**Growth by sex** (mean total length, cm, at formation of each annulus):

| Sex | Annulus I | Annulus II | Annulus III |
|---|---|---|---|
| Female | 27.1 | 37.7 | 46.5 |
| Male | 25.1 | 32.6 | 38.7 |

- Female growth was significantly faster than male growth for Annuli I–III (p < 0.001).
- Females reached 37.7 cm at Age II, nearly the 38.7 cm that males needed three years to attain.
- Between Ages II and III, males grew 5.8 cm and females 9.3 cm.

**Length–otolith relationship:**
- Measurements from 396 fluke gave a correlation coefficient of 0.89.
- Fish length was derived from half otolith width using a linear formula (intercept C = 9.16; regression y = 5.10 + 0.55701x for 385 fish).

**Age composition of the catch:**
- 1- and 2-year-olds made up 77% of the 1958 catch and 90% of the 1959 catch.
- 1-year-olds were 45% of hook-and-line landings in 1958 and 66% in 1959.
- Sport and trawl length distributions were nearly identical (1958: 1,395 sport vs. 503 trawl fish; 1959: 2,301 sport vs. 849 trawl fish), so trawl age data were treated as representative of the sport catch.

**Conclusions and management implications:**
- It takes about 1 year and 3 months of growth for fluke to reach a size that the sport fishery can land.
- Because the catch relies on 1- and 2-year-old fish and has almost no buffer from older cohorts, the sport fishery will keep fluctuating with each year's spawning success.
- Fishermen lose the growth potential (weight as well as length) of fish that are harvested young.
