# Growth, Mortality and Movements of a Sanctuary Population of Maskinonge (*Esox masquinongy*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #esocidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 study by E. J. Crossman (University of Toronto), abstracted from a 1954 M.A. thesis, examines a [[Maskinonge]] population in the Nogies Creek Fish Sanctuary, Ontario, using trap-net capture, marking and recapture from 1951 to 1953. The study estimated the population at 769 to 1,122 fish in July 1953 for the studied section, documented a high annual mortality (about 70%) after the third year of life, and described seasonal movement patterns. The author concludes that the sanctuary is underused: fish should be moved to open waters, and the sanctuary could serve as a nursery for hatchery-reared fish held for one or two years before release.

## Background & Description

- **Study area:** An 80-acre lake (Big Marsh, mean depth 8 ft) and about four miles of meandering stream (20–30 yards wide, mean depth about 10 ft). The studied section comprised the lake and roughly one mile of stream north to Watson's Bridge.
- **Context:** The [[Kawartha Lakes]] support heavy sport fishing pressure. The sanctuary was closed to angling and to fish immigration and emigration, so its population was effectively unexploited apart from natural mortality.
- **Funding:** Toronto Anglers' and Hunters' Association; the study was abstracted from a University of Toronto M.A. thesis.
- **Sampling gear:** Six-foot trap nets (2-inch mesh, 100 ft leads) and eight-foot nets (2-inch mesh, 150 ft leads), set from May to early July 1951–1953, plus October sets in 1952 and 1953 for transplanting.
- **Marking:** In 1951 fish were fin-clipped; in 1952 and 1953 fish were tagged with [[Floy Tags]]-type single-hole plastic tags sewn to the preopercle with monofilament nylon, following [[Fry 1947]]. Tagging was done in a galvanized tank once surface water exceeded 70°F.
- **Ageing:** Scales were impressed onto cellulose acetate slides using the [[Arnold Impression Method]].

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Forage and diet:** The sanctuary held an extreme abundance of four- to six-inch forage minnows, likely supporting fast first-year growth. Sunfish (9,855 caught 1951–1953) may have been eaten by II- to IV-year-old fish. Only 829 perch, suckers and bullheads combined were taken, suggesting limited forage for fish over IV years.
- **Mortality hypotheses:** Winter kill in shallow Big Marsh, or insufficient forage of adequate size, possibly leading to [[Cannibalism]] of smaller maskinonge by large individuals. Large fish (over 80 inches) reappearing in fall catches in disproportionate numbers was cited as supporting cannibalism.
- **Growth theory:** Following [[Eddy and Carlander 1942]], growth in early life is most responsive to environment, which may explain faster first-year growth in a small lake than in larger lakes.
- **Movement:** Movement was tied to season, with [[Seasonal Movement]] mainly upstream in summer and predominantly downstream in fall.

## Key Findings

**Population and sampling**
- 995 maskinonge were handled in total; 770 were marked (releases) and 225 were recaptured at least once.
- Population estimate by the Schumacher method (with DeLury's revised 95% limits): 998 (764–1,101) in 1951, 657 (508–929) in 1952, and 912 (769–1,122) in July 1953. Schnabel and Petersen estimates were also computed for comparison; Schnabel and Schumacher values for 1952 were judged low, and Petersen values high.
- Tag losses were 1.3% (1952) and 2.8% (1953).

**Size and age**
- Overall standard length ranged from 23 to 91 cm, with a mean of 53.0 cm.
- Age-groups II, III and IV predominated. Young fish (I and II, possibly III) were under-represented because of gear selectivity.

**Mortality**
- Catch curves ([[Ricker 1948]] method) showed about 70% annual mortality after the third year. Mark–recapture cohort analysis supported this figure.
- Other area lakes (Pigeon, Sturgeon, Scugog) showed about 40% annual mortality from age III onward.
- Test angling found no fish older than six years, suggesting the scarcity of older fish was not an artifact of netting.

**Growth**
- First-year length was 26.3 cm S.L., exceeding the 17.8 cm reported by [[Schloemer 1936]] for Wisconsin lakes by 8.5 cm, and slightly above the 25.5 cm reported for other Ontario lakes.
- Growth was curvilinear, with annual increments declining sharply from the first to the fourth year and remaining fairly uniform from years four to eight.
- Individual variation averaged a 15 cm spread about the mean for each year-class.

**Movement**
- 274 fish were tagged in 1952 and 332 in 1953; recaptures were 16.05% and 17.15% (1952 and 1953 tagged fish, respectively), with 54 recaptures and 47 recaptures in the years cited.
- Summer movement was limited; one fish was caught ten times at netting sites only 20 feet apart.
- Fall movement was far more common, mainly downstream. One fish travelled 25 miles from Pigeon Lake to Chemong Lake in nine months.

**Management recommendations**
- Move older fish (II-year-old and older) from the sanctuary to open lakes, where they would enter the sport fishery sooner than hatchery fish.
- Retain fish over 30 inches as breeding stock.
- Use the sanctuary as a one- to two-year nursery for hatchery fish, which could grow rapidly on abundant minnows and be marked to assess their contribution to angler catches.
- The author notes that the sanctuary had been functioning "as a museum rather than a sanctuary" until fish were utilized.
