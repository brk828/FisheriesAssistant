# Swimming Ability and Behavior of Native and Nonnative Fishes in High Water Velocities

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae #poeciliidae #catostomidae #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This study compares the swimming performance and behavior of native and nonnative fishes commonly found in [[Arizona Streams]] when exposed to high water velocities. Fish of similar mean length were subjected to stepwise increases in water velocity in a laboratory [[Swim Tunnel Testing]] apparatus until they could no longer hold station. Nonnative [[Fathead Minnow]] and [[Red Shiner]] swam with ability similar to native [[Speckled Dace]] and [[Spikedace]], and nonnative [[Green Sunfish]] were comparable to native [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Bluehead Sucker]]. Several native species also showed behavioral responses to high velocity, such as holding position in the current and using the mouth to grip the substrate, which may confer energetic advantages in swift water. The authors conclude that differences in swimming ability alone do not explain the differential removal of nonnative fishes by [[Flood Events]], and that behavioral responses to high flow are more likely the mechanism allowing native fishes to persist in streams during floods.

## Background & Description

Many native fishes of the southwestern United States have experienced declines and shifts in distribution, largely because of interactions with nonnative species. Nonnative fishes often appear to be affected disproportionately by floods, but the mechanism behind this pattern was not well understood. The authors proposed that differences in swimming ability or behavioral responses to high water velocities could explain the observed patterns of [[Differential Displacement]], particularly in canyon-bound streams where flood flows are strong and sustained.

Laboratory swimming tests were conducted on four nonnative species and several native species commonly found in Arizona streams (listed in the study's Table 1). Earlier work had documented shifts in fish assemblages toward predominantly native species after floods in southwestern streams. Swimming test procedures vary among studies, which makes direct comparisons difficult. The authors standardized their methods, using fish of similar mean length and a common stepwise protocol, to allow comparisons among species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Floods are a major disturbance in desert and semi-arid stream systems. They shift fish assemblages by displacing fish downstream, altering habitat, and changing the balance between native and nonnative species. Water velocity varies widely with discharge, so a fish's ability to hold position and its behavioral strategies in fast current are important factors in whether it persists through a flood. Behavioral strategies identified in this study, such as sheltering near the substrate and gripping with the mouth, suggest that fish can reduce energetic costs in high flow even when their maximum swimming speeds are similar to those of other species.

The study also notes that assemblage shifts after flooding may reflect differences in the physical stresses of high velocity, not only differences in competitive ability or predation. Understanding these mechanisms is relevant to managing native fish populations in regions where nonnative species are widespread.

## Key Findings

- **Similar swimming ability across species pairs:** Nonnative fathead minnows and red shiners had swimming abilities similar to native speckled dace and spikedace. Nonnative green sunfish performed comparably to native Gila topminnow and bluehead sucker.
- **Behavioral responses matter:** Several native species showed behavioral responses to high velocity, including holding position and using their mouths to grip the substrate. These responses may provide energetic advantages in swift water.
- **Swimming ability alone does not explain displacement:** Differences in swimming performance do not appear sufficient to explain the proportionate removal of nonnative fishes during floods.
- **Mechanism proposed:** Behavioral responses to high flow are considered the more likely mechanism by which native fishes persist in streams during flood events.
- **Methodological approach:** Fish of similar mean length were tested with a stepwise velocity increase in a laboratory swim tunnel until they could no longer maintain position, allowing standardized comparisons.
- **Management implication:** Flood-driven shifts in fish communities may reflect how species respond behaviorally to high velocity, which should be considered when managing native fish in [[Arizona Streams]].
