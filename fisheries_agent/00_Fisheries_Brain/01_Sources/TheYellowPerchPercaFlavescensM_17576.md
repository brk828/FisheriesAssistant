# The Yellow Perch, *Perca flavescens*, in the Klamath River

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percidae

## Executive Summary

This 1956 study by Millard Coots of the [[California Department of Fish and Game]] examines the [[Yellow Perch]] (*Perca flavescens*) in the [[Klamath River]] of California, a population that was not native to the drainage. The primary aim, begun in 1951, was to determine whether yellow perch affect young [[Salmonids]] in the river. Stomach examinations of 731 perch and live-trap tests indicated that perch will eat young salmonids under artificial conditions, but no salmonids were found in wild perch stomachs. The study also covers age and growth, length-weight relationships, condition, spawning, parasitism, and the sport fishery.

## Background & Description

- **Native range:** Eastern United States and Canada, including the Hudson Bay drainage south to Ohio, and Nova Scotia to the Dakotas. Wider distribution resulted from fish-cultural activities.
- **Introduction to California:** Reported by [[Curtis (1949)]] to have first been introduced in 1891, with several later importations. By 1918 they were widely distributed in the [[Sacramento-San Joaquin River]] drainage, then appeared to disappear, though occasional specimens are still taken, concentrated in Snodgrass Slough, Sacramento County.
- **First Klamath River record:** Four specimens were brought by anglers from [[Copco Lake]] to the [[Fall Creek State Fish Hatchery]] on May 4, 1946. A 6-inch perch was later collected above the U.S. Highway 101 bridge at Klamath, near the river mouth, on June 14, 1951.
- **Origin hypothesis:** Perch probably either descended the river from Oregon or were "unofficially" planted in Copco Lake. Their origin is not definitively known.
- **Other localities:** Present in Lost River near Tulelake (Siskiyou County) and in irrigation canals east of Tulelake (Modoc County).
- **Collection methods:** Riffle-type [[Fyke Net]]s of one-half-inch stretched mesh, [[Gill Net]]s, chemical treatment of backwater dredge ponds, hook and line, and a mechanical fish trap at the [[Klamathon Egg Collecting Station]], 16.5 miles below [[Copco Dam]]. Fyke and gill netting were unsatisfactory for stomach analysis because fish digested or regurgitated food during prolonged confinement.
- **Associated fishes:** [[Pacific Lamprey]], [[King Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus tshawytscha*), [[Silver Salmon]] (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*), [[Steelhead Rainbow Trout]], [[Klamath Small-scaled Sucker]], [[Klamath Tui Chub]], [[Klamath Speckled Dace]], [[Brown Bullhead]], [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Pumpkinseed]], and sculpin (*Cottus* sp.).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat use:** Yellow perch are well distributed in the Klamath River wherever suitable conditions exist, notably backwater dredge ponds and quiet stream stretches. They are not numerous relative to other fishes in these habitats.
- **Downstream movement:** A marked influx of perch from Copco Lake into the Klamath River occurs when water spills over the crest of the 126-foot-high Copco Dam. In January 1951, considerable numbers were trapped and fyke-netted at Klamathon during spill, with fewer caught when no water was spilled. Many trapped during spill were alive. Survival through the power generating turbines, described as the reportedly harmful Francis type, is unknown.
- **Competition and predation concerns:** Perch can compete with trout for food and prey on their young. Studies cited indicate poor trout survival and growth where perch are present. Perch can also become stunted, becoming a "problem" fish, especially in smaller waters or under light angling pressure.
- **Diet:** Small crustaceans, mollusks (particularly small snails), aquatic insect larvae and nymphs, and fish. Algae, aquatic vegetation, and bark fragments were also present.
- **Predators:** Yellow perch have been recovered from the stomachs of fish-eating birds, especially mergansers. An adult steelhead caught near Fall Creek on October 12, 1951, had a 4-inch perch in its stomach.
- **Spawning environment:** The river was spawning at water temperatures of 44 to 59°F during the study period (see Key Findings).
- **Parasites and disease:** Very little evidence was observed. A few fish had lamprey scars, and several bore identical lesions posterior to the soft dorsal fin in March 1953. Parasitologist Harold Wolf found no myxosporidia but many bacteria in the lesions. No parasitic nematodes (*Contracaecum spiculigerum*, common in Klamath centrarchids and salmonids) were found during stomach examinations.

## Key Findings

**Predation on young salmonids**
- Of 731 perch stomachs examined (474 empty), 257 contained food and 34 contained fish remains (small chubs, dace, suckers, yellow perch, and green sunfish). No salmonids were found.
- An additional 149 perch were checked for salmonids; none were found.
- Fish did not appear in the diet until about the second year.
- Live-trap and aquarium tests:
  - Six 8-inch perch and 21 steelhead fingerlings (about 2 inches) in a floating live trap: all fingerlings gone within 5 days.
  - 17 king salmon fingerlings with one 7-inch perch in an aerated aquarium: all eaten in 11 days.
  - 15 steelhead fingerlings with two adult perch: all consumed within 72 hours.

**Age and growth**
- Scales from 140 perch were read. Age classes: I (39), II (61), III (33), IV (4), V (3).
- Average calculated fork length at each annulus: 3.38 in (I), 5.64 in (II), 7.30 in (III), 8.63 in (IV), 10.10 in (V).
- Average calculated increments: 3.38, 2.26, 1.66, 1.33, and 1.47 inches.
- Annulus formation was completed March through May. Season growth was completed in October and November.
- Growth was comparable to other waters, per comparison with [[Carlander (1950, 1953)]].

**Length, weight, and condition**
- Total of 848 fish measured, ranging from 1.2 to 10.4 inches fork length.
- Fork length and weight were taken from 276 fish.
- Least squares length-weight relationship: log W (g) = −4.9915 + 3.0343 log L (mm). The Summary section reports the slope as 3.0348, a discrepancy within the source.
- Condition coefficients were in the 1.65 to 1.78 range (Table 4), with little change across lengths. Condition was well below Carlander's average values, possibly related to late-fall and early-winter collection, when falling temperatures may have reduced metabolic rate. The fish were not sexed.

**Spawning**
- Reported spawning temperatures: 45 to 55°F (Curtis, 1949). Eggs are laid in ribbons.
- No spawning was observed directly.
- March 28, 1951: egg ribbons (about 1.25 in wide, 6 to 12 in long) recovered from tules about half a mile below Fall Creek. A fyke net the next day caught 19 ripe perch (7 to nearly 10 in). River temperature at noon was 47°F.
- First spent perch: April 4, 1951. Last ripe fish: May 18, 1951, at a morning temperature of 59°F. April 1951 water temperatures ranged from 44 to 56°F.

**Sport fishery**
- Negligible compared with salmon and steelhead fisheries. Trout anglers usually discard perch as "trash" fish; catfish anglers in dredge ponds often keep them.
- Most angler-caught fish are 5 to 8 inches long. No bag limit or season applies.
- Copco Lake (over 1,500 surface acres) supports an abundant population with few anglers. Unverified reports of fish up to one pound exist; the largest observed was about 12 inches and under 0.5 lb.
- Popular baits: worms, salmon eggs, small crayfish, and grasshoppers.
- Eating quality is high, with flesh described as white, firm, flaky, and sweet. Skinning and filleting removed the "fishy or mossy tang" that anglers associated with Klamath fish in warm summer months.

**Management conclusions**
- Introduction of yellow perch into other waters should be approached with caution, particularly where trout are present.
- Additional study is needed on fish survival through Copco's power turbines.
