## Executive Summary

This 1960 paper by Robert E. Vincent (Cornell University) documents experimental introductions of fresh-water [[Alewife]] (*Alosa pseudoharengus*, syn. *Pomolobus pseudoharengus*) from [[Cayuga Lake]] into various Adirondack Mountain and Finger Lakes region ponds during summer 1958. The study had two objectives: (1) to develop a practical method for transporting alewives (as eggs or adults), and (2) to determine survival and reproductive success following introduction into novel water bodies. The author found that artificial propagation via egg incubation was impractical due to asynchronous ovarian maturation and fungal infection risk, but that careful transport of live adults enabled successful establishment and natural spawning in several test ponds, demonstrating the viability of alewives as a transplantable forage fish for salmonid-stocked lakes.

## Background & Description

- **Landlocked/dwarf freshwater alewife (Cayuga Lake stock):** average length ~5 inches (127 mm)
- **Lake Ontario alewives:** 6–10 inches (Stone 1947)
- **Marine (sea-run) alewives:** 10–11 inches (Belding 1921)
- **Scales:** extremely deciduous; easily dislodged by thrashing of co-seined larger fish
- **Skeletal structure:** a "peculiar" frontal cranial structure renders alewives highly susceptible to cerebral hemorrhage from handling trauma — identified as primary cause of capture/transport mortality
- **Sexual dimorphism in ratio:** 6.6 males per female recorded in a 3-week Cayuga Lake sample (n=641), though gravid females proved more fragile/susceptible to handling mortality than males
- **Growth in study ponds:**
  - Pond F adults: grew from 5.0 in (33 fish/lb) prestocking to 5.9 in (10.7 fish/lb) after 55 days
  - Pond F young-of-year: averaged 1.7 in after ~40 days; grew to 2.9 in mean length by following spring (May 1959)
  - Black Pond adults: grew 1.3 in (to 6.3 in, 10/lb) over the season
  - Black Pond young-of-year: bimodal distribution — 3.0 in (95/lb, n=65, range 2.7–3.5 in) and 1.3 in (1,500/lb, n=67, range 1.1–1.7 in), indicating two distinct spawning events

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Role as forage fish:** Central management interest is the alewife's function as forage for [[Warm-Water Fish]] and salmonid species, including [[Lake Trout]] (*Cristivomer namaycush*), [[Landlocked Salmon]] (*Salmo salar*), and [[Kokanee]] (*Oncorhynchus nerka*).
- **Habitat associations tested:**
  - **Pond I** (Cornell Experimental Fish Hatchery): 0.2-acre, 5-ft deep, dense *Chara* mat, sparse plankton — good adult survival, no reproduction detected
  - **Pond F**: similar size/depth, no *Chara*, fertilized (100 lb of 6-12-6), strong plankton bloom — successful reproduction (60 YOY recaptured) and overwinter survival
  - **Chatfield Pond**: cool, spring-fed, 0.2 acre, supports [[Smallmouth Bass]] (*Micropterus dolomieu*), profuse *Chara* growth — total loss of stocked population by August
  - **Black Pond**: 25-acre, cool, deep (58 ft) Adirondack lake with diverse native fish community (lake trout, landlocked salmon, kokanee, [[Common Sucker]], [[Common Shiner]], [[Bluntnose Minnow]], [[Northern Redbelly Dace]], [[Brown Bullhead]]) — successful establishment with two distinct spawning cohorts recovered
- **Predation interactions:** Overwintering landlocked salmon (6–8 in) observed attempting to feed on young alewives in Pond F; several dead alewives and salmon found together after ice-out, suggesting predation-related mortality event.
- **Vegetation effects:** Dense *Chara* growth correlated with reproductive failure in both Pond I and Chatfield Pond, suggesting this macrophyte may inhibit spawning or egg/larval survival — a key ecological caveat for site selection in future introductions.
- **Thermal physiology:** Late-season mortality during transport was not attributable to high ambient temperature (all fish collected at 66–67°F lake surface temps); author cites [[Hoar 1952]] hypothesis that exhausted thyroid function post-spawning may be the physiological trigger for characteristic alewife die-offs, consistent with Graham's (1956) acclimation-failure explanation.

## Key Findings

1. **Collection methods:** Nylon seining (75-ft, 0.25-in mesh) at gravel shoals was most effective (up to 500 fish/haul); trap netting produced higher catch (~1,000 fish) but unacceptable immediate mortality (23%) from webbing-induced cerebral trauma.
2. **Artificial propagation is impractical at scale:** Ovaries mature asynchronously (intermittent natural spawning), making it difficult to harvest large batches of ripe eggs without unripe "green eggs"; incubation temperatures (60–70°F) promote rapid fungal growth.
3. **Transport tolerances established:**
   - Up to 500 fish (7 fish/gallon) per 90-gallon tank with oxygen aeration, 65–70°F water
   - 4-hour haul with 350 fish/tank: 6% mortality
   - 8-hour haul: ~10% mortality
   - Mortality spiked sharply after spawning began (late July): 33% (4-hr trip) to 90% (subsequent trips), unaffected by cooling water to 45°F
   - Differential mortality by sex during transit: sexing of 161 dead fish showed disproportionate female mortality relative to the 6.6:1 male:female ratio in the source population, indicating gravid females are more fragile
4. **Successful introductions:** Two of four evaluated ponds (Pond F and Black Pond) showed successful natural spawning, growth, and (in Pond F) overwinter survival of young — demonstrating that transplanted freshwater alewives can establish self-sustaining populations under suitable conditions.
5. **Failed introductions:** Pond I and Chatfield Pond, both characterized by dense *Chara* growth, showed no evidence of successful reproduction, implicating submerged vegetation density as a limiting ecological factor.
6. **Optimal timing:** Early-season transport (before spawning onset) is recommended to minimize handling/transport mortality; risk increases substantially as the spawning season progresses.
7. **Management implication:** The study supports the feasibility of using landlocked/dwarf alewife stocks (e.g., from [[Cayuga Lake]]) as a versatile forage fish for stocking Adirondack trout and salmon lakes, contingent on careful transport timing and receiving-pond habitat characteristics.
