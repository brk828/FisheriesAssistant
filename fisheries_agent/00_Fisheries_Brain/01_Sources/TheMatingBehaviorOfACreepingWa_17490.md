# Mating Behavior of the Creeping Water Bug, *Ambrysus occidentalis* (Hemiptera: Naucoridae)

#entomology #aquatic-insects #naucoridae

## Executive Summary

This 1974 study in *The American Midland Naturalist* (Vol. 92, No. 1, pp. 234–239) describes the behavior of the [[Creeping Water Bug]], [[Ambrysus Occidentalis]], a predatory aquatic insect in the family [[Naucoridae]]. The author, George D. Constantz, presents what is described as the first behavioral account for the family. It has two aims: to build a behavioral inventory (an [[Ethogram]]) and to describe [[Mating Behavior]]. All observations were made in the laboratory on bugs collected from two natural populations in Arizona. From 10 mating sequences, the author built a model of mating with time intervals. The bugs showed no behavior that could be read as [[Courtship]], and the paper offers three hypotheses to explain this.

## Background & Description

- **Group:** Creeping water bugs are aquatic predators found across North and South America. The taxonomy of Naucoridae had been studied intensively, but their behavior was "virtually unknown" at the time.
- **Species:** *A. occidentalis* (LaRivers, 1951) is a common naucorid in southern Arizona. It is about 12 mm long and somewhat flattened, with a cryptically colored dark dorsal surface and thickened front (raptorial) femora.
- **Sampling sites:**
  - [[Monkey Spring]] (Santa Cruz Co.): bugs cling to the sides and bottom of a cement canal that drains the spring.
  - [[Seven Springs]] (Maricopa Co.): bugs cling to exposed tree roots on a creek bank.
- **Collection and housing:** Natural populations were sampled on 10 March and 14 April 1973 and transported immediately to the lab. Two or more individuals were held in glass bowls (26 cm diameter) with one rock (about 6 × 8 cm) as substrate. Behavior was recorded on magnetic tape.
- **Inventory method:** Two individuals were introduced serially in all possible sexual combinations. Behavior patterns characteristic of the species were noted, along with the stimuli that consistently released them. The full inventory is listed in Appendix 1 (about 40 named behavior patterns, summarized below).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Predation and prey.** At Monkey Spring the bugs probably prey on young [[Poeciliopsis]] fish that swim in the slower water along the canal walls. Feeding is simple. A bug orients toward fin movements of nearby fish, then performs a [[Raptorial Freeze]] or a lunge. After a successful capture, the first puncture is usually just posterodorsal to a gill. Within an hour the bug moves its bite to other body areas by manipulating the prey with its raptorial tibiae.

**Population density and spacing.** At Monkey Spring, density was about one individual per 3 sq ft of stream bottom. The author considers this spacing wide enough that aggressive encounters are infrequent in the field.

**Intraspecific aggression and cannibalism.** In the observation bowls, first encounters were long (up to 8 sec) and very animated. Both individuals tried to turn their opponent's ventral side toward their beak. Aggression decreased over time, and within 1 hour it was almost nil. Aggression often ended in [[Cannibalism]].

**Habitat and behavior.** Terrestrial crawling is rapid, and the bugs can right themselves by "flipping." Bubble replenishment is linked to water depth. At about 4 inches the bug floats to the surface, while at about 6 inches it swims headfirst to the surface, tips its abdomen up, and then returns to the substrate. [[Bubble Stroking]] by the rear legs probably increases oxygen diffusion into the air store (after Wigglesworth, 1964).

## Key Findings

**1. Behavioral inventory.** Appendix 1 lists the species' repertoire, including:
- Communication and aggression: [[Abdominal Bobbing]] (possibly aggressive intent), fencing (about 2 sec), and random leg movements when a copulating male is bumped by a third bug.
- Locomotion: aquatic and terrestrial crawling, swimming, stalking, scooping (possibly feeding), and flipping.
- Feeding: lunges, grabs, raptorial freezes (up to 10 min), and exploratory groping.
- Maintenance and reproduction: bubble replenishment, bubble stroking, genitalia wiping, and rhythmic genitalia extension.

**2. Mating sequence model (Fig. 1).** The model is based on 10 mating sequences and runs roughly as follows:
- The male orients to the female and grabs and holds her pronotum (holding on).
- He mounts her and rubs her genitalia with his rear legs.
- Genitalia eversion and copulation follow.
- During copulation, the male performs cycles of genitalia pumping and wiping, and multiple-stroke leg movements.
- The pair uncouples, and the male rides the female before dismounting.

**3. No courtship was observed.** The author states that no component of the sequence could be interpreted as courtship. The male's orientation toward the female appeared to be a nonspecific response to movement.

**4. Possible sites of mate choice and isolation.**
- Struggling by the female is the only step where mate choice could have occurred.
- Rubbing of the female's genitalia by the male may be important for interspecific reproductive isolation. In one observed case, a male that failed to rub the female's genitalia had labored coupling and a poor fit.

**5. Timing.** Table 1 gives mean intervals and ranges for the numbered steps in Figure 1, several of them estimated.
- Cycle a was normally repeated three or four times before uncoupling (mean for this cycle 6.3 min, range 3–16 min).
- Cycle b was normally repeated 3–5 times. It was skipped once.
- The longest interval listed (the final step) had a mean of 82.2 min (range 25–174 min).
- Single-stroke genitalia wiping was faster at the start of a series (about 1.0 strokes/sec) than at the end (about 0.5 strokes/sec).

**6. Promiscuity.** Individuals of both sexes mated repeatedly in the lab, and the behavior of a female did not change after her first mating. Males attempted copulation with other males. This homosexual behavior matched heterosexual behavior until genital contact, when the "superior" male immediately released the other male and swam away. Sexual discrimination therefore occurred only at genital contact.

**7. Hypotheses for the lack of courtship.** The author offers three explanations:
1. **Infrequent contact:** if sexes meet rarely, promiscuity without courtship may be favored, because the cost of copulatory attempts with other males is outweighed by never missing a potential mate (after Emlen, 1973).
2. **Low need for isolating mechanisms:** if the species seldom meets related species, strong specific discrimination by females may not be favored, so male courtship would offer little advantage (after Mayr, 1963).
3. **Both combined:** the author suggests this may apply at Monkey Spring. No other naucorid lives in that part of the system, and the density of about 1 per 3 sq ft may make sexual encounters infrequent.

**8. Contrast with other Hemiptera.** Repeated copulations within one mount (cycle b) appear to differ from typical hemipteran mating. In *Rhinocoris* (Parker, 1969), [[Lygus]] (Strong et al., 1970), and [[Pyrrhocoris]] (Zdarek, 1970), males leave soon after uncoupling. Those species may meet females more often, through host plants or higher density, so males gain from inseminating many females. In contrast, the author suggests *A. occidentalis* males seldom meet females, and their fitness may depend on thoroughly inseminating fewer females.

**9. Suggested future work.** The author recommends more information on the life history, behavior, and density of this and other naucorids to test these hypotheses.
