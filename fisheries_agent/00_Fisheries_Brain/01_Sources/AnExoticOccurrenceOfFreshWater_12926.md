# An Exotic Occurrence of Fresh Water Drum Fish

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Sciaenidae

## Executive Summary
This short report by William J. Breed (Museum of Northern Arizona) documents the anomalous discovery of a single tooth on the surface of the [[Supai Formation]] near the top of Court House Rock, Arizona. Initially suspected to be a Permian-age fossil due to its geological context, the specimen was subsequently identified by Dr. Robert R. Miller (Curator of Fishes, University of Michigan Museum of Zoology) as the upper pharyngeal molar plate of **Aplodinotus grunniens** (the [[Freshwater Drum]]). Because this species does not naturally occur anywhere near the Verde Valley of Arizona, the find represents an exotic, out-of-context occurrence — almost certainly the result of human transport rather than natural deposition. The authors propose the tooth may have served as an ornament or fetish object carried into the region by prehistoric Native American peoples.

## Background & Description
- **Specimen identified:** A single tooth, described as having a "strange" and "weird" appearance.
- **Anatomical identity:** Upper pharyngeal molar plate — a hardened, grinding dental structure characteristic of the [[Sciaenidae]] family, used for crushing hard-shelled prey (e.g., mollusks).
- **Preservation state:** Noted as having a "fresh appearance," which was the initial clue that the specimen was not contemporaneous with the surrounding Permian-age [[Supai Formation]] rock — a key taphonomic observation that flagged it as anachronistic and intrusive.
- **Species diagnosis:** Confirmed taxonomically as *Aplodinotus grunniens* Rafinesque by a fish taxonomy specialist, based on the distinctive molariform pharyngeal tooth morphology unique to this species.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
- **Native range of Aplodinotus grunniens:** Widely distributed across the [[Mississippi Basin]], a large freshwater riverine and lacustrine system.
- **Nearest population to find site:** The Rio Grande Valley, Texas — still hundreds of miles from the Verde Valley, Arizona discovery site.
- **Habitat type:** Freshwater drum are benthic-feeding fish typically associated with large rivers and lakes, using their molariform pharyngeal teeth to feed on hard-bodied invertebrates such as mussels and snails.
- **Context of discovery:** The find site itself (Supai Formation, Court House Rock) is an arid terrestrial/geological setting entirely disconnected from the species' aquatic habitat, reinforcing that the tooth's presence is due to anthropogenic transport rather than any natural ecological or depositional process.

## Key Findings
- The tooth was found on the **surface** of the Supai Formation, not embedded within it, and was initially mistaken for a Permian fossil due to its geological context.
- Fresh preservation state indicated recent origin rather than deep-time fossilization.
- Positive identification as *Aplodinotus grunniens* pharyngeal molar plate confirms the specimen is biologically inconsistent with the local Arizona fauna and geologic age.
- The nearest extant population of [[Freshwater Drum]] is in the Rio Grande Valley, Texas, implying long-distance transport of the tooth into the Verde Valley region.
- The authors hypothesize prehistoric human transport — potentially as an **ornament or fetish object** — given the tooth's unusual, eye-catching morphology, though this cultural interpretation remains speculative.
- The paper calls for documentation of similar exotic finds elsewhere, suggesting this occurrence may reflect a broader (but underreported) pattern of trade or ceremonial use of aquatic animal parts among prehistoric peoples of the American Southwest.
- This finding highlights the value of interdisciplinary collaboration between [[Ichthyology]], geology, and archaeology in correctly interpreting anomalous specimens in the fossil/geologic record.
