## Executive Summary

This document is a U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service Biological Opinion (dated February 7, 1994) issued under Section 7 of the [[Endangered Species Act]] regarding a proposed headcut repair project on [[Cienega Creek]], Pima County, Arizona. The species of concern is the endangered [[Gila Topminnow]] (*Poeciliopsis occidentalis*), a small livebearing fish restricted to the Gila, Sonora, and de la Concepcion River drainages. The Service concluded that the proposed streambed stabilization project is **not likely to jeopardize** the continued existence of the Gila topminnow, despite anticipated short-term adverse effects, because it prevents long-term habitat destruction from an advancing headcut that threatens to drain 2.5 miles of upstream cienega (marsh-like) habitat. No critical habitat has been designated for this species.

## Background & Description

- **Life history strategy**: Small-bodied, short-lived, livebearing (viviparous) fish with high fecundity, characteristics that make population size estimation difficult via standard sampling methods.
- **Body size/type**: Small fish (implied by comparison to invasive [[Mosquitofish]], *Gambusia affinis*, a similarly small poeciliid competitor/predator).
- No detailed morphometric data (fin counts, coloration, length measurements) are provided in this regulatory document, as it is a habitat-management and compliance opinion rather than a taxonomic description.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Habitat type**: Endemic to "[[Cienega]]" habitats — spring-fed, low-gradient wetland/marsh stream systems characterized by surface flow across broad, vegetated floodplains rather than confined incised channels.
- **Geographic range**: Historically among the most common fishes in the Gila River and tributaries (Hubbs and Miller 1941); now restricted to a fraction of historic range across Arizona, New Mexico, and Sonora, Mexico.
- **Population status**: Only **nine natural populations** remain, of which Cienega Creek is the largest and most ecologically intact. Cienega Creek is one of only **two natural populations on public lands**, and one of only **three populations of the nine** free of nonnative mosquitofish.
- **Community structure**: Unusually, the middle and upper reaches of Cienega Creek retain a fish community with **no nonnative fish species present** — a rare condition among remaining Gila topminnow habitats (Simms 1991; Brown and Abarca 1992).
- **Threats**:
  - Habitat destruction: water diversion, stream downcutting (headcutting), backwater draining, vegetation clearing, channelization, water impoundment.
  - Biological threats: competition with and predation by nonnative [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*).
  - Physical process of concern: an unchecked headcut (4.5 ft high) with no natural grade control (e.g., bedrock) could migrate upstream, converting shallow marshy cienega habitat into a deeply incised, fast-flowing, structurally simple channel — degrading habitat suitability.
- **Reintroduction efforts**: Historically poor success — only ~9% survival across more than 300 stocked sites since the 1930s, underscoring the importance of protecting remaining natural populations like Cienega Creek.
- **Most stable subpopulation**: Located above the confluence with [[Gardner Canyon]], approximately 0.75 miles upstream of the headcut site, based on 1993 post-flood sampling.

## Key Findings

- **Biological Opinion**: Non-jeopardy determination — the proposed headcut repair is not likely to jeopardize the continued existence of the Gila topminnow.
- **Project description**: Stabilization of a 4.5-ft headcut via slope regrading, erosion control matting, rock placement, sandbags/logs anchored with rebar, and revegetation with native [[Willow]] (*Salix gooddingii*) and [[Deergrass]] (*Muhlenbergia repens*); temporary electric fencing to exclude livestock for 100 yards (50 yards each direction) around the repair site.
- **Effects analysis**:
  - Long-term effects: highly **beneficial** — prevents upstream headcut migration and preserves high-quality upstream habitat.
  - Short-term effects: **adverse** — potential direct mortality of fish during in-channel construction, increased downstream sedimentation, and risk of accidental petroleum/pollutant introduction.
  - Risk of project failure/further destabilization deemed **low** relative to likelihood of successful stabilization.
- **Cumulative effects**: None anticipated; no non-Federal actions identified as reasonably certain to co-occur.
- **Incidental Take Statement**:
  - Take cannot be quantified numerically for Gila topminnow due to sampling limitations and rapid population turnover.
  - Take threshold trigger: **more than 15 dead fish of any species** observed within or downstream of the project area during implementation triggers reinitiation of consultation.
- **Reasonable and Prudent Measures**: (1) minimize take during project actions; (2) maintain accurate records of actions/effects on the species and habitat.
- **Terms and Conditions**: Minimize disturbance to wetted channel; prevent pollutant entry into surface waters; submit written report with photo documentation within two months of project completion; notify Service before any repair/replacement work.
- **Conservation Recommendations**: Monitor headcut repair site at regular intervals for 1–2 years post-implementation, including photographic documentation, to assess success/failure of stabilization.
- **Regulatory context**: Reinitiation of consultation required if take limits are exceeded, new information reveals unconsidered impacts, the action is modified, or new species/critical habitat designations affect the action area.
