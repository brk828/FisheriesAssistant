# Early Pleistocene Paleoclimatic Record from the Sonoran Desert, Arizona

#palynology #paleoecology #pleistocene-pollen #paleoclimatology

## Executive Summary

This document is a two-part *Science* item (Vol. 138, January 1961). The first part is a short behavioral-pharmacology note by Weitzman, Ross, Hodos and Galambos on morphine's effect on lever pressing, proposed as a tool for studying pain perception. The main body is a palynological report by Jane Gray (University of Arizona, Tucson) on three pollen spectra from early Pleistocene lake sediments in Safford Valley, Arizona. The pollen suggests a cooler and/or wetter climate than today, with vegetation zones lowered and some northern taxa present. The author links the record tentatively to the Nebraskan glacial stage. No fish, aquatic invertebrates or aquatic chemistry are reported.

## Background & Description

**Behavioral note (Weitzman et al.).** The authors report that lever-pressing behavior was altered by morphine in proportion to dose, and that the duration of the effect also rose with dose. Because the response tracks morphine's analgesic action, the authors argue that the afferent input driving lever pressing is pain-related. The paper presents the method as a possible tool for pain research. The excerpt gives no sample sizes or statistics beyond a reference to a dose-response figure. The note cites [[Békésy Audiometry Methods]] and [[Brain Stimulation Research]] in its references.

**Pollen study (Gray).** The paper addresses how Pleistocene climate fluctuations affected biota in nonglaciated regions, using [[Stratigraphic Pollen Analysis]]. Its setting is the Safford–San Simon valley trough in Graham County, southeastern Arizona. Late Cenozoic lacustrine and alluvial sediments accumulated there until about the middle Pleistocene. Later down-cutting by the Gila and San Simon rivers exposed these deposits to depths of several hundred feet. Vertebrate fossils from the upper sequence are considered transitional Blancan–Irvingtonian, roughly middle-Kansan in North American glacial terms. These fossils lie 400 to 600 ft above the pollen-bearing valley-floor sediments.

Pollen came from shallow drill cores made during watershed dam exploration. Well-preserved, abundant grains came from only one core. Some core cuttings and a few outcrop samples also yielded pollen, though outcrop material was mostly barren. The author states that the stratigraphic control is insufficient to firmly relate the column 1 spectrum to the others.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Setting:** Valley floor elevations are about 3000 to 3200 ft. The valley borders the Pinaleno Mountains (about 10,700 ft) to the southwest and the Peloncillo and Gila ranges (about 7000 ft) to the northeast.
- **Climate today:** Mean annual rainfall is about 10 inches, mostly from short, intense July–August thundershowers. Creosote bush (*Larrea*), saltbush (*Atriplex*), snakeweed (*Gutierrezia*), burroweed (*Haplopappus*), mesquite (*Prosopis*) and *Ephedra* dominate the valley floor.
- **Modern vegetation zones (rising with elevation and rainfall):**
  - Desert grassland: about 3500–4800 ft
  - Oak (juniper–pine) woodland: about 4800–6500 ft
  - Ponderosa pine: about 6500–8000 ft
  - Douglas fir–white fir: above about 8000 ft
- **Pollen transport:** Modern sediment samples show that pollen reaches the valley by wind, water, or both. Juniper, oak and pine were higher in Gila River mud than in storage-tank gyttja. The author attributes this to limited water transport from upland Gila Range sources.
- **Paleo-lake:** Lake beds are about 2000 ft thick and extend over an area of at least 35 miles. Water-level changes inferred from sediments suggest climatic oscillations, possibly of interstadial magnitude.

## Key Findings

1. **Behavioral method (Weitzman et al.):** Morphine altered lever pressing in proportion to dose, and effect duration also correlated with dose. The authors interpret this as evidence that the lever-pressing input relates to pain, making the method a candidate tool for [[Pain Perception Research]].

2. **Early Pleistocene vegetation was markedly different from today.** High percentages of juniper, sagebrush, pine and oak in the core spectra contrast with the modern lower Sonoran flora. Pine reached 52.6 percent in the Kennecott core. The base of the sequence (43 to 48 ft) suggests an open savanna–parkland with grasses, forbs and perennial shrubs. Woodland became more important in the uppermost horizon, with pine dominant at low elevations.

3. **Boreal and subalpine taxa reached the lowlands.** Pollen of alder, birch, Douglas fir (?), fir, hop-hornbeam, maple and spruce appears in the spectra. The author reads this as evidence that vegetation zones were lowered during the early Pleistocene.

4. **Range extensions:** Elm pollen indicates a genus no longer native to the Southwest. Hop-hornbeam and birch also point to northern species reaching this latitude.

5. **Sagebrush paradox:** High sagebrush (*Artemisia*) percentages may reflect immigration of a northern shrubby *Artemisia*, or a shift in abundance of local herbaceous species under altered climate. Contemporary sediments show only 1.95 percent sagebrush pollen even in Huachuca Canyon, which the author calls high for southern Arizona. The author therefore sees the fossil abundance as unexplained by present distributions.

6. **Climate inference:** The record indicates a climate at least temporarily cooler and probably wetter than today. The author also suggests that winter rains played a larger role than now. The inverse relationship between tree pollen plus sage and chenopods supports a moist-to-dry shift.

7. **Glacial correlation (tentative):** Based on stratigraphic position, the "cool" flora and the large lake, the Safford spectra may reflect some phase of the [[Nebraskan Glacial Stage]]. Alternatively, they may record a pre-Nebraskan cooling equivalent to the [[Donau Glacial Stage]]. The author states that more data on sedimentation rates and stratigraphic control are needed for exact correlation.

8. **Data limitations:** The sample base is small and largely from one productive core. Stratigraphic control is limited. The author argues the records are still worth publishing because more data may not be available for several years.
