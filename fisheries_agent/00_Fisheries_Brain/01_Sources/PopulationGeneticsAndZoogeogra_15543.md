# Population Genetics and Zoogeography of Australian Freshwater Golden Perch (*Macquaria ambigua*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #percichthyidae

## Executive Summary

This 1992 study by [[Michael K. Musyl]] and [[Clive P. Keenan]] used starch-gel and polyacrylamide electrophoresis to compare golden perch (*Macquaria ambigua*) from four drainage basins on either side of the [[Great Dividing Range]]. Of 18 presumed loci, nine were polymorphic or showed fixed differences among populations. The authors conclude that the [[Lake Eyre Basin]] population is probably an unrecognized allopatric species, that the [[Fitzroy Basin]] population is best treated as an allopatric subspecies of the Murray-Darling form, and that the [[Bulloo River]] population is either an intermediate evolutionary unit or a hybrid. Divergence is linked to Tertiary tectonic and climatic events.

## Background & Description

- **Study taxon:** [[Golden Perch]] (*Macquaria ambigua*), a basal percoid of the family [[Percichthyidae]], known as yellowbelly in New South Wales and callop in South Australia.
- **Size and maturity:** Golden perch can reach 23 kg and 76 cm total length but are commonly under 5 kg. Males mature at two to three years (about 19 cm); females at about four years (about 40 cm).
- **Reproduction:** Eggs are nonadhesive and pelagic, hatching in about 33 h. Spawning occurs from October to March, when water temperatures exceed 23.6°C and water levels rise, for example after spring-summer floods. These observations come from Murray-Darling populations and may not apply elsewhere.
- **Taxonomic context:** [[MacDonald 1978]] synonymized three Australian percichthyid genera into *Macquaria*. [[Johnson 1984]] considered the broader Percichthyidae polyphyletic. Earlier electrophoretic work on [[Macquarie Perch]] and [[Murray Cod]] had already revealed east-west divergence across the Great Dividing Range.
- **Conservation and fishery:** Golden perch support recreational fisheries. Commercial catches fell between 1976 and 1984 (New South Wales: 241,769 to 77,663 kg; Victoria: 7,127 to 3,221 kg; Murray River reach fishery in South Australia: 122,744 to 96,649 kg). Habitat modification, pollution, dams and overfishing are implicated in the decline.
- **Prior morphology:** [[Musyl 1990]] showed that Dawson River fish could be separated from Murray-Darling fish with 100% confidence using ten morphological characters.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Sampling:** Tissue samples were collected from July 1986 to December 1990 from localities in four basins:
  - Murray-Darling (Murray River, Lake Keepit, Condamine River, Murrumbidgee and Wakool rivers, among others)
  - Lake Eyre (Barcoo and Diamantina rivers)
  - Bulloo-Bancannia internal drainage (Bulloo River)
  - Fitzroy (Dawson and Nogoa rivers, east of the Great Dividing Range)
- **Barriers:** The Great Dividing Range separates coastal and interior basins. Freshwater fish east of it are thought to have been isolated during Pliocene tectonic events or stream captures.
- **Impoundment effects:** Lake Keepit has isolated its fish from downstream recruitment for about 30 years (about 10 generations). Its mean heterozygosity was markedly lower than in the Murray River and Condamine River samples.
- **Historical geography:** Uplift of the Flinders Ranges, pluvial silt deposition and an arid phase at the end of the Tertiary separated the central Lake Eyre basin from the Murray-Darling system. Uplift also deflected the Murray River southward near Morgan.
- **Unique local fauna:** Dalhousie Springs in the Lake Eyre basin hosts several endemic taxa (including a hardyhead, a catfish and a desert goby complex), reflecting the region's isolation.

## Key Findings

- **Genetic variation:** Of 18 presumed loci, nine were polymorphic at the P<0.99 criterion (ADH*, EST*, G3PDH*, GPI-A*, GPI-B*, sMDH*, PROT-1*, PROT-2*, sSOD*). Mean observed heterozygosity was H = 0.036 ± 0.017 (s.e.), comparable to values for other fish. No Hardy-Weinberg deviations were significant (none of 16 tests).
- **Between-basin heterogeneity:** Chi-square tests showed highly significant heterogeneity among basins (P<0.001, χ² = 1779.91, d.f. = 39). Within the Murray-Darling basin, heterogeneity was not significant, so samples were pooled.
- **Lake Eyre population:** Showed a fixed allelic difference at PROT-2* (allele 145) and fixed differences at EST*, G3PDH* and GPI-A* relative to the Murray-Darling and Fitzroy populations. Nei's D = 0.23 from these populations. The authors propose it is a new allopatric species, tentatively called the "central Australian form."
- **Fitzroy vs Murray-Darling:** D = 0.06, with allele-frequency differences but no fixed differences. The authors propose treating the Fitzroy population as an allopatric subspecies (*M. ambigua oriens*).
- **Bulloo population:** Polymorphic at three loci that are fixed between the eastern and western groups, and shares the PROT-2* allele with the Lake Eyre population (Nei's D = 0.07). It may be a hybrid zone with backcrossing, or an intermediate unit. Table 4 shows mixed phenotypes at four variable loci in six fish.
- **Gene flow:** F_ST = 0.760, indicating 76% of variation lies between populations. Estimated effective migration was N_m = 0.08 fish per generation (fewer than one per 10 generations), so each basin behaves as a separate gene pool.
- **Divergence timing:** Using molecular-clock calibrations (D = 1.0 ≈ 20 million years, later revised to 18.9 million years), the authors estimate Lake Eyre/Murray-Darling separation at about 4.6 to 4.35 million years BP and later splits at about 1.4 to 1.2 million years BP. These dates are consistent with the first significant inland dry phase at the end of the Tertiary.
- **Taxonomic outcome:** The authors propose a revised list of Australian percichthyids (Table 5), including *Macquaria* sp. B (Lake Eyre golden perch) and a Bulloo River subspecies as new taxa, and note that allopatric populations fixed at more than 20% of loci can be treated as separate species.
- **Future work:** The authors suggest testing the Bulloo hybridization hypothesis with more samples and monitoring Lake Keepit for loss of genetic diversity. A closing letter (1993) from Musyl to [[W.L. Minckley]] proposes applying similar genetics methods to [[Flannelmouth Sucker]].
