# Geological and Palynological Studies of Early Lake Erie Deposits

#palynology #quaternary-geology #early-lake-erie

## Executive Summary

This 1966 study by [[Lewis, Anderson, and Berti]] of the [[Geological Survey of Canada]] uses [[Piston Coring]], [[Echo Sounding]], [[Seismic Reflection Profiling]] and SCUBA observations to reconstruct a postglacial low-water stage of [[Lake Erie]], named [[Early Lake Erie]]. Geological evidence (relict beaches, wave-cut terraces, buried channels, buried shallow-pond plant detritus) indicates water levels at least 20 m and possibly 30 m below present. Two radiocarbon dates from buried organic material, combined with regional glacial-lake chronology, place the low stage's inception at roughly 12,370 to 12,400 years ago. Lake levels then rose rapidly, driven by [[Isostatic Rebound]] of the [[Buffalo Outlet]], and probably reached modern levels 9,000 to 10,000 years ago. A [[Pollen Analysis]] of three cores supports this sequence and provides a dating and correlation tool for sediments too poor in organic matter for radiocarbon work.

## Background & Description

The paper is part of a broader survey of unconsolidated deposits in the Lake Erie basin, conducted from the research vessel C.C.G.S. Porte Dauphine under the auspices of the [[Great Lakes Institute]], University of Toronto. Sediment cores were taken with piston corers having an inside diameter of 4.9 cm, made from AX drill casing, to depths of up to 11 to 12 m below the lake bed. Three cores (1240, 1244 and 2226) were analysed palynologically.

The lake bed has two main bottom-deposit types:

- **Nearshore and ridge-top areas:** clay till or dense glacio-lacustrine clay, usually overlain by a thin (a few cm), patchy, rippled sand lag often containing pebbles and detrital shell fragments. Bedrock outcrops along parts of the eastern basin shore and in the western islands region.
- **Offshore areas:** a soft, faintly laminated, grey silty clay mud, with pelecypod, gastropod and ostracod shells and wood chips or plant debris concentrated within or at its base.

Mud thickness reaches approximately 5 to 10 m in the western basin, 20 to 25 m in the central basin and 40 m in the eastern basin. The eastern basin is basin-shaped, with a maximum depth of 66 m off Long Point. The mud thins toward shore and reaches zero thickness at the mapped boundaries.

Key bathymetric and stratigraphic features mapped from echograms include shallow western basins, a ridge between Pelee Point and Lorain, Ohio (the [[Pelee-Lorain Ridge]]), an incipient ridge between Erieau, Ontario, and Cleveland, Ohio, and a broad ridge between Long Point and Erie, Pennsylvania (the [[Long Point-Erie Ridge]]), which is 17 to 40 km wide. Depth contours of the till and dense-clay surface are considered reliable to about 35 m in offshore areas.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Evidence of low-level stages**

- **Long Point-Erie Ridge:** The western flank is wave-eroded till with a sandy lag, while sand thicker than 1 m occurs at 14 to 18 m depth. The authors favour interpreting this sand as a [[Relict Beach Deposit]] from a lake about 20 m lower than present, with large storm waves approaching from the prevailing west.
- **Northern central basin:** A gently southward-dipping, wave-eroded terrace extends beneath the mud to 21 to 37 m depth. Laminations in underlying glacio-lacustrine clays were truncated at six locations between 22 and 31 m.
- **Erieau-Cleveland Ridge:** Sorted (possibly beach) sand overlies the ridge at 22 to 26 m depth.
- **Western Lake Erie:** Possible desiccation cracks, arranged in 4- and 5-sided polygons 15 to 30 cm across, were observed by diving at 11 m depth about 1 km southwest of Pelee Point. Plant detritus at 11 to 14 m depth accumulated in shallow, isolated basins with marshy foreshores. [[Hartley]] (1961b) reported similar buried plant beds at 8.1 to 14.2 m.
- **Submergence:** Flooded valley mouths and marshes along the western shore indicate submergence. [[Moseley]] (1904) traced marsh remains and stream valleys beneath Sandusky Bay sediments to 32 ft or more below present level.

**Buried channels**

Channels cut across the Pelee-Lorain and Long Point-Erie ridges are buried beneath Recent mud. Their floors, about 30 m below present water level, appear to have served as interbasinal discharge routes that drained the central basin eastward during the low stage.

**Lake level control**

The present level of Lake Erie is controlled by a bedrock sill at the head of the [[Niagara River]] at Buffalo, New York. The authors state that no other lower outlets are known for postglacial time, so the low stage must be explained by differential uplift of this outlet. Glacial loading depressed the crust, and rebound after deglaciation tilted former shorelines. [[Isobase]] contours on strandline elevations were used to build an uplift curve for the Buffalo outlet, using data from [[Lake Whittlesey]], [[Lake Warren]] and [[Lake Iroquois]].

**Palynological context**

Pollen is abundant and well preserved in the postglacial silty clay, although absolute pollen frequency falls rapidly downward in late-glacial deposits. Sediments are interpreted as probably continuous and undisturbed since low-level Early Lake Erie at the sites studied. Pollen diagrams are divided into zones following [[Sears]] (1942), with climatic inferences drawn from correlation with other regional records, including [[Potter]] (1947), [[Kapp and Gooding]] (1964) and [[Gallein and Ogden]] (1965).

## Key Findings

**1. Timing of the low stage**

- Early Lake Erie began about 12,370 years ago (and possibly as early as 12,600 years ago), at levels as much as 100 ft (30 m) or more below present.
- The abstract gives an inception of about 12,400 years ago, with the lake at approximately 470 ft above sea level.
- Inception is bracketed between 12,370 and 12,790 years B.P. by the ages of the last glacial lakes in the Erie basin and the beginning of [[Lake Iroquois]].

**2. Key radiocarbon dates**

| Sample | Date (years B.P.) | Lab no. | Context |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buried plant detritus | 11,300 ± 160 | GSC-382 | Core 1240, 14 m below lake level, 4 m below sediment-water interface |
| Buried driftwood | 10,200 ± 180 | GSC-330 | Core 2226, 28.5 m below lake level, 6 m below sediment-water interface |
| Rooted plants, Tupperville, Ont. | 11,860 ± 170 and 12,000 ± 200 | GSC-211 and S-172 | Elevation 582 ft a.s.l., giving a direct minimum age for Early Lake Erie |

The 11,300-year date marks the establishment of vegetation and organic deposition at low levels.

**3. Lake-level recovery**

- Rapid uplift of the Buffalo outlet raised water levels and flooded drained parts of the basin.
- The present level was probably reached 9,000 to 10,000 years ago.
- An assumed Lake Iroquois strandline age of 11,000 to 11,500 years B.P. constrained the uplift curve.
- Maximum water depth at the core 1240 site was estimated at 6 m.

**4. Pollen stratigraphy**

- Zone I: a [[Picea Maximum]] (spruce, with a brief fir maximum), reflecting a cold, moist climate south of the retreating ice.
- Zone II: a [[Pinus Maximum]] (mainly Pinus banksiana), reflecting a shift to cool, dry conditions, followed by a Pine-Oak-Elm-Hickory forest.
- Zone III: not clearly represented in the Lake Erie profiles, though present in Sears' northcentral Ohio profiles.
- Zone IV: high deciduous hardwood pollen with oak dominant, indicating a warmer, drier climate.
- Zone V: a slight increase in pine, hemlock and beech with Cyperaceae prominent, suggesting a moister, cooler climate. It appears absent in core 2226 and truncated in core 1240, possibly due to discontinuous sedimentation or coring loss.
- At 10.2 ft in core 1240, non-tree pollen reaches 75% of total arboreal pollen, mostly Gramineae, Rosaceae and Spiraea-like pollen, interpreted as a local marsh signal.

**5. Interpretation of the low stage**

- The western basin was essentially drained, and plant detritus accumulated in residual shallow ponds.
- Central-basin water discharged eastward through the buried channels.
- Wave action during the transgression eroded the basin at successively higher levels, producing a sand-covered terrace and beaches.
- The fine fraction was redeposited as soft silty clay mud in offshore basins.

**6. Applied conclusions**

- Palynology can correlate sediment sequences across the basin and with the surrounding region.
- Pollen zones with radiocarbon control can date sediments that lack enough organic matter for radiocarbon analysis.
- The authors suggest that the low-level lake's littoral sands and gravels provide a rationale for commercial sand and gravel exploration in Lake Erie.
- Further work on cores from the deep eastern basin, and on central and western cores taken carefully to preserve the soft surface mud, is needed to resolve the missing zone V and the absence of a high non-tree pollen zone in core 2226.
