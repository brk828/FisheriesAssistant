# Arizona Groundwater Law: Historical Development

#hydrogeology #water-law #groundwater-policy

## Executive Summary

This Project Bulletin (January 1977) by attorney Robert Fleming summarizes the historical development of [[Groundwater Law]] in [[Arizona]]. It traces the shift from early surface-water-oriented statutes to the [[Groundwater Code of 1948]], the contested [[Bristor v. Cheatham]] decisions on private ownership, the [[Jarvis Decisions]] on export and consumptive use, the [[FICO Decision]] on the reasonable use doctrine, and federal challenges based on the [[Winters Doctrine]]. The paper is a legal history rather than a biological study. It does not report fish, species, or ecological field data, apart from a brief reference to a federal case concerning the [[Devil's Hole Pupfish]].

## Background & Description

The document is an editorial contribution to the [[Arizona Water Resources Research Center]] publication series. It is written by Robert Fleming, J.D., a 1976 graduate of the [[University of Arizona College of Law]] then in private practice in Tucson. It draws its doctrinal definitions from [[Southwestern Groundwater Law]] by John R. Chalmers, Arid Lands Resource Information Paper No. 4 (1974).

**Groundwater doctrines defined in the paper:**
- **[[English Rule]] (absolute ownership):** Water beneath land belongs to the landowner, who may use any amount absent malice, without liability to neighbors.
- **[[American Rule]] (reasonable use):** A modification of the English rule. Groundwater must be used reasonably on the land of origin and may not be transported off it where neighbors are harmed.
- **[[Correlative Rights]]:** Landowners hold a proprietary right limited to beneficial use on their own land. In shortages, each owner is limited in proportion to the area of overlying land owned.
- **[[Prior Appropriation]]:** Not based on land ownership. "First in time, first in right" establishes seniority, and water may be transferred to any beneficial-use location.

**Historical milestones cited:**
- 1864: First codification of Arizona Territorial laws, recognizing prior appropriation; no mention of groundwater.
- 1866: Congressional statutory recognition of prior appropriation.
- 1877: [[Desert Land Act]], cited as conditioning federal land grants on prior appropriation.
- 1904: [[Howard v. Perrin]], in which the Arizona Supreme Court declared percolating waters the property of the overlying landowner (dicta).
- 1919: Water rights permit system enacted, governing waters in streams and "definite underground channels."
- 1948: [[Groundwater Code of 1948]] passed in a special session under Governor Sidney P. Osborn.
- 1908: [[Winters v. United States]] established the federal reservation doctrine.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The paper addresses groundwater as a finite public and private resource under increasing stress. It does not describe aquatic habitats, fish populations, or water chemistry in detail. Relevant environmental references include:

- **Depletion concerns:** The 1952 court warned of "the inevitable exhaustion of all underground waters in the State of Arizona" if private ownership were upheld.
- **Desert dependence:** The paper notes that desert communities depend "in large measure on subsurface water sources."
- **Aquifer conditions:** Pumping by the City of Tucson in Avra Valley and Santa Cruz Valley was linked to water-table decline. Pumping from the Papago Tribe's San Xavier Reservation area was also alleged to lower the water table.
- **Aquatic species case:** [[United States v. Cappaert]] (1976) involved an injunction restraining a neighboring landowner from pumping groundwater in amounts that would endanger the Desert Pupfish in Devil's Hole, Death Valley National Monument, Nevada.
- **Critical Groundwater Areas:** Designated areas where groundwater supplies are not sufficient to supply a reasonably safe amount of irrigation water. The paper reports 10 such designated areas under the 1948 code.

## Key Findings

- **Early law:** Prior appropriation was widespread in the West before Arizona's first territorial codification in 1864. Early statutes ignored groundwater, likely because of limited information about aquifers.
- **1919 gap:** The 1919 permit act covered surface waters and "definite underground channels." The existence of such channels had not been demonstrated, though one case was decided on a stipulation that water under the Santa Cruz River bed flowed in one.
- **Well proliferation (1948):** The state land commissioner reported nearly 3,000 registered wells in central and southeastern Arizona, plus several hundred unregistered wells. This prompted the 1948 code, which was required by the federal government as a condition for funding the Central Arizona Project.
- **Permitting:** Under the 1948 code, drilling new irrigation wells in designated areas requires State Land Department approval. Replacing nonproductive irrigation wells and drilling new domestic wells are not regulated. The State Supreme Court upheld the code against constitutional challenge.
- **Bristor v. Cheatham (1952):** The court initially rejected private ownership of percolating groundwater in favor of public ownership, relying on the Desert Land Act of 1877.
- **Bristor reversal (1953):** On rehearing, the court reversed itself by a 3-2 vote, holding that Howard v. Perrin had become a rule of property and adopting the reasonable use variation.
- **Jarvis I (1969):** The court held that reasonable use barred transporting water off the land of origin where neighbors would be adversely affected. Neighbors are presumed affected in a Critical Groundwater Area.
- **Jarvis II (1970):** Allowed Tucson to export water equal to the quantity not used to irrigate retired farmland within the Critical Groundwater Area. Domestic uses in the Critical Groundwater Area were suggested to be unregulated.
- **Jarvis III (1976):** The court approved the findings of special master Porter Murry. Exports were limited to the consumptive use of retired farmland, about half of the water historically pumped, since the other half percolates back into the aquifer. Historical use is to be measured over several years, not the single year of maximum pumpage.
- **FICO decision:** Held that water pumped from a Critical Groundwater Area must be used on the land within that designated area, restraining southern Arizona mines from using groundwater from adjacent lands. The court also stated that municipal and domestic uses warrant preferential treatment over mining uses.
- **Injunction on Tucson:** The State Supreme Court upheld an April 12, 1972 injunction, sought by mining companies, that prevented Tucson from increasing groundwater exports from the Santa Cruz Valley Critical Groundwater Area.
- **Winters doctrine (federal reserved rights):** Extended in 1971 to all federal reservations. Three methods for quantifying reserved water are identified: the irrigable acres test, the open-ended method, and the present needs and uses application.
- **Pending federal case:** A U.S. Department of Justice complaint on behalf of the Papago Indian Tribe seeks to enjoin pumping that lowers the water table under the San Xavier Reservation. The City of Tucson's Santa Cruz Basin pumping could be affected.
- **Statutory preference:** A.R.S. Sec. 45-147B ranks public values for competing surface water permit applications in this order: domestic and municipal uses; irrigation and stock watering; power and mining; recreation and wildlife, including fish. The paper notes this applies to surface water, not groundwater.
- **Ongoing uncertainty:** The paper concludes that Arizona groundwater allocation remains shaped by court interpretations of reasonable use, municipal export rights, and federal reserved water claims, with major decisions still pending.
