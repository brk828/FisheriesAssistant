# Removal and Quantification of Asian Tapeworm from Bonytail Chub Using Praziquantel

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This short communication by Dave L. Ward evaluates [[Praziquantel]] as a treatment for [[Asian Tapeworm]] (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) infecting the endangered [[Bonytail Chub]]. The Asian tapeworm is an invasive parasite in North America that can cause high mortality in young fish, and endangered cyprinids are especially vulnerable. Because the parasite is hard to control, the author tested whether a single praziquantel bath could remove it from fish without harming them. The study used infected [[Red Shiner]]s as a parasite source, transferred worms into uninfected bonytails, and then treated the fish at four dosages. Praziquantel at 6.0 mg/L and higher appears to have removed all tapeworms in the treated fish, but the OCR-degraded text makes some details uncertain.

## Background & Description

**Study species**

- **Bonytail Chub** (*Gila elegans*) is an endangered cyprinid. Hatchery-reared fish were used because wild stocks were not available for testing, and genetic concerns limited their use for propagation. The fish were confirmed free of Asian tapeworm before the study.
- **Red Shiner** (*Cyprinella lutrensis*) served as the infected donor host. Shiners carrying Asian tapeworms were collected from the Verde and Salt rivers in Arizona and transported to the laboratory.
- **Asian Tapeworm** (*Bothriocephalus acheilognathi*) is a parasitic cestode with a rapid life cycle. It was probably introduced into North America with imported fishes, and it infects hatchery and ornamental fish.

**Drug**

- **Praziquantel** is an anthelmintic used in humans and animals. It is soluble in alcohol and only slightly soluble in water, so it was first dissolved in isopropyl alcohol before being added to the treatment water.

**Study design**

- Groups of 24 bonytails were treated with one of four praziquantel dosages (0.7, 1.5, 6.0, and 36.0 mg/L) for different durations.
- Control groups (0 mg/L) received the same handling without the drug.
- Treatments were run in aerated 1.8-m-diameter tanks holding 454 L of water at roughly 21–22 °C.
- Fish were held in perforated buckets within each tank, which allowed water circulation while keeping the fish off the bottom.
- Bonytails were held for 20 days after infection so the tapeworms could establish.
- Half of the treated fish were dissected immediately. The remaining fish were held in fresh water for 30 days and then dissected to test for delayed effects.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- Asian tapeworm infections are linked to high mortality in young age classes of fish. Infection rates in natural populations vary widely, so the author did not try to standardize infection levels across fish.
- Asian tapeworms can be spread through fish movement, and effective treatment is needed in hatcheries and ornamental fish operations to prevent further spread.
- Earlier work on Asian tapeworm treatment in other fish species had used various drugs and dosages, but little information was available on effects in endangered fishes.
- The study design was intended to support later nonlethal methods for estimating tapeworm loads in individual fish, which would allow monitoring of wild and captive populations.

## Key Findings

- **Infection method:** Asian tapeworms were removed from infected red shiners and manually inserted into the intestines of 105 treatment bonytails (mean total length roughly 140–153 mm across groups). Each fish was infected separately to replicate natural infection.
- **Worm establishment:** Table 2 reports numbers of worms inserted and recovered per fish. Percentages of worms that became established ranged widely, including 40.5% and 34.9% in some groups and much lower values at higher dosages.
- **Efficacy:** Praziquantel at 6.0 mg/L and higher appears to have removed all Asian tapeworms in treated bonytails. The OCR text is partly garbled, so exact efficacy figures and the duration needed for each dosage should be checked against the original publication.
- **Delayed effects:** Fish held for 30 days after treatment were dissected to check for regrowth or delayed effects. The source text does not clearly report any mortality or side effects; this should be verified in the full article.
- **Practical implication:** The results suggest that a single praziquantel bath at an effective dosage could be used to clear Asian tapeworms from endangered fish and hatchery stocks. Further work is needed on nonlethal methods to measure tapeworm loads in individual fish.
