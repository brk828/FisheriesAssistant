# Movements of Fishes in Aravaipa Creek, Arizona

#ichthyology #marine-biology #Cyprinidae #Catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This Arizona State University Master of Science thesis (Darrell John Siebert, December 1980) reports a mark-recapture study of native fishes in [[Aravaipa Creek]], a desert stream in southeastern Arizona. About 2,100 fishes of three species were tagged in 1978-79. Fish movements radiated seasonally from and toward a central [[Aravaipa Gorge]]: fish dispersed out of the gorge in winter and moved into it in summer, presumably in response to high water temperatures. Floods had little influence on movement patterns, and home ranges or territories were not evident in most individuals.

## Background & Description

Most prior movement research concerned sport or commercially important species, and little had been compiled for native [[Southwestern Fishes]]. Earlier knowledge was largely anecdotal and concerned large, once-common species such as the [[Colorado Squawfish]] and [[Razorback Sucker]], which reportedly ran upstream in great numbers in spring. Aravaipa Creek was chosen because it supports a nearly intact native fish fauna, and because large, long-lived fishes were seasonally abundant in some reaches and absent from others.

The study area is a perennial reach of roughly 40 stream-km that recedes into its bed about 7.6 km upstream of the confluence with the [[San Pedro River]]. The stream passes through three sections: an open upper valley (about 10 km), the narrow gorge (about 17 km, with walls exceeding 200 m), and a steep-sided lower valley (about 14 km).

Three of the seven native fishes reach at least 10 cm total length and were marked:
- *Gila robusta* (Roundtail Chub), a minnow (Cyprinidae)
- *Pantosteus clarki* (Desert Mountain-Sucker), a sucker (Catostomidae)
- *Catostomus insignis* (Sonoran Sucker), a sucker (Catostomidae)

Fish were captured by electrofishing and tagged with colored, individually numbered anchor tags (Floy Tag and Manufacturing Company) inserted through the dorsal musculature.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Discharge.** Flow is highly seasonal and variable. Mean winter discharge is 0.32 m³/s, and summer shows a bimodal pattern with a lower mode of 0.14 m³/s and an upper mode near 0.28 m³/s. Discharge as low as 0.009 m³/s was recorded on 20 August 1940. Floods are generally long-term in winter and abrupt, short-term in summer; the maximum instantaneous flow was 566 m³/s (19 August 1919). Flood probabilities are highest in February-April and July-September.

**Temperature.** Two thermal seasons were identified. Mean winter water temperature (November-March) was 13.10 ± 2.17 °C (limits 4.0 to 20.5 °C); mean summer temperature was 23.93 ± 2.02 °C (limits 17.0 to 33.2 °C). Transitional spring and autumn temperatures averaged 16.64 ± 0.94 °C. Water within the gorge was 3.1 to 8.8 °C cooler at midday in summer than at other stations, because canyon walls shade the water and evaporation cools it, while winter gorge temperatures were lower than outside.

**Channel structure.** The channel is unstable, with long braided runs separated by shallow turbulent riffles (less than 15 cm deep at modal discharge). Pools are infrequent, scoured around obstructions such as boulders, canyon walls, and fallen trees, reaching 0.75 to 1.0 m deep and occasionally 1.5 m. Most pools other than the most permanent are transient.

## Key Findings

- **Scale:** About 2,100 native fishes were tagged; 219 (10.4%) were recaptured at least once, and 23 were caught two or more times. One Sonoran Sucker was captured on five occasions.
- **Universal movement:** All tagged fishes were inclined to move. Most recaptured individuals (83.7% in lower and 80.5% in upper segments) had moved more than 100 m from their tagging point.
- **No species or sex differences:** Mean distances moved did not differ significantly among species or between sexes.
- **Directional, gorge-centered movement:** Fish in the upper segment moved upstream, away from the gorge, in winter and toward it in summer. Fish in the lower reaches moved toward the gorge in summer and downstream away from it in winter. Bernoulli trial analyses showed directional trends within segments to be significant.
- **Distance decreases with proximity:** Movement toward the gorge by lower-reach fish declined sharply as tagging sites approached the gorge, confirmed by regression.
- **Maximum recorded movement:** Some fish traversed nearly the entire system; a few movements were described as spectacular.
- **Stationary individuals:** Of the "stay-at-home" fish, 56% in the upper reach and 7% downstream showed no movement and were recaptured where tagged.
- **Residency in stable pools:** Mean residency in one stable pool against a cliff was 140.5 ± 117.7 days (n = 10), with one fish remaining 372 days. Mean residency in unstable pools was 89.4 ± 40.3 days (n = 10), and the longest there was 195 days.
- **Floods not a driver:** Major floods in December 1978 and March 1979 made no apparent difference to dispersal or movement patterns, and drought and flood were judged unlikely causes.
- **Temperature and light as likely drivers:** The author proposed that thermal preference, possibly through [[Orthokinesis]], and light avoidance drive gorgeward summer movement. Summer temperatures in lower and upper reaches approached or exceeded lethal limits (30.5 °C cited for [[Loach Minnow]] and roundtail chub die-offs), and local residents reported summer fish die-offs in lower reaches.
- **Reproduction unlikely as the cause:** Movement direction did not match spawning-run patterns. Fish moved into the gorge in February-May, the spawning season, and out of it in October-November, when catostomids again reached spawning condition.
- **Home ranges rare:** Home ranges, well documented for other stream fishes, were indicated for only a few individuals. The unstable substrate probably prevents the homing cues needed for them.
