# Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program (ISMP) - FY93 Annual Report

#ichthyology #marine-biology #freshwater-ecology #cyprinidae #catostomidae #endangered-species #colorado-river-basin

## Executive Summary
This report details the FY93 findings of the **Interagency Standardized Monitoring Program (ISMP)**, a long-term collaborative effort (est. 1986) between the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, Colorado Division of Wildlife, and Utah Division of Wildlife Resources. The program aims to track population trends of endangered fishes—primarily the [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (historically termed Colorado squawfish)—across the Colorado, White, Yampa, and Green Rivers.

Key mechanisms include standardized electrofishing for juvenile/adult fish, seining for Young-of-Year (YOY) cohorts, and PIT tagging for mark-recapture data. A significant methodological evaluation (McAda 1993) determined that aerial video measurement of backwater habitat did not significantly improve the correlation with YOY [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] catch rates, leading to a recommendation to discontinue this technique for broad-scale monitoring. Population trends indicate a decline in [[Razorback Sucker]] catch rates in the Green River, contrasted with an increase in adult Colorado squawfish catch rates in the Green and White Rivers, driven by a strong year class.

## Background & Description
While this report is primarily focused on population monitoring rather than detailed morphology, it notes taxonomic overlaps and physiological responses to capture methods:
*   **Gila Species Complex:** The report highlights a taxonomic shift in the [[Westwater Canyon]] population, noting an increased prevalence of [[Roundtail Chub]] relative to [[Humpback Chub]], suggesting potential hybridization or shifting population dynamics within the *Gila* complex.
*   **Electro-narcosis Response:** Detailed observations were made regarding the physiological response of Colorado squawfish to electrofishing settings. At specific low-voltage settings (190-200 volts, 3-4 amps, 60 pulses/second), fish exhibited "electro-taxis" and went into narcosis *without* becoming rigid (stiff), allowing for rapid recovery upon release into live tanks. This contrasts with previous years where higher-voltage settings (Coffelt VVP-15) resulted in physical injuries to specimens.

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The monitored species occupy specific niches within the large-river ecosystems of the Upper Colorado River Basin:
*   **Nursery Habitat:** [[Backwaters]] serve as the critical nursery habitat for post-larval and Age-0 Colorado squawfish. The surface area and availability of these backwaters are hypothesized to directly correlate with annual reproductive success (year-class strength).
*   **Refugia:** [[Black Rocks]] and [[Westwater Canyon]] serve as vital refugia for [[Humpback Chub]] populations, canyon-bound reaches that support continued recruitment.
*   **Competitive Pressures:** The niche of native fishes is increasingly compressed by non-native predatory species. Catch rates for **introduced predators** (Walleye, Northern Pike, Smallmouth Bass) remain higher than historical baselines from the late 1970s/early 1980s, suggesting increased predation pressure or competition for resources in the mainstem rivers.
*   **River-Specific Variance:** The [[Yampa River]] backwaters (specifically the confluence with Sand Creek) demonstrated a strong dependency on hydrologic conditions, where high water levels correlated with a massive influx of captured Colorado squawfish (25 individuals) via trammel netting, compared to near-zero captures during a low-water year (1992).

## Key Findings
1.  **Population Trends (Colorado Pikeminnow):** Reproductive success has been consistently low in the [[Colorado River]] mainstem in recent years, whereas the [[Green River]] shows more variable but generally higher reproductive success.
2.  **Adult Catch Rates:** Adult catch rates for Colorado squawfish have increased in the Green and [[White River]], attributed to a strong year class moving through the population (trackable via length-frequency distribution). No significant change was detected in the Yampa or Colorado rivers.
3.  **Razorback Sucker Decline:** Catch rates for [[Razorback Sucker]] have specifically declined in the Green River, a concerning trend for this endangered species.
4.  **Methodology Shift (Gear):** Electrofishing gear was upgraded (Coffelt Mark XXII VVP) to reduce fish injury; this successfully resulted in zero observed injuries to Colorado squawfish in the White/Yampa river monitoring for FY93, compared to 8 injuries in FY92.
5.  **Aerial Video Discontinuation:** A 3-year assessment (1989-1991) concluded that aerial video measurement of backwater surface area does not significantly enhance the predictive power of catch-per-unit-effort (CPUE) data for YOY reproductive success monitoring on a broad scale.
6.  **Yampa River Trammel Netting:** New testing of trammel netting in Yampa River backwaters (supplementing electrofishing) proved highly effective in high-water years, capturing 25 individuals, suggesting this method should be continued and potentially intensified (sampling twice per season).
7.  **Chub Taxonomy:** The [[Humpback Chub]] population in Westwater Canyon is exhibiting a demographic shift toward [[Roundtail Chub]], prompting recommendations to suspend standard monitoring there in favor of a dedicated Gila Taxonomy Study.
