## Executive Summary

This 1966 study investigates the physiological mechanism underlying insecticide resistance in [[Mosquitofish]] (*Gambusia affinis*) by comparing endrin-resistant populations (from insecticide-contaminated cotton field ditches near Belzoni, MS) with endrin-susceptible populations (from an uncontaminated pond near State College, MS). Using bioassays and gas chromatography, the authors determined that resistant fish tolerated body endrin concentrations up to **214.28 ppm** without dying, while susceptible fish died at much lower thresholds. Critically, the study demonstrates that resistance is **not** due to mechanical exclusion, reduced uptake rate, or excretion, but rather to **physiological toleration of massive endrin accumulation** in tissues. The paper also identifies the **gills** (not the gut/swallowing) as the primary route of endrin entry, and documents a novel "secondary poisoning" phenomenon where a live resistant fish released enough endrin to kill susceptible fish sharing its water.

## Background & Description

- **Species Studied:** *Gambusia affinis* (Baird and Girard), the western mosquitofish, family Poeciliidae.
- **Populations Compared:**
  - **Resistant strain:** Collected from a ditch draining heavily-insecticide-treated cotton fields near [[Belzoni, Mississippi]] (Humphreys County). Fields received weekly applications of DDT, toxaphene, endrin, or methyl parathion.
  - **Susceptible strain:** Collected from an isolated, uncontaminated wooded pond near [[State College, Mississippi]] (Oktibbeha County), at least 2 miles from any insecticide source.
- **Test Compound:** [[Endrin]], a chlorinated hydrocarbon (organochlorine) insecticide, prepared as a 1% acetone solution and diluted in tap water (pH 7.8, hardness 28 ppm).
- **Methods:**
  - Static bioassays to determine 36-hr TLm (median tolerance limit) values.
  - Warburg respirometry to measure oxygen consumption with and without endrin exposure.
  - Gas chromatography (Wilkens Aerograph 600-C; Dow 200 on Chromosorb W column) following Florisil column cleanup, to quantify tissue endrin residues.
  - A custom rubber-membrane "head vs. body" exposure apparatus to isolate uptake route (gills/head vs. general body surface).
  - Feeding vs. starvation trials to test swallowing as an entry pathway.
  - Endrin-release assays: exposing fish to endrin, then transferring to clean water and bioassaying susceptible fish in that water to detect released toxicant.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- The Belzoni site exemplifies chronic agricultural insecticide runoff and direct aerial spray-drift contamination of freshwater ditches — a common exposure pathway in mid-20th-century cotton agriculture.
- Field-collected resistant fish showed strong **seasonal variation** in body endrin burden: **6.8–11.95 ppm** in late August (peak spraying season) vs. only **0.73–0.88 ppm** in late February (off-season), reflecting both differences in seasonal insecticide application intensity and physiological turnover.
- The study raises significant implications for **biological magnification** and food-web toxicology: heavily contaminated resistant fish acted as a reservoir of endrin, and the near-total absence of top carnivores in contaminated waters was suggested to reflect ecological consequences of this bioaccumulation, even as fish themselves showed some capacity to "interrupt" magnification via endrin release.
- The authors caution that static toxicity tests, while producing higher raw mortality values than constant-flow systems, still yield reproducible *relative* comparisons between resistant and susceptible populations, and may better approximate real fluctuating field conditions (where endrin concentrations spike then decline via downstream transport, sediment binding, or biological uptake) than continuous-flow exposures.
- Cross-referenced with related resistance findings in other species/compounds: [[DDT Resistance in Gambusia Affinis]], [[Methyl Parathion Resistance]], and [[Golden Shiner]] endrin uptake patterns (unpublished data cited).

## Key Findings

### Endrin Tolerance Differences
- **36-hr TLm**: Resistant fish ≈ **1,000 ppb**; susceptible fish ≈ **1 ppb** (1000-fold difference).
- In a 500 ppb endrin exposure: susceptible fish reached 32% mortality in **25 minutes**, whereas resistant fish required **144 hours** to reach the same mortality level.
- Susceptible mortality was concentrated in a narrow concentration range; resistant mortality increased gradually across a wide range, complicating median-mortality determination.
- Both populations were less susceptible in fall/winter than in late summer.

### Mechanism of Entry (Rejecting Swallowing/Exclusion)
- Feeding vs. starvation trials showed **no meaningful difference** in mortality onset between fed and starved resistant fish, undermining swallowing as the dominant uptake route.
- Rate calculations: to account for measured uptake (10.48 ppm in 40 min at 250 ppb) via drinking alone, each fish would need to swallow ~42× its body weight in water — deemed implausible.
- **Head vs. body exposure experiment** (custom rubber-membrane apparatus): Of 33 susceptible fish with heads exposed to 1,000 ppb endrin, **100% died within 31 minutes** (mean 19.8 min); only 5 of 33 fish with bodies exposed died (mean 22.2 min death time, likely from membrane constriction injury, not endrin).
- Gas chromatography confirmed: fish with **heads exposed** contained an average of **13.32 ppm** endrin vs. **2.91 ppm** in fish with **bodies exposed** — roughly a **6-fold greater uptake via the head/gill region** compared to general body surface.
- **Conclusion: Gills are the primary pathway of endrin entry**, contradicting Mount (1962), who proposed gut/swallowing as the primary route in bluntnose minnows.

### Rate of Uptake: Resistant vs. Susceptible
- Bioassay-based dilution series showed **no consistent difference** in uptake rate between resistant and susceptible fish — both extracted endrin from solution at similar rates.
- However, direct residue analysis (gas chromatography) showed a **more rapid uptake in susceptible fish** than resistant fish under comparable exposure conditions (Table 7), suggesting bioassay-based rate comparisons underestimate physiological differences.
- This finding, combined with the head/body experiment, **rules out mechanical exclusion** as the resistance mechanism, since resistant fish took up equal or greater amounts of endrin than lethal doses for susceptible fish yet survived.

### Oxygen Consumption
- Baseline O₂ consumption was similar between resistant and susceptible populations in the absence of endrin.
- At **moderate endrin levels (~20 ppb)**: susceptible fish showed abrupt, sustained **increases in oxygen consumption** coinciding with poisoning symptoms (hyperactivity, spasms, racing) until death; resistant fish showed no change.
- At **high endrin levels (~1,000 ppb)**: resistant fish developed similar poisoning symptoms and showed comparable oxygen consumption increases as susceptible fish — indicating the oxygen-demand response tracks symptom onset rather than population-specific physiology.

### Endrin Release ("Secondary Poisoning")
- Both live and dead endrin-exposed fish released measurable endrin into clean tap water, sufficient to kill susceptible bioassay fish, even after being rinsed multiple times in tap water and organic solvents (acetone, pentane).
- Release amount was **positively correlated with prior exposure concentration** but was **not proportional** to internal body burden.
- **Landmark experiment:** A single resistant female exposed to 1,000 ppb endrin for 36 hr was placed in 10 L of tap water with 5 susceptible fish. All 5 susceptible fish died within **38.5 hours**; the resistant female showed no poisoning symptoms and survived — demonstrating a novel form of **secondary/indirect poisoning** in aquatic food webs.
- Differences in release rates between live vs. dead fish, and resistant vs. susceptible fish, were minor — suggesting the released endrin may be superficially bound (e.g., adsorbed to skin) rather than actively excreted from internal tissues/organs, consistent with findings by Weiss (1965) on dieldrin.

### Tissue Residue Levels
- Field-collected resistant fish: **6.8–11.95 ppm** (August, peak season) vs. **0.73–0.88 ppm** (February, off-season).
- Field-collected susceptible fish: essentially **0 ppm** endrin detected.
- Pooled resistant fish samples after **2-week exposure to 500 ppb**: **201.61–214.28 ppm** endrin — survived despite these extreme concentrations, directly supporting the "physiological toleration" hypothesis.
- Tissue/organ distribution ranking (highest to lowest endrin residue): **gut > liver > heads > gills > heart/spleen > bodies > eggs**, consistent with findings by Weiss (1965).

### Mechanism of Resistance — Conclusion
- The study **rejects** mechanical exclusion, reduced uptake rate, and enhanced excretion (e.g., via kidneys, contra Mount 1962) as resistance mechanisms.
- The authors conclude resistance is due to **increased physiological toleration of massive endrin accumulation** in tissues, though the biochemical basis for avoiding toxicity at such high burdens remains **unknown**.
- No evidence of metabolic/chemical alteration of endrin was found in tissue extracts (contrasting with Kunze and Laug's 1953 findings in rat kidneys).

### Broader Implications
- Documents a previously undescribed ecological hazard: resistant fish functioning as **living reservoirs** of bioaccumulated pesticide capable of poisoning other organisms via passive release into surrounding water — a food-web contamination pathway distinct from classic predator-ingestion secondary poisoning.
- Suggests static toxicity testing, despite methodological criticism (e.g., Holden 1962), may better reflect real-world fluctuating exposure conditions than constant-flow systems.
