# Hybridization Between Three Species of Sunfish (*Lepomis*)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This Illinois Natural History Survey bulletin (Biological Notes No. 46, November 1961) by [[William F. Childers]] and [[George W. Bennett]] tests whether hybrid sunfish could serve as a stocking fish for small warm-water ponds. The stated problem is overpopulation and stunting in ponds, which makes populations useless for recreation or food. The authors produced all six possible F1 hybrids among [[Bluegill]], [[Green Sunfish]], and [[Red-Ear Sunfish]] in the laboratory and attempted the same crosses in outdoor ponds.

Laboratory crosses showed no direct genetic isolation among the three species. Field crosses succeeded only for two combinations, and F1 sex ratios and F2 reproduction varied strongly by hybrid type. Growth comparisons in uncrowded ponds showed little difference between hybrids and parent species.

## Background & Description

The authors frame hybrid sunfish as a practical extension of agricultural hybridization. The main motivation is population control: many warm-water fishes reproduce so successfully that they stunt, and a hybrid with reduced reproductive potential that still grows large would improve pond fisheries.

Prior work cited in the bulletin includes:
- [[Moenkhaus]] (1911), who found that eggs of each tested species could be fertilized by sperm of any other species, with developmental success correlated with taxonomic closeness.
- [[Thompson]] (1935), who reported hybrids ranging from a few up to 10 per cent of some fish populations.
- [[Hubbs and Hubbs]] (1931–1933), who hybridized green and pumpkinseed sunfish and reported that hybrids grew faster than parents, were predominantly male, and appeared sterile.
- [[Luce]] (1937), who produced hybrids by hand-stripping gametes into fingerbowls.
- [[Ricker]] (1948) and [[Krumholz]] (1950), who produced hybrids in pond stocking trials.
- [[Lagler and Steinmetz]] (1957), who crossed pumpkinseed and bluegill.

A comparison table (Table 1) summarizes about 20 intrageneric *Lepomis* crosses from these studies, covering pumpkinseed, orangespotted, longear, and other species. Results ranged from many fry raised to sexual maturity to no spawning at all.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Study sites.** Parent fish were collected from several central Illinois lakes and ponds, including Lake Italy, Fairmount Quarry, Farmer City Country Club Pond, Big Pond (Utterback's Farm), Allerton Lake, and Taylor's Pond. Experimental ponds ranged from 0.02 to 0.9 acre in the field crosses, and up to 2.6 acres in the growth trials.

**Methods of hybrid production.** Three approaches were used:
1. Isolating a ripe male of one species with a ripe female of another in an aquarium.
2. Hand-stripping eggs and milt into fingerbowls or petri dishes (the "laboratory cross," following Luce's method).
3. Stocking adult males of one species with adult females of another in a pond containing no other fish (the "field cross").

The authors note that embryonic and larval environments differed among these methods and may have affected viability, morphology, and growth.

**Reproductive isolation terms.** The authors define *direct genetic isolation* as incompatibility between gametes of different species. *Indirect genetic isolation* refers to differences in spawning time, spawning habitat, or mating behavior.

**Notation.** Crosses are written male first. *BG* means a male bluegill crossed with a female green sunfish, and *GB* is the reciprocal.

**Spawning biology.** Sunfish sperm can fertilize eggs for a very short period, probably less than 60 seconds under natural conditions, after release into water. Male sunfish build, fan, and guard nests over prolonged periods.

## Key Findings

### Laboratory crosses
- Several thousand free-swimming F1 hybrids were produced from each of the six possible P1 crosses.
- Incubation at 70°F began hatching at 47 hours after fertilization, and none hatched later than 61 hours. Fry became free-swimming on the fifth or sixth day.
- The authors conclude there is **no direct genetic isolation** among bluegill, green sunfish, and red-ear sunfish.
- Eggs accidentally incubated at 70–95°F produced BG hybrids with very short caudal peduncles.

### Field crosses
- Sixteen small ponds (0.02–0.9 acre), each containing no fish, were stocked with adults of opposite sexes from different species (Table 2).
- Hybrids were produced in 4, and possibly 5, of the 16 ponds.
- **R × G** and **G × B** each produced large numbers of F1 hybrids when tried in two ponds.
- **B × G** trials were invalid because both ponds became contaminated with male green sunfish.
- **B × R** trials were invalid: three ponds were contaminated or dried up.
- **R × B** produced 11 young (0.25–0.50 inch) in one pond, which the authors judged probably RB F1 hybrids based on sexing of the adults.
- **G × R** produced no evidence of hybridization in three ponds.

### Sex ratios of F1 hybrids (Table 3)

| F1 hybrid | Sexed | % males |
|---|---|---|
| GR | 252 | 48 |
| RG | 457 | 70 |
| GB | 44 | 70 |
| BG | 142 | 97 |
| BR | 155 | 97 |
| RB | 175 | 100 |

- Only the GR hybrids approached a 50:50 ratio.
- Sex was determined by dissection, or by color, abdomen size, and urogenital opening during spawning. Observation-based sexing was tested by dissection and showed no errors.
- The 97 per cent male result for BG (142 fish) matches [[Hubbs and Hubbs]] (1933), who reported 81 per cent males of 43 aquarium-reared BG hybrids. Ricker (1948) reported 98 per cent males of 428 BR hybrids from field crosses; this study found 97 per cent of 110.

### Reproductive success of F1 hybrids (Table 4)
- **RG** and **GB** hybrids (each about 70% male) produced very large F2 generations.
- **GR** hybrids produced only a small F2 generation, in a 0.1-acre gravel pit pond.
- **BG**, **BR**, and **RB** hybrids produced no F2 generation in any of the authors' ponds. Ricker (1948) reported BR reproduction in two ponds, so the authors could not explain the difference.
- The absence of females was ruled out: a few F1 females were captured in each pond and released.
- Possible causes for GR failure included:
  1. Females prevented from spawning until eggs were overripe.
  2. Lack of synchrony between sperm and egg release.
  3. Fungal infection: about 9 of 10 nests with eggs had eggs scattered outside them, and most eggs were infected by fungi. Non-infected eggs moved to the laboratory did not hatch.
- Aggressive male–male interference during spawning was observed, with rival males invading spawning nests.

### Growth of F1 hybrids
Growth was recorded in ponds stocked with 200–10,000 fry per surface acre. Ponds differed in size, depth, basin shape, and fertility, so the authors made no valid comparisons among hybrid types. Selected examples:
- **RB:** About 2,500 fry (1,000 per acre) in a 2.6-acre pond. After about 10 months, 463 fish averaged 4.0 inches. Later fish averaged 5.3 inches.
- **BR:** About 1,100 fry in a 0.9-acre pond. By about two growing seasons, fish averaged 7.4 inches and 0.35 pound.
- **GR:** About 1,000 fry in a 0.1-acre pond. At about 2 years, 15 fish averaged 7.7 inches and 0.41 pound.
- **RG:** About 1,300 fry in a 0.9-acre pond. In 1958, 327 fish averaged 6.8 inches.
- **BG:** 61 fry in a 0.3-acre pond. After about 3 years, 10 fish averaged 8.6 inches. Figure 5 shows seven of these fish averaging 0.74 pound.
- **GB:** 322 fry (800 per acre) in a 0.4-acre pond. After two growing seasons, 44 fish averaged 6.9 inches.

### Growth comparisons with parent species
**Experiment 1: BG F1 vs green sunfish**
- 171 green sunfish (about 0.75 in) and 171 BG hybrids (about 1.0 in) were stocked in an 0.8-acre gravel pit pond on July 10, 1958, with no other fish.
- Ten months later, 74% of green sunfish (127 fish) and 83% of BG hybrids (142 fish) were recovered (Table 5).
- Male BG hybrids and green sunfish grew at approximately the same rate. Hybrid males averaged 0.4 inch longer, but they started about 0.25 inch longer.
- BG hybrid males of 4.8–5.7 inches weighed considerably more than green sunfish males of similar length. BG hybrids were much deeper bodied.
- Density was 28 pounds per acre at termination, compared with more than 100 pounds per acre historically in this pond.

**Experiment 2: GR F1 vs green sunfish and red-ear sunfish**
- 200 GR hybrids (4.6 in), 200 green sunfish (4.2 in), and 200 red-ear sunfish (3.5 in) were stocked in a 1.1-acre pond from August 1–15, 1958.
- Recovery in spring 1959 was 74.5% (149) for GR hybrids, 68% (136) for green sunfish, and 63.5% (127) for red-ear sunfish (Table 7).
- Terminal biomass was 26, 21, and 14 pounds per acre for GR hybrids, green sunfish, and red-ear sunfish respectively, against more than 150 pounds per acre historically.
- All three groups increased about 2 inches in length and nearly doubled their weight.

### Summary conclusions
1. No direct genetic isolation exists among bluegill, green sunfish, and red-ear sunfish.
2. Only the G × B and R × G field crosses produced significant numbers of F1 hybrids.
3. Only GR hybrids had a near-equal sex ratio. Most other types were heavily male.
4. RG and GB hybrids produced large F2 generations, GR produced few, and BG, BR, and RB produced none.
5. Growth comparisons in uncrowded ponds showed little difference between hybrids and parent species.
6. Under high population density, hybrids might outgrow parents through better competition for food and space. This was not tested directly.

The bulletin's practical implication is that the most promising hybrid for pond stocking was the GR hybrid, which grew as fast as its parents in uncrowded ponds and showed low reproduction, though fungus-infected eggs and male interference limited its F2 output.
