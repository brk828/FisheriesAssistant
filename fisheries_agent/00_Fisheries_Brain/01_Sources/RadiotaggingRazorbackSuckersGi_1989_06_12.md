# Radiotagging Razorback Suckers: Gila River, Arizona 1988–1989

#ichthyology #freshwater-ecology #Catostomidae #endangered-species

## Executive Summary

This U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service / Arizona State University study used surgically-implanted radio transmitters to track the movements and habitat use of [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*), a federally endangered [[Catostomidae|catostomid]], following reintroduction into the Gila River, eastern Arizona. Two cohorts were released: (1) 14 fish (4 wild adults from [[Lake Mohave]] plus 10 hatchery-reared fish from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]]) on 24 May 1988, and (2) 9 hatchery-reared fish on 12 October 1988, following high early mortality in the first group. Tracking documented predominantly **downstream** movement, substantial short-term mortality (largely attributed to scavenger/predator activity), transmitter/battery failures, and ultimate loss of contact with the majority of fish — possibly due to emigration into [[San Carlos Reservoir]], where signal attenuation was severe. The study establishes baseline habitat-use data (channel width, depth, velocity, substrate) critical for razorback sucker reintroduction and recovery planning in the [[Gila River]] system.

## Background & Description

- **Species**: *Xyrauchen texanus*, distinguished by a pronounced dorsal keel ("razorback") — not detailed morphometrically in this report, which focuses on movement/telemetry.
- **Source populations**:
  - 4 wild adults trammel-netted from [[Lake Mohave]] (Arizona–Nevada), 20–23 May 1988 (3 males, 1 female).
  - 10 hatchery-propagated F1 fish, ages 4, 6, or 7 years, from [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery]] (New Mexico), tagged 24 May 1988.
  - 9 additional 4-year-old hatchery fish from Dexter, tagged 30 August 1988, released 12 October 1988.
- **Tagging method**: Internal radio transmitters surgically implanted; fish held for post-surgical recovery (outdoor pond) prior to the October cohort's release. Two October-cohort fish died during recovery (one next day, one after a month); transmitters were recovered and re-implanted into healthy replacement fish.
- **Mortality signs**: Recovered carcasses frequently showed partial consumption, missing cranial elements, or disarticulated skeletons/vertebrae — attributed to scavengers (e.g., soft-shell turtle, raccoon, coyote, feral pig, avian predators) or predators (bite marks on transmitter casings, raptor-inflicted scarring observed on associated channel catfish).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

- **Release site**: Gila River at Old Safford Bridge, Greenlee Co., Arizona.
- **Habitat occupied** (May 1988 release, n=30 measurements): mid-channel sandy runs, pools, eddies, and backwaters averaging **10 ± 5 m wide, 0.4 ± 0.3 m deep, 0.21 ± 0.17 m/sec current velocity**; substrate predominantly sand, sand/gravel, or mixed gravel-cobble-bedrock in eddies.
- **Habitat occupied** (October 1988 release, n=11): slightly narrower/similar channels, **10 ± 3 m wide, 0.5 ± 0.4 m deep, 0.30 ± 0.20 m/sec velocity** — velocity difference from spring cohort not statistically significant.
- **Movement patterns**:
  - May cohort: 3 fish moved **upstream** (mean 7.2 km, range 0.1–23.7 km, at large 10 d on average); 11 fish moved **downstream** (mean 11.9 km, range 1.6–32.0 km, contact maintained 19 d on average).
  - October cohort: 7 of 9 fish moved downstream (mean 8.1 km, range 0.1–37.2 km), remaining viable an average of 39 d (range 1–126 d); 2 fish lost contact shortly after release.
  - Strong **downstream bias** overall; minimal upstream movement.
- **Associated species/behavior**: Some tagged fish observed schooling with **common carp** and **channel catfish** in eddy-pool habitats with structural cover (undercut banks, root wads, organic debris).
- **Broader habitat survey**: Radio-tracking by truck/ATV along 128 km of Safford Valley irrigation canals/drains and the Gila River mainstem from San Jose diversion dam to the [[San Carlos Reservoir]] delta yielded **no additional contacts**, despite shallow depths favorable to detection.
- **Reservoir signal attenuation**: At [[San Carlos Reservoir]], transmitter detection range dropped sharply with depth (undetectable at 3 m; ~5 m range at 1 m depth) versus 50–150+ m in air — likely due to high water conductivity and/or aging transmitter/receiver equipment. Extensive shoreline/deltaic searches near Coolidge Dam over 3 days detected no signals.
- **Comparative context**: Habitat use patterns broadly resembled those reported for razorback sucker in the [[Yampa River]] and [[Green River]] (Colorado/Utah), though the Gila River differs substantially in size and channel characteristics from these upper Colorado River Basin streams.

## Key Findings

1. **High early mortality**: Of 14 fish released in May 1988, only 2 remained alive with functional transmitters by ~32 days post-release; 8 died (transmitters recovered), 2 lost contact within 12 hr, and 2 died/shed transmitters without recovery.
2. **Downstream movement dominance**: Both cohorts showed a strong tendency toward downstream dispersal, with only a minority (3 of 14 in the first cohort) moving upstream.
3. **Predation/scavenging as a major mortality factor**: Multiple recovered carcasses and transmitters bore physical evidence (bite marks, disarticulation, missing tissue) consistent with predation or post-mortem scavenging.
4. **Transmitter/battery limitations**: Shorter contact periods in the October cohort were attributed to battery depletion, as many units approached their rated operational lifespan.
5. **Unresolved fates**: The ultimate fate of 9 fish (out of 23 total tagged) remains unknown; deep substrate burial of transmitters was considered unlikely given successful recovery of units buried to 0.3 m elsewhere. Emigration into [[San Carlos Reservoir]] is proposed as a plausible explanation, complicated by severe signal attenuation in deep water.
6. **Habitat characterization**: Occupied habitats were consistently wide, sandy, mid-channel runs of modest depth and velocity, with secondary use of eddies, backwaters, and pools featuring structural cover — informing future [[Razorback Sucker]] reintroduction site selection.
7. **Recommendations**: The authors call for additional study, including telemetry in more confined/controlled habitats and intensive fish sampling within [[San Carlos Reservoir]] to resolve unknown fates and better assess reservoir habitat suitability.
