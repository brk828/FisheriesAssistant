# Extinctions of North American Fishes During the Past Century

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This review by [[Robert R. Miller]], James D. Williams, and Jack E. Williams documents the extinction of 3 genera, 27 species and 13 subspecies (40 taxa in total) of North American freshwater fishes during the past 100 years. The paper's stated purpose is to provide a comprehensive list of recently extinct fishes and a detailed account of each extinction, including taxa presumed extinct after numerous negative surveys and taxa poorly known but presumed lost.

Losses occurred in every region except northern Canada and Alaska. The authors report that more than one factor contributed to 82% of the extinctions. Habitat alteration, introduced species, pollution, hybridization and overharvesting are the recurring causes. The authors expect the rate of loss to increase as more of the native fauna becomes endangered or threatened.

## Background & Description

The paper addresses a documentation gap: extinctions are hard to confirm, especially for small fishes, cryptic species, and fish in hard-to-sample habitats. Some taxa were thought extinct and later rediscovered, including the [[Owens Pupfish]] (found in Fish Slough, California, in 1964) and the [[Shoshone Pupfish]] (rediscovered in 1986 after a genetic bottleneck had reduced it). The authors cite the American Fisheries Society list of 103 endangered, 114 threatened and 147 special-concern fish taxa as evidence of the wider crisis.

Each account in the paper follows a common pattern: type locality or range, biology where known, last confirmed collection, and suspected causes. Accounts cover taxa ranging from lampreys and ciscoes of the Great Lakes to desert springfishes, Mexican goodeids and Texas gambusias. Figures are scientific drawings by Sara V. Fink, and an appendix lists taxa erroneously reported as extinct.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Extinctions cluster in a few regions, each with its own dominant pressures:

- **[[Great Lakes]]**: three species and two subspecies lost. Overfishing, [[Sea Lamprey]] predation (established in the 1940s), eutrophication and hybridization drove declines in ciscoes, while the [[Blue Pike]] declined under enrichment, oxygen depletion, introduced fishes and overharvest.
- **[[Great Basin]]**: one species and six subspecies lost. Spring diversion and pumping, and introduced [[Largemouth Bass]], bullfrogs, carp and trout are the recurring themes.
- **[[Parras Valley]]** (Coahuila, Mexico): three species, including a monotypic genus, lost after spring flows failed and habitats were altered for wineries, mills and irrigation.
- **[[Valley of Mexico]]**: one genus and three species (the [[Mexican Dace]], *Evarra*) lost as lakes, springs and canals dried through agriculture, groundwater removal and urban growth.
- **[[Rio Grande]]**: two species and two subspecies lost, linked to dams, water diversion, salinity rise from irrigation, and introduced fishes.

Taxa are also broadly grouped by family. Cyprinidae (minnows and shiners) account for 16 taxa, the most of any family. Salmonids and cyprinodontids account for seven taxa each.

## Key Findings

**Scale and causes**
- 40 taxa lost: 27 species, 13 subspecies, and 3 genera.
- Physical habitat alteration was cited for at least 73% of taxa, the most common cause.
- Introduced species were cited for 68%.
- Chemical alteration or pollution and hybridization were each cited for 38%.
- Overharvesting was cited for 15%.
- More than one factor contributed to 82% of extinctions.

**Timing and trend**
- 19 of the 40 taxa disappeared after 1964.
- The authors argue that present laws and recovery activities are inadequate, and they are especially concerned about the Mexican ichthyofauna, where fishes are poorly known, protection laws are weak, and the human population is growing quickly.

**Notable cases**
- The [[Miller Lake Lamprey]] (*Lampetra minima*), a dwarf parasitic lamprey (72–129 mm TL in 45 specimens) from Oregon, was poisoned in the 1950s to control predation on introduced trout. Its last collection was 17 July 1952.
- The [[Longjaw Cisco]] was one of the largest Great Lakes ciscoes (up to 38 cm). Its last Lake Michigan record was in 1967, and its last Lake Huron record was in 1975.
- The [[Blue Pike]] catch in Lake Erie was 5–10 million kg annually until the mid-1950s, falling to 35,834 kg by 1959. The last confirmed specimen was taken in 1965.
- The [[Tecopa Pupfish]] was the first creature officially declared extinct under the [[Endangered Species Act]] of 1973. Its extinction followed hot-spring bathhouse development and hybridization with the [[Amargosa Pupfish]].
- The [[June Sucker]] collapsed during the mid-1930s drought, after diversion dams, hybridization with Utah sucker and exotic introductions. The authors describe a surviving hybrid population, *C. liorus mictus*, as distinct from the original form.
- The [[Silver Trout]] of New Hampshire was lost to introduced fishes and hybridization with hatchery brook trout. Its last records are from 1926 (Christine Lake) and 1930 (Dublin Pond).
- The [[Thicktail Chub]] was one of the most common Central Valley fishes. Only 11 of 34 museum lots with dates (32%) were collected after 1900. Its last record is from 1957, at Steamboat Slough.
- The [[Alvord Cutthroat Trout]] was lost through hybridization with introduced rainbow trout, after stocking in the early 1930s.
- The [[Harelip Sucker]] was widespread in the eastern United States. Its last collection was in 1893, and the authors suspect siltation and pollution reduced its mollusk prey and visual feeding.
- The [[Ameca Shiner]] was represented by only 46 of 2,875 fishes collected from the upper Rio Ameca basin (1939–1969). Its last capture was on 21 April 1969.
- The [[Durango Shiner]] is believed to have lost half its drainage's fish fauna (only 6 of 11 species still occur), with largemouth bass a major factor.
- The [[San Marcos Gambusia]] and [[Amistad Gambusia]] were lost partly to contamination by mosquitofish and habitat change. Extinction of the Amistad species followed the flooding of its spring by Amistad Dam.

**Methodological points**
- Extinction is difficult to confirm; the authors distinguish taxa presumed extinct after many negative surveys from those poorly known.
- Rediscovery is possible, but the chances of rediscovery are declining.
- Many accounts cite [[Hybridization]] as a final step once populations were reduced, but the authors note that its role is often unknown because the species' biology is poorly known.
