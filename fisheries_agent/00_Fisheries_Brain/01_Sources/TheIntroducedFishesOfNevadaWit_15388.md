# The Introduced Fishes of Nevada, with a History of Their Introduction

#ichthyology #marine-biology #salmonidae

## Executive Summary

This historical review, by Robert R. Miller and J. R. Alcorn, documents fish species deliberately or accidentally introduced into [[Nevada]] waters since 1873. At least 39 species and subspecies were introduced, and 24 kinds are known to persist in the state. The authors compiled records from fish commission reports, hatchery statistics, and their own collecting from 1934 to 1943. They state that the list approaches a complete tabulation but note that private and county plantings were never fully recorded. The paper gives an annotated list of established species and of introductions that failed, along with management recommendations on [[Hybridization]] and on the spread of [[Green Sunfish]].

## Background & Description

The authors note that the first successful introductions occurred between 1873 and 1877, when cutthroat trout were planted in central and eastern Nevada and catfish and [[Sacramento Perch]] were stocked in Washoe Lake. Over the following decades, attempts were made to establish at least 31 species (39 kinds). Of the 24 kinds now present, three species (four forms) are native to Nevada, so the introduced fauna is roughly two-thirds the size of the native species count, though far fewer than the number of indigenous local subspecies.

Information came from U.S. Fish Commission and Bureau of Fisheries reports, Nevada Fish Commission reports (1877 to 1894, 1895 to 1896, 1907 to 1916), and Nevada Fish and Game Commission reports (1917 to 1942). Reports for 1897 to 1906 could not be located. Many records are incomplete: some list only total fish numbers, while others use generic names such as "bass," "bream," or "sunfish" that prevent species identification. The taxonomy of [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] follows Jordan and Evermann, and the general classification follows Hubbs and Lagler.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

Nevada's fish fauna is shaped by its basin structure. Many isolated creeks and springs exist across the state, and the authors suggest that this allows separate-stream planting to prevent undesirable crosses. The native [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] lived in the drainage of Pluvial Lake Lahontan, and its pure strains have become rare because of hybridization with introduced [[Yellowstone Cutthroat Trout]] and [[Rainbow Trout]].

Several introductions had clear ecological consequences:

- **Hybridization:** Rainbow trout hybridized extensively with cutthroat trout and displaced native cutthroat populations in many rivers. Brook and cutthroat trout do not hybridize.
- **Competition and predation:** [[Common Carp]] became very abundant in lower river courses, warm lakes including [[Lake Mead]], and isolated springs. [[Lake Trout]] in [[Lake Tahoe]] were believed to have greatly reduced or virtually eliminated Lahontan cutthroat trout and the royal silver trout.
- **Pelican predation:** Carp, catfish, and Sacramento perch were recorded as food items of white pelicans at [[Pyramid Lake]].
- **Mosquito control:** [[Mosquitofish]] were valued in the Fallon area for controlling mosquitoes and gnats, and as forage for [[Largemouth Bass]].
- **Lake Tahoe:** Brook trout, lake trout, and golden trout were all introduced into the Tahoe basin, which connects to the [[Truckee River]].

## Key Findings

**Species now established in Nevada (annotated list summarized):**

- **Salmonidae (trouts):** [[Brown Trout]] (including Loch Leven strains, which the authors could not separate from brown trout; 210,000 Loch Leven trout were planted in 1941 across four counties). [[Lahontan Cutthroat Trout]] were widely distributed in the early 1900s, with 1,347,151 "blackspotted trout" distributed in 1910. [[Rainbow Trout]] (Coast rainbow trout, *irideus*) was introduced from 1879 to 1880 onward, with more than 11,000,000 eggs and fish allotted to Nevada from 1908 to 1941. [[Brook Trout]] received 3,474,345 eggs and fish from federal hatcheries between 1901 and 1941 and is now among the most common trouts in the state's higher streams. [[Lake Trout]] were first recorded in a 1889 plant in Lake Tahoe, and 48,000 were planted there on May 28, 1896. Utah Lake cutthroat trout were introduced before 1881 into Spring Valley, and hybrid trout were tested from 1910.
- **Cyprinidae (minnows):** [[Common Carp]] were first recorded in 1881, with 2,763 planted between 1882 and 1891. [[Goldfish]] were probably released by aquarium owners and bait users. The Utah chub was introduced by Mormon settlers into springs in White Pine and Lincoln counties.
- **Ictaluridae (catfishes):** [[Channel Catfish]] are known only from the Colorado River drainage, including Lake Mead. Brown bullhead were among the first fishes introduced, around 1877, and are abundant in lower Humboldt, Truckee, Walker, and Carson rivers. Northern and southern black bullheads and white catfish also occur or have been planted.
- **Poeciliidae:** Western [[Mosquitofish]] were introduced into the lower Carson River about 1934.
- **Percidae:** Yellow perch were first planted in 1930 and are now common in Lahontan Reservoir and the lower Carson River.
- **Centrarchidae (sunfishes):** [[Largemouth Bass]] became abundant after about 1910, with 195,050 eggs and fish sent by the federal government between 1909 and 1941. Smallmouth bass were stocked in 1888 but apparently failed, and only a 1942 record from Lake Mead survives. [[Bluegill]] was recorded from 1909 to 1910 and was distributed heavily from 1940 to 1941. [[Green Sunfish]] are a serious competitor that does not reach game size, so the authors recommend against further spread. [[Sacramento Perch]] were stocked in 1877 and are now common in the lower Carson and Truckee drainages and in Walker, Washoe, and Pyramid lakes. Black crappie were stocked several times since 1924.

**Introductions that failed or remain uncertain (summarized):**

- [[Grayling]] (Montana grayling): 50,180 eggs received in 1941, with 15,000 planted in a spring; establishment unconfirmed.
- [[Chinook Salmon]]: 250,000 eggs in 1879 and about 390,000 spawn later; plants considered failures.
- Coho salmon (1913), sockeye salmon (1936 to 1938, several hundred thousand fish), chum salmon (1939), and Columbia River steelhead: no records of persistence.
- Common shad (1884 to 1886, Colorado River), whitefish (1877 to 1880), landlocked Atlantic salmon (1881 to 1892), tench (1885), and [[Golden Trout]] (250,000 eggs shipped to Lake Tahoe in 1919): no recent records.
- [[Amargosa Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon nevadensis*): a native species from the Amargosa basin, found in a Las Vegas spring in 1940 to 1941, presumably introduced as forage fish. Identity was confirmed by the authors.

**Management recommendations:**

- Do not stock cutthroat and rainbow trout in the same creek because they hybridize extensively and cutthroat are speedily eliminated. Plant them in separate streams.
- Brook and cutthroat trout may share waters because they do not hybridize.
- Do not expand the range of [[Green Sunfish]].
