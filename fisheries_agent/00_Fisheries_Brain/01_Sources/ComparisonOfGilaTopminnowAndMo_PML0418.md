# Comparison of Gila Topminnow and Mosquitofish as Biological Control Agents of Mosquitoes

#ichthyology #marine-biology #poeciliidae

## Executive Summary

This final report (November 9, 2001) from the [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]], Research Branch, evaluates whether the native [[Gila Topminnow]] could replace the introduced [[Western Mosquitofish]] for mosquito control in constructed wetlands in the American Southwest. The study was funded by the [[United States Bureau of Reclamation]] and the Arizona Game and Fish Department Heritage Fund. Mosquitofish are widely used as larvivorous biocontrol agents but compete with and prey upon the endangered Gila topminnow, which has been listed as endangered since 1967. The authors concluded that topminnow are roughly as effective as mosquitofish at controlling mosquitoes under experimental conditions, and recommended use of both native species for future mosquito control.

## Background & Description

The Gila topminnow was once one of the most common fishes in the lower [[Colorado River]] system before 1940, but declined drastically over the following two decades. Mosquitofish have been introduced worldwide for mosquito control, and are considered robust and tolerant of low oxygen, temperature extremes and chemical toxins. Earlier work showed that many native fishes perform as well as or better than mosquitofish in densely vegetated habitats, a point attributed to mosquitofish being mainly associated with open water.

The study compared four aspects of both species:

- **Critical thermal maxima (CTM)** in laboratory tests at 20 °C acclimation.
- **Acute ammonia tolerance** using median lethal concentrations (LC50s) of unionized ammonia.
- **Simulated emergent vegetation penetration** in a 5 × 0.5 m linear raceway.
- **Diet and food selectivity** in 1,900-L outdoor artificial wetland tanks over a two-week foraging study (September 17 to October 1, 2000).

Fish were collected from [[Bubbling Ponds Hatchery]] (mosquitofish) and [[Boyce Thompson Arboretum]] (topminnow, later supplemented by a population near Tortilla Flat). All fish were treated with Praziquantel (0.67 ppm for 24 h) to control Asian tapeworm.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

The American Southwest is arid and rapidly urbanizing, which has increased the number of wastewater treatment wetlands. These wetlands provide water purification and habitat, but also breed mosquitoes (Family Culicidae) that can transmit encephalitis and malaria. Pesticide use is undesirable, and mosquito resistance to pesticides has renewed interest in biological control. Wetland ammonia typically declines downstream through bacterial oxidation, and unionized ammonia depends on temperature and pH. Thermal patchiness from evaporation and shading by algae and emergent vegetation is common in desert waters, which may allow topminnow to escape lethal hot spots.

Mosquitofish are predominantly found in open water, whereas topminnow were usually observed close to artificial vegetation and used it for cover.

## Key Findings

**Critical thermal maxima**
- CTM values did not differ widely between species; intraspecific differences exceeded interspecific differences.
- Topminnow jumped out of the water as temperatures approached CTM; mosquitofish adults congregated near the surface and never jumped.

**Ammonia tolerance**
- Mosquitofish tolerated approximately twice the ammonia concentration of topminnow.
- Female mosquitofish were more tolerant than males; juveniles of both species were unavailable for testing.
- Because temperature and pH were held constant, the study could not separate ionized from unionized toxicity, but unionized ammonia values were reported.

**Vegetation penetration**
- Both species used high-density simulated vegetation little.
- At low density, both species increased activity, and mosquitofish were more active than topminnow.
- Topminnow stayed close to vegetation and used it for cover when disturbed, making them harder to remove from the raceway.

**Diet and food selection**
- Gut fullness peaked in the evening (1800–0000 h in most groups), so field sampling was conducted at 1800–2100 h.
- Empty stomachs were common (54% of mosquitofish, 40% of topminnow); stomach and lower intestine data were pooled.
- Diet overlap was 0.61 between male and female topminnow and 0.74 between male and female mosquitofish, so diets were summarized without sex.
- 33 food categories were documented for mosquitofish and 21 for topminnow.
- Both species rapidly reduced mosquito larvae and pupae; topminnow eliminated larvae within one day, and both species had consumed nearly all mosquito life stages by day five.
- Both species showed positive selection for mosquitoes, then shifted toward chironomid midges after mosquitoes were depleted.
- Topminnow diets were dominated by "other organic material" (mostly algae), indicating omnivory; mosquitofish exhibited cannibalism on all but the last sampling day, while topminnow did not.
- Ostracods, cladocerans and dragonfly larvae (Corduliidae) dominated control tanks; dragonfly larvae were too large to be eaten.

**Overall conclusions and recommendations**
- Both species were equally effective at maintaining mosquito populations at low levels under experimental conditions.
- Differences in ammonia tolerance and fish activity were judged unlikely to be important in most artificial wetlands.
- Neither species reduced chironomid populations noticeably.
- Topminnow are native and historically widespread, so using them would support conservation and reduce mosquitofish stocking.
- The [[Arizona Game and Fish Department]] recommends use of both [[Gila Topminnow]] and [[Desert Pupfish]] (*Cyprinodon macularius*, also endangered and previously shown to control mosquitoes) for future mosquito control.
