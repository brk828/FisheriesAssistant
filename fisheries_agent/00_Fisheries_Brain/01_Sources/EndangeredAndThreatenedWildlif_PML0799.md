# Critical Habitat Designation for the Spikedace and Loach Minnow (Federal Register, March 27, 2007)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This final rule from the [[U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service]] designates critical habitat for the [[Spikedace]] (*Meda fulgida*) and [[Loach Minnow]] (*Tiaroga cobitis*) under the [[Endangered Species Act]]. Approximately 522.2 river miles (840.4 km) are designated across five stream complexes in Arizona and New Mexico. Both species have declined to an estimated 10 to 15 percent or less of their historical range. The rule revises a 2000 designation that was set aside by a federal court in 2004, and it reflects extensive public comment, peer review, and exclusions of Tribal, private, and Verde River lands under section 4(b)(2).

## Background & Description

**Spikedace (*Meda fulgida*)** is a small, slim minnow under 3 inches (75 mm) in length, olive gray to brownish dorsally with silvery sides and vertically elongated black specks. It has spines in the dorsal fin. It was first collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro in Arizona and described by Girard in 1856. It is the only species in the genus *Meda*.

**Loach Minnow (*Tiaroga cobitis*)** is a small, slender, elongated minnow under 3 inches (80 mm) in length, olive overall with black mottling or splotches. Breeding males show vivid red to red-orange markings on fin bases, mouth, lower head and often the abdomen. It was first collected in 1851 from the Rio San Pedro and described by Girard in 1865.

Both species are members of [[Cyprinidae]]. The rule notes roughly 235 cyprinid species in North America, and other small native minnows (e.g., longfin dace and speckled dace) can be confused with them.

**Regulatory history.** The 2000 designation (65 FR 24328) was challenged by industry and county groups and set aside by the U.S. District Court for the District of New Mexico on August 31, 2004. A new proposed designation was published in December 2005 and a revised final rule was issued in 2007.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Habitat.** Both fish depend on recurrent flooding and a natural hydrograph to maintain habitat and to give them a competitive edge over invasive nonnative aquatic species. Adult spikedace prefer moderate to swift currents over sand, gravel and cobble, with adult water depths generally under 11.8 inches (30 cm). Loach minnow favor shallow, swift, turbulent riffles with cobble substrates and are rare or absent where fine sediments fill interstitial spaces.

**Historical and current range.** Spikedace was once common throughout much of the [[Gila River Basin]] and now occupies the upper Gila, East, West and Middle Forks of the Gila River (New Mexico), and the middle Gila, lower San Pedro, [[Aravaipa Creek]], Eagle Creek and Verde River (Arizona). Loach minnow was once common across the same basin and now survives in portions of the upper Gila, San Francisco, Blue and Tularosa rivers and in Aravaipa Creek. The present range of both species is estimated at about 10 to 15 percent of historical range, and abundance within occupied areas ranges from common to very rare. New Mexico populations declined in recent years.

**Threats.** Habitat destruction, competition and predation by [[Nonnative Aquatic Species]] (e.g., [[Red Shiner]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Flathead Catfish]], [[Green Sunfish]], [[Smallmouth Bass]]) are the main drivers of decline. Documented threats to the designated units include livestock grazing, water diversions, nonnative fishes, recreation, road construction, mining, channelization, and fire with associated ash and sediment. Parasites and pathogens, including the [[Asian Tapeworm]], [[Anchor Worm]] and [[Ich]] (*Ichthyophthirius multifiliis*), are also threats.

**Diet.** Both species feed mainly on riffle-dwelling aquatic insects. Spikedace consume mayflies, true flies, caddisflies, stoneflies and dragonflies; in one Gila River study mayflies made up 71 percent frequency of occurrence. Loach minnow consume true fly larvae and mayfly nymphs as key food items.

**Water quality and temperature.** Both species require permanent flowing water with no or minimal pollutant levels. A University of Arizona study (Bonar et al. 2005) found no loach minnow survived 30 days at 32 °C and 100 percent survival at 28 °C; spikedace showed 100 percent survival at 30 °C in the same study.

## Key Findings

**Critical habitat designated.**
- Total: approximately 522.2 river miles (840.4 km), including 338.1 mi (544.1 km) in Arizona and 251.5 mi (404.8 km) in New Mexico (after exclusions, per Table 2).
- Five river complexes: Verde River; Black River; Lower San Pedro/Middle Gila/Aravaipa Creek; San Francisco and Blue Rivers; and Upper Gila River.
- Lateral extent set at 300 feet (91.4 m) on each side of bankfull stage, chosen because the [[100-Year Floodplain]] was not mapped on standard topographic maps.

**Occupancy criterion.** A stream segment is considered occupied if the species was detected within the last 10 years, or if the segment is directly connected to a segment with records from that period. The rule defends the 10-year window as reasonable given the species' elusiveness and the 2 to 3 year life expectancy (about three to four generations).

**Primary constituent elements (PCEs).** Five PCEs were defined for each species: (1) permanent flowing water with low pollutants and suitable velocities and depths; (2) sand, gravel and cobble substrates with low fine sediment, maintained by a natural hydrograph; (3) streams with low gradient, suitable temperatures, pool-riffle-run-backwater components and an abundant aquatic insect base; (4) habitat devoid of, or tolerant of, nonnative aquatic species; and (5) connective corridors in intermittent reaches.

**Exclusions under section 4(b)(2).**
- Tribal lands of the San Carlos Apache, White Mountain Apache and Yavapai-Apache Tribes were excluded based on tribal management plans and government-to-government relations.
- Phelps Dodge Corporation lands on the Gila River and Eagle Creek were excluded based on voluntary management plans.
- The lower Verde River (below the Prescott and Coconino National Forest boundary) was excluded for significant and disproportionate economic impact; the upper Verde, which holds about 91 percent of known spikedace locations, remains designated.
- Eagle Creek was removed from spikedace critical habitat because the last record was in 1989.

**Economic analysis.** Estimated total post-designation costs were $25.2 to $100.3 million over 20 years (discounted annual $1.4 to $6.7 million). The lower Verde River alone accounted for up to $64.5 million (undiscounted), more than half the total. Impacts were mainly to water use, recreation and livestock grazing.

**Regulatory effects.** Section 7 consultation is required only for federal actions. The designation does not restrict private activity on private land without a federal nexus, does not establish refuges, and does not require restoration. The NEPA analysis resulted in a Finding of No Significant Impact, so no Environmental Impact Statement was required.

**Public process.** Three comment periods (December 2005 to February 2006; June to July 2006; October 2006) and two public hearings (Silver City, NM and Camp Verde, AZ) produced roughly 73 comments in total, and the rule responded to peer-review and public concerns (e.g., changing "nonnative aquatic fishes" to "nonnative aquatic species" in PCE 4 and clarifying the reasons for the 300-foot lateral extent).

**Effective date:** April 20, 2007.
