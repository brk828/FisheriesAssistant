# Lake Chichancanab Sediments and the Late Classic Maya Drought, with Companion Nature Items

#paleolimnology #freshwater-ecology #gastropoda #ostracoda

## Executive Summary

This document is an excerpt from a 1995 issue of *Nature*, not a fish study. Its central item is a research letter by [[Hodell]] and colleagues that uses a 4.9-m sediment core from [[Lake Chichancanab]], Yucatan, Mexico, to reconstruct Holocene climate. The authors find that the interval 1,300–1,100 yr BP (about AD 800–1000) was the driest of the middle to late Holocene, coinciding with the collapse of [[Classic Maya Collapse|Classic Maya civilization]]. The excerpt also contains a [[Sabloff]] News & Views commentary on that work, a materials-science paper on garnet inclusions in diamonds from the Mir kimberlite, a News & Views piece on vortex melting in superconductors, and the tail end of a paper on [[Catalytic Antibodies]]. Only the lake letter contains aquatic biological data, mainly shell-based proxies from gastropods, ostracods and foraminifera.

## Background & Description

The Maya civilization developed in Mesoamerica around 3,000 years ago and collapsed around AD 750–900 after its Classic period. Climate change had been proposed as a contributor, but pollen-based reconstructions were equivocal because human-driven deforestation alters vegetation in ways that resemble climate shifts. The authors therefore used lake sediments, which avoid this confound.

Lake Chichancanab is the largest closed-basin lake in Yucatan. Its name means "little sea" in Yucatec Maya. The lake is sulphate-rich (2,545 mg/l) with high salinity (total dissolved solids about 4,000 mg/l). Today the water is at or near saturation with respect to both [[Calcite]] (CaCO3) and [[Gypsum]] (CaSO4). Gypsum does not precipitate in the open lake now, but it occurs in littoral areas and at depth in the sediments.

The core was retrieved in 6.9 m of water from the central basin using a piston corer and a sediment-water interface corer. It was dated by [[Radiocarbon Dating]] of charcoal, seed and shell material. Regional climate today has a mean annual temperature of 25.4 °C and 1,300 mm of rain per year, with a wet season from May to September and a dry season from October to April.

No fish are reported in the document. The biological material consists of molluscs, ostracods and a benthic foraminifer used as indicators of past lake conditions.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Proxies and assumptions.** The authors assume that downcore shifts in the calcite-to-gypsum ratio reflect the hydrologic budget of the lake, which is controlled by the [[Evaporation-Precipitation Ratio]] (E/P). Shell carbonate δ18O is used as a second proxy. High E/P (dry climate) should raise shell δ18O and the gypsum-to-calcite ratio. Low E/P (wet climate) should lower both.

**Isotopic context.** In 1993 the mean lake-water δ18O was 3.24‰, slightly below the 3.4–5.4‰ range reported in 1973. This water is isotopically heavier than the regional weighted-mean precipitation (−4.0‰), which the authors attribute to evaporative loss of H2(16)O. In 1973, rain and spring water at Chichancanab measured −6.1‰ and −4.7‰, respectively. Dry-season evaporation controls the lake's oxygen-isotope composition.

**Dating complications.** Shells from aquatic taxa are artificially old because they incorporate ¹⁴C-deficient carbon from local limestone. This [[Hard-Water Lake Error]] is estimated at about 1,200 years from the aquatic regression intercept. Terrestrial materials, which derive carbon from atmospheric CO2, were used for the age-depth model. Both regressions have similar slopes, indicating a linear sedimentation rate of about 0.5 mm per year.

**Benthic and terrestrial indicators.** Below about 8,200 yr BP the sediments contain land snails (e.g., *Gastrocopta* and *Cecilioides*), indicating a terrestrial environment. The basin was dry during the late Glacial and earliest Holocene. A correlative core from Lake Esmeralda, the southern basin, also contained terrestrial gastropods before 8,000 yr BP.

**Lake filling.** Filling at about 8,200 yr BP, marked by the onset of gypsum precipitation, is linked to sea-level rise during the last deglaciation, which raised the freshwater aquifer of Yucatan. Between about 7,750 and 7,260 yr BP, abundant, well-preserved tests of the benthic foraminifer [[Ammonia Beccarii]] were present, and high E/P conditions were inferred.

**Charcoal.** A 7-cm charcoal layer just above the sulphur increase was dated to 7,560 ± 35 yr BP. Its δ13C of about −14.4‰ indicates C4 vegetation, probably burned grasses. Similar increases in charcoal near the Pleistocene/Holocene boundary elsewhere in Central America are attributed to natural or human-induced fire.

**Holocene moist and dry phases.** Relatively wet conditions prevailed from about 7,100 to 3,000 yr BP, with high calcite and low sulphur. This matches the Early to Middle Holocene moist period documented across the Neotropics, which has been linked to changes in seasonal insolation driven by the Earth's precessional cycle. The record is similar to that of [[Lake Miragoane]], Haiti, which was moist from about 7,000 to 4,000 yr BP. Drying began around 3,000 yr BP, and the interval 2,200–1,200 yr BP shows high sulphur and rising δ18O. Miragoane's dry period (2,500–1,500 yr BP) appears to be about 300 years earlier, which the authors attribute to underestimated dating error at that site.

## Key Findings

- **Peak aridity at about AD 800–1000.** The driest interval is ~1,300–1,100 yr BP. The peak is at 1,140 ± 35 yr BP (dated from a seed at 65 cm). Calendar conversion using a decadal tree-ring dataset gives an age range of about AD 800–1000, with the peak near AD 922.
- **Timing matches the collapse.** The authors state that this drying corresponds approximately to the Classic Maya collapse between AD 750 and 900.
- **First unambiguous evidence.** The authors claim these are the first unambiguous records of climate drying between AD 800 and 1000 in the region. Aside from the lake-filling period (8,000–7,200 yr BP), this is the driest episode of the past 8,000 years.
- **Limits of a single record.** The authors state that the Chichancanab record alone cannot establish regional late Classic drying. Low lake stands in central Mexico (1,400–900 yr BP) and increased fires in Costa Rica may mark the same shift. The response to climate was probably not uniform across the geologically, ecologically and climatically diverse Maya region.
- **Spatial pattern unresolved.** The collapse seems to have been strongest in the southern Maya lowlands, with populations shifting north. The authors call for further study of the magnitude and extent of the AD 800–1000 drought.
- **Commentary on the implications.** Sabloff argues that drought would have increased the vulnerability of southern lowland cities already under environmental stress. He notes that two centuries of dryness could also have hindered recovery, and he raises the broader question of how severe internal stresses must become before relatively minor climate shifts trigger collapse. Northern lowland sites such as [[Chichén Itzá]], [[Uxmal]], [[Kabah]] and Sayil flourished in this period.
- **Diamond inclusion study (separate item).** Trace-element data from chrome pyrope garnets in three peridotitic diamonds from the [[Mir Kimberlite Pipe]] (MR92/9, MR129/15, AV49) show heterogeneous distributions indicating rapid, disequilibrium crystal growth. The authors argue these diamonds crystallized shortly before kimberlite eruption about 360 Myr ago, despite Sm-Nd model ages up to 3.2 Gyr reported for other peridotitic inclusions. Cr2O3 ranges from 7.9 to 11.8 wt% and CaO from 1.3 to 2.6 wt%. Analytical uncertainties are 10–20% for rare-earth elements and 5–10% for other trace elements.
- **Other items.** A News & Views piece by Nelson discusses vortex melting in superconductors and related polymer physics. A partial conclusion from a paper on [[Catalytic Antibodies]] argues that altering catalytic residues and their presentation may help evolve antibody catalysts toward higher activity, which the authors suggest would also reveal more about enzymes.
