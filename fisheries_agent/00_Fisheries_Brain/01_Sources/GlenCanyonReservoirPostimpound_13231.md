# Lake Powell Post-Impoundment Fishery Investigation, 1966 (Progress Report No. 4)

#ichthyology #marine-biology #centrarchidae

## Executive Summary

This progress report from the cooperative [[Lake Powell]] study (Utah Division of Fish and Game and Arizona Game and Fish Department) covers February 1, 1966 to January 31, 1967, the fourth year of post-impoundment work on the reservoir behind [[Glen Canyon Dam]]. Utah handled physical and chemical limnology and fishery development; Arizona handled creel census, harvest, and plankton. The report documents a drought-reduced reservoir, strong thermal and chemical stratification driven by the [[Colorado River]] inflow, and a fishery dominated by [[Largemouth Bass]], [[Rainbow Trout]] and introduced or established non-game species. Rainbow trout stocking depended on a single 1964 year class, and natural trout reproduction was largely unsuccessful.

## Background & Description

Impoundment began in January 1963. The reservoir is a Southwestern desert reservoir filled under the [[Colorado River Storage Project]] (Section 8 funding). Fish stocking from 1963 to 1966 combined state agencies and the Bureau of Sport Fisheries and Wildlife. Cumulative stocking to date was about 13.2 million rainbow or kamloops trout, 2.9 million largemouth bass, 635,000 [[Kokanee Salmon]] fingerlings (1963-1964) and 44,050 [[Black Crappie]] (1964-1965). The 1966 plant was roughly 2.1 million 2-3 inch rainbow trout released by aircraft from May 6 to 11 at eight sites.

Sampling used horizontal and vertical gill nets, seines, electroshocking, and hook and line, with creel interviews at Wahweap, Lone Rock, Warm Creek, Bullfrog Bay, Hall's Crossing and Hite. Seventeen fixed stations were monitored for temperature, visibility and chemistry, and chemical samples were analyzed for [[Dissolved Oxygen]], pH, alkalinity, hardness, sulfate, chloride, silica, iron, phosphate and organic constituents.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Water management.** Reservoir elevation fluctuated sharply. Storage at the start of 1966 was 8,775,000 acre-feet (elevation 3535 ft, 67,524 acres). Spring runoff raised it to 9,501,000 acre-feet (3545 ft, 71,958 acres), flooding littoral vegetation that gave young fish cover. Runoff was poor, about 6,712,000 acre-feet against a normal of nearly 11 million. The reservoir fell 18 ft by year's end (elevation 3517), with storage about 850,000 acre-feet lower. The report judged this drawdown harmful to early life stages, especially [[Centrarchidae]] that spawned late.

**Thermal regime.** [[Lake Powell]] behaved as monomictic. A permanent [[Thermocline]] formed between early June and November, and the reservoir circulated the rest of the year. Winter surface temperatures fell to 39°F in the upper reservoir and 48°F near the dam. Average hypolimnion temperatures were 5-10°F colder than in 1965. Surface temperatures peaked at 80-83°F in July, and the summer thermocline ran from about 30 to 100 ft. The [[Colorado River]], entering at roughly 34°F in winter, flowed along the reservoir bottom as a [[Density Current]] from late December until March 1967.

**Water quality.** Summer runoff from the Colorado River carried organic matter into the thermocline. This produced low dissolved oxygen (2.6-3.2 ppm at thermocline depths in August), and water quality degraded as the summer progressed. Turbidity from the Colorado River affected more than two-thirds of the reservoir during runoff. The report judged that lower clarity mattered most when fish occupied depths where the river entered. Phosphate fell from 0.70 ppm (1964) to 0.60 ppm (1965) to 0.40 ppm (1966). Secchi readings in March averaged 12 ft and in April 27 ft, while Colorado River readings stayed below one foot.

**Vegetation and plankton.** Rooted aquatic plants were sparse. Russian thistle, tamarisk and saltbush established between high and low water marks and provided cover while water rose. Filamentous algae and periphyton grew on rock walls and submerged structures. Plankton data were reported separately by the Arizona Game and Fish Department.

## Key Findings

**Largemouth bass ([[Largemouth Bass]])**

- Scale analysis covered 584 bass, 361 trout and 28 black crappie. Condition factors used 731 bass, 843 trout and 28 crappie.
- Growth increased up-reservoir. Mean calculated total length (combined areas) was 227 mm at age I, 348 mm at age II and 410 mm at age III. Dam-to-Gunsight bass averaged 214 mm at age I, while Rincon-to-Tapestry bass averaged 249 mm. Growth in the lowest 50 miles was the slowest in the reservoir.
- Lower-reservoir growth rose with each year class, while upper-reservoir first-year growth fell (265 mm for the 1963 class to 234 mm for the 1965 class). By 1965 the upper-reservoir advantage had nearly disappeared.
- Condition factors averaged 1.87 at 120 mm, 2.35 at 255 mm, 2.66 at 420 mm and 3.27 at 495 mm. Factors were highest in March and April, before spawning, and lowest in July and August.
- Spawning was observed from late April in Wahweap Bay, but nests in shallow water were small and apparently produced little recruitment. Most recruitment likely came from deeper nests. Surface water temperatures in April (59°F and 58°F) were marginal for spawning. Nests were abandoned by mid-May.
- Creel-sampled bass harvest (February-October 1966) was an estimated 57,363 fish. The 1963 class made up 9%, the 1964 class 38% and the 1965 class about 52-53%. The text notes that no 1963 fish were taken from June onward. Fish near 12 inches were most common, and over 80% of creel-caught bass measured 10-17 inches.
- Food habits (244 stomachs): fish were the dominant food by volume in the lower reservoir (99.2% of volume), mainly [[Green Sunfish]] and other centrarchids. Crayfish appeared only in upper-reservoir diets (8.0-8.2% occurrence in age II and III bass). Carp and [[Rainbow Trout]] were also eaten.

**Rainbow trout ([[Rainbow Trout]])**

- Trout concentrated in Wahweap Bay and the main channel within two miles of the dam. Catch per net hour fell about 80% between the dam and Antelope Creek, about 75% from Antelope to Warm Creek, and about 50% above Aztec Canyon.
- Mean calculated lengths were 164 mm, 285 mm and 350 mm at the end of years one, two and three. Growth declined for each successive year class (180 mm at age I for the 1965 plant versus 216 mm for 1964).
- The 1964 class dominated the population and fishery. It made up 94% of the 1966 harvest, which was estimated at 39,733 fish. The 1963 and 1965 classes contributed 2% and 4%.
- Marked fish showed movement out of Wahweap Bay into the main channel and through the dam to the tailrace fishery. Population estimates showed a decline of about 75% between the 1965 summer and April 1966, and to about 5% of original numbers by the end of summer 1966.
- Summer vertical distribution (July-September) placed most trout at 20-80 ft, with dissolved oxygen of 3.2-3.6 ppm in the trout zone. Trout were concentrated near penstock depth, which ranged from 45 to 62 ft during netting. The report notes that penstock currents, temperature and plankton availability drew trout into low-oxygen water.
- Condition factors in 1966 were lower than in 1964 and 1965 across most lengths. Condition peaked at 1.95 in March and April, held near 1.50 from May to August, and fell to 1.25 by November. Natural reproduction was described as largely unsuccessful.

**Black crappie ([[Black Crappie]])**

- Twenty-one crappie were collected between April 1966 and January 1967, all in the Wahweap to Kane Creek area. They averaged 258 mm (April fish) and 240 mm (later fish) at capture.
- Mean growth was 108 mm in the first year and 103 mm in the second. Condition factor averaged 1.59.
- Young-of-the-year crappie collected in March 1967 provided the first evidence of successful reproduction by introduced black crappie.

**Channel catfish and walleye**

- [[Channel Catfish]]: 189 fish were taken from May to October 1966. Three year classes (1963, 1964 and 1965) were present, with modal lengths near 450, 338 and 213 mm. The 150-250 mm group was the most abundant of any yearling class to date, which the authors read as the first clearly successful class since impoundment. A nodule-forming internal parasite was common in late-year fish.
- [[Walleye]]: Four were netted (379, 432, 490 and 560 mm; ages IV, IV, III, VI). Walleye were not considered an important fishery, and the report treats them as probably spawned in lotic habitat before dam closure.

**Non-game species**

- [[Carp]] dominated the non-game catch and ranged from 150 to 430 mm, with length peaks at 170, 260, 300, 330 and 370 mm. The report concluded that Lake Powell is less than favorable carp habitat. Carp concentrated near the surface (above 74°F and within 40 ft).
- [[Green Sunfish]] declined in the creel and among large bass but remained widespread. Lengths ranged from 20 to 210 mm, and a substantial decline in large individuals was noted after 1965.
- [[Bluegill Sunfish]] increased in the upper reservoir: 57 were taken in 1966, with lengths of 170-270 mm (median and mode 220 mm). They were absent from bass stomachs that year.
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (the Gila robusta elegans form) occurred mainly in Wahweap Bay, between 230 and 420 mm, and appeared to spawn in early June.
- [[Colorado Squawfish]] (440-620 mm) and [[Flannelmouth Sucker]] (280-450 mm, mode near 370 mm) were taken in small numbers. Flannelmouth sucker was the third most abundant non-game fish and was increasing.
- A single red shiner was seined in Wahweap Bay. Fathead minnow and mosquitofish, abundant in the first years of impoundment, were absent after 1964.

**Overall conclusions**

- The report describes an environment in which summer stratification, density-current effects and drawdown strongly shaped fish habitat. Growth patterns varied sharply along the reservoir axis.
- Stocking success depended heavily on a single year class, and the authors recommended marking trout by reservoir area in future plantings.
