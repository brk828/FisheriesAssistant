# Literature Review: Thermal Requirements and Tolerances of Organisms Below Glen Canyon Dam

#ichthyology #marine-biology #cyprinidae

## Executive Summary

This draft literature review, prepared by Richard A. Lechleitner, assesses how water temperature governs the survival, growth, reproduction and life cycles of organisms in the [[Colorado River]] below [[Glen Canyon Dam]] and in the [[Grand Canyon]]. The dam releases cold water from the hypolimnion of [[Lake Powell]], so the tailwater is cold and thermally stable. Any move toward warmer releases, for example through a [[Selective Withdrawal]] structure, could alter the fauna. The review covers several taxa: the amphipod [[Gammarus Lacustris]], aquatic insects, the parasitic [[Asian Tapeworm]], the alga [[Cladophora Glomerata]], [[Epiphytic Diatoms]], and native and nonnative fishes. Its central conclusion is that the thermal tolerances of most local organisms are poorly known, so laboratory testing and multi-year experimental studies are needed before any prediction of ecosystem response.

## Background & Description

The document is an unfinished draft. Its main body is a set of short literature summaries, one for each organism group, followed by three large tables of fish temperature data and a long cited-literature list of about 80 entries. The tables give upper and lower lethal temperatures, optimum growth temperatures and preferred temperatures for native and nonnative fishes (Table 1), spawning periods and egg-hatching temperatures (Table 2), and egg and larval survival at different acclimation temperatures (Table 3). The review's stated aim is to predict how temperature changes would affect the downstream biota.

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**Gammarus lacustris (scud).** Smith (1973), working with a Minnesota population, found that *G. lacustris* acclimated to 18 °C had a 96-hour TL50 of 26 °C and a 30-day TL50 of 22–24 °C. Survival was 95% after 30 days at 18 °C. Reproduction was optimal at 18 °C and declined at 15, 21 and 24 °C. Temperatures below 15 °C were not tested. A viable population persists in Glen Canyon, where water never exceeds 15 °C, so its thermal tolerance may differ from the Minnesota population. The review calls for studies in both canyons.

**Aquatic insects.** Below deep-release dams, macroinvertebrate diversity is commonly reduced (Ward and Stanford 1979). The review argues that:
- A rapid spring warming is needed to trigger hatching in some species and emergence synchrony in others.
- Low summer temperatures may exclude species that cannot grow fast enough to compete.
- Many insects need a winter chill near 0 °C to break diapause, and the tailwater winter temperature stays near 7 °C, which may prevent completion of life cycles.

The insect fauna below Glen Canyon Dam is described as impoverished and dominated by aquatic Diptera, which make up as much as 85% of the standing crop of drifting invertebrates (Leibfried and Blinn 1988). Tributaries in Grand Canyon host a more diverse fauna and could act as sources for recolonization if conditions improved. Fraley (1979) observed higher macroinvertebrate density but lower diversity below a shallow reservoir. Mean summer temperatures there were 9.1 °C, about 3 °C warmer than upstream. The review proposes an experimental system fed with epilimnetic water from Lake Powell to test naturally colonized communities over several years.

**Asian tapeworm.** *[[Bothriocephalus Acheilognathi]]*, introduced to the United States with grass carp, has a complex life cycle: eggs are shed in feces, a coracidium hatches after 96 hours at 20 °C, copepods serve as intermediate hosts, and adults mature in the intestine of a fish. The parasite has been found in [[Humpback Chub]] from Grand Canyon. In 1990, 50% of larval humpback chub (13–35 mm total length) were infected, and none were infected in 1989. It has also been reported in the Virgin River in several endangered and native fishes. Granath and Esch (1983b) found that temperature strongly affected maturation and growth. At 20 °C, infected mosquitofish showed no maturation to egg-producing worms, while at 25 and 30 °C mature egg-producing worms dominated. Egg hatching was maximal at 30 °C. The review notes that warmer releases (above 20 °C) could increase the parasite's spread.

**Cladophora glomerata.** Thermal tolerances differ among races. In the Great Lakes, *Cladophora* dominated algal assemblages when temperatures rose above 15 °C and thrived up to 25 °C. In seven Ontario rivers, it dominated up to 18 °C, with an upper thermal tolerance of 23.5 °C (Wong et al. 1978). Whitton (1967) recorded large growths in a heated diversion of the River Wear at 25–26 °C, versus 15 °C in the main river. Lake Michigan populations had a thermal optimum of 28–31 °C (Lester et al. 1988). The review concludes that responses in Glen and Grand Canyon populations must be determined before predictions can be made.

**Epiphytic diatoms.** Diatoms on *Cladophora* changed within two weeks of exposure to warmer water. Species at 18 and 21 °C were smaller and grew closer to the substrate, replacing larger, upright, light-shielding species that dominate in cold tailwaters (Blinn et al. 1989). The threshold for this shift lay between 12 and 18 °C. Because grazers prefer upright diatoms (Sumner and McIntire 1982), a shift in diatom composition could affect macroinvertebrates below the dam. The review also links higher outflow temperatures to humpback chub reproduction, since optimal hatching occurs at 19–20 °C (Marsh 1985).

**Native and nonnative fishes.** The review refers to Tables 1–3 for temperature requirements. In broad terms, the tables show:
- Table 1 lists upper and lower lethal temperatures, optimum growth temperatures and preferred temperatures for roughly 20 native and nonnative species, including [[Humpback Chub]], [[Razorback Sucker]], [[Bonytail Chub]], [[Colorado Squawfish]], [[Rainbow Trout]], [[Brown Trout]], [[Brook Trout]], [[Common Carp]], [[Channel Catfish]], [[Striped Bass]], [[Walleye]] and [[Smallmouth Bass]].
- Table 2 gives spawning periods and egg-hatching temperatures. For example, humpback chub spawn in the Colorado River from late spring to midsummer, with optimal hatching at 19–20 °C, and razorback sucker have an optimum of 20 °C.
- Table 3 gives egg hatching success, time to hatch and fry survival at various acclimation temperatures. Values for several species are given by temperature.

Native species in the table are often reported with a narrow optimum range. Many cold-water species, including trout, show increased mortality above about 20 °C.

## Key Findings

- **Scud thermal tolerance:** The Minnesota-derived *Gammarus lacustris* data show a 30-day TL50 of 22–24 °C and an optimum reproduction temperature of 18 °C. Glen Canyon populations may be adapted differently, because the tailwater never exceeds 15 °C.
- **Insect life cycles:** Hypolimnetic releases disrupt spring warming cues and winter chill, potentially reducing diversity and reproductive success. Aquatic Diptera dominate the drift, and the insect fauna needs laboratory tolerance testing.
- **Recolonization sources:** Grand Canyon tributaries support more diverse macroinvertebrates than the mainstem and could supply species if conditions became favorable.
- **Asian tapeworm risk:** Infection reached 50% of sampled larval humpback chub in 1990 but was absent in 1989. Warmer water, roughly 20–30 °C, favors maturation and spread.
- **Cladophora and diatoms:** *Cladophora* tolerates up to about 23–31 °C depending on the population, and diatom communities shift at 12–18 °C. Both changes could cascade to grazers.
- **Fish data:** Tables 1–3 provide lethal, optimum and spawning temperatures for native and nonnative species, which the review considers the core basis for managing thermal releases.
- **Research gaps:** The review identifies a need for laboratory tests on the dominant insects, multi-year experimental systems fed by epilimnetic Lake Powell water, and population studies of Gammarus and Cladophora in both canyons.
