# Warmwater Native Fish Culture at a Coldwater Hatchery: Rearing Endangered Colorado River Fishes at Willow Beach

#ichthyology #marine-biology #catostomidae

## Executive Summary

This document is a conference proceedings paper from the symposia *Restoring Native Fish Populations and Habitats with a Hatchery Revival* (Las Vegas, Nevada, 1998 and 1999), published by the [[U.S. Fish And Wildlife Service]] Southwest Region in 2005. It describes how [[Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery]] (WBNFH), originally a coldwater trout facility, was modified into a solar-heated, partly recirculating system for rearing endangered warmwater fishes of the [[Colorado River]]. The authors report that solar collectors, well water and recirculation allow native fish to be reared in a cold hypolimnetic water supply. They also report the hatchery's role in [[Razorback Sucker]] and [[Bonytail Chub]] recovery under [[Endangered Species Act]] biological opinions. The paper concludes that hatchery propagation preserves genetic material but cannot replace habitat and ecosystem restoration.

## Background & Description

**Hatchery history.** WBNFH was established in 1960 to produce and distribute [[Rainbow Trout]] in the lower Colorado River basin, partly to mitigate impoundment by Lakes Mead and Mohave. In the 1960s and early 1970s, up to 700,000 trout (about 90,000 kg) were stocked annually into Lake Mohave. Those tailwater fisheries declined because of [[Striped Bass]] predation and a reduced forage base (Walters et al. 1996). The hatchery now distributes about 66,000 kg of trout annually to management areas including upper Lake Mohave, the tailwaters below Davis Dam, and several Indian reservations.

**Facility design.** WBNFH is located 18 km downstream of [[Hoover Dam]] and draws cold hypolimnetic water (12–14 °C year-round) from [[Lake Mead]]. Its original culture plant included:
- Forty outdoor concrete raceways (30.5 m × 2.4 m × 1.2 m deep).
- Thirty-six indoor concrete tanks (6 m × 0.75 m × 0.75 m deep).
- Pumps of 25, 50 or 75 hp drawing river water in a flow-through design.

**Endangered fish program.** In 1973 WBNFH was among the first hatcheries to rear threatened and endangered fishes (Hamman, pers. comm.). Under the [[Endangered Species Act]], the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service is responsible for listed species, and many recovery plans call for controlled propagation in federal hatcheries. Species reared on station include:
- [[Razorback Sucker]] (*Xyrauchen texanus*)
- [[Bonytail Chub]] (*Gila elegans*)
- [[Humpback Chub]] (*Gila cypha*)
- [[Colorado Pikeminnow]] (*Ptychocheilus lucius*)

These four species survive to adulthood in the wild but show little or no successful recruitment to maturity. The hatchery became active in native fish technology development in 1995 (abstract).

## Ecological & Environmental Context

**River water quality.** River water carries pathogens and parasites, including bacterial gill disease, *Ich*, *Columnaris*, *Gyrodactylus*, *Chilodonella* and *Costia*. These inhibit growth and survival, so the hatchery uses [[Ultraviolet Sterilization]] and biological filtration. Biological filter media host bacteria that convert ammonia to nitrite and nitrate.

**Climate and energy.** The authors cite more than 340 sunny days per year, about 7 cm of annual rain, and summer temperatures up to 50 °C. These conditions make [[Solar Water Heating]] inexpensive, and this solar power warms the river water. Well water at about 18 °C is used with the solar panels to regulate temperature.

**Genetic conservation.** Controlled propagation is presented as a way to retain genetic diversity in declining populations and return it to the system. A multi-agency effort captured up to 100,000 wild larval razorback suckers annually from spawning sites and brought them to WBNFH. Adults collected from two river sites (river miles 35 and 65) were spawned at WBNFH, [[Dexter National Fish Hatchery And Technology Center]] and [[Mora National Fish Hatchery]]. These facilities produced up to 300,000 larvae for distribution across the Colorado River system.

**Habitat context.** The authors state that hatchery culture "does provide for the long-term conservation and management of genetic material," but it "does not replace the preservation and restoration of habitats and ecosystems."

## Key Findings

**1. Endangered warmwater fish can be reared at a coldwater hatchery.** Modifications to WBNFH indicate that it is possible to rear endangered native fishes in a facility built for trout. Solar heating and well water make this feasible.

**2. Recirculation and solar heating are the core design elements.** The main modifications were:
- **Reservoir and aquaria:** Four connected concrete tanks form a reservoir. A submersible pump (95 L/min) lifts water to roof-mounted solar collectors, and the warmed water feeds eighty-five 35-L aquaria for egg hatching and larval growth. Two electric portable heaters maintain reservoir temperature at night.
- **Recirculating raceways:** Four recirculating systems were created from existing trout raceways. A slot (50 cm × 30 cm) joins two parallel raceways, and a submersible pump (225 L/min) returns water from tail to head. Turnover is generally 3 to 4 days.
- **Outdoor raceway modification:** Twelve of the outdoor raceways were isolated from the gravity-fed trout system. A 3/4 hp pump (112 L/min) moves water through a [[Diatomaceous Earth Filter]] and an in-line UV sterilizer (120 W) to solar panels above the raceways.
- **Biofiltration and aeration:** Biological filter media (about 1.75 m³ in the recirculating system) supports nitrifying bacteria. A regenerative blower supplies air to aquaria, tanks and filters.

**3. Rearing protocols.** Larval fish are fed up to ten times daily with live brine shrimp, and syphoning is needed to remove excess feed and waste. Larger fish are fed prepared feed by belt feeder.

**4. Cost.** Table 1 estimates the equipment and supplies needed to convert one pair of raceways to solar-heated recirculating water at **$24,850** (1999 dollars). The authors note that initial costs may vary because the solar collection mats and biological filter media are petroleum-based products.

**5. Production and stocking.**
- Razorback suckers: larvae are grown to 100–150 mm and stocked into rearing facilities along the Colorado River in Arizona and Nevada for grow-out to 250–300 mm. Some fish are kept at WBNFH and grown to 300 mm before release.
- Bonytail: larvae from the broodstock at Dexter NFH&TC are reared to 250 mm before release into the Colorado River.
- Colorado Pikeminnow and Humpback Chub: the authors are exploring large-scale production. Warmwater culture techniques were developed at WBNFH over the previous five years.

**6. Seasonal extension.** Well water is used in single-use flow-through raceways during winter to add rearing space. The authors note that the well water can extend the growing season into colder months, resulting in faster growth.

**7. Future directions.** The authors state that technology development and controlled propagation must continue to prevent loss of genetic resources and to maintain management options for recovery. Systems that reduce space and energy needs while improving water quality are expected to help. Bead filters may be used to remove solids and ammonia and free space now used by biofilters.

**Conclusion.** Hatchery propagation secures genetic material for the long term, but the authors state that it is not a substitute for restoring habitats and ecosystems.
