# Letter from James E. Deacon to Clark Hubbs on Fish Refugia in Nevada

## Executive Summary
This 1975 letter from Dr. James E. Deacon (University of Nevada, Las Vegas) to Dr. Clark Hubbs (University of Texas, Austin) documents early efforts to establish conservation **refugia** for imperiled desert fish species in Nevada and the broader Colorado River basin. Deacon outlines existing and planned refugium projects, ranks priority species for conservation attention, and identifies collaborators and institutions involved in these efforts. The letter is a key historical record of the coordinated, multi-institutional response to the endangerment of desert fishes in the arid Southwestern United States during the mid-1970s, a formative period for [[Endangered Species Act]] implementation and desert fish conservation biology.

## Background & Description
The correspondence responds to a proposal ("the refugium idea") to establish captive or semi-natural holding populations for fish species threatened by habitat loss, groundwater depletion, and introduced species in Nevada's desert spring systems. Deacon reports that refugia — either **artificial** or **natural** — were already in varying stages of development for several taxa, including:

- **[[Cyprinodon diabolis]]** (Devils Hole pupfish)
- **[[Empetrichthys latos]]** (Pahrump poolfish)
- **[[Crenichthys baileyi]]** (White River springfish)
- **[[Gila robusta jordani]]** (Pahranagat roundtail chub)
- **[[Moapa coriacea]]** (Moapa dace)
- **[[Cyprinodon nevadensis mionectes]]** (Ash Meadows Amargosa pupfish subspecies)
- **[[Cyprinodon nevadensis pectoralis]]** (Warm Springs Amargosa pupfish subspecies)
- **[[Crenichthys nevadae]]** (Railroad Valley springfish)

Additionally, the **Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery** had successfully reared **[[Xyrauchen texanus]]** (razorback sucker) and **[[Ptychocheilus lucius]]** (Colorado pikeminnow), and was attempting propagation of **[[Gila elegans]]** (bonytail chub) that same spring. The **Phoenix Zoo** had also developed preliminary plans for an on-site refugium, supported in part by funding from the New York Zoological Society (facilitated through Dr. Robert R. Miller).

## Ecological & Environmental Context
The species discussed are largely endemic to isolated **desert spring and spring-fed stream systems** in the Great Basin and lower Colorado River drainage — habitats characterized by small population sizes, high endemism, and extreme vulnerability to groundwater pumping, spring capture, habitat modification, and introduced non-native species. Localities implicated include:

- **Ash Meadows**, Nevada — home to multiple pupfish (*Cyprinodon*) subspecies and the Ash Meadows stock of **[[Rhinichthys osculus]]** (speckled dace)
- **Moapa Valley** — habitat of *Moapa coriacea*
- **Pyramid Lake** — habitat of **[[Chasmistes cujus]]** (cui-ui), a lake sucker
- **White River drainage** — habitat of *Crenichthys baileyi* and the White River stock of *Rhinichthys osculus*
- **Virgin River drainage** — habitat of **[[Plagopterus argentissimus]]** (woundfin) and **[[Lepidomeda mollispinis]]** (Virgin spinedace)
- **Lower Colorado River basin** — habitat of large-river catostomids and cyprinids (*Xyrauchen texanus*, *Ptychocheilus lucius*, *Gila elegans*)

This letter reflects growing recognition in the 1970s that fragmented, isolated desert aquatic habitats required **ex situ** and **refugial in situ** conservation strategies to buffer against imminent habitat loss, complementing in-situ protection of natural spring systems. The described network of species anticipates later formal recovery efforts for [[Desert Fishes Council]]-associated taxa.

## Key Findings
- **Existing/developing refugia (1975)** for eight Nevada-endemic species/subspecies: *Cyprinodon diabolis*, *Empetrichthys latos*, *Crenichthys baileyi*, *Gila robusta jordani*, *Moapa coriacea*, *Cyprinodon nevadensis mionectes*, *Cyprinodon nevadensis pectoralis*, and *Crenichthys nevadae*.
- **Willow Beach National Fish Hatchery** had successfully propagated *Xyrauchen texanus* and *Ptychocheilus lucius*, with *Gila elegans* propagation attempts planned for Spring 1975 — an early hatchery-based approach to Colorado River big-river fish conservation.
- **Phoenix Zoo** was developing a refugium program, partly funded through the New York Zoological Society via Dr. Robert R. Miller.
- Deacon provided a **ranked priority list** of Nevada species recommended for refugium inclusion, in descending order of conservation urgency:
  1. *Moapa coriacea*
  2. *Gila robusta jordani*
  3. *Empetrichthys latos*
  4. Ash Meadows stock of *Rhinichthys osculus*
  5. *Chasmistes cujus*
  6. *Cyprinodon diabolis*
  7. *Cyprinodon nevadensis pectoralis*
  8. *Cyprinodon nevadensis mionectes*
  9. *Pantosteus clarki intermedius*
  10. *Lepidomeda albivallis*
  11. White River stock of *Rhinichthys osculus*
  12. *Eremichthys acros*
  13. *Gila elegans*
  14. *Xyrauchen texanus*
  15. *Plagopterus argentissimus*
  16. *Lepidomeda mollispinis*
  17. *Crenichthys baileyi*
  18. *Crenichthys nevadae*
  19. *Relictus solitarius*
- Deacon notes this list is not exhaustive and emphasizes the value of a **coordinated, multi-institutional program** for desert fish conservation.
- Additional contacts identified as potential collaborators: Dr. Dave Koch (Desert Research Institute, UNR), Jim Yoakum (BLM, Reno), Tom Trelease and Dale Lockard (Nevada Fish and Game, Reno), and Dr. Jim LaBounty (Bureau of Reclamation, Denver).
- The letter was circulated (CC'd) to a broad network of leading ichthyologists and conservation scientists of the era, including Robert R. Miller, W. L. Minckley, Anthony Echelle, Douglas Jester, William Koster, Jim Williams, Rudolph Miller, Salvador Contreras, Carl L. Hubbs, Bobby Whiteside, and Bill Sigler — indicating the collaborative, cross-institutional nature of early desert fish conservation planning.
